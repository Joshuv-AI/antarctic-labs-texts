Association between model of anesthesiology care and outcomes after cataract surgery: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Sylvie D. Aucoin, Jamal Alkadri, David Maberley, Chengchun Yu, Daniel I. McIsaac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Cataract surgery is usually performed with topical anesthesia and sedation, which may be provided through a number of different care models with or without involvement of a physician anesthesiologist. We sought to describe anesthesiology care for cataract surgery in Ontario, Canada, and examine the association between the model of anesthesiology care and health service and perioperative outcomes after cataract surgery.Methods:We conducted a population-based study of patients who underwent cataract surgery in Ontario using linked health administrative data between 2012 and 2021. The primary outcome was a composite of death, acute hospital admission, or emergency department visit up to 7 days postoperatively. We also collected data on health system costs and operative complications (from physician billing codes). We used multilevel, multivariable regression to estimate adjusted associations between anesthesiology care — fee-for-service anesthesiology care (FFS), care from an anesthesiology care team (ACT), or no physician anesthesiologist care — and outcomes.Results:Of 1 271 251 patients who underwent cataract surgery, 670 754 (52.8%) received FFS care, 256 760 (20.2%) received ACT care, and 343 737 (27.0%) had no physician anesthesiologist care. Adjusted analyses estimated slightly lower odds of the primary composite outcome for patients who received FFS care (n = 8682, 1.3%; adjusted odds ratio [OR] 0.93, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.88 to 0.99) or ACT care (n = 3034, 1.2%; adjusted OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.89) compared with those with no physician anesthesiologist care (n = 5056, 1.5%). Odds of operative complications were lower and overall costs were higher with any type of physician-provided anesthesiology care. Primary results differed by whether care was provided in a teaching or nonteaching hospital.Interpretation:Care provided or overseen by a physician anesthesiologist was associated with significantly lower odds of an emergency department visit, hospital admission, or death; however, effect sizes were small and of limited clinical importance, and costs were higher. Given these findings, the use of physician anesthesiology care for routine cataract surgery should be reconsidered. Cataracts are a leading cause of visual impairment,1 with an incidence of 56% among people aged 75 years or older.2 Visual impairment due to cataracts can lead to falls, anxiety, depression, and reduced quality of life.3 Cataract surgery is the most common surgical procedure performed in Canada,4 with approximately 350 000 procedures performed annually.5 As the population in Canada ages, access to safe cataract care, including reducing wait times, is a priority.6,7

Advances in surgical techniques mean that cataract surgery is now quicker and less invasive. Routine cataract surgery is performed under topical anesthesia with intravenous, oral, or no sedation,8,9 both within and off hospital premises. The structures and processes of care around cataract surgery, such as preoperative testing and the presence of physician anesthesiologists, differs substantially across jurisdictions.10–13 Sedation may be delivered by a physician anesthesiologist, anesthesia assistant, respiratory therapist, nurse, or the operating surgeon, or by an anesthesiology care team (ACT). In the latter model, 1 supervising physician anesthesiologist is responsible for up to 5 patients concurrently, with anesthesia assistants or nurses providing 1-on-1 care. Data are lacking regarding anesthesiology care for patients undergoing cataract surgery in Canada and the association between different models of anesthesiology care and perioperative outcomes.11,14

Given health care costs and current limitations in anesthesiology human resources, understanding the processes and outcomes of anesthesiology care is important to ensure optimal matching of providers to areas of need. We sought to describe provision of anesthesiology care for cataract surgery in Ontario and estimate the association between models of anesthesiology care and health service use and perioperative outcomes after cataract surgery. We hypothesized that physician anesthesiologist care for routine cataract surgery would not be associated with clinically meaningful improvement in perioperative outcomes compared with those performed with no physician anesthesiologist care.

Methods

Study design and data sources

We conducted a historical observational cohort study (Apr. 1, 2012, to Mar. 31, 2021). Data were acquired through ICES, a provincial health data research institute that houses routinely generated health data for all physician and hospital care for residents of Ontario, Canada (approximate population 14 million). We constructed the analytic data set using deterministic linkage based on an anonymized unique identifier. Databases relevant to this study are listed in Appendix 1, Section 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.250814/tab-related-content. We reported findings in accordance with the Reporting of Studies Conducted using Observational Routinely Collected Health Data statements. We developed and published the study protocol a priori (https://osf.io/9y3mt/).

Cohort

We identified all adults who underwent their first episode of unilateral cataract surgery, defined by their first Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) billing code E140A (surgeon fee-for-service [FFS] code for cataract surgery) in the study period (Apr. 1, 2012, to Mar. 31, 2021). The date of surgery was used as the index date. Physician billing codes in Ontario are valid in identifying physician FFS care.15

Exposure

The exposure was model of anesthesiology care, defined using a 3-level categorical variable: FFS physician anesthesiologist care (FFS anesthesiology code E140C or E023C billed concurrently with E140A), ACT (performed at 1 of the 8 centres using Ontario Ministry of Health–funded ACT models), or no physician anesthesiologist care (procedure not performed at a listed ACT centre and no OHIP anesthesiology code billed, meaning that sedation was likely managed by treating ophthalmologist with nonphysician anesthesiologist support). The reference category was the group with no physician anesthesiologist care.

Covariates

Because the association between model of anesthesiology care and outcomes could be confounded by patient, provider, or health system factors, we identified all available covariates in our databases that we postulated could act as confounders. We identified the operative surgeon for each cataract using the deidentified physician billing number. We also identified the type of premise according to whether the surgery was conducted at a teaching hospital (defined as group A, D, H, and L Ontario hospitals using standard Ministry of Health classification)16 or nonteaching hospital, or within or out of hospital premises. Surgeries conducted in ambulatory centres that were part of hospitals (teaching or nonteaching) were categorized as occurring within hospital premises, and those conducted at facilities that had no affiliation with hospitals were categorized as occurring out of hospital premises. Of the 8 ACT sites, 4 were categorized as teaching hospitals, 3 were nonteaching hospitals and 1 was categorized as a teaching out-of-hospital site. Patient-level covariates included sex, age, year of surgery, socioeconomic status (based on neighbourhood income quintile), rural residency, any emergency department visit in the year before surgery, any acute hospitalization in the year before surgery, residence in long-term care at index date, receipt of home care at index date, each Elixhauser comorbidity (based on a 3-year lookback),17 frailty index score,18 preoperative anesthesiology consult, preoperative internal medicine consult, and filling a preoperative opioid or benzodiazepine prescription in the 6 months before surgery.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was a composite of death, acute hospital admission, or emergency department visit on or up to 7 days after the index date. Secondary outcomes included all individual components of the composite primary outcome, as well as the primary outcome defined up to 28 days. We also collected total health system costs up to 7 and 28 days postoperatively. We estimated patient-level costs using a validated methodology that captures costs from the perspective of the Ministry of Health, accounting for direct and indirect costs.19 Because anesthesiology care could influence the incidence of intraoperative surgical complications, we created an exploratory outcome variable that was a composite of intraoperative and postoperative surgical billing codes consistent with procedural complication. This variable was coded as present if prespecified OHIP billing codes (E127, E950, E138, E141, E142, E144, E145, E146, E148, E149, E151, E152, E155, or E940) were used on or up to 7 days after the index date (Appendix 1, Section 2). These codes have been used in previous studies of cataract surgery outcomes.20

Statistical analysis

We compared descriptive data at the index date, stratified by type of anesthesiology care, using absolute standardized differences.21 We calculated the proportion of cataract surgeries performed, overall and by year, by type of anesthesiology care. We calculated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the Wilson method.

We estimated unadjusted and adjusted associations between model of anesthesiology care and outcomes using multilevel, multivariable regression. Because ACT models are hospital-specific and out-of-hospital premises are not uniquely identifiable, we clustered at the level of the operative surgeon (using generalized estimating equations), while adjusting for premise type using 2 binary variables (teaching status and in v. out of hospital) as fixed effects. Other fixed effects included sex (binary), age (restricted cubic spline [RCS] with 3 knots), year of surgery (RCS 3 knots), neighbourhood income quintile (5-level categorical), rural versus urban (binary), any emergency department visit in the year before surgery (binary), any acute hospital admission in the year before surgery (binary), residence in long-term care at index date (binary), receipt of home care at index date (binary), Elixhauser comorbidities (each as binary terms), frailty index score (RCS 3 knots), receipt of preoperative anesthesiology consult (binary), receipt of preoperative internal medicine consult (binary), and having filled a preoperative opioid (binary) or benzodiazepine (binary) prescription. For binary outcomes, we used logistic regression to generate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs. For skewed, continuous outcomes, we used generalized linear models with a log-link and γ distribution to generate ratios of means and 95% CIs. We estimated adjusted absolute risk differences using marginal standardization from the multivariable logistic regression model, with 95% CIs based on the percentile method from 1000 bootstrap samples created with replacement.22 We used a 2-sided α of 0.05 to identify statistically significant results. We considered absolute standardized differences greater than 0.10 as clinically important.

Sensitivity analyses

To test the robustness and assumptions underlying our primary analysis, we conducted a series of prespecified sensitivity analyses (adjusting for the same covariates as above, unless specified), where analysis was limited to patients younger than 75 years, patients aged 75 years or older, patients with a frailty index score less than 0.21, patients with a frailty index score of 0.21 or greater, patients whose procedures were performed at out-of-hospital premises, patients without a preoperative opioid prescription, and patients with a preoperative opioid prescription. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis with a binary exposure of any anesthesiology care.

We conducted 4 post hoc sensitivity analyses limited to procedures performed at a teaching hospital, procedures performed at a nonteaching hospital, excluding procedures performed by surgeons with the lowest 5% of cataract surgery volumes, and excluding procedures with E140A and concurrent OHIP billing codes E142, E148, E151, and 155 billed on the same day as these could represent a combined primary procedure as opposed to a secondary procedure treating an intraoperative complication.

Ethics approval

This project was authorized by the privacy office at ICES. The use of data in this study was authorized under section 45 of Ontario’s Personal Health Information Protection Act and did not require research ethics board approval.

Results

A total of 1 271 251 first cataract surgeries were performed over the study period; 670 754 (52.8%) patients received FFS anesthesiologist care, 256 760 (20.2%) patients received ACT care, and 343 737 (27.0%) had no physician anesthesiologist care. Patient characteristics did not differ meaningfully among the 3 exposure groups, except for operative settings and rural residence. Surgeries with FFS anesthesiologist care were less frequently performed in a teaching hospital (6.2%) than those with ACT care (64.0%) or no physician anesthesiologist care (24.2%), and surgeries with ACT care were less frequently performed in out-of-hospital settings (21.3%) than those with FFS anesthesiologist care (36.7%) and no physician anesthesiologist care (42.1%).

Primary outcome

The rate of primary outcome events was 1.3% (8682 events among patients who received FFS anesthesiologist care, 1.2% (3034 events) among patients who received ACT care, and 1.5% (5065 events) among patients who did not receive physician anesthesiologist care. Events were primarily driven by emergency department visits (Table 2). Compared with patients with no physician anesthesiologist care, the unadjusted OR of the primary outcome for patients who received FFS care was 0.88 (95% CI 0.85 to 0.91) and 0.80 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.84) for those who received ACT (Appendix 1, Section 3). After adjustment, effect estimates were attenuated but remained statistically significant for FFS anesthesiologist care (adjusted OR 0.93, 95% CI 0.88 to 0.99) and ACT care (adjusted OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.89). The number needed to treat (derived from the adjusted marginal absolute risk difference in Table 2) was 1025 for FFS anesthesiologist care and 535 for ACT care.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses for the primary outcome are presented in Figure 2 (numeric results are reported in Appendix 1, Section 6). Analyses limited to care provided in teaching hospitals demonstrated no difference in outcomes for FFS anesthesiologist care compared with no physician anesthesiologist care (adjusted OR 1.01, 95% CI 0.86 to 1.19) and attenuated the estimate for ACT care (adjusted OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.75 to 0.99). In analysis limited to care in nonteaching hospitals, the effect estimate for FFS care (adjusted OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.97) was materially unchanged, whereas for ACT care (adjusted OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.83), the effect estimate was larger than in the full population analysis. Effect estimates for FFS anesthesiologist care in a number of patient subgroups were also not significantly different than care without a physician anesthesiologist (those aged ≥ 75 yr, those living with frailty, and those who received an opioid prescription in the 6 mo before surgery). Post hoc analysis excluding possible combined procedures demonstrated no difference in outcomes for FFS anesthesiologist care (adjusted OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.02) and attenuated the estimate for ACT (adjusted OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.79 to 0.92).

Interpretation

In this population-based cohort study of models of anesthesiology care for cataract surgery, care provided or overseen by a physician anesthesiologist (either billed as FFS anesthesiologist care or as a procedure performed in 1 of the 8 centres using Ministry of Health–funded ACT models) was significantly associated with lower odds of emergency department visits, hospital admissions, or death within 7 days of surgery than if sedation was delivered without physician anesthesiologist involvement, Effect sizes were small, however, and the significance of these differences varied by location of care and patient subgroup. Whether these estimates represent clinically meaningful differences in the face of low event rates, driven primarily by emergency department visits, must be considered. Costs were higher overall for care involving physician anesthesiologists. These results suggest a complex association between models of anesthesiology care, health system structure, clinical outcomes, and value in cataract surgery.

Cataracts are a common indication for surgery4 and, in contemporary practice, serious adverse events are rare (7-day all-cause postoperative mortality rate was 1 in 10 000 in our study). Although our data suggest that patients receiving care provided or overseen by a physician anesthesiologist had statistically significantly lower odds of the primary composite outcome of any emergency department visit, admission, or death within 7 days, the number needed to treat, compared with involving no physician anesthesiologist, was 1025 for the FFS anesthesiologist group and 535 for the ACT group. As our primary composite outcome was largely driven by emergency department visits, one interpretation is that hundreds of patients need to receive care from a physician anesthesiologist to avoid a single additional emergency department visit. The lack of consistency in direction of effect in subgroup analyses, including a statistically different result for patients who received ACT when analyses were limited to teaching hospitals, contributes to further uncertainty about the causality and clinical importance of the primary results.

Understanding why care overseen by physician anesthesiologists was associated with lower odds of emergency department visits and possibly eye-specific complication rates, along with inconsistency in subgroup analyses, is difficult when using administrative data. However, differences in the locations where different anesthesiology care models were delivered may provide important insights. Few surgeries with FFS anesthesiologist care were performed in teaching hospitals, while those with ACT care were mainly in hospital-based settings. Although our models adjusted for care location and clustered results by surgeon, differences in structures and processes of care not captured by our data, along with other unmeasured confounders, may limit generalizability across all patients undergoing cataract surgery and could partly explain divergent results seen in subgroup analyses. For example, patients cared for at out-of-hospital locations were less frequently overseen by a physician anesthesiologist, and these locations may have less capacity to manage acute events, leading to greater need for emergency department visits, regardless of anesthesiology care model. Conversely, a surgeon may choose to operate in a setting with physician anesthesiologists if surgical complexity is anticipated.

Our sensitivity analyses demonstrated substantial heterogeneity across patient groups and care locations. Benefits of physician anesthesiologist care were attenuated or absent in several patient subgroups (patients aged ≥ 75 yr, those with frailty, those using opioids preoperatively under FFS anesthesiologist care) and care locations (out of hospital, teaching hospital). In teaching hospitals, FFS anesthesiologist care was not associated with improved outcomes, and ACT effects were attenuated for ACT care compared with the primary analysis. In contrast, the analysis of nonteaching hospitals had larger effect sizes for ACT care than in the overall cohort. These findings suggest that site-specific structures and processes, such as staffing patterns and monitoring resource, may modify the impact of physician anesthesiologist involvement. Possible explanations are that teaching hospitals have better staffing and resources, and that ophthalmology trainees improve a site’s capacity to manage acute, unexpected events. Conversely, in nonteaching hospitals, where resources may be more limited, ACT models may provide greater system-level support.

Secondary outcomes largely mirrored the primary findings. Involvement of physician anesthesiologists was associated with small reductions in the composite outcome at 28 days and with lower odds of billing codes consistent with possible intraoperative or postoperative complications. Absolute differences and resulting effect sizes were small. Although our exploratory analysis of possible intraoperative or postoperative complications suggests benefit for FFS anesthesiologist and ACT care, post hoc exclusion of procedures likely representing combined procedures attenuated associations with ACT care and eliminated those for FFS anesthesiologist care. This is a limitation of the use of surgical billing codes as a surrogate marker for surgical complications, since they do not capture indication or variation in billing practices among surgeons. As a result, we cannot draw definitive conclusions regarding model of anesthesiology care and risk of surgical complications.

Overall, our findings add to the growing body of research evaluating physician anesthesiologist–delivered care for routine cataract surgery. Previous studies have reported few significant differences in intraoperative complications,11,13,14,23 visual acuity improvements,24 or patient satisfaction8,13 when comparing care with or without physician anesthesiologists, but definitive results remain elusive. However, changes in practice over the past 2 decades — whereby anesthesiologist services in some Canadian and international jurisdictions have been withdrawn from routine cataract surgery as care has transitioned to nurse-led sedation models25 — suggest that routine care overseen by physician anesthesiologists is not a necessity.12,23,26 In Ontario, a consistently high proportion (approximately 75%) of cataract surgeries were performed with physician anesthesiologist care over the study period. In comparison, reported rates of physician anesthesiologist care in other jurisdictions vary from 0% (France),13 to 3% (United Kingdom),12 to 89% (United States).11 Given the ongoing crisis in anesthesiology human resources in Canada, which has led to widespread operating room closures, 25,27 and the higher health care system costs and minimal impact on patient outcomes estimated in our study, alternative delivery models, including ACTs and sedation provided by nonphysician providers, should be strongly considered. This will help improve access to both cataract surgery and other surgical procedures by optimizing use of scarce anesthesiology resources.

Limitations

We prespecified our protocol to reduce performance bias, and adjusted for postulated confounders and care delivery location, while clustering by surgeon, to address possible differences in perioperative processes of care. However, misclassification bias was possible. Although physician billing codes are typically accurate regarding provision of procedural care, we were unable to ascertain the indication codes defining procedural complications. Unmeasured confounding could limit causal inference. As cohort entry required billing for a cataract procedure, we could not estimate the association between anesthesiology care and day-of-surgery cancellations. Administrative data do not capture other important outcomes like visual acuity or patient satisfaction. How results generalize beyond Ontario cannot be assumed. Costs of care reflected only costs from the perspective of the provincial Ministry of Health, so do not account for out-of-pocket expenses incurred by patients. Although the approach used has been validated and is widely used, indirect costs rely on estimation, which decreases the granularity of cost ascertainment. Although formal health economic analyses were beyond the scope or our study and underlying data, formal cost-effectiveness analyses should inform changes to policy and practice, as should perspectives of patients, surgeons, and anesthesia providers.

Conclusion

In this population-based cohort study of patients who underwent cataract surgery, care provided or overseen by a physician anesthesiologist was associated with significantly lower odds of emergency department visit, hospital admission, or death within the first 7 days after surgery, mainly driven by lower odds of emergency department visits. However, these effects were inconsistent in sensitivity analyses and are unlikely to be clinically meaningful. Given the additional cost of physician anesthesiologist care, its impact on anesthesiology human resources, and limited clinical benefit, the use of physician anesthesiology care for routine cataract surgery should be reconsidered.
