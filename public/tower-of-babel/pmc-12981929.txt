A pilot randomized trial of a virtual perioperative smoking cessation bundle in a tertiary care hospital
Authors: Michal Pillar, Rahnuma Sara, Dianne Burditt, Roopal Kaur Rai, Lauren Monteiro, Raunika Lertnamvongwan, Michelle Mozel, Richard Merchant, Susan M Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Smokers have increased complications after surgery. We sought to study the feasibility of a structured virtual smoking cessation bundle.Methods:We conducted a pilot parallel randomized controlled trial involving adult smokers scheduled for elective surgery 1 or more weeks after enrolment. The intervention bundle consisted of an emailed video and brochure, QuitNow referral, and pharmacy referral. The control group received usual care of uncoordinated advice on smoking cessation. Perioperative caregivers, but not participants, were blinded to group allocation. The primary outcome was the participant’s self-reported uptake of smoking cessation information and available resources.Results:We enrolled the target 30 patients (15 intervention and 15 control). The recruitment rate was 0.8 patients/wk; 59% (30/51) of all eligible patients were enrolled. By day of surgery, 1 patient withdrew from the study and 1 was lost to follow-up. The median (interquartile range [IQR]) number of smoking cessation resources used was higher in the intervention group than in the control group on the day of surgery (1 [IQR 1 to 3] v. 0 [IQR 0–1], p = 0.002), 30 days after surgery (1 [IQR 0 to 2] v. 0 [IQR 0 to 0], p = 0.01), and 8 weeks after randomization (1 [IQR 0 to 1] v. 0 [IQR 0 to 0], p = 0.003), but not different at 6-month follow-up (1 [IQR 0 to 1] v. 0 [IQR 0 to 0], p = 0.1).Conclusion:Patients in the intervention group reported more use of smoking cessation resources than those in the control group. This pilot trial demonstrated that a virtually delivered preoperative smoking cessation bundle was feasible and acceptable to patients.Trial registration:ClinicalTrials.gov, no. NCT04487548.

As of 2022, 3.7 million Canadians (12% of the population older than 15 yr) reported being current cigarette smokers.1 Smokers have a higher risk of death and complications after surgery than nonsmokers.2,3 Cardiovascular complications,4 increased risk of bleeding during surgery,5 and wound healing complications6 mediate smoking-associated morbidity. It is widely established that implementation of perioperative smoking cessation programs has the potential to reduce morbidity and mortality. For example, intensive interventions have been associated with reduced wound complications (relative risk 0.31, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.16 to 0.62).7 Despite the benefits, most anesthesiologists do not routinely offer advice on smoking cessation to their patients,8,9 and advice, counselling, and pharmacotherapy for smoking cessation are not routine in preadmission clinics.10–12 Moreover, the COVID-19 pandemic reduced the opportunity for in-person contact with patients to implement smoking cessation interventions, and many preadmission clinics continue to use virtual appointments for preoperative assessments,13 raising the question of whether remotely delivered interventions for smoking cessation are feasible or effective.

To mitigate the barrier of in-person contact, various remotely delivered means are being explored, such as telehealth, 14 home delivery of medications,15 mobile applications, 16–20 or virtual reality.21 Although promising, these emerging interventions currently lack the definitive results of established smoking cessation therapies initiated in person. Previous perioperative studies, which relied on in-person contact with patients in preadmission clinics, have shown that smoking cessation bundles — consisting of brief advice, a brochure, a referral to a telephone quit line, and provision of nicotine replacement therapy [NRT]22 —are effective in improving both short-term and long-term smoking cessation, resulting in quit rates of 14% as of the day of surgery, 29% at 30 days after surgery, and 25% at 1 year after surgery.23 Restrictions during the COVID-19 pandemic that disrupted in-person clinical care and clinical trial processes,24 combined with relatively low rates of smoking in British Columbia,1 presented challenges to patient recruitment.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to examine the feasibility of a remotely delivered smoking cessation bundle by establishing the recruitment rate and the degree of access to available resources. These feasibility outcomes are crucial for the planning of a larger study examining the effectiveness of implementing a fully virtual smoking cessation bundle in a perioperative setting on patients’ perioperative outcomes (e.g., smoking rates, complication rates, hospital length of stay).

Methods

We conducted a pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) with 2 parallel arms, designed by our multidisciplinary team of anesthesiologists, researchers, medical students, and a patient partner. The study took place at Royal Columbian Hospital in New Westminster, BC, between February 2022 and August 2023.

Inclusion criteria included current adult smokers scheduled for elective surgery 1 or more weeks after enrolment. Potential patients were identified on the basis of operating room (OR) bookings and current smoking history, as indicated on their preanesthetic questionnaire, which is usually filled out on paper at the time of their surgical booking and included in the surgical booking package. We excluded patients who underwent emergency surgery, nontobacco smokers, patients who were pregnant or breastfeeding, those unable to provide informed consent in English, and those currently enrolled in another research study involving smoking cessation. Our target sample size was 30 participants, which was selected for convenience, as is customary for a pilot study.

Surgeons were aware of the study and we contacted only patients whose surgeon agreed that their patients could be approached. The study was also posted on ReachBC, an online database of current research studies in BC, and preadmission clinic personnel and surgical offices were informed of the study and could refer patients directly to the study.

We verified current smoking status by patient self-report during screening for eligibility. After informed consent, obtained by telephone, and collection of baseline data, patients were randomized into control and intervention groups. Randomization was computer generated in randomly permuted blocks of sizes 2, 4, and 6 using the ralloc program in Stata version 13.25 The randomization file was uploaded in blinded fashion to REDCap,26,27 a Web-based software platform created to facilitate research and data collection. Blinding of study participants and research staff was not possible, given the nature of the intervention; regular perioperative care providers were blinded. Outcome assessors (i.e., the research assistants making the follow-up calls) were blinded using a follow-up log that contained contact information but did not indicate participant group.

Intervention

The study team developed the intervention bundle — consisting of an emailed video link, a brochure, a Quit-Now telephone referral, and a pharmacy referral — based on prior literature, following the Ask, Advise, Refer framework.11 The bundle was similar to one that has previously been shown to be effective when initiated in person in the preadmission clinic.22 Additionally, the study team included a patient partner (D.B.), who suggested a mindfulness resource be included in the intervention smoking cessation bundle to help cope with the stress of the upcoming surgery, along with smoking cessation. The intervention bundle resources are further detailed in Appendix 1, available at www.canjsurg.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cjs.016224/tab-related-content, and summarized in Figure 1.

The intervention smoking cessation bundle, consisting of an email encouraging smoking cessation; a link to a video outlining the benefits of quitting smoking before surgery; a link to mindfulness resources; an attached brochure, entitled “Butting Out for Safer Surgery”; and referrals to the quitnow.ca telephone support quit line and to a pharmacy capable of delivering nicotine replacement therapy.

The intervention group was emailed the smoking cessation bundle immediately after enrolment, baseline data collection, and randomization. The control group received uncoordinated advice on smoking cessation (usually brief advice by the preadmission clinic nurse or anesthesiologist, but no offer of accompanying resources or referrals), which was standard at the time of the study. All other perioperative care was the same between groups. On the day of surgery, patients were asked to fill out a short questionnaire evaluating their smoking habits. We collected data on postoperative complications by chart review using the Postoperative Morbidity Survey.28 We conducted follow-up over the phone using a standardized questionnaire to collect patients’ smoking status information at 30 days after surgery, 8 weeks after randomization, and 6 months after surgery. We stored all data in REDCap instruments created for the study.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was participants’ self-reported uptake of smoking cessation information and available resources, which we measured by asking the patient to report how many resources they recalled using or accessing. For example, if a patient read the intervention brochure and received nicotine replacement from the pharmacy, this would count as 2 resources used. Patients who recalled receiving but not reading or watching a resource (e.g., brochure or video) were not considered to have used it. Secondary outcomes included recruitment rate (defined as the number of eligible patients who consented to participate in the study per week), self-report of smoking status, self-reported number of cigarettes per day, hospital length of stay, and postoperative complications.

Statistical analysis

We used Stata 13 for all analyses. We considered p values less than 0.05 as significant. For between-group comparisons of proportions, we used the Fisher exact test (e.g., for usage of video, brochure, mindfulness, QuitNow, NRT). For continuous variables, we used 2-sided t tests unless data were skewed, in which case we used the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (e.g., for number of resources used).

Our prespecified metrics for this pilot study to progress to a full-size RCT required a recruitment rate of more than 2 patients per week and that more than 80% of materials or referrals be received by patients in the intervention group. In addition, we needed more than 40% uptake of at least 1 intervention (e.g., have watched video, read the brochure, attempted mindfulness, at least 1 helpline phone call, or at least 1 pharmacologic smoking cessation aid received) in the intervention group.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Fraser Health Research Ethics Board (no. 2017099).

Results

Recruitment was slower than expected as it took 37 weeks to enrol 30 patients (average of 0.8 patients/wk). During the study period, we identified 147 potential patients from surgical wait-lists, of which we screened 71, found 51 to be eligible, and enrolled 30 (59%) (Figure 2). There was 1 formal withdrawal, and 1 patient was lost to follow-up by the day of surgery, with 3 additional losses to long-term follow-up. All patients were identified via surgical wait-lists and OR booking packages; none were identified via health care worker referrals or ReachBC. Recruitment began on Feb. 10, 2022, enrolment closed on Nov. 2, 2022, once we reached our recruitment goal. Follow-up was completed on Nov. 28, 2023.

We found that, at all time points except 6-months after surgery, the intervention group used more resources than the control group. There was higher use of at least 1 resource in the intervention group across all time points. The most used resources on the day of surgery, used by more than half of the intervention group, were NRT and the QuitNow service. The least used resource was the mindfulness resource. As expected, at all time points, the control group (who were not given these resources), did not report using the smoking cessation video, brochure, or mindfulness resources. The control group was not restricted from receiving any of these resources from physicians or preadmission clinic staff, although there was no coordinated referral pathway for smoking cessation during the study period.

At the 30-day postoperative time point, the most used resource was NRT, used by 8 people (53%) in the intervention group, while 8 weeks after randomization, the most used resource was the QuitNow call (reported by 8 intervention group patients). At the 6-month follow-up, the most used resources in the intervention group were the QuitNow call (n = 7, 47%) and NRT (n = 6, 40%).

The video, brochure, and mindfulness resources were sent in the same email, but there were variable responses as to whether each resource was received as of the day of surgery. Seven (47%) patients recalled receiving the video, 10 (67%) patients recalled receiving the brochure, 6 (40%) patients recalled receiving the mindfulness, and 10 (67%) patients were delivered NRT. Interestingly, 9 patients in the intervention group and 1 patient in the control group reported receiving at least 1 QuitNow call; however, according to the QuitNow phone records, 12 (80%) patients in the intervention group and 0 patients in the control group received calls.

The cost of NRT was covered by the provincial smoking cessation program for most patients, but study funds were used for 3 patients who were found to be ineligible for the provincial program (the total cost for the 3 ineligible patients was $484.63).

There were conflicting responses regarding which smoking cessation resources were most helpful (Table 3). Aside from NRT and smoking cessation resources or coaching, patients said that giving them more time before the surgery to quit smoking would have helped them stop or reduce smoking before surgery. Only 1 patient thought the videos were informative and 4 patients thought the brochure was helpful.

Discussion

We found that preoperative recruitment using OR booking packages to identify potential patients for this pilot smoking cessation RCT was feasible at a single tertiary care hospital. Our key finding was that the intervention group — who received a remotely delivered smoking cessation bundle (including the smoking cessation video, brochure, mindfulness, NRT delivery, and the QuitNow phone call) — used more smoking cessation resources than the control group at all time points. Uptake of smoking cessation resources because of the intervention was confirmed. We were able to successfully measure smoking cessation at various time points. We found that 7 (47%) patients in the intervention group and 3 (20%) control patients had stopped smoking on the day of surgery.

In conducting this pilot RCT, we were encouraged to encounter patients with a genuine interest in improving their health before surgery and willingness to try using the resources available to them. Nevertheless, our average recruitment rate of 0.8 patients per week was less than our progression criteria to recruit more than 2 patients per week. Recruitment at our site was challenging in several ways, including low smoking rates in BC,1 difficulty reaching patients for research purposes by phone, and difficulty identifying patients more than 1 week before their surgery, given the late arrival of OR booking packages and the high proportion of urgent surgeries in our institution. Additionally, current smoking status was sometimes ambiguous or incomplete in OR booking packages. However, there was more than 40% uptake of interventions in the intervention group, indicating the feasibility of the intervention bundle itself. A larger RCT targeting clinical outcomes would be difficult to power at a single site, but given the reasonable uptake and utilization of resources by the intervention group in this pilot, a full-size RCT could be considered at a site with a high volume of surgical patients who smoke (e.g., settings where the population’s smoking rates are higher). A multicentre RCT could also be considered to increase the recruitment rate.

On the day of surgery, smoking cessation rates were 47% and 20% in the intervention and control groups, respectively. Based on the effect size of this study — and others showing relative risk reductions for perioperative smoking cessation interventions in a similar range22,29–31 — if we expect the cessation rate to be around 40% in the intervention group and 20% in the control group, then we would need to enrol 236 patients (α of 0.05 and β of 90%).32 Accounting for 10% withdrawal or loss to followup from the study on the day of surgery (6.7% in this study), we would need 260 patients to demonstrate the expected effectiveness of this intervention bundle. Progression to full-scale clinical trials would require greater adherence from patients, which could perhaps be achieved by more reminder calls. In this pilot, many patients received the videos and brochures but did not watch or read them, and many did not remember receiving them at all, even though all the materials were sent in the same email. To increase future uptake of video and brochure resources, the study team could provide clearer instructions to emphasize the importance of reading the email carefully and in its entirety. The team could also conduct follow-up calls with patients to make sure resources were received and viewed.

Intensive perioperative interventions for smoking cessation are known to be more effective than brief advice; however, they are more expensive and resource dependent.33 The strength of this perioperative RCT is that it provided a smoking cessation program with low resource needs but still had elements of more intensive interventions such as ongoing NRT and phone support. Furthermore, very few studies look at smoking cessation rates beyond 3 months, and our study followed patients for 6 months after surgery.33 We did not stratify randomization in this pilot, but a future larger trial might consider stratifying by ambulatory versus inpatient surgery since the motivation for quitting and exposure to resources can differ between disease or surgical intensities, a phenomenon that has previously been shown in comparisons of quit rates among patients with diseases of varying severity.34 Stratification could also be done by baseline smoking characteristics (e.g., nicotine dependence).

Some barriers to perioperative smoking cessation include inadequate knowledge of smoking cessation interventions, lack of time to receive interventions or resources, beliefs about capabilities, and the social or professional roles of surgeons. In contrast, some facilitators of smoking cessation include advice on quitting (such as that provided in routine surgical care), positive reinforcement, and smoking cessation training.35

Limitations

This study had limitations, including that it was a pilot study with the primary goal of determining intervention feasibility, not efficacy. This was also a virtual study and most interviews were conducted over the telephone. We did not conduct any biological confirmation of cessation with exhaled carbon monoxide or cotinine.36 However, previous studies have shown relatively good concordance between self-reported and biochemically verified smoking cessation.37 We also had a small sample size (n = 30), which was selected for convenience rather than calculated for any specific measures, and unbalanced baseline characteristics, which may have influenced the estimated effect sizes. This study also relied on provincially funded NRT, so costs of NRT could be a barrier to a multicentred study involving other jurisdictions and to real-world feasibility, particularly since some of our patients did indicate that costs were a barrier to accessing smoking cessation resources. All provinces and territories in Canada have NRT funding programs, although exact eligibility criteria vary,38 so some local variations in the bundle would be required for a nationwide study.

Given that all the interventions in the smoking cessation bundle were low risk and acceptable to patients, and in-person delivery of these resources has already been established in the literature as both clinically effective and cost-effective, another consideration could be to approach this problem as a quality improvement initiative to focus on wider scale delivery of all the interventions to all patients who smoke perioperatively.7,39 In this study, we implemented this intervention bundle in the preadmission clinic but outpatient surgical clinics could also be used to initiate smoking cessation interventions earlier in a patient’s surgical journey.40

Conclusion

This pilot RCT showed that a virtually delivered smoking cessation bundle, delivered before surgery, is feasible in terms of acceptance to patients. Recruitment feasibility could be enhanced by involving multiple study sites. Therefore, although our pilot did not meet full progression criteria to immediately proceed to a full-scale single-site study, a multicentred study would be feasible to demonstrate the clinical effectiveness of a virtual perioperative smoking cessation bundle.
