Almost all primes are not needed in Ternary Goldbach
Debmalya Basak, Raghavendra N. Bhat, Anji Dong and Alexandru Zaharescu

Abstract
. The ternary Goldbach conjecture states that every odd number m ⩾ 7 m\geqslant 7 can be written as the sum of three primes. We construct a set of primes ℙ \mathbb{P} defined by an expanding system of admissible congruences such that almost all primes are not in ℙ \mathbb{P} and still, the ternary Goldbach conjecture holds true with primes restricted to ℙ \mathbb{P} .



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary: 11P32. Secondary: 11P55, 11L07, 11L20

1.
Introduction

In a letter to Euler in 1742, Goldbach stated that every even integer
m\geqslant 4
can be written as the sum of two primes. This conjecture, known as Goldbach’s conjecture, has been the subject of extensive research over the years. Although Goldbach’s conjecture is still unsolved, many striking results have been obtained in several different directions. One such direction consists of results which show that almost all even positive integers can be written as a sum of two primes. The first non-trivial, although conditional, result in this direction was obtained by Hardy and Littlewood
[
13
]
. Concerning the exceptional set (that is, the set of possible even integers that cannot be written as a sum of two primes), denoted by
\mathcal{E}
, Hardy and Littlewood
[
13
]
showed that assuming the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, the estimate

E(X)\ll_{\varepsilon}X^{1/2+\varepsilon}

holds for any
\varepsilon>0
where
E(X)=\#\{n\leqslant X:n\in\mathcal{E}\}
. Since then, many results in this direction have been obtained over the years, see Vinogradov
[
35
]
, Van der Corput
[
32
]
, Cudakov
[
7
]
, Estermann
[
9
]
, Vaughan
[
34
]
, Montgomery and Vaughan
[
26
]
, Chen and Liu
[
5
,
6
]
, Goldston
[
11
]
, Li
[
20
,
21
]
, and Lu
[
25
]
. Currently the best known unconditional bound, proved in 2018, obtained by Pintz
[
29
]
, states that there is a constant
X_{2}
such that for any
X>X_{2}
,

E(X)<X^{0.72},

where
E(X)=\#\{n\leqslant X:n\in\mathcal{E}\}
.

Another direction consists of results that show that all large even numbers can be represented as a sum of a prime and a number that is a product of bounded number of prime factors. Results in this direction were established by Rényi
[
30
]
, Pan
[
27
]
, and Wang
[
36
]
, culminating with Chen’s theorem
[
3
,
4
]
, which shows that every large even number can be written as a sum of a prime and a product of at most two prime factors. Generalizations of Chen’s theorem have also been obtained. Lu and Cai
[
23
,
24
]
proved more general versions of Chen’s theorem for short intervals and arithmetic progressions. Cai
[
2
]
further proved that every large enough even integer
n
is a sum of a prime less than
n^{0.95}
and a number with at most two prime factors. An explicit version of Chen’s theorem was proved by Bordignon, Johnston and Starichkova
[
1
]
.

In yet another direction, results have been obtained showing that all large enough even numbers can be written as a sum of two primes plus a bounded number of powers of 2. The reader is referred to Linnik
[
22
]
, Gallagher
[
10
]
, Li
[
19
]
, Heath-Brown and Putcha
[
15
]
, Pintz and Ruzsa
[
28
]
, as well as Khalfalah and Pintz
[
17
]
.

In his correspondence with Euler, Goldbach also proposed that every odd integer
m\geqslant 7
can be written as the sum of three primes. This is known as the ternary Goldbach conjecture (also called Goldbach’s weak conjecture). Hardy and Littlewood showed
[
12
]
assuming the generalized
Riemann hypothesis, that every sufficiently large odd number is a
sum of three primes. Vinogradov
[
35
]
used his new estimates on exponential sums over primes to prove unconditionally that the ternary Goldbach conjecture is true for all sufficiently large odd numbers. More recently, Helfgott
[
16
]
proved the conjecture for all odd numbers larger than or equal to 7.

In the present paper, our primary aim is to construct a set of primes
\mathbb{P}
such that almost all primes are not in
\mathbb{P}
and still, the ternary Goldbach conjecture holds true with primes taken from
\mathbb{P}
. We will prove the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists a set of primes
\mathbb{P}
such that

(a)

For any positive integer
B
,

\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{(\log X)^{B}\cdot\#\{p\in\mathbb{P}:p\leqslant X\}}{X}=0,\quad\textnormal{and}

(b)

Every odd number
m\geqslant 7
is the sum of three primes in
\mathbb{P}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
relies on a concrete construction of a set of primes
\mathbb{P}
satisfying a growing class of congruence constraints as the size of the primes increase. This is responsible for our set
\mathbb{P}
to satisfy condition (a) in the statement of Theorem
1.1
. As we shall see later, the above class of congruence constraints are governed by products of small primes. At each stage of our construction, we employ the Hardy–Littlewood Circle method in order to establish asymptotic formulas for the number of representations as sums of three primes from
\mathbb{P}
. Collectively, all the stages together imply condition (b) in the statement of Theorem
1.1
.

Structure of the Paper

The paper is organized as follows. We introduce some standard notations in Section
2
. Section
3
outlines our approach to proving Theorem
1.1
, showing that the proof hinges on a crucial lemma. Section
4
addresses the construction of our specific set of primes and demonstrates that this construction fulfills condition (a) of our key lemma. The setup for applying the Hardy–Littlewood method, which is used to address condition (b) of the lemma, is also introduced at the end of Section
4
. Sections
5
and
6
provides the details of the minor arc and major arc estimates respectively. The delicate decomposition of the arithmetic factor is presented across Sections
7
and
8
. Finally, in Section
9
, we complete the proof of part (b) of the equivalent lemma, thereby concluding the proof of our main theorem.

3.
Proof of Main Theorem: Overall Strategy

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we will prove the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix a positive integer
A
. There exists
u_{A}>0
such that for all
u\geqslant u_{A}
,

(a)

There exists a set
\mathbb{P}_{u}
of primes in
[u,2u]
satisfying

(3.1)

\displaystyle\#(\mathbb{P}_{u})\leqslant\frac{u}{(\log u)^{A}},\quad\textnormal{and}

(b)

For any odd number
m\in[4u,5u]
, there exist
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\in\mathbb{P}_{u}
such that
m=p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}
.

Assuming Lemma
3.1
, let us first prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We begin with the following construction. Fix absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}
such that

(3.2)

\displaystyle c_{1}<1<c_{2}<\frac{5c_{1}}{4}<c_{3}<\frac{5c_{2}}{4}<c_{4}<\frac{5c_{3}}{4}<2<\frac{5c_{4}}{4}.

For each
A\in\mathbb{N}
, Lemma
3.1
provides us with
u_{A}
. Define
\ell_{A}
be the smallest integer such that

(3.3)

\displaystyle u_{A}\leqslant c_{1}2^{\ell_{A}}.

For each
\ell_{A}\leqslant\ell<\ell_{A+1}
, we apply Lemma
3.1
repeatedly with the choices
u_{i}=c_{i}2^{\ell}
, where
1\leqslant i\leqslant 4
. By Lemma
3.1
, we obtain the sets of primes
\mathbb{P}_{A,u_{i}}\subseteq[c_{i}2^{\ell},2c_{i}2^{\ell}]
. Let
\mathbb{P}_{A,\ell}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{4}\mathbb{P}_{u_{i}}
. Then for each
\ell_{A}\leqslant\ell<\ell_{A+1}
we have:

(i)

\mathbb{P}_{A,\ell}\subseteq[c_{1}2^{\ell},2c_{4}2^{\ell}]
is a set of primes satisfying

\#(\mathbb{P}_{A,\ell})\leqslant\frac{4c_{4}2^{\ell}}{(\log c_{1}2^{\ell})^{A}}.

(ii)

For any odd number
m\in[2^{\ell+2},2^{\ell+3}]
, there exist
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\in\mathbb{P}_{A,\ell}
such that
m=p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}
.

To see this, observe that the first condition is satisfied by maximizing the right hand side of (
3.1
) as
u
varies over the
u_{i}
’s. For the second condition, note that for any
m\in[2^{\ell+2},2^{\ell+3}]
,
m\in[4u_{i},5u_{i}]
for some
1\leqslant i\leqslant 4
.

Let us define

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}=\{p\textrm{ prime}:p\leqslant 20u_{1}\},\quad\mathbb{P}_{1}=\bigcup_{A\geqslant 1}\bigcup_{\ell_{A}\leqslant\ell<\ell_{A+1}}\mathbb{P}_{A,\ell}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}_{0}\cup\mathbb{P}_{1}.

Condition (b) from the statement of Theorem
1.1
holds trivially for all odd
m
up to
20u_{1}
due to Helfgott
[
16
]
. So we may assume
m>20u_{1}
. Suppose
m\in[2^{\ell+2},2^{\ell+3})
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
m>20u_{1}
, we must have
\ell\geqslant\ell_{1}
. Suppose
\ell_{A}\leqslant\ell<\ell_{A+1}
for some
A\in\mathbb{N}
. By our construction of the set
\mathbb{P}_{A,\ell}
, there exist
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\in\mathbb{P}_{A,\ell}\subseteq\mathbb{P}
such that
m=p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}
. Therefore condition (b) is satisfied.

Now we prove condition (a). Fix
X\geqslant 2
large. Let
A_{0}
to be the smallest positive integer such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle X<c_{1}2^{\ell_{A_{0}}}.

Let
B_{0}
be the smallest positive integer such that

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sqrt{X}<c_{1}2^{\ell_{B_{0}}}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\#\{p\in\mathbb{P}:p\leqslant X\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant A<A_{0}}\sum_{\ell_{A}\leqslant\ell<\ell_{A+1}}\#\{\mathbb{P}_{A,\ell}\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant A<B_{0}}\sum_{\ell_{A}\leqslant\ell<\ell_{A+1}}\#\{\mathbb{P}_{A,\ell}\}+\sum_{B_{0}\leqslant A<A_{0}}\sum_{\ell_{A}\leqslant\ell<\ell_{A+1}}\#\{\mathbb{P}_{A,\ell}\}

(3.7)

\displaystyle\ll_{B_{0}}\sqrt{X}\log X+\frac{X}{(\log X)^{B_{0}-1}}\ll_{B_{0}}\frac{X}{(\log X)^{B_{0}-1}}.

Note that
u_{A}
and
\ell_{A}
are monotonically increasing as
A
increases. Hence for any fixed positive integer
B
, letting
X
tend to infinity, we can choose
B_{0}=B+1
in (
3.6
) and (
3.7
), thereby satisfying condition (a).
∎

The proof of Lemma
3.1
is more intricate and will be presented across the remainder of the paper.

4.
Initial Setup

In this section, we lay some preliminary groundwork for the proof of Lemma
3.1
.

4.1.
Residue Class Constructions

Let
A\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed and suppose
u
be sufficiently large depending on
A
. Let

(4.1)

\displaystyle z=3A\log\log u.

Enumerate the primes
p_{1}<p_{2}<\dots<p_{k}\leqslant z
and let

(4.2)

\displaystyle q_{0}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}.

Then we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle q_{0}=\prod_{p<z}p\asymp e^{z}=e^{3A\log\log u}=(\log u)^{3A}.

For each
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, let

(4.4)

\displaystyle a_{j}=\lceil p_{j}^{1/3}\rceil+1

and define

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{j}=\mathcal{R}_{j,1}\cup\mathcal{R}_{j,2}\cup\mathcal{R}_{j,3},

where

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{j,1}=\{1,2,\dots,a_{j}\},\quad\mathcal{R}_{j,2}=\{a_{j},2a_{j},\dots,a_{j}^{2}\}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\mathcal{R}_{j,3}

\displaystyle=\{a_{j}^{2},2a_{j}^{2},\dots,a_{j}^{3}\}.

We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leqslant j\leqslant k
, there exist
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}\in\mathcal{R}_{j}
such that
n\equiv r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}\mod p_{j}.

Proof.

Since we are looking for solutions modulo
p_{j}
, it suffices to consider
n\in\mathbb{Z}/p_{j}\mathbb{Z}
, i.e.
n=1,2,\cdots,p_{j}
. For any
n
, since
a_{j}>p_{j}^{1/3},
we can write
n
in base
a_{j}
, that is,

n=w_{2}a_{j}^{2}+w_{1}a_{j}+w_{0},

where
w_{i}<a_{j}
for
i=1,2,3
. Note that
a_{j}\geqslant 3
. We now discuss in cases.

Case 1
: Suppose
w_{i}\neq 0
for all
w_{i}
. In this case, choosing
r_{1}=w_{0},r_{2}=w_{1}a_{j},
and
r_{3}=w_{2}a_{j}^{2}
will yield the desired result.

Case 2
: Suppose exactly one of the
w_{i}
is 0. Assume that
w_{0}=0
. Then, if either
w_{1}
or
w_{2}
is greater than 1, say
w_{1}\geqslant 2
, then we have
n=w_{2}a_{j}^{2}+(w_{1}-1)a_{j}+a_{j}
. Otherwise we write
n=a_{j}^{2}+a_{j}=(a_{j}-1)a_{j}+a_{j}+a_{j}
. Now assume
w_{1}=0
. Again, if either
w_{0}
or
w_{2}
is greater than 1, then we are done. Otherwise,
n=a_{j}^{2}+1=(a_{j}-1)a_{j}+a_{j}+1
. Finally, assume
w_{2}=0
. If either
w_{0}
or
w_{1}
is greater than 1, then split same as in other sub-cases. Otherwise,
n=a_{j}+1
, then
n=(a_{j}-1)+1+1
.

Case 3
: Suppose exactly two of the
w_{i}
are 0. Assume
n=w_{2}a_{j}^{2}
. If
w_{2}\geqslant 3
, then we have
n=a_{j}^{2}+a_{j}^{2}+(w_{2}-2)a_{j}^{2}
. If
w_{2}=2
, then
n=a_{j}^{2}+(a_{j}-1)a_{j}+a_{j}
. If
w_{2}=1
, and if
p_{j}>8
, then
n=a_{j}+a_{j}+(a_{j}-2)a_{j}
; otherwise, we write
n=a_{j}+1+(a_{j}-1)
. Now, assume
n=w_{1}a_{j}
. If
w_{1}\geqslant 3
, then split in the same way as the above sub-case. If
w_{1}=2
, then
n=a_{j}+(a_{j}-1)+1
. If
w_{1}=1
, then
n=1+1+(a_{j}-2)
. Finally, consider the case when
n=w_{0}
. If
w_{0}\geqslant 3
, then
n=1+1+(w_{0}-2)
. Otherwise, we will write
w_{0}+p_{j}
instead as the triple sum. Note that
w_{0}+p_{j}
is at most
p_{j}+2
. Since
a_{j}^{3}\geqslant(p_{j}^{1/3}+1)^{3}=p_{j}+7
, we can always write
w_{0}+p_{j}=w^{\prime}_{2}a_{j}^{2}+w^{\prime}_{1}a_{j}+w^{\prime}_{0}
, where
w^{\prime}_{2}>0
, and so will be reduced to previous cases.
∎

Note that

(4.7)

\displaystyle\#(\mathcal{R}_{j})=3a_{j}=3(\rceil p_{j}^{\frac{1}{3}}\rceil+1)=3p_{j}^{\frac{1}{3}}+O(1).

Let
{\mathcal{R}}_{0}
be the set of integers
r\in[1,q_{0}]
such that
(r,q_{0})=1
and for each
1\leqslant j\leqslant k
,

r\equiv r_{j}\bmod p_{j},

for some
r_{j}\in\mathcal{R}_{j}
. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, for
u
large with respect to
A
, we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle\#(\mathcal{R}_{0})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\#(\mathcal{R}_{j})

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(3p_{j}^{\frac{1}{3}}+O(1))\asymp q_{0}^{1/3}(\log u)^{\varepsilon},

for any
\varepsilon>0
. Define
\mathbb{P}_{u}
to be the set of primes
p
in
[u,2u]
such that
p\equiv r\bmod q_{0}
for some
r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}
. Trivially, one has

(4.9)

\displaystyle\#(\mathbb{P}_{u})\leqslant\frac{u}{q_{0}}\#(\mathcal{R}_{0})\ll\frac{u(\log u)^{\varepsilon}}{(\log u)^{2A}}

for any
\varepsilon>0
. Therefore, in order to prove Lemma
3.1
, it suffices to show that for any odd
m\in[4u,5u]
that there exist
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\in\mathbb{P}_{u}
such that

m=p_{1}+p_{2}+p_{3}.

We will do so using the Hardy–Littlewood Circle method.

4.2.
Major and Minor Arc Definitions

Let

(4.10)

\displaystyle S(\alpha)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\leqslant k\leqslant 2u\\
k\bmod q_{0}\in\mathcal{R}_{0}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(k)e(k\alpha)

where
\Lambda(n)
denotes the Von-Mangoldt function. Let

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(m)=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}u\leqslant k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\leqslant 2u\\
k_{i}\bmod q_{0}\in\mathcal{R}_{0}\\
k_{1}+k_{2}+k_{3}=m\end{subarray}}{\sum\sum\sum}\Lambda(k_{1})\Lambda(k_{2})\Lambda(k_{3}).

Our goal is to provide an asymptotic formula for
\mathfrak{R}(m)
which we achieve in (
9.4
) below. To proceed, we begin by noting that the orthogonality relation

\int_{0}^{1}e(\alpha h)\,\differential\alpha=\begin{cases}1&\text{ when }h=0,\\
0&\text{ when }h\neq 0,\end{cases}

gives

(4.12)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(m)=\int_{0}^{1}S(\alpha)^{3}e(-m\alpha)\,\differential\alpha.

Therefore to prove condition (b) of Lemma
3.1
, we will estimate the integral on the right hand side of (
4.12
) To do so, we first define the major and minor arcs. Let
P=(\log u)^{B}
,
Q=u(\log u)^{-B}
where
B>0
will be chosen later in terms of
A
. When
1\leqslant a\leqslant q\leqslant P
and
(a,q)=1
, let

(4.13)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}(q,a)=\left\{\alpha:|\alpha-a/q|\leqslant 1/Q\right\}.

We call
\mathfrak{M}(q,a)
the major arcs. Let
\mathfrak{M}
denote the union of the
\mathfrak{M}(q,a)
’s. The set
\mathfrak{m}=[0,1]\backslash\mathfrak{M}
forms the minor arcs.
When
a/q\neq a^{\prime}/q^{\prime}
and
q,q^{\prime}\leqslant P
, one has

\left|\frac{a}{q}-\frac{a^{\prime}}{q^{\prime}}\right|\geqslant\frac{1}{qq^{\prime}}\geqslant\frac{1}{P^{2}}>\frac{2}{Q}.

Thus the
\mathfrak{M}(q,a)
are pairwise disjoint. By (
4.12
), we have

(4.14)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}(m)=\int_{\mathfrak{M}}S(\alpha)^{3}e(-\alpha m)\,\differential\alpha+\int_{\mathfrak{m}}S(\alpha)^{3}e(-\alpha m)\,\differential\alpha.

We treat the integral over the minor arcs, major arcs and the associated arithmetic factor across Sections

,
6
,
7
and
8
.

5.
Minor Arcs

Our goal in this section is to estimate the sum

(5.1)

\displaystyle S(\alpha)=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\leqslant k\leqslant 2u\\
k\equiv r\bmod q_{0}\end{subarray}}\Lambda(k)e(k\alpha).

when
\alpha\in\mathfrak{m}
. We first record the following estimate for exponential sums over primes.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
u\geqslant 1
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and consider a reduced fraction
a/q
such that
|\alpha-a/q|\leqslant 1/q^{2}
. Then

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant u}\Lambda(k)e(k\alpha)\ll\bigg(\frac{u}{q^{1/2}}+u^{4/5}+u^{1/2}q^{1/2}\bigg)(\log u)^{4}.

Proof.

See
[
8
, Ch. 25]
.
∎

Using the orthogonality relation

\mathbbm{1}_{k\equiv r\bmod q_{0}}=\frac{1}{q_{0}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{q_{0}-1}e\bigg(\frac{\ell(k-r)}{q_{0}}\bigg),

we write

(5.3)

\displaystyle S(\alpha)=\frac{1}{q_{0}}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{q_{0}-1}e\bigg(\frac{-\ell r}{q_{0}}\bigg)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\leqslant k\leqslant 2u\end{subarray}}\Lambda(k)e\bigg(k\bigg(\alpha+\frac{\ell}{q_{0}}\bigg)\bigg).

To treat the innermost sum in (
5.3
), we will derive a variant of Lemma
5.1
to address the special case when
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and there exists some reduced fraction
a/q
such that
|\alpha-a/q|\leqslant\gamma/q^{2}
, for some
\gamma\geqslant 1
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
u\geqslant 1
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and consider a reduced fraction
a/q
such that
|\alpha-a/q|\leqslant\gamma/q^{2}
for some
\gamma\geqslant 1
. Then

(5.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant u}\Lambda(k)e(k\alpha)\ll\bigg(\frac{\gamma^{1/2}u}{q^{1/2}}+u^{4/5}+u^{1/2}q^{1/2}\bigg)(\log u)^{4}.

Proof.

Dirichlet’s theorem on Diophantine approximation (see
[
33
, Lemma 2.1]
) asserts that for any real
\alpha
and for any real number
M\geqslant 1
, there exists a rational number
a_{1}/q_{1}
, where
1\leqslant q_{1}\leqslant M
, and
(a_{1},q_{1})=1
, such that
\lvert\alpha-a_{1}/q_{1}\rvert\leqslant 1/(q_{1}M)\leqslant 1/q_{1}^{2}
. Choose
\alpha
to be as in the statement of the lemma and
M=2q
. Then
1\leqslant q_{1}\leqslant 2q
,
(a_{1},q_{1})=1
, and

(5.5)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\alpha-\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{1}{2qq_{1}}\leqslant\frac{1}{q_{1}^{2}}.

By Lemma
5.1
, we have

(5.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant u}\Lambda(k)e(k\alpha)\ll\bigg(\frac{u}{q_{1}^{1/2}}+u^{4/5}+u^{1/2}q_{1}^{1/2}\bigg)(\log u)^{4}.

In the special case when

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant u}\Lambda(k)e(k\alpha)\ll\bigg(\frac{u}{q^{1/2}}+u^{4/5}+u^{1/2}q^{1/2}\bigg)(\log u)^{4}.

Otherwise assume that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{qq_{1}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left\lvert\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}-\frac{a}{q}\right\rvert\leqslant\left\lvert\frac{a_{1}}{q_{1}}-\alpha\right\rvert+\left\lvert\alpha-\frac{a}{q}\right\rvert\leqslant\frac{1}{2qq_{1}}+\frac{\gamma}{q^{2}}.

This implies that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q_{1}^{1/2}}\leqslant\frac{(2\gamma)^{1/2}}{q^{1/2}}.

Then using (

) and (

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant u}\Lambda(k)e(k\alpha)\ll\bigg(\frac{\gamma^{1/2}u}{q^{1/2}}+u^{4/5}+u^{1/2}q^{1/2}\bigg)(\log u)^{4}.

Therefore combining (

∎

7.

7.1.

Let us define

\displaystyle f(q):=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant q\\
(a,q)=1\end{subarray}}e\left(-\frac{am}{q}\right)\sum_{r_{i}}\sum_{\ell_{i}}e\left(\frac{-(\ell_{1}r_{1}+\ell_{2}r_{2}+\ell_{3}r_{3})}{q_{0}}\right)\frac{\mu(q_{\ell_{1}})\mu(q_{\ell_{2}})\mu(q_{\ell_{3}})}{\varphi(q_{\ell_{1}})\varphi(q_{\ell_{2}})\varphi(q_{\ell_{3}})}.

7.2.
Arithmetic Arguments involving GCDs

For any

(7.3)

\displaystyle q=\tilde{q}\hat{q},

\displaystyle\tilde{q}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|q_{0}\\

Note that
(\hat{q},q_{0})=1

(\hat{q},\tilde{q})=1

q_{0}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}

<z

(7.5)

\displaystyle d=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p|q_{0},p|\tilde{q}\end{subarray}}p.

\displaystyle q_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{qq_{0}}{(qq_{0},aq_{0}+\ell q)}=\frac{\tilde{q}\hat{q}q_{0}}{(\tilde{q}\hat{q}q_{0},aq_{0}+\ell\tilde{q}\hat{q})}=\frac{\tilde{q}\hat{q}q_{0}}{(\tilde{q}q_{0},aq_{0}+\ell\tilde{q}\hat{q})}=\hat{q}\frac{\tilde{q}q_{0}}{(\tilde{q}q_{0},aq_{0}+\ell\hat{q}\tilde{q})}.

Using (

\displaystyle f(q)=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant\tilde{q}\hat{q}\\

. This implies that

\displaystyle f(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\frac{\mu(\hat{q})}{\varphi(\hat{q})^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant\tilde{q}\hat{q}\\
(a,\tilde{q})=1\\
(a,\hat{q})=1\end{subarray}}e\bigg(-\frac{am}{\tilde{q}\hat{q}}\bigg)\sum_{r_{i}}\sum_{\ell_{i}}e\bigg(\frac{-(\ell_{1}r_{1}+\ell_{2}r_{2}+\ell_{3}r_{3})}{\hat{q}q_{0}}\bigg)\prod_{i=1}^{3}\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{\tilde{q}q_{0}}{(\tilde{q}q_{0},aq_{0}+\ell_{i}\tilde{q})}\bigg)

(7.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\frac{\mu(\hat{q})}{\varphi(\hat{q})^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant\tilde{q}\hat{q}\\
(a,\tilde{q})=1\\
(a,\hat{q})=1\end{subarray}}e\bigg(-\frac{am}{\tilde{q}\hat{q}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{q_{0}-1}\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{\tilde{q}q_{0}}{(\tilde{q}q_{0},aq_{0}+\ell\tilde{q})}\bigg)\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}e\left(\frac{-\ell r}{\hat{q}q_{0}}\right)\bigg)^{3}.

Now let us examine the Möbius factor in the inner sum in (
7.7
). We write

(7.8)

\displaystyle\mu\bigg(\frac{\tilde{q}q_{0}}{(\tilde{q}q_{0},aq_{0}+\ell\tilde{q})}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\mu\bigg(\frac{\tilde{q}q_{0}/d}{(\tilde{q}q_{0}/d,aq_{0}/d+\ell\tilde{q}/d}\bigg),

which is nonzero if and only if

\tilde{q}/d
divides
a
. Since
(a,q)=1
, we have
(a,\tilde{q})=1
. So the only way for this to happen is when
d=\tilde{q}
, which means that
\tilde{q}
divides
q_{0}
. We now evaluate
f(q)
in two cases.

Case 1:
\tilde{q}
does not divides
q_{0}
.
By the discussion above, this case will force the the Möbius factor in the inner sum in (
7.7
) to be zero for all
a
. Then
f(q)=0
.

Case 2:
\tilde{q}
divides
q_{0}
.
In this case
d=\tilde{q}
. Then we have

(7.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{q}q_{0}}{(\tilde{q}q_{0},aq_{0}+\ell\tilde{q})}=\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},aq_{0}/\tilde{q}+\ell)}.

Substituting (
7.9
) into the representation of
f(q)
in (
7.7
), we get

(7.10)

\displaystyle f(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\frac{\mu(\hat{q})}{\varphi(\hat{q})^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant\tilde{q}\hat{q}\\
(a,\tilde{q})=1\\
(a,\hat{q})=1\end{subarray}}e\bigg(-\frac{am}{\tilde{q}\hat{q}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{q_{0}-1}\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},aq_{0}/\tilde{q}+\ell)}\bigg)\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}e\bigg(\frac{-\ell r}{\hat{q}q_{0}}\bigg)\bigg)^{3}.

Using (
7.10
), we can evaluate
f
at
\tilde{q}
. We see that if
\tilde{q}
divides
q_{0}
then

\displaystyle f(\tilde{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant\tilde{q}\\
(a,\tilde{q})=1\end{subarray}}e\bigg(-\frac{am}{\tilde{q}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{q_{0}-1}\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},aq_{0}/\tilde{q}+\ell)}\bigg)\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}e\bigg(\frac{-\ell r}{q_{0}}\bigg)\bigg)^{3}.

Similarly, we get

(7.11)

\displaystyle f(\hat{q})=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\frac{\mu(\hat{q})}{\varphi(\hat{q})^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant\hat{q}\\
(a,\hat{q})=1\end{subarray}}e\bigg(-\frac{am}{\hat{q}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{q_{0}-1}\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},\ell)}\bigg)e\bigg(\frac{-\ell r}{q_{0}}\bigg)\bigg)^{3}.

Moreover, we also have

(7.12)

\displaystyle f(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{q_{0}-1}\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},\ell)}\bigg)\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}e\left(\frac{-\ell r}{q_{0}}\right)\bigg)^{3}.

7.3.
Decomposition of
f
using Dirichlet Convolution

Our objective here is to decompose
f
as

f(q)=(g*h)(q).

where
g
and
h
are arithmetic functions. Furthermore if
\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}h(v)<\infty
, then by (
7.2
), it follows that

(7.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}f(q)=\bigg(\sum_{w=1}^{\infty}g(w)\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}h(v)\bigg).

Let us construct our functions
g
and
h
. We define
g
as follows:

(7.14)

\displaystyle g(w)=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if }w\nmid q_{0},\\
\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant w\\
(a,w)=1\end{subarray}}e\left(-\frac{am}{w}\right)\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{q_{0}-1}\frac{\mu}{\varphi}(\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},aq_{0}/w+\ell)})\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}e(\frac{-\ell r}{q_{0}})\bigg)^{3}\quad&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Let
h
be defined as follows:

(7.15)

\displaystyle h(v)=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if }(v,q_{0})>1,\\
\frac{\mu(v)}{\varphi(v)^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant v\\
(a,v)=1\end{subarray}}e\left(-\frac{am}{v}\right)\quad&\text{else}.\end{cases}

By the discussion in
[
8
, Ch. 25]
,
h
is multiplicative and
\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}h(v)
converges absolutely. Thus we get

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}h(v)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\text{ prime}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}h(p^{j})

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\text{ prime}\\
p|q_{0}\end{subarray}}(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}h(p^{j}))\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\text{ prime}\\
p\nmid q_{0}\end{subarray}}(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}h(p^{j}))

(7.16)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\nmid q_{0}\\
p|m\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-1)^{2}}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\nmid q_{0}\\
p\nmid m\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{1}{(p-1)^{3}}\right).

We write

(7.17)

\displaystyle(g*h)(q)=\sum_{c|q}g(c)h\left(\frac{q}{c}\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}c|q,c|q_{0}\\
(q/c,q_{0})=1\end{subarray}}g(c)h\left(\frac{q}{c}\right)=g(d)h(q/d),

where
d=(\tilde{q},q_{0})
. If
\tilde{q}
doesn’t divide
q_{0}
, then
d\neq\tilde{q}
which implies that
h(q/d)=0
. Therefore we have
f(q)=(g*h)(q)
in this case. If
\tilde{q}
divides
q_{0}
, then
d=\tilde{q}
which implies that

\displaystyle(g*h)(q)

\displaystyle=g(\tilde{q})h(\hat{q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\frac{\mu(\hat{q})}{\varphi(\hat{q})^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a_{1}\leqslant\tilde{q}\\
1\leqslant a_{2}\leqslant\hat{q}\\
(a_{1},\tilde{q})=1\\
(a_{2},\hat{q})=1\end{subarray}}e\bigg(-\frac{a_{1}m}{\tilde{q}}-\frac{a_{2}m}{\hat{q}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{q_{0}-1}\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},a_{1}q_{0}/\tilde{q}+\ell)}\bigg)\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}e\bigg(\frac{-\ell r}{\hat{q}q_{0}}\bigg)\bigg)^{3}

(7.18)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\frac{\mu(\hat{q})}{\varphi(\hat{q})^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant\tilde{q}\hat{q}\\
(a,\tilde{q})=1\\
(a,\hat{q})=1\end{subarray}}e\bigg(-\frac{am}{\tilde{q}\hat{q}}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{q_{0}-1}\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},aq_{0}/\tilde{q}+\ell)}\bigg)\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}e\bigg(\frac{-\ell r}{\hat{q}q_{0}}\bigg)\bigg)^{3}=f(q).

Therefore it follows that
f=g*h
. By (
7.16
), we already have

\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}h(v)>0

for any odd integer
m
. Hence it suffices to study the function
g
which we do in the following section.

8.
Arithmetic Factor: Further Decomposition

8.1.
Initial Steps

Recall that in (
7.14
), we defined
g(w)=0
if
w\nmid q_{0}
. We begin by writing

(8.1)

\displaystyle G(q_{0}):=\sum_{w=1}^{\infty}g(w)=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\sum_{w|q_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant w\\
(a,w)=1\end{subarray}}e\bigg(-\frac{am}{w}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{\ell=0}^{q_{0}-1}\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},aq_{0}/w+\ell)}\bigg)\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}e\bigg(\frac{-\ell r}{q_{0}}\bigg)\bigg)^{3}.

Our objective is to show that
G(q_{0})>0
. We denote
g(w)
by
g(w,q_{0})
to show the dependence on
q_{0}
. Expanding the expression for
g(w,q_{0})
, we obtain

\displaystyle g(w,q_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q_{0}^{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant a\leqslant w\\
(a,w)=1\end{subarray}}e\left(-\frac{am}{w}\right)\underset{r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}{\sum\sum\sum}\underset{0\leqslant\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3}\leqslant q_{0}-1}{\sum\sum\sum}e\left(\frac{-\ell_{1}r_{1}}{q_{0}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{2}r_{2}}{q_{0}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{3}r_{3}}{q_{0}}\right)

(8.2)

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},aq_{0}/w+\ell_{1})}\bigg)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},aq_{0}/w+\ell_{2})}\bigg)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{q_{0}}{(q_{0},aq_{0}/w+\ell_{3})}\bigg).

Recall that
q_{0}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}
. For
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
, define

\displaystyle u_{i}\equiv\begin{cases}1\mod p_{i}\\
0\mod p_{j}\quad\text{ for $j\neq i$}.\end{cases}

Then by the Chinese remainder theorem, for
i=1,2,3
, and
j=1,2,\dots,k
, there exist unique
a_{j},

\ell_{ij}\in\mathbb{Z},
and
r_{ij}\in\mathcal{R}_{j}
such that

\displaystyle\ell_{i}

\displaystyle=\ell_{i1}u_{1}+\ell_{i2}u_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{ik}u_{k},

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=a_{1}u_{1}+a_{2}u_{2}+\cdots+a_{k}u_{k},

(8.3)

\displaystyle r_{i}

\displaystyle=r_{i1}u_{1}+r_{i2}u_{2}+\cdots+r_{ik}u_{k}.

Note that

\displaystyle\ell_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\ell_{i1}\bmod p_{1},

\displaystyle\ell_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\ell_{i2}\bmod p_{2},

(8.4)

\displaystyle\ell_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\ell_{ik}\bmod p_{k},

for
i=1,2,3
. An analogous set of equations hold for
r_{i}
as well. For
b\in\mathbb{N}
, define

(8.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(b):=\sum_{t_{1}=0}^{3}\cdots\sum_{t_{b}=0}^{3}\frac{1}{\varphi(p_{1})^{t_{1}}\cdots\varphi(p_{b})^{t_{b}}}\dbinom{3}{t_{1}}\dbinom{3}{t_{2}}\cdots\dbinom{3}{t_{b}}.

We prove the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 8.1
.

For any
b\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

(8.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(b)=\frac{(p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{b})^{3}}{\varphi(p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{b})^{3}}.

Proof.

We will prove it by induction. For the base case, when
b=1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t_{1}=0}^{3}\frac{1}{(p_{1}-1)^{t_{1}}}\dbinom{3}{t_{1}}=1+\frac{3}{p_{1}-1}+\frac{3}{(p_{1}-1)^{2}}+\frac{1}{(p_{1}-1)^{3}}=\frac{p_{1}^{3}}{\varphi(p_{1})^{3}},

Now assume (
8.6
) holds for
\mathcal{C}(j)
for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Then we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(j+1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t_{1}=0}^{3}\cdots\sum_{t_{j}=0}^{3}\sum_{t_{j+1}=0}^{3}\frac{1}{(p_{1}-1)^{t_{1}}\cdots(p_{k}-1)^{t_{j}}(p_{j+1}-1)^{t_{j+1}}}\dbinom{3}{t_{1}}\dbinom{3}{t_{2}}\cdots\dbinom{3}{t_{j}}\dbinom{3}{t_{j+1}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}(j)\sum_{t_{j+1}=0}^{3}\frac{1}{(p_{j+1}-1)^{t_{j+1}}}\dbinom{3}{t_{j+1}}=\frac{p_{j+1}^{3}}{(p_{j+1}-1)^{3}}\mathcal{C}(j)=\frac{(p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{j+1})^{3}}{\varphi(p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{j+1})^{3}}.

Hence by induction, the proof follows.
∎

8.2.
Decomposition of
G

We are now ready to prove the following key lemma which provides the decomposition of
G(q_{0})
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose
q_{0}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}
where
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{k}
denote the first
k
primes. Then we have

G(q_{0})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}G(p_{j}).

Proof.

We will proceed by induction. When
k=1
, the claim is true by definition. For clarity, we show the subtle arguments involving the case
k=2
. By (
8.1
), we write

(8.7)

\displaystyle G(p_{1}p_{2})=g(1,p_{1}p_{2})+g(p_{1},p_{1}p_{2})+g(p_{2},p_{1}p_{2})+g(p_{1}p_{2},p_{1}p_{2}).

By the definition of
g
in (
8.2
), we get

\displaystyle g(1,p_{1}p_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(p_{1}p_{2})^{3}}\sum_{r_{1}\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}\sum_{r_{2}\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}\sum_{r_{3}\in\mathcal{R}_{0}}\sum_{\ell_{1}=0}^{p_{1}p_{2}-1}\sum_{\ell_{2}=0}^{p_{1}p_{2}-1}\sum_{\ell_{3}=0}^{p_{1}p_{2}-1}e\left(\frac{-\ell_{1}r_{1}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{2}r_{2}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{3}r_{3}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}p_{2},p_{1}p_{2}+\ell_{1})}\bigg)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}p_{2},p_{1}p_{2}+\ell_{2})}\bigg)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}p_{2},p_{1}p_{2}+\ell_{3})}\bigg).

Using (
8.3
) and the discussions involving (
8.4
), we can write

\displaystyle g(1,p_{1}p_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{1}^{3}p_{2}^{3}}\underset{r_{11},r_{21},r_{31}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}{\sum\sum\sum}\hskip 2.84544pt\underset{r_{12},r_{22},r_{32}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}{\sum\sum\sum}\hskip 2.84544pt\underset{0\leqslant\ell_{11},\ell_{21},\ell_{31}\leqslant p_{1}-1}{\sum\sum\sum}\hskip 2.84544pt\underset{0\leqslant\ell_{12},\ell_{22},\ell_{32}\leqslant p_{2}-1}{\sum\sum\sum}e\bigg(\frac{-\ell_{11}r_{11}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\bigg)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{12}r_{12}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times e\left(\frac{-\ell_{21}r_{21}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{22}r_{22}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{31}r_{31}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{32}r_{32}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}p_{2},p_{1}p_{2}+\ell_{11}u_{1}+\ell_{12}u_{2})}\bigg)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}p_{2},p_{1}p_{2}+\ell_{21}u_{1}+\ell_{22}u_{2})}\bigg)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}p_{2},p_{1}p_{2}+\ell_{31}u_{1}+\ell_{32}u_{2})}\bigg).

Since
\mu
and
\varphi
are multiplicative, we rewrite
g(1,p_{1}p_{2})
as

\displaystyle g(1,p_{1}p_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{1}^{3}p_{2}^{3}}\underset{r_{11},r_{21},r_{31}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}{\sum\sum\sum}\hskip 2.84544pt\underset{r_{12},r_{22},r_{32}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}{\sum\sum\sum}\hskip 2.84544pt\underset{0\leqslant\ell_{11},\ell_{21},\ell_{31}\leqslant p_{1}-1}{\sum\sum\sum}\hskip 2.84544pt\underset{0\leqslant\ell_{12},\ell_{22},\ell_{32}\leqslant p_{2}-1}{\sum\sum\sum}e\left(\frac{-\ell_{11}r_{11}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{12}r_{12}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\times e\left(\frac{-\ell_{21}r_{21}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{22}r_{22}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{31}r_{31}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{32}r_{32}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}}{(p_{1},\ell_{11}u_{1})}\bigg)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{2}}{(p_{2},\ell_{12}u_{2})}\bigg)

(8.8)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}}{(p_{1},\ell_{21}u_{1})}\bigg)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{2}}{(p_{2},\ell_{22}u_{2})}\bigg)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}}{(p_{1},\ell_{31}u_{1})}\bigg)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{2}}{(p_{2},\ell_{32}u_{2})}\bigg).

For
j=1,2
and
i=1,2,3
, if
p_{j}\mid(p_{1}p_{2}+\ell_{i})
, then by (
8.4
),
p_{j}\mid\ell_{ij}
. Moreover, since
\ell_{ij}
runs through the complete residue class of
p_{j}
, if
p_{j}\mid\ell_{ij}
,
\ell_{ij}=0
is the only solution. Therefore for each
i
and
j
, we can divide the sum over
\ell_{ij}
into two cases,
\ell_{ij}=0
and
\ell_{ij}\neq 0
. If
\ell_{ij}=0
,

(8.9)

\displaystyle e\left(\frac{-\ell_{ij}r_{ij}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)=1\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\left(\frac{p_{j}}{(p_{j},\ell_{ij}u_{j})}\right)=1.

On the other hand, if
\ell_{ij}\neq 0
, then
p_{j}\nmid\ell_{ij}
, so

(8.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\left(\frac{p_{j}}{(p_{j},\ell_{ij}u_{j})}\right)=-\frac{1}{\varphi(p_{j})}.

Since
r_{ij}
and
q_{0}/p_{j}
are both coprime to
p_{j}
, we deduce that

(8.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell_{ij}=1}^{p_{j}-1}e\left(\frac{-\ell_{ij}r_{ij}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)=-e\left(\frac{-\ell_{ij}r_{ij}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right).

Observe that when we substitute (
8.10
) and (
8.11
) back into (
8.8
), the sign in front of the exponential sum and
\mu/\varphi
cancel out. Since each
\ell_{ij}
can be divided into the above two cases, there are in total

cases, and so using (
8.9
)-(
8.11
), we get

(8.12)

\displaystyle g(1,p_{1}p_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{C}(2)}{p_{1}^{3}p_{2}^{3}}\underset{r_{11},r_{21},r_{31}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}{\sum\sum\sum}\hskip 2.84544pt\underset{r_{12},r_{22},r_{32}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}{\sum\sum\sum}1=\frac{1}{\varphi(p_{1}p_{2})^{3}}\mathcal{R}_{1}^{3}\mathcal{R}_{2}^{3}=\frac{1}{\varphi(p_{1}p_{2})^{3}}\mathcal{R}_{0}^{3},

where the second equality follows from Lemma
8.1
. Similarly as above, we have

\displaystyle g(p_{1},p_{1}p_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(p_{1}p_{2})^{3}}\sum_{a=1}^{p_{1}-1}e\left(\frac{-am}{p_{1}}\right)\underset{r_{11},r_{21},r_{31}\in\mathcal{R}_{1}}{\sum\sum\sum}\hskip 2.84544pt\underset{r_{12},r_{22},r_{32}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}{\sum\sum\sum}\hskip 2.84544pt\underset{0\leqslant\ell_{11},\ell_{21},\ell_{31}\leqslant p_{1}-1}{\sum\sum\sum}\hskip 2.84544pt\underset{0\leqslant\ell_{12},\ell_{22},\ell_{32}\leqslant p_{2}-1}{\sum\sum\sum}

\displaystyle\quad\times e\left(\frac{-\ell_{11}r_{11}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{12}r_{12}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{21}r_{21}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{22}r_{22}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{31}r_{31}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)e\left(\frac{-\ell_{32}r_{32}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}p_{2},ap_{2}+\ell_{11}u_{1}+\ell_{12}u_{2})}\bigg)\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}p_{2},ap_{2}+\ell_{21}u_{1}+\ell_{22}u_{2})}\bigg)

(8.13)

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\bigg(\frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}p_{2},ap_{2}+\ell_{31}u_{1}+\ell_{32}u_{2})}\bigg).

Note that if
p_{2}\mid(ap_{2}+\ell_{i})
, then
p_{2}\mid\ell_{i2}
, and if
p_{1}\mid(ap_{2}+\ell_{i})
, then
\ell_{i1}\equiv-ap_{2}\bmod p_{1}
. Since
\ell_{i1}
runs through the entire residue class modulo
p_{1}
, only one such
\ell_{i1}
could satisfy the congruence condition. The same argument forces
\ell_{i2}
to be 0. When
\ell_{i1}\equiv-ap_{2}\bmod p_{1}
, we have

(8.14)

\displaystyle e\left(\frac{-\ell_{i1}r_{i1}}{p_{1}p_{2}}\right)=e\left(\frac{ar_{i1}}{p_{1}}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\mu}{\varphi}\left(\frac{p_{1}}{(p_{1},\ell_{i1}u_{1})}\right)=1.

On the other hand, when
\ell_{i1}\not\equiv-ap_{2}\bmod p_{1}
, then we deduce that
