Introduction

Pattern matching is a fundamental problem in computer science with applications in text search, bioinformatics, log analysis, and data mining
[
Pandiselvam et al., 2014
,
Tahir, 2017
,
Faro and Lecroq, 2013
,
Abrishami et al., 2013
,
Hakak et al., 2019
,
Amit et al., 2014
,
Neamatollahi et al., 2020
,
Capacho et al., 2017
,
Cheng et al., 2013
,
Bushong et al., 2020
,
Johannesmeyer et al., 2002
]
.
In its basic form, given a pattern
P
of length
m
and a text
T
of length
n
with symbols from alphabet
\Sigma
with size
\sigma
, the task is to decide whether
P
occurs in
T

as a contiguous substring
; that is, whether there exists an index
i\in\{1,\dots,n-m+1\}
such that

\textsf{T}_{i:i+m-1}=\textsf{P}.

This problem has been studied for decades, and many efficient linear-time solutions such as the Knuth–Morris–Pratt and Boyer–Moore algorithms are well known
[
Knuth et al., 1977
,
Boyer and Moore, 1977
]
.

Traditionally, pattern matching aims to find exact occurrences of a pattern, yet in many practical applications such rigidity is unrealistic
[
Ukkonen, 1985
,
Landau and Vishkin, 1988
,
Navarro, 2001
,
Galil and Park, 1990
]
. The pattern or the text may contain uncertain or corrupted symbols due to noise, ambiguity, or small variations; for example when accounting for mutations in genomic data. This motivates the study of algorithms that search for substrings close to the pattern under standard distance measures such as Hamming distance
[
Abrahamson, 1987
,
Chan et al., 2023
,
Jin and Xu, 2024
]
.
Another common way to model uncertainty is to assume that the positions of the corrupted symbols are known. This is typically handled by introducing a
wildcard
symbol, which matches any single symbol of the alphabet
\Sigma

[
Fischer and Paterson, 1974
,
Indyk, 1998
,
Kalai, 2002
]
. This gives rise to the problem of
pattern matching with wildcards
, where across both the pattern and the text, the total number of wildcard positions is at most
k
.
Since each wildcard can stand for any symbol of
\Sigma
, a pattern with
k
wildcards implicitly represents up to
|\Sigma|^{k}
different strings.

Adding wildcards makes the pattern matching problem harder. With a fixed pattern, the search space is small. For example, let the text be
\textsf{T}=\texttt{CACCGGCT}
and the pattern be
\textsf{P}=\texttt{CG}
. For this instance, there is only one match. If we instead use
\textsf{P}^{\prime}=\texttt{C?}
, where “?” refers to the wildcard symbol, then every occurrence of
C
in
T
becomes a match. This simple change increases the number of matches from
1
to
4
. Thus, even limited uncertainty can drastically increase the complexity of the problem.

A substantial body of work has studied pattern matching with wildcards in either the pattern or the text
[
Kalai, 2002
,
Bathie et al., 2024b
,
Amir et al., 2004
]
.
For this problem, Fischer and Paterson
[
Fischer and Paterson, 1974
]
gave an

\mathcal{O}(n\log m\log\sigma)
algorithm, and later Indyk
[
Indyk, 1998
]
gave a randomized
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
algorithm.
Kalai
[
Kalai, 2002
]
subsequently obtained a randomized
\mathcal{O}(n\log m)
algorithm, while the first deterministic
\mathcal{O}(n\log m)
algorithm was given by Cole and Hariharan
[
Cole and Hariharan, 2002b
]
.

In this paper, we study pattern matching with wildcards in a fully dynamic setting.
Both the text and the pattern may change through insertions, deletions, or substitutions, including updates to wildcard positions.
Our goal is to maintain pattern-matching queries in sublinear time.
For dynamic pattern matching, most prior work considers settings without wildcards. In these studies,
dynamism may arise in several ways: the text may be updated dynamically, the pattern may be updated dynamically, or both may change over time.
All three cases have been studied extensively.
For dynamic text with a static pattern, Amir et al.
[
Amir et al., 2007a
]
presented an
algorithm that preprocesses a text of length
n
and a pattern of length
m
in
\mathcal{O}(n\log\log m+m\sqrt{\log m})
time, reporting all new occurrences of the pattern after each text update in
\mathcal{O}(\log\log m)
time.

For static text with a dynamic pattern, efficient suffix tree based solutions were introduced by Weiner
[
Weiner, 1973a
]
and later improved by McCreight
[
McCreight, 1976
]
, Ukkonen
[
Ukkonen, 1995
]
, Farach
[
Farach, 1997
]
, and others
[
Kärkkäinen and Sanders, 2003
]
.
For a fixed finite alphabet, these algorithms preprocess the text in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time and answer pattern queries in
\mathcal{O}(m+\mathit{occ})
time, where
\mathit{occ}
is the number of occurrences of the pattern.
Finally, in the fully dynamic setting where both text and pattern may change, several algorithms have been developed
[
Gu et al., 1994
,
Ferragina and Grossi, 1995
,
Sahinalp and Vishkin, 1996
,
Alstrup et al., 2000
]
.
For example, Sahinalp and Vishkin
[
Sahinalp and Vishkin, 1996
]
support insertions and deletions in
\mathcal{O}(\log^{3}n+m)
time per update, with query times comparable to Weiner’s static algorithm.
Alstrup, Brodal, and Rauhe
[
Alstrup et al., 2000
]
handle insertions, deletions, and substring moves in
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}n\log\log n\log^{*}n)
time per update, and answer pattern searches in
\mathcal{O}(\log n\log\log n+m+\mathit{occ})
time.

Our work extends these dynamic pattern matching studies to consider wildcards. We provide a randomized algorithm that, after
\mathcal{O}(n\log^{2}n)
preprocessing, supports updates and queries in amortized time

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(kn^{\frac{k}{k+1}}+k^{2}\log n\right),

which is sublinear for
k=o(\log n)
and truly sublinear for constant
k
. We also show that this bound is almost tight: under SETH, no algorithm with subquadratic preprocessing time can achieve truly sublinear update time when
k=\Omega(\log n)
.
In addition to the general result, we design specialized algorithms for two restricted settings, with the complexity expressed in terms of the number of non-wildcard symbols in the pattern.

(i)

When the wildcard positions are fixed, we obtain preprocessing in
\mathcal{O}(nw)
time and support updates in time
\mathcal{O}(w+\log n)
time, where
w
is the number of non-wildcard symbols.

(ii)

When the pattern contains at most two non-wildcard symbols, we give a deterministic algorithm based on FFT and block decomposition techniques, with preprocessing time
\mathcal{O}(n^{1.8})
and update time
\mathcal{O}(n^{0.8}\log n)
.

Organization of the paper.

Section
2
gives a high-level overview of our techniques and main results.
In Section
4
, we introduce formal definitions and preliminaries.
The general dynamic pattern matching with wildcards setting is studied in Section
5
, followed by algorithms for sparse pattern matching regimes in Section
6
.
Finally, in Section
7
we establish a conditional lower bound on the update time of any algorithm for the dynamic pattern matching with wildcards problem.

2
Results and Techniques

We study the problem of dynamic pattern matching in the presence of wildcard symbols.
Our contributions fall into two main lines.

General setting.

In the most flexible version of the problem, both the text and the pattern may contain wildcards, and updates may insert, delete, or substitute symbols (including wildcards).
For this setting, we design randomized algorithms with preprocessing time
\mathcal{O}(n\log^{2}n)
and update/query time

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\big(kn^{k/(k+1)}+k^{2}\log n\big).

The bound is truly sublinear for constant
k
, and remains sublinear whenever
k=o(\log n)
.
This gives the first general framework for handling fully dynamic wildcards on both sides of the matching problem.

Sparse patterns.

We also investigate regimes where the number of non-wildcards in the pattern is small.
This additional structure enables more efficient algorithms:

•

If the pattern contains at most two non-wildcard symbols, we obtain a deterministic algorithm based on FFT and block decomposition, with preprocessing time
\mathcal{O}(n^{1.8})
and update time
\mathcal{O}(n^{0.8}\log n)
. Though the setting might seem simple, this is the most technical part of the paper, with the most challenging component captured by the
Range-Pair
problem (Problem
6.1
), which we solve and use in this regime.

•

If all wildcards are confined to the pattern and their positions are fixed in advance, we design a randomized algorithm with preprocessing time
\mathcal{O}(nw)
and update time
\mathcal{O}(w+\log n)
, where
w
is the number of non-wildcard positions.

Finally, we establish a conditional lower bound on the update and query time for any algorithm solving dynamic pattern matching with wildcards.

Together, these results give a unified picture of the problem’s complexity.

This leads us to a frequency-based classification of symbols: those that occur at most
\tau
times in
T
are called
rare
, while the rest are considered
frequent
.
When
P
contains a rare symbol, we verify all possible candidate matches that align that rare symbol.

Given this observation, we can assume without loss of generality that every symbol in the pattern is frequent. In this case, the number of candidate positions in the text that could match the pattern is large. The key insight, however, is that the number of distinct frequent symbols is limited to
n/\tau
. This restriction allows us to design an alternative algorithm based on standard data structures.

The main data structure we use in our algorithm is the
polynomial rolling hash table
. At a high level, it provides a succinct summary of substrings that (i) updates efficiently after a single-symbol change and (ii) lets us handle wildcards. We treat wildcards by partitioning the string into contiguous non-wildcard segments: the hash is computed only over these segments, and two strings match under the wildcard semantics if and only if all corresponding non-wildcard parts have equal hashes. In this way, wildcards are naturally ignored, and correctness reduces to verifying equality of the fixed pieces. To support dynamic updates, we maintain these hashes in balanced BSTs such as treaps
[
Seidel and Aragon, 1996
]
, which allow insertions, deletions, and substitutions in logarithmic time, and combine them with fully dynamic LCS to handle candidate completions with polylogarithmic overhead
[
Charalampopoulos et al., 2020
]
.

As we discussed earlier, our algorithm achieves truly sublinear update time when the number of wildcards is constant, and remains sublinear as long as the number of wildcards is
o(\log n)
.
A natural question is whether these bounds can be improved further, perhaps matching the polylogarithmic update times known for the static case.
Unfortunately, our results suggest otherwise.
We show that once the number of wildcards reaches
\Omega(\log n)
, no algorithm with reasonable preprocessing can achieve truly sublinear update time with subquadratic preprocessing unless the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) fails.
This hardness result follows from a reduction from the orthogonal vectors problem.

Given our results, several natural questions remain open:

•

Can we design algorithms with truly sublinear update time when
k
is not constant but still
o(\log n)
? For constant
k
, can we further improve the update and query bounds?

•

Can we obtain truly sublinear algorithms for cases where
k=\Omega(\log n)
, provided the wildcards have some special structure?

The first direction appears particularly promising for future work.
In line with the second, we provide algorithms achieving truly sublinear update times for certain resticted settings.
We first consider the setting where the wildcard positions are fixed in advance and the number of non-wildcard symbols is sublinear.
In this case, it suffices to maintain rolling hash values only for the
\mathcal{O}(k)
non-wildcard positions, which enables sublinear update times for both the pattern and the text.

For the special case where the pattern contains at most two non-wildcard symbols, the static version of the problem can already be solved efficiently by applying FFT: by encoding blocks of the text as polynomials and using convolution, one can align the two characters across the text. In the dynamic setting, however, a single update can change many convolution values, so the FFT method alone is insufficient. To address this, we combine block decomposition with the frequent/rare symbol technique: the text is divided into blocks, only the affected blocks are recomputed after an update, rare symbols are treated explicitly, and frequent ones are handled in the convolution structures. This approach ensures that each update can be processed within sublinear time, achieving preprocessing complexity
\mathcal{O}(n^{\tfrac{9}{5}})
and query complexity
\mathcal{O}(n^{\tfrac{4}{5}})
.

3
Related Work

String pattern matching has a long history.
A key milestone was the
Knuth–Morris–Pratt (KMP)
algorithm
[
Knuth et al., 1977
]
, which gave a linear-time solution via prefix-function preprocessing.
Around the same time, the
Boyer–Moore
algorithm
[
Boyer and Moore, 1977
]
introduced efficient skipping heuristics.
These breakthroughs sparked a rich line of research
in string matching—including automaton-based methods such as Aho–Corasick
[
Aho and Corasick, 1975
]
, hashing strategies like Rabin–Karp
[
Karp and Rabin, 1987
]
, and data-structure techniques based on suffix trees and arrays
[
Weiner, 1973b
,
Wu et al., 1990
]
.
Here we briefly mentionn some of these studies in several categories.

Pattern matching with wildcards.

Wildcards add substantial flexibility but also complexity.
Fischer and Paterson
[
Fischer and Paterson, 1974
]
gave an early algorithm with
\mathcal{O}(n\log m\log\sigma)
runtime, later improved by Indyk and Kalai
[
Indyk, 1998
,
Kalai, 2002
]
and by Cole and Hariharan
[
Cole and Hariharan, 2002a
]
to
\mathcal{O}(n\log m)
deterministically.
Clifford and Clifford
[
Clifford and Clifford, 2007
]
presented a simpler algorithm with similar guarantees.
More recent work includes filtering approaches by Barton et al.
[
Barton, 2014
]
and average-case analyses by Kopelowitz and Porat
[
Kopelowitz and Porat, 2022
]
, which separate the complexity of wildcard matching from exact matching.

Dynamic pattern matching.

Weiner
[
Weiner, 1973b
]
gave the first efficient solution for the case of a fixed text and a dynamic pattern, supporting queries in
\mathcal{O}(|P|+\text{tocc})
time after linear preprocessing.
Amir et al.
[
Amir et al., 2007b
]
studied the complementary case of a dynamic text with a fixed pattern, showing that new occurrences can be maintained in
\mathcal{O}(\log\log m)
time per update.
More recently, Monteiro and dos Santos
[
Monteiro and dos Santos, 2024
]
revisited dynamic patterns with a suffix-array based framework, achieving amortized
\mathcal{O}(\log|T|)
update time for both symbol and substring edits.

Approximate matching.

Approximate matching has been studied extensively, with edit distance by Levenshtein
[
Levenshtein, 1966
]
, longest common subsequence (LCS) by Needleman and Wunsch
[
Needleman and Wunsch, 1970
]
, and dynamic time warping (DTW) analyzed by Bringmann and Künnemann
[
Bringmann and Künnemann, 2015
]
as the most common similarity measures.
Ukkonen
[
Ukkonen, 1985
]
gave early algorithms for edit distance, and Masek and Paterson
[
Masek and Paterson, 1980
]
introduced the Four-Russians speedup.
However, conditional hardness results show strong barriers: Backurs and Indyk
[
Backurs and Indyk, 2014
]
proved that strongly subquadratic edit distance would refute
\mathsf{SETH}
, and Bringmann and Künnemann
[
Bringmann and Künnemann, 2015
]
extended these lower bounds to LCS and DTW.
A detailed survey of algorithmic techniques is given by Navarro
[
Navarro, 2001
]
.

Dynamic and approximate wildcard matching.

Dynamic and approximate variants of wildcard matching have also been studied.
Clifford et al.
[
Clifford et al., 2018
]
considered dynamic data structures for exact matching with wildcards, Hamming distance, and inner product (DynEM), establishing both upper and lower bounds.
Crochemore et al.
[
Crochemore et al., 2015
]
gave algorithms for online matching with wildcards under updates, while Gog and Navarro
[
Gog and Navarro, 2017
]
designed compressed-text versions supporting efficient updates.
Charalampopoulos et al.
[
Charalampopoulos et al., 2020
]
developed polylogarithmic-update algorithms for the dynamic longest common substring problem with wildcards, and Kociumaka, Radoszewski, and Starikovskaya
[
Kociumaka et al., 2019
]
proved SETH-hardness for the longest common substring with
k
mismatches while giving approximation algorithms.
Most recently, Bathie, Charalampopoulos, and Starikovskaya
[
Bathie et al., 2024a
]
provided a fine-grained analysis of approximate matching with wildcards and mismatches, presenting new structural lower bounds and efficient occurrence representations.

4
Preliminaries

We consider a finite alphabet
\Sigma
of size
|\Sigma|=\sigma
.
Our input consists of two strings: a
text

\textsf{T}\in(\Sigma\cup\{\texttt{?}\})^{*}
of length
n
, and a
pattern

\textsf{P}\in(\Sigma\cup\{\texttt{?}\})^{*}
of length
m
.
Both the text and the pattern together may contain at most
k
wildcard symbols, denoted by
?
.
A wildcard symbol can match any symbol from the alphabet.

For a string
S
and
1\leq\ell\leq r\leq|S|
, we use
S_{\ell:r}
to denote the substring

S_{\ell}S_{\ell+1}\dots S_{r}.

For example, if
S=\texttt{abcde}
, then
S_{2:4}=\texttt{bcd}
.
We say that
P

matches
a substring
\textsf{T}_{i:\,i+m-1}

(
1\leq i\leq n-m+1
) if for every
1\leq j\leq m
, either

\textsf{P}_{j}=\textsf{T}_{i+j-1}
,
\textsf{P}_{j}=\texttt{?}
or
\textsf{T}_{i+j-1}=\texttt{?}
.
The set of all matching positions is denoted by

\Gamma(\textsf{P},\textsf{T})=\{\,i\mid 1\leq i\leq n-m+1,\ \textsf{P}\text{ matches }\textsf{T}_{i:i+m-1}\,\}.

The concatenation of two strings
S
,
S^{\prime}
, denoted
S\cdot S^{\prime}
,
is defined by appending
S^{\prime}
to the end of
S
.
For instance, if
S=\texttt{ab}
and
S^{\prime}=\texttt{cd}
, then

S\cdot S^{\prime}=\texttt{abcd}
.

We classify symbols in
\Sigma
as
frequent
or
rare
according to a frequency threshold.
Let the threshold value be

\tau=\bigl(n^{k}\log^{7}n\bigr)^{\tfrac{1}{k+1}}.

A symbol
c\in\Sigma
is called
rare
if it occurs fewer than
\tau
times in
T
, and
frequent
otherwise.

For each symbol
c\in(\Sigma\ \cup\texttt{?})
, we maintain the set

\mathfrak{R}(c)=\{\,i\mid 1\leq i\leq n,\ \textsf{T}_{i}=c\,\},

which stores all positions where
c
appears in
T
.
In addition to membership queries for
\mathfrak{R}(c)
, we also require efficient support for
lower bound queries
, i.e., finding the smallest index in
\mathfrak{R}(c)
that is greater than or equal to a given value. For handling the updates and queries efficiently, we use balanced binary search trees.

Fact 4.1

(Balanced BST support)
.

Each set
\mathfrak{R}(c)
can be maintained under dynamic text updates such that membership queries, lower bound queries, and updates are all supported in
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
time, using a balanced binary search tree
[
Cormen et al., 2022
]
.

Many of our algorithms are randomized and succeed with high probability. Throughout this paper, we say an event occurs
with high probability
if it holds with probability at least
1-1/n
.

The foundation for our analysis, based on polynomial hashing, is detailed in
Handling Matching Query
. We use a polynomial rolling hash with a prime modulus
p

[
Karp and Rabin, 1987
]
to efficiently support matching queries. The hash function is formally defined as follows.

Definition 4.1

(Polynomial Rolling Hash)
.

Let
S
be a string over an ordered alphabet
\Sigma=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{|\Sigma|}\}
.
We define a mapping
\mathrm{\pi}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathrm{\pi}(c_{j})=j
for
1\leq j\leq|\Sigma|
.
The
polynomial rolling hash
of
S
is

H_{b,p}\left(S\right)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{|S|}\mathrm{\pi}(S_{i})\cdot b^{\,|S|-i}\right)\bmod p,

where
b
is chosen uniformly at random from
\{1,2,\dots,p-1\}
, and
p
is a large prime modulus.

We leverage the following lemma from
Handling Matching Query
to prove our probabilistic claims in the paper.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\{(S_{1},S^{\prime}_{1}),\dots,(S_{N},S^{\prime}_{N})\}
be a collection of
N
pairs of distinct strings,
each string drawn from the alphabet
\Sigma
and of length at most
x
.
Then the probability that there exists at least one pair
(S_{i},S^{\prime}_{i})
such that

H_{b,p}\left(S_{i}\right)=H_{b,p}\left(S^{\prime}_{i}\right)
is bounded by

\tfrac{N\,x}{p}.

Dynamic Updates

As described earlier, both the pattern and the text may change dynamically. We support the following operations:

•

Pattern Update:
Insert, delete, or replace a symbol at position
i
in the pattern, where the new symbol
c\in\Sigma\cup\{\texttt{?}\}
for
1\leq i\leq m
.

•

Text Update:
Insert, delete, or replace a symbol at position

Collision Probability.
A

occurs when two distinct strings
S_{1}\neq S_{2}
yield the same hash value, i.e.,
H_{b,p}\left(S_{1}\right)=H_{b,p}\left(S_{2}\right)
. Since our hash function is chosen randomly from a universal family, we can bound the probability of such an event.

Lemma 4.5

(Pairwise Collision Probability)
.

For any two distinct strings
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, the probability that they collide under the hash function from Definition

is

\Pr[H_{b,p}\left(S_{1}\right)=H_{b,p}\left(S_{2}\right)]\;\leq\;\frac{\max(|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|)}{p}.

The probability is over the random choice of the base
b
.

Proof.
The hash values are computed as:

H_{b,p}\left(S_{1}\right)=R_{S_{1}}(b),\quad H_{b,p}\left(S_{2}\right)=R_{S_{2}}(b)

where
R_{S_{1}}(x)
and
R_{S_{2}}(x)
are polynomials of degrees
|S_{1}|-1
and
|S_{2}|-1
respectively.

Since

, the polynomial

is a non-zero polynomial of degree at most
\max(|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|)-1

The hashes collide when

, i.e., when

is a root of

modulo
p

By the Schwartz–Zippel lemma

\Pr\!\left[H_{b,p}\left(S_{1}\right)=H_{b,p}\left(S_{2}\right)\right]\;\leq\;\frac{\max(|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|)-1}{p}\;<\;\frac{\max(|S_{1}|,|S_{2}|)}{p}.

Polynomial rolling hash functions have several computational properties that make them well-suited for our problem.
By precomputing

Lemma 4.6
.

Let

and

be two strings over the same ordered alphabet, with lengths
|S|=\ell_{1}
and
|S^{\prime}|=\ell_{2}

Let

be the polynomial rolling hash function from Definition

If

are precomputed for all

H_{b,p}\left(S\right)
and

\mathcal{O}(1)

Proof.
By the definition of the polynomial rolling hash, we have:

H_{b,p}\left(S\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{1}}\pi(S_{i})\cdot b^{\,\ell_{1}-i}\bmod p,

H_{b,p}\left(S^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{2}}\pi(S^{\prime}_{j})\cdot b^{\,\ell_{2}-j}\bmod p.

The concatenation
S\cdot S^{\prime}
has length
\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}
, and its hash is:

H_{b,p}\left(S\cdot S^{\prime}\right)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{1}}\pi(S_{i})\cdot b^{\,\ell_{1}+\ell_{2}-i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell_{2}}\pi(S^{\prime}_{j})\cdot b^{\,\ell_{2}-j}\right)\bmod p.

The first term can be factored as:

\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell_{1}}\pi(S_{i})\cdot b^{\,\ell_{1}-i}\right)\cdot b^{\ell_{2}}=H_{b,p}\left(S\right)\cdot b^{\ell_{2}}\bmod p.

Thus,

H_{b,p}\left(S\cdot S^{\prime}\right)=\left(H_{b,p}\left(S\right)\cdot b^{\ell_{2}}+H_{b,p}\left(S^{\prime}\right)\right)\bmod p.

If
b^{\ell_{2}}\bmod p
is precomputed, this formula requires only one modular multiplication, one modular addition, and one modular reduction, all of which take
\mathcal{O}(1)
time.
Therefore, the concatenation hash can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time.

5
Fully Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards

In this section, we consider the general case of
dynamic pattern matching with wildcards problem
. Note that here both the text
T
and the pattern
P
may contain wildcard symbols. We assume that updates consist only of single-character substitutions, and do not include insertions or deletions.
This simplifies the presentation of the main ideas.
We will later explain in Remark
5.2
how the algorithm extends to handle insertions and deletions as well.

Our solution is based on maintaining a modified version of the text, denoted by T’, where every rare symbol is replaced with a special placeholder ‘#‘. Therefore, T’ is defined as

\textsf{T'}_{i}\;=\;\begin{cases}\#&\text{if }\textsf{T}_{i}\text{ is rare},\\
\textsf{T}_{i}&\text{if }\textsf{T}_{i}\text{ is frequent}.\end{cases}

Note that the frequency of symbols may change during the dynamic updates.
Whenever the frequency of a symbol crosses the threshold
\tau
, we update T’ by replacing its occurrences with ‘#‘ (if it becomes rare) or restoring them to the original symbol (if it becomes frequent). To implement this, we maintain for each symbol
c\in\Sigma
the occurrence set
\mathfrak{R}(c)
. When the text is updated at position
i
from
c_{\text{old}}
to
c_{\text{new}}
, we remove
i
from
\mathfrak{R}(c_{\text{old}})
and insert it into
\mathfrak{R}(c_{\text{new}})
. If the frequency of a frequent symbol falls below
\tau
, we mark its
\tau
occurrences in T’ with the placeholder ‘#‘; conversely, if a rare symbol becomes frequent, we restore its actual symbol at those positions. Each such change involves only
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
positions, giving an update cost of
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
. The full procedure is formalized in Algorithm
5
.

The preprocessing phase (Algorithm
1

\mathfrak{R}(\cdot)
for each symbol, and computing the frequent set
F
. After each update, Algorithm
5
efficiently maintains these structures.

Given this setup, the matching procedure proceeds differently depending on whether the pattern contains a rare symbol. If it does (Case 1, Algorithm
2
), then any valid match must align that rare symbol either with one of its actual occurrences in the text or with one of the wildcard positions in the text. This observation allows us to restrict attention to candidate substrings centered around these positions in T, checking them directly for consistency with the rest of the pattern.

If, on the other hand, the pattern contains no rare symbols (Case 2, Algorithm
3
), then each wildcard may be filled with either a frequent symbol or the placeholder ‘#‘. We systematically enumerate all possible completions of both the pattern and the text under this rule and verify each candidate against T’ using efficient LCS queries. Since the number of completions depends only on the number of wildcards and the frequency threshold, this approach avoids recomputation from scratch after updates and remains tractable when the number of wildcards is small.

The overall procedure is summarized in Algorithm

6
Sparse Pattern

In this section we study
sparse
patterns, namely patterns
P\in(\Sigma\cup\{\texttt{?}\})^{m}
in which only a few positions are non-wildcard and the remaining positions are wildcards. Let
S=\{i\in[m]:\textsf{P}_{i}\in\Sigma\}
be the set of non-wildcard indices and let
s=|S|\ll m
.
Such sparse patterns naturally arise in applications such as biological sequence analysis, where only a few positions of a motif are fixed while the rest may vary, or in intrusion detection, where signatures often contain only a few fixed bytes.
In this section we consider two regimes: (i) the case where the pattern contains at most two non-wildcard symbols, and (ii) the case where the wildcard positions in the pattern are fixed in advance and the text
T
contains no wildcards.
Throughout this section we work in a
substitution-only
dynamic model: updates may change symbols in the text or in the pattern, but no insertions or deletions are allowed, so both lengths
n
and
m
(and, in the fixed-wildcard regime, the wildcard index set) remain fixed.

6.1
At Most Two Non-Wildcard Symbols

In this section, we study a special case of
Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards
under the additional assumption that the pattern
P
contains at most two non-wildcard symbols at any time.
We focus on this substitution-only regime of the problem, and the text
T
contains no wildcards.
But in Remark
6.4
and Remark
6.5
we will explain how the algorithm can be extended to handle insertions/deletions in the pattern as well as wildcards in the text.
Moreover, in this regime we are also able to count the exact number of matches in the same asymptotic complexity.
For consistency with the other sections we only present the decision version in the main algorithm, but Remark
6.6
explains how counting can also be supported.
For this setting, we design an algorithm with preprocessing time
\mathcal{O}(n^{9/5})
, pattern update time
\mathcal{O}(1)
, text update time
\mathcal{O}(n^{4/5}\log n)
, and query time
\mathcal{O}(n^{4/5})
.

A general observation in sparse patterns is that leading or trailing runs of
?
can be ignored when reasoning about matches.
These wildcards only shift the range of permissible starting positions but do not affect the relative positions among the concrete symbols.
Consequently, we can conceptually divide any pattern into three parts: a prefix of wildcards, a block of concrete symbols (the
query block
), and a suffix of wildcards:

\underbrace{\texttt{?}\dots\texttt{?}}_{\text{prefix wildcards}}\;\underbrace{c_{1}\dots c_{k}}_{\text{query block}}\;\underbrace{\texttt{?}\dots\texttt{?}}_{\text{suffix wildcards}}.

After removing the leading and trailing wildcards, let the remaining concrete positions in
P
be
i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}
with symbols
c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{k}
.
A substring
\textsf{T}_{s:s+m-1}
matches
P
if and only if

\textsf{T}_{s+i_{j}-1}=c_{j}\quad\text{for all }j=1,\dots,k.

Equivalently, a match occurs when the concrete symbols appear in the text at positions that preserve the relative offsets
i_{j+1}-i_{j}
.
This perspective allows match queries to be reduced to checking whether these symbols appear together at the required distances within the allowed range of starting positions.

In the special case where the pattern
P
contains at most two non-wildcard symbols, the matching task reduces to checking whether two symbols occur in the text at a specific distance from each other.
This motivates the following formal problem.

Problem 6.1

(Dynamic Range-Pair Query)
.

Let
\Lambda
be an alphabet of size
\lambda
.
The input is a dynamic string
\mathcal{T}\in\Lambda^{\eta}
.
The task is to support the following operations:

•

\texttt{UpdateSymbol}(i,c)
: update the symbol at position
i
to
c\in\Lambda
.

•

\texttt{PairQuery}(l,r,a,b,d)
: return the number of indices
i\in[l,\,r-d-1]
such that

\mathcal{T}_{i}=a
and
\mathcal{T}_{i+d+1}=b
, where
a,b\in\Lambda
and
d\geq 0
.

This setting is closely related to the classical
Gapped String Indexing
problem
[
Bille et al., 2022
]
.
In Gapped String Indexing, one preprocesses a static string
S
and answers queries given by two patterns
P_{1},P_{2}
and a gap interval
[\alpha,\beta]
, asking whether there exist occurrences at positions
i
and
j\geq i
such that
j-i\in[\alpha,\beta]

[
Bille et al., 2022
]
.
In the special case where
|P_{1}|=|P_{2}|=1
and
\alpha=\beta=d+1
, this reduces to deciding or counting occurrences of two symbols whose positions differ by exactly
d+1
.
The Dynamic Range-Pair Query problem captures this special case while additionally restricting valid pairs to lie within a query range
[l,r]
, and allowing the underlying text to be updated dynamically.
Viewed this way, the problem can also be interpreted as a dynamic, range-restricted variant of
Shifted Set Intersection
(equivalent to 3SUM indexing in the static setting)
[
Bille et al., 2022
,
Aronov et al., 2024
]
.

To tackle this problem, we use a block decomposition approach.
The text is partitioned into consecutive blocks, and for each block we maintain precomputed counts of symbol pairs whose left endpoint lies inside the block.
Updates are handled lazily: when only a small number of positions in a block are modified, we record these changes explicitly and leave the precomputed counters unchanged; once the number of pending changes exceeds a fixed threshold, the block is rebuilt from scratch.
For a query range
[l,r]
, blocks that lie entirely inside the query interval—referred to as
full blocks
—are answered using their precomputed counters, with a correction for any pending updates stored for those blocks.
Only positions belonging to blocks that intersect the query interval partially—namely, the boundary blocks at the left and right ends of the range—are handled by explicit scanning.

Theorem 6.2
.

By combining the preprocessing procedure (Algorithm

the update procedure (Algorithm

and the query procedure (Algorithm

the data structure answers the Dynamic Range-Pair Query Problem
(Problem

•

\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{\beta}\log\beta\cdot\lambda^{2}\;+\;\eta\beta\right),

•

per-query time

\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tfrac{\eta}{\beta}\cdot\mu\;+\;\beta\right),

•

\mathcal{O}\!\left(\beta\;+\;\tfrac{\eta\log\beta\cdot\lambda^{2}}{\mu}\right),

•

Proof. Preprocessing time.

Block partitioning and initializing
\overline{\mathcal{T}}
and the sets

\mathcal{O}(\eta)

(a,b)

\mathcal{O}(\eta\beta).

For
\mathsf{Pair}_{i,j,a,b,d^{\prime}}

(\frac{\eta}{\beta})^{2}

\lambda^{2}

\mathcal{O}(\beta\log\beta)

\mathcal{O}\!\left(\left(\tfrac{\eta}{\beta}\right)^{2}\cdot\beta\log\beta\cdot\lambda^{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{\beta}\log\beta\cdot\lambda^{2}\right).

Query time.

[l,\,r-d-1]

The two boundary fragments together contain at most

For full blocks inside

When

When

j=

\beta

The base contribution from block

\mathsf{Pair}_{i,\,j+1,\,a,\,b,\,d^{\prime\prime}}

\mathcal{O}\!\big(\frac{\eta}{\beta}\cdot\mu+\beta\big)

Amortized update time.

An update

\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{i}}

The set
\mathsf{Pending}_{b}

\mathcal{T}

\overline{\mathcal{T}}

\mathcal{O}(\beta)

\mathcal{O}(\beta)

The table

\mathcal{O}(\eta\cdot\lambda^{2})

Correctness of the count.

Boundary fragments are checked directly in

For full blocks with

(i) if the left endpoint is pending (

\mathcal{T}_{i+d+1}=b

Now for the main problem, the algorithm distinguishes the possible cases depending on how many non-wildcard symbols appear in the pattern.
If the pattern contains a rare symbol, then all its occurrences in the text can be inspected directly, and since at most one further non-wildcard position needs to be checked, the query runs in
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
time.
If the pattern contains only frequent symbols, three subcases arise.
With no non-wildcards the pattern trivially matches any substring of length

(a constant-time check).
With exactly one non-wildcard, the query reduces to checking whether that symbol appears in the valid range of positions, which can be done using the set
\mathfrak{R}(c)
in
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
time.
Finally, with two non-wildcards the problem reduces to asking whether two symbols appear in the text at the required distance, which is exactly the Range-Pair Problem defined earlier.
To support this reduction we maintain a mapping
\phi:\Sigma\to\Lambda
that assigns all rare symbols to 0 and gives each frequent symbol a distinct nonzero code, so that the mapped text
\mathcal{T}
serves as input to the Range-Pair structure.
The procedures for preprocessing, queries, and updates are summarized in Algorithms

, and

.

Combining the preprocessing in Algorithm

, the query procedure in Algorithm

, and the text/pattern updates in Algorithm

\textsf{T}\in\Sigma^{n}
):

•

preprocessing time
\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{9}{5}})
,

•

pattern-update time
\mathcal{O}(1)
,

•

\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{4}{5}}\log n)
(amortized),

•

per-query time
\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{4}{5}})
.

Proof.
We follow a similar approach to Theorem
5.1
, classifying symbols into frequent and rare, but with a modified choice of
\tau
. Since the number of frequent symbols is at most
\frac{n}{\tau},
we leverage this property for the optimization.

Case 1:
P
contains a rare symbol.

If
P
contains a rare symbol, we iterate over all its occurrences in
T
. For each occurrence at position
j
, a check is performed to determine whether
P
matches the substring
\textsf{T}_{j-i+1,j-i+m}
by verifying the symbol at the other non-wildcard position (if any) in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time, since at most one other position requires checking. The total time complexity for this case is
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
.

Case 2:
P
contains only frequent symbols and wildcards.
The pattern
P
falls into one of three categories, each handled independently:

i)

No Non-Wildcard Symbols.

In this case,
P
consists entirely of wildcards and trivially matches any substring of
T
of length
m
. We only need to verify that
m\leq n
, which is a constant-time check.

ii)

One Non-Wildcard Symbol.

Assume
\textsf{P}_{i}=c
, where
c
is a non-wildcard symbol, and all other positions in
P
are wildcards.
We aim to find a position
j
in
T
such that
\textsf{T}_{j}=c
and there are at least
i-1
symbols to the left and
m-i
symbols to the right of
j
.
Equivalently, we seek any index
j
such that

i\leq j\leq n-m+i\quad\text{and}\quad\textsf{T}_{j}=c.

We derive the answer using the set
\mathfrak{R}(c)
.
To do so, we look for the smallest index in
\mathfrak{R}(c)
that is greater than or equal to
i
.
If this index is at most
n-m+i
, then it constitutes a valid match; otherwise, no match exists.

iii)

Two Non-Wildcard Symbols.

Suppose
\textsf{P}_{i}=c
,
\textsf{P}_{j}=c^{\prime}
, and all other positions in
P
are wildcards.
A match of
P
in
T
corresponds to positions
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}
in
T
such that:

•

\textsf{T}_{i^{\prime}}=c
,
\textsf{T}_{j^{\prime}}=c^{\prime}
,

•

j-i-1\;=\;j^{\prime}-i^{\prime}-1
(equal gap),

•

i\leq i^{\prime}
and
j^{\prime}\leq n-m+j
.

Let
d\coloneqq j-i-1
be the

(number of symbols strictly between the two concrete positions), so the constraint is
j^{\prime}=i^{\prime}+d+1
.
Equivalently, the query is:

\text{Does there exist }i^{\prime}\in[l,r]\text{ such that }\textsf{T}_{i^{\prime}}=c\text{ and }\textsf{T}_{i^{\prime}+d+1}=c^{\prime}\,?

This is exactly a range-pair instance as in Problem
6.1
.

Reduction to the
Range-Pair Problem
structure.

We build the input
\mathcal{T}
and alphabet
\Lambda
for the Range-Pair problem as follows.
Let
\tau>0
be the frequency threshold used to classify

vs.

symbols in the text.
We maintain a mapping
\phi:\Sigma\to\Lambda
with:

\displaystyle\Lambda

\displaystyle\;=\;\{0,1,2,\dots,M\},\qquad M\coloneqq\big\lceil 2n/\tau\big\rceil,

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }x\text{ is \emph{rare} (has}\;|\mathfrak{R}(x)|\leq\tau\text{)},\\[2.0pt]
z\in\{1,\dots,M\},&\text{if }x\text{ is \emph{frequent}},\end{cases}

|\Lambda|=\lambda\leq M{+}1=\mathcal{O}(n/\tau)
.
We then define the Range-Pair text by

\mathcal{T}_{t}\;\coloneqq\;\phi(\textsf{T}_{t})\qquad\text{for all }t=1,\dots,n.

Query translation.

For a two-symbol pattern
(c,c^{\prime})
with gap

[l,r]

Mapping invariants and updates.

We maintain two simple invariants:

(i)

Every

\phi(x)\in\{1,\dots,M\}
.

(ii)

Every

\phi(x)=0
.

On a text update
\textsf{T}_{\mathrm{pos}}\leftarrow x
, we update
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{pos}}\leftarrow\phi(x)
and maintain the invariants:

•

Promotion.
If a rare symbol
x

\tau

\{1,\dots,M\}
(freeing one if needed by demoting a symbol below) and set
\phi(x)
to that code. This costs
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
work to retag occurrences, but such promotions happen only after
\Omega(\tau)
increments, so the amortized cost is
\mathcal{O}(1)
per update per symbol.

•

Demotion.
If a frequent symbol
y

\leq\tau/2

\phi(y)\leftarrow 0

\mathcal{T}
to

\mathcal{O}(\tau)

\Omega(\tau)

\mathcal{O}(1)
amortized per symbol.

•

Otherwise, simply write
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{pos}}\leftarrow\phi(x)
(no remapping).

With the slack
M=\lceil 2n/\tau\rceil

\leq\tau/2

\lambda=\mathcal{O}(n/\tau)

By Theorem

, operating on
\mathcal{T}\in\Lambda^{n}

•

•

\mathcal{O}\!\big(\tfrac{n\log\beta\cdot\lambda^{2}}{\mu}+\beta\big)

In the rare symbol case (Case 1), queries take
\mathcal{O}(\tau)

\beta=n^{\frac{4}{5}},\quad\mu=n^{\frac{3}{5}},\quad\tau=n^{\frac{4}{5}}.

With this choice:

•

The amortized update time becomes:

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n\log n\cdot(\frac{n}{\tau}+1)^{2}}{\mu}\right)=\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{2}{5}}(n^{\frac{1}{5}}+1)^{2}\log n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{4}{5}}\log n).

•

The query time is:

\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n}{\beta}\cdot\mu+\beta+\tau\right)=\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{4}{5}}).

•

The preprocessing time is:

\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\frac{n}{\beta}\right)^{2}\cdot\beta\log\beta\cdot(\frac{n}{\tau}+1)^{2}\;+\;n\beta\right)=\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{6}{5}}(n^{\frac{1}{5}}+1)^{2}\log n\;+\;n\cdot n^{\frac{4}{5}})=\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{9}{5}}).

\Box

Remark 6.4

(Insertions and deletions in the pattern)
.

The algorithm also extends to handle insertions and deletions in the pattern
P
.
Such operations only change the length of
P
, and therefore affect only the range
[l,r]
over which we search for answers.
The underlying data structures and their update/query times remain unchanged.

Remark 6.5

(Wildcards in the text)
.

Our approach also handles wildcard symbols in the text
T
.
This is achieved by mapping the wildcard ‘
?
‘ to a dedicated symbol in the Range-Pair Problem alphabet (e.g., code
1
), and ensuring that no other symbol maps to this code.
Unlike ordinary symbols, wildcards in the text are treated specially: they are exempt from the frequent/rare classification.
During a query
\texttt{PairQuery}(l,r,a,b,d)
, we additionally evaluate the three variants where either or both endpoints are mapped wildcards, namely

\texttt{PairQuery}(l,r,\phi(\texttt{?}),\phi(b),d)
,

\texttt{PairQuery}(l,r,\phi(a),\phi(\texttt{?}),d)
,
and

\texttt{PairQuery}(l,r,\phi(\texttt{?}),\phi(\texttt{?}),d)
.
This allows wildcard positions in the text to be treated consistently as matching any symbol.

Rare endpoints.

If, in any of the four queries above (the base query plus the three wildcard variants), an endpoint symbol is a
rare
symbol, we replace the corresponding
\texttt{PairQuery}(
,.,.,.,.). call by a direct scan over the occurrences of that rare symbol in the valid range.
Each such scan costs
\mathcal{O}(\tau)
since
|\mathfrak{R}(a)|\leq\tau
for rare
a
.

Remark 6.6

(Counting matches)
.

In contrast to our other settings, for the at-most-two non-wildcard case we can compute not only existence but also the exact number of matches within the same asymptotic bounds.
If the pattern contains a rare symbol, we already enumerate all of its occurrences in
T
, so counting adds no extra cost.
If the pattern has no non-wildcard symbols, every position is a match.
If it has exactly one non-wildcard symbol, the answer is the number of occurrences of that symbol in the relevant range, which can be maintained in
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
time using a Fenwick tree
[
Fenwick, 1994
]
.
Finally, if it has two non-wildcard symbols, the query reduces to the Range-Pair problem, for which we have already shown how to compute the exact number of matching pairs.
Thus, counting queries can be answered within the same time complexity as decision queries.

6.2
Fixed Wildcard Positions in Pattern

In this subsection we study the dynamic pattern matching problem with fixed wildcards: all
k
wildcard symbols appear only in the pattern
P
, their index set
W=\{i\in[m]:\textsf{P}_{i}=\texttt{?}\}
is known in advance and immutable, and the text
T
contains no wildcards. Both
P
and
T
evolve only via substitution updates, and insertions and deletions are not allowed; therefore updates to
P
are confined to the solid coordinates
[m]\setminus W
, while updates to
T
may change any
\textsf{T}_{i}\in\Sigma
. We parameterize by the number of non-wildcard positions
\omega=|[m]\setminus W|
and target the sparse regime
\omega\ll m
. To handle this setting, we adopt a masked polynomial rolling hash that ignores the fixed wildcard indices: equivalently, we view
P
as a concatenation of solid blocks separated by wildcards and compute the hash only over these blocks, enabling comparison against substrings of
T
under substitutions while treating wildcard coordinates as don’t-cares; this leads to the following definition.

Definition 6.7
.

Let
S
be a string of length
\ell
, and let
P
be a pattern with
k
wildcard positions

w_{1}<w_{2}<\dots<w_{k}
, where each
w_{i}
is the index of the
i
-th wildcard in
P
. We define

H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(S\right)=H_{b,p}\left(\,S_{1:w_{1}-1}\cdot S_{w_{1}+1:w_{2}-1}\cdot\dots\cdot S_{w_{k}+1:\ell}\,\right),

where
H_{b,p}\left(\cdot\right)
is the polynomial rolling hash from Definition
4.1
.

The value
H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(S\right)
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(k)
time by concatenating the precomputed hash values of the

k+1
non-wildcard intervals, using Lemma
4.6
. Moreover, after each update in
S
,
the value of
H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(S\right)
can be updated in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time.
Let us now define an auxiliary vector
A
, where

A_{i}=H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(\textsf{T}_{i:i+m-1}\right)\qquad\text{for }1\leq i\leq n-m+1,

so that
A_{i}
stores the hash of the length-
m
substring of
T
starting at position
i
, with
the fixed wildcards of
P
ignored.

Lemma 6.8
.

If the pattern
P
decomposes into
l
non-empty intervals separated by its wildcard positions, then the
vector
A
can be constructed in
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot l)
time.

Proof.
The vector
A
has
n-m+1
entries, where each entry is of the form

A_{i}=H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(\textsf{T}_{i:i+m-1}\right)
. Since
P
decomposes into
l
non-empty intervals, the substring

\textsf{T}_{i:i+m-1}
is split into at most
l
corresponding segments. The hash of each segment can be computed
in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time using the polynomial rolling hash from Definition
4.1
. Concatenating
these
l
segment hashes into
H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(\textsf{T}_{i:i+m-1}\right)
requires
\mathcal{O}(l)
time by
Lemma
4.6
. Therefore, computing one entry of
A
costs
\mathcal{O}(l)
time, and
computing all
\mathcal{O}(n)
entries requires
\mathcal{O}(nl)
time in total.

\Box

Together, Definition
6.7
and Lemma
6.8
provide the key ingredients for
efficient preprocessing in the sparse-pattern setting. The modified hash
H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(S\right)
ensures that
wildcards are ignored, while the vector
A
stores precomputed values for all substrings of
T

that need to be compared against
P
. The lemma guarantees that this preprocessing can be done in

\mathcal{O}(n\cdot l)
time, where
l
is the number of non-empty intervals in
P
.
Algorithm
12
then presents the full method for handling updates.
Intuitively, when the pattern has only a few fixed symbols and the rest are wildcards, we can ignore
wildcard positions in all computations. Let
C=\{c_{1}<\cdots<c_{\omega}\}
be the indices of the
non-wildcard positions in

. For every window
\textsf{T}_{i:i+m-1}
of the text, we

.
We also maintain the single value
H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(\textsf{P}\right)
. A match exists iff

H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(\textsf{P}\right)
appears among
\{A_{1},\dots,A_{n-m+1}\}
; this can be checked efficiently with a membership query, e.g., in
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
time using a balanced BST.

If the pattern changes at a non-wildcard position, we simply recompute
H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(\textsf{P}\right)
and test

Algorithm 12

Theorem 6.9
.

Algorithm
12
maintains the dynamic pattern matching problem with fixed wildcards
for a pattern
P
of length
m
containing at most
\omega
non-wildcard positions.
It requires preprocessing time
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot\omega)
and supports updates to either
P
or
T

in
\mathcal{O}(\omega+\log n)
time per update.
The algorithm always detects a match correctly when one exists.
When no match exists, it reports “no match” with high probability.

Proof.
We first construct the vector
A
, where each entry
A_{i}
stores the hash of the substring

\textsf{T}_{i:i+m-1}
restricted to the non-wildcard positions of
P
.
Since
P
contains at most
\omega
non-wildcards, all values of
A
can be computed in

\mathcal{O}(n\cdot\omega)
time (Lemma
6.8
).
The initial hash value
H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(\textsf{P}\right)
is computed in
\mathcal{O}(\omega)
time.

Update on
P
.

If a symbol of
P
is modified, only its contribution to
H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(\textsf{P}\right)
changes.
By the rolling-hash property (Definition
6.7
), this modification can be updated in constant time.

Update on
T
.

Suppose that symbol
\textsf{T}_{j}
of the text is modified.
Each non-wildcard position
q
of
P
aligns with
\textsf{T}_{j}
in exactly one substring
\textsf{T}_{i:i+m-1}
, namely where

i=j-q+1
.
Consequently, at most
\omega
entries of
A
are affected.
Each affected entry can be recomputed in constant time, so the update to
T
requires
\mathcal{O}(\omega)
time.

Checking for matches.

To test whether a match exists, the algorithm checks if

H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(\textsf{P}\right)\in\{A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{n-m+1}\}.

If equality holds for some
i
, then
P
and
\textsf{T}_{i:i+m-1}
coincide on all non-wildcard positions, which by definition means that
P
matches
T
at position
i
.
This can be checked with a membership query, i.e., verifying whether

H^{\prime}_{b,p}\left(\textsf{P}\right)\in\{A_{1},\dots,A_{n-m+1}\}
,
which can be performed in
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
time using a balanced BST.

\Box

The general statement above can be specialized to different regimes of sparsity.

.

If the number of non-wildcard positions satisfies
\omega\leq n^{1-\delta}
for some constant

,
then Algorithm

runs in preprocessing time
\mathcal{O}(n^{2-\delta})
and supports updates in
\mathcal{O}(n^{1-\delta})
time.

Moreover, since the number of non-wildcard positions can never exceed the pattern length

, we can directly derive the following bound.

Corollary 6.11
.

Since
\omega\leq m
, the algorithm also admits the trivial upper bound obtained by setting
\omega=m
.
In this case, Algorithm
12
requires preprocessing time
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot m)
and update time
\mathcal{O}(m+\log n)
.

7
Hardness of Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards

In this section, we establish a conditional lower bound for Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards through a reduction from the
Orthogonal Vectors
problem.
Our goal is to show that any significant improvement in the query time for Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards would imply a breakthrough for
Orthogonal Vectors
,
which is widely believed to be unlikely under the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (
\mathsf{SETH}
).

Problem 7.1

(The Orthogonal Vectors Problem)
.

Given a set
A
of
n
vectors from
\{0,1\}^{d}
, the
Orthogonal Vectors
problem asks whether there exists a pair of distinct vectors

U,V\in A
such that their inner product is zero:

\sum_{h=1}^{d}U_{h}\cdot V_{h}=0.

That is, no coordinate position contains a
1
in both
U
and
V
.

Williams
[
Williams, 2005
]
establishes the following connection between
Orthogonal Vectors
and
\mathsf{SETH}
.

Theorem 7.2

(
[
Williams, 2005
]
)
.

If there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that, for all constants
c
, the
Orthogonal Vectors
problem on a set of
n
vectors of
dimension
d=c\log n
can be solved in

2^{o(d)}\cdot n^{2-\varepsilon}
time,
then
\mathsf{SETH}
is false.

We now show a reduction of
Orthogonal Vectors
to Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards.
This reduction forms the backbone of our lower bound.

Lemma 7.3
.

An instance of
Orthogonal Vectors
with a set
A
of
n
vectors in dimension
d
can be reduced to an instance of Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards with
pattern length
m=d+2
and text length
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot d)
.
If Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards can be solved with preprocess time
h(n)
and query time
g(n)
, then
Orthogonal Vectors
can be solved in

\mathcal{O}(h(d\cdot n)+d\cdot n\cdot g(d\cdot n))
time.

Proof.
Let
A=\{V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{n}\}
, where each
V_{i}
is a
d
-dimensional binary vector.
For each vector
V_{i}
, construct a modified vector
V^{\prime}_{i}
by replacing every
1
in
V_{i}
with
0
, and every
0
in
V_{i}
with a wildcard symbol. Define the pattern and text as

\textsf{P}=\#V^{\prime}_{1}\#,\quad\textsf{T}=\#V_{1}\#V_{2}\#\cdots\#V_{n}\#,

where
\#
is a delimiter not in
\{0,1,\texttt{?}\}
.
By construction,
P
has length
m=d+2
and
T
has length
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot d)
.
We claim that
P
matches a substring of
T
if and only if the
Orthogonal Vectors
instance contains an orthogonal pair.
Indeed, suppose
\textsf{P}=\#V^{\prime}_{i}\#
matches
\#V_{j}\#
in
T
.
Then, for each coordinate
k
:

•

If
V_{i,k}=1
, then
V^{\prime}_{i,k}=0
. Since
0
can only match
0
, we must have
V_{j,k}=0
.

•

If
V_{i,k}=0
, then
V^{\prime}_{i,k}=\texttt{?}
, which matches both
0
and
1
.

Thus, there is no coordinate where both
V_{i,k}=1
and
V_{j,k}=1
, which means
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
are orthogonal.
The converse follows by reversing this reasoning.

To check all pairs, we iteratively transform the pattern into each
\#V^{\prime}_{i}\#
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Each transformation requires at most
d
symbol changes, followed by a query to check if the pattern matches a substring of
T
.
Over all
n
vectors, this yields
\mathcal{O}(d\cdot n)
updates and queries.
Each query costs
g(d\cdot n)
, so the total runtime is

\mathcal{O}(d\cdot n\cdot g(d\cdot n)).

\Box

The reduction allows us to transfer lower bounds from
Orthogonal Vectors
to Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards.

Theorem 7.4
.

Assuming
\mathsf{SETH}
holds, the fully dynamic wildcard pattern matching problem with

k=\Omega(\log n)
wildcards cannot be solved with preprocessing time

\mathcal{O}(n^{2-\delta})
and query time
\mathcal{O}(n^{1-\varepsilon})

for any constants
\delta,\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.
Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards admits an algorithm with
query time
g(n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{1-\varepsilon})
and preprocessing time

h(n)=\mathcal{O}(n^{2-\delta})
, for some constants
\varepsilon,\delta>0
.

By Lemma
7.3
, an instance of the
Orthogonal Vectors
problem with
n
vectors in dimension
d
can be reduced to an instance of Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards of text length
\mathcal{O}(d\cdot n)
and pattern length
d+2
.
The reduction implies that
Orthogonal Vectors
can then be solved in

\mathcal{O}\!\left(h(d\cdot n)+d\cdot n\cdot g(d\cdot n)\right).

Substituting the assumed bounds for
h(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
, we obtain

\mathcal{O}\!\left((d\cdot n)^{2-\delta}+d\cdot n\cdot(d\cdot n)^{1-\varepsilon}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(d^{2-\delta}\cdot n^{2-\delta}+d^{2-\varepsilon}\cdot n^{2-\varepsilon}\right).

Now set
d=c\log n
for a fixed constant
c>0
.
Since
d=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, both
d^{2-\delta}
and
d^{2-\varepsilon}
grow subexponentially in
d
, i.e.,

d^{2-\delta}=2^{o(d)}\quad\text{and}\quad d^{2-\varepsilon}=2^{o(d)}.

Thus the total running time becomes

\mathcal{O}\!\left(2^{o(d)}\cdot n^{2-\delta}+2^{o(d)}\cdot n^{2-\varepsilon}\right)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(2^{o(d)}\cdot n^{2-\min\{\delta,\varepsilon\}}\right).

This runtime contradicts Theorem
7.2
, which asserts that such an improvement for
Orthogonal Vectors
would refute
\mathsf{SETH}
.
Therefore, under
\mathsf{SETH}
, no such algorithm for Dynamic Pattern Matching with Wildcards can exist.

\Box
