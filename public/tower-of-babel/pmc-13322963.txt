Artificial intelligence-enhancement of flow cytometry data accelerates the identification of measurable residual chronic lymphocytic leukemia
Authors: April Chiu, Jansen N Seheult, Min Shi, Dragan Jevremovic, Clarissa E Jordan, Mathew J Weybright, Michael M Timm, Gregory E Otteson, Horatiu Olteanu, Sameer A Parikh, Pedro Horna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlow cytometry (FC) is essential for detecting measurable residual disease (MRD) in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL), but its use is limited by the expertise and time required for manual analysis. We developed an artificial intelligence (AI) pipeline, Clustering/Classification of All Events, Dimensionality reduction, Downsampling, and Aberrancy Scaling (CCADDAS), to automatically enhance raw FC files, streamlining CLL MRD detection using a single-tube 10-color panel. FC files from 166 MRD-positive and 61 MRD-negative cases were processed in a cloud environment. Automated steps included error correction (FlowCut), clustering (PARC), dimensionality reduction (UMAP), anomaly detection against negative controls, and cluster-informed downsampling that preserved rare MRD events. A deep neural network trained on expert-defined normal subsets enabled automated gating. AI-enhanced files were analyzed in standard FC software, yielding results highly concordant with conventional expert review (R² = 0.98). Downsampling reduced cellularity by 85% and file size by 78%, while retaining low-level MRD events. An AI-generated aberrancy scale distinguished CLL MRD from background B cells with excellent performance (AUC = 0.98). Manual analysis time decreased from 9.0 to 0.9 min per case (90% reduction). CCADDAS provides a largely unsupervised, software-agnostic method that accelerates and simplifies CLL MRD detection without compromising test performance compared to conventional analysis, enabling broader adoption of FC-based MRD testing.Subject terms: B-cell lymphoma, Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is a lymphoid neoplasm characterized by accumulation of clonal mature B-cells in the peripheral blood, bone marrow, lymph nodes, and/or spleen [1]. CLL is currently the most common leukemia in the Western Hemisphere, with approximately 24,000 new cases diagnosed in the United States in 2025 [2]. Although patients with CLL typically present with indolent disease and can be managed by observation alone, most eventually require therapeutic intervention due to progressive or symptomatic disease. CLL-directed therapeutic options have dramatically expanded over the last decade and continue to do so, which consist of chemoimmunotherapy (including CD20 targeting monoclonal antibodies such as rituximab and obinutuzumab), agents targeting B-cell receptor signaling (e.g., ibrutinib), B-cell lymphoma 2 inhibitors (BCL2i, such as venetoclax), and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CART) therapy [3]. While many patients do achieve complete remission (CR) according to the 2018 International Workshop on Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (iwCLL) criteria [4], residual disease may still be detectable, termed measurable (or minimal) residual disease (MRD), by using highly sensitive assays. MRD detection in patients with CLL following therapy is of great significance as this has been established as an independent prognostic factor for progression free survival and overall survival [5–7]. A negative MRD status in CLL patients is increasingly used as a surrogate endpoint to establish efficacy and consideration for cessation of therapy [8–12].

CLL MRD can be assessed by a variety of methodologies including flow cytometry (FC), allele-specific oligonucleotide quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ASO-qPCR), next generation sequencing (NGS), droplet digital PCR (ddPCR), and cell-free DNA analysis [13]. Of these, FC is the most assessable, fastest, and likely most cost effective; and therefore most widely used. Furthermore, FC does not require prior knowledge of disease-specific immunoglobulin sequences or immunophenotypic abnormalities at diagnosis. However, while modern FC platforms are capable of achieving the recommended assay sensitivity of at least 0.01% (10−4), which is recommended by both 2018 iwCLL [4] and 2026 NCCN guidelines [14], molecular methods have the advantage of detecting even lower MRD levels (up to 10−6) which may have prognostic significance in some settings. In addition, conventional FC MRD testing is typically performed by manual gating and analysis of cell populations on two-dimensional dot plots, and heavily relies upon technical expertise of clinical laboratory staff. Contributing to this highly manual and time-consuming process is the large amount of data analyzed, requiring robust computational processing power and advanced software analytical capabilities compared to non-MRD-based FC analysis. Therefore, the availability of MRD testing by FC is generally confined to large academic centers and reference laboratories.

To overcome these limitations, we implemented a cloud-based computational artificial intelligence (AI) pipeline recently developed by our group [15], which simplifies and accelerates the identification and quantification of MRD without compromising test performance compared to conventional analysis. This pipeline, CCADDAS (Clustering and Classification of All Events, Dimensionality reduction, Downsampling, and Aberrancy Scaling), transforms raw data files produced by the FC analyzer into markedly smaller, AI-annotated, and software-agnostic files, and provides a measure of aberrancy in comparison to negative controls. We applied this pipeline on a large cohort of FC data files produced from our 10-color CLL MRD clinical assay, and compared the pipeline’s performance against that of conventional FC CLL MRD analysis.

Methods

Patient and sample selection

For training of the pipeline, 29 negative control samples (15 bone marrow aspirates and 14 peripheral bloods) were selected; including 24 CLL MRD negative cases analyzed at Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN, between January 2021 and July 2023; and 5 normal marrow specimens procured from hip replacements and analyzed between January and May 2021. The selection of controls was based on adequate representation of benign normal subsets as assessed by expert analysts.

A separate research cohort included 227 specimens (74 bone marrow aspirate and 153 peripheral blood) received for CLL MRD flow cytometric analysis at Mayo Clinic in Rochester, MN, between April 2023 and February 2024; including 166 specimens from 122 patients reported as MRD-positive (0.002% to < 5%), and 61 specimens from 45 patients reported as MRD-negative by conventional FC MRD analysis. The average patient age was 62 years old (range: 42–86 years old), and the male to female ratio was 2.3. Of all positive samples in this cohort, 18 (11%) showed an atypical CLL immunophenotype, including lack of CD5 (n = 15), bright CD20 (n = 3) and/or bright CD22 expression (n = 2).

A subsequent validation cohort consisted of 192 samples (75 bone marrow aspirate and 117 peripheral blood) received for CLL MRD flow cytometric analysis at Mayo Clinic in Rochester, MN between January and June 2025; including 91 specimens from 84 patients reported as MRD-positive (0.002% to <5%), and 101 specimens from 84 patients reported as MRD-negative by conventional FC MRD analysis. In this validation cohort, the average patient age was 68 years old (range: 35 to 87 years old), and the male to female ratio was 2.7.

Cohort sizes were determined by the number of consecutive specimens meeting inclusion criteria during the study period and were considered sufficient to evaluate algorithm performance and agreement with conventional flow cytometric MRD analysis. The sample sizes are comparable to those used in prior studies evaluating automated or machine-learning approaches for flow cytometry MRD analysis. This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (with exemption for informed consent).

Flow cytometry

A single-tube, 10-color CLL MRD panel was developed and validated to a lower limit of detection of 0.002% (2 × 10−5). The panel consisted of a cocktail of the following fluorescently labeled antibodies: CD5-BV480, CD19-PE-Cy7, CD20-APC-H7, CD22-APC, CD38-APC-R700, CD43-BV605, CD45-PerCP-Cy5.5, CD200-BV421, kappa-FITC, and lambda-PE (Supplementary Table 1). Up to 1.5 million total events were acquired on 3 FACS Lyric flow cytometers (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA) which were similarly utilized for controls and sample specimens (Supplementary Table 2).

CCADDAS artificial intelligence pipeline

Our previously described CCADDAS pipeline deployed on Google Vertex AI [15] was trained on our CLL MRD panel using 29 negative controls specimens, each manually annotated with 13 defined cell types (B-cells, hematogones, blasts, basophils/dendritic cells, plasma cells, T-cells, NK cells, monocytes, granulocytes, erythroblasts, debris, doublets/aggregates, and others) (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Information 1). Ungated sample data files produced by the analyzer were processed through our pipeline using publicly available machine learning algorithms for removal of acquisition errors (FlowCut) [16], dimensionality reduction [Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)] [17], clustering [phenotyping by accelerated refined community-partitioning (PARC)] [18], and single event classification (Tensorflow and Keras) [19] (Fig. 1B).

As previously described [15], downsampling was achieved by first clustering all events using default PARC settings, followed by per-cluster random downsampling to a maximum of 5000 events per cluster (clusters smaller than 5000 events were not downsampled). An “upsampling factor” was calculated as the ratio of the number of original to downsampled events per cluster. An aberrancy scale parameter was calculated by first merging sample and control events, overclustering with PARC (high resolution settings), and scaling the fraction of sample events per high resolution cluster to maximize visualization and separation of high values (clusters unique to the sample) (Supplementary Appendix). UMAP coordinates were calculated using a relatively high minimum distance (low resolution) to facilitate visualization of small clusters; and a 2-dimensional map of PARC clusters (cluster UMAP) was produced based on repulsed UMAP coordinates of clusters centroids. A deep neural network (DNN) Class parameter captured the results of a DNN event classifier trained with control annotations (Supplementary Information 1) and 13 features (3 light scatter values and 10 fluorescent intensities). The resulting pipeline exports were formatted as standard flow cytometry (.fcs) files containing downsampled raw fluorescence data, intact metadata, original spillover matrix, and seven AI parameters (Fig. 1C).

Manual analysis of FC files

Analysis of AI-enhanced FC files was performed on Kaluza, version 2.3 (Beckman Coulter). Templated editable gates were used for exclusion of doublets and debris, and to gate on mononuclear cells based on light scatter properties. “Other/debris” class events with aberrancy scale values lower than 500 (not a defined subset and highly represented in negative controls) were excluded for cleaner visualization. Fixed gates were pre-defined to automatically color-code normal subsets based on “DNN Class” values, and events classified as B-lineage cells were displayed on two-dimensional dot plots for visual identification of MRD. A rectangular colored gate on an “aberrancy scale” vs. CD19 dot plot was used to explore potential MRD subsets with aberrancy scale values above 500. Analysis of original FC files was performed on Infinicyt v 2.0 (BD Biosciences), using traditional manual gating on multiple 2-dimensional dot plots, based on previously described strategies adapted to our panel [20, 21]. This analysis was performed by various trained frontline staff as part of our clinical laboratory workflow. All AI-enhanced files from the research cohort were analyzed by one author (AC), while blinded of the prior reported results; with a second blinded review by another author (PH). All AI-enhanced pipeline exports from the validation cohort were analyzed by a third author (MW). For timing studies, 2 frontline technologists with experience in traditional analysis were trained on AI-assisted analysis by one of the authors (PH), including 2 h of hands-on training, individual analysis of at least 100 cases from the validation cohort, and feedback by e-mail from the authors. These two technologists then individually analyzed 20 randomly selected cases from the research cohort, using both AI-enhanced files and conventional analysis of original files. Two-wash periods were applied, where the technologists were blinded of the prior results of AI-enhanced files, as well as prior reported results.

Statistical analysis

Mean ranks of “aberrancy scale” values between MRD-positive and MRD-negative cases, and manual analysis times between AI-enhanced files and conventional analysis of original files were compared using the Mann–Whitney non-parametric test. Events per file and file size comparisons were performed using the paired t test. Percentages of retained events after downsampling were studied using the unpaired t test. The performance of fluorescent and AI parameters to identify MRD was studied using receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curves based on 2000 randomly selected events per group (MRD events from positive cases, and normal B-cells from negative cases). The performance of the AI cell classifier was studied using F1 scores. All statistical analyses and non-FC data plots were performed using Graph-Pad Prism, version 10.3.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Confidence intervals (CI) for % positive and % negative agreements were calculated as “exact” Clopper-Pearson values. For illustration purposes only (not part of the analysis workflow), total or gated events from analyzed pipeline exports were merged, downsampled to a maximum of 25,000 events per DNN class, and studied on conventional UMAP embeddings using FCS Express, version 7.22.0031 (De Novo Software, Pasadena, CA).

Results

Cluster-informed downsampling preserves MRD events

To simplify FC analysis of CLL MRD, we developed a data processing pipeline that removes redundant events, while preserving immunophenotypically distinct minute subsets including MRD populations. When applied to FC data from 227 samples (research cohort), our cluster-informed downsampling strategy reduced the average number of events per case from 1.1 million to 165,010 cells (85% cellularity reduction, p < 0.0001), which is similar to the number of events studied in routine leukemia/lymphoma immunophenotyping (100,000–200,000 events) [22]. The average file size was also markedly reduced from 62.1 to 15.2 MB (78% data reduction, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2A), eliminating the need for specialized software or advanced computational processing power for manual analysis of MRD data.

Upon analysis of downsampled pipeline exports from our research cohort, CLL MRD was detected in all 145 positive samples above recommended assay sensitivity (MRD ≥ 0.01%; 100% positive agreement with conventional analysis, CI: 94–100%), and in 17 of 21 samples below this threshold (MRD < 0.01%; 81% positive agreement with conventional analysis, CI: 58–95%). All 4 samples with MRD < 0.01% by conventional analysis, but MRD negative by analysis of downsampled exports were typical CLL cases (Supplementary Table 3). No MRD subsets were identified on downsampled analysis of exports from 61 negative samples (MRD = 0%; 100% negative agreement with conventional analysis, CI: 94–100%).

Low-level ( ≤ 0.1%) MRD events were always found on single small clusters ( < 5000 events) that were not subjected to downsampling per our pipeline’s design, except for a single case with MRD at limit of detection (0.002%; original cluster size = 12,071) (Supplementary Fig. 4). As our AI-assisted gating strategy is not necessarily restricted to cluster assignments, minor operator-dependent variability of gated MRD events was noted when analyzing cases with very low MRD levels ( < 0.01%). As expected from the pipeline’s design, large MRD populations corresponding to large clusters (>5000 events) were downsampled proportionally to their size, but always yielding easily detectable subsets (Fig. 2B). Due to preferential downsampling of non-MRD populations, the actual percentage of low-level MRD events (<0.5%) detected on downsampled pipeline exports (without “upsampling” correction) was on average 5.4 times higher than MRD percentages on the original files, facilitating visual identification of these relatively rare subsets (Fig. 2C). Of note, the extent of downsampling for each cluster was recorded as an “upsampling factor” parameter by the AI pipeline, allowing for precise quantitation of any gated subset, including MRD (see below).

AI automatically gates normal hematopoietic subsets

To eliminate the time-consuming expert gating of benign subsets including normal B-cells for CLL MRD analysis, we trained a DNN cell classifier using 29 negative controls gated by expert analysts to completely automate the gating process. This classifier demonstrated robust performance across all cell populations (macro-average F1 score = 0.93) (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 1), and allowed for automated display of B-cell maturation patterns on sample specimens, using templated gates based on DNN class values (Fig. 3B). While the interoperator consistency for manual gating of myeloblasts, NK cells and plasma cells was suboptimal with the antigens utilized on this panel (Supplementary Table 4); automated gating of these background subsets was useful for the visual assessment of antigen expression on B lymphoid cells.

A quantitative measure of aberrancy simplifies MRD detection

The immunophenotype of CLL MRD is variable and significantly overlaps with normal B-cell maturation on conventional 2-dimensional plots. However, a clear divergence of MRD events from all other hematopoietic subsets can be demonstrated on a non-linear dimensionality reduction (UMAP) plot when considering all antigens simultaneously (Fig. 3C). To simplify the identification of B-cell subsets that diverge from normal based on all antigens analyzed, our pipeline included a quantitative cluster-based anomaly estimate relative to negative controls (“aberrancy scale”)(Supplementary Appendix). The vast majority (94.5%) of MRD subsets from positive cases (research cohort) were rapidly identified on an aberrancy scale vs. CD19 plot by exploring a limited number of mononuclear subsets with aberrancy scale values above 500 (>95% sample representation compared to controls) (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 2A-B). Of these, 21 cases (14%) benefited from a more accurate delineation of the neoplastic population using a single (or rarely two) cluster-UMAP gates (Fig. 4D). Only 9 positive cases (6%) showed MRD subsets with aberrancy scale values below 500, 8 of which were positive below the recommended assay sensitivity of 0.01%. These minute MRD subsets remained easily identifiable on B-cell maturation dot-plots generated by automated identification of benign subsets (Supplementary Fig. 2C).

“Aberrancy scale” values of MRD subsets from the research cohort were significantly higher (mean ± 2 SD: 969 ± 144) compared to those of background B-lymphoid cells (mean ± 2 SD: 395 ± 401) (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 5A). On histogram plots, fluorescence intensities for all antigens evaluated showed extensive overlap between MRD and non-MRD B lymphoid events (Fig. 5B). In contrast, aberrancy scale values adequately discriminated most MRD events from benign B lymphoid cells. As expected, the aberrancy scale parameter showed superior performance in distinguishing MRD from benign B-lymphoid (AUC: 0.98) or mononuclear (AUC: 0.99) cells, compared to all single antigen fluorescence values from which it was calculated (Fig. 5C).

Optimal performance of AI-assisted MRD analysis

True percentages of MRD events on the research cohort were accurately estimated using the “upsampling factor” parameter, showing excellent quantitative correlation with values reported by conventional analysis of the original files (linear regression slope = 0.9832, intercept = 0.003%, R squared = 0.98, P < 0.0001, mean bias = −0.016%) (Fig. 6A). This performance was confirmed on a separate validation cohort (192 additional samples), showing similar strong quantitative correlation between conventional and AI-assisted analysis (linear regression slope = 0.9861, intercept = 0.003%, R squared = 0.9975, P < 0.0001, mean bias = +0.014%) (Fig. 6B). When considering both the research and validation cohorts together (419 samples), CLL MRD was detected in all 225 positive samples above recommended assay sensitivity (MRD ≥ 0.01%; 100% positive agreement with conventional analysis, CI: 98–100%), and in 27 of 32 samples below this threshold (MRD < 0.01%; 84% positive agreement with conventional analysis, CI: 67–95%). No MRD subsets were identified on downsampled analysis of exports from a total of 162 negative samples (MRD = 0%; 100% negative agreement with conventional analysis, CI: 98–100%) (Fig. 6C).

When studying 20 random samples with equal representation of negative, low-positive (0.01–0.1%), mid-positive (0.1–1%) and high positive (1–5% MRD) cases, the average manual analysis time of AI-enhanced pipeline exports as independently performed by two frontline laboratory technologists was less than one minute per case (mean = 0.87, SD = 0.20; median = 0.87, interquartile range: 0.73–1.02 min). In contrast, the average manual analysis time of original files by the same two technologists (after a 2 week wash period) was approximately 9.0 min (mean = 8.97, SD = 2.90; median = 8.93, interquartile range = 7.24–10.29 min) (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

In this study we utilized a largely unsupervised AI pipeline (Clustering and Classification of All Events, Dimensionality reduction, Downsampling, and Aberrancy Scaling/CCADDAS) [15] that transforms raw CLL-MRD FC data into markedly compacted (78% data reduction), AI-annotated, and software-agnostic FC files. This AI pipeline utilizes a “deviation from normal” strategy by outputting a single calculated parameter (“aberrancy scale”), accelerating the identification of MRD subsets independent of immunophenotypic variability that may be encountered in CLL (e.g. bright CD20, absent CD5 or CD43). The production of the aberrancy scale and overall pipeline training required only 29 negative control samples, without the need of positive samples or examples of atypical immunophenotypes. Furthermore, CCADDAS outperformed all other single antigens (including CD5, CD20, CD43, and CD200) in distinguishing MRD populations from background benign B-lymphoid cells, simplifying and accelerating detection of CLL-MRD by reducing analyzed events by 85% and manual analysis time by 90% (from 9.0 to 0.9 min per case). This in turn results in significantly shorter turnaround time and reduction of labor cost. Finally, by efficiently eliminating redundant events to achieve file size reduction, while preserving MRD populations, the CCADDAS pipeline eliminates the need for highly specialized software or advanced computational processors which may be cost inhibitive outside of reference laboratory settings.

Few studies have previously evaluated the utility of machine learning in the flow cytometric assessment of CLL MRD. Dimensionality reduction plots (Barned-Hutt SNE) color-coded to depict antigen expression have been utilized to explore immunophenotypically distinct subsets during CLL MRD analysis [23], although the added value of this approach was not formally evaluated. A trained self-organizing map (FlowSOM) showed good performance in detecting CLL MRD when deployed within a specific analysis software (Kaluza), but this performance depended on the targeted immunophenotypic variant being represented in the training cohort [24]. A proprietary AI pipeline (DeepCyto) was trained with gated flow cytometry files from CLL MRD cases and showed good performance in identifying MRD-positive cases, but resulted in significant quantitative discrepancies and limitations in detecting atypical immunophenotypes [25]. More recently, a proprietary cluster classifier within a commercial analysis software (Infinicyt) showed good performance in detecting CLL MRD, but the proposed manual analysis workflow was complex and efficiency gains were not clearly demonstrated [26]. Our group previously reported substantial analysis time savings using a DNN event classifier trained to detect benign and neoplastic subsets on our CLL MRD panel [19, 27]. However, the DNN by itself showed significant quantitative discrepancies with the ground truth, failed to capture rare atypical CLL immunophenotypes, and required a large training cohort (202 cases) which limits scalability to other panels and platforms.

Multiple flow cytometry panels have been proposed for the detection of CLL MRD based on available fluorochromes, preferred discriminatory antigens, favored analysis strategies, and a desire to simplify processing using less analysis tubes. Initial efforts by the European Research Initiative on CLL (ERIC) introduced a 4-color/5-tube panel which included 13 antigens: CD3, CD5, CD14, CD19, CD20, CD22, CD38, CD43, CD45, CD79b, CD81, kappa and lambda [28]. This was later streamlined into a 6-color/2-tube panel (disposing of CD14, CD45, kappa and lambda) [20], an 8-color/1-tube panel (eliminating CD38), and finally a 6-color/1-tube panel (further disposing of CD3 and CD22) [29]. Some groups have proposed different abridged 6 to 8-color single-tube panels that diverge from the ERIC approach by prioritizing antigens that facilitate gating of lymphoid subsets (CD2, CD3 and/or CD45) [30–32], discriminate CLL from background B-cells (ROR1 and/or CD160) [31–34], or help characterize abnormal B-cells as CLL (CD23) [32]. Others, including the Euroflow Consortium, have instead advocated for the inclusion of more antigens on two 8-color analysis tubes [26, 35]. Our 10-color single tube assay evaluates 7 antigens also assessed in other commonly utilized CLL MRD panels (Supplementary Table 5), with the addition of CD200 to facilitate MRD identification when overexpressed [36] and help raise the possibility of a B-cell lymphoproliferation other than CLL when negative [37]. On limited dilution studies, our flow cytometry panel produced similar results to MRD testing for tumor-specific immunoglobulin (IG) rearrangements down to 0.001% (Supplementary Fig. 3). We also re-introduce kappa and lambda, which was originally deprioritized from the 4-color ERIC panel based on a now outdated evaluation of kappa:lambda ratios within CD19/CD5-double-positive events [20]. In our panel, immature B-cells (hematogones) are easily excluded by their bright expression of CD38, resulting in clearly divergent light chain expression patterns between mature B-cells (polytypic bright kappa and lambda expression) and CLL MRD (light chain-negative or dimly restricted expression) (Fig. 3B, C). In addition, identification of CLL MRD with overt light chain restriction is accelerated by the utilization of UMAP and color coding for kappa and lambda (Supplementary Fig. 2B), similar to what we have previously described in the setting of Sezary cell detection based on T-cell receptor β chain-restriction [38].

Several limitations of our study need to be considered. While we have demonstrated similar encouraging results on other FC panels in our practice [38–40], adaptability of our pipeline to other laboratories and platforms remains to be validated. The current pipeline’s runtime using our experimental build (15–20 min per case) precludes immediate analysis after acquisition, although cloud computational resources are easily scalable and the impact of this short runtime is minimal with no manual pre-processing needed before file upload. Four samples with MRD levels below 0.01% by traditional analysis (2.4% of all positive samples) were not detected by AI-assisted analysis, which is not surprising given the gradual loss of concordance at these low MRD levels when comparing two different methodologies [21, 41, 42]. Finally, while AI-enhancement accelerates the identification of MRD by providing rapid visualization, it does not provide an automated MRD result, improve gating accuracy of MRD events (Supplementary Fig. 5), or eliminate the need for expert identification and interpretation of suspected populations on traditional 2-dimensional dot plots.

In conclusion, our AI-assisted CCADDAS pipeline largely simplifies and accelerates manual analysis of CLL MRD by FC, without compromising test performance compared to conventional analysis. Its small-sized export is compatible with any clinical FC software, computing platform and analysis strategy. In addition, this solution can potentially be adapted to other CLL MRD antibody panels, MRD testing of other hematologic malignancies, or flow cytometer platforms simply by retraining it with a small number of negative controls samples. We are actively pursuing providing CCADDAS as a cloud-based computing pipeline that can be made available to other laboratories to train, validate and deploy clinically for MRD testing by FC.
