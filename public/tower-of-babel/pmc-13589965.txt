Mixed Ovarian Dissecting Gonadoblastoma Coexisting with Dysgerminoma, Trophoblastic Tumor, and Leydig Cell Tumor in a Phenotypically Normal 46, XX Female: A Case Report
Authors: Wenxian Xu, Ketan Chu, Yizhou Huang, Yibing Lan, Jianhong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGonadoblastoma (GB) is a rare gonadal neoplasm consisting of germ cells and immature sex cord stromal cells, defined as an in situ malignant tumor that is accompanied by various aggressive malignant components in some cases. GB predominantly arises in patients with gonadal dysgenesis carrying Y chromosome material, while its occurrence in phenotypically normal females with a 46, XX karyotype is extremely rare.Case PresentationWe report an 18-year-old female who presenting with progressive virilization, including generalized acne and voice deepening, as well as long-standing menstrual irregularities. Laboratory tests revealed markedly elevated serum testosterone and positive human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG); pelvic imaging demonstrated rapid enlargement of an ovarian mass within a short period. Laparoscopic left adnexectomy with fertility-sparing surgery was performed, and final histopathology confirmed dissecting GB mixed three distinct components: dysgerminoma, trophoblastic tumor, and Leydig cell tumor. Peripheral blood karyotyping yielded a normal 46, XX result. Sex hormone levels and regular menstrual cycles were restored rapidly after complete tumor resection. Multidisciplinary oncology consultation recommended adjuvant chemotherapy given the invasive components. However, the patient declined systemic chemotherapy and opted for strict long-term surveillance instead.ConclusionThis case describes a unique mixed dissecting GB in a patient with intact female gonadal development and a normal 46, XX karyotype, combined with a literature review of comparable rare cases. The coexistence of spontaneous virilization, tumor-secreted hCG, and multiple malignant germ cell/stromal components renders this case clinically distinctive. Early surgical intervention, standardized pathological subtyping, karyotype testing, fertility-sparing surgery, and prolonged oncologic follow-up are essential for similar patients.Keywords: gonadoblastoma, hyperandrogenism, gonadal tumor, irregular menstruation, voice deepening, acne

Introduction

Gonadoblastoma (GB) is a rare gonadal neoplasm composed of germ cells and immature sex cord derivatives.1 It has been considered as in situ germ cell malignancy that can be associated with malignant components. Over 90% of GB cases are identified in patients with 46, XY gonadal dysgenesis harbouring Y chromosome fragments, whereas GB developing in individuals with a fully normal 46, XX karyotype and intact ovarian function represents an extremely rare clinical paradox.2 Given its remarkable rarity and limited cumulative case data in published literature, reliable population-based incidence figures for GB among 46, XX females with normal gonads are unavailable to date.3

Herein, we report an 18-year-old female with normal ovarian development and a confirmed 46, XX karyotype who presented with rapidly progressive virilization, positive human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), and a large ovarian mixed dissecting GB containing multiple distincthistological components. We summarize her diagnostic workup, fertility-sparing surgical management and postoperative follow-up outcomes, alongside a systematic review of relevant published literature. This report aims to raise clinicians’ awareness of this rare entity and provide diagnostic and therapeutic references for adolescents presenting with unexplained severe hyperandrogenism and adnexal masses.

Case Presentation

An 18-year-old nulligravid female with no sexual history presented with a 3-year history of recurrent irregular menstruation. Treatment with oral contraceptives was given at other hospitals to normalize her cycles, but irregular bleeding recurred upon discontinuation; she subsequently used the medication intermittently. Approximately six months before admission, she developed voice deepening and generalized acne. Four months prior to admission, she had one week of normal menstruation followed by persistent irregular bleeding. When seen in the outpatient clinic three months before admission, laboratory evaluation revealed a serum testosterone (TT) level of 18.7 nmol/L and a left ovarian cyst measuring 4.1 × 3.8 × 2.9 cm. Continuous treatment with oral contraceptives tablets stopped the bleeding after one week, and she had one normal menstrual cycle after drug withdrawal, with no further bleeding until admission. After two oral contraceptives cycles, her serum TT level rose sharply to 42 nmol/L, and an adrenal ultrasound was normal. At her last outpatient visit, her TT level was > 52 nmol/L, and pelvic ultrasound revealed a mixed cystic-solid mass in the left adnexal region measuring 8.9 × 8.5 × 7.1 cm, with solid vascularity. She was the first child of non-consanguineous parents, delivered full-term via cesarean section. Her mother had no history of specific medication use during pregnancy. She was born with female external genitalia and had normal growth and development. Menarche occurred at age 11 years, with previously regular menses. Her 13-year-old younger sister is healthy and has already experienced menarche.

On admission, physical examination revealed stable vital signs. The patient’s height was 168 cm, weight 60 kg. Cutaneous examination demonstrated multiple acne lesions distributed on the face, shoulders, neck, chest, back, buttocks, and thighs (Figure 1). Breast development was consistent with Tanner stage V. On pelvic examination, the vaginal length was approximately 7.0 cm. Rectal examination revealed a left adnexal mass of approximately 7.0 cm in diameter, which was of medium consistency, mobile, and non-tender. Serum hCG (192 international units per liter (IU/L), normal: < 5.3 IU/L), 17α-hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP) (> 60 nmol/L, normal: 0–8.55 nmol/L), TT (> 52 nmol/L, normal: 0.69–2.60 nmol/L), and squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC) (6.6 ng/mL, normal: < 3.0 ng/mL) levels were elevated. Serum luteinizing hormone (LH) was < 0.30 IU/L, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) < 0.30 IU/L, estradiol (E2) 661.00 pmol/L, progesterone (P) 7.97 nmol/L, and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) 2.20 ng/mL. Routine blood test, blood biochemistry, electrolytes, thyroid hormones, cortisol, and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) were all within normal limits. Tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), cancer antigen 125, 153, 199 and human epididymis protein 4 (HE4), were also within normal ranges.

Physical examination. The patient had diffuse hyperandrogenic acne lesions on the chest (A) and back (B).

Transrectal ultrasonography revealed a mixed cystic-solid mass in the left adnexal region, measuring 9.4 × 9.2 × 6.3 cm; the cystic component contained clear fluid, measured approximately 5.0 × 4.7 × 3.9 cm, and was well-defined with a regular shape and a capsule-like echo, while the solid component demonstrated medium-to-high echogenicity with focal microcystic changes. The entire mass was well-circumscribed, appeared to have a capsule, and exhibited slightly abundant intralesional blood flow signals. Pelvic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a round mass located in the anterior-superior region of the uterus, measuring approximately 8.3 × 8.1 × 6.9 cm; the mass was predominantly solid with internal cystic degeneration, appeared isointense on T1-weighted imaging and slightly hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging, with ill-defined margins, and on contrast-enhanced imaging, the solid components demonstrated marked enhancement (Figure 2).

Imaging findings. Transrectal ultrasonography revealed a mixed cystic-solid mass (A) with slightly abundant intralesional blood flow signals (B). Pelvic contrast-enhanced MRI revealed a round mass in the anterior-superior region of the uterus. This mass appeared slightly hyperintense on axial T2-weighted imaging (C), with the solid component exhibiting pronounced enhancement on axial post-contrast images (D).

The patient underwent laparoscopic left adnexectomy. Intraoperatively, 150 mL of clear yellow free fluid was noted in the pelvis, with no gross abnormalities of the uterus or right adnexa. The left ovary was cystically enlarged (9.0 × 8.0 × 7.5 cm), containing brown fluid within a thick-walled cyst, without adhesions. The mass was resected intact. Gross examination confirmed a multilocular cystic-solid mass (largest locule 8.0 cm) containing yellowish-brown paste-like fluid, with solid elevations and a friable, necrotic cut surface (Figure 3). Intraoperative frozen section suggested a steroid cell tumor, with a focal germ cell component not excluded. Based on the preoperative communication with the patient and the intraoperative discussion with the patient’s family, fertility-sparing surgery (ipsilateral adnexectomy, partial omentectomy, and peritoneal biopsies) was performed. Final pathology confirmed dissecting GB with dysgerminoma, trophoblastic tumor, and Leydig cell tumor components. (Figure 3).

(B–D) Histopathological examination with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, (B) ×200, (C) ×400, (D) ×200: (B) The black arrow points to gonadoblastoma, the tumor cell nests with central large germ cells and peripheral Sertoli-like/granulosa-like cells. (C) The black arrow indicates dysgerminoma composed of cells with round nuclei and clear cytoplasm. The red arrow indicates Leydig interstitial cells with eosinophilic cytoplasm and round, pale-staining nuclei. The blue arrow indicates the trophoblast cells. (D) The two syncytiotrophoblastic cells located centrally.

Postoperative chromosome analysis revealed a 46, XX karyotype (Figure 4). Following multidisciplinary discussion between the oncology and pathology departments, the patient was recommended to receive BEP chemotherapy. However, she declined chemotherapy due to personal factors and requested close follow-up instead. The patient resumed spontaneous menstruation 25 days postoperatively, with regular cyclic bleeding thereafter. Laboratory rechecking on day 13 of the second postoperative menstrual cycle demonstrated full normalization of endocrine and tumor markers: LH 12.7 IU/L, FSH 8.36 IU/L, E2 1304 pmol/L, P 0.91 nmol/L, TT 1.1 nmol/L, hCG < 0.2 IU/L, and SCC 2.7 ng/mL. Follow-up pelvic ultrasound performed at 3 and 6 months postoperatively revealed no residual pelvic masses or abnormal lesions. Lifelong surveillance consisting of regular serum tumor marker testing and serial pelvic imaging is warranted, with reference to standard surveillance for ovarian malignancies.

Karyogram. Chromosomal analysis demonstrated a normal female karyotype.

Discussion

In this case, the patient presented with normal menarche and regular menstrual cycles initially, followed by gradual menstrual irregularities and rapidly progressive female virilization over a short period, manifesting as a deepened voice, acne, and hirsutism. Genital physical examination confirmed normal female external genitalia. Sex hormone testing revealed acute, rapidly progressive hyperandrogenism, and imaging evaluation demonstrated a markedly enlarged adnexal mass. Accordingly, androgen-secreting ovarian tumor was regarded as the primary clinical diagnosis. The patient was an undergraduate student and explicitly denied any sexual history during outpatient consultation. However, routine hospital testing showed a positive hCG result. Given the coexistence of amenorrhea and positive hCG, the attending physician initially suspected the patient had concealed her sexual history, and thus inquired about sexual activity directly and privately (excluding family members). Subsequent gynecological examination confirmed an intact hymen with no obvious laceration, and clinical pregnancy was excluded. The elevated hCG was therefore attributed to ectopic hCG secretion by the ovarian tumor. Based on these findings, precise histopathological typing of the ovarian tumor became the key focus of multidisciplinary discussion between gynecologists, sonologists and radiologists prior to surgery.

For adolescent females presenting with abrupt virilization and profound hyperandrogenism, a thorough differential diagnosis must encompass several key entities: congenital adrenal hyperplasia, characterized by persistently elevated baseline 17-OHP and typically presenting prior to puberty; exogenous androgen exposure from unregulated supplements or anabolic agents, identifiable via careful medication history review and resolution of hormonal abnormalities following cessation of the offending agent; adrenal cortical adenoma or carcinoma associated with deranged adrenal steroidogenesis; and ovarian androgenic neoplasms, most commonly Sertoli–Leydig cell tumors and steroid cell tumors, as well as the rare mixed dissecting GB identified in the present case.

GB is a rare tumor composed of germ cells and immature sex cord derivatives, first reported by Scully in 1953.4 The tumor predominantly afflicts children and adolescents who range from 1 to 38 years old, and bilateral involvement occurs in approximately 40% of cases.5 Patients typically present with a female phenotype, virilizing features, and gonadal dysgenesis. Over 90% of GB patients carry a Y chromosome (most commonly 45, X/46, XY or 46, XY).2 However, GB has also been reported in patients with a normal 46, XX karyotype, though some suggest cryptic Y-chromosome mosaicism may be undetectable.2 A review of 22 GB cases without gonadal dysgenesis found a significantly higher proportion of 46, XX females (91%, 20/22) than 46, XY males (9%, 2/22).3 The present case is a GB with a normal 46, XX karyotype and no gonadal dysgenesis.

GB with a normal 46, XX karyotype and concurrent virilization is rarely reported in the literature. Most described cases present with abdominal pain or distention, abdominal mass, or abnormal vaginal bleeding.1 Chandrapattan et al1 reported a 9-year-old case of GB presenting with virilization, abnormal vaginal bleeding, with an age of onset considerably younger than that of our patient. Kanagal et al6 reported a case of a 14-year-old case of GB with generalized hirsutism and a huge pelvic mass, both presenting with hyperandrogenism and positive hCG with clinical manifestations similar to those of the present patient. Apart from these two cases, which share some clinical and laboratory features with our patient, no other similar cases have been reported to date, although their pathological findings differed from ours.

Histologically, classic GB is characterized by nests of primitive germ cells and immature sex cord cells separated by fibrous connective tissue. In addition to the classic form, a distinct histologic subtype termed “dissecting GB” was first described by Kao et al in 2016,7 who delineated three histologic patterns: solid expansile, anastomosing, and cord-like. Immunohistochemically, this variant shares similar features with classic GB, supporting that they represent different morphologic manifestations of the same disease. The dissecting variant has been proposed as an intermediate stage between classic GB and malignant germ cell tumors, with its presence potentially foreshadowing the development of other malignant germ cell components.8 Although the histologic appearance of GB in individuals with normal development resembles that in disorders of sex development (DSD) patients, gonadal function and clinical features differ substantially.9 While the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, some pathological studies suggest that these tumors may arise through distinct molecular pathways.9

In 1987, the GBY locus was proposed to explain Y-chromosome-related susceptibility to GB. It was localized to Yp11.1-Yq11, where TSPY is the key oncogene. In the normal testis, TSPY regulates the cell cycle and promotes spermatogonial proliferation.10 When aberrantly expressed in dysgenetic gonads or the ovary, TSPY binds cyclin B, enhances CDK1, accelerates G2/M progression, and drives uncontrolled proliferation. TSPY may also disrupt regulation by antagonizing its X-linked homolog, TSPX.11 However, in 46, XX GB cases lacking Y chromosome, the pathogenesis is unclear. Some propose that TSPX may contribute to GB development, though no such cases have been reported.11 Alternatively, Y-chromosome material may be present only in tumor tissue (gonadal mosaicism or fragments undetectable in blood).12 Using SRY-FISH, some investigators have detected Y-chromosome deletions in GB tumor tissue.2

Depending on the components, GB is classified as pure or mixed. Among reported GB cases, 50–60% coexist with malignant germ cell tumors. A high incidence of coexistence with other malignant germ cell tumors, particularly dysgerminoma, has also been observed in GB patients with a 46, XX karyotype.6 Yin et al9 reviewed 14 previously reported cases of ovarian GB with a 46, XX karyotype, with ages ranging from 9 to 34 years (mean 17.4 years). Among these 14 patients, 8 (57%) were over 18 years of age, 4 (28.6%) had fertility, and 12 (86%) had unilateral involvement. All reported cases showed coexisting germ cell tumors: 9 with dysgerminoma, and others with yolk sac tumor, immature teratoma, embryonal carcinoma, choriocarcinoma, or mixed malignant germ cell tumors.

GB treatment requires consideration of age, gonadal status, karyotype, social gender, psychological status, and fertility desires.13 The core principle is complete tumor resection while preserving endocrine and reproductive function.13 Surgery is the primary modality and must be individualized, with concurrent karyotyping. For patients with a normal karyotype and pure GB or GB with only dysgerminoma, unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy confers a favorable prognosis. When GB is associated with more aggressive germ cell tumors (eg, yolk sac tumor, embryonal carcinoma, immature teratoma, choriocarcinoma), prognosis differs and comprehensive evaluation is warranted; adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy may be necessary.9

This report describes a young patient with mixed GB who underwent fertility-sparing surgery, with grossly normal contralateral ovary identified intraoperatively; however, contralateral ovarian biopsy was not performed, representing a key study limitation. Postoperative karyotyping confirmed 46, XX, and all sex hormones/tumor markers normalized rapidly after surgery. Two additional major unavoidable limitations constrain interpretation of this case: the patient declined further genetic screening (including for Y-chromosome material) and adjuvant chemotherapy due to personal reasons. Beyond these patient-specific clinical limitations, this work carries the inherent limitations of a single case report, and the observations cannot be generalized to all patients with similar tumors.

Observation-only management carries substantial long-term oncologic risks, including pelvic recurrence, distant metastasis of trophoblastic tumor components, and delayed malignant progression of residual microscopic germ cell lesions. Accordingly, lifelong intensive surveillance of the remaining ovary, serial serum tumor marker monitoring, and regular pelvic imaging are mandatory for this patient. Regular psychological and reproductive related counselling will also be provided during long-term follow-up.

Conclusion

We report a unique case of dissecting GB with multiple distinct components in a phenotypically normal 46, XX female. This case broadens the differential diagnosis to support early recognition in adolescents with rapid-onset virilization, severe hyperandrogenism, complex adnexal masses, and unexplained hCG elevation. As an in situ germ cell malignancy with malignant components, this tumor warrants comprehensive evaluation encompassing hormone profiling, pelvic imaging, chromosomal karyotyping, and precise pathological subtyping. Fertility‑sparing surgery is a feasible option for young patients; however, rigorous long‑term surveillance with serial tumor markers and pelvic imaging is mandatory when adjuvant chemotherapy is declined for invasive components. Multidisciplinary collaboration facilitates accurate diagnosis, individualized treatment planning, fertility preserving treatment strategies, and minimization of recurrence risk.
