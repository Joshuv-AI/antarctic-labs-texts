The real layering field of Brownian loop soup and the Gaussian multiplicative chaos
Sayantan Maitra

Abstract
. We consider the random field defined by the layering numbers of the Brownian loop soup in a bounded simply connected domain in the complex plane. We call this the layering field and show that, after a suitable renormalization, it converges to the subcritical Gaussian multiplicative chaos. The main technique for our proof is the Wiener-Itô chaos expansion. We also calculate the n n -point functions of the layering field, show their conformal covariance and discuss their behavior near the boundary of the domain.



1.
Introduction

The Brownian loop measure
\mu^{loop}
is a
\sigma
-finite conformally invariant measure on the set of all unrooted Brownian loops in the complex plane. The Brownian loop soup (BLS), denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}
, is the Poisson point process having intensity
\lambda\mu^{loop}
,
\lambda>0
. Given the collection of loops in
\mathcal{L}^{\lambda}
in a domain
D\subseteq\mathbb{C}
and a point
z\in D
, let
N_{\lambda,D}(z)
be the number (with a
+
or
-
sign) of loops whose interiors contain
z
. We call this the
layering number
of the BLS at the point
z
. For a real number
\beta
, we call the collection

(1.1)

\displaystyle e^{\beta N_{\lambda,D}(z)},\quad z\in D

the
layering field
of the BLS in D. Our goal in this article is to show that, as
\lambda\to\infty
and
\beta\to 0
, this field converges to the
Gaussian Multiplicative Chaos
(GMC).

We shall make all the above terms more precise and mathematically rigorous in the next section. Let us first discuss some of the background of this problem. Since its introduction by Lawler and Werner
[
LW04
]
, the Brownian loop soup has received much attention. Apart from the fact that it can be seen as the scaling limit of the random walk loop soup on discrete
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
lattice
[
LTF07
]
, various authors have studied its connection to other models in probability theory and statistical physics. For instance, it is known that when the intensity is low, the outer boundaries of the clusters of Brownian loops in the soup is a Conformal Loop Ensemble (CLE)
[
Wer06
, Theorem 3]
. The CLE is a conformally invariant collection of simple (i.e. non-self-intersecting) and non-crossing loops in the plane. Also, when
\lambda=\frac{1}{2}
, the occupation time of the BLS has been shown to have the same law as that of the square of the Gaussian free field (GFF). This is the continuum version of an isomorphism result due to Le Jan
[
LJ10
]
; see
[
WP21
, Section 3.4]
for a discussion on this issue. Another interesting object, which has many similarities with the layering field defined above, is the
Winding field
. This is constructed from the winding numbers at a point
z
, say
N_{w}(z)
, which is the sum over all the winding numbers corresponding to the loops (that cover
z
) in a realization of the BLS. In an article by van de Brug, Camia and Lis
[
vdBCL18
]
, the authors study the (imaginary) winding field (i.e. objects of the form
e^{i\beta N_{w}(z)},z\in D,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
). They show that, roughly speaking, this object exists as a random distribution taking values in a Sobolev space of negative index. For a more comprehensive review of the Brownian loop soup and its connections to various areas of mathematical physics, we refer the reader to the article
[
Cam17
]
and the references therein.

Our motivation for the present paper comes from the works of Camia, Gandolfi, Kleban
[
CGK16
]
and Camia, Gandolfi, Peccati and Reddy
[
CGPR21
]
. These studied the imaginary layering field
\{e^{i\beta N_{\lambda,D}(z)},z\in D\}
. The former computed their
n
-point correlation functions and showed the conformal covariance of these
n
-point functions and the latter proved that with an appropriate renormalization, these fields converge to a (tilted) imaginary Gaussian Multiplicative Chaos by making use of a technique known as
Wiener-Itô chaos expansion
.

The present article continues this line of research and provides the complementary results for the real-valued layering field. However, there are some key differences with the imaginary case, which we note here. While the imaginary multiplicative chaos exists as a random element in the space
H^{s}
(the Sobolev space with some index
s<0
)

[
JSW20
]
, the real GMC, which we shall denote by
M_{\xi}
, is a random measure and our proof requires a slightly different set of arguments. Furthermore, unlike in the imaginary case, the unboundedness of the real-valued field introduces new challenges for our analysis. More importantly, in dimension 2,
M_{\xi}
is known to be non-degenerate (i.e. not identically zero) only when
0<\xi<2
. This is known as the
sub-critical phase
of the GMC. In the critical and super-critical phases (i.e. when
\xi\geq 2
), one can still make sense of the multiplicative chaos using other techniques, but the GMC has different behaviors compared to the sub-critical phase. At this point, our result only covers a part of the sub-critical phase. In other words, using the standard technique of chaos decomposition, we are only able to connect the layering field with the sub-critical GMC.

Organization:
The plan for this paper is as follows. In Section
2
we briefly review the definitions and other relevant notions that we are going to study. The main results are stated in Section
2.4
. The first two main results (Theorems
2.6
and
2.7
) describe the construction and prove the existence of the Poissonian and Gaussian realizations of the layering model. Their proofs are sketched in Section
3
. The final main result (Theorem
2.9
) connects the Poisson layering field with the Gaussian one, and its proof can be found in Section
4
. Since many of the calculations required for these proofs are routine, we have postponed them to the Appendix sections.

2.
Preliminaries and results

2.1.
The loop measure

Let
D
be either a simply connected open domain in
\mathbb{C}
with smooth (i.e.
C^{1}
) boundary or
\mathbb{C}
itself. In this section, we are going to recall the definition of the unique (up to a multiplicative constant) non-trivial measure
\mu_{D}
on simple loops in
D
that satisfies the
conformal restriction property
. This basically means that the collection of measures
\{\mu_{D}\}_{D\subset\mathbb{C}}
behaves well under conformal maps; see below for a precise definition.

We shall start by defining the
Brownian loop measure

\mu^{loop}
. For us, a
loop
is a continuous curve
\gamma:[0,t_{\gamma}]\to\mathbb{C}
such that
\gamma(0)=\gamma(t_{\gamma})
. The time-length
t_{\gamma}
is a positive number associated with
\gamma
. We are not going to distinguish two loops that can be obtained as a time-shift of one another.
1
1

1

Note however that, we do distinguish a loop and its reverse. In other words, each unrooted loop has an orientation. But this only contributes a factor of 2 to most of our calculations.
This is called
unrooting
the loops and the collection of all unrooted loops in
D
is denoted by
\mathcal{K}(D)
. This is a metrizable space and has a Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}(D))
on it. We refer the reader to
[
Law05
, Chapter 5]
or
[
LW04
]
for more details regarding the topology of this space.

Now we are ready to define the Brownian loop measure. For a fixed time
t>0
and
z\in D
, let
\mu^{br}_{D}(z,z;t)
denote the law of the Brownian bridge of time length
t
from
z
to itself, conditioned to stay inside
D
. This induces a probability measure on
\mathcal{K}(D)
, which we also denote by
\mu^{br}_{D}(z,z;t)
. Then the
Brownian loop measure

\mu^{loop}_{D}
is defined on the measurable set
(\mathcal{K}(D),\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K}(D)))
as follows:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mu^{loop}_{D}(\cdot)=\int_{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\pi t^{2}}\mu^{br}_{D}(z,z;t)(\cdot)\,dt\,dz.

This is not a finite measure. In particular, since the above time integral is unbounded at
0
, the total mass of
\mu^{loop}_{D}
is infinite. It is also known that this is conformally invariant, in the sense that, if
f
is a conformal map from
D
onto another domain
D^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{C}
, then
f\circ\mu^{loop}_{D}=\mu^{loop}_{D^{\prime}}
(Stating this precisely requires some care, as one needs to use Brownian time change under the conformal transformation. See
[
LW04
, Proposition 6]
).

Given a
\gamma\in\mathcal{K}(D)
, we shall use the notation
\bar{\gamma}
to denote its
hull
, i.e. the complement of the unique component of
D\setminus\gamma([0,t_{\gamma}])
that touches the boundary
\partial D
(or, the complement of the unique unbounded component, in case
D
is unbounded).

For this paper we will only need to consider the boundaries of the loops under the Brownian loop measure. It is known that, with probability 1, the boundary
\partial\bar{\gamma}
of a Brownian loop
\gamma
defines a simple (i.e. non self-intersecting) loop in
D
(see the discussion in p. 148 and Proposition 6 in
[
Wer08
]
). Let
S(D)
be the set consisting of all simple loops in
D
. In this collection we only consider the traces of the loops and ignore their time parametrization. By a slight abuse of notations, we denote by
\gamma
the image (or the trace) of the loop
\gamma
. The
\sigma
-algebra on
S(D)
, denoted by
\mathcal{S}(D)
, is generated by collections of loops that are contained in annular regions in
D
. In particular, all the sets of loops that we shall consider in the sequel (e.g. those defined in (
2.1
) and (
2.1
)) are measurable with respect to
\mathcal{S}(D)
.

Since each loop in
\mathcal{K}(D)
defines one in
S(D)
(via the boundary of the hull of the first loop), the Brownian loop measure
\mu^{loop}_{D}
induces a measure on
S(D)
, which we simply call
\mu_{D}
. The resulting family
\{\mu_{D}\}_{D}
of measures on simple loops, indexed by domains
D\subset\mathbb{C}
, has the following two properties, known as
conformal restriction property
:

•

If
D^{\prime}\subset D
, then the restriction of
\mu_{D}
to
D^{\prime}
is
\mu_{D^{\prime}}
.

•

If
f:D\to D^{\prime}
is a conformal equivalence between two domains
D,D^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{C}
, then

f\circ\mu_{D}=\mu_{D^{\prime}}
, in the sense that, for all measurable subsets
A
of
S(D^{\prime})
,

(2.2)

\displaystyle(f\circ\mu_{D})(A):=\mu_{D}(\gamma\in S(D)\mid f(\gamma)\in A)=\mu_{D^{\prime}}(A).

It was shown in
[
Wer08
, Theorem 2]
that the above two conditions fully characterize the family of measures, up to multiplication by a positive constant. In other words, on the set of all planar simple loops, the measures
\mu_{D}
induced by the Brownian loop measures
\mu^{loop}_{D}
form the unique family satisfying the conformal restriction property.
2
2

2

We should point out that, aside from drawing boundaries of Brownian loops, there are other ways of obtaining the measure
\mu
(up to a constant factor). See
[
Wer06
]
for a discussion on how this can be done using the boundaries of the critical percolation clusters.

Let us fix some useful notations for the sequel. Henceforth, we shall write
d(D)
,
d(\gamma)
to denote the diameters of the domain
D
and the (image of) a loop
\gamma
. For
z,w\in D
and
R>\delta>0
we define the following naturally occurring collections of simple loops and their mass under the measure
\mu_{D}
, as follows,

\displaystyle A_{\delta,D}(z)

\displaystyle=\{\gamma\in S(D)\mid z\in\bar{\gamma},d(\gamma)\geq\delta\},\quad\alpha_{\delta,D}(z)=\mu_{D}(A_{\delta}(z))

\displaystyle A_{\delta,D}(z,w)

\displaystyle=\{\gamma\in S(D)\mid z,w\in\bar{\gamma},d(\gamma)\geq\delta\},\quad\alpha_{\delta,D}(z,w)=\mu_{D}(A_{\delta}(z,w))

\displaystyle A_{\delta,D}(z|w)

\displaystyle=\{\gamma\in S(D)\mid z\in\bar{\gamma},w\notin\bar{\gamma},d(\gamma)\geq\delta\},\quad\alpha_{\delta,D}(z|w)=\mu_{D}(A_{\delta}(z|w))

(2.3)

\displaystyle A_{\delta,R,D}(z)

\displaystyle=\{\gamma\in S(D)\mid z\in\bar{\gamma},d(\gamma)\in(\delta,R)\},\quad\alpha_{\delta,R,D}(z)=\mu_{D}(A_{\delta,R}(z)),

and

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{\delta,D}(z)

\displaystyle=\{\gamma\in S_{D}\mid z\in\bar{\gamma},\gamma\nsubseteq B(z,\delta)\},\quad\bar{\alpha}_{\delta,D}(z)=\mu_{D}(\bar{A}_{\delta,D}(z))

(2.4)

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{\delta,R,D}(z)

\displaystyle=\{\gamma\in S_{D}\mid z\in\bar{\gamma},\gamma\nsubseteq B(z,\delta),\gamma\subseteq B(z,R)\},\quad\bar{\alpha}_{\delta,R,D}(z)=\mu_{D}(\bar{A}_{\delta,R,D}(z))

where
B(z,\delta)
denotes the open disk around
z
with radius
\delta
.
R
and
\delta
are sometimes called the
infrared
and
ultraviolet
cutoffs, respectively. Note that the above two methods of implementing the cutoff
\delta
are equivalent, since we clearly have
\bar{A}_{\delta,D}(z)\subsetneq A_{\delta,D}(z)\subsetneq\bar{A}_{\delta/2,D}(z)

for all
\delta>0
and
z\in D
, and therefore,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\bar{\alpha}_{\delta,D}(z)\leq\alpha_{\delta,D}(z)\leq\bar{\alpha}_{\delta/2,D}(z).

However, cutoffs based on diameter of loops seem to behave well when two or more points are concerned, e.g. when calculating two-point functions of the layering fields. Therefore, most of our subsequent calculations will make use of the quantities defined in (
2.1
).

We recall from
[
CGK16
, Lemma A1]
that the masses of some of the collections appearing above can be computed exactly.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
z\in D
and
0<\delta<R<\infty
be such that
B(z,R)\subset D
. Then we have,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\delta,R,D}(z)=\bar{\alpha}_{\delta,R,D}(z)=\frac{1}{5}\log\frac{R}{\delta},

Let us also record a useful consequence of the above. If
D
is a bounded domain,
z\in D
and
\delta>0
is small, we have
A_{\delta,D}(z)\subset A_{\delta,d(D),\mathbb{C}}(z)
, since every loop in
D
must also be a loop in
\mathbb{C}
of diameter less than that of
D
itself. Thus,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\delta,D}(z)\leq\alpha_{\delta,d(D),\mathbb{C}}=\frac{1}{5}\log\frac{d(D)}{\delta},

for all small
\delta>0
.

Before stating the next result, we mention the so-called
thinness property
, first introduced in
[
NW11
]
. Roughly speaking, a measure on some class of compact curves in the plane is said to be
thin
, if it gives only finite mass to the collection of large curves intersecting a bounded region. Lemma 4 of
[
NW11
]
proved that the measure
\mu=\mu_{\mathbb{C}}
, defined on the outer boundaries the Brownian loops in the entire complex plane, is thin. More formally, for any non-empty bounded set
B\subset\mathbb{C}
, this property can be written as

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mu(\gamma\mid d(\gamma)>R,\gamma\cap B\neq\emptyset)<\infty,\text{ for all }R>0.

Given a domain
D
, we shall often use the notation
d_{z}=d_{z,D}=d(z,\partial D)
for the shortest distance between a point
z\in D
and the boundary of
D
. The following result sheds light on the behavior of the loop measure near
\partial D
. We note that, by its virtue, for nice enough domains
D
one can extend the quantity
\alpha_{d_{z},D}(z)
to the whole
\bar{D}
. This observation will be useful later for describing the behavior of the layering field at the boundary (see Corollary
2.8
). We also note that, even though we can prove our main theorems only for bounded domains, the following proposition can be stated for some unbounded domains too.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\alpha_{\delta,D}(z)
and
\bar{\alpha}_{\delta,D}(z)
be as in (
2.1
) and (
2.1
).

(i)

For each
r>0
, there are finite constants
C_{\mathbb{H}}(r),\bar{C}_{\mathbb{H}}(r)>0
such that for all
z\in\mathbb{H}
,

(2.9)

\displaystyle\alpha_{r\cdot d_{z},\mathbb{H}}(z)=C_{\mathbb{H}}(r),\text{ and }\bar{\alpha}_{r\cdot d_{z},\mathbb{H}}(z)=\bar{C}_{\mathbb{H}}(r).

Moreover,
C_{\mathbb{H}}(r),\bar{C}_{\mathbb{H}}(r)\sim\log(1/r)
as
r\to 0
and
C_{\mathbb{H}}(r),\bar{C}_{\mathbb{H}}(r)\downarrow 0
as
r\to\infty
.

(ii)

For any point
z_{0}
on the boundary of the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
and any sequence
\{z_{n}\}_{n}
in
\mathbb{D}
converging to

, we have
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{d_{z_{n}},\mathbb{D}}(z_{n})=C_{\mathbb{D}}<\infty

.

(iii)

Let

c_{D}(z_{0})=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\alpha_{d_{z_{n}},D}(z_{n})<\infty
. Moreover, the constants

are bounded away from

and

over
\partial D
.

The proof is postponed to Appendix
A
. Finally, let
\alpha_{D}(z,w)
be the
\mu_{D}
-mass of all loops in

covering both points
z
and

we know that

(2.10)

\displaystyle g(z,w)=\alpha_{D}(z,w)-\frac{1}{5}\log_{+}\frac{1}{|z-w|},\quad z,w\in D

. Let us rephrase this in the following result.

.

The function
\alpha_{D}(\cdot,\cdot)

2.2.
The loop soup and its layering field

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and
D
a bounded simply connected domain in
\mathbb{C}
with a
C^{1}
boundary. Given a parameter
\lambda>0
, the
Brownian loop soup
on
D
is a Poisson point process
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}=\mathcal{L}_{\lambda,D}
on simple loops in D (i.e. on
S(D)
) with intensity
\lambda\mu_{D}
.
3
3

3

The BLS is usually defined as a Poisson point process on unrooted (not necessarily simple) loops in
D
, having the intensity
\lambda\mu^{loop}_{D}
. But since our concern is with the layering by loops, which is only dependent on their outer boundaries, by an abuse of terminology, we refer to the point process on simple loops as the Brownian loop soup.
More precisely, for each measurable subset
A
of
S(D)
,
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(A)
is Poisson random variable with intensity
\lambda\mu_{D}(A)
and for disjoint measurable subsets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{k}
, the random variables
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(A_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(A_{k})
are independent. Note that, almost surely one can find loops
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots\in S(D)
such that
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\delta_{\gamma_{i}}
and we write
\gamma\in\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
if
\gamma=\gamma_{i}
for some
i
.

We can now define the layering field on
D
. A loop
\gamma\in S(D)
is said to
cover
or
layer
a point
z\in D
if
z\in\bar{\gamma}
. Given a BLS
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
, to each loop
\gamma\in\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
we now assign a sign
X_{\gamma}
, which are i.i.d. random variables taking values
+1
or
-1
, each with probability
1/2
. Equivalently, on
\Omega
we can define two independent Brownian loop soups,
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda/2}^{+}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda/2}^{-}
, each with intensity
\frac{\lambda}{2}\mu_{D}
and assign the value
+1
to all loops realized in the former point process and
-1
to those in the latter. That these two descriptions are indeed equivalent can be seen from the relation

(2.11)

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\cap A}X_{\gamma}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda/2}^{+}(A)-\mathcal{L}_{\lambda/2}^{-}(A),\quad A\in\mathcal{S}(D).

The
layering number

N_{\lambda,D}(z)
is the random variable that counts the number of signed loops in the BLS (with their signs) covering
z
. But due to the fact that the tiny loops covering
z
have infinite mass (as is evident from the definition (
2.1
) of the Brownian loop measure), in almost every realization of
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda/2}^{\pm}
there are infinitely many tiny loops covering every point of
D
. This leads to
N_{\lambda,D}(z)
being almost surely undefined. To address this, we can remove these tiny loops from consideration by introducing a parameter
\delta>0
, the so-called
ultraviolet cutoff
, and restrict ourselves to the collection
A_{\delta,D}(z)
of loops of diameter at least
\delta
which cover
z
. With these notions, finally we can define the main object of our study.

Definition 2.4
.

(i)

The
layering number
of the BLS at
z\in D
with ultraviolet cutoff
\delta>0
is,

(2.12)

\displaystyle N^{\delta}_{\lambda,D}(z):=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\cap A_{\delta,D}(z)}X_{\gamma}=\mathcal{L}_{\lambda/2}^{+}(A_{\delta,D}(z))-\mathcal{L}_{\lambda/2}^{-}(A_{\delta,D}(z)).

We note that, since almost surely there are only finitely many loops covering
z
with diameter larger than
\delta
(because
\alpha_{\delta,D}(z)=\mu_{D}(A_{\delta}(z))<\infty
), the above quantity is almost surely finite.

(ii)

For
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
\delta>0
, we define the
(real) layering field
on
D
as

(2.13)

\displaystyle V^{\delta}_{\beta,\lambda,D}(z):=e^{\beta N^{\delta}_{\lambda,D}(z)},\quad z\in D.

We shall also use the following notations frequently in the sequel to denote the renormalized layering fields:

(2.14)

\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\lambda,\beta,D}(z)=\delta^{2\Delta(\lambda,\beta)}V^{\delta}_{\lambda,\beta,D}(z)=e^{\beta N^{\delta}_{\lambda,D}(z)+2\Delta(\lambda,\beta)\log\delta}

with
\Delta(\lambda,\beta)=\frac{\lambda}{10}(\cosh(\beta)-1)
. Note that
\Delta(\lambda,\beta)\geq 0
for all
\lambda>0,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

Similarly, when
0<\delta<R<d(z,\partial D)
for some
z\in D
, we define

(2.15)

\displaystyle N^{\delta,R}_{\lambda,D}(z)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\cap A_{\delta,R,D}(z)}X_{\gamma}\text{ and }V^{\delta,R}_{\beta,\lambda,D}(z)=e^{\beta N^{\delta,R}_{\lambda,D}(z)}.

These are the layering number and the layering field at
z
with the ultraviolet cutoff
\delta
and infrared cutoff
R
.
\tilde{V}^{\delta,R}_{\beta,\lambda,D}
is similarly as above.

We will often ignore the parameters
\lambda
,
\beta
and the domain
D
when they are fixed and clear from the context.

In the following we provide yet another description of the layering numbers. This will help us connect the Poisson layering fields with Gaussian fields on
D
. Suppose
S_{\pm}(D)=\{\pm 1\}\times S(D)
is the collection of all marked simple loops in
D
with the product
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{S}_{\pm}(D)
. Then the
loop soup
on
D
with intensity parameter
\lambda>0
can be thought of as a Poisson random measure (PRM)
N_{\lambda}
on
S_{\pm}(D)
with intensity
\nu_{\lambda}:=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{+1}+\delta_{-1})\otimes\lambda\mu_{D}
. More precisely, for each measurable
A\subset S_{\pm}(D)
,
N_{\lambda}(A)
is Poisson random variable with intensity
\nu_{\lambda}(A)
and for disjoint sets
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\in\mathcal{S}_{\pm}(D)
, the random variables
N_{\lambda}(A_{1}),\ldots,N_{\lambda}(A_{k})
are independent. It is easy to see that the layering number defined in (
2.12
) is equivalent to the following representation. For
\delta\geq 0
,
z\in D
and a marked loop
x=(\epsilon,\gamma)\in S_{\pm}(D)
, let

(2.16)

\displaystyle h^{\delta}_{z}(x)=h^{\delta}_{z}(\epsilon,\gamma)=\epsilon\mathbf{1}_{A_{\delta}(z)}(\gamma)

be the
locally exploding kernels
, according to the terminology introduced in
[
CGPR21
]
. Note that, when
\delta=0
, we use the notation
h_{z}=h^{0}_{z}
.
With these notations in hand, we can rewrite the layering number at
z
with the ultraviolet cutoff
\delta>0
as,

(2.17)

\displaystyle N^{\delta}_{\lambda,D}(z)=N_{\lambda}(h^{\delta}_{z})=\int_{S_{\pm}(D)}h^{\delta}_{z}(x)N_{\lambda}(dx).

2.3.
The Gaussian layering field and the multiplicative chaos

In this section we introduce the other main object of our study, viz. the Gaussian layering model. However, it will be beneficial to first recall the main ingredients of the theory of Gaussian multiplicative chaos.

As before, we assume that
D\subset\mathbb{C}
is a bounded simply connected domain with smooth boundary. Let
G_{D}(z,w)
,
z,w\in D
, denote the Green’s function on
D
. Recall that, at least formally, this can be defined as the expected local time at
w
of the Brownian motion starting from
z\in D
, before it hits the boundary
\partial D
of
D
. In notations, we can write

(2.18)

\displaystyle\int_{D^{\prime}}G_{D}(z,w)\,dw=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{z}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{D}}\mathbf{1}\{B_{s}\in D^{\prime}\}\,ds\right],

where
D^{\prime}
is an open subset of
D
,
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a planar Brownian motion started at
z
and
\tau_{D}
is the first time it exits
D
. It is known that
G_{D}
is a symmetric function on
D\times D
with a logarithmic singularity at
z=w
(i.e. it blows up like a constant multiple of
\log(|z-w|^{-1})
as
w\to z
). The Green’s function can also be defined as the fundamental solution to the Laplace equation on
D
with Dirichlet boundary condition.

The
Gaussian free field
(GFF)
X
on
D
is usually defined as a generalized centered Gaussian process with the covariance given by the Green’s function
G_{D}(\cdot,\cdot)
. More generally, we can define the GFF
X
to be a Gaussian process with covariance given by

(2.19)

\displaystyle K(z,w)=\log_{+}\frac{1}{|z-w|}+g(z,w),\quad z,w\in D

where
g:D\times D\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and bounded. The reader is referred to, for instance, the lecture notes
[
WP21
, Chapter 3]
for a rigorous introduction to the continuum GFF.

The
Gaussian multiplicative chaos
(GMC) on
D
, denoted hereafter by
M_{\xi,D}
, can formally be viewed as a random measure on
D
defined as an exponential of the GFF
X
(cf.
[
RV14
]
)

(2.20)

\displaystyle M_{\xi,D}(dz)=e^{\xi X(z)}\,dz,

where
\xi>0
is a parameter. Since the GFF
X
can only be shown to exist as a generalized function (i.e. as a Schwartz distribution), it is not possible to rigorously make sense of its exponential.
To precisely define the GMC, one introduces a cutoff or an approximation of the GFF
X
. There are several equivalent ways of approximating the GFF, and we can fix one for the time being. Suppose that there is a sequence
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of centered Gaussian processes on
D
which approximates the GFF
X
in a suitable sense. Then we can define the random measures
M^{n}_{\xi,D},n\geq 1
, on
\mathcal{B}(D)
by,

(2.21)

\displaystyle M^{n}_{\xi,D}(dz)=e^{\xi X_{n}(z)-\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}(X_{n}(z)^{2})}\,dz.

It was proved by Kahane that the above sequence of random measures converges almost surely to a random measure
M_{\xi,D}
in the topology of weak converges, and that this limit is unique in the sense that it does not depend on the specific approximation of the GFF. This shows that the object defined in (
2.20
) can be given meaning, provided that we can find a suitable approximation of the process
X
. Moreover, Kahane showed that
M_{\xi,D}
is non-degenerate (i.e. it does not vanish almost surely) if and only if
\xi<2
. This is known as the
sub-critical regime
of the GMC. In all other cases (i.e. in the
critical
,
\xi=2
, and
super-critical
,
\xi>2
, cases) the GMC
M_{\xi,D}
is fully degenerate. Let us also mention here that, when
\xi<2
,
M_{\xi}
has finite moments of order
p\in(0,4/\xi^{2})
, in the sense that

(2.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(M_{\xi}(E)^{p})<+\infty,\text{ for all compact }E\subset D.

In particular,
M_{\xi}
has finite second moments only when
\xi<\sqrt{2}
. For these reasons, in our main result (Theorem
2.7
) we restrict ourselves to this section of the subcritical regime. We refer the reader to the survey article
[
RV14
]
for a thorough review of this area.

Now let us construct the Gaussian layering field. This is analogous to the Poissonian layering field from Definition
2.4
. Recall that
S(D)
is the collection of all simple loops in
D
with the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{S}(D)
and
S_{\pm}(D)
is the set of all signed simple loops with the product
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{S}_{\pm}(D)
. Let
G_{0}
be a centered
Gaussian random measure
(or, white noise) on
S(D)
with
control

\mu_{D}
. By this we mean that, for all
B\in\mathcal{S}(D)
,
G_{0}(B)
is a centered Gaussian random variable and
\mathbb{E}[G_{0}(B)G_{0}(C)]=\mu_{D}(B\cap C)

for all
B,C\in\mathcal{S}(D)
. Let
G
denote a Gaussian random measure on
S_{\pm}(D)
with control
\nu=\frac{1}{2}(\delta_{+1}+\delta_{-1})\otimes\mu_{D}
. This is the natural extension of
G_{0}
to
S_{\pm}(D)
and we are sometimes going to use the relationship,

(2.23)

\displaystyle G(h^{\delta}_{z})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}G_{0}(A_{\delta,D}(z)),\quad(\delta>0,z\in D),

where
h^{\delta}_{z}
was defined in (
2.16
).

Definition 2.5
.

For
\delta>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, the
Gaussian layering field (GLF) on
D
with ultraviolet cutoff
\delta
is

(2.24)

\displaystyle W^{\delta}_{\xi}(z)=e^{\xi G(h^{\delta}_{z})}=e^{\xi G_{0}(A_{\delta}(z))},\quad z\in D.

With
\Delta_{\xi}=\xi^{2}/20
, we use the notation

(2.25)

\displaystyle\tilde{W}^{\delta}_{\xi}(z)=\delta^{2\Delta_{\xi}}W^{\delta}_{\xi}(z)=e^{\xi G_{0}(A_{\delta}(z))+2\Delta_{\xi}\log\delta},\quad z\in D,

for the renormalized GLF.

2.4.
Main results

We are now ready to state our main results. Recall from Definition
2.4
the real valued layering field of the Brownian loop soup in
D
, denoted by
V^{\delta}_{\lambda,\beta}=V^{\delta}_{\lambda,\beta,D}
. In the following, we identify the renormalized fields defined in (
2.14
) with the measures
\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(z)\,dz
on
D
. Let
M_{+}(D)
be the space of all non-negative measures on
(D,\mathcal{B}(D))
. We equip
M_{+}(D)
with the topology of weak convergence of measures, i.e. for
\nu,\nu_{n}\in M_{+}(D)
,
n\geq 1
,
\nu_{n}\to\nu
if for all continuous bounded function
\varphi:D\to\mathbb{R}
(notation:
\varphi\in C_{b}(D)
),
\nu_{n}(\varphi)=\int_{D}\varphi\,d\nu_{n}\to\int_{D}\varphi\,d\nu=\nu(\varphi)
as
n\to\infty
. Roughly speaking, taken together, the next results show that as
\delta\to 0,\beta\to 0
and
\lambda\to\infty
the random measures
\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\lambda,\beta}
converges to a "tilted" Gaussian multiplicative chaos on
D
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\delta>0
,
\lambda>0
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
and
D
be a bounded simply connected domain in
\mathbb{C}
. If
\Delta(\lambda,2\beta)=\frac{\lambda}{10}(\cosh(2\beta)-1)<1
, there is a random Borel measure
V_{\lambda,\beta}=V_{\lambda,\beta,D}
in
M_{+}(D)
such that as
\delta\to 0
,
\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\lambda,\beta,D}\to V_{\lambda,\beta}
in probability as members of
M_{+}(D)
.

We shall call the random measure
V_{\lambda,\beta,D}
, obtained above, the
(real) Poisson layering field on
D
. The next theorem concerns the Gaussian random variables
\tilde{W}^{\delta}_{\xi,D}
introduced in Definition

2.5
. It shows that we can obtain a well-defined object out of them by removing the ultraviolet cutoff
\delta
and provides the relation of this limiting object with the Gaussian multiplicative chaos. As above, here
\tilde{W}^{\delta}_{\xi}
are identified with the measures
\tilde{W}^{\delta}_{\xi}(dz)=\tilde{W}^{\delta}_{\xi}(z)\,dz
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose
0<\xi<2
and
D\subset\mathbb{C}
is bounded simply connected domain with smooth boundary. Then the following hold.

(1)

There is a random element
W_{\xi}=W_{\xi,D}
in
M_{+}(D)
, such that as
\delta\to 0
,
\tilde{W}^{\delta}_{\xi}\to W_{\xi}
weakly in probability.

(2)

W_{\xi,D}
is almost surely absolutely continuous with respect to the Gaussian multiplicative chaos
M_{\xi,D}
on
D
. Moreover, the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
W
with respect to
M
is non-random and is given by the formula,

(2.26)

\displaystyle\frac{dW_{\xi,D}}{dM_{\xi,D}}(z)=e^{\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\Theta_{D}(z)},\quad z\in D,

where
\Theta_{D}(z)=\frac{1}{5}\log d_{z}+\alpha_{d_{z},D}(z)
and recall that
d_{z}=d(z,\partial D)
.

W_{\xi}
will be called the
(real) Gaussian layering field
(GLF). Let us observe that, given Proposition
2.2
, the expression of the Radon-Nikodym derivative in (
2.26
) implies that the field
W_{\xi,D}
vanishes near the boundary of
D
. This should be compared with the analogous expression
[
CGPR21
, eq. (1.6)]
which appears in the case of imaginary layering field. In that situation, again because of the behavior of
\Theta_{D}
near
\partial D
, the imaginary layering field blows up with respect to the imaginary GMC.

Corollary 2.8
.

The right side of (
2.26
) converges to
0
as
z\to\partial D
. Thus, on
\partial D
, the Gaussian layering field
W_{\xi,D}
is singular with respect to the GMC .

In the above statement, we only require that
z
goes towards the boundary
\partial D
of
D
, in the sense that
d(z,\partial D)\to 0
. This is more general than the assumption that
z\to z_{0}
for some point
z_{0}\in\partial D
. Since
\bar{\mathbb{D}}
is compact, all sequences have convergent subsequences, and thus the statement follows from Proposition
2.2
. Finally, we state our main result that links the Poisson and the Gaussian layering fields.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
0<\xi<\sqrt{2}
be fixed and
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be bounded and dimply connected. As
\lambda\to\infty
and
\lambda\beta^{2}\to\xi^{2}
, the Poisson layering fields
V_{\lambda,\beta,D}
converge weakly to the Gaussian layering field
W_{\xi,D}
.

By
weak convergence
here we mean that the convergence is in the sense of finite-dimensional distributions, i.e. if
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\in C_{b}(D)
, we have,

(2.27)

\displaystyle(V_{\lambda,\beta}(\varphi_{1}),\ldots,V_{\lambda,\beta}(\varphi_{n}))\implies(W_{\xi}(\varphi_{1}),\ldots,W_{\xi}(\varphi_{n})),

as
\lambda\to\infty
,
\lambda\beta^{2}\to\xi^{2}
.

Remark 2.10
.

Before finishing this section, we highlight the important difference between the hypotheses of the above theorems regarding the parameter
\xi
. Since we will exploit the standard multiplicative chaos theory to show the existence of the Gaussian layering field, we can state Theorem
2.7
for the whole subcritical range
\xi\in(0,2)
. On the other hand, Theorem
2.9
can only be stated for the range
\xi\in(0,\sqrt{2})
as the technique of chaos expansion requires that the fields be bounded in
L^{2}
.

3.
Existence of the Poisson and Gaussian layering fields

This section is dedicated to the proofs of Theorem
2.6
and Theorem
2.7
. As our treatments of the Poisson and the Gaussian cases are similar, some aspects of their existence proofs can be unified. To this end, we explicitly calculate the one and two-point functions of
V^{\delta}_{\beta}=V^{\delta}_{\beta,\lambda,D}
and
W^{\delta}_{\xi}=W^{\delta}_{\xi,D}
and also discuss their ultraviolet limits (i.e. when
\delta\to 0
). Using these observations, we prove the existence of
V_{\lambda,\beta,D}
in Section
3.2
and and that of
W_{\xi,D}
in Section
3.3
. Since the intensity
\lambda>0
and the domain
D
are fixed in this section, let us ignore them whenever possible.

3.1.
Correlation functions

First, let us consider the Poisson fields. Recall that
\Delta(\lambda,\beta)=\Delta(\beta)=\frac{\lambda}{10}(\cosh(\beta)-1)
and we introduce the following new notations:
d_{z,w}=d^{D}_{z,w}:=|z-w|\wedge d(z,\partial D)
and
d_{w,z}=d^{D}_{w,z}:=|z-w|\wedge d(w,\partial D)
for
z,w\in D
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
z,w\in D
be two distinct points. Then,

(i)

For every
\delta>0
, we have,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(V^{\delta}_{\beta}(z))=

\displaystyle e^{\lambda\alpha_{\delta,D}(z)(\cosh(\beta)-1)}=e^{10\Delta(\beta)\alpha_{\delta}(z)}.

And when
d_{z}=d(z,\partial D)>R>\delta>0
we have,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(V^{\delta,R}_{\beta}(z))=

\displaystyle\left(R/\delta\right)^{2\Delta(\beta)}

where
V^{\delta,R}_{\beta}
was defined in (
2.15
).

(ii)

As
\delta\to 0
, the limit

(3.3)

\displaystyle\langle V_{\beta}(z)\rangle_{D}:=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(z))=d_{z}^{2\Delta(\beta)}e^{10\Delta(\beta)\alpha_{d_{z},D}(z)}

exists.

(iii)

Suppose
|z-w|>\delta,\delta^{\prime}>0
. Then for all
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
we have,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(V^{\delta}_{\beta}(z)V^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta^{\prime}}(w)\right)=e^{\lambda\alpha_{\delta}(z|w)(\cosh(\beta)-1)}e^{\lambda\alpha(z,w)\left(\cosh(\beta+\beta^{\prime})-1\right)}e^{\lambda\alpha_{\delta^{\prime}}(w|z)(\cosh(\beta^{\prime})-1)}

The notations
\alpha_{\delta}(z|w),\alpha(z,w)=\alpha_{|z-w|}(z,w)
etc were defined in (
2.1
).

(iv)

The limit

\displaystyle\langle V_{\beta}(z)V_{\beta}(w)\rangle_{D}:=\lim_{\delta,\delta^{\prime}\to 0}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(z)\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(w))

(3.5)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(d_{z,w}d_{w,z})^{2\Delta(\beta)}e^{10\Delta(2\beta)\alpha(z,w)}e^{10\Delta(\beta)\alpha_{d_{z,w}}(z|w)}e^{10\Delta(\beta)\alpha_{d_{w,z}}(w|z)}

The proofs of the above formulae are in Appendix
B.1
. We next show that the convergence obtained in Lemma
3.1
for the 2-point function holds in
L^{1}(D\times D)
when
\Delta(2\beta,\lambda)<1
. Since
\Delta(2\beta,\lambda)\to\frac{\xi^{2}}{5}

as
\lambda\to\infty,\lambda\beta^{2}\to\xi^{2}
, this condition therefore requires
0<\xi<\sqrt{5}
, and in particular covers the range mentioned in Theorems
2.7
and
2.9
. (See Remark 1.2 in
[
CGPR21
]
for a comment on this restriction.)

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
\Delta(2\beta)<1
. As
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\to 0
, we have

(3.6)

\displaystyle\int_{D}\int_{D}\,dz\,dw\,|\mathbb{E}(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(z)\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(w))-\langle V_{\beta}(z)V_{\beta}(w)\rangle|\to 0.

The above is reminiscent of
[
CGPR21
, Theorem 3.3]
and
[
vdBCL18

Although we do not need to consider general
n
-point functions for the proof of our main results, for the sake of completeness we include their formulae in the following proposition and show that they satisfy a conformal covariance property. The proof follows the same line of arguments as that of

Proposition 3.3
.

(i) For
n\geq 2

\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n}\in R

(3.7)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta_{j},D}(z_{j})\right)=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\delta^{2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta(\beta_{j})}\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}V^{\delta}_{\beta_{j},D}(z_{j})\right)=:\phi_{D}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n};\bm{\beta})

exists finitely, with
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})

(ii) Let
D,D^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{C}
be two conformally equivalent bounded open domains and suppose

\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})

. Then,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\phi_{D^{\prime}}(z^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,z^{\prime}_{n};\bm{\beta})=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}|f^{\prime}(z_{j})|^{2\Delta(\beta_{j})}\right)\phi_{D}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n};\bm{\beta}).

Let us now turn to the Gaussian layering fields
\tilde{W}^{\delta}_{\xi}

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\xi>0

\Delta_{\xi}=\xi^{2}/20<1/4

(i)

For every

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\tilde{W}^{\delta}_{\xi}(z)]=\delta^{2\Delta_{\xi}}e^{\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\alpha_{\delta,D}(z)}\to d_{z}^{2\Delta_{\xi}}e^{\frac{\xi^{2}}{2}\alpha_{d_{z},D}(z)}=:\langle W_{\xi}(z)\rangle.

(ii)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta,\delta^{\prime}\to 0}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{W}^{\delta}_{\xi}(z)\tilde{W}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\xi}(w))=

(iii)

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
2.6

Here we will show that the layering fields
\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}
, with the ultraviolet cutoff
\delta>0
, converge to a random measure
V_{\beta}
as
\delta\to 0
. But first, we record the simple fact that, for every measurable subset
B
of
D
, as
\delta\to 0
the random variables
\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(B)
converge in mean.

Lemma 3.5
.

For all
B\in\mathcal{B}(D)
,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(B))=\int_{B}d_{z}^{2\Delta(\beta)}e^{10\Delta(\beta)\alpha_{d_{z},D}(z)}\,dz<\infty.

Proof.

By Fubini’s theorem, (
3.1
) and (
3.3
) we have, for all
\delta>0
,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(B))=\int_{B\cap\{d_{z}>\delta\}}d_{z}^{2\Delta(\beta)}e^{10\Delta(\beta)\alpha_{d_{z},D}(z)}dz+\int_{B\cap\{d_{z}\leq\delta\}}\delta^{2\Delta(\beta)}e^{10\Delta(\beta)\alpha_{\delta,D}(z)}dz.

Thus, using (
2.7
) we get,

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(B))-\int_{B\cap\{d_{z}>\delta\}}d_{z}^{2\Delta(\beta)}e^{10\Delta(\beta)\alpha_{d_{z},D}(z)}dz\right|\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B\cap\{d_{z}\leq\delta\}}\delta^{2\Delta(\beta)}e^{10\Delta(\beta)\alpha_{\delta,D}(z)}dz

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{B\cap\{d_{z}\leq\delta\}}\delta^{2\Delta(\beta)}e^{10\Delta(\beta)\frac{1}{5}\log\frac{d(D)}{\delta}}dz

(3.14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d(D)^{2\Delta(\beta)}|B\cap\{z\in D\mid d_{z}\leq\delta\}|.

As
\delta\to 0
the r.h.s. in the above converges to
0
. This gives the expression for the limit in (
3.12
). It is easy to see that it is finite, since
D
is bounded and for
B\in\mathcal{B}(D)
we have

(3.15)

\displaystyle\int_{B}d_{z}^{2\Delta(\beta)}e^{10\Delta(\beta)\alpha_{d_{z},D}(z)}\,dz\leq\int_{B}d_{z}^{2\Delta(\beta)}e^{10\Delta(\beta)\frac{1}{5}\log\frac{d(D)}{d_{z}}}\,dz=d(D)^{2\Delta(\beta)}|B|<\infty,

again using (
2.7
) to get the upper bound.
∎

The next proposition finishes the proof of Theorem

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded simply connected domain with smooth boundary. Also suppose
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
\Delta(2\beta)<1
. Then, for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(D)
,

(3.16)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(B)=:V_{\beta}(B)

exists in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and is finite. Moreover, the above defines a random element
V_{\beta}=V_{\beta,\lambda,D}
in
M_{+}(D)
, such that in probability

(3.17)

\displaystyle\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}\to V_{\beta}\text{, as }\delta\to 0

as elements of
M_{+}(D)
(i.e. in the topology of weak convergence).

Proof.

The first part of this proof borrows the arguments from
[
CGPR21
, Theorem 3.3]
and
[
vdBCL18
, Theorem 5.1]
. Fix a set
B\in\mathcal{B}(D)
and note that for each fixed
\delta>0
,
\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(B)
has finite second moment. Let us prove that
\{\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(B)\}_{\delta}
is Cauchy in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. To this end, let
\delta,\delta^{\prime}>0
and note that we can apply Fubini’s theorem to get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(B)-\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(B))^{2}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{D}\int_{D}(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(z)-\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(z))(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(w)-\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(w))\mathbf{1}_{B}(z)\mathbf{1}_{B}(w)\,dz\,dw\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}\int_{D}\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(z)-\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(z))(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(w)-\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(w))]\mathbf{1}_{B}(z)\mathbf{1}_{B}(w)\,dz\,dw

(3.18)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{D}\int_{D}|\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(z)-\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(z))(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(w)-\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(w))]|\,dz\,dw.

As
\delta,\delta^{\prime}\to 0
, by Lemma
3.2

\displaystyle\int_{D}\int_{D}|\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(z)-\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(z))(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(w)-\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(w))]|\,dz\,dw

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}\int_{D}|\mathbb{E}(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(z)\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(w))-\mathbb{E}(\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(z)\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(w))-\mathbb{E}(\tilde{V}^{\delta}_{\beta}(z)\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(w))+\mathbb{E}(\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(z)\tilde{V}^{\delta^{\prime}}_{\beta}(w))|\,dz\,dw
