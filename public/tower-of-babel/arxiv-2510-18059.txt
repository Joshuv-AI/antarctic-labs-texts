Traveling Waves in the McKean-Vlasov Equation under Sakaguchi-Kuramoto Interaction with Phase Frustration
Jesenko Vukadinovic

Abstract
. We study the McKean–Vlasov equation for weakly coupled oscillators subject to the Sakaguchi–Kuramoto interaction. While the Kuramoto interaction provides a good approximation for small, densely connected networks, time delays in larger networks lead to symmetry-breaking phase offsets (frustrations). The Sakaguchi–Kuramoto interaction is the simplest such generalization, featuring a single frustration parameter. We establish the existence of a continuous global phase transition from incoherence to coherence, in the form of a propagating asymmetrically extended von Mises probability distribution function (AvMPDF). The corresponding traveling wave equation reduces to a system of two equations in two unknowns: the order parameter for the AvMPDF and the wave speed. The analysis relies on an appropriate asymmetrical extension of the modified Bessel function.

1.
Introduction

Oscillators found in nature are typically modeled mathematically as limit-cycles: they are derived from systems of nonlinear differential equations whose dynamics are characterized by relaxation to a globally attracting limit-cycle. The description of a large number of interacting limit-cycle oscillators is therefore a large system of nearly identical mutually coupled subsystems that is mathematically intractable and requires reduction. The phase reduction approach, in which each subsystem is replaced by one equation per oscillator that governs its phase was initially proposed by Winfree
[
26
]
. It relies on the separation between fast timescales for the relaxation to the limit-cycles, and slow timescales for their mutual interaction. It was justified rigorously by Kuramoto
[
14
]
, who employed perturbative methods that apply when the coupling between oscillators is weak, that is, when mutual perturbations are sufficiently small relative to their intrinsic frequencies so as not to significantly perturb the amplitudes and destabilize the globally attracting limit-cycles, while causing large deviations their phases.

Each limit cycle, when unperturbed, is viewed as a uniform oscillator
\dot{\theta}_{i}=\omega_{i}
,
where
\omega_{i}
is the intrinsic frequency of the oscillation. Averaging methods (see
[
14
]
) yield the following system of
N
coupled phase equations that govern the time evolution of
N
interacting oscillators,

\dot{\theta}_{i}=\omega_{i}+\frac{\mu}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma_{ij}(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}),\ \ \ i=1,\dots,N.

The parameter
\mu>0
represents the interaction strength. The
i
-th equation describes how the
i
-th limit cycle adjusts its phase velocity according to the input from other oscillators via the so-called phase interaction functions (PIFs),
\Gamma_{ij}
. PIFs can be computed as convolution integrals involving terms from the original limit cycle model (e.g.
[
10
]
). More precisely, averaging over the full phase cycle yields the formula,

(1.1)

\Gamma_{ij}(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}z_{j}(\tilde{\theta})p_{ij}(\tilde{\theta},\theta+\tilde{\theta})\ d\tilde{\theta},

where
(z_{j})
is the perturbation function, and
(p_{ij})
is the so-called phase response (resetting) curve (PRC), and which are computed experimentally or numerically from the original limit cycle model. When the oscillators are identical and the interactions are global and equally weighted, then
\Gamma_{ij}=\Gamma=\partial_{\theta}W
, where
W
is the so-called interaction potential.

In order to achieve maximal tractability while capturing the essence of spontaneous synchronization, Kuramoto proposed equally weighted purely sinusoidal coupling between identical oscillators,
\Gamma_{ij}(\theta)=\Gamma(\theta)=\sin\theta
. The sinusoidal coupling mimics oscillators adjusting their phase velocities to each other: it vanishes when oscillators are in phase or anti-phase, it pushes oscillators together when they are in near phase and apart when they are in near anti-phase. Furthermore, a single-mode interaction permits a mean-field reformulation, in which the interactions can be understood by means of macroscopic mean-field quantities – the order parameter, and the average phase – rather than directly. This, in turn, significantly simplifies the dynamics of synchronization. Although highly symmetric and idealized, the resulting so-called Kuramoto model is considered the paradigm of synchronization, providing a unifying underpinning for various mechanisms of synchronization. For
N\gg 1
, depending on the distribution of intrinsic frequencies
\omega_{i}
, it predicts a phase transition from the incoherent state to a single coherent state, in which a single cluster of oscillators spontaneously synchronize at a certain threshold
\mu_{c}
(see Strogatz et al.
[
19
,
20
]
).

The Kuramoto model requires modifications in order to improve its physical and biological plausibility, as well as to give rise to diverse dynamical phenomena that go well beyond the stationary mono-stable single-cluster coherence. Neurobiological applications in particular are abundant with phase-reduced models. The visual cortex is an intricate network featuring dynamical phenomena that cannot be accounted for in the Kuramoto framework (e.g. multi-stable multi-cluster states and periodic switching between them, dynamical instabilities, traveling waves, disorder in spatial patterns observed in the cortex). The main shortcoming of the the Kuramoto model from the neurobiological perspective stems from its lack of an explicit spatial embedding. Global and uniform coupling of the system is a good approximation in a small densely connected network, but it loses its validity for large populations of neurons distributed throughout the visual cortex. A crucial step toward neurobiological plausibility is the incorporation of time delays between distant subsystems into the PIFs. This can be achieved by assuming that the oscillators occupy positions
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
on a d-dimensional ring or lattice, and then allowing distance-dependent time delays
\tau_{ij}\propto|x_{i}-x_{j}|
. A simple way to account for time delays is by explicitly incorporating phase offsets
\alpha_{ij}\propto|x_{i}-x_{j}|
into the system:

\dot{\theta}_{i}=\omega_{i}+\frac{\mu}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sin(\theta_{j}-\theta_{i}-\alpha_{ij}),\ \ \ i=1,\dots,N.

Phase offsets have significant dynamical implications as they introduce symmetry breaking frustrations into the model, which cause the interaction functions to push the phases away from absolute synchrony. This, in turn, leads to traveling wave dynamics in order to minimize frustration. Other, much more intricate dynamics are also predicted, such as spatial wave patterns reminiscent of those observed in cortical activity
[
27
]
.

The so-called Sakaguchi-Kuramoto model featuring a single frustration parameter
\alpha_{ij}=\alpha
goes back to Sakaguchi and Kuramoto
[
17
]
. It provides the simplest possible asymmetric extension of the Kuramoto model that captures the essence of symmetry breaking in synchronization processes. While the model has attracted renewed interest in recent years (see
[
4
]
and references therein), a rigorous mathematical treatment of the question of phase transition from incoherence to coherence characterized by traveling-waves is still lacking. As is the case with the Kuramoto model, the reformulation of the model in the mean-field limit of infinitely many noisy oscillators lends itself much more readily to rigorous mathematical analysis.

Sakaguchi
[
18
]
extended the Kuramoto model to include stochastic fluctuations in the intrinsic frequencies. Adding a noisy forcing term to each deterministic equation in the phase-reduced model (assuming
\omega_{i}=0
), one arrives at its Langevin counterpart,

(1.2)

dX_{t}^{i}=\frac{\mu}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Gamma(X_{t}^{j}-X_{t}^{i})+\sqrt{2D}dB_{t}^{i},\ \ \ i=1,\dots,N,

where
B_{t}^{i}
are
N
independent Brownian motions. The presence of fluctuation does not have dramatic qualitative implications for the phase transition for the Kuramoto model. Quantitatively, however, it contributes to a high degree of coherency of the single-cluster state. Provided that
\Gamma
is smooth, it is known
[
16
]
that we can pass to the so-called mean-field (thermodynamic, continuum) limit. Consider the empirical measure

\rho^{(N)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}}.

Then
\mathbb{E}\left(\rho^{(N)}\right)
converges as
N\to\infty
in the sense of weak convergence of probability measures to some measure
\rho
satisfying the following nonlinear nonlocal parabolic PDE:

(1.3)

\partial_{t}\rho=D\partial^{2}_{\theta}\rho+\mu\partial_{\theta}\left(\rho\partial_{\theta}(W\star\rho)\right).

Equation (
1.3
) can also be understood as the Fokker-Planck equation for the McKean SDE

\dot{X}_{t}=\mu\left(W^{\prime}\star\rho\right)(X_{t},t)+\sqrt{2D}dB_{t},

where
\rho=\rm{Law}(X_{t})
, which also arises as the overdamped limit of the Vlasov-Fokker-Planck equation.

Various works dealing with the phase transition for different interaction potentials exist already
[
5
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
15
,
21
]
. They require the interaction potential
W
to possess a symmetry – either oddness
[
21
]
(considered nonphysical), or evenness (considered physical). Furthermore, the vast majority of them are based on tools from nonlinear functional analysis, in particular Crandall–Rabinowitz type arguments for the local analysis, and the so-called Rabinowitz alternative for the global analysis. The most comprehensive paper up to date is
[
5
]
, which contains explicit criteria for the existence of both continuous and discontinuous phase transitions based on the Fourier coefficients of the interaction potential for quite general interactions by exploiting the symmetry in the problem. Applications include the Kuramoto model, the Hegselmann–Krausse model for opinion dynamics, the Keller–Segel model for bacterial chemotaxis, the Onsager model for liquid crystal alignment, and the Barré–Degond–Zatorska model for interacting dynamical networks.

In
[
8
]
, P. Constantin and J. V. considered the phase transition for the McKean-Vlasov equation for the Kuramoto interaction,
W(\theta)=-\cos\theta
and proved that at
\mu_{c}=2
, equation (
1.3
) undergoes a continuous, global phase transition: for
\mu\leq\mu_{c}
, the incoherent state is the unique solution, and for
\mu>\mu_{c}
, there is an additional coherent solution, unique up to rotation.
‘Continuous’
refers to the fact that the coherent state emanates from the incoherent state through a pitchfork bifurcation for the order parameter at the bifurcation point, and
‘global’
to the fact that the solution persists for all
\mu>\mu_{c}
.

The point of view that only even interaction potentials are physical, and the fact that most tools in existence do not apply directly resulted in leaving interactions lacking symmetry without rigorous mathematical treatment. But as we argued above, in phase reduced models one has to account for many competing processes, and symmetry cannot be expected in realistic neurobiological settings. One of the most celebrated phase-reduced models is the Hodgkin-Huxley model that was proposed to describe the propagation of action potentials in excitable cells that include neurons, cardiac myocytes, certain endocrine cells, and even some plant cells
[
13
]
. Hansel et al.
[
11
]
computed numerically the phase reduction, the phase response curve for the synaptic coupling, and the convolution integral (
1.1
), to arrive at a three-mode truncation of the Fourier series for the PIF
\Gamma_{ij}
. In
[
12
]
, Hansel et al. proposed a reduction to a two-mode PIF. In general, this leads us to the study of two-mode interaction:

(1.4)

W(\theta)=-(\cos(\theta-\alpha_{1})-\varepsilon\cos 2(\theta-\alpha_{2})).

That led the author to consider
[
25
]
the McKean-Vlasov equation with the two-mode interaction potential,

W(\theta)=-(\cos\theta-\varepsilon\cos 2(\theta-\alpha)),

with two parameters,
\varepsilon>0
that modifies the strength of the symmetry-breaking mode, and
\alpha\in[0,\pi/2)
that de-phases the relative position of the two modes. The case
\alpha\in[0,\pi/4)
renders the second mode repulsive, hence the two modes competitive, while
\alpha\in(\pi/4,\pi/2]
renders both modes attractive and hence cooperative. For the asymmetric case
\alpha\in(0,\pi/2)
, the author was only able to rule out coherent steady states, leaving the question of the transition dynamics fully open. For the symmetric case
\alpha=0
, it was proven that the presence of the repulsive second mode has no qualitative dynamical effect: the system undergoes a continuous global transition at
\mu_{c}=2
. For
\alpha=\frac{\pi}{2}
, both modes are due to a ferromagnetic interaction and favor in-phase locking. This competition introduces much more dynamical complexity, and the system undergoes further bifurcations with respect to not only
\mu
but also
\varepsilon
. The M. Lucia and J. V.
[
15
]
proved that in the case
0<\varepsilon<1/4
the stationary solution undergoes two continuous global continuous phase transitions, at
\mu_{c}^{(1)}=2
and
\mu_{c}^{(2)}=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}
, and no discontinuous phase transitions. For
\varepsilon>1/4
, the picture is further complicated by the fact that discontinuous phase transitions are also possible.

The paper at hand tackles the simplest possible asymmetric extension, the Sakaguchi-Kuramoto interaction
W(\theta)=-\cos(\theta-\alpha)
and extends the result of
[
8
]
with a remarkably similar conclusion: at
\mu_{c}=2\sec\alpha
,
\alpha\in(0,\pi)
, equation (
1.3
) undergoes a continuous, global phase transition from incoherence to traveling wave coherence: for
\mu\leq\mu_{c}
, the incoherent state is the unique solution, while for
\mu>\mu_{c}
, there is an additional unique coherent solution in form of a traveling wave. As in the works
[
15
,
25
]
, the author makes use of the fact that for interaction potentials with a finite number of Fourier modes, the stationary and the traveling wave counterparts of equation (
1.3
) allow for a finite-dimensional reduction rendering them more tractable. The treatment of thereof, however, requires asymmetrical extension of the von Mises distribution and the modified Bessel functions, rendering the mathematical analysis much less trivial.

2.
Stationary and traveling-waves McKean-Vlasov equations

Setting for convenince
D=1
, we rewrite (
1.3
) as

(2.1)

\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{\theta}\left(\rho\partial_{\theta}\xi\right)=0,

where

\xi=-\log\rho-V,\ \ \text{and}\ \ V=\mu W\star\rho.

Additionally, we introduce the nonlinear nonlocal transformation

\psi=\exp(-\xi)=\rho\exp(\mu W\star\rho).

The stationary (steady-state) equation then reads

-\rho\partial_{\theta}\xi=c,

for some constant
c
. Rewritten in terms of
\psi
, it becomes

\partial_{\theta}\psi=c\exp(\mu W\star\rho).

Periodicity implies
c=0
, so
\psi=Z^{-1}
for some constant
Z>0
, thus yielding the following stationary McKean-Vlasov equation (SMVE)

(2.2)

\displaystyle\rho=Z^{-1}\exp(-\mu W\star\rho),

\displaystyle Z=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp(-\mu W\star\rho)\ d\theta.

In order to derive the equation for finding the traveling waves (TWMVE), we set out from the standard ansatz
\rho=\rho(\theta-kt)
. Upon substituting it into (
2.1
), we obtain

\partial_{\theta}(k\rho-\rho\partial_{\theta}\xi)=0,

hence TWMVE becomes

(2.3)

\partial_{\theta}\rho+(\mu W^{\prime}\star\rho+k)\rho=c,

subject to normalization

\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\rho(\theta)\ d\theta=1,

for some to be determined constants
k
and
c
. Rewritten in terms of
\psi
, equation (
2.3
) reads

(2.4)

\partial_{\theta}\psi+k\psi=c\exp(\mu W\star\rho).

3.
Finite-dimensional reduction

Equations (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) are nonlinear and nonlocal differential-integral equations. For interaction potentials with a finite number of Fourier modes, they allow for a finite-dimensional reduction rendering them more tractable. Here, we consider only the one-mode interaction potential with a phase shift
\alpha\in[0,\pi]
,
W(\theta)=-\cos(\theta-\alpha)
.

For a given PDF
\rho
on the circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we introduce the notation for averages

\left<f\right>_{\rho}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\theta)\rho(\theta)\ d\theta
. Further, let us introduce introduce the order parameter,
r_{0}
, measuring the degree of coherence of
\rho
, together with the average phase,
\theta_{0}\in[0,2\pi)
, implicitly via the equation

r_{0}e^{\theta_{0}i}=\left<e^{\theta i}\right>_{\rho}.

Introducing the notation
r=\mu r_{0}
, we can rewrite it as

r=\mu\left<e^{(\theta-\theta_{0})i}\right>_{\rho}.

Equating the real and imaginary parts, we have

(3.1)

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\mu\left<\cos(\theta-\theta_{0})\right>_{\rho}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left<\sin(\theta-\theta_{0})\right>_{\rho}.

The potential
V
can then be rewritten as

\displaystyle V(\theta)=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(\theta-\theta_{0}+\theta_{0}-\tilde{\theta}-\alpha)\rho(\tilde{\theta})\ d\tilde{\theta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mu[\cos(\theta-\theta_{0}-\alpha)\left<\cos(\theta-\theta_{0})\right>_{\rho}+\sin(\theta-\theta_{0}-\alpha)\left<\sin(\theta-\theta_{0})\right>_{\rho}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-r\cos(\theta-\theta_{0}-\alpha).

Introducing
\phi=\theta-\theta_{0}-\alpha
, (
3.1
) becomes

(3.2)

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\mu\left(\cos\alpha\left<\cos\phi\right>_{\rho}-\sin\alpha\left<\sin\phi\right>_{\rho}\right)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\cos\alpha\left<\sin\phi\right>_{\rho}+\sin\alpha\left<\cos\phi\right>_{\rho}.

The stationary state equation (
2.2
) then becomes

(3.3)

\displaystyle\rho(\theta)

\displaystyle=Z^{-1}\exp(r\cos\phi)

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\exp(r\cos\phi)\ d\phi,

where
r
is to be determined from (
3.1
). Rewritten in terms of modified Bessel functions, it reads

(3.4)

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\mu\cos\alpha\frac{I_{1}(r)}{I_{0}(r)}

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sin\alpha I_{1}(r).

This system is overdetermined: the second equation is only satisfied if
\alpha=0
,
\alpha=\pi
, or
r=0
(leading to the trivial solution
\rho=1/(2\pi)
). Therefore, for
\alpha\in(0,\pi)
the only possible stationary solution is the trivial one, while for
\alpha=0
(or
\alpha=\pi
), nontrivial solutions are obtained by solving the equation

\frac{\sec\alpha}{\mu}=\mathcal{C}_{1}(r):=\frac{I_{1}(r)}{rI_{0}(r)}.

It is well known
[
8
,
15
]
that the function
\mathcal{C}_{1}:[0,\infty)\to(0,\frac{1}{2}]
is a decreasing bijection. Hence, for
\alpha=0
and
\mu>2
(or
\alpha=\pi
and
\mu<-2
), the above equation has a unique pair of solutions
\pm r
with
r=\mathcal{C}_{1}^{-1}(1/|\mu|)
, and the steady state PDF is obtained in the form of a von Mises PDF,

\rho(\theta)=\frac{e^{r\cos(\theta-\theta_{0})}}{2\pi I_{0}(r)},

which is unique up to the choice of
\theta_{0}
.

The traveling-wave equation (
2.3
), on the other hand, becomes

(3.5)

\partial_{\phi}\rho+r\sin\phi\rho+k\rho=c

subject to normalization
\left<1\right>_{\rho}=1
. Rewritten in terms of
\psi
it becomes

(3.6)

\partial_{\phi}\psi+k\psi=c\exp(-r\cos\phi).

Equation (
3.5
), in addition to the unknown function
\rho
, involves three unknown parameters
r
,
k
and
c
. It turns out that the equation can be reduced to two equations in unknowns
r
and
k
involving an asymmetrical extension of the modified Bessel functions. The PDF
\rho
is then found in the form of an asymmetrical extension of the von Mises PDF that is completely determined by
r
and
k
. The constant
c
is subsequently determined from the normalization via the formula

c=\frac{1}{2\pi}(r\left<\sin\phi\right>_{\rho}+k).

4.
Asymmetrical extension of the von Mises distribution and the modified Bessel functions

For given
k,r\not=0
, let
\chi=\chi_{k,r}
be the (unique) solution of the equation

(4.1)

\frac{d\chi}{d\phi}+(r\sin\phi+k)\chi=k.

This solution can be written in terms of the modified Bessel functions. The function
\psi=\chi\exp(-r\cos\phi)
satisfies

(4.2)

\frac{d\psi}{d\phi}+k\psi=k\exp(-r\cos\phi),

and its Fourier coefficients,

a_{n}(k,r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\psi(\phi)\cos n\phi\ d\phi,\ \ b_{n}(k,r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\psi(\phi)\sin n\phi\ d\phi,\ n\in\mathbb{Z}

are easily computed as

a_{n}(k,r)=\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}+n^{2}}I_{n}(-r),\ \ b_{n}(k,r)=\frac{kn}{k^{2}+n^{2}}I_{n}(-r).

Here,

(4.3)

I_{n}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos n\phi\exp(r\cos\phi)\ d\phi,\ n\in\mathbb{Z},\ \ r\in\mathbb{R}

is the modified Bessel function. Therefore,

(4.4)

\chi_{k,r}(\phi)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big[\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}+n^{2}}(-1)^{n}I_{n}(r)\cos n\phi+\frac{nk}{k^{2}+n^{2}}(-1)^{n}I_{n}(r)\sin n\phi\Big]\exp(r\cos\phi).

We extend this formula continuously to include the traces
\chi_{0,r}(\phi)=I_{0}(r)\exp(r\cos\phi)
, and
\chi_{k,0}(\phi)\equiv 1
.

Definition 4.1
.

For given
k,r\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, let

C_{n}(k,r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos(n\phi)\chi_{k,r}(\phi)\ d\phi,\ \ S_{n}(k,r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin(n\phi)\chi_{k,r}(\phi)\ d\phi,

and let

X_{n}(k,r)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}C_{n}(k,r)\\
S_{n}(k,r)\end{array}\right].

For given
k,r\in\mathbb{R}
we refer to

\rho_{k,r}(\phi)=\frac{\chi_{k,r}(\phi)}{2\pi C_{0}(k,r)}

as the asymmetrically extended von Mises PDF (AvMPDF).

Let us ulso introduce functions

\mathcal{C}_{n}(k,r)=\frac{C_{n}(k,r)}{rC_{0}(k,r)},\ \ \mathcal{S}_{n}(k,r)=-\frac{S_{n}(k,r)}{rC_{0}(k,r)},

and the map

\mathcal{X}_{n}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\owns(k,r)\mapsto\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{C}_{n}(k,r)\\
\mathcal{S}_{n}(k,r)\end{array}\right].

Further let

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}(k,r)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n}(k,r)}{\mathcal{C}_{n}(k,r)},

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{n}(k,r)

\displaystyle:=\mbox{sign}(k)\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{n}(k,r)^{2}+\mathcal{S}_{n}(k,r)^{2}}.

It is easy to see that
\rho_{k,r}
satisfies (
3.5
) if and only if
k
and
r
satisfy

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}(k,r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\cos\alpha}{\mu}

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}(k,r)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin\alpha}{\mu},

or, equivalently,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}(k,r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{1}(k,r)

\displaystyle=\tan\alpha.

5.
The statement of the main result

Now we are able to state the main result regarding the phase transition from incoherence towards coherence, which in the case of the phase shift rendering the problem asymmetrical manifests itself through traveling waves. The proof, however, will require establishing some properties of asymmetrically extended modified Bessel functions.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider TWMVE, (
2.3
), associated with the one-mode interaction potential with a phase shift
\alpha\in[0,\pi]
,

(5.1)

W(\theta)=-\cos(\theta-\alpha).

For
\alpha\in[0,\pi/2)
(or
\alpha\in(\pi/2,\pi])
), the system (
4.6
) undergoes a supercritical (or subcritical) pitchfork bifurcation in
\mu
at the bifurcation point
\mu_{\alpha}=2\sec\alpha
. For
\mu\leq\mu_{\alpha}
(or
\mu\geq\mu_{\alpha}
), the trivial solution
r=0
is the unique solution, while for
\mu>\mu_{\alpha}
(or
\mu<\mu_{\alpha}
), an additional pair
(\mu,k_{\alpha}(\mu),\pm r_{\alpha}(\mu))
of solutions emanates from the bifurcation point
(\mu,k_{\alpha}(\mu_{\alpha}),\pm r_{\alpha}(\mu_{\alpha}))=(2\sec\alpha,\tan\alpha,0)
. This bifurcation diagram is global in the sense that the curve

\Gamma_{\alpha}=\{(k_{\alpha}(\mu),\pm r_{\alpha}(\mu))\ \big|\ \mu\geq\mu_{\alpha}(\mbox{or}\ \mu\leq\mu_{\alpha})\}

can also be parametrized by means of an increasing (or decreasing) bijection
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}:[0,\infty)\to[\tan\alpha,\infty)
(or
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}:[0,\infty)\to(-\infty,\tan\alpha]
):

\Gamma_{\alpha}=\{(\mathcal{K}_{\alpha}(r),\pm r)\ \big|\ r\geq 0\},

exhausting all possible nontrivial solutions.

For
\alpha=\pi/2
, the trivial solution is unique regardless of
\mu
.

Consequently, equation (
2.3
) undergoes a phase transition at the point
\mu_{\alpha}=2\sec\alpha
. More precisely, for
\alpha\in(0,\pi/2)
(or
\alpha\in(\pi/2,\pi)
), whenever
\mu\leq\mu_{\alpha}
(or
\mu\geq\mu_{\alpha}
), the incoherent (unsynchronized) state
\rho_{\infty}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}
is the unique solution. For
\mu>\mu_{\alpha}
(or
\mu<\mu_{\alpha}
), there exists an additional coherent traveling-wave solution

\rho(t,\theta)=\rho_{k_{\alpha}(\mu),r_{\alpha}(\mu)}(\theta-\theta_{0}-\alpha-k_{\alpha}(\mu)t),

which is unique up to the arbitrary choice of
\theta_{0}\in[0,2\pi)
. For the speed of the traveling wave, we have
k_{\alpha}(\mu)>\tan\alpha>0
(
k_{\alpha}(\mu)<\tan\alpha<0
). The PDF
\rho_{k(\mu),r(\mu)}
is the asymmetrically extended von Mises probability density function corresponding to the speed
k_{\alpha}(\mu)
, and
r_{\alpha}(\mu)
. Furthermore, for
\alpha\in(0,\pi/2)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\to\mu_{\alpha}^{-}}k_{\alpha}(\mu)=\tan\alpha

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to\pi/2}k_{\alpha}(\mu)=\infty

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\to\infty}k_{\alpha}(\mu)=\infty

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\to\infty}r_{\alpha}(\mu)=\infty.

The constant
c
is then given by the formula
c=\frac{k}{2\pi C_{0}(k,r)}
.

For
\alpha=0
(or
\alpha=\pi
), equation (
2.3
) undergoes a phase transition at the point
\mu=2
(
\mu=-2
) to a coherent steady-state
\rho(\theta)=\rho_{k_{\alpha}(\mu),r_{\alpha}(\mu)}(\theta-\theta_{0})
. For
\alpha=\pi/2
, the incoherent state is unique regardless of
\mu
.

6.
Properties of the asymmetrically extended modified Bessel functions

In the following proposition, we summarize some important properties of the modified Bessel functions of the first kind
I_{n}
. (See
[
3
,
15
]
, and references therein).

Proposition 6.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, consider the second-order modified Bessel differential equation

(6.1)

x^{2}y^{\prime\prime}+xy^{\prime}-(x^{2}+n^{2})y=0.

(1)

Functions
I_{n}
with the integral representation (
4.3
) are solutions of (
6.1
) of the first kind, and they have the power series representation

I_{n}(r)=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{p!(n+p)!}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{n+2p},

while their squares have the power series representation

(6.2)

I_{n}(r)^{2}=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{((n+p)!)^{2}}{{2n+2p}\choose{2n+p}}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2n+2p}.

In particular,

(6.3)

I_{0}(r)^{2}=\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2p)!}{(p!)^{4}}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2p}.

(2)

We have the following symmetry relations:

I_{-n}(r)=I_{n}(r)

and

I_{n}(-r)=(-1)^{n}I_{n}(r).

(3)

We have the following two recursion formulas:

(6.4)

2nI_{n}^{\prime}(r)=I_{n-1}(r)+I_{n+1}(r),

and

(6.5)

\frac{2nI_{n}(r)}{r}=I_{n-1}(r)-I_{n+1}(r).

(4)

The following inequality due to Soni holds:

0<I_{n+1}(r)<I_{n}(r),\ \text{for}\ r>0,\ \text{and}\ \ n=0,1,2,\dots.

(5)

The function
g(r,t)=\exp\left(\frac{r}{2}(t+t^{-1})\right)
is the generating function for modified Bessel functions of the first kind. More specifically, for
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
,

g(r,t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{n}(r)t^{n}.

(6)

We have

\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{n}(r)t^{n}\right)\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{n}(r)(-1)^{n}t^{-n}\right)=1.

In particular,

(6.6)

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n}I_{n}(r)^{2}=1.

(7)

The following so-called Jacobi-Anger expansion holds for for
r,\theta\in\mathbb{R}

exp(r\cos\theta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{n}(r)\cos n\theta.

We use this proposition to prove the following:

Lemma 6.2
.

(1)

For
k,r\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have the following recursion formula:

(6.7)

A(k,n)X_{n}(k,r)=-\frac{r}{2}(X_{n-1}(k,r)^{\perp}-X_{n+1}(k,r)^{\perp}),

where

A(k,n)=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}k&n\\
-n&k\end{array}\right],

together with the standard notation

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a\\
b\end{array}\right]^{\perp}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-b\\
a\end{array}\right].

(2)

For
k,r\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathcal{C}_{1}(k,r)=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n\mathcal{R}_{n}(k,r)^{2},

and

\mathcal{S}_{1}(k,r)=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}k(-1)^{n+1}\mathcal{R}_{n}(k,r)^{2}.

(3)

We have

(6.8)

\displaystyle C_{0}(k,r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}+n^{2}}(-1)^{n}I_{n}(r)^{2}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}+n^{2}}(-1)^{n+1}I_{n}(r)^{2},

with traces
C_{0}(k,0)=1
and
C_{0}(0,r)=I_{0}(r)^{2}
. Furthermore, we have the following power series expansion,

(6.9)

C_{0}(k,r)=1+\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}a_{k,p}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2p},

with

a_{k,p}=\frac{1}{(p!)^{2}}\sum_{n=-p}^{p}(-1)^{n+1}\frac{n^{2}}{n^{2}+k^{2}}{{2p}\choose{p+n}},\ p=1,2,\dots.

(4)

We define

(6.10)

\eta_{p,j}(k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos^{2p}\left(\theta/2\right)\theta^{j}\cosh(k\theta)d\theta,&j\ \mbox{even}\\
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos^{2p}\left(\theta/2\right)\theta^{j}\sinh(k\theta)d\theta,&j\ \mbox{odd}\end{array}\right.

and

(6.11)

\sigma_{p,j}(k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos^{2p-1}\left(\theta/2\right)\sin\left(\theta/2\right)\theta^{j}\sinh(k\theta)d\theta,&j\ \mbox{even}\\
\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos^{2p-1}\left(\theta/2\right)\sin\left(\theta/2\right)\theta^{j}\cosh(k\theta)d\theta,&j\ \mbox{odd}\end{array}\right.

Then for
p=0,1,2,\dots
,

(6.12)

a_{k,p}=\frac{2^{2p-1}}{(p!)^{2}}\frac{k\eta_{p,0}(k)}{\sinh(k\pi)}=\frac{(2p)!}{(p!)^{2}\Pi_{n=1}^{p}(n^{2}+k^{2})}.

(5)

We further have,

(6.13)

\displaystyle C_{1}(k,r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}+n^{2}}(-1)^{n}I_{n}(r)I_{n}^{\prime}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{r}C_{0}(k,r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}pa_{k,p}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2p-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{2\sinh(k\pi)}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}p\eta_{p,0}(k)\frac{r^{2p-1}}{{(p!)^{2}}},

(6.14)

\displaystyle S_{1}(k,r)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{r}\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{n^{2}}{k^{2}+n^{2}}(-1)^{n}I_{n}(r)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{k}{r}\left(C_{0}(k,r)-1\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{k}{2}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}a_{k,p}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{2p-1}.

\displaystyle=-\frac{k}{2\sinh(k\pi)}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}p\sigma_{p,0}(k)\frac{r^{2p-1}}{{(p!)^{2}}},

and

\mathcal{T}_{1}(k,r)=\frac{\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}p\sigma_{p,0}(k)\frac{r^{2p}}{(p!)^{2}}}{\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}p\eta_{p,0}(k)\frac{r^{2p}}{(p!)^{2}}}.

(6)

We have:

(a)

C_{0}(k,r)\geq 1
with the equality holding for
r=0
;
C_{0}
is even with respect to both
k
and
r
.

(b)

C_{1}(k,r)\geq 0
(
\leq 0
) for
r\geq 0
(
r\leq 0
), with equality holding for
r=0
;
C_{1}
is even with respect to
k
and odd with respect to
r
.

(c)

S_{1}(k,r)\leq 0
(
\geq 0
) for
kr\geq 0
(
kr\leq 0
), with the equality holding for
kr=0
.
S_{1}
is odd with respect to both
k
and
r
.

(d)

\mathcal{T}_{1}
is odd with respect to
k
and even with respect to
r
.

Proof.

(1)

follows by taking the Fourier transform in (
4.1
), integrating by parts, and employing trigonometric addition formulas.

(2)

follows by taking the dot and the cross product with
X_{n}
, respectively, in equation (
6.7
), and then evaluating the telescoping series.

(3)

C_{0}(k,r)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\chi_{k,r}(\phi)\ d\phi

) yield (
6.8

6.9
).

(4)

In order to arrive at the integral representation (
6.12
) for
a_{k,p}
, we also introduce

b_{k,p}=\frac{1}{(p!)^{2}}\sum_{n=-p}^{p}(-1)^{n+1}\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}+k^{2}}{{2p}\choose{p+n}},

and then observe that by virtue of the Binomial Theorem,

a_{k,p}+b_{k,p}=\frac{1}{(p!)^{2}}\sum_{n=-p}^{p}(-1)^{n+1}{{2p}\choose{p+n}}=0.

Recall the integral representation of the binomial coefficients,

{{2p}\choose{p+n}}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{(1+z)^{2p}}{z^{p+n+1}}\ dz.

Hence

b_{k,p}=\frac{1}{(p!)^{2}2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{F_{k,p}(z)}{z^{p+1}},

where

F_{k,p}(z)=(1+z)^{2p}\sum_{n=-p}^{p}(-1)^{n+1}\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}+k^{2}}z^{n}.

By virtue of Laurent’s theorem,
(p!)^{2}b_{k,p}
is the

, which remain the same in

for

Therefore, recalling the Fourier expansion

\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}+k^{2}}e^{in\theta}=\pi k\frac{\cosh(k(\pi-|\theta|))}{\sinh(k\pi)},\theta\in[-\pi,\pi],

we obtain

\displaystyle b_{k,p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{(p!)^{2}2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(1+e^{i\theta})^{2p}e^{-ip\theta}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{k^{2}}{n^{2}+k^{2}}e^{n(\pi+\theta)i}\ d\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2^{2p-1}}{(p!)^{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos^{2p}\left(\theta/2\right)\frac{k\cosh(k\theta)}{\sinh(k\pi)}\ d\theta,

and the expression for

follows. Integration by parts shows that

satisfies the recursion relation

(6.15)

\eta_{p,0}(k)=\frac{2p(2p-1)}{4(p^{2}+k^{2})}\eta_{p-1,0}(k),

so

\eta_{p,0}(k)=\frac{(2p)!}{4^{p}\Pi_{n=1}^{p}(p^{2}+k^{2})}\frac{2\sinh(k\pi)}{k},

and (
6.12
) follows.

(5)

The relations (

(6)

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
f

be two entire functions with real and nonnegative coefficients in their power series expansions, that is, let
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}x^{n_{k}}
and
g(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}b_{k}x^{n_{k}}
, where
\{n_{k}\}
is an increasing sequence of nonnegative integers,
a_{k},b_{k}>0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. If the sequence
\{a_{k}/b_{k}\}
is strictly decreasing towards zero, then the function
x\mapsto f(x)/g(x)
is also strictly decreasing towards zero on
(0,\infty)
.

.

The function

7.1.
Proof of the main result

Let
\alpha\in[0,\pi/2)
. The case
\alpha\in(\pi/2,\pi]
will follow by virtue of the invariance of the problem under
(\mu,\alpha)\mapsto(-\mu,\pi-\alpha)
. The case
\alpha=\pi/2
is obvious as the first equation (
4.6
) only has the trivial solution
r=0
.

The bifurcation problem for the system (
4.6
) can be recast as the optimization problem for the function
\mathcal{R}_{1}(k,r)
subject to the constraint
\mathcal{T}_{1}(k,r)=\tan\alpha
, that is on the curve

\Gamma_{\tan\alpha}=\{(k,r)\ \Big|\ \mathcal{T}_{1}(k,r)=\tan\alpha\}.

The lack of horizontal or vertical asymptotes for the graph is justified by the limits (
7.1
) and (
7.3
). From (
7.5
), we easily infer

\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}\partial_{k}\mathcal{R}_{1}(k,r)&\partial_{r}\mathcal{R}_{1}(k,r)\\
\partial_{k}\mathcal{T}_{1}(k,r)&\partial_{r}\mathcal{T}_{1}(k,r)\end{array}\right|=0\ \ \ \text{if and only if}\ \ r=0.

Therefore, the global maximum for the problem is attained at
(k,r)=(\tan\alpha,0)
and is given by
\mathcal{R}_{1}(\tan\alpha,0)=\frac{\cos\alpha}{2}
. The bifurcation point for the original bifurcation problem is
\mu_{\alpha}=2\sec\alpha
.
