Fatal Tension Pneumocephalus Associated with Central Nervous System Infection Caused by an Extended-Spectrum β-Lactamase-Producing Klebsiella pneumoniae Harboring Hypervirulence-Associated Genes

Background

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a Gram-negative bacterium commonly found in the environment and on the mucosal surfaces of living organisms. Infections caused by K. pneumoniae harboring hypervirulence-associated genes frequently present with multi-site or metastatic dissemination. It is a major cause of hospital-acquired pneumonia1,2 and a significant risk factor for severe community-acquired pneumonia,3 as well as other infections, including liver abscesses and meningitis.4 While central nervous system (CNS) involvement is increasingly documented, it predominantly arises secondary to metastatic seeding from an established extracranial focus.5 Cases of K. pneumoniae meningoencephalitis lacking an overt extracranial source remain uncommon. Furthermore, the spontaneous development of tension pneumocephalus in this setting is exceptionally rare, particularly in the absence of preceding neurotrauma, recent neurosurgical intervention, or occult skull base defects.6

Although hypervirulence-associated K. pneumoniae is well recognized for its capacity to cause systemic infection, intracranial infections complicated by pneumocephalus are exceedingly rare. Here, we describe a fatal case of fulminant meningoencephalitis and subsequent tension pneumocephalus caused by an extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing K. pneumoniae isolate harboring hypervirulence-associated genes in a patient with polycystic liver and kidney disease. The aim of this report is to describe the aggressive clinical and radiological trajectory of this CNS infection and to highlight the value of integrating molecular diagnostics for early recognition of hypervirulence-associated and antimicrobial-resistant K. pneumoniae phenotypes.

Case Presentation

A 49-year-old female was admitted to our intensive care unit (ICU) with fever and a rapidly progressive decline in consciousness. Her past medical history was notable for a 40-year diagnosis of polycystic liver and kidney disease, which remained structurally present upon admission. Furthermore, she reported chronic livestock exposure and a history of oral minocycline therapy for brucellosis lasting over a year; however, this treatment had been discontinued prior to the current presentation. The trajectory of her present illness began six months prior with persistent, generalized headaches. Four days preceding admission, she developed a new-onset fever, which was initially self-managed with loxoprofen. Eight hours before transfer to our facility, her neurological status acutely deteriorated, characterized by sudden confusion and agitation. An initial non-contrast head computed tomography (CT) scan obtained at a local hospital demonstrated bilateral frontal subdural effusions and ethmoid sinusitis, with no overt evidence of cerebral edema. However, during inter-hospital transfer, she precipitously progressed into a deep coma accompanied by respiratory arrest, necessitating emergent endotracheal intubation. Upon ICU admission, she was hemodynamically unstable, requiring continuous norepinephrine infusion to sustain the mean arterial pressure. Initial neurological examination revealed a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 3T, bilaterally dilated pupils (4.5 mm) unreactive to light, and positive nuchal rigidity. Given the patient’s profound hemodynamic instability and rapid progression to a comatose state, advanced abdominal imaging, such as contrast-enhanced CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), could not be safely performed upon admission to definitively exclude an infected hepatic cyst or occult liver abscess.

Initial laboratory investigations (Table 1) revealed a pronounced systemic inflammatory response coupled with profound thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy. Key peripheral blood indices included a white blood cell count of 17.03 × 109/L, a platelet count of 5 × 109/L, and a fibrinogen level of 7.53 g/L. Systemic inflammatory markers were markedly elevated, with a C-reactive protein of 28.4 mg/dL and a procalcitonin of 32.22 ng/mL. Concurrently, an absolute lymphocyte count of 0.26 × 109/L and an immunoglobulin G level of 501 mg/dL indicated a significantly compromised baseline immune status. A repeat non-contrast head CT obtained upon admission demonstrated diffuse cerebral, cerebellar, and brainstem edema, characterized by the effacement of cerebral sulci, compression of the lateral ventricles, and the loss of gray-white matter differentiation (Figure 1A). Electroencephalography revealed widespread, persistent low-voltage activity. Given the compelling signs of a central nervous system infection, an urgent lumbar puncture was performed. The procedure recorded an extreme opening pressure of 780 mmH2O, yielding yellowish, turbid cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (Figure 1B). CSF biochemical and cytological analyses confirmed a severe purulent profile: profound pleocytosis (15,950 × 106/L leukocytes), extreme hypoglycorrhachia (glucose, 0.1 mmol/L), and elevated protein (3.57 g/L). Gram staining of the CSF sediment identified the presence of filamentous Gram-negative bacilli.

Based on the preliminary CSF findings, empirical antimicrobial therapy with intravenous meropenem (2 g every 6 hours) was initiated promptly. A comprehensive neuroprotective strategy was simultaneously deployed to manage the severe intracranial hypertension, encompassing deep sedation, targeted temperature management, and aggressive osmotic therapy, alongside mechanical ventilation and vasopressor support. Due to the continued worsening of acute kidney injury, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated on the 3rd day of hospitalization. A lumbar puncture performed on the 3rd day showed that the cerebrospinal fluid pressure was still high (560 mmH2O) and there was significant bloody cerebrospinal fluid, reflecting ongoing intracranial infection. Despite these aggressive interventions, the patient’s neurological status steadily declined, culminating in the loss of brainstem reflexes by day 5. A repeat non-contrast head CT on day 6 unveiled a striking radiological progression: worsening diffuse cerebral edema accompanied by the de novo appearance of extensive, multifocal pneumocephalus and intracranial hemorrhage, with no overt evidence of an external structural breach (Figure 1C). The clinical course was ultimately characterized by refractory septic shock and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, leading to the patient’s death on day 7 of hospitalization.

Microbiological Characterization

Upon ICU admission (day 1), prior to the initiation of empirical antimicrobial therapy, two sets of peripheral blood cultures (aerobic and fungal) alongside a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) culture were obtained. Both the initial blood culture (with a rapid time-to-positivity of 7 hours) and the CSF culture yielded K. pneumoniae. To monitor pathogen clearance, follow-up cultures were subsequently drawn; the repeated CSF culture on day 3 and blood cultures on day 4 both returned negative. Species identification of the initial isolates was achieved utilizing matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Quantitative antimicrobial susceptibility testing, conducted via the VITEK 2 automated system and interpreted according to contemporary Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) or European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines, confirmed the isolate as an extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) producer (ESBL test: positive). The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values demonstrated high-level resistance to advanced-generation cephalosporins (eg, ceftriaxone MIC ≥ 64 μg/mL) and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (MIC ≥ 320 μg/mL), while retaining in vitro susceptibility to carbapenems (eg, imipenem MIC ≤ 0.25 μg/mL) (Table 2). Notably, macroscopic assessment of the culture plates revealed a distinct hypermucoviscous colony morphology. The string test was positive, indicating a hypermucoviscous phenotype that has been associated with hypervirulence in K. pneumoniae, although it is not by itself definitive for hvKP classification.7

To delineate the genetic determinants underlying this aggressive phenotype, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) was performed on the admission CSF sample. Total nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) were extracted utilizing the GenK Magnetic Bead-based DNA/RNA Extraction Kit on a GensLab Rhea Automatic Nucleic Acid Extraction System. Library preparation was conducted using the GenK Pathogen Microbial DNA/RNA Library Preparation Kits, and sequencing was executed on the GensKey M Sequencing Platform. The analysis revealed robust sequencing metrics, with 39,077 specific sequence reads mapped to K. pneumoniae, representing a relative abundance of 96.93% and an overall genome coverage of 46.15% (Figure 2). More importantly, bioinformatic interrogation against the predefined clinical targeted database identified a distinct hypervirulence-associated genetic repertoire. The detection of the rmpA (regulator of mucoid phenotype A) gene mechanistically corroborates the capsular overproduction observed in vitro via the string test. Concurrent identification of iucA and iroB signifies the capacity for enhanced siderophore biosynthesis—specifically aerobactin and salmochelin—which are pivotal for bacterial acquisition of iron and successful systemic dissemination.8 In addition to these virulence factors, the resistome profile unveiled multiple antimicrobial resistance determinants, notably CTX-M-15-like and AAC(6′)-Ib-cr, alongside sul1, sul2, and dfrA27. Together, these findings suggest an ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae strain with multidrug resistance determinants and a hypervirulence-associated molecular profile.9

Discussion

In this report, we describe a rare and severe presentation of fulminant CNS infection complicated by the rapid evolution of tension pneumocephalus. Although K. pneumoniae has been increasingly implicated in adult bacterial meningitis, the vast majority of these infections occur via metastatic dissemination from a distinct extracranial focus, characteristically a pyogenic liver abscess.10 Given our patient’s 40-year history of polycystic liver disease and the rapid positivity of peripheral blood cultures upon admission, the possibility of an occult infected hepatic cyst serving as a source for hematogenous seeding cannot be definitively excluded. However, due to the patient’s extreme hemodynamic instability and precipitous neurological decline, advanced abdominal contrast-enhanced imaging was not clinically feasible. Therefore, rather than definitively classifying it as an absolute primary meningoencephalitis, this case is most accurately characterized as a CNS infection without an overt extracranial focus. Following the intracranial infection, the patient spontaneously developed a massive amount of intracranial gas, without any prior neurotrauma, recent neurosurgery, or obvious anatomical abnormalities, which eventually progressed to life-threatening tension pneumocephalus.11 By triangulating conventional phenotypic evidence, notably a positive string test, with the mNGS detection of key hypervirulence-associated genetic loci, we classify the isolate as an ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae harboring hypervirulence-associated genes, consistent with a probable hypervirulent phenotype. Consequently, we frame this clinical case as hypervirulence-associated K. pneumoniae meningoencephalitis complicated by tension pneumocephalus, while recognizing that the mechanism of intracranial gas formation remains uncertain. Despite maximal supportive care, the clinical outcome remained inexorably fatal.

In characterizing this isolate, we observed supportive genotype-phenotype convergence. The mNGS detection of the rmpA gene provides a direct mechanistic basis for the hypermucoviscous colony morphology observed in vitro.12 Concurrently, the identification of iucA and iroB supports the presence of molecular determinants related to enhanced siderophore (aerobactin and salmochelin) biosynthesis, which is critical for successful systemic dissemination.13 Notably, the coexistence of these hypervirulence loci with established resistance determinants (CTX-M-15-like and AAC(6′)-Ib-cr) is compatible with a convergent K. pneumoniae phenotype, in which antimicrobial resistance determinants coexist with hypervirulence-associated loci.14 Nevertheless, the boundaries of our microbiological evidence must be explicitly acknowledged. Because whole-genome sequencing (WGS), multi-locus sequence typing (MLST), and definitive capsular serotyping were not performed, absolute clonal classification is unattainable. Therefore, we classify this isolate as a “probable” hvKP, or an ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae harboring hypervirulence-associated genes.

Compared to previously reported cases of K. pneumoniae-associated pneumocephalus, which predominantly occur secondary to neurotrauma, surgical interventions, or mixed polymicrobial infections,6,11 the spontaneous development of tension pneumocephalus driven by a monomicrobial convergent K. pneumoniae strain remains exceptionally rare. Furthermore, classic hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (hvKP) isolates typically retain susceptibility to first-line antimicrobial agents.13 In contrast, the coexistence of ESBL production (mediated by CTX-M-15-like) in our isolate severely limited empirical therapeutic options. Recent epidemiological observations have shown that multidrug-resistant and highly virulent Klebsiella pneumoniae strains are significantly more invasive and have a higher mortality rate than classic hvKP or conventionally resistant strains.15 This factor may have accelerated the rapid deterioration of clinical condition observed in our case.

Tension pneumocephalus is characterized by the progressive accumulation of intracranial gas, which generates a significant mass effect that leads to sulcal effacement, ventricular compression, and ultimately, brain herniation.11 Although gas-producing intracranial infections are predominantly associated with obligate anaerobes or a mixed synergistic microbiota, K. pneumoniae metabolism may represent one plausible intrinsic contributor to intracranial gas production. In a hypoxic and necrotic microenvironment, specific K. pneumoniae strains can undergo mixed-acid fermentation, producing carbon dioxide and hydrogen.6 However, without direct gas composition analysis or histopathological confirmation, it is premature to attribute the pneumocephalus exclusively to bacterial metabolism. Therefore, bacterial gas production should be interpreted as a possible contributor rather than the sole explanation for the pneumocephalus observed in this case. Consequently, a broad and rigorous differential diagnosis remains essential. First, occult anatomical defects must be meticulously evaluated. Although serial CT imaging did not reveal a definitive skull base fracture or sinus-intracranial fistula, the patient’s history of ethmoid sinusitis suggests the potential for microperforations, which could serve as a conduit for air infiltration. Second, iatrogenic factors warrant consideration. Performing a lumbar puncture in the setting of intracranial hypertension, particularly with cerebrospinal fluid drainage, can precipitate a negative pressure gradient. This gradient may draw air into the cranial vault through undetected dural tears or spinal defects.16,17 Finally, despite negative anaerobic culture results, occult co-infection with gas-producing anaerobes cannot be definitively excluded due to the inherent limitations of standard culture methodologies. Therefore, the progression to tension pneumocephalus in this patient is likely a multifactorial process rather than an isolated metabolic event.

The patient’s past medical history also increases susceptibility to this disease. Structurally, the patient’s long-standing polycystic liver and kidney disease may have compromised systemic pathogen clearance. The extensive cystic replacement of hepatic parenchyma inherently distorts sinusoidal architecture, potentially altering the local microenvironment and impairing normal reticuloendothelial function, thereby potentially increasing susceptibility to transient bacteremia or occult infection.18 The patient’s prolonged minocycline exposure is also worth noting. Beyond its antimicrobial activity, minocycline has immunomodulatory effects, including suppression of microglial activation and attenuation of selected inflammatory pathways. Although these properties are therapeutically beneficial in several neuroinflammatory contexts, whether long-term minocycline exposure contributed to impaired CNS immune surveillance in this patient remains speculative. Therefore, this observation should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than causal.

During treatment, the clinical management was strictly guided by the rapid deterioration of the patient’s condition. We did not perform any local central nervous system interventions, including intrathecal antimicrobial therapy or external ventricular drainage. Neurosurgical decompression was not pursued because the tension pneumocephalus occurred in the context of diffuse malignant cerebral edema, profound thrombocytopenia (platelet count of 5 × 109/L), severe coagulopathy, refractory septic shock, and the subsequent loss of brainstem reflexes, making any invasive intervention clinically unfeasible and highly hazardous. Given the patient’s extreme intracranial hypertension (initial opening pressure of 780 mmH2O), any invasive alteration of cerebrospinal fluid dynamics carried an unacceptable risk of precipitating catastrophic brain herniation.19 Consequently, our approach heavily relied on an optimized systemic pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) strategy. We utilized high-dose, extended-infusion intravenous meropenem (2 g every 6 hours) to maximize target attainment and ensure adequate blood-brain barrier penetration. This strategy was rationally selected based on the isolate’s in vitro susceptibility profile and aligns with contemporary IDSA guidelines for managing severe ESBL-producing Gram-negative CNS infections.20 The patient ultimately died from severe intracranial infection and tension pneumocephalus.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant mention. First, as a single-case observation, these findings inherently lack broad generalizability. Second, although phenotypic assays and mNGS provided robust diagnostic insights, the absence of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and definitive capsular serotyping precludes the absolute classification of this isolate as a confirmed hvKP strain. Furthermore, the targeted nature of the mNGS panel inherently constrained our comprehensive resistome mapping, explaining why additional or uncharacterized resistance determinants were not captured. Third, without direct intracranial gas analysis or neuropathological examination, the precise etiology of tension pneumocephalus remains uncertain. We cannot definitively prove in vivo bacterial gas production or exclude occult micro-anatomical defects and iatrogenic air entry from lumbar punctures. Thus, the discussed mechanisms represent plausible associations rather than definitive causal pathways.

Conclusion

We describe a fatal case of fulminant meningoencephalitis without an overt extracranial focus, associated with rapid-onset tension pneumocephalus and an ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae isolate harboring hypervirulence-associated genes. While the exact origin of the intracranial gas is likely multifactorial and cannot be conclusively attributed to bacterial metabolism alone, the devastating clinical course underscores the extreme invasiveness of such probable hypervirulent variants within the central nervous system. This report highlights the value of integrating phenotypic assays with molecular diagnostics, such as mNGS, to recognize convergent pathogen phenotypes, while maintaining a broad differential diagnosis for spontaneous pneumocephalus in neurocritical care.
