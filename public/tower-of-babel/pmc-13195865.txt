Prognostic value of PRAME gene promoter methylation and expression in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Jintao Zheng, Guoqiang Cao, Jialing Zhao, Longjie Yu, Xiujin Ye, Junru Chen, Haiyang Xie, Jianhui Li, Changku Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPRAME is a member of the cancer–testis antigen family and exhibits abnormally high expression in various solid tumors. Epigenetic regulation plays a critical role in the development and progression of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Alterations in promoter methylation of specific genes have been widely recognized as valuable biomarkers for predicting cancer risk and assessing prognosis.ObjectiveThis study systematically investigated the biological function and clinical relevance of PRAME promoter methylation in HCC.MethodsUsing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort, we identified methylation-associated prognostic genes and examined the association between PRAME promoter methylation, its mRNA expression levels, overall survival (OS), and clinicopathologic characteristics. We then validated these findings in an independent HCC cohort from Shulan (Hangzhou) Hospital using MethylTarget multiplex gene methylation detection technology. To investigate the potential role of PRAME in vascular invasion and metastasis, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) at both histological and cellular levels.ResultsAnalyses across three HCC cohorts consistently showed that the PRAME promoter is generally hypomethylated in tumor tissues, and this hypomethylation was significantly correlated with elevated PRAME mRNA expression. In both the TCGA and Shulan (Hangzhou) Hospital cohorts, PRAME promoter hypomethylation was associated with more advanced clinicopathologic features and shorter OS. Moreover, PRAME promoter methylation status was strongly linked to vascular invasion and distant metastasis in HCC. Further functional analyses revealed significant upregulation of Wnt signaling and cell adhesion–related pathways in tissues with high PRAME expression, a finding corroborated by single-cell RNA sequencing data.ConclusionHypomethylation of the PRAME promoter is closely associated with poor overall survival, adverse clinicopathologic features, vascular invasion, and metastasis in HCC patients. It plays a pivotal role in regulating gene expression and promoting tumor invasiveness. These findings suggest that PRAME promoter methylation may serve as a promising epigenetic biomarker for prognostic evaluation in HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02152-7.

Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most common malignancies worldwide, ranking sixth in incidence and third in cancer-related mortality, with its incidence continuing to rise in recent years [1]. As the predominant subtype of liver cancer, HCC is primarily driven by established risk factors, including chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections, excessive alcohol consumption, and aflatoxin exposure [2, 3]. Prolonged exposure to these etiological agents drives the progressive accumulation of genetic and epigenetic alterations that activate oncogenes and inactivate tumor suppressor genes, thereby promoting hepatocarcinogenesis and disease progression [4]. Current standard treatment approaches for HCC include surgical resection, local ablative therapy, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), liver transplantation, and supportive care [5]. Despite substantial advances in therapeutic strategies over the past few decades, patients with HCC continue to face high rates of recurrence and metastasis, resulting in a persistently poor overall prognosis. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify robust prognostic biomarkers that can guide treatment optimization and improve survival outcomes.

PRAME (Preferentially Expressed Antigen in Melanoma) is a member of the cancer–testis antigen family, located on chromosome 22q11.22 and encoding a 509-amino acid protein [6]. PRAME can be recognized by HLA-A*24 and presented to cytotoxic T lymphocytes, thereby triggering tumor cell lysis [7]. While PRAME expression is largely absent in normal adult somatic tissues, with limited expression restricted to the testes, endometrium, and certain adrenal and ovarian tissues, it is frequently upregulated in a wide range of malignancies. Elevated PRAME levels have been closely associated with tumor invasion and metastasis in cancers such as lung cancer [8], breast cancer [9], gastric cancer [10], and uveal melanoma [11]. Nevertheless, its role in promoting invasiveness in HCC remains inadequately characterized. Epigenetic mechanisms, particularly DNA methylation, play a pivotal role in regulating gene expression in tumor cells. Aberrant DNA methylation in promoter regions has been shown to contribute to genomic and chromosomal instability, disrupt normal gene expression patterns, and critically influence tumor initiation and progression [12]. Among these epigenetic alterations, differential methylation at CpG sites represents a key regulatory mechanism. Because abnormal promoter methylation often occurs early in tumorigenesis, it holds considerable promise for early cancer detection, disease monitoring, and prognostic assessment [13]. Despite growing evidence implicating PRAME in multiple cancer types, the precise role of its DNA methylation in driving HCC invasion and metastasis has not yet been clearly elucidated.

Cell adhesion molecules (CAMs) play a critical role in the initiation of tumor invasion and metastasis [14]. CAMs are a class of transmembrane proteins that primarily mediate adhesion either between adjacent cells or between cells and the extracellular matrix. Based on structural and functional characteristics, CAMs are categorized into several major families, including the cadherins, the immunoglobulin superfamily, integrins, selectins, and other glycoprotein adhesion molecules [15]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that certain CAMs are aberrantly regulated under oncogenic signaling, thereby contributing to key biological processes such as cell adhesion, migration, and invasion. These dysregulated CAMs are closely linked to metastatic potential and clinical outcomes in cancer patients [16]. This study aims to systematically investigate the relationships among PRAME promoter methylation, PRAME gene expression, clinicopathologic features, survival outcomes, and cell adhesion molecules, with the goal of further clarifying the biological function and clinical relevance of PRAME in HCC.

Materials and methods

Methylation differential analysis

We downloaded HCC methylation array data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data portal (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/) and selected 50 paired tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples from the Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip. Differentially methylated sites were analyzed using the R package ChAMP for data preprocessing. The methylation level of each gene’s promoter region was defined as the average β-value of all methylation probes within that promoter. Differentially methylated genes were filtered using the following criteria: (1) DNA methylation sites located in the promoter region; (2) |log₂FC| > 0.15; and (3) FDR < 0.05. To validate the differential methylation of the PRAME promoter, we also downloaded two methylation datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database: GSE113017 (29 paired HCC and adjacent tissue samples) and GSE89852 (37 paired samples). Data processing was performed using the same methods as in the TCGA cohort. All public data were used in compliance with TCGA and GEO policies.

Transcriptome differential analysis

We retrieved gene expression data and relevant clinical data for HCC from the TCGA database and analyzed the expression profiles of the same 50 paired tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the R packages DESeq2, edgeR, and limma, with the following filtering criteria: (1) genes annotated as protein-coding by the R package AnnoProbe; (2) |log₂FC| > 0.15; and (3) FDR < 0.05. The final set of DEGs was obtained by taking the intersection of the results from the three methods. Subsequently, we performed a combined analysis of the DNA methylation data, transcriptome data, and clinicopathologic information of these DEGs to explore their biological significance and clinical value.

Screening of DNA methylation-associated prognostic genes based on public databases

We used publicly available data from TCGA and GEO to identify 92 candidate genes whose DNA methylation levels were negatively correlated with mRNA expression. By integrating methylation array data with matched clinical information, we performed univariate Cox proportional hazards regression and log-rank tests to identify three methylation-associated prognostic genes, PRAME, SFN, and TBX15, based on the criteria HR ≠ 1 (p < 0.05) and log-rank test p < 0.05. Next, these genes were combined with clinical variables in a bidirectional stepwise regression, and the optimal prognostic signature was selected by minimizing the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). PRAME was retained in the final model (AIC = 184.13) and emerged as the core target gene. Using clinical data and methylation levels of the PRAME promoter region, we constructed a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model. A linear combination of methylation levels, clinical parameters, and their corresponding regression coefficients was used to derive a risk score formula, which was then applied to calculate an individual risk score for each patient. Patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on these scores, and survival differences between the two groups were further evaluated.

Shulan (Hangzhou) hospital cohort

To validate the methylation status of the PRAME promoter in HCC, we collected 80 tissue samples, including 20 paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues, from 60 patients who underwent surgical resection at Shulan (Hangzhou) Hospital between March 2016 and September 2023. All samples were histologically diagnosed as HCC, and clinical follow-up data were available for all 60 patients. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shulan (Hangzhou) Hospital (Ethics code: LCYJ-IIT-2025011), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Detailed clinical information is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Cell culture and treatment

Human HCC cell lines HepG2 (RRID: CVCL_0027) and Huh-7 (RRID: CVCL_0336) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The human immortalized liver cell line HepLi5 was kindly provided by Professor Lanjuan Li (State Key Laboratory for Diagnosis and Treatment of Infectious Diseases, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) [17]. All cell lines were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Cat# 30030, Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cat# 10091148, Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Cat# 15070063, Gibco, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. For drug treatments, we used 5-Aza-2′-deoxycytidine (decitabine, 5-AZA-CdR), a cytidine analog that incorporates into DNA and irreversibly inhibits DNA methyltransferases (DNMT). Bobcat339 is a potent and selective cytosine-based inhibitor of TET enzymes and is widely used in epigenetic studies. Previous studies have shown that among five liver cancer cell lines, PRAME expression is relatively low in HepG2 and relatively high in Huh-7 [18]. Accordingly, cells were treated with 10 µM decitabine or 50 µM Bobcat339 for 48 h. Treated cells were harvested for methylation and hydroxymethylation sequencing, which was performed by Genesky Biotechnology Inc. (Shanghai, China) using Illumina MiSeq and NovaSeq platforms. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) transfection

siRNAs were purchased from Gema Gene (Shanghai, China). The siRNA sequences used in this study were as follows: siPRAME-1: 5′-UCUCUAUGUGGACUCUUUAUUTT-3′ and 5′-AAUAAAGAGUCCACAUAGAGATT-3′; siPRAME-2: 5′-CUACCCACCUUGGCGAAAUUUTT-3′ and 5′-AAAUUUCGCCAAGGUGGGUAGTT-3′; si-Control: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′ and 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′. Cells were transfected with siRNAs using Lipofectamine 2000 (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantitative real time polymerase chain reaction (qRT–PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (Aidlab, RN2802). Genomic DNA removal and cDNA synthesis were performed using the reverse transcription kit (Aidlab, PC7001). qRT–PCR was carried out on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Relative gene expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal control. ΔΔCt = (Cttarget gene-Ctinternal control)sample-(Cttarget gene-Ctinternal control)control. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Western blotting

Cells in good growth condition were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and lysed on ice for 30 min in prechilled RIPA buffer (Cat# 89901, Thermo, USA) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Cat# 78440, Thermo Scientific, USA). Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Cat# FD2001, Fdbio Science, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Cat# ISEQ00010, Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA (Cat# A8020, Solarbio, China) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies are listed in Table S3. After three washes with TBST buffer (Cat# T1080, Solarbio, China), membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were detected using ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (Cat# 32209, Thermo, USA) and quantified with ImageJ software. Target protein levels were normalized to GAPDH and expressed as fold changes relative to control samples. Data are shown as mean ± SD.

DNA methylation sequencing

We assessed the quality and quantity of isolated DNA using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To analyze single-site and regional methylation levels of the PRAME promoter in HCC tissues, we applied the MethylTarget multiplex gene methylation assay (Genesky Biotechnology Inc., Shanghai, China) on the Illumina MiSeq platform. Using MethPrimer (https://www.urogene.org/methprimer/), we analyzed the PRAME promoter region spanning 2,000 bp upstream of the transcription start site to 1,000 bp downstream of the first exon (chr22:22561265–22557165) and identified 83 CpG sites and six CpG-rich regions. Based on this analysis, we designed primers targeting seven methylated regions within the promoter. Detailed information on the MethylTarget primer sequences and their corresponding target regions is provided in Supplementary Table S4. Primer design and optimization were performed using the proprietary software Methylation FastTarget V4.1 (Shanghai GeneCowin Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). During design, the software systematically excluded potentially interfering genomic features, including common single-nucleotide polymorphisms (minor allele frequency ≥ 0.01) and short tandem repeats (repeat number ≥ 6), to improve amplification specificity and detection accuracy. Only primers that yielded clear single bands and successfully amplified bisulfite-converted human genomic DNA were selected for downstream experiments. DNA methylation levels were expressed as beta values ranging from 0 (completely unmethylated) to 1 (completely methylated), and classified as follows: unmethylated (beta value: 0–0.2), hypomethylated (beta value: 0.2–0.6), and hypermethylated (beta value: 0.6–1). The methylation frequencies of PRAME detected by MethylTarget across samples are summarized in Supplementary Table S5. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Single-cell sequencing analysis

The single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE242889 was downloaded and processed for each individual sample using the R package Seurat (http://satijalab.org/seurat/). After initial data preprocessing and quality control, we excluded cells that met any of the following criteria: fewer than 500 detected genes, mitochondrial gene content exceeding 25% of total expressed genes, or identification as doublets. Next, we identified 2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs). Using these HVGs, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, and the top 50 principal components were selected for downstream analysis. Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony package. Cell clustering was then carried out with the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions, yielding 11 distinct cell clusters. To annotate these clusters, we used the FindAllMarkers() function in Seurat to identify DEGs with high discriminatory power between clusters. Cell types were assigned based on established marker genes reported in the literature and known canonical markers [19]. Details of the cluster-specific biomarkers are provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3). Differences in PRAME promoter methylation between groups were assessed using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on data distribution. Based on OS data, we used the R package Survminer to determine the optimal cut-off value for stratifying patients into risk groups. The association between PRAME expression or promoter methylation and HCC overall survival was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the strength and direction of monotonic relationships between two continuous variables. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to identify prognostic factors. For qRT–PCR and WB experiments, differences between two groups were compared using Student’s t-test. All P values were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001).

Results

Identification of PRAME as a methylation-associated prognostic gene based using the AIC

The workflow for selecting methylation-associated prognostic genes is shown in Fig. 1A, which ultimately identified PRAME. Using the median methylation level of the PRAME promoter region as the cutoff, we stratified the TCGA cohort into hypermethylated and hypomethylated groups for survival analysis. Patients in the hypomethylation group had significantly shorter OS than those in the hypermethylation group (Fig. 1B). In the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model optimized by the AIC, PRAME promoter methylation levels remained significantly associated with patient survival (Fig. 1C). Based on these variables, we developed a nomogram to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS (Fig. 1D). The calibration plot indicated strong agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Fig. 1E). We then calculated individual risk scores using the Cox regression coefficients and applied the median cutoff, determined with the survminer R package, to classify patients into high- and low-risk groups for Kaplan–Meier analysis. This revealed significantly worse OS in the high-risk group compared with the low-risk group (Fig. 1H). Figure 1F shows the heatmap of parameters included in the Cox model, and Fig. 1G displays the survival status and risk-group assignments for all patients in the cohort. We further performed differential methylation analysis for all CpG sites within PRAME between tumor and adjacent normal tissues and found significant hypomethylation at every site in tumors (Fig. 1I-K). Validation in two independent GEO cohorts confirmed that all 11 CpG sites were significantly hypomethylated in HCC tumor tissues relative to normal tissues (Fig. 1L and M). Additionally, PRAME expression was significantly upregulated in HCC compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1N).

The methylation of the PRAME promoter is associated with expression in HCC

According to the annotation of the Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip, 11 valid probes were located within the PRAME gene, 10 of which mapped to the promoter region (TSS1500, TSS200, or 5′UTR), and only cg17648213 was situated in the gene body (Fig. 2A). We examined the correlation between methylation at these 11 differentially methylated sites and PRAME expression. With the exception of cg17648213 in the gene body (Fig. 2B), all other sites exhibited a significant negative correlation with PRAME expression (Fig. 2C-L). Sequencing results showed that, compared with HepLi5 cells, the PRAME promoter region in both Huh-7 and HepG2 cells was hypomethylated, whereas hydroxymethylation levels did not differ significantly (Fig. S1A, S1B). We then treated these two HCC cell lines with Bobcat339 or decitabine for 48 h. Bobcat339 treatment had no significant effect on PRAME promoter hydroxymethylation, mRNA levels, or protein expression (Fig. 2M, O-R). In contrast, decitabine treatment markedly reduced promoter methylation (Fig. 2N) and upregulated both PRAME mRNA and protein expression (Fig. 2O-R).

Systematic methylation assessment of CpG-enriched regions in the Shulan cohort

To validate the promoter hypomethylation observed in tumor tissues from the TCGA and GEO cohorts, we performed MethylTarget sequencing on samples from the Shulan Hospital validation cohort. MethPrimer analysis of the PRAME promoter region not only confirmed previously reported CpG sites but also revealed six CpG-rich regions within this locus (Fig. 3A). Based on these findings, we designed specific primers and systematically evaluated methylation levels across seven methylated target regions in the promoter. Compared with adjacent normal tissues, tumor tissues showed significantly reduced methylation at nearly all CpG sites (Fig. 3B-H and Fig. S2A–S2G).

PRAME promoter methylation and expression predict OS in HCC

In the TCGA HCC cohort, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that high PRAME expression was significantly associated with poorer OS (Fig. S3A). Further analysis revealed that 10 of the 11 differentially methylated CpG sites, those located in the promoter region, were significantly hypomethylated in patients with worse OS (Fig. S3C–S3L). In contrast, cg17648213, the only CpG site in the gene body, was not significantly associated with OS (Fig. S3B) and showed no clear correlation with PRAME expression (Fig. 2B). Consistent findings were observed in the Shulan Hospital validation cohort: hypomethylation at promoter CpG sites was also strongly linked to unfavorable OS (Fig. 4A-H). Moreover, when individual CpG sites were grouped into methylated target regions, hypomethylation in all regions except Target3 and Target4 was associated with poorer OS in HCC patients (Fig. 4I-L and Fig. S4A–S4C).

Promoter hypomethylation and high expression of PRAME are associated with invasive clinical phenotypes in HCC

We examined the association between PRAME expression and promoter methylation levels with clinicopathologic features in HCC patients. Analysis of TCGA data showed that PRAME expression was significantly elevated in patients with vascular invasion, high serum AFP levels, advanced T stage, higher AJCC stage, and poor pathological grade (Fig. S5A). We then assessed the relationship between PRAME promoter methylation and these clinical characteristics and identified five CpG sites with significant associations. Specifically, cg11225751, cg01966129, and cg17336139 were significantly hypomethylated in patients exhibiting vascular invasion, high AFP levels, advanced T stage, higher AJCC stage, and poor pathological grade (Fig. S5B–S5F). The methylation level of cg20667684 did not differ significantly only in the context of vascular invasion, whereas cg05208878 was associated with vascular invasion, high AFP levels, and pathological grade. At the level of methylated target regions, patients with hypomethylation at Target1 and Target6 frequently presented with severe vascular invasion, metastasis, advanced T stage, and higher AJCC stage (Fig. 5A-E). This subgroup also displayed elevated levels of multiple tumor biomarkers, increased Ki-67 positivity, and larger tumor size (Fig. 5F-N), along with varying degrees of hypomethylation at Target2 and Target7. Notably, all five key CpG sites identified above were located within these four target regions (Target1, Target2, Target6, and Target7).

PRAME facilitates vascular invasion and metastasis in HCC: Mechanistic analysis at both cellular and tissue levels.

Using PRAME expression levels, we stratified HCC tissues in the TCGA cohort into high- and low-expression groups. Differentially expressed genes (|log₂FC| > 1, P < 0.05) were identified, and GO enrichment analysis revealed multiple significantly enriched pathways (Fig. 6A). We then performed pathway enrichment analysis on the single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE242889 based on PRAME expression and found that 28 pathways significantly enriched at the tissue level (FDR < 0.05) were also recapitulated at the cellular level (Fig. 6B). Visualization of these 28 pathways at the cellular level showed marked upregulation of processes related to cell adhesion, mitosis, and Wnt signaling in malignant cells with high PRAME expression (Fig. 6C). In addition, in T/NK cells and Kupffer cells with high PRAME expression, pathways such as negative regulation of the cell cycle and Wnt signaling also exhibited an upward trend (Fig. S6E, S6F). Further GSEA confirmed significant enrichment of the cell adhesion pathway in the high PRAME expression group at both tissue and cellular levels, with 14 core genes consistently contributing to this enrichment (Fig. 6D and E). Subsequent correlation analysis identified eight key molecules significantly associated with both PRAME expression and promoter methylation: PCDHB2, PCDHGB2, PCDH17, PCDHB16, CDH8, CDH11, NECTIN1, and CELSR1 (Fig. 6F). All of these molecules, except PCDHGB2, were significantly upregulated in tissues with high PRAME expression (Fig. 6G). To assess functional relevance, we transfected Huh-7 cells with two distinct PRAME-targeting siRNAs. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by Western blotting, and silencing of PRAME resulted in a significant reduction in CDH11 and NECTIN1 protein levels (Fig. 6H).

Discussion

Aberrant DNA methylation is a key epigenetic driver of tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis [20]. Given the high stability and detectability of DNA methylation marks, their utility in tumor prognosis assessment and risk stratification has attracted considerable interest. In this study, we systematically evaluated the association between promoter methylation profiles of candidate genes and survival outcomes in HCC and identified PRAME as a key epigenetic marker under multivariate conditions.

Log-rank tests and univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that promoter methylation levels of PRAME, SFN, and TBX15 were significantly associated with OS, with PRAME showing the most pronounced differential expression (log₂FC = 9.71). However, after integrating clinicopathological variables and performing bidirectional stepwise regression using the AIC for model optimization, only PRAME was retained in the final model. This indicates that PRAME contributes unique prognostic information not captured by other covariates in a multivariate setting. Importantly, even after further adjustment for potential confounders, including serum AFP levels and pathological grade, the association between PRAME promoter methylation and patient survival remained statistically significant (Fig. S7), reinforcing its potential as an independent prognostic factor in clinical practice. Notably, although SFN and TBX15 were excluded from the final model, their prognostic relevance may be partially accounted for by other clinical or molecular variables included in the analysis. Alternatively, their effects might be context-dependent, restricted to specific molecular subtypes or clinical scenarios. Further validation in larger cohorts and functional studies will be necessary to clarify their precise roles in HCC progression.

DNA methylation is one of the most important epigenetic mechanisms regulating gene expression and is generally inversely associated with transcriptional activity [21]. During tumorigenesis, promoter hypermethylation frequently silences tumor suppressor genes, whereas oncogenes can be aberrantly activated through DNA hypomethylation. In this study, we systematically characterized the methylation landscape of the PRAME promoter region and observed consistent hypomethylation at CpG sites near the promoter in tumor tissues. Methylation levels at these sites were significantly and negatively correlated with PRAME expression. Notably, cg17648213, the only CpG site located within the gene body, showed no significant correlation with expression and lacked prognostic value. This finding underscores the regional heterogeneity of DNA methylation–expression relationships across the genome [22], suggesting that the promoter region serves as a critical regulatory element for PRAME transcription. A hypomethylated promoter can facilitate transcription factor binding to enhancer or promoter elements, thereby promoting gene expression [23]. Consistent with this, our experiments demonstrated that decitabine treatment markedly reduced PRAME promoter methylation and concomitantly increased both mRNA and protein levels. In contrast, treatment with the TET inhibitor Bobcat339 had no significant effect on PRAME expression. Supporting this observation, Ren et al. reported that in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) cells, loss of TET1 or TET2 did not alter global DNA methylation levels, whereas TET3, the most abundantly expressed isoform, was primarily responsible for modulating DNA methylation [24]. Given that DNA methylation dynamics are more sensitive to changes in DNMT activity than to TET activity [25], we propose that aberrant PRAME expression in HCC is more likely driven by dysregulation of DNMT-mediated methylation rather than alterations in TET enzyme function. Future studies employing DNMT-specific knockout or overexpression models will be essential to pinpoint the exact enzymes responsible for the methylation imbalance at the PRAME promoter.

Compared with traditional monitoring approaches, DNA methylation biomarkers have demonstrated superior sensitivity and specificity for the early detection of HCC [26]. In this study, we found that hypomethylation at CpG sites within the PRAME promoter region is significantly associated with shorter OS in HCC patients. When individual CpG sites were aggregated into methylation target regions, hypomethylation of Target1, Target2, Target6, and Target7 showed the strongest association with poor prognosis. These four regions collectively encompass five key CpG sites: cg01966129, cg17336139, cg20667684, cg11225751, and cg05208878. Furthermore, PRAME promoter hypomethylation was closely linked to multiple aggressive clinicopathological features, including microvascular invasion (MVI), portal or hepatic vein tumor thrombosis (PVTT/HVTT), distant metastasis, advanced tumor stage, larger tumor diameter, elevated Ki-67 proliferation index, and increased serum levels of tumor markers such as AFP and PIVKA-II. (MVI is a critical pathological feature of HCC strongly associated with recurrence and metastasis [27]; combined measurement of AFP and PIVKA-II improves long-term prognostic prediction [28]; and high Ki-67 expression reflects a highly proliferative tumor state [29].) Together, these associations suggest that the methylation status of the PRAME promoter may reflect the intrinsic biological aggressiveness of HCC. Similar to the well-established link between GSTP1 hypermethylation and HCC development and poor outcomes [30], PRAME promoter hypomethylation may also serve as a potential prognostic indicator. Pan-cancer analysis revealed a consistent pattern of PRAME promoter hypomethylation coupled with elevated expression across multiple tumor types (Fig. S8). Moreover, numerous studies have associated high PRAME expression with poor prognosis, therapeutic resistance, and disease progression in solid tumors [31, 32], acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [7], and Hodgkin lymphoma [33], further supporting its broad oncogenic and prognostic relevance. Notably, compared with single-CpG-site analysis, regional methylation profiles more comprehensively capture local chromatin architecture and transcriptional regulatory activity [34]. Accordingly, the Target regions exhibited greater stability and stronger clinical utility in predicting OS, vascular invasion, and AJCC stage. In summary, the methylation characteristics of the PRAME promoter region may represent a novel molecular tool for risk stratification and prognostic assessment in HCC. Importantly, aberrant DNA methylation signatures often arise early in carcinogenesis, and circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) retains the methylation patterns of its tissue of origin [35]. Methylation profiling of cfDNA has emerged as a valuable noninvasive approach for tumor detection and outcome prediction [36]. Future studies should therefore investigate the feasibility of detecting PRAME promoter methylation in plasma cfDNA to facilitate the clinical translation of liquid biopsy-based strategies for HCC management.

Currently, PRAME functions not only as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker but also as a tumor-associated antigen that is minimally expressed in normal tissues yet capable of eliciting immune responses, positioning it as a highly promising target for immunotherapy [37]. For instance, a PRAME-targeted DNA vaccine has been shown to enhance activation of both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, leading to improved survival in patients with high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) [38]. Amir et al. engineered T cells expressing PRAME-specific T-cell receptors (TCRs) that recognized a broad range of cancer types, including melanoma, AML, and carcinomas of the colon, cervix, lung, and breast, and effectively controlled tumor growth in orthotopic mouse models [39]. Moreover, early-phase clinical trials have demonstrated that PRAME-specific TCR-T cell therapy is well tolerated in patients with hematologic malignancies [40]. The successful clinical translation of PRAME-directed immunotherapies will require continued refinement of therapeutic strategies alongside a deeper understanding of the complex interactions between PRAME expression and the tumor microenvironment.

CAMs play a pivotal role in tumor progression and immune evasion [41]. Their dysregulation promotes tumor cell proliferation, invasion across tissue boundaries, metastasis, escape from immune surveillance, and survival within the tumor microenvironment. In this study, HCC tumors with high PRAME expression showed significant enrichment in the cell adhesion and Wnt signaling pathways. Consistent with these findings, in vitro experiments demonstrated that PRAME knockdown reduced protein levels of CDH11 and NECTIN1, suggesting a potential role for PRAME in regulating invasive pathways. Although we did not directly establish a transcriptional regulatory relationship between PRAME and these downstream adhesion genes, prior studies indicate that PRAME can act as a transcriptional regulator or as a component of a regulatory complex to modulate gene expression in other malignancies [42, 43]. Thus, promoter demethylation-driven PRAME overexpression may reshape the adhesion molecule expression profile by enhancing the activity of transcription factor complexes. CDH11 has been shown to promote tumor growth and metastatic progression via activation of the Wnt pathway [44], while elevated NECTIN1 expression is associated with poor prognosis in HCC and enhances tumor cell migration [45]. Moreover, activation of Wnt signaling disrupts normal cell polarity and adhesion, a critical step in tumor progression and metastasis [46]. Notably, Chen et al. reported that PRAME regulates adhesion molecule expression through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway to drive invasion and metastasis in cervical cancer [47]. In our analysis, the PRAME-high group exhibited significant enrichment in Wnt-related signaling, implying that PRAME may indirectly modulate CAM expression and tumor invasiveness by potentiating Wnt activity. Future studies employing ChIP-seq or promoter reporter assays will be essential to determine whether PRAME directly regulates the transcription of these adhesion genes.

In summary, this study highlights the potential oncogenic role of PRAME in HCC from an epigenetic regulatory perspective and its strong association with aggressive clinical phenotypes. However, we did not perform direct migration or invasion assays, nor did we establish in vivo metastasis models; thus, the influence of PRAME on tumor invasive capacity remains largely inferred from clinical correlations and pathway enrichment analyses. Additionally, the direct transcriptional regulatory relationship between PRAME and Wnt signaling components or CAMs has not yet been fully elucidated. Nonetheless, the robustness of our conclusions is supported by multi-cohort validation, single-cell RNA sequencing, and in vitro perturbation experiments. Given the technical simplicity, high sensitivity, and stability of DNA methylation detection, we propose that methylation levels at the PRAME promoter hold promise as a clinically viable prognostic biomarker for HCC and may serve as a valuable target for adjunctive diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.
