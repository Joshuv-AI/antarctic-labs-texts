Loosen, trap, and pull technique for retrieval of an entrapped stent in a previously stented left main coronary artery
Authors: Ahmed M. Khairy, Ahmed Emara, Ameer Awashra, Ahmed Ali Fahim, Abdelaziz Sheridah, Sameh Nassar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPercutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of left main (LM) bifurcation lesions carries a high risk of complications, including stent entrapment, a rare but potentially life-threatening event. Management often requires surgical intervention, though percutaneous techniques can sometimes provide an alternative solution.Case presentationA 63-year-old male with hypertension and diabetes mellitus presented with non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). PCI was performed using the T and small protrusion (TAP) technique for a 1,1,1 Medina-classified an LM bifurcation lesion. Following deployment of the LM–left anterior descending (LAD) stent, the left circumflex (LCx) stent became entrapped in the LM stent, resulting in fracture and shaft separation during attempted retrieval. A stepwise approach was used: (1) multiple balloon inflations to loosen the entrapped stent; (2) shaft trapping within the guide catheter; and (3) en bloc removal of the system. Final correction included deploying an additional stent to address LM stent deformation. The patient remained stable with no post-procedural complications.ConclusionsThis case highlights the “loosen, trap, and pull” technique as a viable percutaneous alternative for retrieving an entrapped stent within a previously deployed LM stent, potentially avoiding the need for emergent surgical intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05370-8.

Introduction

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is an invasive, non-surgical procedure designed to alleviate the occlusion of coronary arteries and restore the blood flow to the heart [1]. During PCI, several complications can occur, including access site bleeding, stent thrombosis, and stent malposition. Despite the widespread availability of PCI, the entrapment of coronary stent has been reported in very rare cases, but mostly it requires emergent surgical intervention [1, 2]. However, in some cases surgical intervention can be avoided and the stent can be rescued by different percutaneous techniques [3–5]. In this case report, we describe our method to retrieve a dislodged, unexpanded stent entrapped in a previously deployed LM-LAD stent.

Case presentation

We present a 63-years-old male patient with a past medical history of hypertension and diabetes mellitus who presented with non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI). During coronary angiography, through right femoral access (6 Fr), he was found to have a LM bifurcation lesion with a medina classification of 1,1,1. We subsequently decided on T and small protrusion (TAP) stenting technique to LM-LAD- LCx bifurcation lesion. We use the TAP technique as the angle between LAD and LCx was wide and also the patient presented acutely (NSTEMI). TAP is a simple technique that does not require multiple balloon inflations and is less time-consuming than DK-crush. Both LAD and LCx were wired, and after deployment of the LM-LAD stent, the LCx was rewired. Predilatation was performed using a 2.5 × 20 mm balloon to open the stent struts before attempting LCx stent delivery. Single balloon inflation was performed in the LCx before attempting to deliver the second stent, without performing a kissing balloon inflation, as this step is not routinely done in provisional stenting before second stent placement. While delivery of the LCx stent, it was entrapped in the LM stent, just at the ostium of the LCx, within the struts of the LM stent, and its shaft was fractured and separated while forceful pulling of the stent, leaving the unexpanded stent with its balloon entrapped in the LM (Supplementary Video 1), (Fig. 1) (Fig. 2). CABG was considered, but the patient refused surgery; therefore, PCI remained the only revascularization option. The size of stent was 3.5 × 38 for LM and 3.0 × 38 for LCx. Although a simple crossover stenting strategy from the LM to the LAD with a drug-coated balloon (DCB) for the LCx would have been preferable considering the non-dominant and diffusely diseased Circumflex artery, this option was not feasible due to the unavailability of DCB in our catheterization laboratory at the time of the procedure. Therefore, a TAP technique was performed instead. Steps of rescue

(1) We first attempted to pass a 1.25 × 12 mm balloon over LAD wire, inflating the balloon on nominal pressure distal to the impacted stent with the pullback of the inflated balloon to retrieve the stent, but failed this technique, denoting strong impaction in the LM stent (Supplementary Video 2). (2) We decided to try to loosen the bonds between the LCx and LM stents. We did this by repeatedly inflating a large noncompliant balloon 5 × 8 mm over the LM-LAD wire in the LM stent. This led to good stent apposition, and the LCx stent became less impacted in the LM stent. This step was fundamental to the technique (Supplementary Video 3). (3) The next mission was to trap the fractured shaft stent balloon using a 2.5 × 20 mm balloon inflated at the tip of the guiding catheter, trapping the shaft (Supplementary Video 4). (4) The last step was to pulled back the entire system (catheter, balloon, stent, and wires), successfully retrieving the stent (Supplementary Video 5). (5) Correction of LM-LAD stent longitudinal deformity by deploying another stent in the LM coronary artery overlapping the deformed stent with good results (Supplementary Video 6). (6) The patient remained well during follow-up, with no chest pain, no ECG changes, and stable hemodynamics. (Fig. 3). The final results are presented in (Supplementary Video 7). Discussion

Stent loss during PCI is an infrequent yet challenging complication, with an incidence of approximately 0.3% [6]. Stent entrapment is a potentially life-threatening complication of PCI that can lead to grave outcomes like coronary dissection, thrombus formation, embolization, or even death [7].

Stent loss can be managed with either stent deployment/crushing or retrieval [6, 8]. We report a case of stent entrapment in which a new stent got entrapped before expansion in a previously deployed stent in the LM vicinity.

Deployment or crushing was not feasible in our case due to shaft fracture and its position in the left main, respectively. Several techniques for retrieving entrapped stents from the coronary arteries have been described, including using balloon catheters, two twisted guide wires, basket devices, and loop snares; nevertheless, surgical intervention is required in a few cases [7, 9, 10].

The most commonly used stent retrieval technique is the small balloon technique, in which a profile balloon is advanced through the stent beyond its distal edge, inflated, and withdrawn along with the stent into the guiding catheter [8]; This technique was initially attempted in our case but failed, indicating a strong impaction of the unexpanded stent within the previously deployed LM stent. Therefore, we tried trapping the shaft of the stent in the guide catheter using a 2.5 × 20 mm balloon followed by pulling the guide catheter, but it also failed due to severe entrapment of the stent in the deployed LM stent.

We decided to change our strategy and attempted to loosen the bonds between the two stent metals first before trying retrieval of the entrapped stent; this was done through multiple inflations of a large noncompliant balloon 5 × 8 mm balloon over the LM-LAD wire in the LM stent (approximately 10 recurrent inflations) leading to good stent apposition and subsequently the LCx stent became less impacted in LM stent. The successful stent retrieval was achieved through a combination of mechanical effects. Radial expansion of the deployed LM stent increases inter-strut spacing and reduces geometric interlocking between metallic meshes. Simultaneous cyclic balloon inflation–deflation produces transient elastic deformation and micro vibratory forces along the stent struts, which disrupt static friction and adhesion at contact interfaces. These repetitive mechanical oscillations, together with redistribution of stress, collectively weaken the frictional and interlocking bonds anchoring the trapped stent. The cumulative effect of these mechanisms allows progressive disengagement and safe withdrawal of the entrapped stent, avoiding the need for surgical intervention. This step was not intended to crush the entrapped stent, as the stent balloon was still inside and prevented crushing; rather, it was performed to loosen the entrapped stent and facilitate its retrieval. This is the basic step in this technique, after that, the entrapped stent became loosened, then trapping the shaft again and pulling whole system resulted in successful retrieval. We suggested to loosen first, then trap and pull back an entrapped stent as an additional technique to the standard lost-stent algorithm when a lost stent is entrapped in a previously deployed LM stent. Although proximal optimization technique (POT) was not performed after LM–LAD stent implantation, it might have reduced the likelihood of this complication; however, the current report primarily aims to demonstrate a practical approach for managing such rare and challenging situations when they occur.

In addition, intravascular imaging (IVUS or OCT) would have further optimized the final LM result, particularly in the presence of overlapping stent layers; however, these modalities were not available in our catheterization laboratory at the time of the procedure.

Conclusion

We describe how we succeeded in retrieving LCx stent entrapped at a previously deployed stent in the LM. We loosened the entrapped stent by recurrently inflating a large balloon in the previously deployed stent in the LM-LAD, trapping the stent shaft in the guiding catheter, and finally pulling the whole system.
