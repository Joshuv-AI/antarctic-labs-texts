1
Introduction

The primary objective of this paper is to develop a unified framework for two fundamental families of numbers, the Bernoulli numbers and the Euler numbers, and to introduce new definitions and computational techniques that arise from this shared structure. By relating these numbers to ordered partitions (compositions) of integers, we reveal a novel connection linking number theory, combinatorics, and special functions. In the sections that follow, we provide the necessary definitions and establish the key relations.

2
Definitions

2.1
Ordered Partitions of n

An ordered partition (composition) of
n
is a sequence of positive integers
\lambda=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})
such that:

b_{1}+b_{2}+\ldots+b_{k}=n

where the order of the components matters and the number of components
(k)
is called the length of the partition.

2.2
Bernoulli Numbers

Jacob Bernoulli
(1654-1705), a Swiss mathematician and physicist, first systematically studied Bernoulli numbers in 1713. These numbers emerged during his investigations of the sums of powers of natural numbers and became a fundamental tool in number theory and analysis
[
1
]
. Over time, the connection of this sequence of numbers with the expansions of many functions, including the zeta function, has been established; thus, Bernoulli numbers hold great importance in mathematics. In general, the sum
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k^{r}
can be written as follows
[
2
]
:

\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k^{r}=\sum_{k=0}^{r}\frac{B_{k}\,r!\,n^{r-k+1}}{k!(r-k+1)!}

where
B_{k}
is independent of
r
and are called Bernoulli numbers.

2.3
Euler Numbers

Leonhard Euler
(1707-1783) studied Euler numbers in 1755 in his book
Institutiones Calculi Differentialis

[
3
]
. These numbers, which initially appeared in the context of expansions of trigonometric series, gradually found extensive applications in combinatorics and graph theory. By discovering the connection between these numbers and specific permutations, Euler laid foundations that are still used in combinatorial research today. These numbers form a sequence
E_{n}
of natural numbers defined by the
Taylor series
as follows
[
4
]
:

\frac{1}{\cosh(t)}=\frac{2}{e^{t}+e^{-t}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{E_{n}}{n!}t^{n}

2.4
Zeta Function

The Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
was first studied by
Leonhard Euler
in the 18th century for real numbers
(s>1)
, but
Bernhard Riemann
(1826-1866) extended it to complex numbers in 1859
[
7
]
.This function is defined for
\text{Re(s)}>1
as:

\zeta(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{s}}

Riemann showed that this function can be
analytically continued
for all complex numbers except
s=1
(where it has a simple pole). This function has a deep connection with the distribution of prime numbers
[
7
]
.

2.5
Hurwitz Zeta Function

The Hurwitz zeta function, a generalization of the Riemann zeta function, was introduced by
Adolf Hurwitz
(1859-1919) in 1882. For
\text{Re(s)}>1
, it is defined as an absolutely convergent series
[
8
]
:

\zeta(s,x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+x)^{s}};\quad\frac{d}{dx}\zeta(s,x)=-s\,\zeta(s+1,x)

This function can be extended to other values of
s
by analytic continuation, except at
s=1
where the function has a simple pole.

Generalized harmonic functions
H_{x}^{s}
can be expressed via the Hurwitz zeta function:

H_{x}^{s}=\sum_{n=1}^{x}\frac{1}{n^{s}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(n+x+1)^{s}};

H_{x}^{s}=\zeta(s)-\zeta(s,x+1);

\frac{d}{dx}(H_{x}^{s})=s\,\zeta(s+1,x+1)

2.6
Gamma Function

The
Gamma Function
was first introduced by the Swiss mathematician
Leonhard Euler
in his endeavor to generalize the factorial function to non-integer values. Useful details on the origin and evolution of the Gamma function can be found in
[
5
]
. Euler introduced the following analytic function during the years 1729 and 1730:

\Gamma(n)=\int_{0}^{1}(-\ln(t))^{n-1}dt,\quad(n>0)

which, by an elementary change of variable, leads to the following equation
[
5
]
:

\Gamma(n)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{n-1}e^{-t}dt,\quad(n>0)

Connection with the factorial function
[
5
]
:

\Gamma(n+1)=n\Gamma(n)=n!

Some specific values
[
5
]
:

\Gamma(1)=1,\quad\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\sqrt{\pi},\quad\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}

Efforts have been made and formulas have been provided that describe the Gamma function. One such formula for
\text{Re}(x)>-\frac{1}{2}
in article
[
6
]
, page 12, is as follows:

\Gamma(x+1)=\frac{\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi}}{e^{x+\frac{1}{2}}}\times e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta(2j,x+1)}{(2j+4j^{2})2^{2j}}}

(1)

In addition to the Gamma function itself, its logarithmic derivative, the Digamma function, is also very important. To compute this derivative, we proceed as follows.

H_{x}^{2j}=\zeta(2j)-\zeta(2j,x+1);

\Gamma(x+1)=\frac{\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{2\pi}}{e^{x+\frac{1}{2}}}\times e^{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{H_{x}^{2j}-\zeta(2j)}{(2j+4j^{2})2^{2j}}};

From article
[
9
]
, page 50, we know:

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta(2j)}{(2j+4j^{2})2^{2j}}=\ln\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{e}}

(2)

Therefore:

\Gamma(x+1)=\frac{\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{2}}{e^{x}}\times e^{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{H_{x}^{2j}}{(2j+4j^{2})2^{2j}}};

We define the function
A
to simplify the differentiation of the Gamma function as follows:

A=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{H_{x}^{2j}}{(2j+4j^{2})2^{2j}}

(3)

Therefore:

\Gamma(x+1)=\frac{\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{2}}{e^{x-A}};

(4)

\frac{d}{dx}\Gamma(x)=\frac{\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{2}}{e^{x-A}}\left(\ln\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{dA}{dx}\right)

2.7
Digamma Function

The
Digamma Function
is obtained by differentiating the logarithm of the Gamma function as follows:

\frac{d}{dx}\Gamma(x)=\Gamma(x)\psi(x);

\Gamma(x+1)\psi(x+1)=\frac{\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{x+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{2}}{e^{x+A(x,-\frac{1}{2})}}\left(\ln\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{dA}{dx}\right);

\psi(x+1)=\ln\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{dA}{dx};

\psi(x+1)=\ln\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta(2n+1,x+1)}{(2n+1)2^{2n}}

(5)

2.8
Polygamma Functions

Polygamma Functions
are the logarithmic derivatives of the Gamma function, defined as follows:

\frac{d^{m+1}}{dx^{m+1}}\ln\Gamma(x)=\psi^{m}(x);

\frac{d\psi(x+1)}{dx}=\psi^{1}(x+1);

\psi^{1}(x+1)=\frac{1}{x+\frac{1}{2}}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta(2n+2,x+1)}{2^{2n}};

\psi^{2}(x+1)=\frac{-1}{\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2n+2)\zeta(2n+3,x+1)}{2^{2n}};

\psi^{3}(x+1)=\frac{2}{\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{3}}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2n+2)(2n+3)\zeta(2n+4,x+1)}{2^{2n}};

In general, based on differentiation rules, we have:

\psi^{y}(x+1)=(-1)^{y-1}\left(\frac{(y-1)!}{\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{y}}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2n+y)!\,\zeta(2n+y+1,x+1)}{2^{2n}(2n+1)!}\right)

(6)

\psi^{y}(x+1)=(-1)^{y-1}\left(\frac{(y-1)!}{\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{y}}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(2n+y)!\,\zeta(2n+y+1,x+1)}{2^{2n}(2n)(2n+1)(2n-1)!}\right)

Let:

f(n)=\frac{1}{2^{2n}(2n)(2n+1)}

(7)

Then:

\psi^{y}(x+1)=(-1)^{y-1}\left(\frac{(y-1)!}{\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{y}}-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{f(n)(2n+y)!\,\zeta(2n+y+1,x+1)}{(2n-1)!}\right)

(8)

\frac{1}{3!}&1&0&\cdots&0\\
\frac{1}{5!}&\frac{1}{3!}&1&\cdots&0\\

K_{e}(n)

E_{2n}=(2n)!\times K_{e}(n)

(35)

K_{b}(n)=\begin{vmatrix}1&0&0&\cdots&1\\

K_{e}(n)=-\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\frac{K_{e}(n^{\prime})}{(2(n-n^{\prime}))!}\quad\quad n\geq 1

These relations provide independent computational methods for

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we have established a unified framework that connects Bernoulli numbers and Euler numbers to ordered partitions of integers through the functions
K_{b}(n)
and
K_{e}(n)
. By deriving recursive relations and combinatorial interpretations for these functions, we have developed novel methods for computing these important sequences, which originate from expansions of special functions such as the zeta, Gamma, and polygamma functions.

This connection not only reveals a deeper interplay between number theory and combinatorics, but also offers practical advantages for numerical evaluations. Representing these numbers through ordered partitions provides a better understanding of their structure and enables more efficient computations. This approach may pave the way for future research in generalizing these relationships to other numerical sequences and their applications in analytic and combinatorial number theory.
