Coincidence double-tip scanning tunneling spectroscopy
Yuehua Su, Guoya Zhang, Dezhong Cao, Chao Zhang

Abstract
The development of new experimental techniques for direct measurement of many-body correlations is crucial for unraveling the mysteries of strongly correlated electron systems. In this work, we propose a coincidence double-tip scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS) that enables direct probing of spatially resolved dynamical two-body correlations of sample electrons. Unlike conventional single-tip scanning tunneling microscopy, the double-tip STS employs a double-tip scanning tunneling microscope (STM) equipped with two independently controlled tips, each biased at distinct voltages ( V 1 V_{1} and V 2 V_{2} ). By simultaneously measuring the quantum tunneling currents I 1 ​ ( t ) I_{1}(t) and I 2 ​ ( t ) I_{2}(t) at locations j 1 j_{1} and j 2 j_{2} , we obtain a coincidence tunneling current correlation ⟨ I 1 ​ ( t ) ​ I 2 ​ ( t ) ⟩ ¯ \overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle} . Differentiating this coincidence tunneling current correlation with respect to the two bias voltages yields a coincidence dynamical conductance. Through the development of a nonequilibrium theory, we demonstrate that this coincidence dynamical conductance is proportional to a contour-ordered second-order current correlation function. For the sample electrons in a nearly free Fermi liquid state, the coincidence dynamical conductance captures two correlated dynamical electron propagation processes: (i) from j 1 j_{1} to j 2 j_{2} (or vice versa) driven by V 1 V_{1} , and (ii) from j 2 j_{2} to j 1 j_{1} (or vice versa) driven by V 2 V_{2} . For the sample electrons in a superconducting state, additional propagation channels emerge from the superconducting condensate, coexisting with the above normal electron propagation processes. Thus, the coincidence double-tip STS provides direct access to spatially resolved dynamical two-body correlations, offering a powerful tool for investigating strongly correlated electron systems.

Introduction

There are many unsolved mysteries in the field of strongly correlated electron systems, including unconventional superconductivity, itinerant magnetism, quantum spin liquids, quantum phase transitions and quantum criticality
(
Stewart 2001
;
Lee et al. 2006
;
Stewart 2011
;
Zhou et al. 2017
)
. A central challenge in this field is the development of innovative experimental methods and advanced techniques to uncover the fundamental physics governing these enigmatic phenomena. Recently, several coincidence detection techniques have been proposed as promising tools for direct measurement of two-body correlations of strongly correlated electron systems
(
Su and Zhang 2020
;
Su et al. 2021
;
Devereaux et al. 2023
)
. For instance, the coincidence angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (cARPES) can enable direct measurement of two-body correlations in particle-particle channel, offering a pathway to investigate the mechanism of unconventional superconductivity
(
Su and Zhang 2020
;
Devereaux et al. 2023
)
. Similarly, the newly proposed coincidence inelastic neutron scattering (cINS) provides a technique to detect two-spin correlations, opening new avenues for exploring quantum spin liquid physics
(
Su et al. 2021
)
.

In this work, we propose a new coincidence technique for spatially resolved measurement of two-body correlations, a coincidence double-tip scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS). This technique utilizes a double-tip scanning tunneling microscope (STM) setup, constituting a fundamental extension of conventional single-tip STM beyond single-particle measurements. Schematic illustrations of both single-tip and double-tip STM configurations are provided in Fig.
1
. Since its invention, the single-tip STM has played a pivotal role in advancing atomic-resolution topography and local electronic structure studies across diverse materials in various fields of science and technology
(
Binnig and Rohrer 1987
;
Fischer et al. 2007
;
Chen 2008
)
. In this work, we focus on its extension in probing the local electronic structure of materials, scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS). The single-tip STS measures the spatially resolved quantum tunneling current
I_{j}(V)
, where
j
denotes the spatial location and
V
is the applied bias voltage. By differentiating the tunneling current with respect to the bias voltage, the single-tip STS yields the local dynamical conductance

\sigma_{j}(V)=\frac{d}{dV}I_{j}(V).

(1)

Introduce an effective current operator

J_{A}(j\sigma;t,t_{1};\omega)=-ic_{j\sigma}(t)c^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}(t_{1})e^{-i\omega(t_{1}-t)}+h.c.,

(2)

where
c_{j\sigma}
(
c^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}
) are annihilation (creation) operators for the sample electrons at location
j
with spin
\sigma
. In a nonequilibrium theory,
\sigma_{j}(V)
can be shown to follow

\sigma_{j}(V)\sim i\sum_{\sigma}\int_{c}dt_{1}\langle T_{c}J_{A}(j\sigma;t,t_{1};-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV)\rangle.

(3)

Here
T_{c}
is a contour-time ordering operator defined along a time contour
t_{i}\rightarrow t\rightarrow t_{i}
, where
t_{i}
is the initial time and
t
represents the observation time. The integral
\int_{c}dt_{1}
is performed along this closed time contour. In our study, we take
t_{i}\rightarrow-\infty
.
\langle O\rangle
defines the ensemble statistical average of an observable operator
O
. It can be shown that
\sigma_{j}(V)
is proportional to the local electronic single-particle spectral function (or density of states). Therefore, the single-tip STS can provide crucial insights into the single-particle electronic structure at specific locations on the sample surface.

In a coincidence double-tip STS measurement, two independent tips enable simultaneous electron tunnelings between sample locations
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
to their respective probes. The tunneling currents measured at each probe at time
t
are denoted as
I^{(1)}_{1}(t,n)
and
I^{(1)}_{2}(t,n)
, where the integer
n
represents different measurements. We introduce a coincidence tunneling current observable
I_{d}^{(2)}
in experiment, which captures the simultaneous tunneling current events by a post-experiment coincidence counting method
(
Cao and Su 2024
)
as

I_{d}^{(2)}=\frac{1}{N_{n}}\sum_{n}I^{(1)}_{1}(t,n)\cdot I^{(1)}_{2}(t,n),

(4)

where
N_{n}
is the total number of measurements. Theoretically, the coincidence tunneling current observable
I_{d}^{(2)}
can be described by a statistical average correlation function
I_{d}^{(2)}=\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}
, where
I_{1}(t)
and
I_{2}(t)
represent the local current operators at locations
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
, respectively. We can then introduce a coincidence dynamical conductance as

\sigma_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})=\frac{d}{dV_{2}}\frac{d}{dV_{1}}\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}.

(5)

It can be shown in a nonequilibrium theory that

\sigma_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})\sim-\sum_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}G_{J}^{(2)}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV_{1},-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV_{2}),

(6)

where
G_{J}^{(2)}
is a contour-time relevant second-order current correlation function defined as

\displaystyle G_{J}^{(2)}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

(7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{c}dt_{2}dt_{1}\langle T_{c}J_{1A}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t,t_{1};\omega_{1})J_{2A}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t,t_{2};\omega_{2})\rangle.\,\,\quad

Here
J_{1A}
and
J_{2A}
are two effective current operators defined by

\displaystyle J_{1A}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t,t_{1};\omega_{1})=-ic_{j_{1}\sigma_{1}}(t)c^{\dagger}_{j_{1}\sigma_{1}}(t_{1})e^{-i\omega_{1}(t_{1}-t)}+h.c.,

\displaystyle J_{2A}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t,t_{2};\omega_{2})=-ic_{j_{2}\sigma_{2}}(t)c^{\dagger}_{j_{2}\sigma_{2}}(t_{2})e^{-i\omega_{2}(t_{2}-t)}+h.c.

(8)

By comparing
\sigma_{j}(V)
with
\sigma_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})
, it shows that while
\sigma_{j}(V)
provides the spatially resolved single-particle dynamical information,
\sigma_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})
reveals the spatially resolved dynamical two-body correlation functions. Therefore, the coincidence double-tip STS is a promising technique for studying the spatially resolved dynamical two-body correlations of the esoteric strongly correlated electron systems.

Notably, multiple-tip STM techniques have been developed over nearly three decades
(
Byers and Flatté 1995
;
Niu et al. 1995
;
Shiraki et al. 2001
;
Nakayama et al. 2012
;
Voigtländer et al. 2018
;

;

)
. These techniques enable nanoscale charge transport measurements like a multimeter at the nanoscale
(

;
Nakayama et al. 2012
;
Voigtländer et al. 2018
)
, probing the single-particle charge-current relevant Green’s functions of sample electrons
(
Byers and Flatté 1995
;
Niu et al. 1995
)
. A new approach employing double-tip STM to probe disorder-induced current-current correlations has been proposed recently for investigating electron diffusion propagation in disordered metals
(
Leeuwenhoek et al. 2020a
)
. Since such current-current correlations originate from disorder-induced statistical effects

)

II
Single-tip scanning tunneling spectroscopy

In this section, we will revisit how the single-tip STS works in study of the local electronic structure of sample material. Fig.
1
(a) shows a schematic diagram of the single-tip STM. When the tip approaches the sample surface within tunneling distance (typically sub-nanometer scale) and a finite bias voltage is applied, a quantum tunneling current emerges between the sample and tip. This tunneling phenomenon is further illustrated schematically in Fig.
2
.

Figure 2:
Schematic illustration of quantum tunneling current formation. Panels (a)-(c) show the single-particle spectral functions of the sample (left) and tip (right), with color-shaded regions indicating occupied ones. Here,
W_{s}
and
W_{p}
denote the work done by the surface electric field as an electron tunnels across the sample or tip surface. The single-electron energies of the sample and tip are given by
E_{s}
and
E_{p}
, while
\mu_{s}
and
\mu_{p}
represent their respective chemical potentials. The effective single-electron energies,
\varepsilon_{s}=E_{s}-\mu_{s}
and
\varepsilon_{p}=E_{p}-\mu_{p}
, are defined relative to these chemical potentials. (a) At sample-tip separation
d\gg d_{0}
(tunneling distance), the two systems remain decoupled with zero net tunneling current. (b) When the sample and tip are brought to tunneling proximity (
d\simeq d_{0}
), their Fermi energies equilibrate, also with zero net tunneling current. (c) Application of a tip bias voltage
V

-eV
, creating a nonequilibrium condition that drives finite tunneling current.

Consider the configuration shown in Fig.
2
(a), where the sample-tip separation
d
significantly exceeds the characteristic tunneling distance
d_{0}\simeq 5-10
Å
(
Fischer et al. 2007
)
. Under this condition, the electrons of the sample and tip remain decoupled, preventing quantum tunneling across the large-spacing vacuum barrier. For the electrically neutral sample and tip, their chemical potentials
\mu_{s}
and
\mu_{p}
differ, with the respective work functions approximately defined by
\phi_{s}=W_{s}-\mu_{s}
and
\phi_{p}=W_{p}-\mu_{p}

(
Ashcroft and Mermin 1976
)
. Here
W_{s}
and
W_{p}
are contributions from the surface effects. The color-shaded regions in Fig.
2
represent occupied single-electron spectral distributions. For our subsequent study, we assume that the tip consists of a metallic material whose electrons are in a nearly free Fermi liquid state.

When the sample and tip approach within the tunneling distance range (
d\simeq d_{0}
), electrons of minute quantity momentarily tunnel from the side with the higher Fermi level to the side with the lower Fermi level. This process equilibrates the Fermi energies of the sample and tip upon reaching final equilibrium state
(
Ashcroft and Mermin 1976
)
. To quantitatively describe this equilibrium state, we define the effective single-electron energies
\varepsilon_{s}=E_{s}-\mu_{s}
and
\varepsilon_{p}=E_{p}-\mu_{p}
, where
E_{s}
and
E_{p}
denote the single-electron energies of the sample and tip, respectively, as illustrated in Fig.
2
(b).

Application of a bias voltage
V
modifies this equilibrium configuration, as shown in Fig.
2
(c). The tip single-electron energies experience a uniform shift of
-eV
, creating an energy window
(-eV,0)
where the sample single-electron spectral distributions remain occupied while the corresponding tip single-electron spectral distributions become unoccupied. This nonequilibrium population change causes electrons to tunnel between the sample and tip, generating a finite quantum tunneling current. Crucially, while the unbiased system (b) maintains equilibrium, the biased configuration (c) represents a nonequilibrium dynamic state. This fundamental distinction necessitates a nonequilibrium theory to appropriately describe the voltage-driven tunneling phenomena.

II.1
Basic principle: Equilibrium linear-response theory

Let us review the basic principle of the single-tip STS from an equilibrium linear-response theory. Suppose at time
t<t_{i}
, the sample-tip system is in the unbiased equilibrium state of Fig.
2
(b). The sample-tip system can now be described by an ensemble density matrix

\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\beta[K_{s}^{(0)}+K_{p}^{(0)}]},

(9)

where
K_{s}^{(0)}
and
K_{p}^{(0)}
are defined by

\displaystyle K_{s}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{s}-\mu_{s}\sum_{i\sigma}c^{{\dagger}}_{i\sigma}c_{i\sigma},

\displaystyle K_{p}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{p}-\mu_{p}\sum_{i\sigma}d^{{\dagger}}_{i\sigma}d_{i\sigma},

(10)

and the partition function
Z
is defined as
Z=\operatorname{Tr}\{\exp[-\beta(K_{s}^{(0)}+K_{p}^{(0)})]\}
.
H_{s}
and
H_{p}
are the respective Hamiltonians of the sample and tip electrons, and
c^{{\dagger}}_{i\sigma}(c_{i\sigma})
and
d^{{\dagger}}_{i\sigma}(d_{i\sigma})
are the corresponding electron creation (annihilation) operators at location
i
with spin
\sigma
.

At time
t_{i}
, a bias voltage
V
is applied to the system, inducing a shift of
-eV
of the tip single-electron energies. Consequently, the sample and tip Hamiltonians with respect to the respective chemical potentials transform into the following forms:

\displaystyle K_{s}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle K_{s}=K_{s}^{(0)},

\displaystyle K_{p}^{(0)}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle K_{p}=K_{p}^{(0)}-eV\sum_{i\sigma}d^{{\dagger}}_{i\sigma}d_{i\sigma}.

(11)

The new Hamiltonian of the sample-tip system becomes

K_{T}=K+V_{t},\quad K=K_{s}+K_{p},

(12)

where the tunneling interaction
V_{t}
is defined by

V_{t}=T_{0}\sum_{\sigma}(d^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}c_{j\sigma}+c^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}d_{j\sigma}).

(13)

Here we model the tunneling interaction
V_{t}
with atomic resolution, where the sample electrons at location
j
can tunnel through the vacuum barrier to the corresponding location
j
of the tip, and vice versa. This approach is for the atomic resolution of STM
(
Tersoff and Hamann 1983
;
Tersoff and Hamann 1985
)
. It employs a simplified representation where quantum tunneling occurs exclusively through an atomically confined channel between the sample and tip. Our treatment is very similar to two theoretical perspectives: (i) an electrode-atom-electrode model of the STM junction
(
Lang 1985
)
, and (ii) the identification of the tip apex atom as the dominant contributor to the sample-tip interaction
(
Chen 1988
;
Chen 1990
)
. It should be noted that the location of the tunneling tip electrons may be represented using various notations. The choice of
j
is made here solely for simplicity. The tunneling constant
T_{0}
is taken to be independent of both spin and spatial location. Under finite bias voltage, the tunneling interaction
V_{t}
gives rise to a finite quantum tunneling current across the vacuum barrier, as illustrated schematically in Fig.
2
(c).

The tunneling current operator can be defined by
I=e\frac{dN_{p}}{dt}
, where
N_{p}
is the number operator of the tip electrons. From the Heisenberg equation of motion, it can be shown that

I=-\frac{ieT_{0}}{\hbar}\sum_{\sigma}(d^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}c_{j\sigma}-c^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}d_{j\sigma}).

(14)

For an arbitrary observable operator
O
, its expectation value at time
t>t_{i}
is given by

\overline{\langle O(t)\rangle}\equiv\langle O_{H}(t)\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{0}O_{H}(t)],

(15)

where
O_{H}(t)
denotes the Heisenberg picture representation of
O(t)
, evolving according to

O_{H}(t)=U_{H}^{\dagger}(t,t_{i})O(t)U_{H}(t,t_{i}).

(16)

The time-evolution operator
U_{H}(t,t_{i})
takes the form

U_{H}(t,t_{i})=T_{t}\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t_{i}}^{t}dt_{1}K_{T}(t_{1})\right],

(17)

with
T_{t}
being a time-ordering operator. The interaction picture representation
O_{I}(t)
is analogously defined through

O_{I}(t)=U_{0}^{\dagger}(t,t_{i})O(t)U_{0}(t,t_{i}),

(18)

where the time-evolution operator
U_{0}(t,t_{i})
is given by

U_{0}(t,t_{i})=T_{t}\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t_{i}}^{t}dt_{1}K(t_{1})\right].

(19)

Introducing the
S
matrix operator as
S(t,t_{i})\equiv U_{0}^{\dagger}(t,t_{i})U_{H}(t,t_{i})
, we have the following transformation relation

O_{H}(t)=S(t_{i},t)O_{I}(t)S(t,t_{i}),

(20)

where
S(t_{i},t)=[S(t,t_{i})]^{\dagger}
. The explicit form of
S(t,t_{i})
can be shown as following:

S(t,t_{i})=T_{t}\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t_{i}}^{t}dt_{1}V_{I}(t_{1})\right],

(21)

where
V_{I}(t)
represents the tunneling interaction in the interaction picture.
With these definitions, we can derive the expectation value of the tunneling current as

\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}=\langle S(t_{i},t)I_{I}(t)S(t,t_{i})\rangle,

(22)

where
I_{I}(t)
represents the tunneling current operator in the interaction picture and
\langle O\rangle\equiv\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_{0}O]
denotes the ensemble statistical average. The conservation of the tip electron number in the initial equilibrium state implies
\langle d_{j\sigma}\rangle=\langle d^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}\rangle=0
,
which immediately gives
\langle I_{I}(t)\rangle=0
. Within the linear-response approximation, we can obtain the expression

\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{t_{i}}^{t}dt_{1}\langle[I_{I}(t),V_{I}(t_{1})]\rangle.

(23)

This result represents the well-established linear-response theory for the quantum tunneling current in the single-tip STS
(
Mahan 1990
)
.

For the case we discuss here,
K
is time independent. Thus, the expectation value of any observable
\overline{\langle O(t)\rangle}
is initial time
t_{i}
independent. In this case,
O_{I}(t)
has an equivalent expression

O_{I}(t)=e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}Kt}O(t)e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}Kt}.

(24)

Since
[K_{s}^{(0)}+K_{p}^{(0)},\sum_{i\sigma}d_{i\sigma}^{\dagger}d_{i\sigma}]=0
,
c_{j\sigma,I}(t)
and
d_{j\sigma,I}(t)
have the following expressions

c_{j\sigma,I}(t)=c_{j\sigma}(t),\ d_{j\sigma,I}(t)=e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}eVt}d_{j\sigma}(t),

(25)

where
c_{j\sigma}(t)
and
d_{j\sigma}(t)
are defined as

\displaystyle c_{j\sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}K_{s}^{(0)}t}c_{j\sigma}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}K_{s}^{(0)}t},

\displaystyle d_{j\sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}K_{p}^{(0)}t}d_{j\sigma}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}K_{p}^{(0)}t}.

(26)

Introducing the notations
A^{(\pm)}
as

A^{(+)}(t)\equiv\sum_{\sigma}d^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}(t)c_{j\sigma}(t),\ A^{(-)}(t)\equiv[A^{(+)}(t)]^{\dagger},

(27)

V_{I}
and
I_{I}
can be reexpressed as

\displaystyle V_{I}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{0}\sum_{a=\pm}A^{(a)}(t)e^{ia\phi(t)},

\displaystyle I_{I}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{ieT_{0}}{\hbar}\sum_{a=\pm}a\cdot A^{(a)}(t)e^{ia\phi(t)},

(28)

where
\phi
is defined by

\phi(t)=-\frac{1}{\hbar}eVt.

(29)

With these definitions,
\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}
can be show to follow

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}=-\frac{ieT_{0}^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dt_{1}

\displaystyle[\chi_{R}(t-t_{1})e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}eV(t-t_{1})}

(30)

\displaystyle-\chi_{A}(t_{1}-t)e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}eV(t-t_{1})}],

where
\chi_{R}
and
\chi_{A}
are defined by

\displaystyle\chi_{R}(t-t_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\theta(t-t_{1})\langle[A^{(+)}(t),A^{(-)}(t_{1})]\rangle,

\displaystyle\chi_{A}(t-t_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\theta(t_{1}-t)\langle[A^{(+)}(t),A^{(-)}(t_{1})]\rangle.

(31)

Here we have set
t_{i}\rightarrow-\infty
in Eq. (
30
). Since
\chi_{A}(t)=[\chi_{R}(-t)]^{*}
and
\chi_{A}(\omega)=[\chi_{R}(\omega)]^{*}
, where
\chi_{R}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dt\chi_{R}(t)e^{i\omega t}
and
\chi_{A}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dt\chi_{A}(t)e^{i\omega t}
, it can be shown that

\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}=\frac{2eT_{0}^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\operatorname{Im}\chi_{R}(-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV).

(32)

Let us introduce the imaginary-time Green’s function defined as
\chi(\tau)=-\langle T_{\tau}A^{(+)}(\tau)A^{(-)}(0)\rangle
. Using a standard method
(

;

)
,

can be obtained through analytic continuation
\chi_{R}(\omega)=\hbar\,\chi(i\nu_{n})\big|_{i\nu_{n}\rightarrow\hbar\omega+i\delta^{+}}

\displaystyle\chi_{R}(\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sum_{\sigma}\iint d\varepsilon_{1}d\varepsilon_{2}A_{\sigma}^{(c)}(\varepsilon_{1})A_{\sigma}^{(d)}(\varepsilon_{2})

\displaystyle\times\frac{n_{F}(\varepsilon_{2})-n_{F}(\varepsilon_{1})}{\hbar\omega+\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}+i\delta^{+}},

where

and

denote the single-particle spectral functions of the sample and tip electrons, respectively, defined by

\displaystyle A_{\sigma}^{(c)}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\operatorname{Im}G^{(c)}_{j\sigma}(i\omega_{n})\big|_{i\omega_{n}\rightarrow\varepsilon+i\delta^{+}},

\displaystyle A_{\sigma}^{(d)}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\operatorname{Im}G^{(d)}_{j\sigma}(i\omega_{n})\big|_{i\omega_{n}\rightarrow\varepsilon+i\delta^{+}}.

(34)

Here

with
G^{(c)}_{j\sigma}(\tau)=-\langle T_{\tau}c_{j\sigma}(\tau)c_{j\sigma}^{\dagger}(0)\rangle
and

with

) and (

), the expectation value of the tunneling current can be expressed as

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{eT_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\hbar}\sum_{\sigma}\int d\varepsilon A^{(c)}_{\sigma}(\varepsilon-eV)A^{(d)}_{\sigma}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\times[n_{F}(\varepsilon)-n_{F}(\varepsilon-eV)],

where

is the Fermi-Dirac distribution function.

Let us introduce a local dynamical conductance

\sigma_{j}(V)=\frac{d}{dV}\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}.

(36)

In the low-temperature limit with the following approximations,

n_{F}(x)\simeq\theta(-x),\ \frac{dn_{F}(x)}{dx}\simeq-\delta(x),

(37)

\sigma_{j}(V)
simplifies to

\sigma_{j}(V)=\frac{e^{2}T_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\hbar}\sum_{\sigma}A^{(c)}_{\sigma}(-eV)A^{(d)}_{\sigma}(0).

(38)

For typical experimental configurations where the tip consists of a metallic material, the spectral function of the tip electrons is spin independent,

A^{(d)}_{\sigma}(\xi)=A_{d}(\xi).

(39)

It takes the form

A_{d}(\xi)=2\pi\rho_{d}(\xi),\ \rho_{d}(\xi)\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\delta(\xi-\xi_{\mathbf{q}}),

(40)

where
\rho_{d}(\xi)
represents the density of states of the tip electrons with energy dispersion
\xi_{\mathbf{q}}
. In contrast, the spectral function of the sample electrons has the general expression

\displaystyle A^{(c)}_{\sigma}(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{Z_{s}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}(e^{-\beta E_{\alpha}}+e^{-\beta E_{\beta}})\langle\alpha|c_{j\sigma}|\beta\rangle\langle\beta|c^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}|\alpha\rangle

(41)

\displaystyle\times\delta(\varepsilon+E_{\alpha}-E_{\beta}),

where
Z_{s}
denotes the partition function of the sample electrons, while
E_{\alpha}
and
E_{\beta}
are their eigenenergies. Eq. (
38
) reveals that the local dynamical conductance can probe directly the single-particle spectral function of the sample electrons, establishing the single-tip STS as a powerful technique for atomic-scale electronic structure characterization.

II.2
Basic principle: Nonequilibrium theory

In this part, we will develop an alternative nonequilibrium theory for the single-tip STS, which can naturally generalize to the double-tip STS case. The theory begins with the contour-ordered
S
-matrix defined as
S_{c}(t_{i},t_{i})=T_{c}[S(t_{i},t)S(t,t_{i})]
, which follows

S_{c}(t_{i},t_{i})=T_{c}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{c}dt_{1}V_{I}(t_{1})}.

(42)

Here the time contour runs from
t_{i}
to
t
and back to
t_{i}
as
t_{i}\rightarrow t\rightarrow t_{i}
. Within this framework, the observable operator in the Heisenberg picture takes the compact form as

O_{H}(t)=T_{c}[S_{c}(t_{i},t_{i})O_{I}(t)].

(43)

The tunneling current expectation value at time
t>t_{i}
is then expressed as

\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}=\langle T_{c}[S_{c}(t_{i},t_{i})I_{I}(t)]\rangle.

(44)

In the linear-response approximation, this simplifies to

\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{c}dt_{1}\langle T_{c}[I_{I}(t)V_{I}(t_{1})]\rangle,

(45)

where
V_{I}(t)
and
I_{I}(t)
are given in Eq. (
28
). Crucially, the conservation of the tip electron number leads to two distinct contributions to
\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}
,

\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}=\overline{\langle I_{+}(t)\rangle}+\overline{\langle I_{-}(t)\rangle},

(46)

with the explicit expressions of
\overline{\langle I_{+}(t)\rangle}
and
\overline{\langle I_{-}(t)\rangle}
given by

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{+}(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e\left(\frac{-iT_{0}}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\int_{c}dt_{1}\langle T_{c}[A^{(+)}(t)A^{(-)}(t_{1})]\rangle

(47)

\displaystyle\times e^{i[\phi(t)-\phi(t_{1})]},

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{-}(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-e\left(\frac{-iT_{0}}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\int_{c}dt_{1}\langle T_{c}[A^{(-)}(t)A^{(+)}(t_{1})]\rangle

(48)

\displaystyle\times e^{-i[\phi(t)-\phi(t_{1})]}.

Building upon the well-established nonequilibrium Green’s function formalism
(
Langreth 1976
;
Rammer 2007
;
Haug and Jauho 2008
)
, we introduce the contour-ordered Green’s functions

\displaystyle G_{c}(j\sigma;t_{1},t)=-i\langle T_{c}c_{j\sigma}(t_{1})c^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}(t)\rangle,

(49)

\displaystyle G_{d}(j\sigma;t_{1},t)=-i\langle T_{c}d_{j\sigma}(t_{1})d^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}(t)\rangle,

(50)

which allows us to express the correlation function as

\langle T_{c}[A^{(+)}(t)A^{(-)}(t_{1})\rangle=G_{c}(j\sigma;t,t_{1})G_{d}(j\sigma;t_{1},t).

(51)

Consequently,
\overline{\langle I_{+}(t)\rangle}
becomes as

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{+}(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{eT_{0}^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\sigma}\int_{c}dt_{1}G_{c}(j\sigma;t,t_{1})G_{d}(j\sigma;t_{1},t)

(52)

\displaystyle\times e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}eV(t_{1}-t)}.

Let us introduce the greater and lesser Green’s functions

\displaystyle G^{>}_{d}(j\sigma;t_{1},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\langle d_{j\sigma}(t_{1})d^{{\dagger}}_{j\sigma}(t)\rangle,

\displaystyle G^{<}_{d}(j\sigma;t_{1},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\langle d^{{\dagger}}_{j\sigma}(t)d_{j\sigma}(t_{1})\rangle.

(53)

The contour-ordered Green’s function can be reexpressed into the following form:

\displaystyle G_{d}(j\sigma;t_{1},t)

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta_{c}(t_{1}-t)G^{>}_{d}(j\sigma;t_{1},t)+\theta_{c}(t-t_{1})G^{<}_{d}(j\sigma;t_{1},t),\quad

where
\theta_{c}(t_{1}-t)
and
\theta_{c}(t-t_{1})
are step functions defined on the time contour. The Fourier transformations of the greater and lesser Green’s functions are defined as

\displaystyle G^{(l)}_{d}(j\sigma;\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dtG^{(l)}_{d}(j\sigma;t)e^{i\omega t},

\displaystyle G^{(l)}_{d}(j\sigma;t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\omega G^{(l)}_{d}(j\sigma;\omega)e^{-i\omega t},

(55)

where
l=>
or
<
, and we have assumed the time-translation invariance
G^{(l)}_{d}(j\sigma;t_{1},t)=G^{(l)}_{d}(j\sigma;t_{1}-t)
.
The expression of
\overline{\langle I_{+}(t)\rangle}
then transforms to

\overline{\langle I_{+}(t)\rangle}=-\frac{eT_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\sigma l}\int d\omega G^{(+)}_{c,l}(j\sigma;\omega-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV)G^{(l)}_{d}(j\sigma;\omega),

(56)

where
G^{(+)}_{c,l}
are defined by

G^{(+)}_{c,l}(j\sigma;\omega)=\int_{c}dt_{1}\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\theta}^{(l)}_{c}(t_{1}-t)G_{c}(j\sigma;t,t_{1})e^{-i\omega(t_{1}-t)}.

(57)

Here the contour step functions are defined as

\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\theta}^{(l)}_{c}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\theta_{c}(t),&l=>,\\
\theta_{c}(-t).&l=<.\end{array}\right.

(58)

For a metallic tip with nearly free electronic states, it can be easily shown that

G^{(l)}_{d}(j\sigma;\omega)=\frac{2\pi i}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}G^{(l)}_{d}(\xi_{\mathbf{q}})\delta(\omega-\frac{1}{\hbar}\xi_{\mathbf{q}}),

(59)

where
G^{(l)}_{d}
are given by

G^{>}_{d}(\xi_{\mathbf{q}})=n_{F}(\xi_{\mathbf{q}})-1,\,G^{<}_{d}(\xi_{\mathbf{q}})=n_{F}(\xi_{\mathbf{q}}).

(60)

The final expression of
\overline{\langle I_{+}(t)\rangle}
becomes

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{+}(t)\rangle}

(61)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{ieT_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\sigma l}\int d\xi G^{(+)}_{c,l}[j\sigma;\frac{1}{\hbar}(\xi-eV)]G^{(l)}_{d}(\xi)A_{d}(\xi),\qquad

where
A_{d}(\xi)
is the single-particle spectral function of the tip electrons defined in Eq. (
40
).

Following an analogous derivation to that of
\overline{\langle I_{+}(t)\rangle}
, we can obtain the other tunneling current component

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{-}(t)\rangle}

(62)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{ieT_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\sigma l}\int d\xi G^{(-)}_{c,l}[j\sigma;\frac{1}{\hbar}(\xi-eV)]G^{(l)}_{d}(\xi)A_{d}(\xi),\qquad

where
G^{(-)}_{c,l}
is defined by

G^{(-)}_{c,l}(j\sigma;\omega)=\int_{c}dt_{1}\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\theta}^{(l)}_{c}(t-t_{1})G_{c}(j\sigma;t_{1},t)e^{i\omega(t_{1}-t)}.

(63)

Let us define the composite Green’s function

G_{c,l}(j\sigma;\omega)=G^{(+)}_{c,l}(j\sigma;\omega)-G^{(-)}_{c,l}(j\sigma;\omega).

(64)

It allows us to express the total tunneling current as

\overline{\langle I(t)\rangle}=-\frac{ieT_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\sigma l}\int d\xi G_{c,l}[j\sigma;\frac{1}{\hbar}(\xi-eV)]G^{(l)}_{d}(\xi)A_{d}(\xi).

(65)

Applying the low-temperature approximations defined by Eq. (
37
), we obtain

\frac{d}{dV}G^{(l)}_{d}(\xi+eV)=-e\delta(\xi+eV).

(66)

Furthermore, for the tip electrons near the Fermi energy, we make the additional approximation

\frac{d}{dV}A_{d}(\xi+eV)=0.

(67)

Combining these results, the local dynamical conductance can be shown to follow

\sigma_{j}(V)=\frac{ie^{2}T_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\sigma l}G_{c,l}(j\sigma;-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV)A_{d}(0).

(68)

Let us introduce the following effective current operators

J_{A}(j\sigma;t,t_{1};\omega)=J^{(+)}_{A}(j\sigma;t,t_{1};\omega)+J^{(-)}_{A}(j\sigma;t,t_{1};\omega),

(69)

where
J^{(+)}_{A}
and
J^{(-)}_{A}
are defined as

\displaystyle J^{(+)}_{A}(j\sigma;t,t_{1};\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ic_{j\sigma}(t)c^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}(t_{1})e^{-i\omega(t_{1}-t)},

\displaystyle J^{(-)}_{A}(j\sigma;t,t_{1};\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ic_{j\sigma}(t_{1})c^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}(t)e^{i\omega(t_{1}-t)}.

(70)

Next, let us define a first-order current correlation function

G_{J}(j\sigma,\omega)=G^{(+)}_{J}(j\sigma,\omega)+G^{(-)}_{J}(j\sigma,\omega),

(71)

with
G^{(+)}_{J}
and
G^{(-)}_{J}
given by

\displaystyle G^{(+)}_{J}(j\sigma,\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{c}dt_{1}\langle T_{c}J^{(+)}_{A}(j\sigma;t,t_{1};\omega)\rangle,

\displaystyle G^{(-)}_{J}(j\sigma,\omega)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{c}dt_{1}\langle T_{c}J^{(-)}_{A}(j\sigma;t,t_{1};\omega)\rangle.

(72)

The following relations hold

\displaystyle J^{(-)}_{A}(j\sigma;t,t_{1};\omega)=[J^{(+)}_{A}(j\sigma;t,t_{1};\omega)]^{{\dagger}},

(73)

\displaystyle G^{(-)}_{J}(j\sigma,\omega)=-[G^{(+)}_{J}(j\sigma,\omega)]^{*}.

(74)

Furthermore, it can be shown that

G_{J}(j\sigma,\omega)=\sum_{l}G_{c,l}(j\sigma;\omega).

(75)

Consequently, the local dynamical conductance can be rewritten as

\sigma_{j}(V)=\frac{ie^{2}T_{0}^{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\sigma}G_{J}(j\sigma,-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV)A_{d}(0).

(76)

This is one main result for the single-tip STS we have obtained from the nonequilibrium theory. Eqs. (
71
) and (
74
) show that
\sigma_{j}(V)
has another equivalent expression

\sigma_{j}(V)=\frac{-e^{2}T_{0}^{2}}{\pi\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\sigma}\operatorname{Im}G^{(+)}_{J}(j\sigma,-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV)A_{d}(0).

(77)

Additionally, it can be shown that

-2\operatorname{Im}G^{(+)}_{J}(j\sigma,\omega)=\hbar A^{(c)}_{\sigma}(\hbar\omega).

(78)

It allows us to recover the previous result we have obtained from equilibrium linear-response theory, Eq. (
38
).

II.3
Fermi liquid state

Consider a system where the sample electrons are in a nearly free Fermi liquid state described by the Hamiltonian

K_{s}^{(0)}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}.

(79)

Here
c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}
is defined by

c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i}c_{i\sigma}e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{R}_{i}}.

(80)

The single-particle spectral function
A^{(c)}_{\sigma}(\varepsilon)
can be show to follow

A^{(c)}_{\sigma}(\varepsilon)=2\pi\rho_{c}(\varepsilon),\ \rho_{c}(\varepsilon)\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\delta(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}),

(81)

where
\rho_{c}(\varepsilon)
denotes the density of states of the sample electrons. In this case, the local dynamical conductance reduces to

\sigma_{j}(V)=\frac{4\pi e^{2}T_{0}^{2}}{\hbar}\rho_{c}(-eV)\rho_{d}(0).

(82)

Eq. (
82
) demonstrates that the local dynamical conductance can provide directly the local density of states of the sample electrons in Fermi liquid state.

II.4
Superconducting state

Consider a superconductor where the sample electrons are in a spin-singlet superconducting state described by a BCS mean-field theory. The Hamiltonian is given by

K_{s}^{(0)}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Psi^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}&\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}\\
\Delta^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}&-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}\end{array}\right)\Psi_{\mathbf{k}},

(83)

where
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|e^{i\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}
represents the superconducting gap function and
\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}
is the Nambu spinor operator defined as

\Psi_{\mathbf{k}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}c_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow}\\
c^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}\end{array}\right).

(84)

Perform a Bogoliubov transformation

\left(\begin{array}[]{l}c_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow}\\
c^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}\end{array}\right)=U_{\mathbf{k}}\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow}\\
\alpha^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}\end{array}\right),\ U_{\mathbf{k}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mu_{\mathbf{k}}&-\nu_{\mathbf{k}}\\
\nu^{*}_{\mathbf{k}}&\mu_{\mathbf{k}}\end{array}\right),

(85)

where
\mu_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}{E_{\mathbf{k}}})}
,
\nu_{\mathbf{k}}=e^{i\theta_{\mathbf{k}}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}}{E_{\mathbf{k}}})}
with
E_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\varepsilon^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}+|\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}}
. This transformation diagonalizes the BCS mean-field Hamiltonian to

K_{s}^{(0)}=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}E_{\mathbf{k}}(\alpha^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow}\alpha_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow}+\alpha^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{k}\downarrow}\alpha_{-\mathbf{k}\downarrow})+E_{0},

(86)

where
E_{0}=-\sum_{\mathbf{k}}E_{\mathbf{k}}
.

Following the standard procedures
(
Mahan 1990
;
Coleman 2015
)
, the single-particle spectral function can be obtained as

A^{(c)}_{\sigma}(\varepsilon)=\frac{2\pi}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}[\mu_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\delta(\varepsilon-E_{\mathbf{k}})+|\nu_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\delta(\varepsilon+E_{\mathbf{k}})].

(87)

The local dynamical conductance in the superconducting state then takes the form

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}(V)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi e^{2}T_{0}^{2}}{N\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}[\mu_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\delta(eV+E_{\mathbf{k}})+|\nu_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\delta(eV-E_{\mathbf{k}})]

(88)

\displaystyle\times\rho_{d}(0).

For the special case of
s
-wave pairing (
\Delta_{\mathbf{k}}=\Delta
), it simplifies to

\sigma_{j}(V)=\theta(|eV|-\Delta)\frac{4\pi e^{2}T_{0}^{2}}{\hbar}\rho_{c}(0)\rho_{d}(0)\frac{|eV|}{\sqrt{(eV)^{2}-\Delta^{2}}},

(89)

where we have assumed that the density of states of the sample electrons remains approximately constant near the Fermi energy in the normal state.

III
Coincidence double-tip scanning tunneling spectroscopy

In this section, we develop a new scanning tunneling spectroscopy technique, a coincidence double-tip STS. This technique extends conventional single-tip STS by enabling coincidence measurement of two spatially resolved tunneling currents. The resulting coincidence dynamical conductance can provide direct access to the spatially resolved two-body correlations of sample electrons.

III.1
Basic principle

The coincidence double-tip STS can be implemented using the double-tip STM setup depicted schematically in Fig.
1
(b). In contrast to conventional single-tip STM, this configuration utilizes two independently biased probe tips, each producing a quantum tunneling current. The two tunneling currents are measured in coincidence, quantified by the correlation function
\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}
. Below, we develop a nonequilibrium theory for the coincidence tunneling currents in the double-tip STS. The formalism constitutes a systematic second-order extension of single-tip STS theory, which naturally incorporates the two-body correlations of sample electrons.

Suppose prior to time
t_{i}
, the sample-tip composite system resides in an unbiased equilibrium state described by the density matrix

\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\beta[K_{s}^{(0)}+K_{p_{1}}^{(0)}+K_{p_{2}}^{(0)}]},

(90)

where the partition function
Z=\operatorname{Tr}\{\exp[-\beta(K_{s}^{(0)}+K_{p_{1}}^{(0)}+K_{p_{2}}^{(0)})]\}
normalizes the equilibrium distribution, and the three Hamiltonians are defined by

\displaystyle K_{s}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{s}-\mu_{s}\sum_{i\sigma}c^{{\dagger}}_{i\sigma}c_{i\sigma},

\displaystyle K_{p_{1}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{p_{1}}-\mu_{p_{1}}\sum_{i\sigma}d^{{\dagger}}_{1,i\sigma}d_{1,i\sigma},

(91)

\displaystyle K_{p_{2}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{p_{2}}-\mu_{p_{2}}\sum_{i\sigma}d^{{\dagger}}_{2,i\sigma}d_{2,i\sigma}.

Here
d^{{\dagger}}_{1,i\sigma}(d_{1,i\sigma})
and
d^{{\dagger}}_{2,i\sigma}(d_{2,i\sigma})
are the creation (annihilation) operators for the tip-1 and tip-2 electrons, respectively, with
\mu_{p_{1}}
and
\mu_{p_{2}}
representing the corresponding chemical potentials.

At time
t_{i}
, two bias voltages
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are applied, inducing two finite quantum tunneling currents. The subsequent dynamical evolution of the composite system is governed by the total Hamiltonian

K_{T}=K+V_{t},

(92)

where

K=K_{s}+K_{p_{1}}+K_{p_{2}}

(93)

incorporates the modified Hamiltonians

\displaystyle K_{s}=K_{s}^{(0)},

\displaystyle K_{p_{1}}=K_{p_{1}}^{(0)}-eV_{1}\sum_{i\sigma}d^{{\dagger}}_{1,i\sigma}d_{1,i\sigma},

(94)

\displaystyle K_{p_{2}}=K_{p_{2}}^{(0)}-eV_{2}\sum_{i\sigma}d^{{\dagger}}_{2,i\sigma}d_{2,i\sigma},

and the tunneling Hamiltonian
V_{t}

V_{t}=V_{t_{1}}+V_{t_{2}}

(95)

involves two tunneling interactions given by

\displaystyle V_{t_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{1}\sum_{\sigma}(d^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}c_{j_{1}\sigma}+c^{\dagger}_{j_{1}\sigma}d_{1\sigma}),

\displaystyle V_{t_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{2}\sum_{\sigma}(d^{\dagger}_{2\sigma}c_{j_{2}\sigma}+c^{\dagger}_{j_{2}\sigma}d_{2\sigma}).

(96)

Here, the tunneling interactions are defined to originate from two spatially localized processes, with electrons tunneling between the sample at distinct locations
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
and their respective tip positions. For conciseness, we have adopted the following simplified notations for the tip electron operators

d_{1\sigma}\equiv d_{1,j_{1}\sigma},\,d_{2\sigma}\equiv d_{2,j_{2}\sigma},

(97)

with the creation operators
d^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}
and
d^{\dagger}_{2\sigma}
defined analogously. The two corresponding tunneling current operators are defined by
I_{1}=e\frac{dN_{p_{1}}}{dt}
and
I_{2}=e\frac{dN_{p_{2}}}{dt}
, which take the forms

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{ieT_{1}}{\hbar}\sum_{\sigma}(d^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}c_{j_{1}\sigma}-c^{\dagger}_{j_{1}\sigma}d_{1\sigma}),

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{ieT_{2}}{\hbar}\sum_{\sigma}(d^{\dagger}_{2\sigma}c_{j_{2}\sigma}-c^{\dagger}_{j_{2}\sigma}d_{2\sigma}).

(98)

At observable time
t>t_{i}
, the two tunneling currents are measured in coincidence. This can be descried by the coincidence tunneling currents defined by

\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}=\langle[I_{1H}(t)I_{2H}(t)]\rangle,

(99)

where
I_{1H}(t)
and
I_{2H}(t)
denote the tunneling current operators in the Heisenberg picture defined following Eq. (
43
). The commutation
[I_{1}(t),I_{2}(t)]=0
ensures the reality of the observable value of
\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}
because

[I_{1H}(t)I_{2H}(t)]^{\dagger}=[I_{1H}(t)I_{2H}(t)].

(100)

The coincidence tunneling currents have a contour-ordered representation

\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}=\langle T_{c}S_{c}(t_{i},t_{i})I_{1I}(t)I_{2I}(t)\rangle,

(101)

where
S_{c}
follows Eq. (
42
) with the modified tunneling Hamiltonian in the interaction picture

V_{I}(t)=V_{1I}(t)+V_{2I}(t).

(102)

The two individual tunneling interactions in the interaction picture are given by

\displaystyle V_{1I}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{1}\sum_{a=\pm}A_{1}^{(a)}(t)e^{ia\phi_{1}(t)},

\displaystyle V_{2I}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{2}\sum_{a=\pm}A_{2}^{(a)}(t)e^{ia\phi_{2}(t)},

(103)

with the fermionic bilinears
A_{1}^{(a)}
and
A_{2}^{(a)}

(a=\pm)
defined as

\displaystyle A_{1}^{(+)}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}d^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}(t)c_{j_{1}\sigma}(t),\ A_{1}^{(-)}(t)\equiv[A_{1}^{(+)}(t)]^{\dagger},

\displaystyle A_{2}^{(+)}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}d^{\dagger}_{2\sigma}(t)c_{j_{2}\sigma}(t),\ A_{2}^{(-)}(t)\equiv[A_{2}^{(+)}(t)]^{\dagger}.\quad\qquad

(104)

Here the time-dependent operators evolve as

\displaystyle c_{j\sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}K_{s}^{(0)}t}c_{j\sigma}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}K_{s}^{(0)}t},

\displaystyle d_{1\sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}K_{p_{1}}^{(0)}t}d_{1\sigma}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}K_{p_{1}}^{(0)}t},

(105)

\displaystyle d_{2\sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}K_{p_{2}}^{(0)}t}d_{2\sigma}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}K_{p_{2}}^{(0)}t},

with similar time evolutions of
c^{\dagger}_{j\sigma}(t)
,
d^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}(t)
and
d^{\dagger}_{2\sigma}(t)
. The phase factors
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
are defined by

\phi_{1}(t)=-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV_{1}t,\,\phi_{2}(t)=-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV_{2}t.

(106)

The tunneling current operators in the interaction picture are similarly given by

\displaystyle I_{1I}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{ieT_{1}}{\hbar}\sum_{a=\pm}a\cdot A_{1}^{(a)}(t)e^{ia\phi_{1}(t)},

\displaystyle I_{2I}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{ieT_{2}}{\hbar}\sum_{a=\pm}a\cdot A_{2}^{(a)}(t)e^{ia\phi_{2}(t)}.

(107)

By expanding the
S_{c}
matrix in a Taylor series, the perturbation contributions to the coincidence tunneling currents can be calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}

(108)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle I_{1I}(t)I_{2I}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}\right)\int_{c}dt_{1}\langle T_{c}V_{I}(t_{1})I_{1I}(t)I_{2I}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\iint_{c}dt_{2}dt_{1}\langle T_{c}V_{I}(t_{2})V_{I}(t_{1})I_{1I}(t)I_{2I}(t)\rangle.

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\cdots.

Due to the conservation of the electron numbers in the two tips, we have
\langle d_{1\sigma}\rangle=\langle d^{\dagger}_{1\sigma}\rangle=0
and
\langle d_{2\sigma}\rangle=\langle d^{\dagger}_{2\sigma}\rangle=0
. Consequently, both the zeroth- and first-order contributions to the coincidence tunneling currents vanish,
i.e.
,

\langle I_{1I}(t)I_{2I}(t)\rangle=0,

(109)

and

\langle T_{c}V_{I}(t_{1})I_{1I}(t)I_{2I}(t)\rangle=0.

(110)

Thus, the lowest-order non-vanishing contribution arises from the second-order expansion of the
S_{c}
matrix,

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\iint_{c}dt_{2}dt_{1}\langle T_{c}V_{I}(t_{2})V_{I}(t_{1})I_{1I}(t)I_{2I}(t)\rangle.

It can be further shown that
\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}
can decompose into a sum over several contributions

\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}=\sum_{a_{1}a_{2}=\pm}\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}^{(a_{1}a_{2})},

(112)

where these contributions take the explicit forms as

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}^{(a_{1}a_{2})}

(113)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(eT_{1}T_{2})^{2}}{\hbar^{4}}\iint_{c}dt_{2}dt_{1}e^{ia_{1}[\phi_{1}(t)-\phi_{1}(t_{1})]+ia_{2}[\phi_{2}(t)-\phi_{2}(t_{2})]}

\displaystyle\times a_{1}a_{2}\cdot\langle T_{c}A_{1}^{(a_{1})}(t)A_{2}^{(a_{2})}(t)A_{2}^{(\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{a}_{2})}(t_{2})A_{1}^{(\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{a}_{1})}(t_{1})\rangle.

Here we have introduced the notations

\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{a}_{1}\equiv-a_{1},\,\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{a}_{2}\equiv-a_{2}.

(114)

Let us begin by considering
\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}^{(++)}
. To facilitate our analysis, we introduce the following contour-ordered Green’s functions for the sample electrons

\displaystyle G_{c}(j_{1}\sigma_{1}t_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2}t_{2};j_{2}\sigma_{2}t_{2}^{\prime},j_{1}\sigma_{1}t_{1}^{\prime})

(115)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-i)^{2}\langle T_{c}c_{j_{1}\sigma_{1}}(t_{1})c_{j_{2}\sigma_{2}}(t_{2})c^{\dagger}_{j_{2}\sigma_{2}}(t_{2}^{\prime})c^{\dagger}_{j_{1}\sigma_{1}}(t_{1}^{\prime})\rangle,\qquad

and for the tip electrons

\displaystyle G_{d_{1}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t_{1},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\langle T_{c}d_{1\sigma_{1}}(t_{1})d^{\dagger}_{1\sigma_{1}}(t)\rangle,

\displaystyle G_{d_{2}}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t_{2},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\langle T_{c}d_{2\sigma_{2}}(t_{2})d^{\dagger}_{2\sigma_{2}}(t)\rangle

(116)

With these definitions, we derive the following expression as

\displaystyle\langle T_{c}A_{1}^{(+)}(t)A_{2}^{(+)}(t)A_{2}^{(-)}(t_{2})A_{1}^{(-)}(t_{1})\rangle

(117)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}G_{c}(j_{1}\sigma_{1}t,j_{2}\sigma_{2}t;j_{2}\sigma_{2}t_{2},j_{1}\sigma_{1}t_{1})\cdot G_{d_{1}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t_{1},t)

\displaystyle\times G_{d_{2}}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t_{2},t).

Following the methodology established in the nonequilibrium theory for the single-tip STS, we also introduce the greater and lesser Green’s functions for the tip electrons:

\displaystyle G^{>}_{d_{1}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t_{1},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\langle d_{1\sigma_{1}}(t_{1})d^{{\dagger}}_{1\sigma_{1}}(t)\rangle,

\displaystyle G^{<}_{d_{1}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t_{1},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\langle d^{{\dagger}}_{1\sigma_{1}}(t)d_{1\sigma_{1}}(t_{1})\rangle,

(118)

and

\displaystyle G^{>}_{d_{2}}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t_{2},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\langle d_{2\sigma_{2}}(t_{2})d^{{\dagger}}_{2\sigma_{2}}(t)\rangle,

\displaystyle G^{<}_{d_{2}}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t_{2},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\langle d^{{\dagger}}_{2\sigma_{2}}(t)d_{2\sigma_{2}}(t_{2})\rangle.

(119)

The contour-ordered Green’s functions for the tip electrons can then be expressed as

\displaystyle G_{d_{1}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t_{1},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta_{c}(t_{1}-t)G^{>}_{d_{1}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t_{1},t)

(120)

\displaystyle+\theta_{c}(t-t_{1})G^{<}_{d_{1}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t_{1},t),

\displaystyle G_{d_{2}}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t_{2},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta_{c}(t_{2}-t)G^{>}_{d_{2}}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t_{2},t)

(121)

\displaystyle+\theta_{c}(t-t_{2})G^{<}_{d_{2}}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t_{2},t).

In analogy with Eq. (
55
), we further introduce the Fourier transformations of the greater and lesser Green’s functions
G^{(l)}_{d_{1}}
and
G^{(l)}_{d_{2}}
(where
l=>
or
<
). This allows us to express
\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}^{(++)}
as

\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}^{(++)}=\left(\frac{eT_{1}T_{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}l_{1}l_{2}}\iint d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}G^{(++)}_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\omega_{1}-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV_{1},\omega_{2}-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV_{2})G^{(l_{1})}_{d_{1}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},\omega_{1})G^{(l_{2})}_{d_{2}}(j_{2}\sigma_{2},\omega_{2}),

(122)

where
G^{(++)}_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}
is defined by

G^{(++)}_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=\iint_{c}dt_{2}dt_{1}\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\theta}^{(l_{1})}_{c}(t_{1}-t)\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\theta}^{(l_{2})}_{c}(t_{2}-t)G_{c}(j_{1}\sigma_{1}t,j_{2}\sigma_{2}t;j_{2}\sigma_{2}t_{2},j_{1}\sigma_{1}t_{1})e^{-i\omega_{1}(t_{1}-t)-i\omega_{2}(t_{2}-t)}.

(123)

Here,
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\theta}^{(l)}_{c}(t)
follows the definition given in Eq. (
58
). The functions
G^{(l_{1})}_{d_{1}}
and
G^{(l_{2})}_{d_{2}}
in Eq. (
122
) adopt forms analogous to Eq. (
59
),

G^{(l_{1})}_{d_{1}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};\omega_{1})=\frac{2\pi i}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}G^{(l)}_{d_{1}}[\xi^{(1)}_{\mathbf{q}}]\,\delta[\omega_{1}-\frac{1}{\hbar}\xi^{(1)}_{\mathbf{q}}],\ G^{(l_{2})}_{d_{2}}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};\omega_{2})=\frac{2\pi i}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}G^{(l_{2})}_{d_{2}}[\xi^{(2)}_{\mathbf{q}}]\,\delta[\omega_{2}-\frac{1}{\hbar}\xi^{(2)}_{\mathbf{q}}].

(124)

Next, we define the spectral functions for the tip electrons

A_{d_{1}}(\xi_{1})=2\pi\rho_{d_{1}}(\xi_{1})=\frac{2\pi}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\delta[\xi_{1}-\xi^{(1)}_{\mathbf{q}}],\ A_{d_{2}}(\xi_{2})=2\pi\rho_{d_{2}}(\xi_{2})=\frac{2\pi}{N}\sum_{\mathbf{q}}\delta[\xi_{2}-\xi^{(2)}_{\mathbf{q}}],

(125)

where
\xi^{(1)}_{\mathbf{q}}
and
\xi^{(2)}_{\mathbf{q}}

denote the energy dispersions of the tip-1 and tip-2 electrons, respectively. Using these definitions,
\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}^{(++)}
can be reexpressed into the following form:

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}^{(++)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{eT_{1}T_{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}l_{1}l_{2}}\iint d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}G^{(++)}_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\frac{1}{\hbar}(\xi_{1}-eV_{1}),\frac{1}{\hbar}(\xi_{2}-eV_{2}))G^{(l_{1})}_{d_{1}}(\xi_{1})G^{(l_{2})}_{d_{2}}(\xi_{2})

(126)

\displaystyle\times A_{d_{1}}(\xi_{1})A_{d_{2}}(\xi_{2}).

The remaining three contributions can be derived in a similar manner. Their detailed forms are provided in Appendix
A
.

We define the generalized correlation function
G_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}
as

G_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=-\sum_{a_{1}a_{2}=\pm}(-1)^{a_{1}\cdot a_{2}}G_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}^{(a_{1}a_{2})}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

(127)

Using this definition,
\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}
can be shown to follow

\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}=\left(\frac{eT_{1}T_{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}l_{1}l_{2}}\iint d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}G_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\frac{1}{\hbar}(\xi_{1}-eV_{1}),\frac{1}{\hbar}(\xi_{2}-eV_{2}))G^{(l_{1})}_{d_{1}}(\xi_{1})G^{(l_{2})}_{d_{2}}(\xi_{2})A_{d_{1}}(\xi_{1})A_{d_{2}}(\xi_{2}).

(128)

This represents the fundamental result for the coincidence tunneling currents in our proposed double-tip STS.

Let us introduce a coincidence dynamical conductance for the double-tip STS:

\sigma_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})=\frac{d}{dV_{2}}\frac{d}{dV_{1}}\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}.

(129)

Following the treatment in Eq. (
66
), we consider the low-temperature limit where the tip electron Green’s function derivatives become

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dV_{1}}G^{(l_{1})}_{d_{1}}(\xi_{1}+eV_{1})=-e\delta(\xi_{1}+eV_{1}),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dV_{2}}G^{(l_{2})}_{d_{2}}(\xi_{2}+eV_{2})=-e\delta(\xi_{2}+eV_{2}).

(130)

Furthermore, we assume the tip electron spectral functions remain constant near the Fermi level,

\frac{d}{dV_{1}}A_{d_{1}}(\xi_{1}+eV_{1})=0,\,\frac{d}{dV_{2}}A_{d_{2}}(\xi_{2}+eV_{2})=0.

(131)

Under these approximations, the coincidence dynamical conductance follows

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{e^{2}T_{1}T_{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}l_{1}l_{2}}G_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1},\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2})

(132)

\displaystyle\times A_{d_{1}}(0)A_{d_{2}}(0),

where the effective frequencies
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}
and
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2}
are defined as

\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}=-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV_{1},\,\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2}=-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV_{2}.

(133)

Building on the approach in Eq. (
69
), we introduce the following effective current operators

\displaystyle J_{1A}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t,t_{1};\omega_{1})

(134)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J^{(+)}_{1A}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t,t_{1};\omega_{1})+J^{(-)}_{1A}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t,t_{1};\omega_{1}),

\displaystyle J_{2A}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t,t_{2};\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J^{(+)}_{2A}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t,t_{2};\omega_{2})+J^{(-)}_{2A}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t,t_{2};\omega_{2}),

where the constituent current operators
J^{(+)}_{1A}
and
J^{(-)}_{1A}
for the tip-1 electrons are given by

\displaystyle J^{(+)}_{1A}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t,t_{1};\omega_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ic_{j_{1}\sigma_{1}}(t)c^{\dagger}_{j_{1}\sigma_{1}}(t_{1})e^{-i\omega_{1}(t_{1}-t)},

\displaystyle J^{(-)}_{1A}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t,t_{1};\omega_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ic_{j_{1}\sigma_{1}}(t_{1})c^{\dagger}_{j_{1}\sigma_{1}}(t)e^{i\omega_{1}(t_{1}-t)},

(135)

and similarly for the tip-2 electron current operators
J^{(+)}_{2A}
and
J^{(-)}_{2A}
are defined by

\displaystyle J^{(+)}_{2A}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t,t_{2};\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ic_{j_{2}\sigma_{2}}(t)c^{\dagger}_{j_{2}\sigma_{2}}(t_{2})e^{-i\omega_{2}(t_{2}-t)},

\displaystyle J^{(-)}_{2A}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t,t_{2};\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ic_{j_{2}\sigma_{2}}(t_{2})c^{\dagger}_{j_{2}\sigma_{2}}(t)e^{i\omega_{2}(t_{2}-t)}.

(136)

Let us define the second-order current correlation function

\displaystyle G^{(2)}_{J}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{c}dt_{2}dt_{1}\langle T_{c}J_{1A}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t,t_{1};\omega_{1})J_{2A}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t,t_{2};\omega_{2})\rangle.

Remarkably, this connects to our earlier expression through

G^{(2)}_{J}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=-\sum_{l_{1}l_{2}}G_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2}).

(138)

This relation reveals the deep connection between the current fluctuations and the sample electron correlations. Thus, the coincidence dynamical conductance takes its final form

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{e^{2}T_{1}T_{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}G^{(2)}_{J}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1},\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2})

(139)

\displaystyle\times A_{d_{1}}(0)A_{d_{2}}(0),

with
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}
and
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2}
defined in Eq. (
133
). This represents a cornerstone result in our double-tip STS theory, demonstrating that the coincidence dynamical conductance directly probes the two-body correlations of sample electrons through the second-order current correlation function. Therefore, the double-tip STS provides a powerful technique for extracting the sample electron correlation features that are inaccessible in conventional single-tip STS measurements.

III.2
Fermi liquid state

Let us study the double-tip STS of the sample electrons in a nearly free Fermi liquid state, which is described by the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
79
). Starting from the coincidence dynamical conductance given in Eq. (
139
), our first objective is to calculate the second-order current correlation function
G^{(2)}_{J}
.
According to Wick’s theorem
(
Langreth 1976
;
Rammer 2007
;
Haug and Jauho 2008
)
,

G^{(2)}_{J}
consists of two distinct contributions

G^{(2)}_{J}=G^{(2)}_{J,D}+G^{(2)}_{J,E},

(140)

where the direct term
G^{(2)}_{J,D}
is given by

G^{(2)}_{J,D}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})=G_{J}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},\omega_{1})\cdot G_{J}(j_{2}\sigma_{2},\omega_{2}),

(141)

and the exchange term
G^{(2)}_{J,E}
will be derived in detail below. Correspondingly, the coincidence dynamical conductance also decomposes into two contributions

\sigma_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})=\sigma^{(D)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})+\sigma^{(E)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2}).

(142)

From Eq. (
141
), the direct conductance term
\sigma^{(D)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}
takes the form

\sigma^{(D)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})=\sigma_{j_{1}}(V_{1})\cdot\sigma_{j_{2}}(V_{2}),

(143)

where
\sigma_{j_{1}}
and
\sigma_{j_{2}}
are given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{1}}(V_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{ie^{2}T_{1}^{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\sigma_{1}}G_{J}(j_{1}\sigma_{1},-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV_{1})A_{d_{1}}(0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{2}}(V_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{ie^{2}T_{2}^{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\sum_{\sigma_{2}}G_{J}(j_{2}\sigma_{2},-\frac{1}{\hbar}eV_{2})A_{d_{2}}(0).

(144)

In the Fermi liquid state, these expressions reduce to (via Eq. (
82
))

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{1}}(V_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi e^{2}T_{1}^{2}}{\hbar}\rho_{c}(-eV_{1})\rho_{d_{1}}(0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{2}}(V_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi e^{2}T_{2}^{2}}{\hbar}\rho_{c}(-eV_{2})\rho_{d_{2}}(0).

(145)

Now, let us calculate the exchange contributions
G^{(2)}_{J,E}
and
\sigma^{(E)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}
. The exchange term
G^{(2)}_{J,E}
can be partitioned into four components

G^{(2)}_{J,E}=\sum_{a_{1}a_{2}=\pm}G^{(a_{1}a_{2})}_{J,E},

(146)

G^{(2)}_{J,E}

139
), the exchange contribution
\sigma^{(E)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}
can be expressed compactly as

\displaystyle\sigma^{(E)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{e^{2}T_{1}T_{2}}{\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\operatorname{Re}\left[\mathscr{G}_{R}(\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1},-\Delta\mathbf{R})\mathscr{G}_{R}(\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2},\Delta\mathbf{R})\right]

\displaystyle\times A_{d_{1}}(0)A_{d_{2}}(0),

\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}
and
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2}
are defined in Eq. (
133
).
While the direct conductance
\sigma^{(D)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}
arises from two independent local electron tunneling events at each tip location, the exchange conductance
\sigma^{(E)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}
emerges from two correlated nonlocal dynamical processes: (i) tunneling electrons detected at tip-1 have propagated nonlocally from tip-2’s location, while (ii) those detected at tip-2 have conversely propagated from tip-1’s location.
More precisely, the definition of
\mathscr{G}_{R}
reveals that the exchange conductance
\sigma^{(E)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}
captures two correlated dynamical electron propagation processes: (i) from
\mathbf{R}_{j_{1}}
to
\mathbf{R}_{j_{2}}
(or vice versa) under
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}
dynamics driven by the bias voltage
V_{1}
, and (ii) from
\mathbf{R}_{j_{2}}
to
\mathbf{R}_{j_{1}}
(or vice versa) under
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2}
dynamics driven by the voltage
V_{2}
. Therefore, the exchange conductance
\sigma^{(E)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}
directly probes two-electron dynamical propagation in the sample, providing direct access to the dynamical two-body correlations that transcend the single-particle physics.

III.3
Superconducting state

Let us now consider the double-tip STS of the sample electrons in the superconducting state described by the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
83
). The total coincidence dynamical conductance also consists of two contributions: the direct term
\sigma_{j_{1}j_{2}}^{(D)}
and the exchange term
\sigma_{j_{1}j_{2}}^{(E)}
,

\sigma_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})=\sigma^{(D)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})+\sigma^{(E)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2}).

(160)

The direct term
\sigma^{(D)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}
is the product of the individual conductances at two tips as

\sigma^{(D)}_{j_{1}j_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})=\sigma_{j_{1}}(V_{1})\cdot\sigma_{j_{2}}(V_{2}),

(161)

where the two individual conductances are given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{1}}(V_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi e^{2}T_{1}^{2}}{N\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}[\mu_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\delta(eV_{1}+E_{\mathbf{k}})+|\nu_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\delta(eV_{1}-E_{\mathbf{k}})]

(162)

\displaystyle\times\rho_{d_{1}}(0),

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{2}}(V_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi e^{2}T_{2}^{2}}{N\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf{k}}[\mu_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\delta(eV_{2}+E_{\mathbf{k}})+|\nu_{\mathbf{k}}|^{2}\delta(eV_{2}-E_{\mathbf{k}})]

(163)

\displaystyle\times\rho_{d_{2}}(0).

To compute the exchange contributions, we introduce the Nambu spinors

\Phi_{j_{1}}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{l}\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{c}_{j_{1}\uparrow}(t)\\
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{c}_{j_{1}\downarrow}^{\dagger}(t)\end{array}\right),\ \Phi_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}(t)=\left(\begin{array}[]{ll}\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{c}^{\dagger}_{j_{1}\uparrow}(t)&\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{c}_{j_{1}\downarrow}(t)\end{array}\right),

(164)

where the modified operators
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{c}_{j_{1}\sigma}(t)
and
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{c}^{\dagger}_{j_{1}\sigma}(t)
are defined as

\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{c}_{j_{1}\sigma}(t)=c_{j_{1}\sigma}(t)e^{i\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}t},\ \accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{c}^{\dagger}_{j_{1}\sigma}(t)=c^{\dagger}_{j_{1}\sigma}(t)e^{-i\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}t}.

(165)

Here
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}
is given by Eq. (
133
). We define the current operator
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{J}_{1A}
as

\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{J}_{1A}(j_{1};t,t_{1};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1})=\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{J}^{(+)}_{1A}(j_{1};t,t_{1};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1})+\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{J}^{(-)}_{1A}(j_{1};t,t_{1};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}),

(166)

with the components
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{J}^{(+)}_{1A}
and
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{J}^{(-)}_{1A}
are defined by

\displaystyle\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{J}^{(+)}_{1A}(j_{1};t,t_{1};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1})=i\Phi^{\dagger}_{j_{1}}(t_{1})\Phi_{j_{1}}(t),

\displaystyle\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{J}^{(-)}_{1A}(i;t,t_{1};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1})=-i\Phi^{\dagger}_{j_{1}}(t)\Phi_{j_{1}}(t_{1}).

(167)

This operator satisfies

\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{J}_{1A}(j_{1};t,t_{1};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1})=\sum_{\sigma_{1}}J_{1A}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t,t_{1};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}).

(168)

Analogously, we define the Nambu spinors
\Phi_{j_{2}}(t)
and
\Phi_{j_{2}}^{\dagger}(t)
by using
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{c}_{j_{2}\sigma}(t)
and
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{c}^{\dagger}_{j_{2}\sigma}(t)
, the latter of which are defined similarly by Eq. (
164
) where
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}
is substituted by
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2}
. Here
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2}
has also been defined in Eq. (
133
). This yields

\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{J}_{2A}(j_{2};t,t_{2};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2})=\sum_{\sigma_{2}}J_{2A}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t,t_{2};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2}),

(169)

where the current operator

IV
An extended double-tip scanning tunneling spectroscopy

IV.1
Basic principle

In Sec.
III
, we introduced a coincidence double-tip STS for measuring two tunneling currents simultaneously. Here, we extend this concept to measurements at different times by defining the observable

\overline{\langle I_{1}(t_{1})I_{2}(t_{2})\rangle}_{c}=\langle[I_{1H}(t_{1})I_{2H}(t_{2})]_{c}\rangle,

(187)

where the symmetrized current product is given by

[I_{1H}(t_{1})I_{2H}(t_{2})]_{c}=\frac{1}{2}[I_{1H}(t_{1})I_{2H}(t_{2})+I_{2H}(t_{2})I_{1H}(t_{1})].

(188)

This quantity is real-valued and satisfies

\overline{\langle I_{1}(t_{1})I_{2}(t_{2})\rangle}_{c}=\operatorname{Re}\overline{\langle I_{1}(t_{1})I_{2}(t_{2})\rangle},

(189)

with

\overline{\langle I_{1}(t_{1})I_{2}(t_{2})\rangle}=\langle T_{c}S_{c}(t_{i},t_{i})I_{1I}(t_{1})I_{2I}(t_{2})\rangle.

(190)

Here the time contour is defined as
t_{i}\rightarrow t_{f}\rightarrow t_{i}
with
t_{f}=\operatorname{max}\{t_{1},t_{2}\}
.

To second order in perturbation theory, we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{1}(t_{1})I_{2}(t_{2})\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{-i}{\hbar}\right)^{2}\iint_{c}dt_{2}^{\prime}dt_{1}^{\prime}\langle T_{c}V_{I}(t_{2}^{\prime})V_{I}(t_{1}^{\prime})I_{1I}(t_{1})I_{2I}(t_{2})\rangle.

The correlation function decomposes into four components

\overline{\langle I_{1}(t_{1})I_{2}(t_{2})\rangle}=\sum_{a_{1}a_{2}=\pm}\overline{\langle I_{1}(t_{1})I_{2}(t_{2})\rangle}^{(a_{1}a_{2})},

(192)

where each term takes the form

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{1}(t_{1})I_{2}(t_{2})\rangle}^{(a_{1}a_{2})}

(193)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(eT_{1}T_{2})^{2}}{\hbar^{4}}\iint_{c}dt_{2}^{\prime}dt_{1}^{\prime}e^{ia_{1}[\phi_{1}(t_{1})-\phi_{1}(t_{1}^{\prime})]+ia_{2}[\phi_{2}(t_{2})-\phi_{2}(t_{2}^{\prime})]}

\displaystyle\times a_{1}a_{2}\cdot\langle T_{c}A_{1}^{(a_{1})}(t_{1})A_{2}^{(a_{2})}(t_{2})A_{2}^{(\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{a}_{2})}(t_{2}^{\prime})A_{1}^{(\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{a}_{1})}(t_{1}^{\prime})\rangle.\,\qquad

Following the methodology of Sec.
III
, we evaluate these terms with the results presented in Appendix
C
.

Introducing the correlation function

\displaystyle G_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1}t_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2}t_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

(194)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{a_{1}a_{2}=\pm}(-1)^{a_{1}\cdot a_{2}}G_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}^{(a_{1}a_{2})}(j_{1}\sigma_{1}t_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2}t_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2}),\,\qquad

where
G_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}^{(a_{1}a_{2})}
are defined in Appendix
C
, we derive the current correlation as

\displaystyle\overline{\langle I_{1}(t_{1})I_{2}(t_{2})\rangle}_{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{eT_{1}T_{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}l_{1}l_{2}}\iint d\xi_{1}d\xi_{2}\operatorname{Re}[G_{c,l_{1}l_{2}}(j_{1}\sigma_{1}t_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2}t_{2};\frac{1}{\hbar}(\xi_{1}-eV_{1}),\frac{1}{\hbar}(\xi_{2}-eV_{2}))]

(195)

\displaystyle\times G^{(l_{1})}_{d_{1}}(\xi_{1})G^{(l_{2})}_{d_{2}}(\xi_{2})A_{d_{1}}(\xi_{1})A_{d_{2}}(\xi_{2}).

This expression parallels Eq. (
128
) for simultaneous coincidence double-tip STS.

We define the coincidence dynamical conductance for the extended double-tip STS as

\sigma_{j_{1}t_{1},j_{2}t_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})=\frac{d}{dV_{2}}\frac{d}{dV_{1}}\overline{\langle I_{1}(t_{1})I_{2}(t_{2})\rangle}_{c}.

(196)

It evaluates to

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{1}t_{1},j_{2}t_{2}}(V_{1},V_{2})

(197)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{e^{2}T_{1}T_{2}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\right)^{2}\sum_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\operatorname{Re}[G^{(2)}_{J}(j_{1}\sigma_{1}t_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2}t_{2};\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1},\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2})]

\displaystyle\times A_{d_{1}}(0)A_{d_{2}}(0),

where
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{1}
and
\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}_{2}
are given in Eq. (
133
), and
G^{(2)}_{J}
represents the second-order current correlation function

\displaystyle G^{(2)}_{J}(j_{1}\sigma_{1}t_{1},j_{2}\sigma_{2}t_{2};\omega_{1},\omega_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{c}dt_{2}^{\prime}dt_{1}^{\prime}\langle T_{c}J_{1A}(j_{1}\sigma_{1};t_{1},t_{1}^{\prime};\omega_{1})J_{2A}(j_{2}\sigma_{2};t_{2},t_{2}^{\prime};\omega_{2})\rangle.

Notably, when
t_{1}=t_{2}
, Eq. (
197
) reduces to the simultaneous coincidence measurement result given in Eq. (
139
), demonstrating consistency between the extended and simultaneous coincidence double-tip STS formalisms.

V
Discussions and conclusion

In this work, we propose a coincidence double-tip STS for spatially resolved measurement of dynamical two-body correlations. This technique extends conventional single-tip STS by using a double-tip STM setup, where two independently biased tips (with voltages
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
) simultaneously measure quantum tunneling currents
I_{1}(t)
and
I_{2}(t)
at distinct locations
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
. From the coincidence tunneling current correlation
\overline{\langle I_{1}(t)I_{2}(t)\rangle}
, we define a coincidence dynamical conductance via differentiation with respect to the bias voltages
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
. Our nonequilibrium theory reveals that the coincidence dynamical conductance directly probes a contour-ordered second-order current correlation function, offering a powerful approach to study strongly correlated electron systems.

To demonstrate the capabilities of the coincidence double-tip STS, we have applied the nonequilibrium theory to calculate the coincidence dynamical conductances for two electronic states, a nearly free Fermi liquid and a superconducting state. The coincidence dynamical conductance comprises two distinct components, the direct conductance and the exchange conductance. The direct conductance arises as a product of the individual single-tip STS dynamical conductances associated with each probing tip, reflecting independent local tunneling processes. The exchange conductance is a fundamentally nonlocal contribution which captures correlated electron propagations between the two tips, where (i) electrons detected at tip-1 originate from tip-2’s location, and (ii) conversely, electrons detected at tip-2 propagate from tip-1’s location. Crucially, the exchange conductance can provide direct experimental access to dynamical two-body correlations that cannot be described within single-particle physics. In superconducting systems, this term reveals particularly rich physics as it incorporates both the conventional normal electron propagations and the superconducting two-particle processes arising from the condensates. Thus, through the exchange conductance, the coincidence double-tip STS offers unique insights into the dynamical two-body properties of superconducting condensates.

We further generalize this technique to time-resolved measurement by introducing a temporal delay
\Delta t=t_{2}-t_{1}
between the two tunneling current detections. The resulting extended coincidence dynamical conductance acquires a phase factor
\exp(i\Delta\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}\cdot\Delta t)
where
\Delta\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}=e(V_{1}-V_{2})/\hbar
. This formalism imposes the following experimental condition for simultaneous coincidence double-tip STS measurements

|\Delta\accentset{{\cc@style\underline{\mskip 10.0mu}}}{\omega}\cdot\Delta t|\ll 1.

(205)

In most strongly correlated electron systems, the dominant correlations originate from highly localized physics, typically characterized by correlation lengths comparable to interatomic distances (a few
Å
). This presents significant experimental challenge for resolving the two-body nonlocal correlations using coincidence double-tip STS, as it requires precise control of tip separations at these extremely short length scales. The recent discovery of unconventional superconductivity and Mott physics in magic-angle twisted bilayer graphene
(
Cao et al. 2018
)
offers a promising alternative platform. In this system, the exotic correlated phenomena are intimately relevant to the moiré superlattice, whose characteristic periodicity (
\sim 13~\text{nm}
) defines a mesoscopic length scale. This length scale is amenable to investigation via coincidence double-tip STS, as modern double-tip STM setups have successfully achieved tip separations within the
\sim 30~\text{nm}
range
(
Nakayama et al. 2012
;
Leeuwenhoek et al. 2020b
)
, making such measurements experimentally feasible. Consequently, moiré quantum materials emerge as a platform for implementing coincidence double-tip STS to probe nonlocal dynamical two-body correlations in strongly correlated electron systems.

In summary, building upon the nonequilibrium theoretical framework developed in this work, we propose a coincidence double-tip STS for direct measurement of spatially resolved dynamical two-body correlations. This technique provides a powerful tool for investigating the exotic physics of strongly correlated electron systems.
