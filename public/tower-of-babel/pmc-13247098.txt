Comparison of the long-term impact of laparoscopy and open surgery on the prognosis of patients with locally advanced gastric cancer
Authors: Ming-cong Deng, Ken Chen, Qi-mei Bao, Yi-xing Huang, Chun-kai Zhang, Yu-ke Zhong, Han-yi He, Dan Zu, Chen Liang, Hai-dong Liu, Yang-chan Hu, Guo-xia Liu, Yan-hua He, Wei-xin Wu, Ji Jing, Yin Shi, Yi-an Du, Yao-shu Teng, Zu Ye, Xiang-dong Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Currently, the choice of surgical procedure for patients with locally advanced gastric cancer remains controversial. This study aimed to investigate the difference in prognosis between laparoscopy and open surgery in patients with locally advanced gastric cancer and explored the potential patients suitable for laparoscopy. Gastric cancer patients who underwent surgical treatment at Zhejiang Cancer Hospital from 2008 to 2019 were included in this study. We found that surgical method was an independent risk factor for the prognosis of patients with gastric cancer (p < 0.01). The prognosis of laparoscopic surgery is better than that of open surgery two years after surgery (p < 0.05) but shows no difference in overall survival (p > 0.05); in patients with locally advanced cancer, a better prognosis is demonstrated as early as six months after undergoing laparoscopic surgery (p < 0.05). Patients who were male, aged over 60 years, had a history of smoking and drinking, had tumors located in the upper stomach, exhibited poor tumor differentiation, and had normal serum AFP and CA72-4 levels experienced significantly better survival with laparoscopic surgery compared to open surgery (p < 0.05). Finally, a prognostic model in locally advanced cancer patients was developed. Our study suggests that laparoscopy for patients with locally advanced-stage gastric cancer can lead to a better prognosis in these specific situations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48276-x.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a malignant tumor of epithelial origin that originates in the stomach and is the fifth most common cancer globally and has the fifth highest mortality rate among all malignant tumors; adenocarcinoma is the most common type of gastric cancer with high invasiveness and metastatic ability, accounting for approximately 90% of all gastric cancers1. According to data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) of the World Health Organization, in 2020, there were approximately 1.04 million new cases of gastric cancer and 769,000 deaths worldwide2. China is a country with a high incidence of stomach cancer, second only to lung cancer, with about 456,000 new cases in 2020, accounting for 43.9% of global stomach cancer cases3. Above all, gastric cancer remains a serious public health problem due to regional differences, age distribution characteristics and treatment challenges4..

At present, there are many new options for gastric cancer treatment, including molecular targeted therapy, immunotherapy and neoadjuvant radiotherapy, but surgery is still the most important treatment method. The different surgical approaches can be divided into open surgery (OS) and laparoscopic surgery (LS). Current research shows that LS is less traumatic than OS, with less intraoperative bleeding and faster postoperative recovery, which is reflected mainly in the length of the surgical incision, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative venting time, and hospitalization time5. In addition, LS has less impact on the immune function of patients, which can improve the safety of treatment and reduce the incidence of complications6. In recent years, LS has been widely used to treat gastric cancer and has become mainstream in the treatment of early gastric cancer. However, there is no clear trend in which surgical approach to choose in locally advanced GC patients and is highly dependent on the accurate assessment of the patient (including the patient’s general status, biological characteristics of the tumor, etc.), as well as the surgeon’s own experience, and therefore is highly subjective. There is a lack of clinical data on which surgical approaches are more suitable for patients with locally advanced GC or which patients are more suitable for LS. Therefore, using reasonable methods to explore cancer patients who are more suitable for a certain surgical approach will help clinicians choose more favorable treatments for patients based on their actual situation, thereby improving the accuracy of clinical decision-making and the prognosis of GC.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study included 4,268 patients with gastric cancer who were diagnosed and underwent surgery at Zhejiang Cancer Hospital, affiliated with the Chinese Academy of Sciences, over a 12-year period from 2008 to 2019. Samples with extreme levels of serum CEA (> 5000), CA125 (> 5000), CA242 (> 1200), CA19.9 (> 12000), and AFP (> 12000) were excluded (n = 3), as these values reached the upper limits of the equipment in our hospital and were significantly outliers. The dataset comprises de-identified information, including demographics, clinicopathologic characteristics, treatment details, and survival status. Patients were selected based on the following criteria: (1) clear clinicopathological staging (AJCC-TNM 8th edition) performed by Professional surgeons; (2) underwent either radical laparoscopic or radical open surgery, which were performed by senior surgeons; and (3) availability of data on age, gender, tumor status and location, and pre-treatment serum levels of tumor markers. Patients with postoperative residual gastric cancer, unsuccessful surgery, conversion from laparoscopic to open surgery or malignant metastases in other organs were excluded. The selection of the surgical approach in this study and the postoperative chemotherapy regimen was based on the Chinese Laparoscopic Gastric Cancer Surgery Operation Guidelines (2007 and 2016 editions), to ensure as much as possible that all treatment options follow the recommended guidelines. Exceptions were also made based on patient preferences or specific health conditions that contraindicated a particular surgical approach. Follow-up for these postoperative gastric cancer patients was conducted at regular intervals of 3–6 months, with data collection continuing until May 2022. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Cancer Hospital (IRB-2022-371) and conformed to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. We declare that all participants and/or their legal guardians provided informed consent for study participation.

Variables

The following variables were included in this study: gender, age, body mass index (BMI), family history, smoking and drinking history, surgical methods (laparoscopy and open), tumor location, differentiation, nerve invasion, pTNM stage (AJCC-TNM 8th edition), and serum levels of various tumor markers. Survival information was obtained through telephone follow-up and review of medical records.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were analyzed via the two-sample t test or rank sum test, whereas categorical variables were analyzed via the chi-square test. Multivariate regression was employed to identify independent risk factors associated with prognosis. Multivariate imputation via Chained Equations is performed for filling in missing values via “mice” package. Propensity score matching (PSM) is used to screen samples at a 1:2 ratio to adjust the baseline. The “survival” package was used for prognosis analysis. Overall survival was calculated via Kaplan–Meier curves and compared via the log-rank test. The “jskm” package was used for landmark survival analysis. Stratified regression analysis was conducted to identify subpopulations that may benefit from laparoscopic surgery. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was used to identify independent prognostic factors for overall survival. Data analyses were performed via R version 4.3.1, and a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effects of different surgical methods on the prognosis of patients with gastric cancer

The results of multivariate Cox regression analysis indicated that the cumulative survival rate of patients who received LS was significantly higher than that of patients who underwent OS (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.725, p = 0.009). To further analyze the effect of different surgical approaches on the prognosis of gastric cancer patients, we employed propensity score matching to control for the impact of other potential confounding factors (Table 3). The overall survival curve between those who underwent laparoscopy and those who underwent open surgery was not significantly different (p > 0.05). However, landmark analysis revealed that within the first two years post-surgery, there was no significant difference in survival between the two surgical methods (p > 0.05). Notably, patients who underwent laparoscopy had better survival rates two years after surgery (log-rank test, p = 0.037). This finding suggests that while the two surgical approaches do not significantly affect prognosis within the initial two years after surgery, laparoscopic surgery is associated with improved long-term survival outcomes compared with open surgery.

Effects of different surgical methods on the prognosis of patients with locally advanced gastric cancer

After analysis of the survival curves of patients with different stages of gastric cancer who underwent surgical procedures, a significant difference in survival rates after two years was observed, suggesting that laparoscopy may provide a better long-term prognosis (Fig. 1A). Patients in stages I to II (n = 1,285) were classified as early-stage, whereas those in later stages were classified as locally advanced (n = 615). For early-stage patients, there was no significant difference in survival between the two surgical methods, indicating that the choice of surgical approach is not a key prognostic factor (Fig. 1B). In contrast, for locally advanced-stage patients, landmark analysis revealed that those who underwent laparoscopic surgery presented improved survival rates after 6 months postoperatively compared with those who received open surgery, suggesting a favorable long-term prognostic value (log-rank test, p = 0.037) (Fig. 1C). To further investigate the survival differences among specific locally advanced-stage patients who underwent different surgical methods, we performed a survival analysis across multiple groups. However, there were no statistically significant differences in the prognostic impact of the two surgical approaches within the same stage (p > 0.05) (Fig. 1D).

To assist in clinical decision-making and select the most favorable surgical approach for patient prognosis, we conducted stratified regression on patients with moderate and locally advanced gastric cancer to screen for characteristics suitable for different surgical methods, thereby improving the prognosis of surgical patients. We transformed continuous variables such as age and BMI into categorical variables on the basis of habits and social consensus. The stratification thresholds for patients were as follows: age at 60 years; BMI at 18.5 and 24.0; and serum levels of CEA, CA19.9, CA125, AFP, CA242, and CA72.4 at 5, 37, 35, 10, 15, and 6.9 (U/mL), respectively, in accordance with the thresholds defined by the World Health Organization and tailored to align with the practical context of China. We subsequently performed stratified Cox regression on features such as age, gender, personal history, and tumor location and generated a forest plot (Fig. 2). The forest plot revealed that the overall survival rates of patients with male gender (HR = 0.63, p < 0.05), a history of smoking and drinking (HR = 0.56, p < 0.05 and HR = 0.35, p < 0.01), a tumor location in the upper area (HR = 0.34, p < 0.05), poor tumor differentiation (HR = 0.56, p < 0.05), an age over 60 years (HR = 0.66, p < 0.05), and normal serum AFP (HR = 0.69, p < 0.05) and CA72-4 (HR = 0.58, p < 0.05) levels who underwent laparoscopy were better than those of patients who underwent open surgery. This means that patients with these characteristics are more likely to benefit from laparoscopy.

Establishing a Cox regression model to evaluate the prognosis of patients with locally advanced gastric cancer

Finally, based on the characteristic variables obtained in the previous section, we conducted multivariate Cox regression to establish a prognostic model for patients with locally advanced gastric cancer (Fig. 3A). The risk score was calculated based on the HR values of different variables. The forest plot revealed that surgery methods (HR = 1.455), serum levels of AFP (HR = 1.902) and CA72-4 (HR = 1.557) and pTNM stage (HR = 2.203, 3.117, and 8.440, respectively) were independent risk factors for the prognosis of patients with locally advanced gastric cancer. We then divided the samples into high- and low-risk groups based on the median risk score of the patients and plotted the survival curve (Fig. 3B). We found that the prognosis of the high-risk group was significantly worse than that of the low-risk group (log-rank test, p < 0.001).

A nomogram was developed to predict the OS of patients with locally advanced-stage gastric cancer (Stages IIIA, IIIB, IIIC, and IV) who underwent different surgical methods (open vs. laparoscopic surgery) (Fig. 4A). The nomogram incorporated significant prognostic factors identified through Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, including age, gender, tumor differentiation, TNM stage, tumor location, and specific tumor markers (AFP, CA72-4). The constructed nomogram demonstrated good predictive accuracy and discrimination ability, with a concordance index (C-index) of 0.723. Each variable in the nomogram was assigned a point value based on its contribution to the prognosis, with the total score corresponding to a specific probability of survival at 1, 3, and 5 years. The calibration curves for the nomogram showed good agreement between the predicted and observed survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years, indicating reliable performance (Fig. 4B). The developed nomogram provides a robust tool for predicting survival in locally advanced-stage GC patients and can assist clinicians in making informed decisions regarding surgical methods and overall treatment strategies.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the different effects of laparoscopy and open surgery on the survival of patients with locally advanced GC. We also identified the specific characteristics of locally advanced-stage patients who may benefit from laparoscopy. Finally, we constructed a prognostic model to predict the different outcomes of locally advanced GC patients who underwent surgery.

The occurrence and progression of gastric cancer are the result of multiple factors and stages, including genetic, dietary, environmental and other factors7. Generally, risk factors for GC include family history, smoking and drinking history, diet, and Helicobacter pyloriinfection8. There are currently multiple comprehensive treatment options for gastric cancer, and surgery remains one of the most important strategies9. At present, endoscopic resection method has been used for the treatment of T1 stage gastric cancer patients in some areas10. However, some researches have declared that endoscopic resection has significant limitations due to high rates of lymph node metastasis and residual, and its long-term prognosis is worse than that of laparoscopic surgery11,12. So in this study, we raise a different opinion in the current situation.

The advantages of minimally invasive gastric surgery, such as laparoscopy, were first reported in 199513,and its effectiveness and advantages over open surgery have been confirmed by subsequent clinical trials14–17. Compared with open surgery, laparoscopy results in less perioperative blood loss, fewer postoperative complications, and faster recovery of intestinal function, but it requires a longer surgical time, which places higher demands on surgeons and requires more investment in learning and training18,19. In addition, patients who underwent laparoscopy had a lower postoperative complication rate than those who underwent open surgery did (20% vs. 46%; p = 0.007) and had higher compliance with adjuvant chemotherapy (p= 0.003)20, although van der Veen et al.21 found no difference in the incidence of postoperative complications. A study comparing the differences between laparoscopic surgery and open surgery in early unplanned reoperation (EURO) after cancer gastrectomy in Japan demonstrated that the EURO rate 24 h after surgery was significantly lower in the LS group than in the OS group (p= 0.019), which means that LS is beneficial for improving the early postoperative efficacy in patients with gastric cancer22. Moreover, stage T4a GC patients and patients with malnutrition were more likely to benefit from LS because of the lower rate of postoperative complications and favorable outcomes23,24..

Our study did not find a significant difference in overall survival between the LS and OS groups. Similarly, a single-center randomized controlled trial revealed no significant difference in long-term survival rates between two different surgical methods for treating locally advanced gastric cancer25. Another multicenter randomized clinical trial demonstrated that for patients with locally advanced gastric cancer, their long-term survival rate after LS was not lower than that of patients who experience OS or short-term survival26,27. A meta-analysis conducted by Best et al28. revealed no evidence to suggest any differences in short-term or long-term outcomes between laparoscopic gastrectomy and open gastrectomy. However, the scarcity of data and wide confidence intervals indicate that significant benefits or harms of laparoscopic gastrectomy cannot be ruled out. Interestingly, landmark analysis in our study revealed that the LS group had a higher survival rate than the OS group did two years after surgery. Landmark analysis is a statistical analysis method used to analyze specific time points in research. It is usually used to evaluate the changes in effects before and after a specific time point. Two years after surgery, differences in survival rates began to appear among patients who underwent different surgical methods, suggesting that the LS group may have fewer long-term complications or lower postoperative recurrence rates, which requires further research29. Besides, we assume that the reduced surgical stress and lower risk of postoperative complications with laparoscopic surgery might lead to a lower likelihood of cancer recurrence, contributing to improved long-term survival. A single-center retrospective study revealed that the overall survival rate in the laparoscopic group was significantly greater than that in the open group (p< 0.001), and it was also an independent protective factor for prognosis, which is consistent with our study30. In addition, a multicenter, retrospective cohort study revealed that laparoscopic-assisted total gastrectomy for gastric cancer is feasible in terms of long-term outcomes, including survival and recurrence rates31. Currently, there is no literature confirming the impact of different surgical methods on the prognosis of gastric cancer patients with local metastasis. In the past, palliative chemotherapy was often the main treatment for patients with liver metastasis. However, retrospective studies in recent years have found that active surgical intervention (radical surgery and resection of metastatic lesions) can improve the prognosis of advanced liver metastasis gastric cancer patients to a certain extent32. Patients with local metastases usually have shorter survival times. However, they may experience fewer short-term complications, like postoperative bleeding and infections from endoscopic surgery10.

The results of multivariate Cox regression analysis in our study demonstrated that surgery method, age, clinical stage, BMI and serum levels of tumor markers, etc., were independent risk factors affecting overall survival. In addition, we identified potential characteristics that could benefit from LS and established a prognostic model based on these characteristics to evaluate the prognostic features of patients undergoing different surgeries. After assessing factors like patient status and medical conditions, those patients who qualify may be more frequently recommended for endoscopic surgical treatment. In addition, it is crucial to conduct more rigorous randomized controlled trials to validate these findings. Wang et al.33 also reported that LS was an independent protective factor for overall survival among patients with intra-abdominal infectious complications (HR = 0.54, p < 0.05), and the 5-year overall survival rate was higher in patients with intra-abdominal infectious complications who underwent LS than in those who underwent OS (p= 0.042). Another retrospective cohort study of patients with advanced gastric cancer in Japan indicated that the 5-year recurrence-free survival rate and overall survival rate of the LS group were 89.6% and 94.4%, respectively, whereas those of the OS group were 75.8% and 78.5%, respectively, which means that, to some extent, LS is acceptable in terms of long-term outcomes for advanced cancer patients and may be applicable for the treatment of advanced cancer34. Other studies have also shown that clinical stage, age and BMI are independent risk factors for overall survival in patients with GC35,36.

However, our study has several limitations. First, due to its retrospective nature and limited data availability, key variables such as performance status, comorbidities, detailed surgical parameters (e.g., extent of lymph node dissection, surgeon experience), and postoperative complications could not be fully adjusted for, leaving potential residual confounding—particularly in long-term outcomes. Although surgical year was not included as a covariate, baseline characteristics (including tumor stage and era-related proxies like chemotherapy regimens) were well balanced after propensity score matching, suggesting that temporal trends alone are unlikely to account for the observed survival differences. Nevertheless, unmeasured time-dependent changes in surgical technique or perioperative care may still introduce bias. Second, the long enrollment period coincided with evolving practices in laparoscopic surgery, which may contribute to era-related bias. Additionally, limited follow-up details prevented us from identifying specific events driving survival changes in the landmark analysis, and more comprehensive short-term outcome data would further strengthen the findings. Third, while our nomogram demonstrated good internal validity, it lacks external validation. As a single-center retrospective study, its generalizability is limited, and we emphasize the need for future multicenter studies to validate the model. Nevertheless, our study identifies patient subgroups most likely to benefit from laparoscopic surgery and presents a practical prognostic tool that may aid in personalized treatment planning for locally advanced gastric cancer.
