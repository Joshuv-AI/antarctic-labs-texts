Abstract
. In previous work, we proved that the continuous roots of a monic polynomial of degree d d whose coefficients depend in a C d − 1 , 1 C^{d-1,1} way on real parameters belong to the Sobolev space W 1 , q W^{1,q} for all 1 ≤ q < d / ( d − 1 ) 1\leq q<d/(d-1) . This is optimal. We obtained uniform bounds that show that the solution map “coefficients-to-roots” is bounded with respect to the C d − 1 , 1 C^{d-1,1} and the Sobolev W 1 , q W^{1,q} structures on source and target space, respectively. In this paper, we prove that the solution map is continuous, provided that we consider the C d C^{d} structure on the space of coefficients. Since there is no canonical choice of an ordered d d -tuple of the roots, we work in the space of d d -valued Sobolev functions equipped with a strong notion of convergence. We also interpret the results in the Wasserstein space on the complex plane.

1.3.
Hyperbolic polynomials

Let us briefly comment on the case of hyperbolic polynomials, in which
canonical choices of continuous parameterizations of the roots exist and stronger results hold true.
We refer to
[
21
]
. A monic polynomial
P_{a}
of degree
d
is called
hyperbolic
if
all its
d
roots (counted with multiplicities) are real. The space
\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
of monic hyperbolic polynomials of degree
d

can be identified with a semialgebraic subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(via the coefficient vector
a
).
Ordering the roots of
P_{a}\in\operatorname{Hyp}(d)
increasingly,
induces a continuous solution map

\lambda^{\uparrow}=(\lambda^{\uparrow}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\uparrow}_{d}):\operatorname{Hyp}(d)\to\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\lambda^{\uparrow}_{1}\leq\lambda^{\uparrow}_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda^{\uparrow}_{d}
.
Bronshtein’s theorem
[
7
]
(see also
[
16
]
) states that

\displaystyle(\lambda^{\uparrow})_{*}:C^{d-1,1}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))

\displaystyle\to C^{0,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}),

\displaystyle(x\mapsto P_{a(x)})

\displaystyle\mapsto(x\mapsto\lambda^{\uparrow}(P_{a(x)})),

is well-defined and bounded, where
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is open.
Hereby the space
C^{d-1,1}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))=\{f\in C^{d-1,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d}):f(U)\subseteq\operatorname{Hyp}(d)\}
carries the trace topology of the Fréchet topology of
C^{d-1,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Theorem 1.11

(
[
21
]
)
.

The map

(\lambda^{\uparrow})_{*}:C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))\to W^{1,q}_{\operatorname{loc}}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is continuous, for all
1\leq q<\infty
.

But
(\lambda^{\uparrow})_{*}:C^{d}(U,\operatorname{Hyp}(d))\to C^{0,1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is not continuous as shown by an example in
[
21
]
.

In the real case, the map
(\cdot)^{\uparrow}:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
(that orders the coordinates increasingly) is a Lipschitz right-inverse of
[\cdot]:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
,
thus a canonical version of an Almgren embedding.

The proof of
Theorem

1.11
follows the same general strategy as the one of
Theorem

1.2
but it is much simpler.

1.4.
Outline of the proof of
Theorem

1.2

We first give a full proof of the special case of radicals and then tackle the general case, using the result for radicals.

Radical case

Here the polynomials take the simple form

Z^{d}=g\quad\text{ and }\quad Z^{d}=g_{n},\quad n\geq 1,

where we assume that
g_{n}\to g
in
C^{d}(\overline{I},\mathbb{C})
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
\lambda,\lambda_{n}:I\to\mathbb{C}
be continuous functions satisfying
\lambda^{d}=g
and
\lambda_{n}^{d}=g_{n}
.

The proof essentially consists of two parts.
First, on the complement of the zero set
Z_{g}
of
g
, we show that
the distance of
\lambda^{\prime}(x)
and
\theta^{r(x)}\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(x)
, where
\theta
is a
d
-th root of unity and the power
r(x)\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
is chosen such that
the distance of
\lambda(x)
and
\theta^{r(x)}\lambda_{n}(x)
is minimal,
tends to zero as
n\to\infty
. Then we use the dominated convergence theorem; the domination is guaranteed by a result of Ghisi and Gobbino
[
10
]

which we recall, in slightly adapted form, in
Proposition

5.7
.

Secondly, on the accumulation points
\operatorname{acc}(Z_{g})
of
Z_{g}
,
the derivative
\lambda^{\prime}
vanishes (where it exists).
Using the uniform bounds for the
L^{q}
-norm (for
1\leq q<d/(d-1)
) of
\lambda_{n}^{\prime}
given in
Proposition

5.7
,
we prove that
\|\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(\operatorname{acc}(Z_{g}))}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

This is enough to conclude the proof since
Z_{g}\setminus\operatorname{acc}(Z_{g})
has measure zero.

General case

The proof of the general case proceeds by induction on the degree of the polynomials.
It follows the overall strategy of our proof of the optimal Sobolev regularity of the roots in
[
18
]
; see also
Theorem

7.1
.

Without loss of generality we may assume that the polynomials
P_{\tilde{a}_{n}}
, for
n\geq 1
, and
P_{\tilde{a}}
are in Tschirnhausen form, i.e., the coefficients of
Z^{d-1}
vanish identically.
(For notational clarity, we consistently equip the coefficients of polynomials in Tschirnhausen form with a “tilde”.)
Let
\lambda,\lambda_{n}:I\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
be continuous parameterizations of the roots of
P_{\tilde{a}}
,
P_{\tilde{a}_{n}}
, respectively.

On the zero set
Z_{\tilde{a}}
of the coefficient vector
\tilde{a}
, all roots of
P_{\tilde{a}}
are equal to zero,
hence,
\lambda^{\prime}(x)=0
for all
x\in\operatorname{acc}(Z_{\tilde{a}})
, where
\lambda^{\prime}(x)
exists.
In analogy to the radical case, we show that
\|\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(\operatorname{acc}(Z_{g}),\mathbb{C}^{d})}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, for all
1\leq q<d/(d-1)
.
To this end, we modify in
Theorem

7.5
the uniform bounds found in
[
18
]
.

For each
x_{0}
in the complement of
Z_{\tilde{a}}
, we find an interval
I^{\prime}\subseteq I
containing
x_{0}

on which the polynomial
P_{\tilde{a}}
splits and, for large enough
n
, also
P_{\tilde{a}_{n}}
splits.
More precisely, on
I^{\prime}
and for large
n
, we have simultaneous splittings into polynomial factors

P_{\tilde{a}}=P_{b}P_{b^{*}}\quad\text{ and }P_{\tilde{a}_{n}}=P_{b_{n}}P_{b_{n}^{*}},

where

•

d_{b}:=\deg P_{b}=\deg P_{b_{n}}<d
, and

•

there exist bounded analytic functions
\psi_{i}
with bounded partial derivatives of all orders
such that the coefficients of
P_{b}
and
P_{b_{n}}
are given by

\displaystyle b_{i}

\displaystyle=\tilde{a}_{k}^{i/k}\,\psi_{i}(\tilde{a}_{k}^{-2/k}\tilde{a}_{2},\ldots,\tilde{a}_{k}^{-d/k}\tilde{a}_{d}),

\displaystyle b_{n,i}

\displaystyle=\tilde{a}_{n,k}^{i/k}\,\psi_{i}(\tilde{a}_{n,k}^{-2/k}\tilde{a}_{n,2},\ldots,\tilde{a}_{n,k}^{-d/k}\tilde{a}_{n,d}).

The same is true for the second factors in the splitting
and similar formulas hold for the coefficients of the factors after putting them in Tschirnhausen form.
Here
k\in\{2,\ldots,d\}
is chosen such that
|\tilde{a}_{k}(x_{0})|^{1/k}\geq|\tilde{a}_{j}(x_{0})|^{1/j}
for all
2\leq j\leq d
,
which entails
|\tilde{a}_{n,k}(x_{0})|^{1/k}\geq\frac{2}{3}|\tilde{a}_{n,j}(x_{0})|^{1/j}
for all
2\leq j\leq d
and large enough
n
.
Note that
\tilde{a}_{k}
and
\tilde{a}_{n,k}
are bounded away from zero on
I^{\prime}
.

Using that the composition from the left with an analytic function is continuous on the space of
C^{d}
maps (see
Proposition

2.1
),
we conclude that

\displaystyle\|b-b_{n}\|_{C^{d}(\overline{I^{\prime}},\mathbb{C}^{d_{b}})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty;

and similarly for the second factors.
This allows us to argue by induction on the degree; for the precise induction argument see
Proposition

8.16
.

Finally, the proof of
Theorem

1.2
will be completed in

8.17

with an application of Vitali’s convergence theorem. The uniform integrability follows
from the uniform bounds proved in
[

Notation

The
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{m}
.
If not stated otherwise, “measurable” means “Lebesgue measurable” and “almost everywhere” means
“almost everywhere with respect to Lebesgue measure”. For measurable
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we usually write

|E|=\mathcal{L}^{m}(E)
.

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
\|z\|_{p}
denotes the
p
-norm of
z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
If
f:E\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
, for measurable
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
, is a measurable map, then we set

\|f\|_{L^{p}(E,\mathbb{C}^{d})}:=\big\|\|f\|_{2}\big\|_{L^{p}(E)}.

For us a set is countable if it is either finite or has the cardinality of
\mathbb{N}
.

A selection of a set-valued map
F:X\to 2^{Y}
between sets
X
and
Y

is a map
f:X\to Y
such that
f(x)\in F(x)
for all
x\in X
.
A parameterization of
F
is a pair
(f,Z)
, where
f:X\times Z\to Y

is such that
F(x)=\{f(x,z):z\in Z\}
for all
x\in X
.
For instance, the roots of a monic polynomial
P_{a}
of degree
d
form a set-valued map
\mathbb{C}^{d}\ni a\mapsto\Lambda(a)\in 2^{\mathbb{C}}

and a parameterization of the roots is a map
\lambda:\mathbb{C}^{d}\times\{1,\ldots,d\}\to\mathbb{C}
with
\Lambda(a)=\{\lambda(a,1),\ldots,\lambda(a,d)\}
for all
a\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

2.
Function spaces

Let us fix notation and recall background on the function spaces used in this paper.

2.1.
Hölder–Lipschitz spaces

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then
C^{k}(U)
is the space of
k
-times continuously differentiable complex valued functions
with its natural Fréchet topology.
If
U
is bounded, then
C^{k}(\overline{U})
denotes the space of all
f\in C^{k}(U)

such that each
\partial^{\alpha}f
,
0\leq|\alpha|\leq k
,
has a continuous extension to the closure
\overline{U}
. Endowed with the norm

\|f\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U})}:=\max_{|\alpha|\leq k}\sup_{x\in U}|\partial^{\alpha}f(x)|

it is a Banach space.
For
0<\gamma\leq 1
, we consider the Hölder–Lipschitz seminorm

|f|_{C^{0,\gamma}(\overline{U})}:=\sup_{x,y\in U,\,x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\|x-y\|_{2}^{\gamma}}.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\gamma\leq 1
, we have the Banach space

C^{k,\gamma}(\overline{U}):=\{f\in C^{k}(\overline{U}):\|f\|_{C^{k,\gamma}(\overline{U})}<\infty\},

where

\|f\|_{C^{k,\gamma}(\overline{U})}:=\|f\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U})}+\max_{|\alpha|=k}|\partial^{\alpha}f|_{C^{0,\gamma}(\overline{U})}.

We write
C^{k,\gamma}(U)
for the space of
C^{k}
functions on
U
that
belong to
C^{k,\gamma}(\overline{V})
for each relatively compact open
V\Subset U
,
with its natural Fréchet topology.

2.2.
Lebesgue spaces

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be open and
1\leq p\leq\infty
.
We denote by
L^{p}(U)
the Lebesgue space with respect to the
m
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{m}
,
and
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(U)}
is the corresponding
L^{p}
-norm.
For Lebesgue measurable sets
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
we also write
|E|=\mathcal{L}^{m}(E)
.

Assume that
U
is bounded.
A measurable function
f:U\to\mathbb{C}
belongs to the weak
L^{p}
-space
L_{w}^{p}(U)
if

\|f\|_{p,w,U}:=\sup_{r\geq 0}\Big(r\,|\{x\in U:|f(x)|>r\}|^{1/p}\Big)<\infty.

For
1\leq q<p<\infty
we have (cf.
[
11
, Ex. 1.1.11]
)

(2.1)

\|f\|_{q,w,U}\leq\|f\|_{L^{q}(U)}\leq\Big(\frac{p}{p-q}\Big)^{1/q}|U|^{1/q-1/p}\|f\|_{p,w,U}

and hence

L^{p}(U)\subseteq L_{w}^{p}(U)\subseteq L^{q}(U)\subseteq L_{w}^{q}(U)

with strict inclusions.
We remark that
\|\cdot\|_{p,w,U}
is only a quasinorm.
Its
p
-th power is
\sigma
-subadditive but not
\sigma
-additive (see
[
18
, Section 2.2]
).

We remark that for continuous functions
f:U\to\mathbb{C}
we have (and use interchangeably)

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(U)}=\|f\|_{C^{0}(\overline{U})}
.

2.3.
Sobolev spaces

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq q\leq\infty
,
we consider the Sobolev space

W^{k,q}(U):=\{f\in L^{q}(U):\partial^{\alpha}f\in L^{q}(U)\text{ for }|\alpha|\leq k\},

where
\partial^{\alpha}f
are distributional derivatives. Endowed with the norm

\|f\|_{W^{k,q}(U)}:=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq k}\|\partial^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{q}(U)}

it is a Banach space.

2.4.
A result on composition

In the following proposition we use the norm

(2.2)

\|f\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})}:=\max_{0\leq j\leq k}\sup_{x\in U}\|d^{j}f(x)\|_{L_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})}

on the space
C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell}):=(C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}))^{\ell}
, where
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}

and
L_{j}(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
is the space of
j
-linear maps with
j
arguments in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and values in
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
be open, bounded, and convex.
Let
\psi\in C^{k+1}(\overline{V},\mathbb{R}^{p})
. Then

\psi_{*}:C^{k}(\overline{U},V)\to C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{p})
,
\psi_{*}(\varphi):=\psi\circ\varphi
, is well-defined and continuous.
More precisely, for
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
in a bounded subset
B
of
C^{k}(\overline{U},V)
,

\|\psi_{*}(\varphi_{1})-\psi_{*}(\varphi_{2})\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{p})}\leq C\,\|\psi\|_{C^{k+1}(\overline{V},\mathbb{R}^{p})}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})},

where
C=C(k,B)
.

This result must be well-known;
we give a short proof in
Section

A.2
.

2.5.
Absolutely continuous curves in a metric space

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
Let
1\leq q\leq\infty
.
A curve
\gamma:I\to X
in a complete metric space
(X,\mathsf{d})
belongs to
AC^{q}(I,X)
if there exists
m\in L^{q}(I)
such that

(2.3)

\mathsf{d}(\gamma(x),\gamma(y))\leq\int_{x}^{y}m(t)\,dt,\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in I,~x\leq y.

In that case, the limit

\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{\mathsf{d}(\gamma(x+h),\gamma(x))}{|h|}=:|\dot{\gamma}|(x)

exists for almost every
x\in I
and is called the
metric speed
of
\gamma
at
x
.
Furthermore,
|\dot{\gamma}|\in L^{q}(I)
and (
2.3
) holds with
m
replaced by
|\dot{\gamma}|
;
one has
|\dot{\gamma}|\leq m
almost everywhere in
I
for any
m
that satisfies (
2.3
).
See
[
3
, Definition 1.1.1]
.

The
q
-energy

\mathcal{E}_{q}:C^{0}(I,X)\to[0,\infty]
is defined by

\mathcal{E}_{q}(\gamma):=\begin{cases}\int_{I}(|\dot{\gamma}|(t))^{q}\,dt&\text{ if }\gamma\in AC^{q}(I,X),\\
\infty&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

2.6.
Absolutely continuous curves in
\mathbb{C}^{d}

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
Let
1\leq q\leq\infty
.
A continuous curve
\gamma:I\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
belongs to
AC^{q}(I,\mathbb{C}^{d})
with respect to the metric
induced by
\|\cdot\|_{2}
if and only if

\gamma
is differentiable at almost every
x\in I
,
the derivative
\gamma^{\prime}
belongs to
L^{q}(I,\mathbb{C}^{d})
, and

\gamma(y)-\gamma(x)=\int_{x}^{y}\gamma^{\prime}(t)\,dt,\quad\text{ for all }x,y\in I,~x\leq y.

In that case,

|\gamma^{\prime}|(x)=\|\gamma^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}\quad\text{ for almost every }x\in I.

See
[
3
, Remark 1.1.3]
.

3.
d
-valued Sobolev functions

3.1.
Unordered
d
-tuples of complex numbers

The symmetric group
\operatorname{S}_{d}
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
by permuting the coordinates,

\sigma z=\sigma(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}):=(z_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,z_{\sigma(d)}),\quad\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d},~z\in\mathbb{C}^{d},

and thus induces an equivalence relation.
The equivalence class of
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})
is the
unordered tuple

[z]=[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
.
Let us consider the set

\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}):=\{[z]:z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\}

of unordered complex
d
-tuples.
It is a complete metric space if equipped with the metric

\mathbf{d}([z],[w]):=\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\delta(z,\sigma w),

where

\delta(z,\sigma w):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|z-\sigma w\|_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|z_{i}-w_{\sigma(i)}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

It follows that the induced map
[\cdot]:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
is Lipschitz.

We will also represent the element
[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]
of
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
by the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\llbracket{z_{i}}\right\rrbracket
, where

\left\llbracket{z_{i}}\right\rrbracket
denotes the Dirac mass at
z_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
.
If normalized, i.e.,
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\llbracket{z_{i}}\right\rrbracket
, then, in this picture,
\mathbf{d}
is induced by
the
L^{2}
based Wasserstein metric on the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{C}
; see
Section

11.1
.
3
3

3

This is the reason for the factor
1/\sqrt{d}
in the definition of
\mathbf{d}
.

3.2.
d
-valued Sobolev functions

Due to Almgren
[
2
]
, see also
[
9
]
,
there exist an integer
N=N(d)
, positive constants
C_{i}=C_{i}(d)
,
i=1,2
, and an
injective Lipschitz mapping
\Delta:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
with Lipschitz constant
\leq C_{1}
and
Lipschitz constant of
\Delta|^{-1}_{\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))}
bounded by
C_{2}
. Moreover, there is a
Lipschitz retraction of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
onto
\Delta(\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
.
Almgren used this bi-Lipschitz embedding to define Sobolev spaces of
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
-valued functions:
for open
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
1\leq q\leq\infty
set

W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})):=\{f:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}):\Delta\circ f\in W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})\}.

For an equivalent intrinsic definition of
W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
, see
[
9
, Definition 0.5 and Theorem 2.4]
.
Then
W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
carries the metric

(3.1)

(f,g)\mapsto\|\Delta\circ f-\Delta\circ g\|_{W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})}

which makes it a complete metric space (where functions that coincide almost everywhere are identified).

Lemma 3.1
.

The space
W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
with the metric given in (
3.1
) is complete.

Proof.

A Cauchy sequence
f_{n}
in
W^{1,q}(U,\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
is by definition
a Cauchy sequence
\Delta\circ f_{n}
in
W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
The completeness of
W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})
implies that
\Delta\circ f_{n}
converges to a function
h\in W^{1,q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
It remains to show that there exists
f:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
such that
h=\Delta\circ f
almost everywhere in
U
.
There exist a subsequence
\Delta\circ f_{n_{k}}
and a nonnegative function
g\in L^{q}(U)

such that
(\Delta\circ f_{n_{k}})(x)\to h(x)
and
\|(\Delta\circ f_{n_{k}})(x)\|_{2}\leq g(x)
for almost every
x\in U

(cf.
[
13
, Theorem 2.7]
).
For each such
x
, it follows that
f_{n_{k}}(x)
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
and
hence it converges in
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
. So there is a function
f:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
such that
f_{n_{k}}\to f
almost everywhere in
U

and hence
\Delta\circ f_{n_{k}}\to\Delta\circ f
almost everywhere in
U
.
By the dominated convergence theorem,
\|\Delta\circ f_{n_{k}}-\Delta\circ f\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})}\to 0
and thus
\|\Delta\circ f_{n}-\Delta\circ f\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N})}\to 0
,
since
\Delta\circ f_{n}
is a Cauchy sequence.
Since the limit is unique, we have
h=\Delta\circ f
almost everywhere.
∎

3.3.
Almgren’s embedding

Let us recall Almgren’s construction of
\Delta
.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that

H:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

is an
Almgren map
if there is a unit complex number

\theta\in\mathbb{C}
such that
H([z])
is an array of
d
real numbers
\eta(z_{i}):=\mathrm{Re}(\theta z_{i})
arranged in increasing order, i.e.,

H([z])=H([z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}])=(\eta(z_{\sigma(1)}),\ldots,\eta(z_{\sigma(d)}),

where
\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}
is chosen so that
\eta(z_{\sigma(1)})\leq\eta(z_{\sigma(2)})\leq\cdots\leq\eta(z_{\sigma(d)})
.
We also say that
H
is the Almgren map associated to the real linear form
\eta
.

By Almgren’s combinatorial lemma (see e.g.
[
9
, Lemma 2.3]
) there exists
\alpha=\alpha(d)>0
and a finite set of linear forms

\Lambda=\{\eta_{1},\ldots\eta_{h}\}
, where
\eta_{l}(z):=\mathrm{Re}(\theta_{l}z)

for unit complex numbers
\theta_{l}
, with the following property: given any set of
d^{2}
complex numbers,
\left\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{d^{2}}\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}
,
there exists
\eta_{l}\in\Lambda
such that

(3.2)

|\eta_{l}(z_{k})|\geq\alpha|z_{k}|\quad\textrm{ for all }k\in\big\{1,\ldots,d^{2}\big\}.

For instance, we may take
h=2d^{2}+1
and as
\{\theta_{1},\ldots\theta_{h}\}
the set of all
h
-th roots of unity.
Let
H_{l}
denote the Almgren map associated to
\eta_{l}
.
Almgren’s embedding
\Delta:\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

, is then defined by

(3.3)

\displaystyle\Delta([z])=h^{-1/2}(H_{1}([z]),\ldots,H_{h}([z])).

On each accumulation point

Lemma 3.7

[

, Theorem 2.4 and Proposition 2.7]
, almost everywhere in
I
,

\|(\Delta\circ f_{n})^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq\|Df_{n}\|_{2}\leq\mathbf{s}_{1}(f,f_{n})+\|Df\|_{2},

using that
\|Df_{n}\|_{2}
is independent of the parameterization of
f_{n}
.
Since
\sup_{n\geq 1}\mathbf{s}_{1}(f,f_{n})+\|Df\|_{2}
is in
L^{q}(I)
, by assumption,
the dominated convergence theorem implies that

\|(\Delta\circ f-\Delta\circ f_{n_{k}})^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I,\mathbb{R}^{N})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }k\to\infty.

This implies that

\|(\Delta\circ f-\Delta\circ f_{n})^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(I,\mathbb{R}^{N})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

by
Lemma

4.6
.
Clearly, also

\|\Delta\circ f-\Delta\circ f_{n}\|_{L^{q}(I,\mathbb{R}^{N})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

by the Lipschitz property of
\Delta
and
\|\mathbf{d}(f,f_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\to 0
.

Conversely, assume that

(4.6)

\|\Delta\circ f-\Delta\circ f_{n}\|_{W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{R}^{N})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

By Morrey’s theorem, we have

\|\mathbf{d}(f,f_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
So there exists
n_{0}\geq 1
such that (
4.4
) holds.
By
Corollary

4.5
, we have the opposite of (
4.5
):
provided that all the derivatives exist at
x_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}_{1}(f,f_{n})(x_{0})\leq C(d)\,\|(\Delta\circ f-\Delta\circ f_{n})^{\prime}(x_{0})\|_{2},\quad n\geq n_{0}.

As above, this and the assumption (
4.6
) imply that there is a subsequence
(n_{k})
such that,
for almost every
x_{0}\in I
,

\mathbf{s}_{1}(f,f_{n_{k}})(x_{0})\to 0\quad\text{ as }k\to\infty.

By the dominated convergence theorem, we conclude

\|\mathbf{s}_{1}(f,f_{n_{k}})\|_{L^{q}(I)}\to 0\quad\text{ as }k\to\infty,

and, in turn, by
Lemma

4.6
,

\|\mathbf{s}_{1}(f,f_{n})\|_{L^{q}(I)}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

For the domination, observe that, by
[
9
, Theorem 2.4 and Proposition 2.7]
, almost everywhere in
I
,

\displaystyle\sqrt{d}\cdot\mathbf{s}_{1}(f,f_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\|Df\|_{2}+\|Df_{n}\|_{2},

\displaystyle\|Df_{n}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(d)\,\|(\Delta\circ f_{n})^{\prime}\|_{2}\leq C(d)\,\big(\|(\Delta\circ f-\Delta\circ f_{n})^{\prime}\|_{2}+\|(\Delta\circ f)^{\prime}\|_{2}\big)

and the supremum over all
n\geq 1
of the right-hand side is in
L^{q}(I)
, by assumption.
The proof of
Theorem

3.11
is complete.

The continuity problem for radicals

This section is devoted to the radical case, i.e.,
solutions of the equation

where

is a suitable function.
The goal is to prove the following theorem.

.

Let

be an integer. Let

Let
g_{n}\to g
in

for all

.

The distance
\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{\operatorname{rad},I}
is induced by
\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{I}
;
see
Definition

5.5
.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer. Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
Let
g_{n}\to g
in

as

.
Let

Then

(5.4)

.

Corollary

Section

.

Theorem

.

5.1.
Unordered
d
-tuples of radicals

Let us consider the set

\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{rad},d}(\mathbb{C}):=\{[z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}]\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C}):z_{1}^{d}=z_{2}^{d}=\cdots=z_{d}^{d}\}.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
d
be a positive integer and
\theta
a
d
-th root of unity.
For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
we define the unordered
d
-tuple

\left[{\lambda}\right]_{\theta}:=[\lambda,\theta\lambda,\theta^{2}\lambda,\ldots,\theta^{d-1}\lambda].

Note that, for all
a\in\mathbb{C}
,

\left[{a\lambda}\right]_{\theta}=a\left[{\lambda}\right]_{\theta}=\lambda\left[{a}\right]_{\theta}
.

We have the equivalent representation

\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{rad},d}(\mathbb{C})=\{[\lambda]_{\theta}:\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\}.

The restriction of the metric
\mathbf{d}
to
\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{rad},d}(\mathbb{C})
is very simple:

Lemma 5.4
.

For
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{C}
,

\mathbf{d}(\left[{\lambda}\right]_{\theta},\left[{\mu}\right]_{\theta})=\min_{1\leq j\leq d}|\lambda-\theta^{j}\mu|.

In particular, the map
\mathbb{C}\ni\lambda\mapsto\left[{\lambda}\right]_{\theta}\in\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{rad},d}(\mathbb{C})
is Lipschitz with Lipschitz constant
\leq 1
.

Proof.

Clearly, the minimum over
\operatorname{S}_{d}
in the definition of
\mathbf{d}(\left[{\lambda}\right]_{\theta},\left[{\mu}\right]_{\theta})

is attained on a permutation induced by a rotation
\theta^{i}\mapsto\theta^{i+j}
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}(\left[{\lambda}\right]_{\theta},\left[{\mu}\right]_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\Big(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\theta^{i}\lambda-\theta^{\sigma(i)}\mu|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}=\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\Big(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\theta^{i}\lambda-\theta^{i+j}\mu|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\min_{1\leq j\leq d}\Big(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\lambda-\theta^{j}\mu|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}=\min_{1\leq j\leq d}|\lambda-\theta^{j}\mu|

as claimed.
∎

5.2.
The distance
\mathbf{d}_{\operatorname{rad},E}^{1,q}

The distance
\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{I}

from
Definition

3.6
induces a distance
\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{\operatorname{rad},I}
on
W^{1,q}(I,\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{rad},d}(\mathbb{C}))
.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
f,g\in W^{1,q}(I,\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{rad},d}(\mathbb{C}))
and fix a
d
-th root of unity
\theta
.
By
Proposition

3.4
, there exist
\lambda,\mu\in W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{C})
such that

f=\left\llbracket{\lambda}\right\rrbracket+\left\llbracket{\theta\lambda}\right\rrbracket+\cdots+\left\llbracket{\theta^{d-1}\lambda}\right\rrbracket,\quad g=\left\llbracket{\mu}\right\rrbracket+\left\llbracket{\theta\mu}\right\rrbracket+\cdots+\left\llbracket{\theta^{d-1}\mu}\right\rrbracket.

For
x\in I
, let

r(x):=\min\Big\{r\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}:|\lambda(x)-\theta^{r}\mu(x)|=\mathbf{d}(f(x),g(x))\Big\}

and set

\mathbf{s}_{\operatorname{rad},0}(f,g)(x):=\mathbf{d}(f(x),g(x)).

For
x\in I
such that
Df(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\left\llbracket{\theta^{i}D\lambda(x)}\right\rrbracket
and
Dg(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{r-1}\left\llbracket{\theta^{i}D\mu(x)}\right\rrbracket
exist in the sense of
Definition

3.5
,
set

\mathbf{s}_{\operatorname{rad},1}(f,g)(x):=\max_{\theta}|D\lambda(x)-\theta^{r(x)}D\mu(x)|,

where the maximum is taken over all
d
-th roots of unity.
Then
\mathbf{s}_{\operatorname{rad},1}(f,g)(x)
is defined for almost every
x\in I
.
It is independent of the choices of
\lambda
,
\mu
, and
\theta
.

For any measurable subset
E\subseteq I
, we set

\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{\operatorname{rad},E}(f,g):=\|\mathbf{s}_{\operatorname{rad},0}(f,g)\|_{L^{\infty}(E)}+\|\mathbf{s}_{\operatorname{rad},1}(f,g)\|_{L^{q}(E)}.

That
\mathbf{s}_{\operatorname{rad},i}(f,g)
, for
i=0,1
, are Borel measurable can be seen as in
Lemma

3.7
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval and
E\subseteq I
a measurable set.
Let
f,g\in W^{1,q}(I,\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{rad},d}(\mathbb{C}))
. Then:

(1)

\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{\operatorname{rad},E}(f,f)=0
.

(2)

\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{\operatorname{rad},E}(f,g)=0
implies
f=g
on
E
.

(3)

\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{\operatorname{rad},E}(f,g)=\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{\operatorname{rad},E}(g,f)
.

In particular,
\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{\operatorname{rad},I}
is a semimetric on
W^{1,q}(I,\mathcal{A}_{\operatorname{rad},d}(\mathbb{C}))
.

Proof.

(1) In this case, for any
x\in I
,
we have
r(x)=0
and the assertion is obvious.

(2) If
\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{\operatorname{rad},E}(f,g)=0
then
\mathbf{d}(f,g)=0
on
E
(since
\mathbf{d}(f,g)
is continuous).

(3) This is immediate from the definition.
∎

5.3.
Proof of
Corollary

5.2

Recall that
\lambda
,
\lambda_{n}
are absolutely continuous and belong to
W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{C})
, and that
\Lambda:=[\lambda]_{\theta}
,
\Lambda_{n}:=[\lambda_{n}]_{\theta}
are the curves of

).
For each
1\leq j\leq d
and
x\in I
,
where
\lambda^{\prime}(x)
and
\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(x)
exist,

\big||\lambda^{\prime}(x)|-|\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(x)|\big|=\big||\lambda^{\prime}(x)|-|\theta^{j}\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(x)|\big|\leq|\lambda^{\prime}(x)-\theta^{j}\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(x)|.

.

5.4.
Ghisi and Gobbino’s higher order Glaeser inequalities

The following proposition is a variant of the results obtained in
[
10
]
.

Proposition 5.7

(
[
18
, Proposition 1]
)
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer,
\gamma\in(0,1]
, and
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
a bounded open interval.
Let
g\in C^{k,\gamma}(\overline{I})
be a complex valued function.
Then there exists a nonnegative function
m\in L^{p}_{w}(I)
, for
p=\frac{k+\gamma}{k+\gamma-1}
, with

\|m\|_{p,w,I}\leq C(k)\,\max\Big\{|g^{(k)}|_{C^{0,\gamma}(\overline{I})}^{1/(k+\gamma)}\,|I|^{1/p},\|g^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}^{1/(k+\gamma)}\Big\}

such that

Corollary 5.8

(
[
18
, Corollary 2]
)
.

Let
d
be a positive integer.
Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
For any continuous function
f:I\to\mathbb{C}
such that
f^{d}=g\in C^{d-1,1}(\overline{I})
,
we have

f^{\prime}\in L^{p}_{w}(I)
, where

, and

\|f^{\prime}\|_{p,w,I}\leq C(d)\,\max\Big\{|g^{(d-1)}|_{C^{0,1}(\overline{I})}^{1/d}|I|^{1/p},\|g^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}^{1/d}\Big\}.

5.5.
Proof of
Theorem

5.1

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer and
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
a bounded open interval.
Let
g_{n}\to g
in
C^{d}(\overline{I},\mathbb{C})
as
n\to\infty
.
Let
\Lambda,\Lambda_{n}:I\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be the curves of unordered solutions of (
5.2
).
We have to show that

\mathbf{d}^{1,q}_{\operatorname{rad},I}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{n})\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for all
1\leq q<d/(d-1)
.

Let
\lambda,\lambda_{n}:I\to\mathbb{C}
be continuous functions satisfying

\lambda^{d}=g\quad\text{ and }\quad\lambda_{n}^{d}=g_{n}.

By
Corollary

5.8
,
\lambda,\lambda_{n}\in W^{1,q}(I)
,
for all
1\leq q<d/(d-1)
,
and

\Lambda=[\lambda]_{\theta}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Lambda_{n}=[\lambda_{n}]_{\theta}.

Remark 5.9
.

By assumption, all derivatives of order
\leq d
of
g
and
g_{n}
extend continuously to
the endpoints of the interval
I
.
In particular, also
\lambda
and
\lambda_{n}
extend continuously to
\overline{I}
.
For technical reasons, we will work with the compact interval
\overline{I}
.

We consider the zero set of

in
\overline{I}

Z_{g}:=\{x\in\overline{I}:g(x)=0\},

and its complement in

\Omega_{g}:=I\setminus Z_{g}=\{x\in I:g(x)\neq 0\}.

The set of accumulation points of
Z_{g}
is denoted by

.
Let

for the rest of the section.

Strategy of the proof of
Theorem

5.1

Step 0:

We prove that

\|\mathbf{s}_{\operatorname{rad},0}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}=\|\mathbf{d}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Thus, it suffices to show that, for all
1\leq q<p
,

(5.6)

\|\mathbf{s}_{\operatorname{rad},1}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{n})\|_{L^{q}(I)}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Step 1:

We show that, for each

,

\mathbf{s}_{\operatorname{rad},1}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{n})(x)\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

and thus conclude,
using the dominated convergence theorem,
that,
for all

,

\|\mathbf{s}_{\operatorname{rad},1}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{n})\|_{L^{q}(\Omega_{g})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Step 2:

We prove that for each
\epsilon>0
there exist a neighborhood
U
of
\operatorname{acc}(Z_{g})
in
\overline{I}

and
n_{0}\geq 1
such that,
for all

,

\|\lambda_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{q}(U)}\leq C(d,q,|I|)\,\epsilon,\quad n\geq n_{0}.

Note that for

we have
\lambda(x)=0
and
\lambda^{\prime}(x)=0
(if the latter exists).
The set

has measure zero.

Step 3:

At this stage, it is not difficult to combine the results of Step 1 and Step 2 to
complete the proof of (

) and hence of

.

Step 0. Uniform convergence

Lemma 5.10
.

If
g_{n}\to g
in
C^{0}(\overline{I})

as
n\to\infty

and
\lambda,\lambda_{n}\in C^{0}(\overline{I})
are such that
\lambda^{d}=g
and

, then

\|\mathbf{d}(\left[{\lambda}\right]_{\theta},\left[{\lambda_{n}}\right]_{\theta})\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

This follows from
Corollary

.
Here is a direct argument: for fixed

,

\prod_{j=1}^{d}|\lambda(x)-\theta^{j}\lambda_{n}(x)|=|\lambda(x)^{d}-g_{n}(x)|=|g(x)-g_{n}(x)|

Step 1. Continuity on
\Omega_{g}

Lemma 5.11
.

Let
x\in\Omega_{g}
. Then
\lambda^{\prime}(x)
and
\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(x)
exist for sufficiently large

.
Let
1\leq j\leq d
.
If

|\lambda(x)-\theta^{j}\lambda_{n}(x)|\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

then also

|\lambda^{\prime}(x)-\theta^{j}\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(x)|\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\Omega_{g}
.
Then
g(x)\neq 0
and there is
n_{0}\geq 1
such that
g_{n}(x)\neq 0
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
Fix
n\geq n_{0}
.
So
\lambda(x)\neq 0
,
\lambda_{n}(x)\neq 0
, and the derivatives
\lambda^{\prime}(x)
and
\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(x)
exist.
Differentiating
\lambda(x)^{d}=g(x)

\lambda^{\prime}(x)=\lambda(x)\cdot\frac{1}{d}\frac{g^{\prime}(x)}{g(x)},

and analogously for
\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(x)

For each

\displaystyle|\lambda^{\prime}(x)-\theta^{j}\lambda_{n}^{\prime}(x)|

\displaystyle=\Big|\lambda(x)\cdot\frac{1}{d}\frac{g^{\prime}(x)}{g(x)}-\theta^{j}\lambda_{n}(x)\cdot\frac{1}{d}\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(x)}{g_{n}(x)}\Big|

We have
\big|\frac{g^{\prime}(x)}{g(x)}-\frac{g_{n}^{\prime}(x)}{g_{n}(x)}\big|\to 0

∎

.

For each

,

Step 2. On accumulation points of

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
g\in C^{d}(\overline{I})
.
If

,

By Taylor’s formula,

.
If

, then

leads to a contradiction.
∎

In the following,
I(x_{0},\delta)
denotes the open

in

.

Proof.

By
Lemma

5.14
,
g(x_{0})=g^{\prime}(x_{0})=\cdots=g^{(d)}(x_{0})=0
.
Fix
\epsilon>0

(1) The polynomial map

(2) This follows from

6.4.
Tschirnhausen form

We say that a monic polynomial

P_{a}(Z)=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}Z^{d-j}

is in
Tschirnhausen form
if
a_{1}=0
.
Every
P_{a}
can be put in Tschirnhausen form by the substitution, called
Tschirnhausen transformation
,

P_{\tilde{a}}(Z)=P_{a}(Z-\tfrac{a_{1}}{d})=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=2}^{d}\tilde{a}_{j}Z^{d-j}.

Note that

(6.4)

\tilde{a}_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{j}C_{i}a_{i}a_{1}^{j-i},\quad 2\leq j\leq d,

where the
C_{i}
are universal constants and
a_{0}=1
.

For clarity of notation, we consistently equip the coefficients of polynomials in Tschirnhausen form with a “tilde”.

6.5.
Splitting

The following well-known lemma (see e.g.
[
1
]
or
[
5
]
) is a consequence of the
inverse function theorem.

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
P_{a}=P_{b}P_{c}
, where
P_{b}
and
P_{c}
are monic complex polynomials without common root.
Then for
P
near
P_{a}
we have
P=P_{b(P)}P_{c(P)}

for analytic mappings of monic polynomials
P\mapsto b(P)
and
P\mapsto c(P)
,
defined for
P
near
P_{a}
, with the given initial values.

Proof.

The splitting
P_{a}=P_{b}P_{c}
defines on the coefficients a polynomial mapping
\varphi
such that
a=\varphi(b,c)
,
where
a=(a_{i})
,
b=(b_{i})
, and
c=(c_{i})
. The Jacobian determinant

\det d\varphi(b,c)
equals the resultant of
P_{b}
and
P_{c}
which is non-zero by assumption.
Thus
\varphi
can be inverted locally.
∎

If
P_{\tilde{a}}
is in Tschirnhausen form and if
\tilde{a}\neq 0
, then
P_{\tilde{a}}

splits
, i.e.,

P_{\tilde{a}}=P_{b}P_{c}
for monic polynomials
P_{b}
and
P_{c}
with positive degree and without common zero.
For, if
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
denote the roots of
P_{\tilde{a}}
and they all coincide, then since

\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{d}=\tilde{a}_{1}=0

they all must vanish, contradicting
\tilde{a}\neq 0
.

Let
\tilde{a}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{a}_{d}
denote the coordinates in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.
Fix
k\in\{2,\ldots,d\}
and let
\tilde{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\cap\{\tilde{a}_{1}=0,\,\tilde{a}_{k}\neq 0\}
;

\tilde{p}
corresponds to the polynomial
P_{\tilde{a}}
in Tschirnhausen form.
We associate the polynomial

\displaystyle Q_{\underline{a}}(Z):=\tilde{a}_{k}^{-d/k}P_{\tilde{a}}(\tilde{a}_{k}^{1/k}Z)=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=2}^{d}\tilde{a}_{k}^{-j/k}\tilde{a}_{j}Z^{d-j}=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=2}^{d}\underline{a}_{j}Z^{d-j},

\displaystyle\underline{a}_{j}:=\tilde{a}_{k}^{-j/k}\tilde{a}_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,d,

where some branch of the radical is fixed.
Then
Q_{\underline{a}}
is in Tschirnhausen form and
\underline{a}_{k}=1
;
it corresponds to a point
\underline{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\cap\{\underline{a}_{k}=1\}
.
By
Lemma

6.6
, we have a splitting
Q_{\underline{a}}=Q_{\underline{b}}Q_{\underline{c}}
on some open ball

B(\underline{p},\rho)

centered at
\underline{p}
with radius
\rho>0
.
In particular, there exist analytic functions
\psi_{i}
on
B(\underline{p},\rho)
such that

\underline{b}_{i}=\psi_{i}\big(\tilde{a}_{k}^{-2/k}\tilde{a}_{2},\tilde{a}_{k}^{-3/k}\tilde{a}_{3},\ldots,\tilde{a}_{k}^{-d/k}\tilde{a}_{d}\big),\quad i=1,\ldots,\deg Q_{\underline{b}}.

The splitting
Q_{\underline{a}}=Q_{\underline{b}}Q_{\underline{c}}
induces a splitting

P_{\tilde{a}}=P_{b}P_{c}
, where

(6.5)

b_{i}=\tilde{a}_{k}^{i/k}\psi_{i}\big(\tilde{a}_{k}^{-2/k}\tilde{a}_{2},\tilde{a}_{k}^{-3/k}\tilde{a}_{3},\ldots,\tilde{a}_{k}^{-d/k}\tilde{a}_{d}\big),\quad i=1,\ldots,d_{b}:=\deg P_{b};

likewise for
c_{j}
.
Shrinking
\rho
slightly, we may assume that
\psi_{i}
and all its partial derivatives are bounded on
B(\underline{p},\rho)
.
Let
\tilde{b}_{j}
denote the coefficients of the polynomial
P_{\tilde{b}}
resulting from
P_{b}
by the
Tschirnhausen transformation. Then, by (
6.4
),

(6.6)

\tilde{b}_{i}=\tilde{a}_{k}^{i/k}\tilde{\psi}_{i}\big(\tilde{a}_{k}^{-2/k}\tilde{a}_{2},\tilde{a}_{k}^{-3/k}\tilde{a}_{3},\ldots,\tilde{a}_{k}^{-d/k}\tilde{a}_{d}\big),\quad i=2,\ldots,d_{b},

for analytic functions
\tilde{\psi}_{i}
which, together with all their partial derivatives, are bounded on
B(\underline{p},\rho)
.

6.6.
Universal splitting of polynomials in Tschirnhausen form

The set

K:=\bigcup_{k=2}^{d}\{(0,\underline{a}_{2},\ldots,\underline{a}_{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}:\underline{a}_{1}=0,~\underline{a}_{k}=1,~|\underline{a}_{j}|\leq 1\text{ for }j\neq k\}

is compact. For each point
\underline{p}\in K

there exists
\rho(\underline{p})>0
such that we have a splitting

P_{\tilde{a}}=P_{b}P_{c}
on the open ball
B(\underline{p},\rho(\underline{p}))
, and we fix this splitting;
cf.
Section

6.5
.
Choose a finite subcover of
K
by open balls
B(\underline{p}_{\delta},\rho_{\delta})
,
\delta\in\Delta
.
Then there exists
\rho>0
such that for every
\underline{p}\in K
there is a
\delta\in\Delta
such that

B(\underline{p},\rho)\subseteq B(\underline{p}_{\delta},\rho_{\delta})
.

To summarize, for each integer
d\geq 2
we have fixed

(1)

a finite cover
\mathcal{B}
of
K
by open balls
B
,

(2)

a splitting
P_{\tilde{a}}=P_{b}P_{c}
on each
B\in\mathcal{B}
together with analytic functions
\psi_{i}
and

\tilde{\psi}_{i}
which are bounded on
B
along with all their partial derivatives,

(3)

a positive number
\rho
such that for each
\underline{p}\in K
there is a
B\in\mathcal{B}
such that

B(\underline{p},\rho)\subseteq B
(note that
\rho
is a Lebesgue number of the cover
\mathcal{B}
).

Remark 6.7
.

Additionally,
there exists
\chi>0
such that for all pairs
P_{\tilde{a}_{1}}=P_{b_{1}}P_{c_{1}}
and
P_{\tilde{a}_{2}}=P_{b_{2}}P_{c_{2}}

in some fixed
B\in\mathcal{B}
, where
\underline{a}_{1,k}=\underline{a}_{2,k}=1
,

(6.7)

\min_{P_{b_{1}}(\mu_{1})=0,\,P_{c_{2}}(\nu_{2})=0}|\tilde{a}_{2,k}^{1/k}\mu_{1}-\tilde{a}_{1,k}^{1/k}\nu_{2}|>\chi\cdot|\tilde{a}_{1,k}|^{1/k}|\tilde{a}_{2,k}|^{1/k}.

Indeed, by shrinking the balls
B(\underline{p},\rho(\underline{p}))
, we may assume that

(6.8)

\min_{Q_{\underline{b}_{1}}(\underline{\mu}_{1})=0,\,Q_{\underline{c}_{2}}(\underline{\nu}_{2})=0}|\underline{\mu}_{1}-\underline{\nu}_{2}|>\chi_{\underline{p}}>0,

for all pairs
Q_{\underline{a}_{1}}=Q_{\underline{b}_{1}}Q_{\underline{c}_{1}}
and
Q_{\underline{a}_{2}}=Q_{\underline{b}_{2}}Q_{\underline{c}_{2}}
in
B(\underline{p},\rho(\underline{p}))
.
Then take a finite subcover
\mathcal{B}
of
K
and let
\chi
be the minimum of the respective
\chi_{\underline{p}}
.
Multiplying (
6.8
) by
|\tilde{a}_{1,k}|^{1/k}|\tilde{a}_{2,k}|^{1/k}

and observing that the roots of
P_{b_{i}}
,
P_{c_{i}}
are the roots of
Q_{\underline{b}_{i}}
,
Q_{\underline{c}_{i}}

times
\tilde{a}_{i,k}^{1/k}
, gives (
6.7
).

Definition 6.8
.

We will refer to the data fixed in (1), (2), and (3) including
\chi>0
such that (
6.7
) holds
as a
universal splitting of polynomials of degree
d
in Tschirnhausen form

and to
\rho
as the
radius of the splitting
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a bounded open interval.
Let
P_{a}
,
P_{a_{n}}
, for

, be monic polynomials with

.

,

,

Let

be the curves of unordered roots of

(1)

in

(2)

\widehat{\mathbf{d}}^{1,q}_{I}(\tilde{\Lambda},\tilde{\Lambda}_{n})\to 0
, for all

Proof.

(1) This follows easily from (

) and
Proposition

2.1
.

(2) Fix

.
By

6.5
,
\|\mathbf{d}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\to 0

as well as
\|\mathbf{d}(\tilde{\Lambda},\tilde{\Lambda}_{n})\|_{L^{\infty}(I)}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
Assume that
\widehat{\mathbf{d}}^{1,q}_{I}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.
By
Theorem

3.11
,
we have

\|\Delta\circ\Lambda-\Delta\circ\Lambda_{n}\|_{W^{1,q}(I,\mathbb{R}^{N})}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for any Almgren embedding
\Delta:{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
(see (

)).
Let

be an Almgren map with associated real linear form
\eta
(see

).
The Tschirnhausen transformation
shifts

and

by

and

Proposition 8.2
.

Let

be an integer.
Let

be a bounded open interval.
Let

By

\int_{U^{\prime}}A^{1}_{n}(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\to 0\quad\text{ and }\quad\int_{U^{\prime}}B^{1}_{n}(x^{\prime})\,dx^{\prime}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Thus there is a subsequence
(n_{k})
such that
A^{1}_{n_{k}}(x^{\prime})\to 0
and
B^{1}_{n_{k}}(x^{\prime})\to 0
for almost every
x^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}
as
k\to\infty
.
For each such
x^{\prime}
,

Theorem

3.11
implies that
A^{2}_{n_{k}}(x^{\prime})\to 0
and
B^{2}_{n_{k}}(x^{\prime})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
,
which are defined in analogy to
A^{1}_{n}(x^{\prime})
and
B^{1}_{n}(x^{\prime})
with
p
replaced by
q
.
Set
r=\frac{q+p}{2q}
.
Then, as in (
10.3
),
we see that
\{A^{2}_{n}:n\geq 1\}
and
\{B^{2}:n\geq 1\}
are uniformly integrable,
because
\{F^{2}_{n}:n\geq 1\}
is bounded in

.

Then

A.2
and Tonelli’s theorem imply that

\|F^{2}-F^{2}_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}})}\to 0\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\partial_{1}F^{2}-\partial_{1}F^{2}_{n_{k}}\|_{L^{q}(U,\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}})}\to 0

as

. Since the partial derivatives

2\leq j\leq m

(n_{k})
such that

4.6

10.3.
A multiparameter version of
Theorem

1.2

Definition 10.3
.

Let
U=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{m}
be a bounded open box in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Let
f,g:U\to{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be such that the restriction to each segment in
U
parallel to the coordinate axes belongs to
W^{1,q}
:
writing

x=\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}e_{j}=x_{i}e_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}x_{j}e_{j}=:x_{i}e_{i}+\underline{x}_{i}

and
f_{\underline{x}_{i}}(x_{i}):=f(x_{i}e_{i}+\underline{x}_{i})
, we have
f_{\underline{x}_{i}},g_{\underline{x}_{i}}\in W^{1,q}(I_{i},{\mathcal{A}}_{d}(\mathbb{C}))
for all
1\leq i\leq m

and all
\underline{x}_{i}\in U_{i}:=\prod_{j\neq i}I_{j}
.
Define

\mathbf{s}_{1}(f,g)(x):=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\mathbf{s}_{1}(f_{\underline{x}_{i}},g_{\underline{x}_{i}})(x_{i}).

Theorem 10.4
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer.
Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a bounded open box,
U=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{m}
.
Let
a_{n}\to a
in
C^{d}(\overline{U},\mathbb{C}^{d})
.
Let
\Lambda,\Lambda_{n}:U\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
be the maps of unordered roots of
P_{a}
,
P_{a_{n}}
, respectively.
Then

\|\mathbf{s}_{1}(\Lambda,\Lambda_{n})\|_{L^{q}(U)}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

for all
1\leq q<d/(d-1)
.

Proof.

Fix
1\leq q<d/(d-1)
. Without loss of generality let
i=1
and set
\underline{x}_{1}=x^{\prime}
and
U_{1}=U^{\prime}
.
For
x^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}
,
consider

A_{n}(x^{\prime})=\int_{I_{1}}(\mathbf{s}_{1}(\Lambda_{x^{\prime}},(\Lambda_{n})_{x^{\prime}})(x_{1}))^{q}\,dx_{1}.

Then
A_{n}(x^{\prime})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, by
Theorem

1.2
.
The family
\{A_{n}:n\geq 1\}
is uniformly integrable: with
r
as defined in (
10.2
)
we have (see (
10.3
))

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq 1}\int_{U^{\prime}}A_{n}(x^{\prime})^{r}\,dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq|I_{1}|^{r-1}\sup_{n\geq 1}\int_{U^{\prime}}\int_{I_{1}}(\mathbf{s}_{1}(\Lambda_{x^{\prime}},(\Lambda_{n})_{x^{\prime}})(x_{1}))^{qr}\,dx_{1}\,dx^{\prime}.

Let
\lambda_{x^{\prime}}
,
(\lambda_{n})_{x^{\prime}}
be a continuous parameterization of the roots of
P_{a(\cdot,x^{\prime})}
,
P_{a_{n}(\cdot,x^{\prime})}
, respectively.
Then

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{s}_{1}(\Lambda_{x^{\prime}},(\Lambda_{n})_{x^{\prime}})\|_{L^{qr}(I_{1})}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\Big(\|\lambda_{x^{\prime}}^{\prime}\|_{L^{qr}(I_{1},\mathbb{C}^{d})}+\|(\lambda_{n})_{x^{\prime}}^{\prime}\|_{L^{qr}(I_{1},\mathbb{C}^{d})}\Big)

by

7.1
, as

. By assumption, the right-hand side is bounded by a constant that is independent of
x^{\prime}
and
n
.
Thus, uniform integrability follows from
Theorem

A.1
.
So Tonelli’s theorem and

A.2
imply the theorem.
∎

11.
Interpretation of the results in the Wasserstein space on
\mathbb{C}

In this section, we interpret our results in the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{C}
.
This point of view allowed Antonini, Cavalletti, and Lerario to study optimal transport between algebraic hypersurfaces in

\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}
in
[
4
]
. We will also finish the proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

11.1.
The roots as a probability measure

With a monic polynomial
P_{a}(Z)=Z^{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}Z^{d-j}
,
where
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
,
we may associate in a natural way a probability measure
\mu(a)
on
\mathbb{C}
defined by

\mu(a):=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{P_{a}(\lambda)=0}m_{\lambda}(a)\cdot\left\llbracket{\lambda}\right\rrbracket,

where
m_{\lambda}(a)
denotes the multiplicity of
\lambda
as a root of
P_{a}
and
\left\llbracket{\lambda}\right\rrbracket
is the Dirac mass at
\lambda
.

Let us endow the set
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{C})
of probability measures on
\mathbb{C}
(with its Euclidean structure)
with the
q
-Wasserstein distance
W_{q}
, for
q\geq 1
,
and denote the resulting metric space by
\mathscr{P}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
.

Then we get a map

\mu:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathscr{P}_{q}(\mathbb{C})

which, besides
\Lambda:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
from
Section

6.3
, is another incarnation of the solution map.
The image of
\mu
can be indentified with
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
or with the quotient of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
by the symmetric group

\operatorname{S}_{d}
. The restriction of the
W_{2}
-metric to
\mu(\mathbb{C}^{d})\subseteq\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
is given by

W_{2}([z],[w])^{2}=\min_{\sigma\in\operatorname{S}_{d}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|z_{j}-w_{\sigma(j)}|^{2},

and thus coincides with the metric
\mathbf{d}
on
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
from
Section

3.1
. (For this reason we chose the factor
1/\sqrt{d}

in the definition of
\mathbf{d}
.) We get a similar expression for
q\neq 2
, but all of them are equivalent.

It turns out that
\mu(\mathbb{C}^{d})
is geodesically convex in
\mathscr{P}_{q}(\mathbb{C})
.

11.2.
Some distances on
AC^{q}(I,X)

Let
(X,\mathsf{d})
be a complete metric space and
AC^{q}(I,X)
the set introduced in
Section

2.5
.
The set
AC^{q}(I,X)
can be seen as a subset of the metric space
C^{0}(I,X)
with the uniform norm.
Additionally, the metric speed or the
q
-energy can be used in a natural way to measure “closeness”
in
AC^{q}(I,X)
. Thus, we define, for
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in AC^{q}(I,X)
,

\operatorname{dist}^{s}_{q}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):=\sup_{x\in I}\mathsf{d}(\gamma_{1}(x),\gamma_{2}(x))+\big\||\dot{\gamma}_{1}|-|\dot{\gamma}_{2}|\big\|_{L^{q}(I)}

and

\operatorname{dist}^{e}_{q}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):=\sup_{x\in I}\mathsf{d}(\gamma_{1}(x),\gamma_{2}(x))+\big|\mathcal{E}_{q}(\gamma_{1})-\mathcal{E}_{q}(\gamma_{2})\big|.

Both
\operatorname{dist}^{s}_{q}
and
\operatorname{dist}^{e}_{q}
are metrics on
AC^{q}(I,X)
.

11.3.
Boundedness and continuity of the map
\mu_{*}

In the following, we identify
\mu(\mathbb{C}^{d})
with
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
.
Recall that the map
[\cdot]:\mathbb{C}^{d}\to\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{C})
which sends an ordered
d
-tuple to the corresponding unordered one is Lipschitz.

Lemma 11.1
.

Let
\lambda:I\to\mathbb{C}^{d}
be an absolutely continuous curve and let
\gamma:I\to\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{C})
be
defined by
\gamma(x):=[\lambda(x)]
, for
x\in I
.
Then the metric speed of
\gamma
is given by

|\dot{\gamma}|(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\lambda^{\prime}(x)\|_{2}\quad\text{ for almost every }x\in I.

Proof.

For
x\in I
and
|h|
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle W_{2}(\gamma(x),\gamma(x+h))=\mathbf{d}([\lambda(x)],[\lambda(x+h)])\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\lambda(x)-\lambda(x+h)\|_{2}.

Let
\sigma_{h}\in\operatorname{S}_{d}
be such that

\mathbf{d}([\lambda(x)],[\lambda(x+h)])=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\lambda(x)-\sigma_{h}\lambda(x+h)\|_{2}.

We claim that, for sufficiently small
|h|
,
\sigma_{h}
lies in the stabilizer group of
\lambda(x)
, i.e.,
\sigma_{h}\lambda(x)=\lambda(x)
.
Otherwise, there is a sequence
h_{n}\to 0
such that
\sigma_{h_{n}}\lambda(x)\neq\lambda(x)
.
Since
\operatorname{S}_{d}
is finite, by passing to a subsequence, we may assume that
\sigma_{h_{n}}=:\sigma
is independent of
n
.
But continuity of
\lambda

implies

\mathbf{d}([\lambda(x)],[\lambda(x+h_{n})])=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\lambda(x)-\sigma\lambda(x+h_{n})\|_{2}\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty,

and hence
\sigma\lambda(x)=\lambda(x)
, a contradition.

By the claim, for small enough
|h|
,

\mathbf{d}([\lambda(x)],[\lambda(x+h)])=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\|\lambda(x)-\lambda(x+h)\|_{2}.

Now the assertion follows easily.
∎

Appendix A

A.1.
Vitali’s convergence theorem

Let
(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)
be a measure space with nonnegative measure
\mu
(finite or with values in
[0,\infty]
).
A set of functions
\mathcal{F}\subseteq L^{1}(\mu)
is called
uniformly integrable
if

\lim_{C\to+\infty}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\int_{|f|>C}|f|\,d\mu=0.

Theorem A.1

(De la Vallée Poussin’s criterion
[
6
, Theorem 4.5.9]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a finite nonnegative measure.
A family
\mathcal{F}
of
\mu
-integrable functions is uniformly integrable if and only if
there exists a nonnegative increasing function
G
on
[0,\infty)
such that

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{G(t)}{t}=\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\int G(|f(x)|)\,\mu(dx)<\infty.

In such a case, one can choose a convex increasing function
G
.

Recall that a sequence of complex valued measurable functions
f_{n}
on
X
is said to

converge in measure
to
f

if, for all
\epsilon>0
,

\mu(\{x\in X:|f(x)-f_{n}(x)|\geq\epsilon\})\to 0\quad\text{ as }n\to\infty.

Theorem A.2

(Vitali’s convergence theorem
[
6
, Theorem 4.5.4]
)
.

Let
\mu
be a finite measure.
Suppose that
f
is a
\mu
-measurable function and
\{f_{n}\}
is a sequence of
\mu
-integrable functions.
Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(1)

f_{n}\to f
in measure and
\{f_{n}\}
is uniformly integrable.

(2)

f
is integrable and
f_{n}\to f
in
L^{1}(\mu)
.

A.2.
Proof of
Proposition

2.1

We follow
[
8
]
in which Hölder–Lipschitz spaces are treated;
the proofs simplify considerably.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
be open, bounded, and convex.
For brevity, we will simply write
\|\cdot\|_{k}
for the
C^{k}
norm from (
2.2
).

Lemma A.3
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{\ell}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a linear map.
Then
\psi_{*}:C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})\to C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{p})
is linear and continuous
with operator norm
\|\psi_{*}\|=\|\psi\|
.

Proof.

For
k=0
and
\varphi\in C^{0}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
,
\|\psi\circ\varphi\|_{0}\leq\|\psi\|\|\varphi\|_{0}
.
Let
k\geq 1
and
\varphi\in C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
.
Then
d(\psi\circ\varphi)=\psi\circ d\varphi
and the statement follows by induction.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a bilinear map.
Then
\psi_{*}:C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{1}})\times C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{2}})\to C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{p})

is bilinear and continuous with
\|\psi_{*}\|\leq C(k)\|\psi\|
.

Proof.

For
k=0
and
\varphi_{i}\in C^{0}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{i}})
,
i=1,2
,

\|\psi_{*}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})\|_{0}\leq 2\|\psi\|\|\varphi_{1}\|_{0}\|\varphi_{2}\|_{0}.

For
k\geq 1
and
\varphi_{i}\in C^{1}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{i}})
,
i=1,2
,

d(\psi_{*}(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}))=(\psi_{1})_{*}(d\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})+(\psi_{2})_{*}(\varphi_{1},d\varphi_{2}),

where
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are the bilinear maps

\displaystyle\psi_{1}

\displaystyle:L(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{1}})\times\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{2}}\to L(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{p}),\quad(h_{1},y_{2})\mapsto(x\mapsto\psi(h_{1}(x),y_{2})),

\displaystyle\psi_{2}

\displaystyle:\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{1}}\times L(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{2}})\to L(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{p}),\quad(y_{1},h_{2})\mapsto(x\mapsto\psi(y_{1},h_{2}(x))).

Thus the statement follows by induction.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\varphi\in C^{k}(\overline{U},V)
.
Then
\varphi^{*}:C^{k}(\overline{V},\mathbb{R}^{p})\to C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{p})
,
\varphi^{*}(\psi):=\psi\circ\varphi
, is well-defined, linear, and continuous.
More precisely, for
\psi\in C^{k}(\overline{V},\mathbb{R}^{p})
,

\|\varphi^{*}(\psi)\|_{k}\leq C(k)\,\|\psi\|_{k}(1+\|\varphi\|_{k})^{k}.

Proof.

The statement for
k=0
is clear.
Now let us proceed by induction on
k
and assume that the statement holds for
k-1
.
By
Lemma

A.4
and the induction hypothesis,

\displaystyle\|d(\psi\circ\varphi)\|_{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|d\psi\circ\varphi\|_{k-1}\|d\varphi\|_{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,\|d\psi\|_{k-1}(1+\|\varphi\|_{k-1})^{k-1}\|d\varphi\|_{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,\|\psi\|_{k}(1+\|\varphi\|_{k})^{k}

and the assertion for
k
follows easily.
∎

Now we are ready to prove
Proposition

2.1
which is reformulated in the following lemma.

Lemma A.6
.

Let
\psi\in C^{k+1}(\overline{V},\mathbb{R}^{p})
. Then

\psi_{*}:C^{k}(\overline{U},V)\to C^{k}(\overline{U},\mathbb{R}^{p})
,
\psi_{*}(\varphi):=\psi\circ\varphi
, is well-defined and continuous.
More precisely, for
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
in a bounded subset
B
of
C^{k}(\overline{U},V)
,

\|\psi_{*}(\varphi_{1})-\psi_{*}(\varphi_{2})\|_{k}\leq C\,\|\psi\|_{k+1}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{k},

where
C=C(k,B)
.

Proof.

For
k=0
, we have

\|\psi\circ\varphi_{1}-\psi\circ\varphi_{2}\|_{0}\leq C\,\|d\psi\|_{0}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{0}\leq C\,\|\psi\|_{1}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{0}.

Let us proceed by induction on
k
and assume that the statement holds for
k-1
.
We have

\displaystyle\|d(\psi\circ\varphi_{1})-d(\psi\circ\varphi_{2})\|_{k-1}

\displaystyle=\|(d\psi\circ\varphi_{1}).(d\varphi_{1}-d\varphi_{2})-(d\psi\circ\varphi_{2}-d\psi\circ\varphi_{1}).d\varphi_{2}\|_{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|d\psi\circ\varphi_{1}\|_{k-1}\|d\varphi_{1}-d\varphi_{2}\|_{k-1}+C\,\|d\psi\circ\varphi_{2}-d\psi\circ\varphi_{1}\|_{k-1}\|d\varphi_{2}\|_{k-1},

where we used
Lemma

A.4
in the last step.
By
Lemma

A.5
,

\displaystyle\|d\psi\circ\varphi_{1}\|_{k-1}\|d\varphi_{1}-d\varphi_{2}\|_{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,\|d\psi\|_{k-1}(1+\|\varphi_{1}\|_{k-1})^{k-1}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{k}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\,\|\psi\|_{k}(1+\|\varphi_{1}\|_{k-1})^{k-1}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{k}.

By the induction hypothesis,

\displaystyle\|d\psi\circ\varphi_{2}-d\psi\circ\varphi_{1}\|_{k-1}\|d\varphi_{2}\|_{k-1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\,\|d\psi\|_{k}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{k-1}\|\varphi_{2}\|_{k}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\,\|\psi\|_{k+1}\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|_{k}\|\varphi_{2}\|_{k}.

Now the statement follows easily.
∎
