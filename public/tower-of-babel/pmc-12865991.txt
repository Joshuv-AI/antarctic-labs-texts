Stakeholder perception of quality management of investigator-initiated clinical trials
Authors: Wenqiang Li, Kailibinuer Ailimu, Nanxi Jia, Hongling Chu, Yiming Zhao, Liyuan Tao, Siyan Zhan, Lin Zeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInvestigator-initiated clinical trials have flourished in China. However, research on stakeholders’ perceptions and management capacity of investigator-initiated clinical trials, which are crucial for optimizing resource utilization, timely identifying barriers affecting high-quality Investigator-initiated clinical trials output, and providing evidence for establishing, improving, and evaluating the quality management system for Investigator-initiated clinical trials, is rare. However, much room for improvement still exists. Introduction

Clinical research can be divided into investigator-initiated clinical trials (IITs) and industry-sponsored clinical trials (ISTs) based on the sponsor and funding source [1, 2]. IITs have scientific and medical merit and are developed and sponsored by independent investigators in academic institutions (nonpharmaceutical company researchers). They are designed to address clinical problems rather than generate profit, and are conducted without the direct involvement of pharmaceutical companies [3–5] . However, the IIT sponsor may request pharmaceutical support, such as funding, drug products, or both [6]. IITs differ significantly from ISTs in terms of sponsorship, research stage, value orientation, purpose, type, resources, management, and conflicts of interest; they primarily address socially significant issues neglected by ISTs, such as identifying new indications for marketed drugs, comparing clinical diagnostic and treatment methods, and studying rare diseases [4, 5, 7–12]. Their diverse research types, active exploration, high innovation, strong representation of study subjects, reduced conflicts of interest, and direct engagement with clinical problems play an essential role in generating clinical evidence that can drive policy changes [1–3, 5, 7, 12–14]. However, IITs face significant challenges, such as high complexity, inadequate researcher qualifications, insufficient resources, and regulation and self-management difficulties. Although regulatory authorities have provided detailed guidelines for ISTs, the requirements for IITs remain unclear, leading to trial quality and transparency issues [15, 16].

Recently, IITs have attracted the attention of the Chinese medical community, with their number and sample size increasing rapidly, surpassing those of ISTs. However, significant challenges remain in producing high-quality research achievements and achieving clinical translation [7, 17]. These challenges can be addressed by establishing a comprehensive quality management system and complying with relevant policies and regulations to better standardize IITs and fulfill the unmet clinical needs in China [18].

Quality management systems for IITs vary significantly across countries. Developed countries, such as Europe and North America, began conducting IITs earlier. Therefore, their management systems and experience are relatively mature. Conversely, the Chinese stakeholders’ understanding and management of IITs remain in the initial stages. In China, IITs are funded, approved, and overseen by academic institutions, government bodies, or foundations, which results in diverse management departments, loose oversight, and significant challenges in standardized regulation. Furthermore, the self-management level among stakeholders needs improvement, and regulatory policies and laws specifically targeting IITs are still lacking, with guidance primarily derived from Good Clinical Practice, Measures for the Ethical Review of Biomedical Research Involving Humans, and so on. In 2021, the National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China issued the Administrative Measures for Investigator-Initiated Clinical Trials in Healthcare Institutions (trial version) (referred to as the Administrative Measures) and published the final version in 2024 [19]. The Administrative Measures provided guidance and standardization for IIT quality management at the macropolicy level. However, healthcare institutions should establish and refine systematic, standardized, and implementable quality management and support systems for IITs based on the Administrative Measures, which may improve the IIT quality in China.

Research data on the perception and understanding of IITs and their standardized management practices among various stakeholders from healthcare institutions are lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate stakeholders’ perceptions of the standardized quality management of IITs across different levels of healthcare institutions, thereby identifying potential weaknesses and barriers in a timely manner and providing a foundation for implementing a comprehensive system.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional survey study conducted in China. Electronic surveys were distributed by the Beijing Haidian District Center for Advancing Quality in Clinical Research to all healthcare institutions conducting clinical research within this District from June to September 2024, thereby achieving complete institutional coverage. Within each institution, eligible stakeholders were invited to participate voluntarily by completing the questionnaire. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cross-sectional studies [20]. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital Medical Science (Project No.: IRB00006761-M2023250; Approval Date: April 25, 2023) and conducted in accordance with the requirements and the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent to participate was obtained electronically. Informed consent information was presented on the first page of the online questionnaire, and participants were informed that completing and submitting the questionnaire indicated their consent to participate.

Participants

The study participants were stakeholders from the healthcare institutions, including medical personnel, research administrators, methodologists, and other personnel. Participants received an online link or QR code to access the survey, which was designed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) software version 13.5.1 (Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN, USA). Participants completed a 10–20 min self-administered questionnaire in their preferred format. The survey provided information about the study overview, privacy protection, right to refuse participation, and contact information and clearly stated that filling out or submitting the survey indicated consent to participate. Participants who answered all three attention check questions incorrectly or those with more than 5% missing responses were excluded from the study.

Survey instrument

Based on the Administrative Measures, the Declaration of Helsinki, and the ICMJE standards regarding ethics, registration, and other clinical research requirements, the survey addressed six domains, including (1) registration and filing: concerning clinical trial registry platforms and the National Medical Research Registration and Filing Information System (https://www.medicalresearch.org.cn); (2) ethical review: focusing on the processes and requirements for ethical review; (3) informed consent: regarding the standards for signing informed consent; (4) study protocol: focusing on protocol implementation specifications and adherence; (5) data management: concerning the requirements for data quality, storage, and traceability; and (6) laboratory and biobank: involving laboratory records and requirements for sample collection, transportation, and storage [21–23]. Additional items on general demographic information and training needs were also included. Not all questions were included in the study, but the most critical core questions were carefully selected for analysis (see Supplementary Method 1).

Potential exposures of interest

Potential influencing factors included hospital level, education level, years in research, familiarity, distinction ability, and types of stakeholders. Details of the exposure factors are provided in Supplementary Method 2.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes included the total number of correct answers and weighted correct rates for each domain and the six domains, which were calculated as follows:

Weighted correct rate of each domain = (total correct answers)/(total valid answers, excluding missing values).

Weighted correct rate of each domain = (total correct answers)/(total valid answers, excluding missing values).

Total weighted correct rate of all domains = (sum of the weighted correct rates of each domain)/6.

Total weighted correct rate of all domains = (sum of the weighted correct rates of each domain)/6.

The outcomes for each domain were considered discrete quantitative variables, whereas the outcomes for all domains were considered approximately continuous.

The secondary outcomes included the distribution of the total number of correct answers and the weighted correct rate across all domains, grouped by hospital level.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected using REDCap. Statistical analyses and figure generation were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Quantitative data were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) based on the data distribution and analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test or Mann–Whitney U test. Qualitative data were presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. A multiple linear regression model was used to investigate the effect of potential exposures on the total number of correct answers and weighted correct rate across all domains. A negative binomial regression model was used to investigate the effect of potential exposures on the total number of correct answers and weighted correct rates for each domain. Adjusted factors included hospital level, education level, years in research, familiarity, distinction ability, medical personnel, research administrators, methodologists, and other personnel. Generalized variance inflation factors were calculated to evaluate multicollinearity among all influencing factors. Additionally, potential interaction effects were assessed. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 783 individuals were enrolled in this study. Of the 783 individuals, 6 were excluded because more than 5% of the data were missing, and 60 were excluded because all three attention check questions were answered incorrectly. Finally, 717 (91.6%) individuals were included in the final analysis. A total of 25 detailed survey questions were included in the analysis.

Participant characteristics

Table 1 shows the characteristics of the 717 participants. The median (IQR) age of the participants was 39.0 (34.0–45.0) years. Of the 717 participants, 526 (74.6%) were female, 382 (54.2%) had a graduate level, 124 (17.6%) had more than 15 years in research, 410 (58.2%) reported being familiar with clinical research methods, and 342 (48.5%) were able to differentiate between IITs and ISTs. A total of 610 participants (86.5%) were medical personnel, 70 (9.9%) were research administrators, 12 (1.7%) were methodologists, and 108 (15.3%) were other personnel. When grouped by hospital level, statistically significant differences were observed for the aforementioned factors (P < 0.05).

Analysis of stakeholder perceptions and influencing factors

The distribution of total correct answers and weighted correct rates across all six domains was 20.0 (15.0–23.0) and 0.8 (0.6–0.9), respectively. Figure 1 shows the total correct answers and weighted correct rates for all domains by exposure factors. Participants from higher-level hospitals, those with higher education levels, longer years in research, greater familiarity, and higher distinction ability, administrators, and methodologists performed better in terms of total correct answers (methodologists: P = 0.016, others: P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between the medical personnel and other personnel. The weighted correct rate for each exposure factor was consistent with the total correct answers across all domains. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the distribution of the total number of correct answers and the weighted correct rate across all domains, grouped by hospital level.

Distribution of Total Correct Answers and Weighted Correct Rates for the Six Domains by Exposure Factors

Figure 2 shows the total correct answers for each domain by exposure factors. Participants from higher-level hospitals, those with higher education levels, those with longer years in research, those with greater familiarity, those with higher distinction ability, and administrators performed better across each domain (P < 0.01). Medical personnel performed worse in the ethical review domain (5.0 [4.0–6.0] vs. 6.0 [4.0–7.0], P = 0.026), whereas methodologists performed better in the informed consent domain (4.0 [2.8–4.0] vs. 3.0 [2.0–4.0], P = 0.036). No significant differences were observed for other personnel. The weighted correct rate for each domain by exposure factors was consistent with the total correct answers in terms of distribution and statistical significance (Supplementary Fig. 2). Figure 3 shows the results of the multivariable analysis of the total correct answers and weighted correct rates across all domains. Participants from secondary (β = 1.57, P = 0.019) and tertiary hospitals (β = 2.10, P < 0.001) performed significantly better than those from primary hospitals in terms of total correct answers. Participants with bachelor (β = 3.85, P < 0.001) and graduate levels (β = 6.74, P < 0.001) performed significantly better than those with associate level. Additionally, participants familiar with clinical research methods (β = 4.65, P < 0.001) and those who could differentiate between IITs and ISTs (β = 4.65, P < 0.001) performed significantly better. Research administrators demonstrated superior performance (β = 2.40, P < 0.001). The results for the weighted correct rates were identical. The results of the multivariable analysis for the total correct answers across each domain are shown in Supplementary Table 1; the potential influencing factors were summarized.

Training needs

The main training needs identified by stakeholders were clinical research design (77.4%), statistical analysis methods (66.2%), and ethical standards and patient protection (62.3%), with participants from tertiary hospitals showing a lower demand (Supplementary Table 2). Most participants believed that training should be conducted annually, and a combination of online and offline sessions was the preferred training method (73.3%).

Discussion

The results showed that the stakeholders performed well overall. However, much room for improvement in the awareness of clinical studies and their management among stakeholders within healthcare institutions still exists. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies [24–26]. Different roles have varying levels of perception regarding clinical research knowledge [27]. Participants from higher-level hospitals, those with higher education levels, those with greater familiarity, those with higher distinction ability, and administrators performed significantly better in terms of the total number of correct answers and weighted correct rates across all domains. However, the specific effects of these factors varied across different domains, indicating that the underlying mechanisms and pathways of influence are distinct.

Participants with higher education levels, familiarity, and distinction ability tended to perform better in each domain. This finding indicates that higher levels of scientific research training and experience lead to a more comprehensive understanding of IITs and their management. Participants with higher educational levels typically receive systematic training in research methods and ethical standards, enabling them to better execute complex tasks. Furthermore, familiarity with clinical research processes and methods leads to a deeper understanding of the requirements for the six domains, better risk identification, and optimized execution. Higher distinction ability between IITs and ISTs allows stakeholders to address the different requirements of each type of study in research design and implementation. Conversely, insufficient opportunities, abilities, and knowledge gaps hinder clinical research quality [28, 29]. Furthermore, differences in education level may affect clinical research perceptions [24, 30]. Clinical physicians have a poor understanding of basic clinical research concepts, which may be affected by hospital and education levels [31]. Fewer than 40% of the nurses could distinguish between IITs and ISTs, indicating limited knowledge of clinical research [32].

Prospective registration and filing of clinical trials ensure research transparency, avoid selective reporting bias [33–36]. Studies have shown that the prospective registration rates are low, indicating a lack of awareness among stakeholders [22, 37]. Standardized data and laboratory/biobank management are crucial to ensuring the security, traceability, reproducibility, completeness, and authenticity of data and samples. A previous study developed a specific data management strategy to overcome the associated challenges [38]. Data collection and storage using an electronic data capture system has significant advantages [10]. Support from medical personnel of various backgrounds is vital to biobank activities. However, stakeholders have limited knowledge of biobank management and insufficient training, and participants with a medical background are more willing to donate biological samples [26, 39, 40]. With the widespread implementation of related regulations and technological methods, the practices of registration and filing, data management, and biobank management are gradually becoming standardized, reducing differences based on hospital level, years in research, and roles [7]. Nonetheless, stakeholders with higher educational levels and familiarity still outperform others due to their understanding of related standards.

Effective oversight requires a rigorous assessment of the scientific validity and ethical compliance of the studies. Ethical review is critical to protecting participants’ rights, including informed rights, safety, and privacy. Higher-level hospitals often have dedicated ethics committees and management teams. However, hospitals’ resource allocation and system construction can influence stakeholder performance in ethical review. First, ethical review in higher-level hospitals is often more stringent and standardized, including document review and committee checks. Second, research administrators directly participate in organizing and coordinating research projects, resulting in a better understanding of ethical review policies and procedures, thus outperforming others. However, further exploration is required to determine whether strict review procedures and scientific guidelines enhance the ethical awareness and capabilities of other stakeholders. Previous studies have shown that clinicians’ understanding of ethical review requires improvement [41]. Additionally, domestic stakeholders’ understanding of the purchase of clinical trial insurance and privacy protection remains insufficient [24, 30]. The current barriers are limited funding, weak regulatory and administrative systems, and a shortage of human and material resources [28, 42].

Informed consent is crucial for ensuring the participants’ informed rights and autonomy, whereas the study protocol helps ensure the scientific validity and feasibility of the research [10, 18]. Higher-level hospitals typically have more comprehensive ethics and academic committees, specialized research and methodological teams, standardized informed consent processes, and study protocol design services. These institutions have advantages in resource allocation, personnel competence, ethical requirements, risk control, and patient experience, especially in complex and multicenter trials. Consequently, they can better conduct informed consent and develop detailed study protocols according to regulations and guidelines, thereby effectively protecting participants’ rights, improving compliance, and reducing bias. Therefore, these institutions performed better in terms of informed consent and study protocol development. Previous studies have shown that healthcare professionals require a deeper understanding of informed consent standards [43].

Training can improve stakeholders’ capabilities, reduce information gaps, and successfully implement regulatory systems [18, 28]. Training opportunities are abundant, but enhancing training effectiveness is crucial. Training formats, including in-person meetings and webinars, can disseminate knowledge related to clinical research and its management [10, 24, 30]. Previous studies have highlighted the need to acquire concepts and skills regarding clinical research, particularly those related to statistics [44]. This study showed that some researchers still lack a clear understanding of the sequencing of key events in clinical research, such as enrolling study participants after ethical approval, continuing research after protocol amendment approval, and prospective registration. Additionally, tertiary hospitals exhibited slightly lower demand and frequency for training than primary and secondary hospitals, indicating that further efforts are needed to improve training outreach and effectiveness at the grassroots level, ensuring that it is targeted and impactful. Additionally, developing a combined online and offline training model is essential for future deployment.

Targeted improvement measures can be proposed for healthcare institutions at different levels based on the survey results. For tertiary hospitals, where research resources and regulatory awareness are relatively strong, quality management should focus on refining standard operating procedures, strengthening coordination in multicenter studies, and improving the efficiency of project monitoring. For secondary hospitals, which often face challenges such as insufficient dedicated research personnel and limited training opportunities, capacity can be gradually enhanced through the establishment of management support by external agencies, expert training, and the adoption of simplified yet standardized quality management tools. For primary hospitals, where awareness of IIT quality management is relatively weak, priority should be given to basic training, strengthening project implementation and management capabilities, and establishing collaborative support mechanisms with higher-level hospitals.

The study’s strengths lie in its collection of data on the perceptions of standardized quality management of IITs among stakeholders from healthcare institutions at various levels across Beijing, China. Given the multitude of policies and regulatory requirements, IIT practices remain complex in most countries. The findings of this study are crucial for identifying quality management issues for IITs, which are of great global concern.

This study has some limitations. First, this was a cross-sectional study that included healthcare institutions from some regions in Beijing, which may limit the representativeness and generalizability of the findings. However, the hospitals included covered all levels, and Beijing was one of the first pilot regions for the Administrative Measures, which offered a degree of representativeness. Second, some survey questions relied on participants’ self-reporting, which might have resulted in discrepancies between perceived and actual understanding. However, the findings revealed consistency between self-perceived familiarity and distinction ability and the actual responses provided.

Conclusion

The stakeholders from the healthcare institutions performed well overall. Participants from higher-level hospitals, those with higher education levels, greater familiarity, and higher distinction ability, and administrators performed significantly better. Further efforts are needed to improve training at the grassroots level, improve targeted training programs, and develop a combined online and offline training model. Furthermore, a quality management system for IITs in China should be established, improved, and evaluated.
