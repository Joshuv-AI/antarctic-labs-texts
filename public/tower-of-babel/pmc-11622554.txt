Trends and determinants of child stunting in Ethiopia: a longitudinal analysis of sociodemographic factors and regional disparities
Authors: Yilikal Tesfaye Haile, Tesfahun Zewde Legisso
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Stunting remains a critical issue affecting child health in Ethiopia, with significant implications for growth and development. Despite efforts to address stunting, understanding its determinants and trends over time is essential for developing effective interventions. The objective of this study is to examine the trends and determinants of child stunting in Ethiopia, focusing on sociodemographic factors and regional disparities. This longitudinal observational study utilized data from the Young Lives project covering the period from 2002 to 2016. An ordinal logistic regression model with generalized estimating equation (GEE) was used to analyse 7,680 observations from 1,536 children across Ethiopia. Variables included sociodemographic factors, access to resources, and parental education levels. Over the study period, there were notable improvements in child stunting rates, influenced by variables such as household wealth, maternal education, access to safe drinking water, and urban versus rural residence. Children in households with higher wealth and maternal education levels experienced lower rates of stunting. Regional disparities were evident, with a greater prevalence in Amhara and lower rates in Addis Ababa.The findings underscore the importance of targeted interventions to address socioeconomic disparities and improve resource access. Enhancing maternal education, ensuring access to safe water, and addressing regional disparities are critical to reducing child stunting rates in Ethiopia.

Background of the study

Stunting is defined as hindered growth and development in children due to chronic malnutrition, typically occurring during the first 1,000 days of life from conception to a child’s second birthday. It is characterized by low height-for-age compared to established growth standards [1]. Stunting is a critical indicator of child health and nutrition and is influenced by factors such as inadequate nutrient intake, poor maternal health and nutrition, frequent infections, and insufficient access to healthcare and clean water [2].

Children who suffer from stunting may never reach their full potential height, and their cognitive development may be limited. These children are at a distinct disadvantage from the start, which has lasting effects as they grow into adulthood. They experience difficulties with learning in school, have lower earning potential as adults, and encounter barriers to engaging in their communities. Recent global statistics indicate that stunting continues to be a significant problem affecting children worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. The global prevalence of under 5 children experiencing stunting has decreased from 204.2 million (33%) to 148.1 million (22.3%) steadily over the period from 2000 to 2022. If the present patterns persist, it is anticipated that 128.5 million children (19.5%) will suffer from stunted growth by 2030 [3].

The prevalence of stunting varies greatly among regions, with South Asia having the highest rates globally, affecting approximately 37.7% of children under five years of age. Countries such as India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Nepal are known for their high stunting rates in this region. In sub-Saharan Africa, stunting affects approximately 27.4% of children under five years of age. Nations such as Nigeria, Ethiopia, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Tanzania often report high stunting rates due to poverty, food insecurity, and inadequate healthcare access [2].

Stunting continues to be a major public health issue in Ethiopia, impacting a significant number of children. According to the 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic Health Survey (EMDHS), 37% of children under five years of age are affected by growth stunting. This means that more than one-third of children in this age group suffer from chronic malnutrition, hindering their growth and development, with 12% being classified as severely stunted [4]. These findings indicate that the crises of child malnutrition remain serious in Ethiopia and require an analysis that carefully investigates what factors are involved in the patterns observed.

Several studies have been conducted to identify the determinants that affect child growth in Ethiopia [5–12]. However, most of the studies have used a cross-sectional and case‒control study design, which provides a snapshot of malnutrition incidence and associated factors at a single point in time and focuses on specific regions or communities within Ethiopia. This study sought to analyse the multifaceted factors influencing shifts within the ordinal categories of stunting over time at the national level. Tracking the changes and patterns in stunting levels over time offers valuable insights crucial for tailored interventions and policy formulation aimed at enhancing child health and well-being within the Ethiopian context. Understanding trends and patterns over time is essential for policymakers and healthcare practitioners to devise effective strategies aimed at improving child health and well-being. By identifying the factors influencing these trends, stakeholders can amend interventions to target specific areas of need. Ultimately, the findings of this study could inform evidence-based interventions and policy recommendations designed to mitigate the prevalence of stunting in Ethiopia, thereby improving the overall health and well-being of children in the country.

Methods

Study design

This study utilized a longitudinal observational design to monitor shifts in the ordinal classifications of stunting levels over time within a representative cohort of children in Ethiopia. Longitudinal investigations offer a crucial avenue for exploring the enduring impacts of diverse determinants on child growth and development, providing insights into the cumulative effects and trajectories of these factors over time [13, 14].

Data sources and population

The study utilizes data sourced from Young Lives, a longitudinal survey spanning 15 years aimed at exploring the changing dynamics of childhood poverty in Ethiopia, India, Peru, and Vietnam [15]. In each of these countries, the project tracks 2000 children aged one from 2002 to 2016. These children were selected from five primary regions in Ethiopia, namely, Tigray, Amhara, Oromia, the SNNP, and Addis Ababa, which together represent more than 90% of the country’s population.

The Young Lives study used a sentinel site surveillance system for sampling, employing a combination of multi-stage, purposive, and random sampling methods to select children. Households were randomly selected within each study site, while the sites themselves were chosen based on specific criteria. To ensure the sustainability of the study and facilitate follow-up surveys, a set number of carefully selected sites were used. These sites were chosen with a focus on disadvantaged populations, ensuring a balanced representation of Ethiopia’s regional diversity and capturing both rural and urban differences.

Throughout the survey, detailed data were gathered regarding the nutritional status of 2000 children and their household characteristics during the follow-up period (2002, 2006, 2009, 2011, and 2016). However, during the dataset examination, some observations were found to be incomplete. Consequently, only complete cases were included in the analysis for this study. The final sample consisted of 7,680 observations collected from 1,536 children at five time points for stunting over the follow-up period.

Study variables

The Young Lives dataset provides valuable insights into children’s nutritional status. In this study, the children’s stunting was measured using height-for-age z scores. These z scores are calculated by comparing the child’s measurements with those of a ‘healthy’ reference population defined by the 2007 WHO criteria. The response variables analysed included the level of stunting, which was obtained by categorizing z scores as 0 for not stunted, 1 for moderately stunted and 2 for severely stunted, as observed over time. These categories were accurately coded numerically to represent their ordinal nature.

Potential predictors considered in this study were time, sex of the child, age of the child, place of residence, religion of the child, parental education level, household size, wealth index, access to safe drinking water, access to sanitation, and access to adequate fuel for cooking, which could contribute to changes in stunting levels. In the young lives study wealth index is the primary measure of socio-economic status of households. It is constructed from three indices: housing quality, access to services, and ownership of consumer durables. In this study, the wealth index is calculated by averaging two factors: housing quality and ownership of consumer durable goods, while excluding variables related to access to services such as water, electricity, and sanitation, as these are likely to be highly correlated with the original wealth index. Content validity was assessed to ensure that the constructed index accurately measures the concept of health. The resulting values range from 0 to 1, with a higher score indicating better socioeconomic status.

Statistical model

The use of a longitudinal approach allows for the examination of malnutrition trends over time. Understanding how stunting levels change across different periods is crucial for identifying patterns, assessing progress, and recognizing potential emerging issues. Longitudinal ordinal logistic regression was used to explore the factors influencing changes in stunting levels. To determine how various factors contribute to changes in the ordinal categories of stunting levels over time, we used a longitudinal proportional odds regression model. This type of model can handle the ordered nature of the categories and estimate the effects of predictors on the odds of moving between categories. Parameter estimation technique

Generalized estimating equations (GEEs) are valuable parameter estimation techniques for longitudinal proportional odds regression models due to their robustness, efficiency, flexibility, and ease of implementation [16]. GEE does not require specifying the full distributional form of the data or the correlation structure explicitly, making it suitable for analysing longitudinal data with complex correlation structures. The GEE framework allows for the estimation of population-averaged effects and the interpretation of average effects of covariates on the outcome over time while accounting for the within-subject correlation structure, making it suitable for longitudinal data with repeated measures [17]. Additionally, GEE provides consistent estimates even when the correlation structure is misspecified, making it efficient and accessible for researchers and practitioners with varying levels of statistical expertise. Statistical analysis was conducted using R version 4.4.1 with the multgee package.

Results

Trends in stunting levels

Figure 1 presents valuable insights into the trends in stunting levels, namely, nonstunted, moderately stunted, and severely stunted, from 2002 to 2016. The proportion of nonstunted children, represented by the blue line, peaks in 2009 and then shows a slight decline, consistently comprising the largest group. This suggests initial improvements in factors contributing to child growth, such as nutrition and healthcare, although the decline after 2009 indicates potential challenges in sustaining these improvements.

The proportion of moderately stunted children, depicted by the green line, exhibited minor fluctuations without a clear trend. This finding suggests that while some progress has been made in addressing moderate stunting, it has not been consistent over the years.

Severely stunted children, indicated by the red line, remain consistently low and stable, suggesting that severe stunting, though less prevalent, continues to affect a small but persistent portion of the population. This persistence indicates ongoing issues that prevent the complete eradication of severe stunting, possibly due to entrenched socioeconomic factors, persistent poverty, or inadequate access to critical resources.

Overall, the data suggest that while there has been progress in reducing stunting, particularly in the none stunted category, efforts need to be intensified and sustained to effectively address moderate and severe stunting. The persistence of stunting at moderate and severe levels underscores the need for comprehensive and long-term interventions targeting the underlying causes.

Characteristics of children, mothers and households

Table 1 provides insights into the associations between demographic and socioeconomic factors and the prevalence of stunting among children at baseline. It is evident that there is a notable difference in stunting between male and female children. Across the categories of moderately and severely stunted, male children tended to have higher proportions than did their female counterparts. For instance, in the severely stunted category, 62.66% of male children were affected, while 37.34% of female children were affected. In terms of place of residence, rural areas had greater proportions of stunted children than did urban areas. Regional differences are also notable, with the Tigray, Amhara, and Oromiya regions exhibiting relatively greater stunting than southern nationalities and peoples (SNNPs) and Addis Ababa.

Results of GEE ordinal logistic regression model

Covariates were selected based on prior research, theory, expert knowledge, and the available secondary data. The selection prioritized variables known to influence the outcome of interest and available in the dataset. Multicollinearity was assessed by examining correlations between covariates, and variables with high correlations were excluded to ensure model stability and avoid redundancy. This approach enhanced the model’s interpretability and grounded it in existing literature and theory. Following this, the best-fit model was determined using the Quasi-likelihood under the Independence model Criterion (QIC), facilitating the comparison of QIC values across different local odds ratio structures. As shown in Table 2, the findings revealed that the exchangeable structure outperformed the other models, as it had the lowest QIC value, demonstrating its suitability for the dataset.

Place of residence was also a significant predictor, with children in rural areas having slightly lower odds of being less stunted than their urban counterparts (OR = 0.75, p < 0.05). Additionally, the sex of the children was a strong predictor, with female children having significantly greater odds of being less stunted than male children (OR = 1.53, p < 0.001).

Age in months negatively influenced the odds of being less stunted (OR = 0.97, p < 0.01), indicating that older children were more likely to be stunted.

Socioeconomic factors played a crucial role, with a higher wealth index significantly increasing the odds of children being less stunted (OR = 5.96, p < 0.001). Access to safe drinking water slightly decreased the odds of being less stunted (OR = 0.87, p < 0.05), while access to adequate fuels for cooking significantly decreased the odds (OR = 0.74, p < 0.01). Access to sanitation increase the odds of being less stunted (OR = 1.19, p < 0.01).

Parental education levels were also significant. Children whose mothers had primary education were 31% more likely to be not stunted (OR = 1.31, p < 0.05), while those whose mothers had secondary education or higher were 43% more likely to be not stunted (OR = 1.43, p < 0.1) compared to children of mothers with no education.

The regional differences were notable, with children from Amhara having lower odds of being stunted than those from Tigray (OR = 0.76, p < 0.05). There was no significant difference observed between Oromiya and SNNP. Conversely, children from Addis Ababa had greater odds of being less stunted (OR = 1.53, p < 0.05).

Overall, the findings highlight the importance of temporal trends, place of residence, gender, age, socioeconomic status, access to resources, parental education, and regional differences in influencing child stunting outcomes.

Discussion

The findings of this study align with and extend those of previous research on the determinants of child stunting, providing a comprehensive understanding of the multifaceted influences on this critical health outcome.

The significant improvement in the odds of children being less stunted from 2002 to 2016 mirrors the global progress reported by the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) and Hawkes et al. in reducing stunting rates through improved health policies, economic growth, and targeted nutritional interventions [18]. The results corroborate these global trends and underline the effectiveness of ongoing public health efforts.

This study revealed that children in rural areas are more likely to be stunted than are those in urban areas, a finding consistent with the findings of numerous studies globally. Similar urban-rural disparities in child nutrition outcomes have been attributed to factors such as improved access to healthcare, education, and nutritional resources in urban areas [19]. This finding reinforces the need for targeted interventions in rural settings to bridge the health gap.

The observation that female children are less likely to be stunted than male children has also been documented in other studies. Boys are generally found to be more susceptible to stunting than girls in several developing countries [20]. This difference may be due to biological factors or gender-based care practices that favour female children.

The negative association between age and the likelihood of being less stunted suggests that older children face cumulative nutritional deficits, a finding supported by other research. It has been observed that stunting tends to increase with age, likely due to extended exposure to inadequate nutrition and poor health conditions [21]. This finding underscores the importance of early intervention to prevent stunting from persisting as children grow.

These findings on the positive impact of a higher wealth index on reducing stunting are consistent with the broader literature. Research has shown that economic improvements significantly reduce child stunting rates by enhancing access to better food, healthcare, and living conditions [22]. This highlights the critical role of socioeconomic development in improving child health outcomes.

The significant effects of access to safe drinking water and adequate cooking fuels on reducing stunting align with studies by Biruk et al., who found that improved water and sanitation significantly reduce the prevalence of stunting by preventing waterborne diseases and improving overall health [23]. This finding suggests that improving basic infrastructure can have substantial health benefits.

The positive influence of maternal education on child stunting is well documented. A study by Yorke et al. suggested that mothers with higher education levels experience greater benefit in reducing child malnutrition compared to mothers with lower education levels [24]. This study supports this notion, showing significant reductions in stunting with higher maternal education levels. Similarly, fathers’ education also contributes to better child health, although the impact is less pronounced than that of mothers’ education, which is consistent with a study conducted by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation [25].

The no regional differences observed in this study, but those from Addis Ababa being less likely, reflect findings from regional health assessments in Ethiopia. Significant regional variations in stunting rates have been observed, often associated with differences in socioeconomic conditions and public health infrastructure [26]. This underscores the need for region-specific strategies to address stunting effectively.

In conclusion, this study reaffirms and extends the understanding of various factors influencing child stunting, aligning with global and regional research. The findings emphasize the importance of continuing and enhancing multifaceted interventions, particularly those targeting rural areas, socioeconomic development, access to resources, and parental education, to further reduce stunting rates and improve child health outcomes.

One of the key limitations of our analysis is the inability to incorporate sampling weights into our ordered logistic regression model with GEE. The ordLORgee function from the geepack R package, which we used for this analysis, does not support the inclusion of weights, limiting our ability to adjust for potential sampling biases arising from the survey design. While the geeglm function in the same package allows for the use of weights, it is restricted to binary outcome variables, which is not suitable for our ordinal data structure. As a result, we chose to proceed with the ordLORgee function, as it was the most appropriate method for our data type, even though this meant not accounting for sampling weights in our analysis.

Another limitation of this study relates to the wealth index used as a measure of socio-economic status (SES). The index was reconstructed by including factors such as housing quality and ownership of consumer durables, while excluding access to services like water, sanitation, and electricity to avoid multicollinearity. This approach allowed us to assess the independent effects of these services on stunting, as access to basic services directly influences child health. However, by separating wealth from service access, we may have obscured their interconnectedness, as wealthier households generally have better access to these services. This complicates the interpretation of the independent effects of wealth and service access on stunting outcomes. Lastly, while the wealth index ranges from 0 to 1, with higher scores indicating better SES, it may not capture small but potentially significant wealth differences that could influence stunting outcomes. Although the index is useful for ranking households by SES, it may fail to reflect subtle variations in wealth that could have meaningful impacts on child health.

Conclusions

This study identified key determinants of child stunting in Ethiopia, showing significant improvements from 2002 to 2016. Despite this progress, disparities persist. Rural residence, older age, and lower socioeconomic status increase the risk of stunting, while female children are less likely to be stunted. Higher wealth, parental education (especially maternal education), and access to safe drinking water and adequate cooking fuels reduce stunting odds.

Regional differences highlight that children in Amhara are more prone to stunting, whereas those in Addis Ababa fare better, indicating the need for region-specific strategies. To sustain and accelerate progress, targeted efforts addressing socioeconomic inequalities, improving resource access, and enhancing educational interventions are crucial. Future policies should prioritize these areas to further reduce child stunting and promote healthier growth and development.

To reduce child stunting in Ethiopia, we recommend the following: Enhance rural health and nutrition programs by increasing investment in rural health infrastructure and services. Early childhood interventions to improve nutrition and healthcare from birth to age five should be prioritized. Socioeconomic inequalities should be addressed through strengthened social protection programs and the promotion of income-generating activities for low-income families. Community-based infrastructure projects can improve access to safe drinking water and adequate cooking fuels.

Promote parental education, especially for mothers, by expanding educational opportunities and integrating nutrition and health education into school curricula. To develop region-specific strategies to address unique challenges, particularly in high-stunting areas such as Amhara. Gender-sensitive approaches should be implemented to ensure equal care and nutritional support for male and female children. Monitoring and evaluation systems should be strengthened to track progress and refine interventions continuously. Public awareness should be increased through campaigns and local collaborations to educate communities on the importance of nutrition, hygiene, and healthcare in preventing stunting. These targeted actions can effectively address the multifaceted factors influencing child stunting and promoting healthier growth and development for children in Ethiopia.
