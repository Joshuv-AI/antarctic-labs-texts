The impact of EMS transport models on pediatric life-saving interventions and outcomes in emergency care: a prospective observational study based on the REPEM survey
Authors: Caner Turan, Eylem Ulas Saz, Ali Yurtseven, Oihane Morientes Carbajo, Danilo Buonsenso, Ahmed Soliman, Sevcan Bilen, Sevcan Bilen, Gamze Gokulu, Pinar Cay, Hayri Levent Yilmaz, Mehmet Adnan Ozturk, Murat Dogan, Utku Ozer, Tugce Kalin, Gamze Gokalp, Sule Demir, Candemir Karacan, Nilden Tuygun, Ahmet Birbilen, Ozlem Teksam, Anil Er, Aykut Caglar, Nihan Sik, Murat Duman, Ramazan Gurlu, Nilgun Erkek, Aytac Goktug, Ihsan Ozdemir, Alkan Bal, Halise Akca, Esra Ture

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric patients requiring life-sustaining interventions (LSIs) constitute a critically ill population characterized by a high risk of rapid clinical deterioration, necessitating precise and timely medical interventions. Emergency Medical Services (EMS) transport strategies vary, with the Franco-German (FG) (physician-led) “stay and play” model emphasizing advanced prehospital care and the Anglo-American (AA) (paramedic-led) “scoop and run” approach prioritizing rapid transport. This study evaluates the influence of transport modality on prehospital LSIs, clinical outcomes, and the personnel performing these interventions.MethodsThis prospective, multicenter, observational study included 16 emergency departments (EDs) across four European countries over a four-week period. Children (≤ 18 years) who received LSIs in the ED were included. Patients were stratified by transport modality: FG-EMS (physician-led) and AA-EMS (paramedic-led). The study analyzed the rate and type of LSIs performed, the personnel administering interventions, prehospital transport times, and clinical outcomes including pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission and mortality.ResultsA total of 133,634 patients visited during the study period, of whom 12,087 (9.0%) were transported via EMS. Among these, 4.7% (n = 631) received LSIs in the ED and were included in the study. The median daily ED visits were 136 (IQR:110–251), with 8 ambulance arrivals (IQR:5–12). Most LSIs were performed on patients transported by parents (56.7%), while 43.3% were EMS transports. Among EMS transports, 18.6% utilized the physician-led (FG) model, and 81.4% used the paramedic-led (AA) model. LSIs during transport occurred in 19.4% of EMS patients, significantly more in FG than AA (43.1 vs. 13.9%) (p < 0.001). No invasive procedures were performed in AA transports, which had shorter transport times compared to FG (median 20 vs. 31 min) (p = 0.001). Most patients requiring LSIs presented with medical emergencies (75.1%). FG transports had higher PICU admission (41.2% vs. 21.6%) and mortality rates (9.8% vs. 1.8%) (p = 0.004 and p = 0.012, respectively).ConclusionThe transport modality significantly influenced the pre-hospital management and clinical outcomes of children requiring LSIs. FG transports were associated with more comprehensive pre-hospital interventions but longer transport times and higher PICU admission and mortality rates. These findings highlight the need for optimizing pre-hospital care and transport protocols to improve outcomes in critically ill children.

Introduction

Pediatric patients requiring life-sustaining interventions (LSIs) represent a high-risk population with unique physiological and clinical challenges that necessitate timely and precise prehospital care [1, 2]. Emergency Medical Services (EMS) systems vary in their approach to prehospital management, with the Anglo-American (AA) (paramedic-led) “scoop and run” model focusing on rapid hospital transport and the Franco-German (FG) (physician-led) “stay and play” modelemphasizing on-scene stabilization by physicians [3, 4]. While both systems have been studied extensively in adult trauma populations, their comparative effectiveness in pediatric emergency care remains unclear [5, 6].

Current research has primarily examined EMS usage patterns, interventions performed, and outcomes in adult populations, with limited focus on pediatric patients [7, 8]. Additionally, previous pediatric EMS studies have primarily compared EMS-transported patients to non-EMS patients rather than evaluating the differences between EMS transport models themselves [9, 10]. Given the distinct physiological responses and emergency care needs of children, it is essential to determine whether the choice of EMS model affects the frequency of prehospital LSIs, transport times, and clinical outcomes, including mortality and pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admissions [11, 12]. This is particularly important as studies indicate that pre-hospital interventions may significantly impact survival and neurological outcomes in critically ill pediatric patients [13, 14].

To address this knowledge gap, this study provides the first multicenter, prospective observational comparison of the AA and FG EMS models in pediatric emergency transport across multiple European countries. By evaluating the prevalence of LSIs performed in each EMS model, the personnel administering these interventions, and the associated clinical outcomes, this study aims to inform EMS policy and training programs. Understanding these differences is crucial for optimizing EMS protocols, ensuring appropriate resource allocation, and improving pediatric emergency care strategies. Our findings will contribute to the growing field of pediatric emergency medicine by identifying best practices for prehospital interventions and transport strategies, ultimately improving survival and long-term patient outcomes.

This study was designed with the following prespecified hypotheses: (1) The FG-EMS model will be associated with a higher rate of prehospital LSIs compared to the AA-EMS model, due to physician-led advanced interventions at the scene, (2) Transport times will be longer in the FG-EMS model because of the emphasis on on-site stabilization, whereas the AA-EMS model will prioritize faster transport to the emergency department (ED), (3) PICU admission rates will be higher for children transported via the FG-EMS model, as early interventions may identify more severe cases requiring intensive care, (4) Mortality rates will be higher in the FG-EMS model due to its frequent utilization in critically ill patients requiring immediate physician-led care, (5) The total number of LSIs performed in the ED will not differ significantly between the two models, as the AA-EMS model compensates for fewer prehospital interventions with increased procedures upon arrival at the hospital. These hypotheses guide the study’s investigation of how different EMS transport models influence pediatric prehospital care, LSIs, and patient outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting and participants

This prospective, multicenter, observational study included 16 ED sites from 4 European countries (Table 1). Participants were recruited through the Pediatric Emergency Medicine (PEM) section of the European Society for Emergency Medicine (EUSEM) and the Research in European Pediatric Emergency Medicine (REPEM) Network.

Participating centers and emergency department characteristics

In this multi-center study, conducted across 16 pediatric EDs (13 of the 16 participating emergency departments were located in Turkey, while Spain, Italy, and the United Kingdom each contributed one center), all patients requiring or undergoing LSI over a 4-week period were systematically evaluated prospectively (Fig. 1). Data collection was performed by attending physicians (pediatric emergency physician or emergency physician or pediatrician) using standardized case report forms distributed via Google Drive to ensure uniformity and precision. These forms were reviewed, verified, and finalized by the responsible physicians before being compiled. All data recorded day by day from the centers over a 4-week period were subsequently centralized and submitted to the principal investigator.

Demographic data of the patients (age, gender), the daily number of patients and ambulances admitted, modes of transportation to the ED (AA-EMS and FG-EMS models of ambulance), the rates and types of LSIs performed in the ED and ambulances, and the personnel administering these interventions [physician, nurse, emergency medical technician (EMT), Advanced Emergency Care Technician (AECT)] were analyzed. Additionally, patient outcomes, including mortality and PICU admission rates, and the effects of LSIs in the transport were assessed.

The sample size calculation was conducted to ensure sufficient power to detect statistically significant differences in the primary outcome, which is the mortality rate of critically ill pediatric patients transported by the two EMS models. Based on a previously conducted multi-center pilot study, we estimated that the mortality rate would differ by 10% between the two groups (7). Using G*Power analysis software (version 3.1; Düsseldorf, Denmark), we performed a two-tailed test for proportions with the following parameters; 80% power, 0.10 effect size (based on expected differences in mortality rates from pilot data), Significance level (α) = 0.05 and Allocation ratio: 1:1. This calculation indicated that a minimum sample size of 150 patients was required to detect a statistically significant difference in mortality between the two EMS models with 95% confidence and a margin of error of ± 5%.

Definition and interventions

LSI was defined as cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), endotracheal tube placement, non-invasive positive pressure application (CPAP/BiPAP), electrical defibrillation or cardioversion, transcutaneous pacemakers, thoracentesis, pericardial puncture, central venous catheter placement, peripheral arterial line placement, massive fluid replacement (≥ 40 ml/kg), massive blood transfusions, and vasopressor therapy such as dopamin or adrenaline or noradrenaline.

Data source characteristics and quality

To ensure data accuracy, completeness, and consistency, we implemented the following quality assurance procedures: (1) Standardized Data Collection Protocol, (a) a predefined case report form (CRF) was developed, including precise definitions for each variable to ensure consistency across all study sites, (b) all participating centers were trained in uniform data collection procedures to reduce variability in data abstraction; (2) Real-Time Data Entry and Verification, (a) data were collected at the time of patient arrival and intervention, minimizing recall bias, (b) a centralized data monitoring team reviewed all submissions daily for inconsistencies, missing data, and potential errors, (c) any ambiguous or missing entries were flagged and clarified immediately with the reporting physician; (3) Interobserver Agreement and Validation, (a) a subset (10%) of cases was randomly selected for independent review by a second investigator to assess interobserver reliability, (b) discrepancies in data entry were resolved through consensus discussions with senior investigators; (4) Minimization of Reporting Bias, (a) to reduce subjectivity, only documented clinical interventions and outcomes were included in the dataset, (b) all LSIs were defined based on international pediatric emergency medicine guidelines, ensuring objective classification; (5) Quality Control Across Study Sites, (a) regular data audits were conducted across all 16 sites to ensure compliance with the standardized protocol, (b) any deviations from the protocol were addressed through retraining sessions with data collectors.

Ethics and human subjects

General approval was obtained from the Ege University Medical Research Ethics Committee (Date: 05.07.2017, No:17 − 2/14). Each institution participating in the study obtained approval from their own institutional review boards/ethics committees and it was determined whether informed consent was required by the participants. Informed consent was obtained from parents and, when necessary, from children. The procedures were by the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional or regional) and the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (version 25.0; Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) was used for data analysis. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check data for normality before statistical analysis. The continuous variables were given as mean ± standard deviation, median [interquartile range (IQR), min-max] and the categorical variables were given as number and percentage. Mann–Whitney U and X2 tests were used for comparison. Multivariate and univariate logistic regression analyzes were used. Two-sided tests at α = 0.05 level were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 133.634 patients visited 16 participant EDs during the study period (Fig. 1). Among the pediatric ED admissions, 12.087 patients (9.0%) were transported via EMS, and 4.9% (n = 652) of them received LSI in the ED. During the study period, 17 patients were excluded due to missing data (such as characteristic features, unreported LSIs, or outcomes), and 4 patients were excluded because their data were either unsuitable for the study or contained errors. Overall, 631 (4.7%) patients were included in the study (Fig. 1). The demographic characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 2. The median daily number of patient visits and ambulance arrivals in pediatric EDs was 136 (minimum 38 – maximum 1057, IQR: 110–251) and 8 (minimum 0 – maximum 27, IQR: 5–12), respectively (Table 2).

More than half (56.7%) of the patients who received LSI were transported to the ED by their parents; while 43.3% were admitted via EMS (Table 2). The median duration of arrival at the ED was 25 min (IQR 15–40 min). Furthermore, it was noted that the majority of patients (85.7%) were assessed by a pediatric emergency physician (Table 2).

Among the patients transported to the ED by EMS, 18.6% (n = 51) were accompanied by a physician (FG-EMS model) whereas 81.4% (n = 222) were accompanied by an AECT, paramedic, and/or registered nurse (AA-EMS model) (Table 2). Only 19.4% (n = 53) of patients who required such interventions and were transported to the ED by EMS underwent LSIs during their transport. The frequency of LSIs was significantly higher in FG models than in AA models (43.1% vs. 13.9%, respectively) (Table 3) (p < 0.001). Moreover, the frequency of immediate LSI procedures, pharmacological interventions, and hemodynamic LSI administration during transport was significantly higher in the FG modality compared to the AA modality (Table 3) (both, p < 0.001). It is noteworthy that no invasive procedures, such as endotracheal intubation and intraosseous access, were performed during the AA transport modality (Table 3). Additionally, the time required to reach the ED was significantly longer for EMS teams accompanied by a physician in comparison to those without a physician (median, 31 min vs. 20 min, respectively) (p = 0.001).

The majority of patients who received LSI in the ED were medical emergencies (75.1%), while 14.7% (n = 93) were life-threatening emergencies, 6.3% (n = 40) were trauma and 3.8% (n = 24) were surgical patients (Table 4). More than one-third of these patients (34.1%) received a life-saving procedure in the ED; the most frequently procedures were bag-valve-mask (BMV) (11.7%), endotracheal intubation (EI) (9.6%), intraosseous (IO) (7.7%) and chest compression (CC) (4.3%), respectively (Table 4).

Discussion

This study provides the first direct comparison of the Anglo-American (AA) and Franco-German (FG) EMS models in pediatric emergency transport. Also, it found that the FG “stay and play” EMS model was associated with significantly higher rates of prehospital LSIs but also with longer transport times when compared to the AA “scoop and run” model. Furthermore, patients transported via FG-EMS had significantly higher PICU admission and mortality rates than those transported via AA-EMS. While these findings align with existing assumptions about EMS transport models, our study provides empirical evidence quantifying these differences specifically within a pediatric population, an area that remains underexplored in emergency medicine research. The findings could inform EMS protocol development, refine pediatric emergency training programs, and guide policy decisions regarding the optimal prehospital care approach for critically ill children.

Prior studies on EMS transport models have primarily focused on adult trauma populations, where rapid transport is often associated with improved survival outcomes [13, 14]. However, pediatric emergency care presents unique challenges, as the physiological responses to trauma and critical illness differ from those in adults. While prior research has compared EMS-transported pediatric patients to non-EMS transports, no large-scale, multicenter study has directly examined the impact of EMS transport modality (FG vs. AA) on prehospital LSIs and pediatric outcomes [6, 8]. Our study addresses this gap by demonstrating that although FG-EMS results in a higher rate of prehospital interventions, this does not necessarily correlate with better survival outcomes.

It is of paramount importance that EMS personnel receive adequate training and are equipped with the appropriate resources to provide care to pediatric patients. WHO emphasizes that effective EMS systems are integral to healthcare, particularly in managing critically ill children [15]. The study further substantiates this conclusion by revealing that a significant proportion of patients transported by EMS required LSIs; however, only 19.4% of these interventions were performed during the transport phase.

Our results highlight the critical trade-off between prehospital interventions and transport time in pediatric EMS. While the FG-EMS model emphasizes on-scene stabilization, the longer transport times observed in our study may negatively impact overall patient outcomes. This finding suggests that delays in definitive hospital care may outweigh the benefits of additional prehospital interventions in some critically ill pediatric patients. These findings underscore the importance of balancing rapid transport with necessary prehospital care, particularly in time-sensitive conditions such as respiratory failure and circulatory shock. The literature supports the notion that timely and appropriate interventions are crucial for improving survival rates in pediatric emergencies [16, 17]. The lack of such interventions in the AA-EMS model could potentially contribute to poorer outcomes, as indicated by the higher mortality rates observed in patients transported via this model.

The demographic characteristics of the present study reveal that a significant proportion (56.7%) of those requiring LSIs were transported by their parents, highlighting a potential gap in EMS utilization for pediatric emergencies. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies which have indicated that a significant proportion of parents may choose to use private transportation for their children due to a perceived inadequacy of EMS or a lack of awareness regarding the urgency of their child’s condition [18–20]. This result has significant implications, indicating that parent-focused educational initiatives could enhance EMS utilization rates, thereby improving the timely and appropriate care of critically ill children.

Furthermore, the study’s results indicate that patients transported by EMS had higher rates of admission to the PICU compared to those brought in by parents (41.2% vs. 23.7%) (p = 0.008). This finding reinforces the critical role of EMS in managing pediatric emergencies and suggests that EMS transport may be associated with more severe cases requiring intensive care [21].

These findings have important implications for EMS system optimization. One potential approach is the development of hybrid EMS models that incorporate targeted prehospital interventions without significantly delaying transport. EMS training programs should prioritize interventions with the highest clinical impact while minimizing unnecessary on-scene delays [20–22]. Additionally, further integration of pediatric-specific training for EMS providers could help optimize decision-making in the field, ensuring that transport strategies are tailored to the unique needs of critically ill children [1].

The generalizability of our findings is influenced by several factors. This study was conducted across 16 EDs in four European countries, providing a diverse and multicenter dataset that enhances external validity. By including both tertiary academic centers and regional hospitals, our findings reflect a broad range of EMS transport settings, making them more applicable to well-developed EMS systems in high-resource settings. However, the results may not be directly generalizable to EMS systems outside of Europe, particularly in low-resource settings where prehospital care infrastructure, EMS staffing, and access to pediatric emergency servicesdiffer significantly. FG and AA EMS models represent two distinct approaches to emergency transport, but hybrid or alternative EMS systems in other regions may not fit neatly into these categories.

Additionally, variability in EMS protocols, prehospital intervention guidelines, and hospital admission criteria across study sites could limit direct applicability to other healthcare systems. Differences in EMS response times, availability of physician-led teams, and training levels of paramedics may also influence how these findings translate to other regions.

Another important consideration is the potential influence of dispatch practices on the type of EMS team responding to each call. In several EMS systems, physician-led FG teams are preferentially dispatched when the initial call description suggests a high-acuity or life-threatening emergency, whereas AA units may respond to a broader spectrum of lower- to moderate-acuity cases. This selective dispatching may have resulted in a disproportionate number of critically ill children being attended by FG teams, which could partly explain the higher rates of prehospital interventions, PICU admissions, and mortality observed in this group. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with the understanding that dispatch algorithms themselves may have acted as an intrinsic source of confounding.

Despite these limitations, the fundamental question of balancing prehospital interventions with transport efficiencyis relevant across all EMS systems. Our findings provide empirical evidence that can inform global discussions on optimizing pediatric EMS care, particularly in regions considering modifications to existing transport strategies. Future studies should evaluate EMS transport models in broader geographic and socioeconomic contexts to further strengthen external validity.

This study has several limitations: (1) Selection bias may be present as hospitals with well-established EMS systems were included, potentially limiting generalizability. Additionally, sicker patients may have been more frequently transported via FG-EMS, possibly overestimating its association with higher mortality and PICU admissions. While we included diverse hospitals and standardized inclusion criteria, future studies should adjust for illness severity using propensity score matching. (2) The geographical imbalance (13 vs. 1, 1, 1 ED centers) may limit generalizability and may reflect differences in national pediatric EMS structures and research engagement. (3) Unmeasured confounders such as comorbidities, EMS provider experience, and hospital-level variability may have influenced the results. Although we used standardized data collection, the lack of detailed severity scores limits our ability to fully control for confounding. (4) Information bias due to variability in documentation may have led to misclassification bias. We minimized this through real-time data entry, centralized verification, and interobserver reliability checks. (5) Hospital variability in PICU admission thresholds and EMS protocols could have influenced outcomes. Some hospitals may have a lower threshold for PICU admissions, skewing comparisons. We mitigated this by analyzing standardized transport times and intervention rates rather than relying solely on admissions.

Future studies should focus on severity-adjusted analyses to better control for patient complexity using detailed illness severity scores and propensity score matching. Expanding research to different geographic regions and EMS systems, including low-resource settings, would improve understanding of transport strategies globally. Further investigation into hybrid EMS models that combine elements of both FG and AA systems could help optimize prehospital care. Additionally, research on pediatric-specific EMS training and protocol modifications, such as balancing on-scene interventions with transport efficiency, is needed to guide improvements in pediatric emergency care.

This study has several strengths. First, it is the first multicenter, prospective study to directly compare the AA and FG EMS transport models in pediatric emergency care, addressing a significant gap in the literature. Second, the large sample size across 16 hospitals in four European countries enhances external validity and allows for meaningful comparisons between EMS models. Third, real-time data collection using standardized case report forms reduced recall bias and ensured consistency in data reporting. Fourth, the inclusion of objective transport time measurements, prehospital interventions, and clinical outcomes provides a comprehensive analysis of the impact of EMS transport models on pediatric patients requiring life-saving interventions. Finally, our findings offer practical insights for optimizing EMS protocols and training, supporting evidence-based improvements in pediatric emergency transport strategies.

Conclusions

The current study provides the first large-scale comparative analysis of the Anglo-American (AA) and Franco-German (FG) EMS transport models in pediatric emergency care. The findings emphasize that while the FG-EMS model is associated with more prehospital interventions, the associated delays in transport may lead to increased PICU admission and mortality rates. These results highlight the need for further research into the optimal balance between prehospital stabilization and timely hospital transport, ensuring that pediatric EMS protocols are evidence-based and designed to maximize survival outcomes.
