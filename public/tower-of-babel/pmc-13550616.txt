Immune niche composed of C1Q+ macrophages/SFRP2+ CAFs/CD8+ T cells drives immunotherapy response in HER2-positive gastric cancer
Authors: Yuhan Liao, Xinhua Chen, Haiyun Chen, Lunbo Xie, Yunfei Zhi, Hao Xu, Xinghua Zhuo, Shupeng Hu, Liping Zhao, Chuang Lin, Yaoying Li, Liang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression is a distinctive feature of a subgroup of gastric cancer (GC) but the underpinning characteristics of the immune microenvironment and mechanisms remain unclear. In the study, spatial transcriptomics and single-cell RNA sequencing are used on treatment-naïve HER2-positive and HER2-negative GC specimens to characterize the tumor microenvironment and spatial architecture with the aim of defining how HER2 status shapes the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and its cellular interactions. We find that C1Q⁺ macrophages spatially and functionally interact with CD8⁺ T cells via Galectin-9 (LGALS9), which is downregulated in HER2-positive tumors. Knocking down LGALS9 enhancesCD8⁺ T cell function and suppresses tumor growth in a CD8⁺ T cell-dependent manner, further amplified by anti-PD-1 treatment. Conversely, SFRP2⁺ cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) promote CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion through CXCL12-CXCR4 signaling, and knocking down CXCL12 reverses this immunosuppressive effect. HER2-positive tumors are characterized by an immune-favorable niche enriched in C1Q⁺ macrophages and CD8⁺ T effector memory cells but a decrease in SFRP2⁺ CAFs, suggesting its potential as a predictive biomarker for response to combined anti-PD-1 and anti-HER2 therapies. These findings provide insight into the spatial and immune landscape of HER2-associated GC and may inform future precision treatment strategies.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) constitutes a major global health burden, predominantly in East Asian nations1,2. In 2020, GC recorded over one million new cases and caused approximately 768,000 deaths globally, making it the fifth most common cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide3,4. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) represents a pivotal therapeutic target in GC5, with an estimated positivity rate ranging from 15 to 20% among affected patients. HER2-positive GC exhibits high malignancy and poses major challenges due to variable responses and complex resistance mechanisms to targeted therapies such as trastuzumab, highlighting the need for further investigation into its molecular and immune features6–8.

Recently, the addition of immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) to HER2-targeted regimens has shown promising results9–12. In the phase III KEYNOTE-811 trial, first-line pembrolizumab combined with trastuzumab and chemotherapy significantly enhanced objective response rates in metastatic HER2-positive gastric and gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma compared with trastuzumab and chemotherapy alone. The treatment response indicates that both the immune microenvironment and spatial heterogeneity of HER2-associated GC may play a combined role in shaping therapeutic outcomes. However, its underlying mechanism is still unclear. Specifically, HER2 overexpression has been shown to influence immune signaling and cytokine networks in cancer cells, which can, in turn, modulate immune cell recruitment and function independent of antibody treatment. HER2 signaling promotes the secretion of chemokines such as CCL2, which recruit monocytes and drive their polarization into tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Consistently, immunosuppressive TAM subsets are enriched in HER2-positive GC and are associated with poor prognosis13. In addition, HER2 suppresses STING expression through the AKT pathway, leading to reduced CD8+ effector T-cell infiltration and impaired activation through modulation of immune checkpoint molecules14. These intrinsic effects of HER2 amplification on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) provide the rationale for our focus. In particular, how HER2 signaling shapes the TIME and its cellular interactions remains poorly understood.

The limited availability of treatment-naïve tissue from HER2-positive GC underscores the value of such samples, further constrained by the need for specimens of sufficient size, typically at least that of a mung bean, for accurate HER2 assessment. Early-stage tumors often provide inadequate material, while advanced cases commonly receive neoadjuvant therapy before resection, restricting access to untreated tissue. Overcoming this challenge, we applied spatial transcriptomics (ST) and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to treatment-naïve HER2-positive and -negative GC samples to delineate the spatial immune landscape. Using these complementary approaches, we sought to define how the immune microenvironment in HER2-positive GC is organized and how its cellular interactions may influence response to PD-1-based therapy.

In this study, by integrating ST and scRNA-seq data from treatment-naïve HER2-positive and -negative GC samples, we identify a distinct immune niche characterized by macrophages, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) and CD8+ T cells. The spatial configuration of these subpopulations is closely associated with responsiveness to PD-1 blockade combined with anti-HER2 therapy. These findings offer a refined molecular and spatial understanding of HER2-positive GC and provide a potential predictive biomarker for immunotherapy efficacy, laying the groundwork for precision treatment strategies.

Results

ST and scRNA-seq analyses revealed the spatial heterogeneity and immune microenvironment in HER2-positive GC

To elucidate the heterogeneity of HER2-associated GC, we analyzed a total of 385 treatment-naïve HER2-negative GC cases, 63 treatment-naïve HER2-positive GC cases, and 45 post-immunotherapy cases across internal and external cohorts.

For discovery analyses, we generated an internal GC cohort comprising scRNA-seq and ST data from eight treatment-naïve patients (Supplementary Table 1). Based on HER2 expression, the samples were categorized into HER2-positive (scRNA-seq, n = 4; ST, n = 3) and HER2-negative groups (scRNA-seq, n = 4; ST, n = 3). For internal scRNA-seq, 62,669 high-quality cells were retained from a total of 77,166 cells across eight samples after quality control. For ST, 240,648 spots (bin 50) were captured across six tumor samples. Following clustering and annotation of the scRNA-seq data, a total of two epithelial cell subpopulations, six immune cell subpopulations (including T and NK, myeloid, B and plasma) and three stromal cell subpopulations (including endothelial, fibroblasts and pericytes) were identified (Fig. 1B, C). Next, the result of differential abundance analysis showed that HER2-positive group exhibited a higher proportion of tumor cells, with marked differences in T and NK, myeloid, and fibroblasts within the tumor microenvironment (TME) (Fig. 1D, F, G). Subsequently, pathway enrichment analysis was performed for major cell types. In the HER2-positive group, stromal cell subpopulations exhibited reduced activity in extracellular matrix (ECM) receptor interaction and angiogenesis pathways, accompanied by upregulation of genes associated with general inflammatory processes. Myeloid cell subpopulations also showed increased expression of immune- and inflammation-related genes, reflecting a heightened inflammatory transcriptional state, without implying a strictly pro- or anti-tumor function (Fig. 1E). To further explore spatial heterogeneity between HER2-positive and HER2-negative GC, ST data were analyzed using the STEP workflow15. Using an algorithm that integrates spatial expression profiles of highly variable genes to define spatial organizational domains, the tissue architecture was resolved into eight distinct domains, each characterized by a unique cellular composition. Using CARD (version 1.1), a deconvolution approach that integrates single-cell annotations with spatial transcriptomic data, we mapped the spatial distribution of cell types across tissue sections and characterized the cellular composition within each domain (Fig. 1H, I, Supplementary Figs. 1, 2 and Supplementary Data 2).

For external scRNA-seq data, 212,569 high-quality cells were retained across 34 treatment naive GC samples after quality control. These samples were assembled from multiple publicly available datasets. Ten tumor samples (GC01-GC10) were obtained from OMIX001073, which included only patients who had received no chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or drug treatment prior to resection. Three untreated samples (GSM5004180-GSM5004182) were retrieved from GSE163558, and one additional treatment-naïve sample (GSM5580154) from GSE184198. To further expand the cohort, we leveraged GSE268238, an integrated dataset comprising GSE201347 and GSE254188, both of which exclusively enrolled patients without neoadjuvant or prior therapy; based on metadata from these two studies, 20 treatment-naïve samples were extracted from the provided rds file. In total, these sources yielded 34 untreated primary GC samples for downstream analysis.

We next stratified the external untreated primary GC scRNA-seq datasets into HER2-positive and HER2-negative groups. In the internal cohort, epithelial (Epi) cells were extracted and subjected to clustering (Supplementary Fig. 3A), and subsequently stratified into HER2-positive and HER2-negative groups based on clinical annotations (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Differential expression analysis of Epi cells between the two groups was performed, and HER2 scores were generated using marker genes enriched in the HER2-positive group (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Comparison of HER2 scores between internal HER2-positive and HER2-negative tumors (Supplementary Fig. 3D) confirmed the validity of this scoring approach. We next integrated the external scRNA-seq datasets from primary GC samples. After batch correction, cells were jointly embedded for dimensionality reduction, followed by unsupervised clustering and lineage-based cell-type annotation (Supplementary Fig. 4). In the external cohort, epithelial cells were extracted and clustered (Supplementary Fig. 5A). HER2 scoring based on internal-cohort HER2 signatures was then applied to identify putative HER2-positive epithelial clusters (Supplementary Fig. 5B–D). For each single-cell sample, those with >50% HER2-positive epithelial clusters were classified as predicted HER2-positive cases (Supplementary Fig. 5E).

We next incorporated bulk RNA-seq data from the TCGA-STAD primary cohort. mRNA expression matrices and copy-number variation (CNV) profiles were obtained from cBioPortal, and samples were classified as HER2-positive (CNV ≥ 2, n = 54) or HER2-negative (CNV < 2, n = 352). HER2-positive tumors exhibited markedly elevated ERBB2 CNV together with significantly increased ERBB2 mRNA expression, confirming the reliability of this CNV-based HER2 stratification (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Using the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm, which predicts immune-checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) responsiveness based on RNA-seq profiles, we observed that the proportion of predicted ICI responders was substantially higher in the HER2-positive group compared with the HER2-negative group. This pattern is consistent with prior reports highlighting enhanced immunotherapy sensitivity in HER2-positive GC (Supplementary Fig. 6B, C).

Subpopulation analysis of T/NK cells and B cells reveals immune differentiation differences between HER2-positive and HER2-negative GC

We extracted all T and NK cells for subpopulation analysis. Initially, T and NK cells were categorized into several subsets, including CD4, CD8, NK, Proliferation, and ILC. CD4 and CD8 subsets were further divided into more specific categories (Fig. 2A, B and Supplementary Figs. 7, 8). We next performed differential abundance analysis, which revealed a significant increase in CD8+ T cells in the HER2-positive group compared with HER2-negative group. Further subpopulation analysis indicated that in HER2-positive samples, CD4+ naïve T and central memory T (Tcm) cells were markedly reduced, while CD4+ exhausted T (CD4 Tex) cells were significantly increased in the HER2-positive group. Among CD8⁺ T-cell subsets, CD8+ effector memory (CD8+ Tem) cells were markedly enriched in the HER2-positive group (Fig. 2C, D), a finding further validated in the external post-immunotherapy bulk (Supplementary Fig. 6G) and primary scRNA-seq GC cohort (Supplementary Fig. 9). In addition, pseudotime analysis validated the consistency between T cell annotation and their differentiation trajectories (Fig. 2K). Characterization of myeloid cell subpopulations and functional crosstalk between C1Q+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells in GC

TAMs represent one of the main tumor-infiltrating immune cell types16. We comprehensively characterized the myeloid cell landscape in GC. Myeloid cells were initially classified into three major subpopulations: monocyte-macrophages, dendritic cells, and granulocytes. Among them, the monocyte-macrophage compartment was further subdivided into one monocyte subset (FCN1+) and TAM subsets (C1QC+, SPP1+, and MKI67+) (Fig. 3A, B and Supplementary Figs. 11, 12). To assess how these subsets vary with HER2 status, we compared their abundance between HER2-positive and HER2-negative tumors. Monocytes and C1Q+ macrophages were markedly increased in HER2-positive GC (Fig. 3C, D), a pattern consistently validated in the external post-immunotherapy bulk (Supplementary Fig. 6F) and primary scRNA-seq GC cohort (Supplementary Fig. 13B–E). C1QC encodes one of the three polypeptide chains (C1QA, C1QB and C1QC) that form the C1q complex, and single-cell studies have identified C1QC-expressing TAMs with antigen-presentation and T-cell-interactive programs, supporting a role for C1q+ macrophages in promoting CD8+ T-cell responses17. Pseudotime analysis indicated a potential differentiation relationship between monocytes and tissue-resident macrophages (TRMs) (Fig. 3E). To explore potential interactions between myeloid cells and T cells, we performed cell–cell communication analysis using CellChat. The analysis predicted an LGALS9 (Galectin-9) ligand–receptor interaction between C1Q+ macrophages and CD8 T cells, which appeared weaker in the HER2-positive group compared to the HER2-negative group (Fig. 3F, Supplementary Fig. 13A, F and Supplementary Data 1). To validate this finding, MFC cells were co-cultured with bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) at a 1:1 ratio for 48 h, followed by flow cytometry analysis. Compared to the NC MFC cell group, co-culture with HER2-overexpressing MFC cells increased the proportion of C1Q+ macrophages. (Fig. 3G, H and Supplementary Fig. 14A). Finally, we corroborated these results in situ by imaging tissue sections. mIF showed that the HER2-positive group exhibited a higher abundance of C1Q+ macrophages compared to the HER2-negative group (Fig. 3I, J), which confirmed the scRNA-seq results. MDK-mediated regulation of C1Q+ macrophages and SFRP2+ CAFs by HER2-positive GC cells

To gain further insight into how HER2-positive tumors modulate the proportions of C1Q+ macrophages and SFRP2+ CAFs, we next analyzed cell-cell communication networks using CellChat, aiming to uncover shared pathways through which HER2-positive GC cells may modulate the abundance of these two key microenvironmental populations. CellChat analysis revealed that Epi cells from HER2-positive samples exhibited both a higher number and greater strength of interactions with C1Q+ macrophages and SFRP2+ CAFs (Fig. 5A–D). To further characterize these interactions, we identified the specific ligand–receptor pairs mediating Epi-derived signaling toward C1Q+ macrophages and SFRP2+ CAFs in HER2-positive versus HER2-negative samples. As shown in Fig. 5E, F and Supplementary Data 1, both C1Q+ macrophages and SFRP2+ CAFs shared common epithelial ligands, including MIF and MDK. We next examined the expression of the corresponding receptors for MIF and MDK in C1Q+ macrophages and SFRP2+ CAFs from HER2-positive and HER2-negative samples. The MIF receptors CD44, CD74, and CXCR4 were expressed at lower levels in C1Q+ macrophages from the HER2-positive group compared with the HER2-negative group. In contrast, among the MDK receptors, ITGA4 and ITGB1 were markedly upregulated in C1Q+ macrophages from HER2-positive samples (Fig. 5G and Supplementary Data 1). Moreover, SDC2 showed the highest expression among the MDK receptors and exhibited a trend toward higher expression in SFRP2+ CAFs from HER2-positive samples (Fig. 5H and Supplementary Data 1). Therefore, we further validated the regulatory roles of the shared epithelial ligand MDK and its downstream ligand–receptor pairs—MDK-ITGA4_ITGB1 in modulating the proportion of C1Q+ macrophages and MDK-SDC2 in influencing the proportion of SFRP2+ CAFs—in HER2-positive tumors. Flow cytometry analysis of co-cultures of PBMC-derived macrophages from healthy donors with GC cells derived from HER2-negative or HER2-positive patients revealed that HER2-positive GC cells increased both the proportion of C1Q+ macrophages and the fraction of ITGA4+ITGB1+ cells within C1Q+ macrophages, compared with HER2-negative GC cells. Treatment with an MDK neutralizing antibody (α-MDK nAb) reversed these effects, and the addition of recombinant MDK (rMDK) restored them to levels comparable to co-culture with HER2-positive GC cells alone (Fig. 5I). Conversely, compared with GC cells derived from HER2-negative patients, HER2-positive GC cells co-cultured with CAFs isolated from gastric tumors of independent patients reduced the proportion of SFRP2+ CAFs while increasing the fraction of SDC2+ cells within SFRP2+ CAFs. Treatment with α-MDK neutralizing antibody restored both the proportion of SFRP2+ CAFs and SDC2 expression, whereas the addition of recombinant MDK reversed these effects to levels observed in co-culture with HER2-positive GC cells alone. Previous studies have shown that SFRP2 functions as a Wnt pathway antagonist18, and MDK has been reported to activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling in several cancer contexts19. Therefore, it is plausible that MDK may indirectly suppress SFRP2 expression through activation of the Wnt pathway, thereby limiting the differentiation of CAFs toward the SFRP2+ phenotype. Consistently, the addition of anti-Wnt3a neutralizing antibody reverted the suppression of the SFRP2+ CAF phenotype induced by HER2-positive GC cells (Fig. 5J). To further explore why MDK expression is elevated in HER2-positive tumors, we examined the association between MDK protein levels and proteins related to the canonical downstream signaling pathways of HER2 using the STAD proteomic cohort. MDK protein expression showed positive correlations with proteins associated with the ERK, AKT, and JAK-STAT pathways, with the strongest association observed for the ERK pathway (Supplementary Fig. 20A). To further validate this relationship, HER2 was overexpressed in MFC cells, which resulted in increased MDK expression together with elevated pERK2/ERK2 levels as detected by Western blotting. Notably, treatment with the ERK inhibitor SCH772984 reversed these changes (Supplementary Fig. 20B). These results suggest that HER2 signaling may promote MDK expression, at least in part, through activation of the ERK pathway. Western blot analysis of tumor tissues showed increased MDK expression in tumors derived from HER2-overexpressing MFC cells compared with the NC group (Supplementary Fig. 20C). Reduced LGALS9 in C1Q+ macrophages preserves cGAS–STING-mediated CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity

Previous studies have shown that LGALS9 can promote STING degradation, contributing to an immunosuppressive microenvironment20. Given that cGAS–STING activation can reprogram TAMs from an M2- to an M1-like phenotype and enhance intratumoral CD8+ T cell infiltration and effector function21,22, we hypothesized that C1Q+ macrophage-derived LGALS9 may suppress the cGAS–STING pathway by reducing STING levels, thereby attenuating IFN-I production and modulating CD8+ T cell activity. To investigate the role of LGALS9 in regulating the cGAS–STING pathway, bone marrow-derived C1Q+ macrophages from mice were subjected to LGALS9 knockdown or overexpression. Western blot analysis revealed that LGALS9 knockdown increased, whereas LGALS9 overexpression decreased, the levels of cGAS–STING pathway components, indicating an inverse relationship between LGALS9 expression and cGAS–STING signaling in these cells (Fig. 7A). To further validate the receptor–ligand-mediated communication between C1Q+ macrophages isolated from HER2-positive GC tissues and CD8+ T cells, macrophages were co-cultured with CD8+ T cells purified from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of healthy donors. To ensure intervention specificity, we treated co-cultures with recombinant LGALS9 (rLGALS9) and a neutralizing antibody (α-LGALS9 nAb). Consistent with the Western blot results, inhibition of the cGAS–STING pathway using the small-molecule inhibitor SN-011 reversed these effects, leading to increased CD8+ T cell exhaustion marker expression. We further assessed the expression of the corresponding receptor CD44 on CD8+ T cells. Flow cytometry analysis showed that CD44 expression was upregulated following co-culture with patient-derived HER2-positive GC C1Q+ macrophages, further increased upon rLGALS9 treatment, and returned to the level observed with C1Q+ macrophages alone after subsequent addition of α-LGALS9 nAb (Fig. 7B and Supplementary Fig. 22A). We next performed in vivo validation using rLGALS9 and α-LGALS9 nAb. Compared with anti-PD1 monotherapy, adoptive transfer of macrophages resulted in slower tumor growth. This inhibitory effect was reversed by rLGALS9 treatment, whereas the addition of α-LGALS9 nAb restored the slower tumor growth (Fig. 7C–F). mIF analysis (Fig. 3J) revealed a shorter distance between C1Q+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells in HER2-positive GC tissues. Consistently, co-culture experiments showed that C1Q+ macrophages enhanced CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity compared with controls, and this effect was further augmented by LGALS9 knockdown (Fig. 6A, B). To determine whether the closer spatial proximity of C1Q+ macrophages confers a positive influence on CD8+ T-cell effector function, we assessed LGALS9 expression in C1Q+ and C1Q− macrophages isolated from HER2-positive gastric tumors of independent patients and found markedly lower LGALS9 levels in C1Q+ compared with C1Q− macrophages by Western blotting (Fig. 7G). To further validate this observation, LGALS9 was overexpressed in macrophages isolated from HER2-positive gastric tumors, and Western blotting confirmed increased LGALS9 expression (Supplementary Fig. 24A). In addition, to verify LGALS9 secretion by C1Q+ macrophages, ELISA was performed on the culture supernatants. The results showed that LGALS9 levels in the supernatant of C1Q+ macrophages were higher than those of C1Q− macrophages, and this increase was reversed after LGALS9 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 24B). These observations suggest that a higher abundance of C1Q+ macrophages, their closer spatial positioning to CD8+ T cells, and their intrinsically lower LGALS9 expression collectively contribute to enhanced CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity in HER2-positive GC. Compared with the 48-h time point, cytotoxicity of CD8+ T cells purified from PBMCs of healthy donors in the control group declined, and exhaustion marker expression increased. Furthermore, macrophages isolated from HER2-positive gastric tumors exhibited distinct functional characteristics. SFRP2+ CAFs impair CD8+ T cell antitumor activity and attenuate anti-PD-1 therapy efficacy via the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis

To confirm the crosstalk between SFRP2+ CAFs and CD8+ T cells, FACS-isolated SFRP2+ CAFs from murine subcutaneous tumors were co-cultured with activated CD8+ T cells from spleens of syngeneic mice at a 3:1 ratio for 48 h. Flow cytometry revealed that, compared with control treatment, co-culture with SFRP2+ CAFs increased the expression of exhaustion markers in CD8+ T cells, an effect reversed by CXCL12 knockdown (Fig. 8A, B and Supplementary Fig. 18B). Consistent with these findings, mIF analysis showed that HER2-positive tumors contained a lower proportion of SFRP2+ CAFs despite exhibiting shorter distances between SFRP2+ CAFs and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4J). These observations suggest that, although SFRP2+ CAFs can promote CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and are positioned in closer proximity to CD8+ T cells, their overall reduced abundance and the low expression of CXCL12 in SFRP2+ CAFs in HER2-positive GC may mitigate this suppressive influence on CD8+ T-cell cytotoxic function. To determine the in vivo relevance of this immunosuppressive interaction, we next evaluated its impact on tumor growth. A subcutaneous tumor model was established by co-inoculating SFRP2+ CAFs with MFCs into syngeneic mice. The results showed that, compared to the control group, SFRP2+ CAFs significantly promoted MFC tumor growth in the host mice, whereas CXCL12 knockdown reversed this effect (Fig. 8C–I). We found that decrease of CD8+ T cells with anti-CD8 antibodies abrogated the tumor-promoting effect of CXCL12 overexpression (Fig. 8J–M). Finally, we assessed how these stromal interactions influence checkpoint blockade efficacy. Anti-PD-1 treatment of MFC tumor-bearing mice revealed that SFRP2+ CAFs impaired therapeutic efficacy, whereas CXCL12 knockdown restored the response (Fig. 8N–Q). SFRP2+ CAFs acquire a senescence-associated phenotype that drives CXCL12-CXCR4-dependent T-cell exhaustion

Previous studies have shown that senescent fibroblasts can form a tumor-permissive niche through abundant CXCL12 secretion, as demonstrated in bladder tissue23. These findings suggest that senescence-associated CAFs may similarly contribute to CXCL12-CXCR4-mediated immunoregulation in GC. Consistent with the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, Western blot analysis of FACS-isolated SFRP2+ and SFRP2− CAFs from murine subcutaneous tumors showed that SFRP2− CAFs expressed lower levels of the senescence markers p16 and p21 compared with SFRP2+ CAFs. Upon doxorubicin (DXR) treatment, p16 and p21 levels increased in both CAF subsets, with a greater induction observed in SFRP2+ CAFs than in SFRP2− CAFs (Fig. 9A). qPCR analysis further showed that CXCL12 levels in FACS-isolated SFRP2− CAFs from primary murine subcutaneous tumors were lower than those in SFRP2+ CAFs. Following doxorubicin (DXR) treatment, CXCL12 expression increased in both subsets, with a greater induction observed in SFRP2+ CAFs compared with SFRP2− CAFs (Fig. 9B). SA-β-gal staining further supported differential senescence in CAF subsets. After 1 day of dye treatment, the percentage of SA-β-gal-positive cells was slightly higher in SFRP2+ CAFs than in SFRP2− CAFs. By day three, the SA-β-gal positivity rate in SFRP2+ CAFs was markedly higher than in SFRP2− CAFs. Consistent with prior reports showing that senescent cells undergo pronounced morphological alterations (Varela-Eirín et al.)24, senescent CAFs in our system exhibited a more rounded and enlarged cell appearance (Fig. 9C). To further validate that senescence-associated CAFs contribute to CXCL12–CXCR4-mediated immunoregulation in GC, we performed co-culture assays using CAFs isolated from HER2-positive GC tissues and CD8+ T cells purified from PBMCs of healthy donors. Flow cytometry showed that, compared with SFRP2− CAFs, co-culture with SFRP2+ CAFs increased the proportion of exhaustion markers in CD8+ T cells, whereas treatment with a CXCL12 neutralizing antibody (α-CXCL12 nAb) returned these marker levels. Following DXR treatment—applied to SFRP2− CAFs, SFRP2+ CAFs, and SFRP2+ CAFs with α-CXCL12 nAb—CD8+ T cells exhibited a further increase in exhaustion-marker expression across all corresponding conditions (Fig. 9D and Supplementary Fig. 22B). To further validate receptor–ligand-mediated communication between SFRP2+ CAFs isolated from HER2-positive GC tissues and CD8+ T cells purified from PBMCs of healthy donors, and to ensure intervention specificity, co-cultures were treated with recombinant CXCL12 (rCXCL12) and the CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100. Flow cytometry analysis showed that co-culture with SFRP2+ CAFs increased the proportion of exhaustion markers on CD8+ T cells. Treatment with rCXCL12 further elevated these markers, whereas concurrent administration of AMD3100 reversed this effect. We further assessed the expression of the corresponding receptor CXCR4 on CD8+ T cells. Flow cytometry analysis showed that CXCR4 expression was upregulated following co-culture with SFRP2+ CAFs, further increased upon rCXCL12 treatment, and reversed after subsequent addition of AMD3100 (Fig. 9E). We next performed in vivo validation using rCXCL12 and AMD3100. In MFC tumor-bearing mice treated with anti-PD-1, SFRP2+ CAFs reduced therapeutic efficacy. This effect was reversed by AMD3100 treatment, whereas subsequent administration of rCXCL12 again impaired efficacy (Fig. 9F–I). To assess the long-term functional consequences of these interactions, we performed assays measuring CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity over 90 h. Compared with the 48-h time point, cytotoxicity of patient-derived HER2-positive GC CD8+ T cells in the control group declined, and exhaustion marker expression increased. C1Q+ macrophages enrichment and SFRP2+ CAFs decrease define a spatial niche predictive of PD-1 inhibitor response in HER2-positive GC

Cell type deconvolution analysis of the ST data via CARD revealed a distinct niche in HER2-positive GC, characterized by enrichment of C1Q+ macrophages and CD8+ Tem cells, alongside decrease of SFRP2+ CAFs. This observation prompted us to compare the spatial domains associated with treatment outcomes. In the HER2-positive group, domain 4 exhibited this immune-enriched profile, whereas domain 8 showed the opposite pattern (Fig. 10A and Supplementary Data 3). These findings suggest that domain 4 may serve as a promising predictive biomarker for the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1 inhibitors combined with anti-HER2 targeted therapy in HER2-positive GC, while domain 8 may indicate poor therapeutic response. To directly substantiate the spatial architecture identified in our analysis, we conducted additional validation on the same set of samples, confirming the co-localization of C1Q+ macrophages, CD8+ T cells, and SFRP2+ CAFs at single-cell resolution. These data further supported the enrichment of C1Q+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells, along with the reduction of SFRP2+ CAFs in HER2-positive tumors (Fig. 10B). Moreover, in HER2-positive GC tumors, SFRP2+ CAFs were positioned closer to both C1Q+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells, and C1Q+ macrophages similarly exhibited reduced distances to CD8+ T cells, providing spatial feasibility for the domain 4-associated predictive niche (Fig. 10C, D and Supplementary Data 1). Examination of clinical GC specimens further supported these spatial patterns, and mIF validated both the proportional differences and distance relationships among these key cell populations (Fig. 10E). To test the functional relevance of these domains, we recapitulated the niche in vivo. Anti-PD-1 treatment in MFC tumor-bearing mice demonstrated that C1Q+ macrophages enhanced therapeutic efficacy, whereas co-injection of SFRP2+ CAFs with tumor cells subcutaneously attenuated this response. Next, we treated MFC tumor-bearing mice with a combination of C1Q+ macrophages, SFRP2+ CAFs, and anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody. Compared to monotherapy, C1Q+ macrophages significantly suppressed tumor growth, an effect that was reversed by the addition of SFRP2+ CAFs (Fig. 10F, G). To further assess how these cellular compositions influenced T cell exhaustion, we investigated corresponding changes in the expression levels of exhaustion markers in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells, including LAG3, PD-1 and TIM3 (Fig. 10J). Spatial immune-niche composition predicts clinical outcomes and treatment response specifically in HER2-positive GC

To strengthen the clinical relevance of our findings, we performed mIF to examine the association between spatial immune niche features and patient outcomes, including progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS). Tumor tissues from GC patients with favorable clinical outcomes (n = 20) exhibited a higher proportion of C1Q+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells, along with a lower proportion of SFRP2+ CAFs, compared with patients with less favorable outcomes (n = 20) (Supplementary Fig. 26A). To correlate spatial immune niche features with patient involving PD-1 inhibitors and anti-HER2 therapies, we performed mIF validation. Compared with non-responders (n = 6 patients), responders (n = 6 patients) exhibited a higher proportion of C1Q+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells, along with a lower proportion of SFRP2+ CAFs (Supplementary Fig. 26B). These findings indicate that a spatial immune niche enriched in C1Q+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells, but with fewer SFRP2+ CAFs, is associated with improved patient outcomes and enhanced therapeutic responses. To further dissect the functional state of C1Q+ macrophages within the tumor ecosystem, we quantified the subset co-expressing LGALS9 using mIF analysis. Notably, despite the overall increase in C1Q+ macrophages in patients with favorable outcomes, the proportion of C1Q+LGALS9+ macrophages was lower in these patients compared with those with unfavorable outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 26C). To integrate spatial immune niche features with established mechanisms of resistance to HER2-targeted therapy in GC, we used mIF across different patients to examine the relationship of C1Q+ macrophages, CD8+ T cells, and SFRP2+ CAFs with PI3K/AKT pathway activation (pAKT high, n = 5 patients; pAKT low, n = 5 patients) (Supplementary Fig. 26D) and MET amplification (MET high, n = 5 patients; MET low, n = 5 patients) (Supplementary Fig. 26E), with no significant differences observed, indicating that the niche is independent of these resistance mechanisms. Additionally, to assess intratumoral heterogeneity within clinical specimens, we compared immune niche features across tumor blocks from the same tumor exhibiting uniform versus focal HER2 expression, with no significant differences observed (Supplementary Fig. 27). Furthermore, we examined the presence of the spatial immune niche in tumor tissues from HER2-low GC patients (including HER2+ and HER2++/FISH− cases). Compared with HER2-positive tumors, HER2-low tumors exhibited lower proportions of C1Q+ macrophages and CD8+ T cells, alongside a higher proportion of SFRP2+ CAFs, confirming the strict HER2-specificity of this niche (Supplementary Fig. 27). To confirm that our in vivo model reliably recapitulates the clinical phenotype of human HER2-positive GC, we evaluated tumor growth under baseline and varied therapeutic conditions. At baseline, HER2-overexpressing tumors (MFC-HER2 + IgG) exhibited a slight growth advantage over control tumors (MFC-NC + IgG), consistent with the oncogenic nature of HER2. Both anti-HER2 monotherapy in the HER2-overexpressing setting and functional anti-PD-1 monotherapy in the NC setting (MFC-NC + anti-HER2 + anti-PD-1) exerted comparable, moderate tumor-suppressive effects relative to their respective baselines. Crucially, the combination of anti-HER2 and anti-PD-1 therapies in MFC-HER2 mice yielded the most profound tumor growth inhibition, significantly outperforming all other treatments. This synergistic therapeutic response directly mirrors the clinical efficacy of combined targeted and immune checkpoint blockade observed in patients, robustly validating the translational reliability of our in vivo model (Supplementary Fig. 28A–C). To further confirm that our murine model phenocopies the mechanistic cellular crosstalk of human GC, we performed mIF profiling of untreated baseline tumors. Consistent with our human data, HER2-overexpressing tumors exhibited a significantly higher abundance of C1Q+ macrophages with reduced LGALS9 expression compared to the NC group (Supplementary Fig. 28D). Concurrently, we observed a diminished proportion of SFRP2+ CAFs coupled with decreased CXCL12 secretion, alongside downregulated CXCR4 expression on infiltrating CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. 28E, F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that our murine model faithfully mirrors the spatial immune architecture of human GC. Furthermore, the stark contrast with the NC baseline proves this immune niche is strictly HER2-specific. To further determine whether the identified immune-niche features are specific to the HER2-positive subtype or represent a broader phenomenon across GC, we further evaluated additional molecular subgroups. Comparisons between EBV-negative and EBV-positive GC (Supplementary Fig. 29A), between microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) and mismatch-repair-deficient (dMMR) GC (Supplementary Fig. 29B), and between TP53-wild-type and TP53-mutant GC (Supplementary Fig. 29C) revealed no significant differences in the proportions of C1Q+ macrophages or SFRP2+ CAFs. These findings indicate that the spatial immune-niche features characterized in this study are specific to the HER2-positive subtype. To explore whether similar immune-contexture patterns may also be associated with HER2 status in other tumor types, we further analyzed publicly available bulk transcriptomic cohorts from two breast cancer datasets and one colorectal cancer dataset. ssGSEA was used to evaluate the enrichment scores of CD8-Tem, Macro-C1Q, and CAF-SFRP2 signatures. While these analyses did not reveal statistically significant differences, HER2-positive samples in these cohorts showed only subtle and variable trends toward higher CD8-Tem and Macro-C1Q signature scores and lower CAF-SFRP2 scores compared with HER2-negative samples. In contrast, the coordinated and robust immune niche observed in HER2-positive GC was not evident in these tumor types (Supplementary Fig. 30). However, these trends were less pronounced and lacked statistical significance compared with those observed in GC, suggesting that this coordinated immune niche is more specifically and robustly associated with HER2-positive GC rather than representing a general feature across HER2-driven malignancies. To assess the clinical relevance of CAF-SFRP2_sigs, we performed a systematic analysis across multiple STAD cohorts and immunotherapy datasets. Kaplan–Meier analyses in several STAD cohorts consistently showed that patients with high CAF-SFRP2_sigs had worse overall survival, indicating a stable association between this fibroblast-related program and poor prognosis (Supplementary Fig. 31A). We then compared CAF-SFRP2_sigs between responders and non-responders in multiple public immunotherapy cohorts. Overall, non-responders tended to exhibit higher CAF-SFRP2_sigs, whereas responders showed relatively lower scores, suggesting that the SFRP2-associated CAF program may be linked to immunotherapy resistance (Supplementary Fig. 31B). Consistently, survival analyses in immunotherapy cohorts further showed that high CAF-SFRP2_sigs was associated with worse progression-free survival or overall survival, while low CAF-SFRP2_sigs was associated with more favorable clinical outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 31C). These results suggest that the SFRP2-associated CAF program may contribute to an immune-suppressive or immune-excluded tumor microenvironment and support its potential utility as a predictive biomarker in HER2-positive GC.

Discussion

HER2 overexpression characterizes a distinct molecular subtype found in approximately 15–20% of GC cases6–8. In the phase III KEYNOTE-811 trial12, the addition of pembrolizumab to first-line trastuzumab and chemotherapy significantly improved objective response rates in patients with metastatic HER2-positive gastric and gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma, compared to the regimen without pembrolizumab. Based on these findings, the FDA granted accelerated approval for this triplet regimen in May 2021, with full approval following in 2025. Although patients with high PD-L1 expression (e.g., CPS ≥ 10) are generally considered more likely to benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors, accumulating clinical evidence indicates that PD-L1 CPS alone does not consistently predict treatment response in GC. For example, recent trials such as PANDA and KEYNOTE-585 reported limited predictive value of PD-L1 CPS for pathological response or event-free survival26,27, underscoring the need to identify additional determinants of immunotherapy response within the tumor immune microenvironment.

Obtaining treatment-naïve specimens is especially challenging: accurate HER2 assessment mandates biopsy cores at least 5 mm in length (equivalent to a mung bean), yet early-stage lesions frequently yield insufficient tissue, and advanced tumors undergo neoadjuvant therapy before resection, precluding access to untreated samples28. Consequently, obtaining sufficient untreated HER2-positive samples for integrative studies remains difficult, highlighting the exceptional value of the specimens analyzed in our work. Current clinical guidelines recommend large endoscopic biopsies to mitigate intratumoral heterogeneity, but even so, fewer than half of screened patients meet the criteria for reliable HER2 testing29. In contrast, ST enables high-resolution mapping of gene expression, allowing intratumoral spatial heterogeneity to be resolved within each tumor sample.

Previous studies, which employed ST or scRNA-seq technologies in GC have several limitations. First, many of them focused predominantly on metabolic pathways, with limited resolution of immune cell subpopulations or their spatial interactions30. Critically, samples were neither profiled for nor stratified by HER2 expression, precluding the identification of HER2-specific immunological niches. Moreover, computational predictions of cell-cell communication (e.g., via CellChat) were rarely validated through functional assays such as co-culture or in vivo models, leaving mechanistic insights largely speculative31. Without spatial resolution of immune-stromal architecture, these studies felled short in developing effective predictive biomarkers32. Furthermore, prior studies investigating immunotherapy response in GC have largely relied on bulk transcriptomic analyses to derive predictive gene signatures33,34. In contrast, our study integrates ST with scRNA-seq sequencing in treatment-naïve samples to delineate how HER2 amplification shapes the spatial organization and interactions of immune cells within the tumor microenvironment. Our study integrated ST and scRNA-seq in treatment-naïve HER2-positive GC, providing an unaltered view of the baseline immune landscape.

The stromal-myeloid niche plays a pivotal role in modulating immune-related therapies in cancers35–37. For example, in GC peritoneal metastasis (GCPMs), THBS2+ mCAFs and SPP1+ TAMs mediate immunotherapy resistance via the C3-C3AR1 axis. Blocking this pathway with a C3AR1 antagonist reduces TAMs, restores CD8+ T cells, and enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy, offering a potential strategy to overcome resistance35. Moreover, multiple CAF subpopulation have been reported to promote tumor progression and facilitate immune evasion38–43. In this study, we report an immune niche in HER2-positive GC, defined by the enrichment of C1Q+ macrophages and CD8+ Tem cells, and a marked reduction of SFRP2+ CAFs. Notably, this spatially organized immune niche was reproducibly observed across spatial transcriptomic profiles from multiple patients despite interpatient variability, and remained stable across tumor blocks from the same patient exhibiting uniform or focal HER2-positive expression, supporting the robustness of this immune architecture despite intrapatient HER2 heterogeneity in HER2-positive GC. This spatially distinct immune microenvironment was revealed through an integrative approach combining single-cell and spatial transcriptomics and was validated by mIF and functional assays. In our cohort, the C1Q+ macrophage/SFRP2+ CAF/ CD8+ T-cell niche was present in the majority of HER2-positive tumors, and tumors harboring this niche exhibited features associated with improved immunotherapy responsiveness.

Using Bayesian Analytics for Spatial Segmentation (BASS, version 1.1.0.016)15, we performed unsupervised clustering of spatial transcriptomic profiles based on highly variable genes to identify distinct spatial domains within HER2-positive GC. Importantly, this analysis revealed domain 4 as a prototypical immune-favorable region, characterized by enrichment of C1Q+ macrophages and CD8+ Tem cells, alongside decrease of SFRP2+ CAFs. This domain may serve as a potential predictive biomarker for favorable response to combined anti-PD-1 and anti-HER2 therapy. In contrast, domain 8, exhibiting an opposing cellular composition, may be indicative of poor therapeutic outcomes.

Taking together, we demonstrate that C1Q+ macrophages enhance CD8+ T cell effector function and improve the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy, an effect further augmented by LGALS9 knockdown. Conversely, SFRP2+ CAFs interact with CD8+ T cells via the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis to induce T cell exhaustion and impair immunotherapeutic efficacy. Importantly, our findings introduce an immune-spatial paradigm in HER2-positive GC, with important mechanistic and translational implications. This niche offers a foundation for precision immunotherapy and highlights the value of integrating ST and scRNA-seq technologies to decode the tumor immune microenvironment.

Building on these insights, several therapeutic avenues merit consideration. First, spatial or transcriptomic detection of the C1Q+ macrophage/SFRP2+ CAF/CD8+ T-cell niche may enable biomarker-driven patient selection to prioritize individuals most likely to benefit from current combination regimens. Targeting the LGALS9 pathway in macrophages may be achievable through emerging LGALS9-neutralizing antibodies or glycan–lectin interaction inhibitors that are currently under preclinical development. Similarly, the CXCL12–CXCR4 axis has been extensively explored in oncology, with CXCR4 antagonists such as AMD3100 already clinically approved for other indications and investigational agents under active evaluation. In addition, targeting pathways that may disrupt this niche—such as MDK signaling, ERK activation, or CAF/myeloid regulatory programs—could represent rational combination strategies for patients who do not benefit from current therapies. These stromal-targeting strategies could be integrated with anti-HER2 therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors to potentiate antitumor immunity.

Methods

Ethics statement

All experiments involving patients were approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (NFEC-202209-K4) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent, including consent for the publication of potentially identifying clinical details, was obtained from all participants. Participants were not compensated. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Southern Medical University (SMUL202503007).

Clinical samples

We collected untreated GC samples from patients who underwent surgical resection at Nanfang Hospital between July 2022 and August 2023 (n = 8). Detailed patient information, including sex and age, is provided in Supplementary Table 1. Sex was not considered as a variable in the study design and analysis because previous studies showed no significant sex differences. Nevertheless, we acknowledge the importance of sex differences; the sex distribution of all samples is detailed in the supplementary materials for transparency. Based on preoperative biopsy results assessed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)44, the samples were classified into HER2-positive (n = 4) and HER2-negative (n = 4) groups. HER2 status was subsequently re-evaluated on the corresponding surgical resection specimens used for scRNA-seq and ST analyses to ensure consistency and accuracy prior to sequencing. While our single-center, small-cohort design may introduce selection bias and limit broad generalizability, we mitigated these effects through strict inclusion criteria.

Method for scRNA-seq data analysis

Data integration and determination of the major cell types

The remaining 62,669 out of 77,166 cells from eight samples were integrated together, and batch effects were correct using Harmony (v1.2.0)45. Firstly, gene expression matrices were normalized to total cellular read count, and cell-cycle scores were calculated using Seurat(v4.1.0) CellCycleScoring function. Then, Seurat SCTransform function was applied for the normalized data to remove cell cycle effect and select 2500 highly variably genes (HVG) for downstream analysis. We further removed cells with mitochondrial genes expression ratio higher than 25% and hemoglobin related genes expression ratio higher than 20%. Following that, we scaled and calculated PCA for cleaning HVG. The RunHarmony function was conducted to remove batch effect. The FindNeighbors constructed a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph with “reduction = harmony” parameter, and FindClusters function with “resolution = 0.6” parameter was carried out to cluster cells into different groups. The RunUMAP function was then applied to do the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensional reduction and visualization. The main cell types were identified on the basis of predicted and known marker genes acquired from the SingleR(v1.8.1) (https://github.com/LTLA/SingleR) and CellMarker database (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellMarker/)46.

Sub-cell type analysis

Raw gene expression count of cells for sub-cell type were extracted from previous cleaned data and created a new Seurat object. Gene expression matrices were normalized to total cellular read count, and highly variably genes (HVG) selected from the normalized data using Seurat SCTransform function with default parameters. Batch effects were corrected using R package “harmony (v1.2.0).” Following dimensional reduction and clustering used the same procedure for whole cell population.

Trajectory and pseudotime analysis

Trajectory and pseudotime analysis were conducted by monocle3 (Version 1.3.4)47. The algorithms place the cells along a trajectory corresponding to a biological process by taking advantage of an individual cell’s asynchronous progression under an unsupervised framework. The trajectories were visualized as UMAP plot. For slingshot analysis, 2000 cells were randomly selected and HVG were extracted from the Seurat data by Slingshot (version 2.10.0) software package48, and downstream analyses, including visualization were performed by R package ggplot2 (version 3.5.0) and pheatmap (version 1.0.12). To keep the data analysis consistent, cell coordinates of UMAP generated by Seurat were passed to monocle and slingshot.

Cell-cell communication analysis

CellChat49 (v1.6.1) was used for Inference and analysis of cell-cell communication. Gene expression matrix and cell type labels are exported from Seurat object, then create a CellChat object using “createCellChat.” Cellular communication Modeling, analysis and visualization are conducted by following standard procedures. CellChat (http://www.cellchat.org/) a tool that enables us to quantitatively estimate and analyze the cell-cell communication network with scRNA-seq data as the input. Thereafter, it offers capabilities for further data exploration, analysis as well as visualization. Through defining the outgoing and incoming interaction strengths as the out- and in-degree centrality measures in weighted cell-cell communication networks, the remarkable signaling mechanisms and targets were analyzed. The out-degree represents the sum of the communication probabilities of the outgoing signaling from each cell type. Meanwhile, the in-degree represents the sum of the communication probabilities of incoming signaling to each cell type. The alterations in the outgoing and incoming signaling were analyzed in all critical pathways. The information flow of signaling represents the sum of the communication probabilities between cell-cell interactions. To identify the alterations in critical signaling mechanisms, the information flow of signaling mechanisms in treatment with distinct stages was compared with controls. The interactions between cell types were inferred, and differential interaction weight of 0.1 was set as the threshold value. The ligand-receptor pairs were compared between controls and treatment with distinct stages.

Method for stereo-seq data analysis

Domain division and identification

Clustering or domain identification is an important step in data analysis and the foundation for downstream analysis. Subsequent research, such as cell type or tissue annotation and differential expression, relies on effective clustering. Bayesian Analytics for Spatial Segmentation (BASS, version 1.1.0.016)15, an algorithm to identify spatial organizational domains, was used to combine the spatial expression profiles of highly variable genes to cluster the space and obtain different domains, revealing the spatial structure of the organization.

Based on histology and clinical studies, conduct preliminary identification of the identified domains and determine the domain regions of interest and research significance in biology.

Cluster division and cell type identification

In addition to using BASS to identify the domains, we also use Seurat50 (v4.1.0) for unsupervised clustering of spots. The spots from all samples were integrated together, and batch effects were correct using harmony (v1.2.0)45.

Firstly, gene expression matrices were normalized to total cellular read count, and Cell-Cycle scores were calculated using the Seurat CellCycleScoring function.

Then, the Seurat SCTransform function was applied for the normalized data to remove cell cycle effect and select 2500 highly variably genes (HVG) for downstream analysis.

Following that, we scaled and calculated PCA for cleaning HVG. The RunHarmony function was conducted to remove batch effect. The FindNeighbors constructed a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph with “reduction = harmony” parameter, and FindClusters function with “resolution = 0.1” parameter was carried out to cluster cells into different groups. The RunUMAP function was then applied to do the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) dimensional reduction and visualization.

The main cell types were identified on the basis of calculated and known marker genes acquired from the Findmarker and CellMarker database (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/CellMarker/).

Spots annotation

We used Conditional Autoregressive Model-based Deconvolution (CARD, version 1.1)51, a deconvolution analysis method, to map the annotation results of single-cell data onto spatial transcriptome slices, so that we can see the approximate distribution of each cell type in spatial position. Based on the results of deconvolution, we can focus the distribution of cell types on specific domains.

Distribution of marker gene expression

The differences in gene expression in different regions often reflect specific functional differences. Based on the calculated and screened marker genes, draw their spatial expression distribution maps and observe whether they are highly expressed in the target region.

Extraction and differentiation of murine bone marrow-derived macrophages

Six hundred fifteen or C57BL/6 mice (both sexes, 6–8 weeks old) were euthanized and briefly immersed in 75% ethanol for sterilization. Hind limbs were dissected, and the surrounding muscle tissue was carefully removed without damaging the joints. Femurs and tibias were rinsed with 75% ethanol followed by PBS. Bone marrow cells were flushed from both ends of the bones using a syringe filled with DMEM medium. After centrifugation, red blood cells were lysed with RBC lysis buffer for 2 min and neutralized with DMEM (3–5 times the lysis volume). The cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min. A portion of the resulting cell pellet was directly subjected to fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for downstream analyses. For co-culture experiments with MFC cells, the remaining cells were resuspended in complete DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 20 ng/mL recombinant murine M-CSF (PeproTech), and 10 ng/mL recombinant murine IL-4 (PeproTech) to induce macrophage differentiation. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator, with medium changes every 2–3 days. Differentiation was typically achieved within 6–7 days.

Isolation of spleens and tumor tissues

To prepare single-cell suspensions, mouse spleens and tumors were harvested in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. Tumor tissues were minced and digested in DMEM containing 0.5 mg/mL collagenase IV and 0.1 mg/mL DNase I (both from Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 1 h with shaking, followed by mechanical dissociation on frosted glass slides. The resulting suspensions were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer (Solarbio Life Science). This method is suitable for subsequent cancer-associated fibroblast (CAF) sorting or CD8+ T cell cytokine detection and exhaustion marker analysis, depending on the experimental needs.

Spleens were harvested and mechanically dissociated in the same manner as tumor tissues, but without enzymatic digestion. The resulting suspensions were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. Red blood cells in spleen samples were lysed using 2 mL RBC lysis buffer (Leagene) for 2 min at room temperature and then neutralized with DMEM. The splenocytes were subsequently used for CD8+ T cell sorting.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

Immune cells were resuspended in staining buffer and pre-incubated with Fc block (BioLegend) for 10 min. Surface markers were then labeled with specific antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using Fixation/Permeabilization Concentrate and Diluent (eBioscience, 1:3 ratio) for 30 min, followed by two washes with 1× permeabilization buffer. Intracellular antibodies were then added and incubated under the same conditions. Finally, cells were fixed using fixation buffer and analyzed on a BD flow cytometer. For cytokine detection, cells were stimulated with a cell activation cocktail containing brefeldin A (BioLegend) for 4–6 h prior to staining.

CD8+ T cells were isolated using a mouse CD8+ T-cell isolation kit (STEMCELL) via magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS). C1Q+ macrophages (c MHC-II+CX3CR1+C1Q+) were isolated from BMDMs using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) on a BD FACS Aria II Cell Sorter52. SFRP2+ CAFs (CD45−EpCAM−CD31−SFRP2+) were similarly sorted from murine subcutaneous tumors using the same FACS platform53. Antibody panels and reagents are detailed in online Supplementary Table 2.

Co-culture of macrophages or CAFs with MFC cells

MFC cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Macrophages were derived from murine bone marrow cells cultured in complete DMEM supplemented with 20 ng/mL recombinant murine M-CSF and 10 ng/mL IL-4 (PeproTech) for 6–7 days to induce differentiation. CAFs were isolated from murine subcutaneous tumors by FACS. MFC cells were seeded and incubated with either Macrophages or CAFs at a 1:1 ratio (MFC: macrophages or MFC: CAFs). After 48 h of co-culture, cells were collected for subsequent flow cytometry analyses.

Co-culture of CD8+ T cells with macrophages or CAFs

Mouse CD8+ T cells were isolated using MACS and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were activated with anti-CD3 (2 μg/mL) and anti-CD28 (5 μg/mL) antibodies for 72 h. C1Q+ macrophages and SFRP2+ CAFs were isolated by FACS and co-cultured with activated CD8+ T cells at ratios of 2:1 (C1Q+ macrophages: CD8+ T cells) and 3:1 (SFRP2+ CAFs: CD8+ T cells), respectively, for 48 h. Cytokine production and expression of exhaustion markers in CD8+ T cells were subsequently assessed by flow cytometry.

In vivo macrophage adoptive transfer experiment

On day 0, 5 × 105 MFC cells were subcutaneously implanted into 615 or C57BL/6 mice. The following day, bone marrow-derived C1Q+ macrophages (5 × 105 cells per mouse) were intravenously administered into the tumor-bearing mice. A second dose of C1Q+ macrophages was delivered on day 7 post-implantation using the same injection protocol.

Subcutaneous co-injection of MFC cells and CAFs

To establish the subcutaneous tumor model, MFC cells were co-injected with FACS-sorted SFRP2+ CAF+ cells into the right flank of 615 or C57BL/6 mice. A total of 1 × 106 MFC cells were mixed with 3 × 106 CAFs in 100 μL of a 1:1 mixture of PBS and Matrigel (Corning), and the resulting suspension was injected subcutaneously.

Mice

Six hundred fifteen mice (both sexes, 6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Aniphe Biolaboratory, and C57BL/6J mice (both sexes, 6–8 weeks old) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Southern Medical University. All animal procedures were conducted under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Mice were housed in a facility maintained at an ambient temperature of 22 ± 2 °C and 40–60% humidity under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Experimental and control animals were bred separately with free access to sterilized food and water. Both male and female mice were randomly assigned across all experimental and control groups in every in vivo experiment described in this study. Sex was not considered as a variable in the study design and analysis because previous studies have shown no significant sex-related differences. Details regarding the number of animals used in each experiment are provided in the figure legends and the Nature Portfolio Reporting Summary. At the ethical endpoint of each experiment, all mice were euthanized by an overdose of isoflurane until loss of consciousness, followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death, in strictly accordance with institutional animal care guidelines.

Tumor growth and treatment

For subcutaneous xenograft models, MFC GC cells (1 × 106 per mouse) were injected subcutaneously to induce solid tumor formation54. After 19 days, mice were euthanized as described above. In certain experiments, mice were intraperitoneally injected with clodronate liposomes (150 μL per mouse, twice weekly) to deplete TAMs. In other experiments, anti-mouse CD8 antibodies (200 μg per mouse, twice weekly) were administered to deplete CD8+ T cells, or anti-mouse PD-1 antibodies (200 μg per mouse, twice weekly) were used to block PD-1 signaling. Corresponding isotype control antibodies were administered in experiments involving CD8 or PD-1 blockade.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

FFPE tissue sections (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using a pressure cooker. After blocking with 3% BSA for 30 min at room temperature, primary antibodies were sequentially applied, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and TSA fluorophore development (PerkinElmer)55,56. Each staining cycle was followed by microwave-mediated antibody stripping in citrate buffer. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and slides were mounted using antifade medium. Images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope or the Panoramic MIDI II slide scanner (3DHISTECH) for whole-slide imaging. All images were subsequently processed and quantitatively analyzed using HALO image analysis software to evaluate marker expression, spatial localization, and cell–cell interactions.
