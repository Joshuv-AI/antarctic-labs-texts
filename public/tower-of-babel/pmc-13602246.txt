Nutcracker syndrome with pelvic congestion syndrome: A case report
Authors: Dambar Sah, Abhishek Pandey, Sabina Rijal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nutcracker syndrome is a rare clinical entity characterized by external compression of left renal vein (LRV) disturbing the outflow of blood into inferior venacava. We present a case of a 35-year-old female who presented to gynecology outpatient department (OPD) with abdominal pain of right lower quadrant initially which later shifted to left flank associated with dysmenorrhea. Laboratory investigations revealed microscopic hematuria and anemia. Imaging with computed tomography revealed decreased aorto-mesenteric angle with dilatation of left renal vein, left ovarian vein, pelvic veins on left side and cross collateral venous dilatation on right side of pelvis suggesting nutcracker morphology leading to pelvic congestion syndrome. This case demonstrates atypical clinical presentation of NCS with shifting pain.

Introduction

Nutcracker syndrome is an uncommon clinical entity which is characterized by external compression of left renal vein (LRV) disturbing the outflow of blood into inferior venacava (IVC), often with dilatation of distal LRV [1]. The terms Nutcracker syndrome and Nutcracker phenomenon differ from each other based on the presence or absence of clinical symptoms. Nutcracker phenomenon is the morphological pattern whereas Nutcracker syndrome is the presence of nutcracker morphology with clinical symptoms [[2], [3], [4]]. Typically, there is compression of LRV between aorta and superior mesenteric artery, which is known as anterior nutcracker syndrome [4,5]. Posterior nutcracker syndrome is a rare entity in which the retro-aortic left renal vein is compressed between the aorta and the vertebral body [[4], [5], [6]].

The patients usually present with hematuria, abdominal or flank pain, left sided varicoceles and orthostatic proteinuria [5,7]. It is less commonly associated with chronic fatigue syndrome, dyspareunia, dysuria, dysmenorrhea, secondary hypertension and increased frequency of polycystic changes of the ovaries [1,7,8]. The differential diagnoses of nutcracker syndrome is wide and includes pancreatic tumors, lymphadenopathy, retroperitoneal masses, low retroperitoneal fat, and pregnancy [5].

We present a case of 35-year-old female with nutcracker syndrome who presented to gynecology outpatient department (OPD) in a tertiary care hospital with abdominal pain of right lower quadrant initially which later shifted to left flank.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old female presented to gynecology OPD in a tertiary care hospital with the complaint of intermittent abdominal pain for 8 months. She reported pain in the right lower quadrant for 3 months initially which later shifted to left flank. There was associated history of dysmenorrhea and increased fatigue as well. On examination, mild tenderness was noted in left flank region.

Laboratory investigation revealed anemia and microscopic hematuria. Renal function test and thyroid function test results were within normal limits. Abdominal ultrasound didn’t reveal significant abnormality as the scan was suboptimal due to presence of bowel gas. Due to persistent symptom, computed tomography (CT) scan of abdomen and pelvis was performed.

CT scan showed decreased distance between abdominal aorta and superior mesenteric artery measuring approx. About 4.8 mm with decreased aorto-mesenteric angle measuring about 15.17 degrees leading to compression of left renal vein with ‘beak sign’ causing tortuous dilatation of left renal vein (∼9.6 mm) (Fig. 1). Beak angle measuring approximately 37.6 degrees at the aortomesenteric portion of left renal vein was also noted (Fig. 2). Dilatation of left ovarian vein (∼9.8 mm) (Fig. 3), pelvic veins on left side (∼7.9 mm) (Fig. 4) and cross collateral venous dilatation on right side of pelvis (∼7.6 mm) (Fig. 5) were also noted. Discussion

Nutcracker syndrome (NCS) is a condition in which there is radiological evidence of nutcracker morphology in a symptomatic patient [4,7]. The anatomical description of nutcracker phenomenon was originally described by Grant and Balt [9]. The usual type is anterior nutcracker syndrome in which there is compression of LRV between aorta and SMA [1,4,5]. Our case belongs to this category. Posterior nutcracker syndrome, in which there is compression of retro-aortic LRV between the aorta and vertebra is less common [[4], [5], [6]].

NCS is a rare clinical entity although the presence of nutcracker morphology has been increasingly described due to advancement in radiology [7]. Poyraz et al. [10] reported a presence of anterior nutcracker morphology among 4.1% of his study population [10]. However, the exact prevalence of NCS is not known [11]. The prevalence of NCS has been reported to be higher among females in several studies [1,7,12]. The presented case includes a female patient as well. However, asymptomatic nutcracker phenomenon doesn’t show such predilection and recent studies report do not show gender predominance [3]. The case has been reported across all age groups from childhood to eighth decade but patients usually present in second to third decade [1,3,13]. However, the patient was in her fourth decade in our case.

The clinical presentation of NCS varies widely. Hematuria (present in 13%-100% of cases), flank or abdominal pain, pelvic pain, and varicocele are the common symptoms [7,8,14]. Aggravation of symptoms with physical activity and low BMI is reported [1,5]. Less common symptoms are dyspareunia, dysmenorrhea, fatigue, orthostatic intolerance abnormal uterine bleeding, nausea, and anemia [7,11]. Hematuria is due to rupture of thin-walled septum between the varices and the collecting system in the renal fornix as result of renal venous hypertension [11,15]. Flank and abdominal pain have been postulated to be due to venous congestion triggered inflammation [11]. Left flank pain can be due to passage of blood clots along the ureter [1]. Pelvic congestion syndrome is due to retrograde transmission of elevated LRV pressure into the left ovarian vein leading to pelvic venous dilatation. The patients can present with symptoms like varicosities, chronic pelvic pain, dyspareunia and dysmenorrhea [7]. For diagnosis of NCS, there should be presence of symptom for more than 6 months and compression should be demonstrated using any imaging modality [4]. Our patient had atypical presentation with initial symptom of pain in the right lower abdomen which later shifted to left flank. She also complained of dysmenorrhea which can be a part of pelvic congestion syndrome.

Investigation with blood examinations, urinalysis, urine culture, cytology, urethra-cystoscopy, CT urography and renal biopsy may be performed to rule out common renal pathologies in addition to the imaging modalities [1].

Abdominal ultrasonography with Doppler evaluation is usually the first imaging modality. It is a readily available, noninvasive tool with sensitivity 69%-90% and specificity of 89%-100% [3,7]. Positional variability is observed in peak velocities and thus measurement of peak velocity ratio is supposed to be more predictive [1]. The diagnostic criteria for NCS is a peak systolic velocity ratio more than or equal to 4.2-5.0 between the aorto-superior mesenteric and hilar LRV segments [7]. However, Doppler ultrasound has positional variability and technical difficulties like small sampling area, requirement that the patient be fasted for 6-8 hours and interobserver variability limiting its use [3,5]. In our case, the abdominal scan was suboptimal due to presence of excessive bowel gas and thus information pointing towards the diagnosis couldn’t be gathered using ultrasonography.

Axial scans including CT and MRI are the other useful diagnostic tools used in cases where ultrasonography is inconclusive [3,6]. CT angiography and MR angiography can help in the visualization of the LRV compression point [11]. Axial imaging modalities are also helpful in excluding other differential diagnoses and assisting in surgical planning [1,7]. On CT, “beak sign” is visualized, which is sharp narrowing of the LRV with a triangular shape at the aortomesenteric portion [11,15,16]. The aorto-mesenteric angle is a useful parameter to assess in CT. The normal angle between the aorta and SMA is from 40 to 90 degrees [13]. Kim et al. [16] reported a cut-off of 41 degrees on sagittal CT with sensitivity of 100% and specificity of 55.6% [16]. In our case, the angle measured about 15.17 degrees which supports the diagnosis of NCS in the presence of symptoms. A LRV diameter ratio (hilar-aorto-mesenteric) of ≥4.9 is also supportive of diagnosis in which the ratio of diameters of LRV at the hilum and narrowed aortomesenteric segment is taken [13,15]. It is also useful in demonstration of pre-stenotic dilatation of LRV with perirenal and/or gonadal vein varices [11]. The diameter of normal left gonadal vein measures approximately 3 mm in diameter [1]. There was dilatation of left ovarian vein, pelvic veins on left side and cross collateral venous dilatation on right side of pelvis in our case suggesting pelvic congestion syndrome.

MRI shows the same features as described in the CT scans with added advantage of being radiation-free, advisable in some populations (e.g., children). Best imaging quality was observed with T2-TRUFI (true fast imaging with steady-state free precession) [3,11]. However, high cost and long scanning time limit its use [7].

Retrograde venography and intravascular ultrasound are reported to be gold standard for diagnosis of NCS by some authors [7,11]. Reno-caval pressure gradient monitoring is done with contrast venography with pullback pressure gradient of ≥1 mmHg supporting the diagnosis. However, no consensus has been made regarding the pressure gradient cut-off point [7]. It can also demonstrate reflux of contrast into gonadal and adrenal veins from periureteral and perirenal venous collaterals [11]. However, general consensus is not present and these procedures are not widely performed [1,7].

Treatment of the patient relies on severity and reversibility of the condition as well as the age of the patient. Management options range from observation, pharmacotherapy to surgical procedures. Medical management is done with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors for treatment of orthostatic proteinuria, with or without aspirin to aid in renal perfusion in the pediatric population [3]. Minimally invasive endovascular treatments are getting popular due to decreased postoperative morbidity. Open surgical techniques may be LRV transposition, patch venoplasty without LRV transposition, nephropexy, gonadal vein transposition and renal autotransplantation [3,8].

Conclusion

Nutcracker syndrome is a rare clinical entity that has wide clinical manifestations. Patients with suspicion of NCS should be evaluated with imaging modalities like ultrasonography and CT scans. The imaging findings described earlier in the presence of symptoms help in the diagnosis of NCS.
