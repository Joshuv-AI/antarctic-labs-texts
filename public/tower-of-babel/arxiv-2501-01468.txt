Phase-Field Modeling of Fracture under Compression and Confinement in Anisotropic Geomaterials Preprint: To appear in International Journal for Numerical and Analytical Methods in Geomechanics ( https://doi.org/10.1002/nag.3933 ).
Maryam Hakimzadeh, Carlos Mora-Corral, Noel Walkington, Giuseppe Buscarnera, Kaushik Dayal

Abstract
Strongly anisotropic geomaterials, such as layered shales, have been observed to undergo fracture under compressive loading. This paper applies a phase-field fracture model to study this fracture process. While phase-field fracture models have several advantages — primarily that the fracture path is not pre-determined but arises naturally from the evolution of a smooth non-singular damage field — they provide unphysical predictions when the stress state is complex and includes compression that can cause crack faces to contact. Building on a recently-developed phase-field model that accounts for compressive traction across the crack face, this paper extends the model to the setting of anisotropic fracture. The key features of the model include: (1) a homogenized anisotropic elastic response and strongly-anisotropic model for the work to fracture; (2) an effective damage response that accounts consistently for compressive traction across the crack face, that is derived from the anisotropic elastic response; (3) a regularized crack normal field that overcomes the shortcomings of the isotropic setting, and enables the correct crack response, both across and transverse to the crack face. To test the model, we first compare the predictions to phase-field fracture evolution calculations in a fully-resolved layered specimen with spatial inhomogeneity, and show that it captures the overall patterns of crack growth. We then apply the model to previously-reported experimental observations of fracture evolution in laboratory specimens of shales under compression with confinement, and find that it predicts well the observed crack patterns in a broad range of loading conditions. We further apply the model to predict the growth of wing cracks under compression and confinement. Prior approaches to simulate wing cracks have treated the initial cracks as an external boundary, which makes them difficult to apply to general settings. Here, the effective crack response model enables us to treat the initial crack simply as a non-singular damaged zone within the computational domain, thereby allowing for easy and general computations.

Introduction

Many geomaterials in the environment are anisotropic.
In particular, transverse isotropy is a characteristic feature of various types of rock, including sedimentary and metamorphic rocks.
Many foliated metamorphic rocks, namely schists, slates, gneisses, and phyllites, generally have a distinct directional dependence on their strength
Goo 93
.
Furthermore, stratified / layered sedimentary structures, such as sandstone and shale, have layers that are closely spaced, resulting in a mechanical response that is transverse isotropic, that is, the response is isotropic in the bedding plane of the layers and distinct in the out-of-plane direction
Ama 12
;
Wit 90
;
TK 01
;
CQH 22
;
CZCF 22
.
A key aspect that is the focus of this paper is modeling the failure of such rocks in settings that include compression and confinement, which is a typical stress state in natural and engineered settings.

Related prior work.

Many laboratory studies have been carried out to determine the impact of anisotropy on the strength of various transversely isotropic rocks
NSHF 97
;
BBNB 15
;
SB 17
;
TKJ 06
.
To evaluate the strength of the rock sample, triaxial compression tests with different confining pressures were used in these studies.
To model this behavior, some studies have developed failure models to study the strength of rocks and its dependence on the orientation of the bedding plane.
For example, Jaeger
Jae 60
extrapolated the Coulomb-Navier criterion to introduce a single plane of weakness theory, recognizing two predominant failure modes: sliding along the weak plane and failure traversing the rock matrix.
Tien and Kuo
TK 01
extended the failure criterion proposed by Jaeger and substituted the Mohr-Coulomb approach with the Hoek-Brown model, and then tested their model with experimental data
TKJ 06
.

In addition to developing failure criteria, the inclusion of anisotropy within the constitutive law is important to predict the effect of the orientation of the bedding plane on the elastoplastic response and fracture of the material.
To include the anisotropy of the elastic response in the constitutive law, it is straightforward to use classical homogenization methods, e.g.
Bac 62
.
To model the anisotropic inelastic response, many studies have extended the existing isotropic criteria to the anisotropic setting, such as generalizing the von Mises criterion
Hil 48
and the Mohr-Coulomb criterion
Par 68
to account for anisotropy; a summary is provided in
SWB 16
.
In addition, recent work, for example,
ZSYB 18
;
ZB 22
;
MSH 22
, has investigated the effect of anisotropy on the strength of transversely isotropic rocks.
In
ZSYB 18
, they have used finite element methods to simulate the anisotropic elastoplastic constitutive model proposed by
SWB 16
, thereby providing insight into the deformation and strength of transversely isotropic material under axial and lateral confinements, including capturing the zigzag failure mode observed in layered rocks.

While these prior works have focused on the role of anisotropy in the elastic and inelastic responses, the role of anisotropy in the fracture behavior has been much less studied.
However, the anisotropy of fracture is an important aspect of the overall behavior, and plays a key role in a vast range of phenomena ranging from the fracture of apple flesh
KV 93
, to single crystals
QA 03
, to geomaterials
NM 08
.
Recent work that aims to model the anisotropy in the fracture behavior include strategies that make the fracture energy anisotropic, e.g.,
HK 05
;
HK 09
. However, these studies are restricted to weakly twofold anisotropic settings and cannot capture the behaviour of material with strongly anisotropic surface energies
TRB+ 13
.

Phase-field fracture models.

Phase-field fracture models introduce a new field, in addition to the deformation, that indicates if a material element is intact or fractured, and then regularize this phase-field using higher-order derivatives.
The higher-order derivatives endow the phase-field with sufficient smoothness to enable the use of standard numerical methods to predict crack growth without specifying crack paths beforehand.
That is, rather than sharp singular cracks as in the classical approach to fracture, cracks are now regularized to be non-singular variations of the phase-field, making them amenable to standard numerical approaches.
The elastic response is coupled to the phase-field by setting that the regions that are fractured cannot carry any load, that is, the elastic energy density goes to zero in fractured regions.
However, the combination of crack regularization to a finite volume and setting the fractured elastic energy to zero causes unphysical behavior, such as crack growth under compression.

This issue, which also allows unphysical contact and interpenetration of crack faces, motivated efforts such as
MWH 10
;
AMM 09
, to improve the phase-field model for compressive loading.
In
MWH 10
, they proposed a model that decomposes the elastic energy into tensile and compressive parts, and allows the crack to grow only due to the tensile part of the energy.
In
AMM 09
, they proposed decomposing the elastic energy into compressive hydrostatic, tensile hydrostatic, and deviatoric parts, and allowed the crack to sustain only the compressive hydrostatic loads.
This has been extended to the finite deformation setting in
TZZL 22
;
XYSW 23
;
NS 24
;
TZG+ 19
; however, these works also do not consider the orientation of the crack.
Other modifications of the energy decompositions include
VZC+ 24
that proposed the star-convex model as a modification of the volumetric-deviatoric decomposition;

WSH 21
that extended the spectral decomposition to account for mixed mode fracture;
and
vDEEI 20
to account for anisotropy.
These approaches are reviewed and compared in
ZJT 22
;
GND+ 24
;
Rah 22
;
Cla 21
and elsewhere.
However, these approaches have a key shortcoming: they do not account for the orientation of the crack.
Compressive stresses along the crack face and across the crack face are both treated in exactly the same way; i.e., tension/compression across the crack faces is indistinguishable from tension/compression along the crack faces in these models.

An important departure from the idea of energy decompositions is the work by
SK 19
, that introduced the idea that the crack orientation plays an essential role in determining the response.
Based on this idea,
SK 19
;
FC20b
;
FC20a
;
Agr 16
proposed modifications of the stress tensor to account for the difference in crack-normal and crack-parallel tractions.
While our approach in
HADMC 22
builds on the ideas of

, an important distinction is that we modify the energy and hence our model is hyperelastic.
In contrast, as shown in
HADMC 22

To account for the role of material anisotropy of the fracture process, taking inspiration from phase-field models of crystal growth,
LPM+ 15
proposed a strongly anisotropic surface energy model by using higher derivatives of the fracture phase parameter.
This results in a fourth-order system of partial differential equations (PDEs) for the displacement and crack (phase-field) variables, which they solve using a finite element method with additional smoothness.
Building on this,

;
BSC 23
;

2.A
Basic Phase-Field Model

A variational (energy-based) model for quasistatic brittle fracture was proposed by
FM 98
.
This model is characterized by the minimization of the potential energy
\displaystyle E
given by:

E[{\mathbfit y},\Gamma]=\int_{\Omega\setminus\Gamma}W(\nabla{\mathbfit y})\ \mathrm{d}\Omega+G_{c}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma)+\text{ work due to external loads }

(1)

where
\displaystyle{\mathbfit y}
and
\displaystyle\Gamma
represent the deformation and the crack surface;
\displaystyle\Omega
represents the body in the reference configuration;
\displaystyle W
is the elastic energy density; and
\displaystyle G_{c}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
represent the work to fracture and the Hausdorff area of
\displaystyle\Gamma
, respectively.
The model asserts that the deformation and the crack surface are obtained by minimizing
\displaystyle E
subject to irreversibility, i.e., the crack cannot heal.

This model is very challenging to use to solve realistic boundary-value problems.
A regularized phase-field version of this model was given by Ambrosio and Tortorelli
AT 90
;
AT 92
, inspired by
MM 77
;
Mod 87
:

E[{\mathbfit y},\phi]=\int_{\Omega}\left(\phi^{2}+\eta_{\epsilon}\right)W(\nabla{\mathbfit y})\ \mathrm{d}\Omega+G_{c}\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{(1-\phi)^{2}}{4\epsilon}+\epsilon|\nabla\phi|^{2}\right)\ \mathrm{d}\Omega+\text{ work due to external loads }

(2)

where
\displaystyle\phi({\mathbfit x})
is a scalar phase field that quantifies the level of damage at every referential point
\displaystyle{\mathbfit x}
, and is sufficiently smooth to allow standard FEM approximation.
Specifically,
\displaystyle\phi
, bounded by
\displaystyle 0\leq\phi\leq 1
, indicates that the material is intact at
\displaystyle\phi({\mathbfit x})\simeq 1
and completely fractured at
\displaystyle\phi({\mathbfit x})\simeq 0
.
The small positive quantity
\displaystyle\eta_{\epsilon}
is used to ensure that the overall problem remains positive definite even if
\displaystyle\phi
is
\displaystyle 0
in finite regions; for brevity, we will not write it out explicitly further. In this paper, a value of
\displaystyle\eta_{\epsilon}=$\displaystyle 1\text{\times}{10}^{-3}\text{\,}$
is used for numerical calculations.

In physical terms, if the elastic energy is large, the solid can relieve this energy by reducing the value of
\displaystyle\phi
in regions with large elastic energy density in the first integral in (
2
), i.e., it fractures to reduce the elastic energy.
However, the fracture process requires work, and that is accounted for by the second integral in (
2
).

2.B
Accounting for the Contact of Crack Faces

A key shortcoming of the model in Section
2.A
is that it does not distinguish between compressive, shear, and tensile loadings across the crack.
Therefore, all of these types of loadings can lead to fracture which is unphysical.
Various works have proposed decompositions of the energy in different ways to try to prevent this, e.g.

;
CLK 23
;
Cla 21
;
VZC+ 24
;
TZG+ 19
;
vDEEI 20
;
WSH 21
;
ZJT 22
.
However, as noted by
SK 19
, and discussed further with some examples in
HADMC 22
, it is essential to account for the crack orientation.
For instance, tension across the crack face cannot be resisted by a crack, whereas tension along the crack face will be resisted as well as if the material was intact (Fig.
1

\displaystyle W_{d}

However,

is not an independent field; rather it is a functional of

2.C
Transverse Isotropic Homogenized Elastic Response

Layered rocks can be modeled as transversely isotropic, i.e., given a layer normal or structural director denoted by

The transverse isotropic homogenized elastic response is a classical result in micromechanics, and we summarize the result here following

the stiffness tensor can be represented as a matrix

2.D
Anisotropy of the Work to Fracture

In a general anisotropic material, the work to fracture depends on the fracture direction.
Specifically in stratified rock, crack propagation is much easier along the layers rather than across the layers.
In a homogenized model with structural director
\displaystyle{\mathbfit a}

\displaystyle{\mathbfit a}
being much larger than the work to fracture for propagation in the plane normal to
\displaystyle{\mathbfit a}

In the context of phase-field fracture, a simple way to include anisotropy of the fracture is to replace
\displaystyle|\nabla\phi|^{2}

\displaystyle\nabla\phi\cdot{\mathbfit A}\nabla\phi

\displaystyle{\mathbfit A}

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}
is a 4-th order tensor, “:” denotes a 2-nd order contraction between tensors, and
\displaystyle\nabla\nabla\phi

\displaystyle\phi

2.E
Variational Regularized Definition of the Crack Normal

The phase-field model that accounts for crack face closure in (
3

\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}({\mathbfit x})
as an essential element; this is required to be able to distinguish the modes in Figure

In principle,
\displaystyle\phi({\mathbfit x})
has sufficient information to obtain
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
simply by computing
\displaystyle\pm\nabla\phi
.
In practice, this leads to severe difficulties related to the numerical evaluation: for instance, the numerical evaluation is unstable near the crack tip, and it is not well-defined in the interior of the crack away from the crack faces
HADMC 22
;
Agr 16

In our previous work
HADMC 22
where this model was formulated, we used
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}({\mathbfit x})
as an independent vector field that replaced the scalar field
\displaystyle\phi({\mathbfit x})

\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
related to the level of fracturing and the orientation provided the crack face normal.
That is, the free energy in

and hence we avoided completely the challenge of computing
\displaystyle\nabla\phi
.
While this worked well in
HADMC 22

Considering the general anisotropic setting, we would account for anisotropy through terms of the form
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}\cdot{\mathbfit A}{\mathbfit d}
and

\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}

as the crack face normal; Figure

\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}

For a given field
\displaystyle\phi({\mathbfit x})
, we define
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
as the minimizer of the functional:

{\mathbfit d}=\argmin_{\mathbfit p}\int_{\Omega}\left(H_{l_{1}}(|\nabla\phi|,g_{m})\left|{\mathbfit p}\otimes{\mathbfit p}-\phi^{2}\widehat{\nabla\phi}\otimes\widehat{\nabla\phi}\right|^{2}+\beta_{1}H_{l_{2}}(\phi,\phi_{m})(|{\mathbfit p}|-\phi)^{2}+\beta_{2}|\nabla{\mathbfit p}|^{2}\right)\ \mathrm{d}\Omega,\quad\text{ given }\phi({\mathbfit x})

(8)

where
\displaystyle H_{l}(x,x_{m})
is a smooth approximation of the step function centered at
\displaystyle x_{m}
, for which we use
\displaystyle H_{l}(x,x_{m})=0.5+0.5\tanh\left(\frac{x-x_{m}}{l}\right)
, and
\displaystyle\widehat{\nabla\phi}
is the unit vector in the direction
\displaystyle\nabla\phi
.

We understand this functional term-by-term below.
As a preliminary, we recall from studies of liquid crystals, e.g.
Eri 91
, that unit vector fields often lead to singularities.
Therefore, our aim is to obtain
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
as the normal in the vicinity of the crack, but allow it to take any other value in the undamaged solid — where the direction of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
is unimportant — to prevent the formation of singularities.
Similarly, since we do not care about the orientation of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
away from the crack, we apply natural boundary conditions to (
8
).
Further, we highlight that we want
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
to not flip as we go from one face of a crack to the other.

Consider the first term in (
8
).
The square term aims to align
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
with
\displaystyle\pm\nabla\phi
on the crack faces, and aims to make the magnitude of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
equal to
\displaystyle\phi
in accord with the second term.
Further, the leading step function aims to enforce this only at high values of
\displaystyle|\nabla\phi|
to select the crack faces, and not within the crack volume or away from the crack.

Consider the second term in (
8
).
The square term aims to set the magnitude of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit d}
proportional to
\displaystyle\phi
, but the leading step function aims to enforce this only within the crack volume.
This ensures that the normal vector does not vanish and “flip around” within the crack volume.

Finally, the third term in (
8
) is a standard regularization to provide some smoothness and prevent singularities in regions such as crack tip or elsewhere.
While this is used to impose regularity, it is kept small.

In (
8
), we use
\displaystyle l_{1}=0.01
,
\displaystyle g_{m}=10
,
\displaystyle l_{2}=0.001
,
\displaystyle\phi_{m}=0.98
,
\displaystyle\beta_{1}=0.8
, and
\displaystyle\beta_{2}=0.001
.
While the specific values are obtained from numerical experiments to get good performance, the heuristics are as follows.
First, the regularization term is small and meant only to preserve smoothness, and hence
\displaystyle\beta_{2}\ll\beta_{1}
.
Second, the first and second contributions are both of relatively equal importance, and hence
\displaystyle\beta_{1}\simeq 1
.
Third, we want
\displaystyle l_{1}\ll 1
,
\displaystyle l_{2}\ll 1
to have rapid transitions in the step function.
Fourth, we use
\displaystyle\phi_{m}
close to
\displaystyle 1
to ensure that only the fractured regions contribute to the second term.
Finally, we use
\displaystyle g_{m}=10
to ensure that only the crack faces contribute to the first term.

2.F
Equilibrium, Evolution, and Irreversibility of Fracture

In the standard approach to phase-field modeling, given the free energy
\displaystyle E[{\mathbfit y},\phi]
, we find the equilibrium state as the configuration that corresponds to the energy-minimizing state, by solving the Euler-Lagrange equations
\displaystyle\updelta_{{\mathbfit y}}E={\bf 0}
and
\displaystyle\updelta_{\phi}E=0
.
Further, evolution — for instance, driven by time-varying external loads — is modeled simply by using the equilibrium equations at all times, when we neglect effects such as inertia and time-dependent material response
AD 17
;
CAB+ 22
;
NWD 21
;
NWD 22
.
Under fairly general conditions, this can be shown to satisfy the constraints of thermodynamics
KMW+ 22
;
PF 90
.
This approach, however, does not permit history-dependence.

In fracture, on the other hand, the growth of cracks is irreversible.
A standard approach to enforce this is as follows.
Given
\displaystyle\phi_{0}
as the solution at a given time, the solution
\displaystyle\phi
for the next time is obtained by minimizing over all admissible fields that satisfy the pointwise constraint that
\displaystyle\phi\geq\phi_{m}
at all points at which
\displaystyle\phi_{0}\geq\phi_{m}
.

While this is generally applied as an inequality constraint, we use the following robust and effective variational penalization.
We augment
\displaystyle E
with a term of the form:

E\to E+m\int_{\Omega}H_{l_{2}}(\phi_{0},\phi_{m})(\phi-\phi_{0})^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\Omega

(9)

where
\displaystyle m
is a large penalty parameter, set as
\displaystyle 4\text{\times}{10}^{5}\text{\,}\mathrm{GPa}
in our calculations.
The regularized step function
\displaystyle H_{l}(\phi_{0},\phi_{m})
is activated only at points where
\displaystyle\phi_{0}\gtrsim\phi_{m}
, and hence the overall penalty forces
\displaystyle\phi
at those points to not evolve further (i.e., neither to decrease nor increase). As stated in the description of (
8
),
\displaystyle l_{2}=0.001
, and
\displaystyle\phi_{m}=0.98
.

Formation of Wing Cracks Under Compressive Loading

Wing cracks in anisotropic materials are tensile cracks that propagate from pre-existing flaws under compressive loads.
They contribute significantly to crack propagation and material failure in various applications, including in rock mechanics
NNH 82
;
PB 10
;
BE 98
.
These cracks typically initiate at the tips of the flaws and grow at an angle to the direction of applied compressive stress.
The propagation of wing cracks is strongly influenced by material anisotropy where the fracture toughness and energy release rates vary with direction.
Under compressive loads in an anisotropic setting, there are complex stress concentrations at flaw tips, and this drives the growth of complex fracture patterns, including secondary shear cracks.
A key aspect of wing cracks is that the compressive stress causes crack face contact. It is essential to account for this appropriately, however standard phase-field fracture models do not distinguish between compressive traction across the crack face that causes contact and compressive traction that is parallel to the crack face
SSK 22
;
HADMC 22
. However, the model proposed in this paper distinguishes between these tractions and is able to appropriately model crack face contact.

Several prior works have applied phase-field fracture mechanics to investigate the formation and propagation of wing cracks
XXH+ 24
;
LWCL 21
;
SDTD 21
;
BS 18
;
ZSVS 17
, and have proven effective in capturing these complex propagation modes, demonstrating good agreement with the expected physical behavior.
However, an important shortcoming in these prior studies is that they have modeled the initial crack as a boundary of the domain.
That is, the initial crack is treated as an external surface and is a geometric feature of the computational domain.
In this work, we are able to treat the initial crack through the damage field
\displaystyle\phi
: our computational domain is a simple rectangle, and the interior crack is prescribed through the initial condition for
\displaystyle\phi
.
This provides an important advantage: namely, by treating the crack as a damaged zone rather than as a special surface, it enables us to model wing cracks in a completely general way.
For instance, if the initial crack was not simply imposed but rather was the outcome of a prior loading, our approach is seamless and does not require any special treatment to then further observe the growth of wing cracks.
On the other hand, in the current method of treating this as an external surface, it would require a much more difficult process of identifying the crack geometry — which can itself be complex — replacing it with an external boundary and so on, which is extremely challenging.
Here, we highlight that our approach is enabled by the crack response model that enables us to obtain the correct crack response under crack face contact in the regularized setting without needing to use a cumbersome description of the crack faces as external surfaces.

Discussion

In this study, we have developed a homogenized phase-field model for fracture that is anisotropic in both elasticity and fracture energy and is able to account for compressive loads across the crack face building on our previous work

We applied this approach to model crack growth in transverse isotropic layered materials under various complex loadings, and are able to capture the significance of anisotropic crack growth when compared to fully-resolved calculations and experimental observations.

We have further studied the important and challenging case of wing cracking.
We have shown that the model is able to capture wing cracking under compression using a phase-field approach.
Unlike prior phase-field models of wing cracks that use an external boundary to model the initial crack, we demonstrate that we can capture this by modeling the initial crack through the phase-field.
This provides much more generality since we do not have to introduce external boundaries within the domain.

We highlight that we have assumed that there is zero friction between crack faces for simplicity.
