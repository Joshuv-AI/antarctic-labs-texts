A minimal-incision and minimal-soft-tissue-injury (MISI) technique for robot-assisted total knee arthroplasty: a preliminary report
Authors: Ho-Jung Jung, Jonggu Shin, Jong-Keun Kim, Peter K. Sculco, Joong-Il Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRobot-assisted total knee arthroplasty (TKA) with functional alignment enables precise bone cuts and soft-tissue preservation; however, array placement often requires additional or extended incisions, which may increase surgical invasiveness. To address these limitations, we developed the Minimal-Incision and Minimal-Soft-Tissue-Injury (MISI) technique, combining robotic precision with true minimally invasive principles.MethodsThe MISI technique utilizes an approximately 10-cm midline incision with a mini-medial parapatellar approach. It allows secure array fixation through the primary incision while applying functional alignment principles to preserve the soft-tissue envelope. Femoral pins are inserted intra-incisionally, and tibial fixation employs a hybrid approach: one intra-incisional pin and a second pin placed through a 5-mm stab incision to reduce skin tension, particularly in flexion. Bone registration and intraoperative planning are performed using the MAKO robotic system within a functional alignment philosophy. All bone cuts are performed using a mobile window technique at 90° flexion, except for the anterior chamfer cut, which is carried out at 120° flexion to allow adequate clearance between the saw blade and the tibial array. This technique was performed in 82 patients, through which we achieved encouraging early wound healing and high patient satisfaction.ConclusionThe MISI technique offers a reproducible, incision-sparing approach to minimize soft-tissue trauma in robotic TKA. Prospective studies evaluating complication rates, recovery, and patient-reported outcomes are warranted to validate its clinical benefits and determine optimal patient selection.

Background

Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) gained significant popularity for its early clinical benefits such as reduced pain and faster recovery [1–3]. Although some studies report acceptable long-term results with MIS-specific implants [4], conventional MIS-TKA has generally fallen out of favor due to narrow indications, technical difficulty, higher complication risks, and lack of long-term advantages [5–7].

With the advent of robot-assisted TKA and personalized alignment, greater emphasis has been placed on preserving the native soft-tissue integrity [8, 9]. Although personalized alignment with robotic systems has demonstrated superior early clinical outcomes compared with conventional TKA, several challenges remain. Specifically, the installation of tracking arrays typically necessitates either additional incisions for extra-incisional pin placement or the extension of the primary incision for intra-incisional pin placement. These requirements may be associated with pin-site-related complications and wound healing problems, potentially increasing surgical invasiveness and offsetting the benefits of soft-tissue preservation [10, 11].

To address these limitations, we developed the Minimal-Incision and Minimal-Soft-Tissue-Injury (MISI) technique. This approach uses approximately 10-centimeter incision, enables secure array fixation through a small primary incision with minimal additional dissection, and applies functional alignment while preserving the soft-tissue envelope. Through these principles, the MISI technique aims to integrate the precision of robotic TKA with the fundamental objectives of MIS-TKA.

Methods

Patient selection

Between June 2025 and December 2025, this technique was performed in 82 consecutive patients who met the inclusion criteria. Indications for the MISI technique include non-obese patients with body mass index (BMI) < 30 kg/m, patients with a non-muscular habitus (mostly females) and a preoperative knee range of motion greater than 90°. Contraindications include a fragile or compromised skin envelope, marked obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2), patella baja.

Surgical technique

Preoperative planning

Preoperative planning

Preoperative planning

Preoperative computed tomography (CT) scans (Siemens Somatom, Siemens Medical Solutions, USA) are obtained for the MAKO Robotic Arm Interactive Orthopaedic System (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) to construct a three-dimensional model of individual anatomy, and to plan implant positioning within a functional alignment philosophy.

2.Surgical approach

2.Surgical approach

Surgical approach

A midline skin incision of approximately 10 cm is made, with the proximal extent one fingerbreadth above the superior pole of the patella and the distal extent reaching the lower border of the tibial tubercle. (Fig. 1) A mini-medial parapatellar approach is then performed using a mobile window technique, which involves the dynamic mobilization of the skin incision to provide adequate visualization of different joint compartments. This is achieved by applying gentle traction to the operative side and controlled release on the opposite side to balance tension across the small incision. After arthrotomy, preliminary patellar resurfacing is performed if necessary to improve exposure. The anterior cruciate ligament is resected from the intercondylar notch, whereas the posterior cruciate ligament is preserved whenever possible for the CR implant.

3.Bone pin and array placement

3.Bone pin and array placement

Bone pin and array placement

For robotic registration, 3.2-mm array bone pins are applied to minimize additional soft-tissue injury. The femoral pins are inserted through the primary incision without any additional exposure. The first femoral pin is placed 3 mm proximal and 3 mm anterior to the medial epicondyle, directed approximately 20° distally and 20° posteriorly to avoid interference with the femoral cutting plane and to decrease skin tension. (Fig. 2A) Using the pin sleeve, the second pin is inserted with a proximal entry point and a trajectory parallel to the first to achieve stable fixation. (Fig. 2B) Femoral checkpoints are placed around the medial epicondyle, and the femoral array is then mounted on the bone pins. Using a hybrid technique, the first tibial pin is inserted at the lowest point of the primary incision (Fig. 3A), whereas the second pin is placed parallel to the first through a distal 5-mm stab incision made at the sleeve-defined point, allowing extra-incisional fixation and further minimizing soft-tissue violation. (Fig. 3B) Instead of a standard tibial array, a modified long array—originally designed as a pelvic array for robot-assisted hip arthroplasty—was utilized, as its increased length and more distal orientation effectively prevents mechanical interference with the robotic arm during bone preparation through a limited incision. Tibial checkpoints are then placed according to the standard protocol.

4.Bone registration

4.Bone registration

Bone registration

After array and checkpoint placement, bone registration and verification are performed to confirm the accuracy of the CT-based model and establish real-time tracking of the femur and tibia. Following registration, mediolateral balance is evaluated according to the preoperative plan, with the extension gap measured in slight flexion (approximately 10°) and the flexion gap assessed between 85° and 105° of knee flexion. Intraoperative planning is then refined on the MAKO system in accordance with the functional alignment philosophy, which aims to preserve the soft-tissue envelope, as previously described.

5.Bone preparation

5.Bone preparation

Bone preparation

During bone preparation, visualization is maintained using the previously described mobile window technique. All bone resections are performed with the MAKO robotic system according to the intraoperative final plan. With the knee flexed to approximately 90°, the distal femoral, posterior femoral, posterior chamfer, and proximal tibial cuts can be made without interference from either the femoral or tibial array (Fig. 4A). However, during the anterior chamfer resection, the proximally positioned tibial array may obstruct the saw blade. To avoid this, the anterior chamfer cut is performed with the knee flexed to approximately 120°, allowing adequate clearance between the saw blade and the tibial array (Fig. 4B).

6.Implantation

6.Implantation

Implantation

Femoral and tibial trial implants are inserted (Fig. 5), and gap balancing is re-evaluated. The appropriate polyethylene insert thickness is selected to ensure balanced gaps in both extension and flexion. After copious irrigation is performed, cemented implantation is carried out using standard technique.

7.Postoperative rehabilitation and wound check

7.Postoperative rehabilitation and wound check

Postoperative rehabilitation and wound check

On postoperative day (POD) 1, patients begin ambulation with a walker and continue ankle pumps and basic isometrics. From POD 2, continuous passive motion (CPM) is initiated and range of motion (ROM) is advanced as tolerated, alongside gait training. After discharge, a follow-up at POD 4 weeks is scheduled to assess ROM progression and wound status. (Fig. 6)

Discussion

Initially introduced in the early 2000s, MIS-TKA gained rapid popularity due to its promise of reduced pain, faster recovery, and improved early outcomes through smaller incisions and less soft-tissue disruption [1–3, 12, 13]. However, as high-quality evidence accumulated, it became clear that MIS-TKA failed to demonstrate significant long-term advantages over conventional surgery [7, 14, 15]. Moreover, conventional MIS-TKA remained excessively demanding; its steep learning curve, limited visualization, and restricted working space contributed to higher error rates, implant-related complications, and wound problems, leading to a decline in its popularity [4, 6, 7]. In this context, the integration of robot-assisted TKA with personalized alignment now enables genuine native soft-tissue preservation and helps overcome prior technical challenges, making a truly minimally invasive approach more attainable [16].

For femoral pin insertion, traditional intra-incisional placement required extending the main incision too long and causing soft-tissue damage, whereas extra-incisional placement necessitated additional incision on thigh and injured surrounding soft tissues, including the vastus medialis. Moreover, multiple attempts and transcortical pin trajectories at the femoral diaphysis, often encountered with extra-incisional femoral fixation, are recognized as risk factors for pin-related periprosthetic fractures. In our technique, two intra-incisional femoral pins are placed parallel with specific angulation just anterior and proximal to the medial femoral epicondyle, avoiding interference with bone cuts and potentially reducing the risk of pin-related periprosthetic fractures.

Traditionally, tibial pin placement using an extra-incisional technique has been associated with complications such as fracture, oozing and superficial infection [11, 17–19]. In contrast, intra-incisional placement of tibial pins can increase skin tension, particularly when both femoral and tibial pins are positioned within the primary incision, which results in elevated tension during knee flexion and increases the risk of skin laceration or necrosis [10, 20, 21]. Our hybrid pin technique may reduce these risks by placing one pin intra-incisionally and the other extra-incisionally, thereby decreasing fracture risk, wound complications, and skin tension during the 90°–120° of flexion required for bone preparation. The extra-incisional pin can be inserted through a 0.5-cm stab incision without suturing, minimizing soft-tissue trauma.

Notably, traditional extra-incisional pin placement in the diaphysis has been recognized as a significant stress riser, with pin-related periprosthetic fracture rates reported to be as high as 4.8% [22]. To address this concern, recent literature has demonstrated the safety of all-intra-incisional pin placement, reporting remarkably low fracture rates of 0.09% to 0.3% [19, 23, 24]. However, accommodating all tracker pins within the primary incision often necessitates incision lengthening to maintain adequate visualization. In contrast, the MISI technique is performed through a minimal midline incision approximately 10 cm. Our clinical experience with 82 consecutive cases demonstrates the short-term safety and feasibility of the MISI technique. During the follow-up period, no wound-healing–related complications were observed, even with the strictly limited incision of approximately 10 cm. Although longer follow-up is required for definitive evaluation, no cases of pin-related periprosthetic fractures were identified. Taken together, these refinements enhance procedural safety while maintaining the fundamental principles of MIS-TKA.

There are some remaining limitations. First, the technique may involve a considerable learning curve and could be technically demanding for some surgeons. However, because robotic assistance combined with the functional alignment philosophy enables consistent soft-tissue preservation regardless of the surgeon’s experience, we believe that once the learning curve associated with the mobile window technique is overcome, the MISI approach can be readily adopted. Second, because the indication for this approach cannot be applied to all patients, further studies are needed to determine which patient populations are most suitable. Proper patient selection may help maximize its advantages. Third, while this issue was not observed in our cohort, intra-incisional femoral pins may be unstable due to their proximity to the femoral bone cuts. In addition, in knees with smaller femoral dimensions, interference with the femoral box may occur in posterior-stabilized designs despite angulation; therefore, caution is required. Fourth, although the technique minimizes soft-tissue trauma, its clinical benefits remain to be confirmed in future studies.

In conclusion, the MISI technique overcomes several disadvantages and limitations of conventional MIS-TKA and robot-assisted TKA. By combining an incision-sparing approach with minimal soft-tissue disruption, it addresses previous limitations and provides a safer and more practical method for achieving robot-assisted MIS-TKA.
