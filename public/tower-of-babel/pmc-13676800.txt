Serum Baclofen Concentrations in Four Dogs With Baclofen Toxicosis Treated With In‐Series Carbon Hemoperfusion and Hemodialysis
Authors: Amanda Spillane, Jiwoong Her, Nahvid Etedali, Joanna Finstad, Catherine Langston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo report serum baclofen concentrations before, during, and after in‐series carbon hemoperfusion (cHP) and hemodialysis (HD) in four dogs with severe baclofen toxicosis.Series SummaryThis series involves four dogs with serum samples collected before, during, and after in‐series cHP/HD. The extraction ratio (ER, %) of cHP and HD, half‐life during cHP/HD, and total extracorporeal elimination constant were calculated. Posttreatment samples were collected to determine endogenous half‐life and intrinsic elimination constant. Two dogs received both cHP/HD and mechanical ventilation, while the other two received cHP/HD alone. The range of ingested dosages was 4–24 mg/kg. The median time from suspected ingestion to cHP/HD initiation was 7 h (range: 5–15 h). Extracorporeal therapy was performed using an intermittent hemodialysis platform in two cases and a continuous renal replacement therapy platform in two cases. The median duration of cHP/HD was 3 h (range: 2.2–4 h), with a median total blood volume processed of 1.3 L/kg (range: 1–1.5 L/kg), equating to 15 times (range: 12–18.2 times) the estimated blood volume (80 mL/kg) in dogs. Serum baclofen concentration was reduced during cHP/HD by a median 69% (range: 46%–80%). The median ER for cHP was 61% (range: 60%–81%) and for HD was 4% (range: −0.5% to 11.5%). The calculated half‐life during cHP/HD was 1.9 h (range: 1.1–2.5 h), compared with 5.8 h (range: 3.1–13.6 h) after cHP/HD. The median extracorporeal elimination constant was 0.34 (range: 0.08–0.37), which was 2.8 times faster than the intrinsic elimination constant of 0.12 (range: 0.05–0.23).New or Unique Information ProvidedIn‐series cHP/HD reduced serum baclofen concentrations in this small series of dogs, highlighting its potential role in the treatment of severe baclofen intoxication.

Introduction

Baclofen (4‐amino‐3‐[p‐chlorophenyl]butyric acid) is a centrally acting skeletal muscle relaxant used in people for the treatment of muscle spasticity. Its mechanism of action is agonism of γ‐aminobutyric acid‐B receptors, which reduces calcium influx into presynaptic nerve terminals, decreasing excitatory neurotransmitter release within the spinal cord and brain [1, 2]. Baclofen is rapidly and completely absorbed after oral administration, reaching peak plasma concentration within 2–3 h [3]. It has a half‐life of 2–6 h in people and is predominantly eliminated unchanged in urine [1, 3, 4].

In dogs, baclofen intoxication typically occurs through the accidental ingestion of an owner's medication, with clinical signs reported at dosages as low as 0.7 mg/kg and fatalities at 2.3 mg/kg [5, 6]. Neurologic signs predominate, including CNS depression, ataxia, vocalization, agitation, lethargy, weakness, coma, tremors, miosis, seizures, and blindness. Other clinical signs include vomiting, regurgitation, hypersalivation, diarrhea, general malaise, bradycardia or tachycardia, hypothermia, arrhythmias, hypotension or hypertension, respiratory depression, tachypnea, pulmonary edema, and urinary incontinence [6].

The traditional therapy for baclofen intoxication includes induction of emesis (if recently ingested and the patient is not neurologically impaired) or gastric lavage, intravenous lipid emulsion (ILE), single‐dose oral activated charcoal with a cathartic, and supportive care, including provision of ventilatory support and anticonvulsant medication as indicated [5, 6]. Because it is small (213 Daltons) with minimal protein binding (30%) and a low volume of distribution (0.7 L/kg), baclofen is an ideal drug for extracorporeal toxin removal (ECTR) [7]. Hemodialysis (HD) alone [8, 9, 10] and in‐series activated charcoal hemoperfusion (HP) [11] have been described in previous reports of baclofen toxicosis; however, no studies reporting the efficacy of activated carbon HP/HD (cHP/HD) have been previously published. Activated carbon HP can be performed alone and may be beneficial if HD is not available. This case series reports the use of cHP/HD for severe baclofen toxicosis in four dogs, providing serum baclofen concentrations before, during, and after ECTR.

Case 1

A 1.5‐year‐old intact female mixed‐breed dog weighing 26.2 kg was presented to The Ohio State University for vomiting and unresponsiveness after ingesting baclofen tablets 1 h earlier (estimated ingested dose: 24 mg/kg). On presentation, the dog was laterally recumbent, comatose, bradycardic (heart rate: 50/min), hypopneic (respiratory rate: 6/min), mildly hypothermic (rectal temperature: 37.6°C [99.7°F]), and normotensive, with severe ptyalism, generalized muscle flaccidity, bilateral miosis, absent menace response, and decreased pupillary light reflexes. The dog became apneic, was endotracheally intubated1, and received manual positive pressure ventilation. Mechanical ventilation (MV) was started using synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation2 in pressure‐control mode, with ventilator settings and monitoring performed as appropriate for a patient undergoing MV. Intralipid3 (1.5 mL/kg, IV, and 0.25 mL/kg/min, constant rate infusion [CRI]) was administered and was discontinued after 15 min in favor of performing emergent ECTR to enhance baclofen elimination. Immediately before ECTR, a grand mal seizure lasting 30 s occurred, for which midazolam4 (0.5 mg/kg, IV) and propofol5 (0.76 mg/kg, IV) were administered.

A double‐lumen temporary dialysis catheter6 was placed aseptically into the right external jugular vein via percutaneous modified Seldinger technique. In‐series cHP/HD was performed on a continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) platform7 , 8, with a cHP column9 added to the circuit10 in a predialyzer configuration (Figure 1; Table 1). The treatment was prescribed to process approximately 15 blood volumes through the cHP column (estimated blood volume: 80 mL/kg). Unfractionated heparin11 was administered to target an activated clotting time (ACT) of 160–200 s. In‐series cHP/HD was initiated 5 h after baclofen ingestion, achieving a 72% reduction in serum baclofen concentration (Table 2; Figure 2).

Case 2

A 10‐month‐old neutered female Labrador Retriever weighing 24 kg was presented to The Ohio State for neurologic signs after ingestion of baclofen tablets 3 h earlier (estimated ingested dose: 12–16 mg/kg). On presentation, the dog was laterally recumbent, comatose, mildly hypothermic (rectal temperature: 36.5°C [97.7°F]), and normotensive, with generalized muscle flaccidity, bilaterally myotic pupils, and absent segmental reflexes. The dog had markedly shallow respiratory excursions, became apneic, was endotracheally intubated1, and received manual positive pressure ventilation. MV was started with synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation2 in volume‐control mode. Ventilator settings were adjusted and monitoring was performed as appropriate for a patient undergoing MV. Intralipid3 (1.5 mL/kg, IV, and 0.25 mL/kg/min, CRI) was administered for 1 h.

After initiation of MV, ECTR was used to enhance the elimination of baclofen. A double‐lumen dialysis catheter18 was placed using the same technique as in Case 1. In‐series cHP/HD was performed using the same machine7,8, circuit8,9, and anticoagulation11 as for Case 1 (Figure 1; Table 1). In‐series cHP/HD was initiated 5.5 h after baclofen ingestion, achieving a 68.2% reduction in serum baclofen concentration (Table 2; Figure 3). During ECTR, a nasogastric tube16 was placed to administer 120 mL activated charcoal19. One hour after ECTR was initiated, the dog became hypotensive and received two IV fluid boluses of 10 mL/kg balanced electrolyte solution20, followed by norepinephrine13 (0.2 µg/kg/min, CRI). The dog experienced ventricular tachycardia and a suspected seizure event during ECTR and was administered lidocaine21 (2 mg/kg, IV, twice, followed by 50 µg/kg/min, CRI), magnesium sulfate22 (0.3 mEq/kg, IV), and midazolam4 (0.5 mg/kg, IV, followed by 0.5 mg/kg/h, CRI).

After completion of ECTR, the dog required an additional 10.5 h of MV. The dog was extubated 26 h after presentation and discharged 76 h after presentation.

Case 3

A 1‐year‐old intact male mixed‐breed dog weighing 30.1 kg was presented to a local emergency hospital for neurologic signs after ingestion of an unknown medication 3–4 h earlier. Although the ingested medication was initially suspected to be acetaminophen or naproxen, it was later confirmed to be baclofen (estimated ingested dose: 7 mg/kg). On presentation, the dog was mentally inappropriate, pyrexic (rectal temperature: 40.3°C [104.5°F]), tachycardic (heart rate: 190/min), and normotensive, with generalized ataxia. Emesis was induced with apomorphine24 (0.02 mg/kg, IV), and the dog was then transferred to the Schwarzman Animal Medical Center for hospitalization and supportive care. Upon presentation, the dog was laterally recumbent, obtunded, normothermic, and persistently tachycardic (heart rate: 160/min), with inappropriate vocalization, bilaterally absent menace response, anisocoria, and urinary incontinence. The dog was administered butorphanol25 (0.3 mg/kg, IV), a 4‐mL/kg hypertonic saline26 bolus, and a 17‐mL/kg bolus of balanced electrolyte solution19. Given the dog's abnormal neurologic status, it was decided to pursue ECTR.

A double‐lumen dialysis catheter18 was placed using the same technique as for Case 1. In‐series cHP/HD was performed on an intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) platform27 , 28, with a cHP column29 added to the circuit30 in a predialyzer31 configuration (Figure 1; Table 1). The treatment was prescribed to process approximately 12 blood volumes through the cHP column, and systemic heparinization11 was performed as for Case 1. In‐series cHP/HD was initiated 15 h after baclofen ingestion, achieving a 46% reduction in serum baclofen concentration (Table 2; Figure 4).

By the next morning (approximately 24 h after completion of ECTR), the dog's neurologic signs had resolved, and the patient was discharged.

Case 4

A 1‐year‐old neutered female Brittany Spaniel weighing 13.7 kg was presented to a local emergency hospital for vomiting 1 h after ingestion of baclofen (estimated ingested dose: 4 mg/kg). The dog initially appeared clinically normal but became mentally inappropriate with generalized ataxia. After receiving maropitant (1 mg/kg, IV), ondansetron (0.4 mg/kg, IV), ILE (200 mL), and a bolus of balanced electrolyte solution (10 mL/kg, IV), the dog was transferred to Ohio State for ECTR. Upon presentation, the dog was obtunded, tachycardic (heart rate: 180/min), tachypneic (respiratory rate: 40/min), and hypertensive (oscillometric blood pressure: 185/125 mm Hg, MAP 147 mm Hg), with generalized muscle flaccidity, bilaterally miotic pupils, and absent segmental reflexes.

A double‐lumen dialysis catheter32 was placed using the same technique as in Case 1. In‐series cHP/HD was performed on an IHD platform27,32, with a cHP column33 added to the circuit34 in a predialyzer35 configuration (Figure 1; Table 1). Systemic heparinization11 was performed as for Case 1. The treatment was prescribed to process approximately 20 blood volumes through the cHP column. In‐series cHP/HD was initiated 8 h after baclofen ingestion, achieving an 80% reduction in serum baclofen concentration (Table 2; Figure 5).

Sample Collection and Handling (Cases 1–4)

During ECTR, heparinized blood was sampled pre‐carbon, post‐carbon, and post‐dialyzer at approximately 40‐ to 60‐min intervals in Cases 1, 2, and 4, and at approximately every two blood volumes in Case 3 (Figure 1). The samples were placed in serum separator tubes36 and centrifuged, and serum was frozen at −80°C until analysis was performed. The samples were shipped on ice to Michigan State University Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory (for Cases 2–4) or The Ohio State University (for Case 1), where serum baclofen concentrations (ng/mL) were determined via liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry.

Discussion

This case series outlines the potential benefit of cHP/HD in the management of severe baclofen toxicosis in four dogs and reports on their serum baclofen kinetics. Estimated ingested doses of the dogs ranged from 4 to 24 mg/kg, within the range reported to cause toxicosis [6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 14, 15, 16, 17]. A lower survival rate in dogs has been associated with ingestion of higher dosages of baclofen [6], highlighting the need for rapid and effective treatment.

Baclofen is the second most common toxin removed with ECTR [14], yet data pertaining to its kinetics during overdose and extracorporeal removal remain scarce [8, 10, 11]. Furthermore, the single report on in‐series HP/HD [11] was performed using an activated charcoal column, which is no longer readily available. Activated cHP offers improved hemocompatibility, decreased leaching and inflammation, and increased surface area available for adsorption [18]. Additionally, use of a CRRT platform has not been described in previous reports [8, 9, 10, 11]. This type of platform may be advantageous for patients also receiving MV, as ECTR can be performed in the ICU, which can optimize staffing and patient care. Most dogs tolerated in‐series cHP/HD well, although hypotension occurred in Cases 1 and 2, likely related to the extracorporeal circuit volume (>15%) or baclofen toxicosis itself.

After cHP/HD, dogs had a 45.5%–79.5% reduction in serum baclofen concentration. Most baclofen was removed via the cHP column, with the median ER ranging from 60% to 80.5%, in contrast to the median ER from HD of −0.5% to 11.5%. This is likely due to the cHP column being placed upstream of the dialyzer, resulting in a lower concentration gradient for baclofen removal through HD, as ER from HD was relatively low on both IHD and CRRT platforms. However, the post‐carbon baclofen concentration for Case 1 was markedly higher than the other cases, yet the ER from HD was low. Inefficient baclofen removal likely occurred because of the low dialysate flow rate of the CRRT platform [19], since previous studies have demonstrated effective baclofen removal with HD on an IHD platform [9, 10]. It is unclear why there was a negative baclofen ER in Case 1 using HD, but possible explanations include sample analytical error and sample handling errors. In Cases 2 and 4, cHP/HD shortened the baclofen serum elimination half‐life from 13.6 and 5.8 h to 1.1 and 1.4 h, respectively. This reduction in elimination constant resulted in baclofen being cleared 6.6 and 3.1 times faster than intrinsic elimination, calculated based on serum concentrations after ECTR, although it should be noted that elimination rate differs based on drug concentration [20]. This highlights the benefit of cHP/HD for severe baclofen toxicosis, as faster baclofen clearance likely results in shorter hospitalization and MV time (if required).

Conversely, the serum baclofen elimination half‐life and elimination constants showed opposing results for Case 1, despite achieving a 72.3% reduction in serum baclofen concentration. Figure 2 also demonstrates a plateau in pre‐carbon serum baclofen concentration during cHP/HD, despite post‐carbon serum baclofen concentration being markedly lower, indicating continued baclofen extraction via the cHP column. Normal pharmacokinetic principles may not apply during drug overdoses [21], and it is possible that baclofen underwent more significant extravascular redistribution than described by its low volume of distribution of 0.7 L/kg [7].

Another explanation is continued absorption of baclofen from the gastrointestinal tract. These mechanisms may result in an artificially elevated intrinsic clearance constant or depressed extracorporeal elimination constant. The calculated constants for Case 1 may not be reliable representations of clearance. The rapid decrease in serum baclofen concentration before cHP/HD in Cases 1 and 2 may also be due to extravascular redistribution, as both patients were clinically worsening during this time, highlighting the importance of beginning ECTR as soon as possible. Furthermore, Cases 1 and 2 remained intubated and on MV for multiple hours after ECTR, possibly because baclofen was bound at the target site and unavailable for ECTR. A longer cHP/HD session may have been beneficial in those cases. A limitation of the current case series is the use of a single‐pool model, which may oversimplify the pharmacokinetics occurring in patients with massive overdose.

For Case 3, serum samples were collected during cHP/HD at prespecified blood volumes, as opposed to set time intervals. As the blood pump speed (mL/min) may be variable during the treatment, this may be an advantageous way to represent data when compared with sampling at specific time intervals. When collecting samples for toxin analysis, it is important to record the instantaneous blood flow rate and collect posttreatment blood samples, as ER, baclofen half‐life after cHP/HD, and elimination half‐lives could not be calculated for Case 3. Moreover, for Case 2, there were three outlier data points that were not included in the solid trend line (Figure 3), possibly due to sample handling or analytical variation.

The current case series demonstrates that in‐series cHP/HD is effective at decreasing serum baclofen concentration, highlighting its role in the treatment of severe baclofen intoxication. It is unknown whether HD alone versus in‐series cHP/HD is more efficient at baclofen removal. In‐series cHP/HD was performed as the treating institutions had both modalities available, and a renal replacement platform was desired for the patients that concurrently underwent MV so they could remain in the ICU. Given that all patients with pharmacokinetic data had ongoing baclofen removal at the end of treatment and remained neurologically impaired, a longer treatment time is recommended for future patients. A higher dialysate flow rate is also likely to be beneficial. If the patient is not undergoing MV or can be managed on MV outside of an ICU, in‐series cHP/HD on an IHD platform is reasonable. Future prospective studies comparing cHP alone, HD alone, and in‐series cHP/HD, with larger sample sizes and standardized sampling protocols, should be conducted to determine the optimal modality and duration of ECTR for baclofen toxicosis.
