Wastewater-Based Surveillance of Human Papillomavirus Genotype Diversity
Authors: Pauline Michon, Nicolas Cluzel, Yvon Maday, Jean-Luc Prétet, Sabrina Guérin, Rose Lecat, Thomas Thiébault, Vincent Maréchal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionCan wastewater-based epidemiology monitor population-level circulation of human papillomavirus (HPV) genotypes?FindingsIn this 1-year cross-sectional study of wastewater from the Paris, France, metropolitan area, 27 HPV genotypes were detected, with a median of 6 genotypes per sample. HPV-66, HPV-6, and HPV-53 were the most frequently detected genotypes, whereas HPV-16 and HPV-18 were detected less often.MeaningThese findings suggest that wastewater-based epidemiology can monitor HPV genotype circulation at the population level and complement existing surveillance to improve understanding of HPV epidemiology and support public health decision-making.

Introduction

Human papillomaviruses (HPVs) are responsible for more than 30% of infection-attributable cancers worldwide.1 Although most HPV infections are transient and asymptomatic, persistent infection can lead to cervical cancer, the principal HPV-associated malignant neoplasm, as well as anogenital cancers (anus, penis, vagina, and vulva) and oropharyngeal cancers.2 Cervical cancer remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality, with more than 660 000 new cases and nearly 350 000 deaths annually.3 In France, about 6400 cancers are linked to HPV infections every year, of which approximately 25% concern men.4 HPVs are nonenveloped viruses with circular double-stranded DNA of approximately 8000 base pairs belonging to the Papillomaviridae family, differing in tissue tropism, prevalence, and oncogenic potential.5 Mucosal HPVs belong primarily to the Alphapapillomavirus genus and are classified as high-risk (HR) or low-risk (LR) according to their association with malignant or benign lesions. On the basis of epidemiological evidence, the International Agency for Research on Cancer has categorized several HR-HPV genotypes as carcinogenic (group 1), probable carcinogenic (group 2A), or possible carcinogenic (group 2B).6 Seventeen genotypes (HPV-16, HPV-18, HPV-26, HPV-31, HPV-33, HPV-35, HPV-39, HPV-45,HPV-51, HPV-52, HPV-56, HPV-58, HPV-59, HPV-68, HPV-69, HPV-73, and HPV-82) are currently considered causal agents of invasive cervical cancer,7 whereas LR-HPV types such as HPV-6, HPV-11, HPV-42, and HPV-43 are predominantly involved in condylomas and nonmalignant lesions.8

In France, 3 prophylactic HPV vaccines have been available over time: a quadrivalent vaccine (HPV-6, HPV-11, HPV-16, and HPV-18) commercially available since 2007, a bivalent vaccine (HPV-16 and HPV-18) available since 2010, and a nonavalent vaccine (HPV-6, HPV-11, HPV-16, HPV-18, HPV-31, HPV-33, HPV-45, HPV-52, and HPV-58) available since 2018 and progressively replacing the quadrivalent vaccine in the national immunization program.9 HPV vaccination has been recommended for girls since 2007 and extended to boys aged 11 to 14 years in 2021, with catch-up vaccination now possible up to age 26 years. HPV vaccination coverage among 15-year-old girls increased to 45% in 2023; however, this level remains markedly below the 80% target set by France’s 2021 to 2030 national cancer control strategy.4 In France, cervical cancer screening combines cytology for women aged 25 to 29 years and HR-HPV testing from age 30 years onward, yet extensive genotyping is not routinely performed.10 Although it is estimated that 70% to 85% of adults will contract at least 1 HPV infection during their lifetime,11 the diversity of circulating HPV genotypes is only partially documented, with limited information on younger populations, unscreened women, and men. Large-scale epidemiological studies would be required to comprehensively characterize HPV genotype diversity at the population level, but such studies are costly, time-consuming, and most often restricted to screened women. There is, therefore, a need for complementary and cost-efficient epidemiological strategies capable of characterizing the diversity of HPV genotypes circulating within the general population. Such an approach is particularly valuable for evaluating the impact of vaccination campaigns on the prevalence of vaccine-targeted genotypes, as well as for detecting nonvaccine genotypes that may increase in frequency under vaccination pressure.

Because of their presence in vaginal secretions, urine, feces, and saliva,12,13,14 HPV can be detected in wastewater, albeit at very low concentrations.15,16 The diversity of HPV in wastewater has been previously analyzed by untargeted sequencing, with or without prior enrichment.17,18,19,20,21,22,23 Nontargeted sequencing approaches may lack sensitivity since only a few HPVs of the Alphapapillomavirus genus were detected. Owing to the low HPV quantity in wastewater, amplicon sequencing techniques based on broad-spectrum nested polymerase chain reaction (PCR) have been proposed to specifically investigate the diversity of HPVs.15,24,25,26 However, these PCRs do not amplify all genotypes equally, which can lead to a biased assessment of their relative abundance in wastewater. To our knowledge, only one team has used a Luminex-based multiplex platform for high-throughput analysis of HPVs, but only HPV-6, HPV-16, and HPV-18 were detected, suggesting that this technique was not sensitive enough to account for the diversity of circulating HPVs.27 Importantly, the lack of standardization across these techniques limits the comparability of results between studies, and these approaches most often rely on sequencing and bioinformatics infrastructures that are not universally accessible.

In this study, we combined quantitative and qualitative strategies to assess temporal trends in HPV viral load and genotypic diversity in wastewater samples collected twice weekly from a large Parisian wastewater treatment plant between January 2024 and January 2025. A broad-spectrum quantitative PCR (qPCR) was developed to quantify the HPV load, with normalization based on human excretion markers (CrAssphage and mitochondrial DNA [mtDNA]) and the flow rate of the wastewater treatment plant. Targeted detection of 28 HR-HPV and LR-HPV types was performed using a commercially available multiplex PCR assay. Through high-frequency sampling and optimized extraction and detection processes, we demonstrate the feasibility of monitoring the ecological diversity of HR-HPV and LR-HPV in wastewater as an indicator of circulating HPV genotypes in the general population. This approach could complement existing surveillance systems by enabling inclusive (men, women, and all age groups), noninvasive, and cost-effective monitoring of HPV diversity at the population level, particularly in the context of vaccination.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This longitudinal, cross-sectional wastewater-based epidemiology study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines.28 Raw wastewater was collected twice weekly from January 24, 2024, to January 29, 2025, at a wastewater treatment plant serving approximately 1 million population equivalents in the Paris, France, metropolitan area. As this study analyzed pooled wastewater samples without human participants or identifiable personal data, ethics approval was not required, as confirmed by an institutional ethics committee representative.

Laboratory Analyses

Wastewater samples were concentrated and nucleic acids extracted using a standardized protocol. HPVs of the Alphapapillomavirus genus were quantified using a broad-spectrum qPCR assay (SPF10-WW) and normalized using crAssphage and mtDNA. HPV genotyping was performed using the Allplex HPV28 multiplex PCR assay. Detailed laboratory protocols are provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

Statistical Analysis

The level of statistical significance was set to 5% (P < .05). All statistical tests were 2-sided. Longitudinal viral concentrations were smoothed using a bayesian model accounting for sampling variability, measurement error, and censoring due to detection limits. A detailed description of this model is provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1. Detection reproducibility was evaluated with McNemar tests (mid-P method); quantification reproducibility was evaluated using Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for censored data. Bootstrap resampling assessed the impact of reduced sampling frequency on genotype detection. HPV concentrations normalized by crAssphage were compared across full and subsampled datasets using the root mean squared error and the cover rate, which compares the 95% credible interval of the signals. Analyses were conducted using custom scripts in Python software version 3.9.18 (Python Foundation).29

Results

High-Frequency Monitoring of HPVs in Wastewater

A total of 100 raw wastewater samples, collected twice weekly in Paris between January 24, 2024, and January 29, 2025, were analyzed for HPV DNA. A broad-spectrum primer set (SPF10-WW), derived from the previously described SPF10 primers,30 was developed to improve amplification across HPV genotypes from wastewater.

HPV genomes were detected in all samples, with a mean concentration of 7.3 × 103 genome equivalents (GE)/L (range, 7.3 × 101 to 3.0 × 105 GE/L) (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). To limit confounding arising from extraction variability, PCR inhibition, or fluctuations in the human population served by the wastewater system, CrAssphage DNA and human mtDNA were quantified in parallel as normalization markers.31,32 After mathematical smoothing of raw data, CrAssphage and mtDNA concentrations exhibited similar temporal patterns (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). Daily HPV loads, obtained by multiplying concentrations by wastewater flow rates, showed moderate fluctuations over time (Figure 1).

Measurement of HPV Genotype Detection Frequency

HPV genotype diversity was assessed using a semiquantitative multiplex qPCR (Allplex HPV28 Detection). Reproducibility was evaluated by analyzing samples both immediately after extraction and after 1 month of storage at −80 °C. A genotype was considered present if detected in at least 1 replicate. Genotype detection frequency (eTable 2 in Supplement 1) and relative abundance (eTable 3 and eFigure 3 in Supplement 1) were evaluated, with the latter restricted to genotypes detected in both replicates. Except for HPV-43, HPV-59, HPV-73, and HPV-82, detection frequencies did not differ significantly between fresh and stored extracts. However, since the number of discordant pairs was lower than 10 for 13 genotypes, these results should be interpreted with caution, given the resulting low statistical power. Storage had a measurable negative association with quantification of HPV-73, HPV-39, and HPV-43, but no significant association with HPV-6, HPV-11, HPV-16, HPV-42, HPV-44, HPV-53, HPV-56, HPV-59, HPV-66, or HPV-68. Interpretation remains limited for several genotypes due to high proportions of censored data (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). For instance, fewer than 10 samples were jointly quantified for HPV-11, HPV-16, HPV-44, HPV-59, and HPV-68. Although a large effect size (Cohen d, –2.1) was observed across the 6 jointly quantified HPV-16 samples, this difference disappeared when the full dataset was considered (Cohen d, –0.1), reflecting the low detection rate across replicates.

A diverse range of HPVs was detected throughout the year, with 1 to 12 genotypes simultaneously identified in both replicates (median, 6 genotypes) (Figure 2; eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). The detection rate was defined for each genotype as the proportion of positive samples in which a given genotype was detected at least once. The 95% CIs for detection rates were estimated using a bootstrap resampling approach. On the basis of detection frequency, genotypes were classified into 3 statistically distinct groups. HPV-66 (97%; 95% CI, 93%-100%), HPV-53 (89%; 95% CI, 83%-95%), and HPV-6 (93%; 95% CI, 88%-97%) were detected in nearly 90% of samples (Figure 3A and eFigure 4 in Supplement 1). A second group, including HPV-39 (72%; 95% CI, 63%-81%), HPV-73 (69%; 95% CI, 60%-78%), HPV-42 (60%; 95% CI, 50%-70%), HPV-56 (58%; 95% CI, 48%-68%), and HPV-43 (55%; 95% CI, 45%-64%), was detected in more than one-half of the samples. Notably, among genotypes targeted by the quadrivalent and nonavalent vaccines used in France, only HPV-6 was frequently detected. Two high-risk carcinogenic genotypes not covered by the vaccines, HPV-39 and HPV-56, were also detected at high frequency. The remaining types were detected in fewer than 30% of samples—including HPV-59 (27%; 95% CI, 19%-35%), HPV-45 (11%; 95% CI, 6%-18%), HPV-35 (8%; 95% CI, 3%-14%), HPV-33 (5%; 95% CI, 1%-10%) HPV-58 (3%; 95% CI, 0%-7%), HPV-31 (3%; 95% CI, 0%-7%), HPV-51 (3%; 95% CI, 0%-7%), and HPV-52 (2%; 95% CI, 0%-5%)—or were absent (eg, HPV-61). HPV-16 and HPV-18, the genotypes most commonly associated with cervical cancer, were detected in 21% (95% CI, 13%-29%) and 5% (95% CI, 1%-10%) of samples, respectively. HPV-42 and HPV-43, which are associated with condylomas, were present in over 55% of samples, whereas HPV-11, which is responsible for most genital warts along with HPV-6, was detected in 22% (95% CI, 14%-31%) of samples. Except for samples collected between May and June 2024, genotype detection remained relatively consistent throughout the study period.

Impact of Subsampling on the Monitoring of HPVs in Wastewater

High-frequency sampling strategies may be limited by financial and logistical constraints. To assess the effect of reduced sampling frequency, weekly, bimonthly, and monthly subsampling strategies were compared with the biweekly reference dataset. Genotype detection frequencies and 95% CIs steadily widened as sample numbers decreased (Figure 3). For common genotypes such as HPV-39, monthly sampling broadened the uncertainty interval to 50% to 92%, compared with 63% to 81% under biweekly sampling. Low-frequency types were disproportionately affected; for HPV-16, detection frequency intervals ranged from 13% to 29% (biweekly) to 0% to 42% (monthly). Monthly sampling would fail to detect up to 17 genotypes, compared with 5 genotypes with biweekly sampling. Temporal modeling of total HPV load showed progressively poorer performance with decreasing sampling frequency across both root mean squared error and coverage rate metrics (Figure 4).

Temporal Patterns of Highly Detectable HPV Genotypes in Wastewater

Genotypes detected in over 90% of samples, with at least 40% of samples above the limit of quantification in the Allplex HPV28 assay, specifically HPV-66 and HPV-6, were selected for subsequent temporal modeling. Viral loads for each genotype are presented as daily flows or normalized to crAssphage DNA or mtDNA (Figure 5). Both HPV-6 and HPV-66 exhibited relatively stable viral loads, varying by approximately 1.5 log units irrespective of normalization method. Circulation remained low and stable over the first two-thirds of the study period (up to September 2024) and increased during the final third, as reflected by a higher proportion of samples exceeding the limit of quantification.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study found that wastewater-based epidemiology offers a valuable complementary approach to monitoring pathogen circulation, particularly when individual-level testing is limited or biased toward specific subpopulations. This is especially relevant for HPVs, for which more than 80% of individuals are estimated to acquire at least 1 infection by age 45 years.11 In France, HR-HPV testing for cervical cancer screening is currently restricted to women aged 30 years and older, excluding younger women, who exhibit the highest HPV prevalence, as well as men, although approximately 30% of men are estimated to carry genital HPV and more than 20% HR-HPV types.33 Moreover, routine screening primarily distinguishes HPV-16 and HPV-18 from all other HR types combined. Consequently, clinical surveillance provides an incomplete picture of HPV circulation. While HPVs have previously been detected in wastewater, previous approaches have not enabled robust temporal monitoring or characterization of genotype diversity.24

In this study, we estimated the overall HPV load using an improved version (SPF10-WW) of the broad-spectrum SPF10 primers.30 The short amplicon size (65 bp) makes this approach suitable for detecting fragmented viral DNA in wastewater. HPVs were consistently detected in all samples, at low concentrations and with limited temporal variability. Among commonly detected genotypes, HPV-66 (97%) and HPV-6 (93%) exhibited relatively stable viral loads.

The relative prevalence of HPV genotypes can be initially inferred from their detection frequency in women with normal cytology. The 10 most frequently detected oncogenic types in France34 were detected in wastewater in this study, with detection frequencies broadly consistent with clinical observations (eFigure 4 in Supplement 1); notably, HPV-16 was detected 4.8 times more frequently than HPV-18, closely matching the 4.9-fold clinical difference. Three genotypes—HPV-66, HPV-6, and HPV-53—were detected in more than 89% of wastewater samples. The most common HR-HPV genotypes were HPV-39 and HPV-56, followed by HPV-59, HPV-16, HPV-45, HPV-35, HPV-33, HPV-18, HPV-58, HPV-31, HPV-51, and HPV-52. It is noteworthy that HPV-6, HPV-11, HPV-16, and HPV-18 are included in the vaccine that has been used in France since 2007. Some discrepancies between wastewater and clinical data merit discussion. Although HPV-51 and HPV-52 are prevalent in women with normal cytology in France, they were detected infrequently in our samples, and HPV-61 was not detected. At first glance, this could suggest lower sensitivity of the Allplex HPV28 assay. Given the manufacturer’s limit of detection, these differences are unlikely to result from assay limitations and may instead reflect differences in HPV shedding dynamics, and regional differences in genotype diversity24 and relative abundance. Such factors may contribute to the discrepancies between our wastewater-based data from Paris and genotype prevalence estimates from national clinical surveillance.

Routine cervical screening is designed to identify women at risk of cervical precancer, rather than to characterize HPV circulation at a population level. This would provide an incomplete picture for several reasons: the unique anatomical site screened (the cervix); the age range of women screened, which represents those with the lowest HPV prevalence; and the fact that the screening tests target only 13 to 15 HR-HPV types, with low analytical sensitivity for identifying HPV-16 and HPV-18 individually, and the other HR-HPV types in pooled groups. In contrast, wastewater-based epidemiology reflects HPV circulation across the entire contributing population, including individuals not covered by routine screening and infections originating from multiple anatomical sites. As such, it provides complementary population-level information that may improve the interpretation of temporal changes in HPV genotype diversity especially at the HPV vaccination era. The frequent detection of HPV-39, HPV-56, and HPV-66 in wastewater, despite their lower representation in cervical screening studies, illustrates the potential of this approach to reveal aspects of HPV circulation that are not fully captured by routine cervical surveillance.

Limitations

This study has limitations that should be mentioned. Interpreting genotype-specific detection in wastewater requires integrating epidemiological, biological, and behavioral determinants. Wastewater measurements should, therefore, be interpreted as indicators of population-level circulation rather than direct estimates of infection prevalence. Beyond population prevalence, wastewater concentrations depend on the magnitude and duration of viral shedding from various biological compartments (vaginal secretions, urine, saliva, and feces), which may vary by age, sex, and vaccination status. A recent cohort study in women aged 18 to 25 years showed that cervical viral load reaches a plateau 2 months after infection and persists for 13 to 20 months.35 However, substantial genotype-specific differences exist in the duration of genital HPV clearance.36 The contribution of oral HPV, potentially linked to mouthwash use, remains uncertain, but should not be overlooked given the high prevalence of oral HR-HPV in men (6.6%-15.0%) and women (3.6%-6.8%), with substantial variations depending on country, age, and number of sexual partners in particular.37 Similarly, oral clearance also appears genotype-dependent, with LR-HPV generally clearing faster than HR-HPV.38 These heterogeneous shedding pathways likely contribute to divergences between wastewater profiles and clinical prevalence. The optimal monitoring duration and sampling frequency for wastewater-based HPV surveillance remain to be established. Our findings indicate that weekly sampling provides a reliable representation of HPV genotype detection frequencies, while further studies are needed to define the minimum monitoring period required for robust and cost-effective surveillance.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study of 100 wastewater samples collected over 1 year, we developed and applied a standardized, cost-effective protocol for the qualitative and quantitative monitoring of circulating HPVs in wastewater. The use of multiplex PCR overcame the technical limitations of sequencing-based approaches, potentially enabling broader implementation of this monitoring strategy. This approach complemented clinical screening by capturing HPV circulation in undertested populations and provided genotype-level temporal data. In addition, this approach could be particularly valuable in settings where routine HPV surveillance is limited or unavailable. In low-resource countries, surveillance could initially focus on major urban areas, where sewer networks are generally available and a large proportion of the population is connected. In this context, wastewater-based epidemiology could provide the first population-level estimates of circulating oncogenic HPV genotypes. Nevertheless, the dynamics of genotype-specific shedding and the relative roles of genital, urinary, and oral compartments remained incompletely understood. Expanding wastewater-based epidemiology to multisite, multiyear surveillance and integrating vaccination coverage and sexual-behavior data will be essential to fully interpret HPV diversity patterns in wastewater and to strengthen wastewater-based epidemiology as a tool for population-level HPV surveillance.
