Endoscopic management of postcholecystectomy complications at a Nigerian tertiary health facility
Authors: Olusegun Isaac Alatise, Patrick Ayodeji Akinyemi, Afolabi Olumuyiwa Owojuyigbe, Titilayo Adenike Ojumu, Adeleye Dorcas Omisore, Adewale Aderounmu, Aburime Ekinadese, Akwi Wasi Asombang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCholecystectomy and common bile duct exploration for biliary stone disease are common hepatobiliary surgeries performed by general surgeons in Nigeria. These procedures can be complicated by injury to the biliary tree or retained stones, requiring repeat surgical intervention. This study presents the experience of using endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) in the management of hepatobiliary surgery complications at the academic referral center of Obafemi Awolowo University Teaching Hospital (OAUTHC) Ile-Ife, Nigeria.MethodsAll patients with postcholecystectomy complications referred to the endoscopy unit at OAUTHC from March 2018 to April 2023 were enrolled. Preoperative imaging included a combination of abdominal ultrasound, CT, MRI, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), and T-tube cholangiogram. All ERCP procedures were performed under general anesthesia.ResultsSeventy-two ERCP procedures were performed on 45 patients referred for postcholecystectomy complications. The most common mode of presentation was ascending cholangitis [16 (35.6%)], followed by persistent biliary fistula [12 (26.7%)]. The overall median duration of symptoms after cholecystectomy was 20 weeks, with a range of 1-162 weeks. The most common postcholecystectomy complication observed was retained stone [16 (35.6%)]. Other postcholecystectomy complications included bile leakage, bile stricture, bile leakage with stricture, and persistent bile leakage from the T-tube in 12 (26.7%), 11 (24.4%), 4 (8.9%), and 2 (4.4%) patients, respectively. Ampullary cannulation during ERCP was successful in all patients (45, 100%). Patients with complete biliary stricture (10/12) required hepaticojejunostomy.ConclusionEndoscopic management of postcholecystectomy complications was found to be safe and reduce the number of needless surgeries to which such patients are exposed. We recommended prompt referral of such patients for ERCP.

Introduction

Recent trends have shown a slight increase in the incidence of gallstone disease in Nigeria and other African countries [1–4]. The increase in incidence, which may be real or apparent, has been associated with changes in diets from traditional high-fiber diets to more westernized diets as well as increased use of hormonal contraception [2, 5]. Most patients with gallstone disease are asymptomatic. However, approximately 20% of these asymptomatic gallstones will result in symptoms over 15 years of follow-up. These gallstones may lead to complications such as cholecystitis, cholangitis, choledocholithiasis, gallstone pancreatitis, and, rarely, cholangiocarcinoma [6].

Cholecystectomy, with or without common bile duct exploration, is the treatment of choice for gallstone disease and its complications [7]. These surgeries can be performed via open or laparoscopic approaches. Until recently, most centers in Nigeria have performed surgery via an open approach, as few centers have mastered laparoscopic surgery. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the gold standard treatment for symptomatic gallstone disease worldwide [8, 9]. Both the laparoscopic and open cholecystectomy approaches are at risk of complications [10]. Common complications following cholecystectomy include biliary injury, which can lead to bile leakage or biliary stricture, retained common bile duct (CBD) stones, postcholecystectomy syndrome, postcholecystectomy diarrhea, vascular injury/bleeding, abscess formation, and bowel injury [10, 11]. Biliary injury and retained common bile stones are the most common and require urgent intervention. Various researchers have classified bile duct injuries on the basis of peripheral leakage, central leakage, biliary stricture, and associated vascular injury [12–14]. Thus, several classification systems exist, ranging from simple Bismuth systems to more complicated systems, such as the ATOM (Anatomic, Time Of detection, Mechanism) classification published by the European Association for Endoscopic Surgery (EAES) [15].

The occurrence of these complications is associated with significant morbidity, mortality, and financial burdens [16]. Early recognition and prompt management are associated with better outcomes. Early recognition can be achieved by close clinical monitoring of the patient, and this can be confirmed by radiological investigations, such as an abdominal ultrasound scan, computerized tomography (CT) scan, and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP). While a few of these patients who have postcholecystectomy complications require salvage surgery, most patients can be managed endoscopically where facilities and skills are available. Endoscopic therapy is currently widely recommended as a first-line therapy [15]. Endoscopic therapy includes sphincterotomy, stenting, and the placement of nasobiliary drains [17]. All these therapeutic procedures can be performed during endoscopy retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). Endoscopic intervention aims to eliminate the pressure gradient across the sphincter of Oddi, promoting preferential bile flow into the duodenum and allowing the leak to heal [18].

Surgery was the only treatment modality available in Nigeria until 2018, when ERCP was introduced in our center. The morbidity and mortality associated with repeat surgery in these patients are usually high except at a specialized center [19, 20]. Since the introduction of ERCP, patients with complications following cholecystectomy with or without common bile duct exploration have been referred to our center. This study presents our initial experience in managing such patients, highlighting the endoscopic procedures performed and their outcomes at the Obafemi Awolowo University Teaching Hospitals Complex (OAUTHC), Ile-Ife, Nigeria.

Patients and method

Study design and location

This prospective study was conducted at the Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Unit of Obafemi Awowo University Teaching Hospital Complex, Ile Ife, from February 2018 to April 2023. The unit received referrals from patients with biliary injury after cholecystectomy from all over Nigeria and Ghana. The institution has facilities for both ERCP and open surgery.

Sample size and sampling technique

The study subjects were all consecutive patients referred to the unit for ERCP and therapeutic endoscopic interventions postcholecystectomy within the reference period. Patients who had bile leaks after liver surgery were excluded from the study.

Data collection

Data were collected via a standard protocol designed for the study. The data collected included demographic characteristics and previous hospitalization due to biliary pathology, time of surgery and presentation, last treatment before presentation, clinical presentation, site of the leak and other ERCP findings, various therapeutic endoscopic interventions, one-month postprocedure outcomes and complications related to these interventions.

Before ERCP, all patients had undergone preliminary investigations, such as hemograms, PT/PTT, INR, liver function tests, renal function tests, blood glucose level tests, serology for HIV, hepatitis C and B, and ultrasonography of the abdomen. Most patients also underwent an abdominal CT scan and MRCP before the procedure. Electrolyte and clotting factor derangement were routinely corrected before the procedure. As indicated, the clotting factor derangement was updated via the use of vitamin K and fresh frozen plasma. Those who had peritonitis from bile leakage at presentation were managed with peritoneal washout and drain insertion via laparotomy to control the leakage before ERCP.

The clinical details and imaging features were reviewed at multidisciplinary meetings, which involved endoscopists, radiologists, anesthesiologists, nurses, and their trainees, to assess the appropriateness of the referral and the preoperative diagnosis.

ERCP was performed under general anesthesia with cuffed endotracheal intubation administered, with most of the patients placed in a prone position except when patients’ conditions did not allow for such placement. In these cases, the procedure was performed in the supine position. The procedures were performed via the Evis Exera II Video duodenoscope TjfQ180V. The carm was used for real-time fluoroscopic visualization of the biliary tree after the injection of contrast agent into the biliary tree. The contrast agent used was ScanluxR 370 mg/ml, which contains 755 mg/ml of iopamidol as the active substance. This mixture was diluted with normal saline in equal proportions before injection.

Selective cannulation of the CBD was attempted via a standard sphincterotome and hydrophilic guidewire (Boston Scientific Corp., Natick, MA, USA). If initial attempts at cannulation were unsuccessful, cannulation was attempted via an ERCP cannula. Precutting was performed via a needle knife to facilitate access to the biliary tree if the previous effort failed. Cholangiography was used to assess evidence of biliary tree abnormalities, such as extravasation of bile to suggest biliary leakage, biliary stricture, or filling defects to suggest retained stones (Fig. 1). For those with bile leakage, sphincterotomy and a removable plastic biliary stent (7 Fr or 10 Fr by 9 cm–7 cm) or a fully covered self-expanding metal stent were inserted to allow anterograde drainage. The location of the lesion and availability of the stent generally guide the length of the stent. Any other abnormal findings at ERCP (e.g., retained stones) were treated as needed. The external drains inserted during laparotomy for patients with bile leaks were removed as soon as the drain became inactive following ERCP. Patients with incomplete strictures underwent the following procedure, which included the insertion of a plastic or metal stent and the dilatation of the stricture via a hurricane balloon. ERCP was repeated within six weeks to 3 months after the resolution of the biliary leak. Cholangiography was used to confirm the healing of the biliary fistula and the absence of a biliary stricture or other pathology, such as choledocholithiasis. All the patients who had complete stricture of the biliary tree underwent Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy. Percutaneous biliary drainage (PTBD) was used for those unsuitable for surgery. Because most of the patients were referred from other institutions, they were all sent back to their primary institutions after the completion of treatment. However, our research staff followed up with the patients by phone for one month after the procedure. The criteria used to determine the resolution of complications include clinical features with normalization of liver function tests, cessation of external drain output (when present), and resolution of intraabdominal collection on follow-up ultrasound with no further complications.

The data were analyzed via IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The data are presented in tables and charts. Continuous variables, such as age and the duration of symptoms, are presented as medians and ranges. ERCP and fluoroscopic images were also preserved to further demonstrate findings from the procedure. The presenting complaints were classified into four categories: recurrent ascending cholangitis, peritonitis, persistent bile fistula, and jaundice. Patients who presented with recurrent ascending cholangitis presented with a constellation of recurring symptoms, such as upper abdominal pain, fever with chills and rigor, and intermittent jaundice. These patients had been admitted multiple times before referral and were placed on antibiotics each time they experienced symptoms. Patients classified as having peritonitis were those who had uncontrolled bile leakage leading to bilious peritonitis at presentation. Patients who had peritonitis had abdominal pain, abdominal distension, and bile leakage from the wound. All these patients underwent laparotomy and peritoneal lavage, as well as the insertion of an external drain(s) into the abdomen to control the leakage before performing ERCP. ERCP was performed electively in all clinically stable patients, usually within five days of surgical intervention. Patients with persistent fistulas were those who experienced bile duct injury at the referring hospital and subsequently underwent open or laparoscopic peritoneal lavage and insertion of an abdominal drain to control bile leakage. Two patients who underwent open cholecystectomy and common bile duct exploration with the insertion of T-tubes complicated by biliary fistula were categorized as having persistent biliary fistula. Patients with jaundice were those who developed jaundice after cholecystectomy with no significant fever or upper abdominal pain. These patients had elevated serum bilirubin and deranged liver enzymes following cholecystectomy.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Ethics and Research Review Board of the Obafemi Awolowo University Teaching Hospitals Complex (protocol number ERC/2019/01/08). All participants signed an informed consent form as approved by the board. For patients younger than 18 (the age of consent in Nigeria), assent was obtained from the patients, and consent was obtained from the parents of all the patients.

Results

Forty-five patients (12 (26.7%) men and 33 (73.3%) women) with a median age of 39 years (range 8–63 years) who were referred for postcholecystectomy complications were included in the study. Seventy-two ERCP procedures were performed on these patients, accounting for 9.0% of 800 ERCP procedures performed during the study period. Among the 45 patients, 23 (51.1%) underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy, 20 (44.4%) underwent open cholecystectomy, and 2 (4.4%) underwent open cholecystectomy and common bile duct exploration with the insertion of a T-tube.

As shown in Table 1, the most common mode of presentation was ascending cholangitis [16 (35.6%)], followed closely by persistent biliary fistula [12 (26.7%)]. The overall median duration of symptoms postcholecystectomy before referral was 20 weeks, with a range of 1-162 weeks. However, the median duration of symptoms for patients who presented with recurrent ascending cholangitis was 42 weeks (range 12–162 weeks); the median duration of symptoms for patients with peritonitis was 2.5 weeks (range 1–6 weeks); the median duration for patients with fistulas was 4.5 weeks (range 2–24); and the median duration of symptoms for patients who presented with jaundice was 36 weeks (range 3-108 weeks).

For patients with retained stones postcholecystectomy, 10 of the 16 patients (62.5%) had stones extracted with extraction balloons, and 5 (31.3%) had stone extraction and placement of plastic stents, which were removed after six weeks. These patients had multiple stones that we could not extract on the first attempt. One patient had a plastic stent placed to relieve the leak and jaundice pending surgery due to the size of the stone. Endoscopically, the biliary stones measured approximately 2 cm in diameter (Fig. 3).

Five of the eleven patients with bile leaks had leakage from the cystic duct, with no other injuries to the biliary tree (Fig. 4). A total of 4/5 of the patients had plastic stents, whereas one had a fully covered self-expanding metal stent placement. Another 5/11 (45.5%) of the patients with bile leakage underwent balloon stone extraction and plastic stent placement. The leaks were mainly from the cystic ducts due to distal obstruction from the stones. One nine-year-old patient underwent complete transection of the bile duct. He was referred to an interventional radiologist for percutaneous biliary drainage. The patient was eventually referred to a transplant surgeon outside the country for surgery due to a lack of facilities to operate on pediatric patients with bile duct injuries.

Most of the patients (8/12) who had bile duct strictures underwent open cholecystectomy. Similarly, 10/12 patients with bile duct stricture had complete stricture of the biliary tree and eventually underwent hepaticojejunostomy. The remaining 2 out of 12 patients had incomplete strictures dilated via hurricane balloons and stent placements (Figs. 4 and 5). One patient had a plastic stent placed, and the other had a fully covered 10 × 8-cm self-expanding metal stent. The ability of the patient to pay for the procedure and availability was the deciding factor for the two patients. When a plastic stent was used, we preferably placed a 10Fz, 9-cm plastic stent. Eight surgical procedures were performed at OAUTHC, while two patients elected to have their surgery performed at an HPB surgery center outside the country.

Among the four patients who had bile leakage with stricture, two patients each had open cholecystectomy and laparoscopic cholecystectomy. They all had balloon dilatation of the strictures and placement of plastic stents. One of the patients had three procedures, while one was referred for surgery, which was performed at the referring center.

The ampullary cannulation rate in this group of patients was 100%. One patient had mild acute pancreatitis, which was managed conservatively, and was discharged after four days. One patient with bile duct stricture experienced stent migration and eventually underwent hepaticojejunostomy due to stricture recurrence. One patient, however, died. The patient had a biliary fistula and CBD stones after laparoscopic cholecystectomy. The patient was referred for ERCP 4 months after laparoscopic cholecystectomy. He presented with an external drain in the abdomen that produced approximately 400–600 ml of bile each day. The patient had plastic stents placed during the first ERCP. The external drain was removed 48 h after ERCP. He underwent a second ERCP after 12 weeks when stones were extracted from the biliary tree and the plastic stent was removed. He was discharged 48 h after the second ERCP but was readmitted with features suggestive of peritonitis five days after the second ERCP. He died before salvage surgery could be performed. Aside from this patient, no major complications were observed in any of the patients who underwent ERCP. One patient had features of mild pancreatitis, which was managed conservatively.

Discussion

We present our growing experience in managing patients with postcholecystectomy complications. To our knowledge, this report on endoscopic management of postcholecystectomy complications is the first from Nigeria and possibly the first from the West African subregion. A recent review of endoscopy capacity in West Africa revealed that ERCP is not routinely performed [21]. Given the recent increase in the number of cholecystectomies performed in our environment due to the increasing burden of gallstones, there is a growing need for the simultaneous development of salvage procedures in the West African subregion. This becomes very relevant because few patients with postcholecystectomy complications are not stable enough to withstand surgery.

Surgical outcomes in West African countries are inferior [22]. The same report highlighted ‘failure to rescue’ as one of the cardinal reasons for poor outcomes following surgery in Africa. Surgical complications are known to occur globally. However, few patients succumb to complications, primarily due to the availability of facilities and skill sets for prompt recognition and treatment of complications. Many countries outside of West Africa have developed minimally invasive techniques to manage complications following cholecystectomy. In recent years, endoscopic and percutaneous interventions have essentially replaced surgery as first-line treatment for most postcholecystectomy complications. The management of patients, including the specific approach, is usually influenced by local expertise and the type and severity of the injury [11]. With the increasing interest in minimal access surgical procedures for gallstone disease in the West African subregion and the expected increase in the incidence of biliary injury following surgery, it is essential to develop endoscopic and percutaneous interventions in the West African subregion [15, 23, 24].

Our data revealed that yearly cases have increased steadily except during the COVID-19 pandemic lockdown. This may not necessarily mean that the incidence of postcholecystectomy complications is increasing in Nigeria, as many factors may explain this observation. The awareness of the availability of ERCP is growing in the country; thus, many patients who would have had unnecessary surgery are now being referred for ERCP. Similarly, patients who would have traditionally been referred for treatment outside the country are now being referred to our center. This helps us build critical numbers to train gastroenterologists and surgeons in the skill set.

Surprisingly, many patients with these complications underwent open surgery. Open surgery is a salvaging technique in cases where the laparoscopic approach becomes very difficult. If the number of complications from open surgery is equal to that of the laparoscopic approach, there is an urgent need to train surgeons in the country on the act of performing safe open procedures. Until recently, the number of cholecystectomies performed in most institutions has been very low, thus making it very difficult for most general surgeons to be adequately familiar with the procedure before becoming independent surgeons. Does this also suggest that patients requiring cholecystectomy in Nigeria should be referred to regional centers to increase the number needed to train our new generation of surgeons? Unfortunately, many of our patients will be lost to alternative medical practitioners because of their unwillingness to travel out of their environment for treatment. Currently, treatment is out of pocket in Nigeria. Traveling to a regional center increases patients’ travel costs and, eventually, the cost of care. Establishing regional centers for cholecystectomy may ultimately be less expensive when the cost of managing postcholecystectomy complications is factored in.

The most common complication in our series was stone retention in the biliary tree following surgery. This may suggest that most patients were not adequately prepared for the procedure. To reduce the cost of care, most surgeons perform only abdominal Ultrasound scan (USS) before cholecystectomy. While an abdominal USS performed by a skilled operator may provide adequate information on whether there is a stone in the biliary tree before cholecystectomy, it is operator dependent. When some basic tell-tale signs are observed, such as multiple gallstones, a dilated biliary tree, elevated alkaline phosphatase, and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), such patients should undergo other imaging modalities, such as abdominal MRI/MRCP and abdominal CT, to evaluate the biliary tree. It is better to assume that there is a stone in the bile duct when the image is not clear than to proceed with surgery. As shown in this series, the risk of bile duct leakage increases significantly when there are concomitant bile duct stones.

The ampullary cannulation rate recorded in this series should not be interpreted to suggest that ERCP is a simple procedure with few complications. Another report from our group revealed that our ampulla cannulation rate is approximately 91% [25]. ERCP is a complex procedure with a steep learning curve. It can also be associated with significant morbidities, such as pancreatitis, bleeding, and stent migration. The risk of pancreatitis is high in patients with stone disease [26]. Thus, all therapeutic endoscopists should use good monitoring strategies for any complications. In our series, only mild pancreatitis was observed in a young woman managed for a retained stone in the bile duct. She was treated conservatively with bowel rest, fluid, analgesics, and antibiotics. After stent removal, following treatment for bile leakage and bile duct stones, special attention should be given to such patients before discharge. We recorded mortality in one patient who developed bile leakage from the cystic duct postcholecystectomy. Occlusion cholangiography was performed, which revealed no leakage. Unfortunately, the patient experienced leakage and developed irreversible shock before presenting in the hospital. Subsequently, we provide our patients with detailed instructions on tell-tale signs of danger and the need to present early.

Another important finding of this study is the duration of symptoms before presenting for ERCP. The median duration of symptoms was 20 weeks. Except for one patient with a bile leak that presented within one week of surgery, many patients were delayed in the referral center. After laparotomy at most of the referral centers and the placement of external drains, many patients are left for weeks in anticipation of the closure of the leak. Our group advised that such patients should be referred as soon as they are stable for quick intervention. This will reduce the length of hospital stay and the number of work hours lost due to this complication.

Unfortunately, the technique utilized for each patient is influenced by prosthesis availability and the procedure’s cost. Since our patients pay out of pocket, we deployed multimodal techniques to treat our patients. Several studies have shown that sphincterotomy alone may be sufficient in some patients with bile leaks [15, 27]. We performed sphincterotomy on all our patients, and many had stents placed. The duration between the first and second procedures also varies widely among our patients because most patients need to look for money to pay for the procedure. We could also not provide adequate information on the optimal time to plan the second procedure. However, many authors believe that the double-stage procedure should be performed within three months [11, 28].

This study is not without limitations. The number of cases was small, and most patients underwent open surgeries. However, this study provides baseline data on the effectiveness of endoscopic therapy in managing postcholecystectomy complications in Nigeria. We found the procedure safe and reduced the number of needless surgeries to which such patients are exposed. We recommended prompt referral of such patients for ERCP.

Conclusion

As the number of surgical procedures performed for gallstones in Nigeria has increased, the incidence of complications associated with these surgeries has also increased. We found that endoscopic management of postcholecystectomy complications was safe and reduced the number of needless surgeries to which such patients are exposed. We recommended prompt referral of such patients for ERCP.
