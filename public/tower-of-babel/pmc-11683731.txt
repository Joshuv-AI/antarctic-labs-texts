Description of tissue profiles, thalamic location, and recommended penetrating captive bolt placement in cadaver heads from domesticated 24- to 48-mo-old male yaks (Bos grunnians)
Authors: Emma M Hamilton, Kurt D Vogel, Ashlynn A Kirk, Michael Rossi, Karly N Anderson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractYaks are a domesticated species utilized for meat, fiber, and transportation in many countries. In the United States, yak meat is growing in popularity due to its classification as a “healthy” meat. Penetrating captive bolt (PCB) is an approved method of preslaughter stunning and euthanasia for cattle. At the time of our study, peer-reviewed literature or industry guidelines for PCB stunning or euthanasia did not appear to exist for yaks. This study aimed to identify and describe an ideal placement based on the location of the thalamus, part of the brain that is important for sensibility, and to describe the tissue depths at this placement. To do this, 10 hide-on cadaver heads from yaks were sourced from a population of 30 yaks aged 24 to 48 mo, which had a mean hot carcass weight of 180.1 ± 52.0 kg (±SD). Source animals were stunned in a frontal location with a handheld PCB during normal slaughter procedures at the collaborating slaughter establishment. Cadaver heads were stored in a walk-in freezer for 96 d before head processing. Head weight, total head length, horn tip spread, and horn base circumference were measured. Heads were split down the midline using a band saw. Heads were imaged and tissue thicknesses of the exposed surface of each head were measured. Thalamic depth and measurements to describe the location of the thalamus relative to external head anatomy were recorded from these images. Results are reported as mean ± SD. On average, the total length of the head from the poll to the nose was 37.74 ± 2.83 cm. The center of the thalamus was located 11.86 ± 1.32 cm below the poll: 23.88 ± 1.56% down the frontal plane of the head. The exposed diameter of the thalamus on the frontal plane of the head was 3.43 ± 0.31%. Thus, an ideal PCB placement for yaks is located approximately 25% of the way down the frontal plane of the face of the animal. Tissue depth measurements are reported as mean ± SD followed by a 97.5% one-sided upper reference limit (URL). Soft tissue thickness was 16.03 ± 3.26 mm (URL: 23.77 mm), cranial thickness was 30.69 ± 4.34 mm (URL: 40.97 mm), and total tissue thickness was 46.71 ± 7.15 mm (URL: 63.68 mm). Thalamic depth was 92.22 ± 11.60 mm (URL: 119.74 mm). These results show that an ideal PCB placement for yaks is located at 23.88 ± 1.56% of the distance from the poll to the nose down the frontal plane of the head and a penetration depth of 92.22 ± 11.60 mm is required to reach the thalamus.

Introduction

Yaks belong to the bovine subfamily, Bovidae (Jialin, 1998) and are a popular species providing food and fiber in various countries with an estimated global population of 14.2 million (Wiener et al., 2003). Ninety-two percent of the world’s yaks are located in China, and the country yields approximately 270,000,000 kg of yak meat each year (Luo et al., 2006). In Bhutan, yaks are kept for their milk and fiber and can also be used for transportation (Dorji et al., 2022). After they are no longer used to provide milk or fiber, surplus yaks are harvested (Wiener et al., 2003). Luo et al., (2006) reported that yak meat is very palatable and rich in myoglobin. Additionally, the total combined essential amino acid levels (histidine, glutamic acid, arginine, and alanine) were reported as 1,100.3 mg/100 g higher than Chinese yellow cattle (Luo et al., 2006). Yak round steak and ribeye each contain less than 3 g of fat, with less than 1 g of saturated fat per serving, qualifying it to be labeled as low fat, low sodium, and a “healthy” meat (United States Department of Agriculture Food Safety Inspection Service (USDA FSIS), 2007; Mireless DeWitt, 2011).

In the United States, there have been 1,600 yaks registered with the USYAK organization in the last 6 yr and there are 1 to 2 new breeders emerging into the production space each year (R. Higdon, USYAKS, Ridgeway, CO, personal communication). Within the United States, yaks are processed in facilities similar to cattle. Yaks are considered an exotic species and are eligible for voluntary inspection by the USDA FSIS (2021), as described in 9 CFR 352 (United States Electronic Code of Federal Regulations, 2024a). In order to comply with the Humane Methods of Slaughter Act (7 USC 1901) and the regulations that enforce it (9 CFR 313) (United States Electronic Code of Federal Regulations, 2024b; United States House of Representatives Office of the Law Revision Counsel, 2024) all animals must be rendered immediately insensible before further slaughter procedures can occur. Due to the unpredictable behavior of yaks, it is important for human safety and animal welfare that they do not undergo any slaughter procedures without successful preslaughter stunning (CFIA, 2021).

Stunning ensures that animals cannot experience pain and distress during slaughter procedures (Terlouw et al., 2016a; de la Cruz et al., 2018). Mechanical methods of stunning, such as penetrating captive bolt (PCB) and gunshot, cause an immediate loss of sensibility by damaging the brain. A PCB is designed to cause concussion and destruction of brain tissue, creating tears, lesions, and the disturbance of blood flow (Posner et al., 2008; Oliveira et al., 2018). In addition to destruction from the bolt, bone fragments from the penetration site can cause further damage to the brain, and the void created from the retracting bolt promotes brain hemorrhaging (Karger, 1995; Viel et al., 2009; Oliveira et al., 2018). Within the brain, the thalamus is an important structure to target due to the role it plays in maintaining sensibility (Terlouw et al., 2016b; de la Cruz et al., 2018; Terlouw and Le Neindre, 2024).

Penetrating captive bolt is an approved method of stunning for cattle (HSA, 2016; CFIA, 2019; EFSA, 2020; AVMA, 2024). During slaughter, death results from exsanguination (Meat Institute, 2024). Penetrating captive bolt is also an approved method of euthanasia in cattle when an adjunctive method, such as an injection of KCl, a second PCB application, pithing, or exsanguination, is applied (AVMA, 2020; AABP, 2023). Accurate PCB placement is essential to the success of rendering the animal insensible with a single PCB application and preventing the animal from experiencing a shallow depth of concussion (Gregory et al., 2009; Derscheid et al., 2016; de la Cruz et al., 2018; Oliveira et al., 2018). Gunshot is also an approved method of preslaughter stunning and euthanasia for cattle (HSA, 2016; CFIA, 2019; AVMA, 2020, 2024; AABP, 2023). Whether PCB or gunshot is used it is important to ensure accurate placement that maximizes the potential for thalamic contact so that the animal is rendered immediately insensible. Therefore, it is important to know the anatomy and location of the brain of the animal in order to maximize the potential for immediate and sustained insensibility.

At the time of our study, there did not appear to be any industry guidelines or peer-reviewed studies related to the head anatomy or PCB use for yaks, however, such studies have been conducted for other bovids. There are 2 commonly approved PCB placements for cattle: 1) at the intersection of 2 imaginary lines, each drawn from the outer corner of the eye and connecting to the base of the opposite horn, or equivalent position in polled cattle (AVMA, 2020, 2024; AABP, 2023) and 2) at the midline of the face, halfway between the top of the poll and an imaginary line connecting the outside corner of each eye (AVMA, 2020, 2024; AABP, 2023). The use of a poll shot is not recommended for the PCB stunning of cattle (CFIA, 2019; Meat Institute, 2024); however, it has been found to be effective as a second step of euthanasia for dairy cattle, following a frontal PCB application (Robbins et al., 2021, 2023).

Current guidelines for the stunning and euthanasia of water buffalo recommend a frontal placement located at the intersection of 2 imaginary lines connecting the lower side of one horn to the upper side of the contralateral horn. However, accurate PCB placement is required and animals need to be exsanguinated immediately following loss of consciousness in order for this placement to be successful (AVMA, 2020; AABP, 2023). The skull of a water buffalo is thicker and wider in comparison to cattle with thicker skin covering the head and body (Schwenk et al., 2016). As such, it is recommended that a high-caliber firearm such as a centerfire high-powered rifle or shotgun is used to stun or euthanize water buffalo (AVMA, 2020, 2024; AABP, 2023).

Currently, several recommendations exist for the stunning and euthanasia of bison, including both placement and method. A common placement located on the forehead approximately 2.5 cm above the imaginary line connecting the bottom of each horn is approved with the use of gunshot (CFIA, 2019; AVMA, 2020, 2024; AABP, 2023). Another placement is located midline just behind the poll and is recommended with the use of a free-flight PCB equipped with a long bolt (Meat Institute, 2024). In contrast, the AVMA (2024) describes that the poll placement may only be used for stunning bison when a high-powered rifle is used. Lastly, a placement located directly along the midline of a line connecting the medial aspect of the horns which is recommended for use with a free-flight PCB equipped with a long bolt, or a firearm with a minimum of 1,355.8 Nm of muzzle energy (Meat Institute, 2024).

It was not yet known at the time of our study whether the head morphology and thalamic location of yaks represent that of the cattle (bos taurus or bos indicus), water buffalo, or bison, or if their head characteristics are unique. The objectives of this study were to identify and describe an ideal frontal PCB placement based on external landmarks which maximizes the potential for bolt-thalamus contact, and to characterize the tissue thicknesses and thalamic depth at that placement.

Materials and Methods

Animal Use Protocol

The yaks from which the heads were obtained were slaughtered at a commercial slaughter establishment under inspection by the United States Department of Agriculture Food Safety and Inspection Service (USDA FSIS) in accordance with the Humane Methods of Slaughter Act (7 USC 1901) (United States Electronic Code of Federal Regulations, 2024b) and the regulations that enforce it (9 CFR 313) (United States House of Representatives Office of the Law Revision Counsel, 2024). Specifically, all source yaks were stunned in a frontal location with a 0.25R caliber PCB (Jarvis Model PAS-Type C Super Heavy Duty, Order #: 4144059) and 0.25R 6 GR powder cartridges (Jarvis Red 0.25R 6 GR cartridges, Order #: 1176025). After stunning and subsequent exsanguination, all heads were removed from their respective carcasses. Because live animals were not directly manipulated in this study, it was not necessary to submit an animal use protocol to the University of Wisconsin—River Falls (UWRF) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). This exemption from IACUC approval followed the precedent established by Anderson et al. (2019, 2021a, 2021b, 2024a, 2024b, 2024c) and Hamilton et al. (2023).

Description of Cadaver Heads

Ten cadaver heads were obtained from a group of 30 male yaks who ranged 24 to 48 mo in age and were imported from Alberta and Saskatchewan, Canada and slaughtered at a commercial slaughter establishment in Wisconsin, USA. Source animal age and hot carcass weight (HCW) for the heads included in this study could not be separated from the population of 30 yaks. A power calculation was not conducted prior to this study because the mean tissue thicknesses and associated SD values were unknown and only 10 heads were available for sampling. Heads were removed from their respective carcasses following exsanguination and the hide, mandible, and horns were left intact; tongues were removed from each head at this time. The average HCW for the group of 30 yaks was 180.1 ± 52.0 kg (mean ± SD). Individual head identification could not be obtained to allow exclusive reporting of the mean HCW for source yak of the 10 heads that were included in this study.

After each head was removed from its respective carcass, it was placed in a large cardboard box (combo bin) with a plastic liner on a wooden pallet. All heads were frozen at −17.78 to −23.89 °C for 16 to 28 h before the cardboard box was placed in the bed of a pickup truck and transported 146 km under ambient temperature to the UWRF Meat Science Laboratory and Animal Welfare Lab. On the day of transport, the mean daily temperature was 18.76 °C (measured at 44.8 °N, 91.51 °W, Chippewa Valley Regional Airport Weather Station, Eau Claire, WI on June 9, 2022). Immediately upon arrival at UWRF, each head was individually packaged in a large black contractor bag and placed in a large storage tote (Sterilite 1466 – 27 Gallon Industrial Tote, Sterilite Corp., Townsend, MA), with lids off and 2 heads per tote. The heads were held in a walk-in freezer at −16.5 ± 0.2 °C (mean ± SD) for 96 d until data collection. All heads remained frozen until data collection commenced. The use of chilled heads has been evaluated in both swine and Holstein cows > 30 mo of age (Anderson et al., 2021a, 2024a) and allows for the brain to stay intact when heads are split, as the tissue is more rigid. This allows for better identification of brain structures (Kline et al., 2019; Anderson et al., 2021a, 2024a). At the time of head processing, the exposed internal cranial surface temperature for all heads was −0.1 ± 1.3 °C (mean ± SD).

Head Processing and Measurements

On the day of head processing, all heads were removed from the walk-in freezer and transported to a walk-in cooler adjacent to the abattoir room within the UW-River Falls Meat Science Laboratory. Immediately prior to data collection, each head was removed individually from its bag and the following external head measurements were collected using a flexible measuring tape (Singer 218 60 in, The Singer Company Ltd., Boston, MA): poll to nose distance (cm), distance from the poll to the top of the eyes (cm), distance between optic orbits (cm), distance between horn tips (cm), and horn base circumference (cm).

Poll to nose distance referred to the distance from the tip of the nose to the crest of the poll at the midline. Distance from the poll to the top of the eyes referred to the distance from a straight line across the top of both eyes to the crest of the poll at midline. Distance between optic orbits referred to the distance between the medial canthi of the eyes (Anderson et al., 2021b, 2024b, 2024c). Distance between horn tips referred to the length of a straight line between the centers of the horn tips. Horn base circumference referred to the distance around the outside of the horn bases directly adjacent to the poll for each yak; this measurement was averaged between the 2 horn bases for each head. Head weight (kg) was also recorded for each head, with horns intact, using a benchtop scale that was calibrated prior to use (WI-125, Avery Weigh-Tronix, Fairmont, MN).

After these external head measurements were taken, each head was split down the sagittal plane at the midline using a Hobart 6801 Vertical Meat Band Saw (Hobart, Troy, OH) equipped with a blade that was 0.06 mm thick, 360.68 cm long, 4 teeth/2.54 cm, and a hook angle of 3 °C (Product #: C78529545, Bunzl Processor Division, Riverside, MO). Digital images were then taken 50.8 cm directly above and perpendicular to the exposed cut surface for each side of each head, as shown in Fig. 1. For each side of each head, all digital images included a 15 cm ruler for reference and the following images were obtained: 1) head and 15 cm ruler and 2) head, 15 cm ruler, and a red wooden dowel at the site of PCB application used by the collaborating slaughter establishment. Thermal images (Model E8 FLIR Systems, Boston, MA) were collected from each side of each head once split to record the maximum exposed intracranial temperature; these were also taken 50.8 cm directly above and perpendicular to the exposed cut surface of each side of each head but did not include a ruler or wooden dowel. Head processing methods were adapted from Anderson et al. (2019, 2021a, 2021b, 2024a, 2024b, 2024c) and Hamilton et al. (2023).

Determination of an Ideal Placement

Following head processing, all images were uploaded to a computer. An image from each side of each head with only the 15 cm ruler on the surface of the split head was uploaded to Adobe Illustrator (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA). The thalamus was identified and outlined with a uniform circle for each side of each head. For heads where the thalamus was observed to be distorted in shape or from normal anatomic location in comparison to nearby brain structures, further measurements based on the location of the thalamus were not taken. Then, a box was placed such that one corner was located at the center of the thalamus and the dorsal aspect of the box was in alignment with the frontal plane of the head. The rostral aspect of the box extended dorsally from the center of the thalamus and marked the ideal placement for maximizing bolt-thalamic contact. The caudal aspect of the box was located parallel to this line and perpendicular to the dorsal aspect, in alignment with the poll of the head. The circle and box were used to facilitate tissue depth and thalamic measurements.

Tissue Depth and Thalamic Measurements

After all images were edited in Adobe Illustrator (Adobe Inc.) to determine an ideal placement to maximize the potential for thalamic contact, an image analysis software (SketchandCalc, iCalc, Inc., Palm Coast, FL) was used to measure soft tissue thickness, cranial thickness, thalamic depth, thalamic diameter, and the distance from the poll to the ideal placement.

Soft tissue thickness, cranial thickness, and thalamic depth were recorded at the ideal placement and can be visualized in Fig. 1. Soft tissue thickness referred to the distance from the surface of the skin where the PCB would be applied to the external aspect of the cranium and cranial thickness referred to the distance from the most external aspect of the cranium to the most internal aspect of the cranium (Anderson et al., 2019, 2021a, 2021b, 2024a, 2024b, 2024c; Hamilton et al., 2023). Total tissue thickness referred to the total distance from the surface of the skin where the PCB would be applied to the interior aspect of the cranium and was determined by adding soft tissue thickness and cranial thickness measurements for each head, as described by Anderson et al. (2019, 2021a, 2021b, 2024a, 2024b, 2024c) and Hamilton et al. (2023). Thalamic depth referred to the distance from the external surface of the skin at the ideal placement to the center of the thalamus (Anderson et al., 2024c), and was measured along the rostral aspect of the box. Thalamic diameter referred to the diameter of the thalamus, as observed from the exposed brain of each head, as described by Anderson et al. (2024c). Measurements from both sides of each head were averaged together prior to statistical analysis so that there was one value per measurement for each head.

To describe the location of the ideal placement on the head, the distance from the top of the poll to the center of the thalamus perpendicular to the head was recorded; this measurement is referred to as poll to ideal placement distance. The poll to ideal placement distance was then divided by the total length of the head (i.e., the top of the poll to the nose) to determine a ratio that represented the location of the thalamus on the head on a percent basis. The distance of the ideal placement relative to the top of eyes was determined by subtracting the poll to ideal placement distance from the total distance from the poll to the top of eyes. To understand the ideal placement relative to the position of the eyes and the poll, we calculated the percentage of the distance from the poll to the top of the eyes located above the ideal placement; this was determined by dividing the poll to ideal placement distance by the top of eyes to ideal placement. The distance from the eyes to the nose was determined by subtracting the poll to eyes distance from the poll to nose distance.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive statistics for continuous data outcomes (head weight, poll to nose, poll to top of eyes, between eyes, horn tips spread, horn base circumference, soft tissue thickness, cranial thickness, total tissue thickness, thalamic depth, thalamic diameter, and poll to ideal placement distance) were determined via the UNIVARIATE procedure within SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1 (Statistical Analysis System Institute, Inc., Cary, NC). Additionally, the histogram statement was used to assess normality for each of these variables.

Head characteristics (poll to eyes, distance between eyes, horn tip spread, horn base circumference, poll to ideal placement distance, and thalamic diameter) were assessed for linear relationships with total tissue thickness and thalamic depth using models constructed within the REGRESSION procedure of SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1 (Statistical Analysis System Institute, Inc.). Relationships between regression variables were recognized as significant when P ≤ 0.05.

To determine an estimate of the bolt length required to reach the brain and the thalamus for the majority of yaks, upper reference limits (URL) were calculated, as described by Anderson et al. (2021a, 2024b, 2024c). Specifically, a t-based prediction interval with a 97.5% one-sided reference range was calculated for normally distributed data with unknown mean and standard deviation (Horn, 1988) along with a 90% CI for the URL (URL 90% CI) for soft tissue thickness, cranial thickness, total tissue thickness, and thalamic depth. Using R Studio version 2023.03.0 (R Core Team, 2023), the tidyverse and ggbeeswarm packages of R version 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2023) were used to calculate all URLs, URL 90% CIs, and associated plots.

Results and Discussion

Source Animal Characteristics

Source animal and head characteristics can be observed in Table 1 and are reported as mean ± SD. The mean HCW was 180.1 ± 52.0 kg for the group of 30 male yaks 24 to 48 mo of age that were the source of the heads included in this study. The HCW of male yaks in North America has not been well documented, so it is unknown whether the HCW observed is representative of the North American yak population at slaughter. Zi et al. (2004) reported a similar HCW for Juilong yak steers and bulls at 54 mo of age (205.9 kg and 169.2 kg, respectively). Although the source yaks for the cadaver heads in our study were up to 30 mo younger than the yaks in the Zi et al. (2004) study, these appear to be the most similar HCW reported for yaks. Zi et al. (2004) reported 106.0 kg and 112.9 kg HCW for bulls at 30 to 40 mo of age and 54 mo old cows, respectively. Additionally, Jialin et al. (1998) reported that crossbred (female domestic yaks inseminated with wild yak semen) yaks had an average HCW of 71.17 kg at 18 mo of age. Improved yak production systems, different environments, and differences in sex and age may have contributed to the differences in HCW of yaks observed between these studies.

Head characteristics, tissue thicknesses, thalamic depth, and ideal placement descriptors for yak cadaver heads

The exposed intracranial temperature for the yak heads included in this study was −0.13 ± 1.31 °C. For all yak heads included in this study (n = 10) the average head weight was 19.69 ± 5.26 kg. This value lies between the 2 reported head weights of hornless water buffalo at under 30 mo and over 30 mo, which are reported as 16.6 kg and 22.0 kg, respectively (Gregory et al., 2009). It should be noted that all yak heads included in this study were weighed with the horns intact. Glardon et al. (2018) reported the average head weight of male bison to be greater than 32 kg. This is indicative that the average head weight of male yaks is less than what was previously reported for male bison.

Average distance from poll to nose was 49.59 ± 3.80 cm. This represents the total length of the head and similar measurements have been reported elsewhere for cattle. Schiffer et al. (2014) observed this distance to be 41.5 cm for German Angus and Galloway bulls, cows, and steers. Bene et al. (2007) measured the head length of beef cows and observed head length ranged from 47.9 cm for Herefords to 52.8 cm in Lincoln Reds. Red Angus cows had an average head length of 50.0 cm, which was the breed that most closely resembled the yak heads evaluated in our study. Ozkan et al. (2019) also observed the skull length of cattle, without the hide, to be an average of 52.95 cm. Similarly, Çakar et al. (2024) observed the skull length, without the hide, for 12- to 14-mo-old male Simmental and Holstein cattle; the Simmental cattle had an average head length of 48.60 cm and the Holstein cattle had an average head length of 49.05 cm. These findings are both within 1 cm of the head length we observed for yak heads in the present study. The head length of Holstein cattle has been reported to increase with age (Matthews et al., 1975). Heifers at 18 mo of age had a head length of 49 cm, which is very similar to the yak heads included in our study, and first lactation cows had a head length of 52 cm (Matthews et al., 1975). Overall, related studies have found varying head lengths for cattle which have a broad range encompassing the average head length found for cadaver yak heads in this study. Because of this variability, further research is needed to determine whether yaks can reliably be compared to cattle when choosing PCB placements.

Some studies have reported head lengths on other bovids such as water buffalo and bison. Ozkan et al. (2019) found that total skull length for water buffalo, with the hide removed, was 47.20 cm, which is less than what was measured in this study for yak heads. Because Ozkan et al. (2019) measured skull length with the hide removed, it is difficult to determine whether yak heads are actually longer than the heads of water buffalo. Shakleton et al. (1975) measured head length (from the occipital crest to the tip of the premaxilla) in bison and reported an average of 53.47 cm which is greater than that of the yak heads in this study. Although limited studies exist evaluating head length in these other species at this time, at this time the average head length for yak heads in our study was greater than that of water buffalo, and less than that of bison.

On average, the distance from the eyes to nose was 29.74 ± 2.12 cm and distance from the poll to the top of eyes was 19.85 ± 2.08 cm, taking up approximately 40% of the frontal plane of the head. The distance between the medial aspect of the optic orbits was 23.20 ± 3.24 cm. Schiffer et al. (2014) observed this distance to be 18.3 cm for Bos taurus beef cattle, and reported it as 18.2 cm, which is less than we observed for male yaks 24 to 48 mo of age. On average, the distance between horn tips was 90.45 ± 4.59 cm and horn base circumference was 26.88 ± 2.00 cm.

Description of an Ideal Placement

An ideal placement on the front of the head was determined based on the location of the thalamus. The thalamus is an important structure to target for mechanical stunning, as it contains part of the ascending reticular activation system (ARAS). The ARAS plays a key role in the maintenance of consciousness (Terlouw et al., 2016a; de la Cruz et al., 2018; AVMA, 2024; Terlouw and Le Neindre, 2024). In humans with TBI, damage to the ARAS was associated with a decreased state of consciousness (Jang and Kwon, 2020). Additionally, Redinbaugh et al. (2020) described that the central lateral thalamus was responsible for consciousness in rhesus macaques and that the stimulation of the central lateral thalamus resulted in a state of arousal in anesthetized rhesus macaques. This placement was defined such that the PCB would be placed on the frontal surface of the yak’s head directly above the center of the thalamus and perpendicular to the frontal plate of the head. Thalamic diameter and the position of the ideal placement relative to external features of the head are reported in Table 1. Results are reported as means ± SD. The distance from the poll to ideal placement was 11.86 ± 1.32 cm and the distance from the ideal placement to nose was 37.74 ± 2.83 cm. On average, the ideal placement was located 8.00 ± 1.64 cm above the eyes. The location of the ideal placement relative to the poll, eyes, and nose can be observed in Fig. 2. The ideal placement was located 23.88 ± 1.56% of the way down the head, from the poll. The distance from the poll to the top of eyes was 19.85 ± 2.08 cm. Of the 19.85 cm between the poll and the top of eyes, the ideal placement was located at 59.93 ± 5.57% of this distance from the poll. This differs from a recommendation of PCB placement for cattle, especially long-faced cattle, located halfway (50%) between the poll and the eyes (Gilliam et al., 2016, 2018; AVMA, 2020, 2024; AABP, 2023) by approximately 10%. Average thalamic diameter was 1.70 ± 0.20 cm and the thalamus comprised 3.43 ± 0.31% of the total head length. This percentage represents the total proportion of the front of the head where a PCB could be placed such that the thalamus is targeted. The aforementioned proportions equate to a location range of 22.17% to 25.59% down the frontal plane of the head where the PCB could be placed to target the thalamus. A common proportion of 25% of the way down the frontal aspect of the head could be used for reference for individuals tasked with stunning.

The location of the ideal placement is 23.88 ± 1.56% down the frontal plane of the head and the thalamic diameter encompasses 3.43 ± 0.31% of the distance from the poll to the nose. This yields a recommended PCB placement that is located at 25% of the proportion from the poll to the nose, maximizing potential for bolt-thalamic contact in cadaver heads from yaks. Tissue thickness measurements and thalamic depth at the ideal placement can be observed in Table 1 (means ± SD) and Table 2 (URL and URL 90% CI). For all measurements, results are reported as means ± SD. Soft tissue thickness was 16.03 ± 3.26 mm (URL: 23.77 mm; URL 90% CI: 20.87 to 26.68 mm). The mean soft tissue thickness observed in this study was greater than the 6.31 mm soft tissue thickness of Holstein cows > 30 mo of age reported by Anderson et al. (2024a); this was measured at the location of a stun targeted at the common frontal recommended placement for cattle (at the intersection of 2 imaginary lines reaching from the outside corner of the eye to the opposite side of the poll). Cranial thickness was 30.69 ± 4.34 mm (URL: 40.97 mm; URL 90% CI: 37.12 to 70.03 mm). The mean cranial thickness observed for male yaks 24 to 48 mo of age in our study is thicker than the cranial thickness of 19.67 mm reported by Kline et al. (2019) for B. taurus and cranial thickness of 17.81 mm reported by Anderson et al. (2024a) for dairy cows at frontal placements. It is unknown whether this difference is due to species or the age of animals in each study.

Total tissue thickness was 46.71 ± 7.15 mm (URL: 63.68 mm; URL 90% CI: 57.32 to 70.0 mm). The mean total tissue thickness observed for male yaks 24 to 48 mo of age in our study is thicker than the mean skin and bone thickness of 17.33 mm observed by Oliveira et al. (2018) for Nelore cattle (Bos indicus) after stunning with a pneumatic PCB at a frontal location described as the intersection between 2 lines drawn from the base of each horn to the corner of the opposite eye. It should be noted that the corner of the eye used by Oliveira et al. (2018) to mark the stun location in their study was not specified. Our findings suggest that the cranial thickness and total tissue thicknesses of male yaks 24 to 48 mo of age are greater than those currently reported for cattle > 400 kg (Oliveira et al., 2018). Anderson et al. (2024a) reported a total tissue thickness of 24.13 mm for dairy cows, which is less than what we observed for yaks. This is corroborated by the findings of Anderson et al. (2024d) who reported a total tissue thickness of 27.11 mm for Holstein cows > 30 mo of age at a frontal stun placement. Together, these findings suggest that the total tissue thickness of male yaks 24 to 48 mo of age is greater than that which is currently reported for mature dairy cows.

It is important to understand the depth to thalamus, because the thalamus must be directly penetrated, damaged via bone chips, or have received enough concussive force to render the animal immediately insensible; therefore, the goal is to choose a PCB device that offers a penetration depth sufficient to reach the thalamus to maximize the success of the application (Terlouw et al., 2016b; Oliveira et al., 2018). On average, the thalamic depth of the yak heads was 92.22 ± 11.60 mm (URL: 119.74 mm; URL 90% CI: 109.43 to 130.05 mm). Schwenk et al. (2016) reported median thalamic depths of 36.6 mm and 74.0 mm for bovine bulls and water buffalo at a frontal location following stunning. The median thalamic depth for male yaks 24 to 48 mo of age observed in our study was 93.00 mm. However, for bovine bulls and water buffalo > 30 mo of age, Schwenk et al. (2016) reported median thalamic depths of <40 mm and approximately 90 mm, respectively. The median thalamic depth for water buffalo > 30 mo of age is similar to the median and mean thalamic depth we observed for yaks.

The URL values can be used to predict whether a PCB device with a known bolt penetration depth would have the capacity to reach the brain or thalamus for 97.5% of male yaks 24 to 48 mo of age. These values are very useful to the individuals tasked with selecting PCB devices for stunning and euthanasia, as well as manufacturers of PCB devices (Anderson et al., 2021b, 2024b, 2024c). All URL values, URL 90% CIs, and sample distributions for tissue thickness measurements and thalamic depth at the ideal placement can be observed in Fig. 3. Our findings suggest that a penetration depth of 63.68 mm would be required to reach the brain at the ideal placement for 97.5% of male yaks 24 to 48 mo of age. We estimate that a penetration depth of 119.74 mm would be required to reach the thalamus at the ideal placement for 97.5% of male yaks 24 to 48 mo of age. To the best of our knowledge, there is not a commercially available PCB device with a bolt travel distance greater than 114.3 mm (Casagrande et al., 2020; Anderson et al., 2021b, 2024b, 2024c; Frazer et al., 2023).

Significant (P < 0.05) linear relationships were identified between head weight and both total tissue thickness (Fig. 4) and thalamic depth (Fig. 5). At this time, it is unknown whether head weight is linked to age or HCW for male yaks. The relationship between head weight, age, and HCW, in addition to tissue depth parameters, warrants further research. Derscheid et al. (2016) calculated a relationship between head weight and skull thickness in bovine calves aged 6 to 19 mo (227 to 500 kg), which was defined as the total distance from the most external part of the skull with the hide removed, to the most internal part of the skull where it meets the brain. This is very similar to our definition of cranial thickness. For all calves in the referenced study, head weight did not have a significant linear relationship with skull thickness. To the best of our knowledge, this has not been explored in bovines of similar age due to bovine PCB research focusing predominantly on cattle < 30 mo.

It is important to note that all cadaver heads included in this study were stunned in a frontal location with a PCB by plant personnel at the collaborating slaughter establishment. As a result, it is unknown whether these stuns were at the ideal placement for each yak and it was not possible to determine the extent of brain damage that would have been incurred at the ideal placement. Because a PCB was not applied at the ideal placement, there would not have been value in TBI assessment with a scoring system or assessment of hemorrhage. Varying levels of disruption to the brain tissue resulted from the PCB stun that was applied at the collaborating establishment. As such, only heads where the thalamus could be clearly identified were used for thalamic contact plane assessment. Additionally, Gilliam et al. (2012) cautioned against the use of frozen and thawed heads for the assessment of TBI. It is unknown whether a PCB application at the ideal placement would have rendered these yaks immediately insensible. The relationship of the ideal placement that we described for male yaks 24 to 48 mo of age to the various recommended frontal placements for cattle, water buffalo, or bison is not known and warrants investigation.

Implications

The determination of an ideal PCB placement for yaks provides the basis for the development of guidelines for the stunning and euthanasia specific to yaks. It also better equips the individuals who perform these tasks with valuable information to safeguard animal welfare within slaughter plants and on farms. Further studies evaluating the impact of PCB use in this ideal frontal placement on the brain tissue, as well as the efficacy of its use at rendering an animal immediately insensible are warranted. Additionally, the evaluation of an alternative poll placement would be valuable to determine whether that is a viable option if the frontal ideal placement were not accessible. At this time, our data suggest that an ideal placement located at 25% of the way down the frontal plane of the head between the poll and nose of the animal maximizes the potential for bolt-thalamic contact when utilizing a PCB to stun or euthanize yaks. We expect that a bolt travel distance of 120 mm is required to ensure that the bolt will directly contact the thalamus for 97.5% of male yaks aged 24 to 48 mo.
