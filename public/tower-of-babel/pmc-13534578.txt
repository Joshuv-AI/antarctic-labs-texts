Single-cell profiling decodes patagium development in gliding mammal
Authors: YouFu Lin, ShuangXian Huang, LiFeng Che, HaoZhe Zhao, YaJun Wang, MingFei Yang, Cheng Ai, Jue Ruan, XiaoPing Wang, Li Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gliding patagium represents a key adaptation for mammalian flight, but its cellular development remains unexplored. Using single-nucleus RNA sequencing of embryonic flying squirrel patagium and dorsal skin, we construct a single-cell atlas of patagium development and identify two distinct fibroblast subpopulations (Fp2 and Fr) highly enriched in the patagium. These fibroblasts are characterized by the patagium upregulation of Wnt5a, Fgf7, and Fgf10, and are associated with patagium morphogenesis through dermal-epidermal putative communication interactions between dermal fibroblasts (Fp2 and Fr) and epithelial basal keratinocytes. Specifically, Fp2 fibroblasts are potentially involved in distal dermal condensation and epithelial thickening together with elevated Wnt5a expression, while both Fp2 and Fr fibroblasts could play a role in epithelial polarization and thickening through Fgf7 and Fgf10, as suggested by ex vivo assays. Our data suggest that gliding patagium development results from the co-option of conserved WNT and FGF signaling pathways within a specialized fibroblast-epithelial context, illustrating how modifications of conserved developmental programs give rise to derived morphological traits.Subject terms: Evolutionary biology, Evolutionary genetics, Embryogenesis

Introduction

Mammals exhibit remarkable diversity in locomotion and have acquired specialized traits in the evolutionary process of adapting to different forms of movement. Across terrestrial, arboreal, aquatic, and aerial environments, mammalian lineages have evolved distinct anatomical and physiological innovations to optimize survival. Among these adaptations, gliding locomotion represents a critical transitional strategy between terrestrial and aerial movement, bridging the ecological niche between ground-dwelling and fully volant species1,2. Gliding mammals occupy a distinctive evolutionary position, lacking the flapping flight mechanisms of bats and birds but achieving extended aerial mobility through aerodynamic structures. This adaptation has arisen convergently in multiple extant mammalian lineages, including flying squirrels (Rodentia, Sciuridae), marsupial gliders (Diprotodontia, Petauridae), and colugos (Dermoptera, Cynocephalidae)1, each evolving analogous morphological solutions to meet the demands of arboreal-to-aerial transitions3,4.

Central to gliding locomotion is the patagium, a specialized lateral skin membrane extending between the forelimbs and hindlimbs1,5,6. The patagium integrates elastic fibers, muscle bundles, and vascular networks to enhance durability and maneuverability7,8. It functions as an airfoil, modulating lift and drag forces during gliding7,9. Its biomechanical efficiency enables precise control over glide trajectories, allowing animals to evade predators, access dispersed food resources, and traverse fragmented habitats with minimal energy expenditure9,10, underscoring the importance of this evolutionary innovation.

Despite its functional importance, the developmental origins and regulation of the gliding patagium remain poorly understood. Previous studies on marsupial gliders (Petaurus breviceps, Petauroides volans, and Acrobates pygmaeus), using comparative genomics and bulk transcriptomics, have identified Emx2 as a critical transcription factor to regulate Wnt5a, driving patagium formation11,12; however, gaps remain at the cellular level, limiting insights into how different cell types orchestrate their contribution to patagium development. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) technology provides a means to dissect the cellular complexity of tissue morphogenesis. By profiling gene expression at a cellular resolution, snRNA-seq can reveal the dynamic trajectories and cell-cell communication networks underlying organogenesis13. Applying such approaches to gliding patagium development could elucidate the cellular basis of this morphological innovation.

In this study, we utilized snRNA-seq to investigate the cellular mechanisms of the developing patagium of the complex-tooth flying squirrel (Trogopterus xanthipes, Pteromyidae, Rodentia), a rodent species renowned for its gliding adaptations. We focused on the morphogenetic stages from pre-patagium to patagium bud appearance and patagium growth. This approach has generated a single-cell atlas of mammalian gliding patagium development. By integrating comparative transcriptomics with functional validation, we identified two distinct fibroblast subtypes (Fp2 and Fr) that are markedly enriched in the developing patagium, and importantly, uncovered that the dermal-epidermal signaling axis, mediated by interactions between dermal fibroblasts (Fp2 and Fr) and epithelial basal keratinocytes, is likely associated with the patagium morphogenesis. Our findings reveal how developmental programs are co-opted to generate this derived morphological trait for mammalian gliding adaptation.

Results

T. xanthipes reference genome

We carried out de novo sequencing and a chromosome-scale genome assembly of T. xanthipes, generating 913.83 Gb of genomic sequence with a 254-fold coverage (Fig. 1). T. xanthipes has an estimated genome size of ~3.17 Gb, and assembly-produced contig and scaffold N50 sizes of 4 Mb and 192 Mb, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). A genome completeness assessment (BUSCO alignment: 98.4%) indicated a nearly complete and high-quality genomic assembly. The T. xanthipes genome is predicted to contain 23,706 protein-coding genes (Supplementary Table 2).

Histological characteristics of T. xanthipes patagium

Histological analysis revealed spatiotemporal cellular characteristics during patagium morphogenesis. At the pre-patagium stage [Carnegie stage (CS) 16], dermis layer cells of both lateral skin (the pre-patagium domain) and dorsal skin exhibited a homogeneous distribution pattern. In contrast, from patagium emergence to growth stages (CS17-CS22), dermis layer cells of the patagium displayed a heterogeneous distribution pattern (refer to as the cell densification region, CDR), with an obviously higher density at the distal end (fold change > 3, P < 0.0001, Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig. 2a, c), compared to the homogeneous distribution of the dorsal skin. Besides CDR, the epithelial layer of the patagium during the developmental stages (CS17-CS22) was thicker above the CDR (fold change > 1.5, P < 0.05, Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Fig. 1a, b and Supplementary Fig. 2b, d) than the corresponding dorsal skin. These histological features were also reported in the patagium of marsupial11,12. In addition, from CS19 to CS22, the keratinocyte cells in the basal layer of the patagium’s distal end were clearly separated from the basement membrane, indicating epithelial cell polarization, in comparison to the tight attachment to the basement membrane in the dorsal skin (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). The observation of polarization occurring in the epithelium at the patagium’s distal end suggested the potential enhanced cell migration capabilities, which is a histological feature having not been described in marsupial patagium.

Single-cell profiles of T. xanthipes patagium

After quality control and doublet exclusion, we obtained high-quality single-cell profiles for a total of 208,596 nuclei from 20 samples (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 3). These cells were categorized into 13 major cell types (Fig. 3a, b and Supplementary Table 4). During patagium development, the main cellular components were fibroblasts (48.29–75.99%) and epithelial cells (including basal keratinocytes and spinous keratinocytes, 2.22–23.36%) (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 1). In both comparisons (CS17 pa vs CS16 ls and CS17-22 pa vs ds), the proportions of fibroblasts and epithelial cells were significantly increased in the patagium compared to non-patagium tissues (lateral or dorsal skin) (Fig. 3d, e). Notably, these two cell types showed different cell density distributions between the patagium developmental stages (CS17-CS22) and pre-patagium lateral skin (CS16) (Fig. 3f). Moreover, the fibroblast cell density distribution in the patagium during the developmental stages (CS17-CS22) differed from that in the dorsal skin (Fig. 3f).

Fibroblast profiles during patagium development

Fibroblast cells were partitioned to 7 subpopulations, including three progenitor (Fpro1-Fpro3) subpopulations that expressed Trps1, Ebf2, Pdgfra, Lrig1, Dlk1 and rRNA genes (Rps12, Rps19, Rplp1, Rps15a and Rps27a)14–17, the reticular fibroblast (Fr) subpopulation characterized by Fn1, Fbn1, Col1a2, Col1a1 and Dlk118,19, two papillary fibroblast (Fp1-Fp2) subpopulations that expressed Lef1, Axin2, Lrig1 and Col23a118,20,21, and a proliferation status (ProliF) subpopulation defined by a high expression of cycle genes (Top2a, Mki67 and Cenpf)22,23 (Fig. 4a, b).

Differential abundance analyses in both comparisons (CS17 pa vs CS16 ls and CS17-22 pa vs ds) showed that two subpopulations were enriched in patagium (Fp2, median log fold change: +0.8 [CS17 pa vs CS16 ls] / + 3.41 [CS17-22 pa vs ds]; Fr, median log fold change: +2.38 [CS17 pa vs CS16 ls] / + 1.1 [CS17-22 pa vs ds]) (Fig. 4c). These enrichment patterns can also be observed when patagium are compared with the corresponding dorsal skin at each developmental stage (Supplementary Fig. 4a). In addition, the proportions of Fp2 and Fr were significantly higher in the patagium compared with non-patagium tissues (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). Importantly, to further assess whether these patterns were reproducible independently of the abundance analysis, we performed a WGCNA analysis using bulk RNA-seq data and evaluated module associations with patagium within both comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 5). Two gene modules exhibited significant associations with patagium formation and development. Both modules exhibited increased activity in Fp2 and Fr, supporting an association of these fibroblast subpopulations with patagium development.

We also performed differential expression analyses between Fp2/Fr and the other fibroblast subpopulations to elucidate their possible function. We identified 163 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in Fr, and 153 in Fp2 (Fig. 4d, e, Supplementary Data 2 and Supplementary Data 3). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis suggested that Fr exhibited a high expression of genes related to extracellular matrix organization (GO:0030198 and GO:0032963), regulation of cell-substrate adhesion (GO:0010810, GO:0098609 and GO:0031589), regulation of locomotion (GO:0040017 and GO:0030336) and regulation of anatomical structure size (GO:0090066) (Fig. 4f). Fp2 exhibited a high expression of genes related to cell-cell signaling (GO:0007267), establishment or maintenance of apical/basal cell polarity (GO:0035089), negative regulation of locomotion (GO:0040013), regulation of cell-cell adhesion (GO:0098609) and growth (GO:0040007) (Fig. 4g). Interestingly, 33 DEGs were shared between Fp2 and Fr and enriched in the pathway of developmental growth involved in morphogenesis (GO:0060560) (Fig. 4h, Supplementary Data 2 and Supplementary Data 3).

To investigate the cross-species conservation of patagium-specific cell populations (Fp2/Fr), we compared our dataset with published bat patagium single-nucleus transcriptomic data using MetaNeighbor and SAMap. These two approaches consistently revealed a strong correspondence between the Fr fibroblast population in the flying squirrel patagium and the Pdgfd⁺mesenchymal progenitors (PDMPs) in the bat patagium. MetaNeighbor analyses using either species as the reference yielded high AUROC values (>0.8) for the Fr-PDMPs pairing (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b), and SAMap similarly assigned the highest alignment score to this pair (0.341; Supplementary Fig. 6c). Therefore, consistent with previous reports identifying PDMPs and FbI1-associated gene sets as key contributors to bat chiropatagium development24,25, we found that these gene sets are also specifically co-expressed in the Fr subpopulation of the flying squirrel patagium (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e). In contrast, the correspondence between Fp2 in the flying squirrel patagium and Meis2⁺mesenchymal progenitors (MMPs) in the bat patagium was weaker and less specific. MetaNeighbor yielded only moderate AUROC values (0.66–0.70), and SAMap assigned a lower alignment score (0.161), with MMPs showing comparable or higher similarity to other fibroblast populations (Supplementary Fig. 6a-c). Together, these results support an evolutionarily conserved role for Fr in mammalian patagium development, whereas Fp2 may represent a lineage-specific specialization in the flying squirrel.

A previous study has shown that the Wnt5a is able to drive cell densification12. Intriguingly, we found that, among the DEGs, Fp2 also highly expressed Wnt5a, suggesting that Fp2 may play a role in CDR during patagium development (Figs. 2b and 4i). Further clustering analyses of 153 Fp2-specific DEGs found that 50 DEGs (e.g., Eml5, Wnt5a, Fgf7 and Fgf10, Fig. 4j and Supplementary Data 3) were highly expressed in all patagium developmental stages (CS17-CS22), including Wnt5a, compared to lateral and dorsal skin. In situ hybridization (ISH) analyses showed strong signals for the Eml5 and Wnt5a genes in the dermis regions at the distal end of patagium during developmental stages (CS17-CS22), compared with low or undetectable signals in the dorsal skin (Fig. 5), suggesting their patagium-specific expression and potential role in Fp2 subpopulation cellular functions.

Differential abundance analyses in both comparisons (CS17 pa vs CS16 ls and CS17-22 pa vs ds) showed that the basal keratinocytes were enriched in patagium (median log fold change: +1.49 [CS17 pa vs CS16 ls] / + 2.47 [CS17-22 pa vs ds]) (Fig. 6c). This enrichment pattern can also be observed when patagium is compared with the corresponding dorsal skin at early (CS17) and late (CS22) stages (Supplementary Fig. 7).

We also performed differential expression analyses in basal keratinocytes to elucidate their possible function. We identified 316 DEGs in basal keratinocytes (Fig. 6d). GO enrichment analyses indicated that basal keratinocytes exhibited high expression of genes related to tissue morphogenesis (GO:0048729), cell adhesion (GO:0098609 and GO:0031589) and positive regulation of cell migration (GO:0030335) (Fig. 6e). Further clustering analyses of 316 DEGs in basal keratinocytes showed that, compared to lateral and dorsal skin, 20 genes (e.g., Lgr6, Ccdc3, Magi2 and Cachd1) exhibited sustained high expression levels from CS17 to CS22, potentially linked to epithelial thickening, and 19 genes (e.g., Grid2, Pxdnl, Hmcn1 and Sema6a) maintained an elevated expression pattern from CS19 to CS22, which may correlate with epithelial cell polarization (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Data 4).

Crosstalk between fibroblast and epithelial subpopulations

To investigate fibroblast-epithelial crosstalk during patagium development, we applied CellChat to infer ligand-receptor interactions from the transcriptomic profiles of 7 identified fibroblast and 2 epithelial subpopulations, thereby constructing a putative fibroblast-epithelial communication landscape.

Following Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction, we identified ligand-receptor interactions involving Fgf7, Fgf10, Wnt5a and Wnt2 that were significantly enriched in the patagium across both comparisons (CS17 pa vs CS16 ls and CS17-22 pa vs ds), particularly within inferred Fr/Fp2-to-epithelium communication (Fig. 7a, b). The enriched ligand-receptor interactions were mapped to FGF (Fgf7, Fgf10), canonical WNT (Wnt2) and non-canonical WNT (ncWNT, Wnt5a) signaling pathways (Fig. 7c). The proportions of fibroblasts expressing Fgf7, Fgf10, Wnt5a or Wnt2 across the 7 fibroblast subpopulations were significantly elevated in patagium tissue in both comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 8). In contrast, per-cell expression analysis of ligand-positive cells showed no significant differences between CS17 patagium and CS16 lateral skin, whereas Wnt5a and Fgf10 were significantly upregulated in CS17-CS22 patagium relative to dorsal skin (Supplementary Fig. 8). These results suggest that the enrichment of ligand-receptor interactions is accompanied by an increased abundance of ligand-expressing fibroblasts, and that per-cell expression changes occur in a context-dependent manner.

We next examined these candidates signaling programs during patagium morphogenesis. Temporal analysis showed that FGF and ncWNT signaling remained active from CS17 to CS22, consistent with potential sustained involvement during epithelial morphogenesis (Fig. 7d, e). Notably, the FGF signaling pathway predicted communication from fibroblasts to basal keratinocytes in the developing patagium (Fig. 7d). Intriguingly, ISH analysis further showed that Fgf7 and Fgf10 were highly expressed in the distal patagium, extending to the overlying thickened epidermis, at stages CS17-CS22 (Fig. 8a and Supplementary Fig. 9a-d). Explants cultured in mouse skin with dermally implanted beads soaked in recombinant Fgf7 and Fgf10 proteins displayed a significant increase in epidermal thickness compared to PBS-treated controls (Fig. 8b, c and Supplementary Fig. 10). In vitro wound healing assays further raised the possibility that recombinant Fgf7 and Fgf10 proteins significantly enhanced epithelial cell migration (Fig. 8d, e), suggesting that these factors may influence epithelial dynamics during tissue remodeling.

For ncWNT signaling, Wnt5a was prominently active during patagium development (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f). Intriguingly, Wnt5a is highly expressed in the distal patagium at stages CS17-CS22 (Fig. 5). Given its established role in promoting epithelial thickening12,28, we examined whether Wnt5a contributes to epidermal development. Consistently, skin explants cultured with Wnt5a-treated beads exhibited significantly greater epidermal thickness compared to PBS-treated controls (Fig. 8b, c and Supplementary Fig. 10). Together with the expression patterns, these findings suggest that Wnt5a may promote the thickening of the distal patagium epithelium during development.

These results collectively suggest that FGF (Fgf7/10) and ncWNT (Wnt5a) signaling represent candidate pathways potentially associated with epithelial cell migration and epithelial thickening, with Fr and Fp2 dermal fibroblasts serving as putative ligand-expressing subpopulations.

Discussion

Our study establishes a single-nucleus transcriptomic atlas of the flying squirrel patagium throughout embryonic development, enabling exploration of the cellular basis of this specialized gliding adaptation. We not only identify distinct fibroblast subpopulations but also infer candidate fibroblast-epithelial communication programs associated with patagium formation. This approach advances beyond previous gene- or pathway-level analyses to propose candidate cellular players and potential interactive mechanisms behind this trait innovation.

We identify two distinct, patagium-enriched fibroblast subpopulations i.e., Fp2 and Fr, that display distinct transcriptional and functional profiles compared to non-patagium fibroblasts. More importantly, our data suggest that inferred dermal-epidermal communication between these fibroblasts (Fp2/Fr) and basal keratinocytes (Bas) from CS17 to CS22 may contribute to patagium morphogenesis. These findings suggest the hypothesis that the emergence of specialized flight structures may involve spatiotemporally coordinated interactions between specific dermal fibroblast subpopulations and epithelial cells.

Our cross-species analyses indicate that the Fr subpopulation in the flying squirrel patagium shared the closest transcriptional homology with bat PDMPs (Supplementary Fig. 6a-c)24. This suggested that the subpopulation Fr may represent a conserved fibroblast subpopulation involved in patagium development. This inference is further corroborated by the distinct enrichment of patagium-related gene programs within Fr cells (Supplementary Fig. 6d, e)24,25. Notably, our study reveals that these same gene sets are not only positively selected but also specifically co-expressed in the Fr subpopulation within the gliding patagium (Supplementary Fig. 9), highlighting their evolutionary conservation in patagium development. More importantly, we further uncover a distinct fibroblast subpopulation, Fp2, which is specifically marked by the enrichment of Eml5 and Wnt5a gene sets. The previous study has shown that the Wnt5a is able to drive cell densification12. It suggests that the Fp2 subpopulation may promote distal fibroblast densification and contributes specifically to gliding patagium formation in the flying squirrel. Together, these results suggest that the development of the gliding patagium depends on the cooperative functions of both Fp2 and Fr subpopulations, which is distinct from the fibroblast regulation observed in the bat chiropatagium.

The central finding of our study is the identification of candidate dermal-epidermal communication programs between Fp2/Fr fibroblasts and basal keratinocytes that may contribute to patagium morphogenesis. Our data suggest that Fp2 fibroblasts are associated with inferred non-canonical WNT (ncWNT) communication with basal epithelial cells, primarily via the ligand Wnt5a. Notably, Wnt5a is markedly enriched in Fp2 fibroblasts compared to other fibroblast subpopulations across our dataset. Wnt5a is a known key activator of the ncWNT pathway, which plays an essential role in driving epithelial thickening12,28. Previous bulk transcriptomic analyses in the marsupial sugar glider identified high Wnt5a expression as vital to patagium formation and established Emx2 as its upstream regulator11,12. Interestingly, our bulk transcriptomic data from the placental flying squirrel revealed that Emx2 is also differentially expressed at higher levels in the patagium compared to dorsal skin, consistent with its proposed role in regulating Wnt5a-mediated patagium formation (Supplementary Fig. 11). These observations suggest that Emx2-associated regulatory mechanisms may be conserved between marsupial and placental gliders.

On the other hand, both Fp2 and Fr fibroblasts exhibited elevated expression of fibroblast growth factors Fgf7 and Fgf10, which were associated with inferred communication with the epithelial Basal layer and promoted epithelial cell migration (Fig. 8d, e). While previous studies have implicated Fgf10 in epidermal stratification and appendage budding, and Fgf7 in basal keratinocyte expansion during embryonic skin development29, our study identified distinct fibroblast subpopulations in the gliding patagium and characterized their specific signaling crosstalk with the epithelium. Crucially, our data suggest that these inferred Fp2/Bas and Fr/Bas communications likely play a role in the expansive growth and patterning required during patagium formation. Thus, our work identifies a dermal-epidermal communication framework as a candidate feature associated with the evolutionary development of the gliding patagium.

Overall, our study suggests that phenotypic innovations like the patagium emerge not from the evolution of entirely new genes or pathways, but through the spatial (the crosstalk between fibroblast and epithelial cells) and temporal (from CS16 to CS22) reorganization of conserved developmental programs. Specifically, our data suggest that the patagium develops possibly using core WNT (especially ncWNT) and FGF signaling pathways, which are known to be important for general skin development, but deploys them in a different cellular context. The key innovation may lie in the distinct expression signatures acquired by fibroblast subpopulations (Fp2 and Fr), including high Wnt5a expression in Fp2 and elevated Fgf7 and Fgf10 in both Fp2 and Fr. These subpopulations appear to form inferred communication relationships with epithelial cells (e.g., Fp2-basal keratinocytes via Wnt5a, and Fp2/Fr-basal keratinocytes via Fgf7/Fgf10), which may contribute to the distinct histological morphogenesis of the patagium (Fig. 9). Furthermore, comparative analysis with marsupial gliders and bats suggests both conserved developmental programs and lineage-specific adaptations, highlighting diverse evolutionary paths to analogous functional innovations.

In this model, Fp2 fibroblasts may contribute to distal dermal cell densification and epithelial thickening via Wnt5a secretion, while both Fp2 and Fr fibroblasts are candidates for influencing epithelial polarization and thickening through the secretion of FGF ligands (Fgf7 and Fgf10). Spi: spinous keratinocytes.

In summary, we propose a mechanistic hypothesis of evolutionary innovation, in which morphological diversity can be generated through the cell-dependent redeployment and integration of conserved developmental toolkits. Specifically, our findings implicate Fp2/Fr fibroblasts and associated ncWNT/FGF signals in patagium morphogenesis. However, current evidence is primarily derived from transcriptional profiling and in vitro functional assays (e.g., bead implantation in mouse skin explants), lacking direct in vivo loss-of-function validation, clarification of endogenous cellular sources, or in situ signaling axes. Thus, this mechanistic framework and candidate molecular interactions require further functional verification, including in vivo perturbations and cross-species comparisons.

Methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and were approved by the relevant ethical committees. Sampling of T. xanthipes specimens was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shaanxi Institute of Zoology (approval no. SDL22001GZ) and the Animal Research and Ethics Committee of Yunnan University (approval no. ynucae20190050). Mouse experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and were approved by the Animal Research and Ethics Committee of Yunnan University (approval no. ynucae20190050).

Genomic and embryonic single-nucleus transcriptomic (snRNA-seq) sampling

T. xanthipes specimens were housed at the Shaanxi Institute of Zoology under institutionally approved conditions. Female individuals aged 2.5–6 years were used for timed mating experiments. Animals were housed in outdoor enclosures under a natural photoperiod and ambient climatic conditions, with food and water available ad libitum. Adult individuals used for genomic sampling and pregnant females used for embryo collection were euthanized by intramuscular injection of an overdose of Zoletil 50 (tiletamine-zolazepam), in accordance with the approved institutional protocol. Death was confirmed by the absence of reflexes and respiration prior to tissue or embryo collection. Muscle tissue from an adult T. xanthipes was sampled to obtain genomic DNA (gDNA), to produce the reference genome for sn-RNA analyses. The gDNA was extracted using a DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, 51306) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

T. xanthipes embryos, representing five embryonic stages (CS16, CS17, CS19, CS21 and CS22) according to the Carnegie System30,31, were also sampled (Supplementary Table S5). CS16 represents the stage before the appearance of patagium, CS17 represents the stage of patagium bud emergence, and CS19-CS22 represent stages of patagium differentiation and growth. Patagium from CS17 to CS22, and the pre-patagium region, i.e., lateral skin from CS16 and dorsal skin from CS16-CS22, were collected from each embryo for single-nucleus (sn) RNA sequencing. The single nuclei were isolated using a nuclei isolation kit (cat. no. NUC201-1KT, Sigma-Aldrich).

Genome sequencing, assembly and annotation of T. xanthipes

To obtain a chromosome-scale genome of T. xanthipes, gDNA was used for Illumina short-read, PacBio long-read and 10X Genomics sequencing, as well as high-throughput chromatin conformation capture (Hi-C) sequencing. For Illumina short-read sequencing, a library with a 350 bp insert size was constructed based on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (Supplementary Table S6). For PacBio sequencing, SMRTbell libraries were constructed and sequenced on a PacBio Sequel platform. The 10X Genomics libraries were constructed using a Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3’ Kit v3 (10X Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA), and the libraries were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system. The Hi-C library was constructed and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq platform.

We performed a genome assembly using FALCON32 based on the short-read, PacBio long-read and 10X Genomics sequencing data to generate the initial contigs, followed by polishing with pbsmrtpipe in SMRT Link to improve base accuracy. SSPACE-LongRead33 and PBJelly (PBSuite/Documentation/Home) were used to construct and fill gaps in scaffolds. The resulting scaffolding was further optimized using Fragscaff (fragScaff download|SourceForge.net). Pilon34 was employed to correct small-scale errors using short-read data to obtain the assembled genome. The Hi-C reads were then mapped to construct the chromosome-scale assembled genome. Raw Hi-C paired-end reads underwent quality control by removing adapter sequences and low-quality reads using fastp35. The cleaned Hi-C reads were aligned to the polished PacBio contigs using Bowtie2, with stringent parameters (-L 30) to minimize mismatches and nonspecific alignments36. Only uniquely aligned paired reads were considered for further analysis. HiC-Pro was employed for duplication removal, sorting, and quality assessment to build raw inter- and intrachromosome contact maps37. LACHESIS was used to cluster, order, and orient the contigs into chromosome-level sequences to construct a chromosome-scale genome assembly of T. xanthipes.

To evaluate the quality of the genome assembly, we assessed its integrity using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO version 5.2.2) with a database of mammals (mamalia_odb10)38. The short-read and PacBio long-read data were aligned to the assembled genome sequence using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (bwa version 0.7.17) with default parameters39. Subsequently, the alignment of sequencing reads to the genome assembly and coverage analysis was assessed using SAMtools version 1.2140.

We annotated the T. xanthipes genome combining ab initio, transcriptome, and homology-based prediction. In the case of ab initio prediction, Augustus version 3.5.0 (http://bioinf.unigreifswald.de/augustus), Genscan version 1.041, SNAP version 2006-07-2842 and GlimmerHMM version 3.0.4 (https://ccb.jhu.edu/software/glimmerhmm/) were utilized. For transcriptome-based prediction, samples of 8 (kidney, heart, brain, skin, blood, muscle, lung and liver) and 12 (patagium, muscle, brain, skin, blood, pituitary, lung, pons, cerebellum, heart, eye and medulla oblongata) tissues were collected and used for the PacBio long-read and Illumina short-read transcript sequencing, respectively, which were performed with IsoSeq pipeline version 4.2.0 (https://github.com/PacificBiosciences/IsoSeq) and Stringtie version 3.0.0 (https://github.com/gpertea/stringtie), respectively (Supplementary Table S7). These transcripts were subsequently aligned to the assembled genomes using PASA version 2.4.143. For the homology-based method, we used Genewise version 2.4.144 and nonredundant amino acid sequences from Cricetulus griseus (hamster), Dipodomys ordii (kangaroo rat), Marmota marmot (marmot), Ictidomys tridecemlineatus (tree squirrel), Mus musculus (mouse) and Homo sapiens (human) as homologous protein. Subsequently, all gene annotation results were input into EVM version 2.0.0 for consolidation45.

Gene function predictions were carried out using three approaches. Initially, BLASTP version 2.14.046 was used to undertake similarity and homology searches within the orthologous gene database, setting an E-value cut-off at 1e-03 and an identity value at 30%. Subsequently, a domain similarity prediction strategy using InterProScan version 5.64-96.0 (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro) was utilized to investigate the conserved motifs, domains, and amino acid sequences. Finally, the KEGG pathway was predicted with KOBAS version 3.0 (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn/).

Embryonic histological analysis of Trogopterus xanthipes

To compare the histological changes of patagium and lateral and dorsal skin development, embryos with three samples per stage were dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series and embedded in paraffin. Transverse sections (5 μm) of the trunk were cut and prepared for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene (20 min each for xylene I and II) and rehydrated through a series of alcohol solutions (anhydrous ethanol I and II for 5 min each, and 75% alcohol for 5 min), followed by washing with water. For hematoxylin staining, sections were stained for 5 min, washed, differentiated, and blued in Scotts Tap Water. For eosin staining, sections were dehydrated in 85% and 95% alcohol (5 min each) and stained with eosin for 5 min. After final dehydration in 100% ethanol (5 min each for ethanol I, II and III) and clearing in xylene (5 min each for xylene I and II), sections were mounted with neutral gum. The stained sections were observed under a microscope, and images were captured for analysis.

Patagium and the corresponding dorsal skins from the embryos were used to quantify the epidermal thickness and cell density of dermis regions. The epidermal thickness was quantified with FIJI47, and the Adaptive Threshold algorithm implemented in OpenCV version 4.10.048. In the dermis regions, five to eight 100 μm diameter circles were drawn from the basement membrane towards the tissue center, with each circle passing through the center of the previous one. The cell density of the dermis regions was quantified using FIJI’s ‘Analyze’ function to measure average cell numbers within each circle. Statistical significance was determined using two-tailed unpaired t-tests.

Single-nucleus (sn)RNA-sequencing (seq) and quality control

The snRNA-seq libraries were generated using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ GEM Library & Gel Bead Kit v3.1 (cat. no. 1000121, 10X Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The amplified libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq platform (Illumina) by Biolink (Kunming), to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. The resulting files were aligned to the assembled genome of T. xanthipes and pre-processed using the Cell Ranger single-cell software suite (v8.0.1) (10X Genomics; https://www.10xgenomics.com/support/software/cell-ranger/8.0). We used Scrublet version 0.2.3 to predict and remove potential doublets, setting the expected_doublet_ rate to 0.0649. After the removal of predicted doublets, we further filtered out potential low-quality cells by examining the number of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and the proportion of reads mapping to the exonic regions per cell. Cells with total UMI counts outside the 1st to 99th percentile range or more than 5% mitochondrial content were excluded from the analysis.

Cell clustering and cell-type annotations

We initially used Harmony50 to correct for batch effects between samples. The cell population and subpopulation clustering processes were then performed using Scanpy version 1.10.251, including: (1) normalizing the UMI counts by the total count per cell, followed by log transformation; (2) selecting the 3,000 most highly variable genes and scaling the expression of each to zero mean and unit variance; (3) applying principal components analysis (PCA) and then using the top 30 principal components to calculate a neighborhood graph with 50 neighbors (n_neighbors = 50), followed by Louvain clustering at a resolution of 1.0; and (4) visualizing the data in a two-dimensional (2D) space using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). We performed cell type annotation based on the expression of canonical marker genes related to specific cell types published in previous studies.

Comparative analytical frameworks

To identify patagium-associated cellular and molecular programs while accounting for developmental stages and tissue contexts (lateral or dorsal skin), we performed two complementary comparative analyses. First, we compared CS17 patagium with the pre-patagium region (CS17 pa vs CS16 ls). Second, we grouped the patagium samples from CS17 to CS22 together and grouped the corresponding dorsal skin samples together and compared these two groups (CS17-22 pa vs ds). Unless otherwise indicated, all downstream analyses were based on these two comparisons.

Differential abundance testing (DAB)

Differences in cell abundance between patagium and non-patagium were assessed with MiloR version 2.1.352 using a k-nearest neighbor (KNN) graph-based test. Analyses were performed within two primary comparisons (CS17 pa vs CS16 ls and CS17-22 pa vs ds). In addition, stage-specific comparisons between patagium and stage-matched dorsal skin (CS17, CS19, CS21 and CS22) were performed to assess the consistency of abundance patterns across developmental stages. To mitigate potential biases arising from unequal cell numbers across biological samples, we performed downsampling at the sample level prior to Milo analysis. For each sample, cells were randomly selected to reach a target number defined as 90% of the minimum observed cell count. This procedure balanced cell representation across samples while retaining most of the available cells. We first used the buildGraph function to construct a KNN graph, using 50 principal components (d = 50). Next, we used the neighborhoods function to assign cells to neighborhoods based on their connectivity over the KNN graph. A different sample was included as a covariate in the testNhoods function. The log fold change in number of cells between patagium and non-patagium in each neighborhood were used for visualization.

Construct co-expression networks of bulk RNA

We constructed gene co-expression networks for each tissue using the Weighted Gene Correlation Network Analysis (WGCNA) R package (version 1.73)53. An unsigned adjacency matrix was built by raising the Pearson correlation matrix to a soft-thresholding power, which was transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM). Network dendrograms were generated via average linkage hierarchical clustering based on the dissimilarity TOM (1-TOM), and modules were identified using the cutreeDynamic function. Highly correlated modules were subsequently merged using the mergeCloseModules function with a merge cut height of 0.25. For each module, the module eigenvector (ME) was defined as the principal component of the standardized gene expression profiles. To assess module-trait associations, we calculated Pearson correlations between MEs and phenotypic traits. Statistical significance was evaluated using the corPvalueStudent function, which implements a Student’s t-test. Modules showing a correlation coefficient > 0.8 and P < 0.05 with the patagium phenotype were considered significantly associated, and their constituent genes were extracted for downstream analyses. Module-trait relationships were visualized in labelled heatmaps displaying correlation coefficients and corresponding P values.

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) identification

Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.46.0) to compare gene expression between patagium and non-patagium tissues in the bulk RNA-seq data54. Raw read counts were modeled using a negative binomial generalized linear model, and statistical significance was assessed with the Wald test. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the FDR. Genes with an adjusted P value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Volcano plots were generated using the ggplot2 R package to visualize the expression distribution.

For single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data, DEGs between subpopulations were determined using the function FindAllMarkers (Seurat version 5.3.0). We used the function FindMarkers (Seurat v5.3.0) to identify DEGs between patagium and non-patagium tissues within both comparisons. DEGs were identified using an average log2 fold-change (avg_LFC) threshold of 0.30, pct.1 threshold of 0.30 and an adjusted P value (p.adj) of less than 0.05. Gene ontology analysis of DEGs was performed using Metascape55. The average gene expression was calculated using the AverageExpression function (Seurat). Heatmaps were generated using pheatmap version 1.0.1256 and ClusterGVis version 0.1.1 (https://github.com/junjunlab/ClusterGVis).

Cross-species cellular transcriptome similarity analysis

To identify potential correspondences between cell type annotation in this study and those in the bat dataset24, we employed two complementary methods to assess cross-species cell type similarity. First, we performed MetaNeighbor analysis57 to evaluate cell type replicability across species. Orthologous gene pairs were predicted using OrthoFinder v2.2.658 with protein sequences as input, and only one-to-one orthologous were retained for subsequent analysis. Seurat objects were transformed into SingleCellExperiment format, and the unsupervised MetaNeighborUS function (with fast_version = TRUE) was applied to assess which cell types replicated across datasets. For each target cell type, the ranking of individual cells was aggregated into a cell-type-level score, expressed as the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), reflecting the degree of similarity to the reference cell type. Second, we performed SAMap analysis59 to assess evolutionary conservation at the cellular neighborhood level. Reciprocal OrthoFinder analysis between flying squirrel and bat coding genes was used to construct a gene-gene bipartite graph. Data integration was performed using the SAMap sm.run function with pairwise = True. The resulting mapping scores, obtained via the “get_mapping_scores” function, projected the two species’ datasets into a joint, lower-dimensional manifold representation, and the alignment score quantified the mapping strength between cell types across the two atlases.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Cell-cell communication among different cell subpopulations in different tissues was analyzed using CellChat v.2.2.060,61. To assess the communication probability, we followed the methodologies outlined in Jin et al.60,61. Only interactions detected in at least 10 cells per interacting cell group were retained. For snRNA-seq data, raw expression values were used for communication probability inference (raw.use = TRUE).

To minimize biases arising from unequal cell numbers across cell subpopulations, we performed stratified downsampling prior to CellChat analysis. Specifically, for each cell subpopulation within a given comparison, an equal number of cells was randomly sampled from each tissue based on the minimum observed cell count, thereby matching cell numbers between tissues and reducing abundance-driven inference bias. To ensure the robustness of inferred ligand-receptor interactions, we performed 100 independent iterations of downsampling followed by CellChat analysis, each with a distinct random seed. For each ligand-receptor pair and iteration, we calculated the mean communication probability across sender-receiver cell subpopulation pairs. We then estimated, for each iteration, the differences in mean communication probability between patagium and non-patagium tissues within each comparison (CS17 pa vs CS16 ls, CS17-22 pa vs ds). The statistical significance of these differences was assessed using a one-sample Wilcoxon signed-rank test (two-tailed), comparing the distribution of differences across iterations to a null median of zero. Resulting P values were subsequently corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, controlling the false discovery rate (FDR). Ligand-receptor pairs with an adjusted P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ligand expression analysis

To evaluate whether the enrichment of ligand-receptor interactions between patagium and non-patagium tissues reflected changes in the proportion of ligand-positive cells across all fibroblast subpopulations or differences in per-cell expression levels within ligand-positive cells, we applied two complementary approaches within each comparison (CS17 pa vs CS16 ls, and CS17-22 pa vs ds). First, a cell was defined as ligand-positive if its raw count was ≥ 1. For each ligand, we compared the proportion of positive cells between patagium and non-patagium tissues using chi-square tests and visualized the results as stacked bar plots. Second, to evaluate per-cell expression independently of cell abundance, we calculated the sample-level mean expression of each ligand exclusively from ligand-positive cells and compared values between patagium and non-patagium tissues using two-tailed Mann-Whitney U tests. All statistical analyses were performed in Python, with significance set at P < 0.05.

In situ hybridization (ISH)

The embryos were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 24 h. After dehydration in 20% and 30% sucrose overnight, the trunks were embedded in OCT and sectioned to generate 10-μm thick transverse sections. The sliced sample was circled with a Pap Pen. Proteinase K (20 μg/ml; Beyotime, ST533) was digested at 37 °C for 15 min. After washing with pure water, the samples were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Beyotime, ST477) three times, for 5 min each time. A total of 4 probes was designed for hybridization (Supplementary Data 5). For each probe, prehybridization solution was dripped onto slides and incubated at 60 °C for 1 h. The prehybridization solution was then removed, and the probe hybridization solution was dripped at a concentration of 1 μg/ml. Hybridization was performed overnight in a thermostatic incubator at 60 °C, followed by 2× Sodium Saline Citrate (SSC; Invitrogen, AM9763), a 65 °C wash, 2× 1.5 h, 0.5× SSC, 45 °C wash, 2× 1 h. Subsequently, the sections were washed for 2× 30 min with 0.1 M Maleic Acid-Tween Buffer (MSBT; Solarbio, G0051), followed by treatment with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Beyotime, ST025), and then incubated overnight with alkaline phosphatase-coupled anti-DIG antibody (1:2000, Roche cat# 11093274910). After being washed with 1× MABT for 2 h, the sections were stained with BM purple AP substrate (Roche cat# 11442074001) for 0.1 to 1 h. Finally, the sections were captured using a Pannoramic scanner (3DHISTECH Pannoramic MIDI, Hungary).

Cell migration using wound healing assay

Epithelial cells (Human keratinocytes cells, HaCaT cells, Procell, CL-0090) were cultured in 12-well plates until 100% confluent. A standardized wound was created using a scratch insert (Ibidi Culture-Insert 3 Well: 80369). After washing the wells twice with PBS to remove cell debris, the cells were treated with recombinant proteins Fgf7 (10 μg/ml), and Fgf10 (10 μg/ml) diluted in serum-free medium (Cusabio, Fgf7: CSB-MP008634HU-WD and Cusabio, Fgf10: CSB-MP008616HU-WD). The cells were then incubated at 37 °C for 12 and 24 h. Wound areas were imaged at 10× magnification immediately after creating the wound (t = 0 h) and at the end of the incubation period (t = 12 and 24 h). The residual wound width was measured using ImageJ v1.53 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The wound healing rate = (original wound area-actual wound area at the end of the incubation period)/original wound area ×100.

Dorsal skin explant culture

Outbred ICR mice (Mus musculus; CD-1 genetic background) were obtained from Yunnan University. Female mice aged 6-12 weeks were used for timed mating experiments. Animals were housed in individually ventilated cages under a 12 h light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2 °C and 55 ± 5% humidity, with food and water available ad libitum. Timed pregnancies were established by housing one male with up to two females per cage in the late afternoon. The presence of a vaginal plug the following morning was considered embryonic day (E) 0.5. Pregnant ICR mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation in accordance with the protocol approved by the Animal Research and Ethics Committee of Yunnan University (approval no. ynucae20190050), and death was confirmed before embryo collection. The embryonic dorsal skins of E14.5 were used for the explant culture. Mouse E14.5 was selected because the external morphological landmarks observed at this stage closely correspond to those seen in flying squirrel embryos at around CS21, at which the morphological differences between the patagium and the dorsal skin epidermis are most pronounced. Affi-Gel Blue beads (Bio-Rad, 1537301) were prepared and soaked in recombinant proteins using a protocol adapted from Feigin et al. 12. Briefly, beads were washed in sterile PBS, air-dried, and incubated with solutions containing recombinant Fgf7 (cusabio, CSB-MP008634HU-WD), Fgf10 (cusabio, CSB-MP008616HU-WD) or Wnt5a (R&D Systems, 645-WN/CF) proteins. Control beads were incubated with PBS under the same conditions. Explant cultures were performed in three independent experiments. Specifically, complete medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin) containing 4% agarose was poured into culture dishes and allowed to solidify. Under a dissecting microscope, protein-soaked or control beads were placed onto the solidified medium using fine forceps. Freshly isolated dorsal skin explants were then positioned on top of the beads, with the epidermal side facing upward. Explants were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 72 hours. After culture, explants were fixed, embedded in OCT compound, and cryosectioned for histological analysis. Statistical significance was determined using two-tailed unpaired t-tests.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Sample sizes were determined based on biological material availability and experimental feasibility. Independent biological replicates were defined as samples obtained from distinct embryos or animals. All statistical analyses were performed using R and Python, unless otherwise stated. Specific statistical tests, definitions of replicates, and visualization methods are described in the relevant Methods sections and figure legends. Multiple testing correction was applied where appropriate; for transcriptomic and cell–cell communication analyses, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method was used. No data were excluded from the analyses in this study. Randomization and blinding were not performed, as sample groups were defined by tissue type or developmental stage and did not involve intervention allocation. For computational analyses, automated pipelines were employed and fixed random seeds were set wherever applicable to support reproducibility.
