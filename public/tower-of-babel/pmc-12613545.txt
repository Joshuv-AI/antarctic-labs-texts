The landscape of dynamic tumor immunophenotyping on neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with trastuzumab for the treatment of HER2-positive breast cancer
Authors: Hongtao Jin, Yaru Yang, Changjian Yan, Mei Liu, Xue He, Dan Yuan, Wei Zhang, Yunqing Mu, Chaoling Wu, Xiaoni Liu, Jinyuan Xiao, Qizhao Long, Min Li, Xiaoyu Hao, Yuqi Zhang, Wenxin Qi, Yan Liu, Zining Yu, Dan Guo, Zijian Zhao, Yanzhao Li, Weilong Zhang, Xiuru Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFor HER2-positive breast cancer (BC), patients who achieve pathologic complete response (pCR) are predicted to have better clinical outcomes. With the advent of the era of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) combined with targeted therapy, the pCR rate of HER2-positive BC has increased significantly, but about 50% of these patients still do not respond to targeted therapy. The dynamic changes in the tumor microenvironment (TME) may crucially influence pCR outcomes in HER2-positive BC. However, there is no specific immunophenotyping study that correlates with the efficacy during treatment.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive spatial expression profiling analysis on 94 ROIs procured from 28 HER2-positive BC patients, encompassing both their initial diagnostic stage and postoperative status. Leveraging DSP techniques, we precisely mapped and quantified the TME at these critical time points for patients.ResultsNotably, we classified specimens into four groups: IM1, IM2, TM1, and TM2. IM1 and TM1, characterized by CD4⁺ naïve T cells and naïve B cells, showed higher pCR rates, whereas IM2 and TM2 exhibited lower rates. In addition, baseline IM1 patients who were classified as TM1 postoperatively achieved a 100% pCR rate, while baseline IM2 patients who were classified as TM2 postoperatively had a poor pCR rate of only 10%. The immunophenotypes defined by spatial transcriptomics were reproducible in both TCGA and single-cell datasets, and spatial analysis further revealed structured cellular transitions and functional heterogeneity at the tumor–immune interface.ConclusionOur dynamic immunophenotyping can provide prediction of the efficacy for NAC with trastuzumab in HER2-positive BC patients, and provide a basis for the selection of subsequent treatment regimens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-025-02483-8.

Introduction

HER2-positive breast cancer (BC) patients account for 15–20% of all breast cancer types. It is usually associated with poor prognosis, high aggressiveness, and a high rate of tumor recurrence [1–6]. With the advent of the era of molecular targeted therapy, dual HER2 blockade regimens, such as the combination of pertuzumab and trastuzumab with chemotherapy, have greatly improved the prognosis of patients with HER2-positive BC [7–11]. Pathologic complete response (pCR) is an important metric for early assessment of treatment response in neoadjuvant regimens, and patients with pCR have longer disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) than those without pCR [12–16]. Although the addition of HER2-targeted agents to NAC significantly improves the incidence of pCR in early-stage HER2-positive BC, about 50% of patients still have residual disease after treatment [17–19]. The identification of accurate and reliable biomarkers to predict the response of HER2-positive BC to HER2-targeted therapies has become imperative.

BC is classified as moderately immunogenic, with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and HER2-positive BC considered the two most immunogenic subtypes [20–23]. The efficacy of immunotherapeutic agents for HER2-positive BC is expected to be favorable due to the better response observed in highly mutated tumors with high infiltration of immune cells. Studies have shown that cytotoxic treatments and anti-HER2 drugs can further activate the immune system through immunogenic cell death and antibody-dependent cytotoxicity (ADCC), respectively. The immune system, in turn, plays an important role in the prognosis of HER2-positive BC and contributes to the efficacy of trastuzumab [24–29]. Therefore, understanding the tumor microenvironment (TME) in HER2-positive BC is a key element in the study of biomarkers for this disease.

Many studies have focused on the role of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), histological grading, and other factors in predicting the efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy for HER2-positive BC [23, 30–33]. In the past decade or so, TILs have been very meaningful in the prediction of pCR in neoadjuvant therapy for HER2-positive BC [34–37]. High levels of TILs predict better response to trastuzumab therapy and higher pCR rates in HER2-positive BC [38–41]. However, only using TIL, which forms part of TME, as a predictor for studying the efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy in HER2-positive BC is incomplete. Most of the current studies of TME in HER2-positive BC are based on hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained tissue sections, which do not allow for analysis of TME at the comprehensive and genomic level. In only one case where spatial proteomics was characterized before and after neoadjuvant therapy for HER2-positive BC, McNamara et al. found that proteomic changes were beneficial in identifying tumors that eventually developed pCR [42]. In spatial omics research, the tumor–immune interface (also referred to as the tumor–inflammation boundary zone) represents a spatially resolved region located at the junction between malignant tumor cells and adjacent immune-infiltrated stromal areas [43]. This region is functionally important as it harbors direct interactions between tumor and immune cells, often enriched in CD45⁺ leukocytes, macrophages, and other components of the TME. Prior studies have demonstrated that this interface region is transcriptionally distinct from both tumor cores and normal tissues and plays a crucial role in immune surveillance, therapeutic response, and immune evasion [44].

To explore changes in the TME before and after neoadjuvant therapy at spatial level in HER2-positive BC patients, we analyzed and processed baseline and postoperative specimens from 28 HER2-positive BC patients treated with NAC in combination with trastuzumab using GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiling (DSP) technology to accurately differentiate the tumor samples by subtypes across the temporal dimension. We developed and validated an immunophenotyping approach across multiple datasets.

Methods

Patients and samples

30 HER2-positive BC patients were enrolled in this study, all of whom were treated with NAC in combination with trastuzumab, but not all patients received pertuzumab treatment (Supplementary Table 1). For each patient, both baseline (core needle biopsy before treatment) and postoperative (surgical excision) FFPE tumor specimens were collected, yielding 60 FFPE samples in total. All samples were assembled onto tissue microarrays (TMA) for spatial profiling. pCR was determined by a pathologist at Shenzhen People’s Hospital and defined as the absence of any residual invasive carcinoma in the breast and axilla (pCR = ypT0/is, ypN0). The above study protocol meets the ethical demands of Shenzhen People’s Hospital. The pathological response to neoadjuvant therapy was evaluated using both the Miller–Payne (MP) grading system and the Residual Cancer Burden (RCB) classification. The MP system compares tumor cellularity between pre-treatment core biopsies and post-treatment surgical specimens, and assigns a 5-grade score based on the reduction in tumor cells [45]. In contrast, the RCB system incorporates measurements of the residual tumor bed and nodal involvement into a composite score (RCB-0 to RCB-III), providing a quantitative estimate of residual disease burden. RCB scores were calculated using the standardized online calculator provided by MD Anderson Cancer Center (www.mdanderson.org/breastcancerRCB), and are considered more comprehensive due to inclusion of nodal response [46].

GeoMx DSP RNA assay sample preparation and digital spatial profiling

Unless otherwise specified, all steps followed the manufacturer’s standard protocol for the GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler (DSP) RNA assay. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue Sect. (4 μm) were mounted on positively charged slides and baked at 60 °C for 1.5 h. Slides were deparaffinized in a xylene substitute (CitriSolv), rehydrated through graded ethanol (100%, 95%), and rinsed in PBS. For antigen retrieval, sections were incubated in 1× Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) at 99 °C for 20 min, cooled to room temperature, and washed in PBS. RNA targets were exposed by incubation with Proteinase K at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by a PBS wash. Post-fixation was performed with 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) and NBF stop buffer, followed by PBS rinses. H&E scanning was performed on Motic EasyScan (Motic, Xiamen, China).

In situ hybridization was performed using the GeoMx Human Whole Transcriptome Atlas – Human RNA for NGS (NanoString Technologies, Bothell, WA, USA), with slide processing by the GeoMx RNA Slide Prep Kit for FFPE (NanoString). Morphology and ROI guidance used the GeoMx Solid Tumor TME Morphology Kit (Human RNA Compatible) (NanoString). Slides were loaded onto the GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler (NanoString Technologies, Bothell, WA, USA) for ROI selection and UV-photocleavage, and oligonucleotide tags were collected into the GeoMx DSP Collection Plate (NanoString). Libraries were prepared according to the GeoMx NGS workflow and sequenced on the MGISEQ-2000 (MGI Tech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China).

ROI selection was guided by H&E staining and immunofluorescence markers—PanCK (tumor), CD45 (immune), and CD68 (macrophage)—with a focus on tumor–immune interfaces. Two patients were excluded because no qualified ROIs could be obtained at either time point, leaving 28 patients with evaluable samples (Supplementary Table S1 and S2). In patients who achieved pathologic complete response (pCR), where residual invasive carcinoma was absent, immune-enriched ROIs were preferentially selected (CD45/CD68). All ROI selections were reviewed and confirmed by two board-certified pathologists.

Raw sequencing data from the GeoMx® DSP RNA assay (Whole Transcriptome Atlas) were processed using the GeoMx NGS Pipeline v2.2.0.2. ROIs were also filtered out if the proportion of trimmed reads (reads with low-quality bases and sequencing adapters removed), or aligned reads (reads mapped to the reference sequences) was less than 80% of raw reads. Additionally, ROIs with sequencing saturation < 50% were removed to ensure adequate coverage and library complexity. Deduplicated reads (after PCR duplicate removal) were retained for quantification. A total of 94 ROIs were retained for analysis, with an average of 1.92 ± 0.9 ROIs per sample (Supplementary Table S1). Among these, 87 ROIs were classified as tumor-associated and 7 as immune-stromal-associated. The average raw read count per ROI was 4,880,408, and the mean UMI Q30 score was 0.982. All ROIs passed quality control (Supplementary Table S2 and S3).

Differential expression analysis

All differential expression analyses were performed using Limma package (version 3.54.2) in R (version 4.2.2) [47]. Thresholds for both the p-value and a log fold-change (p-value < 0.05, logFC >0.3) were applied to select the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) related to treatment response (pCR or non-pCR) in all tumor samples, baseline tumor samples, and postoperative tumor samples (Supplementary Tables 4–8). DEGs associated with the immune classification were selected by the thresholds for p-value < 0.05 and logFC >1. ClusterProfiler (version 4.6.2) was used for KEGG enrichment analysis based on the DEGs associated with each class [47, 48]. Immune–related and cancer-related pathways were chosen from the identified pathways to represent primary functional characteristics of each class.

Establishment of gene-signatures’ prediction for treatment response

Two genes were selected based on their differential expression associated with pCR: one of the most highly upregulated genes and one of the most highly downregulated genes. As well as analyzing the expression levels of these two genes individually, we also evaluated their expression ratio as a composite marker. Therefore, three signatures were used for prediction: Gene A expression, Gene B expression and the Gene A/Gene B expression ratio. A logistic regression model was constructed for each signature to assess its predictive capacity for treatment response. We adopted LogisticRegression from scikit-learn (version 1.1.2) in Python (version 3.9.15) to train the classifier using a stratified 5-fold cross-validation method as implemented in StratifiedKFold. Area under the curve (AUC) of receiver operating characteristics (ROC) was computed to evaluate classifier performance.

Estimation of immune cell fraction

The xCell package (version 1.10) was used to estimate the composition of cells in the expression matrix of DSP samples [49]. At each time point, we used t-tests to analyze the relationship between cell types and treatment response to screen for cell types with a significant correlation (p < 0.1) with treatment response. As a result, 13 cell types, B-cells, CD4 + naïve T-cells, CD8 + T-cells, CD8 + central memory T cell (CD8 + Tcm), Class-switched memory B-cells, lymphatic endothelial cells (LEC), Mast cells, Monocytes, naïve B-cells, NKT, Plasma cells, pro B-cells, and gamma-delta T (Tgd) cells, were selected as candidate features for immune classification.

Construction of immune classification model

A finite-loop immune classification method was employed to categorize samples at each time point. The method consisted of three steps: in step 1, the number of clusters (K = 2) and the minimal number of cells (n_cell = 6) were set. In step 2, we used the combn function to generate all possible combinations of 6 to 13 cell types from the 13 candidate cell types. For each combination, the ExecuteCNMF method from the CancerSubtypes package (version 1.24.0) [50] was applied to cluster the samples into two groups. In step 3, we then evaluated each clustering result by calculating the average silhouette width and the consistency of treatment response within clusters. For each clustering result, we assessed the consistency between clusters and treatment response using the average of the maximum proportion of pCR or non-pCR samples within each cluster. To explore different immune classification models, all possible combinations of 6 to 13 immune cell types were generated from the 13 candidate types, and steps 2 and 3 were repeated for each combination. Only clustering solutions with an average silhouette score >0.75 and response consistency >0.75 were considered acceptable. Among these, the combination of cell types yielding the highest average silhouette width and response consistency was selected as the optimal immune classification model. In the optimal clustering solution, a cell type was defined as the signature cell of a cluster if its average score in that cluster was higher than in the other cluster. The code for this analysis is available on GitHub (https://github.com/yuandyuand13/BRCA_immune_classification).

Validation of immune classification in TCGA-BRCA

To validate the efficiency of optimal immune classification model in baseline, we downloaded the mRNA HTseq-FPKM data and survival data of TCGA Breast Cancer (BRCA) cohort from UCSC Xena platform (Supplementary Table 9) [50]. Only 62 HER2-positive samples were selected as the validation data with reference to the known HER2 status in clinical table as previously described [51]. Ensembl ids were converted into genes using the one Ensembl id with the highest median values per gene. The cell enrichment scores were done by xCell and scaled across samples. For each sample, we calculated the average score of signature cells in each cluster and assigned it to the cluster corresponding to the signature cells with the higher average score. Survminer package (version 0.4.9) and Survival packages (version 3.5-5) were employed for survival analysis of the DEG in TCGA-BRCAs. Samples were stratified into high and low groups according to the cutoff value of each gene obtained by the surv_cutpoint function. Overall survival analysis was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test.

Validation of immune classification at the single-cell level

Single-cell RNA-seq data comprising 71,945 cells from mammary gland tissues of eight pretreatment HER2-positive breast cancer (BC) patients were collected, including 47,362 cells from GSE161529 and 24,583 from GSE180286, all generated using 10x Genomics platforms (Supplementary Tables 10–13) [52, 53]. Downstream analysis was performed using Scanpy v1.11.1 [54]. Quality control was applied to retain cells with a mitochondrial gene expression fraction < 15%, total UMI counts >1,000, and gene counts >500. Doublets were predicted and excluded using the Scrublet al.gorithm with default parameters. After filtering, 8,853 high-quality cells remained for further analysis.

Batch correction was performed using the Harmony algorithm. Clustering was conducted with a resolution parameter set to 2, and dimensionality reduction and visualization were achieved using UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection). Cell type annotation was performed based on canonical marker genes and gene set scoring using the xCell reference [55], with signature scores calculated using UCell v2.2.0 [56]. Patient-wise clustering based on cell-type composition was performed using Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF) v0.28 (https://github.com/renozao/NMF).

Spatial transcriptomic data collection and analysis

Spatial transcriptomic data were obtained from one treatment-naïve HER2-positive breast cancer patient, publicly available from the 10x Genomics Visium dataset (https://www.10xgenomics.com/datasets/human-breast-cancer-visium-fresh-frozen-whole-transcriptome-1-standard).

Standard downstream processing was performed using spacexr v2.2.1 (https://github.com/dmcable/spacexr). Spatial deconvolution was conducted with the RCTD algorithm to infer cell-type proportions across tissue spots. Spatial domain expansion distances were calculated based on tumor regions using SpaTopic v0.1.0 [57]. Gene signature enrichment analysis was performed using UCell v2.11.1 [56], applying functional gene sets derived from the IOBR v0.99.0 package [58].

Pathway enrichment analysis

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using Reactome, GO, KEGG and MSigDB datasets to elucidate the functions of DEGs in different immune subtypes [48, 59–62]. In the enrichment pathway analyses of pCR and non-pCR as well as TM1 and TM2, we used Reactome, GO and KEGG databases for the analyses. For pathway enrichment analysis of IM1 and IM2, we used the MSigDB database. The p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) method, and the adjusted p-values (q-values) were obtained. Pathways with q-value < 0.1 were considered statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

In the clinical data, continuous variables were presented as means (SD), and categorical variables were expressed as counts (percentages). Fisher’s exact test and t-test were utilized to examine the correlation between categorical or continuous variables and immune classification. A significance level of p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Since each patient’s sample may contain two immunotypes, we classify patients according to the most prominent immunophenotype when conducting clinical analysis at the patient level.

Results

Gene-signatures’ prediction for treatment response in HER2-positive BC

A total of 94 ROIs were retained for analysis from 28 patients; specifically, 87 were tumor-associated and 7 were immune-stromal–associated. ROIs were prioritized at tumor–immune interfaces using H&E/IF morphology (tumor/PanCK, green; CD45⁺ immune cells, yellow; CD68⁺ macrophages, red; nuclei/DNA, gray), as illustrated in Fig. 1A. An overview of the study workflow—including sample collection, ROI selection, GeoMx DSP profiling, data preprocessing/normalization, immunophenotyping, and external validation—is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. By demonstrating the DEGs in the pCR and non-pCR groups, we found that many of them were immune-related genes. Among them, the DEGs in non-pCR were mainly related to metabolic regulation (AZGP1, THRA, FABP4), tumor proliferation and metastasis (AZGP1, STC2, OSTN). DEGs in the pCR group mainly included genes related to immune response and defense (e.g. LYS, S100A11, IFI30, HLA-DPB1, FCGR3A and IGHM) and genes related to metabolism and signaling (e.g. SPP1, TMSB10, CTSB and S100A10) (Fig. 1B). Pathways enriched in pCR include many immune-related pathways such as Fc gamma R-mediated phagocytosis, MHC class II antigen presentation and immune response-activating cell surface receptor signaling pathway, etc. Whereas the pathways enriched in the non-pCR group are mostly related to gene expression regulation and hormone pathways, such as RNA Polymerase I Transcription Initiation and hormone binding (Fig. 1C). Moreover, we found that OR4Q2 and AZGP1 were significantly up-regulated in the non-pCR group compared to the pCR group. Among them, OR4Q2 was the most up-regulated gene overall, while AZGP1 was the most up-regulated immune-related gene. Conversely, GCNT1 and SPP1 were significantly up-regulated in the pCR group compared to the non-pCR group, with GCNT1 being the most up-regulated gene and SPP1 the most up-regulated immune-related gene (Fig. 1D). To construct gene signatures for predicting treatment response, we selected two genes for each comparison: the most significantly upregulated gene and the most significantly downregulated gene associated with pCR status. In addition to evaluating the individual expression levels of these two genes, we calculated the expression ratio between them. Thus, three predictive indicators were derived from each group: the upregulated gene expression, the downregulated gene expression, and their ratio. Each of the three metrics (the two individual gene expression levels and their ratio) was evaluated for its predictive performance. In all samples, the combined expression of GCNT1/OR4Q2 was a better predictor of treatment effectiveness compared to the expression of GCNT1 and OR4Q2 individually (AUCGCNT1=0.77; AUCOR4Q2=0.77; AUCOR4Q2/GCNT1=0.85, Fig. 1E). In the baseline group, ITIH1 and MMP11 were the most up-regulated and down-regulated genes with differential expression. Expression of ITIH1/MMP11 was a better predictor of treatment efficacy compared with expression of ITIH1 and MMP11 alone (AUCITIH1=0.78; AUCMMP11=0.79; AUCITIH1/MMP11=0.91, Fig. 1E). In the postoperative group, HMGCS2 and RGS1 were the most up-regulated and down-regulated genes with differential expression. Expression of HMGCS2/RGS1 was a better predictor of treatment effectiveness compared with individual expressions of HMGCS2 and RGS1 (AUCHMGCS2=0.92; AUCRGS1=0.87; AUCHMGCS2/RGS1=0.99, Fig. 1E).

Immune classification of HER2-positive BC samples at baseline

At baseline, we used finite-loop immune classification method in order to obtain various immune classification models. With this approach, we selected one of the optimal cell combinations from 5812 cell combinations as the signature cell for immunophenotyping the baseline group, and we successfully classified it into IM1 and IM2 (Fig. 2A-C). We screened the baseline group ROIs to prioritize regions that contained both tumor regions (green), CD45 (yellow), CD68 (red), and DNA (grey) (Fig. 2D). The signature cells including Tgd cells, naïve B-cells, CD4 + naïve T-cells and B-cells were more abundant in IM1, whereas class-switched memory B-cell, LEC and CD8 + Tcm were more abundant in IM2 (Fig. 2E). In particular, the amount of B-cells in IM1 was most significant compared to IM2 (p = 0.0059, Fig. 2F), while LEC had the remarkable amount in IM2 compared to IM1 (p = 3e-06, Fig. 2F). We displayed the main immune-related DEGs in IM1 and IM2 in the form of heatmaps. Among them, the main up-regulated immune genes in IM1 were related to the expression of B-cell functional genes (e.g. IGHM, IGHG1, IGHG2, IGKC, IGHG3, IGHG4, IGHA1). In addition, cluster analysis also showed that the DEGs that were mainly upregulated in IM2 included genes related to the innate immune system (e.g. DEFB4B), genes related to coagulation (e.g. KNG1), and genes related to the GPCR pathway (e.g. TGFB2, PAK5, PENK, GALR2) (Fig. 2G). However, no significant differences in hormone receptor-related genes were seen between the two immune immunophenotypes (T-test, p > 0.5, Fig. 2H). Based on the DEG analysis shown in Fig. 2I, HNRNPH1, ERCC1, SRSF2, ZNF124, and MEI1 were identified as the top five genes significantly upregulated in the IM1 subtype compared to IM2. These genes are mainly involved in RNA splicing, transcriptional regulation, and DNA repair, biological processes that are often associated with enhanced genomic stability and less aggressive tumor phenotypes. In contrast, KCNE2, ADCY4, MINAR2, SPAG6, and MED12L were the top upregulated genes in the IM2 group relative to IM1. These genes are linked to cellular signaling pathways, transcriptional modulation, and cell motility, and their elevated expression may contribute to increased tumor invasiveness and metastatic potential observed in the IM2 subtype (T-test, p < 0.05, Fig. 2I). IM1 subtype exhibited enrichment of pathways primarily related to B cell function, cell proliferation, immune response regulation, and cellular stress responses. Notable examples include GAVISH 3CA metaprogram B cells respiration and GOBP B cell receptor signaling pathway, indicating a functionally active and adaptive immune microenvironment. In contrast, the IM2 subtype was enriched for cytokine and interferon-associated pathways, such as HALLMARK interferon alpha response, GAVISH 3CA metaprogram b cells interferon, and GOMF immune receptor activity. These enriched pathways reflect heightened interferon signaling and altered cytokine dynamics, which are closely linked to immune regulation within the tumor microenvironment. The predominance of such pathways in IM2 may signify dysregulated immune activation and a shift toward an immunosuppressive or immune-evasive phenotype, potentially contributing to reduced treatment responsiveness (Fig. 2J). The NAC with trastuzumab treatment showed distinct response between IM1 and IM2. Patients of IM1 had more possibility to present pCR after NAC with trastuzumab than IM2 (p = 0.006, Fig. 2L).

Immune classification of BRCA samples at postoperative

At postoperative, we selected one of the optimal cell combinations from 5812 cell combinations in the same way as the signature cell for immunophenotyping the postoperative group, and we successfully classified it into TM1 and TM2 (Fig. 3A-C). We show the typical ROIs for pCR and non-pCR samples in TM1 and the typical ROIs for pCR and non-pCR samples in TM2, respectively (Fig. 3D). The signature cells of monocytes, naïve B-cells, LECs, class-switched memory B-cell and CD4 + naïve T-cells were abundant in TM1, whereas pro B-cells and NKT were more abundant in TM2 (Fig. 3E). Among them, the immune score and microenvironment score were significantly higher in TM1 than in TM2. Class-switched memory B cells and CD4 + naïve T cells were more enriched in TM1 compared with TM2 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3F). We displayed the main immune-related DEGs in TM1 and TM2 in the form of heatmaps. Cluster analysis revealed that the main differential genes expressed in TM1 were associated with major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-related genes, including HLA-DRA, HLA-DPB1, HLA-E, HLA-A, and CIITA. In addition, TRAC and CD3E, which are expressed on the surface of T-lymphocytes and play an important role in T-lymphocyte adaptive immunity, were also significantly expressed. We also observed that C3, IL7R, ACKR1 and PTGDS, related genes involved in complement and inflammatory responses, were also significantly expressed in TM1. While NGF, TMSB15A, and BMP10 were significantly expressed as part of the cytoskeleton in TM2 (Fig. 3G). Additionally, there were no differences in the hormone receptor-related gene expression of ESR1, ESR2, PGR and ERBB2 among TM1 and TM2 (T-test, p > 0.05, Fig. 3H). Based on the differential expression analysis illustrated in Fig. 3I, LAT, SELL, COL23A1, LIMD2, and MPEG1 were identified as the top five genes significantly upregulated in the TM1 subtype. Among these, LAT, SELL, and MPEG1 are primarily involved in immune and inflammatory responses, while SELL and MPEG1 also participate in cell adhesion and migration, suggesting an active immune microenvironment and potential for enhanced antitumor immunity in TM1. In contrast, TMPRSS11B, FBXO36, IFNA14, FUT9, and OR2G3 were the most significantly upregulated genes in TM2 compared to TM1. Currently, these genes have not been well characterized in the context of BC, and their functional relevance in tumor biology remains to be further explored. (T-test, p < 0.05, Fig. 3I). The main roles of the enriched pathways in TM1 focus on the regulation of immune responses, immune cell activity and signaling, and immune surveillance and killing in the TME, including the T Cell Receptor Signaling Pathway, the B Cell Receptor Signaling Pathway and the PD-L1 Expression and PD-1 Checkpoint Pathway. Enrichment of these immune pathways reflects potent anti-tumor immune responses as well as immune checkpoint regulation. The activation of these pathways contributes to more effective infiltration, recognition and clearance of tumor cells by immune cells in the TME (Fig. 3J). The expression of immune genes associated with antigen presentation, cell adhesion, co-inhibition, ligand and receptor showed higher expression in TM1 than in TM2 (Fig. 3K). After performing response statistics on postoperative samples of patients with both immunophenotyping, we found that TM1 had a higher pCR rate to NAC combined with trastuzumab treatment (Fisher’s exact test, p = 0.001, Fig. 3L).

The dynamic tumor immunophenotyping during treatment

Comparing baseline immune typing with the postoperative immune typing, we can find that 11 samples in IM1 turned to TM1 after neoadjuvant treatment and 18 samples in IM1 became TM2. However, 11 IM2 turned to TM1, while 51 IM2 became TM2 after neoadjuvant treatment (Fig. 4A). By combining the immune typing before and after treatment and using response to drug therapy as an outcome, we can see that the IM1 + TM1 group had the best response to NAC with trastuzumab, with a 100% pCR rate. The IM2 + TM2 group had the worst treatment efficacy, with only 10% of patients showing a pCR (Fisher’s exact test, p = 4.56e-10, Fig. 4B). We show the ROIs for one patient in each of the four groups IM1 + TM1, IM1 + TM2, IM2 + TM1, and IM2 + TM2 for the baseline and postoperative groups (Fig. 4C). Plotting the Venn diagram across immune typing combinations of signature cells, we found that both IM1 and TM1 showed CD4 + naïve T cells and naïve B cells abundant (Fig. 4D). In contrast, IM2 and TM2 share no signature cells expression (Fig. 4E). In addition, we found that these two combinations of the best and worst treatment effects share common intersecting genes. The results showed that the genes co-expressed in IM1 and TM1 focused on antigen presentation (ERAP2, TAPBP, HLA-DPB1, RFX5), T cell activation (LAT and TNFRSF14) and immune escape (TYK2). The main biological functions of genes co-expressed in IM2 and TM2 are focused on signaling (GALR2, NR2E1, SEMA3D and ESRRB) and immune and defense responses (LEAP2 and SEMA3D) (Fig. 4D-G).

Multi-level validation and spatial mapping of tumor–immune heterogeneity in HER2-positive breast cancer

We selected 62 HER2-positive samples from the TCGA database as validation data for analysis. After clustering the featured cells corresponding to higher average scores, we found that the samples from these independent databases were consistent with the baseline group subtype classification in our article (Fig. 5A). High expression of Tgd cells, CD4 + naïve T cells and B cells represented longer OS (Supplementary Fig. 2 A). Comparing the differences between IM1 and IM2, we found that stroma score, microenvironment score and LEC were higher in the IM2 group compared to IM1 (T-test, p < 0.05, Fig. 5B). There is a strong correlation between the relative abundance of immune subtypes (IM2/IM1 ratio) in different cell types in the DSP baseline cohort and TCGA cohort in our study (Fig. 5C). Additionally, we plotted the OS curves for TCGA-BRCA samples based on the highly expressed genes identified in IM1 and IM2. The results suggested that high expression of RAD21, BSPRY, TATDN1 and NUDCD1 in IM1 corresponded to low prognostic risk. These genes are linked to cell cycle regulation and DNA repair (RAD21 and TATDN1) as well as protein regulation and cell migration (NUDCD1 and BSPRY). There were also a number of genes in IM1 whose high expression represented better OS in patients, mainly including transcriptional and translationally regulated genes (NUP98, PABPC4, EIF3H) as well as signaling and cell cycle regulatory genes (RNF139). In contrast, high expression of ANAPC13, CAPN6, SLC25A29, and TUBAL3 in IM2 correlated with poorer OS. These genes are involved in cell cycle regulation and protein degradation (ANAPC13 and CAPN6) as well as metabolism and mitochondrial function (SLC25A29 and TUBAL3). Furthermore, we found that immunophenotyping was not associated with tumor stage and number of lymph node metastases (Supplementary Fig. 2B-C). Their aberrant expression in BC may contribute to tumor proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and increased invasiveness.

To characterize the cellular heterogeneity and spatial organization of the tumor–immune interface in HER2-positive BC, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomic analyses on treatment-naïve tumor samples. Using scRNA-seq data from four HER2-positive BC patients, we identified 15 distinct cell types based on canonical marker expression and gene set scoring (Fig. 5D, Supplementary Fig. 3A–B). Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) clustering of cell-type abundance revealed subtype-associated cellular patterns, with B cells and LECs displaying distinct enrichment profiles that were consistent with the IM1 and IM2 immunophenotypes derived from bulk transcriptomic data (Fig. 5E). Gene signature scoring further confirmed that IM1-specific genes were enriched in B cells, while IM2-specific genes were preferentially expressed in LEC (Fig. 5F, Supplementary Fig. 3C), supporting the relevance of these cell types to distinct immune microenvironment states.

We next analyzed spatial transcriptomic data from one HER2-positive BC patient to investigate how tumor and immune cell populations were distributed within the tissue. Tumor-enriched regions were delineated using RCTD-based deconvolution, with remaining regions defined as tumor-adjacent microenvironment (Fig. 5G, Supplementary Fig. 3D). The spatial distribution of known tumor markers such as EPCAM, CD24, and KRT19 further confirmed the accurate identification of tumor regions (Supplementary Fig. 3E).

To examine the spatial relationships between tumor cells and the surrounding immune microenvironment, we constructed distance-based expansion models centered on the boundary between tumor-enriched regions and adjacent tissues (Fig. 5H). This boundary, which we consider to represent a spatial correlate of the histologically defined inflammatory–tumor interface, served as a reference point for analyzing gradients in cellular composition and functional properties. Along this spatial axis, we observed a clear transition from tumor-dominated regions to immune-enriched zones (Fig. 5I). Functional pathway analyses of tumor cells at varying distances from the interface revealed significant differences between regions proximal to the boundary (ROI1, 0 to − 30 μm) and those located deeper within the tumor core (ROI2, − 70 to − 100 μm), particularly in pathways related to immune interactions and tumor progression (Fig. 5J).

Association of immunophenotypes and clinical characteristics

By comparing the clinical characteristics of IM1 and IM2, we revealed significant disparities in age, MP grading, and RCB classification between IM1 and IM2 (Fig. 6A, p<0.05). In the postoperative group, only MP grading and RCB classification was significantly different between TM1 and TM2. Age of patients did not differ between TM1 and TM2 (Fig. 6B). Based on patient-level clinical data, significant differences were observed between the IM1 and IM2 immunophenotypes, as well as between the TM1 and TM2 immunophenotypes, in terms of age, Miller–Payne (MP) grade, residual cancer burden (RCB) classification, and pCR status. Patients classified as IM1 or TM1 immunophenotypes were younger and exhibited better responses to neoadjuvant therapy compared to those with IM2 or TM2 immunophenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 4 and Fig. 5). We display a summary plot of the four BC immunophenotypes in Fig. 6C.

Discussion

Early studies on HER2-positive BC primarily focused on the role of HER2 as a tumor marker and its potential as a target for therapy, with less emphasis on the role of the immune microenvironment. While targeted therapies such as trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and T-DM1 have demonstrated significant efficacy in treating HER2-positive BC, some patients eventually develop resistance, limiting the overall therapeutic effect. Consequently, immunotherapy is emerging as a new avenue for treating HER2-positive BC. The immune microenvironment plays a crucial role in determining the effectiveness of immunotherapy. The presence of immunosuppressive mechanisms within this microenvironment can undermine the response to immunotherapy, whereas an increase in activating immune cells, such as TILs, can enhance therapeutic outcomes. However, the heterogeneous and complex TME continues to have a critical impact on cancer progression and treatment response in HER2-positive BC. Refining tumor subtypes and understanding their spatial organization remain challenging. Utilizing digital spatial technologies, we investigated the interactions and spatial arrangements of various cell types within HER2-positive BC, capturing these heterogeneities and identifying dynamic tumor-immune subtypes within this cancer type.

Most initial studies on the immune microenvironment focused on TILs, revealing that high TIL levels are generally associated with a better prognosis in patients with HER2-positive BC. Elevated TILs suggest not only a heightened sensitivity to HER2-targeted therapies but also a potentially improved response to immunotherapy [22, 33, 35, 40, 63]. The clinical relevance of TILs in HER2-positive BC has also been evaluated in neoadjuvant therapy. In a meta-analysis including five randomized clinical trials testing multiple anti-HER2-based therapies in the neoadjuvant setting, significantly higher pCR rates were observed in the high TIL subgroup [64, 65]. In our study, we found that immune subtypeswith predominant expression of TILs such as naïve B cells, CD4 + naïve T cells, and B cells responded more efficiently to treatment, showing high rates of pCR. Furthermore, we identified that subtypes with better drug response had higher expression of immune-related genes, such as IGHM, IGHG1, IGHG2, IGKC, IGHG3, IGHG4, IGHA1, C3, IL7R, ACKR1, and PTGDS. The enrichment pathways of these genes were closely associated with TILs and immune checkpoints.

Yet TILs represent only a small fraction of TME. The components of the TME and their intricate interactions play a pivotal role in influencing the response to NAC and targeted therapy among HER2-positive BC [34, 35, 66–74]. However, our understanding of the dynamic changes that occur in the tumor immune microenvironment during treatment remains limited, with only one DSP-based study using digital spatial analysis to model longitudinal tissue specimens for predictive purposes using spatial proteomics [42]. In addition, fewer studies have characterized HER2-positive BC. Recently, Shao et al. identified four subtypes based on genomic, transcriptional, protein, metabolic, and immunologic multi-omics data from 191 postoperative patients with HER2-positive early BC [75]. However, our study was the first to not only analyze the dynamics of the immune microenvironment in specimens before and after treatment of HER2-positive BC but also to successfully perform dynamic immunophenotyping. Patients in the baseline group were divided into two immunophenotyping groups, IM1 and IM2, and further refinement of the immunophenotyping preoperatively and postoperatively was also done for TM1 and TM2, respectively. The IM1 group had a stronger antitumor immune response, high expression of immune checkpoint genes (HLA-DPB1, TNFRSF14), and higher abundance of Tgd cells, memory B-cells, and CD4 + primary T-cells. In contrast, the IM2 population had a smaller proportion of immune cells, suggesting that its immune microenvironment may be more conducive to tumor growth. Postoperative staging also showed significant differences, with the TM1 group having a similar immune cell composition to IM1, and CD4 + primary T cells and naïve B cells being highly expressed in this subtype. IM1 and TM1 had better neoadjuvant efficacy compared to IM2 and TM2, and the pCR rate for this group of patients whose baseline group was IM1 and who became TM1 after treatment was 100%. The pCR rate of patients who were IM1 at baseline and became TM2 after treatment decreased. This suggests that the immune microenvironment is dynamically changing during neoadjuvant therapy and has an impact on the efficacy of neoadjuvant therapy. Then, during neoadjuvant therapy, the assessment of patients’ neoadjuvant therapy efficacy can not only be based on imaging assessment, but also by re-performing puncture biopsy to clarify the changes of the immune microenvironment in the patient’s tumor in order to adjust a more precise and personalized treatment plan.

To strengthen the biological interpretability of our immune subtype classification, we integrated single-cell and spatial transcriptomic data from treatment-naïve HER2-positive BC. The scRNA-seq analysis revealed that the IM1 and IM2 subtypes are characterized by distinct cellular compositions: IM1 samples were enriched in B cells and CD4⁺ naïve T cells, while IM2 samples showed increased proportions of LEC and CD8⁺ central memory T cells. These cell-type-specific patterns were consistent with our bulk transcriptomic clustering and highlighted the cellular basis underlying the immune subtype divergence.

Furthermore, the analysis of gene signatures specific to each cell type demonstrated that IM1 markers were predominantly found in B cells, while IM2 markers were more prevalent in endothelial-like cell populations. This suggests that immune subtypes not only vary in terms of immune activity but also exhibit unique architectural features of the immune ecosystem. Moreover, spatial transcriptomic analysis pinpointed that immune cells were more concentrated near the tumor borders, creating a well-organized tumor-immune interface. The composition of cell types shifted across different spatial zones, from the tumor’s core to the surrounding immune-rich regions, highlighting a structured and diverse spatial distribution within the immune microenvironment.

Our findings highlight the potential of dynamic immune classification as a predictive biomarker for response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy combined with trastuzumab in HER2-positive BC. To translate this immunophenotyping strategy into a clinically relevant assay, several key steps are required. First, a simplified, robust gene panel—based on the top differentially expressed genes or immune cell-type signatures identified in our study—should be developed and optimized for routine use on clinically available FFPE samples. This could take the form of a targeted spatial transcriptomics assay or multiplexed IHC-based surrogate. Second, clinical validation in large, prospective, multicenter cohorts is essential to confirm the reproducibility, robustness, and predictive power of the classification across different platforms and treatment regimens.

Ideally, these validation trials should be integrated into studies that mirror the current gold standard treatments, such as dual HER2 inhibition and antibody-drug conjugates. Further, combining these with current clinicopathologic characteristics and genomic tests could enhance the precision of risk categorization and treatment choices. Moreover, developing assays to a regulatory standard necessitates evaluating factors like inter-laboratory reliability, cost-effectiveness, and time efficiency to ensure their practical application in clinical settings. Should this immune classification system be confirmed, it could help tailor the intensification or reduction of neoadjuvant treatments, aiming to boost patient results without unnecessarily overtreating them.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we performed an exhaustive analysis of molecular profiling changes and tumor immune microenvironment changes in HER2-positive BC patients before and after treatment with NAC combined with trastuzumab. We found that immune subtypes more abundant in CD4 + naïve T-cells and naïve B-cells in both primary and postoperative specimens had the best effect on chemotherapy combined with trastuzumab treatment. Most importantly, our baseline and postoperative immunophenotyping of HER2-positive BC patients could provide a basis for more precise treatment in the future.
