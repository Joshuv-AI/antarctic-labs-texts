The dynamics of body composition in yellow-feathered broilers
Authors: Sai Zhang, Long Li, Jinling Ye, Qiuli Fan, Zhongyong Gou, Zongyong Jiang, Shouqun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The objectives of this study were to: 1) fit the growth curve of body weight, components, and composition according to the Gompertz model; 2) predict body energy from body protein and fat based on multiple linear regression; and 3) identify the best predictors of body protein or fat based on an allometric growth model. A total of 840 1-d-old Qingyuan Partridge chickens were assigned to two gender groups (initial body weight: male 27.31 ± 0.12 g, female 27.54 ± 0.49 g), with six replicates of 70 chicks each. The birds were weighed every 10 d over a 120-d period. The body components (breast or thigh muscle and abdominal fat) were measured by dissection, and body composition (water, protein, fat, and ash content) was measured by whole carcass homogenization. The Gompertz model was used to fit the growth curve, and the derivative function was further applied to assess the daily growth rate. Multiple linear regression was used to calculate the body protein and fat energy content. Allometric functions were fitted to compare the growth rate of body components and composition to body weight. In male broiler, the Gompertz equation for body protein mass was Wt=464.30×e-e-0.027×t-55.67; and the equation for body fat mass was Wt=192.50×e-e-0.042×t-46.24. In female broiler, the equation for body protein mass was Wt=328.56×e-e-0.027×t-52.01; and the equation for body fat mass was Wt=437.81×e-e-0.024×t-71.93. The growth of male breast muscle and female thigh muscle were the most proportional to body protein deposition, as exponent was close to 1. In conclusion, the Gompertz model efficiently depicted body weight, components, and composition change. The Gompertz equation for body protein and fat can be applied to further model daily nutrient and energy requirements.

Introduction

Yellow-feathered broilers are Chinese local breed, mostly raised in southern China, and account for approximately 30%–40% of the broiler market. They are especially favored by Chinese consumers due to their superior flavor and taste compared with white feathered broilers (Wang et al., 2022). However, the growth curve and body composition of these indigenous breeds remain largely unknown, and it hinders the application of a precision nutrition strategy for yellow feathered broilers, since growth curve and body composition are key information to develop dynamic model for nutrient requirement. Therefore, research on the dynamics of body composition in yellow-feathered broilers is of great significance. Various nonlinear mathematical models (e.g., Gompertz model, Logistic model, and Richards model, etc.) have been used to simulate poultry growth curve and nutrient deposition (Rizzi et al., 2013; Sakomura et al., 2011; Şengül and Kiraz, 2005), among which the Gompertz model is mostly preferred due to better data fitness and parameters with explicit biological meaning (Caldas et al., 2019).

The essence of broiler production is to convert nutrients into high-quality poultry products. A comprehensive understanding of body composition dynamics is therefore fundamental to elucidating feed utilization, animal growth, and ultimately, to guiding broiler production strategies. Firstly, body composition around the time of slaughter determines the marginal changes in carcass values (de Lange et al., 2001), serving as a rule of thumb for deciding the best market age. Secondly, knowledge of body composition aids improvement of growth efficiency and carcass quality, since lean tissue growth rate and ratio of lean-to-fat directly affect body weight gain and carcass characteristics (Bochno et al., 2005; Gous, 2014; Rizzi et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2009). Thirdly, in terms of nutrient requirement, the deposition rate of body protein and fat is of prime importance (Gous et al., 1999) because: 1) both of which dominantly determine the dietary energy requirement; and 2) deposition of body protein is the largest determining factor of dietary amino acid requirement. In summary, a comprehensive understanding of body composition dynamics facilitates the development of dynamic requirement models using factorial approaches.

The objectives of this study were to 1) determine the Gompertz model for body weight, components, and composition; 2) develop a prediction model of body energy content based on body protein and fat using multiple linear regression; and 3) determine the best predictor among body components for body protein using an allometric growth relationship. The ultimate goal of this work was to develop a dynamic nutrient requirement model for indigenous Chinese broiler breeds using a factorial approach.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangdong Academy of Agricultural Sciences Management Guide (GAASIAS-2023-083).

Experimental animals and design

Qingyuan Partridge chickens (Guangdong Tiannong Food Co., Ltd., Qingyuan, Guangdong, China) were used as the yellow-feathered broiler model in this study. A total of 840 1-d-old Qingyuan Partridge chicken (420 males and 420 females, respectively) were assigned to two gender groups using a completely randomized design. Each group contained six identical floor pens (biological replicates), with 70 birds per pen. Birds were reared from d 1 to 120.

Feed and water were provided ad libitum throughout the trial (Table 3 and Fig. 1). All birds were housed in an environmentally controlled room with controlled lighting. All birds were subjected to a continuous light regime for the first three days. Subsequently, the light was gradually reduced by 1.5 h/week until a 10-h light regime was achieved. Temperature was maintained at 30 °C in the first 14 d and gradually reduced to 25 °C on d 30.

Measurements and methods

Body weights were measured (Table 2) every 10 d and 2 chickens with median weight from each replicate were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation. Before euthanasia, the chickens were fasted for 12 h to eliminate digesta in the gastrointestinal tract. After euthanasia, one carcass was used to dissect the breast muscle, thigh muscle and abdominal fat for body components. The other carcass was used for body composition analysis based on the comparative slaughter method. To ensure a sufficient sample for chemical analysis, additional birds were sampled and homogenized during the first month (d 1, 10, 20, and 30). Four birds per replicate pen were homogenized on d 1 and 10, three birds per replicate pen on d 20, and two birds per replicate pen on d 30.

Comparative slaughter method

Whole carcass was processed according to comparative slaughter method as follows: 1) coarse crushing, the carcasses including feather were processed using poultry grinder (model 160, Xingtai YuSheng Machine Manufacturer, Xingtai, Hebei, China); 2) fine crushing, the bone mud mill (GN-200, Xingtai YuSheng Machine Manufacturer, Xingtai, Hebei, China) was used for further ground; 3) drying, the samples were sterilized at 105 °C for 15 min, and then dried at 65 °C for 48 h; 4) pulverization, the dried samples were ground the third time with a cyclone mill (CT 293 Cyclotec, FOSS Tecator AB, Höganäs, Sweden). The processed carcass was weighed in each step. The quadrant method was applied to collect samples for chemical analysis.

Chemical analysis

Crude fat content was determined using an automatic fat analyzer (KOM-XT15i extractor, ANKOM Technology Co., Macedon, NY, USA) referring to method 920.39 (AOAC, 2023). The crude protein content was analyzed using a Kjeltec Auto Analyser (KjeltecTM 8200, FOSS Tecator AB, Höganäs, Sweden) according to established procedure (GB/T 6432-2018; China National Standard, 2018). Ash content was determined according to the established procedure (GB/T 6438-2007; China National Standard, 2007) using Muffle furnace (SX2-12-10, Dongtai Hongxiang Electric Furnace Manufacturing Co., Ltd., Dongtai, Jiangsu, China). The gross energy (GE) was analyzed with a bomb calorimeter (Parr 6400, Parr Instruments Co., Moline, IL, USA) following the AOAC method (2023). The relative weight of body water, crude fat, crude protein and ash as a percentage of body weight were calculated for each replicate.

Data statistics and analysis methods

Data model fitting

Growth curves for body components (breast muscle, thigh muscle, and abdominal fat) and body composition (water, protein, fat, and ash) in Qingyuan Partridge chickens were fitted using the Gompertz model (Eq. [1]) proposed by Gous et al. (1999).

The derivative of the Gompertz equation was further analyzed (Eq. [2]).Wt=WA×e-e-B×t-ti,dWt/dt=K×Wt×lnWA/Wt,where Wt is the body weight at age t (d); WA is the asymptotic mature weight (maximum mature weight); B is the maturity rate; ti is the age at which the maximum growth rate was reached.

An allometric growth model was used to correlate body components or composition in relation to body weight.(Eq. 3)Y=a×Xbwhere Y is the weight of body components (breast muscle, thigh muscle, abdominal fat) or body composition (water, protein, fat, or ash); X is the body weight; a is the intercept of linear regression on the logarithm of Y; b is the relative growth (allometric growth) coefficient.

Two independent variables (X1 is protein and X2 is fat content) were used to fit a multiple linear regression model (least square method) to predict body energy content.Y=β0+(β1×X1)+β2+(β2×X2)+ε,where Y is the body energy content (kcal); β0 is the intercept; β1 is the partial regression coefficient for body protein; X1 is body protein content/g; β2 is partial regression coefficient of body fat; X2 is body fat content/g; ε is error term.

Statistical analysis

Power analysis was performed using the pwr package in R (version 4.4.2). The data of body components (breast muscle, thigh muscle, and abdominal fat), body composition (water, protein, fat, and ash), and body energy were analyzed by the aov() function. The Tukey HSD test via the HSD.test() function from the agricolae package, was used to analyze the mean value when the variance analysis was significant. The statistical significance level is P < 0.05, and the extremely significant level is P < 0.01. The nls() function (nonlinear least squares) was used to fit the data according to the Gompertz model. The gender effect of the curve parameters was tested by analysis of variance, using the aov() function. Discussion

The daily nutrient requirement of broilers was estimated using a factorial approach (NRC, 2012), which quantifies the daily nutrient needs based on the daily body nutrient deposition and the efficiency of nutrient deposition. Comparative slaughter is the core methodology of this study. It should be noted that the model developed in this study was based on the entire feathered carcass. In line with this study, Caldas et al. (2019) homogenized the whole body (including feathers) to construct growth curves, whereas Emmans and Gous (2025) investigated the feather-free carcass. It was acknowledged that the amino acid composition of feathers differs from that of the remaining tissues. Feathers, which are primarily composed of keratin, contain the highest levels of methionine and cysteine (Li et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2024). Moreover, feather growth reaches maturity earlier than that of the feather-free body (Wecke et al., 2017). Therefore, separating feathers from the carcass provides more informative data than homogenizing the entire feathered carcass. The feather-free body also conforms more closely to the Gompertz model (Emmans and Gous, 2025), thereby enhancing the comparability of data among reported literature.

Despite the limitations of analyzing the whole feathered carcass, the methodology employed in this study remains aligned with the overarching objective. The primary aim of modeling protein and fat deposition is to develop a dynamic prediction model for daily nutrient requirements. Consequently, even when feathers and the feather-free body are analyzed separately, the total daily requirement must ultimately be integrated. Further research is also warranted to compare a single Gompertz model for feathered carcasses with the combined results of two separate Gompertz models—one for feathered carcasses and another for feather-free carcasses—by summing their predictions. Additionally, the differences in maturation timing between feathers and the feather-free body may vary between strains. Compared to white-feathered broilers, which typically reach feather maturity between 21 and 28 d, yellow-feathered broilers exhibit a slower growth rate, causing the feather growth curve to shift rightward, potentially extending to 35 to 42 d or later (Li et al., 2020). Thus, the disparity between feathers and the feather-free body may be less pronounced in yellow-feathered broilers, a hypothesis that warrants further investigation. Moreover, the contribution of feather protein deposition to overall protein deposition is relatively minor in broilers compared to layers (Fatuhe et al., 2004; Zelenka et al., 2011). Wecke and Liebert (2019) reported that feather protein deposition accounted for 4.6% of total body protein deposition during week 1, increasing to an average of 14.6% by week 5 in Ross 308 broilers. Therefore, based on the aforementioned reasons and given that the substantial workload required to separate feathers from the carcass, this study analyzed the entire feathered carcass.

Good candidate models need to be both mathematically and biologically sensible. The Gompertz model has three built-in parameters with clear biological meaning (Eq. [1]). The biological relevance of these parameters lies in their direct correspondence to key growth features. Specifically, the asymptotic weight (WA) represents the theoretical mature weight that the animal would achieve if allowed to grow indefinitely, while the inflection age (ti) corresponds to the age at which the maximum growth rate is attained. Liu et al. (2013) simulated the growth of Qingyuan partridge chicken based on Gompertz model with the mature weight of 1932.32 and 1652.17 g for the male and female, respectively, lining well with this observation (male: 2190.23 g; female: 1780.32 g). However, mature weight of white broilers was reported to be greater than those in this study for yellow broilers, owing to different growth potential (Caldas et al., 2019; Henn et al., 2014; Marcato et al., 2008).

The study observed that body protein deposition reaches a plateau at around 120 d of age, whereas fat deposition continues to increase, especially in female broilers. In fact, protein accretion is ultimately governed by genetics, which sets an upper limit on deposition, while fat deposition is more heavily influenced by diet (Marcato et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2012). However, note that broilers were fed the same diet, but they presented very distinct fat deposition trend, suggesting gender associated with genetics and endocrine played an important role. This study found a positive correlation between body components (abdominal fat, breast, or thigh muscle) and age, lining well with other reports (Goliomytis et al., 2003; Gous et al., 1999; Sütó et al., 1998). Body weight is a comprehensive indicator of fat, muscle, and bone accretion. As sexual maturity approached during the middle and late stages of growth, the rate of abdominal fat deposition increased rapidly (Archer and Sobotik, 2022). Practical nutritional strategies should involve appropriate restriction of dietary energy during this period to prevent excessive abdominal fat accumulation from compromising carcass quality (Sahraei and Mohammadi Hadloo, 2012).

The inflection is a built-in parameter of the Gompertz model that corresponds to the location of the line where the concave curve becomes convex. Biologically, it is related to the maximum growth rate, or the highest metabolic demand (Tjørve and Tjørve, 2017). Unlike the Logistic model, the Gompertz model is a right-skewed curve with an inflection point at 37% of the maximum weight (setion 2.4.1 WA is asymptotic value), and it is broadly applied to most of the animal growth (Tjørve and Tjørve, 2017). Conversely, the Logistic model is a symmetric curve with the inflection point at 50% of the maximum weight, and this model is typical for describing plant growth (van der Klein et al., 2020). The 37% growth point is not merely a theoretical concept; it holds practical value in both breeding and nutrition. Achieving the full genetic potential and maximizing body weight under practical production conditions is challenging. The inflection point, occurring at 37% of the maximum weight, can serve as a reliable predictor of maximum growth potential, which is pivotal for breeding evaluations (Uckardes and Narinc, 2014). In terms of nutrition and feeding, the “37% maximum weight” marker allows for the precise estimation of the growth inflection timing, thereby facilitating the formulation of nutrient-dense feeds to maximize growth potential (Tjørve and Tjørve, 2017).

The inflection point for leg muscle growth in male broilers occurs 19 d later than that in female, whereas the inflection for breast muscle growth in male broilers occurs 8 d earlier. Emmans (2022) noted that the timing of inflection point is determined by the Gompertz rate parameter B, and B should remain constant within the same sex (Emmans and Gous, 2025). Thus, the ages at which the inflection points occur for all body components should be identical for each sex. The challenge of fitting Gompertz models to individual body components while maintaining a consistent B parameter led Emmans and Gous (2025) to propose a correction method that adjusts the mature weight (A) of each component to achieve a uniform B across all components. To further enhance the rational use of Gompertz model, the correction method proposed by Emmans and Gous (2025) needs to be seriously considered. Although correcting parameter B is mathematically sound, numerous studies have reported distinct growth patterns between breast and leg muscles. The development of the breast muscle, which is primarily composed of fast-twitch fibers (Cheng et al., 2022), is dominated by hypertrophy (Scheuermann et al., 2004). In contrast, the development of the thigh muscle, which is primarily composed of slow-twitch fibers (Cheng et al., 2022), involves both hypertrophy and hyperplasia (Ren et al., 2024).

The daily weight gain was usually included as an independent variable to predict energy requirement (Afrouziyeh et al., 2022; Gou et al., 2022) by assuming body energy deposition and weight gain were linearly correlated. However, such an assumption was based on the prerequisite that body protein: fat ratio remains relatively constant. In the present study, the body protein fat ratio significantly decreased with age, suggesting a correlation between body energy deposition and weight gain will deviate from linearity. Therefore, it is necessary to factorize total energy deposition into body protein and fat energy (Ewan, 2001), as proposed by the factorial method, which is more competitive and plausible. In addition, directly measuring the energy densities of body protein and fat is extremely challenging; therefore, employing multiple linear regression offers a more practical and feasible approach (Caldas et al., 2019).

In this study, the regression models were constructed based on body energy, protein, and lipid contents with ages. Consequently, the derived energy density coefficients represent the average energy densities of body protein and fat over the entire experimental period. The majority of relevant studies retain the constant term in multiple linear regression models (Caldas et al., 2019; Lancaster, 2022; Moran and Drouillard, 2005). Theoretically, the constant term lacks a clear biological interpretation; however, it is statistically significant and therefore included in the models. While Caldas et al. (2019) acknowledged the statistical significance of the constant term, it was noted that “not of much biological value,” emphasizing that the focus should primarily be on the energetic contributions of protein and fat rather than the constant. Evidently, the constant term represents the energy contribution from non-protein and non-fat tissues, which may include a small amount of carbohydrates (glycogen) and free energy reserves within the body. It may also reflect some inevitable systematic errors that warrant further investigation.

The present study found body energy content in males and females as 6.23 and 5.72 kcal/g for protein, 8.19 and 8.93 kcal/g for fat. Energy densities of body protein and fat are theoretically expected to exhibit minimal sex differences. However, the observed discrepancy may be due to the use of pooled energy density coefficients over the entire experimental period, as mentioned earlier. Since the deposition curves for protein and fat differ between sexes, the pooled coefficients may also vary accordingly. Protein energy was higher compared to Okumura and Mori (1979) who reported 5.66 kcal/g and Caldas et al. (2019) with 5.45 kcal/g. Fat energy in the present study was lower compared to Okumura and Mori (1979) (9.35 kcal/g) and Caldas et al. (2019) (8.95 kcal/g). Note that different strains were assessed among these studies, and such disparity in body energy may imply differential amino acid or fatty acid profiles in body protein or fat (Caldas et al., 2019) among distinct strains. It is also important to note that the observed differences in the energy values of body protein and fat may be attributed to the lack of correction (Emmans and Gous, 2025) as mentioned earlier.

It should be noted that the deposition rates of body protein and lipid reported in this study represent the potential, or genetically-determined values. Variations in feed composition and practical production conditions can lead to discrepancies between deposition rates observed in this study and those under different production environments. Consequently, obtaining on-site data (e.g., body weight, breast muscle ratio, and abdominal fat ratio) across a range of production conditions would greatly enhance the model’s applicability in diverse settings. Given that muscle and abdominal fat represent major body protein and fat stores, respectively, this study employed an allometric model linking the weights of breast or leg muscle, and abdominal fat with body protein and fat contents (Shafey et al., 2013). The allometric model had been classically applied to predict animal metabolic rate, and has been well-known as “three-quarter power law” (West et al., 1997). In this study, the model also linked body weight, components, and composition based on a power function, and in fact it was widely used to compare and predict body components or composition (Govaerts et al., 2000; Sakomura et al., 2003; van der Klein et al., 2017). In special circumstances when the exponent equals 1, the allometric growth is also known as isoangular growth, such as the case from Sakomura et al. (2003). Note that allometric relationship is not always linear (exponent ≠ 1), and thus is defined as heteroangular growth (Huxley, 1993).

Relative to body weight, components (breast muscle, thigh muscle, and abdominal fat), and composition (body protein, fat, and ash) presented higher growth rate, as exponent of their allometric function was greater than 1. This observation implied that prediction of body components and composition cannot simply be based on the proportion of body weight. Breast and thigh muscles are major protein components in broilers and thus can serve as an ideal predictor for protein deposition. However, note that the development of the breast (Fig. 2) and thigh muscle (Fig. 3) was very different between males and females. According to the allometric model that regressed breast or thigh muscle weight on body protein (Fig. 5), breast muscle for the male and thigh muscle for the female were better predictors of body protein, since their exponent was closer to 1, implying better linearity. As discussed earlier, note that the growth rate of male thigh muscle peaked almost 3 weeks later than that of the female, while the growth rate of male breast muscle peaked 1 week earlier (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The inflection day of the male breast muscle (d 60.29) and female thigh muscle (d 60.53) were relatively closer to the inflection day of body weight (d 53.00). In simple terms, improved proportionality was reflected by similar curve patterns, which was partially mirrored by of the timing of the inflection point.

Conventional feeding programs typically divide the growth period into 3 to 4 feeding phases. However, the nutritional requirements of broilers are nonlinear and change daily; thus, overnutrition may occur in the early stage of each phase, while undernutrition emerges in the later stage (Hadinia et al., 2018; Moss et al., 2021). Thus, developing growth models for body weight, protein, or fat deposition in yellow-feather broilers holds practical significance for the precise and dynamic nutritional requirements. This approach overcomes the limitations of traditional phase feeding, providing a key strategy for precision nutrition (Hadinia et al., 2018; Moss et al., 2021).

Conclusions

The Gompertz model developed in this study efficiently depicted body growth, components, and composition change of Qingyuan Partridge chicken. Body protein and fat energy in the male were 6.23 and 8.19 kcal/g, and those of the female were 5.72 and 8.93 kcal/g. In male broiler, the Gompertz equation for body protein mass was Wt=464.30×e-e-0.027×t-55.67; and the equation for body fat mass was Wt=192.50×e-e-0.042×(t-46.24). In female broiler, the equation for body protein mass was Wt=328.56×e-e-0.027×(t-52.01); and the equation for body fat mass was Wt=437.81×e-e-0.024×(t-71.93). The Gompertz equation for body protein and fat can be applied to further model daily nutrient and energy requirement. It is also important to note the necessity of corrections when interpret the Gompertz model.
