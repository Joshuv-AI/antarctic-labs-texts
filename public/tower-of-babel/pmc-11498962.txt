Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis treatment outcomes and associated factors at Yirgalem General Hospital, Sidama Region, South Ethiopia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Assefa Hamato Kebede, Hassen Mamo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe spread of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) poses a significant challenge to TB control efforts. This study evaluated the treatment outcomes and associated factors among patients receiving treatment for MDR-TB in southern Ethiopia.MethodsA retrospective follow-up study covering ten years, from 2014 to 2023, analyzed the records of confirmed cases of pulmonary TB admitted to Yirgalem General Hospital, an MDR-TB treatment initiation center in the Sidama Region. To compare the successful treatment outcomes across the years, a chi-square test of independence was conducted. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify factors associated with treatment outcomes for MDR-TB.ResultsOut of 276 confirmed MDR-TB cases, 4(1.4%) were diagnosed with resistance to second-line drugs (SLDs). Overall, 138 patients achieved favourable treatment outcomes, resulting in a treatment success rate of 50.0% [95% CI 44.1–55.9%]. Among these 138 patients, 105(76.1%, 95 CI 68.7–83.5%) were cured, while 33(23.9%, 95 CI 16.5–31.3%) completed their treatment. The successful treatment outcomes varied significantly across the years, ranging from 3.6% in 2020 to 90% in 2021. The analysis indicated a statistically significant difference in treatment outcomes when considering data from 2014 to 2023 (χ2 = 44.539, p = 0.001). The proportion of patients with deaths, lost-to-follow-up (LTFU), treatment failures and not evaluated were 7.9% [95% CI 4.8–11.2%], 10.9% [95% CI 7.2–14.6%), 2.2% [95% CI 1.1–3.3%), and 28.9% [95% CI 23.7–34.2%] respectively. Individuals with a positive HIV status had significantly lower odds of a favorable treatment outcome [AOR = 0.628, 95% CI (0.479–0.824), p = 0.018]. Similarly, patients with a BMI of less than 18 are more likely to have unfavorable treatment outcomes compared to those with a BMI of 18 or higher [AOR = 2.353, 95% CI 1.404–3.942, p < 0.001].ConclusionThe study revealed a concerning 1.4% prevalence of additional resistance to SLDs. The 50% rate of unfavorable treatment among MDR-TB cases exceeds the target set by the WHO. A significant number of patients (10.9%) were LTFU, and the 28.9% categorized as ‘not evaluated’ is also concerning. Enhanced strategic interventions are needed to reduce such cases, and factors associated with poor treatment outcomes should receive greater attention. Future prospective studies can further explore the factors influencing improved treatment success.

Introduction

Despite remarkable progress in the control of tuberculosis (TB) globally, the emergence of multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains particularly poses a major challenge to effective treatment [1]. These strains are resistant to the most commonly used anti-TB drugs, requiring longer and more complex treatment regimens with more expensive and toxic drugs [2]. Moreover, access to accurate and timely diagnosis of TB is crucial for effective treatment. However, many regions, especially low- and middle-income countries, face challenges in accessing quality diagnostic tools, such as GeneXpert machines or culture-based methods [3–8]. This leads to delays in diagnosis and initiation of treatment as well as evaluation of treatment outcomes. TB treatment typically involves a long course of multiple drugs taken over several months [9]. Ensuring patient adherence to the treatment regimen is critical for successful outcomes and preventing the development of drug resistance. However, adherence remains a further challenge due to various factors, including the complexity of the treatment, side effects of medications, and socioeconomic barriers [10].

Co-infections and comorbidities are yet other challenges. TB often occurs alongside other health conditions, such as HIV/AIDS, diabetes, and malnutrition and complicate TB treatment, increase the risk of treatment failure, and lead to poor outcomes [11–14]. Integrated management of these conditions is essential but can be challenging due to resource limitations and fragmented healthcare systems and demographic challenges among others. Diagnosing and treating pediatric TB is more challenging in that children often have paucibacillary TB making it harder to detect through standard diagnostic methods, pediatric-specific formulations of anti-TB drugs are often limited, leading to the need for dose adjustments and off-label use; and children may face more challenges with adherence, leading to sub-optimal treatment outcomes [15–18]. Furthermore, social and structural factors, such as poverty, stigma, lack of awareness, and weak healthcare systems, hinder early diagnosis, treatment initiation, and successful treatment completion [19, 20].

Disease surveillance is key to control and manage infectious diseases in general [21]. Regular surveillance of TB plays a crucial role in addressing drug resistance by providing valuable insights into the patterns and dynamics of resistance development, treatment outcomes, and the effectiveness of public health interventions [22]. While significant progress has been made in TB surveillance, some important gaps remain. Some of a few areas where knowledge gaps exist in TB surveillance include under-reporting and case detection, DR-TB surveillance, pediatric TB surveillance, timeliness of data, sub-national and marginalized populations, and integration of data sources [23]. TB is known to be under-reported, particularly in low-resource settings, and many cases go undiagnosed or unreported due to limitations in healthcare access, inadequate diagnostic capacity, and weak surveillance systems [24, 25]. This study aimed at assessing drug resistance, treatment outcomes, and associated factors in a cohort of pulmonary MDR-TB patients enrolled in the programmatic management of DR-TB at Yirgalem General Hospital (YGH), which is MDR-TB treatment initiation center in Sidama National Regional State (SNRS), southern Ethiopia.

Methods

Study site

A retrospective follow-up study was conducted at YGH in Yirgalem town, located in south Ethiopia, from February to December 2023. Yirgalem is situated 47 km away from Hawassa city which serves as the capital of the SNRS and is 319 km from Addis Ababa. The Hospital provides inpatient and outpatient care to a population of over three million patients and also serves as a referral center for nearby areas such as Oromia and the South Ethiopia Peoples' Regions administrative zones.

SNRS is one of the largest, recently emerged and most densely populated administrative Regions in the country. According to the census and population projection conducted by the Ethiopia Statistical Service [26], the Region has an estimated population of over 5 million. The majority of the Region's inhabitants are rural dwellers, and agriculture is the primary livelihood, similar to other Regions in Ethiopia. The Region has over 600 public health facilities and 84 private clinics that are actively involved in TB treatments through the directly observed therapy (DOT) course initiative. Private sector health facilities contribute to TB control and management activity in Ethiopia [27, 28]. The Hospital is administratively governed by the SNRS and has been serving as the only treatment initiation center for MDR-TB since the end of 2013. YGH has been functioning as one of the country's treatment initiation centers (TICs) for the past decade, providing medical care for curative and rehabilitative treatments across various units, including MDR-TB treatment services. Additionally, the center is responsible for monitoring several treatment follow-up centers (TFCs) for patients undergoing ambulatory outpatient treatment care through the DOT program.

Study population and data collection

From January 2014 up to December 2023, a total of 308 cases of MDR-TB were enrolled at the YGH MDR-TB TIC. For this study, we included 276 cases of pulmonary MDR-TB from the registry and medical records. Structured data collection tools were developed based on the national MDR-TB registration book and existing literature to gather demographic and medical information. Two trained nurses were employed to collect the data under supervision. The collected characteristics included sex, age, residence, year of enrollment, baseline information such as sputum smear, treatment category (new or re-treatment), prior drug exposure, HIV status, other co-morbid diseases, confirmed method of diagnosis, resistance type, body-mass-index (BMI), and treatment outcomes.

MDR-TB diagnosis in Ethiopia

The diagnosis of MDR-TB in Ethiopia, as it is elsewhere, involves a multi-step process [29–32]. The initial step is a clinical diagnosis. Patients with a history of previous TB treatment, especially those who have relapsed, failed treatment, or defaulted, are at an increased risk of developing MDR-TB and are considered presumptive cases of the condition. Additionally, patients presenting with persistent symptoms of TB, such as cough, fever, weight loss, and night sweats, despite receiving appropriate first-line TB treatment, are also considered presumptive cases of MDR-TB.

The presumptive TB cases then undergo bacteriological confirmation, during which sputum samples are collected and examined microscopically for the presence of acid-fast bacilli (AFB). If AFB are detected, the samples are further tested using rapid molecular diagnostics, such as the Xpert MTB/RIF assay [33], to detect the presence of MTB and determine resistance to RIF, a key first-line TB drug. Samples with RIF resistance are considered highly suggestive of MDR-TB and require further testing.

Confirmed MDR-TB cases undergo comprehensive drug susceptibility testing (DST) to determine resistance patterns to a wider range of anti-TB drugs, including INH, ethambutol, and injectable agents (e.g., kanamycin, amikacin, capreomycin) [34]. This information is crucial for designing an appropriate and effective treatment regimen for the patient. DST is typically performed using culture-based methods, such as the proportion method or the mycobacterial growth indicator tube (MGIT) system, which can take several weeks to obtain results [35]. Chest X-rays and, in some cases, chest computed tomography (CT) scans are used to assess the extent and severity of pulmonary involvement, which can guide treatment decisions [36]. Due to the high co-occurrence of TB and HIV in Ethiopia, all TB patients, including those with MDR-TB, are tested for HIV [37]. HIV infection can impact the clinical presentation, treatment response, and management of MDR-TB. Finally, comprehensive clinical, social, and psychosocial assessments are conducted to evaluate the patient's overall health status, comorbidities, and potential barriers to treatment adherence. This information is used to develop a personalized treatment plan and to identify any necessary support or interventions to ensure successful treatment outcomes.

Operational definitions

All terms used in the study are operationally defined and classified according to recommended guidelines [38]. Presumptive TB refers to a patient who presents with symptoms or signs suggestive of TB (previously known as a TB suspect). Rifampicin-resistant TB was defined as the detection of resistance to RIF using either phenotypic or genotypic methods, with or without co-resistance to other anti-TB drugs. Multidrug-drug resistant TB (MDR-TB) was defined as caused by MTB strains that are resistant to at least both and RIF and INH. The term cured was used to indicate patients with no evidence of treatment failure, where three or more consecutive cultures taken at least 30 days apart showed negative results after the intensive phase. Treatment completed referred to patients who showed no evidence of failure but did not have a record of three or more consecutive cultures taken at least 30 days apart showing negative results after the intensive phase. Treatment failure was defined as a patient whose treatment was terminated or required a permanent regimen change of at least two anti-TB drugs. The term died referred to a confirmed TB patients who passed away for any reason before or during the course of treatment. Lost-to-follow-up (LTFU) included patients whose treatment was interrupted for two consecutive months or more. Not evaluated referred to a TB patient for whom no treatment outcome is assigned. This includes cases “transferred out” to another treatment unit as well as cases for whom the treatment outcome is unknown to the reporting unit. Treatment success or favorable treatment outcome was used to describe patients who met the definitions of cure or treatment completion. Poor or unfavorable treatment outcome was used to describe patients who died, were LTFU, and/or experienced treatment failure during the course of treatment.

Data quality control

Data quality was ensured by comparing the collected data with the structured checklist encoding. Checkups were made in consultation with the designated nurses, and regular communication was maintained with them. The data was manually entered into Microsoft Excel spreadsheets after cleaning and exported to SPSS version 16 (SPSS Inc.) for analysis.

Data processing and analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to calculate frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, while continuous variables were summarized using mean and median ranges. A comparison between male and female patients was conducted to examine demographics, clinical factors, and treatment outcomes, which were presented as percentage proportions. Descriptive and regression models were utilized to identify factors associated with favorable and unfavourable treatment outcomes. To determine factors independently associated with unfavorable treatment outcomes, stepwise backward logistic regression analysis was employed. A statistical significance level of p < 0.05 was deemed to be significant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

Between 2014 and 2023, a total of 308 cases were enrolled in the MDR-TB center at YGH for treatment initiation. However, 32 of these were excluded as they were referred from outside the SNRS. Therefore, only 276 cases were considered. Among these 276 cases, 175(63.4%) were male patients and 101(36.4%) were female patients, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of approximately 2:1. The mean age was 28.95 ± 12.882 years, with a median age of 26 (interquartile range [IQR] = 20–35) years. Among the cases, 160(57.97%) patients were between 20 and 35 years of age, with the minimum and maximum ages being 1 and 80 years, respectively. The majority (89.1%) of the patients were from rural areas of the region, and the highest number of cases were from Aroresa, Chire, Arbegona, and Hula districts, which are located more than 100 km away from the treatment initiation center, as well as Hawassa city. Moreover, 180(65.2%) of the participants were non-employees. In terms of the year of treatment initiation, 50(18.1%) patients were enrolled in 2018, representing the highest proportion, followed by 36(13.0%) patients enrolled in 2023 (Table 1). Medical and anthropometric characteristics

Among the 276 cases, 210(76.1%) had previously received anti-TB drugs (retreatment cases) the rest were new at enrollment. At enrollment, all patients were tested for HIV co-infection, and 21(7.6%) patients tested positive. Additionally, 16(5.8%) patients had comorbid diseases other than HIV co-infections, with diabetes mellitus being the most common. The average BMI was 17.1736, with a median IQR of 17.000 (15.2000–18.9000), and 79.3% of the patients had a baseline BMI value ≥ 18 kg/m2 (Table 2). All bacteriologically confirmed MDR-TB cases were also screened and treated based on confirmatory drug susceptibility test (DST) results before and after treatment initiated. Based on DST result, 14(5.1%) were resistant to both INH and RIF (13 retreatment and 1 new cases). Four (1.5%) MDR-TB cases were diagnosed with additional resistance to SLDs at enrollment, with levofloxacin being the dominant drug resistance observed in 3 MDR-TB female patients who had previous exposure to ant-TB drugs. Treatment outcomes

The final treatment outcomes of 80 patients (29.1%, 95% CI 23.74–34.24%) had no recorded treatment outcomes in the treatment center. This includes 1 patient in 2017 (N = 30), 3 patients in 2018 (N = 50), 3 patients in 2019 (N = 28), 26 patients in 2020 (N = 28), 18 patients in 2022 (N = 33), and 29 patients in 2023 (N = 36). The overall treatment success rate was 50%(138/276) [95% CI 44.1–55.9%]. Of these 138 favorable treatment cases, 105(76.1%, 95% CI 68.7–83.5%) patients were declared cured, while 33(23.9, 95% CI 16.5–31.3%) completed their treatment. In the ten-year retrospective follow-up study, 22 patients (7.9%, 95% CI 4.8–11.2%) died during treatment, 30(10.9%, 95% CI 7.2–14.6%) were LTFU, 6 patients (2.2%, 95% CI 1.1–3.3%) experienced treatment failure, and 80 (28.9%, 95% CI 23.7–34.2%) fell into the ‘not evaluated’ category (Table 3). Regarding the adverse effects of the drugs, 120 out of 276(43.5%) developed drug side effects following the initiation of treatment. The proportion of male patients with favourable treatment outcomes was 48.0% (84/175) and that of female participants in the cohort was 53.5% 54/101. However, the relative ratio of treatment success (cured + complete) among male participants was approximately 1.6(84/54) times compared to females. Treatment outcomes varied significantly from year to year, with the lowest success rates observed in 2020 (3.6%), 2023 (11.1%), and 2022 (36.4%). Slight changes were noted between 2016, 2017, and 2018, while the highest success rate (90%) was recorded in 2021. The difference in successful treatment outcomes between the years were statistically significant (χ2 = 44.539, p = 0.001).

Factors affecting treatment outcomes

Out of variables tested in the bivariate and multivariable logistic regression (Table 4), individuals with a positive HIV status had significantly lower odds of a favorable treatment outcome compared to those with a negative HIV status (adjusted odds ratio (AOR) = 0.628, 95% CI 0.479–0.824, p = 0.018]. Individuals having a BMI < 18 had significantly higher risk of having unfavourable treatment outcome compared to those having BMI of ≥ 18 (AOR = 2.353, 95% CI 1.404–3.942, p < 0.001). The AOR of 0.628 suggests that being HIV positive is linked to a higher risk of unfavorable outcomes compared to being HIV negative. The lower odds ratio indicates a significant negative impact on treatment success among those who are HIV reactive. The AOR of 2.353 indicates that having a low BMI is associated with a significantly higher risk of unfavorable outcomes. This means that underweight patients are more likely to experience negative treatment results. Overall, the analysis identifies HIV positivity and low BMI as significant factors negatively impacting treatment outcomes for MDR-TB patients. Discussion

The management of MDR-TB necessitates increased resources for detection, successful treatment, and effective reduction of its burden. In 2015, Ethiopia successfully achieved the millennium development goals established for TB and expressed its commitment to expedite the eradication of TB by 2035 as part of a national strategic plan [39]. This plan emphasizes the implementation of robust TB case-finding strategies and the utilization of rapid diagnostic technologies to address the gap in identifying missed TB cases and combat MDR-TB threat.

Our study indicated that 76.1% of MDR-TB patients had previously undergone treatment for TB, and 5.1% exhibited resistance to both INH and RIF (for new/previous exposure), while 1.4% of MDR-TB cases demonstrated additional resistance to SLDs (pre-XDR/TB) at the baseline and treated within the cohort of MDR-TB. A study that analyzed an aggregated and individual patient data [40] using six eligible studies reporting treatment outcome on 1993 MDR-TB patients in Ethiopia found a pooled treatment success of 59.2% highlighting treatment success among MDR-TB patient was below acceptable range. In a study in the northwestern part of Ethiopia which analyzed retrospective data from 2011 to 2021 [41], the treatment success rate was 77.1%.

A systematic review published in 2014 [42] reported high prevalence of MDR-TB in the range of 3.3–46.3%, and two studies reported XDR-TB in the range of 1–4.4% in Ethiopia. The review concluded that ‘most powerful’ predictor of the emergence of MDR-TB reported in Ethiopia was previous exposure to anti-TB drug treatment. This review indicated that MDR-TB in Ethiopia is a serious public health problem that needs to be addressed urgently. A subsequent similar systematic review and meta-analysis study published in 2017 [43] reported an overall prevalence of MDR-TB among newly diagnosed and previously treated TB patients to be 2% (95% CI 1–2%) and 15% (95% CI 12–17%), respectively for 16 eligible studies assessed. This study also found that previously treated TB patients were at a higher risk of developing a MDR-MTB infection compared to newly diagnosed cases. The study concluded that the burden of MDR-TB remains high in Ethiopian settings, especially in previously treated TB cases although for the past 10 years (2006 to 2014) the overall MDR-TB prevalence showed a stable time trend.

Later studies conducted in the eastern [44], central [45], and southern Ethiopia [46, 47] have implicated previous exposure to anti-TB treatment as a common risk factor for MDR-TB. However, it is worth noting that possible mismanagement of anti-TB drug-sensitive cases at various levels can be a contributing factor to the emergence of drug resistance. The finding of 1.4% of cases diagnosed with additional resistance to core SLDs in our study is alarming and poses a threat to the national TB end strategy, as the majority of cases remain undiagnosed, putting the community at risk and increasing the likelihood of disease progression towards another form of MDR-TB, such as XDR-TB. Notably, the findings from our study recommend future community-based studies to obtain updated a nationwide figure.

In the current cohort study, 50.0% of patients achieved successful treatment outcomes is lower than the rates reported in hospital-based studies conducted in northwest and northeast Ethiopia [41]. Our study notably reports an 7.9% [95% CI 4.8–11.2%] death rate among patients during the treatment course, which is comparable with a hospital-based study by Belachew et al. 9.3% [41]. Furthermore, all patients diagnosed with additional resistance to SLDs died after enrollment, which may reflect the level of patient-centered services in the treatment center. The fact that all patients with additional resistance died highlights the need for improved patient-centered services and treatment outcomes. Furthermore, our study found that 50.0% of MDR-TB cases had unsuccessful treatment outcomes in the entire cohort. This figure is fivefold higher than the expected estimate by the WHO, which should be less than 10% [48]. Additionally, the current finding is much higher than the national estimates of MDR-TB patients with unfavorable treatment outcomes [49, 50]. The observed discrepancy might be due to variations in sample sizes, socio-demographic factors, and clinical conditions of the participants. The relatively higher proportions of patients with poor treatment outcomes may increase the risk of additional developments of MDR-TB. Addressing this gap in TB control is costly and has an impact on the quality of life, as it may lead to individuals developing XDR-TB. Thus, enhanced monitoring and targeted evaluation of patients are necessary to save lives.

Additionally, in the present study, a significant number of MDR-TB cases (28.9%) were found to have no determined records of treatment outcome after having considerable treatment period in the meantime. The reason for not evaluating and determining the patients' status early might be due to a lack of access to reliable laboratory testing. For example, 89.9% of cases in the current study were confirmed using GeneXpert, which only detects MTB and RIF’s resistances [51]. Therefore, improved laboratory access should be considered in the future to the greatest extent possible in the area. In Ethiopia, like any other sub-Saharan African country, MDR-TB detection rates are under-reported due to factors such as a lack of reliable, quick, and affordable diagnostic testing [52]. This not only affects treatment outcomes but also hinders effective infection control and the implementation of evidence-based measures for MDR-TB control.

Our study identified HIV co-infection, and low BMI as significant predictors for unfavorable treatment outcomes in MDR-TB patients. Several previous reports from Ethiopia [45, 47, 53–56] have shown HIV co-infection as a significant predictor of poor treatment outcomes, highlighting the need for integrated TB-HIV services and comprehensive care for patients with both conditions. A similar retrospective study from Ethiopia [45] has shown a significant association between low BMI and unfavorable MDR-TB treatment outcome. The presence of non-HIV comorbidities also suggests a trend towards unfavorable outcomes, though further research may be needed to clarify this relationship. Addressing nutritional status and managing HIV co-infection could be critical in improving treatment outcomes in this population. On the other hand, high impact studies like [47] showed that direct contact with known patients with TB, history of cigarette smoking and living in rural were significant risk factors for lower treatment outcome among MDR-TB patients.

Conclusion

The findings highlight the challenges and gaps in the management of MDR-TB in Ethiopia. The high proportion of previously treated cases is likely a re-infection after cure from the previous, and the emergence of additional resistance to SLDs are concerning challenge to the effectiveness of the program control. The treatment success rate falls short of the WHO target (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}≥ 90). The death rate among patients, particularly those with additional resistance to SLD, and LTFU is alarming and calls for improved patient-centered services. The presence of MDR-TB cases with no assigned treatment outcomes reflects the limitations in laboratory access and the need for better diagnostic tools. Addressing these issues requires increased resources, improved laboratory capacity, and the implementation of integrated and comprehensive care for MDR-TB patients, particularly those with co-morbidities such as HIV and those who are underweight. Ultimately, a multi-sectored approach and strong commitment from the government and stakeholders are crucial to effectively manage, control and eliminate MDR-TB in Ethiopia.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. It relied on existing medical records, which may have had incomplete or missing data on important variables such as patient demographics, clinical characteristics, treatment regimens, and outcomes. The patients included in the retrospective cohort may not have been representative of the overall MDR-TB population, as they were selected based on availability of data, which can introduce selection bias. The study may not have been able to adequately control for all potential confounding factors, such as comorbidities, disease severity, access to healthcare, socioeconomic and genetic factors, which can influence treatment outcomes. Additionally, the data may not reflect the current treatment landscape, as the management of MDR-TB has evolved over time. The study also had limited information on long-term outcomes and was unable to account for uncontrolled factors, such as patient adherence to treatment or the impact of social determinants of health. These limitations can affect the internal and external validity of the study findings, and the results should be interpreted with caution when making decisions about clinical practice or policy.
