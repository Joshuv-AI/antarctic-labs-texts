Quantitative analysis of bone cement dispersion height in percutaneous vertebroplasty and kyphoplasty and comparison of therapeutic effects
Authors: Zai Chao Ma, Maimaitiyibubaji Abudukadier, Zheng Zhang, Zeng Qiang Yang, Biao Li, Yong Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStudies have shown that there are significant individual differences in the prognosis of osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures. The underlying mechanisms are closely related to the choice of surgical method, the distribution of bone cement within the vertebral body, and the degree of recovery of the injured vertebral height. Although some scholars have explored this issue, there is still a distinct lack of evidence from systematic comparative studies. How to achieve optimal cement distribution and individualized and precise treatment remains a key concern for spine surgeons.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted to include 106 patients with single-segment osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures admitted to the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University from May 2019 to February 2025, and they were divided into percutaneous vertebroplasty group (n=55) and percutaneous vertebral kyphoplasty group (n=51) according to surgical methods. The postoperative VAS scores, Oswestry dysfunction index, localized posterior convexity angle of the injured vertebrae, cement dispersion height and its percentage, and the incidence of postoperative complications at 6-month follow-up were compared between the two groups, to investigate the influence of cement dispersion height on surgical outcomes based on the quantitative analysis of imaging between PVP and PKP.ResultsThere was no statistically significant difference in the general data of the two groups (p>0.05); the two groups showed significant differences in perioperative surgery-related indexes (p<0.05).VAS score, Oswestry dysfunction index, and localized posterior convexity angle of the injured vertebrae were lower than that of the preoperative period on the first day after surgery and at 6 months after surgery in both groups (p all <0.05); the differences in Oswestry dysfunction index and localized posterior convexity angle of the injured vertebrae were significant in the comparison of the two groups (p all <0.05); ODI index and localized posterior convexity angle of the injured vertebrae were significantly different in the PKP group as compared with the PVP group at 6 months after surgery (p all >0.05); and ODI index and localized posterior convexity angle of the injured vertebrae were significantly different in the two groups. The difference between the PKP group and the PVP group was statistically significant (p<0.05); the difference between the PKP group and the PVP group was statistically significant (p<0.05); the difference between the PKP group and the PVP group in the ODI index at 6 months postoperatively was statistically significant (p>0.05).The differences in the heights of the anterior vertebral column, middle column and vertebral body, and their cement dispersion heights and the percentage of dispersion heights between the two groups were statistically significant (p all <0.05); the heights of the anterior vertebral column, middle column and vertebral body, and their cement dispersion heights and percentage of dispersion heights of the PKP group were significantly superior to those of the PVP group after surgery (p all <0.05).The differences in the rates of cement leakage and adjacent vertebral fracture between the two groups were significant (p all <0.05); the rates of cement leakage and the risk of secondary fracture of vertebral body of neighboring segments between the two groups were significantly different, and the PKP group was better. (p<0.05).ConclusionsBoth PVP and PKP can significantly improve pain and other symptoms and improve the prognosis of patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture.The PVP procedure has significant advantages in terms of operation time, frequency of intraoperative imaging monitoring, and volume of cement infusion. However, the PKP group was superior in terms of postoperative vertebral body height recovery, control of cement leakage, improvement of visual analog score (VAS) for pain, and recovery of Oswestry dysfunction index (ODI). The latest study further found that the three-dimensional dispersion height of the cement in the vertebral body and its percentage of the vertebral body volume can be used as a potential imaging predictor to evaluate the efficacy of the two surgical procedures, which provides a new reference dimension for the selection of the clinical surgical procedures and prognosis assessment.

Background

The incidence of osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF) increases significantly with the aging of the population. Epidemiological data show that the annual incidence rate of OVCF in people ≥ 65 years old reaches 14.3%, and the proportion of females accounts for more than 72% [1], and most of them are characterized by persistent low back pain and discomfort as the first symptom, which affects the quality of life and to some extent increases the rate of disability and mortality greatly [2, 3]. Percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) was firstly applied to the treatment of vertebral hemangioma by Galibert et al. in 1984 [4]. After years of technology iteration, its indications have been expanded to osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, and is now listed as the first-line treatment option for OVCF [5]. Based on the iteration of PVP, innovative techniques such as percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) and woven container vertebroplasty (WCV) have been developed [6]. Studies have shown that there are significant individual differences in the prognosis of patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, and the underlying mechanisms are closely related to the choice of surgical procedures, the distribution characteristics of the cement in the vertebral body, and the degree of repositioning of the injured vertebral height [7], although some scholars have proposed to categorize the distribution of the cement into a mass, a mixed type, and a diffuse type, which does not have a significant effect on the outcome of the surgery [8], so compared with analyzing the cement diffusion in a three-dimensional perspective, the results are not significant [9]. Therefore, instead of analyzing cement dispersion from a three-dimensional perspective, it is more appropriate to analyze it from its pathogenesis, biomechanical perspective and vertebral height recovery [9]. Currently, there are few comparative studies in this area, and how to obtain better cement distribution and individualized and precise treatment is still an important issue for spine surgeons [10]. In the present study, a retrospective cohort study was conducted to investigate the influence of the dispersion height of bone cement in each division of the three-column theory on the surgical outcomes of patients with OVCF treated by different surgical modalities.

Materials and methods

Study participants

One hundred and six patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF) admitted to the Department of Spine Surgery of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University between May 2019 and February 2025 were divided into the PVP group (n = 55) and the PKP group (n = 51) according to the surgical methods. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, and all patients gave routine informed consent and voluntarily signed an “informed consent form” on the basis of full knowledge of the details of the treatment plan.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the first time to receive unilateral approach percutaneous vertebral body, percutaneous vertebral body kyphoplasty patients: (2) by the bone density test and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examination, diagnosed as a fresh fracture and the combination of osteoporotic changes: (3) the number of fractured vertebrae is not more than two; (4) the degree of compression of the injured vertebrae is not more than two thirds of the original height of the vertebral body.Exclusion criteria were (1) Clinical manifestations related to nerve root injury: (2) History of allergy to the components of bone cement: (3) Pathological fracture due to tuberculosis, infection, etc.: (4) Combination of serious underlying diseases (such as tumors, immune system disorders, etc.) or inability to cooperate with the operation for other reasons: (5) History of previous spinal surgery: (6) Body mass index (BMI) more than 35 kg/m²: (7) Burst fracture caused by high-energy injury: (8) Combination of coagulation disorders.

Surgical procedure

Preoperative preparation

After admission, all patients underwent imaging evaluation, including X-ray film, CT scan and MRI examination, aiming at assessing the urgency and chronicity of the fracture, the degree of compression and stability of the vertebral body; bone mineral density to assess the bone quality of the patients; routine blood biochemistry, 25-hydroxyvitamin D3, bone metabolism, and tumor markers for different genders to assess the basic condition of the patients.All procedures were performed by the same team of experienced senior attending physicians. The surgical instruments used for all patients in this cohort were sourced from a single manufacturer. Additionally, standardized protocols for perioperative medication and nursing care were consistently applied to minimize potential confounding factors.

Surgical method

In the PVP group, patients were placed in the prone position, and the target vertebral segment was precisely localized by the C-arm imaging system. After standard disinfection of the surgical area and spreading of the towel, local infiltration anesthesia was applied with 2% lidocaine hydrochloride, which was applied to the skin, subcutaneous tissue and periosteum layer by layer. A 5-mm longitudinal skin incision was made at the preselected puncture site, and the upper quadrant of the external projection of the pedicle was positioned as the starting point for the puncture, with the body of the puncture needle angled at an angle of 20 to 30 degrees from the sagittal plane. The direction of needle insertion was adjusted under real-time visualization: orthopantomography confirmed that the needle trajectory had reached the inner margin of the pedicle, and after the tip of the needle penetrated the cortex of the posterior wall of the vertebral body as shown in the lateral image, the needle was slowly inserted for a further 2–3 millimeters. The final positioning criteria were: the axis of the needle coincided with the central coronal plane of the vertebral body in the orthopantomogram, and the puncture operation was terminated when the tip of the needle was located in the anterior third of the vertebral body in the lateral image. Bone cement was prepared in standardized proportions, and then slowly injected into the vertebral body through the cement injector under fluoroscopic monitoring, and the injection was terminated when the filling material approached the anterior aspect of the posterior wall of the vertebral body. The injection pressure was maintained for 2–3 min. After the initial solidification of the bone cement, the puncture needle was removed, the puncture point was covered with sterile dressing, and the amount of bone cement injected and the operation time were recorded in detail.

PKP group: After routine positioning and disinfection, slowly insert the puncture needle to reach the posterior margin of the vertebral body at about 5 mm anteriorly, gently withdraw the needle core, insert a bone drill through the puncture channel, and drill to the anterior middle third of the vertebral body under fluoroscopic guidance, and then implant a special balloon dilatation catheter after withdrawing the bone drill. The balloon is positioned in the area of fracture responsibility and expanded to a pre-determined volume by applying pressure in small increments to create a cemented cavity. After carefully removing the balloon, the cement in the drawing stage was filled into the expansion cavity by the supporting injection device, and the rest of the procedure was the same as that of the PVP group.

Postoperative management

After the operation, the patient should be bedridden for 6–8 h, and gradually develop moderate early activities under the protection of thoracolumbar spine support. Oral calcium carbonate and vitamin D were recommended for 1 year after surgery, and regular injection of deslumizumab was standardized to implement the whole anti-osteoporosis treatment. X-rays and CT scan should be completed within 3 days after surgery to evaluate the distribution of bone cement and the recovery of the height of the injured vertebrae.

Main observation indicators

Surgery-related indexes

Record the operation time, the amount of bone cement injected, and the number of intraoperative fluoroscopies for each patient.

Efficacy evaluation indexes

Compare the VAS score and Oswestry Dysfunction Index (ODI) of the two groups at 1 day and 6 months after surgery [11, 12].

Imaging-related indexes

(1) Lateral X-rays were used to assess the localized posterior convexity angle of the injured spine in both groups at preoperative and 6 months postoperatively.

(2) Based on the postoperative lateral X-ray films, the following indexes were quantitatively analyzed in the two groups of patients:

Complications

The incidence of cement leakage, cement leakage is defined as intraoperative spillage of cement through the fracture fracture space or the vertebral venous system into the extravertebral region (including the intervertebral space, paraspinal tissues or anterior vertebral structures). The number of cases of leakage was recorded and the leakage rate was calculated by real-time observation through intraoperative fluoroscopy, and the leakage rate was calculated as (number of cases of leakage/total number of surgeries)×100%.

Incidence of recurrent fracture of injured vertebrae and neighboring vertebrae: regular outpatient follow-up was carried out after the operation, and the X-ray films were reviewed at 6 months after the operation to assess: (1) re-fracture of the operated vertebrae: the number of cases in which the original operated vertebrae suffered a second fracture in 6 months after the operation was counted. (2) fracture of the neighboring vertebrae: the observation of new fractures of the adjacent vertebrae of the injured vertebrae during the follow-up period was carried out. Calculate the incidence of compound fracture, new fracture rate = (number of re-fracture cases of the operated vertebrae + number of neighboring vertebral body fracture cases)/total number of cases×100%.(Figure 1).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software for Windows version 27.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and two independent samples t-test was used for comparison between groups; count data were expressed as number of cases (rate), and χ2 test was used for comparison between groups. The difference was regarded as significant if P<0.05.At the same time we have applied the Bonferroni correction to account for multiplicity when comparing outcomes across different time points.

Correlation between cement dispersion height% and ODI score between two cohorts of patients

Statistical analysis revealed that the cement distribution parameters of the two cohorts were differentially correlated with the postoperative functional recovery indexes: the percentage of cement dispersion height in the anterior column of the vertebral body, the percentage of maximum dispersion height and the Oswestry Dysfunction Index (ODI) scores at 6 months after the operation were significantly negatively correlated, but the correlation between the cement dispersion height in the mid-column of the vertebral body and ODI scores did not reach the threshold of statistical significance. Therefore, both PVP and PKP can improve the pain and other discomfort symptoms of OVCF patients, and the three-dimensional dispersion height of the cement in the vertebral body and its percentage of the vertebral body volume can be used as a potential imaging predictor to evaluate the efficacy of the two procedures, which can provide a new reference dimension for the selection of clinical procedures and prognosis assessment.(Fig. 3).

Discussion

In recent years, with the aging of the population, the prevalence of vertebral compression fractures secondary to osteoporosis has increased significantly [14]. The pathogenesis of this disease includes a series of pathophysiological mechanisms, including the irritation of nerve endings caused by micro-movement of the fracture end, abnormal stress distribution in the vertebral body, and the decrease of spinal load-carrying function [15]. At this stage, percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) and percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) are the preferred treatments for OVCF and occupy an important position in clinical applications. They are effective in relieving pain and improving function, but individual differences in postoperative outcomes are closely related to the degree of vertebral height recovery, and the cement dispersion pattern is a key factor affecting height recovery [16–18].

Comparative analysis in this study showed that the number of fluoroscopy, the amount of cement injected and the duration of surgery in the PVP group were less than that in the PKP group, which may be related to the operation characteristics of PVP without balloon dilatation. PKP can partially restore the vertebral height and optimize the uniformity of cement distribution through balloon pre-dilatation, but it requires higher operation precision to balance the risk of cement leakage. The VAS score, ODI index and Cobb’s angle of both groups improved significantly compared with the preoperative period at 6 months after surgery, suggesting that the two surgical procedures were effective in improving the clinical symptoms of patients with OVCF, which is consistent with the findings of a recent multicenter study [19]. It is worth noting that no statistically significant difference was found between PKP and PVP in terms of the percentage of dispersion height of cement in the vertebral mid-column after surgery (P >0.05). From an anatomical point of view, the middle vertebral column is composed of a dense trabecular network and subendplate bone, and its structural rigidity is significantly higher than that of the anterior column [20], which may limit the space for intraoperative vertebral height recovery. Even though the morphology of the vertebral body was remodeled by PKP balloon expansion, the longitudinal dispersion of the cement in the mid-column region was still limited by the bone density gradient and hydrodynamic properties.It is noteworthy that this study found no statistically significant difference in the cement dispersion height ratio in the middle column of the vertebral body between the PKP and PVP groups postoperatively. Furthermore, no significant correlation was observed between the cement dispersion height ratio in the middle column and the postoperative ODI index in either the PKP or PVP group. From an anatomical perspective, the middle column of the vertebral body comprises a dense trabecular network and subchondral bone, with structural stiffness significantly higher than that of the anterior column [25]. This may limit the potential for intraoperative vertebral height restoration. Even with vertebral morphological remodeling achieved through PKP balloon expansion, the longitudinal dispersion of cement in the middle column remains constrained by bone density gradients and hydrodynamic characteristics. Micro-CT studies showed that the diffusion of bone cement in cancellous bone showed a “central aggregation effect”, and the cement was more likely to form radial branches along the trabecular space in the mid-column region rather than extending vertically [21], which may explain the convergence of the dispersion heights of the middle column between the two surgical procedures.

The influence of the cement dispersion pattern on the early and mid-column outcomes is controversial. The traditional classification of the dispersion pattern into clump, mixed and diffuse is not clear, but its correlation with clinical prognosis is not clear. Based on the progress of biomechanical research, the cement distribution characteristics of the anterior and middle columns of the vertebral body may be more valuable for assessment: the proportion of the dispersion height of the anterior column reflects the coverage of the pressure load zone by the cement, while the dispersion height of the middle column is closely related to the stability of the vertebral body structure. Ideally, cement should be preferentially distributed in the mid-column region to enhance axial loading capacity and reduce the risk of re-fracture in the adjacent vertebrae. Recent three-dimensional finite element analysis showed that insufficient dispersion of cement in the mid-column could lead to stress shielding effect and increase the probability of postoperative vertebral body collapse [22]. In addition, the percentage of the maximum dispersion height of the cement (i.e., the longitudinal distribution range of the cement) was positively correlated with the risk of leakage, and over-injection may trigger the rigidity of the vertebral body, which may lead to the concentration of stress in the neighboring vertebrae [1]. Further analysis showed that the percentage of anterior vertebral column and maximum dispersion height of the vertebral body were significantly negatively correlated with the improvement of ODI at 6 months after surgery. Based on the theory of spinal load transfer, the anterior column is the main load-bearing area of the pressure load, and the adequate dispersion of the cement can rebuild the mechanism of “three-column linkage” of the vertebral body, and improve the dynamic stability by dispersing the stress [23]. This finding suggests that preoperative finite element simulation can be used to predict the cement dispersion pattern and screen out high-risk patients with underfilled anterior columns, so that PKP or composite reinforcement techniques (e.g., cement-screw system) can be individually selected to optimize the therapeutic efficacy [24].

In this study, we found that the PKP group was superior to the PVP group in terms of anterior midcolumn height restoration, cement dispersion height and its percentage. The mechanism may be due to the fact that balloon dilatation can remodel the vertebral body morphology and enlarge the space for cement diffusion, while PVP depends on the natural diffusion and is prone to uneven local filling. It is worth noting that the leakage rate of PKP group was lower than that of PVP group, which further confirmed the importance of precise control of cement distribution to reduce complications. In recent years, the use of computerized navigation combined with high-viscosity bone cement has significantly improved the dispersion control of PVP, which may reduce the difference in the efficacy of the two procedures [25].

Limitations

There are still some shortcomings in this study: (1) Both thoracic and lumbar vertebral compression fractures were included in this study, and the biomechanical recovery characteristics and the occurrence of complications may be different. (2) Only patients who underwent unilateral puncture approach were included in this study, and bilateral puncture approach was not considered, so different puncture approaches may have some influence on the distribution pattern of bone cement. (3) When grouping the shape and distribution of bone cement based on DR only, CT three-dimensional reconstruction technology can be introduced to further refine the grouping of the distribution of bone cement to enhance the persuasive power of this study.(4) This study involved repeated measures of the data, but only applied multiplicity correction without further model adjustments. In the future, the analysis could be enhanced by employing a simple mixed-effects model or ANCOVA, adjusted for baseline values, to avoid multiple unpaired tests.(5) This retrospective study carries a certain degree of selection bias, and different grouping methods may introduce subjective bias in the results. Therefore, long-term follow-up, large-sample, and multicenter studies are needed to further evaluate the efficacy of intravertebral bone cement distribution in treating vertebral compression fractures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the cement dispersion pattern needs to be evaluated by combining the biomechanical properties of the vertebral body in a multidimensional way. In the future, artificial intelligence image analysis technology can be introduced to dynamically quantify the correlation between cement distribution and spinal mechanical parameters, which will provide new ideas for the selection of surgical procedures and the prediction of efficacy.
