Druggable genome CRISPR screening identifies the KEAP1/NRF2 axis as a mediator of PD-L1 expression
Authors: Fidan Seker-Polat, Magdalena Rogozinska, Youngho Ban, Fatih Abdula, Kadir Buyukcelebi, Ping Xie, Jie Fan, Neda Abbaszadeh, Yasemin Kingham, Seyedeh Zahra Paylakhi, Bin Zhang, Mazhar Adli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cancer cells rapidly induce PD-L1 expression in response to inflammatory cytokines such as IFNγ from cytotoxic T cells. Increased surface PD-L1 is a primary mechanism of cancer cells evading cytotoxic T-cell-mediated immune clearance. Identifying how cancer cells increase PD-L1 expression may yield clinically relevant immune checkpoint regulators. However, the key regulators and molecular mechanisms mediating rapid PD-L1 induction are yet to be understood entirely. To identify targetable mechanisms controlling cytokine-induced PD-L1 expression, we performed functional CRISPR gene KO screening with a custom-designed sgRNA library that targets “druggable” genes. We performed the screening in 6 different cancer lines: 3 ovarian (OVCAR4, CaOV3, and SKOV3) and three pancreatic cancer (MiaPaca2, ASPC1 and KP4) cell lines. The screening recovered the known regulators of PD-L1 expression and uncovered several novel regulators of PD-L1 that control its expression in all cell lines or in a cancer-type-specific fashion. For example, while genetic or pharmacological depletion of CSNK1A1 results in reduced PD-L1 expression in ovarian cancer cells, CDK1 depletion modulates PD-L1 in pancreatic cancer cell lines. Significantly, we discovered that KEAP1 depletion or pharmacological inhibition diminishes PD-L1 in all cell lines tested (n = 6). Mechanistically, KEAP1 depletion-mediated reduced PD-L1 is due to transcriptional repression of the PD-L1 gene by NRF2 activation. As such, depletion of NRF2 restores PD-L1 expression, while its overexpression leads to diminished PD-L1 expression. Supporting this, pharmacological NRF2 activation resulted in significant antitumor immunity with increased cytotoxic effector T cell infiltration and reduced exhausted T cells, resulting in smaller xenografted tumors. These findings establish the KEAP1/NRF2 axis as a novel and potentially druggable mechanism of IFNγ-meditated PD-L1 expression in cancer cells.

Introduction

T cell-mediated immune response protects against abnormal cells, such as pathogen-infected or cancer cells. The differential detection and elimination of abnormal cells are mediated by the interaction between T cell receptors (TCR) and the peptide-major histocompatibility complexes (MHC) on aberrant cells. This interaction is tightly regulated by a series of stimulatory and inhibitory interactions between receptors and their ligands on T cells and aberrant cells. These regulatory mechanisms are called immune checkpoints. By regulating the level and activity of T cells, these immune checkpoints determine the immune clearance of abnormal cells and autoimmune reactions. Therefore, understanding and targeting these checkpoints have groundbreaking clinical implications in several diseases, including cancer.

Among all immune checkpoints, the interaction between PD-L1 on cancer cells and PD-1 on T cells has emerged as the most critical because of its proven therapeutic value in several malignancies1–4. In addition to interacting with PD-1, PD-L1 can also interact with CD80 to deliver inhibitory signals to activated T cells5,6. The engagement of these T-cell receptors with PD-L1 ligands on cancer cells dampens the T-cell cytotoxicity1,7. Therefore, cancer cells rapidly upregulate PD-L1 expression in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interferon-gamma (IFNγ) and tumor necrosis factor alfa TNFα released from T cells and NK cells in the tumor microenvironment8–10. Significantly, recent evidence indicates immune-independent protective roles of PD-L1 as well. While the cell surface PD-L1 dampens anti-tumor immunity, the intracellular PD-L1 contributes to radio- and chemo-resistance2,8 by directly regulating the mRNA stability of DNA damage response genes11. The antibodies targeting this interaction have resulted in remarkable clinical benefits in several cancer types. However, a durable response is limited to a minority of patients, and intrinsic therapy resistance is common. High PD-L1 levels on cancer cells or in the immune microenvironment are associated with poor patient survival and is a significant predictor of immune checkpoint inhibitors’ success in several cancer types1,8. Therefore, understanding how cancer cells upregulate PD-L1 to hijack immune checkpoint mechanisms to evade anti-tumor immunity has critical therapeutic relevance.

In this study, we investigated how cancer cells rapidly control their cell surface PD-L1 protein levels in response to IFNγ, a cytokine released by T or NK cells that drives rapid PD-L1 expression in the tumor microenvironment1,8. To achieve this, we used an unbiased CRISPR-based loss of function screening to identify molecular regulators of PD-L1 protein levels in response to IFNγ. We have performed the screening using a custom-designed sgRNA library that targets “druggable” human gene products in 6 different cancer cell lines representing pancreatic or ovarian cancer. Notably, the unbiased approach identified both known as well as several novel regulators that mediate PD-L1 expression in both pancreatic and ovarian cancers and a cancer-origin-dependent fashion. We genetically and pharmacologically validated several cancer-specific screening hits. More critically, our findings revealed KEAP1 as a critical positive regulator of PD-L1 levels in both cancer types. Mechanistically, we show that KEAP1 depletion-leads to reduced PD-L1 levels due to its transcriptional repression by activated NRF2 transcription factor. As such, pharmacological activation of NRF2 resulted in smaller tumors in vivo while reshaping the immune microenvironment to promote antitumor immunity. These findings highlight the complex role of the KEAP1/NRF2 axis in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment.

Results

Unbiased CRISPR screening to identify druggable regulators of PD-L1 downstream of IFNγ

Cancer cells rapidly respond to pro-inflammatory cytokines released by cytotoxic T and NK cells by upregulating cell surface PD-L18. We, therefore, initially screened a panel of human cancer cell lines (Fig. 1a) to identify the cells that rapidly upregulate PD-L1 in response to interferon-gamma (IFNγ), the critical inflammatory cytokine released from cytotoxic immune cells in the tumor microenvironment12. We observe that the pancreatic and ovarian cancer cell lines we tested respond to IFNγ by rapidly upregulating surface PD-L1 levels as measured by flow cytometry (Fig. 1a). Encouraged by these, we aimed to identify potentially druggable pathways that control IFNγ-mediated rapid PD-L1 expression. To this end, we utilized a custom-designed sgRNA library that targets ~1400 genes whose protein products or their homologs are targetable with small molecule chemicals. We picked these “druggable” genes based on a literature review and published gene-drug interaction databases13. We then devised a screening strategy (Fig. 1b) to identify the druggable genes that regulate IFNγ-induced rapid PD-L1 regulators. We picked the MiaPaca2 cell line that robustly upregulates PD-L1 in response to IFNγ. The Cas9-expressing MiaPaca2 cells were lentivirally infected with the sgRNA library at a low multiplicity of infection (MOI = ~ 0.25). After a few days of puromycin selection, the sgRNA library expressing cells (~500X cells/sgRNA) were expanded and, 15 days later, were treated with IFNγ (0.05 µg/ml). After 48 h of treatment, cells were flow cytometry sorted into PD-L1-low expressing (Bottom 25%, n = 3) and high (Top 25%, n = 3) expressing cells (Fig. 1c). The genomic DNA was isolated, the sgRNA backbone was amplified by PCR, and the barcodes were counted through NGS sequencing. The differential enrichment of sgRNA barcodes and gene-level enrichment analysis in PD-L1-Low vs. High cells were assessed by Beta-binomial modeling using the CB2 tool14.

We focused on the enrichments of barcodes in PD-L1-Low cells, indicating that the targets of these sgRNAs are required for IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression. In other words, these genes are potentially targetable to reduce PD-L1 expression in cancer cells to increase anti-tumor immunity. We assessed enrichments at the gene level (normalized gene enrichment or CRISPR z-score and p-values) (Fig. 1d) and sgRNA-level enrichments using log fold change and the absolute number of sgRNAs in the top 5% or 105 of sgRNAs in the PD-L1-Low cells (Fig. 1e, f). Notably, the analysis revealed almost all known critical regulators of PD-L1 that were represented in our library. For example, the top enriched genes in PD-L1-Low cells were CD274 (encoding PD-L1); IFNGR1 and IFNGR2, receptor subunits that each is required for IFNγ mediated signaling15 and JAK1 & JAK2, the critical kinases downstream of interferon receptors and upstream of STAT transcription factors that directly bind to PD-L1 promoter15 (Fig. 1d–f). These findings highlighted the robustness of our screening strategy to discover key regulators of PD-L1. Notably, the unbiased screening has also yielded several previously uncharacterized modulators as critical regulators of PD-L1. KEAP1 was particularly notable among them as it is one of the top enriched genes in PD-L1-low cells (Fig. 1d–f).

CRISPR screening identifies common and cancer-specific PD-L1 regulators

PD-L1 expression is highly cancer-specific8,16,17, indicating the differences in the cell-autonomous molecular networks that govern PD-L1 expression and the tumor microenvironment. To increase the rigor of our findings and see if there are cell type or cancer-specific regulators of PD-L1, we performed the screening in 5 additional cancer cell lines of pancreatic (ASPC1 and KP4) and ovarian (OVCAR4, CaOV3, and SKOV3) cancer origin. Significantly, the unsupervised clustering analysis of screening hits showed that the pancreatic and ovarian cancer cells clustered separately (Fig. 2a). In line with these, our screening identified cancer type-specific regulators and the eight genes that were common across all cell types. Specifically, we identified ~40 genes whose depletion is selectively associated with low PD-L1 expression in pancreatic cancer cells. In contrast, ~65 genes were associated with low PD-L1 expression, specifically in ovarian cancer cells and the depletion of ~15 genes were inversely correlated with PD-L1 in both cancer types, indicating that their depletion further induced PD-L1 expression in all cancer cells (Fig. 2a, boxes on the right). To validate our screening hits, we initially picked CDK1 and CSNK1A1 as representative genes whose depletion selectively regulates IFNγ-induced PD-L1 in pancreatic and ovarian cancer cell lines, respectively. Critically, in line with the screening data, the flow cytometry analysis shows that depletion of CDK1 (Cyclin-dependent kinase 1) (Supplementary Fig. 1a), a central regulator of G2 and mitosis cell cycle phases significantly and selectively downregulates PD-L1 only in pancreatic cancer lines (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 1b). In contrast, depletion of CDK1 with two independent sgRNAs did not reduce PD-L1 levels but increased it in ovarian cancer cells (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 1c). Notably, the pharmacological inhibition of CDK1 with R0-3306, further corroborated the genetic data and showed that CDK1 inhibition selectively downregulates PD-L1 in KP4 and ASPC1 pancreatic cancer cell lines (Fig. 2d), whereas the same dose does not alter IFNγ induced PD-L1 levels in neither CaOV3 nor OVCAR4 ovarian cancer lines (Fig. 2e). In contrast to CDK1, we identified CSNKA1A1, which encodes for Casein kinase 1 alpha 1, as a selective regulator of PD-L1 in ovarian cancer cells. In line with this, genetic depletion of CSNK1A1 (Supplementary Fig. 1d) selectively downregulates IFNγ-mediated PD-L1 expression in ovarian cancer cells (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 1e) but not in pancreatic cancer cells (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 1f). KEAP1 depletion diminishes IFNγ-mediated PD-L1 levels

Next, we focused on genes whose depletion robustly downregulates PD-L1 across all cancer cell lines. The list contained known regulators, such as CD274 (encoding PD-L1), IFNGR1 (Interferon gamma receptor 1), IFNGR2 (Interferon gamma receptor 2) and JAK1 (Janus Kinase 1) JAK2 (Janus Kinase 2). The list also contained IFNAR1, MAP3K7, and KEAP1. Notably, IFNAR1 encodes interferon alfa and beta receptor subunit. Although these type I interferons are known to regulate PD-L1 expression also through JAK/STAT pathway18,19, our findings indicate a critical role of IFNAR1 downstream of type II interferon, IFNγ as well. MAP3K7 encodes for TAK1 (Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)-activated kinase 1), which plays a critical role in several proinflammatory cytokine-mediated signaling that converges on TGFβ, WNT and NF-κB TFs, which are known players in PD-L1 expression20,21. Finally, our screening identified KEAP1, a gene whose depletion downregulates PD-L1 expression across all cell lines tested. Notably, KEAP1 is a binding partner and repressor of NRF2, whose transcriptional activity is required to balance oxidative and xenobiotic stress22,23. How KEAP1 depletion regulates PD-L1 expression is not known.

We, therefore, set our experiments to validate KEAP1 as a robust regulator of PD-L1 and understand the molecular mechanism of this regulation. To this end, we designed additional sgRNAs targeting KEAP1 and tested their impact on basal and IFNγ-induced PD-L1 expression in multiple cell lines. Notably, the depletion of KEAP1 with two independent sgRNAs in two pancreatic and two ovarian cancer cell lines resulted in a significant depletion of surface PD-L1 protein levels measured by flow cytometry (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). Notably, PD-L1 can be regulated at multiple levels, including transcription, protein maturation or cell membrane localization, the latter of which has been shown to be regulated by CMTM624,25. Critically, in line with flow cytometry findings, our cell fractionation experiments demonstrated that KEAP1 depletion results in a substantial reduction in not only membrane-bound PD-L1 levels (Fig. 3b) but also global PD-L1 levels as measured by western blot of total cell lysates in both cell lines (Fig. 3c) indicating that KEAP1-depletion mediated reduced cell surface PD-L1 is not due to miss localization but globally reduced protein levels. To further complement these findings and assess whether reduced surface PD-L1 level in KEAP1-depleted cells is due to delayed kinetics or cell death, we utilized the Incucyte live-cell imaging platform to simultaneously evaluate cell proliferation and cell surface PD-L1 expression followed by IFNγ treatment. Critically, KEAP1-depleted cells had comparable proliferation rates (Fig. 3d, small inlet), indicating that KEAP1 depletion is not lethal to the cells. However, in line with our findings, KEAP1-depleted cells have significantly lower PD-L1 production than control sgRNA-expressing cells (Fig. 3d, e). These findings support the hypothesis that functional KEAP1 is a critical regulator of PD-L1, and KEAP depletion leads to a global reduction in PD-L1 levels. Genetic depletion or pharmacological inhibition of KEAP1 reduce PD-L1 at the transcriptional level

Next, we investigated whether pharmacological inhibition of KEAP1 will have a comparable effect on PD-L1 levels. To this end, we used Bardoxolone, an established inhibitor of KEAP126–29. Critically, in line with KEAP1 depletion at the genetic level, pharmacological inhibition of KEAP1 resulted in a dramatic decrease in total (Fig. 4a) as well surface level PD-L1 levels (Fig. 4b, c) in multiple cell lines as assessed by western blot and flow cytometry. Notably, Bardoxolone treatment led to a substantial increase in total NRF2 levels, as anticipated (Fig. 4a). Our live cell imaging experiments further corroborated these findings and showed that bardoxolone treatment significantly reduced PD-L1 levels in multiple cell lines (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 2c). These findings led us to investigate whether KEAP1 depletion or pharmacological inhibition results in altered transcription of the PD-L1 gene. We, therefore, assessed PD-L1 mRNA expression in cells where KEAP1 is either genetically depleted or pharmacologically inhibited. We observed a significant reduction in IFNγ-induced PD-L1 mRNA levels in multiple cell lines where KEAP1 is either genetically altered (Fig. 4e) or pharmacologically inhibited (Fig. 4f). KEAP1 depletion mediates PD-L1 repression due to transcriptional activation of NRF2

Notably, one of the primary roles of KEAP1 is to bind and continuously inhibit the NRF2 transcription factor, leading to its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation22,30. In line with this, we observe that KEAP1 depletion results in significant nuclear accumulation of NRF2 (Fig. 5a), leading to the hypothesis that the downregulation of PD-L1 mRNA expression is due to increased nuclear NRF2 accumulation. To test this, we seek to upregulate total NRF2 levels without altering the KEAP1 levels. Therefore, we first used dimethyl fumarate (DMF), a clinically used chemical activator of NRF231. Notably, DMF blocks the interaction between KEAP1 and NRF2 by binding to several critical domains in KEAP1 and blocks its ability to ubiquitinate and degrade NRF232. As anticipated, treating cells with DMF leads to substantial nuclear accumulation of NRF2 (Supplementary Fig. 4c) and significant downregulation of PD-L1 in multiple cell lines as measured by flow cytometry (Fig. 5b, c). These results supported the hypothesis that KEAP1 depletion-mediated reduction in global PD-L1 is due to NRF2 activation. To further test this hypothesis, we decided to genetically upregulate global NRF2 levels using a doxycycline-controllable promoter, as shown in Fig. 5d. Notably, the dose-dependent increase in NRF2-levels led to comparable levels of decrease in IFNγ-induced PD-L1 levels as measured by flow cytometry (Fig. 5e). To further test our central hypothesis and directly assess that NRF2 is a negative regulator of PD-L1 gene expression, we depleted cells of NRF2 using two independent sgRNAs. Cells expressing NRF2 targeting sgRNAs had substantially less NRF2 compared to control sgRNA-expressing cells (Fig. 5f). This notable depletion of NRF2 protein level led to reduction in the protein and mRNA levels of established NRF2 targets NQO1, SLC7A11 and BLVRB (Fig. 5f and Supplementary Fig. 4g, h). More critically, NRF2 depletion resulted in a significant increase in basal PD-L1 mRNA and, more notably, in IFNγ-induced PD-L1 mRNA levels (Fig. 5g), supporting our overall hypothesis that NRF2 is a negative regulator of PD-L1 gene expression. Furthermore, we used inhibitor ATRA in IFNγ-induced cells to inhibit NRF2 pharmacologically33 and observed a significant increase in surface PD-L1 as measured by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e). To further test that NRF2 activation is the critical player that leads to reduced PD- L1 expression downstream of KEAP1 inhibition, we performed bardoxolone treatment in NRF2-depleted cells. Significantly, we observed that reduced PD-L1 expression in bardoxolone-treated cells is largely rescued when cells are depleted with NRF2, supporting the overall hypothesis that NRF2 is a key player downstream of KEAP1 depletion that inhibits PD-L1 expression at mRNA level (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 4f). NRF2 is a transcription factor that binds to critical regulator elements and regulates its target genes. Critically, previous studies nominated an enhancer of the PD-L1 gene as the putative binding site of NRF234, suggesting that NRF2 binds to an enhancer element proximal to the PD-L1 gene promoter. We therefore tested to see if KEAP1 depletion leads to NRF2 binding to this regulatory element. We performed chromatin immunoprecipitation and qPCR measurement (ChIP-qPCR) to check the enrichment of NRF2 at this enhancer in control and KEAP1-depleted cells. These results highlighted significant enrichment of NRF2 at the PD-L1 enhancer site (Fig. 5i), indicating direct evidence that NRF2 binds to the PD-L1 enhancer element when KEAP1 is depleted.

NRF2 activation results in smaller tumors and promotes antitumor immunity in vivo

To test the role of the KEAP1/NRF2 axis in vivo in an immune-competent mouse model, we used a mouse NB490 pancreatic cell line. We first validated that depletion of KEAP1 (Fig. 6a) in this cell line reduced the surface (Fig. 6b) PD-L1 protein levels. Notably, KEAP1 inhibition led to reduced infiltration of exhausted T cells (TEX) (Fig. 6j) and TAM (Fig. 6k) despite no difference in the infiltration of other immune cell populations within CD45+ tumor infiltrates (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Among the infiltrated cell populations, our expanded analysis revealed significant reduction in PD-L1 expression within dendritic cell (DC) and DC1 subpopulations following CDDO-me treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5c). In addition to the reduced infiltration of the protumorigenic cells, we also found that KEAP1 inhibition enhanced an M1-like antitumor phenotype in TAM, characterized by the expression of CD80 (Fig. 6l). Furthermore, supporting the above findings, there was a significant increase in IFNγ and TNFα secretion by infiltrating CD8+ T cells in tumors treated with the KEAP1 inhibitor (Fig. 6m), suggesting that the reduced tumor volumes in KEAP1 inhibitor-treated mice are due to increased antitumor immunity.

Discussion

Cytokine-induced upregulation of PD-L1 on the surface of cancer cells is a key mechanism of escape from immune surveillance. Here, we aimed to reveal potentially “druggable” regulators of IFNγ-mediated PD-L1 expression. To this end, we have utilized a custom-designed sgRNA library that targets ~1400 druggable genes whose protein products can be targeted with small molecule inhibitors. There are several advantages of such a sub-genomic library. Relatively smaller library size increases the feasibility of performing CRISPR screening in a limited number of cells or in settings where a large number of cells is not an option, such as in vivo screenings. More critically, because the screening is performed on genes with small molecule inhibitors, the CRISPR screening hits can be readily validated pharmacologically as well. Thus, the findings have greater translational applicability and clinical utility.

We performed unbiased screening using cell lines representing ovarian and pancreatic cancer. We picked these cancers because of the limited clinical success of in both cancers despite significant evidence that antitumor immunity plays a critical role in the clinical outcome of these diseases. The current 5-year survival rate is 7.5%, which is the worst prognosis and lowest absolute improvement in survival of all carcinomas36. Although pancreatic cancers are generally considered “immune-cold,” i.e., devoid of infiltrating immune cells37, there is a plethora of evidence that the immune system is a critical determinant of progression for this lethal disease38. Furthermore, the success of immune checkpoint therapy in certain pancreatic patients (high microsatellite instability or mismatch repair deficiency) and the potential efficacy of autologous T cell-based therapies39,40 are promising evidence that modulators of immune checkpoints may prove to be successful in pancreatic cancer. In contrast to pancreatic, ovarian cancers are generally immune hot, and tumor cells express significant levels of PD-L1. As such, high PD-L1 expression is associated with aggressive OC progression, peritoneal dissemination, and reduced patient survival41,42. However, immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-L1/PD-1 have been only moderately successful (5–15% response)43,44, indicating perhaps additional roles of PD-L1, such as contributing to DNA repair activities by stabilizing mRNAs of DNA damage response genes11.

PD-L1 is often aberrantly regulated in cancer through multiple mechanisms, including gene amplification and ectopic promoter translocation, as seen in lymphomas and stomach adenocarcinoma45–47. Notably, 3′-UTR structural variations and truncations, observed in several cancers, also lead to increased PD-L1 expression and immune evasion48. In addition to these genetic alterations, transcriptional reprogramming in cancers also drives higher PD-L1 expression. For example, most known oncogenic transcription factors, such as MYC49, HIF1α50, NF-κB51, and BRD452 that regulate the expression of many genes are known regulators of PD-L1 transcription. In addition to these endogenous factors, cancer cells may also rapidly upregulate PD-L1 expression in response to pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interferon-gamma (IFNγ) and tumor necrosis factor alfa TNFα released from T cells and NK cells in the tumor microenvironment8–10. Furthermore, several kinase-targeting therapies and radiotherapy, which may alter the milieu of TME, also lead to increased PD-L1 expression and thereby increase tumor cells’ ability to inhibit immune and drug response53,54.

Here, we described a general approach to discover cell-type-specific regulators of PD-L1, the major checkpoint modulator that has been successfully targeted and yielded unprecedented clinical response in several cancers4,16. Recent genome screening in single cell lines revealed CMTM6 and CMTM4 as critical regulators involved in cell surface localization of PD-L124,25. Significantly, recent evidence indicates PD-L1 also has immune-independent protective roles. While the cell surface PD-L1 dampens anti-tumor immunity, the intracellular PD-L1 contributes to radio- and chemo-resistance2,8 by directly regulating the mRNA stability of DNA damage response genes11. These findings indicate that PD-L1 modulators that target not only cell surface but also the global pool of PD-L1 may yield a more promising therapeutic response. To this end, the KEAP1/NRF2 axis that we discovered here controls PD-L1 at the transcriptional level, reducing the cells’ global pool of PD-L1 protein.

The KEAP1/NRF2 axis maintains redox, protein homeostasis, and inflammation in the cells23,30. Stress conditions lead to the dissociation of NRF2 from KEAP1, and the stabilized NRF2 translocates to the nucleus where it binds to the antioxidant response element (ARE) in the genome30. In this context, NRF2 drives the expression of its canonical targets, confirming NRF2’s well-established role as a transcriptional activator for these genes. In contrast to this well-appreciated role of NRF2, our findings show that NRF2 may also act as a transcriptional repressor in the context of PD-L1 transcriptional regulation, highlighting the context-dependent and gene-specific nature of NRF2’s regulatory mechanisms. Our findings show that activation of NRF2 leads to a global reduction in PD-L1 levels, indicating that KEAP1 depletion and hence NRF2 activation may lead to enhanced antitumor immunity.

Notably, there has not been a clear consensus on the role of the KEAP1/NRF2 axis in cancer initiation of progression because activation of NRF2 has paradoxical roles in cancer development. An unbiased screening in a mouse ovarian cancer cell line showed that KEAP1 targeting gRNAs were depleted in high PD-L1 expressing cells55, supporting our findings that KEAP1 depletion leads to lower PD-L1 expression. However, a study in THP-1 monocyte cells showed that KEAP1 depletion results in higher PD-L1 levels56. Similarly, PD-L1 expression was positively correlated with nuclear NRF2 levels in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma samples57. A recent work by Li et al. also supported KEAP1 as a negative regulator of PD-L1 levels in non-small cell lung cancer58. These contrasting findings likely reflect the cancer type-specific nature of KEAP1-PD-L1 regulatory networks. In fact, lung adenocarcinoma predominated with KEAP1 inactivating mutations and lung squamous cell carcinoma with NRF2 activating mutations almost exclusively have an overall higher mutational burden with higher PD-L1 expression59. As a result of this cancer type-specific regulation, the non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) model employed by Li et al. may exhibit fundamentally different molecular regulatory mechanisms compared to the pancreatic and ovarian cancer models utilized in our study. While findings from Li et al. indicate that KEAP1 controls PD-L1 through direct protein ubiquitination, our work demonstrates that KEAP1 depletion indirectly regulates PD-L1 transcriptional level in NRF2-dependent manner. As such, we see reduced PD-L1 mRNA expression following KEAP1 knockout and or NRF2 overexpression. Another point to consider regarding the paradoxical role of KEAP/NRF2 is whether cancer-associated mutations in NRF2 lead to aberrant DNA binding and hence transcriptional activity of NRF2, which we show is a critical determinant of cytokine-induced PD-L1 expression. Regardless, our findings underscore the importance of clarifying and targeting cancer-specific role of the KEAP1-NRF2-PD-L1 axis.

Importantly, the role of KEAP1/NRF2 in overall tumorigenesis is multifaceted. In this study, we show that acute activation of NRF2 downregulates PD-L1, indicating a potential antitumor immune function of acute KEAP1 depletion or NRF2 activation. Supporting our findings, it has been reported in a murine cancer model, that KEAP1 knock-down cells, which have higher NRF2 levels, resulted in smaller tumors than wild-type cells. Notably, when the tumor cells derived from Keap1-depleted mice were transplanted into nude mice, higher tumorigenicity was observed60, perhaps indicating the differential impact of chronic vs acute KEAP1 depletion on overall carcinogenesis. Supporting this hypothesis, constitutive NRF2 activation promoted tumor initiation but impaired progression to advanced-grade tumors61. On the other hand, perhaps supporting our overall hypothesis, it has been shown that NRF2 activation inhibits chemical-induced carcinogenesis in animal models by detoxifying reactive oxygen species (ROS). However, persistent oncogene-mediated NRF2 activation has also been shown to promote tumor formation by lowering intracellular ROS and conferring a more reduced intracellular environment62. These examples indicate a paradoxical role of the KEAP1/NRF1 axis in tumor initiation vs tumor maintenance. Therefore, our findings may also capture the difference in acute vs. chronic roles of NRF2, as suggested by genetic vs pharmacological activation of NRF263. A deeper understanding of the KEAP1/NRF2 axis is needed to reveal these seemingly contradictory roles.

Our study has some methodological limitations. First of all, we focused on “druggable” genes in our screening. Despite several previously noted advantages of such targeted screening, the sgRNA library does not target the entire human genome. Hence, some significant regulators of PD-L1 might have been missed. Also, we performed the screening in vitro, which lacks physiologically relevant culture conditions and a tumor microenvironment. Even though we validated our findings in a tumor model, demonstrating that reduced tumor size is solely due to the reduced PD-L1 levels on cancer cells is challenging, because chemical inhibition of KEAP1 by CDDO-me treatment also reduces PD-L1 levels on other cell types, such as DC and DC1 subpopulations in the tumor microenvironment. In the light of these findings, a comparable in vivo screening may yield additional or new PD-L1 regulators. There are several reports supporting the link between NRF2 activation and resistance to ICIs64–67. Considering this literature, we do not think our findings suggest a combination therapy with KEAP1 inhibitor and ICIs will be synergistic, because KEAP1 would reduce the overall PD-L1 levels, which is needed for ICIs to work. Instead, we suggest that KEAP1 inhibition may have a better potential to be used in the cases that do not respond to the ICIs. In those circumstances, KEAP1 inhibition or NRF2 activation would reduce the PD-L1 expression in tumor cells, enhancing the infiltration of antitumor immune cells. Finally, although this screening is one of the most extensive PD-L1-focused screening in terms of the number of cell types (n = 6), expanding the cell line numbers representing additional cancers will further improve the rigor of these findings, strengthen the role of KEAP1/NRF2 axis as PD-L1 regulator and may yield additional new regulators.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture conditions: Human pancreatic cancer cell line MIA PaCa-2 (ATCC, #CRL-1420) was cultured in DMEM (Gibco, #11965-092) with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (Fisher scientific, #SH3091003), 2.5% Horse Serum (Fisher Scientific, #16-050-122) and 1% Penicillin–streptomycin (Life Technologies, #15140–122). Human pancreatic cancer cell line KP4 (RIKEN, #RCB1005) was cultured in IMEM (Gibco, #12440-053) with 20% Fetal Bovine Serum and 1% Penicillin–streptomycin. Human pancreatic cancer cell line AsPC-1 (ATCC, #CRL-1682) and human ovarian cancer cell line OVCAR4 (Millipore Sigma, #SCC258) were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Thermo Scientific, #11875093) with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum and 1% Penicillin–streptomycin. Human ovarian cancer cell line Caov-3 (ATCC, #HTB-75) and SKOV3 (ATCC, #HTB-77) were cultured in DMEM with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum and 1% Penicillin–streptomycin. Mouse pancreatic cancer cell line NB490 is a kind gift from Dr. Nabeel Bardeesy at MGH/Harvard Medical School and were cultured in DMEM with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum and 1% Penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were cultured and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 and 95% air.

Inhibitor/activator/chemical treatments

Cells were treated with 50 ng/mL recombinant IFNγ (human: PeproTech, #300-02 and mouse: PeproTech, #315-05) for 48 h.CDK1 inhibitor RO-3306 (Selleck Chemicals, #S7747) treatment was 5 µM for 24 h and CSNK1A1 inhibitor D4476 (Millipore, #218696) treatment was 2.5 µM for 48 h. Cells were treated with 2.2 µM Bardoxolone (MedChem Express, #HY-14909) or CDDO-me (Tocris, #6646) for 72 h. DMF (MedChem Express, #CS-0909) treatment was 100 µM for 48 h. ATRA (StemCell Technologies, #72262) treatment was 1 µM for 48 h. Cells were treated with indicated doses of Doxycycline (Sigma, #D9891) for 48 h to drive NRF2 over-expression.

Analysis of surface PD-L1 expression

Cells were pelleted and washed with dPBS (Gibco, #14190-144). Washed cell pellet were incubated with PE-PD-L1 antibody (human: Biolegend, #393607, mouse: Biolegend, #124307, human APC-PDL2: Biolegend, #329608) in dark for 30 min. Cells were pelleted again and washed twice with dPBS with 2% FBS. Cells were then pushed through 70-micron filters and analyzed in Flow Cytometry. Initial gating was done using an unstained control sample and PE signal was collected to analyzed surface PD-L1 expression. Analysis was conducted using FlowJo software with a representative gating strategy given in Supplementary Fig. 6a and median fluorescence intensity values were analyzed for the samples.

Druggable CRISPR library generation

Gene targets in the library were selected based on the presence of a specific or more general inhibitor targeting that specific gene or its protein product. Custom designed human druggable library targets 1422 genes and it is composed of 7 gRNAs/gene as well as 500 non-targeting gRNAs. Library is cloned into a vector (Addgene, #52963). Cloned library was verified with sequencing.

CRISPR screening of pancreatic and ovarian cancer cell lines and identification of potential targets

Stably Cas9 expressing cells (3 pancreatic and 3 ovarian cancer cell lines, 3 replicates/cell line) were infected with Human Druggable Library virus with an MOI of 0.4. Following transduction, infected cells were selected with 2.5 ug/mL puromycin (Invivogen, ant-pr). Cells were then cultured for several doublings and after the last passage, PD-L1 expression of the cells were induced using IFNγ for 48 h. At the end of IFNγ induction, cells were stained for surface PD-L1 and sorted based on the level of surface PD-L1 expression. For each replicate, bottom 25% (low-expressors) and top 25% (high-expressors) of PD-L1 expressing cells were separately collected. DNA was isolated from the collected populations (Invitrogen, #K182001). DNA for each replicate was PCR-amplified using custom oligo pairs that amplify the library (Supplementary Table 1). Amplified DNA was run on 1% agarose gel and correct size was excised and isolated from the gel (Qiagen, #28704). Finally, libraries were sequenced to identify enriched gRNAs in PD-L1 low and high populations, which are inhibitors and inducers of PD-L1 expression in the cells. Analysis of the sequencing data was carried out using CB2 package14. To identify the enriched genes in PD-L1 low populations in both pancreatic and ovarian cancer cells, genes that have z-score (LogFC) > 0 and p_pb < 0.05 for at least in 2 pancreatic or 2 ovarian cell lines were filtered and selected. Common genes that are present in both pancreatic and ovarian cell lines are accepted as common targets for both cancer types. The genes that are specifically enriched in pancreatic, but not ovarian cell PD-L1 low populations, were identified by selecting the genes that have z-score (LogFC) > 0 and p_pb < 0.05 for at least 2 pancreatic cell lines. Then, the “common genes” identified on the first step were removed from the list. Same analysis was also used to identify ovarian cell specific targets. Analysis to identify the depleted genes in PD-L1 low populations was carried out the same way, however, this time, genes were filtered for z-score (LogFC) < 0 and p_pa < 0.05. Gene-level CRISPR enrichment scores (log fold change) in PD-L1-low vs PD-L1 high cells and gene-specific significance (p values) scores for all 6 cell lines are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Validation of CRISPR hits and single-gene knock-out generation

Single gRNA oligos targeting CDK1, CSNK1A1, KEAP1 or NRF2 were purchased from IDT and sequences (listed below) were obtained from both our Human Druggable Library and previously published Brunello Library (Addgene, #73178) or Brie Library (Addgene, #73633) for mouse sequences (Supplementary Table 2). 10 µM from each oligonucleotide pair were mixed using annealing buffer (10 mM Tris, pH = 8, 50 mM NaCI, 1 mM EDTA) in a total volume of 50 µl and incubated at 95 °C for 5 min. Then, they were allowed to slowly cool down to RT. Next, annealed oligos were diluted (1:200) using nuclease free water. The ligation reaction was performed using BsmBI (NEB, #RO739) digested 50 ng backbone p413 (Addgene, #52961), 1 µl from diluted oligos, and T4 ligase (NEB, #MO202) overnight at 16 °C. The next day, 2.5 µl of the ligation reaction was transformed into NEB stable competent E.coli (NEB, #C3040H) and incubated overnight in the presence of ampicillin selection. Next, several colonies were picked and grown overnight. The next day, plasmid DNA was isolated using Qiaprep spin mini prep kit (Qiagen, #27206) and sent to Sanger sequencing for validation of successful insertion. After validation of intact gRNA sequences in backbone, constructs were separately transfected to HEK293T to package viral particles. These viruses were then used to infect the cell lines. Infected cell lines were selected using Puromycin and cultured for several passages before testing knock-out efficiency. In the case of the successful knock-out, cells were stained for surface PD-L1, and median fluorescence intensity of non-targeting gRNA infected and single-gene knock out cells were measured using Flow cytometry as described above.

Western blotting

For whole-cell Western Blotting experiments, cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #89900) and protein concentrations were determined using BCA assay (Thermo, #23225). Protein samples were mixed with 4X Laemni buffer (Boston Bioproducts, #BP-110R) and boiled at 95 °C for 10 min. Samples were then loaded to NuPAGE 4–12%, Bis-Tris gradient gel (Thermo, #NP0335) and run at 120 V for about 1.5 h. Proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membrane using iBlot dry transfer system (Fisher Scientific, #IB301032). Next, membranes were blocked using 5% milk dissolved in TBS-T (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween 20; pH 7.6) for 1 h at room temperature (RT). After blocking, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (Supplementary Table 3) overnight at 4 °C. Next day, membranes were washed with TBS-T 3 times for 10 min. Then, they were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. After the incubation, membranes were washed 3 times for 10 min. Membranes were then covered with western blot detection reagents (Thermo Fisher, #37074) and visualized using the iBright imaging system. For collecting membrane and nuclear fractions, cells were separated into fractions following manufacturer’s instructions (Cell Signaling Technologies, #9038). Protein from fractions were blotted as described above.

Live cell imaging

Incucyte Live cell imaging system (Sartorius) was coupled with PE-PD-L1 antibody (Biolegend, #393607) to measure PD-L1 protein levels in cells.

To analyze the PD-L1 levels in control and KEAP1 knock-out cells, PD-L1 was induced using IFNg and the system was set to collect images every 2 h in phase and red channels (phase: proliferation of the cells, red: PD-L1 levels). To analyze the effect of Bardoxolone on PD-L1 levels, cells were treated with Bardoxolone and immediately system was set to collect images every 2 h. After 24 h, IFNg was added, and system collected images for over 96 h. For doxycycline inducible NRF2 over-expression experiment, cells were treated with IFNg and increasing doses of doxycycline and images were collected for over 72 h in every 2 h.

QRT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated using Zymo Research Quick RNA miniprep kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Zymo Research, #R1055). Extracted RNA was converted to cDNA using High-capacity RNA to cDNA kit following manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Scientific, #4387406). To analyze PD-L1 mRNA levels, cDNAs were used for Q-RT PCR reaction. SYBR Green (Fisher Scientific, #4385612), nuclease-free water and primers (Supplementary Table 4) were combined with cDNAs, and RT-PCR reaction was carried out (Applied Biosystems).

Doxycycline-inducible NRF2 over-expression

pSLIK_hygro_3xflag_NRF2(RR) plasmid (Addgene, #136535) was modified to add EcoRI cut site to the ends of Flag-NRF2 sequence and to eliminate the EcoRI cut site in the NRF2 coding sequence. For the mutagenesis of the site, a primer pair to generate a single nucleotide change without changing the coded amino acid (position 1083, A to G, glutamic acid) was designed (F: CTGAAAGCACAGCAGAGTTCAATGATTCTGAC and R: GTCAGAATCATTGAACTCTGCTGTGCTTTCAG). To add the EcoRI cut site, another primer pair was used (F: ATACGAATTCGCCCTTATGGACTACAAAGACCATGACGGT and R: TATCGAATTCGCCCTTCTAGTTTTTCTTAACATCTGGCTTCT). All PCR reactions were performed using NEB high fidelity master mix (#M0541) following manufacturer’s instructions. Amplicons of these two separate PCR reactions were run on 1% agarose gel, and PCR products were purified using gel extraction kit (Qiagen #28706). Purified PCR products were annealed for 1 min at 95 °C. The annealed product was then amplified with a final PCR reaction and isolated from 1% agarose gel. FUW-tetO-SOX9 plasmid (Addgene, #141404) and generated BRF2-Flag were digested with EcoRI-HF® (NEB, #R3101) enzyme following manufacturer’s protocol. Digested DNA was ligated using T4 DNA ligase (NEB, #M0202) overnight at 16 °C. Ligated product was then transformed to competent bacteria (NEB, #C3040H) and plated. Colonies were grown and plasmid DNA was isolated using Qiaprep spin miniprep kit (Qiagen, #27206). The sequence was verified with Sanger sequencing. The plasmid was used to generate viruses along with the external Rtta3 (Addgene, #58750). Infected cells were induced with the indicated doses of Doxycycline to drive NRF2 over-expression.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation and qPCR enrichment analysis

The histones were cross-linked to DNA by adding 37% Formaldehyde to the culture media to a final concentration of 1% and incubating at 37 °C for 15 min. The cross-linking was quenched by incubation with 2.5 M Glycine at 37 °C for 5 min. After washing the cells with protease inhibitor (Thermo Scientific, #A32965) containing PBS, cells were collected with scraping and pelleted. The pellet was re-suspended in SDS Lysis Buffer (1% SDS, 10 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.1) with 1X Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Single-Use Cocktail (Thermo Scientific, #78442). Cells were then sonicated (Bioruptor Pico, #B01080010, 10 cycles 30 s on/30 s off) to generate 200–500 bp fragments. Sonicated cells were spun at max speed, and supernatant was collected. The supernatant was treated with the ChIP Dilution Buffer (0.01% SDS, 1.1% Triton X-100, 1.2 mM EDTA, 16.7 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.1, 167 mM NaCl) and incubated with NRF2 antibody (Cell Signaling, #12721) overnight. Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Scientific, cat. # 10003D) and Dynabeads Protein A (Thermo Scientific, cat. # 10001D) were prepared, washed, and incubated with the samples. Beads were then separated from the samples using magnet and re-suspended with Low Salt Immune Complex Wash Buffer (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.1, 150 mM NaCl), then LiCl Immune Complex Wash Buffer (0.25 M LiCl, 1% NP40, 1% deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.1) and finally with TE Buffer pH 8.0 (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.0). Beads were eluted with a DTT (Thermo Scientific, # P2325) containing elution buffer (1X TE, pH 8.0, 1% SDS, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT). Cross-links were reversed by incubation at 65 °C overnight. Following Proteinase K treatment (Thermo Scientific, #EO0491) DNA was extracted with phenol/chloroform. To assess the binding of NRF2 to PD-L1 enhancer region, Chip-QPCR was conducted using F: ATTACAGGCATGAGCCACTGCT and R: TGACCATATGTGAATTCCCATATCCGA primers.

In vivo experiment

All in vivo experiments were performed according to the ethical guidelines and the approved protocols by Northwestern University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee under Protocol #IS00014888. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. 4 × 106 NB490 cells were injected to flank of female 4–6-week-old C57BL/6 mice (Charles River Laboratories). After palpable tumor formation, mice were randomized and separated into two groups. While the control arm was treated with vehicle (Cremophor-EL: DMSO: PBS 1:1:8) the experiment arm was treated with CDDO-me (Tocris, #6646) for 5 days (7.5 mg/kg, daily). Tumor sizes were measured using caliper and tumor volumes were calculated as mm3.

Tumor immune infiltration analysis with high-dimensional spectral flow cytometry (CyTEK) panel

Harvested tumors were processed to obtain single-cell suspensions with collagenase/DNase digestion and passed through 70 μm pore strainers. Next, the single-cell suspensions underwent stimulation with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (50 ng/ml), ionomycin (5 μg/ml), and brefeldin A (10 μg/ml) to induce IFNγ and TNFα production. To prevent non-specific antibody binding to Fc receptors on immune cells, stimulated single-cell suspensions were incubated with anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody (Stem Cell Technologies, clone 2.4G2). Following this, live/dead dye (Zombie yellow, Biolegend, #423103 or UV-blue, Thermo, #L23105) and fluorophore-conjugated anti-mouse antibodies targeting cell surface markers (Supplementary Table 5) were added. Following PBS wash, the cells were fixed with 1× Fixation/Permeabilization reagent (eBioscienceTM, #00-5223-56) for 20 min at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were washed twice with 1× Permeabilization/wash buffer (eBioscienceTM, #00-8333-56) and subjected to intracellular staining (ICS) with an antibody cocktail for 45 min at room temperature. Finally, the cells were washed and suspended in PBS for acquisition using a three-laser CYTEK AURORA spectral flow cytometry or LSRII flow cytometry. To ensure accurate analysis, single staining of each antibody with corresponding fluorescence on splenocytes served as a reference for spectral unmixing (CYTEK). Data analysis was performed using either the OMIQ web application (https://www.omiq.ai/) or the FlowJo software.
