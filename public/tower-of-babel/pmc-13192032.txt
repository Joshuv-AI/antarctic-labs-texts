Tracing the in vivo behavior of nucleated cultured red blood cells generated from hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells
Authors: Lei Xu, Zhou Yang, Liqing Liang, Jing Zhang, Xin Yuan, Pengcong Liu, Qian Dong, Xuetao Pei, Mingyi Qu, Wen Yue, Xiaoyan Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRed blood cells generated from stem cells, called cultured RBCs (cRBCs), may alleviate the shortage of blood supplies worldwide. The intermediate product, nucleated cRBCs, may further bypass the inconvenience of RBC manufacturing. Elucidating the in vivo behavior of cRBCs, especially the interaction between cRBCs and macrophages, will aid in understanding the effects of their transfusion and facilitate the clinical translation of these cells.MethodsIn this study, we generated large-scale nucleated cRBCs from cord blood mononuclear cells using G-Rex bioreactors and evaluated their in vivo distribution, maturation and function by infusing these cells into irradiated NOD/SCID mice and comparing them with the properties of infused mature human RBCs.ResultsSimilar to the infusion of mature human RBCs, in the absence of macrophage depletion, the infusion of cRBCs improved the maintenance of the red blood cell count and hemoglobin concentration, but the infused human cells were barely detected in the peripheral blood of the mice. Immature cRBCs accumulated in various organs, including the spleen, bone marrow and liver, at 2 h postinfusion, independent of macrophage depletion. Macrophages phagocytose mature foreign RBCs while nursing immature cRBCs until maturation; the difference in the innate expression of cRBCs during maturation might account for this effect. In the short term after transfusion, the spleen and bone marrow were the major sites of cRBC maturation. In the liver, macrophages primarily functioned to clear mature foreign RBCs. cRBCs matured, enucleated, and were released into the circulation within one to three days after infusion. Pulmonary accumulation of cRBCs and embolism were not observed after the cRBC infusion.ConclusionsNucleated cRBCs can mature in vivo, and their transfusion advances our understanding of their potential as future transfusion products.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-04998-3.

Background

Blood transfusions play important roles in recovery from bleeding, surgery, and hematopoietic disease treatment. In low- and middle-income countries, the gap between the need for and supply of blood was estimated to be approximately 102 million units in 2017 [1]. Deriving red blood cells (RBCs) from stem cells represents a promising alternative, although the technique currently remains costly.

Hematopoietic stem cells from peripheral blood, cord blood, bone marrow, and pluripotent stem cells, as either embryonic stem cells or induced pluripotent stem cells, as well as immortalized erythroid progenitors, have been shown to give rise to RBCs [2, 3]. Early in 2011, a landmark study suggested the possibility of infusing cultured RBCs (cRBCs) [4]. However, the full translation of cRBCs into clinical practice has not yet been achieved. Although cRBCs exhibit characteristics similar to those of physiological erythrocytes, their therapeutic potential at the clinical level remains to be elucidated. Accordingly, determining the behavior of cRBCs in animal models before their clinical translation is highly important. Immune-deficient mice with macrophage depletion were chosen for an in vivo study of cRBC function, and a series of reports have demonstrated the successful generation of cRBCs with the morphology of reticulocytes and described the survival of these cells in vivo [5, 6]. However, clinical-grade cRBCs are still difficult to generate and verify because of the unique biological characteristics of RBCs and the large number of cells in RBC transfusion units.

The shortcomings in large-scale cRBC manufacturing include the following: (1) The enucleation rate of the cRBCs generated in vitro is low. The purification of enucleated cRBCs involves the loss of a large number of cultured cells. (2) Even with up-to-date technology, the final stage of cRBC production in vitro involves reticulocytes but not biconcave RBCs, indicating that in vivo maturation of reticulocytes is needed [7]. (3) cRBC manufacturing is costly, both in terms of cell culture and purification, and involves the consumption of large amounts of time and manpower. Considering the severe blood supply shortages in low- and middle-income countries, the need for affordable alternatives is particularly urgent. Nucleated erythrocytes have been suggested as a possible substitute for mature RBCs in blood transfusions because their manufacturing procedure is relatively simple and less costly [8]. Given these considerations, we would like to clarify the applicability of the use of nucleated RBCs in anemia recovery to address the shortage of blood transfusions. The in vivo behavior of nucleated cRBCs is therefore highly important for the clinical translation of cRBCs.

Species differences hamper preclinical studies of human cRBCs. Numerous animal models have been developed for in vivo studies of stem cell derivatives [9]. However, the most successful immunodeficient mouse model retains macrophages and granulocytes, which reject foreign cRBCs. Although strategies such as reticuloendothelial system saturation and combined liver cytokine humanization have been used, detecting and tracing cRBCs infused into immunodeficient mice are difficult [10, 11]. Despite the absence of appropriate animal models, a thorough evaluation of the safety and efficacy of cRBCs is necessary prior to the clinical translation of these stem cell derivatives. In addition, the macrophages that play a very important role in each step of RBC development should be the focus of a better analysis of the in vivo behavior of cRBCs [5, 6, 12]. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the distribution and prognosis of cRBCs in immunodeficient mice, as well as the interaction between cRBCs and the remaining innate immune cells, to lay a theoretical foundation for the optimization of preclinical studies of cRBCs in the future.

Methods

Preparation of red blood cells and macrophages

Human cord blood (CB) samples (n = 3) were obtained from the Umbilical Cord Blood Bank, Beijing, China. The procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Institute of Radiation Medicine (Approval No. AF/SC-08/02.192). CB mononuclear cells (MNCs) were cultured in G-Rex100® (Wilson Wolf Manufacture, MN, USA). The generation of cRBCs in vitro was performed in three steps using a previously published protocol, with modifications [13–15]. The detailed protocol is provided in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section.

The native mature CB-RBCs were separated by Ficoll‒Hypaque (1.077 g/L; TBD Science, Tianjin, China) density gradient centrifugation. Briefly, cord blood was diluted 1:10 with PBS and then carefully layered over the medium and centrifuged. Following centrifugation, the upper layers (including plasma, mononuclear cells, and separation medium) were aspirated and discarded. The pelleted red blood cells at the bottom of the tube were then harvested and washed with PBS for transfusion.

Immortalized mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (iBMDMs) were kindly provided by Song et al. [16].

Experiments using nonobese diabetic/severe combined immunodeficient (NOD/SCID) mice

NOD/SCID/-LtSz-scid/scid (NOD/SCID) mice (females, 8–12 weeks old) were obtained from commercial vendors (Experimental Animal Center of the Academy of Military Medical Sciences). The mice were housed according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Laboratory Animal Center.

We used two mouse models to determine the fate of cRBCs in vivo. (1) Irradiated mouse model: Before the cells were injected, the mice were sublethally irradiated with 2.5 Gy using a 60Co source (158.33 cGy/min). (2) Macrophage-depleted and irradiated mouse model: Before the cRBC injection, the macrophages in the mice were depleted by administering an intravenous injection of liposome-encapsulated CL2MP (clodronate liposomes, CLs; Liposoma, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) on days − 3 (100 µL), − 1 (50 µL), and − 1 (50 µL) of irradiation. The mice injected with an equal volume of PBS-filled liposomes were used as macrophage-intact controls. Additional treatment: When indicated, the mice were injected intraperitoneally with 5 × 109 human CB-RBCs or an equal volume of PBS 24 h before irradiation, and we named this treatment “macrophage saturation”. Both mouse models were sublethally irradiated with 2.5 Gy (day 0, time of transfusion) to induce anemia, although the erythroid lineage is less severely affected than other hematopoietic lineages in animals with pancytopenia [17–19]. The mice were then subjected to a tail vein injection of cRBCs (1 × 108 cells per mouse) or CB-RBCs (1 × 108 cells per mouse) that had been previously washed and labeled with CFSE (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. An equal volume of saline was injected as a negative control. A total of 78 NOD/SCID mice were randomly allocated to the indicated groups. Typically, 6–8 mice were allocated to each group, and 3–4 mice in each subgroup were sacrificed for examination (Supplementary Materials and Methods). The animals were randomized into groups using the Rand function in Microsoft Excel 2021 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA).

A total of 2 µL of blood was collected from the tail vein of the recipient mouse into heparinized tubes at each predetermined time point after transfusion and colabeled with anti-mouse Ter119, anti-human CD71, anti-human CD235a, and the nucleic acid stain Syto16 to determine the kinetics and maturation of human erythrocytes; otherwise, 10 µL of heparinized blood samples was collected and analyzed using hematology analyzer (Celltac E-MEK7222, Nihon Kohden, Japan). For tail vein injection and peripheral blood collection, the mouse was secured in a restraint device with its tail exposed. The injection or blood collection was performed promptly without anesthesia. At the indicated time points, the mice were euthanized through CO2 inhalation; the lungs, liver, and spleen were removed; and bone marrow cells were obtained by flushing the bones of the hind legs. Single cells extracted from various organs were counted and stained with the same antibodies as those used to label the blood samples. Samples from each group were processed alternatively to reduce potential confounders. Investigators who performed the animal treatment and sample collection were aware of the treatment group allocation, and blinding was used during the outcome assessment and during the data analysis, especially for the flow cytometry analysis.

The experimental procedures with animal models were performed and are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

In vitro RBC adhesion and phagocytosis assay

Briefly, 5 × 105 iBMDMs stained with PKH26 were cultured at 37 °C overnight to allow the cells to attach to the plate. Human CB-RBCs, mouse PB-RBCs, or human cultured RBCs (1 × 106) were stained with CFSE and cocultured in DMEM supplemented with 15% mouse serum for 2 h. Unbound RBCs were removed by washing the plate six times with PBS, and cell adhesion and phagocytosis were observed under a microscope. The number of RBCs attached and engulfed by iBMDMs was measured using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as the means ± standard errors of the means. The differences between groups were analyzed using ANOVA with Tukey’s test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All the statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software.

Results

Large-scale generation of cRBCs from CB-MNCs in vitro

We developed a multistep induction protocol that combines cytokines and small molecules to mimic the stages of erythropoiesis and improve the yield and maturity of CB-MNC-derived erythroid cells. The method comprises three major steps, as illustrated in Fig. 1a and detailed in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section.

After 21 days of culture, we obtained 9 × 109 cRBCs (> 98% erythroid cells) from one G-Rex100 flask, which was equivalent to the number of RBCs obtained from 2 mL of peripheral blood (Fig. 1b). Wright–Giemsa staining revealed that the morphology of cRBCs gradually matured (Supplementary Fig. S1). The expression of proteins regulating erythropoiesis was altered during induction (Supplementary Fig. S2). These cultures yielded cells that were similar to mature erythrocytes with a bright pink cytoplasm, no or a low nuclear/cytoplasmic ratio, and very condensed nuclei (Fig. 1c). Their globin expression pattern was similar to that of their counterparts, the native mature RBCs from CB (Supplementary Fig. S3). More than 73% of the cells were CD71 positive, and 98% of the cells expressed CD235a, including approximately 10% of the Syto16−CD235a+ enucleated RBCs (Fig. 1d), whereas the majority of the cells did not express myelomonocytic or megakaryocytic antigens (CD14 for monocytes, CD3 for T cells, CD19 for B cells, and CD56 for NK cells and CD34 for progenitors) (Fig. 1e). These nucleated cRBCs were immediately transfused into NOD/SCID mice.

The infusion of nucleated cRBCs improved erythroid parameters in irradiated mice

NOD/SCID mice were irradiated with 2.5 Gy of total body irradiation and then divided into 3 groups that received an immediate intravenous injection of (1) 1 × 108 cRBCs, (2) 1 × 108 mature RBCs (directly isolated from human cord blood), or (3) an equal volume of saline as a control to evaluate the effect of a cRBC transfusion (Fig. 2a). After 35 days of observation, all the mice survived, indicating that the in vitro-induced erythrocytes were not toxic to the mice. Irradiation resulted in anemia, with the lowest RBC count and hemoglobin content detected on approximately day 14 postirradiation. Based on the body weight and hemograms of the recipient mice at 2 h and 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 days after transfusion, the results observed with nucleated cRBC transfusion were similar to those obtained with the mature RBC transfusion and significantly better than those obtained with a saline transfusion, particularly during the depletion phase prior to the nadir (day 14). An infusion of human erythrocytes promoted the recovery of body weight and maintained peripheral blood erythrocyte count and hemoglobin levels in the mice during the progression of anemia (Fig. 2b; Table 1).

An assessment of the proportion of mTER119−hCD235a+ human erythrocytes in mouse peripheral blood and the proportion of Syto16+ nucleated erythrocytes in this population at different time points after the cRBC transfusion revealed that mTER119−hCD235a+ human erythrocytes were barely detectable in peripheral blood at 2 h after transfusion; 0.09 ± 0.03% of mTER119−hCD235a+ human erythrocytes were detected at 24 h, after which the proportion gradually decreased to barely detectable at 72 h (Fig. 2c). Prior to infusion, cRBCs had a low enucleation rate (~ 10%), whereas RBCs isolated by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation were predominantly enucleated (Fig. 1c). Immediately after irradiation and infusion, the human erythrocytes detected in the PB of recipient mice were also mostly enucleated, suggesting the selective entry of mature erythrocytes into circulation. Afterward, under irradiation stress, an enucleation analysis using the nuclear dye Syto16 revealed an increased enucleation rate of cRBCs from 1 day to 3 days after injection. By day 3, the enucleation rate of cRBCs in the recipient mouse PB was almost 100%, suggesting the gradual maturation of cRBCs in vivo (Fig. 2d). In the mature human RBC transfusion group, no human erythrocytes were detected in the recipient mouse PB during the experimental period. Human erythrocytes were undetectable in all the organs from all 3 groups on day 3 (data not shown).

Using hemograms generated before irradiation as a baseline, we found that the RBC count and hemoglobin content did not dramatically decrease at 2 h after irradiation. Furthermore, compared with the saline injection, the infusion of human erythrocytes did not significantly alter hemogram parameters. During the progression of anemia, transfusions of cRBCs or RBCs maintained relative RBC counts and a hemoglobin content greater than 80% compared with approximately 67% in the saline group on day 3. These relative levels remained above 50% in the transfusion groups but remained above approximately 30% in the saline group on day 14 (Table 1). In contrast, the highest percentage of detectable human RBCs in the mouse peripheral blood was only 0.11%. Consequently, infused human cRBCs or RBCs may support murine hematopoiesis through an indirect mechanism rather than through direct supplementation of the circulating RBC mass.

Nucleated cRBCs accumulated in the spleen and BM of NOD/SCID mice for short-term maturation

Considering that the most evident alteration in human RBCs in recipient mouse PB occurred within 24 h posttransfusion (Fig. 2c, d) and that the positive effect of a foreign RBC infusion could be detected early by infusion day 3 (Fig. 2b), we focused on the short-term behavior of the infused cells. Human RBC reconstitution in immunodeficient mice can be prevented by macrophages [20, 21]. We depleted macrophages in NOD/SCID mice by treating them with clodronate liposomes (CLs) and labeled cRBCs with the fluorescent dye CFSE before transfusion to track the distribution of cRBCs in vivo. CFSE+ cRBCs were subsequently injected into the tail vein of mice irradiated with 60Co-ɣ (Fig. 3a). Based on the results of the hemogram, the infusion of human RBCs increased the RBC count and hemoglobin index, but the short-term advantage was negated by CL treatment (Table 2). Previous studies have indicated the immediate tissue retention of infused RBCs within hours after the infusion [22, 23]. Accordingly, we harvested the PB, liver, spleen, lung, and bone marrow (BM) from NOD/SCID mice 2 h after the transfusion for further analysis.

Flow cytometry analyses of PB samples demonstrated that CL treatment reduced human RBC clearance from the mouse circulation (Fig. 3b). Although cRBCs and RBCs were both detectable in the PB 2 h after injection and CL treatment, the number of RBCs was greater than that of cRBCs, suggesting that the preferred organ for nucleated cRBCs was not peripheral blood. The results of the examination of human cRBC and RBC retention in recipient mouse organs revealed an inverse trend compared with that of PB. The proportions of mTER119−CFSE+ cells (human erythroid cells) in the spleen, bone marrow, and liver of mice infused with cRBCs were significantly higher than those in RBC-infusion controls, both with and without CL treatment, suggesting that the homing of immature RBCs was independent of macrophages (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. S4). Among the evaluated organs, the spleen was the main organ that trapped human RBCs, as shown by the increased proportion of mTer119−CFSE+ cells among total residual RBCs in the spleens of both the cRBC- and RBC-transfused mice (Fig. 3b). Under macrophage saturation conditions, the infused cRBCs gradually matured in the mouse organs, as shown by the significant increase in the number of Syto62− erythrocytes and decrease in the number of CD71+ erythrocytes 3 days after the injection (Fig. 3c).

By examining enucleated human erythrocytes (Syto62−CD235a+) as mature RBCs and decreasing CD71 expression, which represents the maturation of RBCs, we discovered that macrophage depletion with CL changed the proportion of mature cells among the infused RBCs at 2 h posttransfusion. In the spleen, compared with the intact macrophage system, macrophage depletion (CL) resulted in a significant increase in the percentage of mature erythrocytes in the RBC injection group and a statistically significant decrease in the mature erythrocyte ratio in the cRBC injection group. The BM showed a similar decreasing trend, and a difference was not detected in the liver (Fig. 3d). When the distribution of cRBCs between organs at 2 h posttransfusion was compared, the liver contained the greatest number of mature cRBCs, whereas immature cRBCs were localized mostly in the spleen (Fig. 3e). We hypothesized that macrophages in the spleen function primarily as maturation promoters when foreign RBCs are not fully mature. Consistent with these findings, spleen is the site of extramedullary erythropoiesis under stress conditions [24, 25]. A similar previous study, which used developmentally early erythroblasts for transfusion, also demonstrated their preferential homing to the spleen [26]. In the BM, macrophages function chiefly as feeders of human cRBCs as well. In contrast, the liver mostly clears foreign and aged RBCs. Very few human RBCs were retained in the lungs, which is consistent with previous reports that the lungs are not an organ for RBC development [27]. In addition, the lower retention of RBCs in the lungs is less likely to cause embolism in the recipient.

Interactions between macrophages and infused human erythrocytes

Macrophages are the key regulators of RBC maturation and enucleation, aged or damaged RBC turnover, and foreign RBC clearance [20, 27, 28]. CL treatment significantly reduced the number of macrophages, particularly in the spleen, which is also the primary location of macrophages. However, the macrophages could not be completely cleared under these circumstances (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. S5). Different infusions of RBCs, cRBCs or saline did not significantly alter the macrophage content in the spleen, BM, liver, or lungs at 2 h posttransfusion, but the number of macrophages that colocalized with exogenous transfused erythrocytes (mF4/80+CFSE+ cells) differed significantly among the different infusion groups; these cells may represent macrophages attached to or engulfing erythrocytes. In the spleen, BM, and liver, macrophages that colocalized with human cRBCs were much more abundant than macrophages that colocalized with human mature RBCs (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. S5). This trend was consistent with the ratio of human erythrocytes that accumulated in the organs of the different infusion groups. Comparisons between different organs revealed that the cRBCs that colocalized with macrophages mostly accumulated in the spleen, followed by the BM, liver, and lungs of mice with or without macrophage depletion (Fig. 4b), which was again consistent with the ratio of human cRBCs that accumulated in different organs.

We analyzed the interaction of infused erythrocytes with mouse macrophages in the spleen, BM and liver. In the flow cytometry plots, CFSE and mF4/80 double-positive signals represented the colocalization of human erythrocytes with mouse macrophages, and CFSE single-positive signals represented free human erythrocytes. The percentage of the mF4/80+CFSE+ population among the total CFSE+ human cells represented the accessibility of human erythrocytes to mouse macrophages. This ratio in the mature RBC-infusion group was higher than that in the cRBC infusion group, suggesting that mature human RBCs were preferentially trapped, whereas the total number of mature RBCs was lower than that of cRBCs in these organs. A trend was detected in both the CL-treated and non-CL-treated groups at 2 h posttransfusion in all the evaluated organs (Fig. 4c). The prompt response and rapid clearance of foreign mature RBCs by recipient macrophages may be responsible for this effect.

Because we discovered that mature human RBCs largely accumulated in the liver of the recipient mice (Fig. 3e), the colocalization of human erythrocytes with mouse macrophages in the liver was analyzed using organ sections prepared at 2 h posttransfusion (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. S6). We found that CL treatment reduced the number of macrophages (percentage of mF4/80+ cells). The percentage of macrophages that phagocytosed human erythrocytes (percentage of mF4/80+CFSE+ cells) in the cRBC infusion group was much higher than that in the RBC infusion group, consistent with the flow cytometry results, suggesting that the number of human erythrocytes that accumulated in organs substantially affected the ratio of macrophages that trapped human erythrocytes.

Normal tissue morphology after cRBC transfusion

Three days after transfusion, the bone, liver, and lungs of the recipient mice were harvested, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) for the morphological analysis (Fig. 5). No differences were observed between the cRBC, RBC, or saline infusion groups treated with or without CLs. Acute necrosis, inflammatory cell infiltration, and the destruction of organ structures were not detected in any of the analyzed groups. Considering the long-term recovery benefit after the transfusion of human erythrocytes, we expect cRBC transfusions to be safe.

The innate properties of cRBCs determine the interaction between macrophages and cRBCs

Given that macrophages respond differently to erythrocytes with different maturation statuses, we compared the gene expression profiles of erythrocytes at different developmental stages. We previously performed a global transcriptome analysis in which cord blood hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and four gradually matured erythroid progenitors (P2, CD71+CD235−; P3, CD71+CD235med; P4, CD71+CD235hi; and P5, CD71−CD235hi), as well as enucleated mature RBCs, were analyzed [29]. We focused our analysis on genes that were specifically expressed in mature RBCs but not in the developmentally closest erythroid progenitor cells (P5 stage) to clarify the general differences between mature RBCs and immature cRBCs, and identified 414 genes that were differentially expressed. Cataloged by the enrichment of Gene Ontology biological processes, these genes were highly enriched in pathways related to the regulation of the immune response, innate immune response, inflammatory response, and negative regulation of cytokine production involved in the immune response (Fig. 6a). In addition, by consulting the relevant literature and performing an integrative bioinformatic analysis, we focused on the mRNAs associated with macrophage attachment, which have been suggested to be involved in the interaction between erythrocytes and macrophages in erythroblastic islands (EBIs) [28, 30], mRNAs encoding blood groups that play major roles in transfusion rejection and mRNAs that have been reported to be involved in erythrocyte aging and turnover [31, 32]. As expected, the expression of the typical “do not eat me” signal CD47 [33] gradually decreased during the maturation of RBCs. The expression of most of the adhesion molecules involved in EBI formation and erythroblast maturation peaked around developmental stages P3–P4, whereas the highest levels of blood group-coding genes and erythrocyte aging-related genes occurred mostly in the final stage, namely, in mature RBCs (Fig. 6b). Based on the expression patterns of CD71 and CD235a (Fig. 1d), cRBCs primarily exhibited characteristics of the P4 and, to some extent, P5 stages. This molecular phenotype suggests that these cells are prone to interact with macrophages while potentially evading premature phagocytosis.

The interactions between mouse macrophages and human erythrocytes at different developmental stages were examined in vitro, in which mouse macrophages and human cRBCs or mature RBCs were mimicked. Mouse macrophage iBMDMs were first allowed to adhere to culture dishes and then incubated with different types of human erythrocytes or mature mouse RBCs (which were isolated from peripheral blood). After 2 h of coculture, mature human RBCs and cRBCs were observed to adhere to or be engulfed by iBMDMs in the presence of mouse serum. In contrast, mouse RBCs did not adhere to macrophages. Similar to the in vivo findings, macrophages preferentially phagocytosed foreign mature RBCs, followed by immature human cRBCs and mature mouse RBCs (Fig. 6c). Immature human erythrocytes were mostly attached to mouse macrophages instead of being engulfed by these macrophages, which was typically observed after a coincubation with mature human RBCs (Fig. 6d).

The interaction of human erythrocytes with mouse macrophages differed markedly between organs in vivo. Imaging revealed a preferential association of nucleated erythroid cells with splenic macrophages, in contrast to the predominant engulfment of enucleated RBCs by hepatic macrophages (Fig. 6e).

The results of the bioinformatic analysis together with imaging data revealed that high expression of adhesion molecules on nucleated cRBCs helped them attach to macrophages to complete the final stage of maturation, whereas the aging of mature RBCs together with the loss of “do not eat me” signaling activated innate immunity, resulting in macrophage engulfment and red blood cell turnover.

Discussion

Although red blood cells derived from diverse stem cells have shown potential as transfusion products and a proof-of-principle study has demonstrated the feasibility of their infusion, further preclinical studies should be performed before the translation of these cultured RBCs. In this study, we aimed to exploit the convenience of manufacturing nucleated RBCs to explore the clinical applicability of these cRBCs. When nucleated cRBCs were infused at the same dosage as RBCs, they matured immediately and ameliorated irradiation-induced anemia. As shown in this study, the transfused nucleated cRBCs that initially accumulated in the spleen and bone marrow fully matured in these organs within 3 days after the infusion and were released into circulation. In contrast, the infused mature human RBCs were promptly engulfed by macrophages in the liver. This rapid clearance suggested that their positive effect was mediated indirectly rather than directly by increasing the circulating red blood cell mass. Notably, the indirect mechanism observed here may not fully recapitulate human physiology because of interspecies differences. Therefore, these findings require confirmation in more translational models. Overall, nucleated cRBCs could be used as substitutes for timing blood transfusions to address the problem of limited blood product availability.

In our study, the maximal number of foreign cRBCs in the circulation was observed on day 1 after transfusion. Previous studies have reported that transfused human RBCs are trapped in the mouse liver and spleen and are destroyed within 2 h by phagocytosis [10]. The extended presence of cRBCs in the circulation suggests that immature RBCs homed to their niches immediately after transfusion, and the early time for their release into the circulation was approximately 24 h. Cultured RBCs reached maximum enucleation at approximately 72 h posttransfusion, both in the PB and organs, although these cells were phagocytosed immediately after maturation. At very early time points (2 h), the infusion of human erythrocytes did not significantly affect the hemogram indices. The subsequent significant improvement in these parameters observed in the RBC- and cRBC-transfused groups at 72 h posttransfusion compared with the saline control group suggested that infused foreign erythrocytes contributed to erythropoiesis or erythroid preservation. Taking these timing data into account, we would expect the functional time of cRBCs to be approximately 24–72 h posttransfusion. cRBCs may be used in the treatment of anemia and as a supportive therapy for patients with cancer.

When regular blood transfusions are performed, hemolysis is a common problem faced by patients. The interaction between RBCs and macrophages is key in the response to extravascular hemolysis [34]. Because the phagocytosis of nucleated cRBCs has rarely been observed in both in vivo cRBC transfusion and in vitro macrophage coculture and because the expression profile of nucleated cRBCs is not immunogenic, we would expect no hemolysis after a transfusion of nucleated cRBCs. The beneficial effect on hemogram indices was abrogated when recipient mouse macrophages were depleted using CLs, indicating that macrophages play an important role in alleviating anemia, although further investigations are needed to fully elucidate their functions. When mature RBCs are transfused, they are first detected in the circulation and are then captured and phagocytosed through the liver and spleen. Macrophages are crucial for EPO production, erythroblast island function, and iron recycling. We propose that they first function by providing support for the terminal maturation of cRBCs and that the major sites for this process are the spleen and bone marrow. The homing of cRBCs might not depend on macrophages because we did not detect alterations in the homing preference when macrophages were depleted using CLs within the first 2 h after transfusion. However, the number of macrophages that phagocytosed human RBCs was significantly altered, and the highest colocalization of signals from human RBCs and macrophages was detected in the spleen, where macrophage depletion resulted in a reduced cRBC maturation rate. Similarly, the spleen is the primary organ in which immature cRBCs are located. A possible path of cRBCs postinfusion might be that cRBCs first migrate to the spleen and bone marrow, where they mature and are released into circulation. Mature cRBCs were immediately phagocytosed by macrophages in the liver and spleen. In homogenous transfusions, macrophages may function only in cRBC maturation and aged RBC turnover. Both of these factors contribute to counteracting anemia. Considering the turnover of cRBCs, iron overload should be considered when cRBCs are used for transfusions.

Conclusions

Overall, we observed the behavior of cRBCs after transfusion. Nucleated cRBCs migrate to the spleen and bone marrow immediately after transfusion and are then released into circulation in an enucleated state. These findings reveal a defined in vivo maturation pathway and advance our understanding of the potential of these cells as a future transfusion product.
