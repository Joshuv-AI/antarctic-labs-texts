Health follows football: a reflexive thematic analysis of culture, masculinity, and men’s wellbeing in recreational football
Authors: Christopher McDermott, Aisling McGrath, Tom Egan, Laura Finnegan, Eoin King, Noel Richardson, Michael Harrison, Steve Daly, Paula Carroll
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMen carrying the greatest burden of preventable ill health are among the least likely to be reached by conventional health promotion. Football-based initiatives have demonstrated capacity to engage such men, yet the cultural mechanisms through which sustained engagement and health outcomes are achieved remain poorly understood. This paper explores how football culture and the organizational culture of the Football Cooperative (FC) initiative, an Irish social enterprise using recreational football as a gender-responsive mechanism for men’s health, interact to sustain engagement and create conditions in which health benefits follow. An interpretive qualitative design was adopted, using Reflexive Thematic Analysis guided by Braun and Clarke (2022) to examine how men make sense of football, masculinity, participation, and health within particular social and cultural contexts. Data were drawn from semi-structured and pitch-side interviews with 33 participants across two FC sites. Schein’s (2010) three-level organizational culture framework informed theme development. Three interdependent themes were constructed. The first established that football functions as a basic cultural assumption in masculine identity and requires no health framing to activate. The second examined how the culture of FC’s games aligns with, and sustains, existing meanings, through design, through reliability, through the reproduction of inclusive norms. And the third: when these cultures align, men who would not engage through conventional health routes experience meaningful health and social outcomes through something they already value. The findings suggest that effective gender-responsive health promotion for men may be dependent on creating cultural conditions which reduce social risk and are familiar to men.

Introduction

Men’s health in context

Men carry a disproportionate burden of chronic disease, premature mortality, and preventable ill health; across Europe, men die on average 5.3 years younger than women, a gap that narrows to 3.3 years in Ireland (Central Statistics Office 2023, Eurostat 2024). Among the most deprived men in Europe, life expectancy differences of 5 to 10 years compared with more advantaged men have been documented, alongside substantially higher rates of premature mortality from cardiovascular disease, injury, and suicide (Baker et al. 2014, OECD 2023, Richardson and McGrath 2025). Successive Irish national men’s health policies (National Men’s Health Policy 2008–2013, Healthy Ireland—Men 2017–2021, and Healthy Ireland Men 2024–2028) have called for coordinated, gender-responsive responses to improve male health outcomes, yet successfully engaging the men who carry the greatest burden of ill health remains a persistent challenge, as such men are the least likely to be reached by conventional health promotion.

The factors contributing to this disproportionate burden operate at both structural and social levels. At the structural level, health services have historically been organized around forms of engagement, disclosure, and clinical self-monitoring that do not align with how many men understand health, relationships, and support within their social worlds (Robertson 2007, Sharp et al. 2025). Health beliefs and behaviours can also serve as resources through which masculinity is constructed and demonstrated, with help-seeking and expressions of vulnerability carrying social costs in certain contexts (Courtenay 2000, Addis and Mahalik 2003). A systematic review by Seidler et al. (2016) found that masculine norms influence how men experience distress, seek support, and engage with services, while also demonstrating that men do engage when support is familiar, accessible, and non-stigmatizing. Similarly, Lynch et al. (2018) identified peer judgement, expectations of self-reliance, and cultural norms around coping as barriers for young Irish men. Consequently, contemporary men’s health scholarship increasingly argues that the issue is less male reluctance and more a mismatch between service design and the ways men engage with their health and one another (Galdas et al. 2023). The men at greatest risk are therefore often those least well served by conventional health promotion approaches (Robertson 2007).

Gender-responsive approaches in community settings

Gender-responsive health promotion is a multidimensional approach. It goes beyond where services are delivered. It encompasses the settings within which engagement takes place, the values and communication styles of those delivering it, a strengths-based rather than deficit orientation towards men, and the creation of culturally meaningful, socially safe, and non-stigmatizing conditions for participation (Osborne et al. 2018, Sharp et al. 2025). Location alone is not enough, even though reorienting services towards community settings has been shown to be effective. What gender competency actually requires is the whole design, not just the venue, working with, rather than against how men understand health, relationships, and support (Osborne et al. 2018, Galdas et al. 2023).

Men engage more readily when participation feels familiar, socially embedded, and free from the stigma of formal help-seeking (Bottorff et al. 2015, Robertson et al. 2018). The mechanisms here are relational, not clinical. Social connection. Peer accountability. Shared physical endeavour. These operate as active ingredients, not incidental features (Oliffe et al. 2020a). Rather than constructing separate health-identified spaces that require men to name a need before they can enter, gender-responsive design embeds health within activities men already do (Carroll et al. 2014, 2018, Sharp et al. 2025). Clinical settings often underestimate self-consciousness, fear of judgement, and the social costs of being seen to struggle. Group-based activity with peers is one of the conditions most likely to overcome them (Bell et al. 2023).

For underserved men, the design of the setting is not merely the delivery vehicle for a health intervention; it is the intervention (Oliffe et al. 2020b). Many men connect through doing. Activity-based programming reduces the pressure to self-disclose, and community-based sport and physical activity (PA) settings can therefore act as a hook, drawing men into sustained engagement through something they already value before any health goal is made explicit (Timm et al. 2025). Notably, the use of football and football settings has been shown to be effective gender-responsive approaches for men.

Recreational football produces broad-spectrum physical and mental health benefits (Krustrup and Bangsbo 2015, Milanovic et al. 2019, Eather et al. 2023, Sawa et al. 2025). However, men are not primarily motivated to play for these benefits (Frydendal et al. 2022). People manage their health through competencies already available to them (Nutbeam 2008). For many men, those competencies are organized through football rather than health awareness: men’s health behaviours are enacted through participation in football bringing physical routine, peer accountability, stress regulation, and social connection (Bruun et al. 2014, Oliffe et al. 2020a).

The Football Fans in Training (FFIT) study demonstrated that embedding health behaviour change within the cultural architecture of professional football clubs could reach underserved men (Hunt et al. 2014, Pringle et al. 2014). Men described joining because it felt ‘right’ rather than threatening their sense of themselves as men (Hunt et al. 2014) with emotional attachment to the club itself a predictor of weight loss success (Pietsch et al. 2022). Irrespective of the professional setting, sports club identity and an inclusive atmosphere are more decisive than programme content in sustaining engagement among underserved men (Timm et al. 2025) with a shared football language and identity also active ingredients (Dixon et al. 2019). Therefore, for many men, football does not need to be reframed through a health lens, as they will seek it naturally, through existing, conventional routes.

Football culture, masculinity, and health

In many national and cultural contexts, football occupies a distinctive place within men’s health promotion. Its significance extends well beyond PA. Across generations, it has functioned as a cultural field through which masculine identities, values, and forms of belonging are learned, reproduced, and reinforced (Burgess et al. 2003, Harding 2022). It is also the most widely played sport among adult men in Europe, with a reach unmatched by any other PA (Hulteen et al. 2017). Schein’s (2010) three levels capture how this culture manifests masculine norms (see Table 1). Football is the focus here, but the cultural mechanisms at issue are likely to operate wherever a sport or shared activity holds a comparable position in men’s lives.

Football culture is full of visible artefacts. Stadia as gladiatorial arenas, kits and jerseys as declarations of tribal allegiance, the rituals of matchday, chanting, choreographed supporter displays, all of it a visible grammar of collective masculine belonging (Giulianotti 2002). Two teams, a defined playing pitch and clear boundaries within which the contest is contained and aggression becomes legitimate. This is the game’s physical structure, and it reinforces everything mentioned above. For the men who play rather than watch, though, the pitch itself is the primary artefact. The format of the game, the physical contest it enables, and the familiar structure of competitive play together constitute a cultural space most of these men have known since boyhood (Žikić 2016, Frydendal et al. 2022). The espoused values of the game openly reward physical prowess, courage, and disciplined competition, with rules that channel aggression into purposeful form. Competition and recognition are the primary meanings men attach to recreational football (Frydendal et al. 2022). However, football’s deepest cultural work is done through unarticulated, unconscious beliefs and assumptions at a basic level. Playground football functions as a primary signifier of masculine status in boys as young as ten (Swain 2000) and collectively, courage and belonging are transmitted through a hidden curriculum running beneath the formal surface of male football training (Cushion and Jones 2014). For many men, this curriculum is engrained into their male identity and underpins their attachment to the game as both players and supporters (Giulianotti 2002, Vallerand et al. 2008).

Football Cooperative (FC)

Founded in 2017, the Football Cooperative initiative (FC) is an Irish social enterprise (SE) that uses recreational pick-up football as a gender-responsive approach to engaging men in health and wellbeing. Running year-round across two community astroturf sites, the FC initiative operates two to three evenings per week, open to men of all ages and abilities, charges €5 per game, and uses flexible game formats coordinated by Volunteer Game Coordinators who play alongside participants (Carroll et al. 2023). Games are recreational in focus and run for 60–90 minutes on half of a full-size astroturf pitch, typically at seven- to eleven-a-side depending on attendance, with teams of mixed age and ability selected on the night.

Existing evidence suggests that FC reaches men with elevated cardiovascular risk profiles, with 83.1% of participants presenting with three or more modifiable risk factors at baseline (Daly et al. 2024). Sustained involvement has been associated with improvements in aerobic fitness, reductions in waist circumference, lower levels of loneliness, and reduced cardiovascular risk (Daly et al. 2025a). Participation is also consistent with the upper ranges of recommended exercise guidelines (Everard et al. 2026). Additionally, a social return on investment (SROI) evaluation estimated that every €1 invested generated €17.60 in social value (Daly et al. 2025b).

Purpose of the paper

This paper sets out to better understand how the FC initiative achieves sustained engagement of men. Understanding how FC’s culture is produced, maintained, and experienced by participants matters beyond FC itself: it can support others who wish to use football, or other culturally meaningful sports and activities, as a mechanism to improve male health elsewhere.

Methods

Research design

This study adopts an interpretive qualitative design, using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) guided by Braun and Clarke (2022). The aim is to examine how men make sense of football, masculinity, participation, and health within particular social and cultural contexts. A constructionist epistemological position is taken throughout. Participants’ accounts are read as meaning-making, produced within specific cultural and social conditions, not as transparent reports of experience. Themes, in turn, are understood as constructed by the analyst through active engagement with the data. They are not pre-existing patterns waiting to be found within it.

Assumptions and positionality

Braun and Clarke (2022) position reflexivity not as a procedure for neutralizing the researcher, but as an ongoing, generative engagement with how the researcher’s perspective shapes the analysis. The lead author is a 39-year-old man, plays recreational football, has coached at various levels, and has worked in football. He played in FC games at both sites during data collection. He knew some participants through play before he ever interviewed them. None of this is incidental to the analysis. That proximity supports rapid pattern recognition and a felt understanding of what participants describe, but it also demands discipline. The real risk is not projection. It is under-reading: treating familiarity as completeness, missing the points where a participant’s account diverges from one’s own. A reflexive diary was maintained throughout the study. Entries were brought to research team meetings, where reflections were explored, interpretations challenged, and, at times, alternative readings offered and further literature suggested.

Data collection

Data were drawn from four sources (see Table 2). Primary data collection across two FC sites between 2023 and 2024 included semi-structured interviews at Site 1 (n = 4) and informal pitch-side interviews at Site 1 (n = 9) and Site 2 (n = 6). Interview guides addressed motivations to participate, game experience, social and relational dimensions of play, and the impact of participation on daily life. Interviews were conducted in person or via Zoom, audio recorded with consent, and transcribed verbatim. Semi-structured interview data collected at Site 2 as part of the FC initiative feasibility study (n = 14; April 2021) were also re-analysed within the scope of the original ethical approval (Carroll et al. 2023, Daly et al. 2025b), giving a combined dataset of 33 actively playing men across both sites.

The dataset was considered analytically sufficient when engagement with new transcripts was generating elaboration and nuance within existing patterns rather than new conceptual territory, consistent with Braun and Clarke’s (2022) conception of reflexive sufficiency rather than data saturation.

Data analysis and use of theoretical resources

The analysis was informed by several theoretical resources that entered at different stages of the research process. Schein’s (2010) model of organizational culture informed the study’s initial conceptual interest in how culture is expressed, maintained, and reproduced within FC. During analysis, Bourdieu’s (1990) concepts of habitus, field, and capital, together with self-determination theory (Standage and Ryan 2020), became useful sensitizing concepts for interpreting participants’ accounts of masculine identity, belonging, motivation, and sustained participation. Consistent with Reflexive Thematic Analysis, these theories were used as interpretive resources rather than as a priori coding frameworks or deductive analytic structures. Coding was developed from engagement with participants’ accounts, while theoretical concepts were brought into dialogue with the developing analysis to deepen and refine interpretation (Braun and Clarke 2022, Byrne 2022).

Of these, Bourdieu’s concepts describe habitus, the durable dispositions acquired through participation that orient behaviour without conscious reflection; field, the social arena with its own rules and stakes within which practice unfolds; and capital, the forms of value, including masculine status, that participation accrues (Bourdieu 1990, Harding 2022). Self-determination theory distinguishes motivation by quality rather than quantity; its most autonomous form, integrated regulation, describes activity so closely aligned with identity and sense of self that it requires no external justification (Standage and Ryan 2020).

Analysis followed the six phases described by Braun and Clarke (2022), understood as iterative and recursive rather than sequential. During familiarization, all transcripts were read in full before coding, noting content, tone, and what was felt to be significant. Codes (n = 44) were generated inductively across the full dataset; these were then organized into nine preliminary clusters before moving to theme development. Table 3 presents the thematic structure constructed through this process.

The reflexive diary was integral to this process. Each entry began with an initial reaction to a transcript or pattern, moved to interrogating that reaction, distinguishing whether it reflected genuine interpretive insight or familiarity with the culture, and reconciled with prior expectations. The diary entries that proved most analytically productive were not those that confirmed existing expectations, but those where a participant’s account diverged from them, since interrogating that divergence forced a more rigorous reading than confirmation would have required. Engagement with the literature on recreational football, masculine identity, and men’s health throughout analysis supported this process; the movement between data and theory is consistent with the constructionist orientation of RTA and with Byrne’s (2022) account of how theoretical sensitivity develops through analysis rather than preceding it.

Theme development was the most demanding phase. Themes were revised across drafts as the argument became clearer. Quotes appear throughout the findings to illustrate the analytic point being made, not to serve as evidence in themselves; each was selected as the clearest instantiation of a pattern identified across the dataset (Braun and Clarke 2022). Coding began inductively, from participants’ accounts. But theme development became increasingly shaped by a different question: how did football culture and FC’s organizational culture intersect across Schein’s (2010) three levels? Schein functioned, instead, as a sensitizing lens during the later stages of theme development, a way of interpreting and organizing patterns rather than generating them. Themes were constructed through engagement with the data, then refined through dialogue with relevant theory, consistent with Braun and Clarke’s (2022) reflexive approach. The work of Schein (2010), together with Bourdieu’s concepts and self-determination theory, helped illuminate relationships between football culture, organizational culture, and participant experience; none determined the content of the themes themselves. What the final thematic structure reflects, then, is an interpretive argument about cultural alignment, not a simple organization of patterned topics within the data.

Theme 1: ‘You see a football in the park and you’re thinking kick it, kick it’: the culture of football and its appeal to men

For men who have grown up with it, football is more than a sport. It operates as a cultural field, a site where identity is constituted, masculine capital accrues, and a particular sense of self is produced and reproduced through participation (Burgess et al. 2003, Harding 2022). In Schein’s (2010) terms, football operates here as a basic assumption, so deeply embedded in identity, habit, and masculine self-conception that it requires no health framing to activate and no external justification to sustain.‘You see a football in the park and you’re thinking kick it, kick it, you know, that’s what you want to do.’ (P12)This account was echoed by participants across both sites and appeared to reflect something that existed before the initiative itself. These men did not come looking for health benefits; they came because they wanted to play football. The relationship between football and masculine identity described by Burgess et al. (2003) is evident here not simply as theory, but in how participants oriented themselves towards the game. Drawing on Bourdieu’s concepts of habitus, field, and capital, Harding (2022) argues that football’s association with legitimate masculinity becomes taken-for-granted knowledge, reproduced through participation rather than conscious reflection. P12 seemed to be describing more than a preference for football; his comments pointed to an identification with the game that had developed early in life and been reinforced over time, to the extent that participation no longer required deliberate motivation. For the lead author, this account felt particularly familiar.

‘You see a football in the park and you’re thinking kick it, kick it, you know, that’s what you want to do.’ (P12)

From a self-determination theory perspective, such a deeply embedded connection to football reflects integrated regulation (Standage and Ryan 2020). Andersson et al. (2025) similarly found that health-related reasons ranked well below social and intrinsic motivations when participants explained why they played. This suggests that engagement may be more readily sustained when programmes build on an existing cultural attachment rather than asking men to enter a health-identified space. At the same time, that attachment depends on the game remaining worth attending. Participants described the importance of competitive intensity alongside an open format, arguing that without these features the experience loses much of its appeal to them. P13 captured this point directly:‘I’m not a good golfer right, but I played golf last week and I was absolutely useless. The reason I was useless wasn’t the competition. It was no money on the line. I need that competitiveness [in football].’ (P13)P11 described what the wrong format looks like: a five-a-side enclosure ‘akin to cage fighting’, compressed, walled rather than lined, with no breaks and no space. Those conditions, he suggested, produce a version of recreational football that excludes most men at this life stage by making meaningful participation difficult. Taken together, the various accounts suggest that the game has to work as football before it can work as anything else.

‘I’m not a good golfer right, but I played golf last week and I was absolutely useless. The reason I was useless wasn’t the competition. It was no money on the line. I need that competitiveness [in football].’ (P13)

The version that works preserves competitive intensity in an open format, neither too contained to satisfy nor so aggressively hierarchical that it pushes men away. Anderson (2010) and Pringle and Hickey (2010) both identify the tension between inclusive and traditional masculine sporting cultures as one that men actively and continuously negotiate. What is significant here is that this negotiation appears to have been resolved largely through participant practice rather than formal rule, which suggests the culture of the game is doing work that no intervention manual could specify. Format is not incidental to engagement; it is central to the mechanism through which engagement is sustained.

The pitch, however, is more than a venue for competition. What it holds, and what few other adult settings permit, is the expression of qualities, intensity, creativity, aggression, and tenacity that have little legitimate outlet elsewhere in male life. Harding’s (2022) Bourdieuian reading helps explain why: participation in the footballing field disrupts the habitual dispositions men carry from other social fields, so that emotional intensity and competitive expression become permissible in a way they are not in the workplace, in domestic life, or in most other spaces men occupy. The social risk that would accompany those qualities elsewhere is left at the gate, and P5 captures the permission structure this creates:‘There are very few other spheres in life when grown adults, men, would go on about creativity and that kind of thing, but they’re happy to talk about it in relation to Paul Scholes [Professional football player].’The pitch functions here as a sanctioned channel for qualities that have few legitimate outlets elsewhere in adult male life and as a shared language through which men can express those qualities with reduced social risk. P2 approaches the same dynamic from a different angle:‘I wouldn’t classify myself as an aggressive person, not even in the slightest, but on the football pitch I do like to have a little bit of bite, a bit of tenacity, and I don’t really have an outlet for that in other aspects of my life.’P6 names what sport now carries in the absence of other such arenas:‘I feel like sport is now the only arena where men can get that same kind of, I don’t know how to describe it, primal fulfilment.’For many of these men, the pitch is not one option among many but increasingly one of the few remaining options. Within it, men can be strong, skilful, and known by other men in ways that few other adult contexts make possible, and P6 names what that recognition means:‘I’m kind of good at football, and people, whenever I don’t play, always want me to come back. So that’s a big confidence boost, for my own personal self-esteem.’Being recognized on the pitch, by other men, for a quality legible within masculine culture, appears to offer something that work, domestic life, and other adult settings do not consistently provide. For others the connection is more fundamental still, bound up not in recognition but in continuity of self:‘I have to play at least once a week, otherwise I just wouldn’t feel right. If I don’t play, I don’t feel like myself.’ (P15)P15 appears to be describing more than a disrupted preference. His account points towards an interruption to a valued sense of self, with participation among other men appearing to play an important role in restoring it. It is within that field, assembled around competition, masculine expression, and a sense of belonging among men, that the experience of flow becomes possible. Eight participants across the dataset independently described the same experience, the consistency suggesting the mechanism is not individual but produced by the game itself. Csikszentmihalyi (1990) names this state ‘flow’, and P10 provides a clear account of it:‘It’s almost like you’re in flow, you know, you’re playing an hour and a half, you’ve forgotten about everything and just at that time you’re not thinking about the past or the future, anything like that, you’re just in the present for an hour and a half. I would have taken up the piano, done yoga, meditation, and that’s served me well, but there’s something about being in a group dynamic that I think is a huge thing.’Shared goals and real-time coordination deepen absorption in ways that individual activity may not (Nakamura and Csikszentmihalyi 2012). What P10 identifies as important, the group dynamic, aligns closely with the social conditions described by participants throughout the data. Flow seems to function here not as a supplementary benefit of playing football, but as a central mechanism of engagement, operating within a game men already understand and a culture they already inhabit.

‘There are very few other spheres in life when grown adults, men, would go on about creativity and that kind of thing, but they’re happy to talk about it in relation to Paul Scholes [Professional football player].’

‘I wouldn’t classify myself as an aggressive person, not even in the slightest, but on the football pitch I do like to have a little bit of bite, a bit of tenacity, and I don’t really have an outlet for that in other aspects of my life.’

‘I feel like sport is now the only arena where men can get that same kind of, I don’t know how to describe it, primal fulfilment.’

‘I’m kind of good at football, and people, whenever I don’t play, always want me to come back. So that’s a big confidence boost, for my own personal self-esteem.’

‘I have to play at least once a week, otherwise I just wouldn’t feel right. If I don’t play, I don’t feel like myself.’ (P15)

‘It’s almost like you’re in flow, you know, you’re playing an hour and a half, you’ve forgotten about everything and just at that time you’re not thinking about the past or the future, anything like that, you’re just in the present for an hour and a half. I would have taken up the piano, done yoga, meditation, and that’s served me well, but there’s something about being in a group dynamic that I think is a huge thing.’

This theme suggests that the cultural conditions supporting engagement in football-based initiatives do not need to be created from scratch. Participants described these conditions as already existing prior to engagement with the initiative itself.

The design challenge is not to create motivation but to meet it, in a format that lets football be football and holds open the gate these men already want to walk through. Theme 2 will now examine how the FC initiative does exactly that.

Theme 2: ‘You’re a random assortment of people, that culture is very, very open and inclusive’: the culture of FC games

This theme examines how the culture of FC games aligns with the football culture Theme 1 described and moves across the levels of Schein’s (2010) framework from the visible artefacts that communicate welcome before a man has played a single game, through the espoused values that shape how participants behave towards one another, to the basic assumptions so deeply held that they reproduce themselves without instruction. That movement across levels is central to the story of Theme 2.

Visible artefacts

‘Why is there such a good culture? It’s because you’re a random assortment of people. You’re not streamed into a skill level. That culture is inclusive, very, very open and inclusive. And I think that’s probably the key to success.’ (P13)P13 identifies something beyond a welcoming atmosphere; he is describing a structural condition, the absence of hierarchy by ability, age, or background that makes a particular kind of culture possible. The culture of FC games rests on a foundation of deliberate and values-led design choices, enabling conditions, and participant behaviours that together constitute what Schein (2010) would recognize as a functioning organizational culture operating at depth.

‘Why is there such a good culture? It’s because you’re a random assortment of people. You’re not streamed into a skill level. That culture is inclusive, very, very open and inclusive. And I think that’s probably the key to success.’ (P13)

The €5 fee, the cashless online payment, the drop-in format, and the community astroturf location, taken individually, each is a practical convenience; but taken together, they constitute a coherent and deliberate statement about who is welcome, made before a man has played a single game. P11 frames the fee in terms that go beyond cheapness:‘What sort of gym or participatory exercise are you going to get, an hour, an hour and a half, decent quality exercise, enjoyment, in a structured way for five or six quid? It just doesn’t happen these days.’For P15, a PhD student living alone in a new country, the fee was not philosophical but material:‘If the game fee was €10 or €15, I wouldn’t have played as much. I’d probably only play once a week or even once every two weeks because that’s all I could afford.’P8 names what the flexible format means for a man whose week is structured around obligations to others:‘I could organise myself and be responsible for my own time, rather than other people depending on me. I can’t really emphasise that enough.’Read together, these design features appear to communicate a presumption of welcome rather than a process of qualification. Hunt et al. (2014) demonstrated that gender-responsive design in context and delivery, rather than content alone, was central to reaching high-risk men who had not engaged with other health services. Timm et al. (2025) similarly identify cost, distance, and flexibility as important influences on engagement decisions for underserved men.

‘What sort of gym or participatory exercise are you going to get, an hour, an hour and a half, decent quality exercise, enjoyment, in a structured way for five or six quid? It just doesn’t happen these days.’

‘If the game fee was €10 or €15, I wouldn’t have played as much. I’d probably only play once a week or even once every two weeks because that’s all I could afford.’

‘I could organise myself and be responsible for my own time, rather than other people depending on me. I can’t really emphasise that enough.’

These artefacts, in Schein’s (2010) terms, signal the FC initiative’s values before any direct interaction has taken place. Those values indicate that men do not need to justify their presence or demonstrate a particular level of fitness to belong.

Consistency is what sustains that communication, and when P1 describes FC as ‘the Immoveable Feast in my diary’, he is naming what reliable delivery produces over time: a fixture so dependable that it has become part of his weekly life structure. That reliability is therefore more than an operational detail; it is what makes that welcome credible.

The coordination behind that reliability represents a form of labour performed by Volunteer Game Coordinators whose role extends beyond administration to the ongoing maintenance of the norms and expectations that sustain the culture of the initiative.

Espoused values

Artefacts lower the barrier; what determines whether men stay is the culture they meet when they cross it. Through participant practice rather than formal design, FC games appear to address barriers that Bell et al. (2023) identify as resistant to conventional health promotion, the fear of judgement and self-consciousness that group-based activity among similar others can help overcome. Robertson et al. (2018) identify trust as central to men’s sustained engagement in community sport, built through flexibility, active inclusion, and a no-judgement environment. Participants described that trust not as an outcome of participation but as a pre-existing feature of the pitch environment.

That trust is not unconditional, and P22 captures bluntly what happens when someone tests it:‘Nobody tolerates that sh*t here. Fit in or f**k off basically.’What it produces for someone arriving for the first time is described by P16:‘It was a bit daunting at first, being with a completely new group of people. But everyone was welcoming, and someone met me on the first night and was in touch before the game, which made a big difference.’The culture that enforces the norm is the same one that makes the welcome possible. Oliffe et al. (2020b) argue that men-friendly community spaces function as the intervention rather than merely supporting it. Participants’ accounts suggest that the culture men meet at FC games cannot be fully specified in an initiative manual. Instead, it is produced and maintained through participant interaction, with important implications for how initiatives such as FC are understood, replicated, and scaled.

Basic assumptions

A more revealing question about the culture of FC games is not what it produces but how it sustains itself across time and changing membership without formal structure. The data suggest a process consistent with Schein’s (2010) description of cultural reproduction, operating through participant behaviour and basic assumptions so taken for granted that alternative ways of behaving become unlikely rather than merely prohibited. Bourdieu’s (1990) concept of habitus offers one way of understanding how this occurs without formal instruction, and the participant accounts give these processes visibility. P21 describes what happens when someone tests them:‘Sometimes when you have a larger group, you get the odd person who comes down and starts shouting a lot, but they’re told not to do that pretty quickly. With the culture that’s developed over time, they stop.’P6 speaks to the same assumptions from a different perspective:‘As an older guy I always try to make sure everybody feels included, everybody gets a bit of the ball, because that’s what we’re all there for.’These accounts suggest that participants have absorbed the basic assumptions underpinning FC games deeply enough to reproduce and extend them without prompt. Robertson et al. (2013) found that effective community sport programmes often produce a similar outcome, whereby participants actively sustain the environment that supports them. In this sense, the culture of FC games can be understood as operating at the deepest level of Schein’s framework, held not through rules or formal structures but through assumptions that participants have made their own.

‘Sometimes when you have a larger group, you get the odd person who comes down and starts shouting a lot, but they’re told not to do that pretty quickly. With the culture that’s developed over time, they stop.’

‘As an older guy I always try to make sure everybody feels included, everybody gets a bit of the ball, because that’s what we’re all there for.’

That reproduction is not seamless, and the same data that show the culture holding also show it being tested, negotiated, and occasionally disrupted when new participants arrive carrying different assumptions. What was notable was that the culture absorbed these tensions without formal intervention, suggesting the underlying assumptions are durable rather than fragile. At the same time, inclusion should not be read as settled. Participants from a range of national and ethnic backgrounds described FC games as a route into community and belonging, yet the data also contain accounts of friction attributed to cultural and language differences, and one participant’s observation that men from certain minority backgrounds could be treated differently for equivalent conduct on the pitch. Inclusion at FC games is therefore best understood as an ongoing accomplishment, actively maintained and occasionally strained, rather than a fixed property of the setting.

What this theme makes clear for practice is that the culture of FC games is not a set of features that can be lifted and transferred. Initiatives that replicate the artefacts without understanding the cultural reproduction operating beneath them will get the surface without the substance. What sustains engagement is a culture that men find worth staying in, built slowly, maintained collectively, and operating at a depth that no initiative specification can reach. Theme 3 goes on to explore what happens when those cultural conditions hold over time.

Theme 3: ‘It keeps me well’: when cultures align, health and community outcomes follow

This theme examines what happens when the two cultures this paper has described operate together. Football arrives in these men as a cultural assumption already embedded in identity. It requires no health framing to activate, as Theme 1 showed. Then Theme 2 showed that FC games hold that gate open for the men who arrive carrying it. When the two cultures align, men who may never have engaged through a conventional health gateway can experience meaningful health benefits, such as improved mental health, reduced social isolation, and better management of cardiovascular risk, through a football gateway instead. No one was recruited on the basis of health need, and the initiative was not explicitly designed around individual health concerns. Health outcomes came through participation in something men already wanted to do, in a setting that supported continued engagement.‘It’s definitely a replacement for the pub, at that time in the night where you’d be thinking about going out for a pint…’ (P9)P9 is describing something more specific than a lifestyle swap. What he names is football occupying a culturally legitimate place in the evening, through which social connection and physical release that might otherwise have been sought elsewhere became possible, without any explicit health framing prompting it. P9 went on to describe a significant shift in his mental health:‘…I think my mental health has dramatically improved. I was on antidepressants. I came off them.’ (P9)P15 describes a similar process during a period of depression:‘I was in a really bad place. Football became my escape. Going for a run wouldn’t have helped, because while I ran, my mind would still be working, overthinking everything. But when I played football, I had to focus on the game, on my teammates. My mind switched off from all my problems.’The health profile of the men who arrived matters here, and Daly et al. (2025a) found that 83.1% of FC participants carried three or more cardiovascular risk factors at baseline, while 62.9% reported good to excellent health. This apparent gap between objective risk and subjective health perception may reflect what Nutbeam (2008) and subsequent men’s health scholarship describe as health literacy functioning as a personal asset: the context-specific knowledge through which people navigate their health in their own terms rather than those clinicians would prefer (Oliffe et al. 2020a, Galdas et al. 2023). Participants’ accounts suggest that health literacy was often expressed through football rather than through clinical language or self-identification with health concerns, and FC games appears to have engaged men within that familiar context.

Contemporary men’s health literature increasingly argues that the issue is less male resistance to help-seeking and more a mismatch between how services are designed and how men engage with their health and with one another (Seidler et al. 2016, Galdas et al. 2023, Richardson and McGrath 2025). FC games appear to avoid activating that tension, not by challenging prevailing norms directly but by not presenting themselves as a health service in the first place. The evidence base supports this interpretation consistently: activity-based approaches reduce stigma without requiring emotional disclosure (Robertson et al. 2018); framing through sports identity reduces scepticism (Timm et al. 2025); men describe sport-based settings as acceptable in ways clinical environments are not (Bell et al. 2023); and the greatest gains in comparable initiatives have been found among the most isolated participants, linked to informal and non-judgemental environments (McGrath et al. 2022).

Across these strands of evidence, a consistent pattern is visible. The mechanism appears to lie less in health content than in setting, less in what is offered than in where and how it is offered, and FC games appear to reflect that pattern without explicitly targeting health outcomes. Oliffe et al. (2020b) argue that men-friendly community spaces do not merely support interventions but often function as the intervention itself. Within that context, FC games appear to demonstrate how health benefits can follow from participation in an activity valued for reasons other than health.

FC games also disrupt male social isolation at precisely the life stage when disruption is often hardest to find. Loneliness follows a U-shaped distribution across adulthood (Victor and Yang 2012), yet for men in this study midlife appeared to be a period in which existing social structures were under increasing pressure rather than stability. P9 names what it means to make new friends in his thirties:‘I’ve made new friends, which is a great thing to be able to say in your 30s, because I don’t think many lads really make new friends when they’re in their 30s.’Beyond sustaining existing relationships, FC games create new ones across demographics and communities that would not otherwise intersect. P15 describes how the game itself generates the conditions for that interaction:‘Once you start playing, you naturally talk to each other. You need to know each other’s names to call for passes. And then, you ask, where are you from? From there, we start talking.’What appears significant here is that the game may be performing a form of social infrastructure work that formal community initiatives struggle to replicate. Participants described connection as a product of participation rather than as its stated purpose, and that distinction may help explain why it works for men who would resist a programme explicitly designed around connection. The culture described in Theme 2, its reliability, cooperative ethos, and non-judgemental character, appears not merely to shape experience but to create the conditions through which these outcomes become possible and accumulate over time. Among FC participants, Daly et al. (2025a) documented reduced loneliness alongside health gains associated with sustained participation, with reductions in waist circumference emerging at 11 months and more regular attendance associated with greater benefit, consistent with wider evidence that team sport produces stronger mental and social outcomes than individual activity at higher frequencies (Eather et al. 2023). These findings have implications well beyond subjective wellbeing: social isolation carries a mortality risk comparable to major behavioural risk factors (Murthy 2023), and cohort evidence confirms it remains independently associated with all-cause mortality (Lennartsson et al. 2022).

Over time, the men inside FC games have become its most credible advocates without being asked to:‘If you asked the lads how many of you would be playing twice a week without this group, a high percentage wouldn’t. It should be done everywhere where they have a decent facility.’ (P13)These are not men who entered FC games as a health intervention, and yet they are now making the public health case for it themselves, with a precision and conviction that would not be out of place coming from a health professional. That shift, from participant to advocate, is itself evidence of what the alignment of these two cultures produces. Men who arrived carrying football and found a culture that held them have internalized not only the basic assumptions of FC games but their value, and they are now reproducing both. Connell (2020) argues that masculinities are sustained within institutional contexts and that those contexts narrow as men move through adulthood; as Theme 1 established, football is one of the few that has not. FC games appear to function as one such context, holding open a space where a man can be strong, competitive, and known by other men, while quietly doing something else entirely. Krustrup and Krustrup (2018) establish football as broad-spectrum medicine. FC games appear to offer a mode of delivery that men will engage with precisely because it was never presented as medicine. For gender-responsive health promotion, the implication is that the most effective design may not be the one that targets men most precisely, but the one that builds conditions in which men choose to engage on their own terms, through a gateway they already had reason to enter.

Synthesis and implications

A single mechanism runs across the three themes. Men arrive already carrying football as a basic cultural assumption, the FC initiative’s organizational culture aligns with that assumption rather than reframing it, and health and social outcomes accumulate through the sustained participation this alignment makes possible. Whether that engagement could support more deliberate health promotion is a fair question, and the findings suggest it could, on the culture’s own terms and timeline. Football operated here as the kind of hook Timm et al. (2025) describe, drawing men into sustained engagement before any health goal was made explicit. Although the men in this study were not recruited through health framing, many increasingly articulated the health and social value of participation, and some now advocate for the initiative on that basis. One implication, to be tested rather than assumed, is that deliberate additions such as health checks could be introduced once engagement is established, in consultation with the men themselves, taking hold through the same participant-led norms that Theme 2 shows absorbing change rather than being designed in from the outset as in explicitly health-framed programmes (Hunt et al. 2014). Leveraging is therefore a question of timing and legitimacy. Whether such additions could extend health gains without disturbing the conditions that produce engagement remains an empirical question and a priority for future research.

Limitations

The experiences of those who chose not to attend, or who attended and did not return, are not captured here, and therefore, the extent of non-participation among men in the surrounding communities is unknown. While we know from previous work that FC games reach men carrying elevated cardiovascular risk, participants to date have been drawn from a relatively educated, urban population (Daly et al. 2024). Future work should explore non-participation by tracking how engagement changes over time and test whether these findings hold in other settings, other communities, and other culturally meaningful activities beyond football.

Conclusion

This study examined how men experience and sustain participation in FC games across two community sites. Football operates as more than a recreational activity. It is a culturally meaningful space through which masculine identity, belonging, competition, and social connection are expressed and maintained, and the FC initiative’s organizational culture aligns with those meanings. This creates conditions in which participation feels familiar, legitimate, and worth returning to. Health benefits follow, but not because men have entered a health intervention. They follow because men continue participating in something they already value.

There are implications here for gender-responsive health promotion. Engagement was sustained through cultural resonance, trust, inclusivity, and the reproduction of shared norms through participants themselves. Therefore, practitioners should ensure that deeper cultural conditions that make participation meaningful and sustainable for men are addressed alongside programme content and delivery.

Ultimately, the FC initiative resembles football that generates health-related outcomes more than a health intervention that uses football. The mechanism is not the delivery of health content. It is the alignment between a culture men already value and an organizational environment that lets them participate on their own terms. The FC initiative provides the context for these findings, but cultural resonance and organizational alignment are not unique to the FC initiative. The same dynamics could plausibly hold wherever recreational football creates conditions men find meaningful and worth returning to, and for men least likely to engage through conventional health promotion routes, that distinction may matter most.
