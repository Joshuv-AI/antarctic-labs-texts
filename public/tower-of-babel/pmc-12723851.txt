Validation of predictive factors for hospital admission in elderly patients transported by emergency medical services: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Makoto Suzuki, Daisuke Usuda, Tomohisa Nomura, Manabu Sugita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aging global population has led to rising emergency department (ED) visits and hospital admissions among older adults, many of whom have complex medical and social backgrounds. A substantial proportion of these visits may be avoidable, particularly among individuals receiving home medical care. Such avoidable hospitalizations can lead to adverse outcomes such as functional decline and iatrogenic complications, while placing additional strain on healthcare systems. Identifying predictive factors for hospital admission in this population is essential for improving care quality, optimizing resource utilization, and guiding decision-making in prehospital settings.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study at the Juntendo University Nerima Hospital in Japan. “Tokyo Rule” (TR), a policy enacted on August 1, 2009, by the Tokyo Metropolitan Government, applies to difficult emergency transport cases and mandates that designated hospitals accept unanticipated ambulances. TR criteria included: (1) the patient’s clinical condition was generally moderate or mild; and (2) ≥ 5 medical institutions refused ambulance transfer requests or 20 min had elapsed since the selection of medical institutions by an emergency medical technician. If the case meets both criterion (1) and criterion (2), it is regarded as a TR case. Our institution proactively accepts patients transferred by ambulance, including those under TR. Patients aged 75 years or older who were receiving house call medical services at home or in a nursing home (NH) and were transported to our hospital by emergency medical service (EMS) were included in this study. Clinical, demographic, and situational data were obtained from electronic medical records. The primary outcome was hospital admission. Univariate analyses were performed to identify potential predictors, followed by multivariate logistic regression analysis to determine independent predictors. A two-tailed significance level of 0.05 was applied.ResultsOf the 853 patients transported to our hospital during the study period, 738 met the inclusion criteria. Among them, 503 were admitted, and 235 were discharged from the ED. In the univariate analysis, factors significantly associated with admission included a lower Glasgow Coma Scale score; higher National Early Warning Score; oxygen therapy in the ED; and suspected diagnoses of infection, cerebrovascular disease, or cardiovascular disease. In the multivariate analysis, independent predictors included suspected infection, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, digestive disease, oxygen therapy in the ED, and reduced consciousness. Minor trauma and transient loss of consciousness were associated with lower odds of hospital admission.ConclusionsThis is the first study to identify clinical and situational factors associated with hospital admission among older patients receiving home call medical services at home or in the NH who were transported by EMS. Among these patients, hospital admission was associated with suspected infection; cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and digestive diseases; oxygen therapy in the ED; and reduced consciousness. These findings may help guide triage and decision-making in emergency care for this vulnerable population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-025-01414-6.

Introduction

The aging global population has led to a substantial increase in emergency department (ED) visits and hospital admissions among older adults, who often present with complex medical conditions and social backgrounds that complicate acute care. Several studies have indicated that geriatric populations have an increased likelihood of requiring hospitalization and a greater risk of adverse outcomes, especially those experiencing cognitive impairment [1–3]. Moreover, the management of older ED patients warrants greater resources and more specialized skills [4].

Importantly, not all ED visits or hospitalizations among older individuals are necessary; a substantial proportion of these cases, particularly among individuals receiving home medical care, involve conditions that can be managed in non-hospital settings. These home medical care patients are a unique subgroup owing to their distinct baseline conditions, triage needs, or decision structures. Prevention of otherwise avoidable hospital transfers is key to minimizing healthcare costs and limiting or preventing this vulnerable population from functional decline, iatrogenic complications, and psychological stress [5, 6]. For example, patients with ambulatory care-sensitive conditions can benefit from improved symptom monitoring, advance care planning (ACP), medication adherence, and coordinated primary care interventions [5, 6]. Understanding the factors truly associated with hospital admission in this population is crucial for improving triage decisions, guiding emergency medical services (EMS) transport protocols, and allocating healthcare resources effectively. In this context, the framing of “avoidable” or “unnecessary” transfers is clinically meaningful. “Avoidable transfers” are defined as ED visits for issues that could have been effectively managed without compromising patient safety in a nursing home (NH) or primary care setting [7]. For NH residents, avoidable ED transfers are common and associated with poor outcomes. Up to 40% of all ED visits originating from NHs can be prevented through better on-site care, including making better decisions [7–9].

Transfer decisions are influenced by numerous factors, including not only acute illness severity and facility resource availability but also non-medical aspects, such as staff perceptions, breakdowns in communication, concerns over legal liability, and the personal preferences of families or caregivers [10, 11]. This multifactorial decision-making process underscores the need for clear clinical indicators and structured protocols to support optimal triage. Several interventions have been implemented to reduce unnecessary hospital transfers, including geriatric hotlines, telehealth services, and improvements to primary care networks [12–15].

Additionally, several predictors of ED transfer have been identified, including male sex, polypharmacy, multiple comorbidities, and rural residence [8]. However, there is limited evidence related to the potential clinical factors in older patients receiving home care that lead to hospital admissions following EMS transport [16]. In this retrospective observational study, we aimed to identify clinical and situational factors associated with hospital admission among older patients receiving home medical care who were transported to the ED by EMS.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Juntendo University Nerima Hospital, a 490-bed community hospital in Nerima City, Tokyo, Japan. Verbal and written informed consent were deemed non-essential. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Juntendo University Nerima Hospital (approval number: E24-0452) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

Data were collected by reviewing electronic medical records at our hospital. All older patients (age ≥ 75 years) who received house call medical services at home or in NH settings and were transferred to our hospital between January 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024, were enrolled in the study. The inclusion criteria were all transferred cases, while the exclusion criteria were cases lacking any parameters or cardiopulmonary arrest on arrival (CPAOA). After enrollment, the following information was collected: age, sex, type of residence (home or NH), comorbidities (cognitive impairment or need for home oxygen therapy), social background factors (polypharmacy (taking more than five drugs), support/nursing care levels, living alone, or prior consultation with doctor (in-person examination or telehealth consultation), obtaining ACP, person who decided upon transport (doctor, nurse, or patient/family), diagnosis (infection, bone fracture, cardiovascular disease, endocrine metabolic disease, cerebrovascular disease, respiratory disease, digestive disease, minor trauma, or syncope), and outcome (admission or non-admission). The following information was also collected upon ED arrival: Glasgow coma scale (GCS) score; vital signs, including heart rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, body temperature (°C), respiratory rate, and saturation of percutaneous oxygen; use of oxygen therapy in the ED; and National Early Warning Score (NEWS). Support/nursing care levels were defined by the government, based on an assessment of care requirements. The long-term care insurance (LTCI) system provides society-wide support for individuals in need of long-term care due to factors such as advanced age [17]. In Japan, all adults aged ≥ 40 years are required to enroll in the LTCI system, which aims to provide mutual assistance: society as a whole covers the expenses of long-term care, reducing individuals’ financial burdens due to long-term care costs [17]. The support levels are defined as follows: support care level 1 as 1, support care level 2 as 2, nursing care level 1 as 3, nursing care level 2 as 4, nursing care level 3 as 5, nursing care level 4 as 6, and nursing care level 5 as 7 [17]. Within this system, care levels indicate a greater dependency upon external care than support levels do, and within each category, increased care needs are represented by higher numbers [17]. We also defined the diagnosis as suspected by the ED physician’s assessment. Similarly, minor trauma, such as contusions and lacerations, did not warrant hospitalization. Additionally, “Tokyo Rule” (TR), a policy enacted by the Tokyo Metropolitan Government on August 1, 2009, for difficult emergency transport cases, mandates that designated hospitals accept unanticipated ambulances [18]. TR criteria are as follows: (1) the patient’s clinical condition was generally moderate or mild; and (2) ≥ 5 medical institutions refused ambulance transfer requests, or 20 min had elapsed since the selection of medical institutions by an emergency medical technician. If the case met criterion (1) and criterion (2), it was regarded as a TR case. Our institution proactively accepts patients transferred by ambulance, including those under TR.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome measure was hospital admission. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations. Normal distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. To identify factors predicting admission, we performed univariate analysis to compare these parameters between the admission and non-admission groups. For comparison, potential confounding variables were entered into the univariate model, and factors significant in the univariate analysis were then included in the multivariate analysis to adjust for confounding factors. In the univariate analysis, Student’s t-test was used for continuous variables, and the χ² or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify the predictors of admission in the multivariate analysis. All data analyses were performed using the STATA® software package (version 10; STATA Corp LP). A statistical two-tailed significance level of 0.05 was applied, and all hypothesis testing was also two-tailed.

Results

During the research period, 3,940 older patients were transferred to our hospital, of whom 853 were receiving home medical care. Of these, 115 (13.5%) were excluded due to missing parameters (n = 61) or CPAOA (n = 54), with 738 ultimately included in the study. A table comparing excluded patients without CPAOA with those included is provided in the appendix. Of the 738 included patients, 503 were admitted to the hospital and 235 were not. Details of patient enrollment are shown in Fig. 1. Baseline, demographic, clinical characteristics, and outcome data were obtained for all patients.

In the univariate analysis, the following factors were significantly associated with hospital admission: age; ACP; decision to transfer made by healthcare professionals or nurses; NEWS; GCS; heart rate; body temperature; pulse oximetry; use of oxygen therapy in the ED; and major diagnostic categories, including infectious disease, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, digestive disease, minor trauma, or syncope (Table 1). However, excluding 115 patients owing to missing parameters may have introduced a risk bias. Therefore, sensitivity analysis was used to assess the validity of the study results. Sensitivity analyses were performed using non-parametric tests, namely the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. In the non-parametric analyses, age, NEWS, GCS score, heart rate, body temperature, and respiratory rate were associated with admission (Table 2). Comparing the results from the Student’s t-test and the Mann-Whitney U test, pulse oximetry showed inconsistent findings. Variables that were significant in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression. For continuous variables, those found to be significant by both Student’s t-test and the Mann-Whitney U test were included. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified the GCS score, oxygen therapy in the ED, and major diagnosis categories (infectious disease, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, digestive disease, minor trauma, and syncope) as independent predictors of admission (Table 3).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to identify clinical and situational factors associated with hospital admission among older patients receiving home call medical services, at home or in NH settings, who were transported to the ED by EMS. In this observational study of 738 older patients who were transferred to our hospital by EMS, we identified key factors associated with hospital admission.

Our five major findings are summarized as follows. First, patients suspected of having cerebrovascular disease by ED physicians were more likely to be admitted, reflecting the clinical urgency associated with conditions, such as stroke, which typically warrant immediate evaluation, intervention, and inpatient management.

Second, patients who required oxygen therapy upon ED arrival were more likely to be admitted, underscoring the impact of respiratory compromise, such as hypoxemia, on the decision to hospitalize even in the absence of other severe physiological abnormalities.

Third, patients diagnosed with infectious, cardiovascular, or digestive diseases were markedly more likely to be hospitalized. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that infections and cardiovascular conditions are among the most common reasons for hospitalization in older adults receiving home care [19]. Among older or comorbid individuals, even minor infections may necessitate close monitoring and antibiotic therapy in a hospital setting. The persistence of digestive diseases as a significant predictor of admission reflects the high clinical burden of gastrointestinal bleeding, obstruction in older adults, which often require immediate inpatient management and monitoring [20].

Fourth, minor trauma and syncope were associated with a substantially lower likelihood of hospitalization. While the findings for minor trauma were expected, the result for syncope may appear counterintuitive. One potential explanation is that many cases involve transient, non-life-threatening syncope, such as vasovagal syncope, which does not require inpatient care.

Fifth, commonly used clinical scoring systems, including the NEWS and vital signs (heart rate, body temperature, and respiratory rate) were not statistically significant predictors of hospital admission in our model. Although these indicators reflect a patient’s clinical status, they may not directly inform the admission decision as effectively as the diagnostic categories or presenting symptoms. NEWS is a widely used tool for assessing the acute severity of patients’ conditions, originally developed to identify clinical deterioration and ensure timely medical interventions [21, 22]. Although these scores can be effective in identifying acute changes, they were neither designed nor validated as standalone tools for determining the need for hospitalization, particularly in older patients with complex chronic conditions. Consistent with this, we suspect NEWS did not independently predict admission in our final model. This may be because NEWS primarily captures acute physiological instability but not the broader clinical or social context that strongly influence hospitalization decisions in home-care populations.

Furthermore, older patients often present with atypical symptoms and blunted vital sign responses, which can reduce the discriminative ability of such scoring systems for predicting admission necessity.

Furthermore, our findings suggest that clinical judgments by healthcare professionals regarding the decision to transfer patients do not always accurately predict the need for hospitalization. Given that healthcare professionals’ decisions to request EMS were not consistently associated with actual hospital admissions, there is potential to improve triage accuracy.

This study has several limitations. First, this study had a single-center design limited to older patients residing in a specific medical area of Tokyo, Japan; therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to other regions or different healthcare systems. Second, 13.5% of the patients were excluded owing to missing data or CPAOA. This is because our ED physicians failed to obtain information from the ED. Accordingly, exclusion bias may have occurred, and our results may not accurately reflect reality. This is reflected in our methodology. Third, this study excluded cases managed entirely within the home care system without EMS transport, potentially introducing selection bias, as only transported cases were analyzed. Consequently, the results cannot comprehensively address “unnecessary” admissions. Fourth, data on the length of hospitalization for admitted patients were not collected, preventing multivariate analysis to identify contributing factors. Fifth, social determinants (e.g., caregiver availability and facility resources) were not analyzed, although they influenced admission decisions. Sixth, we did not gather data on medical expenses, which would have been useful in assessing potential cost reductions.

Conclusion

This study is the first to identify clinical and situational factors associated with hospital admission among older patients receiving home call medical services or residing in NH who were transported by EMS. Patients requiring oxygen therapy and those suspected of having infectious, cerebrovascular, or cardiovascular diseases at the initial evaluation should be considered for proactive hospital admission owing to the severity of their conditions. While these findings do not establish causality or directly identify unnecessary admissions, they may inform the development of prehospital triage frameworks to help identify potentially unnecessary transfers among home-care patients.
