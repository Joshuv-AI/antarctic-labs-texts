Comparative evaluation of 2D and 3D YouTube videos as supplementary educational resources for vitreoretinal surgery training
Authors: Zubeyir Yozgat, Halime Kubra Sahin, Gizem Ilayda Dumlupinar, Mehmet Cem Sabaner
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Ophthalmologists and ophthalmology residents (ORs) are increasingly turning to the internet for medical information, underscoring the significant role that YouTube videos, particularly three-dimensional (3D) ones, play in lifelong learning. This study aimed to compare the content and quality of 3D YouTube videos with two-dimensional (2D) videos as supplementary educational tools for vitreoretinal surgery. Data collected included video length (minutes), time elapsed since upload (days), number of views, likes, dislikes, vitreoretinal surgery type, and visualization system. Video popularity and interaction were calculated using the video power index, interaction index, and viewing rate. Two senior ophthalmologists (SOs) and 2 ORs evaluated the videos using the DISCERN, Global Quality Score, and usefulness scoring systems. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intra-class correlation coefficient. A total of 392 videos were screened, with 67 2D and 67 3D videos deemed appropriate for inclusion. While 2D videos had significantly more views, likes, interaction index, and viewing rate than 3D videos (P < .001 for all), 3D videos were rated higher by ORs across all scoring systems (P < .05 for all). Inter-rater reliability was confirmed to be good, with the lowest intra-class correlation coefficient being 0.796 for SOs (95% confidence interval: 0.668–0.875) and 0.814 for ORs (95% confidence interval: 0.698–0.886). In conclusion, side-by-side 3D YouTube videos offer a valuable supplementary educational tool, enhancing depth perception and enabling both SOs and ORs to better understand the complexities of ocular surgeries, particularly vitreoretinal procedures. These videos can also be used to observe new procedures and refresh previously acquired knowledge of past surgeries.

1. Introduction

E-learning has gained significant traction in medical education.[1,2] Although there were early concerns about its effectiveness compared to traditional in-person learning, studies have demonstrated that e-learning outcomes are generally on par with face-to-face instruction.[3] With the growing interest in supplementary educational resources among both senior ophthalmologists (SOs) and ophthalmology residents (ORs), there is increasing focus on how medical professionals acquire knowledge through online platforms, such as surgery videos, and evaluate the impact of their digital training on patient care.

This facilitates better knowledge acquisition and reduces the time needed to learn various ophthalmic surgical procedures and skills.[4–6] In this context, platforms like YouTube, Vimeo, EyeTube, and Eyesurgeryvideos, which already feature numerous ophthalmological surgery videos, highlight the growing importance of educational video sharing in ophthalmology.[7] However, the eye’s complex three-dimensional (3D) structure poses challenges. Understanding ocular anatomy is difficult in the standard two-dimensional (2D) formats used by most sources, making it harder to conceptualize surgical procedures abstractly. The lack of a third dimension in these videos can result in the omission of critical surgical or educational steps.[8–10]

3D videos incorporate innovative technology that enhances the understanding of sensitive and precise intraocular manipulations related to vitreoretinal (VR) surgery. This technology has already proven beneficial in surgical practice,[9,10] and digital 3D models may also serve as a valuable additional educational tool.[8] Consequently, YouTube videos have gained increasing attention as supplementary educational and informational tools, particularly in the academic world. As the most popular video-sharing platform globally, YouTube provides an open, accessible, and integrated digital space where users can upload, share, view, and comment on videos. Additionally, YouTube-based studies, both basic and clinical, have been conducted for various purposes, such as evaluating educational quality and accuracy.[4,6,11,12] To our knowledge, no study has yet examined the content and quality of YouTube videos to compare the effectiveness of “side-by-side” 3D VR surgery videos with 2D videos as supplementary educational tools for ophthalmology healthcare professionals.

Hence, this study aimed to compare the content and quality of 3D YouTube videos with 2D videos as supplementary educational tools for VR surgery, targeting ophthalmologists and residents.

2. Methods

2.1. Design of the study

This cross-sectional, register-based study was designed, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Since this study analyzed publicly available YouTube videos without involving any patient data, no ethical approval was required. A YouTube search was conducted on June 3, 2024, using the keywords “vitreoretinal surgery,” “retinal surgery,” “PPV (pars plana vitrectomy),” and “vitrectomy” in the online search box at https://www.youtube.com, via the Mozilla Firefox browser (v126.0.1). Video selection was randomly assigned. The search was performed without requiring a user login, and the browser’s cache, cookies, and history were cleared beforehand. Standard search preferences were set to “sort by relevance.”

2.2. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The following criteria were used for inclusion: VR surgery, reliable content and relevance to VR surgery, and side-by-side 3D videos. Videos were excluded if they had any of the following features: animation videos, disabled likes, low resolution (<720p), or 3D formats other than side-by-side, such as Anaglyph Red/Cyan 3D or Top-and-Bottom 3D.

2.3. Data recording and calculations

The following parameters were recorded for both 3D and 2D videos: video length (minutes), time elapsed since upload (days), number of views, likes, dislikes, upload source (physician, medical company, or others), type of VR surgery (retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, proliferative diabetic retinopathy, epiretinal membrane or internal limiting membrane peeling, macular hole, vitreomacular traction, endophthalmitis, proliferative vitreoretinopathy, and silicone oil removal), and visualization system.

Video popularity was determined by the number of likes and views. Since YouTube no longer displays the number of dislikes, the video power index [number of likes/(number of likes + dislikes) × 100] could not be calculated. Viewer interaction was measured using the interaction index (II) [(number of likes − number of dislikes)/total number of views × 100]. For videos with zero likes, the II was considered zero. Due to the current YouTube data limitations, this index was calculated without factoring in dislikes. The viewing rate was determined by dividing the number of views by the number of days elapsed since the upload.

2.4. Video analyses

Videos were evaluated by 2 SOs (ZY and MCS), who are active VR surgery performers, and 2 ORs (GID and HKS) undergoing VR surgery training, using the DISCERN, Global Quality Score (GQS), and usefulness scoring systems. Evaluators were blinded to each other and to all other video aspects, including the number of likes, dislikes, and views.

Videos were viewed at the highest possible resolution from the source. 3D videos were watched binocularly on the same 3D 4K TV (LG, 50LB670V, LG Electronics Inc., Korea), using side-by-side 3D option on, with polarized 3D glasses (LG, AG-F310, LG Electronics Inc., Korea) (Fig. 1). 2D videos were also viewed on the same screen without 3D glasses and with the 3D feature turned off. All videos were watched while seated upright at a distance of 2.5 m from the screen.

The DISCERN, a 15-question scoring system where each item is rated on a scale from 1 to 5 to assess the reliability and objectivity of medical information related to surgery (Table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/MD/O151, which illustrates DISCERN scoring system). The DISCERN score ranges from 15 to 75 points, categorizing content as excellent (63–75 points), good (51–62 points), fair (39–50 points), poor (27–38 points), or very poor (15–26 points).

The GQS system allows users to evaluate a video’s content on a 5-point Likert scale (Table, Supplemental Digital Content 2, http://links.lww.com/MD/O152, which illustrates global quality scoring system). The GQS assesses video quality, streaming, and the usability of the information presented in YouTube videos, with ratings ranging from 1 to 5.

Usefulness scores were determined based on 6 criteria: definition, indication, contraindication, procedure, complications, and prognosis. Each content area was scored 0 to 1 point, with a maximum of 6 points possible. A score of 0 to 2 indicated poor video content with insufficient information about VR surgery, rendering it useless. A score of 3 to 4 indicated moderate video content that provided useful information but inadequately covered certain topics or surgical stages. A score of 5 to 6 indicated high-quality content that conveyed comprehensive and accurate surgical information.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software, version 22.0 (IBM SPSS, Chicago, IL). Descriptive statistics, including percentages, means ± standard deviation, or medians with interquartile range, were used to summarize baseline characteristics depending on data normality. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to analyze the distribution of parameters for normality. For nominal variables, the chi-square test was applied. The Mann–Whitney U test was employed to analyze the scoring system results for 2D and 3D videos evaluated by SOs and ORs. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). An ICC value >0.8 indicated good repeatability, while >0.9 indicated excellent repeatability. All analyses were performed at a 95% CI, with P values <.05 considered statistically significant.

3. Results

After applying the exclusion criteria, 67 3D and 67 2D videos out of a total of 391 videos were selected for analysis (Fig. 2) (Table, Supplemental Digital Content 3, http://links.lww.com/MD/O153, which illustrates the list of 3D and 2D VR surgery videos used in the study). Of the 3D videos, 42 were uploaded by physicians, 22 by medical companies, and 3 by unknown sources. In comparison, 39 of the 2D videos were uploaded by physicians, 12 by medical companies, and 16 by unknown sources (P = .003). The majority of the 3D visualization systems in the videos were the Alcon NGENUITY 3D Visualization System (Alcon Laboratories, Fort Worth, TX) and the TrueVision 3D Visualization System (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), with 51 and 6 videos, respectively; 10 videos featured an unknown visualization system. While the visualization system was clearly visible in most 3D videos, it was not specified in the majority of 2D videos. Most 3D videos (n = 43) supported 4K resolution, whereas the majority of 2D videos (n = 50) supported 1080p resolution (P < .001).

4. Discussion

This study showed that 3D videos had a lower viewing rate and II compared to 2D videos, but they contained the best videos in terms of resolution. 3D VR surgery YouTube videos were found to be a higher quality and supplementary educational tool among ORs compared to 2D videos. In this comparison, no significant difference was found between 2D and 3D among SOs.

Medical students and healthcare professionals are increasingly turning to the internet for medical information, highlighting the critical role YouTube videos play in lifelong learning.[11] Multimedia resources for medical education have existed long before the rise of social media, with YouTube videos possibly evolving from the use of videotapes in the past. While these videos are now more abundant and easily accessible, monitoring their quality has become more challenging.[12–14]

The adoption of 3D technology in media has surged over the last decade.[15] In the medical domain, prior studies have demonstrated that 3D technology has already made its way into the operating room, yielding positive outcomes, especially for surgeons.[6,9,10,16] Alcon Laboratories, in collaboration with TrueVision 3D, developed a 3D Visualization System leveraging the latest advances in ophthalmic 3D technology. This system allows ophthalmologists to operate using a high-definition 3D display rather than a traditional microscope, offering enhanced depth perception that is unavailable on standard operating room displays.[10,16] In this study, most 3D visualization systems used in the videos were the Alcon NGENUITY 3D Visualization System (Alcon Laboratories) and the TrueVision 3D Visualization System (Leica). While the visualization system was visible in most 3D videos, it was not specified in the majority of 2D videos. Additionally, the majority of 3D videos (43/67) supported 4K resolution, whereas most 2D videos (50/67) were in 1080p resolution. These results suggest that 3D video technology could serve as a valuable supplementary educational tool for VR surgery procedures.

In a study by Chhaya et al,[9] which evaluated VR surgery video recordings using red-blue passive 3D glasses among medical students, the 3D group outperformed the 2D group on anatomy comprehension questions (P < .050). Similarly, in our current study, we observed comparable results with 3D YouTube videos, though we used side-by-side 3D and polarized glasses. Additionally, while Chhaya et al[9] focused solely on medical students, this study included evaluations from both SOs and ORs. On the other hand, Gobeka et al[6] demonstrated that 3D phacoemulsification surgery videos on YouTube, when used as supplementary educational tools, could enhance depth perception for ORs, and they found that 3D videos outperformed 2D videos in this group. In line with Gobeka et al’s[6] findings, we observed similar results in our study with VR surgery videos. Moreover, Zheng et al[17] demonstrated that 3D video surgical training tutorials can improve the speed of specific steps in cataract surgery for novice ophthalmology trainees compared to 2D. Similarly, Wang et al[18] reported that 3D visualization system–assisted cataract surgery showed a significant advantage in medical education compared to traditional microscopic cataract surgery: Likewise, Aravind et al[19] demonstrated that 3D enhances depth perception in cataract surgery, scleral buckling, and intravitreal injection, serving as an effective tool for skill transfer compared to 2D. While 3D video technology enhances surgical precision and offers SOs and ORs greater access to medical and surgical knowledge, its role as a supplementary educational tool has not been extensively studied in VR surgery. Early literature on 3D technology in ophthalmic surgical education primarily focused on the effectiveness of 3D computer animations as a complement to traditional surgery videos.[20] Another study explored the potential use of digital 3D models as an additional educational resource.[8] Despite these findings, limited research has compared the effectiveness of 3D surgical videos with 2D videos as supplementary educational tools for VR surgery, particularly for ORs, and even SOs. According to the present study, 2D YouTube videos on VR surgery garnered significantly more views, likes, elapsed time since upload, II, and viewing rate than 3D videos. This disparity in views may be due to the specialized equipment required to view 3D content, such as imaging systems and special glasses, leading to a higher II for 3D videos.

The 3D video technology, particularly beneficial in ophthalmology, offers a more ergonomic approach to learning and performing surgery, with the ultimate goal of reducing surgeon fatigue while enhancing surgical learning, success rates, and precision.[21–24] In the current study, all scoring systems—including DISCERN, Usefulness Score, and GQS—demonstrated that 3D surgical videos received significantly higher scores than 2D videos among ORs. The high resolution and enhanced depth perception in 3D videos likely explain why these videos were rated higher. Utilizing 3D videos as a supplementary educational tool may enable both SOs and ORs to fully grasp the complexity and intricacies of ocular surgeries, particularly VR procedures. The improved depth perception offered by 3D videos can be especially valuable in understanding ocular anatomy during more delicate and complex surgeries like VR surgery. In relation to the research topic, 3D technology is emerging as a pioneering and game-changing tool in surgery. A growing body of evidence suggests that 3D surgery is just as safe and effective as traditional microscopic techniques. This indicates that 3D video technology will likely become widely utilized in the near future, not only as a supplementary educational tool for ORs but also for enhancing proficiency, surgical expertise, and performance among ophthalmologists.

The main strengths of this study are the use of 3 international standardized scoring systems (DISCERN, GQS, and usefulness) and the inclusion of both ORs and SOs as raters. To our knowledge, no previous studies have analyzed 2D and 3D YouTube VR surgery videos using this methodology. Further research is warranted to investigate the educational impact of high-quality 3D and virtual reality videos, particularly within ophthalmology residency programs, to better understand the learning outcomes associated with this emerging technology.

4.1. Limitations

While the current study addresses critical aspects of the utility of 3D versus 2D YouTube videos for supplementary educational purposes, it has certain limitations, including a relatively small number of videos, a limited number of raters, the inclusion of only side-by-side 3D YouTube videos, the evaluation of videos solely from the YouTube platform, and the assessment of only non-complicated VR surgeries. Additionally, sub-720p videos were excluded due to their extremely low image quality, as this could compromise accurate data analysis and interpretation. Given the scarcity of complete 3D VR surgery videos, edited or shortened videos containing only the relevant surgical procedures were also included in the study. Furthermore, since YouTube disabled the dislike count on December 13, 2021, the video power index could not be calculated and was omitted from the II calculations. Despite these limitations, it is known that Chhaya et al[9] conducted their study using red-blue 3D glasses. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comparative study of 2D and side-by-side 3D VR surgery YouTube videos viewed with polarized 3D glasses, demonstrating that 3D videos were more effective than 2D videos as supplementary educational tools for ORs.

5. Conclusion

Although YouTube VR surgery videos may not be suitable for beginners, they serve as valuable supplementary educational tools for reinforcing previously acquired surgical techniques and exploring new procedures. In the present study, ORs rated 3D VR surgery videos as more useful and of higher quality compared to 2D videos. However, SOs perceived no significant difference in benefit between 2D and 3D videos. As virtual reality technology advances, surgical procedures and operating room environments are expected to become more immersive and perceptible through enhanced visual experiences.
