The effect of port development on the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution
Authors: Xina Ji, Xingong Ding, Yalong Zhang, Chunlei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study, based on panel data from 49 coastal cities in China from 2007 to 2019, combines the Tapio decoupling model with econometric methods to innovatively explore the impact of port development on the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution. The results of the Tapio decoupling model indicate that, with the continuous advancement of the Chinese government’s pollution reduction policies, 89.8% of coastal cities had achieved decoupling between urban development and environmental pollution by 2019. The panel cointegration test results reveal a long-run cointegrated relationship between port development and the decoupling of urban development-hinterland pollution. The findings from FMOLS and DOLS estimation techniques demonstrate that port development has significantly promoted the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution in China’s coastal cities. A heterogeneity analysis based on geographic regions shows significant regional differences in the impact of port development on decoupling. In the southeastern and southern coastal cities, port development has significantly facilitated the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution. However, in the Bohai Rim coastal cities, the goal of achieving decoupling through port development has not yet been fully realized.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24754-6.

Introduction

Coastal cities in China occupy a crucial position in international supply chains and global economic competition, and they are also integral components of the Belt and Road Initiative1. As the core regions of China’s urban agglomerations and the highest form of urban development, the rapid expansion and high population density of coastal cities have significantly driven sustained economic growth, particularly in key cities such as Qingdao and Shanghai2. According to the China Statistical Yearbook 2020, the GDP of China’s coastal cities reached 30.57 trillion yuan in 2019, accounting for 31% of the national GDP. However, the economic prosperity of coastal cities has often come at the cost of environmental degradation. Over time, the extensive economic development model has intensified the impact of human activities on regional environments, resulting in severe damage to the natural and ecological systems in coastal areas3,4. Confronted with increasingly severe ecological challenges, local governments have gradually realized that environmental protection is key to achieving green development in coastal regions. In recent years, a central focus has been on how to effectively control pollutant emissions while promoting economic growth. The academic community has employed the concept of “decoupling” to quantify the relationship between economic growth and environmental pollution5. Consequently, local governments are actively promoting a shift from traditional extensive growth models to green development, striving to achieve a decoupling of urban development from environmental pollution6.

The role of ports in promoting the economies of coastal cities is directly reflected in their impact on hinterland economies. Coastal ports typically serve specific cities, and these cities are heavily reliant on the transportation services provided by ports7. However, while achieving rapid development, coastal cities also face numerous challenges, such as environmental pollution and ecological degradation. Nevertheless, ports themselves possess the potential to transform these contradictions into drivers of sustainable urban development8. As hubs connecting cities to the ocean, ports not only propel the process of economic globalization but also become a driving force of regional prosperity and “incubator” for innovative services by aggregating diverse economic activities8. As critical distribution centers for industrial and agricultural products in trade systems, ports facilitate the flow of goods and inter-regional connectivity through intermodal water and land transportation infrastructure and international trade logistics, playing a pivotal role in the global logistics network9,10. Furthermore, ports drive regional economic growth and industrial chain development by fostering industrial integration and service agglomeration, thereby enhancing social and economic benefits11. Ports not only contribute to the prosperity of coastal cities but also strengthen regional cohesion, reduce economic disparities, and promote economic integration12,13. Despite the multifaceted and significant roles that ports play in the development of coastal cities, their impact on the green development of these cities remains a topic worthy of in-depth exploration.

Existing literature, based on empirical models rooted in decoupling theory, has explored the relationship between urban development and environmental pollution in coastal cities14–17. Additionally, a few studies have investigated factors influencing the decoupling of urban development and environmental pollution. For instance, Wu et al.18 analyzed the nonlinear relationship between industrial upgrading and economic-environmental decoupling using a panel threshold regression model. Zhong et al.19 employed a PSTR model to examine the impact of the digital economy on carbon emission decoupling. Huang et al.5 emphasized the critical role of financial development in decoupling economic growth and carbon emissions, while Ying et al.20 studied the effects of ICT agglomeration on decoupling. However, the impact of port development on the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution in coastal cities has not been sufficiently examined. Therefore, investigating the influence of port development on the decoupling status of coastal cities not only provides a theoretical foundation for promoting port development and achieving green economic transformation in these cities but also offers empirical evidence for policymaking aimed at ensuring the sustainable development of coastal cities and their hinterland environments.

This study is theoretically grounded in the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis and aims to explore the mechanism through which port development influences the relationship between urban development and hinterland pollution. The EKC hypothesis has been widely recognized as one of the key theoretical tools for analyzing the relationship between environmental degradation and economic development21,22. Applying this framework to the transportation sector not only facilitates a systematic understanding of the specific environmental impacts of transport activities but also provides a theoretical basis for assessing the feasibility of achieving sustainable development through policy interventions. Moreover, the EKC framework enables comparative analysis of the heterogeneous environmental effects of different regions and modes of transportation under the context of economic growth23,24.

The EKC hypothesis is primarily based on three economic effects: the scale effect, the composition effect, and the technique effect. The scale effect refers to the increase in output and energy consumption driven by economic growth, particularly when ecological impacts and resource depletion are not adequately considered. In contrast, the composition and technique effects emerge as economic development advances, as public and corporate awareness of environmental issues rises. This growing awareness prompts reductions in pollution emissions through industrial restructuring and technological innovation, thereby mitigating or even improving environmental conditions25. In the transportation sector, the EKC hypothesis remains applicable. On one hand, the scale effect is reflected in governments’ increased investment in fossil fuel-based transportation to enhance transport capacity and stimulate economic activity, which often exacerbates pollution. On the other hand, the composition effect involves a shift from industry-led to service-oriented economies, with transportation activities transitioning toward greener, low-carbon modes. This shift can be facilitated by policy-driven promotion of environmentally friendly transport systems. Finally, the technique effect highlights government investment and dissemination of green transportation technologies, which plays a critical role in achieving emissions reduction targets23,25.

Although numerous studies have explored the EKC hypothesis in the transportation sector22,25–29, there remains a lack of systematic research that incorporates ports, which is a critical transportation hub, into the EKC theoretical framework. To address this research gap, this study, for the first time, treats ports as an independent unit of analysis to examine their dynamic impact on the decoupling process between urban economic development and environmental pollution, while further investigating the regional heterogeneity of these effects.

This study aims to innovatively explore the impact of port development on the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution. Specifically, it focuses on addressing the following two questions: (1) Does port development influence the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution? (2) If an influence exists, does it exhibit heterogeneity? To answer these questions, this study will employ the Tapio decoupling model and econometric methods, leveraging panel data from 49 coastal cities in China from 2007 to 2019 to conduct an in-depth analysis of the impact of port development on the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution.

This study presents two main innovations: First, it explores the role and mechanisms of port development in the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution. This perspective differs from previous research, which primarily focuses on macro-level factors such as industrial upgrading, the digital economy, and financial development, by emphasizing the unique pathways of ports as key driving factors. Second, the study divides 49 coastal cities into three distinct coastal city clusters and conducts a regional comparative analysis to reveal the varying impacts of different levels of port development on the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section “Literature review” reviews the relevant literature; Section “Methodology and data” outlines the research methodology and describes the data; Section “Results and discussion” presents the results and provides a discussion; finally, Section “Conclusions and policy recommendations” concludes the study and offers corresponding policy recommendations.

Literature review

Environmental pollution has consistently been a critical issue for the sustainable development of human society, prompting extensive academic research on the decoupling relationship between urban development and pollution. These studies are conducted at various levels, encompassing analyses of individual cities, urban agglomerations, and national scopes. At the level of individual cities, Pan et al.30 found that Wuhan, a major city in central China, is transitioning from weak decoupling (WD) state to strong decoupling (SD) state between economic growth and carbon emissions. Similarly, Yang et al.31 discovered that Chengdu has gradually shifted its decoupling status toward SD state, achieving an ideal state. Other studies focus on cities within a single province to reveal regional disparities. Research by32 on 13 prefecture-level cities in Jiangsu Province indicates that cities in southern and northern Jiangsu exhibit WD state, while those in central Jiangsu display weak negative decoupling (WND) state. At the urban agglomeration level, studies often focus on the decoupling performance of regional economic clusters. For example, Luo et al.33 found that after 2015, the dependence of the Central Plains Urban Agglomeration on resources and the environment significantly decreased. Similarly, Li et al.34 analyzed the decoupling status of 85 cities along the Yangtze River Economic Belt. They found that by 2009, 61% of these cities had achieved decoupling and were progressively moving toward stronger decoupling, while 25% achieved decoupling after 2009. Wang et al.35 examined the Pearl River Delta urban agglomeration and revealed that its decoupling status between economic development and carbon emissions fluctuated, predominantly reflecting WD state. In the Yangtze River Middle Reaches urban agglomeration, Hu et al.36 observed that most cities demonstrated better decoupling states after 2010, though high-energy-consuming industrial cities showed slight reversals. Wei et al.37 further analyzed the stability and sustainability of decoupling across 19 urban agglomerations in China from 2007 to 2020. They found that decoupling states were generally unstable and fluctuating, with most urban agglomerations remaining in a non-decoupled state. On a national scale, scholars have examined broader trends in urban decoupling. For instance, Wei et al.38 studied 168 cities across seven key regions in China and found that atmospheric pollution and socioeconomic growth achieved an ideal state of SD state by 2018. However, the sustainability and stability of overall decoupling remained poor. Kang et al.39 analyzed data from 264 prefecture-level cities and noted that an increasing number of cities achieved SD state over time. In contrast, Li et al.40 and Shan et al.41 analyzed 296 and 294 cities, respectively, revealing significant regional disparities in decoupling. Most cities either achieved WD state or had not yet fully decoupled.

In recent years, research on the decoupling between economic growth and pollution has become relatively mature, but studies focusing on the driving factors behind this decoupling relationship remain limited. Wu et al.18 calculated the elasticity values of economic-environmental decoupling using panel data from Chinese prefecture-level cities. Subsequently, employing a panel threshold regression model with various air quality indicators as threshold variables, they conducted an in-depth analysis of the nonlinear relationship between industrial structure upgrading and economic-environmental decoupling. The results indicate that industrial structure upgrading has a significant threshold effect on the elasticity of economic-environmental decoupling. Zhong et al.19 used a panel smooth transition regression model to empirically analyze the impact of the digital economy on carbon emissions decoupling in 30 provinces in China from 2010 to 2019. The findings indicated that the digital economy promotes carbon emissions decoupling, but its promoting effect gradually weakens with the digital economy developing. Huang et al.5 emphasized the significant role of finance in achieving decoupling between economic development and carbon emissions. The results showed that financial development effectively promoted decoupling in the Europe and Central Asia region. Ying et al.20 used panel data from 257 cities in China from 2003 to 2017, the study examined the short-run and long-run effects of ICT agglomeration on decoupling. The empirical results indicated that ICT agglomeration negatively affects short-run decoupling trends but positively influences long-run decoupling pathways.

While ports can significantly drive regional economic growth, they may also become major sources of environmental pollution. Some studies have raised critical concerns in this regard. For example, Sharma42 pointed out that port-related transportation activities can lead to severe water pollution, particularly from heavy metal contaminants, which pose long-term threats to ecosystems and human health due to their high toxicity and persistence. In addition, pollutants such as oil residues and waste, frequently discharged during routine port operations, are not necessarily due to major accidents—can cause latent, cumulative damage to the marine environment43. Other studies have shown that the combustion of marine fuels emits conventional air pollutants and greenhouse gases, contributing to acid rain and smog, thereby exacerbating health risks and climate change, and creating compound environmental pressures44. Notably, compared to some other countries, China has in recent years adopted more proactive policy measures to promote the green transformation of ports. In particular, the release of the Action Plan for Port Pollution Prevention and Control in 2015 marked a significant policy initiative aimed at fostering green port development. This action plan includes measures such as improving environmental standards, promoting the use of shore power and LNG fuels, establishing emission control zones for ships, strengthening pollutant treatment and monitoring, and enhancing emergency response capabilities. These efforts have effectively mitigated the negative environmental externalities associated with port operations.

The EKC hypothesis has been widely regarded as one of the key theoretical tools for analyzing the relationship between environmental pollution and economic development21,22. Applying this theory to the transportation sector not only facilitates a systematic understanding of the specific environmental impacts of transportation activities but also provides a theoretical basis for evaluating the feasibility of achieving sustainable development through policy intervention. Moreover, the EKC hypothesis allows for comparative analysis of the heterogeneous environmental impacts of different regions and transportation modes within the context of economic growth23,24.

The existing literature has extensively explored the EKC hypothesis in the transportation sector. Some studies focus on a single mode of transport, such as road transport26,29,45 or railways28, to test the validity of the transportation-related EKC. In contrast, most research incorporates multiple transportation modes in assessing the presence of an EKC. For example, Hussain et al.46 and Chi et al.47 examine road, rail, and air transport; Erdogan et al.21 analyze the EKC using data from the aviation and rail sectors; Peng et al.23 emphasize the regional variation and complexity of China’s transportation EKC, aiming to test the EKC hypothesis for road and rail transport in China. Additionally, Pata et al.25 were the first to include four different transportation modes (road, maritime, rail, and air) into their analysis of EU countries, investigating the link between energy consumption and environmental pollution across modes, while considering the interplay between EKC hypothesis and sector-specific literature.

However, no existing study has incorporated ports, which is a crucial transportation infrastructure, into the EKC analytical framework to explore their dynamic influence on the decoupling between urban development and environmental pollution at different stages of port development. Compared with other modes of transport, ports significantly contribute to urban economic growth but also generate considerable energy consumption and pollutant emissions through port construction and cargo handling activities. From the perspective of EKC hypothesis, the impact pathway of port development helps to elucidate how cities transition from “scale-driven pollution” to “technology-driven decoupling.” Therefore, within the EKC framework, it is necessary to treat ports as an independent analytical unit for in-depth investigation.

Based on existing research, although some scholars have explored various factors influencing the decoupling of urban development and environmental pollution, such as industrial upgrading, the digital economy, and financial development at the macro level, there has been no systematic analysis from the perspective of port development. As integral components of coastal cities, ports play multiple critical roles, yet their impact on urban green and sustainable development remains underexplored. To address this gap, this paper takes port development as the analytical entry point and innovatively investigates its influence on the decoupling of urban economic growth and hinterland pollution in coastal cities. The study further categorizes 49 coastal cities into three distinct urban clusters to examine the heterogeneity of decoupling effects under varying levels of port development. The contributions of this paper are twofold: first, it is the first to systematically reveal the mechanisms through which port development influences the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution, thereby enriching the current understanding of driving factors; second, by introducing a regional comparative perspective, it highlights the differentiated impacts of port development levels on decoupling outcomes, offering theoretical support for local governments in formulating targeted and differentiated green development policies.

Based on the above research objectives and analytical framework, this paper proposes the following two hypotheses:

H1

Higher port cargo throughput correlates with reduced decoupling elasticity (i.e., stronger urban development-pollution decoupling).

H2

The decoupling effect of port development is stronger in southeastern/southern coastal cities than in Bohai Rim cities due to differences in industrial structure and policy enforcement.

Methodology and data

Tapio decoupling model

“Decoupling” refers to the weakening or severing of connections between two or more interrelated systems. This concept is used to describe changes in the relationship between economic activities and environmental degradation48. The widely applied decoupling models currently include the OECD model and the Tapio model. Given the limitations of the OECD decoupling model, such as its susceptibility to temporal factors and the relatively broad classification of decoupling types, Tapio introduced improvements. By integrating relative and absolute quantities and redefining the decoupling elasticity based on the ratio of growth rates, the Tapio decoupling model generates more stable results that are not influenced by changes in statistical dimensions48–50. Tapio’s classification criteria consist of three main categories: negative decoupling, decoupling, and coupling. Based on these categories, threshold values for the decoupling elasticity are set at 0, 0.8, and 1.2, which are further divided into eight specific decoupling types according to the characteristics of growth rates. This classification method is considered an effective tool for analyzing decoupling phenomena16. Econometric model and estimation

This study covers 49 coastal cities in China, utilizing annual data from 2007 to 2019, with the aim of investigating the impact of port development on the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution in China’s coastal cities. To achieve this research objective, the Tapio decoupling model is employed to calculate the decoupling elasticity of urban development and hinterland pollution, which serves as the dependent variable in this study. Port development is treated as an independent variable, while population size, per capita GDP, and industrial structure are included as control variables. Accordingly, the econometric model adopted in this study is as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{TAP}_{i,t}=\alpha\:+{\beta\:}_{1}{PORT}_{i,t}+{\beta\:}_{2}{POP}_{i,t}+{\beta\:}_{3}{PGDP}_{i,t}+{\beta\:}_{4}{IND}_{i,t}+{\epsilon\:}_{i,t}$$\end{document}

Within this model framework, the subscript \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i$$\end{document} represents coastal cities in China, while the subscript \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t$$\end{document} denotes the year. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document} represents the constant term or interception. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:$$\end{document} denotes the coefficients associated with the variables. Specifically, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:TAP$$\end{document} represents the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution in coastal cities, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:PORT$$\end{document} represents the level of port development in coastal cities, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:POP$$\end{document} denotes the population size of coastal cities, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:PGDP$$\end{document} refers to per capita GDP, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:IND$$\end{document} corresponds to the industrial structure.

In this study, we employ FMOLS and DOLS estimation techniques to assess the long-run relationships among variables. OLS may lead to distorted conclusions in parameter estimation due to spurious regression issues. FMOLS, an improved version of OLS, effectively addresses estimation bias caused by endogeneity of the dependent variable and autocorrelation of the disturbance term by utilizing a semi-parametric estimation technique to resolve correlation issues. This significantly enhances the reliability of the estimates53,55,56. As a regression method specifically designed for cointegrated systems, FMOLS is widely applied in time series modeling and demonstrates high reliability and robustness in handling endogeneity problems, particularly in small sample contexts57.

Another estimation technique, DOLS, is an alternative single-equation estimation method with the primary advantage of effectively accommodating the mixed integration properties of different variables within a cointegration framework. Moreover, the cointegration vectors generated by the DOLS estimation technique exhibit efficiency in an asymptotic sense, further enhancing the accuracy of the estimates58. The results obtained from estimating the model parameters using FMOLS and DOLS are largely consistent, confirming the congruence of the two methods and further validating the reliability and robustness of the model59.

The Tapio decoupling model is a classical tool for assessing the relationship between economic activity and environmental pollution. Its core lies in revealing the “elasticity” between the two, thereby identifying states of decoupling or coupling48,50,54. FMOLS and DOLS are well-established econometric methods used to examine the long-run cointegration relationships among variables in non-stationary panel data60,61. In recent years, some studies have begun to integrate the Tapio decoupling model with FMOLS/DOLS estimation techniques to explore the underlying mechanisms influencing the decoupling of economic development and environmental pollution5,53. This combined approach has demonstrated both strong theoretical foundations and empirical feasibility.

This study is the first to apply the Tapio decoupling model in conjunction with FMOLS/DOLS estimation techniques to the port sector, systematically evaluating the long-run mechanism through which port development influences the decoupling between urban development and hinterland pollution. This methodological design not only extends existing research but also offers a new analytical approach for understanding the relationship between port development and economic–environmental decoupling, demonstrating both methodological innovation and exploratory value.

Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) is a widely used non-parametric method for evaluating port efficiency62–64. However, traditional DEA models typically focus on static efficiency within a single period, making it difficult to capture the dynamic evolution of efficiency over time65–67. Although some studies have incorporated the Malmquist index to enhance the dynamic analytical capacity of DEA68–70, the core focus remains on efficiency assessment itself, offering limited insight into the long-term mechanisms through which port development influences the dynamic decoupling relationship between urban growth and hinterland pollution. Moreover, DEA models generally treat pollutants as “undesirable outputs,” emphasizing resource allocation and performance optimization at the operational level of ports71,72, while paying relatively little attention to the macro-level interactions between economic development and environmental pollution.

Therefore, the combination of the Tapio decoupling model with the FMOLS/DOLS estimation techniques can more effectively reveal the dynamic decoupling mechanism and its long-run effects, making it more suitable for analyzing the evolving relationship between port development and the decoupling of economic growth from pollution. Accordingly, given the research objectives, the Tapio-FMOLS/DOLS approach demonstrates greater adaptability and explanatory power compared to the DEA method, providing more targeted empirical evidence to inform policymaking.

Sample and data

Using the Tapio decoupling model, the analysis reveals differences in the decoupling states reflected by the decoupling elasticity under conditions of economic growth and economic recession20. The analysis of the sample data indicates that most cities were in a phase of economic growth during most of the study period. Therefore, to ensure greater accuracy and validity of the model’s estimation results, this study excluded four cities in an economic recession phase (out of a total of 53 coastal cities in China). Consequently, the final analysis sample covers panel data from 49 coastal port cities in China over the period from 2007 to 2019.

The city-level PM2.5 emissions data used to calculate the decoupling value were obtained from the Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group (https://sites.wustl.edu/acag/datasets/surface-pm2-5/#V6.GL.02). This dataset integrates advancements in chemical transport models, satellite observation technologies, and ground-based monitoring data, offering extensive spatial coverage, long-run stability, high precision, and exceptionally high spatial resolution (0.01°) 75. This study employs city-level PM2.5 data, which allows for a more accurate identification of the heterogeneity in the relationship between port development and the decoupling of urban development from hinterland pollution. Compared to province-level data, this approach provides a more comprehensive reflection of regional disparities and spatial precision in the port-driven decoupling process, thereby enhancing the explanatory power of the findings and the relevance of the policy implications. To mitigate the interference of inflation on the data, GDP figures for different years were adjusted for price changes and uniformly converted into real GDP based on the year 2000.

Ports, serving as transportation hubs connecting maritime and inland waterways, leverage intermodal transport facilities and goods distribution functions to ensure the safe passage of vessels while significantly contributing to urban economies through industrial integration and service agglomeration74. Port cargo throughput, defined as the total volume of goods handled by a port within a given statistical period, is a core indicator for measuring the production scale and operational capacity of a port. Its level is directly tied to a port’s international competitiveness and status75. As the most fundamental metric of port and terminal production, throughput is widely used in port-related research and production efficiency evaluations76. Given its importance, port cargo throughput has been broadly recognized as a key performance indicator for assessing port productivity77. Therefore, this study uses port cargo throughput to measure the level of port development in coastal cities. The data are sourced from the Ministry of Transport of the People’s Republic of China (MOT). Due to data availability constraints regarding port green transition, this study primarily uses cargo throughput as a proxy for port development. Future research could introduce a green port index or unit emission efficiency indicators to more comprehensively capture the sustainable development level of ports.

This study incorporates three control variables: population size, per capita GDP, and industrial structure. The selection of these indicators is based on the IPAT equation, representing population, affluence, and technological factors, which are widely used in existing research53,78,79. The data were sourced from the China City Statistical Yearbook (CCSY). Descriptions of the variables are provided in Table 2. To avoid spurious regression and eliminate heteroscedasticity, logarithmic transformations were applied to the variables port development, population size, and per capita GDP. Results and discussion

Description of the decoupling

This study employs the Tapio decoupling model to calculate the decoupling elasticity of urban development and hinterland pollution for 49 coastal cities in China from 2007 to 2019. Based on the decoupling states shown in Fig. 1, the cities were categorized into four groups, as the study focuses solely on cities during periods of economic growth: SD state, WD state, EC state, and END state. The geographical distribution of decoupling states across the cities in 2007, 2011, 2015, and 2019 is illustrated in Fig. 2.

In 2007, the decoupling states between urban development and hinterland pollution in China’s coastal cities exhibited distinct characteristics. Among them, 38 cities demonstrated WD state, while 11 cities achieved SD state. Overall, cities in a state of WD accounted for as much as 78%, indicating that the economic development of most cities still relied to some extent on the consumption and degradation of the hinterland environment. However, the degree of dependence and environmental damage was relatively low. The Chinese government introduced a series of policy measures aimed at curbing the rapid growth of high-pollution and high-energy-consumption industries and projects, optimizing industrial and energy structures, and promoting the rapid development of high-tech industries. These policies significantly reduced the dependence of economic growth in coastal cities on the environment, aligning with the characteristics of WD state33.

By 2011, the decoupling states of coastal cities in China had undergone significant changes, expanding from the two types observed in 2007 (SD state and WD state) to four categories (SD state, WD state, EC state, and END state). Among these, WD state accounted for 43% of the total, END state for 35%, EC state for 18%, and SD state for only 4%. Specifically, cities such as Ningbo, Quanzhou, and Zhangzhou maintained their WD state from 2007 to 2011. In contrast, cities like Dalian, Qinhuangdao, and Binzhou shifted from SD state in 2007 to WD state in 2011, indicating that their economic growth continued to come at the expense of environmental pollution, an alarming trend. Moreover, coastal cities in Guangdong Province, Guangxi Province, and Hainan Province transitioned from WD state in 2007 to END state in 2011, reflecting a significant acceleration in environmental pollution compared to economic growth. This highlight increased environmental pressures and the growing negative impact of economic growth on the environment. The relatively high level of economic development in China’s coastal regions is largely driven by urbanization and industrialization. However, this process has also been accompanied by substantial energy consumption and pollutant emissions, leading to heightened challenges to the region’s environmental carrying capacity80.

By 2015, more than 80% of coastal cities had achieved SD state between port-city development and environmental pollution. To achieve this goal, the General Office of the State Council of China issued the 2014–2015 Energy Conservation, Emission Reduction, and Low-Carbon Development Action Plan in 2014. This plan proposed measures such as optimizing industrial structures, adjusting energy consumption patterns, advancing key energy conservation and emission reduction projects, and promoting technological innovation to reduce major pollutant emissions and address air pollution in key regions (The Central People’s Government of the People’s Republic of China, 2014).

By 2019, the proportion of coastal cities achieving decoupling state reached as high as 89.8%. As shown in Table A1 (Appendix), 36 cities were in a state of strong decoupling, and 8 cities were in a state of weak decoupling. This result indicates that the vast majority of cities have achieved decoupling between economic development and environmental pollution. Since 2007, the Chinese government has steadily strengthened the formulation and implementation of emission reduction policies, covering critical areas such as energy conservation, emission reduction, and clean energy development81. In 2013 and 2018, the government introduced two key action plans: the Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan and the Three-Year Action Plan for Winning the Blue-Sky Defense Battle. These plans established industry-wide implementation pathways for comprehensive air pollution management, targeting reductions in major air pollutants and a decrease in the frequency of heavily polluted weather73. Through years of sustained efforts and continuous optimization, these policies have gradually yielded significant and stable decoupling outcomes.

Figure 3 illustrates the evolution of decoupling relationships across different geographic regions. Between 2007 and 2019, the southern coastal cities experienced a gradual shift in decoupling status—from weak decoupling to expansive negative decoupling and expansive coupling—ultimately achieving strong decoupling. In contrast, the southeastern coastal cities demonstrated a more positive trajectory, evolving from an initial predominance of weak decoupling to one increasingly dominated by strong decoupling. These two regions, with relatively strong economic foundations and faster industrial upgrading, transitioned earlier from traditional manufacturing to low-pollution industries such as modern services and high-tech sectors, thereby alleviating environmental pressure. In addition, policies such as stricter pollution emission regulations and the promotion of clean energy further facilitated the decoupling of economic growth from environmental pollution. As shown in Fig. 3, the decoupling status of southeastern and southern coastal cities is better than that of the Bohai Rim region, which is closely related to the former’s earlier industrial upgrading, higher proportion of service industries, and stricter enforcement of environmental policies.

In comparison, the Bohai Rim coastal cities exhibited a coexistence of weak and strong decoupling in 2007, which evolved into a predominance of weak decoupling by 2011, accompanied by instances of expansive negative decoupling and expansive coupling. Since then, the region has failed to achieve a sustained decoupling, remaining in a state of coexistence among multiple decoupling types. Dominated by traditional heavy and chemical industries and heavily reliant on port transportation, the region faces substantial environmental carrying pressure. Although various environmental governance and improvement measures have been implemented, constraints such as industrial path dependence and high transformation costs have hindered stable reductions in pollutant emissions. As a result, the region remains in a prolonged state characterized by expansive negative decoupling, expansive coupling, and other mixed decoupling patterns.

Pre-analysis testing results

The non-stationarity of macroeconomic time series may lead to spurious correlations between variables, making it essential to conduct stationarity tests before analyzing economic data59. In this study, various panel unit root tests, including LLC and CIPS, were employed to test unit roots in the variables. The results are presented in Table 4. The results of the panel unit root tests show that all the studied variables passed the stationarity tests, either at the level or after first-order differencing, confirming that all variables are stationary series. Therefore, it is necessary to further perform panel cointegration tests to verify the long-run equilibrium relationships between the variables.

This study employs four cointegration test methods to analyze the cointegration relationships in panel data: the Pedroni cointegration test82, the Kao cointegration test83, the Westerlund and Edgerton cointegration test84, and the Westerlund ECM cointegration test. The null hypothesis for all these tests is the absence of cointegration. The results of the cointegration tests for the variables are presented in Table 5. All the results of the cointegration tests show a significant rejection of the null hypothesis of no cointegration, indicating the presence of cointegration relationships among the panel data.

Regression results

FMOLS and DOLS estimation techniques were employed to estimate the long-run coefficients of the variables. The estimation results are presented in Table 6. According to the FMOLS and DOLS estimation results, a 1% increase in PORT corresponds to a 0.106% and 0.245% decrease in the TAP, respectively. This suggests that higher levels of port development are associated with lower decoupling elasticity, indicating that port development contributes to achieving the decoupling of urban development and hinterland pollution. The negative impact of port development on decoupling estimated using FMOLS and DOLS methods in this study is consistent in direction with the promoting effect of financial development in Huang et al.5, but the mechanisms differ: ports primarily promote decoupling through logistics optimization and industrial agglomeration rather than capital allocation. Compared with the digital economy research of Zhong et al.19, the port variables in this study exhibit more stable long-term effects.

The mechanisms through which port development promotes the decoupling of urban growth from hinterland pollution exhibit multifaceted characteristics and demonstrate distinctive features compared to other types of transportation infrastructure. First, as integrated transport hubs connecting maritime and inland waterways, ports are equipped with multimodal transport facilities and serve as centers for the aggregation and transshipment of land and waterborne cargo, ensuring the safe passage of vessels76. By integrating various transportation modes, ports enhance the efficiency of sea-land intermodal logistics while reducing dependence on traditional road transport. This, in turn, effectively curbs emissions of air pollutants such as NOx, PM2.5, CO, and SO2 from motor vehicle exhaust—pollutants that have been proven to adversely affect human health, agricultural productivity, and climate change85.

While rail transport can partially relieve the burden on road networks, it also contributes to environmental pollution through the leakage of lubricants, fuel combustion, and the use of preservatives on railway ties, which release polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and heavy metals into surrounding soil, air, and vegetation86. In addition, railway equipment such as transformers and capacitors may leak polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), pollutants that accumulate over time in ecosystems and pose serious risks to both biodiversity and human health87. Air transportation, on the other hand, generates substantial greenhouse gas emissions due to aircraft operations and airport activities, thereby accelerating global warming. Its high energy consumption and resource intensity also impose significant challenges to sustainable development88.

In contrast, port cities not only function as transport hubs but also act as growth poles with strong agglomeration effects, driving regional market integration and resource coordination, and thereby generating economic benefits and social welfare89. This process of market integration improves supply chain efficiency and reduces transport-related losses, which in turn alleviates environmental pressures. Furthermore, port development facilitates the reorganization of economic space and industrial agglomeration, strengthening urban fiscal capacity and enabling local governments to allocate more resources to the construction and management of environmental infrastructure90. Additionally, as strategic infrastructure prioritized by national policy, ports benefit from financial subsidies, foreign investment, and institutional reforms91, which support the adoption of advanced environmental technologies and green infrastructure. These advantages help control pollution at the source and effectively mitigate the diffusion of environmental degradation into hinterland areas.

For the control variables, when POP increases by 1%, TAP increases by 0.234% and 0.217%, respectively. This indicates that larger population sizes are associated with higher decoupling elasticity, implying that population growth hinders the decoupling of urban development from hinterland pollution. As the population expands, coastal cities face increasing demands for energy and resources, which further exacerbate environmental pressures and weaken the coordination between urban development and the hinterland environment. Similarly, a 1% increase in per capita GDP leads to TAP rising by 0.649% and 0.324%, respectively. This suggests that the growth of per capita GDP also hampers the decoupling of urban development from hinterland pollution. The rise in per capita GDP reflects an increase in residents’ income levels, which drives higher consumption demand. However, high levels of consumption are often accompanied by greater resource use and pollution emissions, thereby obstructing the process of decoupling urban development from hinterland pollution. In contrast, a 1% increase in IND results in a 1.963% decline in the decoupling elasticity. This demonstrates that industrial structure upgrading facilitates the decoupling of urban development from hinterland pollution. Industrial structure upgrading signifies that port cities are placing greater emphasis on introducing innovative technologies and transitioning to modern industries. This reduces pollutant emissions during production processes, decreases the proportion of high-pollution industries, and contributes to achieving an effective decoupling of urban development from pollution emissions.

Granger causality test results

Panel estimation techniques are limited in fully revealing the causal relationships and their directions between the variables under study92. Therefore, this study employs the Granger causality test to explore the causal relationship between port development and the decoupling of urban development-hinterland pollution. As shown in Table 7, there is a bidirectional causal relationship between PORT and TAP. As important trade and logistics hubs, ports not only drive economic growth in coastal cities but also generate socioeconomic benefits that enhance investments in pollution control and environmental technologies, thereby facilitating the decoupling of urban development from hinterland pollution93. However, despite their critical role in national economies and global trade, ports’ contributions to local economies are gradually diminishing due to increasing environmental concerns, heightened awareness of their negative impacts, and the relocation of port-related activities, which constrain further port development94.

Additionally, a bidirectional causal relationship is observed between POP and TAP. Population growth drives increased production and consumption activities, exacerbating urban environmental pollution and hindering the decoupling of urban development from environmental pollution95. Conversely, improvements in pollution control and urban environmental quality enhance the attractiveness of cities, which in turn fosters further population growth96. Similarly, the Granger causality test reveals bidirectional relationships between PGDP and TAP, as well as between IND and TAP. Growth in per capita GDP often relies on the development of high-pollution industries, which, while providing economic benefits to cities, inevitably harm the urban environment, thereby impeding the decoupling process97. However, achieving decoupling entails advancements in environmental and production technologies, which not only improve productivity and economic efficiency but also further drive growth in per capita GDP. Likewise, the bidirectional causal relationship between IND and TAP indicates that with the optimization and transformation of industrial structures, cities can achieve decoupling of development and pollution. Moreover, the very process of decoupling urban development from pollution serves as a critical driver for upgrading and transforming industrial structures98.

Mechanism analysis results

In the mechanism analysis section, we introduce environmental regulation as a moderating variable to examine the mechanism through which port development influences the decoupling between urban development and hinterland pollution. Compared with previous studies, this research is the first to combine the Tapio decoupling model with FMOLS/DOLS methods in the port sector and introduce environmental regulation as a moderating variable, revealing the mechanism through which port development promotes decoupling via green transformation. This addresses the gap in existing literature, which often focuses on macro-level factors while neglecting the specific role of transportation hubs. Following the approach of Chang et al.102 and Chen et al.103, we construct an index of environmental regulation intensity based on the frequency of environment-related keywords, such as environment, low carbon, environmental protection, air, green, PM2.5, chemical oxygen demand, carbon dioxide, PM10, ecology, pollutant discharge, emission reduction, pollution, eco-friendly, sulfur dioxide, and energy consumption—appearing in municipal government work reports. Specifically, we calculate the proportion of these keywords within each report and multiply it by the share of the secondary industry’s added value in the city’s regional GDP to derive the environmental regulation intensity index.

Further empirical analysis reveals that the interaction term between port development and environmental regulation in the model is negative and statistically significant (− 0.006, p < 0.01) in Table 8, indicating that in regions with stricter environmental regulation, the positive effect of port development on the decoupling of urban economic growth from hinterland pollution is more pronounced. This result highlights the critical moderating role of environmental regulation in promoting green development, consistent with the findings of Liu et al.104. Environmental regulation enhances the role of ports in promoting urban green transformation and environmental sustainability through multiple pathways, which can be understood from both technological and industrial perspectives. On the one hand, stricter environmental regulations effectively accelerate the green technological upgrades in ports, including the construction and application of shore power systems, the promotion of LNG-powered vessels, and the adoption of intelligent scheduling systems, thereby reducing energy consumption and pollutant emissions at the source. On the other hand, environmental regulation also facilitates the optimization of industrial structure. Under the pressure of rising environmental compliance costs, traditional high-pollution enterprises are either compelled to transition toward cleaner production or forced to exit the market. Consequently, the hinterland areas influenced by port development gradually shift toward the introduction of low-carbon green industries, significantly enhancing the positive impact of port development on the decoupling between economic growth and environmental pollution. For instance, the “Green Port” action plans implemented in recent years by Shanghai Port and Ningbo-Zhoushan Port have effectively reduced carbon emissions and PM2.5 emissions per unit of cargo throughput. These practical experiences demonstrate that, against the backdrop of increasingly stringent environmental regulations, port development not only drives urban green and low-carbon transformation but also significantly improves the level of decoupling between urban development and hinterland pollution through technological innovation and industrial upgrading.

Heterogeneity analysis results

This section explores the heterogeneity analysis based on geographic regions, with specific regional divisions outlined in Table 9. The results of the heterogeneity analysis are presented in Table 10. The findings indicate that the impact of port development on the decoupling between urban development and hinterland pollution varies significantly across different geographic regions. Specifically, port development exerts a significantly negative effect on the decoupling in both the southeastern and southern coastal cities, whereas no significant relationship is observed between port development and the decoupling index in the Bohai Rim coastal cities.

The underlying causes of these regional disparities can be understood from two perspectives: regional economic structures and policy contexts. On the one hand, southeastern and southern coastal regions, such as Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Guangzhou, exhibit higher levels of economic development and more optimized industrial structures, characterized by high-end manufacturing and modern service industries. These sectors tend to be less environmentally dependent and generate relatively low levels of pollution102. As a result, during the process of port development, these cities are better positioned to balance economic growth with environmental protection, thereby achieving a decoupling between urban development and hinterland pollution. In contrast, the Bohai Rim region, including cities like Tianjin and Qinhuangdao, has long relied on heavy industrial sectors such as steel, petrochemicals, and coal. These industries are not only highly dependent on port transportation but also produce significant pollution emissions. The lack of a significant decoupling effect from port development in the Bohai Rim region may stem from its heavy industry-dominated industrial structure and high-carbon energy dependency, which keep port logistics activities closely tied to high-pollution industries. Additionally, weaker enforcement of environmental policies and delayed adoption of green technologies further diminish the green spillover effects of ports. Consequently, while port development may stimulate economic growth in this region, it also exacerbates environmental pressures, making effective decoupling more difficult to achieve103,104.

On the other hand, significant differences also exist across regions in the formulation and implementation of environmental protection policies. Governments in the southeastern and southern coastal regions have made greater efforts to promote green development, with stricter enforcement of environmental regulations. These regions have also actively advanced the green transformation of ports by increasing investment in green technologies, promoting the use of clean energy, and strengthening the regulation of port pollutant emissions. Such measures have effectively mitigated the negative environmental impact of port operations on their hinterlands72. In contrast, although the Bohai Rim region has also begun promoting green transformation in recent years, the effectiveness of its environmental policies remains limited due to constraints such as industrial foundations, fiscal capacity, and policy enforcement105,106. As a result, despite ongoing port development, the ecological benefits it may bring have not been fully reflected in the decoupling relationship.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

This study uses panel data from 49 coastal cities in China from 2007 to 2019, combining the Tapio decoupling model and econometric methods to investigate the impact of port development on the decoupling of urban development from hinterland pollution. The results of the decoupling model indicate that the relationship between urban development and hinterland pollution in China’s coastal cities exhibited four decoupling states: SD state, WD state, EC state, and END state. However, by 2019, the proportion of coastal cities achieving decoupling state had reached as high as 89.8%. Since 2007, the Chinese government has continuously strengthened the formulation and implementation of pollution reduction policies. Although the initial effects of these policies were delayed, their sustained promotion over the years gradually yielded results, ultimately enabling coastal cities to achieve a stable state of decoupling between urban development and pollution.

The results of panel cointegration tests reveal a long-run cointegration relationship between port development and the decoupling of urban development from hinterland pollution. Further estimation of long-run coefficients using FMOLS and DOLS demonstrates that port development in China’s coastal cities contributes to the decoupling of urban development from hinterland pollution. Despite the severe ecological challenges faced during rapid development, port development shows potential to transform these conflicts into drivers of urban growth. Ports play a crucial role in the development of coastal cities, promoting their social and economic benefits. Heterogeneity analysis based on geographic regions reveals significant differences in the impact of port development on decoupling across different areas. In southeastern and southern coastal cities, port development significantly facilitates the decoupling of urban development from hinterland pollution, with these cities gradually moving away from traditional growth models that come at the expense of the environment. In contrast, in Bohai Rim coastal cities, the long-run reliance on heavy industry and the high dependence on port transportation have made it difficult to effectively control pollution emissions. Consequently, these cities have not yet achieved decoupling between urban development and hinterland pollution through port development.

The development of ports in the Bohai Rim region has not significantly contributed to the decoupling of urban development from hinterland pollution, indicating the absence of an effective policy framework that coordinates economic growth with environmental protection. In contrast, the southeastern and southern coastal regions have demonstrated a positive interaction between port development and the decoupling of urban economies from environmental degradation, offering valuable experiences from which the Bohai Rim region may draw lessons. Based on the specific characteristics of the Bohai Rim, this study proposes the following three policy recommendations: First, accelerate industrial green transformation and technological innovation. Given the region’s current dominance by heavy and chemical industries and its high pollution intensity, it is recommended to establish regional green manufacturing demonstration zones. These zones should promote green transition pathways such as equipment upgrades, energy-saving retrofits, and cleaner production in traditional heavy industries. Second, strengthen green port construction to reduce environmental externalities. Considering the high dependency of heavy and chemical industries on port logistics—while stimulating port economies, they also exacerbate environmental pressures—it is advisable to first implement shore power assessment mechanisms at key hub ports such as Tianjin and Qingdao. Providing electricity price subsidies for berthed vessels using shore power and promoting the replacement of diesel-powered vehicles and machinery in port areas with electric or hydrogen alternatives can further support these efforts. Lastly, enhance policy enforcement capacity and fiscal support. In response to weak policy implementation and limited fiscal support for environmental governance and green transformation in the region, it is essential to strengthen government oversight and performance evaluation mechanisms. Establishing real-time monitoring and data-sharing systems for pollutant emissions will improve transparency and policy effectiveness, ultimately advancing the dual goals of economic development and ecological protection.

This study has limitations. Using port cargo throughput as the core variable to measure port development may not fully capture the actual performance of ports in terms of green transformation and sustainable development. In the future, access to more comprehensive and standardized indicators of green port development would help to further reveal the multidimensional relationship between port development and urban sustainability.
