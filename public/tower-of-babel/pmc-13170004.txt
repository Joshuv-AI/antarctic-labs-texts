Distinct mutational landscape and clonal evolution of POLE-mutated endometrial cancer: geographic variation and insights into multiple classifier status
Authors: Yaolin Song, Xinsheng Gu, Peng Zhao, Xuchen Zhang, Huiqing Jia, Xiaoming Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPOLE-mutated (POLEmut) endometrial cancer (EC) represents a distinct molecular subtype with a favorable prognosis. However, current knowledge is derived largely from Western-centric datasets, and population-specific differences in mutational landscapes remain insufficiently explored. In addition, the biological relevance of variant type and the evolutionary dynamics of rare tumors with concurrent POLEmut and microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) are poorly understood.MethodsWe performed a comprehensive multi-cohort analysis by integrating the largest real-world dataset of POLEmut EC to date from a Chinese medical center (QDFY, n = 1,274) with public data from TCGA and CPTAC. Clinicopathological features, mutational spectra, and survival outcomes were compared across cohorts. In addition, multi-regional whole-exome sequencing was conducted to reconstruct the clonal architecture of a rare case harboring a concurrent POLE P286R mutation and MSI-H.ResultsIn the QDFY cohort, we identified a distinct mutational profile in which the prevalence of the V411L variant (36.8%) was comparable to that of the P286R hotspot (37.6%). Notably, the favorable prognosis of POLEmut EC appeared consistent across ethnic backgrounds and variant types in our preliminary short-term follow-up, even in the presence of high-grade histology or concurrent TP53 mutations, which were enriched for the R213 nonsense variant. Evolutionary analysis of the rare POLE P286R/MSI-H case demonstrated that POLE P286R was a truncal event, whereas MSI-H arose as a subclonal event, providing a compelling hypothesis for the observed intratumoral heterogeneity.ConclusionsThis study refines the molecular landscape of POLEmut EC by demonstrating a potentially high prevalence of the V411L variant in the Chinese population and showing that the favorable prognosis of POLEmut EC remains largely consistent across different ethnic backgrounds and specific hotspot mutations in this real-world cohort. These findings support universal POLE testing to avoid overtreatment and provide a hypothesis-generating basis for large-scale, multi-center studies with longer follow-up to validate the distinct mutational spectrum across diverse Chinese regions and confirm the long-term prognostic stability of cases with multiple molecular classifiers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08176-0.Keywords: Endometrial cancer, POLE mutation, V411L, Prognosis, Clonal evolution

Introduction

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is a common gynecological malignancy, with increasing incidence and mortality rates worldwide [1, 2]. In 2013, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) study redefined the classification of EC from traditional histomorphology into four molecular subtypes with distinct prognostic characteristics: POLE ultramutated, microsatellite instability hypermutated, copy number-low (CN-L), and copy number-high (CN-H) [3]. As a unique subtype, the POLE ultramutated subtype is characterized by an exceptionally high tumor mutation burden (TMB), distinct mutation patterns, intense tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), and a favorable prognosis, even in high-grade or advanced diseases [3–5]. Therefore, POLE-mutated (POLEmut) status, which is defined as pathogenic mutations within the exonuclease domain of the POLE gene that lead to an ultramutated phenotype, has been incorporated into the World Health Organization (WHO) classification and major clinical guidelines (NCCN, ESGO/ESTRO/ESP), guiding prognostic risk stratification, treatment de-escalation, and adjuvant therapy [6–9].

Despite these developments, the clinical application of POLEmut classification presents new challenges. Current insights have been primarily derived from Western population-centric open databases, in which the P286R variant represents the principal hotspot [3]. The mutational spectrum of Asian populations, particularly the occurrence and clinical significance of other hotspots such as V411L, remains poorly studied [10, 11]. In addition, the association between POLEmut and other molecular alterations, including microsatellite instability high (MSI-H), creates diagnostic difficulties. Although POLE mutations, particularly P286R, are generally mutually exclusive with MSI-H, isolated co-occurrence cases have been reported, and the evolutionary dynamics underlying these “multiple-classifier” tumors remain unclear [12–14].

To address these issues, we integrated a large real-world institutional cohort from China (QDFY, n = 1,274) with public data from TCGA and CPTAC and conducted a comprehensive analysis. This study aims to characterize the mutational landscape of POLEmut EC in the Chinese population, with a specific focus on the high prevalence and clinical phenotype of the V411L variant. Additionally, through multi-regional whole-exome sequencing (WES) of a representative case, we delineate the evolutionary dynamics underlying the rare co-occurrence of the POLE P286R mutation and MSI-H, providing novel insights into the intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) of this subtype.

Materials and methods

Study design and cohort

This study included three cohorts: the QDFY cohort (n = 1,274), the TCGA-UCEC cohort (n = 529) [3, 15], and the CPTAC-UCEC cohort (n = 103) [16]. The overall study design and patient selection process are shown in Fig. 1. In the clinical practice at QDFY, targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) for molecular classification was routinely recommended for all newly diagnosed EC patients to guide their adjuvant therapy and prognostic risk stratification, regardless of tumor grade or stage. Therefore, this cohort represents an unselected, real world population.

The overall study design and patient selection process

In the QDFY cohort, 133 patients with POLEmut were identified among 1,274 consecutive EC cases that underwent NGS at the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University between January 2021 and October 2025. For genetic analyses, a complete POLEmut cohort (n = 189) was constructed, comprising 133 cases from QDFY, 49 cases from the TCGA-UCEC dataset, and 7 cases from the CPTAC-UCEC dataset. For clinicopathological and survival analyses, 17 cases in the QDFY cohort were excluded because of external consultations (n = 15) and insufficient pathological information (n = 2, biopsy tissue only). For the CPTAC-UCEC cohort, a total of 153 sequencing datasets were retrieved. To ensure the accuracy of the patient-level analysis, we identified 103 unique tumor cases, while the remaining 50 datasets were designated as normal control samples (including adjacent normal, myometrium, and enriched normal tissues) and were excluded from the molecular classifier assessment.

All histopathological slides (H&E/WSI) were independently reviewed by two pathologists (S.Y.L. and X.X.M.) to confirm diagnosis, grade, and stage. This study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (QYFY WZLL 30566, date: 2025–02-01). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective study design.

DNA extraction and quality control

For all samples in the QDFY cohort, genomic DNA was extracted from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues using the Tiangen paraffin-embedded tissue DNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality and concentration were evaluated using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Samples with a tumor cell content of ≥20% and a total DNA amount of ≥ 100 ng passed quality control and were advanced to library preparation.

Genetic testing

Molecular classification of the entire QDFY cohort (n = 1,274) was performed using the BPTM assay (Amoy Diagnostics, Xiamen, China), an amplicon-based NGS panel designed to detect mutations in BRCA1/2, POLE, and TP53, and to assess MSI status. Libraries were generated using the HRR Gene Mutation Detection Kit (Amoy Diagnostics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was conducted on the NextSeq CN500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Bioinformatic analysis and variant calling were performed using the AmoyDx Data Analysis System v17.

WES and clonal evolution analysis

To investigate the intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) of a representative case (QDFY-ECPS-120), genomic DNA was extracted from two spatially distinct tumor regions (Block 1 and Block 7), and whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed at Geneplus (Shenzhen, China). Briefly, genomic DNA was fragmented to 300–500 bp using a Covaris S2 ultrasonicator (Covaris, Inc.), and indexed libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Target region capture was achieved by hybridization with biotinylated probes targeting the whole exome. Sequencing was conducted on the DNBSEQ-T7RS platform (MGI Tech, Beijing, China), achieving a mean sequencing depth of ≥500X.

Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19), and somatic variants were identified using a standard bioinformatics pipeline. To reconstruct clonal architecture, variant allele frequencies (VAFs) of somatic mutations were analyzed. Mutations detected in both blocks with high cancer cell fraction (CCF) were defined as truncal (clonal) events, whereas mutations restricted to a single region or with lower CCF were classified as branch (subclonal) events.

Data acquisition for public cohorts

For the TCGA-UCEC and CPTAC-UCEC cohorts, images were obtained from the TCGA website (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and the Cancer Imaging Archive (https://pathdb.cancerimagingarchive.net/), respectively. Clinical data, including histological type, molecular subtype, and follow-up information, were extracted from the cBioPortal website (https://www.cbioportal.org). Missing clinical information was supplemented by downloading original pathology reports from cBioPortal. A total of 529 patient records were retrieved from TCGA, of which 22 lacked molecular subtyping information, and 49 were classified as POLEmut. In the CPTAC-UCEC cohort (n = 103), molecular subtyping information was available for 101 cases, with 2 cases excluded due to incomplete data, and 7 were identified as POLEmut.

Molecular classification and data analysis

In the QDFY cohort, pathogenic POLE mutations were defined strictly according to the scoring system established by León-Castillo et al., including P286R, V411L, S297F, S459F, A456P, F367S, M444K, L424I, M295R, P436R, and D368K [17]. In the TCGA and CPTAC cohorts, in addition to these variants, R705W, L424V, and F367C were also classified as POLEmut. Molecular classification followed the 5th edition of WHO algorithm, categorizing EC into four groups: POLEmut, MMRd, NSMP, and p53abn [6, 9]. For cases with multiple classifiers, categorization was strictly performed according to the hierarchical structure of the WHO diagnostic algorithm, where a pathogenic POLE exonuclease domain mutation is recognized as the dominant feature. Therefore, tumors harboring such mutations were classified as the POLEmut subgroup, regardless of any concurrent MMRd or p53abn status.

For TP53 mutation analysis, to ensure accurate assignment to the p53abn molecular subtype under the WHO classification workflow, only variants with potential clinical relevance were included. All detected TP53 variants underwent a strict central review process and were annotated using multiple databases, including ClinVar, COSMIC, OncoKB and the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) TP53 database. Variants classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic, as well as variants located at established hotspot codons, were defined as p53abn (TP53-mutated) status and included in the primary prognostic and correlational analyses of the multiple molecular classifiers. Since the QDFY cohort primarily utilized tumor-only sequencing, germline polymorphisms were strictly filtered out using large population databases (e.g., gnomAD, 1000 Genomes, and dbSNP). Furthermore, the interference from unconfirmed germline variants or clonal hematopoiesis was minimized by integrating variant allele frequencies (VAFs) with the establised somatic mutational signatures, such as the characteristics C > T transitions driven by POLE mutations.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and interpretation

FFPE tissue samples were sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Antibodies against MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6 were purchased from ZSGB-BIO (Beijing, China). Loss of MMR protein expression was defined as the complete absence of nuclear staining in tumor cells, with positive staining results of internal control cells (e.g., stromal cells or lymphocytes). Furthermore, according to the established pathological consensus, “subclonal (heterogeneous) loss” was strictly defined as the complete absence of nuclear expression in an discrete and clearly demarcated area of the tumor, adjacent to regions with retained intact expression. To completely exclude pre-analytical or fixation artifacts, intact positive staining of internal control cells within the deficient tumor areas was strictly required.

Follow-up

Patients were followed up through telephone interviews and review of electronic medical records. The follow-up period began on the date of pathological diagnosis and ended on the date of death from any cause. For the QDFY cohort, follow-up was censored on October 31, 2025. Follow-up data for the TCGA-UCEC cohort were obtained from the cBioPortal website. Survival outcomes included overall survival (OS), defined as the interval from initial diagnosis to death from any cause, and disease-free survival (DFS), defined as the interval from surgery to disease recurrence or death from any cause.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Categorical variables are presented as counts (n) and percentages (%), and comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using the Student’s t-test for two groups or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for three or more groups. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test.

Percentages were calculated based on the number of cases with available data for each variable. For comparative analysis between the P286R and V411L subgroups, a patient with concurrent pathogenic POLE P286R and L424I mutations was assigned to the P286R group, based on the established driver role of the P286R alteration.

Results

Clinical characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the clinicopathological analysis cohort (n = 172) are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 56 years (range, 33–78 years). A total of 115 cases (66.9%) were recorded with FIGO grade 1–2, and 57 cases (33.1%) with grade 3. Deep myometrial invasion (DMI, >50%) was observed in 40 cases (40/171, 23.4%), lymph node metastasis in 19 cases (19/164, 11.6%), and lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI) in 38 cases (38/150, 25.3%). To prevent classification bias, all cross-cohort statistical comparisons among the QDFY, TCGA and CPTAC datasets were strictly conducted according to the FIGO 2009 criteria. Based on this system, most patients presented with early-stage disease (FIGO stage I–II, 137/172, 85.6%). Notably, when the QDFY cohort was independently reclassified using the updated 2023 FIGO staging system, 24 patients (24/116, 20.7%) were downstaged from stage II to stage IAmPOLEmut because of their POLEmut status, which increased the proportion of FIGO stage I cases in the QDFY cohort from 69.0% to 89.7%.

We further compared clinicopathological features across the three data sources. Although no significant differences were observed in age, tumor type, multiple-classifier status, or recurrence (p > 0.05), significant heterogeneity was detected in prognostic risk factors. Compared with the TCGA/CPTAC cohorts, patients in the QDFY cohort more frequently presented with low-grade tumors (79.3% vs. 34.7%; p < 0.001) and early-stage disease (90.5% vs. 70.3%; p = 0.009). Additionally, the QDFY cohort showed significantly lower rates of DMI (p = 0.002), lymph node metastasis (p = 0.018), and LVSI (p = 0.008). These findings demonstrate significant clinicopathological differences between the QDFY real-world cohort and the public datasets, confirming heterogeneity among cohorts from different sources.

Morphological spectrum of POLEmut tumors

Histologically, the cohort predominantly comprised ECs (95.9%, Table 1). Microscopic examination revealed characteristic morphological features frequently associated with POLEmut tumors. Most tumors exhibited high-grade nuclear atypia and prominent TILs or peritumoral lymphocytic aggregates (Fig. 2A, B). Diverse morphological changes were also observed, including mucinous differentiation (Fig. 2C), squamous metaplasia (Fig. 2D), tumor giant cells (Fig. 2E), and microcystic, elongated, and fragmented (MELF) patterns of myometrial invasion (Fig. 2F). Distinct mutational landscape in the QDFY cohort: high prevalence of V411L

The frequency of each pathogenic POLE mutation is shown in Table 2. In the genetic cohort (n = 189), P286R and V411L were the most frequent mutations, accounting for 39.2% (74/189) and 33.3% (63/189), respectively. In the QDFY cohort, the proportions of P286R and V411L mutations were almost identical, at 37.6% (50/133) and 36.8% (49/133), respectively. This contrasted with Western cohorts, including the combined TCGA/CPTAC dataset, in which P286R was significantly more frequent than V411L (42.9% [24/56] vs. 25.0% [14/56]). To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that in a large Chinese cohort, the mutation rate of V411L is comparable to that of P286R. These results suggest potential ethnic or geographic differences in the mutational spectrum of POLEmut EC.

Landscape of multiple molecular classifiers

Among the 189 POLEmut cases, 23.8% (45/189) exhibited multiple molecular classifiers (Table 4). The most common overlap was POLEmut–p53abn (n = 32), followed by POLEmut-MMRd (n = 7) and triple-classifier tumors (POLEmut-MMRd-p53abn, n = 6). In this context, analysis of TP53 hotspot mutations revealed specific enrichment of the R213* nonsense mutation (20/58), which was predominantly associated with the POLE P286R variant (Table 2), suggesting a characteristic mutational pattern in these ultramutated tumors.

Intratumoral heterogeneity and branching evolution in a rare POLE P286R/MSI-H Co-occurring case

Typically, the POLE P286R mutation is mutually exclusive with MSI-H status [12, 13]. However, in the QDFY cohort, we identified a rare case (Case ID: QDFY-ECPS-120) harboring both a POLE P286R mutation and an MSI-H phenotype. This case provided seven spatially distinct tumor tissue blocks available for analysis, offering an opportunity to investigate intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH).

To examine ITH at the molecular level, WES was performed on two spatially distinct regions (Block 1 and Block 7). WES analysis of Block 1 confirmed the presence of the POLE P286R mutation and an MSI-H mutational signature, with an extremely high TMB (16,479 mutations). Notably, subclonal mutations were detected in MMR genes, including MSH2 (c.1287 G > T, p.Q429H; VAF 12.4%), PMS2 (c.251-25 G > A; VAF 7.38%), and MSH6 (c.560A > C, p.K187T; VAF 1.24%). Notably, IHC of Block 1 showed a subclonal loss pattern (heterogeneous expression) for all four MMR proteins (MLH1, PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6; Fig. 3A), with absent expression confined to approximately 20–50% of the tumor area. This heterogeneous protein expression corresponded to the low VAFs observed by WES, indicating that dMMR in this region is driven by a specific tumor subclone rather than the entire tumor population. To provide a clear mechanistic connection beyond spatial correlation, in silico functional profiling was performed. Both the MSH2 (p. Q429H) and MSH6 (p.K187T) missense variants were classified as “Deleterious” by MutationTaster 2025. These concurrent mutations constitute a severe double-hit that likely disrupts the structural core and chromatin-recruitment domains of the MutSα complex, completely abolishing the mismatch repair capability and directly driving the MSI-H phenotype restricted to this region.

In contrast, Block 7 was classified as MSS, although it harbored the same POLE P286R driver mutation and a high TMB (8,906 mutations). This region exhibited a high-frequency PMS2 splice-site mutation (c.251-25 G > A; VAF 34.25%) and an MSH6 variant (c.3739A > G, p.T1247A; VAF 2.66%). IHC demonstrated partial local loss of PMS2 expression, with MLH1, the heterodimer partner of PMS2, showing markedly reduced staining intensity. In contrast, MSH2 and MSH6 showed consistently strong staining (Fig. 3B). This distinct staining pattern indicates that the PMS2 splice variant might have impaired the stability of the MutLα (MLH1–PMS2) complex. To validate this hypothesis in the absence of viable RNA-seq data from archival FFPE tissues, we evaluated the PMS2 intronic variant using the deep-learning tool SpliceAI. The analysis revealed a moderate splice-gain effect (score: 0.20), suggesting a hypomorphic alteration rather than a complete transcript loss. This functional prediction aligns with our IHC observation of partial, weak MutLα expression pattern. Thus, preserved MutSα (MSH2–MSH6) expression and residual MLH1 function appear sufficient to maintain an MSS phenotype in this specific subclone.

Clonal evolution analysis based on WES data suggested that the POLE P286R mutation was a truncal (early) clonal event present in both regions, whereas MSI-H appeared to be a subclonal (later) event (Fig. 3C, D). In addition, the highly specific spatial concordance between distinct functional mutations and corresponding focal protein loss patterns strongly argues against random epigenetic silencing. These findings support a branching evolutionary model in which a primary POLEmut progenitor clone eventually differentiated into distinct subclones: one subclone (represented by Block 1) acquired secondary deleterious MSH2/MSH6 mutations leading to an MSI-H phenotype, whereas another spatially disassociated subclone (represented by Block 7) evolved into an independent lineage dominated by a hypomorphic PMS2 mutation while retaining an MSS phenotype. However, as this observation is based on a single patient, it serves as a compelling hypothesis rather than a universally generalizable mechanism, further investigation in large cohorts is necessary.

Clinical outcomes and prognostic impact of POLE mutations

Survival analysis was performed in 161 patients from the QDFY and TCGA cohorts. In the TCGA cohort (n = 49), the median follow-up was 46.4 months (range, 1.8–225.5 months); two patients experienced disease progression and two died, confirming the favorable prognosis of this subtype compared with other molecular groups. In the QDFY cohort (n = 112), the median follow-up was 21.0 months (range, 0.6–54.2 months), and two patients experienced recurrence. Despite the shorter follow-up duration, outcomes remained consistently favorable (Figure S2).

We further evaluated the prognostic impact of multiple molecular alterations (Fig. 4). While Kaplan–Meier analysis showed non-significant p values for OS and DFS between POLEmut cases and those with multiple molecular classifiers, it should be noted that these statistical comparisons are underpowered due to the small sizes of soecific groups (e.g., n = 6 for triple-classifier tumors). Furthermore, given the limited number of recurrence events and the relatively short follow-up period in our cohort, these findings should not be intepreted as definitive prognostic equivalency. Instead, our findings provide a preliminary observation of short-term outcomes for patients with multiple classifiers. Both larger cohorts and longer follow-up are essential to confirm whether POLEmut status functions as an independent predictor of favorable prognosis compared with traditional clinicopathological risk factors.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a high frequency of the V411L variant in a Chinese population, delineated branching evolution and intratumoral heterogeneity in a rare concurrent POLE P286R/MSI-H case, and confirmed POLEmut status as an independent prognostic biomarker.

When comparing clinicopathological features across the three cohorts, significant differences were observed in tumor grade, FIGO stage, DMI, lymph node metastasis, and LVSI. Notably, our real-world QDFY cohort contained a higher proportion of early-stage and low-grade tumors compared to the TCGA and CPTAC cohorts. These differences may be related to a combination of methodological and biological factors. Public datasets such as TCGA and CPTAC may inadvertently enrich for more advanced or aggressive cases due to specific tissue requirements of comprehensive multi-omics projects. Furthermore, population-specific genetic backgrounds and environmental factors may contribute to the observed geographic variation in the clinicopathological landscape of endometrial cancer.

Despite substantial heterogeneity in clinical characteristics, survival analysis demonstrated consistently excellent outcomes across cohorts, indicating that POLEmut is a more fundamental and robust predictor of prognosis than traditional pathological factors, consistent with previous reports [14, 18]. Regarding the multiple-classifier cases, our preliminary data showed no significant difference in short-term survival compared to single POLEmut cases. However, these results should be interpreted rigorously. Due to the small sample sizes of specific groups and limited overall recurrence events in our cohort, the statistical power is currently insufficient to conclusively determine long-term prognostic equivalence. Larger multi-center cohorts and extended follow-up are required to confirm these findings, especially for cases with concurrent TP53 mutations.

Regarding the mutational landscape, P286R is widely recognized as the most prevalent of the 11 established hotspot mutations, a finding confirmed in our analysis of Western cohorts (TCGA and CPTAC). This pattern is also consistent with two prior small-scale studies from China, which reported P286R as the predominant variant (5/17 vs. 2/17 for V411L and 5/10 vs. 1/10 for V411L, respectively) in EC [19, 20]. However, a key finding from our larger-scale QDFY cohort is the unexpectedly high prevalence of the V411L mutation, which was nearly equivalent to that of P286R (36.8% vs. 37.6%). This result indicates that the mutational spectrum of POLEmut EC is not globally uniform but may be influenced by potential ethnic or geographic heterogeneity. However, as this is a single-center study, we cannot exclude the possibility of a local founder effect or a specific regional genetic signature. Thus, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. While our results point towards significant geographic heterogeneity, further validation in large-scale, multi-center cohorts comprising various ethnic and geographic subgroups across China is necessary to establish a pan-Chinese POLE mutational landscape. Importantly, because tumors harboring V411L showed comparable clinicopathological features and preliminary short-term prognostic outcomes compared to those with P286R, it is critical that molecular testing panels, especially those utilized in Chinese cohorts, should include the V411L site to avoid potential false-negative results. In the future, the precise annotation of such pathogenic hotspot mutations and the integration of multi-cohort omics data will increasingly rely on advanced computational methods. For instance, the application of deep learning frameworks in variant annotation [21] and the development of predictive analysis models for complex cancer classification [22] represent the necessary methodological basis for further improving the molecular classification of EC.

POLE P286R mutation is mutually exclusive with MSI-H. Even in rare cases where MSI-H was detected in a tumor with the P286R mutation, these defects typically do not impair the overall function of MMR [12, 13]. While the mechanism remains unclear, one hypothesis suggests that the P286R mutation leads to an ultramutated phenotype that results in an exceptionally high TMB, which may create evolutionary pressure favoring the selection of tumor cells that acquire secondary mutations or epigenetic alterations to alleviate this pressure, rather than the development of MSI-H [23].

Our multi-region genomic analysis of the concurrent POLE P286R/MSI-H case (n = 1) was consistent with the evolutionary trajectory described by Haradhvala et al., in which POLE exonuclease domain mutations precede the development of dMMR [13]. Specifically, the POLE P286R mutation represented a truncal event present in both Block 1 and Block 7, whereas mutations in MMR genes (PMS2, MSH2, and MSH6) were subclonal and restricted to specific regions. These findings indicate that the hypermutated phenotype was driven primarily by the POLE pathogenic mutation, with dMMR arising later as a subclonal evolutionary branch. We hypothesize that this tumor avoided catastrophic genomic instability because the secondary MMR gene alterations were subclonal or hypomorphic (manifesting as variants of uncertain significance or partial protein loss), enabling tumor cells to maintain high immunogenicity without exceeding the threshold of viability.

From a clinical perspective, the distinct molecular profiles of Block 1 (MSI-H) and Block 7 (MSS) highlight the ITH that may be encountered during pathological diagnosis. In addition, the complex IHC expression pattern observed in Block 7, in which a PMS2 splice-site variant disrupted the MutLα complex (weak MLH1, loss of PMS2) while preserving the MutSα complex (MSH2/MSH6), demonstrates that MMR protein expression can be heterogeneous and that focal loss of MMR proteins does not necessarily result in an MSI-H phenotype. These observations underscore the importance of multi-regional sampling, NGS sequencing, and careful interpretation of IHC staining, particularly in high-grade EC with ambiguous morphological features. Beyond accurate diagnosis, defining precise molecular classifiers is critical to help clinicians avoid overtreatment and guide specific therapeutic interventions. As the field advances, exploring targeted therapeutic strategies and drug discovery against complex molecular pathways [24] will further enhance the clinical translation of these molecular insights.

Our study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, multi-center validation is required to confirm the generalizability of our findings. Second, since the real-world QDFY cohort relied on tumor-only NGS-testing, the rare presence of private germline variants or clonal hematopoiesis could not be entirely excluded. Moreover, comparing mutational frequencies between fundamentally different sequencing platforms introduces inherent technical biases. Thus, discrepancies in concurrent mutation frequencies across geographical cohorts may partially reflect the different detection limits among these populations, rather than purely ethnic or biological heterogeneity. Third, for the TCGA and CPTAC cohorts, histological re-evaluation was restricted to available digital H&E slides, which may not fully capture all morphological characteristics. Fourth, given the excellent prognosis of POLEmut EC, as well as the relatively short median follow-up of the QDFY cohort, our survival analyses regarding long-term overall survival and disease-free survival may be statistically premature. Finally, due to the rarity of specific POLE hotspot mutations and multiple-classifier phenotypes, our subgroup survival comparisons may be statistically underpowered to detect underlying clinicopathological or prognostic differences.

In summary, this study leveraged the largest real-world cohort to date to characterize the molecular landscape of POLEmut EC and identified a high prevalence of the V411L variant in the Chinese population. We demonstrated that the favorable prognosis of POLEmut EC represents a robust biological feature that is independent of ethnicity, variant type, and morphological pattern. In addition, analysis of the rare POLE P286R/MSI-H co-occurring case supports a “POLE-first” evolutionary model. Collectively, these findings suggest that POLE testing should be routinely implemented in clinical practice and should include the V411L site to prevent overtreatment, particularly in patients with high-grade histology or TP53 mutations.
