Identification and validation of cigarette smoking-related genes in predicting prostate cancer development through bioinformatic analysis and experiments
Authors: Duocheng Qian, Xin’an Wang, Tengfei Lv, Dujian Li, Xi Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The morbidity and mortality rates of prostate cancer (PCa) are high among elderly men worldwide. Several factors, such as heredity, obesity, and environment are associated with the occurrence of PCa. Cigarette smoking, which is also an important factor in the development of PCa, can lead to genetic alterations and consequently promote PCa development. However, the smoking-induced genetic alterations in PCa are unclear. This study aimed to identify the potential smoking-related genes associated with PCa development. The smoking-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) which included lots of PCa datasets. DEGs were subjected to protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis to identify the hub genes. The pathways in which these hub genes were enriched were identified. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset was used to examine the expression of smoking-related genes in PCa samples and estimate their value in predicting tumor progression and prognosis. In total, 110 smoking-related DEGs were got from GSE68135 dataset which included microarray data of PCa patients with smoking or not and 14 smoking-related key genes associated with PCa were identified from PPI network. The expression of the following seven key genes was altered in TCGA PCa patients: EWSR1, SRSF6, COL6A3, FBLN1, DCN, CYP2J2, and PLA2G2A. EWSR1, SRSF6, FBLN1, and CYP2J2 also influenced PCa progression. Additionally, EWSR1 influenced disease-free survival. In the logistic regression model, CYP2J2, which exhibited the highest risk scores, was identified as the risk gene for PCa. We also found one of the smoking-related genes: EWSR1 was truly upregulated in clinical PCa patients and influenced PCa cells invasion and proliferation. This study identified the function of smoking-related genes involved in the progression of PCa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01645-2.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) has the highest incidence among all types of cancer in men. In 2024, the morbidity rate of PCa in the United States of America was approximately 30%, indicating that PCa affected more than one in five elderly men [1]. PCa is the second most common cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. In China, the morbidity and mortality rates of PCa are rapidly increasing [2]. Various factors, such as heredity, race, obesity, and environment are risk factors for PCa occurrence [3, 4]. Cigarette smoking is also a risk factor for PCa [5].

Smoking is involved in the pathogenesis of almost all stages of PCa [5]. Thus, smoking can affect the occurrence, progression, prognosis, and drug response of PCa [6–10]. Previous studies have reported the pathological mechanism of cigarette smoking in PCa. Cigarette smoke is reported to mediate PCa pathogenesis by modulating gene expression. Exposure to cigarette smoke alters the levels of some genes associated with hormone metabolism or androgen receptors [11–13]. Thus, genetic alteration is a potential factor mediating the effect of smoking on PCa development. However, the specific gene alteration associated with smoking that leads to PCa development has not been identified.

With technology development, specific gentic alterations occurred in the process of PCa can be detected [14]. Further, using bioinformatic methods, researchers can be able to more systematicly identify the genetic alterations involved in the process of PCa occurrence. Lots of studies have used bioinformatic methods to find hub genes which many be important for PCa development. For example, a study had reported that the expression change of MSMB maybe important for obese people suffered PCa [3]. In addition, lots of genes play an important role in the procedure of benign prostate hyperplasia developing into PCa [15]. Further, a study also analyzed the function of cuproptosis, a new type of regulated cell death, related genes function in promoting PCa progression [16]. So, in this study, we tried to use bioinformatic methods to identify potential smoking-related genes associated with PCa development.

The microarray dataset (GSE68135) comprising the data of both smoking and non-smoking patients with PCa was retrieved. Differential analysis revealed 110 differently expressed genes (DEGs) between non-smoker and smoker groups. DEGs were subjected to protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis, which revealed 14 hub genes. Using Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, we found these hub genes mainly enriched in extracellular matrix organization and arachidonic acid metabolism. The expression of these hub genes was examined in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets. Seven genes (EWSR1, SRSF6, COL6A3, FBLN1, DCN, CYP2J2, and PLA2G2A) important for PCa occurrence were detected from PPI network. Finally, the role of these genes in PCa progression and prognosis was analyzed by TCGA data and we found one of the seven genes: EWSR1 was central for PCa progression. We further analyzed the expression and function of EWSR1 in clinical PCa samples and PCa cells. We found that EWSR1 was truly increased expression in clinical PCa samples and promoted PC-3 cells invision and proliferation. This study demonstrated that smoking-related genes are important for PCa development.

Materials and methods

Data source

Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) database is a public database comprising the microarray data of several disease samples [17]. Meanwhile, TCGA (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/) database is a large tumor public database comprising the sequencing and clinical data of various cancers [18]. Chinese Prostate Cancer Genome and Epigenome Atlas (CPGEA) (http://www.cpgea.com/) comprises the microarray data of Chinese patients with PCa [19]. The gene expression and clinical data were obtained from these databases.

Data handling

The microarray data of patients with PCa with or without smoking history were obtained from the GEO database based on the following search criteria: (1) availability of a clear definition of smoking and non-smoking patients with PCa. (2) Availability of the data in the GEO database. (3) Availability of at least three samples for each group of patients. This study retrieved the GSE68135 dataset that met the criteria. GSE68135 dataset included the microarray data of 20 PCa patients with smoking and 5 PCa patients without smoking which can help us finding the different expression genes (DEGs) in patients with smoking or not. Further, TCGA database included the clinical information and microarray data of 497 PCa patients and 52 normal control. In addition, CPGEA database included microarray data of 136 paired tissues of PCa patients. The primary data from the public databases were normalized using the R package ‘limma’ [20].

Survival and methylation analysis

Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA; http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), an online web tool, can be used to analyze the effects of genes on the survival status of patients depending on the information of PCa patients from TCGA database [21]. The disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) statuses were analyzed by GEPIA. Additionally, the methylation level of genes was determined using the online web tool UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) also depending on the methylation information of PCa patients from TCGA database [22].

Identification of DEGs, construction of PPI network and mutation analysis

The DEGs in the GSE68135 dataset were determined based on the following criteria: adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change (FC)| > 1. Next, these DEGs were represented using a volcano map, which was constructed using the R package “DESeq2” which can reflect the DEGs more directly [23]. A PPI network of the DEGs was constructed using the STRING online database (http://string-db.org/) and the raw data were downloaded from STRING [24]. Then the raw PPI data from STRING was reconstructed using Cytoscape software and the hub genes in the PPI network were identified using the plug-in: Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) [25]. In MCODE, a gene which marked as a clustered or seed gene was indicated as a hub gene. Then, using an online web tool bioinformatics (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/), we analyzed the mutation types of these hub genes when PCa happened depending on TCGA data [26].

GO and KEGG analysis

The pathways of hub genes enriched were detected by GO analysis and KEGG pathways [27]. The enrichment of hub genes in the following GO terms was determined: biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components. Additionally, the pathways in which hub genes were also enriched were determined using KEGG analysis. GO annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using Metascape (http://metascape.org/) [28]. The scatter plots were generated using the online web tool bioinformatics (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/) [26]. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Regression model

A logistic regression model was constructed using “forestplot” R software package which can reflect the risk that dysregulation of smoking-related genes in causing PCa [29]. A nomogram was constructed to display the results of the regression model using the R software which can reflect the results more directly. The risk hub genes for PCa development were determined using the logistic regression model.

Clinical specimen collection

PCa and para-cancerous samples were collected at Tongji Hospital, School of Medicine, Tongji University. The sample collection procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, School of Medicine, Tongji University (SBKT-2021-220). The samples were collected from the patients after informing them about the experiment and obtaining informed consent. In total, 8 patients who provided both tumor tissues and adjacent normal prostate tissues were included in the study. The subunit of each group was confirmed by professional pathologist. In addition, the clinical information of patients included in the study was shown in Supplemental Table 1.

Cell culture and transfection

PC-3 PCa cell line was purchased from the Chinese Academy of Science Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). The cell culture and transfection process was carried out as previous study described [30]. Before experiements were performed, cells used in the study were confirmed without mycoplasma. The EWSR1 knock down (shEWSR1) lentivirus was constructed by Youze Biotechnology Company (Guangzhou, China). The shEWSR1 sequence was got from the previous study [31].

Cell invasion and proliferation assay

After 48 h of transfection, we carried on the cell invasion experiment. The process of cell invasion and proliferation was done as previous study depicted [15]. Then, the cell number in each field was acquired by ImageJ.

Western blot

Western blot (WB) experiment was down as previously bewrited [32]. In addition, the primary antibodies against EWSR1 (1:1000, Catalog No. 133318) and β-actin (1:1000, Catalog No. ab8226) were purchased by abcam company (Abcam UK, Cambridge, UK).

Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis

The expression of EWSR1 in clinical specimens was examined using IHC analysis. Tumor samples were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin. The samples were sliced into 4 µm-thick sections and fixed. The sections were subjected to antigen retrieval and immunostaining as described previously [33]. Anti-EWSR1 (Catalog No. 133318, 1:500) antibody was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), while the second antibody (Catalog No. A0216, 1:1000) was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Company (Shanghai, China). The IHC score was determined independently by two professional pathologists.

Statistical analysis

Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments and are represented as mean ± standard deviation. Means between two groups were analyzed using the Student’s t-test, while those between more than two groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05. All statistical analysis in this study was performed using SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS Inc., USA). In addition, data from public databases were handled by R software (R version 4.0.3) with different packages.

Results

Identification of hub genes associated with smoking in PCa

The DEGs between smoking and non-smoking patients with PCa were identified using the GSE68135 dataset, which satisfied the criteria for data handling. Next, the DEGs between non-smoking and smoking patients with PCa were represented using a volcano map. In total, 110 DEGs were identified in the GSE68135 dataset (68 upregulated genes and 42 downregulated genes) (Fig. 1A). The hub genes among these DEGs were identified using a PPI network constructed using the online web tool STRING. MCODE, a plug-in of Cytoscape, was used to reconstruct the PPI network and identify the hub genes in the PPI network. Among the 110 DEGs, 14 hub genes were identified in the PPI network (Fig. 1B). Expression of smoking-related genes in patients with PCa

The differential expression of 14 smoking-related genes between non-cancerous prostate tissues and PCa tissues was examined using the microarray data downloaded from TCGA database. The following seven smoking-related genes were differentially expressed between non-cancerous and tumor tissues: EWSR1, SRSF6, COL6A3, FBLN1, DCN, CYP2J2, and PLA2G2A (Fig. 3A). As TCGA database mainly included the data from the Western population, the differential expression of 14 smoking-related genes in Chinese patients with PCa was examined using the CPGEA dataset. All previously identified smoking-related genes, except COL3A1 and CYP4F8, were differentially expressed between PCa and non-cancerous tissues (Fig. 3B). Then the mutation types of these 7 genes were also analyzed. We found that the most common mutation of these 7 genes in PCa is missense mutation which suffered in COL6A3, EWSR1, FBLN1, and DCN (Figure S1). In addition to modulating gene expression, cigarette smoking influences DNA methylation [34]. Thus, the methylation level of smoking-related genes in PCa was examined. The methylation level of DCN was not analyzed owing to the lack of data. The methylation level of other genes, except SRSF6, was altered in PCa (Figure S2). Smoking-related genes as risk factors for PCa

Next, the smoking-related genes that can serve as risk factors for PCa were identified. A logistic regression model was constructed to examine the expression of smoking-related genes in PCa. A nomogram was constructed to reflect the results of the logistic regression model. The nomogram revealed that SRSF6, DCN, and CYP2J2 were the genes with the highest risk scores for PCa (Fig. 4A). Expression of smoking-related genes in different tumor stages

Smoking is reported to affect tumor progression [7]. Thus, this study examined if these smoking-related genes can affect PCa progression. Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) staging is extensively used for the evaluation of malignant tumors [35]. Thus, the expression of smoking-related genes in different TNM stages of PCa was determined using TCGA datasets. The expression of smoking-related genes was examined only in the T and N stages of PCa as metastasis was not observed in most patients. EWSR1, SRSF6, FBLN1, and CYP2J2 influenced primary tumor progression (Fig. 5). However, the expression of these genes was not altered upon node metastasis (Figure S3). EWSR1 upregulated in clinical PCa samples and prmoted PCa cells proliferation and invasion

The expression patterns of smoking-related genes were examined in clinical PCa samples. As EWSR1 influenced the occurrence, development, and prognosis of PCa, it was hypothesized to play an important role in PCa development. The expression of EWSR1 in clinical PCa samples obtained from Tongji Hospital was examined. The protein expression of EWSR1 was upregulated in clinical PCa samples and the typical IHC images were shown (Fig. 7A, B). Then we tried to confirm whether EWSR1 can affect PCa cells function. So, we constructed EWSR1 knck down lentivirus and transfected it into PC-3 PCa cells. We found that the lentivirus can truly decrease the protein level of EWSR1 in PC-3 PCa cells (Fig. 7C). Then, we used CCK-8 experiments to find whether the cell proliferation ability would change when EWSR1 knock down. We found after EWSR1 knock down, the proliferation of PC-3 cells would decrease (Fig. 7D). Same results can also found in cell invasion ability. When EWSR1 decrease expression in PCa cells, the cell invasion would also decrease rapidly (Fig. 7E). Discussion

The incidence of smoking is decreasing worldwide although the beneficial effect has been minimal. In 121 countries, more than 350 million individuals are smokers with 67% being men [36]. Smoking is a risk factor for the occurrence of several cancers. Cigarette smoking delivers several carcinogens, including tobacco-specific nitrosamines, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and volatile organic compounds [37]. The correlation between smoking and lung cancer has been previously reported. In addition to lung cancer, cigarette smoking is associated with the occurrence of several carcinomas, including breast cancer, bladder cancer, and colorectal cancer [38–40]. Additionally, smoking is a risk factor for PCa.

Smoking can impact the occurrence, progression, survival, and drug response of PCa. One study reported that male smokers were associated with a higher risk of developing fatal PCa than male non-smokers [41]. Additionally, smoking can interfere with the beneficial effect of androgen deprivation therapy on PCa. Patients with a smoking history are associated with a decreased time of developing castration-resistant PCa [42]. Additionally, the outcomes of radiotherapy in smoking patients were worse than those in non-smoking patients [43]. Furthermore, smoking can significantly increase the risk of PCa metastasis [44]. Previous studies have demonstrated that smoking can also affect the survival time of patients with PCa. The survival time of smoking patients is shorter than that of non-smoking patients [8, 44, 45]. These findings indicate that smoking is a risk factor for PCa.

The mechanism mediating the effect of smoking on PCa development has been elucidated. Genetic alteration is one of the main factors mediating the effect of smoking on PCa development [5]. Smoking alters several metabolism-related genes. Previous studies have demonstrated the correlation between GSTM1 polymorphism and smoking. Smokers with a GSTM1 null phenotype are associated with an increased risk of developing PCa [11]. The cytochrome p450 (CYP) family enzymes are also involved in hormone metabolism, especially in estrogen and testosterone metabolism [46]. Previous studies have demonstrated that several members of the CYP family, such as CYP1A1, CYP2D6, and CYP2C19 are altered in smoking patients [11, 12, 47]. Additionally, the expression of some genes can serve as biomarkers for predicting the clinical outcome of patients with PCa [48]. Furthermore, smoking can affect CpG hypermethylation of multiple genes involved in PCa development [49]. These studies indicate that smoking-induced genetic alteration is an important etiological factor for PCa development.

This study aimed to examine the role of smoking-induced genetic alterations in PCa. Seven key smoking-related genes in PCa were identified and their functions were verified. These genes were involved in the occurrence, development, and prognosis of PCa. In addition, we also verified the expression and function of one of the smoking-related genes: EWSR1 in clinical PCa samples and cells. We found that EWSR1 can truly impact PCa development. The findings of this study demonstrated that smoking-induced genetic alteration is an etiological factor for PCa.

Ewing sarcoma breakpoint region 1 (EWSR1) is located on chromosome 22q12.2 and has been proved for the occurrence of ewing sarcoma [50]. The protein encodes by EWSR1 that is involved in various cellular processes, such as gene expression, cell signaling, and RNA processing and transport [51]. The function of EWSR1 in tumor has been widely reported. Studies have found that EWSR1 is important for the development of many types of cancer including lung cancer, Salivary Gland Neoplasms, and ewing sarcoma [52–54]. In this study, we also proved the role of EWSR1 in PCa. This study further verified the key role of EWSR1 in the development of multiple types of tumor.

CYP2J2 is a number of Cytochrome P450s (CYPs), with the highest score in nomagram for PCa occurrence, that can metabolizes endogenous substrates [55]. The dysregulation of CYP2J2 is correlated with many types of cancers. A study has reported that the variant mutation of CYP2J2 is a risk factor for lung cancer development [56]. In addition, patients with breast cancer always with CYP2J2 dysregulation [57].CYP2J2 is also important for the occurrence of urinary system tumors. A study found CYP2J2 can lead to the occurrence of kidney renal clear cell carcinoma [58]. In addition, CYP2J2 can affect the effect of ellagic acid in treating PCa [59].

Except EWSR1, we also found that SRSF6, FBLN1, and CYP2J2 can influence PCa progression. Serine/arginine-rich splicing factor 6 (SRSF6) was initially identified as a monoclonal antibody that recognizes phosphorylated serine/arginine-rich (SR) RNA-binding proteins [60]. SRSF6 is important for RNA constitutive and alternative splicing [60]. SRSF6 can causing the occurrence of many types of cancers. Studies have reported the amplification of SRSF6 can widely found in colorectal cancer [61, 62]. Further, SRSF6 is also important for squamous-cell carcinoma and lung cancer [63, 64]. In addition, a study also reported that SRSF6 can cause PCa by modulating histone-chaperone HIRA splicing to orchestrate AR and E2F activity [65]. Fibulin 1 (FBLN1) is located on the long arm of chromosome 22 (22q13) and its product is a secreted glycoprotein involved in the formation of the fibrous extracellular matrix [66]. FBLN1 has also found be central for cancer development. A study reported that FBLN1 can influence prognosis of cutaneous melanoma [67]. FBLN1 can also be a biomarker for predicting cervical cancer occurrence [68]. FBLN1 is also important for predicting urinary system tumors development. FBLN1 was found important for bladded cancer recurrence [69]. In addition, FBLN1 has also found can predict the progression of PCa [70]. All these studies confirmed the significant role of SRSF6, FBLN1, and CYP2J2 for cancer development including PCa.

However, this study has some limitations. This study identified 110 DEGs between smoking and non-smoking patients with PCa. PPI network analysis revealed 14 hub genes whose functions were analyzed in PCa. However, this study did not analyze the functions of other DEGs that may play a critical role in PCa. Additionally, this study examined the expression of genes using datasets from different databases. The results of the bioinformatics analysis must be verified using clinical samples. Although the function of smoking-related genes in PCa was analyzed using bioinformatics methods, the specific mechanisms of these genes in PCa development were not elucidated. Further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanism involved in the genetic alteration of these genes and the consequent development of PCa. This is the first study to systematically analyze the potential smoking-related genetic alterations in PCa.

Conclusion

Among the 110 smoking-related DEGs from GSE68135 datasets, seven key genes were identified to be involved in the development of PCa and one of them EWSR1 is truly an oncogene for PCa.
