Scheduled Remote Review of Continuous Glucose Monitoring Data with Targeted Intervention versus Standard Care in Children with Type 1 Diabetes: A Parallel-Groups Study
Authors: Frances Ellen Gehrmann, Lauren Haddow, Peter William James Delaney, Grant James Smith, Craig E. Taplin, Timothy W. Jones, Elizabeth A. Davis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study evaluated the impact of scheduled review of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data with targeted remote intervention on glycaemic and person-reported outcomes in children with type 1 diabetes (T1D).MethodsChildren aged 5–14 years with T1D using CGM were recruited from two metropolitan clinics at Perth Children’s Hospital in Western Australia; one clinic group was assigned to intervention and the other to control. The intervention group underwent CGM review by a diabetes educator every 14 days. Participants with >10% decrease in time in range (TIR: 3.9–10.0 mmol/L) from the previous review period, or who were not meeting consensus CGM targets, were prioritised and offered personalised remote advice via phone or email; the number receiving contact was constrained based on allocated educator time in each 14-day period. Those improving or meeting targets received positive feedback by SMS. Change in TIR from baseline to 6 months was compared between groups using difference-in-difference analysis. Person-reported outcomes were assessed using Type 1 Diabetes and Life (T1DAL) and Diabetes Treatment Satisfaction (DTSQ) questionnaires.ResultsA total of 113 children were recruited, 60 in the intervention group (53% male, age 10.2 [2.7] years, baseline TIR 66.9% [13.4], HbA1c 7.2% [1.3]) and 53 in the control group (51% male, age 9.6 [2.6] years, baseline TIR 69.4% [13.4], HbA1c 6.9% [0.8]). Most participants used automated insulin delivery systems; 64% in the control and 72% in the intervention. Across 14-day periods, mean (95% CI) 71% (67, 75) of participants in the intervention group were identified for targeted support; 39% (30, 49) received remote advice. Positive feedback was sent to 38% (32, 43) in each 14-day period. Compared with the control group, the intervention group showed a non-significant 3.1 percentage point increase in TIR (β = 3.1, p = 0.11). Over 6-months, mean TIR decreased in the control group (69.4%–66.6%) and was unchanged in the intervention group (66.9%–67.0%). No significant between-group differences were observed in T1DAL or DTSQ scores, limited by low end-of-intervention questionnaire response rates.ConclusionRegular CGM review identified many participants not meeting CGM targets, and over half received targeted intervention. However, the intervention did not significantly improve TIR or person-reported outcomes. While it did not bring participants into target range in this well-controlled cohort, the model may have greater effect in children with lower baseline TIR. Future trials should evaluate its impact in higher-risk youth.

Introduction

A key goal of the management of Type 1 diabetes is to prevent complications by maintaining blood glucose levels as close to normal as possible [1]. Until recently a target HbA1c of <7.0% was recommended to minimise long term complications of diabetes [2], however most recent international guidelines recommend a target HbA1c of ≤6.5% in those with access to advanced diabetes technologies [3]. With the advent of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), glycaemic targets have expanded to include CGM metrics, with percentage of sensor glucose time in target range (TIR [3.9–10.0 mmol/L]), time below range (TBR [<3.9 mmol/L and <3.0 mmol/L]) and time above range (TAR [>10.0 mmol/L and >13.9 mmol/L]), along with mean sensor glucose and coefficient of variation (CV) used as measures of glycaemic control [4]. Population based registries have shown that most young people with T1D do not achieve these targets [5–7]. Within Australia, population-based registries report that only 18–27% of youth with T1D achieve a HbA1c <7.0% [8, 9].

The model of care recommendations for young people living with diabetes has changed little over the last 4 decades. In-person clinical visits four times a year remains standard care [1]. Whilst models of care remain unchanged, diabetes technology has advanced significantly over the past decade with continuous glucose monitoring (CGM), insulin pumps and integrated advanced hybrid closed-loop insulin delivery (AHCL). Clinical and real-world trials of CGM use in youth demonstrate reduction in HbA1c and increase in TIR with CGM use, however, with CGM alone most youth still do not meet glycaemic targets [10–12]. AHCL systems have further improved glycaemic outcomes but even with these systems many do not achieve optimal glycaemic goals [13]. Real-world glycaemic outcomes of Western Australian youth show AHCL systems deliver an average 8% increase in TIR, but only 39% of youth meet TIR targets of >70% [14] evidencing that technology use improves outcomes but on its own does not deliver optimal glycaemic outcomes.

This suggests that models of care to maximise outcomes need to be developed and tested. In Western Australia, CGM is fully subsidised and is used in over 95% of young people with T1D. The use of CGM data to detect changes in glycaemic outcomes provides an opportunity to adapt our model of care to deliver more personalised and targeted care for young people with diabetes. We therefore designed a study to test a novel model of care aiming to investigate if frequent review of CGM data by health care providers to detect adverse changes in glycaemic control that is followed by targeted intervention to those requiring additional support can improve glycaemic and patient reported outcomes compared to those receiving the current traditional care model.

Methods

Study Design

This was a quasi-experimental, parallel-groups study comparing outcomes between an intervention clinic and a comparison clinic before and after implementation of the intervention using a difference-in-differences analysis. Children with T1D were recruited from two metropolitan clinics with comparable mean age and HbA1c at Perth Children’s Hospital, a tertiary paediatric diabetes centre in Western Australia, between November 2023 and May 2024. Children recruited from one clinic were allocated to the control group and the other clinic allocated to the intervention group, with intervention delivered by a diabetes educator already working within that clinic. Each clinic was led by different clinicians, but had an identical model of care, procedures and access to multi-disciplinary team specialties. This method of allocation was chosen to mimic a real-life clinical model, where children and families receive care from and develop relationships with a consistent team. A rolling recruitment was conducted, meaning that some participants had started the intervention prior to final participants being recruited. All children willing to use CGM and data-share with the clinic were invited to participate; non-English speaking families were excluded. All children attending the clinic had access to CGM funding through the NDSS and those not using CGM were doing so by choice. All eligible children were invited to participate with information sent via email and families invited in person when they attended their routine clinic visits.

Intervention

The intervention model of care was compared with a control group receiving conventional outpatient care. All participants had access to conventional care which consisted of 3-monthly multi-disciplinary clinic visits and 24-h phone support with a Diabetes Nurse Educator (DNE)-run triage line during the day and an emergency medical (Endocrinology trainee) line after hours. The intervention consisted of remote review of CGM data by a diabetes educator every 14-days. Glycaemic metrics were ranked based on ascending TIR, international CGM metrics targets and change in TIR as pre-defined major and minor flags. Flags of concern were determined using international consensus CGM metrics targets [3] and baseline analysis of a sample of the hospital cohort’s CGM data. Criteria were further separated into major flags which identified approximately 50% of the local population of youth on CGM and combined major and minor flags identifying approximately 70%. Major flags were defined as TIR <50%, >10% reduction in TIR over 2 weeks, >4% TBR and <75% sensor wear. TIR between 51 and 69% was assigned a minor flag. Positive flags included meeting all CGM metric targets or metrics demonstrated significant improvement in metrics from the last 14-day. The participants’ number of concerning flags and then ascending TIR were used to prioritise the 24 individuals each 14-day that were offered remote clinical review; this was capped to allow time for meaningful clinical consultation within the diabetes nurse’s allocated time of two 4-h slots per 14-day. Participants were offered the option of clinical review with a text message via WhatsApp (Meta Inc.), a secure messaging platform that allows confirmation of messages being received and read. If participants opted for clinical review, they chose to receive either a phone call with the research diabetes nurse educator (DNE) or an email with clinical support based on CGM and pump data review. Participants who met positive flags received a text message via WhatsApp with positive feedback regarding their CGM report. The intervention period ran for 26 weeks. To best reflect clinical practice, the research DNE was recruited from the intervention group’s routine clinical team to utilize the clinical relationship that develops in real-world clinical care.

Data Collection

Demographic and clinical data were collected from the Western Australia Children’s Diabetes Database (WACDD). Variables collected included age, sex, diabetes duration, postcode, insulin delivery regimen and HbA1c. Postcode data were used to classify the participants’ area of residence and calculate relative socio-economic disadvantage using the Australian Bureau of Statistics Socio-Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA) Index of Relative Socioeconomic Disadvantage (IRSD), with quintiles 1 and 2 considered socioeconomic disadvantage [15]. CGM data were retrieved from device-specific platforms (CareLink™, Glooko® and LibreView) with CGM reports reviewed online by the study team every 14-day to identify major, minor and positive flags and CGM data exported at the end of study period for analysis of outcomes. Clinical service delivery data were collected on a 14-day basis including number of participants enrolled, number meeting major and minor concerning flags and positive flags, number offered contact, number opting for contact and number receiving positive feedback. Clinician time taken reviewing the data of and contacting those that were offered intervention was recorded each 14-day cycle. Study data and digital consent were collected and managed using REDCap, electronic data capture tools [16, 17].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the change in TIR from baseline to end-of-intervention. Secondary glycaemic outcomes included change in HbA1c and glycaemic metrics across the study period, including TBR (<3.9 mmol/L and <3.0 mmol/L), TAR (>10.0 mmol/L and >13.9 mmol/L) and mean sensor glucose.

Person-reported outcomes collected included quality of life (QoL), assessed using the Type 1 Diabetes and Life (T1DAL) Questionnaire for children and parents, and treatment satisfaction was assessed using the Diabetes Treatment Satisfaction Questionnaire (DTSQs) for parents and teens. Questionnaires were completed at baseline and 6 months later in both the intervention and control groups. The DTSQ change (DTSQc) was administered at the end of intervention in parents and teens; it assesses change in satisfaction with a treatment regimen over time, with a more positive score indicating increased satisfaction with treatment regimen and a more negative score reflecting decreased satisfaction. Children ≥8 years are validated to complete age specific versions of the TIDAL and teens ≥12 years can complete the DTSQs and DTSQc.

Statistics

Mean (SD) is presented for continuous measures and frequencies (%) for categorical measures. A difference-in-differences (DID) analysis under a linear mixed model framework was used to determine the association between the intervention and changes in the primary outcome over time. The model included a random intercept for participant, effects for group (control, intervention), phase (start, end) and an arm by phase interaction (the parameter of interest) and key demographic/clinical characteristics (regimen, age, diabetes duration, sex). Additional sensitivity analyses were also conducted: standard ANCOVA, a linear mixed effects beta regression, a non-covariate adjusted DID model and a robust regression using iteratively reweighted least squares to reweight influential cases. Intervention effects for secondary glycaemic outcomes were estimated using regression models of change scores adjusting for key sociodemographic characteristics as were person reported outcomes (change in T1DAL and DTSQs scores). Where residual outliers were noted, sensitivity analyses using robust regression modelling were also conducted. Further sensitivity tests of secondary outcomes were also conducted using standard ANCOVA and generalized linear mixed models with distributional family appropriate for the data (e.g., negative binomial, beta, Gaussian).

As an exploratory, hypothesis-generating exercise, interaction models were conducted to explore differences in intervention effects by three sociodemographic factors: age group (<12 years, ≥ 12 years), baseline TIR (<50%, 50–69%, ≥70%), and area of socioeconomic disadvantage (lower IRSD = quintiles 1–2, higher IRSD = quintiles 3–5). For all tests, a p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The number of participants who met CGM metric flags in each 14-day period is presented with mean (SD) percentage of participants, and clinician time is presented as mean (SD) minutes per 14 days and per participant. No correction for multiple testing was applied.

Ethics Approval

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Child and Adolescent Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee in Western Australia (RGS0000005749). It is registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12623000955617) https://www.anzctr.org.au/. Recruitment took place from November 2023 to May 2024. All parents, guardians or next of kin provided written informed consent for their child to participate in this study.

Discussion

This study in children with T1D tested whether frequent remote CGM monitoring and review followed by targeted intervention resulted in improved glycaemic outcomes. Although the study identified that a high proportion of participants (67–71%) did not meet glycaemic targets, the intervention was not associated with significant improvements. Although the primary difference-in-differences analysis did not provide evidence that the TIR change differed between the intervention and control groups, descriptively, the control group showed a small statistically significant decline in TIR and the intervention group showed no evidence for significant change. Whilst these within-group patterns may warrant further investigation in larger studies in the future, they should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis generating rather than evidence of an intervention effect.

When stratified by baseline TIR, age, and IRSD, there was no evidence for a differential intervention effect based on patient characteristics; however, numbers in the groups were small and the study was not powered to detect these differences. Visual inspection of the effect sizes and confidence intervals suggests future adequately powered research should explore whether these types of interventions differ in their effectiveness for those with a baseline TIR <50% and those aged 12 years and older. No significant change in QoL or treatment satisfaction was identified in questionnaires.

There are limited studies using remote monitoring of glucose levels between clinics in youth with T1D. A randomized control trial by Ganrud et al. [18] of 117 youth aged 8–17 with mean HbA1c 8.9% (0.67) compared weekly review of pump uploads (using self-measured or CGM glycaemic data) with standard outpatient care. Whilst results were non-significant, a trend of lower HbA1c at 6 months was seen in their intervention group (−0.35%) compared to the control (−0.05%). The magnitude of glycaemic change was greater compared to our study; however, older mean age and higher HbA1c at baseline make direct comparison difficult. Teenagers (aged 13–17 years) demonstrated greater glycaemic gains compared to younger age groups; this supports the need to further explore effects of intervention in a teenage age group.

Crossen et al. performed a feasibility study in youth aged 1–20 (mean 9 years) comparing standard care (mean Glycaemic Management Index [GMI] 7.5%) to monthly CGM review (mean GMI 7.6%) and contact by a clinician if CGM metrics demonstrated excessive hyperglycaemia (>15% time above 13.9 mmol/L) or hypoglycaemia (>5% of the time below 3.9 mmol/L) [19]. This model showed a treatment effect (−0.25%) on GMI, with greater reduction seen in those with a higher baseline GMI [19], also suggesting potential for greater gain in those with poorer baseline glycaemia.

A series of studies conducted by a single institution in the newly diagnosed T1D population, the Teamwork, Targets and Tight Control (4T) Study utilized an automated dashboard that extracted data from Dexcom Clarity to remotely monitor and prioritize those requiring intervention [20–22]. Alerts for intervention were comparable to our study; data were reviewed on a weekly basis; and participants were prioritized based on number of alerts and ascending TIR and contact was provided to 22 top-priority participants each week by electronic message through an electronic health record. Whilst there was no significant difference in HbA1c at 12 months between those receiving remote monitoring compared to standard care in the 4T cohort, TIR was 8.8% higher in those receiving weekly remote monitoring [20–22]. It may be speculated that newly diagnosed families may be more receptive to advice and behaviour change before individual diabetes management behaviours have established.

A strength of our study was the inclusion of children using any available CGM technology despite this requiring review across multiple platforms. It should be noted that the study DNE was involved in the clinical care of participants in the intervention group, allowing for the real-world clinical relationship that develops between a child/family to be incorporated into this trial model of care.

A limitation of this study was that participants had relatively optimized glycaemic outcomes at baseline, although most still did not meet consensus targets. Given mean TIR was approaching targets at baseline (66% intervention and 69% control), there was a smaller margin for improvement following intervention, making detection of significant effect on glycaemic outcomes less likely. Only 11% of participants started with TIR <50% compared to 32% of eligible children who did not enrol in the study, limiting the power to detect significant change in this group despite visual appearance of effect in this subgroup. There was a self-selection bias with the cohort that consented to participate in the trial, mostly already very motivated with a low mean HbA1c (6.9% control and 7.2% intervention). This makes results of this study different to generalize across the wider population of children with T1D and future work will need to address barriers to adopting this model for those with poorer glycaemic outcomes.

Another limitation is the lack of randomization, with control and interventions groups allocated to two existing clinic groups, with matched models of service delivery but run by different clinicians. This makes it impossible to separate intervention effects from unique clinic features. The incomplete questionnaire completion at end of intervention is another limitation. Small numbers of completed questionnaires compromise power to detect a significant difference in treatment satisfaction or quality of life between the intervention and control groups and there may be differences in the populations who completed or did not complete questionnaires making any effects potentially subject to bias.

Ongoing optimization of target population, triggers for contact and modes of communication will be important to create a successful model of care. Previous similar studies have shown that children with lower TIR or HbA1c and older, teenage participants made greater glycaemic gains with remote CGM review and intervention [18, 19] and subgroup analysis of this cohort showed a non-significant suggestion of greater gains in these populations. Thus, it is plausible that targeting children within our population with lower TIR and the adolescent subgroup could result in more significant improvement in glycaemic outcomes. Further exploration of this model should include adolescents, where contact could be targeted at the young person with diabetes rather than their parent/guardian, as well as prioritizing individuals with lower TIR who stand to make greater gains with this form of intervention. Furthermore, when considering implementation of a new model of care, feasibility is dependent on resource availability and allocation. Data have been collected to complete a full economic evaluation of this model of care.

In this trial, CGM reports were manually reviewed from different device platforms by members of the study team and flags manually allocated to participants. This is a labour-intensive process and would not be feasible across a large clinical cohort. To offer this model of care to all, optimization of processes and contact methods is needed. Adoption of an automated algorithm to identify and prioritize patients who met criteria for contact would be necessary. In this study, an average of 7.6 min per participant per 14 days was spent on clinical review and contact, which may not be feasible across a large cohort. Reduction in time spent per individual could be achieved by using predominantly messaging services to streamline contact; this has been used successfully in similar models [20, 21], but such an intervention requires further stakeholder engagement and co-design as it is likely a range of preferred methods of receiving information across patients and families exists. Further refinement of clinical flags of concern for intervention could also be considered to prioritize clinician time and target those at greatest risk.

Conclusion

In a group of children with generally optimized glycaemia and high technology use, frequent review of CGM with targeted remote intervention over 6 months identified a large proportion of participants for remote intervention. In its current form, in this population, it is unlikely that the intervention will result in clinical improvements. However, targeted delivery of the intervention to those with suboptimal glycaemic outcomes, or adolescent groups, warrants further exploration. Automating key processes such as CGM review and prioritization/ranking processes to streamline patient identification and contact may improve clinical feasibility and wider adoption.
