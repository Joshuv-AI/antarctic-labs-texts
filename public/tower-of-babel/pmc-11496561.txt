Barriers and Facilitators of Pediatric Obesity Prevention and Management (POPM) Programs in Korea: Focusing on the Questionnaire About the Linkage in Community Level
Authors: Sujin Park, Hyo Seon Jeong, Young-Min Noh, Eungu Kang, Yong Hee Hong, Sochung Chung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecent global trends indicate a rise in pediatric obesity, reflecting patterns also observed in South Korea. Given its significant impact on chronic disease prevalence in adulthood, pediatric obesity poses potential societal challenges. For pediatric obesity-related prevention or management programs in community level to operate effectively, there needs to be a clear understanding of barriers and facilitators of the programs. This study aims to establish a foundation for policy implementation, contributing to pediatric obesity prevention and management (POPM) in Korea.MethodsA survey was conducted among program providers involved in domestic POPM programs. A total of 577 individuals completed the survey, including those working in elementary and middle schools (n = 508) and public health centers (n = 69) nationwide. The questionnaire comprised 67 questions covering characteristics of respondents, purpose and contents of POPM programs, measurement of program outcome, level of inter- and intra-institutional linkage, difficulties in operating programs and factors that facilitate programs. A 5-point Likert scale was used for most questions. Descriptive statistics was employed to analyze characteristics of respondents in POPM programs. The level of linkage in POPM programs was assessed using perceived importance and actual degree of linkage. The difficulties in operating POPM programs were analyzed based on agreement responses, and facilitating factors of program activation were analyzed based on importance responses.ResultsThe domestic POPM program showed low actual linkage compared to its perceived importance, both between institutions and among professions within institutions. Difficulties in operating the program included securing availability of students, encouraging participation of reluctant students and development of new programs. The survey suggested that schools require support from parents, guardians and family members, while public health centers need professional providers to facilitate such programs.ConclusionThe study highlights the urgent need for strategies to address pediatric obesity in South Korea. Weak institutional linkages hinder effective programs. Challenges include student availability, participation, and the need for innovative programs. New approaches to build partnerships in harmony among institutions are necessary. Implementing findings into policy can help prevent obesity in Korean children and adolescents.

INTRODUCTION

Pediatric obesity is a public health concern, due to its association with comorbidities including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), dyslipidemia, hypertension, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, obstructive sleep apnea, polycystic ovary syndrome and psychiatric problems during childhood.1 The prevalence of obesity in children and adolescents is expected to increase drastically, rising from 10% to 20% in boys and from 8% to 18% in girls worldwide from 2020 to 2035.2 A predictive epidemiologic model estimates that if the obesity trend in the United States in 2017 continues, 57% of children aged 2 to 19 years will have obesity by 2050.3 It is noteworthy that a recent increase in the prevalence of severe obesity has also been reported in Korea.4 The prevalence of pediatric obesity, defined as body mass index (BMI) ≥ 95th percentile for the corresponding sex and age in the 2007 growth charts, increased from 8.7% in 2007 to 15.0% in 2017 in Korean children aged 6 to 18 years according to the National School Health Examination.5

Given that pediatric obesity is the result of a complex interaction between biological, behavioral and environmental factors, intervention at multiple levels and settings is critical for both short- and long-term effectiveness.6 The Clinical-Community Integration Framework by Dietz et al.7 addressed such necessary change by integrating existing clinical and community services and improving coordination of services by clinicians, public health practitioners and community organizations. Utilizing this new conceptualization as a reference, other chronic diseases and preventable conditions may also be managed effectively. Recent guidelines for pediatric obesity recommend that public health agencies, community organizations, health care systems, health care providers and community members collaborate to expand access to pediatric obesity prevention programs and increase community resources.8 While other developed countries have implemented school- or community-based obesity prevention programs, such systematic programs with long-term plans are rare in Korea. For pediatric obesity-related prevention and management programs to operate effectively, a clear understanding of barriers and facilitators for program sustainability is essential.

Our research aims to serve as a basis for future policy implementation and ultimately contribute to the prevention of obesity in children and adolescents in Korea. There has been insufficient information on the actual conditions and barriers to school-based and public health center-based pediatric obesity prevention or management programs in Korea. Thus, we conducted a basic survey on providers of obesity prevention and management programs (hereinafter referred to as program providers) and investigated the current status of, as well as barriers and facilitators of obesity prevention and management programs for children and adolescents.

METHODS

Study design and population

This survey was conducted on program providers participating in domestic pediatric obesity prevention and management (POPM) programs. Questionnaires were distributed and collected via mobile and web platforms.

The survey was conducted nationwide in collaboration with the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency and the Korean Health Teachers Association. The survey targeted practitioners and programs providers at 6,163 elementary schools, 3,258 middle schools (according to the 2022 Education Statistics Annals) and 258 public health centers (according to the 2021 Status of Local Healthcare Organizations) nationwide. The pilot survey results estimated an average of 2.5 individuals per institution, leading to an estimated population of 24,198 people. The final survey respondents consisted of individuals who agreed to participate, had been involved in related programs for more than six months and provided responses to all questions. A total of 577 individuals completed the survey (508 from schools and 69 from public health centers), representing a response rate of 2.2% for schools and 10.7% for public health centers (Table 1).

The structure of a survey

We developed the final set of questions through a review of previous literature, creation of preliminary questions, validation by experts and pilot testing. The questionnaire comprised a total of 67 questions, covering general characteristics of the survey respondents, characteristics of POPM programs, level of inter- and intra-institution linkage, difficulties in operating programs and factors that facilitate programs. To ensure the validity of the survey, eight experts from academia, schools and public health centers reviewed the survey. With the exception of subjective and nominal questions, a 5-point Likert scale was utilized to capture responses, tailored to discern differences between school and public health center programs. We conducted descriptive statistics separately for schools and public health centers.

Measurement and statistical method

To assess the characteristics of the survey respondents and the status of POPM programs, descriptive statistics such as averages, frequencies and proportions were applied, distinguishing disparities between schools and public health centers.

To evaluate the level of linkage in POPM programs, our study surveyed “the perceived importance of linkage” and “the actual degree of linkage.” A 5-point scale assessed the perceived importance and the actual level of linkage between various entities involved in POPM programs. The importance was determined by the percentage of respondents who rated linkage or collaboration as “important” or “very important.” Similarly, the actual level of linkage or collaboration was measured by the percentage of respondents who rated the current state of linkage as “sufficient” or “very sufficient.” Subsequently, the two indicators were compared to examine the discrepancy between the perceived importance and the actual degree of linkage in POPM programs.

Difficulties in operating POPM programs were analyzed based on responses that answered “agree” or “strong agree.” Similarly, facilitating factors contributing to POPM program activation were analyzed based on responses that answered facilitating factors to be “important” or “very important.”

Ethics statement

This study informed participants about the purpose, procedures, expected benefits, and risks before the survey began, and included responses from those who voluntarily provided written consent. Approval from the Institutional Review Board of Soonchunhyang University Bucheon Hospital (SCHBC 2023-08-011-001) was obtained prior to the study.

RESULTS

Status of POPM programs

The average number of dedicated personnel participating in a program was 2.4 at schools and 2.8 at public health centers.

The average annual budget was KRW 1.91 million ($1,364.29 USD) for schools and KRW 12.94 million ($9,242.86 USD) for public health centers. The annual budget for institutions varied widely, ranging from KRW 500 thousand to KRW 88 million (approximately $440 to $77,000 USD). As for schools, a high percentage of respondents (33.3%) reported that a budget dedicated solely for POPM programs was not prepared. Additionally, most children and adolescents participating in POPM programs of schools and public health centers were aged between 7 and 12 years.

Purpose and contents of POPM programs

School program providers identified the main purpose of POPM programs as obesity prevention (82.9%), intervention (73.2%), and awareness-raising (29.3%). On the other hand, public health center program providers responded with prevention (95.7%), awareness-raising (58.0%), and intervention (42.0%) as the purpose of POPM programs. Both schools and health centers prioritized obesity prevention as the primary objective of POPM programs. However, schools focused on intervention, while public health centers aimed to raise awareness.

Additionally, while schools emphasized education (91.5%), counseling (48.4%), and monitoring (47.8%) as primary components of such programs, public health centers prioritized education (94.2%), monitoring (63.8%), and provision of goods (30.4%) (Fig. 1).

Measurements of POPM program outcomes

To evaluate the effectiveness of POPM programs, 85.6% of schools and 84.1% of public health centers primarily utilized anthropometric measurements, such as weight, BMI and blood pressure of students that completed POPM programs. In addition, schools assessed changes in diet and physical activity (59.8%) and related knowledge on nutrition and physical activity (35.8%). Public health centers similarly assessed related knowledge (65.2%), changes in diet and physical activity (62.3%), and participant satisfaction (62.3%). Compared to schools, public health centers focused relatively more on participant experience and satisfaction (Table 2).

Level of linkage between institutions (inter-institutions)

In assessing the perceived importance and the actual degree of linkage between institutions, program providers in schools identified the following institutions as the most significant to cooperate with: schools (90.0%), public health centers (80.3%), and community sports facilities (72.6%). However, the actual level of linkage differed as follows: schools (60.0%), public health centers (27.6%), and hospitals (18.3%). Notably, the actual linkage between public health centers and community sports facilities was relatively lower than the corresponding perceived importance of linkage. Similarly, public health centers prioritized cooperation with schools (97.1%), public health centers (89.9%), and community sports facilities (69.6%), yet the actual level of linkage differed, demonstrating linkage in orders of public health centers (75.4%), schools (63.8%), and community sports facilities (33.3%). In both cases, the actual degree of linkage fell short of the perceived importance of linkage (Fig. 2).

Level of linkage between professions within an institution (intra-institution)

We investigated the level of linkage among different professions within an institution. At schools, cooperation with the following order of professions was deemed important: physical education teachers (94.1%), school nurses (92.3%), nutrition teachers (91.1%), and homeroom teachers (89.6%). However, the actual degree of linkage followed a different pattern, with the highest linkage observed in school nurses (76.0%), followed by physical education teachers (61.6%), and nutrition teachers (54.3%). Notably, administrators exhibited a substantial disparity between their actual degree of linkage (79.7%) and their perceived importance of linkage (24.4%). Public health center program providers prioritized linkage with exercise specialists (98.6%), nutritionists (97.1%), administrators (71.0%), and heads of the institution (65.2%). Particularly, medical personnel showed a significant gap between the actual degree (55.1%) and the perceived importance of linkage (15.9%). In both schools and public health centers, the actual degree of linkage was lower in general than the perceived importance of linkage between professions (Fig. 3).

Difficulties in operating programs

Difficulties in operating POPM programs in schools included student availability during program hours (94.1%), a shortage of new program curriculums (90.9%) and low student engagement (90.2%). Public health center providers encountered difficulties in generating new programs annually (92.7%), securing program hours (89.8%), and encouraging student participation (85.5%). Although there were differences in details between schools and public health centers, their fundamental difficulties were unanimous. In particular, the lack of participation in students and inadequate support from parents or guardians were notable barriers to a successful operation of the programs, reflecting recent social stigma (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study that investigated the barriers to effective POPM programs and the actual level of inter- and intra-institutional linkage in Korea. Efforts to prevent childhood obesity have sporadically occurred in Korea, but there was insufficient data to provide foundation for further intervention. Thus, our study focused on improving obesity prevention and management programs for children and adolescents.

Recent public health strategies have adapted an ecological approach that emphasizes the importance of the environment surrounding an individual.910 The ecological model states that an individual behavior is determined by various aspects at individual, interpersonal, organizational, community and policy levels.10 Thus, effective strategies to prevent childhood obesity require a comprehensive approach that includes not only personal but also environmental factors. Given the usual course of childhood obesity, which often prolongs into adolescence and adulthood, the medical costs associated with childhood obesity amount to nearly $1.3 billion dollars annually in the United States.11 Pediatric obesity caused a per capita increase of $56.52, $14.27, $46.38, and $1,975.06 for costs in nonhospital healthcare, outpatient visits, medication, and inpatient care, respectively.11 There has also been a substantial rise in not only direct healthcare costs, such as medical costs, but also indirect costs. Since prevention of pediatric obesity is more cost-effective than long-term obesity management, policymakers must encourage POPM programs to save medical resources and improve health of the general population. Public health interventions are important to reduce childhood obesity rates through prevention. However, it often takes time to fully realize its benefits.12

Funding is a major barrier to yet an essential element of program initiation and maintenance.13 In South Korea, budgets for school programs primarily come from the Office of Education or the Ministry of Education, while public health centers receive their funding from the Ministry of Health and Welfare or local governments (data not shown). Budgets for schools were sometimes not accounted properly, unlike in public health centers where funding were budgeted, though variations exist by institutions.

In both schools and public health centers, the main components of the programs were education, counseling and monitoring, with education at the highest priority. However, despite the emphasis on education, both schools and public health centers primarily utilized anthropometric measurements such as weight, BMI and blood pressure as the main method to assess the effectiveness of the programs. This assessment method may not align well with the main purpose of the programs, as it focuses more on physical health indicators instead of the figures that signify prevention, management and awareness of childhood obesity. Such discordance suggests a potential gap between the program objectives and the metric system used to evaluate the effectiveness of POPM programs. Stakeholders involved in POPM programs may need to reconsider their evaluation methods and focus on assessing changes in knowledge, attitudes and behaviors, as well as the effectiveness of educational intervention and counseling sessions.

Actual linkages were found to be insufficient compared to the perceived importance of linkage in both schools and public health centers. It is emphasized that there exists a disparity between the recognition of its significance and the actual performance. Partnership of institutions and professions is necessary to prevent and manage obesity in children and adolescents, yet little effort has been dedicated to evaluate the performance of such partnerships. Addressing the complex, multifactorial causes of increasing pediatric obesity prevalence requires a shift in thinking, one that values collaboration.14 In the case of POPM programs in schools, school nurses are solely in charge, often without any authority or framework to seek cooperation with other professionals. Therefore, it is crucial to establish an inter- and intra-institutional linkage system for POPM programs.

According to program providers of schools and public health centers have struggled to secure program hours. In South Korea, approximately three-quarters of elementary school students in fourth to sixth grade and half of elementary school students in first to third grade spend time on supplementary studying in after-school institutions.15 Thus, it is also important to consider including obesity prevention education during regular school hours. Another difficulty was the lack of active participation of obese students in POPM program. This is presumed to be due to insufficient awareness and stigma associated with obesity. In recent years, there has been increasing focus on obesity as a condition associated with significant stigma, alongside a growing acknowledgment of its prevalence harm.16 Families who engage in obesity management programs often have low engagement and retention rates.17 To solve this problem, it is necessary to improve awareness of pediatric obesity throughout society. As for difficulties in generating new programs, it is necessary to take various factors such as age and region into account. Previous studies have pointed out various difficulties in operating obesity prevention programs, including the lack of budget and time availability, financial hardship and inadequate insurance coverage for long‐term treatment.1819 Park and Choo20 reported that factors hindering the prevention of childhood obesity include the current absence of child-centered obesity prevention programs, lack of promotion of public health programs and insufficient government support in Korea. Hayes et al.21 also recommended capacity to deliver within an already overcrowded curriculum, supports needed to do so, and clear specification of intervention components within a conceptual framework to facilitate evaluation.

We also conducted surveys on the facilitators of POPM programs. Program providers in both schools and public health centers reported active student participation and support from parents and families, also identified as major difficulties in operating POPM programs, as the most important facilitators. Parents and guardians should be encouraged to get involved in school and community programs that improve nutritional status and physical activity of their children. In order to facilitate the programs, insufficient budget was pointed out as an additional problem that needs to be solved at the community or institutional level. Obesity prevention relies on the supportiveness of school climates, and collaboration among stakeholders can increase the likelihood of implementing effective practices.22 Additionally, successful interventions have taken a broader community view and oriented prevention efforts across multiple community sectors and settings.23 Determinants of pediatric obesity occur at multiple levels including family, community and policy.24 To accelerate prevention of pediatric obesity, strengthened political commitment is essential.25

Although this study has limitations due to its reliance on subjective reports by program providers, it is significant in its attempt to reflect opinions directly from providers of childhood obesity prevention programs by conducting a nationwide, large-scale survey. Future studies targeting children, adolescents and parents who had participated in the POPM programs may add a different perspective on POPM programs.

In conclusion, we need to improve awareness for pediatric obesity across society and encourage participation of unwilling target students and guardians. As for barriers to obesity prevention programs, such as lack of resources and support, intriguing programs that take characteristics of the community into account are essential, as well as human resources and budgetary support are. Finally, establishing a linkage system within professions and between institutions is necessary. New approaches to build concordant partnerships among institutions are pivotal for the future of the next generation.
