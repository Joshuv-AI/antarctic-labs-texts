Quantization Of Probability Measures In Maximum Mean Discrepancy Distance
Zahra Mehraban, Alois Pichler

Abstract
Accurate approximation of probability measures is essential in numerical applications. This paper explores the quantization of probability measures using the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) distance as a guiding metric. We first investigate optimal approximations by determining the best weights, followed by addressing the problem of optimal facility locations. To facilitate efficient computation, we reformulate the nonlinear objective as expectations over a product space, enabling the use of stochastic approximation methods. For the Gaussian kernel, we derive closed-form expressions to develop a deterministic optimization approach. By integrating stochastic approximation with deterministic techniques, our framework achieves precise and efficient quantization of continuous distributions, with significant implications for machine learning and signal processing applications.

Quantization of probability measures · maximum mean discrepancy distance · kernel methods · optimization

Introduction

Accurate approximations of probability measures are essential when working with measures numerically. A commonly used approach is the approximation of probability measures by a simple measure, often referred to as
quantization
.

The accuracy of the approximation has been measured differently in the literature, with significant recent developments relying on the Wasserstein distance.
Foundational work on approximation quality in Wasserstein distance is provided in
Graf and Luschgy [6]
, including the approximation quality in Wasserstein distance.
Additional theoretical advancements, along with applications, are extensively covered in the works of Pagès, such as
Bally et al. [1]
,
Pagès [19]
, and
Pagès and Printems [20]
.

This paper investigates an alternative approach: approximating probability measure using the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD).
In this framework, the probability measure is embedded into a suitable Hilbert space of functions, where a norm is employed for comparison.
Specifically, MMD leverages a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), enabling a computationally efficient evaluation for discrepancies between distributions.

Reliable approximations of probability measures are crucial across numerous applications.
In optimization under uncertainty, these results are indispensable to guarantee continuity of the stochastic program.
Other fields that benefit include statistical learning, information theory, mathematical finance, data compression, signal processing, and machine learning, where infinite-dimensional distributions are often impractical to handle.
The primary challenge is identifying a finite set of points that best represent the original distribution while minimizing the approximation error.
For a deeper exploration of related topics, see
Gersho and Gray [5]
,
Gray and Neuhoff [7]
,
Widrow and Kollár [27]
,
Hubara et al. [9]
,
Liang et al. [14]
and
Rajasekar and Pushpalatha [23]
.

Recently, MMD has gained prominence as a powerful tool for comparing probability distributions. Unlike the Wasserstein distance, which is computationally expensive due to the optimization problem over transport plans, MMD offers a more efficient alternative. By utilizing kernel functions to assess discrepancies in a high-dimensional feature spaces, the MMD is particularly well-suited for large-scale statistical learning tasks where minimizing computation time is essential.

Quantization in Wasserstein distance suffers from the curse of dimensionality, as discussed in
Bolley et al. [3]
.
For this reason, regularization techniques have been developed and investigated as well, cf.
Lakshmanan and Pichler [10]
,
Lakshmanan and Pichler [11]
, as well as accelerated computational strategies, cf.
Lakshmanan et al. [12]
.
More recently, MMD has been applied to quantization problems.
For instance,
Feng et al. [4]
introduce Deep Weibull Hashing (DWH), combining MMD quantization with a Weibull constraint to enhance nearest neighbor search.
Experimental results demonstrate that DWH outperforms existing methods by better preserving pairwise similarities. Similarly,
Teymur et al. [26]
developed sequential algorithms for MMD minimization, including a new non-myopic approach and a mini-batch variant to improve efficiency.
Other notable contributions include
Pronzato [22]
, who evaluate various MMD-based quantization algorithms – such as kernel herding, greedy minimization, and sequential Bayesian quadrature – and
Xu et al. [28]
, who introduces adaptive kernel methods for particle-based approximation, achieving improvements in robustness and errors reduction.
Further applications are found in
Guo et al. [8]
and
Li et al. [13]
.

In this paper, we progress optimizing quantization by minimizing MMD through a Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) algorithm.

2
Mathematical setting

To formally define the optimization problem consider a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
{(\mathcal{H}_{k},\,\|\cdot\|_{k})}
of functions on
\mathcal{X}
with kernel
k
. The inner product that ensures the reproducing property for this space is given by

\langle k(x,\cdot)|\ k(y,\cdot)\rangle_{k}=k(x,y)

(cf.
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan [2]
for a discussion of RKHS spaces).
Given a general measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{X}
, the
kernel embedding function
is defined as

\mu_{k}(\cdot)\coloneqq\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(\cdot,\xi)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi).

(2.1)

The mapping
\mu\mapsto\mu_{k}
embeds measures
\mu
into
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, and the
maximum mean discrepancy
distance between two measures
\mu
and
\nu
is given by

\MMD_{k}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\|\mu_{k}-\nu_{k}\|_{k}.

Unlike the Wasserstein distance, the MMD does not require the measures
\mu
and
\nu
to have the same mass. In other words, they do not necessarily need to satisfy the condition
\mu(\mathcal{X})=\nu(\mathcal{X})
for the distance to be well-defined.
Explicitly, the maximum mean discrepancy distance of two measures on
\mathcal{X}
is

\displaystyle\MMD_{k}(\mu,\nu)^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime})

(2.2)

\displaystyle\quad-2\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)\nu(\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime})+\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\nu(\mathrm{d}\xi)\nu(\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime}).

A recent review on the subject can found in
Muandet et al. [15]
.

This paper focuses on the quantization of measures, specifically the approximation of a measure
\mu
by a simple, discrete measure of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\cdot\delta_{x_{i}},

where the goal is to achieve the best possible approximation.
Our focus is on probability measures.
Given a probability measure
P
, the quantization problem involves finding a discrete
probability
measure of the form

P^{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\cdot\delta_{x_{i}}

(2.3)

that approximates
P
by as accurately as possible.
In (
2.3
), the weights
p_{1},\ p_{2},\dots,\ p_{n}
are non-negative and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1
, and
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac measure located at
x
.
1
1

1

The Dirac measure is
\delta_{a}(A)\coloneqq\one_{A}(a)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }a\in A,\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

The support points
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
of the finite measure
P^{n}
are called
quantizers
, the embedding of the approximating measure is the function

P^{n}_{k}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\cdot k(x_{i},\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{k},

cf. (
2.1
).
With that, the quantization problem is

minimize

\displaystyle\|P_{k}-P_{k}^{n}\|_{k}

(2.4a)

in

\displaystyle\bm{p}=[p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n}]\text{ and }\bm{x}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}],

(2.4b)

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1,

(2.4c)

\displaystyle p_{i}\geq 0,\text{ and}

(2.4d)

\displaystyle x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\text{ for }i=1,\dots,n,

(2.4e)

where the expanded objective (
2.4a
) is

\displaystyle\|P_{k}-P_{k}^{n}\|_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,x_{i})\,\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}k(x_{i},x_{j})\,p_{i}\,p_{j}

(2.5)

by (
2.2
).

3
The quantization weights

We shall address the problem of optimal quantization (
2.4a
)–(
2.4e
) in consecutive steps: first, we describe the optimal weights in the unconstrained problem (
2.4a
) and the problem (
2.4a
)–(
2.4d
); next, we consider the problem of finding the optimal quantizers (
2.4e
) separately. Given these results, we shall finally address the problem of locating the optimal quantization points.

3.1
Approximations of general measures

Consider the following general version of the problem (
2.4a
) first to identify the optimal weights for fixed
x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
(
x_{i}\not=x_{j}
for
i\not=j
):

minimize

\displaystyle\|\mu_{k}-\mu_{k}^{n}\|_{k},

(3.1)

where

\displaystyle\mu^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\,\delta_{x_{i}}\text{ and }\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\in\mathbb{R}.

Here,
\mu
is a general measure, not necessarily a probability measure.
The optimization problem aims at identifying the optimal weights
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}
of the approximating discrete measure
\mu^{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\,\delta_{x_{i}}
.

Proposition 1
.

The optimal weights
\bm{\mu}=[\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}]
in (
3.1
) are given by

\bm{\mu}=\bm{K}^{-1}\,\bm{m},

(3.2)

where
\bm{K}
is the matrix with entries

\bm{K}_{ij}=k(x_{i},x_{j}),\qquad i,j=1,\dots,n,

and the entries of the vector
\bm{m}
are evaluations of the embedding of the initial function at the support points,

m_{i}\coloneqq\mu_{k}(x_{i})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(x_{i},\xi)\,\mu(d\xi)

for
i=1,\dots,n
.

Proof.

The necessary conditions of the optimization problem with objective (
3.1
) are

0=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mu_{i}}=-2\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,x_{i})\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}\,k(x_{i},x_{j}),

where (
2.5
) expands the Lagrangian

L(\bm{\mu})=L(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})\coloneqq\|\mu_{k}-\mu_{k}^{n}\|_{k}^{2}

explicitly. As the matrix
\bm{K}
is symmetric, the assertion (
3.2
) is immediate.
Finally, note that

\mu_{k}(x_{i})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(x_{i},\xi)\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)

by the definition in (
2.1
), and hence the assertion.
∎

Corollary 2
.

For fixed support points
\bm{x}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, the optimal maximum mean discrepancy distance is given by

\MMD{(\mu,\mu^{n})}^{2}=\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime})-\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\,\bm{m}.

(3.3)

Proof.

With
\bm{\mu}=\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}
from Proposition
1
, the objective (
2.5
) is

\displaystyle\MMD{(\mu,\mu^{n})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mu_{k}-\mu_{k}^{n}\|_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime})-2\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\,\bm{m}+\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{K}\bm{K}^{-1}\,\bm{m}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi)\mu(\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime})-\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\,\bm{m},

thus the assertion.
∎

Remark 3
.

For
\xi\in\mathcal{X}
fixed, consider the function
f(\cdot)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}\,k(x_{i},\cdot)
with weights
\bm{w}=\bm{K}^{-1}k(\xi,\bm{x})
, where
k(\xi,\bm{x})\coloneqq\bigl(k(\xi,x_{1}),\dots,k(\xi,x_{n})\bigr)
.
By construction, it holds that
f(x_{i})=k(x_{i},\xi)
, that is, the function
f(\cdot)
interpolates
k(\cdot,\xi)
at
x_{1}\dots,x_{n}
.
By the representer theorem (cf.
Schölkopf et al. [25]
), the function
f(\cdot)
has the smallest norm (
\|\cdot\|_{k}
) among all functions interpolating the function
k(\cdot,\xi)
in the points
x_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
.
That is,

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}k(x_{i},\xi)\bm{K}_{ij}^{-1}k(x_{j},\xi)=\|f\|_{k}^{2}\leq\|k(\cdot,\xi)\|_{k}^{2}=k(\xi,\xi).

This inequality corresponds to
\MMD(\delta_{\xi},\delta_{\xi}^{n})\geq 0
for the specific measure
\mu=\delta_{\xi}
in Corollary
2
.

3.2
Approximation of probability measures

Even for an initial probability measure
\mu=P
in (
3.1
), the optimal approximating measure
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\cdot\delta_{x_{i}}
, given in Proposition
1
above, is – in general – not a probability measure: the weights
\mu_{i}
in (
3.2
) are not necessarily non-negative, and the weights do not sum to one.

We have the following result on the optimal weights of the probability measure approximating a probability measure
P
.

Theorem 4
.

For fixed support points
\bm{x}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, the probability measure with the smallest maximum mean discrepancy distance in (
2.4a
)–(
2.4c
) has weights

\displaystyle\bm{p}=\left(\bm{K}^{-1}-\frac{\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}\right)\bm{m}+\frac{\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}},

(3.4)

where
\bm{m}
is the vector with entries

m_{i}=\E k(\cdot,x_{i})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(x_{i},\xi)\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)=P_{k}(x_{i})

(3.5)

and
P_{k}(\cdot)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(\cdot,\xi)\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
is the embedding of
P
, cf. (
2.1
), and
\bm{1}\coloneqq[1,\dots,1]
.

Proof.

The Lagrangian function of the optimization problem (
2.4
) with constraints (
2.4c
) is

\displaystyle L(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n};\lambda)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\|P_{k}-P_{k}^{n}\|_{k}^{2}+\lambda\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime})-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,x_{i})\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}k(x_{i},x_{j})\,p_{i}\,p_{j}+\lambda\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}-1\right).

To extract the first order conditions, differentiate the Lagrangian function with respect to
p_{\ell}
(
\ell=1,\ldots,n
) and the multiplier
\lambda
, and then equate the derivatives to zero.
This gives the necessary conditions

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial L}{\partial p_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=-2\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,x_{\ell})\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(x_{i},x_{\ell})p_{i}+\lambda,\quad\ell=1,\ldots,n,

(3.6)

and the constraint

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}-1.

(3.7)

Multiplying both sides of the first equation by
p_{i}
and summing with respect to the index
i
, we obtain

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,x_{i})\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

\displaystyle+2\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}k(x_{i},x_{j})p_{i}p_{j}+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=0.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1
from (
3.7
), the last term simplifies to
\lambda
.
Therefore, the system of equations becomes

\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,x_{i})\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}k(x_{i},x_{j})p_{i}p_{j}-\lambda=0.

As
\int_{\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,x_{i})\,P(\mathrm{d}\xi)=\E k(\cdot,x_{i})
for
i=1,..,n
, we have

\lambda=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\E k(\cdot,x_{i})-2\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}k(x_{i},x_{j})p_{i}p_{j},

(3.8)

that is,

\displaystyle\lambda=2\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{m}-2\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{K}\bm{p},

(3.9)

where
\bm{p}={[p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{n}]}^{\top}
is the vector of probabilities,

\bm{m}={[m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{n}]}^{\top}
is the vector of expected values of the kernel function, and
\bm{K}
is the kernel matrix.

Given the value obtained for
\lambda
and the specified constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=1
, we conclude from (
3.6
) that

\displaystyle\bm{K}\bm{p}+\bm{1}\left(\lambda/2\right)=\bm{m},

(3.10)

where
\bm{1}
is an
n
-dimensional column vector of ones. Multiplying equation (
3.10
) by
\bm{p}^{\top}
yields
\lambda
, and since
\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{1}=1
, we find the equations

\displaystyle\bm{K}^{\prime}\bm{p}^{\prime}=\bm{m}^{\prime},

(3.11)

where

\displaystyle\bm{K}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{K}&\bm{1}\\
\bm{1}^{\top}&0\end{pmatrix},\,\,\,\bm{p}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{p}\\
\lambda/2\end{pmatrix}\,\,\,\text{and}\,\,\,\bm{m}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{m}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

(3.12)

The Woodbury matrix identity (or Sherman–Morrison formula) can be applied to compute the inverse

\displaystyle\mathbf{K^{\prime}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{K}^{-1}-\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}{\left(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\right)}^{-1}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}&\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\left(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\right)^{-1}\\
\left(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\right)^{-1}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}&-\left(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\right)^{-1}.\end{pmatrix},

(3.13)

From this the optimal solution for
\bm{p}
in (
3.4
) and

\lambda/2=\frac{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}-1}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}

derive.
Instead of verifying (
3.13
), we validate the result (
3.4
) for the optimal weights derived in Theorem
4
.
We may verify (
3.11
) with respect to the expression for
\bm{p}
from (
3.4
) and by substituting the value of
\lambda
from (
3.9
).
Specifically, we need to demonstrate (
3.12
), that is,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bm{K}\bm{p}+\bm{1}\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{m}-\bm{1}\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{K}\bm{p}\\
\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{p}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{m}\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

To verify the expression, we calculate each term on the left-hand side.
First, note that

\displaystyle\bm{K}\bm{p}=\bm{m}-\frac{\bm{1}\,\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}+\frac{\bm{1}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}},

where we assume (
3.4
).
Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\bm{1}\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{m}=\bm{1}\left(\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}-\frac{\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\,\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}+\frac{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}\right)

and

\displaystyle-\bm{1}\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{K}\bm{p}=-\bm{1}\left(\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}-\frac{\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\,\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}+\frac{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}\right).

By combining these expressions, we observe that the terms involving
\bm{1}
and
\bm{m}
cancel appropriately, leaving

\displaystyle\bm{K}\bm{p}+\bm{1}\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{m}-\bm{1}\bm{p}^{\top}\bm{K}\bm{p}=\bm{m}.

Thus, the left-hand side reduces to
\begin{pmatrix}\bm{m}\\
1\end{pmatrix}
, as required.
This confirms that the optimal weights provide a precise representation of the continuous distribution.
∎

The following corollary characterizes the objective by the optimal probabilities identified above.

Corollary 5
.

For fixed support points
\bm{x}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, the objective with optimal weights is

\displaystyle\MMD(P,P^{n})^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})P(\mathrm{d}\xi)P(\mathrm{d}\xi^{\prime})-\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}+\frac{(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}-1)^{2}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}.

Proof.

Involving the optimal vector
\bm{p}
in (
3.4
) in Theorem
4
from (
2.5
)

\displaystyle{\MMD(P,P^{n})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|P_{k}-P_{k}^{n}\|_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})P(\mathrm{d}\xi)P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

\displaystyle\quad-2\left(\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}-\frac{\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}+\frac{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}\right)\bm{m}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}-\frac{\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}+\frac{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\cdot\bm{K}\cdot\left(\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}-\frac{\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}+\frac{\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}\right).

Collecting terms, it follows that

\displaystyle\MMD(P,P^{n})^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})P(\mathrm{d}\xi)P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

\displaystyle\qquad-(2-1)\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}+(2-1-1)\frac{\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}-(2-1-1)\frac{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}\bm{1}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}}{(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1})^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad-(1+1)\frac{\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}{(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1})^{2}}+\frac{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}{(\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1})^{2}}.

The expression simplifies to

\displaystyle\MMD(P,P^{n})^{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}k(\xi,\xi^{\prime})P(\mathrm{d}\xi)P(\mathrm{d}\xi)

(3.14)

\displaystyle\qquad-\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}\cdot\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{m}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}-2\frac{\bm{m}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}}+\frac{1}{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{K}^{-1}\bm{1}},

giving the final assertion.
∎

4.2

P_{k}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}k(x,y)P(\mathrm{d}y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(\sigma^{2}+\ell^{2})}}e^{-\frac{{(x-\mu)}^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}+\ell^{2})}}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}.

The approximating measure thus has the embedding

P^{n}_{k}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\ell^{2}}}e^{\frac{{(x-x_{i})}^{2}}{2\ell^{2}}}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}.

In the same way, the vector
\bm{m}
is given explicitly via (
3.5
) by the entries

m_{i}=P_{k}(x_{i})=\E_{\xi\sim P}k(x_{i},\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(\sigma^{2}+\ell^{2})}}e^{-\frac{{(x_{i}-\mu)}^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}+\ell^{2})}},\quad i=1,\dots,n,

that is,
m_{i}=P_{k}(x_{i})
.
With that, the approximation quality is available explicitly using (
3.3
) by

\displaystyle\MMD{(P,\mu^{n})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|P_{k}-\mu_{k}^{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(2\sigma^{2}+\ell^{2})}}-\frac{1}{2\pi(\sigma^{2}+\ell^{2})}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}e^{-\frac{(x_{i}-\mu)^{2}+(x_{j}-\mu)^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}+\ell^{2})}}\bm{K}(\bm{x})^{-1}_{ij},

and the optimal weights are
\mu={\bm{K}(\bm{x})}^{-1}\,\bm{m}(\bm{x})
by Proposition
1
.

Deterministic optimization methods can handled this objective, and stochastic gradient methods are not needed any longer for this special case.

The method extends from
\mathbb{R}
to normal distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
analoguously.

.

k^{\prime}(z)=-\frac{z}{\ell^{2}}k(z)\text{ and }P^{\prime}_{k}(x)=-\frac{x-\mu}{\ell^{2}+\sigma^{2}}P_{k}(x)

so that computing the gradient in (

6
Summary

The paper explores quantization in the context of maximum mean discrepancy distance for measures, with a particular focus on probability measures.
In quantization, a given measure is approximated by a simple measure that consists of many support points and corresponding weights. This approach makes the original measure tractable for numerical computations.

Typically, the approximating measure is not itself a probability measure.
To address this, the paper provides a method for determining the optimal weights, ensuring that the approximating measure is a probability measure.
The task of computing the optimal locations, however, presents a non-linear optimization problem. The paper introduces a reformulation on a product space, which allows for the application of stochastic gradient methods.

Future work will involve numerical experiments in higher dimensions, as well as the quantization of stochastic processes. This will link to the theory of nested optimal transport, as elaborated in
Pflug and Pichler [21]
, for example.
