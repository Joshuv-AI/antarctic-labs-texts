Feasibility of whole heart 3D shape reconstruction from sparse echocardiographic views using CT-derived simulations
Authors: Yuan Wang, Hao Xu, Wenliang Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo verify the feasibility of reconstructing whole heart from 2D ultrasound sparse views. This study introduces a series of models for whole-heart reconstruction and conducts a benchmark study to evaluate the impact of pose information on reconstruction accuracy using synthetically generated 2D ultrasound sparse views from CT volumes, with the CT volumes serve as the label. Experiments demonstrated that 3D cardiac reconstruction from only 2D views had limited accuracy (mean Dice similarity coefficient (DSC): ). With precise pose information, four standard echocardiographic views achieved high precision with a mean DSC of . Even non-standard views combined with imprecise pose information significantly outperformed methods lacking pose information (mean DSC: ). Estimation of left ventricular volume further confirmed the superior reliability of reconstructions incorporating pose information. Reconstructing 3D heart from 2D ultrasound views is feasible. Pose information critically improves the accuracy of 3D reconstruction to support downstream clinical tasks, particularly for pathological cases. Introduction

The most widely used imaging modality for cardiac assessment worldwide is ultrasound (US)1, due to its affordability, safety, and higher temporal resolution compared to other modalities. However, most US systems and echo protocols are limited to 2D imaging2, which rely on geometric assumptions to estimate cardiac volumes and function, often leading to imprecise measurements. In contrast, a 3D model of the heart can provide more direct and accurate measurements of cardiac volumes and wall motion, which are crucial to the diagnosis and monitoring of cardiovascular diseases3. In addition, high-resolution 3D cardiac models are essential for virtual surgical planning4 and visualization in educational contexts5. Many research studies focus on 3D cardiac models generated from volumetric imaging modalities, such as cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT)6–12. These modalities offer specific advantages and challenges, including limited availability, high costs, exposure to ionizing radiation, and lower temporal resolution. 3D ultrasound probe represents another potential option, but it still faces challenges such as limited spatial and temporal resolution, poor signal-to-noise ratio, and complex noise characteristics13.

Only a limited number of studies have focused on building 3D heart representations from 2D echocardiographic views14–17. Reconstructing a 3D heart model from 2D cross-sectional views faces several limitations: (1) Most studies on 3D heart reconstruction have focused on reconstructing either a single structure, such as the left ventricle (LV), or a binary volume of multiple structures. The former lacks comprehensive information about the entire heart, while the latter makes it difficult to distinguish closely connected structures, such as the LV and the left ventricular myocardium (LVM). (2) Given the high anatomical and functional similarity of cardiac structures between individuals, accurate alignment of each view is critical to ensure a reliable and personalized 3D cardiac reconstruction. Pose information can facilitate alignment and can be obtained through external tracking devices18–22 with high accuracy, or through deep learning-based pose estimation methods23 that still exhibit substantial errors. It remains unclear to what extent pose information can improve reconstruction accuracy or whether imprecise pose data would compromise the robustness of the reconstructed shape. (3) Existing reconstruction models in the literature used single or multiple cross-sectional slices as input, but there has been little systematic analysis of the contributions of different views and the most efficient view combinations to achieve a high-fidelity 3D anatomical cardiac model.

This work designs a series of models and experiments to address these challenges using synthesized 2D echocardiographic view segmentations with corresponding 3D ground truth, which can also be obtained by segmenting real ultrosound images in clinical practice24–26. Related works

Image-based 3D reconstruction

Image-based 3D reconstruction is a long-established, ill-posed problem defined within the scope of computer vision and graphics. The goal of this process is to recover the 3D structure and geometry of a target object or scene from a set of input images. This task has a wide range of applications in various fields, including robotics, virtual reality, and medical imaging.

Early efforts in reconstructing 3D geometry from 2D imagery largely depended on extracting cues from light and shadow28 or texture29–31. However, the stringent assumptions and specialized knowledge required by these traditional techniques often limit their applicability to real-world scenarios. The emergence of deep learning has significantly advanced this field, with models achieving remarkable results on large-scale datasets and overcoming key challenges in 3D reconstruction from 2D images. A notable early contribution came from Choy et al. in 2016 with 3D-R2N232, which employed a recurrent neural network (RNN) integrated with convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to directly learn the mapping from 2D images to 3D shapes using synthetic data. This approach circumvented the need for manual feature engineering, a departure from traditional pipelines. However, its RNN architecture led to prolonged training and reconstruction quality that varied with input order. Around the same time, Wu et al.33 explored a different path by combining Variational Autoencoders (VAE)34 and Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)35 in the 3D-VAE-GAN model for voxel generation. Despite its promise, this method faced challenges related to training instability and convergence speed, while the VAE component struggled to produce high-fidelity outputs from complex datasets. To mitigate these GAN-related issues, Edward et al.36 introduced 3DIWGAN, a refined training method built upon the 3D-VAE-GAN framework, aimed at stabilizing both the training and generation phases. In the same year, Wu et al.37 developed MarrNet, which leveraged intermediate representations like depth, contour, and surface normals to guide and improve the voxel reconstruction process. A different strategy was taken by Tatarchenko et al.38 with the OGN model, which introduced an octree-based deep convolutional decoder for generating volumetric outputs with enhanced computational and memory efficiency. In 2018, Wu et al.39 pushed the boundaries of single-view reconstruction with ShapeHD, which integrated adversarially learned shape priors with deep generative models. Progress in multi-view reconstruction was marked by Xie et al., who first introduced Pix2Vox in 2019, featuring a context fusion module within an encoder-decoder architecture to overcome the inefficiencies of RNNs40. They subsequently improved upon this with Pix2Vox++ in 2020, incorporating an attention aggregation mechanism to significantly enhance performance on multi-view and high-resolution tasks27. Another attention-based approach, AttSets41, was introduced by Yang et al. for robust feature aggregation across varying viewpoints. More recently, Xiong et al.42 contributed SV3D-CDFF, a single-view voxel reconstruction model that employs cross-domain feature fusion to bridge the gap between different data domains, thereby improving both the global structure and local details of reconstructed shapes.

3D heart reconstruction

3D heart reconstruction is a cornerstone of precision medicine43, providing detailed anatomical information that supports automated assessments of cardiac morphology and function, facilitating early diagnosis and treatment of cardiovascular diseases. Furthermore, high-resolution 3D models of the heart are essential for applications such as physics-based simulations, virtual surgical planning44, and educational visualizations45. In clinical practice, the primary imaging modalities for the heart are computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and echocardiography, making 3D reconstruction from these sources a focal point of research. A comprehensive overview of different 3D representations and AI-based reconstruction techniques can be found in reference46.

Significant work has been done in whole-heart segmentation across modalities. For instance, Yang et al.47 enhanced the auxiliary loss function of a pre-trained C3D network to develop a universal, fully automated fine-grained segmentation model applicable to both CT and MRI. Meanwhile, Yu et al.48 designed a 3D fractal network with a self-similar architecture, incorporating deep supervision to address the vanishing gradient problem, specifically for segmenting the whole heart and great vessels from volumetric MRI. In a different vein, Xu et al.49 proposed a multi-stage model to generate new anatomical labels from existing segmentation outputs. To overcome the inefficiency of processing entire volumes when only a small region of interest exists, cascade strategies have been explored. A notable example is CFUN by Xu et al.50, a fully automated method that combined Faster R-CNN with 3D U-Net, improving segmentation accuracy through a Region Proposal Network (RPN). Subsequent work51 further refined CFUN by modifying its loss function and network architecture, leading to better convergence and accuracy. Voxel-based approaches, however, can face challenges with surface reconstruction and topological consistency. To address this, surface-based methods have been developed. For example, Yang et al.52 introduced a novel surface loss based on current representations to optimize mesh topology, yielding whole-heart reconstructions with correct anatomical topology. Similarly, Kong et al.8 utilized graph convolutional networks to predict vertex deformations on a predefined mesh template, successfully reconstructing multiple cardiac structures with consistent topology. Efforts have also focused on reconstruction from sparse data, particularly from MRI. Chen et al.53 developed MR-Net, a network capable of accurate 3D mesh reconstruction from sparse labels. Sinclair et al.54 proposed a joint learning framework, Atlas-ISTN, for segmentation and registration, which constructs template maps and generates topologically consistent segmentations via deformation fields. Xu et al.11 contributed LC-U-Net, a label completion network designed to reconstruct the full heart from sparse MRI slice labels, and later12 investigated combinations of slice shifting and spatial transformation networks to optimize motion compensation and improve segmentation accuracy.

Research specifically focused on constructing 3D heart models from 2D echocardiography is comparatively limited. Early work by Krishnan et al.14 demonstrated 3D reconstruction of the left ventricular (LV) endocardial surface from a limited set of aligned porcine echocardiographic views. Stojanovski et al.15 adapted the Pix2Vox40 architecture to develop a pipeline for reconstructing 3D cardiac anatomy from standard 2D views. A more advanced generative framework was introduced by Laumer et al.16, capable of inferring high-resolution, personalized 4D (3D + time) surface meshes from 2D echocardiography videos. Their most recent work17 combines CNNs and graph neural networks (GNNs) to generate a personalized 4D mesh of the LV blood pool, achieving high temporal resolution.

All methods in this study are carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. An overview of the proposed pipeline is presented in Fig. 1. To investigate the feasibility of 3D heart shape reconstruction from 2D slices, 2D echocardiographic views are synthesized by slicing 3D segmentations from CT images, which enables the use of 3D ground truth for evaluation. C-Pix2Vox and CE-Pix2Vox accept only 2D slices as input. UNet3D-A and UNet3D-M share the same model structure and accept a sparse volume aligned with 2D slices and corresponding pose information as input. The key distinction between UNet3D-A and UNet3D-M lies in the training and testing datasets: UNet3D-A is trained using sparse volumes constructed with precise pose information, while UNet3D-M is trained with imprecise pose information, leading to misalignment of slices in the sparse volumes. Networks

Channel-wise Pix2vox

The general Pix2Vox++27 network architecture consists of parallel encoder and decoder branches for each input view. The output is then passed through the merger and refiner blocks. The decoder, in particular, is both memory- and computationally intensive due to the 3D deconvolutional kernels. Stojanovski et al.15 introduced E-Pix2Vox by incorporating a 3D convolution at the end of the encoder module along the input image dimension, significantly reducing both memory consumption and computational cost (as shown by the green arrows in Fig. 2). However, both models treat all structures as a single shape and produce a binary volume, which complicates subsequent analysis, especially when distinguishing between closely connected different intracardiac structures.

Overview of the proposed channel-wise Pix2Vox networks. n is the number of input echo views and N is the size of the output volume. The black arrows show the framework of the Pix2Vox++ network27. The green arrows indicate the modifications made by E-Pix2Vox15 to the original network architecture. The network proposed in this paper changes the output channels of the decoder, merger, and refiner from 1 to the number of categories C (shown in red font).

Therefore, this study introduces a subtle revision of Pix2Vox++ and E-Pix2Vox by adjusting the number of output channels from 1 to C (the number of categories, including background and intracardiac structures) in the decoder, merger, and refiner stages. This revised approach, referred to as channel-wise Pix2Vox++/E-Pix2Vox (C-Pix2Vox/CE-Pix2Vox), enables the reconstruction of various intracardiac structures instead of just a binary volume. As shown in Fig. 2 (in red font), only the first and last convolutional layers of each module are modified, ensuring that no significant additional computational cost is introduced. Reference15 has demonstrated the effectiveness of Pix2Vox-based models in reconstructing 3D heart shapes, thus C-Pix2Vox and CE-Pix2Vox are used as the baseline for reconstructive models without pose information.

Volumetric networks

An effective method for correlating pose information with 2D slices and integrating it into a model is to interpolate multiple slices into a 3D volume based on their spatial alignment parameters. Therefore, the model that incorporates pose information is a 3D U-Net, with both the input and output represented as grid volumes of size voxels. The output, consisting of C channels, represents the probability of each voxel belonging to the background and cardiac structures. The network includes three pairs of max-pooling and deconvolution layers, each with a stride of , resulting in four different resolutions. The number of convolutional kernels are (16, 32), (32, 64), (64, 128), (128, 256) for the encoder and bottle neck, and (128, 128), (64, 64), (32, C) for the decoder.

To investigate the impact of pose precision on reconstruction accuracy, the same model architecture is trained separately with precise and imprecise pose information, denoted as UNet3D-A and UNet3D-M, respectively. Details of the training and test datasets are provided in Section 3.2.

Dataset

Supervised learning requires paired data for training and validation of the designed network. However, obtaining such paired datasets is often challenging in practice. textcolorredThis study utilizes a healthy public dataset and a non-healthy private dataset to conduct the experiments.

The public dataset consists of 1,000 synthetic cardiac meshes generated by Stojanovski et al.15. This cohort is created by adjusting the weights of the modes that explain of the variance in shape within a statistical shape model (SSM)55, designed for electro-mechanical simulations. The slicing process of the echo views follows the methodology outlined in15. The 3D ground truth in the reference is a binary occupancy volume, whereas in this study, it includes labels for various cardiac structures. Additionally, labels representing particularly small volumes are excluded, leaving only four labels: left ventricle myocardium (LVM), right ventricle myocardium (RVM), left atrium myocardium (LAM), and right atrium myocardium (RAM).

The private dataset consists of 3D heart volumes, which are segmentations from high-resolution clinical Coronary Computed Tomography Angiography (CCTA) with suspected coronary artery disease, which often presents with significant geometric remodeling of the left ventricular morphology. The 2D echocardiographic views are extracted from these 3D volumes to simulate the clinical scanning positions of the probe. Further details of the dataset are provided in the following sections.

CT images and segmentations

This study includes a total of 606 CT volumes at different phases from 265 anonymized patients with suspected coronary artery disease, all sourced from Jinhua Municipal Central Hospital, Zhejiang, China. The volumetric images are segmented using the network proposed by Xu et al.56. The segmentation results are assessed and validated by a sonographer with over five years of experience. The volume masks encompass ten cardiac structures: LV, LVM, right ventricle (RV), left atrium (LA), right atrium (RA), ascending aorta (AA), pulmonary artery, left vein, right vein, and appendage components. Since the last four labels are either not clearly visible or typically not annotated in echocardiograms, only the first six cardiac structures are retained in this study.

Cardiac coordinate system

To generate spatially consistent volume data of the ground truth, a cardiac coordinate system is defined using the A4C and A2C views. The axes are defined as follows: 1) the longitudinal axis extends from the apex to the center of the mitral valve, corresponding to the line of intersection between the two views, i.e., the long axis of the LV; 2) the transverse axis is perpendicular to the longitudinal axis within the apical four-chamber plane; 3) the cross product of the first two vectors defines the third axis. The volume is determined by the bounding box centered on the centroid of the 3D segmentation labels. It is then expanded by along all three axes. Finally, the volume is normalized to a resolution of voxels using nearest-neighbor interpolation. The input sparse volume data for UNet3D-A and UNet3D-M is generated in a similar manner.

Deviation and misalignment simulation

In clinical practice, it is challenging to ensure that all collected echocardiograms are from standard views. To simulate such deviations, rotational and translational variations are introduced for the scanning range of each view. The rotational deviation is implemented by sequentially rotating around the and axes, with both rotations following a uniform distribution within . The translational deviation follows a uniform distribution within voxels along each of the three coordinate axes. This process is used for data augmentation during model training for both UNet3D-A and UNet3D-M.

To investigate the impact of imprecise pose information on the robustness of reconstruction, misalignment simulation is applied by rotating and translating slices following the same distributions as the deviations. This intentionally introduces misalignment when combining multiple views and is applied only in the training and testing of UNet3D-M.

It is important to distinguish between deviation and misalignment. Deviation alters the scanning plane away from the standard view while maintaining precise pose information. In contrast, misalignment slices the sectional view and then introduces errors into its pose information. To assess the robustness of UNet3D-M, 10 imprecise test datasets are constructed, each generated with different random seeds for deviation and misalignment.

Results

Implementation details

The dataset is divided into training, validation, and testing subsets with a ratio of 8:1:1. Unless otherwise specified, experiments are conducted using four standard echocardiographic views (A4C, A2C, PLAX, and PSAX-AV), as these views are essential for clinical archiving and represent the most widely adopted imaging protocol in routine practice. Pix2Vox-based models are trained with standard slices and augmented with random cropping. UNet3D-A is augmented by deviation and tested with standard views. UNet3D-M is trained with augmentations of deviation and motion and is evaluated on 10 misaligned test datasets. The input size for Pix2Vox-based models is , where n represents the number of echo views. The input size for the UNet3D-based model is . The reconstruction outputs of all models are . Model deployment and training are carried out in Python using the PyTorch deep learning library on a server equipped with two NVIDIA Tesla P40 GPUs, each with 24GB of RAM. Cross-entropy is chosen as the loss function, and the Adam optimizer57 is employed with an initial learning rate of 0.001, using a cosine annealing strategy for learning rate decay. Networks are trained for 200 epochs with a batch size of 4. Unless otherwise specified, the data in the experimental result tables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (std), which indicates the accuracy and robustness of the models respectively.

When precise pose information of standard echo views is provided, UNet3D-A shows a significant improvement in reconstruction accuracy, achieving the highest mean DSC of , surpassing CE-Pix2Vox by . However, given that clinicians do not always have access to perfectly standard echo views and pose information may contain errors, misaligned sparse volumes (as in UNet3D-M) lead to a decrease in reconstruction accuracy (mean DSC: ). Despite this, UNet3D-M still outperforms methods without pose information and exhibits robust performance in handling non-standard views and pose errors, as evidenced by its smaller standard deviation (DSC: 1.49, HD95: 0.06, ASSD: 0.08) compared to C-Pix2Vox and CE-Pix2Vox. Among all methods, reconstruction of RAM poses the greatest challenges, as indicated by the significantly lower DSC and higher ASSD values.

Furthermore, we observe notable variability across atrial and ventricular structures, with the right atrial myocardium (RAM) consistently exhibiting the lowest accuracy and highest error metrics. We attribute the inferior performance primarily to the inherent geometric complexity of the atria. Compared to the ventricles, which have relatively thick walls and more regular, ellipsoidal shapes, the right atrium possesses a thin, highly irregular wall with regions of high curvature and intricate connections to major vessels (e.g., superior and inferior vena cava). These anatomical features make precise segmentation challenging, as the model must delineate fine, convoluted boundaries that are prone to partial volume effects and ambiguous edges in medical images. Such geometry-induced difficulties are a well-recognized challenge in image segmentation tasks, particularly for thin-walled structures.

In clinical practice, special attention is also given to these abnormal cases to assess cardiac function and inform diagnosis and treatment. An intuitive explanation for how pose information improves the accuracy of 3D reconstruction is as follows: comparing sparse slice information to the backbone of a heart, only when the backbone is placed in the correct pose can the geometric structure of the heart be accurately represented. This, in turn, enables more precise inference of unobserved regions, with errors accumulating throughout this process. A visualization of this process is shown in Fig. 5. The LV, akin to the root or backbone of the AA, guides the reconstruction of the AA. Consequently, the UNet3D model, which achieves a more accurate reconstruction of the LV at the connection site, also reconstructs the AA more precisely. Conversely, the CE-Pix2Vox model exhibits increased reconstruction errors for the AA. This phenomenon is similarly reflected in other anatomical regions.

Left ventricular volume evaluation

In echocardiography-based cardiac assessment, left ventricular volume is a critical parameter for evaluating cardiac function, particularly the left ventricular ejection fraction, which is derived from end-systolic and end-diastolic volumes. Clinicians typically rely on their experience to mentally visualize the 3D structure of the heart, with left ventricular volume assessment based on assumptions about its geometric shape. 3D heart reconstruction eliminates the need for such assumptions. To assess the reconstruction accuracy for clinical applications, the LV volume estimation error is compared across the conventional Area-Length method (A-L), the Biplane Simpson method (B-Simpson), and the predicted 3D reconstruction in this study. Though A-L and B-Simpson methods suffers from real-world ultrasound artifacts in clinic, their underlying geometric assumptions regarding LV shape remain valid. Since all methods in this study use high signal-to-noise ratio CT slices as input, this comparison is fair. The estimation errors are evaluated using mean absolute error (MAE) and mean signed error (MSE). MAE reflects the precision of volume estimation, while MSE indicates whether the model tends to overestimate (positive value) or underestimate (negative value). Given that the 10 test datasets are constructed with random seeds, the error in UNet3D-M is not expected to be biased, and thus, UNet3D-M is evaluated across all 10 datasets. This experiment is conducted exclusively on the CCTA dataset, as the SSM dataset lacks LV labels. The results are presented in Table 5.

Discussion

This work presents a pipeline that explores the impact of pose information on 3D cardiac reconstruction and evaluates the contribution of each echocardiographic view. First, a method is introduced to synthesize 2D echo views with 3D volumetric ground truth, simulating the scanning process of a curvilinear probe in a clinical setting. Next, the C-Pix2Vox and CE-Pix2Vox networks are proposed, enabling whole-heart reconstruction rather than binary volume output. Subsequently, precise pose information is integrated into the echo views, demonstrating a significant performance improvement with UNet3D-A. The introduction of disturbances to pose information for motion simulation further highlights the model’s robustness under conditions of imprecise pose information. Finally, the reconstruction performance of different view combinations is systematically analyzed.

The results in Table 3 demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed channel-wise networks. Both C-Pix2Vox and CE-Pix2Vox are capable of reconstructing the entire heart with multi-label output, rather than a binary volume, without sacrificing reconstruction precision. Experiments conducted on a public dataset with healthy cases and a private dataset with unhealthy cases (Tables 3 and 4) demonstrate that a human heart can be reconstructed with reasonable accuracy from four standard 2D anatomical views, with or without pose information. CE-Pix2Vox outperforms C-Pix2Vox on both datasets. It is hypothesized that the simple geometry of the heart makes a more compact model less prone to overfitting. UNet3D-A achieves a significant improvement in mean DSC compared to CE-Pix2Vox, with the improvement being more pronounced in the CCTA dataset (SSM: vs. CCTA: ). This suggests that pose information plays a critical role in cardiac reconstruction accuracy, particularly when dealing with abnormal or unhealthy hearts. This is intuitively logical, as pose information inherently encodes the alignment of echo views, allowing the model to focus on the shape completion task from sparse volumetric data. Furthermore, the alignment provides more detailed information regarding the intersections of different views. In contrast, pose-agnostic models must simultaneously address pose estimation and shape completion tasks. The high reconstruction accuracy of UNet3D-A demonstrates that when echocardiographic views are sufficiently standardized and accompanied by accurate pose information, only four sparse echo views are required to reconstruct highly accurate cardiac anatomical structures. While non-standard echo views and imprecise pose information reduce reconstruction accuracy, performance remains superior to that of Pix2Vox-based models, further highlighting the critical importance of pose information. The reconstruction of AA and LVM presents additional challenges due to the unique geometry of the LVM and the fact that only a single view (PLAX) passes through the AA.

Table 5 indicates that models without pose information do not lead to significant improvements in LV volume estimation, limiting their clinical utility. In contrast, UNet3D-A and UNet3D-M demonstrate superior reliability. Table 6 further confirms that the results of UNet3D-A and UNet3D-M are closer to the ground truth. Table 7 highlights that different combinations of echo views offer distinct advantages in cardiac reconstruction. The increase in the number of views inherently provides complementary spatial information, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the reconstruction. While the four most commonly used standard views are sufficient to reconstruct a 3D cardiac shape with reasonable accuracy, adding additional planes results in a modest improvement.

The key contribution of this paper is the design of a series of models and training protocols aimed at investigating the impact of pose information on 3D cardiac reconstruction using synthetic data that simulates the acquisition of echocardiographic views in a clinical setting. However, this research has limitations, and the model and experiments leave considerable room for further exploration.

The echo-view segmentation masks in this study are derived from 3D objects (mesh or CT segmentations) rather than directly from ultrasound data. This is currently challenging due to the lack of corresponding 3D heart ground truth labels in clinical ultrasound images. Future improvements could be achieved through methods such as ultrasound simulation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that 3D cardiac reconstruction from only standard 2D views has limited accuracy and reliability, but adding even not that precise pose information, four standardard echo views can adequately reconstruct an accurate 3D heart shape to support downstream tasks in clinical practice. Precise pose information is particularly important when reconstructing unhealthy hearts. This study indicates a potential future research direction to improve the accuracy of personalized 3D heart reconstruction by acquiring echo view pose information, thereby aiding clinical diagnosis.
