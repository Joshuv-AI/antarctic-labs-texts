Genetic diversity and geographic distribution of Fasciola species from cattle in eight provinces of South Africa
Authors: Sophy Nukeri, Mokgadi Pulane Malatji, Mita Eva Sengupta, Birgitte Vennervald, Anna-Sofie Stensgaard, Mamohale Chaisi, Samson Mukaratirwa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFasciolosis, caused by Fasciola hepatica and Fasciola gigantica, is a globally significant parasitic disease affecting livestock and humans. Both species exist in South Africa, however, information on their geographical distribution and genetic diversity is limited. This study investigated the genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationships of Fasciola species in South Africa using Pepck, COI and ITS markers. Two hundred and seven Fasciola spp. specimens analysed in this study showed that 74.4% of isolates identified as F. hepatica and 25.6% as F. gigantica based on the Pepck gene. The geographical distribution revealed that F. hepatica occurred in all surveyed provinces but was dominant in the temperate zones of South Africa. Fasciola gigantica was confined to subtropical zones, with co-occurrence observed in Limpopo, Mpumalanga, and KwaZulu-Natal provinces. The COI and ITS genes in both Fasciola species generated fourteen and eight haplotypes respectively, with low to moderate haplotype diversity (Hd = 0.287 – 0.509 for COI and 0.056 – 0.239 for ITS) and low nucleotide diversity (Pi = 0.00085 – 0.00284 for COI and 0.00024 – 0.00034 for ITS), suggesting recent population diversification between the two species. The study samples yielded six haplotypes based on COI, with three haplotypes comprising most samples i.e. Hap-1 (F. gigantica) and Hap-5 and Hap-6 (F. hepatica). With ITS, two haplotypes represented most samples, i.e. Hap-1 (F. gigantica) and Hap-3 (F. hepatica). There was no observed unique distribution of haplotypes based on provinces or agro-ecological climatic zones for both markers. Phylogenetic analysis of both COI and ITS genes confirmed the grouping of the haplotypes and reiterated their relatedness. However, one isolate identified as F. gigantica in COI and with F. hepatica in ITS based on BLAST and further formed haplotypes with F. gigantica and F. hepatica haplogroups for COI and ITS, respectively. This suspected hybrid Fasciola form was from an animal with a mixed infection. These findings highlight the complex genetic structure of Fasciola spp. populations in South Africa and provide new insights into their epidemiology, with implications for targeted control strategies and further investigation of cryptic/hybrid lineages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25952-y.Subject terms: PCR-based techniques, Genetic markers, Haplotypes

Introduction

Fasciola hepatica (Linnaeus, 1758) and F. gigantica (Cobbold, 1856) are digenean parasites implicated in the aetiology of fasciolosis, an important parasitic disease which affects the livestock industry and public health globally1–3. Fasciola hepatica is widely distributed and occurs mainly in temperate areas while F. gigantica is restricted to tropical zones of Africa and Asia4,5. In some areas where both species coexist, parthenogenic/hybrid forms have been reported due to hybridisation between F. hepatica and F. gigantica 5. These forms are thought to be a third causative agent of the disease6–8. To date, this population has been reported from various Asian countries such as Vietnam9,10, China11, Japan12, Korea13,14, Bangladesh15, Nepal16, and Myanmar17 as well as from some regions of Africa such as Egypt18,19 and Chad20.

Fasciola species have been traditionally identified based on fluke morphometrics21, however, parthenogenic Fasciola flukes show intermediate morphological measurements between F. hepatica and F. gigantica, often making it difficult to distinguish between F. hepatica and F. gigantica22. DNA sequences of the nuclear ribosomal internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1), ITS2, and 28S rRNA gene23–25 and mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (COI) and/or NADH dehydrogenase 1 (ND1)12,26 have been widely used to distinguish F. hepatica and F. gigantica. However, the use of only one of these markers cannot distinguish hybrid Fasciola forms from F. hepatica and F. gigantica, since these flukes could display ITS-1 sequences identical to F. hepatica and mitochondrial NDI sequences identical to F. gigantica27. Recently, there has been an introduction and wider use of new and targeted primers such as phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (Pepck), DNA polymerase delta (pold) and fatty acid binding protein type I (FABP) gene markers to distinguish parthenogenic Fasciola flukes based on the nuclear DNA region28–30. The introduction of these genetic markers has facilitated the accurate identification of Fasciola species and hybrid forms in endemic regions31–33.

To date, data on the molecular identity of Fasciola isolates from definitive hosts in South Africa is scanty and localised. Reported studies solely identified Fasciola spp. based on ITS and COI markers3,34,35 and identified overlapping distribution of Fasciola species in Mpumalanga36 and KwaZulu-Natal provinces3,34. Despite this, there has been no attempt to determine whether intermediate forms or hybrids of Fasciola33,37 exist in South Africa by utilising reliable genetic markers in localities where the two species co-occur. Therefore, to precisely distinguish F. hepatica and F. gigantica, this study aimed to characterise Fasciola spp. isolates collected from cattle slaughtered at abattoirs located in eight South African provinces, using ITS, COI and Pepck gene markers. This study reports the first overlapping distribution of both Fasciola species in cattle in the Limpopo province, where infections of both species occurred within the same farm, which contrasts with the Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal provinces, where the two species co-occurred in the same region but on different farms.

Results

Characterisation of Fasciola spp. based on Pepck Multiplex PCR

Molecular characterisation of Fasciola spp. was conducted on extracted DNA from 207 specimens collected at abattoirs in eight South African provinces and analysed with the Pepck marker. This showed 74.4% (154/207) of isolates with bands at 241 bp consistent with F. hepatica, and these were from Limpopo (3/154), Mpumalanga (23/154), Gauteng (83/154), North West (6/154), Free State (14/154), KwaZulu-Natal (6/154), Eastern Cape (12/154) and Northern Cape (7/154) provinces. For F. gigantica, 25.6% (53/207) isolates amplified at 510 bp and were from Limpopo (46/53), Mpumalanga (5/53), KwaZulu-Natal (1/53), and the positive F. gigantica control from Zimbabwe (1/53) (Supplementary Fig. S1). None of the processed specimens generated both bands, thus indicating that no specimen was identified as Fasciola hybrid form. All specimens originating from the Eastern Cape, Free State, Gauteng, Northern Cape and North West provinces were identified as F. hepatica, while Limpopo, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal provinces recorded both F. hepatica and F. gigantica, although F. gigantica was predominantly recorded in the Limpopo province (Fig. 1).

Haplotype and phylogenetic analysis based on the COI gene

Of the 207 Fasciola isolates analysed with the Pepck marker, only sequences from 197 specimens (420 bp) formed part of the COI analysis as sequencing of the remaining ten isolates failed or the isolates produced significantly shorter sequences than the expected size and were excluded from further analysis. These were from Limpopo (47/197), Mpumalanga (26/197), Gauteng (79/197), North West (6/197), Free State (14/197), KwaZulu-Natal (7/197), Eastern Cape (7/197), Northern Cape (10/197) provinces and Zimbabwe (1/197). The sequences generated in this study were submitted to GenBank under the accession numbers PQ772295-PQ772347 and PQ772872-PQ773025.

Haplotype analysis was performed on 234 COI sequences, where 197 sequences were from this study and 37 were published sequences obtained from GenBank (details on accession numbers are as shown in Supplementary Table S1). The analysis yielded 14 haplotypes (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 2), of which six consisted of our samples (Fig. 2), which confirmed the Pepck species identification, and 8 consisted of GenBank sequences. Fasciola gigantica formed seven haplotypes, of which three consisted of isolates from this study. Haplotype Hap-1 comprised the most sequences, consisting of isolates from Limpopo, Mpumalanga, KwaZulu-Natal provinces, and the positive control from Zimbabwe, and the GenBank sequences from South Africa and Zimbabwe. This haplotype is separated from haplotype Hap-2 and Hap-3, which also consisted of samples from this study by one mutation step at positions 55 and 228, respectively (Supplementary Table S1), indicating that they share a common ancestor. Compared to F. hepatica haplotypes, the F. gigantica haplotypes were relatively closely related to the Fasciola sp. haplotype (Hap-12), which may indicate either shared ancestry or possible hybridisation events involving F. gigantica.

Map of South Africa showing the distribution of Fasciola spp. haplotypes based on COI and ITS genetic markers.

Fasciola hepatica formed five haplotypes, of which three haplotypes included a combination of isolates from this study and those from GenBank, while two haplotypes comprised GenBank sequences only (details of accession numbers as given in Supplementary Table S1). Specimens from haplotype Hap-5 were from the Gauteng, Mpumalanga, Limpopo, Eastern Cape, Northern Cape, North West and Free-State provinces. In addition to these were GenBank sequences from South Africa, Spain, Iran and Zimbabwe. Haplotype Hap-6 constituted isolates from the Gauteng, Mpumalanga, Northern Cape, North West, Free State, Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal provinces and GenBank sequences from Austria, Algeria, Iraq, South Africa, Uruguay, Tunisia, Peru, Egypt and Brazil. Haplotype Hap-4, which consisted of an isolate from the Eastern Cape province, was more closely related to haplotype Hap-5 than Hap-6, meaning the two share the most recent common ancestor. These two haplotypes (Hap-4 and Hap-5) were separated by two mutational steps at positions 20 and 185 (Table 1) which is indicative of a distant relation.

Haplotype and phylogenetic analysis based on the ITS gene

The haplotype analysis was performed on 200 ITS sequences, where only 170 (695 bp) sequences were from this study and 30 were from the GenBank (details on accession numbers as given in Supplementary Table S3). The remaining thirty-seven specimens failed sequencing despite several attempts by various sequencing facilities. The study sequences were from Limpopo (25/170), Mpumalanga (14/170), Gauteng (71/170), North West (5/170), Free State (12/170), KwaZulu-Natal (7/170), Eastern Cape (17/170) and Northern Cape (9/170) provinces. The sequences were submitted to GenBank under the accession numbers PQ722571–PQ722739. Haplotype analysis yielded eight haplotypes (Supplementary Table S3, Fig. 4a), four of which consisted of study samples. Fasciola gigantica sequences from the study formed two haplotypes; the novel haplotype Hap-1 included isolates from all provinces where F. gigantica was recorded in this study (Limpopo, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal), the positive control from Zimbabwe, and the GenBank sequences from Algeria, Iran, Burkina Faso, South Africa, Chad, India, Kenya and Egypt. Haplotype Hap-2 consisted of isolates exclusively from the Limpopo province, and these two haplotypes were separated by one mutational step at position 654 (Table 2).

Fasciola hepatica also formed two haplotypes, and haplotype Hap-3 comprised most of the study sequences, and consisted of samples from Gauteng, Mpumalanga, Limpopo, Eastern Cape, Northern Cape, North West, Free State, KwaZulu-Natal and GenBank sequences from Iran, China, Switzerland, South Africa, Andorra, Tunisia, Algeria, Bolivia, Egypt and China (Supplementary Table S3). This haplotype also included isolate LPVN-2 identified as F. hepatica by ITS, but formed haplotype (Hap-2) with F. gigantica isolates with COI (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Haplotype Hap-4 consisted of sequences from the Free State and Gauteng provinces, which separated from haplotype Hap-3 by three mutational steps at positions 30, 31 and 32 (Table 2). Compared to COI, the ITS Fasciola spp. haplotypes Hap-7 (China) and Hap-8 (Zimbabwe) were closely related to both F. gigantica and F. hepatica, respectively.

The ITS phylogenetic tree formed two supported monophyletic clades, with one representing F. gigantica and Fasciola sp., and the other representing F. hepatica (Figure 4b). The specimens showed low variability compared to the COI gene, with genetic distances ranging between 0.1–1.2% (Supplementary Table S4). The F. gigantica haplotypes confirmed the close relationship with Fasciola sp. from China (Hap-7) (Supplementary Table S4; Fig. 4b). However, Fasciola sp. (haplotype Hap-8) from Zimbabwe showed a distinct clade from both F. gigantica and F. hepatica (Fig. 4b) but showed a genetic distance of 0.6% from F. hepatica, which was lower than the 0.9% with F. gigantica (Supplementary Table S4).

Genetic diversity between F. hepatica and F. gigantica based on the COI and ITS genes

In addition to the above analyses on genetic diversity, neutral indices, including Tajima’s D and Fu’s Fs, were used to assess nucleotide diversity and population expansion. The COI gene indicated that the overall haplotype diversity was higher with F. hepatica (0.509) compared to F. gigantica (0.287), which is an indication of varied genetic diversity between the two species. Conversely, the ITS marker generated the highest haplotype diversity with F. gigantica (0.239) and the lowest with F. hepatica (0.056) (Table 3). Provincially, Mpumalanga province recorded the lowest COI haplotype diversity (0.485) and the highest diversity (0.60) was noted in North West province (Table 4). However, based on the ITS, the Free State province yielded the highest haplotype diversity (0.385), and the Gauteng province recorded the lowest (0.028) (Table 5). The nucleotide diversity ranged from 0.00085–0.00284 with COI and 0.00024–0.00034 with ITS, and the highest nucleotide diversity was recorded in F. hepatica with COI and F. gigantica with ITS (Table 3). The highest COI nucleotide diversity for F. hepatica was recorded in the North West province (0.00338) and the lowest in the Mpumalanga province (0.00273) (Table 4). However, the Free State province yielded the highest ITS nucleotide diversity (0.00166) and the Gauteng province the lowest (0.00012) (Table 5). Fasciola hepatica showed a significant value of 2.57330 (p<0.05) on Tajima’s D test, while a negative Tajima’s D (− 0.58376) was obtained for F. gigantica based on COI. Both F. hepatica (− 1.16975) and F. gigantica (− 0.08237) populations yielded negative Tajima’s D values, but these were not statistically significant. The Fu’s Fs test showed values that ranged from − 0.625 to 3.075 for COI and 0.388 to 0.393 for ITS (Table 3). Provincially, the COI neutrality tests yielded positive values in all provinces, whereas in ITS the Gauteng province yielded negative values for both Tajima’s D and Fu’s Fs (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

Results of this study successfully identified F. hepatica and F. gigantica based on the three genetic markers (Pepck, COI, ITS). Furthermore, the results reiterated that F. hepatica is the most widely distributed and common species in South Africa and occurred in all sampled provinces. The species was recorded exclusively in the Eastern Cape, Free State, Gauteng, Northern Cape, and North West provinces. The presence of F. gigantica in Limpopo, Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal provinces highlighted the co-occurrence of the two Fasciola species, which was previously reported in cattle in KwaZulu- Natal and Mpumalanga3,34,36.

The distinct haplotypes for F. gigantica further illustrate the population structure of these species. Furthermore, the haplotype structure of the study samples corresponded with previous studies, whereby two novel ITS haplotypes of both F. hepatica and F. gigantica were reported, but the COI gene showed more diversification, with three or four novel haplotypes reported in those studies3,34. However, the haplotype diversity observed for F. hepatica in COI (Hd = 0.509) was moderate, while diversity for F. gigantica in ITS (Hd = 0.239) was lower than previously reported3,34,36. Low haplotype and nucleotide diversity are indicative of recent population bottlenecks or founder effects36 which might relate to the fact that the flukes are not indigenous to South Africa but have rather spread from Eurasia5. The reason further relates to the fact that Fasciola spp. have an adaptation to their environment, including the presence of intermediate hosts and gene flow across the species’ ranges. Positive Fu’s Fs values in both ITS and COI for F. hepatica suggest the presence of rare haplotypes and potential population expansion38. Additionally, a significantly positive Tajima’s D (2.57330) in the COI gene of F. hepatica suggests non-neutral evolution, likely due to balancing selection39. In contrast, negative Tajima’s D values in F. gigantica may reflect that this species’ population is increasing despite being recorded in three study provinces. These patterns highlight differing evolutionary histories and demographic processes in the two species. For the two markers, Fu’s Fs values for COI included negative values whilst for ITS the values were positive. This likely reflects distinct evolutionary pressures on mitochondrial and nuclear genomes. Mitochondrial COI, due to its higher mutation rate, maternal inheritance, and lack of recombination, is more sensitive to recent demographic events. In contrast, nuclear ITS may reflect a more stable or structured population history. Similar patterns have been reported in parasitic nematodes, where COI exhibited significantly higher variability, while ITS remained more conserved40.

Provincial data highlighted intraspecific variation in F. hepatica populations from the Limpopo, KwaZulu-Natal, Free State and Eastern Cape provinces and in F. gigantica from the Mpumalanga province based on the COI gene. Moreover, the ITS gene showed no polymorphism in F. hepatica populations from the Eastern Cape, Mpumalanga, North West, KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo and Northern Cape provinces, and in F. gigantica from the Mpumalanga province. This lack of genetic variation within populations may have contributed to the observed overall low haplotype and nucleotide diversities. Moreover, the overall low nucleotide diversity values in addition to moderate haplotype diversity values indicate a high number of closely related haplotypes thus suggesting a possible recent population expansion41. The evidence of cattle movement as highlighted in a previous study42 between the provinces may also be a reason for the low diversity.

Although we did not observe any unique haplotypes depicting geographically isolated lineages, the geographical distribution of the two Fasciola species has reiterated the wider geographical expansion of F. hepatica shifting to warmer areas, compared to the more temperate regions which the species has been known to inhabit5. The geographical distribution of the two species aligns with the ecological niches and distribution of their snail intermediate hosts (IHs) in South Africa37. It also further highlights the effects of cattle movement from one province to another in introducing the species to new areas as highlighted by Nukeri et al.42. The movement of cattle between provinces facilitates the spread of Fasciola species by introducing infected animals into new regions. If suitable intermediate snail hosts are present, the parasite can establish and reproduce locally, expanding its geographical distribution. Fasciola hepatica was shown to be widespread across all provinces, reflecting the presence of its snail IHs, Galba truncatula and Pseudosuccinea columella, which were previously reported to occur in all South African provinces except the Northern Cape43. Some of these provinces have recent reports of the occurrence of the invasive snail species P. columella35,44,45, which coincided with the distribution of F. hepatica, and in some provinces, the overlapping distribution of the two species3.

In the current study, F. gigantica was primarily detected in warmer provinces such as Limpopo, Mpumalanga, and KwaZulu-Natal, although its main snail IH, Radix natalensis, occurs in all nine provinces of South Africa43. Furthermore, these provinces have also highlighted the overlapping distribution of both F. hepatica and F. gigantica, which is consistent with the distribution of G. truncatula and R. natalensis43. Furthermore, P. columella has also been presumed to be responsible for the secondary spread and possibly the overlapping distribution of the Fasciola species, considering that it has been confirmed to transmit F. gigantica in Eastern Cape and KwaZulu-Natal provinces35and F. hepatica in Gauteng province45. Taking into consideration the invasive nature of P. columella, the continual dispersal of F. hepatica, F. gigantica and potentially Fasciola hybrids should be expected, which will further complicate the epidemiology of fasciolosis in South Africa.

Unlike the novel haplotypes, COI haplotype Hap-4 and ITS Hap-2 were notably unique to the Eastern Cape and Limpopo provinces, respectively, potentially representing geographically isolated lineages. This observation is consistent with other studies, which reported the emergence of localised haplotypes in response to limited gene flow due to geographical barriers or seasonal extinctions and recolonisations46 of the intermediate snail host. Additionally, these findings highlight how host snail population dynamics, including extinction and recolonisation, may shape the genetic structure of parasitic populations in response to seasonal variations and founder effects, and provide support to current models of host-parasite co-evolution47.

The Pepck marker did not detect hybrids in this study, which is not unusual as it has previously been associated with misidentification errors29. However, one isolate (LPVN2) was identified as F. gigantica based on the COI marker and as F. hepatica according to the ITS marker, suggesting the potential presence of an intermediate Fasciola form. This corresponds with the classification of Fasciola hybrid forms as one of the flukes that could display ITS-1 sequence identical to F. hepatica and mitochondrial NDI sequence identical to F. gigantica27. This isolate was from a farm in Limpopo province, where overlapping distribution was recorded due to mixed infections of both Fasciola species in the same animal, probably resulting in the surge of hybrids. Previous studies have reported the occurrence of aspermic forms (hybrid forms) of F. hepatica occurring in both Mpumalanga and KwaZulu-Natal3, and to the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of this phenomenon in cattle in South Africa. The dispersion, invasion and adaptation of F. hepatica in warmer/hot areas in South Africa may have promoted the emergence of this hybrid form of Fasciola. Considering that this isolate was obtained from a case of a mixed infection in cattle from a breeding farm, this phenomenon may be at an initial stage, potentially driven by the rapid spread of the invasive P. columella, which is now widely distributed in South Africa43. This species has been previously detected with suspected Fasciola hybrid in South Africa35 and Zimbabwe48 and potentially pushing the distribution of both F. gigantica35, especially F. hepatica45, to areas where the environmental conditions were previously considered not conducive for G. truncatula.

Conclusion

This study underscores the genetic diversity and complex population structure of Fasciola spp. in South Africa, providing critical insights into their evolutionary relationships and epidemiology. The study further reports the first evidence of the co-occurrence of both Fasciola species in the Limpopo province of South Africa. The co-occurrence of F. hepatica and F. gigantica in Limpopo, Mpumalanga, and KwaZulu-Natal provinces as well as the presence of a suspected intermediate form of Fasciola underscores the need for region-specific strategies, particularly in areas with overlapping species. Hence, understanding the genetic diversity and distribution of Fasciola spp. is critical for developing effective control measures as the genetic variability within these populations might also affect drug efficacy patterns, as observed in other parasitic trematodes. More investigations should be conducted on the farms where the suspected hybrid Fasciola was detected, including an assessment of the presence of intermediate hosts. Future research should also integrate ecological, and genomic approaches to clarify the role of these lineages in fascioliasis transmission dynamics.

Methods

Use of experimental animals statement

The authors declare that the study was conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. The protocols for this study were approved by the University of KwaZulu-Natal Ethics Committee (AREC/020/020PD), the South African National Biodiversity Institute National Zoological Gardens Animal Research Ethics and Scientific Committee (SANBI/RES/P2021/10) and the Department of Agriculture, Land Reform and Rural Development Section 20 permit (12/11/1/1/18 (1866)). Procedures were carried out in accordance with the commonly accepted norms of veterinary best practice.

Study design and collection of adult Fasciola spp. specimens

Liver flukes were harvested from livers of 71 naturally infected cattle originating from Limpopo (n = 17), Mpumalanga (n = 10), Gauteng (n = 22), Free State (n = 4), KwaZulu-Natal (n = 8), Eastern Cape (n = 4), North West (n = 4) and Northern Cape (n = 2) provinces. Liver flukes were expressed to their provinces of origin, each representing a population. Two-hundred and seven adult Fasciola spp. specimens were analysed from Limpopo (n = 49), Gauteng (n = 83), Mpumalanga (n = 28), Free State (n = 14), North West (n = 6), Northern Cape (n = 7), Eastern Cape (n = 12) and KwaZulu-Natal (n = 7) provinces of South Africa, between June 2021 and August 2023. Specimens were preserved in 70% ethanol prior to transportation to the laboratory at the Pretoria Zoological Gardens, South Africa National Biodiversity Institution (SANBI).

Genomic DNA extraction

Liver fluke specimens were individually washed several times with distilled water to remove residual ethanol, and pat dried with tissue to remove excess water. A 20 mg section on the posterior end of each fluke was excised using a sterile scalpel blade and transferred to a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. DNA was extracted using the Quick-DNA Tissue Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research Corporation) following the manufacturer’s instructions with an overnight incubation period at 56 °C. The isolated DNA was quantified with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and stored at 20 °C until needed.

Polymerase chain reaction

Three primer sets were selected for the identification of Fasciola spp. and to study the genetic diversity. The Pepck primers were selected and used for the initial identification due to their ability to distinguish between species without sequencing. A single-step multiplex PCR was performed using Pepck primers Fh-Pepck-F: 5′-GATTGCACCGTTAGGTTAGC-3′; Fg- Pepck-F: 5′-AAAGTTTCTATCCCGAACGAAG-3′; and Fcmn-Pepck-R: 5′-CGAAAATTATGGCATCAATGGG-3′ for F. hepatica and F. gigantica, respectively28,49. The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 1.5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 61 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, and then a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 mins28. The COI and ITS genes, which have been previously validated and used for identification of Fasciola species in South Africa were then used to confirm Pepck results, barcode the different species with both nuclear and mitochondrial markers, and further analysis of potential hybrid species. The COI and ITS genes were amplified using the primers FHCOI (forward: 5′-TTGGTTTTTTGGGCATCCT-3′) and RHCOI (reverse: 5′-AGGCCACCACCAAATAAAAGA-3′), and S30FE (forward: 5’-GTCGTAACAAGGTTTCCGTA-3′) and S49E6 (reverse: 5′-TATGCTTAAATTCAGCGGGT-3′)34,50, respectively. The cycling conditions were followed as described by34 at 94 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles 95 °C for 1 min, 1 min of annealing at 55 °C for ITS and 59 °C for COI, 72 °C for 1 min and final extension at 72 °C for 7 mins. The PCR reaction mixture contained 2 µl template DNA, 12.5µl DreamTaq PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, SA), 1µl of each primer (10 pmol) and 8.5µl (7.5 µl for Pepck) double distilled water, totalling 25 µl. DNA from a Zimbabwean F. gigantica specimen and F. hepatica from Mpumalanga confirmed by3 were used as positive controls, and distilled DNA-free water was used as a negative control and included in each PCR run. Amplicons were separated on 2% agarose gel stained with 1µl SYBR-Safe gel stain (Thermo Fisher Scientific/Invitrogen, USA) at 120 V for 45 mins. Bands were visualised under UV light.

Sequencing

For each positively amplified sample from ITS and COI genes, 20µL PCR product was cleaned up and purified prior to cycle sequencing, using a mixture of 2µl alkaline phosphatase FastAP™ and 1µl exonuclease 1 (Applied Biosystems, USA) enzymes, and incubated at 37 °C for 15 mins followed by 85 °C for 15 mins. Cycle sequencing was conducted using BigDye Terminator™ v3.1 cycle sequencing kit (Applied Biosystems, USA) where a 10µl reaction, consisting of 5 µl PCR product, 2.55 µl sequencing buffer, 1.45 µl BigDye and 1 µl primer were incubated at 94 °C for 2 mins, 85 °C for 10s, 50 °C for 10s and 60 °C for 2.5 mins. Thereafter, the sequencing reaction was purified with SAM solution and subsequently loaded into a 96 well plate and analysed using the ABI PRISM 3500™ automated Genetic Analyser (Applied Biosystems, USA) for electrophoresis. Sequencing Analysis Software, SeqA™ (Applied Biosystems, USA) was used for base-calling and scoring.

Sequence and haplotype analysis and phylogenetic trees

Forward and reverse sequences were assembled, edited, aligned and consensus sequences were created using the BioEdit program51. Multiple alignments with homologous sequences obtained from the GenBank database were attained using Clustal W52. Aligned sequences were trimmed to a common length of 350 nucleotides for COI and 695 nucleotides for ITS genes. DNA Sequence Polymorphism (DnaSP) software (v 5.10.1) (Barcelona, Spain)53 was used to generate haplotypes for the dataset and estimate genetic diversities. TCS haplotype networks were generated using PoPArt (Wellington, New Zealand)54. jModeltest 2.355 was used to select the best fit model for our dataset, and the Hasegawa-Kishino-Yano (HKY+G)56 and general time reversible (GTR-G) models under the AIC information criterion were selected for COI and ITS, respectively. Maximum likelihood and neighbour-joining (NJ) trees were generated using PAUP* 4.057. The nodal support was estimated using 1000 bootstrap pseudo-replicates. Bayesian inferences were executed in MrBayes 3.1.2y58. Four Markov chains were run for 5 million generations, until the standard deviation of the split frequencies was less than 0.01. The first 500,000 trees were discarded as burn-in.
