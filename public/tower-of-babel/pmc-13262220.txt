Phenolic profile, antioxidant and antibacterial activities of Balanites aegyptiaca fruit peel
Authors: Saleh A Mohamed, Azza M Abdel-Aty, Rashad M Saleh, Omar A M Al-Bar, Yaaser Q Almulaiky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Balanites aegyptiaca fruit possesses bioactive properties, which may be attributed to its phenolic compounds. However, identifying these phenolic compounds has become increasingly relevant to understanding such activities. Generally, fruit peel contains high content of phenolic compounds compared with flesh. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the profile of phenolic compounds in B. aegyptiaca fruit peel extract (BAFPE) and evaluate their antioxidant and antibacterial activities.ResultsIn this study, the great phenolic and flavonoid contents of 1040 mg GAE/100 g DW and 287 mg CE/100 g DW were detected in BAFPE, respectively. The main phenolic compounds identified by the HPLC analysis of BAFPE were gallic acid, apigenin-7-glucoside, rutin, quercetin, and catechin. The antioxidant activity of BAFPE was assessed by 2,2- diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,2′-azino-bis-[3-ethyl-benzothiazoline]-6-sulfonic acid (ABTS), ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP), phosphomolybdenum complex (PMC) and reducing power (RP) assays. The BAFPE showed an efficient antibacterial activity against five human pathogenic bacterial strains with low minimum inhibition concentration (MIC) values. After 8 to 10 h, every examined bacterial strain was entirely dead in the time-killing curves. The correlation of phenolic compounds and antibacterial activity has been discussed. These findings demonstrated that BAFPE can serve as the free radicals neutralizer and antibacterial agent.Keywords: Balanites aegyptiaca, Fruit peel, Phenolic compounds, Antioxidant, Antibacterial

Introduction

Balanites aegyptiaca L Delile (family: Zygophyllaceae) is a desert tree commonly known as a desert date. It has been grown in Egypt for about 4,000 years and is indigenous to Africa and some regions of the Middle East [1]. Numerous products, including charcoal, lumber, and feed, are made from desert dates. Numerous records showed that dates from various desert regions were utilized to treat a wide range of illnesses. Anticonvulsant, hepatoprotective, antioxidant, hypoglycemic, anti-venom, anticancer, antiangiogenic, and antiulcer activities have been demonstrated for the desert date stem bark extract [2–7]. Additionally, the extract from desert date kernels shown anticancer properties [8]. Furthermore, in the retina of diabetic mice, the desert date fruit extract increased endothelium protection and reduced the oxidative stress brought on by streptozocine [9]. The desert date included a variety of phytochemical substances in different areas [10]. Alkaloids such N-trans-feruloyltyramine and N-cisferuloyltyramine were found in the bark of desert dates [11]. There were also a lot of steroidal saponins and yamogenin glycosides in the roots and bark [12].

The natural bioactive phenolic compounds are extensively used in food, chemical, and pharmaceutical industries. Nowadays, various food products could be fortified with natural phenolic compounds to expand the shelf-life of the foods, to turn them to functional foods and /or to incorporate them in active food packaging and edible films [13]. Numerous benefits for human health, including anticancer, anti-inflammatory, antidiabetic, anti-snake, and antibacterial properties, are provided by these phenolic compounds and their strong antioxidant capabilities [14–20]. Moreover, the antioxidant-phenolic compounds could be added to the food for upgrading its nutritional value and reduced the risk of dangerous diseases [21].

Generally, the fruit peels contained higher levels of bioactive phenolic compounds and antioxidant activities than that in fleshes [22, 23]. The phenolic compounds and their functional properties of Balanites aegyptiaca fruit peel have not been studied previously. Therefore, the purpose of this work was to assess the profile of phenolic compounds of B. aegyptiaca fruit peel extract (BAFPE) and to identify and quantify its phenolic compounds using HPLC. The antioxidant and antibacterial activities of BAFPE have been also evaluated. The correlation of phenolic compounds and antibacterial activity has been also discussed.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Fresh fruits of locally grown desert date (Balanites aegyptiaca) were obtained from Horticulture Institute Research, Agriculture Research Centre (ARC), Giza, Egypt, identified by Botany department, ARC, Giza, Egypt. Voucher specimen was deposited in Botany department herbarium with a number of 411–2025#. The ripe fruits were collected immediately after harvesting and the fruit peels were separated after one day of harvesting at room temperature (27 °C).

Pathogenic bacteria

Five human-pathogenic bacterial strains included: Streptococcus pyogenes ATCC19615 (SP), Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC27853 (PA), Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213 (SAU), Enterococcus faecalis ATCC29212 (EF), and Streptococcus agalactiae ATCC12386 (SA) were obtained from the hospital of King Abdulaziz University, Jeddah, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.

Preparation of plant extract

The fruit peel of B. aegyptiaca was washed by distilled water and dried at 40 °C for 8 h using an oven. Then, the dried peels were crushed into powder. Two g of the peel powder was soaked in 20 ml of different solvents: ethanol, acetone, and methanol (80 %) under shaking (New Brunswick incubator shaker, USA) at 200 rpm and room temperature for 24 h. The peel extracts were filtered through filter paper Whatman No. 1. Each filtrate designated as a B. aegyptiaca fruit peel extract (BAFPE). For antibacterial activity, the BAFPE extract was evaporated at 50 °C and dissolved in 0.1% of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO).

Determination of the total phenolic content

According to Velioglu et al. [24], the total phenolic content of BAFPE was determined. The reaction mixture was incubated for five minutes at room temperature with 800 µl of distilled water, 100 µl of the BAFPE, and 100 µl of the Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. After adding 500 µl of 20% sodium carbonate, the mixture was allowed to sit at room temperature for 20 minutes. At 750 nm, absorbance was measured. Determination of the total flavonoid content

The total flavonoid content of the BAFPE was determined according to Zhishen et al. [25]. The reaction mixture contains: 0.25 ml of the BAFPE, 0.075 ml of 5 % NaNO2, and 1.25 ml of distilled water were incubated for 10 min. After adding 0.15 ml of 10% AlCl3 and incubating the mixture for 10 minutes, 0.5 ml of 1.0 M NaOH and 0.275 ml of distilled water were added. At 510 nm, absorbance was measured. Using catechin as a standard, the total flavonoid content (mg catechin equivalent (CE)/100 g DW) was calculated from a calibration curve.The Standard calibration curve equation for flavonoid content determination follow the linear equation:

HPLC analysis of phenolic compounds

According to Kim et al. [26], the phenolic compounds of the BAFPE were identified and quantified using high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis using an Agilent Technologies 1100 series liquid chromatography system that included a diode-array detector and an auto sampler. Separation and determination were carried out on a 150×4.6 mm XDB-C18 column at 40ºC. Acetonitrile (solvent A) and 2% acetic acid in water (v/v) (solvent B) made up the mobile phase. With a gradient programmed as follows: 100 to 85% (in 30 minutes), 85 to 50% (in 20 minutes), 50 to 0% (in 5 minutes), and 0 to 100% (in 5 minutes) of solvent B, the whole run duration was 70 minutes at a flow rate of 1 ml/min. Ten minutes were allotted for reconditioning after the run. At 320 nm, the acquired peaks were simultaneously observed. Prior to injection, each sample was passed through a 0.45 µm Acrodisc syringe filter (Gelman Laboratory, MI). The volume of injected sample was 10 µl. Congruent retention durations and UV spectra were used to identify the acquired peaks, which were then compared to standards of commercial phenolic compounds. To determine phenolic content using HPLC, the general technique involves creating a calibration curve from known standards to quantify individual compounds. The calculation for total phenolic content is often based on a formula such as W=C×V/M, where W is the final polyphenol content, C is the concentration calculated from the calibration curve, V is the final extracted solution volume, and M is the mass of the sample.

Evaluation of antioxidant activity

DPPH radical scavenging assay

Ao et al. [27] described the 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) method used for determining the BAFPE's free radical scavenging activity. The reaction mixture contains: 900 µl of 0.1 mM DPPH dissolved in methanol and 100 µl the BAFPE were incubated for 30 min at room temperature. The absorbance was recorded at 517 nm. DPPH scavenging % = [(O.D. control – O.D. sample)/O.D. control] x 100. The phenolic content of the BAFPE extract that reduces 50% of DPPH free radicals is known as the IC50 value.

ABTS radical scavenging assay

The antioxidant activity of the BAFPE was measured using the ABTS (2, 2'-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzo-thiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) reagent [28]. ABTS was produced by reacting 7 mM ABTS in H2O with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate (K2S2O8), store in the dark at room temperature for 16 h. The ABTS solution was diluted to give an absorbance of 0.750 ± 0.025 at 734 nm in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The reaction mixture contains: 1 ml of ABTS reagent and 0.1 ml of the BAFPE were incubated for 1 min at room temperature and the reduction in absorbance was recorded at 734 nm. ABTS scavenging % = [(O.D. control – O.D. sample)/O.D. control] x 100. The phenolic content of the BAFPE extract that reduces 50% of ABTS free radicals is known as the IC50 value.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power assay (FRAP)

The procedure described by Benzie and Strain [29] was used with minor modifications. The principle of this method is based on the reduction of a ferric 2,4,6-tripyridyl-s-triazine complex (Fe3+-TPTZ) to its ferrous, colored form (Fe2+-TPTZ) in the presence of antioxidants. The FRAP reagent contains: 2.5 ml of 10 mM TPTZ solution in 40 mM HCl, 2.5 ml of 5 mM FeCl3 and 25 ml of 0.3 M sodium acetate buffer, pH 3.6. This reagent was prepared daily and warmed to 37ºC. After combining 200 µl of BAFPE with 1.8 ml of FRAP reagent, the mixture was incubated for 10 minutes at 37ºC. At 593 nm, the absorbance was measured. For the calibration curve, a standard solution of FeSO4.7H2O was utilized. The phenolic concentration of BAFPE that releases 0.05 mM FeSO4 is known as the EC50 value.

Phosphomolybdenum complex (PMC) assay

The antioxidant activity of the tested extract was also examined by the formation of a phosphomolybdenum complex according to Prieto et al. [30]. The reaction mixture contains: 4 mM ammonium molybdate, 28 mM sodium phosphate, 600 mM sulfuric acid, and 50 µl of the BAFPE were incubated for 1h at 95ºC. The absorbance at 695 nm was measured after cooling. The phenolic concentration of BAFPE that results in an absorbance of 0.5 at 695 nm is known as the EC50.

Reducing power assay

The reducing power was determined according to the method of Oyaizu [31]. Different concentrations of the BAFPE were mixed with 2.5 ml of 0.2 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 6.6 and 2.5 ml of 1% potassium ferricyanide. The reaction mixture was incubated at 50 ºC for 20 min. After then, 2.5 ml of 10% trichloroacetic acid was added; the mixture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The upper layer 5 ml was mixed with 5 ml deionised water and 1 ml of 0.1% of ferric chloride, and the absorbance was measured at 700 nm. Higher absorbance indicated higher reducing power. The phenolic concentration of BAFPE that results in an absorbance of 0.5 at 700 nm is known as the EC50.

Determination of antibacterial activity

According to Bauer et al. [32], the antibacterial activity of the BAFPE against five human-pathogenic bacterial strains on Mueller-Hinton agar was evaluated using a well-diffusion method. The plates were covered with 100 µl of diluted inoculum (108 CFU/ml) of each organism suspension, adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard turbidity, and left to harden. A well borer was used to cut out wells on the agar medium in an aseptic setting. 50 µg GAE of the BAFPE or 50 µg of augmentin (as positive control) dissolved in DMSO was added to these wells, and DMSO served as a negative control. For 24 hours, the plates were incubated at 36ºC±1ºC. The diameter (mm) of a distinct growth-inhibition zone against every tested microbe was used to gauge the antibacterial activity.

Determination of Minimum inhibition concentration (MIC)

The MIC of the BAFPE was tested by the agar dilution method in accordance with the National Committee for Clinical Laboratory Standards (formerly them NCCLS), according to protocols M7-A7 [33]. Different concentrations of the BAFPE were added to the wells on Muller-Hinton Agar-media inoculated by 108 CFU/ml of each pathogenic bacterium and incubated at 36±1 ºC for 24 h. A positive control was augmentin. The lowest phenolic concentration of BAFPE that prevents bacterial growth is known as the MIC value.

Time-Kill kinetics

The MIC of Augmentin and BAFPE in Muller-Hinton Broth for every bacterial strain is used to determine time-kill kinetics. A number of colonies of every bacterial strain were cultured aerobically in Muller-Hinton Broth for an entire night prior to the time-killing kinetics tests. In aerobic trays, cultures were incubated at 37 °C. To generate antibiotic concentrations as previously mentioned, BAFPE or Augmentin was applied to each well. No antibiotic was added to the control well. After that, cultures were incubated aerobically at 37 °C. Following the incubation of the BAFPE and Augmentin separately into the wells, 0.001 ml of the cultures were aseptically removed using sterilized loop samples at pre-established time points of 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 24 hours. The samples were then streaked on the blood agar plate evenly over the medium surface in three directions, rotating the plate by about 60 degrees to guarantee even distribution. Once the petri dish lid is in place, let the agar surface dry for 3 to 5 minutes before incubating it for 24 hours at 36 °C. The plate counter was used to count the number of bacterial colonies.

All experimental procedures were carried out in compliance with relevant guidelines.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed by a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post-test. The results were expressed as means ±SD (n=3). Differences are considered significant at P< 0.05.

Results and discussion

The selection of the best solvent is an important step during the extraction process. Table 1 screens the total phenolic and flavonoid contents in the B. aegyptiaca fruit peel (BAFP) using different solvents (methanol, ethanol, and acetone). The BAFP methanol extract showed significant (P < 0.05) higher phenolic and flavonoid contents (1040 ± 28 mg GAE/100 g DW and 287 ± 5.8 mg CE/100 g DW, respectively) compared to the BAFP ethanol and acetone extracts. Therefore, the BAFP methanol extract (BAFPE) was used throughout the study. 80% methanol was used for extraction of phenolic compounds from garden cress and chia [34, 35]. The obtained phenolic and flavonoid levels of the BAFPE were higher than that reported for the date fruit peels of Khalas, Shishi and Barhee cultivars (155-315 mg GAE/100 g and 18-45 mg CE/100 g, respectively) [36, 37], sixteen date cultivars grown in Saudi Arabia (122-247 mg GAE/100 g and 27-199 mg CE/100 g, respectively) [38], three date cultivars irrigated by the municipal water (289-308 mg GAE/100 g and 17-42 mg CE/100, respectively) and sewage water (314-402 mg GAE/100 g and 34-59 mg CE/100 g, respectively) [39].

Various bioactive-phenolic compounds were identified and quantified in the BAFPE using the HPLC analysis as displayed in Fig. 1 and Table 2. Rutin, quercetin, catechin, apigenin-7-glucoside, chlorogenic acid and gallic acid were the major phenolic compounds of the BAFPE and represented 20.3, 14.3, 14.1, 12.6, 11.7 and 9.2 % of total identified phenolic compounds, respectively. Most of the identified phenolic compounds of the BAFPE are potent flavonoids and phenolic acids. These identified compounds possessed effective bio-functional activities such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, anti-snake, and anticancer [17, 40–42].

The antioxidants of phenolic compounds are different in their types, structures, actions, and sensitivity. Thus, as indicated in Table 3, various antioxidant assays were employed to gauge the BAFPE's antioxidant activity. The BAFPE showed a strong antioxidant activity with IC50 scavenge values of 14 ± 0.22 and 0.76 ± 0.021 µg GAE/ml using the DPPH and ABTS assays, respectively. These results were near to values determined by DPPH and ABTS assays for ascorbic acid as standard (16 ± 0.36 and 1.2 ± 0.052 µg GAE/ml, respectively). Besides, the BAFPE showed EC50 values of 4.2 ±0.08, 7.92 ±0.15 and 12.7±0.18 µg GAE/ml using PM, FRAP and RP assays, respectively. A low IC50 or EC50 values reflect the high antioxidant activity. The correlation coefficients (R2) between the BAFPE phenolic concentrations and antioxidant activities among all the tested antioxidant assays are very strong and ranged from 0.947 to 0.994. The B. aegyptiaca leaves extract showed low antioxidant activity with IC50 182.02 GAE µg/ml using the DPPH assay [43]. Approximately similar to our findings, the date palm peels of the Khalas, Barhee, Shishi cultivars showed antioxidant activity with EC50 4.0, 5.4 and 7.5 GAE µg/ml using the FRAP assay [36, 37]. Also, the fruit peels of three date cultivars scored IC50 12.69-16.8 GAE µg/ml using the DPPH assay [39]. Besides, the fruit peels of sixteen date cultivars grown in Saudi Arabia scored IC50 values varied from 1.65 to 8.25 GAE µg/ml and 0.5 to 4.45 µg/ml using the DPPH and ABTS assays, respectively [38].

Some gram-positive and gram-negative bacterial strains are resistant to several antibiotics as well as some phytochemicals [40, 44]. So, the discovering of an alternative antibacterial agent from a safe and natural source is an important step to solve the antibiotic-resistant as a community-wide problem. The antibacterial activity of the BAFPE was assessed against five drug resistance human pathogenic-bacteria: Streptococcus pyogenes (SP), Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC27853 (PA), Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213 (SAU), Enterococcus faecalis ATCC29212 (EF), and Streptococcus agalactiae ATCC12386 (SA). The BAFPE showed stronger and significantly higher (P < 0.05) antibacterial activity against all the tested bacterial strains (14-25 mm inhibition zones) than the Augmentin as a positive control (9.0–12 mm inhibition zones) (Table 4). The antibacterial activity was previously reported for B. aegyptiaca leave extract against Escherichia coli with 8.15 mm inhibition zone, while it was not effective against Staphylococcus aureus [43]. Also, B. aegyptiaca bark extract significantly inhibited the growth of bacteria isolated from well water [45].

The MIC values were measured to detect the phenolic concentration of the BAFPE that inhibited the growth of each tested pathogenic bacteria. The BAFPE exhibited significantly lower MIC values (P < 0.05) against all the tested bacteria (12 to 46 µg GAE/ml) compared to the Augmentin as a positive control (60 to 84 µg GAE/ml) (Table 4). The higher MIC value of125 µg/ml was reported for the B. aegyptiaca leaves extract against E. coli [43]. Additionally, the MIC of the BAFPE and Augmentin were used for the determination of the time-killing curves, where all tested bacterial strains were completely dead after 8 to 10 h (Fig. 2).

The strong antibacterial activity of the BAFPE might be related to its phenolic compounds and their potent antioxidant activities. These antioxidant compounds have the ability to inhibit the microbial growth by oxidative damage. Furthermore, the rutin, quercetin and various phenolic acids of BAFPE have a great power to inhibit the growth of several pathogenic bacterial strains as reported by Almuhanna et al. [46] and Lobiuc et al. [47]. Moreover, these identified phenolic compounds showed markedly enhanced antibacterial activity when combined with antibiotics [40, 41, 48].

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that the BAFPE was a powerful source for bioactive-phenolic compounds and possessed potent antioxidant and antibacterial activities. The BAFPE could be added to the food as a dietary supplement to upgrade its nutritional value and to reduce the risk of dangerous diseases. Moreover, BAFPE shows in vitro potential as a source of bioactive compounds for further investigation. The graphical abstract of the study has been presented in Fig. 3.

Limitations

Other components in the Balanites aegyptiaca fruit peel besides phenolic compounds, were not analyzed in this study and they are likely to contribute also to their biological activity could be done in a future study.
