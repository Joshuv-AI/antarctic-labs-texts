From Instruction to Event: Sound-Triggered Mobile Manipulation
Hao Ju, Shaofei Huang, Hongyu Li, Zihan Ding, Si Liu, Meng Wang, Zhedong Zheng

Abstract
Current mobile manipulation research predominantly follows an instruction-driven paradigm, where agents rely on predefined textual commands to execute tasks. However, this setting confines agents to a passive role, limiting their autonomy and ability to react to dynamic environmental events. To address these limitations, we introduce sound-triggered mobile manipulation, where agents must actively perceive and interact with sound-emitting objects without explicit action instructions. To support these tasks, we develop Habitat-Echo, a data platform that integrates acoustic rendering with physical interaction. We further propose a baseline comprising a high-level task planner and low-level policy models to complete these tasks. Extensive experiments show that the proposed baseline empowers agents to actively detect and respond to auditory events, eliminating the need for case-by-case instructions. Notably, in the challenging dual-source scenario, the agent successfully isolates the primary source from overlapping acoustic interference to execute the first interaction, and subsequently proceeds to manipulate the secondary object, verifying the robustness of the baseline.

Introduction

Recent advances in Embodied AI have witnessed remarkable progress in mobile manipulation, enabling robots to navigate unstructured environments and interact with physical objects
(
31
;
3
;
46
)
.
Such synergy between mobility and interaction empowers agents to perform long-horizon household tasks such as object rearrangement
(
13
;
11

8

(

Current paradigm predominantly studies mobile manipulation under an instruction-conditioned setting, where the task goal and intended interaction are explicitly specified by a natural language instruction
(
40
;
29
;
42
;
48
)
.
While effective for user-specified chores, this paradigm is ill-suited for the stochastic nature of daily life.
For example, events such as a boiling kettle or a ringing doorbell typically happen unexpectedly and require timely intervention, where the latency inherent in human-in-the-loop interaction renders timely intervention impossible.
This uncertainty is further compounded when multiple events occur concurrently or in rapid succession,
i.e
.
, waiting for a phone call from friends while expecting a doorstep delivery from a courier simultaneously.
The indeterminate sequence of such events prevents the user from formulating a valid instruction plan in advance.

This limitation underscores the discrepancy between static, predefined instructions and dynamic, sound-triggered scenarios (See Figure
1
).
Although recent efforts have attempted to generalize instructions ranging from concrete commands
(
44
)
to abstract goals
(
39
)
and ambiguous queries
(
12
;
21
)
, addressing these stochastic events remains an open challenge.
In this context, we find that sound signals play a pivotal role.
For instance, if the phone rings prior to a knock at the door, the robot should prioritize picking up the phone before retrieving the delivery.
In such cases, robotic actions are modulated by stochastic sounds rather than predefined text instructions.
Specifically, sounds serve a threefold function:
(1) temporal trigger indicating the start of a stochastic event;
(2) semantic cue guiding the robot’s action selection; and
(3) spatial cue revealing the event’s location.
Based on these insights, we introduce sound-triggered mobile manipulation, comprising SonicStow, SonicInteract, and Bi-Sonic Manipulation, where acoustic signals replace conventional textual instructions as the trigger signal.
SonicStow and SonicInteract present unique challenges beyond traditional visual manipulation:
(1) Acoustic semantic understanding, which requires the robot to interpret sounds to determine target locations and actions.
(2) Sound-conditioned interaction, which demands the execution of articulated interactions solely triggered by audio.
The Bi-Sonic Manipulation task further elevates the complexity by introducing dual sound sources, which demands the agent to prioritize the primary source amidst acoustic interference and subsequently locate and interact with the secondary sound source.

To validate our proposed tasks, we introduce
Habitat-Echo
, a simulation platform that seamlessly integrates acoustic rendering with interactive physical environments.
Built upon the visual and physical capabilities of Habitat
(
34
;
27
)
, Habitat-Echo addresses the absence of sound signals in prior simulators.
Specifically, we leverage room impulse responses to convolve source sound with the environment’s geometry, thereby ensuring physically accurate sound propagation and spatialization from the source to the receiver.
Leveraging this platform, we construct a benchmark tailored for sound-triggered mobile manipulation.
The dataset encompasses many interactive assets, categorized into: (1) static silent rigid objects, (2) sound-emitting rigid objects, and (3) sound-emitting articulated objects.
We utilize this dataset to train policy models and further introduce an Omni-modal Large Language Model (Omni-LLM) as a high-level task planner to understand acoustic events and decompose complex manipulation goals.
The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

•

Novel Sound-Triggered Tasks.

Distinct from traditional instruction-following paradigms, we formulate three novel manipulation tasks triggered by stochastic acoustic signals.
In these tasks, robotic actions are autonomously triggered by stochastic sound events rather than static predefined instructions, better reflecting the unpredictable and dynamic nature of real-world household scenarios.

•

New Audio-Visual-Physical Simulation Platform.

We introduce Habitat-Echo, a platform that integrates acoustic and visual rendering with interactive physical environments.
By incorporating sound-emitting objects, Habitat-Echo facilitates large-scale episode generation and closed-loop online interaction, establishing a robust foundation for future research in embodied audio-visual learning.

•

Hierarchical Baseline and Validation.

We develop a hierarchical framework that is composed of a high-level task planner to interpret acoustic cues and several low-level executors for precise control.
We leverage an Omni-LLM as the task planner and policy models as executors. The baseline framework achieves promising performance on the proposed benchmark.

3
Task Definition and Skill

3.1
Task Definition

We formally define three novel sound-triggered tasks: SonicStow, SonicInteract, and Bi-Sonic Manipulation.
SonicStow and SonicInteract are mobile manipulation tasks with a single sound source. Bi-Sonic Manipulation, a scenario with dual sound sources. See Figure
2
for more details.

SonicStow.

This task simulates a scenario where an agent actively perceives an acoustic signal, localizes its source, and performs Pick and Place operations on household objects.
Distinct from standard instruction-following paradigms that rely on predefined instructions, the agent must autonomously identify the target and conduct interaction solely based on the stochastic audio event.
In each episode, the agent is initialized with a random pose and start location, while a single sound source, randomly selected from three objects (Phone, Alarm, Furby) within the scene (see Figure
2
(a)).
To introduce visual clutter, two distractor objects are randomly sampled from the YCB dataset for each episode.
All objects are placed on elevated receptacles (e.g., existing furniture or inserted tables) instead of floor.

SonicInteract.

This task entails localizing an acoustic event and performing manipulation on articulated objects. Different from picking and placing objects in SonicStow, this task requires the agent to precisely control the end-effector and manipulate articulated mechanisms,
i.e
.
, opening a door or turning off a faucet in the sink (see Figure
2
(b)).
In each episode, the agent is initialized with a random pose and start location. The target single-sound source is randomly sampled from two categories: a doorbell or a sink with running water.
Object placement follows category-specific constraints: doorbells are fixed to the existing doors within the scene, whereas sinks are placed on the floor with arbitrary orientations and locations.

Bi-Sonic Manipulation.

The Bi-Sonic Manipulation task integrates the challenges of SonicStow and SonicInteract, requiring the agent to manipulate both rigid and articulated objects (see Figure
2
(c)). Additionally, this task introduces three more challenges.
The first challenge of this task is the presence of two simultaneous sound sources, which creates a complex acoustic field featuring signal dynamics and superposition.
Unlike single-source tasks, the agent must distinguish and interact with both sources sequentially based on a given priority (
i.e
.
, the main source followed by the secondary source). The categories and spatial locations of these sources are randomized.
The second challenge of this task is two-time interactions. After interacting with the first object, the sound of the first object stops, and there is only one sound source in the scene. The agent has to execute skills and interact with the second object.
Furthermore, to enhance visual complexity, we still introduce unrelated objects
(
4
)
to serve as visual clutter.

3.2
Skill

Similar to previous work
(
10
;
30
;
37
)
, the proposed tasks are decomposed into different subtasks. Each subtask is solved by an individual skill. A task is considered completed successfully if and only if all constituent skills are successfully executed in sequence. There are five skills in the proposed three tasks.

Navigate.

The action space consists of four actions:
MoveForward
,
TurnLeft
,
TurnRight
, and
Stop
.
Specifically,
MoveForward
translates the agent by 0.5m along its local forward axis, while rotational actions (
TurnLeft
,
TurnRight
) execute
90^{\circ}
in-place rotations to the left and right, respectively.
The
Stop
action terminates the episode without changing the agent’s pose and location.
An episode is considered successful if and only if the agent executes
Stop
while positioned within a region of 0.5m radius of the goal.

Pick.

The agent operates in an 8-DoF continuous action space. The first seven dimensions control the arm’s joint angles, driving the end-effector to the target pose via the kinematic chain
(
1
)
, while the final dimension modulates the gripper state.
A Pick skill is considered successful with two conditions:
(1) Grasping: The gripper action is positive while an object is within a 15cm snapping threshold of the end-effector, triggering a stable grasp.
(2) Retraction: The agent effectively holds the object and returns the arm to a predefined resting pose, ensuring a smooth transition to subsequent subtasks.

Place.

The agent operates in an 8-DoF action space identical to that of the Pick skill.
The success conditions for the Place skill are twofold:
(1) Releasement: The gripper opens to release the object, ensuring it settles within 0.15cm of the target position.
(2) Retraction: Upon release, the arm reverts to the default resting pose, preparing for the next subtask.

Open Door.

Operating within the 8-DoF action space, the agent’s end-effector attaches to the handle of the door and drives it along its own functional trajectory.
The skill is considered successful with two conditions:
(1) Opening: While connected with the door handle, the agent must drive the door’s joint angle to a specified target threshold.
(2) Retraction: The agent disengages from the door handle and returns the arm to the resting pose.

Close Sink.

Similar to the Open Door skill, the agent utilizes the 8-DoF action space to manipulate the sink’s rotary mechanism.
The end-effector must grasp the faucet handle and apply torque to rotate it along its functional axis.
Success is determined by two conditions:
(1) Closure: While maintaining grasp, the agent must rotate the handle to the zero-degree threshold within a tolerance of 0.2 rad.
(2) Retraction: Upon completing the rotation, the agent releases the handle and reverts the arm to the resting pose.

Simulator and Benchmark

4.1
Simulator: Habitat-Echo

Existing simulators
(
18
;
24
;
34
)
prioritize physical interaction with visual observations, while others
(
6
;
7
)
incorporate audio-visual rendering specifically for navigation tasks (see Table
1(a)
).
There remains an absence of simulators that integrate sound rendering with physical interaction.
We address this limitation by introducing Habitat-Echo, an extension of Habitat
(
34
)
that enables sound rendering alongside physical interaction.

To enable high-fidelity acoustic simulation within the environment, we leverage precomputed Room Impulse Responses (RIRs) in SoundSpaces
(
6
)
.
An RIR serves as an accurate transfer function characterizing the multipath propagation between a source and a receiver
(
20
)
.
RIRs provide high-fidelity acoustic features by incorporating two critical physical factors:
(1) Geometry-aware propagation: Generated via bidirectional path tracing, RIRs implicitly capture intricate wave phenomena, including direct paths, specular reflections, and reverberation. In this way, rendered sounds are consistent with the room’s 3D geometry.
(2) Material-dependent scattering: The RIR computation accounts for acoustic material properties by mapping semantic annotations to frequency-dependent absorption and transmission coefficients.
This enables the simulator to reflect distinct acoustic interactions with diverse surfaces.
With the help of RIRs, Habitat-Echo synthesizes the auditory observation by performing time-domain convolution between the source waveform and the corresponding RIR on the fly.
Furthermore, exploiting the property of the acoustic field, our simulator supports simultaneous multi-source rendering by superimposing the convolved signals from distinct sources, which is essential for the Bi-Sonic Manipulation task.
The final output is rendered as binaural audio, providing the agent with realistic spatial cues analogous to human auditory perception.

4.2
Benchmark

Sounding Objects and Audio Assets.

In addition to standard YCB objects
(
4
)
, we introduce five more categories of interactive sounding objects.
For SonicStow, we introduce graspable rigid objects, including phone, alarm, and furby. These objects can be manipulated via Pick and Place skills.
For SonicInteract, we incorporate articulated objects, including the sink and door. Unlike rigid items, these objects possess kinematic joints and are constrained to move along fixed functional axes. For the sink, we incorporate a dynamic water flow that is physically coupled to the faucet handle, moving synchronously with the handle’s rotation.
To foster robust acoustic generalization, we also collect a diverse library of real-world sounds, implementing a strict one-to-many mapping where each object category is associated with multiple unique sound instances (see Table
1(b)
).
This design forces the agent to learn semantically meaningful audio-visual associations rather than overfitting to specific frequency patterns or waveforms, thereby ensuring high acoustic diversity of the benchmark.

Episode Generation.

We employ a procedural randomization process to generate episodes for training and testing.
In each episode, the sound for the target object is determined by hierarchically sampling a sound category and then a specific audio instance.
Visual complexity is introduced by instantiating random silent objects as distractors alongside the target object.
The agent is initialized at a random position, and target objects (excluding two doorbells in the scene) are also placed at random positions within the scene.
We also validate the solvability of every episode by ensuring a traversable path connects the start and goal, and we strictly verify that interactive objects have accessible frontal regions for valid manipulation, especially for sinks and doors.
An episode is considered successful if and only if the required skills are successfully executed in sequence.

5
Baseline

Figure 3
:

Overview of the proposed baseline.

The sound-triggered task planner processes initial audio-visual observations to reason and generate a high-level skill chain from the skill library. Guided by this chain, specialized policy models are sequentially activated to generate low-level actions and interact with the environment.

The proposed baseline comprises two core components,
i.e
.
, Sound-triggered Task Planner and Policy Models (see Figure
3
).
Sound-triggered task planner acts as the high-level reasoner, analyzing initial observations to predict a logical skill chain.
Following this chain, the policy models serve as the low-level executors, sequentially translating each assigned skill into concrete actions to complete the corresponding subtasks.

5.1
Sound-triggered Task Planner

Motivated by MCP
(
2
)
, we propose a sound-triggered task planner that operates as the high-level reasoning module. This module is responsible for translating environmental cues into a plan of skills,
i.e
.
, skill chain (see Figure
3
).
The input for the task planner is twofold, comprising initial sensory observations and a skill library.
Regarding observations, the planner utilizes the sound to reason about the target’s category and relative orientation, while the head RGB provides an initial egocentric view to perceive the scene layout partially.
Crucially, to prevent information leakage, we restrict the planner’s input to strictly align with the observation space of the Navigate skill, ensuring no additional information is accessed.
Complementing these observations, the skill library is provided as a textual prompt for the task planner.
The library defines the semantic functionality of all available skills to help the reasoning of the planner.
By combining these multi-modal observations with the skill descriptions, the task planner outputs an ordered chain of skills.
This chain acts as a high-level directive, guiding the sequential activation of the following policy models to complete the task.

5.2
Policy Model

Policy models serve as low-level executors, translating the skill chain from the task planner into concrete actions and sequentially interacting with the environment.
Policy models in the proposed baseline share a similar architecture comprising modality encoders, a temporal module, and actor-critic heads.
The temporal module and actor-critic modules maintain a consistent structure following prior works
(
6
;
10
)
, and the modality encoders are tailored to the specific observation space of different skills.
For the Navigate skill, the observation space includes audio and head RGB, and the modality encoders are designed based on task complexity.
In SonicStow and SonicInteract, the audio observations are converted into spectrograms and processed by a vanilla CNN encoder
(
6
)
.
In Bi-Sonic Manipulation, audio observations are transformed into Mel-spectrograms, and the encoder is LanguageBind
(
50
)
. An additional text encoder is introduced to specify the target sound source.
For manipulation skills, including Pick, Place, Open Door, and Close Sink, the observation spaces consist of head and arm depth, which are processed by a standard CNN encoder
(
6
)
.

6
Experiment

6.1
Implementation

Implementation Detail.

We optimize all policy models using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm
(
28
)
on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU.
The training process spans
5\times 10^{5}
environment steps.
Structurally, the visual and audio encoders remain frozen to ensure efficiency, and the Actor and Critic networks are Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) with a hidden dimension of 512.
We utilize the Adam optimizer
(
17
)
with an initial learning rate of
3\times 10^{-4}
enabled with linear decay, a momentum of
1\times 10^{-5}
, and a maximum gradient norm of 0.5.
Regarding PPO hyperparameters, we set the discount factor of 0.99, GAE parameter of 0.95, and clipping range of 0.2.
The update phase involves 2 epochs with 2 mini-batches, using a rollout length of 128 steps.
The value loss coefficient is set to 0.5, and the entropy coefficient is 0.1.
For each task, the dataset is split into 660 instances for training.
The testing dataset comprises 222 instances for both SonicStow and SonicInteract, and 355 instances for Bi-Sonic Manipulation.

Baseline.

We train low-level policy models individually and then compose them with varying task planners, implementing three types of baselines.
First, the
Individual
baseline evaluates each skill in isolation. This baseline bypasses the task planner to measure each skill without the influence of cascading errors.
Second, the
Oracle
baseline employs a privileged task planner that provides the ground-truth skill chains, serving as a performance upper bound for the task planning.
Finally, we integrate mainstream Omni-LLMs as task planners to evaluate audio-visual reasoning capabilities, including Qwen2.5-Omni (3B, 7B)
(
41
)
and Qwen3-Omni-30B-Instruct
(
43
)
.
These task planners are all training-free.
We refer to these baselines directly by their own names.

6.2
Result

Quantitative Result of SonicStow.
We present the evaluation results on the SonicStow task in Table
2(a)
.
The Oracle baseline, which utilizes ground-truth planning, establishes the performance upper bound with an overall success rate of 40.99%.
The Individual baseline reflects the standalone success rate of each policy model (e.g., 88.73% for Pick), decoupling low-level execution and high-level planning.
Regarding task planning, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B achieves the highest accuracy of 78.38%, surpassing both the smaller Qwen2.5-Omni-3B and the larger Qwen3-Omni-30B.
Driven by this, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B generates the highest number of correct skill chains, thereby attaining the best overall execution performance of 27.48% among all task planners.

Quantitative Result of SonicInteract.
The evaluation results on the SonicInteract are shown in Table
2(b)
.
With ground-truth task planning, the Oracle baseline achieves a 27.90% overall success rate, which is the theoretical upper bound of the policy models.
Among the evaluated task planners, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B also emerges as the most effective one.
It attains a planning rate of 71.17%, outperforming both Qwen2.5-Omni-3B and Qwen3-Omni-30B.
The success rate of planning directly translates to execution success, and Qwen2.5-Omni-7B achieves the top overall performance of 19.72%.
Conversely, we observe unsatisfactory cases in Qwen2.5-Omni-3B during the Close Sink subtask.
Instead of responding to the sound of running water with the appropriate skill, Qwen2.5-Omni-3B consistently hallucinates a Pick and Place sequence, resulting in a near-zero success rate for this specific task.

Quantitative Result of Bi-Sonic Manipulation.
Table
2(c)
presents the results for the Bi-Sonic Manipulation task.
In this dual-source environment, Qwen3-Omni-30B achieves the highest task planning accuracy, showing superior robustness against acoustic distraction.
In contrast, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B fails to maintain its dominance in the task planning, receiving performance comparable to Qwen2.5-Omni-3B.

Ablation Study and Further Discussion

Effect of Input Modality for the Navigate Skill.

We evaluate the impact of different visual modalities, including head RGB, head depth, and their corresponding encoders for the Navigate skill (see Table
3(a)
).
First, the model relying solely on head depth with a CNN encoder yields the lowest performance. This underperformance arises because depth observations lack semantic texture, making it difficult for the model to learn meaningful associations between visual cues in depth observations and the semantic information contained in audio observations.
Second, models using head RGB achieve better results, with the trainable CNN encoder performing slightly better than the frozen DINOv2 due to its training process that helps the model to adapt to the indoor environment.
Third, integrating both head RGB and head depth yields the best overall performance. This combination proves effective because the modalities are complementary. Head RGB provides semantic context to align with audio sources, while depth offers precise geometric distance information.
Despite the performance of the dual-modality method, we select head RGB with the DINOv2 encoder as our default configuration to maintain an optimal balance between performance and training efficiency.

Effect of Input Modality for the Pick Skill.

We investigate the impact of depth modalities on the Pick skill in Table
3(b)
, comparing the efficacy of head depth and arm depth.
Using head depth alone yields the lowest success rate of 57.20%, primarily because the target object often falls outside the head camera’s optimal viewing range during manipulation.
In contrast, the model relying solely on arm depth achieves a higher success rate of 66.66%, as the arm depth can directly observe the target when aligned.
We observe that integrating both depth sources results in the best performance (88.73%).
The two views prove complementary: the arm depth provides precise object-to-gripper distance, while the head depth offers global context to assist the arm in returning to the resting pose.

Effect of Input Modality for the Open Door Skill.

For the Open Door skill, we validate the impact of different depth inputs, specifically analyzing head depth and arm depth (see Table
3(c)
).
Models relying on a single depth source achieve comparable performance, with the head depth only model (Model II) slightly outperforming the arm depth only model (Model I).
We attribute this margin to the fact that head depth provides a global view of the door’s structure, whereas the arm camera often suffers from initial misalignment with the door’s specific affordance (
i.e
.
, the handle).
Crucially, combining both modalities (Model III) results in a dramatic performance boost to 62.16%.
This improvement stems from the complementary nature of the inputs: the head depth offers the global context necessary to locate the door frame, which in turn allows the agent to leverage the arm depth for precisely perceiving the target affordance for accurate manipulation.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce sound-triggered mobile manipulation, a novel paradigm that shifts agents from passive instruction-following to active, sound-driven interaction.
To support this paradigm, we develop Habitat-Echo, a data platform integrating audio rendering with physical interaction, and establish a hierarchical baseline comprising a high-level task planner and low-level policies.
Extensive experiments validate that the proposed baseline enables agents to actively localize and manipulate sound-emitting objects.
Notably, in challenging dual-source scenarios, the baseline demonstrates the capability to interact with the primary source amidst acoustic interference from a secondary signal, and successfully proceed to manipulate the subsequent target.

Prompt for Task Planning

To ensure a fair comparison, we utilize a standardized prompt structure across all task planners.
The input consists of two components: a system prompt and a user prompt.
The system prompt defines the detailed rules and logic for task planning, while the user prompt provides the environmental context, including visual and acoustic observations.

Prompt for Task Planning in SonicStow and SonicInteract.

The system prompt for SonicStow and SonicInteract is structured into four components.
The first component defines the strictly allowed skill vocabulary.
The second establishes the logical mapping between audio and the corresponding skill chains.
The third specifies the planning constraints and the mandatory JSON output format.
Finally, the fourth component provides a structural template for the response.
Complementing this, the user prompt supplies the visual and acoustic observations to ground the planning process.
