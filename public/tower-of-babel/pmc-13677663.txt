Expanding role of nuclear medicine in cardiac amyloidosis: insights from the EJNMMI collection
Authors: Mahdi Zirakchian Zadeh, Medhat M Osman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advances in nuclear medicine are reshaping the evaluation of cardiac amyloidosis, as highlighted by the recent collection published in EJNMMI (collection link: https://link.springer.com/collections/igfdhcddjg). This commentary summarizes key insights from these studies, emphasizing the evolving shift from conventional diagnostic imaging toward a more integrated and comprehensive approach. Emerging developments include novel amyloid PET tracers, improved diagnostic accuracy with SPECT/CT, multimodality imaging strategies, and enhanced assessment of systemic disease burden extending beyond the left ventricle. There is also growing interest in longitudinal monitoring, pre-symptomatic detection, and the role of artificial intelligence. Together, these advances position nuclear medicine as an essential tool for disease characterization, risk stratification, and treatment assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41824-026-00326-3.Keywords: Cardiac amyloidosis, Novel PET tracers, SPECT/CT, Multimodality imaging, Quantitative imaging, Artificial intelligence

Introduction

Cardiac amyloidosis is an increasingly recognized cause of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), resulting from extracellular deposition of misfolded proteins within the myocardium (Gertz et al. 2015, 2005; Monge et al. 2011; Sommer et al. 2014; Gertz 2013; Zavaleta-Monestel et al. 2026) (Fig. 1). Delayed diagnosis remains a major contributor to adverse outcomes (Gertz et al. 2015; Gertz 2013). Until 2016, endomyocardial biopsy was the only reliable diagnostic method, but the introduction of amyloid-avid bone scintigraphy has enabled a non-biopsy diagnosis of transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis (ATTR-CM) in appropriate clinical settings (Spielvogel et al. 2024) (Fig. 2). SPECT/CT further improves localization and diagnostic accuracy, while whole-body imaging may provide additional information on systemic disease burden (American Society of Nuclear Cardiology ASNC 2022).

The authors reviewed with interest the recent collection on cardiac amyloidosis and nuclear medicine in EJNMMI (collection link: https://link.springer.com/collections/igfdhcddjg). As outlined in this collection, recent literature reflects a clear shift from conventional diagnostic imaging toward novel radiotracers, quantitative assessment, therapy monitoring, artificial intelligence integration, and broader systemic disease characterization. The following short communication summarizes key findings from recent original articles, reviews, and editorials (Simple Figure Summary is included in a Supplementary Material 1).

Novel PET tracers and molecular imaging

Recent work on fluorine-18 florbetazine highlights a promising amyloid-targeting PET tracer with early evidence of myocardial uptake, particularly in AL amyloidosis—an important step forward given that conventional bone scintigraphy is mainly sensitive for ATTR (Li et al. 2026). Building on the shared β-sheet structure of amyloid seen in cardiac amyloidosis and Alzheimer’s disease, this tracer showed high-affinity binding to both AL and ATTR deposits in preclinical studies, along with favorable kinetics. In early human data, background myocardial activity was low, while a patient with biopsy-proven AL disease demonstrated intense cardiac uptake on delayed PET/CT, supporting potential clinical utility (Li et al. 2026). Overall, Florbetazine may allow more direct visualization of amyloid deposition, although larger studies are still needed. However, these PET tracers remain investigational and are not currently part of routine clinical practice. Future prospective multicenter studies will be essential to define their clinical role, particularly for AL amyloidosis and equivocal cases.

Total-body 11C-PiB PET/CT further expands this concept by enabling whole-body assessment, shifting the view of cardiac amyloidosis from an isolated cardiomyopathy to a systemic disease (Fig. 3). A study in EJNMMI showed significant amyloid deposition not only in the heart but also in organs such as the bone marrow, lungs, and spleen, with clear inter-organ connectivity patterns (Hong et al. 2025). Importantly, higher extracardiac amyloid burden was associated with worse survival, highlighting the prognostic value of this approach (Hong et al. 2025). Overall, total-body PET provides a more integrated view of disease burden and may improve risk stratification and understanding of disease progression.

Hybrid imaging and multi-modality approaches

Studies combining 11C-PiB PET/CT with 99mTc-DPD scintigraphy show that these modalities offer complementary insights (Hong et al. 2025) (Fig. 4). Bone scintigraphy remains highly specific for ATTR cardiac amyloidosis, while PET imaging enables more direct assessment of overall amyloid burden across different subtypes. This dual approach improves diagnostic accuracy, supports better subtype differentiation, and adds prognostic value (Hong et al. 2025). Overall, integrating amyloid PET with bone scintigraphy provides a more complete picture—combining the specificity of DPD for ATTR with the broader disease assessment offered by PET imaging. In clinical practice, bone scintigraphy remains the cornerstone of noninvasive ATTR diagnosis, whereas hybrid PET approaches currently serve primarily as research tools with potential future applications in disease characterization and subtype differentiation.

Advances in bone scintigraphy and SPECT/CT

Multiple studies show that SPECT/CT improves diagnostic accuracy compared with planar imaging, particularly in equivocal Perugini grade 1 cases. In these scenarios, planar imaging can be misleading, as uptake often reflects blood pool activity or adjacent structures rather than true myocardial involvement. In one cohort, SPECT/CT confirmed true myocardial uptake in only a small minority of grade 1 cases, with most representing non-specific activity (Poledniczek et al. 2026). Thoracic SPECT/CT allows better localization, reduces false positives, and improves diagnostic confidence (Poledniczek et al. 2026).

Beyond localization, quantitative SPECT/CT enables a more objective assessment of myocardial tracer uptake and may complement visual interpretation (Zhao et al. 2025). Overall, these findings reinforce the role of SPECT/CT as the preferred imaging approach for accurate diagnosis and localization in patients with suspected ATTR cardiac amyloidosis.

Imaging treatment response and disease-modifying therapy

Studies have explored the impact of disease-modifying therapy—particularly tafamidis—on scintigraphic markers in ATTR cardiac amyloidosis, suggesting a potential role for serial imaging in treatment monitoring. In one study (19 patients on tafamidis vs. 9 controls), myocardial tracer uptake on 99mTc-PYP scintigraphy showed a significant reduction during the first year of therapy, followed by stabilization over time, indicating an early therapeutic effect with sustained disease control in hereditary ATTR cardiomyopathy (Yu et al. 2025). This was further supported by genotype-specific data in patients with the Ala97Ser mutation, where initial decreases in uptake transitioned into long-term stability, reinforcing the potential of scintigraphy as a biomarker for response (Yu et al. 2025). Similarly, studies using 99mTc-DPD scintigraphy have shown that changes in tracer uptake may reflect treatment effects over time (Ungericht et al. 2025). Overall, these findings suggest that nuclear imaging may play an increasing role in longitudinal follow-up and treatment assessment. However, quantitative scintigraphic assessment remains investigational, and no standardized imaging criteria currently exist for defining treatment response. Larger prospective studies are required before serial quantitative imaging can be incorporated into routine clinical practice.

Artificial intelligence in cardiac amyloidosis imaging

Recent multi-center studies demonstrate that artificial intelligence can detect and quantify cardiac amyloidosis across multiple tracers, scanners, and institutions (Salimi et al. 2025; Spielvogel et al. 2026; Shiri et al. 2025; Muller et al. 2025) (Fig. 5). In a multi-center study using over 500 labeled patients and 3,215 additional scans, a deep learning model accurately detected and scored ATTR cardiac amyloidosis across multiple tracers and scanners, achieving AUCs above 0.9 (Salimi et al. 2025). This automated approach may improve diagnostic consistency and enable earlier detection in clinical practice (Salimi et al. 2025).

AI has also been applied specifically to high-risk populations such as patients with severe aortic stenosis, where cardiac amyloidosis is often underdiagnosed (Shiri et al. 2025; Muller et al. 2025). In 263 patients with severe aortic stenosis, ATTR cardiac amyloidosis was present in 10.3%. AI models using pre-TAVI (transcatheter aortic valve implantation) data showed good diagnostic performance, with 4D-CT strain achieving the highest accuracy, supporting a noninvasive approach for detecting ATTR-CM in this high-risk population (Shiri et al. 2025; Muller et al. 2025). Despite these promising results, AI algorithms require external validation, standardized implementation, and prospective multicenter evaluation before widespread clinical adoption.

Cardiac amyloidosis and aortic stenosis

Recent studies highlight the overlap between transthyretin cardiac amyloidosis and severe aortic stenosis. Computed tomography-derived aortic valve calcium scoring can assist in localizing radionuclide signal and improving interpretation of scintigraphy. In 263 patients with ATTR-CM, aortic valve calcification was present in 58% and strongly predicted aortic stenosis severity (Bonnans et al. 2026). Both calcium score and visual grading were also independent predictors of mortality, highlighting their prognostic value (Bonnans et al. 2026). These findings have important implications for patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement, supporting targeted screening strategies and improved prognostic assessment in this population.

Beyond left ventricular assessment

Emerging data emphasize the importance of evaluating right ventricular amyloid burden using quantitative SPECT/CT, which has been associated with adverse outcomes. In 76 patients with ATTR cardiac amyloidosis, diffuse increased RV uptake on SPECT/CT was associated with more advanced disease and worse biomarkers (Zhao et al. 2025). In 53 patients, increased RV and LV uptake predicted adverse cardiac events, highlighting the prognostic value of quantitative SPECT/CT (Zhao et al. 2025).

Soft tissue tracer uptake—including in subcutaneous fat—offers another dimension of disease assessment, correlating with systemic amyloid burden and independently predicting mortality (Tingen et al. 2024). This reinforces a broader shift in ATTR imaging: beyond binary cardiac diagnosis, quantitative and extracardiac uptake patterns may better capture total-body disease burden and risk stratification, though this remains an area of active investigation rather than established clinical practice.

Early detection and screening strategies

Pre-symptomatic screening approaches combining genetic testing and scintigraphy have been explored, particularly in hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis (Holcman et al. 2025; Tingen et al. 2024) (Fig. 6). These strategies may enable detection of disease before overt cardiac involvement develops. In 100 asymptomatic relatives, scintigraphy detected cardiac amyloidosis in 8.2%, while 20.5% carried pathogenic mutations without imaging evidence, supporting the role of combined genetic and scintigraphic screening for early detection of ATTR (Holcman et al. 2025). Although these findings are encouraging, screening strategies remain investigational and should currently be interpreted within specialized clinical and research settings.

Standardization and clinical practice

Editorials and survey-based studies highlight considerable variability in acquisition protocols, reconstruction methods, quantitative assessment, and reporting across institutions (Gotuzzo et al. 2024). Standardization of imaging protocols and harmonization of quantitative methodologies will be essential to ensure reproducibility and facilitate broader clinical implementation. Prospective multicenter validation studies remain necessary before many emerging techniques can be incorporated into routine practice.

Current limitations and unmet needs

Despite these exciting advances, several important challenges remain. Many emerging technologies—including amyloid PET, quantitative SPECT biomarkers, whole-body PET, and artificial intelligence—are currently available only in specialized centers. Broader implementation will require standardized imaging protocols, multicenter validation, improved accessibility, and clear evidence demonstrating incremental clinical value. Future studies should also establish standardized quantitative criteria for treatment monitoring and define how these technologies can best complement existing clinical pathways.

Conclusion

The field is evolving toward better diagnostic accuracy with SPECT/CT and hybrid imaging, deeper molecular insight from amyloid PET tracers, more precise quantification of disease burden—including systemic involvement—and improved longitudinal monitoring with therapy-response imaging and AI. Although many of these technologies remain investigational, the collective evidence suggests that nuclear medicine is evolving from a predominantly diagnostic discipline toward an increasingly quantitative, prognostic, and therapy-guiding specialty. As these approaches mature through standardization and prospective validation, they have the potential to substantially improve personalized care for patients with cardiac amyloidosis.
