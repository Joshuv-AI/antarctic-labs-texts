Defining the role of β-cell IRE1α/XBP1 pathway and its gene regulatory network components in non-obese diabetic mice
Authors: Hugo Lee, Khagani Eynullazada, Qiaodan Ou, Junha Shin, Sushmita Roy, Feyza Engin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The unfolded protein response sensor, IRE1α, acts through its regulated IRE1α-dependent decay (RIDD) activity or transcription factor XBP1 to determine cell fate and survival. While blunting RIDD activity prevents diabetes in type 1 diabetes preclinical model non-obese diabetic mice, β-cell-specific function of XBP1 at different stages of disease remains unknown. Here we show that deletion of Xbp1 in β-cells (Xbp1β-/-) of non-obese diabetic mice before insulitis is protective against diabetes. Histological and transcriptomic analyses indicate that following a transient loss of maturity, β-cells of Xbp1β-/- mice exhibit reduced insulitis, apoptosis, and antigenicity phenocopying Ire1αβ-/- mice with no changes in RIDD activity. Comparative transcriptome and regulatory network analyses reveal a largely shared component between the Ire1αβ-/- and Xbp1β-/- mice as well as network components unique to Xbp1β-/-, indicative of IRE1α-independent roles of XBP1. Our findings define the role of β-cell IRE1α/XBP1 and identify previously unrecognized regulatory networks and nodes of this pathway.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a chronic inflammatory disease caused by autoimmune-mediated destruction of β-cells1–4. Previous work has shown that β-cells exhibit dysregulated unfolded protein response (UPR) in mouse models of T1D and human patients5–11. The UPR is activated in response to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress to restore cellular homeostasis. Inositol-requiring enzyme 1α (IRE1α) acts as one of the highly conserved sensors of the UPR12–15. IRE1α exerts pleiotropic functions16–18 through its kinase and RNase activities. With its kinase domain, IRE1α can phosphorylate c-Jun N-terminal Kinase (JNK) or IκB kinase (IKK) to induce inflammation and apoptosis19,20. During prolonged stress, with its RNase domain, it degrades select mRNAs and miRNAs through a process called regulated IRE1α-dependent decay (RIDD)21. Hyperactive RIDD can initiate caspase-dependent apoptosis and activate the NLRP3 inflammasome22–24. IRE1α can also use its RNase activity to generate transcriptionally active spliced XBP1 (sXBP1) that can regulate UPR target genes by binding to UPR elements25–28.

Work from several groups has suggested an important role for the IRE1α/XBP1 pathway in pancreatic β-cell homeostasis8,15. β-cell-specific embryonic deletion of Ire1α leads to impaired insulin secretion29, while its deletion in adult β-cells leads to hyperglycemia due to defects in proinsulin translation and trafficking30,31. Deletion of Xbp1 in β-cells of an unstressed mouse strain, C57BL/6 J mice, led to hyperglycemia due to impaired insulin secretion and proinsulin processing32, a phenotype that was further exacerbated when mice were fed with a high-fat diet33. While studies highlighted the importance of the IRE1α/XBP1 pathway in the proper function of β-cells on a non-stressed mouse background, the β-cell-specific function of this pathway under autoimmune stress has remained elusive. We previously showed that β-cell-specific deletion of Ire1α in a well-established T1D preclinical model, non-obese diabetic (NOD) mice, prior to insulitis (Ire1αβ-/-), induces a transient dedifferentiation of β-cells and a diminished expression of islet autoantigens that ultimately leads to protection from T1D34. Given that IRE1α can show pleiotropic functions, whether the T1D protection in NOD mice upon Ire1α deletion in β-cells was due to loss of IRE1α’s kinase, RIDD, or XBP1 activity has remained unknown.

In this study, we enhance our understanding of the function of β-cells UPR by generating a β-cell-specific Xbp1 deletion model in T1D. A key finding of our study is that loss of XBP1 in β-cells of NOD mice confers protection against T1D through mechanisms that are similar to those observed in Ire1αβ-/- mice. In addition, by using single-cell RNA sequencing datasets from Ire1αβ-/- and Xbp1β-/- mice, this work identifies the gene regulatory network components of these UPR sensors in β-cells. Our analyses reveal not only the shared transcriptional targets of Ire1α and Xbp1, but also the unique regulatory programs of these genes. We show that these unique programs are associated with developmental processes, immune response as well as genes involved in metabolism. These findings provide compelling evidence for the importance of β-cell IRE1α/XBP1 pathway in a preclinical autoimmune diabetes model and can inspire potential therapeutic strategies for targeting the IRE1α /XBP1 branch of the UPR for this disease.

Results

Deletion of Xbp1 in β-cells of NOD mice protects against T1D

To determine the function of β-cell XBP1 in T1D, we deleted Xbp1 specifically in β-cells of NOD mice (Xbp1β-/-) by mating Xbp1fl/fl mice with Xbp1fl/fl; Ins2CreERT mice and inducing the deletion at the preinsulitis stage as we previously described34 (Fig. 1A). We examined Xbp1 deletion efficiency by performing immunofluorescence (IF) assay on the pancreatic sections from 8- (Fig. 1B) and 24-week-old (Fig. 1C) mice using anti-XBP1 and anti-insulin antibodies. We detected significantly reduced XBP1 levels at both time points although the reduction in XBP1 levels was relatively less at the later point likely due to significant and progressive decline of XBP1 expression in β-cells of wild-type NOD mice prior to diabetes onset5,35. Starting from 3 weeks of age, we measured body weight (Fig. 1D) and the blood glucose levels of control and knockout mice weekly up to 50 weeks of age (Fig. 1E). Next, we measured serum insulin and proinsulin levels of 6-week-old Xbp1fl/fl and Xbp1β-/- mice and found that while insulin levels (Fig. 1H) were significantly decreased, proinsulin (Fig. 1I) was markedly elevated in knockout mice in comparison to littermate control mice, indicating impaired β-cell function. Consequently, proinsulin: insulin ratio (Fig. 1J) was significantly increased (more than 3-fold) in knockout mice, suggesting β-cell stress. However, at 24 weeks of age, serum insulin (Fig. 1K) and proinsulin (Fig. 1L) levels as well as the proinsulin: insulin ratio (Fig. 1M) of euglycemic Xbp1β-/- mice were comparable to those of non-diabetic control mice, pointing to restored β-cell function and alleviated stress by this age.

A similar observation was seen when the same pancreatic sections were stained with anti-insulin and anti-somatostatin antibodies (Fig. 2B, D). To elucidate the islet inflammation (insulitis), we analyzed hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained pancreatic sections from 8 weeks of age Xbp1fl/fl and Xbp1β-/- mice and showed that knockout mice had markedly reduced islet infiltration by immune cells (Fig. 2E). To quantify the extent of immune infiltration, we performed insulitis scoring on pancreatic sections and determined that both aggressive and non-aggressive insulitis were markedly diminished in Xbp1β-/- mice at 8 weeks of age (Fig. 2F). Consistent with reduced insulitis, the percentage of intact islets that are free from immune infiltration was markedly high in Xbp1β-/- mice compared to control mice (Fig. 2G). IF staining of pancreata for immune subpopulation markers CD3, CD4, CD8, and B220 revealed apparent reduction in the number of these cells in Xbp1β-/- mice at 8 weeks of age (Fig. 2H). At 31 weeks of age, islet immune infiltration (Fig. 2I) and aggressive and non-aggressive insulitis (Fig. 2J) showed a greater degree of difference between control and knockout islets. The percentage of intact islets was significantly higher in Xbp1β-/- mice compared to control mice (Fig. 2K). While islets of control mice exhibited marked accumulation of by CD3, CD4, CD8, and B220 positive cells, knockout islets showed minimal presence of these cells at 31 weeks of age (Fig. 2L).

We next examined the distributions and proportions of immune cell populations involved in T1D pathogenesis within the pancreas, spleen, and pancreatic lymph nodes (PLNs) of 21-week-old control and knockout mice. Specifically, we analyzed T cells (CD4+ and CD8+), Tregs (CD25+, FoxP3+), macrophages (F4/80+), B cells (CD19+), and dendritic cells (CD11c+). Our analysis showed significantly higher the percentage of CD19+ B cells, and markedly reduced percentages of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the pancreata of XBP1β-/- mice (Fig. 2M). The percentage of T regulatory cells (Tregs), macrophages, or dendritic cells did not show significant alterations in the pancreas of knockout animals (Fig. 2M). There were no differences in any of these immune cell populations within the spleens of Xbp1β-/- mice compared to control animals (Fig. 2N), while a moderate reduction in CD8+ T cells and a marked increase in F4/80+ cells were detected in PLN of knockout animals (Fig. 2O). Hence, deletion of Xbp1 in β-cells in NOD mice significantly reduced the relative representation of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells specifically in the pancreas. This phenotype was reminiscent of IRE1αβ-/- mice where the percentage of CD4+ T cells showed a trend towards declining, and the percentage of CD8+ T cells was significantly diminished. However, increased percentage of CD19+ B cell phenotype as well as alterations in representation of CD8+ T cells and F4/80+ cells in PLN were unique to XBP1β-/- mice. Taken together, these data suggest that significantly decreased islet infiltration by immune cells particularly reduced frequency of islet-reactive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells contributed to the β-cell survival in Xbp1β-/- mice.

To examine whether Xbp1 deletion alters proliferation in β-cells, we performed IF staining with anti-insulin and anti-Ki67 antibodies and observed similar levels of proliferation in β-cells of Xbp1fl/fl and Xbp1β-/- mice (Fig. 2P, Q). Furthermore, quantification of TUNEL staining revealed no significant differences in apoptosis at 8 weeks of age in β-cells of Xbp1β-/- mice, but a marked reduction at 31 weeks of age compared to Xbp1fl/fl mice (Fig. 2R, S). Altogether, our findings show that Xbp1β-/- mice have significantly reduced levels of inflammation and apoptosis culminating into protection from T1D.

Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals reduced expression of β-cell maturity markers and autoantigens in Xbp1β-/- mice

To gain insight into the molecular mechanisms by which Xbp1β-/- mice exhibit protection against T1D, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on dispersed islet cells from Xbp1fl/fl and Xbp1β-/- mice at 5 weeks of age. UMAP visualization separated the cells into distinct populations consisting mainly of β-, α-, and δ-cells, with a smaller population of ductal, endothelial, and immune cells (Fig. 3A). Notably, while the cells from Xbp1fl/fl and Xbp1β-/- mice mostly cluster together, the β-cell populations are separated into two distinct populations (Fig. 3A). We then examined the expression of islet cell markers within the β-cell clusters and found that expression of α- and δ-cell markers (Ttr, Rbp4) were markedly increased (Fig. 3C), while markers of mature β-cells were significantly decreased (MafA, Slc2a2, Ucn3) (Fig. 3C). Meanwhile, the expression of disallowed genes (Olfm1, Oat, Ndrg4, Mgst3, and Ly6a), whose regulation is tightly controlled to be highly repressed in mature β-cells37,38 were substantially increased (Fig. 3D) in β-cells of Xbp1β-/- mice, suggesting loss of maturity identity. Furthermore, we observed significantly increased expression of progenitor genes (Fabp3, Gast, Aldh1a3, CD81)39–41 in β-cells of Xbp1β-/- mice (Fig. 3E). The significant decrease in β-cell markers and marked increase in disallowed and progenitor genes suggested that β-cells of Xbp1β-/- mice were undergoing dedifferentiation, a phenotype that we observed in NOD Ire1αβ-/- mice34.

Stressed β-cells can alter recruitment of immune cells, antigen expression, and presentation. Indeed, we detected significantly decreased expression of antigens (Ptprn, Ins1, Ins2, and Chga) in β-cells of Xbp1β-/- mice (Fig. 3F). In addition, expression of genes that inhibit the immune response was significantly upregulated (e.g., Ildr2, H2-T23), while those that activate it were downregulated (e.g., Tnfrsf23, Traf3ip2) (Fig. 3G). Lastly, to investigate whether Xbp1 deficiency could cause potential compensatory upregulation of IRE1α/RIDD activity42,43, we examined the expression of RIDD target genes within the β-cell cluster. As expected, mRNA expression of RIDD targets was significantly upregulated in NOD Ire1αβ-/- mice upon loss of IRE1α’s RNAse activity (Fig. 3H). However, we did not detect any significant changes in the expression of RIDD target genes in β-cells of Xbp1β-/- mice (Fig. 3H), suggesting that loss of Xbp1 does not cause a compensatory increase in RIDD activity. The lack of RIDD activity and its maladaptive consequences, including cell death24,44 in β-cells of Xbp1β-/- mice, were consistent with the diabetes protection phenotype observed in these mice. Altogether, these findings suggest that dedifferentiated β-cells have reduced antigen expression and increased immune inhibitory gene profile at this time point and that this reduced immunogenicity allowed these cells to escape immune attack.

Gene regulatory network analysis using single-cell transcriptome datasets from Xbp1β-/- and Ire1αβ-/- mice

To gain insight into the mechanism by which dedifferentiated β-cells trigger appropriate transcriptional changes, we aimed to define shared and unique regulatory programs across the Xbp1β-/- and Ire1αβ-/- mice by performing gene regulatory network (GRN) analysis. However, doing so is challenging because environmental conditions (mouse facility, water pH, etc.) as well as usage of different tissue specific Cre lines can lead to inconsistent results in gene knockout studies, making it hard to interpret the results. This becomes even more prominent for studies with NOD mice, as it is well established that slight changes in environment can impact the microbiome and alter disease progression and transcriptional program45–47. Hereby using the same Cre line, same deletion induction modality, and housing conditions, we generated single-cell transcriptome datasets from Xbp1β-/- and Ire1αβ-/- mice34, allowing to perform a robust and reliable comparative study of the genotype-specific GRN analysis.

We applied Monocle348 to carry out initial filtering and depth normalization of the data. We identified 14 clusters, which we annotated based on the expression of known marker genes. Of the 14, clusters 1 and 4 expressed β-cell markers (Ins1, Ins2) (Fig. 4B, C, Supplementary Fig. 1A–C), cluster 2 expressed α-cell marker (Gcg) and cluster 3 expressed δ-cell marker (Sst). Importantly, 90% of the cells we labeled as β-cells in the Xbp1β-/- genotype agreed with those from Fig. 3. To identify the GRNs associated with β-cells across the three genotypes, we applied MERLIN to the β-cell clusters50 (Supplementary Fig. 2A,B). MERLIN is an expression-based Gene Regulatory Network inference tool that predicts regulators of individual genes and gene modules. MERLIN identified 47791 edges at a high confidence of 80%, connecting 2166 regulators to 15784 target genes (Supplementary Data 1). The genes were further organized into 161 modules of at least 10 genes (Supplementary Data 2) of which 65 were enriched in a Gene Ontology (GO) process (Supplementary Data 3) and 7 were enriched in a sequence motif of a transcription factor. We characterized the expression patterns of these modules based on a t-test of genes in each module between the different genotypes (e.g., Xbp1β-/- and Ire1αβ-/- versus control (Fig. 4D)). Most of the modules exhibited either a common down regulation pattern between the two genotypes (13 modules, 701 genes, Shared-Down, (Fig. 4D)) or common upregulated (46 modules comprising 3199 genes, Shared-Up, (Fig. 4E)) in the mutants versus wild type, with more genes exhibiting the Shared-Up versus the Shared-Down pattern (Supplementary Data 4). Three modules comprising 534 genes were specifically down-regulated in Xbp1β-/- compared to Ire1αβ-/- (Xbp1β-/- Down, Fig. 4F), and 6 modules comprising 2769 genes were specifically up-regulated in the Xbp1β-/- compared to Ire1αβ-/- (Xbp1β-/- Up, Fig. 4G, Supplementary Data 4).

Modules with the Shared-Down pattern were enriched for metabolic processes and associated with regulators such as prolactin regulatory element-binding (Preb) and nucleoside diphosphate kinase 2 (Nme2 also known as NDPK) (Fig. 4D bottom). Preb is known to regulate insulin in pancreatic β-cells upon glucose stimulation51, whereas Nme2, has protective roles in the liver under a high-fat diet52. In addition, targeting a hormonal complex consisting of fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP4), adenosine kinase (ADK), and NDPK (FABP4-ADK-NDPK) preserves β-cell mass and enhances β-cell function to protect against diabetes in multiple models, including NOD mice53. Modules with the Shared-Up pattern were additionally enriched for developmental processes and associated with genes like Onecut1 (also known as Hnf6), Gata5, and Nfe2l3 (also known as Nrf3). Onecut1/Hnf6 is involved in monogenic recessive and monogenic dominant, as well as multifactorial, diabetes54 while Gata5 zinc-finger transcriptional factor is important in the development and differentiation of numerous tissues, including many endodermally-derived organs55. Nrf3 is known to regulate antioxidant responses, cell differentiation, inflammatory responses, lipid homeostasis, and immune responses56 (Fig. 4E). Modules exhibiting downregulation in Xbp1β-/- mice (Xbp1β-/- Down) were enriched for metabolic processes (Fig. 4F), while modules specifically upregulated in Xbp1β-/- compared to Ire1αβ-/- (Xbp1β-/- Up) were enriched for differentiation, development, and immune processes (Fig. 4G). The largest Xbp1β-/- Up module (679 genes), 3970, was also enriched for XBP1 motif, further supporting this module to be regulated by XBP1. Taken together, these results suggest that IRE1α and XBP1 function in β-cells using many of the same transcriptional programs that include both developmental and metabolic processes; however, there are several processes, especially related to cell fate specification, that are enhanced in Xbp1β-/- compared to Ire1αβ-/-.

Alterations in expression among control, Xbp1β-/-, and Ire1αβ-/- could be mediated through changes in regulatory network structure (gain or loss of connections); through changes in node expression levels; or through changes in the strength of regulation while using a similar structure. To assess the extent to which the β-cell GRN changed between the control, Xbp1β-/-, and Ire1αβ-/- genotypes, we selected modules exhibiting a differential expression pattern and association with β-cell biology, metabolic, and immune processes, and applied a regression approach to each module’s GRN structure that we previously developed57 (See Methods).

When comparing modules exhibiting the Shared patterns, most exhibited a change in node levels between the control and both knockout models (Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Data 1) but the structure of the networks and strength of connections were largely similar across control and knockout mice. A few exceptions were GRN components of modules (Supplementary Data 5), which were centered on regulators activating transcription factor 5 (Atf5) and anti-silencing function protein 1 homolog a (Asf1a). Atf5 was predicted as a regulator of 2 Shared-Down modules and 94 genes including module 3339 which exhibited a lost a connection to methylenetetrahydrofolate dehydrogenase 2 (Mthfd2), a mitochondrial enzyme of the folate-mediated one-carbon metabolism pathway, and gained connections to zinc finger RANBP2-type containing 2 (Zranb2) and TATA-Box binding protein associated factor 15 (Taf15) in the control compared to the deletion genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Asf1a was a top regulator of module 3897 with the Shared-Up pattern (136 genes), which was enriched for cilium morphogenesis a key process for proper functioning of pancreatic islets and ductal cells58,59. Asf1a exhibited a change in regulation strength defined by the regression weight to its target genes (Supplementary Fig. 3B). We observed more differences in GRN structure when comparing Xbp1β-/- and Ire1αβ-/- (Fig. 4H,I, Supplementary Fig. 3C-E, Supplementary Data 1). For example, module 3513 (Xbp1β-/- Down, Fig. 4H) enriched for immune response function included three regulators phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit gamma (Pik3cg), myosin light chain kinase 2 (Mylk2), and LYN proto-oncogene, Src family tyrosine kinase (Lyn) that exhibited change in GRN structure at the connectivity level between Xbp1β-/- and Ire1αβ-/-. Pik3cg, which had the largest number of target connections, changed in strength (Plex, Slc6a14, and Ccl3) between the two genotypes. Pik3g’s own regulation was also different, with its two regulators Lyn and Btbd11, exhibiting a weaker connection in Xbp1β-/- compared to Ire1αβ-/-. This GRN also included Mylk2, one of the top regulators for genes downregulated in Xbp1β-/- (Fig. 4F) and exhibited a differential regulation of its targets chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 3 (Ccl3) (also known as macrophage inflammatory protein 1-alpha (Mip-1α)), a gene that plays a role in inflammatory responses through binding to the receptors CCR1, CCR4 and CCR5, and Gm32089.

Another module 3918 (Fig. 4I), exhibiting Xbp1β-/- Up pattern and enriched for immune response processes, included several top regulators, ArfGAP with coiled-coil, ankyrin repeat and PH domains 1 (Acap1), and interferon regulatory factor 4 (Irf4), that were up-regulated in expression in Xbp1β-/- compared to Ire1αβ-/-. The Acap1, whose expression was shown to be positively associated with the infiltrating level of immune cells in tumor microenvironment and the expression of immune checkpoint molecules60, exhibited stronger connections to its targets (e.g., Arhgap27os3, S1pr4, and Garp) in Xbp1β-/- compared to Ire1αβ-/-. In contrast, Irf4, a transcription factor and key regulator of immune cell development and function, exhibited an opposite pattern with stronger connections in Ire1αβ-/- compared to Xbp1β-/-. Irf4 and its targets are more downregulated in Ire1αβ-/- than in Xbp1β-/-, suggesting a relatively enhanced expression of an immune response program in Xbp1β-/- compared to Ire1αβ-/-.

We also examined the selected genes for the biological processes outlined in Fig. 3 using both modules and GRNs (Supplementary Data 6, Supplementary Fig. 4). Known XBP1 targets were largely associated with Shared-Down phenotype, which is consistent with XBP1’s role as a transcriptional activator for mediating Ire1α responses. In contrast, genes in the dedifferentiation (Fig. 3E) and auto immune functions (Fig. 3F, G) were largely in the Xbp1β-/- Up or Xbp1β-/- Down modules, which are modules exhibiting differential expression compared to Ire1αβ-/-. Finally, we examined the extent to which the regulatory connections with these genes were rewired (change in strength of regulation measured by regression weight) between the genotypes (Supplementary Fig. 4). The target genes that exhibited more rewiring include Gastrin (Gast) and fatty acid binding protein 3 (Fabp3), both of which are implicated in β-cell function and health (Supplementary Fig. 4, Supplementary Data 6). Gast, a fetal hormone, is a marker for reversible human β-cell reprogramming in diabetes61, while Fabp3 is associated with fatty acid metabolism and can regulate β-cell viability, Pdx1 gene expression, and the insulin-secreting function under lipotoxic conditions62. Partial depletion of Fabp3 in a high fat diet mouse model improves whole-body insulin sensitivity63.

Taken together, our GRN comparisons across the genotypes show that between the control and both deletion mutants, most of the GRN preserves its structure while the participating genes are differentially regulated between the control and mutants. There is a greater change when comparing Xbp1β-/- to Ire1αβ-/- both at the node and connection level for several regulatory hubs indicative of unique roles of XBP1-independent of IRE1α.

To assess the cell non-autonomous impact of these perturbations, we repeated our MERLIN GRN network analysis with the α-cells (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B, Supplementary Data 7, 8). This GRN connected 2,164 regulators to 14,494 targets at a confidence of at least 0.8 which were further organized into 98 modules of at least 10 genes. Fifty modules exhibited similar patterns of Shared-Down (Supplementary Fig. 6A), Shared-Up (Supplementary Fig. 6B), Xbp1β-/- Down (Supplementary Fig. 6C), or Xbp1β-/- Up (Supplementary Fig. 6D), but to a lesser extent compared to β-cells (Shared-Up: 3, Shared-Down: 1, Xbp1β-/- Up: 3, Xbp1β-/- Down: 5 (Supplementary Fig. 6, Supplementary Data 9,10). 48 of the total 98 modules were enriched in a GO process, which spanned processes such as cell cycle, development, as well as stress response (Supplementary Data 11). We ranked regulators based on the difference in importance in each cell type (Supplementary Data 12). Among the top regulators were Gpx4 in β-cells, and Nfat5 and Meg3 in α-cells. Gpx4 is highly expressed in β-cells in comparison to other cell types and protects β-cells from oxidative damage and ferroptosis, iron accumulation-associated cell death, which makes it crucial for β-cell survival64. The role of Nfat5 and Meg3 in α-cells is not well understood; however, both these regulators have been shown to be important in apoptosis65,66.

Discussion

Here, we report that deletion of Xbp1 in β-cells of NOD mice prior to insulitis protects mice against T1D. Xbp1β-/- mice largely phenocopy Ire1αβ-/- mice by showing a transient dedifferentiation, reduced autoantigens, islet inflammation, and apoptosis. As IRE1α plays multiple roles other than activating Xbp1, linking its loss-of-function phenotypic outcome in β-cells of NOD mice specifically to XBP1 is significant, especially since T1D protective effects of pharmacological inhibition of IRE1α was previously attributed to inhibition of its RIDD activity in the same model35.

By using two data sets, we not only identified the shared transcriptional targets of IRE1α and XBP1, but also unique regulatory programs of these genes. Our studies indicate that these unique programs are associated with developmental processes, immune response as well as genes involved in metabolism. Regulators associated with such genes could serve as novel targets to modulate the activity of the pathways they regulate to better understand the dedifferentiation and autoimmune phenotypes and potentially followed up as future therapeutic targets.

IRE1α is necessary for Xbp1 splicing and activation, however, previous gene deletion studies showed that IRE1α deficiency does not always mimic the phenotypes observed in XBP1-deficient mice67. While the differences in phenotypes may stem from the experimental and/or environmental conditions, IRE1α can engage multiple pathways independent of XBP1 activation. It is also possible that XBP1 null mice can have unique phenotypes due to loss of unspliced XBP1 (uXBP1). uXBP1 does not possess transcriptional activity. It shuttles between the nucleus and the cytoplasm and regulates its target genes post-translationally by mediating their degradation or nuclear exclusion68–71. While the function and targets of uXBP1 remain poorly understood, it can play a modulatory role in the UPR, control autophagy through FoxO1, protect endothelial cells from oxidative stress, regulate cell cycle, and vascular calcification72. Hence, it is likely that the unique transcriptional nodes and regulations observed in β-cells of Xbp1β-/- mice, at least in part, are reflections of uXBP1-mediated regulation.

We previously reported that protein levels of sXBP1 peak at 5 weeks of age and then gradually decrease in β-cells of NOD mice5. Considering our current study, we propose a two-phase model for the temporal action of IRE1α/XBP1 in T1D progression. In phase one, during the early stages of the disease (prior to insulitis), it is likely that metabolic stress induced by weaning on already stressed NOD background (Glis3 and Xrcc4 mutations on this background render β-cells sensitive to ER stress73) leads to hyperactivity of XBP1, triggering exacerbated ER stress. Inhibiting IRE1α/XBP1 pathway during this stage is beneficial and results in protection from T1D. In phase two, at the prediabetic stage (once insulitis is established), in the presence of a persistent inflammatory milieu, cells fail to adapt to stress and XBP1 levels start declining, leading to loss of XBP1-mediated adaptive responses (e.g., oxidative stress handling, ER biogenesis etc.), while terminal UPR (RIDD activity) becomes significantly elevated at this time point35. Hence, inhibition of RIDD at this stage would be beneficial. This notion is supported by the findings that inhibiting RIDD activity with imatinib at the prediabetic stage protects NOD mice against diabetes74. These works suggest the importance of timing and mode of administration of ER modulating agents in the clinic setting. As currently several ER pathways modulating drugs are being tested in clinical trials for individuals with new onset T1D15, our findings have the potential to pave the way for the development of more specific and fine-tuned therapeutic strategies.

Methods

The animal care and experimental procedures of this study were carried out in accordance with the recommendations of the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The protocol (#M005064-R01-A03 by F.E. for mice) was approved by the University of Wisconsin-Madison Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Mouse lines and tamoxifen injections

As the diabetes incidence is significantly higher in female NOD mice, we used only female mice in this study. Female NOD/ShiLtJ mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. All animals had ad libitum access to food (Envigo 2919) and water and were housed at 20-24 °C on a 12 hours light/12 hours dark cycle. Mice were bred and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the University of Wisconsin-Madison under approved protocols. The Xbp1 floxed mice were a gift from Dr. Laurie Glimcher (Harvard University)43. Ins2CreERT/+ mice were a gift of Dr. Douglas Melton (Harvard University)75. Mice were backcrossed to NOD background for more than 20 generations. The genetic backgrounds of all intercrossed mouse models were verified by Jackson Laboratory’s Genome Scan Service and the congenicity was reported as 100%. To induce Cre recombinase activity, tamoxifen (T5648; Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in sterilized corn oil (C8267; Sigma-Aldrich) by shaking overnight in a 37 °C incubator. The solution was protected from light, and 10 mg/ml tamoxifen was administered to dams the day after delivery via intraperitoneal injection twice every 24 hours for five consecutive days. Weekly blood glucose measurements were performed after 6 hours of fasting through the tail tip using a CONTOUR Next glucometer (Ascensia) with CONTOUR Next test strips (Ascensia). Mice with a fasting blood glucose level of ≥250 mg/dL for two consecutive measurements were classified as diabetic. Animals were observed daily for health status, any mice that met IACUC criteria for euthanasia were immediately euthanized. Experiments were performed on female mice between 3 and 50 weeks of age.

Histological analyses

For paraffin sections, pancreata from mice were fixed with 10% zinc formalin overnight and paraffin-embedded, and 5 µm sections of the pancreata were generated. For frozen sections, the harvested pancreata were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (VWR), embedded in OCT (Sakura) and frozen, and 10 µm sections of the pancreata were generated. Staining was performed after blocking with 5% (paraffin) or 10% (frozen) normal goat serum with the following antibodies: anti-Insulin (Linco, 4011-01; 1:200, and Agilent, IR002; 1:6), anti-Glucagon (Cell Signaling, 2760S; 1:100), anti-Somatostatin (Santa Cruz, sc-74556; 1:50), anti-Ki67 (Cell Signaling, 9129; 1:100), anti-CD3 (Novus Biologicals, NB600-1441; 1:100), anti-CD4 (Cell Signaling, 25229; 1:20), anti-CD8 (Cell Signaling, 98941; 1:150), anti-B220 (eBioscience, 14-0452-85; 1:200), anti-XBP1 (Santa Cruz, sc-7160; 1:20 and homemade; 1:20), Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A-11073; 1:400), and Alexa Fluor 568 (Invitrogen, A-11036; 1:400) using established protocols. After staining, slides were mounted with antifade mounting medium containing 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Vector Laboratories).

Antigen retrieval was performed by using citrate buffer pH 6.0 (paraffin) or HistoVT (Nacalai Tesque) (frozen). Insulitis scoring was performed on step sections (three levels separated by 200 µm) of paraffin-embedded and hematoxylin-eosin-stained pancreatic tissue. “Peri-insulitis” is defined as a focal aggregation at one pole of the islet and in contact with the islet periphery. “Non-aggressive insulitis” refers to lesions with a clear, and often extensive, islet infiltrate occupying less than 50% of the islet area, whereas “aggressive insulitis” refers to an extensive infiltrate, where lymphoid cells invade the entire islet and intermingle with endocrine cells, showing extensive signs of β-cell damage. The images of the pancreatic sections were obtained using a Nikon A1R-SI+ confocal microscope and a Nikon epifluorescence microscope. The images were analyzed by ImageJ or Fiji. Two blinded individuals independently performed manual analyses and insulitis scoring.

TUNEL assay

DeadEnd Fluorometric TUNEL assay (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI) was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded pancreatic sections according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunophenotyping

Prior to organ dissection, mice were perfused with 20 mL PBS to eliminate contaminating blood leukocytes. Single-cell suspensions of the pancreata were prepared by Collagenase P (Sigma-Aldrich) digestion. Cells from pancreatic lymph nodes and spleen were prepared by physical dissociation. All stainings began with incubation with TruStain FcX anti-mouse CD16/32 (BioLegend). Antibodies used for subsequent stainings were anti-CD45 (30-F11), -CD19 (1D3/CD19), -CD3 (145-2C11), -CD4 (17A2), -CD8 (53-6.7), -CD25 (PC61), CD11b (M1/70), -CD11c (N418), and -F4/80 (BM8) (all from BioLegend; 1:100). Intracellular Foxp3 (Biolegend, 150D; 1:20) staining was performed according to eBioscience’s protocol. Samples were acquired with a Cytek Aurora Spectral Cytometer, and data were analyzed with FlowJo software (Tree Star, Inc.). Gating strategy is provided (Supplementary Method).

Islet Isolation

Islets were isolated using the standard collagenase/protease digestion method76. Briefly, the pancreatic duct was cannulated and distended with 4 °C collagenase/protease solution using Collagenase P (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in 1x Hank’s balanced salt solution and 0.02% bovine serum albumin. The protease reaction was stopped using RPMI 1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Islets were separated from the exocrine tissue using Histopaque-1077 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Hand-picked islets were cultured overnight at 37 °C in RPMI-1640 media containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic/antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific) before use in experiments.

ELISA

Serum insulin and proinsulin levels of mice were quantified by ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Ultra-Sensitive Mouse Insulin ELISA, Crystalchem; Mercodia Rat/Mouse Proinsulin ELISA). All samples were run in duplicate.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Single-cell transcriptome analysis on islets was followed as previously described34,36,76. Briefly, isolated islets were cultured overnight and dissociated for 30 minutes at 37 °C using Accutase (Innovative Cell Technologies). Library preparation was performed as instructed by the Chromium Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kit v2 (10x Genomics). Final libraries were quantified using the Qubit High Sensitivity DNA Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Dataset pre-processing, clustering, and batch effect removal

We used Monocle348 to carry out initial filtering and depth normalization of the data. Cells with a unique molecular identifier (UMI) count of below 5000 were excluded. To identify differential gene expression (DGE) between Xbp1fl/fl and Xbp1β-/- mice, DGE analysis was performed on β-cells that were subsetted based on insulin expression. Violin plots were generated using the ggplot function in R.

Combining the Xbp1 and Ire1α deletion scRNA-seq datasets produced a matrix of 16911 cells and 32933 genes. Initial analysis showed batch effects between these two datasets. We used scVI to perform batch effect correction on the Monocle normalized data, dimensionality reduction, and clustering of the cells from both batches of data. scVI was applied to the normalized matrix from Monocle3. Visualization of the corrected matrices showed that biologically similar samples (e.g., controls) from both batches grouped together. We annotated the cell clusters for different pancreatic cell types by visualizing the expression level of known cell type markers in scVI clusters and identified α-, δ-, and two β-cell clusters. Specifically, we used Gcg for α-cells, Sst for δ-cells, and Ins1 and Ins2 for β-cells. We further characterized the clusters based on differentially expressed genes in each cluster after batch effect correction (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. 1).

Gene Regulatory Network Inference analysis

We performed GRN analysis on the two β-cell clusters (clusters 1 and 4) and the α-cell cluster (cluster 2). We selected genes expressed in at least 50 cells in each cluster and then took the union of gene sets of clusters as the gene set used in gene regulatory network inference. This resulted in a total of 16849 genes in our dataset. Next, we applied the gene regulatory network inference algorithm to each of these clusters of interest with Modular regulatory network learning with per gene information (MERLIN) algorithm50. MERLIN is a probabilistic graphical model based on dependency network inference, which gives the regulatory information among genes and transcription factors with a modularity fashion. We set up a stability selection framework by creating 100 subsamples. For each subsample, we randomly selected 50% from the processed expression matrix and ran MERLIN algorithm on it. The final GRN for both β- and α-cells was defined by regulator target genes with at least 80% confidence. We also defined consensus modules by selecting the gene pairs that are in the same MERLIN module in more than 60% subsamples.

We assigned each module a phenotype (e.g., Shared-Up, Xbp1β-/- Down) based on the t-test of genes in each module between the different genotypes. For this purpose, we took pseudobulk expression for each sample and identified differentially expressed modules between deletion and control samples using our MATLAB script (https://github.com/Roy-lab/pancreatic_cell_grns). This was done only for modules with 10 or more genes. We also performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis for modules of at least 5 genes using the hypergeometric test followed by a false discovery rate (FDR) correction method of Benjamini and Hotchberg. An FDR of 0.05 was used to determine whether a module was enriched in a GO process. We prioritized modules by considering their biological relevance based on multiple criteria such as the assigned phenotype, GO enrichment of module genes, and enriched regulators per module.

Differential network analysis

We selected modules based on based on their expression patterns, enrichment of pancreas-related metabolic processes and association with known β-cell marker genes (Supplementary Data 5). When comparing the two controls with the knockout samples, we used both β-cell clusters 1 and 4. When comparing knockout samples among each other, we used clusters 1 and 4 separately, focusing on results from cluster4, which exhibited a greater change in the knockout compared to control.

To perform network rewiring analysis, we calculated the regression weights between the regulator and targets for the network from each genotype and sample. We calculated regression coefficients between regulators and targets for each genotype using corresponding genotype-specific cells. To select GRN edges with the greatest differences between genotypes, we filtered the edges based on a z-score weight. Briefly, for shared-Down and Shared-Up modules, we took mean of regression weights for control and knockout samples separately for each edge and took the difference between mean regression weights of control and knockout samples. We converted these scores into a z-score and filtered out edges falling below 1.5 standard deviations. For Xbp1β-/- Down and Xbp1β-/- Up, we applied the same procedure to regression weights of Xbp1β-/- and Ire1αβ-/- samples alone focusing on cluster 4. For node color, we obtained zero-mean normalized expression for each gene across all cells together and then took the mean of normalized expression values for each gene from each genotype-specific expression submatrices. We used Cytoscape version 3.10 for network visualization.

Comparison of α- and β-cell GRNs

To compare the GRNs of β- and α-cells, we focused on the top regulators. To identify the top regulators, we calculated the predictive power of each regulator using each cell type’s inferred GRN using a procedure described previously77. Predictive power is defined as the reduction in accuracy observed when removing a specific regulator from its functional role in predicting the expression of target genes based on the expression levels of its regulators. Regulators were sorted based on the difference in their predictive power between β- and α-cell GRNs (Supplementary Data 12). Regulators with the most positive difference were considered as top regulators for β-cells, while regulators with the most negative difference were considered as top regulators for α-cells.

Statistics & reproducibility

For all experiments, the statistical analyses, error bars, and number of biological replicates are described in the figure legends. A minimum of n = 3 or more biological replicates were used for all statistical analyses. Sample sizes were based on pilot experiments and previously published work5,34,36. For quantification of IF images and insulitis scoring, each sample was blinded for data analysis to prevent bias. No data were excluded from the analyses. Data are represented as mean ± SEM and were analyzed in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).
