Anoximixis: Mixing of anoxic bottom waters influences biogeochemical and ecological processes in lakes
Authors: Lienne R Sethna, Meredith A Holgerson, Adam J Heathcote, Ana M Morales, Katherine J Stenehjem, David C Richardson, Roxane Maranger, Michelle B Balmer, Nicole D Wagner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change is reshaping lake ecosystems globally, notably through alterations to thermal stratification and mixing regimes. This synthesis describes “anoximixis,” the complete mixing of a stratified waterbody with anoxic bottom waters, and reviews the biogeochemical and ecological implications of anoximixis outside of seasonal turnover events. We posit that climate-driven changes to temperature, wind patterns, and storm events may lead to increased frequency of anoximixis in freshwater ecosystems, which could in turn alter biogeochemical cycling, flux, and sequestration of nutrients, contaminants, and C. Anoximixis may also reorganize lake trophic structure, favoring fast-growing phytoplankton species and cyanobacteria, which will alter energy and C transfer to higher trophic levels. Current lake management strategies do not fully address anoximixis-driven water quality degradation, and some may even unintentionally promote anoximixis. Finally, we recommend that future research needs to test the predictions presented here to expand our understanding and management of anoximixis.Keywords: climate change, lake stratification, biogeochemistry, lake management, anoxia

Climate change is fundamentally altering the physical and biogeochemical dynamics of lakes worldwide, with profound effects on ecosystem function (Butcher et al. 2015, Woolway et al. 2020). One of the key processes affected is lake mixing regimes, which play a critical role in regulating sediment redox potential (Text S1; Smith et al. 2011, Molot et al. 2021), internal nutrient release (James 2017a, Mesman et al. 2021), carbon (C) cycling (Bartosiewicz et al. 2019, Ray and Holgerson 2023), and food web structure (North et al. 2014). Thermal stratification is intensifying due to increasing global air temperatures, earlier onset of stratification, enhanced thermal shielding from increased eutrophication and browning, and reduced average wind speeds (Jankowski et al. 2006, Woolway et al. 2017), which may allow historically well-mixed, shallow lakes to intermittently stratify and develop bottom water anoxia (Bartosiewicz et al. 2019). At the same time, more frequent and severe storm events are disrupting stratified conditions in deeper dimictic or monomictic lakes at atypical moments, introducing intermittent mixing periods in lakes that were previously more stable seasonally (Klug et al. 2012, Holgerson et al. 2022). When these atypical mixing events include the mixing of anoxic bottom waters, termed “anoximixis” by Sethna and colleagues (2025), there can be profound effects to lake physical, chemical, and biological properties. In this synthesis, we present a set of mechanistic predictions for how lake ecosystems could shift if climate-driven changes in stratification and disturbance regimes increase the frequency of anoximixis. Given the uncertainty surrounding these interacting processes, there is a critical need to test how changes in anoximixis influence aquatic ecosystem function, particularly in shallow and mid-depth lakes that may be most sensitive to these dynamics.

When anoximixis occurs outside the traditional bounds of spring and fall turnover in temperate climates, it can fundamentally alter ecosystem function for days to weeks or months (figure 1; Isles et al. 2015, Vidon et al. 2018, Sethna et al. 2025). We contend that the effects of anoximixis are distinct from seasonal turnover due to the occurrence in the middle of the growing season when light availability and warm temperatures can favor algal growth and respiration. First, periods of stratification can promote anoxia and the release of nutrients and contaminants from sediments to bottom waters, along with the build-up and flux of greenhouse gases (GHGs; Bastviken et al. 2004, LaBrie et al. 2023, Davidson et al. 2024). When anoximictic events occur, these nutrients, contaminants, and GHGs can be pulsed from anoxic bottom waters to the surface, leading to enhanced primary productivity (Wilhelm and Adrian 2008, Giling et al. 2017), contaminant bioaccumulation (Borch et al. 2010, Linnik et al. 2023), and GHG emissions (Davidson et al. 2024, Rabaey and Cotner 2024). Episodic shifts in dissolved oxygen levels may also disrupt aquatic food webs and seasonal community succession, influencing species distributions and trophic transfer efficiency in food webs (Wilhelm and Adrian 2008, Filstrup et al. 2014, Heathcote et al. 2016).

In this synthesis, we formally define “anoximixis” following Sethna and colleagues (2025) and review the potential biogeochemical and ecological implications for lake ecosystems. We predict that climate change will increase the frequency of anoximixis through two main pathways: (1) warming temperatures may increase stratification and amplify bottom water anoxia in previously oxic and well-mixed systems, thereby creating anoximixis at the next turnover event (Rose et al. 2016, Bartosiewicz et al. 2019, Jane et al. 2021, LaBrie and Maranger 2024, Rose et al. 2024), and (2) more severe and frequent storm events may induce atypical summertime anoximixis in dimictic or monomictic lakes (Kimura et al. 2012, Klug et al. 2012). We present several predictions for how lake ecosystems could shift if climate change increases the frequency of anoximixis and assert the critical need to examine how climate-driven changes in anoximixis could affect aquatic ecosystem function, particularly in shallow lakes and ponds.

Anoximixis: a definition

We define anoximixis as the complete mixing of a stratified waterbody with anoxic bottom waters. Here, we focus on short-duration, intermittent mixing of anoxic bottom waters rather than more classically observed seasonal turnover in monomictic and dimictic lakes, which may also be characterized as anoximixis. We emphasize complete mixing events, rather than partial mixing, although we recognize that partial mixing events, including those caused by seiches or Langmuir circulation, can also entrain nutrient-rich hypolimnetic waters into the upper water column or disturb sediments, both of which can contribute to internal nutrient loading and degrade water quality (Kelly et al. 2024, Gushulak et al. 2025). The magnitude of anoximixis-driven ecosystem responses is expected to scale with the duration and extent of preceding anoxia, hypolimnetic volume, and nutrient accumulation. Thus, although shallow lakes and ponds often have smaller hypolimnetic volumes than deeper lakes, they may also be more prone to rapid anoxia and repeated mixing events.

The concept of polymixis has long been documented for shallow systems that intermittently stratify and mix (Cortés et al. 2021, Søndergaard et al. 2023b, Wagner et al. 2023, Swann et al. 2024); however, anoximixis would only occur in polymictic lakes that stratify for long enough to produce anoxic bottom waters, which is not inherent to all polymictic systems (Sethna et al. 2025). Furthermore, repeated anoximictic events may amplify ecosystem responses by promoting positive feedbacks between anoxia, nutrient release, productivity, and respiration that are not typically observed during seasonal turnover (Lewis et al. 2024). Such cycles can amplify internal nutrient loading—mobilizing nutrient-rich, reduced bottom waters and sediment porewaters that can sustain high productivity even in lakes with relatively low external nutrient inputs (Nürnberg and LaZerte 2016). Due to the global increase in water temperature, anoxia, and severe storm events (Klug et al. 2012, Diffenbaugh et al. 2013, O’Reilly et al. 2015, Doubek et al. 2021, Jane et al. 2023), we predict anoximixis will become more prevalent, especially in north-temperate regions where lakes are abundant and thermal shielding is most likely to contribute to enhanced stratification, even in very shallow systems (Houser 2006, Messager et al. 2016, Lehner et al. 2025).

Where is anoximixis most likely to occur?

Prediction: Anoximixis is most likely to occur in shallow waterbodies but could be observed in deeper systems with increasingly large storm events

Anoximixis is most likely to affect globally abundant small and shallow lakes and ponds due to physical features that make them more likely to experience intermittent stratification and mixing, including smaller fetch, higher geometric ratio (surface area: depth), and increased potential for high biologic oxygen demand at the sediment-water interface (Downing 2010, Golosov et al. 2012, Kirillin et al. 2012, Holgerson et al. 2022). Specifically, small waterbodies are often wind-sheltered, which allows warming to strengthen stratification (Read et al. 2014, Winslow et al. 2015). Yet, shallow lakes and ponds have a low water volume making stratification more easily disrupted by declines in surface water temperature or storm events (Andersen et al. 2017, Woolway et al. 2019, Holgerson et al. 2022). Shallow lakes also have relatively lower hypolimnetic volumes and, if biological oxygen demand is sufficiently high, will quickly develop anoxia and could lead to subsequent anoximictic events (figure 2). Increased primary productivity can also increase thermal shielding that promotes hypolimnetic cooling and strengthens stratification, especially in shallow lakes where water clarity has a large influence on stratification (Houser 2006, Bartosiewicz et al. 2019). Increases in the frequency and intensity of heat waves can also promote short-term thermal stratification and primary productivity (Bartosiewicz et al. 2016, Szydlowski et al. 2025, Tran-Khac et al. 2025), further increasing the likelihood of anoximixis.

We predict anoximixis is likely to occur in waterbodies as a function of depth and area (shaded area) if there are stable conditions between mixing events for anoxia to develop (small and shallow waterbodies), if a big enough event occurs to completely mix the waterbody (large and deep waterbodies), or if shielding from lake greening or browning promotes the formation of a weak thermocline (large and shallow waterbodies). Below the shaded area, waterbodies are likely frequently mixing without enough time for anoxia to develop in the hypolimnion. Most examples of anoximixis come from shallow lakes and ponds with a maximum depth (zmax) less than 10 m (Andersen et al. 2017, Cortés et al. 2021, Søndergaard et al. 2023a, Kirol et al. 2024), with depth thresholds tending to increase with surface area (figure 2; Kirillin and Shatwell 2016). For example, polymictic lakes with sufficiently stable stratification periods and high respiration rates at the sediment-water interface can form hypolimnetic anoxia. Subsequently, anoximixis can pulse nutrients to surface waters and stimulate primary productivity. Because of their large surface area relative to their depth, shallow lakes are particularly susceptible to low-energy mixing events, which can resuspend nutrients and promote cyanobacterial blooms (Søndergaard et al. 2023a, Robbins et al. 2024). Additionally, Holgerson and colleagues (2022) found that many temperate ponds (zmax < 4 m, area < 0.04 km2) stratify intermittently throughout the growing season and, although anoximixis was not measured directly, could help explain why ponds are estimated to contribute more than 40% of methane emissions from lentic systems, globally (Holgerson and Raymond 2016, Rosentreter et al. 2021).

Anoximixis can also occur in larger, deeper lakes, especially due to large storms (e.g., tropical cyclones, Klug et al. 2012, figure 2). For example, anoximixis has been documented in the isolated, shallow bays of Lake Champlain (USA—Canada border) and Clear Lake (CA, USA; Cortés et al. 2021, Kirol et al. 2024). The susceptibility of larger and deeper systems to anoximixis likely relates to productivity, with meso- to eutrophic conditions capable of driving benthic anoxia due to high sediment biological oxygen demand (Kirillin and Shatwell 2016, Wagner et al. 2023, LaBrie and Maranger 2024). Although meso- to eutrophic systems may be most likely to experience anoximixis, low-nutrient lakes that respond rapidly to changes in temperature and increased nutrient availability may also be susceptible (Farrell et al. 2020, Reinl et al. 2021). For example, declines in hypolimnetic oxygen concentrations and changes in thermal stratification have recently been observed in multiple, remote, oligotrophic lakes without direct anthropogenic pressures or significant watershed nutrient inputs (Favot et al. 2019, Favot et al. 2023, Sethna et al. 2025, Szydlowski et al. 2025, Tran-Khac et al. 2025). Under our hypothesis, these changes suggest anoximixis has the potential to strongly influence systems that lack eutrophication-related water quality challenges associated with land use change.

Impacts to internal nutrient loading

Prediction: Anoximixis will increase the internal loading of benthic nutrients to surface waters during the growing season

Oxygen availability at the sediment-water interface is a major control on aquatic biogeochemical processes (Text S1), including nutrient cycling. The sensitivity of sediment-bound and pore water nutrients to redox state means that internal loading rates will be significantly affected by anoximixis (Molot et al. 2021). The sediment release of phosphorus (P) can increase by an order of magnitude under anoxic conditions relative to oxic conditions (Prairie et al. 2001, Anderson et al. 2021). For example, sediment P release was 44 times greater under anoxic compared to oxic conditions in Lake of the Woods, a relatively shallow (zmean = 8 m), northern boreal lake that does not have significant external P inputs (James et al. 2015, James 2017b). The internal loading of sediment-derived P contributed up to 36% of the Lake of the Woods P budget, the majority of which was sourced from deposited sediments rather than particulate P associated with particle resuspension during mixing events (James 2017a). Because of these elevated diffusive fluxes of P from the sediments to the overlying water, hypolimnetic nutrient concentrations can exceed epilimnetic concentrations by two orders of magnitude under anoxic conditions. Following anoximixis, whole water column P concentrations can increase significantly, even if only a small portion (<10%) of the lake experienced bottom water anoxia. For example, a shallow lake (Cedar Lake, WI, USA) had an accumulation of >800 µg L−1 dissolved P in the hypolimnion, which doubled epilimnetic TP concentrations from 100 to ∼200 µg L−1 following mixing (James et al. 2015). These internal fluxes of P may account for over 15% of the annual P inputs and can be more important than physical resuspension of sediment-bound P (James 2017a). We anticipate that even short periods of anoxia (weeks) followed by anoximixis would further increase internal P loading.

Similarly, anoximixis may critically alter the nitrogen (N) cycle by switching between anoxic and oxic conditions, especially at the sediment-water interface. Under anoxic conditions before anoximixis occurs, N will exist primarily as ammonium due to organic matter mineralization and limited nitrification (the conversion of ammonium to nitrate; Seitzinger 1988, Roland et al. 2018). As a result, hypolimnetic ammonium concentrations can increase at rates four times greater than under oxic conditions (Carey et al. 2022). When anoximixis occurs, dissolved inorganic N (DIN), primarily as ammonium, can load to overlying waters influencing phytoplankton community structure (see below), while introducing oxygen to bottom waters and surface sediments, and promote the nitrification-denitrification couple (Seitzinger 1988, Gilbert et al. 2016, Roland et al. 2018, Zhang, 2022). Therefore, we anticipate that anoximixis will increase DIN loading to the water column as well as enhance benthic denitrification as bottom waters are reoxygenated and restratify. It remains to be tested whether anoximixis will enhance or reduce the ability of lentic ecosystems to remove N, thus changing downstream export.

Impacts to phytoplankton

Prediction: Anoximixis will increase phytoplankton biomass and biodiversity, but changes to community composition and timing will depend on antecedent biological communities and physicochemical parameters

Mixing alters the physical (light, temperature) and geochemical (nutrient resource availability and solubility) environment, effectively acting as a disturbance that can shape phytoplankton and community composition in predictable ways (box 1; Padisák 1993, Reynolds 1994, Stockwell et al. 2020). We hypothesize that anoximixis will generally increase phytoplankton biomass over seasonal to multi-seasonal timescales by pulsing bioavailable nutrients into surface waters, particularly in lakes where productivity is nutrient-limited. These effects will differ depending on lake antecedent conditions (trophic state, community composition) and the timing, frequency, and magnitude of the anoximictic event. For example, anoximixis may sustain or increase phytoplankton biomass in a eutrophic lake where sediment nutrient pulses alleviate nutrient limitation during periods of high productivity or rapid growth (Nixdorf 1994, Wilhelm and Adrian 2008). Over time, increased productivity may contribute to a positive feedback loop of organic matter production and decomposition increasing biological oxygen demand, amplifying nutrient pulses in future anoximictic events. In an oligotrophic lake, however, where the phytoplankton community is functionally adapted to nutrient scarcity and sediment nutrient loads are expected to be smaller, anoximixis may initially decrease biomass by reducing functional groups that are sensitive to mixing (days to weeks, Reynolds 1994). Over multiple growing seasons, repeated anoximictic events may contribute to eutrophication and long-term increases in phytoplankton biomass.

Anoximixis facilitates internal P loading and cyanobacterial blooms

Anoximixis-driven nutrient loading is a significant contributor to blooms, even in systems with minimal watershed nutrient inputs. For instance, contemporary monitoring in remote lakes of northern Minnesota demonstrated that anoximictic events coincided with increases in epilimnetic phosphorus concentrations and cyanobacterial blooms (Sethna et al. 2025). In these lakes, differences in the magnitude and timing of mixing events indicate that once an anoximictic event occurs, the lake may not fully re-establish strong density gradients, increasing vulnerability to subsequent mixing. For example, the first mixing event observed in Burnt Lake followed a period of relatively strong and sustained stratification, whereas stratification recovered only briefly before the second event, resulting in a shorter interval of stability prior to renewed mixing. Hypolimnetic anoxia facilitates phosphorus release from sediments, and repeated mixing events transport this internally loaded phosphorus to surface waters, sustaining cyanobacterial blooms. These observations underscore the role of anoximixis as a mechanism for amplifying internal P loading and bloom intensity independent of external nutrient inputs as well as driving subsequent anoximictic events.

Burnt Lake is a meso-eutrophic remote lake within the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness in northern Minnesota. The lake experienced two cycles of anoximixis in 2023, as indicated by crashes in water column stability (blue line; in J m−2) when dissolved oxygen (DO) concentrations were anoxic (DO = 0 mg L−1) in late July and late August. These anoximictic events, denoted by vertical, gray bars, increased water column total phosphorus (TP) concentrations (orange bars; in µg L−1) which likely explains the increase cyanobacteria (measured as the pigment phycocyanin in µg L−1; green bars).

We also expect that different aspects of biodiversity would respond differently to anoximixis. In the 5–15 days following a disturbance, we expect short-term increases in taxonomic and functional richness because mixing would redistribute nutrients and reduce competitive exclusion (Hambright and Zohary 2000, Reynolds 2006). If anoximixis promotes cyanobacterial, bloom-forming taxa, community evenness may decline as one or a few species dominate. Therefore, frequent anoximixis could initially sustain or enhance richness but ultimately reduce evenness if cyanobacterial blooms persist. The net effect on phytoplankton diversity will depend on how richness and evenness interact under repeated anoximictic events (Morales et al. in review).

Anoximixis-driven functional and compositional changes in phytoplankton community structure will also likely vary with lake characteristics. For example, if anoximixis decreases surface temperatures in a small or medium eutrophic lake that is eutrophic but not turbid, large centric or chain-forming diatoms should dominate (Padisák et al. 2009). Alternatively, if anoximixis resuspends sediment and increases turbidity, low-light tolerant filamentous cyanobacteria, such as Planktothrix spp. or Raphidiopsis spp. are expected to dominate (Reynolds et al. 2002). Nutrient pulses generated by anoximixis may also affect phytoplankton assemblages by favoring phytoplankton that respond rapidly to pulsed resources (i.e., R-strategists; Reynolds 1994). The frequency and duration of stratification influence the rates of nutrient upwelling, where prolonged periods of stratification followed by mixing typically introduce a larger nutrient pulse compared to shorter periods of stratification (Nixdorf 1994, Wilhelm and Adrian 2008).

When anoximixis is followed by restratification, the pulsed nutrient delivery combined with stable, warm surface waters may favor taxa capable of rapidly exploiting transient resource availability (figure 1). Cyanobacteria may be particularly advantaged under these conditions due to the high surface to volume ratio of cyanobacterial cells, luxury nutrient uptake and storage, tolerance of low-light and fluctuating redox environments, high affinity for ammonium, and, in some taxa, N-fixation (Glibert et al. 2016, Lürling et al. 2018, Reinl et al. 2021). Therefore, the internal loading of bioavailable N, especially as ammonium, and P often contributes to the onset and persistence of nuisance and toxic blooms (Nürnberg 1988, Carey et al. 2012, Swann et al. 2024). Internally loaded ammonium benefits non-N2 fixing, toxin producing cyanobacteria such as Microcystis spp., which can outcompete other phytoplankton given their higher affinity for ammonium and resistance to ammonia-toxicity (Flores and Herrero 2005, Donald et al. 2013, Gardner et al. 2017, Hampel et al. 2019).

Anoximixis can also alter the relative release of N and P from sediments, leading to variability in N:P ratios across lakes. These changing ratios influence which cyanobacterial species dominate and the toxicity of blooms (Wagner et al. 2019, Warner et al. 2025). When P is released in excess of N, N-fixing filamentous cyanobacteria are favored (Søndergaard et al. 2003, North et al. 2014, Paerl et al. 2016). In contrast, high ammonium concentrations and elevated N:P ratios promote non–N-fixing, toxin-producing taxa. Toxin production and composition also vary with nutrient stoichiometry, in which higher N:P ratios generally increase microcystin and cylindrospermopsin production and favor more N-rich cyanotoxins (Wagner et al. 2019, Chaffin et al. 2023, Warner et al. 2025). Because these metabolites differ in toxicity, variability in N:P supply driven by anoximixis could alter both cyanobacterial community composition and the potential health risks of blooms (Monchamp et al. 2014).

Impacts to consumers

Prediction: Increased frequency of anoximixis will alter trophic transfer to food webs, with efficiency depending on phytoplankton quality

Consumer response to anoximixis remains largely unexplored, yet we contend that anoximixis could affect consumers through changes to food webs and physiological constraints (figure 3). First, we expect that anoximixis could either positively or negatively affect food webs and trophic transfer depending on phytoplankton communities. For example, if anoximixis increases the production of high quality and nutritious phytoplankton, bottom-up effects may support greater secondary and tertiary production (Dickman et al. 2008). In contrast, if anoximixis promotes low-quality cyanobacteria and a shift from nutritious taxa, energy flow into the food web may be truncated (Filstrup et al. 2014, Heathcote et al. 2016). For instance, anoximixis may favor the proliferation of filamentous cyanobacteria, which can be challenging for zooplankton to consume (Bednarska et al. 2014) and often lack essential fatty acids that could further limit zooplankton growth (figure 3b; Martin-Creuzburg et al. 2008). These changes in primary producers and consumers will likely affect fish community structure. Greater production of non-cyanobacteria phytoplankton can favor fast-growing, smaller planktivorous fish species due to readily available food sources over time (Meerhoff et al. 2012), whereas increased production of cyanobacteria can lead to a trophic dead end, limiting consumer abundance and diversity, especially for piscivores (Moustaka-Gouni and Sommer 2020).

(a) Under well-oxygenated conditions, diverse phytoplankton communities support efficient trophic transfer to zooplankton and fish, sustaining productive aquatic food webs. (b) Anoximixis can alter phytoplankton community composition and water column oxygen dynamics, potentially favoring filamentous or low-nutritional-quality cyanobacteria that are difficult for zooplankton to consume and lack essential fatty acids. Reduced food quality and short-term hypoxia may suppress zooplankton filtration, growth, and productivity, weakening energy transfer to higher trophic levels. (c) Repeated anoximixis events may further degrade habitat quality by reducing whole-water-column oxygen concentrations and promoting cyanobacterial blooms. These conditions can lead to physiological stress or mortality in sensitive taxa such as fish and zooplankton, while favoring tolerant or opportunistic species, including invasive taxa such as zebra mussels. Anoximixis may also affect consumers due to physiological constraints relating to changes in water column temperatures and oxygen concentrations (North et al. 2014). If anoximixis decreases whole water column oxygen even for a short period (figure 1), organisms like Daphnia spp. may face reduced growth despite their ability to tolerate some hypoxia (Karpowicz et al. 2020). Even short-term exposure to hypoxia can suppress filtration rates, reduce assimilation efficiency, and increase metabolic stress in zooplankton, leading to declines in growth and productivity (figure 3b; Ekau et al. 2010, Doubek et al. 2019). When anoximixis occurs during the growing season, reductions in oxygen concentrations could disproportionately constrain secondary production, even if epilimnetic oxygen concentrations recover rapidly through atmospheric exchange (figure 3c).

Low oxygen conditions may also support non-native species, as many are tolerant to a range of oxygen concentrations, and may have a competitive advantage in anoximictic systems. For example, zebra mussels (Dreissena polymorpha) evolved rapidly to thrive under a range of oxygen conditions and can tolerate very low oxygen concentrations due to their ability to filter large volumes of water to extract oxygen (figure 3c; De Ventura et al. 2016). Fish communities may also respond strongly to these changing oxygen dynamics; tolerant species such as common carp may persist or expand under low-oxygen conditions, whereas coldwater fishes that require both cool temperatures and high oxygen concentrations may experience habitat compression (Jacobson, Stefan and Pereira 2010, Hansen et al. 2022, Byers et al. 2023, Jane et al. 2024). However, the relative importance of oxygen limitation versus rising water temperatures in shaping fish responses remains poorly resolved, highlighting the need for further research on how anoximixis alters habitat quality for aquatic consumers. As the impacts of anoximixis on aquatic consumers are largely unexplored, this is an important avenue for future work.

Impacts to heavy metals and contaminants

Prediction: Anoximixis will increase mobility and concentration of heavy metals and other anthropogenic contaminants in the water column and incorporation into the pelagic food web

Anoximixis-driven changes in redox state (Text S1) increase the likelihood that contaminants including heavy metals, pesticides and herbicides, or perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are released from sediments and enter the water column, where they cause concerns for water quality and ecosystem health. Bioreactive and toxic heavy metals as well as metalloids, pesticides, and PFAS can all accumulate in sediments, and are often associated with humic organic matter or iron and manganese oxyhydroxides (Maranger and Pullin 2003, Liu et al. 2011, Simpson et al. 2021, Linnik et al. 2023). Even brief periods of anoxia can lead to metals and other contaminants dissociating from these chemical complexes as microbes use various metal oxyhydroxides and sulfate as electron acceptors (Banks et al. 2012). For instance, anoxia promotes arsenic mobilization (Ye et al. 2020, Fung et al. 2022) along with the production of toxic methylmercury (Matthews et al. 2013). Additionally, PFAS had lower absorption potential to sediments under anoxic conditions (Reif et al. 2022).

When anoximixis occurs, contaminants can be mobilized from the bottom waters and sediment-water interface to the surface, where they can be transported into food webs and create public health concerns (Bentzen et al. 1996, Saber et al. 2020, Fung et al. 2022). Contaminants, including mercury, arsenic, and silver, can all be assimilated by phytoplankton and bioaccumulated in higher trophic levels in the food web (Matthews et al. 2013, Barrett et al. 2019, Fung et al. 2022). The nutrient status of phytoplankton can regulate bioaccumulation of contaminants into the base of the food web (Conine et al. 2017, Zheng et al. 2022). For example, P-limited phytoplankton can have higher mercury loads, which are transferred to Daphnia spp. (Karimi et al. 2007). However, if nutrient pulses prevent P-limitation, the mercury loads in phytoplankton and Daphnia spp. can remain low caused by growth dilution (Karimi et al. 2007, Peace et al. 2021). The effect of anoximixis on contaminant transfer is an important knowledge gap with public health implications.

Impacts to aquatic carbon cycling

Prediction: Increased frequency of anoximixis will increase greenhouse gas fluxes to the atmosphere while reducing carbon burial rates

Shallow lakes and ponds that are susceptible to anoximixis also play an outsized role in C cycling, contributing significantly to both global GHG emissions (Holgerson and Raymond 2016, Rosentreter et al. 2021) and C sequestration (Downing et al. 2008). Mixing may play an important role in explaining why shallow systems are biogeochemical hotspots for C cycling (Ray and Holgerson 2023, Søndergaard et al. 2023b, Davidson et al. 2024, Rabaey and Cotner 2024). Specifically, anoxia that develops in stratified bottom waters promotes the buildup of carbon dioxide (CO2) and methane (CH4), while increasing C burial in the sediments by reducing decomposition (Bartosiewicz et al. 2019). Intermittent water column mixing disrupts these dynamics, potentially increasing GHG emissions to the atmosphere and altering redox conditions that affect C burial.

First, anoximixis may affect aquatic GHG fluxes by venting GHGs stored in bottom waters to the atmosphere. During periods of stratification, CO2 and CH4 are produced and stored in bottom waters, and diffusion to the atmosphere is limited by stratification (Bastiken et al. 2004) . Prolonged stratification provides more time for CO2 to be recycled into the food web (Rabaey et al. 2024) and for CH4 to be consumed by methane oxidizing bacteria before the GHGs reach the water’s surface (Thottathil et al. 2019, van Grinsven et al. 2021). Anoximixis, however, increases the chance that GHGs will vent to the atmosphere and potentially be hot moments for GHG flux (Søndergaard et al. 2023b, Davidson et al. 2024, Rabaey and Cotner 2024). Anoximixis often occurs rapidly, which further reduces the opportunity for CH4 oxidation and increases emissions (Thottathil et al. 2019, Vachon et al. 2019). We therefore expect that anoximixis will enhance GHG emissions from many lakes and ponds; however, the magnitude and temporal variability of these effluxes need further study. For instance, anoximixis may have variable effects on hypolimnetic oxygen concentrations following mixing (figure 1), which we expect will regulate GHG production and storage. Linking mixing events to GHG emissions has been limited by sampling methodology, but the advance of affordable automated chambers (Bastviken et al. 2020, Sø et al. 2024) provides potential to test these hypotheses.

Second, anoximixis may affect C burial in sediments by altering oxygen concentrations at the sediment-water interface and in pore water. Oxygen exposure time directly controls organic C preservation in sediments (Hartnett et al. 1998, Sobek et al. 2009). During stratification, colder temperatures and anoxia slow decomposition and increase C burial (Sobek et al. 2009, Bartosiewicz et al. 2019). As such, anoximixis could potentially alter C burial in contrasting ways depending on whether systems were previously well-mixed or dimictic. Well-mixed systems that begin to exhibit short stratification periods (i.e., weeks) may see limited effects on burial assuming routine mixing returns. Yet, dimictic systems predisposed to stratification and bottom water anoxia could experience lower C burial due to anoximixis because increased oxygen and bottom water temperatures could increase respiration and CO2 production. The strength of effects will likely relate to the magnitude and duration of temperature and oxygen changes; for example, a typically stratified lake may have cold bottom waters that warm during anoximixis and will remain elevated for the remainder of the summer season (Klug et al. 2012). Sediment anoxia, in contrast, may not be affected by anoximixis or may quickly reset, limiting effects to organic C burial. Anoximixis may also contribute to sediment resuspension, further reducing C burial and promoting internal nutrient recycling or CO2 production and evasion in the water column, although the relative contribution of sediment resuspension to lake C budgets remains largely unquantified (Urban et al. 2004, Hanson et al. 2015, Hall et al. 2019). The degree to which anoximixis will affect C burial across different systems remains an open question.

Management strategies to limit anoximixis

As we have shown through the above set of predictions, we expect anoximixis to exacerbate issues already concerning lake managers, including internal loading of nutrients and contaminants, harmful phytoplankton blooms, and enhanced C emissions. If climate change increases the frequency of anoximixis, managers may see water quality problems at unexpected times or in unexpected places (e.g., late season cyanobacterial blooms in remote lakes). The tools to combat internal nutrient loading may also mitigate negative effects of anoximixis, including aeration, alum treatments, or dredging. The efficacy of these treatments, briefly reviewed here, ranges widely across case studies, and their effectiveness in relation to the potential increasing frequency of lake anoximixis remains a knowledge gap.

Aeration, alum treatments, and dredging generally aim to alter the redox potential of bottom sediments and increase their buffering capacity to limit the release of internal nutrients that lead to damaging phytoplankton blooms. Aeration promotes the iron-P trap and denitrification, minimizing available P and N through instrumented water column oxygenation (Cooke 2005, Bormans et al. 2016, Holmroos et al. 2016). Aluminum salts (alum) and lanthanum-modified bentonite (Phoslock) additions reduce in-lake P concentrations and sediment P release in both dimictic and polymictic lakes (Huser et al. 2016, Nürnberg and LaZerte 2016, Brattebo et al. 2017), whereas dredging actively removes sediment to reset a lake’s nutrient budget (Riza et al. 2023). These methods reduce water column nutrient availability with varying degrees of success.

Aeration can be successful (Moore et al. 2012, Dent et al. 2014, Moore et al. 2015, Munger et al. 2016) but has also been shown to have no effect on water quality in highly agricultural landscapes (Lerminiaux et al. 2024) and to exacerbate eutrophication because of unintended anoximixis (box 2, Lake Carmi; Kirol et al. 2024). Both alum and Phoslock may effectively reduce P pulses that result from anoximixis, especially in systems where external loading is controlled. However, the longevity of alum treatments widely varies with P control ranging from only months post-application to over 20 years (Welch and Cooke 1999, 2005) suggesting that a better understanding of sediment buffering capacity is required (Burgin and Loecke 2023). Indeed, anoximixis may reduce the capacity of these treatments to last by reducing their potential redox buffering with fluctuating oxygen conditions. Dredging is both costly and is often not effective for long term reductions in nutrient availability (Jing et al. 2019). Although the total potential internal load is minimized through the dredging of legacy nutrients and contaminants, anoximixis complicates the long-term recovery of these systems as some level of internal load will remain. This will be exacerbated if external nutrient sources persist allowing for the continued accumulation of redox-sensitive iron-bound P at the sediment-water interface (Kleeberg and Kohl 1999).

Aeration-induced anoximixis increases nutrient loading and cyanobacteria biomass

Whole-lake aeration is a management strategy aimed at reducing internal P loading in eutrophic lakes by oxygenating bottom waters, thus altering the redox potential at the sediment-water interface (Jilbert et al. 2020). Depending on the strength of the aeration system, this remediation strategy may also mix or destabilize the lake water column, making it more sensitive to wind mixing. An aeration system was deployed in Lake Carmi, a historically eutrophic, shallow lake (zmax = 10 m) in northern Vermont, USA, during the ice-free seasons of 2019 through 2024 to reduce internal sediment P loads that contribute to persistent cyanobacteria blooms. The aerator bubbled oxygen along the deep trench of the lake and fully mixed the water column, shifting the lake from a dimictic to polymictic regime. The generators operating the system, however, frequently malfunctioned during warm summer months, which allowed the lake to re-stratify and increased the volume of hypolimnetic anoxia (Kirol et al. 2024). As a result, anoximixis became prevalent and drove multiple nutrient pulses in the lake that aligned with wind- or aeration-driven anoximixis (Kirol et al. 2024).

Prior to aeration, growth of Lake Carmi phytoplankton was generally N-limited or co-limited by N and P (Volponi et al. 2023) and was dominated by N-fixing filamentous cyanobacteria. Over the first three years of aeration (2019–2021), cyanobacteria biomass increased, and the lake experienced a compositional shift in the fall to buoyant coccoid taxa (Microcystis spp.), which displaced the fall diatom bloom and contributed to the overall increase in biomass (Morales et al. unpublished data). This case study is a cautionary tale for lake management, demonstrating that shallow eutrophic and hypereutrophic lakes may be particularly sensitive to anoximixis, which can amplify nutrient loading and cyanobacteria blooms.

Ultimately, if climate change increases the frequency of anoximixis, managing the harmful effects will be important and understanding the interaction of this phenomenon with currently available management techniques will be key. We recommend that managers increase monitoring efforts to track thermal stratification with high-frequency temperature and dissolved oxygen sensors across the water column. This should be combined with a better understanding of the buffering capacity of sediments and how this may vary geographically. Dendritic reservoirs and large lakes with shallow bays may need to be monitored in multiple locations to catch anoximictic events. Without high-frequency measurements, managers may miss anoximixis and attribute nutrient loading to other sources, leading to restoration plans that do not fully treat the problem and are unlikely to provide long-term benefits.

Directions for future research

Anoximixis differs fundamentally from seasonal turnover in both its timing and biogeochemical consequences. Unlike seasonal mixing of dimictic and monomictic lakes, anoximixis occurs intermittently and often outside spring or fall turnover, facilitating oscillating redox conditions. These fluctuating conditions are not captured by traditional models of stratification and mixing regimes (e.g., Hutchinson and Löffler 1956, Lewis Jr., 1983) and can therefore generate novel ecosystem responses. Periods of anoxia are well known to govern nutrient, contaminant, and C dynamics in lakes; anoximixis allows for these often negative effects to cascade through the water column to potentially influence phytoplankton, food webs, contaminant availability, and GHG release.

The interactive effects of climate change and eutrophication will continue to alter the physical structure and stratification regimes of shallow lakes and ponds, increasing the potential for anoximixis. As our synthesis highlights, we predict that the increasing frequency and intensity of anoximixis can initiate cascading effects on redox-sensitive biogeochemical processes as well as energy and contaminant transfer through lake food webs. Although considerable progress has been made in understanding the ecological consequences of persistent anoxia, the dynamic nature of anoximixis introduces novel challenges that remain poorly constrained. Oscillating redox conditions may fundamentally alter nutrient cycling, phytoplankton blooms, food web dynamics, and GHG fluxes in ways that are not captured in our study of consistently anoxic or oxic conditions or seasonal turnover (Gilbert et al. 2016, Mermillod-Blondin et al. 2024). Our synthesis underscores the need to better understand when and where anoximixis occurs as well as to investigate the effects of repeated anoxic-oxic mixing cycles on lake biogeochemistry and biological responses including elemental stoichiometry, community composition, trophic structure, and elemental transfer. For example, future work could include how different lake types, including those of different trophic status and geomorphic shapes, respond to anoximixis both on the short term as well as on an interannual basis.

We recommend that future research prioritize high-resolution, year-round monitoring of lake thermal structure, oxygen dynamics, and biogeochemical responses, particularly in systems where anoximixis may occur. This could be combined with a better understanding of the redox potential and buffering capacity of their bottom sediments, which may act to modulate responses and vary geographically. Such datasets will allow us to improve thermal modeling to leverage surface heat flux and physical properties (e.g., Cortés et al. 2021) to predict anoximixis, which is currently lacking for shallow, polymictic systems as well as better understand the relative importance of internal versus external processes on the mass balance of nutrients, contaminants, and GHGs. Additionally, future research can test the predictions we put forward relating to the ecosystem consequences of anoximixis. Ultimately, understanding how lakes respond to the dual pressures of warming and nutrient enrichment is essential for forecasting ecological trajectories and informing management decisions. By refining our tools and expanding our focus to include the dynamics of anoximixis, we can better anticipate and mitigate the cascading impacts of climate change on inland waters.
