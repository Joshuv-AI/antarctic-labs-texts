A New Approach to Compute Linear Landau Damping
M. Pelkner, K. Hallatschek, M. Raeth

Abstract
We present a semi-analytical method for calculating exact time-domain solutions to linear Landau-damping problems. Unlike traditional residue-based approaches, our framework avoids the need to compute complex zeros of the dielectric function. We illustrate the method for an electrostatic, unmagnetized plasma with kinetic ions and adiabatic electrons by deriving the response of both the ion density and the ion distribution function to an initial density perturbation. We further show that the construction can be formally extended to linear Vlasov-Maxwell problems with a uniform background magnetic field and a Maxwellian equilibrium distribution.

Introduction

To ensure the accuracy of simulations in analytically intractable regimes, numerical predictions must be compared against available exact solutions whenever possible. However, exact solutions of the Vlasov equation are notoriously difficult to derive, and benchmarks are therefore often restricted to indirect diagnostics or simplified limits. A classical example is the Landau damping test, which measures the decay rate of electron Langmuir oscillations in the presence of a static ion background (Fig.
1
)
[
8
,
2
]
. Such residue-based analyses are most effective in the late-time regime, where the solution is governed by a small number of dominant poles of the dielectric function. Resolving the transient response near the initial time, however, requires an increasingly large number of poles. Determining these poles numerically can be challenging and, depending on the background distribution function, may even be impossible if the dielectric function cannot be analytically continued sufficiently far into the lower complex half-plane. In this work, we present a semi-analytical method that circumvents pole summation entirely.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. First, we rederive the exact solution of the linearized Vlasov equation in Fourier space under the assumptions of quasi-neutrality, an unmagnetized plasma, and adiabatic electrons. These assumptions are motivated by the current configuration of the BSL6D code
[
8
,
11
]
and serve to simplify the initial presentation. We then introduce the semi-analytical method by calculating the ion density response to an initial ion density perturbation. Building on this result, we determine the response of the linearized ion distribution function, which requires treating the resonant singularity on the real axis associated with the continuous spectrum of Case-van Kampen modes. Finally, we provide a formal argument showing that the extension to magnetized configurations follows the same logic, provided that the background magnetic field is uniform and the equilibrium distribution is Maxwellian. The explicit construction in this setting involves significant additional algebraic complexity and is therefore left for future work.

2
Analytic distribution-function response

In the following, we assume that collisions can be neglected, magnetic fields are absent, and the distribution function
f(x,v,t)
of a particle species can be separated into an equilibrium part
f_{0}(v)
and a small perturbation
f_{1}(x,v,t)
,

f(x,v,t)=f_{0}(v)+f_{1}(x,v,t).

(1)

Our goal is to calculate the time evolution of
f_{1}
from an arbitrary initial condition
f_{1}(x,v,t=0)
, such as the pure density perturbation considered in the next section. The stationary background is assumed to be of the form

f_{0}(v)=n_{0}f_{\text{M}}(v),

(2)

where
n_{0}
is the constant equilibrium density and
f_{\text{M}}(v)
is the Maxwellian distribution,

f_{\text{M}}(v)\coloneq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}v_{\text{th}}}e^{-\frac{v^{2}}{2v_{\text{th}}^{2}}},\hskip 20.00003ptv_{\text{th}}\coloneq\sqrt{\frac{T}{m}}.

(3)

Here,
T
denotes the temperature. Since we restrict our analysis to small perturbations
f_{1}
, nonlinear terms can be neglected, and the perturbation is governed by the linearized Vlasov equation
[
4
]
,

\partial_{t}f_{1}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla f_{1}-\frac{q}{m}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla_{\bm{v}}f_{0}=0.

(4)

Because the homogeneous, unmagnetized background distribution (
2
) is translationally invariant and Eq. (
4
) is linear, spatial Fourier modes evolve independently. For each wave vector, we may rotate the coordinate system such that the
x
-axis is aligned with the wave vector. Denoting the corresponding coordinate and velocity component by
x
and
v
, respectively, we can therefore restrict the following discussion to the representative one-dimensional problem

\partial_{t}f_{1}+v\partial_{x}f_{1}-\frac{q}{m}\partial_{x}\phi\partial_{v}f_{0}=0.

(5)

The electrostatic potential
\phi
is determined by imposing quasineutrality,
n_{\text{e}}=n_{\text{i}}
, together with an adiabatic electron response
[
7
]
,

\phi(x,t)\coloneq\frac{T}{qn_{0}}\int dv\,f_{1}(x,v,t)=\frac{T}{qn_{0}}n_{1}(x,t).

(6)

Thus,
\phi
is first order in the perturbation amplitude. To construct a solution of the linearized Vlasov equation (
5
) that satisfies a given initial condition,
f_{1}(x,v,t=0)
, we follow Case
[
1
]
and introduce

f_{1}^{+}(x,v,t)=\theta(t)f_{1}(x,v,t),

(7)

where the Heaviside step function is defined as

\theta(t)\coloneq\begin{cases}0&t<0\\
1&t\geq 0\end{cases}.

(8)

To transform
f_{1}^{+}
to the Fourier domain, we use the Fourier transform convention

\hat{g}(k,\omega)\coloneq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dxdtg(x,t)e^{-i(kx-\omega t)},

(9)

where
\hat{g}
denotes the Fourier transform of
g
. Inserting
f_{1}^{+}
into Eq. (
5
), using the convention (
9
), and assuming
\operatorname{Im}(\omega)>0
to ensure convergence of the Fourier integral of
f_{1}^{+}
, we obtain

\displaystyle-i\omega\hat{f}_{1}^{+}(k,v,\omega)+

\displaystyle ikv\hat{f}_{1}^{+}(k,v,\omega)-\frac{q}{m}ik\hat{\phi}^{+}(k,\omega)\partial_{v}f_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\hat{f}_{1}(k,v,t=0).

(10)

In a slight departure from the notation specified in Eq. (
9
),
\hat{f}_{1}(k,v,t=0)
refers specifically to the spatial Fourier transform of the initial condition. For the following calculations, it is convenient to treat
\omega
as a real variable. Since
\operatorname{Im}(\omega)>0
, we introduce a small positive parameter
\epsilon>0
and perform the substitution
\omega\rightarrow\omega+i\epsilon
,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
. Solving Eq. (
10
) for
\hat{f}_{1}^{+}
then yields

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{1,\epsilon}^{+}(k,v,\omega)\coloneq

\displaystyle\frac{kv}{\omega-kv+i\epsilon}f_{\text{M}}(v)\frac{qn_{0}}{T}\hat{\phi}_{1,\epsilon}^{+}(k,\omega)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{i}{\omega-kv+i\epsilon}\hat{f}_{1}(k,v,t=0),

(11)

where, according to Eq. (
6
),

\frac{qn_{0}}{T}\hat{\phi}_{1,\epsilon}^{+}(k,\omega)=\int dv\hat{f}_{1,\epsilon}^{+}(k,v,\omega).

(12)

3
Semi-analytical density response

We now assume a single-mode density perturbation at
t=0
, i.e.,

f_{1}(x,v,t=0)=f_{\text{M}}(v)e^{ikx},

(13)

and compute the density response for
t>0
. General initial perturbations will be discussed in the next section. For the perturbation (
13
), Eq. (
10
) admits a plane-wave solution,

\hat{f}_{1}^{+}(k,v,\omega)\coloneq\hat{f}_{1}^{+}(v,\omega)e^{ikx},

(14)

which consequently imposes a plane-wave structure on
\hat{\phi}
according to Eq. (
6
),

\hat{\phi}(k,\omega)=\frac{T}{qn_{0}}\int dv\hat{f}_{1}^{+}(v,\omega)e^{ikx}=\frac{T}{qn_{0}}\hat{n}_{1}^{+}(\omega)e^{ikx}.

(15)

To keep the notation concise, we use the same label for
\hat{f}_{1}(k,v,\omega)
and
\hat{f}_{1}(v,\omega)
and set
k=1
, since the solution for an arbitrary
k
can be recovered from the self-similarity of the distribution-function response (
11
). Integrating Eq. (
11
) with respect to velocity yields

\displaystyle\hat{n}^{+}_{1,\epsilon}(\omega)=

\displaystyle-\hat{n}^{+}_{1,\epsilon}(\omega)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dv\frac{vf_{\text{M}}(v)}{v-(\omega+i\epsilon)}

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dv\frac{f_{\text{M}}(v)}{v-(\omega+i\epsilon)}.

(16)

By expressing the velocity integrals in terms of the plasma dispersion function
Z(\zeta)

[
10
]
,

Z(\zeta)\coloneq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\frac{e^{-x^{2}}}{x-\zeta}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{for}\hskip 20.00003pt\operatorname{Im}(\zeta)>0,

(17)

and nondimensionalizing with
m=T=1
, we obtain

\hat{n}^{+}_{1,\epsilon}(\omega)=-\frac{i}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{Z(\xi)}{2+\xi Z(\xi)},\hskip 20.00003pt\text{where}\hskip 20.00003pt\xi\coloneq\frac{\omega+i\epsilon}{\sqrt{2}}.

(18)

As written, Eq. (
18
) is defined only in the upper complex half-plane owing to the definition (
17
). Consequently, evaluating the
t>0
density response by Fourier inversion,

\hat{n}_{1}^{+}(t)=-\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{Z(\omega)}{2+\omega Z(\omega)}e^{-i\sqrt{2}\omega t},

(19)

requires the use of the analytic continuation of the plasma dispersion function
[
3
]
,

Z(\zeta)\coloneq i\sqrt{\pi}e^{-\zeta^{2}}-2D(\zeta),\hskip 20.00003pt\zeta\in\mathbb{C},

(20)

where

D(\zeta)\coloneq e^{-\zeta^{2}}\int_{0}^{\zeta}dxe^{x^{2}}.

(21)

Equation (
20
) defines an entire function; Fig.
3
shows the Dawson function
D(\zeta)
along the real axis. In the numerical evaluations below, we use the implementation of
D(\zeta)
provided by Wolfram Mathematica
[
15
]
.

,
D(\zeta)

1/\zeta

|\zeta|\rightarrow\infty
.

Since
Z
is entire and the zeros of the denominator in (
19
) are confined to the lower complex half-plane (Fig.
3
), the integrand in (
19
) is free of singularities along the integration contour. However, decomposing
\hat{n}^{+}_{1}
into its real and imaginary components along the real
\omega
line,

\displaystyle\hat{n}_{1}^{+}(\omega)=

\displaystyle\frac{2e^{-\xi^{2}}}{\pi\xi^{2}e^{-2\xi^{2}}+4(1-\xi D(\xi))^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{-\pi\xi e^{-2\xi^{2}}+4D(\xi)\left(1-\xi D(\xi)\right)}{\pi\xi^{2}e^{-2\xi^{2}}+4(1-\xi D(\xi))^{2}},

(22)

shows that the real part is symmetric and exhibits Gaussian decay in
\omega
, whereas the imaginary part is antisymmetric and decays only as
\omega^{-1}
(Fig.
5
). The slow decay of the imaginary component leads to poor convergence of the Fourier integral (
19
); consequently, truncating the integration domain induces spurious Gibbs oscillations (Fig.
5
).

To eliminate this algebraic tail, we symmetrize the density response in Fourier space,

\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(\omega)\coloneq\hat{n}_{1}^{+}(\omega)+\hat{n}_{1}^{+}(-\omega).

(23)

In the time domain, this corresponds to replacing the one-sided response by its time-symmetric extension, thereby removing the jump at
t=0
. The
\omega^{-1}
tail is a direct consequence of this jump, since the density response,

n_{1}^{+}(x,t)=\int dv\,f_{1}^{+}(x,v,t),

(24)

inherits the causal step at the initial time, and the Fourier transform of a step function,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{i\omega t}\theta(t)=\operatorname{p.v.}\left(\frac{i}{\sqrt{2\pi}\omega}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\delta(\omega),

(25)

contains a principal-value contribution proportional to
\omega^{-1}

[
6
]
. Using Eq. (
22
), we obtain

\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(\omega)=\frac{2e^{-\xi^{2}}}{\pi\xi^{2}e^{-2\xi^{2}}+4(1-\xi D(\xi))^{2}},\hskip 20.00003pt\xi=\frac{\omega}{\sqrt{2}},

(26)

and define

\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(t)\coloneq\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(\omega).

(27)

Numerical Fourier inversion of the symmetrized density response
n_{1}^{\text{sym}}
, evaluated over the domain
\omega\in[0,4]
. The initial condition is accurately recovered.

Physically, the symmetrized density response
n_{1}^{\text{sym}}(t)

(Fig.
6
) corresponds to a specially prepared scenario
in which the distribution function evolves from a structured perturbation at

t=-\infty
into a pure density perturbation at
t=0
. For
t>0
, this
perturbation subsequently decays through phase mixing. It may appear that other
choices of the
t<0
continuation of
n_{1}
could also remove the spectral tail,
provided that their evolution from
t=-\infty
yields the prescribed initial
condition at
t=0
. However, this is not the case: the symmetry of the initial
condition, together with the time-reversal invariance of the underlying dynamics, determines a unique
t<0
solution. We elaborate on this point in the next section.

The two principal sources of numerical error are the truncation error due to the finite integration domain and the quadrature error introduced by discretizing the Fourier integral. To derive an error bound for the former, we neglect the quadrature error and consider the exact integral in Eq. (
27
). For the spectrum considered here, we use the empirically verified bound

\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(\omega)<e^{-\frac{\pi\omega^{2}}{16}}\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\omega.

(28)

For the truncation error
E_{a}(t)
,

E_{a}(t)\coloneq n_{1}(t)-\int_{0}^{a}d\omega\cos(\omega t)\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(\omega),

(29)

we obtain (Fig.
8
)

|E_{a}(t)|\leq\int_{a}^{\infty}d\omega e^{-\frac{\pi\omega^{2}}{16}}=2\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{a\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\right).

(30)

To estimate the quadrature error, we first note that the spectrum (
26
) is analytic in a strip of width
2c
around the real axis, where
c
is the minimum distance from the dominant Landau pole to the real axis (Fig.
8
). For our choice of parameters,
c\approx 0.85
. Since the integrand is analytic in this strip, the numerical integration can be performed efficiently using the trapezoidal rule on a uniform grid with spacing
h
. Within the regime of analyticity, the trapezoidal rule exhibits spectral convergence of the quadrature error
|E_{h}|

[
13
]
, i.e.,

|E_{h}|\leq\frac{2M}{e^{2\pi c/h}-1},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptM\coloneq\sup_{\left|s\right|<c}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(\omega+is)\right|d\omega.

(31)

Consequently, the quadrature error can be readily reduced below the level of the truncation error, leaving the latter as the dominant source of numerical uncertainty.

4
Semi-analytical distribution-function response

Any solution of the homogeneous linearized Vlasov equation (
5
) admits the decomposition

f_{1}(x,v,t)=f_{1}^{+}(x,v,t)+f_{1}^{-}(x,v,t),

(32)

where
f_{1}^{+}
is defined by Eq. (
7
) and
f_{1}^{-}
is defined by

f_{1}^{-}(x,v,t)\coloneq\theta(-t)f_{1}(x,v,t).

(33)

While
\hat{f}_{1}^{+}
solves Eq. (
10
) for
\operatorname{Im}(\omega)>0
,

(-i\omega+ikv)\hat{f}_{1}^{+}(k,v,\omega)-\frac{q}{m}ik\hat{\phi}^{+}(k,\omega)\partial_{v}f_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\hat{f}_{1}(k,v,t=0),

(34)

\hat{f}_{1}^{-}
solves

(-i\omega+ikv)\hat{f}_{1}^{-}(k,v,\omega)-\frac{q}{m}ik\hat{\phi}^{-}(k,\omega)\partial_{v}f_{0}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\hat{f}_{1}(k,v,t=0)

(35)

for
\operatorname{Im}(\omega)<0
. As can be seen from Eq. (
11
), the source terms on the right-hand side of Eqs. (
34
) and (
35
) induce a slow
\sim\omega^{-1}
decay in the individual spectra of
\hat{f}_{1}^{+}
and
\hat{f}_{1}^{-}
. In the combined spectrum
\hat{f}_{1}=\hat{f}_{1}^{+}+\hat{f}_{1}^{-}
, however, these terms cancel. Thus, provided that the imposed initial condition admits a smooth solution
f_{1}
with compact support, the combined spectrum decays faster than any inverse power of
\omega

[
12
]
. This rapid decay ensures that truncation errors in the Fourier inversion become negligible outside a finite integration domain.

For the special case of a time-symmetric initial condition, time-reversing the governing differential equation for
\hat{f}_{1}^{+}
, Eq. (
34
), and comparing the result with Eq. (
35
) yields

\hat{f}_{1}^{-}(v,\omega)=\hat{f}_{1}^{+}(-v,-\omega)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{if}\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\phi}^{+}(-\omega)=\hat{\phi}^{-}(\omega).

(36)

Thus, according to Eqs. (
6
) and (
32
),
\hat{f}_{1}
is constructed by time-symmetrizing the
\hat{f}_{1}^{+}
solution, in agreement with the results of the previous section. Analogously, for a time-antisymmetric initial condition satisfying

f_{1}(x,v,t=0)=-f_{1}(x,-v,t=0),

(37)

one finds

\hat{f}_{1}^{-}(v,\omega)=-\hat{f}_{1}^{+}(-v,-\omega)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{if}\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\phi}^{+}(-\omega)=-\hat{\phi}^{-}(\omega),

(38)

i.e.,
\hat{f}_{1}
is constructed by time-antisymmetrizing the
\hat{f}_{1}^{+}
solution. Arbitrary initial conditions may be analyzed by decomposing the initial state into symmetric and antisymmetric components and treating each contribution as outlined above. Alternatively, one may compute
\hat{f}_{1}^{+}
and
\hat{f}_{1}^{-}
independently.

Returning to the time-symmetric case, we recall Eq. (
11
),

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{1,\epsilon}^{+}(k,v,\omega)\coloneq

\displaystyle\frac{kv}{\omega-kv+i\epsilon}f_{\text{M}}(v)\hat{n}_{1,\epsilon}^{+}(\omega)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{i}{\omega-kv+i\epsilon}\hat{f}_{1}(k,v,t=0),

(39)

where
\omega,\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}
and
\epsilon>0
. Symmetrizing this expression in
\omega
yields

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{1,\epsilon}^{\text{sym}}(v,\omega)\coloneq

\displaystyle\frac{\omega-v}{(\omega-v)^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}vf_{\text{M}}(v)\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(\omega)+

\displaystyle\frac{\epsilon}{(\omega-v)^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}n_{0}f_{\text{M}}(v)-ivf_{\text{M}}(v)\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{asym}}(\omega)\right),

(40)

with
\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{asym}}(\omega)\coloneq\hat{n}_{1}^{+}(\omega)-\hat{n}_{1}^{+}(-\omega)
. Taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
and applying the Sokhotski-Plemelj theorem gives the distributional limit

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\hat{f}_{1,\epsilon}^{\text{sym}}(v,\omega)=\>

\displaystyle\operatorname{p.v.}\left[\frac{1}{\omega-v}vf_{\text{M}}(v)\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(\omega)\right]+

\displaystyle\pi\delta(\omega-v)\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}n_{0}f_{\text{M}}(v)-ivf_{\text{M}}(v)\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{asym}}(\omega)\right),

(41)

where
\operatorname{p.v.}
denotes the Cauchy principal value. Equation (
41
) identifies the Case–van Kampen eigenmodes, represented by the delta distributions, as the continuous spectrum of the linearized Vlasov operator
[
1
,
14
]
. In particular, the damped Landau modes are not part of this spectrum; rather, they emerge from a superposition of these eigenmodes. To compute the Fourier inversion of the symmetrized distribution-function response,

\displaystyle f_{1}^{\text{sym}}(v,t)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d\omega}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\hat{f}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(v,\omega)e^{-i\omega t}

\displaystyle\coloneq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int\frac{d\omega}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\hat{f}_{1,\epsilon}^{\text{sym}}(v,\omega)e^{-i\omega t},

(42)

we evaluate the delta-function contribution in Eq. (
41
) analytically and compute the remaining principal-value term,

\operatorname{p.v.}\int\frac{d\omega}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{1}{\omega-v}vf_{\text{M}}(v)\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(\omega)e^{-i\omega t},

(43)

numerically. Apart from the resonant singularity at
\omega=v
, the integrand in Eq. (
43
) is well behaved because
\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}
decays rapidly. For numerical integration along the real
\omega
-axis, we subtract the singular part by introducing the regularized integrand (Fig.
9
)

\hat{g}_{\text{reg}}(v,\omega)\coloneq\frac{1}{\omega-v}vf_{\text{M}}(v)\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(\omega)-c(v)\frac{e^{-(\omega-v)^{2}}}{\omega-v},

(44)

where

c(v)\coloneq vf_{\text{M}}(v)\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{sym}}(v).

(45)

The exponential factor in Eq. (
44
) is crucial: it removes the pole locally without introducing a spurious
\omega^{-1}
tail.

To recover the full solution, we first Fourier-invert the subtracted singular term,

\hat{p}(v,\omega)\coloneq c(v)\frac{e^{-(v-\omega)^{2}}}{\omega-v},

(46)

analytically
[
6
]
,

p(v,t)=ic(v)\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\operatorname{erf}\bigg(\frac{t}{2}\bigg)e^{-ivt},

(47)

where

\operatorname{erf}(\zeta)\coloneq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\zeta}e^{-x^{2}}\,dx,\hskip 20.00003pt\zeta\in\mathbb{C}.

(48)

The full time evolution of the distribution function is then obtained by combining the numerical integral of the regularized integrand with the analytically evaluated terms,

\displaystyle f^{\text{sym}}_{1}(v,t)=

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\omega}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\hat{g}_{\text{reg}}(v,\omega)e^{-i\omega t}+p(v,t)

\displaystyle+f_{\text{M}}(v)\bigg(1-iv\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\hat{n}_{1}^{\text{asym}}(v)\bigg)e^{-ivt}.

(49)

49
). At fixed time, all terms in Eq. (
49
) are suppressed by at least a Gaussian factor in velocity. Hence, the perturbation
f_{1}
vanishes at large velocities for any fixed time (Figs.
11
and
11
).

At fixed velocity, Eq. (
49
) is more naturally analyzed by decomposing the perturbation
f_{1}
into an adiabatic part and a non-adiabatic perturbation
h
,

f_{1}(x,v,t)=h(x,v,t)-\frac{qn_{0}}{T}\phi(x,t)f_{\text{M}}(v).

(50)

With the adiabatic-electron closure, the time evolution of the non-adiabatic perturbation
h
is governed by the inhomogeneous equation

\partial_{t}h+v\partial_{x}h=f_{0}\partial_{t}n_{1}.

(51)

A general solution of Eq. (
51
) consists of a homogeneous and a particular contribution,
h=h_{\text{hom}}+h_{\text{part}}
, where
h_{\text{hom}}
satisfies the free-streaming equation

\partial_{t}h_{\text{hom}}+v\partial_{x}h_{\text{hom}}=0.

(52)

Since
\phi\to 0
and
\partial_{t}n_{1}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, only the homogeneous contribution persists asymptotically. Thus, at fixed velocity,
f_{1}
approaches the free-streaming solution after the Landau-damped contribution has decayed (Fig.
12
).

To illustrate the asymptotic value of
h_{\text{hom}}
, we normalize Eq. (
49
) by a Maxwellian and evaluate it at a time
\tau
for which the Landau-damped terms are sufficiently suppressed and
\operatorname{erf}(\tau/2)\approx 1
. This gives the asymptotic estimate

(53)

as shown in Fig.

5
Formal generalization

We finally discuss under which conditions the outlined construction of a numerically well-behaved response can be applied to general initial conditions within the linearized Vlasov-Maxwell framework. The derivation of more general response functions beyond those presented so far is left for future work; our current objective is to show that the numerical method itself is, in principle, extensible.

To this end, the magnetic field is decomposed into a zeroth-order contribution, representing a strong external background field, and a small first-order perturbation,

\bm{B}\coloneq\bm{B}_{\text{ext}}+\bm{B}_{\text{pert}}.

(56)

We assume that
\bm{B}_{\text{ext}}
is time-independent and transforms as
\bm{B}_{\text{ext}}\rightarrow-\bm{B}_{\text{ext}}
under time reversal, consistent with
\bm{v}\rightarrow-\bm{v}
. Moreover, as before, we assume a Maxwellian equilibrium distribution,

f_{0}^{s}(\bm{v})=n_{0s}\left(\frac{m_{s}}{2\pi T_{s}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}e^{-\frac{m_{s}v^{2}}{2T_{s}}},

(57)

where
s
denotes the species index. The linearized, three-dimensional Vlasov equation then takes the form

\partial_{t}f^{s}_{1}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla f^{s}_{1}+\bm{F}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{v}}f^{s}_{0}+\left(\bm{v}\times\bm{B}_{\text{ext}}\right)\cdot\nabla_{\bm{v}}f^{s}_{1}=0,

(58)

and the perturbing force reads

\bm{F}\coloneq\frac{q_{s}}{m_{s}}\left(\bm{E}+\bm{v}\times\bm{B}_{\text{pert}}\right).

(59)

In Eq. (
58
), we have absorbed the
q_{s}/m_{s}
prefactor into the definition of the external magnetic field. The self-consistent fields are defined by

\bm{E}=-\nabla\phi-\partial_{t}\bm{A}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\bm{B}_{\text{pert}}=\nabla\times\bm{A}.

(60)

In the Lorenz gauge, the equations governing
\phi
and
\bm{A}
are
[
5
]

\left(\Delta-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\partial_{t}^{2}\right)\phi=-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_{0}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\left(\Delta-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\partial_{t}^{2}\right)\bm{A}=-\mu_{0}\bm{j},

(61)

where

\rho\coloneq\sum_{s}q_{s}\int d^{3}v\,f^{s}_{1}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\bm{j}\coloneq\sum_{s}q_{s}\int d^{3}v\,\bm{v}f^{s}_{1}.

(62)

We now set
c=1
and proceed analogously to Section
2
. For
\operatorname{Im}(\omega)>0
, the positive-time solution
\hat{f}_{1}^{s+}
is determined by

\displaystyle\left(-i\omega-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{v}+\bm{v}\times\bm{B}_{\text{ext}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{v}}\right)\hat{f}_{1}^{s+}+

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{F}}^{+}(\bm{k},\omega)\cdot\nabla_{\bm{v}}f_{0}^{s}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt=\hat{f}_{1}(\bm{k},\bm{v},t=0),

(63)

whereas for
\operatorname{Im}(\omega)<0
, the negative-time solution
\hat{f}_{1}^{s-}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left(-i\omega-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{v}+\bm{v}\times\bm{B}_{\text{ext}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{v}}\right)\hat{f}^{s-}_{1}+

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{F}}^{-}(\bm{k},\omega)\cdot\nabla_{\bm{v}}f_{0}^{s}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\hat{f}_{1}(\bm{k},\bm{v},t=0).

(64)

Here, prefactors of
(2\pi)^{-3/2}
from the spatial Fourier transform have been absorbed into the initial conditions. Equations (
63
) and (
64
) implicitly require spatial homogeneity of both the magnetic background and the equilibrium distributions, since any spatial dependence would introduce convolutions in Fourier space instead of multiplications, rendering the semi-analytical treatment analytically intractable.

Analogously to
f_{1}^{+}
and
f_{1}^{-}
, the scalar and vector potentials are decomposed as

\phi^{+}\coloneq\theta(t)\phi,\hskip 10.00002pt\bm{A}^{+}\coloneq\theta(t)\bm{A},

(65)

and

\phi^{-}\coloneq\theta(-t)\phi,\hskip 10.00002pt\bm{A}^{-}\coloneq\theta(-t)\bm{A}.

(66)

To transform the positive-time fields into the frequency domain and obtain an expression for
\hat{\bm{F}}^{+}
, we assume
\operatorname{Im}(\omega)>0
and first apply the Fourier transform (
9
) to the second time derivative of
\phi^{+}
,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{i\omega t}\partial_{t}^{2}\phi^{+}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{i\omega t}\partial_{t}^{2}\phi

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left[e^{i\omega t}\partial_{t}\phi\right]_{0}^{\infty}-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{2\pi}}(i\omega)e^{i\omega t}\partial_{t}\phi^{+}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\dot{\phi}(0)-\frac{i\omega}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left[e^{i\omega t}\phi\right]_{0}^{\infty}-\omega^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{i\omega t}\phi^{+}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(-\dot{\phi}(0)+i\omega\phi(0)\right)-\omega^{2}\hat{\phi}^{+}.

(67)

Proceeding analogously for
\bm{A}^{+}
, we obtain from Eq. (
61
)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{A}}^{+}(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}-\omega^{2}}\left[-\mu_{0}\hat{\bm{j}}^{+}_{1}(\bm{k},\omega)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\dot{\bm{A}}(0)-i\omega\bm{A}(0)\right)\right],

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}^{+}(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}-\omega^{2}}\left[\frac{\hat{\rho}^{+}_{1}(\bm{k},\omega)}{\epsilon_{0}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\dot{\phi}(0)-i\omega\phi(0)\right)\right].

(68)

Conversely, for the advanced fields we assume
\operatorname{Im}(\omega)<0
and obtain from Eq. (
66
)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{A}}^{-}(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}-\omega^{2}}\left[-\mu_{0}\hat{\bm{j}}^{-}_{1}(\bm{k},\omega)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\dot{\bm{A}}(0)-i\omega\bm{A}(0)\right)\right],

\displaystyle\hat{\phi}^{-}(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}-\omega^{2}}\left[\frac{\hat{\rho}^{-}_{1}(\bm{k},\omega)}{\epsilon_{0}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left(\dot{\phi}(0)-i\omega\phi(0)\right)\right].

(69)

Adding
f_{1}^{s+}
and
f_{1}^{s-}
now cancels the source terms in Eqs. (
63
) and (
64
). Likewise, the initial-field terms in Eqs. (
68
) and (
69
) cancel upon summation. Consequently, provided that the initial states
f_{1}^{s}(t=0)
are consistent with a smooth evolution of the coupled system, the resulting spectrum again decays faster than any inverse power of
\omega

[
12
]
. Whether a symmetrization similar to that presented in Section
2
is possible, however, depends crucially on the boundary conditions chosen for the electric and magnetic potentials.

The construction admits the following physical interpretation. Once the initial conditions for the distribution functions and the fields at
t=0
are specified, a unique distribution-function perturbation is prepared at
t=-\infty
, together with a self-consistent radiation field. Owing to the use of advanced potentials for
t<0
, the plasma absorbs this radiation over the interval
t\in(-\infty,0]
until the prescribed state at
t=0
is reached. For
t>0
, the perturbation decays through standard Landau damping, and the radiation field is re-emitted into the environment. The resulting formulation is manifestly time-symmetric.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we have shown that exact time-domain solutions of linear Landau-damping problems can be obtained by numerical Fourier inversion without determining the complex zeros of dielectric functions. The central idea of the method is to construct the distribution-function perturbation
f_{1}
as a superposition of the forward-evolving solution
f_{1}^{+}
and the backward-evolving solution
f_{1}^{-}
, both of which are uniquely determined by the initial condition
f_{1}(x,v,t=0)
. Equivalently, the construction can be viewed as implicitly defining a prehistory of the distribution function that evolves from
t=-\infty
toward the prescribed initial condition at
t=0
, thereby removing the discontinuity associated with a one-sided response. Provided that the initial condition admits a smooth time evolution and that
f_{1}
remains compactly supported, the spectrum of
f_{1}
decays superalgebraically. Spectral contributions outside a finite frequency interval are therefore negligible.
