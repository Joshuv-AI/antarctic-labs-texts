Efficacy and Safety of Quarterly Dosing SHR-1918 in Patients With Suboptimally Controlled Hyperlipidemia
Authors: Wei Zhong, Jing Zhang, Dan Zhu, Hanjun Pei, Wanling Wu, Rongjie Huang, Delu Yin, Xiaohong Gao, Bin Li, Chao Lv, Min Zhu, Ying Zhu, Zhixiong Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSHR-1918 is a long-acting angiopoietin-like 3 antibody with established lipid-lowering efficacy at 600 mg once every 4 weeks or once every 8 weeks.ObjectivesThis randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study assessed the efficacy and safety of SHR-1918 at an extended dosing interval (once every 12 weeks) in patients with hyperlipidemia.MethodsPatients who did not achieve their low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) targets after statin run-in were randomized (2:1) to receive subcutaneous SHR-1918 (600 or 1,200 mg) or placebo once every 12 weeks for 24 weeks. The primary endpoint was percent change in LDL-C from baseline to week 24.ResultsForty-four patients were randomized to SHR-1918 600 mg (n = 14), SHR-1918 1,200 mg (n = 16), or placebo (n = 14). At week 24, the percent change in LDL-C from baseline was −20.8% (95% CI: −28.4 to −13.1), −25.1% (95% CI: −32.4 to −17.7), and −2.2% (95% CI: −10.1 to 5.7) in the 3 groups, respectively. The between-group difference (SHR-1918 vs placebo) was −18.6% (95% CI: −29.5 to −7.6; P = 0.0015) for 600 mg and −22.9% (95% CI: −33.7 to −12.1; P = 0.0001) for 1,200 mg. The percent change in triglyceride was −49.8% (95% CI: −61.7 to −37.9) and −58.8% (95% CI: −70.3 to −47.3) with SHR-1918 600 mg and 1,200 mg compared with −1.2% (95% CI: −13.6 to 11.2) with placebo, showing between-group differences of −48.6% (95% CI: −65.8 to −31.4; P < 0.0001) and −57.6% (95% CI: −74.6 to −40.6; P < 0.0001).ConclusionsSHR-1918, at a once every 12 weeks dosing frequency, further reduced LDL-C and triglyceride levels in hyperlipidemic patients inadequately controlled with background lipid-lowering therapies. (Evaluation of the Efficacy and Safety of Multiple Subcutaneous Injections of SHR-1918 in Patients With Hyperlipidemia in Poor Control; NCT06471218)

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1 In China, the ASCVD burden, especially ischemic heart disease, showed an increasing trend over the past 3 decades.2 Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is an established causal risk factor for ASCVD; both the magnitude and duration of LDL-C exposure contribute to an individual's ASCVD risk.3, 4, 5 Triglyceride (TG) is also strongly associated with ASCVD as indicated by the combined Copenhagen studies.6

Conventional treatment options for elevated lipid levels include statins, cholesterol absorption inhibitors, and proprotein convertase subtilisin kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors.7 Moderate- to high-intensity statin therapies are expected to reduce LDL-C by 30% to more than 50%.7 However, high-intensity statin therapy may result in an increased risk of muscular and hepatic adverse reactions in the Chinese population.8,9 According to a systematic review, the risk of side effects with high-intensity statin therapy was almost twice of that with moderate-intensity therapy.10 Adding cholesterol absorption inhibitors or PCSK9 inhibitors to statin therapy can further reduce the ASCVD risk, but only to a limited extent of 6% and 15%, respectively.11,12 According to the latest cross-sectional study in China, the achievement rate of LDL-C target was only 16.7% and 18.0% among adults at high and very high ASCVD risk, and nontreatment rates were 36.9% and 14.4%, respectively, suggesting insufficient use of guideline-recommended lipid-lowering regimens.13 Lipid control in Chinese patients could be further optimized through different administration routes and dosing intervals.

Angiopoietin-like 3 (ANGPTL3), a hepatic protein involved in the regulation of lipoprotein metabolism and homeostasis, has emerged as a novel target for lipid regulation. ANGPTL3 inhibits lipoprotein lipase and endothelial lipase activities, which in turn reduces the hydrolysis of lipoproteins, leading to increased serum lipid levels.14, 15, 16 Genetic and clinical studies have shown that loss-of-function mutations in the ANGPTL3 gene were associated with low concentrations of TG, LDL-C, and other lipids.16,17 The simultaneous regulation of major lipoproteins makes ANGPTL3 an important therapeutic target for lipid management. Several ANGPTL3-targeting agents are currently available or under clinical development. Evinacumab, an ANGPTL3 monoclonal antibody administered by intravenous infusion once monthly, is approved as add-on therapy for patients with homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia.18 Zodasiran and solbinsiran are small interfering RNA therapeutics targeting the expression of ANGPTL3 in the liver, and can yield 14% to 20% LDL-C reduction with once-every-12-weeks subcutaneous administration.19,20

SHR-1918 is a long-acting, fully-human, monoclonal antibody directed against ANGPTL3. The phase 1 dose-ascending study of SHR-1918 demonstrated favorable safety and tolerability across 100 mg to 1,200 mg dose levels, achieving promising lipid-lowering effects with an extended half-life of up to 53.5 days.21 In a previous phase 2 study, SHR-1918 at 150 to 600 mg administered once every 4 weeks or 600 mg once every 8 weeks significantly reduced LDL-C and TG levels in hyperlipidemic patients.22 This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial was conducted to further explore the efficacy and safety of SHR-1918 at an extended dosing interval, once every 12 weeks in Chinese patients with hyperlipidemia suboptimally controlled with standard lipid-lowering therapies.

Methods

Study population

This study enrolled hyperlipidemic patients aged 18 to 75 years with fasting LDL-C levels at moderate or higher risk for ASCVD according to the Chinese Guidelines for Lipid Management 2023 (protocol p.48) (Supplementary Material) and had a fasting TG level of ≤5.6 mmol/L. Key exclusion criteria included genetically or clinically diagnosed homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia; NYHA functional classification of III or IV; history of acute coronary syndrome, percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass grafting, peripheral arterial revascularization, or cerebrovascular disease within 1 month before screening; severe arrhythmia within 3 months before screening; uncontrolled hypertension at screening or before randomization; use of SHR-1918 or any other ANGPTL3 inhibitors within 6 months before screening; within 5 half-lives of taking medications or food contraindicated with statins; or inadequate organ function. The full eligibility criteria are listed in the protocol (p.48) (Supplementary Material).

The protocol and all amendments were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Meizhou People's Hospital (lead center) and Ethics Committees of the other study centers. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice, and local laws and regulations. All patients provided written informed consent.

Study design

This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial was conducted at 8 hospitals in China (NCT06471218). An illustration of the study design is shown in Figure 1. Eligible patients entered a run-in period, during which they received background statins, with or without other lipid-lowering agents, for 4 to 8 weeks until lipid stabilization. At the end of the run-in period, those who did not achieve their target LDL-C levels (protocol p.52) (Supplemental Appendix) became eligible for randomization. In the treatment period, patients were randomized in a 2:1 ratio to receive subcutaneous SHR-1918 (at 600 mg or 1,200 mg) once every 12 weeks or placebo once every 12 weeks for 24 weeks. Background lipid-lowering therapies remained stable. On treatment completion, patients were followed for 8 weeks for safety observation.Figure 1Study DesignSC = subcutaneous administration; Q12 W = once every 12 weeks.

Randomization was conducted using a centralized interactive web response system. Block randomization was used with a block size of 6. The randomization code was generated with SAS (version 9.4) by a randomization specialist. All patients, investigators, and other staff involved in the study were masked to treatment allocation. SHR-1918 and placebo injections were identical in appearance, labeling, and packaging.

Endpoints and assessments

The primary endpoint was the percent change in serum LDL-C from baseline to week 24. The key secondary endpoint was the percent change in serum TG from baseline to week 24. Other secondary endpoints included the absolute changes in LDL-C and TG from baseline to week 24, percentage and absolute changes in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), non-HDL-C, apolipoprotein B, Lipoprotein (a), total cholesterol, and apolipoprotein A1 from baseline to week 24, LDL-C target attainment rate in the overall population and in ASCVD risk subgroups at week 24, as well as safety, pharmacokinetics, and immunogenicity.

Blood samples for efficacy, pharmacokinetics, and immunogenicity assessments were collected on day 1 at predose, day 85 at predose, and on day 169 of the treatment period. Serum lipid levels were determined using a validated turbidimetric inhibition immunoassay, and serum concentration of SHR-1918 was determined using a validated enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay at the central laboratory. Safety assessments included adverse events (AEs), serious AEs, vital signs, physical examinations, 12-lead electrocardiogram, and laboratory tests. AEs were monitored throughout the study and coded according to the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (Version 27.0). AE severities were graded as mild, moderate, or severe.

Statistical analysis

This study sequentially compared the efficacy of SHR-1918 1,200 mg once every 12 weeks and 600 mg once every 12 weeks vs placebo, with the primary endpoint of percent change in LDL-C from baseline to week 24. Because the primary endpoint is defined only at the single time point of week 24 (the week 12 visit was used solely for exploratory analyses), sample size estimation was based on a 2-independent-sample Student's t-test, without adjustments for repeated measures or baseline covariates. Assuming a between-group difference of at least 30% for each dose level, an SD of 20%, and a 2-sided significance level of 0.05, 11 patients in each of the SHR-1918 600 mg, SHR-1918 1,200 mg, and placebo group would provide 90% power in detecting superiority of each SHR-1918 dose cohort over placebo. With an assumed dropout rate of 20%, a total of 42 patients was planned for this study.

Efficacy and safety were analyzed in all randomized patients who had received at least 1 dose of the study treatment. Pharmacokinetics was analyzed in all randomized and treated patients who had at least 1 pharmacokinetic datum. Immunogenicity was analyzed in all randomized and treated patients who had nonmissing predose and at least 1 postdose immunogenicity datum. The percent change in LDL-C from baseline was analyzed using the mixed model for repeated measures, with the treatment group, study visits, and interaction between treatment group and study visits as fixed effect, participants as random effect, and the baseline LDL-C level as a covariate. Missing data were handled under the missing-at-random assumption. The between-group difference was initially estimated using an unstructured covariance matrix. If convergence is not achieved, a compound symmetry covariance matrix would be applied. The percent change in TG, as well as other continuous endpoints, were analyzed using the same methods. Non-normally distributed continuous data (change and percent change from baseline in lipoprotein [a]) were analyzed using robust regression via M-estimation. For the binary outcome of LDL-C target attainment rates, the exact method was used to calculate the rate differences and 95% CIs between the SHR-1918 groups and the placebo group, and the chi-square test or Fisher exact test was used for intergroup comparisons, with P values calculated. LDL-C reduction was also analyzed in prespecified subgroups. No other multiplicity adjustment was applied beyond the hierarchical testing strategy. Safety and immunogenicity data were summarized using descriptive statistics. Serum concentration of SHR-1918 was summarized and plotted over time for pharmacokinetic analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted with SAS (version 9.4).

Results

Patients

Between July 2, 2024, and May 16, 2025, 44 eligible patients were randomized and treated with SHR-1918 600 mg once every 12 weeks (n = 14), SHR-1918 1,200 mg once every 12 weeks (n = 16), or placebo (n = 14). Forty-two of 44 patients (95.5%) had completed the study treatment, and 2 (4.5%) patients (one in each of the SHR-1918 1,200 mg and placebo group) discontinued study treatment because of protocol deviations (Supplemental Figure 1). The demographics and baseline characteristics were generally balanced across treatment groups (Table 1). The mean (SD) age was 54.6 (10.1) years. Most patients were male (37 of 44, 84.1%). The mean (SD) baseline LDL-C was 2.54 (0.85) mmol/L, and the mean (SD) baseline TG was 1.65 (0.73) mmol/L. Efficacy

All 44 patients were included in the efficacy analysis. An unstructured matrix was ultimately used for the final analysis of the primary endpoint. SHR-1918 considerably lowered serum LDL-C level compared with placebo. At week 24, the least squares mean percent change in LDL-C from baseline was −20.8% (95% CI: −28.4 to −13.3) in the SHR-1918 600 mg group, −25.1% (95% CI: −32.4 to −17.7) in the SHR-1918 1,200 mg group, and −2.2% (95% CI: −10.1 to 5.71) in the placebo group (Table 2, Figure 2A). The between-group difference vs placebo was −18.6% (95% CI: −29.5 to −7.6; nominal P = 0.0015) for SHR-1918 600 mg and −22.9% (95% CI: −33.7 to −12.1; nominal P = 0.0001) for SHR-1918 1,200 mg. Sensitivity analyses with other structure models suggested consistent findings. A waterfall plot for individual LDL-C changes is shown in Figure 2B. SHR-1918 suggested consistent efficacy in LDL-C reduction over placebo across clinically relevant subgroups, including age, body mass index, baseline LDL-C and TG levels, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and ASCVD risk (Figure 3). The absolute change in LDL-C at week 24 was −0.64 mmol/L (95% CI: −0.85 to −0.43) with SHR-1918 600 mg and −0.67 mmol/L (95% CI: −0.87 to −0.47) with SHR-1918 1,200 mg, which were considerably lower than the −0.13 mmol/L (95% CI: −0.35 to 0.09) with placebo (Table 2). The between-group difference was −0.51 mmol/L (95% CI: −0.81 to −0.21; nominal P = 0.0016) for the 600 mg group and −0.54 mmol/L (95% CI: −0.84 to −0.24; nominal P = 0.0007) for the 1,200 mg group. Overall, 21.4% (3 of 14), 31.3% (5 of 16), and 7.1% (1 of 14) of patients in the SHR-1918 600 mg, 1,200 mg, and placebo groups had achieved their LDL-C targets after 24 weeks. The forest plot shows the difference between SHR-1918 and placebo in the least squares mean percent change in LDL-C from baseline to week 24. Error bars indicate 95% CIs. ASCVD = atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease; BMI = body mass index; other abbreviations as in Figure 2.

Serum TG substantially decreased with SHR-1918 treatment (Table 2, Figure 2C). At week 24, SHR-1918 600 mg and 1,200 mg resulted in changes of −49.8% (95% CI: −61.7 to −37.9) and −58.8% (95% CI: −70.3 to −47.3) in TG, respectively. Compared with the −1.2% (95% CI: −13.6 to 11.2) change in the placebo group, the between-group difference was −48.6% (95% CI: −65.8 to −31.4; nominal P < 0.0001) and −57.6% (95% CI: −74.6 to −40.6; nominal P < 0.0001). Individual TG changes are shown in Figure 2D. The change in absolute TG also favored SHR-1918 600 mg (−0.87 mmol/L; 95% CI: −1.17 to −0.57) and 1,200 mg (−1.04 mmol/L; 95% CI: −1.33 to −0.75) over placebo (0.06 mmol/L; 95% CI: −0.25 to 0.37; Table 2). Reductions were also observed in non–HDL-C, apolipoprotein B, total cholesterol, HDL-C, and apolipoprotein A1 following 24 weeks of SHR-1918 treatment compared with placebo (Supplemental Table 1). Lipoprotein (a) did not show obvious changes from baseline in all 3 treatment groups.

Safety

Of the 44 patients included in the safety analysis, 33 (75.0%) reported at least 1 treatment-emergent AE (TEAE). The TEAE incidence rate was 73.3% (22 of 30) in the SHR-1918 groups and 78.6% (11 of 14) in the placebo group (Table 3). Most of the TEAEs were mild in severity. Treatment-related AEs were reported by 26.7% (8 of 30) SHR-1918-treated patients and 35.7% (5 of 14) placebo-treated patients. Serious AEs were reported by 6.3% (1 of 16) patient in the SHR-1918 1,200 mg group (angina unstable) and 7.1% (1 of 14) patients in the placebo group (anal abscess and hemorrhoids), but none of which were related to the study treatment. Pharmacokinetics and immunogenicity

The 30 patients treated with SHR-1918 were included in the pharmacokinetic analysis. The SHR-1918 concentration-time profile is shown in Supplemental Figure 2. The geometric mean (GeoCV%) trough concentration of SHR-1918 was 21.6 (36.0) μg/mL on days 85 and 32.6 (31.5) μg/mL on day 169 at the 600-mg dose level, and 64.8 (29.8) μg/mL on day 85 and 97.0 (30.7) μg/mL on day 169 at the 1,200 mg dose level.

Antidrug antibodies were not detected in any patients at any timepoints during the study.

Discussion

In this randomized controlled phase 2 trial, SHR-1918, a long-acting subcutaneous ANGPTL3 inhibitor, considerably lowered serum lipid levels in patients with hyperlipidemia inadequately controlled with standard lipid-lowering therapies (Central Illustration). Compared with placebo, SHR-1918 600 mg and 1,200 mg once every 12 weeks lowered serum LDL-C by 18.6% and 22.9% and serum TG by 48.6% and 57.6% from baseline to week 24. LDL-C reduction with SHR-1918 was consistent across clinically relevant subgroups. Study Overview

In this randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial conducted in China, subcutaneous ANGPTL3-antibody SHR-1918, at 600 mg or 1,200 mg once every 12 weeks, substantially lowered LDL-C and TG compared with placebo. Error bars indicate 95% CIs. ANGPTL3 = angiopoietin-like 3; Q12 W = once every 12 weeks; LDL-C = low-density lipoprotein cholesterol; TG = triglyceride.

To our knowledge, SHR-1918 is the first ANGPTL3 monoclonal antibody with a once every 12 weeks or quarterly dosing frequency. The lipid-lowering efficacy of SHR-1918 was comparable to that of other agents targeting ANGPTL3. Zodasiran and solbinsiran are ANGPTL3-targeted small interfering RNA molecules administered as once every 12 weeks subcutaneous injections. Zodasiran, at doses ranging from 50 mg to 200 mg, resulted in LDL-C reductions of 14% to 20% and TG reductions of 51% to 63% compared with placebo.19 Solbinsiran, at doses ranging from 100 mg to 800 mg, lowered LDL-C and TG concentrations by a maximum of 16.8% and 52.5%, respectively, compared with placebo.20 Plozasiran is a small interfering RNA targeting apoprotein C3, a glycoprotein that reduces the metabolism of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins via the same pathway of lipoprotein lipase inhibition.23 In the phase 2 trials of plozasiran, once every 12 weeks plozasiran lowered TG by 48.8% to 62.4% in patients with mixed hyperlipidemia and severe hypertriglyceridemia.24,25 At the same once every 12 weeks dosing frequency, the magnitude of LDL-C reduction with SHR-1918 compared favorably to that with ANGPTL3 small interfering RNA, and the magnitude of TG reduction was similar to that with apoprotein C3 small interfering RNA. Nevertheless, direct comparisons between trial results should be interpreted with caution. In terms of mechanism of action, small interfering RNA molecules primarily inhibit hepatic synthesis and secretion of ANGPTL3, whereas the monoclonal antibody SHR-1918 acts systematically to inhibit circulating ANGPTL3 throughout the body. Accordingly, compared to the liver-targeting property of small interfering RNA, the systematic distribution of SHR-1918 may enable more comprehensive inhibition of the target, potentially contributing to greater LDL-C reduction.

The present study is the first to explore both the once every 12 weeks dosing interval and the higher 1,200 mg dose of SHR-1918. The dose selection rationale was based on the phase 1 dose-ascending study of SHR-1918, in which the LDL-C reductions from baseline to day 85 ranged 36.9% to 39.0% across the 750 mg, 900 mg, and 1,200 mg dose levels, suggesting a plateau in efficacy at higher doses. The findings from the phase 1 trial support the feasibility of once every 12 weeks administration for the high-dose regimen and informed the selection of the 600 mg and 1,200 mg doses in this study. The previous phase 2 trial demonstrated that SHR-1918 at 300 to 600 mg once every 4 weeks or 600 mg once every 8 weeks lowered LDL-C by 21.7% to 29.9% and TG by 51.7% to 63.2% compared with placebo.22 In the present study, a lower dosing frequency had achieved LDL-C and TG reductions to similar extents. The durable efficacy of SHR-1918 is attributed to the optimized molecular design, where the Fc segment is modified through amino acid mutation to protect SHR-1918 from lysosomal degradation.21 Compared with the once every 4 weeks and once every 8 weeks regimens of SHR-1918, as well as other lipid-lowering therapies requiring daily (statins) or once every 2 weeks to once every 4 weeks administration (PCSK9 inhibitors), once every 12 weeks dosing offers greater convenience and flexibility and may reduce the treatment burden for patients requiring long-term management. Evidence from a retrospective observational study shows that a longer dosing interval was associated with better patient adherence in real-world practice. The 12-month patient adherence was 77% for inclisiran, a small interfering RNA administered twice yearly for LDL-C lowering, compared with 67% for the PCSK9 inhibitor evolocumab that requires administration every 2 weeks or 1 month.26 Although real-world data for SHR-1918 is currently not available, data from other lipid-lowering agents suggest great potential of once every 12 weeks SHR-1918 in improving patient adherence and treatment compliance in long-term lipid management. SHR-1918's subcutaneous route of administration is also more acceptable than the approved monthly intravenous infusion of evinacumab, another ANGPTL3 monoclonal antibody.

Reductions in HDL-C and apolipoprotein A1 were observed during the study, which are consistent with the mechanism of ANGPTL3 inhibition. ANGTPL3 inhibits endothelial lipase, an enzyme that hydrolyzes phospholipids in HDL particles and promotes HDL clearance.27,28 The inhibition of ANGPTL3 restores endothelial lipase activity, leading to a reduction in circulating HDL-C levels. This effect is biologically intrinsic to ANGPTL3 inhibition and has been consistently observed with other ANGPTL3-targeted therapies including evinacumab, zodasiran, and solbinsiran.20,29,30 Importantly, the HDL-C reduction associated with ANGPTL3 inhibition does not offset the benefits derived from lowering atherogenic lipoproteins. In the preclinical studies of evinacumab, a considerable decrease in atherosclerotic lesion size was observed despite decreased HDL-C levels.17 Carriers of ANGPTL3 loss-of-function mutations also reported having lower risk of ASCVD even with lower HDL-C levels.17

Although the magnitude of a clinically meaningful lipid reduction is not defined, the proportion of patients achieving their LDL-C targets may provide insight to SHR-1918's clinical relevance. Both the overall and the ASCVD risk-stratified LDL-C target attainment rates were numerically higher in patients treated with SHR-1918 than placebo. However, because the absolute number of patients was small, the clinical significance of once every 12 weeks SHR-1918 remains to be confirmed in future trials with a larger sample size and a longer follow-up time. It is also worth noting that given the exploratory nature of this study and the absence of previous data for the once every 12 weeks dosing regimen, a 30% difference in LDL-C reduction between SHR-1918 and placebo was prespecified for sample size estimation. In retrospect, this assumption was somewhat optimistic, as the observed LDL-C reductions were numerically smaller. However, it is important to emphasize that this study was designed primarily to assess the therapeutic potential of SHR-1918 administered at an extended dosing interval, and it successfully demonstrated superiority over placebo. This exploratory study was not intended for confirmatory hypothesis testing, and the sample size was not powered to formally compare the 2 active dose groups. For future phase 3 trials involving SHR-1918 long-term administration, the effect size estimates derived from the current study, particularly the observed LDL-C reductions, should inform sample size calculations.

SHR-1918 was well-tolerated at 600 mg and 1,200 mg. All TEAEs were mild or moderate in severity. The incidence of TEAE was comparable between the SHR-1918 group (73.3%) and the placebo group (78.6%), while the incidence of treatment-related AEs was lower in the SHR-1918 group (26.7% vs 35.7%). Two patients had serious AEs, but none of which were treatment-related. Compared with AEs observed in previous SHR-1918 studies, no new safety signals were identified. The AE profile of SHR-1918 was consistent with that of other ANGPTL3-targeting agents, with the most frequently reported AEs being upper respiratory tract infection, urinary tract infection, injection site reactions, and increased blood sugar.19,20,30,31 Notably, the incidence of increased alanine aminotransferase was lower in SHR-1918 than placebo (6.7% vs 14.3%), and the incidence of increased aspartate aminotransferase was similar between the 2 groups (6.7% vs 7.1%), suggesting limited impact of SHR-1918 on hepatic function, with a favorable safety profile. Antidrug antibodies were not observed during the study.

Study limitations

Specifically, this is a small clinical study with only 44 patients, and the safety follow-up was relatively short. These features limited our ability to detect less frequent AEs and evaluate long-term cardiovascular outcomes. The small sample size also introduced certain imbalances in the baseline characteristics between study groups. For example, 4 patients (25%) in the SHR-1918 1,200 mg group had received PCSK9 inhibitors at baseline, whereas none of the patients in the other 2 groups had received PCSK9 inhibitors. Although such imbalance is not uncommon in trials with a small sample size, this may affect the interpretation of the study results. Sensitivity analyses should be planned for future small-sample studies to address the between-group imbalances. Moreover, placebo was used as control rather than other available ANGPTL3-targeting agents; therefore, we cannot draw conclusions on how SHR-1918 compares with other lipid-lowering agents based on cross-trial comparisons. In addition, as this was an exploratory study without prespecified hypothesis testing for efficacy comparisons between the 2 SHR-1918 dose levels, the optimal regimen for once every 12 weeks dosing remains to be confirmed in future studies. This study enrolled Chinese patients, and most of the participants were male, which limited the generalizability of our findings. Last, free ANGPTL3 levels were not measured during the study.

Conclusions

SHR-1918 resulted in substantial and durable LDL-C and TG reductions with a manageable safety profile in patients with hyperlipidemia inadequately controlled with standard lipid-lowering therapies. By achieving additional lipid lowering, SHR-1918 holds the potential to further improve lipid management that is not fully achieved by statins alone. Its efficacy, along with the once every 12 weeks or quarterly dosing frequency, makes SHR-1918 a novel and convenient treatment option for lipid management, supporting the initiation of later-phase trials.
