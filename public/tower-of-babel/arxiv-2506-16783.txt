Quadratic estimates for the H ∞ H^{\infty} -functional calculus of bisectorial Clifford operators
F. Colombo, F. Mantovani, P. Schlosser

Abstract
. The H ∞ H^{\infty} -functional calculus is a two-step procedure, introduced by A. McIntosh, that allows the definition of functions of sectorial operators in Banach spaces. It plays a crucial role in the spectral theory of differential operators, as well as in their applications to evolution equations and various other fields of science. An extension of the H ∞ H^{\infty} -functional calculus also exists in the hypercomplex setting, where it is based on the notion of S S -spectrum. Originally this was done for sectorial quaternionic operators, but then also generalized all the way to bisectorial fully Clifford operators. In the latter setting and in Hilbert spaces, this paper now characterizes the boundedness of the H ∞ H^{\infty} -functional calculus through certain quadratic estimates. Due to substantial differences in the definitions of the S S -spectrum and the S S -resolvent operators, the proofs of quadratic estimates in this setting face additional challenges compared to the classical theory of complex operators.

1.
Introduction

The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for sectorial operators was introduced by A. McIntosh in
[
51
]
and has been extensively studied in subsequent works such as
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
37
]
. This calculus extends the holomorphic functional calculus, see
[
36
,
44
]
, to unbounded operators. It plays a significant role in the spectral theory of differential operators and their applications. Precisely, this calculus is a crucial tool in the theory of maximal regularity for parabolic evolution equations, in resolving Kato’s square root problem and in its applications to partial differential equations, including systems of elliptic partial differential operators and Schrödinger operators. For further details see the books
[
43
,
45
,
46
]
.

In general, the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus defines unbounded operators. However in
[
31
]
the authors M. Cowling, J. Doust, A. McIntosh, and A. Yagi employed ideas from harmonic analysis to characterize its boundedness for sectorial operators via suitable quadratic estimates.

In a Banach module
V
over the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
, the spectral theory for Clifford operators
T:\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq V\rightarrow V
differs from the classical complex theory due to the noncommutativity of the spectral parameter
s
, which is a paravector, and the operator
T
. In this framework, the left and the right
S
-resolvent operators

(1.1)

S_{L}^{-1}(s,T):=Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\overline{s}-TQ_{s}[T]^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{R}^{-1}(s,T):=(\overline{s}-T)Q_{s}[T]^{-1},

involve the inverse of the second order operator

(1.2)

Q_{s}[T]:=T^{2}-2s_{0}T+|s|^{2},\qquad\text{with }\operatorname{dom}(Q_{s}[T])=\operatorname{dom}(T^{2}).

Hence the corresponding spectrum (called the
S
-spectrum) was introduced in
[
29
,
28
]
, see also the books
[
19
,
20
]
, and is based on the invertibility of this exact operator, namely

(1.3)

\rho_{S}(T):=\big\{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\;\big|\;Q_{s}[T]^{-1}\in\mathcal{B}(V)\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma_{S}(T):=\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\setminus\rho_{S}(T),

where
\mathcal{B}(V)
denotes the set of bounded, everywhere defined operators.

Quaternionic and Clifford operators are fundamental in various areas of mathematics and physics. For example, they appear in quaternionic quantum mechanics, see
[
1
,
11
]
, as well as in vector analysis, differential geometry, and hypercomplex analysis. We also point out that the spectral theorem based on the
S
-spectrum holds also for quaternionic and Clifford operators, see
[
2
,
22
]
. For further details, see the concluding remarks at the end of this paper.

Due to the noncommutative nature of the Clifford numbers, there are two
S
-resolvent operators (
1.1
) needed to define the
S
-functional calculus, that is the analogue
of the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus, in this setting. Specifically, for left slice hyperholomorphic functions, we utilize the left
S
-resolvent operator, while for right slice hyperholomorphic functions, the right
S
-resolvent operator is employed, to define

(1.4)

f(T):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}f(s)\quad\text{and}\quad f(T):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}f(s)ds_{J}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T),

where
U
is a suitable open set that contains the
S
-spectrum and
J
is one of the imaginary units of
\mathbb{R}_{n}
.

The generalization of (
1.4
) to an
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for at least left slice
hyperholomorphic functions was introduced in 2016, see
[
3
,
16
,
19
]
. It applies to sectorial or bisectorial right linear operators
T
and to functions
f
that may grow polynomially at zero and at infinity. The calculus is constructed by the product

(1.5)

f(T):=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T),

of the inverse the regularizer
e(T)
, with the regularized operator
(ef)(T)
. Both expressions are understood as the
\omega
-functional calculus, a calculus for bisectorial operator similar to (
1.4
). This calculus is then well-defined since it can be shown that it is independently of the choice of the regularizer
e
. Detailed properties were examined in recent work
[
49
]
inspired by the universality property of the
S
-functional calculus
[
21
]
.

A persistent challenge has been extending this calculus to right slice hyperholomorphic functions. Due to the noncommutative multiplication of the Clifford numbers, this can not be done in a symmetric way. As noted in
[
19
, Remark 7.2.2]
, this definition would depend on the choice of the regularizer function
e
. In the recent paper
[
24
]
, we addressed this unresolved issue by demonstrating that right linear operators can also admit an
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus for right slice hyperholomorphic functions, utilizing the framework of multivalued operators (also known as right linear relations).

The aim of this paper is now to investigate the boundedness of the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
1.5
). In particular, we find certain quadratic estimates which characterize the boundedness of the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus in the sense that for every bounded function
f
, also
f(T)
is bounded. The precise statement of this characterization, see also Theorem
4.7
, is as follows: Let
T:D(T)\subseteq V\rightarrow V
be closed, injective and bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. For some
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
let furthermore
\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
and
N^{\infty}(D_{\theta})
be the function spaces from Definition
3.2
and Definition
3.4
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

i)

There exists a constant
c\geq 0
, such that
f(T)\in\mathcal{B}(V)
and

\|f(T)\|\leq c\|f\|_{\infty},\qquad f\in\mathcal{N}^{\infty}(D_{\theta});

ii)

For some/each
0\neq f\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, there exist
c_{f},d_{f}>0
, such that for every
v\in V

c_{f}\|v\|\leq\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|f(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq d_{f}\|v\|.

iii)

For some/each
0\neq f\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, there exist
d_{f},\widetilde{d}_{f}\geq 0
, such that for every
v\in V

\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|f(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq d_{f}\|v\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|f(tT^{*})v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\widetilde{d}_{f}\|v\|.

The plan of the paper: Section
2
contains preliminaries on Clifford algebras and Clifford modules. In Section
3
, we survey the basic facts on bisectorial operators and the
S
-functional calculus. In Section
4
, we will prove the main result, the characterization of bounded operators defined via the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus in Theorem
4.7
. Section
4
puts our results into perspective with some concluding remarks on applications and research directions.

2.
Preliminaries on Clifford Algebras and Clifford modules

Clifford algebras are associative algebras that generalize complex numbers and quaternions. They provide a mathematical framework for studying operator theory in a noncommutative setting. There are various approaches to extending spectral theory from complex operators to vector operators. The setting we focus on is that of slice hyperholomorphicity, which offers the advantage that
n
-tuples, or more generally
2^{n}
-tuples, of noncommuting operators can be embedded into a Clifford algebra. With this notion of holomorphicity in the Clifford setting, the concept of spectrum for Clifford operators becomes both very clear and explicit.

We now fix the algebraic and functional analytic setting in which we work. The underlying algebra we will consider is the real
Clifford algebra

\mathbb{R}_{n}
over
n

imaginary units

e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
, which satisfy the relations

e_{i}^{2}=-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad e_{i}e_{j}=-e_{j}e_{i},\qquad i\neq j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

More precisely,
\mathbb{R}_{n}
is given by

\mathbb{R}_{n}:=\Big\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}s_{A}e_{A}\;\Big|\;s_{A}\in\mathbb{R},\,A\in\mathcal{A}\Big\},

using the index set

\mathcal{A}:=\big\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{r})\;\big|\;r\in\{0,\dots,n\},\,1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{r}\leq n\big\},

and the
basis vectors

e_{A}:=e_{i_{1}}\dots e_{i_{r}}
. Note that for
A=\emptyset
the empty product of imaginary units is the real number
e_{\emptyset}=1
. Furthermore, we define for every Clifford number
s\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
its
conjugate
and its
absolute value

(2.1)

\overline{s}:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}(-1)^{\frac{|A|(|A|+1)}{2}}s_{A}e_{A}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|s|^{2}:=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}s_{A}^{2}.

An important subset of the Clifford numbers are the so called
paravectors

\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:=\big\{s_{0}+s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n}\;\big|\;s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in\mathbb{R}\big\}.

For any paravector
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we define the
imaginary part

\operatorname{Im}(s):=s_{1}e_{1}+\dots+s_{n}e_{n},

and the conjugate and the modulus in (
2.1
) reduce to

\overline{s}=s_{0}-s_{1}e_{1}-\dots-s_{n}e_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|s|^{2}=s_{0}^{2}+s_{1}^{2}+\dots+s_{n}^{2}.

The sphere of purely imaginary paravectors with modulus
1
, is defined by

\mathbb{S}:=\big\{J\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\;\big|\;J_{0}=0,\,|J|=1\big\}.

Any element
J\in\mathbb{S}
satisfies
J^{2}=-1
and hence the corresponding hyperplane

\mathbb{C}_{J}:=\big\{x+Jy\;\big|\;x,y\in\mathbb{R}\big\}

is an isomorphic copy of the complex numbers. Moreover, for every paravector
s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
we consider the corresponding
(n-1)
-sphere

[s]:=\big\{s_{0}+J|\operatorname{Im}(s)|\;\big|\;J\in\mathbb{S}\big\}.

A subset
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is called
axially symmetric
, if
[s]\subseteq U
for every
s\in U
.

Next, we introduce the notion of slice hyperholomorphic functions
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{n}
, defined on an axially symmetric open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

Definition 2.1

(Slice hyperholomorphic functions)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be open, axially symmetric and consider

\mathcal{U}:=\big\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;\big|\;x+\mathbb{S}y\subseteq U\big\}.

A function
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{n}
is called
left
(resp.
right
)
slice hyperholomorphic
, if there exist continuously differentiable functions
f_{0},f_{1}:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{n}
, such that for every
(x,y)\in\mathcal{U}
:

i)

The function
f
admits for every
J\in\mathbb{S}
the representation

f(x+Jy)=f_{0}(x,y)+Jf_{1}(x,y),\quad\Big(\text{resp.}\;f(x+Jy)=f_{0}(x,y)+f_{1}(x,y)J\Big).

ii)

The functions
f_{0},f_{1}
satisfy the
compatibility conditions

f_{0}(x,-y)=f_{0}(x,y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{1}(x,-y)=-f_{1}(x,y).

iii)

The functions
f_{0},f_{1}
satisfy the
Cauchy-Riemann equations

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{0}(x,y)=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f_{1}(x,y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial y}f_{0}(x,y)=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{1}(x,y).

The class of left (resp. right) slice hyperholomorphic functions on
U
is denoted by
\mathcal{SH}_{L}(U)
(resp.
\mathcal{SH}_{R}(U)
). In the special case that
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are real valued, we call
f

intrinsic
and the space of intrinsic functions is denoted by
\mathcal{N}(U)
.

Next, we turn our attention to Clifford modules over
\mathbb{R}_{n}
. For a real Hilbert space
V_{\mathbb{R}}
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
and corresponding norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
, we define the corresponding
Clifford module

V:=\Big\{\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}v_{A}\otimes e_{A}\;\Big|\;v_{A}\in V_{\mathbb{R}},\,A\in\mathcal{A}\Big\},

and equip it with the
inner product

(2.2)

\langle v,w\rangle:=\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}\langle v_{A},w_{B}\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}\,\overline{e_{A}}\,e_{B},\qquad v,w\in V,

and the generated
norm

\|v\|^{2}:=\operatorname{Sc}\langle v,v\rangle=\sum\nolimits_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\|v_{A}\|_{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\qquad v\in V.

For any vector
v=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}v_{A}\otimes e_{A}\in V
and any Clifford number
s=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{A}}s_{B}e_{B}\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, we establish the left and the right scalar multiplication

\displaystyle sv:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(s_{B}v_{A})\otimes(e_{B}e_{A}),\qquad\textit{(left-multiplication)}

\displaystyle vs:=

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{A,B\in\mathcal{A}}(v_{A}s_{B})\otimes(e_{A}e_{B}).\qquad\textit{(right-multiplication)}

From
[
23
, Lemma 2.1]
we recall the well known properties of these products

\displaystyle\|sv\|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|s|\|v\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|vs\|\leq 2^{\frac{n}{2}}|s|\|v\|,\qquad\text{if }s\in\mathbb{R}_{n},

\displaystyle\|sv\|

\displaystyle=\|vs\|=|s|\|v\|,\hskip 137.42685pt\text{if }s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

The sesquilinear form (
2.2
) is now also right-linear in the second, and right-antilinear in the first argument, i.e. for every
u,v,w\in V
,
s\in\mathbb{R}_{n}
, there holds

\displaystyle\langle u,v+w\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle u,v\rangle+\langle u,w\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle v,ws\rangle=\langle v,w\rangle s,

\displaystyle\langle v+w,u\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v,u\rangle+\langle w,u\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle vs,w\rangle=\overline{s}\langle v,w\rangle.

Moreover, there also holds

\langle v,sw\rangle=\langle\overline{s}v,w\rangle.

3.
Bisectorial operators and the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus

For any Hilbert module
V
we will denote the set of
bounded, everywhere defined, right-linear operators

\mathcal{B}(V):=\big\{T:V\rightarrow V\text{ right-linear}\;\big|\;\operatorname{dom}(T)=V,\,T\text{ is bounded}\big\},

as well as the space of
closed operators

\mathcal{K}(V):=\big\{T:V\rightarrow V\text{ right-linear}\;\big|\;\operatorname{dom}(T)\subseteq V\text{ is right linear},\,T\text{ is closed}\big\}.

In order to introduce bisectorial operators we consider for every
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
the
double sector

\omega
D_{\omega}

(3.1)

D_{\omega}:=\big\{re^{J\phi}\;\big|\;r>0,\,J\in\mathbb{S},\,\phi\in I_{\omega}\big\},

using the union of intervals

I_{\omega}:=(-\omega,\omega)\cup(\pi-\omega,\pi+\omega).

Definition 3.1

(Bisectorial operators)
.

An operator
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
is called
bisectorial of angle

\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, if its
S
-spectrum (
1.3
) is contained in the closed double sector

\sigma_{S}(T)\subseteq\overline{D_{\omega}},

and for every
\varphi\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
there is

(3.2)

C_{\varphi}:=\sup\limits_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus(D_{\varphi}\cup\{0\})}|s|\|S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)\|<\infty.

Next, we define the class of functions for which the following
\omega
-functional calculus is defined.

Definition 3.2
.

For every
\theta\in(0,\pi)
let
D_{\theta}
be the double sector from (
3.1
). Then we define the function space

(3.3)

\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta}):=\bigg\{f\in\mathcal{N}(D_{\theta})\;\bigg|\;\exists\alpha>0,\,C_{\alpha}\geq 0:|f(s)|\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}|s|^{\alpha}}{1+|s|^{2\alpha}}\bigg\}.

Note that this class is a little smaller than the one in
[
23
, Definition 3.1]
. However, in view of the characterization in Theorem
4.7
, it is enough to consider these classes of functions. In accordance to
[
23
, Definition 3.5]
, we define for those functions the so called
\omega
-functional calculus
.

Definition 3.3

(
\omega
-functional calculus)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
we define the
\omega
-functional calculus

(3.4)

f(T):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{\varphi}\cap\mathbb{C}_{J}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{J}f(s),

where
\varphi\in(\omega,\theta)
and
J\in\mathbb{S}
are arbitrary and the integral (
3.4
) is independent of that choice.

Note, the the path integral (
3.4
) is understood in the sense

(3.5)

f(T)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum\limits_{\pm}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}S_{L}^{-1}(re^{\pm J\varphi},T)\frac{\mp\operatorname{sgn}(r)e^{\pm J\varphi}}{J}f(re^{\pm J\varphi})dr.

According to
[
23
, Theorem 3.11]
, this
\omega
-functional calculus now satisfies the important product rule

(3.6)

(gf)(T)=g(T)f(T),\qquad g,f\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta}),

which can now be used to enlarge the class of functions (
3.3
) for which the functional calculus
f(T)
is defined. Principally it is possible to allow left and right slice hyperholomorphic functions which are polynomially growing at zero and at infinity, see
[
23
, Proposition 5.2]
and
[
24
, Definition 4.7]
. However, in this paper we will only concentrate on the following classes of bounded intrinsic functions.

Definition 3.4
.

For every
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
let
D_{\theta}
be the double sector from (
3.1
). Then we define the function space of bounded, intrinsic functions

\mathcal{N}^{\infty}(D_{\theta}):=\big\{f\in\mathcal{N}(D_{\theta})\;\big|\;f\text{ is bounded}\big\}.

In the fashion of
[
23
, Definition 5.3]
, we can then define for those functions the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus.

Definition 3.5

(
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be injective and bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\infty}(D_{\theta})
, we define the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus

(3.7)

f(T):=e(T)^{-1}(ef)(T),

where
e(s):=\frac{s}{1+s^{2}}\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
and the corresponding operator
e(T)
via (
3.4
) is injective.

Finally we recall the definition of the adjoint operator in the Clifford setting

Definition 3.6
.

For every
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
with
\overline{\operatorname{dom}}(T)=V
, we define the
adjoint operator

T^{*}\in\mathcal{K}(V)
as the unique operator with the property

\langle T^{*}w,v\rangle=\langle w,Tv\rangle,\qquad v\in\operatorname{dom}(T),w\in\operatorname{dom}(T^{*}),

and with the domain

\operatorname{dom}(T^{*}):=\big\{w\in V\;\big|\;\exists v_{w}\in V:\langle v_{w},v\rangle=\langle w,Tv\rangle,\,v\in\operatorname{dom}(T)\big\}.

An important result, which we will need in the sequel, is regarding the
S
-spectrum and the
S
-functional calculus of the adjoint operator. For a proof see
[
25
]
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
V
be a Hilbert module and
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be injective and bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then also
T^{*}
is injective and bisectorial of the same angle
\omega
. Moreover, for every
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\infty}(D_{\theta})
,
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
3.7
) of
T^{*}
connects to the one for
T
in the sense

f(T^{*})=f(T)^{*}.

4.
Quadratic estimates

In the previous section we have now introduced the main definitions regarding the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus and the spaces of slice hyperholomorphic functions that we will use. In this section, we will leverage these facts to prove the main results of this paper, which are the characterization of bounded operators defined via the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus.

It is clear from the definition of the
\omega
-functional calculus (
3.4
), that every
f\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, the operator
f(T)
is bounded and everywhere defined. This section investigates the question, what happens to the boundedness of the operator
f(T)
, for bounded functions
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\infty}(D_{\theta})
, defined via the unbounded
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
3.7
).

The central outcome of this section is Theorem
4.7
, which characterizes the operators
T:\operatorname{dom}(T)\subset V\to V
for which the functional calculus
f(T)
is bounded. This theorem is established in the context where
V
is a Hilbert module. To prove this theorem, we first need to establish several preparatory results.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, and
(f_{n})_{n}\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, for some
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, with

(4.1)

M:=\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}<\infty\qquad\text{and}\qquad M^{\prime}:=\sup\limits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\|f_{n}(T)\|<\infty.

Moreover, we assume that there converges

(4.2)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{n}=:f,\quad\text{uniformly on compact subsets of }D_{\theta}.

Then there is
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\infty}(D_{\theta})
, its
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus
f(T)\in\mathcal{B}(V)
is a bounded operator, and there converges

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{n}(T)v=f(T)v,\qquad v\in V.

Proof.

First of all, due to the convergence (
4.2
) and the boundedness (
4.1
), there clearly is

|f(s)|=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}|f_{n}(s)|\leq\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|f_{n}\|_{\infty}\leq M,\qquad s\in D_{\theta}.

Since the convergence (
4.2
) is uniform on compact subsets, the limit function is also holomorphic, i.e. we have proven
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\infty}(D_{\theta})
. Let us now consider the regularizer function
e(s)=\frac{s}{1+s^{2}}
, from Definition
3.5
. It can now easily be checked that

(4.3)

|e(s)|=\frac{|s|}{|1+s^{2}|}\leq\frac{1}{\cos(\theta)}\frac{|s|}{1+|s|^{2}},\qquad s\in D_{\theta}.

Since
f,(f_{n})_{n}
are bounded, there is then clearly
ef,(ef_{n})_{n}\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
. Consider now some arbitrary
0<a\leq b<\infty
and
\varphi\in(\omega,\theta)
. Then with the help of the parametrized integral (
3.5
) and the bound (
3.2
) of the resolvent operator, we can estimate the difference

\displaystyle\|(ef)

\displaystyle(T)-(ef_{n})(T)\|

0<a\leq b<\infty
, and with
e(s)=\frac{s}{1+s^{2}}
, we consider the function
(eg^{2})_{a,b}
from (
4.7
). Using the representation (
4.8
) of
(eg^{2})_{a,b}(T)
, we then estimate

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)(eg^{2})_{a,b}(T)f(T)v

\displaystyle\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg\|g(tT)\int_{(-b,-a)\cup(a,b)}(eg^{2})(\tau T)\frac{d\tau}{\tau}f(T)v\bigg\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg(\int_{(-b,-a)\cup(a,b)}\|g(tT)g(\tau T)\|\|(eg)(\tau T)f(T)v\|\frac{d\tau}{|\tau|}\bigg)^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\theta}^{2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}}{\cos^{2}(\theta)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg(\int_{(-b,-a)\cup(a,b)}\|g(tT)g(\tau T)\|\|g(\tau T)v\|\frac{d\tau}{|\tau|}\bigg)^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|},

where in the last line we commuted
g(\tau T)
and
f(T)
, which is allowed by
[
23

, and used Lemma
4.3
i) together with (
4.3
), in order to estimates
e(\tau T)f(T)
. Using now also the inequality (
4.22
) with the function
\Psi(\tau)=\|g(\tau T)v\|\mathds{1}_{(-b,-a)\cup(a,b)}(\tau)
, we furthermore get

(4.27)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)(eg^{2})_{a,b}(T)f(T)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\leq\Big(\frac{C_{\theta}^{2}C_{\beta}^{2}\pi}{2\cos(\theta)\beta^{2}}\Big)^{2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{(-b,-a)\cup(a,b)}\|g(\tau T)v\|^{2}\frac{d\tau}{|\tau|}.

Let us now choose two monotone sequences
a_{n}\rightarrow 0^{+}
,
b_{n}\rightarrow\infty
. Then by (
4.9
), there converges

(4.28)

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}g(tT)(eg^{2})_{a_{n},b_{n}}(T)f(T)v=g(tT)(eg^{2})_{0,\infty}f(T)v,\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.

In order to show that this limit converges also in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\frac{dt}{|t|})
, we analogously to (
4.27
) show that for any
m\geq n
there holds the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)

\displaystyle\big((eg^{2})_{a_{n},b_{n}}(T)-(eg^{2})_{a_{m},b_{m}}(T)\big)f(T)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{C_{\theta}^{2}C_{\beta}^{2}\pi}{2\cos(\theta)\beta^{2}}\Big)^{2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{(-b_{m},-b_{n})\cup(-a_{n},-a_{m})\cup(a_{m},a_{n})\cup(b_{n},b_{m})}\|g(\tau T)v\|^{2}\frac{d\tau}{|\tau|}.

Since

). In particular there converges the

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)(eg^{2})_{a_{n},b_{n}}(T)f(T)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}=(eg^{2})_{0,\infty}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)f(T)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}.

Performing now the limit

\displaystyle(eg^{2})_{0,\infty}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)f(T)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{C_{\theta}^{2}C_{\beta}^{2}\pi}{2\cos(\theta)\beta^{2}}\Big)^{2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{(-b_{n},-a_{n})\cup(a_{n},b_{n})}\|g(\tau T)v\|^{2}\frac{d\tau}{|\tau|}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{C_{\theta}^{2}C_{\beta}^{2}\pi}{2\cos(\theta)\beta^{2}}\Big)^{2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(\tau T)v\|^{2}\frac{d\tau}{|\tau|}.

Finally, since we assumed

g(t)\in\mathbb{R}
because

(eg^{2})_{0,\infty}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{g(t)^{2}}{1+t^{2}}dt>0,

from (

C_{g}=\frac{C_{\theta}^{2}C_{\beta}^{2}\pi}{2\cos(\theta)\beta^{2}(eg^{2})_{0,\infty}}
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\Psi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow V
be a continuous function. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a Borel measurable function
\Psi_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow V
, such that for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, there is

i)

\|\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\|=\|\Psi(t)\|
,

ii)

\|\Psi(t)\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{Sc}\langle\Psi(t),\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\rangle
.

Proof.

It is clear that for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
there is

\|\Psi(t)\|=\sup_{\|v\|=1}|\operatorname{Sc}\langle\Psi(t),v\rangle|.

Hence there exists some
v_{t}\in V
, with

(4.30)

\|v_{t}\|=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\Psi(t)\|\leq\Big(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Big)|\operatorname{Sc}\langle\Psi(t),v_{t}\rangle|.

Let us consider
I_{0}:=\big\{t\in\mathbb{R}\;\big|\;\Psi(t)=0\big\}
, and define

(4.31)

\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t):=0,\qquad t\in I_{0}.

Then it is clear that the assertions i), ii) are satisfied for every
t\in I_{0}
.

Next, consider
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{0}
. First, we can rewrite the inequality (
4.30
) as

\frac{|\operatorname{Sc}\langle\Psi(t),v_{t}\rangle|}{\|\Psi(t)\|}\geq\frac{1}{1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}}.

Since
\Psi
is continuous, also the mapping
t^{\prime}\mapsto\frac{|\operatorname{Sc}\langle\Psi(t^{\prime}),v_{t}\rangle|}{\|\Psi(t^{\prime})\|}
is continuous on the open set
\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{0}
. Hence, there exists some
\delta_{t}>0
, such that

\frac{|\operatorname{Sc}\langle\Psi(t^{\prime}),v_{t}\rangle|}{\|\Psi(t^{\prime})\|}\geq\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon},\qquad t^{\prime}\in(t-\delta_{t},t+\delta_{t}).

Now, the sets
(t-\delta_{t},t+\delta_{t})
,
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{0}
, form an open cover of
\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{0}
. Since
\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{0}
is a Lindelöf space, there exists a countable subcover

\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{0}=\bigcup\nolimits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(t_{n}-\delta_{t_{n}},t_{n}+\delta_{t_{n}}).

Setting now

I_{1}:=(t_{1}-\delta_{t_{1}},t_{1}+\delta_{t_{1}})\qquad\text{and}\qquad I_{n}:=(t_{n}-\delta_{t_{n}},t_{n}+\delta_{t_{n}})\setminus\bigcup\nolimits_{k=1}^{n-1}I_{k},\qquad n\geq 2,

we obtain a family of disjoint Borel sets, with the property

I=\biguplus\nolimits_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\Psi(t)\|\leq(1+\varepsilon)|\operatorname{Sc}\langle\Psi(t),v_{t_{n}}\rangle|,\qquad t\in I_{n}.

If we now define

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t):=\|\Psi(t)\|v_{t_{n}},\qquad t\in I_{n},

this function satisfies

\|\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\|=\|\Psi(t)\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|\Psi(t)\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)|\operatorname{Sc}\langle\Psi(t),\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\rangle|,\qquad t\in I_{n}.

If we now choose

(4.32)

\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t):=\operatorname{sgn}\big(\operatorname{Sc}\langle\Psi(t),\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\rangle\big)\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t),\qquad t\in I_{n},

this function
\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t)
satisfies both stated properties i), ii).

Altogether, since
\mathbb{R}=I_{0}\uplus\biguplus_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}
, we have defined in (
4.31
) and (
4.32
) the function
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
for every
t\in(0,\infty)
and the lemma is proven.
∎

Consider now the group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{-1,1\}
equipped with the classical multiplication of real numbers and the discrete topology, i.e. the topology where every subset of
\{-1,1\}
is open. Then the
\mathbb{Z}
-fold direct product

(4.33)

G:=\{-1,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}}=\big\{(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\;\big|\;a_{k}\in\{-1,1\}\big\}

is a compact topological group by Tychonoff’s theorem. Moreover, we denote by
\mu
the normalised Haar measure on
G
, i.e. the unique measure measure
\mu
, defined on the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
G
, with the properties

(4.34)

\mu(G)=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu(aB)=\mu(B),\qquad a\in G,\,B\subseteq G\text{ Borel set}.

The upcoming Lemma
4.6
now proves that the projectors
p_{n}:G\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
, defined by

(4.35)

p_{n}(a):=a_{n},\qquad a=(a_{k})_{k}\in G,

form an orthonormal system with respect to the Haar measure
\mu
. For the sake of completeness, we will also give a proof of this statement.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
G
be as in (
4.33
) and
\mu
the Haar measure from (
4.34
). Then the projectors (
4.35
) satisfy the orthonormality condition

(4.36)

\int_{G}p_{n}(a)p_{m}(a)d\mu(a)=\delta_{n,m},\qquad n,m\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

Let us define for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the sets

A_{n}:=\big\{a\in G\;\big|\;a_{n}=1\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad B_{n}:=\big\{a\in G\;\big|\;a_{n}=-1\big\}.

With the characteristic functions
\mathds{1}_{A_{n}}
and
\mathds{1}_{B_{n}}
of these sets, we are able to write the projector
p_{n}
as the difference

p_{n}=\mathds{1}_{A_{n}}-\mathds{1}_{B_{n}},

and consequently the product of two projectors as

p_{n}p_{m}=(\mathds{1}_{A_{n}}-\mathds{1}_{B_{n}})(\mathds{1}_{A_{m}}-\mathds{1}_{B_{m}})=\mathds{1}_{A_{n}\cap A_{m}}-\mathds{1}_{A_{n}\cap B_{m}}-\mathds{1}_{B_{n}\cap A_{m}}+\mathds{1}_{B_{n}\cap B_{m}}.

This means that the integral (
4.36
) writes as

\displaystyle\int_{G}p_{n}p_{m}d\mu

\displaystyle=\mu(A_{n}\cap A_{m})-\mu(A_{n}\cap B_{m})-\mu(B_{n}\cap A_{m})+\mu(B_{n}\cap B_{m})

\displaystyle=\mu\big((A_{n}\cap A_{m})\uplus(B_{n}\cap B_{m})\big)-\mu\big((A_{n}\cap B_{m})\uplus(B_{n}\cap A_{m})\big)

(4.37)

\displaystyle=\mu\big\{a\in G\;\big|\;a_{n}=a_{m}\big\}-\mu\big\{a\in G\;\big|\;a_{n}\neq a_{m}\big\}.

In the case
n=m
, the right hand side of (
4.37
) reduces to

\int_{G}p_{n}p_{m}d\mu=\mu\big\{a\in G\;\big|\;a_{n}=a_{n}\big\}-\mu\big\{a\in G\;\big|\;a_{n}\neq a_{n}\big\}=\mu(G)-\mu(\emptyset)=1.

In the case
n\neq m
, we choose the special element
e=(e_{k})_{k}\in G
with

e_{k}:=\begin{cases}-1,&k=m,\\
1,&k\neq m,\end{cases},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z},

and rewrite the first set on the right hand side of (
4.37
) as

\displaystyle\big\{a\in G\;\big|\;a_{n}=a_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=e\big\{e^{-1}a\;\big|\;a\in G,\,a_{n}=a_{m}\big\}=e\big\{b\in G\;\big|\;(eb)_{n}=(eb)_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=e\big\{b\in G\;\big|\;b_{n}=-b_{m}\big\}=e\big\{b\in G\;\big|\;b_{n}\neq b_{m}\big\}.

With this representation of the set and the property (
4.34
) of the Haar measure, the right hand side of (
4.37
) vanishes, as

\displaystyle\int_{G}p_{n}p_{m}d\mu

\displaystyle=\mu\big(e\big\{b\in G\;\big|\;b_{n}\neq b_{m}\big\}\big)-\mu\big\{a\in G\;\big|\;a_{n}\neq a_{m}\big\}

\displaystyle=\mu\big\{b\in G\;\big|\;b_{n}\neq b_{m}\big\}-\mu\big\{a\in G\;\big|\;a_{n}\neq a_{m}\big\}=0.\qed

Based on the preparatory results above, we are now in a position to prove our main result, which is the characterization of boundedness of the Clifford
H^{\infty}
- functional calculus.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
V
be a Hilbert module and
T\in\mathcal{K}(V)
be injective and bisectorial of angle
\omega\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
. Then for every
\theta\in(\omega,\frac{\pi}{2})
, the following statements are equivalent:

i)

There exists some
c\geq 0
, such that for every
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\infty}(D_{\theta})
there is
f(T)\in\mathcal{B}(V)
and

(4.38)

\|f(T)\|\leq c\|f\|_{\infty};

ii)

For some/each
0\neq g\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, there exist constants
c_{g},d_{g}>0
, such that

(4.39)

c_{g}\|v\|\leq\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq d_{g}\|v\|,\qquad v\in V.

iii)

For some/each
0\neq g\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, there exist constants
\widetilde{c}_{g},\widetilde{d}_{g}\geq 0
, such that

(4.40a)

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{c}_{g}\|v\|,\qquad v\in V,

(4.40b)

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT^{*})v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{d}_{g}\|v\|,\qquad v\in V.

Proof.

For the implication
i)\Rightarrow iii)
let
0\neq g\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
be arbitrary, and
v\in V
. Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is

\displaystyle\int_{(-2^{n},-2^{-n})\cup(2^{-n},2^{n})}\|g(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-n}^{n-1}\int_{(-2^{k+1},-2^{k})\cup(2^{k},-2^{k+1})}\|g(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}

(4.41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-n}^{n-1}\int_{(-2,-1)\cup(1,2)}\|g(t2^{k}T)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}.

Using the group
G
in (
4.33
), the Haar measure (
4.34
), and the projectors (
4.36
), we can use the orthonormality (
4.36
), to write the sum on the right hand side of (
4.41
) as

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=-n}^{n-1}\|g(t2^{k}T)v\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k,l=-n}^{n-1}\int_{G}p_{k}(a)p_{l}(a)d\mu(a)\operatorname{Sc}\big\langle g(t2^{k}T)v,g(t2^{l}T)v\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\operatorname{Sc}\bigg\langle\sum\limits_{k=-n}^{n-1}p_{k}(a)g(t2^{k}T)v,\sum\limits_{l=-n}^{n-1}p_{l}(a)g(t2^{l}T)v\bigg\rangle d\mu(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\bigg\|\sum\limits_{k=-n}^{n-1}p_{k}(a)g(t2^{k}T)v\bigg\|^{2}d\mu(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{G}\bigg\|\bigg(\sum\limits_{k=-n}^{n-1}p_{k}(a)g(t2^{k}\,\cdot\,)\bigg)(T)v\bigg\|^{2}d\mu(a),

where in the last equation we used the identity
g(t2^{k}T)=g(t2^{k}\,\cdot\,)(T)
from (
4.23
) as well as the linearity of the
\omega
-functional calculus. In this form we can now use the assumed boundedness of the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus (
4.38
) with the function
f(s)=\sum_{k=-n}^{n-1}p_{k}(a)g(t2^{k}s)
, to estimate

(4.42)

\sum\limits_{k=-n}^{n-1}\|g(t2^{k}T)v\|^{2}\leq c^{2}\int_{G}\bigg\|\sum\limits_{k=-n}^{n-1}p_{k}(a)g(t2^{k}\,\cdot\,)\bigg\|_{\infty}^{2}d\mu(a)\|v\|^{2}.

For every
a\in G
,
t\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
s\in D_{\theta}
, we can now estimate the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
-norm in (
4.42
) by

\bigg|\sum\limits_{k=-n}^{n-1}p_{k}(a)g(t2^{k}s)\bigg|\leq\sum\limits_{k=-n}^{n-1}|g(t2^{k}s)|\leq C_{\beta}\sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{|t2^{k}s|^{\beta}}{1+|t2^{k}s|^{2\beta}}.

Choosing now
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
|ts|\in[2^{k_{0}},2^{k_{0}+1}]
, we can split up the sum to further estimate

\displaystyle\bigg|\sum\limits_{k=-n}^{n-1}p_{k}(a)g(t2^{k}s)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq C_{\beta}\sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{-k_{0}-1}|t2^{k}s|^{\beta}+C_{\beta}\sum\limits_{k=-k_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{|t2^{k}s|^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\beta}\sum\limits_{k=-\infty}^{-k_{0}-1}(2^{k+k_{0}+1})^{\beta}+C_{\beta}\sum\limits_{k=-k_{0}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2^{k+k_{0}})^{\beta}}=\frac{2C_{\beta}}{1-2^{-\beta}}.

Since this estimate is true for every
s\in D_{\theta}
, and since
\mu(G)=1
by (
4.34
), it turns (
4.42
) into

\sum\limits_{k=-n}^{n-1}\|g(t2^{k}T)v\|^{2}\leq c^{2}\int_{G}\frac{4C_{\beta}^{2}}{(1-2^{-\beta})^{2}}d\mu(a)\|v\|^{2}=\frac{4c^{2}C_{\beta}^{2}}{(1-2^{-\beta})^{2}}\|v\|^{2}.

Plugging this now into (
4.41
), gives

\int_{(-2^{n},-2^{-n})\cup(2^{-n},2^{n})}\|g(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\leq\int_{(-2,-1)\cup(1,2)}\frac{4c^{2}C_{\beta}^{2}}{(1-2^{-\beta})^{2}}\|v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}=\frac{8\ln(2)c^{2}C_{\beta}^{2}}{(1-2^{-\beta})^{2}}\|v\|^{2}.

Since this is true for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can take the limit
n\rightarrow\infty
and obtain the first inequality (
4.40a
), namely

(4.43)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\leq\frac{8\ln(2)c^{2}C_{\beta}^{2}}{(1-2^{-\beta})^{2}}\|v\|^{2}.

For the second inequality (
4.40
), we note that by Theorem
3.7
and (
4.38
), there is

\|g(T^{*})\|=\|g(T)^{*}\|=\|g(T)\|\leq c\|g\|_{\infty}.

This means that the assumption (
4.38
) is also satisfied for the adjoint operator
T^{*}
. In the same way as for
T
in (
4.43
), we can now also derive the estimate

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT^{*})v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\leq\frac{8\ln(2)c^{2}C_{\beta}}{(1-2^{-\beta})^{2}}\|v\|^{2}.

Next, let us prove the implication
\text{“}ii)\Rightarrow i)\text{“}
, and let
0\neq g\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
be the one function for which (
4.39
) is satisfied. In a
first step
we will verify (
4.38
) for
f\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
. We know from Proposition
4.4
, that there exists some constant
C_{g}\geq 0
, such that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)f(T)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\leq C_{g}^{2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}.

Thus, with the given inequality (
4.39
) for the vector
f(T)v
, we then get

c_{g}^{2}\|f(T)v\|^{2}\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)f(T)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\leq C_{g}^{2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\leq C_{g}^{2}d_{g}^{2}\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\|v\|^{2}.

Since this estimate is true for every
v\in V
, we conclude

(4.44)

\|f(T)\|\leq\frac{C_{g}d_{g}}{c_{g}}\|f\|_{\infty},\qquad f\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta}).

In the
second step
we will extend (
4.44
) to all
f\in\mathcal{N}^{\infty}(D_{\theta})
. To do so, let us consider for every
0<\alpha\leq 1
the function

e_{\alpha}(s):=\begin{cases}\frac{s^{\alpha}}{(1+s^{2})^{\alpha}},&\text{if }\operatorname{Sc}(s)>0,\\
\frac{(-s)^{\alpha}}{(1+s^{2})^{\alpha}},&\text{if }\operatorname{Sc}(s)<0,\end{cases}\qquad s\in D_{\theta}.

Then there clearly is
e_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
, and since
f
is bounded, also
e_{\alpha}f\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
. This means, that from the already proven inequality (
4.44
), we get

(4.45)

\|(e_{\alpha}f)(T)\|\leq\frac{C_{g}d_{g}}{c_{g}}\|e_{\alpha}f\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{C_{g}d_{g}}{c_{g}\cos(\theta)}\|f\|_{\infty},

where in the second inequality, we used (
4.3
) to obtain the uniform upper bound

|e_{\alpha}(s)f(s)|\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}|s|^{\alpha}}{|1+s^{2}|^{\alpha}}\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}|s|^{\alpha}}{\cos^{\alpha}(\theta)(1+|s|^{2})^{\alpha}}\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{2^{\alpha}\cos^{\alpha}(\theta)}\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\cos(\theta)},\qquad s\in D_{\theta}.

Next, it follows from a convergence result due to Vitali
[

, Proposition 5.1.1]
, that there converges
\lim_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}e_{\alpha}(s)=1
, uniformly on compact subsets of
D_{\theta}
. This means that from Lemma
4.1
, we then conclude
f(T)\in\mathcal{B}(V)
, as well as the strong convergence

\lim\limits_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}(e_{\alpha}f)(T)v=f(T)v,\qquad v\in V.

In particular, using this convergence together with (
4.45

f(T)
satisfies the norm estimate

\|f(T)v\|=\lim\limits_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}\|(e_{\alpha}f)(T)v\|\leq\frac{C_{g}d_{g}}{c_{g}\cos(\theta)}\|f\|_{\infty}.

For the implication
\text{“}ii)\Rightarrow iii)\text{“}
let us fix
0\neq g\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
and consider some arbitrary
v\in V
. Then we know from Lemma
4.5
with
\Psi(t)=g(tT^{*})v
, that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists some
\Psi_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow V
, with

\|\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\|=\|g(tT^{*})v\|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|g(tT^{*})v\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{Sc}\big\langle g(tT^{*})v,\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\big\rangle,\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

From the assumed lower bound in (
4.39
), there then follows the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT^{*})v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\operatorname{Sc}\big\langle g(tT^{*})v,\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\big\rangle\frac{dt}{|t|}=(1+\varepsilon)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\operatorname{Sc}\big\langle v,g(tT)\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\big\rangle\frac{dt}{|t|}

(4.46)

\displaystyle=(1+\varepsilon)\operatorname{Sc}\bigg\langle v,\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(tT)\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\frac{dt}{|t|}}_{=:w}\bigg\rangle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|v\|\|w\|.

Moreover, for this vector
w
, we can now apply the inequality (
4.22
), to get an estimate between the two quadratic integrals

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(\tau T)w\|^{2}\frac{d\tau}{|\tau|}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigg\|g(\tau T)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(tT)\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg\|^{2}\frac{d\tau}{|\tau|}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{C_{\theta}C_{\beta}^{2}\pi}{2\beta^{2}}\Big)^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|\Psi_{\varepsilon}(t)\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}

(4.47)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{C_{\theta}C_{\beta}^{2}\pi}{2\beta^{2}}\Big)^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT^{*})v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}.

Applying now first the estimate (
4.47
) and then (
4.46
), gives

\displaystyle\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(\tau T)w\|^{2}\frac{d\tau}{|\tau|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT^{*})v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\theta}C_{\beta}^{2}\pi}{2\beta^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT^{*})v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)C_{\theta}C_{\beta}^{2}\pi}{2\beta^{2}}\|v\|\|w\|.

Using now also the lower bound of the quadratic estimate for
w
in (
4.40a
), then gives

\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT^{*})v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq\frac{(1+\varepsilon)C_{\theta}C_{\beta}^{2}\pi}{2\beta^{2}c_{g}}\|v\|.

Finally, for the implication
\text{“}iii)\Rightarrow ii)\text{“}
let us fix
0\neq g\in\mathcal{N}^{0}(D_{\theta})
and consider some arbitrary
v\in V
. It then follows from Theorem
4.2
, as well as the representation (
4.12
) of
g_{a,b}^{2}(T)
, that

(g^{2})_{0,\infty}v=\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\rightarrow 0^{+}\\
b\rightarrow\infty\end{subarray}}g_{a,b}^{2}(T)v=\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\rightarrow 0^{+}\\
b\rightarrow\infty\end{subarray}}\int_{(-b,-a)\cup(a,b)}g^{2}(tT)v\frac{dt}{t}.

Taking now the scalar part of the inner product of this equation with the vector
v
, then gives

\displaystyle(g^{2})_{0,\infty}\|v\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\rightarrow 0^{+}\\
b\rightarrow\infty\end{subarray}}\int_{(-b,-a)\cup(a,b)}\operatorname{Sc}\langle g^{2}(tT)v,v\rangle\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\rightarrow 0^{+}\\
b\rightarrow\infty\end{subarray}}\int_{(-b,-a)\cup(a,b)}\operatorname{Sc}\langle g(tT)v,g(tT^{*})v\rangle\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)v\|\|g(tT^{*})v\|\frac{dt}{|t|}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT^{*})v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where in the last line we used Hölder’s inequality. In this form we can now use the assumed quadratic estimate (
4.40b
), to get the stated lower bound in (
4.39
)

\frac{(g^{2})_{0,\infty}}{\widetilde{d}_{g}}\|v\|\leq\bigg(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\|g(tT)v\|^{2}\frac{dt}{|t|}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}.\qed

5.
Concluding remarks on applications and research directions

Unlike complex holomorphic function theory, the noncommutative setting of the Clifford algebra allows multiple notions of hyperholomorphicity for vector fields. Consequently, different spectral theories emerge, each based on the concept of spectrum that relies on distinct Cauchy kernels. The spectral theory based on the
S
-spectrum was inspired by quaternionic quantum mechanics (see
[
11
]
), is associated with slice hyperholomorphicity, and began its development in 2006. A comprehensive introduction can be found in
[
20
]
, with further explorations done in
[
4
,
5
,
28
]
. Applications on fractional powers of operators are investigated in
[
16
,
17
,
19
,
39
,
40
]
and some results from classical interpolation theory, see
[
9
,
12
,
48
,
54
]
, have been recently extended into this setting
[
30
]
. In order to fully appreciate the spectral theory on the
S
-spectrum, we recall that it applies to sets of noncommuting operators, for example with the identification
(T_{1},...,T_{2^{n}})\leftrightarrow T=\sum_{A}T_{A}e_{A}
it can be seen as a theory for several operators. Extending complex spectral theory to vector operators broadens its applicability to various areas of research fields such as:

Quaternionic formulation of quantum mechanics.
The interest in spectral theory for quaternionic operators is motivated by the 1936 paper
[
11
]
on the logic of quantum mechanics by G. Birkhoff, J. von Neumann. There, the authors showed that Schrödinger equation can be written basically in the complex or in the quaternionic setting, see also the book of Adler
[
1
]
.

Vector analysis.
The gradient operator with nonconstant coefficients in
n
dimensions, represents various physical laws, such as Fourier’s law for heat propagation and Fick’s law for mass transfer diffusion. This can be expressed as

T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{i}(x)\partial_{x_{i}},\qquad x\in\Omega,

and is associated with various boundary conditions, as discussed in
[
23
]
. Here,
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
represent the imaginary units of the Clifford algebra
\mathbb{R}_{n}
, and the coefficients
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are assumed to belong to
C^{1}(\overline{\Omega})
and satisfy appropriate bounds.

Differential geometry.
Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian manifold of dimension
n
, and
U
a coordinate neighbourhood of
M
. In this neighbourhood, we can find an orthonormal frame of vector fields
E_{1},\dots,E_{n}
, such that the Dirac operator on
(U,g)
can be expressed as the
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(U,\mathcal{H})
differential operator

(5.1)

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\nabla_{E_{i}}^{\tau},

where
\nabla_{E_{i}}^{\tau}
denotes the covariant derivative with respect to the vector field
E_{i}
. For more details see the book
[
41
]
. As a special case the paper
[
10
]
considers the Dirac operator in hyperbolic and spherical spaces, where (
5.1
) takes the explicit forms

\mathcal{D}_{H}=x_{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}-\frac{n-1}{2}e_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{D}_{S}=(1+|x|^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}-nx,

where for
\mathcal{D}_{H}
we pick
(x_{1},...,x_{n-1},x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
(x_{1},...,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
\mathcal{D}_{S}
, respectively.

Hypercomplex analysis.
The well-known Dirac operator and its conjugate

D=\partial_{x_{0}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{D}=\partial_{x_{0}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\partial_{x_{i}},

are widely investigated in
[
32
]
as well as in
[
41
]
. Slice hyperholomorphic functions can also be viewed as functions in the kernel of the global operator

G=|\operatorname{Im}(x)|^{2}\partial_{x_{0}}+\operatorname{Im}(x)\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}},

introduced in
[
18
]
, where
\operatorname{Im}(x)=x_{1}e_{1}+\dots+x_{n}e_{n}
. For integers
\alpha,\beta,m
, the operators of the Dirac fine structure on the
S
-spectrum are defined as

T_{\alpha,m}=D^{\alpha}(D\overline{D})^{m}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{T}_{\beta,m}=\overline{D}^{\beta}(D\overline{D})^{m}.

The fine structures on the
S
-spectrum constitute a set of function spaces and their related functional calculi. These structures have been introduced and studied in recent works
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
33
,
34
]
, while their respective
H^{\infty}
-versions are investigated in
[
26
,
35
]
.

The
H^{\infty}
-functional calculi also have a broader context in the recently introduced fine structures on the
S
-spectrum. These are function spaces of nonholomorphic functions derived from the Fueter-Sce extension theorem, which connects slice hyperholomorphic and axially monogenic functions, the two main notions of holomorphicity in the Clifford setting. The connection is established through powers of the Laplace operator in a higher dimension, see
[
38
,
52
,
53
]
and also the translation
[
27
]
.

It is important to highlight that in the hypercomplex setting there exists another spectral theory based on the monogenic spectrum, which was initiated by Jefferies, McIntosh and J. Picton-Warlow in
[
47
]
. This theory is founded on monogenic functions, which are functions in the kernel of the Dirac operator, and their associated Cauchy formula (see
[
32
]
for a comprehensive treatment). The monogenic spectral theory, based on monogenic functions
[
32
]
, has been extensively developed and is well described in the seminal books
[
42
]
and
[
50
]
. These texts provide an in-depth exploration of the theory, with particular emphasis on the
H^{\infty}
-functional calculus in the monogenic setting.
