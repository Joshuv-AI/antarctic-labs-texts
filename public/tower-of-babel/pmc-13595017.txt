1 | Single‐Dose Intravenous Iron vs Oral Iron for Postpartum Anemia: A Multi‐Center Randomized Controlled Trial (PRIORITY)

Rupsa C. Boelig1; Mrityunjay C. C. Metgud2; Shivaprasad S. S. Goudar3; Elizabeth M. M. McClure4; Denise Babineau5; Janet L. Moore4; Richard Derman6 On behalf of PRIORITY Trial Group

1Sidney Kimmel Medical College at Thomas Jefferson University, Philadelphia, PA; 2Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College, KLE Academy of Higher Education and Research (KAHER), Belagavi, India; 3Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College, Belagavi, India; 4RTI International, Research Triangle Park, NC; 5RTI International, Chapel Hill, NC; 6Thomas Jefferson University, Philadelphia, PA

Objective: Anemia remains a persistent contributor to maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide. Postpartum (pp) anemia impacts future reproductive health. We aimed to determine whether a single infusion of intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (IV FCM) up to 1 g is superior to 60 mg oral iron twice daily for treatment of pp anemia.

Study Design: This is a parallel two‐arm multi‐center randomized controlled trial in 4800 mothers at 8 international sites in the NICHD Global Health Network for Women's and Children's Health Research (NCT05590260). Eligible mothers were screened for moderate anemia (hemoglobin [Hb] 7.0–9.9 g/dL) at 6–48 hours pp and randomized to either IV FCM or oral iron. Primary outcome was Hb ≥ 11.0 g/dL at 6 weeks pp. The trial was powered to detect ≥10% increase in the risk of achieving the primary outcome overall and within South Asian and sub‐Saharan African populations. Key secondary outcomes were analyzed in hierarchical manner: Hb change and pp depression (Edinburgh pp depression scale >10) at 6 weeks; Hb change and PPD at 6 months. All analyses were intention to treat, and relative risks (RR)/mean differences (MD) were adjusted for site.

Results: 4857 participants were randomized: 2428 to IV FCM and 2429 to oral iron. Baseline characteristics were similar (Table). Those randomized to IV FCM were significantly more likely to achieve Hb ≥ 11.0 g/dL at 6 weeks overall (RR 1.25 [1.20, 1.29]), and regionally (South Asia RR 1.20 [1.14, 1.25], sub‐Saharan Africa RR 1.37 [1.29, 1.46]). IV FCM resulted in significantly greater improvement in Hb at 6 weeks (MD 0.69 [0.60,0.77]). This persisted at 6 months (MD 0.45 [0.36,0.54]) (Figure), although statistical significance cannot be concluded due to hierarchical testing. There was no difference in the risk of PPD at 6 weeks (RR 0.87 [0.64–1.19]) or 6 months (RR 1.06 [0.73–1.54]).

Conclusion: IV FCM is superior to oral iron in achieving a non‐anemic state and improving Hb at 6 weeks. Use of IV FCM in the postpartum period can rapidly address maternal anemia with lasting effects that have important implications for future reproductive health.

2 | Is Alternate Day Oral Iron as Effective as Daily Iron at Treating Anemia in Pregnancy?

Fiona E. O'Toole1; Fionnuala M. M. McAuliffe2; Grace Mealy3; Marie Galligan4; Joan Fitzgerald1; Jennifer Walsh1 On behalf of UCD Clinical Research Centre

1National Maternity Hospital, National Maternity Hospital, Ireland; 2University College Dublin, Dublin, Ireland; 3UCD Perinatal Research Centre, School of Medicine, University College Dublin; The National Maternity Hospital, Dublin, Ireland; 4University College Dublin Clinical Research Centre (UCD CRC), Dublin, Ireland

Objective: Recent studies have demonstrated better fractional and total iron absorption with alternate day dosing of ferrous salts in non‐pregnant iron deficient and anemic cohorts. The objective of this trial was to determine if alternate day ferrous fumarate was non‐inferior to daily dosing for the treatment of newly confirmed iron deficiency anemia in pregnancy.

Study Design: This was an open label phase IV randomized controlled trial, where healthy women with singleton pregnancies, a hemoglobin of <10.5 g/dL and a ferritin of <30 µg/L between 14–34 weeks gestation were randomized to 305 mg ferrous fumarate (=100 mg elemental iron) with either alternate day or daily dosing for a treatment period of 4 weeks. The primary outcome was the difference in hemoglobin rise after 4 weeks of treatment, sample size of 184 (92 in each arm) was powered at 90% level to detect a difference between the groups of less than 0.4 g/dL to demonstrate non‐inferiority.

Results: A total of 203 women were randomised and primary outcome data was available for 192. Regression analysis considering the haemoglobin (Hb) and gestational age at baseline showed that the alternate day group had 0.35 g/dL (CI −0.16 to −0.53) less rise compared to the daily group after 4 weeks treatment (p = <0.001), mean Hb 10.34 g/dL compared with 10.61 g/dL. Per protocol analysis with participants who were ≥80% compliant showed 0.43 g/dL less Hb rise in the intervention arm compared to the control CI (–0.62 to −0.24) p = <0.001. Estimated blood loss at delivery was significantly higher in the alternate day group compared to the daily group (Median 440 mL vs. 365 mL; p = 0.013). There were no significant differences in the incidence of any reported side‐effects between the two dosing regimens (97%% vs 96% p = 0.7).

Conclusion: Non‐inferiority could not be supported by this trial. The results favor daily iron for rapid management with good compliance further enhancing this effect.

3 | Impact of 2024 World Health Organization Hemoglobin Altitude Adjustment on Postpartum Anemia Rates in Guatemala

1Instituto de Nutrición de Centro América y Panamá, Guatemala City, Guatemala; 2Instituto de Nutrición de Centro América y Panam, Guatemala City, Guatemala; 3University of Colorado, Denver, CO; 4RTI International, Research Triangle Park, NC; 5Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College, KLE Academy of Higher Education and Research (KAHER), Belagavi, India; 6Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College, Belagavi, India; 7Kinshasa School of Public Health, Lemba, Congo, (Congo–Kinshasa); 8University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, NC; 9University Teaching Hospital–Children's Hospital, Lusaka, Zambia; 10University of Alabama at Birmingham, Birmingham, AL; 11Lata Medical Research Foundation, Nagpur, India; 12Boston University, Boston, MA; 13The Aga Khan University, Karachi, Pakistan; 14Columbia University School of Medicine, New York, NY; 15International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Dhaka, Bangladesh; 16University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA; 17Moi University School of Medicine, Eldoret, Kenya; 18University of Indiana, Indianapolis, IN; 19Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development, Bethesda, MD; 20Thomas Jefferson University, Philadelphia, PA; 21Sidney Kimmel Medical College at Thomas Jefferson University, Philadelphia, PA

Objective: In 2011 the WHO recommended adjusting hemoglobin for altitude to avoid misdiagnosis of anemia or polycythemia in these populations. In 2024, the WHO updated its calculated adjustment formula. We aimed to determine how this update impacted rates of anemia in a postpartum population in Guatemala, as this has significant public health implications.

Study Design: This is a secondary analysis of postpartum patients in Guatemala screened for inclusion in the PRIORITY randomized trial for management of postpartum anemia. Patients delivering at > = 28‐week gestation were eligible. Those with known hemoglobinopathy, recent intravenous iron, or need for transfusion were excluded. We compared rates of moderate or severe anemia (Hb < 10.0 g/dL) based on WHO adjustment in 2024 vs the WHO 2011. Subject specific risk ratios (RR and 95% CIs) were estimated using a generalized linear mixed model with a Poisson distribution and log link, including a fixed effect for altitude adjustment scenario and a random intercept to account for repeated classifications within participants.

Results: Among 2493 screened patients (Table 1), applying the WHO 2024 adjustment resulted in significantly increased rates of moderate/severe anemia (RR 1.71 (1.59–1.84)) compared to the WHO 2011 adjustment. With each adjustment there was progressively higher rates of anemia compared to unadjusted. Applying the WHO 2011 altitude adjustment (−0.5 g/dL), increased the estimated risk of moderate/severe anemia by 69% (RR 1.69 (1.54, 1.85)), and the 2024 altitude adjustment (−1.1 g/dL), increased the estimated risk almost three‐fold (RR = 2.88 (2.56, 3.24)) (Figure 1).

Conclusion: The WHO 2024 altitude adjustment resulted in significantly higher rates of diagnosis of moderate or severe anemia compared to the 2011 adjustment. Moderate/severe anemia has likely been under‐diagnosed and ‐managed in higher altitude populations. Our findings support the importance of implementing the 2024 WHO altitude adjustment.

1University Teaching Hospital–Children's Hospital, Lusaka, Zambia; 2University Teaching Hospital, Lusaka, Zambia; 3University of Alabama at Birmingham, Birmingham, AL; 4RTI International, Research Triangle Park, NC; 5Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College, KLE Academy of Higher Education and Research (KAHER), Belagavi, India; 6Jawaharlal Nehru Medical College, Belagavi, India; 7Kinshasa School of Public Health, Lemba, Congo, (Congo–Kinshasa); 8University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill, NC; 9The Aga Khan University, Karachi, Pakistan; 10Columbia University School of Medicine, New York, NY; 11International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Dhaka, Bangladesh; 12University of Virginia, Charlottesville, VA; 13Instituto de Nutrición de Centro América y Panamá, Guatemala City, Guatemala; 14University of Colorado, Denver, CO; 15Lata Medical Research Foundation, Nagpur, India; 16Boston University, Boston, MA; 17Moi University School of Medicine, Eldoret, Kenya; 18University of Indiana, Indianapolis, IN; 19Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development, Bethesda, MD; 20Thomas Jefferson University, Philadelphia, PA; 21Sidney Kimmel Medical College at Thomas Jefferson University, Philadelphia, PA

Objective: Postpartum anemia affects an estimated 30–80% in low/middle income countries (LMIC), but population based studies are limited by lack of standardized screening protocols. We aimed to compare the rates of postpartum anemia based on hemoglobin (Hb) screening performed 6–48 hr postpartum (PP) across eight sites in seven LMICs.

Study Design: This is a secondary analysis of postpartum patients at eight trial sites in seven low middle income countries screened for inclusion for a randomized trial (PRIORITY). Eligible participants were age 14–49 years and delivering at least 28 weeks gestation. Those with known hemoglobinopathy, recent use of intravenous iron, or need for transfusion were excluded from Hb screening. Eligible patients were screened for inclusion between 6–48 hrs postpartum. Rates of non‐anemia, or mild, moderate, severe anemia (Hb ≥11.0, 10.0–10.9, 7.0–9.9, <7.0 respectively) were compared by site and region. Estimated risks and risk ratios (95% CIs) of moderate/severe anemia for each site were obtained by fitting a Poisson regression model with a log link and robust variance estimation including fixed effects for site, maternal age, and gestational age at delivery.

Results: Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. Overall 25.9% of screened patients had moderate/severe anemia. Adjusted risk of anemia (Hb < 10.0 g/dL) was higher in Africa than Asia (35.9% vs 18.9%; RR 1.90, 95% CI 1.80–2.01). Zambia had the highest adjusted risk (81.6%) which was a six‐fold higher compared to Belagavi, India (12.9%; RR 6.33, 95% CI 5.89–6.80) (Figure 1A). Exclusion due to prior intravenous iron treatment was more common in Asia compared to Latin America or Africa (Figure 1B).

Conclusion: In this contemporary postpartum global cohort, we identified that rates of postpartum anemia vary substantially across region and sites. Exploring the impact of antenatal anemia management protocols as well as environmental factors contribute to these differences is essential for improving postpartum anemia prevention and care globally.

Objective: Postpartum (PP) anemia affect 30–70% of individuals. in low/middle income countries (LMICs), yet optimal timing of PP hemoglobin (Hb) screening is lacking, and the not well definied. We compared anemia rates when Hb was assessed at 6–12h, 12–24 hr, or 24–48 hr postpartum to inform screening protocols for postpartum anemia.

Study Design: This secondary analysis included postpartum patients screened for the PRIORITY randomized trial at eight sites in seven LMICs. Patients aged 14–49, delivering at least 28‐week gestation were eligible. Those with known hemoglobinopathy, recent intravenous iron, or need for transfusion were excluded. Eligible patients were screened between 6–48 hrs PP. Exact timing of the Hb assessment depended on study team availability rather than pre‐specified protocol. Adjusted marginal means and mean differences of Hb across postpartum blood collection periods were estimated using linear regression with robust standard errors, including fixed effects for site, maternal age and gestational age at delivery. Effect modification by site and region was assessed with an interaction term. Data presented with 95% confidence intervals.

Results: Baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. Adjusted mean Hb was significantly lower at 12–24 hours (MD −0.15 g/dL (–0.21 to −0.09)) and 24–48 hours (MD −0.27 g/dL(–0.34 to −0.21) compared with 6–12 hours postpartum. Rates of moderate or severe anemia (Hb < 10.0 g/dL) were higher when assessed 12–24 hr or 24–48 hr vs 6–12 hr PP (RR 1.10 (1.04–1.17) and 1.18 (1.11, 1.26) respectively) (Figure 1A–C). Timing effects did not differ by region (p = 0.299) but did vary across sites (p < 0.001).

Conclusion: Hemoglobin levels were lower and the detection of anemia was higher when screening was performed 12–48 hours postpartum compared with 6–12 hr. Screening too early may underestimate the true burden of postpartum anemia and lead to missed treatment opportunities.

6 | Systemwide Initiative to Improve Iron Deficiency Identification and Treatment in Pregnancy

Colby D. Mallett1; Juliana Peña Hofmann2; Chloe Lessard3; Uma Kelavkar1; Lauren Hennis2; Hunter Sharp2; Megan D. Whitham2

1Virginia Tech Carilion School of Medicine, Roanoke, VA; 2Carilion Clinic, Roanoke, VA; 3Kaiser Permanente, Santa Clara, CA

Objective: Iron deficiency anemia is associated with adverse maternal outcomes and remains under‐recognized and undertreated in pregnancy. This study evaluates the impact of a systemwide iron deficiency anemia protocol, integrating reflex ferritin testing and structured intravenous iron workflows on detection, treatment, and maternal hematologic outcomes in pregnancy.

Study Design: We conducted a pre and post quality improvement evaluation across a multisite obstetric service line. The pre period included 2691 deliveries and the post period included 1020 deliveries. The primary process measure was ferritin testing among patients with hemoglobin <11 g/dL. Additional measures included iron treatment among patients with ferritin less than <30 ng/mL, prenatal intravenous iron use, hemoglobin at admission for delivery and postpartum, and anemia during pregnancy. Multivariable regression adjusted for demographic and obstetric covariates. Monthly interrupted time series analyses assessed implementation milestones.

Results: Ferritin testing among patients with hemoglobin <11 g/dL increased from 12.7% to 44.0% (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] 4.81, 95% CI 3.68 to 6.30). Iron treatment among patients with ferritin <30 ng/mL increased from 36.7% to 60.4% (AOR 2.46, 95% CI 1.49 to 4.13). Prenatal intravenous iron use increased from 11.0% to 18.2% (AOR 1.96, 95% CI 1.03 to 3.78). The proportion of deliveries complicated by anemia during pregnancy decreased from 40.6% to 25.4% (AOR 0.46, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.56). Postpartum hemoglobin increased modestly (adjusted mean difference 0.22 g per dL, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.43).

Conclusion: Systemwide integration of reflex ferritin testing and structured intravenous iron pathways improved iron deficiency detection, increased treatment uptake, and reduced anemia during pregnancy across a multisite obstetric population.
