Therapeutic Outcomes of Shelf-Stable, Cryopreserved Amniotic Membrane in Recalcitrant Keratopathy
Authors: Milanka Stevanovic, Gina Ciarmiello, Robert Eden, Robert Schultze
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeKeratopathy can be caused by multiple factors including neuropathy, inflammation, radiation, among others. Amniotic membrane (AM) is known to have anti-inflammatory and anti-scarring properties, which helps promote epithelialization. Herein, we assessed application of AM to promote epithelialization in the treatment of recalcitrant keratopathy.Patients and MethodsA single-center, retrospective review was performed of consecutive patients with recalcitrant keratopathy that failed to respond to conservative treatment who were subsequently treated with shelf-stable cryopreserved AM. Corneal staining, corneal sensitivity, tear osmolarity, uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA), and complications were assessed before treatment as well as one-week, one-month, and three-months post-treatment.ResultsA total of 34 eyes of 18 subjects were included in the analysis. All eyes were diagnosed with recalcitrant keratopathy (n=34), of which 7 (20.6%) eyes had reduced corneal sensation. Eyes were treated with shelf-stable AM for an average of 2.0 ± 0.6 days. Corneal staining scores significantly improved from 1.52 ± 0.68 at baseline to 0.50 ± 0.85 at 1 week (p=0.01), 0.25 ± 0.43 at 1 month (p<0.001), and 0.47 ± 0.91 at 3 months (p=0.007). The percentage of eyes with no corneal staining was 70.0% at 1-week, 72.7% at 1-month, and 73.7% at 3-months. Corneal sensitivity improved from 2.3 ± 1.4 at baseline to 1.6 ± 0.9 at 1 week (p=0.31), 1.38 ± 0.7 at 1 month (p<0.001), and 1.11 ± 0.32 at 3 months (p=0.002). UCVA changed from 0.24 ± 0.26 at baseline to 0.14 ± 0.16 at 1 week (p=0.41), 0.18 ± 0.23 at 1 month (p=0.013), and 0.11 ± 0.08 at 3 months (p=0.08). Tear osmolarity did not significantly change from baseline at any timepoint. No complications or adverse events were noted.ConclusionShelf-stable cryopreserved AM transplantation led to a significant improvement in corneal epithelization, corneal sensitivity, and visual acuity in patients with recalcitrant keratopathy.Keywords: amniotic membrane, cryopreservation, dry eye disease, neurotrophic keratopathy, keratopathy, superficial punctate keratopathy

Introduction

Keratopathy, which is characterized by damage to the corneal epithelium, can be caused by a variety of factors, including autoimmune diseases (eg Stevens-Johnson syndrome or ocular cicatricial pemphigoid), infections (eg, herpetic keratitis), chronic inflammation, exposure, or complications from contact lens wear and ocular surgeries. Conservative treatment often includes patient education, lifestyle and dietary modifications, preservative-free lubricants, and topical immunomodulators depending on the etiology.1 However, some patients may not notice relief for many weeks. In one study, onset of relief was only noted by 32% of keratoconjunctivitis sicca patients within 1 week and by 73% of patients within 3 weeks of treatment with topical cyclosporine A.2 Hence, a subset of patients are non-responsive to treatment, and these recalcitrant keratopathy cases may persist for weeks or months, often requiring long-term management and close monitoring.

Neurotrophic keratopathy is a major cause of recalcitrant keratopathy due to its underlying mechanism of impaired corneal innervation.3 Damage to the trigeminal nerve, which supplies sensory innervation to the cornea, leads to reduced or absent corneal sensitivity. This sensory loss disrupts the normal protective reflexes, most notably the blink reflex, which is essential for even tear distribution and preventing excessive exposure of the ocular surface.4 Moreover, corneal nerves play a crucial trophic role—they release neuropeptides and growth factors that promote epithelial cell proliferation and differentiation.5 As a result, neurotrophic keratopathy often manifests as a chronic condition that is highly resistant to conventional therapies.

For such cases of recalcitrant keratopathy, amniotic membrane (AM) has potential to deliver biological components to help jump start the healing process and provide anti-inflammatory and anti-scarring properties.6 AM treatment, particularly with the self-retained, sutureless cryopreserved form (Prokera®; BioTissue Holdings Inc, Miami, FL), has been shown to provide rapid and sustained benefits in recalcitrant keratopathy.7,8 In the multi-center DREAM study involving patients with moderate to severe dry eye,8 placement of cryopreserved AM for an average of 4.9 ± 1.8 days resulted in significant improvements in ocular surface health within one week, which were sustained at both one and three months.8 Additionally, studies have shown that improvement in both signs and symptoms of dry eye following cryopreserved AM treatment is associated with corneal nerve regeneration at one and three months.7 These findings suggest that cryopreserved AM not only accelerates healing in recalcitrant cases within the first week but also promotes long-term ocular surface stability over several months.

Recently, a shelf-stable cryopreserved AM has been developed to optimize patient comfort. Shelf-stable cryopreserved AM can be applied to the ocular surface and secured with either a bandage contact lens or a collagen shield. Importantly, shelf-stable cryopreserved AM–unlike dehydrated or lyophilized AM–retains key biological components such as HC-HA/PTX3 and preserves the anti-inflammatory properties, as evidenced by its ability to reduce inflammatory mediators secreted by activated pro-inflammatory macrophages.9 The preserved biological composition and activity of shelf-stable cryopreserved AM suggests that the clinical benefits may extend to patients with mild-to-moderate recalcitrant keratopathy, similar to those reported with self-retained, sutureless cryopreserved AM in more moderate-to-severe cases. In this study, we evaluated the use of the new shelf-stable cryopreserved AM for the treatment of recalcitrant keratopathy to assess the change in corneal signs (corneal staining, corneal sensitivity, tear osmolarity, uncorrected visual acuity) at early (1-week) and longer term (3-months) timepoints.

Materials and Methods

A single-center retrospective chart review was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki on consecutive patients with recalcitrant keratopathy that failed to respond to conservative treatment who were subsequently treated with cryopreserved AM. The study was reviewed and deemed exempt per 45CFR 46.101(b) by Sterling Institutional Review Board (Atlanta, GA). Appropriate measures were undertaken and maintained to protect the confidentiality of study participants. Subjects were included in the study if they had dry eye disease with corneal staining, received treatment with shelf-stable cryopreserved AM between September 2024 and March 2025, and had follow-up data on corneal epithelialization status. Medical histories and results from comprehensive ophthalmic examinations were reviewed, and de-identified data was collected related to demographics, diagnosis, prior therapies, concomitant therapies, corneal staining, corneal sensitivity, tear osmolarity, uncorrected visual acuity, complications, and adverse events.

The AM utilized for treatment in our institution was shelf-stable cryopreserved AM (CAM360 AmnioGraft®; BioTissue Holdings Inc, Miami, FL). Shelf-stable cryopreserved AM was applied as follows: forceps were used to remove the AM from its packaging, and a cellulose eye sponge (Weck-cel; Beaver Visitec International, Waltham, MA) was used to blot the tissue surface and remove excess storage solution to reduce slippage. The AM was then placed on the ocular surface with the gridded side of the backing paper facing the eye. Using the sponge and forceps, the tissue backing paper was carefully removed, and the AM was smoothed over the cornea. A bandage contact lens (Air Optix Night & Day; Alcon, Geneva, Switzerland) or a 72-hour dissolvable collagen shield (Oasis Medical, Glendale, CA) was placed over the AM, and the eyelids were partially taped shut (Nictavi, Los Angeles, CA). Patients were instructed to remove the tape after two to three days.

Patients generally returned at 1-week, 1-month, and 3-months after AM application. At the visits, slit-lamp examination with fluorescein staining was performed, and the severity of corneal staining was recorded as 0 (no staining), trace (minimal staining), 1+ (mild staining), 2+ (moderate staining), or 3+ (severe staining). Corneal sensitivity was performed as previously described using a non-contact esthesiometer (Brill; Brill Engines, Barcelona, Spain).10 The esthesiometer produces pulses of air at five different intensities (approximately 2 to 10 mbar): level 1 (regular), 2 (regular), 3 (regular), 4 (hyposensitivity), and 5 (severe hyposensitivity). The device was aimed at the central cornea, and the lowest level that the patient could sense the air puff at was recorded. Reduced corneal sensitivity was defined as a level 4 reading or higher. Tear osmolarity was assessed using the TearLab Osmolarity System (Trukera Medical; Southlake, TX) immediately after blinking according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with a tear sample obtained directly from the tear film meniscus in the central portion of the temporal third of the lower eyelid. Tear osmolarity was defined as normal if ≤308 mOsm/L and abnormal if >308 mOsm/L.11 Complications, adverse events, and uncorrected visual acuity were also assessed.

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.0 (IBM; Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous data were reported as mean ± standard deviation and categorical data were described using frequencies and percentages. The Wilcoxon Signed Ranks Test, or the non-parametric related samples t-test, was used to assess continuous variables across timepoints. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 34 eyes (17 OD, 17 OS) of 18 subjects (18 females) with an average age of 60.5 ± 10.1 years met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis. Based on medical history and ocular examination, recalcitrant keratopathy was caused by dry eye disease in all 34 eyes, of which 4 (11.8%) eyes had Sjogren’s syndrome, 2 (5.9%) eyes had anterior basement membrane dystrophy, 2 (5.9%) eyes had filamentary keratitis, and 1 (2.9%) eye had recurrent corneal erosions. Thirteen (38.2%) eyes were noted to be post-Laser-Assisted In Situ Keratomileusis (LASIK). At presentation, all patients had corneal staining despite prior treatments with punctal plugs (n=27, 79.4%), cyclosporine (n=14, 41.2%), lifitegrast ophthalmic solution (n=12, 35.3%), topical steroids (n=4, 11.8%), and artificial tears (n=2, 5.9%). The patient’s average corneal staining score was 1.52 ± 0.68 (mild superficial punctate keratopathy), tear osmolarity was 303.8 ± 11.9 mOsm/L (mild abnormality), corneal sensitivity score was 2.4 ± 1.3 (within normal specification), and visual acuity was 0.24 ± 0.26 logMAR. Reduced corneal sensitivity was observed in 7 eyes (20.6%), and abnormal tear osmolarity (>308 mOsm/L) was observed in 61.8% of eyes.

Eyes were treated with shelf-stable cryopreserved AM for an average of 2.0 ± 0.6 days. The shelf-stable cryopreserved AM was retained on the ocular surface with a collagen shield in 26 cases (76.5%) and a bandage contact lens in 8 cases (23.5%). Concomitant treatment included cyclosporine (n=17, 50.0%), artificial tears (n=15, 44.1%) and lubricating drops (n=12, 35.3%).

At the 1-week visit (6.1 ± 5.5 days after AM placement), corneal staining scores significantly improved from 1.52 ± 0.68 at baseline to 0.50 ± 0.85 (p=0.011) (Figure 1). The percentage of eyes with no corneal staining was 70.0%. Corneal sensitivity improved from 2.3 ± 1.4 at baseline to 1.6 ± 0.9 at 1 week (p=0.31). Visual acuity slightly improved from 0.24 ± 0.26 logMAR at baseline to 0.14 ± 0.16 logMAR (p=0.41), and tear osmolarity digressed from 303.8 ± 11.9 mOsm/L at baseline to 312.8 ± 16.8 mOsm/L (p=0.07).

Representative case. 60-year-old female presented with chronic (>3 years) dry eye (A) despite treatment with lifitegrast, cyclosporine, serum tears, punctal plugs and topical steroids (only provided short-term relief). AM was placed, which resulted in significant improvement in corneal staining and symptoms at 2 days post-treatment (B).

At the 1-month visit (1.2 ± 0.3 months post-AM placement), the average corneal staining score significantly improved to 0.25 ± 0.43 (p=0.001), corneal sensitivity significantly improved to 1.38 ± 0.7 (p<0.001), and visual acuity significantly improved to 0.18 ± 0.23 logMAR (p=0.013) compared to baseline. The average tear osmolarity was 306.2 ± 9.3 mOsm/L (p=0.23).

At the 3-month visit (3.8 ± 1.3 months post-AM placement), 11 patients (21 eyes) returned for follow-up. The average corneal staining score was 0.47 ± 0.91 (p=0.007 compared to baseline). The average corneal sensitivity score was 1.11 ± 0.32 (p=0.002 compared to baseline), tear osmolarity was 312.0 ± 20.4 mOsm/L (p=0.67), and visual acuity was 0.11 ± 0.08 logMAR (p=0.08).

Collectively, the percentage of eyes with no corneal staining was 0% at baseline, 70.0% at 1-week, 72.7% at 1-month, and 73.7% at 3-months. The percentage of eyes with normal corneal sensitivity was 79.4% at baseline, 90.9% at 1-week, 100% at 1-month and 100% at 3-months. Regarding eyes with reduced corneal sensitivity at baseline, the percentage of eyes with normal sensitivity was 0% at baseline, 88.8% at 1-month, and 100% at 3-months. The percentage of eyes with normal tear osmolarity scores of ≤308 mOsm/L was 38.2% at baseline, 40.0% at 1-week, 52.0% at 1-month, and 52.6% at 3-months (Table 1). No complications or adverse events were noted in the treatment.

Discussion

Dry eye disease is increasingly recognized as a complex, multifactorial disease in which tear film instability, ocular surface inflammation, and neurosensory abnormalities play etiological roles.12 If unaddressed, this interplay between the ocular surface, tear film, and sensory nerves can contribute to escalating and persisting symptoms. For example, dysfunction or degeneration of corneal nerves can lead to reduced trophic supply to corneal epithelial cells and a decrease in afferent signaling to the brain leading to loss of ocular surface homeostasis.5 This can result in reduced tear secretion, decreased blinking rate, and ocular surface inflammation.13

Many therapies, such as topical immunomodulator agents or artificial tears, can take several weeks before meaningful clinical improvement is observed, as they often only address one underlying factor of the disease process. For patients with more advanced disease, particularly those with recalcitrant or slow-resolving corneal disorders, more aggressive interventions may be necessary. In these cases, AM treatment may be an effective option, providing anti-inflammatory, anti-scarring, and regenerative properties that help restore the ocular surface and promote epithelial healing.4,6

Our findings demonstrate that AM therapy provided objective improvements in patients with recalcitrant keratopathy secondary to dry eye disease. Corneal staining significantly improved within 1 week and was sustained for at least 3 months. This was accompanied with a significant improvement in uncorrected visual acuity at 1 month. Additionally, corneal sensitivity significantly improved at 1 and 3 months, and all eyes with reduced corneal sensitivity had normal sensation. Further studies are warranted to determine whether this improvement correlates with increased blink rate, tear production, and reduced inflammation. Interestingly, despite the significant improvement in corneal staining and sensitivity, the tear osmolarity was not noted to similarly improve. Previous studies have reported no clinically significant relationships between tear osmolarity and corneal fluorescein staining, tear breakup time or dry eye symptoms.14,15 This may be because the osmolarity of tears sampled from the tear meniscus differs from the localized environment over the cornea and from the sites where other outcome measures are obtained. The severity of meibomian gland dysfunction was also not measured in our study, which may have an effect on tear osmolarity.16,17

Our results are consistent with prior studies using cold-storage cryopreserved AM (CAM). John et al7 showed treatment with cold-storage CAM for 3.4 ± 0.7 days led to a significant increase in corneal sensitivity at 1- and 3-months and was accompanied with a significant improvement in ocular signs and symptoms of moderate to severe dry eye disease. Furthermore, McDonald et al8 showed cold-storage CAM applied on the ocular surface for 2 to 7 days significantly improved signs and symptoms of moderate-to-severe dry eye at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months, with no significant difference observed between treatment duration.18 We similarly observed improvements in corneal integrity, sensitivity, and visual acuity following treatment with cold-storage CAM at 1-week, 1-month, and 3-months. Our study further demonstrates that shelf-stable CAM provides comparable clinical benefits to cold-storage CAM in patients with more mild to moderate disease. This aligns with recent data showing that shelf-stable CAM retains similar levels of biological components and anti-inflammatory properties as cold-storage CAM.9

Despite the promising benefits demonstrated by shelf-stable cryopreserved AM, there are limitations to note with this study. First, it was a retrospective study relying on records within the medical charts and reliance on patients to return for their regularly scheduled visits. Only 11 patients (21 eyes) returned for their 3-month follow-up visit and further studies are needed to verify the long-term effectiveness of AM treatment. The small sample size could result in the study being underpowered to detect smaller but potentially significant changes, especially in secondary outcomes. Additionally, there was no control group receiving comparative treatment to assess the adjunctive beneficial treatment of AM. Nonetheless, pre-treatment data was used as a comparison in which patients were already recalcitrant to prior treatments.

Conclusion

Shelf-stable cryopreserved AM transplantation led to a significant improvement in corneal epithelization, corneal sensitivity, and visual acuity in 18 patients (34 eyes) with recalcitrant keratopathy. Nevertheless, randomized controlled trials are required to substantiate and confirm our results. Additional studies could also evaluate if the improvement of corneal sensitivity was associated with improvement in corneal nerve density (as measured by in vivo confocal microscopy) and/or evaluate the use of adjunctive treatments to help modulate tear osmolarity to yield potentially better outcomes, particularly at later timepoints.
