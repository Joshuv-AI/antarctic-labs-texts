Brucella abortus-induced neck abscess: a case report in an urban area of Miranda, Venezuela
Authors: Walter Argenis Montero Carrillo, Alba María Mendoza Perdomo, Celina Elster de Fernández, Griselda Rodriguez, Martin Carballo, Jesús A Torres-Coy, Verónica Rosas, David A Forero-Peña
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrucellosis is a zoonosis acquired direct contact with infected animals or by consumption of unpasteurized animal by-products. The most common complications involve the musculoskeletal system, such as sacroiliitis or vertebral osteomyelitis. However, skin and soft tissue involvement is uncommon, and cervical abscess are rarely reported. We report the case of a 19-year-old male patient, with no medical history, from the metropolitan region of Caracas, Venezuela, with no history of travel or direct contact with farm animals, but regular consumption of cheese. In July 2024, he presented with undulating fever, hypor1exia and general malaise. One month later his fever intensified, preceded by chills, followed by the development of a painful, erythematous, warm swelling in the anterior neck, which evolved into an abscess requiring drainage. Purulent material yielded Brucella abortus biovar 1 confirmed by Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR). The patient received Rifampicin and Doxycycline with full recovery. To our knowledge, this is the first recorded case of human brucellosis with this presentation in Venezuela, and the first reported case of cervical abscess caused by Brucella abortus. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining epidemiological surveillance of brucellosis and strengthening quality control and hygiene measures throughout the dairy production and distribution chain.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13702-y.Keywords: Brucellosis, Brucella abortus, Neck abscess, Venezuela

Background

Brucellosis is a zoonotic disease caused by Brucella species, Gram-negative, non-motile, facultative intracellular coccobacilli [1]. Owing to its clinical presentation and geographic origin, the disease has been historically referred to as ‘remittent fever’, ‘undulant fever’, ‘Mediterranean fever’, or ‘Malta fever’. The genus Brucella is named after General Sir David Bruce, who led the Malta Fever Commission in 1886 and identified Brucella melitensis as the causative organism of the disease [2]. It is transmitted to humans thourgh contact with fluids from infected animals (sheep, cattle, goats, pigs, dogs, etc.) or through consumption of products derived from them, such as unpasteurised milk or cheese [3, 4]. The global incidence of brucellosis has been estimated at between 1.6 and 2.1 million new human cases per year [5]. Accordingly, brucellosis is not confined to livestock-farming areas but is also a health problem in many regions worldwide, including Latin America [6].

Acute disease may present with insidious, intermittent or undulant fever, accompanied by nocturnal diaphoresis, arthralgias, myalgias, lumbago, weight loss, fatigue, headache, weakness, and hyporexia, but may also manifest as localized focal disease affecting one or more systems, including osteoarticular, hematopoietic, urogenital, cardiovascular or neurological systems [7, 8]. Cutaneous involvement has been described in brucellosis, with a prevalence ranging from 1.6% to 14% [9–13]. A recent meta-analysis reported skin manifestations in 6% of the patients [14]. Several studies have reported abscess formation in brucellosis, most commonly involving the spine, spleen, liver, and renal parenchyma, probably due to hematogenous spread. Multiple cases of thyroid abscesses have also been described [15–18]. However, abscess formation in the neck without thyroid involvement is rarer and has been primarily associated with Brucella melitensis [19, 20]. To our knowledge, cases of cervical abscesses caused by other Brucella species have not been reported.

In Venezuela, seroprevalence in animals, determined by Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA), has been estimated at 16.0% (95% CI: 3.0%–36.0%) [21]. Endemic regions have been documented, such as the southern part of Maracaibo Lake and the states of Zulia, Merida, Barinas, Apure and Monagas [21–27]. The most frequently identified Brucella species in animals in Venezuela is Brucella abortus [24]. Data on human brucellosis in Venezuela are limited. We found one case study conducted between 1997 and 2012, reporting 120 confirmed cases among 2,988 samples, corresponding to a seropositivity rate of 4% [28]. However, comprenhensive data on the prevalence of human brucellosis in the country remains limited [29]. The most recent data date back to 2011, when five cases of human brucellosis were reported [30]. Here, we present a case of a cervical abscess caused by Brucella abortus in an immunocompetent young adult from El Hatillo, Miranda whose only identified epidemiological risk factor was the consumption of dairy products purchased near his urbanization.

Case presentation

A 19-year-old male patient, with no known medical history, and coming from an urban area of El Hatillo, Miranda, Caracas, Venezuela, presented referring that on July 10, 2024, he presented with fever of 38.5°, of undulating pattern, as well as hyporexia and general malaise, so he was evaluated in another institution, laboratory test revealed bicytopenia (leukopenia and thrombocytopenia), and screening studies, including dengue were negative, he received symptomatic management and was discharged. On August 10, 2024, he presented again with fever of 39–40 °C, predominantly in the evening, accompanied by chills, nocturnal diaphoresis and unquantified involuntary weight loss. One month later, he presented with monoarthralgia of the left knee. for which a nuclear magnetic resonance scan of the knee was performed on September 27, 2024. This revealed bone oedema in both condyles and the tibial plateau, as well as mild hydrarthrosis, this was treated with anti-inflammatory drugs without drainage.

Upon admission on October 1, 2024, there was evidence of tachycardia (150 beats per minute) and anxiety, as well as increased volume in the anterior region of the neck, with erythema, heat, and pain that limited mobility (Fig. 1). Cervical adenopathies, painful to palpation, were also documented. Neurologically, he was conscious but withdrawn, tachylalia and with distal postural tremor. The patient had no travel history in the last year and no direct contact with sheep, cattle, dogs, horses, or pigs. He only mentioned regular consumption of raw dairy products such as cheese, butter, and yoghurt, which he bought from an informal street vendor near his home.

He remained on outpatient follow-up, but on October 17, 2024, he was still experiencing tachycardia and postural tremor. There was also a greater increase in volume in the anterior cervical region, as well as signs of inflammation in the anterior face of the neck. On physical examination, the area was painful and sensitive to palpation, and there was functional limitation and cervical adenopathy. Treatment with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and propranolol was indicated. An echocardiogram was performed, reporting an LVEF of 75% with no evidence of regional contraction disorders, valvulopathies, effusion or pericardial thickening. On October 21, 2024, he was evaluated by the Endocrinology Specialist, who performed a cervical ultrasound. The ultrasound report indicated a collection in the anterior cervical region with echogenic characteristics and irregular walls, which was suggestive of an abscess. It was therefore decided to perform an echo-guided puncture with prior asepsis and antisepsis. Purulent content was obtained and samples were sent for Gram staining and culture. Empirical antibiotic therapy was indicated, with Sultamicillin and Clindamycin to be administered for seven days.

On 2 November, 2024, culture of the cervical abscess secretion yielded Gram-negative colonies. Automated identification using the Vitek 2 system (bioMérieux, France) provided a presumptive identification of Brucella melitensis with a 50% probability score. Consequently, the primary culture was referred to the Rafael Rangel National Institute of Hygiene (INHRR)—Venezuela’s national bacteriological reference center—for definitive identification. Simultaneously, the patient’s treatment regimen was adjusted to oral doxycycline (100 mg twice daily) and rifampicin (600 mg once daily) for six weeks. By 7 November, 2024 at follow-up, he no longer had tachycardia or adenopathy, and the enlargement had decreased considerably. On 18 February, 2025, (approximately two months after completing treatment), it had resolved completely.

Microbiological diagnosis

On November 12, 2024, serum, EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood, and the primary culture were transferred to the INHRR for confirmatory testing. Serological analysis revealed reactivity via the qualitative Rose Bengal test (Fig. 3). Subsequent quantitative slow agglutination testing (SAT), performed according to the Wright method (1897), demonstrated a total antibody titer of 1:6400. The 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME) test yielded an IgG-specific titer of 1:400; these findings are highly indicative of an active Brucella infection [31].

Bengal rose test: (A) Patient serum showing characteristic macroagglutinations indicating a positive test result. (B) Negative control

Under Biosafety Level 3 (BSL-3) conditions, a subculture of the clinical isolate was performed from the transport medium to a 5% sheep blood agar plate and a chocolate agar plate, which were incubated at 35 ± 2 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere. After 48 h, gray, smooth colonies were observed on both plates, corresponding to Gram-negative coccobacilli, with positive catalase and oxidase biochemical characteristics and negative indole, which is consistent with a preliminary identification compatible with Brucella. Several colonies were taken from the blood agar subculture to make a bacterial suspension in 0.85% saline solution until an optical density of 0.5 MacFarland was reached. We used a GN identification card (corresponding to Gram-negative bacilli) from the Vitek® 2 COMPACT automated system (Biomerieux Industria, France) to identify the microorganism. The results were inconclusive assigning a 50% probability to Brucella melitensis and a 50% probability to Ochrobactrum grignonense. Consequently, we performed a PCR test for identification.

Simultaneously, several colonies from the blood agar subculture were placed in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes containing 1X TE buffer (10 mM Tris-1 mM EDTA), which were placed in a thermal block at 100 °C for 1 h. Genomic DNA was extracted from this suspension and whole blood in EDTA using the QIAmp DNA Blood Mini Kit® (Qiagen, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, identification was performed using the AMOS PCR technique (acronym for abortus, melitensis, ovis, and suis, which are the Brucella species most frequently involved in infection in humans and animals) under the original cycling conditions and final reagent concentrations described by Bricker and Halling [32]. Genomic DNA from Brucella species was used as positive controls in the reaction, namely: B. abortus biovar 1, B. abortus strain RB51, B. melitensis biovar 1,2 and 3, and B. suis biovar 1. Mili-Q water was used as a negative control. The PCR products (8 µL) were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide and photographed (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Discussion

Brucellosis is a zoonosis caused by Brucella spp, most commonly transmitted from animals to humans through unpasteurized milk, cheese and other dairy products [6]. Here we present the case of an immunocompetent patient from an urban region whose only epidemiological history is the consumption of dairy products unpasteurized dairy near his home, who presents with undulant fever and subsequently developed an abscess in the neck region without thyroid involvement. A retrospective study evaluated 542 patients with brucellosis and reported a prevalence of neck abscess of 4%, but did not specify whether or not there was thyroid involvement, nor did they identify the species [33]. Only two case reports [20, 34] and one case included in a case series [19] describe the presence of an abscess without thyroid gland involvement, but only in two cases was the species identified, with Brucella melitensis being documented. To our knowledge, based on a search of available literature, there are no previous reports of cervical abscesses caused by Brucella abortus.

Brucella species are taken up by macrophages in local tissues, enter the circulation via regional lymph nodes and seed throughout the body, with tropism for the reticuloendothelial system [35]. Cervical lymph nodes (CLN) play an important role in oral infections, both as an effective initial trap for bacterial invaders and as a potential reservoir for chronic pathogens. CLN role during orally acquired human brucellosis has been previously documented [36]. In this case, it is hypothesised that after ingestion, the bacteria invaded the mucosa and were transported intra-intracellularly through the CLN with subsequent formation of a focal abscess due to the inability of macrophages to clear the ingested organisms. Signs of deep tissue abscess result from the mass effect of inflamed tissue or direct involvement of underlying structures such as the thyroid [37]. In this case, although there was no collection or inflammation of the thyroid (thyroiditis), the thyroid was displaced due to the mass effect of the abscess. This could partly explain the patient’s symptoms of palpitations and tachycardia, despite his normal thyroid function. Brucellar thyroiditis is characterized primarily by a prodrome of generalized myalgia, low-grade fever and fatigue. This is followed by high-grade fever, hyporexia, weight loss, insomnia, palpitations, tachycardia, headaches and severe anterior cervicalgia radiating to the auricular region, as well as a painful goitre [38–41]. In most cases, Brucella melitensis is identified. Inflammatory parameters vary from slightly elevated [16, 42] to moderately elevated [43, 44], while thyroid function tests are usually within normal [39, 44, 45]. However, deviations, such as lower TSH and elevated T4, are possible [15, 16, 41, 43, 46].

Differential diagnosis of cervical space-occupying lesions in adults includes three main scenarios: congenital anomalies, neoplasms, and infections. The clinical history, together with clinical examination and imaging studies, tends to favor an infectious origin. The most common infectious causes of abscess formation (bacterial and viral) include Staphylococcus and Streptococcus species, Mycobacterium tuberculosis and atypical mycobacterial infections, Bartonella henselae, Toxoplasma gondii, Epstein-Barr virus and Actinomyces israelii. Brucella spp. remains an uncommon pathogen for cervical abscess [47–49]. Diagnosis of Brucella infection can be made by culture, serological tests and nucleic acid amplification assays. Modern automated blood culture systems allow detection of acute cases within the routine incubation protocol of 5–7 days, although longer incubation may be required [50]. In this case, the culture made from simple of the neck abscess was sent to the clinical laboratory, growth of Brucella species was initially detected in culture. The isolate was subsequently forwarded for nucleic acid testing, which ultimately confirmed the organism as Brucella abortus.

Human brucellosis is caused primarily by B. melitensis, B. abortus and B. suis [51, 52]. In endemic areas, Brucella infections represent a serious public health problem that results in significant morbidity and economic losses, for which the World Health Organization (WHO) and the World Organization for Animal Health (OIE) have classified it as a notifiable disease [53]. B. abortus is the predominant species (81.9%) in serological diagnostic tests for Brucella spp. in animals in China [54]. Brucella abortus has seven recognized biovarieties, with biovarity 1 being the most prevalent in Latin America [55]. In Venezuela there are few published data, however, with the implementation of an epidemiological surveillance system, an regional analysis of 211 cheese and dairy farms found a prevalence of Brucella positivity (using the ring test in milk) of 8%, with those that deliver milk to the dairies and those that have individual tanks being the most affected with 19% and 21%, respectively [56]. Although regional, these results highlight a significant localized burden that may reflect broader national trends. The presence of the bacteria in dairy herds directly translates to a public health risk, acting as the primary source of human infection through the food chain. This link between animal reservoirs and human cases is clearly driven by the consumption of unpasteurized dairy products. A national study of human brucellosis in the country analyzed patients with suspected brucellosis between 1997 and 2012, diagnosing 120 positive cases out of 2,988 samples analyzed, with a seropositivity rate of 4%. Most cases were men (80.8%) with an average age of 38 years. 66% of the cases were related to the consumption of unpasteurized dairy products [28].

One of the limitations of our case was the lack of information on where the infection occurred or the supplier of the infected products. However, we suspect that the patient contracted brucellosis through consumption of unpasteurized dairy products. It is therefore essential to maintain epidemiological surveillance in the country and to strengthen quality control and hygiene strategies for dairy products, from the farm to the point of dispatch, as we are still an endemic area. Finally, we must not forget that a detailed clinical history together with relevant microbiological tests can lead to a timely diagnosis and appropriate treatment.

Conclusion

This study presents a case of human brucellosis in Venezuela with an unusual presentation: a neck abscess caused by Brucella abortus, a species that had not previously been reported in this clinical context. A detailed case history and physical examination, together with a high level of clinical suspicion based on epidemiological factors, are essential for the early diagnosis and timely treatment of brucellosis. It is essential to strengthen quality control and hygiene of these products from the farm to distribution to prevent this disease, especially in all processes related to the production of dairy products.
