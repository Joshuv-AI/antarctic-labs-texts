Clinical significance and outcomes of left-sided gallbladder for cholecystectomy: A retrospective cohort study based on 12-year single-center experience
Authors: Hong Seok Han, Seung Hwan Lee, Geun Hyeok Yang, Sun-Hyung Joo, Hyun Cheol Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgrounds/AimsLeft-sided gallbladder (LSG) is a rare biliary anomaly where the gallbladder (GB) is situated to the left of the ligamentum teres, without situs inversus. Most cases are not preoperatively identified during routine evaluations, which tend to focus more on surgical pathology than detailed anatomical variations. This study investigated the clinical implications of LSG in patients undergoing cholecystectomy.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 7,487 consecutive cholecystectomies performed at a single center between June 2014 and October 2025. LSG was confirmed by intraoperative findings and operative photographs/videos. The indication for surgery, operative time, GB perforation, drain insertion, and postoperative complications were assessed and compared with non-LSG cases.ResultsLSG was identified in 23 patients (0.3%). Mean operative time was comparable between LSG and non-LSG groups (43.5 vs. 42.4 min, p = 0.845). GB perforation rates were similar (17.4% vs. 17.8%, p = 0.964). Drain insertion was less frequent in LSG patients, although this did not reach statistical significance (8.7% vs. 27.3%, p = 0.046; continuity-corrected p = 0.078). No intraoperative or postoperative complications, including bile duct injury, occurred in the LSG group. Preoperative imaging failed to diagnose any case of LSG.ConclusionsThe incidence of LSG (0.3%) in our series was consistent with previous reports. Standard laparoscopic cholecystectomy can be performed in most LSG cases. Nonetheless, surgeons should remain alert to the possibility of associated biliary or vascular anomalies to ensure safe dissection.Keywords: Left-sided gallbladder, Laparoscopic cholecystectomy, Anatomic variation, Portal vein, Ligamentum teres

INTRODUCTION

Left-sided gallbladder (LSG), also known as sinistroposition, is defined as the localization of the gallbladder (GB) to the left of the ligamentum teres in the absence of situs inversus. Since Hochstetter first reported this anomaly in 1886, LSG has been described in 0.1%–1.2% of the population [1,2]. LSG is classically classified into three categories: LSG associated with situs inversus, true LSG (T-LSG) where the cystic duct crosses from right to left over the common hepatic duct, and right-sided round ligament gallbladder (R-LSG) where the round ligament is anomalously positioned on the right side [3].

Although rare, LSG is clinically significant due to its frequent association with variations in the cystic duct, cystic artery, and portal venous anatomy [2,4]. Unawareness of these variations can predispose patients to bile duct or vascular injury, particularly during minimally invasive hepatobiliary procedures.

Preoperative imaging, such as ultrasonography (US), computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), frequently fails to identify this anomaly during routine initial evaluations in general clinical practice. This is because the diagnostic focus is primarily placed on common surgical pathology [5,6]. Even when subtle indicators, such as rightward deviation of the ligamentum teres or abnormal segment IV morphology, are present, they are often overlooked. Consequently, LSG is typically diagnosed intraoperatively.

Several case reports and small case series have described incidental intraoperative detection and the technical challenges associated with LSG; however, comprehensive analyses with large sample sizes remain limited [6-10]. The present study aimed to assess whether LSG affects operative complexity or postoperative morbidity in patients undergoing cholecystectomy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

A retrospective cohort study was conducted at Kyung Hee University Hospital at Gangdong. All patients undergoing cholecystectomy between June 2014 and October 2025 were screened (n = 7,487).

Definition and classification of left-sided gallbladder

LSG was defined as a GB located to the left of the ligamentum teres. Diagnoses were confirmed using intraoperative findings and surgical photographs/videos. LSG was categorized into T-LSG and R-LSG. T-LSG was defined as a GB located to the left of the ligamentum teres, with the cystic duct crossing from right to left. R-LSG was defined as a GB located to the left of an anomalously positioned right-sided round ligament. No cases of situs inversus viscerum were identified.

Preoperative imaging review

Preoperative US, CT, and MRCP were retrospectively reviewed by a radiologist blinded to the operative findings. The review assessed hepatobiliary and portal venous anatomy to identify anatomical variations potentially suggestive of LSG. Specific findings included gallbladder bed location, the position of the ligamentum teres, segment IV morphology, and portal vein branching patterns.

Surgical setting & technique

Patients were positioned supine with the head elevated 30° and the right side elevated 30° for laparoscopic cholecystectomy. The standard laparoscopic approach used a four-port technique: a 5-mm transumbilical port, a 12-mm epigastric port, a 5-mm right subcostal port, and a 5-mm right flank port (a three-port technique omitted the right flank port). The epigastric port was inserted in a rightward direction across the midline, traversing the falciform ligament. The operator used the epigastric and right subcostal ports, while the first assistant provided support through the right flank port. The second assistant operated a 0° 5-mm camera to maintain visualization of the operative field (Fig. 1). Regarding dissection strategy, the retrograde approach, a standard infundibulum-first (bottom-up) technique targeting Calot’s triangle, was routinely used.

Surgical setting for laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

In selected cases without significant inflammation or adhesion, where reduced scarring was preferred, a three-port technique omitting the right flank port was used. In certain cases, a slight leftward adjustment of the epigastric port direction was performed to obtain more favorable traction and operative exposure for a leftward-displaced GB. In specific cases, the anterograde approach, a fundus-first (dome-down) dissection technique, was selectively adopted when anatomical displacement hindered safe dissection around Calot’s triangle or when adequate operative visualization could not be secured. This allowed for stepwise mobilization of the GB from the fundus toward the infundibulum. Importantly, indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence cholangiography was routinely used in all patients in both groups to clearly delineate the extrahepatic biliary tree and minimize the risk of biliary injury.

Operative data

The surgical approach (laparoscopic or robotic), number and alignment of ports, dissection technique (retrograde or anterograde), operative time, GB perforation, drain insertion, conversion rate, complications, and length of stay (LOS) were recorded.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were compared using independent t-tests. Categorical variables were analyzed using Chi-square with Yates’ continuity correction or Fisher’s exact tests. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Analysis System (SAS) version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Ethical considerations

This retrospective study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Kyung Hee University Hospital at Gangdong (IRB No. 2026-04-059). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design. The research was conducted according to the principle of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki “Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects.”

RESULTS

Incidence and characteristics

Of 7,487 cholecystectomy cases, 23 (0.3%) involved LSG. Demographic characteristics did not differ significantly from non-LSG cases. Mean age and sex distribution were comparable between the LSG (52.3 years, 52.2% male) and non-LSG (55.3 years, 47.9% male) groups. The total cohort had a mean body mass index (BMI) of 25.07 kg/m², above the Korean obesity criterion (BMI > 25). Both the LSG patients (24.84 kg/m²) and the non-LSG patients (25.07 kg/m²) demonstrated body habitus within the overweight-to-obese range, with no significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.803). The surgical indications for the entire patient cohort were as follows: acute cholecystitis in 2,740 patients (36.6%), chronic cholecystitis in 346 (4.6%), cholelithiasis in 3,583 (47.9%), GB polyps in 374 (5.0%), GB adenomas in 179 (2.4%), and GB cancer in 95 (1.3%). Among LSG patients, cholelithiasis was the most common indication (15 patients, 65.2%), followed by acute cholecystitis (4, 17.4%), adenoma (2, 8.7%), chronic cholecystitis (1, 4.3%), and polyp (1, 4.3%), with no cases of GB cancer (Table 1).

Operative findings

Table 2 presents the operative details of patients with LSGs. Of the cases, 16 patients (69.6%) were classified as T-LSG, while 7 patients (30.4%) had R-LSG (Fig. 2, 3). Laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed in 22 cases (95.7%), and one patient (4.3%) underwent robotic cholecystectomy (Fig. 4). The standard four-port technique was applied in 20 cases (87.0%). In the remaining 3 cases (13.0%), reduced-port surgery was chosen due to grossly non-inflamed GBs without severe adhesion or anatomical difficulties. One patient underwent three-port robotic cholecystectomy based on surgeon preference with patient agreement, while two female patients underwent three-port laparoscopic cholecystectomy to minimize abdominal scarring for cosmetic reasons.

The standard epigastric port direction through the right side of the falciform ligament was maintained in 17 cases (73.9%). In 6 cases (26.1%, all categorized as T-LSG) with significant leftward displacement of the GB bed, a slight left-sided adjustment of the epigastric port direction was performed to achieve a more favorable dissection angle. Standard retrograde dissection was used in 19 cases (82.6%). In 4 cases (all categorized as T-LSG), an anterograde approach was necessary. This was due to the GB bed being significantly shifted to the left and firmly adherent, which obstructed proper surgical visibility and prevented safe conventional mobilization of Calot’s triangle. Consequently, a fundus-first (anterograde) dissection was initially adopted instead of the standard infundibulum-first (retrograde) dissection.

Operative outcomes

Table 3 shows the perioperative outcomes. The mean operative time did not differ significantly between the groups (43.5 vs. 42.4 minutes, p = 0.845). The incidence of GB perforation was comparable between groups (17.4% vs. 17.8%, p = 0.964). Drain insertion was markedly less frequent in the LSG group compared with the control group (8.7% vs. 27.3%, p = 0.046). However, in a more detailed analysis, this significance was attenuated and no longer met the threshold after applying Yates’ continuity correction (p = 0.078). The mean length of hospital stay was similar between groups (3.78 vs. 4.23 days, p = 0.641). In the LSG group, there were no cases of conversion to open surgery, intraoperative transfusion, bile duct injuries, or postoperative complications. All patients recovered uneventfully and were discharged according to the planned schedule.

Pathology

Histopathologic examination indicated that chronic cholecystitis was the predominant finding, observed in eight patients (34.8%), followed by gallstones (21.7%), adenomas (21.7%), and acute cholecystitis (13.0%). 6 Cases (26.1%) demonstrated differences between preoperative and postoperative diagnosis. 4 Cases initially misidentified as gallstones were revealed to be polyps or adenomas upon postoperative pathology, whereas 2 cases preoperatively classified as acute cholecystitis were later verified as chronic cholecystitis (Table 2).

Retrospective imaging findings

A retrospective review of targeted imaging was performed in the LSG group (Table 4). Segment IV hypoplasia was identified in 22 patients (95.7%), and a right anterior portal vein (RAPV) originating from the umbilical portion of the left portal vein (ULPV) was observed in 22 patients (95.7%). An overt LSG bed position was recognized in 3 patients (13.0%), whereas right-sided deviation of the ligamentum teres was identified in 4 patients (17.4%). Only one patient lacked both segment IV hypoplasia and RAPV from ULPV on retrospective imaging review.

DISCUSSION

This study, one of the largest single-center analyses of LSG, demonstrates that LSG does not increase operative difficulty or postoperative morbidity during laparoscopic cholecystectomy. These findings support previous small-scale studies suggesting that LSG can be managed with standard technique [5,8,11].

Consistent with prior studies, routine preoperative US and MRCP frequently overlooked LSG during standard clinical interpretation focused on common surgical pathology [2,3,12]. However, a retrospective review targeting intraoperative diagnoses revealed recognizable imaging clues suggestive of a LSG in most patients when images were re-evaluated with specific suspicion for this structural anomaly. These clues included abnormal segment IV (S4) morphology, portal venous branching anomalies, leftward displacement of the GB bed, and right-sided deviation of the ligamentum teres (Table 4). In selected cases, these findings were clearly demonstrated on imaging, such as RAPV from ULPV (Fig. 5), a clearly LSG position (Fig. 6), and GB localization to the left of the ligamentum teres (Fig. 7), thereby facilitating preoperative recognition of LSG.

Previous studies have suggested that imaging may occasionally indicate the diagnosis of LSG when S4 is poorly defined or when the ligamentum teres deviates to the right [3,4,12-14]. Despite these radiologic clues, most cases are still detected incidentally during surgery [3,4]. The present study further suggests that LSG may be recognized preoperatively when characteristic radiologic clues, such as segment IV hypoplasia and RAPV from ULPV, are carefully assessed with increased awareness of this rare anatomical variation (Table 4). Bridging the gap between routine initial oversight and targeted retrospective identification may improve preoperative planning and operative safety. At the same time, surgeons should remain attentive to unexpected anatomical variations during surgery, as intraoperative recognition is crucial for the safe management of LSG [8,11].

Despite the frequent association of LSG with biliary or vascular anomalies, no complications occurred in our study. The operation time, rates of GB perforation and drain insertion, and hospital stay showed no significant differences between the LSG and non-LSG groups. These findings demonstrate that intraoperative and postoperative outcomes were comparable between the LSG and non-LSG groups, suggesting that LSG did not introduce additional operative difficulty.

Subtle anatomical differences may require heightened vigilance but do not mandate routine port modification. Most LSG cases were managed using standard laparoscopic methods without substantial modification [8,11]. In some cases, minor intraoperative technical modifications may provide more favorable operative exposure and facilitate safer dissection in selected LSG cases. In selected cases with a prominently LSG, a slight leftward adjustment of the epigastric port direction was sufficient to provide better operative exposure. Similarly, in selected cases with poor visualization of Calot’s triangle, an anterograde approach was sufficient to facilitate safer dissection by improving visualization. The present findings may provide practical technical considerations for surgeons encountering unexpected LSG in real clinical practice.

Robotic cholecystectomy was performed in only a single case in our series without complications. Our limited experience precludes any definitive conclusions regarding the role of robotic surgery in LSG, and larger studies are needed.

LSG, although uncommon, does not appear to increase operative difficulty or morbidity during laparoscopic cholecystectomy when recognized intraoperatively. In our series, operative time, perforation rate, and postoperative outcomes were comparable to those of non-LSG cases, suggesting that standard laparoscopic techniques are generally applicable. These findings are consistent with previous reports, indicating that most LSG cases can be managed without routinely modifying port placement or dissection strategy [8,11].

However, the clinical importance of LSG extends beyond routine cholecystectomy. LSG has been associated with variations in the cystic duct, cystic artery, portal venous branching, and biliary confluence [2-4,12-14]. In more complex hepatobiliary procedures, such as major hepatectomy and liver transplantation, unrecognized anatomical variations can substantially increase technical complexity and the risk of severe intraoperative complications. Therefore, heightened intraoperative awareness and careful anatomical identification are critical when LSG is encountered.

This study is limited by its retrospective, single-center design and the relatively small number of LSG cases, which restrict statistical power and generalizability. Although temporal changes in surgical practice may have influenced outcomes over the study period, the use of a contemporaneous control group mitigates, but does not eliminate, this concern. Furthermore, detailed operative technical variables, such as epigastric port direction or the use of anterograde dissection, were not systematically recorded in the non-LSG cohort, limiting direct comparison of operative modifications between groups. Larger multicenter studies are required to validate these findings and to further evaluate robotic approaches in selected patients.

In conclusion, LSG occurred in 0.3% of cholecystectomy cases without adversely affecting surgical outcomes. Standard laparoscopic cholecystectomy appears feasible for LSG, with minor case-specific modifications. Preoperative and intraoperative recognition of LSG and associated anatomical variations remains important for operative planning and surgical management.
