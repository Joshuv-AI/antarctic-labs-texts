Association of sleep duration and quality with age at natural menopause: results from NHANES 2005–2018
Authors: Qin Xie, Yanan Zhu, Bo Chen, Yajun Zhang, Meng Zhao, Yanli Lan, Xiaozhen Quan, Xuezhou Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile sleep is closely related to menopause, existing research has primarily focused on the impact of menopause on sleep, leaving the influence of sleep patterns on the timing of menopause largely unexplored. This study aimed to investigate the associations of sleep duration and quality with age at natural menopause.MethodsThis study included women with complete data on sleep patterns and natural menopausal age from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) cycles 2005–2018. Logistic and linear regression models, progressively adjusted for demographic, lifestyle, and health-related covariates, were used to examine the relationships of sleep duration and quality with menopausal age, analyzed both as categorical (early/late menopause) and continuous variables. Restricted cubic splines were applied to assess nonlinear associations. Subgroup analyses and alternative menopausal age classifications were also performed.ResultsA total of 2,157 women were included. Compared with medium sleep duration (7–9 hours), short sleep duration (< 7 hours) was associated with higher odds of early menopause in the initial model (OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.08–1.84, p = 0.01), but this association was attenuated in the fully adjusted model. Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear U-shaped association between sleep duration and early menopause (p for nonlinearity = 0.04), and an inverted U-shaped association with natural menopausal age (p for nonlinearity < 0.01), both peaking around 7.5 hours. Poor sleep quality remained significantly associated with early menopause after full adjustment (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.01–1.66), but not with late menopause. In subgroup analyses, short sleep duration showed a stronger association with early menopause in women with BMI ≥ 28 and those without hormone use. Interestingly, poor sleep quality was positively associated with late menopause among women with ≥ 3 live births and no history of oral contraceptive use.ConclusionsShort sleep duration and poor sleep quality may be associated with early menopause, with evidence of nonlinear relationships and subgroup heterogeneity. In certain populations, poor sleep quality may also be linked to later menopause. These findings highlight the complex role of sleep in reproductive aging, requiring prospective studies to clarify causal pathways.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04508-w.Keywords: Menopause, Sleep duration, Sleep hours, Sleep quality, NHANES

Introduction

Menopause marks the irreversible decline of ovarian function, the cessation of menstruation, and the end of a woman’s reproductive capacity—a major life transition signaling reproductive and somatic aging [1, 2]. Defined as the final menstrual period, natural menopause typically occurs between ages 45 and 55 [3]. Deviation from this normative age range is associated with increased health risks. Early menopause, for instance, is linked to higher incidence of osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, neurological and psychiatric disorders, and elevated overall mortality [3, 4]. Conversely, late menopause constitutes a recognized risk factor for endometrial and lung cancers [5, 6]. In general, the risk of adverse outcomes rises as menopause occurs earlier or later than the typical window [4, 7]. Thus, identifying modifiable factors that may contribute to abnormal menopause timing is of significant clinical and public health importance.

Emerging evidence suggests that sleep may play a role in ovarian aging and the timing of menopause. The circadian regulation of sleep is closely intertwined with the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian (HPO) axis, which governs reproductive hormone synthesis, folliculogenesis, and ovulation [8]. Disrupted sleep patterns have been associated with alterations in reproductive hormone profiles, including follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), estrogen, and progesterone [9, 10]. For example, each additional hour of daily sleep has been associated with 3.9% higher mean estradiol levels. More directly, poor sleep quality has been identified as an independent risk factor for premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)—a condition characterized by menopause before age 40 [11]. A meta-analysis of 38 studies involving 4,968 POI cases and 5,158 controls found that sleep deprivation was associated with a more than threefold increased risk of POI (OR 3.34, 95% CI 2.36–4.72) [12]. In a mouse model, sleep deprivation led to depletion of ovarian primordial follicles via stress hormone-mediated activation of follicular recruitment [13]. Furthermore, among women undergoing in vitro fertilization, each additional hour of sleep was associated with an increase of approximately 1.5 oocytes retrieved, suggesting a potential link between sleep and ovarian reserve [14].

Despite this accumulating evidence, few studies have directly examined the relationship between sleep patterns and the timing of natural menopause. The limited available research is constrained by several factors. First, existing studies often rely on small, non-representative samples, limiting generalizability. Second, most have examined either sleep duration or sleep quality in isolation, precluding assessment of their independent effects. Third, the absence of a standardized definition for early and late menopause across studies presents a major challenge for evidence synthesis. Various classification schemes have been used—such as dichotomizing menopausal age at < 40 vs. ≥40 years [15], or categorizing into three groups (e.g., < 45, 45–55, > 55 years) [16]. Fourth, potential nonlinear relationships between sleep duration and menopausal timing have not been systematically investigated, despite evidence that both short and long sleep may be detrimental to health outcomes. Fifth, whether the association between sleep and menopausal timing varies across subgroups defined by demographic, lifestyle, or clinical characteristics remains unknown.

To address these knowledge gaps, the present study leveraged data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). NHANES offers several distinct advantages for this research question. Its nationally representative, population-based design ensures generalizability; the large sample size provides adequate statistical power for stratified analyses; and the availability of standardized data on sleep duration, sleep quality, and comprehensive covariates enables rigorous adjustment for potential confounders. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) investigate whether sleep duration and sleep quality are associated with early menopause (< 45 years), late menopause (> 55 years), and continuous menopausal age; (2) explore potential nonlinear dose–response relationships using restricted cubic splines; (3) assess the robustness of findings through multiple sensitivity analyses, including alternative definitions of menopausal age; and (4) identify subgroups that may be more susceptible to sleep-related effects on menopausal timing. By addressing these objectives, this study aims to provide a more nuanced understanding of the role of sleep in reproductive aging and to inform potential sleep-focused interventions for preventing abnormal menopause timing.

Materials and methods

Study population

Data were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationally representative, complex-stratified, multistage, cross-sectional survey of the U.S. population conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). It is important to note upfront that the cross-sectional design of this study precludes any inference of causality; the observed associations between sleep patterns and menopausal timing should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. NHANES is uniquely suited for investigating the relationship between sleep and menopausal timing due to its rigorous sampling methodology, large and diverse sample, and comprehensive data collection on sleep behaviors, reproductive history, and a wide range of covariates through standardized in-person interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory testing.

For this study, we used data from seven NHANES survey cycles conducted between 2005 and 2018, as questions related to sleep duration and sleep quality were first introduced in 2005. Participants who met the following inclusion criteria were included:

(1) with no regular period because of natural menopause; (2) with complete information on age at menopause and sleep measures. The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of the screening process for the selection of eligible participants

After applying these criteria, a total of 2,157 women with complete data on age at menopause were included in the final analysis. Among them, 2,153 participants had available information on sleep duration, and 2,155 participants had data on sleep quality.

Exposure measurement

The exposures of interest in this study were the sleep duration and sleep quality. Both measures were based on self-reported data.

Sleep duration on usual weekday or workday was self-reported. For survey cycles from 2005 to 2016, sleep duration was derived from the question: “How much sleep do you usually get at night on weekdays or workdays?“. In the 2017–2018 cycle, the corresponding item was: “Number of hours usually sleep on weekdays or workdays”. Sleep durations were categorized into three groups: < 7 hours, 7 to 9 hours, and ≥ 9 hours.

Sleep quality was evaluated based on participants’ responses to the question, “Ever told doctor had trouble sleeping?”, which was included across all seven NHANES cycles from 2005 to 2018. Participants who responded “yes” were classified as “sleep with trouble” while those who answered “no” were classified as “sleep without trouble”.

Outcome ascertainment

Menopausal status was determined based on responses to the question, “Had regular periods in past 12 months?”. Participants who answered “no” were further asked the reason via the follow-up question, “Reason not having regular periods”, with response options including pregnancy, breastfeeding, menopause, and others. Those who reported menopause as the reason were subsequently asked whether they had undergone a hysterectomy or had both ovaries removed. Participants who answered “yes” to either question were excluded from the analysis.

The final analytical sample included only postmenopausal women who had experienced natural menopause and had complete information on their age at menopause. Age at menopause was categorized into three groups according to established definitions in the epidemiological and clinical literature [17]: early menopause (< 45 years), normal menopause (45–55 years), and late menopause (> 55 years). These thresholds were selected for several reasons. First, the 45-year cutoff for early menopause aligns with the widely accepted definition of “early menopause” as menopause occurring before age 45, which is distinct from premature ovarian insufficiency (POI, defined as menopause before age 40). This distinction is clinically important, as women with early menopause face elevated risks of osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality, though the magnitude of risk may differ from those with POI [3, 18, 19]. Second, the 55-year cutoff for late menopause corresponds to the upper limit of the typical menopausal age range and has been consistently associated with increased risks of endometrial, breast cancers and lower cognitive function [20–22]. Third, the 45–55 years range for normal menopause represents the normative window within which approximately 80–90% of women naturally experience menopause, based on large cohort studies [23, 24].

Covariates

Potential confounders were selected based on prior literature and included age, race, education level, marital status, family poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI), diabetes, hypertension, alcohol use, smoking status, physical activity, age at menarche, pregnancy history, number of live births, hormone use, and birth control pills use. All variables were derived from the NHANES questionnaire data.

Race was categorized into Mexican American, non-Hispanic black, non-Hispanic white, other Hispanic, and other races. Education level was categorized into “no more than high school” and “college or college above” based on the “Education level—Adults 20+” questionnaire. Marital status was grouped as “married or living with a partner” versus “widowed or divorced or separated or never married”. PIR was categorized as ≤ 1.30, 1.31–1.85, > 1.85 according to the previous study [25].

Smoking status was divided into “never”, “previous”, “occasional”, “daily” based on responses to the items “smoked at least 100 cigarettes in life”, “Do you now smoke cigarettes”. Physical activity was categorized into “inactive”, “moderate” and “vigorous” by the answers to questions “Does your work involve vigorous-intensity activity that causes large increases in breathing or heart rate like carrying or lifting heavy loads, digging or construction work for at least 10 minutes continuously?” and “Does your work involve moderate-intensity activity that causes small increases in breathing or heart rate such as brisk walking or carrying light loads for at least 10 minutes continuously?”. Age at menarche was grouped as < 10, 10–16 and > 16 [26]. Hormone use (ever use of estrogen/progesterone) and birth control pills use (ever use of birth control pills) were treated as separate binary variables based on the corresponding NHANES questions. The number of live births was obtained by the question “How many deliveries live birth results?”. The status of diabetes, hypertension, drinking, pregnancy history, hormone use was coded as yes and no by the question “Doctor told you have diabetes”, “Told had high blood pressure − 2 + times”, “Had at least 12 alcohol drinks/lifetime?”, “Ever been pregnant?”, “Ever use female hormones?”.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25.0) and R (version 4.3.1). Baseline characteristics for continuous variables were summarized as weighted means with standard errors, while categorical variables were presented as weighted frequencies with percentages.

We acknowledge that NHANES employs a complex multistage sampling design and that the use of sampling weights is recommended for generating nationally representative estimates. However, the primary objective of this study was to examine associations between sleep patterns and menopausal timing, rather than to produce prevalence estimates generalizable to the U.S. population. For research questions focused on etiological associations rather than descriptive inference, unweighted analyses are commonly employed and considered acceptable, particularly when the associations of interest are believed to be consistent across population subgroups [27]. Moreover, previous NHANES-based studies investigating similar etiological research questions have published unweighted analyses, with the rationale that weighting is not strictly required when the goal is to assess exposure-outcome relationships rather than to estimate population parameters [28, 29]. Nevertheless, we acknowledge this as a methodological consideration and discuss its implications in the limitations section.

For missing data, continuous variables were imputed using the mean of available values, and categorical variables were treated by creating a separate “unknown” category. This approach was chosen for several reasons. First, the proportion of missing data for key covariates was relatively low, which might mitigate the potential for substantial bias. Second, this approach is transparent and easily reproducible. Three, this method was commonly employed in large studies [30].

Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, depending on data distribution, and the chi-square test for categorical variables. The baseline comparisons presented in Table 1 involved multiple statistical tests across numerous demographic, lifestyle, and clinical characteristics. These comparisons were intended to describe the study population and identify potential confounders for subsequent regression analyses, rather than to test specific hypotheses. Accordingly, no adjustment for multiple testing was applied in these descriptive analyses [31].

To examine the association of sleep duration and sleep quality with menopausal timing, three regression models were constructed using either logistic regression (for categorical outcomes: early, normal, or late menopause) or linear regression (for continuous age at menopause). A sequential adjustment strategy was employed to evaluate the impact of different categories of confounders on the observed associations. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for body mass index (BMI). This intermediate model was designed to isolate the confounding effect of BMI, given its established bidirectional relationship with both sleep disturbances [32–34] and reproductive aging [35–37]. Model 3 further adjusted for all remaining covariates, including demographic characteristics (race, education level, marital status, poverty-to-income ratio), lifestyle factors (alcohol use, smoking status, physical activity), reproductive history (age at menarche, pregnancy history, live birth history, hormone use, birth control pill use), health conditions (diabetes, hypertension), and the other sleep variable (i.e., sleep quality when sleep duration was the exposure, and vice versa). This full model aimed to estimate the independent association of each sleep measure with menopausal timing after accounting for a comprehensive set of potential confounders.

Sleep duration was analyzed both categorically (< 7 h, 7–9 h, ≥ 9 h) and continuously. The dose–response relationship between sleep hours and age at menopause was evaluated using restricted cubic splines.

Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for categorical outcomes and as beta coefficients with 95% CIs for continuous outcomes. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses in two parts.

First, we performed subgroup analyses to examine potential heterogeneity in the association between sleep patterns and age at menopause. Subgroups were defined a priori based on factors identified in the biological and epidemiological literature as possible effect modifiers of sleep physiology, reproductive aging, or both [11, 12]. These included BMI status, physical activity level, pregnancy history, live birth history, hormone use, diabetes, hypertension, oral contraceptive use, and—mutually adjusted—sleep duration or sleep quality. These subgroup analyses were exploratory in nature and intended to generate hypotheses for future research. Formal statistical interaction tests were not performed; therefore, observed differences between subgroups should be interpreted descriptively rather than as evidence of statistically significant effect modification [38].

Second, to evaluate the impact of varying definitions of menopausal timing, we repeated our analyses using two alternative classifications from the literature [39, 40]: i.e. Early menopause was defined as age < 40 years vs. non-early menopause (≥ 40 years). ii. Early, normal, and late menopause were defined as age < 40 years, 40–50 years, and > 50 years, respectively.

Result

Characteristic of the study population by sleep duration

Of the 2,153 participants, 53.7% reported sleep duration of 7–9 hours, 25.8% reported short sleep (< 7 hours), and 20.0% reported long sleep (≥ 9 hours). Compared with the medium sleep group, participants with short sleep were younger, more likely to be daily smokers, had earlier menarche, higher BMI, and lower hormone use (all P < 0.05). Those with long sleep had lower educational attainment, lower PIR, less physical activity, and higher hypertension prevalence (all P < 0.05). Detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Mean age at natural menopause was significantly higher in the medium sleep group (49.26 ± 5.44 years) than in short (48.49 ± 5.78) and long sleep groups (48.69 ± 5.48) (P < 0.05). The distribution of menopausal age categories also differed significantly across groups (P = 0.04).

Association of sleep duration with early or late time menopause among overall participants

As shown in Table 2, short sleep duration (< 7 hours) was significantly associated with early menopause in the crude model (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.08–1.84) and after BMI adjustment (OR 1.41, 95% CI 1.08–1.83). This association was attenuated and became non-significant after full adjustment (OR 1.25, 95% CI 0.95–1.65). Long sleep (≥ 9 hours) showed no significant association with early menopause in any model. Neither short nor long sleep was associated with late menopause (all P > 0.05). Continuous sleep duration (per hour) was not significantly associated with early or late menopause in any model.

Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed a nonlinear U-shaped association between sleep duration and early menopause (P for nonlinearity = 0.04), with the lowest risk at approximately 7.5 hours (Fig. 2a). Clinically, this indicates that both shorter and longer sleep durations are associated with increased probability of entering menopause before age 45, with optimal sleep around 7.5 hours conferring the lowest risk.

The relationship between sleep hours and early or late menopause by the restricted cubic splines analyses. a sleep hours and early menopause. b sleep hours and late menopause. c sleep hours and age at menopause. The outcome was adjusted for BMI, drinking, smoking, diabetes, hypertension, physical activity, education, race, marital, PIR, pregnancy, live birth, hormone use, and menarche age

A significant inverted U-shaped association was observed between sleep duration and continuous menopausal age (P for nonlinearity < 0.01), peaking around 7.5 h (Fig. 2c). This pattern suggests that women sleeping approximately 7.5 h tend to experience menopause at the oldest ages, while those with shorter or longer sleep tend to have earlier menopause. The inverted U-shape indicates a “optimal zone” of sleep duration associated with delayed reproductive aging, with diminishing returns on either side.

No nonlinear association was found for late menopause (P for nonlinearity = 0.40) (Fig. 2b).

Characteristics of the study population by sleep quality

Among 2,155 participants, 34.9% reported sleep trouble. Compared to those without sleep trouble, these women had earlier menarche, higher BMI, and higher prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, hormone therapy, and oral contraceptive use (all P < 0.05; Supplemental Table 1). Mean menopausal age was significantly younger in the sleep-trouble group (48.53 vs. 49.15 years, P = 0.02), with higher prevalence of both early and late menopause (P = 0.02).

Association of sleep quality with early or late menopause among overall participants

Poor sleep quality was significantly associated with early menopause in all models (Model 3: OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.01–1.66, P = 0.04), but not with late menopause (all P > 0.05) (Table 2). In continuous analyses, poor sleep quality was consistently associated with younger menopausal age across all models (Model 3: β = -0.58, 95% CI -1.08 to -0.09, P = 0.02).

Subgroup analysis of the association of sleep duration and sleep quality with early or late menopause

Short sleep was significantly associated with early menopause only among women with BMI ≥ 28 (OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.06–2.17) and those without hormone use (OR 1.48, 95% CI 1.09–1.99) (Supplemental Table 2). No other subgroups showed significant associations, and no associations were observed for late menopause. Per additional hour of sleep duration was not significantly associated with early or late menopause across all subgroups (Supplemental Table 3).

Poor sleep quality was significantly associated with early menopause in several subgroups, including moderately active women, those with pregnancy history, ≤ 3 live births, no hormone therapy, and no birth control pill use (Fig. 3). Among women with > 3 live births and no birth control pill use, poor sleep quality was associated with late menopause (OR 2.64, 95% CI 1.36–5.14; OR 2.14, 95% CI 1.18–3.90) (Fig. 3). Subgroup analyses were exploratory; interaction tests were not performed, and findings require confirmation [38].

Sensitivity analyses of the association of sleep duration and quality with early or late menopause

When menopausal age was reclassified using alternative cutoffs (< 40 vs. ≥40 years; or < 40, 40–49, ≥ 50 years), the associations for sleep duration were attenuated and no longer significant (Table 3). In contrast, poor sleep quality remained significantly associated with early menopause across all alternative definitions (e.g., < 40 vs. ≥40: OR 1.60, 95% CI 1.08–2.37), while no association was observed with late menopause (Table 3).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional analysis of 2,157 women from NHANES 2005–2018, we observed that poor sleep quality was consistently associated with earlier menopause across all models and sensitivity analyses, representing the most robust finding of this study. Sleep duration showed a more complex relationship: while short sleep was associated with early menopause in unadjusted and BMI-adjusted models, this association was attenuated after full covariate adjustment. Nonlinear analyses revealed a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and early menopause, with lowest risk at approximately 7.5 h, and an inverted U-shaped relationship with continuous menopausal age, peaking around the same threshold. Subgroup analyses suggested that the association between short sleep and early menopause may be more pronounced among women with higher BMI and those without hormone use, though these exploratory findings require confirmation.

Our findings align with prior research linking poor sleep quality to diminished ovarian reserve [41] and premature ovarian insufficiency [11, 12]. A meta-analysis of 38 studies found that sleep deprivation was associated with a more than threefold increased risk of POI (OR 3.34) [12], consistent with our observation that poor sleep quality predicts early menopause. The U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and early menopause observed in our study parallels similar nonlinear associations reported for cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality [28, 42–44], suggesting that both insufficient and excessive sleep may be detrimental to health across multiple domains.

However, our results contrast with a prospective study of 3,090 Japanese women aged 35–56 years by Nagata et al., which reported that short sleep duration (≤ 6 hours) was associated with later onset of menopause compared to 7 hours of sleep (adjusted HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.78–0.99) [45]. This discrepancy may be attributed to several methodological and population differences. First, Nagata et al. employed a prospective cohort design, whereas our cross-sectional design precludes determination of temporal relationships. Second, the study population differed substantially—Japanese women versus a predominantly non-Hispanic white U.S. sample—and emerging evidence suggests that sleep-health associations may vary across ethnic groups [46, 47]. Third, categorization of sleep duration varies. Furthermore, our use of restricted cubic splines revealed a nonlinear U-shaped relationship that was not assessable in the categorical analysis of Nagata et al. Fourth, our fully adjusted model included a more comprehensive set of covariates, including sleep quality, physical activity, and hormone use. Despite these differences, both studies converge on the importance of sleep for reproductive aging and underscore the need for prospective studies with objective sleep measures and diverse populations.

The U-shaped association between sleep duration and early menopause suggests that both insufficient and excessive sleep may accelerate ovarian aging, with an optimal range around 7.5 hours. This pattern is clinically meaningful: women sleeping < 7 hours had approximately 40% higher odds of early menopause in crude analyses, while those sleeping > 9 hours showed a trend toward increased risk. The convergence of both categorical and nonlinear analyses on the 7.5-hour threshold strengthens the inference that this duration may represent an optimal range for reproductive health.

The consistent association between poor sleep quality and early menopause across all models and sensitivity analyses suggests that sleep quality may be a more robust predictor of menopausal timing than duration alone. The persistence of this association after adjusting for numerous covariates, including BMI, lifestyle factors, and reproductive history, supports an independent relationship. Notably, in sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions of early menopause (< 40 years), the association remained significant (OR 1.60), indicating that this finding is not an artifact of threshold selection.

Subgroup findings revealed that the association between short sleep and early menopause reached statistical significance only among women with BMI ≥ 28 and those without hormone use. While exploratory, this pattern suggests that obesity may potentiate the effects of short sleep on ovarian aging—a hypothesis consistent with evidence that both factors independently accelerate reproductive decline [35–37]. The finding that poor sleep quality was associated with late menopause among women with > 3 live births and no oral contraceptive use, though unexpected, highlights the complexity of sleep-reproduction relationships and warrants investigation in future studies.

Several biologically plausible mechanisms may explain these associations, though evidence varies by pathway. Established evidence from human studies indicates that sleep disturbances activate the HPA axis [48, 49], which in turn can affect reproductive hormones [50], follicle development [51], menstruation [52], and uterine receptivity [53], etc. Animal models provide experimental evidence that sleep deprivation accelerates follicular depletion through stress hormone-mediated pathways [13]. Emerging but speculative mechanisms include inflammation [54, 55] and gut microbiota alterations [56], though their direct relevance to human menopausal timing requires confirmation. The convergence of these pathways on ovarian function supports biological plausibility, while highlighting gaps requiring further research.

Our findings suggest that sleep may represent a modifiable factor associated with the timing of menopause, though several caveats warrant consideration. Given the cross-sectional, observational nature of this study, causal relationships cannot be inferred, and these results should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive.

The observed U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and early menopause, with lowest risk around 7.5 hours, raises the hypothesis that both insufficient and excessive sleep may be relevant to reproductive aging. If confirmed in prospective studies, this could inform sleep health education emphasizing not only avoiding short sleep but also recognizing that very long sleep may not confer additional benefits. However, at present, these findings are insufficient to support clinical recommendations or interventions targeting sleep duration for menopause prevention.

The consistent association between poor sleep quality and early menopause generates the hypothesis that addressing sleep disturbances might have implications for reproductive health. Women with sleep complaints—particularly those with higher BMI or no hormone use, where associations appeared stronger in exploratory analyses—may represent priority populations for future research. However, it would be premature to recommend sleep-focused interventions specifically for menopause timing based on these observational data.

More broadly, these findings add to the growing body of evidence linking sleep to women’s health across the lifespan and underscore the need for prospective studies designed to test whether improving sleep duration and quality can influence reproductive aging. Such studies should employ objective sleep measures, standardized exposure definitions, and diverse populations to establish whether the associations observed here represent causal relationships.

In summary, while these results generate interesting hypotheses about the role of sleep in reproductive aging, they do not provide a basis for clinical action. The primary contribution of this study is to highlight sleep as a potential focus for future etiologic and intervention research, not to guide current clinical practice.

This study has several strengths. First, by leveraging NHANES data, we analyzed a relatively large and diverse sample. Second, we simultaneously examined both sleep duration and sleep quality in relation to menopausal timing. Third, we employed multiple classification schemes for menopausal age, conducted extensive subgroup analyses, and used restricted cubic splines to assess nonlinear relationships.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First and foremost, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal interpretation of the observed associations. As sleep patterns and menopausal age were assessed concurrently, we cannot determine whether sleep disturbances precede and contribute to early menopause, or whether the hormonal and physiological changes accompanying the menopausal transition influence sleep quality and duration. Reverse causality and unmeasured confounding cannot be ruled out.

Second, the use of self-reported data introduces potential recall bias and measurement error. Specifically, sleep duration was assessed based on a single question asking about usual sleep on weekdays or workdays, without accounting for weekend sleep or daytime napping. Sleep patterns often vary between weekdays and weekends [57, 58], and napping is common among middle-aged and older women [59, 60]. The exclusion of weekend sleep and naps may lead to misclassification of habitual sleep duration, potentially underestimating or overestimating true sleep exposure. Sleep quality was measured using a single item (“Ever told doctor had trouble sleeping?“) rather than a validated multi-dimensional instrument such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), which was only available in the 2005–2008 NHANES cycles. This simplified classification may not fully capture the complexity of sleep disturbances, including difficulties with sleep onset, maintenance, early awakening, or non-restorative sleep, though the binary indicator has been used in previous NHANES-based studies of sleep and health outcomes [61, 62].

Third, the handling of missing data using mean imputation and an “unknown” category for categorical variables may introduce residual confounding, though the low missingness rate mitigates this concern.

Fourth, despite adjustment for a comprehensive set of covariates, the possibility of residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be eliminated. Several variables that may influence both sleep patterns and menopausal timing were not available in the NHANES dataset and therefore could not be accounted for in our analyses, such as depression [63, 64], or shift work [65, 66] etc. The absence of these variables means that the observed associations could be partially or entirely attributable to confounding by these unmeasured factors.

Fifth, our analyses did not incorporate NHANES sampling weights, strata, or clusters, which means that our findings may not be directly generalizable to the broader U.S. population. Sixth, the numerous subgroup analyses performed increase the risk of type I error due to multiple testing. Moreover, formal interaction tests were not conducted to assess whether observed differences across subgroups represent true effect modification or chance variation. As such, these exploratory findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory [38].

In summary, while we adjusted for a wide range of measured confounders, residual confounding by unmeasured factors—particularly sleep disorders, mental health conditions, and occupational exposures—remains a significant limitation. The findings should be interpreted with this caveat in mind, and prospective studies with comprehensive assessment of these potential confounders are needed to establish whether the associations observed here reflect causal relationships.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional analysis of NHANES data identified associations between sleep patterns and the timing of natural menopause. The most robust finding was the consistent association between poor sleep quality and earlier menopause, which persisted across all regression models (fully adjusted OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.01–1.66) and sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions of early menopause. This association was independent of sleep duration, BMI, lifestyle factors, and reproductive history, suggesting that sleep quality may be a relevant factor in reproductive aging.

In contrast, the association between sleep duration and menopausal timing was more complex. While short sleep (< 7 hours) was associated with early menopause in unadjusted models, this relationship was attenuated after full covariate adjustment. Nonlinear analyses revealed a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and early menopause, with lowest risk at approximately 7.5 hours, and an inverted U-shaped relationship with continuous menopausal age, peaking around the same threshold. These patterns suggest that both insufficient and excessive sleep may be relevant, though the findings are less robust than those for sleep quality.

Subgroup analyses, while exploratory, suggested that the association between short sleep and early menopause may be more pronounced among women with higher BMI and those without hormone use, generating hypotheses for future research.

Due to the cross-sectional, observational design, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than causal. The primary contribution of this study is to highlight poor sleep quality as a potentially modifiable factor warranting investigation in prospective studies designed to establish temporal relationships and elucidate underlying mechanisms. Future research should employ objective sleep measures, comprehensive assessment of potential confounders (including sleep disorders, depression, and shift work), and diverse populations to determine whether improving sleep quality can influence the timing of menopause and its associated health consequences.
