Quality of life among geriatric population living with functional disabilities: A comparison between urban and rural populations of West Bengal
Authors: Aritraa Hazra, Sukanta Majumdar, Shuvankar Mukherjee, Avijit Paul, Nabanita Bhattacharyya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:Global elderly population’s growth is evident, with projections reaching 2 billion by 2050, while India’s geriatric population is expected to rise to 11.6% by 2026. Disparity in quality of life (QOL) and disabilities between urban and rural elderly exist, where rural elderly maintain active roles, while urban counterparts face economic insecurity post retirement.Aims:The study explores QOL differences among geriatric individuals with functional disabilities.Materials and Methods:This is a cross-sectional descriptive observational study conducted among geriatric populations from urban Dhapa and rural Khalia, who were surveyed. The sample size was 100 for both urban and rural areas. Data collection involved interviews with predesigned, pretested, semistructured schedules. Systematic random sampling was used for participant selection.Result:Among the study populations, 64% and 62% were male population, 63% and 76% were from general caste, 43% and 42% were in the age group of old to very old (70–79 years), 41% and 26% were gainfully employed, 18% and 96% were belonging to lower-middle to lower socioeconomic class in urban and rural areas, respectively. The study revealed 28% urban and 49% rural study population and 28% were disabled. While assessing QOL, statistically significant differences were found in the environmental domain and social relationship domain.Conclusion:Around 1/3rd in urban areas and nearly ½ in rural regions were disabled. Older individuals, Muslim community, general caste, and lower socioeconomic class were predicting factors. Physical, psychological, and social-relationship domain scores were higher among rural population, and the environmental domain mean score was higher among the urban populations.

Introduction

The global phenomenon of an aging population is attributed to improved survival rates and increased life expectancy. India currently boasts the second-largest population of elderly individuals worldwide. This demographic is expected to undergo a substantial shift, with the proportion of older persons projected to surge from 8% in 2015 to an estimated 19% by the year 2050.[1] The longer life expectancies have not necessarily translated to healthy aging, and the older population is plagued by unique challenges in accessing health care.[2] The phenomenon of population aging is acknowledged as a global reality, affecting both developed and developing nations. In the developing world, the elderly population is growing due to demographic transitions, yet their well-being is deteriorating due to the accelerated erosion of traditional family structures, alongside swift processes of modernization and urbanization.[3] The longer life expectancies have not necessarily translated to healthy aging, and the older population is plagued by unique challenges in accessing health care.[2] The health resources in India and access to them are also inequitably distributed and more urban-biased, making rural older persons face greater restrictions in access to health care.[4] Quality of life (QOL) is an individual’s perception of their life position in context of the society and culture they live in.[5] The quality of life for older individuals should not diminish solely due to the aging process. A high quality of life signifies that the older population is experiencing healthy and positive aging. This includes a perception of reduced risk of disease and disability, robust mental and physical functionality, and active participation in life.[6] Measuring disability is a complex and multifaceted task. It can affect individuals on a temporary or permanent basis at any given time. Moreover, as people reach old age, they often encounter a growing challenge of disability. The International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF) advocates for a comprehensive perspective on disability, considering it as a bio-psycho-social model.[7] It defines disability as an umbrella term for impairments, activity limitations, and participation restrictions, referring to the negative aspects of the interaction between an individual and that individual’s contextual factors, that is, environmental and personal factors.[8] Functional assessment is an integral part of the multidisciplinary evaluation of disability of the elderly.[9] A community-based assessment of functional disability can predict the need for assistance for this age group.[10] Furthermore, it is crucial to gain insight into the factors linked with functional disability to develop and execute interventions aimed at enhancing their functional well-being. Research has demonstrated that functional disability not only hampers the capacity to maintain independent living but also diminishes their overall QOL.[11] Having trouble with social participation, mobility, social interaction, and other disability conditions may have a bad negative influence on the QOL.[1213] There is a prevailing lack of enthusiasm for providing care to the elderly and a limited awareness of the concept of QOL in India. Earlier studies indicate that overall QOL is markedly higher in urban areas compared to rural areas. However, contrary to this pattern, our hypothesis suggests that in rural regions, elderly individuals continue working until their physical capabilities allow them to do so. They experience a sense of empowerment, status within their families and social circles, and economic independence. In contrast, in urban settings, older adults tend to work within certain age limits defined by their occupations. Beyond this point, they may grapple with economic insecurity and a loss of influence, which can lead to a reduced QOL.[14] The percentage of individuals aged 60 years and above has been on a steady rise. By the year 2020, a historic shift occurred, where the population of individuals aged 60 and older surpassed the number of children under 5 years old. It is projected that by 2050, the global population of individuals aged 60 years and older will reach 2 billion, a significant increase from the current 841 million.[15] In the case of India, the geriatric population constituted approximately 7% in 2001, a figure anticipated to escalate to 11.6% by the year 2026.[16] According to the 2011 census, 8% of the total population fell into the 60 years and above category, with 8.1% residing in rural areas and 7.9% in urban areas.[17] This study aims to find out the difference in QOL among the geriatric population living with functional disability residing in the slums of Kolkata and rural areas of West Bengal.

Materials and Methods

A community-based descriptive observational study cross-sectional in design was conducted among geriatric population at Dhapa under the jurisdiction of Borough VII, ward 57, Kolkata Municipal Corporation (KMC), which is the urban field practice area, and also in Khalia subcenter, Bally-Jagachha block of Howrah, which is the rural field practice area of Community Medicine Department, Calcutta, National Medical College, Kolkata, from January to March, 2020.

Inclusion criteria – Geriatric population living in the slum for more than 3 years and who gave consent to participate were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria – Severely ill and difficult-to-communicate persons were excluded from the study.

Considering prevalence (p) of 49.6% in urban and 50.0% in rural areas among geriatric people with full/partial disability in West Bengal[18] with the confidence interval at 95% and an allowable error (L) of 10%, the sample size (n) was calculated as 100 for both urban and rural areas using the formula n = z2pq/L2, where z = 1.96, q = (1-p).

After getting permission from Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval letter no – EC/CNMC/254, dated – 20.01.2020), informed consent from study subjects was taken and data were collected with the help of predesigned, pretested, and semistructured schedules for general information; Lawton Brody Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) Scale[19] for disability and WHOQOL-BREF[20] for assessing QOL by one-to-one interview method were used. It was seen that 407 geriatric persons were residing in urban field practice areas as per family health care records. To collect data, the systematic random sampling method was followed. The first study subject was selected randomly from the first four study populations. Then every fourth one was included, who were fulfilling the inclusion and exclusion criteria. If a study subject found absent on the day of data collection, then the next one was included. Similarly, from rural areas, 100 study subjects were selected out of 352 geriatric populations using systematic random sampling method by selecting every third one.

Transformed score = [(actual raw score − lowest possible raw score)/possible raw score range] × 100.

This transformation converts the lowest and highest possible scores to 0 and 100, respectively. The scores between these values represent the percentage of the total possible raw score achieved.

Statistical analysis

Data entry and analysis were done in SPSS (v16). Frequency distribution tables were used for descriptive statistics. Chi-square test and Mann–Whitney U test were used, and the level of significance was considered as 5%.

Discussion

The current study encompassed both urban and rural elderly populations residing in the eastern region of India. Our study indicated that a significant proportion of the population in both areas were male, aligning with the findings of a study conducted by Kumari R[24] and Chowdhury A.[25] Additionally, our study revealed that the majority of the population belonged to the very old age group. In contrast, Kumari R[24] and Chowdhury A[25] found a higher prevalence of the young-old age group in their respective studies. Furthermore, our study identified a higher literacy rate among individuals from the Hindu community in both study areas, mirroring the results of the study done by Kumari R.[24] Notably, a substantial portion of the study population in our research lived alone, which corresponded with similar findings in the study done by Chowdhury A.[25] In our current study, we observed that one-third of the urban and half of the rural study population were experiencing some form of disability. Significant predictors of disability included older age groups, individuals from the general caste, the Muslim community, and those belonging to lower-middle socioeconomic classes. In contrast, a study done by Kumari R[24] identified significant predictors of disability as the young-old age group, males, urban dwellers, and those living with their spouses. Regarding QOL, the mean scores in the physical and psychological domains were higher among the rural study population, while the mean score in the environmental domain was higher among the urban population compared to their counterparts. This finding contrasted with the study findings done by Mudey et al.,[26] which reported higher mean scores in the physical and psychological domains for the rural population and higher scores in the environmental and social-relation domains for the urban population. Similarly, a study by Barbosa AP[27] indicated a higher mean score in the social domain among the urban population and a higher mean score in the physical domain among the rural population. Additionally, Krishnappa L[28] found that mean scores in all four domains were higher among the urban population, a trend consistent with studies conducted by Kritika et al.[29] in Dehradun and Praveen V.[30]

Limitations

The study’s primary limitation was the inability to cross-check the disability score of the IADL scale through physical or clinical examinations.

Conclusion

The study found that a significant portion of the old population, around one-third in urban areas and nearly half in rural regions, were disabled. This prevalence was more pronounced among older individuals, the Muslim community, those belonging to the general caste, and individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds. Interestingly, there were no disabled individuals in the working-age group. Notably, there were substantial disparities in QOL scores across all four domains. In the rural study population, the mean scores for the physical, psychological, and social-relationship domains were higher compared to the urban population. Conversely, the mean score for the environmental domain was higher among the urban population. The findings from this study underscore the importance of targeted interventions aimed at enhancing the QOL for geriatric individuals with disabilities, with a specific focus on females, older age groups, and those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds. Moreover, these interventions should address disparities in QOL across various domains, taking into account factors such as gender, age, marital status, employment, religion, caste, and literacy status.
