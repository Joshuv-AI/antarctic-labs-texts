1.
Introduction

A Banach algebra
\mathscr{A}
is Arens regular when the two Arens products defined on its second Banach dual
\mathscr{A}^{**}

coincide. This is equivalent to saying that one of the products (and so both products are) is separately weak
∗
-continuous making
\mathscr{A}^{**}
a semitopological algebra when equipped with the weak
∗
-topology. It is worthwhile to note, that due to Grothendieck’s criterion, this is in turn equivalent to that every element in the Banach dual
\mathscr{A}^{*}

is weakly almost periodic, i.e.,
\mathscr{A}^{*}={\seis{\mathscr{WAP}}}(\mathscr{A}).
This fact is not going to be used in the present article, but was essential in our study of Arens irregularity
of Banach algebras in harmonic analysis in the articles
[
40
]
and
[
41
]
.
As seen already in these past two articles and as we shall further see presently, unlike C
∗
-algebras, the usual algebras in harmonic analysis such as the group algebra, and the Fourier algebra in most cases, have turned out to be all non-Arens regular
(even in an extreme way).
At the very beginning, about seventy years ago,
Richard Arens proved in
[
1
]
that the convolution algebra
\ell^{1}
is Arens irregular.
A long exciting story followed, and so much has already been written on the subject ever since.
For more details, see the surveys
[
23
]
and
[
51
]
, the book
[
15
]
, the memoirs
[
16
]
and
[
17
]
, and our most recent survey
[
39
]
where the results of the actual article were announced.

There are two different types of utmost non-Arens regularities, arising naturally from the way the algebras are decided to be non-Arens regular.

Since
\mathscr{A}
is Arens regular if and only if
\mathscr{A}^{*}=\mathscr{WAP}(\mathscr{A})
, the degree of non-Arens regularity of an algebra
\mathscr{A}
may be measured
by the size of the quotient space
\frac{\mathscr{A}^{*}}{{\seis{\mathscr{WAP}}}(\mathscr{A})}.
In the extreme situation (i.e., when
\frac{\mathscr{A}^{*}}{{\seis{\mathscr{WAP}}}(\mathscr{A})}
is as large as
\mathscr{A}^{*}
) Granirer called such an algbera
\mathscr{A}
extremely non-Arens regular (enAr for short) and proved that this was the case for the Fourier algebra of a non-discrete second countable locally compact group
G
(see
[
58
, Corollaries 6 and 7]
).
Granirer’s result was extended by Hu in
[
68
]
to locally compact groups
G
having their local weight
w(G)
greater or equal to their compact covering
\kappa(G).

On the dual hand, the group algebra is
isometrically enAr, and so enAr, for any infinite locally compact group
G
. This was proved in
[
52
]
when
G
is discrete, in
[
8
]
when
\kappa(G)\geq w(G)
, and finally in
[
37
]
for the general case.
The semigroup algebra
\ell^{1}(S)
is isometrically enAr for any infinite weakly cancellative discrete semigroup
(see
[
52
]
). Here, a Banach algebra is isometrically enAr when
\frac{\mathscr{A}^{*}}{{\seis{\mathscr{WAP}}}(\mathscr{A})}
contains an isometric copy of
\mathscr{A}^{*}.

This topic was our main focus in
[
40
]
and
[
41
]
, where

\ell^{1}
-bases approximating lower and upper triangles of products of elements in
\mathscr{A}
were the reason behind the non-Arens regularity of
\mathscr{A}
, and in many cases the extreme non-Arens regularity of
\mathscr{A}
.
This unified approach dealing with the extreme non-Arens regularity helped us to improve and extend the above-cited results to a large class of Banach algebras. The combinatorics of the locally compact group
G
and its dual object
\widehat{G}
were the tools to construct these
\ell^{1}
-bases for many algebras which are associated to
G
in harmonic analysis.

Non-Arens regularity may also be measured by the degree of defect of the separate weak
∗
-continuity of
Arens products. In the extreme situation (i.e., when separate weak
∗
-continuity of the product in
\mathscr{A}^{**}
happens only when one of the elements in the product is in
\mathscr{A}
), Dales and Lau called the algebra
\mathscr{A}
strongly Arens irregular
( see
[
16
]
). This will be our main focus in the present paper.
A more refined
\ell^{1}
-bases in
\mathscr{A}
are necessary to show the strong Arens irregularity of Banach algebras associated to a locally compact group
G.

Again the reason making these refined
\ell^{1}
-bases available are the combinatorics in
G
and
\widehat{G}.

The paper is organized as follows. After a preliminary section recalling the basic definitions and results needed in the paper, we present our general theory in Sections 3 and 4. The new definitions of some special
\ell^{1}
-bases, we call F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base, are given for any Banach algebra
\mathscr{A}

We illustrate our general theory with three sections, starting with the group algebra and the measure algebra of a non-compact locally compact group and the semigroup algebra of an infinite weakly cancellative, right cancellative, discrete semigroup. Then come the weighted analogues of these algebras. These are studied when the weight is diagonally bounded.
Using some special subsets of

or the cardinality of the discrete semigroup.

The penultimate section deals with the Fourier algebra of a compact group.
The cases we study first are compact groups with local weight having

Using the irreducible representations of

With the availability of the required F

In the last section, we shall extend our results to the case when the group is the product

The algebraic structures and the topological centres of the Banach algebras

,

or

will be our final conclusion.

Some of these results have been proved in the past by various authors as we shall indicate as we go along.
In addition to the unification of all these results, there will be many other new results.

2.
Some basic definitions and notation

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a Banach algebra.
The elements in
\mathscr{A}
will be denoted by miniscule Roman letters.
The elements in the Banach dual
\mathscr{A}^{*}
of
\mathscr{A}
will be denoted by majuscule Roman letters.
Majuscule Greek letters will be used to denote the elements in the second dual of the algebra.
With the exceptions of the product in
\mathscr{A}
and its first Arens extensions to
\mathscr{A}^{**}

and
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
, all the module actions will be denoted by ”
\cdot
”.
With the elements denoted as such, there will be no confusion as to which module action is considered.

2.1.
Arens products

Recall that the Arens module actions are defined via the adjoint of continuous bilinear mappings (see
[
1
]
and
[
2
]
). That is, if

\mathscr{E}_{1}\times\mathscr{E}_{2}\to\mathscr{E}_{3}\quad\text{ is a continuous bilinear mapping}

on some normed spaces
\mathscr{E}_{i}
(
i=1,2,3
), then its (3
1
2)-adjoint given by

\mathscr{E}_{3}^{*}\times\mathscr{E}_{1}\to\mathscr{E}_{2}^{*}\quad\text{is again a continuous bilinear mapping},

and so in turn, its (3
1
2)-adjoint may be be taken. The process can be repeated indefinitely.

Here are the module actions we shall be using throughout the paper.
We start with a Banach algebra
\mathscr{A}
and regard it as a left
\mathscr{A}
-module with its operation
as the left module action of
\mathscr{A}
on itself; that is

\mathscr{A}\times\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A},\quad\text{where}\quad a\cdot b=ab.

Then by taking the (3
1
2)-adjoint, we shall regard

\mathscr{A}^{*}
as a right
\mathscr{A}
-module, where the natural right module action of
\mathscr{A}
on
\mathscr{A}^{*}
is given by

(1)

\mathscr{A}^{*}\times\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{A}^{*},\;\mbox{ where }\;\langle S\cdot a,b\rangle=\langle S,ab\rangle\;\mbox{ for }\;S\in\mathscr{A}^{*},\;a,b\in\mathscr{A}.

This is followed (taking again the (3
1
2)-adjoint) by regarding
\mathscr{A}^{*}
as a left
\mathscr{A}^{**}
-module, where the natural left module action of
\mathscr{A}^{**}
on
\mathscr{A}^{*}
is given by

(2)

\mathscr{A}^{**}\times\mathscr{A}^{*}\to\mathscr{A}^{*},\;\text{where}\;\langle\Psi\cdot S,a\rangle=\langle\Psi,S\cdot a\rangle\quad\text{for}\;\Psi\in\mathscr{A}^{**},\;S\in\mathscr{A}^{*},a\in\mathscr{A}.

This induces the first (left) Arens product in
\mathscr{A}^{**}
making
\mathscr{A}^{**}
a Banach algebra. For
\Phi,\Psi\in\mathscr{A}^{**},
the product is given by

(3)

\mathscr{A}^{**}\times\mathscr{A}^{**}\to\mathscr{A}^{**},\quad\text{where}\quad\langle\Phi\cdot\Psi,S\rangle=\langle\Phi,\Psi\cdot S\rangle\quad\text{for}\quad S\in\mathscr{A}^{*}.

The first Arens product in
\mathscr{A}^{**}
is the unique extension of the product in
\mathscr{A}
such that

(i)

\Phi\mapsto\Phi\Psi:\mathscr{A}^{**}\to\mathscr{A}^{**}\quad\text{ is weak${}^{*}$-weak${}^{*}$-continuous}
for each
\Psi\in\mathscr{A}^{**},

(ii)

\Phi\mapsto a\Phi:\mathscr{A}^{**}\to\mathscr{A}^{**}\quad\text{is weak${}^{*}$-weak${}^{*}$-continuous}
for each
a\in\mathscr{A}.

The first topological centre of
\mathscr{A}^{**}
may therefore be defined as

T_{1}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})=\{\Psi\in\mathscr{A}^{**}:\Phi\mapsto\Psi\Phi:\mathscr{A}^{**}\to\mathscr{A}^{**}\quad\text{is weak${}^{*}$-weak${}^{*}$-continuous}\}.

Note that Item (ii) above means that
\mathscr{A}\subseteq T_{1}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**}).

If we start with
\mathscr{A}
as a right
\mathscr{A}
-module with the right module action given by the transpose of the operation in
\mathscr{A}
, that is
a\odot b=ba
for
a,b\in\mathscr{A},
then taking the transpose of the left action
of
\mathscr{A}^{**}
on itself reached at the third step, will give the second (right) Arens product.
We make an exception here and denote the second Arens product of two elements
\Phi
and
\Psi
in
\mathscr{A}^{**}

by
\Phi\diamond\Psi
and the actions leading to this product by
"\odot"
so that, for every
S\in\mathscr{A}^{*}
and
a,b\in\mathscr{A}
, we have

(4)

\begin{split}&\langle\Phi\diamond\Psi,S\rangle=\langle\Psi\odot\Phi,S\rangle=\langle\Psi,\Phi\odot S\rangle,\quad\text{where}\\
&\langle\Phi\odot S,a\rangle=\langle\Phi,S\odot a\rangle\quad\text{and}\\
&\langle S\odot a,b\rangle=\langle S,a\odot b\rangle=\langle S,ba\rangle\end{split}.

We note that the second Arens product in
\mathscr{A}^{**}
is the unique extension of the product in
\mathscr{A}
such that

(i)

\Psi\mapsto\Phi\diamond\Psi:\mathscr{A}^{**}\to\mathscr{A}^{**}\quad\text{is weak${}^{*}$-weak${}^{*}$-continuous}
for each
\Phi\in\mathscr{A}^{**},

(ii)

\Phi\mapsto\Phi\diamond a:\mathscr{A}^{**}\to\mathscr{A}^{**}\quad\text{is weak${}^{*}$-weak${}^{*}$-continuous}
for each
a\in\mathscr{A}.

The second topological centre of
\mathscr{A}^{**}
may therefore be defined as

T_{2}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})=\{\Psi\in\mathscr{A}^{**}:\Phi\mapsto\Phi\diamond\Psi:\mathscr{A}^{**}\to\mathscr{A}^{**}\quad\text{is weak${}^{*}$-weak${}^{*}$-continuous}\}.

Again Item (ii) means that
\mathscr{A}\subseteq T_{2}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**}).

When these two ways yield the same product, i.e., if these two products coincide, we say that the algebra
\mathscr{A}
is
Arens regular
.
It is clear that the algebra is Arens regular if and only if one of the Arens products (and so both are) is separately weak
∗
-weak
∗
-continuous, i.e.,

T_{1}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})=T_{2}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})=\mathscr{A}^{**}.

Such a situation happens for instance when
\mathscr{A}
is a
C^{*}
-algebra. This was first deduced from the fact that the second dual of a
C^{\ast}
-algebra can be seen as an algebra of operators on a Hilbert space, a result proved by Sherman in
[
108
]
and Takeda in
[
110
]
. Later on, in their seminal paper
[
12
]
, Civin and Yood reproduced Takeda’s proof and brought up explicitly the Arens regularity of

-algebras.
A different proof of this can also be found in
[
7

.

When the Banach algebra is not Arens regular, i.e., when

\mathscr{A}\subseteq T_{i}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})\subsetneq\mathscr{A}^{**}\quad\text{for}\quad i=1\quad\text{or}\quad 2,

or equivalently when
\mathscr{A}^{*}\neq\mathscr{WAP}(\mathscr{A})
,
we say that the algebra is
Arens irregular
or
non-Arens regular
.

In the extreme situation of
T_{1}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})=\mathscr{A}
(respectively,
T_{2}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})=\mathscr{A}
),
Dales and Lau called the algebra
\mathscr{A}

left
(respectively,

)
strongly Arens irregular
.
In the very extreme situation of
T_{1}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})=T_{2}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})=\mathscr{A}
, the algebra
\mathscr{A}

is
s
trongly Arens irregular (sAir), see
[
16
]
.
Note that when
\mathscr{A}
is commutative (as itis the case with the Fourier algebra

studied in the last two sections),

and

, that is,

\displaystyle T_{1}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})=T_{2}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})

\displaystyle=\{\Psi\in\mathscr{A}^{**}:\Phi\Psi=\Psi\Phi\quad\text{for every}\quad\Phi\in\mathscr{A}^{**}\}

\displaystyle=\{\Psi\in\mathscr{A}^{**}:\Phi\diamond\Psi=\Psi\diamond\Phi\quad\text{for every}\quad\Phi\in\mathscr{A}^{**}\}.

In this case, we will be talking just about the strongly Arens irregularity.

It should be also recalled that
T_{1}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})
and
T_{2}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})
may be different, and it may even happen that

, i.e.,

[

[

[

and so the (second) topololgical centre of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
cannot be defined for
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}

unless
S\odot a
and
\Phi\odot S
are in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})
for every
a\in\mathscr{A}
,
S\in\mathscr{A}
and
\Phi\in{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
,
which happens for example when
\mathscr{A}
is commutative.
Here, we must use the space
{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})
instead. This is the closed linear span in
\mathscr{A}^{*}
of
\mathscr{A}^{*}\odot\mathscr{A}
, which again by Cohen’s factorization theorem, is equal to
\mathscr{A}^{*}\odot\mathscr{A}
when
\mathscr{A}
has a bounded left approximate identity.

Since

{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})\odot\mathscr{A}\subseteq{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})
and
{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}\odot{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})\subseteq{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})
,
the same procedure as before yields the second Arens product in
{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}

making
{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
a Banach algebra: As in (
4
), the product of
\Phi,

\Psi\in{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
is given by

\langle\Phi\diamond\Psi,S\rangle=\langle\Psi\odot\Phi,S\rangle=\langle\Psi,\Phi\odot S\rangle\quad\text{for all}\quad S\in{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A}).

This product is the unique extension of the product in
\mathscr{A}
to
{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
such that

(i)

\Psi\mapsto\Phi\diamond\Psi:{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}\to{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}\quad\text{is weak${}^{*}$-weak${}^{*}$-continuous}
for each
\Phi\in{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*},

(ii)

\Phi\mapsto\Phi\diamond a:{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}\to{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}\quad\text{is weak${}^{*}$-weak${}^{*}$-continuous}
for each
a\in\mathscr{A}.

The (second) topololgical centre of
{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
may therefore be defined as

\displaystyle T_{2}Z({\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*})=\{\Psi\in{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}:\Phi\mapsto\Psi\diamond\Phi:

\displaystyle{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}\to{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}

\displaystyle\quad\text{is weak${}^{*}$-weak${}^{*}$-continuous}\}.

Note again that
\mathscr{A}\subseteq T_{2}Z({\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}).

Note also if
\Phi\odot S
and
\Phi\odot\Psi

are the actions of
{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
on
\mathscr{A}^{*}
and
\mathscr{A}^{**}
as defined with
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})
in (
5
) and (
6
), respectively, then

\Phi\odot S=\widetilde{\Phi}\odot S\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi\odot\Phi=\widetilde{\Phi}\diamond\Psi,

where
\widetilde{\Phi}
is picked by Hahn-Banach Theorem in
\mathscr{A}^{**}
such that
\pi(\widetilde{\Phi})=\Phi
.
Here
\pi:\mathscr{A}^{**}\to{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
is the adjoint of the inclusion map

{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})\hookrightarrow\mathscr{A}^{*}
.

3.
Factorizations of
\mathscr{A}^{*}
and
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A}^{*})
and topological centres

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a Banach algebra and
\eta
be a cardinal number. Then the Banach dual

\mathscr{A}^{*}
of
\mathscr{A}
(respectively,
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})
)
is said to have the
\eta
-factorization property if for every bounded family of functionals

(S_{\lambda})_{\lambda<\eta}
contained in
\mathscr{A}^{*}
,
(respectively,
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})
),
there exist a bounded family
(\Psi_{\lambda})_{\lambda<\eta}
in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
(usually in the unit ball of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
) and a single functional
T\in\mathscr{A}^{*}
(respectively, in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})
)
such that the factorization formula
S_{\lambda}=\Psi_{\lambda}\cdot T

holds for all
\lambda<\eta
.

This factorization was obtained and used, together with the so-called Mazur property, by Neufang in
[
100
]
to show that the group algebra of a non-compact locally compact group is strongly Arens irregular. In some cases, the same result was also obtained by the same author for the measure algebra in
[
101
]
.

The factorization property (and some of its variants) was also used to study ideals and right cancellation in Banach algebras and semigroup compactifications with an Arens product such as

L^{1}(G)^{**}
,
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)^{*}
and the
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}
-compactification
G^{LUC}
of non-compact locally compact groups,
\ell^{\infty}(S)^{*}
and the Stone-Čech compactification of infinite discrete semigroups
S
having some cancellation properties,
A(G)^{**}
and
\mathscr{UC}_{2}(G)^{*}
for a class of non-metrizable locally compact groups. See for example,
[
36
]
,
[
45
]
,
[
46
]
,
[
47
]

and
[
48
]
.

We define next a weaker factorization property, but we keep the same terminology.
This weak
\eta
-factorization property was introduced and used in
[
44
]
. It was used, together with the Mazur property, to show that the Fourier algebra of a certain class of non-metrizable groups is strongly Arens irregular.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a Banach algebra,
\eta
be a cardinal number and
X_{1},...,X_{m}
be subsets of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
each with cardinality at least
\eta
. Then the Banach dual

\mathscr{A}^{*}
of
\mathscr{A}

is said to have the
\eta
-factorization property through
\prod_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}
if for every bounded family of functionals

\{S_{\lambda}:\lambda<\eta\}
contained in
\mathscr{A}^{*}
,
there exist a bounded family
\{(\Psi_{\lambda k})_{k=1}^{m}:\lambda<\eta\}
in
\prod_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}

and a finite family of functionals
\{T_{k}:k=1,...,m\}
in
\mathscr{A}^{*}

such that the factorization formula

(8)

S_{\lambda}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Psi_{\lambda k}\cdot T_{k}

holds for all
\lambda<\eta
.

If the elements
\{\Psi_{\lambda}=(\Psi_{\lambda k})_{k=1}^{m}:\lambda<\eta\}
can be chosen in
\prod_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}
independently of the elements
S_{\lambda}
, then we say that
\mathscr{A}^{*}
has the uniform

\eta
-factorization property. (This is going to be so in all the cases treated in the paper.)

Remarks 3.2
.

(i)

Putting

T=(T_{k})_{k=1}^{m}\in{\mathscr{A}^{*}}^{m}
, we may write the factorization formula (
8
) as

S_{\lambda}=\Psi_{\lambda}\cdot T\quad\text{for all}\quad\lambda<\eta.

(ii)

The
\eta
-factorization property just defined is one-sided and should have been labeled ”left” since we are using the left module action
\Psi\cdot T

of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
on
\mathscr{A}^{*}
(see
5
).
The mirror
\eta
-factorization property may also be defined using the action
\Psi\odot T
of
{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
on
\mathscr{A}^{*}
. Noting that
the left action
\Psi\odot T
of
{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
on
\mathscr{A}^{*}
is the same as the right action
T\cdot\Psi
of
{\seis{\mathscr{RUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
on
\mathscr{A}^{*}
induced by the left action
a\cdot T
of
\mathscr{A}
on
\mathscr{A}^{*}
, where
\langle a\cdot T,b\rangle=\langle T,ba\rangle
for
b\in\mathscr{A},

we may say that
\mathscr{A}^{*}
has the right
\eta
-factorization property through
\prod_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}

when the identity (
8
) in Definition
3.1
is changed to

S_{\lambda}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Psi_{\lambda k}\odot T_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}T_{k}\cdot\Psi_{\lambda k}=T\cdot\Psi_{\lambda}

for all
\lambda<\eta
.

In order to apply this factorization property in Theorems
3.5
and
3.8
dealing with the topological centres of
\mathscr{A}^{**}
and
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}

Let
\mathscr{E}

(i)

Notation 3.4

Throughout the paper, with the set

and

of
\mathscr{A},
this is the algebra of all bounded operators

is a

of
\mathscr{A}
if

for

.
The composition of operators is the operation in

.

Recall also that in a Banach algebra

, a

(brai for short)

\lim_{\alpha}\|ae_{\alpha}-a\|=0\quad\text{

Any weak
∗
-limit of a brai is a right identity in

when

has the first Arens product. Bounded left approximate

is a bounded approximate identity

When

has a bai bounded by

,

is a closed right ideal in

and a closed left ideal in

and

[

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a Banach algebra,
\eta
be an infinite cardinal and
X_{1},...,X_{m}
be subsets of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
each of cardinality at least
\eta
.
Suppose that
\mathscr{A}
has the
\eta
-Mazur property and its dual
\mathscr{A}^{*}
has the
\eta
-factorization property through
\prod_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}
. Then

(i)

for any weak
∗
-closed subsemigroup
\mathscr{S}
of
\mathscr{A}^{**}
with
\widetilde{X}\subseteq\mathscr{S}
, we have
T_{1}Z(\mathscr{S})=\mathscr{S}\cap\mathscr{A}.

(ii)

In particular,
T_{1}Z(\mathscr{A}^{**})=\mathscr{A}
, that is,
\mathscr{A}
is left strongly Arens irregular.

Proof.

Let
\Psi\in T_{1}Z(\mathscr{S})
and
(S_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
be a net in the unit ball of
\mathscr{A}^{*}
of cardinality at most
\eta
with
S_{\lambda}\to 0
in the weak
∗
-topology. Factorize
(S_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
through
\prod_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}
as

S_{\lambda}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Psi_{\lambda k}\cdot T_{k}

for each
\lambda<\eta
, where
\{(\Psi_{\lambda k})_{k=1}^{m}:\lambda<\eta\}
is a bounded net in

\prod_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}
and
\{T_{k}:k=1,...,m\}\subset\mathscr{A}^{*}
.
Let
\widetilde{\Psi_{\lambda k}}
be the corresponding extensions in
\widetilde{X}
,
and take
m
subnets of
(\widetilde{\Psi_{\lambda k}})_{\lambda<\eta}
(indexed by the same set) with weak
∗
-limit
\widetilde{\Psi_{k}}
in
\mathscr{S}
for each
k=1,...,m.

Denote for simplicity the
m
subnets also by
(\widetilde{\Psi_{\lambda k}})_{\lambda<\eta}
,
k=1,...,m,
and keep
(S_{\lambda})
to denote the corresponding subnet of the original net in the unit ball of
\mathscr{A}^{*}
.
It is then easy to check that
\sum_{k=1}^{m}\widetilde{\Psi_{k}}\cdot T_{k}
is the weak
∗
-limit of the subnet
(S_{\lambda})
, and so it is zero.
Since
\Psi\in T_{1}Z(\mathscr{S})
, using (
7
), this gives

(9)

\begin{split}\lim_{\lambda}\langle\Psi,S_{\lambda}\rangle&=\lim_{\lambda}\left\langle\Psi,\sum_{k=1}^{m}\Psi_{\lambda k}.T_{k}\right\rangle=\lim_{\lambda}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle\Psi.\Psi_{\lambda k},T_{k}\right\rangle\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lim_{\lambda}\left\langle\Psi\widetilde{\Psi_{\lambda k}},T_{k}\right\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle\Psi\widetilde{\Psi_{k}},T_{k}\right\rangle\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left\langle\Psi,\widetilde{\Psi_{k}}\cdot T_{k}\right\rangle=\left\langle\Psi,\sum_{k=1}^{m}\widetilde{\Psi_{k}}\cdot T_{k}\right\rangle=0.\end{split}

The

-Mazur property implies then that

.

(i)

-closed subsemigroup

For the proof of the second theorem we need the following lemma
due essentially to Lau and Ülger in
[
91
]
.
Let

I(\mathscr{A})=\{\Psi\in{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}:\mathscr{A}\Psi\subseteq\mathscr{A}\}.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
\mathscr{A}
is a Banach algebra with a bai
(e_{\alpha})
, then
I(\mathscr{A})\subseteq T_{1}Z({\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}).

When the bai is bounded by
1
,
M_{r}(\mathscr{A})\cong I(\mathscr{A})
.

Proof.

It is clear that
I(\mathscr{A})
is a norm-closed subalgebra of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
. To see that
I(\mathscr{A})\subseteq T_{1}Z({\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}),
let
\Psi\in I(\mathscr{A})
and
(\Phi_{\alpha})
be a net
in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
with weak
∗
-limit
\Phi
.
Then for every
S\in\mathscr{A}^{*}
and
a\in\mathscr{A},
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha}\langle\Psi\Phi_{\alpha},S.a\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\alpha}\langle(a\Psi)\Phi_{\alpha},S\rangle=\lim_{\alpha}\langle(a\Psi),\Phi_{\alpha}\cdot S\rangle=\lim_{\alpha}\langle\Phi_{\alpha},S\cdot(a\Psi)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Phi,S\cdot(a\Psi)\rangle=\langle(a\Psi)\Phi,S\rangle=\langle\Psi\Phi,S.a\rangle,

where the fourth equality is due to the fact that

a\Psi\in\mathscr{A}
.
Therefore,
\Psi
is in the first topological centre of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*},
as wanted.

Next we proceed to identify
I(\mathscr{A})
with
M_{r}(\mathscr{A}).

Note first that
(e_{\alpha})
is a weak
∗
-convergent net in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}

and has the identity in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
as its limit.
To see this, take any weak
∗
-cluster point
E
of
(e_{\alpha})
in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
.
Since
(e_{\alpha})
is a brai,
E
is a right identity in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
,
and so
E
belongs in particular to
I(\mathscr{A})
. Since
(e_{\alpha})
is blai, we also have

Ea=a
for every
a\in\mathscr{A}.
Therefore
E\Psi=\Psi
for every
\Psi\in{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}

since
E\in I(\mathscr{A})\subseteq T_{1}Z({\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}).
Being the identity,
E
must be the unique
weak
∗
-limit of
(e_{\alpha}).

Now if
T\in M_{r}(\mathscr{A})
, then
(Te_{\alpha})
is a net in
\mathscr{A}
which is weak
∗
-convergent in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
.
To see this, take two subnets
(Te_{\beta})
and
(Te_{\gamma})
with weak
∗
-limits

\Psi
and
\Psi^{\prime}
in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
, respectively (this is possible since

(Te_{\alpha})
is bounded in
\mathscr{A}
).

Then again since
(e_{\beta})
is a brai, we have

Te_{\gamma}=\lim_{\beta}T(e_{\gamma}e_{\beta})\quad\text{for each}\quad\gamma,

and so for every
S.a\in{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\Psi,S.a\rangle=\langle E\Psi,S.a\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\gamma}\lim_{\beta}\langle e_{\gamma}Te_{\beta},S.a\rangle=\lim_{\gamma}\lim_{\beta}\langle T(e_{\gamma}e_{\beta}),S.a\rangle

\displaystyle=\lim_{\gamma}\langle Te_{\gamma},S.a\rangle=\langle\Psi^{\prime},S.a\rangle,

as wanted.
Denote the weak
∗
-limit of
(Te_{\alpha})
by
\Psi
(note that
\Psi
depends on
T
).

Consider now for each
\Phi\in I(\mathscr{A})
, the right multiplier
R_{\Phi}\in M_{r}(\mathscr{A})
given by

R_{\Phi}(a)=a\Phi\quad\text{ for every}\quad a\in\mathscr{A}

and consider the linear map

\mathscr{I}:I(\mathscr{A})\to M_{r}(\mathscr{A}),\quad\mathscr{I}(\Phi)=R_{\Phi}.

Since, for every
T\in M_{r}(\mathscr{A})
and
a\in\mathscr{A}
, we have

Ta=\lim_{\alpha}T(ae_{\alpha})=\lim_{\alpha}aTe_{\alpha}=a\Psi=R_{\Psi}(a),

we see that
\mathscr{I}

is surjective (remember
\Psi
depends on
T
). Moreover

\|\mathscr{I}(\Phi)\|\leq\|\Phi\|=\|E\Phi\|=\lim_{\alpha}\|R_{\Phi}e_{\alpha}\|\leq\|R_{\Phi}\|=\|\mathscr{I}(\Phi)\|,

where the second inequality is due to the condition that
(e_{\alpha})
is bounded by
1
.
So when the bai is bounded by
1
,
I(\mathscr{A})
and
M_{r}(\mathscr{A})
are isometrically isomorphic
and
I(\mathscr{A})\subseteq T_{1}Z({\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}).

∎

Definition 4.1

\ell^{1}(\eta)

Remarks 4.2
.

If

is an
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base with a constant

in a normed space
\mathscr{E},
then we note the following:

(i)

For every function

in

This is obtained simply by letting

and noting that

\left|\langle T,\sum_{n=1}^{p}z_{n}a_{\alpha_{n}}\rangle\right|=\left|\sum_{n=1}^{p}z_{n}c_{\alpha_{n}}\right|\leq\|c\|_{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{p}|z_{n}|\leq\|c\|_{\infty}K\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{p}z_{n}a_{\alpha_{n}}\right\|.

.

(ii)

The closed linear span of an

-base is isomorphic to

(iii)

The elements

for every
\alpha<\eta.

-bases. This will enable us to include more

Remarks 4.4

(i)

Clearly every

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\mathscr{E}
be a Banach space with an

-selective base

with constant

Then, for each

Proof.

Let

Since the spans

\Psi x
is also (topologically) left invariant and
regarded as a Borel measure on
G^{LUC}
, it has its support contained in
L
.

In fact, already in 1951, Arens himself showed that the semigroup algebra
\ell^{1}
with convolution
is non-Arens regular. For this, he produced two distinct invariant means
\Phi
and
\Psi
in
{\ell^{1}}^{**}
(see
[
1
]
). This gave him immediately

\Phi\Psi=\Psi\neq\Phi=\Psi\Phi\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi\diamond\Psi=\Phi\neq\Psi=\Psi\diamond\Phi,

which means that

T_{1}Z({\ell^{1}}^{**})\neq{\ell^{1}}^{**}\quad\text{ and }\quad T_{2}Z({\ell^{1}}^{**})\neq{\ell^{1}}^{**}.

In 1957, Day used the same argument in
[
20
]
to show that
L^{1}(G)
is non-Arens regular for many infinite discrete groups, including all Abelian ones.
This same approach was followed by Civin and Yood in their seminal paper
[
12
]
where
L^{1}(G)
was shown to be non-Arens
regular for any infinite locally compact Abelian group.
Their method relied
again on Day’s result when the group is discrete.

Once an infinite F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base is available in
\mathscr{A}
, the theorems in this section unify
all these results in
\mathscr{A}^{**}
,
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}
and
M_{r}(\mathscr{A})^{**}
and many subsemigroups in these algebras.

We start with right cancellation.
We say that a set

in an algebra
is right cancellable if for every nonzero element

in the algebra ,

for some

. When
\Gamma=\{x\}
is right cancellable, we simply say that
x
is right cancellabe.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a Banach algebra with a bai and an F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base
\mathscr{L}
for some infinite cardinal
\eta

Let
X

4.11

\mathscr{S}
be any subsemigroup of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(\mathscr{A})^{*}

\mathscr{A}^{**}

\widetilde{X}\subseteq\mathscr{S}.

(i)

If
\mathscr{L}
is of type 1 or 2, then there are at least
2^{2^{\eta}}
many right cancellable elements in
\mathscr{S}
.

(ii)

If
\mathscr{L}
is of type 3, then there are at least
2^{2^{\eta}}
many right cancellable sets in
\mathscr{S}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}:=\bigcup\limits_{\alpha<\eta}A_{\alpha}\{a_{\alpha}^{k}:k=1,...,m\}
be an F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base in
\mathscr{A}
.
Let
p
and

be two different cofinal ultrafilters in
\beta\eta
together with their corresponding elements
(\Psi_{pk})_{k=1}^{m}
and
(\Psi_{qk})_{k=1}^{m}
in
\prod_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}.

Pick two disjoint members
I
of
p
and
J
of

For each
k=1,...,m,

\Psi_{pk}
is the weak
∗

(a_{\alpha}^{k})_{\alpha\in I}

∗

\alpha<\eta
and

we may take by Remark
4.13
, an operator

such that

Then, for each

is

we obtain the same number of points
\Psi_{pk}
in

for each

This clearly implies that there are at least

many elements

in

We claim first that each of the sets

In the case when

\mathscr{S}

for each

This proves Statement (ii) when

.∎

Theorem 5.2
.

\sum_{n=1}^{N}c_{n}\Psi\Psi_{p_{n}k}=0\quad\text{ for every}\quad k=1,...,m.

Let

Pick pairwise disjoint members

of the ultrafilters

6.
Application I: Group algebra, semigroup algebra and measure algebra

A number of Banach algebras in harmonic analysis
were proved in the past to be strongly Arens irregular and to carry a rich algebraic structure.
In the rest of the paper, we see that F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-bases are indeed available in
almost
all these algebras, and so with the approach developed in the previous sections, we shall unify almost all what is known so far on the subjects since the introduction by Arens of his two products in 1951.
Some further interesting new cases are also obtained.

6.1.
Some definitions

Before we proceed with our first application, we summarize first our definitions and some properties.
For a locally compact group
G
with a fixed left Haar measure, we will be considering two cardinal invariants of
G
.
The
local weight
of
G
is the least cardinality of an open base at the identity of
G
.
The
compact covering number
of
G
is the least cardinality of a compact covering of
G
.

The group algebra
L^{1}(G)
is the Banach space of
all equivalence classes of scalar-valued functions which are integrable with respect to the Haar measure under convolution, where as usual, two functions are equivalent if and only if they differ only on a set of Haar measure zero.
By
L^{\infty}(G)
we understand the Banach dual of
L^{1}(G)
.
This
is the C
∗
-algebra of all essentially bounded locally
measurable functions on
G
.

When
G
is an infinite discrete semigroup, we shall also use
L^{1}(G)
and
L^{\infty}(G)
instead of the usual
\ell^{1}(G)
and
\ell^{\infty}(G)
, respectively.

The C
∗
-algebra

{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)
is the space of
all bounded
right uniformly continuous
functions on
G
.
These are the bounded functions on
G

such that for every
\epsilon>0
,
there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
e
such that

|f(s)-f(t)|<\epsilon\quad\text{whenever}\quad st^{-1}\in U.

These are also the bounded functions on
G
which are left norm continuous in the sense that

s\mapsto{}_{s}{f}:G\to\mathscr{CB}(G)

is continuous, where
\mathscr{CB}(G)
is the space of continuous bounded scalar valued functions equipped with its supremum norm and
{}_{s}f
is the left translate of
f
by
s\in G.

In the literature, this C
∗
-algebra is denoted by
C_{ru}(G)
,
LC(G)
,
{\seis{\mathscr{LC}}}(G)
or
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)
. We are using the latter notation.
As known
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)
can be obtained as
L^{1}(G)\ast L^{\infty}(G)
, and so we have
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(L^{1}(G))={\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)
.

The C
∗
-subalgebra of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)
consisting of functions vanishing at infinity will be denoted by
C_{0}(G)
.

The C*-subalgebra of
L^{\infty}(G)
consisting of functions vanishing at infinity will be denoted by
L_{0}^{\infty}(G),

that is,

L_{0}^{\infty}(G)=\{f\in L^{\infty}(G):\|f\|_{G\setminus K}\to 0\;\text{as}\;K\to G\},

where
\|f\|_{G\setminus K}
is the essential supremum norm of
f
on
G\setminus K
.

The space

M(G)
is the usual measure algebra consisting of
bounded Radon measures on
G
.
The measure algebra
M(G)
is the dual space of

C_{0}(G)
,
and by the well-known Wendel’s theorem,
M(G)
is the multiplier algebra of
L^{1}(G)
, that is
M(L^{1}(G))=M(G)
.

6.2.
F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-bases

We start with the group algebra of a non-compact locally compact group.
Variants of the construction below can be found in many articles dealing with the topological centre and the algebraic structure of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)^{*}
and
L^{1}(G)^{**}
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
G
be a non-compact locally compact group with a compact covering
\eta
. Then the group algebra
L^{1}(G)

has an F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base of type 2.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be a fixed left Haar measure and
\Delta
be the modular function on
G.

Let

\{K_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha<\eta}
be a compact covering of
G
made of symmetric subsets of
G
with nonempty interiors and with
\lambda(K_{\alpha})\leq 1
for each
\alpha
.
Then put

V_{\alpha}=\bigcup\limits_{\beta<\alpha}K_{\beta}\quad\text{for each}\quad\alpha<\eta,

and observe that
(V_{\alpha})_{\alpha<\eta}
is an increasing family of symmetric subsets of
G

with nonempty interiors and
\kappa(V_{\alpha})\leq\alpha
for each
\alpha<\eta.

Now choose inductively elements
x_{\alpha}\in G
such that

(23)

({V_{\alpha}}x_{\alpha})\cap(V_{\beta}x_{\beta})=\varnothing\quad\text{ when }\quad\alpha\neq\beta.

This is possible because, for every
\alpha<\eta,

\kappa\left(V_{\alpha}(\bigcup_{\beta<\alpha}{V_{\beta}}x_{\beta})\right)\leq\alpha.

Let now, for each
\alpha<\eta,

A_{\alpha}
be the set of all continuous positive functions
\varphi
with
compact supports contained in
V_{\alpha}

and with
\|\varphi\|_{1}=1.

We check that the conditions in Definition
4.8
are satisfied so that

(24)

\mathscr{L}=\bigcup_{\alpha<\eta}(A_{\alpha}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha}})

is indeed an F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base of type 2 for
L^{1}(G)
.

First note that the closed linear span of the set
A_{\eta}
is
L^{1}(G).
Since the functions in
A_{\alpha}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha}}

are supported in
V_{\alpha}x_{\alpha},
it is also clear that the intersection of the linear spans of each pair of sets
A_{\alpha}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha}}
is zero.
Let then
\varphi
be any function in the linear span of
\mathscr{L}

of the form

\varphi=\sum_{n=1}^{p}\varphi_{n}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}},

where each
\varphi_{n}
(
n=1,...,p
) is in the linear span of
A_{\alpha_{n}}
.

Then, for each
n=1,...,p
,

\varphi_{n}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}}=\Delta(x_{\alpha_{n}}^{-1})(\varphi_{n})_{x_{\alpha_{n}}^{-1}}

(the right translate of
\varphi_{n}
by
{x_{\alpha_{n}}^{-1}}),
and so

(25)

\begin{split}\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{p}\varphi_{n}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}}\right\|_{1}&=\int_{G}\left|\sum_{n=1}^{p}\varphi_{n}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}}\right|d\lambda=\int_{G}\sum_{n=1}^{p}\left|\varphi_{n}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}}\right|d\lambda\\
&=\sum_{n=1}^{p}\|\varphi_{n}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}}\|_{1}=\sum_{n=1}^{p}\|\varphi_{n}\|_{1},\end{split}

where the second equality is due to the fact that the supports of the functions
\varphi_{n}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}}
are disjoint. The third equality shows that
\mathscr{L}
is selective (with
K_{1}=1
) while the last equality shows the validity of Condition (
F2
) in Definition
4.8
with
K_{2}=1
. For, when
p=1
, the identity (
25
) simply says that

\left\|\varphi\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha}}\right\|_{1}=\|\varphi\|_{1}\quad\text{ for every}\quad\varphi\in\langle A_{\alpha}\rangle.

Note that Condition (
f3
) (and so Condition (
F3
) in Definition
4.9
) is also valid here with
m=1
,
a_{\alpha}
and
b_{\alpha}
may be simply taken as
\delta_{x_{\alpha}}
and
\delta_{x_{\alpha}^{-1}}
, respectively, for every
\alpha<\eta
.

This ends the proof for
L^{1}(G)
.
∎

The same construction gives F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-bases when
G
is a discrete semigroup which respects certain cancellation properties, where
\eta
is the cardinality of
G.

Recall first that a semigroup
G
is called
weakly cancellative
if the sets

s^{-1}t=\{u\in G:su=t\}\quad\text{and}\quad ts^{-1}=\{u\in G:us=t\}

are finite for every
s,t\in G
. The semigroup
G
is
left (right) cancellative

when
s^{-1}t
(
ts^{-1}
, respectively ) has at most one element.
We shall use the notations

s^{-1}B=\{u\in G:su\in B\}\quad\text{ and}\quad A^{-1}B=\bigcup_{s\in A}s^{-1}B,

where
s\in G
and
A,B\subseteq G
. The sets
Bs^{-1}
and
BA^{-1}

are defined similarly.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
G
be an infinite discrete weakly cancellative, right cancellative semigroup
with cardinality

\eta
. Then the semigroup algebra
\ell^{1}(G)

has an F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base of type 1.

Proof.

Enumerate
G
as
G=\{s_{\alpha}:\alpha<\eta\}
, and let for each
\alpha<\eta,

G_{\alpha}=\{s_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\}.

Then

\{G_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha<\eta}
is an increasing cover of
G
with

|G_{\alpha}|=\alpha
for every
\alpha<\eta.
Collect then by induction a set
\{x_{\alpha}:\alpha<\eta\}
such that

(26)

G_{\alpha}x_{\alpha}\cap G_{\beta}x_{\beta}=\varnothing\quad\text{for every}\quad\alpha\neq\beta.

This is possible since
G

is weakly left cancellative, and so

|G_{\alpha}^{-1}G_{\beta}|\leq\max\{\alpha,\beta\}<\eta.

Then, let for each
\alpha<\eta,

A_{\alpha}
consist of all point-mass measures
\delta_{x}
with
x\in G_{\alpha},
i.e.,

A_{\alpha}=\{\delta_{s_{\beta}}:\beta<\alpha\}.

Now by construction,
the linear spans of each pair of sets
A_{\alpha}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha}}
have zero intersection.
Moreover, since
G
is right cancellative,
xx_{\alpha}\neq yx_{\alpha}
for every
x\neq y
in
G
and
\alpha<\eta,
and so
the supports of the point measures
\delta_{x}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha}}
(that is
\{xx_{\alpha}\}
) are disjoint for every
\alpha<\eta

and
x\in A_{\alpha}
.
So the elements
\delta_{x}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha}}
in
\mathcal{L}
are all distinct for distinct
pairs
(x,\alpha)\in G_{\alpha}\times\eta.

It is then easy to see that

(27)

\mathscr{L}=\bigcup_{\alpha<\eta}(A_{\alpha}\ast\delta_{x_{\alpha}})

is in fact an
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base
in
\ell^{1}(G)
(with
K=1
), and so it is an F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base of type 1 in
\ell^{1}(G)
.
∎

6.3.
Algebraic structure

Recall first the following facts.

(i)

In
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)^{*}
,
non-trivial left ideals of finite dimensions exist if and only if
G
is amenable,
they are generated by elements defined by mixing topological left invariant means (TIMs) together with coefficients of finite dimensional representations of
G
(see
[
32
]
and
[
43
]
). A TIM is a positive linear functional
\Psi
in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)^{*}
or
L^{1}(G)^{**}
with
\langle\Psi,1\rangle=1

and

(28)

a\Psi=\langle 1,a\rangle\Psi=\left(\int_{G}a(x)d\lambda\right)\Psi\quad\text{for every}\quad a\in L^{1}(G).

Since there is one TIM (the left Haar measure) when
G
is compact, and there are
2^{2^{\eta}}
TIMs in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)^{*}
when
G
is non-compact and amenable, at least
\max\{|\widehat{G}_{F}|,2^{2^{\eta}}\}
many distinct left ideals of finite dimension exit in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)^{*}
when
G
is infinite and amenable.
Here
\eta
is the compact covering of
G
and
\widehat{G}_{F}
is the set of all finite-dimensional
representations of
G
.

(ii)

In
L^{1}(G)^{**}
, there are two types of left ideals of finite dimension (and the mixture of both). The first type exists whenever
G
is not discrete. They are generated by elements
\Psi
in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)^{\perp}
. Since
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)=L^{\infty}(G)\cdot L^{1}(G)=L^{1}(G)\ast L^{\infty}(G)
, these elements are easily seen to satisfy
L^{1}(G)^{**}\Psi=\{0\}
.
Since these elements may be seen as the difference of two distinct right identities in
L^{1}(G)^{**}
, and since there are at least
2^{2^{\omega}}
right identities in
L^{1}(G)^{**}
(
[
73
]
, see also
[
51
, Remak 4.6.2(3)]
),
we see that the number of left ideals of finite dimension in
L^{1}(G)^{**}
is
at least
2^{2^{\omega}}
for any non-discrete group.
The second type of finite-dimensional left ideals (which are not generated by annihilators of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)
) exists if and only if
G
is amenable; they are generated as in
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)^{*}

by elements defined by mixing TIMs together with coefficients of finite dimensional representations of
G
(see
[
32
]
and
[
43
]
). Since the number of TIMs in
L^{1}(G)^{**}
is
1
when
G
is compact and
2^{2^{\eta}}
when
G
is non-compact and amenable, we see that the number of finite-dimensional left ideals in
L^{1}(G)^{**}
is at least equal to
\max\{2^{2^{w}},|\widehat{G}_{F}|,2^{2^{\eta}}\}
for any infinite locally compact group.
For more details, see
[
32
]
and
[
43
]
.

(iii)

In
M(G)^{**}
, A TIM is a positive linear functional
\Psi\in M(G)^{**}
satisfying the identity (
28
) for every
a\in M(G)
.
TIMs exist in

if and only if
G
is amenable, see for example
[
79
, Corollary 4.3]
,
[
18
, Propositions 1.21 and 5.10]
,
or Proposition 1.4 in our recent article
[
42
]
.
So again, arguing as for
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)^{*}
in
[
32
]
or applying the general theorem proved in
[
43
, Theorem 2.8]
, non-trivial left ideals of finite dimensions exist in
M(G)^{**}
if and only if
G
is amenable,
they are generated by elements defined by mixing topological left invariant means (TIMs) together with coefficients of finite dimensional representations of
G
.
Since the number of TIMs in
M(G)^{**}
is
1
when
G
is compact and at least
2^{2^{\eta}}

when
G
is non-compact and amenable, we deduce that at least
\max\{|\widehat{G}_{F}|,2^{2^{\eta}}\}
many distinct left ideals of finite dimension exit in

when

is infinite and amenable.

7.
Application II: Weighted group, semigroup and measure algebras

Our approach applies also to the weighted (semi)group algebra and the weighted measure algebra when the weight is diagonally bounded.

7.1.
Some definitions

Recall that a
weight
on a semigroup
G
(with a topology) is a continuous function

w:G\to(0,\infty)
which is submultiplicative, that is,

w(st)\leq w(s)w(t)\quad\text{for every}\quad s,t\in G.

If
G
is a group with identity
e
, we shall assume that
w(e)=1.

The weight function
w
is called

diagonally bounded
on
G
if there exists
K>0
such that

w(s)w(t)\leq Kw(st)\quad\text{for every}\quad s,t\in G.

For the analogues of the theorems proved in the previous section,
we recall the following weighted spaces:

•

L^{\infty}(G,w^{-1})
consists of all Borel measurable functions
S
on
G
such that

w^{-1}S\in L^{\infty}(G)
.

•

L_{0}^{\infty}(G,w^{-1})
is the subspace of those functions
S\in L^{\infty}(G,w^{-1})
such that

w^{-1}S\in L_{0}^{\infty}(G)
.

•

{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G,w^{-1})
is the subspace of those functions
S\in L^{\infty}(G,w^{-1})
such that

w^{-1}S\in{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G)
.

•

C_{0}(G,w^{-1})
is the subspace of those functions
S\in{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G,w^{-1})
such that

w^{-1}S\in C_{0}(G)
.

The norm of
S
in these weighted spaces is given by
\|w^{-1}S\|
so that
the space and its weighted space are isometric.

•

The weighted group algebra
L^{1}(G,w)

consists of all Borel measurable functions
a
on
G
such that
wa\in L^{1}(G).

•

The weighted semigroup algebra
\ell^{1}(G,w)
is defined in the same way when
G
is a discrete semigroup.

•

The weighted measure algebra
M(G,w)
consists of all regular compacted-Borel measures
a
on
G

such that
wa\in M(G).

The norm of
a
in these weighted spaces is given by
\|wa\|
, so that
the space and its weighted space are isometric. The definition of

has been rectified recently by Stokke in
[

]
by adding the adjective ”compacted”.

As it is the case when

both

and

are
Banach algebras under convolution since

is submultiplicative,
and so
the Arens products may be defined on

and

Moreover, as known

is a closed two-sided ideal
of
M(G,w)
. The weighted Wendel’s theorem is also true, so that

is the multiplier algebra of

, see
[

, Lemma 2.3]
and
[

, Theorem 3.2]
.

The spaces

and
L^{1}(G,w)^{*}
are identified by the usual dual pairing. And since

{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G,w^{-1})=L^{\infty}(G,w^{-1})\cdot L^{1}(G,w)

(see
[
60
]
for the thorem and
[

for the corrected proof), the actions of
{\seis{\mathscr{LUC}}}(G,w^{-1})^{*}
on
L^{\infty}(G,w^{-1})

and

may be defined as previously.

The Banach space decompositions used in the previous section are true
in the weighted situation, the proofs are as in
[

,

Theorem 7.1

Let

Let

and let

We claim that

is an F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base in

) in Example

is true, the rest is clear and follows as in Example

.

Note first that for every

we have

\frac{\varphi}{w}\ast\frac{\delta_{x_{\alpha}}}{w(x_{\alpha})}=\frac{1}{w(x_{\alpha})}{\Delta({x_{\alpha}^{-1}})\left(\frac{\varphi}{w}\right)_{x_{\alpha}^{-1}}}\quad(\text{the right translate of}\;\frac{\varphi}{w}\;\text{by}\;x_{\alpha}^{-1}).

Let now

be a function in the closed linear span of
\mathscr{L}
. Then
\varphi
has
the form

\varphi=\sum_{n=1}^{p}\frac{\varphi_{n}}{w}\ast\frac{\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}}}{w(x_{\alpha_{n}})},

where each

(

A_{\alpha_{n}}
.
Accordingly,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{p}\left(\frac{\varphi_{n}}{w}\ast\frac{\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}}}{w(x_{\alpha_{n}})}\right)\right\|_{1,w}=

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{n=1}^{p}w\left(\frac{\varphi_{n}}{w}\ast\frac{\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}}}{w(x_{\alpha_{n}})}\right)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{p}\int_{G}w\left|\left(\frac{\varphi_{n}}{w}\ast\frac{\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}}}{w(x_{\alpha_{n}})}\right)\right|d\lambda

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{p}\left\|\frac{\varphi_{n}}{w}\ast\frac{\delta_{x_{\alpha_{n}}}}{w(x_{\alpha_{n}})})\right\|_{1,w}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{p}\int_{G}\left|w\frac{1}{w(x_{\alpha_{n}})}{\Delta({x_{\alpha_{n}}^{-1}})\left(\frac{\varphi_{n}}{w}\right)_{x_{\alpha_{n}}^{-1}}}\right|d\lambda

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{p}\int_{G}\left|\frac{w(yx_{\alpha_{n}})}{w(y)w(x_{\alpha_{n}})}\varphi_{n}(y)\right|d\lambda(y)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=1}^{p}\int_{G}\frac{1}{K}|\varphi_{n}(y)|d\lambda(y)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{n=1}^{p}\left\|\frac{\varphi_{n}}{w}\right\|_{1,w},

where as in Theorem
6.1
, the second equality follows from the fact that the functions under the sum have disjoint supports. The third equality proves then that
\mathscr{L}
is selective. The inequality is due to the diagonal boundedness of
w,
and checks the validity of Condition
(
F2
) in Definition
4.8
. For, this inequality simply becomes

K\left\|\frac{\varphi}{w}\ast\frac{\delta_{x_{\alpha}}}{w(x_{\alpha})}\right\|_{1,w}\geq\left\|\frac{\varphi}{w}\right\|_{1,w}

when

.
As in the previous example, note that Condition (
f3
) (and so Condition (
F3
) in Definition

and

∎

Lemma 8.4

(Hu)
.

Let

be a
\sigma
-compact non-metrizable locally compact group with unit element

and local weight
\eta,

and let

be an open base at

.
Then there exists a decreasing family
(N_{\alpha})_{0\leq\alpha\leq\eta}

of normal subgroups of

(that is,

whenever

) such that

(i)

N_{0}=G
and
N_{\eta}=\{e\}
,

(ii)

N_{\alpha+1}\subset N_{\alpha}\cap O_{\alpha}
for all

(iii)

is compact for each
\alpha>0
,

(iv)

is metrizable but

\alpha<\eta
,

(v)

is
\eta

.

\widehat{G}
containing
\eta
many representations of a fixed dimension

. For,
since each
\pi_{\alpha}
has finite dimension, we may define

Theorem 8.5
.

Let
G
be a compact non-metrizable group with a local weight
\eta

\eta

has an F

-base of type 3 with constants

and

Proof.

Let

8.4.
F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-bases for compact connected groups

The results on infinite products of compact groups seen in the previous subsection can be transferred to compact connected groups

having a certain infinite ”dimension”. This will be our aim in the present subsection. The arguments are based on some structure theorems, where
G
is sandwiched between two groups having the form of the groups studied in the previous subsection (see
[

).

8.4.1.
The
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
-property.

We shall develop in this subsection the arguments used in the proof of Theorem
8.7

to include other compact groups having the
\mathcal{Z}
-property.
For this we introduce another property of a family of representations of
G
called the
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
-property of level
\eta

for some infinite cardinal
\eta
, as well as a new definition of a d-rank of a group.
The
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
-property is easier to handle than the
\mathcal{Z}
-property, while the d-rank of a group
is a kind of a dimension of a group based on which the required representations with the
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
-property are constructed.
We shall see then that all compact connected groups with infinite d-rank have the
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
-property.

Since
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
-property of level
\eta
yields
\mathcal{Z}
-property of level
\eta
, and this type of groups
satisfies Condition (
30
) in Lemma
8.3
(i), their Fourier algebras will
contain an F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-base of type 2.

As with the previous cases, this will enable us to obtain in the last subsection
9.1
the strong Arens irregularity not only of
A(G)

when

belongs to this class of groups, but of

where

is any locally compact amenable group. The centre of

will be seen to be
the Fourier-Stieltjes

We shall also deduce some rich algebraic structures in the algebras related to these groups.

Definition 8.9
.

Let
G
be a locally compact group with its dual enumerated as
\widehat{G}=\left\{\sigma_{\alpha}\colon\alpha<\eta\right\}
. We say
that

has the
\mathcal{Z^{\prime}}
-property
of level

when
\widehat{G}
contains a subset

such that if

L_{\alpha}=\bigcap_{\beta<\alpha}(\ker\sigma_{\beta}\cap\ker\pi_{\beta})\quad\text{for every}\quad\alpha<\eta,

then

G=L_{\alpha}\ker\pi_{\alpha}\quad\text{ for every}\quad\alpha<\eta.

Notation 8.10
.

For a locally compact group
G
with a subgroup
L
,
let

(i)

A_{L}(G)=\{\varphi\in A(G):\varphi(nx)=\varphi(x)\quad\text{for every}\quad x\in G,n\in L\}.

(ii)

R_{L}:A(G)\to A(L)
be the restriction map.

(iii)

Q_{L}:A(G/L)\to A_{L}(G)
be given by

, when

is normal and

is the quotient homomorphism.

Lemma 8.11
.

Let
G
be a compact group such that

, where

and

are normal subgroups of

with
L
compact and
M
closed. Then, for every

Proof.

Consider the following diagram

\begin{CD}A(G/L)@>{Q_{L}}>{}>A_{L}(G)\\
@V{}V{j}V@V{}V{R_{M}}V\\

Then

and

are isometric isomorphisms (see
[

, Theorem 2.4.2]
,
while the map

is the isometric isomorphism induced by the topological isomorphism

Since the diagram commutes,

has to be an isometric isomorphism as well.
∎

8.4.2.
d-rank of a compact group

We proceed now to prove that the
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
-property is available for compact connected groups with an infinite dimension in the sense of Definition
8.14
below.
Since our task concerns elements picked from the dual of
G
, we shall call our dimension of
G
the dual rank of
G
.
When the dual rank is infinite, we shall see that it coincides with the local weight
\eta
of the group.
So by Lemma
8.3
, for these groups there are F
\ell^{1}(\eta)
-bases of type
2
in
A(G)
. With these bases, as before, we deduce the strong Arens irregularity as well as the algebraic structure in the algebras.
Recall that when the group is abelian,
A(G)
is strongly Arens irregular independently the dual rank of the group since we know from Section
6
that
L^{1}(\widehat{G})
is strongly Arens irregular for any locally compact group
G.

Before we give the precise definition of a dual rank of a compact group, we recall the following facts.

The torsion-free rank
r_{0}(H)
of an abelian discrete group
H
is the cardinality of a maximal independent subset of elements in
H
whose order is infinite. So if
t(H)
denotes the torsion subgroup of
H
, then
r_{0}(H)
is the maximal number of independent elements in
H/t(H)
and
r_{0}(H)=r_{0}(H/t(H)),
see

[
63
, A13]
. Note that if the quotient
H/t(H)
is countable, then
r_{0}(H)
may be either finite or countable.
If the quotient
H/t(H)
is uncountable, then
r_{0}(H)
is uncountable and in fact
r_{0}(H)=|H/t(H)|
.

There are several notions of dimension of a topological space. The main ones coincide in some
instances such as separable metrizable spaces or compact abelian groups, see the discussion at
[
66
, 8.25]
or
[
25
, Chapter 7]
.
We take that given in
[
63
, Definition 3.11]
.
With this definition,
the dimension
\dim G
of a compact abelian group
G
is the same as the torsion-free rank of its dual group
\widehat{G}
, see
[
63
, Theorem 24.28]
. So we may define the dual rank of a
compact abelian group

G
as

\mathrm{d-rank}(G)=\dim G=r_{0}(\widehat{G}).

When
G
is a connected compact abelian group, its dual group
\widehat{G}
is torsion free, see for example
[
63
, Theorem 24.25]
, and
so
\widehat{G}
contains a subgroup isomorphic to
\bigoplus_{\alpha<r_{0}(\widehat{G})}\mathbb{Z}
and it is contained in its divisible hull

\bigoplus_{\alpha<r_{0}(\widehat{G})}\mathbb{Q}
(the divisible hull being also torsion free, has the stated form, see for example the proof of
[
63
, Theorem A. 14]
).
Hence, the following observation follows.

(32)

\begin{split}&\widehat{G}\;\text{ is countable (i.e.,}\;G\text{is metrizable and}\;w(G)=w)\;\text{if and only if}\\
&r_{0}(\widehat{G})\leq w.\;\text{ Otherwise,}\;r_{0}(\widehat{G})=|\widehat{G}|=w(G).\end{split}

Hence for any connected compact abelian group, we have the three following cases

(33)

\begin{split}\mathrm{d-rank}(G)&=r_{0}(\widehat{G})\quad\text{ when}\;w(G)=w\;\text{and}\;r_{0}(\widehat{G})\quad\text{is finite},\\
\mathrm{d-rank}(G)&=r_{0}(\widehat{G})=|\widehat{G}|=w(G)\quad\text{ when}\;r_{0}(\widehat{G})=w=w(G),\\
\mathrm{d-rank}(G)&=r_{0}(\widehat{G})=|\widehat{G}|=w(G),\;\text{when}\;w(G)>w,\end{split}

where
w(G)
is the local weight of
G
.

We need also to recall the commutator
G^{\prime}
of a topological group
G.

This is the subgroup of
G
generated by the commutators
sts^{-1}t^{-1}
of all elements
s,t\in G.

The subgroup
G^{\prime}
is normal but it is not in general closed in
G
.
The quotient
G/N
of
G
by any normal subgroup
N
is abelian if and only if
G^{\prime}\subseteq N;
in particular
G/G^{\prime}
is abelian.
A compact connected group is said to be semisimple when
G=G^{\prime}.

When
G
is a compact connected group, the commutator
G^{\prime}
of
G
is closed and connected,
[
66
, Theorem 9.2]
. There is actually a family of simple simply connected compact Lie groups
\{S_{j}:j\in J\}

such that
G^{\prime}
is a quotient of the product
\prod_{j\in J}S_{j}
,
[
66
, Theorem 9.19]
. The cardinal card
J
is then an isomorphy invariant of
G
, denoted as
\aleph(G)
, see
[
66
, Page 496 or 469]
.

Now every compact connected group
G
is a semidirect product of
G^{\prime}
and the compact connected abelian group
G/G^{\prime}
(see
[
66
, Theorem 9.39]
), and so
G
is homeomorphic to
G^{\prime}\times G/G^{\prime}
.
So we may define the dual rank of
G
of a compact connected group as

\mathrm{d\!-\!rank}(G)=\aleph(G)+r_{0}(\widehat{G/G^{\prime}}).

Finally, recall from with
[
63
, Theorem 3.12]
that the dimension
\dim G
of a totally disconnected space is
0
.
Accordingly, since every compact group
G
is homeomorphic to
G_{0}\times G/G_{0}
(
[
66
, Theorem 10.38]
), where
G_{0}
is the connected component of
G
,
we may define the dual rank of a compact group
G
as the dual rank of its connected component. Thus, we have

Definition 8.14
.

The
dual rank
of a compact group
G
is given by

\mathrm{d\!-\!rank}(G)=\mathrm{d\!-\!rank}(G_{0})=\aleph(G_{0})+r_{0}(\widehat{G_{0}/G_{0}^{\prime}}),

where
G_{0}
denotes the connected component of
G
and
\aleph(G_{0})
is the isomorphy invariant as defined above.

Remarks 8.15
.

We remark here some immediate consequences of the definition of the dual rank of a compact group
G
.

(i)

When
G
is abelian,
G^{\prime}
is trivial, and so
\mathrm{d\!-\!rank}(G)=r_{0}(\widehat{G_{0}}).

(ii)

When
G
is connected, it follows from
[
66
, Theorem 9.55 and Theorem 9.52]

that
\mathrm{d\!-\!rank}(G)
is finite if and only if
\dim G
is finite. Indeed, these two theorems yield the
following equivalent statements

(a)

\mathrm{d\!-\!rank}(G)=\aleph(G)+r_{0}(\widehat{G/G^{\prime}})<\infty.

(b)

G^{\prime}
s a compact Lie group and
G/G^{\prime}
is a finite dimensional compact abelian
group.

(c)

\dim G<\infty.

(iii)

When
G

Lemma 8.17

Since

\begin{split}w(G)&=w(G^{\prime})+w(G/G^{\prime})\\

Lemma 8.19
.

Let
G
be a compact abelian group and let

be a totally disconnected subgroup of

.
If

has finite dual rank, then so has

. In other words, if

.

Proof.

If

is infinite, we can find an infinite set
\{\chi_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset\widehat{G}
consisting of independent characters with
\chi_{n}^{k}\neq 1
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Since
\widehat{G/H}
is isomorphic to
H^{\perp}=\{\chi\in\widehat{G}\colon\chi(H)=\{1\}\}
and
r_{0}(\widehat{G/H})
is finite, we see that
\langle\chi_{n}\rangle\cap H^{\perp}=\{1\}
for all but finitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Pick
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\langle\chi_{n_{0}}\rangle\cap H^{\perp}=\{1\}
.
Then
\chi_{n_{0}}^{k}\notin H^{\perp}
for any

, hence
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\chi_{n_{0}}\vphantom{|}\right|_{{}_{H}}}^{k}\neq 1
for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Having a character of infinite order,

cannot be totally disconnected (see
[
63
, Theorem 24.26 or Corollary 24.18]
). We conclude that

must be finite.
∎

Lemma 8.20
.

Let
G
be a compact connected group and let
H
be a totally disconnected normal subgroup of
G
.
Then

(i)

w(G/H)=w(G)
.

(ii)

If
G/H
has a finite dual rank, then so has
G
.

Proof.

The first assertion follows from
[
66
, Proposition 12.26]
.

We divide the proof of the second assertion into two cases:

Case I:
G
is semisimple, i.e.,
G^{\prime}=G
. In this case, the dual rank of
G
is simply

\mathrm{d\!-\!rank}(G)=\aleph(G)
.
Let
\widetilde{G}=\prod_{j\in J}S_{j}
be the simply connected semisimple compact group whose quotient is

G
by the map
\pi_{\mbox{\tiny$G$}}:\widetilde{G}\to G^{\prime}=G
.
Then

G/H
is also a quotient of
\prod_{j\in J}S_{j}
by the map
q\circ\pi_{\mbox{\tiny$G$}}

where
q:G\to G/H
is the quotient map.
Since
H
is totally disconnected, we see that
\ker(q\circ\pi_{\mbox{\tiny$G$}})
is totally disconnected.
So, by the uniqueness theorem proved in
[
66
, Theorem 9.19]
(i), we see the family of simple Lie groups
\{S_{j}:j\in J\}
defining
\aleph(G)
is the same as the one defining
\aleph(G/H)
. Therefore
\aleph(G)=\aleph(G/H),
and so the claim is clear.

Case II:
G
is an arbitrary compact connected group
. In this case

\mathrm{d\!-\!rank}(G)=\aleph(G)+r_{0}(\widehat{G/G^{\prime}}).

Then
G=Z(G)G^{\prime}
, since
G=Z_{0}(G)G^{\prime}
by
[
66
, Theorem 9.24]
.
Since every totally disconnected normal subgroup of a connected group is central (see for instance
[
66
, Proposition A4.27 or Lemma 6.13]
), we see that
H
is central, i.e.,
H\subset Z(G)
.
Therefore we can write
G/H
as

G/H=(Z(G)G^{\prime})/H=(Z(G)/H)(G^{\prime}H/H).

It follows that each of the groups
(Z(G)/H)
and
(G^{\prime}H/H)
has a finite dual rank, since
the group
G/H
has a finite dual rank by assumption.
On one hand, using Lemma
8.19
, we see that
Z(G)
has a finite dual rank, i.e.,
r_{0}(\widehat{Z(G)})
is finite. And because
G/G^{\prime}
is a quotient of
Z(G)
since it is topologically isomorphic to
Z(G)/(G^{\prime}\cap Z(G))
by the second isomorphism theorem, we deduce that
r_{0}(\widehat{G/G^{\prime}})\leq r_{0}(\widehat{Z(G)})
, and so
r_{0}(\widehat{G/G^{\prime}})
is finite.

On the other hand, as
G^{\prime}H/H
is topologically isomorphic to
G^{\prime}/(H\cap G^{\prime})
, it follows
that
G^{\prime}/(H\cap G^{\prime})
has a finite dual rank, and so Case I implies that
G^{\prime}
has a finite dual rank, i.e.,
\aleph(G)
is finite.
Accordingly,
\mathrm{d\!-\!rank}(G)=\aleph(G)+r_{0}(\widehat{G/G^{\prime}})
is finite, as wanted.
∎

Lemma 8.21
.

Every compact connected group with an infinite dual rank
\eta
has the
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
-property of level

By Lemma
8.17
