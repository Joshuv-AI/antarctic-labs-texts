The disturbance of intestinal microbiome caused by the novel duck reovirus infection in Cherry Valley ducklings can induce intestinal damage
Authors: Lijie Li, Baishi Lei, Wuchao Zhang, Weizhu Wang, Chuanchuan Shang, Yibin Hu, Kuan Zhao, Wanzhe Yuan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNovel duck reovirus disease is an infectious disease mainly caused by novel duck reovirus (NDRV), which is characterized by spleen necrosis and persistent diarrhea in ducks. However, the pathogenic mechanism of NDRV infection in Cherry Valley ducks remains unclear. To investigate the distribution of NDRV in the intestines of Cherry Valley ducks, intestinal morphogenesis, intestinal permeability, inflammatory cytokines, and the expression of tight junction proteins (TJPs), we introduced NDRV via intramuscular infection. The diversity and composition of ileum flora and content of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) were analyzed using Illumina MiSeq sequencing. The relationship between changes in the intestinal microbial community and intestinal damage in Cherry Valley ducks infected with NDRV was also assessed to offer new insights into the pathogenesis of NDRV and intestinal flora composition. The results showed that intestinal inflammation and barrier dysfunction occurred following NDRV infection. Additionally, a significant reduction in dominant bacterial species and a decrease in SCFA content within the intestinal microbiota led to weakened colonization resistance and the enrichment of opportunistic pathogens, exacerbating intestinal damage post-NDRV infection. Notably, TJPs and inflammatory cytokine disruptions were linked to a decline in SCFA-producing bacteria and an accumulation of pathogenic bacteria. In summary, changes in the ileum intestinal flora and disruptions to the intestinal barrier were associated with NDRV infection. Consequently, disturbances in intestinal flora caused by NDRV infection can lead to intestinal damage. These findings may offer us a new perspective, targeting the gut microbiota to better understand the progression of NDRV disease and investigate its underlying pathogenesis.Key words: Novel duck reovirus, Gut microbiome dysbiosis, Intestinal barrier dysfunction, Short-chain fatty acids, Ileum

Introduction

The gut microbiota is now recognized as the largest symbiotic ecosystem within the host and has been shown in several studies to play an essential role in maintaining gut homeostasis (Kim et al., 2016). The host gut contains hundreds of millions of bacterial species, including both pathogenic and non-pathogenic species, as well as commensal and opportunistic bacteria. However, an imbalance between commensal and pathogenic bacteria can lead to ecological imbalances in the gut microbiome (Ahrne and Hagslatt, 2011; Sartor and Wu, 2017; Imhann et al., 2018). Commensal bacteria are crucial in regulating the maturation of the mucosal immune system, while pathogenic bacteria can cause immune dysfunction, potentially leading to disease development (Zyrek et al., 2007; Glavan et al., 2016). Lymph nodes, lamina propria, and epithelial cells make up the intestinal mucosal immune system, which serves as a protective barrier for intestinal integrity, preventing pathogen invasion (Alam and Neish, 2018). The composition of the gut microbiota is regulated by the normal mucosal immune system (Shi et al., 2017). However, inflammation triggered by an abnormal immune response can disrupt the balance of gut bacteria (Shi et al., 2017). Significant changes in the microbiome are key characteristics of intestinal mucosal infections, which can result in intestinal diseases (Spor et al., 2011). It has also been reported that infections can disturb the ecological equilibrium between the host and its gut bacteria. Given the potential relationships between the gut microbiota and viral infections, as well as the important roles of the gut microbiota in regulating immunity and inflammatory diseases (Kamada et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2015), this study focused on investigating the changes in gut microbiome composition following novel duck reovirus (NDRV) infection and the interactions between gut microbes and intestinal damage.

NDRV is a highly pathogenic strain of waterfowl from the Reoviridae family, known to cause tissue damage in these waterfowl (Zhang et al., 2021). First identified in Fujian, China, in 2005, NDRV has had a significant economic impact on the duck farming industry (Chen et al., 2019). Cherry Valley ducks, mule ducks, and Pekin ducks are particularly susceptible to this virus, with infected ducks exhibiting symptoms such as watery diarrhea, liver hemorrhage, and spleen necrosis (Chen et al., 2012). The emergence of NDRV infections has resulted in substantial economic losses to duck farming in China. Notably, some studies have reported diarrhea and abnormal cecum intestinal flora composition in NDRV-infected Cherry Valley ducklings. However, the cause of the gastrointestinal symptoms associated with NDRV infection remains unknown. Additionally, there are no studies on how NDRV affects the ileum gut microbiota and the health of the gut.

To date, there have been no studies on the ileum flora of NDRV-infected Cherry Valley ducklings. Therefore, in this study, we explored the characteristics of bacterial community composition in the ileum of ducklings following viral infection, assessing whether there is a potential association between intestinal flora and the intestinal epithelial barrier during NDRV infection. This study provides a preliminary foundation for understanding the interactions between intestinal microorganisms and intestinal damage following NDRV infection in ducklings.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All experiments involving ducks were conducted in accordance with the principles of biosafety and bioethics, following the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee of Hebei Agricultural University, China.

Animals and virus

Cherry Valley ducks were purchased from a duck farm in Baoding, China. The NDRV virus was preserved in our laboratory, with a virus titer of 107.28 TCID50/100 µL. The process for inoculating DEF cells with the virus is as follows: the NDRV virus suspension was adsorbed in DEF cells for 2 h, followed by the removal of the virus solution, two washes with PBS, addition of 2% FBS-DMEM, and further incubation at 37°C and 5% CO2 until the cells exhibited approximately 80% cellular lesions. The virus was then collected, centrifuged, and the supernatant was stored in a -80°C refrigerator for future use.

Ducklings and infection

Thirty one-day-old healthy Cherry Valley ducklings were randomly assigned to either the control or infected group. They were housed in a negative pressure isolator in the animal facility with ad libitum access to food and water. The ducklings were reared until day 3 and were then infected via intramuscular injection with 0.2 mL of either NDRV or PBS solution. On day 15 post-infection (dpi), the ducklings in both groups were euthanized and subjected to autopsy.

Histopathological analyses

Samples of spleen and ileum were aseptically collected from the ducklings and fixed in 4% formaldehyde solution. Pathological changes were assessed using the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining method (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and goblet cell counts in the ileum were performed. Additionally, villus height and crypt depth were measured using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Immunofluorescence assays

Paraffin sections of ileum tissue were deparaffinized and antigen-repaired, followed by blocking with 5% goat serum for 30 min. NDRV σC antibody (prepared in our laboratory) was then applied dropwise, and the sections were incubated flat in a wet box at 4°C overnight. The sections were subsequently incubated with FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 50 min at room temperature, protected from light. Finally, the sections were mounted, and images were captured.

Detection of the ileum antioxidant capacity

The levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) in ileum tissues were measured using kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from ileum tissue using TRIzol (Takara, Dalian, China) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Absolute real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed to quantify NDRV S1 gene expression and determine viral loading. Relative RT-qPCR was conducted to measure mRNA levels of tight junction proteins and cytokines. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method. Bacterial 16S rRNA gene sequencing

Total RNA from ileum contents was extracted using the OMEGA Soil DNA Kit (M5635-02) (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. The quantity of DNA was measured using a NanoDrop NC2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States), and DNA quality was assessed via agarose gel electrophoresis. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Nano DNA LT Library Prep Kit, and sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. The data were analyzed using QIIME2.

Determination of SCFA in ileum

To extract short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) from ileum contents, 1 g of ileum contents was diluted in sterile distilled water at a ratio of 1:4 to 1:8 (w/v) and thoroughly mixed. The mixture was then centrifuged at 5000×g for 10 min, and the supernatant containing SCFAs was filtered. SCFAs were analyzed using HPLC.

Statistical and bioinformatics analysis

Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. One-way ANOVA was employed for statistical comparisons. The results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

NDRV Infection reduced the microbial diversity in the ileum

Species accumulation curves are commonly used to measure and predict the increase in species richness within a community as the sample size increases. They are widely employed to determine the adequacy of sample size and estimate community richness. The results showed that with the addition of new samples, the curve exhibited a sharp increase, indicating that the sample size was sufficient (Fig. 1A).

(B) Richness and diversity of ileum microbial populations, values are shown as min to max with the mean value calculated for each group and Statistical tests were performed using post hoc ANOVA. (C) Principal coordinate analysis of ileum microbial populations. (D) Cluster analysis based on jaccard distance algorithm. ∗P<0.05, ∗∗P<0.01,∗∗∗P<0.001.

To assess the differences between the microbial populations in the ileum of the NDRV-infected and control groups, multiple α diversity indices of richness and diversity were used for analysis. At 15 dpi, the richness and diversity of the samples in the NDRV-infected group were significantly lower (P < 0.05) compared to the control group. This reduction was reflected in the abundance index Chao1, Observed species, and the diversity indices Shannon and Simpson. Typically, the richness and diversity indices of microbial populations increase significantly (P < 0.05) with the maturation of ducks in a healthy environment (Fig. 1B). Therefore, the results indicated that species richness and diversity in the ileum of Cherry Valley ducklings decreased following NDRV infection, with a corresponding block in the increase of microbial population diversity.

To further explore the relationship between bacterial populations and their phylogenetic correlations, Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was performed using the Jaccard index phylogenetic tree. In PCoA, samples that are closer together have more similar microbial population compositions, while those farther apart are more distinct. PCoA analysis revealed significant differences in the composition of ileum microbial populations between the NDRV-infected and control groups (Fig. 1C). This finding was further corroborated by hierarchical clustering of samples using Jaccard's distance algorithm, where samples from the NDRV-infected groups clustered distinctly from those of the control groups (Fig. 1D).

NDRV Infection altered the structure of community in the ileum and SCFAs content

Taxonomic analyses identified Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Verrucomicrobia as the most abundant phylum in the ileum of both NDRV-infected and uninfected Cherry Valley ducklings (Fig. 2A). Compared to the uninfected control group, the NDRV-infected group exhibited a significant decrease in the abundance of Firmicutes, from 80.62% to 38.97%, and Bacteroidetes, from 15.16% to 10.62%. Conversely, NDRV infection significantly increased the relative abundance of Proteobacteria from 2.87% to 46.85%, Actinobacteria from 0.29% to 0.93%, and Verrucomicrobia from 0.37% to 3.82% compared to the control group (P < 0.05). These findings indicate that NDRV infection caused substantial changes in the microbial population composition of the ileum at the phylum level.

At the family level, NDRV infection also resulted in significant changes in the ileum microbial populations of Cherry Valley ducklings (Fig. 2B). In the infected group, the abundance of Enterobacteriaceae within Proteobacteria significantly increased from 2.8% to 58.24% (P < 0.01), Clostridiaceae within Firmicutes increased from 0.89% to 16.83% (P < 0.01), and Verrucomicrobiaceae within Verrucomicrobia increased from 0.031% to 2.12% (P < 0.05). Additionally, NDRV infection significantly reduced the abundance of Enterococcaceae, Ruminococcaceae, Streptococcaceae, Leuconostocaceae, and Erysipelotrichaceae within Firmicutes, Bacteroidaceae within Bacteroidetes, and Lachnospiraceae within Proteobacteria compared to the uninfected group. These results suggest that NDRV infection disrupted the composition of the ileum microbial population, and certain beneficial bacteria may have been significantly inhibited by the growth of pathogenic bacteria.

The total number of ASVs shared between groups C and M was 148, while the unique ASVs in groups C and M were 1,041 and 671, respectively (Fig. 3A). Additionally, cluster analyses of the top 20 genera highlighted significant differences in distribution due to NDRV infection (Fig. 3B). To identify specific bacterial taxa associated with NDRV infection, the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) method was employed to compare the ileum microbiota of healthy controls and NDRV-infected ducks. The structure and predominant bacteria of the ileum microbiota in the control and NDRV groups are depicted in cladograms (Fig. 3C). Some bacterial taxa in the ileum samples differed significantly at the genus level. NDRV infection notably reduced the relative abundance of the genera Enterococcus, Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, and Corynebacterium (LDA > 2) while increasing the relative abundance of genera such as Faecalibacterium, Adlercreutzia, Subdoligranulum, Escherichia, Alistipes, Eubacterium, Sutterella, and Bilophila (LDA > 2) compared to the control group (Fig. 3D). These genera, which include pathogenic bacteria, showed increased abundance following NDRV infection but were not found in high abundance in the control samples (Fig. 3E). Additionally, levels of SCFAs were reduced in the ileum samples from NDRV-infected ducks (Fig. 3F). In summary, NDRV infection caused significant changes in the composition of the ileum flora, which affected the colonization of intestinal bacteria.

NDRV Targeted intestinal epithelial cells and caused intestinal structure injury

At 3 dpi, infected ducklings began to display clinical signs such as lethargy, anorexia, and diarrhea. Compared to the control group, NDRV-infected Cherry Valley ducklings exhibited significant growth retardation (Fig. 4B), with daily weight gain showing a declining trend from day 3 to day 15, leading to severe underweight conditions (Fig. 4C). Mortality was observed starting from the third day of infection, continuing until the 12th day (Fig. 4D). Notably, necrotic foci were clearly visible in the spleen, and hyperemia was observed in the ileum mesentery at 15 dpi, whereas ducklings in the control group remained healthy and free of lesions (Fig. 5A). Histopathological analysis revealed no lesions in the control ducklings throughout the experiment. However, in the infected group, significant lesions were observed in both the spleen and ileum tissues. Analysis of splenic tissue showed widespread macrophage infiltration with diffuse distribution (blue arrows), along with increased necrotic cellular debris (red arrows) and reduced brownish-yellow pigmentation (orange arrows). In the ileum, epithelial cells of the mucosa were detached (black arrows), with lymphocytic infiltration (green arrows) and scattered distribution (red arrows) in the lamina propria, along with occasional capillary dilation (orange arrows) (Fig. 5B). Additionally, the infected group exhibited a significantly higher peripheral index of the spleen compared to the control group (Fig. 5C). In the ileum, crypt depth was significantly increased, while villus height and the villus height-to-crypt depth ratio were significantly decreased compared to the control group (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, NDRV infection at 15 dpi reduced the antioxidant capacity of the ileum, with significantly lower levels of SOD and GSH-Px and significantly higher levels of MDA compared to the control group, indicating the presence of oxidative stress in the ileum (Fig. 5E). These results suggest that NDRV infection impaired intestinal morphology and caused destruction of the intestinal structure, thereby hindering digestion and absorption processes.

To determine whether intestinal damage was a hallmark of NDRV infection in ducklings, we first examined antigen distribution in the ileum (Fig. 5F). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that NDRV antigens were primarily expressed in the intestinal mucosa of the ileum, indicating strong invasion by NDRV. In contrast, no positive virus signals were detected in the control samples. Additionally, viral load in the ileum was quantified using absolute RT-qPCR, which showed a significant increase in viral load in the infected group (Fig. 5G). The number of goblet cells in the ileum was also significantly reduced in the NDRV-infected group compared to the control group (Fig. 5H). These findings suggest that NDRV infection leads to intestinal damage in Cherry Valley ducks.

NDRV Infection damages ileum mucous layer construction and tight junctions, and promoted local intestinal inflammation

Given that mucins and trefoil peptides are key components of the mucus layer, we explored the impact of NDRV infection on the expression of mucous layer proteins. Trefoil factor 2 (TFF2), a secreted protein expressed in the intestinal mucosa, and mucin-2 (MUC2), an important protective and antimicrobial mucin, which serves as a major barrier component of the intestinal mucous layer, were significantly downregulated at 15 dpi (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6A). These results suggest that NDRV infection compromises the construction of the ileum mucus layer.

NDRV Infection damages intestinal tight junction and increases intestinal permeability. (A) The tight junction gene expression of TFF2,MUC2,ZO-1and Occludin at 15 dpi. (B)The expression of inflammatory cytokines at 15 dpi. ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, and ∗∗∗P < 0.001.

Tight junctions between epithelial cells are crucial for maintaining intestinal mucosal permeability, which in turn regulates material transport and osmotic homeostasis. Occludin, a transmembrane protein, and zona occludens-1 (ZO-1), a cytoplasmic attachment protein, are key components of tight junctions. Their expression and distribution are closely linked to the permeability of epithelial cells. To assess the effect of NDRV infection on tight junction proteins (TJPs), we measured the mRNA levels of ZO-1 and Occludin. Both were significantly downregulated in the ileum at 15 dpi (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6A), indicating that NDRV infection damaged the tight junctions of ileum mucosal epithelial cells, leading to increased intestinal permeability.

Furthermore, NDRV infection triggered an amplification of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the ileum of infected Cherry Valley ducklings. The mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory markers, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-17A (IL-17A), and interleukin-22 (IL-22), were significantly elevated at 15 dpi compared to controls. Conversely, the mRNA levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines, interleukin-10 (IL-10) and interleukin-4 (IL-4), were significantly reduced following NDRV infection (Fig. 6B).

Associations between ileum microbiota, TJPs and cytokines

This study examined the correlation between the expression of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines, TJPs, and the ileum microbial populations. Focused analyses were conducted on the 23 most dominant bacterial genera, each with a relative abundance of ≥0.1% in the samples (Fig. 7). The results indicated that the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, and IL-22 was negatively correlated with the genera Enterococcus, Streptococcus, Weissella, Pediococcus, Corynebacterium, Candidatus Arthromitus, Coprobacillus, Ruminococcus, and Facklamia. In contrast, these pro-inflammatory cytokines showed significant positive correlations with Clostridium (family Clostridiaceae), Escherichia, Alistipes, Faecalibacterium, and Bilophila. On the other hand, anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-4 and IL-10, as well as TJPs (TFF2, MUC2, ZO-1, and Occludin), displayed the opposite trend. These anti-inflammatory markers and TJPs were negatively correlated with the genera linked to pro-inflammatory responses and positively correlated with beneficial genera. Notably, ZO-1 was significantly positively associated with several SCFA-producing genera, including Bacteroides, Coprobacillus, and Lactobacillus.

Discussion

The gut plays a vital role in poultry health, hosting diverse microbial communities within the gastrointestinal tract. These microbial populations act as an auxiliary organ system, contributing significantly to the growth and development of animals, as well as the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis (Alam and Neish, 2018). However, there are no previous studies investigating the effects of NDRV infection on the ileum microbiota of Cherry Valley ducklings. This study aimed to analyze the changes in the composition of the ileum microbial community following NDRV infection and to explore the interactions between intestinal flora dysbiosis and intestinal injury. The findings provide new insights into the relationship between microbiology and virology, shedding light on how microbial imbalances may contribute to viral-induced intestinal damage.

The intestinal ecosystem relies on the diversity and integrity of the intestinal flora, which plays a crucial role in maintaining intestinal health (Shi et al., 2017). Previous studies have reported alterations in the diversity of the intestinal flora in Cherry Valley ducklings following viral infections (Luo et al., 2019; Luo et al., 2021). In this study, NDRV infection led to a significant reduction in microbiota diversity, as indicated by changes in the α and β diversity indices. This suggests a substantial shift in the composition of the ileum microbiota compared to control ducks, consistent with the findings of Luo et al. (2019). We further analyzed differences in the structure and composition of the ileum intestinal flora between normal and NDRV-infected ducks at the phylum, family, and genus levels, identifying specific bacterial taxa associated with NDRV infection. It is well established that Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes are the dominant bacterial phyla in the ileum flora of ducks (Luo et al., 2021). Our study observed these three dominant phyla in both control and infected groups, consistent with previous findings in Muscovy ducks (Chen et al., 2019). However, following NDRV infection, the relative abundances of Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, and Actinobacteria increased, while those of Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes decreased compared to the controls. It has been reported that Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes play important roles in intestinal microbiota metabolism in animals (Ley et al., 2008; Kohl, 2012). Specifically, Firmicutes are associated with the digestibility of roughage (Mohd Shaufi et al., 2015). In this study, NDRV-infected ducklings exhibited symptoms of diarrhea and weight loss. We speculate that the reduced relative abundance of Firmicutes may impair nutrient digestion and absorption, contributing to the growth retardation and diarrhea observed in NDRV-infected ducklings.

At the family level, the relative abundance of Enterobacteriaceae was significantly higher in the NDRV-infected group than in the control group, a finding consistent with the results observed by Li et al. (2018). Similar to intestinal inflammation in mammals, where facultative anaerobic Enterobacteriaceae tend to thrive while the abundance of obligate anaerobes declines (Seksik et al., 2003; Gophna et al., 2006; Baumgart et al., 2007), this study also showed a significant reduction in the relative abundance of Enterococcaceae and Streptococcaceae (Li et al., 2018). At the genus level, the relative abundance of certain opportunistic pathogens increased with disease. Notably, there was an increase in the genera Adlercreutzia, Subdoligranulum, Escherichia, Alistipes, Eubacterium, Sutterella, and Bilophila (LDA > 2) in the NDRV-infected group compared to the control group. The abundance of Bilophila was significantly elevated in NDRV-infected Cherry Valley ducklings. Interestingly, Bilophila is a pathogenic bacterium known to cause systemic inflammation in specific-pathogen-free mice (Feng et al., 2017). Similarly, the relative abundance of Sutterella increased significantly after NDRV infection, with some reports linking Sutterella to inflammatory bowel disease (Derqaoui et al., 2022). Additionally, the abundance of Alistipes was higher in the infected group, a genus associated with pathogenicity in colorectal cancer (Parker et al., 2020). The relative abundance of Escherichia also increased, a finding consistent with reports of elevated Escherichia populations in the ileum of piglets infected with rotavirus (Mao et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2022). Escherichia species can cause a wide range of intestinal disorders, from self-limiting diarrhea to hemorrhagic colitis (Kaper et al., 2004). The increase in these genera, associated with NDRV infection, suggests a potential predisposing factor for gastrointestinal abnormalities. These findings indicate that NDRV infection led to a decrease in beneficial bacteria and an increase in harmful bacteria in the ileum of Cherry Valley ducklings. The growth of pathogenic bacteria may have significantly inhibited beneficial bacterial populations, ultimately leading to ecological dysregulation of the intestinal microflora and persistent diarrhea. In conclusion, NDRV infection caused an abnormal composition of the ileum flora, which affected the colonization of the intestinal microbiota. Therefore, the pathogenicity of NDRV is closely associated with changes in the intestinal flora.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are the end products of fermentation by the intestinal microbiota and play a crucial role in regulating mucosal immune responses and antiviral defenses, particularly in maintaining intestinal barrier function (Sanderson, 2004; Wang et al., 2022). SCFAs primarily include acetate, propionate, and butyrate. Interestingly, this study found that the abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria was significantly altered following NDRV infection in ducklings. This was reflected by a decrease in the relative abundance of key bacterial genera, such as Streptococcus, Enterococcus, Lactobacillus, and Roseburia. Among these, the genus Streptococcus is a major producer of acetic acid, which provides essential energy to the host. Lactobacillus and Enterococcus are prominent producers of lactic acid and propionic acid, while Roseburia is the primary bacterium responsible for producing butyric acid, a critical component in enhancing intestinal barrier function. However, NDRV infection led to a reduction in SCFA levels. Based on the findings related to gut microbiota, we speculate that the disruption of SCFA-producing bacteria in the ileum contributed to this decrease in SCFAs. SCFAs are vital for maintaining health and combating disease (Luo et al., 2019). Butyric acid and propionic acid, in particular, have been shown to inhibit intestinal inflammation by modulating the gene expression of inflammatory cytokines (Inan et al., 2000; Singh et al., 2014). Therefore, the observed reduction in SCFAs and their associated bacteria may exacerbate intestinal inflammation during NDRV infection, contributing to intestinal injury.

Gut ecosystems rely on the diversity and integrity of gut flora, which play a critical role in maintaining gut health. The gut microbiota has been reported to support the survival of intestinal epithelial cells (Ohland and Macnaughton, 2010). Additionally, intestinal flora helps maintain intestinal barrier function by promoting epithelial cell proliferation, enhancing the integrity of the intestinal epithelium, and regulating tight junction proteins (Sharma et al., 2010; Ashida et al., 2011). Studies have demonstrated that the loss of intestinal flora can lead to intestinal mucosal damage (Rakoff-Nahoum et al., 2004). This highlights the importance of normal intestinal microbiota colonization in preserving intestinal barrier function (Luo et al., 2021). Our study showed that NDRV impairs the intestinal microbiota, suggesting that the loss of intestinal flora may contribute to intestinal damage. We found that NDRV was detectable in the ileum after infecting Cherry Valley ducklings and was primarily distributed in the mucosa. NDRV infection caused severe disruption of the intestinal structure, resulting in villi blunting and atrophy, which reduced the intestinal absorptive surface area and further worsened intestinal damage. This imbalance in intestinal microbiota and the resultant intestinal injury led to clinical symptoms of diarrhea and weight loss in NDRV-infected ducklings. Similar findings were reported by Luo et al. (2021). NDRV infection also affected the survival of intestinal epithelial cells, causing damage to the ileum mucosa. This mucosal injury disrupted the expression and formation of tight junction proteins (ZO-1 and Occludin), as well as TFF2 and MUC2, which are vital for maintaining the integrity of the intestinal mucosa. ZO-1 and Occludin are key components of tight junction proteins (Günzel and Fromm, 2012), while TFF2 and MUC2 are involved in the construction of the ileum mucus layer (Hansson, 2012). Our results align with previous studies (Li et al., 2018; Luo et al., 2021; He et al., 2022). The primary mechanism contributing to the disruption of intestinal mucosal integrity appears to be ileum oxidative stress induced by NDRV. Maintaining redox balance is crucial for the survival of intestinal epithelial cells (Yang et al., 2024). Viruses have been shown to cause cellular damage by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and disrupting redox homeostasis (Khomich et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019). Viral infections also promote the production of oxidants, hindering the synthesis of antioxidant enzymes such as CAT, SOD, and GSH-Px, ultimately disturbing redox balance and promoting viral replication (Rehman et al., 2018). In this study, we found that NDRV infection increased MDA activity and reduced SOD and GSH-Px levels in the ileum, indicating a decline in antioxidant capacity. Similar findings were reported by Jiahui He et al. in the Muscovy duck model (He et al., 2022). Based on these results, we propose that NDRV compromises intestinal mucosal integrity by impairing antioxidant defense mechanisms. Goblet cells, which are secretory cells in the intestinal epithelium, regulate the mucus barrier and mucosal immunity. The second mechanism contributing to the damage of intestinal mucosal integrity was the upregulation of various pro-inflammatory cytokines in the ileum following NDRV infection, which led to increased intestinal permeability. It has been reported that TNF-α increases intestinal epithelial tight junction permeability, playing a key role in promoting intestinal inflammation (Ye et al., 2006; Chen et al., 2015). TNF-α increases tight junction permeability by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway (Yu et al., 2018). Additionally, TNF-α induces the production of other pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and IL-1β, triggering a cascade, disrupts tight junction proteins, resulting in damages the intestinal barrier (Cao et al., 2013). In our study, NDRV infection significantly induced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the ileum while significantly reducing the expression of TJPs. This suggests that NDRV compromises intestinal barrier function by weakening the integrity of the tight junctions. Furthermore, the observed reduction in goblet cell numbers after NDRV infection suggests that the virus damages the intestinal mucosa, leading to reduced immune function.

In our correlation analyses, we investigated specific microbiota associated with TJPs and cytokines in NDRV-infected ducklings, revealing links between systemic inflammation, microbial translocation, and epithelial damage. The abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria such as Enterococcus, Streptococcus, Ruminococcus, and Faecalibacterium was negatively correlated with pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, and IL-22. Conversely, the relative abundance of acetate-producing Clostridiaceae was positively correlated with anti-inflammatory factors IL-4 and IL-10, as well as tight junction proteins TFF2, MUC2, ZO-1, and Occludin. Notably, pathogenic Escherichia was positively correlated with pro-inflammatory cytokines. Interestingly, changes in gut microbiota appear to influence the expression of inflammatory cytokines. Kamiya et al. demonstrated that SCFA-producing Streptococcus thermophilus can lead to the upregulation of IL-17A by Peyer's patch-resident T lymphocytes (Kamiya et al., 2016). Similarly, Chen J. et al. reported that Collinsella is involved in the expression of the pro-inflammatory factor IL-17A and the exacerbation of rheumatoid arthritis (Chen et al., 2016). Thus, the upregulation of pro-inflammatory factors and downregulation of anti-inflammatory factors are associated with a decrease in SCFA-producing bacteria and an increase in pathogenic bacteria, contributing to the development of intestinal inflammation during NDRV infection. In conclusion, the intestinal damage observed in NDRV-infected Cherry Valley ducklings is linked to disturbances in the intestinal flora. Specifically, NDRV infection resulted in a decline in beneficial bacteria, an enrichment of opportunistic pathogens, and a reduction in SCFA-producing bacteria. This study provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms of NDRV and lays a theoretical foundation for understanding the pathogenesis of NDRV.
