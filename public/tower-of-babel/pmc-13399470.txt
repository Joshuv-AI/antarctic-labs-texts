Effectiveness of Workplace Noise-risk Classification with Hearing Protection in Preventing Occupational Hearing Loss
Authors: XuanYu Chen, LianGong Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of combining workplace noise-risk classification with a hearing protection program to prevent occupational noise-induced hearing loss (ONIHL).Methods:This retrospective cohort study included 400 male noise‑exposed workers from an automotive manufacturing company. Based on workshop‑level protection strategies implemented by the company, participants were assigned to either a control group (n = 200, traditional measures) or an observation group (n = 200, risk classification plus standardized hearing protection). Outcomes assessed at the end of 2023 included proper personal protective equipment use, average noise exposure, speech-/high‑frequency hearing loss, high‑frequency standard threshold shift (STS), and ONIHL incidence. Statistical comparisons were performed using independent t tests, χ2 tests, and logistic regression.Results:Baseline characteristics were comparable between the groups (P > 0.05). At follow‑up, the observation group showed significantly better outcomes (all P < 0.05): higher personal protective equipment compliance (87.50% versus 63.00%), lower noise exposure (86.26 ± 1.99 versus 88.72 ± 1.77 dB(A)), and lower rates of speech‑frequency hearing loss (20.00% versus 52.50%), high‑frequency hearing loss (35.00% versus 65.00%), high‑frequency STS (5.00% versus 20.00%), and ONIHL (1.00% versus 5.50%). Job-type subgroup analyses indicated a significant protective effect in stamping (STS: 3.85% versus 15.00%, P = 0.017), but not in welding (4.48% versus 13.85%, P = 0.061); machining showed a significant reduction (3.64% versus 14.55%, P = 0.047).Conclusion:The integrated strategy was associated with improved protection compliance, reduced noise exposure, and lower hearing loss rates. However, due to the non‑randomized, workshop‑level assignment, causal inferences should be made cautiously. Prospective studies are needed to confirm causality.

INTRODUCTION

Occupational noise-induced hearing loss (ONIHL), affecting over 10% of the global workforce, typically results from continuous or intermittent noise exposure and causes irreversible cochlear hair cell damage.[123] Beyond hearing impairment, ONIHL is often accompanied by tinnitus, sleep disorders, and cardiovascular risks, creating substantial health and economic burdens.[45] Traditional protection models have limited effectiveness because of factors such as inadequate noise reduction, regulatory gaps, low worker awareness, and poor personal protective equipment (PPE) compliance.[678] Therefore, more targeted and systematic protection strategies are necessary.

Risk classification, which categorizes jobs into tiers (mild to extremely severe) based on noise-exposure levels and duration, represents a systematic advancement beyond uniform protection models by enabling targeted resource allocation.[9] Concurrently, comprehensive hearing protection programs integrate exposure monitoring, engineering controls, PPE, and audiometric surveillance.[10] The combination of risk classification and structured protection forms a closed-loop system that is hypothesized to be more effective than conventional programs (which typically involve annual monitoring, basic PPE, and general training). This study evaluated an integrated strategy based on the risk classification framework (GBZ/T 229.4-2012),[11] which prescribes tier-specific engineering controls, protection standards, surveillance frequency, and training and is supported by workshop-level enforcement.

The proposed pathway is as follows: risk stratification based on exposure intensity and duration enables targeted measures. Higher-risk positions receive engineering controls (e.g., silencers and isolation) to reduce noise at the source, stricter PPE requirements, and more frequent audiometric surveillance, whereas lower-risk positions focus on routine monitoring and training. This targeted approach was expected to improve protection compliance, reduce actual noise-exposure levels, and subsequently lower the incidence of hearing loss and ONIHL compared with uniform protection strategies.

Based on this rationale, this study systematically evaluated the comprehensive effectiveness of integrating workplace noise-risk classification with a structured hearing protection program to prevent ONIHL and related auditory damage. By examining this combined model, the research aimed to generate robust scientific evidence and a practical, scalable solution for improving noise hazard protection. The findings are intended to inform policy and promote systematic advancement of occupational health and safety management in high-noise industries.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

General Information

Information

This retrospective cohort study included 400 eligible male workers who were divided into control and observation groups based on the enterprise’s historical implementation date of hearing protection measures. Both groups were observed over the same 3-year period (January 2021 to December 2023). During this time, the control group (n = 200) received traditional noise protection, whereas the observation group (n = 200) received noise-risk classification management combined with a standardized hearing protection program. The two groups were drawn from separate workshops (Workshop A and Workshop B) within the same factory, which had independent management but similar production processes (e.g., stamping, welding, and machining) and comparable baseline noise-exposure levels (January 2021: control group: 88.82 ± 1.85 dB(A) and observation group: 88.84 ± 1.51 dB(A); P = 0.890). Workshop-level descriptors (worker-to-supervisor ratio, shift structure, machine age, production volume, and safety training frequency) were similar. Workers did not move between workshops during the study period. The hearing protection programs were implemented as part of company policy. The retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Hunan Prevention and Treatment Institute for Occupational Diseases (Affiliated Prevention and Treatment Institute for Occupational Diseases of University of South China) (Approval No. 2025-001-KY). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Of the 526 male workers initially identified from the occupational health database as potentially eligible as of January 2021, 468 met the inclusion criteria. Exclusions (n = 68) were applied for interruption of noise work ≥3 months (n = 21), incomplete noise-risk classification or hearing protection records (n = 18), allergy to noise protection equipment materials (n = 9), and other non-occupational hearing loss factors (n = 20; including drug-induced [n = 12] and other ear diseases [n = 8]. The final analysis included 400 workers (200 per group).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) continuous work in noise-exposed positions for ≥1 year by January 2021, with a baseline noise-exposure level of ≥85 dB[12]; (2) age 18–55 years at baseline, without serious systemic diseases; (3) remained employed and did not transfer to non-noise positions during 2021–2023; (4) no documented congenital hearing impairment, ear diseases, drug-induced hearing loss, or other non-occupational hearing loss factors; and (5) no history of diabetes.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) work interruption for ≥3 months; (2) incomplete records; and (3) allergy to noise-protection equipment materials.

Group assignment (control or observation) was determined by the company’s administrative policy implemented in January 2021, not by the researchers.

Study Methods

Control Group

The control group received the enterprise’s traditional noise protection: annual comprehensive noise monitoring covering all shifts, including steady-state and impulsive noise; uniform-specification soundproof earplugs (noise reduction value ≥20 dB) for all noise-exposed workers; and annual occupational health examination (including pure-tone audiometry). This approach lacked targeted noise-risk classification management, systematic hearing protection training, protective equipment compatibility testing, and fit testing to verify individual attenuation.

Noise Exposure Assessment for Outcome Comparison

To ensure the measurement validity, participants had been in their current jobs for >1 year and could work a full shift. A total of 48 noise monitoring points were established across stamping (18), welding (16), and machining (14). Individual noise exposure was measured for a full shift using a Casella dBadge2 dosimeter (Casella, Bedford, UK). The dosimeter microphone was placed at the shoulder midpoint. The dosimeters had a 55.0–140.3 dB(A) dynamic range and a 3 dB exchange rate. For impulsive noise, the dosimeter was set to impulse mode (peak, C-weighted, maximum 140 dB), following the protocol specified in GB/T 14367-2022. Each dosimeter was calibrated before use with a Casella 120/2 Acoustic Calibrator (Casella, Bedford, UK). For each job, 1–3 participants were monitored across three shifts. Implementation Fidelity and Intensity

Implementation fidelity was extracted from company records. Engineering controls (e.g., silencers and vibration damping pads) installed on all Level III and IV machines (n = 23) between March and June 2021 were verified by quarterly safety audits (100% compliance). Soundproof rest areas for Level II–IV positions were available during all shifts, with 92% of eligible workers using these areas at least once per shift. For Level II–IV positions, 2-h break schedules were implemented, with documented supervisor-monitored adherence of 88%. Training attendance was 95% for new workers and 98% for refresher training. Annual REAT fit-testing was completed for all observation group workers (2021–2023); 87% achieved the target attenuation on the first test and 100% after individualized instruction. Prescribed noise monitoring frequencies were followed for 94% of scheduled measurements. These data indicate high implementation fidelity.

Statistical Analysis

SPSS 21.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for analysis. Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (‘x ± s); categorical data as percentages (%). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. For variables meeting normality and homogeneity assumptions, parametric tests were used; otherwise, nonparametric equivalents were applied. Independent samples t tests were used for between-group comparisons of continuous variables, and χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables. For the primary continuous outcome (noise-exposure level), analysis of covariance adjusted for baseline noise exposure, age, years of noise exposure, and job type, with group as the fixed factor; adjusted mean differences and 95% confidence intervals (CI) are reported. For the primary binary outcome (new‑onset ONIHL), binary logistic regression adjusted for the same covariates; adjusted odds ratios and 95% CI are reported. No data were missing. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The incidence of speech- and high-frequency hearing loss in the observation group was significantly lower than that in the control group (P < 0.001). Regarding severity, among the 11 cases in the control group, 7 were classified as mild, 3 as moderate, and 1 as severe; all 2 cases in the observation group were mild. DISCUSSION

This retrospective cohort study evaluated the 3-year effectiveness of combining workplace noise-risk classification with a standardized hearing- protection program. Compared with traditional protection, the integrated strategy was associated with significant improvements in all key hearing-health indicators. Baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups. At the 3-year follow-up, the observation group showed superior outcomes in noise-exposure control and hearing health.

Exposure reduction through engineering and administrative controls: The observation group achieved a mean noise exposure reduction of approximately 2.5 dB(A) relative to the control group. While modest, a 3 dB reduction corresponds to a doubling or halving of acoustic energy.[22] This reduction likely resulted from source-level engineering controls (e.g., silencers and damping pads) on higher-risk equipment and administrative controls (e.g., 2-h breaks with 88% adherence), which limited daily noise dose. PPE fit and compliance improvements: Proper PPE use was significantly higher in the observation group (87.50%) than in the control group (63.00%). Critically, the program included annual REAT fit testing to verify real-world attenuation, addressing the conventional limitation of overestimated labeled protection values.[23] The combination of high compliance and verified fit likely contributed to the observed exposure reduction and hearing outcomes.

Surveillance and early response: The observation group’s more frequent audiometry (semiannual for high‑risk positions) and immediate corrective action upon STS detection likely contributed to the significantly lower rates of STS and ONIHL, suggesting reduced progression to established hearing loss.

The observation group also showed a significantly lower incidence of high‑frequency STS (5.00% versus 20.00%) and speech‑frequency hearing loss (20.00% versus 52.50%). These findings suggest that the integrated strategy may delay early auditory damage and reduce progression to established hearing loss. The higher risk in the control group aligns with the high burden of hearing loss reported in similar occupational settings,[24] and longitudinal data from a high-risk cohort indicate that cumulative noise exposure exhibits a significant dose–response association with the risk of ONIHL.[25] This finding suggests that a comprehensive strategy centered on risk stratification may be associated with both reduced early-stage damage and a lower diagnostic risk of end-stage occupational diseases in this cohort.

Broader implications for occupational health in China: Workplace noise is a recognized occupational hazard in Chinese manufacturing. The evaluated risk-classification strategy aligns with the national standard GBZ/T 229.4‑2012,[11] although its full implementation varies across provinces because of differences in enforcement, enterprise size, and resources. The present findings suggest that risk‑stratified hearing protection could offer a practical, resource-efficient approach, particularly for small‑ and medium-sized enterprises. Further research should investigate contextual factors influencing implementation success, such as local regulatory capacity and industry structure.

While this study yielded positive results regarding the effectiveness of the combined hearing protection measures, several limitations must be acknowledged to contextualize the findings. First, the quasi‑experimental design (non‑randomized workshop assignment) limits causal inference, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors (e.g., management culture and supervision intensity) cannot be excluded. Second, generalizability is restricted by the retrospective, single‑center design, exclusive focus on male workers in one automotive company, and the lack of individual‑level data on smoking, alcohol use, hypertension, and ototoxic drug exposure. Third, the 3‑year follow‑up is relatively short for a chronic disease, and pure‑tone audiometry lacks the sensitivity of more objective measures (e.g., otoacoustic emissions). Based on these limitations, future research should prioritize the following: (1) prospective cluster‑randomized trials or quasi‑experimental designs with more workshops to better account for clustering and workshop‑level confounding; (2) multicenter studies including diverse industries (e.g., textiles and mining), small‑ and medium-sized enterprises, and female workers to improve generalizability; (3) collection of comprehensive individual‑level covariate data, including smoking, alcohol, hypertension, ototoxic drug use, and co‑exposures to solvents or vibration; (4) extended follow‑up beyond 3 years to evaluate the long‑term sustainability of hearing protection; and (5) inclusion of objective physiological measures such as otoacoustic emissions for the early detection of auditory damage.

CONCLUSION

This study suggests that combining workplace noise-risk classification with a structured hearing conservation program is associated with reductions in ONIHL and early auditory damage, along with improvements in protection compliance and noise exposure control. Compared with conventional protection models, this integrated strategy demonstrated significant advantages in reducing early hearing damage progression and appeared to optimize protection resource allocation in the study cohort. This integrated strategy may serve as a practical and effective approach to workplace hearing health protection.
