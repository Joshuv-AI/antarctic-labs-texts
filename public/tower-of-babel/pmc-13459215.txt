Access to renal function information to improve pharmaceutical care in direct oral anticoagulant users: a cluster randomized trial protocol
Authors: Imane Badran, Justine Clarenne, Céline Mongaret, Aline Ohl-Hurtaud, Coralie Barbe, Florian Slimano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDirect oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are commonly prescribed for the management of thromboembolic diseases and are known to increase the risk of bleeding. Renal impairment is a risk factor for bleeding in DOACs users, making renal function access essential for community pharmacists during the prescription review and dispensing process. However, community pharmacists often have limited access to patients’ renal function information. Giving Community Pharmacists full access to the renal function information could help to mitigate this preventable part of the risk of bleeding by optimizing DOACs choice and dosage, thereby helping to reduce the risk of bleeding events. This study aims to assess the impact of providing Community Pharmacists with access to patients’ renal function information when dispensing DOACs on the occurrence of clinically relevant bleeding events three months after dispensing.MethodsA prospective, comparative, randomized, cluster, multicenter trial involving 96 community pharmacies, allocated into two groups: intervention (systematic access to renal function) and control (usual care). A total of 1920 patients will be included over a 24-month period and followed for 6 months. The primary endpoint is the occurrence of clinically relevant bleeding—major and nonmajor—occurring within three months after DOACs dispensing. Secondary endpoints include detail of pharmacist interventions and an assessment of the feasibility of providing renal function access to Community Pharmacists.DiscussionThis study will assess the clinical impact of providing Community Pharmacists with access to renal function information. The findings have the potential to inform best practices and drive meaningful changes in ambulatory pharmaceutical care.Trial registrationThe trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06739603, December 17th, 2024. https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06739603?term=NCT06739603&rank=1.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03413-3.

Background

The use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) for the prevention and treatment of thromboembolic disease has a favorable risk-benefit ratio in both preventive and curative approvals, in addition to improvement in quality of life [1–3]. Three DOACs are currently approved in France: dabigatran, a factor II inhibitor; rivaroxaban and apixaban, both factor X inhibitors. However, DOACs rank first among the medications associated with adverse events and serious adverse events requiring hospitalization, mainly due to the increased bleeding risk [4]. Certain clinical situations present a particularly high risk of bleeding under DOACs, notably renal impairment, even of moderate grade, because of the predominantly renal elimination of these drugs. Indeed, dose adjustment is recommended in the summary of product characteristics (SmPC) of DOACs for patients with reduced estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) (for dabigatran or rivaroxaban) or increased serum creatinine (apixaban). Furthermore, several studies have reported that renal impairment is associated with a risk of overexposure that increase the risk of bleeding in patients ≥ 80 years old with non-valvular atrial fibrillated treated by DOAC [5–7]. Therefore, the prescription and follow-up of DOAC treatment is contingent on monitoring of renal function, and if necessary, dose adjustment to account for impairment.

Community pharmacists (CPs) dispense DOACs daily after reviewing the prescription. In France, the Best dispensing practices guidelines for CP recommend that pharmacists take account of all relevant information, including lab results, for processing the prescription review [8]. A previous study performed by our group have shown that access to eGFR enabled the detection of drug-related problems (DRP), primarily dosing issues (mostly supratherapeutic dosage) and contra-indications [9]. However, in daily practice in primary care, CPs do not systematically have access to renal function data, which constitutes a barrier to perform appropriate pharmacist intervention when it is required.

Improvements in information technology and in tools for communication and data sharing among healthcare providers—such as secure messaging systems and shared electronic medical records—make it possible to implement systematic access to renal function by CPs. Demonstrating the clinical relevance of renal function systematic availability for CPs would support its deployment. This study aims to assess the clinical relevance of systematic availability of renal function for CPs when reviewing prescription and dispensing DOACs in clinical ambulatory practice.

The primary objective of the HEMORRAG’IR study is to assess whether providing CPs with renal function results during DOAC dispensing (initial or refill) affects the occurrence of clinically relevant bleeding (defined as the composite of major and nonmajor clinically relevant bleeding) at three months.

With an alpha risk of 5%, power of 90%, and a two-sided test, the required number of subjects was estimated at 724 per group.

The cluster randomization design requires adjusting the sample size using the formula where m is the cluster size and ρ is the intra-cluster correlation coefficient. The ICC typically ranges between 0.001 and 0.05 [11], with higher correlation when randomizing pharmacies rather than hospitals. Assuming ρ = 0.01 and 20 patients per pharmacy, the design effect (DE) is 1.19. Thus, 1724 patients (862 per group) are required.

Considering the 10% attrition rate, 1920 patients (960 per group) will be included.

The sample size calculation was performed using NQuery 4.0.

Randomization

Since the study aims to assess the effectiveness of an organizational intervention, the pharmacy will be the unit of randomization. Randomization will be performed before starting recruitment of patients in each pharmacy. Block randomization will be performed using a computer-generated randomization algorithm in a 1:1 ratio using SAS software (version 9.4., SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The intervention consists of systematic retrieval by the CP of the most recent RF value (less than 12 months old). All patients in each pharmacy will therefore be assigned to either the “intervention” group (systematic RF search) or the “control” group (no systematic RF search).

Recruitment

Procedure for recruitment is as follows: Information about the study will be provided to each eligible patient by the CP using a written information leaflet. An informational poster will also be displayed in each pharmacy. After verifying inclusion and non-inclusion criteria, if the patient agrees to participate, an informed consent form will be provided. The patient will be considered included once the consent form is signed.

Study procedures

Initial data collection will be performed by the CP (data related to the DOAC) at inclusion, and by the clinical research associate (sociodemographic data and medical history) by phone within 10 days after inclusion.

Two follow-up calls will take place at 3 and 6 months by the clinical research assistant. At each follow-up, modifications in DOAC therapy and the occurrence of any bleeding or thromboembolic events will be recorded. Event occurrence will be search using an adapted question from the “Bleeding Assessment Tool” of the Scientific and Standardization Committee of the International Society of Thromboembolisis and Hemostasis, ISTH) [12].

Study interventions

Intervention group

The CP must retrieve the patient’s RF data (the most recent assessment from within the past 12 months) by any means: printed laboratory results provided by the patient, consultation of online shared electronic health record, by contacting the medical laboratory or the patient’s GP, or by contacting a hospital if the patient had been recently hospitalized. Relevant biological values are serum creatinine or estimated glomerular filtration rate (by the aMDRD or CKD-EPI equations). If multiple values are available, the lowest value should be taken into consideration. RF data must be retrieved within 7 days of inclusion. No biological tests will be performed specifically for the purposes of the study.

Using RF data, the CP performs a pharmacist intervention according to the best dispensing practices. If the prescription differs from what is recommended based on RF information, then the CP will contact the prescriber or GP to propose an intervention. The physician may accept or reject the proposal. No modification to the DOAC prescription will be made without approval from either the patient’s GP or the physician who initially wrote the prescription.

Control group

The CP will not systematically search for RF. However, if RF is available, a pharmacist intervention will be performed as usual. The Control group has been designed to reflect as more as possible the usual care as a real-world comparator.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome is the occurrence of a clinically relevant bleeding, defined as the composite of major and nonmajor clinically relevant bleeding. Bleeding is defined as major if it is clinically overt and associated with a decrease in hemoglobin level of ≥ 2.0 g/dL; if bleeding leads to the transfusion of ≥ 2 units of red cells; or if bleeding is intracranial or retroperitoneal, occurs in another critical site, or contributes to death. Nonmajor clinically relevant bleeding is defined as overt bleeding that does not meet the criteria for major bleeding but is associated with medical intervention, unscheduled medical consultation, regardless of subsequent management (no treatment, medical management with or without hospitalization, or surgery) or discomfort or impairment of activities of daily life, within three months of dispensing the DOAC. Bleeding events will be assessed using a questionnaire adapted from the ISTH Scientific and Standardization Committee “Bleeding Assessment Tool”12. It includes closed questions about epistaxis, cutaneous bleeding, bleeding from small wounds, oral bleeding, gastrointestinal bleeding, and hematuria.

Clinical data

The following data will be recorded for all participants: age, sex, level of education, occupation, marital status, place of residence, living situation (living alone, with a partner, or with another family member), presence of home assistance (nurse, home help, meal delivery, etc.), help with medication administration, medical history (systemic arterial embolism, pulmonary embolism, deep vein thrombosis, atherosclerotic heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction, heart failure, coagulation disorders, cardiac arrhythmias), concomitant medicines. Regarding the DOAC, the following data will be recorded: drug, dose, indication and duration of treatment, first prescription or refill.

Data analysis

Data management

The study will use two distinct databases to separately store research data and contact information. Potentially identifying contact information for the participants, such as name and phone number, will be stored in a separate database unconnected to the research database. In the research database, all participants will be designated by an ID number. Electronic data will be saved on a secure server with restricted access. The individual de-identified participant data (including data dictionary), statistical code, and any other materials will be available from the Research on Health University Department upon request.

Data monitoring

There is no formal data monitoring committee. The research team ensures that all the evaluations were conducted, and the data were accurately recorded and reported according to the protocol.

Statistical analysis

No interim analysis will be performed.

Continuous variables will be expressed as mean ± standard deviation or using the median and interquartile range. Categorical variables will be expressed as number and percentage. The primary endpoint will be analyzed using a generalized linear mixed model with a logit link function, including a random cluster effect to account for the grouped nature of the data. The analysis will be conducted on an intention-to-treat (ITT) basis, meaning that patients will be analyzed according to the group to which they were originally assigned. The analysis of secondary endpoints will follow the same methodology.

For all outcome measures, two sensitivity analyses will be performed:

An “as-treated” (per protocol) analysis: patients who switched randomization arms will be analyzed according to their actual exposure (patients in the “no intervention” group who ultimately received an intervention because the CP accessed RF, and conversely, patients in the “intervention” group for whom the CP did not access RF information),An “m-ITT” (modified ITT) analysis: patients who did not adhere to their initially assigned randomization arm will be excluded from the analysis.

Discussion

The hypothesis of this study is that systematic access to patients’ renal function information for CPs when dispensing DOACs reduces the occurrence of bleeding events. The use of oral anticoagulants carries a significant iatrogenic risk [4]. This risk is heightened because populations using these medications more frequently have impaired renal function [13]. The risk is therefore particularly high in this population [14] and is not significantly reduced with DOACs compared with indirect anticoagulants (such as VKAs) [15, 16]. However, dose adjustments appear to reduce this risk for DOAC [17].

Hospital-level evidence already shows that impaired RF is associated with a risk of overdosage, and hospital pharmacists can reduce this risk via targeted interventions that are largely accepted by physicians [18]. This study, conducted in the primary care setting, aims to explore new strategies to improve medication safety, in a combination of a three-pronged approach: a drug class with high iatrogenic potential in renal impairment (DOACs), a primary care health professional dispensing medicines (CP), and systematic access to a key lab value parameter (renal function) to optimize pharmaceutical care.

Based on previously published data on access to renal function [9, 19], and on the relevance of specifically examining the case of DOACs [20], the expected benefit in the present study is a reduction in the prevalence of adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs) related to DOACs among patients for whom CPs systematically check renal function, compared with patients whose CPs do not have access to this information. This prospective study will allow examination of the full spectrum of patients treated with DOACs, not only those who are hospitalized, as in pharmacovigilance studies [4]. It will also make it possible to explore the impact of integrating renal function data into DOAC dispensing in terms of clinically relevant events leading to unplanned healthcare utilization.

The role of CPs is evolving and enables them to meet growing population needs in the field of primary care. In France, the Best Dispensing Practices guidelines published in 2016 state that CPs may draw on all relevant information necessary for their expertise, including lab values [8]. Yet in practice, CPs have limited access to such data, and the scientific literature on the impact of this access on prescription review by CPs is scarce. In a previous 5-month non-comparative prospective study conducted by our team, the 70 participating CPs were required to ask all patients aged 65 years and over to provide their most recent lab values. In total, 442 patients agreed to participate, enabling CPs to identify 99 DRPs that resulted in pharmacist interventions—mainly dose adjustments (76%)—which were accepted by the prescribing physician or the patient’s GP in 50% of cases. When the medical orders of these 442 patients were then retrospectively reviewed by an expert panel, 184 DRPs were detected, and the proposed interventions were also predominantly dose adjustments (72%). Interestingly, when clinicians rated the clinical relevance of the interventions proposed by CPs and by the expert panel, those proposed by CPs had a positive clinical impact—at least minor—in 54% of cases, compared with 84.7% for the expert panel [9].

Training and experience in using renal function information in clinical practice have already been identified as critical factors [21, 22]. In a qualitative study from Canada, this need was also expressed as the importance of having protocols or tools to support CPs in adjusting medical prescriptions based on RF [21]. In the present study, the decision to focus CP actions on a single drug class, and to provide a practical tool to standardize the actions required for patients with impaired renal function and treated with DOACs, aims to reduce challenges related to training and experience, thereby increasing the confidence of participating CPs. Another strength of this study lies in the quality and clinical relevance of the data that will be generated. Indeed, patient follow-up calls at three and six months will enable us to explore the clinical relevance of pharmaceutical interventions longitudinally, rather than relying solely on existing clinical impact scoring systems [23, 24].

This study will involve nearly one hundred community pharmacies, which is substantial and represents a logistical challenge that partly depends on how participating professionals perceive and value involvement in clinical research. Barriers to participating in clinical research programs are well documented: limited knowledge and skills in clinical research, lack of time, budget constraints, and insufficient support from peers and the institutions sponsoring the research [25]. In a systematic review of qualitative studies (including surveys), the authors included 19 studies, 4 of which specifically focused on CPs. Across all available data, the main facilitators for engaging in clinical research included interest in the research topic, the expected benefits for patients, and the perceived attractiveness of the pharmacy profession [26], a positive relationship with the research team [26], and fair (especially financial) compensation for the time spent on the project [26]. Among the reported barriers, the lack of experience in clinical research was highlighted, leading to reduced confidence when explaining the study to patients, presenting its aims and implications, and addressing data protection considerations [27, 28]. From a more practical standpoint, a Dutch survey reported that CPs were more likely to participate in research when they clearly perceived its relevance to their practice, when patient selection procedures were not too burdensome, when study duration was sufficiently long to allow manageable workload planning, and when their involvement was transversal, all features that correspond to the present study protocol [29]. With a topic that is easy to communicate and explain, relevant to a large number of patients given the drug class involved, simple patient inclusion procedures, and longitudinal patient follow-up handled by the study sponsor rather than the pharmacists, we believe this study offers an appealing model to encourage participation of CPs in clinical research. To our knowledge, only one French study has examined this issue through surveys and semi-structured interviews, and it reported similar findings [30].

The formulation of pharmacist interventions following the prescriptions review is well established and widely practiced within the profession [31]. However, the practice of incorporating laboratory test results into this analysis is less common. The present study does not impose any specific method for obtaining RF value, nor any particular estimation method, in order to reflect the realities and constraints of ambulatory practice. Regarding the formulae used to obtain the relevant value, laboratory reports generally provide three values: creatinine clearance (Cockcroft–Gault) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (aMDRD or CKD-EPI). It is well established that the preferred value for dose adjustment of DOACs is creatinine clearance, as the other formulae tend to overestimate glomerular filtration rate [32]. Therefore, CPs are instructed, when multiple estimates are available, to systematically use the lowest value to standardize practice.

One of the secondary objectives of this study is also to assess the difficulty of accessing the lab value information, as the sharing and availability of this data is far from systematic. The results will help determine to what extent sharing this information is effective in the context of emerging coordinated-care models. In the longer term and based on the model developed in Canada—particularly in the province of Québec following the adoption of new legislation in 2015—it may be possible for CPs to prescribe laboratory tests to enhance the level of pharmacist analysis and adjust doses accordingly when required [33].

The HEMORRAG-IR study aims to demonstrate that integrating renal function information into the prescriptions review by CPs can generate pharmacist interventions with at least a minor clinical impact, namely by reducing the incidence of bleeding events in patients with renal impairment treated with DOACs. The expected results will support the systematic provision of renal function information in daily practice as a lever to reduce medication-related harm.
