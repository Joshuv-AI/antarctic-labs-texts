A simple constitutive framework for thinning–thickening transitions in dense granular–fluid mixtures
Authors: Yadong Wang, Wei Wu, Xiaogang Guo, Hongcheng Jiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A unified constitutive framework is developed for dense fine-granular mixtures exhibiting apparent shear thinning and continuous or discontinuous shear thickening in the macro-viscous flow regime. The effective granular stress is decomposed into viscoplastic structural and suspension-stress contributions. The former is obtained from a hypoplastic model combined with a Herschel–Bulkley rheology, whereas the latter encompasses the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu (I_m)$$\end{document}μ(Im) rheology and the Wyart–Cates frictional-contact transition, and shows stress-dependent jamming state. Benchmark calculations show that the proposed formulation captures the transition between shear-thinning and shear-thickening behaviour. A depth-averaged long-wave analysis further shows that non-monotonicity induced by discontinuous shear thickening may persist to promote instability in the macro-viscous flow regime.

Introduction

Granular materials interacting with fluids are involved in a wide range of geotechnical and geo-environmental processes, including internal erosion, piping, sediment transport, debris flows, tailings transport, and fluid-driven particle migration in soils [20, 23, 26]. Depending on the solid fraction, confining stress, interstitial-fluid viscosity, and shear rate, a granular–fluid mixture may behave as a quasi-static granular skeleton, a viscous suspension, or a rapidly sheared granular flow dominated by particle inertia and collisions [4, 7, 8, 23, 58, 60, 64, 65]. After flow onset, such mixtures may further exhibit apparent shear thinning, continuous shear thickening (CST), or discontinuous shear thickening (DST) [9, 12, 37]. Developing a unified constitutive framework for these coupled responses remains challenging, especially for dense fine-granular mixtures in which structural resistance, viscous and collisional effects, and stress-activated shear thickening may coexist.

For relatively coarse granular–fluid mixtures, substantial progress has been made by describing the constitutive response through transitions among quasi-static, macro-viscous, and grain-inertial regimes [4, 33, 51, 53, 58, 60]. In such regime-based descriptions, a modified inertial number can be used to characterise the competition among the shear timescale, the particle-inertial timescale, and the viscous timescale of the interstitial fluid [2, 8]. The resulting friction and dilatancy laws compactly represent enduring frictional contacts, collisional momentum transfer, and macro-viscous resistance, forming a useful constitutive basis for coarse granular–fluid deformation [5, 55]. However, these formulations generally preserve a monotonic stress–shear-rate relation and do not explicitly account for the stress-activated transition from lubricated to frictional contacts once short-range interparticle repulsion is overcome [10]. This omission is particularly consequential for dense fine-granular suspensions, since DST-induced non-monotonicity can affect gravity-driven free-surface flows through inertia-free surface-wave instabilities and jammed-front-controlled propagation [7, 16].

Dense fine-granular suspensions may exhibit a sequence of regimes, including finite resistance near flow onset, apparent shear thinning, macro-viscous flow, and subsequent CST, DST, or shear jamming [12, 13, 18, 35]. These behaviours reflect the coexistence and competition of structural resistance and suspension-flow resistance. The structural resistance is associated with residual particle-skeleton strength and short-range attractive or structuring effects [24, 41, 49], whereas the suspension-flow resistance arises from viscous lubrication, hydrodynamic interactions, collisional effects, and stress-activated frictional contacts [34]. As the relative contribution of structural resistance decreases, the response becomes increasingly governed by the suspension-flow resistance [15]. At low applied stresses, particles may remain separated by thin lubrication layers or stabilising short-range repulsive forces, so that the suspension follows a relatively frictionless or weakly frictional branch. Once the applied stress exceeds a characteristic repulsive stress scale, direct frictional contacts are activated and may proliferate throughout the particle network [19, 31, 44]. The resulting frictional network increases the resistance to shear and drives the suspension towards a frictional branch with a lower effective jamming volume fraction [37]. A constitutive description must therefore accommodate both the low-rate structural response and the stress-activated suspension response.

Existing constitutive approaches describe different parts of this coupled problem [21, 46]. Elastoplastic models incorporating critical state provide a classical framework for describing rate-independent granular strength, dilatancy, and quasi-static shear deformation [29, 43, 45, 63, 66]. As an alternative route to critical-state soil modelling, hypoplastic models describe granular deformation through nonlinear stress-rate equations, without requiring an explicit yield surface, plastic potential, or flow rule [30, 40, 56, 57, 59]. Beyond quasi-static soil mechanics, related μ (I)μ(I)-type and hybrid critical-state–μ (I)μ(I)-type granular-flow formulations represent frictional and dilatant flow regimes through viscous, inertial, or mixed timescale ratios [3, 5, 23, 54, 55]. When supplemented by phenomenological yield-stress laws such as Bingham-type or Herschel–Bulkley-type descriptions [11, 15, 25, 39], these frameworks can represent residual strength and post-yield rate dependence, but not the stress-activated lubricated-to-frictional transition caused by overcoming short-range repulsion.Fig. 1Conceptual illustration of the mechanical evolution of a fine-granular suspension from flow initiation to apparent shear thinning, macro-viscous flow, and subsequent shear thickening, together with the dominant interparticle interactions

By contrast, Wyart–Cates-type models and related suspension-rheology descriptions capture shear thickening through a stress-dependent fraction of frictional contacts and a corresponding stress-dependent jamming state [48, 61]. In many suspension-rheology descriptions, the low-stress branch is commonly treated as viscous, lubricated, or frictionless, whereas more general continuum mixture-theory formulations have incorporated frictional-contact evolution and granular skeleton effects in dense fine-particle suspensions [6]. For geomechanical granular–fluid mixtures, including debris-flow materials and soft Earth suspensions [28, 38], residual/yield structural resistance may nevertheless need to be retained, for example through a Herschel–Bulkley-type viscoplastic representation [14]. This is consistent with observations that finite yield stress in adhesive or cohesive non-Brownian suspensions can be governed by frictional constraints and shear-jammed microstructures [41, 49].

This study develops a simple constitutive framework for dense granular–fluid mixtures covering low-rate yielding, apparent shear thinning, macro-viscous flow, and shear-thickening transitions. The effective granular stress is decomposed into a viscoplastic structural stress, representing particle-skeleton resistance near flow onset, and a suspension stress incorporating viscous, collisional, and stress-activated frictional-contact effects. Apparent shear thinning is associated with the decreasing relative dominance of the viscoplastic structural stress, while CST and DST arise from the stress-activated evolution of the suspension stress towards a frictional-contact-dominated state through a stress-dependent jamming state. The formulation provides a unified constitutive description of thinning–thickening transitions in dense granular–fluid mixtures.

Section 2 introduces the mechanical picture and effective-stress decomposition, followed by the constitutive formulation and validation in Sects. 3–5. Section 6 examines a minimal depth-averaged free-surface application, and Sect. 7 summarises the main conclusions and limitations.

Theoretical framework

Mechanical picture of fine-granular suspensions

In the present work, a pragmatic size-based distinction is adopted for descriptive purposes [6]: mixtures with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_s \gtrsim 100~\mu \textrm{m}$$\end{document}ds≳100μm are referred to as relatively coarse granular–fluid mixtures, whereas those with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_s \lesssim 100~\mu \textrm{m}$$\end{document}ds≲100μm are referred to as fine-granular suspensions. This distinction is introduced solely to organise the benchmark materials considered here, and should not be interpreted as a universal regime boundary.

Fine-granular suspensions are idealised here as systems in which two effective resistance contributions coexist and compete: structural resistance and suspension-flow resistance, as presented schematically in Fig. 1. The structural resistance denotes the particle-skeleton resistance retained near flow onset, including residual or yield strength associated with unresolved contact, bonding, or structuring effects [14, 22, 42, 49, 62]. The suspension-flow resistance denotes the flow-induced resistance during shearing, including viscous lubrication, hydrodynamic interactions, collisions, and stress-activated frictional contacts after the repulsive barrier is overcome [44, 61].

Within this mechanical picture, an apparent shear-thinning branch is attributed to the decreasing relative dominance of the structural resistance as shearing develops, whereas CST and DST are attributed to the stress-activated growth of frictional-contact networks once the thin lubrication layers or stabilising repulsive barrier is overcome. This idealisation motivates the effective-stress decomposition introduced below, in which the structural resistance and the suspension-flow resistance are represented by separate stress contributions.

Scope of constitutive analysis

The constitutive framework is developed under steady homogeneous simple shear at fixed volume. This idealised loading isolates the local constitutive response from geometric change, evolving stress paths, and boundary-induced heterogeneity [18, 44, 53], allowing the respective roles of structural resistance, suspension-flow resistance, and stress-activated frictional transition to be identified before addressing more general boundary-value problems.

Constitutive formulation of the effective granular stress

The constitutive formulation of the effective granular stress is developed in this section based on the decomposition introduced in Sect. 2. Section 3.1 first recalls the previous formulation for relatively coarse mixtures as a baseline, followed by the formulations of the viscoplastic structural stress and suspension stress in Sects. 3.2 and 3.3, respectively. The former represents low-rate yielding and apparent shear thinning, whereas the latter incorporates stress-activated frictional contacts to capture CST and DST. Together, these two stress contributions provide a unified constitutive description of thinning–thickening transitions.

Remark

The proposed formulation decomposes the effective granular stress into a viscoplastic structural stress and a suspension stress. The former, given by Eq. (13), captures the low-rate yielding and apparent shear-thinning branch, whereas the latter, defined by Eqs. (24), accounts for the viscous, collisional, and repulsion-controlled frictional-contact contributions responsible for CST, DST, and jamming.

The observed rheological transitions are interpreted as changes in the relative contributions of the structural and suspension stresses within the effective granular stress, rather than as switches between separate constitutive laws. This provides a unified mechanical interpretation of the transition from solid-like yielding to suspension flow and frictional thickening.

By combining these two stress contributions within the effective-stress framework of Eqs. (1)–(3), the present model describes the transition from flow onset and structural shear thinning to macro-viscous flow, stress-activated thickening, and frictional flow or jamming.

Case I: Debris-flow rheology without DST

To assess the generality of the proposed framework in the absence of DST, the model is compared with the experimental data reported by Kostynick et al. for natural debris-flow materials [28]. In these experiments, the flow behaviour is governed by frictional contacts and viscous interactions, while repulsion-controlled frictional activation leading to DST is not evident.

Within the present framework, this situation corresponds to a regime in which the stress-activated frictional-transition mechanism introduced in Sect. 3 does not play a dominant role, and the response is governed by the interplay between the viscoplastic structural stress and the suspension stress. The data therefore provide a suitable benchmark for examining the transition from quasi-static to macro-viscous behaviour without invoking DST.

Figure 8 shows the comparison between the model predictions and the experimental measurements. As shown in Fig. 8a, the shear stress increases monotonically with shear rate for all solid fractions, without exhibiting the abrupt stress jump characteristic of DST. The corresponding apparent viscosity shown in Fig. 8b displays a pronounced shear-thinning behaviour at low shear rates, followed by a gradual approach towards a macro-viscous regime at higher shear rates. This trend reflects the progressive reduction of the relative contribution of the viscoplastic structural stress as the suspension-flow resistance becomes dominant.

This provides further support for the consistency of the model across different rheological regimes.

Case III: Stress-activated response with finite structural resistance

This case addresses suspensions in which a finite viscoplastic structural stress is present at flow onset for different solid fractions. Such behaviour is relevant for dense fine-granular suspensions encountered in geotechnical contexts, where the material may not start from a purely viscous state. The comparison is made with the data of Richards et al.[41].

The model predictions in Fig. 11 indicate that, at low shear rates, the stress is dominated by a finite viscoplastic structural contribution, giving rise to a plateau at flow onset. The dimensionless shear stress is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau ^*_m = \tau _m / \tau _y$$\end{document}τm∗=τm/τy. As the shear rate increases, the relative contribution of the viscoplastic structural stress decreases and the response transitions towards a macro-viscous regime, accompanied by a monotonic decrease in relative viscosity. Although the flow curve steepens at higher stresses, no distinct shear-thickening regime emerges in the viscosity–shear-rate representation. This behaviour is consistent with the framework of Richards et al. [41], in which frictional contacts are active from the onset of flow in the presence of a finite yield stress, while adhesive constraints are progressively relaxed. In contrast to classical DST, which arises from a stress-induced transition between lubricated and frictional states, such a transition is absent here. The resulting response is therefore governed by yielding and structural relaxation over an extended stress range, precluding the abrupt viscosity increase characteristic of DST.

These results indicate that yielding and shear thickening are governed by competing stress scales rather than independent mechanisms. The presence of a finite yield stress imposes a dominant threshold that controls both flow initiation and microstructural evolution, thereby delaying or suppressing the emergence of DST (see a similar trend in Fig. 5). The absence of a distinct shear-thickening regime thus reflects the predominance of yielding over stress-activated contact transitions in this class of suspensions.

Case IV: Discontinuous shear thickening across viscous–inertial regimes

The final case examines the influence of particle inertia on the DST response and its interaction with the stress-activated frictional transition. The objective is to demonstrate that the proposed framework can describe discontinuous shear thickening across macro-viscous, inertia-influenced, and transitional regimes within a single constitutive formulation.

The comparison is made with the data of [34]. In this case, the inertial contribution is introduced through the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha _i$$\end{document}αi, which controls the relative importance of collisional effects in the modified inertial number. Different regimes are explored by varying the stress level and the inertial weighting factor.

As shown in Fig. 12, the model captures the main classes of behaviour observed in the benchmark data. When inertial effects remain weak at the transition point, the response retains the macro-viscous structure observed in Sect. 4. As the inertial contribution increases, the high-stress branch progressively departs from viscous scaling and approaches a grain-inertial regime. In the limiting case, the DST transition occurs directly within the inertia-dominated regime. Intermediate cases exhibit a smooth crossover in which frictional activation and viscous–inertial transition occur over overlapping ranges of stress and shear rate.

These results indicate that the proposed model provides a unified description of DST across different flow regimes, without requiring separate constitutive laws for viscous and inertial conditions. The transition remains governed by the stress-activated evolution of the frictional state variable f, while the asymptotic behaviour of the high-stress branch is controlled by the balance between viscous and inertial contributions.

Depth-averaged long-wave analysis in a macro-viscous limit

This section presents a minimal free-surface application of the proposed constitutive framework, focusing on whether DST-induced non-monotonicity survives depth averaging and can trigger shallow-flow instability [16]. More complete boundary-value problems, such as front propagation and constant-speed travelling states [7], require the full coupled formulation and are beyond the scope of the present work.

The analysis is restricted to a macro-viscous limiting case, using the material parameters of Case II in Sect. 5.2. The flow is assumed to be shallow, slowly varying, and inertia-free in the momentum balance. The solid fraction Φ Φ is taken as spatially uniform and constant in time, capillary effects are neglected, the pressure field is hydrostatic, and no-slip and traction-free conditions are imposed at the base and free surface, respectively. Conclusions

This study develops a simple constitutive framework for thinning–thickening transitions in dense granular–fluid mixtures by decomposing the effective granular stress into viscoplastic structural and suspension-stress contributions. The main conclusions are: The thinning–thickening transition is governed by the changing relative contributions of the two effective-stress components. The viscoplastic structural stress controls residual/yield resistance and apparent shear thinning at low shear rates, whereas the suspension stress becomes dominant as shearing develops. Stress-activated frictional contacts and the associated evolution of the jamming state give rise to CST and DST.The benchmark cases show that both DST and non-DST responses can be reproduced within the same constitutive formulation by varying the corresponding material parameters, demonstrating the unified character of the framework across distinct granular–fluid rheological responses.The form of the thinning–thickening response reflects the interplay among structural resistance, frictional-contact activation, and particle inertia. Finite residual/yield resistance can delay, mask, or suppress a distinct DST branch, whereas increasing particle inertia shifts the high-stress response from macro-viscous towards grain-inertial behaviour.The depth-averaged analysis shows that DST-induced non-monotonicity can persist after depth averaging. In the macro-viscous limit, a negative depth-averaged constitutive slope produces anti-diffusive long-wave growth, indicating a possible constitutive origin of free-surface instability without inertia.As a constitutive framework rather than a complete engineering model, its extension to geotechnical and geo-environmental boundary-value problems will require coupling with evolving porosity, pore-fluid flow, and spatial heterogeneity, together with further calibration and validation against well-controlled experiments.
