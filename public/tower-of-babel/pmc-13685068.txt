Ureteral stricture prediction of occurrence after ureteroscopic lithotripsy
Authors: Yuchen Gao, Shuai Liu, Jun Luo, Nueraihemaiti Yimingniyizi, Xiao Xu, Hongmin Zhou, Yunze Dong, Bowen Chen, Hao Chen, Yanhua Chen, Xiangcheng Zhan, Ding Liu, Xudong Yao, Tiancheng Xie, Yunfei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesUreteral stricture is a significant complication after ureteroscopic lithotripsy (URSL). This study aimed to identify risk factors for post-URSL ureteral stricture and develop a nomogram to predict the timing and probability of stricture occurrence within 1–3 years postoperatively, thereby facilitating individualized follow-up.MethodsWe enrolled patients who underwent holmium laser ureteroscopic lithotripsy (URSL) at a single institution between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2021, and were diagnosed with postoperative ureteral stricture. Baseline characteristics, in-hospital data, surgical indicators, computed tomography data, and laboratory test results of patients with and without stenosis were compared. Cases (n = 70) were patients diagnosed with postoperative ureteral stricture; controls (n = 70) were selected using propensity score matching (age, sex, stone laterality, surgery date) from patients without stricture. T-tests, Pearson’s chi-squared, or Fisher’s exact tests, LASSO and Cox regression analyses, receiver operating characteristic and Kaplan–Meier curve analyses, and nomogram analysis were used to predict post-URSL ureteral stricture risk factors and timing.ResultsHypertension incidence (P = 0.038), ureteral stent placement time (P = 0.002), stone location (P = 0.04), and hydronephrosis grade (P < 0.001) differed significantly between the groups, and stone impaction were independent risk factors for ureteral stricture. The nomogram showed good discrimination (C-index 0.720) and calibration. Preoperative fever appeared as a protective factor but should be interpreted cautiously due to potential confounding.ConclusionThe nomogram, which based on hydronephrosis grade, ureteral stone location, and incarcerated stones can predict post-URSL ureteral stricture risk and may guide personalized follow-up. External validation is needed before clinical application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12894-026-02201-6.Keywords: Lithotripsy, Nomograms, Pathologic constriction, Regression analysis, Urolithiasis

Introduction

Urolithiasis is a common urological disease affecting patients worldwide [1]. The incidence is 10.1% in the USA [2], 3.5–15% across different European regions [1], and 4.0% in China [3]. Additionally, the recurrence rate is 67% [4]. Shockwave lithotripsy (SWL), ureteroscopy (URS), and percutaneous nephrolithotomy are the most important surgical treatments for urolithiasis. Although the stone-free rate is higher following URS than after SWL, URS has a high complication rate [5]. However, with technological improvements, the URS utilization rate has increased.

URS is highly effective for urolithiasis but carries notable complications. Intraoperative complications occur in up to 11.5% of cases, including bleeding (4.5%) and ureteral injury requiring stenting (1.8%); postoperative fever/infection affects 6.3% [6, 7]. Compared with shockwave lithotripsy, URS offers higher stone-free rates but more complications [8]. A main complication of URS is postoperative ureteral stricture [9], which can lead to ureteral obstruction; severe obstruction may cause hydronephrosis, ultimately affecting kidney function. According to recent meta-analyses, ureteral stricture is one of the most concerning complications, with an overall incidence of 1.7–2.9% and an upward trend [10, 11]; in impacted stones, the rate can reach 24% [12]. Many factors contribute to the occurrence of ureteral stricture, including ureteral injury, use of large-caliber surgical instruments [13], viral infection [14], and radiotherapy [15]. A meta-analysis indicated that the incidence of ureteral stricture following urolithiasis treatments is correlated with the type of treatment, stone location and impaction, preoperative hydronephrosis, and intraoperative perforation [16]. Treatment of established strictures is challenging and often requires endoscopic or reconstructive surgery [17, 18] with high recurrence rates.. Therefore, prevention through identification of modifiable risk factors is critical.

Multiple factors, including ureteral injury, stone impaction, and prolonged surgery, have been associated with post-URS ureteral stricture [12]. However, previous studies have mostly used correlation or logistic regression analyses, which can identify risk factors but are unable to predict recurrence time or rate. In this study, we aimed to apply LASSO-Cox regression and nomogram analysis to not only identify risk factors but also predict the probability and timing of ureteral stricture within 1–3 years after URSL, providing a tool for personalized follow-up.

Patients and methods

This study aimed to identify and evaluate risk factors associated with ureteral stricture following ureteroscopy (URS) through a retrospective case–control design, which complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital of Tongji University (approval number: 21K105). Patient consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of this study. Our results are reported in line with the Strengthening the Reporting of Cohort, Cross-Sectional, and Case–Control Studies in Surgery 2021 criteria [19] and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines [20].

Patient data were obtained from Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital. This study retrospectively included patients who underwent holmium laser ureteroscopic lithotripsy (URSL) at the Department of Urology between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2021, and were diagnosed with postoperative ureteral stricture. The diagnosis of ureteral stricture was confirmed by imaging (e.g., computed tomography urography, intravenous urography, or retrograde pyelography) and/or ureteroscopy during follow-up.

As controls, we included patients who underwent URSL during the same period and did not develop postoperative ureteral stricture. To minimize selection bias and ensure comparability between groups, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) using the following variables: age (± 2 years), sex, stone laterality, and date of surgery (± 3 months). The matching process was conducted using the “MatchIt” package (version 4.5.1) in R software (version 4.1.2) with the nearest neighbor matching algorithm and a caliper width of 0.02. This procedure successfully matched 70 controls to the 70 cases.

Data collection and inclusion and exclusion criteria

Ureteral stricture was diagnosed based on imaging findings (computed tomography urography [CTU], intravenous urography [IVU], or retrograde pyelography) and/or ureteroscopy during follow-up, confirming the presence of luminal narrowing with or without proximal ureteral and renal pelvic dilation. The time of diagnosis was defined as the date of the first imaging or endoscopic confirmation of stricture. Functional assessment (e.g., diuretic renography) was not routinely performed but was available in a subset of patients. Only patients with at least 12 months of follow-up were included to ensure adequate observation for stricture occurrence.

The data collection and the inclusion are exclusion criteria are detailed in Online Resource 1.

Surgical procedure

All procedures were performed by experienced urologists using a standardized technique. A holmium laser machine (Lumenis Pulse 100H) was used with a semirigid ureteroscope (Karl Storz, 8/9.8Fr). A ureteral access sheath (12/14Fr, Cook Medical) was used at the surgeon’s discretion (in 2.86% of cases). Laser settings were power 10–20 W and frequency 10–20 Hz. Stone fragmentation was continued until fragments were ≤ 2 mm. A double-J stent was placed postoperatively in all patients and removed after 2–4 weeks depending on stone burden and ureteral condition. Operative time was recorded from cystoscope insertion to stent placement.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) and were compared using independent sample t-tests if the data exhibited s normal distribution with homogeneous variance, or with the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%) and were compared using Pearson’s chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. A P-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

LASSO and Cox regression analyses

LASSO regression was used for the preliminary screening of indicators to be included in the Cox regression. To this end, the R software package “glmnet” was used, and the interval between two operations, the occurrence of ureteral stricture, and 22 clinical indicators were integrated. Additionally, 10-fold cross-validation was established to obtain the optimal model.

Cox regression models were used to identify the risk factors for post-URSL ureteral stricture. Models were established using univariate, multivariate, and LASSO–Cox regressions. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed using stepwise backward selection. Prognostic factors (P < 0.05) in multivariate or LASSO regression analyses were used to construct modal plots. The R software package “survival” was used to integrate the interval between operations, ureteral stricture occurrence, and related clinical indicators, and the prognostic significance of these features was assessed using Cox regression. The overall prognostic significance, C-index, and statistical significance were calculated.

Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis

The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) was calculated to assess the discriminative ability of the models using the R package “pROC” (version 1.17.0.1). The patient follow-up time and risk score calculated using Cox regression were obtained, ROC analyses were performed at 365, 730, and 1,095 days, and the AUC and confidence intervals (CI) were evaluated using the CI function of pROC to obtain the final AUC results. The process was conducted 1,000 times randomly. An AUC > 0.7 indicated a reasonable estimation.

Kaplan–Meier curve analysis

The Kaplan–Meier (K–M) method was used to analyze the incidence of post-URSL ureteral stricture. The R package “maxstat” was used to set the minimum group sample size to 25% and the maximum sample size to < 75%. The best risk score cutoff was obtained using Cox regression. Based on this cutoff value, the patients were divided into high- and low-risk groups. The “survfit” function of the R package “survival” was further used to analyze the prognostic difference between both groups. The log-rank test was used to evaluate significant prognostic differences between the different groups of samples. Finally, a K–M curve was plotted.

Prognostic model and heatmaps of clinical indicators

To demonstrate changes in the stenosis incidence with increasing risk score visually and identify stenosis-related protective and risk factors, the relationship between different risk scores and patients’ follow-up time, ureteral stricture events, and changes in meaningful clinical indicators was analyzed using Cox regression analysis. The results are presented as heatmaps.

Nomogram analysis

A nomogram based on the risk factors to predict the risk of ureteral stricture after URSL was developed. The C-index and K–M and ROC curves were used to evaluate nomogram accuracy, and calibration charts were used to verify the discriminative ability of the nomogram. Based on the results of the multivariable and LASSO–Cox regression, a nomogram was established using the R software package “rms” to integrate statistically significant clinical features and evaluate the prognostic significance of these features in 140 samples. The C-index, 95% CI, and P-values of the model were calculated.

Results

During the study period, a total of 9124 URSL procedures were performed at our institution. Of these, 238 patients were diagnosed with postoperative ureteral stricture, yielding an incidence of 2.61%. After applying exclusion criteria (incomplete data, follow-up < 12 months), 70 patients were included as cases. The control group consisted of 70 patients selected via propensity score matching from the remaining patients without stricture (n = 8886). Baseline characteristics were well balanced after matching (Table 1).

Clinical indicators

We used the t-test and Pearson’s chi-square to compare 22 clinical indicators between the case and control groups. The incidence of hypertension was lower in the case than in the control group (P = 0.038). The ureteral stent placement time was longer in the case than in the control group (P = 0.002). Stone location in the ureter (P = 0.040) and hydronephrosis grade (P < 0.001) differed significantly between groups (Online Resource 2).

We performed multivariate correlation analysis between the 22 clinical indicators and post-URSL ureteral stricture. Hypertension (P = 0.039), ureteral stent placement time (P = 0.001), and hydronephrosis grade (P < 0.001) remained associated with ureteral stricture (Online Resource 3).

Cox and LASSO regression analyses

We evaluated 22 clinical indicators using univariable Cox regression analysis, with significant overall prognostic differences (log test = 0.002, sc test = 0.002, Wald test = 0.005) and a C-index of 0.716. Three indicators in particular exhibited statistical significance: stone location in the ureter (P = 0.037), hydronephrosis grade (P < 0.001), and neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio (NLR) (P = 0.023). Using LASSO–Cox regression analysis, we first integrated the interval between operations, the occurrence of ureteral stricture, and the 22 clinical indicators. We set the lambda value to 0.092 (Fig. 1B) and conducted the analysis on a subset of 137 out of the total 140 samples using the R software package “glmnet. We used six relevant clinical indicators for model construction (Fig. 1C) and performed Cox regression to assess their prognostic significance in 137 samples. The overall prognostic difference was significant (log test < 0.001, scan test < 0.001, and Wald test < 0.001), and the C-index was 0.691. Four indicators in particular were statistically significant: stone location in the ureter (P = 0.023), hydronephrosis grade (P < 0.001), preoperative fever (P = 0.047), and stone impaction (P = 0.003). The results are presented in Table 2.

The incidence of stenosis gradually increased with increasing risk scores. The presence of preoperative fever was a protective factor, and its occurrence exhibited a downward trend with increasing risk scores. A high hydronephrosis grade, stone impaction, and stone location in the upper portion of the ureter were risk factors. With increasing risk scores, the grades of these indicators showed upward trends. The results are presented as a heatmap.

K–M curve of Cox regression results

We visualized multivariable-Cox-KM and LASSO-Cox-KM curves based on the risk scores calculated using Cox regression. The best cutoff value of the risk score for multivariable Cox-KM was 2.068 (Fig. 1G), with significant differences observed (P < 0.001). The best cutoff value of the risk score for the LASSO-Cox-KM curve was 1.814 (Fig. 1H), with significant differences (P < 0.001).

Nomogram analysis

According to the results of the multivariable Cox regression and LASSO–Cox regression, we used hydronephrosis grade, stone impaction, stone location in the ureter, NLR, and preoperative fever presence for nomogram analysis. Hydronephrosis grade, stone impaction, and stone location in the ureter were statistically significant. We then established a nomogram to evaluate the prognostic significance of these features in the 140 samples by integrating data on the interval between operations, the occurrence of ureteral stricture, and the abovementioned three features. The C-index of the overall model was 0.720 (95% CI: 0.6588–0.782, P < 0.001). A nomogram and calibration curve are shown in Fig. 2A–B.

We also plotted K–M (Fig. 2C) and ROC curves (Fig. 2D) based on the nomogram risk score. The best cutoff value of the risk score was 0.184 (P < 0.001). The AUC values for 365, 730, and 1,095 days were 0.77, 0.74, and 0.78, respectively.

Discussion

Although multiple factors have been associated with post-URSL ureteral stricture [12], including ureteral injury [21], stone impaction [22], and prolonged operative time [13], no previous clinical studies have used LASSO–Cox regression and nomogram analyses to analyze the timing and risk factors of post-URSL ureteral stricture. Moretto et al. discovered that the prevalence of ureteral stricture related to URS varied significantly, with stone impaction markedly increasing the incidence of stricture [16]. In this study, we reviewed the clinical indicators of patients diagnosed with ureteral stricture after holmium laser URSL using a case–control design. We found that hydronephrosis grade, stone impaction, and ureteral stone location were independent risk factors for post-URSL ureteral stricture using LASSO-Cox regression and nomogram analyses. These findings are consistent with previous studies [11, 22, 23]. However, it is important to consider the demographic characteristics of our cohort. The study population predominantly consisted of middle-aged and older males (mean age 53 years, 71% male), which may limit the generalizability of our results to other populations. Male patients in this age group often have benign prostatic hyperplasia, which can increase the technical difficulty of ureteroscopy, particularly for proximal ureteral stones, potentially raising the risk of ureteral injury and subsequent stricture. In contrast, female patients have shorter and straighter urethras, making ureteroscopic procedures generally easier. Due to sample size constraints, we were unable to perform sex- or age-stratified analyses. Future studies with larger and more diverse populations should investigate whether the impact of these risk factors varies by sex and age.

Cox regression and nomogram analyses revealed that, with increasing hydronephrosis grade, the probability of ureteral stricture was significantly higher, and occurrence time was significantly shorter. Similarly, Al-Nabulsi et al. retrospectively analyzed 297 patients who underwent URS to treat impacted renal stones. They identified hydronephrosis grade, residual fragments, and intraoperative ureteric injury as risk factors for post-URS ureteral stricture [23]. In addition, our nomogram demonstrated a significantly higher probability of ureteral calculi in the proximal than the distal ureter causing stenosis. Consistent with this finding, Brito et al. also found that the proximal ureter was more likely to develop stenosis during treatment of ureteral calculi with pneumatic ballistic URSL. Furthermore, the incidence rates of stenosis in different locations of the ureter were 44%, 8.3%, and 4.7% [24], respectively. Li et al. suggested that the upper ureter is more likely to be damaged during URSL than the mid and lower ureters, leading to ureteral stricture [25]. In our study, ureteral stricture incidence was markedly higher in patients with stone impaction than in those without. Elashry et al. summarized 5,133 cases of URS, reporting that 12 patients developed postoperative ureteral stricture, all of whom had impacted stones [22]. Legemate et al. found that the intraoperative complication rate of impacted stones was higher than that of non-impacted stones [26].

In addition to the factors identified in our study, numerous other variables have been implicated in the pathogenesis of post-URS ureteral stricture. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Moretto et al. [11] confirmed stone impaction and proximal stone location as significant risk factors, consistent with our findings. However, factors such as stone size, operative time, intraoperative ureteral injury, and the use of high-power laser settings have also been reported [12, 27]. In our analysis, stone size and operative time did not reach statistical significance. This may be attributed to sample size limitations, collinearity with stone impaction (impacted stones tend to be larger and require longer operative time), or incomplete data capture. Notably, recent studies have highlighted the potential impact of laser technology. Villani et al. [28] reported a high ureteral stricture rate associated with high-power, high-frequency Thulium fiber laser lithotripsy, suggesting that energy settings may play a critical role. Similarly, Tan et al. [10] emphasized the importance of ureteral access sheath use and stone burden in stricture formation. Unfortunately, our retrospective design did not allow us to collect detailed information on these variables, including the use of ureteral access sheaths, specific laser power settings, intraoperative ureteral injuries, and stone composition. The absence of these potentially important confounders limits the comprehensiveness of our nomogram and may have influenced the observed associations. Future prospective studies should incorporate these variables to develop more robust predictive models.

Additionally, we included indicators such as preoperative fever presence and NLR, which have been used to predict prognoses in patients with infectious diseases. Guan et al. found that NLR could be used as a predictor of systemic inflammatory response syndrome incidence after flexible URSL [29]. We observed that preoperative fever appeared to be associated with a lower risk of ureteral stricture. This finding is counterintuitive and should be interpreted with extreme caution. It may reflect residual confounding or selection bias rather than a true protective effect. For instance, patients with preoperative fever may have received more aggressive antibiotic therapy and closer perioperative monitoring, potentially mitigating inflammation and ureteral damage. Alternatively, fever could be a marker for earlier presentation and shorter duration of stone impaction. However, given the lack of biological plausibility and the potential for unmeasured confounding, this result should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Further studies are needed to explore the relationship between preoperative infectious status and ureteral stricture.

However, previous studies have arrived at different conclusions regarding the causes of ureteral stricture. Fam et al. suggested that intraoperative ureteral perforation, ureteral mucosal injury, stone size, and stone impaction duration were not causes of ureteral calculi after URS [27]. This discrepancy may be due to differences in study populations, surgical techniques, or definitions of stricture, highlighting the need for standardized reporting.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces potential selection and information bias, despite propensity score matching. Second, the sample size, while reasonable for a single-center study, is modest and may limit statistical power to detect weaker associations. Third, the diagnosis of ureteral stricture relied mainly on imaging, with functional assessment not uniformly available, which may lead to misclassification. Fourth, we lacked data on several potentially important variables, such as ureteral access sheath use, laser energy settings, intraoperative ureteral injury, and stone composition. These omissions may affect the completeness of our risk model. Fifth, the nomogram was developed and validated internally only; no external validation was performed. Finally, the time of ureteral stricture diagnosis was defined as the time at which the patients underwent URS; this time differs from the actual time of ureteral stricture in the patient, warranting further research. Therefore, our model should be considered exploratory, and its performance may be overoptimistic. Future multi-center prospective studies with larger cohorts and comprehensive variable collection are needed to validate and refine the predictive tool.

In conclusion, in this study, we analyzed risk factors for post-URSL ureteral stricture using Cox regression and nomogram analyses. High-grade hydronephrosis, proximal ureteral stone location, and stone impaction are independent risk factors for ureteral stricture after URSL. A nomogram incorporating these three factors demonstrated good predictive performance for stricture occurrence within 1–3 years postoperatively. This tool may assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients for personalized follow-up. However, the findings are preliminary and require external validation before clinical implementation. Future multi-center, longer follow-up studies should be conducted to validate the predictive efficacy of this model.
