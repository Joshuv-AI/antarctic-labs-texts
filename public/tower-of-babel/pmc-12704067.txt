Delandistrogene Moxeparvovec gene therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy: the way forward in South Asian countries
Authors: Priyanka Madaan, Santosh Kumar Mishra, Jithangi Wanigasinghe, Jitendra Kumar Sahu
Published: 2025

Preamble

With growing use of gene therapies for rare diseases in clinical practice, it becomes important to analyse utility and challenges of the recently-approved gene therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) in South Asian context. DMD is a rare, X-linked severe childhood muscular dystrophy caused by pathogenic variations (mostly deletions) in dystrophin gene. It leads to progressive weakness with loss of ambulation in adolescence culminating in early mortality.1 Despite several advancements over the last few decades, there is a significant unmet need for a curative therapy for DMD (Fig. 1a). The accelerated approval of Delandistrogene Moxeparvovec gene therapy for DMD by the US FDA for ambulatory individuals(aged ≥4y) and later for non-ambulatory individuals lifted hopes of patient families.2,3 However, following a safety communication due to reported fatal acute liver failure in two non-ambulatory patients with DMD, the US FDA recently took action to approve new labelling of Delandistrogene Moxeparvovec including the addition of a Boxed Warning and limiting the indication to ambulatory patients only. With these recent developments, we review the current status of this therapy and share viewpoints on several unique challenges relevant to South Asia (Supplementary File). Delandistrogene moxeparvovec is an adeno-associated virus vector-based gene therapy targeted for expression of SRP-9001 dystrophin protein, a shortened dystrophin retaining key functional domains.3 Offered as one-time intravenous infusion, it is contraindicated in exon 8 or 9 (or both) deletion in dystrophin gene. Its evidence landscape is expanding (Supplementary File).4, 5, 6, 7 Common adverse events include vomiting, decreased appetite, and nausea.3 Efficacy outcomes suggest consistent improvement in microdystrophin expression but inconsistent response in North Star Ambulatory Assessments and other functional measures.4, 5, 6, 7 At present, it cannot be considered curative due to age and stage limitations, complexity of re-dosing (immunological sensitivity), and unavailability of long-term data. However, early administration (in absence of significant muscle damage) increases effectiveness.

South Asia: peculiarities and challenges

South Asia is a rapidly-growing, ethnically-diverse region. Economically, this has low-middle-income countries with problems like education, poverty, health, nutrition, and a relatively low government health expenditure.8 There are several peculiarities and challenges for DMD care in South Asia (Fig. 1b).

Limited natural history studies from the subcontinent report a comparatively older age at presentation and diagnostic lag for DMD likely contributed by delay in clinical and genetic diagnosis, limited awareness, and poor screening infrastructure.9 With limited access to newer therapies, oral steroids (mostly prednisolone with varied protocols) and rehabilitative measures are the current standards of care in South Asia.10 Bone health assessments using DEXA are variably done with limited focus on rehabilitative and orthopaedic aspects.10 Gene therapy administration requires specialized storage and handling facilities which are scarce in the region. The research on DMD here is predominantly limited to observational studies.

The economic barrier due to exorbitant cost of gene therapy, priced at over 3 million USD, makes it unaffordable. Limited governmental allocations and negligible insurance coverage result in predominant out-of-pocket expense for gene therapies. Socioeconomic disparities further exacerbate the issue requiring predominant access through managed access and compassionate programs.

Also, public awareness is low in South Asia. Misinformation and cultural beliefs contribute to scepticism and hesitation among patient families and social stigma discourages open discussion about genetic disorders. Complex and fragmented regulatory landscape is another obstacle with inconsistent drug approval processes. The lack of clear policies for gene therapies complicates approvals, often requiring additional local clinical trials.

Potential solutions and the way forward

Regardless of access and economic barriers, it is possible to improve accessibility of gene therapy through some potential solutions (Fig. 1b, Supplementary File).

Capacity building

Infrastructure building focussed on diagnostics, biobanking, bio-manufacturing, establishment of centres of excellence (CoE; for gene therapy administration and adverse event monitoring) with patient registries and clinical trial networks is crucial for development of indigenous gene therapy program. Human resource development include training facilities for physicians, geneticists, and researchers in gene therapy, ethical considerations, clinical trial management, and interdisciplinary collaboration.

Role of governance and regulatory frameworks

Appropriate regulatory and ethical frameworks with clear, streamlined, and expedited regulatory pathways are much needed.

Partnerships and subsidies

Establishment of regional disease committees can provide insights into standardized treatment protocols, facilitating conduct of clinical trials and collaboration among stakeholders. Further, incentivization of pharmaceutical companies and public-private partnerships for research can make the situation better. International collaboration can help in knowledge transfer. Innovative financing schemes and insurance coverage mechanisms might help in equitable access.

Community engagement

Patient advocacy groups can help in raising awareness, promoting early diagnosis, and pushing for policy changes.

Early focused screening

Prenatal diagnosis for high-risk pregnancies can reduce disease prevalence. Newborn screening pilots with creatine kinase muscle isoform (CK-MM) report high sensitivity and moderate specificity. However, screening all newborn boys may be expensive in resource-limited settings and focussed screening of boys with motor delay and/or family history at 18mo-vaccination visit with creatine phosphokinase or CK-MM might be a feasible option.

Development of guidelines for appropriate candidate selection

Developing guidelines for patient selection for gene therapies in South Asia require careful consideration of local healthcare infrastructure, resource availability, cost-effectiveness, and population needs.

Conclusion

Gene therapy represents a transformative hope but realizing its potential in South Asia requires a strategic and multifaceted approach. With the appropriate investments in research, policy, and partnerships, South Asia can contribute meaningfully to global innovation and rare disease treatments. Further, possibilities of inclusion into mainstream and future challenges for gene therapy recipients are still unclear.
