HyCoVAD: A Hybrid SSL-LLM Model for Complex Video Anomaly Detection
Mohammad Mahdi Hemmatyar, Mahdi Jafari, Mohammad Amin Yousefi, Mohammad Reza Nemati, Mobin Azadani, Hamid Reza Rastad, Amirmohammad Akbari

Abstract
Video anomaly detection (VAD) is crucial for intelligent surveillance, but a significant challenge lies in identifying complex anomalies, which are events defined by intricate relationships and temporal dependencies among multiple entities rather than by isolated actions. While self-supervised learning (SSL) methods effectively model low-level spatiotemporal patterns, they often struggle to grasp the semantic meaning of these interactions. Conversely, large language models (LLMs) offer powerful contextual reasoning but are computationally expensive for frame-by-frame analysis and lack fine-grained spatial localization. We introduce HyCoVAD, Hybrid Complex Video Anomaly Detection, a hybrid SSL-LLM model that combines a multi-task SSL temporal analyzer with LLM validator. The SSL module is built upon an nnFormer backbone which is a transformer-based model for image segmentation. It is trained with multiple proxy tasks, learns from video frames to identify those suspected of anomaly. The selected frames are then forwarded to the LLM, which enriches the analysis with semantic context by applying structured, rule-based reasoning to validate the presence of anomalies. Experiments on the challenging ComplexVAD dataset show that HyCoVAD achieves a 72.5% frame-level AUC, outperforming existing baselines by 12.5% while reducing LLM computation. We release our interaction anomaly taxonomy, adaptive thresholding protocol, and code to facilitate future research in complex VAD scenarios.

1
Introduction

Video anomaly detection (VAD) is a fundamental problem in computer vision with wide-ranging applications in surveillance, safety monitoring, and autonomous systems. The central objective is to identify events that deviate from normative visual patterns. Early research in this domain emphasized anomalies characterized by low-level irregularities such as unusual trajectories or motion patterns, but they primarily captured simple, single-agent deviations.
In real-world environments, however, anomalies are often defined by complex interactions among multiple entities rather than by isolated behaviors. Examples include verbal disputes escalating into physical altercations, unattended packages in crowded spaces, or vehicles violating coordinated traffic flow. These interaction-driven anomalies present unique challenges: they emerge from higher-order relationships between agents, unfold over extended temporal horizons, and require context-sensitive reasoning that bridges perception and semantics. To address these challenges, the ComplexVAD dataset
Mumcu et al. 2025
was recently introduced, encompassing 40 interaction anomaly classes across 217 videos. Baselines on this dataset, as shown in
Mumcu et al. 2025
, reveal a low performance for state-of-the-art methods (60% AUC), highlighting a persistent gap between conventional anomaly detection paradigms and the demands of interaction reasoning.

Two complementary research directions have attempted to bridge this gap. Self-supervised learning (SSL) has become a dominant paradigm for VAD, leveraging proxy tasks such as motion prediction, frame reconstruction, and spatial puzzles to learn normal spatiotemporal patterns from unlabeled video. SSL methods are efficient and generalize well to unseen environments. However, recent surveys
Raja et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2024a
emphasize their limitations in hierarchical reasoning: while they can capture atomic visual actions, they struggle to infer semantic meaning in multi-agent interactions.

As a second approach, the advent of large language models (LLMs) has opened new opportunities for vision-language reasoning in video. Training-free approaches leverage LLMs to classify or explain anomalies based on captions or symbolic rules. These methods excel at contextual interpretation but face two critical barriers: (1) high computational cost, as billions of parameters are applied to redundant video frames, and (2) limited spatial precision, as subtle anomalies often evade text-based reasoning.

This paper proposes to unify the efficiency of SSL with the semantic reasoning of LLMs in a detect-validate hybrid framework. Our approach leverages SSL as a proposal generator for potential anomalies and an LLM as a semantic validator for uncertain cases.

The contributions of this work are threefold:

•

We implemented a hybrid anomaly detection framework that integrates an SSL module with a training-free LLM-based stage for complex, interaction-driven events.

•

We introduced a coarse-to-fine filtering mechanism where an efficient SSL module acts as an anomaly proposal generator, reducing the number of requests sent to the LLM validator by approximately 50% while preserving accuracy.

•

Our proposed method establishes the state-of-the-art in detecting anomalies on the ComplexVAD dataset, achieving 72.5% AUC, outperforming existing methods by 12.5%.

2.1
Self-Supervised Learning Approaches

SSL approaches aim to learn meaningful representations from unlabeled data by formulating pretext tasks that capture the inherent structures in video sequences. These tasks enable models to understand normal patterns, facilitating the detection of deviations indicative of anomalies.
A key milestone is the SSMTL framework
Georgescu et al. 2021
, which employs multi-task learning (MTL) (e.g., motion prediction, frame reconstruction) to capture diverse normal patterns and enhance the model’s ability to detect anomalies without relying on labeled datasets. Following this,
Panariello et al. 2022
introduced a consistency-based self-supervised loss to ensure anomaly score alignment across different augmentations of the same video snippet, which significantly improves temporal localization performance.
Barbalau et al. 2023
enhanced the original SSMTL model, naming it SSMTL++. They integrate multi-head self-attention, segmentation prediction, inpainting, pose estimation, and adversarial pseudo-anomaly learning to enrich spatiotemporal representations.

Kesuma et al. 2024
introduced StridedConv3D-VideoSwin into the SSMTL framework, combining the local spatial inductive biases of CNNs with the global modeling capabilities of transformers. By embedding 3D convolutions into the VideoSwin
Liu et al. 2022
architecture, the method enhances feature extraction for video anomaly detection and achieves improved performance on multiple benchmarks while maintaining lower memory usage despite increased computational cost.
Wang et al. 2025
proposed SC-MAFC, a semantic consistency-based framework that integrates appearance, optical flow, and pose through a three-branch encoder-decoder architecture, enforcing semantic consistency and enhancing multi-attribute feature complementarity.

2.2
Language-Guided Anomaly Detection

The advent of LLMs, MLLLMs and VLLMs has opened new avenues for VAD by enabling models to understand and reason about video content through natural language
Tang et al. 2025
,
Xiao et al. 2025
,
Zhao et al. 2025
. These models can generate textual descriptions of video frames eliminating the need for task-specific fine-tuning and assess their normality based on learned knowledge
Chen et al. 2024
,
Tariq et al.
. For example, VideoLLM
Chen et al. 2023
linearizes video frames into token embeddings and feeds them into frozen LLMs to perform multimodal reasoning across eight different video tasks. Video-LLaMA
Zhang et al. 2023
and its successor Video-LLaMA2
Cheng et al. 2024
fuse visual and audio encoders with LLaMA-based backbones, enabling instruction-following over spatial and temporal inputs. Building upon this trajectory, VideoLLaMA3
Zhang et al. 2025
adopts a vision-centric philosophy. Rather than relying on massive low-quality video-text data, it prioritizes high-quality image-text pretraining and introduces innovations like any-resolution vision tokenization and dynamic token pruning.

The capabilities of these models have naturally inspired applications in downstream tasks such as anomaly detection.
A pioneering work, CLIP-AD
Chen et al. 2024
, leveraged CLIP’s cross-modal alignment to score frame anomalies via handcrafted prompts (e.g., ”a suspicious event”). However, this approach struggles with dynamic scenes due to its static textual queries. A significant leap came with LAVAD
Zanella et al. 2024
, which adopts a truly training‑free, caption‑then‑reason pipeline. They generate frame captions by a BLIP‑2
Li et al. 2023
, followed by GPT‑4-based aggregation and anomaly evaluation.
VadCLIP
Wu et al. 2024
explores weakly-supervised vision-language alignment to further refine anomaly detection without requiring dense labels or task-specific retraining. Building on this foundation, the same team introduced AVadCLIP
Wu et al. 2025
, which extends the CLIP-based framework to incorporate audio-visual collaboration for robust anomaly detection.

An emerging direction in language-guided VAD is the integration of rule-based reasoning, which enables models not only to detect deviations in raw visual features but also to interpret them through explicitly defined or induced logical structures. A pioneering work in this vein is AnomalyRuler
Yang et al. 2024
, which frames anomaly detection as a two-stage reasoning process. The architecture enables zero-shot, interpretable anomaly detection and achieves strong results across multiple standard benchmarks. Building upon this paradigm, other studies further explore rule-based and symbolic reasoning.
SlowFastVAD
Ding et al. 2025
employs a dual-speed framework, where a fast visual anomaly detector is coupled with a retrieval-augmented LLM that reasons over ambiguous segments using external knowledge. From a symbolic angle, VED-SR
Zeng et al. 2025
adopts a training-free framework that applies symbolic regression to discover logical expressions governing normal event patterns.

Collectively, these methods reflect a growing trend toward structured, interpretable, and multimodal reasoning in VAD.

2.3
Research Gap Analysis

Existing approaches in VAD demonstrate complementary strengths and inherent limitations. SSL methods excel at capturing localized spatiotemporal features and low-level motion patterns; however, they often fail to capture high-level semantic context and inter-agent dependencies, achieving only 60% AUC on the ComplexVAD benchmark. In contrast, LLM-based techniques are capable of reasoning over interactions and extracting semantic meaning, yet they typically introduce significant computational overhead. To overcome these challenges, We introduce HyCoVAD, a unified framework that employs SSL to detect frames suspected of anomaly and leverages LLM reasoning to validate and confirm these suspicious frames. This integration of SSL efficiency with LLM semantic understanding leads to a substantial performance gain on ComplexVAD, significantly surpassing existing baselines.

3
Methodology

To address the challenges of complex video anomaly detection, we propose a novel two-stage framework that integrates a self-supervised deep multitask architecture with a training-free LLM-based module. In the first stage, the deep multitask model scans the video and selects a subset of suspect frames that are more likely to contain anomalous events, based on low-level spatiotemporal deviations. In the second stage, a vision language model (VLM) and a LLM are combined within a specialized architecture to semantically detect and confirm the truly anomalous frames of this suspect set.

Our approach is designed to efficiently identify potential anomalies based on low-level spatiotemporal deviations and then semantically verify these proposals using high-level contextual information, significantly reducing computational overhead compared to end-to-end LLM-based methods.

Task-specific weights are learned using GradNorm. Stage 2 performs semantic verification: frame captions are generated and refined using temporal and embedding-based alignment, and a LLM evaluates each frame against automatically generated environment-specific rules. However, in the subsequent three frames, the scooter suddenly turns, an illegal and dangerous maneuver. This behavior is flagged as anomalous by the system, highlighting its ability to capture context-dependent rule violations.

3.1
Object Detection

Our analyses are conducted at both the frame level and the object level. Considering objects individually allows the model to attend to specific entities within a scene, while frame-level processing provides a more holistic view of all objects together. As an initial step, we apply object detection on every frame using YOLOv12
Tian et al. 2025
. For each object detected in frame
i
, we construct an object-centered temporal sequence by cropping the corresponding bounding box from the neighboring frames
{i-t,\ldots,i-1,i,i+1,\ldots,i+t}
. Each cropped region is resized to
64\times 64
pixels. The resulting sequence forms the object-centric input for our framework.

3.2
Multi-Task Self-Supervised Deep Learning Module

Our deep model is built upon a MTL paradigm, where a shared visual encoder is trained jointly on multiple self-supervised tasks. This strategy allows the model to learn complementary representations from different supervisory signals. Each task is designed to capture a specific aspect of video data: temporal structure, spatial composition, or relational interaction.

3.2.1
Shared Backbone

Our framework is built upon a shared spatio-temporal encoder that provides common representations for all task-specific branches. We explore two encoder architectures with distinct design principles, and their performance is empirically validated in our experimental section.

As a baseline, we adopt the CNN+CvT encoder introduced by
Barbalau et al. 2023
, which integrates convolutional layers for local feature extraction with convolutional vision transformers (CvT) to capture global dependencies.

To further improve performance, we employ the nnFormer encoder
Zhou et al. 2023
, originally proposed for segmentation tasks in complex medical domains. We hypothesize that its hierarchical transformer design is uniquely suited for our multi-task VAD framework. Unlike single-level architectures, nnFormer progressively extracts features at multiple levels of abstraction, allowing it to jointly capture both fine-grained irregularities (e.g., subtle motion deviations) and long-range contextual patterns (e.g., complex interactions). This flexible feature sharing across tasks that rely on different scales of representation is particularly beneficial for our diverse self-supervised tasks. Furthermore, nnFormer’s demonstrated robustness to noisy and heterogeneous data directly translates to the VAD problem, where subtle anomalies are often inconsistent and difficult to model. We provide a detailed ablation study in
4.5
, which demonstrates that the nnFormer backbone provides a performance gain over the CNN+CvT baseline, justifying its increased complexity for the task of complex anomaly detection.

3.2.2
Middle Frame Prediction with Memory-Augmented Autoencoding

To enhance the architecture of the middle-frame prediction task in
Barbalau et al. 2023
, we incorporate the memory-augmented design of MemAE
Gong et al. 2019
, which retrieves prototypical patterns from a learned memory via attention-based querying. To further improve reconstruction quality, we utilize temporal skip connections from early decoder layers, enabling the decoder to retain fine-grained spatiotemporal details.

The decoder receives both pooled encoder features and temporal skip connections, augments them through memory retrieval, expands them spatially, and reconstructs the middle frame using upsampling and convolutional layers. Skip connections at multiple resolutions are fused into the decoder pathway to improve structural accuracy.

The reconstruction loss is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{middle}}=\|I_{t}-\hat{I}_{t}\|_{1}

(1)

where
I_{t}
is the ground-truth middle frame and
\hat{I}_{t}
is the predicted frame. This task promotes temporal consistency and enhances the model’s sensitivity to subtle anomalies.

3.2.3
Motion Irregularity

To capture temporal consistency, we train a lightweight classifier to distinguish between regular and irregular motion in short frame sequences
Barbalau et al. 2023
. The model receives an intermediate feature map and processes it through two convolutional blocks with residual connections. The flattened features are passed through two fully connected layers to produce binary predictions.
This task encourages the encoder to be sensitive to motion dynamics and discontinuities, such as harsh movements.

We use the cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{irreg}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y^{(i)}\log\hat{y}^{(i)},

(2)

where
y^{(i)}
is the regular / irregular ground-truth label and
\hat{y}^{(i)}
is the predicted class probability.

3.2.4
Jigsaw Puzzle (Spatial Prediction)

To improve spatial understanding, the model is trained to solve a
jigsaw puzzle
task: an object is divided into 2 × 2 shuffled patches and the model predicts the original position of each. This is formulated as
4
independent multi-class classification problems, one per patch, where each patch must be assigned to its correct spatial location. This task encourages learning of object structures and spatial layout.

The decoder head processes encoder features through a convolutional block followed by adaptive average pooling, reducing the feature map to a
2\times 2
grid. Each resulting patch feature vector is then passed through a shared two-layer MLP head consisting of a linear projection to an intermediate feature space, a ReLU activation, dropout regularization, and a final linear layer that outputs a classification over the original patch positions.

The training objective is:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{s-jigsaw}}=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\log\hat{y}_{i,y_{i}},

(3)

where
y_{i}
is the true position index of patch
i
, and
\hat{y}_{i,y_{i}}
is the predicted probability for the correct class.

3.2.5
SocialGRU

Considering the path of moving objects, this task enables us to monitor their positions and verify whether they follow a normal trajectory. Since the behavior of a moving object changes with respect to nearby objects in proximity, we propose a network that tracks the path of each object relative to the paths of neighboring objects.

We employ a lightweight GRU unit both as encoder and decoder
Alahi et al. 2016
, combined with an interleaved attention mechanism. This attention network is trained jointly with the GRUs to fuse the behavior of neighboring objects into the representation of the target object. The attention weights allow the model to focus on the most influential neighbors by assigning them higher scores.

To improve the Social GRU, we additionally concatenate the hidden state of the encoder with the object feature vector extracted over the past 7 frames. This augmented representation provides the model with explicit object identity information, enabling it to relate past trajectories to object-specific features.
Our model is trained to predict the position of each object at the 8th frame given its positions over the previous 7 frames, by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) loss:

\mathrm{MSE}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl\|y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}\bigr\|^{2}

(4)

where
y_{i}
is the ground-truth position and
\hat{y}_{i}
is the predicted position of object
i
.

3.2.6
Task-Specific Weight Learning

Multitask learning often suffers from imbalanced task optimization, where some tasks dominate training, while others lag. To address this, we adopt
GradNorm

Chen et al. 2018
, which dynamically adjusts task weights by aligning their gradient magnitudes to balance training rates.

The total loss is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\sum_{i=1}^{T}w_{i}(t)\cdot\mathcal{L}_{i}(t)

(5)

where
w_{i}(t)
are task-specific weights learned during training. GradNorm updates these weights based on each task’s relative training speed, computed using the normalized loss ratio
\tilde{L}_{i}(t)=\mathcal{L}_{i}(t)/\mathcal{L}_{i}(0)
. The target gradient norm for each task is:

G^{\text{target}}_{i}(t)=\bar{G}(t)\cdot\left(\frac{\tilde{L}_{i}(t)}{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{j=1}^{T}\tilde{L}_{j}(t)}\right)^{\alpha}

(6)

where
\bar{G}(t)
is the average gradient norm and
\alpha
controls the degree of balance.

The learned weights
w_{i}
are used for inference, which ensures consistency between training and anomaly scoring.

3.3
LLM-Based Semantic Reasoning and Anomaly Verification

The process begins with frame-level captioning using a pretrained VLM, which generates descriptive textual summaries of visual content. To ensure alignment between visual information and generated descriptions, we apply a caption refinement step based on multimodal embedding similarity, enhancing semantic fidelity for challenging frames (e.g., those with blur, occlusion, or ambiguity).
Next, to provide the LLM with contextual grounding, we sample a set of unlabeled frames’ captions from the test dataset and prompt the LLM to infer environment-specific norms by analyzing their captions. This allows the model to generate a set of context-aware rules that distinguish normal from abnormal behavior.
Finally, each suspect frame’s refined caption and the extracted contextual rules are presented to the LLM. Through a structured prompt, the LLM performs a verification task to determine whether the described scene constitutes an anomaly.

By explicitly integrating the LLM’s decision-making in rule sets along with semantic frame captions, this approach reduces ambiguity and improves interpretability compared to caption-only input. The structured prompt ensures consistent, rule-based reasoning that can generalize across scenes.

3.3.1
Caption Generation

To enable semantic reasoning via an LLM over visual content, we convert video frames into natural language descriptions through a captioning stage. This process abstracts low-level pixel data into textual representations that describe human activities, object interactions, and scene contexts relevant for anomaly detection.

3.3.2
Caption Refinement

Automated frame-level captions in video-language datasets often exhibit noise or weak semantic alignment with visual content, largely due to motion blur, occlusions, or captioning errors. To address this, we employ a caption refinement strategy that enhances temporal and visual consistency using a joint embedding space.

To improve semantic alignment, each original suspect frame’s caption is refined by selecting the most similar alternative from a local temporal window based on cosine similarity to the frame’s visual embedding, demonstrated as

\hat{c}_{t}=\arg\max_{c_{j}\in\mathcal{W}_{t}}\,\text{Sim}(\text{Embed}_{\text{img}}(f_{t}),\text{Embed}_{\text{txt}}(c_{j})),

(7)

where
\hat{c}_{t}
is the refined caption selected for frame
t
,

\mathcal{W}_{t}
denotes the local temporal window of candidate captions around frame
t
,

f_{t}
represents the visual content of frame
t
,
and
c_{j}
is a caption candidate within
\mathcal{W}_{t}
.
To maintain lightweight preprocessing while fully utilizing all caption content, we adopt a sentence-level chunking strategy optimized for small text encoders. Given a caption composed of
N
sentences
\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{N}\}
, we construct overlapping chunks by concatenating
m
consecutive sentences with an overlap of
o
sentences between chunks, where
1\leq o<m
. This approach produces semantically rich but compact textual segments, enabling robust encoding under the constraints of lightweight embedding models.

The
k
-th chunk is defined as:

\displaystyle c_{k}

\displaystyle=s_{k}\,\|\,s_{k+1}\,\|\,\dots\,\|\,s_{k+m-1}

(8)

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad k=1,\,1+(m-o),\,1+2(m-o),\,\dots

The total number of chunks (demonstrated by
K
) is given by:

K=\left\lfloor\frac{N-m}{m-o}\right\rfloor+1

(9)

Each chunk
c_{k}
, taken from the same caption, is independently processed using the text encoder
\text{Embed}_{\text{txt}}(\cdot)
. This maps each chunk into a shared latent space:

z_{k}=\text{Embed}_{\text{txt}}(c_{k}),\quad z_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(10)

where
d
is the dimension of the shared latent space. To obtain a single representation for the entire caption, the embeddings of all its chunks are aggregated. Here,
\text{Pool}(\cdot)
is an aggregation function used to combine chunk embeddings, commonly implemented as mean or max pooling. The resulting caption-level embedding is given by Eq.
11
.

z_{\text{caption}}=\text{Pool}(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{K})

(11)

We have explored both
local
(temporal window) and
global
(full video) modes. The local search prioritizes temporal coherence, while the global search maximizes semantic alignment.
This refinement process improves temporal coherence and semantic fidelity in downstream tasks.

3.3.3
Rule Generation

Our framework incorporates an unsupervised rule generation module that uses LLM to derive general, context-aware rules for distinguishing normal from anomalous behaviors in video data, based on precomputed frame-level captions.

Given the high frame count per video, analyzing every caption is inefficient and often unnecessary. To address this, we sample 20 frames per video so that representative behaviors are captured efficiently, while ensuring temporal and contextual diversity.
Each sampled caption is processed with structured prompts designed to guide the LLM in generating precise, environment-specific rules. These rules describe normative behaviors (e.g., orderly pedestrian flow, traffic compliance) and potential anomalies (e.g., sudden movements, unexpected actions, obstructions).

Since rule generation occurs per frame, outputs may contain redundancy or contradictions. We address this via an aggregation step, consolidating similar rules, resolving conflicts, and enforcing consistent formatting to create a unified rule set per video. Normal and anomaly rules are separated clearly, duplicates are removed, and all unique anomaly cases are retained. In the presence of contradictory rules, the aggregation follows a worst-case principle, adopting the stricter rule to ensure anomalies are not overlooked. This guarantees both completeness and robustness in the final rule set. Collectively, these rule sets provide explicit semantic grounding for the anomaly verification stage.

3.3.4
Anomaly Detection via LLM

The final anomaly detection stage uses the LLM to decide over the refined frame captions and corresponding rule sets that define normal and anomalous behaviors. For each suspect frame
f_{t}
, with caption
c_{t}
, we provide the LLM with explicit rules for normality and anomaly, denoted as:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{normal}}=\{r^{(n)}_{1},r^{(n)}_{2},\dots\},\quad\mathcal{R}\ _{\text{anomaly}}=\{r^{(a)}_{1},r^{(a)}_{2},\dots\}.

(12)

The LLM is queried using a structured prompt designed to enforce precise and interpretable anomaly detection. The prompt instructs the LLM to analyze the scene using only the provided rules and description, and to output whether the scene is anomalous, along with which rules are broken or preserved. The LLM output for frame
f_{t}
is represented as Eq.
13
,

y_{t}=\text{LLM}\big(c_{t},\mathcal{R}_{\text{normal}},\mathcal{R}_{\text{anomaly}}\big),

(13)

where
y_{t}
contains a detection of anomaly status and an explanation referencing the rules.

3.4
Exponential Majority Smoothing

To obtain stable binary predictions and compute AUC scores, we adopt
Exponential Majority Smoothing (EMS)
as a post-processing step, following
Yang et al. 2024
. EMS combines Exponential Moving Average (EMA) with local Majority Voting to reduce temporal noise in raw frame-level binary predictions. This post-processing is used during evaluation to enhance prediction consistency.

Experiments

This section compares our model with baselines and state-of-the-art methods in terms of detecting anomalies. We conduct an extensive ablation study to justify our main design choices regarding the proposed components, SSL module hyperparameters, prompt design, and LLM and VLM setups. 4.1
Experimental Setup

Dataset.

We evaluated our model using the ComplexVAD dataset
Mumcu et al. 2025
, a recently introduced large-scale benchmark designed to address the limitations of existing VAD datasets. Unlike prior datasets that primarily focus on simple anomalies involving single-object deviations, ComplexVAD emphasizes
interaction-based anomalies
, where the anomalous behavior arises from the relationships between multiple objects or agents.

The ComplexVAD dataset comprises a total of 217 video sequences, captured at resolution of 1920×1080 pixels over various times of the day and days of the week, using a static camera overlooking a two-lane street with adjacent sidewalks and a crosswalk on the campus of the University of South Florida. The dataset is divided into:

•

Training Set:
104 videos containing only normal activities.

•

Testing Set:
113 videos, which include both normal and anomalous events.

A distinctive feature of ComplexVAD is its focus on
complex anomalies
that result from interactions between objects. Examples include:

•

A person leaving a package unattended on the sidewalk.

•

Two individuals engaging in a physical altercation.

•

A skateboard moving without a rider.

These scenarios are challenging because the individual objects (e.g., person, package, skateboard) are common and not inherently anomalous. It is the context and interaction between these objects that constitute the anomaly. This necessitates models to understand not just object presence but also their relationships and interactions over time.

For the full procedure, given that the dataset contains nearly 1,800,000 frames,making exhaustive processing computationally infeasible, we sampled every 10
th
frame from the test videos, resulting in approximately
180,000
being processed.

Evaluation Metrics.
Following standard practice, we primarily report the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) to evaluate detection performance. For LLM-based methods, which inherently produce binary (normal/abnormal) predictions rather than continuous anomaly scores, we convert their outputs into calibrated confidence scores using the approach described in Section
3.4
. This allows us to compute AUC fairly across all models. Additionally, we report precision, recall, F1-Score, and ROC-AUC to compare binary classification performance by the SSL module. All metrics are computed at the frame level using the provided ground truth annotations.

Implementation Details.
All experiments were conducted as part of a unified pipeline, using 10 NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs (24GB VRAM each) and a total of 150GB of system RAM. The training and inference pipelines were developed using PyTorch 2.0 with CUDA 12.1. We utilized
YOLOv12n
model for object detection. For the LLM component of our system, we deployed
LLaMA 3.3
with 70 billion parameters. Please refer to Appendix
6
for a detailed analysis of the variant. To generate captions for the frame, we used
LLaVA-NeXT-Video-7B-hf

Liu et al. 2024a

Zhang et al. 2024
model in 32 bit Float mode and
ImageBind

Girdhar et al. 2023
embedding model was leveraged in our Caption Refinement module.

4.2
Training Procedure

The training phase is dedicated solely to the SSL component of our pipeline. We adopt a structured multi-phase training scheme. Since our model involves multiple proxy tasks, we employ task-specific loss functions: the L2 loss is used for the reconstruction-based Middle Frame Prediction task, and the cross-entropy loss is applied to classification tasks such as Irregularity Detection and both Jigsaw tasks. Optimization is performed using the AdamW optimizer, with learning rate decaying by a factor of 0.1 every 10 epochs.

The training proceeds in three progressive phases:

•

Phase 0 (Encoder Only)
: The shared encoder is trained while keeping all decoders frozen, as proposed in
Brock et al. 2017
.

•

Phase 1 (Decoder Unfreezing)
: Decoder layers are gradually unfrozen, beginning from the output and moving inward layer by layer over successive epochs
Howard and Ruder 2018
.

Phase 2 (Full Model Training)
: The entire network, including the encoder and all decoders, is trained jointly. The optimizer is reinitialized and a unified learning rate is applied across the model.

This progressive strategy functions as a form of architectural curriculum learning. It helps establish strong shared representations before introducing the full task-specific learning burden, which is crucial for avoiding negative transfer and ensuring stable convergence in multi-task settings.

4.3
Inference Efficiency

After completing training, we proceed to the inference phase, where the full pipeline operates in a two-stage manner.

First, each input frame is passed through the trained SSL module, which acts as a coarse anomaly detector. Frames are flagged as potentially abnormal if their total computed loss from the SSL tasks exceeds a fixed threshold of 0.3. These flagged frames are then forwarded to the LLM-based component.
This component performs captioning, semantic alignment, rule induction, reasoning, and smoothing to determine final anomaly scores for the selected frames.

The training-free stage leverages large pretrained models for rule-based inference, yielding interpretable outputs grounded in semantic understanding. Importantly, by introducing this coarse-to-fine filtering design, we reduced the number of requests sent to the LLM by approximately 50%, while still maintaining strong anomaly detection performance. This highlights the efficiency gains of the proposed pipeline without sacrificing accuracy.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced HyCoVAD, a novel hybrid framework designed to address the challenges of complex, interaction-driven video anomaly detection. Our approach integrates the efficiency of a SSL module for generating anomaly proposals with the semantic reasoning capabilities of a LLM for validation. Specifically, HyCoVAD employs an nnFormer-based multi-task SSL temporal analyzer to efficiently identify frames exhibiting low-level spatiotemporal irregularities. These suspicious frames are then subjected to a semantic verification stage, where the LLM leverages auto-generated, context-specific rules to confirm high-level, interaction-based anomalies.
Extensive experiments on the challenging ComplexVAD dataset demonstrate the effectiveness of this detect–validate strategy. HyCoVAD achieves a state-of-the-art frame-level AUC of 72.5%, outperforming existing baselines , while also reducing the computational overhead associated with frame-by-frame LLM analysis.

Future work can explore several promising research directions. First, extending HyCoVAD to a multimodal setting by incorporating audio signals could further enrich anomaly detection, as many real-world anomalies, such as verbal disputes, carry strong auditory cues. Second, while our coarse-to-fine filtering mechanism already reduces LLM usage, additional efficiency gains may be achieved through knowledge distillation, transferring reasoning capabilities from the large LLM into a smaller, task-specific model for anomaly verification. Moreover, developing a more dynamic and adaptive rule generation process that evolves throughout a video sequence could further strengthen semantic reasoning. Finally, evaluating HyCoVAD’s generalizability across broader datasets with diverse and complex anomalies would provide a stronger foundation for validating the potential of hybrid SSL-LLM architectures in the video anomaly detection domain.
