Sirt5 preserves mitochondrial homeostasis to improve cardiac function in ischemia-reperfusion injury by inhibiting ANT2 lysine lactylation modification
Authors: Simeng Li, Siman Shen, Yongfang Hong, Kun Ding, Suyun Chen, Jianning Chen, Changsen Wang, Yaofeng Wen, Guixi Mo, Lili Yu, De-Li Shi, Liangqing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocardial ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury, a prevalent heart disease, disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis, affecting heart energy metabolism. The role of mitochondrial protein Sirt5 in protecting the heart from IR injury is explored in mice. Reduced Sirt5 levels in IR-injured hearts correlate with increased protein lactylation and impaired mitochondrial function. Sirt5 overexpression mitigates, while its knockout worsens, mitochondrial and cardiac damage. Sirt5 interacts with ANT2, reducing its lysine lactylation to enhance its association with VDAC1. This interaction improves cardiac function post-injury. Introduction

Myocardial ischemia is a common cause of cardiac dysfunction resulting from a significant decrease in coronary blood flow [1–3]. Promptly restoring coronary blood flow is essential to save viable heart muscle cells near the ischemic area; however, this process can also lead to a secondary phase of heart damage called ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury [4]. This injury can cause an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative damage in the heart, which results in dysfunction and death of myocardial cells and ultimately impairs overall heart function. Since the heart requires a large amount of energy, any mitochondrial dysfunction can greatly disrupt oxidative metabolism, which is responsible for over 95% of the heart’s energy production [5, 6]. During myocardial I/R injury, energy metabolism is disturbed, oxidative stress increases, mitochondrial dynamics are altered, and mitochondrial autophagy is activated [7]. Consequently, focusing on mitochondrial function has become a promising therapeutic approach for treating heart failure [8]. Sirtuin proteins are part of a highly conserved family of NAD+-dependent histone deacetylases that play a role in regulating various important signaling pathways. In mammals, there are seven sirtuin paralogs, named Sirt1 through Sirt7. Sirt1 is mainly located in the nucleus but can move to the cytoplasm under certain conditions [9, 10]. Sirt2 is mostly found in the cytoplasm but accumulates in the nucleus during the transition from the G2 to M phase of the cell cycle [11]. Sirt3, Sirt4, and Sirt5 contain mitochondrial targeting sequences and are primarily localized within the mitochondria. Both Sirt6 and Sirt7 are nuclear proteins [12–14], with Sirt6 mainly associated with chromatin [15] and Sirt7 predominantly found in the nucleolus [16]. Sirtuin proteins have gained significant attention due to their crucial role in regulating histone deacetylation [17]. Additionally, several sirtuins exhibit non-histone deacetylase and mono-ADP-ribosyltransferase activities, influencing processes such as cell proliferation, senescence, DNA damage response, oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular metabolism [18, 19]. Growing evidence suggests that sirtuins are deeply involved in various post-translational modifications (PTMs) essential for heart function and are promising targets for cardiovascular disease therapies [18, 20, 21]. Sirt5, in particular, has a unique enzymatic function that removes negatively charged lysine modifications [22–24], although it acts as a very weak deacetylase [25]. For instance, Sirt5 prevents lysine succinylation to support cardiac metabolism in cardiomyopathy and enhance heart function [26, 27]. In mouse models of diabetic cardiomyopathy, loss of Sirt5 worsens heart damage by increasing lysine malonylation and reducing the stability of the glutathione S-transferase P (GSTP1) protein [28]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms by which Sirt5 regulates other PTMs related to mitochondrial balance and heart protection remain unclear.

Lactate, a significant carbon-containing metabolic product from the cellular glycolysis pathway, serves as both a crucial energy source and a signaling molecule. Lactylation, a newly identified type of protein post-translational modification (PTM), plays a role in regulating gene expression and function through metabolism [29]. However, the impact of lactate signaling and protein lactylation in cardiovascular diseases is not yet well understood. Increased intracellular lactate levels can stimulate heart regeneration in zebrafish by promoting a metabolic shift toward glycolysis [30] and can improve heart failure in mice by maintaining sarcomere structure via lactylation of α-myosin heavy chain [31]. Histone lactylation can activate gene expression involved in cardiac repair after a myocardial infarction [32]. Conversely, elevated lactate following myocardial infarction has been shown to induce endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition by lactylating Snail1, which contributes to cardiac fibrosis and worsens heart function [33]. Additionally, lactic acid has been found to encourage pulmonary fibrosis by triggering myofibroblast differentiation through pH-dependent activation of TGFβ signaling [34], and high blood lactate levels are associated with increased mortality in heart failure patients [35]. Thus, lactate-driven protein lactylation may have varying effects on heart function depending on the context, but further research is needed to understand how these modifications influence mitochondrial function after cardiac injury.

Results

Sirt5 protects mitochondrial and cardiac functions in IR injury

Although several Sirtuin proteins are involved in heart failure, ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury, and cardiac hypertrophy, the roles of the mitochondrial Sirt4 and Sirt5 in these conditions are still not well understood [21]. To investigate this, we first conducted western blot analyses to assess changes in Sirt protein levels in mouse ventricular heart tissues subjected to 45 min of ischemia followed by 6 h of reperfusion (IR injury). We found that levels of Sirt1-3 proteins increased, while Sirt4-7 levels decreased after IR injury (Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis of Sirt1-7 proteins showed that Sirt5 experienced the most significant reduction in the IR-damaged myocardium (Fig. 1B). Consequently, we focused our study on Sirt5. Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed reduced Sirt5 protein expression in the left ventricle of IR-injured mouse hearts (Fig. 1C). Notably, heart tissue from patients undergoing IR due to heart valve replacement also exhibited lower SIRT5 protein levels compared to healthy heart tissue (Fig. 1D). These findings suggest that cardiac injury suppresses Sirt5 protein expression, which could have clinical relevance. Additionally, in HL-1 cardiomyocytes exposed to 12 h of hypoxia followed by 4 h of reoxygenation (HR injury), Sirt5 protein levels were also decreased (Fig. 1E). We then confirmed that Sirt5 is predominantly located in the mitochondria of HL-1 cells (Fig. 1F) and verified that its expression in this organelle is reduced following HR injury (Fig. 1G).

We then explored how altering Sirt5 expression affects mitochondrial balance and heart function. The effectiveness of shRNA-induced knockdown in HL-1 cells, cardiac-specific conditional knockout, and adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9)-mediated cardiac overexpression of Sirt5 were confirmed through western blot analysis (Fig. S1). In HL-1 cells, MitoTracker staining revealed that increasing Sirt5 levels prevented mitochondrial fragmentation caused by hypoxia-reoxygenation (HR) treatment, while reducing Sirt5 worsened this fragmentation (Fig. 2A). Similarly, mitoSOX staining showed that Sirt5 overexpression lowered mitochondrial superoxide levels induced by HR injury, whereas Sirt5 knockdown further elevated them (Fig. 2B). Additionally, assessments using ATP assays, JC-1 dye for mitochondrial J-aggregate formation, Seahorse technology to measure mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) openness demonstrated that Sirt5 overexpression restored ATP production, enhanced mitochondrial membrane potential, improved mitochondrial respiration, and inhibited the opening of the mptpin HR-damaged HL-1 cells; conversely, Sirt5 knockdown produced opposite effects (Fig. 2, C to E, Fig. S2). To investigate Sirt5’s role in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury in vivo, C57BL/6 mice with AAV9-mediated cardiac Sirt5 overexpression and cardiac-specific conditional Sirt5 knockout underwent IR as previously described. Echocardiography, triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining, and hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining showed that Sirt5 overexpression significantly enhanced heart function by increasing ejection fraction and fractional shortening, reducing infarct size, and decreasing inflammatory cell infiltration, whereas Sirt5 knockout worsened cardiac dysfunction and damage caused by IR injury (Fig. 2, F to H). Transmission electron microscope (TEM) analysis revealed that Sirt5 overexpression preserved mitochondrial integrity disrupted by IR in heart tissue, while conditional Sirt5 knockout aggravated mitochondrial abnormalities (Fig. S3). Collectively, these findings indicate that Sirt5 supports mitochondrial function and provides cardioprotection following IR injury.

IR injury increases protein lactylation modifications to disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis

Since sirtuin proteins play key roles in regulating post-translational modifications (PTMs), we initially examined protein lactylation, crotonylation, succinylation, hydroxybutyrylation, and acetylation in mouse hearts subjected to ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury. Our findings revealed that lactylation showed the most significant increase (Fig. S4, A to E). Correspondingly, elevated lactate levels were detected in IR-injured hearts and in HL-1 cells treated with hypoxia-reoxygenation (HR) (Fig. S5, A and B). To confirm the specificity of protein lactylation induced by IR and HR, HL-1 cells were treated with dichloroacetate (DCA), which reduces lactate production, and sodium lactate (NaLa), which promotes protein lactylation [29, 36]. Previous studies have identified lysine lactylation as a prominent and novel PTM driven by lactate [29]. Consistent with this, western blot, immunofluorescence, and immunohistochemistry analyses using a pan-lysine lactylation antibody (pan-Kla) demonstrated that DCA suppressed, while NaLa enhanced, IR- and HR-induced protein pan-Kla modifications (Fig. 3, A to D). In myocardial tissues subjected to IR injury, lactate levels decreased with DCA treatment and increased with NaLa treatment (Fig. 3E). Notably, overexpressing Sirt5 reduced protein pan-Kla levels in HR-treated HL-1 cells and IR-injured heart tissues, whereas knocking down or knocking out Sirt5 had the opposite effect (Fig. 3, F to I). These results suggest that reduced Sirt5 expression in injured heart tissue leads to elevated protein lysine lactylation, which may impair mitochondrial function and harm cardiac performance.

Next, we investigated how changes in protein lactylation affect mitochondrial homeostasis. As anticipated, lowering lactate levels in HR-treated HL-1 cells with DCA lessened mitochondrial fragmentation, decreased mitochondrial superoxide dismutase levels, improved ATP production, and increased mitochondrial membrane potential. In contrast, enhancing protein lactylation through NaLa treatment worsened mitochondrial homeostasis and function (Fig. S6, A to D). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of heart sections from IR-injured mice showed that intraperitoneal DCA injections improved mitochondrial structural organization, whereas NaLa further damaged mitochondrial integrity (Fig. S6E). Additionally, DCA significantly reduced infarct size and inflammatory cell infiltration, as demonstrated by TTC and histological staining (Fig. S6, F and G), and improved cardiac ejection fraction and fractional shortening, as measured by ultrasound (Fig. S6H).

Identification of Sirt5-regulated protein lactylation modifications

We employed 4D label-free proteomics to identify proteins with altered lactylation in mouse ventricular heart tissues after ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury, as well as proteins interacting with Sirt5 in HL-1 cells (Fig. 4, A and B). Heat map analysis revealed significant differences in protein expression between sham and IR groups (fold change > 1.2 or < 0.67, P < 0.05), with 481 proteins downregulated and 182 upregulated (Fig. 4C). Volcano plot analysis identified 191 proteins with differential lactylation between sham and IR-injured groups (fold change > 1.2 or < 0.83, P < 0.05); among these, 30 proteins with 37 lactylation sites were upregulated, while 161 proteins with 429 lactylation sites were downregulated (Fig. 4D). Gene ontology (GO) analysis showed that these differentially lactylated proteins were enriched in biological processes such as precursor metabolite generation and energy production (Fig. 4E). Functional classification using Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) indicated that most of these proteins are involved in energy production and conversion (Fig. 4F). Notably, 40% of the upregulated and 52% of the downregulated lactylated proteins in IR-injured hearts were predicted to localize to mitochondria (Fig. 4G).

Mass spectrometry revealed 92 proteins co-precipitating with Sirt5. Among these potential Sirt5-interacting proteins, only ANT2, an inner mitochondrial protein, showed increased lactylation in IR-injured cardiac tissue (Fig. 5A), linking reduced Sirt5 expression to elevated ANT2 lactylation after IR injury. Collision-induced dissociation (CID) analysis identified lysine 163 (K163) within a conserved GLGDCLV(Kla)IYK motif on ANT2 as a primary lactylation site (Fig. 5, B and C). Using HDOCK software and protein structure data from the RCSB database, we predicted interactions between ANT2 and Sirt1-7. While all Sirt proteins could bind ANT2 (Fig. 5D, Fig. S7), Sirt5 exhibited the strongest binding affinity, indicated by the most negative docking score (Fig. 5, D and E). Further analysis showed that ANT2 forms hydrogen bonds and salt bridges with Sirt proteins, especially Sirt5, suggesting a stable interaction. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments confirmed the physical association between endogenous Sirt5 and ANT2 in HL-1 cells and heart tissue (Fig. 5, F and G), as well as between HA-tagged Sirt5 and FLAG-tagged ANT2 expressed in HEK293T cells (Fig. 5, H and I). Therefore, ANT2 is identified as a novel Sirt5-interacting partner, consistent with their co-localization within the mitochondrial matrix.

Sirt5 inhibits lysine lactylation modifications of ANT2

The increase in ANT2 lysine lactylation caused by injury was not due to a rise in the overall ANT2 protein levels (Fig. 6, A and B). Moreover, no significant changes were observed in ANT2 lysine crotonylation (Kcr), succinylation (Ksuc), acetylation (Kac), or ß-hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb) following hypoxia-reoxygenation (HR) in HL-1 cells or ischemia-reperfusion (IR) in heart tissue (Fig. S8), indicating that ANT2 is particularly sensitive to lysine lactylation. Indeed, treatment with sodium lactate (NaLa) increased ANT2 lysine lactylation both in vitro and in vivo, while dichloroacetate (DCA) had the opposite effect (Fig. 6, C to F). As anticipated, overexpressing Sirt5 in HL-1 cells or heart tissue reduced ANT2 lysine lactylation (Fig. 6, G and H). We found that the Sirt5 activator MC3138 suppressed, whereas the Sirt5 inhibitor nicotinamide enhanced, ANT2 lysine lactylation when administered to the heart or applied to HL-1 cells (Fig. S9). These findings strongly indicate that Sirt5 inhibits ANT2 lysine lactylation.

To further investigate the role of lysine 163 (K163) in ANT2 lactylation, this residue was replaced with arginine (ANT2-K163R) (Fig. S10A). Expression of this mutant in HEK293T cells showed reduced sensitivity to lysine lactylation compared to wild-type ANT2 (Fig. S11A). Similar results were observed in mice following AAV9-mediated expression of ANT2 and ANT2-K163R via tail vein injection (Fig. S10B and Fig. S11B). In HL-1 cells subjected to HR and myocardium exposed to IR, ANT2-K163R exhibited markedly lower lysine lactylation than the wild-type protein under both normal and injury conditions (Fig. 6, I and J). Thus, K163 is a key site responsible for ANT2 lactylation.

To determine if K163 is targeted by Sirt5-regulated lactylation, FLAG-tagged ANT2 or ANT2-K163R were overexpressed with or without Sirt5 in HEK293T cells, followed by immunoprecipitation using FLAG antibodies. Western blot analysis revealed that Sirt5 suppressed lysine lactylation of both ANT2 and ANT2-K163R (Fig. 6K). Conversely, knocking down Sirt5 via shRNA in HEK293T cells increased lysine lactylation of exogenous FLAG-tagged ANT2 but had minimal effect on ANT2-K163R (Fig. 6L). Comparable results were obtained in the myocardium after AAV9-mediated Sirt5 overexpression and conditional knockout (Fig. 6, M and N). Notably, Sirt5 overexpression further reduced lactylation of ANT2-K163R, suggesting that other lysine residues in ANT2 may also be lactylated and are likely targets of Sirt5 due to its potent activity in removing negatively charged lysine modifications.

ANT2-K163R improves mitochondrial and cardiac functions

Since Sirt5 controls the lysine lactylation of ANT2, we further examined how it functionally interacts with ANT2 and the ANT2-K163R mutant in heart muscle cells. In HL-1 cells transfected with shRNA for either control or Sirt5 knockdown under hypoxia-reoxygenation conditions, ANT2-K163R was more effective than ANT2 at enhancing mitochondrial health by decreasing mitochondrial fragmentation and superoxide dismutase levels, while boosting mitochondrial ATP production, membrane potential, and oxygen consumption rate (Fig. 7, A to D). Likewise, in both control (Sirt5fl/fl) and Sirt5 conditional knockout mice subjected to ischemia-reperfusion injury, ANT2-K163R improved heart function more efficiently than ANT2. Specifically, echocardiography, TTC staining, and tissue analysis showed that AAV9-driven overexpression of ANT2-K163R in the heart significantly increased ejection fraction and fractional shortening (Fig. 7E), reduced infarct size relative to the area at risk without altering the area at risk itself (Fig. 7F), and lowered inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 7G). These positive effects of ANT2-K163R were linked to better mitochondrial integrity (Fig. S12). Therefore, we propose that ANT2-K163R may compete with lactylated ANT2 to safeguard mitochondrial function in cardiomyocytes. Consequently, it can counterbalance the ischemia-reperfusion injury-induced rise in ANT2 lysine lactylation, thereby preserving mitochondrial homeostasis and enhancing cardiac function similarly to Sirt5 overexpression.

Sirt5 regulates the interaction of ANT2 with VDAC1 in mitochondrial homeostasis

Previous studies have shown that ANT2 and VDAC1 are located on the inner and outer mitochondrial membranes, respectively, and work together to efficiently transfer energy metabolites across mitochondria [22]. In HL-1 cells stably expressing either ANT2 or the ANT2-K163R mutant, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments using an ANT2 antibody that detects both forms revealed that ANT2-K163R interacts more strongly with endogenous VDAC1 than wild-type ANT2 does (Fig. 8A). Notably, hypoxia-reoxygenation (HR) treatment significantly reduced the interaction between ANT2 and VDAC1, whereas the interaction between ANT2-K163R and VDAC1 was less affected (Fig. 8A). Similarly, in heart tissue, there was a strong interaction between ANT2-K163R and VDAC1, which was moderately diminished by ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury (Fig. 8B). These findings not only confirm a physical interaction between ANT2 and VDAC1 but also highlight the crucial role of ANT2 lysine lactylation in regulating the formation of the ANT2-VDAC1 complex.

Next, we examined the role of Sirt5 in modulating the interaction between endogenous ANT2 and VDAC1. In HL-1 cells and heart tissues, treatment with the Sirt5 activator MC3138 enhanced the interaction between ANT2 and VDAC1, although this effect was diminished by HR or IR injury (Fig. S13, A and B). Conversely, the Sirt5 inhibitor nicotinamide worked together with HR or IR injury to further inhibit the ANT2-VDAC1 interaction (Fig. S13, C and D). Consistent with the effects of the Sirt5 activator, overexpressing Sirt5 in HL-1 cells or heart tissues increased the ANT2-VDAC1 interaction, but this enhancement was counteracted by HR or IR injury (Fig. 8, C and D). Finally, combining IR injury with conditional knockout of Sirt5 resulted in a complete loss of Sirt5 protein and strongly suppressed the interaction between ANT2 or ANT2-K163R and VDAC1 (Fig. 8E). Since ANT2 and VDAC1 are well-known key regulators of mitochondrial function [38–40], these results demonstrate that Sirt5 is essential for controlling the interaction between these mitochondrial proteins by modulating ANT2 lysine lactylation (Fig. 8F). Additionally, there may be a Sirt5-mediated regulation of the ANT2-VDAC1 interaction that is independent of lactylation. It is possible that Sirt5 binding to ANT2 promotes the formation of the ANT2-VDAC1 complex, and this interaction could be weakened when Sirt5 is absent.

Discussion

In this research, we showed that Sirt5 plays a protective role in the heart during myocardial ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury by preserving mitochondrial balance. We observed that myocardial IR injury caused a decrease in Sirt5 expression and mitochondrial dysfunction, which correlated with increased lysine lactylation of ANT2. The reduction in Sirt5 protein expression after myocardial IR is primarily initiated at the mRNA level. In a cellular model that mimics myocardial IR injury, cardiomyocytes subjected to H/R exhibit significant decreases in both SIRT5 mRNA and protein levels, indicating that the down-regulation occurs at the transcriptional level [41]. Clinical sample studies further show that in heart-failure patients with markedly reduced ejection fraction, both SIRT5 protein and mRNA are decreased, supporting the conclusion that this down-regulation results from transcriptional control [42]. At the mechanistic level, Previous research has shown that the decrease in SIRT5 expression after I/R injury is caused by the downregulation of its upstream transcriptional activator, HOXB5. Specifically, I/R injury reduces HOXB5 levels, which in turn leads to decreased transcriptional activation of the Sirt5 gene [43]. Overexpressing Sirt5 improved mitochondrial balance and heart function. At the cellular level, Sirt5 overexpression effectively preserved mitochondrial network integrity under hypoxia-reoxygenation conditions, as evidenced by reduced mitochondrial fragmentation and decreased superoxide accumulation. These morphological and oxidative stress parameters were functionally corroborated by metabolic assays showing that Sirt5 restoration enhanced ATP production, stabilized mitochondrial membrane potential, attenuated mPTP opening, and improved bioenergetic capacity-including basal respiration, ATP-linked respiration, and maximal respiratory capacity. Conversely, genetic ablation of Sirt5 exacerbated all these pathological endpoints, confirming that endogenous Sirt5 is indispensable for mitochondrial resilience against HR stress. Notably, among these mitochondrial parameters, the mitochondrial permeability transition pore emerged as a particularly sensitive target of Sirt5-mediated protection. Calcein AM-based flow cytometry revealed that Sirt5 overexpression significantly attenuated mPTP opening under HR conditions, whereas Sirt5 knockdown markedly exacerbated pore formation and calcein release. The mPTP represents a non-specific channel spanning the inner mitochondrial membrane, and its pathological opening represents a pivotal commitment point in reperfusion-induced cell death, triggering matrix swelling, cristae disruption, outer membrane rupture, and the release of pro-apoptotic factors such as cytochrome c [44]. The ability of Sirt5 to suppress mPTP opening likely contributes mechanistically to the observed preservation of mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP synthesis, as sustained pore closure is prerequisite for maintaining the proton gradient and oxidative phosphorylation. The translational relevance of these in vitro findings was validated in vivo using both gain and loss of function mouse models. Echocardiographic analysis revealed that cardiac-specific Sirt5 overexpression significantly preserved left ventricular contractile function post-IR, as reflected by increased ejection fraction and fractional shortening, whereas conditional Sirt5 knockout markedly aggravated cardiac dysfunction. These functional improvements correlated with reduced myocardial infarct size and attenuated inflammatory cell infiltration in Sirt5-overexpressing hearts, and the opposite in Sirt5-deficient hearts. Mechanistically, Sirt5 interacts with ANT2 and prevents its lactylation at lysine 163, promoting the interaction between ANT2 and VDAC1, which is essential for proper mitochondrial metabolism and function. These results reveal a regulatory network among key mitochondrial proteins and offer new understanding of the molecular mechanisms that maintain mitochondrial balance during myocardial IR injury.

Sirtuin proteins play crucial roles in protein post-translational modifications (PTMs) that are vital for heart function [18, 20, 21]. Sirt5 is a mitochondrial protein with relatively weak deacetylase activity [25]. Growing evidence indicates that Sirt5 acts as a major regulator of cellular homeostasis and is important in various aspects of myocardial energy metabolism [45]. We found that Sirt5 protects mitochondrial balance and cardiac function after myocardial IR injury. Our findings align with previous studies showing that loss of Sirt5 leads to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with impaired heart function and worsens IR injury by enlarging the infarct size [26, 46]. Indeed, Sirt5 has been demonstrated to preserve cardiac function and improve survival in mice under pressure overload [47, 48]. It is also involved in multiple metabolic pathways related to mitochondrial and cardiac functions, including glycolysis, fatty acid oxidation, and reactive oxygen species detoxification [45]. Sirt5 primarily removes negatively charged lysine modifications [22–24]. Numerous studies suggest that Sirt5 regulates protein lysine succinylation, significantly impacting heart metabolism, disease, and function [26, 27, 46, 48, 49]. However, it remains unclear whether and how Sirt5 is involved in other types of PTMs that are important for maintaining mitochondrial balance and cardiac function.

We have presented strong evidence revealing a new function of Sirt5 in regulating protein lysine lactylation, which helps maintain mitochondrial balance and protects the heart from injury caused by ischemia-reperfusion (IR). Firstly, the absence of Sirt5 in heart muscle results in elevated protein lactylation, which is associated with impaired mitochondrial and cardiac performance. Secondly, and importantly, Sirt5 directly interacts with ANT2 and suppresses its lactylation, primarily occurring at the K163 residue. Thirdly, increasing Sirt5 levels or blocking ANT2 lysine lactylation produces positive effects by enhancing mitochondrial stability and heart function. Lactylation has been recognized as a new type of protein post-translational modification (PTM) and a common histone marker within cells, playing a crucial role in regulating metabolism-related gene expression and cellular activities [29]. In the hearts of newborn mice, widespread changes in protein lactylation correlate with shifts in cardiac metabolism and regeneration [48]. Histone lactylation can stimulate the expression of genes involved in glycolysis and activate repair-related transcriptional responses, promoting heart repair and reducing IR injury [32, 50]. However, elevated lactate levels following a heart attack can also trigger cardiac fibrosis and worsen heart dysfunction by increasing lactylation of proteins linked to the endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition [33]. Therefore, the effects of protein lactylation on heart metabolism may vary depending on which proteins are lactylated. Among the 92 Sirt5-interacting proteins identified, we selected ANT2 for detailed characterization because it was the only mitochondrial protein exhibiting significantly increased lactylation in IR-injured hearts, and computational analysis revealed the strongest binding affinity between Sirt5 and ANT2 compared to other Sirt family members. Additionally, ANT2’s established functional partnership with VDAC1, a critical regulator of mitochondrial metabolism, further supported its prioritization. However, we recognize that other Sirt5 interactors may also contribute to mitochondrial protection. Sirt5 regulates diverse metabolic pathways including fatty acid oxidation and ROS detoxification through multiple protein interactions. While our study establishes the Sirt5-ANT2-VDAC1 axis as a key mechanism, we do not exclude the possibility that additional interactors among the remaining 91 candidates may play complementary roles in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and protecting against IR injury. Systematic investigation of these proteins in future studies will help to fully elucidate Sirt5-mediated cardioprotection.

ANT2 is a protein located in the inner membrane of mitochondria, encoded by the SLC25A5 gene on the human X chromosome. It plays a crucial role in maintaining the mitochondrial membrane potential and offers protection against apoptosis [39]. However, its role in maintaining heart balance is not well understood. Our study showed that ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury did not alter ANT2 protein levels but specifically increased its lysine lactylation modification, which was linked to decreased expression of Sirt5. Notably, we found that Sirt5 physically interacts with ANT2 and identified a conserved lysine residue (K163) on ANT2 as a site regulated by Sirt5-mediated lactylation. Therefore, reducing ANT2 lysine lactylation-either by enhancing Sirt5 activity or by mutating the K163 residue (ANT2-K163R)-can restore mitochondrial balance and improve heart function following IR injury. These results indicate that ANT2 lysine lactylation, resulting from loss of Sirt5 function, leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and impacts cardiac energy metabolism. Supporting this, previous studies have shown that ANT2 deficiency in mice causes mitochondrial disorganization and heart failure, likely due to disruption of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore [51]. Thus, proper ANT2 function is essential for mitochondrial integrity and protecting the heart. Overall, these findings provide functional evidence that Sirt5 regulates cardiometabolic processes by controlling ANT2 lysine lactylation. They suggest that targeting ANT2 lactylation could offer cardioprotective benefits in disease and may serve as a therapeutic approach to improve heart function after IR injury.

We found that ANT2 physically binds to VDAC1, the most abundant protein in the outer mitochondrial membrane that controls mitochondrial function in both healthy and diseased states [38, 40]. Humans and mice have three VDAC isoforms (VDAC1, VDAC2, and VDAC3), which work together with ANT proteins to regulate the exchange of metabolites, ions, and nucleotides between the cytosol and the mitochondrial matrix, thereby influencing mitochondrial function [52]. Notably, when ANT2 undergoes lysine lactylation, it can no longer interact with VDAC1, whereas a lactylation-resistant form of ANT2 (ANT2-K163R) shows stronger binding to VDAC1. This study reveals a new mechanism that controls the formation of the VDAC1-ANT2 complex, which is important for mitochondrial energy metabolism. Although the exact role of VDAC1 in ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury is not fully understood, recent studies suggest it may help protect the heart. For example, deleting VDAC1 in H9c2 heart cells leads to apoptosis caused by oxidative stress and increased glycolytic stress [53]. In ischemic hearts, reducing VDAC1 levels disrupts the metabolic shift by affecting the beta-oxidation of long-chain fatty acids [54]. It is likely that increased lysine lactylation of ANT2 disrupts its complex with VDAC1, causing VDAC1 to close and thereby blocking the movement of mitochondrial metabolites in and out [55]. Therefore, preventing ANT2 lactylation can enhance its interaction with VDAC1, improving mitochondrial function and helping maintain cellular balance in hearts damaged by IR injury. However, we cannot rule out that the binding of ANT2 to Sirt5 might also promote its interaction with VDAC1 independently of lactylation inhibition, since completely removing Sirt5 also strongly reduces the complex formation between ANT2-K163R and VDAC1.

This study offers a theoretical foundation for developing new therapeutic approaches to treat heart failure. Targeting Sirt5-regulated lactylation of ANT2 could be a potential strategy for managing IR-induced cardiac damage. Additionally, these findings may aid in identifying biomarkers for diagnosing cardiac dysfunction. However, while we have uncovered a novel mechanism by which Sirt5 maintains mitochondrial homeostasis to enhance cardiac function, further research is also needed. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while our research demonstrate a marked increase in global protein lactylation following I/R injury and identify ANT2 K163 lactylation as a key target of SIRT5, we cannot exclude the possibility that other SIRT5-regulated acyl modifications, particularly succinylation, malonylation, and glutarylation, may also contribute to the observed cardioprotective effects. Given that SIRT5 is a well-established deacylase with high efficiency toward multiple negatively charged lysine modifications, the phenotypic changes attributed to SIRT5 manipulation in our models might partially arise from the cumulative regulation of several acylation types rather than lactylation alone. Second, although we provide compelling evidence that ANT2 lactylation reduces its physical interaction with VDAC1, the precise molecular mechanism by which lactylation at the K163 residue disrupts this protein-protein association remains undefined. Whether this occurs through electrostatic repulsion, conformational changes in ANT2, or altered oligomerization status requires further structural and biophysical investigation. Third, while we validated key findings in HL-1 cardiomyocytes, mouse heart tissues, and limited human surgical samples, we did not perform functional validation in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes. Therefore, the translational relevance of the SIRT5-ANT2-VDAC1 axis to human cardiac pathophysiology awaits confirmation in more physiologically relevant human cardiac models. Addressing these limitations in future studies will be essential to fully establish lactylation-specific SIRT5 signaling as a therapeutic target for myocardial I/R injury.

Materials and methods

Aim 1: Acquisition of human myocardial tissue samples

Collection of clinical cardiac tissue samples

Excess right atrial tissue was obtained from three patients undergoing tricuspid valve replacement surgery at the Affiliated Hospital of Guangdong Medical University, without any clinical complications. A control biopsy, weighing about 15 mg wet, was collected after the initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass but prior to inducing cardiac arrest. An ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury biopsy of similar weight was taken near the control site 10 min following the release of the aortic cross-clamp. All tissue samples were promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for later analysis.

Aim 2: Establishment of HR model and IR model

HR injury in HL-1 cells

HL-1 cells are a high-performance, continuously dividing cardiomyocyte line derived from mouse atrial myocytes [56]. HL-1 cells were cultured in complete medium (Gibco) with 10% of fetal calf serum (Gibco) until 85% to 90% confluence. The complete medium was replaced by DMEM containing 4.5 g/L of D-glucose (Gibco), and cells were incubated under 1% O2, 5% CO2 and 94% N2 atmosphere for 12 h (hypoxia). Reoxygenation was performed by culturing cells in complete medium under 5% CO2, 95% air atmosphere for 4 h. Immediately after reoxygenation period, the plate was withdrawn from the normoxic incubator, medium aspirated, and cells rinsed twice with ice-cold PBS. Monolayers were detached with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (37 °C, 2–3 min), neutralised with complete medium, pooled into 15-ml tubes, and centrifuged (300 × g, 5 min, 4 °C). The pellet was washed once with ice-cold PBS, re-centrifuged, and either lysed directly in RIPA buffer or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent analyses.

Myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury in mice

C57BL/6 mice (male, 8–10 weeks old, body weight 22–25 g) were obtained from SPF Biotechnology (Beijing, China) and housed under specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2 °C and 50–60% humidity. Mice were provided with standard chow (AIN-93G, 4% fat) and water ad libitum and acclimatized for at least 7 days prior to experimentation. Mice were randomly assigned to either the sham-operated or ischemia-reperfusion (IR) group. For each experimental condition, a total of 8 to 10 mice per group were used. All surgical procedures were performed under aseptic conditions. Anesthesia was induced with 5% isoflurane in 100% oxygen for 2 min, followed by endotracheal intubation using a 24G intravenous catheter. Mechanical ventilation was maintained using a rodent ventilator (tidal volume: 200–250 µL, respiratory rate: 110–120 breaths/min). Anesthesia was maintained with 2% isoflurane throughout the procedure. A left thoracotomy was performed via the fourth intercostal space, and the pericardium was gently opened to expose the heart. The left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery was identified and ligated using an 8 − 0 polypropylene suture approximately 2–3 mm distal to its origin. The ligature was tied as a slipknot, with the free ends exteriorized through the chest wall to allow for later reperfusion. Ischemia was confirmed by visually observing pallor of the left ventricular wall and ST-segment elevation on the electrocardiogram. After 45 min of ischemia, the ligature was gently loosened to initiate reperfusion. The chest was closed in layers using 6 − 0 silk sutures, and the mice were allowed to recover on a heating pad (37 °C) until fully awake. Sham-operated mice underwent the same surgical procedure without LAD ligation.

Aim 3: Genetic interventions

Plasmid and shRNA transfection

HEK293T cells (from the Chinese Academy of Sciences cell bank) and HL-1 cells (Millipore) were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. These cells were transfected with expression plasmids or gene-specific shRNAs (listed in Table S1) using Lipofectamine 3000 or Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen), respectively. A non-targeting shRNA (sh-Ctrl) was used as a control for specificity. Cells were analyzed 48 h after transfection.

AAV9-mediated gene overexpression in the myocardium

C57BL/6 mice (male, 8–10 weeks old, body weight 22–25 g) were obtained from SPF Biotechnology (Beijing, China) and housed under SPF conditions with a 12-hour light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2 °C and 50–60% humidity. Mice were provided with standard chow and water ad libitum and acclimatized for at least 7 days prior to experimentation.

AAV9-packaged ANT2 and ANT2-K163R at a concentration of 2 × 1011 viral genomes (vg)/mL were suspended in 100 µL of PBS. Purified viruses were injected into the tail vein using a 29G insulin injection needle, and mice were allowed to recover their body temperature in a warm environment. Successful targeting of myocardial region was verified using AAV9-packaged GFP. For each experimental condition, a total of 10 mice per group were used. Cardiac tissues were subjected to IR injury 14 days after virus injection. Histological examination and TTC staining were performed 24 h after IR injury, and ultrasound quantitative imaging was acquired 48 h after IR injury.

Lentivirus packaging and generation of stable HL-1 cell lines

HEK293T cells were transfected with 3 µg of plasmid carrying the gene of interest, 3 µg of pMD2.D lentivirus envelop plasmid, and 6 µg of PsPax lentiviral packaging plasmid, using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). They were first cultured for 6 h in OptiMEM I reduced serum medium (Gibco, USA). After replacing with complete medium (Gibco, USA), the virus-containing supernatant was collected at 48 h and 72 h post-transfection, filtered, and concentrated using Lenti-X concentrator (TaKaRa, Japan). HL-1 cells were cultured in complete medium for 24 h, then in fresh medium with 25 µL of concentrated lentivirus and 10 ng/mL of polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 48 h. Stable cell lines were established by selection with 4 mg/mL of puromycin (InvivoGen, France) for 2 weeks and maintained in 1 mg/mL of puromycin.

Conditional knockout mice

KO mice were housed under SPF conditions in individually ventilated cages (IVC) at 22 ± 2 °C, 50–60% humidity, 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, with ad libitum access to autoclaved water and standard irradiated chow, and were allowed a minimum 7-day acclimatisation before any experimental manipulation. Mice carrying loxP sites flanking exon 3 of the Sirt5 gene were created by the Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China). Mice expressing Cre/Esr1 specifically in the heart, driven by the alpha-myosin heavy chain 6 (Myh6) promoter, were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory. Cardiac-specific conditional knockout (cKO) of Sirt5 was triggered by administering tamoxifen at a dose of 60 mg/kg through daily intraperitoneal injections for five consecutive days, while the SIRT5fl/fl group mice were not treated with tamoxifen. Age- and sex-matched 8–12 weeks mice were used for all experiments, with 8–10 mice per genotype per experimental condition.

Genotyping of conditional Sirt5 knockout mice

For genotype validation, myocardium was harvested from mice 5 days after the last tamoxifen injection. Animals were deeply anesthetized with 5% isoflurane, euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the whole heart was rapidly excised within 30 s. The apex approximately 1 mm³ was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Genomic DNA was isolated from the frozen apex using a standard phenol–chloroform protocol. Western blot analysis was also conducted to assess myocardial Sirt5 protein levels in both Sirt5fl/fl and Sirt5 cKO mice. We also combined tail-clip genotyping for verification. 2–3 mm tail-tip was clipped, incubated at 55 °C overnight in 100 µl alkaline lysis buffer (25 mM NaOH, 0.2 mM EDTA, pH 12.0), neutralised with 100 µl 40 mM Tris-HCl (pH 5.0), and centrifuged; 1 µl supernatant was used directly for PCR.

PCR genotyping was performed with primers flanking the loxP sites (forward: 5′-CCACGCACCCACCCATCAAGT-3′, reverse: 5′-CTGCCTGCAAGCCGTGTAGAGAAG-3′). Reactions (25 µl) contained 0.2 µM primers flanking the floxed Sirt5 exon 3 and 2× Taq Master Mix. Cycling: 95 °C 3 min; 35 × (95 °C 30 s, 60 °C 30 s, 72 °C 45 s); 72 °C 5 min. Products were resolved on 1.5% agarose gels; intact floxed allele 1508 bp, recombined allele 847 bp.

Aim 4: Assessment of mitochondrial function

ATP concentration detection

Total ATP was quantified with the ATP Detection Assay Kit (Abcam, UK). HL-1 cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in 96-well plates, treated as indicated, washed with cold PBS and lysed in 100 µL ice-cold Assay Buffer 23. Insoluble material was removed by centrifugation at 13 000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. Fifty microlitres of supernatant or ATP standards were transferred to a white microplate, mixed with 50 µL of freshly prepared reaction buffer containing Assay Buffer 23, OxiRed probe, Converter Mix B and Developer Mix N, and incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Luminescence was read at 535 nm excitation and 587 nm emission. A standard curve ranging from 0.2 to 10 nmol per well was constructed for every plate, and sample values were interpolated after subtraction of the blank.

MitoTracker staining

HL-1 cells were grown on glass coverslips and stained using either 100 or 200 nM of Mito-tracker green (Cell Signaling Technology) in an incubator at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 atmosphere for 30 min, by following the manufacture’s protocol. Images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope at a wavelength of 488 nm, and data was processed using the Zen software (Zeiss).

MitoSOX assays

Mitochondrial superoxide production was assessed with MitoSOX™ Red (Invitrogen). HL-1 cardiomyocytes grown on 12-mm glass coverslips were washed twice with warm HBSS and loaded with 5 µM MitoSOX Red for 15 min at 37 °C in the dark. After two additional HBSS washes, cells were maintained in phenol-red-free DMEM and immediately imaged with a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope (561 nm excitation, 580–610 nm emission). For quantitative analysis, five random fields per coverslip were acquired with identical laser power and gain settings. Mean fluorescence intensity per cell was calculated with ImageJ after background subtraction.

Flow cytometry

HL-1 cells were washed with PBS and collected after trypsin digestion. Subsequently, 1 mL of JC-1 staining working solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added to collected cells and thoroughly mixed. After incubation at 37 °C for 20 min, the cells were washed twice with 1 x JC-1 staining buffer. Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer and FlowJo software (Becton, Dickinson and Company).

Seahorse extracellular flux assay

This was performed using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test kit (Agilent Technologies, USA) by following the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, HL-1 cells were seeded onto a Seahorse XF 24-well culture microplate (2 × 104 cells per well) and cultured overnight. They were rinsed twice with detection solution and maintained in the XF assay medium. Solutions of 1 mM oligomycin, 2 mM p-trifluoromethoxy carbonyl cyanide phenylhydrazone, and 0.5 mM rotenone plus antimycin A were successively added to the culture at defined time intervals. Mitochondrial OCR was analyzed using the Seahorse XFe 24 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience), and the results were expressed as pmol/minute as described [57].

TEM

After deep anesthesia with 5% isoflurane, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation and the hearts were excised within 30 s. 1 mm3 of tissue was excised from the apical region of the heart. Myocardial tissues were rinsed with 10 mM PBS solution and fixed with a mixture of 2% formaldehyde and 2% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 2–4 h, followed by post-fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide (SPI-CHEM) at 4 °C for 1–2 h. Samples were dehydrated with increasing ethanol concentrations and then embedded in resin. Ultrathin sections were cut and stained with uranyl acetate, followed by staining with lead citrate. Sections were examined using a JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope.

Aim 5: Assessment of cardiac function

Evans blue-TTC staining

Mice were subjected to anesthesia 24 h post-IR injury, and the left anterior descending coronary artery was temporarily blocked at the site of the initial ligation. Subsequently, 1 mL of 1% Evans blue solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was injected into the left ventricular cavity. The heart was rapidly removed, rinsed three times to eliminate excess dye, flash-frozen, and cut into 1 mm thick slices from the apex to the baseline. The sections were then soaked in 1% TTC solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and incubated at 37 °C and imaged using a digital camera. The infarct size and myocardial area at risk were calculated using digital planimetry and analyzed from multiple consecutive sections using the NIH ImageJ software.

H&E staining

After deep anesthesia with 5% isoflurane, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation 24 h after IR surger and the hearts were excised within 30 s. The hearts were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C, dehydrated through graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin. Serial transverse sections of 5 μm thickness were obtained from the apex to the baseline using a rotary microtome (Leica, Germany). For histopathological evaluation, sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through descending ethanol concentrations, and stained with H&E following standard protocols. Briefly, sections were incubated with Harris hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, and counterstained with eosin Y for 30 s. After dehydration and clearing, slides were mounted with neutral balsam and visualized under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemistry, after deep anesthesia with 5% isoflurane, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation and the hearts were excised within 30 s. myocardial tissues were fixed in 4% formaldehyde at room temperature for 18 hours and embedded in paraffin. Sections were deparaffinized using xylene and rehydrated in decreasing concentrations of ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed by treating the sections with antigen retrieval solution (Beyotime, China) for 20 minutes in a steamer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 4% H2O2, followed by blocking with QuickBlock (Beyotime, China). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies targeting key proteins Sirt5 (monoclonal, 1:200, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight, washed several times with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20, and incubated with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime, China). Labelled proteins were visualized using 3,3’- diaminobenzidine substrate.

Echocardiography

Under isoflurane anesthesia (0.5–1.5% in O₂), mice were positioned on a temperature-regulated rail mount (37 °C ± 0.5 °C). High-frequency echocardiography was carried out with a Prospect system (S-Sharp, Taiwan) using a 40-MHz single-element probe. From the parasternal long-axis window, M-mode recordings centered on the papillary muscles provided data for fractional shortening (FS) and ejection fraction (EF).

Aim 6: Investigation of protein lactylation and underlying molecular mechanism

Mitochondrial isolation

Following the instructions for the Beyotime kit (Beyotime, China), HL-1 cells were collected from 10 cm dishes, washed twice with ice-cold PBS, and resuspended in 1 mL mitochondrial isolation reagent A containing 1 mM PMSF on ice for 15 min. The suspension was homogenized 20 strokes, centrifuged at 600 g for 10 min to remove nuclei, then the supernatant was centrifuged at 11 000 g for 10 min to pellet mitochondria. The pellet was washed once with storage buffer, centrifuged again, and resuspended in 50–100 µL storage buffer on ice or stored at −80 °C.

Measurement of lactate levels

This was performed using the Lactate Colorimetric Analysis kit (Biovision, USA), by following the manufacturer’s instructions. After deep anesthesia with 5% isoflurane, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation and the hearts were excised within 30 s. 20–30 mg myocardial tissue or 5–10 × 105 HL-1 cells were homogenized in lysis buffer and sonicated on ice at 300 W for 3 min (3 s on, 7 s off). The suspension was centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant and lactate levels were quantified using a lactate analyzer.

Western blot and Co-IP

After deep anesthesia with 5% isoflurane, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation and the hearts were excised within 30 s. We collected 300 mg from the apical region of the mouse heart; For cell samples, we prepared 1–2 × 10⁷ cells. Tissues and cells were lysed in RIPA buffer added with 1 mM of phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) as described [58]. After determining protein concentrations using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Beyotime, China), the lysates were mixed with loading buffer and boiled at 100 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies targeting key proteins Sirt1 (monoclonal, 1:1000, Abcam), Sirt2 (monoclonal, 1:1000, Abcam), Sirt3 (monoclonal, 1:1000, Abcam), Sirt4 (monoclonal, 1:1000, Abcam), Sirt5 (monoclonal, 1:1000, Abcam/CST), Sirt6 (monoclonal, 1:1000, Abcam), Sirt7 (monoclonal, 1:1000, Abcam), ß-tubulin (monoclonal, 1:1000, CST), L-lactyllysine (monoclonal, 1:1000, PTM BIO), crotonyllysine (monoclonal, 1:1000, PTM BIO), succinyllysine (monoclonal, 1:1000, PTM BIO), ß-hydroxybutyryllysine (monoclonal, 1:1000, PTM BIO), acetyllysine (monoclonal, 1:1000, PTM BIO), ANT2 (monoclonal, 1:500, Abcam), HA (monoclonal, 1:1000, Biotech), Flag (monoclonal, 1:1000, Biotech), VDAC1 (monoclonal, 1:500, Abcam), followed by peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG HRP, 1:10000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China; Goat anti-Mouse IgG HRP, 1:10000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Protein bands were visualized using Luminata Western HRP substrate (Millipore, USA) and Tanon 5200 chemiluminescence imaging system (Shanghai).

For Co-IP, approximately 500 µg of proteins in IP lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 1 µg/mL leupeptin, 1 µg/mL aprotinin, 1 mM Na3PO4, 3 mM Na4P2O4, 5 mM NaF, 1 mM PMSF, pH 7.5) were mixed with 2 µg of an appropriate antibody targeting key proteins ANT2 (monoclonal, 5 µg, Abcam), HA (monoclonal, 5 µg, Genscript Biotech), Flag (monoclonal, 5 µg, Genscript Biotech), VDAC1 (monoclonal, 5 µg, Proteintech) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, 30 µL of protein A/G agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added to the mixture, which was further incubated for 4 h. The beads were then washed several times with lysis buffer and boiled in 2 x sample buffer. The supernatant was subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis followed by western blot analyses.

In-gel digestion and mass spectrometry

The identification of Sirt5-interacting using mass spectrometry were performed as previously described [59]. HL-1 cells were homogenized in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.6, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) containing a mixture of protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and shaken on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, the cell lysate was subjected to immunoprecipitation through incubation with 2 µg of Sirt5 antibody at 4 °C overnight. Protein G-coupled magnetic beads were washed with lysis buffer and incubated with the cell lysate at 4 °C for 4 h. They were then collected by centrifugation, washed five times with the lysis buffer, and boiled with 2 x SDS sample buffer. Bound proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with coomassie brilliant blue. Individual lanes were excised and treated with trypsin at 2.5 to 10 µg/µL in 25 mM of NH4HCO3 at 37 °C for 20 h. Digested peptides were extracted thrice using 60% acetonitrile/0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. The pooled fractions were completely dried using a vacuum centrifuge.

LC-MS/MS analysis was conducted on a Q Exactive mass spectrometer interfaced with an Easy-nLC 1000 liquid chromatography system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Raw data were searched using the version 2.2 of MASCOT engine (Matrix Science) and compared with the mouse UniProt database using the version 1.4 of Proteome Discoverer (Thermo Fisher Scientific USA) for protein identification. The peptide mass tolerance and the MS/MS tolerance were set at 20 ppm and 0.1 Da, respectively, and the false discovery rate (FDR) was ≤ 0.01.

4D label-free lactylation modification proteomics

Differentially lactylated proteins following myocardial IR injury in mice were identified as previously reported [60, 61]. Samples were collected from the left ventricular apex and cells were pulverized in liquid nitrogen. The powder was solubilized in lysis buffer (50 µM PR-619, 1% Triton X-100, 50 µM NAM, 10 µM dithiothreitol, 1% protease inhibitor cocktail, 3 µM trichostatin A, 2 µM EDTA). This mixture was then subjected to high-intensity ultrasonication on ice using an ultrasonic processor (Scientz). After adding an equal volume of saturated phenol-trimethylamine followed by vortexing for 5 min, the sample was centrifuged at 5000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The phenol phase was carefully transferred to a new tube for protein precipitation using saturated ammonium sulfate and methanol at −20 °C for at least 6 h. After centrifugation, the pellet was washed with cold methanol and acetone. Precipitated proteins were dissolved in 8 M of urea, and the concentration was determined using the BCA kit (Beyotime). The solution was subjected to reduction in 5 mM dithiothreitol at 56 °C for 30 min, and then alkylation in 11 mM iodoacetamide for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. The protein sample was treated in 100 mM triethylamine bicarbonate and digested overnight with trypsin at a 1:50 mass ratio. For the enrichment of lactylation-modified peptides, tryptic peptides were dissolved in NETN buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 0.5% NP-40) and incubated with anti-lactyllysine antibody conjugated agarose beads (PTM BIO) at 4 °C overnight. After thorough washing with NETN buffer and water, peptides were eluted with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, vacuum-dried, and analyzed by LC-MS/MS. The spectra were searched against the Mus_musculus_10090_SP_20220107.fasta database and a reverse decoy database using the MaxQuant software. Trypsin/P was set as the enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. The precursor mass tolerance was 20 ppm and the fragment ion tolerance was 0.04 Da. Fixed modification was set for carbamidomethylation on cysteine, and variable modifications included lactylation on lysine and oxidation on methionine. The false discovery rate was kept below 1%.

Molecular docking

Protein structures for mouse Sirt1 (Q923E4), Sirt2 (Q8VDQ8), Sirt3 (Q8R104), Sirt4 (Q8R216), Sirt5 (Q8K2C6), Sirt6 (P59941), Sirt7 (Q8BKJ9) and ANT2 (P51881) were obtained from the RCSB database (https://www.rcsb.org/). They were processed on the Molecular Operating Environment platform (MOE 2019.1) with the force field selection set to Amber 10, including the removal of water and ions, protonation, addition of missing atoms, completion of missing groups, and protein energy minimization. The HDOCK server was used to predict the relevant binding by employing a hybrid docking strategy and setting each protein as a rigid molecular scaffold. The docking score was calculated using the knowledge-based iterative scoring function provided by ITScorePP, with a more negative value suggesting strong binding. Subsequently, the Pymol2.1 software was used for visual analysis of the model.

Aim 7: Statistical analysis

All data were collected from 3 to 5 independent experiments with multiple samples. Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots. Parametric tests were used for normally distributed data, while non-parametric alternatives were applied when normality assumptions were violated. Parametric tests analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni multiple comparisons test or unpaired two-tailed t-test. Statistical significance is indicated in the figures and legends.
