Altered Notch pathway-related protein expression in cord blood of preeclampsia offspring: a pilot study
Authors: Ouxuan Jin, Yixin Wu, Liwei Tang, Jingyun Yang, Meixian Zhang, Lizhen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to test the hypothesis that the NCOR2/DLL-4/Jagged-1 axis is dysregulated in cord blood of offspring from mothers with preeclampsia (PE) and gestational hypertension (GH), favoring an anti-angiogenic profile. We compared expression levels of these Notch pathway components among PE, GH, and healthy control groups to explore their potential role in fetal vascular programming.MethodsThis is a prospective cohort study, including 17 mother-offspring pairs with gestational hypertension, 17 mother-offspring pairs with preeclampsia, and 34 healthy mother-offspring pairs as controls. Standardized questionnaires were used to collect family and birth information, medical records were used to collect clinical data, and Western blot was employed to measure the expression levels of NCOR2, DLL-4, and Jagged-1 in cord blood. We analyzed the differences in offspring cord blood proteins among the three groups.ResultsCord blood protein expression revealed significant dysregulation of the NCOR2/DLL-4/Jagged-1 axis. Compared to the control group (1.36 ± 0.44), the GH (1.00 ± 0.12) and PE (0.94 ± 0.15) groups were characterized by a significant downregulation of NCOR2 (p = 0.001). Furthermore, a progressive and significant increase in DLL-4 (p = 0.001) was observed across the control (0.82 ± 0.28), GH (0.98 ± 0.17), and PE (1.27 ± 0.40) groups, which was accompanied by a significant stepwise decrease in Jagged-1 expression (control: 1.36 ± 0.28, GH: 1.02 ± 0.12, PE: 0.87 ± 0.18, p < 0.001).ConclusionThe cord blood levels of NCOR2 and Jagged-1 proteins were significantly decreased in offspring of PE and GH, while DLL-4 protein expression was significantly increased in offspring of PE. These findings suggest that dysregulated Notch signaling, characterized by an anti-angiogenic profile in the fetal circulation, may contribute to the vascular programming observed in offspring of preeclamptic pregnancies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09086-1.Keywords: Preeclampsia, NCOR2, DLL-4, Jagged-1

Introduction

Preeclampsia and gestational hypertension represent two prevalent forms of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy [1]. Preeclampsia (PE) is a major contributor to maternal and neonatal mortality [2]. Epidemiological data from 2019 indicate that PE affects approximately 2%-5% of pregnancies and is a leading cause of perinatal mortality in both mothers and offspring [3]. Globally, an estimated 4 million women are diagnosed with PE annually, resulting in over 70,000 maternal and 500,000 infant deaths [3, 4]. Studies suggest that offspring of preeclamptic pregnancies face significantly elevated risks of cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurological diseases in adulthood [3, 5, 6]. Among these, preeclampsia exerts a significant impact on cardiovascular development in offspring. The underlying mechanisms are thought to involve multiple pathophysiological changes, with aberrant angiogenesis and endothelial dysfunction considered core components [7, 8].

Based on the central role of aberrant angiogenesis and endothelial dysfunction in PE pathogenesis, increasing evidence suggests that these vascular abnormalities extend beyond the maternal circulation to affect the offspring. Studies have demonstrated a disrupted balance between pro- and anti-angiogenic factors in the fetal-placental circulation of preeclamptic pregnancies [9]. Furthermore, during the neonatal period, offspring of preeclamptic pregnancies are characterized by endothelial cell dysfunction and altered microvascular development [8]. Clinical studies further reveal that PE offspring exhibit significantly reduced total vascular density by 3 months of age [10] and decreased cerebral blood flow in parietal and occipital lobes during school age (7–10 years) [11]. Animal models corroborate these observations, showing impaired cerebral perfusion and microvascular reactivity in PE offspring as early as postnatal day 5, likely linked to aberrant expression of angiogenesis-related proteins [12]. Collectively, these findings point to a programmed state of systemic angiogenesis dysregulation—including alterations in the cerebral vasculature—in offspring exposed to preeclampsia. However, the molecular mechanisms by which the preeclamptic environment programs persistent alterations in offspring angiogenesis—and how these alterations intersect with key developmental signaling pathways—remain poorly understood.

Angiogenesis is a complex process precisely regulated by multiple signaling pathways, among which the Notch signaling pathway plays a critical role. As an evolutionarily conserved system regulating cell fate determination, proliferation, and apoptosis, the Notch signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in angiogenesis and neurogenesis [13]. Jagged-1 and DLL-4 are two ligands of the Notch receptor, which exert opposing effects on angiogenesis: Jagged-1 knockout mice display reduced dermal vascular branching, while its overexpression enhances neovascularization [14]. Conversely, the DLL-4/Notch axis regulates vascular sprouting by modulating tip cell numbers—pathway inhibition increases tip cells and branching, whereas activation suppresses both [15]. Nuclear receptor corepressor 2 (NCOR2, also known as SMRT) is a transcriptional suppressor of Notch signaling, which mediates gene silencing and participates in diverse biological processes, including hematopoiesis and endothelial cell development [16, 17]. Existing studies have shown that NCOR2 might plays a regulatory role in mice angiogenesis [18]; however, current research remains at the animal experimental stage, with a primary focus on its involvement in anti-tumor angiogenesis [19]. A 2014 study in zebrafish demonstrated that NCOR2 knockdown led to upregulation of DLL-4 protein, promoting arterial endothelial cell fate while ultimately suppressing the differentiation of arterial endothelial cells [17]. A recent study analyzing the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database identified NCOR2 as a preeclampsia-associated gene for the first time, reporting elevated NCOR2 expression in maternal serum and placental tissues [20]. However, to date, no studies have investigated NCOR2 expression in fetal cord blood or its role in offspring vascular programming in humans.

In the present study, we hypothesized that the NCOR2/DLL-4/Jagged-1 axis is dysregulated in cord blood of offspring from mothers with PE and GH, favoring an anti-angiogenic profile. This study adopts a prospective cohort design, recruiting mothers with preeclampsia (PE), gestational hypertension (GH), and normotensive pregnancies from the obstetric department of our institution. Through analysis of cord blood and clinical data, we aimed to explore the potential role of this axis in fetal vascular programming.

Methods

Participants

The hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) groups in this study comprised GH and PE. A total of 17 mother-offspring pairs were enrolled in each of the GH and PE groups, along with 34 healthy mother-offspring pairs as controls.

The inclusion criteria comprised pregnant women in their third trimester admitted for delivery at our hospital’s obstetrics department. The diagnostic criteria for preeclampsia and gestational hypertension were defined by the Chinese Guidelines for the Management of Hypertensive Disorders in Pregnancy [2]. Preeclampsia was defined as high blood pressure (≥ 140/90 mmHg) after 20 weeks of pregnancy and excess protein in urine (≥ 0.3 g in 24 h, or urinary protein/creatinine ratio ≥ 0.3, or random urine protein ≥ 1+). In the absence of proteinuria, preeclampsia was diagnosed if the woman has any one of the following organ or system dysfunctions: involvement of major organs (heart, lungs, liver, kidneys), or hematologic, digestive, or neurological abnormalities, or placental-fetal complications [2]. Gestational hypertension was defined as high blood pressure (≥ 140/90 mmHg) after 20 weeks of pregnancy with no detectable proteinuria [2]. Exclusion criteria included pre-existing type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus, pre-existing chronic hypertension, other severe pregnancy-associated complications, active infectious diseases, and multiple gestation. Body Mass Index (BMI) and smoking status were not used as exclusion criteria; instead, they were included as covariates in the statistical analysis.

Data collection

Data were collected through standardized questionnaires(Supplementary file 1) and medical record systems. After explaining the study purpose, the questionnaire procedures, and data confidentiality measures to the participating mothers and their families, informed consent was obtained and questionnaires were administered. Simultaneously, relevant clinical data were collected from medical records.

The questionnaire primarily collected the following information: (1) General demographic characteristics of the mothers (date of birth, education level, occupation, annual household income, etc.), obstetric history, pre-pregnancy height and weight, gestational weight gain, smoking exposure during pregnancy (active/passive), folic acid supplementation, and medication use; (2) Neonatal information: gestational age, birth weight, length, Apgar scores, and mode of delivery. Data collection was performed by postgraduate students specializing in pediatrics who had undergone standardized training.

Cord blood collection and protein analysis

Immediately after placental delivery, 2 mL of cord blood was collected into EDTA-anticoagulated tubes and stored at -80 ℃ until analysis. Protein expression levels of NCOR2, DLL-4, and Jagged-1 were determined by Western blotting. All Western blotting experiments and subsequent densitometry analyses were performed by investigators blinded to the clinical grouping (control, GH, and PE).

Whole blood samples were lysed using T-PER Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Pierce, USA). Frozen blood samples were thawed at 37 ℃ with agitation to ensure complete dissolution and thoroughly mixed by inversion. A 300 µL aliquot of each whole blood sample was transferred to a new tube, and 15 µg of GAPDH (His-tagged) (Recombinant human GAPDH protein, Proteintech, USA) was added per 300 µL of whole blood. The mixture was then combined with 300 µL of protein extraction buffer (T-PER Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent, Thermo Pierce, USA) and vigorously shaken for 30 min. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 ℃, the supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China). For each sample, a 200 µL aliquot was diluted with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to a final concentration of 5 µg/µL.

A 200 µL volume of diluted protein sample was mixed with 40 µL of 5× protein loading buffer (containing β-mercaptoethanol) and incubated at 65 ℃ for 10 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 5 min at room temperature. The supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL centrifuge tube.

For each sample, 60 µg of protein was separated on 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidenefluoride (PVDF) membranes. Prior to transfer, the PVDF membrane was activated by immersion in methanol for 20 s and then equilibrated in Tris-Glycine transfer buffer (containing 5% methanol) for at least 5 min. The SDS-PAGE gel was equilibrated in the same transfer buffer for at least 30 min. Protein transfer was performed under cooling conditions at a constant voltage of 100 V for 2 h, ensuring the membrane remained thoroughly wetted throughout the process.

Following transfer, the membranes were immersed in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (T-TBS) containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated at room temperature for 1 h to block non-specific binding. The membranes were then washed three times with T-TBS for 5 min each. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies: rabbit DLL − 4 polyclonal antibody (1:1000, Proteintech, USA), mouse Jagged − 1 monoclonal antibody (1:2500, Proteintech, USA), rabbit NCOR − 2 polyclonal antibody (1:10000, Proteintech, USA), or Anti-His Tag antibody (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). After washing four times with T-TBS, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies - goat anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Pierce, USA) or goat anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Pierce, USA) - for 1 h at room temperature.

Protein bands were visualized by SuperSignal® West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (Thermo, USA). Membranes were exposed to X-ray film (Huadong Medicine, China) for 5–10 min, followed by development and fixation. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Target protein expression levels were normalized to GAPDH (His-tagged), and the reported values represent the average of three technical replicates per sample.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages). Differences among the control, GH (gestational hypertension), and PE (preeclampsia) groups were assessed using the chi-square (χ²) test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Differences among the three groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, followed by the LSD post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Associations between protein levels and continuous variables were examined using Pearson correlation analysis. Inter-group protein differences across categorical variables were examined using t-tests.

To account for potential confounding factors that differed significantly among groups or were clinically recognized to influence protein expression, multivariate linear regression analysis was performed. Model 1 was constructed with GH, PE, and variables meeting criterion (1) (those with p < 0.05 in univariate analyses). To account for clinically relevant factors, Model 2 was then built by adding variables from criterion (2) (those with potential clinical relevance to angiogenesis or protein expression regulation, e.g., gestational age, neonatal gender). To avoid overfitting given the modest sample size, if the number of candidate variables exceeded 3–5 per outcome, Model 3 was further developed by sequentially removing variables with the largest p-values. The primary exposures of interest (GH and PE) were forced to remain in all models regardless of statistical significance. When multiple correlated variables (e.g., maternal weight and maternal BMI) were simultaneously significant or clinically relevant, only BMI was included in the final model to avoid multicollinearity. Results from the linear regression models are presented as unstandardized β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 30.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Missing data were excluded from the analysis. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analysis

To evaluate the robustness of our multivariate regression findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis using a backward stepwise approach. Throughout this stepwise elimination process, we monitored the stability of the associations between the main exposures (GH and PE) and the protein levels (NCOR2, DLL-4, Jagged-1) in terms of direction, magnitude (β coefficients), and statistical significance. The absence of substantial changes in these estimates would indicate that our primary findings are not unduly influenced by any specific set of covariates, thereby supporting the robustness of the conclusions.

Comparison of cord blood NCOR2, DLL-4, and Jagged-1 among control, GH, and PE groups

The expression levels of cord blood NCOR2, DLL-4, and Jagged-1 proteins in the control, GH, and PE groups are presented in Fig. 1. Unadjusted one-way ANOVA revealed significant differences in protein expression levels among the three groups: NCOR2 levels were significantly lower in the GH (1.00 ± 0.12) and PE (0.94 ± 0.15) groups compared to the control group (1.36 ± 0.44, p = 0.001) (Fig. 1A and D); DLL-4 levels showed a progressive and significant increase from the control group (0.82 ± 0.28) to the GH group (0.98 ± 0.17) and further to the PE group (1.27 ± 0.40, p = 0.001) (Fig. 1B and D); Jagged-1 levels exhibited a significant stepwise decrease across the three groups (control: 1.36 ± 0.28, GH: 1.02 ± 0.12, PE: 0.87 ± 0.18, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1C and D).

Univariate analysis of Notch pathway-related proteins in cord blood

Univariate analyses were performed to identify potential factors associated with NCOR2, DLL-4, and Jagged-1 levels in cord blood, using Pearson correlation for continuous variables and independent t-tests for categorical variables. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analyses were considered potential confounders and were subsequently entered into multivariate linear regression models.

As shown in Table S1 in Supplementary file 3, NCOR2 levels were significantly negatively correlated with several maternal anthropometric measures, including pre-pregnancy weight (r = -0.336, p = 0.013), pre-pregnancy BMI (r = -0.307, p = 0.024), pre-delivery weight (r = -0.389, p = 0.004), and pre-delivery BMI (r = -0.343, p = 0.011). Additionally, NCOR2 showed negative correlations with amniotic fluid index (r = -0.274, p = 0.047) and 1-minute Apgar score (r = -0.556, p < 0.001). A trend toward significance was observed for maternal age (r = -0.230, p = 0.094), maternal height (r = -0.231, p = 0.093), and birth weight (r = -0.243, p = 0.076).

For DLL-4, a significant negative correlation was found with birth length (r = -0.242, p = 0.047). Both gestational age (r = -0.220, p = 0.072) and birth weight (r = -0.204, p = 0.095) showed trends toward negative correlation with DLL-4, though neither reached statistical significance.

Jagged-1 levels were significantly positively correlated with parity (r = 0.327, p = 0.006), gestational age (r = 0.259, p = 0.033), and birth length (r = 0.243, p = 0.046). Maternal height showed a trend toward negative correlation with Jagged-1 (r = -0.228, p = 0.061).

As presented in Table S2 in Supplementary file 3, NCOR2 levels did not differ significantly across any categorical variables examined. DLL-4 levels were significantly higher in mothers without iron supplementation during pregnancy compared to those with supplementation (1.14 ± 0.37 vs. 0.91 ± 0.32, p = 0.014), and in those who received antenatal dexamethasone administration compared to those who did not (1.12 ± 0.40 vs. 0.92 ± 0.32, p = 0.038). Jagged-1 levels showed a trend toward lower levels in mothers who received dexamethasone (p = 0.094) and in those without iron supplementation (p = 0.116), although these did not reach statistical significance.

Multivariate analysis of Notch pathway-related proteins in cord blood

Based on the univariate analysis results described above, candidate covariates with p < 0.05 were entered into Model 1, together with the primary exposures of interest (GH and PE). Model 2 was then constructed by adding clinically relevant factors (e.g., gestational age, neonatal gender) to Model 1. To avoid multicollinearity, when correlated variables such as maternal weight and maternal BMI were simultaneously present, only BMI was retained. For NCOR2 and Jagged-1, given the modest sample size and to avoid overfitting, a third model (Model 3) was developed by sequentially removing variables with the largest p-values, while retaining GH and PE, until the final model contained only 3–5 covariates per outcome.

The results of linear regression analysis are presented in Tables 2, 3 and 4. In Model 3, NCOR2 levels remained negatively associated with GH (β = -0.286, p = 0.003), PE (β = -0.295, p = 0.001), amniotic fluid index(β = -0.002, p = 0.035), and 1-minute Apgar score (β = -0.309, p < 0.001). DLL-4 levels showed a positive association with PE in Model 1 (β = 0.410, p < 0.001). This association remained robust after further adjustment for gestational age and neonatal gender (Model 2: β = 0.415, p < 0.001). For Jagged-1, in Model 3, Jagged-1 levels were significantly negatively associated with GH (β = -0.306, p < 0.001) and PE (β = -0.444, p < 0.001), and positively associated with parity (β = 0.126, p = 0.001) and gestational age (β = 0.040, p = 0.007). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings, as the associations remained stable throughout the stepwise removal of covariates.

Discussion

Preeclampsia programs vascular and endothelial dysfunction in offspring

Accumulating evidence suggests that the adverse intrauterine environment induced by preeclampsia (PE) has long-lasting consequences on the vascular health of offspring [7, 21]. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that the adverse cardiovascular impact of preeclampsia (PE) on offspring begins early in life and persists into adulthood. After adjustment for birth weight and its interactive effects, neonates born to preeclamptic mothers exhibit higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure, as well as increased fractional shortening, ejection fraction, and cardiac index [22]. During childhood and adolescence, those exposed to PE in utero continue to show elevated blood pressure compared to their peers from normotensive pregnancies [23, 24]. By young adulthood, individuals born after maternal hypertension in pregnancy have a more adverse cardiovascular risk profile than those born from normotensive pregnancies [25]. These observations support the concept of fetal programming, whereby an insult during critical periods of development induces persistent structural and functional alterations in the cardiovascular system [8].

The vascular endothelium, a key regulator of vascular tone and homeostasis, appears to be particularly vulnerable to such programming effects. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated impaired endothelial function in offspring of HDP [26]. For instance, Jayet et al. reported reduced flow-mediated dilation in young offspring of preeclamptic mothers living at high altitude in Bolivia, providing direct evidence of persistent endothelial dysfunction in this population [26–28]. This impairment appears to be most pronounced when the preeclamptic pregnancy is complicated by placental dysfunction resulting in a small-for-gestational-age (SGA) infant. Kvehaugen et al. demonstrated that at 5–8 years of age, children born from preeclamptic pregnancies with SGA exhibited significantly reduced endothelial function, as measured by reactive hyperemia index, compared to children from non-SGA pregnancies [27]. The underlying mechanisms are likely multifactorial, involving impaired vasodilation, subclinical atherosclerosis formation, inflammation, and dysregulated epigenetic regulation of endothelial functions [28].

Beyond functional endothelial alterations, structural vascular changes characterized by impaired angiogenesis have also been documented in offspring exposed to preeclampsia. Evidence from animal models has demonstrated that preeclampsia programs reduced brain cortex angiogenesis in neonatal offspring, characterized by decreased vascular density and perfused vessels [29]. These morphological alterations are accompanied by imbalances in circulating angiogenic factors (VEGF/PlGF) and impaired endothelial cell function, including reduced migration and cytoskeletal dysregulation [29]. Consistent with these experimental findings, clinical evidence indicates that an antiangiogenic environment persists in the fetal circulation of preeclamptic pregnancies, with elevated sFlt-1 and reduced PlGF in umbilical cord blood, as well as dysfunctional endothelial progenitor cells compromising neovascularization capacity [21]. Notably, these angiogenic biomarkers have been clinically utilized for the molecular diagnosis of preeclampsia [30], underscoring the clinical relevance of angiogenic dysregulation in PE pathophysiology and its potential implications for offspring vascular programming.

In summary, preeclampsia programs both endothelial dysfunction and structural angiogenic deficits in offspring, manifesting as impaired vascular development across multiple vascular beds. These programmed vascular alterations may contribute to the increased long-term cardiovascular and neurocognitive risks observed in individuals exposed to preeclampsia in utero. Understanding the molecular mechanisms governing these angiogenic disturbances is crucial for identifying early biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets.

Preeclampsia-induced vascular programming in offspring: evidence from altered Notch signaling

In this study, offspring of preeclamptic pregnancies exhibited significantly higher DLL-4 levels and lower Jagged-1 levels in cord blood compared to controls. As two key ligands of the Notch signaling pathway, DLL-4 and Jagged-1 proteins exert opposing effects on angiogenesis [31]. Specifically, DLL-4 inhibits angiogenesis by activating Notch signaling, leading to reduced tip cell formation and decreased vascular branching [15]. In contrast, Jagged-1 promotes vascular formation and branching [14] while antagonizing DLL-4 mediated effects [31]. These angiogenic mechanisms are highly relevant to the pathogenesis of preeclampsia, which is characterized by inadequate uterine spiral artery remodeling and widespread endothelial dysfunction, ultimately resulting in placental hypoperfusion and impaired fetal oxygen and nutrient supply [32].

Given their critical roles in angiogenesis, DLL-4 and Jagged-1 have been closely linked to the development of preeclampsia. Our study demonstrated progressively decreasing cord blood Jagged-1 expression levels across control, GH, and PE groups, consistent with previous placental and cord blood studies. Research has confirmed that DLL-4 and Jagged-1 modulate proliferation and differentiation of cord blood endothelial progenitor cells [33, 34]. Animal studies revealed their expression in both maternal/fetal endothelial cells and trophoblasts, with dynamic level changes during placental development [35]. Immunohistochemical analyses of human placentas have further revealed that Jagged-1 expression increases significantly at term in healthy pregnancies, whereas it is markedly reduced in preeclamptic placentas [36]. Spatial analysis has identified deficient Jagged-1 signaling in the preeclamptic placental vasculature, underscoring the importance of Notch signaling in vascular development and preeclampsia pathogenesis [37].

Interestingly, while direct evidence of DLL-4 upregulation in preeclamptic cord blood or placenta remains limited, our study revealed a progressive increase in DLL-4 expression across the control, GH, and PE groups - a trend that contrasts sharply with that of Jagged-1. This inverse relationship aligns with the functional antagonism between these two ligands [31], and may offer novel insights into the vascular mechanisms underlying hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Whether this opposing expression pattern reflects a compensatory response or contributes directly to the angiogenic imbalance observed in preeclampsia offspring warrants further investigation. Elucidating the precise molecular interactions between DLL-4 and Jagged-1 in the fetal circulation may provide a deeper understanding of Notch-mediated vascular programming and inform future therapeutic strategies.

The offspring of preeclampsia patients exhibited altered expression levels of NCOR2 protein

In this study, offspring of preeclamptic pregnancies exhibited significantly lower NCOR2 levels in cord blood compared to controls. As a key transcriptional corepressor in the Notch signaling pathway, NCOR2 participates in the regulation of diverse biological processes by mediating the silencing of target genes [16]. Mechanistically, both NCOR2 and Notch receptors interact with the downstream transcription factor CSL [CBF1/Su(H)/Lag-1] to modulate gene expression [16]. Upon ligand binding, the intracellular domain of Notch receptor (NICD) is translocated to the nucleus, where it binds CSL and activates target gene transcription. In the absence of NICD binding, CSL recruits multiple corepressor proteins—including NCOR2—to suppress target gene expression [16]. Thus, NCOR2 serves as a critical modulator of Notch pathway output, and alterations in its expression may influence the expression of other pathway components.

In our study, NCOR2 protein levels showed a progressive decrease across the control, GH, and PE groups, whereas DLL-4 exhibited an opposing trend. This inverse relationship is consistent with the functional antagonism between NCOR2 and DLL-4/Notch signaling, suggesting that reduced NCOR2 expression may contribute to the dysregulation of Notch-mediated vascular development in hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. Supporting this notion, a 2014 study demonstrated that NCOR2 knockdown led to upregulation of DLL-4 protein, promoting arterial endothelial cell fate while ultimately inhibiting endothelial differentiation [17]—a pattern that parallels our observations in cord blood. Although the Notch pathway has been implicated in the pathogenesis of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, mechanistic studies exploring the specific role of NCOR2 in this context remain limited.

Notably, a recent study published in November 2024 analyzing the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database identified NCOR2 as a preeclampsia-associated gene for the first time [20]. However, they reported significantly elevated NCOR2 expression in the serum and placental tissues of preeclamptic mothers—a finding that contrasts with our results in cord blood and appears inconsistent with the established biological effects of Notch signaling. This discrepancy likely reflects compartment-specific (maternal vs. fetal) and tissue-specific (placenta vs. blood) NCOR2 regulation, which may represent mirror-image epigenetic and proteomic responses to stress—a phenomenon increasingly recognized in studies of the maternal-fetal interface, where opposing molecular changes occur in the two compartments under pathological conditions. In this context, elevated maternal levels may contribute to placental dysfunction, while fetal downregulation could reflect a distinct regulatory adaptation. However, given that whole cord blood was used in this study, the specific cellular source of NCOR2 (e.g., fetal endothelial cells versus circulating leukocytes) remains unclear; therefore, drawing functional conclusions regarding its role in sustaining angiogenesis is currently premature. Direct evidence supporting any functional interpretation is lacking. Future studies dissecting NCOR2’s role in maternal versus fetal vasculature, ideally at the cellular level, are warranted.

Other factors influencing Notch ligand levels

We also found that Jagged-1 levels were positively correlated with both gestational age and parity. The association with gestational age may reflect temporal regulation of Notch signaling during pregnancy, potentially facilitating placental vascular maturation [38]. However, to our knowledge, gestational age- and parity-dependent changes in Jagged-1 expression have not been previously reported in human cord blood studies, warranting further investigation into the regulatory mechanisms underlying these associations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this exploratory study demonstrates that preeclampsia may be associated with altered expression of key developmental regulators in offspring cord blood, including downregulation of NCOR2 and Jagged-1; and upregulation of DLL-4. These preliminary findings suggest that dysregulated Notch signaling, characterized by an anti-angiogenic profile in the fetal circulation, may contribute to the vascular programming observed in offspring of preeclamptic pregnancies. However, given the modest sample size, limited statistical power, and observational nature of this study, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Direct functional evidence from in vitro or animal models is needed to confirm these observations and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Limitation

This study has several limitations

First, power analysis revealed that the statistical power for detecting group differences was 0.676 for NCOR2, 0.659 for DLL-4, and 0.700 for Jagged-1, all below the conventional threshold of 0.80. These relatively low power values reflect the modest sample size of this exploratory study (n = 17 per group for GH and PE, n = 34 for controls), which may limit the generalizability of our findings and precluded detailed subgroup analyses.

Second, the questionnaire used for data collection was developed specifically for this study and has not been formally validated. Its use for collecting sensitive clinical information (e.g., household income, smoking exposure) may introduce recall bias or reporting bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of these covariates.

Third, the cellular origin of the Notch pathway proteins measured in cord blood cannot be determined from our experimental approach. While DLL-4, Jagged-1, and NCOR2 are known to be membrane-bound proteins expressed in specific cell types such as cytotrophoblasts, syncytiotrophoblasts, and vascular endothelial cells, we used whole blood samples with total protein extraction, which captures proteins from all circulating cell populations but does not allow identification of the specific cellular sources. Additionally, the cellular composition of whole blood may vary across groups, potentially affecting the stability of endogenous housekeeping proteins; to mitigate this, we employed an exogenous His-tagged GAPDH normalization strategy, as detailed in the Methods section. We acknowledge that while this exogenous normalization strategy effectively eliminates biases associated with endogenous GAPDH variability and experimental operation errors, it does not fully exclude biological variability arising from inherent differences in the cellular composition of cord blood samples between normotensive and preeclamptic pregnancies.

We further acknowledge that analyzing placental villous tissue would provide more direct and biologically relevant information regarding Notch pathway dysregulation in preeclampsia, particularly given the critical role of cytotrophoblast-endothelial interactions in spiral artery remodeling—a process fundamentally altered in preeclampsia. However, placental tissue was not collected in this cohort, representing an important limitation of the study design. Before proceeding to in vitro or animal models, future investigations should prioritize placental tissue analysis to validate our findings and to determine the specific cellular origins of the observed expression changes at the maternal-fetal interface.

Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, warranting validation in larger prospective cohorts with standardized instruments. More importantly, mechanistic investigations using animal models of preeclampsia and in vitro functional assays are essential to establish causality and to elucidate the precise role of Notch signaling—particularly the functional antagonism between DLL-4 and Jagged-1 and its regulation by NCOR2—in the vascular programming of preeclampsia offspring. Such studies will help determine whether the observed expression changes directly contribute to endothelial dysfunction and altered angiogenesis, or merely serve as bystander biomarkers of the underlying pathology.
