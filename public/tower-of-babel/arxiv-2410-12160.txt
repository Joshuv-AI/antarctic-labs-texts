When to Trust Your Data: Enhancing Dyna-Style Model-Based Reinforcement Learning With Data Filter
Yansong Li, Zeyu Dong, Ertai Luo, Yu Wu, Shuo Wu, Shuo Han

Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms can be divided into two classes: model-free algorithms, which are sample-inefficient, and model-based algorithms, which suffer from model bias. Dyna-style algorithms combine these two approaches by using simulated data from an estimated environmental model to accelerate model-free training. However, their efficiency is compromised when the estimated model is inaccurate. Previous works address this issue by using model ensembles or pretraining the estimated model with data collected from the real environment, increasing computational and sample complexity. To tackle this issue, we introduce an Out-of-distribution data filter that removes simulated data from the estimated model that significantly diverges from data collected in the real environment. We show theoretically that this technique enhances the quality of simulated data. With the help of the OOD data filter, the data simulated from the estimated model better mimics the data collected by interacting with the real model. This improvement is evident in the critic updates compared to using the simulated data without the OOD data filter. Our experiment integrates the data filter into the model-based policy optimization algorithm. The results demonstrate that our method requires fewer interactions with the real environment to achieve a higher level of optimality than model-based policy optimization, even without a model ensemble.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL)
(
Sutton and Barto 2018
;
Li 2018
)
algorithms can be classified into model-free and model-based algorithms.
Model-free algorithms
(
Mnih et al. 2015
;
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
do not maintain an explicit form of the environment dynamics and can be applied in environments whose dynamics are hard to model.
However, one drawback of model-free algorithms is that they require a large number of interactions with the environment during training, which can be impractical in physical systems, where each interaction takes place in the real world.

In comparison, model-based algorithms
(
Doll, Simon, and Daw 2012
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Chua et al. 2018
;
Deisenroth and Rasmussen 2011
)
maintain an estimated model of the environment dynamics, such as neural networks for the transition kernel and the reward function. Traditional model-based algorithms allow for planning based on the estimated model, though they may suffer from model bias, leading to policies that are far from optimal. Without model bias, model-based methods can find optimal policies with fewer interactions with the environment.

Besides traditional model-based algorithms, Dyna-style algorithms
(
Sutton 1991
;
Janner et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2019
)
also maintain an estimated model. Instead of relying on planning with the estimated model, Dyna-style algorithms use data simulated from the estimated model to accelerate model-free methods while updating the estimated model. This approach reduces the impact of bias in the estimated model. One example of Dyna-style algorithms is model-based policy optimization (MBPO)
(
Janner et al. 2019
)
, which estimates the model of the environment and simulates data from the estimated model to speed up soft actor-critic
(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
.

One weakness of Dyna-style algorithms is that some data simulated from the estimated model do not resemble those obtained from interactions with the real world. Using such data may instead slow down model-free training. Previous works address this issue either by collecting a large amount of data through random interactions with the real environment to pre-train the estimated model before model-free training, which is costly in the real world due to the numerous interactions required, or by simply reducing the rollout length, which is conservative. In fact,
Janner et al. (2019)
and
Sutton (1991)
use these two strategies together. Additionally, to reduce the variance of the data simulated from estimated models,
Janner et al. (2019)
used an ensemble of models, which is computationally expensive.

In this paper, we tackle this problem by introducing a method called
out-of-distribution data filter
. In a nutshell, our method filters out data points simulated from the estimated model if the data points are far from those interacted with the real model before sending the data for model-free training.
This method can be regarded as an instance of Out-of-distribution detection
(
Nasvytis et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Hsu et al. 2020
)
with the real distribution being the transition kernel and the exploration policy of the real environment. We theoretically show that the out-of-distribution data filter not only lowers the drift of the rollout trajectory but also lowers the parametric drift of
Q
-network updates. Thus, with the help of the Out-of-distribution data filter, the data simulated from the estimated model better mimics the data collected by interacting with the real model in terms of
Q
-network updates than it would without the Out-of-distribution data filter.

Our experiments are conducted in the
MuJoCo

(
Todorov, Erez, and Tassa 2012
)
environment.
The results show that when applying the out-of-distribution data filter to model-based policy optimization, it achieves state-of-the-art performance even without using a model ensemble.

Related work

Dyna-style algorithms
: An early example of a Dyna-style algorithm is Dyna-
Q

(
Sutton 1991
)
, which estimates the transition kernel during

Q
-learning and uses data simulated from the estimated model to accelerate the training of model-free
Q
-learning. In deep reinforcement learning, model-based policy optimization
(
Janner et al. 2019
)
employs an ensemble of neural networks to estimate both the transition kernel and the reward function. The algorithm then uses simulated data from the estimated model to expedite the training of soft actor-critic
(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
. More recently,
Zheng et al. (2022)
modify the model-based policy optimization algorithm by introducing an extra critic regularization term in the training of soft actor-critic, and their method achieves similar performance to model-based policy optimization in
MuJoCo

(
Todorov, Erez, and Tassa 2012
;
Brockman et al. 2016
)
environment without model ensemble.

Besides model-based policy optimization, earlier works that apply Dyna-style techniques to deep RL include the Model-Ensemble Trust-Region Policy Optimization
(
Kurutach et al. 2018
)
and its modifications
(
Clavera et al. 2018
;
Luo et al. 2021
)
.
Luo et al. (2021)
provide a theoretical guarantee of monotonic improvement. However, their experiments on
MuJoCo
do not demonstrate state-of-the-art performance, so we did not include their algorithms in our experiments.

Several works explore the trade-off between data simulated from the estimated model and the data interacted with the real model; see
Lai et al. (2022)
;
Shen et al. (2023)
for example.
In this work, we introduced the out-of-distribution data filter. We achieved SOTA performance (requiring less interaction to achieve the same level of suboptimality) compared to model-based policy optimization on the
MuJoCo
environment. Also, to the best of our knowledge, we are the first to analyze when Dyna-style algorithms perform well from an out-of-distribution perspective.

Model-based reinforcement learning
: Dyna-style algorithms are a subset of model-based reinforcement learning algorithms. In addition to Dyna-style methods, there are other approaches such as PILCO
(
Deisenroth and Rasmussen 2011
)
, iLQG
(
Tassa, Erez, and Todorov 2012
)
, and Guided Policy Search
(
Levine and Abbeel 2014
;
Levine, Wagener, and Abbeel 2015
;
Zhang et al. 2016
;
Finn, Levine, and Abbeel 2016
)
.
Wang et al. (2019)
provides a review that summarizes commonly used model-based RL algorithms.

Out-of-distribution detection
: Out-of-distribution detection
(
Hsu et al. 2020
;
Yang et al. 2024
;
Sedlmeier et al. 2019
)
is a technique used in machine learning to identify data points that deviate from the expected distribution. Recently,
Nasvytis et al. (2024)
introduced several out-of-distribution concepts to reinforcement learning. Specifically, the Out-of-distribution problem we focus on includes sensory and semantic anomaly detection, as defined in
Nasvytis et al. (2024)
, which are used to determine whether states are generated from the real environmental model.

Biased gradient learning
: In Dyna-style algorithms, data simulated from the estimated model is sent to the model-free algorithm for training. The gradient used for the update rule contains the error that is derived from the estimation error of the estimated model. Based on that, the model-free training can be reformulated as a Biased Gradient Learning problem. Previous works
(
Ajalloeian and Stich 2021
;
Wang and Giannakis 2020
;
Li and Han 2022
)
considered the additive noise and showed error bounds in gradient-based optimization methods and the convergence rate when error is bounded under certain conditions. In this work, we relax the requirement of additive noise and prove that the shifting of
Q
-network updates is upper bounded after each update with smoothness assumptions.

Preliminary

A Markov decision process is a tuple
(S,A,\mathbb{P},r,\gamma,\mu)
where
S
is the state space,
A
is the action space,
\mathbb{P}\colon S\times A\to\Delta(S)
is the transition kernel,
r\colon S\times A\to\mathbb{R}
is the reward function, and
\gamma\in[0,1]
is the discount factor. The term
\Delta(S)
represents the set of all probability distributions over the state space
S
. A parameterized policy
\pi_{\phi}
parameterized by
\phi
is defined as
\pi_{\phi}\colon S\to\Delta(A)
.
The initial state
s_{1}
is sampled from a distribution
\mu
.
We abuse the notation of
\mathbb{P}
and define the transition of a policy as

\mathbb{P}(s,\pi_{\phi})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}(s)}\mathbb{P}(s,a).

(2)

Throughout this paper, the term
model
refers to the transition kernel. The reward function is known to the learner. One can easily generalize our result to the case that the model refers to both the transition kernel and the reward function, see
Janner et al. (2019)
for example.
In the following analysis, we consider an Markov decision process with continuous state and action spaces.
The transition kernel is assumed to be Gaussian and defined as

\mathbb{P}(s,a)\triangleq(\mu(s,a),\sigma^{2}(s,a))^{\top}

(3)

and

\mathbb{P}(s,\pi_{\phi}(s))\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}(s)}\left[(\mu(s,a),\sigma^{2}(s,a))^{\top}\right].

(4)

We abuse the notation
\mathbb{P}(s,a)
to represent the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu(s,a),\sigma^{2}(s,a))
with mean
\mu(s,a)
and variance
\sigma^{2}(s,a)
. Assuming that the transition distribution is Gaussian is common in continuous state Markov decision processs
(
Deisenroth and Rasmussen 2011
;
Janner et al. 2019
)
and robotics
(
Peters, Vijayakumar, and Schaal Sept.29-30 2003
;
Kober and Peters 2014
;
Brunke et al. 2021
;
Johannink et al. 2019
)

Also, we use the following notations:

\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}(s)}\mu(s,a)\triangleq\mu_{\pi_{\phi}(s)}(s)

(5)

and

\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\phi}(s)}\sigma^{2}(s,a)\triangleq\sigma^{2}_{\pi_{\phi}(s)}(s).

(6)

Thus,

s^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}(s,\pi_{\phi}(s))\Leftrightarrow s^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\pi_{\phi}(s)}(s),\sigma^{2}_{\pi_{\phi}(s)}(s)).

(7)

For simplicity, our theoretical results are derived using a single-variable Gaussian distribution. These results can be easily generalized to multivariate Gaussian distributions by replacing the mean with a vector and the variance with a covariance matrix. See
Deisenroth and Rasmussen (2011)
and
Janner et al. (2019)
for examples.

Bounding the data shifting

We denote
(s,a)
as the state-action pair in a data point taken from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{real}}
and
(\hat{s},\hat{a})
as the state-action pair in a data point taken from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{est}}
. The next state interacted with the real model is
s^{\prime}\sim\mathbb{P}(s,a)=\mathcal{N}(\mu(s,a),\sigma^{2}(s,a))
and the next state simulated from the estimated model is
\hat{s}^{\prime}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}(\hat{s},\hat{a})=\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}(\hat{s},\hat{a}),\hat{\sigma}^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a}))
.

Assumption 4.1
.

The mean of the estimated transition kernel is Lipschitz, i.e.,

\|\hat{\mu}(s,a)-\hat{\mu}(\hat{s},\hat{a})\|\leq L_{S\times A}\|(s,a)^{\top}-(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}\|.

(8)

for all
(s,a)\in S\times A
and
(\hat{s},\hat{a})\in S\times A

Assumption
4.1

Definition 4.2

(Kernel density estimation (KDE) and efficient sample size)

We use a kernel function

with

and

Theorem
4.3
states that the shifting distance between a state simulated from the estimated model
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
and a state collected by interacting with the real environment
\mathbb{P}^{*}
is primarily controlled by three factors: the distance between the data points
(\hat{s},\hat{a})
and
(s,a)
, the variance of the real transition kernel
\sigma^{2}
, and the variance of the estimated transition kernel
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
. In practice, we can manually bound the variance
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
of the estimated model. The real transition model used in our experiment is deterministic with random initial states, so the terms involving
\sigma^{2}
and
\hat{\sigma}^{2}
do not dominate in our case. In other words, the state shifting can be effectively bounded by using the Out-of-distribution data filter, as the dominating term is
\|(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}-(s,a)^{\top}\|
.

In the remaining analysis, we will show that the distance between the updated parameters of
Q
-network using data points from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{est}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{real}}
is bounded by the distance between those data points.

Bounding the value function update shifting

To simplify the analysis, we use Deep Q-Network
(
Mnih et al. 2015
)
as the model-free algorithm instead of soft actor-critic in the theoretical analysis, thereby considering only a finite action space in this and the next subsection. The results of this analysis can also provide insights for soft actor-critic, as soft actor-critic similarly uses two
Q
-networks as critics.

To formally state the theorem, we denote one data point from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{real}}
as
d\triangleq(s,a,s^{\prime})^{\top}
and one data point from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{est}}
as
\hat{d}\triangleq(\hat{s},\hat{a},\hat{s}^{{}^{\prime}})^{\top}
.
We define the
Q
-network as
Q(s,a;\theta)
, where
\theta
represents the parameters.
The target value
y
is defined as

y(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime};\theta^{-}),

(15)

where
s^{\prime}
is the next state,
\theta^{-}
are the parameters of the target network, which are periodically updated to match the
Q
-network parameters
\theta

The Loss function
L(\theta)
of Deep Q-Network is defined as

L(\theta)=\frac{1}{2}(y(s,a)-Q(s,a;\theta))^{2}.

(16)

The update rule for the parameters of
Q
-network is

\theta_{t+1}\leftarrow\theta_{t}-\alpha\nabla_{\theta}L(\theta_{t}).

(17)

By simple derivation, the above update rule becomes:

\theta_{t+1}\leftarrow\theta_{t}+\alpha(y(s,a)-Q(s,a;\theta_{t}))\nabla_{\theta}Q(s,a;\theta_{t})

(18)

The corresponding target and updates using a data point
(\hat{s},\hat{a},\hat{s}^{\prime})
from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{est}}
are:

\hat{y}(\hat{s},\hat{a})=r(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\gamma\max_{\hat{a}^{\prime}}Q(\hat{s}^{\prime},\hat{a}^{\prime};\theta^{-}),

(19)

and

\hat{\theta}_{t+1}\leftarrow\theta_{t}+\alpha(\hat{y}(\hat{s},\hat{a})-Q(\hat{s},\hat{a};\theta_{t}))\nabla_{\theta}Q(\hat{s},\hat{a};\theta_{t}).

(20)

Q
-network and reward function:

Assumption 4.4

Q
-network, and

-network itself are Lipschitz, i.e.,

\displaystyle\|r(s,a)-r(\hat{s},\hat{a})\|

(21)

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\theta}Q(s,a;\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}Q(\hat{s},\hat{a};\theta))\|

\displaystyle\|Q(s,a;\theta)-Q(\hat{s},\hat{a};\theta))\|\,

\displaystyle\leq L_{3}\|(s,a)^{\top}-(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}\|,

for all

Out-of-distribution data filter

In Algorithm
2
, we are applying rejections to data samples to achieve the purpose of data filter. Though in Line
5
of Algorithm
2
, we define the state-action pairs as the data to be taken or rejected, in practice, we find that only considering the state is sufficient. Therefore, in our experiments, we only consider data acceptance and rejection on the level of states instead of state-action pairs. For the choice of rejection level
\epsilon_{k}
, we use 2 different strategies,
static data rejection
and
dynamic data rejection
.
In this section, we denote the estimated model in episode
k
as
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{k}
.

Static Data Rejection
: Static data rejection fits much closer to the default semantics of data rejection defined in our Algorithm
2
. We pick a static value
\epsilon
such that if any
\hat{s}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{est}}
and
\min_{s\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{real}}}\|\hat{s}-s\|>\epsilon
, we will reject
\hat{s}
.

Dynamic Data Rejection
: In dynamic data rejection, the rejection level changes as the training process evolves. Because the estimated model
\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{k}
becomes closer to the true model
\mathbb{P}^{*}
as
k
increases, we want to choose
\epsilon_{k}
such that less data will be rejected as
k
increases. However, finding the optimal value
\epsilon_{k}
for each
k
is challenging. From a practical perspective, we first sort all the data points
\hat{s}
in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{est}}
by the metric
\min_{s\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{real}}}\|\hat{s}-s\|
in descending order.
Then, in episode
1
, we choose
\epsilon_{k}
such that all model-generated data points are eliminated except the first rollout step. In episode
K
, we accept all model-generated data. For episodes between 1 and
K
, we use linear interpolation to determine the percentage of data points to eliminate.

In both approaches, we need to efficiently solve the minimization problem
\min_{s\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{real}}}\|\hat{s}-s\|
. We first considered it as a nearest neighbor problem and approximate the problem as Approximate Nearest Neighbour. We choose one of the most robust and scalable Approximate Nearest Neighbour algorithms, Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW), introduced by
Malkov and Yashunin (2018)
.
By applying the principle of skip-lists introduced in
Pugh (1990)
, HNSW achieves amortized
O(log(n))
time to perform search, insertion, and deletion operations.

In our case, we initialize the HNSW index at the time we create the replay buffer for the data set of the real environment
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{real}}
. Whenever there is a step in the real environment, the new state
s
will be inserted into the HNSW index.
During the filter process, we query the nearest neighbor from the HNSW with
\hat{s}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{est}}
to obtain
\min_{s\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{real}}}\|\hat{s}-s\|
.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed an enhancement to Dyna-style model-based reinforcement learning algorithms through the introduction of an out-of-distribution (OOD) data filter. This approach addresses a key limitation of existing methods, where inaccurate model-generated data can hinder the efficiency and effectiveness of model-free training. By filtering out simulated data that significantly diverges from real-environment interactions, our method improves the quality of the training data, leading to faster convergence and higher final performance.

Our theoretical analysis demonstrated that the OOD data filter reduces the drift in rollout trajectories and limits the parametric shift during
Q
-network updates, thereby providing a more stable and reliable learning process. Empirical results on various
MuJoCo
environments validated our approach, showing that the modified model-based policy optimization algorithm with the Out-of-distribution data filter outperforms standard model-based policy optimization, even without the need for model ensembles.

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha\gamma\left(\left\|\max_{a_{1}^{\prime}}Q(\hat{s}_{1}^{\prime},\hat{a}_{1}^{\prime};\theta^{-})\right\|L_{2}\delta d+\left\|\nabla_{\theta}Q(s,a;\theta_{t})\right\|L_{4}\delta s\right)

(65)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha L_{3}\delta d\left\|\nabla_{\theta}Q(\hat{s}_{1},\hat{a}_{1};\theta_{t})\right\|+L_{2}\delta d\|Q(s,a;\theta_{t})\|

(66)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(L_{1}\delta d+r(s,a))L_{2}\delta d+\alpha\gamma(L_{1}\delta d+L_{4}\delta s)\left\|\nabla_{\theta}Q(s,a;\theta_{t})\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha\gamma(L_{4}\delta s+\|\max_{a}Q(s,a;\theta^{-})\|)L_{2}\delta d+\alpha L_{3}\delta d\left(L_{2}\delta d+\left\|\nabla_{\theta}Q(s,a;\theta_{t})\right\|\right)

(68)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha L_{2}\delta d\|Q(s,a;\theta_{t})\|

(69)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(L_{1}L_{2}+L_{3}L_{2}+\gamma L_{4}L_{2}){(\delta d)}^{2}+\alpha r(s,a)L_{2}\delta d

(70)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha(\gamma L_{1}+L_{3}+\gamma L_{4})\left\|\nabla_{\theta}Q(s,a;\theta_{t})\right\|\delta d+\alpha\gamma\|\max_{a}Q(s,a;\theta^{-})\|L_{2}\delta d+\alpha L_{2}\|Q(s,a;\theta_{t})\|\delta d

(71)

Adding assumption

to the upper bound then we derive:

\displaystyle\|\hat{\theta}_{t+1}-\theta_{t+1}\|

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}(\alpha,\gamma)\|d-\hat{d}\|^{2}+C_{2}(\alpha,\gamma)\|d-\hat{d}\|

where

Proof.

From Theorem

, with probability

we have

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\|d-\hat{d}\|&\leq\|(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}-(s,a)^{\top}\|+\|\hat{s}^{\prime}-s^{\prime}\|\\

Taking square on both side, we get

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\|d-\hat{d}\|^{2}&\leq\left((1+L_{S\times A})\|(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}-(s,a)^{\top}\|+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}}\right)^{2}\\

&\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}+2(1+L_{S\times A})\|(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}-(s,a)^{\top}\|\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}}\\
&\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}}\\

&\quad+\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}}\end{array}

Let
C_{3}=1+L_{S\times A}
, then

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}\|\hat{\theta}_{t+1}-\theta_{t+1}\|&\leq C_{1}\|d-\hat{d}\|^{2}+C_{2}\|d-\hat{d}\|\\
&\leq C_{1}(\alpha,\gamma)(C_{3}\|(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}-(s,a)^{\top}\|)^{2}\\
&\quad+C_{3}\left(2C_{1}(\alpha,\gamma)\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}}+2C_{1}(\alpha,\gamma)\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}}+C_{2}(\alpha,\gamma)\right)\|(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}-(s,a)^{\top}\|\\
&\quad+C_{1}(\alpha,\gamma)\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}}\right)\\
&\quad+C_{2}(\alpha,\gamma)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}}+\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}}\right)\\
&\leq C_{1}(\alpha,\gamma)C_{3}^{2}\|(s,a)^{\top}-(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}\|^{2}\\
&\quad+C_{3}\left(2C_{1}(\alpha,\gamma)\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}}+2C_{1}(\alpha,\gamma)\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})}{\epsilon}}+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}}\right)+C_{2}(\alpha,\gamma)\right)\|(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}-(s,a)^{\top}\|\\
&\quad+C_{1}(\alpha,\gamma)\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})+\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\epsilon}+\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(\hat{s},\hat{a})}{\epsilon}}+\sqrt{\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}(s,a)}{\sigma}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}(s,a)}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}(s,a)\epsilon_{\mathrm{KDE}}}}\right)\\

&=C_{1}(\alpha,\gamma)C_{3}^{2}\|(s,a)^{\top}-(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}\|^{2}\\
&\quad+C_{2}(\alpha,\gamma)C_{3}\|(s,a)^{\top}-(\hat{s},\hat{a})^{\top}\|\\
