Call for a ban on gender-centric verbal violence through women-centric offensive abuses elicited in the names of mothers, sisters & daughters in the mass media and public spaces
Authors: Raman Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVerbal violence targeting women has become an increasingly visible issue in contemporary Indian society. Among the myriad forms of abuse, the use of highly offensive, gender-specific verbal abuses, including derogatory words elicited in the names of mothers, sisters, and daughters, has gained prominence in Indian cinema, television, social media, OTT platforms, and public gatherings. These abuses are deeply rooted in societal attitudes that normalize disrespect and violence against women, often blurring the lines between free speech and hate speech. Gender-centric verbal violence—especially sexualized slurs directed at women is pervasive in Indian public life and mediated spaces, contributes to gender-based humiliation and harms women’s mental and social well-being, and normalizes sexual violence. In India, the most common street-level and digital insults exchanged between men are sexualized, women-centric, and gender-centric verbal abuses, including derogatory terms to mothers, sisters, and daughters. Ironically, these expressions are rarely used against women—they are used by men, toward men, yet they revolve around the sexual humiliation of women. This linguistic pattern reflects a deeply patriarchal worldview where a man’s honor is symbolically tied to the sexual purity of his female relatives, and where dominance is asserted by imagined sexual violation of women. Thus, even in all-male exchanges, these abuses are gendered in content but gender-neutral in target, functioning as ritualized performances of masculine aggression. Existing Indian law provides some criminal remedies under the Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986 and Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023, relevant provisions like those replacing IPC Sections 294 and 509 continue to criminalize: Obscene or sexually offensive acts or utterances in public places; and Words, gestures, or acts intended to insult the modesty of a woman. However, these offenses still depend on context and intent, meaning not every abusive word automatically qualifies. This highlights a legal threshold gap. The law currently recognizes such abuse as criminal only when it crosses specific evidentiary boundaries. In contrast, socially, it has already crossed the moral and psychological threshold of violence; the Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, and there are mechanisms for media and digital regulation (e.g., Ministry of Information and Broadcasting rules; IT Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code Rules, 2021). However, the enforcement shows high thresholds for proving obscenity and “insult to modesty,” and these mechanisms do not comprehensively prevent routine public or broadcast use of sexualized slurs. This paper examines the impact of such language on societal norms, the need for regulatory intervention, and the imperative to uphold the values of respect, dignity, and gender equality in Indian civilization. This paper argues for a targeted policy package modelled on smoke-free public-place bans (tobacco control)—combining explicit prohibitions in defined public spaces and regulated media, sustained public health education, decriminalized administrative penalties for repeat public use (fines, community service), and more vigorous digital content takedown/enforcement—to reduce normalization of gender-centric verbal sexual violence. We propose a draft regulatory framework for phased pilot implementation, discuss legal and constitutional considerations, and call for empirical evaluation of health and social benefits.

Introduction

Indian society, traditionally rooted in values of respect toward elders and women, faces a paradoxical coexistence with pervasive gender-based verbal abuses. The normalization of such language in mainstream media and daily discourse reflects and perpetuates misogynistic attitudes. The use of deeply offensive, women-centric curses has transcended informal conversations and entered the realm of entertainment and digital media, where they are often used for shock value or comedic effect. However, these words do not just offend; they reinforce harmful stereotypes, normalize violence, and contribute to a culture where disrespect towards women is trivialized.

Language shapes norms. Repeated public use of sexualized slurs for example, vernacular insults are not merely crude speech—when targeted at women, it is a form of gender-based verbal violence. Such language sexualizes family relations as insults, demeans the target, and contributes to a social environment that tolerates or trivializes sexual aggression. Studies of sexual victimization and harassment in public spaces across India indicate that verbal harassment is common and considered serious by women, with associated impacts on mobility, mental health, and social participation.[12]

Public policy can reshape social norms: India’s ban on smoking in many public places and workplaces (COTPA rules, Prohibition of Smoking in Public Places Rules, 2008, and subsequent state/central enforcement) demonstrates that statutory prohibition, signage, public education, and penalties can successfully change public behavior and reduce exposure to second hand harms. This paper examines legal, ethical, and implementation frameworks to argue for a comparable, evidence-informed public-health approach to reduce gender-centric verbal sexual abuse in Indian public and media spaces.[34]

While freedom of speech is protected under the Indian Constitution, this right is not absolute and must be balanced against the principles of dignity, respect, and public morality. The use of highly derogatory language targeting women infringes on their fundamental rights to dignity and equality. The Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986, and the BNS (Bhartiya Nyaya Sanhita) 2023, Sections 79, 294–296, address speech that incites hatred or threatens public order and societal harmony. Moreover, ethical journalism and responsible content creation should refrain from propagating or normalizing such language.[56]

Gender-centric verbal violence, particularly the use of derogatory terms directed at women, including derogatory terms to mothers, sisters, and daughters, constitutes a significant public health concern. This form of verbal abuse not only promotes misogyny but also has profound implications for the mental and emotional well-being of individuals and communities. It is essential to understand the public health implications of such language to develop strategies to address and mitigate its impact.

Prevalence of Men-to-Men Verbal Violence Through Women-Centric Abuses, including derogatory terms to mothers, sisters, and daughters: The Threshold of Violence in Indian Society

In India, the most common street-level and digital insults exchanged between men are sexualized, women-centric abuses. Ironically, these expressions are rarely used against women—they are used by men, toward men, yet they revolve around the sexual humiliation of women.

This linguistic pattern reflects a deeply patriarchal worldview where a man’s honor is symbolically tied to the sexual purity of his female relatives, and where dominance is asserted by imagined sexual violation of women. Thus, even in all-male exchanges, these abuses are gendered in content but gender-neutral in target, functioning as ritualized performances of masculine aggression.

Sociolinguistic and Cultural Context

Across various linguistic regions, including Hindi, Punjabi, Bhojpuri, and Marathi, as well as others, equivalent expressions exist that sexualize female kin to insult male adversaries. Empirical sociolinguistic studies and ethnographic observations show that these abuses are among the most frequently used expletives in informal male interactions (on the streets, in traffic, in workplace banter, and in sports). They appear in Bollywood scripts, stand-up comedy, and digital memes, further reinforcing normalization. The male peer group becomes a primary site where such language is learned, performed, and rewarded as a sign of “toughness” or “authentic masculinity.” This creates a linguistic environment where violence is coded into everyday masculinity. Psychological and Behavioral: The use of women-centric slurs among men serves several social-psychological functions: Dominance assertion, verbal equivalent of physical intimidation, bonding: normalized aggression creates group solidarity among men through shared profanity. Dehumanization training: repetitive use of sexualized language desensitizes speakers to the emotional impact of such violence. Consequently, this habitual verbal aggression acts as a psychological rehearsal for physical aggression. When frustration escalates, the transition from verbal to physical violence becomes easier—the threshold of restraint is already crossed linguistically.

The “Threshold of Violence” Mechanism

When men abuse men using women’s bodies as metaphors for domination, sexual violence is symbolically enacted in language. This symbolic violence is not harmless: It lowers empathy toward real victims of sexual and physical violence. It normalizes sexualized aggression as an acceptable communication style. It establishes a cultural baseline where aggression is validated through sexual humiliation. Thus, men-to-men verbal violence through women-centric slurs is a threshold of physical violence—it is the point where aggression enters public expression and begins shaping social behavior.

Gender-centric verbal violence through women-centric offensive abuses targeted at mothers, sisters and daughters in social media, Cinema and OTT in India

Gender-centric verbal violence, particularly through offensive abuses aimed at women, manifests prominently in various forms of media, including social media, cinema, and Over-The-Top (OTT) platforms in India. This pervasive issue highlights the damaging stereotypes and societal attitudes entrenched within contemporary culture, which often target women as mothers, sisters, and daughters. The language employed in these narratives not only reflects the prevalent patriarchal mindset but also reinforces discriminatory views, contributing to a cycle of abuse and objectification.

In social media, the proliferation of offensive remarks and derogatory comments aimed at women has escalated, creating a toxic environment for discourse. Mothers, sisters, and daughters find themselves at the receiving end of slurs and insults that dehumanize and belittle their experiences and contributions. This verbal violence is not confined to the private sphere but is amplified by the collective nature of social media, where anonymity often emboldens aggressors. As a result, the public humiliation of women becomes normalized, damaging their mental health and undermining their social standing.

Cinema and OTT platforms have historically played a dual role in this context. On one hand, they have the potential to challenge gender stereotypes and portray women in empowering roles; on the other hand, they often perpetuate harmful narratives that glorify abusive language and reinforce traditional gender roles. Films and shows that depict women as mere supporting characters or objects of lust contribute to a narrative that diminishes their complexities and realities. The dialogue used in these productions frequently includes misogynistic language that trivializes the experiences of women, particularly mothers, who are often depicted as either saints or sinners, lacking agency and individuality.

This representation extends beyond fiction into the lived realities of women, as they encounter similar rhetoric in their personal lives. The derogatory language that is acceptable in film and online spaces leaks into daily conversations, influencing societal attitudes towards women. As young girls and women internalize these messages, the impact of gender-centric verbal violence is profound; it shapes their self-image and public identity, often leading to a diminished sense of self-worth.

To combat this issue, it is essential to foster awareness and advocate for more responsible portrayals of women in media. Encouraging creators to adopt language that respects and values women’s experiences can contribute to a cultural shift. Media literacy programs that educate audiences about the implications of gendered language can empower individuals to challenge and reject verbal violence. By addressing the root causes of these harmful narratives, society can take significant steps toward fostering an environment of respect and equality for women, ultimately breaking the cycle of verbal violence that pervades contemporary culture.

Gender-Centric Verbal Abuses at Indian Educational Campuses: The Case of Normalizing Peer Behavior

In Indian campuses, the use of gender-centric verbal abuses, including derogatory terms to mothers, sisters, and daughters, often emerges as a normalized aspect of peer behavior. This phenomenon raises critical questions about the cultural dynamics of student interactions, the perpetuation of misogyny, and the implications for individual and communal well-being. Understanding how these terms function within peer behavior is essential for addressing their harmful effects and fostering a more respectful academic environment.

The Role of Peer Behavior in Normalizing Verbal Abuse: In many peer groups, the use of derogatory language can serve to establish dominance or acceptance. Students may adopt such language to align with their peers, fostering a culture where misogynistic behavior is not only tolerated but encouraged. Students who refrain from using such language may fear social ostracism or be labelled as “weak” or “uncool.” This dynamic can compel individuals to participate in or endorse verbal abuse to maintain their social standing. Reinforcement of Masculine Identity: The use of terms, such as verbal abuses, including derogatory terms to mothers, sisters, and daughters, often correlates with the reinforcement of toxic masculinity, wherein aggressive behavior and derogation of women are mistakenly equated with strength and power. Male students may feel pressured to exhibit this behavior to validate their masculinity. The desire to demonstrate toughness or a lack of sensitivity can lead male peers to engage in verbal abuse as a performance of their identity, often overshadowing the moral implications of their language.

Consequences of Gender-Centric Verbal Abuse: Impact on Women: (a) Psychological Distress: The repetition of derogatory terms directed at women can lead to significant mental health issues, including anxiety, depression, and lowered self-esteem. Women may feel marginalized and disrespected, which can affect their academic engagement and social interactions. (b) Limited Participation: An environment rife with gender-centric verbal abuse can discourage women from participating in discussions, events, or leadership roles, perpetuating their marginalization within academic spaces. Effects on Male Students; (a) Emotional Repression: Male students may internalize aggressive behaviors as normative, limiting their ability to express vulnerability or connect emotionally with peers. This emotional repression can contribute to unhealthy coping mechanisms and problems in future relationships. (b) Normalization of Aggression: Continuous engagement in verbal abuse can desensitize male students to the implications of their language and behavior, leading to an increased likelihood of aggression in other aspects of their lives. Through education, clear policies, and the promotion of positive behavior, institutions can work towards eliminating verbal abuses and fostering a culture of respect and equality that benefits the entire campus community.

Public Health and Preventive Implications

Impact on mental health

The use of gender-specific verbal abuse contributes to a toxic environment that adversely affects the mental health of women. Repeated exposure to derogatory terms can lead to: Increased Anxiety and Depression: Women who are frequently subjected to such language may experience heightened feelings of anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem. The psychological toll can manifest in various ways, including social withdrawal and diminished quality of life. Normalize Violence and Intimidation: Verbal abuse can undermine a woman’s sense of safety and security, making them feel vulnerable in public and private spaces. This normalization of verbal violence can create an environment where more severe forms of abuse are tolerated or accepted.

Impact on community health

Beyond individual consequences, gender-centric verbal violence has broader implications for community health: Erosion of Social Cohesion: The acceptance of derogatory language within a community undermines social cohesion and promotes a culture of disrespect. This erosion can lead to division, hostility, and an overall decline in community trust. Intergenerational Effects: Children who witness or experience gender-based verbal violence may internalize these behaviors, perpetuating cycles of violence and abuse across generations. This can lead to long-term societal issues, including increased rates of domestic violence and mental health disorders.

From a public health perspective, Men-to-men verbal violence should be recognized as an early marker of community aggression. Interventions aimed at language regulation, school-based awareness, and gender-sensitivity education can function as primary prevention strategies for broader violence. De-normalizing these slurs—through bans in public media, signage in schools/workplaces, and administrative penalties—can raise the social threshold against violence. Men-to-men use of women-centric verbal abuses, including derogatory terms to mothers, sisters, and daughters, is not a benign cultural quirk—it is a systemic manifestation of gendered aggression, symbolic sexual violence, and the linguistic threshold of physical violence in Indian society. Recognizing and addressing this phenomenon is essential for any policy framework that seeks to reduce gender-based violence, promote respectful speech, and transform masculine norms toward non-violence.

Background and Legal Context

Criminal and statutory provisions

Under the BNS, 2023, relevant provisions like those replacing IPC Sections 294 and 509 continue to criminalize: (1) Obscene or sexually offensive acts or utterances in public places; and (2) Words, gestures, or acts intended to insult the modesty of a woman.[6] However, these offenses still depend on context and intent, meaning not every abusive word automatically qualifies. This highlights a legal threshold gap—the law currently recognizes such abuse as criminal only when it crosses specific evidentiary boundaries. In contrast, socially, it has already crossed the moral and psychological threshold of violence. The Indecent Representation of Women (Prohibition) Act, 1986, targets indecent representation in advertisements/publications and has been applied primarily to visual/advertising media rather than conversational abuse.[5]

Media and digital regulation

The Ministry of Information & Broadcasting issues guidelines and has regulatory authority over broadcast content (and rules for cable and television). The Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, establish processes for grievance redressal and the removal of unlawful content online, and impose obligations on large online intermediaries and publishers.[78]

Prevalence and Public-Health Impact

Multiple empirical studies and surveys of sexual victimization in public spaces across Indian cities highlight frequent experiences of verbal harassment and the perception of such behaviors as severe forms of gender-based victimization. Verbal sexual harassment reduces women’s sense of safety, restricts public mobility, and contributes to chronic stress and mental-health effects.

While rigorous national prevalence data on the use of specific slurs is limited, qualitative and quantitative research in urban India documents the routine use of sexualized insults, street remarks, and sexually explicit taunts, which women experience as humiliating and threatening. These behaviors have measurable social costs (reduced workforce participation, educational attendance, and help-seeking) that justify a public-health policy response.[12]

Rationale for a Tobacco-Style Public Ban Approach

The Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products (Prohibition of Advertisement and Regulation of Trade and Commerce, Production, Supply and Distribution) Act, 2003—commonly known as the COPTA Act, 2003—is an Indian law enacted to protect public health by regulating tobacco use and reducing exposure to tobacco products. The Act prohibits smoking in public places, bans direct and indirect advertisement of cigarettes and other tobacco products, and restricts the sale of tobacco to minors and within a specified distance of educational institutions. It also mandates prominent health warnings on tobacco product packaging and empowers authorities to enforce compliance through penalties for violations. Overall, COPTA aims to discourage tobacco consumption, prevent initiation among children and youth, and reduce the health risks associated with tobacco use in India.[1]

Why compare to tobacco regulation?

Tobacco bans in public places shift a harmful public behavior through a mix of legal prohibition in defined spaces, signage, public information campaigns, administrative penalties, and enforcement mechanisms—yielding measurable reductions in exposure and normative change.

Call to Action

A multi-pronged approach involving legislative action, industry responsibility, community awareness, and individual accountability is essential to eradicate gender-centric verbal violence. The entertainment industry must introspect and adopt guidelines to prevent the propagation of abusive language. Social media companies should implement robust moderation policies. Civil society and educational initiatives should promote awareness about the harmful effects of such language and encourage respectful dialogue.

A tobacco-style public ban is not a magic bullet. But tobacco control demonstrates that law, signage, administrative penalties, and public-education campaigns can change social behavior and reduce exposure to harm. Applying a similar public-health and regulatory logic to gender-centric verbal sexual abuse offers a means to renormalize language that sexualizes and humiliates women.

Key to success will be: clear legal design that protects legitimate expression; administrative, not purely criminal, enforcement; and wide investment in education and normative change (school curricula, workplace training, mass media). Evidence generation through pilots and systematic evaluation must accompany any roll-out. Discussion

Indian cinema and television have historically reflected societal values, but in recent times, there has been a disturbing trend of glorifying vulgar language, including gender-centric abuses. OTT platforms, with their greater creative freedom, sometimes cross ethical boundaries, often portraying such language without moderation. This normalization influences public perception and possibly incites violent behavior. Responsible content creation and self-regulation within the industry are essential to prevent the dissemination of such harmful language. Social media platforms offer a space for free expression, but also serve as a breeding ground for online harassment and verbal violence against women. Women frequently face gender-based slurs, threats, and abuses, which are often amplified due to anonymity and a lack of strict moderation. The virality of offensive language contributes to societal desensitization, making it imperative that digital platforms adopt stringent policies to curb such abuses. India’s civilization embodies respect, compassion, and gender equality. The use of derogatory language, especially targeting women, contradicts these fundamental values. Promoting respectful communication and condemning gender-based verbal violence is crucial to upholding these principles. Educational institutions and civil society organizations should work to sensitize the public to the importance of maintaining civility and respecting women’s dignity.

Gender-centric verbal violence against women represents a pressing public health issue, with far-reaching implications for individual and community well-being. Addressing this challenge requires concerted efforts from public health officials, community leaders, educators, and policymakers. By fostering a culture of respect and dignity, we can mitigate the harmful effects of such language and contribute to a healthier, more equitable society.

Conclusion

Language shapes societal attitudes and behaviors. The normalization of women-centric verbal abuses in Indian cinema, television, social media, and public spaces undermines the fabric of a respectful and egalitarian society that India strives to uphold. Gender-centric verbal sexual abuse in India—including vernacular sexualized slurs targeted at women and their family relations—is a public-health and human-rights problem that warrants coordinated policy action.

Building on the model of tobacco-free public spaces, we recommend a phased, rights-sensitive regulatory package that combines prohibitions in defined public and media spaces, administrative penalties, enhanced digital takedown and media regulation, and sustained public education. We call on policymakers, health researchers, legal scholars, media regulators, and civil-society organizations to collaborate on pilot implementation and rigorous evaluation.

Limitations

This paper presents a policy proposal and conceptual analysis, drawing on existing statutes, regulatory instruments, and published studies; it is not an empirical study. Specifically prohibited-phrase schedules and finely tuned enforcement rules require participatory drafting, pilot testing, and legal vetting. Additionally, scholarly evidence measuring the direct causal impact of language bans on health outcomes is limited; hence, the need for the proposed research agenda.
