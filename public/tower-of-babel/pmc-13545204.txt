Mucosal melanoma in 1481 patients in the Netherlands. An overview of metastatic patterns, main driver mutations and the efficacy of therapeutic strategies
Authors: E Jutten, S Kruijff, M H M Oonk, M F Lutke Holzik, B L van Leeuwen, K P Wevers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMucosal melanomas are a rare melanoma subtype with poor prognosis. This project aimed to identify the main driver mutations of the different mucosal melanomas and to evaluate the efficacy of targeted/immunotherapies in patients with metastatic disease in current practice. In addition, the baseline clinicopathological characteristics, metastatic patterns, and primary treatment strategies and survival of mucosal melanoma subtypes were studied.MethodsThe Dutch nationwide pathology databank (Palga) and the Netherlands Comprehensive Cancer Organisation (IKNL) were queried for all patients with a diagnosis of mucosal melanoma (1989-2021).ResultsThe dataset included a total of 1481 patients. Mutation status was assessed in 397 patients and in 170 patients (43%) a driver mutation was discovered. Patients with metastatic disease who received targeted/immunotherapy did show a better survival. The liver and lung were the predominant locations of the first metastasis.ConclusionsThe data in the present study implies that patients with metastatic disease mucosal melanoma might benefit from targeted/immunotherapies. Molecular analysis should be considered in these patients.

Introduction

Mucosal melanomas are a rare melanoma subtype, accounting for approximately 1.3% of all melanomas, representing 0.03% of all new cancer diagnoses [1]. They can arise on any mucosal surface, but primary lesions are most frequently located in the upper airway, vulvovaginal region, or anorectum [2]. Less commonly, they are found in the urinary tract, esophagus, stomach, small and large intestine or in the internal female genital organs [1].

In contrast to cutaneous melanoma, the etiology, risk factors, and pathogenesis of mucosal melanomas are poorly understood [1, 3, 4]. Additionally, the absence of early symptoms and the often advanced stage at diagnosis contribute to the paucity of effective treatment options and poor prognosis. This results in a 2-year overall survival rate of 45% and a 5-year survival rate of less than 25% [1, 4, 5]. To date, surgery is the mainstay of treatment in mucosal melanomas. However, therapeutic strategies depend on the tumor location and complete resection can be challenging due to surrounding critical anatomical structures [1]. Radiotherapy may provide local control, but does not seem to be associated with improved survival rates [3].

For cutaneous melanoma, the therapeutic landscape has been transformed by the introduction of immuno- and targeted therapies, leading to improved survival outcomes [6]. In contrast, little is known about the mutational landscape of mucosal melanoma subtypes, and clinical trials analyzing the efficacy of these therapies in mucosal melanoma patients are scarce.

Given the rarity of this disease, knowledge regarding mucosal melanomas and especially the metastatic patterns, mutation rate, and the efficacy of different treatment modalities including targeted- and immunotherapies, is limited. Since well-established protocols for the treatment of mucosal melanomas are lacking, analyzing the current practice outcomes will be valuable in this rare disease. Therefore, this project aims to identify the main driver mutations across mucosal melanoma subtypes in current practice, to evaluate the efficacy of targeted and immunotherapies in patients with metastatic disease, and to study and compare the baseline clinicopathological characteristics, metastatic patterns, primary treatment strategies, and survival of mucosal melanoma subtypes in the Netherlands.

Methods

Data

The Netherlands Comprehensive Cancer Organization (IKNL) and the Dutch nationwide pathology databank (Palga) were queried for all patients with a diagnosis of mucosal melanoma [7, 8]. The Netherlands Comprehensive Cancer Organization manages the Netherlands Cancer Registry (NCR), and registers anonymized clinical data of all patients that are diagnosed with cancer in the Netherlands. Palga is a nationwide registry of all pathology reports, including molecular testing results, in the Netherlands. From the NCR registry, all patients with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of mucosal melanoma were requested and linked with the pathology reports from Palga. The available IKNL (August 2021 release) and linked PALGA (August 2021 release) anonymized data were used. The different mucosal melanoma subtypes included were upper airway melanoma, pulmonary melanoma, esophageal melanoma, gastrointestinal melanoma (without anorectal), anorectal melanoma, internal female genital melanoma, vulvar melanoma and urinary tract melanoma.

The ethical committee of the IKNL and the privacy and scientific committee of Palga approved this research (K20.407).

Mutations

Mutations were analyzed per mucosal melanoma subtype. Percentages of the most common mutations were calculated, including BRAF, KIT, and NRAS.

Baseline characteristics and metastatic patterns

Analyses were performed on the following clinicopathological characteristics for the different mucosal melanoma subtypes: sex, age, date of diagnosis and disease stage. As there is no universal staging system for mucosal melanomas, there are various staging systems in use for the different mucosal melanomas [9, 10]. Therefore, in this study disease stage was categorized as local disease (tumor without involvement of regional lymph nodes or distant metastases), regional disease (tumor with involvement of regional lymph nodes but without distant metastases) and distant disease (tumor with distant metastases). To analyze metastatic patterns location of first metastasis was evaluated.

Treatment and survival

Different primary treatment strategies (surgery, surgery + radiation therapy, surgery + chemotherapy, surgery + targeted/immunotherapy, radiation therapy, radiation therapy + targeted/immunotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted/immune therapy, other (combined) (supporting) therapy, no therapy) were evaluated per mucosal melanoma subtype and disease stage. Since the IKNL database does not clearly distinguish between targeted and immunotherapy in this dataset, these therapies were grouped together.

For patients with distant metastatic disease, survival was analyzed for patients who received targeted/immunotherapy versus patients who did not receive this therapy and overall survival curves were plotted according to treatment. For patients with local and regional disease overall survival curves were plotted and analyzed for the most commonly applied primary treatment modalities (surgery, surgery + radiation therapy, radiation therapy and no therapy). In addition, survival curves were plotted per mucosal melanoma subtype.

Statistics

The (one-way) ANOVA was used to analyze parametric continuous data, chi-square test and Kruskal-Wallis were used to analyze categorical variables. Kaplan-Meier survival tables and survival curves were plotted to display survival rates over time, according to (the most common) treatments and melanoma subtype. Survival differences were assessed using log-rank statistics. Survival was defined as the number of months between the date of diagnosis and the date of death. Univariable and multivariable Cox regression analysis were performed to assess prognostic factors for survival. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA/SE 17.0. All tests were 2-sided, confidence intervals were set at 95%, and a p-value of <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Mutations

The IKNL database included a total of 1481 patients. Mutation status was available in 397 patients (27%). Table 1 presents the mutation status across the different mucosal melanoma subtypes. In 170 of 397 patients (43%) a driver mutation was identified. The highest mutation rates were observed in vulvar melanomas (56%) and gastrointestinal melanomas (53%). The lowest mutation rates were found in pulmonary melanomas (0%), anorectal melanomas (37%) and upper airway melanomas (39%). Overall, the most common driver mutation in mucosal melanoma patients was NRAS (22%), followed by KIT (13%) and BRAF (9%). However, in patients with vulvar melanoma, anorectal melanoma and urinary tract melanoma, KIT was the most frequently mutated gene (31%, 21% and 14% respectively).

Baseline characteristics and metastatic patterns

Baseline characteristics of patients across mucosal melanoma subtypes, as well as the location of the first distant metastasis, are shown in Table 2. Sex differed significantly between subtypes, with a female percentage of 73% (p = 0.000), explained by the large share of vulva melanomas and internal female genital melanomas (42% of all mucosal melanoma cases). The mean age at diagnosis was 70 years, with no significant difference between melanoma subtypes (p = 0.195). Incidence did not significantly change over time (p = 0.885). Stage at diagnosis was significantly different (p = 0.000); for esophageal melanomas and gastrointestinal melanomas more patients presented with distant disease at diagnosis (51 and 57%) whereas in the reaming subtypes 61% of patients presented with local disease only. There was a significant difference in the location of the first distant metastasis (p = 0.000). In esophageal, gastrointestinal, anorectal, and urinary tract melanomas metastases presented mostly in the liver (39%, 43%, 43%, and 60% respectively). Distant disease in upper airway melanomas, internal female genital melanomas and vulva melanomas comprised predominantly pulmonary metastases (34%, 29% and 42% respectively).

Treatment and survival in patients with local and regional disease

For patients with local and regional disease Table 3A presents the different primary treatment modalities per melanoma subtype. Most patients underwent surgery as primary treatment strategy (73.2%), followed by surgery + radiation therapy (11.0%). All patients with a urinary tract melanoma underwent surgery as primary treatment. Patients with vulvar melanoma also predominantly underwent primary surgery (91.7%), additional surgery due to irradical resection was performed in 12.9% of resected patients. Radiation therapy as a sole treatment modality was used in patients with esophageal melanoma (31.8%), internal female genital melanoma (14.7%) and patients with a pulmonary melanoma (14.3%). Figure 1a shows overall survival curves for patients with local disease and regional disease according to the most commonly applied primary treatments (surgery, surgery + radiation therapy, radiation therapy and no therapy). Patients who underwent surgery alone or surgery combined with radiation therapy demonstrated the best survival (p = 0.000). There was no significant difference in survival between those two treatment regimens (p = 0.327).

Treatment and survival in patients with metastatic disease

Table 3B presents the different primary treatment modalities per melanoma subtype for patients with distant disease. Of these patients 28.4% received no treatment at all. Especially patients with distant disease pulmonary melanoma underwent no therapy (66.7%). In contrast, among patients with distant disease vulvar melanoma only 15.4% received no treatment, while 50.0% still underwent surgery as primary metastatic treatment. Figure 1b shows the overall survival curves for patients with distant disease according to primary treatment. Survival differed significantly (p = 0.000), with the poorest survival observed in patients who received no therapy.

Of the 209 patients with metastatic disease mucosal melanoma 34 patients (16.3%) received targeted/immunotherapy. Figure 2 displays overall survival curves comparing patients with metastatic disease who received targeted/immunotherapy with those who did not. Patients who received targeted/immunotherapy demonstrated a significantly better overall survival compared to patients who did not receive this treatment (p = 0.028). The median overall survival of patients who received targeted/immunotherapy was 303 days and the median overall survival of patients who did not receive this treatment was 163 days, suggesting that patients with mucosal melanoma may benefit from targeted/immunotherapy. The median survival time following initiation of targeted/immunotherapy in patients with metastatic disease was 244 days.

Survival rates of the different mucosal melanoma subtypes

Survival curves of the different mucosal melanoma subtypes are shown in Fig. 3 (3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 3 year, 5 year and median overall survival rates of the different mucosal melanoma subtypes are shown in appendix table 1). Overall, there was a significant difference in survival between mucosal melanoma subtypes (p = 0.000), with the best survival observed in patients with vulvar melanoma (median survival 34 months) and the poorest survival in patients with a pulmonary melanoma (median survival 5 months). The estimated 5-year survival for all patients with mucosal melanomas was 23.1%, with a median survival of 20.4 months.

Factors associated with overall survival on univariable and multivariable regression analysis

The above results are supported by the Cox regression analysis (Table 4). In univariable regression analysis, nearly all variables were associated with survival. In the multivariable analysis, sex was no longer significantly associated with survival (HR 0.909, p = 0.230). As expected, higher disease stage was associated with worse survival (HR 1.550, p = 0.000 for regional disease compared to local disease and HR 3.008, p = 0.000 for distant disease compared to local disease). Also, a higher age at diagnoses was a predictor of a worse survival (HR 1.022, p = 0.000 per additional year). Survival outcomes improved over time, with hazard ratios of 0.801 p = 0.003 for patients diagnosed in 2000–2010 compared with 1989-1999 and 0.715 p = 0.000 for patients diagnosed in 2011-2021 compared with 1989–1999. With regard to treatment, surgical intervention was associated with a significantly improved survival (HR 0.048, p = 0.000), and patients who received targeted/immunotherapy had a higher likelihood of survival (HR 0.518, p = 0.001).

Discussion

By analyzing this 30-year Dutch cancer registry dataset including 1481 patients we were able to identify the currently known main driver mutations across the different mucosal melanoma subtype. Baseline clinicopathological characteristics, metastatic patterns and different practiced treatment strategies were analyzed. Subsequent survival outcomes were evaluated and compared. In patients with distant disease, survival was analyzed for patients who received targeted/immunotherapy versus patient who did not received this treatment.

Mutations

Mutation status was assessed in 397 patients. In 43% a driver mutation was present. NRAS was the most frequently mutated gene (22%), followed by KIT (13%) and BRAF (9%). These mutation frequencies are higher than those reported in a recent review of Nassar & Tan [6]. Their review, including 65 (prospective and retrospective) studies with a total of 3985 patients of different anatomic sites, revealed a NRAS mutation frequency of 8%, a KIT mutation frequency of 13% and a BRAF mutation frequency of 6%. The mutation frequencies differ in comparison to cutaneous melanoma, in which approximately 40–60% of patients harbor a BRAF mutation, 1–10% a KIT mutation and 10–25% by a NRAS mutation [11, 12]. As knowledge of the mutational landscape in mucosal melanoma continues to expand, opportunities for the development and application of targeted therapies may increase. This could ultimately contribute improved survival for patients with this rare melanoma type with dismal prognosis. In practice, performance of a molecular analysis of mucosal melanomas should always be considered in order to evaluate the possibilities of targeted therapies in these patients when systemic targeted therapy is available.

Baseline clinicopathological characteristics and metastatic patterns

In the present study, the predominant location for mucosal melanoma was the upper airway (34%), closely followed by the vulva (30.2%) and anorectum (17%). Even more rare types included pulmonary melanomas, urinary tract melanomas and esophageal melanomas. These findings are consistent with studies in the European and American population [13–15]. However, in a Chinese population Lian et al. reported mucosal melanoma tumors most commonly in the lower GI tract and upper airways and less common at gynecological sites [16]. This finding might suggest that ethnicity and/or environmental factors may play a role in the development of a mucosal melanoma. The present study shows that mucosal melanoma is a disease of advanced age for all subtypes, with a median age at diagnosis of 71 years. Most patients presented with local disease (61%), however, patients with esophageal or gastrointestinal melanoma were more likely to present with distant disease at diagnosis and exhibited poorer survival outcomes.

The relatively large sample size of this study allowed the finding of metastatic patterns. Predominant first distant metastatic sites for almost all melanoma subtypes were the liver (27%), lungs (24%) and distant lymph nodes (10%). These findings are in accordance with those reported by Lian et al., who analyzed metastatic patterns in 706 patients with mucosal melanoma [16]. This underscores the importance of including these sites in metastatic work-up and follow-up.

Treatment strategies and survival

Patients with distant disease who received targeted/immunotherapy showed a better survival (median overall survival 303 days) in comparison to patients who did not receive this therapy (median overall survival 163 days). While the introduction of targeted and immunotherapies for the management of patients with cutaneous melanoma has represented a major breakthrough in clinical practice and has become a preferred first line approach for patients with advances or metastatic cutaneous melanoma, the development of systemic therapies for mucosal melanomas has progressed more slowly [17, 18]. However, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved three immunotherapies for the treatment of patients with metastatic (mucosal) melanoma: the monoclonal antibody anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) Ipilimumab, the monoclonal antibodies anti-programmed cell death receptor 1 (PD-1) Pembrolizumab and Nivolumab; plus the combination of Ipilimumab and Nivolumab [19].

A systematic review of Li et al. [19] identified 41 studies with 1262 melanoma patients and studied the overall response rate (ORR), progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) of these immunotherapies in patients with metastatic mucosal melanoma. For most patients who received ipilimumab monotherapy, the ORR ranged from 0% to 17%, the median PFS was less than 5 months, and the median OS was less than 10 months. For patients who received nivolumab or pembrolizumab monotherapy, most studies showed an ORR of more than 15% and a median OS of more than 11 months. The combined administration of anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 agents showed better results than single-agent therapy with an ORR of more than 33.3%. The authors concluded that immunotherapies, especially anti/PD/1 monoclonal antibodies alone and in combination with anti-CTLA-4 monoclonal antibodies, are promising for patients with metastatic mucosal melanoma. These findings are consistent with the survival data in this study, although in this study it is unknown which specific type of targeted/immunotherapies patients received. Recently, a study of Blank et al. [20] concluded that patients with resectable locoregional cutaneous melanoma benefit from neoadjuvant immunotherapy followed by surgery compared to surgery followed by adjuvant immunotherapy. Also the study of Patel et al. researched whether pembrolizumab given both before surgery and after surgery as compared with pembrolizumab given as adjuvant therapy alone, would increase event-free survival among patients with resectable stage III or IV melanoma. They concluded that event-free survival was significantly longer among those who received pembrolizumab both before and after surgery than among those who received adjuvant pembrolizumab alone. However, since only four out of the total 313 patients had mucosal melanoma, the authors were unable to conclude whether the value of neoadjuvant pembrolizumab would be different for this melanoma subtype [21]. Therefore, further research on the efficacy of (neo)adjuvant targeted and immunotherapies in mucosal melanoma patients must be conducted in the future.

Another recent development in the treatment of melanoma is the advent of LAG-3 inhibitors, such as relatlimab. An anti-LAG-3 inhibitor is a monoclonal antibody that blocks the LAG-3 immune checkpoint receptor, enhancing T-cell activation and antitumor immunity [22]. In combination with anti-PD-1 therapy (e.g., nivolumab), it improves progression-free survival in metastatic melanoma in general [23]. However, data specific to mucosal melanoma are limited. A retrospective study of Tawbi et al. [24] including a small cohort of mucosal melanoma patients (23 treated with relatlimab-nivolumab vs. 28 with nivolumab alone) did not reveal a statistically significant improvement in progression-free survival with the combination. Nonetheless, the biological rationale for LAG-3 blockade remains, and further studies are needed to clarify its role in patients with mucosal melanoma [22].

Given the rarity of this malignancy it can be considered to treat mucosal melanoma patients only in, or after consultation with, expertise centers. In addition, it would be beneficial for future mucosal melanoma patients if studies will be established for mucosal melanoma patients alongside studies for cutaneous melanoma patients.

In patients without distant metastases, surgical resection was most frequently the primary treatment (74%) for all mucosal melanoma subtypes in this study. It underscores surgery as the mainstay of treatment for mucosal melanoma like previously concluded in the literature [15, 25, 26]. Adjuvant therapy was administered in 11.3% of these patients, mainly radiotherapy. Patients who underwent surgery and adjuvant radiation therapy did not show a better overall survival compared to patients that underwent surgery alone. However, there will be at least some selection bias in these data. In addition, details on local control, recurrence, clinical symptoms and more precise staging are lacking. Previously, Kelly et al. [27] did not find a survival benefit either for adjuvant radiotherapy in patients with anorectal melanoma, although they did report a lower local recurrence rate following surgery plus radiotherapy (17%) compared with rates reported for surgery alone (50%).

Survival per mucosal melanoma subtype

In this large database study, patients with vulvar melanoma demonstrated the best survival. This was consistent in the multivariable analysis. Schaefer et al. [14] and Mehra et al. [28] similarly reported a better prognosis for vulvar melanomas in comparison to other localizations of the primary tumor, and Al-Haseni et al. [15] and Altieri et al. [29] showed a better survival for patients with a mucosal melanoma in the genitourinary tract in general. However, in the study of Lian et al. in the Chinese population, patients with nasal pharyngeal and oral, gastrointestinal, gynecological and urological all had a similar survival [16]. We hypothesized that the significant better survival of patients with a vulvar melanoma may be attributable to the relatively easy detection of vulvar melanomas, which is also reflected in the fact that only 6.3% of the patients with vulvar melanoma have metastatic disease at presentation. Patients with a pulmonary melanoma showed the poorest survival. A literature comparison for this entity was not feasible since this very rare melanoma subtype is not well described in literature, apart from some case reports. Interestingly there is some discussion in literature regarding the existence of primary pulmonary melanomas. Some authors argue that melanomas detected in the lungs actually represent metastases from occult cutaneous melanomas, while other studies favor the hypothesis that melanoma can arise in the lung as a primary tumor from residual melanoblasts [30–32]. Since primary pulmonary melanoma is an entity recognized by the thoracic World Health Organization classification [33], we included this subtype in our study.

Limitations

A limitation of this study is the retrospective nature of this national database design. The study relies on epidemiologic data which depend on accurate coding, these coding practices may have evolved over time. The lack of a standardized staging system for mucosal melanomas may have led to potential misclassification in extent of disease. Also, some important issues are missing, like local recurrence data, the rationale for choosing treatments and the specific type of immuno/targeted therapies administered. This may have led to a treatment bias and withholds further in depth analyses. In addition, it is unknown whether mutation analyses have followed the complete same procedure which might have resulted in data heterogeneity. Still, this is one of the largest cohorts of mucosal melanoma and it gives a unique insight in mucosal melanomas.

Conclusion

Mucosal melanomas are a rare entity with a dismal prognosis. This study shows that in 43% a driver mutation is present and that patients with distant disease mucosal melanoma may benefit from targeted/immunotherapies. Therefore molecular analysis should be routinely considered in order to evaluate the possibilities of targeted therapies when systemic agents are available for adjuvant or palliative treatment. It will be beneficial for future mucosal melanoma patients if mucosal melanoma can be included in trials researching targeted therapies and newer immunotherapies. Predominant first distant metastatic locations are the liver, lungs and distant lymph nodes. For patients with only locoregional disease, surgical resection is the most frequent primary treatment. The addition of adjuvant radiation therapy does not ensure a better survival. Among all mucosal melanoma subtypes, patients with a vulvar melanoma show the best survival and patients with a pulmonary melanoma the poorest.
