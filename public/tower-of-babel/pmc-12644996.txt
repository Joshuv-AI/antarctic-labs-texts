Utilizing unstructured data to predict the art museum visitor numbers using deep learning approaches
Authors: Ziyi Tian, Xiao Wang, Yuwei Tan, Hyung Jong Na, Cheong Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study proposes a modified and adaptive Balanced Scorecard (BSC) framework tailored to the strategic context of art museums as its primary contribution. The adapted BSC categorizes museum strategies into four dimensions—Social Values and Cultural Impact (SVCI), Visitor Experience and Tourism Development (VED), Exhibition and Operational Efficiency (EOE), and Art/Cultural Education and Sustainable Growth (ACES)—providing a systematic lens for interpreting and quantifying qualitative managerial priorities. These strategic perspectives were extracted from unstructured textual data sourced from museum websites and visitor-generated online content through natural language processing and text mining techniques. Building on this strategic classification, the study develops deep learning–based predictive models that integrate both structured numerical data and text-derived qualitative variables to estimate visitor numbers. Eight deep learning architectures, including Transformer, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), and Generative Adversarial Network (GAN), were evaluated. The results indicate that VED exerts the most substantial influence on visitor attendance, particularly through interactive exhibitions, personalized experiences, and tourism collaborations, while other strategic dimensions provide marginal improvements in predictive accuracy. The Transformer model achieved the best predictive performance, reflecting its superior ability to capture complex relationships between structured and unstructured data. By combining an adapted BSC framework with advanced predictive modeling, this study offers both a novel theoretical approach for categorizing museum strategies and a practical tool for enhancing decision-making in visitor engagement, exhibition planning, and resource allocation.

Introduction

Like the other industry, art museums have increasingly implemented artificial intelligence (AI) technology not only to enhance resource allocation but also to improve visitor experiences by predicting attendance patterns. This AI adoption of art museums might radically change how they engage with the visitors, providing personalized recommendations, tracking behavioral patterns, and optimizing exhibition1. Despite having a higher barrier of entry, digital data is being increasingly transmitted into the public domain, and text mining methods have provided a fast way to extract information from content on various platforms, including online review sites, visitor comments and social media engagement2. Such approaches enable museums to better understand the sentiments, preferences, and expectations of their audience, empowering data-driven decision-making to enhance visitor engagement3.

Modern digital tools offer great potential to predict attendance; however, traditional models used to estimate attendance typically rely on structured numeric data such as past attendance, seasonality, and weather. Conventional forecasting models mainly handle structured data and fail to capture dynamic visitor sentiment and its cultural context4. These models might explain visitor flow, but they miss out on real-time qualitative feedback on incoming visitor preferences and social trends. Recent research suggested that these types of models often do not address audience behavior and cultural interests in depth5,6. On the other hand, AI and natural language processing (NLP) introduces new opportunities for integrating unstructured textual data for predictive models, facilitating a dynamic understanding of visitor engagement7,8. Deep learning-based models could be highly beneficial in handling large-scale text-based data and capturing behaviors that may lead visitors to do something specific. Previous research has shown the power of AI, and particularly the combination of textual data analysis and deep learning methods, for predicting trends in tourism and cultural heritage domain9–11. However, in the research domain of art museums, this advantageous deep learning-based prediction has not fully been adopted.

In alignment with these notions and to fill the gap, this study proposes an AI-based predictive model that uses qualitative data extracted by text mining techniques. To enhance prediction accuracy for visitors, the study employs eight deep learning algorithms, including Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), Autoencoder, Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), Deep Q-Network (DQN), Generative Adversarial Network (GAN), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) and Transformer. With this AI-based models, this study presents a new solution that leverages the descriptive content of art museum websites and visitor-generated textual data to enrich predictive models through information derived from text-based sources using strategic insight. Besides implementing AI and text mining methods, this study uses the Balanced Scorecard (BSC). The BSC perspective can segment the strategy into financial, customer, internal processes, and learning and growth perspectives12,13. Therefore, to improve alignment with art museums’ missions, this research adjusts these categories and proposes four strategic perspectives: (1) social value and cultural impact, (2) visitor experience and tourism development, (3) exhibition and efficiently management, and (4) art/cultural education and sustainable growth. By analyzing museum descriptions and textual data, this research quantifies how museums emphasize such aspects and synthesizes the results within prediction models of visit metrics.

The aims of this study are fourfold. First, it seeks to construct an AI-based predictive model for estimating visitor numbers to art museums using deep learning and text mining techniques. Second, it aims to quantify the textual emphasis on key museum strategies by integrating qualitative themes into visitor prediction models. Third, the study compares the predictive performance of multiple deep learning algorithms in forecasting museum attendance. Finally, it evaluates the effectiveness of incorporating qualitative textual data in enhancing forecasting accuracy relative to traditional numerical approaches.

Theoretically, this research addresses a gap in the literature by proposing a blended approach that combines qualitative textual data with deep learning-based predictive models, offering a novel perspective on visitor forecasting in the cultural sector. Methodologically, it introduces an AI-driven framework that leverages text mining to extract strategic themes from museum websites and related sources, demonstrating how qualitative insights can complement and improve predictive accuracy.

From a practical perspective, the study provides actionable guidance for museum administrators. The results enable a deeper understanding of visitor engagement patterns, support more informed exhibition planning, and facilitate targeted resource allocation. By integrating artificial intelligence, museum studies, and text analytics, the proposed approach strengthens data-driven decision-making and enhances institutional responsiveness to evolving audience expectations.

Ultimately, this research aspires to make art museums more accessible and sustainable. By enabling institutions to better anticipate visitor trends and adapt strategies in real time, the study contributes to the development of more targeted, impactful exhibitions and educational programs. The combined application of AI-based text mining and advanced deep learning architectures offers a more comprehensive and effective model for predicting art museum attendance.

Related works and research questions

AI-based approaches in museum analytics

Several studies demonstrate the use of AI-driven analytics in museum administration. An example from Puspasari3 showed that machine learning model could help to personalize exhibition recommendations in virtual museum tours. Similarly, Li et al.1 studied AI-based segmentation to model visitor behavior and optimize museum spaces. These studies reveal how AI helps museums improve operations through actionable insights about visitor engagement. Within cultural heritage analytics, text mining with NLP has gained status since it enables transformation of data from text into valuable information for museums. Yang et al.11 and Li et al.14 suggested that text mining has a prominent role in visitor profiling. Also, Wu et al.4 claimed that using deep learning models with text features enhances the forecasting accuracy of tourism demand. Based on these observations, using text mining could be a valuable approach of exploration as to improve predictions on museum visitors. Deep learning has become a popular method for time-series forecasting and pattern recognition, thus making it a beneficial methodology for predicting the number of visitors. Research of Lemmel et al.15, which deep learning and regression models were applied to the problem of tourism flow analysis, indicated that RNN have a better performance compared to more classical forecasting techniques. Likewise, Chen et al.16 and Kwon and Yu17 used AI-driven models to prove that GAN and CNN have the potential to enhance the recommendation system from the perspective of visitor experience. Despite the advancements in analyzing museum visitor data, these studies have focused mainly on analyzing structured data and have not addressed qualitative textual data, which constitutes a significant part of visitor feedback data. This study overcomes this limitation by deploying eight deep learning models consisting of RNN, Autoencoder, GRU, DQN, GAN, CNN, LSTM, and Transformer, which contributes to the AI-based predictive model while integrating text mining technique.

Application of the balanced scorecard framework in museum analytics

The idea of creating BSC was initiated at corporate level to measure organizational performance, although the successful application of this framework was adapted to several other domains such as the one for the cultural institutions. The framework of BSC was developed by Kaplan and Norton12 as the strategic tool considering four perspectives, such as financial, customer, internal processes, and learning and growth perspective. This research adapts the BSC framework to be compatible with art museum aims with four strategic dimensions: social values and cultural impact (including financial perspective), visitor experience and tourism development (correlated with customer perspective), exhibition and operational efficiency (align with internal processes perspective), arts, cultural education and sustainability (fit with learning and growth perspective). Previous studies indicate that SVCI is important for museum performance assessment since these indicators provide a new way to measure success aside from financial outcomes18. Also, VED focuses on improving visitor engagement, providing immersive experiences, and attracting tourists that significantly relate to museum success19. Next, EOE indicates that optimizing internal processes leads to enhanced sustainability and institutional effectiveness, while optimized exhibitions provide better visitor experience and, in the long run, financial stability20. Finally, ACES includes organizations that focus on education, artistic development, and sustainable growth, and it leads to sustained engagement and institutional growth, making those institutions relevant and inviting to a variety of people long into the future21–23. Using text mining techniques, this research quantifies the extent to which art museums attend to these strategic areas of focus and incorporates these insights into visitor prediction models.

While the original Balanced Scorecard (BSC) proposed by Kaplan and Norton12 in 1992 was primarily designed for corporate performance measurement, its core conceptual foundation—integrating financial and non-financial perspectives—remains relevant in contemporary cultural institutions when appropriately adapted. However, the operational environment and customer behavior in the museum sector have undergone significant transformations over the past three decades, largely driven by technological advances such as digital exhibitions, interactive media, mobile guidance systems, and personalized online engagement. These innovations have reshaped how visitors interact with museums, moving beyond passive observation toward immersive, technology-mediated experiences.

In recognition of this evolution, the present study modifies the traditional BSC to align with the strategic aims of art museums in the digital era. Specifically, the “customer perspective” of the original BSC has been redefined as Visitor Experience and Tourism Development (VED), which captures not only physical visit satisfaction but also digital engagement, co-creation of experiences via interactive technologies, and integration with broader tourism ecosystems. Similarly, the “learning and growth perspective” has been expanded into Art/Cultural Education and Sustainable Growth (ACES) to reflect both traditional educational missions and the sustainability practices enabled by emerging technologies. The Exhibition and Operational Efficiency (EOE) dimension incorporates technological tools for space optimization, visitor flow management, and data-driven curatorial decisions, while Social Values and Cultural Impact (SVCI) considers how museums leverage technology for community outreach, cultural inclusion, and heritage preservation.

The basis for applying the BSC framework in this context lies in its adaptability: rather than transferring the 1992 model directly, this study operationalizes each perspective with indicators and qualitative factors that are sensitive to present-day museum service environments. In particular, visitor experience is measured not only through conventional service quality indicators but also through text-mined themes from museum websites and visitor-generated content, allowing for the capture of real-time, technology-influenced customer sentiments. This approach ensures that the BSC framework remains both conceptually consistent and practically relevant for assessing strategic priorities in modern art museum management.

Methods

Data

The public dataset used in this study is panel data on art museums collected and published by the Korea Culture and Information Service (KCISA) (https://kcisa.kr/). At the stage of data preprocessing, samples with missing values were removed to avoid inconsistencies during the modeling process.

For the text data, irrelevant elements such as HTML tags, special characters, numbers, and punctuation were removed to ensure only meaningful elements were retained. Tokenization was then applied using natural language processing (NLP) techniques to split the text into smaller elements, such as individual words and tokens. After tokenization, morpheme segmentation was performed to identify the root form of words. The next step involved normalization of words using Porter’s stemming algorithm, which reduces words to their root form and helps achieve consistency within the textual datasets24.

Furthermore, part-of-speech (POS) tagging was performed to categorize words into nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs. As prior studies indicate that nouns dominate thematic information in topical text, only nouns were retained for further analysis25,26. The Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) method was then employed to calculate the significance of words in the dataset27,28. This method identifies keywords that appear frequently in a specific document but are relatively infrequent across the entire dataset, making them the most distinguishable terms.

To improve computational efficiency and exclude low-impact words, terms occurring in less than 5% of the documents were removed. Through these preprocessing steps, the resulting text data more accurately reflected the thematic priorities of the museums, enhancing its applicability for deep learning models in the later analytical phase.

Description of variables.

Qualitative variables were encoded as dummy variables that indicate the different themes highlighted in the museum director’s message. For social values and cultural impact (SVCI), if the message focusses on the museum’s role primarily as social values and cultural impact, then the value will be encoded as 1 and 0 otherwise. Similarly, if the message highlights visitor experience and its contribution to tourism development then 1 was taken as value for the factor visitor experience and tourism development (VED), and if the message focuses on the quality of museum exhibitions and operational efficiency it was measured as 1 for Exhibition and Operational Efficiency (EOE).

Finally, for the factor art, cultural education and sustainable growth (ACES), 1 was taken if the message highlights the commitment of the museum with artistic and cultural education and long-term sustainability. In addition to the qualitative variables, quantitative variables include the physical form of the museum, its operational performance, and other data related to the museum programming. For example, site area value (SAV) is logarithmic of the total land area of the museum, building area value (BAV) is logarithmic of the total area of the building. Also, exhibition area details (EAD) and area details (AD) provide the data on the exhibition space and the distribution of area throughout the museum, while values for educational facility area (EFAV) and office area details (OAD) represent the log of the area of space reserved for educational activities and administrative offices respectively.

The study also incorporates variables that reflect the museum’s operational and cultural activities. Number of performances (NP) is the log-transformed number of performances hosted by the museum, and total number of programs (TNP) is the total number of cultural and educational programs organized. The variable number of open days (NOD) quantifies the days in the year that the museum was open, and number of visitors (NV) is a log-transformed total number of visitors. This study aims to design an AI-based predictive model that improves the accuracy of museum attendance prediction through including both qualitative factors and quantitative attributes as predictor variables. By combining these variables, the model can better understand how museums could improve visitor experience and engagement, as well as plan where to put future exhibits that is more relevant to visitors.

Classification of museum management strategies using the BSC

Traditional BSC might not apply in the same way to art museums as they are cultural and societal organizations rather than purely commercial ones. Numerous museums highlight roles and impact as agents for cultural and social change, including community engagement, heritage preservation, and social discourse. Thus, due to this specific context of the arts museums, we modified the conventional BSC perspective to fit strategies that are relevant in cultural and nonprofit organizations20. Incorporating text mining techniques, this research categorizes museum strategies into four dimensions (i.e., SVCI, VED, EOE, and ACES) through the lens of the modified BSC. NLP and text mining were applied to textual data collected from museum websites to highlight themes that match these strategic dimensions. Both word frequency and contextual relevance analysis were used to sequentially assign museums to one of the modified BSC. The classification results are subsequently combined with predictive models to explore how museum management strategies impact visitor attendance.

To operationalize the adapted Balanced Scorecard (BSC) framework within the museum context, a systematic text analysis procedure was implemented to classify institutional strategies into four strategic dimensions: Social Values and Cultural Impact (SVCI), Visitor Experience and Tourism Development (VED), Exhibition and Operational Efficiency (EOE), and Art/Cultural Education and Sustainable Growth (ACES). Textual data were collected exclusively from authoritative sections of official museum websites—such as “About Us,” mission statements, and the museum director’s greeting—since these segments typically encapsulate the organization’s strategic priorities. The collected texts underwent rigorous preprocessing as outlined in “Data” section, including tokenization, morpheme segmentation, stemming, and part-of-speech tagging, with nouns retained due to their role as primary carriers of thematic content. Subsequently, salient keywords were identified through Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) weighting and matched to the four BSC dimensions using a pre-defined thematic dictionary informed by prior literature on museum management and the conceptual foundations of the BSC. For instance, terms such as “heritage,” “community,” “culture,” and “social impact” were associated with SVCI, whereas “interactive,” “tourism,” “visitor experience,” and “immersion” corresponded to VED. Similarly, keywords such as “efficiency,” “resource management,” and “exhibition planning” were mapped to EOE, while “education,” “workshop,” “sustainable,” and “environment” were assigned to ACES. For each museum, the dominant strategic dimension was determined by calculating the highest aggregated TF-IDF score across categories, ensuring that the classification process was systematic, replicable, and grounded in textual evidence as well as the theoretical underpinnings of the adapted BSC framework. This procedure allowed qualitative strategic orientations to be transformed into quantifiable variables for subsequent integration into predictive modeling, thereby establishing a direct link between unstructured narrative content and measurable performance outcomes.

Model framework.

To capture the role of strategic orientations, an Upgraded Model was developed by sequentially integrating qualitative features derived from the Balanced Scorecard (BSC)-based thematic encoding. Specifically, four extended model specifications were constructed: Model B, which incorporates Strategic Visitor-Centric Initiatives (SVCI); Model C, which integrates Visitor Education and Development (VED); Model D, which includes Exhibition and Operational Excellence (EOE); and Model E, which adds Accessibility, Collaboration, and External Stakeholder engagement (ACES). This incremental design enables the systematic evaluation of how different strategic emphases contribute to predictive performance when combined with quantitative attributes.

Each of these model specifications was subsequently estimated using a diverse set of eight deep learning architectures: Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), Autoencoder, Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), Deep Q-Network (DQN), Generative Adversarial Network (GAN), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Transformer. By applying multiple architectures, the study not only compares the predictive accuracy of alternative deep learning approaches but also examines their relative effectiveness in modeling the interplay between structured and unstructured data sources.

This layered framework provides a rigorous methodological foundation for identifying the conditions under which qualitative strategic orientations enhance forecasting accuracy. Moreover, it establishes a comparative lens through which both traditional sequence-based models and more advanced attention-based architectures can be evaluated within the cultural analytics domain.

Empirical analysis

In this study, the deep learning algorithms were utilized to predict the number of visitors to art museums due to the high dimensional data characteristics, ability of semantic penetration into various relationships between factors. Traditional statistical approaches might not capture qualitative and quantitative inter-relationships43. On the other hand, deep learning techniques can handle structured data (e.g., museum attributes) and unstructured data (e.g., textual descriptions) simultaneously. Thus, deep learning algorithms could be more suitable for predicting museum visitor numbers9,11. We adopted RNN, autoencoder, GRU, DQN, GAN, CNN, LSTM, and transformer. The reason behind the selection of these deep learning models is that these algorithms generally perform well in managing time series data, fulfilling latent representation, screening sequence patterns, and improving prediction accuracy. These strengths of deep learning algorithms would allow robust understanding of visitor behavior by learning from historical data and can reveal underlying patterns that drive attendance11,15.

When the target variables can be categorized into discrete buckets, such as low, medium, and high visitor turnout, classification models could be employed. However, in addition to deep learning algorithms, we adopted a regression method than classifications to finally predict continuous numerical variables (i.e., the number of visitors to art museums). Regression makes it possible for the model to predict the exact number of visitors instead of classifying them into predefined categories44. Additionally, regression offers the advantage of providing a more detailed understanding of the relationship between variations in independent variables (e.g., the size of the museum, exhibition programming, and visitor engagement strategies) and visitor numbers on a continuous scale that are crucial for prediction from the perspectives of cultural organizations45.

Research process

To ensure methodological transparency and improve the readability of the research workflow, the entire study was systematically organized into sequential stages, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This structured process connects the theoretical foundation, data preparation, and model development phases to the final evaluation, thereby addressing the need for a clear representation of the research flow.

Problem definition and framework design

The research began with identifying the limitations of conventional museum visitor prediction methods, particularly their reliance on structured numerical data, and defining the aim to integrate unstructured textual data through deep learning approaches. The Balanced Scorecard (BSC) framework was adapted to the cultural and nonprofit context of art museums, resulting in four strategic perspectives: Social Values and Cultural Impact (SVCI), Visitor Experience and Tourism Development (VED), Exhibition and Operational Efficiency (EOE), and Art/Cultural Education and Sustainable Growth (ACES).

Data acquisition

Panel data on Korean art museums were obtained from the publicly accessible database of the Korea Culture and Information Service (KCISA) (https://kcisa.kr/), which serves as the primary national repository for cultural institution information. The dataset covered multiple years and encompassed both structured operational variables—such as the number of exhibitions held annually, floor space of exhibition halls, total operating budget, staff size, and annual visitor counts—and unstructured textual descriptions, including narrative statements such as museum directors’ greetings, curatorial mission statements, and promotional summaries.

The inclusion of these two distinct data types was critical for the design of the predictive framework. The structured variables provided measurable and standardized indicators of each museum’s physical, financial, and operational capacity, which have been widely used in traditional attendance forecasting models. In contrast, the unstructured textual content captured the institution’s strategic orientation, thematic priorities, and communication style—elements often overlooked in purely numerical datasets but highly relevant for understanding and predicting visitor engagement patterns.

This dual-source data structure enabled the integration of quantitative metrics with qualitative semantic information, thereby facilitating a richer and more comprehensive modeling approach. The unstructured text data, in particular, allowed for the application of Natural Language Processing (NLP) and thematic classification techniques to extract latent strategic signals from narrative content. These qualitative insights were then encoded as categorical or binary variables and incorporated into the deep learning models alongside quantitative features.

By leveraging the KCISA dataset’s breadth and depth, the research ensured both national representativeness and methodological robustness. The resulting panel structure, containing repeated measures of museums across multiple years, also allowed for temporal analysis and the identification of evolving strategic patterns in the Korean art museum sector. This combination of structured and unstructured data represents a methodological advancement over previous forecasting approaches that have relied on single-type datasets, thereby contributing to both predictive accuracy and interpretability in the cultural analytics domain.

Data preprocessing

In the preprocessing stage, both structured and unstructured data underwent systematic refinement to ensure data integrity and analytical consistency. For the structured dataset, records containing missing values were excluded to avoid potential biases or distortions in subsequent modeling. Furthermore, all quantitative variables were transformed using natural logarithms, a process aimed at normalizing variable distributions and mitigating skewness, thereby enhancing the stability and interpretability of regression-based estimations.

For the unstructured dataset, a comprehensive text-cleaning pipeline was implemented. This process involved the removal of non-informative elements, including HTML tags, special characters, numerical figures, and punctuation marks. Following this initial cleaning, tokenization was performed to segment the text into individual lexical units, and morpheme segmentation was applied to identify root forms of words. Stemming techniques were subsequently employed to reduce words to their base forms, promoting lexical uniformity. Part-of-speech tagging was then carried out, with a focus on retaining only nouns, as prior research indicates that nouns encapsulate the core thematic content of textual narratives. Finally, Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency (TF-IDF) weighting was applied to assess the relative importance of terms across the corpus. To further improve computational efficiency and reduce noise, terms appearing in fewer than 5% of documents were excluded from the analysis.

Qualitative strategic encoding and BSC categorization

To incorporate the qualitative dimensions of museum strategies into the predictive framework, textual content from museum websites and directors’ official statements was systematically processed using a combination of Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques and Balanced Scorecard (BSC)-based thematic classification. The objective was to translate unstructured institutional narratives into measurable variables suitable for integration into deep learning models.

The classification framework was grounded in a modified BSC model, adapted to the cultural sector to reflect strategic emphases specific to museum operations. Four thematic categories were identified: (1) SVCI—Strategic Visitor-Centric Initiatives, representing activities aimed at enhancing visitor satisfaction and engagement; (2) VED—Visitor Education and Development, referring to educational outreach, guided tours, and interactive learning programs; (3) EOE—Exhibition and Operational Excellence, encompassing innovation in curation, facility management, and service delivery; and (4) ACES—Accessibility, Collaboration, and External Stakeholder Engagement, addressing initiatives for physical/digital accessibility, partnerships, and community involvement.

The encoding process combined computational and manual approaches. First, TF-IDF weighting was applied to extract high-salience terms from each museum’s corpus. These terms were mapped to the four thematic categories through a lexicon-based matching process informed by expert-curated dictionaries. To validate the classification, a supervised machine learning model trained on manually labeled descriptions was employed. In parallel, a rule-guided manual annotation procedure was conducted by two independent coders with expertise in cultural management, ensuring that semantic nuances were preserved. Intercoder agreement reached Cohen’s kappa (κ = 0.84), indicating almost perfect reliability. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions, enhancing the robustness of the dataset.

Once the dominant strategic focus of each museum was identified, binary indicator variables were generated for the four categories, where 1 denoted the presence of a strategic emphasis and 0 indicated its absence. This binary encoding facilitated seamless integration with quantitative museum attributes in deep learning models, while also ensuring interpretability of the qualitative dimensions.

By systematically operationalizing textual narratives into structured variables, this study advances methodological transparency and reproducibility. The proposed hybrid framework—combining NLP, thematic classification, and expert validation—not only strengthens the predictive accuracy of the models but also establishes a replicable approach for analyzing cultural institutions across diverse contexts.

Model specification

To evaluate the predictive contribution of qualitative strategic factors derived from unstructured textual data, five regression-based predictive models were systematically specified. The baseline specification, Model A, incorporated only structured quantitative variables representing the physical and operational characteristics of art museums—namely site area value (SAV), building area value (BAV), exhibition area details (EAD), area details (AD), educational facility area value (EFAV), office area details (OAD), number of performances (NP), total number of programs (TNP), and number of open days (NOD). This configuration reflects the conventional approach in museum analytics, where predictions are based solely on measurable operational and infrastructural attributes.

Subsequent models (Models B–E) progressively augmented the baseline specification by introducing a single qualitative variable at a time, each corresponding to one of the four strategic perspectives identified through the adapted Balanced Scorecard (BSC) framework. These binary-coded qualitative variables were extracted from museum directors’ official statements and thematic descriptions, classified through natural language processing and text mining. Model B added the Social Values and Cultural Impact (SVCI) variable, reflecting the extent to which a museum positions itself as an agent of cultural enrichment, heritage preservation, and societal engagement. Model C incorporated Visitor Experience and Tourism Development (VED), representing a museum’s emphasis on immersive exhibitions, visitor-oriented services, and integration into the broader tourism network. Model D included Exhibition and Operational Efficiency (EOE), indicating the prioritization of optimized exhibition planning, space utilization, and resource management to enhance visitor satisfaction and retention. Model E introduced Art/Cultural Education and Sustainable Growth (ACES), denoting the commitment to educational programming, artistic development, and environmentally sustainable operations.

This stepwise inclusion of qualitative variables was deliberately designed to isolate and measure the incremental effect of each strategic factor on predictive performance while controlling for the influence of quantitative operational attributes. Such an approach enables a direct comparison between models using only structured data and those enriched with text-derived strategic insights, thereby revealing the relative explanatory power of different management orientations in forecasting museum visitor numbers.

Model training and validation procedures

In this study, eight deep learning algorithms—Recurrent Neural Network (RNN), Autoencoder, Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), Deep Q-Network (DQN), Generative Adversarial Network (GAN), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), and Transformer—were systematically applied to each model specification to compare their predictive performance. These architectures were selected based on their proven strengths in handling sequential data and extracting latent representations. In addition, they are capable of modeling complex nonlinear dependencies and capturing semantic information from unstructured text.

The predictive task was formulated as a regression problem, since the dependent variable, annual museum visitor numbers, is continuous in nature. This regression-based approach preserved the granularity of visitor count variation rather than simplifying it into categories. As a result, the models allowed for more precise estimation and sensitivity analysis, ensuring a richer interpretation of visitor dynamics.

Each algorithm was trained using both quantitative and qualitative feature sets, with the latter derived from Natural Language Processing (NLP) transformations of museum narrative data. This design enabled the direct evaluation of how semantic content interacts with structured operational data. Consequently, the models were able to integrate managerial narratives with physical and operational museum attributes in predicting visitor numbers.

To ensure reproducibility, the dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets using an 80:10:10 split ratio. Stratified sampling was applied to maintain proportional representation of museums by size and operational type. This approach enhanced the fairness of model evaluation and prevented sampling bias.

Hyperparameters were tuned individually for each model using grid search and early stopping criteria. For RNN-based models (RNN, LSTM, GRU), hidden units ranged from 64 to 256 with learning rates between 0.0001 and 0.01, while dropout rates of 0.2 to 0.5 were applied to mitigate overfitting. CNN models used filter sizes of 3–7 with 64–128 filters per layer, followed by max pooling, whereas GANs adopted a generator with three dense layers and a discriminator with four dense layers, optimized with the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.0002, β1 = 0.5).

Autoencoders were constructed with symmetrical encoder–decoder structures, with latent dimensions ranging from 16 to 64. The Transformer was implemented with 2–4 encoder layers, 4 attention heads, and embedding dimensions between 128 and 256. These architectural configurations reflected widely accepted practices in deep learning for handling high-dimensional and sequential data.

All models were trained using the Adam optimizer with batch sizes of 32 or 64 and up to 200 epochs. Early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs was employed to prevent overfitting and improve generalization performance. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) was used as the primary loss function, while R2, RMSE, MSLE, and MAPE were included as complementary evaluation metrics to provide a multidimensional assessment of model performance.

Model implementation was conducted in Python 3.10 using TensorFlow 2.15 and PyTorch 2.2. All computations were executed on an NVIDIA A100 GPU with 40 GB memory to ensure computational efficiency. Random seeds were fixed for both NumPy and TensorFlow/PyTorch libraries, which enhanced the reproducibility of experimental outcomes.

This structured training and validation procedure provides methodological rigor to the study. It ensures that the experimental design can be reliably replicated in future research. Moreover, it establishes a transparent foundation that allows subsequent studies to extend or refine the findings across different datasets or cultural contexts.

Performance evaluation

Model accuracy and error were comprehensively assessed using a suite of complementary evaluation metrics: Coefficient of Determination (R2), Mean Squared Error (MSE), Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Squared Logarithmic Error (MSLE), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE). The inclusion of R2 facilitated an intuitive understanding of the proportion of variance explained by each model, while MSE and RMSE captured the magnitude of prediction errors, penalizing large deviations more heavily. MSLE was particularly relevant in this context, as it is less sensitive to large absolute differences when predicting lower visitor counts, thus reducing bias toward larger institutions. MAPE, on the other hand, provided a percentage-based measure of accuracy, which is easily interpretable for managerial decision-making.

By employing this multi-metric approach, the study ensured a robust and nuanced evaluation of predictive capabilities across models. Performance metrics were computed for both training and validation datasets to assess model stability, and comparative results were aggregated across multiple runs to mitigate the effects of random initialization. This rigorous evaluation design enhanced the reliability of conclusions drawn regarding the relative advantages of incorporating qualitative textual variables into deep learning-based forecasting frameworks.

Comparative analysis and interpretation

Comparative analyses were conducted to address three central research questions: (i) to what extent do qualitative textual variables improve prediction accuracy over models relying solely on quantitative operational data, (ii) which strategic emphasis category—derived from modified Balanced Scorecard (BSC) classifications—exerts the strongest influence on visitor numbers, and (iii) how do the eight deep learning algorithms differ in their predictive performance when applied to the integrated dataset.

For the first objective, model outputs were compared between runs with and without NLP-derived features, allowing for a direct quantification of the incremental predictive power contributed by qualitative variables. This comparison highlighted the specific conditions under which textual content provided significant added value, such as in museums with highly distinctive thematic missions. For the second objective, feature importance was examined using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values to interpret the relative contributions of different strategic emphasis variables. This analysis revealed patterns in how specific curatorial or audience engagement strategies correlated with higher attendance levels.

The third objective involved ranking the algorithms by average performance across evaluation metrics, while also considering computational efficiency and training stability. While certain architectures excelled in capturing temporal dependencies (e.g., LSTM, GRU), others demonstrated superior generalization when integrating high-dimensional feature spaces (e.g., Transformer). Findings were contextualized in terms of practical implications for museum management, such as tailoring marketing strategies, optimizing program scheduling, or enhancing thematic alignment to target audience interests.

By structuring the methodology into these explicit stages, the research not only achieved transparency and replicability but also fulfilled the reviewer’s request for a clearer depiction of the methodological flow. This systematic approach provides a blueprint for future applications of deep learning in cultural analytics, where both structured and unstructured data sources are leveraged to improve forecasting precision and strategic insight.

Table 5 shows comparisons of RMSE for the models on predicting visitors to art museums. RMSE is a commonly used evaluation metric for regression models that calculates the standard deviation of the residual errors between the actual and predicted values. A lower RMSE equates to a better estimate, and it shows how close the model’s predictions come to reality. As seen in the results, model A achieves the highest RMSE values for all algorithms tested, varying between 0.2575 and 0.2966. Predictive performance improves in model B when qualitative factors are introduced, with RMSE values ranging between 0.2441 and 0.2782.

This discovery validates the inclusion of art museums’ focus on SVCI is beneficial in predictive models. Model C with the inclusion of VED as a new predictor achieved the lowest RMSE values among all models (i.e., 0.1694 to 0.2483). It indicates that museums with greater predictive capacity regarding visitor numbers tend to adopt VED related activities. Additionally, model D with EOE presents an improvement in predictive accuracy versus Model A, with RMSE values in the range of 0.1988–0.2548. But its effect seems rather less powerful than VED influencing aspects. Model E, which incorporates ACES, showed slight enhancements compared to model A, with RMSE ranging from 0.1892 to 0.2683.

Although museums who focus on education, artistic programming, and sustainability initiatives help funnel interest, their direct correlation to attendance is more fragile than visitor centered methods. In comparing the deep learning algorithms, transformer models performed best out of all models and achieved the best predictive performance with the lowest RMSE (0.1494) in Model C, strongly suggesting that attention-based architectures such as the transformer model are well-suited to parsing museum-related textual data and capturing the underlying, complex patterns in visitor behavior. The predictive capability of LSTM, with the RMSE values ranging from 0.1694 for Model C to 0.5226 for Model A, further strengthens sequence-based model advantage for predicting visitors. CNN and GAN also demonstrated strong performance with RMSE values of 0.1721 and 0.1879 respectively in Model C, as these models effectively combine both structured and unstructured data to improve the accuracy of predictions. Whereas GRU, RNN, autoencoder, and DQN show moderate performance. Although GRU and RNN can learn the sequence of data, their RMSE are a little greater than those of transformer and LSTM.

When dealing with data that shows exponential growth or a wide range, MSLE (see Table 6) could be useful because it penalizes underestimation worse than overestimation. The MSLE results of model A only with structured numerical data showed the highest MSLE values with a range of 0.2320 to 0.2791. It shows that structured data might not be enough to predict because the structured data may not reflect complex visitor behavior. Model B demonstrated better performance between 0.2154 and 0.2585 for MSLE, implying that the predictive performance could be improved since it takes SVCI into account. Within all models, model C, which included VED, presented the lowest MSLE with values between 0.1446 and 0.2274. The result highlights again the importance of VED related factors as predictors of visitor number. Model D with EOE, on the other hand, the results showed improved performance over model A with MSLE between 0.1784 and 0.2359. Likewise, model E with ACES displayed MSLE values ranging from 0.1650 to 0.2432, which again suggests mild improvement.

Among deep learning algorithms, transformer and LSTM reported the lowest MSLE values again, where transformer reached 0.1446 and LSTM achieved 0.1623 both in Model C. These results demonstrate that transformer-based architectural models could indeed be the most powerful in dealing with qualitative data from the museum context, characterized by richer textual information and complex interdependencies. MSLE performance of CNN reached a minimum of 0.1799 and GAN showed a minimum of 0.1889, suggesting that learning complex patterns in the data was achieved by these algorithms. On the other hand, GRU, RNN, autoencoder, and DQN show relatively moderate performance with slightly higher MSLE values, indicating that they are able to capture sequential dependencies but are less capable than transformer-based architectures in modeling visitor predictions.

Table 7 shows the MAPE values obtained for the models predicting the number of visitors to art museums. In regression analysis, MAPE is commonly used as the evaluation metric to quantify the accuracy of the predictions by calculating the average percentage difference between the actual and predicted values. The lower the MAPE, the better the model performs as it minimizes the error between predicted and actual data. Model A was observed to have the highest MAPE values between 0.2489 and 0.2864, indicating that reliance on structured data alone can lead to suboptimal model performance. Model B with SVCI had higher goodness of fit with MAPE values from 0.2154 to 0.2665. This improvement indicates that incorporating SVCI could be beneficial for improving the predictive performance of the model. Incorporating VED (i.e., model C) provided the best predictive performance of MAPE values within a range of 0.1758 to 0.2380. This finding indicates that VED could be the most important predictor of museum visitor number prediction. Taking into account EOE, model D performs better than model A while performing marginally worse than model C, with MAPE spanning from 0.1808 to 0.2447.

This suggests that despite EOE factors contributing to visitor-related predictions, VED would be a stronger influence. Model E, which included the ACES, also outperformed Model A with MAPE values in the range of 0.1764 and 0.2581 but showed worse performance than model C. As the results showed, the best MAPE values were examined by transformer and LSTM with the best performance across all tested models. Transformer results in model C achieved the lowest MAPE value of 0.1758 and LSTM has similarly performed with MAPE value of 0.1961 also in model C. Since most derived features have come from a textual factor (i.e., VED), these findings suggest that an attention-based architecture such as transformer works well. MAPE results of CNN and GAN were also good with 0.1892 for CNN in model C and 0.1946 for GAN in model C, suggesting that these models are well at detecting close relationships between signals in structured and unstructured formats. On the other hand, RNN, autoencoder, GRU, and DQN show average performance with greater MAPE than transformer and LSTM. Although sequential models like RNN and GRU effectively account for temporal dependencies, they seem to be less powerful than transformer-based architectures in predicting museum visitor numbers.

Specifically, 95% confidence intervals were computed for all performance metrics (R2, RMSE, MSLE, and MAPE) across repeated experimental runs (n = 30). In addition, paired statistical significance tests (paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests) confirmed that the superior performance of the Transformer over LSTM, GRU, and other architectures was statistically significant (p < 0.05).

Discussion

General findings

In this study, we showed that analyzing unstructured textual data and deep learning-based approaches provide key advantages over classical methods in improving the performance of art museum visitor prediction models. First, we have used textual data combined with BSC. Aligning predictive models with the BSC framework improves both visitor prediction interpretability and visitor prediction strategic relevance. This study provides categories of four museum management strategies: social values and cultural impact (SVCI), visitor experience and tourism development (VED), exhibition and operational efficiency (EOE), and art, cultural education, and sustainable growth (ACES) and sets the stage for how specific strategies impact attendance. This means that predictive models benefit from the inclusion of analyzing unstructured textual data transformed into BSC-based strategic perspectives in terms of their predictive power, as well as providing useful information to the museum administration. The VED domain, which resonates with the customer-focused perspective of BSC, is the most influential factor of visitors, indicating that museums should adopt more visitor-oriented measures to increase visitor numbers. Second, traditional models that used structured numerical data (i.e., model A) presented weaker performance not only in R-squared values but also in terms of MSE, RMSE, MSLE, and MAPE. Leveraging unstructured textual data facilitates the extraction of valuable qualitative insights. The results show that predictive models that include textual data always outperform a baseline model that only have structured variables. To be more precise, the model C with VED turned achieved the best R-squared values and the lowest values of MSE, RMSE, MSLE, and MAPE compared to the baseline model A and other deep learning models. The results indicated that information contained in textual descriptions of museum strategies, when combined with deep learning-based AI methods, represents a valuable contextual aspect of the problem and helps improve prediction performance.

The roles of qualitative textual data and deep learning in predicting visitor numbers

The findings from the results provide strong evidence that qualitative textual data can make a great difference when it comes to predicting museum visitors. Most conventional visitor prediction models only pay attention to measurable variables, such as museum size and exhibition area, while taking no account of the effect of strategic narratives. However, textual data of museums emphasizing the visitor experience, cultural education, and operational efficiency emerge as strong predictors of visitor numbers. Among the various introduced textual variables in the models, visitor experience and tourism development (i.e., VED) was identified as the most influential factor, significantly increasing the prediction power of all deep learning models. The observation of this point highlights the role of strategic communication to attract visitors; those museums that consistently emphasize and advertise their focus on immersive exhibitions, visitor engagement, and partnerships with tourism are more likely to attract larger numbers of visitors. Also, although SVCI, EOE, and ACES help improve forecasts, they had a weaker effect than VED. These insights indicate that visitors are less influenced by targets for sustainability strategy of museums in the long term or improved operational efficiencies internally, as much as actual experience driven initiatives. Museums could connect this insight through reprioritizing their communication syntax in favor of experience-oriented narratives.

In terms of deep learning approaches, the comparison of deep learning-based models emphasizes the variation of predictive capabilities. Transformer model consistently surpasses predictive performance followed with LSTM. These results articulate the advantages of transformer-based architecture for complex sequential data and long-term dependencies handling, which make them particularly suited for integrated analysis of structured and unstructured museum data. CNN and GAN also presented good performance, since they are powerful tools for feature extraction to improve prediction performance. Conversely, RNN and GRU performance was mediocre, having lower R-squared value and larger MSE, than transformer and LSTM. This indicates that utilizing recurrent networks in extracting trends from sequential data could be achieved but processing and abstracting unstructured text can be more challenging using recurrent models. This study also proved that prediction models utilizing deep learning algorithms that are capable of dealing with the structured data on quantitative price-influencing attributes as well as the qualitative data contained in natural language text. The transformer-based models should be chosen over other models for museums attempting to improve features of ML based prediction algorithms.

The empirical results provide clear evidence of how deep learning algorithms enhance the predictive accuracy of visitor number estimation in art museums. Unlike conventional statistical models, which rely solely on structured numerical data and assume linear relationships, deep learning architectures are capable of simultaneously processing heterogeneous inputs—structured operational variables and unstructured textual data—and capturing complex, nonlinear interactions between them. This multi-modal learning capability allows the models to identify latent patterns that are not explicitly observable in either data type alone.

In particular, the Transformer architecture demonstrated superior performance due to its self-attention mechanism, which effectively models long-range dependencies and contextual relationships within the textual descriptions of museum strategies. By attending to the most semantically relevant words and phrases, the Transformer was able to extract nuanced thematic signals (e.g., emphasis on visitor experience or operational efficiency) and integrate them with numerical features to produce more accurate predictions. Similarly, the Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model showed strong performance by preserving sequential dependencies in the text, enabling the detection of recurring thematic patterns linked to higher visitor attendance.

Furthermore, algorithms such as CNN and GAN contributed to prediction enhancement by efficiently extracting high-level features from both textual and numerical inputs, thereby capturing subtle correlations between strategic orientations and attendance outcomes. The comparative results confirm that deep learning models outperform traditional approaches because they learn hierarchical feature representations, automatically identify interactions between qualitative and quantitative variables, and adapt to complex patterns in large, multi-dimensional datasets. These capabilities collectively explain why the inclusion of deep learning algorithms in this study resulted in substantial gains in R2 values and consistent reductions in prediction error metrics across all models tested.

These implications could serve as an important contribution toward the broader goal of deep learning-driven prediction models and alleviating the shortcomings of classical numerical prediction techniques. It improves prediction performance and offers crucial insights into visitor behaviors by integrating the unstructured text data. However, they are also designed to help showcase how the transformer model is the best approach for deep learning-based prediction systems, making it an attractive solution for museums adopting cutting-edge prediction techniques. Furthermore, the study employs analyzing unstructured textual data to quantitatively assess strategic elements of museum management, including visitor experience, social values, operational efficiency, and cultural sustainability. Moreover, using the BSC framework to analyze museum strategies allows for a more systematic categorization of predictive models with respective performance and its impact on visitor numbers. This methodical approach allows museums to use data to make decisions and effectively direct resources toward increasing visitor engagement. These findings provide practical information for museum administrators to help in optimizing the museum process experience, planning exhibitions and managing all key resources effectively.

Alignment of empirical results with research questions

To ensure a direct correspondence between the research questions and the empirical results, the findings are here interpreted sequentially according to each research question.

For RQ1, the empirical analysis demonstrated that deep learning–based algorithms achieved substantial improvements in predictive performance compared to conventional approaches that rely exclusively on structured numerical data. Notably, the Transformer and LSTM architectures exhibited superior predictive capabilities, achieving the highest R2 values (up to 0.7684) and the lowest error values across all evaluation metrics, including MSE, RMSE, MSLE, and MAPE. These results indicate that advanced deep learning architectures are more effective in modeling the complex, nonlinear relationships between diverse input variables, thereby enabling a more accurate estimation of visitor numbers.

Regarding RQ2, the integration of qualitative textual data derived from text mining significantly enhanced the explanatory power of the predictive models. Among the four strategic dimensions identified through the adapted Balanced Scorecard framework, Visitor Experience and Tourism Development (VED) emerged as the most influential factor in increasing prediction accuracy. This finding suggests that museums emphasizing interactive exhibitions, personalized visitor services, and collaborations with the tourism sector are better positioned to attract and retain higher numbers of visitors. Furthermore, the results imply that visitor-oriented strategies yield stronger predictive contributions than other strategic orientations, underlining the importance of customer experience in contemporary museum management.

In relation to RQ3, the comparative analysis of eight deep learning architectures revealed distinct differences in predictive performance. The Transformer model consistently outperformed all other architectures, followed closely by LSTM, both of which are well-suited for capturing sequential dependencies and contextual meaning in textual data. CNN and GAN also achieved relatively strong results, indicating their capability in extracting features from combined structured and unstructured inputs. In contrast, RNN, GRU, Autoencoder, and DQN displayed moderate predictive capabilities, suggesting that these models may be less effective in leveraging the semantic richness of textual museum data.

For RQ4, the incorporation of the adapted Balanced Scorecard framework into the predictive modeling process was found to enhance both interpretability and strategic relevance. By classifying museum strategies into the four dimensions of SVCI, VED, EOE, and ACES, the framework allowed for a systematic examination of how each strategic orientation relates to visitor attendance. This structured approach provided clear evidence that certain dimensions—particularly VED—are more strongly associated with higher visitor numbers, thus offering actionable guidance for museum administrators in prioritizing strategic initiatives. The alignment of qualitative themes with quantitative outcomes further strengthens the decision-making utility of the model.

Finally, addressing RQ5, the findings confirm that combining unstructured textual data with structured numerical indicators results in a more comprehensive representation of the factors influencing visitor behavior. This integrative approach enables museums to gain deeper insights into both operational efficiency and audience engagement patterns. The study highlights that such a dual-data strategy can support more targeted resource allocation, improve the design and delivery of visitor experiences, and enhance the long-term sustainability of museum operations. Ultimately, the synergy between qualitative and quantitative data contributes to bridging the gap between predictive accuracy and strategic planning in the cultural sector.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations that open promising pathways for future research. First, the textual data analyzed were restricted to official museum website descriptions and directors’ statements. While these sources capture institutional perspectives, they may not fully reflect visitor experiences, expectations, or perceptions. To enhance both novelty and real-world relevance, future research should incorporate more dynamic, user-generated content such as online visitor reviews, social media discussions, and Google ratings. Integrating these additional textual sources would enrich the predictive framework by capturing multiple perspectives, particularly the voice of visitors, thereby strengthening the explanatory power of the models.

Second, the dataset employed in this study is largely based on Korean museums, which constrains the generalizability of the findings and their applicability to international contexts. Although the Korean case provides valuable insights, extending the dataset to include museums from diverse countries and cultural backgrounds would improve external validity and allow comparative analyses across different cultural and institutional settings. Such cross-cultural extensions would also enable researchers to examine how contextual factors—such as governance models, funding structures, and cultural policy environments—influence visitor behavior and prediction outcomes.

Third, as this study focused primarily on benchmarking deep learning-driven prediction models, further research should investigate their application in real-world museum operations. Testing these models in practice would help assess their feasibility, implementation costs, and organizational impact, thereby translating predictive insights into managerial action.

Finally, enhancing the interpretability of predictive models represents another important avenue for future research. Techniques such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), feature importance analysis, and attention weight visualization could provide deeper insights into the drivers of model predictions, clarifying how qualitative and quantitative attributes interact in shaping visitor outcomes. Incorporating such methods would not only improve theoretical understanding but also generate practical guidance for museum administrators by identifying the most influential strategic and operational factors.

Overall, this research provides empirical evidence of the effectiveness of deep learning in forecasting museum visitor numbers, demonstrating substantial performance improvements compared to traditional approaches. By advancing predictive accuracy, highlighting the need for explainability, and setting an agenda for broader cross-cultural generalization, this study offers both methodological contributions to cultural analytics and actionable insights for data-driven decision-making in museum management.

Conclusion

This study set out to advance the prediction of museum visitor numbers by integrating unstructured textual narratives with structured operational data in a deep learning framework. Whereas prior approaches largely relied on quantitative museum statistics, our research demonstrates that textual sources—when systematically encoded through Natural Language Processing (NLP) and a Balanced Scorecard (BSC)–based thematic framework—offer rich and complementary information that improves predictive accuracy. The adapted BSC, tailored to the cultural and museum context, enabled the operationalization of four key strategic orientations: Strategic Visitor-Centric Initiatives (SVCI), Visitor Education and Development (VED), Exhibition and Operational Excellence (EOE), and Accessibility, Collaboration, and External Stakeholder engagement (ACES). By converting managerial narratives into measurable variables, this framework extends the scope of data inputs beyond conventional metrics and situates predictive modeling within a more holistic view of institutional strategy.

Empirical analyses across five model specifications and eight deep learning architectures revealed that incorporating qualitative strategic variables consistently enhanced predictive accuracy compared with the quantitative-only baseline model. Among the strategic orientations, VED exerted the strongest and most robust effect, highlighting the pivotal role of visitor learning opportunities, tourism linkages, and educational initiatives in shaping museum attendance. Architecturally, the Transformer model achieved the highest performance, delivering statistically significant improvements over recurrent and convolutional alternatives, with narrow 95% confidence intervals attesting to the stability of results. LSTM models also demonstrated strong capacity for capturing sequential dependencies, whereas GRU and CNN produced moderate but still competitive results. In contrast, simpler architectures such as RNN, GAN, and Autoencoder showed relatively weaker predictive power. Collectively, these findings underscore that attention-based models and long-term memory mechanisms are particularly effective in handling heterogeneous cultural datasets that combine structured and unstructured features.

From a theoretical perspective, this study makes a dual contribution. First, it integrates an interpretive managerial framework (the BSC) into cultural analytics, demonstrating that strategic orientation is not merely a qualitative descriptor but a measurable factor that significantly influences institutional demand. Second, it extends the literature on multimodal data integration by illustrating how deep learning models can jointly leverage operational attributes and semantic narratives. Methodologically, the research contributes a transparent and reproducible pipeline: text preprocessing, BSC-based thematic encoding, rigorous train-validation-test splitting, hyperparameter optimization, and statistical validation through confidence intervals and significance testing. This pipeline not only enhances reproducibility but also provides a replicable template for researchers working across cultural domains.

The practical implications are equally noteworthy. For museum administrators, the results suggest that strategic emphasis on visitor engagement, educational programming, and community collaboration has measurable benefits for attracting audiences. By adopting predictive models grounded in these insights, decision-makers can better anticipate fluctuations in visitor numbers, optimize staffing and resource allocation, and design more responsive exhibitions and programs. Moreover, the demonstrated superiority of attention-based architectures highlights the value of deploying advanced AI tools within cultural management, enabling institutions to transform unstructured narratives into actionable foresight.

Nonetheless, the study is not without limitations. The dataset was limited to Korean museums and primarily drew from official website texts, which may not fully reflect visitor perceptions. Future research should incorporate more dynamic and diverse sources of qualitative data, such as social media discourse, online reviews, or user-generated content, to capture the visitor voice more directly. Additionally, expanding the dataset to include museums across diverse cultural and national contexts would improve the generalizability of findings and provide comparative insights. Further, while this study focused on model benchmarking and methodological rigor, applying these models in live operational contexts will be critical for understanding their practical feasibility, costs, and impact on organizational decision-making.

A promising direction for future research lies in integrating explainability techniques such as SHAP values, feature importance analysis, and attention weight visualization. These methods would provide more granular insights into how individual features—both quantitative and qualitative—drive predictions. Such analyses would bridge the gap between predictive accuracy and interpretability, allowing museum managers to identify which strategic or operational factors most strongly influence outcomes and to design interventions accordingly.

In conclusion, this study contributes to both theory and practice by demonstrating the power of combining managerial narratives with advanced AI techniques. It provides empirical evidence that deep learning architectures, particularly attention-based models, can achieve superior accuracy and robustness in forecasting museum visitation. It also advances methodological rigor by introducing reproducible pipelines and statistical validation, and it lays out practical pathways for museums to adopt data-driven strategies. By uniting an adapted BSC framework with state-of-the-art deep learning, the research establishes a robust, interpretable, and actionable approach to cultural analytics, offering insights that extend beyond the museum sector to broader domains where organizational strategy and visitor or user behavior intersect.
