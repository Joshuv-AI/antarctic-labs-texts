A Prioritized Database of Substances of Environmental Concern Used in Marine Coatings
Authors: Gavyn C. Lastoria, Yuming Zhao, Karl J. Jobst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Degradation of marine coatings over time is a continuous source of ocean pollution, resulting in the release of paint particles that can leach biocides and other chemical additives into the marine environment. While the toxicity of some biocides is known, the identities and environmental impacts of most chemical substances used in marine coatings remains a major knowledge gap. Marine coatings are highly complex, containing many chemical additive types, including adhesion promoters, antifouling agents, catalysts, cross-linking agents, and many others. The present study reports on a prioritized database of chemical substances associated with marine coatings, identifying 3075 chemical substances, 1485 of which were classified as substances of potential environmental concern through hazard assessment and environmental fate modeling. Additionally, 59 chemical substances were recommended as prioritized substances for environmental monitoring, including 33 fluorinated substances, 12 organosilicon substances, and 6 chlorinated organics. Temporal analysis revealed an increase in the diversity of chemical additives employed in coating formulations, including perfluorinated and organosilicon substances as the basis of fouling-release compositions. This study lays a foundation for better understanding the complexity of marine coatings and highlights a pressing need for elucidating and classifying a broader range of marine coating additives that can adversely impact the environment.

Introduction

Marine coatings used on ships serve multiple purposes, ranging from corrosion protection, resistance to harsh sea conditions and biofouling, to increasing fuel efficiency and reducing emissions. Coatings share many similarities with plastics and are often based on epoxy resin, polyurethane, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) or acrylic polymers. , Different polymers confer distinct structural and mechanical properties on the coating. Epoxy resins provide effective protection against corrosion, while PDMS polymers create a slippery hydrophobic surface that prevents biofouling and increases fuel efficiency. In addition to the polymeric component, coating formulations incorporate numerous chemical additives, such as biocides, adhesion promoters, corrosion inhibitors, hardeners, pigments, slip aids, and plasticizers, to achieve desired performance characteristics. During their service lifecycles, marine coatings undergo gradual surface erosion and structural degradation, releasing paint particles and leaching chemical additives into the marine environment. While the leaching of some biocidal compounds, such as tributyltin oxide (TBT-O) and their environmental impacts has been well documented, − many chemical additives remain poorly characterized, with significant knowledge gaps persisting about their environmental fate.

Biocides, commonly used to prevent attachment of micro- and macroorganisms to the ship hull, are among the few marine coating additives with well-documented environmental impacts, − though regulatory measures have been applied to only a handful. In 2001, members of the International Maritime Organization (IMO) agreed to restrict the use of TBT substances, which came into effect in 2008. In 2023, the use of Irgarol 1051, an organic biocide, was banned by the IMO, making it only the second biocide regulated to date by the IMO. As the application of toxic TBT came to an end, the use of copper biocides, particularly copper oxide and copper thiocyanate, became increasingly popular in marine coatings. , To increase effectiveness, additional booster biocides were often applied in copper-based coatings, such as Chlorothalonil, Diuron, Irgarol 1051, and Tralopyril. , Though not internationally restricted, copper-based biocides and many organic booster biocides are toxic to nontarget organisms, ,, leading to restrictions in local jurisdictions such as the restriction of Diuron in the UK, The Netherlands, and Denmark. To address environmental concerns, fouling-release coatings are promoted as low-toxicity alternatives as they lack biocidal additives, and instead are comprised of silicone nonstick polymers, such as PDMS elastomers, , or fluoropolymers, such as polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE). Perfluorinated substances are known to be highly persistent in the marine environment , and can bioaccumulate in aquatic organisms, − leading to biomagnification. Silicone-based polymers persist in the marine environment, particularly in sediment, though do not tend to bioaccumulate. Compared to (micro)­plastics resulting from land-based sources, paint particles released by marine coatings are complex, containing many more chemical additives that are potentially harmful to the environment. Through a polymer fingerprinting approach, a study of microplastics in the German Bight attributed a significant amount of the observed microplastics to paint particles released from ship coatings. Accurate profiling of chemical additives in marine coatings is thus a critical step toward comprehensive evaluation of the environmental impacts of marine coatings.

In this work, the PlasticMAP methodology of Wiesinger et al. was adapted to generate a database of more than 3000 chemical substances associated with marine coatings. The database contains detailed information on chemical composition, regulatory listings, production volumes, additive type, hazard classifications and environmental fate predictions. In total, 1485 substances of potential environmental concern (SPEC) and 564 substances of potential health concern (SPHC) are identified. To guide environmental risk assessments and monitoring studies, 59 substances of the highest risk (based on environmental fate modeling) are identified as prioritized substances for monitoring (PSM). Through creation of this database, this work has emphasized the fate assessment of both biocidal and nonbiocidal additives in marine coatings, working toward filling critical knowledge gaps about the chemical diversity and toxicological significance of marine coating additives.

Methods

Identification of Relevant Sources

References were obtained through a literature search performed in SciFinder using the keyword “Marine coatings”. The search retrieved 1087 publications, including research articles, review articles, and patents. Among them, 247 references were excluded due to the absence of extractable information (i.e., CAS numbers were not listed). Manual review further eliminated 211 references deemed irrelevant. The final curated data set contained 628 relevant sources (380 journal articles/reviews, 248 patents).

Obtaining Chemical Information for Relevant Substances

Chemical identifiers, regulation status, − production volumes, − hazard information , and environmental fate predictions were obtained when available.

SMILES, InChi, InChiKey and Monoisotopic Masses were obtained in September 2024 through a batch search of CAS numbers using the CompTox Chemical Database. If substances were not listed in CompTox, the PubChemPy program in Python/3.12.6 was used to obtain additional SMILES notations, while InChi and InChiKey entries were obtained using the OpenBabel program. The chemical composition data was obtained from interpreting SMILES notations (see Table S1 in Supporting Information S1). For polymeric entries in the database, the SMILES notations for the monomer units were listed. As the properties of polymers likely differ significantly from that of the monomer unit, these were not used for environmental fate predictions. The polymeric entries in the database are listed in Sheet S1B of Supporting Information S2. A final extraction was performed in February 2026 prior to publication to obtain any additional SMILES, InChi, InChiKey and Monoisotopic Masses which were unavailable during the initial data extraction. These values are listed in blue font in Sheet S1 of SI2.

Presence on regulatory lists such as the Domestic Substances List (DSL), Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA) and Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals (REACH) was obtained from SciFinder by filtering “Regulatory Data by List”.

Production volumes were obtained from the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) Existing Chemicals Database, the US EPA Chemical Data Reporting (US EPA CDR) database, and the REACH Registered Substances List. Substances were marked as high production volume chemicals (HPVCs) if they were listed in OECD, or listed with a production of at least 1,000,000 lb/yr in the US EPA CDR, or of at least 1000 tonnes in the REACH database. Due to differences in reporting of production volume ranges between the EPA and REACH databases, the criteria for identifying HPVCs differed between the two databases. Since OECD lists HPVCs but does not report specific production volumes, only a binary classification (yes “Y” or no “N”) is reported.

Environmental parameters including partition coefficients, bioaccumulation data and persistence predictions were obtained from EPI Suite (version 4.11), using neutral SMILES notations as inputs. It is important to note that this approach may not accurately reflect the behavior of ionizable species. Inorganics, organometallics, and polymer mixtures fall outside of the predictive capabilities of EPI Suite and therefore results for these substances were excluded (further discussion in Section ). LogKow values were obtained using the ECOSAR package (version 1.11). Bioconcentration factors (BCF) and bioaccumulation factors (BAF) were obtained using the BCFBAF package (version 3.01). Substances with either a calculated BCF or BAF of 2000–4999 L/kg or 5000 L/kg or greater were listed as “Bioaccumulative (B)” or “Very Bioaccumulative (vB)”, respectively. BAF includes diet as a means of accumulation, which predominates when logKow is greater than 4.5. The larger of the two prediction outputs (BCF or BAF) was used to assign B or vB status. Persistence predictions were obtained from the BIOWIN package (version 4.10) from models representing aerobic aqueous conditions (models 3–6) as well as the LEV3EPI package. Substances were labeled as “Persistent (P)” if the predicted water, soil or sediment half-life from LEV3EPI was 60 days or greater, in accordance with USA EPA criteria, or if BIOWIN models 3 and 4 predicted the degradation time as “Months”, or if listed as “Not readily biodegradable” by BIOWIN models 5 and 6. Substances were labeled as “Very Persistent (vP)” if the water, soil or sediment half-life predicted by LEV3EPI was 180 days or greater, or if predicted as “Refractory” by BIOWIN models 3 and 4. In the case of disagreement between prediction models (i.e., one model predicts “persistent”, while another model predicts “very persistent”), the more severe prediction was used to assign P/vP status. BIOWIN prediction models provide numerical outputs, which were converted to time scales (on the order of hours, days, weeks etc.), and biodegradability potential according to Table S2.

Globally harmonized system (GHS) hazard classifications were obtained from SciFinder. GHS hazard codes were ranked and assigned a value of 0.5 (less hazardous) or 1 (more hazardous); see Table S3 for hazard conversions. Endocrine disruptor hazards were obtained from the REACH Endocrine Disruptor Assessment.

Extracting Usage Information and Additive Types Using Generative AI

To obtain chemical usage and additive type information from compiled references, Generative AI was used as a tool to streamline the extraction of information. To perform the extraction, Perplexity AI equipped with the GPT-4o model developed by OpenAI (accessed October-November 2024) was used. 292 journal articles and reviews were downloaded for analysis, the remaining 88 were inaccessible. To optimize performance, a step-by-step prompting process was applied (details are provided in Supporting Information S1). For extractions, two files were provided–one of the 292 references, and the 2011 Additives Handbook by Koleske et al. The prompt outlined a four-step process: (1) Read the reference provided (with the file name specified in the prompt) and list every chemical substance in the reference. (2) For each chemical substance, extract a quote describing usage/function in marine coatings. AI was instructed to extract direct quotations without paraphrasing or searching for outside information. (3) Read the Additives Handbook, assigning each chemical substance an additive type by matching of chemical usage information against the additive type descriptions in the Additives Handbook. AI was instructed to not search outside the designated sources and to assign only one additive type for each substance, or to list the additive type as “Unknown” if a clear match could not be found. (4) Obtain CAS numbers for each extracted chemical substance. This allowed for transposition of extracted information into the marine coating database. The above procedure was repeated for all 292 references. To ensure the accuracy of the information reported by Generative AI, all extracted information was human verified. Chemical usage quotes were verified through manual cross-checking of direct quotations against original reference sources. Incorrect or paraphrased quotes were reported as incorrect and disregarded (c. 26%, Figures S1 and S2). Assigned additive types were validated by confirming their presence in the Additives Handbook; if absent, the additive type was reported as incorrect and disregarded. False negatives were not further investigated, as such, we note that chemical usage and additive type information based on this methodology is not comprehensive of all substances present in the database. The database may comprise additional chemical additive types not identified in this work. All CAS registry numbers were validated through a search in SciFinder to confirm their accuracy and chemical identity. Incorrect CAS numbers were reported as incorrect, after which human verified CAS numbers were used for data transposition. Results of the AI analysis are discussed further in Section S1.3 of SI1 and Sheet S7 of SI2. The AI implementation in this methodology has been comprehensively documented to ensure full compliance with the guidelines for the application of AI technology in research set out by ACS ES&T Water.

Identifying Substances of Concern & Prioritized Substances for Monitoring

SPHC and SPEC substances were identified by reviewing hazard information, environmental fate predictions and production volumes.

SPHC substances were characterized by one or more of the following hazard classifications: carcinogen (C), mutagen (M), reproductive toxicity (RT), specific target organ toxicant upon repeated exposure (STOT-RE), or endocrine disruptor (ED). SPEC substances met one or more of the following criteria: (i) possessed any GHS aquatic toxicity hazard code, or (ii) was predicted to persist and/or bioaccumulate by environmental fate modeling. If a substance did not meet these hazard criteria (Table ), the level of concern was assigned as “low”. If no hazard information was available, the level of concern was assigned as “unknown”. SPHCs satisfying the hazard criteria and listed as a HPVC were assigned as “high” concern, whereas hazardous chemicals produced in low volume (i.e., non-HPVCs) were assigned “medium” level of concern. The SPEC and SPHC lists are provided on Sheets S2 and S3 of SI2, respectively. We note that while environmental fate modeling offers a useful initial screening tool for compilation of the database, experimental studies are required to confirm persistence and bioaccumulative potential.

Prioritized substances for monitoring (PSM, n = 59) were identified as HPVCs classified as “P or vP” and “B or vB”, as well as non-HPVCs classified as “vB” and “vP” with a predicted half-life in water of at least 180 days. The decision to prioritize only vB/vP non-HPVCs was one of practicality because relaxing these criteria to include B/P non-HPVCs lengthened the PSM list considerably (n = 142), though this does not indicate that non-HPVCs are of lesser importance. However, we note that using aquatic toxicity as a criterion reduces the number of non-HPVCs from 39 to 7 substances, eliminating emerging contaminants that are not yet well characterized. A final manual review of the PSM list revealed 7 compounds (e.g., 4 silanes, 2 isocyanates and a sulfonyl fluoride) which are likely to decompose upon reaction with (sea)­water. These substances have been flagged in Sheet S4 in SI2.

Results and Discussion

Chemical Substances Used in Marine Coatings: Composition, Hazards, Production, and Usage

In total, 3075 chemical substances were identified from 628 references. SMILES structures were obtained for 2040 substances (66.3%), which were used to group substances by composition (see Figure ). SMILES were unavailable for 1035 substances, mostly polymers and inorganic materials, and therefore were not included in chemical composition groupings. In the absence of SMILES structures (used as input for environmental fate computations) and GHS hazard codes (n = 992), no information was available to guide SPHC/SPEC assignment, therefore these substances were reported as unknown risk based on this methodology. This is not to say that these substances are environmentally irrelevant. For instance, polysiloxanes are known to persist in the environment, while many heavy metal inorganics are toxic to aquatic organisms. In total, 564 SPHCs and 1485 SPECs were identified (see Section 3.4.1). 582 chemical substances were identified as HPVCs in one or more databases. Coverage of chemical substances on the DSL, TSCA and REACH regulatory lists is reported at 28.7% (n = 881), 37.3% (n = 1148) and 43.0% (n = 1321), respectively. However, very few substances on these lists are regulated or restricted. In total, 11/811 (1.4%) substances on the DSL are regulated under the Canadian Prohibition of Certain Toxic Substances (PCTS). This includes six tributyltin substances, four PFAS, and one chlorinated alkane (1-chlorododecane). An additional 34 substances not on the DSL are regulated under the PCTS (17 tributyltins, 15 PFAS, and one chlorinated alkane). Only one substance listed on the TSCA is regulated (zinc chromate). A total of 36/1321 (2.7%) substances listed on REACH are subject to restriction according to Annex XVII, while 30/1321 (2.3%) are listed as Substances of Very High Concern. Two substances are subject to authorization according to Annex XIV. This information is listed in columns F-M on Sheet S1 of SI2.

In total, 46 unique chemical additive types were identified, covering 530 substances. Biocidal and Non-biocidal Antifouling Substances

Historically, biofouling was prevented using organotin substances, particularly TBT compounds, with triphenyltin (TPT) compounds used to a lesser extent. Organotins are highly toxic, disrupting energy production and signal transduction, and can impair reproductive function. Consequently, manufacturers reverted to copper-based biocides, which have been widely used for centuries. Copper oxide is the primary biocide of use, though copper thiocyanate is often used to provide a white pigmentation. Copper-based coatings are frequently coformulated with organic booster biocides to enhance efficacy against resistant algal species. Organic biocides, including Dichlofluanid, Diuron, Irgarol 1051, and Tralopyril, employ varied mechanisms of action. Irgarol 1051 causes oxidative damage and induces apoptosis, while Tralopyril reduces ATP synthesis through mitochondrial uncoupling. Due to its toxicity, Irgarol 1051 was restricted by the IMO in 2023, the first international coating restriction imposed since 2008. However, most organic biocides remain unregulated despite their known environmental risks.

In recent years, fouling-release coatings have emerged as promising nonbiocidal alternatives to traditional antifouling solutions. Fouling-release coatings are engineered with fluorinated or silicone-based polymers to hinder initial attachment of fouling organisms. Moreover, any accumulated fouling can be easily removed during transit by hydrodynamic forces. Fouling-release coatings also contain other additives to increase mechanical strength and promote adhesion of the coating to the hull, , among other functions, and are often “hybridized” with other polymers, such as polyurethane and polyethylene glycol to increase strength. , While fluoropolymers are often considered to be of low concern, coatings functionalized with perfluorinated slip aids may leach PFAS as the coating surface erodes over their service lifetimes. Many PFAS are highly bioaccumulative and have been detected in a wide range of aquatic organisms. − Despite this, current international regulations do not restrict the usage of PFAS in marine coatings. In this work, 41 perfluorinated substances were identified that share a perfluoroalkyl chain > C4, while 114 substances meet the broader PFAS definition outlined by the OECD in 2021; that is, a compound containing at least one fully fluorinated carbon atom. Usage of fluoropolymer and silicone fouling-release substances has increased since 2008 (see Figure a), and silicone-based polymers, particularly polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), represent the state-of-the-art in fouling-release technology. Silicone polymers persist in the aquatic environment, particularly in sediment, though demonstrate lower bioaccumulative potential.

Adhesion Promoters and Cross-Linking Agents

The application of adhesion promoters and cross-linkers has grown in parallel with the increasing usage of fouling-release substances (see Figure b). Traditional organotin formulations simply incorporate “free biocides” into the coating without the use of any adhesion promoters, which would leach from the matrix over time. Self-polishing copolymer paints were first developed in the 1970s, in which TBT formulated with methyl methacrylate enabled incorporation into the polymer matrix, which increased service life by controlling biocide leaching rates through gradual polymer erosion. Copper-based paints are typically formulated in the same manner. However, the low surface energy of fouling-release coatings necessitates the use of adhesion promoters, commonly tri- or tetra-hydrolyzable silanes, which bind through hydrolysis and condensation reactions. Trihydrolyzable silanes often contain an amino, methacrylate, or isocyanato group to bridge the polymer and the bound adhesion promoter. Cross-linking agents (often isocyanates) are added to enhance mechanical strength by reacting with hydroxyls and amines to form urethane or urea linkages, respectively. Cross-linking agents can also contain acrylate, carboxy, or vinyl functional groups.

Catalysts

The incorporation of catalytic additives in coatings has increased with usage of fouling-release substances (see Figure c). Dialkylated organotin compounds are commonly used in marine coating production, particularly as curing catalysts in fouling-release systems. Notably, these additives are exempt from IMO restrictions on organotin compounds. Organotin catalysts are typically used in 0.01–0.10% by weight of the final formulation, far lower than the organotin biocide usage range of 3–4% by weight. Dibutyltin catalysts are most commonly applied in fouling-release coatings coformulated with urethane, which catalyze the isocyanate–hydroxyl reaction. Other dialkylated tins, including dimethyltin (DMT) and dioctyltin (DOT) compounds, , also demonstrate effective curing properties. Catalytic efficiency usually increases with higher tin content, while alkyl chain length critically influences hydrophobicity and toxicity. Dibutyltin compounds are particularly toxic to aquatic species. , Although any residual catalyst is presumed to remain entrapped within the polymer matrix, surface degradation could result in leaching over time. For example, dibutyltin dilaurate, a commonly used catalyst, has been documented as a chemical additive leached into artificial seawater from antifouling paints along with TBT and monobutyltin (MBT) impurities.

Other Chemical Additives Identified in this Work

Other chemical additives identified include compounds designed to improve abrasion-resistance, corrosion inhibitors, hardeners, and plasticizers. Abrasion-resistance improvers are engineered to enhance coating durability through optimization of the strength, hardness, and thickness of coatings. These additives are particularly critical for ice-breaking vessel coatings, where extreme environmental conditions demand exceptional durability. These properties can be achieved with the use of silica, as identified in this work. Corrosion inhibitors are added in marine coatings to prevent rusting of the hull. Zinc oxide is widely used in commercial marine coatings, due to its dual functionality as both an effective antifouling agent and corrosion inhibitor. , Hardeners, also referred to as curing agents, are used in coatings to promote the formation of polymer networks by creating molecular bridges between polymer chains. As such, many hardeners are also considered cross-linking agents. Plasticizers increase the durability of the coating by modifying the polymer matrix to enhance flexibility and thus reduce brittleness and crack propagation under mechanical stress. Phthalates and phosphates increase flexibility, such as dibutyl phthalate and triphenyl phosphate.

Temporal Trends of the Marine Coating Industry

Initial toxicological studies of TBT in the aquatic environment were first reported in the early 1980s, predating its international regulatory action by more than two decades. Monitoring the trends of industry is essential for identifying emerging substances of concern in marine coating technologies. Since 2008, the marine coating industry has significantly increased its reliance on copper-based biocides and organic biocides, as well as fluorinated and silicone-based fouling-release substances (see Figure a). While copper-based biocides have undergone minimal chemical innovation despite decades of use, organic biocides exhibit substantially greater structural complexity and diversity. Organic booster biocides are commonly incorporated in copper-based antifouling coatings, with many compounds repurposed from agricultural applications. Despite their proven efficacy as antifoulants, organic biocides pose significant ecological risks owing to their toxicity to nontarget organisms. Moreover, degradation of organic biocides can produce substances of equal or even greater toxicity, eventually leading to the international ban on the use of Irgarol 1051. Indeed, both Irgarol 1051 and its primary degradation product, M1, are highly toxic , and persistent in water and sediment. , To date, no other organic biocides are facing international restrictions, despite extensive toxicological evidence demonstrating their high toxicity to nontarget marine organisms. ,,−

Similar to copper biocides, polysiloxane antifouling agents have experienced limited innovation and advances. Recently, some methacrylate or propenoate-functionalized silane derivatives have been developed to facilitate incorporation into polymeric matrices (see Figure S12 in SI1 for developmental timeline). In contrast, perfluorinated substances used in fluoropolymer coatings have evolved significantly. One of the early examples is polychlorotrifluoroethylene (PCTFE) wax, which was originally developed under U.S. Navy patents as a temporary protective coating to be used while the ship is docked for servicing. This system relies on hydrophobic interactions for hull adhesion, with performance enhanced through formulation with anionic surfactants, such as sodium lauroyl sarcosinate. Most perfluorinated substances produced during the 1990s contained hydroxyl groups, enabling hydrogen bonding interactions or copolymerization with urethane. More modern PFAS have methacrylate, vinyl, or halogenated head groups, which can undergo radical polymerization or atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) for incorporation into polymeric matrices. , Despite this, it is probable that eventual leaching of incorporated PFAS will occur as the coating surface is degraded over time. Moreover, new PFAS variants still emerge (see Figure ) despite the known environmental impacts of PFAS, and efforts to regulate them.

It is worth noting that the evaluation of antifouling agents alone provides incomplete assessment of marine coating toxicity, as these complex formulations typically contain multiple chemical additives. Comprehensive examination of all components is therefore critical to establish their complete environmental hazardous profile.

There are limitations to the temporal analysis. Chemical substances referenced in patents may not be used in commercial coating formulations. Moreover, patents do not provide information on any substances that have been developed as trade secrets. Only references in English were considered in this work, eliminating any substances referenced in non-English literature. Only patents were considered in this approach to prevent any misinterpretation of temporal data (for example, many organotin compounds are referenced in research articles post-2008, though in the context of their identification from marine coating pollution, not their continued use). Despite these limitations, the temporal analysis of disclosed substances is beneficial in that it provides a glimpse of the types of additives that could be used as coating technologies and environmental regulations evolve.

Substances of Concern Used in Marine Coatings

Substances of Potential Health Concern (SPHC) and Substances of Potential Environmental Concern (SPEC)

In total, 564/3075 SPHC and 1485/3075 SPEC substances were identified in this work. The SPHCs met one or more specified health hazard criteria (see Section ), and include 217 carcinogens (C), 142 mutagens (M), 299 reproductive toxicants (RT), 406 targeted organ toxicants upon repeated exposure (STOT-RE), and 17 endocrine disruptors (ER). Examples include copper oxide, a biocide (C, RT, STOT-RE), zinc oxide, a biocide and corrosion inhibitor (C, M, RT, STOT-RE), dibutyltin dilaurate, a catalyst (M, RT, STOT-RE), silica (C, STOT-RE), and many others (see Sheet S3 in SI2). Exposure to marine coatings can occur during manufacturing, application, servicing, and ablation. While Canadian workplace safety regulations mandate full disclosure of health-hazardous substances in manufactured products, environmental hazard reporting remains exempt from the current requirements. According to the SPEC list developed in this work, 701 substances were identified as toxic to the aquatic environment, and 1172 substances were classified as persistent, with 659 of them being very persistent. 143 substances were identified as bioaccumulative, among which 107 substances are very bioaccumulative. 274 substances lacked environmental fate predictions and were only characterized by aquatic toxicity GHS hazard codes. We note that environmental fate modeling serves as a useful screening tool to guide future monitoring and assessment, but does not confirm environmental risk. Overall, fluorinated and organosilicon substances are predicted to have the highest bioaccumulative potential (see Figure S14), though environmental fate results obtained for these classes should be interpreted with caution (further discussed in Section 3.3.3). Included are 39 perfluorinated substances, of which 22 were predicted to bioaccumulate. All perfluorinated substances were predicted to be very persistent in the environment (t1/2 ≥ 180 days). Polysiloxanes generally have a low bioaccumulative potential but show environmental persistence. Certain cyclic siloxanes may bioaccumulate in aquatic organisms, , and seven of such substances were identified in this work. Cyclic siloxanes, such as D4, D5, and D6, are classified by ECHA as persistent and bioaccumulative substances, prompting the recommendation to phase out the use of these substances. In total, there are 29 different chemical additive types in the SPEC list, including adhesion promoters, biocides, catalysts, plasticizers, slip aids, and others.

Prioritized Substances for Monitoring (PSM)

Among the 1485 SPEC, 59 chemical substances are prioritized for environmental monitoring. Included are 14 HPVCs, which are presented in Table . Only three HPVCs are listed on the REACH restriction list (Annex XVII), cyclic siloxanes D4, D5 and D6. 43/59 substances are classified as very persistent and very bioaccumulative with aqueous half-lives of 180 days or greater (see Sheet S4 of SI2). These include 33 fluorinated (20 per/-polyfluorinated) and six chlorinated substances, most of which were developed as antifouling agents. While many of the perfluorinated substances are not produced in high volume, their increasing appearance in patent disclosures provides a glimpse of the types of PFAS coming down the developmental pipeline. Examples include PF-37 and PF-40 (see Figure ), developed in 2020 and 2023, respectively that were designed for incorporation into polymer matrices. However, degradation of the coating surface may result in the release of PFAS monomers, or side-chain PFAS functionalized microplastics to the environment. If this occurs, environmental fate modeling suggests these substances may persist and bioaccumulate, however studies are required to determine release rates of PFAS from coated surfaces, as well as any products of (bio)­transformation of the (Figure ). Beyond fouling-release additives, our work identified a broad range of chemical additive types, including antiblocking agents, surface modifiers, and UV absorbers/stabilizers, which have not been investigated for potential adverse environmental impacts.

Limitations of Environmental Fate Modeling

While the SPEC and PSM lists aim to prioritize emerging contaminants and lay the groundwork for future environmental monitoring studies, it is also important to note the limitations of the models employed in the present study. EPI Suite is a quantitative structure–property relationship (QSPR) model, that is trained primarily on neutral, small organic molecules. Inorganic and organometallic substances, as well as highly halogenated species and organosilicons, tend to fall outside the scope of EPI Suite’s predictive abilities due to their absence in data training sets. To address this, any fate predictions obtained for inorganics, organometallics, and polymer mixtures were disregarded and therefore were only included in the SPEC list if characterized by GHS aquatic toxicity codes. As organometallic substances form the basis of many former and current marine coating formulations, those with no available aquatic toxicity codes were manually reviewed to prevent exclusion of potentially environmentally relevant substances. In total, 30 organotin and 6 organocopper substances were manually flagged for inclusion in the SPEC list (Sheet S2 in SI2), due to their structural similarity to related substances of known toxicity.

EPI Suite predictions for organosilicon and fluorinated substances were retained, though it is noted that degradation and bioaccumulation studies are required to confirm environmental risk suggested by fate modeling. Comparison of predicted logKow, logBCF, and logBAF with experimental values (see Figures S15 and S16 in SI1) reflects the challenges associated with modeling PFAS partitioning. Predicted and experimental values diverge significantly for common ionizable PFAS, such as PFOA (Figure S16), reflecting the paucity of experimental data available to train accurate models, and the refinement of such models remains an active area of research. Although more sophisticated computations, such density functional theory (DFT), can in principle provide more reliable estimates, such calculations are computationally intensive and impractical for large chemical inventories. Moreover, the PFAS on the PSM list are predominantly nonionic, neutral PFAS, such as the fluorotelomer alcohols, for which EPI Suite predictions show excellent agreement with experimental values , and predictions obtained from COSMOtherm (see Table S5 in SI2), though we note these results should be interpreted with caution. Li et al. showed that combining EPI Suite with DFT based methods for a large set of mixed halogenated n-alkanes significantly increased the number of fluorinated compounds classified as bioaccumulative. Therefore, we suggest that while EPI Suite provides a valuable screening tool, its predictions may underestimate the number of bioaccumulative PFAS.

While environmental fate predictions have served as a useful screening tool for compiling of the priority list, it should be emphasized that some of the additives (e.g., PFAS derived fouling release substances) are designed to form covalent bonds within the polymer matrix, meaning that the PFAS ultimately released during weathering may differ structurally from the precursor molecules used in the coating formulation. Consequently, the predicted environmental behavior of the precursor does not necessarily represent the behavior of the polymer-bound form or its transformation products. This highlights the need for dedicated studies on the leachability, degradation and (bio)­transformation of side-chain PFAS present in paint particles.

Recommendations for Protection of the Marine Environment

This study identifies numerous hazardous chemical additives used in marine coatings. The following recommendations are suggested to protect of marine ecosystems.

Elimination of PFAS in Fouling-Release Coatings

PFAS poses significant risks to the aquatic environment. − Perfluorooctanesulfonate (PFOS) was first widely detected in aquatic organisms over 20 years ago. Biomagnification of PFAS can result in human exposure through contaminated food sources, such as fish. Temporal analysis shows that PFAS usage in marine coatings may be increasing since 2008 despite growing awareness of their environmental risks. In 2022, the IMO issued a ban on the shipboard use of PFOS-containing aqueous film-forming foams (AFFF) due to their ecological hazards. However, there are no international regulations against the usage of PFAS in marine coating formulations. Concerningly, new PFAS compounds continue to be proposed for marine coating applications. The database presented in this work contains 41 PFAS (Sheet S6 of SI2). Seven are fluoropolymers, and include PTFE, PCTFE, ethylene-tetrafluoroethylene (ETFE), ethylene-chlorotrifluoroethylene (ECTFE), fluorinated ethylene propylene (FEP), poly­(vinylidene fluoride-co-hexafluoropropylene) (PVDF-HFP), and perfluoro­(methyl vinyl ether)-tetrafluoroethylene (PMVE-TFE). While fluoropolymers are often assumed to be of low concern, shorter chain homologues [e.g., waxes and oils of PCTFE (PF-1)] have the potential to bioaccumulate. An additional 28 PFAS are designed for incorporation into a nonfluorinated polymeric backbone, producing side-chain fluorinated polymers (SCFPs). As of 2025, both Canada and Europe are proposing legislation to ban PFAS as an entire class of compounds, , but analytical tools needed to support environmental monitoring and enforcement are in their infancy for SCFPs. Current Canadian legislation allows for the continued use of <C7 PFAS (e.g., PF-3, 7, 17, 20, 24, 26, 28–31), nH-PFAS, characterized by the replacement of one fluorine atom with a hydrogen (CnF2nH) (e.g., PF-32–34), and perfluoropolyethers (e.g., PF-25, 27). In the same vein, current European regulations against C9–C14 PFCAs and related substances (CnF2n+1–C, n = 8–13) are evaded by 15/41 PFAS in our database (PF-3, 7, 17, 20, 24–31, 32–34), which will decline to 9/41 (PF-3, 7, 20, 25, 27, 31, 32–34) when regulations on perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA) and related substances (C6F13C−) come into effect. No equivalent federal regulations exist in the United States, though individual states are increasingly enacting their own legislation. A major concern is that marine coatings formulated with new-generation SCFPs will leach PFAS into the marine environment as the coating surface degrades. Therefore, this study recommends swift action to evaluate the degradation and transformation of PFAS and SCFP used in marine coatings and incorporation of understudied PFAS, including SCFP in environmental monitoring programs. Furthermore, we recommend expanding international regulations to restrict coatings formulated with <C7, nH-PFAS or perfluoropolyethers that appear to evade current regulations. It is important to note that while environmental fate modeling has served as a valuable screening tool, modeling of PFAS is challenging (see Section 3.4.3) and therefore environmental fate modeling results remain somewhat uncertain. Studies are required to compliment environmental fate modeling performed in this work to confirm persistence and bioaccumulation potential, as well as to identify any degradation products.

Phase out Toxic Organic Biocides and Consider Natural Alternatives

To date, Irgarol 1051 remains the only organic biocide subject to international restriction, while numerous other organic biocides with demonstrated ecotoxicity continue to be used. For example, Diuron exhibits similar toxicity to Irgarol 1051 and persists in surface water. More alarmingly, Diuron undergoes environmental degradation to form 3,4-dichloroaniline, a product that not only exceeds the parent compound’s toxicity but shows confirmed genotoxic effects on many aquatic organisms. Other biocides like Dichlofluanid, are persistent and accumulate in marine sediment. This reflects the need to phase out organic biocides and replace them with safer alternatives. Natural antifouling agents have been studied for more than 20 years, most of which remain challenging to synthesize, limiting their practical application in marine coatings. However, exceptions exist, such as nicotinamide and 2,5,6-tribromo-1-methylgramine, which are easily synthesized and highly effective antifoulants. Notably, the latter compound exhibits up to 6-fold greater effectiveness than tributyltin oxide. Future research should prioritize the development of environmentally friendly, cost-effective natural antifoulants with minimal ecological impact, while maintaining competitive performance.

Performing Comprehensive Environmental Impact Studies

Current environmental impact studies are relatively scarce and remain disproportionally focused on the fate of (in)­organic biocides. ,,, While pollution from marine coatings has been demonstrated through the detection of paint particles and heavy metals, ,, the chemical complexity of marine coatings warrants comprehensive hazard assessments to identify the many chemical additives and their associated environmental impacts. We suggest that a prioritized list of chemical substances in marine coatings (biocidal and nonbiocidal) based on available environmental fate information (such as the PSM list presented in this work) can aid in achieving this objective. Most of the substances are amenable to standard GCMS or LCMS analysis, as recommended in Sheet S4 of SI2. An important class of high-risk substances identified in this work are side-chain PFAS, which can be detected using pyrolysis GCMS, as proposed in the pioneering study by Skedung et al. The present work reveals a diverse range of side-chain fluoropolymers that could potentially be integrated into marine coatings. By combining pyrolysis GCMS with nontargeted screening, it may be possible to distinguish side-chain PFAS formulations, characterize their degradation products, and provide a novel tool to regulators for monitoring and enforcement.

Conclusions

This study applies a prioritization scheme based on hazard assessment and environmental fate modeling for the purposes of identifying nonbiocidal and biocidal substances of health concern (n = 564) and environmental concern (n = 1485) used in marine coatings. Among these, 59 high-risk substances are prioritized for environmental monitoring due to their anticipated persistence and bioaccumulation potential. This database integrates critical parameters, such as production volumes and environmental fate predictions, offering a systematic framework for assessing the environmental impacts of additives in various marine coatings. Prioritization of chemical substances reveals that over 70% of substances of the highest concern are fluorinated or silicon-based, highlighting that “non-toxic” fouling-release technologies require further study to determine their true environmental impacts. Moving forward, studies are required to determine the leachability of new coating formulations. To compliment the priority list, we suggest future studies characterize the environmental fate of the prioritized substances through degradation and transformation studies. Of particular concern is the increasing popularity of fluoropolymer based fouling release coatings, underlining the need to develop new analytical methods (e.g., pyrolysis GCMS and nontargeted screening) that can identify side-chain fluoropolymers in marine coatings. It is worth highlighting that these fouling-release marine coatings are characterized by poor mechanical strength, and therefore require extensive use of adhesion promoters, cross-linking agents, catalysts, and other chemical additives. The presence of additional additives also increases the likelihood of additive leaching during the service life of these coatings. As such, this work underlines the urgent need to expand environmental monitoring studies by targeting multiple chemical additive types, including compounds integrated into polymer coatings and their degradation products.
