Extended Inflammatory Osteitis Pubis With Associated Myositis and Femoral Neuropathy in an Amateur Football Player: A Case Report
Authors: Sousan Kolahi, Mojtaba Varshochi, Sepideh Tahsini Tekantapeh, Mohammad Mahdi Tehrani Ghadim, Amirreza Jahanshahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 42‐year‐old male amateur football player presented with a 17‐day history of progressive groin pain, primarily on the right, radiating to the perineum, associated with limitation of hip motion. Because of proximal weakness and elevated inflammatory markers, polymyositis and an infectious process were initially suspected. Electromyography demonstrated a subacute incomplete axonal lesion of the right femoral nerve likely due to iliopsoas compression. Joint aspiration yielded purulent‐appearing fluid with 850 WBC/mm3 and 80% PMNs, although cultures remained negative. MRI demonstrated bone marrow edema at the pubic symphysis, fluid collections in the adductor and iliacus muscles, right iliopsoas bursitis, and mild hip effusion, supporting a diagnosis of osteitis pubis with associated inflammatory myositis and bursitis rather than primary infection or polymyositis. Empirical antibiotics and corticosteroid therapy resulted in clinical and laboratory improvement. This case highlights the diagnostic challenge of osteitis pubis presenting with femoral neuropathy and inflammatory findings that may mimic infectious or inflammatory myopathy.Keywords: femoral neuropathy, osteitis pubis, pubic symphysis, repetitive microtrauma to the symphysis

Introduction

Osteitis pubis (OP) is a rare, noninfectious inflammatory condition affecting the pubic symphysis, predominantly observed in athletes involved in sports requiring repetitive stress, such as football [1, 2, 3]. However, OP presenting with femoral neuropathy and marked inflammatory laboratory abnormalities is uncommon and may closely mimic infectious, inflammatory, or even neoplastic conditions, creating a significant diagnostic challenge. It commonly presents with chronic groin pain, tenderness over the pubic symphysis, and functional limitation; however, atypical presentations may include adjacent myositis, bursitis, or compressive neuropathy [4, 5]. The pathophysiology is thought to involve repetitive microtrauma to the symphysis, leading to inflammation, periarticular tissue involvement, and occasionally associated myositis or bursitis [3, 6, 7].

Multiple factors, including repetitive microtrauma and biomechanical stress, contribute to OP [5, 8, 9]. Coexisting myositis and bursitis can obscure the clinical picture and may mimic infectious or inflammatory disorders, complicating diagnosis and delaying management [6, 10]. Early recognition is critical to prevent chronicity and allow timely intervention [1, 11].

Diagnostic evaluation relies on clinical assessment supported by imaging modalities, particularly MRI, which is highly sensitive in detecting inflammation, edema, and involvement of adjacent muscles or bursae [2, 12, 13]. In atypical cases, electromyography and synovial fluid analysis may further aid in distinguishing OP from infection or other mimics [3, 14].

Case History/Examination

A 42‐year‐old male amateur football player was admitted with a 17‐day history of progressive right‐sided groin pain radiating to the perineum. The pain was continuous, gradually increased in intensity over the 17‐day period, and limited his ability to walk. During this symptomatic period, the patient also reported fever and diaphoresis. There was no history of urinary symptoms, diarrhea, or morning stiffness. On initial examination, both hips exhibited limited range of motion, with complete restriction on the right side due to pain. Other joints, sacroiliac joints, and the lumbar spine were normal. Proximal muscle weakness of the lower limbs prompted an initial consideration of polymyositis. Neurological examination suggested involvement of the femoral nerve, given proximal lower‐limb weakness and the subsequent electrophysiological findings.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Electromyography revealed a subacute incomplete axonal lesion of the right femoral nerve at the pelvis, supporting a compressive neuropathy likely secondary to iliopsoas involvement. Although there were no clear clinical signs of arthritis, joint aspiration was performed to exclude infection; 5 mL from the left hip and 9 mL from the right hip yielded thick synovial fluid. Initial treatment with indomethacin was insufficient. Because of persistent pain, elevated inflammatory markers, and purulent‐appearing joint fluid, empirical intravenous vancomycin and meropenem were administered for 15 days. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated CRP [122 mg/L], ESR [87 mm/h], ferritin [584 ng/mL], and WBC [15 × 103/μL]. Muscle enzymes (CK [43 U/L], aldolase [11.2 U/L]) and autoantibodies were unremarkable. In the differential diagnosis, inflammatory and neoplastic processes were considered. Infiltrative neoplastic lesions were deemed less likely because the lesions crossed the articular surface, involved the pubic symphysis, and lacked cortical destruction or periosteal reaction. MRI findings alone could not definitively distinguish infectious from noninfectious inflammatory disease. However, the absence of cortical destruction, periosteal reaction, or organized abscess, together with the synovial fluid analysis showing 850 WBC/mm3 with 80% neutrophils and negative cultures, argued against septic arthritis or aggressive osteomyelitis. The final differential diagnosis included osteitis pubis with associated infectious or inflammatory myositis and bursitis. After initial broad‐spectrum antibiotic coverage and persistent symptoms despite NSAID therapy, treatment was escalated to naproxen 1000 mg daily and prednisolone 25 mg for 1 month. This decision was based on the lack of a clear infectious source, negative cultures, and MRI findings suggestive of inflammatory osteitis pubis. The patient was advised rest and avoidance of sports activities, followed by a gradual return to activity. A physiotherapy program focusing on core stabilization and flexibility exercises was also recommended.

Conclusions and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Subsequent clinical improvement was noted with normalization of inflammatory markers and recovery of hip range of motion. At 3.5 months of follow‐up, the patient remained clinically well and had returned to his normal daily activities. Follow‐up pelvic MRI (Figure 3A–D) showed significant reduction in bone marrow edema and soft tissue inflammation, with resolution of the fluid collection around the symphysis pubis, consistent with the patient's clinical improvement. After steroid tapering, inflammatory markers such as CRP and ESR increased again at 3 months, although no recurrent groin pain was reported. Functionally, the patient had returned to his normal daily activities, with normalization of hip range of motion.

Discussion

Osteitis pubis is a rare inflammatory condition of the pubic symphysis, often associated with athletes who participate in sports involving repetitive stress and sudden changes in direction [1, 2]. The condition is characterized by groin pain, tenderness over the pubic symphysis, and functional limitations [3]. The pathogenesis of osteitis pubis is believed to involve repetitive microtrauma, inflammation, and stress injury to the pubic symphysis, leading to bone marrow edema and periarticular soft tissue changes [4, 5]. However, in our patient, the combination of bilateral groin pain, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated inflammatory markers created a diagnostic dilemma by mimicking polymyositis and possible infection. This atypical presentation underscores the case‐specific diagnostic challenge of osteitis pubis when it is complicated by extra‐articular inflammatory involvement.

The primary approach to managing OP is conservative, emphasizing activity modification, NSAIDs, and a structured rehabilitation program to restore strength and balance in the adductor and abdominal muscles [1, 15, 16]. Physical therapy protocols focusing on core stabilization, flexibility, and progressive loading have demonstrated efficacy in facilitating recovery and preventing recurrence [17, 18].

In cases refractory to standard conservative treatment, advanced interventions such as corticosteroid injections, shockwave therapy, or ozone therapy may be considered [12, 19]. Comprehensive management of coexisting myositis or bursitis is essential, as these conditions can prolong recovery and complicate rehabilitation [6, 10].

Early diagnosis and individualized rehabilitation programs are pivotal for athletes, allowing a safe return to play while minimizing the risk of recurrence [5, 14, 20]. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for OP in athletes presenting with groin pain, particularly when laboratory and electromyography findings are inconclusive [1, 3, 4]. Multimodal imaging and targeted therapy generally result in favorable outcomes [2, 7]. There is a four‐week interval between the onset of symptoms and the appearance of radiological findings.

Epidemiological studies in professional and amateur football players indicate that OP accounts for a significant proportion of chronic groin pain cases, highlighting the need for awareness among clinicians managing athletes [8, 13, 14, 15]. Risk assessment and early intervention, including correction of training loads and biomechanical imbalances, are associated with faster recovery and reduced recurrence rates [5, 8, 16, 18].

Ultimately, a multidisciplinary approach involving sports physicians, radiologists, and physiotherapists is recommended to optimize outcomes in athletes with OP and associated myositis or bursitis [6, 19]. Return to play in osteitis pubis should be guided not only by symptom resolution, normalization of inflammatory markers, and restoration of function [18, 19], but also by a structured rehabilitation program and activity modification. A gradual progression from rest to core strengthening, flexibility work, and sport‐specific loading is recommended to reduce the risk of recurrence. In the present case, the patient resumed normal daily activities after treatment and remained asymptomatic at follow‐up, suggesting that symptom‐guided rehabilitation was effective.

This case illustrates the overlap between mechanical stress‐related injury, inflammatory osteitis pubis, and infectious mimicry. The sports‐related microtrauma likely contributed to the underlying pubic symphysis pathology, while the elevated inflammatory markers and fluid collections raised concern for infection. At the same time, proximal weakness and MRI evidence of surrounding soft tissue inflammation suggested an inflammatory process rather than isolated mechanical injury. Accordingly, distinguishing among these overlapping mechanisms required integration of clinical examination, serial laboratory studies, imaging, and microbiologic results. MRI is the most sensitive imaging modality for osteitis pubis and plays a crucial role in the diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression [6, 7]. The imaging findings in our patient, including bilateral symphyseal edema, fluid collections in the adductor and iliopsoas muscles, and mild right hip effusion, are consistent with inflammatory osteitis pubis complicated by myositis and bursitis. Although imaging can help characterize the extent of inflammation, it is often insufficient to distinguish between infectious and noninfectious etiologies, necessitating clinical correlation and laboratory evaluation [8, 9].

Compared with previously reported cases, the present case is distinctive because the clinical picture extended beyond isolated pubic symphysis pain to include proximal weakness and inflammatory soft tissue involvement, closely mimicking infection or polymyositis. This case illustrates a rare presentation of bilateral inflammatory osteitis pubis with associated myositis and femoral neuropathy in an adult male athlete. Comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, including MRI and electromyography, was essential in distinguishing this condition from infectious and neoplastic processes. The patient's significant clinical and radiological recovery following corticosteroid therapy underscores the potential inflammatory etiology of osteitis pubis in such contexts. Early recognition and targeted anti‐inflammatory treatment may prevent chronic pain, functional limitation, and misdiagnosis of septic arthritis. Long‐term follow‐up is recommended to monitor for recurrence and guide optimal tapering of corticosteroids. Osteitis pubis should be considered in athletes with groin pain, inflammatory laboratory findings, and femoral neuropathy. Multimodal assessment, including MRI, EMG, and fluid analysis, is essential to distinguish inflammatory osteitis pubis from infectious or myopathic conditions.
