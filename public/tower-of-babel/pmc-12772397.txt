Preparation and Characterization of Porous Propellants by Template Leaching with PAAK
Authors: Yufang Song, Weiliang Zhou, Leqin Xiao, Xiaoxia Jian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Porous propellants have garnered considerable interest due to their rapid burning characteristics. To prepare propellants with a high gas generation rate, the template leaching method was employed to create porous gun propellants, utilizing low molecular weight potassium polyacrylate (PAAK) as a pore-forming agent. PAAK was kneaded with nitrocellulose (NC) to produce 7-perforated gun propellants, which were subsequently immersed in water to obtain the porous structure. The pore structure and combustion behavior of the porous propellants were analyzed with varying PAAK additions. The morphology of the porous propellants was observed by using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Pore diameter, pore volume, pore area, and their distributions were characterized through mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP). Additionally, a closed vessel test was conducted to investigate the combustion performance. The results indicate that different pore sizes significantly affect the combustion performance of the porous propellant, with pores smaller than 0.1 μm and larger than 1 μm exhibiting markedly different combustion performance gains. Furthermore, the porous propellants prepared with PAAK demonstrated a higher combustion gas generation rate, with a burning time t m of only 1.40 ms.

Introduction

The rapid evolution of weapon systems has led to rigorous performance standards for traditional munitions, especially concerning the specifications of their propellants. The emergence of new conceptual charge technologies, such as traveling charge technology, caseless cartridge charge technology, and variable burning rate propellant technology, has introduced new requirements regarding the burning rates and energy release characteristics of propellants. , In parallel, modern energetic formulations increasingly regard the propellant grain as an engineered microstructure rather than a passive bulk solid. In composite solid propellants, transition-metal and rare-earth perovskite oxides such as LaFe x Co1–x O3 and LaCo x Mn1–x O3 have been reported to promote the thermal decomposition of common oxidizers (e.g., ammonium perchlorate (AP) and ammonium nitrate (AN)), reduce the apparent activation energy, and increase the burning rate. , Density-functional theory and kinetic studies on LaMn0.4Fe0.6O3 and related multication lanthanum perovskites indicate that mixed B-site cations and oxygen-vacancy defects create highly active redox sites that facilitate oxidizer breakdown and pressure rise. ,

Porous propellants have attracted significant attention due to their unique combustion modes, which can dramatically accelerate gas generation. During combustion, high-temperature and high-pressure gases infiltrate internal voids and ignite pore walls, thereby promoting convective burning. The instantaneous increase in reaction surface area and the associated energy transport can greatly elevate the burning rate compared with laminar burning of dense propellants. , Recent studies on nanoferrite catalysts (for example, CoCuNiFe2O4 and NiCoCr2O4) and alloy-decorated metal fuels (e.g., aluminum microspheres coated with Cu/Ni nanoparticles) show that such additives can intensify gas generation rate in AP/AN-based propellants by lowering the decomposition temperature of the oxidizer and accelerating its thermolysis. − Additional work on multication ferrites and high-surface-area ferrite networks has reported shortened combustion time, steeper pressure-rise behavior, and enhanced gas-generating rate under confinement. −

Numerous approaches have been investigated to introduce porosity into energetic grains. Dissolvable salt porogens, such as KNO3, are commonly used; however, their loadings can reach high levels that may disrupt the colloidal structure and compromise granule homogeneity. Foaming routes have produced microcellular gun propellants with markedly higher gas generation rate than dense analogues. , Emulsion, microfluidic, and freeze-templating approaches can yield porous or even hollow energetic microspheres with controlled wall thickness and very high internal void fractions; in nitrocellulose- or ADN-based systems, these architectures can spread flame rapidly through the interior without catastrophic loss of mechanical integrity during handling. − In parallel with these pore-templating strategies, perovskite and nanoferrite catalysts are increasingly adopted as burning-rate modifiers in AP/AN composite propellants, where they reshape oxidizer thermolysis and increase gas-generating rate. ,,

It is widely acknowledged that pore architecture (pore size distribution, pore volume fraction, and pore area/surface area) plays a decisive role in the combustion of porous propellants; however, quantitative structure–property relationships linking these pore metrics to combustion performance parameters such as ignition/burning time t m , dynamic vivacity L, and maximum pressure p m remain incomplete. Recent machine-learning and artificial neural network (ANN) models have begun to correlate oxidizer decomposition kinetics, activation energy, and gas generation rate with formulation chemistry and microstructure, indicating that combustion response can be predicted directly from structural descriptors. Together with first-principles calculations on LaMn0.4Fe0.6O3 and related lanthanum perovskites, , these results support the view that nanoscale reactive sites, pore connectivity, and convective combustion inside the grain must be considered jointly when designing high-performance propellants.

In this study, potassium polyacrylate (PAAK) is introduced as a pore-forming agent for nitrocellulose (NC)-based porous propellants. NC/PAAK porous grains were fabricated by kneading PAAK with NC, followed by immersion in water to dissolve PAAK and generate pores in the NC matrix. The pore morphology and structure were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and the pore diameter, pore volume, pore area, and their distributions were measured by mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP). A closed vessel test was then conducted to investigate the combustion performance. By correlating the pore diameter, pore volume, pore area, and their distributions with gas generation rate, the study provides experimental evidence and mechanistic insight into the design and application of high-burning-rate porous propellants. Importantly, unlike catalyst-assisted AP/AN systems, ,, the NC/PAAK system achieves rapid gas generation primarily through engineered multiscale porosity, without deliberate introduction of transition-metal oxides or metallic nanoadditives into the propellant matrix.

Template Leaching Method

The template leaching method is a widely used method for preparing porous materials, which has attracted more attention , because of its simple preparation process and good structure controllability. The theory of this technology is to prepare a homogeneous mixture through the matrix and a leachable component and then leach the soluble component, leaving pores in the matrix to form a porous structure.

Experimental Section

Materials

NC powders (nitrogen content, 11.8–12.4%) were prepared by Sichuan Nitrocell Co., Ltd. Potassium hydroxide (KOH, purity ≥ 85.0%), isopropanol (analytically pure, AR), ethanol (AR) and acetone (AR) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Acrylic acid (chemical purity, CP) and ammonium persulfate (purity ≥ 98.0%) were supplied by Shanghai Lingfeng Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. All of the reagents were used as received without further purification. Safety notice: All energetic materials (NC and propellant samples) were handled in small batches under appropriate safety controls, including personal protective equipment, conductive tools to minimize static discharge, and remote ignition of combustion tests within a blast chamber.

Synthesis and Molecular Weight of PAAK

Acrylic monomers are easy to initiate polymerization under heat and radiation because of their high chemical activity. Different synthetic methods lead to differences in molecular weight, product properties, or application direction. For example, when the molecular weight of PAAK is low, the solution is a dilute liquid (<10,000), and when the molecular weight increases to a certain extent, it becomes an elastic colloid, which is not conducive to mixing well with NC. In this study, low molecular weight PAAK was used as a pore-forming agent to prepare porous gun propellants.

The aqueous solution polymerization method was adopted, while the ammonium persulfate was applied as an initiator and the isopropanol was used as a chain transfer agent. First, the neutralization reaction of KOH and acrylic acid was carried out, and then the polymerization was run to synthesize lower molecular weight PAAK. The synthetic route was shown as follows:

KOH and acrylic acid were neutralized to form compound potassium acrylate with cooling and then added to an isopropanol aqueous solution in the flask for stirring. Under the protection of N2 at 80 °C, the potassium acrylate and ammonium persulfate were added dropwise for 1 h. The ammonium persulfate content was 3% of the monomer, and the isopropyl alcohol content was 1.5 times that of the monomer. Then the product was distilled under reduced pressure to remove isopropanol, and the obtained PAAK solution was dried, pulverized for the PAAK particles, and screened through a mesh size of 100, as shown in Figure .

PAAK particles.

The molecular weight of PAAK was measured by viscosimetry. The relationship between the intrinsic viscosity of the polymer solution and the molecular mass was expressed by empirical eq : Mη=η/0.003380.48 1 where M η is the viscous average molecular weight of polymers, η is the intrinsic viscosity.

Each test was repeated about 3–5 times, and the error between the measured values was not more than 0.2 s. The average value of credibility was the final intrinsic viscosity measurement value. The results (see Section ) indicate that a low molecular weight PAAK (on the order of 103) was successfully synthesized, consistent with a liquid/solid product that mixes well with NC.

Preparation of Porous Propellant

NC powders, PAAK, ethanol, and acetone were mixed and kneaded in a kneading machine for 3 h, and the mixture was then extruded to prepare the 7-perforated gun propellants. The gun propellants were cut into 2 mm granules and then dried in a drying oven at 40 °C for 5 days so that the solvent could be completely removed from the propellants.

The dried granules were placed in 250 mL of distilled water and immersed in a constant temperature shaker. Each dose was about 10 g. The granules were immersed at 50 °C for 72 h and replaced with water every 4 h. During this immersion, PAAK gradually dissolved out of the propellant grains, leaving behind pores in the NC matrix. Propellant samples with 10, 20, and 30% PAAK content by weight (named PK-10, PK-20, and PK-30, respectively) were prepared following this procedure. After leaching, the porous propellant granules were removed from the water and dried at 50 °C in a vacuum oven to eliminate moisture. The final products were translucent, porous NC grains. Morphology Characterization

SEM (Quanta FEG 250, FEI) was used to observe the surface and cross sections of porous propellants. The specimens were fractured after freezing in liquid nitrogen for scanning.

Pore Structure

The pore size distribution, pore volume, and pore area of porous propellants were researched by MIP (AutoPore IV 9500, Micromeritics), which provided a basis for studying the combustion performance. The specimens were placed in a vacuum oven at 50 °C for 8 h to make them sufficiently dry before testing.

During MIP, mercury is forced into the pores under controlled pressure, and the volume intruded at each pressure is recorded. From the intrusion data, we obtained the cumulative pore volume and pore area as functions of the pore diameter. The MIP technique assumes cylindrical pores for the calculations. It should be noted that MIP can have limitations: the high pressures may compress polymer pore walls, potentially underestimating pore volume for very compressible samples, and the method is insensitive to pores larger than the instrument’s pressure limit or to completely open-through pores.

Closed Vessel Test

The burning characteristics of the specimens were investigated with a 98.95 mL vessel. The loading density of the propellants is 0.2 g·cm–3. Specimens were fired by NC with a nitrogen content of 12.0%, the igniting pressure was 10 MPa, and the data sampling interval was 0.001 ms.

The p–t curves of the propellants were plotted, and the corresponding L–B curves were also calculated and displayed. Since the L–B curve does not involve the assumption of Vieille’s law, and the data processing is not related to the shape function and the combustion velocity function, it can accurately reflect the actual combustion law of the propellant; thus, the L-B curve is often used to analyze the gas generation status of the propellant. L=dp/dtp·pm 2 B=ppm 3 where L is the dynamic vivacity and B is the relative pressure.

For context, recent combustion studies on AP/AN-based composite propellants frequently incorporate nanoperovskite catalysts such as LaFexCo1–x O3 and LaCoxMn1–x O3 to accelerate oxidizer decomposition and increase burning rate. , Nanoferrite systems (e.g., NiCoCr2O4 and CoCuNiFe2O4) and alloy-decorated metal fuels (for example, aluminum microspheres coated with Cu/Ni nanoparticles) provide similar catalytic effects, lowering the apparent activation energy of AP or AN and increasing the gas generating rate in strand and closed vessel measurements. ,, Such catalytic formulations are typically characterized by differential scanning calorimetry/thermogravimetry (DSC/TG), closed vessel tests, and-more recently, by artificial neural network analysis that links kinetic parameters to burning-rate prediction. The closed vessel test protocol used in this study (vessel volume of 98.95 mL, loading density of 0.2 g·cm–3, ignition pressure of about 10 MPa, data sampling interval of 0.001 ms) provides an experimental baseline for NC/PAAK porous propellants that do not contain transition-metal catalysts.

For PK-10, the mass loss after leaching was approximately 12.5%, which is close to the initial PAAK content and is therefore consistent with expectations. For PK-20 and PK-30, however, the measured mass losses were 27.9 and 40.7%, respectively, which exceed the initial PAAK contents of 20 and 30%. This suggests that in addition to PAAK removal, a fraction of NC was also lost during leaching, particularly at higher PAAK loadings.

A plausible explanation is that at higher PAAK contents, the NC “pore walls” formed after leaching are thinner and mechanically less robust. As a result, small NC fragments or low-molecular-weight NC fractions may detach and be removed together with PAAK during immersion. In other words, increasing the PAAK content produces thinner pore walls, which in turn promotes partial NC loss during extended leaching.

Despite this minor NC loss, the dominant contribution to the total mass loss is still attributed to PAAK removal, indicating that the leaching process was effective. After leaching, the propellant granules, particularly PK-20 and PK-30-were visibly more porous and lower in apparent density. For PK-30, the granules became mechanically fragile, consistent with the high porosity generated. In contrast, PK-10 retained a comparatively higher structural integrity, in line with its lower pore volume. This morphological trend further supports the conclusion that a higher PAAK content leads to higher porosity, greater mass loss, and increased fragility.

In summary, the immersion process successfully removed the pore-former and produced porous propellant grains. The efficiency of PAAK removal was high, and any slight NC loss at higher porosities indicates the need to optimize leaching conditions (perhaps using a gentler solvent or a shorter time) if minimal NC dissolution is desired.

Inner structure of the porous propellants.

At a low PAAK content (PK-10), the porous propellant exhibits only a limited population of pores, and most of these pores are submicrometer in size (tens to hundreds of nanometers in diameter). The pores mainly appear as isolated, closed, or semiclosed voids embedded in an otherwise continuous NC matrix. The pore walls in this state are relatively thick, and the overall structure remains mechanically robust. This morphology is consistent with the relatively low mass loss during leaching and the comparatively high bulk density measured for PK-10.

When the PAAK content is increased (PK-20), the pore number density rises significantly. In addition to the numerous submicrometer pores already present in PK-10, pores with characteristic diameters on the order of 1 μm and above become more frequent. Evidence of local pore coalescence is also seen: adjacent pores begin to merge, locally thinning the surrounding NC pore walls. As a result, the structure at this stage develops a mixed micro–macro porous architecture. A fine pore network is still present, but it is accompanied by a growing population of larger, partially interconnected voids. This transition suggests the early formation of preferential pore pathways that can facilitate gas penetration during combustion.

At the highest PAAK loading (PK-30), the porous propellant develops into a highly porous, sponge-like network. Pores are both abundant and relatively large, with many reaching several micrometers in diameter. The pore walls are visibly thin, and multiple pores have merged to form irregular channels, indicating that at 30 wt % PAAK, the template domains become effectively percolated and, after leaching, leave behind an open, highly connected pore network. This morphology is consistent with the large mass loss observed during immersion (reflecting extensive template removal and partial loss of NC at high porosity) and the reduced mechanical robustness of PK-30. Overall, the evolution from PK-10 to PK-30 shows a clear transition from isolated submicrometer voids within a continuous NC framework to an interconnected, channel-like pore system, which is expected to enhance internal gas flow and promote convective combustion.

These SEM observations support the combustion results discussed later: in PK-20 and especially PK-30, large, connected pores are expected to facilitate rapid gas flow and convective flame penetration during combustion, supporting rapid pressurization and high dynamic vivacity.

Compared with Figures and , it can be observed that there are fewer pores on the surface and more pores in the interior of the porous propellants. This is due to the deformation of the granule's surface under the force during cutting, which blocked part of the pores on the surface. There is noticeable deformation in Figure (d). For clarity, propellant granules were cryofractured after liquid-nitrogen cooling prior to SEM observation. Some tearing or local collapse of thin pore walls during fracture cannot be completely excluded, particularly in PK-30. Nonetheless, the overall increase in pore openness with an increasing PAAK content is unambiguous.

Burning Characteristics

Figure shows the experimental curves obtained from the closed vessel tests. It is observed that the combustion characteristics of the three porous propellants are obviously different. With the increase of PAAK content from 10 to 30%, the burning time decreases from 2.27 to 1.01 ms, with a corresponding decrease in time at maximum dp/dt from 2.01 to 0.55 ms. Maximum dp/dt increases from 242 to 591 MPa·ms–1. Meanwhile, the corrected peak pressure p m shows a slight upward trend (about 284 MPa for PK-20 and about 292 MPa for PK-30), indicating that the faster gas generation not only shortens the pressurization time but also steepens the pressure increase in the vessel. These results demonstrate that increasing PAAK content, and thus increasing the internal pore network formed by template leaching, directly enhances the combustion response of the propellant under confined conditions.

p–t, dp/dt–t and L–B curves for porous propellants.

From Figure c, it can be observed that the dynamic activity increases with the increase of PAAK addition, and the L of PK-30 is up to 25 MPa–1·s–1. In the early stage, the increased burning surface is achieved due to the flame and hot gas penetrating the inner pores, and the propellants exhibit a progressive combustion characteristic accordingly. After the maximum of L, the dynamic vivacity begins to decrease because the burning surface decreases as the infiltration of the flame and hot gas is completed. According to the laminar combustion mechanism for dense gun propellants, the dynamic vivacity depends on the propellant compositions and actual burning surface area. It is used to assess the propellant geometric progressivity and the fracture degree of the propellant surface area. For porous propellants, the burning rate has no exact meaning, and the gas-generating rate is better for understanding the combustion mechanism. Especially, pore diameter, pore area, pore volume, and their distributions all influence the gas generating rate. In other words, unlike dense single-base or deterred propellants, where vivacity mainly reflects external surface regression, the L-B curve captures how quickly the internal pore network becomes involved in burning. The early rise of L corresponds to the rapid ignition of pore walls throughout the interior of the grain, which temporarily increases the effective burning surface and produces strongly progressive behavior. After this internal surface is consumed, L decreases, and combustion becomes less progressive. This progressive–then-regressive sequence is a characteristic feature of porous propellants prepared by template leaching, in which internal pores act as immediate channels for flame penetration and convective heat transfer rather than forcing the flame to advance only from the outer surface inward.

Combining the data from MIP, it indicates that PK-30 has a larger porosity and pore volume. The pore volume is mainly distributed in diameters larger than 1 μm. Although the total pore area is slightly lower, the gas generation rate is the fastest. PK-10 has the largest pore area, and the pore volume is mainly distributed in a diameter of <0.1 μm; however, the gas generation rate is the lowest. It means that the microporous structure has an important effect on combustion performance.

During the combustion process, high-temperature gas propagates in the pores, and convective heat transfer can be easily achieved in the combustion of the porous propellants due to the gas penetration. While the pores are too small, it will result in greater resistance to gas passing through the pores and make it difficult to conduct heat transfer. As can be seen from Figure a, when the pore diameter is large, the combustion gas can easily pass through the pores at a pressure p 0. While the pore diameter is <d 0, the gas hardly passes through the pores under the same pressure (Figure b). Then, heat transfer is difficult to continue, resulting in a slow gas-generating rate. Therefore, the gas generation rate of PK-10, which is mainly distributed in pores with diameters <0.1 μm, is relatively low. At the same time, there are many pores larger than 1 μm in PK-30. When the granules are ignited, the resistance of gas entering from the surface to the interior is small, and convective combustion is performed, resulting in a high gas generation rate. This shows that total porosity alone is not sufficient to predict combustion behavior. The decisive factors are the connectivity and accessibility of pores of micron-level diameter. PK-10 contains mostly submicron pores (<0.1 μm) that are often closed or semiclosed, so hot gas penetration is hindered and convective burning is limited. PK-30, in contrast, contains a large fraction of pores ≥ 1 μm that have merged into channels, providing continuous pathways from the surface toward the interior of the grain. As a result, hot gas can rapidly penetrate and ignite internal surfaces, producing a shorter burning time tm and a higher maximum dp/dt. Therefore, a percolating network of micron-scale pores promotes rapid convective combustion and high dynamic vivacity, whereas a structure dominated by isolated submicron pores mainly increases specific surface area but does not guarantee fast gas generation.

These results indicate that controlled porous-network formation via template leaching represents an effective structural route complementary to conventional catalytic modification to achieve rapid pressure buildup and high energy release within a short time scale. Tailoring the pore size distribution toward an interconnected micron-scale network, therefore, appears to be a viable design strategy for porous propellants aimed at fast internal ballistic response.

Beyond direct comparison among PK-10, PK-20, and PK-30, it is useful to position these NC/PAAK porous propellants relative to those of catalytic energetic systems reported in the literature. Mixed B-site lanthanum perovskites such as LaFexCo1-xO3 and LaCoxMn1-xO3 have been shown to lower the decomposition temperature of AP and to modify its multistep thermolysis pathway by supplying redox-active Co/Mn/Fe centers and abundant oxygen-vacancy sites. , First-principles and kinetic studies on LaMn0.4Fe0.6O3 and related La-based perovskites indicate that multication substitution can accelerate AP breakdown, shorten ignition/burning time, and increase pressure rise in composite propellants. , Nanoferrite catalysts (e.g., NiCoCr2O4 and CoCuNiFe2O4) and alloy-decorated metal fuels play a complementary role: these systems decrease the apparent activation energy of AP or AN and act as burning-rate modifiers, often producing a higher gas generation rate and shorter overall combustion time than unmodified formulations. ,,

The PK-20 and PK-30 specimens exhibit very short t m (about 1 ms) and high dynamic vivacity L (L m up to approximately 25 MPa–1·s–1), indicating rapid gas generation and strong convective combustion under closed vessel conditions. The physical origin, however, differs from that of the catalyst-assisted AP/AN systems noted above. In the NC/PAAK porous propellants, rapid gas generation is achieved by engineered multiscale porosity, for example, a large fraction of pores larger than 1 μm in PK-30 and a substantial population of pores smaller than 0.1 μm in PK-10, which increases internal burning surface area and facilitates flame spread throughout the grain. , When pore diameter is <0.1 μm, gas penetration is hindered and heat transfer becomes difficult, resulting in a relatively low gas generating rate; when pore diameter is larger than 1 μm, combustion gas can more easily penetrate the grain, convective combustion is promoted, and the gas generation rate increases sharply. , These observations indicate that architected porosity generated through PAAK template leaching can deliver gas generation rate, dynamic vivacity, and tm values that approach those of catalyst-modified AP/AN composite propellants, while avoiding intentional addition of transition-metal nanoadditives

Conclusion

In summary, porous nitrocellulose-based propellants were fabricated by incorporating low-molecular-weight potassium polyacrylate (PAAK) as a removable template, followed by aqueous leaching. The resulting grains exhibit controllable pore architectures, ranging from predominantly submicrometer voids to highly connected micrometer-scale channels, depending on PAAK loading. Mercury intrusion porosimetry and SEM indicate that the evolution toward an open, percolated pore network enables rapid convective combustion and leads to high gas-generation rates and elevated dynamic vivacity in closed-vessel tests.

The template-leaching route relies on conventional mixing and water-based extraction, suggesting good scalability and potential manufacturability without reliance on exotic processing steps, such as microfluidic templating. The structural acceleration of combustion identified here is conceptually complementary to catalytic approaches that lower decomposition barriers, indicating an opportunity for future integration of tailored porosity with catalytic additives to further optimize the internal ballistic response.

Finally, the concept of deliberately engineered pore networks may extend beyond single-base propellants: analogous designed-porosity energetic composites could be adapted to other gas-generating systems, and related porous matrices may, in principle, be repurposed postcombustion as functional adsorbent or catalytic media. This broader applicability underscores the relevance of pore-structure engineering not only to propellant performance but also to advanced energetic materials design in general.
