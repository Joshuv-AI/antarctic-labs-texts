Extensive Calcification of a Ventriculoperitoneal Shunt Causing Late Mechanical Failure in a Child
Authors: Fernando Sandoval Martinez, Jose de Jesus Lomeli Ramirez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Calcification of ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) systems is a rare late complication that may result in mechanical shunt failure and the recurrence of hydrocephalus. We report the case of a nine-year-old girl with a longstanding VPS who presented with progressive headache, vomiting, vertigo, decreased visual acuity, and clinical signs of raised intracranial pressure. Radiographs and computed tomography demonstrated extensive calcification involving both the intracranial ventricular catheter and the extracranial distal shunt tract, associated with recurrent hydrocephalus. Given the anticipated risk of intraventricular or intraparenchymal hemorrhage associated with the removal of the densely adherent calcified catheter, the original shunt was left in situ, and a new contralateral VPS was inserted. The patient experienced complete resolution of symptoms following surgery and was discharged neurologically intact. Although rare, extensive VPS calcification should be considered in patients presenting with delayed shunt dysfunction years after implantation. Recognition of extensive intracranial catheter calcification is particularly important, as attempts at catheter removal may carry a significant risk of hemorrhage. In carefully selected patients, placement of a new contralateral shunt while leaving the calcified catheter in situ may represent a safe and effective surgical strategy.

Introduction

Hydrocephalus is a disorder of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics characterized by the excessive accumulation of CSF within the cerebral ventricles, resulting in ventricular dilatation [1]. Ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS) is one of the most commonly performed procedures for the treatment of hydrocephalus [2,3]. Despite its effectiveness, VPS is associated with multiple complications, including infection, obstruction, fracture, migration, and mechanical dysfunction, some of which require surgical revision [2,3].

Calcification of the silicone shunt catheter is an uncommon late complication that typically develops years after implantation [3-5] and is associated with long-term material degradation and local tissue reaction [3,4,6]. Progressive calcification may result in catheter rigidity and tethering, predisposing to mechanical dysfunction, fracture, obstruction, and pain along the shunt tract [3-6]. In a retrospective pediatric radiographic series, catheter calcification was identified in 58 of 472 patients (12.3%), with a mean interval of approximately nine years and 10 months after shunt insertion (range: 4-14 years) [7]. Calcification was also associated with subsequent catheter fracture in that cohort, with 23 of 59 calcified shunts fracturing and an odds ratio of 6.12 for fracture in the presence of calcification [7].

The precise pathophysiological mechanism of shunt calcification remains incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms include chronic material degradation, repetitive mechanical stress, local tissue reaction, and dystrophic mineral deposition [3,4,6,8]. Silicone catheter surfaces may undergo progressive structural degradation, including surface irregularities and microfissures, which may facilitate mineral deposition [8]. Mechanical stress appears particularly relevant at the cervical portion of the shunt, where repeated movement between the mobile neck and relatively fixed chest wall may contribute to catheter deterioration [4,6].

Most reported cases involve the extracranial portion of the catheter, particularly the cervical and upper thoracic subcutaneous tract [2-4]. Intracranial catheter calcification is considerably less common and may present additional diagnostic and surgical challenges [2,5]. Clinically, patients may present with headache, vomiting, visual disturbance, symptoms of raised intracranial pressure, or other manifestations of shunt dysfunction [2,3,9]. Local symptoms may include pain along the shunt tract, restricted neck movement, skin irritation, and palpable thickening associated with catheter tethering [3,4].

We report a case of extensive VPS calcification involving both the intracranial ventricular catheter and extracranial shunt tract in a child presenting with late mechanical shunt failure. The case highlights the unusual extent of intracranial calcification and the surgical considerations involved in managing a densely adherent calcified ventricular catheter.

Case presentation

A nine-year-old girl with a history of congenital hydrocephalus treated with placement of a right-sided VPS at two years of age presented to the outpatient clinic accompanied by her family with a one-week history of progressively worsening headache, vertigo, decreased visual acuity, and multiple episodes of vomiting. The patient had remained clinically stable since the initial shunt insertion and had no previous shunt revisions or documented shunt-related complications. The progressive nature of her symptoms raised concern for late VPS dysfunction.

On physical examination, the patient appeared anxious, mildly dehydrated, and lethargic. Neurological examination demonstrated early papilledema and bilateral Babinski signs without additional focal neurological deficits, findings consistent with raised intracranial pressure.

Radiological evaluation included anteroposterior skull and cervical spine radiographs (Figure 1) together with computed tomography (CT) of the brain and cervical spine (Figure 2). Imaging demonstrated recurrent hydrocephalus associated with extensive calcification involving both the intracranial ventricular catheter and the extracranial distal shunt tract, findings consistent with late shunt dysfunction secondary to catheter calcification.

Following neurosurgical assessment, surgical intervention was undertaken. Because of the technical difficulty and potential risks associated with the removal of the chronically calcified and adherent ventricular catheter, the original shunt system was left in situ, and a new left-sided VPS was inserted to restore CSF diversion.

Postoperatively, the patient was managed with a short course of broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics before de-escalation to oral antibiotics. Prophylactic antiseizure medication was administered for two weeks in accordance with the local postoperative protocol following intracranial surgery. The postoperative course was otherwise uneventful, with complete resolution of the presenting symptoms and no new neurological deficits. The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 3. At the four-week outpatient follow-up, she was clinically well with good postoperative recovery and no recurrence of the presenting symptoms. Postoperative CT imaging was not performed due to parental preference. No alternative imaging modality such as magnetic resonance was locally available.

Discussion

Epidemiology and clinical relevance

VPS calcification is an uncommon late complication of CSF diversion and generally occurs after prolonged implantation [3,4,6-9]. Although much of the literature consists of individual case reports and small case series, a retrospective pediatric radiographic study provides more substantial evidence regarding its frequency and clinical consequences. Siddiqui et al. identified calcification in 58 of 472 patients (12.3%), with a mean interval of nine years and 10 months after shunt insertion (range: 4-14 years) [7]. Among calcified shunts, 23 of 59 subsequently fractured, and calcification was associated with a significantly increased risk of fracture [7]. These findings suggest that radiographically evident calcification, although not necessarily clinically symptomatic in every patient, may have important mechanical consequences.

The present case occurred seven years after shunt implantation and demonstrates extensive calcification involving both the extracranial tract and the ventricular catheter. Most previously reported cases describe calcification predominantly affecting the extracranial portion of the catheter, particularly the cervical subcutaneous tract [2-4,6-11]. Intracranial catheter calcification has been described but appears considerably less common [2,5]. The extensive involvement of both catheter components in our patient therefore represents an unusual manifestation of this complication.

Pathophysiology, material factors, and prevention

The mechanism of VPS calcification is likely multifactorial. Material degradation, repetitive mechanical stress, local tissue reaction, and dystrophic mineral deposition have all been proposed [3,4,6,8]. Studies of retrieved shunt material have demonstrated the progressive structural degradation of silicone tubing, including surface irregularities, fissures, loss of structural integrity, and deposition of calcium phosphate-containing material [4,6,8]. These changes appear to be related, at least in part, to the duration of implantation [4,6,8].

Mechanical stress may be particularly important at the cervical portion of the shunt, where repeated tensile and bending forces occur between the mobile neck and relatively fixed chest wall [4,6,8]. Stannard and Rollins described radiographically visible calcification around the cervicothoracic catheter in three patients, with associated catheter disconnection in two [6]. Such findings support the concept that chronic mechanical stress and progressive material degradation may contribute to catheter mineralization and subsequent mechanical failure.

Catheter composition may represent an additional contributing factor. Calcification has been reported predominantly in barium-impregnated silicone catheters [3,8]. Salim et al. suggested that plain silicone tubing may be less susceptible to this complication [3]. In a small long-term observational series, Yamamoto et al. found no calcification in catheters without barium particles and proposed that barium sulfate incorporated within the silicone may contribute to cracking and provide a site for mineral nucleation [12]. These observations provide a possible explanation for differences in susceptibility between catheter materials.

The potential role of infection in subsequent shunt calcification remains uncertain. Tissue reactions surrounding CSF shunts may occur in association with bacterial shunt infection, but the available evidence does not establish previous infection as an independent risk factor or direct cause of catheter calcification [13]. Chronic inflammatory or foreign-body reactions may contribute to the local tissue environment surrounding an implanted catheter, but their specific contribution to subsequent mineralization remains unclear.

These findings also have implications for prevention. Although the available literature suggests that barium-free catheter systems may theoretically reduce mineral deposition [3,12], the evidence is derived predominantly from small observational studies, case reports, and analyses of retrieved catheter material. Catheter composition may nevertheless be considered as one factor when selecting a new shunt system, alongside the broader evidence regarding infection prevention, mechanical performance, and device characteristics. Further prospective studies are required to determine whether particular catheter materials meaningfully reduce clinically significant calcification.

Clinical presentation and diagnosis

The clinical manifestations of VPS calcification are variable and depend on the extent and anatomical distribution of catheter degradation. Patients may present with headache, vomiting, visual disturbance, lethargy, or other manifestations of raised intracranial pressure resulting from shunt dysfunction [2,3,9]. Others may develop localized pain, restricted neck movement, skin irritation, or palpable thickening along the shunt tract as a consequence of catheter calcification and tethering [3,4,10,11].

In the present case, the patient developed progressive symptoms over one week, including headache, vomiting, vertigo, and decreased visual acuity, with clinical evidence of raised intracranial pressure. Imaging demonstrated recurrent hydrocephalus together with extensive calcification involving both the intracranial ventricular catheter and extracranial shunt tract. This combination of delayed neurological symptoms and extensive catheter calcification supported the diagnosis of late mechanical shunt dysfunction.

Plain radiographs remain useful for demonstrating linear catheter calcification, particularly along the extracranial cervical and thoracic portions, and can also identify associated discontinuity or fracture [3,4,6,10]. CT provides complementary information regarding ventricular size and the extent of intracranial catheter calcification and may therefore be particularly valuable when operative intervention is being considered [2,5].

Surgical management and implications

Management should be individualized according to the patient's clinical presentation, shunt function, imaging findings, and anatomical extent of calcification [2,5,9-11]. Complete removal and replacement of a calcified shunt may be appropriate in selected patients. However, chronic calcification can result in dense adherence of the catheter to surrounding tissues, making removal technically difficult and potentially hazardous [2,5,8,9].

Previous reports illustrate the potential complications associated with the removal of extensively calcified shunt components. Sakai et al. reported bleeding from the ventricular wall during the removal of a calcified catheter associated with ventricular-wall calcification [5]. Other reports have described catheter fracture and difficulties with the extraction of calcified components [7,9]. These findings support a cautious approach when removal of a densely adherent intracranial catheter is considered.

In our patient, the extensive calcification and adherence of the ventricular catheter influenced the decision to leave the original right-sided catheter in situ and insert a new contralateral left-sided VPS. This strategy restored CSF diversion while avoiding manipulation of the chronically adherent ventricular catheter. Similar individualized approaches have been described when removal of calcified shunt components was considered hazardous [2]. The favorable clinical outcome in our patient suggests that preservation of a densely adherent calcified catheter with placement of a new contralateral shunt may represent a potential option in carefully selected patients; however, this approach cannot be considered universally appropriate based on the available evidence or a single case.

In the present case, postoperative CT was not performed because of parental preference, limiting objective radiological assessment following the new shunt insertion. Nevertheless, the patient experienced complete resolution of the presenting symptoms, was discharged on postoperative day 3, and remained clinically well at the four-week follow-up. Longer-term follow-up remains important because patients with longstanding shunt systems remain susceptible to further mechanical complications over time.

Conclusions

Extensive calcification of VPS systems is a rare but important late complication that should be considered in patients presenting with delayed shunt dysfunction many years after implantation. When extensive intracranial catheter calcification is present, surgical management should be individualized, as removal of a densely adherent ventricular catheter may carry a significant risk of hemorrhage. In carefully selected patients, preservation of the calcified catheter with placement of a new contralateral VPS may provide an alternative approach for restoring CSF diversion when catheter removal carries substantial surgical risk.
