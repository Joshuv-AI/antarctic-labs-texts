Determinants of diarrhea and acute respiratory infection among under-five children in urban-rural Somalia: a nationally representative cross-sectional study
Authors: Sahardid Hussein Ibrahim, Zhongliang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute respiratory infection (ARI) and diarrhea are major public health concerns among children under-five years of age. Somalia has one of the highest under-five mortality rates in sub-Saharan Africa, with diarrhea and ARI accounting for most deaths. No national studies have explicitly assessed the determinants of diarrhea and ARI among children in urban and rural settings. This study aimed to assess the prevalence and determinants of diarrhea and ARI among children under-five in urban and rural Somalia.MethodsWe analyzed nationally representative data from the 2020 Somali Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS) of 7732 under-five-year-old children with complete responses to the variables studied. Descriptive analyses were used to summarize the respondents’ characteristics. Multiple logistic regression was used to assess the determinants of diarrhea and ARI. We present our findings as adjusted odds ratios with a 95% confidence interval (95% CI), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. We employed sample weights, stratification, and clustering to account for the sampling design of the SHDS. We adhered to the STROBE checklist for manuscript reporting.ResultsThe prevalence of ARI was 10.7% and 12.2% in urban and rural areas, respectively, whereas that of diarrhea was 8.8% and 11.0%, respectively. Residence in the North-West, child age of 0–23 months, prelacteal feeding, maternal age of 15–24 and 25–34 years, no media exposure, and belonging to a poor wealth index were significantly associated with higher odds of diarrhea and ARI among under-fives. Being the first child in birth order, average size at birth, institutional delivery, antenatal care visits, and improved sources of drinking water and sanitation facilities were significantly associated with lower odds of diarrhea and ARI among children aged under-five years in an urban-rural setting in this study.ConclusionDiarrhea and ARI morbidity in children are significant health challenges in urban and rural areas of Somalia. Background

Globally, diarrhea and acute respiratory infections (ARIs) are significant contributors to under-five mortality rates (U5MRs) [1, 2]. Diarrhea is characterized as the passage of three or more loose or watery stools per day or a frequency exceeding the individual’s typical pattern [3], whereas ARI is characterized by the presence of a cough accompanied by short, rapid breathing often associated with respiratory distress and mortality in young children [4]. Diarrhea and ARI account for nearly one-quarter of all mortalities in children under-five years of age [5]. Despite representing 62% of the global under-five population, low- and lower-middle-income countries (LMICs) account for over 90% of pneumonia- and diarrhea-related mortalities [2]. The high burden of these diseases in LMICs is driven by factors such as inadequate sanitation, limited access to clean water, malnutrition, and low immunization coverage [6]. Moreover, the comorbidity of these infectious diseases exacerbates child vulnerability, making them the leading cause of death among children under-five years of age [7, 8]. In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), the risk of dying before the age of five is approximately 14 times higher than that in high-income countries, with a substantial share of these deaths attributed to diarrheal diseases and ARI [9]. In response to the survival threat posed by these diseases, Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.2 aims to reduce under-five mortality to a target of 25 per 1000 live births by 2030 [10]. The World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended that states ensure that all children have access to essential healthcare services without undue financial burden as part of their efforts to reduce morbidity and mortality in children under-five years of age [9].

Over the past three decades, substantial progress has been made in reducing under-five mortality rates. Several LMICs, including Ethiopia, Malawi, and Uganda in SSA and Bangladesh, Mongolia, and Uzbekistan in Asia, have achieved reductions exceeding 75% between 1990 and 2021 [11]. These gains surpassed the global average decline of 59%, demonstrating that significant advancements are achievable in diverse settings. While improvements in socioeconomic conditions have played a major role in lowering under-five mortality in some LMICs [12, 13], others continue to face challenges owing to slow economic growth, fragile health systems, and political instability [14]. To further reduce under-five mortality in these contexts, policymakers require context-specific evidence of modifiable risk factors at the population level in low-income countries.

Somalia, the easternmost country in SSA, remains one of the countries with higher U5MRs, ranking third after Nigeria and Niger, with more than 100 deaths per 1000 live births [15]. Diarrhea and ARI are among the leading causes of these deaths [16]. Although Somalia has made progress, reducing its U5MRs from 179 deaths per 1000 live births in 1990 to 106 in 2022, the decline remains modest compared to neighboring countries, such as Ethiopia and Kenya, which have reduced their U5MRs to 46 and 41 deaths per 1000 live births, respectively [17]. The persistently high incidence of diarrhea and ARI likely contributed to the sluggish decline in child mortality in Somalia. Given the unacceptably high U5MRs in Somalia and limited progress relative to regional peers, there is an urgent need for targeted interventions to improve maternal and child health outcomes. Therefore, assessing the determinants of diarrhea and ARI in Somalia is critical for designing effective strategies to accelerate the reduction of preventable under-five deaths.

Several studies conducted within the region and beyond have associated under-five diarrhea and ARI with a range of child, maternal, and household-level factors [7, 8, 18–22]. In Somalia, the 2020 SHDS provides important prevalence estimates and insights into the health needs of hard-to-reach populations, including nomadic, rural, and urban households. However, there is a lack of in-depth analysis of the underlying factors associated with diarrhea and ARI. A recent nationally representative study in Somalia focused solely on diarrhea using pooled estimates [23], while a few similar studies conducted thus far did not specifically represent children under-five years [24, 25].

To address this gap, the present study examined diarrhea and ARI among children under-five years of age, disaggregated by urban and rural residence. Children from nomadic strata were excluded from this specific analysis to allow a focused assessment of settled urban-rural inequalities. This urban-rural stratification is critical for identifying disparities across population groups and aligns with the SDG 3 agenda to ‘leave no one behind’ and advance progress toward Universal Health Coverage (UHC) [26]. The absence of a comprehensive, national-level analysis examining both conditions limits evidence-based policymaking and the design of targeted interventions that are essential for improving child survival in Somalia. To meet the 2030 target of SDG 3.2, Somalia requires an accelerated annual decline in under-five mortality of 11.1%, compared with the slow 2.7% reduction achieved between 2006 and 2016 [27]. By assessing the prevalence and determinants of diarrhea and ARI among under-five children across urban and rural settings, this study seeks to fill the existing knowledge gap and provide evidence to inform targeted policies and programs that can strengthen under-five health outcomes and hasten progress toward national and global child survival goals.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a secondary analysis of a cross-sectional study that assessed the prevalence and determinants of diarrhea and ARI in children under-five years of age in urban and rural settings in Somalia. Somalia is the easternmost country in the Horn of Africa, encompassing an area of 637,657 km2, with terrain characterized by plateaus, plains, and highlands. Somalia has Africa’s’ longest coastline, spanning 3,333 km beside the Gulf of Aden to the north and the Indian Ocean to the east and south. It shares borders with Djibouti to the northwest, Ethiopia to the west, and Kenya to the southwest. Somalia has a hot tropical climate characterized by a few seasonal fluctuations, with daily temperatures ranging from 30 °C to 40 °C. The country has four seasons: Spring and Autumn are rainy, whereas Summer and Winter are dry [28].

Data source and sampling procedures

We analyzed data from 7732 under-five children extracted from the 2020 SHDS, which was conducted in 2018/2019 and published in 2020. This nationally representative cross-sectional survey was publicly available, and permission was obtained to access the datasets. The study protocols adhered to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration, and oral informed consent was obtained from all participants by trained survey enumerators prior to data collection. The SHDS employed a multistage stratified sampling design to ensure national representation. In the first stage, enumeration areas (EAs) were selected with a probability proportional to the population size. In the second stage, a fixed number of households were systematically selected within each EA. A three-stage stratified cluster design was used for urban and rural areas, and a two-stage design was used for the nomadic regions. Except for the Banadir region, which is entirely urban, all administrative regions were stratified into urban, rural, and nomadic areas, yielding 55 sampling strata. Owing to security constraints, the rural and nomadic strata of the Bay, Lower Shabelle, and Middle Juba were excluded, resulting in 47 strata in the final sample [28]. To ensure comparability across regions, primary sampling units (PSUs) were selected with a probability proportional to the number of households, and 30 households were sampled from each PSU.

The survey targeted women aged 15–49 years and children under-five years as the main population. A total of 16,486 women were interviewed (11,876 married and 4,610 unmarried) using standardized demographic and health survey (DHS) household and women’s questionnaires. Information on all children under-five years of age was obtained from maternal or caregiver reports. The analytical sample included 7732 children under-five years of age living in urban and rural areas with complete data on diarrhea and ARI, as well as on all explanatory variables within the two weeks preceding the survey. Children with missing information on either the outcome variables or key explanatory variables were excluded from the analysis. The SHDS is a valuable resource for health and population studies. It informs program development, shapes policies, and tracks progress toward international and national targets. The final report of the 2020 SHDS provides additional details on the sampling procedures, survey instruments, and settings [28].

Study variables and measures

Outcome variables

The study variables were classified as outcome and explanatory variables. The primary outcome variables were diarrhea and ARI. These two distinct binary outcomes were analyzed independently using separate multiple logistic regression models. In the SHDS, mothers were asked to respond to the inquiry, “Did (child name) have diarrhea or symptoms of ARI (e.g., cough or difficulty breathing) within 2 weeks before the survey?”. The responses were recorded as “Yes” if the child had ever experienced diarrhea or ARI if not, they were classified as “No.”

Explanatory variables

This study explored 15 explanatory variables. The explanatory variables included selected child, maternal, household, and environmental factors. Child factors included age (0–23, 24–35, 36–47, 48–59 months), sex (male, female), birth order (1, 2–3, ≥ 4), baby size (average, larger than average, smaller than average), and prelacteal feeding (yes, no). Maternal factors included age (15–24, 25–34, 35–49 years), education (no formal education, primary, secondary, or higher), exposure to media (no, yes), place of delivery (institutional or home), parity (≤ 2, 3, and ≥ 4), and frequency of antenatal care (ANC) visits (none, 1, and ≥ 2). Despite the WHO recommendation of eight ANC contacts, ANC uptake in Somalia is low; thus, ANC was categorized as none, 1, and ≥ 2 visits to reflect the local context [29]. Household factors included wealth index (poor, middle, and rich), source of drinking water (improved or unimproved), and type of sanitation facility (improved or unimproved). Finally, environmental factors included the region of residence (northwest, northeast, central, and south).

The SHDS survey data categorized media exposure into three categories: “read newspapers, magazines,” “listen to the radio,” and “watch television,” “weekly or at least once a week, less than once a week, or not at all.” To simplify the analysis, we dichotomize the predictor variable into “exposure to media” and “not exposure to media.” Additionally, the survey applied principal component analysis to categorize household wealth levels based on assets. The original categorization consisted of five quintiles (poorest, poor, middle, rich, and richest). For simplification, we classified them into three categories. Poor (combining categories 1 and 2), middle (combining categories 3 and 4), and rich (representing category 5). This simplification approach has been commonly applied in demographic and health survey-based studies in Somalia and beyond [4, 29–31]. The categorization of sources of drinking water and toilet facilities was based on the WHO and UNICEF Joint Monitoring Program (JMP) for water and sanitation guidelines [32]. Improved water sources included drinking water obtained from tube wells/boreholes, piped water, protected dug wells, protected springs, rainwater, and packaged/bottled water. In contrast, unimproved water sources included unprotected dug wells, springs, and surface water collected directly from rivers, lakes, dams, streams, canals, irrigation channels, and ponds. A household sanitation facility was regarded as unimproved if household members used pit latrines without a slab/open latrine, flush to somewhere else, bucket toilets, or hanging latrines. Improved sanitation was defined as flush/pour-flush toilets, slab pit latrines, ventilated improved latrines, and composting toilets, as defined by the JMP [33]. The selection and categorization of the study variables were based on their theoretical relevance and significant association with the outcome variables and the data available in the SHDS and were guided by prior research findings identifying key factors associated with diarrhea and ARI in similar contexts [7, 18–20, 23].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and multiple logistic regression models for complex survey data. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sample characteristics, such as frequencies and percentages. Survey-weighted multiple logistic regression models were fitted separately for diarrhea and ARI. Bivariate (unadjusted) logistic regression analysis was first performed separately for diarrhea and ARI to examine the crude associations between each explanatory variable and the outcomes. Variables with a significant p-value ≤ 0.05 in the bivariate analysis were included in the survey-weighted multiple logistic regression models to estimate the adjusted odds ratios. We selected binary logistic regression because of the binary (dichotomous) categorization of the study outcomes. Multiple logistic regression is an appropriate analytical approach for examining the association between binary outcomes and multiple explanatory variables. By modeling the log-odds of the outcome, this method ensures that the estimated probabilities are constrained between 0 and 1 [34]. Moreover, multiple logistic regression enables the simultaneous assessment of determinants operating at different levels while controlling for potential confounding factors, and it is widely applied in DHS-based studies of child health outcomes [4, 21, 31, 35, 36]. All categorical explanatory variables were incorporated into the regression models using dummy (indicator) coding, whereby each category was represented by a binary variable (1 = presence of the category; 0 = otherwise), with one category omitted and treated as the reference group [6, 22, 37]. This approach avoids imposing artificial ordering on nominal variables and prevents multicollinearity. The estimated coefficients therefore represent comparisons between each category and the reference group. Reference categories were selected based on analytical considerations to facilitate meaningful interpretation of the results; in several instances, more advantaged groups (e.g., older child age, higher maternal education, and higher wealth status) were used as the baseline to assess relative differences in the odds of the outcome among less advantaged groups, consistent with previous studies [22, 30, 31, 38]. We assessed multicollinearity among the explanatory variables using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), applying a traditional threshold of VIF < 3. The VIF values ranged from 1.0 to 2.5, signifying that multicollinearity was not a serious concern in the final models. The regression coefficients were estimated using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). Statistical significance was assessed using Wald tests, and the results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model adequacy was evaluated using standard goodness-of-fit procedures [39, 40]. An odds ratio greater than 1 indicates higher odds of the outcome relative to the reference category, whereas an odds ratio less than 1 indicates lower odds of the outcome. Before data analysis, we cleaned the data and confirmed that no missing values remained in the final analysis. Following the DHS analytical guidelines and prior studies [7, 19, 41], we accounted for stratification, clustering, and sampling weights using the svy command in Stata to ensure representativeness and precise statistical estimation. Data analysis using a cluster sampling design and stratification, along with complex sample statistics, accommodates the complex survey sample design and unequal selection probabilities, thus improving the statistical reliability of the estimates [42]. To account for this, we implemented a comprehensive sampling plan that specified the factors that Stata must account for during the data analysis of the sampling weights, strata, and clusters. Data were analyzed using Stata version 16. This study adhered to the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE)” guidelines for the preparation and reporting of cross-sectional studies [43].

Results

Sample characteristics

Of the children’s characteristics analyzed, 3477 (44.9%) were aged between 48 and 59 months, 4056 (52.5%) were male, 5584 (72.2%) had an average (normal) baby size at birth, and 4492 (58.1%) were not introduced to prelacteal feeding. Regarding maternal characteristics, 3615 (46.8%) were aged between 25 and 34 years (Table 1). A higher proportion of mothers, 6493 (83.9%) and 6410 (82.9%), did not have access to the media​ or had any formal education, while two-thirds of the mothers, 5765 (74.6%), had given birth at home. Meanwhile, 4825 (62.4%) did not have ANC visits. Regarding household-level characteristics, close to half, 3337 (43.2%), were from a poor household wealth index, 4358 (56.4%) and 4199 (54.3%) participants had access to improved water and sanitation facilities, respectively. Table 1 presents the distribution of sample characteristics based on residential areas. The prevalence of diarrhea in our study was 8.8% (95% CI: 7.81–9.68) and 11.0% (95% CI: 9.87–11.85) in urban and rural areas, respectively. The prevalence of ARI was 10.7% (95% CI: 9.62–11.64) and 12.2% (95% CI: 11.31–13.21) in urban and rural areas, respectively.

Determinants associated with diarrhea and ARI

The results of the bivariate analysis showed significant explanatory variables that exhibited statistical significance in their unadjusted association with diarrhea and ARI among under-five children in urban and rural areas. Children from the North-West and North-East regions, those receiving prelacteal feeding, mothers were aged 15–24 and 25–34 years, those with no media exposure, and those from families classified under poor household wealth index were found to have higher unadjusted odds ratios of diarrhea and ARI among children under-five years of age (Table 2). Additionally, notable predictors associated with reduced odds included average baby size at birth, institutional birth, ANC attendance, and improved water and toilet facilities.

Following multiple logistic analysis, children living in households in urban and rural areas in the northwest were 1.70 and 1.53 times more likely to have diarrhea compared to children in the southern region, respectively. In contrast, children with an average size at birth were 32% and 45% less likely to have diarrhea compared to children with smaller sizes at birth. Children who were prelacteal fed were 1.32 times more likely to have diarrhea compared to children who were not prelacteal fed in rural areas.

Children born to mothers aged 15–24 and 25–34 years were 1.60 and 1.64 times more likely to have diarrhea, respectively, compared to those born to mothers aged 35–49 years in urban areas. In addition, children born to mothers with primary education were 42% and 39% less likely to have diarrhea, respectively, compared to those born to mothers with secondary or higher education levels in urban and rural settings. Additionally, children born to mothers with no media exposure were 1.56 times more likely to have diarrhea compared to those born to mothers with media exposure (Table 2). Children from households classified under the poor and middle wealth index were 1.72 and 1.92 times more likely to have diarrhea, respectively, compared to children from households classified under the rich wealth index in rural areas. Children whose mothers gave birth in healthcare institutions were 40% less likely to have diarrhea compared to under-five children whose mothers gave birth at home in rural areas. Children born to mothers with 1 ANC visit were 36% and 39% less likely to have diarrhea compared to mothers with 2 or more ANC visits in urban and rural areas, respectively. Children from households classified under improved water and sanitation facilities were 38% and 37% less likely to have diarrhea, respectively, compared to children from households classified under unimproved water and sanitation facilities in rural areas (Table 2).

Discussion

This study assessed the determinants associated with diarrhea and ARI among children under-five years of age in urban-rural settings in Somalia using the 2020 SHDS. The association between numerous crucial parameters, including child, maternal, and household-level factors on diarrhea and ARI, was assessed. Region of residence, child size at birth, maternal age, access to media, wealth index, institutional delivery, ANC visits, and household drinking water and toilet facilities were significant predictors of diarrhea and ARI among children under-five years of age in this study. This quantitative evidence can inform the development of targeted initiatives to reduce diarrhea and ARI and eventually contribute to a reduction in under-five mortality associated with these illnesses.

The prevalence of ARI in our study was 10.7% and 12.2% in urban and rural areas, respectively, whereas that of diarrhea was 8.8% and 11.0%, respectively. The prevalence of ARI in urban and rural areas in our study was higher than that reported in a study in SSA [38] and rural Ethiopia [31]. However, this prevalence is much lower than that reported for urban and rural areas in India [44]. Regarding diarrhea, the prevalence of diarrhea in urban areas in this study was lower than that in semi-urban regions of Ethiopia [45], while similar to our findings, the prevalence of diarrhea in rural areas has also been reported in Ethiopia [46]. The difference in the prevalence of ARI and diarrhea in our study compared with previous studies may be attributed to variations in geographical contexts and the risk factors studied, such as birth weight, institutional delivery, sanitation facilities, and access to clean drinking water. Differences in the population, methodologies applied, and discrepancies in the study period could also have contributed to the variation in ARI and diarrheal proportions across studies. For instance, our study used mothers’ reported symptoms over a two-week recall period. Other studies, such as Murarkar et al. [44] in India, used a one-month recall period of ARI symptoms that potentially differed in terms of the severity of symptoms and contextual factors. This variation in recall duration can influence prevalence estimates due to under-or overestimation. Similarly, although Merera et al. [31] and Ferede et al. [46] also utilized Demographic and Health Survey data with standardized case definitions, discrepancies in field implementation, demographic characteristics, or respondent understanding of key symptom-based questions of diarrhea and ARI, such as cough and difficulty in breathing, may affect the comparability of the reported prevalence of diarrhea and ARI estimates. The higher prevalence of under-five ARI and diarrhea has implications for Somalia in achieving SDG target 3.2 and reducing preventable deaths in under-five children. This may explain why Somalia has achieved minimal progress in reducing U5MRs [28]. Health policymakers and practitioners must develop initiatives to minimize ARI and diarrheal infections to reduce U5MRs.

Our findings indicate that the determinants associated with under-five child diarrhea and ARI in Somalia vary among populations, such as urban and rural residents. Children living in the north-west and north-east regions were more likely to have higher odds of child diarrhea and ARI compared to children living in the south region. This was not surprising, as these regions are known to have highlands, mountains, and climatic fluctuations, which are cold in winter or dry and dusty in summer, followed by recurrent natural disasters [28]. Therefore, children under-five years of age are more prone to a higher incidence of ARI. During cold weather, children remain indoors for extended periods, increasing their exposure to germs, fungi, and viruses [31]. Higher freezing temperatures increase the likelihood that viruses, bacteria, and influenza remain airborne, creating respiratory droplets that can damage the airways and facilitate bacterial infections, primarily ARI. The infancy stage, limited accessibility to improved sources of water and sanitation facilities, and household dependence on charcoal for cooking and heating rooms during cold seasons could be other reasons for the higher odds of ARI and diarrhea in those regions [31, 47]. Furthermore, the higher likelihood of diarrheal diseases in the region could be due to the drier periods. Rising temperatures and dry seasons have been linked to a higher occurrence of diarrheal diseases [48, 49]. According to Lee et al. [48], a 5 °C increase in the mean daily maximum temperature led to a 37% increase in diarrheal illnesses and dehydration among children in Cape Town. In our study, children aged 0–23 months and those introduced to prelacteal feeding were more likely to have diarrhea and ARI compared to children aged 48–49 months and those not prelacteally fed in rural areas. Ferede et al. revealed that children aged 6–23 and 24–35 months were more likely to have diarrhea than older children in rural Ethiopia [46]. The likelihood of diarrhea in this age group has also been reported in Ghana [7]. Children aged 6–24 months begin supplementary feeding and crawling and may come into contact with a contaminated environment, exposing them to diarrheal infections. Higher exposure to rotavirus and an immature immune system at this age may also contribute to the higher odds of diarrhea and ARI. In contrast, older children age–36–47 months have been associated with a protective factor for the comorbidity of diarrhea and ARI [19]. The effect of age on health in children under-five years has also been reported in SSA [22]. Similarly, prelacteal feeding is prone to a higher risk of contamination and may affect the gut microbiome, leading to a higher risk of newborn infections [50]. Initiating breastfeeding within the first hour of life is essential for newborn protection against pathogens, exclusive breastfeeding duration, and colostrum production for nutrition and immunity, while delaying or prelacteal feeding increases the likelihood of developing earlier infections, such as diarrhea and ARI, among children under-five [19, 51–53].

Compared with previous findings [47], the likelihood of ARI was lower among first-born children than among children with a birth order of four or more in rural areas. A study in India associated children with a second birth order with less likelihood of having ARI compared to those with three or more [54]. First-born babies receive more parental time and attention, whereas subsequent babies may receive less attention during early life. This disparity in parental attention can lead to unfavorable health effects, such as fever, in children with higher birth orders [55]. Later-born babies in larger families have a higher risk of hospitalization for respiratory illnesses and eye and ear infections, presumably because they receive less parental attention [56]. In this study, child size at birth was significantly and consistently associated with diarrhea and ARI. Children of average size at birth were less likely to have diarrhea and ARI than those of smaller size at birth. These findings are supported by other studies on SSA and LMICs [38, 57, 58]. This may be attributed to the child’s small size at birth and the prevalence of medical disorders, such as malnutrition, chronic diseases, or other congenital disorders, which subsequently increase the child’s susceptibility to infections. Most children with small birth sizes have a higher probability of developing diseases, such as diarrhea and ARI, which can lead to mortality. Infants with low birth weights are more vulnerable to morbidity and mortality, which can result in diarrheal diseases [58]. Hence, increased attention is necessary to address the health needs and obstacles to mitigate the likelihood of diarrhea and other pediatric diseases. Children born to mothers aged 15–24 and 25–34 years were more likely to have diarrhea and ARI, respectively, compared to children born to mothers aged 35–49 years in urban areas, as supported by previous studies [41, 58, 59]. This may result in younger mothers lacking the financial resources and knowledge necessary to nurture their children’s health. Younger motherhood may also have obstacles, including neglect and an increased risk of disease transmission for both mothers and babies. Furthermore, older mothers may have more experience in preventing diarrhea in children under-five years of age [41]. Health education interventions should target young mothers to reduce diseases in children under-five years of age. Children born to mothers with primary education were less likely to have diarrhea compared to children born to mothers with secondary or higher educational levels in urban and rural areas. Evidence from a multi-country analysis of 57 LMICs indicates that in 18 countries, children of educated mothers had a higher prevalence of diarrhea compared with children of uneducated mothers, highlighting the context-specific nature of this relationship [60]. This paradoxical association may reflect differences in childcare practices, as more educated mothers are more likely to participate in the workforce, potentially leading to alternative caregiving arrangements. Dietary practices may also play a role, as the consumption of fast foods or formula feeding, which is more common among educated mothers, may increase the risk of foodborne illness [61, 62]. There is a need for targeted health education and promotion strategies to support optimal childcare and feeding practices across all educational levels. Furthermore, children born to mothers with no formal education were more likely to have ARI compared to children born to mothers with secondary or higher educational levels in urban areas. This finding is consistent with that of previous studies [31, 38]. Education may enhance mothers’ healthcare-seeking behavior and follow medical advice, as well as adherence to appropriate childcare practices, such as exclusive breastfeeding until 6 months, proper complementary feeding, and vaccinations, which can minimize the chances of diarrhea and ARI. Likewise, educated mothers might have more control over their babies’ healthcare decisions and be well informed about accessing and applying child health information [7]. Children born to mothers with no media exposure were more likely to have diarrhea and ARI compared with children born to mothers with media exposure in urban areas. Our results are consistent with those of previous studies [38, 58]. Media exposure promotes mothers’ knowledge of the utilization of maternal healthcare services, including ANC visits, institutional deliveries, postnatal care, vaccinations, and suitable feeding practices for children, potentially lowering their likelihood of contracting infections [63]. Preventable health measures frequently transmitted through radio, television, and newspapers are indirectly associated with the occurrence of diseases such as diarrhea. However, media accessibility might be affected by low socioeconomic factors and the availability of social infrastructure, such as electricity, which might be lacking in most households in LMICs [58].

The economic status of households with children significantly influences the incidence of diarrhea and ARI among children under-five [31, 58]. These findings are consistent with our findings. Children from households classified under the poor and middle-wealth index were more likely to have diarrhea and ARI compared to children from households classified under the rich wealth index. This finding is consistent with that of the SSA [41]. This could be due to the affordability of better nutrition for children from wealthier households. Similarly, the risk factors linked to childhood diarrhea, such as unsanitary conditions and contaminated water, are minimized among wealthier families [64]. Mothers from wealthier households are expected to use healthcare services, access media, and have good sanitation and hygienic cooking measures, resulting in a reduced incidence of diarrhea and other infectious diseases among children under-five [58, 65]. Children from wealthier families may experience healthier and more stable living conditions with enhanced access to health-promoting resources compared to children from poor families [35]. Better wealth is associated with a lower likelihood of diarrhea and ARI among children under-five [4, 7, 38].

Children born to mothers who had antenatal visits and those born in healthcare institutions were less likely to have diarrhea and ARI than children born at home in rural areas. Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies [23, 38, 66, 67] but contrast with those from the Gambia [68]. This can be explained by the fact that mothers delivering at healthcare institutions may receive better information regarding childcare, prevention of diarrheal diseases, and advice on exclusive breastfeeding, which may reduce the likelihood of infections that can result in diarrheal illnesses [66]. Similarly, antenatal visits enhance mothers’ understanding of pregnancy risks, sanitation, hygiene measures, and proper feeding practices for their newborns, all of which contribute to reducing the likelihood of childhood diseases. Prenatal care encompasses any medical attention provided to the mother and child to prevent diarrhea; thus, mothers with insufficient prenatal care had a greater probability of experiencing diarrhea episodes under the age of five years [67]. In this study, children from​ households classified under the improved drinking water and sanitation facilities were less likely to have diarrhea and ARI, compared to children form households classified under the unimproved drinking water and sanitation facilities in rural areas. Our findings are consistent with similar conclusions for LMICs [31, 58, 59]. A possible explanation for this could be contamination during water storage, distribution of unsafe piped water, or lack of water treatment practices [47]. In rural regions, where diarrhea frequently contributes to morbidity, water treatment interventions, such as chlorination, significantly reduce the prevalence of under-five diarrhea [69]. The use of unimproved sources of drinking water and sanitation facilities has been associated with higher odds of diarrhea and ARI compared to those associated with improved sources of drinking water and sanitation facilities [31, 46].

The strengths and limitations

This is the first Somali study to examine numerous crucial determinants, including child, maternal, household, and environmental factors, of diarrhea and ARI in children under-five years of age in urban and rural areas. These findings should assist policymakers in understanding this health threat and implementing appropriate governmental measures. The national representativeness of the SHDS data enables our findings to be generalizable to the entire country. Despite the small urban-rural disparities observed, this disaggregation reveals valuable insights for context-specific and targeted public health interventions, which aligns with the aim of the SHDS. However, our study has some limitations. Our study analyzed self-reported data; therefore, mothers reported diarrhea and ARI symptoms that were not derived from clinical investigations and were consequently subject to recall bias. Nonetheless, restricting our samples to the most recent live births occurring within five years prior to the 2020 SHDS diminished the probability of recall bias. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of the survey could not correctly establish a causal relationship; only an association was considered when evaluating the study’s findings. As with all secondary analyses, there are potential risks, which we mitigated through rigorous data cleaning and validation procedures prior to analysis. Moreover, coding and data extraction remained substantially unchanged [70]. Finally, the study only utilized quantitative approaches to evaluate the determinants of diarrhea and ARI among children under-five years of age. Hence, a qualitative study may offer a more narrative perspective of the data, facilitating a more profound examination of the research subjects. Despite these limitations, the application of a complex survey design accounts for the stratification, clustering, and sample weights of the SHDS datasets, thereby enhancing the precision of the estimates. Furthermore, this study utilized recent SHDS data to provide valuable insights into child health in a low-visibility research setting.

Conclusion

This study examined the prevalence and determinants of diarrhea and ARI among children under-five years of age in Somalia, indicating a slightly higher prevalence than that in other SSA countries. The key associated factors included region of residence, birth order, child size at birth, prelacteal feeding, maternal age, media exposure, wealth index, place of delivery, ANC visit frequency, and household access to improved water and sanitation. These findings imply the necessity for geographically and contextually tailored interventions, such as promoting early breastfeeding, enhancing maternal health outreach through mass media, increasing access to ANC care, and increasing the use of improved household water and sanitation facilities, especially in underprivileged areas. Enhancing these areas may significantly reduce childhood infections and promote equitable health outcomes in urban and rural settings.

Policy implications of the finding

This study highlights the ongoing prevalence of diarrhea and ARI in children under five years of age in Somalia, with ARI having a relatively more significant health impact. Policy responses must prioritize region-specific interventions that enhance maternal health services, especially antenatal care and institutional deliveries, while concurrently advancing access to safe drinking water, sufficient sanitation, and reliable health communication through the media. Assisting rural households with sustainable water storage is essential. Furthermore, the observed protective effect of normal birth weight underscores the need to enhance maternal nutrition and antenatal services, which are essential for optimizing maternal health and favorable birth outcomes.
