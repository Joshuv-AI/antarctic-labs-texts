Dual-positive anti-aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase and anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 antibody-related rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease in a patient with severe coronavirus disease 2019 complicated by probable pulmonary aspergillosis and pneumothorax: a case report
Authors: Kazuki Tanaka, Akira Kawamura, Kensuke Kita, Kyohei Matsushita, Ryo Suzuki, Takumi Nagasaki, Yuya Yamamoto, Kotaro Yamada, Ryuuichi Nakamura, Norimichi Akiyama, Shun Matsuura, Naoki Koshimizu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOccasionally, severe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) can lead to rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease (RP-ILD) by triggering or unmasking various myositis-specific autoantibodies (MSAs). Although dual positivity for anti-aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (ARS) and anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 (MDA5) antibodies is extremely rare, its management is exceptionally complex when complicated by secondary infections. Identifying a specific anti-ARS subtype is often difficult, but clinical features such as amyopathic phenotypes and refractory lung involvement often confirm a non-Jo-1 antibody subtype, which carries a poor prognosis.Case presentationA 77-year-old female patient was admitted with COVID-19. Despite standard treatment, her respiratory condition progressively deteriorated. Initial laboratory findings revealed bicytopenia, suggesting underlying immune dysregulation. Further evaluation demonstrated dual positivity for anti-ARS and anti-MDA5 antibodies, leading to a diagnosis of RP-ILD, for which tacrolimus was initiated. During the clinical course, she developed probable COVID-19-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (CAPA). Despite multimodal therapy, including careful management of drug–drug interactions between antifungal and immunosuppressive agents, the patient experienced recurrent pneumothorax and died on day 29.ConclusionsThis case illustrates an extreme therapeutic challenge of managing the quadruple burden of advanced age, dual MSA positivity, severe COVID-19, and opportunistic fungal infection. Clinicians should recognize that initial bicytopenia and refractory RP-ILD in patients with severe COVID-19 may be early clinical indicators of an underlying, subclinical autoimmune state. The early identification of these features is essential for balancing intensive immunosuppression and infection control in complex cases.Keywords: Anti-ARS antibody, Anti-MDA5 antibody, Dual positivity, Rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease, COVID-19-associated pulmonary aspergillosis, Pneumothorax

Background

Polymyositis/dermatomyositis (PM/DM) is a subgroup of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, with myositis-specific autoantibodies (MSAs) detected in a substantial proportion of patients [1]. Among these, anti-aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase (ARS) antibodies are commonly identified, and clinical phenotypes vary significantly depending on the antibody subtype. For instance, non-Jo-1 subtypes (e.g., anti-PL-7, anti-PL-12, and anti-KS) are frequently associated with amyopathic phenotypes and more refractory interstitial lung disease (ILD) compared with the anti-Jo-1 subtype [2]. Meanwhile, anti-melanoma differentiation-associated gene 5 (MDA5) antibodies represent a distinct clinical subset strongly associated with rapidly progressive (RP)-ILD [3]. Although these antibodies typically present with distinct clinical phenotypes, rare cases of dual positivity have been reported [4].

Severe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) often involves cytokine storms requiring intensive immunosuppressive therapy, including corticosteroids (CS) [5]. Notably, several studies have emphasized the clinicopathological similarities between COVID-19 and anti-MDA5 antibody-positive DM-ILD [6, 7]. Moreover, viral infections have been considered potential triggers or immunological modifiers in the development or unmasking of MSAs. Intensive therapy is essential; however, it also increases the risk of fatal opportunistic infections, such as COVID-19-associated pulmonary aspergillosis (CAPA), and mechanical complications, including pneumothorax and pneumomediastinum.

To the best of our knowledge, reports of dual-positive anti-ARS and anti-MDA5 antibodies identified during severe COVID-19 remain extremely limited. Given the clinical heterogeneity within the anti-ARS spectrum, our case underscores the therapeutic challenges and phenotypic features encountered in this complex setting, further complicated by CAPA and mechanical pulmonary complications.

Case presentation

A 77-year-old female patient presented to our institution due to a 5-day history of fever, cough, fatigue, and anorexia. Her medical history was significant only for hypertension, and there was no family history of connective tissue diseases. She had received four doses of the COVID-19 vaccine, the last of which was administered 18 months earlier. Upon admission, her vital signs were as follows: temperature, 39.7 °C; blood pressure, 121/81 mmHg; heart rate, 89 beats/min; and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) in room air, 94%. Chest auscultation revealed no abnormal breath sounds, and evident muscle weakness, arthralgia, or skin rash was not observed. The laboratory findings revealed decreased white blood cell count (2.7 × 10⁹/L, reference range [RR]: 3.3–8.6 × 10⁹/L) and low lymphocyte counts (0.5 × 10⁹/L, [RR]: 1.1–2.9 × 10⁹/L). The patient’s platelet count was 11.7 × 10⁴/µL (RR: 15.8–34.8 × 10⁴/µL), which indicated bicytopenia. The observed bicytopenia, especially the profound lymphopenia, suggested early-stage immune dysregulation associated with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection, although a preexisting subclinical autoimmune condition remained a possibility. The laboratory tests revealed elevated levels of C-reactive protein (5.97 [RR: 0.01–0.14] mg/dL), lactate dehydrogenase (LD) (369 [RR: 124–222] U/L), and ferritin (1,143.4 [RR: 4.6–204] ng/mL). However, the patient’s creatine kinase (84 [RR: 41–153] U/L), Krebs von den Lungen-6 (392 [RR: ≤ 499] U/mL), and D-dimer (0.6 [RR: ≤ 1.0] µg/mL) levels were normal. The β-D-glucan (14 [RR: < 20] pg/mL) and serum galactomannan (0.2 [RR: < 0.4] index) levels were normal. The patient tested positive for COVID-19 antigen in the nasopharyngeal swab examination (≥ 5,000 [RR: 0.01–0.99] pg/mL). No specific variant analysis was conducted; however, according to the national genomic surveillance data from the National Institute of Infectious Diseases in Japan, the KP.3 lineage and its sublineages were the predominant strains nationwide during the time our patient was admitted (August 2024). Chest radiography and computed tomography (CT) scan revealed ground-glass opacities and reticular opacities predominantly in the peripheral regions of the right and left lungs (Fig. 1). The patient was diagnosed with COVID-19 moderate severity (level I) on day 4 from symptom onset. Then, she was hospitalized because of advanced age and hypertension, which are risk factors for severe disease. Antiviral treatment with remdesivir (200 mg on the first day, followed by 100 mg every 24 h for 10 days) was started. Due to exertional hypoxemia (as evidenced by an SpO₂ in room air of 91%) on day 2, intravenous dexamethasone (6 mg every 24 h for 5 days) was added in accordance with the guidelines COVID-19 moderate severity (level II). The oxygen flow rate at rest was increased to 2 L/min. Hence, empirical antibacterial therapy with intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g every 24 h for 4 days) was initiated for possible secondary bacterial infection on day 3. Sputum culture at admission revealed only Staphylococcus epidermidis. With increased oxygen demand (oxygen flow rate at rest: 4 L/min), oral baricitinib (BAR) at a dose of 4 mg was added on day 5. Further, the patient’s D-dimer levels increased to 12.1 [RR: ≤ 1.0] µg/mL. Although imaging showed no evidence of venous thromboembolism, prophylactic subcutaneous heparin (5,000 U every 12 h) was initiated. On day 7, the patient’s SpO₂ decreased to approximately 90% (oxygen flow rate: 8 L/min), which required a switch to high-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy (oxygen flow rate: 40 L/min, fraction of inspired oxygen [FiO2]: 70%). The CS regimen was changed to intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy (1 g every 24 h for 3 days), followed by oral maintenance prednisolone at a dose of 45 mg (1 mg/kg) for 2 weeks. Subsequently, the dose was tapered to 35 mg. Biological agents were changed to intravenous tocilizumab (TCZ) 400 mg for 2 days. Antibiotic therapy was escalated to the broader-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam (4.5 g every 8 h for 9 days intravenously), following the detection of Escherichia coli (extended-spectrum beta-lactamase–producing and quinolone-resistant).

Despite such intensive treatment, the patient persistently presented with elevated LD and ferritin levels. She also had intermittent episodes of fever. As the standard COVID-19 therapies, including BAR and TCZ, were unsuccessful, an assessment for underlying autoimmune processes or opportunistic infections was performed. Therefore, autoantibody and fungal marker levels were tested on day 9. On day 12, oral candidiasis was observed; thus, oral itraconazole therapy was initiated. On day 13, both anti-ARS (31.2 [RR: < 24.9] U/mL) and anti-MDA5 (38 [RR: < 31] U/mL) antibodies were found to be positive. The aldolase levels were normal (6.7 [RR: 2.7–7.5] U/L), and the antinuclear antibodies were positive at a titer of 1:80 ([RR: < 1:40], speckled type: 1:80, anti-NuMA-1 antibody: 1:40). Regarding the classification criteria, the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology/American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) 2017 score was 3.4 points, and Sontheimer’s criteria for clinically amyopathic dermatomyositis were not met due to the absence of characteristic rashes. Pulmonary aspergillosis was suspected due to the elevated β-D-glucan (122 [RR: < 20] pg/mL) and serum galactomannan (2.8 [RR: < 0.4] index) levels. Due to severe respiratory failure and the high risk associated with bronchoscopy under high-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was not conducted. Consequently, itraconazole was continued to treat the oral candidiasis and address the emerging suspicion of CAPA. At this stage (day 13), although CAPA was clinically suspected, mycological evidence remained inconclusive; thus, itraconazole could not yet be immediately switched to first-line agents such as voriconazole or isavuconazole. Given the significant risk of cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A4-mediated drug–drug interactions (DDIs) between azoles and the patient’s concurrent immunosuppressant regimen—particularly the risk of tacrolimus (TAC) toxicity—itraconazole was continued under close monitoring while awaiting further diagnostic confirmation. Chest CT scan performed on the same day revealed peripheral opacities in both lungs, partially consolidated, with mild collapse of the right lung (Fig. 3A). Due to the absence of a suitable puncture site, thoracic drainage was not carried out at this time, and the patient was managed conservatively with rest and monitoring. With consideration of worsening Aspergillus infection in a significantly immunosuppressed state, only oral TAC at a dose of 3 mg was added as an immunosuppressant. On day 16, a follow-up CT scan showed that the consolidation in both lungs had further progressed, and mediastinal emphysema was newly detected (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, due to worsening of the right secondary pneumothorax, chest tube drainage was performed on the same day (Fig. 4A). Persistently elevated LD levels and worsening pulmonary opacities prompted repeat testing, which showed further increases in β-D-glucan (257 [RR: < 20] pg/mL) and serum galactomannan (3.7 [RR: < 0.4] index) levels. Aspergillus fumigatus was also detected in the sputum culture. Considering the progressive elevation of fungal biomarker levels and clinical deterioration, antifungal therapy was switched to intravenous micafungin (150 mg every 24 h for 6 days) on day 23. Micafungin was selected because it avoids the complex CYP3A4-mediated metabolic pathways associated with voriconazole, thereby providing robust antifungal coverage while minimizing the risk of unpredictable fluctuations in the blood levels of concurrent TAC and BAR. The patient was diagnosed with “probable CAPA” based on the 2021 European Confederation of Medical Mycology/International Society for Human and Animal Mycology (ECMM/ISHAM) consensus criteria [8]. The Entry criterion was met because of a high-titer SARS-CoV-2 antigen positivity upon admission. Given the presence of refractory fever and the development of new pulmonary infiltrates on CT despite standard COVID-19 management, the Clinical/imaging criterion was also met. Finally, the patient achieved the Mycological criterion because her serum galactomannan index was significantly elevated (2.8; threshold > 0.5). Although BAL was not performed, the combination of these three components (Entry, Clinical/imaging, and Mycological) is sufficient for a “probable CAPA” classification based on the ECMM/ISHAM algorithm. Two consecutive positive results for serum β-D-glucan and Aspergillus fumigatus isolation from sputum culture further strengthen the diagnosis. The patient’s oxygen saturation increased as evidenced by the FiO2 decreasing from 60% to 35%–40%, and the pneumothorax also improved, leading to chest tube removal on day 26 (Fig. 4B). However, sudden chest pain and hypoxemia requiring a FiO2 of 100% developed on day 29, indicating recurrence of right pneumothorax. Although the thoracic drainage was reinserted (Fig. 4C), the patient’s general condition continued to deteriorate, and she died of progressive respiratory failure on day 29.

Discussion and conclusions

The coexistence of dual-positive anti-ARS and anti-MDA5 antibodies in the setting of severe COVID-19 is an exceptionally rare and therapeutically challenging clinical scenario.

Dual positivity for MSAs is extremely uncommon, as anti-ARS and anti-MDA5 antibodies are typically mutually exclusive. Including our case, a total of 15 cases of dual-positive anti-ARS and anti-MDA5 antibodies were identified and summarized (Table 2) [4, 9–16]. Anti-MDA5-positive RP-ILD is generally associated with a poor prognosis [17–19]. As summarized in the matrix, most patients (71.4%) survived following intensive triple therapy (CS, calcineurin inhibitors, and intravenous cyclophosphamide [IVCY]), whereas our patient followed a fatal course despite multimodal treatment. Several factors distinguish this case from prior reports. First, the patient (77 years) was significantly older than the median age of previous cases (51 years), aligning with advanced age (≥ 60 years) as an independent predictor of reduced survival in PM/DM-ILD [11]. Second, although we did not identify the specific anti-ARS subtype, the absence of clinical or laboratory evidence of myositis and the exceptionally rapidly progressive clinical course of ILD clinically confirm non-Jo-1 antibodies (anti-PL-7, anti-PL-12, or anti-KS). According to a large-scale study of Japanese patients, compared with the anti-Jo-1 subtype, the non-Jo-1 subtypes are more frequently associated with amyopathic phenotypes and a higher risk of severe or refractory ILD [20]. Our patient’s clinical course, characterized by rapid pulmonary deterioration and the absence of myositis, strongly reflects the phenotype typically observed in patients positive for non-Jo-1 ARS antibodies as described in these reports. Third, despite the possible theoretical representation of overlapping antibody detection as technical cross-reactivity, the high titers of both antibodies (anti-ARS, 31.2 [RR: < 24.9] U/mL and anti-MDA5 38 [RR: < 31) U/mL) detected via well-validated specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays suggest a legitimate overlapping autoimmune response rather than nonspecific binding. Most importantly, the quadruple burden of advanced age, COVID-19, dual antibody positivity, and CAPA complication created a unique therapeutic challenge. Unlike many previously reported survivors with dual antibody positivity who received IVCY, our patient was ineligible for IVCY because of active CAPA and the cumulative immunosuppressive effects of recent biologic use (BAR and TCZ). This observation underscores that CAPA coexistence not only worsens the respiratory status but also severely restricts the use of intensive immunosuppressants required to control refractory RP-ILD.

The clinicopathological similarities between severe COVID-19 and anti-MDA5-positive RP-ILD, characterized by cytokine storms and hyperferritinemia, are well documented [6, 7]. In the present case, we cannot confirm whether the dual autoantibody positivity existed subclinically before infection or was newly induced. However, given the complete absence of preexisting symptoms and the rapid clinical deterioration following COVID-19, the viral infection could be the critical trigger to induce or unmask this specific autoimmune phenotype—a phenomenon increasingly recognized as “COVID-19–induced autoimmunity” [20–23]. Potential mechanisms include molecular mimicry between SARS-CoV-2 proteins and human autoantigens, or the “unmasking” of a subclinical autoimmune predisposition via a cytokine storm–induced inflammatory milieu. Specifically, MDA5 acts as a cytoplasmic sensor for viral RNA; therefore, its overexpression during SARS-CoV-2 infection or following mRNA vaccination may break immune tolerance in susceptible individuals, potentially causing MSA production [20–23]. In the current case, standard COVID-19 therapies, including BAR and TCZ, were infective, prompting us to investigate these underlying autoimmune processes. This investigation ultimately revealed the dual-positive status that drove the refractory RP-ILD. According to these features, our case is best characterized as a “clinically amyopathic” phenotype within the dermatomyositis spectrum, where the viral trigger manifested predominantly as isolated, life-threatening lung involvement.

The management of this case was severely inhibited by CAPA. Intensive triple therapy is a widely accepted standard treatment approach for RP-ILD [24]. However, due to active CAPA and the recent administration of BAR and TCZ, the initiation of IVCY was considered deleterious. Furthermore, our antifungal strategy was carefully tailored, balancing diagnostic certainty with pharmacological safety. Initially (day 13), although CAPA was suspected, mycological evidence remained inconclusive to mandate an immediate switch to first-line agents. Given the patient’s critical status, we continued itraconazole while awaiting further diagnostic confirmation, avoiding the introduction of complex pharmacodynamic variables prematurely. By day 23, once “probable CAPA” diagnosis was confirmed using multiple markers, we escalated the antibiotic therapy. The primary constraint in selecting an agent was the high risk of cytochrome P450 3A4-mediated DDIs between azoles and the patient’s existing immunosuppressants. Voriconazole, a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor, poses a severe risk of TAC toxicity—a critical concern considering TAC’s narrow therapeutic window and the patient’s precarious renal and hepatic status. Additionally, potential interactions with BAR and TCZ created an unacceptably high risk of metabolic instability. Although isavuconazole causes fewer DDIs, it was not included in our hospital’s formulary for immediate use during patient’s treatment. Consequently, we selected micafungin as the most clinically sound alternative, providing robust antifungal coverage while lowering the risk of developing systemic toxicity and unpredictable fluctuations in the blood levels of concurrent medications. This therapeutic dilemma—the need for intensive immunosuppression versus the risk of fatal fungal dissemination—is a hallmark of managing virus-induced autoimmune ILD. The original ECMM/ISHAM criteria for CAPA were primarily developed for patients in the intensive care unit. Nevertheless, our patient fulfilled most of the clinical and mycological components of probable pulmonary aspergillosis. In this case, the intensive care unit criteria for aspergillosis were met, supporting our CAPA diagnosis, which was pivotal in our therapeutic decision-making. Although the patient lacked classic host factors for invasive aspergillosis, fulfillment of these specific diagnostic criteria provided a robust clinical framework justifying the prompt initiation of targeted antifungal therapy. This classification significantly influenced our clinical approach: we recognized that CAPA and RP-ILD coexistence posed an exceptionally high mortality risk. Consequently, we managed CAPA aggressively while simultaneously navigating the challenging balance of initiating immunosuppression for RP-ILD. Through this rigorous adherence to diagnostic criteria, we were able to optimize our therapeutic strategy despite the complex clinical dilemmas. However, we must acknowledge the diagnostic limitations of relying solely on sputum cultures and serum biomarkers. In patients without neutropenia or admitted to the intensive care unit, serum β-D-glucan and galactomannan are generally less sensitive and specific than BAL-based testing [8]. Sputum isolates of Aspergillus may represent airway colonization rather than invasive disease, and β-D-glucan testing can yield false-positive results due to various clinical factors. Although BAL was clinically prohibitive in our patient, these diagnostic limitations should be considered when interpreting CAPA probability in similar clinical settings.

Spontaneous pneumothorax and pneumomediastinum are poor prognostic indicators in both anti-MDA5-positive ILD (17.6%) and severe COVID-19 (3%) [25, 26]. The mechanisms include the Macklin effect, where alveolar pressure ruptures the fragile parenchyma damaged by cytokine storms or traction fibrosis [27–30]. In our case, the patient developed recurrent and refractory pneumothorax within 1 month of onset, reflecting severe alveolar destruction. Such mechanical complications, particularly when combined with secondary infection, significantly increase mortality and often represent the terminal stage of the disease.

This study has several limitations. First, early CS therapy for COVID-19 might have masked the physical signs of PM/DM, thereby delaying MSA screening. A more proactive screening for MSAs could have been considered if subtle physical findings had been detectable, potentially allowing for earlier intervention. Second, definitive diagnostic procedures for myositis, such as electromyography and magnetic resonance imaging findings, were not performed. Due to the patient’s critical respiratory status and the requirement for continuous high-flow oxygen therapy, the risks associated with transport and prolonged positioning for these examinations were considered clinically prohibitive. Third, the treatment for RP-ILD was limited. CS pulse therapy and TAC were initiated. However, IVCY was withheld due to active CAPA and the cumulative immunosuppressive effects of BAR and TCZ. Adjunctive therapies such as intravenous immunoglobulin were also not implemented. Fourth, antifungal therapy was selected based on a cautious assessment of potential DDIs, particularly the risk of CYP34A inhibition affecting TAC levels. Although agents such as isavuconazole may be considered in similar scenarios, therapeutic decisions must balance antifungal potency with pharmacological safety in critically ill, immunosuppressed patients. Finally, the specific anti-ARS subtype was not determined. However, as discussed, the clinicoradiological features—notably the lack of myositis and the rapid disease progression—strongly reflect a non-Jo-1 antibody subtype. The potential presence of this antibody, alongside anti-MDA5, likely underpinned RP-ILD’s overwhelming and refractory nature in this case.

Ultimately, the fatal outcome was attributed to the convergence of four critical prognostic factors: advanced age, MSA dual positivity, the physiological stress of COVID-19, and the complication of CAPA. This quadruple burden created an overwhelming clinical scenario where aggressive immunosuppression required for RP-ILD inevitably increased susceptibility to severe opportunistic infection.

In conclusion, this case underscores the importance of MSA screening in treatment-refractory COVID-19. Balancing intensive immunosuppression for RP-ILD with the risk of fatal infections such as CAPA while cautiously managing complex DDIs remains a critical challenge that requires early recognition, multidisciplinary coordination, and individualized risk assessment. Clinicians should be aware that initial bicytopenia and refractory RP-ILD may be early clinical indicators of an underlying, subclinical autoimmune state in patients with severe COVID-19, even without overt myositis.
