Public awareness of cardiovascular disease and its risk factors in Palestine: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Mohammad Yaser Hasan Awad, Bashar Yaser Hasan Awad, Haya Jebreen Mohammed Warasna, Farah Bilal Yousef Shahin, Maya Sabboh, Afnan W.M. Jobran, Tarek A. Owais
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVD) are the leading global cause of mortality, projected to account for 23.3 million deaths annually by 2030, presenting a significant public health burden. While largely preventable through addressing modifiable risk factors, low-resource settings like Palestine face a disproportionate CVD burden. Public awareness of CVD risk factors and symptoms is therefore essential for mitigating this burden. This study aimed to assess general knowledge of CVD among Palestinians and identify factors influencing awareness to inform community health programs.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 410 Palestinian in the West Bank from March to May 2024. Participants were recruited from ten cities, and a validated questionnaire adapted from a prior study was used. The survey collected demographic data, clinical characteristics, and knowledge of CVD risk factors and symptoms as well as attitudes towards prevention services. R Statistical Software was used for data analysis which included descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and logistic regression to explore the relationships between demographic factors and CVD knowledge, whereby a p-value of ≤ 0.05 was set up as a significance level.ResultsAmong the 410 participants 45% of participants reported no physical activity, 40% of the respondents reported that their lifestyle was either highly stressful or stressful. CVD knowledge varied, with 67% identifying four or more CVD types, 35% knowing all five heart attack symptoms, and 49% recognizing stroke symptoms. Additionally, 66% of the participants identified key CVD risk factors. Over 75% of the participants expressed interest in using prevention services, such as blood pressure measurement (84%) and smoking cessation counseling (75%) at local pharmacies.ConclusionThis study highlights moderate awareness of cardiovascular health among adults in the West Bank but identifies significant knowledge gaps regarding key risk factors such as hypertension, smoking, and inactivity. The prevalence of these modifiable risk factors underscores the need for targeted public health intervention. Promoting education and healthier lifestyle choices could reduce cardiovascular disease risks in the West Bank, warranting further research to assess the effectiveness of these efforts and explore sociocultural barriers to improved awareness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25734-1.

Background

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are conditions that affect the circulatory system and include but are not limited to coronary heart disease (CHD), cerebrovascular disease, peripheral arterial disease, rheumatic heart disease, congenital heart disease, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism [1, 2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that CVD accounts for the greatest number of fatalities worldwide among all diseases [1] and is a subset of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) that account for the majority of deaths globally. In 2008, CVD accounted for 30% (17.5 million) of all deaths worldwide. Of these, 6.2 million deaths were attributable to stroke and 7.3 million to CHD. By 2030, this number is predicted to rise to 23.3 million. Because CVD has a major influence on health and is expected to increase, its worldwide burden is a serious concern [1].

CVD is considered one of the leading preventable causes of mortality worldwide because most of its risk factors, such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, obesity, smoking, inactivity, stress, poor eating habits, and diabetes, can be controlled or prevented [3]. Public knowledge of CVD and its modifiable risk factors is essential to empower individuals to adopt healthier attitudes, behaviors, and lifestyle choices [4].

Various studies have shown differences in the occurrence of heart attack symptoms between men and women [5–7]. Women often experience prodromal symptoms, such as fatigue and breathlessness, before a heart attack, which can differ from the typical chest pain experienced by men [8]. This difference in symptom presentation can delay the seeking of medical care among women compared to men [9]. Healthcare providers should be aware of these variations and maintain a high suspicion of heart attacks, especially in patients with known risk factors, even when symptoms are atypical.

Improving knowledge of heart attack and stroke symptoms can lead to better patient outcomes, whereas understanding CVD risk factors empowers individuals to reduce their risk [10]. Addressing risk factors is crucial in combating CVD, especially in developing countries, where awareness plays a significant role in promoting healthy behaviors and reducing complications. This is particularly important for women, whose awareness of heart disease is often lacking, leading to higher mortality rates [11, 12]. Studies show that only 44% of U.S. women recognize heart disease as their leading cause of death (down from 65% in 2009), with young women, Black women, and Latinas exhibiting the lowest awareness [12]. This knowledge deficit contributes to delayed care-seeking and 55% lower cardiac rehabilitation referrals, exacerbating the mortality rate [13, 14]. In Palestine, CVD accounts for 37.6% of deaths (2019 MOH data); however, to our knowledge, no study has assessed public awareness of CVD symptoms or risk factors [15]. Given the unique sociopolitical circumstances in the West Bank, accessibility to healthcare services is often affected by movement restrictions and fragmented healthcare systems. Existing research highlights barriers to secondary prevention, including checkpoints and healthcare fragmentation [15]. However, primary prevention remains unaddressed. In the Palestinian context, checkpoints refer to physical barriers and security crossings that restrict movement between cities and regions in the West Bank. These checkpoints often limit patients’ access to healthcare facilities and continuity of care, contributing to delays in diagnosis and management.

Methods

Study design, setting, and period

A cross-sectional survey with a descriptive approach was conducted to assess the level of knowledge of CVD and its risk factors among Palestinian individuals aged 18 years and older in the West Bank, Palestine. The study population comprised individuals from ten cities in the West Bank: Hebron, Jerusalem, Ramallah, Jenin, Tulkarm, Bethlehem, Nablus, Salfit, Qalqilya, and Jericho. The data collection period was from March to May 2024.

Aim

This study aimed to assess general knowledge of CVD among Palestinians and identify factors influencing awareness to inform community health programs.

Participants

Palestinian individuals aged 18 years and older residing in the West Bank were eligible for inclusion in the study. In contrast, individuals younger than 18 years, those living outside the West Bank, and those who declined participation by selecting ‘No’ in the consent question were excluded. A total of 410 participants were recruited based on a sample size calculation using the Raosoft online calculator [16], assuming a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and 50% response distribution. The population size, sourced from the Palestinian Central Bureau of Statistics (PCBS), was approximately 3,250,000 as of July 11, 2023 [17].

Research instruments

This study used a validated, standardized questionnaire derived from prior research conducted in Kuwait [18]. The questionnaire was translated from English to Arabic by healthcare professionals and subsequently transformed into an online format to facilitate population access and streamline the categorization and organization of data. The online questionnaire was shared via various social media platforms, including Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and email. To ensure ethical and transparent recruitment, invitations were posted as public announcements on social media and in community WhatsApp groups with no direct messaging of individuals. A general invitation email was distributed to students by the university administration, with no direct access to the email addresses by the research team. All participation was voluntary and contingent upon explicit informed consent via the official survey link, adhering to the approved ethical protocol. This multi-platform approach was adopted to maximize reach and facilitate diverse recruitment by utilizing available, popular media capable of quickly accessing various population segments. The questionnaire, which was pre-tested, consisted of 27 closed-ended questions divided into four sections. The first section contained 17 questions that collected data on the participants demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, sex, marital status, education, occupation, residence, monthly income, personal health, height, weight, smoking, exercise, healthy food, lifestyle, and family history of CVD. The second section had four questions about the participants’ health conditions, including any chronic diseases, medications, and recent readings of blood pressure, blood cholesterol, and blood glucose, as well as the last time they checked these indicators. The third section had four questions that assessed knowledge of six CVD types, five heart attack symptoms, five stroke symptoms, and nine CVD risk factors. The fourth section had two questions that explored the participants’ opinions on the role of pharmacists and nurses in CVD prevention and management and their willingness to use CVD prevention services in community pharmacies.

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Palestine Polytechnic University (Ref No. KA/41/2024/2). This study complied with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration. Participants were informed of the study’s objectives, right to withdraw, privacy protection, and data confidentiality on the initial page. Electronic informed consent was obtained at the initiation of the questionnaire, allowing participants to decline participation by clicking the ‘No’ option. Once the participants agreed, they completed an online questionnaire.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the R Statistical Software (version 4.1.3; R Core Team, 2022). The median and interquartile range (IQR) for each numerical variable were calculated. Descriptive statistics for categorical variables were presented as frequency distributions and percentages. The demographic variables of the respondents regarding their knowledge levels were compared using cross-tabulation with a chi-squared test. However, to conduct a more detailed analysis, we used binary logistic regression at the univariate and multivariate levels. This provided an opportunity to detect and compare the odds ratios (OR) and adjusted odds ratios (aOR) of the significant variables concerning knowledge levels. We set a p-value of 0.05 or lower as the threshold for statistical significance.

Knowledge of CVD types, symptoms, and risk factors

CVD types

The median score for knowledge of the six types of CVD among participants was 4, with 67% recognizing four or more types. CHD (88%) was the most known type of CVD, followed by congenital heart disease (78%).

Heart attack symptoms

The median score for knowledge of the five heart attack symptoms was 4. Chest pain or discomfort was the most frequently mentioned heart attack symptom with 92% participants reporting it, followed by 88% who stated “difficulty breathing or shortness of breath,” 73% " Feeling weak, lightheaded, or faint, as well as Pain or stiffness in the arms or shoulder.”

Stroke symptoms

In terms of knowing the five symptoms of stroke, the median was 4: 49% recognized five symptoms, the most known symptom among them was loss of balance or coordination (83%), followed by sudden difficulty seeing in one or both eyes (81%).

Risk factors

The respondents’ knowledge of CVD risk factors was better than their knowledge of CVD types and symptoms of heart attack or stroke. In terms of knowledge about the nine CVD risk factors, the score for the median was 9.0:3.7% did not know any CVD risk factors, 6.7% marked one to four, 24.2% five to seven, and 66.0% identified eight or nine risk factors. The most common risk factors were smoking, obesity, positive family history of CVD, hypertension, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, and high LDL and cholesterol levels. Overall, CVD knowledge was assessed as a continuous variable. Answers to the 25 questions regarding the six types of CVD, five symptoms of heart attack, five symptoms of stroke, and nine CVD risk factors were scored as correct or incorrect, and participants received one point for each correct response. Overall, CVD knowledge was moderate to high (median score 20/25), with 56.6% scoring ≥ 20 correct answers.

Regression analysis

In the multiple logistic regression model, we found that those aged 21–30 years had higher odds of having better CVD knowledge (aOR = 4.28, 95% Cl: 2.44–7.62, p < 0.001) than those aged less than 20 years. Employment status analysis showed that being unemployed (aOR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.02–0.64, p = 0.015) or self-employed (aOR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.03–0.57, p = 0.009) was significantly associated with lower odds of gaining knowledge about CVD than those who worked in the government sector. Housewives (aOR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.05–0.75, p = 0.019) and private sector employees (aOR = 0.18, 95% Cl: 0.04–0.75, p = 0.021) had lower odds of having better CVD knowledge than government sector workers. Notably, current smokers had 66% lower odds of having high CVD knowledge (aOR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.18–0.65, p = 0.001), while those with a family history of CVD had 2.3-fold higher odds.

It was found that people with a chronic disease had significantly lower odds of acquiring higher CVD knowledge (aOR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.15–0.78, p = 0.012) than those without a chronic disease. A lack of daily consumption of healthy foods was also associated with lower odds of having higher CVD knowledge (aOR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.35–0.92, p = 0.022) compared to those who consumed healthy foods daily. Other factors, such as gender identity, monthly income, BMI categories, and educational level, were insignificant variables for CVD knowledge. In the univariate analysis, it was determined that having a university or postgraduate degree led to higher odds of having more knowledge of CVD (OR = 3.05, 95% Cl: 1.62–6.00.62.00, p = 0.001) than those who only completed a pre-university education. Pharmacy-based prevention intent

Respondents were asked about their intentions regarding CVD prevention services in their local pharmacies. Among the surveyed participants, more than three-quarters agreed to the possibility of using services, such as weight measurement 363 (89%), blood pressure measurement 346 (84%), and advice on a healthy diet 321 (78%).

Discussion

This study sheds light on the current status of perceptions and attitudes towards CVD in the West Bank. Of the participants, 67% recognized four or more types of CVD. Participants demonstrated fairly good knowledge of heart attack and stroke symptoms, with 35% and 49%, respectively, recognizing all five main symptoms of each. Chest pain was the most frequently acknowledged heart attack symptom (92%), while sudden dizziness, difficulty walking, and loss of balance or coordination were the most common symptoms of stroke (83%). Knowledge of risk factors surpassed that of symptoms, with smoking being the most identified risk factor for CVD. Knowledge tended to be higher among younger adults aged 21–30, non-smokers, and those with a family history of CVD, while it was lower among individuals with chronic illnesses, unhealthy diets, or those who were unemployed or self-employed.

Among the surveyed participants, more than three-quarters agreed to the possibility of using CVD prevention services if they were provided in their local pharmacies, such as weight measurement (89%), blood pressure measurement (84%), and blood glucose measurement (84%). The public was not as knowledgeable about the less intuitive symptoms of heart attacks, such as pain or discomfort in the jaw, neck, or back. Risk factors such as stress and diabetes were also not commonly known. Participants were less welcoming of the idea of smoking cessation advice, possibly because they were unsure of what it might entail.

NCDs are the main cause of death worldwide, a trend that has made its way into low and middle-income countries, which are ill-equipped to face such complex public health issues [15]. Previous studies have examined some aspects of CVD in the West Bank [19–22]. However, this study is the most comprehensive to date, as it measures awareness of CVD types, risk factors, and warning symptoms of heart attack and stroke, as well as participants’ feelings towards nurses and pharmacists regarding the help they offer in CVD prevention. The relevance of this analysis comes from the fact that ischemic heart disease causes 25.3% of all deaths in the West Bank, making it the most prevalent cause of mortality. Cerebrovascular and hypertensive heart diseases are among the top ten causes of death in the West Bank [23]. All three of the previous conditions are largely dependent on modifiable risk factors, and Séguro et al. confirmed the logical correlation between ignorance of cardiovascular preventive measures and mortality rates [24], indicating that therapeutic education is key to lowering morbidity and mortality rates. The public’s knowledge of and attitude towards CVD have been studied in many countries worldwide [18, 25–41]. This study allows us to see how the West Bank compares to these countries and estimates the effect of conflict on CVD knowledge. This also makes it possible to study knowledge trends over time. For example, the results showed an increased awareness of CVD risk factors and stroke symptoms compared to the results of a master’s thesis conducted 10 years ago on the population of the West Bank [21].

Most participants (67%) recognized four or more types of CVD, which is higher than the results recorded in Kuwait [18] and Bangladesh [25]. The population of the West Bank best identified CHD and congenital heart disease as types of CVD, which is probably the case because the public associates CVD with conditions relating to the heart only, as seen in Kuwait [18] and Bangladesh [25].

The participants had fairly good knowledge of heart attack symptoms. The most recognized symptom was chest pain or discomfort, similar to that in most other countries. A total of 92% of the respondents recognized this typical symptom, similar to what is seen in the USA [27], but lower than that in Germany [28] and higher than that in China [29], Ireland [30], Kuwait [18], Malaysia [33], Iran [31], Bangladesh [25], and Ghana [32]. Difficulty in breathing or shortness of breath was the second most recognized symptom (88%), similar to the US [27] but higher than that in most other countries [18, 25, 28–30, 32, 33]. 73% knew that pain or discomfort in the arms or shoulder could be an heart attack symptom that is relatively high compared to many other countries [18, 25, 29, 31, 33], but lower than the rates recorded in the USA [27], Germany [28], and Syria [35]. 73% reported that a heart attack can manifest as feeling weak light-headed or faint, which is similar to Syria [35], lower than in the USA [27] and Germany [28], but higher than most compared other countries [18, 25, 29, 33, 34]. The least-known symptom in the West Bank and almost every other country [18, 27, 28, 33–35] is pain or discomfort in the jaw, neck, or back. In the West Bank, almost half of the participants recognized it, which is higher than in Kuwait [18], Syria [35], Malaysia [33], Tanzania [34], and Bangladesh [25]. The only countries where this symptom was recognized by more than half of the population were the USA [27] and Lebanon [40]. The cornerstone of managing myocardial infarction is the rapid restoration of blood flow [42], which means that any hesitance in accessing medical help could cause irreparable damage or even death. Therefore, spreading information on the different manifestations of heat attacks, with a focus on atypical lesser-known symptoms, is crucial.

Awareness of stroke symptoms was decent but could be improved. The most identified symptom was sudden dizziness, trouble walking, or loss of balance or coordination, with 83% of the participants recognizing it, which is similar to the results in Jordan [41] but higher than those in the UAE [37], Kuwait [18], Ireland [30], Tanzania [34], Thailand [39], and Spain [36]. A total of 81% identified sudden trouble seeing in one or both eyes, which is similar to the results of the study by Jordan [41] but higher than those of many other studies [18, 25, 29, 30, 36, 38, 39]. Both sudden numbness or weakness of the face, arm, or leg and sudden confusion or trouble speaking or understanding others were identified by 80% of respondents, which is lower than in Jordan [41] but higher than in many other countries [18, 25, 29, 30, 36, 38, 39]. The least recognized stroke manifestation was severe headache with no known cause; only 67% identified it, which was also the least recognized in Jordan [41] and Bangladesh [25]. As with heart attacks, knowledge of the early symptoms of stroke is essential because the prognosis is largely dependent on the rapidity of the intervention. Delay in presenting to the hospital is a multi-layered issue in the West Bank, considering the obstacles in accessing healthcare, from checkpoints to the reluctance to seek medical care due to financial reasons. Achieving proper results requires systematic changes to life in West Bank [43, 44]. However, from a health point of view, raising awareness of the early symptoms of heart attack and stroke will help patients and their families recognize when to access healthcare facilities, thereby lowering the morbidity rates of these two diseases.

Participants’ knowledge of risk factors was better than that in other countries. For example, 93% were aware that smoking causes CVD, which is similar to Lebanon [40] and Ghana [32], higher than in Saudi Arabia [38], Kuwait [18], Ireland [30], Jordan [41], Tanzania [34], and Bangladesh [25], but lower than in Syria [35] and Iran [31]. Only 19% reported being smokers. This indicates that knowledge and attitude are correlated with smoking. However, laws and campaigns that limit the use of tobacco should be improved and updated, especially with new threats on the rise, such as e-cigarettes, which are reportedly used by 19.7% of university students in Palestine [45].

86% recognized obesity as a risk factor for CVD which is higher than Saudi [38], Lebanon [40], Jordan [41], Ghana [32], and Bangladesh [25]. However, it is lower than that of Kuwait [18], Syria [35], and Iran [31]. Of the participants, 34.9% reported being overweight or obese. Several studies have reported that obesity is becoming more prevalent in the Middle East [46], with people adopting a more Westernized diet and a sedentary lifestyle [47]. In this study, 85% reported being aware that family history is a risk factor for CVD, which is high compared to other countries such as Kuwait [18], Lebanon [40], Ireland [30], Syria [35], Iran [31], and Ghana [32]. Of the participants, 85% were aware that hypertension was a risk factor for CVD, with only 7.6% of participants reporting being hypertensive; however, the actual prevalence of hypertension is most likely higher, since a previous study in Palestine reported that only 51.0% of people with hypertension were aware of their diagnosis [22]. An unhealthy diet and physical inactivity were similarly recognized by 84% of the participants. These two factors are largely impacted by occupation, as land confiscation prohibits farmers from producing local healthy food for their communities [43], and the prevalence of poverty means people have less freedom in choosing healthy options, especially with growing food insecurity [48]. On the other hand, the lack of safe walkable spaces where people can walk or exercise freely can pose an obstacle to physical activity [43]. A total of 79% were aware of hypercholesterolemia as a risk factor, which is less than in Lebanon [40], Syria [35], and Jordan [41], but higher than in Kuwait [18], Tanzania [34], Bangladesh [25], and Ghana [32]. 77% recognized stress as a risk factor, which was relatively low compared to the knowledge of other factors. Recognizing the effect of stress on CVD is of major importance in areas with high levels of mental distress. Losing access to food, medicine, and safe spaces not only directly affects diet and physical health, but it also makes an individual stuck in a loss spiral where losing resources causes stress, which leads to more loss of resources [49]. Stress has both acute and chronic effects on cardiovascular health; it can also impact health in indirect ways, as it is associated with adopting unhealthy behaviors such as smoking and health negligence [50]. 67% of individuals named diabetes as a risk factor which is lower than in other Levantine countries [35, 40, 41]. But higher than in Kuwait [18], Saudi [38], and Bangladesh [25]. In almost all of the aforementioned countries, diabetes seems to be less recognized than the other risk factors which is alarming considering the increasing numbers of diabetic patients [51]. Possible solutions to increase cardiovascular awareness are known and have been mentioned in many studies [18]. They include creating media campaigns, sharing healthcare professionals’ expertise, executing current laws, and implementing new ones to make sure the public is aware of the dangers of smoking, processed foods, and physical inactivity on cardiovascular health. When it comes to the West Bank, these campaigns should focus on educating individuals on diabetes and stress since they were the two least known factors. However, experts from the West Bank were only able to suggest limited solutions since cultivating tangible results requires radical effort [15].

Blood cholesterol was the least measured risk factor, almost half of the participants had never checked their blood cholesterol, possibly because it is not available as a rapid affordable test as is the case with blood glucose, blood pressure, and weight. Almost half of the participants reported not exercising at all. Two-thirds did not eat healthy food every day and only 8.3% reported living a life free from stress. Attitudes towards taking proper medication were poor, with less than 20% taking medication for conditions considered as risk factors for heart attack and stroke. Knowledge of risk factors is not translating into healthy attitudes in these areas, which could either be because individuals don’t view themselves as being susceptible to CVD or because there are obstacles prohibiting them from adopting the behaviors they believe they should be doing. A number of these obstacles can be traced back to the conflict. For example, living a life free of stress is simply unattainable under occupation [43]. So is exercising and choosing the foods one wishes, especially considering how dependent the economy is on foreign aid, which does not allow for sustainable community building [49]. Another major obstacle is accessing healthcare facilities to undergo preventive or therapeutic measures, as getting adequate health services is complicated by segregation and poverty [43].

Our analysis found that those who worked government jobs had more CVD knowledge than those who were unemployed, self-employed, working in the private sector, and housewives. Other studies have found that working correlates with better health literacy, possibly because of the skills one learns on the job related to using the web and processing information [52], as well as learning from colleagues’ experiences. We also found that people aged 21–30 had higher awareness levels of CVD. This could be explained by the fact that this demographic is getting an academic education and is more familiar with using the internet, which could be a viable source of information. Healthcare organizations should make use of this as targeting the younger population to prevent metabolic syndrome and its consequent CVD before it happens is a better plan than trying to cure it after [46]. People with a family history of CVD had better knowledge of the matter, possibly because they feel like they are more at risk, in addition to the information they have gained from doctor visits with their relatives. This result is similar to that seen in Kuwait [18] and Cameroon [26]. There is some correlation between knowledge and attitude when it comes to modifiable risk factors, for example, non-smokers and people on healthier diets had better CVD knowledge. Finally, even though biological sex often benefits females in CVD, with premenopausal women showing protection from obstructive CHD [53] there was no significant difference in knowledge between men and women.

Medical doctors’ role in helping people reduce their cardiovascular risk is essential. However, accessing doctors can be difficult compared to nurses and pharmacists, especially in rural areas. Another issue is that hospital discharge planning is of low quality, meaning that patients who undergo treatment for any CVD may face difficulties in changing their lifestyles [54]. When asked about their preferred healthcare provider alongside doctors, most participants considered visiting a nurse over a pharmacist to offer advice on a healthy diet, exercise, and smoking cessation, as well as measuring blood pressure, glucose, and cholesterol. The participants were most reluctant to take advice on a healthy diet and exercise. However, they recognized pharmacists’ role in managing prescribed medications. Pharmacists are the first point of care for many, and one systematic review found that the majority of community pharmacists’ interventions have favorable outcomes [55]. Therefore, measures should be taken to amplify pharmacists’ roles in the West Bank, such as offering educational materials to distribute, proper training to better communicate with patients, and a funding model that allows pharmacists to provide a wider array of services [56, 57].

Participants were enthusiastic about benefiting from healthcare services if they were provided in local pharmacies, especially weight and blood pressure measurements. This may be due to the prevalence of community pharmacies, especially in the context of a shortage of specialized doctors [44].

This study explored the general public’s knowledge of CVD. These results will allow future interventions to be more precise in achieving the community’s needs. Future studies should analyze the public’s attitudes and lifestyles more thoroughly and include more diverse demographics. Based on our findings, we suggest that future campaigns should focus on less-known symptoms of heart attacks and strokes, the importance of adhering to a healthy lifestyle, and how to achieve it in a conflict zone such as the West Bank, where resources are limited and stress is inevitable. These campaigns should target demographics with lower knowledge scores, such as unemployed and self-employed people.

Conclusion

This study provides profound insights into the awareness rates of the cardiovascular health and risk factors associated with them among adults in the West Bank. This study shows that people in the region are moderately aware of cardiovascular health, but they are not fully informed about factors such as high blood pressure, smoking, and physical inactivity that may cause the disease. In addition, the study also indicates that a high level of modifiable risk of disease signals the need for immediate public health interventions in the area. A better education system and advocating for healthier life choices by individuals can significantly reduce the workload of CVD in the West Bank. Thus, the following research should involve the long-term effect that these interventions will have on the people and the understanding of the social and cultural issues impeding the awareness of cardiovascular health.
