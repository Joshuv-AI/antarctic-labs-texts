Artificial Intelligence–Mediated Teledentistry for Ageing Populations: Toward Intelligent Intermediary Support in Home-Based Oral Care
Authors: Seyed Ali Mosaddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid ageing of the global population is reshaping oral health care delivery, with increasing numbers of older adults receiving care in home-based or dependent settings where access to dental services remains limited. Although teledentistry has emerged as a potential solution, its current implementation is constrained by passive communication models, variable diagnostic reliability, and limited usability among elderly patients. Evidence suggests that the effectiveness of remote dental assessment depends less on the communication modality than on image quality, patient guidance, and clinician–patient interaction. In parallel, artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated expanding roles in elderly health care as a supervisor, communicator, and decision support system, yet its integration into dentistry remains largely limited to diagnostics. This Perspective proposes a shift in which AI functions as an intelligent intermediary, structuring communication, guiding data acquisition, and supporting clinical triage. Despite challenges in adoption and ethics, AI-mediated teledentistry may enable a transition toward patient-centred oral health care.Key words: Telemedicine, Artificial intelligence, Geriatric dentistry, Aged, Home care services, Oral health

The unmet need: Oral care beyond the clinic

The global demographic shift toward an ageing population is fundamentally reshaping the landscape of oral health care.1 Globally, population ageing is accelerating. By 2030, 1 in 6 individuals worldwide will be 60 years or older, and the number of adults in this age group is projected to rise from 1 billion in 2020 to 1.4 billion, reaching more than 2 billion by 2050.2 In parallel, life expectancy has increased substantially over recent decades, further expanding the proportion of older adults requiring long-term health care support.3,4 Increasing life expectancy, retention of natural dentition, and the growing prevalence of chronic conditions have collectively intensified the demand for dental care among older adults.5,6 At the same time, a substantial proportion of this population receives care in home-based or institutional settings, where physical limitations, frailty, and dependence on caregivers restrict access to conventional dental services.7,8 As a result, oral health care delivery is increasingly moving away from the traditional clinic-centred model toward more distributed, preventive approaches.9

This transition is clinically significant. In older adults, oral health is closely linked to systemic health, nutritional status, and overall quality of life.10,11 Inadequate oral hygiene and delayed management of oral conditions have been associated with adverse outcomes, including pain, impaired mastication, and increased risk of systemic complications such as aspiration pneumonia.12, 13, 14 Preventive and continuous care are therefore not merely desirable but essential in this population, particularly among those who are functionally dependent or medically compromised.9,15

Despite this growing need, access to timely and appropriate dental care in these settings remains limited.16,17 Workforce constraints, logistical challenges, and the episodic nature of traditional dental care models contribute to unmet needs, often leading to delayed diagnosis and treatment.18 Consequently, there is an urgent need to rethink how oral health care can be delivered effectively outside the clinical environment, particularly for vulnerable elderly populations.

The limits of current teledentistry

Teledentistry has been increasingly promoted as a strategy to improve access to oral health care, particularly in underserved and remote populations.19,20 Its expansion has been accelerated by advances in digital communication technologies and, more recently, by the necessity of remote care models during global health crises.20,21 In principle, teledentistry offers clear advantages, including improved accessibility, a reduced need for in-person visits, and the potential for early detection and monitoring of oral conditions.19,22 In contemporary dental practice, teledentistry is commonly implemented through synchronous video consultations, asynchronous store-and-forward exchange of photographs or radiographs, remote triage of urgent complaints, postoperative follow-up, oral hygiene counselling, and referral pathways linking general practitioners with specialists. These models have been increasingly incorporated into public and private health care systems in countries such as Australia, the United States, Brazil, Saudi Arabia, and the United Kingdom, where they have been used to improve access for rural communities, reduce unnecessary travel, and enhance continuity of care.23, 24, 25 However, implementation remains heterogeneous, with substantial variation in reimbursement policies, regulatory frameworks, digital infrastructure, and clinician uptake across jurisdictions. Its current implementation also remains largely an extension of traditional communication methods rather than a redefinition of care delivery.

A central limitation lies in its predominantly passive structure. Most teledentistry models rely on unstructured patient-reported symptoms, static photographs, or video calls, placing the burden of data acquisition on patients or caregivers, who often lack the necessary skills to produce clinically useful information.22 This limitation is particularly pronounced in elderly populations, in whom physical constraints, reduced dexterity, and limited digital literacy further compromise the quality of transmitted data.8,26 As a result, the diagnostic value of remote consultations is highly variable and frequently insufficient for confident clinical decision-making.22

Emerging evidence suggests that the effectiveness of teledentistry is not primarily determined by the communication modality itself but by the quality and interpretability of the information exchanged. For instance, remote postoperative assessments have demonstrated comparable overall accuracy across modalities such as phone consultations, patient-generated images, and video calls, with no clear superiority of more technologically advanced tools.27 Notably, live video interactions, in which clinicians can guide patients in real time, tend to yield more diagnostically useful information, underscoring the importance of structured interaction rather than passive data transmission.27

These findings highlight a fundamental issue: current teledentistry models often lack an intelligent mechanism to actively mediate the interaction between patient and clinician. In this context, intelligent mechanisms refer to digital systems capable of guiding, organising, and interpreting information during the remote consultation process. Examples include artificial intelligence (AI)–driven adaptive questionnaires that translate patient-reported symptoms into structured clinical histories; natural language processing tools that summarise key concerns; real-time image quality assessment systems that provide feedback on lighting, focus, framing, and angulation during photograph capture; and automated triage algorithms that prioritise urgent cases based on reported symptoms and visual findings.24,28 Such tools can reduce communication gaps, improve the consistency and completeness of transmitted information, and allow clinicians to focus on decision-making rather than data clarification.29 This need is particularly relevant in geriatric care, where patient frailty, caregiver dependence, and variable digital literacy often require more supportive and adaptive communication pathways.8,15 Consequently, the challenge is not simply to expand teledentistry but to redesign how information is generated, transmitted, and interpreted within remote dental care.

Current teledentistry, therefore, faces several interrelated limitations: the absence of standardised data acquisition protocols, inconsistent image quality, reliance on subjective symptom reporting, limited capacity for real-time patient or caregiver guidance, reduced diagnostic confidence in complex cases, and variable integration into clinical triage workflows. These barriers are particularly relevant in older adults, for whom reduced dexterity, sensory impairment, cognitive limitations, and low digital readiness may further compromise remote assessment. Recent reviews have similarly emphasised that although teledentistry can improve access and reduce unnecessary visits, its effectiveness remains constrained by diagnostic uncertainty, infrastructure limitations, legal and privacy concerns, and uneven implementation across clinical settings.22,24,27,30

AI-mediated models may help overcome these limitations by adding an active structuring layer between the patient and clinician. For example, AI systems can guide patients or caregivers during image capture by providing feedback on lighting, focus, angulation, and completeness; convert unstructured symptom descriptions into standardised clinical information; identify missing or poor-quality data before clinician review; and support preliminary risk stratification to distinguish cases suitable for monitoring from those requiring urgent in-person care. In this way, AI does not replace the dentist but improves the quality, consistency, and clinical usability of information transmitted through teledentistry, thereby transforming remote care from a passive communication model into a more interactive and decision-supportive system.28,31, 32, 33

AI as an intelligent intermediary

Addressing the limitations of current teledentistry requires more than incremental technological improvements; it demands a conceptual shift in how remote care is structured. Rather than viewing AI as a diagnostic adjunct or automation tool, it can be more productively understood as an intelligent intermediary that actively mediates the interaction between patient, caregiver, and clinician.31,32,34 In this model, AI does not replace clinical expertise but reorganises the pathway through which information is generated, interpreted, and acted upon.33 The operational structure of this AI-mediated model, including data acquisition, triage, and feedback mechanisms, is illustrated in Figure 2.

In elderly health care, AI has already demonstrated capabilities that extend beyond diagnosis, functioning as a supervisor, communicator, and decision support system that facilitates continuous monitoring and interaction.31 These roles are particularly relevant to dentistry, where remote care depends on the quality, structure, and context of patient-provided information.22,32 Translating these functions into teledentistry suggests a more dynamic system in which AI supports not only clinical decision-making but also the processes leading up to it.

Within this framework, AI can assume several interconnected roles. First, as a communication translator, it can convert unstructured patient-reported symptoms into clinically meaningful data by guiding users through adaptive questioning and structured input.33 Second, as a data acquisition guide, AI can assist patients and caregivers in capturing diagnostically relevant images or videos by providing real-time feedback on positioning, lighting, and focus, directly addressing one of the principal limitations of current remote assessments.27 Third, as a triage and prioritisation tool, AI can analyse incoming data to stratify risk, identify urgent conditions, and determine whether in-person care is required or if remote monitoring is sufficient.33

Beyond these functions, AI may also serve as a caregiver support system, particularly in geriatric settings where daily oral care is often performed by nondental personnel. By providing step-by-step guidance on oral hygiene, denture maintenance, and postoperative monitoring, AI can extend preventive care into routine daily practice.15,31 Importantly, this approach aligns with the need for continuous, low-intensity interventions to maintain oral health in dependent populations.15 In addition, voice-enabled and simplified AI-driven interfaces may allow users to interact with the system through spoken commands, conversational prompts, and visual guidance. For example, AI can instruct patients or caregivers to reposition the camera, open the mouth wider, move the tongue, improve lighting, or repeat an image when image quality is insufficient. Such systems may also collect symptom information through spoken responses, remind users of oral hygiene procedures, and simplify navigation for individuals with limited manual dexterity or visual impairment. By enabling hands-free, intuitive, and user-friendly interaction, these tools may reduce cognitive and technical barriers while improving accessibility, usability, and data quality during remote dental assessments, particularly for older adults and their caregivers.26,31,35,36

This reconceptualisation positions AI not as a replacement for teledentistry but as the missing structural layer that transforms it from a passive communication channel into an interactive, adaptive care system. By embedding intelligence into the process of data generation and exchange, AI-mediated teledentistry has the potential to enhance diagnostic reliability, improve efficiency, and create a more accessible, patient-centred model of care, particularly for elderly individuals receiving care outside traditional clinical environments.31, 32, 33

Barriers, risks, and real-world constraints

Despite its conceptual promise, implementing AI-mediated teledentistry in geriatric care poses several important challenges that must be carefully considered. Foremost among these are issues related to adoption. Older adults do not engage with digital health technologies uniformly, and barriers such as limited digital literacy, technology-related anxiety, reduced confidence, and low perceived usefulness can significantly hinder uptake.26,36,37 In many cases, interaction with digital platforms is mediated by caregivers, whose own workload, training, and familiarity with technology directly influence the feasibility of implementation.36,37 Evidence from geriatric health care consistently highlights that successful adoption depends less on technological sophistication than on usability, trust, and integration into existing care routines.31,36,37

Clinical reliability represents another critical concern. Remote dental assessment remains inherently dependent on the quality and completeness of transmitted data. Even with AI-supported guidance, variability in image acquisition, patient compliance, and contextual factors may affect diagnostic accuracy.22,27 There is a risk of both overtriage, leading to unnecessary in-person visits, and underestimation of disease severity, potentially delaying essential treatment.22 These limitations underscore the need to position AI-mediated teledentistry within a hybrid care model, where remote assessment complements rather than replaces direct clinical examination.22,27

Ethical and legal considerations further complicate implementation.38 The use of AI in health care raises questions regarding data privacy, ownership, and security, particularly when sensitive health information is transmitted across digital platforms.29,32,33 In addition, the distribution of responsibility between clinician and algorithm remains unclear, especially in cases of diagnostic error or adverse outcomes.29,32,33 Ensuring transparency, explainability, and regulatory compliance will be essential for building trust among both patients and practitioners.29,32 A further concern is that AI-mediated teledentistry may introduce new forms of clinical and ethical risk if algorithmic outputs are inaccurate, biased, insufficiently explainable, or used without adequate professional oversight. Risk management should therefore include continuous validation, audit trails, error-reporting mechanisms, clinician review of AI-generated recommendations, and clear escalation protocols when the quality of submitted data is insufficient or when urgent findings are suspected. Equally important, older adults and caregivers, as the original owners and providers of health data, may have limited awareness of how their images, voice inputs, and clinical information are stored, processed, shared, or used for algorithmic improvement.29,32,33 Therefore, informed consent procedures should be simple, transparent, and adapted to the cognitive and digital literacy levels of the target population, with explicit explanations of data privacy, user rights, withdrawal options, and human oversight. To promote effective adoption, AI literacy should also be incorporated into implementation strategies through caregiver training, demonstration-based learning, simplified educational materials, voice-guided instructions, and repeated support within home care or nursing home settings.39,40 Such measures are essential to ensure that AI-mediated teledentistry is not only technically feasible but also safe, transparent, and acceptable for ageing populations.

Finally, system-level barriers must be acknowledged. The integration of AI-mediated teledentistry into routine care requires appropriate infrastructure, including reliable Internet connectivity, device availability, and technical support.19,41 Moreover, reimbursement models and regulatory frameworks in many health care systems are not yet aligned with remote or AI-assisted care delivery.41,42 Without addressing these structural factors, even well-designed technological solutions may fail to achieve meaningful clinical impact.

Future directions: Toward continuous, AI-supported oral care

The integration of AI into teledentistry invites a broader reconsideration of how oral health care is conceptualised and delivered, particularly for ageing populations. Rather than functioning as isolated technological enhancements, AI-mediated systems may enable a transition from episodic, clinic-centred care to continuous, home-based monitoring and prevention.9,15,31 This shift is especially relevant in geriatric contexts, where oral health status is dynamic and closely influenced by daily behaviours, caregiver involvement, and systemic health conditions.15,36

In this emerging model, oral health care becomes embedded within the patient’s daily environment. AI-supported platforms could facilitate ongoing assessment of oral conditions, provide real-time guidance on hygiene practices, and enable early identification of changes warranting professional intervention.31,35 When integrated with other digital health technologies, such as wearable devices, smart home systems, or electronic health records, these platforms may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of patient health, supporting personalised and anticipatory care strategies.31 Such approaches align with broader trends in elderly health care, where AI is increasingly used for monitoring, feedback, and decision support in real-world settings.31

For successful implementation, AI-mediated teledentistry should be introduced through pragmatic, stepwise models that align with existing clinical pathways rather than functioning as stand-alone technologies. Initial deployment may focus on high-yield applications such as postoperative follow-up, preventive monitoring in dependent older adults, triage of urgent complaints, and caregiver-supported oral hygiene supervision. These measures may promote adoption and enhance effectiveness by increasing user confidence, reducing technical barriers, improving the quality and completeness of transmitted data, and enabling more consistent triage decisions. In addition, pilot programs within community services, nursing homes, and domiciliary dental care networks can help refine workflows, evaluate cost-effectiveness, and generate real-world evidence before wider scale-up. With appropriate governance, reimbursement pathways, and clinician oversight, such models may become a practical extension of preventive and supportive oral health care for ageing populations.24,28,31

Importantly, the future of AI-mediated teledentistry is unlikely to be defined by full automation but by hybrid models that combine digital support with clinical oversight.32,33 Dentists will remain central to diagnosis, treatment planning, and complex decision-making, while AI facilitates information flow, enhances efficiency, and supports preventive care.32,33 This redistribution of roles has the potential to reduce unnecessary clinical visits, optimise resource allocation, and improve access to care for individuals who are otherwise underserved.19,22

Ultimately, the successful evolution of this model will depend on aligning technological innovation with the realities of geriatric care.34,38 Systems must be designed with simplicity, adaptability, and caregiver integration at their core, ensuring that they are not only effective but also usable and acceptable in everyday contexts.26,35,36 If these challenges are addressed, AI-mediated teledentistry may play a pivotal role in transforming oral health care delivery from a reactive, intervention-focused discipline into a more proactive, accessible, and patient-centred system that extends beyond the walls of the dental clinic.
