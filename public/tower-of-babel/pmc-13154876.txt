Predicting high-flow nasal cannula failure: beyond static indices toward dynamic physiologic assessment
Authors: Jay Prakash, Kalavathy Swarna, Bodhisatwa Choudhuri
Published: 2026

To the Editor

We have read with great interest the recent narrative review that provides a comprehensive overview of the predictors of failure of high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) in critically ill patients [1]. The authors have discussed the commonly used clinical indices, including the respiratory rate–oxygenation (ROX) index, PaO₂/FiO₂ (P/F) ratio, SpO₂/FiO₂ (S/F) ratio, and the newly proposed composite indices.

However, the major limitation of the narrative review is the lack of methodological rigor in the presentation of the results. In the context of clinical prediction trial, the importance of using standardized frameworks like PRISMA and TRIPOD is increasingly being recognized as essential for critically appraising the results of the included studies. Narrative reviews are more likely to be influenced by the presentation of results, without the assessment of the quality of the included studies. The results may be biased toward the presentation of certain results over others. In the context of the prediction of failure of HFNC in critically ill patients, the results of the included studies are heterogeneous in terms of the definition of failure and the timing of the assessment of the predictors of failure.

Another critical issue that needs consideration is the heterogeneity associated with the definition of HFNC failure, which has a considerable impact on the performance of the predictive indices. The failure criteria have been variably defined in the various studies, including the escalation of non-invasive ventilation, the need for intubation, the requirement for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and mortality within specific time periods. The heterogeneity associated with the definition of the composite outcome makes the results difficult to interpret, particularly since the prediction models for intubation may not perform similarly for other composite outcomes.

The review highlights the ROX index as the most extensively studied predictor of HFNC success or failure [2]. However, growing evidence suggests that its predictive performance is moderate rather than definitive. A meta-analysis has reported pooled areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) in the range of 0.74–0.79, indicating modest discrimination [3]. This implies that the ROX index cannot reliably distinguish between patients who will succeed with or fail HFNC therapy on an individual basis. In practice, this level of performance may be insufficient to support high-stakes decisions, such as early intubation, when used as a standalone threshold. Accordingly, we have emphasized that the ROX index should be interpreted as a dynamic bedside monitoring tool, where trends over time—rather than a single cutoff value—provide more clinically meaningful guidance for escalation decisions. Additionally, the optimal threshold of the ROX index has been found to vary significantly in different studies based on the severity of the disease and the time of measurement [4, 5]. Thus, the ROX index is likely more of a monitoring parameter than as a fixed binary theshold, especially for the escalation of ventilation.

An additional dimension that warrants greater emphasis is the temporal trajectory of physiological variables during HFNC therapy. Increasing evidence suggests that trends in respiratory rate, oxygenation parameters, and serial ROX index measurements during the first 6–12 h may provide stronger prognostic information than single time-point measurements [2, 4]. Consequently, the ROX index should likely be interpreted as a dynamic, trend-based monitoring parameter rather than a fixed decision threshold for escalation to invasive ventilation.

Additionally, the review briefly presents new indices and machine learning models, which seem to be promising for better accuracy in predictive models. However, it has been observed that the predictive models used in critical care have limited external validity when used outside of the derivation cohorts. The variations in the epidemiology of disease, monitoring, and intubation can have a substantial effect on the predictive models.

Lastly, the conversation would be enhanced by an increased focus on the physiological effects of delayed intubation, especially in the context of patient self-inflicted lung injury (P-SILI). Indeed, the prolonged effort of breathing in the hypoxemic patient may worsen lung injury and negatively impact patient outcomes [6]. Thus, the clinical objective would not only include the prediction of the failure of HFNC but also the timing of the escalation of respiratory support.

In summary, the predictors of HFNC failure remain an evolving area with considerable implications for critical care practice. Future studies must focus on the standardized outcome measures, physiological monitoring, and validation of the predictive models for the effective translation of the predictive indices into decision-support systems that can ultimately lead to the improvement of patient care.
