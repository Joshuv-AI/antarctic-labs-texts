Protective role of Vaccinium myrtillus’s (Bilberry) antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects in experimental lower limb ischemia-reperfusion injury
Authors: Ali Dogan Dursun, Hüseyin Demirtas, Alperen Kutay Yıldırım, Abdullah Özer, Saban Cem Sezen, Aysegul Kucuk, Işın Gunes, Veli Cengiz Ozalp, Mustafa Arslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSkeletal muscle ischemia–reperfusion (IR) injury is a common clinical problem encountered in vascular and orthopedic surgery, trauma, and transplantation, and is characterized by pronounced oxidative stress and structural tissue damage. Vaccinium myrtillus (bilberry) is rich in anthocyanins and has demonstrated antioxidant and vasoprotective properties in various organ systems; however, its effects on skeletal muscle IR injury have not been previously investigated.MethodsTwenty-four male Wistar rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): Sham (surgical exposure without IR, vehicle-treated), IR (ischemia–reperfusion, vehicle-treated), IR + Vaccinium myrtillus (ischemia–reperfusion treated with bilberry extract, 100 mg/kg intraperitoneally), and Vaccinium myrtillus only (bilberry extract without IR). A standardized commercial bilberry extract (Herbal Liquids, HealthAid®, London, UK) was used. Biochemical analyses included total antioxidant status (TAS), total oxidant status (TOS), oxidative stress index (OSI), and paraoxonase-1 (PON-1) activity. Histopathological evaluation of gastrocnemius muscle tissue was performed using hematoxylin–eosin staining.ResultsIschemia–reperfusion resulted in significantly higher TOS, OSI, and PON-1 activity and significantly lower TAS levels compared with Sham and bilberry-only groups (p < 0.05). Treatment with Vaccinium myrtillus significantly increased TAS and reduced TOS, OSI, and PON-1 activity compared with the untreated IR group (p < 0.05). Histopathological analysis demonstrated significantly lower scores for muscle fiber degeneration, vascular congestion, nuclear internalization, and leukocyte infiltration in the IR + bilberry group.ConclusionsThis experimental study provides evidence that Vaccinium myrtillus extract attenuates oxidative stress and reduces histopathological damage in skeletal muscle subjected to ischemia–reperfusion injury. The observed protective effects appear to be primarily related to modulation of redox balance. These findings support further investigation of bilberry as a potential supportive antioxidant strategy in conditions associated with skeletal muscle ischemia–reperfusion injury.

Introduction

Ischemia–reperfusion (IR) injury is a critical pathophysiological process encountered in a wide range of clinical settings, including vascular and orthopedic surgery, trauma, transplantation, and acute limb ischemia [1–4]. Although reperfusion is essential for tissue survival, the restoration of blood flow paradoxically exacerbates cellular injury through complex mechanisms involving oxidative stress, inflammatory activation, and microvascular dysfunction [5–7]. Skeletal muscle is particularly susceptible to IR injury because of its high metabolic demand and sensitivity to hypoxia. Severe skeletal muscle IR may result not only in local tissue necrosis but also in systemic complications such as metabolic derangements and multi-organ dysfunction [8–11]. Consequently, identifying strategies that can attenuate oxidative and structural damage in skeletal muscle IR remains a significant research priority.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a central role in the pathogenesis of IR injury. Excessive ROS generation during reperfusion initiates lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and DNA damage, thereby amplifying tissue injury beyond the ischemic phase [12–16]. The resulting imbalance between oxidant production and endogenous antioxidant defenses contributes to endothelial barrier disruption, apoptotic signaling, and inflammatory responses [17–19]. These mechanisms underscore the importance of targeting redox homeostasis as a potential therapeutic approach in IR injury.

Polyphenolic compounds derived from natural sources have attracted increasing attention due to their antioxidant and cytoprotective properties [20–23]. Among these, anthocyanin-rich berries are of particular interest because of their strong radical-scavenging capacity and documented vascular benefits [24–27]. Vaccinium myrtillus (bilberry), a European berry species, is especially rich in anthocyanins, including delphinidin, cyanidin, petunidin, peonidin, and malvidin glycosides [28–31]. Experimental studies have demonstrated that bilberry and its anthocyanin-rich extracts exert protective effects in myocardial, renal, and cerebral ischemia–reperfusion models, primarily through antioxidant mechanisms and preservation of cellular integrity [32–35]. In addition, clinical studies have reported that bilberry supplementation improves endothelial function and reduces systemic oxidative stress markers, supporting its potential relevance in vascular pathophysiology [36–46].

The pathophysiology of skeletal muscle IR injury is driven by a combination of excessive ROS production, mitochondrial impairment, endothelial activation, and leukocyte-mediated microvascular obstruction [2, 6, 9, 13]. During reperfusion, increased endothelial permeability and leukocyte adhesion disrupt capillary perfusion and exacerbate tissue injury beyond the initial ischemic insult [4, 6]. These processes are not organ-specific and share common mechanistic pathways across myocardial, renal, cerebral, and skeletal muscle IR models [7, 9, 13].

Bilberry-derived anthocyanins have been reported to modulate oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling pathways in various experimental settings. In particular, inhibition of nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) activation and downstream pro-inflammatory cytokine expression has been demonstrated in both in vitro and in vivo models [47–49]. Furthermore, bilberry supplementation has been associated with improved microvascular function, reduced leukocyte–endothelial interactions, and enhanced capillary perfusion in ischemia–reperfusion models [33, 38]. These properties suggest that bilberry may influence key mechanisms relevant to skeletal muscle IR injury, which is highly dependent on microcirculatory integrity.

Despite substantial evidence supporting the antioxidant and vasoprotective effects of bilberry in different organ systems, its impact on skeletal muscle ischemia–reperfusion injury has not been previously investigated. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first experimental study to evaluate the effects of Vaccinium myrtillus extract in a lower limb IR model. By combining biochemical indices of oxidative status with histopathological assessment of muscle tissue, the present study aimed to investigate whether bilberry administration could attenuate oxidative stress and structural muscle injury. These findings may contribute to a better understanding of the potential role of bilberry-derived polyphenols as supportive agents in conditions associated with skeletal muscle ischemia–reperfusion injury, particularly in vascular and orthopedic surgical contexts [39–43].

Material and method

Animals

This experimental study was conducted using male Albino Wistar rats aged 12 weeks, weighing between 270 and 320 g. All animals were obtained from and maintained at the certified Experimental Research Center of Gazi University, Ankara, Türkiye. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (Approval No: G.Ü.ET-25.039, dated 19.04.2025) and carried out in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and established principles for animal welfare.

Rats were housed individually under controlled environmental conditions with a room temperature of 20–21 °C, relative humidity of 45–55%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All animals had ad libitum access to standard laboratory rodent chow and purified drinking water throughout the study period.

Vaccinium myrtillus extract preparation

A commercially available bilberry extract (Vaccinium myrtillus; Herbal Liquids, HealthAid®, London, UK) was used in this study. According to the manufacturer’s certificate of analysis, the extract was supplied as a standardized solution at a concentration of 330 mg/mL in a 1:1 ethanol–distilled water mixture and contained not less than 25% anthocyanins, expressed as cyanidin-3-glucoside equivalents.

For experimental use, the extract was aseptically diluted with the same ethanol–distilled water mixture (1:1) to obtain a final concentration of 50 mg/mL. The extract was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 100 mg/kg, consistent with previously published experimental protocols employing the same preparation [32, 40]. Based on the final extract concentration (50 mg/mL) and individual body weights (270–320 g), the intraperitoneal injection volume ranged between approximately 0.54 and 0.64 mL per rat. The final ethanol concentration in the injected volume was below 5%.

Experimental design and ischemia–reperfusion protocol

Twenty-four rats were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 6 per group):

Sham group: anesthesia and surgical exposure without ischemia–reperfusion, receiving intraperitoneal vehicle (1:1 ethanol–distilled water);IR group: ischemia–reperfusion with intraperitoneal vehicle;IR + VM group: ischemia–reperfusion treated with Vaccinium myrtillus extract (100 mg/kg, intraperitoneally);VM group: Vaccinium myrtillus extract (100 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) without ischemia–reperfusion.

Sham group: anesthesia and surgical exposure without ischemia–reperfusion, receiving intraperitoneal vehicle (1:1 ethanol–distilled water);

Sham group: anesthesia and surgical exposure without ischemia–reperfusion, receiving intraperitoneal vehicle (1:1 ethanol–distilled water);

IR group: ischemia–reperfusion with intraperitoneal vehicle;

IR group: ischemia–reperfusion with intraperitoneal vehicle;

IR + VM group: ischemia–reperfusion treated with Vaccinium myrtillus extract (100 mg/kg, intraperitoneally);

IR + VM group: ischemia–reperfusion treated with Vaccinium myrtillus extract (100 mg/kg, intraperitoneally);

VM group: Vaccinium myrtillus extract (100 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) without ischemia–reperfusion.

VM group: Vaccinium myrtillus extract (100 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) without ischemia–reperfusion.

Sample size was determined based on comparable preclinical ischemia–reperfusion studies in rodent models [12, 15–19].

General anesthesia was induced by intramuscular ketamine (45 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg). Supplemental ketamine (20 mg/kg) was administered as required to maintain adequate anesthesia depth. Animals were placed in the supine position on a heating pad to maintain body temperature.

In the Sham group, a midline laparotomy was performed without vascular occlusion, followed by intraperitoneal vehicle administration at the corresponding time point. In the IR and IR + VM groups, ischemia was induced by clamping the infrarenal abdominal aorta using an atraumatic microvascular clamp for 45 min, followed by 120 min of reperfusion after clamp removal.

In the IR + VM group, Vaccinium myrtillus extract (100 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) was administered immediately after removal of the aortic clamp, at the onset of reperfusion. Thus, the extract was present throughout the entire reperfusion period (120 min) prior to tissue harvesting. Animals in the VM group received Vaccinium myrtillus extract intraperitoneally 45 min after laparotomy, corresponding to the ischemic duration applied in the IR groups, but without vascular occlusion.

At the end of the experimental period, rats were euthanized under deep anesthesia with ketamine overdose (100 mg/kg). Blood samples were collected via abdominal aortic puncture. Following euthanasia, bilateral gastrocnemius muscles were harvested; the left gastrocnemius muscle was allocated for histopathological evaluation, whereas the right gastrocnemius muscle was used for biochemical analysis.

Histopathological evaluation

Left gastrocnemius muscle tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, routinely processed, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned longitudinally at 4–5 μm thickness. Longitudinal sections were preferred to allow optimal evaluation of myofibrillar integrity, fragmentation, and nuclear internalization. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E).

All slides were evaluated under light microscopy by a pathologist blinded to group allocation. Five randomly selected, non-overlapping high-power fields (×400) per section were examined. Histopathological parameters included myofiber degeneration, vascular congestion, nuclear internalization, fragmentation/hyalinization, and leukocyte infiltration.

A semi-quantitative scoring system (0–3), adapted from previously published ischemia–reperfusion studies [8, 11, 32], was used. Scores were defined as follows:

0 = absent (< 5% involvement),

1 = mild (5–25%),

2 = moderate (25–50%),

3 = severe (> 50%) involvement per high-power field.

Biochemical evaluation

Right gastrocnemius muscle tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Tissue samples were homogenized in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) and centrifuged at 10,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were used for biochemical analyses.

Total antioxidant status (TAS) and total oxidant status (TOS) were measured using commercially available colorimetric assay kits based on ABTS radical cation decolorization and ferrous ion oxidation principles, respectively (Rel Assay Diagnostics, Gaziantep, Türkiye; Catalog Nos: RL0017 for TAS and RL0024 for TOS), according to the manufacturer’s instructions [11, 12]. The oxidative stress index (OSI) was calculated as:

OSI = (TOS [µmol H₂O₂ equivalent/L] ÷ TAS [mmol Trolox equivalent/L]) × 100 [11].

Paraoxonase-1 (PON-1) activity was measured spectrophotometrically using a commercial assay kit (Rel Assay Diagnostics, Gaziantep, Türkiye; Catalog No: RL0094) and expressed as units per liter (U/L). All biochemical measurements were performed in duplicate. Assays were conducted on tissue supernatants without additional protein normalization, in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and Q–Q plot test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. The results were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by Dunn’s test or one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s test. The results were expressed as mean ± standard error of means (SEM) and median (interquartile range [IQR]) values, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Ischemia–reperfusion (IR) injury represents a complex pathophysiological process in which tissue damage arises not only from ischemic hypoxia but also from the paradoxical exacerbation of injury during reperfusion. The abrupt reintroduction of oxygen triggers excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation through mitochondrial respiration, NADPH oxidase activation, and xanthine oxidase pathways, overwhelming endogenous antioxidant defenses and initiating oxidative damage [9, 10, 13]. This oxidative burden leads to lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage, ultimately impairing cellular integrity and promoting myocyte injury [13]. In skeletal muscle, these mechanisms are accompanied by microvascular dysfunction and inflammatory cell recruitment, resulting in structural degeneration, congestion, and leukocyte infiltration [2, 4, 6]. In the present study, these pathological features were consistently observed in the IR group and quantified using established histopathological scoring parameters.

In parallel with structural injury, disruption of redox homeostasis plays a central role in IR pathogenesis. Total antioxidant status (TAS), total oxidant status (TOS), and the oxidative stress index (OSI) are widely used indicators that collectively reflect the balance between antioxidant capacity and oxidative burden [2, 9]. In our study, IR was associated with a significant reduction in TAS and marked elevations in TOS and OSI, confirming the presence of pronounced oxidative stress in skeletal muscle tissue. Administration of Vaccinium myrtillus extract significantly improved these parameters, with increased TAS and reduced TOS and OSI values in the IR + VM group compared with untreated IR animals. These findings indicate a restoration of redox balance rather than complete normalization, consistent with a partial protective effect under acute IR conditions.

Paraoxonase-1 (PON-1) is an HDL-associated antioxidant enzyme that contributes to the hydrolysis of lipid peroxides and plays a role in limiting oxidative injury [44, 45]. In contrast to chronic oxidative stress models in which PON-1 activity is often suppressed, the present study demonstrated a significant increase in PON-1 activity in the acute IR group. This observation may reflect a potential adaptive or stress-related enzymatic response during the acute reperfusion phase; however, this interpretation remains speculative, as the present study was not designed to evaluate temporal dynamics or underlying regulatory mechanisms of PON-1 activity. Following bilberry treatment, PON-1 activity was significantly reduced compared with the IR group, approaching levels observed in non-ischemic controls. This pattern suggests that attenuation of oxidative stress by bilberry may reduce the need for acute compensatory upregulation of endogenous antioxidant enzymes. Importantly, mechanistic conclusions regarding PON-1 regulation should be interpreted cautiously, as the present study was not designed to evaluate temporal enzyme dynamics.

The antioxidative effects observed in this study are consistent with a substantial body of experimental literature demonstrating the protective properties of Vaccinium myrtillus in ischemia–reperfusion and oxidative injury models involving different organs. Previous studies have shown that bilberry supplementation reduces lipid peroxidation markers such as malondialdehyde (MDA), enhances endogenous antioxidant systems, and preserves mitochondrial function in brain, liver, heart, and kidney tissues [27, 29–32, 40]. In myocardial IR models, bilberry anthocyanins improved coronary flow, reduced lactate dehydrogenase release, and decreased arrhythmia incidence at appropriate concentrations, effects attributed primarily to antioxidant activity [31]. Similar protective effects have been reported in renal and hepatic IR injury, where bilberry administration attenuated histopathological damage and improved oxidative stress parameters [32, 34, 40]. The present findings extend these observations by demonstrating that bilberry also confers structural and biochemical protection in skeletal muscle subjected to IR injury, a tissue that has received comparatively limited attention in the literature.

Beyond its antioxidative properties, bilberry has been widely reported to exert anti-inflammatory effects through modulation of molecular signaling pathways, particularly inhibition of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) activation and downstream cytokine expression [47–49]. Experimental and clinical studies have shown reductions in pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and hsCRP following bilberry supplementation [47–49]. In the present study, direct biochemical measurement of inflammatory cytokines was not performed. Therefore, conclusions regarding anti-inflammatory effects are based on histopathological observations, particularly reduced leukocyte infiltration and vascular congestion in bilberry-treated groups. These findings suggest an indirect anti-inflammatory association but should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of cytokine-level modulation.

The choice of intraperitoneal administration was made to ensure reproducible systemic exposure and minimize variability associated with gastrointestinal absorption in rodents. This route is widely used in experimental IR models evaluating natural compounds and polyphenols [32, 35, 40]. Using body surface area normalization, the administered dose of 100 mg/kg in rats corresponds to a human equivalent dose of approximately 1.1 g/day for a 70-kg adult, which falls within the range of standardized bilberry extract doses reported in human clinical studies (500 mg–2 g/day) [33, 50]. Although oral administration would be more directly translational, the calculated human equivalent dose supports the clinical relevance of the present findings. Although oral administration would better reflect natural exposure and clinical use, intraperitoneal delivery was selected in the present study to ensure controlled systemic bioavailability and minimize variability related to gastrointestinal absorption. Future studies incorporating oral administration and chronic dosing regimens are warranted to further enhance translational relevance.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, inflammatory cytokines and molecular signaling pathways were not directly measured, limiting mechanistic interpretation of anti-inflammatory effects. Second, histopathological evaluation was based on longitudinal sections and semi-quantitative scoring rather than quantitative cross-sectional area analysis. Third, functional assessments of muscle performance were not included, and structural preservation does not necessarily equate to functional recovery. Finally, the anthocyanin profile of the commercial bilberry extract was not independently verified by chromatographic analysis. These limitations highlight areas for future investigation.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that Vaccinium myrtillus extract attenuates oxidative stress and reduces histopathological damage in skeletal muscle subjected to ischemia–reperfusion injury. The observed effects are primarily attributable to antioxidative mechanisms, with indirect histological evidence suggesting modulation of inflammatory responses. These findings provide novel experimental evidence supporting the protective role of bilberry in skeletal muscle IR injury and warrant further studies incorporating molecular inflammatory markers and functional outcome measures to fully elucidate its therapeutic potential.

Conclusion

This experimental study demonstrates that Vaccinium myrtillus (bilberry) extract attenuates oxidative stress and reduces histopathological damage in skeletal muscle subjected to acute ischemia–reperfusion injury. The observed improvements in global oxidative stress parameters (TAS, TOS, OSI) and tissue morphology indicate that bilberry confers a protective effect primarily through antioxidative mechanisms. Although direct inflammatory and molecular analyses were not performed, the reduction in leukocyte infiltration and structural degeneration suggests a potential indirect modulation of inflammatory processes. These findings provide novel experimental evidence supporting the role of bilberry as a supportive antioxidant agent in skeletal muscle ischemia–reperfusion injury and warrant further mechanistic and translational investigations.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, this investigation was conducted in an experimental rat model with an acute ischemia–reperfusion protocol; therefore, extrapolation of the findings to human clinical settings should be approached with caution. Long-term outcomes and chronic effects of bilberry administration were not evaluated.

Second, although oxidative stress was comprehensively assessed using validated global indices (TAS, TOS, OSI) and PON-1 activity, specific oxidative and antioxidant markers such as malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione (GSH), and catalase (CAT) were not measured. While TAS/TOS/OSI provide a reliable overview of redox balance and are widely used in ischemia–reperfusion studies [11, 32, 40], inclusion of these additional markers would offer more detailed mechanistic insight. In addition, widely used skeletal muscle injury biomarkers such as creatine kinase (CK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) were not evaluated, which may be considered a limitation in terms of comprehensive biochemical characterization of muscle injury. Future studies incorporating these markers would further strengthen the biochemical assessment of skeletal muscle injury.

Third, direct biochemical measurements of inflammatory mediators and molecular signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB, MAPK, cytokines, apoptosis-related proteins such as caspase-3 and Bax/Bcl-2) were not performed. Consequently, conclusions regarding anti-inflammatory or anti-apoptotic effects are indirect and based on histopathological observations rather than molecular confirmation. In addition, specific inflammatory biomarkers such as TNF-α, IL-6, or myeloperoxidase (MPO) were not measured, which limits the ability to directly quantify and mechanistically interpret the inflammatory response. Future studies integrating biochemical inflammatory markers and molecular pathway analyses would further strengthen the mechanistic understanding of these findings. Apoptosis-related analyses (e.g., TUNEL staining or evaluation of apoptosis-associated molecular markers) were not performed, which limits the ability to directly assess cell death mechanisms underlying the observed histopathological changes.

Fourth, histopathological evaluation was limited to semi-quantitative analysis of longitudinal sections, and functional muscle outcomes such as contractility, perfusion, or electromyographic activity were not assessed. As structural preservation does not necessarily equate to functional recovery, this represents an important limitation.

Finally, although a standardized commercial bilberry extract was used, batch-specific chromatographic characterization (HPLC/LC-MS) was not performed. Therefore, the contribution of individual anthocyanins or other phytochemical components cannot be precisely delineated, and potential solvent-related effects cannot be entirely excluded [20, 31, 35, 46].

Despite these limitations, the present findings establish a solid experimental foundation for future studies integrating molecular inflammatory profiling, functional outcome measures, dose–response analyses, and detailed phytochemical characterization to further elucidate the therapeutic potential of bilberry in skeletal muscle ischemia–reperfusion injury.
