An inverse problem for a nonlinear biharmonic operator
Janne Nurminen †, Suman Kumar Sahoo §

Abstract
. An inverse problem for a nonlinear biharmonic operator is under consideration in the spirit of [ 25 , 26 ] . We prove that a general nonlinear term of the Q = Q ⁡ ( x , u , ∇ u , Δ ​ u ) Q=Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u) associated to a nonlinear biharmonic operator can be recovered from the local Cauchy data set. The proof uses first order linearization method, Runge approximation, and uniqueness results for the linearized inverse problem.

1.
introduction and main results

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n\geq 2
, and let

(1.1)

\displaystyle Q=Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=Q(x,z,X,q)\in C^{k}\left(\mathbb{R},C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}),C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n}),C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\right),\quad k\geq 3.

With this notation, consider the following semilinear fourth-order biharmonic operator:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{Q}u:=\Delta^{2}u+Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,

associated with the Navier boundary condition
(u,\Delta u)=(f_{0},f_{1})
on
\partial\Omega
. The boundary measurements corresponding to (
1.2
) can be encoded in terms of the Navier-Neumann map as follows:

\mathcal{N}_{Q}(f_{0},f_{1}):=(\partial_{\nu}f_{0},\partial_{\nu}f_{1})\quad\text{for small boundary data }f_{0},f_{1}.

Alternatively, one could consider the boundary measurements in terms of Cauchy data sets:

C_{Q,\mathcal{N}}=\left\{(u|_{\partial\Omega},\partial_{\nu}u|_{\partial\Omega},\Delta u|_{\partial\Omega},\partial_{\nu}\Delta u|_{\partial\Omega}):u\text{ solves }\mathcal{L}_{Q}u=0\text{ in }\Omega\right\}.

The inverse problem we are interested in is to recover the nonlinear term
Q
from the knowledge of the Cauchy data set
C_{Q}
. More precisely, we work with the local Cauchy data. Let
\delta>0
and
w\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
be a fixed solution of (
1.2
). Then the local Cauchy data set is defined as:

C_{Q}^{w,\delta}=\left\{(u|_{\partial\Omega},\partial_{\nu}u|_{\partial\Omega},\Delta u|_{\partial\Omega},\partial_{\nu}\Delta u|_{\partial\Omega}):u\text{ solves }\mathcal{L}_{Q}u=0\text{ in }\Omega,\text{ and }\|u-w\|_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\delta\right\}.

Our main result states that if
w
is a common solution of
\mathcal{L}_{Q_{j}}u=0
for
j=1,2
and the corresponding Cauchy data sets satisfy
C_{Q_{1}}^{w,\delta}\subset C_{Q_{2}}^{w,\delta}
, then
Q_{1}=Q_{2}
near
w
. More precisely, we have:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
w
solve
\mathcal{L}_{Q_{1}}w=\mathcal{L}_{Q_{2}}w=0
in
\Omega
, where
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
satisfy (
1.1
). Suppose for some
\delta,C>0
we have
C_{Q_{1}}^{w,\delta}\subseteq C_{Q_{2}}^{0,C}.

Then there exists
\epsilon>0
such that

Q_{1}(x,w(x)+\lambda,\nabla w(x)+\overline{\lambda},\Delta w(x)+\lambda)=Q_{2}(x,w(x)+\lambda,\nabla w(x)+\overline{\lambda},\Delta w(x)+\lambda),

for
x\in\overline{\Omega}
,
|\lambda|\leq\epsilon
, and
\overline{\lambda}=(\lambda,\ldots,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

As a corollary, we have the following result:

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Suppose
\mathcal{N}_{Q_{1}}(f_{0},f_{1})=\mathcal{N}_{Q_{2}}(f_{0},f_{1})
for small
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
. Then

Q_{1}(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=Q_{2}(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u).

If two nonlinearities
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
do not have a common solution, then there is a gauge invariance for the inverse problem; see
[
26
]
. If
\phi\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies
\phi=0
on
\partial\Omega
and
\partial^{k}_{\nu}\phi=0
on
\partial\Omega
for
k=1,2,3
, then

\mathcal{L}_{Q}u=0\quad\text{in }\Omega\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\mathcal{L}_{T_{\phi}}v=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,

where
v=u-\phi
, and
T_{\phi}Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=\Delta^{2}\phi-Q(x,u+\phi,\nabla(u+\phi),\Delta(u+\phi)).

Our next result shows that if one knows the Cauchy data for a nonlinearity
Q
and for solutions close to a given solution
w
, then one can recover
Q
near points
(x,w(x))
up to the gauge mentioned above.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
w
solve the equation
\mathcal{L}_{Q_{1}}w=0
in
\Omega
. Suppose for some
\delta,C>0
we have

C_{Q_{1}}^{w,\delta}\subseteq C_{Q_{2}}^{0,C}
, where
Q_{i}
satisfy (
1.1
) for
i=1,2
.
Then there exists
\epsilon>0
such that

Q_{1}(x,w(x)+\lambda,\nabla w(x)+\overline{\lambda},\Delta w(x)+\lambda)=T_{\phi}Q_{2}(x,w(x)+\lambda,\nabla w(x)+\overline{\lambda},\Delta w(x)+\lambda),

for
x\in\overline{\Omega}
,
|\lambda|\leq\epsilon
, and
\overline{\lambda}=(\lambda,\ldots,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

1.1.
State of the Art

The linearization of nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) is a well-established and powerful method for simplifying and solving complex problems. This method involves linearizing a nonlinear PDE near a given solution, using the inverse/implicit function theorem, making it easier to analyze and solve locally. We employ similar ideas to solve our inverse problem.

The inverse problem for a linear second-order elliptic equation was initially posed by Calderón for the conductivity equation in 1980
[
14
]
, where he established results for the linearized equation. Since then, numerous researchers have explored the linearized Calderón problem in various settings; see
[
14
]
. In 1987, Sylvester and Uhlmann
[
49
]
solved the nonlinear inverse problem for a linear second-order elliptic PDE. There is a substantial body of literature available on inverse problems related to second-order linear elliptic PDEs, and we refer to the survey
[
52
]
for further details.

Inverse problems involving nonlinear PDEs began with Isakov’s work
[
25
]
. In
[
25
]
, he demonstrated that a nonlinear term
a(x,z)
in a nonlinear Schrödinger equation
\Delta u+a(x,u)=0
can be recovered from the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
\Lambda_{a}=\partial_{\nu}u|_{\partial\Omega}
, using the first-order linearization technique under suitable assumptions on

a(x,z)
. Similar results can be found in
[
24
,
25
]
.
These works all relied on the following assumption: either
a(x,0)=0
or
\partial_{u}a(x,u)\leq 0
. Such assumptions ensured well-posedness and the maximum principle, which were crucial in closing the argument. Subsequent studies, particularly
[
23
,
48
]
, weakened these requirements. Notably, their results establish that the nonlinear term
can be recovered in the reachable set

(1.3)

\displaystyle E_{a}=\{(x,z):x\in\overline{\Omega}\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,z=u(x)\,\,\mbox{for some solution of $\Delta u+a(x,u)=0$}\},

and in general, the set
E_{a}
cannot be all of
\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}
; see the counterexample in
[
25
]
. Recently,
[
26
]
eliminated the sign condition on

a(x,u)
and solved an inverse problem for the equation
\Delta u+a(x,u)=0
, for a general nonlinear term

a(x,u)
. For more results related to inverse problems for nonlinear equations, we refer to the survey
[
47
]
.

The higher-order linearization method was initially introduced by Kurylev, Lassas, and Uhlmann
[
33
]
to tackle inverse problems for nonlinear wave equations. While the first-order linearization relies on techniques from linear theory, the higher-order approach provides a powerful framework for addressing nonlinear inverse problems that remain unresolved in the linear case
see
[
27
,
32
,
34
]
, and the references therein. Subsequent works
[
35
,
19
,
38
]
extended this method to nonlinear elliptic equations. Additionally, we highlight the works
[
15
,
16
]
, which explore inverse problems for porous media equations where nonlinearity appears in the principal part of the equation rather than lower-order terms. For further exploration of similar approaches, we refer to the works
[
29
,
41
,
42
,
17
,
28
]
and the references cited in these articles.

The study of inverse problems for higher-order elliptic equations is relatively limited compared to second-order elliptic equations. This area of research began with the works of Krupchyk, Lassas, and Uhlmann
[
30
,
31
]
. For more recent contributions, see
[
10
,
45
]
. In a recent study
[
11
]
, the authors investigated an inverse problem for a nonlinear biharmonic operator involving a third-order anisotropic perturbation. This is another instance where nonlinearity is used as a tool to solve inverse problems that remain unsolved for their linear counterparts. We refer to the works
[
22
,
7
,
8
,
12
,
2
]
for more results, and discussion on inverse problems related to higher-order operators. To the best of our knowledge, there are no existing studies addressing inverse problems for nonlinear biharmonic operators where a generic nonlinear coefficient depends on

u,\nabla u,\Delta u
. We address this problem in this article using first order linearization.

Let us briefly describe the method used (for a more complete description we refer to
[
26
]
). The key idea is to linearize the problem and construct solutions near a fixed solutions to the nonlinear problem. This is done in two parts. First we construct solutions that depend continuously on the solutions to the linearized equation. These will have a specific form that in applying estimates using Cauchy data. When we have two nonlinear equations
\Delta^{2}u+Q_{i}(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=0
,
i=1,2
, this first solution operator gives that the solutions will depend continuously on the corresponding linearized equations. But we would need the solutions to depend on the same linearized solutions, that is, for solutions on the linearized equation for
\Delta^{2}u+Q_{1}(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=0
. This is made possible by the second solution operator. Then we combine these solution operators with the mapping properties of the first solution operator and results for the linearized inverse problem to conclude the proof of our main results.

The rest of the article is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we construct the first solutions operator. Section
3
is dedicated to Cauchy data estimates and in Section
4
we use these results to construct the second solution operator. In Section
5
we show that the coefficients of the linearized equation agree for
i=1,2
. Section
6
gives the proof of our main results and Section
7
gives a Runge approximation result used in the proof of the main results. Finally, in the Appendix
A
we show some technical calculations used in Section
2
.

2.
A smooth solution map

The goal of this section is to provide a good solution map for implementing the linearization method. We address the situation when the linearized problem is well-posed in some suitable function space. The latter situation when the linearized problem is not well-posed can be dealt with using Fredholm alternatives, using the arguments used in
[
26
]
. Because for most of the Dirichlet data (up-to a finite dimension space) it is well-posed by the Fredholm theory.

We use the notation
A\lesssim B\implies A\leq CB
for some constant
C
, throughout the rest of the article, and the value of that constant might change from line to line. We start with recalling the following regularity result from
[
21
, Theorem 2.19]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
F\in C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
and
(f_{0},f_{1})\in C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)\times C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
. Then the following boundary value problem

(2.1)

\displaystyle(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)u

\displaystyle=F\quad\qquad\,\,\mbox{in $\Omega$},

\displaystyle(u,\Delta u)

\displaystyle=(f_{0},f_{1})\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$},

admits a unique solution
u_{F,f_{0},f_{1}}=G_{A,X,V}(F,f_{0},f_{1})
and satisfies

(2.2)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u_{F,f_{0},f_{1}}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\lesssim\left(\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\left\lVert f_{0}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert f_{1}\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}\right),

where the implicit constant is independent of
F,f_{0}
and
f_{1}
.

In the next Lemma we study the linearized equation using a fixed point argument.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
Q\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R},C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}),C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n}),C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}))
and
w\in C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
be a solution of
\Delta^{2}w+Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)=0
in
\Omega.

Let
G_{Q}(F,f_{0},f_{1})
be the solution operator of

\begin{cases}\Delta^{2}u+\partial_{q}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)\Delta u+\nabla_{p}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)\cdot\nabla u+\partial_{u}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)u=F&\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
(u,\Delta u)=(f_{0},f_{1})&\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Define
R_{v}(r)=R(v+r)
, where
R:C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\to C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
is given by

(2.3)

\displaystyle R(h)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}[\partial_{u}Q(x,w+th,\nabla(w+th),\Delta(w+th))-\partial_{u}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)]h\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}[\nabla_{p}Q(x,w+th,\nabla(w+th),\Delta(w+th))-\nabla_{p}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)]\cdot\nabla h\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}[\partial_{q}Q(x,w+th,\nabla(w+th),\Delta(w+th))-\partial_{q}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)]\Delta h\,\mathrm{d}t.

Let
v\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
be fixed. Next we define
T_{v}:C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\to C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
by
T_{v}(r)=-G_{Q}(R_{v}(r),0,0)
. Under the above assumptions, there exists
\delta>0
such that the map
T_{v}|_{B_{\delta}}\colon B_{\delta}\to B_{\delta}
is a contraction, where
B_{\delta}
is given by
B_{\delta}=\{u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}):\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\delta\}
. Furthermore,

(2.4)

\left\lVert T_{v}(h)\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\lesssim\left\lVert v+h\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}^{2},\quad h\in B_{\delta}.

Consequently, there exists a unique
r\in B_{\delta}
solving the fixed point equation
r=T_{v}(r)
. The function
r
is also the unique solution of

(2.5)

\displaystyle P_{Q}r

\displaystyle=-R(v+r)\quad\text{in }\Omega,\quad\mbox{and}\quad(r,\Delta r)

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega,

where
P_{Q}r=\Delta^{2}r+\partial_{q}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)\Delta r+\nabla_{p}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)\cdot\nabla r+\partial_{u}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)r.

Proof.

We start with proving the fact that
T_{v}
is a mapping from
B_{\delta}
into itself for
\delta>0
small enough. Let
v,r\in B_{\delta}
for
\delta\leq 1
. By Lemma
2.1
we obtain

(2.6)

\displaystyle\left\lVert T_{v}(r)\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}=\left\lVert G_{Q}(R_{v}(r),0,0\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left\lVert R(v+r)\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})},

and by the fundamental theorem of calculus we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert R(v+r)\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{1}[\partial_{u}Q(x,w+t(v+r),\nabla(w+t(v+r)),\Delta(w+t(v+r)))-\partial_{u}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)](v+r)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}[\nabla_{p}Q(x,w+t(v+r),\nabla(w+t(v+r)),\Delta(w+t(v+r)))-\nabla_{p}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)]\cdot\nabla(v+r)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}[\partial_{q}Q(x,w+t(v+r),\nabla(w+t(v+r)),\Delta(w+t(v+r)))-\partial_{q}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)]\Delta(v+r)\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}\partial_{u}Q(x,w+st(v+r),\nabla(w+st(v+r)),\Delta(w+st(v+r)))(v+r)\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle\quad+\Bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}\nabla_{p}Q(x,w+st(v+r),\nabla(w+st(v+r)),\Delta(w+st(v+r)))\cdot\nabla(v+r)\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle\quad+\Bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{ds}\partial_{q}Q(x,w+st(v+r),\nabla(w+st(v+r)),\Delta(w+st(v+r)))\Delta(v+r)\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\partial^{2}_{u}Q(x,w+st(v+r),\nabla(w+st(v+r)),\Delta(w+st(v+r)))t(v+r)^{2}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle+\Bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{u}\nabla_{p}Q(x,w+st(v+r),\nabla(w+st(v+r)),\Delta(w+st(v+r)))\cdot t\,\nabla(v+r)(v+r)\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle+\Bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{q}\partial_{u}Q(x,w+st(v+r),\nabla(w+st(v+r)),\Delta(w+st(v+r)))\cdot t\,\Delta(v+r)(v+r)\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle+\Bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}_{p}Q(x,w+st(v+r),\nabla(w+st(v+r)),\Delta(w+st(v+r))):t\,\nabla(v+r)\otimes\nabla(v+r)\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle+\Bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{q}\partial_{q}Q(x,w+st(v+r),\nabla(w+st(v+r)),\Delta(w+st(v+r)))\cdot t\,(\Delta(v+r))^{2}\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle+\Bigg\lVert\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{q}\nabla_{p}Q(x,w+st(v+r),\nabla(w+st(v+r)),\Delta(w+st(v+r)))\cdot t\,\cdot\nabla(v+r)\,\Delta(v+r)\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\Bigg\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}.

Next using Lemma
A.1
, we observe that,
\left\lVert R(v+r)\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert(v+r)\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}^{2}\leq C\left\lVert(v+r)\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}^{2}\leq C\delta^{2}.

This shows that
T_{v}
is a mapping from
B_{\delta}
into itself for small
\delta
. The second inequality above proves (
2.4
).

We next prove that
T_{v}
is indeed a contraction mapping. To this end, let
r_{1},r_{2}\in B_{\delta}
. Then utilizing the definition
T_{v}
, we obtain that
T_{v}(r_{1})-T_{v}(r_{2}):=G_{Q}(R_{v}(r_{2}),0,0)-G_{Q}(R_{v}(r_{1}),0,0)
, and it satisfies

(2.7)

\displaystyle\left\lVert T_{v}(r_{1})-T_{v}(r_{2})\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert R_{v}(r_{1})-R_{v}(r_{2})\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}.

We now focus on estimating
C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
-norm of
R_{v}(r_{1})-R_{v}(r_{2})
. To this end, using Lemma
A.2
we deduce that

\displaystyle\left\lVert R_{v}(r_{1})-R_{v}(r_{2})\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert u_{1}-u_{2}\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}(C_{1}\delta t+C_{2}\delta^{2}\,t^{2}).

Next choosing
\delta
small enough we see that

\displaystyle\left\lVert T_{v}(r_{1})-T_{v}(r_{2})\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq

\displaystyle\left\lVert R_{v}(r_{1})-R_{v}(r_{2})\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert u_{1}-u_{2}\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert r_{1}-r_{2}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}.

This shows that
T_{v}
is a contraction map, and by the Banach fixed point theorem we have that the equation
r=T_{v}(r)
has a unique solution in
B_{\delta}
.
∎

The following lemma provides solutions to the nonlinear equation in the vicinity of the fixed solution
w
. Our goal is to construct a well-defined solution map
S_{Q,w}
, where

S_{Q,w}:\text{small solutions of the linearized problem}\longrightarrow\text{solutions of the nonlinear equation close to }w.

More precisely, we establish the following result:

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
Q\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R},C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}),C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n}),C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}))
.
Let
w\in C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
be a solution of

\Delta^{2}w+Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)=0.

Define the coefficients

\displaystyle A(x)=\partial_{q}Q(x,w(x),\nabla w(x),\Delta w(x)),\quad X(x)=\nabla_{p}Q(x,w(x),\nabla w(x),\Delta w(x)),

\displaystyle V(x)=\partial_{u}Q(x,w(x),\nabla w(x),\Delta w(x)).

Then the following holds:

1.

There exist constants
\delta,C>0
and a
C^{k-1}
map
\Phi=\Phi_{Q,w}:B_{\delta}\to B_{\delta}
satisfying

\Phi(B_{\delta})|_{\partial\Omega}=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\Phi(0)=D\Phi(0)=0.

Moreover, the following estimate holds:

(2.8)

\|\Phi(v)\|_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\|v\|_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}^{2},

and the map
S_{Q}=S_{Q,w}:B_{\delta}\to C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, defined by

u=S_{Q,w}(v)=w+v+\Phi(v)

is a
C^{k-1}
map satisfying

(2.9)

\Delta^{2}u+Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=\Delta^{2}v+A\Delta v+X\cdot\nabla v+Vv\quad\text{in }\Omega,

with
S_{Q,w}^{\prime}(0)v=v
. In particular, if
v
solves

\Delta^{2}v+A\Delta v+X\cdot\nabla v+Vv=0,

then
u=S_{Q,w}(v)
solves

\Delta^{2}u+Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=0.

2.

Conversely, if
\delta
is sufficiently small, then for any solution
u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
of

\Delta^{2}u+Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=0

with
\|u-w\|_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\delta
, there exists a unique solution
v\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
of

\Delta^{2}v+A\Delta v+X\cdot\nabla v+Vv=0

such that
u=S_{Q,w}(v)
. The function
v
is explicitly given by

(2.10)

v=G_{Q}(0,(u-w)|_{\partial\Omega},\Delta(u-w)|_{\partial\Omega}),

and satisfies the estimate

\|v\|_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\lesssim\left(\|u-w\|_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\|\Delta(u-w)\|_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\right).

Proof.

The goal is to construct the map
S_{Q}
. We look for the solution
u
of (
2.9
) in the form

u=w+v+r
and formulate a fixed point equation for
r
. A use of Taylor series expansion implies

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}u+Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}(w+v+r)+Q(x,w+v+r,\nabla(w+v+r),\Delta(w+v+r))

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}w+\Delta^{2}(v+r)+Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)

\displaystyle\qquad+[A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V](v+r)+R_{v}(r),

where
R_{v}(r)=R(v+r)
is given by

\displaystyle R(h)

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}[\partial_{u}Q(x,w+th,\nabla(w+th),\Delta(w+th))-\partial_{u}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)]h\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{1}[\nabla_{p}Q(x,w+th,\nabla(w+th),\Delta(w+th))-\nabla_{p}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)]\cdot\nabla h\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{1}[\partial_{q}Q(x,w+th,\nabla(w+th),\Delta(w+th))-\partial_{q}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)]\,\Delta h\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
w
is a solution of
\Delta^{2}u+Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=0
, this implies
u
solves
(
2.9
) if
r
satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}r+A\Delta r+X\cdot\nabla r+Vr=-R_{v}(r)\quad\mbox{in $\Omega$}.

By Lemma
2.2
, we have that for each
v\in B_{\delta}
there is a unique
r=r_{v}
satisfying the above equation. Hence the mapping
v\mapsto r_{v}
is well-posed for
v\in B_{\delta}
. Next we show that this map is indeed a
C^{k-1}
map using implicit function theorem. To this end, define the map

(2.11)

\displaystyle F:C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\times C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\rightarrow C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\quad\mbox{by}\quad F(v,r)=r-T_{v}(r)=r+G_{Q}(R_{v}(r),0,0).

Note that
F
satisfies
F(0,0)=0
. Next we see that
F\in C^{k-1}
, since
R_{v}\in C^{k-1}
and
G_{Q}
is linear. Moreover,
(D_{r}F)|_{(0,0)}(h)=h.

This implies it is a linear isomorphism from
C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
into itself. Hence by the implicit function theorem, there exist open balls
B_{\delta_{1}}
and
B_{\delta_{2}}
and a
C^{k-1}
map
\Phi:B_{\delta_{1}}\rightarrow B_{\delta_{2}}
such that

\displaystyle F(v,\Phi(v))=0.

Note that
r_{v}
found by Lemma
2.2
is the unique solution of
F(v,\cdot)=0
in
B_{\delta}
for
v\in B_{\delta}
. Thus we conclude that for
\delta<\min\{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\}
,
r_{v}\in B_{\delta_{2}}
, and
\Phi(v)=r_{v}
. Consequently, we have show that for each
v\in B_{\delta}
there is a unique
r_{v}\in B_{\delta}
such that
u=w+v+r_{v}
is a solution of (
2.9
). Moreover, the map

\displaystyle S_{Q,w}:B_{\delta}\rightarrow C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\quad\mbox{ given by}\quad\mbox{$u_{v}=S_{Q,w}(v)$ is $C^{k-1}$}.

We next show that
\Phi
satisfies the other properties. By (
2.4
) we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert r\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}=\left\lVert T_{v}(r)\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert(v+r)\right\rVert^{2}_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left(\left\lVert r\right\rVert^{2}_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\right).

This further entails
\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\geq\left\lVert r\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}(1-C\delta)
, since
\left\lVert r\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\delta
.
Next using the fact that
\Phi(v)=r
and
\delta
small enough we conclude that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Phi(v)\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert v\right\rVert^{2}_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})},

which in turn implies (
2.8
), and
\Phi(0)=0
. The preceding estimate further gives that
D\Phi(0)=0
. This completes the proof of first part.

We now focus on the converse statement.
Suppose
u
solves
\Delta^{2}u+Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=0
in
\Omega
and
\left\lVert u-w\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq\delta
. Denote
\tilde{u}=u-w
and we wish to construct
v
solving the linear partial differential equation
(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)v=0
in
\Omega
such that
\tilde{u}=v+\Phi(v)
. Let
\phi
be the unique solution of (
2.1
) given by Lemma
2.1
with
F=0
and
(f_{0},f_{1})=(\tilde{u},\Delta\tilde{u})|_{\partial\Omega}
. Denote
v=\phi
, this gives

\displaystyle\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left(\left\lVert\tilde{u}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\left\lVert\Delta\tilde{u}\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\right).

It remains to show that
r=u-w-v
satisfies
r=\Phi(v)
. To this end, we observe that

\displaystyle(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)r

\displaystyle=(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)(u-w)

\displaystyle=Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)-Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)-(A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)(u-w)

\displaystyle=Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)-Q(x,(w+v+r),\nabla(w+v+r),\Delta(w+v+r))-(A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)(v+r)

\displaystyle=-R_{v}(r).

The first part of the proof ensures that
r=\Phi(v)
if
\delta
is chosen small enough. This proves that
u=S_{Q,w}(v)
. To show the uniqueness of
v
we assume that
u=S_{Q,w}(v_{1})=S_{Q,w}(v_{2})
. Then by definition we have
v_{1}-v_{2}=r_{1}-r_{2}=\Phi(v_{1})-\Phi(v_{2})
. Since
(v_{1}-v_{2})
solves

\displaystyle(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)u

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega$}

\displaystyle(u,\Delta u)=(0,0)\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$}.

Hence by Lemma
2.1
we have that
v_{1}=v_{2}
, proving the uniqueness of
v
. The proof is complete.
∎

To state our next result we recall that
\mathcal{V}_{A,X,V}=\{u\in{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}:(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)u=0\}.

Lemma 2.4
.

In the setting of Lemma
2.3
, if
v
is small and solves
(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)u=0
for
A=\partial_{q}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)
,
X=\nabla_{p}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)
and
V=\partial_{q}Q(x,w,\nabla w,\Delta w)
. Define

\displaystyle A_{v}(x)

\displaystyle:=\partial_{q}Q(x,S_{Q,w}(v),\nabla S_{Q,w}(v),\Delta S_{Q,w}(v)),

\displaystyle X_{v}(x)

\displaystyle:=\nabla_{p}Q(x,S_{Q,w}(v),\nabla S_{Q,w}(v),\Delta S_{Q,w}(v)),

\displaystyle V_{v}(x)

\displaystyle:=\partial_{u}Q(x,S_{Q,w}(v),\nabla S_{Q,w}(v),\Delta S_{Q,w}(v)).

If
v\in\mathcal{V}_{A,X,V}
is small, then the map
DS_{Q,w}
is an isomorphism from
\mathcal{V}_{A,X,V}
onto
\mathcal{V}_{A_{v},X_{v},V_{v}}
.

Proof.

Define
v_{t}=v+th
for
t
small and
h\in\mathcal{V}_{A,X,V}
. Then
u_{t}=S_{Q,w}(v_{t})
solves
\Delta^{2}u+Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=0
in
\Omega
. Since
u_{t}
is smooth in
t
, the function
\dot{u}_{0}=\partial_{t}u_{t}|_{t=0}=DS_{Q,w}(v)h
satisfies

\displaystyle(\Delta^{2}+A_{v}\Delta+X_{v}(x)\cdot\nabla+V_{v}(x))\dot{u}_{0}=0.

This shows that
DS_{Q,w}
is a mapping from
\mathcal{V}_{A,X,V}
into
\mathcal{V}_{A_{v},X_{v},V_{v}}
.

Suppose
v\in V_{A,X,V}
is small. For
t
small define
u_{t}=S_{Q,S_{Q,w}(v)}(t\tilde{h})
, then by the converse part of the Lemma
2.3
, we have
u_{t}=S_{Q,w}(v_{t})
for a unique small solution
v_{t}\in\mathcal{V}_{A,X,V}
and it is given by

\displaystyle v_{t}=G_{Q}(0,(u_{t}-w)|_{\partial\Omega},\Delta(u_{t}-w)|_{\partial\Omega}).

Since
S_{Q,w}(v_{0})=u_{0}=S_{Q,S_{Q,w}(v)}(0)=S_{Q,w}(v)
, this implies
v=v_{0}
. Furthermore, differentiating the relations
u_{t}=S_{Q,w}(v_{t})
and
u_{t}=S_{Q,S_{Q,w}(v)}(t\tilde{h})
with respect to
t
we obtain

\displaystyle DS_{Q,w}(v)\dot{v}_{0}=\dot{u}_{0}=DS_{Q,S_{Q,w}(v)}(0)\tilde{h}=\tilde{h}

This shows that
DS_{Q,w}:\mathcal{V}_{A,X,V}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}_{A_{v},X_{v},V_{v}}
surjective.

Next we show that for
h\in\mathcal{V}_{A,X,V}
such that
DS_{Q,w}(v)h=0
, then
h=0
. Since
S_{Q,w}(v)=w+v+\Phi(v)
, this together with
DS_{Q,w}(v)h=0
implies
h+D\Phi(v)h=0
. However,
D\Phi(0)=0
together with
Q
being at least
C^{2}
entails
\left\lVert D\Phi(v)\right\rVert\leq\frac{1}{2}
, when
v
is small enough. This implies
\left\lVert h\right\rVert\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert h\right\rVert\implies h=0
. Thus
DS_{Q,w}
is bijective and bounded, and by open mapping theorem it is an isomorphism. This completes the proof.
∎

3.
Estimates for solutions based on their Cauchy data

We follow the method presented in
[
26
]
. The proofs of the following Lemmas are similar to the ones in
[
26
]
*Section 3 but we record them here for completeness.

Let
u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, then we define three operators
B_{\partial\Omega,\mathcal{N}}\colon C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\to C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)\times C^{3,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)\times C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)\times C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega),

B_{\partial\Omega,\mathcal{N}}^{N}\colon C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
L\colon C^{4,\alpha}(\Omega)\rightarrow C^{\alpha}(\Omega)
by

(3.1)

\displaystyle B_{\partial\Omega,\mathcal{N}}(u)

\displaystyle:=(u|_{\partial\Omega},\partial_{\nu}u|_{\partial\Omega},\Delta u|_{\partial\Omega},\partial_{\nu}\Delta u|_{\partial\Omega}),

(3.2)

\displaystyle B_{\partial\Omega,\mathcal{N}}^{N}(u)

\displaystyle:=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert\partial_{\nu}u\right\rVert_{C^{3,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert\Delta u\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert\partial_{\nu}\Delta u\right\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)},

(3.3)

\displaystyle Lu

\displaystyle:=(\Delta^{2}+A(x)\Delta+X(x)\cdot\nabla+V(x))u.

Here
V,A\in C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
and
X\in C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Then there is a constant

C>0
such that for any
u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
one has

(3.4)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\lesssim\left(\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert Lu\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right).

Proof.

We first define the Banach space
X=C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)\times C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\times H^{2}(\Omega)
, with

\left\lVert(f,F,u)\right\rVert_{X}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)},

and an operator
T\colon C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\to X
,

T(u)=(u|_{\partial\Omega},Lu,j(u)).

Here
j\colon C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\to H^{2}(\Omega)
is the natural inclusion operator. Now
T
is continuous, injective and linear. If we would also have that
T
is bijective onto
\mathrm{Ran}(T)
then by the open mapping theorem we would have a bounded inverse
S\colon\mathrm{Ran}(T)\to C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
and thus

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}=\left\lVert STu\right\rVert_{C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert Tu\right\rVert_{X}

for any
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, which would prove the claim.

To show bijectivity of
T
we need to prove that
\mathrm{Ran}(T)
is closed. Let
u_{j}\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
T(u_{j})\to(f,F,u)
in
X
. Then
u_{j}|_{\partial\Omega}\to f
in
C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)
,
Lu_{j}\to F
in
C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
and
u_{j}\to u
in
H^{2}(\Omega)
. On the other hand also
u_{j}|_{\partial\Omega}\to u|_{\partial\Omega}
in
H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
,
Lu_{j}\to Lu
in
H^{-2}(\Omega)
and thus by uniqueness of limits we get
Lu=F
and
u_{\partial\Omega}=f
. Then by elliptic regularity
u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
and thus
T(u)=(f,F,u)
which shows that
\mathrm{Ran}(T)
is closed.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Then there is a constant

C>0
such that for any
u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
one has

(3.5)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\lesssim\left(B_{\partial\Omega,\mathcal{N}}^{N}(u)+\left\lVert Lu\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\right).

Proof.

We argue by contradiction, thus suppose that for any
m
there is
u_{m}\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
such that

(3.6)

\left\lVert u_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}>m\left(B_{\partial\Omega,\mathcal{N}}^{N}(u_{m})+\left\lVert Lu_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\right).

By Lemma
3.1
we have
\left\lVert u_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left(\left\lVert u_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert Lu_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\left\lVert u_{m}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right)
, and if we scale
u_{m}
so that
\left\lVert u_{m}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}=1
we obtain
\left\lVert u_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left(\left\lVert u_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert Lu_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+1\right).

A combination of this with (
3.6
) entails

(3.7)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(B_{\partial\Omega,\mathcal{N}}^{N}(u_{m})+\left\lVert Lu_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+1\right)\leq C\left(\frac{1}{m}\left\lVert u_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+1\right),

and hence the sequence
u_{m}
is uniformly bounded in
C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
for
m
sufficiently large.

By
[
1
]
*Theorem 1.34 there is a compact embedding from
C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
to
C^{4}(\overline{\Omega})
and thus there is converging subsequence of
u_{m}
, denoted by
u_{m}
, such that
u_{m}\to u\in C^{4}(\overline{\Omega})
. For this subsequence we also get from (
3.6
), (
3.7
) that

Lu_{m}\to 0,\quad B_{\partial\Omega,\mathcal{N}}(u_{m})\to 0

in the corresponding spaces. This implies that
B_{\partial\Omega,\mathcal{N}}(u)=0
and
Lu=0
by the uniqueness of limits. Now by unique continuation we must have
u=0
. But this is a contradiction since
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\left\lVert u_{m}\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}=1
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
Q\in C^{k}(\mathbb{R},C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}),C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n}),C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}))
and
u_{1}\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfy
\Delta^{2}u_{1}+Q(x,u_{1},\nabla u_{1},\Delta u_{1})=0
in
\Omega
. If
u_{2}\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
is any other solution of
\Delta^{2}u+Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=0
in
\Omega
and
\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq M
for
i=1,2
, then

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left\lVert u_{1}-u_{2}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C(M,Q)\cdot B_{\partial\Omega,\mathcal{N}}^{N}(u_{1}-u_{2}).

Proof.

Denote
v=u_{1}-u_{2}
. Then from Lemma
3.1
we get

(3.9)

\displaystyle\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert Lv\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert\Delta^{2}v\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right).

Hence we still need to estimate
\left\lVert\Delta^{2}v\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}
and
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}
. Now if we denote
v_{t}=tu_{2}+(1-t)u_{1}
, then

\displaystyle-\Delta^{2}v

\displaystyle=Q(x,u_{2},\nabla u_{2},\Delta u_{2})-Q(x,u_{1},\nabla u_{1},\Delta u_{1})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}Q(x,v_{t},\nabla v_{t},\Delta v_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{u}Q(x,v_{t},\nabla v_{t},\Delta v_{t})v+\nabla_{p}Q(x,v_{t},\nabla v_{t},\Delta v_{t})\cdot\nabla v+\partial_{q}Q(x,v_{t},\nabla v_{t},\Delta v_{t})\Delta v\,\mathrm{d}t.

Thus we conclude from Lemma
A.1

\displaystyle\left\lVert\Delta^{2}v\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C(M,Q)\left(\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\left\lVert\nabla v\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}+\left\lVert\Delta v\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\right).

Notice that here we use the assumption
\left\lVert u_{i}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq M
.
Applying Sobolev embeddings, interpolation of
L^{p}
-spaces and Young’s inequality with
\varepsilon
(as in the proof of
[
26
]
*Lemma 3.3; the steps are the same) we get

\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\left\lVert\nabla v\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert\nabla v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\left\lVert\Delta v\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert\Delta v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Combining this with (
3.9
) implies

(3.11)

\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left(\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\right).

To estimate
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}
we use a Carleman estimate (see e.g.
[
18
]
*Theorem 4.1.): there are
C,\tau_{0}>0
and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
such that when
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
, one has

(3.12)

\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{\tau}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{C}{\tau^{3/2}}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\Delta u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\frac{C}{\tau}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

for any
v\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
. Using only

\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{C}{\tau^{3/2}}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\Delta u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\frac{C}{\tau}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

to estimate
\frac{1}{\tau^{3/2}}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\Delta v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
and combining with (
3.12
) we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{\tau}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\nabla v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{1}{\tau^{3/2}}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\Delta v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\tau^{3}}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\Delta^{2}v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\frac{C}{\tau}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\nabla v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\frac{C}{\tau^{3/2}}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\Delta v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\frac{C}{\tau^{5/2}}\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\nabla(\Delta v)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

Using the observation (
3
) and choosing
\tau=\tau(M,Q)
large but fixed gives

\displaystyle\tilde{C}\left(\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\nabla v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\Delta v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\nabla v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\Delta v\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}+\left\lVert e^{\tau\varphi}\nabla(\Delta v)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\right).

Then
c(M,Q)\leq e^{\tau\varphi}\leq C(M,Q)
implies
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(M,Q)\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{H^{3}(\partial\Omega)}
.
This together with (
3.11
) proves the claim.
∎

4.
Controlling Cauchy data

In Section
2
, we have constructed a smooth solution map for the equation,
\Delta^{2}u+Q(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=0
in
\Omega,
however that is not enough to proceed further. Therefore the goal of this section is construct a smooth solution map
T_{Q_{2},w_{2}}
for the nonlinear equation
\Delta^{2}+Q_{2}(x,u,\nabla u,\Delta u)=0
in
\Omega
parameterized on solutions
v
of

\Delta^{2}v+A_{1}\Delta v+X_{1}\cdot\nabla v+V_{1}v=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega$},

which will be particularly useful when proving the main results.
There are two primary reasons for constructing such a map:

1.

Suppose
u_{1}=S_{Q_{1},w_{1}}(v_{1})
. To proceed we need to construct a solution

for the nonlinear equation with

in such a way that it has the same Cauchy data as

and depends smoothly on the solution

. The inclusion of Cauchy data gives the first requirement, but the second is not guaranteed by the first solution operator constructed in Section

2.

Another challenge arises when identifying the first-order derivatives, namely

\partial_{u}Q(x,w_{i},\nabla w_{i},\Delta w_{i}),\quad\nabla_{p}Q(x,w_{i},\nabla w_{i},\Delta w_{i}),\quad\partial_{q}Q(x,w_{i},\nabla w_{i},\Delta w_{i}),

for
i=1,2
. This identification relies on the linearization method, which requires differentiating the solution maps
S_{Q_{i},w_{i}}
in the same direction
v
. However, to use the same parameter

for both solution operators
S_{Q_{i},w_{i}}
,

must satisfy

(\Delta^{2}+A_{i}\Delta+X_{i}\cdot\nabla+V_{i})v=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,

for

To this end, we define the following function spaces:

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle:=\{u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}):u=\partial_{\nu}u=\Delta u=\partial_{\nu}\Delta u=0\,\,\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$}\},

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle:=\{(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)u:u\in Y\}.

Lemma 4.1
.

Then the function spaces
Y
and
Z
are Banach spaces.

Proof.

It is clear that
Y
are Banach space, since it is closed. One can also show that
Z
is a Banach space. Let
u_{n}=Lv_{n}\in Z
be such that
u_{n}\to u\in C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
. Since
B_{\partial\Omega,\mathcal{N}}(u_{n})=0
for all

, we have by Lemma
3.2
that
\left\lVert v_{n}-v_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert Lv_{n}-Lv_{m}\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}.

Hence
v_{n}
is a Cauchy sequence in
Y
which implies the existence of
v\in Y
so that
v_{n}\to v
in
C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
. Now
\left\lVert u_{n}-Lv\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}=\left\lVert L(v_{n}-v)\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert v_{n}-v\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}.

Thus
u_{n}\to u=Lv
in
C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
which says that
Z
is a Banach space.
∎

The following lemma demonstrates the existence of a bounded projection operator
P:C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\rightarrow C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\cap Z
. For this, recall from Section
3
the operator
L\colon C^{4,\alpha}(\Omega)\to C^{\alpha}(\Omega),

Lu=(\Delta^{2}+A(x)\Delta+X(x)\cdot\nabla+V(x))u.

We define the formal adjoint of
L
to be
L^{*}\colon C^{4,\alpha}(\Omega)\to C^{\alpha}(\Omega),

L^{*}u=\Delta^{2}u+\Delta(A(x)u)-\nabla\cdot(Xu)+V(x)u.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
Y
and
Z
be as above. There is a projection operator
P:C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\rightarrow C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\cap Z
given by
P(u)=Ly,

where
y\in C^{8,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
is the unique solution of

(4.1)

\begin{cases}L^{*}(Ly)=L^{*}u&\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
y=\partial_{\nu}y=\Delta y=\partial_{\nu}\Delta y=0&\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
y_{1},y_{2}
be two solutions for the above boundary value problem and denote
y=y_{1}-y_{2}
. Then
L^{*}(Ly)=0
and

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}L^{*}(Ly)\cdot y\,dx=\int_{\Omega}(Ly)^{2}\,dx

since
y
satisfies the boundary conditions in (
4.1
). Thus
y_{1}=y_{2}
and solutions to (
4.1
) are unique.

Existence of solutions can be seen by using standard arguments as follows. Define a coercive bilinear form
B(u,v)=(Lu,L^{*}v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\gamma_{1}(\Delta u,\Delta v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\gamma_{2}(\nabla u,\nabla v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\gamma_{3}(u,v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
,
u,v\in Y_{2},
for
\gamma_{j}>0
,
j=1,2,3
, sufficiently large depending on the coefficients of
L
. Using the Riesz representation theorem gives existence of weak solutions in
H^{4}_{0}(\Omega)
for
L^{*}(Ly)+\gamma y=F\in H^{-4}(\Omega)
. Now the uniqueness argument above gives that one can solve the equation
L^{*}(Ly)=F
uniquely even though Fredholm theory tells us that there is a countable set of eigenvalues where uniqueness of solutions could fail. Then by elliptic regularity for
u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
one has
y\in C^{8,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
.

We are left to prove that
P
, as defined above, is a projection. Let
u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
and
y\in C^{8,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
be the unique solution to (
4.1
). Now

P(Pu)=P(Ly)=Lv,

where
v
is the unique solution of

\begin{cases}L^{*}(Lv)=L^{*}(Ly)&\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
v=\partial_{\nu}v=\Delta v=\partial_{\nu}\Delta v=0&\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

This implies that
w=v-y
solves

\begin{cases}L^{*}(Lw)=0&\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
w=\partial_{\nu}w=\Delta w=\partial_{\nu}\Delta w=0&\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

and hence by unique continuation
v=y
. This implies
P(Pu)=Lv=Ly=P(u)
and thus
P
is a projection onto
C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\cap Z
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
Y
and
Z
be as defined above. Then the operator

\Delta^{2}+A(x)\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V:Y\rightarrow Z

is bounded and bijective, and has a bounded inverse

.

Proof.

By definition of
Z
, we have that
\Delta^{2}+A(x)\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V
is surjective. To show the injectivity, we assume that there is
y\in Y
and
\tilde{y}\in Y
such that

\displaystyle(\Delta^{2}+A(x)\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)y=(\Delta^{2}+A(x)\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)\tilde{y}.

Then the function

solves the following:

(4.2)

\displaystyle(\Delta^{2}+A(x)\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)y

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega$},

\displaystyle(u,\Delta u,\partial_{\nu}u,\partial_{\nu}\Delta u)

\displaystyle=0\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$}.

Therefore by unique continuation principle we conclude that

. This implies the operator
(\Delta^{2}+A(x)\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)
is injective. Moreover, one has

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\Delta^{2}+A(x)\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)u\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}\leq C\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}.

This says that

is a bounded linear operator which is bijective. Hence, by open mapping theorem there exists a bounded inverse

of
\Delta^{2}+A(x)\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V
. This finishes the proof.
∎

7.
Runge approximation

This section is devoted to proving the Runge approximation result for a fourth order biharmonic equation. A linear differential operator
P
satisfies the
Runge Property
if the adjoint operator
P^{t}
satisfies the
Unique Continuation Property
, see
[
37
]
. The notion of classical Runge approximation property dates back to the work of
[
37
,
39
]
. We also refer to some recent works
[
43
,
44
,
36
,
26
]
where Runge approximation property is used to solve certain inverse problem. In this paper we are interested to a quantitative version of Runge property and it is somehow related to the density of solutions of elliptic partial differential equations in large domain into a smaller domain with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm in the smaller domain. This can be achieved using Hahn-Banach theorem. A general set-up for such approximation results can be found in
[
13
]
.

To this end, we recall that
Lu=(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)u.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary, and let
A\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}),X\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
V\in C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
. Assume that
0
is not a Dirichlet eigenvalue of
L
in
\Omega
. Then for any
x_{0}\in\overline{\Omega}
, there is a solution
u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
of
Lu=0
in
\Omega
such that
u(x_{0})\neq 0,\nabla u(x_{0})\neq 0,\nabla^{2}u(x_{0})\neq 0
.

Proof.

Since
A\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}),X\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
V\in C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, we may choose a ball
B
such that
\overline{\Omega}\subset B
and extend
A,X,V
in such a way that
A\in C_{c}^{2,\alpha}(B),X\in C_{c}^{1,\alpha}(B;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
V\in C_{c}^{\alpha}(B)
. We can also choose
B
in such a way that
0
is not an eigenvalue of the operator
Lu=(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)u
. This can be achieved considering the symmetric part of
L
and using the continuous dependency of eigenvalues with respect to domains; see for instance
[
4
,
46
,
50
,
20
]
.

Next by utilizing
[
6
, Theorem 1 in Section 5.4 of Part 2]
there is a small neighborhood
\Omega_{x_{0}}
of
x_{0}
and a function
u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}_{x_{0}})
solving
Lu=(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)u=0
in
\Omega_{x_{0}}
such that
u(x_{0})=\partial_{x_{k}}u(x_{0})=\Delta u(x_{0})=1
,
1\leq k\leq n
. By Lemma
7.3
there exists a
\tilde{u}\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
solving
(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)\tilde{u}=0
in
\Omega
with
\tilde{u}(x_{0}),\partial_{x_{k}}\tilde{u}(x_{0}),\Delta\tilde{u}(x_{0})
being as close to
u(x_{0})=\partial_{x_{k}}u(x_{0})=\Delta u(x_{0})=1
as possible.
∎

Let
L,A,X
and
V
be as before and assume that
0
is not a Dirichlet eigenvalue of
L
in
\Omega
. For a bounded linear functional
\mu
on
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
we say that

is a
very weak solution
of

(7.1)

\begin{cases}L^{*}u=\mu&\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
u=\Delta u=0&\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\end{cases}

if
\int_{\Omega}uL\varphi\,dx=\mu(\varphi)
,
for any
\varphi\in C^{4}(\overline{\Omega})\cap H^{4}_{N}(\Omega)
where
H^{4}_{N}(\Omega):=\{v\in H^{4}(\Omega):\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=\Delta\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=0\}
.
We next show the existence of a very weak solution to (
7.1
).

Proposition 7.2
.

For any bounded linear functional
\mu
on
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
there exists a very weak solution
u\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
,
p<\frac{n+2}{n}
, to (
7.1
).

Proof.

Since
0
is not an eigenvalue of
L
(see
[
21
, Equation 2.17]
), by
[
21
]
*Theorem 2.20 the following boundary value problem has unique solution
u\in W^{4,q}(\Omega)
for every
f\in L^{q}(\Omega)

(7.2)

\begin{cases}Lu=f&\quad\text{in }\Omega\\
u=\Delta u=0&\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Now take
q
such that
2q>n+2
, the condition
2q>n+2
implies that
W^{4,q}(\Omega)\subset W^{2,q}(\Omega)\subset C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})

Let us denote by
X:=\mathrm{Range}(L)\subset L^{p}(\Omega)
. Then for every
\varphi\in X
we have
L\psi=\varphi
for some
\psi\in W^{4,q}_{N}(\Omega)\cap C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
. For a fixed continuous linear functional
\mu
on
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
define an operator

and for this operator we have

\lvert B(L\psi)\rvert\leq C\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{*}\left\lVert\psi\right\rVert\leq C\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{*}\left\lVert L\,\psi\right\rVert.

Here
\left\lVert\mu\right\rVert_{*}=\sup_{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})}=1}\lvert\mu(f)\rvert
.
Hence
B
is bounded on

, and by Hahn-Banach it is bounded on

. Hence the Riesz representation gives the existence of

with the property that
p^{-1}+q^{-1}=1
and

\int_{\Omega}u\varphi\,dx=B(\varphi)\quad\text{for all }\varphi\in L^{q}(\Omega).

For any
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
we have
B(L\psi)=\int\psi\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int u\,L\psi\,=\int L^{*}u\,\psi.

This implies
L^{*}u=\mu
in
\Omega
, where

with

, since

.

Moreover, for open set

one could proceed in the same way to show that

. This can be done using the fact that

is an elliptic differential operator of order

and therefore it has a parametrix which is a pseudodifferential operator of order

; see
[

. Then the combination of these facts along with

conclude the proof.
∎

We now prove the following Runge approximation result. To this end, Let us consider two open sets
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and also consider the sets

\displaystyle N(\Omega_{1})=\{u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega_{1}}):Lu=0\,\,\mbox{in $\Omega_{1}$}\},\,\quad\mbox{and}\quad N(\Omega)=\{u\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}):Lu=0\,\,\mbox{in $\Omega$}\}.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\Omega_{1}\subset\Omega
be two open subsets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
is connected. Suppose
A\in C_{c}^{2,\alpha}(\Omega),X\in C_{c}^{1,\alpha}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and

and
0
is not a Dirichlet eigenvalue of

in

is dense in

with

norm. Moreover, given any

and any

, there exists

such that
\left\lVert u-v|_{\Omega_{1}}\right\rVert_{C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{1})}\leq\epsilon
.

Proof.

We use the Hahn-Banach theorem to prove this result. Let
\mu
be a linear functional on
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}_{1})
that satisfies
\mu(v|_{\overline{\Omega}_{1}})=0
for all
v\in N(\Omega)
. We now extend
\mu
, denoted by
\overline{\mu}
, as a continuous linear functional on
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
in the following way. Define

such that

(7.3)

\displaystyle\overline{\mu}(u):=\mu(u|_{\overline{\Omega}_{1}})\quad\mbox{for all $u\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})$}.

By Riesz representation theorem,
\overline{\mu}
is a Radon measure on
\overline{\Omega}
, and by the definition we have
\operatorname{supp}\overline{\mu}\subset\overline{\Omega}_{1}
. Next we utilize Proposition
7.2
to find a very weak solution
\psi\in L^{p}(\Omega)
of

\displaystyle L^{*}\psi

\displaystyle=\overline{\mu}\quad\mbox{in $\Omega$}\quad\mbox{and},\quad(\psi,\Delta\psi)=0\quad\mbox{on $\partial\Omega$}.

Our next goal is to show that

(7.4)

\displaystyle\psi=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}$}.

Step 1.
Assume that (
7.4
) holds. We then claim that
\mu|_{N(\Omega_{1})}=0
.

Since
\operatorname{supp}\psi\subset\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
, there exists
{\psi_{j}}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega_{1})
with
\psi_{j}\rightarrow\psi
in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. Given any

we can choose
\overline{u}\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
such that
\overline{u}=u
on
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
. This implies

\displaystyle\mu(u)=\overline{\mu}(\overline{u})=\int_{\Omega}\psi\,L\overline{u}=\lim_{j}\int_{\Omega}\psi_{j}\,L\overline{u}=\int_{\Omega_{1}}\psi_{j}\,Lu=0,

and concludes that
\mu|_{N(\Omega_{1})}=0
.

Step 2.
We now prove (
7.4
).

To prove this, we use the fact that support of
\overline{\mu}
is contained in
\overline{\Omega}_{1}
, and combine this with unique continuation principle. Since
\operatorname{supp}{\overline{\mu}}\subset\overline{\Omega}_{1}
, this implies
\psi
solves

L^{*}\psi=0
in
\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
.

Now we are going to use the fact that the coefficients
A,X,V
are compactly supported in
\Omega
. Let us assume that support of
A,X,V
are contained in a compact set
K\subset\Omega
. Then we choose another open set
\Omega_{2}
such that

. Since
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{2}
is connected and
\psi
solves
\Delta^{2}\psi=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega\setminus\Omega_{2}$}
. This implies that
\psi\in C^{4,\alpha}
near
\partial\Omega
. Infact,
\psi
is
C^{4,\alpha}
in
\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
. We now let

and choose a cut-off function
\chi
in such a way that
\chi=1
near
\Omega_{1}
and
\psi\in C^{4,\alpha}
in
\operatorname{supp}(1-\chi)\cap\overline{\Omega}
. Utilizing these facts we deduce

(7.5)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\psi Lv=\int_{\Omega}\psi L(\chi\,v)+\int_{\Omega}\psi L(1-\chi)v

Next using the definition of very weak solution, the first term of above we can write as:

(7.6)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\psi L(\chi\,v)=\int_{\Omega}\psi(\Delta^{2}+A\Delta+X\cdot\nabla+V)(\chi\,v)=\overline{\mu}(\chi v)=\overline{\mu}(\chi v).

Using integration by parts,
\operatorname{supp}(1-\chi)v\subset\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
, and the fact that
\psi
is regular in

, we can write
\int_{\Omega}\psi L(1-\chi)v=\int_{\Omega}L^{*}\psi\,(1-\chi)v+\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}\Delta\psi\,v+\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}\psi\,\Delta v
. We combine this with (

) and (

\displaystyle 0=\overline{\mu}(\chi\,v)+\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}\Delta\psi\,v+\int_{\partial\Omega}\Delta\psi\,\partial_{\nu}v=\mu(v|_{\overline{\Omega}_{1}})+\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}\Delta\psi\,v+\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu}\psi\,\Delta v.

Since

, then by the assumption we have that
\mu(v|_{\overline{\Omega}_{1}})=0
. Next choosing specific boundary values of

we conclude that

on
\partial\Omega
. Therefore we have that
\psi
satisfies

(7.7)

\displaystyle L_{A,X,V}^{*}\psi=0\quad\mbox{in $\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}$ with $(\psi,\partial_{\nu}\psi,\partial_{\nu}^{2}\psi,\partial^{3}_{\nu}\psi)=0$ on $\partial\Omega$.}

This along with unique continuation principle gives

in
\Omega\setminus\overline{\Omega}_{1}
; see for instance

and the references therein. This completes the proof.

Lemma A.1

4.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\partial^{l}_{u}Q(x,f(x),\nabla f(x),\Delta f(x))\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\lvert\partial^{l}_{u}Q(x,f(x),\nabla f(x),\Delta f(x))\rvert

\displaystyle+\sup_{x,y\in\overline{\Omega},x\neq y}\frac{\lvert\partial^{l}_{u}Q(x,f(x),\nabla f(x),\Delta f(x))-\partial^{l}_{u}Q(y,f(y),\nabla f(y),\Delta f(y))\rvert}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{\alpha}}.

Using
M=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})}
, we can obtain an upper bound of
\sup_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\lvert\partial^{l}_{u}Q(x,f(x),\nabla f(x),\Delta f(x))\rvert
which is
\sup_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\left\lVert\partial^{l}_{u}Q(x,\nabla f(x),\Delta f(x))\right\rVert_{C[-M,M]}
. Similarly,
\sup_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\left\lVert\partial^{l}_{u}Q(x,\nabla f(x),\Delta f(x))\right\rVert_{C[-M,M]}
can be bounded by
\sup_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\left\lVert\partial^{l}_{u}Q(x)\right\rVert_{C([-M,M],[-M,M]^{n},[-M,M])}
. Moreover, using
l\leq k
, we obtain

(A.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\lvert\partial^{l}_{u}Q(x,f(x),\nabla f(x),\Delta f(x))\rvert\leq\sup_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}\left\lVert Q(x)\right\rVert_{C^{k}([-M,M],[-M,M]^{n},[-M,M])}.

Similarly, one can bound
\sup_{x,y\in\overline{\Omega},x\neq y}\frac{\lvert\partial^{l}_{u}Q(x,f(x),\nabla f(x),\Delta f(x))-\partial^{l}_{u}Q(y,f(y),\nabla f(y),\Delta f(y))\rvert}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{\alpha}}
by

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in\overline{\Omega},x\neq y}\frac{\lvert\partial^{l}_{u}Q(x,f(x),\nabla f(x),\Delta f(x))-\partial^{l}_{u}Q(y,f(y),\nabla f(y),\Delta f(y))\rvert}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in\overline{\Omega},x\neq y}\frac{\left\lVert Q(x)-Q(y)\right\rVert_{C^{k}([-M,M],[-M,M]^{n},[-M,M])}}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{\alpha}}.

The combination preceding estimates and (
A.1
) concludes the proof. The proof for estimating
C^{\alpha}{(\overline{\Omega})}
norm of other mixed derivatives works analogously.
