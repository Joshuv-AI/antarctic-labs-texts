Correlation between professional resilience, self-efficacy, coping style, and empowering leadership among emergency nurses: a multi-center study
Authors: Yinsen Peng, Hanyue Zeng, Yun Li, Tengxia Chen, Cailian Wang, Miao Peng, Shifang Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmergency nurses typically bear greater work-related stress, and high levels of professional resilience can help them better cope with such pressures while safeguarding their physical and mental well-being. This study aims to assess the current state of professional resilience among emergency nurses and explore the relationship between self-efficacy, coping style, empowering leadership, and professional resilience.MethodsThis study surveyed 650 emergency nurses from 20 hospitals in Sichuan, China, between October and November 2024. The following instruments were used to assess the current status of emergency nurses: the General Demographic Information Questionnaire, the Chinese version of the Emergency Nurses’ Professional Resilience Tool (ENPRT-C), the Generalized Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), the Leadership Empowerment Behavior Scale (LEBS), and the Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ). Pearson correlation analysis, univariate analysis, and multiple stepwise linear regression analysis were employed to explore the relationships between emergency nurses’ professional resilience and various influencing factors, including age, educational levels, professional title, workplace violence experience, self-efficacy, empowering leadership, and coping styles.ResultsOf 650 emergency nurses surveyed, 623 completed valid questionnaires. Emergency nurses’ professional resilience scores averaged (113.05 ± 18.84). The scale dimensions ranked from highest to lowest scores were behavioral strategies, emotional and cognitive characteristics, external support, and professional competence. Correlation analysis revealed that emergency nurses’ professional resilience positively correlated with self-efficacy, empowering leadership, and positive coping, while negatively correlating with negative coping. Univariate analysis indicated that age, educational levels, professional title, position, duration of employment in the hospital and emergency department, specialized nurse qualification, and exposure to workplace violence were factors influencing professional resilience. Multivariate stepwise linear regression analysis revealed that professional title, position, duration of employment in the hospital, duration of employment in the emergency department, specialized nurse qualification, workplace violence experience, self-efficacy (β = 0.402, P <0.001), empowering leadership (β = 0.303, P <0.001), positive coping (β = 0.365, P <0.001), and negative coping (β = -0.301, P<0.001) were the variables independently associated with emergency nurses’ professional resilience, which explained 66.6% of the variance in professional resilience.ConclusionsThe professional resilience of emergency nurses remains at a moderate level and warrants further enhancement. Actionable strategies for management should focus on several key areas, which involve fostering a psychologically safe team climate, integrating stress-management into continuing education, and developing responsive support systems to mitigate the impact of adverse events.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

As the frontline unit responsible for emergency medical services in hospitals, emergency nurses usually faced greater occupational stress compared to nurses in other clinical departments. Research indicated that approximately 82% to 100% of emergency nurses frequently encounter stressful events [1]. Roughly 55% to 97% of emergency nurses were suffered the workplace violence globally [2]. Relevant studies indicated that due to prolonged exposure to immense work pressure, emergency nurses exhibited higher rates of anxiety, depression, compassion fatigue, occupational burnout, and turnover tendencies [3–5].

Resilience refers to an individual’s ability to adapt to developments and changes in their occupational environment [6, 7]. The professional resilience of emergency nurses was defined as their capacity to effectively cope with the pressures of emergency work, make swift and sound decisions when facing occupational challenges, and safeguard their own physical and mental health to adapt to working in the high-pressure and dynamically changing emergency setting [8]. Current research has reached the following consensus. Emergency nurses’ sense of professional benefit, occupational identity, and workplace engagement were significantly positively correlated with their levels of professional resilience [9–12]. Further studies suggested that emergency nurses with higher professional resilience exhibit lower rates of negative psychological experiences [13–16]. In addition, professional resilience of emergency nurses was associated with multiple factors, such as age, professional title, educational attainment, marital status, years of service, cognitive strategies, personality traits, and organizational support [17–19].

However, research on the emergency nurses’ professional resilience still had the following limitations. First, findings from current studies were inconsistent. Research [20] indicated that the ratio of the mean resilience score to the theoretical maximum score among emergency nurses was only 0.59, significantly lower than the resilience scores observed in the general U.S. population (0.80) [21]. On the contrary, some studies [22, 23] suggested that emergency nurses exhibit significantly higher professional resilience than other clinical nurses. Jose et al. [14] supported this view, with their measurements indicating superior professional resilience among emergency nurses compared to nurses in the Central Sterile Supply Department [24] and Intensive Care Units [25]. Furthermore, the existing literature has not yet reached a consensus on the relationship between workplace violence and professional resilience. Hsieh et al. [26] suggested that workplace violence may undermine nurses’ resilience, yet a few studies have reported a positive or nonlinear association between them [27]. This might suggest that the relationship between experiences of violence and professional resilience was not a simple one-way influence, but was moderated by various factors such as individual coping strategies, the level of organizational support, and the timing of measurement, which required further exploration. Second, the relationship between self-efficacy, coping style, empowering leadership and emergency nurses’ professional resilience required further validation. Specifically, self-efficacy was considered a key individual factor influencing occupational resilience [28], while coping strategies (such as fighting, avoiding, or seeking support) might indirectly influence resilience formation by affecting occupational self-identity [29–31]. Furthermore, the empowering leadership as an environmental factor that might influence emergency nurses’ professional resilience required further validation [32].

To address the aforementioned research gap, this study incorporated the career motivation theory. This theoretical model was proposed by London [6] in 1983, which include three components, individual characteristics, situational characteristics, and career decisions and behaviors. In 1997, London et al. [7] further refined the career motivation theoretical model. This model indicated that prospective and retrospective relationships exist among the three components: professional resilience, situational characteristics, and career decisions and behaviors. From a prospective perspective, professional resilience was directly associated with situational characteristics and affected individuals’ career decisions and behaviors. From a retrospective perspective, the greater the positive or negative impact of past career decisions and behaviors, the stronger the influence on professional resilience.

Through an analysis of London’s career motivation theory, it can be concluded that the empowering leadership behaviors experienced by emergency nurses in their work environment serve as situational characteristic factors. These directly influenced their professional resilience levels through prospective relationships. Meanwhile, the positive or negative coping styles adopted by emergency nurses when faced with stressful events in past work experiences function as career decision and behavioral factors. These indirectly impact their professional resilience through retrospective relationships. Furthermore, although self-efficacy is not explicitly listed in career motivation theory, Bandura [33] proposed that it can be regarded as a core individual characteristic variable of professional resilience. Enhancing an individual’s confidence in coping with stress directly influences the formation of professional resilience. The theoretical framework diagram was shown in Fig. 1.

In summary, although previous studies had examined the professional resilience of emergency nurses, research on the factors influencing the professional resilience remains underexplored. Guided by career motivation theory and literature review, this study aimed to assess the levels of professional resilience among emergency nurses and explore its associations with self-efficacy, coping style, and empowering leadership. The findings were expected to provide valuable insights for emergency nursing administrators and informing the development of targeted interventions to enhance occupational resilience among emergency nurses.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a cross-sectional design. From October to November 2024, emergency nurses from 20 hospitals in Sichuan Province were selected as research subjects through convenience sampling. All participating hospitals were tertiary grade‑A general hospitals in Sichuan Province, with annual emergency department visits exceeding 50,000, and participants were rigorously selected according to uniform inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were: (1) possession of a valid nursing license (2). Nurses in the emergency department of a tertiary general hospital (3). provision of informed consent and voluntary participation. Exclusion criteria were: (1) less than 6 months of emergency department experience (2). nurses undergoing standardized training or temporarily absent from emergency duties due to external training programs (3). nurses who had experienced major life events within the past six months, such as pregnancy or bereavement.

Based on Kendall’s sample size estimation method, the required sample size should be calculated as 10 to 20 times the number of study variables [34]. This study included 24 variables. Accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the calculated sample size ranged from 267 to 533 participants. To maximize the reliability of the findings, the study ultimately recruited 650 participants.

Instruments

General demographic information questionnaire

The research team designed this questionnaire and includes 13 items: gender, age, marital status, educational levels, permanent staff status, professional title, position, monthly incomes, duration of employment in the hospital, duration of employment in the emergency department, specialty nurse qualification, main work position, and any workplace violence experience during employment.

The Chinese version of the Emergency Nurses’ Professional Resilience Tool (ENPRT-C)

The ENPRT-C was adapted and translated into Chinese by the research team and is designed to measure professional resilience among emergency nurses [8]. This scale comprises 34 items across four dimensions: Emotional Cognitive Characteristics (13 items), Professional Competence (13 items), Behavioral Strategies (4 items), and External Support (4 items). The scale demonstrated good reliability and validity with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.930. Scoring follows a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never, 2 = Rarely, 3 = Sometimes, 4 = Often, 5 = Always), yielding a total score range of 34 ~ 170. Higher scores indicated greater levels of professional resilience among emergency nurses.

Generalized Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES)

The Chinese version of the GSES was adapted by Wang et al. [35] to measure employees’ self-efficacy. This 10-item, single-dimension scale demonstrates good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.87. Scoring follows a 4-point Likert scale, with a total score range of 1 ~ 40 (1 = Strongly disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Agree, 4 = Strongly agree). Higher scores indicated a higher level of self-efficacy among respondents.

Leadership Empowerment Behavior Scale (LEBS)

The Chinese version of the LEBS, modified by Song [36], consisted of 12 items across four dimensions: job value (3 items), decision participation (3 items), competency (3 items), and support provision (3 items). The scale exhibited good reliability and validity with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.922. Scoring follows a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 2 = Somewhat Disagree, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Somewhat Agree, 5 = Strongly Agree). The total score range is 12 ~ 60, with higher scores indicating a greater perceived level of empowering leadership in the workplace.

Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ)

The SCSQ was developed by Xie [37], comprising 20 items across two dimensions: positive coping (12 items) and negative coping (8 items). The scale exhibited good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.90. Scoring follows a 4-point Likert scale (0 = never, 1 = rarely, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often). When the positive coping dimension score exceeds the negative coping dimension score, it indicates that the individual primarily employs a positive coping style when faced with stressful events. Conversely, the respondent predominantly employed a negative coping style.

Data collection

Questionnaires were collected through a combination of offline and online methods. Offline distribution involved handing out paper questionnaires on-site, while online distribution utilized the Questionnaire Star platform to distribute electronic questionnaires. Paper questionnaires were collected on the spot by researchers and checked for missing responses. All questions in the electronic questionnaires were set as mandatory to ensure complete data collection. Following data collection, a quality assessment was conducted to exclude anomalous questionnaires characterized by excessively short completion times (< 2 min), highly repetitive response patterns, or illogical consistency (identical or contradictory selections). Ultimately, 650 questionnaires were distributed, and 623 valid questionnaires were collected, resulting in an overall valid recovery rate of 95.8%.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS 27.0 software. Descriptive statistics were applied to the demographic characteristics of the study subjects, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range). Pearson correlation analysis was employed to assess the correlations among emergency nurses’ professional resilience, self-efficacy, empowering leadership, and coping style. Differences in professional resilience scores among emergency nurses with varying demographic characteristics were compared using univariate analysis, independent samples t-tests, univariate analysis of variance (ANOVA), or nonparametric tests. In order to identify key factors associated with professional resilience in this exploratory context with multiple candidate variables, a stepwise linear regression analysis was performed. The analysis employed professional resilience as the dependent variable. Independent variables entered into the model comprised those showing statistical significance in prior univariate analyses, along with self-efficacy, empowering leadership, and coping strategies. Furthermore, the diagnostic test was used to assess the collinearity among the study variables to ensure the stability of the regression model. Generally, when the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was < 5, it indicated no significant multicollinearity exists among the study variables [38]. A two-tailed test was used, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

Among the 623 valid data points included in the analysis, 534 (85.7%) were female, and 89 (14.3%) were male, with an average age of 32.79 ± 6.74 years. Educational level was predominantly a bachelor’s degree (66.9%). Professional titles were mostly senior nurse (42.4%). Specialty nurses accounted for 209 (33.5%). The monthly income was primarily between 5,001 and 8,000 (54.4%). Duration of employment in the hospital was mainly 5 ~ 10 years (31.0%). Duration of employment in the emergency department was 1 ~ 5 years (36.3%). The main work position was the emergency resuscitation room (32.7%). Over half of emergency nurses experienced workplace violence during their employment (56.5%). The detailed demographic characteristics of the participants were shown in Table 1.

Multiple linear regression analysis of factors influencing professional resilience among emergency nurses

The results of the multiple stepwise linear regression analysis indicated that emergency nurses’ professional resilience scores exhibited statistically significant differences across professional titles, positions, duration of employment in the hospital, duration of employment in the emergency department, specialty nurse qualification, workplace violence experience during employment, self-efficacy, empowering leadership, positive coping, and negative coping (P < 0.05). This regression model explained 66.6% of the variance in emergency nurses’ professional resilience and was statistically significant (F = 7.385, P < 0.001). Further analysis of regression coefficients revealed that emergency nurses’ professional resilience positively correlated with professional title, position, duration of employment in the hospital, duration of employment in the emergency department, self-efficacy, empowering leadership, and positive coping; it negatively correlated with not being a certified specialist nurse, no experience of workplace violence, and negative coping. Additionally, the multicollinearity diagnosis revealed that the VIF for all variables were < 5, which indicated no multicollinearity among the variables. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the current state of professional resilience among emergency nurses in Sichuan Province, China, and its associated influencing factors. The results indicated that emergency nurses scored (113.05 ± 18.84) on the ENPRT-C, with a mean item score of (3.33 ± 0.55). The total score range of the scale was 34 ~ 170, with a theoretical midpoint of 102. The obtained mean score of 113.05 slightly above this theoretical midpoint, suggesting a moderate level of professional resilience. Given that no established cutoff values existed for this scale, this interpretation should be considered relative to the theoretical midpoint rather than an empirically validated threshold. This finding is consistent with the results of Yi et al. [39]. This indicated that further enhancement of professional resilience among emergency nurses was warranted. According to the mean item scores of each dimension of the scale, the dimensions ranked from highest to lowest were behavioral strategies, emotional cognitive characteristics, external support, and professional competence. The “Behavioral Strategies” dimension scored highest at (3.56 ± 0.86), indicating that emergency nurses typically employed a proactive coping style to cope with work stress. The lowest score was observed in the “Professional Competence” dimension (3.19 ± 0.81). This indicated that emergency nurses still had room for improvement in terms of professional competence, which might be attributed to the fact that most participants in this study were junior emergency nurses. In contrast, the emergency setting was characterized by a high concentration of critically ill patients, a wide variety and complexity of conditions, and high patient turnover and mortality rates, all of which imposed greater demands on the professional competence of emergency nurses.

The five lowest-scoring items were Items 21, 16, 11, 1, and 23. Among these, four items (Item 21, Item 16, Item 11, and Item 1) belonged to the “Professional Competence” dimension, indicating that emergency nurses lack confidence in managing critically ill patients and their families, as well as handling complex situations. Item 23 falls under the “External Support” dimension. Its low score might stem from emergency nurses’ high work stress, inadequate work-life balance, and insufficient family support. Research by Shi et al. [40] indicated that nurses’ professional competence levels significantly associated with their professional resilience. Hsieh et al. [18] further highlighted that family support was a crucial external factor affecting the resilience of emergency nurses. Therefore, integrating prior research with these findings, emergency nursing managers could enhance nurses’ ability to handle complex situations by strengthening professional skill training through continuing education programs. Additionally, regularly organizing family forums to share insights into the daily work of emergency nurses could help family members better understand the pressures and importance of their role, fostering greater understanding and support. This approach will further enhance the professional resilience of emergency nurses.

This study found that emergency nurses with higher professional titles and those serving as preceptor nurse or nursing team leader exhibited higher professional resilience scores, consistent with findings from Yu et al. [17] and Kim et al. [41]. This may be attributed to the fact that as professional titles and roles advance, emergency nurses often assume greater responsibilities and leadership positions. This fostered a stronger sense of belonging to the hospital and department, which led to greater psychological maturity. Consequently, they demonstrated enhanced emotional management and stress-coping abilities [17]. In addition, the duration of employment in the hospital and emergency department were also significant factors influencing emergency nurses’ professional resilience scores. However, compared to the former, duration of employment in the emergency department exerted a more pronounced effect on professional resilience scores. This might be because emergency work was typically complex and unpredictable. Senior emergency nurses may experience more setbacks or frustrations throughout their long careers, and their professional resilience gradually improves with this prolonged exposure. Therefore, emergency nursing managers should prioritize strengthening the professional resilience training of junior emergency nurses to help them better adapt to the high-pressure environment of emergency work. Meanwhile, this study confirmed the significant role of specialized nursing qualifications in enhancing the professional resilience of emergency nurses. On the one hand, specialized nurses generally possessed higher levels of professional identity and job satisfaction compared to general nurses [42, 43]. These factors could translate into intrinsic motivation, helping emergency nurses establish career goals, cope with professional challenges, and consequently demonstrate greater resilience. On the other hand, emergency specialty nurses undergo more specialized and systematic training, possessing a more solid foundation in emergency nursing theory and professional skills. This enabled them to respond to stress or challenges with greater composure and effectiveness.

In particular, the findings of this study also indicated that emergency nurses who have experienced workplace violence score higher on professional resilience. This contrasts with the results reported by Hsieh et al. [26] and Fan et al. [44] but aligns with the findings of Liu et al. [27]. This seemingly contradictory outcome requires careful interpretation from multiple perspectives. On the one hand, this might be attributed to departmental leadership providing proactive support or intervention for emergency nurses who have endured workplace violence incidents. On the other hand, it might also be caused by emergency nurses who have experienced workplace violence often being forced to handle extreme situations, such as managing emotionally agitated patients or companions, which compel them to learn to respond to occupational stress and challenges in a more composed and calm manner. This enhanced their adaptability in the high-pressure emergency work environment, thereby exhibiting higher professional resilience. Additionally, since nurses whose professional resilience was severely compromised by violence might have been reassigned from emergency departments or left the profession altogether, the included sample might only represent a group with inherently higher resilience who were able to endure and continue working. This raised the possibility of survivor bias. Furthermore, influenced by the cross-sectional study design, the positive correlation identified in this research did not imply that workplace violence enhances professional resilience. It more likely revealed a complex, nonlinear relationship between them. Therefore, future longitudinal studies were recommended to clarify the causal relationship and directionality between them. Emergency nursing managers should maintain high vigilance regarding workplace violence incidents and promptly implement proactive interventions for emergency nurses exposed to such violence to minimize the resulting mental health symptoms.

Research indicated that levels of self-efficacy was related to the professional resilience scores among emergency nurses. On the one hand, emergency nurses with higher self-efficacy may exhibit greater professional confidence and emotional regulation, tending to adopt proactive problem-solving approaches when confronting occupational adversity rather than resorting to avoidance or abandonment [45]. On the other hand, emergency nurses with high self-efficacy generally possess more substantial psychological capital to cope with stress or challenges, enabling them to recover more quickly from professional adversity [46]. Furthermore, emergency nurses with high self-efficacy might tend to set more challenging career goals. This goal orientation could stimulate stronger intrinsic motivation in their professional development [47].

Additionally, the empowering leadership was related to the professional resilience scores among emergency nurses. Bester et al. [48] demonstrated that empowering leadership fosters strong organizational belonging and commitment, motivating individuals to strive for higher career goals. Klerk et al. [49] noted that individuals experiencing higher levels of empowering leadership typically exhibit greater work engagement, which facilitates the faster accumulation of work experience and stronger coping abilities during career adversities. Relevant research showed that nurses who perceived higher levels of empowering leadership in their professional environment generally exhibit greater job satisfaction, motivating them to proactively seek better solutions when confronting career challenges [32]. Furthermore, influenced by collectivism and high power distance in traditional Chinese culture, nurses might rely more heavily on guidance and support from superiors. Therefore, when nursing managers demonstrated delegating behaviors, such as sharing information, granting autonomy, and encouraging participation in decision-making, which might be positive to enhance nurses’ psychological commitment to the organization and their professional resilience. However, this hypothesis required empirical testing in future studies.

Lastly, the findings of this study indicate that positive coping is positively correlated with emergency nurses’ professional resilience scores, while negative coping is negatively correlated with these scores. Research has noted that adopting a positive coping style can minimize the adverse effects of stressful events on individuals, playing a crucial role in promoting both physical and mental well-being [50]. Conversely, adopting negative coping styles, such as avoidance or self-blame, might negatively impact an individual’s self-efficacy, thereby diminishing their professional resilience [51]. Meanwhile, previous studies had shown that resilience is closely related to the coping behaviors of nurses exposed to high-pressure clinical environments [52, 53]. Therefore, emergency nursing managers should guide emergency nurses to adopt more positive coping styles when facing stressful events. It was worth noting that within the Chinese cultural context, coping style might exhibit distinct local characteristics. For instance, strategies such as endurance and seeking family support often played a significant role in facing work stress. Future research could further explore the impact of such culturally adaptive coping mechanisms on the professional resilience of emergency nurses.

In summary, the professional resilience of emergency nurses was correlated with personal traits, situational characteristics, and prior career decisions and behaviors. Future emergency nursing managers were advised to cultivate a supportive organizational ecosystem that nurtured professional resilience. This could be achieved by transitioning from traditional hierarchical management models to shared-power leadership approaches and developing new, empowering leadership frameworks to enhance the resilience of emergency nurses. Concurrently, strengthening career stress management was identified as crucial for fortifying psychological defenses against burnout. The implementation of activities such as Balint groups [54] and cognitive behavioral therapy [55] were suggested to help emergency nurses overcome negative beliefs and reduce emotional exhaustion stemming from self-doubt. This fostered positive self-evaluation and enhanced self-efficacy, enabling more substantial professional confidence and a more proactive coping style when facing high-pressure situations or occupational adversity. Furthermore, tiered precision empowerment was recommended to forge core professional resilience competencies. This could be advanced through regular team collaboration workshops [56], tiered education and training [57], crisis scenario simulations [58], and the establishment of a trainer-training model [59] These approaches were designed to help emergency nurses, particularly junior and non-specialist nurses, acquire theoretical knowledge and professional skills aligned with their job responsibilities, thereby enhancing their adaptability in the workplace and confidence in effectively navigating professional challenges. Lastly, emergency nurses should be encouraged to actively participate in specialized nursing training to improve their core competency for job performance and strengthen their capacity to handle stress and challenges. The approaches mentioned above were projected to effectively foster the development of professional resilience among emergency nurses, which emergency nursing administrators should prioritize.

Limitations

This study had the following limitations. First, the sample for this study was drawn exclusively from a single province in China, and the generalizability of the findings requires further validation across broader geographic and cultural contexts. Second, all data were collected via self-report questionnaires, potentially introducing common method bias and social desirability bias, which may affect the estimation of variable relationships. Third, this cross-sectional design precludes inferences about causal relationships among variables. Fourth, the identified factors explain only 66.6% of the variance in emergency nurses’ occupational resilience, indicating moderate explanatory power. Furthermore, the study failed to examine potential mediating or moderating roles of variables such as coping strategies or self-efficacy within the conceptual framework. Finally, this study employed solely quantitative research methods, limiting in-depth analysis of the underlying mechanisms influencing occupational resilience. Therefore, future research should adopt a multi-center, large-sample approach, integrate relevant theoretical frameworks to include more comprehensive variables, and utilize longitudinal designs or mixed-methods research to more robustly examine the complex pathways and causal mechanisms underlying occupational resilience formation. This might provide a more solid foundation for further intervention studies.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicated that the professional resilience of emergency nurses remained at a moderate level and requires urgent enhancement. The main factors influencing emergency nurses’ professional resilience scores include professional title, position, years of service, specialty nurse certification, experiences with workplace violence, self-efficacy, empowering leadership, positive coping style, and negative coping style. Therefore, future emergency nursing managers could help emergency nurses adapt to working in high-pressure emergency settings through targeted approaches, such as building supportive organizational ecosystems, strengthening occupational stress management, and advancing tiered, precise empowerment. This approach might be beneficial to enhance the professional resilience of emergency nurses further and safeguard their physical and mental health.
