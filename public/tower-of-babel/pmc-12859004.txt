Distinct populations of lung capillary endothelial cells and their functional significance
Authors: Joel James, Aleksandr Dekan, Sedat Kacar, Maki Niihori, Takanori Sano, Nolan McClain, Mathews Varghese, Dinesh Bharti, Odunayo Susan Lawal, Marco Padilla-Rodrigez, Dan Yi, Huanling Gao, Robert S. Tepper, Zhiyu Dai, Oleg Gusev, Olga Rafikova, Ruslan Rafikov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The role of the lung’s microcirculation and capillary endothelial cells in normal physiology and the pathobiology of pulmonary diseases is obviously vital. The recent discovery of molecularly distinct aerocytes and general capillary (gCaps) endothelial cells by single-cell transcriptomics (scRNAseq) advanced the field in understanding microcirculatory milieu and cellular communications. However, increasing evidence from different groups indicated the possibility of a more heterogeneous nature of lung capillaries. Therefore, we investigated enriched lung endothelial cells by scRNAseq and identified five novel populations of gCaps with distinct molecular signatures and roles. Our analysis suggests that two major populations of gCaps that express Scn7a(Na+) and Clic4(Cl-) ion transporters form the arterial-to-vein phenotypic transition. We also discovered and named mitotically-active “root” cells (Flot1+ ) on the interface between arterial, Scn7a+ , and Clic4+ endothelium, responsible for the regeneration and repair of the adjacent endothelial populations. Furthermore, the transition of gCaps to a vein requires a venous-capillary endothelium expressing Lingo2. Finally, gCaps disconnected from the zonation represent a high level of Fabp4, other metabolically active genes, and tip-cell markers showing angiogenesis-regulating capacity. The hypoxia-induced models demonstrated that “root” cells exhibit a marked expansion in hypoxia, supporting their role in vascular regeneration and neocapillarization. We also showed a developmental time-course analysis demonstrating an evolution of progenitor (FoxM1+ ) cells, which are progressively replaced by “root” cells during lung maturation, revealing a switch in vascular homeostasis. The discovery of these populations will translate into a better understanding of the involvement of capillary phenotypes and their communications in lung disease pathogenesis.

Introduction

Despite extensive research spanning multiple decades, the field of pulmonary vascular diseases continues to face a scarcity of effective therapeutic options. One contributing factor to this challenge is the limited understanding of lung vascular biology, particularly the heterogeneous nature of endothelial cells (ECs) and the intricate interactions among cells within the pulmonary circulation and beyond. A recent discovery of heterogeneity in lung capillary cells offers a critical insight into the organization of capillary networks and cell-cell communications within the microvasculature1. Indeed, the knowledge of two distinct endothelial populations, aerocytes and general capillary cells (gCaps), could reshape the pulmonary field due to vast differences between these endothelial cell types which were previously attributed to a single type of microvascular EC.

Capillary ECs orchestrate numerous essential pulmonary vascular functions, including maintaining and repairing the capillary network, facilitating angiogenesis, ensuring blood barrier function, and enabling gas and nutrient exchange2,3. They also engage in a complex network of interactions with various cell types, such as inflammatory cells, platelets, fibroblasts, pericytes, smooth muscle, and epithelial cells. Understanding specialized endothelial types enables more comprehensive navigation of this intricate system than analyzing averaged phenotypes. Recent advantages in single-cell transcriptomics analysis have significantly aided this research direction. The data from many research groups based mainly on whole lungs have indicated that the capillary cells are more heterogeneous and potentially feature additional phenotypes beyond the described aerocytes and gCaps4,5. Yet, resolving the capillary endothelium remains a challenge. In this study, we employed highly enriched ECs from rat lungs to explore the heterogeneity of capillary cells with unprecedented resolution. Our investigation resulted in the significant discovery of five distinct phenotypes of gCaps, each exhibiting unique specializations.

Results

Organization of the lung endothelium from single-cell data

To improve endothelial cell viability and enrichment for high-resolution analysis, we isolated CD31-positive cells from rat lungs using anti-CD31 antibody and magnetic beads (Fig. 1a), followed by processing with 10x genomics scRNAseq (Supplementary results Fig. 1a, b). After mapping and annotating isolated ECs on the UMAP plot (Fig. 1b, Supplementary results Fig. 1c–g), we identified five novel populations, termed gCaps A-E. The origin of these newly discovered ECs was determined based on the expression of the Apelin receptor, a previously reported gCaps marker1. Notably, novel gCaps demonstrated the formation of two unique zonations between the pulmonary artery and vein ECs (AV-zonation) involving gCaps A, B, D, and E phenotypes (Fig. 1b). This finding suggests a gradual phenotypic transition in capillary cells between arterial and venous ECs. The term “transition” refers to the gradient of gene expression changes observed among the endothelial cells from arterial to venous regions. This gradient does not imply direct lineage conversion or physical movement of cells but rather a continuum of endothelial phenotypes, suggesting functional specialization along the vascular tree. Each cell type expressed a set of markers, as shown in the heatmap (Fig. 1c), highlighting the uniqueness of the identified cell types. To annotate cells and AV zonation, we utilized various markers based on the most distinguishing features. By employing gene expression cut-offs to emphasize regions of high expression, we effectively illustrated the phenotype transition along AV-zonation in Fig. 1d. The macrovascular ECs from the pulmonary artery and vein express the Von-Willebrand factor (Vwf). The endothelial cell cluster block began at the converging right corner of the two AV zonations, with Elastin (Eln) expression required in a high-pressure environment in the proximal arterial ECs from large pulmonary arteries6. Pulmonary artery ECs continued from this corner, marked by the previously established gap junction gene Gja5, and formed two zonation arms transitioning into general capillary ECs (Kit, AplnR positive). In zonation, the Ackr1 and N2rf2 gene expressions visualized pulmonary vein ECs at the left corner of the endothelial clusters. Novel populations of general capillary cells

Our data indicated five novel phenotypes of what was previously considered a single general capillary cell phenotype (Fig. 1d). Two large clusters of gCaps form the lower arm with gCapA, with the highest expression of the chloride channel (Clic4 + ), and the upper arm, gCapD, highly expresses the sodium channel (Scn7a + ). The zonations converge with the venous-capillary gCapE(Lingo2 + ), which connects to the pulmonary vein ECs. Consequently, gCapA, D, and E create a continuous capillary zonation from the artery to the vein. A small yet persistent subset of gCapB(Flot1 + ) is situated at the interface between pulmonary artery EC and also on gCapA&D (Supplementary data 7 (3D UMAP)). RNA velocity analysis identifies this cell type as the “root” or origin for gCapsA&D and arterial ECs (Supplementary Fig. 1i, i). Due to the highly mitotic nature of these cells and activation of transcriptional factors involved in EC proliferations, such as Jun and Fos7 (Supplementary Fig. 1f, j), we hypothesize that gCapB may play a role in repairing capillary ECs and contribute to capillary rejuvenation and neo-capillary genesis. Finally, gCapC displays distinct separation of the cluster outside the AV-zonation, highly expressing Fabp4 (Fig. 1d). Comparing the new gCaps with arterial and venous ECs revealed similarities between gCapA(Clic4 + ), B(Root), and D(Scn7a + ) with arterial ECs, and gCapE(Lingo2 + ) with venous ECs (Fig. 1e). In contrast, gCapC exhibited a completely different expression pattern. We used SCENIC to calculate regulon specificity scores, which show (Fig.1f, Supplementary Fig. 1j) that the novel phenotypes of gCaps have different transcriptional programs, representing distinct cell populations. Cells were isolated by utilizing the markers described for each gCap phenotype, and the metabolic data were obtained from extracellular flux analysis using the Seahorse instrument. This experiment showed different metabolic characteristics of gCaps in mitochondrial respiration measured by basal and maximal respiration rates and ATP production (Supplementary Fig. 9a-d). Our findings indicate that gCapB is the most metabolically active subpopulation in terms of mitochondrial respiration. We also built a UMAP using the activity of transcription factors (Supplementary results Fig. 1j, k) that resembles the structure of a gene-based UMAP on principal components (Fig. 1b). We have also composed the heatmaps for various cell processes across gCaps and other ECs (Supplementary Fig. 11). Finally, we found the top 5 most specific transcription factors for each phenotype using regulon specificity scores (RSS) (Supplementary results Fig. 1l). We didn’t observe a distinct separation of capillary cells in publicly available human or mouse datasets. Thus, we added our mouse dataset into cross-species integration, in which cells were isolated by FACS using endothelial expression of GFP (Tg(TIE2GFP)287Sato/J mouse). We achieved a three-fold higher resolution for rat ECs (30k cells) compared to mouse sets (8k)8 or human sets (2k) data9,10 (Fig. 1g). The integrated data (Fig. 1h) showed similarity in cell types between various datasets. Notably, the publicly accessible human or mouse datasets possess no clear distinction of pulmonary capillary cells. In particular, human datasets lacked capillary markers Aplnr and Kit but had high Vwf, Eln, Gja5, Nr2f2 expressions related to large vessels ECs, indicating a marked loss of capillary cells9,10 (Supplementary Fig. 2a). This effect can be attributed to the harvesting of cells from post-mortem lung tissues and the preparation process for whole lung sequencing, which is associated with a significant loss of viable capillary cells. In contrast, the publicly available mouse dataset was enriched with capillary cells but only showed a partial separation of cellular phenotypes8. Additionally, the expression of cell type markers in publicly available mouse data was weaker compared to our mouse dataset. Incorporating our mouse dataset collected from mice expressing endothelial GFP, which allowed FACS-based endothelial enrichment, enhanced cross-species integration. We showed that our isolated mouse and rat ECs had a better quality in terms of the number of Unique molecular identifier counts (UMI counts) and/or genes detected in each cell (Supplementary Fig. 2b). This allowed us to successfully identify the newly described phenotypes of gCaps in mouse datasets in addition to rats. We also found differentially expressed genes (Supplementary data 1) for each EC population in the 30k ECs resolution rat dataset and the intersection of cell markers among our mouse and rat datasets (Supplementary data 2).

To establish the endothelial identity of our newly discovered gCap populations, we employed a well-validated genetic fate mapping approach using transgenic mice expressing GFP under Tie2 promoter11,12. Tie2 is a highly specific endothelial cell marker expressed during early vascular development and maintained in mature ECs. In these mice, cells of endothelial origin are labeled with GFP, allowing us to isolate ECs with high specificity. This approach enables fluorescent identification and enrichment of ECs at the time of tissue harvest. Using cell sorting, we isolated GFP-positive cells from the lungs of these mice and performed scRNAseq (Fig. 2a). This analysis revealed the presence of all our newly identified gCap populations within the GFP-positive cells, confirming their endothelial origin (Fig. 2b). In our Tie2-GFP tracing experiments, we observed notably lower recovery rates of lymphatic ECs, aerocytes, gCapE, and gCapB than other endothelial populations comparing to CD31+ pulldown. This differential recovery pattern likely reflects the developmental modulation of Tie2 expression in these highly specialized endothelial subtypes. During the differentiation and specialization of gCapE, gCapB, aerocytes, and lymphatic ECs, Tie2 expression appears to be dynamically regulated, potentially downregulated as these cells acquire their specialized phenotypes. Despite their endothelial origin, the reduced GFP signal in these populations demonstrates the complex transcriptional dynamics during ECs specialization in the pulmonary vasculature. Characteristics of novel general capillary endothelial cells

gCapC (Fabp4 + )

Fabp4+ gCaps form a distinct cluster on the UMAP, resembling lymphatic ECs or aerocytes clusters rather than being present at the AV zonation. This distinct clustering of gCapC suggests that these cells may play a more specialized role, rather than contributing directly to the structural or barrier functions of the capillary network. The Fabp4+ cells are classified as gCaps because they express Aplnr, Cdh5, Myo10, and Ccdc85a, along with other phenotypes of gCaps (Fig. 1d). Further analysis shows that Fabp4+ cells exhibit a high enrichment of genes responsible for ameboid-type migration and cell motility (Fig. 3a, Supplementary data 3). These cells also express genes involved in lipid metabolism (Pparg, CD36, Fabp4/5) and oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 3b,c), indicating their high energy demand compared to other ECs. This finding suggests that these cells rely on lipid-centered metabolism for angiogenic capabilities. Previous studies have demonstrated that angiogenesis depends on lipid metabolism and is primarily impaired in Fabp-deficient mice13. A comparison of the gene list for tip-cell markers revealed that most tip-cell markers are highly expressed in Fabp4+ capillary cells (Fig. 3d). Tip ECs play a crucial role in angiogenesis, guiding the sprouting of new capillaries/vessels and facilitating branching angiogenesis14. For the first time, our study identifies a specific cell population within gCaps in the lung responsible for capillary angiogenesis. These cells are also rendered in published mouse data and humans (Supplementary Fig. 2a). However, Fabp4+ cells are sometimes misidentified as macrophages due to their high motility and expression of various cytokines. Nevertheless, Fabp4+ cells do not express macrophage markers such as CD68 (Supplementary Fig. 3a, Supplementary data 4, 5). This study contributes to the annotation of this particular phenotype of gCaps. To examine the angiogenic properties of Fabp4+ cells, we employed cell sorting to isolate CD31+ cells and CD31+ cells lacking Fabp4+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 3b). The sprouting experiment conducted on matrigel revealed a decreased angiogenic capacity in Fabp4+ population deficient CD31+ cells (Fig. 3e–g). To further substantiate the angiogenic capacity of these cells, we isolated gCapC cells from rat lungs and conducted a vessel-on-chip (VoC) experiment. The monoculture of gCapC in VoC (isolated and identified by Fabp4 + , CD31+ expression in Supplementary Fig. 9i) demonstrated their ability to form an endothelial monolayer and sprouting tip-like cells outside the lumen (Fig. 3i, j), indicating high migratory and angiogenic potential (Supplementary Fig. 9j, k). Fluorescent images displayed distinct features of these cells, including lamellipodia-like structures (Fig. 3h, Supplementary Figs. 3c, 9j). 3gCapB (Root/Flot1 + )

A minor population of gCapB (Flot1 + ) root cells is situated within the AV-zonation at the interface of three cell types: small artery ECs, gCapA(Clic4 + ), and gCapD(Scn7a + ) (Fig. 1b, d, Supplementary results Fig. 1i,i’). We deduced root and end cells from the velocity graph using a Markov chain implemented in CellRank, and identified several root states (Supplementary Fig. 1i Root and End cells), with gCapB among the root cells. The partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) analysis, enhanced by velocity-inferred directionality, revealed a high transcriptional similarity between gCapB cells and Scn7a + , Clic4 + , Lingo2 + , and pulmonary artery cells, which exhibited less similarity to each other than to gCapB (Supplementary Fig. 1.i’). Moreover, gCapB cells appear to be the principal root cells, as all other cells, except for pulmonary veins, are descendants of them based on directed transition probabilities. Consequently, we hypothesize that gCapB cells serve as the primary root cells responsible for regeneration. Our data indicate several important transcriptional factors (TFs) are highly expressed in Root cells, such as Fos and Jun, members of the AP-1 family. These essential transcription factors promote endothelial cell proliferation and angiogenesis (Fig. 4a)15. Conversely, the Foxo transcription factor and Foxo pathways (Fig. 4b) were upregulated in Root cells. The Foxo pathway is a crucial regulator of cellular homeostasis, playing a role in cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and oxidative stress responses16. In ECs, Foxo1 regulates angiogenesis by controlling the expression of VEGF and other angiogenic factors. Additionally, Foxo1 has been implicated in regulating endothelial cell migration, a critical step in angiogenesis17. Overall, Fos, Jun, and Foxo TFs are essential regulators of EC proliferation and angiogenesis, and their dysregulation can contribute to the development of various angiogenic diseases. Thus, the Root cells may play a central role in regulating capillary and arteriole formation and repair by being the source of ECs. Indeed, the Root cells showed a high G2M and S score; the G2M score is a critical marker for indicating mitotically active cells (Fig. 4c). This suggests the role of Root cells as the origin of capillary and pulmonary artery endothelium. Our data also showed high expression of CDKn1a,1c (p21, and p57) isoforms (Fig. 4b), which are necessary for stem cell maintenance and differentiation to harness a high proliferation rate. In addition to this, we utilized the recently published Mellon density analysis for the identification of transient cells18. To complement the PAGA analysis, we utilized temporal scRNA-seq on cells isolated from rat lungs at the 3 time points: 2 weeks, 6 weeks, and 12 weeks of age. This approach provides a high-resolution view of cellular states and gene expression patterns across lung development. We employed pseudotime algorithms to reconstruct the developmental chronology of cells based on their transcriptomic similarities. This analysis revealed potential developmental paths and cellular transitions over the 2-, 6-, and 12-week timepoints. By examining the ratio of unspliced to spliced mRNA, we gained insights into the dynamic states of cells and their likely future gene expression profiles. This method allows us to infer developmental trajectories and cellular fate decisions without relying on stable genetic markers. Our pseudo-time analysis across the 2-, 6-, and 12-week time points strongly correlated with our PAGA analysis results, providing cross-validation of our findings. Both analyses consistently indicated that gCapB cells could serve as origin cells, potentially giving rise to gCapA, pulmonary artery, and gCapD cells (Supplementary Fig. 10a–d). This developmental time course revealed a striking balance between Foxm1+ progenitor and gCapB ECs. Foxm1+ ECs were 1) abundant in 2-week-old rat lungs, 2) progressively decreased in number at 6 weeks, and 3) nearly undetectable in adult (12-week-old) lungs. Contrary, mitotically active gCapB (root) cells were 1) absent in 2-week-old rat lungs, 2) gradually increased in number at 6 weeks, and 3) formed a noticeable population in 12-week-old (adult) lungs (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). This inverse relationship between Foxm1+ progenitor cells and gCapB cells over time suggests a critical developmental transition in lung vasculature. Notably, gCapB is the most transient cell type (lowest Mellon density) among gCaps, A, B, and D (Fig. 4e, f). We found that gCapB also highly expresses Polr2a (Supplementary Fig. 9e, f). The expression of Polr2a in ECs signifies increased transcriptional activity, which could be linked to enhanced cell proliferation, response to stimuli, and active involvement in vascular processes. Moreover, a specific marker for gCapB - Tex14 plays an important function during mitosis. Indeed, if we depleted CD31+ ECs isolated from the lungs by gCapB cells, we observed a significantly decreased proliferation rate of the depleted cells (Supplementary Fig. 9g, h). The heatmap that displays the expression levels of various regenerative markers across different ECs indicates strong expression of specific regenerative markers and is located in the upper part of the hierarchical clustering (Supplementary Fig. 11s). This highlights the potential role of gCapB in vascular regeneration and its potential as a point of origin.

Finally, we investigated hypoxia effects on endothelial cell populations. Upon induction of hypoxia in rats, we observed a sizable increase in the gCapB population (Fig. 4g), while the Foxm1+ progenitor ECs remained relatively unchanged. This observation provides crucial insights into the role of gCapB cells in vascular adaptation to stress conditions. These findings collectively point to the critical role of gCapB cells in maintaining and potentially expanding capillary beds under conditions of high oxygen demand. The rapid and substantial increase in gCapB cells suggests that these cells are uniquely poised to respond to hypoxic stress.

gCapA (Clic4 + ) and gCapD (Scn7a + )

Clic4+ and Scn7a+ cells constitute the two primary populations of gCaps, which play a significant role in constructing the capillary network around the alveoli to facilitate gas transport throughout the organism. These endothelial types are present in two distinct AV-zonations, connecting a small artery to vein endothelium via gCapE (Lingo2 + ) (Fig. 1d). This raises two critical questions: First, do they form distinct capillaries? Second, what unique roles does each type play? Our confocal fluorescent imaging indicates that Scn7a+ and Clic4+ cells can form extensive capillary structures with both phenotypes, particularly around aerocytes (Fig. 5a, Supplementary results Fig. 4a, b). Numerous genes in Clic4+ cells within the top 50 differentially expressed regulate barrier maintenance, function inflammatory cells and ion/fluid trafficking, inflammation, and coagulation control (Supplementary Table 1). Analysis of differentially expressed genes suggests that Clic4⁺ cells may be involved in regulating cell growth, as indicated by the upregulation of Btg2 and Socs3 (Fig. 5b), genes previously associated with growth control19,20. Additionally, elevated expression of Atf3 (Fig. 5b), a transcription factor implicated in endothelial cell activation21, may reflect a role in metabolic adaptation or stress response in this subtype; however, direct functional experiments are needed to validate this potential role. Conversely, Scn7a+ cells exhibit a greater representation of hormonal/soluble factors-based regulation through various receptors. They display highly expressed Vipr1 and Npr3 receptors, which regulate vasodilation22. The Adgr family of G-protein coupled receptors, including CD97, Latrophillin, and Gpr116, is involved in cell-cell interactions, cell adhesion, and migration (Fig. 5b)23. Ephrin-B2 and Calcrl receptors modulate angiogenesis and cell adhesion24,25. Scn7a+ cells also express receptors responsible for proliferation and survival, such as c-Kit, Tie2, Bmpr2, Lifr, and co-receptor Eng (Fig. 5b, Supplementary data1). Lastly, two tyrosine phosphatase receptors, Ptprm and Ptprb, and integrin Itga1, play roles in cell adhesion and maintaining barrier function26. Therefore, we may conclude that transcriptional regulators predominantly program Clic4+ cells, while Scn7a+ cells are primarily controlled by paracrine/autocrine signaling via numerous receptors. Further clarification regarding the differences between these cell types can be found in Fig. 4c, which illustrates the high replicative activity of Clic4+ cells alongside the less proliferative Scn7a+ cells. This suggests that these cell types represent different stages of capillary cell maturation. It is intriguing that one capillary population (Scn7a + ) mainly expresses a sodium transporter, whereas another population (Clic4 + ) highly expresses the intracellular chloride channel, which is also known to translocate on the endothelial membrane. Both channels are essential for controlling the exchange of molecules (ions, solutes, and water) between the blood and the surrounding lung tissue, which is the primary physiological role of capillaries. gCapE (Lingo2 + )

Lingo2+ cells are situated in the AV zonation between two gCaps (Clic4+ and Scn7a + ) types and venous ECs, which characterizes them as venous-capillary cells. Confocal imaging and the spatial data showed the proximity of a vein marked by Ackr1 with gCapE (Lingo2 + ) (Fig. 5d, f). These cells express unique genes, primarily indicative of capillary endothelial characteristics. Firstly, Lingo2+ cells express the Lingo2 gene, which is involved in the Nogo signaling pathway28. Although Nogo signaling mainly pertains to neuronal system development and maintenance, NogoA/B has been reported to contribute to angiogenesis and the inflammatory response in ECs29,30. Consequently, unusually high Lingo2 expression may suggest these cells participate in angiogenesis and chemokine response. Furthermore, Lingo2+ cells express the Ackr3 (Cxcr7) receptor, unlike venous ECs, which predominantly represent Ackr1. Ackr3 specifically interacts with Cxcl1231, modulating angiogenesis, while venous Ackr1 scavenges all CC and CxC chemokines, aiding inflammation regulation32.

ECs perform various functions, such as maintaining barrier integrity, vasodilation, inflammation, and angiogenesis33. Our study identified a high degree of heterogeneity among capillary cells, prompting us to investigate whether proteins with essential roles are differentially expressed across endothelial cell types, reflecting a higher level of specialization than their previously assumed shared functionality among ECs. We employed Western blot analysis to assess protein levels in the EC populations (Supplementary Fig. 13a). Our findings revealed considerable functional heterogeneity among these EC types, supporting the notion of specialized endothelial functions within distinct populations34.

Interactions between populations of endothelial cells

Pulmonary circulation is a highly specialized vascular network, and its proper functioning depends on the coordinated interplay among various cellular components. These components include ECs, smooth muscle cells, pericytes, fibroblasts, and inflammatory cells, which interact via cell-cell communication and local paracrine signaling. Endothelial cell interactions are crucial in forming and stabilizing new blood vessels during angiogenesis and establishing the blood-tissue barrier35. Throughout this process, ECs communicate using various signaling molecules to regulate cell adhesion, migration, and proliferation36. Understanding the heterogeneity in ECs can help us analyze cell-cell interactions in more detail. To systematically investigate these interactions, we performed cell-cell communication analysis using the CellChat algorithm, which predicts intercellular signaling based on known ligand-receptor expression patterns.

The most significant communication signals emerge from Scn7a+ cells in paracrine and autocrine manners (Fig. 6a). Clic4+ cells, aerocytes, and pulmonary artery cells also substantially contribute to cell-cell interactions in the lungs. Additionally, several intriguing cellular communications, suggesting novel phenotypes, are discovered. For instance, Scn7a+ cells communicate with aerocytes using Sema3 and pulmonary arteries using Sema6 for vessel guidance and cellular signaling. The angiogenic chemokine Cxcl12 exhibits outbound signaling from Fabp4+ and arterial ECs. Proliferating c-kit signaling initiates from aerocytes and targets two primary gCaps: Scn7a+ and Clic4 + . Interestingly, Root cells play a vital role in endothelin signaling to aerocytes. Sex difference

Sex-based differences in vascular biology are increasingly recognized as important factors in disease susceptibility, progression, and therapeutic response, particularly in pulmonary conditions such as pulmonary arterial hypertension, which shows strong sex bias. To investigate whether endothelial signaling networks differ between sexes, we performed a connectome analysis comparing ligand-receptor signaling pathways in male and female lung ECs.

The overall connectome analysis revealed that Esam and Reelin (Reln) signaling are preferentially upregulated in males, while non-canonical WNT, Visfatin (Nampt), Progranulin (Grn), EphA/B, and Semaphorin 3 are upregulated in females (Fig. 6b). As a result, the Reelin and Visfatin systems appear to regulate the inflammatory response in a sex-dependent manner. Furthermore, Esam and Ephrins (EphA/B) modulate sex-dependent adhesion to ECs24. Females also possess two pro-survival systems, non-canonical WNT and GRN, which regulate vascular cell proliferation and repair. Sex-specific information about connectome for the different cell types and differential expression genes is available in Supplementary Figs. 6–8 and Supplementary data 6. The limitation of these connectome data is that it should be additionally explored using conventional western blotting and mass spectrometry approaches to validate that transcriptional changes translated into protein levels.

Discussion

The pulmonary microvasculature represents one of the most important, extensive, and specialized vascular networks in the body. Yet, our understanding of its cellular composition has been limited by the assumption that capillary ECs represent a homogeneous population. Our study fundamentally challenges this model by revealing that general capillary cells (gCaps), previously considered a single cell type alongside aerocytes, comprise five distinct populations with unique molecular signatures and specialized functions37. Our comprehensive, high-resolution single-cell transcriptomic analysis of the lung endothelial niche identified five novel gCap subtypes. gCapA (Clic4 + ), these cells express higher levels of the chloride channel Clic4 and appear to be transcriptionally programmed by Elf1, Egr1, Irf1, Stat3, and Atf3 transcription factors, according to SCENIC analysis. Their molecular profile suggests roles in barrier maintenance, ion/fluid trafficking, and inflammatory control. gCapD (Scn7a + ) - characterized by high expression of the sodium channel Scn7a, these cells show extensive receptor-mediated regulation through Vipr1, Npr3, Adgr family members, and growth factor receptors (c-Kit, Tie2, Bmpr2). This suggests they respond dynamically to paracrine/autocrine signals and are programmed by Tcf4, Gata2, Hoxa5, Taf1, and Gtf3c2 transcriptional factors. Subtypes of gCaps A and D make up the majority of the capillary endothelium in the lungs. gCapB (Flot1 + ), a small but highly mitotically active population positioned at the transcriptional interface between pulmonary arterial ECs and two major capillary gCapA/D ECs. These cells express proliferation-associated transcription factors (Jund, Fosb, Sox18) and show the highest G2M scores, suggesting a role as lung resident stem-like cells for vascular regeneration. gCapC (Fabp4 + )38, these cells form a distinct cluster outside the arterial-venous zonation and express markers associated with tip cells, lipid metabolism, and cell motility. Our functional studies confirm their angiogenic capacity, identifying them as the primary pro-angiogenic population within lung capillaries. The transcriptomics program of this subtype is dependent on Ets2, Fosl2, Cebpb, Atf4, and Bcl3 TFs. Finally, gCapE (Lingo2 + ), positioned between gCapA/D and venous ECs, represents a venous-capillary transition zone, expressing unique markers including Lingo2 and Ackr3 that may regulate local inflammatory responses. The uniqueness of these cells is regulated by Lhx6, Pbx3, Zmiz1, Nr3c1, and Cux1 TFs. The transcriptional analysis reveals that these five gCap subtypes represent fundamentally different endothelial programs rather than simply variations of a single cell type. Notably, gCapA, gCapD, gCapB, and gCapC all maintain strong endothelial transcriptional signatures while executing specialized functions. In contrast, gCapE (Lingo2 + ) cells present an intriguing deviation from conventional endothelial programs. Their regulation by transcription factors typically associated with neuronal development (Lhx6, Pbx3) and their expression of Lingo2, a gene involved in neural signaling, suggest these cells may represent a unique adaptation at the venous-capillary interface. This transcriptional divergence could reflect specialized functions in chemokine sensing and inflammatory regulation that distinguish venous-capillary transitions from other endothelial populations. Together, these distinct transcriptional programs underscore that lung capillaries are not simply structural conduits but rather comprise a sophisticated array of specialized ECs, each executing specific functions through unique transcriptional regulation. This transcriptional diversity provides the molecular basis for the functional specialization we observe and offers multiple targets for therapeutic intervention in pulmonary vascular diseases.

This discovery significantly extends the current understanding of lung capillary organization beyond the two-cell model (aerocytes and general capillaries) established by Gillich et al. Our findings reveal that what was considered a single “general capillary” population actually represents a complex ecosystem of specialized ECs. Importantly, we demonstrate that these populations form two distinct zonations creating continuous phenotypic transitions from arterial to venous circulation, suggesting a previously unrecognized level of organization in the pulmonary microvasculature.

The functional specialization of each gCap subtype has profound implications for understanding pulmonary vascular physiology. The complementary expression of sodium (Scn7a) and chloride (Clic4) channels in distinct gCap populations suggests sophisticated regulation of ion transport and fluid balance across the capillary bed. The identification of dedicated regenerative (gCapB) and angiogenic (gCapC) populations provides new insights into how the lung maintains and repairs its vast capillary network. Our temporal analysis, revealing the developmental switch from Foxm1+ progenitors to gCapB root cells, represents a fundamental shift in how the lung maintains vascular homeostasis during maturation. The dramatic expansion of gCapB cells under hypoxic conditions further supports their critical role in vascular adaptation and repair.

The robustness of our findings is underlined by our two complementary approaches: magnetic bead isolation of CD31+ cells from rats and FACS-based isolation of Tie2-GFP+ cells from mice. Both methods yielded consistent identification of these five gCap populations, and our cross-species integration analysis confirmed their presence across rat, mouse, and human datasets. This conservation suggests these populations represent fundamental organizational units of the mammalian pulmonary microvasculature.

The heterogeneity revealed in our study has immediate implications for understanding pulmonary vascular diseases. Different gCap populations may be differentially affected in conditions such as pulmonary hypertension, ARDS, pulmonary fibrosis, COPD, etc. For instance, the identification of gCapC as the primary angiogenic population could be particularly relevant for conditions involving aberrant angiogenesis. At the same time, the regenerative capacity of gCapB cells might be harnessed for vascular repair strategies.

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the critical importance of pulmonary vascular barrier integrity and the current limitations in our therapeutic options. Our findings open new avenues for precision medicine approaches targeting specific endothelial populations. For example, therapies could be designed to target specific endothelial populations that are responsible for regeneration, angiogenesis, inflammatory response, or barrier function.

Limitations and Future Directions: While our study provides unprecedented resolution of lung capillary heterogeneity, several limitations should be acknowledged. Due to their extreme rarity and low abundance, we were unable to capture representative histological images of gCapB in the lung structure reliably, although we were able to perform the gCapB depletion functional study. Our functional studies focused on select populations, and comprehensive functional validation of all identified markers remains to be completed. Future studies using quantitative spatial and multiplexed imaging analysis with distance measurements between cell types will be necessary to establish these anatomical relationships among different endothelial populations. The cell-cell communication analyses, while revealing, are based on transcriptomic inference and require validation through functional studies. Additionally, how these populations change under the disease states requires systematic investigation.

In conclusion, this discovery of five distinct gCap populations fundamentally reshapes our understanding of pulmonary microvascular organization (Fig. 6c). By revealing specialized functions ranging from barrier formation to regeneration and angiogenesis, our findings provide a new framework for understanding endothelial heterogeneity and a more granular view of lung vascular biology and disease. The identification of these populations and their unique molecular signatures opens vast opportunities for developing targeted therapeutics that could revolutionize treatment approaches for pulmonary vascular diseases. As we move forward, this cellular atlas will serve as a foundational resource for the pulmonary vascular community, enabling a more precise understanding of how disruptions in specific endothelial populations contribute to disease pathogenesis.

Methods

Animals

For EC isolation, we used 2, 6 and 12–14 week old Male and Female Sprague Dawley (SD) rats and for mice, we used animals expressing GFP under the direction of endothelial tie2 promoter (Tg(TIE2GFP)287Sato/J mouse, 12–14 weeks old females, The Jackson Laboratory, ME, USA (Strain #:003658)). Rodents were bred following an approved breeding protocol in the University of Arizona and Indiana University’s Animal Care facilities, and all experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. The rodents were kept in a 12-hour light-dark cycle and had ad-libitum access to standard rodent food and water. For hypoxia studies, age matched 7-week-old male and female Sprague Dawley rats were acclimated to either room air or hypoxia for three weeks until they achieved the appropriate breeding age of 10 weeks. At 10 weeks, each mating pair was placed together in a standard cage for mating in their respective room air or hypoxic environments. The hypoxia environment was maintained at an oxygen fraction of 15% in a hypoxia chamber (Biosphere), which simulates an environment at 9,000 feet. Males were removed from the cages once the female was visibly pregnant. Females delivered naturally at term; mother and pups were maintained in the designated room air or hypoxia exposure, and lung were harvested from offspring at the specified age. For rat lung extraction, the animals were anesthetized (Inactin, T133; 100 mg/kg i.p.; Sigma-Aldrich). The trachea was intubated with PE-240 polyethylene tubing (Becton Dickinson) and connected to a Harvard Rodent Ventilator (Model 683; Harvard Apparatus) to facilitate breathing. The thorax was opened, the left atrium was cut, and the lungs were flushed with sterile saline (0.9% NaCl) via a needle inserted into the right ventricle. For mouse lung harvest, the mice were anesthetized with ketamine (Zetamine, MWI, Boise, ID; 501072; 100 mg/kg, i.p.) plus xylazine (AnaSed, MWI, Boise, ID; 510004; 10 mg/kg, i.p.). The trachea was intubated, and animals were connected to a mouse ventilator (MiniVent, Type-845; Harvard Apparatus, South Natick, MA). The thorax was opened, and the lungs were flushed with sterile saline. For all animals, the heart/lung block was excised to euthanize the animals, and lungs were separated from the heart for downstream analyses.

Endothelial cell isolation

To isolate cells, we prepared 10 mL of digestion solution per animal (digestion solution- filter sterilized collagenase/neutral protease (1 mg/mL)), basal DMEM with 1% pen-strep, complete DMEM (10% FBS, 1% pen-strep), and MACS buffer (biotin-free BSA - 0.5% in DPBS) placed on ice for the whole period of isolation. Freshly perfused rat lungs were finely minced into pieces smaller than 1 mm with 1 mL digestion solution and then incubated with 9 mL digestion solution for 50 min with rolling at 37 °C. The mixture was then mixed with an equal volume of complete DMEM and strained with 70 µM and 40 µM sieves. The cells were centrifuged at 600 g for 10 min, washed with basal DMEM, and centrifuged again. The cells were then washed with 10 mL MACS buffer and centrifuged at 200 g for 10 min. The cells were then strained with a 40 µM sieve in 10 mL of MACS buffer and centrifuged at 200 g for 10 min. Next, 20 µL of non-endothelial cell cocktail was added to the cell suspension, and the volume was made up to 100 µL. The suspension was incubated at 4°C for 15 min while the LD column (Miltenyi 130-042-901) was prepared with 2 mL MACS buffer. The suspension was then made up to 500 µL and added to the LD column and washed three times. The eluent was collected and centrifuged at 600 g for 5 min. Next, 20 µL of endothelial cell cocktail was added to the cell suspension, and the volume was made up to 100 µL. The suspension was incubated at 4 °C for 15 min while the MS column (Miltenyi 130-042-201) was prepared. The suspension was then made up to 500 µL and added to the MS column and washed three times. The column was then flushed with 1.5 mL of buffer to collect the positively selected cells. Cell viability of greater than 90% was then determined by trypan blue, and the cells were made up to one million cells/mL. For EC isolation from mice, single cell suspension was performed as described above but the ECs expressing GFP were isolated using FACS on the BD FACSAria III sorter (BD Biosciences). Cells were subjected to capture in droplet emulsions on a Chromium Single-Cell instrument (10x Genomics) and libraries were prepared according to the previously described protocol39. 10x libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina), performed by Novogene and the Center for Medical Genomics at Indiana University.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting (FACS)

Single-cell suspensions were prepared as described above, omitting magnetic bead labeling. Cells were washed with DPBS and centrifuged at 600 × g for 5 min. Pellets were resuspended in 100 µL MACS buffer and incubated for 20 min at room temperature with ~2 µg of primary antibody per sample: CLIC4 (Novus NBP1-00172), FLOT1 (Proteintech 15571-1-AP), FABP4 (ThermoFisher PA5-17248), SCN7A (ThermoFisher BS-12127R), LINGO2 (Novus NBP1-81311), or ETBR (ThermoFisher BS-4198R). After two washes in MACS buffer, cells were incubated for 20 min at room temperature with CD31-FITC (1 µg; Miltenyi 130-126-036) together with a PE-conjugated secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit PE, ThermoFisher P-2771MP; or donkey anti-goat PE, Novus NB7590; 1:100). Cells were washed twice, resuspended in MACS buffer, passed through a 35 µm filter (Falcon 352235), and sorted on a BD FACSAria III (BD Biosciences) for biochemical assays (cells lysed immediately for western blotting) or on a WOLF cell sorter (NanoCellect) for functional assays. The gating/sorting strategy is shown in Supplementary Fig. 3b. Marker validation: Target antigens used for isolation have documented membrane or surface localization on human protein atlas (Clic4 https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000169504-CLIC4/subcellular, Flot1 https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000137312-FLOT1/subcellular, Scn7a https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000136546-SCN7A/subcellular) or publications Lingo240, Fabp441. Our data further confirm surface availability of FABP4 by successful isolation of FABP4+ cells (Supplementary Fig. 9i). gCap subsets were defined as CD31⁺ ECs co-expressing CLIC4 (gCapA), FLOT1 (gCapB), FABP4 (gCapC), SCN7A (gCapD), and LINGO2 (gCapE) with detection of primaries via PE-conjugated secondaries as noted above.

Sprouting assay

Sprouting assay was performed using the extracellular matrix (ECM) Gel from Engelbreth-Holm-Swarm murine sarcoma (Sigma E6909). 50 µl of ECM gel was added to a 96 well plate on ice and placed in the incubator at 37 °C for one hour to solidify. Using the WOLF FACS (supplementary fig. 3b), rat ECs were isolated and 10,000 cells/well were plated with complete endothelial cell medium (ScienCell 1101). The plates were incubated for 1–2 weeks and visualized under a microscope (Revolve, ECHO). The number of tubes and tube length was determined as a function of tube formation capacity by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups. ImageJ software (NIH) was used to determine the total number of tubes and the tube length. Using the freehand line tool, each tube was marked to it’s length. The imageJ scale was set to 0.8375 pixels/µm at the 4x objective.

Confocal imaging

The lungs were embedded in paraffin blocks, sliced into 10µm-thick sections, deparaffinized, and subjected to antigen retrieval. The slides were blocked with 10% BSA for 15 min at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies at a 1:50 dilution for 1 h in the dark. The antibodies used were FABP4 (NOVUS AF1443), CD31 (ThermoFisher PA5-32321 and NOVUS AF3628), CLIC4 (NOVUS NBP1-00172), SCN7A (ThermoFisher BS-12127R), Apelin (ThermoFisher APEL-FITC), Gja5 (Abnova PAB0764), DARC (R&D AF6695), Lingo2 (ThermoFisher PA5-99869). On the following day, slides were probed for 1 h with a secondary antibodies Donkey anti-Goat Secondary Antibody PE (Novus NB7590), Donkey anti-Mouse Secondary Antibody Alexa Fluor™ 488 (Thermo A-21202), Donkey anti-Goat Secondary Antibody PE (Novus NB7590), Donkey anti-Rabbit Secondary Antibody DyLight 650 (Novus NBP1-75644), Donkey anti-goat Alexa Fluor 488 (ThermoFisher A11055), Donkey anti-sheep Alexa Fluor 647 (ThermoFisher A21448) and Donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 555 (ThermoFisher A31572). The slides were washed in TBST and water followed by addition of a drop of ProLong™ Diamond Antifade Mountant with DAPI (ThermoFisher P36962). To obtain confocal and 3D images, microscopy was performed using the Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope with Airyscan at 60X oil immersion and accompanying ZenBlack software (version 2.3, Carl Zeiss Microscopy), and the resulting z-stacked images were processed using imageJ (NIH) and into 3D renders using Imaris software (version 534 10.0, Oxford Instruments).

Western blotting

Cells isolated from FACS were centrifuged and resuspended in RIPA buffer (ThermoFisher 89900) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (ThermoFisher 78441). The lysates were then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min, and the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher 23225) was used to determine the protein concentrations. Next, the samples were treated with 6X Laemmli sample buffer at 95°C for 5 min (Boston Bioproducts BP-111R). Electrophoresis of the proteins was performed on 4% to 20% Mini-PROTEAN TGX stain-free gels (Bio-Rad 4568096), and the proteins were transferred to a membrane using the PowerPac Universal power supply and Trans-Blot Turbo transferring system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc, Hercules, CA). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA and probed at 4 °C overnight with the following antibodies at 1:1000 concentration: ZO-1 (ThermoFisher 40-2200), E-NOS (ThermoFisher MA5-15559), iNOS (ThermoFisher PA1-036), Afadin (Proteintech 55102-1-AP), P-Selectin (ThermoFisher MA1-81809), TGFBR3 (Thermo Fisher PA5-17529), ICAM1 (Proteintech 10020-1-AP), vWF (Proteintech 11778-1-AP), tPA (tissue Plasminogen-activator) (ThermoFisher MA5-32507), FosB (ThermoFisher MA5-15056), Occludin (ThermoFisher 33-1500), PAR1 (ThermoFisher PA5-116040), PAR3 (ThermoFisher PAR3-301AP), PAI1 (ThermoFisher 13801-1-AP), Gja10 (ThermoFisher PA5-68749). Chemiluminescent bands were visualized using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad Laboratories).

Mitochondrial Seahorse assay

The isolated cells were seeded onto a 96-well Seahorse plate, and after visible colonies were observed, the cells were trypsinized and re-seeded. Two days later, the cells were prepared for the mitochondrial stress assay. On the day of the assay, the cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator with XF base medium supplemented with 1 mmol/L pyruvate, 2 mmol/L glutamine, and 10 mmol/L glucose. Oligomycin (10 µmol/L), FCCP (10 µmol/L), and rotenone+antimycin-A (5 µmol/L) were then sequentially added to the flux pack wells. The oxygen consumption rate was measured using the Seahorse Bioscience XF pro 96 extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The data collected was then normalized over total protein per well.

Cell growth rates

CD31-positive endothelial cells were isolated using the WOLF FACS system (Nanocellect). gCapB cells, identified by Flot1 expression, were selectively depleted from a separate cell population, leaving the remaining CD31+ cells. Both the intact CD31+ cells and the gCapB-depleted CD31+ cells were then seeded at a density of 10,000 cells per well onto an iCELLigence ECIS system (Agilent). The cells were cultured under standard conditions, and their growth rates were monitored in real-time. The growth rate was quantified by calculating the slope of the normalized cell index over time, as specified by the manufacturer’s instructions.

scRNA-seq data processing and analysis

Gene expression matrices of 2 female, 2 male rats and one female mouse samples were generated using Cell Ranger v7.0.142 and the mRatBN7.2 and GRCm39 genome references for rat and mouse respectively. R v4.3.043 was used for further analysis until stated otherwise. To ensure the high quality of data ambient RNA contamination was removed using decontX celda44 with raw matrix output from Cell Ranger as an empirical estimate of the distribution of ambient RNA. Low quality cells were filtered out based on gene/molecule dependency using the gene.vs.molecule.cell.filter function from pagoda2 v1.0.10 package with minimal cell size of 800 UMI counts. Cells with >15% of UMI counts coming from mitochondrial RNAs were removed. Doublets were filtered out utilized Scrublet45. R Seurat v4.2.1 has been employed for subsequent analysis46. Data was normalized using SCTransform v2 regularization47 and 3000 highly variable genes were selected across all samples, generated by the SelectIntegrationFeatures function. Only rat samples were integrated using CCA (Canonical Correlation Analysis) and MNN (mutual nearest neighbors) based workflow. To improve integration results, the largest rat sample was used as a reference to which all other samples were mapped. The first 50 principal components were used for to build a UMAP dimensionality reduction model with 500 epochs and negative sample rate equal to 15 parameters to improve accuracy (Fig. 1b with two females, used for illustration, and Supplementary Fig. 1c–e with 2 females and 2 males, used for analysis, Supplementary data 7 (3D UMAP). Cells were clustered by constructing a nearest neighbor graph and the Lovain clustering algorithm with multilevel refinement. All non-endothelial clusters that lacked the expression of Pecam1 and a cluster present only in one male sample were removed. The Seurat steps described above were repeated to ensure that the principal components describe only the endothelial subset of cells.

Clusters were annotated based on known markers using multiple clustering resolutions to ensure that cell annotations match the expression of markers (Fig. 1d). Clusters were annotated based on established marker genes, and multiple clustering resolutions were tested to ensure that annotations aligned with known cell-type specific expression patterns. In cases where a cluster appeared too large and did not correspond well with known cell marker expression, the clustering resolution was increased iteratively until distinct populations reflected known gene signatures. Distribution of UMI counts, the number of detected genes, and cell cycle phase proportions were visualized (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b, f). Batch effect was corrected using the PrepSCTFindMarkers function and conserved differentially expressed genes calculated using the FindConservedMarker function (Fig. 1c, Supplementary data 1, Supplementary Fig. 1g), where genes with maximum p-adjusted values < 0.05 (used for all) were considered as differentially expressed. The resulting table allowed us to identify markers applicable to both sexes and stably expressed among rat samples. Using newly found Scn7a and Clic4 and known Eln and Nr2f2 markers, we made a cell states plot48 (Supplementary Fig. 1h) which shows the separation of endothelial populations based on the expression of marker. Pulmonary artery was compared to pulmonary vein using the FindConservedMarkers function and top 20 genes for each were visualized (Fig. 1e). Heatmaps that do not include lymphatic ECs were constructed using the FindConservedMarkers function without these cells (Supplementary data 3). Genes present in Supplementary data 3 and belonging to GO:0001667 ameboidal-type cell migration and KEGG rno09142 cell motility (Fig. 2a), selected lipid metabolism genes (Fig. 2b), Oxidative metabolism KEGG rno00190 (Fig. 2c), tip cell markers (Fig. 2d)49, and FOXO-mediated transcription genes from Reactome R-HSA-9614085 (Fig. 2j) were visualized. Conserved upregulated transcription factors in Root cells were visualized in Fig. 2i.

Root cells were shown to be statistically different in terms of cell cycle scores (Fig. 2k) from other cell populations, being high in S and G2M scores, which shows its mitotic nature. RNA-velocity-based was completed in in python v3.9.050. Scvelo was used to construct velocity graph and determine the directionality of cell transitions. Root and end cells were identified from velocity graph using Markov chain algorithm (Supplementary Fig. 1i). Then, PAGA graph extended with velocity-inferred directionality was constructed using CellRank v1.5.1 and confirmed root state of gCapB51,52 (Supplementary Fig. 1i). Scn7a + , Lingo2 + , and Clic4+ cells were compared using the FindConservedMarkers function and top 20 genes for each were visualized (Fig. 3b). Finally, sex differences in gene expression among cell types were identified using the FindMarkers function (Supplementary data 6).

Single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC)

Transcription factor activity and regulon specificity scores (RSS) were inferred using SCENIC53 multiruns vsn-pipeline54 and mouse v10 database. To ensure the reproducibility of SCENIC analysis 50 iterations of SCENIC runs were made and only regulons present in 80% of the runs were selected. To decrease computational load only a subset 1000 random cells of each cell type, but all Root (555), Lingo2+ (557) and Fabp4+ (867) cells was used. SCTransfromed normalized values were supplied to SCENIC to decrease batch effect. Transcription factor targets were identified based on their co-expression in single cell data using GENIE355. Next, regulons were identified using TF-motif enrichment analysis using RcisTarget. AUCell53 was employed to measure transcription factor activity (Supplementary Fig. 1j) and calculate regulon specificity scores (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Fig. 1l)56 to determine essential regulators of endothelial populations. UMAP plot was built based on the activity of transcription factors (Supplementary Fig. 1k).

Cross-species analysis

To confirm the presence of identified endothelial populations in other species8,57,58 datasets and prepared a mouse sample prepared by us were used for cross-species integration. Human genes were renamed to rat genes and all samples were processed in the same manner as described in scRNA-seq data analysis, leaving only Pecam1-positive and removing Cd68-negative clusters. Samples were integrated data in the same way as described before, however the number highly variable genes used for integration was decreased down to 2000 to lower the impact of different gene expression across species. Common clusters were found and annotated by the most prevalent annotation in them based on the previously annotated rat data, leaving annotation of rat cells unchanged. The results of cross-species integration were visualized using UMAP plot (Fig. 1h) and compared in terms of the quality and quantity of data (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Fig. 2b). The expression of rat endothelial populations markers was shown in different datasets (Supplementary Fig. 2a).

Cross-species conserved cell type markers were found using mouse and rat data generated from our experiments. The FindMarkers function was used to find differentially expressed genes among endothelial populations in the mouse sample (p-adjusted < 0.05). An intersection of these genes and positively expressed genes in rat endothelial populations from Supplementary data 1 represent cross-species cells type markers (Supplementary data 2). The mouse sample lacked Lingo2+ and Root cells and were not included.

Single-cell transcriptomics of whole rat lungs was performed to identify markers of endothelial populations among many cell types. Separation of endothelial populations was not good enough on whole lung data, but Fabp4+ cells were clearly separated (Supplementary Fig. 3a). Differentially expressed genes in Fabp4+ cells in whole lung were found using the FindMarkers function (Supplementary data 4). Cross-species and cross-sample markers of Fabp4+ cells were found using the Supplementary data 2, 4 and 5.

Cell-cell interaction analysis

Cell-cell communications of lung ECs were inferred using the R implementation of CellChat v1.6.159. Pooled rat SCTransfrom normalized matrix was used to calculate cell-cell communication using receptor-ligand pairs. Sex-unspecific outgoing and incoming cell-cell communications were identified without considering population size to show interactions present in small endothelial populations (Fig. 4a). All ligand-receptor pairs which mediate the activity of pathways were visualized (Supplementary Fig. 5). Sex-specific differences among cell types in cell-cell communications were visualized (Fig. 4b). Number and strength of communications and information flow of pathways were compared in both sexes and visualized (Supplementary Fig. 6a–d). Spatial transcriptomics

Spatial sequencing of deidentified human lung tissue (obtained from PHBI) was conducted using the Stomics Stereo-Seq platform, following the manufacturer’s instructions. All patients provided written informed consent at the transplant site where the tissue was initially collected. Due to sample coding, we cannot identify the exact transplant site in this multi-site study, but each site had its own approved IRB protocol. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Briefly, fresh-frozen lung tissues were embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) compound and sectioned at a thickness of 5 µm using a microtome. The sections were carefully placed onto a 1 cm × 1 cm Stomics chip, which is designed to capture spatially resolved transcriptomic information. The chip was then processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This included tissue fixation, permeabilization, and RNA capture. During these steps, RNA molecules from the tissue sections were hybridized to spatially barcoded capture probes on the chip surface, enabling the preservation of spatial context. Following RNA capture, the chip underwent a series of washing and enzymatic reactions to amplify and label the captured RNA. The labeled RNA was then eluted from the chip for library preparation. The libraries were constructed using a standardized protocol to ensure high-quality sequencing results. Once the libraries were constructed, they were subjected to next-generation sequencing at Stomics Tech.

Spatial transcriptomics processing and data analysis

Human lung specimens were sourced from the PHBI biobank, which has obtained all necessary consents. Since the data are de-identified and PHBI operates across multiple sites, the specific institution’s IRB is unavailable; however, all PHBI sites have approval for their protocols. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. We utilized spatial transcriptomic data processed using Stomics Stereo-seq technology. The dataset generated with Stereo-seq technology was processed using the Stereo-seq pipeline, specifically utilizing the saw_v7.1 spatial_Transcriptomics_workflow through the stereoPipeline_v7.1.sh script. The computational environment was managed by loading the Singularity module. The human reference genome (Homo sapiens.GRCh38.112) was constructed using the singularity exec function to run the genome generation process, ensuring accurate alignment during subsequent analysis. The resulting gene expression matrix was read and filtered using the Stomics toolkit, stereopy, in Python. Low-quality cell bins were filtered out based on the following criteria: min_counts = 80, min_genes = 3, max_genes = 6000, and pct_counts_mt = 20. The filtered Stereo-seq object was then converted into a Seurat object for further downstream analysis in R (v4.3.2). Subsequently, the data was normalized using SCTransform, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) to reduce dimensionality. UMAP plots were generated to visualize the data in reduced dimensions. Additionally, we examined the spatial distribution of relevant cell types by visualizing the expression of specific marker genes within their spatial locations in the tissue. In addition, retrieving one spatial sample dataset (sample no: WSA_LngSP10193346) from Array Express, we constructed the Seurat spatial object, and preprocessed and visualized in its spatial context using SpatialDimPlot (Madissoon et al. 2022)27.

Sprouting angiogenesis assay on a vessel-on-chip platform

As previously described in ref. 60, all the vessel-on-chip assays were realized using microchips made from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) (Fukoku Bussan, Tokyo, Japan). Briefly, all the PDMS chips were treated with a plasma cleaner (Harrick Plasma, NY) before injecting type I collagen solution (Nitta Gelatin, Osaka, Japan) into a cell chamber placed at the center of the chip. Before collagen reticulation, acupuncture needles were inserted from side holes to penetrate the collagen gel. After gel hardening, the needles were removed, and the collagen surfaces were coated with gelatin (MAKER, STATE) while preparing the cell suspension. Cells were loaded from outlets on the chips and incubated to make them attach to the collagen surfaces. Culture media were changed every other day.

Mellon analysis tool

To assess the transitory nature of general capillary subtypes, we employed Mellon for cell-density estimation based on high-dimensional neighbors61. The cell-density can be used to separate stable cell states from transitory and often rare cell states. Stable cell states exist across much longer time scales compared to transitionary cell states, which also reflects the difficulty of capturing them.

Statistics and reproducibility

For all in-vitro assays, The GraphPad Prism software, version 10, was utilized for statistical calculations. The mean value (±SEM) was calculated for all samples, and significance was determined through the unpaired Student t-test or one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post hoc multiple-comparisons test for selected pairs. Statistical significance was achieved when p < 0.05. For scRNA analysis, the details of statistical analyses and cut-offs are indicated in the figure legends. The following additional packages were used for visualization. scCustomize v 1.0.062. (Fig. 1d, e, Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 1a, b, Supplementary Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 3a), SCP v0.4.2 (https://github.com/zhanghao-njmu/SCP) (Fig. 1c, Fig. 2k, Supplementary Fig. 2b), ggplot2 v3.4.263 (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f, g, k), plotly v4.10.1 (https://github.com/plotly/plotly.R) (Fig. 1f), pheatmap v1.0.12 (https://github.com/raivokolde/pheatmap) (Fig. 2a–d, i, j, Supplementary Fig. 1j), SCpubr v1.1.248 (Supplementary Fig. 1h), wordcloud v2.664 (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d). All in-vitro experiments were performed at least twice, except for the vessel-on-chip assay which was conducted once with 3 chip replicates. Single-cell experiments were repeated at least three times, except for the GFP-mouse scRNAseq, hypoxia scRNA-seq and temporal scRNA-seq analyses, which were performed once.
