Comparison of Vancomycin AUC24 Calculation Methods for Neonates and Infants
Authors: Tuomas Laitila, Ulla Sankilampi, Marjo Renko, Merja Kokki, Veli-Pekka Ranta
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveFor neonates and infants receiving intermittent vancomycin infusions, the area under the concentration-time curve during 24 h (AUC24) is often estimated with Bayesian forecasting using one or more measured vancomycin concentrations. When practical peak and trough concentrations are measured at steady state, AUC24 can also be calculated with first-order steady-state equations for a one-compartment model (Sawchuk–Zaske method), but previously this method has been applied only for adults. The objective of this study was to compare AUC24 values obtained with the Sawchuk–Zaske method and two Bayesian models.MethodsAUC24 values were estimated retrospectively for 18 neonates and infants with steady-state peak and trough concentrations using traditional compartmental analysis with a one-compartment model (reference method), the Sawchuk–Zaske method, and Bayesian forecasting with two previously published models. In Bayesian forecasting, both original and modified residual error models were used. In the modified models, the residual error was reduced by setting the additive residual error to zero and the proportional error to 15%.ResultsAUC24 estimates obtained with the Sawchuk–Zaske method differed − 2.7 to 0.9% from the reference method. When both peak and trough concentrations were used in Bayesian forecasting, 61% and 33% of AUC24 estimates obtained with two original models differed less than 15% from the reference method, and these fractions increased to 83% and 72% with the modified models, respectively.ConclusionWhen practical peak and trough concentrations are measured at steady state, the simple Sawchuk–Zaske method is very useful for AUC24 estimation in neonates and infants. In Bayesian forecasting, the reduced residual error model can be used to improve the model fit.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13318-024-00920-5.

Key Points

The simple Sawchuk–Zaske method was shown to be a useful vancomycin AUC24 estimation method for neonates and infants when practical peak and trough concentrations were measured at steady state.In Bayesian forecasting, a practical method to improve the model fit is to replace the original residual error model obtained using patient data with a reduced model based on typical laboratory assay error.

The simple Sawchuk–Zaske method was shown to be a useful vancomycin AUC24 estimation method for neonates and infants when practical peak and trough concentrations were measured at steady state.In Bayesian forecasting, a practical method to improve the model fit is to replace the original residual error model obtained using patient data with a reduced model based on typical laboratory assay error.

Introduction

For neonates and infants receiving intermittent vancomycin infusions, the most popular monitoring methods are trough concentration measurement and Bayesian AUC24 estimation (typical target 400–600 mg/L×h) using one or more concentrations. We recently showed that trough and practical peak measurements at steady state combined with AUC24 calculation using first-order steady-state equations for a one-compartment model (a self-made spreadsheet calculator) are also very useful [1]. The calculations are fast and no special software is needed. The calculations are called Sawchuk–Zaske method [2], and they are often used to analyze adult vancomycin data in the USA [3].

Recently, nine neonatal and pediatric pharmacokinetic models were evaluated for Bayesian forecasting in a large multicenter study [4], and the models by Jacqz-Aigrain et al. [5] and Dao et al. [6] were generally the most accurate and precise.

In our retrospective study, our aim was to compare our earlier AUC24 estimates [1] obtained with the Sawchuk–Zaske method with Bayesian estimates obtained using the two-compartment Jacqz-Aigrain model [5] and the one-compartment Dao model [6].

Methods

Clinical Data

Our retrospective study was approved by the medical director of Kuopio University Hospital (No 114/2019). From the 29 infants in our previous study [1] covering the period 2015–2022, 18 were included based on the availability of plasma creatinine level (PCR) near the start of vancomycin therapy (measured not earlier than 72 h and not later than 24 h from the start of vancomycin therapy).

The dosage regimen based on postmenstrual age (PMA) in British National Formulary for Children [7] was used. All infusions lasted 1 h. The trough concentration was measured nominally after 2 or 3 days, and the practical peak concentration 2 h after the end of the next infusion. Vancomycin was determined using enzyme-multiplied immunoassay technique (EMIT) with the limit of quantitation (LOQ) of 2 mg/L or kinetic interaction of microparticles in solution (KIMS) with a LOQ of 3 mg/L. PCR was measured with an isotope dilution mass spectrometry traceable enzymatic assay.

Compartmental Analysis and Sawchuk–Zaske Method

The reference method was individual compartmental analysis with a one-compartment model and full dosing data without covariates (PKanalix 2021R2; Lixoft, France). With two measured concentrations and two model parameters [clearance (CL) and apparent volume of distribution (Vd)], the model-predicted concentrations were the same as the observed concentrations. The estimated parameters (CL and Vd) remained constant with time, but it was not assumed that steady state had been reached at the time of trough and practical peak measurements.

The calculations with the Sawchuk–Zaske method are described in the Supplementary Material. Also, with this method, the model-predicted concentrations were the same as the observed concentrations. However, CL and other pharmacokinetic parameters will differ from the reference method, if steady state has not been reached.

Bayesian Forecasting

Bayesian modeling was performed with MwPharm++ software (version 2.4.0.316; Mediware, Prague). Table 1 shows the pharmacokinetic parameters for Jacqz-Aigrain [5] and Dao models [6]. The software contains two versions of both models, one with the original residual error parameters and another with modified parameters. In the modified version, the additive residual error was set to zero and the proportional error to 15% (lognormal distribution). Therefore, the residual error was much lower than in the original model, forcing the model-predicted concentrations to move closer to the observed concentrations. Bias values are reported as mean (min–max) where the mean represents the mean percent error (MPE).

In Bayesian forecasting, 15% difference from the reference AUC24 (compartmental analysis) was used as the accuracy criteria, since this interval was used earlier for model-predicted concentrations [4]. Therefore, the percentage of individual AUC24 estimates that remained within ± 15% from the reference AUC24 (P15%) was calculated. However, it is acknowledged that Bayesian AUC24 is obtained as a trade-off between individual concentration data and typical model parameters, and zero bias against the reference AUC24 was not the ultimate target, but rather getting comparable AUC24 values.

Bayesian Forecasting

A priori AUC24 predictions were generally higher than those obtained with the reference method. MPE and P15% for AUC24 were 20% and 28% for the Jacqz-Aigrain model and 40% and 17% for the Dao model, respectively (Table 3).

A posteriori with both trough and peak concentrations, AUC24 P15% for the Jacqz-Aigrain model increased from 61 to 83% when the original model was replaced with the modified model (Table 3). The corresponding change with the Dao model was from 33 to 72%. With both modified models, the range of AUC24 bias values was fairly narrow, − 10 to 21% for the Jacqz-Aigrain model and − 16 to 21% for the Dao model, respectively.

A posteriori with the modified model and plain trough concentration, AUC24 P15% was 67% for the Jacqz-Aigrain model and 72% for the Dao model, respectively (Table 3).

With the Jacqz-Aigrain model, a priori AUC24 bias was over 50% (range 51–66%) for five patients (two with PMA < 29 weeks; one with PMA 29–35 weeks; two with PMA > 35 weeks). For these subjects, a posteriori AUC24 bias with the modified model was 11–35% (one ≤ 15%) using trough concentration, and 5–21% (two ≤ 15%) using both trough and practical peak concentrations, respectively. Additionally, for both patients with PMA > 35 weeks, the estimated volume of distribution at terminal phase (Vz) obtained a posteriori with the modified model was 3.1–3.4 L/kg (unrealistically high). For these patients, a priori Vz was 1.4 L/kg and 1.9 L/kg, and a posteriori Vz with the original model 2.5 L/kg and 2.7 L/kg, respectively.

With the Dao model, a priori AUC24 bias was over 50% for five patients (range 56–104%), and four of them were the same as with the Jacqz-Aigrain model. For these subjects, a posteriori AUC24 bias was 1–26% (two ≤ 15%) using trough concentration, and − 1 to 21% (two ≤ 15%) using both trough and practical peak concentrations, respectively. Among these five patients, a posteriori bias for practical peak concentration was quite high, 27–30%, for three subjects (two with PMA 29–35 weeks and one with PMA > 35 weeks). Their Vd estimates with one-compartment compartmental modeling were at the high end of our data, 0.9–1.2 L/kg, whereas Vd was fixed in the Dao model to 0.628 L/kg (Table 1).

Discussion

In vancomycin monitoring, pharmacokinetic parameters are often calculated in the USA with the Sawchuk–Zaske method using practical peak and trough concentrations at steady state [3], but limiting its use to adults. In this study, it has been shown that this method is also very useful for AUC24 estimation in neonates and infants.

A limitation of the Sawchuk–Zaske method are the required two concentrations at steady state. In this study, steady state had been reached. There was a sufficient time interval between the start of therapy and concentration measurements (at least five elimination half-lives). Therefore, AUC24 values obtained with the Sawchuk–Zaske method were almost identical to the compartmental analysis. Another limitation in the Sawchuk–Zaske method is that the measured concentrations are assumed to be perfectly accurate, which is not realistic. We critically evaluated our concentration data and did find any reasons for exclusions.

Bayesian forecasting is more flexible, but more expertise in pharmacokinetic modeling is required. There are many vancomycin models available for neonates and infants. In this study, the Jacqz-Aigrain and Dao models were used, since these models were generally the most accurate and precise in an extensive model comparison in which model-predicted concentrations were compared with measured concentrations [4]. Our study supplemented and extended the earlier comparison by also comparing AUC24 values, by testing both the original and the modified residual error models, and by paying special attention to those patients whose vancomycin concentrations and AUC24 were markedly over-predicted with the typical model parameters (a priori AUC24 bias over 50%).

In our study, a priori AUC24 predictions were generally higher than those obtained from compartmental analysis (Table 3), indicating that our patients differed from those used in the model building by Jacqz-Aigrain et al. [5] and Dao et al. [6]. This was not an ideal starting point. Still, our study revealed important features in the Jacqz-Aigrain and Dao models, and our results may be helpful in Bayesian forecasting for those neonates and infants who deviate to some extend from the typical patient.

In the modified models, the original residual error model obtained with patient data [5, 6] was replaced with 15% proportional residual error (lognormal distribution) which represents typical laboratory assay error [8]. This approach is commonly used to improve model fit. As expected, AUC24 P15% was markedly higher with the modified model: 83% for the modified versus 61% for the original Jacqz-Aigrain model and 72% versus 33% for the Dao model (Table 3).

Our study showed that special attention in Bayesian forecasting is needed when concentrations are markedly over-predicted with the typical model parameters (a priori AUC24 bias over 50%). With the Jacqz-Aigrain model, Vz values for two patients were a priori 1.4–1.9 L/kg, and a posteriori 2.5–2.7 L/kg with the original and 3.1–3.4 L/kg with the modified model, respectively. The unrealistically high Vz values probably resulted from the very high between-subject variability of V2 in the Jacqz-Aigrain model (CV 102.5%; Table 1) which makes this parameter very flexible in the minimization of Bayesian objective function. With the modified Dao model, a posteriori bias for practical peak concentration was quite high, 27–30%, for three subjects. Their Vd estimates with one-compartment compartmental modeling were 0.9–1.2 L/kg, which are not uncommon in published studies. However, Vd was fixed in the Dao model to 0.628 L/kg (Table 1), which partly explained its inability to accurately model the practical peak concentrations for these three subjects. Alternative models may be better for these patients.

A limitation in this study was the low number of patients. The data were limited to a pair of practical peak and trough concentrations for each patient, the routine clinical data in our hospital. It would be rational to initially select a model with which the mean bias for model-predicted concentrations is close to zero. This was not done in our study since our aim was to evaluate the Jacqz-Aigrain and Dao models.

Conclusion

With steady state trough and practical peak data, AUC24 values obtained with the Sawchuk–Zaske method were almost identical to the compartmental analysis. With modified Jacqz-Aigrain and Dao models, 67–83% of a posteriori AUC24 estimates were within 15% of the values obtained with the compartmental analysis. Because our patient group was small, we cannot make general recommendations. We prefer the simple Sawchuk–Zaske method even though two vancomycin concentrations are required at steady state. With this method, we also calculated CL, Vd , and elimination half-life to evaluate whether they are consistent with the infant’s gestational and postnatal ages, PMA, weight, and PCR.
