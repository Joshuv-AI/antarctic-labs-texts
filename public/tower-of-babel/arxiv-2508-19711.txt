Prototyping of 6.2-mm-Pitch Fiber Positioner Modules for Stage-V Telescope Instrumentation
Malak Galal, Maxime Rombach, Jonathan Wei, Ricardo Araújo, Diane Chapuis, Sébastien Pernecker, Abby Bault, Joseph Harry Silber, Nicholas Wenner, William Van Shourt, Stefane Caseiro, Corentin Magnenat, David Schlegel, Jean-Paul Kneib

Abstract
Small-pitch populated focal planes are a critical enabling technology for the next generation of highly multiplexed astronomical instruments. This work reports on ongoing prototyping activities for 6.2 mm-pitch alpha–beta (theta–phi) fiber positioner modules. Following a concise review of the underlying concept, the performance of trillium-based and independently actuated robotic designs is compared. Key operational parameters including XY positioning repeatability, non-linearity, backlash, and tilt are quantitatively assessed, with emphasis on their implications for overall instrument performance. Initial prototyping results are highly encouraging, indicating performance levels approaching the preliminary design specifications.

One of the main contributors to the success of the SDSS-V and DESI surveys is the Robotic Optical Fiber Positioning Subsystem
[
14
,
15
]
. It lies at the heart of the telescope (the focal plane) and can be considered to be the nervous system of the whole structure. The light collected from the sky by the telescope’s optical train is transmitted all the way through the optical fibers to reach the spectrographs. Since optical fibers need to be placed and actuated precisely to point at the desired targets, robotic positioners have been proposed and have proven to be the ultimate solution. SDSS-V relied on a total of 1’000 alpha-beta robotic positioners (500 on each hemi-sphere) to complete its survey. DESI
[
16

The sheer success of these past surveys, utilizing alpha-beta robotic positioners, has ignited a huge interest in expanding the number of robotic positioners (in turn also optical fibers) to observe even more objects simultaneously.

Accordingly, driven by the very strict scientific requirements of the aforementioned future surveys, the instrumentation of such large-scale projects needs to be adapted.
The number of robotic positioners quickly increased from 5’000 to potentially more than 20’000 robotic positioners (and optical fibers) per survey. For this massive number of positioners to be placed in roughly the same-sized focal plate as previous surveys to match with the dimensions of the rest of the telescope components (e.g. the optical train), the robotic positioners themselves need to be made as small as possible. This challenging but not impossible quest has been led by the Astrobots team at EPFL in collaboration with the team from LBNL. Rombach et al.
[
17

presented the concept of the Fiber Positioning Module (FPM) as a possible solution to facilitate the management and maintenance of such a large number of miniaturized robotic positioners. With the help of two manufacturers namely, the Swiss company Micro Precision Systems (MPS) and the Japanese company Orbray, two prototypes with 6 positioners each have been assembled by each manufacturer. The two manufacturers are basing their prototypes on two different designs, which are the raft and trillium designs. EPFL and LBNL have both received a prototype of each manufacturer (so a prototype of each design).

In this paper, we will thoroughly discuss the different modular designs on which the prototypes have been based. Furthermore, we will present the very first results of the performance of both prototypes by creating a rigorous comparison to enhance our understanding of the different designs.

The fiber positioner modules are intended to host 63 robots with a pitch, center-to-center distance, of 6.2 mm. The robotic architecture is a SCARA similarly to DESI, MOONS or SDSS-V with to axes of rotation: alpha-beta, or theta-phi. The robots alpha and beta arm are the same length: 1.8 mm. The individual patrol area, or workspace, is therefore a plain disk of 7.2 mm diameter. The robots layout inside a module is designed to minimize workspaces overlaps, reducing the risk of collision, while maintaining high internal coverage (Figure
4(a)
).

Up to this point the two prototype studied in this paper are identical. They differ by their mechanical architecture. The MPS raft implements an
individual robots
architecture (Figure
4(b)
). Each robots is independent from the others and uses the triangular chassis for structural integrity. Their alpha and beta axes also move independently from the other; one can rotate the alpha arm of robot without moving its beta arm.

The Orbray raft, however, is based on a
trillium architecture
(Figure
4(c)
). This concept was developped first shown in Silber et al.
[
21
]
where its reference CAD design is also provided in the following link:
https://zenodo.org/record/6354859
.
The units of work are packs of 3 robots called
trilliums
, inserted into a triangular chassis. Their alpha and beta axes are also linked together: rotating the alpha axis will result in a rotation of the beta axis in the same direction as well. This extra rotation needs to be compensated in software, rotating the beta axis of the same amount in the opposite direction.

Despite the difference in mechanical architectures both module designs share the same 3-screws interface that bolts them to the focal plate, as shown in (Figure
3
). Since the robots are also driven by the same 4mm BLDC motors the electronics interface for control is also similar. Finally, each positioner is equiped with the same optical fiber which results in the same fiber interface as well. Therefore, both designs can theoretically both fit in the same focal plane.

XY Positioning Performance Testing by EPFL

A wide range of experimental testing has to be performed on the robotic positioners to verify their compliance with the requirements set by the projects utilizing them
[
22
]
. Previously, for surveys like SDSS-V and DESI, the robotic positioners were individual components that can be manipulated easily outside of the focal plate. However, with the modular solution, the testing requires jigs that are suitable for entire modules and not just individual positioners. Accordingly, in this paper, we have updated our testing jigs to accommodate the entire module and still be able to test the positioners one by one and later possibly all together. This section mainly focuses on the XY positioning performance testing which is a very critical test to assess the performance of the mechanics of the positioners.

4.1
Performance Metrics

In our evaluation, we focus on several key performance metrics. These metrics provide essential insights into the system’s behavior and guide us in optimizing its design and functionality. By meticulously analyzing these performance metrics, we gain a comprehensive understanding of the system’s strengths and areas for improvement.

•

Repeatability

–

Definition: Repeatability refers to the system’s ability to consistently return to the same position when repeatedly commanded to move to a specific target approaching from the same side (clockwise or counterclockwise).

–

Measurement: We perform multiple cycles of positioning the fiber to the same target and record the deviations from the expected position.

–

Desired Outcome: Lower repeatability errors indicate better precision and reliability, this allows us to have confidence in being able to calibrate the system and achieve the required positioning performance.

•

Datum Repeatability

–

Definition: Datum repeatability refers to how repeatable the position at the hard stop datum is reached.

–

Measurement: Moving the positioner arms several times to reach the arms’ datums until it is sure that we hit the datum and measure the position of the fiber tip.

–

Desired Outcome: This is the reference for any homing procedure of the system and must be repeatable.

•

Backlash

–

Definition: Backlash is the play or slack in the system due to mechanical clearances (e.g. gears, couplings).

–

Measurement: We measure the shift in position when going to the same target approaching from a different direction (clockwise and counterclockwise).

–

Desired Outcome: Lower backlash values lead to more precise positioning.

•

Non-linearity

–

Definition: Non-linearity assesses how closely the system’s actual movement follows a linear relationship with the commanded position.

–

Measurement: We compare the actual position (measured centroid) with the expected position at various target locations.

–

Desired Outcome: Ideally, the system should exhibit linear behavior, minimizing deviations from linearity. Due to imperfections in the gears (as shown in Figure
5
), there is non-linearity, but having lower values means the model can be fitted and the error would be small.

Non-linearity

The non-linearity measurement is conducted by commanding the positioner to move by one degree over the full angular range of both alpha and beta arms. As shown in Figure

Angular Tilt Testing

Angular tilt testing is a vital step to ensure the highest performance of the positioner not only mechanically but also optically. Large tilts in the positioner will result in severely degraded optical performance which cannot be easily corrected after having already been installed on the telescope. Therefore, prior tilt testing is absolutely necessary to be able to further improve the prototyping and make sure that the final product exhibits the best performances.

5.2
Tilt Test Concept

Figure 15
:
Schematic showing the angular tilt test concept with an LED source connected to a multi-mode fiber (MMF) held by a robotic positioner; P1-P4 are different XY positions; f is the focal length of the bi-convex lens; (a) scenario without tilt; (b) scenario with tilt.

The tilt test concept
[
22
,
23
]
heavily relies on the disentanglement of the XY movement and the tilt movement. The experimental configuration shown on Figure
15
using the bi-convex lens and the diffuser screen positioned at its focal length is what gives the possibility to measure tilt without any influence from the XY translation. Figure
15
(a) shows an LED source connected to a multi-mode fiber (MMF) which is held by a robotic positioner. The positioner translates in x and y reaching the positions P1 to P4. It should be noted that what we see on the figure are only the principle rays of the diverging beam at each XY position, and the rest of the light rays in the beam are not shown. Irrespective of the XY movement, the point
x_{1}
on the diffuser screen is almost stationary. However, if there is any tilt on the positioner’s side (tilt in fiber or tilt in the positioner’s arms), the principle rays will tilt accordingly, and a new point
x_{2}
will be created. The displacement
\Delta d
corresponds to the difference of
x_{2}
and
x_{1}
and can be calculated using the following equation:

\Delta d=x_{2}-x_{1}

(1)

Accordingly, the tilt angle can be calculated using the following formula:

\omega_{tilt}=tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\Delta d}{l_{focal}}\right)

(2)

where
\omega_{tilt}
is the tilt angle and
l_{focal}
is the focal length of the convex lens.

5.3
Disentanglement Verification Test

The resolution of the system is estimated by the amount of how much the system is disentangled in the XY translation compared to the tilt motion. The resolution is dependent on the focal length of the lens used. To measure the resolution, the module needs to be mounted on translation and tilt stages to calibrate for the amount of translation that occurs when there is a tilt stage. This will define the resolution of our system. Using the tilt setup with the diffuser screen at the correct focal length of the lens, the tilt stage will be moved by a known amount. The light blob on the screen will shift by a specific number of pixels. It should be noted that the diffuser screen acts as a far-field angular mapper achieving this disentanglement between the XY translation and the tilt. The displacement on the screen will be compared to the actual movement of the robot, and the XY translation will be mapped to the angular tilt. In our current setup, a movement of 1 degree on the tilt stage resulted in an actual displacement of the cylinder by 2 mm. Moving 2 mm on the translation stage, resulted in a movement of the blob by 3 pixels (or 0.2223 mm using the conversion factor 0.00741 mm/pixel). We then use the arctan function as follows: arctan(0.2223 mm/600 mm (focal length)), and the disentanglement resolution is then computed to be: 0.0212 degrees.

5.4.1
Short Focal Length Test-bench

The experimental test-bench utilized to measure the angular tilt stack (tilt between the optical fiber and the beta-arm, tilt between the beta-arm and the alpha-arm, tilt between the alpha-arm and the zero-reference) is shown in Figure
16
. The setup has been implemented based on the work presented by Kronig et al.
[
22
,
23
]
. It consists of a light source directly followed by a lens (bi-convex or plano-convex) of 100 mm focal length. The light then propagates through a 250x300 mm diffuser screen made of opal diffusing glass (
\#
43-042) which is placed at the focal length of the lens to ensure the disentanglement between the XY translation and tilt motions. The diffuser screen scatters the incoming light in many directions and results in a uniform light blob. The use of the diffuser screen is highly necessary, as the diffuser acts as an angular scatterer which enables
the full disentanglement of the light source’s translation and tilt
. It is essential to differentiate between the two effects to be able to extract each of them separately. The light blob projected on the screen is then captured by a 10 MP (acA3800-14um) Basler camera with a Fujinon objective lens (HF3520-12m) of a 35 mm focal length lens. The camera is placed about 65 mm away from the diffuser screen to ensure that the field of view covers the entire light blob.

5.4.2
Long Focal Length Test-bench

The setup presented earlier in Figure
16
works very well, but the disentanglement resolution of the system is limited, as it depends on the focal length of lens used. To be able to improve the system’s resolution, the focal length chosen needs to be enlarged. That said, the experimental setup will automatically extend and will require a larger optical table. To be able to solve this problem, the original experimental test-bench by Kronig et al.
[
22
,
23
]
has been modified (see Figure
17
) to be able to fit on the optical table and still use multi-mode fibers in the positioners instead of single-mode fibers. The reason why it is important to utilize multi-mode fibers in the testing instead of single-mode ones is because ultimately the fibers which will be used in the surveys are multi-mode. So, to have a better understanding of the overall performance of the positioners, it is preferred to utilize similar fibers to those installed later when the positioner modules are mounted on the telescope.

The experimental setup (shown in Figure
17
) consists in having the module with the optical fibers connected to the illumination source which is a powerful LED from Thorlabs (MINTF4). The optical fibers used are FBP polymicro fibers with 107 µm core diameter, 140 µm cladding diameter, and 170 µm polyimide coating. A plano-convex lens with focal length of 600 mm is directly placed after the positioners with the convex side facing the positioners. The current lens used covers all positioners. A flat mirror reflects the diverging light coming through the lens and projects it on a viewing screen larger than the opal diffuser used in the previous setup because the beam divergence is larger. In this modified system, we have the freedom to use any type of viewing screen which does not necessarily need to be transparent, as we use it in reflective mode. The viewing screen is placed at the focal length of the lens to ensure the disentanglement between the XY translation and tilt motions. A 20 MP Basler camera (acA5472-17um) having a larger field-of-view with a Fujinon objective lens (HF3520-12m) of a 35 mm focal length lens captures the light blob projected on the screen. The camera is placed about 1200 mm away from the screen to ensure that the entire blob is acquired by the camera.

The vectorial summation is intended to show the tilt result of both
\theta
and
\phi
while taking into account the orientation of the tilt contributions. This clarifies whether the tilt contributions will be summed up or will compensate each other. As can be seen in Figure

\phi

Certainly, the requirements are different from project to the other, but it is worth highlighting that the results are definitely promising considering that this is the first round of prototyping and further improvements to the arms are foreseen.

Another important parameter to consider in the overall tilt is the tilt between the beta arm and the optical fiber. As mentioned earlier in Section
5.1
, the contribution of this tilt is a combination of the tilt of the optical fiber inside the ferrule and the ferrule inside the beta arm. Therefore, it is highly critical to make sure that the process of the optical fiber insertion in the ferrule is very meticulously performed. The optical fibers used in our experiment were available fibers and not intentionally prepared, but it is recommended to use better fibers for future prototypes.

6
Experimental Testing by LBNL

Positioner tests were performed at Lawrence Berkeley National Lab (LBNL) on an Orbray module prototype with 2 Trillium, for a total of 6 positioners. This is a separate prototype than the one tested at EPFL and presented in this paper.

6.1
Basic Trillium Mechanics

Each Trillium unit has three positioners that each have two axes, alpha and beta. The alpha and beta axes are coupled via a transfer gear (see Figure
4(c)
and Silber et al (2022)
[
21
]
for more information). For moves with the beta arm, we define a move as changing the beta angle only. For moves with the alpha arm, we define moves as changing the alpha angle while keeping the beta angle constant. Because the two arms are coupled, when the alpha angle is changing the beta angle will also change. In order to keep the beta angle constant, the beta motor must be commanded to move in equal magnitude but opposite direction to the alpha motor. Since the motors used in the Trillium are the same as the DESI fiber positioners, we use DESI electronics and operate the motors at either of the two DESI speeds: 150 RPM or 9900 RPM
[
16
]
.

6.2
Test Setup

Figure 21
:
Experimental test-bench setup at LBNL.

The experimental setup, shown in Figure
21
, measures the X,Y positions of fibers via a 6.4 MP ZWO camera (ASI178MM). Attached to the camera is a 470 nm bandpass filter. The module is mounted horizontally to the optical bench and a fiducial fiber is added to remove instability in the setup. The fiducial and positioner fibers are back-lit using an integrating sphere that is connected to a 470 nm LED. Positioners are individually commanded to move to different locations, an image is taken before and after each move, and centroids are measured to determine the X,Y positions of the positioner and fiducial fibers. This setup is used for two main tests discussed in sections
6.3
and
6.4
, although it could be adapted for other tests as well.

6.3
High Resolution Arc Tests

The high resolution arc tests performed at LBNL were designed to see positioner movement over the range of each axis. The nominal ranges of alpha and beta are 360 and 180 degrees, respectively
[
21
]
. We perform one degree moves with a motor speed of 150 RPM that span these ranges in both the clockwise and counter-clockwise directions. An example of the centroid positions is shown in Figure
23
for positioner 21. We fit a circle to moves in each direction to measure the center. The centers are then used to calculate the angle between each move and is saved to calculate the angles during the backlash tests discussed in the next section.

Nominally, the center is a fixed location, however we see some deviation in the measured centers in each direction. This is seen in the beta arc of Figure
23
where the centroid positions are different for each direction. The length of the beta arm is fixed, but center of the beta arm will depend on the location of the alpha arm - it will be fixed if alpha is not moving - and it’s possible that, though it was not being commanded, the alpha arm moved slightly during the beta arc test for this positioner.

The positioning results for the 6 positioners are shown in Table
2
in the "Arc Residuals" column. The residual is calculated as the target angle minus the measured angle. The RMS for each positioner in each axis is shown, and is well within 0.3 degrees for all positioners. The performance in the alpha axis is generally better than the performance in the beta axis, likely due to the coupled nature of the Trillium design.

6.4
Backlash Tests

The backlash tests performed at LBNL are similar to those performed at EPFL (described in section
4.1
). In the tests at LBNL, we commanded each positioner to move 20 degrees, alternating between clockwise and counter-clockwise directions, 100 times in each direction at the 9900 RPM motor speed. We use the center points measured in section
6.3
to calculate the angle between each move, called the measured angle. The measured backlash is then calculated as the target angle (20 degrees) minus the measured angle, assuming that the amount each positioner does not move is solely due to the gear backlash. As mentioned in section
4.1
, lower backlash values lead to more precise positioning. This is important for spectroscopic surveys as too large of positioning errors will affect the amount of flux reaching the fiber.

The results of the backlash tests for each of the 6 positioners in the two axes is shown in Table
2
in the “Backlash” column. The RMS of the 6 positioners is calculated and is, on average, around 7 degrees and 12 degrees for the alpha and beta axes, respectively. The measured backlash for the alpha arm is similar to what was measured in section
4.2.3
for the alpha arm for the two MPS prototypes. The measured backlash for the beta arm, however, is nearly double than what was reported in section
4.2.3
. For the MPS prototype the beta and alpha axes are not coupled like in the Orbray prototypes, so it is not unsurprising that the measured backlash of the two prototypes is different. However it is still nearly double what is reported for the Orbray prototype in section
4.2.3
.

6.5
Tip-Tilt Measurement

We measure the tip and tilt angles of the LBNL Orbray module using an optical coordinate measuring machine (CMM) at LBNL. There are three angles that we measure: the angle between ferrule and the beta arm (
f
), the angle between the beta and alpha arms (
\phi
), and the angle between the alpha arm and the positioner axis (
\theta
), which are all described in table
1
and shown in Figure
18(a)
. For our measurements, we remove the fiber and instead use a long ferrule rod. On the CMM the measurements are made with respect to the module axis, and we assume that the positioners are aligned to the module.

We can only take measurements from the top-down view on the CMM, however we are still able to measure the angles
\theta
,
\phi
, and
f
. We are also able to calculate the change in focus by measuring the location of the tip of the ferrule rod. An example of the setup is shown in Figure
24
. We take four measurements for each positioner in the following order:

A.

Alpha at X hardstop, beta fully extended

B.

Move alpha 180 degrees, keeping beta fully extended

C.

Alpha at X hardstop, beta fully extend

D.

Move beta 180 degrees.

We then rotate the module 90 degrees and repeat these measurements for each positioner.
