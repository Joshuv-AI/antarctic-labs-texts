Self-testing and clinical outcomes in Black and White patients on warfarin for atrial fibrillation or venous thromboembolism
Authors: Brindha Rajakumar, Xiaowen Kong, Brian Haymart, Scott Kaatz, Gregory Krol, Mona Ali, Noelle Ryan, Stacy Ellsworth, Beverly Stallings, Tina Alexandris-Souphis, Amy DeLellis, Josh Errickson, James B. Froehlich, Geoffrey D. Barnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWarfarin management requires frequent international normalized ratio (INR) testing. Self-testing may reduce this burden and improve INR control and outcomes. However, concerns exist about variability in utilization and the effectiveness of self-testing across different patient groups.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate INR self-testing utilization and clinical outcomes in Black and White warfarin-treated patients.MethodsIn this retrospective, observational study, Black and White patients who were using warfarin for atrial fibrillation or venous thromboembolism (April 2012 to July 2024) were identified from the Michigan Anticoagulation Quality Improvement Initiative registry. INR control and outcomes were compared between self-testers and non–self-testers. Rates were adjusted by inverse probability weighting; comparisons between racial groups used the negative binomial model. Major and nonmajor bleeding events were defined based on the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria. Moderation analysis examined whether race influences self-testing’s safety and effectiveness.ResultsAmong 5903 warfarin-treated patients (20.5% Black, 79.5% White), self-testing was used by fewer than 1 in 7 patients (15.3% White, 10.1% Black). Self-testers had higher adjusted time in therapeutic range (TTR; 65.3% vs 59.8%; P < .001), fewer extreme INRs, and lower nonmajor bleeding rates (20.9 vs 29.9 per 100 patient-years; P < .0001) than patients who underwent traditional INR testing. Among Black patients, self-testers had higher TTR (58.8% vs 55.4%; P = .0018) and fewer nonmajor bleeds (23.1 vs 33.1 per 100 patient-years; P = .024) than Black patients who underwent traditional INR testing. Among White patients, self-testers had higher adjusted TTR (68.1% vs 61.3%, P < .0001) and fewer major (3.4 vs 4.9 per 100 patient-years, P = .014) and nonmajor bleeds (16.7 vs 26.2 per 100 patient-years, P < .0001) than White patients who underwent traditional INR testing. Thromboembolic events were similar between groups.ConclusionSelf-testing was utilized infrequently, especially among Black patients, but was associated with better INR control and less bleeding than traditional INR testing. Increased utilization and support of self-testing may improve patient outcomes.

Introduction

Warfarin remains a commonly prescribed oral anticoagulant for patients with various thromboembolic conditions, including atrial fibrillation (AF), venous thromboembolism (VTE), and mechanical heart valve replacement. Patients on warfarin require frequent laboratory testing to monitor their international normalized ratio (INR) [1]. When warfarin is poorly controlled, patients experience higher rates of bleeding and thromboembolic complications [2,3].

One intervention to improve INR control is patient self-testing with home INR monitors. In comparison to laboratory- or clinic-based INR testing, patients who performed self-testing have shown similar or higher time in therapeutic range (TTR) [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]; similar or lower rates of ischemic stroke and other thromboembolic events, major bleeding, and death [4,[7], [8], [9]]; and a higher level of convenience and satisfaction with warfarin management [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Self-testing may also serve as a cost-effective alternative to in-clinic warfarin management [11,12].

Although prior studies have found that Black patients on warfarin have lower TTR and higher rates of stroke, major bleeding, and death than White patients when using traditional INR laboratory monitoring, there is limited data on racial differences in INR self-testing utilization and effectiveness [[13], [14], [15]]. This study explores racial differences in self-testing utilization and outcomes to determine if increasing self-testing in higher-risk patient groups is an effective intervention to reduce variability in health outcomes.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the utilization of INR self-testing and associated clinical outcomes between self-testers and non–self-testers in Black and White patients using warfarin.

Methods

We performed a retrospective, observational cohort study using the Michigan Anticoagulation Quality Improvement Initiative (MAQI2) registry. MAQI2 is a Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan–sponsored collaborative of 8 anticoagulation clinics aimed at improving the quality of anticoagulation care in Michigan. At each site, a sample of adult patients starting warfarin (inception cohort) is entered into the MAQI2 clinical registry and followed longitudinally by trained abstractors. All clinical management is independently directed by local providers, nurses, and pharmacists, independent of MAQI2 data collection. Observational clinical data, including the indication for warfarin, demographics, comorbidities, concurrent medications, lab values, bleeding, and thrombotic events, were abstracted from patient medical records and entered into an online data form with standardized variable definitions. Random audits were performed by the MAQI2 coordinating center’s (University of Michigan) team to ensure accuracy. Data collection, research, and quality improvement efforts have been approved by the institutional review boards at the coordinating center and all participating sites.

Given the small sample size of non-White and non-Black warfarin-treated patients in this cohort, Black and White patients on warfarin for stroke prevention in AF or the treatment of VTE between April 2012 and July 2024 were identified from the MAQI2 registry. Patients using warfarin for other indications, including mechanical heart valve replacement, were excluded. We excluded patients with mechanical heart valve replacement because they constitute a relatively small proportion of our overall patient population, have varying target INR ranges, and have different risk profiles than patients with AF or VTE. Race was determined from the medical record based on patient self-reporting. Patients with <3 months of follow-up were excluded. Patients with ≥3 months of consecutive self-testing were classified as self-testers, whereas patients without any documented self-testing were classified as non–self-testers.

The decision to initiate warfarin self-testing was made through discussions between the patient or the patient’s family and the anticoagulation clinic staff or the primary warfarin prescriber. All self-testers received initial screening to assess eligibility and extensive training on how to effectively and safely use home INR monitors, how to report results to the anticoagulation clinic, and the importance of timely monitoring. Warfarin dose adjustments and INR scheduling were managed by the anticoagulation clinic staff.

Rates of bleeding and thrombotic events were calculated from follow-up data. Bleeding events were classified by severity (all, major, and nonmajor) and whether the patient required emergency department evaluation and treatment. Major and nonmajor bleedings were defined by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria [16]. Thrombotic events included ischemic stroke and recurrent VTE. TTR was calculated using linear interpolation, as described by Rosendaal et al. [17]. To assess INR control, we reported mean TTR and the frequency of extremely high (>5) or low (<1.6) INRs.

Self-tester outcome rates (per 100 patient-years) were calculated only for the period of time when eligible patients were using warfarin self-testing. This prevented individual patients from contributing to both treatment groups and isolated warfarin quality metrics only to the period of time when warfarin self-testing was being utilized. Non–self-tester outcome rates were calculated for the entire treatment period. Inverse probability weighting (IPW) was used to balance baseline differences between groups, and outcome rates were compared using negative binomial models. The propensity model was adjusted for baseline demographics, comorbidity, and socioeconomic factors along with concurrent antiplatelet use (Supplementary Figure S1). Baseline differences that persisted after IPW were incorporated into the negative binomial models. Additional adjustments were made to bleeding and thrombotic outcomes based on race, antiplatelet use, and INR testing frequency. In addition, TTR was adjusted by the SAMe-TT2R2 score, a previously validated tool to identify patients at risk for low TTR [18].

To examine how patient races may influence the safety and effectiveness of self-testing, we included race, self-testing, and their interaction in the regression model. A moderating effect was considered present if the interaction term was statistically significant. Moderation was examined for INR control, bleeding, and ischemic stroke.

Results

Of the 5903 patients on warfarin for AF or VTE who met all the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 1208 (20.5%) were Black and 4695 (79.5%) were White (Table). The Black patient group comprised 122 (10.1%) self-testers and 1086 (89.9%) non–self-testers, while the White patient group comprised 720 (15.3%) self-testers and 3975 (84.7%) non–self-testers. The proportion of home testers in the White group was higher than that in the Black group (15.3% vs 10.1%; P < .001). A total of 183 self-testing patients met the inclusion criteria but were excluded because they did not complete at least 3 months of self-testing. There were several notable differences between groups, including self-testers being older, more likely to be female, more likely to have Medicare insurance, and more likely to have hypertension. All other patient characteristics were similar between self-testers and non–self-testers. Among Black patients, self-testers had higher adjusted TTR compared to traditional INR management (58.8% vs 55.4%; P < .0018) and they also had a lower proportion of extremely low INRs (Figures 1 and 2, Supplementary Table S1). Self-testers had fewer nonmajor bleeds (23.1 vs 33.1 per 100 patient-years; P = .024) than traditional INR management (Figures 2 and 3, Supplementary Table S1). Major bleeding and thromboembolic events were similar between Black patients using self-testing and traditional INR management.

Among White patients, self-testers also had higher adjusted TTR compared to traditional INR management (68.1% vs 61.3%; P < .0001) and they also had a lower proportion of extremely low or high INRs (Figures 1 and 2, Supplementary Table S1). Self-testers had less overall bleeding (20.7 vs 32.1 per 100 patient-years; P < .0001) compared to traditional INR management, driven by both fewer major bleeds (3.4 vs 4.9 per 100 patient-years; P = .014) and fewer nonmajor bleeds (16.7 vs 26.2 per 100 patient-years; P < .0001; Figures 2 and 3, Supplementary Table S1). Thromboembolic events were similar between White self-testers and those who utilized traditional INR management.

Moderation analysis found that race (Black vs White) was a significant moderator on INR control (β = −0.0266, SE = 0.0112; P = .0179), all bleeds (β = −0.3098, SE = 0.1535; P = .0436), and major bleeds (β = −0.7671, SE = 0.3219; P = .0172). This indicates that the effect of self-testing on these outcomes is dependent on race and that white patients benefitted the most from self-testing. For example, the estimated TTR in White self-testers was 6.8 points higher than that in White patients who underwent traditional testing, while the estimated TTR in Black self-testers was only 3.4 points higher than that in Black patients who underwent traditional testing (Figure 4, Supplementary Table S2).Figure 4Moderation model path diagrams. Discussion

In this observational, multicenter study of warfarin-treated patients with AF or VTE, patients who self-tested had better INR control and fewer bleeds than patients who used traditional INR laboratory testing. This benefit was seen across both Black and White patients.

Previous studies have also shown similar or higher TTR with self-testing than with traditional lab testing [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. However, only 1 prior study [19] reported findings specific to Black patients, similarly showing lower rates of self-testing as was found in this analysis. Novel findings from our study include an improvement in TTR and nonmajor bleeds among Black patients using self-testing in comparison to traditional laboratory testing.

In addition to better outcomes, self-testing offers convenience for patients and boosts their satisfaction with warfarin management [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. However, self-testing requires highly motivated patients willing to take an active role in their care and the physical capability to perform point-of-care testing. It is unclear how best to identify patients who are appropriate candidates for self-testing and whether similar TTR improvements would occur in a wider group of patients on warfarin. This is particularly relevant given that our study and others have shown lower utilization of self-testing in Black patients [19]. While patients might benefit from increased use of self-testing to improve INR control, reduce adverse events, and enhance their quality of life, identifying which patients are most likely to realize these benefits remains crucial to improve outcomes within an antithrombotic stewardship care model.

In 1 study from 2005, policies and procedures of almost 60% of the surveyed anticoagulation clinics in the United States prohibited INR self-testing for enrolled patients [20]. Clearly, these policies have changed in the 2 decades since that study was conducted. Still, important barriers from 2005 likely persist, including the cost of self-testing instruments and reagent cartridges and the fear that self-testing might lead to unintended self-management.

While patient out-of-pocket cost is often stated as a barrier to self-testing, many studies provide evidence that self-testing is often less costly than in-clinic management from a more global healthcare utilization standpoint. One study, evaluating the cost savings of home INR monitors in the home health setting, found that self-testing would result in $18,810 in total savings to society annually [11]. Another study found that, even with the substantial increase in the frequency of testing from clinic testing to self-testing, an overall cost saving of over $140,000 per 100 patients over a 5-year period would be realized from the reduction in time and travel costs for patients and caregivers, as well as fewer adverse events [12].

Currently in the United States, Medicare pays for self-testing costs if the patient has AF, mechanical heart valves, or VTE as long as the patient has been on warfarin for at least 90 days, has undergone face-to-face training, demonstrates continued proper use of the device, and tests no more than once per week [21]. Most private insurances in the United States also follow Medicare’s coverage policy. One promising antithrombotic stewardship intervention could educate providers and patients about better outcomes, higher quality of life, and lower costs with expanded use of self-testing. Highly motivated patients may also want to explore warfarin self-management, given further improvements in self-efficacy and clinical outcomes in patients [[22], [23], [24]].

Despite the many strengths of this multicenter, chart-abstracted study design, important limitations must be considered. Since our patient population only includes patients from Michigan-based anticoagulation clinics with AF or VTE, generalizability to patients from other geographic locations or other warfarin indications may be limited. Additionally, this is an observational, nonrandomized study, so there may be potential bias in the choice of patients for self-testing, how warfarin dose adjustments and INR testing frequency were managed, and the amount of training patients received. Unmeasured patient factors may also have impacted the results. Finally, data describing staff’s decision-making related to patient selection for self-testing were not available for analysis.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the effectiveness of warfarin self-testing specifically between Black and White patients in the United States. Self-testing can serve as an intervention to achieve better INR control, outcomes, and quality of life in patients on warfarin. Antithrombotic stewardship programs should target eligible patients to increase self-testing in an effort to improve clinical outcomes and patient self-efficacy.

Conclusion

Overall, in this multicenter observational cohort of warfarin-treated patients with AF or VTE, INR self-testing was associated with better anticoagulation control and fewer bleeding events than traditional laboratory monitoring, with benefits observed in both Black and White patients. These findings support INR self-testing as a practical, patient-centered strategy that may improve outcomes and the quality of life for many patients.
