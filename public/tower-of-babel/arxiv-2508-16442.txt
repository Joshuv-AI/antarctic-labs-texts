A limit theorem for Hausdorff approximation by random inscribed polytopes
Mathias Sonnleitner

Abstract
. Approximate a smooth convex body K K with nonvanishing curvature by the convex hull of n n independent random points sampled from its boundary ∂ K \partial K . In case the points are distributed according to the optimal density, we prove that the rescaled approximation error in Hausdorff distance tends to a Gumbel distributed random variable. The proof is based on an asymptotic relation to covering properties of random geodesic balls on ∂ K \partial K and on a limit theorem due to Janson.

1.
Introduction and main result

Let
K_{n}=\conv\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
be the convex hull of independent random points
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
distributed on the boundary
\partial K
of a smooth convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e. a convex compact set with nonempty interior. The Hausdorff distance between
K_{n}
and
K
is given by

\displaystyle\delta_{H}(K_{n},K)=\max_{x\in\partial K}\min_{y\in K_{n}}\|x-y\|,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm. Geometrically, it measures the maximal height of a cap of
K
cut off by a facet of
K_{n}
. We are interested in the asymptotic behavior of the random variable
\delta_{H}(K_{n},K)
as
n\to\infty
.

Assume that
K
belongs to
\mathcal{K}^{3}_{+}
, the set of convex bodies having three times continuously differentiable boundary with everywhere positive Gaussian curvature
\kappa_{K}
. If the random points
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are distributed on
\partial K
according to a continuously differentiable density
h\colon\partial K\to(0,\infty)
with respect to the
(d-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
, then by
[
10
]
(see
[
28
]
for the planar case) it holds that

\hskip-14.22636pt\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)^{\frac{2}{d-1}}\delta_{H}(K_{n},K)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\kappa_{d-1}}\max_{x\in\partial K}\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{K}(x)}}{h(x)}\right)^{\frac{2}{d-1}},

(1)

where
\kappa_{d-1}
is the volume of the
(d-1)
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball and
\overset{\mathbb{P}}{\to}
denotes convergence in probability. Moreover, the right-hand side of (
1
) is minimal for the density

h_{\kappa}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa_{K}(x)}}{v_{\kappa}(K)},\quad x\in\partial K,\qquad\text{where }v_{\kappa}(K):=\int_{\partial K}\sqrt{\kappa_{K}(x)}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x).

(2)

Remark 1
.

The convergence (
1
) holds also in expectation, see
[
9
,
20
]
.

The density
h_{\kappa}
also appears when studying optimal point sets. Namely, the sequence of optimal point sets
P_{n}^{*}=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
minimizing
\delta_{H}(\conv(P_{n}),K)
over all
n
-point sets
P_{n}\subset\partial K
is uniformly distributed with density
h_{\kappa}
, that is,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|P_{n}^{*}\cap A|}{n}=\int_{A}h_{\kappa}(x)\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x)\qquad\text{ for any Borel set }A\subset\partial K,

and, similar to (
1
) for
h=h_{\kappa}
, the optimal points
P_{n}^{*}
satisfy

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{\frac{2}{d-1}}\delta_{H}(\conv(P_{n}^{*}),K)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\vartheta_{d-1}}{\kappa_{d-1}}v_{\kappa}(K)\right)^{\frac{2}{d-1}},

where
\vartheta_{d-1}
is the minimal covering density of
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
by unit balls, see
[
10
,
12
,
27
]
.

If the error of the random approximation of
K
by
K_{n}
is measured in terms of missed volume instead of Hausdorff distance, then more precise asymptotics are available. For example, there is a central limit theorem due to
[
31
]
, see also the variance bounds in
[
23
,
26
]
and related central limit theorems in
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
14
,
17
,
24
,
32
,
34
]
. Moreover, to minimize the volume difference, it is optimal to sample proportional to
\kappa_{K}^{1/(d+1)}
. Random points sampled according to this density behave similarly to optimal points, particularly in high dimension, see
[
21
,
22
,
29
]
.

If
K_{n}
is the convex hull of independent random points distributed in the interior of
K
, it was proven in
[
4
]
for
d=2
that, for suitable scalars
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
, as
n\to\infty
,

\frac{\delta_{H}(K_{n},K)-a_{n}}{b_{n}}\xrightarrow{\rm d}G,

(3)

where
G
is a standard Gumbel random variable, i.e.
\mathbb{P}[G\leq x]=\exp(-e^{-x})
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\xrightarrow{\rm d}
denotes convergence in distribution. Thus, the random variables
\delta_{H}(K_{n},K),n\in\mathbb{N},
exhibit Gumbel fluctuations similarly to maxima of independent random variables. The proof in
[
4
]
relies on a blocking technique together with results from extreme-value theory for weakly dependent random variables. For the case
d\geq 3
we refer to
[
7
]
.

Our main result states that a limit theorem of the form (
3
) holds provided the random points are distributed on
\partial K
with respect to the density
h_{\kappa}
as in (
2
).

Theorem 1
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}_{+}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independently distributed on
\partial K
with density
h_{\kappa}
as given in (
2
) and let
K_{n}=\conv\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
. Let

c_{n}=\Big(\frac{v_{\kappa}(K)}{\kappa_{d-1}}\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)^{\frac{2}{d-1}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha=\frac{1}{(d-1)!}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\frac{d+1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\Big)^{d-2},

where
v_{\kappa}(K)=\int_{\partial K}\sqrt{\kappa_{K}(x)}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x)
is as in (
2
). Then (
3
) holds with

a_{n}=\Big(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{(d-2)\log\log n+\log\alpha}{(d-1)\log n}\Big)\cdot c_{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{n}=\frac{c_{n}}{(d-1)\log n}.

In particular, Theorem
1
implies the convergence in (
1
) for
h=h_{\kappa}
. It remains open to extend it to other sampling densities.

The proof of Theorem
1
is based on a limit theorem of the form (
3
) for maximal spacings due to
[
16
]
, see Lemma
7
below, and an asymptotic relation between the geodesic covering radius of a point set on the boundary
\partial K
and the Hausdorff distance between its convex hull to
K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}_{+}
. Here, for any finite point set
P\subset\partial K
, the geodesic covering radius
\varrho(P)
is given by

\varrho(P)=\inf\Big\{\varrho>0\colon\partial K\subset\bigcup_{x\in P}B_{\gamma}(x,\varrho)\Big\},

where
B_{\gamma}(x,\varrho)
denotes a geodesic ball on
\partial K
with center
x\in\partial K
and radius
\varrho>0
with respect to the metric
\gamma
induced by the second fundamental form, as explained below. The proof of (
1
) relies on the asymptotic relation

\delta_{H}(\conv(P),K)=\frac{\varrho(P)^{2}}{2}(1+o(1))\qquad\text{as}\qquad\varrho(P)\to 0,

proven in
[
12
, Theorem 4]
. For the proof of Theorem
1
we require the following refinement.

Theorem 2
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}_{+}
. There exist
\varrho>0
and
C>0
such that, for any finite point set
P\subset\partial K
with
\varrho(P)\leq\varrho
,

\Big|\delta_{H}(\conv(P),K)-\frac{\varrho(P)^{2}}{2}\Big|\leq C\varrho(P)^{3}.

If
K
is a Euclidean ball and
\varrho(P)
is taken with respect to the Euclidean metric, then Theorem
2
holds even with
C=0
, see e.g.
[
3
, Lemma 7.1]
, and Theorem
1
can be deduced from the limit theorem in
[
30
, Theorem 3.2]
using the Delta Method.

If
K
is a polytope, only the distance from
K_{n}
to the vertices of
K
contributes to the Hausdorff distance. In case of
d=2
, a limit distribution containing one factor per vertex was obtained in
[
4
]
for points distributed inside and in
[
20
]
for points distributed on the boundary. It remains open to obtain analogous results in higher dimensions.

Remark 2
.

The related problem of deriving a limit theorem for the diameter of the random convex hull
K_{n}
was solved in
[
18
]
for points sampled uniformly in the unit ball or on the unit sphere. The limiting distribution is Weibull, and Gumbel if the dimension grows with the number of points
[
13
]
.

2.
Proofs

In the following, as in
[
10
,
12
]
, we equip
\partial K
with a Riemannian structure induced by the second fundamental form. This is combined with a uniform local approximation of the boundary
\partial K
by osculating paraboloids, as used for example in
[
24
]
, to get uniform bounds on the metric structure of
\partial K
. We deduce Theorem
2
from a local version for individual caps. We prove volume asymptotics for small geodesic balls in Lemma
6
below, assuming only
K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}_{+}
, and then use the Delta Method to deduce Theorem
1
from the limit theorem for maximal spacings in
[
16
]
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}_{+}
and
p\in\partial K
. Then there is a unique affine hyperplane
H_{K}(p)
supporting
\partial K
at
p
. Write
\mathrm{proj}_{p}
for the orthogonal projection onto
H_{K}(p)
. Let
U=U^{(p)}
be an open neighborhood of
p
in
\partial K
such that
\mathrm{proj}_{p}
maps
U
homeomorphically onto an open ball
U^{\prime}=\mathrm{proj}_{p}(U)\subset H_{K}(p)
. Identifying
H_{K}(p)
with
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
with
p
as the origin, parametrize
U
in the form
U=\{(u,f^{(p)}(u))\colon u\in U^{\prime}\}
, where
f^{(p)}(u)
is the distance of
u\in U^{\prime}
to its preimage
\mathrm{proj}_{p}^{-1}(u)\in U
. By assumption, the function
f^{(p)}
is three times continuously differentiable and convex on
U^{\prime}
. Moreover, see
[
24
, Section 5]
, for any
\delta>0
there exists
\lambda>0
such that, for all
p\in\partial K
and all
y\in B^{(p)}(0,4\lambda):=\{x\in H_{K}(p)\colon\|x-p\|\leq 4\lambda\}
,

(1+\delta)^{-1}b_{0}^{(p)}(y)\leq f^{(p)}(y)\leq(1+\delta)b_{0}^{(p)}(y),

(4)

where
b_{0}^{(p)}
is the quadratic form induced by the Hessian of
f^{(p)}
at
0
and given by

b_{0}^{(p)}(y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}f_{ij}^{(p)}(0)y_{i}y_{j},\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}.

Here and in the following,
f^{(p)}_{ij}
and
f^{(p)}_{k}
denote partial derivatives of
f^{(p)}
and indices
i,j,k
run from one to
d-1
. The Gaussian curvature
\kappa_{K}(p)
at
p\in\partial K
is given by the determinant of the matrix
(f_{ij}^{(p)}(0))_{i,j}
and remains uniformly bounded between two positive constants. Thus, for any
p\in\partial K
,

c_{K}\|y\|^{2}\leq b_{0}^{(p)}(y)\leq C_{K}\|y\|^{2},\qquad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1},

(5)

where
c_{K},C_{K}>0
depend only on
K
. Let
\delta=1
and fix
\lambda
according to (
4
). For each
p\in\partial K
, choose
U^{(p)}
such that
\mathrm{proj}_{p}(U^{(p)})=B^{(p)}(0,\lambda)
.

For
p\in\partial K
and
u\in B^{(p)}(0,2\lambda)
the second fundamental form is given by

q_{u}(s)=q_{u}^{(p)}(s)=\sum_{i,j}\frac{f_{ij}(u)}{\sqrt{1+\sum_{k}f_{k}(u)^{2}}}s_{i}s_{j}\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}.

Since
\kappa_{K}>0
, the quadratic form
q_{u}^{(p)}
is positive definite. This holds also in the following uniform sense.

Lemma 3
.

It holds for all
p\in\partial K
and all
u\in B^{(p)}(0,2\lambda)
that

q_{u}^{(p)}(s)\geq\frac{c_{K}}{2}\|s\|^{2},\qquad s\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1},

where
c_{K}>0
is as in (
5
).

Proof.

Let
p\in\partial K
and identify
H_{K}(p)
with
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. Let
U=U^{(p)}
and
f=f^{(p)}
be as above. Let
u\in B(0,2\lambda)=B^{(p)}(0,2\lambda)
and
x=(u,f(u))
. For
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
with
u+h\in B(0,3\lambda)
, define

y=\Big(u+h,f(u)+\sum_{k}f_{k}(u)h_{k}\Big)\in H_{K}(x).

Let
x_{1}=(u+h,f(u+h))\in\partial K
. By Taylor’s theorem we find
\xi\in[0,1]
such that

\|x_{1}-y\|=f(u+h)-f(u)-\sum_{k}f_{k}(u)h_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}f_{ij}(u+\xi h)h_{i}h_{j}.

(6)

By (
4
), the angle
\beta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
enclosed between
H_{K}(x)
and
H_{K}(p)=\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
satisfies

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sum_{k}f_{k}(u)^{2}}}=\cos\beta\geq c_{0}

(7)

for some absolute constant
c_{0}>0
. Thus,

\|x-y\|\leq\frac{\|h\|}{\cos\beta}\leq c_{0}^{-1}\|h\|

(8)

and it follows from (
6
) that
y_{1}=\mathrm{proj}_{x}(x_{1})\in H_{K}(x)
satisfies

\displaystyle\|y_{1}-y\|\leq\|x_{1}-y\|\leq(d-1)\max_{i,j}\sup_{z\in B(0,3\lambda)}|f_{ij}(z)|\cdot\|h\|^{2}.

Using (
8
) and continuity of the second derivatives of
f
on
B(0,3\lambda)
, we find
\delta>0
such that

\|x-y_{1}\|\leq\|x-y\|+\|y-y_{1}\|<\lambda,\qquad\text{whenever }\|h\|<\delta.

Thus, for
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
with
\|h\|\leq\delta
it holds that
x_{1}\in U^{(x)}
, which is parametrized via
f^{(x)}
and
H_{K}(x)
. Moreover, the previous assumption
u+h\in B(0,3\lambda)
is satisfied. By uniform continuity of the second derivatives of
f
on
B(0,3\lambda)
we can additionally assume that
\delta
is small enough such that

f_{ij}(u+\xi h)\leq 2\,f_{ij}(u),\qquad\|h\|\leq\delta.

(9)

Since
y_{1}
is the orthogonal projection of
x_{1}
onto
H_{K}(x)
, combining (
5
), (
6
) and (
9
), we deduce that, for any
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
with
\|h\|=\delta
,

q^{(p)}_{u}(h)\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{f_{ij}(u+\xi h)}{\sqrt{1+\sum_{k}f_{k}(u)^{2}}}h_{i}h_{j}=\|x_{1}-y_{1}\|\geq\frac{c_{K}}{2}\|x-y_{1}\|^{2}\geq\frac{c_{K}}{2}\|h\|^{2},

where
c_{K}>0
is as in (
5
). By homogenity, this completes the proof.
∎

The assumption
K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}_{+}
implies that the Riemannian metric induced by the second fundamental form is of class
C^{1}
on
\partial K
. In this metric, the length of a continuously differentiable curve
k\colon[0,1]\to U
connecting two points
k(0)=x
and
k(1)=y
in
U=U^{(p)}
for some
p\in\partial K
is given by

\ell(k):=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{q_{u(\tau)}(u^{\prime}(\tau))}\mathrm{d}\tau,

where
u=\mathrm{proj}_{p}(k)
. If
x
and
y
do not both belong to
U
, we dissect the curve suitably and add. The geodesic distance
\gamma(x,y)
between
x,y\in\partial K
is given by the infimum of lengths over all continuously differentiable curves in
\partial K
connecting
x
and
y
.

If
A\subset U
is measurable with
A^{\prime}=\mathrm{proj}_{p}(A)
, then its Riemannian volume is given by

\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}(A)=\int_{A^{\prime}}\sqrt{\det q_{u}}\mathrm{d}u=\int_{A}\sqrt{\kappa_{K}(x)}\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(x),

and, in general, for
A\subset\partial K
we dissect and add. Thus, the Riemannian volume
\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}
has density
h_{\kappa}
as in (
2
) with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
on
\partial K
. Let

B_{\gamma}(x,r)=\{y\in\partial K\colon\gamma(x,y)\leq r\}

be the (closed) geodesic ball with center
x\in\partial K
and radius
r>0
.

Lemma 4
.

For every
p\in\partial K
, it holds that
B_{\gamma}(p,\varrho_{0})\subset U^{(p)}
with

\varrho_{0}=\lambda\sqrt{c_{K}}/5
, where
c_{K}
is as in (
5
).

Proof.

Let
p\in\partial K
and let
U=U^{(p)}
be as above with
\mathrm{proj}_{p}(U)=B(0,\lambda)
, where we identify
H_{K}(p)
with
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. Let
x\in\partial K
with
\gamma(x,p)\leq\varrho_{0}
and connect
p
with
x
by a continuously differentiable curve
k
in
\partial K
of length
\ell(k)\leq 2\varrho_{0}
with
k(0)=p
and
k(1)=x
. Let
\alpha\in[0,1]
be such that
k([0,\alpha])\subset U
. Then the orthogonal projection
u=\mathrm{proj}_{p}(k)
satisfies
u([0,\alpha])\subset B(0,\lambda)
. It holds by Lemma
3
that

\ell(k)\geq\int_{0}^{\alpha}\sqrt{q_{u(\tau)}(u^{\prime}(\tau))}\mathrm{d}\tau\geq\sqrt{\frac{c_{K}}{2}}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\|u^{\prime}(\tau)\|\mathrm{d}\tau.

(10)

The integral on the right-hand side of (
10
) equals the arc length of the curve
u\colon[0,\alpha]\to B(0,\lambda)
. In particular,

\ell(k)\geq\sqrt{\frac{c_{K}}{2}}\|p-u(\tau)\|,\qquad\tau\leq\alpha.

Then
\|p-u(\tau)\|\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\varrho_{0}}{\sqrt{c_{K}}}<\frac{2}{3}\lambda
for
\tau\leq\alpha
and the curve
u
remains within
B(0,\frac{2}{3}\lambda)
. Since
u
was the restriction of
\mathrm{proj}_{p}(k)
to
B(0,\lambda)
, the curve
k
remains within
U
and thus
x\in U
. This proves that
B_{\gamma}(p,\varrho_{0})\subset U
.
∎

Write
\mathrm{dist}(y,A)=\inf_{x\in A}\|x-y\|
for the Euclidean distance of a point
y
to a set
A
. We have the following local version of Theorem
2
.

Lemma 5
.

There exist
\varrho>0
and
C>0
such that, for any
x,y\in\partial K
, if
\gamma(x,y)\leq\varrho
, then the Euclidean distance between
y
and the support hyperplane
H_{K}(x)
of
\partial K
at
x
satisfies

\Big|\mathrm{dist}\big(y,H_{K}(x)\big)-\frac{\gamma(x,y)^{2}}{2}\Big|\leq C\gamma(x,y)^{3}.

Proof.

Let
\varrho_{0}
be as in Lemma
4
. Fix
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\in\partial K
such that the geodesic balls
B_{\gamma}(p_{1},\varrho_{0}),\dots,B_{\gamma}(p_{m},\varrho_{0})
cover
\partial K
. This is possible by compactness of
\partial K
and the fact that the topology induced by
\gamma
coincides with the one inherited by the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By Lemma
4
, the neighborhoods
U^{(p_{1})},\dots,U^{(p_{m})}
cover
\partial K
. Moreover, for any
\ell=1,\dots,m
, the derivatives of
f^{(p_{\ell})}
up to third order are uniformly bounded on
B^{(p_{\ell})}(0,2\lambda)
by a constant only depending on
K
(and
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
).

Let
x,y\in\partial K
with
\gamma(x,y)\leq\varrho
, where
\varrho\leq\frac{\varrho_{0}}{2}
will be chosen later. In particular, both
x
and
y
belong to some
U=U^{(p_{\ell})}
and the orthogonal projections
x^{\prime}=\mathrm{proj}_{p_{\ell}}(x)
and
y^{\prime}=\mathrm{proj}_{p_{\ell}}(y)
onto
H_{K}(p_{\ell})=\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
are contained in
B(0,\lambda)=B^{(p_{\ell})}(0,\lambda)
. The function
f=f^{(p_{\ell})}
represents
U
in the form
(u,f(u))
where
u\in B(0,\lambda)
. Since

\gamma(x,y)\leq\frac{\varrho_{0}}{2}\leq\inf_{z\in\partial K\setminus U}\gamma(x,z),

(11)

a curve connecting
x
and
y
and having minimal length must be contained in
U
. Let

\mathcal{C}=\{u\in C^{1}([0,1];B(0,2\lambda))\colon u(0)=x^{\prime},u(1)=y^{\prime}\}

be the set of continuously differentiable curves
u\colon[0,1]\to B(0,2\lambda)
connecting
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
. Moreover, for
z_{1},z_{2}\in B(0,2\lambda)
, define a
d-1
by
d-1
matrix
G(z_{1},z_{2})
with entries

G_{ij}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{f_{ij}(z_{1})}{\sqrt{1+\sum_{k}f_{k}(z_{2})^{2}}}

such that, by (
11
), the geodesic distance between
x
and
y
satisfies

\gamma(x,y)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{C}}L(u),\qquad\text{}L(u):=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\langle G(u(\tau),u(\tau))\cdot u^{\prime}(\tau),u^{\prime}(\tau)\rangle}\mathrm{d}\tau,

(12)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard Euclidean inner product. By Lemma
3
and uniform boundedness of the first and second derivatives of
f
, it holds that

c_{G}\|s\|^{2}\leq\langle G(z_{1},z_{2})s,s\rangle\leq C_{G}\|s\|^{2},\qquad z_{1},z_{2}\in B(0,2\lambda),s\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1},

(13)

for some
c_{G},C_{G}>0
depending only on
K
(and
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
). Thus, for every
u\in\mathcal{C}
,

\sqrt{c_{G}}\len(u)\leq L(u)\leq\sqrt{C_{G}}\len(u),\qquad\text{ where }\len(u):=\int_{0}^{1}\|u^{\prime}(\tau)\|\mathrm{d}\tau

is the arc length of the curve
u
. In particular, this holds for the straight line segment connecting
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
which is parametrized by
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{C}
with

\bar{u}(\tau):=x^{\prime}+\tau(y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}),\qquad\tau\in[0,1],

(14)

and has minimal arc length

\len(\bar{u})=\|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\|=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{C}}\len(u).

By standard methods in the calculus of variation, see e.g.
[
8
, Chapter 7]
, the infimum in (
12
) is attained by some
u^{*}\in\mathcal{C}
. The arc length of the curve
u^{*}
satisfies

\sqrt{c_{G}}\|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\|\leq\sqrt{c_{G}}\len(u^{*})\leq\gamma(x,y)\leq L(\bar{u})\leq\sqrt{C_{G}}\|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\|

(15)

and thus the curve
u^{*}
is fully contained in the Euclidean ball

B:=B\Big(x^{\prime},\sqrt{\frac{C_{G}}{c_{G}}}\|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\|\Big).

(16)

Choose
\varrho\in(0,\frac{\varrho_{0}}{2})
such that
\sqrt{\frac{C_{G}}{c_{G}}}\varrho<\lambda
. Then
B\subset B(0,2\lambda)
.

The distance
\mathrm{dist}(y,H_{K}(x))
of
y
to the supporting hyperplane
H_{K}(x)
of
\partial K
at
x
is given by Taylor’s theorem as

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(y,H_{K}(x))

\displaystyle=\frac{f(y^{\prime})-f(x^{\prime})-\sum_{k}f_{k}(x^{\prime})(y^{\prime}_{k}-x^{\prime}_{k})}{\sqrt{1+\sum_{k}f_{k}(x^{\prime})^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\frac{f_{ij}(x^{\prime}+\xi(y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}))}{\sqrt{1+\sum_{k}f_{k}(x^{\prime})^{2}}}(y^{\prime}_{i}-x^{\prime}_{i})(y^{\prime}_{j}-x^{\prime}_{j}),

(17)

for some
\xi\in[0,1]
. For
\xi
as in (
2
), define

\bar{\gamma}(x,y)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{C}}\bar{L}(u),\qquad\bar{L}(u):=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\langle G(x^{\prime}+\xi(y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}),x^{\prime})\cdot u^{\prime}(\tau),u^{\prime}(\tau)\rangle}\mathrm{d}\tau.

(18)

The infimum in the definition of
\bar{\gamma}(x,y)
is attained by some
u\in C
with constant derivative, that is, given by
\bar{u}
as in (
14
), see e.g.
[
8
, Chapter 7]
. This implies

\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}(x,y)=\bar{L}(\bar{u})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\langle G(x^{\prime}+\xi(y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}),x^{\prime})\cdot(y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}),y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\,\mathrm{dist}(y,H_{K}(x))}.

(19)

It follows from (
13
) that

\sqrt{c_{G}}\|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\|\leq\bar{\gamma}(x,y)\leq\sqrt{C_{G}}\|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\|.

(20)

It remains to estimate the difference between
\bar{\gamma}(x,y)
and
\gamma(x,y)
. By optimality of
u^{*}
for (
12
) and
\bar{u}
for (
18
), it holds that

\gamma(x,y)=L(u^{*})\leq L(\bar{u})\leq\bar{\gamma}(x,y)+|L(\bar{u})-\bar{L}(\bar{u})|,

and analogously with
\gamma,L
and
u^{*}
exchanged with
\bar{\gamma},\bar{L}
and
\bar{u}
. This gives

|\gamma(x,y)-\bar{\gamma}(x,y)|\leq\max\big\{|L(\bar{u})-\bar{L}(\bar{u})|,|L(u^{*})-\bar{L}(u^{*})|\big\}.

(21)

Let
u\in\{u^{*},\bar{u}\}
. Then
u
is contained in the ball
B
as in (
16
). Note that, for any
z_{1},\dots,z_{4}\in B(0,2\lambda)
and
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
,

\displaystyle\big|\sqrt{\langle G(z_{1},z_{2})\cdot s,s\rangle}-\sqrt{\langle G(z_{3},z_{4})\cdot s,s\rangle}\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\langle(G(z_{1},z_{2})-G(z_{3},z_{4}))\cdot s,s\rangle|}{\sqrt{\langle G(z_{1},z_{2})\cdot s,s\rangle}+\sqrt{\langle G(z_{1},z_{2})\cdot s,s\rangle}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|G(z_{1},z_{2})-G(z_{3},z_{4})\|_{2\to 2}}{2\sqrt{c_{G}}}\|s\|

\displaystyle\leq C\,\max_{i,j}|G_{ij}(z_{1},z_{2})-G_{ij}(z_{3},z_{4})|\,\|s\|,

(22)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2\to 2}
denotes the spectral norm. Here and in the remainder of the proof, write
C
for a positive constant depending only on
K
whose value may change from line to line. Using (
2
), repeated application of Taylor’s theorem and the uniform bounds on the derivatives of
f
gives

\displaystyle|L(u)-\bar{L}(u)|

\displaystyle\leq C\max_{i,j}\sup_{z\in B}|G_{ij}(z,z)-G_{ij}(x^{\prime}+\xi(y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}),x^{\prime})|\,\len(u)

\displaystyle\leq C\|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\|\,\len(u).

Thus, it follows from (
15
) and (
21
) that

|\gamma(x,y)-\bar{\gamma}(x,y)|\leq C\|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}\|^{2}.

(23)

Recall that
\bar{\gamma}(x,y)=\sqrt{2\,\mathrm{dist}(y,H_{K}(x))}
. Thus, by (
20
) and (
23
),

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{\gamma(x,y)^{2}}{2}-\mathrm{dist}(y,H_{K}(x))\Big|

\displaystyle\leq|\gamma(x,y)+\bar{\gamma}(x,y)||\gamma(x,y)-\bar{\gamma}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq C\gamma(x,y)^{3}.

This completes the proof of Lemma
5
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Choose
\varrho
as in Lemma
5
and let
P
be a finite point set on
\partial K
with
\varrho(P)\leq\varrho
. If the inscribed polytope
\conv(P)
has empty interior, the statement is trivial. Thus, assume that
\conv(P)
has nonempty interior. For every facet
F
of
\conv(P)
, the support hyperplane of
K
with the same outward pointing normal vector as
F
is given by
H_{K}(z_{F})
for some
z_{F}\in\partial K
. Let
h_{F}
be the Euclidean distance between the parallel affine hyperplanes
H_{K}(z_{F})
and
\mathrm{aff}F
(affine hull of
F
). The intersection of
\partial K
with the closed half-space bounded by
\mathrm{aff}F
which does not intersect the interior of
\conv(P)
defines a cap

C^{\partial K}(z_{F},h_{F})=\{y\in\partial K\colon\mathrm{dist}(y,H_{K}(z_{F}))\leq h_{F}\}

of
\partial K
with center
z_{F}
and height
h_{F}
. Since
z\mapsto\min_{x\in P}\gamma(z,x)
is continuous on
C^{\partial K}(z_{F},h_{F})
, there is a point
x_{F}\in C^{\partial K}(z_{F},h_{F})
with

\varrho_{F}:=\min_{x\in P}\gamma(x_{F},x)=\max_{z\in C^{\partial K}(z_{F},h_{F})}\min_{x\in P}\gamma(z,x).

Since the caps
\{C^{\partial K}(z_{F},h_{F})\colon F\text{ is a facet of }\conv(P)\}
cover
\partial K
, it holds that

\varrho(P)=\max_{F}\varrho_{F}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta_{H}(\conv(P),K)=\max_{F}h_{F}.

(24)

Let
F
be a facet of
\conv(P)
. The geodesic ball
B_{\gamma}(x_{F},\varrho_{F})\subset\partial K
has maximal radius among all geodesic balls which are centered in
C^{\partial K}(z_{F},h_{F})
and do not contain any point of
P
in their interior. Thus, the ball
B_{\gamma}(z_{F},\varrho_{F})
intersects
P
and in particular
\mathrm{aff}F
. Let
y_{1}\in B_{\gamma}(z_{F},\varrho_{F})\cap\mathrm{aff}F
.
By (
24
),

\gamma(y_{1},z_{F})\leq\varrho_{F}\leq\varrho(P)\leq\varrho

and thus, by Lemma
5
,

h_{F}=\mathrm{dist}(y_{1},H_{K}(z_{F}))\leq\frac{\gamma(y_{1},z_{F})^{2}}{2}+C\gamma(y_{1},z_{F})^{3}\leq\frac{\varrho_{F}^{2}}{2}+C\varrho_{F}^{3}.

(25)

In order to bound
h_{F}
from below in terms of
\varrho_{F}
, consider the cap

C^{\partial K}(x_{F},h_{F})=\{y\in\partial K\colon\mathrm{dist}(y,H_{K}(x_{F}))\leq h_{F}\}

which is cut off by a translated copy of the support hyperplane
H_{K}(x_{F})
of
\partial K
at
x_{F}
.
The cap
C^{\partial K}(x_{F},h_{F})
intersects
P
because
C^{\partial K}(z_{F},h_{F})
has maximal height among all caps which are centered in
C^{\partial K}(z_{F},h_{F})
and do not contain any point of
P
in their interior. Since
B_{\gamma}(x_{F},\varrho_{F})
intersects
P
only in its boundary, it cannot contain
C^{\partial K}(x_{F},h_{F})
in its interior. Thus, we find
y_{2}\in C^{\partial K}(x_{F},h_{F})
with
\gamma(y_{2},x_{F})=\varrho_{F}
. Again by Lemma
5
,

\frac{\varrho_{F}^{2}}{2}-C\varrho_{F}^{3}=\frac{\gamma(y_{2},x_{F})^{2}}{2}-C\gamma(y_{2},x_{F})^{3}\leq\mathrm{dist}(y_{2},H_{K}(x_{F}))\leq h_{F}.

(26)

Combining (
24
), (
25
) and (
26
) completes the proof of Theorem
2
.
∎

We require the following volume asymptotics for small geodesic balls on
\partial K
. In case of infinite smoothness, these are well-known and more precise asymptotics are available, see e.g.
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
or
[
5
, Lem. 2.3]
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}_{+}
. There are
r_{0}>0
and
C>0
such that, for all
x\in\partial K
and
r\leq r_{0}
,

\big|\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}(B_{\gamma}(x,r))-\kappa_{d-1}r^{d-1}\big|\leq Cr^{d}.

Proof.

Let
\varrho\in(0,\frac{\varrho_{0}}{2})
and
B(p_{1},\varrho_{0}),\dots,B(p_{m},\varrho_{0})
be as in (the proof of) Lemma
5
. Let
x\in\partial K
and
r\leq\frac{\varrho}{2}
. Then
B_{\gamma}(x,2r)
belongs to a region
U=U^{(p_{\ell})}
with parametrization
f=f^{(p_{\ell})}
and
\mathrm{proj}(U)=\mathrm{proj}_{p_{\ell}}(U)=B(0,\lambda)
. Let
y\in B_{\gamma}(x,2r)
and set
x^{\prime}=\mathrm{proj}(x)
and
y^{\prime}=\mathrm{proj}(y)
. Define

\bar{\gamma}(x,y)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{C}}\bar{L}(u),\qquad\bar{L}(u):=\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{\langle G(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\cdot u^{\prime}(\tau),u^{\prime}(\tau)\rangle}\mathrm{d}\tau,

(27)

where
\mathcal{C}
and
G
are as in the proof of Lemma
5
(and
\bar{\gamma}
is as if
\xi=0
). It follows from (
2
) that

\bar{\gamma}(x,y)=\sqrt{\langle G(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\cdot(y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}),y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}\rangle}

and similar to (
23
) we have

|\gamma(x,y)-\bar{\gamma}(x,y)|\leq\bar{C}r^{2},

(28)

where
\bar{C}>0
depends only on
K
. Let
r_{0}<\min\{1,\sqrt{C_{G}}\}\frac{\varrho}{2}
be small enough such that
r_{0}+\bar{C}r_{0}^{2}\leq 2r_{0}
. For
s\leq 2r_{0}
, define the ellipsoid (recall that
G(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})
is positive definite)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x^{\prime},s)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{proj}(\{y\in U\colon\bar{\gamma}(x,y)\leq s\})

\displaystyle=\{y^{\prime}\in B(0,\lambda)\colon\langle G(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\cdot(y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}),y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}\rangle\leq s^{2}\},

which is contained in
B(x^{\prime},s/\sqrt{c_{G}})\subset B(0,2\lambda)
. Its volume is

\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{E}(x^{\prime},s))=\frac{\kappa_{d-1}s^{d-1}}{\sqrt{\det q_{x^{\prime}}}}.

Assume that
r<r_{0}
. By (
28
) we have the inclusions

\mathcal{E}(x^{\prime},r-\bar{C}r^{2})\subset\mathrm{proj}(B_{\gamma}(x,r))\subset\mathcal{E}(x^{\prime},r+\bar{C}r^{2}).

(29)

Similar as in the proof of Lemma
5
, by Taylor’s theorem and the bounds on the derivatives of
f
it holds that, for any
u\in B(x^{\prime},2r/\sqrt{c_{G}})
,

\big|\det q_{u}-\det q_{x^{\prime}}\big|\leq\sum_{\sigma}\Big|\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}G_{i,\sigma(i)}(u,u)-\prod_{i=1}^{d-1}G_{i,\sigma(i)}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\Big|\leq Cr

where the sum is over all permutations of
\{1,\dots,d-1\}
and
C>0
only depends on
K
(and may change from line to line in the remainder of the proof). Therefore, by (
29
) and Lemma
3
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}(B_{\gamma}(x,r))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{proj}(B_{\gamma}(x,r))}\sqrt{\det q_{u}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{E}(x^{\prime},r+\bar{C}r^{2})}\sqrt{\det q_{u}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\leq(1+Cr)\sqrt{\det q_{x^{\prime}}}\mathrm{vol}(\mathcal{E}(x^{\prime},r+\bar{C}r^{2}))

\displaystyle\leq(1+Cr)(1+\bar{C}r)^{d-1}\kappa_{d-1}r^{d-1},

which gives

\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}(B_{\gamma}(x,r))-\kappa_{d-1}r^{d-1}\leq Cr^{d}.

The proof of the lower bound is analogous.

Lemma 7
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{K}^{3}_{+}
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independently distributed on
\partial K
with density
h_{\kappa}
. Let
P_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
and set

V(P_{n})=\sup\Big\{\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}(B_{\gamma}(x,r))}{\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}(\partial K)}\colon x\in\partial K,r>0\text{ with }B_{\gamma}(x,r)\cap P_{n}=\emptyset\Big\}.

Then, as
n\to\infty
,

nV(P_{n})-\log n-(d-2)\log\log n-\log\alpha\xrightarrow{\rm d}G,

(30)

where
\alpha
is as in Theorem
1
and
G
is a standard Gumbel distributed random variable.

More precisely, Lemma
7
follows from the theorem and the second remark on p. 276 in
[
16
]
which refers to the argument employed in
[
15
, Section 8]
. Note that the results in
[
15
,
16
]
were also used in
[
30
]
to obtain a limit theorem for the covering radius on the sphere and in
[
10
]
to obtain (
1
).

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
P_{n}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
be as in the statement of the theorem. By Lemma
6
the assumptions of
[
25
, Theorem 2.1]
are fulfilled and the geodesic covering radius satisfies

\varrho(P_{n})=O_{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{d-1}}\Big).

(31)

Here and in the following, we write
X_{n}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(a_{n})
for random variables
X_{n}
and scalars
a_{n}
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\mathbb{P}[X_{n}\geq Ca_{n}]<\varepsilon
for all
n
large enough. It follows from Theorem
2
and (
31
) that

\delta_{H}(K_{n},K)=\frac{\varrho(P_{n})^{2}}{2}+O_{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)^{\frac{3}{d-1}}\Big).

(32)

In the following, we prove a limit theorem for
\varrho(P_{n})^{2}
. By definition, every geodesic ball with radius larger than
\varrho(P_{n})
contains one of the points of
P_{n}
and by compactness there is
x_{0}\in\partial K
such that the ball
B_{\gamma}(x_{0},\varrho(P_{n}))
does not contain any point of
P_{n}
in its interior. Thus, the random variable
V(P_{n})
defined in Lemma
7
is the Riemannian volume of the ball
B_{\gamma}(x_{0},\varrho(P_{n}))
divided by
\mathrm{vol}_{\gamma}(\partial K)=v_{\kappa}(K)
. It follows from Lemma
6
and (
31
) that

V(P_{n})=\frac{\kappa_{d-1}}{v_{\kappa}(K)}\varrho(P_{n})^{d-1}+O_{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)^{\frac{d}{d-1}}\Big).

Plugging this into the limit theorem (
30
) yields

\frac{\kappa_{d-1}}{v_{\kappa}(K)}n\varrho(P_{n})^{d-1}-\log n-(d-2)\log\log n-\log\alpha\xrightarrow{\rm d}G.

(33)

Applying the uniform Delta Method
[
33
, Theorem 3.8]
to the limit theorem in (
33
) and the function
x\mapsto\frac{d-1}{2}x^{\frac{2}{d-1}}
gives

(d-1)\log n\Big[\Big(\frac{\kappa_{d-1}}{v_{\kappa}(K)}\frac{n}{\log n}\Big)^{\frac{2}{d-1}}\frac{\varrho(P_{n})^{2}}{2}-\frac{\theta_{n}}{2}\Big]\xrightarrow{\rm d}G,

(34)

where

\displaystyle\theta_{n}

\displaystyle=\Big(1+\frac{(d-2)\log\log n+\log\alpha}{\log n}\Big)^{\frac{2}{d-1}}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2}{d-1}\frac{(d-2)\log\log n+\log\alpha}{\log n}+O\Big(\Big(\frac{\log\log n}{\log n}\Big)^{2}\Big).

Combining the limit theorem in (
34
) with the asymptotics in (
32
) completes the proof of Theorem
1
.
∎
