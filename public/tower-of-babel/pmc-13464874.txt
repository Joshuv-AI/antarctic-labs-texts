Predictive validity of peer evaluation in problem-based learning: an exploration in medical education
Authors: Hyerang Park, Jidong Sung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn problem-based learning (PBL), peer evaluation is commonly used alongside tutor evaluation to assess students’ collaborative learning processes and competencies related to clinical reasoning. However, limited evidence exists regarding the predictive validity of peer evaluation in medical education. This study examined whether peer evaluation scores in PBL were predictive of subsequent clinical practice performance and written Korean Medical Licensing Examination (KMLE) scores.MethodsCorrelation analyses were conducted to assess the predictive validity of peer evaluations, along with tutor evaluations and PBL written comprehensive exam scores, in relation to clinical practice course scores and written KMLE scores.ResultsAmong the domains of clinical practice courses, the skills and the attitudes domains showed the highest correlation with total peer evaluation scores, whereas the knowledge domain showed the highest correlation with comprehensive exam scores. Domains of peer evaluation had generally strong correlations with the skills domain of clinical practice courses. Peer evaluation demonstrated lower predictive validity for the written KMLE scores compared to the comprehensive exams.ConclusionsThe findings suggest that peer evaluation in PBL is associated with subsequent clinical practice performance, particularly in skill- and attitude-related domains. Peer evaluation may provide educational value distinct from tutor evaluations and written examinations by reflecting competencies relevant to collaborative learning and clinical practice. Based on these findings, further research is needed to explore the mechanisms underlying peer evaluation and to optimize its implementation in PBL environments.Keywords: Peer evaluation, Predictive validity, Medical education, Problem-based learning

Introduction

Problem-Based Learning (PBL) is a learning approach, typically conducted in small group settings, that is designed to help students integrate, apply, and reinforce newly acquired knowledge within the context of patient problems [1].

PBL develops critical thinking, complex problem-solving, collaboration, effective communication, and the application of knowledge for lifelong learning [2, 3]. It is a learner-centered approach, both in instruction and curriculum, that encourages students to research, merge theory with practice, and apply their knowledge and skills to find effective solutions to given problems [3]. In medical education, PBL focuses less on the quantity of knowledge gained and more on how effectively students can access and apply this knowledge to real-world scenarios.

Declarative knowledge encompasses factual and event-based understanding of what something is, while procedural knowledge pertains to knowledge of how to perform actions or tasks [4, 5]. In medical education, PBL fundamentally differs from conventional curricula by integrating basic and clinical sciences throughout the educational process and promoting learning through engagement with clinical problems [6]. Accordingly, higher levels of performance and competency may be achieved when basic science and declarative knowledge are converted into practical skills or demonstrations [7].

In learner-centered environments like PBL, where active participation and self-directed learning are emphasized [8], assessments that reflect these processes are incorporated into evaluations. The focus on student performance in PBL is primarily on their clinical reasoning process and associated skills, rather than on the content they have memorized or can recall [9]. Therefore, rather than assessing mere mastery of content knowledge, the process skills of PBL, such as problem identification, problem-solving, and application of knowledge, should be assessed [10].

Tutors, students, and problems represent the core components of a PBL curriculum [11, 12]. Unlike traditional teaching, where the instructor is the sole evaluator, PBL encourages diverse perspectives by having students, peers, and instructors all participate in assessments [13]. Both tutor and peer evaluations are frequently employed as essential methods to assess students’ performance in small group discussions, which are central to the problem-solving process in PBL tutorials. It is crucial to examine whether these evaluation methods accurately measure the intended PBL approach.

The reliability and validity of peer assessment have been questioned due to concerns regarding students’ ability to consistently and fairly evaluate their peers [14–16], the lack of expertise in evaluation criteria [16], and lower accuracy compared to assessment by tutors [13]. Additionally, the halo effect—where pre-existing impressions of the person being assessed influence the evaluator’s judgment—has been noted as a challenge to objectivity [17]. Many studies highlight students’ negative perceptions of peer assessment, frequently citing issues such as favoritism or friendship-marking [14, 18], poor evaluation performance among peers [19], and lack of trust [20]. Furthermore, students are often reluctant to report their peers’ unprofessional behavior to avoid jeopardizing personal relationships [21, 22].

Given these challenges, the possible need to re-evaluate the role of peer assessments in determining final grades in PBL courses has been noted [23]. Some universities do not implement peer evaluations in PBL [13, 24], whereas others use a rigorous norm-referenced evaluation method in which students rank all group members [25].

However, students can observe their peers throughout the learning process, acquiring a more comprehensive understanding of their peers’ work than teachers do [26, 27]. Furthermore, it was reported that students feel a greater sense of responsibility and believe that their learning improved when they were engaged in evaluation of their peers [28, 29]. This process thereby helps peers become more effective group members by providing motivation for them to contribute their fair share and improving the team’s ability to collaborate effectively [30]. It also enhances students’ professional behavior, with a particular emphasis on interpersonal skills [22, 31].

Furthermore, peer feedback was reported by students to be more meaningful than the feedback provided by faculty [32]. Peer assessment not only enables medical students to provide constructive feedback regarding knowledge, clinical skills, and professionalism [22], but also encourages them to take greater responsibility for their own learning [18]. Given that multisource feedback has been recognized as an effective method [33, 34], feedback from peers, in addition to the assessment provided by tutors who are traditionally seen as the primary evaluators, is expected to be beneficial in assessing medical students’ overall performance.

This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of peer evaluation by analyzing its predictive validity. Predictive validity refers to the extent to which test scores are associated with criterion measures obtained in the future [35], and it is particularly important because it evaluates how well the assessed competencies can be applied in real-life situations [9]. In medical education, predictive validity is especially critical, as educational assessments are expected to correspond with learners’ future academic achievement and clinical competence. Currently, there is limited research on the predictive validity of peer evaluation in medical education. Therefore, this study examined whether PBL peer evaluation scores were predictive of subsequent clinical practice performance and/or written Korean Medical Licensing Examination (KMLE) scores. In addition, the predictive validity of peer evaluation was compared with that of tutor evaluations and PBL written comprehensive examinations to explore the distinct educational value of peer evaluation within the PBL curriculum.

Methods

Data

Sungkyunkwan University School of Medicine incorporated PBL into its curriculum in the early stages of its establishment. The PBL curriculum was delivered to fourth-year medical students between 2015 and 2019. This study draws on data from 197 students who participated in PBL courses during that period, including 44 students in 2015, 38 in 2016, 37 in 2017, 38 in 2018, and 40 in 2019. In addition to their PBL performance scores, data from their fifth and sixth years — specifically, clinical practice course scores and written KMLE scores — were also included in the analysis. The PBL scores, along with the clinical practice course scores from the fifth and sixth years, were assigned by instructors during routine coursework evaluations to assess student performance. Students who had multiple scores for the same PBL or clinical practice courses due to retakes were excluded from the analysis. The written KMLE is administered once a year, and students who fail the exam on their first attempt can retake it the following year. For students with multiple written KMLE scores, only scores from their first sitting were considered. This study was a retrospective analysis of the data previously collected and provided by the administration office of Sungkyunkwan University School of Medicine, including PBL performance scores, clinical practice course scores, and written KMLE scores. As this study used existing data collected by the administration office, it was deemed exempt from review by the Sungkyunkwan University Institutional Review Board.

Tutorials are a core component of the PBL curriculum and are typically conducted in small groups of 5–7 students. During two tutorial sessions, students collaboratively work through a clinical case. In the first tutorial, students identify the clinical problem and assign learning issues for self-directed study. Between tutorials, students independently study their assigned topics using various learning resources. In the second tutorial, students share and integrate their self-directed learning and apply the acquired knowledge to reanalyze and resolve the problem.

The PBL curriculum was organized into 11 units, each based on a different human organ system. Each unit was conducted over a period of 3–4 weeks. In the fourth-year curriculum, the first semester covered Units 1 through 6, while the second semester included Units 7 through 11. Across these 11 units, a total of 15 courses were offered. Unit 1 covered Pulmonary Medicine, Unit 2 Cardiology, Unit 3 Nephrology, and Unit 4 Endocrinology and Metabolism. Unit 5 included Infectious Diseases and Hematology/Oncology, and Unit 6 focused on Gastroenterology. In the second semester, Unit 7 encompassed Rheumatology, Allergology, Musculoskeletal System, and Dermatology. Unit 8 addressed Neuro-ophthalmology/Neuro-otology, Unit 9 Psychiatry, Unit 10 Reproduction-Development-Geriatric Medicine A, and Unit 11 Reproduction-Development-Geriatric Medicine B.

This study analyzed scores from courses related to fields such as internal medicine, surgery, pediatrics, and obstetrics and gynecology, which are frequently regarded as core clinical specialties in Korea [36–39]. Accordingly, 13 of the 15 PBL courses related to these specialties were included in the analysis; psychiatry (Unit 9) and dermatology (Unit 7) were excluded because they were not related to these core clinical specialties. The analysis utilized results from peer evaluations as well as tutor evaluations and written comprehensive exams conducted within the PBL curriculum. Within the PBL curriculum, peer and tutor evaluations function as behavioral assessments of tutorial activities. The peer and tutor evaluation frameworks were developed and implemented as part of the institutional PBL curriculum at Sungkyunkwan University School of Medicine. Peer evaluations consisted of mutual assessments conducted among students within the same tutorial group and were completed at the end of each PBL unit. Students were asked to assess peers based on observed tutorial performance across the five evaluation domains. Tutors received institutional training regarding tutorial facilitation and evaluation methods through tutor workshops and seminars and assessed and documented student performance during tutorial sessions.

The evaluation domains reflected key competencies emphasized in the PBL tutorial process. Participation assessed whether students actively engaged in small-group discussion activities, while communication evaluated students’ ability to listen to others, express their opinions logically, and make efforts to resolve communication difficulties during group interactions. Logical thinking referred to students’ ability to interpret given information based on prior knowledge and experience and to formulate and verify logical hypotheses. Diligence in learning reflected whether students sufficiently studied materials necessary for problem-solving using appropriate references. Group contribution assessed students’ collaborative attitudes and active contributions to group learning. Peer evaluations consisted of mutual assessments among group members based on five domains: participation, communication, diligence in learning, group contribution, and logical thinking. Peer evaluations comprised four domains until 2016; however, in 2017, logical thinking was added as the fifth domain. Sungkyunkwan University previously included peer evaluation scores in PBL course grades. However, owing to concerns regarding the reliability and validity of these scores, the university awarded points solely based on the submission of peer evaluation forms rather than on the evaluation scores themselves. In this study, the predictive validity analysis of peer evaluations utilized raw scores recorded by group members, rather than submission points awarded by the university for grading purposes. Tutor evaluations reflected tutors’ assessments of student activities across four domains: participation, communication, diligence in learning, and logical thinking.

Peer and tutor evaluations were conducted using structured evaluation forms. The core evaluation domains were consistently maintained throughout the study period, although some differences existed in specific evaluation items across evaluation systems and years. Tutor evaluations were conducted during tutorial sessions, whereas peer evaluations were completed at the end of each PBL unit. Because the duration of courses and the number of cases varied across courses, this study analyzed average domain scores aggregated across cases by the administrative office for grading purposes. Depending on the evaluation domain and year, scores were assigned using 3- or 4-point rating scales and were converted to a common 4-point scale prior to analysis. The peer and tutor evaluation forms were developed and reviewed by faculty members involved in the institutional PBL curriculum and were intended to reflect key competencies emphasized in PBL tutorials, thereby supporting the content relevance of the evaluation domains within the PBL context.

The clinical practice curriculum was administered to medical students in their fifth and sixth years. The clinical practice course evaluations were divided into three domains: knowledge, skills, and attitudes. Knowledge was primarily assessed using written tests and quizzes; skills were evaluated using Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs), Clinical Performance Examinations (CPXs), case-based discussions, and bedside teaching; attitudes were measured based on attendance and practice behaviors. This study analyzed scores from five clinical practice courses associated with the aforementioned core clinical specialties: Internal Medicine Clerkship, Surgery Clerkship 1, Obstetrics and Gynecology Clerkship, Pediatrics Clerkship, and Surgery Clerkship 2.

The KMLE comprises a practical and a written examination, and a medical license is granted only upon passing both. In this study, students’ written KMLE t-scores were obtained from the administration office of Sungkyunkwan University School of Medicine. As practical exam scores were unavailable in the data collected by the administration office, only written exam scores were used for the analysis. Medical students took the written portion of the KMLE in the month of January of their sixth year, just before medical school graduation. School years end in February in Korea.

Research content

For the analysis of predictive validity, the following aspects were examined:

The predictive validity of peer evaluations for clinical practice course performance compared to that of tutor evaluations and PBL written comprehensive exams.

The predictive validity of peer evaluations for clinical practice course performance compared to that of tutor evaluations and PBL written comprehensive exams.

The predictive validity of peer evaluations for written KMLE scores compared to that of tutor evaluations and PBL written comprehensive exams.

The predictive validity of peer evaluations for written KMLE scores compared to that of tutor evaluations and PBL written comprehensive exams.

Analysis methods

Correlation analyses were used to examine the predictive validity of peer evaluations in relation to future performance criteria. These criteria included performance in the knowledge, skills, and attitudes domains of clinical practice courses, as well as written KMLE scores. Additionally, for comparison, correlation analyses were conducted to assess the predictive validity of tutor evaluations and PBL written comprehensive exams in relation to the same criteria.

Discussion

This study investigated the predictive validity of peer evaluations in comparison with tutor evaluations and written comprehensive exams. The findings revealed that, among the domains of clinical practice courses, the skills and the attitudes domains showed the highest correlations with peer evaluations, whereas the knowledge domain and written KMLE scores showed the highest correlations with comprehensive exams. Notably, peer evaluation domains demonstrated stronger correlations with overall clinical practice domains than tutor evaluation domains, challenging the conventional view that tutors are better suited to assess students’ abilities.

This aligns with findings from studies that emphasize the value of peer assessment in evaluating clinical competencies. For example, Marty et al. [40] demonstrated that athletic training students were highly accurate in assessing their peers’ psychomotor skills, and Burnett and Cavaye [41] found that peer assessment among fifth-year surgery students contributed to setting educational goals that were realistically aligned with medical practice.

Peer assessment aligns well with active and self-regulated learning, as it not only involves students more deeply in the learning process, but also promotes the development of key behavioral, cognitive, and metacognitive skills—many of which are essential in professional settings [14]. By encouraging students to take an active role both as learners and as evaluators, peer assessment drives more meaningful participation in tutorial activities, strengthening future professional behavior [26, 31] while improving students’ ability to tackle real-world challenges effectively.

Despite various concerns and limitations, peer evaluations remain a valuable assessment method with significant benefits, highlighting the need to develop ways to effectively implement them, while addressing and overcoming known challenges. Concerns about students’ discomfort in evaluating their peers can be mitigated by ensuring anonymity in the evaluation process. According to Arnold et al. [21], anonymity is a crucial method for fostering genuine participation in peer assessment, as anonymous evaluators do not have to face their peers directly, allowing them to avoid uncomfortable situations and promoting more honest and candid evaluations without damaging teamwork or interpersonal relationships. Anonymity encourages students to provide more candid and objective feedback, alleviating the discomfort associated with peer assessment.

To further address concerns about the accuracy and fairness of peer evaluations, it is essential to provide students with adequate training. While experience and training in assessment skills are important, many students are unfamiliar with the procedures and techniques needed to be effective assessors [16]. This lack of familiarity can lead to inconsistencies in evaluations. However, with adequate experience and guidance, students can make meaningful assessments [14, 42]. Therefore, by offering students sufficient training on the evaluation criteria and content, peer evaluations can become an effective tool for assessing clinical performance competencies.

The results of this study revealed that peer evaluations are better suited than tutor evaluations for predicting skills and attitudes related to clinical practice competencies. PBL promotes the development of essential skills in patient problem-solving [9], so it is expected that learning outcomes from PBL are closely related to the competencies assessed in clinical practice courses. In this study, tutor evaluations showed lower predictive validity for the three domains in clinical practice courses compared to peer evaluations, and even lower compared to comprehensive exams. Tutors, who are traditionally tasked with evaluative roles, may face limitations in providing a comprehensive assessment of tutorial activities, suggesting that their ability to holistically evaluate these broader competencies may be restricted.

The amount of medical knowledge that medical students are expected to learn is considerable, and this has led to a focus on memorization-based learning and education. However, as it becomes increasingly challenging to cover the growing body of medical knowledge within the timeframe of medical school, students’ self-directed learning abilities are becoming correspondingly important. In response, medical education has seen a shift toward learner-centered approaches, including PBL, team-based learning, and flipped classroom methods. To align with these evolving educational methods, instructors must acquire evaluation techniques suited to a range of teaching and learning styles, enabling them to more accurately assess students’ multidimensional competencies. Providing tutors with clear guidance on the distinctions and criteria for assessing knowledge-based evaluations versus tutorial activities, as well as with opportunities for practical exercises, such as mock assessments, could be highly beneficial.

Additionally, the study observed that the correlations between peer evaluations and both the knowledge domain of clinical practice courses and the written KMLE scores were lower than the correlations between comprehensive exams and both the knowledge domain of clinical practice courses and the written KMLE scores. This discrepancy is likely due to peer evaluations not being designed to primarily measure knowledge-based academic performance, resulting in lower predictive validity for such assessments. This finding is meaningful, as it suggests that peer evaluations are capturing different dimensions of student competencies—such as skills and attitudes—rather than just cognitive knowledge. This highlights the complementary value of peer assessments in evaluating competencies that are crucial for clinical practice, yet distinct from those measured by traditional written exams.

Conclusions

The purpose of this study was to analyze the predictive validity of peer evaluation carried out in PBL within medical education. This study demonstrated that peer evaluation scores are strongly associated with skills and attitudes relevant to clinical practice. Based on these findings, peer evaluations can serve as a crucial tool for students to effectively develop core skills related to clinical practice throughout the learning process. Therefore, greater efforts are needed to integrate and utilize peer assessment more effectively in PBL environments. However, this study has several limitations. First, this study primarily focused on the predictive validity of peer evaluation outcomes and did not investigate the underlying mechanisms through which peer evaluation influences collaborative learning and subsequent clinical performance. Second, the study analyzed written KMLE scores but did not include practical examination outcomes, which may limit the interpretation of clinical competency assessment. Future studies should explore these processes using qualitative and process-oriented approaches, such as interviews, observational data, or behavioral coding analyses. In addition, further studies incorporating KMLE practical examination scores are needed to more comprehensively examine the relationship between peer evaluation and actual clinical performance. Future research should also focus on optimizing peer evaluation to fully harness its potential in promoting professional growth and collaborative learning through PBL.
