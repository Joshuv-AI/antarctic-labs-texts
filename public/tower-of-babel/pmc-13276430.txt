Health education needs of stroke patients and caregivers: A qualitative meta-synthesis
Authors: Hong Luo, Jia He, Ping Yang, Yuying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe current health education for stroke patients and their families often fails to take into account the systematic differences in their needs, and most of the approaches are based on the single perspective of either the patients or the caregivers, lacking a systematic integration from a dual perspective.ObjectiveThis study integrates the health education needs of stroke patients and caregivers, to inform the development of tailored health education programs for both stroke patients and their caregivers.Information sourcesA systematic search was conducted across Chinese and English databases including CNKI, Wanfang Database, VIP Chinese Science and Technology Journal Database, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL for qualitative research papers addressing health education needs among stroke patients or caregivers. Background

Stroke, an acute cerebrovascular disease, has become the second leading cause of death globally and the primary cause of adult mortality and disability in China (Ji, 2025; Feigin and Owolabi, 2023). Following a first stroke, approximately 70%−80% of patients lose the ability to live independently, and over 90% transition to community-or home-based rehabilitation after the acute phase, requiring caregivers to provide physical, psychological, and social support (Lu et al., 2024). This situation imposes substantial caregiving and economic burdens on families. With the prevalence of unhealthy lifestyles and insufficient awareness of health management, stroke is increasingly affecting younger populations and recurrence rates are rising (Feigin and Owolabi, 2023). The recurrence rate within one year is 17%, escalating to 41% within five years (Chen et al., 2020), indicating a growing disease burden.

Health education aims to empower individuals to better manage and improve their health through information provision (Levine-Obe and Stillman-Lowe, 2024). Stroke progression is a dynamic and continuous process, and patients have diverse health education needs at different disease stages and time points (Cao et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2024). Providing tailored health education can effectively address modifiable risk factors and reduce recurrence (Deng et al., 2025). Currently, family caregiving remains the predominant care model (Schulz et al., 2020; AARP and NAC, 2020). As essential supporters in patient rehabilitation, caregivers’ health education needs must also be addressed.

Existing reviews have largely focused on either patients or caregivers separately, without directly comparing the two groups across the full disease trajectory. Although several qualitative studies have explored the health education needs of stroke patients or caregivers separately, and a recent meta‑synthesis by Bartoli et al. (2025) examined transitional care needs from the triadic perspectives of healthcare professionals, caregivers, and patients during the hospital‑to‑home transition period, no prior synthesis has: (1) integrated the dual perspectives of patients and caregivers simultaneously across the entire disease trajectory (acute, recovery, and long‑term home care); (2) systematically compared differences in health education needs between these two groups; or (3) proposed a stage‑specific, collaborative health education pathway based on such a comparison. Therefore, the incremental contributions of this study are threefold: it provides a comprehensive integration of health education needs from both patients and caregivers, reveals how these needs differ between the two groups across the full disease course, and offers a practical framework for designing tailored, phase‑appropriate interventions. To address these gaps, this study employs a meta‑synthesis approach to integrate health education needs from both patients and caregivers, aiming to provide a scientific basis for developing tailored, phase‑appropriate health education interventions.

Methods

We conducted a qualitative meta-synthesis according to PRISMA and ENTREQ reporting guidelines (Page et al., 2021; Tong et al., 2012), and was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261293700).

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria require qualitative studies to meet the following conditions: (1) Participant: Stroke patients and caregivers; (2) Interest of phenomena: The health education needs of stroke patients and caregivers; (3) Context, C: The entire process of stroke onset and care; (4) Study design: Qualitative research, including descriptive research, phenomenology, grounded theory, and ethnography, etc. Exclusion criteria are: (1) Repeated publication or incomplete data; (2) Unable to obtain the full text.

Information sources and search strategy

Two researchers independently conducted the literature search across eight databases:China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Database, VIP Database, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL. The search period spanned from the inception of each database to September 30, 2025. Taking the PubMed database as an example, the search strategy is provided in Table 1. For Chinese databases (CNKI, Wanfang, VIP), the search terms were:“脑卒中” OR “中风” OR “脑梗死” OR “脑出血” OR “脑血管意外” OR “卒中” OR “脑栓塞” OR “脑血管疾病” (for stroke); “照顾者” OR “照护者” OR “家庭成员” OR “亲属” OR “配偶” OR “父母” OR “子女” (for caregivers); “教育” OR “健康教育” OR “健康需求” OR “疾病需求” OR “健康素养” (for health education); and “质性” OR “扎根” OR “访谈” OR “民族志” OR “现象学” OR “体验” (for qualitative research). These terms were combined using Boolean operators in the same logic as the PubMed search shown in Table 1.

Study selection and data extraction

Two researchers who have received evidence‑based training were responsible for screening the included literature and extracting the data. Any disagreements between the two researchers during the screening or data extraction process were first discussed between them. If consensus could not be reached, a third researcher was consulted to review the discrepancy and facilitate discussion until a final decision was made. The data extracted included the author, year, country, research subjects, research methods, the phenomenon of interest, and the main results.

Appraisal of methodological quality

Two researchers independently evaluated the literature based on the qualitative research quality assessment criteria formulated by the JBI Center for Evidence-Based Health Care (Joanna Briggs Institute 2025). The evaluation content included 10 items, each of which was evaluated with “yes”, “no”, or “unclear”. Complete satisfaction was rated as grade A, partial satisfaction as grade B, and complete non-satisfaction as grade C. In case of disagreement in the evaluation results, the third researcher would participate in the discussion and make a decision. Finally, the literature with quality assessment grades of A and B were included.

Data synthesis

The meta-themes were determined using Thomas and Harden’s thematic synthesis method, which includes three stages: i) line-by-line coding of relevant texts; ii) organisation of codes into descriptive themes; and iii) development of analytical themes (Thomas and Harden, 2008). Two researchers independently read the literature, summarizing and extracting meaningful concepts and themes, and achieving a relative consensus between them. In case of disagreement, the third researcher would discuss and make a decision. Microsoft Excel was used to organise data during analysis.

Ethical consideration

As this study is a review study, as a meta-synthesis, human participants were not included. Therefore, an ethical committee review was not required.

Results

A total of 2964 documents were retrieved. After preliminary screening and elimination of duplicate documents, 2020 remained. After reading the titles and abstracts, 45 documents were deleted as they did not match the topic. After further reading the full texts and re-screening, 11 documents were finally included (Cao et al., 2025; Zhao and Xue, 2011; Ang et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2015; Jiang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2021; Gong et al., 2022; Finch et al., 2022; Merati-Fashi et al., 2022; Jiao et al., 2024; Ottonello et al., 2025). Data synthesis

After repeatedly reading, critically appraising, and comparing the 11 included studies, the researchers extracted 38 them, then grouped similar findings into 11 categories, and finally synthesized finding into three integrated results: (1)disease knowledge needs; (2)psychological and social support needs, and (3)resource support needs.The complete third‑order synthesis results, including original study quotes mapped to categories and themes, are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Synthesis finding 1: disease knowledge needs

Category 1:basic knowledge needs

After the onset of a stroke, both patients and their caregivers generally exhibit a strong demand for knowledge about the disease, and this demand persists throughout different stages of the disease. During the acute phase, the demand initially manifests as urgent inquiries about basic information about the disease and its prognosis (Ottonello et al., 2025), including confusion about the cause of the stroke (“I really can't specify the cause of the stroke” (Zhang et al., 2021)), risk factors (“But I'm not sure about the risk factors of stroke” (Ang et al., 2013)), and deep concerns about the progression of the disease and the outcome of rehabilitation (“Can he recover like this? What will happen in the future? How long will it take to recover?” (Chen et al., 2015)). As the condition enters the stable phase, the knowledge demand shifts to long-term management and prevention of the disease, such as the identification and handling of early symptoms (“One wouldn't notice cognitive problems at all. If it could have been detected earlier, the recovery would have been better now” (Jiao et al., 2024)) and specific measures for preventing recurrence (“What should one pay attention to avoid another attack?” (Cao et al., 2025)). The persistent desire for knowledge (“I still need to learn more information” (Ang et al., 2013)) highlights their urgent need to obtain systematic and accurate guidance on disease knowledge, in order to be fully prepared for the entire course of disease care.

Category 2: medication management needs

Medication management is a critical component in improving stroke patient outcomes and reducing the occurrence and recurrence of complications, with patients and their caregivers demonstrating a high level of demand for this support. In actual care practices, widespread knowledge gaps and implementation deviations are evident. These manifest as indiscriminate medication practices (“I read the instructions myself and have him take them together-sometimes dozens of pills at once” (Zhao and Xue, 2011)) and inadequate supervision of patient medication adherence (“He knows he has high blood pressure, but he doesn't take his medicine and never checks his blood pressure... Since he seems fine, I just let him be” (Jiang et al., 2017)). This situation highlights their urgent need for systematic, standardized medication guidance. This includes deeper understanding of specific drug functions (“What exactly do these daily medications do?” (Cao et al., 2025), “Is there any medication that can treat this?” (Jiao et al., 2024)), side effects (“I'm more concerned about the side effects of the drugs” (Jiao et al., 2024)), and particularly medications to improve cognitive function (“What kind of medication can address his confusion?” (Cao et al., 2025)). Additionally, they urgently require knowledge of proper medication administration, especially regarding interactions and safety when taking multiple drugs concurrently (“No one told me if these medications can be taken together” (Jiao et al., 2024)). This knowledge is essential for enhancing medication adherence and safety, thereby ensuring treatment efficacy.

Category 3: rehabilitation knowledge needs

Following a stroke, patients often experience varying degrees of functional impairment, creating an urgent need for rehabilitation knowledge among both patients and their caregivers. Rehabilitation is a long-term and specialized process. Patients lack understanding of professional rehabilitation techniques (“What is speech therapy? How is it done?” (Chen et al., 2015)), often engage in rehabilitation activities with considerable uncertainty (“Just exercise however you feel like it” (Zhao and Xue, 2011)), and lack professional rehabilitation guidance (“I wish a specialist doctor could provide some guidance” (Jiang et al., 2017)). While caregivers generally recognize the necessity of rehabilitation, they lack scientific understanding of training intensity and duration (“I kept going as long as he didn't seem tired, but then his knee swelled up” (Zhao and Xue, 2011)). Patients and caregivers seek specific methods for rehabilitation training (“How can we help the patient recover quickly?” (Cao et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2017)), guidance on exercise intensity and frequency (“What specific exercises should be done? How long should each session last?” (Cao et al., 2025)) and the evaluation and adjustment of rehabilitation outcomes (“I want to understand the current status of rehabilitation treatment” (Cao et al., 2025)). Healthcare providers should offer personalized rehabilitation plans based on the patient's condition “The exercises we did before were like kids' games-what good are they? It felt humiliating” (Jiao et al., 2024)). Through face-to-face demonstrations and ongoing corrections (“One-on-one demonstrations would be even better” (Cao et al., 2025)), they should ensure the accuracy and safety of rehabilitation movements to prevent secondary injuries caused by improper training.

Category 4: death education needs

Stroke patients and their caregivers exhibit significant demand for death education. Due to the disease's high disability rate and sudden onset, patients often confront fear and uncertainty regarding life's end. This includes rational understanding of death itself (“guiding them to actively and correctly recognize and approach death” (Gong et al., 2022)) and advance planning for post-mortem affairs (“preparing a will in advance, stating that treatment should cease if I become mentally incompetent or paralyzed” (Jiao et al., 2024)). Some patients exhibit intense negative emotions due to critical illness or pessimistic temperament (“tend to fear death, often becoming irritable and overly anxious” (Gong et al., 2022)), while others seek autonomy in determining end-of-life medical interventions to avoid unnecessary suffering and overtreatment (“Just don't treat me anymore, that's fine” (Jiao et al., 2024)).

Synthesis finding 2: psychosocial support needs

Category 1: psychological support needs

Psychological support is crucial for stroke patients and their caregivers to cope with the impact of the disease and maintain confidence in treatment. They commonly endure immense psychological pressure during the caregiving process. For patients, this primarily manifests as profound anxiety about prognosis and self-worth denial, encompassing fear of physical functional loss (“I constantly worry about being bedridden, unable to move or eat” (Jiao et al., 2024)), shame stemming from dependency (“I don't want others to know my condition; it hurts my pride” (Jiao et al., 2024)), and social withdrawal (“I don't want to talk much” (Jiao et al., 2024)). Caregivers, meanwhile, endure prolonged physical and mental exhaustion. They must manage patients' negative emotions (“He's very depressed. Every time we talk to him, we try to encourage him” (Ang et al., 2013)) while suppressing their own suffering (“I dare not cry in front of her; I just endure it” (Zhang et al., 2021)). They feel trapped in a cycle of unspoken distress (“My heart feels so heavy, yet I have no one to talk to” (Zhang et al., 2021)) and face crushing pressures from family roles and responsibilities (“Not being able to work, over time my husband inevitably complains” (Zhang et al., 2021)). They urgently require professional, timely psychological support to manage these challenges (Zhang et al., 2021), and bear the heavy burden of family roles and responsibilities (“I can't go out to work, and over time, my husband inevitably complains” (Zhang et al., 2021)). They urgently need access to professional, timely psychological counseling to manage negative emotions arising during the caregiving process (Ottonello et al., 2025) and find appropriate outlets for their feelings (“As long as it solves the psychological issues, taking medication or seeing a doctor is fine” (Jiao et al., 2024)).

Category 2: role adaptation needs

Role adaptation represents a significant life challenge faced jointly by stroke patients and caregivers following the onset of illness. This transformative process is accompanied by profound internal conflict and practical pressures. For caregivers, the core need lies in the role reconstruction from family member to primary caregiver. They must assume professional caregiving responsibilities (“She can't be left alone” (Zhang et al., 2021)) and may even sacrifice personal career development (“I might have to quit my job” (Zhang et al., 2021)), struggling to balance caregiving duties with maintaining their own social identity. For patients, adaptation demands stem from shifting from independent individuals to partially dependent persons. They yearn to preserve their pre-illness self-image and dignity (“He's very proud; I dare not let relatives or friends visit him, lest they say he can't take care of himself” (Zhang et al., 2021)), yet must confront the practical hardships brought by physical impairments (“My father used to be quite healthy, but now he sighs every day” (Zhang et al., 2021)), caught in an internal conflict between preserving self-respect and acknowledging dependency needs. This abrupt shift in dual roles plunges both parties into a sense of helplessness, as the family feels “completely out of control” (Ang et al., 2013).

Category 3: family support needs

Family support is the cornerstone for maintaining a stable care system for stroke patients. However, under the pressure of long-term caregiving, internal family support resources often become depleted. For primary caregivers, their needs are directly reflected in an urgent demand for human support. They feel physically and mentally exhausted from bearing the heavy burden of care alone over extended periods (“Caring for him these past few days has been exhausting; I feel like I'm about to get sick myself” (Chen et al., 2015)). They urgently need other family members to provide tangible respite through concrete actions such as rotating caregiving duties (“My sisters and I take turns caring for him” (Chen et al., 2015)) or sharing household responsibilities. For patients, family support manifests more as emotional dependence and companionship needs. Under the blow of illness, they crave family care and protection (“My spouse doesn't ask for much when sick, just that I stay by his side” (Merati-Fashi et al., 2022)), with the mere presence of family members serving as vital psychological comfort. However, in reality, many families struggle to provide sustained, effective support due to members working, living apart, or financial constraints (“Everyone else has to work and doesn't have time” (Chen et al., 2015), “We can only hire a caregiver, but it costs 140 yuan a day-too expensive” (Chen et al., 2015)).

Synthesis finding 3: resource support needs

Category 1: community resource utilization needs

Community resource utilization serves as a vital pillar of the post-discharge care system for stroke patients. However, a significant gap currently exists between available community support and family needs. Stroke patients and their caregivers have limited awareness of community-based services (“I don't know if the community offers rehabilitation” (Chen et al., 2015)). Regarding service accessibility, needs are primarily constrained by dual geographical and economic barriers. In rural areas, scarce medical resources are the foremost challenge (“There are only small clinics nearby, and the health center is quite far away. Going to the hospital is very inconvenient” (Chen et al., 2015)). While urban families enjoy geographical advantages, they face heavy financial burdens due to limited medical insurance coverage (“Even 60,000 yuan isn't covered by medical insurance; we ended up paying 80% out-of-pocket” (Zhao and Xue, 2011)). Regarding service content, demand manifests as an urgent need for specialized rehabilitation and home-based services. Family members generally lack awareness of community health service centers' functions (“I don't fully understand the role of community health services, so utilization is low” (Chen et al., 2015)), yet strongly desire communities to provide professional rehabilitation guidance (“I hope community doctors and nurses can come to my home for acupuncture treatment” (Chen et al., 2015)) and daily care assistance (“Someone to come to my home to clean... . help take care of him (Zhao and Xue, 2011), Find someone in the community to come to the home to care for the patient (Finch et al., 2022)”).

Category 2: post-discharge continuity of care needs

Post-discharge continuity of care is a critical component in ensuring a smooth transition for stroke patients from hospital to home. After discharge, stroke patients and their families express significant anxiety about potential changes in the patient's condition (“Will they get sick again at home?” (Chen et al., 2015)). Regarding professional nursing support, the primary need for continued care manifests as significant anxiety about symptom recognition and emergency management. Caregivers worry about disease progression due to a lack of professional judgment (“If something happens at home, how can we tell what the problem is?” (Cao et al., 2025)), feeling particularly helpless about handling sudden crises and alleviating persistent symptoms (“How should we manage her headaches after she goes home?” (Cao et al., 2025)). They urgently seek targeted dietary management (“For patients with high blood sugar like hers, what should she eat after returning home?” (Cao et al., 2025)) and relapse prevention measures (“What precautions should we take to avoid another episode?” (Cao et al., 2025)).

Category 3: daily living care needs

Daily living care for stroke patients is an urgent requirement throughout the entire disease course, as the condition impairs their ability to perform many activities of daily living. During the acute phase and home care, needs primarily manifest at the basic level of daily living assistance, encompassing urinary care (“After the catheter is removed, I don't know if he can urinate” (Chen et al., 2015)), dietary care (“What should we feed her?” (Cao et al., 2025)), skin hygiene, and pressure ulcer prevention (“Ensure good skin hygiene” (Chen et al., 2015)). As patients enter the recovery phase, their care needs evolve into mastering specialized rehabilitation skills, such as proper back percussion for mucus clearance (“Doesn't back percussion hurt after a long time? How do we do it?” (Cao et al., 2025)), standardized limb massage and movement techniques (“Move hands and feet on schedule” (Chen et al., 2015)), and a strong preference for face-to-face demonstrations and one-on-one guidance (“I think face-to-face instruction is better, and one-on-one demonstrations would be even better” (Cao et al., 2025)) to ensure correct skill acquisition.

Category 4: demand for information acquisition channels

Stroke patients and their caregivers have clearly expressed a definite demand for diversified, personalized and precise information acquisition channels. Besides traditional guidance from medical staff, they also hope to obtain the necessary information through various means, including mixed forms of education (“group meetings, written information, and technological applications” (Finch et al., 2022)), materials that are convenient for repeated reference (“brochures, which are convenient to read and use at any time” (Jiao et al., 2024; Ottonello et al., 2025)), and immediate guidance during operations (“while performing the nursing work, explanations are given simultaneously” (Cao et al., 2025)). However, the existing information channels have significant problems. Caregivers often have to obtain information through informal channels (such as “internet search or recommendations from others” (Zhao and Xue, 2011)), which makes it difficult to ensure the scientificity and effectiveness of the information, and even leads to doubts about professional sources due to conflicting information (“doubting the accuracy of the information” (Merati-Fashi et al., 2022)). They strongly expect that the information provided can take into account both timing and methods, that is, to receive personalized and easy-to-understand guidance when they are emotionally stable and have sufficient time (“hoping to do so when neither oneself nor the medical staff are busy” (Cao et al., 2025)), so as to ensure the accurate understanding and effective application of the information, thereby enhancing the confidence and ability of care.

Stage‑specific differences in health education needs

During the acute phase, both patients and caregivers urgently sought basic disease information (e.g., cause, prognosis, risk factors) (Ang et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2021) and immediate physical care, including urinary and skin care (Chen et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2021). In the recovery and rehabilitation phase, patients’ needs shifted toward functional recovery, rehabilitation techniques, and psychological adjustment (e.g., fear of disability, shame of dependence) (Zhao and Xue, 2011; Chen et al., 2015; Jiang et al., 2017; Jiao et al., 2024), while caregivers focused on acquiring rehabilitation skills, medication management, and recognizing complications (Cao et al., 2025; Zhao and Xue, 2011; Jiang et al., 2017; Ottonello et al., 2025). During long‑term home care, resource support needs (e.g., community rehabilitation services, respite care, financial support) became increasingly prominent (Chen et al., 2015; Zhao and Xue, 2011; Finch et al., 2022; Ottonello et al., 2025), along with death education needs among patients (Gong et al., 2022; Jiao et al., 2024). Shared and distinct needs: patient versus caregiver perspectives

From the dual‑perspective analysis, we further classified the identified needs into three categories.This classification is detailed in Supplementary Table S3.

Shared needs: basic disease knowledge (Ang et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2021); medication safety concerns (Cao et al., 2025; Zhao and Xue, 2011; Jiao et al., 2024); the need for psychological support (Ang et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2021; Jiao et al., 2024); desire for clear, accessible information channels (Cao et al., 2025; Zhao and Xue, 2011; Finch et al., 2022; Merati-Fashi et al., 2022); and continuity of care (e.g., post‑discharge follow‑up, symptom management) (Cao et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2015; Ottonello et al., 2025).

Patient‑specific needs: death education (Gong et al., 2022; Jiao et al., 2024); preservation of dignity (Zhang et al., 2021; Jiao et al., 2024); fear of long‑term disability (Chen et al., 2015; Jiao et al., 2024); autonomy in end‑of‑life decisions (Jiao et al., 2024); and social withdrawal due to self‑worth denial (Jiao et al., 2024).

Caregiver‑specific needs: role adaptation (from family member to primary caregiver) (Zhao and Xue, 2011; Jiang et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2021); family support (e.g., task sharing, rotating care) (Thomas and Harden, 2008; Chen et al., 2015); access to community resources (Chen et al., 2020; Zhao and Xue, 2011; Finch et al., 2022; Ottonello et al., 2025); respite care (Zhao and Xue, 2011; Chen et al., 2015); and management of their own physical and emotional exhaustion (Zhao and Xue, 2011; Chen et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2021).

Comparative analysis of health education needs between patients and caregivers

Through meta-synthesis of 11 qualitative studies, this research systematically identified three major categories of health education needs among stroke patients and caregivers: disease knowledge requirements, psychosocial support needs, and resource support demands. Further analysis revealed shared concerns regarding basic disease information, emotional support, and continuity of care between stroke patients and their caregivers. However, their underlying motivations and core demands exhibit fundamental differences. Patients' health education needs are more self-centered, manifesting as concerns about prognosis and functional recovery, apprehensions about medication side effects, and psychological experiences such as diminished self-worth and social avoidance due to illness (Cao et al., 2025; Zhao and Xue, 2011; Chen et al., 2015; Jiao et al., 2024). Some patients also contemplate end-of-life medical autonomy (Gong et al., 2022; Finch et al., 2022). Caregivers' needs, however, are significantly skewed toward continuity of care. They urgently require specific disease knowledge (e.g., etiology, risk factors) (Ang et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2021) and mastering professional caregiving skills (including medication management, rehabilitation training, and daily care) (Cao et al., 2025; Zhao and Xue, 2011; Chen et al., 2015; Jiang et al., 2017). Simultaneously, they endure prolonged physical and mental exhaustion, emotional distress, and conflicts in family roles (Zhao and Xue, 2011; Ang et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2021), while urgently seeking resources such as community support, nursing services, and emergency guidance (Cao et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2015; Jiang et al., 2017; Ottonello et al., 2025). This disparity indicates that health education targeting these two groups should adopt distinct focuses and strategies.

Discussion

Stroke patients and their caregivers have acquired some disease knowledge, but numerous challenges persist when translating this knowledge into practical caregiving actions. On one hand, most caregivers are non-professionals lacking systematic training, with limited capacity to comprehend and apply specialized knowledge. Even when they acquire some disease-related information, they struggle to accurately apply it in daily care. During physical rehabilitation exercises, they often fail to adjust training intensity and methods based on the patient's specific condition. Some may even resort to self-taught nursing practices during caregiving, leading to incorrect behaviors (Qu et al., 2017). On the other hand, existing health education training primarily relies on centralized lectures, which are simplistic, lack specificity, and offer little interaction. Caregivers find it difficult to pose personalized questions and receive timely answers during these sessions, resulting in suboptimal outcomes (Zhang et al., 2021). Furthermore, while disease information is increasingly accessible through diverse channels, some caregivers remain skeptical of online sources (Merati-Fashi et al., 2022), highlighting the urgent need to enhance health information literacy among patients and caregivers. Therefore, training guidance for caregivers should be strengthened through diverse, personalized approaches such as one-on-one instruction, video tutorials, and simulation exercises. Integrating theoretical knowledge with practical application will enhance caregivers' operational skills and problem-solving abilities, facilitating the effective translation of health knowledge into care practices. Additionally, emphasis should be placed on improving the health information literacy of both patients and their caregivers. Providing convenient learning pathways that meet their anytime, anywhere learning needs will facilitate the effective translation of health knowledge into care practices and enhance the quality of care.

Our findings broadly align with earlier qualitative syntheses that identified disease knowledge, medication management, and psychological support as core needs among stroke patients or caregivers (Bartoli et al., 2025; Zhao and Xue, 2011; Ang et al., 2013). However, compared with the recent meta-synthesis by Bartoli et al. (Bartoli et al., 2025), which examined transitional care needs from the triadic perspective of healthcare professionals, caregivers, and patients, several differences emerge. Bartoli et al. emphasized healthcare professionals as key information providers and system-level barriers to effective transitional care, whereas our dual‑perspective synthesis reveals that patients and caregivers often prioritize distinct needs that may not be fully captured by professional proxies-for example, patients in our review expressed strong concerns about death education and preservation of dignity (Gong et al., 2022; Jiao et al., 2024), which were not prominently reported in Bartoli et al.’s findings. Furthermore, while Bartoli et al. focused specifically on the hospital-to-home transition period, our stage-wise analysis demonstrates that health education needs shift dynamically across the entire disease trajectory, with resource support needs (e.g., community services, respite care) becoming prominent only in the long-term home care phase. Finally, by explicitly comparing patients and caregivers within the same synthesis, we show that patients focus more on self-identity and psychological well-being, whereas caregivers concentrate on mastering practical tasks and securing external resources. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in study scope (single phase vs. whole trajectory), perspective (professional-mediated vs. direct reports), and analytical approach (needs listing vs. Dual-perspective comparison).

The differing needs between stroke patients and their caregivers can be understood from the perspectives of psychological adaptation and role transition. For patients, stroke threatens their sense of self, autonomy, and future life trajectory. Consistent with Lazarus and Folkman’s stress‑coping framework (Lazarus and Folkman, 1984), patients face a primary appraisal of loss-loss of physical function, social roles, and personal identity. Recent evidence further shows that stroke can leave survivors in a liminal state of identity struggle, uncertain of how to reconstruct their sense of self (Hall et al., 2024). Their heightened concerns about dignity, fear of long‑term disability, and death education reflect this inward focus on preserving self‑worth (Tjokrowijoto et al., 2024). In contrast, caregivers undergo a sudden transition from family member to primary caregiver, which creates role ambiguity and overload. Qualitative studies indicate that caregivers, particularly spouses, actively manage the day‑to‑day demands that stroke places on individual, family, and social roles, and experience significant role strain (Nuccio et al., 2024). This drives their urgent need for practical skills (e.g., rehabilitation techniques, medication management), external support (e.g., community resources, respite care), and strategies to manage their own emotional exhaustion (Mendes Pereira et al., 2023). Thus, patients tend to look inward to reconstruct meaning, while caregivers look outward to secure tangible support. Recognizing these distinct psychological drivers is essential for designing phase‑appropriate, role‑sensitive health education interventions.

The health education needs of stroke patients and their caregivers constitute a complex, dynamic set that evolves with disease progression and exhibits multidimensional characteristics based on individual patient and caregiver traits (Su et al., 2025; Wang and Chen, 2022). Patients tend to focus more on their rehabilitation experiences and psychological well-being, while caregivers prioritize acquiring caregiving skills and accessing external resources. Based on these differences, establishing a “two-way collaborative support health education pathway” that is “targeted,” “stage-specific,” and “collaborative” can meet the evolving health education needs of both patients and caregivers. In the acute phase, education should focus on providing accurate disease information and initial emotional reassurance. During recovery, patients need rehabilitation guidance and psychological support, while caregivers require practical skill training. In long‑term home care, the emphasis shifts to linking community resources, respite services, and ongoing professional support. The specific strategies for each stage are detailed in Fig. 2.

The needs of stroke patients and their caregivers span the entire continuum of disease prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, and long-term care, encompassing disease knowledge, psychosocial support, and resource assistance. Research indicates that intervention groups adopting a health management model centered on stroke patient needs demonstrate significantly better control of blood pressure, blood glucose, and other parameters compared to control groups (Wang and Chen, 2022). However, the lack of effective coordination between hospitals, communities, and homes makes it difficult for patients to access continuous, systematic health management after discharge. Comprehensive stroke health management, as an innovative approach, delivers scientific health services encompassing prevention, screening, diagnosis, treatment, management, and rehabilitation for high-risk individuals or patients across pre-hospital, in-hospital, and post-hospital phases (Jiang et al., 2022; Wang and Ji, 2025). During the pre-hospital stage, the focus is on screening high-risk populations and providing health education to enhance disease prevention awareness. During the in-hospital phase, multidisciplinary team collaboration is strengthened to develop personalized treatment and early rehabilitation plans. In the post-hospitalization phase, community-based medical institutions take the lead, collaborating with family resources to provide ongoing medication guidance, rehabilitation training, psychological support, and long-term follow-up. This ensures seamless integration of patient education records, rehabilitation goals, and community resource information, meeting patients' care needs after discharge.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, most of the included studies were conducted in China, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other healthcare systems or cultural contexts. Second, the original studies primarily collected data at single time points, preventing a true longitudinal analysis of how needs evolve within the same individual over time. Third, only English and Chinese databases were searched, which may have introduced language bias. Fourth, we excluded quantitative studies, so some relevant outcome data may not have been captured. Despite these limitations, the meta‑synthesis provides a comprehensive integration of qualitative evidence from both patients and caregivers.

Conclusion

This study employed a qualitative meta-synthesis to delineate a three-dimensional framework of health education needs for stroke patients and caregivers (disease knowledge, psychosocial support, resource support), revealing fundamental differences in their focal needs. These findings indicate that traditional universal health education models are increasingly inadequate for addressing their complex, dynamic, and differentiated requirements. Future health education should establish a systematic pathway based on the differing needs of patients and caregivers, featuring “phased assessment, targeted provision, and collaborative support.” This approach will enable the development and implementation of personalized health education plans for patients and their caregivers, thereby effectively enhancing their caregiving capacity and quality of life.
