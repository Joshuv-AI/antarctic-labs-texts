Transcriptomic analysis of a compatible tobacco-herbivore interaction and the role of jasmonoyl-L-isoleucine hydrolase 1 in response to growth/defense trade-off
Authors: Yueyue Li, Jiwei Xie, Dahai Yang, Qingli Xiao, Chuyun Yang, Wenwen Chen, Meiqin Yi, Pengyang Sang, Qingyou Xia, Genhong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTobacco (Nicotiana tabacum), an allotetraploid species extensively cultivated worldwide, serves as a key model system for plant research but exhibits high susceptibility to insect pests. The comprehensive molecular mechanisms underlying N. tabacum’s response to insect pests remain poorly characterized. In this study, we collected leaf RNA-seq samples from tobacco plants at 0, 6, and 24 h after exposing them to either the aphid (Myzus persicae) or the cotton bollworm (Helicoverpa armigera). The numbers of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) responding to cotton bollworms exceeded those responding to aphids at both 6 and 24 h. Pathway enrichment analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and clustered expression pattern analysis revealed that the most significantly enriched pathways were involved in hormone signal transduction, secondary metabolism, and the regulation of transcription factors. We employed CRISPR/Cas9 technology to disrupt Jasmonoyl-L-isoleucine hydrolase 1 (JIH1), which was significantly upregulated following insect treatment as confirmed by both RNA-seq and qRT-PCR analyses, to validate its role in insect resistance. The weight of cotton bollworms decreased by 25.67% when feeding on ntjih1-1 plants compared to wild-type (WT) plants. In ntjih1-1 plants, transcripts involved in jasmonic acid (JA), salicylic acid (SA), and ethylene (ET) signaling pathways were activated. Concurrently, plant height decreased, whereas primary root length increased. Interestingly, the expression levels of 13 of 15 JAZ genes were repressed in ntjih1-1 plants. The ntjih1-2 plants showed no enhanced resistance to cotton bollworms, potentially due to functional selective evolution of NtJIH1-1 and NtJIH1-2 during tobacco chromosome doubling. Our results indicate that JIH1 may play a vital role in balancing growth and defense in the plant’s immune response to herbivory.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07801-2.Keywords: Transcriptomics, Herbivory, Nicotiana tabacum (tobacco), Jasmonoyl-L-isoleucine hydrolase (JIH1), Growth/defense trade-off, CRISPR/Cas9

Introduction

Plants face persistent herbivory from insects, with herbivorous species accounting for nearly 25% of all eukaryotic species and actively compromising plant defense systems, and these insects exploit plants through diverse feeding strategies [1, 2]. Insect herbivores employ diverse feeding strategies to exploit plants. For instance, chewing insects such as cotton bollworm (Helicoverpa armigera) and lawn armyworm (Spodoptera mauritia) directly consume plant tissues, causing extensive mechanical damage. Conversely, piercing-sucking insects like aphid (Myzus persicae) extract phloem sap, inflicting minimal structural injury but systemically disrupting nutrient allocation to primary growth zones [3, 4].

To counter insect herbivore attacks, plants have evolved diverse defense mechanisms that include both constitutive defenses (e.g., physical barriers like the cuticle and cell wall) and induced defenses triggered through the regulation of multiple metabolic pathways [3, 5, 6]. Induced defense mechanisms involve the integration of plant hormone signaling, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and transcription factor (TF)-mediated transcriptional reprogramming [7–9]. Hormone signal transduction in plants is primarily associated with defense mechanisms, predominantly mediated by jasmonic acid (JA), ethylene (ET), and salicylic acid (SA) signaling pathways. For instance, pathogenesis-related protein 1 C-like (PR1), a SA-dependent defense marker, is significantly induced upon whitefly feeding and transcriptionally regulated by SA [10]. Upon insect herbivore attack, plants rapidly accumulate JA, triggering the expression of defense-related genes [11, 12]; meanwhile, ET modulates plant defense through crosstalk with other herbivory-induced hormonal pathways (e.g., JA or SA), inducing the expression of ET-responsive TFs such as ET responsive factor 1 (ERF1) and ET-insensitive 3 (EIN3) [8, 13].

In contrast, secondary metabolites are biosynthesized through metabolic pathways diverging from those essential for physiological processes, and their production further enhances plant defense responses [14, 15]. Mounting evidence indicates that infestation by the whitefly Bemisia tabaci triggers the release of phenylpropanoid and terpenoid compounds, thereby activating herbivore-induced resistance [16]. Likewise, in both resistant and susceptible soybean varieties challenged by Lamprosema indica, predominant proteins function in flavonoid and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis [17, 18]. Furthermore, alkaloids serve as defensive secondary metabolites against herbivores, exhibiting broad phylogenetic distribution. Studies demonstrate that alkaloid concentrations rapidly increase in Nicotiana species following insect attack, functioning as an inducible chemical defense mechanism [19, 20]. Additionally, TFs critically regulate plant defense. For example, WRKY53 acts as a negative regulator of aphid resistance, likely through modulating secondary metabolite biosynthesis [21]. In birch (Betula platyphylla), BpWRKY6 enhances insect resistance by upregulating JA and terpenoid biosynthesis via mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)-mediated phosphorylation, thereby reducing gypsy moth feeding and delaying larval development [22]. Similarly, in rice (Oryza sativa), the myelocytomatosis protein 2 (MYC2)-JAMYB transcriptional cascade enhances resistance to brown planthoppers (BPH) by directly activating phenylpropanoid pathway genes to boost phenolamide and volatile biosynthesis, enabling both direct defense against BPH and indirect defense via the attraction of parasitoids [23].

The Solanaceae family, encompassing both lethally toxic plants and essential food crops, represents one of the most economically significant and widely utilized plant families. Despite producing toxic defensive compounds, Solanaceae species remain vulnerable to diverse insect herbivores [24, 25]. Furthermore, the allotetraploid species tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) is extensively cultivated and serves as a key model system for studying the Solanaceae family [26–28]. Consequently, investigating tobacco-herbivore interactions is critical both for developing targeted pest control strategies in Solanaceae crops and for advancing our understanding of plant defense signaling mechanisms against insect herbivores.

Recent advances in omics-based high-throughput methods, particularly transcriptomics, have greatly accelerated research on plant defense mechanisms against insect herbivory [29, 30]. Transcriptomics reveals that the leaffolder-resistant rice variety Qingliu combats leaf-chewing leaffolders via JA-mediated phenylpropanoid/flavonoid defenses in leaves and phloem-feeding planthoppers via SA-driven cell wall modification in sheaths [31]. Furthermore, comparative transcriptomics of Tuta absoluta-infested eggplants (Solanum melongena) and tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum) revealed higher transcript levels in the former, demonstrating that SA-related signaling genes play a vital role for eggplant resistance [32]. Similarly, transcriptomic analyses of wheat resistant to Aceria tosichella Keifer identified defense-related genes associated with resistance mechanisms against this eriophyoid mite pest [33]. However, transcriptomic responses in solanaceous plants like N. tabacum to two distinct herbivore types, the phloem-feeding aphid and the chewing insect cotton bollworm, remain uncharacterized. An integrated transcriptomic analysis of tobacco’s response to these insects, encompassing phytohormone signaling, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and TF regulation, is essential. Furthermore, CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing technology enables substantial advances in breeding programs for tomato (S. lycopersicum), rice (O. sativa), tobacco (N. tabacum), and soybean (Glycine max) [34–37].

In this study, transcriptome analysis identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and associated metabolic pathways in tobacco responding to insect infestation. CRISPR/Cas9 technology was then used to generate knockouts of candidate DEGs implicated in resistance, followed by evaluating defense responses in mutant plants. Through Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses, we identified and comparatively analyzed DEGs linked to phytohormone signaling, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and TF regulation. Moreover, we identified two jasmonoyl-L-isoleucine hydrolase 1 (NtJIH1) genes, homologs of herbivore resistance negative regulators in N. attenuate [38], from tobacco’s insect-responsive DEGs and generated CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout mutants for both NtJIH1-1 and NtJIH1-2 genes in cultivated tobacco. These mutants exhibited altered responses to insect feeding, with their defense phenotypes comprehensively characterized. Collectively, our results delineate transcriptional response to herbivory of tobacco and establish CRISPR-edited NtJIH1-null genotypes as valuable tools for mechanistic studies of plant-insect interaction mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Preparation of plant materials and tested insects

The cultivated tobacco (N. tabacum) cultivar Honghuadajinyuan (HHDJY) was sourced from National Tobacco Genetic Engineering Research Center (Yunnan, China) and served as the experimental material for transcriptome analysis, genetic transformation, gene expression studies, and insect resistance assays. Seeds were surface-sterilized sequentially in 75% ethanol for 30 s, then rinsed 1–2 times with sterile distilled water, followed by treatment with 10% sodium hypochlorite for 8–10 min, and thoroughly washed 4–5 times with sterile water before sowing on Murashige and Skoog (MS) solid medium. Germination and seedling growth occurred at 25 °C under 16/8 h light/dark cycles, after which four-week-old plants were used for insect bioassays. Concurrently, roots, stems, and leaves were collected from four-week-old seedlings for tissue-specific expression profiling, while flowers were harvested from mature plants. The piercing-sucking aphid (M. persicae) and chewing cotton bollworm (H. armigera) were maintained on wild-type (WT) tobacco at 18–25 °C under controlled conditions before infestation experiments.

Plant treatments

Treatments for transcriptome sequencing are detailed in Table S1. Untreated leaves served as controls (CK). For cotton bollworm infestation, five second-instar larvae were placed on uniformly sized, actively growing leaves per plant and permitted unrestricted feeding for 2 h until approximately 20% of the leaf area consumption. Locally herbivore-damaged leaves were collected at 6 h and 24 h post-infestation, designated as Ha6h and Ha24h, respectively. For aphid treatment, 20 mixed sub-adult (final instar) and adult aphids were confined to individual leaves using plastic dish cages to prevent escape. After seven days, all aphids were removed, and infested leaves were harvested at 6 h and 24 h post-removal, which were labeled as Mp6h and Mp24h, respectively. All treatments included three biological replicates.

For insect bioassays, WT and ntjih1 mutant plants were maintained under controlled conditions. In cotton bollworm assays, four second-instar larvae were placed on each of three WT and three mutants, followed by four days of unrestricted feeding. The same cohort of larvae was weighed daily and returned to the identical host plant. Each of 12 individual larvae was weighed daily for four consecutive days.

RNA isolation, sample preparation, and transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from leaf tissues using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. To analyze tissue-specific expression profiles, RNA was extracted from roots, stems, leaves, and flowers of tobacco. For RNA-seq, 15 samples were processed, three biological replicates each for CK, Ha6h, Ha24h, Mp6h, and Mp24h. Transcriptome sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) with 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data quality was assessed via FastQC v0.11.2, with subsequent adapter trimming and quality filtering using Trimmomatic v0.36 to generate high-confidence clean data [39]. Clean reads were then aligned to the N. tabacum K326 reference genome (https://ftp.solgenomics.net/genomes/Nicotiana_tabacum/edwards_et_al_2017) via HISAT2 v2.1.0 under default parameters [27]. The raw sequencing data are deposited in the NCBI SRA database under accession number PRJNA739896.

Analysis of DEGs

DEGs were identified from the mapped sequencing reads using DESeq2 (v1.12.4), with a significance threshold of adjusted P-value (Q-value) < 0.05 and |log2(fold change)| >2 between sample pairs. Subsequent functional enrichment analyses involved GO annotation via the GO database (http://geneontology.org/) and KEGG pathway enrichment using ClusterProfiler (v3.0.5) against the KEGG database (http://www.kegg.jp).

Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted via the Bioincloud online platform to cluster samples based on gene expression patterns. Hierarchical clustering analysis was subsequently performed using the unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) in R (v4.0.2). Venn diagrams and heatmaps were generated using TBtools [40], while significantly enriched KEGG terms (Q-value < 0.01) were visualized via the ggplot2 R package. For temporal expression profiling of DEGs, trend analysis was executed using the Short Time-series Expression Miner (STEM) program [41] on the Omicshare platform, with significantly enriched expression patterns identified at a threshold of P ≤ 0.01.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was employed to identify DEGs from the comparison group, following established methodologies [42]. The WGCNA workflow was implemented using the WGCNA R package (v1.72), with gene expression quantified as fragments per kilobase per million mapped reads (FPKM). Genes from the most statistically significant modules (P < 0.01, module-trait correlation >0.8) were annotated via STRING (https://string-db.org/?tdsourcetag=s_pctim_aiom) by querying the Arabidopsis thaliana reference proteome. Subsequently, protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks were reconstructed using Cytoscape v3.7.2 [43] and topologically refined with Gephi v0.9.2 [44]. TFs regulating the identified DEGs were annotated through PlantTFDB (http://planttfdb.gao-lab.org/) [45].

Generation, characterization, and phenotypic analysis of mutant plants

Using the coding sequence (CDS) and amino acid sequence of N. tabacum K326 NtJIH1 (Nitab4.5_0009613g0040) as query sequences, we performed BLASTP searches against the NCBI database. This analysis identified two homologous proteins (XP_016501615.1 and XP_016449310.1) in the N. tabacum TN90 genome assembly (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome/?term=Ntab-TN90), designated as NtJIH1-1 and NtJIH1-2, respectively. To investigate the functional roles of these proteins, we utilized a pORE-Cas9 binary vector constructed in-house based on established CRISPR/Cas9 systems [46]. For targeted mutagenesis of NtJIH1-1 and NtJIH1-2, two pairs of 24-bp DNA-specific sgRNA target oligonucleotides were synthesized (Table S2). Oligonucleotide pairs were annealed and cloned into the BsaI-digested pORE-Cas9 vector via Golden Gate assembly.

The pORE-Cas9 binary vectors harboring both Cas9 and sgRNA expression cassettes were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens LBA4404 via the freeze-thaw method [47] for subsequent tobacco transformation. Tobacco knockout mutants (ntjih1-1 and ntjih1-2) were generated through Agrobacterium-mediated leaf disc transformation [48], with positive transgenic clones selected on kanamycin-containing medium. Genomic DNA of transgenic tobacco plants with kanamycin resistance was extracted from kanamycin-resistant plants using the EasyPure® Genomic DNA Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Target regions flanking the sgRNA recognition sites were amplified by PCR with gene-specific primers. PCR products were subjected to Sanger sequencing, and homozygous mutants were identified by aligning sequencing chromatograms with WT sequences.

Plant height and primary root length were measured in approximately four-week-old ntjih1-1 mutants and WT plants. Plant height was defined as the vertical distance from the soil surface to the apex of the highest leaf, while plant primary root length was measured from the root-shoot junction to the root tip. Phenotypes were documented photographically at two developmental stages (two and four weeks post-germination), with data derived from three independent biological replicates.

Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Candidate DEGs were validated by qRT-PCR with three biological replicates. Gene-specific primers were designed using the online tool PrimerQuest (https://sg.idtdna.com/Primerquest/Home/Index). Due to high sequence similarity between NtJIH1-1 and NtJIH1-2, a single primer pair (NtJIH1-1/2) was employed to simultaneously quantify the expression of both genes. qRT-PCR assays were performed on a qTOWER2.2 real-time PCR system (Analytik Jena AG, Jena, Germany) using Takara’s SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM II. The thermal cycling protocol consisted of 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 60 s [49]. Relative expression levels were normalized to the reference gene NtEF1α (Nitab4.5_0001802g0030) and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [50]. Primer sequences are detailed in Table S3.

Comparison of expression levels of NtJIH1-1 and NtJIH1-2 genes

Due to the inability of qRT-PCR to adequately distinguish the expression levels of NtJIH1-1 and NtJIH1-2, two coding sequence (CDS) fragments of identical length (302 bp) with high sequence similarity were amplified by PCR using a high-fidelity DNA polymerase. The PCR products were separated by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel, after which the target bands were excised and purified using EasyPure® Quick Gel Extraction Kit (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Subsequently, TA cloning was performed to ligate the purified fragments into the pEASY-Blunt Zero vector (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Individual colonies were selected for Sanger sequencing to identify the types of inserted CDS fragments.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 6, with significance levels denoted by asterisks, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001 compared to respective control groups by Student’s t-test.

Results

Comparative RNA-Seq profiling reveals time-resolved transcriptional signatures in tobacco defense against herbivorous insects

To investigate gene expression profiles in tobacco leaves infested with cotton bollworms and aphids compared to untreated tobacco leaves, RNA-Seq analysis was conducted (Fig. 1; Table 1). The average total bases sequenced per transcriptome were 6483.45 Mb (CK), 7134.23 Mb (Ha6h), 7263.98 Mb (Ha24h), 7181.45 Mb (Mp6h), and 6935.93 Mb (Mp24h) (Table 1). A total of 40,771,252 to 58,494,352 reads were obtained from the 15 samples. The GC content of all samples was approximately from 42.77% to 44.50%. Furthermore, more than 93% of the clean reads were mapped to the N. tabacum K326 genome, with uniquely mapped reads exceeding 80%. Seven randomly selected DEGs from genes highly upregulated or downregulated after insect infestation were validated using qRT-PCR (Fig. S1, Table S4), and their expression patterns were consistent with those observed in the RNA-seq analysis. Additionally, PCA of all detected genes showed that the first two principal components (PCs) accounted for 41.66% of the total variation (Fig. 1A). Biological replicates within each treatment group clustered closely together, indicating high reproducibility for each condition. Hierarchical clustering analysis revealed two major clusters, one consisting of CK and the other comprising both aphid and cotton bollworm treatments (Fig. 1B).

Besides, we identified 4234, 4188, 3834, and 3264 DEGs in Ha6h vs. CK, Ha24h vs. CK, Mp6h vs. CK, and Mp24h vs. CK comparison groups, respectively (Fig. 1C). A Venn diagram of DEGs revealed 842 genes common to all insect-infested treatments (Ha6h, Ha24h, Mp6h, and Mp24h) compared to CK (Fig. 1D). Temporal and insect-specific reprogramming of defense pathways in tobacco revealed by integrated GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

GO enrichment analysis was performed to functionally classify the DEGs in tobacco leaves following insect infestation (Fig. 2, Fig. S2 and S3). Three main GO categories including biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF) were identified. The significantly enriched BP terms were analyzed first (Fig. 2, Table S5). The results showed that DEGs involved in response to stimuli were significantly enriched at both Ha6h and Mp6h. However, the numbers of DEGs associated with organonitrogen compound metabolic process and cellular nitrogen compound biosynthetic process increased markedly at Ha24h, whereas the numbers of DEGs involved in response to chemical and response to abiotic stimulus were considerably higher at Mp24h. Specifically, 17 stress-response-related GO terms within BP were identified, including responses to bacteria, fungi, cold, stimulus, chemical, nutrient levels, metal ions, reactive oxygen species, radiation, light intensity, and red light (Table 2, Table S6). Cotton bollworm infestation elicited DEGs associated with significantly more enriched GO terms (15) than aphid infestation (10). Moreover, in the CC category, the majority of activated DEGs at Ha6h and Ha24h were enriched in the cell periphery, plasma membrane, intracellular organelles, and organelles. In contrast, at Mp6h and Mp24h, most DEGs were enriched in the cytoplasm, chloroplast, and plastid (Fig. S2, Table S7). Within the MF category, the most prominent groups of DEGs induced by cotton bollworm at both time points were associated with oxidoreductase activity, structural molecule activity, and RNA binding (Fig. S3, Table S8). Conversely, at Mp6h and Mp24h, DEGs were mainly enriched in cofactor binding and RNA binding. Additionally, this study examined DEGs involved in primary metabolism, including the metabolism of organic acids, fatty acids, amino acids, cell walls, carbohydrates, and plant hormones (Table S9). Notably, DEGs related to plant hormone responses exhibited the most pronounced alterations across all treatments, followed by those involved in organic acid and carbohydrate metabolism. Interestingly, the numbers of downregulated cell wall-related DEGs in aphid treatments were approximately twice as many as in cotton bollworm treatments.

KEGG pathway analysis was then performed to identify major metabolic pathways (P < 0.01) in tobacco involved in response to insect feeding (Fig. 3, Table S10). Compared to CK, DEGs at Ha6h were significantly enriched in 40 metabolic pathways, including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, plant hormone signal transduction, and photosynthesis-antenna proteins. WGCNA identifies insect-specific modules linked to defense pathways with STEM-validated Temporal dynamics

WGCNA was conducted to identify potential module-sample relationships based on DEG expression profiles. 14 co-expressed gene modules were identified (Fig. S4 and S5, Table S11 and S12). Notably, five modules (antiquewhite4, palevioletred3, turquoise, darkmagenta, and plum1) showed the highest correlation (P ≤ 0.01) with CK, Ha6h, Ha24h, Mp6h, and Mp24h, respectively (Fig. S4A and B). To systematically characterize these modules, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed (Tables S11 and S12). Distinct GO terms were enriched across different modules (Table S11), reflecting their divergent biological roles under different treatments. For KEGG analysis, Arabidopsis protein annotations from the STRING database identified module-specific functional themes (Table S12). The palevioletred3 module was enriched in MAPK signaling, starch/sucrose metabolism, plant hormone signaling, and secondary metabolism; the turquoise module was associated with ribosome biogenesis, plant hormone signaling, spliceosome activity, and secondary metabolism; the darkmagenta module was dominated by MAPK signaling, starch/sucrose metabolism, plant hormone signaling, spliceosome, and photosynthesis; and the plum1 module was enriched in ribosome biogenesis, spliceosome, and RNA transport.

Clustered analysis via the STEM program further categorized DEGs into 20 expression subclusters, with key patterns consistent with prior findings: genes in subclusters 13, 16, 17, 18, and 19 (P ≤ 0.01) were upregulated under cotton bollworm infestation at Ha6h or Ha24h, and were primarily linked to MAPK signaling, plant hormone signaling, secondary metabolism, and spliceosome activity. In contrast, genes in subclusters 15 and 20 were induced under aphid infestation at Mp6h or Mp24h, and enriched in functions related to plant hormone signaling, endoplasmic reticulum protein processing, secondary metabolism, ribosome, spliceosome, and RNA transport (Fig. S6, Table S13).

Transcriptome profiling reveals divergent molecular responses to cotton bollworm versus aphid infestation in tobacco

Based on GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses, plant hormone signaling pathways in tobacco underwent significant modulation in response to insect herbivory (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). Among 27 DEGs associated with JA signaling, three lipoxygenase (LOX) genes, three allene oxide synthase (AOS) genes, two allene oxide cyclase (AOC) genes, two 12-oxophytodienoate reductase (OPR) genes, along with jasmonoyl-l-isoleucine hydrolase 1 (JIH1), jasmonate ZIM-domain (JAZ), and MYC2 genes, exhibited marked induction following cotton bollworm feeding at 6 h and 24 h. In contrast, eight SA signaling-related genes, primarily TGA and WRKY70 TFs, were upregulated after aphid infestation. ET signaling was also activated following herbivory, with genes encoding S-AdoMet synthase (SAMS), 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate synthase (ACS), and ERF1 induced expression, whereas 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate oxidase (ACO) genes remained unaltered (Fig. 4, Table S14).

Expression profiles of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with jasmonic acid (JA), salicylic acid (SA), and ethylene (ET) signaling pathways in tobacco leaves following cotton bollworm and aphid infestation at 6 h and 24 h post-exposure

A total of 63 genes involved in secondary metabolism pathways, including phenylpropanoid, flavone/flavonol biosynthesis, terpenoid biosynthesis, and alkaloid biosynthesis, were differentially expressed (Fig. 5, Table S14). In the phenylpropanoid pathway, genes encoding phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL), and cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H) were predominantly induced under cotton bollworm infestation (Ha6h/Ha24h) but suppressed during aphid infestation (Mp6h/Mp24h) (Fig. 5A). Similarly, multiple DEGs associated with flavone/flavonol biosynthesis, including hydroxycinnamoyltransferase (HCT), chalcone synthase (CHS), flavonoid-3-hydroxylase (F3H), and flavonol synthase (FLS), displayed significant upregulation under Ha6h/Ha24h and downregulation under Mp6h/Mp24h (Fig. 5B). Transcriptome analysis revealed a significant activation of the JA signaling pathway following insect attack. Notably, JIH1 was markedly induced post-infestation, which was hypothesized to act as a negative regulator of insect tolerance [38, 52]. Consequently, two JIH1 genes (NtJIH1-1 and NtJIH1-2) were identified in the N. tabacum database by screening the coding and amino acid sequence of N. tabacum variety K326 NtJIH1 (Nitab4.5_0009613g0040) (Fig. 7A). These genes were constitutively expressed in all tested tissues (roots, stems, leaves, and flowers) (Fig. 7B). Crucially, both NtJIH1-1 and NtJIH1-2 exhibited significant upregulation after insect feeding, aligning with transcriptome data (Figs. 4 and 7C).

Discussion

Plants are intelligent organisms capable of rapidly activating defense responses against insect herbivores [53, 54]. For instance, defense-related genes involved in redox homeostasis, phytohormone signaling, and secondary metabolite production were markedly upregulated in peaches following aphid infestation [55]. Similarly, maize responded to insect attacks by inducing genes involved in hormone regulation and specific metabolite synthesis [56]. In our study, we observed significant alterations in DEGs in the following comparison groups, 4234 DEGs in Ha6h vs. CK; 4188 DEGs in Ha24h vs. CK; 3834 DEGs in Mp6h vs. CK; and 3264 DEGs in Mp24h vs. CK (Fig. 1). Previous studies have shown that transcriptional responses to different insects involve distinct sets of plant genes [57]. Therefore, in this study, we focused our analysis on gene enrichment to identify changes in DEGs in tobacco fed on by aphids and cotton bollworms at 6 and 24 h within specific metabolic pathways, including hormone signaling, secondary metabolism, and transcription factor activity (Figs. 4, 5 and 6).

Comparative analysis of tobacco plants infested by aphids versus cotton bollworms at 6 and 24 h post-infestation revealed distinct transcriptional responses. Specifically, cotton bollworm infestation elicited significantly more DEGs associated with 15 enriched GO terms related to biological processes, whereas aphid infestation induced fewer DEGs linked to only 10 enriched GO terms (Table 2). Interestingly, cotton bollworm infestation induces stronger upregulation of DEGs linked to immune effector processes, defense responses to bacteria, and defense responses to fungi in tobacco (Table 2). Conversely, these transcriptional changes are significantly less pronounced in Arabidopsis under chewing insect herbivory [57]. In contrast, aphid infestation stimulates gene enrichment associated with responses to metal ions, reactive oxygen species, radiation, and light intensity in both species (tobacco and Arabidopsis) (Table 2) [57]. Additionally, in Arabidopsis, aphids induced more upregulated genes linked to carbohydrate metabolism [57], while the mevalonate (MVA) pathway was most prominently enriched when Gossypium arboretum responded to cotton bollworm infestation [58]. In this study, cotton bollworm infestation elicited significant changes in hormone signal transduction and ribosome-related pathways, whereas aphid infestation resulted in greater enrichment of photosynthesis and spliceosome-associated genes in tobacco (Fig. 3). These results highlight significant interspecific divergence in plant immune adaptations to insect feeding strategies.

In general, piercing-sucking insects such as aphids and whiteflies primarily induce SA-mediated defenses, whereas plant responses to chewing herbivores are predominantly mediated by JA and ET signaling pathways [59, 60]. Consistent with this pattern, cotton bollworm feeding in our study upregulated genes associated with JA signaling (e.g., LOX, AOS, OPR, JIH1, and MYC2) and ET signaling (e.g., SAMS, ACS, and ERF1). In contrast, aphid feeding significantly enhanced the expression of SA signaling-related genes, such as WRKY70 and TGA (Fig. 4). Interestingly, multiple ACO genes involved in ET signaling were markedly suppressed. This suppression suggests that 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC), a compound previously reported to effectively induce plant stress resistance [61], may accumulate in tobacco leaves during insect herbivory.

Given the evidence of insect-specific plant responses, it is recognized that many secondary metabolites contribute to plant defenses against insect herbivores [62, 63]. In this study, the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway emerged as a focus of interest (Fig. 5A). This pathway produces lignin and a variety of phenolic compounds, which are known to play crucial roles in plant defense, especially against chewing insects [64, 65]. Our results showed that multiple genes encoding key regulatory enzymes in the phenylpropanoid pathway after cotton bollworm damage were significantly upregulated at 6 h, including PAL, 4CL, and C4H (Fig. 5A). These results indicate that the phenylpropanoid pathway is activated in tobacco following cotton bollworm infestation. This activation likely primarily drives lignin synthesis or production of various phenolic substances, which forms a physical barrier in plant defense and further suppresses larval feeding behavior [66, 67]. Similarly, genes associated with flavone and flavonol biosynthesis exhibited differential expressions at Ha6h and Ha24h compared to aphid-infested samples (Mp6h and Mp24h). This pattern suggests accumulation of flavonoid compounds, such as flavonols, in tobacco leaves during cotton bollworm feeding (Fig. 5B). These results align with findings by Nakata et al., showing that soybean leaves accumulate multiple isoflavones upon Spodoptera litura larval attack and by Kundu et al., demonstrating that overexpression of sorghum brown midrib 12 (Bmr12) gene enhances resistance to fall armyworm through JA/hydrogen peroxide-mediated flavonoid defenses [68, 69]. Furthermore, terpenoids, the most abundant class of plant secondary metabolites, play essential roles in plant-insect interactions by releasing volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that mediate chemical signaling within plant populations and between plants and insects [70–72]. This aligns with Wu et a.’s discovery of insecticidal properties in terpenoids derived from Tetraena mongolica [73]. Our RNA-seq results demonstrate upregulation of key terpenoid biosynthetic enzyme genes in tobacco leaves following insect infestation (Fig. 5C). These results indicate that tobacco might activate terpenoid biosynthesis as a defense mechanism against both cotton bollworm and aphid, likely through JA-dependent signaling for the former and SA-JA crosstalk for the latter. Nicotine and related alkaloids serve as both constitutive and inducible defensive metabolites in well-studied Nicotiana species [25, 74]. Among these, nicotine dominates the alkaloid pool (90%–95% total content) in cultivated tobacco varieties [75], exerting neurotoxic effects by agonizing acetylcholine receptors in herbivore nervous systems, a key mechanism underpinning its defensive efficacy [76]. Genomic analyses reveal that expansion of transposable elements (TEs) in tobacco genomes drives expression diversification of duplicated genes, thereby facilitating the evolution of herbivory-induced signaling and defense pathways, including nicotine biosynthesis [77]. Consistent with this evolutionary framework, our study shows that tobacco reprograms anabasine and nicotine levels in response to insect herbivores via functional diversification of key biosynthetic enzymes (e.g., QPRT, IRL, ADC, ODC, PMT, MPO, BBE-like), with significantly stronger induction following cotton bollworm infestation than aphid feeding (Fig. 5D).

Accumulating evidence demonstrates that TFs play essential roles in plant defense against insect herbivores [78, 79]. In this study, bHLH TFs in tobacco that respond to insect herbivory were initially identified. Specifically, multiple bHLH TFs were significantly induced at Ha6h (cotton bollworm-infested) compared to at Mp6h (aphid-infested) (Fig. 6), indicating their potential role in regulating defenses against chewing herbivores, consistent with the established association between JA signaling and responses to chewing herbivores [60, 80]. Moreover, C2H2 TFs (e.g., StZFP2), previously reported to be induced by mechanical wounding and herbivory [81], were dynamically regulated in tobacco leaves during insect infestation (Fig. 6), indicating their contribution to plant tolerance mechanisms. Notably, the numbers of DEGs encoding MYB TFs increased at 24 h compared to 6 h post-aphid feeding (Fig. 6), aligning with studies implicating MYB TFs in defense against phloem-feeding insects [82]. For example, TaMYB19, TaMYB29, and TaMYB44 co-regulate wheat phloem-based defense (PBD) against aphids by orchestrating callose deposition and lectin production through ET signaling crosstalk [82].

At the next stage, we identified JIH1 as a potential negative regulator of herbivore defense (Fig. 7). Based on findings by Woldemariam et al. [38], JIH1 functions as a negative attenuator of plant defenses against herbivores, a role corroborated by integrating transcriptomic data with existing literature. To functionally validate this, CRISPR/Cas9-generated ntjih1 mutants were developed (Fig. 7, Fig. S7). Notably, larval weight of cotton bollworms was significantly reduced when fed on ntjih1-1 plants compared to both ntjih1-2 and WT plants (Fig. 7). Mechanistically, ntjih1-1 plants displayed enhanced activation of JA-, SA-, and ET-signaling pathways, evidenced by upregulation of MYC2, JAR4, PR1b, SAMT, SABP, PR1a/c, PR2, ERF2, ACO, and EFE26 after herbivory (Fig. 7). In contrast, ntjih1-2 plants showed no significant resistance phenotype, which aligned with markedly lower baseline expression of NtJIH1-2 compared to NtJIH1-1 (Table 4). Genomic analyses revealed that NtJIH1-1 and NtJIH1-2 originated from the diploid progenitors S (N. sylvestris) and T (N. tomentosoformis) genomes, respectively (Table 3). This functional dichotomy parallels observations in tobacco aquaporin genes (NtTIP2;4 from T and NtTIP2;5 from S), which display subgenome-specific expression patterns due to selective evolutionary pressures during allopolyploidization [83–85]. Thus, the attenuated defense response in ntjih1-2 likely stems from its inherent low expression, a consequence of subgenome-biased regulatory evolution in allotetraploid tobacco.

Previous studies indicate that growth-defense trade-offs stem from interactions crucial for plant survival [86, 87]. In this study, mutations in ntjih1-1 enhanced insect resistance but impaired tobacco growth, reducing plant height (Fig. 7). This phenotype resembles that jaz mutants in Arabidopsis, which exhibit stunted growth and elevated herbivore resistance [88], mechanistically, under stress conditions, JA conjugates with isoleucine (Ile) to form bioactive JA-Ile, recognized by the coronatine-insensitive 1 (COI1) receptor. The resulting COI1-JA-Ile complex then ubiquitinates JAZ repressors for proteasomal degradation, releasing TFs that activate JA-responsive genes [89]. Specifically, JIH1 has been reported to be located upstream in the JA pathway and to hydrolyze JA-Ile, thereby attenuating its signaling [38]. Our data revealed that ntjih1-1 plants downregulated the expression of most JAZ genes compared to WT plants (Fig. 8). Deficiency in JIH1 likely promotes JAZ protein degradation via SCFCOI1-mediated ubiquitination, thereby enhancing JA synthesis and JA-Ile accumulation. This process might drive the hyperactivation of defense-related genes, including JA-responsive (MYC2, JAR4, and PR1b), SA-responsive (SABP, PR1a/c, and PR2), and ET-responsive (ERF2, ACO, and ERF26) genes. The significant upregulation of these genes likely redirects resources toward secondary metabolite production [90], consequently constraining plant growth, as evidenced by reduced height and root length in ntjih1-1 mutants. This observation aligns with studies demonstrating that JAZ proteins dynamically balance growth-defense trade-offs by modulating resource allocation in response to stress [88]. Collectively, JIH1 may function as a negative regulator of anti-herbivore immunity. Its suppression, potentially correlated with downregulation of JAZ genes, might elucidate the mechanistic basis for optimal growth-defense balance in plants. Future research will focus on elucidating NtJIH1-mediated insect resistance mechanisms through targeted proteomics to quantify JAZ degradation kinetics and JA-Ile accumulation in mutants, recombinant NtJIH1 purification for hydrolysis rate assays of JA-Ile, OPDA-Ile, and coronatine, RNA-seq or yeast library screening to identify novel regulatory targets, and detailed measurement of plant biomass, leaf area, and photosynthetic parameters in mutant lines [38, 52, 88, 91].

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that N. tabacum exhibits insect-specific defense responses: cotton bollworms induce more unique DEGs than aphids through JA/ET-dependent defenses, whereas aphids activate SA-mediated responses. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout confirmed NtJIH1-1 (but not NtJIH1-2) might function as a negative defense regulator, as evidenced by a 25.67% reduction in cotton bollworm larval weight on ntjih1-1 mutants. This phenotype likely results from hyperactivation of JA/SA/ET signaling pathways and concurrent repression of 13 out of 15 JAZ genes. The transcriptional dominance of NtJIH1-1, accounting for 87.5% of expression compared to NtJIH1-2, reflects subgenome-divergent evolution following polyploidization, with the N. sylvestris-derived NtJIH1-1 being prioritized for herbivory adaptation. Collectively, JIH1 could serve as a tunable target for engineering pest-resistant crops with mitigated fitness costs, pending further clarification of insect-specific evasion mechanisms.
