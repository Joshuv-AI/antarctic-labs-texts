Clinical Intervention Following Detection of Incident Diabetes Within a Large Psychiatric Service System: A Chart Review Study
Authors: Pernille Kølbæk, Christopher Rohde, Maria Speed, Henrik Holm Thomsen, Søren Dinesen Østergaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionIndividuals with mental illness have a markedly reduced life expectancy, partly due to a significantly increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes, driven by lifestyle factors and the adverse metabolic effects of psychotropic medications. This study examined whether abnormal laboratory values indicating incident diabetes, when ordered within psychiatric services, were followed by documented interventions.MethodsWe used electronic health record data from the Psychiatric Services of the Central Denmark Region and drew a random sample of patients with incident diabetes detected between 2019 and 2024. Incident diabetes was defined by the first laboratory value ordered by the psychiatric services indicating diabetes: glycated haemoglobin ≥ 48 mmol/mol, fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, plasma glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during a 2‐h oral glucose tolerance test or random plasma glucose. Patients with pre‐existing diabetes, identified through ICD‐10 codes or antidiabetic medication history, were excluded. Manual chart review assessed whether incident diabetes was followed, within 3 months, by documented clinical intervention (such as referral, repeat testing, or medication initiation), or was merely acknowledged without further action. Descriptive statistics and adjusted logistic regression analysis were used to characterise the cohort and examine characteristics associated with lack of intervention.ResultsAmong 416 patients with incident diabetes (57% male, median age 48 years [IQR: 35–59], 33% with schizophrenia or other psychotic disorders), only 37% received a clinical intervention. The most frequent intervention was referral to a general practitioner (27%), while antidiabetic medication was initiated in only 6% of those who received an intervention. The only characteristic associated with lack of clinical intervention was body mass index < 25 kg/m2 (adjusted odds ratio 3.82, 95% CI: 1.92–8.20).ConclusionThis study suggests that appropriate clinical intervention following detection of incident diabetes is lacking. This may inform future strategies to reduce metabolic and cardiovascular risks among individuals with mental illness.

Introduction

Individuals with severe mental illness, including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depressive disorder, have a substantially reduced life expectancy compared to the general population [1, 2, 3]. A key contributor to this excess mortality is their 2–3‐fold increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes (T2D) [4, 5, 6]. There are several reasons underlying this increased risk, including lifestyle factors such as unhealthy diet, smoking, and low physical activity [7, 8], as well as adverse metabolic effects associated with commonly prescribed psychotropic medications [9]. Moreover, individuals with mental illness developing T2D experience higher rates of both microvascular and macrovascular complications [10].

Given the increased risk of T2D, routine metabolic screening in psychiatric treatment settings is recommended to facilitate early detection and intervention [11, 12]. Clinical guidelines recommend a series of clinical interventions when diabetes is detected during routine metabolic screening, including patient education about lifestyle modifications, pharmacological management, referral to specialised diabetes care, and systematic monitoring of glycaemic control and cardiovascular risk factors [13]. Failure to address abnormal glucose levels can lead to progressive metabolic deterioration, increased cardiovascular morbidity, and ultimately, higher mortality [14, 15, 16, 17, 18].

Previous studies have primarily examined screening practices and T2D prevalence rates [4], while there has been less emphasis on the extent of clinical intervention following detection of incident T2D in psychiatric services.

Aims of the Study

The primary aim of this study was to examine the extent to which incident diabetes among patients receiving care in a large Danish psychiatric service was followed by clinical interventions. Secondarily, it aimed to examine patient characteristics associated with a lack of clinical intervention.

Methods

Setting

This study used electronic health record (EHR) data from the Psychiatric Services of the CDR, which provides tax‐funded inpatient, outpatient, and emergency psychiatric care to a catchment population of approximately 1.4 million individuals [19].

Cohort

The inclusion criteria were at least one contact with the Psychiatric Services of the CDR between July 1, 2019, and July 1, 2024, and incident diabetes, which was defined as the first blood sample‐ based laboratory value ordered by the Psychiatric Services in the CDR indicating diabetes: glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 48 mmol/mol, fasting plasma glucose level ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, 2‐h oral glucose tolerance test ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or random plasma glucose level (≥ 11.1 mmol/L). The inclusion criteria did not allow for distinction between type 1 and type 2 diabetes. The date of the first qualifying abnormal value was defined as the index date. If multiple abnormal values were recorded on the same date, the earliest timestamped result was used.

Patients with preexisting diabetes mellitus, defined as the presence of a prior diabetes mellitus diagnosis (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD‐10] codes DE10 to DE14) [20] or prior treatment with antidiabetic medication (Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification system code A10 [21] in the CDR), were excluded. Patients with a blood sample indicating incident diabetes taken in other parts of the CDR, such as general hospitals or general practice, were also excluded.

A random sample of 500 patients was selected from the eligible population for manual chart review. The selection was performed without restrictions on specific variables, ensuring that each individual in the population had an equal chance of being included. During the manual chart review, medical records were screened for documentation of pre‐existing diabetes. Individuals with evidence of diabetes noted in the clinical records, despite the absence of a registered diagnostic code or recorded use of antidiabetic medication, were classified as prevalent cases and excluded from the analyses.

Data on Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics

For patients who met the eligibility criteria, structured data on their demographic (age, sex, marital status, and municipality) and clinical characteristics (psychiatric department within the CDR, contact type, psychiatric diagnoses, medication, number of psychiatric/general hospital inpatient stays, bed days at hospitals in the CDR, number of psychiatric/general hospital outpatient visits in the CDR, and body mass index [BMI]) were extracted from their EHRs.

Clinical Intervention Following the Detection of Incident Diabetes

The EHRs (including free‐text clinical notes) of patients meeting the eligibility criteria were manually reviewed to assess how laboratory values from blood samples indicating incident diabetes (as defined above) were managed over the subsequent 3 months. The review was conducted by a trained research nurse and medical student using a standardized data form developed for the study. Prior to the full review, the reviewers jointly assessed a subset of records to ensure consistent interpretation of the classification criteria.

For each case, reviewers examined clinical notes, laboratory results, medication lists, referrals, and correspondence in the EHR within the 3 months following the index laboratory result. Based on this information, patients were categorized into one of three groups: Not noted and no clinical intervention, defined as no documentation in the EHR indicating that the abnormal result had been acknowledged or addressed by the treating clinicianNoted but no clinical intervention, defined as explicit documentation that the abnormal result had been acknowledged (e.g., referenced in clinical notes) without any subsequent clinical action.Clinical intervention, defined as documentation of a clinical action taken in response to the abnormal result. Actions were categorized as: confirmatory HbA1c or fasting plasma glucose testing, specialist review (hospital‐based), consultation with general practitioner (GP) or specialist in endocrinology, referral for follow‐up and treatment with GP, lifestyle intervention, initiating antidiabetic treatment (e.g., metformin), psychotropic medication changes to reduce adverse metabolic effects, examination of potential complication, or other (e.g., patient education on complications associated with hyperglycaemia).

Statistical Analysis

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were described for the total cohort and stratified by sex. Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile (IQR), while categorical variables are reported as counts and proportions. Differences between sexes were analysed using independent samples t‐tests for continuous variables and chi‐square tests for categorical variables. All relevant assumptions for these tests, including homogeneity of variance, were verified and upheld. To identify predictors of a lack of interventions following blood samples indicative of diabetes, we employed an adjusted multivariable logistic regression model. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Missing data were handled using complete‐case analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using the R statistical software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of randomly 500 patients underwent manual review of the medical notes in their EHRs, and we found that 84 (17%) met one or more exclusion criteria, although their medication and diagnosis lists did not include antidiabetic drugs or a diabetes diagnosis. The characteristics of the remaining 416 patients are listed in Table 1. Their median age was 48 years (IQR 35–59), the majority were male (56%), single (52%), had a diagnosis of a psychotic disorder (33%), and were outpatients (66%) at the time the blood sample indicative of diabetes was taken. Males and females differed statistically significantly in BMI (median of 31 vs. 33, p = 0.014), marital status (p = 0.033), and the number of general hospital outpatient visits (median of 5 vs. 10, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This chart review study was based on EHR data from the Psychiatric Services of the Central Denmark Region. Among patients with laboratory values indicating incident diabetes, clinical action was taken in only 37%, most commonly referral to a general practitioner. Antidiabetic treatment was initiated in just 6% of cases. BMI < 25 kg/m2 was statistically significantly associated with the absence of intervention.

A persistent challenge in both psychiatric and general care is the discrepancy between biochemical evidence of disease and its formal diagnostic registration [22, 23]. In this study, approximately one in six of patients initially appeared to have incident diabetes based solely on laboratory findings and the absence of a recorded diagnosis or antidiabetic treatment. However, manual review of clinical notes revealed that these patients were already known to have diabetes. This finding highlights a significant gap in diagnostic documentation, consistent with prior studies that reported similar patterns of under‐registration and misclassification [24, 25]. Such omissions compromise both research accuracy and clinical care, as unregistered conditions may be overlooked in routine follow‐up, leading to treatment gaps and an elevated risk of morbidity and premature mortality.

The age distribution of patients with laboratory values indicating incident diabetes reported in this study further illustrates the potential delay between biochemical detection and formal diagnosis. Specifically, the median age at diabetes detection based on laboratory results was 48 years, which is substantially younger than the mean age at first recorded diabetes diagnosis in national registers—62 years for individuals with severe mental illness and 67 years for those without [18]. This approximately 14‐year discrepancy suggests that hyperglycaemia may be present—and often detectable—well before diabetes is coded in administrative registers. This may have important clinical implications, as unregistered diabetes may be overlooked in routine decision‐making, as well as methodological implications for register‐based research, where misclassification of disease onset can infer bias. The age span of in the present study (25th percentile: 35 years) also indicates that a substantial fraction may have early‐onset T2D that is associated with faster β‐cell decline, persistent hyperglycaemia, and a higher lifetime burden of microvascular and macrovascular complications [26], all of which substantially reduce quality of life [27]. Moreover, evidence shows that individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes at younger ages have a markedly higher risk of mortality relative to the general population compared with those diagnosed later in life, underscoring the particularly poor long‐term prognosis associated with early‐onset disease [28]. When combined with the cardiometabolic vulnerabilities associated with severe mental illness, this earlier onset likely contributes to substantial excess morbidity and mortality among individuals with psychiatric disorders.

Unfortunately, prior work suggests that individuals with mental illness are less likely to receive comprehensive diabetes care [18] and management of associated conditions, such as hypertension [29] than the general population. In the present study, only 8% of the 37% of cases that received some form of intervention were initiated on antidiabetic medication, a proportion markedly lower than those reported in large primary care cohorts. For example, Gopalan et al. found that among adults with newly diagnosed T2D, approximately 70% of younger‐onset patients (21–44 years) and 60% of mid‐age‐onset patients (45–64 years) began metformin therapy within 1 year [30]. While differences in population, follow‐up time, and care context limit direct comparability, the contrast underscores a profound missed treatment opportunity in psychiatric settings.

In the present study, we found that among those where any clinical actions based on laboratory values indicating incident diabetes were taken, 27% were referred to their GP, suggesting that psychiatric services rely on referral to primary care as the main pathway for diabetes follow‐up, based on the assumption that GPs will subsequently evaluate and manage the abnormal findings. However, evidence from a psychiatric outpatient clinic focusing on metabolic screening suggests that only about one‐third of referred patients contact their GP in response to such referral [31]. Structural barriers, fragmented care pathways, and reduced help‐seeking behaviours among individuals with severe mental illness can disrupt the transition from diabetes detection to actual diabetes treatment. Given that early pharmacological intervention is associated with improved glycaemic control and reduced long‐term risk of complications [32], these findings highlight an urgent need for integrated care models and proactive diabetes management strategies for individuals with psychiatric disorders.

The present study also identified patient factors associated with a lack of intervention. Specifically, we found that patients with a BMI < 25 kg/m2 (i.e., non‐overweight) had much higher odds of no intervention (OR 3.82). One possible explanation is that clinicians may perceive diabetes risk as primarily linked to obesity, which could inadvertently reduce attention to abnormal glucose levels in patients with normal weight. Irrespective of the underlying mechanism, this finding is worrisome because diabetes can occur at any weight, and evidence even suggests that individuals with normal BMI at the time of diagnosis have significantly higher all‐cause mortality compared with those who are overweight or obese, with adjusted hazard ratios for total mortality more than double after multivariable adjustment [33]. Therefore, in the present study, patients with diabetes who were not obese were disproportionately overlooked—an inequity that must be addressed.

Taken together, the findings of the present study highlight an urgent need to strengthen diabetes care within psychiatric services. Our findings also underscore the need for clearer clinical responsibility when abnormal metabolic results are detected in psychiatric settings, including defined procedures for documenting, communicating, and initiating follow‐up actions across care sectors. Abnormal glucose findings or risk estimates derived from machine‐learning models [34] should trigger a structured action plan, supported by electronic alerts that prompt clinicians to respond. Psychiatrists should be equipped—through targeted training and clear protocols—to initiate safe, first‐line treatment such as metformin, when indicated. Structured behavioural interventions, optimization of antipsychotic treatment when metabolically feasible [35], and coordinated lifestyle psychosocial programs represent important levers to improve physical health in this population [36]. Moreover, evidence‐informed models of integrated metabolic care should be implemented, including onsite screening pathways, multidisciplinary clinics, and case management approaches tailored to the cognitive and social vulnerabilities common in individuals with severe mental illness [37]. Finally, national and regional clinical guidelines are needed to stratify patients by risk and specify the roles of each sector, ensuring that no abnormal result goes unmanaged. Strengthening these systems is critical to reducing the excess cardiovascular mortality faced by patients receiving psychiatric care.

The present study had limitations that should be acknowledged. First, despite focusing on newly identified abnormal glucose values, some cases labelled as incident might actually be unrecorded (and hence prevalent) diabetes, as the data were confined to the CDR and covered only a five‐year span. While this could lead to misclassification, any detection of abnormal glucose should still trigger clinical evaluation. Second, a diagnosis of diabetes requires confirmatory testing according to international guidelines in cases where clear clinical diagnosis is absent [38]. This was not consistently documented in the medical records. Moreover, details of importance for interpretation of the laboratory test results, such as fasting status, were unavailable, limiting the interpretation of abnormal glucose values, which may also have reflected transient dysregulation related to acute mental illness or physiological stress rather than persistent hyperglycaemia. Third, the retrospective chart review design limits the completeness and accuracy of intervention data. Clinical actions may have occurred but gone undocumented. For example, follow‐up initiated by psychiatric staff (e.g., lifestyle counselling or advising contact with primary care) may not have been recorded in the EHR. Likewise, follow‐up performed in primary care—such as treatment initiation—was not available in our dataset. These factors likely led to underestimation of true intervention rates. Fourth, although follow‐up time was only 3 months, the median time to action, when such was undertaken, was considerably shorter than 3 months, suggesting that extending the observation period would be unlikely to reveal substantially more interventions triggered by the abnormal index result. Fifth, we did not distinguish type 1 diabetes from T2D, but the former is expected to be relatively rare in this adult psychiatric population. Finally, the findings may not generalize to other healthcare systems with markedly different structures, levels of access to care, or degrees of integration between psychiatric and general medical services. However, the observed pattern that is., limited documentation of metabolic follow up despite clear laboratory indicators—likely has relevance for comparable settings, such as psychiatric services in other Nordic countries.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a clear gap between the identification of laboratory values indicating incident diabetes and the documentation of subsequent clinical action in the EHR. Nearly one in six patients with incident diabetes had no registered diagnosis in their EHR, and only a minority of newly detected cases received any form of clinical intervention. Most documented actions were referrals to primary care, while direct treatment initiation within psychiatric services was uncommon. Patients with a BMI below 25 kg/m2 were particularly unlikely to receive an intervention, highlighting an important high‐risk subgroup that may be overlooked. These findings underscore the need for clearer, threshold‐triggered pathways and improved cross‐sector documentation to ensure timely and consistent metabolic care for patients with mental illness.
