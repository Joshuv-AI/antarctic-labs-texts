Impact of Hospital Environmental Noise on Sleep Quality and Rehabilitation Outcomes in Patients with Acute Leukaemia Receiving Chemotherapy
Authors: XiHui Zhou, YanQin Mao, Zhen Yao, Jing Li, Rong Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:For patients with acute leukaemia receiving chemotherapy, hospital noise may affect sleep and hinder recovery. Understanding these effects and exploring mitigation strategies are crucial for enhancing patient rehabilitation.Objective:This work aimed to evaluate the effects of hospital environmental noise on sleep quality and rehabilitation outcomes amongst patients with acute leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy, providing evidence for improvement in the ward environment.Methods:A retrospective cohort study included 102 patients with acute leukaemia treated between January 2023 and March 2025. On the basis of the noise level of the environment, patients were divided into a high-noise group (n = 52) and a low-noise group (n = 50), receiving equivalent treatment. Primary outcomes were the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Leeds Sleep Evaluation Questionnaire (LSEQ) and bone marrow puncture results. Secondary indicators included perceived noise sources, Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS), Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) and haematologic parameters. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 25.0). Independent sample t-test or Wilcoxon rank sum test was used for continuous variables, and chi-square test or analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used for categorical variables. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.Results:The 24-hour average noise levels of the high- and low-noise groups were 59.9 and 50.5 dBA, respectively. The high-noise group showed significantly higher PSQI, DERS and ISI scores and lower LSEQ scores (P < 0.05). Four weeks after treatment, the high-noise group had higher blast + promyelocyte proportions, longer time to reach <5%, slower white blood cell/neutrophil/platelet count recovery and less C-reactive protein reduction (P < 0.05). The main noise sources were caregivers/visitors and snoring.Conclusion:Hospital environmental noise significantly impairs sleep quality and delays bone marrow recovery in patients with acute leukaemia receiving chemotherapy, negatively affecting physiological rehabilitation and treatment efficacy.

INTRODUCTION

The influence of hospital environmental noise on the sleep quality and rehabilitation outcomes of patients with acute leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy is an important clinical issue that involves the interdisciplinary fields of environmental medicine oncology and rehabilitation science. Hospitalised patients generally face sleep disorders, and patients with acute leukaemia are particularly vulnerable during high-intensity chemotherapy. Their physiological rhythms and immune function are already impaired, and external environmental factors, such as noise, may further exacerbate this problem.[1] As a crucial physiological process for bodily repair, immune regulation and psychological adjustment, sleep is essential for the rehabilitation of patients undergoing chemotherapy.[2] Studies indicated that decreased sleep quality can exacerbate chemotherapy-related fatigue, increase infection risk, reduce treatment compliance and even affect the metabolism and efficacy of chemotherapeutic drugs.[3] Rischer et al.[4] demonstrated that patients undergoing haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation experience multiple symptoms during the acute phase, adversely affecting sleep quality. Hospitals, as the core venue for disease treatment and rehabilitation, directly influence patients’ physiological and psychological states through their environmental quality.[5] Amongst environmental factors, noise, as a ubiquitous environmental stressor, has become a substantial public health concern for medical institutions globally. Münzel et al.[6] indicated that environmental noise affects cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases through various pathways, including sleep disruption, nervous system activation and inflammatory responses.

Sleep is a complex physiological process that includes two stages: non-rapid eye movement (NREM) and rapid eye movement. The deep sleep stage in NREM sleep is crucial for body repair.[7] Noise is transmitted through the auditory system to the central nervous system, activating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to increased secretion of stress hormones (such as cortisol), disrupting the normal transition of the sleep cycle, reducing deep sleep time and increasing the number of awakenings.[8] Long-term sleep disorders may lead to health problems such as decreased immune function and increased risk of cardiovascular disease.[9] Research on hospitalised patients has shown a positive correlation between noise levels and sleep disorders.[10] Current research on hospital noise mostly focuses on general internal medicine patients or those in the intensive care unit, with relatively scarce studies targeting the specific population of patients with acute leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy. Luskin et al.[11] preliminarily revealed sleep disturbances in patients with myelodysplastic syndromes and acute leukaemia. Yu et al.[12] examined sleep disturbances amongst young and middle-aged patients with acute leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy, adopting a biopsychosocial viewpoint. Bagheri-Nesami et al.[13] studied sleep quality and related risk factors amongst Iranian patients with leukaemia receiving chemotherapy. Patients with acute leukaemia require prolonged hospitalisation during chemotherapy, and their sensitivity to the environment may vary across different treatment phases (e.g., induction, remission and consolidation therapy). Therefore, the effect of noise on their sleep and rehabilitation may exhibit unique patterns and mechanisms. Thus, in-depth investigations into the association between hospital environmental noise and sleep quality/rehabilitation outcomes in patients with acute leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy hold considerable clinical importance.

Hospital noise originates from various sources, broadly categorised into equipment-generated, human-generated and structural noises.[14] Equipment-generated noise includes sounds from medical devices such as monitor alarms, infusion pump operation and ventilator airflow. These sounds often have specific frequencies and intensities, potentially causing continuous disturbance to patients. Human-generated noise encompasses conversations during staff operations, conversations amongst patients and families and visitor movements. This type of noise is typically irregular and unpredictable, easily causing alertness and anxiety in patients. Structural noise relates to the sound insulation of ward building materials and water flow in pipes. Structural noise may be more noticeable at night, affecting patient sleep.[15] Regarding noise characteristics, hospital noise exhibits ‘continuous’ and ‘impulsive’ features.[16] The equivalent continuous noise level (Leq) reflects the overall continuous noise level, whereas the maximum noise level (Lmax) indicates the peak intensity of impulsive noise.[17] Studies showed that even when the overall Leq is at a moderate level, frequent impulsive high-level noises (e.g., nighttime alarms or object collisions) are disruptive to patient sleep.[18]

This study aimed to characterise ward noise levels in chemotherapy-treated patients with acute leukaemia (via noise level measurement and source survey), analyse the correlation between noise and sleep quality [by comparing Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), Leeds Sleep Evaluation Questionnaire (LSEQ) scores and Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) results between high- and low-noise groups], explore sleep quality’s mediating role in the noise–rehabilitation outcome relationship [using Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) for emotion regulation, bone marrow puncture indices and blood parameters to assess rehabilitation] and identify key patient-perceived noise sources. The results could inform ward environment optimisation and personalised nursing to improve treatment compliance and medical service quality.

METHODS

Study Subjects

This retrospective cohort study included medical records of 102 acute leukaemia patients who were treated at The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between January 2023 and March 2025. The data of these patients were collected and analysed by researchers who were not involved in patient care at the beginning of this study, 123 patients were included, of which 21 patients were excluded due to serious complications during chemotherapy or recent use of sedatives and hypnotic drugs. The 24-hour Leq in the wards was measured using noise dosimeters. The 24-hour Leq of patient ward was counted. On the basis of previous studies,[19] 52 patients with 24-hour Leq ≥ 55 dBA in the ward were categorised into a high-noise group, and 50 patients with 24-hour Leq < 55 dBA were categorised into a low-noise group. By comparing various indicators between the high-noise group and low-noise group, this study sought to examine how hospital environmental noise affects sleep quality and rehabilitation outcomes in patients with acute leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy. Inclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 20–60 years; (2) acute leukaemia (encompassing acute lymphoblastic leukaemia and acute myeloid leukaemia) confirmed by bone marrow puncture and pathological examination; (3) currently receiving chemotherapy; (4) hospitalisation duration ≥3 days (to exclude transient effects of acute environmental adaptation on sleep) and (5) no history of severe mental illness, cognitive impairment or dysaudia.

Exclusion Criteria

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) combined severe failure of vital organs (e.g., heart, liver and kidney), (2) development of severe complications during chemotherapy (e.g., sepsis and intracranial haemorrhage) and (3) recent use of sedative-hypnotic drugs (which may interfere with sleep assessment results).

Ethical Statement

In compliance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki,[20] this clinical study was reviewed and approved by The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University Ethics Committee before it began (approval number: 2025-972). The study’s purpose, processes and possible risks were fully communicated to subjects or their proxies by researchers, who then secured written informed consent.

Sample Size Estimation

This research was a retrospective controlled clinical trial, where participants were stratified into a high-noise group and a control group in accordance with the levels of environmental noise. For data analysis, independent sample t-test was employed, and G-power software (Version 3.1; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) was utilised to conduct sample size estimation.[21] The parameters were set as follows: on the basis of previous studies,[22] the effect size was selected as 0.6, with sleep quality and rehabilitation outcomes as the main outcomes, two-tailed significance level (α) = 0.05 and statistical power (1−β) = 0.8. The calculation revealed a need for 36 participants per group (72 in total). Ultimately, this study included 102 participants, which not only exceeded the target sample size but also met all the study’s statistical requirements.

Patient Treatment Measures

Data extraction confirmed that both groups received treatment in specialised haematology wards by the same team, with consistent regimens, drug doses, supportive care and nursing protocols. Medical records showed no sedative-hypnotic drug use, avoiding sleep assessment interference.

A retrospective review revealed that the low-noise group’s wards had pre-existing noise reduction measures, such as staff whispering during rounds, daytime-concentrated non-urgent procedures, restricted caregivers/visitors, door/window sound insulation, shock-absorbing cart wheels and three daily nurse compliance checks. The high-noise group followed routine ward management without additional noise reduction interventions.

Data Collection Methods

Noise Level Measurement

This study used a noise dosimeter (Spark 703; Larson Davis 703 series, PCB Group Co., Provo, UT, USA) to measure ward noise levels.

Measurement location: The dosimeter was fixed at the patient’s bedside, 1.5 m above the floor (simulating ear level).

Retrospective data extraction showed that noise measurements were based on existing ward monitoring records. A randomly selected continuous 24-hour period during each patient’s hospitalisation was extracted, with each ward having been measured three times at the same fixed location on 3 non-consecutive days. The average of the three 24-hour measurements was used as the final noise exposure data, serving to reduce random errors and improve assessment reliability.

Collected indicators: Leq and Lmax were recorded, precise to 0.1 dBA.

Noise Source Survey

A self-administered questionnaire was used during hospitalisation to collect patients’ perceptions of the main noise sources. Options included the following: other patients’ family caregivers or visitors, snoring/moaning amongst other patients, toilet flushing, treatment/food cart movement, telephone/television sound, medical equipment operation and healthcare staff operational conversations. Patients could select multiple options.[23] The main noise sources differed slightly between the two groups. The high-noise group was predominantly affected by noise from caregivers/visitors and other patients’ snoring, whereas the low-noise group had lower exposure intensity to these two types of noise, which was consistent with the measured noise level differences.

Sleep Quality Assessment

Sleep quality was assessed using the PSQI questionnaire and LSEQ.[23] The questionnaire collection time was after 4 weeks of treatment.

PSQI was developed by Buysse et al.[24] Cross-cultural validation was conducted by Tsai et al.[25] in the Chinese population, showing good reliability and validity (Cronbach’s α = 0.82–0.83, test-retest reliability r = 0.85, sensitivity of 98.3% and specificity of 90.2% when the total score >5 points as the cutoff). The scale consists of seven dimensions, with a total score range of 0–21 points. The higher the score, the worse the sleep quality. On the basis of Park et al.’s 2014 study,[23] a PSQI total score ≥5 was used as the diagnostic criterion for sleep disturbance in this study.

The LSEQ scale was developed by Parrott and Hindmarch[26] for clinical sleep assessment. At present, no standardised reliability and validity study has been conducted in Mainland China. The present study ensured semantic equivalence through translation back translation procedures. The scale consists of four dimensions, with a total score range of 0–100 points. The higher the score, the better the sleep quality.

Rehabilitation Outcome Assessment

Bone marrow puncture results, including marrow cellularity, percentage of blasts + promyelocytes, and time to bone marrow remission, were collected at patient admission and 4 weeks after treatment.

Emotion Regulation Ability Assessment

DERS is used to assess emotional regulation ability. This scale was developed by Gratz and Roemer[27] and validated in the Chinese population, with good reliability and validity (Cronbach’s α = 0.89, test-retest reliability r = 0.87 and six-factor structural fit index CFI = 0.93). DERS was used to survey patients’ emotional status.[28] It consists of 36 items comprising six domains: lack of emotional awareness (Aware), lack of emotional clarity (Clarity), non-acceptance of emotional responses (Non-accept), impulse control difficulties (Impulse), difficulties engaging in goal-directed behaviour (Goals) and limited access to emotion regulation strategies (Strategies). Each item is scored 1–5 (1 = almost never, 5 = almost always). Each domain score ranges from 6 to 30, with total scores ranging from 36 to 180. A higher score reflects more significant difficulties in regulating emotions. Assessments were conducted at admission (pre-treatment) and 4 weeks after treatment (post-treatment).

Insomnia Severity Assessment

ISI was used to assess the severity of insomnia. This scale was developed by Bastien et al.[29] and validated by Yu[30] in the Chinese population, showing good reliability and validity (Cronbach’s α = 0.81–0.91, test-retest reliability r = 0.78 and correlation coefficient with PSQI r = 0.72). It contains seven items assessing insomnia-related symptoms over the past 2 weeks: difficulty falling asleep, difficulty staying asleep, early-morning awakening, sleep dissatisfaction, interference with daytime functioning, noticeability of impairment due to sleep problems and level of distress caused by sleep problems. Each item is scored 0–4 (0 = none, 4 = very severe), with a total score range of 0–28. The scoring criteria are as follows: 0–7 (no clinically significant insomnia), 8–14 (mild insomnia), 15–21 (moderate insomnia) and 22–28 (severe insomnia). Assessments were conducted at admission and 4 weeks after treatment.

Blood Parameter Survey

The core indicators related to immune function, inflammatory status and stress levels in patients with acute leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy were selected. At admission (pre-treatment) and 4 weeks after the start of treatment (post-treatment), venous blood samples were collected to conduct relevant tests. The specific indicators were as follows: white blood cell (WBC) count reflects basic immune status, with normal reference range of (3.5–9.5) × 10⁹/L[31]; neutrophil (NEUT) count assesses anti-infection capability, with normal reference range of (1.1–6.4) × 10⁹/L[31]; platelet (PLT) count reflects coagulation function and bone marrow haematopoietic rehabilitation, with normal reference range of (136–407) × 10⁹/L[31]; and C-reactive protein (CRP) is a marker of inflammatory response, with normal reference value of <10 mg/L.[32]

Statistical Analysis

SPSS (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) software was used for statistical analysis. An independent sample t-test was applied for intra-group comparison. For continuous variables, Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used for normality testing. If they followed a normal distribution, they were represented as mean ± standard deviation. Independent sample t-test was also used for inter-group comparison. Continuous variables with skewed distributions were represented as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using Wilcoxon rank sum test (Mann–Whitney U test equivalent), with Z as the test statistic. For categorical variables, data were expressed in frequency (percentage) [n (%)] and compared between groups by using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

The study results showed that the 24-hour Leq values were 59.9 dBA in the high-noise group and 50.5 dBA in the low-noise group. Sustained high noise stimulates patients’ auditory systems, disrupts sleep rhythms and causes structural disorders, such as reduced deep sleep and increased awakenings, thereby impairing sleep quality. The high-noise group had an average Lmax of 79.5 dBA (range: 73.3–85.6 dBA), and the low-noise group had 69.2 dBA (range: 59.9–78.7 dBA), indicating frequent sudden high noises in the ward, such as medical equipment alarms. Previous studies have shown that such unpredictable, sudden noises are more disruptive to sleep, easily disrupting sleep homeostasis, leading to sleep fragmentation and exacerbating sleep disorders.[33] Bertollo et al.[34] systematically confirmed that sleep quality is a core protective factor in regulating patients’ mental health (such as depression and anxiety) and health outcomes. High-quality sleep stabilises the HPA axis function, maintains neurotransmitter (such as serotonin and dopamine) balance and reduces the risk of emotional disorders. For patients with acute leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy, their physical weakness and neuroendocrine disorders are caused by the disease and treatment side effects, and their sensitivity to noise is much higher than that of the general population.[353637] In the high-noise group, the high Lmax is more likely to trigger stress reactions in patients. It activates the HPA axis and promotes the secretion of stress-related hormones, such as cortisol, an effect that not only interferes with sleep but may also exert a suppressive impact on immune function, which is unfavourable to patients’ recovery.[38]

This study assessed sleep quality via PSQI and LSEQ. The high-noise group had significantly higher PSQI scores and lower LSEQ scores (both P < 0.05), indicating poorer sleep quality. PSQI covers seven domains (e.g., sleep duration and efficiency), with lower scores reflecting better sleep. LSEQ includes four dimensions (e.g., falling/maintaining sleep), with higher scores indicating better sleep. The high-noise group’s results suggest multidimensional sleep impairments: persistent high noise prolongs sleep latency, disrupts sleep continuity and reduces deep sleep (critical for recovery). This finding aligns with that of Park et al.,[23] who found a 1.2-point PSQI increase per 10 dBA Leq increase in internal medicine patients. The results of the present study provided additional confirmation of the positive association between noise intensity and the extent of sleep disorders, and this also holds true in the special population of acute leukaemia chemotherapy. Overall, the significant difference in PSQI scores clearly confirmed the negative effect of hospital environmental noise on the sleep quality of chemotherapy-treated patients with acute leukaemia.

This study indicated that the total DERS score of the high-noise group (91.7 ± 6.6 post-treatment) was significantly higher than that of the low-noise group (79.6 ± 6.4, P < 0.05). No notable reduction was observed in the scores of six dimensions, including emotion perception and impulse control, indicating that noise exposure not only affects sleep but also exacerbates emotional regulation difficulties. Patients with acute leukaemia are prone to anxiety and depression due to disease threats and chemotherapy side effects (such as hair loss and nausea). Lack of sleep can further reduce the neural activity of the prefrontal cortex (responsible for emotional regulation), making it difficult for patients to effectively recognise and accept their own emotions, and more likely to exhibit impulsive behaviour (such as intolerance to medical procedures).[12] In their study of patients with depression, Javadzade et al.[28] confirmed a bidirectional positive correlation between sleep disorders and difficulty regulating emotions, and this correlation is more significant under disease stress condition. The ISI score of the high-noise group (12.6 ± 5.6 points after treatment) did not significantly decrease (P < 0.05), and 32.7% of patients were still in a state of moderate insomnia, which directly affects their daytime function (such as fatigue and lack of concentration). Papadopoulos et al.[39] conducted a study on patients with lung cancer undergoing chemotherapy and showed that the treatment interruption rate of patients with insomnia was 2.3 times higher than that of patients without insomnia, and the rehabilitation confidence score was significantly lower. This result is highly consistent with the slower rehabilitation of patients in the high-noise group in the present study, suggesting that improving emotions and insomnia symptoms may be a key link in breaking the negative effect of noise on rehabilitation.

This study found that noise harms patients’ subjective sleep and objective rehabilitation. The high-noise group had significantly prolonged bone marrow remission time (P < 0.05) and smaller reduction in primitive/promyelocyte proportion, indicating slower bone marrow recovery. Clinically meaningful, noise may disrupt immune reconstruction and bone marrow rehabilitation via poor sleep, triggering anxiety/depression and reducing treatment cooperation.[40] Lack of sleep can inhibit the activity of natural killer (NK) cells and reduce the body’s immune function.[41] However, patients with acute leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy already have impaired immune function, and disordered sleep can lead to a further increase in the risk of infection and delay the rehabilitation of bone marrow haematopoietic function.[1242] Prior to the treatment in the present study, no significant discrepancy was observed in the proportion of blasts plus promyelocytes between the two groups (P > 0.05). Following the treatment, the proportions of primitive cells + promyelocytes were 4.5 ± 3.2% in the low-noise group and 8.5 ± 4.1% in the high-noise group, with a statistically significant difference observed between these two groups (P < 0.05). The proportion of primitive cells + promyelocytes is a core indicator for evaluating the degree of remission in patients with acute leukaemia after chemotherapy. A proportion of primitive cells + promyelocytes <5% is generally considered as one of the important indicators of bone marrow remission.[43] Following treatment, the proportion of primitive cells + promyelocytes in the low-noise group approached the remission standard, whereas this proportion in the high-noise group remained significantly higher than that in the low-noise group. This finding suggests that after chemotherapy, the rate of abnormal cell clearance in the bone marrow of patients in the high-noise group was reduced, and the process of bone marrow remission was impeded. The possible mechanism is that hospital environmental noise causes sleep disorders in patients, and insufficient sleep inhibits the activity of immune cells such as NK cells, reduces the body’s immune function and diminishes the body’s capacity to clear abnormal haematopoietic cells. Sleep disorders may affect the metabolism and efficacy of chemotherapy drugs, reduce the killing effect of drugs on tumour cells and lead to a slow decrease in the proportion of primitive cells + promyelocytes, thus delaying bone marrow remission. The differences in bone marrow puncture related indicators in this study fully indicate that hospital environmental noise interferes with patients’ sleep quality, thereby affecting immune function and chemotherapy efficacy, delaying the rehabilitation of bone marrow function and the overall rehabilitation process of chemotherapy-treated patients with acute leukaemia. This result provides a key clinical basis for clinical attention and control of hospital environmental noise.

In this study, the rehabilitation rate of blood indicators (such as WBC, NEUT and PLT) in the high-noise group was significantly slower than that in the low noise group, and the decrease in CRP levels was smaller, indicating limited immune function rehabilitation and persistent inflammatory status. The good rehabilitation of WBC and NEUT means that the patient’s bone marrow haematopoietic function and immune function have been more effectively improved, which can better resist risks such as infection. The increase in PLT reduces the risk of bleeding, and safely passing through the chemotherapy period is beneficial to patients. As an inflammatory indicator, the decrease in CRP indicates that the inflammatory response in the patient’s body is better controlled. These changes may be related to the imbalance of neuroimmune regulation caused by sleep disorders. Hospital environmental noise may affect patient rehabilitation through multiple pathways. Firstly, noise can trigger stress reactions in patients, leading to hormonal imbalances in the body, including increased cortisol levels.[44] This hormonal imbalance subsequently suppresses immune function and affects the proliferation and differentiation of haematopoietic cells, resulting in delayed rehabilitation of WBC, NEUT and PLT. Secondly, noise interferes with patients’ rest and sleep, and insufficient sleep can disrupt the body’s immune regulation and tissue repair mechanisms. This disruption of immune and repair mechanisms not only hinders the rehabilitation of haematopoietic function but may also prolong the inflammatory response, leading to a slow decrease in CRP.[45] In addition, long-term exposure to noisy environments can easily lead to adverse emotions, such as anxiety and irritability, thus hindering the rehabilitation process.

Limitations and Future Work

Whilst this study yielded certain meaningful findings, it is not without limitations. Firstly, a retrospective cohort design was employed, which may be susceptible to selection bias and confounding variables. Secondly, the study’s sample size was relatively small, and no long-term follow-up was carried out, which prevents an understanding of the long-term effects of hospital environmental noise on sleep and prognosis amongst patients with acute leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy. Thirdly, the LSEQ scale used in this study has not been validated on a large scale in Mainland China, although it has been ensured semantic equivalence through translation back translation procedures. Fourthly, this study mainly infers noise effects through mean or proportion, and more rigorous research on statistical correlations may be needed in the future. Its psychometric characteristics should be validated in larger samples. A prospective randomised controlled trial design could be considered, along with expanding the sample size and conducting long-term investigations in the population of patients with acute leukaemia receiving chemotherapy. These measures could help further clarify the long-term effects of hospital environmental noise on sleep quality and rehabilitation outcomes in this patient group.

CONCLUSIONS

Hospital environmental noise can lead to a decrease in sleep quality and rehabilitation outcomes for patients with acute leukaemia undergoing chemotherapy. Sleep disorders caused by environmental noise can further delay the rehabilitation of bone marrow function in patients, which has adverse effects on their physiological rehabilitation and chemotherapy efficacy. This study systematically evaluated the multi-dimensional impact of hospital environmental noise on the rehabilitation of patients with acute leukaemia after chemotherapy, covering key indicators such as sleep, immune function, psychological state, and clinical outcomes. The research not only reveals the important role of noise as a controllable environmental factor in haematology wards but also provides evidence-based support for the developing targeted treatments. These findings offer a new entry point for improving the inpatient environment and enhancing the quality of medical services, possessing strong clinical guidance significance and promotion value.
