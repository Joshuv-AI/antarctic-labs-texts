Molecular propensity and stress tolerance of dehydrins from desert plants
Authors: Mughair Abdul Aziz, Faical Brini, Olfa Jrad, Shafeeq Rahman, Manzoor Ahmad, Ranjit Vijayan, Khaled Masmoudi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDehydrins (DHNs) are hydrophilic proteins that accumulate in plant vegetative tissues in response to abiotic stress and during the late stages of seed development. To investigate the genetic basis of stress tolerance in desert plants, full-length cDNA sequences encoding DHN proteins were isolated from three desert plant species including Prosopis cineraria (PcDHN1), Citrullus colocynthis (CcDHN1), and Phoenix dactylifera (PdDHN1 and PdDHN2). This study examined the structural characteristics and potential functional roles of these DHNs in relation to the abiotic stress adaptation of desert plants. Due to their evolution in arid environments, these desert plant DHNs showed unique structural adaptations and functional stability under severe abiotic stress conditions. The sequence analysis identified PcDHN1 and CcDHN1 genes as encoding for Y2SK2- and Y3SK2-type DHNs, respectively, while PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 for FSK3-type DHNs. All four proteins demonstrated strong hydrophilicity, with grand average of hydropathy (GRAVY) scores of less than 0, indicating their water-binding ability under dehydration stress. Structural modelling predicted extensive disordered regions, with random coils and short α-helix as the major structural features. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis suggested that these DHNs may play key roles in cellular regulation, stress response, and metabolic processes. Functional characterization through heterologous expression in yeast knockout mutants (Cdc25 and AXT3K strains) showed a significant enhancement in heat and salinity stress tolerance. AXT3K yeast cells expressing DHNs exhibited a substantial accumulation of K+ over Na+ in saline conditions, resulting in a four-fold increase in the K+/Na+ ratio. Enzymatic assays demonstrated that the DHNs effectively prevented the inactivation of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) under heat stress, dehydration-rehydration, and freeze–thaw cycles, reducing LDH aggregation by 20% to 70%. These DHNs can have a larger ability for osmoprotection and protein stabilization compared to DHNs from non-desert plant species, due to their naturally evolved mechanisms for overcoming extreme abiotic stresses. Thus, our study specifies the potential of desert plant DHNs to enhance abiotic stress tolerance, particularly for developing tolerant crops and stabilizing enzymes under different constraints of reactional processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33879-7.Keywords: DHN, Desert plants, Abiotic stress, Yeast, Thermostability, LDHSubject terms: Plant biotechnology, Molecular biology, Enzyme mechanisms

Introduction

Abiotic stresses heavily inhibit plant growth and productivity. To combat these adverse conditions, plants initiate complex adaptive responses, mainly regulated through the activation of stress-responsive genes. These genes encode for specific protein molecules that play essential roles in plant, specifically enabling them to survive under harsh environmental conditions1. Developing an understanding of these stress-regulating proteins is crucial for improving crop tolerance and enhancing agricultural productivity under climatic challenges. One important group among these proteins is the Group-2 Late Embryogenesis Abundant (LEA2) proteins with its distinct biochemical subgroup known as LEA-D11 or Dehydrins (DHNs)2. These proteins are characterized by a high proportion of charged and polar amino acids, contributing to their exceptional structural flexibility3. DHNs are intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs), which do not have specific secondary or tertiary structures, allowing them to adapt and bind to a diverse range of molecular targets, including proteins, nucleic acids, cellular membranes, and metal ions4. This flexibility enables DHNs to engage in various molecular recognition processes, forming transient interactions in complex protein networks that are essential for plant stress tolerance5.

DHNs are categorized by several conserved sequence motifs namely, the K-, Y-, and S-segments, which are integral to their function in stress tolerance. The K-segments, with the consensus sequence of lys-rich motif (EKKGIMDKIKEKLPG), are found in all DHNs and play a role in binding to anionic phospholipid vesicles, which is facilitated by the formation of an amphipathic helix in this region6. The Y-segments, identified by the core sequence (DEYGNP), are located at the N-terminal region of many DHNs with a strong resemblance to nucleotide-binding sites found in plant and bacterial chaperones7. The serine rich-segment, identified by 7 serine residues (LHRSGSSSSSSSEDD), are hypothesized to influence the nuclear localization of DHNs through phosphorylation, which could support their involvement in nuclear stress responses, possibly by interacting with nuclear structures or transcriptional machinery that regulate stress-response pathways8. In addition to these conserved segments, DHNs often consists of less conserved φ-segments. These φ-segments, which are rich in polar amino acids, glycine, and sometimes proline and alanine, appear in one or more copies between K-segments2. Moreover, a new F-segment has been identified based on the pair of hydrophobic residues it contains (DRGLFDFLGKK)9. Based on the different conserved segments, DHNs can be classified into six distinct groups, such as Kn, SKn, KnS, YnSKn, FSKn, and YnKn.

Plants activate specific DHN genes in different vegetative tissues for enhancing abiotic stress tolerance. In crops like Oryza sativa, rice, OsDHN1 were found to provide increased tolerance to both drought and salt stress, enhancing their growth10,11. DHNs are further known to provide protective functions within cells, acting as molecular chaperones or shields to protect essential enzymes and phospholipids under harsh conditions. For instance, the DHNs ERD10 and ERD14 showed chaperone activities with broad substrate specificity and were able to bind phospholipid vesicles through electrostatic interactions, a property that is particularly essential during cold stress12. In Physcomitrella patens, DHN proteins PpDHNA and PpDHNB have been shown to protect enzyme activity under osmotic and freezing conditions13. Additionally, in Arabidopsis, the KS-type DHN AtHIRD11 has been observed to restore the enzymatic activity after it was denatured by copper ions (Cu2⁺), demonstrating DHNs capacity to prevent heavy metal stress by stabilizing enzyme structures14.

While several studies have been reported on DHN genes in model plants, little is known about them in desert plant species. In desert ecosystems, three prominent species, Prosopis cineraria (Ghaf tree)15, Citrullus colocynthis (Desert squash)16, and Phoenix dactylifera (Date palm)17, not only show abiotic stress tolerance but are also vital to habitat preservation and ecological stability. Genomic analysis of these plants, particularly of their DHN genes, can show key regulatory elements that contribute to their tolerance to adverse conditions. This could have broad applications, from advancing the conservation of desert ecosystems to enhancing breeding efforts aimed at improving abiotic stress tolerance in plant species. It is essential not only for understanding plant adaptation at a molecular level but also for using the role of stress tolerance genes such as DHNs to overcome the challenges in sustainable agriculture and food security due to climate changes.

The aim of this study was to conduct a comparative analysis of the four cloned DHN genes, focusing on the physicochemical properties, disorder propensity, and conserved structural motifs of DHN proteins. Additionally, to assess the DHNs capacity to enhance tolerance to high heat and salinity stresses, the DHN genes were overexpressed in two specific mutant yeast strains, Cdc25 and AXT3K. Recombinant DHN proteins were also produced in Escherichia coli to further investigate their functional roles. The study analysed the protective effects of these recombinant proteins on the enzymatic activities and stability of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) under elevated stress conditions, focusing on their potential as molecular shields. Through examining both the structural characteristics and stress-tolerance capabilities of these DHNs, our study contributes towards understanding the implications of DHNs for developing stress-tolerant crops and biotechnological applications in adverse environments.

Results

Isolation of the DHN genes from the desert plant species

The isolated DHN genes from the three desert plants consisted of the conserved domain of pfam00257 and superfamily (cl20205), indicating them to be encoding for DHN proteins. The full-length coding sequence (CDS) of PcDHN1 gene was 672 bp with an unknown chromosomal location. In addition, the CcDHN1 gene was 513 bp and its mRNA sequence was deposited in National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank under the accession number PQ538790. The PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 genes were 678 bp and 705 bp located on chromosomes 5 and 2, determined based on a BLAST search against the Barhee BC4 reference genome assembly of P. dactylifera (GCA_009389715.1).

Physicochemical properties of the desert plant DHN proteins

The molecular weights of the desert plant DHN proteins varied between 18.30 kDa and 25.91 kDa. Among these, PdDHN2 exhibited the highest molecular weight of 25.91 kDa with 234 amino acids, while CcDHN1 had the lowest molecular weight of 18.30 kDa, consisting of 170 amino acids (Table 1). Analysis of the physicochemical properties revealed that the isoelectric point (pI) of the isolated DHN proteins were relatively high, ranging from 5.23 to 8.70, indicating moderate to basic nature of these proteins. The GRAVY index, which shows the hydrophobicity of a protein, indicated that all the DHN proteins were hydrophilic (GRAVY < 0), suggesting that they are likely to be soluble in aqueous environments. In terms of protein stability, PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 were classified as unstable proteins, with instability indices of 61.65 and 61.20, respectively. In contrast, PcDHN1 and CcDHN1 were relatively stable, with instability indices of 23 and 29.89, respectively. The aliphatic index, which indicates the relative volume occupied by aliphatic residues in the protein, was higher for PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 (59.33 and 53.38) compared to PcDHN1 and CcDHN1 (41.57 and 32.71). This suggests that PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 may possess more aliphatic side chains, contributing to structural rigidity and stability under stress conditions. In the amino acid compositions, the number of acidic and basic amino acids was approximately balanced in PcDHN1 and CcDHN1. However, PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 showed a higher proportion of acidic amino acid residues compared to basic ones, potentially influencing their interactions with other molecules and their role in stress tolerance.

Analysis of the DHN proteins phylogenetic relationship

The phylogenetic relationships among the DHN families from various plant species have been well-documented in previous studies. To provide further understanding of the DHNs from desert plants, a phylogenetic analysis was performed (Fig. 1). This analysis was based on the comparison of the isolated DHN proteins from a wide range of plant species, including model plants and crops. The resulting phylogenetic tree was divided into four specific groups, YnSKn, SKn, Kn, and KS-type of DHN. The first group, YnSKn (49.15%), predominantly consisted of DHNs from plants exhibiting the YnSKn type structure (Fig. 1). It was found that two of the desert plant DHNs, PcDHN1 and CcDHN1 were clustered within this group. Specifically, PcDHN1 was most closely related to DHNs from A. thaliana, Vitis vinifera, and Vitis yeshanensis, while CcDHN1 clustered with DHNs from C. lanatus, C. melo, and C. sativus. This suggests that PcDHN1 and CcDHN1 share evolutionary similarities with the DHNs from these species. The second group (SKn, 28.83%) comprised DHNs with the SKn structure, and it included both PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 from P. dactylifera (Fig. 1). Similar to PcDHN1, these proteins were also closely related to DHNs from V. vinifera and V. yeshanensis. It was found that none of the desert plant DHNs in the current study belonged to the third (Kn, 13.55%) or fourth (KS-type, 8.47%) group in the phylogenetic tree.

Characterization of conserved segments of the DHN proteins

Following the phylogenetic classification of the four-desert plant DHN proteins, we conducted a comparative analysis of their full-length amino acid sequences. This analysis revealed that certain segments of the DHN proteins were highly conserved across the different plants, specifically the Y-segment, S-segment, and K-segment, while other regions exhibited relatively low sequence identity (Fig. 2).

Full-length amino acid sequences of the four-desert plant DHNs with the different segments shown in various colors (peach—Y-segment; purple—S-segment; yellow—K-segment; green—F-segment; brown—poly lysine segment). A block diagram of the conserved segments in the four desert DHNs is below the alignment.

These conserved domains are important for the functional and structural integrity of DHN proteins, which are known for their role in stress tolerance. It was found that the PcDHN1 contained two Y-segments in its N-terminus, a single Ser-rich segment in the middle, and two K-segments in its C-terminus. This structure classified PcDHN1 as a Y2SK2-type DHN (Fig. 2). Similarly, CcDHN1 was composed of three Y-segments in the N-terminus, a Ser-rich region in the middle, and two K-segments at the C-terminus, categorizing it as a Y3SK2-type DHN (Fig. 2). In contrast, PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 from the date palm exhibited a slightly different pattern. These proteins contained a Ser-segment in the N-terminus, a poly lysine chain in the middle, and two K-segments in their C-terminus, indicating a distinct functional role. Furthermore, an F-segment motif was found in the N-terminus of both PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 (Fig. 2). This additional segment places these proteins into the FSK3-type of DHN. The presence of the F-segment in PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 may suggest a unique functional adaptation in response to the extreme environmental conditions in desert regions. The conserved segments identified in the DHN proteins of desert plants associated to the results of the phylogenetic analysis, confirming the consistency of the classification system.

Structural and disorder propensity of the DHN proteins

The secondary structure predictions for PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 using PSIPRED 4 revealed similarities between their structures. Disordered regions were found to be distributed throughout the structure of all the four proteins (Fig. 3). In addition, the random coil was also identified as major portions of these proteins. Furthermore, the most predominant folded secondary structure predicted in these proteins was the α-helix, particularly concentrated in the conserved K-segment, which is essential for forming amphipathic helices that contribute to their molecular chaperone-like activity (Fig. 3).

Molecular localization and functioning of desert plant DHNs

The localization of DHNs was predicted in various plant cell organelles through PSIPRED 4. For PcDHN1, the highest probability of localization was 0.65 in the nuclear region and 0.25 in the cytosol (Fig. 4). There was no significant localization predicted for other organelles like the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, or mitochondria. CcDHN1 showed 0.55 likelihood of localization in the cytosol and 0.45 in the nuclear region, while no significant localization was predicted in other organelles. Similarly, PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 displayed prediction of 0.875 and 0.642 to be localized in the nuclear region. It showed a minor cytosolic presence of 0.125 and 0.358 respectively, and for PdDHN1 and PdDHN2, there was negligible localization in other organelles.

Predictive localization of the desert plant DHN proteins in cellular regions of the plants. Cytosol and nucleus were the common cellular localization regions of the DHN proteins in the plants.

The four DHN proteins, PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 were predicted to exhibit overlapping roles in biological processes, cellular components and metabolic functions. In the biological processes, PcDHN1 exhibited a high posterior probability of 0.92 for metabolic process regulation and 0.913 for gene expression regulation (Fig. 5a). It showed active involvement in RNA biosynthetic process regulation (0.871) and RNA metabolic process regulation (0.749). While in the cellular component functioning, it was found to function in the nucleolus (0.672) and membranes (0.624). Furthermore, its role in metabolic processes showed nucleic acid binding (0.979), DNA binding (0.847), poly(A) RNA binding (0.833), and interactions with cytoskeletal proteins (0.906), actin (0.618), and GTPase proteins (0.58).

Overexpression of DHN genes in the mutant Cdc25 yeast cells under heat stress condition

The mutant yeast strain Cdc25 transformed with coding sequences of PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2, was analysed for heat stress tolerance (Fig. 6a). The assay involved serial dilutions of yeast cultures, which were plated on the rich Yeast extract, peptone, and galactose (YPG) medium containing galactose as the carbon source to induce the expression of the inserted DHN genes. Under the control condition of 25 °C, both yeast cells with the empty vector and those expressing the DHN genes demonstrated survival across all dilutions, indicating no significant difference in growth at the standard temperature (Fig. 6a). However, under the heat stress conditions of 30 °C, 35 °C, and 40 °C, a significant difference in cell growth was observed between the Cdc25 yeast strains containing the empty plasmid pYES2 and the recombinant DHN genes. Cdc25 yeast cells expressing the DHN proteins along with the wild-type yeast strain W303 showed continued growth, with no growth variation across the different heat stress conditions. In contrast, Cdc25 yeast cells with the empty vector exhibited significantly reduced or no growth, suggesting a loss of viability under heat stress.

Growth rate of transformed Cdc25 yeast strain under various heat stress conditions

The impact of different temperature stress conditions on the growth rate of the Cdc25 yeast strain was investigated using a growth curve generated through the measurement of OD at 600 nm over a 48-h period. As shown in Fig. 6b, under control condition of 25 °C, the biomass of yeast cells containing the empty vector, DHN transformed yeast cells, and the wild-type W303 strain all increased gradually from 0 to 48 h, indicating typical yeast growth behavior. However, under heat stress condition of 30 °C, a difference in the growth patterns emerged (Fig. 6c). The Cdc25 yeast cells containing the empty vector showed a significant reduction in growth, with little to no increase in biomass. In contrast, yeast cells transformed with DHN genes exhibited sustained growth under the same heat stress condition, with the highest growth observed in yeast cells expressing PdDHN1 across different time intervals, indicating superior growth compared to the empty vector control. Similar growth trends were observed at higher temperatures of 35 °C and 40 °C (Fig. 6d and e), where DHN expressing yeast and wild-type W303 strain displayed increased growth over time, while the empty vector cells showed no growth. Furthermore, the growth curve analysis revealed specific phases in the yeast’s development under both control and heat stress conditions. The lag phase occurred between 0 and 8 h, where yeast cells adapted to the environment. The log phase, determined by rapid cell division, lasted from 8 to 36 h. After 44 h of incubation, the yeast cells entered the stationary phase, where growth slowed, and cell division stabilized.

Cell density measurement of Cdc25 yeast strains under heat stress conditions

The growth of yeast colonies was further analyzed by measuring cell density at a 10–3 dilution for each stress condition, using ImageJ software for quantitative analysis. Under control conditions (25 °C), a slight but statistically significant difference in cell growth density (p < 0.05) was observed between DHN transformed yeast strains and the empty vector control (Fig. 6f). Tukey’s post-hoc test revealed no significant difference in growth density between yeasts transformed with PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1 and PdDHN2. However, PdDHN1 transformed Cdc25 yeast, exhibited slightly higher growth density of 8.3, indicating superior growth compared to the other DHN transformed Cdc25 yeast cells (Fig. 6f). Under heat stress conditions at 30 °C, 35 °C, and 40 °C, a significant difference (p < 0.001) in cell growth density was observed between DHN transformed yeasts and the empty vector transformed Cdc25 yeast cells (Fig. 6g, h and i). Tukey’s test showed no significant differences in cell growth between yeasts containing PcDHN1, CcDHN1, and PdDHN2, all of which exhibited similar growth patterns. However, a slight difference was observed when compared to the PdDHN1 transformed yeast, which demonstrated the highest cell density under heat stress conditions.

Isolated DHN genes expression in the mutant AXT3K yeast cells under salinity stress condition

To investigate the role of the isolated DHN genes in enhancing halotolerance, the four DHN genes were individually inserted into the salt-sensitive mutant strain AXT3K. Under control condition (0 mM NaCl), the survival rates of the empty vector and DHN transformed AXT3K strains were similar, indicating no significant effect in the absence of salt stress (Fig. 7a). However, when exposed to increasing concentrations of NaCl, differences in survival were observed. The empty vector AXT3K cells showed very poor survival and were unable to grow on YPG medium containing 400 mM NaCl, demonstrating the salt-sensitive nature of the strain (Fig. 7a). In contrast, the DHN transformed AXT3K cells exhibited survival rates similar to those under control conditions, even under high salt stress conditions of 100 mM, 200 mM, and 400 mM NaCl.

Growth analysis of AXT3K cells under salinity stress

The expression of DHN genes effect on the growth of AXT3K cells under salinity stress was examined by measuring OD600 at 10–3 dilution every 4 h for 48 h. Under control conditions (0 mM NaCl), all yeast strains, including the empty vector AXT3K cells, exhibited substantial growth (Fig. 7b). Furthermore, under the salt stress conditions, a significant difference in growth patterns was observed. It was found that at 100 mM NaCl, the empty vector AXT3K cells grew at a slower rate compared to the DHN transformed AXT3K cells and the wild-type W303 strain (Fig. 7c). Among the DHN transformed cells, the highest growth rate was observed for the CcDHN1 transformed AXT3K cells, which showed optimal growth under 100 mM NaCl. In contrast, at 200 mM NaCl and 400 mM NaCl, the empty vector AXT3K cells exhibited little to no growth, while the DHN transformed AXT3K cells and wild-type W303 cells displayed considerable growth under these high salt conditions (Fig. 7d and e). This shows that the DHN genes conferred enhanced salt stress tolerance to the AXT3K cells. Additionally, the growth phases of the DHN transformed AXT3K cells under both normal and salt stress conditions followed a consistent pattern. The cells displayed a lag phase between 0 to 8 h time period, followed by a log phase, from 8 to 36 h, and entered the stationary growth phase after 44 h, indicating normal growth despite the salt stress.

Cell density measurement of AXT3K cells under salt stress

The impact of salt stress on the growth of AXT3K yeast cells transformed with different DHN genes was examined through the cell density analysis of 10–3 yeast cell dilution using ImageJ software. The cell density of both the empty vector and DHN transformed AXT3K cells showed significant growth under the 0 mM NaCl, with only slight differences in cell density between the DHN transformed and the empty vector AXT3K cells (Fig. 7f). However, under salt, a significant difference (p < 0.001) in cell density was observed, with the DHN transformed AXT3K cells showing a higher growth density compared to the empty vector AXT3K cells. Among the DHN transformed strains, the highest cell density was recorded for CcDHN1-transformed AXT3K cells under 100 mM NaCl stress (Fig. 7g). As the NaCl concentration increased to 200 mM and 400 mM, the empty vector AXT3K cells displayed minimal to no growth, while the DHN transformed AXT3K cells continued to grow substantially (Fig. 7h and i). The growth density of the DHN transformed strains was similar to that of the wild-type W303 yeast strain.

The analysis of Na⁺ and K⁺ levels in AXT3K yeast cells under salt stress conditions revealed significant differences between cells transformed with the empty vector and those expressing DHN genes. There was no significant difference in Na⁺ and K⁺ content between the empty vector and DHN transformed AXT3K cells under normal condition of 0 mM NaCl. In the control condition, Na⁺ levels ranged from 21.67 ppm to 22.64 ppm, while K⁺ levels varied between 22.82 and 23.97 ppm (Fig. 8a and b). Furthermore, the wild-type W303 yeast strain displayed Na⁺ and K⁺ concentrations similar to those of DHN transformed cells, with values of 21.89 ppm and 23.35 ppm, respectively.

However, under salinity stress of 200 mM NaCl, a significant difference in Na⁺ accumulation (p < 0.001) was observed between AXT3K yeast cells transformed with the empty vector and the DHN genes (Fig. 8c). The empty vector AXT3K cells accumulated a higher Na⁺ level (53 ppm), indicating their reduced ability to regulate Na+ uptake under stress conditions. In contrast, DHN transformed AXT3K cells exhibited significantly lower Na+ levels, with PdDHN1 transformed cells showing the lowest Na+ accumulation (25.8 ppm). This reduction in Na+ content, similar to that of the wild-type W303 strain, suggests that DHN expression effectively prevents Na+ accumulation under salt stress, reducing it by 44.96% to 50.67%. It was found that PdDHN1 transformation had the highest reduction (50.67%) in Na+ content among the transformed strains. Similarly, a significant difference (p < 0.001) in K+ accumulation was detected. DHN transformed AXT3K cells maintained higher K+ levels than empty vector cells, which exhibited a substantially lower K+ level (27.23 ppm) (Fig. 8d). Among the DHN transformed strains, PdDHN1 expressing cells demonstrated the highest K+ accumulation (62.08 ppm), closely resembling the wild-type W303 strain (57.82 ppm). This indicates that DHN expression enhances K+ retention under salt stress by 54.28% to 66.64%, with PdDHN1 transformation having the highest increase of 66.64% in K+ levels. Furthermore, the K⁺/Na⁺ ratio, a crucial indicator of halotolerance, differed significantly between DHN expressing and empty vector transformed AXT3K yeast cells (Fig. 8e). The DHN expressing AXT3K strain exhibited a four-fold increase in the K+/Na+ ratio, demonstrating improved K+ retention and Na+ exclusion mechanisms (Fig. 8f). This indicates that DHN proteins contribute to enhanced halotolerance by maintaining ionic balance under salt stress conditions.

DHN proteins stabilize the enzyme LDH under different stress conditions

The protective capacity of four DHN proteins against LDH activity loss under heat, dehydration, and freezing stress was assessed. Their effectiveness was compared to bovine serum albumin (BSA), a general protein stabilizer, as well as to LDH exposed to buffer alone without any additional proteins. Under all stress conditions, DHNs exhibited substantial protective effects on LDH compared to BSA, indicating that their protective function extends beyond the generic stabilizing effect of an added protein. After 30 min at 45 °C, LDH in buffer alone lost over half of its activity, reducing to 20% (Fig. 10a), with a further decline to approximately 17% at 55 °C (Fig. 10b). However, in the presence of DHNs, LDH activity was fully preserved after 30 min at 45 °C, retaining 76% to 78% activity, representing a three-fold increase in LDH activity. At 55 °C, 64% to 74% of LDH activity was maintained, showing a 3- to 4-fold enhancement (Fig. 10a and b). Among the tested DHNs, PcDHN1 provided the highest level of protection, preserving 78% of LDH activity at 45 °C and 74% at 55 °C.

(b) LDH activity after 30 min of heat exposure at 55 °C, with and without DHN proteins. (c) LDH activity after two freeze–thaw cycles in the presence and absence of DHN proteins. (d) LDH activity measured post-desiccation, comparing samples with and without DHN proteins. BSA was used as a positive control. Different letters indicate statistically significant differences (p < 0.05), one-way ANOVA, Tukey’s post-hoc test. Data are expressed as mean ± SD from three replicates.

Exposure to two cycles of freezing and thawing significantly reduced LDH activity, with residual activity decreasing below 20%. However, in the presence of DHN proteins, LDH activity was preserved, maintaining between 65.34% and 77.86% of its initial activity, depending on the specific DHN protein (Fig. 10c). A three-fold increase in LDH activity was observed following freeze–thaw treatment after two weeks of stress, with PcDHN1 providing the highest protection, retaining 77.86% of LDH activity.

Similarly, after desiccation, LDH activity reduced to approximately 20% of its initial level. However, DHN proteins effectively stabilized the enzyme, preserving between 63.86% and 76.42% of fresh LDH activity, representing an approximate three-fold increase (Fig. 10d). The highest activity under desiccation stress was recorded in the presence of PcDHN1, which retained 76.42% of LDH activity.

DHNs inhibits LDH aggregation during stress treatments

The LDH is well known for its propensity to form aggregates under stress conditions such as dehydration, heat exposure, and freeze–thaw cycles. The ability of DHN proteins to prevent LDH aggregation during these stress treatments was investigated through the light scattering measurements, with absorbance used as an indicator of aggregation. Two enzyme-to-protein mass ratios (1:1 and 1:2) were examined. After 20 min of heating at 80 °C, LDH exhibited substantial aggregation. However, the presence of DHN proteins significantly reduced aggregation at both mass ratios, decreasing by 33.58% to 47.91% at 1:1 and by 22.92% to 36.15% at 1:2. These reductions showed a greater protective effect compared to BSA (Fig. 11a).

In the dehydration-rehydration stress assay, absorbance measurements revealed that DHN proteins and BSA exhibited similar effects on aggregate formation at a 1:1 mass ratio, with DHNs reducing aggregation by 20% to 30.65%. However, at a 1:2 ratio, DHNs provided stronger protection against aggregation compared to BSA, decreasing LDH aggregation by 24% to 28% (Fig. 11b). During freeze–thaw cycles, both BSA and DHNs effectively maintained LDH stability after two and three cycles at both tested mass ratios (Fig. 11c and d). However, LDH aggregation consistently remained lower in the presence of DHN proteins than with BSA, a non-specific protectant. Following two freeze–thaw cycles, DHNs reduced aggregate formation by 58.62% to 68.96% at a 1:1 mass ratio and by 54.40% to 71.26% at a 1:2 ratio. After three freeze–thaw cycles, DHNs further decreased aggregation by 38.09% to 42.85% at a 1:1 ratio and by approximately 35.92% to 45.82% at a 1:2 ratio.

Discussion

DHNs are major components of plants defence mechanism against various abiotic stresses, contributing to their ability to survive harsh environmental conditions. Initially identified in barley and corn seedlings exposed to dehydration treatments, DHNs were shown to be upregulated under stress conditions such as abscisic acid (ABA) and salt treatments18. Since then, they have been characterized across a wide range of plant species, particularly in those known for their tolerance to extreme environmental stressors. These include halophytes, which thrive in saline environments19,20, drought tolerant plants that can grow under prolonged water scarcity21,22, resurrection plants that can recover from severe desiccation, and hardy plants with general stress tolerance 23–25. Most of these environmental conditions are found in arid regions26.

The isolated DHN proteins shared a range of physicochemical properties, demonstrating similarities with other known DHNs. The PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 proteins encoded by their respective genes were found to be of similar size to the Atriplex halimus DHN, AhDHN proteins27, but smaller compared to the Atriplex canescens DHN, AcDHN proteins28. The pI values of PcDHN1 and CcDHN1 were within similar ranges to those of V. vinifera and wild V. yeshanensis DHNs29, suggesting similar charge characteristics. All the four DHNs had high GRAVY indices, indicating their highly hydrophilic nature. This feature is common in DHNs, which are known to play major roles in stabilizing cellular structures under stress conditions. Furthermore, the aliphatic index, indicating the relative volume occupied by aliphatic side chains such as alanine (Ala), valine (Val), isoleucine (Ile), and leucine (Leu), was high for the isolated desert plant DHNs. This suggests that they have stability over a broad range of temperatures, a characteristic that is similarly observed in V. vinifera DHNs29. These physicochemical properties suggest that the desert plant DHNs may exhibit structural stability and functional adaptability under the extreme heat and salinity stress conditions typical of desert environments30.

A phylogenetic tree was constructed to classify the DHN family proteins based on their segment composition. The results revealed that PcDHN1 and CcDHN1 were grouped within the YnSKn clade, with CcDHN1 showing a closer relationship to melon species, suggesting a common ancestor. This classification associates with previous reports that the YnSKn type is more prevalent in vine plants6, and are among the most abundant neutral to alkaline DHN proteins found in nature31, which is consistent with the findings of CcDHN1. On the other hand, PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 were found to be more closely related to the SKn group of DHNs in the phylogenetic tree, despite containing an F-segment in their protein structure. This associated with earlier studies that suggest FSKn-type DHNs are common among angiosperm trees32. These DHNs can be used as genomic resources for improving the abiotic stress tolerance of angiosperm plants33.

Sequence analysis confirmed that all four isolated DHNs contained the conserved motifs of DHN proteins. PcDHN1 and CcDHN1 were classified as YnSKn-type DHNs, which are well-known for their strong induction by ABA treatment. In wheat, this type of DHN (TaDHN1, TaDHN2, and TaDHN3) is significantly upregulated in response to salt stress34. Similarly, in sunflower, Y3SK2-type DHNs such as HaDhn1 increase in abundance during water deficit stress35. In Brassica juncea and B. napus, this DHN type is expressed in germinating seeds and supports enhanced cold tolerance during seedling emergence36. In contrast, PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 were categorised to be SKn type DHNs, having an S-segment and three K-segments. Well-characterized A. thaliana DHN such as COR47 also share the SK3 motif architecture and are phosphorylated in vivo37. Similar SKn DHNs have been identified in woody plants such as Prunus persica, peach38, and Betula pendula, birch, where exposure to short-day conditions followed by low-temperature treatment significantly increased the expression of a SK2-type DHN gene, in comparison to plants grown under long-day photoperiods12. PdDHN1 and PdDHN2 share structural similarities with Saltbush DHNs27. But the P. dactylifera DHNs also contain an additional F-segment classifying them as FSKn, broadening our understanding of F-segment DHNs previously reported in Arabdisopsis COR4739, Camelina sativa (ERD14) and Rhododendron catawbiense (RcDhn2)40. Our study found that all four DHNs possessed highly conserved S- and K-segments, which suggests they may play a role in phosphorylation and have functions related to dehydration responses and ion binding in plants41.

The secondary structure predictions showed that each protein exhibited a combination of disordered regions and helices that contribute to their molecular chaperone-like activities. In the desert plant DHNs, helices were located in the conserved K-segment, a region crucial for forming amphipathic helices. These helices play a key role in binding to partially unfolded proteins and preventing their aggregation under stress42. It was found that the disordered regions dominated the structure of the four DHNs, signifying their flexibility and ability to interact with diverse molecular targets. Across all four proteins, the K-segment helices is a unifying feature, forming amphipathic structures crucial for stabilizing proteins and maintaining enzymatic functions under stress. The dominance of disordered regions enhances their flexibility, allowing them to adapt to different environmental conditions2. These structural features indicate the protective role of DHN proteins as molecular chaperones, providing stress tolerance in plants. Conserved motifs, such as the K-segment, are essential features that help prevent protein misfolding and aggregation during stress43.

Unlike several DHNs, which are mainly induced under cold and osmotic stress, having different structural variation44, the desert plant DHNs exhibit various unique characteristics. These include the presence of an additional F-segment (PdDHN1 and PdDHN2), higher hydrophilicity, and greater thermal stability, as indicated by their elevated aliphatic indices. These characteristics likely enhance their protective capacity under heat and salt stress conditions45. Moreover, the dominance of intrinsically disordered regions combined with conserved K-segments in desert DHNs may allow for more flexible interactions with diverse stress-damaged proteins. Such adaptations suggest that desert plant DHNs have evolved to maintain protein function and ionic balance more effectively under extreme environmental conditions than their temperate homologs46. They can play important role in developing abiotic stress tolerance in plants47.

The isolated DHNs were predicted to be localized in the nucleus and cytosol, with minimal involvement in other organelles. This prediction is supported by the subcellular localization of wheat DHN-5, which, when overexpressed in onion cells and fused with GFP, was substantially found in the nucleus48. Furthermore, it remains possible that PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 are directed to the nucleus through a phosphorylation mechanism, facilitated by the presence of the S-segment, as observed in maize49. It shows that these DHNs may play roles in regulating nuclear processes related to abiotic stress and contribute to cellular protection in the cytosol. The analysis of PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 gene ontology (GO) term indicated their several roles in cellular regulation, stress response, and metabolic processes. Their strong association with nucleic acid binding, transcriptional regulation, and RNA processing showed their significance in regulating gene expression8. Additionally, their involvement in molecular transport, and signaling pathways indicated their importance in maintaining cellular integrity and responding to abiotic stresses in plants2,50.

Currently, the functional expression analysis of DHN genes from various organisms is increasingly conducted in bacterial and yeast cells exposed to abiotic stresses51. In the present study, to further confirm the stress tolerance properties of PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 genes in a eukaryotic system, these desert plant DHNs were expressed in Saccharomyces cerevisiae yeast strains Cdc25 under heat stress and AXT3K under salinity stress. Both the DHN and empty vector-transformed yeast cells exhibited similar growth characteristics under controlled conditions. However, when subjected to various heat and salinity stress conditions, DHN transformed cells showed a significant growth advantage over the empty vector controls. The DHN transformed cells were more tolerant to these stresses, showing better growth, including larger cell size, an exponential growth rate, higher cell density, and an increased K+/Na+ ratio. These results showed that the DHN genes were functional in the Cdc25 and AXT3K S. cerevisiae strains, enhancing their survival, growth, and tolerance to both heat and salinity stresses. These findings relate with previous studies, such as the overexpression of barley DHN, RhDHN4 that conferred heat and salinity stress tolerance in S. cerevisiae52. Likewise, the expression of the CaDHN gene from Arctic Cerastium arcticum enhanced S. cerevisiae tolerance to abiotic stresses like salinity, H2O2, methylated dioxane (MD), and t-BOOH, by improving redox homeostasis53. Additionally, a wheat DHN-like gene, wzy2, improved tolerance to drought and salinity stress in P. pastoris yeast cells54. Moreover, DHN-like proteins have been shown to play a role in abiotic stress tolerance in the fungus Aspergillus fumigatus55 and Alternaria brassicicola56. Furthermore, recombinant Pennisetum glaucum DHN expressed in E. coli have been reported to improve the tolerance and growth rate under salinity and heat stress compared to control strains in P. pastoris yeast cells57. Our findings support the role of DHN proteins in enhancing the salt tolerance of AXT3K yeast cells by modulating ion homeostasis, particularly by reducing Na+ accumulation and improving K+ retention under salt stress. DHNs, being IDPs, have been shown to function as molecular chaperones under stress conditions. Although DHNs are not transporter proteins themselves, their chaperone-like property may allow them to stabilize specific ion transport systems such as Na⁺/H⁺ antiporters or high-affinity K⁺ transporters, preserving their activity under stress58. In addition, the presence of charged and hydrophilic residues in DHNs may provide weak Na⁺-binding capacity, enabling rapid sequestration of excess ions and buffering cytosolic Na⁺ to prevent toxic accumulation59. In addition, their hydrophilic nature may allow them to function as osmoprotective molecules, maintaining hydration shells around proteins and membranes. Furthermore, by preventing denaturation or aggregation of ion transporters and other key stress-response proteins, DHNs could help maintain proper ion gradients, ensuring that K+ levels are kept high and Na+ levels low under stress conditions60. They could be involved in the signaling regulatory mechanisms, such as interactions with stress-responsive signaling proteins or transcriptional regulators that control ion channel activities or the activation of certain transporters61. This regulatory function could help balance the movement of ions like K+ and Na+, promoting K+ retention and minimizing Na+ influx in response to salt stress, which induces oxidative stress damaging cellular structures, including ion transport mechanisms. DHNs are known to have antioxidant properties, and by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), they may help preserve the function of ion channels and transporters62.

The desert plant DHN proteins that were produced exhibited a higher apparent molecular weight on the gel compared to their theoretical masses calculated from amino acid sequences. Such irregular migration behavior is well-reported for DHNs and other intrinsically disordered proteins, which have specific amino acid compositions rich in charged and hydrophilic residues that lead to reduced SDS binding and altered electrophoretic mobility63,64. As a result, their migration on SDS-PAGE does not strictly show their calculated molecular weights and frequently appear larger than predicted. However, we confirmed the identities of expressed recombinant PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 proteins through Western blot analysis using an anti-His6 antibody, despite their migration profiles.

Protein unfolding is a common physiological response in plant cells under stress, often resulting in the disruption of enzymatic activities and impairing cellular function65. Several factors, including temperature, pH, enzyme and substrate concentrations, as well as the presence or absence of inhibitors and activators, affect enzyme reaction efficiency66. In this study, the desert plant recombinant dehydrin proteins were produced, which had a molecular weight higher than their theoretical masses, a phenomenon commonly observed for DHN proteins67. We demonstrated that DHN proteins isolated from desert plants, PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 effectively protected the stress sensitive enzyme LDH from damage during drying, freezing, and heat exposure. The presence of these DHNs significantly enhanced LDH activity recovery, showing their strong protective role in preserving enzymatic function under stress conditions. DHN proteins provided substantial protection compared to BSA, a widely recognized non-specific enzyme stabilizer. In addition to maintaining LDH catalytic activity, DHN proteins also prevented enzyme aggregation. The protective role of DHNs in maintaining enzyme activity under stress has been widely studied. For example, DHN proteins such as ZmDHN13 have been shown to protect LDH and antioxidant enzymes from oxidative stress-induced damage68. Furthermore, it has been observed that date palm LEA2 proteins prevented the aggregation of LDH enzyme69. Additionally, the reduction of enzyme aggregation, particularly during drying, has been observed in other LEA proteins and explained by the molecular shield hypothesis70. However, it is important to indicate that LDH aggregation during drying is highly dependent on experimental conditions, and LDH can be entirely inactivated by drying even in the absence of aggregation71. It was reported that preventing aggregate formation under stress is essential for preserving protein function72. The ability of barley DHN, RhDHN4 to reduce LDH activity loss during heating, freezing, and desiccation further supports this concept52.

Despite these findings, the molecular mechanisms underlying DHNs’ protective activity remain largely unexplored. Two primary models have been proposed, the molecular shield effect and the disordered chaperone effect73. In both cases, dimer formation enables DHNs to form multimeric complexes around target proteins, thereby shielding them from water limitation-induced damage74. It has been suggested that DHNs exert their chaperone-like function through the molecular shield mechanism, which prevents protein aggregation. However, direct experimental evidence for this hypothesis is still lacking. Additionally, since LDH functions as a tetramer, DHNs might help prevent the dissociation of monomers under heat stress or facilitate their reassembly upon cooling, an aspect that needs further investigation.

The K-segment of wheat DHN, DHN-5 has been found to be crucial for protecting LDH in vitro75, showing its role in preventing protein unfolding under stress. Similarly, other DHNs such as ERD10 and RcDhn5 have demonstrated protective effects on LDH activity during freeze–thaw cycles, with the K-segment playing a significant role58. The K-segment is known to form an amphipathic helix, a structural feature closely associated to chaperone-like activity. This property is essential for stabilizing proteins by preventing misfolding and aggregation, thereby maintaining the enzyme function under stress. The conservation of the K-segment in the four DHNs identified in our study suggests that these proteins likely have similar chaperone-like properties, protecting LDH from stress-induced inactivation. This strengthens the role of DHNs in enhancing stress tolerance by acting as molecular chaperones, ensuring the stability and activity of crucial enzymes under adverse environmental conditions.

Among the four studied DHNs, PdDHN1 and PcDHN1 were the most effective in enhancing abiotic stress tolerance and enzyme stabilization. While this study provides several perspectives into the role of DHNs, future investigations will focus on in planta functional analysis by generating transgenic plants overexpressing DHN genes to study their direct effect on drought, salinity, and temperature stress tolerance. Additionally, studying their protein–protein interaction networks within plant cells will provide a broader understanding of their regulatory functions in stress response pathways. Although recombinant protein identity was confirmed through western blotting, future work will include mass spectrometry-based validation to ensure sequence integrity and detect any post-translational modifications. From an applied perspective, integrating DHNs into crop improvement programs will support agricultural sustainability by developing stress tolerant crops adapted to arid environments.

Conclusion

Desert plant DHNs are highly hydrophilic proteins characterized by disordered regions and random coil structures, which support their role in maintaining cellular hydration under abiotic stress. Their structural flexibility contributes to stress adaptation, as found by improved heat and salinity stress tolerance in yeast knockout mutants expressing DHN genes. Enhanced K⁺ accumulation in AXT3K cells shows their role in maintaining ionic homeostasis under salt stress. Additionally, the chaperone-like activity of these proteins was evident in their ability to stabilize LDH during heat, dehydration-rehydration, and freeze–thaw stress, emphasising their protective function under adverse conditions.

Materials and methods

Collection of plant materials

The leaf samples from the plant material used in this study were collected from natural habitats in the desert regions of the UAE. Species identification was performed and authenticated by experts from the Department of Integrative Agriculture, College of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine, United Arab Emirates University, based on morphological characteristics consistent with established botanical descriptions.

Identification of PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 genes

The sequence of DHN genes from P. cineraria, PcDHN1 (XM_054922987.1) and P. dactylifera PdDHN1 (XM_008800003.2), and PdDHN2 (XM_008793759.4), were identified by analyzing their respective genomes through the NCBI database. While, for C. colocynthis, CcDHN1, no DHN gene sequence was available. To isolate the CcDHN1 sequence, we did a multiple alignment analysis with genomic sequences from closely related species, including Benincasa hispida (XM_039046643.1), Cucumis sativus (XM_011654848.2), Cucumis melo (XM_008453887.3), Cucurbita maxima (XM_023127268.1), and Citrullus lanatus (Cla97C07G135750, http://cucurbitgenomics.org/). This approach enabled the identification of conserved DHN sequence signatures for C. colocynthis. Following identification, specific primers were designed for each DHN gene to facilitate their cloning (Table S1).

Molecular cloning of PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 genes

Total RNA was extracted from leaf samples of each plant species using optimized protocols. For P. cineraria, RNA isolation was performed with the Sigma-Aldrich RNA Extraction Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), while C. colocynthis and P. dactylifera samples were processed using the Bioanalysis Kit (MACHEREY–NAGEL) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed from the isolated RNA as template and using the ProtoScript II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (New England Biolabs). The synthesized cDNAs were subsequently used for amplification through RT-PCR with gene-specific primers designed for each of the DHN genes (Table S1). PCR was carried for 35 cycles, beginning with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by a cycle of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 1 min, concluding with a final extension step at 72 °C for 7 min.

The resulting PCR products were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) and ligated into the pMiniT 2.0 vector using the NEB PCR Cloning Kit (New England Biolabs, Inc.). Recombinant plasmid DNA was extracted from transformed E. coli cells and sequenced in both directions to confirm the sequence identity of the DHN genes. All sequenced gene candidates from the three plants were examined through the Pfam database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi) to confirm the presence of conserved DHN domains.

Phylogenetic analysis of the DHN proteins

Translated protein sequences of the DHN genes were aligned using ClustalW in MEGA 11 software. A phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the alignments using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method within MEGA 11. The tree was generated using p-distance as the substitution model, uniform evolutionary rates, pairwise gap deletion, and 1,000 bootstrap replicates for statistical support. The phylogenetic analysis included the deduced sequences of PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2, along with DHN sequences from various plant species for comparative analysis. These included V. vinifera (VvDHN1–VvDHN4), V. yeshanensis (VyDHN1–VyDHN4), A. thaliana (AtDHN1–AtDHN10), Hordeum vulgare subsp. vulgare (HvDHN1–HvDHN13), P. persica (PpDHN1), R. catawbiense (RcDHN1, RcDHN4), Pinus sylvestris (PsDHN1–PsDHN3), B. hispida (BhDHN1), C. sativus (CsDHN1), C. melo (CmlDHN1), C. lanatus (ClDHN1), C. maxima (CmxDHN1), Zea mays (ZmDHN13), Ipomoea pes-caprae (IpDHN), Thinopyrum elongatum (TeDHN1), Capsella rubella (CrDHN1), C. sativa (CmsDHN1), Glycine max (GmDHN1), Medicago sativa (MsDHN1), Pisum sativum (PstDHN1), Triticum aestivum (TaDHN1, TaDHN2), O. sativa (OsDHN1), Citrus trifoliata (CtDHN1), and Gossypium hirsutum (GsDHN1). The phylogenetic tree was visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) online tool, version 5 (https://itol.embl.de).

Bioinformatic analysis

The analysis of physical and chemical parameters of the DHN proteins were conducted using ExPASy ProtParam76. It included determining molecular mass, theoretical pI, instability index, aliphatic index, and GRAVY. Additionally, conserved domain identification was carried out using NCBI’s CD-Search tool to examine functionally significant regions within the protein. The secondary structure prediction was performed using PSIPRED 477 to analyze protein’s structural conformation. Cellular localization analysis was further conducted for the subcellular distribution of DHN proteins. Moreover, through PSIPRED 477, the GO term functional predictions for proteins were performed to infer the functions of the desert plant DHNs.

Subcloning of PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 genes into pYES2 vector and transformation of S. cerevisiae yeast mutants

The coding sequence of PcDHN1 gene was re-amplified with specific primers having EcoRI and NotI restriction sites added to their 5′ and 3′ end, respectively (Table S2). For CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 genes specific primers with BamHI and EcoRI restriction sites added to their 5′ and 3′ ends, were used for re-amplification (Table S2). The re-amplified PCR products were digested with the respective enzymes and ligated into pYES2 vector digested with the same restriction enzymes for each DHN gene. Minipreps were performed using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) to isolate the recombinant plasmid DNA. For functional analyses, the recombinant pYES2 plasmids containing DHN genes were subsequently transformed into two S. cerevisiae strains with specific stress susceptibilities, a thermosensitive mutant strain, Cdc25 (genotype: MATα, cdc25-2, ade2, his3, leu2, lys2, trp1, ura3), and a salinity-sensitive triple-knockout strain, AXT3K (genotype: Δena1∷HIS3∷ena4, Δnha1∷LEU2, Δnhx1∷KanMX4). The transformation was performed using the Frozen-EZ Yeast Transformation II kit following the manual instruction. In each transformation experiment, empty pYES2 plasmid was used as a negative control.

Post-transformation, yeast cells were cultured in YPG broth at 25 °C for 48 h to reach OD600 nm between 0.4 and 0.8 for optimal gene expression under the galactose-inducible promoter in pYES2. Following the incubation period, a series of ten-fold serial dilutions (10–1 to 10–4) were prepared for each yeast culture in YPG broth.

Heat and salinity stress assays

The heat stress tolerance was evaluated by spotting 5 μl aliquots from each dilution series of transformed Cdc25 yeast strains carrying either the empty pYES2 vector or DHN gene constructs on YPG medium supplemented with 50 µg/ml ampicillin. The assays were incubated under a range of temperatures, 25 °C as control and at 30 °C, 35 °C, and 40°C for heat stress conditions. For salinity stress tolerance, 5 μl from each dilution were spotted onto YPG medium containing different concentrations of NaCl (0 mM as control, 100 mM, 200 mM, and 400 mM NaCl for salinity stress). Transformed AXT3K yeast strains with either the empty vector or DHN genes were incubated at 28 °C, optimal temperature for the growth of yeast. The yeast cells growth under both heat and salt stress conditions were recorded daily over a 2- to 3- day period, allowing for qualitative comparison between transformed strains.

Optical density (OD) measurements

The initial yeast spots from both Cdc25 and AXT3K strains were selected and inoculated into YPG medium to generate yeast suspensions for growth curve analysis. The Cdc25 strain suspensions were prepared with YPG medium under both control and heat stress conditions. While the AXT3K strain suspensions were prepared in YPG medium supplemented with varying concentrations of NaCl. Each yeast suspension was incubated with continuous shaking and the OD600 measurements were taken at 4-h intervals over a 48-h period to monitor yeast growth pattern under each stress condition. The resulting data were used for growth curves analysis under heat and salinity stresses.

Na+ and K+ ion content of AXT3K yeast cells

To analyse ion accumulation under salinity stress, the mutant AXT3K yeast cells transformed with the empty vector, or DHN genes, along with the wild-type yeast strain W303 were cultured in 20 ml YPG medium for 2 days to reach their optimal density. Following incubation, 10 ml of each culture was centrifuged at 8000 × g for 10 min to pellet the cells. This cell pellets were washed thoroughly with ultrapure water for the removal of any residual salts. Cell digestion was performed by adding 3 ml of 65% nitric acid (HNO₃) and 1 ml of sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), followed by heating at 200 °C until dry. After initial drying, an additional 3 ml of 65% HNO3 was added, and the mixture was reheated to dryness to ensure complete digestion78. The resulting ash was dissolved in ultrapure water to prepare samples for ion quantification. The concentration of Na+ and K+ ions was measured using the Inductively Coupled Plasma Emission Optical Spectrometry (ICP-OES, Perkin Elmer).

Colony density measurement

The images of each triplicate sample were captured under identical lighting and magnification settings to ensure consistency to assess colony growth density79. Using ImageJ software, each image was classified into grayscale, and colony boundaries were defined and analysed based on pixel intensity and area80. Colony density was calculated by measuring the total pixel area and the average pixel intensity within each colony, enabling a quantitative assessment of growth under varying stress conditions. To further enhance accuracy, background subtraction and thresholding techniques were applied within ImageJ to isolate colony regions from the surrounding agar, minimizing artifacts from plate inconsistencies. Following image processing, growth densities of treated samples were statistically analysed.

Expression, production, and purification of recombinant PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2 proteins

The DHN ORFs were inserted into the E. coli expression vector pET28a + using EcoRI/NotI restriction sites for PcDHN1 and BamHI/EcoRI restriction sites for CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2. To produce recombinant DHN proteins in E. coli BL21 (DE3), cells were induced with 1 mM IPTG at 37 °C and incubated for 4 h. Furthermore, the cells were lysed in NENT buffer (100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5% NP-40, 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.9) supplemented with the protease inhibitor phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Protein purification was carried out using HisLink Protein Purification Resin following the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega). The purified protein profiles were analyzed through SDS-PAGE, and the concentration of recombinant proteins was measured spectrophotometrically at 280 nm using a molar extinction coefficient calculated from the amino acid sequence through an online tool (http://encorbio.com/protocols/Prot-MW-Abs.htm).

SDS-PAGE and western blot analysis

Protein samples (5 μg each) were separated on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel alongside a protein molecular marker (Thermo Scientific™ PageRuler™ Prestained Protein Ladder). The protein bands were either stained with Coomassie blue for visualization or transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane (Schleicher and Schuell) using a semi-dry blotting system (Bio-Rad). To prevent non-specific binding, the membrane was blocked with PBST (10 × PBS containing Tween 20 at a 1:1000 dilution) supplemented with 5% non-fat dry milk. The blot was then incubated with a peroxidase-conjugated anti-His tag antibody (Abcam) at a 1:2000 dilution in PBST containing 1% non-fat dry milk. We report that DHNs, being intrinsically disordered proteins, exhibit different electrophoretic mobility due to their low SDS-binding affinity. As a result, their migration on SDS-PAGE often appears larger and is not substantially improved by higher-percentage gels.

Investigation of LDH protection assay

LDH from bovine heart was sourced from Sigma–Aldrich and diluted in 10 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, following the manufacturer’s instructions. One μl of 20 μg/μl LDH was combined with 20 μl of buffer, with or without DHN proteins (PcDHN1, CcDHN1, PdDHN1, and PdDHN2) or BSA, at a 1:1 mass ratio (LDH: DHNs/BSA). The samples were then vacuum-dried using a Speed Vac (Savant Speed Vac Plus Model SC110A Concentrator) to a final volume of 6 μl, resulting to a final LDH concentration of approximately 3.3 mg/ml (a 3.3-fold increase compared to the starting concentration of 1 mg/ml). Samples were subsequently rehydrated rapidly with an additional 14 μl of buffer, restoring the original volume. To assess the impact of freezing on LDH activity, the samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen for 1.5 min and thawed at room temperature. Freeze–thaw cycles were performed up to two times. For heat treatment assays, the samples were incubated at 45 °C and 55 °C for up to 30 min. To measure enzyme activity, 1 ml of freshly prepared assay buffer (10 mM sodium phosphate, pH 7.4; 2 mM NADH; 10 mM pyruvic acid) was added to the LDH samples. NADH oxidation was monitored by measuring absorbance at 340 nm over a 3-min period, during which the reaction rate remained linear. The rate of absorbance decrease was used to calculate enzyme activity, using the formula (ΔOD/min) × 8095 = U/l (Biomaghreb kit). All assays were performed in triplicate.

In vitro protein aggregation assay

LDH was used as a model aggregation-prone protein during desiccation–rehydration, freezing–thawing, and heating81. The aggregation of LDH was monitored by measuring the change in absorbance at 340 nm using a spectrophotometer (JENWAY, 7305). For desiccation stress, 100 μl of LDH (1 mg/ml) was incubated with or without DHNs or BSA at 1:1 and 1:2 (enzyme:protectant mass ratios). Samples were partially dried for 2.5 h, reducing the volume to approximately 30 μl (30% of initial). This produced a final LDH concentration of 3.3 mg/ml. Rehydration was performed rapidly by adding ultrapure water in a single step to restore the original 100 μl volume. For heating assays, the samples were exposed to 80 °C for 20 min. To evaluate the effect of freezing on LDH activity, the samples were frozen in liquid nitrogen for 1.5 min and thawed at room temperature, with freeze–thaw cycles repeated up to three times. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate the cell growth of DHN transformed yeast cells and the enzymatic activities of LDH under various treatment conditions. The mean and standard deviation (SD) were calculated for each dataset, with three biological replicates included for both cell growth and enzymatic assays. For comparative analysis, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test to identify statistically significant differences between DHN protein-expressing and non-expressing cells under stress conditions. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homoscedasticity with the Levene’s test. All statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software (v4.5.0).
