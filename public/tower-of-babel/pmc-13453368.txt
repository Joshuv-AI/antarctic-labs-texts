A Single‑Center Retrospective Cohort Study: Impact of Glycemic Control on First‑Line TKI Plus PD‑1 Inhibitors versus TKI Alone in Intermediate‑to‑advanced Unresectable Hepatocellular Carcinoma(HCC) with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM)
Authors: Jiaqi Liu, Xiong Chen, Runxian Wang, Caiyun Peng, Hongli Yu, Wenjing Wang, Weihong Ma, Jie Han, Zhipeng Liang, Yunqiao Yang, Jiamin Cheng, Yinying Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is an independent risk factor for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and the number of HCC patients with comorbid T2DM is increasing. Although tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) monotherapy or combination with programmed death‑1 (PD‑1) inhibitors has become the first‑line standard of care for advanced HCC, most relevant clinical trials excluded patients with poorly controlled glycemia. Consequently, direct comparisons of efficacy and safety between first‑line regimens specifically in T2DM‑combined HCC populations remain insufficiently explored, and the impact of glycemic control on treatment outcomes also remains unclear. Therefore, this study retrospectively compares first‑line TKI monotherapy versus combined regimens in this population, with a focus on the effect of glycemic control status on prognosis, and aims to construct a prognostic nomogram integrating clinical and metabolic indicators to support individualized risk assessment.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed HCC patients with T2DM who received TKI monotherapy or TKI+PD-1 between January 2019 and January 2024. Overall survival (OS) and progression‑free survival (PFS) was analyzed using Cox regression (HR). Cancer‑specific death (with non‑cancer death as competing event) and PFS were evaluated using Fine‑Gray competing risk models (sHR). A prognostic nomogram incorporating independent predictors was developed and internally validated.ResultsA total of 374 patients were enrolled (217 TKI+PD-1, 157 TKI), with 135 per group after Propensity Score Matching (PSM) (1:1). Post-PSM, the TKI group showed significantly inferior median OS (mOS: 20.7 months vs 26.8 months, HR=2.009, 95% CI 1.438–2.807, P<0.001) and PFS (mPFS: 11 months vs 16 months, HR=2.086, 95% CI 1.495–2.911; All P<0.001) compared to the TKI+PD-1 group. In the TKI cohort, poor glycemic control was associated with significantly worse OS (HR=2.125, 95% CI 1.215–3.714, P<0.001) and PFS (HR=2.210, 95% CI 1.292–3.781, P<0.001), but this association was not observed in the TKI+PD-1 cohort. The prognostic nomogram (corrected C index = 0.652) showed acceptable calibration and provided modest stratification patients into three risk groups (median OS: 19.3, 16.5 and 13.8 months, log rank P<0.001). Grade 1–2 diarrhea was lower in the TKI+PD-1 group (16.6% vs 26.8%, P = 0.017), with no increase in high-grade adverse events.ConclusionIn HCC patients with T2DM, TKI+PD-1 offers superior OS and PFS compared to TKI alone. Glycemic control significantly impacts prognosis in patients receiving TKI monotherapy but its independent effect is diminished in those on combination immunotherapy. The proposed nomogram provides an individualized prognostic tool for this patient population.Keywords: hepatocellular carcinoma, diabetes, survival, glycemic control, antineoplastic protocols, nomogram

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common cancer worldwide and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with a heavy disease burden.1 Meanwhile, the global prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) has been continuously increasing, reaching 11.11% by 2024,2 and is projected to rise to 12.96% by 2050. Among HCC patients, the prevalence of T2DM is significantly higher than that of the general population,3,4 and there is a close correlation between these two conditions. Notably, a comprehensive global burden study demonstrated that 4.4% of new HCC cases worldwide in 2022 were attributable to T2DM, with the highest population-attributable fraction observed in the Americas (8.7%). Furthermore, the prevalence of T2DM has shown a steady upward trend from 1990 to 2022, underscoring the increasingly prominent contribution of metabolic factors to the etiology of HCC.5

Mechanistically, T2DM drives HCC progression primarily through hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, and insulin resistance.6,7 Chronic hyperglycemia induces oxidative stress and the accumulation of advanced glycation end products, which promote liver fibrosis and create a tumor-permissive microenvironment. Concurrently, compensatory hyperinsulinemia resulting from insulin resistance activates the insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor and its downstream PI3K/AKT/mTOR and RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK cascades, enhancing proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis.8 Together with the chronic low-grade inflammation, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and altered gut microbiota frequently accompanying T2DM, these mechanisms collectively contribute to hepatocarcinogenesis.7 Consequently, T2DM has been established as an independent risk factor for HCC, with epidemiological studies indicating a 2- to 3-fold increased risk in diabetic patients.

Currently, surgical resection remains the most effective radical treatment for HCC. However, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage and have lost the opportunity for surgery, with a postoperative recurrence rate as high as 70%.9 Tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as sorafenib and lenvatinib have become the cornerstone of first-line treatment for advanced HCC, and the addition of PD-1 inhibitors has further improved outcomes, becoming a new standard of care.10 However, most pivotal clinical trials excluded patients with poorly controlled diabetes and rarely focused on the potential impact of different antidiabetic medications on tumor prognosis. Existing studies have suggested that TKIs can significantly affect glycemic control, leading to glucose fluctuation through multiple mechanisms. Specifically, TKIs may induce hyperglycemia or even new-onset diabetes in approximately 15–40% of patients, likely by impairing pancreatic β-cell function, exacerbating insulin resistance, or inducing systemic inflammation.11 Moreover, both TKIs and immune checkpoint inhibitors can themselves induce hyperglycemia, and PD-1 inhibitors are well recognized to trigger autoimmune type 1 diabetes as a severe immune-related adverse event.12 To avoid these confounders, this study strictly enrolled only patients with pre-existing T2DM confirmed before treatment initiation and excluded any patient with subsequent evidence of treatment-emergent type 1 diabetes, ensuring etiological homogeneity.

Therefore, in this special population of HCC patients with T2DM, how the choice of first-line regimen affects tumor control and patient prognosis, and the role of glycemic control therein, are clinical questions that urgently need to be answered. This study aims to retrospectively compare the efficacy and safety of first-line TKI monotherapy versus TKI plus PD-1 inhibitors, with a particular focus on the impact of glycemic control status on treatment outcomes, and to construct a prognostic nomogram integrating clinical and metabolic variables to support individualized risk assessment for these patients.

Materials and Methods

This study was a retrospective cohort study, including patients diagnosed with intermediate‑to‑advanced unresectable HCC and type 2 diabetes from January 2019 to January 2024. They were divided into the TKI group and the TKI + PD‑1 group based on the first-line treatment regimen they received. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital, with the approval number: KY-2026-3-40-1. Due to the retrospective nature of this study, the committee has waived the requirement for obtaining informed consent from the patients.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age: 18–80 years old; (2) Diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) based on biopsy, imaging examinations (CT/MRI/Ultrasound), and/or laboratory test results, and in accordance with the Chinese guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of primary liver cancer;13 (3) Type 2 diabetes diagnosis confirmed through reviewing medical records and based on fasting plasma glucose (FPG) or HbA1c standards according to the guidelines of the American Diabetes Association;14 (4) Rated as Child-Pugh A or B grade; (5) ECOG score of 0–2. Exclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosed with type 1 diabetes or other types of diabetes; (2) Diabetic conditions discovered after treatment; (3) Presence of other types of tumors; (4) Patients with missing data or missing follow-up; (5) Survival period less than 3 months (Figure 1).

Glycemic Control Stratification

Within each treatment cohort, patients were classified as having good glycemic control (good) or poor glycemic control (poor) based on the baseline first-line treatment with a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level greater than 7% within 3 months.15

Endpoints of the Study

The endpoints of the study include overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). OS is defined as the time from the start of first-line treatment to death due to any cause. PFS is defined as the time from the start of first-line treatment to tumor progression or death according to the RECIST 1.1 criteria.16 Cancer‑specific death (CSD) was defined as death resulting from HCC progression or treatment‑related complications directly attributable to HCC, with deaths from other causes (eg, cardiovascular events, diabetic complications, infections) considered as competing events.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as the median (interquartile range, IQR). Comparisons of continuous variables between groups were conducted using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests. Differences in categorical variables were examined using χ2-tests or Wilcoxon rank sum tests. Kaplan–Meier curves were used for survival analysis, and survival comparisons were performed using Log rank tests. The Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to identify variables affecting patient survival. Variables with a P value < 0.05 in the univariate regression analysis were included in the multivariate regression analysis. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to reduce selection bias. A total of 27 variables (age, gender, BMI, smoking, alcohol, hypothyroidism, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, HBV, HbA1c, ALT, AST, Cr, PLT, NLR, AFP, maximum tumor diameter, macrovascular invasion, lymph node metastasis, distant metastasis, BCLC stage, Child–Pugh score, gastrointestinal bleeding, esophageal varices, splenomegaly, local treatment, and ECOG score) were included in the PSM model. The nearest neighbor matching algorithm was used, with the optimal caliper set at 0.2 and without replacement. The balance between the two groups was evaluated by the standardized mean difference of the matching variables. Generally, a maximum standardized mean difference of 0.1 was considered acceptable Overall survival (OS, all‑cause death) was analyzed using the Cox proportional hazards regression model, with results presented as hazard ratios (HR). Cancer‑specific death (CSD) and progression‑free survival (PFS) were analyzed using the Fine‑Gray competing risk model, with non‑cancer death as the competing event. Results from these analyses are presented as subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR). The Cox model was also used for PFS as a sensitivity analysis (HR). Toxicity reactions were evaluated using the “General Terms for Adverse Events - Patient Report Version”.17 All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Development of a Prognostic Nomogram

To further quantify the individual risk of death, a prognostic nomogram was constructed based on the multivariable Cox regression model incorporating all independent predictors identified in the overall cohort after PSM. The final model included treatment regimen (TKI vs TKI+PD-1), glycemic control (HbA1c <7.0% vs ≥7.0%), antidiabetic drug class (metformin, insulin, others), age, BCLC stage (B vs C), Child-Pugh grade (A vs B), log-transformed AFP (log[AFP+1]), and maximum tumor diameter. The nomogram was internally validated using 200-bootstrap resampling to estimate the optimism-corrected Harrell’s C-index and to generate calibration curves for 12-month overall survival. Patients were then divided into three risk groups (low, medium, high) according to the tertiles of the linear predictor derived from the nomogram, and Kaplan-Meier curves with Log rank tests were used to compare survival differences among the three group.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of the Patients

A total of 395 patients with hepatocellular carcinoma and type 2 diabetes who received targeted or targeted combined immunotherapy were included in this study. Excluding those with inconsistent age (n=3), those with OS less than 3 months (n=10), and those who were lost to follow-up (n=8), a total of 374 cases were finally included in the cohort study. There were 323 male patients and 51 female patients. The average age was 60.94 ± 8.29 years (55, 67). Among them, 217 patients received TKI + PD-1 treatment, 102 patients died of cancer (91%), and 10 patients died of non-cancer causes (8.9%); in the TKI group, 157 patients were included, among whom 95 patients died of cancer (91%), and 9 patients died of non-cancer causes (8.7%). Before PSM, the TKI group was slightly older (63 [57–68] vs 60 [54–67], P = 0.038, SMD=0.233), and the maximum tumor diameter was smaller (2.8 [1.8–5.5] vs 4.1 [2.2–6.7], P = 0.025, SMD=0.171). In addition, there was a significant difference in hyperlipidemia (P = 0.002, SMD=0.157). The use of hypoglycemic drugs (metformin, insulin, others) and other baseline variables showed no significant overall differences. After PSM, the differences in baseline characteristics between the two groups were significantly reduced: age (62 [56–67] vs 61 [56–68], P = 0.718, SMD=0.045) and the maximum tumor diameter (3.0 [1.8–6.2] vs 3.4 [1.9–6.2], P = 0.632, SMD=0.022), and other previously imbalanced variables were corrected (Table 1).

Survival Outcomes

Before PSM, the median OS and median PFS in the TKI + PD-1 group were both longer than those in the TKI group (mOS: 23.7 months, 95% CI: 22–28.4 months vs 19.2 months, 95% CI: 17–24 months; mPFS: 16 months, 95% CI: 14.7–20.1 months vs 11 months, 95% CI: 9–14 months, both P<0.001). (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2). After PSM, this still held true (mOS: 26.8 months, 95% CI: 22.6–32 months vs 20.7 months, 95% CI: 17–24.1 months; mPFS: 16 months, 95% CI: 15–27 months vs 11 months, 95% CI: 10–15 months, both P<0.001) (Figure 2).

The results of survival analysis (OS) showed that treatment method was an independent influencing factor of overall survival in both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis. Compared with TKI+PD-1 group, TKI monotherapy was consistently associated with a higher risk of death, and the association was further strengthened after 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) (univariate: HR=1.870, 95% CI 1.349–2.593, P<0.001; Multivariate analysis: HR=2.009, 95% CI 1.438–2.807, P<0.001). Multivariate analysis further confirmed that HBV positive (HR=3.391), Child-Pugh class B (HR=1.419), increased HbA1c (HR=1.213) and increased maximum tumor diameter (HR=1.074) were also independent predictors of poor OS (all P<0.05) (Table 2).

Progression-free survival (PFS) analysis yielded a trend consistent with OS. The risk of progression in the TKI monotherapy group was significantly higher than that in the TKI+PD-1 group, and the risk was statistically significant before and after PSM, and remained robust in the multivariate model (univariate: HR=1.937, 95% CI 1.398–2.683; multivariate: HR=2.086, 95% CI 1.495–2.911; All P<0.001). In addition, HBV positivity (HR=2.557), Child-Pugh B (HR=1.488) and elevated HbA1c (HR=1.152) were also confirmed to be independent risk factors for shortened PFS (all P<0.05) (Table 2).

Outcomes of Competing Risk Analysis

Competing-risk model analysis showed that after accounting for non-cancer deaths (10 in the TKI+PD-1 group and 9 in the TKI group) as competing events, the cumulative incidence of cancer-related death (CIF) in the TKI group was still significantly higher than that in the TKI+PD-1 group, and the difference was more significant after PSM (Gray’s test: Before PSM, P = 0.002; after PSM, P = 0.002) (Figure 2).

The multivariate Fine-Gray model confirmed that treatment regimen was an independent factor for cancer-related death. Both before and after PSM, the cancer mortality sub-distribution risk (sHR) of TKI monotherapy was significantly higher than that of TKI+PD-1 combination therapy (before PSM: sHR =1.534, 95% CI 1.162–2.023, P = 0.003; After PSM: sHR =1.798, 95% CI 1.288–2.510, p<0.001). This result was consistent with the univariate analysis (before PSM: sHR =1.522, 95% CI 1.161–1.995, P = 0.002; after PSM: sHR =1.618, 95% CI 1.174–2.231, P = 0.003), indicating that the results were robust (Table 3).

In addition, multivariate models showed that elevated HbA1c (sHR =1.239) and HBV positivity (sHR =2.361) were also independent risk factors for cancer death, while hypertension showed a protective association (sHR =0.614) (both P < 0.01, Table 3).

Subgroup Analyses

In order to explore the effect of blood glucose control on the efficacy of different treatment regimens, we divided patients into well-controlled group and poorly-controlled group according to the level of blood glucose control in TKI monotherapy cohort and TKI+PD-1 combination cohort. After PSM matching, the balance of baseline variables in the two cohorts was well corrected (SMD < 0.1, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

In the TKI-monotherapy cohort, poor glycemic control consistently predicted worse outcomes. The K-M curve showed that the OS and PFS of patients in the poorly-controlled group were significantly worse than those in the well-controlled group before and after matching (OS after PSM: P = 0.021; PFS after PSM: P = 0.006). (Supplementary Figures S3–S6). Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that poor glycemic control was an independent risk factor for OS (HR=2.125, 95% CI 1.215–3.714, P<0.001) and PFS (HR=2.210, 95% CI 1.292–3.781, P<0.001). Competing risk model also showed that the cumulative incidence of cancer-related death was higher in this group (Gray test after PSM P<0.05) (Supplementary Figures S7 and S8), which was independently significant in the multivariate Fine-Gray model (sHR=1.902, 95% CI 1.115–3.242, P = 0.018). (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4).

However, the prognostic effect of poor glycemic control was attenuated in the TKI+PD-1 combination cohort. Before matching, the well-controlled group had a slight advantage in OS and PFS (P<0.05) (Supplementary Figures S9 and S11), but there was no significant difference between the two groups after matching (OS: P = 0.286; PFS: P = 0.294). (Supplementary Figure S10 and S12). Competing risk model also showed that the cumulative incidence of cancer-related death was higher in the poor glycemic control group before PSM (Gray test, P<0.05; Supplementary Figure S13), whereas no significant difference was observed after PSM (Gray test, P>0.05; Supplementary Figure S14). Multivariate analysis showed that poor blood glucose control was associated with OS (HR=1.287, 95% CI 0.780–2.123, P = 0.323), PFS (HR=1.430, 95% CI 0.836–2.445, P = 0.191) and cumulative incidence of cancer death (sHR=1.410, P = 0.191. 95% CI 0.830–2.395, P = 0.203) did not reach statistical significance. In this cohort, tumor burden (HR=1.156) and liver function (HR=2.114) were still independent prognostic factors (P<0.001) (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6).

Prognostic Nomogram for Overall Survival

A prognostic nomogram was constructed using the eight independent predictors identified in the multivariable Cox model (Figure 3A). The model showed modest discrimination ability with a bootstrap‑corrected Harrell’s C‑index of 0.652 (optimism = 0.017). Calibration curves for 12‑month overall survival demonstrated excellent agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, with a mean bias <0.02 (Figure 3B).

The linear predictor (LP) is obtained by summing the points of each variable; a higher LP indicates worse prognosis. (B) Calibration curve for 12-month overall survival. Based on the tertiles of the linear predictor, patients were stratified into low‑risk (n=125), medium‑risk (n=124) and high‑risk (n=125) groups. The median overall survival times were 19.3 months, 16.5 months and 13.8 months, respectively (log‑rank P < 0.001) (Figure 3C). The nomogram is available as an online calculator in the supplementary materials.

Adverse Events Analysis

The overall incidence of adverse events was comparable between the two groups. The most common grade 1–2 adverse events in the TKI group were diarrhea (26.8%), pain (19.7%), and hand-foot skin reaction (17.2%); the most common grade 1–2 adverse events in the combination group were diarrhea (16.6%), fatigue (12.4%), and pain (16.1%). Among them, the incidence of grade 1–2 diarrhea in the combination group was significantly lower than that in the TKI group (16.6% vs 26.8%, p = 0.017). The 3–4 grade adverse events were uncommon in both groups, and there was no statistically significant difference in the incidence of severe adverse events at all levels. Compared with TKI monotherapy, combination therapy did not increase the risk of high-level adverse events (Supplementary Table 7).

Discussion

This study systematically compared the efficacy of first-line tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) monotherapy and TKI combined with PD-1 inhibitor in the special population of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) complicated with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and explored the prognostic value of baseline glycemic control status in different treatment backgrounds under the same framework for the first time.

In this study, TKI+PD-1 combination therapy significantly prolonged OS and PFS compared with TKI monotherapy, which is highly consistent with the results of large Phase III clinical trials in recent years. The CARES-310 study confirmed that the median OS of camrelizumab combined with apatinib in the first-line treatment of unresectable HCC was 22.1 months, which was significantly better than that of sorafenib group (15.2 months).18 Although the prespecified superiority of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab versus lenvatinib plus placebo was not achieved in the LEAP-002 study, the combination therapy group still showed a numerical survival advantage (median overall survival: 21.2 months vs19.0 months).19 However, all these pivotal studies excluded patients with diabetes who had poor glycemic control or did not perform in-depth analyses of diabetes subgroups. This study fills this gap in a real-world setting, demonstrating that the benefit of TKI plus PD-1 combination therapy remains and is clinically meaningful even in patients with T2DM.

It is important to note that this study further analyzed the risk of cancer-specific death with the use of competing-risk models. In HCC patients with diabetes, non-cancer death (such as cardiovascular events, diabetic ketoacidosis, infection, etc) is a competing event that cannot be ignored, and the traditional Kaplan-Meier method may overestimate the risk of cancer-related death.20,21 Fine-Gray model showed that the subgroup hazard ratio of cancer-related death in TKI-monotherapy group (sHR =1.798, 95% CI 1.288–2.510, p<0.001) was slightly lower than that in Cox model, suggesting that some deaths were driven by non-cancer causes. However, the risk of cancer-related death was significantly lower in the combination-therapy group, even after adjusting for competing risks, further supporting a net benefit of combination therapy in this population. In addition, this study found that the TKI group had a protective correlation with hypertension compared with the TKI+PD-1 group (sHR =0.614, 95% CI 0.444–0.847, P = 0.003), which may be related to the use of antihypertensive medications and warrants further investigation.22

In the TKI-monotherapy group, poor glycemic control (HbA1c ≥7%) was significantly associated with worse OS and PFS. After PSM, multivariate analysis showed that the risk of death was increased by 1.125 times and the risk of cancer-related death was increased by 0.902 times. The detrimental effect of poor glycemic control on prognosis in the TKI monotherapy group can be explained by multiple interconnected biological mechanisms. First, hyperglycemia directly activates the IGF-1R and its downstream PI3K/AKT/mTOR and RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK signaling pathways, which promote hepatocyte proliferation, inhibit apoptosis, induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and enhance tumor invasion.6,23 Second, the high-glucose microenvironment induces oxidative stress and accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which aggravate liver fibrosis and foster a tumor-permissive milieu.6 Third, and importantly in the context of immunotherapy, hyperglycemia has been shown to impair CD8+ T cell function and promote tumor immune evasion. Glucose metabolism reprogramming in HCC cells enables them to escape immune surveillance, and hyperglycemia-induced upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules may further attenuate antitumor immunity.24,25

In addition, TKI drugs (such as sorafenib and lenvatinib) themselves may cause blood glucose fluctuation by affecting islet β cell function or interfering with insulin signaling pathway,26 and patients with poor baseline blood glucose control are more likely to experience treatment‑related adverse events, leading to dose adjustment and treatment interruption, thereby indirectly affecting the efficacy.27 Collectively, these findings suggest that poor glycemic control not only directly fuels tumor progression but also compromises the host immune response—a dual effect that may explain why the prognostic impact of glycemic control is attenuated in patients receiving PD-1 inhibitors, which can partially restore T cell-mediated antitumor immunity.

The prognostic impact of glycemic control was markedly attenuated in the TKI+PD-1 combination group. Before PSM, good glycemic control was associated with better OS and PFS, but this association was lost after PSM. In multivariate analysis, glycemic control was no longer significant for OS, whereas tumor burden and liver function emerged as more stable prognostic factors. We propose several explanations. First, immunotherapy may override the tumor-promoting effects of hyperglycemia. PD-1 inhibitors restore exhausted T-cell function, and the magnitude of this antitumor effect may surpass the direct protumor effects of hyperglycemia. Although hyperglycemia impairs CD8⁺ T-cell function,24 immune checkpoint inhibitors can partially reverse this immunosuppression, but immune checkpoint inhibitors can partially reverse this immunosuppressive state.25 Thus, under strong immune activation, the relative contribution of metabolic factors may be diluted. Second, competing risks from diabetes‑related complications may play a role. Patients receiving combination therapy may survive longer owing to better tumor control, thereby increasing their exposure to long‑term diabetic complications. Although competing‑risk models adjusted for this effect, complex time‑varying interactions between diabetes‑related mortality and tumor progression may have influenced the significance of glycemic control as a prognostic variable.

In addition, we developed a practical prognostic nomogram that integrates treatment strategy, glycemic control, liver function, tumor burden and other key variables. The nomogram exhibited acceptable discrimination (corrected C-index = 0.652) and acceptable calibration, and successfully separated patients into three distinct risk strata (median OS: 19.3, 16.5 and 13.8 months). To our knowledge, this is the first nomogram specifically designed for HCC patients with T2DM receiving first-line systemic therapy (TKI alone vs TKI+PD-1). This tool may assist clinicians in individualizing prognosis assessment and treatment decisions for this population. However, external validation in independent cohorts is warranted before clinical implementation.

It is important to emphasize that this finding should not be misinterpreted as meaning that glycemic management is not a concern in the combination group. Good glycemic control remains essential for reducing infections, cardiovascular events, and successful treatment completion (minimizing interruptions due to adverse events). Future studies are recommended to explore the effects of more refined indicators such as glycemic variability and homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) in the combination therapy group, which may lead to new findings.

This study has the following limitations: first, as a single-center retrospective study, although known confounding factors were balanced by PSM, there may still be unmeasured confounding bias (socioeconomic status, treatment compliance (patients with poor blood glucose control may have worse treatment compliance), combined medication (antihypertensive drugs, etc). Second, the sample size was limited, especially after the stratification according to glucose level in the combination-therapy group, and some subgroup analyses may have been underpowered. Future prospective studies with larger samples are needed to further observe the results. Moreover, the nomogram was derived from a single center and internal bootstrap validation; external validation is needed to confirm its generalizability. Third, in this study, only baseline HbA1c was used to assess glycemic control, which does not reflect dynamic glycemic changes during treatment, whereas glycemic variability may be a more important prognostic indicator.

Conclusion

In conclusion, In HCC patients with T2DM, TKI+PD-1 significantly improved OS and PFS compared to TKI alone. Glycemic control independently predicted prognosis in the TKI group but not in the combination group, suggesting that the prognostic role of metabolic factors is influenced by the treatment regimen. The proposed nomogram provides an individualized prognostic tool for this patient population, pending external validation.
