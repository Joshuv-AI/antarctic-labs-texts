Quantitative detection of macrophage activation in osteosarcoma comparing 18F-FDG PET/CT with ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI
Authors: Raheleh Roudi, Frezghi Habte, Giovanni Marco Saladino, Iryna Vasyliv, Laura Pisani, Jason Wong, Ali Syed, Heike E. Daldrup-Link
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Targeting CD47 with monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapy activates tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Quantitative imaging methods are important for identifying responders to this novel immunotherapy. The purpose of our study was to investigate whether the metabolic activity of osteosarcomas on 18F-FDG PET/CT changes after CD47 mAb treatment. Twenty female BALB/c mice with intratibial murine K7M2 osteosarcomas, twenty female NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice with intratibial human 143B tumors, and twenty male NSG mice with intratibial human MG63.3 tumors were treated with either phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or murine/human CD47 mAb (n = 10 per arm) and underwent either 18F-FDG PET/CT or ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI (n = 5 per group). Differences in tumor metabolic activity (%ID/g max), tumor T2* relaxation times, TAM (%F4/80), and M1 macrophage polarization (%CD80+) between PBS and CD47 mAb-treated mice were estimated from linear regression. The tumor %ID/g max of CD47 mAb-treated K7M2 tumors (6.58 ± 2.42) was not significantly different compared to PBS-treated K7M2 tumors (8.04 ± 2.91; p = 0.17). Similarly, the tumor %ID/g max of CD47 mAb-treated 143B tumors (9.12 ± 1.68) and MG63.3 tumors (5.44 ± 1.99) were not significantly different compared to PBS-treated 143B tumors (9.38 ± 2.32; p = 0.32 ) and MG63.3 tumors (6.02 ± 0.63, p = 0.79 ). By comparison, K7M2 tumors, 143B tumors, and MG63.3 tumors all demonstrated significantly shorter T2* relaxation times after CD47 mAb treatment compared to PBS treatment (all p < 0.001). All tumors exhibited significantly higher TAM (%F4/80+) and M1 macrophage polarization (%CD80+) after CD47 mAb treatment compared to PBS treatment (all p < 0.05). The metabolic activity of osteosarcomas on 18F-FDG PET/CT does not show significant changes after CD47 mAb treatment. This lowers the risk of observing pseudoprogression and misinterpreting drug-induced inflammation, simplifying routine clinical scan interpretation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at .10.1038/s41598-026-56539-w

Introduction

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary bone tumor in children and young adults, with five-year survival rates of less than 20% in patients with metastatic disease1. Mohanty et al. reported that monoclonal antibodies (mAb) against the transmembrane protein Cluster of Differentiation 47 (CD47) improved the survival of mice with osteosarcoma2. Engagement of CD47 on osteosarcoma cells by signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) on tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) inhibits phagocytosis of tumor cells. CD47 mAb blocks interactions between CD47 and SIRPα, thereby activating TAMs to eliminate osteosarcoma cells3.

Quantitative imaging methods are important for identifying responders to this novel immunotherapy. Ferumoxytol, a Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved iron supplement, can be used “off label” as a macrophage marker4,5. Ferumoxytol is composed of superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, which are phagocytosed by TAM and cause a negative (dark) tumor signal on T2-weighted magnetic resonance (MR) images. Increased TAM activity in response to CD47 mAb can be detected by decreased tumor T2* relaxation times6. While ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI is still considered an experimental imaging technique and not widely available, 18F-FDG PET is routinely used for staging and restaging of patients with metastatic osteosarcoma 7.

Lee et al. reported that an increase of more than 39.8% in osteosarcoma SUVmax between baseline and interim (after the first chemotherapy cycle) 18F-FDG PET/CT was associated with significantly worse event-free survival8. Takemori et al. reported that an increase of more than 62% in osteosarcoma SUVmax between baseline and post-neoadjuvant chemotherapy 18F-FDG PET/CT predicted significantly worse progression-free survival and helped to identify high-risk patients9.

The interpretation of 1⁸F-FDG PET/CT in the setting of cancer immunotherapy remains a significant clinical challenge, particularly due to treatment-induced inflammatory responses that can alter metabolic imaging readouts10,11. Immune-based therapies can promote immune cell infiltration and activation, leading to increased glucose metabolism that may mimic disease progression, a phenomenon referred to as pseudoprogression12. While this effect has been well documented in T cell-mediated immunotherapies, including immune checkpoint blockade, its relevance in the context of macrophage-targeted therapies, such as CD47 blockade, remains poorly understood. Given that TAMs are key mediators of the therapeutic effects of CD47 mAbs, it is critical to determine whether TAM activation influences tumor 1⁸F-FDG uptake and thereby confounds metabolic imaging assessments. To date, the impact of macrophage-driven immune responses on 1⁸F-FDG PET/CT readouts in osteosarcoma has not been systematically investigated. In this context, it is unknown whether CD47 mAb-mediated TAM activation in osteosarcomas leads to increased tumor metabolic activity on 18F-FDG PET. This could complicate traditional assessments of metabolic response to therapy, as detailed above. Therefore, the purpose of our study was to investigate whether the metabolic activity of osteosarcomas on 18F-FDG PET/CT changes after CD47 mAb treatment. Results were compared to ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI, a validated TAM imaging technique, and histopathology as the standard of reference.

Methods

Mouse model

Murine K7M2 and human 143B osteosarcoma cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Human MG63.3 osteosarcoma cells were generated by Crystal Mackall (Stanford, CA) and shared with permission from Chand Khanna (National Cancer Institute, National Institutes of Health)13. These cell lines were cultured and maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 2 mM L-glutamine in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. All cell culture reagents were obtained from Gibco, Invitrogen, USA.

Four- to five-week-old female BALB/c mice, as well as female and male NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice, were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA, USA), respectively. Orthotopic osteosarcomas were established by injecting 1 × 10⁶ K7M2 tumor cells into the tibia of twenty female BALB/c mice, 1 × 10⁶ 143B tumor cells into the tibia of twenty female NSG mice, and 1 × 10⁶ MG63.3 tumor cells into the tibia of twenty male NSG mice, as previously described(Fig. 1). 14. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and access to the proximal tibia was achieved using a sterile needle. A suspension of 1 × 10⁶ tumor cells in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was then carefully injected into the medullary cavity using a 30G syringe to ensure localized tumor establishment. Tumor growth was monitored daily by measuring tumor dimensions with digital calipers. The longest diameter (length) and the perpendicular diameter (width) were recorded, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width2)/2. Once tumors had reached a size of 1 cm3, tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into two groups per tumor type: BALB/c mice received intraperitoneal injections of either PBS (n = 10) or murine CD47 mAb (n = 10, 10 mg/kg, clone MIAP301, BioXcell, Lebanon, NH, USA) on days 1, 3, and 5 . Similarly, NSG mice received intraperitoneal injections of PBS (n = 10 in each group) or human CD47 mAb (n = 10; 10 mg/kg, clone B6H12, BioXcell, Lebanon, NH, USA) on days 1, 3, and 5 , as described previously14. Two days after the last CD47 mAb or PBS injection, five mice within each treatment group were referred to 18F-FDG PET, and five mice within each treatment group were referred to MRI.

All animal experiments were approved by the Stanford University Administrative Panel on Laboratory Animal Care (Protocol 24965). All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations for animal welfare and experimentation, including the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized by isoflurane overdose (Isoflurane, Piramal Critical Care, Bethlehem, PA, USA) prior to tissue collection, in accordance with approved institutional animal care and use protocols. This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

PET/CT scanning

Ten mice in each tumor group (murine K7M2, human 143B, and human MG63.3) underwent 18F-FDG PET/CT before (scan 1) and after (scan 2) treatment with PBS (n = 5) or CD47 mAb (n = 5; Fig. 1). Before each scan, mice were fasted overnight with water available ad libitum. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and received an intravenous injection of 100 ± 5 µCi in 100 to 200 μL of 18F-FDG via the tail vein. After an uptake time of 60 min, all mice underwent PET/CT scanning on a micro-PET/CT system (GNEXT, Sofie Biosciences, Culver City, CA). Static PET data were acquired for 10 min in list-mode using a single bed position. CT scans were obtained immediately after PET for attenuation correction and anatomical localization (80 kVp, 150 µA, 360 projections). PET images were reconstructed using the 3-dimensional ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM3D) algorithm with decay and attenuation correction. PET/CT images were analyzed using the Siemens Inveon Research Workspace Analysis software (Siemens Medical Solutions USA, Inc.). Tumor volumes of interest (VOIs) were manually delineated on co-registered PET/CT, and voxel activity concentrations were decay-corrected and normalized to the net injected activity to yield %ID/g (assuming 1 g/mL tissue density). Mean %ID/g was computed as the average across all voxels in the VOI, and %ID/g max as the highest voxel value within the VOI.

MR imaging studies

Ten mice treated with PBS and ten mice treated with CD47 mAb in each tumor group underwent MRI on a 7 Tesla small animal MRI scanner (Bruker Corp, Billerica, MA). MRI scans included an unenhanced pretreatment scan, an unenhanced posttreatment scan, and an enhanced postcontrast scan 24 h after intravenous infusion of ferumoxytol at a dose of 30 mg Fe/kg body weight (Fig. 1). The following pulse sequences were used: A T2-weighted fast spin echo (FSE) sequence for anatomic reference with a repetition time (TR) of 2500 ms, and an echo time (TE) of 33 ms and a multi-gradient echo (MGE) sequence for T2* mapping with a flip angle of 50 degrees, TR of 800 ms, minimum TE of 3.5 ms, echo spacing of 5 ms and 10 echoes. Field of view (FOV) was 2.56 cm × 2.56 cm, and image matrix was 256 × 256 pixels. T2*-maps were calculated based on multi-echo images from MGE, using a mono-exponential decay model mapped in Paravision 7.1 (Bruker Corp, Billerica, MA.). MR images were exported to Osirix version 8.0 (Pixmeo SARL, Bernex, Switzerland), and the tumor T2* relaxation times were measured through operator-defined regions of interest, as described previously16.

Double immunofluorescence staining and image analysis

All mice were sacrificed after completion of 18F-FDG PET/CT or MRI scans, tumors were harvested, fixed in 10% formalin, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices. One set of tumor sections was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E).

To examine the co-expression of F4/80, a murine macrophage marker, with the TAM subtype biomarkers M1 (CD80) or M2 (CD206), we employed double-immunofluorescence staining as previously described14. In human xenograft tumors, infiltrating macrophages are derived from the murine host; therefore, F4/80 was used to identify host-derived TAMs. Briefly, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS (pH 7.4) for 15 min at room temperature, and washed with PBS. The sections were then permeabilized with PBS containing 0.25% Triton X-100 (PBST). Following a 30-min blocking step with 1% BSA in PBST, the samples were incubated with a mixture of two primary antibodies: rat monoclonal anti-F4/80 antibody (1/200, ab6640, Abcam) and rabbit polyclonal anti-CD80 antibody (1/200, ab254579, Abcam) or anti-F4/80 antibody and rabbit polyclonal anti-CD206 (1/200, ab64693, Abcam). After washing with PBS, tissue slides were incubated with a mixture of two secondary antibodies: goat anti-rat Alexa Fluor 488 (A-11006, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 647 (A-21245, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Finally, the specimens were washed with PBS and mounted using a DAPI-containing mounting medium (00–4959-52, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA).

Images of immunofluorescence slides were captured using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (DMI6000 inverted fluorescence microscope; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Images were acquired at × 20 magnification and processed using ImageJ/Fiji and the MATLAB programming platform (MathWorks)17. Mean fluorescence intensity values were extracted from isolated channel images: blue for DAPI, green for F4/80, and red for CD80 or CD206. Mean fluorescence intensities (MFI) of F4/80, CD80, and CD206 was quantified from three representative fields of view per slide, normalized to DAPI and using global thresholds, averaged for each animal and experimental group, and reported as normalized MFI indices (F4/80 MFI, CD80 MFI, and CD206 MFI, respectively). Relative CD80 and CD206 expression within the TAM population was calculated as the MFI ratio of CD80 or CD206 to F4/80, normalized using global thresholds defined for each cell line, and reported as % M1/TAM and % M2/TAM, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Power analysis: The minimal number of study subjects needed to obtain statistically significant results for this study is 5, as determined by power calculations based on our previous data with preclinical MRI studies in mice with osteosarcomas. As an overall indicator of power, we considered the following calculation: If all CD47 mAb-treated mice demonstrated tumor therapy response and all control mice did not respond, then 5 mice per treatment group would provide 90% power at two-sided 5% error to detect differences in imaging biomarker readouts as small as 1.5 standard deviations. A sample size of 5 will provide 90% power at two-sided 5% error to detect correlations between nanoparticle enhancement and TAM density as low as 0.65.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata (Version 18.5). Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation of the mean (SD). Differences in mean tumor T2* relaxation time ΔT2* = (T2* posttreatment postcontrast – T2* posttreatment precontrast), mean tumor %ID/g, max tumor %ID/g, normalized MFI values (%CD80+, %CD206+, and %F4/80+) and normalized MFI ratios (%M1/TAM, %M2/TAM) between experimental groups were obtained from marginal effects estimated by linear regression. The change in pretreatment and posttreatment tumor %ID/g max and tumor %ID/g mean was calculated as Δ%ID/g max = %ID/g max (posttreatment)—%ID/g max (pretreatment) and Δ%ID/g mean = %ID/g mean (posttreatment)—%ID/g mean (pretreatment). Differences in tumor Δ%ID/g max and Δ%ID/g mean values between CD47 mAb and PBS-treated tumors were obtained from linear regression marginal effect estimates. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correlations between the modality approaches (ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI or 1⁸F-FDG PET/CT) and TAM markers were calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation across all three cell lines and two treatment groups combined.

Results

CD47 mAb therapy activates tumor-associated macrophages

To investigate whether CD47 mAb activates TAM in osteosarcomas, we compared M1 macrophage activation among mice bearing K7M2, 143B, and MG63.3 tumors treated with CD47 mAb compared to tumors treated with PBS. Following 1⁸F-FDG PET imaging, tissue analysis demonstrated increased infiltration of F4/80⁺ and M1-like CD80⁺ macrophages across K7M2, 143B, and MG63.3 tumors (K7M2: F4/80, p = 0.004, CD80, p < 0.001; 143B: F4/80, p = 0.01, CD80, p = 0.008; MG63.3: F4/80, p = 0.003, CD80, p = 0.001; Fig. 2A, B). 18F-FDG PET/CT does not detect macrophage activation in osteosarcomas

At baseline, all osteosarcomas demonstrated strong metabolic activity on 18F-FDG PET/CT scans. The tumor metabolic activity did not show significant changes after three doses of TAM-activating CD47 mAb therapy or after three doses of PBS treatment.

PBS-treated control tumors also demonstrated no significant difference in mean tumor %ID/g at baseline (3.30 ± 1.24%ID/g mean) and post PBS treatment (4.06 ± 1.48%ID/g mean, p > 0.05). Correlation of ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI or 18F-FDG PET/CT with TAM biomarkers

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess associations between macrophage-related markers and the measured parameter, using both mean and maximum values expressed as %ID/g. No statistically significant correlations were observed between overall mean or max of %ID/g and TAM (%F4/80), CD80, CD206, F4/80+CD80+, or F4/80+CD206+ (all p > 0.05). In contrast, T2* relaxation time demonstrated strong and statistically significant associations with specific macrophage subsets. Posttreatment postcontrast tumor T2* was inversely correlated with TAM (%F4/80) (r = − 0.66, p < 0.001), CD80 (r = − 0.77, p < 0.001), and F4/80+CD80+ (r = − 0.66, p < 0.001). Additionally, posttreatment postcontrast T2* showed a moderate positive correlation with F4/80+CD206+ (r = 0.45, p = 0.01). No significant association was observed between T2* and CD206 (r = − 0.10, p = 0.57).

Discussion

Our data showed that the metabolic activity of osteosarcomas on 18F-FDG PET/CT does not show significant changes after treatment with TAM-activating immunotherapy. Across three tumor models (K7M2, 143B, and MG63.3), 18F-FDG PET signal did not differ significantly before and after CD47 mAb therapy and was comparable to PBS-treated controls, demonstrating that TAM activation does not confound tumor 18F-FDG PET signal.

As a reference standard, we found evidence of TAM activation in CD47 mAb-treated tumors on ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI and histology. No significant changes in %ID/g shown on 18F-FDG PET/CT suggest that this imaging modality cannot distinguish between tumors that demonstrate TAM activation versus those that do not. This is clinically important because CD47 mAb mediated TAM activation did not produce PET/CT features consistent with pseudo-progression in our mouse models. In contrast, T cell–based immunotherapies can drive significant localized immune cell infiltration and result in increased tumor 18F-FDG avidity, presenting the diagnostic challenge of pseudo-progression18,19.

In our study, we observed no significant change in 18F-FDG tumor uptake following CD47 mAb therapy, despite histologic evidence of TAM activation, indicating that 18F-FDG PET did not reflect macrophage-related immune responses under these conditions. Although TAM activation could theoretically increase 18F-FDG uptake relative to baseline, this was not observed. Conversely, an effective therapy would be expected to reduce 18F-FDG uptake of the tumor tissue compared with PBS-treated controls on posttreatment imaging studies. However, neither CD47 mAb-treated nor PBS-treated tumors showed a measurable metabolic response within the short follow-up interval of our study. Notably, the absence of a metabolic response in our model is consistent with the limited clinical efficacy reported for CD47-targeted therapies20.

The absence of significant changes in 18F-FDG uptake despite increased macrophage activation may be explained by underlying metabolic mechanisms. 18F-FDG PET primarily reflects cellular glucose uptake and glycolytic activity, which are typically dominated by highly metabolically active tumor cells exhibiting aerobic glycolysis21,22. Wang et al. described that activated macrophages can undergo metabolic reprogramming toward increased glycolysis23. However, the relative contribution of TAM to the overall tumor metabolic signal is insufficient to produce detectable changes in whole-tumor 18F-FDG uptake24. An et al. showed that the level of 18F-FDG uptake in metastatic brain lesions was not significantly correlated with immune cell infiltration, including tumor-associated macrophages24. This suggests that, although TAM are metabolically active, their relative contribution to the overall tumor FDG signal is generally insufficient to produce detectable changes in whole-tumor 18F-FDG uptake. These findings highlight that 18F-FDG PET predominantly reflects tumor cell glycolysis rather than immune cell activity. Li et al. evaluated the relationship between 18F-FDG PET/CT metabolic parameters and immune biomarker expression in lung adenocarcinoma25. They found that PET metrics, including SUV, did not strongly correlate with markers of immune infiltration, suggesting that tumor cell glycolysis remains the dominant contributor to FDG uptake in these lesions. This supports our observation that, although macrophage activation and functional immune responses may be enhanced, such changes are not necessarily reflected as increases in tissue-level glycolytic flux measurable by PET imaging. In addition, CD47-targeted therapy primarily promotes macrophage-mediated phagocytosis and immune effector function, processes that may not be associated with substantial increases in tissue-level glycolytic flux measurable by PET imaging. In a clinical context, a stable 18F-FDG PET signal on 18F-FDG PET/CT or PET/MR scans after CD47 mAb therapy indicates stable disease, while an increased 18F-FDG uptake is highly indicative of genuine progressive metabolic disease, with low risk of confounding information from immune cell activation.

Given the clinical translation of macrophage-directed therapies, including CD47-SIRPα blockade26, Colony-Stimulating Factor-1 Receptor (CSF1R) inhibitors27, and CD40 agonists28, our findings will have to be confirmed with these additional immunotherapies.

While there is growing interest in alternative PET imaging approaches that specifically target TAM, the clinical standard for treatment monitoring of bone sarcomas remains MRI and 18F-FDG PET29. While the logistical burden of an additional imaging test makes it unlikely that 18F-FDG PET will be replaced in a clinical setting, it is most critical to understand what information we can derive from these traditional imaging studies. In addition, a variety of promising new TAM-specific tracers are under development, including those targeting the translocator protein (TSPO), such as 11C-PK11195 or 18F-DPA-714, for imaging activated macrophages in various inflammatory and oncologic settings30,31 as well as radiolabeled anti-CD163 nanobodies and peptides for imaging alternatively activated (M2-like) TAMs32. Additional strategies include radiolabeled antibodies against CD47 and SIRPα33,34, and radiotracers for imaging cytokine or chemokine receptors such as CCR235.

While this study provides important insights, it also has limitations that should be acknowledged and addressed in future work. First, the experiments were conducted in murine osteosarcoma models, which, although useful for mechanistic studies, do not fully recapitulate the heterogeneity and immune complexity of human tumors. Future studies in patients could help bridge this translational gap. Second, human xenograft models in NSG mice have deficiencies in innate immunity, particularly in macrophage development and function, which may affect their ability to fully capture physiologic TAM interactions, particularly polarization dynamics after CD47-targeted therapy. Nonetheless, xenograft models are essential for assessing imaging readouts in human tumors, as they preserve human CD47 biology, human tumor-stroma architecture, and patterns of therapeutic distribution that are not recapitulated in murine models and are critical for translational relevance. We included studies of murine tumors in immunocompetent BALB/c mice to confirm that the observed 18F-FDG PET and MRI signals track macrophage responses under intact immune conditions. This dual-model approach reinforces both the clinical relevance and the immunologic integrity of the findings. Third, only a single posttreatment imaging time point was assessed, which may have missed transient or delayed metabolic changes following CD47 blockade. Future longitudinal imaging studies will enable further characterization of dynamic TAM responses over time. Finally, although ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI and macrophage immunostaining provided evidence of TAM activation, the limited panel of macrophage markers used (F4/80, CD80, CD206) may not fully capture the diversity of macrophage polarization states. Expanding histologic analyses to include additional markers or using single-cell transcriptomics could offer a more comprehensive assessment of immune modulation after CD47 mAb therapy.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important insights into the imaging characteristics of osteosarcomas treated with CD47 mAb therapy. Our findings demonstrate that 18F-FDG PET/CT is insensitive to TAM activation, and that tumor metabolic activity remains unchanged after treatment, reducing the risk of pseudoprogression misinterpretation. In contrast, ferumoxytol-enhanced MRI effectively detected TAM activation, confirming its potential as a sensitive biomarker for monitoring macrophage-targeted immunotherapies. Together, these results highlight the complementary roles of metabolic and macrophage-specific imaging and lay the groundwork for developing more accurate imaging strategies to guide and assess emerging macrophage-based cancer therapies, perhaps through integrated 18F-FDG PET/MR approaches.

Conclusions

In summary, our study demonstrates that TAM activation via CD47 blockade does not lead to a significant increase in 18F-FDG uptake across three osteosarcoma models (K7M2, 143B, and MG63.3). These findings indicate that 18F-FDG PET is not sensitive for detecting TAM-mediated immunologic changes, as alterations in glucose metabolism do not correlate with immune activation within the tumor microenvironment. Furthermore, the absence of tumor metabolic signal changes suggests that pseudoprogression is unlikely to confound imaging results in this setting, as TAM activation does not substantially alter tumor glucose metabolism. These findings highlight the need for alternative imaging modalities, such as ferumoxytol enhanced-MRI, to directly monitor TAM activation and macrophage-targeted immunotherapeutic effects. Importantly, the lack of tumor 18F-FDG metabolic response in our preclinical models is consistent with the limited clinical responses reported in clinical trials of CD47-targeted therapies.
