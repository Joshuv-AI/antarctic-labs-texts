Selenium Nanoparticles-Encapsulated Exosomes That Mitigate Acute Liver Injury via an Oral Carboxymethyl Chitosan/Oxidized Dextran Hydrogel
Authors: Yue Zhang, Qiuxia Zheng, Zongbin Sun, Xueping Zhao, Yanchen Li, Yihua Zhou, Liang Tian, Shang Li, Chongyang Bai, Honglong Zhang, Jun Yan, Jia Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Acute liver injury (ALI), a major global health concern, is associated with limited therapeutic options due to the absence of specific drugs. The key pathological features of ALI, including acute oxidative stress and impaired liver function, provide targets for therapeutic interventions. Herein, an innovative strategy was developed that combines selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) with mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSCs-Exos), utilizing their antioxidant and regenerative properties. In addition, to improve SeNP stability and minimize the rapid in vivo clearance of MSCs-Exos, a stable hydrogel-based delivery platform (Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs) was designed. The Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs system demonstrated excellent stability and effectively delivered Exo-SeNPs into liver cells. Subsequent in vivo experiments confirmed that this system significantly alleviated liver damage by enhancing liver function, suppressing oxidative stress, and accelerating regeneration. This study presents an oral SeNPs-Exos delivery system as a novel approach to mitigate ALI and restore liver function.

Introduction

Acute liver injury (ALI) is a common clinical condition characterized by extensive hepatic cellular damage within a short time frame. It is characterized by an abrupt onset, rapid progression, and high mortality. In severe cases, this condition may progress to liver failure, thereby posing a significant threat to life. Currently, clinical treatments primarily use broad-spectrum antioxidants, such as N-acetylcysteine and ascorbic acid. However, the efficacy of these agents is limited to the acute phase of liver injury. Rapid excretion and poor accumulation at the injury site hinder reparative efficacy. Therefore, developing novel, safe, and efficient biological agents for ALI treatment has become a crucial research objective.

Selenium, an essential trace element, demonstrates significant effects in antitumor activity, oxidative stress regulation, and anti-inflammatory response. − Compared with traditional selenium forms, selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) have garnered significant attention due to their enhanced biological activity and reduced toxicity. Du et al. demonstrated that SeNPs significantly mitigate cadmium-induced acute hepatic toxicity by modulating the Nrf2 signaling pathway involved in oxidative stress. Amin et al. demonstrated that SeNPs protect against acetaminophen-induced hepatotoxicity by enhancing liver function and modulating oxidative stress-related factors. The clinical application of SeNPs is limited by instability and aggregation, compromising their efficacy. Therefore, enhancing their stability and bioavailability through stabilizers is vital for advancing liver injury treatments.

In regenerative medicine, stem cells have become a prominent research focus due to their self-renewal capacity and multidirectional differentiation potential. Among these, human-umbilical-cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (huc-MSCs) primarily mediate their biological effects through the secretion of exosomes (Exos). Exos are extracellular vesicles ranging from 30 to 200 nm in diameter. These vesicles are enriched with bioactive components from their parental cells, including RNA and proteins, and exhibit excellent biocompatibility and low immunogenicity. Exos can precisely modulate the microenvironment of damaged tissues via paracrine mechanisms, thereby influencing local cellular behavior and promoting tissue repair. As a cell-free therapy, Exos effectively promote tissue repair and regeneration. MSC-EVs can modulate the mitochondrial autophagy-associated signaling pathway (E2F1-Atg4B signaling pathway) by transferring DDX5, thereby revealing their nanotherapeutic potential in antiaging and regeneration promotion. Furthermore, by downregulating smoothened, MSCs-Exos suppress the activation and fibrogenesis of hepatic stellate cells, thereby protecting liver function. Moreover, as a natural biologically active carrier, Exos derived from mesenchymal stem cells not only exhibit inherent targeting capability and tissue regeneration potential but also effectively enhance the stability and bioavailability of therapeutic agents. Jang et al. obtained the FDX@mEVs complex by combining the tumor-activated prodrug (FDX) with milk-derived extracellular vesicles (mEVs). Following oral administration of FDX@mEVs, notable antitumor efficacy and enhanced safety were demonstrated in colon tumor-bearing mice. Fang et al. reported ASCs-Exo carrying quercetin and vitamin A (Exo+que) and used it to treat acute liver injury induced by CCl4 in mice. Therefore, Exos may improve SeNPs stability as an Exos-SeNPs combined treatment method. However, a barrier to Exos administration is their rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, which limits their circulation time in vivo and impairs their therapeutic efficacy, necessitating the development of a suitable drug delivery system to transport and release these nanocarriers.

Hydrogels, as an intelligent material featuring high biocompatibility, tunable mechanical properties, and multifunctional drug delivery capabilities, can establish a protective microenvironment to mitigate inflammatory stimuli. Recently, they have demonstrated considerable potential for application in the treatment of liver diseases. To promote liver regeneration, Jin et al. created a biomimetic hydrogel for hepatocyte delivery that mitigates tissue necrosis and inflammation. Oxidation of dextran converts its hydroxyl groups into aldehydes, yielding oxidized dextran (ODex). This modification enhances dextran’s hydrophilicity, colloidal stability, and introduces a net negative charge, thereby improving its biocompatibility and dispersibility for biological applications. ODex demonstrates high water solubility and no cytotoxic effects upon administration, highlighting its potential as a promising material for biomedical applications. Carboxymethyl chitosan (CMC) surpasses chitosan with an enhanced water solubility, biocompatibility, and moisturizing ability. These advantages make it a highly promising material for advanced biomedical applications, especially in developing multifunctional hydrogels. ODex and CMC spontaneously cross-link via the Schiff base reaction at room temperature, forming a hydrogel with controllable degradability and sustained drug release capabilities. This hydrogel facilitates efficient drug encapsulation and protects against physiological clearance, thereby prolonging residence time in vivo and enhancing therapeutic efficacy. , Given its safety and stability, we administered the hydrogel orally. This allowed Exos-SeNPs to be absorbed through the digestive tract and travel via the portal vein to the liver, enhancing the Exos accumulation.

In this study, a novel and stable delivery strategy, namely, the Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs composite system, was developed that is expected to serve as a potential therapeutic approach for liver disease (Scheme ). The physicochemical characteristics of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs were systematically investigated using various methods, including morphological analysis, in vitro release kinetics, and cellular uptake efficiency. To overcome challenges related to rapid drug clearance and immune rejection, an oral hydrogel-based delivery system was employed. The therapeutic efficacy of the Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs complex in treating ALI was assessed through the analysis of biochemical markers, antioxidant capacity, and liver regeneration-related parameters in a mouse model.

Materials and Methods

Materials

PE-conjugated antihuman CD73, PE-conjugated antihuman CD90, PE-conjugated anti-CD105, APC-conjugated antihuman CD34, APC-conjugated antihuman CD45, and APC-conjugated antihuman HLA-DR antibodies were purchased from BioLegend (USA). Exos Concentration Solution (ECS) reagents were obtained from Shanghai Yumeibo Biotechnolog (China). SeNPs were acquired from Nanjing Shennuoqing Biotechnology (China). Dextran, CMC (degree of substitution ≥ 90%), and sodium periodate (NaIO4, purity ≥ 99.5%) were sourced from Shanghai Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology (China). The live/dead cell double staining kit, ABTS, DPPH, ALT, and AST assay kits were all obtained from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology (China). AML12 mouse liver cells were acquired from Wuhan BOSTER Biotechnology (China).

Preparation and Characterization of MSCs-Exos

Following the acquisition of informed consent from donors, human umbilical cords were collected at the First Hospital of Lanzhou University. The tissues were processed under sterile conditions, transferred to preservation solution containers, and promptly transported to the laboratory at 4 °C for further processing. The extraction and culture of huc-MSCs were conducted as previously reported by Mao et al. (Figure S1). Huc-MSCs at passages 3 to 7 were cultured in a CO2 incubator under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) using serum-containing medium (FBS: DMEM/F12 = 1:9). When cell confluency reached 70–80%, the serum-containing medium was removed, and the cells were washed three times with PBS, followed by incubation in serum-free medium (DMEM/F12) for 48 to 72 h. The culture medium was harvested and centrifuged at 3000 g for 5 min to eliminate the cell debris. Subsequently, the supernatant was combined with the ECS reagent using a vortex oscillator and incubated at 4 °C overnight. On the following day, the mixture was ultracentrifuged at 10000g for 60 min, after which the supernatant was discarded. The resulting pellet was enriched with Exos particles. The pellet was resuspended in PBS, transferred to a fresh 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, and centrifuged at 12000g for 2 min. The final supernatant was kept for subsequent analysis.

The particle size distribution of the Exos was determined by using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA; NanoSight NS300, Malvern, UK). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Tecnai G2 Spirit, FEI, USA) was used to assess the morphology of the Exos. In addition, Western blotting was used to detect the expression of membrane protein markers TSG101, CD63, and CD9.

Development and Characterization of Exos-SeNPs

The Exos solution and SeNPs solution were thoroughly combined at a ratio of 5:1 in a PBS buffer system. After coincubation at 37 °C for 2 h, the mixture was ultrafiltered using a 100 kDa centrifugal filter to eliminate unbound SeNPs, yielding Exos-SeNPs.

Characterization of Exos-SeNPs

Encapsulation Efficiency and Drug Loading of Exos-SeNPs

Initially, the absorbance of SeNPs was determined using UV spectrophotometry. Subsequently, an appropriate volume of the 1 mg/mL SeNPs stock solution was diluted to create working solutions with concentrations of 0, 5, 10, and 15 μg/mL. A standard curve was generated based on these measurements, and the absorbance of Exos-SeNPs was likewise determined. Ultimately, the encapsulation efficiency and drug loading capacity of SeNPs in Exos were computed using the following formula: EE% = W 0/W 1 × 100%, DL% = W 0/W 2 × 100%, where W 0 represents the mass of loaded SeNPs, W 1 denotes the total mass of added SeNPs, and W 2 indicates the total mass of Exos-SeNPs.

Structural Characterization of Exos-SeNPs

A 10-μL Exos-SeNPs and SeNPs sample was placed onto a copper grid and allowed to settle for 1 min. After the supernatant was removed using filter paper, a solution of uranyl acetate and hydrogen peroxide was added to the copper grid for staining for 1 min, followed by the removal of the supernatant. After drying at room temperature, images were captured by using TEM (HT7800, HITACHI, Japan).

Particle Size and Zeta Potential Analysis of Exos-SeNPs

Exos and Exos-SeNPs (100 μL each) were diluted 10 times with ultrapure water, equilibrated at 25 °C for 2 min, and then filtered through a 0.22-μm microporous membrane. Their particle size distributions were measured by using NTA.

Stability of SeNPs and Exos-SeNPs

SeNPs (1 mL, containing 65 μg) and Exos-SeNPs (1 mL, containing 500 μg of Exos and 65 μg of SeNPs) were incubated in buffer solutions of varying pH (pH 1.5, 6.8, and 7.4). The degree of dispersion was assessed after 24 h.

Preparation of the CMC/ODex Hydrogel

Preparation of the Oxidized Dextran (ODex)

The preparation of ODex was based on a previous report. The hydroxyl groups of dextran were oxidized to aldehyde groups by using sodium periodate to prepare ODex. A mixture of sodium periodate and dextran solutions was reacted in the dark at 25 °C for 4 h. Then, 2 mL of ethylene glycol was added to terminate the reaction. Subsequently, the mixture was continuously filtered through a 3500 Da dialysis bag for 3 days to remove residual sodium periodate and other small molecules. Finally, the dialysis solution was freeze-dried by using a freeze-dryer to prepare ODex.

Preparation of the CMC/ODex Hydrogel

To prepare the CMC/ODex hydrogel, 0.04 g of ODex and 0.06 g of CMC were separately dissolved in 2 mL of PBS. The CMC/ODex hydrogel (Hydrogel) was formed by thoroughly mixing the two solutions and allowing the reaction to complete.

Characterization of Hydrogel

Gelation Time

A 500 μL ODex solution was thoroughly mixed with a 500 μL CMC solution in a transparent ampule. The gelation time was determined using the tube inversion method, with the criterion for successful gelation being that the mixture does not flow when the ampule is inverted for 30 s.

Injectability

Hydrogel was placed in a pipette and injected in the air or in PBS to verify its injectability.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

The chemical groups and bonds of Dextran, ODex, CMC, and Hydrogel were detected by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) in the wavenumber range of 4000–500 cm–1.

Rheological Performance

The rheological properties of the hydrogel were evaluated by using a rotational rheometer (MCR302, Anton Paar, Austria). First, at a human body temperature of 37 °C, an amplitude sweep test was conducted on the hydrogel with an amplitude range from 1% to 500% and a frequency of 1 rad/s, measuring the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G’’). Second, a frequency sweep was performed at a strain of 1%, with an angular frequency range of 1–100 rad/s. Finally, the strain was set to vary from 1% to 500% with an interval time of 50 s to assess the self-healing and recovery viscoelastic properties of the hydrogel.

Preparation of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs

After mixing 2% ODex and Exos-SeNPs in equal volumes, they were thoroughly mixed with 3% CMC. After a full reaction, Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs were obtained.

Characterization of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs

Morphological and Elemental Analysis of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs

Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs were placed in a 24-well plate and freeze-dried. The micromorphological characteristics of the hydrogel were measured using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Sigma 360, ZEISS, German), and the contents of C, O, N, P, and Se in Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs were analyzed using energy-dispersive spectrometry (EDS).

3D Images of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs

Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs were stained with PKH26 to obtain fluorescently labeled Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs, and 3D imaging of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs was performed by using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM900, ZEISS, Germany).

Swelling Performance of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs

Hydrogel, Hydrogel-Exos, and Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs were individually placed in glass containers containing 37 °C buffer solutions with varying pH values (pH = 1.5, 6.8, and 7.4). The samples were taken out at regular intervals, the excess water on the surface was gently absorbed, and the samples were weighed until the weight remained constant. The formula for calculating the swelling ratio is as follows: swelling ratio% = (W t – W 0)/W 0 × 100%, where W t is the weight of the sample at the measurement time and W 0 is the initial weight of the sample.

Release Performance of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs

Hydrogel-Exos and Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs were stained with PKH26 and dispersed in a centrifuge tube. After gelation was completed at 37 °C, 3 mL of buffer solutions with pH values of 1.5, 6.8, and 7.4 were added separately. The samples were then incubated on a shaker at 500 rpm under controlled conditions. The fluorescence of the supernatant was measured daily to determine the released Exos and Exos-SeNPs. The cumulative release amount was determined over three consecutive days.

Biosafety of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs

Cytotoxicity Assessment

To clarify the impact of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs on the survival and status of AML12 cells, AML12 cells were cultured in the hydrogel extract for 24 h. Then, the live cells and dead cells were stained green and red, respectively, with Calcein-AM and PI from the live/dead cell double staining kit and observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope for the live/dead cell live/dead test.

Blood Compatibility

One healthy 8-week-old male mouse was randomly selected. Its anticoagulant whole blood was collected and centrifuged. Then, 250 μL of PBS was added to 200 μL of blood to dilute it, and 100 μL of the diluted blood was added to distilled water, physiological saline, and hydrogel. After incubation at 37 °C for 1 h, the samples were centrifuged, and the absorbance of the supernatant at 545 nm was measured by an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader. The data were substituted into the hemolysis rate formula to calculate the hemolysis rate. The distilled water group was used as the positive control (100% hemolysis), and the physiological saline group was used as the negative control. The hemolysis rate was calculated using the following formula: HR% = (OD ts – OD nc)/(OD pc – OD nc) × 100%, where OD nc, OD pc, and OD ts are the absorbance of distilled water, physiological saline, and the sample, respectively.

Tissue Compatibility

Thirty male C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks old, weighing 19–21 g) were randomly divided into the control, Hydrogel, Hydrogel-Exos, Hydrogel-SeNPs, and Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs groups. After 7 days of adaptation, the mice were administered drugs once a day for 3 consecutive days. The condition of the mice was observed after the last administration. Two weeks later, the mice were anesthetized; blood was collected from the eyeballs for biochemical index determination, and the main organs were taken for hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining to observe tissue compatibility.

Antioxidant Activity of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs

Hydrogel formulations encapsulating Exos-SeNPs at varying concentrations were prepared, where the Exos concentrations were 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 μg/mL, and the SeNPs concentration was increased accordingly based on the drug loading rate. ABTS or DPPH working solution was added according to the kit instructions, mixed thoroughly, and left to stand at room temperature in the dark. The ABTS and DPPH scavenging rates were detected by measuring the absorbance at 405 and 515 nm, respectively. The scavenging rate was calculated by using the following formula: D% = ((A 0 – (A 2 – A 1))/A 0) × 100% where A 1 is the absorbance of the sample to be tested + working solution, A 2 is the absorbance of the sample to be tested + water, and A 0 is the absorbance of water + working solution.

In Vitro Evaluation of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs on ALI

Evaluation of Cellular Uptake Capacity

Mouse hepatocytes (AML12 cells) were obtained from Wuhan BOSTER Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and maintained in AML12 Cell Complete Medium (Procell Life Science & Technology, China) under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) in a humidified incubator according to manufacturer instructions and established cell culture protocols.

The hydrogel was incubated with the culture medium at a ratio of 1 g/mL for 24 h. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22-μm membrane filter to obtain the hydrogel extract. AML12 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and incubated for 12 h. Then, the cells were cultured with the PKH26-labeled Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs extract for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were fixed and washed with PBS. The cell nuclei were stained with DAPI, and the fluorescence intensity and distribution were observed by a fluorescence microscope after incubation.

Evaluation of Cell Viability

AML12 cells were inoculated into 96-well plates, and different concentrations of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs extracts were added (where the Exos concentration was 25, 50, 100, 200, and 400 μg/mL, and the SeNPs concentration was increased accordingly based on the drug loading rate). The control group consisted of AML12 cells cultured without the addition of the Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs extract, and the blank group had no cells added. After coculturing for 24 h, CCK-8 was added to assess cell viability. The optical density (OD) value was measured using a microplate reader, and the cell viability was calculated according to the following formula: relative cell viability% = (OD t – OD 0)/(OD c - OD 0) × 100%, where OD t, OD c, and OD 0 represent the OD values of the experimental, control, and blank groups, respectively.

Evaluation of the APAP-Induced Cellular Oxidative Damage Model

AML12 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and treated with acetaminophen (APAP) solutions at concentrations of 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 mg/mL. AML12 cells were cultured without APAP in the control group, while no cells were seeded in the blank group. After coculturing for 12 h, CCK-8 reagent was added to assess cell viability, and the optimal modeling concentration of APAP was determined based on the measurement results.

Evaluation of Cell Migration Ability

AML12 cells were seeded in 6-well plates. Once they reached confluence, scratches were made. The model group was treated with a medium containing APAP (concentration of 1 mg/mL), while the treatment group was treated with medium containing APAP (concentration of 1 mg/mL) and the extract of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs (Exos concentration of 400 μg/mL and SeNPs concentration of 52.92 μg/mL). The migration rate of cells at different time points was calculated.

Evaluation of Antioxidant Capacity

AML12 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and allowed to adhere. An injury model was induced by APAP (concentration of 1 mg/mL). After 12 h, the Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs extract was added for intervention treatment (Exos concentration of 400 μg/mL and SeNPs concentration of 52.92 μg/mL). The cells were cocultured for 24 h, and then the MDA protein level was determined. In addition, BODIPY 581/591 C11 staining solution was added to the intervened cells, which were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by fluorescence image acquisition.

To detect ROS, an appropriate volume of diluted DCFH-DA was added to the intervening cells, which were then incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. Images were collected by using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Small Animal In Vivo Imaging

Fluorescence images were obtained using an IVIS Spectrum Imaging System (VISQUE InVivo Smart-LF, Korea) at different time points. Regions of interest were quantified via the total radiant efficiency [(p/s)/(μW/cm2)].

Evaluation of the Tissue Distribution of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs

To evaluate the distribution of orally administered Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs in mice, we conducted an in vivo fluorescence tracing experiment. Exos were stained with PKH26 and Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs were prepared. Six healthy adult C57BL/6J mice were gavaged with Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs. After 6 h, the intestines, livers, and portal vein blood of the mice were collected to observe the distribution of PKH26-Exos-SeNPs.

In vivo Evaluation of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs on ALI

Animals and Experimental Design

To investigate the protective effect of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs on ALI, 60 male C57BL/6J mice (6 weeks old, weighing 19–21 g) were provided by the Lanzhou Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The temperature was maintained at 25 °C, with a 12 h light–dark cycle. All animals were handled in strict accordance with the guidelines of the National Research Council for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. In addition, the animal care and use committee officially approved the experimental procedures (LDYYLL-2025–870).

The C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into the Control, Model, Hydrogel (100 μL), Hydrogel-Exos (100 μL, containing 100 μg Exos), Hydrogel-SeNPs (100 μL, containing 13 μg SeNPs), Exos (100 μL, containing 100 μg Exos), SeNPs (100 μL, containing 13 μg SeNPs), Exos-SeNPs (100 μL, containing 100 μg Exos and 13 μg SeNPs), Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs-L (100 μL, equivalent to 100 μg Exos and 13 μg SeNPs), and Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs groups (100 μL, equivalent to 200 μg Exos and 26 μg SeNPs). After 7 days of adaptation in an SPF-level animal room, all mice except those in the control group were intraperitoneally injected with APAP (300 mg/kg). Twenty-four h later, each group was gavaged once a day.

Biochemical Indicators and Pathological Histology

After three consecutive days of intragastric administration, all mice were subjected to a 12 h fast, followed by blood collection via the orbital sinus. The collected blood was allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h and then centrifuged at 4 °C (3500 rpm, 15 min) to isolate serum, which was subsequently used to evaluate postsurgical liver function recovery. In addition, liver images were captured, and partial liver tissues were excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for HE staining or further analysis of antibody expression levels (Ki-67, PCNA, INOS, and Ly6g) using immunohistochemistry or immunofluorescence techniques. The remaining liver tissues were stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Transcriptome Sequencing

RNA isolation and purification were performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Specifically, TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog number: 15596018) was employed for sample processing in the control, model, and Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs groups (hereafter referred to as Exos-Se). The quantity and purity of the total RNA were assessed by using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Wilmington, DE, USA). RNA integrity was evaluated using a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, USA). Subsequently, mRNA was specifically enriched for cDNA synthesis and double-stranded DNA preparation, followed by size selection and purification using magnetic beads. Finally, paired-end sequencing (PE150) was performed on an Illumina NovaseqTM 6000 platform (LianChuan Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) following the standard protocol.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and t-tests were used to analyze the differences between the two groups. P-values are indicated by asterisks (*) in the figures, with significance at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. ns: nonsignificant.

Results

Characterization and Validation of Huc-MSCs and MSCs-Exos

During the third-generation cell culture, cells were subjected to centrifugation and staining, followed by a flow cytometry analysis of their immunophenotypes. The cells exhibited positive expression for CD90, CD73, and CD105, while showing negative expression for CD34, CD45, and HLA-DR (Figure S2). These findings are consistent with the characteristic surface marker profile of MSCs. , Subsequently, Exos were isolated from the cell supernatant by using ECS reagents, yielding MSCs-Exos (Figure A). TEM revealed that the isolated vesicles exhibited typical cup-shaped or tea-cup-shaped morphologies with well-defined edges (Figure B). NTA further confirmed that the particle size distribution was predominantly centered around 117.9 nm, which is within the standard size range for Exos (Figure D). In addition, Western blot analysis demonstrated positive expression of the Exos-specific markers TSG101, CD63, and CD9, thereby corroborating the identity of the isolated vesicles as Exos (Figures C and S3). Collectively, these findings confirm the successful isolation of MSCs-Exos.

Design and Characterization of Exos-SeNPs

The microstructure and particle size of SeNPs were assessed using TEM. As shown in Figure S4, SeNPs exhibited a regular spherical morphology with diameters ranging from 20 to 30 nm and demonstrated excellent dispersibility. However, after being placed for 24 h, obvious aggregation of SeNPs was observed (Figure S4). Exos-SeNPs were prepared through coincubation at 37 °C followed by ultrafiltration centrifugation. To rigorously confirm the successful preparation of Exos-SeNPs, TEM observations were conducted. As shown in Figure E, simple physical mixing of Exos and SeNPs did not result in the encapsulation of SeNPs by Exos. In contrast, Figure F clearly shows that SeNPs were localized both within the membrane structure and on the surface of Exos, thereby providing direct morphological evidence supporting the successful fabrication of Exos-SeNPs. These findings demonstrate the effectiveness and feasibility of our method. Notably, the absolute value of the zeta potential for Exos-SeNPs was significantly enhanced to 22.7 mV, compared with that of Exos and SeNPs alone (Figure G).

UV–vis was used to assess the encapsulation efficiency and drug loading capacity of the Exos-SeNPs. SeNPs exhibited a prominent absorption peak at 208 nm, whereas Exos displayed negligible absorption at this wavelength. Exos-SeNPs also presented an absorption peak at 208 nm, which was consistent with the characteristic peak of SeNPs (Figure H). Despite the relatively weak absorption intensity resulting from the low concentration of loaded SeNPs, this confirmed the successful loading of SeNPs onto Exos. Subsequently, the absorbance of the SeNPs solution at the maximum absorption wavelength of 208 nm was measured using a UV–vis spectrophotometer, and the linear regression equation was established as y = 0.224x + 0.0114 (R 2 = 0.9998) (Figure S5). Based on these calculations, the encapsulation efficiency of SeNPs in Exos was determined to be 66.15%, with a drug loading capacity of 11.68% (Table S1). In addition, NTA revealed particle sizes of 145.85 and 166.58 nm for Exos and Exos-SeNPs, respectively (Figure I). SeNPs exhibit pronounced aggregation and precipitation across varying pH conditions, whereas Exos-SeNPs display a relatively improved stability. Notably, at physiological pH 7.4, Exos-SeNPs maintain excellent dispersibility (Figure S6).

Preparation and Characterization of Hydrogel

Building on previous studies, we successfully synthesized ODex by oxidizing a portion of the hydroxyl groups in dextran to aldehyde groups using sodium periodate (Figure A). As shown in Figure B, FTIR analysis revealed a weak characteristic peak at 1732 cm–1 corresponding to the aldehyde group (−CHO), thereby confirming the successful synthesis of ODex. A hydrogel was prepared by mixing ODex with CMC (Figure C). The resultant hydrogel was transferred to a vial, and it was observed that the material ceased to flow after 30 s, confirming the successful formation of Hydrogel (Figure D). As depicted in Figure D, Hydrogel can be smoothly extruded through a syringe in PBS solution and is capable of forming the text “Hydrogel”, which confirms its notable shear-thinning properties.

(D) Photographs illustrating the gelation performance of Hydrogel. (E) FTIR spectra of ODex, CMC, and Hydrogel. (F–H) Rheological behavior of Hydrogel.

To further validate the synthesis of Hydrogel at the structural level, FTIR analysis was conducted to examine the chemical groups and bonding characteristics of ODex, CMC, and the CMC/ODex complex (Hydrogel). As shown in Figure E, the broad absorption peak observed at 1669 cm–1 corresponds to the −NC– stretching vibration, which originates from the formation of Schiff base bonds within the hydrogel or its outer layer. This provides molecular-level evidence of the successful synthesis of Hydrogel.

Amplitude sweep analysis revealed that, in the low-strain region (1%–20%), both the G’ and G’’ remained constant, indicating that the hydrogel maintained its structural integrity with stable intermolecular interactions, thereby predominantly exhibiting elastic behavior. As the strain increased, both G’ and G’’ exhibit changes, suggesting that the hydrogel structure began to undergo modification (Figure F). Based on the amplitude sweep results, a strain of 1% was selected for the frequency sweep analysis. Over the entire angular frequency testing range (1–100 rad/s), G’ remained consistently higher than G’’, indicating that the hydrogel predominantly exhibited elastic behavior. However, as the angular frequency increased, both G’ and G’’ progressively increased, suggesting that the elasticity and viscosity of the hydrogel were enhanced under high-frequency conditions (Figure G). Furthermore, the results of multiple alternating step-strain cycling experiments demonstrate that both G’ and G’’ fully recovered to their initial values after cyclic loading, indicating that the hydrogel exhibits robust repeated self-healing capability and the ability to restore its viscoelastic properties (Figure H).

Synthesis and Characterization of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs

Following the successful synthesis of Hydrogel and Exos-SeNPs, we synthesized Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs and conducted a comprehensive characterization analysis. SEM observations revealed that Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs exhibited a typical honeycomb-like porous structure (Figure A). The pore size of Hydrogel was 138.68 ± 32.68 μm, whereas that of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs decreased to 120.31 ± 36.66 μm (Figure S7 and Table S2). This decrease can be attributed to the contraction of the hydrogel polymer chains upon loading with Exos-SeNPs. The uptake experiment results showed that PKH26-labeled Exos-SeNPs exhibited a ring-like structure and surrounded the nuclei of AML12 cells (Figure A). In addition, AML12 cells were subjected to a 12-h injury model induction using different concentrations of APAP (0.25–4 mg/mL). As the concentration of APAP increased, the survival rate of the AML12 cells significantly decreased (Figure B). Based on the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) principle, the optimal APAP concentration for model establishment was determined to be 1 mg/mL.

The protective effect of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs against APAP-induced ALI was systematically evaluated using a C57BL-6J mouse model. The detailed experimental design for the animal study is shown in Figure A. The results showed that the liver surface of healthy mice was smooth and dark red (Figure B). In contrast, following APAP induction, the livers of mice in both the Model group and the Hydrogel group exhibited a pale red appearance and marked necrotic lesions. Notably, Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs exhibited a superior hepatoprotective effect compared with the other groups. Subsequently, histological analysis of the liver in ALI mice was performed using HE staining. As shown in Figures C and S18, the liver morphology of the control group mice was regular, with intact nuclei arranged orderly. In contrast, the livers of the model group mice exhibited prominent vacuolation, extensive necrosis, nuclear loss, and marked inflammatory cell infiltration. These pathological characteristics suggested a severe liver injury. Moreover, the liver morphology in the Hydrogel group mice was comparable to that in the model group, with no evident improvement observed. The nuclei in the Exos, SeNPs, and Exos-SeNPs groups all exhibited a moderate degree of enlargement. However, in the Hydrogel-Exos, Hydrogel-SeNPs, and Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs groups of mice, the necrotic areas of the liver were markedly decreased. Among all treatment groups, the Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs group exhibited the most pronounced protective effect on the liver.

ALT and AST are intracellular enzymes, and upon liver injury, the serum levels of these two enzymes significantly increase. The serum levels of ALT and AST were significantly elevated in the model and hydrogel groups, while the other experimental groups exhibited varying degrees of reduction (Figures D,E, and S19A and B). Notably, among the Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs groups with varying doses, the hydrogel incorporating a high concentration of Exos-SeNPs demonstrated a more pronounced effect on liver function improvement. Liver injury induces a series of pathological alterations in hepatocytes, such as hydropic degeneration, fatty degeneration, and ballooning degeneration. These alterations result in changes in the volume and mass of liver cells, consequently influencing the liver index. The liver index is closely associated with the severity of liver lesions. Typically, a higher liver index indicates more severe liver damage. Compared with the model group, the liver index in the Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs group was significantly reduced (Figures F and S19C), further demonstrating the therapeutic potential of oral Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs for ALI treatment.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD), a key antioxidant enzyme, catalyzes the conversion of toxic superoxide radicals (O2 –·) into hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and molecular oxygen (O2), thereby mitigating oxidative stress-induced damage to hepatic cells. As depicted in Figures G and S19D, in the APAP-induced ALI model (model group), the activity of SOD was markedly reduced compared with that in the control group, indicating a potential impairment of antioxidant defense mechanisms in hepatic cells. This is attributed to the fact that NAPQI, a metabolic product of APAP, induces oxidative stress, which in turn results in a significant reduction in SOD activity. However, following the intervention with Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs, the SOD activity was significantly restored, suggesting an enhancement in antioxidant capacity. Notably, the results presented in Figures H and S19E demonstrate that POD activity was significantly elevated in all groups except the Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs group, compared to the control group. This observation suggests that, in mice with APAP-induced liver injury, the level of oxidative stress is significantly elevated, resulting in increased ROS production and subsequent activation or upregulation of POD expression. , However, in the Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs group, the POD activity was markedly lower compared to that in the other groups. This indicates that Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs treatment may suppress POD activity by either inhibiting oxidative stress or enhancing antioxidant capacity. The changing trend of POD activity further suggests that Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs may effectively alleviate liver injury by modulating oxidative stress levels. Despite the use of the same drug, the administration of Exos-SeNPs at different concentrations yielded markedly distinct outcomes. These further substantiate that high-concentration Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs exhibit superior efficacy in the treatment of ALI.

The expression levels of neutrophil marker Ly6g and cell proliferation marker PCNA in the livers of mice were assessed using immunohistochemical staining. Compared with the control group, the number of Ly6g-positive cells in the liver of the model group was significantly higher. As illustrated in Figures B and S21A, normal proliferating hepatocytes can be observed in the livers of healthy mice. In contrast, a substantial number of inflammatory cells in the active proliferation phase are present in the livers of model group mice. In contrast, Exos, SeNPs, Exos-SeNPs, Hydrogel-Exos, Hydrogel-SeNPs, and Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs all effectively suppressed the excessive proliferation of inflammatory cells while promoting liver cell regeneration, thereby mitigating the progression of ALI in a timely manner (Figures F and S21B). Ki-67 is a nuclear antigen primarily expressed during the G1, S, G2, and M phases of the cell cycle. It serves as a critical marker for late-stage cell proliferation and is extensively utilized to evaluate the extent of cellular proliferation activity. As shown in Figures C,G, S22A, and B, Hydrogel-Exos, Exos-SeNPs, Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs-L, and Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs significantly enhanced the proliferative capacity of hepatocytes. Based on the PCNA and Ki-67 detection results, compared with Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs-L, Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs demonstrated a more significant ability to promote liver regeneration (Figures S21 and S22). The Underlying Mechanism of the Alleviation of ALI by Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs

Principal component analysis revealed distinct clustering among the control, model, and Exos-Se groups, indicating that ALI markedly altered global gene expression profiles, while Exos-Se treatment partially restored the transcriptomic landscape toward the control pattern (Figure A). Heatmap analysis demonstrated substantial transcriptional alterations in the ALI model group compared to the control group, characterized by downregulation of metabolic genes such as Cyp2e1, Fabp1, and Ftl1, and upregulation of inflammatory markers including Serpina1b, Orm1, and Apcs. Notably, Exos-Se treatment partially reversed these changes, as evidenced by restored expression of mitochondrial and detoxification-related genes (e.g., mt-Co3, Uox, and Adh1) and suppression of injury-associated genes such as Ahsg, Fgg, and Hrg, indicating a transcriptional shift toward a more normalized hepatic profile (Figure B and C).

Gene ontology biological process (GOBP) enrichment analysis revealed distinct functional alterations among the groups. The model group exhibited significant enrichment in immune activation and cell cycle processes, such as leukocyte migration, chromosome segregation, and positive regulation of defense response, consistent with inflammatory and proliferative responses in ALI. Notably, the Exos-Se-treated group displayed a GOBP enrichment profile highly similar to that of the control group, with prominent enrichment in fatty acid metabolic, organic acid biosynthetic, and carboxylic acid biosynthetic processes, indicating a restoration of hepatic metabolic functions. This functional convergence suggests that Exos-Se treatment effectively reversed injury-induced transcriptomic perturbations and promoted a return to physiological homeostasis (Figure D). Consistent with the GOBP findings, KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated divergent functional alterations across the groups. The model group was enriched in pathways related to DNA damage response, inflammation, and cell cycle regulation, including DNA replication, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and chemical carcinogenesis. In contrast, both the Exos-Se and control groups showed significant enrichment in metabolic and hormone-related pathways, such as biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, PPAR signaling pathway, and retinol metabolism, reflecting the restoration of hepatic biosynthetic and detoxification functions. The high concordance between the Exos-Se and control groups at both GO and KEGG levels strongly supports the therapeutic role of Exos-Se in reversing liver-injury-associated transcriptional reprogramming (Figure E). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted to further explore the functional impacts of Exos-Se treatment on ALI. The analysis revealed that two critical metabolic pathways, namely, oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid response, were significantly enriched in the Exos-Se group relative to the model group, indicative of enhanced mitochondrial and lipid metabolic activities (Figure F). Conversely, pathways associated with injury, such as the inflammatory response and acute phase response, exhibited negative enrichment, suggesting effective suppression of inflammatory signaling. Collectively, these results evidenced the Exos-Se-induced recovery of hepatic metabolic competence. Significantly, this intervention additionally suppressed proinflammatory transcriptomic networks.

Discussion

ALI is a prevalent clinical condition marked by oxidative stress imbalance, exacerbated inflammation, and massive hepatocyte death. In response to these factors, the hydrogel-based delivery of Exos-SeNPs effectively alleviated ALI. This administration system slowed the rapid systemic clearance of Exos-SeNPs, prolonging their in vivo retention time and maintaining a sustained biological activity.

Oral administration is considered the preferred route for drug delivery due to its convenience, noninvasiveness, and high patient compliance. However, the harsh gastrointestinal environment poses significant challenges, particularly for macromolecules or biologics. Despite these challenges, oral administration offers significant advantages over intravenous and intraperitoneal injections. The Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs intervention significantly diminishes systemic drug clearance and suppresses immune rejection responses. Furthermore, its noninvasive nature enhances treatment tolerability and patient adherence. Researchers have focused on developing novel oral drug delivery strategies to minimize side effects, enhance bioavailability, and improve drug accessibility. Hydrogels possess a porous structure analogous to that of natural tissues, thereby facilitating cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation. The development of the CMC/ODex hydrogel has opened new possibilities for oral drug delivery in liver disease treatment. Hydrogels possess the ability to form stable protective layers that effectively shield drugs from degradation by gastric acid and digestive enzymes, thereby enhancing the drug stability. More importantly, this strategy improves drug bioavailability by modulating the release rate and minimizing hepatic metabolic loss associated with the first-pass effect. This characteristic is of particular significance in the treatment of liver diseases and is especially suitable for the administration of Exos-SeNPs. It not only improves drug stability and bioavailability, ensuring effective concentrations are achieved in the liver, but also minimizes drug distribution in other tissues, thereby reducing the risk of potential side effects and providing patients with a safer and more effective treatment alternative.

During the construction of Exos-SeNPs, Figure G indicates a slight increase in the absolute value of the zeta potential of Exos-SeNPs. Based on the analysis of TEM results, we hypothesize that SeNPs may either attach in situ to the surface of Exos or embed within the Exos membrane. This interaction could increase the surface negative charge density, leading to an elevation in the absolute value of the zeta potential, which suggests an improvement in the stability of Exos-SeNPs. In the swelling experiment, it was observed that the swelling ratio of the hydrogel decreased slightly after the incorporation of the Exos-SeNPs. This may be attributed to the increased cross-linking density of the hydrogel upon the introduction of Exos-SeNPs, which in turn affects its swelling performance. The release experiments demonstrate that Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs function as an effective oral delivery system capable of protecting Exos-SeNPs against the harsh acidic conditions of the stomach and preventing their premature release or degradation. This enables targeted delivery to the intestinal site, where a controlled release is achieved, ultimately enhancing bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy. The hydrogel synthesized from CMC and ODex exhibits distinct pH-responsive properties. In the strongly acidic environment of the simulated gastric fluid (pH = 1.5), minimal drug release was observed. This can be explained by the protonation of amino groups on the CMC molecular chains, which leads to the formation of positively charged -NH3 + groups. These groups generate strong intramolecular electrostatic interactions with the carboxyl groups, resulting in the formation of additional physical cross-linking structures. Consequently, the hydrogel network becomes more compact and effectively encapsulates the drug, preventing its release. When the environmental pH increases to the weakly acidic level characteristic of simulated intestinal fluid (pH = 6.8), the carboxyl groups of CMC undergo deprotonation, forming negatively charged −COO– groups. This generates electrostatic repulsion between polymer chain segments, leading to water absorption and hydrogel expansion. Concurrently, the hydrolysis of Schiff base bonds facilitates a significant release of the encapsulated drug. Under physiological neutral conditions (pH = 7.4), the high ionic strength of the PBS buffer shields the electrostatic repulsion within the hydrogel network, thereby maintaining a low-swelling state and suppressing drug release. This characteristic ensures the stability and safety of the drug in the systemic circulation, indicating its potential as an ideal colon-targeted delivery system.

In vivo fluorescence imaging showed that hydrogel-delivered Exos-SeNPs exhibit strong liver targeting and prolonged retention at the lesion site. As shown in Figure F, hepatic fluorescence signals in all groups declined progressively over time, consistent with the expected metabolic clearance of Exos. The APAP/Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs group exhibited higher fluorescence signals at all time points compared to the Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs group, indicating that the inflammatory microenvironment induced by liver injury enhances hepatic accumulation of Exos-SeNPs. This may be attributed to the “homing” effect of the exosomes. Notably, in liver-injured mice, the signal attenuation profiles of the APAP/Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs and APAP/Hydrogel-Exos groups were comparable with no statistically significant difference, indicating that SeNPs conjugation does not impair the inherent liver-targeting capability of exosomes, thereby enabling the effective integration of targeted delivery and synergistic therapy. In vitro imaging at 24 h postadministration revealed predominant fluorescence localization in the liver, with minimal signal detected in other organs, indicating high target specificity of the system, which enables enhanced drug accumulation at the lesion site and reduced systemic toxicity. In conclusion, Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs represent an efficient and highly specific liver-targeted delivery platform under conditions of liver injury, offering pharmacokinetic evidence that supports its therapeutic efficacy.

Biological safety and toxicity are among the critical parameters for assessing Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs. Through both in vivo and in vitro experiments, we demonstrated that Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs caused no significant adverse effects on the cytotoxicity, serological parameters, or functional integrity of major organs.

This study demonstrated that Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs exhibit substantial therapeutic potential for alleviating APAP-induced ALI. This conclusion is supported by both in vivo and in vitro experimental evidence. The outstanding performance of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs is primarily attributed to their ability to scavenge free radicals and restore the balance of oxidative stress. Oxidative stress arises from the imbalance between ROS production and the antioxidant defense system, leading to intracellular oxidative damage and subsequently triggering various pathological processes. As the primary driver of oxidative stress, ROS directly induces lipid peroxidation reactions. Lipid peroxidation products, such as MDA, can diffuse into other cells or tissues, continuously triggering the release of ROS. This process establishes a vicious cycle and exacerbates the oxidative damage. Thus, maintaining the balance between ROS and lipid peroxidation plays a crucial role in both the prevention and treatment of ALI. At the cellular level, Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs demonstrated significant inhibition of ROS generation in AML12 hepatocytes, coupled with a marked attenuation of MDA production. This suggests that Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs may exert a comprehensive antioxidant effect across the three critical stages of the oxidative stress system (initiation, propagation, and termination).

In addition to balancing oxidative stress, the results demonstrate that Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs effectively modulate the expression of proliferation-associated factors (for example, PCNA and Ki-67), primarily due to the regenerative capacity of Exos. Serving as critical mediators of intercellular communication, Exos facilitate tissue repair and regeneration via diverse mechanisms. They have exhibited substantial therapeutic potential across multiple domains, such as cardiac and skeletal functional regeneration, as well as skin wound healing. − Immunohistochemistry and tissue immunofluorescence analyses revealed that Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs significantly upregulated the expression levels of PCNA and Ki-67 in hepatocytes, thereby promoting hepatocyte proliferation. In addition, transcriptome sequencing results demonstrated that Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs facilitated liver cell regeneration and restored liver function by upregulating the expression of mitochondrial and detoxification-related genes (for example, mt-Co3, Uox, and Adh1) while downregulating the expression of damage-associated genes (for example, Ahsg, Fgg, and Hrg). Critically, Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs maintain the regenerative potential of Exos while concurrently optimizing the antioxidant properties intrinsic to SeNPs. This synergistic interaction further elevates their biological performance; thus, the combination treatment is greater than the sum of its parts.

In summary, this study successfully developed Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs and preliminarily demonstrated their potential value in treating ALI. Future studies will investigate the specific molecular regulatory mechanisms of Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs in treating ALI using techniques such as Western blotting and the real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that a novel liver-protective strategy utilizing Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs has significant and encouraging therapeutic effects in the oral treatment of ALI, specifically through mechanisms, including the amelioration of oxidative stress and the augmentation of hepatic regeneration. Collectively, Hydrogel-Exos-SeNPs represent a promising, safe, efficient, and reliable therapeutic platform for the potential clinical management of ALI, demonstrating considerable translational potential.
