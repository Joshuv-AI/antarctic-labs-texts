Efficient design and production of glycosylated PEDV spike S1 protein
Authors: Eva-Maria Uhlemann, Rinku Patel, Brittany Thivierge, Glenn Hamonic, Wolfgang Köster, Trina Racine, Qiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The spike protein is a structural protein of coronaviruses and can be divided into an amino terminal S1 domain and a carboxyl terminal S2 domain. The S1 domain binds to receptors on the cell surface, so it contains major epitopes for neutralizing antibodies and is an important immunogen for vaccine development. Vaccine research and protein structure—function studies require pure protein preparations, ideally with no tag appended. In this study, we designed constructs encoding porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) S1 protein with tissue plasminogen activator signal peptide and a Protein Select™ tag at the amino terminus. We showed the expression of PEDV S1 protein in the supernatants of mammalian cell cultures after transfection. We further showed the purification of tag-free PEDV S1 protein upon tag cleavage by the Protein Select™ resin from Cytiva. The purified PEDV S1 protein was then characterized by deglycosylation, dynamic light scattering, size-exclusion chromatography with multi-angle light scattering, one- and two-dimensional native-PAGE, and circular dichroism. The immune reactivity of the purified PEDV S1 protein to a hyper-immune serum was demonstrated in an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Taken together, we have expressed, purified, and comprehensively characterized PEDV spike S1 protein without a tag.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56192-3.

Introduction

Porcine epidemic diarrhea virus (PEDV) is the etiological agent of PED in pigs1. PED was reported in the 1970s in England and the virus was isolated a few years later2,3. Since then, PEDV has been circulating across Europe and Asia with sporadic outbreaks4. Highly pathogenic PEDV strains were identified in 2010 when severe PED outbreaks were reported in China and the virus quickly spread into other countries, resulting in significant economic losses4. Porcine epidemic diarrhea is characterized by vomiting, diarrhea, dehydration, and anorexia, causing up to 100% mortality in suckling piglets and growth rate damage in finishing pigs4,5.

PEDV is an enteric coronavirus in the genus Alphacoronavirus of the Coronaviridae family1. The PEDV genome is approximately 28 kb in length, encoding four structural proteins and numerous non-structural proteins1. The spike protein, one of the structural proteins, sits at the outer surface of the virion. Structurally, the spike protein can be divided into an amino terminal S1 domain and a carboxyl terminal S2 domain6. The S1 domain is responsible for binding receptors on the cell surface. As such, S1 contains major epitopes for neutralizing antibodies7,8. The S2 subunit is more conserved and mediates membrane fusion to enable entry of the virus9. Thus, the spike protein, especially the S1 protein, is a preferred target for subunit vaccine development1,8,10–13. In addition, PEDV S1 protein has been demonstrated to be a diagnostic antigen for detecting anti-PEDV antibodies14.

Most vaccine research and protein structure—function studies require highly purified protein preparations. Achieving sufficient purity for recombinant tag-free proteins typically involves combining several general protein fractionation methods, such as differential precipitation, ion-exchange chromatography, and size-exclusion chromatography, into a multistep procedure tailored to the physicochemical properties of the target protein.

A common strategy to simplify purification is the use of affinity tags15,16. Adding a tag enables detection with tag-specific antibodies and rapid purification using affinity chromatography. Among these, the His-tag comprising a short sequence of histidine residues is widely used due to its small size and nearly neutral charge at physiological pH, which generally results in limited impact on protein structure, function, or immunogenicity. However, several cases show that His-tags can interfere with conformation and functions of the target proteins in some cases17–20. Moreover, the His-tag may affect the immune responses to a protein antigen21. In addition, metal affinity chromatography alone often does not achieve the purity required for manufacturing, necessitating further purification steps. Protein affinity tags, such as maltose-binding protein (MBP) or glutathione-S-transferase (GST), may provide higher purity but require additional tag-removal and separation steps22. Combining a protein-based affinity tag with a self-cleaving intein allows producing highly purified target proteins in a single chromatographic step23, but the large size of the protein fusion constructs can reduce expression yields. An ideal affinity tag would be small and self-cleaving, allowing for efficient expression and purification of tag-free proteins in quantities needed for intended studies.

In this study, we report the expression and purification of a tag-free PEDV S1 protein from mammalian cells using a split self-cleaving intein based chromatography system that meets these requirements24. The purified protein was extensively characterized as a first step to using it as an antigen in vaccine development.

Materials and methods

Construct design and plasmid DNA preparation

The PEDV S1 protein expression construct was designed with a 5′ sequence encoding tissue plasminogen activator (TPA) signal peptide for secretion and a Protein Select™ tag (Cytiva) fused to the coding sequence for the S1 protein consisting of amino acids 21 to 734 of the spike protein of PEDV Colorado-2013 (GenBank Accession number KF272920, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/KF272920). We inserted a Ser-Arg-Gln tripeptide at the amino-terminus of the PEDV S1 protein during the cloning process. This sequence is optimal for Protein Select tag cleavage as per manufacturer information. To improve solubility and stabilize the protein structure, a Gly-Ser-Gly-Ser sequence was added at the C-terminus.

In a separate construct, we inserted a hexahistidine-tag between the TPA signal peptide and the Protein Select™ tag to allow assessing Protein Select tag cleavage. The gene sequence was codon optimized for mammalian expression, synthesized, and cloned into the pcDNA3.4 vector (ThermoFisher Scientific) using a proprietary protocol (GenScript Biotech). Transfection grade plasmid DNA was prepared via Endotoxin Free Maxi prep kit (QIAGEN).

Cell culture and transient transfection

Expi293 and ExpiCHO cells cultured in their respective expression media according to manufacturer’s instructions (ThermoFisher Scientific). Cells were maintained in vented, non-baffled shake flasks on a 19 mm-throw orbital shaker at 125 rpm in a humidified incubator st to 37 °C, ≥ 90% relative humidity, and 8% CO₂. Cultures grown under these conditions were used for transfection and protein expression. One day prior to transfection, ExpiCHO cells were seeded to a density of 3–4 × 106 viable cells/ mL and Expi293 cells were seeded to a density of 2.5–3 × 106 viable cells/ mL. On the day of transfection, 100 mL of ExpiCHO cell culture was diluted to 6 × 106 cells /mL and transfected with 0.8 µg of plasmid DNA using ExpifectamineCHO reagent diluted in OptiPro medium. In parallel, 100 mL of Expi293 cells were diluted to 3 × 106 cells/mL and transfected with 1 µg of plasmid DNA with Expifectamine293 reagent diluted in OptiMEM medium (ThermoFisher Scientific).

ExpiCHO cultures were maintained following the ExpiCHO™ Max Titer Expression protocol (ThermoFisher Scientific), including a temperature shift to 32 °C at 18–22 h post-transfection. Protein expression was monitored by daily harvesting of culture supernatants containing secreted proteins (days 1–6 for Expi293 cultures and days 1–7 for ExpiCHO cultures). Supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 4000× g for 30 min at 8 °C.

For protein purification, Expi293 cultures were harvested on day 3 post-transfection. ExpiCHO cultures were harvested on day 3 for non-His-tagged proteins and on day 5 for His-tagged proteins. Cell cultures were centrifuged at 4000× g for 30 min at 8 °C, and the clarified supernatants were filtered through a 0.2 µm polyethersulfone membrane. Filtered supernatants were stored at − 20 °C until further use.

Protein purification on protein select resin

The PEDV S1 protein was purified from the cell culture supernatant at room temperature (RT) on a 1 mL HiTrap Protein Select resin column according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cytiva) using an ÄKTA Pure™ 25 system (Cytiva). In brief, the column was equilibrated with 10 mL phosphate buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4 (Gibco) at a flow rate of 4 mL/min. Then, 40 mL of cell culture supernatant was diluted with 10 mL PBS and immediately applied to the column at a flow rate of 4 mL/min. The column was washed at the same flow rate with 10 mL PBS and then incubated for 22 h at RT to allow tag cleavage. The protein was eluted with 5 mL PBS, pH 7.4, at 2 mL/min and collected in 1.5 mL fractions.

SDS-PAGE, Coomassie blue staining, silver staining, and western blotting

Protein samples were separated on a 4–20% gradient Mini-PROTEAN TGX gel (BioRad) with BioRad Tris–glycine-SDS running buffer at 200 V for 50 min. Gels were stained using Coomassie Blue staining (ThermoFisher Scientific) or silver staining (Pierce) kits.

For Western blotting after SDS-PAGE, the proteins were transferred onto a 0.2 µm nitrocellulose membrane using Trans Turbo Transfer pack (BioRad) or wet tank blotting with Towbin transfer buffer. The membrane was blocked with 3% ECL Prime blocking agent (Cytiva) in PBS buffer, pH 7.4, washed twice with PBS, pH 7.4, supplemented with 0.05% Tween 20, and then twice with PBS, pH 7.4. The membrane was probed with an in-house anti-PEDV S1 pig serum at 1:5,000 dilution for 1 h at RT, washed as described above, and incubated with ReserveAP™ labeled anti-Swine IgG (H + L) antibody (SeraCare-KPL) at 1:2,500. The protein bands were visualized with the alkaline phosphatase substrate kit (BioRad) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Alternatively, the membrane was incubated with an anti-His-tag monoclonal antibody (MilliporeSigma) at 1: 5,000 dilution for 1 h at RT, washed as described above and incubated with IRDye 680CW anti-mouse antibody (Li-Cor Biosciences) at 1:10,000. The reaction was visualized with a Li-Cor NIR scanner.

One- and two-dimensional native-PAGE analyses

One-dimensional non-denaturing PAGE analysis of the purified PEDV S1 was performed using 4–12% gradient Bis–Tris NativePAGE system according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Life Technologies). For Western blotting, the native gel was incubated 2 × 15 min in a NuPAGE Transfer Buffer (Life Technologies) prior to transfer.

For 2D analysis, 5 µg of purified protein were subjected to BN-PAGE as described above. Then a single full lane was cut out of the gel and successively incubated in reducing and alkylating solutions as per manufacturer’s instructions (Life Technologies). The gel strip was then applied to an IPG well of a 4–12% Bis–Tris Zoom™ gel and electrophoresed with the MOPS/SDS running buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) and size exclusion chromatography with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS)

The purified PEDV S1 protein was analyzed on a Wyatt Technology multi-angle light scattering instrument with refractive index (RI) detector in-line with a Cytiva Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column, which was calibrated using bovine serum albumin, connected to a BioRad NGS FPLC system. Size-exclusion chromatography was performed on a sample containing 0.9 mg protein in 500 µL PBS, pH 7.4 supplemented with 300 mM NaCl at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Fractions were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and Western blotting. Acquired data was analyzed using Wyatt software package ASTRA 7.3.2. Glycoprotein analysis was carried out using the Wyatt/Waters conjugate analysis algorithm.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy and thermal stability assay

The secondary structure content of PEDV S1 protein was analyzed by circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy using Applied Photophysiscs CHIRASCAN Plus CD spectrometer. Measurements were obtained from 194 to 280 nm with a bandwidth setting of 2 nm in a 0.5 mm quartz glass cell. The CD spectrum was a mean of three measurements. The resulting CD data was analyzed using the Beta Structure Selection (BeStSel) online tool (https://bestsel.elte.hu/ssfrompdb.php).

For the thermal stability assay, CD spectra were successively recorded while the sample was gradually heated at a rate of 1 °C/min, from 5 to 95 °C, and then cooled back from 95 to 20 °C. The changes in the secondary structure were assessed at 215 nm and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10 to determine the melting temperature of the protein.

The experimentally determined secondary structure content was compared to the predicted from the high resolution PEDV S1 CryoEM structure (PDB ID: 6VV5), using PyMOL.

Protein deglycosylation

To remove the carbohydrate moiety, PEDV S1 protein was treated with PNGaseF under both non-denaturing and denaturing conditions according to manufacturer’s instructions (New England Biolabs). For non-denaturing reactions, 160 µg of purified protein were incubated with 25,000 U of PNGaseF for 21 h at 30 °C in parallel with a no-enzyme control. For denaturing reactions, the deglycosylation was performed with 2 µg of purified protein at 37 °C for 1 h. Samples were analyzed by SDS-PAGE with Coomassie staining and by Western blotting using an anti-PEDV S1 antibody as described previously.

Mass spectrometry analysis

A sample containing 5 µg of purified protein was subjected to SDS-PAGE on a 10% TGX gel (BioRad). The gel was stained with Bio-Safe Coomassie stain G-250 (BioRad). Alternatively, the protein was subjected to 2D BN-PAGE followed by silver staining. Protein bands or 2D spots were excised, and analyzed by ESI-LC–MS following in-gel digestion with trypsin at the Alberta Proteomics and Mass Spectrometry Facility, University of Alberta.

ELISA

Immulon-2 HB U Bottom Microtiter plates were coated with 0.5 µg/mL of one-step purified tag-free S1 protein in 100 µL coating buffer and incubated overnight at 4˚C. Next day, the plates were washed 5 times with ultrapure water and then blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline, pH 7.5, supplemented with Tween 20 (TBST) for an hour. After blocking the plates were washed again and incubated for 2 h at RT with hyperimmune mouse sera or saline-vaccinated control sera fourfold serially diluted, starting at 1:100 in TBST with 1% skim milk. The hyperimmune serum was generated by vaccinating mice with a subunit antigen containing PEDV S1 protein as we previously described25. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Board, University of Saskatchewan (Protocol # 2024-0009)25. All the methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. After washing with ultrapure water, 100 μL of biotinylated goat anti-mouse IgG antibody (ThermoFisher Scientific) diluted 1:5000 with TBST/1% skim milk were added and the plate was incubated for 1 h. The plate was washed 5 times with ultrapure water and 100 μL of streptavidin-AP (Jackson ImmunoResearch) were added to each well and incubated for 1 h at RT. After the incubation, the wells were washed 5 times with ultrapure water followed by incubation with p-nitrophenyl phosphate substrate (Sigma) for 15 min at RT. The reaction was stopped with 0.3 M EDTA, and absorbance was measured at 405 nm with a reference wavelength of 490 nm. The cut-off value was defined as the mean absorbance of the negative controls plus 2 standard deviations. Immunoreactivity was determined as the highest serum dilution that produced an absorbance value above the cut-off.

Results and discussion

Construct design

The aim of this study was to express a secreted tag-free PEDV S1 protein. To achieve this, we fused the coding sequences for TPA signal peptide and Protein Select™ tag (Cytiva) to the sequence encoding the N-terminus of PEDV S1 protein in the pcDNA3.4 expression vector, generating plasmid PS-PEDV S1 (Fig. 1A; GenBank accession number: PZ052629, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/PZ052629). Because an antibody against the Protein Select tag is not available, we generated a His6-PS-PEDV S1 construct with a His-tag inserted between the TPA signal peptide and the Protein Select tag (Fig. 1B; GenBank accession number: PZ052630, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore/PZ052630). Expression of PEDV S1 protein

To express PEDV S1 protein in mammalian cells, Expi293 and ExpiCHO cells were transfected with plasmids PS-PEDV S1 or His6-PS-PEDV S1. Western blotting analysis of the cell culture supernatants with an anti-PEDV S1 antibody showed an increasingly intense protein band around 130 kDa over time, reaching maximum on Day 3 for Expi293 cells for both constructs, on Day 5 for the non-his-tagged construct in ExpiCHO cells, and on Day 3 for the his-tagged construct in ExpiCHO cells after plasmid transfection (Fig. 2A). When an anti-His-tag antibody was used in Western blotting, protein bands with the same apparent molecular weight were detected after His6-PS-PEDV S1 transfection (Fig. 2B). Minor bands between 70 and 80 kDa likely represented protein degradation products. It is noteworthy that the apparent molecular weight of ~ 100–130 kDa is much higher than the theoretical value of 73.85 and 74.69 kDa without and with the His-tag, respectively. This difference is probably due to protein glycosylation as we showed subsequently. Deglycosylation of PEDV S1 protein

The purified PEDV S1 protein migrated as diffuse bands with an apparent molecular weight around 130 kDa, whereas the calculated molecular weight is only 78.3 kDa. Coronaviral spike proteins are well known to be modified by glycosylation9. To determine whether the observed difference between the calculated and apparent molecular weights could be attributed to glycosylation, we performed a protein deglycosylation assay. Indeed, following PNGaseF enzyme treatment, the PEDV S1 protein migrated as a band at ~ 84 kDa, which aligns with the theoretical molecular weight (Fig. 4). Interestingly, the deglycosylated PEDV S1 appeared to react weaker with the PEDV S1 antibody compared to the native glycosylated protein on Western blotting (Fig. 4B). Secondary structure analysis

We experimentally determined the secondary structure content and stability of PEDV S1 via CD spectroscopy. PEDV S1 secondary structure determined by CD consisted of 38.2% β-sheet, 4.7% α-helix, and various other conformations (Fig. 10A,B). This secondary structure is consistent with the high-resolution structure of PEDV spike protein (PDB ID: 6VV5)6, which shows several domains with high β-sheet content, connected by extended loop regions in the S1 domain (Fig. 10C). The CD spectrum also contained a characteristic disulfide bond peak at 225 nm, consistent with multiple disulfide bridges present in the native protein, indicating an intact tertiary structure (Fig. 10D). We also conducted a thermal denaturation experiment to assess conformational stability of the PEDV S1 protein. The PEDV S1 melting point determined from the CD at 215 nm as a function of temperature by non-linear regression was found to be 58.4 °C with 95% confidence interval of 57–60.1 °C (Fig. 10E). The protein denatured at 95 °C showed partial refolding upon cooling the sample to 20 °C with some irreversible denaturation and aggregation (Fig. 10D). Immunoreactivity of recombinant PEDV S1 protein

Using mouse serum samples obtained after immunization with a different subunit vaccine containing PEDV S1 protein or saline performed in our laboratory25, we determined the immune reactivity of the tag-free PEDV S1 in ELISA assay. The purified tag-free PEDV S1 protein reacted with the hyperserum down to 105 dilution, while no reactivity was detected with control serum after saline injections (Fig. 11). In summary, we demonstrated that mammalian cells transfected with the engineered PEDV-S1 construct efficiently expressed and secreted the highly glycosylated protein of interest with a cleavable affinity tag, producing robust yields for downstream purification.

Using the Protein Select tag technology, we achieved a fast and successful one-step purification of the PEDV S1 protein, yielding a 110 KDa glycosylated product and producing a tag-less final protein of approximately 98–99% purity as shown by SDS-PAGE analysis. We further performed comprehensive biophysical characterization of the purified S1 protein, demonstrating its structural integrity and suitability for downstream applications.

The PEDV S1 protein produced in this study provides a valuable reagent for future functional analyses and vaccine development, including applications in vaccine manufacturing. The integrated workflow from rapid expression in mammalian cells to the generation of a highly pure, tag-less protein through a simple and cost-effective one-step purification offers a practical and efficient approach for antigen production. This streamlined process is well suited for vaccine manufacturing pipelines that require speed, scalability, and reproducibility. Moreover, the purification and characterization strategy described here should be readily adaptable for producing spike protein antigens of other coronaviruses.
