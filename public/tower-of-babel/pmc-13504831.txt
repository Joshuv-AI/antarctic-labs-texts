THC-net: an attention-based deep learning model for chromatin compartment prediction from histone modifications
Authors: Junfeng Wang, Xiangchao Meng, Jiquan Shen, Junwei Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe three-dimensional architecture of the genome plays a central role in fundamental biological processes. Chromatin compartmentalization into A compartments (active transcription domains) and B compartments (repressive chromatin domains) not only visually represents genomic functionality but also provides a molecular anatomical perspective for deciphering cell-type-specific epigenetic regulatory networks. However, the inherent high cost of Hi-C technology–including experimental complexity, sequencing depth requirements, and data analysis barriers–has become a significant challenge in resolving cross-cell-type dynamics of compartmentalization.ResultsTo address this, we propose THC-Net, a chromatin compartment prediction method based on a multimodal deep learning architecture. This model integrates the self-attention mechanism of Transformers, the long-sequence modeling capability of the Hyena operator, and the local feature extraction advantages of convolutional neural networks to predict genomic A/B compartments. Across six cell lines (IMR90, HMEC, K562, GM12878, HUVEC, NHEK), THC-Net achieved an average AUROC of 93.1% in cross-cell-type validation. We also performed feature sufficiency and redundancy analysis, which demonstrates that the six histone modification input features exhibit a high degree of statistical redundancy, and the model primarily relies on the strong signals generated by active enhancers or promoters to define A compartments.ConclusionsExperimental results show that THC-net achieved higher average AUROC compared to other methods in predicting chromatin compartment classification. The model exhibits robust performance and versatility across cell lines including GM12878, K562, and IMR90, providing a novel tool for precise chromatin compartment prediction.

Background

Compartmentalization of the three-dimensional genome architecture (A/B compartments) is a key feature in cell fate determination and epigenetic regulation [1]. Compartment A is enriched with actively transcribed genes and open chromatin, while Compartment B is characterized by repressive chromatin [2]. The dynamic equilibrium between these compartments directly underpins biological processes such as cell differentiation and disease onset [3]. Traditional Hi-C techniques for resolving compartmentalization have been limited in large-scale, cross-cell-type studies due to high experimental costs, complex operations, and significant data analysis barriers [4–6].

Previous studies have primarily leveraged epigenomic signals [7] to predict distinct organizational units within the 3D genome. For instance, Fortin et al. [8] derived eigenvectors from the correlation matrix of DNA methylation data and found values ranging from 0.56 to 0.71 to be significantly correlated with A/B compartments; Moore et al. [9] constructed a random forest model (3DGenome) integrating 22 transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) and 10 histone modification colocalization patterns to estimate A/B compartment values; Raineri et al. [10] established a linear regression model based on GC content and DNA methylation data to predict chromatin compartments, achieving an average absolute error of 0.9; Jenkinson's team [11] computed chromatin entropy blocks from DNA methylation information, finding high consistency with TAD boundaries identified by Hi-C technology, with correlations reaching 0.80 across three cell lines. Al Bkhetan et al. [12] integrated ChIA-PET data, transcription factor binding information, and histone modification markers to predict chromatin loop structures using a random forest model (3DEpiLoop), achieving an accuracy of 0.87. HiC-Reg [13] predicts short-range chromatin interactions using a random forest algorithm based on fourteen epigenetic markers across five cell lines. In contrast, Akita [14] and Orca [15] employ convolutional neural networks to directly predict chromatin contact maps from DNA sequences, thereby inferring compartmental structures. Epiphany [16] generates high-resolution short-range chromatin contact maps by integrating multiple epigenetic signals.

However, most of these prediction methods require large amounts or multiple types of data, which are often difficult to obtain for specific cell types. Given that chromatin states are directly correlated with functional activities such as gene expression and transcription factor binding, among existing deep learning approaches, the CoRNN model proposed by Zheng et al. [17] captures the sequence features of histone modifications using GRUs. This model utilizes six histone signals as inputs and achieves an average AUROC of 90.9%. However, this model exhibits limitations in modeling long-range dependencies and extracting local features. Furthermore, traditional machine learning approaches like random forests rely on manual feature engineering, making it challenging to capture the dynamic patterns of histone modifications [18].

According to the difficulties, we propose the THC-Net model. This model also utilizes six histone modification signals as input, integrating the global attention mechanism of Transformers, the long-sequence modeling capability of Hyena, and the local feature extraction ability of convolutional networks. This enables high-precision prediction of A/B compartments from histone modification signals. This model not only provides a low-cost compartment analysis solution for cell types lacking Hi-C data but also reveals potential association patterns between histone modifications and three-dimensional genomic structures through its multimodal feature fusion mechanism.

Methods

THC-net utilizes ChIP-seq signals from six core histone modifications (H3K27ac, H3K4me3, H3K4me1, H3K36me3, H3K27me3, H3K9me3) as input for chromatin compartment classification. Data preprocessing

Data input

This study utilized six types of histone data, achieving alignment between histone modification data and A/B compartment scales by inputting 100 contiguous 1 kb regions as a single sample. The final input formed a 6 × 100 matrix.For each 100 kb genomic region.

ChIP-seq data processing

The input data consists of six histone modification tracks (H3K27ac, H3K4me1, H3K4me3, H3K9me3, H3K27me3, and H3K36me3). To account for sequencing depth and background noise, we utilized the signal values representing the fold-change over input control. To further facilitate model training and reduce the impact of outliers, we applied an inverse hyperbolic sine (arcsinh) transformation to all signal values prior to feeding them into the network.

Hi-C data processing

The ground-truth A/B compartment labels were derived from Hi-C data using a standardized pipeline. The step-by-step procedure is as follows:

Genome Alignment and Resolution: All Hi-C data were mapped to the human reference genome (hg19) and processed at a 100 kb resolution.

Matrix Normalization: We generated the contact matrices using Juicer tools and applied Knight-Ruiz (KR) matrix balancing to normalize the raw contact counts.

Eigenvector Computation: We computed the Observed/Expected (O/E) matrix from the KR-normalized counts. Subsequently, the Pearson correlation matrix was calculated from the O/E matrix, and the first eigenvector (PC1) was extracted to represent the chromatin compartment structure.

Sign Alignment (Reference Correction): Since the sign of the eigenvector assigned by PCA is arbitrary, we aligned the orientation across all chromosomes and cell types. For each chromosome, we calculated the Pearson correlation coefficient between the extracted PC1 values and gene density. If the correlation was negative (r < 0), the signs of the PC1 values were flipped (PC1corrected = -PC1raw) to ensure that positive values consistently correspond to gene-rich, active euchromatin (A compartments).

Label Binarization: Finally, the continuous PC1 values were discretized into binary labels. Genomic bins with positive PC1 values (> 0) were defined as A compartments (label 1), and bins with negative PC1 values (< 0) were defined as B compartments (label 0). Bins with zero values or missing Hi-C data were excluded from training and evaluation.

Missing value imputation

For regions with missing compartment values in the data, this study adopts the cross-cell-type average interpolation method proposed by Zheng et al. in the CoRNN model to fill the gaps. The missing value at each genomic bin is estimated by averaging the ChIP-seq signals of the corresponding bin across other available cell lines, thereby preserving the local genomic signal distribution patterns. For instance, in the NHEK cell line where experimental data for H3K27me3 and H3K36me3 are missing, the missing histone modification values are estimated using the average ChIP-seq signals from other cell lines.

Hyena branch (long-range continuity modeling)

The core design of the Hyena branch is an efficient multi-scale sequence modeling module that extracts features through parallel local and global pathways. Specifically, this branch first captures short-range local patterns in the input sequence using a depthwise separable convolution, while employing frequency-domain filtering based on the fast Fourier transform to model long-range global dependencies of the sequence. Next, it fuses the local convolution output with the restored result from the frequency-domain filtering and introduces a gated linear unit to adaptively select features from the fusion. Finally, residual connections and layer normalization stabilize the training process, and global average pooling along the sequence dimension produces a fixed-dimensional feature vector, thereby achieving multi-level integration of local-to-global information in the genomic sequence.

The branch consists of four steps: residual connection initialization, short convolution, long-frequency filtering, and gating mechanism.

Transformer branch (global dependency modeling)

The core of the Transformer branch is a global context modeler based on self-attention. It allows each position in the input sequence to directly interact with all other positions through a multi-head attention mechanism, thereby capturing long-range dependencies within the entire 100 kb region. After further enhancing features through a feedforward network, the model directly extracts the output vector at the last position of the sequence as the global feature representation of the region. This process requires neither recursion nor convolution and can efficiently construct a global functional association map of the genome sequence.

Architecture hyperparameters

THC-Net uses the following fixed-depth design:

Transformer branch: n_layers stacked TransformerEncoderLayer blocks (default 1, configurable). Each block uses n_heads parallel attention heads and a feed-forward dimension of 4 × hidden_dim.

Hyena branch: a single HyenaOperator block with depthwise convolution (kernel = 3) and learnable frequency-domain filtering.

CNN branch: two depthwise separable convolutional layers (kernels 3 and 5) with GELU activation and LayerNorm.

The Transformer branch contains the majority of trainable parameters and is responsible for modeling global chromatin interactions. The Hyena and CNN branches are lightweight complements that capture long-range continuity and local nucleosome-scale patterns, respectively. All dimensions and kernel sizes are kept constant across experiments unless stated otherwise.

Results

To validate the effectiveness of the THC-net method, we first evaluated its performance using a confusion matrix. Subsequently, THC-net was compared with other chromatin compartment classification methods–including CoRNN and Mean Baseline, Random Forest, and Logistic Regression–on chromosomal samples. The Mean Compartment Value Baseline (Mean Baseline) serves as a proxy for prediction by leveraging the conservation of chromatin structures across cell types. Following the methodology of Zheng et al. we calculated it using a two-step process:(1)Binarization: The ground-truth compartment labels for all cell lines in the training set were binarized (A = 1, B = 0).(2)Averaging: For each genomic bin, we computed the average of these binary labels across the available training cell lines.The resulting score (ranging from 0 to 1) represents the empirical probability of a region being in Compartment A based solely on cross-cell-type conservation. For example, a score of 1.0 indicates that the region is consistently in Compartment A across all training cell lines (conserved), while a score of 0.5 indicates high variability (dynamic region).Since most compartments exhibit cross-cell line conservation, surpassing this baseline is challenging. Furthermore, we conducted ablation experiments to verify the redundancy existing among the six histone modification input features.

Data availability and model training

Build training, validation, and test sets

This study selected ChIP-seq signals for six core histone modifications (H3K27ac, H3K4me3, H3K4me1, H3K36me3, H3K27me3, H3K9me3) across six human cell lines (GM12878, HMEC, HUVEC, IMR90, K562, NHEK), sourced from the ENCODE datahttps://www.encodeproject.org/). After quality control of the raw signals, the genome was partitioned into 1 kb intervals, and the average signal intensity was calculated for each interval.For feature tag classification, the first principal component was determined based on Hi-C data from the GEO database (GSE63525). Positive values of the feature vector correspond to Compartment A (active chromatin), while negative values correspond to Compartment B. Since Hi-C feature vector values remain consistent within 100 kb, labels were extracted from the feature vector values of the first 1 kb subinterval within each 100 kb sample.The model training method employs fivefold cross-validation.Using the target cell line GM12878 as the test subject, the remaining five cell lines (HMEC, HUVEC, IMR90, K562, NHEK) serve as the cross-validation training dataset.All samples from the five non-target cell lines are randomly shuffled and evenly divided into five mutually exclusive subsets (Fold 1 to Fold 5). In each validation round, four subsets served as the training set (approximately 80%), with the remaining subset serving as the validation set (approximately 20%). This process was repeated for 5 validation rounds, ensuring each subset participated as the validation set exactly once. The optimal training model was ultimately derived from these 5 validation rounds. All samples from the target cell line GM12878 were reserved as an independent test set, excluded from both training and validation, to evaluate the model's generalization capability on “unseen” cell lines. This approach ensures both the diversity of training data (covering 5 cell lines) and rigorously validates the model's cross-cell-line predictive capability through an independent test set.

Loss function

For the binary classification task of chromatin compartments, the model employs the standard cross-entropy loss function as its optimization objective. In this binary classification task, the model outputs probability distributions for compartments A and B (normalized via Softmax). Sequence level: Captures patterns of epigenetic signals along the genomic sequence, associated with promoters and enhancers, using a Transformer-like architecture; Epigenetic level: Integrates "chromatin state" information from modifications like H3K27ac and H3K4me3 using convolutional and attention mechanisms; Three-dimensional level: Simulates the propensity for chromatin compartmentalization based on integrated 1D signals, indirectly reflecting higher-order structures.

This “multi-scale integration” enables outstanding performance in scenarios with strong cell-type specificity and complex epigenetic regulatory networks (e.g., endothelial cell characteristics in HMEC, angiogenesis-related regulation in HUVEC), providing a more reliable computational tool for deciphering “genome structure–function relationships.”

Across the six cell lines, THC-Net consistently achieved high AUROC scores and maintained robust performance, indicating its potential for cross-cell-type generalization. Its core value lies not only in algorithmic “multimodal integration” but also in biologically accurate simulation of “genome-wide multiscale regulation.” Compared to traditional methods and unimodal deep learning models, THC-Net provides stronger technical support for tasks such as epigenetic prediction and cell type annotation, while also laying an algorithmic foundation for subsequent mechanistic studies of “genomic structure–function associations.”

Discussion

Although THC-Net exhibits favorable overall performance in cross-cell line validation, we observed pronounced performance heterogeneity across cell lines: it achieves excellent predictive performance in cell lines such as GM12878 and IMR90, yet shows relatively weaker performance in K562 and NHEK. This heterogeneity is likely attributable to the combined effects of multiple factors. First, data quality plays a critical role. For instance, the NHEK cell line lacks experimental data for the H3K27me3 and H3K36me3 histone modifications, and we relied on cross-cell line average imputation to fill these missing tracks. This undoubtedly introduces noise and may obscure its cell-type-specific epigenetic features. Second, the intrinsic biological characteristics of different cell types directly influence the difficulty of compartment prediction. The precise partitioning of A/B compartments is cell-type-specific; as a leukemia cell line, K562 has aberrantly active and complex chromatin states, which may render the inference of compartment boundaries based on canonical histone marks more challenging. Finally, our feature redundancy analysis (Table 4) demonstrates that the model is highly dependent on a small set of core histone modification signals. In cell lines with suboptimal performance, the quality, intensity, or correlation of these key signals with compartment states may be altered. Future work can further enhance the robustness and accuracy of the model in diverse cellular contexts by developing more precise imputation methods for missing data, explicitly encoding cell-type contextual information into the model architecture, and conducting cell-type-specific feature importance analyses.

Conclusion

To address the high cost and data scarcity issues in chromatin A/B compartment analysis using Hi-C technology, this study proposes THC-Net–a fusion model integrating Transformers, the Hyena operator, and convolutional networks–which achieves high-precision prediction using only six core histone modification signals. Across six cell lines (GM12878, HMEC, etc.), the model achieved an average AUROC of 93.1%, which is higher than the performance of traditional machine learning and classical deep learning models in our evaluation. Feature analysis revealed H3K27ac and H3K4me3 as core predictive features (validating the crucial role of enhancer-promoter interactions), while H3K36me3 exhibited cell-specificity.
