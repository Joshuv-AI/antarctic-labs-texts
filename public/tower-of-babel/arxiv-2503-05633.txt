1.
Introduction

The Laplacian eigenmap method introduced by Belkin–Niyogi
[
BN03
]
for dimension reduction in machine learning problems may be thought of as follows: given sample points
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}
in a high dimensional space, one forms a graph Laplacian by introducing edge weights between pairs
X_{i},X_{j}
. Computing the eigenfunctions
\{\zeta_{k}\}_{k=1}^{N}
of this Laplacian that have the smallest non-zero eigenvalues provides a map
(\zeta_{1},\dotsc,\zeta_{N})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\{X_{j}\}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
called the Laplacian eigenmap to
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. It was proposed in
[
BN03
]
that this map preserves the large-scale geometry of the sample data while reducing the dimension to a prescribed value
N
because the graph Laplacians approximate the Laplace-Beltrami operator of the manifold and the latter encodes suitable geometric information.
If the data lies on a
d
-dimensional manifold then, following
[
BN08
]
, one usually has
N>d
because the number of eigenfunctions
needs to be larger than the dimension of the manifold for an accurate representation of this manifold.
For instance, one needs at least three eigenfunctions to construct a three-dimensional
representation of a two-dimensional torus, as shown in
[
AAB+24
, Figure 2]
.
Our motivation is to explore eigenmaps in more general, less smooth settings, offering a broader perspective compared to previous work.

The preceding sketch of the eigenmap method leaves several significant details open.
One is how to choose the edge weights. Belkin–Niyogi suggest several possibilities,
including connecting points
x_{i},x_{j}
with an edge of weight
e^{-|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{2}/\epsilon}
(Gaussian weights)
or choosing a threshold
\epsilon
and connecting points that satisfy
|x_{i}-x_{j}|<\epsilon
.
The highest generality would allow weights
K(x,y)
, but it is typical to only consider functions

K(x-y)
or, in order to fix a length scale
\epsilon
,
K\bigl(\frac{x-y}{\epsilon}\bigr)

for some integrable
K
.

Another natural question is in what sense and under what assumptions the graph Laplacians approximate a Laplacian operator on the manifold. One approach to this problem is to take points
\{X_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
randomly on a manifold and ask whether the graph Laplacians converge to the classical Laplace-Beltrami operator as the length scale
\epsilon\to 0
and the number of points
n\to\infty
. Significant early results of this type are due to Giné–Koltchinskii
[
GK06
]
, who write their random graph Laplacian in the form

(1.1)

D_{\epsilon,n}f(p)=\frac{1}{n\epsilon^{d+2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}K\Bigl(\frac{p-X_{j}}{\epsilon_{n}}\Bigr)(f(X_{j})-f(p)),

with
K
the Gaussian kernel and the points
X_{i}
taken to be i.i.d. with uniform distribution on a compact manifold of dimension
d
without boundary. Normalizing the measure to be unity they denote the Laplace-Beltrami operator by
\Delta
and for a function
f
that is
C^{3}
in a neighborhood of
p
they prove a law of large numbers (LLN)
[
GK06
, Proposition 4.1]
and a central limit theorem (CLT)
[
GK06
, Proposition 4.1]
as follows:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\text{\mbox{\qquad If }}\epsilon_{n}\to 0\text{ and }n\epsilon_{n}^{d+2}\to\infty\text{ then }D_{\epsilon_{n},n}f(p)-\Delta f(p)\overset{\text{prob}}{\longrightarrow}0,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\text{\mbox{\qquad If }}n\epsilon_{n}^{d+4}\to 0\text{ and }n\epsilon_{n}^{d}\to\infty\text{ then }\sqrt{n\epsilon_{n}^{d+2}}\bigl(D_{\epsilon_{n},n}f(p)-\Delta f(p)\bigr)\overset{\text{dist}}{\longrightarrow}sZ.

where
Z
is a standard normal random variable.
A formula for the variance
s^{2}
similar to (
2.3
) below is also given in
[
GK06
, Proposition 4.1]
. This convergence may be taken to be uniform over a family of functions with uniformly bounded
C^{3}
norm with the same proof. Moreover, using a more difficult analysis and with extra factors of the form
|\log\epsilon_{n}|^{-1}
in the hypotheses, the above convergences may be taken to be uniform over points
p
in the manifold and the weak law of large numbers may be upgraded to a strong law (i.e. with almost sure convergence).

A contemporaneous LLN result of Belkin–Niyogi
[
BN08
]
is formulated for
f\in C^{\infty}
and with
\epsilon_{n}=n^{-\frac{1}{d+2+\nu}}
for some
\nu>0
and may be stated in the form

(1.4)

D_{\epsilon_{n},n}f(p)-\Delta f(p)\overset{\text{prob}}{\longrightarrow}0\text{ as }n\to\infty.

It, too, may be taken to be uniform for
f
in a family of functions with uniformly bounded
C^{3}
norm.
Belkin–Niyogi in
[
BN08
, Section 5]
also consider the question of taking a non-uniform probability distribution on the
X_{i}
and describe the limit in this case.

Other significant results at around the same time as the works
[
GK06
,
BN08
,
Sin06
]
are that of Coifman–Lafon
[
CL06
]
, who point out that various kernel eigenmap techniques are equivalent to (heat) diffusion maps and study of the effect of non-uniform probability distributions for sample points in this setting, and Jones–Maggioni–Schul
[
JMS08
]
, who prove that a diffusion map yields a bi-Lipschitz parametrization of a Hölder manifold on a (quantitatively) small neighborhood of a point.

During the nearly 20 years since the preceding papers were written, the question of convergence of graph Laplacians to Laplace-Beltrami operators has been explored by several authors in a number of different contexts, particularly with respect to convergence of spectral data. We note, in particular, the works
[
BIK15
,
BIK19
,
GTGHS20
]
which study operators like (
1.1
) and (
1.5
) in non-probabilistic settings. Other applications of graph Laplacians like (
1.1
) have also been discovered, such as its use in
[
GBT21
]
for non parametric regression. The effectiveness of Laplacian eigenmaps for preserving geometry and representing data in applications, along with various explanations for this effectiveness, have been considered in
[
LS22
,
VS23
,
AV25
]
.

Our main purpose in the present work is to significantly generalize and improve upon the results of Giné–Koltchinskii
[
GK06
]
and Belkin–Niyogi
[
BN08
]
with regard to the following aspects of the theory:

•

Permitting more general kernels
K
that satisfy integral estimates, including those that depend on two parameters and discontinuous kernels that arise naturally in clustering and non-parametric methods;

•

Relaxing the smoothness requirements from
f\in C^{3}
to
f\in C^{2,\theta}
, i.e. with Hölder continuous second derivatives;

•

Allowing non-uniform distribution with density
g
for the sample points
X_{i}
and considering appropriate assumptions on the smoothness of
g
;

•

Considering non-compact manifolds;

•

Considering manifolds with boundary and analyzing the effect of the boundary on the limit of the graph Laplacians.

These extensions of the theory are highly relevant for a broad range of theoretical and applied problems because data sets often have non-uniform structures and natural boundaries, including those that would make it necessary to consider functions of lower regularity.

We follow the approach common to
[
GK06
,
BN08
]
of introducing an operator that averages the difference
f(x)-f(p)
as weighted by the kernel
K
and comparing it to both
D_{\epsilon_{n},n}f(p)
and a suitable Laplacian. Define

(1.5)

D_{\epsilon}f(p)=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\mathbb{E}K\left(\frac{p-X}{\epsilon}\right)(f(X)-f(p)).

Similarly to
[
GK06
, Section 5]
, we can identify the manifold with its image under the local coordinate chart because derivatives of functions are evaluated locally and our global estimates are expressed in integral terms. Thus, consider kernels
K(p,t)
that can depend on two variables
p,t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
although in our notation we write
K(t)
because only dependence on
t
is important for our results.

In Section
2
we consider the scaled difference
\sqrt{n\epsilon_{n}^{d+2}}\bigl(D_{\epsilon,n}f(p)-D_{\epsilon}f(p)\bigr)
between the averaging integral Laplacian (
1.5
) and the random empirical graph Laplacian (
1.1
) at
p
. Our first result, Theorem
2.1
, is a central limit theorem when
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
and
n\epsilon_{n}^{d}\to\infty
, which in particular implies that the difference
D_{\epsilon,n}f(p)-D_{\epsilon}f(p)
converges to zero in probability exactly when
n\epsilon_{n}^{d+2}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
.
An analogous result is in the proof of Proposition 4.1 of
[
GK06
]
, but under stronger smoothness assumptions on
f
and only for the case that
K
is Gaussian.

We next examine the difference
D_{\epsilon}f(p)-\Delta_{K}f(p)
, where
\Delta_{K}
is a Laplacian associated to the kernel
K
(see Definition
2.9
), showing this converges to zero if
f\in C^{2}
(in Theorem
2.10
) and does so at rate
O(\epsilon^{\theta})
if
f\in C^{2,\theta}
(in Theorem
2.11
). These results require the probability density
g
for the sample points to be
C^{1}
or
C^{1,\theta}
respectively. Combining these results gives a central limit theorem when
n\epsilon_{n}^{d+2+2\theta}\to 0
and
n\epsilon_{n}^{d}\to\infty
under the assumption that
f\in C^{2,\theta}
,
g\in C^{1,\theta}
, see Corollary
2.12
. This generalizes the results of Giné–Koltchinskii
[
GK06
]
to a lower order of smoothness and sharpens their conditions (
1.3
) on the rate at which
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
.

At the same time, the preceding result readily yields a weak law of large numbers.
This is similar to the condition for the Belkin–Niyogi law of large
numbers (
1.4
) from
[
BN08
]
.
For the CLT,
if
n\epsilon_{n}^{d+2}\to\infty
and
n\epsilon_{n}^{d+2+2\theta}\to 0
,
our Corollary
2.12
has an
advantage that the exponent
\theta
is explicitly tied to the
smoothness of
f
and
g
.

For both the CLT and the LLN our class of kernels and allowable distributions of sample points are more general than those considered in either of
[
GK06
,
BN08
]
. This does force us to consider more general Laplacians (see Definition
2.9
) and to require an extra cancellation condition in the hypotheses of Theorems
2.10
and
2.11
. However, it also means that we can see understand the role symmetries of
K
and/or the density
g
of the distribution of the sample points could play in the convergence of
D_{\epsilon_{n},n}f(p)
to
\Delta_{K}f(p)
and the structure of
\Delta_{K}
. In particular we note that in those theorems:

•

The cancellation assumption
\nabla f(p)\cdot\int K(-t)td\lambda=0
is valid for even kernels, meaning those for which
K(-t)=K(t)
.

•

If the kernel satisfies
\int K(-t)t_{i}t_{j}=0
for
i\neq j
, then the non-diagonal terms in
\Delta_{K}f(p)
vanish. This happens, in particular, if
K
has product structure
K(t)=K_{1}(t_{1})\dots K_{d}(t_{d})
or if
K
is rotationally symmetric.

•

If the non-diagonal terms of
\Delta_{K}f(p)
vanish and, in addition,
\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{j}}=0
then
\Delta_{K}f
is the classical Euclidean Laplacian. One case in which this occurs is when
g
is even.

We do not discuss the uniformity of our estimates with respect to
f
in a family of functions with bounded norm in the appropriate smoothness space, although the techniques of
[
GK06
]
are applicable in our setting with appropriate additional assumptions.

Section
3
discusses another topic that we believe is not addressed in the literature. We establish that under the conditions we use to prove a central limit theorem for

\sqrt{n\epsilon_{n}^{d+2}}(D_{\epsilon_{n},n}f(p)-\Delta_{K}f(p))

one also has that the resulting normal random variables have asymptotically vanishing correlations when they are evaluated at two distinct points
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. This quantifies the local property of the residual terms in the central limit theorem.

In Section
4
we turn to the situation where the manifold of interest has a boundary or, equivalently, our sampling is done on a subset of the space. A simple motivating example is to consider one-dimensional situation

L_{\epsilon}f(p)=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}K(\frac{p-X}{\epsilon})(f(X)-f(p))=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{B(p,\epsilon)}(f(y)-f(p))d\lambda(y)

with

B(p,\epsilon)=(p-\epsilon,p+\epsilon)\subset\mathbb{R}
. Here
we choose
K=\mathbbm{1}_{(-1,1)}
and
X
is uniformly distributed on
(-1,1)
. If
f\in C^{2}(-1,1)
it is not difficult to show that

\epsilon^{-2}L_{\epsilon}f(p)\xrightarrow[\epsilon\to 0]{}\frac{1}{6}f^{\prime\prime}(p)

at interior points and that this remains valid for
f
having vanishing first derivative at the endpoints, see
[
AAB+24
]
.

To handle the more general boundary structure seen in higher dimensions we introduce, in Definition
4.3
, a new Laplacian operator
\Delta_{K,\mathbb{A}(p)}
that explicitly “sees” the boundary
\mathbb{A}(p)
of the set at the point
p
. A cancellation condition for convergence of
D_{\epsilon_{n},n}f(p)
to
\Delta_{K,\mathbb{A}(p)}f(p)
that is valid for quite general kernels is considered in Theorem
4.5
and simplified in subsequent corollaries. For general kernels we need a very strong vanishing curvature condition on the boundary of our sampling set in order to perform this simplification, see Corollary
4.8
, but if
K
has an even symmetry around an axis that is in some sense normal to the boundary at
p
then no such condition is required and we can even permit a singularity at the point
p
, see Corollaries
4.9
and
4.10
. Finally, in Theorem
4.11
we show that for kernels that lack this symmetry it is possible that the limit of
D_{\epsilon_{n},n}
contains novel boundary terms involving the tangential component of the gradient at the boundary.

Thus,
our work introduces a new boundary-sensitive Laplacian operator and examines its implications for graph Laplacians, demonstrating how boundary effects influence convergence results.
If we define

(1.6)

\Delta_{K,S,g}(p)=\begin{cases}\Delta_{K}(p)&\text{\ if\ }p\in\overset{\circ}{S}\\
\Delta_{K,\mathbb{A}(p)}(p)+\nabla_{T}f(p)\cdot v_{T}(p)&\text{\ if\ }p\in\partial S\end{cases}

where the density
g
is supported in a closed set
S
, with the interior
\overset{\circ}{S}
,
then for any
p\in S
we have

(1.7)

D_{\epsilon}f(p)-\Delta_{K,S,g}f(p)\to 0.

as
\epsilon\to 0

under conditions given in above.
Here
\nabla_{T}f(p)
, the tangential component of
\nabla f(p)
at the boundary point
p\in\partial S
and
v_{T}(p)
is defined by the integral term, involving the curvatures of
\partial S
at
p
, in the right hand side of (
4.11
).
Moreover, (
1.7
) implies the weak LLN (
1.2
) and the CLT (
1.3
) using theorems in Section
2
.

Our results highlight the role of kernel symmetries, sample point distributions and smoothness of the boundary in determining the limiting operator.
We note that several special cases of Corollary
4.9
occur naturally in practice. One such case is when
K
is even, so that
K(-t)=K(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and is dominated by a radial function with finite second moment. A particular case of the latter is when
K
is the indicator function of a bounded even domain.
Another important case is when
K(t)=\psi(\|t\|)
is spherically symmetric around the origin and has a finite second moment.
In such a case, if the manifold has
C^{2}
smooth boundary, our results imply that the empirical Laplacian converges to the Laplacian (
1.6
) with Neumann boundary conditions (
4.7
).

2.
Approximation of the Laplacian

The operator
D_{\epsilon}
has been called the averaging kernel operator. These operators are interesting in general. Koltchinskii and Gine have studied the closely related integral operator

f(p)\mapsto\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\mathbb{E}K\left(\frac{p-X}{\epsilon}\right)f(X),

obtaining interesting results about the spectrum, under the assumption that the kernel is symmetric and square integrable over the product space and thus Hilbert-Schmidt
[
KG00
]
. In this section our main goals are Theorems
2.1
which is an analogue of the CLT for the deviations of the averaging kernel operator from the empirical graph Laplacian for certain class of functions; Theorem
2.11
which estimates the rate of convergence of a particular Laplacian operator (see Definition
2.9
); Corollary
2.12
, which combines these to show convergence of
D_{\epsilon_{n},n}
to the Laplacian.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\{X,X_{j}\}
be i.i.d random variables taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with bounded density
g
that is continuous at
p
and
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Suppose
K\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a kernel satisfying

(2.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{4}+\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert})(|K(t)|^{4}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(t)\right\rvert})dt<\infty.

If
\epsilon_{n}>0
is a sequence such that
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
and

n\epsilon_{n}^{d}\to\infty
,
then

(2.2)

\sqrt{n\epsilon_{n}^{d+2}}(D_{\epsilon_{n},n}f(p)-D_{\epsilon_{n}}f(p))\overset{\text{dist}}{\to}s\mathcal{Z},

as
n
goes to infinity, where
\mathcal{Z}
has the standard normal distribution

and

(2.3)

s^{2}=g(p)\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}f(p)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}f(p)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K^{2}(-t)t_{i}t_{j}d\lambda(t).

Our proof uses Lyapunov’s CLT, which we recall here for the convenience of the reader; a detailed treatment of this result may be found in
[
Bil17
]
.

We note that condition (

Theorem 2.2

(Lyapunov’s CLT)
.

Suppose for each n, the sequence
W_{n,1},\dots,W_{n,r_{n}}
are independent and that,

\mathbb{E}W_{n,j}=0,\qquad\sigma_{n,j}^{2}=\mathbb{E}W_{n,j}^{2},\qquad s_{n}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{r_{n}}\sigma_{n,j}^{2}.

If for some
\delta>0

\lim_{n}\frac{1}{s_{n}^{2+\delta}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{n}}\mathbb{E}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert W_{n,j}\right\rvert}^{2+\delta}=0

is satisfied, Then

\frac{1}{s_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{n}}W_{n,j}
converges in distribution to the standard normal random variable as n goes to infinity.

We also need Taylor’s theorem, for which we use the following notation.

Theorem 2.3
.

(Taylor’s Theorem in Several Variables).
[

Suppose

If

for all

Remark 2.4
.

In Theorem

with

f(a+h)=f(a)+\nabla f(a)\cdot h+\frac{1}{2}h^{T}Hh+R_{a,2}(h)

We also record the following, from which the condition (

) ensures the first through fourth moments of

are finite for

, both when the measure is

Lemma 2.5
.

If
\alpha,\beta,p,r\in[1,\infty)
,

and

\int\left(h(t)^{p}+h(t)^{r}\right)\left(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(t)\right\rvert}^{\alpha}+\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(t)\right\rvert}^{\beta}\right)\,d\lambda(t)<\infty,

K\in L^{\alpha}(|h|^{q}d\lambda)\cap L^{\beta}(|h|^{q}d\lambda)

Proof of Theorem

.

Write

(2.6)

\displaystyle Z_{n}(p)=\sqrt{n\epsilon^{d+2}}(D_{\epsilon,n}f(p)-D_{\epsilon}f(p))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\epsilon^{d+2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(K\left(\tfrac{p-X_{j}}{\epsilon}\right)(f(X_{j})-f(p))-\mathbb{E}K\left(\tfrac{p-X_{j}}{\epsilon}\right)(f(X_{j})-f(p))\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\epsilon^{d+2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(K\left(\frac{-Y_{j}}{\epsilon}\right)\nabla f(p)\cdot Y_{j}-\mathbb{E}K\left(\frac{-Y_{j}}{\epsilon}\right)\nabla f(p)\cdot Y_{j}\right)

\displaystyle Z_{n,2}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n\epsilon^{d+2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(K\left(\frac{-Y_{j}}{\epsilon}\right)R_{p,1}(Y_{j})-\mathbb{E}K\left(\frac{-Y_{j}}{\epsilon}\right)R_{p,1}(Y_{j})\right)

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
X
be an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued random variable such that its density
g
that has bounded

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(t)\right\rvert}\,d\lambda(t)<\infty\text{ and }\nabla f(p)\cdot\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(-t)t\,d\lambda(t)\Bigr)=0,

Proof.

For the first part, using the Taylor expansion equation (
2.4

f

\displaystyle D_{\epsilon}f(p)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\mathbb{E}K\left(\frac{p-X}{\epsilon}\right)\left(f(X)-f(p)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K\left(\frac{p-X}{\epsilon}\right)\nabla f(p)\cdot(y-p)g(y)\,d\lambda(y)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K\left(\frac{p-X}{\epsilon}\right)R_{p,1}(y-p)g(y)\,d\lambda(y)

Each of these terms may be simplified by a change of variables. For the first we then use Taylor expansion of

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(-t)\nabla f(p)\cdot tg(p+\epsilon t)\,d\lambda(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(-t)\nabla f(p)\cdot t\left(g(p)+\nabla g(p+c\epsilon t)\cdot\epsilon t\right)\,d\lambda(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(-t)\left(\nabla f(p)\cdot t\right)\left(\nabla g(p+c\epsilon t)\cdot t\right)\,d\lambda(t).

Note that the term involving

For the second term the change of variables and the expression (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(-t)R_{p,1}(\epsilon t)g(p+\epsilon t)\epsilon^{d}\,d\lambda(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(-t)\bigl(t^{T}Hf(p+r\epsilon t)t\bigr)g(p+\epsilon t)\,d\lambda(t)

Substituting for these two terms in the expression for

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert D_{\epsilon}f(p)-\Delta_{K}f(p)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f(p)\right\rVert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla g(p+c\epsilon t)-\nabla g(p)\right\rVert}\,d\lambda(t)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert g(p)Hf(p)-g(p+\epsilon t)Hf(p+r\epsilon t)\right\rVert}_{op}\,d\lambda(t)

from which the result follows from the integrability of

and the assumptions that

has bounded

∎

Theorem 3.1

Suppose

then

We write

Using Lemma
2.6
and Theorem

we know

\mathbb{E}Z_{n,1}^{2}(p)\to s^{2}(p)
and
\mathbb{E}Z_{n,2}^{2}(p)\to 0

It then remains to show

Recall from (

is a sum of

In the expectation of the product

all terms containing

and

with

will therefore vanish. The remaining

Y_{i}
have the distribution of

we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Z_{n,1}(p_{1})Z_{n,1}(p_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\mathbb{E}K(\frac{p_{1}-X}{\epsilon})\nabla f(p_{1})\cdot(X-p_{1})K(\frac{p_{2}-X}{\epsilon})\nabla f(p_{2})\cdot(X-p_{2})

(3.1)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\mathbb{E}K(\frac{p_{2}-X}{\epsilon})\nabla f(p_{2})\cdot(X-p_{2})\mathbb{E}K(\frac{p_{1}-X}{\epsilon})\nabla f(p_{1})\cdot(X-p_{1}).

The factors in the second term of (

) are bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl|K(\frac{p_{1}-X}{\epsilon})\Bigr|\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f(p_{1})\right\rVert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X-p_{1}\right\rVert}

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f(p_{1})\right\rVert}\int\Bigl|K(\frac{p_{1}-y}{\epsilon})\Bigr|\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y-p_{1}\right\rVert}g(y)\,d\lambda(y)

\displaystyle=\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f(p_{1})\right\rVert}\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\epsilon t\right\rVert}g(p_{1}+\epsilon t)\epsilon^{d}\,d\lambda(t)

\displaystyle\leq\|g\|_{\infty}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f(p_{1})\right\rVert}\epsilon^{d+1}\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}\,d\lambda(t).

so that the whole second term in (
3.1
) is bounded by

\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl|K(\frac{p_{2}-X}{\epsilon})\Bigr|\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f(p_{2})\right\rVert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X-p_{2}\right\rVert}\mathbb{E}\Bigl|K(\frac{p_{1}-X}{\epsilon})\Bigr|\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f(p_{1})\right\rVert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert X-p_{1}\right\rVert}\\
\leq\|g\|_{\infty}^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f(p_{2})\right\rVert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f(p_{1})\right\rVert}\epsilon^{d}\Bigl(\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}\,d\lambda(t)\Bigr)^{2}

and converges to
0
as
\epsilon\to 0
.

Now consider the first term of (
3.1
). We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\mathbb{E}K(\frac{p_{1}-X}{\epsilon})\nabla f(p_{1})\cdot(X-p_{1})K(\frac{p_{2}-X}{\epsilon})\nabla f(p_{2})\cdot(X-p_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\int K(\frac{p_{1}-y}{\epsilon})\nabla f(p_{1})\cdot(y-p_{1})K(\frac{p_{2}-y}{\epsilon})\nabla f(p_{2})\cdot(y-p_{2})g(y)\,d\lambda(y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\|g\|_{\infty}\|\nabla f(p_{1})\|\|\nabla f(p_{2})\|}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(\frac{p_{1}-y}{\epsilon})\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y-p_{1}\right\rVert}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(\frac{p_{2}-y}{\epsilon})\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert y-p_{2}\right\rVert}\,d\lambda(y)

\displaystyle=\|g\|_{\infty}\|\nabla f(p_{1})\|\|\nabla f(p_{2})\|\int|K(-t)|\|t\|\Bigl|K\bigl(\frac{p_{2}-p_{1}}{\epsilon}-t\bigr)\Bigr|\Bigl\|\frac{p_{2}-p_{1}}{\epsilon}-t\Bigr\|d\lambda(t).

This last integral is the convolution
\phi\ast\psi\bigl(\frac{p_{2}-p_{1}}{\epsilon}\bigr)
, where
\phi(t)=|K(-t)|\|t\|
and
\psi(t)=|K(t)|\|t\|
. Our assumption on the moments of
K
ensures
\phi
and
\psi
are in
L^{2}(d\lambda)
; by Parseval’s identity so are their Fourier transforms
\hat{\phi}
and
\hat{\psi}
, and by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality then
\hat{\phi}\hat{\psi}\in L^{1}(d\lambda)
. Using the inverse Fourier transform we can write
\phi\ast\psi\bigl(\frac{p_{2}-p_{1}}{\epsilon}\bigr)=(\hat{\phi}\hat{\psi})^{\vee}\bigl(\frac{p_{1}-p_{2}}{\epsilon}\bigr)
. Since
p_{1}\neq p_{2}
we have
\bigl\|\frac{p_{2}-p_{1}}{\epsilon}\bigr\|\to\infty
as
\epsilon\to 0
, so applying the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma. we find
\phi\ast\psi\bigl(\frac{p_{2}-p_{1}}{\epsilon}\bigr)\to 0
as
\epsilon\to 0
.
∎

4.
Sample points on subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

Thus far we have assumed that our points
X_{j}
are chosen according to a smooth density
g
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. However, in many problems it is more natural that the sample points lie on some proper subset
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the corresponding density
g
might then be expected to be discontinuous at points of
\partial S
. In this section we explore the limiting behavior of averaging kernel operators for points with distributions of this type and show that if
p
is an interior point of
S
the results are much like those in the previous sections but with a differential operator that depends on the asymptotic structure of
S
at
p
, while for
p\in\partial S
it is possible to make smoothness conditions on
\partial S
that permit characterizing limits of averaging kernels in a similar manner.

Definition 4.1
.

If
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p\in S
, define

(4.1)

\mathbb{A}(p)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{S-p}{\epsilon},

if the limit exists in the sense of pointwise convergence a.e. of the indicator functions.

Remark 4.2
.

It is elementary that
\mathbb{A}(p)
is a cone with vertex at the origin. The fact that it is defined only up to sets of zero
\lambda
-measure may be interpreted as saying that the set of rays in
\mathbb{A}(p)
is determined up to a set of zero
(d-1)
-dimensional measure in the unit sphere.

We then modify Definition
2.9
of
\Delta_{K}
as follows.

Definition 4.3
.

Assume
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(t)\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{2}\,d\lambda(t)<\infty
and
let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose
p\in S
is such that (
4.1
) exists.
Define the operator
\Delta_{K,\mathbb{A}(p)}
on the class of
C^{2}
functions on
S
by,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\Delta_{K,\mathbb{A}(p)}f(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}(p)\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{j}}(p)\int_{\mathbb{A}(p)}K(-t)t_{i}t_{j}d\lambda(t)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{g(p)}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(p)\int_{\mathbb{A}(p)}K(-t)t_{i}t_{j}d\lambda(t)

The following observation will be useful.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose a kernel
K
satisfies
K(-t)\|t\|^{2}\in L^{1}(d\lambda(t))
. If, for some vector
v
, the following limit exists then it is independent of
\rho\in(0,\infty]
:

(4.3)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\cap B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-t)v\cdot t\,d\lambda(t).

The same is true if we replace the integration region by
\mathbb{A}(p)\cap B\bigl(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon}\bigr)
.

Proof.

For
0<\rho_{1}<\rho_{2}
we have, as
\epsilon\to 0
,

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\cap(B(0,\frac{\rho_{2}}{\epsilon})\setminus B(0,\frac{\rho_{1}}{\epsilon}))}K(-t)v\cdot t\,d\lambda(t)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|v\|}{\rho_{1}}\int|K(-t)|\|t\|\,\frac{\rho_{1}}{\epsilon}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\setminus B(0,\frac{\rho_{1}}{\epsilon})}d\lambda(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|v\|}{\rho_{1}}\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\setminus B(0,\frac{\rho_{1}}{\epsilon})}d\lambda(t)\to 0

because the integrand is dominated by
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{2}\in L^{1}(d\lambda)
and converges pointwise to zero. The proof for integration over
\mathbb{A}(p)\cap B\bigl(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon}\bigr)
is the same.
∎

Our first result is analogous to Theorem
2.10
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p\in S
be such that (
4.1
) exists, and
X
be an
S
-valued random variable with density
g
that has bounded
C^{1}
norm.
Suppose the kernel
K
satisfies
\int|K(t)|\|t\|^{2}d\lambda(t)<\infty
and

(4.4)

\nabla f(p)\cdot\Bigl(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\cap B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-t)t\,d\lambda(t)\Bigr)\to 0

for some and hence all
\rho>0
.
If
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S\to\mathbb{R}
has bounded
C^{2}
norm then, as
\epsilon\to 0
,

D_{\epsilon}f(p)-\Delta_{K,\mathbb{A}(p)}f(p)\to 0.

Proof.

Fixing
\rho
such that
Hf,\nabla g
are continuous in
\overline{B(p,\rho)}
we compute that

\displaystyle D_{\epsilon}f(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\mathbb{E}K(\frac{p-X}{\epsilon})(f(X)-f(p))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\int_{S}K(\frac{p-y}{\epsilon})(f(y)-f(p))g(y)d\lambda(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d+2}}\int_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}}K(-t)(f(p+\epsilon t)-f(p))g(p+\epsilon t)\epsilon^{d}d\lambda(t).

However, as
\epsilon\to 0
we have

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\setminus B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-t)(f(p+\epsilon t)-f(p))g(p+\epsilon t)d\lambda(t)\right\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\rho^{2}}\|f\|_{\infty}\|g\|_{\infty}\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\frac{\rho^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\setminus B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}d\lambda(t)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\rho^{2}}\|f\|_{\infty}\|g\|_{\infty}\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\setminus B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}d\lambda(t)\to 0,

so that, as
\epsilon\to 0
,

(4.5)

D_{\epsilon}f(p)=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\int_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\cap B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-t)(f(p+\epsilon t)-f(p))g(p+\epsilon t)d\lambda(t)+o(1)

Using the second order Taylor series for
f
and the first order Taylor series for
g
we find there are
r_{t}=p+r\epsilon t
for some
r=r(p,\epsilon,t)\in(0,1)
and
s_{t}=p+s\epsilon t
for some
s=s(p,\epsilon,t)\in(0,1)
so that

\displaystyle{(f(p+\epsilon t)-f(p))g(p+\epsilon t)}=\bigl(\nabla f(p)\cdot\epsilon t+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}t^{T}\cdot Hf(r_{t})\cdot t\bigr)g(p+\epsilon t)

\displaystyle=\epsilon g(p)\nabla f(p)\cdot t+\epsilon^{2}\bigl(\nabla f(p)\cdot t\bigr)\bigl(\nabla g(s_{t})\cdot t\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{2}\bigl(t^{T}\cdot Hf(r_{t})\cdot t\bigr)g(p+\epsilon t).

We substitute these into (
4.5
) to obtain three integrals. If we then subtract
\Delta_{K,\mathbb{A}(p)}f(p)
we may write
D_{\epsilon}-\Delta_{K,\mathbb{A}(p)}=B_{1}+B_{2}+B_{3}+o(1)
, each of which will be seen to converge to zero as
\epsilon\to 0
. The first is

B_{1}=\frac{g(p)}{\epsilon}\int_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\cap B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-t)\nabla f(p)\cdot td\lambda(t),

which converges to zero by (
4.4
).
Writing
S_{p,\rho,\epsilon}=\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\cap B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})
, we have for the second term

\displaystyle|B_{2}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\biggl|\int_{S_{p,\rho,\epsilon}}K(-t)\bigl(t^{T}Hf(r_{t})t\bigr)g(p+\epsilon t)d\lambda-\int_{\mathbb{A}(p)}K(-t)\bigl(t^{T}Hf(p)t\bigr)g(p)d\lambda\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert Hf(r_{t})g(p+\epsilon t)\mathbbm{1}_{S_{p,\rho,\epsilon}}-Hf(p)g(p)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}\right\rVert}_{\text{op}}d\lambda(t),

while the third term satisfies

\displaystyle|B_{3}|

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{p,\rho,\epsilon}}K(-t)\nabla f(p)\cdot t\nabla g(s_{t})\cdot td\lambda-\int_{\mathbb{A}(p)}K(-t)\nabla f(p)\cdot t\nabla g(p)\cdot td\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\nabla f(p)\right\rVert}\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\nabla g(s_{t})\mathbbm{1}_{S_{p,\rho,\epsilon}}-\nabla g(p)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}\right\rvert}d\lambda(t).

Then the integrands for both
B_{2}
and
B_{3}
converge to zero
\lambda
-a.e. as
\epsilon\to 0
using continuity of
Hf
,
g
and
\nabla g
in
\overline{B(p,\rho)}
and the fact that
\mathbbm{1}_{S_{p,\rho,\epsilon}}\to\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}
a.e. as
\epsilon\to 0
. Moreover, the integrands are dominated by
M\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(-t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{2}\in L^{1}(d\lambda)
for some
M
because
f
has bounded
C^{2}
norm and
g
has bounded
C^{1}
norm. The conclusion of the theorem then follows by dominated convergence.
∎

Since
\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}
has limit
\mathbb{A}(p)
in the sense of Definition
4.1
it is natural to wonder whether we can replace the former with the latter in the condition (
4.4
). A simple case where we can do so is when
p
is interior to
S
so
\mathbb{A}(p)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
p
be an interior point of
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The conclusion of Theorem
4.5
remains valid if we replace the condition (
4.4
) with

\nabla f(p)\cdot\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K(t)\cdot t\,d\lambda(t)\Bigr)=0.

Proof.

Choose
\rho>0
so
B(p,\rho)\subset S
and therefore
\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}\cap B\bigl(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon}\bigr)=B\bigl(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon}\bigr)
. The hypothesis gives

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-t)\nabla f(p)\cdot td\lambda(t)=0

in the case
\rho=\infty
, so by Lemma
4.4
we have (
4.4
) and the result follows.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

Since Corollary
4.6
is the case
\mathbb{A}(p)=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\Delta_{K,\mathbb{A}(p)}f(p)=\Delta_{K}f(p)
, it extends Theorem
2.10
. This is not suprising because the result is local.

Turning to the question of whether we can replace
\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}
with
\mathbb{A}(p)
in condition (
4.4
) when
p\in\partial S
, we observe from Lemma
4.4
that this is a matter of estimating

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-t)\nabla f(p)\cdot t\bigl(\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}\bigr)d\lambda(t).

It is convenient to write this in polar coordinates
t=(r,\sigma)
with
d\sigma
the normalized measure on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}
. Since
\mathbb{A}(p)
is a cone its characteristic function can be written as a function of
\sigma
, and we also use
\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}}(r\sigma)=\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{r\epsilon}}(\sigma)
. Our expression becomes

(4.6)

\int_{0}^{\frac{\rho}{\epsilon}}r^{d}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\mathbb{S}}K(-r\sigma)v\cdot\sigma\bigl(\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{r\epsilon}}(\sigma)-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}(\sigma)\bigr)d\sigma\Bigr)dr.

Observe that
r\leq\frac{\rho}{\epsilon}
so
r\epsilon\leq\rho
and we can assume
\rho
is small. Moreover,
\frac{S-p}{r\epsilon}\to\mathbb{A}(p)
a.e. on the finite measure space
(\mathbb{S},d\sigma)
so the set where this function is non-zero has small measure when
\rho
is small. However, this cannot be used to gain a factor of
\epsilon
, so any positive result must either require greater regularity of
\partial S
at
p
or that
\partial S
is very thin at
p
, such as occurs at a cusp.

What is more, even knowing
\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{r\epsilon}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}
is only non-zero on a small set is not sufficient for a result unless we also know
K
cannot be too large on this set. In all of the following results we achieve this by, in essence, assuming
K(r\sigma)
can be replaced with
\sup_{\sigma}|K(r\sigma)|
without affecting its integrability.

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
have
C^{2}
boundary in a neighborhood of
p\in\partial S
and suppose
\partial S
has zero principal curvatures at
p
. Further assume that the kernel
K
satisfies
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(t)\right\rvert}\leq{h(\|t\|)}
for some
h(r)
with
\int r^{d+1}h(r)dr<\infty
.
Then the conclusion of Theorem
4.5
remains valid if the condition (
4.4
) is replaced with

(4.7)

\nabla f(p)\cdot\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{A}(p)}K(-t)td\lambda(t)\Bigr)=0.

Proof.

The integrability hypothesis on
h
gives
\int\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(t)\right\rvert}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert t\right\rVert}^{2}<\infty
, so we need only verify condition (
4.4
) in Theorem
4.5
to establish the result.
Using our cancellation hypothesis and Lemma
4.4
this follows if for any
\delta>0
there is
\rho>0
so

\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\biggl|\int_{B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-t)\nabla f(p)\cdot t\bigl(\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}\bigr)d\lambda(t)\biggr|<\delta

because this limit is independent of
\rho
. Rewriting as in (
4.6
) and bounding
K
by
h
we arrive at the sufficient condition

(4.8)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{0}^{\frac{\rho}{\epsilon}}r^{d}h(r)\Bigl(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\bigl|\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{r\epsilon}}(\sigma)-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}(\sigma)\bigr|d\sigma\Bigr)dr<\delta.

Now choose
\rho>0
so that
Hf
and
\nabla g
are continuous on
B(p,\rho)\cap S
. Observe that
\mathbb{A}(p)
is a half-space (up to a measure zero set) and choose coordinates
(x^{\prime},x_{d})
for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
so the origin is at
p
and
\mathbb{A}(p)=\{(x^{\prime},x_{d})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x_{d}>0\}
. Now
\partial(S-p)
is the graph
\{(x^{\prime},\gamma(x^{\prime})\}
of a
C^{2}
function
\gamma
on a neighborhood of

and

Eva22
, Appendix C]
and
[
Kob21

) that, by shrinking

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{r\epsilon}}(\sigma)-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}(\sigma)\bigr|

\displaystyle=\bigl|\mathbbm{1}_{S-p}(r\epsilon\sigma)-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}(r\epsilon\sigma)\bigr|

) by

∎

The requirement that the curvature at

is zero is very strong. It may be removed in the case that

is an even function on a neighborhood of the origin in the directions spanning

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert K(t)\right\rvert}\leq{h(\|t\|)}

Then the conclusion of Theorem
4.5
remains valid if the condition (
4.4
) is replaced with (
4.7
)

Proof.

As in the proof of Corollary
4.8

\delta>0

\rho>0

\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\biggl|\int_{B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-t)\nabla f(p)\cdot t\bigl(\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}\bigr)d\lambda(t)\biggr|<\delta.

At this point we define

(4.10)

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-(x^{\prime},x_{d}))\nabla f(p)\cdot(x^{\prime},x_{d})\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{J^{\pm}}{\epsilon}}(x^{\prime},x_{d})d\lambda(x^{\prime},x_{d})=0

\displaystyle\bigl|\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}}(x^{\prime},x_{d})-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}(x^{\prime},x_{d})+\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{J^{+}}{\epsilon}}(x^{\prime},x_{d})-\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{J^{-}}{\epsilon}}(x^{\prime},x_{d})\bigr|

\displaystyle=\bigl|-\mathbbm{1}_{0<y_{d}<\gamma(y^{\prime})}(\epsilon x^{\prime},\epsilon x_{d})+\mathbbm{1}_{\gamma(y^{\prime})<y_{d}<0}(\epsilon x^{\prime},\epsilon x_{d})+\mathbbm{1}_{J^{+}}(\epsilon x^{\prime},\epsilon x_{d})-\mathbbm{1}_{J^{-}}(\epsilon x^{\prime},\epsilon x_{d})\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbm{1}_{|y_{d}-(y^{\prime})^{T}\cdot H\gamma\cdot y^{\prime}|<\delta\|y^{\prime}\|^{2}}(\epsilon x^{\prime},\epsilon x_{d})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{|x_{d}-\epsilon(x^{\prime})^{T}\cdot H\gamma\cdot x^{\prime}|<\delta\epsilon\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}}(x^{\prime},x_{d}).

on
B(0,\rho)
because
|\gamma(x^{\prime})-(x^{\prime})^{T}\cdot H\gamma\cdot x|<\delta\|x^{\prime}\|^{2}
.
Then (
4.9
) may be seen to be bounded by
\delta\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d+1}h(r)
as in the proof of Corollary
4.8
, which completes the proof.
∎

A similar idea to that used in Corollary
4.9
may be applied to the case where
\partial S
is smooth in a punctured neighborhood of
p
but only continuous at
p
, provided that both
S
and the kernel
K
are even with respect to a single symmetry axis.

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
have boundary that is
C^{2}
in a punctured neighborhood of
p
and continuous at
p
. Assume there is
\rho>0
so that
S-p\cap B(0,\rho)
is even around an axis of symmetry
n_{p}
in the following sense: for all vectors
x^{\prime}
orthogonal to
n_{p}
we have
x^{\prime}\in(S-p)\cap B(0,\rho)
if and only if
-x^{\prime}\in(S-p)\cap B(0,\rho)
. For the kernel
K
, suppose there is
h
so
|K(t)|\leq h(\|t\|)
and
\int r^{d+1}h(r)dr<\infty
, and that for all
x^{\prime}
orthogonal to
n_{p}
we have
K(x^{\prime})=K(-x^{\prime})
. Then the conclusion of Theorem
4.5
remains valid if the condition (
4.4
) is replaced with (
4.7
). We note that in this case it is possible that
\mathbb{A}(p)
is measure zero and this latter condition is trivial; this occurs if
S
has a cusp at
p
.

Proof.

Taking coordinates
(x^{\prime},x_{d})
with origin
p
so
x_{d}
is in the direction of
n_{p}
we obtain that, on a neighborhood of
p
, we have
\partial(S-p)=\{(x^{\prime},\gamma(x^{\prime})\}
and
S-p=\{(x^{\prime},x_{d})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x_{d}>\gamma(x^{\prime})\}
where
\gamma
is
C^{2}
on each radial interval from the origin in the neighborhood. Take the one-sided Taylor expansion of
\gamma
at the origin along each ray
x^{\prime}=r\sigma^{\prime}
, where
r>0
and
\sigma^{\prime}
is a unit vector in the plane orthogonal to
n_{p}
and write it as
\gamma(r\sigma^{\prime})=D_{\sigma^{\prime}}r+H_{\sigma^{\prime}}r^{2}+o(r^{2})
, where it is possible that
D_{\sigma^{\prime}}=+\infty
, meaning that
S
degenerates in this direction and there are no
x_{d}
so
(x^{\prime},x_{d})\in S-p
for this choice of
x^{\prime}
.
The symmetry yields
D_{-\sigma^{\prime}}=D_{\sigma^{\prime}}
and
H_{-\sigma^{\prime}}=H_{\sigma^{\prime}}
and it is easily seen that we can write
\mathbb{A}(p)-p=\{(r\sigma^{\prime},x_{d})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x_{d}\geq rD_{\sigma^{\prime}}\}
for the cone.

As in Corollaries
4.8
and
4.9
, given
\delta>0
it suffices to show there is
\rho>0
so

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\biggl|\int_{B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-t)\nabla f(p)\cdot t\bigl(\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{S-p}{\epsilon}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{A}(p)}\bigr)d\lambda(t)\biggr|<\delta.

For
(x^{\prime},x_{d})\in B(0,\rho)
we write the indicator functions in the integrand as

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{x_{d}>\gamma(x^{\prime})}(\epsilon x^{\prime},\epsilon x_{d})-\mathbbm{1}_{x_{d}>D_{\sigma^{\prime}}r}(\epsilon x^{\prime},\epsilon x_{d})

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathbbm{1}_{x_{d}>\gamma(x^{\prime})}(\epsilon x^{\prime},\epsilon x_{d})-\mathbbm{1}_{x_{d}>D_{\sigma^{\prime}}r+H_{\sigma^{\prime}}r^{2}}(\epsilon x^{\prime},\epsilon x_{d})\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\bigl(\mathbbm{1}_{x_{d}>D_{\sigma^{\prime}}r+H_{\sigma^{\prime}}r^{2}}(\epsilon x^{\prime},\epsilon x_{d})-\mathbbm{1}_{x_{d}>D_{\sigma^{\prime}}r}(\epsilon x^{\prime},\epsilon x_{d})\bigr).

Substituting the first of these into the integral we obtain the desired estimate by the argument in Corollary
4.8
because the region between the indicator functions is
o(\|x^{\prime}\|^{2})
. Substituting the second of these into the integral gives zero by the symmetry argument in the proof of Corollary
4.9
. The result follows.
∎

To complete our discussion, we consider what can happen at
p\in\partial S
if we have neither the vanishing principal curvature discussed in Corollary
4.8
nor the symmetry discussed in Corollaries
4.9
and
4.10
. For this to be interesting we must suppose that we have the cancellation condition discussed in those results, as this would otherwise cause
D_{\epsilon}f(p)
to diverge as
\epsilon\to 0
.

The following theorem, which implies (
1.6
), is one of our main results.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
have
C^{2}
boundary in a neighborhood of
p\in\partial S
, so
\partial\mathbb{A}(p)
is the tangent plane at
p
. Using
x^{\prime}
as coordinate on
\partial\mathbb{A}(p)-p
let
H
denote the Hessian at
p
of any parametrization of
\partial S-p
with respect to
x^{\prime}
on a neighborhood of
p
.

Assume that the kernel
K
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and satisfies
|K(t)|\leq h(\|t\|)
for some
h(r)
with
\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d+1}h(r)dr<\infty
. If
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}S\to\mathbb{R}
has bounded
C^{2}
norm and the cancellation condition (
4.7
) holds then, as
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
,

(4.11)

D_{\epsilon}f(p)\longrightarrow\Delta_{K,\mathbb{A}(p)}f(p)-\int\limits_{\partial\mathbb{A}(p)-p}K(-x^{\prime})\bigl(\nabla f(p)\cdot x^{\prime}\bigr)\,\bigl((x^{\prime})^{T}\cdot H\cdot x^{\prime}\bigr)d\lambda_{d-1}(x^{\prime})

where
\lambda_{d-1}
is Lebesgue measure on the
d-1
dimensional subspace
\partial\mathbb{A}(p)-p
.

Proof.

We use the proof of Corollary
4.9
. All that has changed is that the term

(4.12)

\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{B(0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon})}K(-(x^{\prime},x_{d}))\nabla f(p)\cdot(x^{\prime},x_{d})\bigl(\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{J^{-}}{\epsilon}}(x^{\prime},x_{d})-\mathbbm{1}_{\frac{J^{+}}{\epsilon}}(x^{\prime},x_{d})\bigr)d\lambda(x^{\prime},x_{d})

is no longer zero by symmetry, and we have
H\gamma(0)=H
. We must show that this term converges to the right side of (
4.11
).

It is helpful to write (
4.12
) in spherical polar coordinates, using
r
for the radius and
\sigma
for a point in the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Then decompose the latter using an angular coordinate
\theta
to the
x_{d}
-axis and a coordinate
\sigma^{\prime}
on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\cap(\partial\mathbb{A}(p)-p)
, which by a slight abuse of notation we label
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
with coordinate
\sigma^{\prime}
. For convenience we arrange that
\theta\in[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]
is
\pm\frac{\pi}{2}
in the positive and negative
x_{d}
directions and zero on
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
, so
\sigma=(\sigma^{\prime},\theta)
and the Jacobian is
\cos\theta
. To shorten the notation we write
Q(\sigma^{\prime})=(\sigma^{\prime})^{T}\cdot H\cdot\sigma^{\prime}
for the quadratic form and note that

\displaystyle\frac{J^{+}}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\{(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma_{d})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}0<\sigma_{d}<r\epsilon Q(\sigma^{\prime})\}

\displaystyle\frac{J^{-}}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle=\{(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma_{d})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}r\epsilon Q(\sigma^{\prime})<\sigma_{d}<0\}.

This provides that the quantity in (
4.12
) is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{\frac{\rho}{\epsilon}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}K(-r\sigma))\nabla f(p)\cdot(r\sigma)\bigl(-\mathbbm{1}_{0<\sigma_{d}<r\epsilon Q(\sigma^{\prime})}+\mathbbm{1}_{r\epsilon Q(\sigma^{\prime})<\sigma_{d}<0}\bigr)(\sigma)d\sigma\,r^{d-1}dr

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-2}}\frac{1}{r\epsilon}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}K(-r(\sigma^{\prime},\theta))\nabla f(p)\cdot(\sigma^{\prime},\theta)\chi_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\theta)\cos\theta\,d\theta\,d\sigma^{\prime}\,\mathbbm{1}_{[0,\frac{\rho}{\epsilon}]}(r)r^{d+1}dr.

where

\chi_{\sigma^{\prime}}=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\arcsin(r\epsilon Q(\sigma^{\prime}))<\theta<0\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}0<\theta<\arcsin(r\epsilon Q(\sigma^{\prime}))\}}.

For fixed
\sigma^{\prime}
, the
\theta
integral may be viewed as integrating against
\frac{1}{r\epsilon}
times the characteristic function of the interval between
0
and
\arcsin(r\epsilon Q(\sigma^{\prime}))
with respect to
\cos\theta\,d\theta
, which converges (weakly) to a Dirac mass of weight
-Q(\sigma^{\prime})
at
\theta=0
. Since the integrand is continous we conclude that, as
\epsilon\to 0
,

\frac{1}{r\epsilon}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}K(-r(\sigma^{\prime},\theta))\nabla f(p)\cdot(\sigma^{\prime},\theta)\chi_{\sigma^{\prime}}\cos\theta\,d\theta\\
\to-Q(\sigma^{\prime})K(-r(\sigma^{\prime},0))\nabla f(p)\cdot(\sigma^{\prime},0)=-Q(\sigma^{\prime})K(-r\sigma^{\prime})\nabla f(p)\cdot\sigma^{\prime}

pointwise for each
r,\sigma^{\prime}
. Moreover, these are dominated by
h(r)\|\nabla f(p)\|
. But integrating
h(r)
in the above gives

(4.13)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{S^{d-2}}|h(r)|r^{d+1}\,dr.

As this is the integral of a radial function over a codimension-
1
subspace, it is independent of the subspace. Integrating the resulting constant with respect to the natural (finite) measure on the Grassmanian of such subspaces then gives
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(r)r^{d+1}dr
, which is finite by hypothesis, so we conclude that (
4.13
) is finite and our dominating function is integrable. Thus by the dominated convergence theorem the quantity in (
4.12
) converges to

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-2}}-Q(\sigma^{\prime})K(-r\sigma^{\prime})\nabla f(p)\cdot\sigma^{\prime}\,d\sigma^{\prime}\,r^{d+1}\,dr.

Here the region of integration is the subspace
\partial\mathbb{A}(p)-p
; writing this via
x^{\prime}=r\sigma^{\prime}
, absorbing
r^{d-2}
into the corresponding Jacobian, and inserting
Q(\sigma^{\prime})=(\sigma^{\prime})^{T}\cdot H\cdot\sigma^{\prime}=r^{-2}(x^{\prime})^{T}\cdot H\cdot x^{\prime}
gives that the limit of the term from (
4.12
) is given by the right side of (
4.11
),
which completes the proof.
∎
