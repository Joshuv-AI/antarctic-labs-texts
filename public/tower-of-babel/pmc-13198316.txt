Caffeine extends lifespan by enhancing lysosomal lipolysis in Caenorhabditis elegans
Authors: Hyemin Min, Eunseok Kang, Gee-Yoon Lee, Laura Bahr, Arjumand Ghazi, Seung-Jae V. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Caffeine is a globally consumed stimulant that has beneficial effects on biological processes, including metabolism and aging, but its causal role in physiology remains incompletely understood. By using the roundworm Caenorhabditis elegans, here we show that caffeine extends lifespan by enhancing lysosomal lipolysis. Caffeine treatment induced transcriptional responses opposite to age-associated gene expression changes. By comparing with three longevity-promoting regimens, including reduced insulin/insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) signaling, mild reductions in mitochondrial function, and dietary restriction (DR), we showed that caffeine induced a DR-like transcriptional change. Comparison with eat-2 mutants (a genetic DR model) identified lysosomal lipases lipl-1 and lipl-2 as commonly upregulated genes. Caffeine increased the expression of lipl-1 and lipl-2, which contributes to lifespan extension and reduced fat storage. Together, these findings indicate that caffeine promotes longevity in a DR-like metabolic alteration by enhancing lysosome-driven lipolysis.

Caffeine, a key component of beverages such as coffee and tea, enhances arousal, vigilance, and psychomotor performance (McLellan et al., 2016, Nehlig et al., 1992, Smith, 2002). Moderate caffeine consumption is linked to reduced risks of various chronic diseases, including non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, cardiovascular diseases, Alzheimer disease, and type 2 diabetes risk (Carlstrom and Larsson, 2018, Gill et al., 2024, Hayat et al., 2021, Poole et al., 2017). Caffeine intake is also implicated in reductions in body weight, body mass index, and waist circumference (Phung et al., 2010, Tabrizi et al., 2019). Moreover, caffeine extends lifespan in several model organisms, including the budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae and the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans (Du et al., 2018, Min et al., 2015, Sutphin et al., 2012, Wanke et al., 2008). In Drosophila melanogaster, however, higher doses generally shorten lifespan, whereas low-dose exposure can extend lifespan (Asbah et al., 2021, Nikitin et al., 2008). In C. elegans, caffeine (1-10 mM) extends lifespan and improves healthspan, with genetic data implicating the roles of stress response and insulin/insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) signaling (Du et al., 2018, Min et al., 2015, Sutphin et al., 2012). Despite these beneficial effects of caffeine on health, underlying molecular mechanisms, in particular for organismal longevity, remain poorly understood.

We sought to investigate how caffeine intake affected lifespan and healthy aging in C. elegans and first confirmed the longevity-promoting effect of caffeine (10 mM) (Fig. 1a; Du et al., 2018; Min et al., 2021; Sutphin et al., 2012). We then performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of caffeine-fed animals (Fig. S1a). We compared the transcriptomic profile with those of young (day 1 of adulthood) and old (day 9 of adulthood) wild-type animals (Lee et al., 2021), and found that transcripts upregulated and downregulated by caffeine were enriched in young and old animals, respectively (Fig. 1b, c). In addition, genes differentially expressed by caffeine displayed a modest negative correlation with those by aging (r = −0.22; Fig. S1b). By using Transcriptomic CLassification via Adaptive learning of Signature States (Lee et al., 2025c), we determined whether longevity conferred by caffeine feeding was similar to any of the three established longevity paradigms in C. elegans: dietary restriction (DR), reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling, and reduced mitochondrial function (Bong et al., 2026). We found that the transcriptomic signature of caffeine feeding was most similar to that of DR among the three analyzed longevity paradigms (Fig. 1d). Consistently, eat-2 mutations did not further increase the extended lifespan caused by caffeine feeding (Fig. 1e) without affecting feeding rates (Fig. 1f). In addition, genetic inhibition of key factors that are crucial for DR-induced longevity, including AMP-activated kinase 2 (AAK-2)/AMPK, DAF-16/forkhead box O (FOXO), helix-loop-helix protein 30 (HLH-30)/transcription factor EB (TFEB), and HSF-1/heat shock factor 1, significantly reduced extended lifespan conferred by caffeine (Fig. 1g-j). RNAi targeting pha-4/forkhead box A (FOXA) indiscriminately decreased lifespan irrespective of caffeine treatment, whereas knockdown of skn-1/nuclear factor erythroid-related factor (NRF) had little effect on caffeine-mediated lifespan extension (Fig. 1k, l). As adenosine receptor antagonism underlies some of the physiological actions of caffeine (Ribeiro and Sebastiao, 2010), we tested whether ador-1, the sole adenosine receptor ortholog in C. elegans, was required for caffeine-mediated lifespan extension. We found that 2 ador-1 loss-of-function mutations had only small effects (−8% and −3%) on longevity caused by caffeine feeding (Fig. 1m, n). These data suggest that caffeine acts through several key factors that mediate DR-induced longevity. In contrast, caffeine further increased the long lifespan of nutrient-sensing defective sensory osm-5 mutants (Fig. 1o), which display longevity (Apfeld and Kenyon, 1999). We further analyzed individual genes differentially expressed by caffeine (absolute fold change > 1.5) and adjusted P value < .05; Fig. S1c). By performing pathway enrichment analysis using WormCat (Higgins et al., 2022, Holdorf et al., 2020) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways (Kanehisa and Goto, 2000), we found that genes responsible for stress response were the most highly enriched term among upregulated genes by caffeine (Fig. 2a), concordant with previous reports (Al-Amin et al., 2016, Brunquell et al., 2018, Min et al., 2015). The second most highly enriched term among the upregulated genes was “Metabolism” (Fig. 2a). In addition, KEGG pathway and gene ontology cellular component terms related to lysosomal activity, including “lysosome” and “lytic vacuole,” were significantly enriched among caffeine-responsive genes (Fig. 2b, c). These data raise the possibility that caffeine promotes longevity by altering the expression of genes related to stress response, metabolism, and lysosome function. We then compared the transcriptomic profile of caffeine-fed animals and that of eat-2 mutants (Tabrez et al., 2017). Although genes in WormCat Category 1 for stress, metabolism, or lysosomes did not show an overall positive correlation between caffeine and eat-2 mutants (Fig. S1d-f), we examined whether the two conditions shared a subset of commonly regulated genes. Specifically, we focused our functional analysis on lipl-1 and lipl-2 (Fig. 2d; See Table S2 for the list of commonly up or downregulated genes), which encode lysosomal lipases that drive lipophagy-linked lipid breakdown (Murphy et al., 2019, O'Rourke and Ruvkun, 2013), because metabolism- and lysosome-related terms were enriched among genes upregulated by caffeine (Fig. 2b, c). We validated the upregulation of lipl-1 and lipl-2 by caffeine feeding using RT-qPCR (Fig. 2e) and transcriptional reporter strains (Fig. 2f, g). By using Oil Red O staining, we showed that caffeine feeding reduced overall lipid levels in WT, while not further decreasing it in eat-2 mutants (Fig. 2h, i). Importantly, we found that the reduced lipid levels by caffeine intake were partially but significantly suppressed by lipl-2(−) lipl-1(−) double mutations (Fig. 2j, k; see Fig. S1g, h for lipl-1(−) and lipl-2(−) single mutant data). Thus, caffeine appears to decrease fat levels by enhancing lysosomal lipolysis via upregulating lipl-1 and lipl-2. Lastly, we showed that lipl-2(−) lipl-1(−) double mutations substantially shortened the extended lifespan conferred by caffeine feeding (Fig. 2l; see Fig. S1i, j for lipl-1(−) and lipl-2(−) single mutant data). In this study, we determined a link between lifespan extension and a lipid-catabolic process induced by caffeine. We showed that caffeine increases lysosomal lipolysis through a DR-like transcriptional change and that this lipid-catabolic process contributes to the pro-longevity effects. In humans, lipase-dependent lipid metabolism, including reduced post-heparin lipoprotein lipase activity and slower triglyceride clearance, decreases with age, along with broader lipid-metabolic remodeling that impairs fat mobilization (Spitler and Davies, 2020). Lower hepatic lipase activity and disrupted endothelial and lysosomal lipases are associated with cardiometabolic disease, age-dependent cognitive decline, and progressive liver pathology (Reiner et al., 2014, Santamarina-Fojo et al., 2004, Yun et al., 2019). Thus, impairments in lipolysis and triglyceride metabolism may serve as functional indicators of biological aging.

The lipid-lowering effects of caffeine are conserved in mammals (Farias-Pereira et al., 2019, Lebeau et al., 2022), but its effects on lifespan remain unclear (Carman et al., 2014). Thus, our studies raise the possibility of caffeine serving as an effective dietary intervention in counteracting age-related metabolic decline (Kang et al., 2024, Kim and Lee, 2025, Lopez-Otin et al., 2023, Park et al., 2024, Yan et al., 2024) by enhancing lysosomal lipolysis and triglyceride turnover. Future studies should determine whether this coupling also operates in mammals by assessing changes in lysosome-dependent lipolysis and functional aging markers upon caffeine consumption. In addition, a detailed understanding of molecular mechanisms shared between caffeine treatment and DR will be critical, particularly because DR is a complex process that induces distinct transcriptional and metabolic effects (Bong et al., 2026). Moreover, because the biological effects of caffeine are dose-dependent, the current findings should be interpreted within specific dose ranges. Nevertheless, as caffeine is a safe compound that has been consumed for a long time in human history, our study suggests it as a promising intervention to promote healthy human aging.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Caenorhabditis elegans Strains

Caenorhabditis elegans strains were maintained at 20°C on standard nematode growth medium plates seeded with OP50 (Park et al., 2025), unless stated otherwise. Detailed information of the strains used in this study is described in the Supplementary Information.

RNA Sequencing Library Preparation and Analysis

WT (N2) animals were grown at 25°C and fed Escherichia coli OP50 either with or without 10 mM caffeine for 72 hours. Approximately 300 animals per biological replicate were harvested in TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, California, USA) and submitted to Macrogen Inc (Seoul, Republic of Korea) for total RNA isolation, library preparation using TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit, and sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq platform with paired-end reads. RNA sequencing data were analyzed following previous studies with minor modifications (Ham et al., 2022, Ham et al., 2024, Lee et al., 2024a, Lee et al., 2024b, Sohn et al., 2025). Detailed analysis methods are described in the Supplementary Information.

Lifespan Assays Using C. elegans

Lifespan assays were performed as previously described (Ji et al., 2024, Kwon and Lee, 2025, Lee et al., 2025a) with minor modifications. Details are described in the Supplementary Information. Survival analyses were performed using the Online Application of Survival Analysis 2 (OASIS2, http://sbi.postech.ac.kr/oasis2) and OASIS portable (OASISp) (Han et al., 2016, Han et al., 2024).

Microscopy and Image Analysis

Animals were mounted on a 2% agarose pad and anesthetized with 100 mM sodium azide (Sigma-Aldrich, 71289-5 G) prior to imaging (Jung et al., 2021, Kim et al., 2024, Min et al., 2025). Images were acquired using a Leica DFC9000 GT deep-cooled sCMOS camera and Leica LAS X imaging software (Leica Microsystems, Germany). Further details are provided in the Supplementary Information.

Measurement of Pharyngeal Pumping Rates

Pharyngeal pumping (feeding) of C. elegans was measured as previously described (Hwang et al., 2025, Lee et al., 2025b) with minor modifications. The pharyngeal pumping rate was counted for 30 seconds and subsequently converted to pumps per minute. Further details are described in the Supplementary Information.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR)

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR were performed as previously described (Bong et al., 2024, Kim et al., 2023) with minor modifications. Further details are provided in the Supplementary Information. Primer sequences used for RT-qPCR analyses in this study are listed in Table S1.

Oil Red O Staining

Oil Red O staining was performed as previously described (Lee et al., 2019) with minor modifications. See Supplementary Information for detailed methods.
