Association of the GLM7 composite metabolic index with gestational diabetes mellitus in early pregnancy: a secondary analysis of a Korean prospective pregnancy cohort
Authors: Qinfei Zhao, Weiquan Hu, Xuxiang Xi, Lingyu Wu, Suping Hu, Yi Liu, Yingjie Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurrogate indices such as the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index are widely used to estimate insulin resistance (IR), yet their ability to discriminate incident gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) in early gestation remains suboptimal. This study evaluated whether the previously developed composite metabolic index GLM7 was correlated with subsequent GDM in Korean singleton pregnant women.MethodsIn this study, we analyzed 582 singleton pregnancies from a Korean pregnancy registry. Multivariable logistic regression and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to evaluate the association between GLM7 and GDM. Discriminative performance was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, and pairwise comparisons were performed using DeLong's test. Robustness was assessed through sensitivity analyses, subgroup analyses, and E-value calculations.ResultsIn the fully adjusted model, GLM7 was independently associated with incident GDM (OR 14.27, 95% CI 5.01–40.67; p < 0.001). RCS analysis showed no evidence of departure from linearity (p for non-linearity = 0.266). The association persisted across sensitivity and subgroup analyses. The E-value of 28.03 suggested that only a very strong unmeasured confounder could nullify the observed association. ROC analysis revealed an AUC of 0.839 (95% CI 0.767–0.912) for GLM7. Pairwise DeLong comparisons showed significantly higher AUCs for GLM7 than for HOMA-IR and for first-trimester fasting plasma glucose (FPG), while differences compared with the TyG index, fatty liver index (FLI), and hepatic steatosis index (HSI) were not statistically significant. Within this cohort, the optimal GLM7 threshold was 6.932, yielding 77.8% sensitivity and 78.2% specificity.ConclusionsGLM7 was independently associated with subsequent GDM.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), defined as hyperglycaemia first recognized during pregnancy, is the most common metabolic disorder of pregnancy, affecting approximately 15% of pregnancies worldwide and imposing substantial health burdens on both mothers and their offspring (1–3). Its etiology is multifactorial and involves interactions among genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors (4). Established risk factors include maternal obesity, advanced maternal age, multiparity, and excessive gestational weight gain (5, 6). GDM substantially increases the risk of adverse perinatal outcomes, including pre-eclampsia, macrosomia, and cesarean delivery, while also increasing the long-term risk of metabolic disorders, such as obesity, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), in both mothers and their offspring (7, 8). Accumulating evidence suggests that early identification and intervention for GDM risk in early gestation can reduce adverse perinatal and postnatal outcomes and improve long-term metabolic health (9, 10). However, individual biomarkers measured during early pregnancy, such as fasting plasma glucose (FPG) or isolated lipid parameters, show limited sensitivity and specificity, restricting accurate risk stratification, and highlighting the necessity for more comprehensive and cost-effective screening methods (11).

Metabolic dysfunction during early pregnancy is consistently associated with the subsequent development of GDM (11). Women who later develop GDM frequently exhibit characteristic metabolic alterations during the first-trimester, including elevated liver enzymes [e.g., alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT)], dyslipidaemia (e.g., elevated triglycerides [TG] and free fatty acids [FFAs]), and altered adipokine profiles, particularly reduced adiponectin (ADPN) (11–14). However, the reported associations between individual liver enzymes, lipid profiles, and GDM risk have been inconsistent across different populations, likely reflecting the complex and interrelated nature of metabolic dysfunction. Notably, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), a hepatic manifestation of systemic metabolic dysfunction, is closely associated with insulin resistance (IR) and an increased risk of GDM (15, 16). However, routine imaging for NAFLD is of limited feasibility during pregnancy, and reliance on single biomarkers inadequately captures the multidimensional metabolic derangements preceding GDM. Consequently, composite metabolic indices that integrate multiple metabolic pathways may provide a more comprehensive assessment of early metabolic dysfunction and improve early GDM risk stratification.

In recent years, composite metabolic indices have gained increasing attention as practical tools for integrating multiple biomarkers into a single, clinically interpretable metric. For example, the ZJU index, a composite index originally developed to identify NAFLD, incorporates FPG, TG, the ALT/aspartate aminotransferase (AST) ratio, and body mass index (BMI) to reflect overall metabolic status (17–20). Compared with individual markers, composite metabolic indices have demonstrated improved performance in identifying metabolic dysfunction, including NAFLD and related metabolic disorders (18–23). However, their application during pregnancy remains insufficiently investigated. In particular, the ZJU index does not incorporate adipokines or FFAs, both of which are important mediators of gestational metabolic adaptation. Considering that both GDM and T2DM originate from similar underlying processes involving IR and glucose-lipid metabolism disturbances, and that lipid and adipokine imbalances often precede hyperglycemia in early pregnancy (24), further evaluation of composite metabolic indices that integrate hepatic, lipid and adipokine-related metabolic information may improve early GDM risk stratification.

Recently, Wang et al. (25) developed and validated the glycolipid metabolism 7-factor (GLM7) index, a composite metabolic index integrating age, TG, pre-pregnancy BMI, FPG, insulin, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), in 26,289 participants from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). The index was subsequently externally validated in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohort and demonstrated favorable performance for identifying cardiometabolic multimorbidity. However, its applicability during early pregnancy, a period characterized by profound metabolic adaptation, remains unknown. To our knowledge, no previous study has evaluated the performance of GLM7 in pregnant women or investigated its association with incident GDM. Therefore, this study provides the first independent pregnancy-specific evaluation of the previously published GLM7 index and extends its application to a clinically distinct outcome. Because GLM7 integrates demographic, glycemic, insulinemic, and lipid-related variables, it may capture broader metabolic disturbances preceding GDM than individual biomarkers alone. We hypothesized that higher GLM7 values at 10–14 weeks of gestation would be associated with an increased risk of subsequent GDM. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the association between GLM7 and incident GDM, characterize its dose-response relationship, and assess its discriminative performance relative to established metabolic markers and composite metabolic indices, including the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, fatty liver index (FLI), hepatic steatosis index (HSI), homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), and FPG.

Methods

Study design

This prospective cohort study analyzed data from participants enrolled in the “Fatty Liver in Pregnancy” registry (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02276144; registered 28 October 2014), conducted at Seoul National University Boramae Medical Center and Incheon Seoul Women's Hospital (26).

Data source

The data for this secondary analysis were obtained from Lee et al.'s (26) PLoS ONE article, “Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease is a risk factor for large-for-gestational-age birthweight” (https://journals.plos.org/plosone). The dataset is distributed under the Creative Commons Attribution License (CC BY), which permits unrestricted reuse, distribution and reproduction in any medium, provided that the original authors and source are appropriately credited. We thank the original investigators and participants for making these data publicly available.

Study participants

The parent study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Boramae Medical Center and the Public Institutional Review Board of the Korean Ministry of Health and Welfare. Therefore, no additional ethical approval was required for this secondary analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at enrollment, and the original study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Reporting followed the STROBE checklist (27).

Originally, between November 2014 and July 2016, 663 singleton pregnancies that met the initial eligibility criteria were screened. All individuals underwent liver ultrasound examinations at 10–14 weeks of pregnancy (26). Forty women were subsequently excluded because of loss to follow-up or delivery before 34 weeks, leaving 623 participants for initial consideration. After applying data-completeness criteria, we excluded 13 participants with missing GDM status, 23 without HSI values, 1 with missing FPG, 3 lacking FLI information, and 1 without FFA measurements. The final analytic cohort comprised 582 singleton pregnancies, of which 36 developed GDM and 546 did not. The primary analyses were performed using a complete-case dataset to ensure complete information on the exposure (GLM7), outcome (GDM), and all prespecified covariates. Multiple imputation was not performed because the proportion of missing data after cohort eligibility screening was low, and the primary outcome (GDM status) could not be imputed reliably when it was missing. Details of participant selection and exclusions are presented in Figure 1.

Flow chart of patient selection.

Data collection

Trained clinical personnel collected baseline demographic and clinical characteristics using a standardized questionnaire, including maternal age, parity, alcohol use, pre-pregnancy BMI, and history of diabetes. After an overnight-fast (>8 h), venous blood samples were collected at 10–14 weeks' gestation for biochemical assays. Laboratory measurements included ALT, AST, total cholesterol (TC), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), TG, HDL, LDL, FPG, and insulin. Hepatic steatosis (HS) was assessed by ultrasonography and graded semi-quantitatively on a scale from 0 to 3 according to established criteria (28). For the sensitivity analyses, NAFLD was defined as a binary variable, with an HS grade ≥1 indicating the presence of NAFLD and grade 0 indicating its absence. IR was estimated using HOMA-IR (29). GDM was assessed at 24–28 weeks of gestation via a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and diagnosed according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria: FPG ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-h post-load glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-h post-load glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L (10, 30). Additional methodological details and variable definitions are provided in the original publication (26).

Assessment of the GLM7 index

The GLM7 index was calculated according to the previously published formula using seven first-trimester maternal parameters: GLM7 = log10 [Age (years) × BMI (kg/m2) × FPG (mg/dL) × Insulin (pmol/L) × TG (mmol/L) × LDL (mmol/L) / HDL (mmol/L)] (25). Fasting insulin values were converted from μIU/mL to pmol/L (1 μIU/mL = 6.0 pmol/L) (31). Lipid parameters were converted from mg/dL to mmol/L using standard factors: TG (÷ 88.57) and cholesterol (÷ 38.67).

For comparison analyses, several established metabolic indices were also evaluated. The TyG index was calculated according to the original published formula: TyG = ln [TG (mg/dL) × FPG (mg/dL)/2] (32). HOMA-IR, the FLI, and the HSI were available in the original cohort dataset and had been calculated by the original investigators according to their respective published formulas (26), which are presented below for completeness. HOMA-IR = fasting insulin (μIU/mL) × FPG (mmol/L) / 22.5 (33). FLI was calculated according to Bedogni et al. (34): FLI = [exp (0.953 × ln (TG) + 0.139 × BMI + 0.718 × ln (GGT) + 0.053 × waist circumference (WC) – 15.745) / (1 + exp (0.953 × ln (TG) + 0.139 × BMI + 0.718 × ln (GGT) + 0.053 × WC – 15.745))] × 100, where TG is measured in mg/dL, BMI in kg/m2, GGT in U/L, and WC in cm. The HSI was calculated according to Lee et al. (35): HSI = 8 × [ALT (IU/L) / AST (IU/L)] + BMI (kg/m2) + 2.

All biochemical measurements were performed on venous samples obtained after an overnight-fast (> 8 h) at 10–14 weeks' gestation. Samples were centrifuged and stored at −70 °C until analysis. The outcome variable was GDM.

Covariates

Covariate selection was guided by directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) (Supplementary Figure S1) and prior literature, with the number of adjusted variables constrained by the “10 events per variable” principle (36 GDM events permitted ≤ 3–4 covariates) (26, 36–38). The final multivariable model adjusted for parity, TC, ADPN, and FFA. The assessment of multicollinearity among independent variables was conducted using the variance inflation factor (VIF) (39). Additional details regarding the original data collection procedures are described in the parent publication (26).

Statistical analysis

GDM status was used to summarize baseline characteristics. Participants were ranked by GLM7 and divided into three approximately equal-sized tertiles denoted as low (T1), intermediate (T2), and high (T3) exposure (25, 40–42). Continuous variables with an approximately normal distribution were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas skewed variables were summarized as the median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were expressed as n (%). Group comparisons for normally distributed continuous variables were conducted using one-way ANOVA, whereas non-normally distributed continuous data were analyzed with the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical outcomes were assessed using Pearson's chi-square test. For the tertile analyses, a p for trend was calculated by assigning ordinal scores (1, 2, and 3) to increasing GLM7 tertiles and entering the resulting variable as a continuous term in the multivariable logistic regression model. The statistical significance of the linear trend was assessed using the Wald test for the regression coefficient of the ordinal variable (43).

The associations of GLM7 with GDM were assessed using multivariable logistic regression and restricted cubic splines (RCS). Multivariable models were fitted sequentially as follows: crude (unadjusted); Model I (adjusted for parity); Model II (parity + FFA); Model III (parity + FFA + TC); and Model IV (parity + FFA + TC + ADPN). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Fully adjusted RCS models were used to characterize the dose-response relationship and assess potential non-linearity. Subgroup analyses were performed after stratification by age, HOMA-IR, HS, and pre-pregnancy BMI (44). Continuous stratification variables were categorized using clinically relevant cut-points, and within each stratum, models were adjusted for the same covariates except the stratification factor (6). Interaction was tested by comparing models with and without multiplicative interaction terms using likelihood-ratio tests.

We performed multiple sensitivity analyses to assess robustness. First, GLM7 was analyzed categorically (tertiles). To evaluate the robustness of the primary findings with respect to obesity and underlying HS (26, 45), sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding participants with pre-pregnancy BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 and, separately, participants with sonographic HS grade ≥ 1. We also evaluated models with NAFLD included as an additional covariate. E-values were determined to measure the least degree of association an unmeasured confounder would require with both the exposure and the outcome to nullify the observed associations (46).

Discriminative performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis by estimating the area under the curve (AUC) and identifying the optimal operating point using Youden's index for GLM7 and the comparator metabolic markers. Pairwise comparisons between GLM7 and each comparator marker were performed using DeLong's test. To explore whether GLM7 provided additional discriminatory information beyond established composite metabolic indices, covariate-adjusted ROC analyses were additionally conducted as sensitivity analyses using predicted probabilities derived from separate multivariable logistic regression models with an identical covariate structure comprising parity, TC, FFA, and ADPN. Each metabolic marker or composite index was added individually to the same base model. The sample size was determined by the available dataset; therefore, a priori power calculation was not performed.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and Free Statistics software (version 2.5; Beijing, China; available at http://www.clinicalscientists.cn/freestatistics), a graphical statistical analysis platform that provides a user-friendly interface for regression analyses, RCS models, and other commonly used epidemiological statistical procedures. All tests were two-sided and significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Relationship between GLM7 index and GDM

All variables exhibited VIFs < 1.2, indicating no multicollinearity (Supplementary Table S2). The Box-Tidwell test confirmed a linear relationship between GLM7 and the risk of GDM (p = 0.326), meeting the linearity requirement for logistic regression (Supplementary Table S3).

GLM7 was evaluated as a continuous variable and also grouped into tertiles. As shown in Table 2, higher GLM7 was strongly associated with incident GDM. As a continuous predictor, GLM7 yielded a crude OR of 24.96 (95% CI 9.66–64.45; p < 0.001) and maintained its significance even after sequential adjustments for parity, FFA, TC and ADPN (Model IV OR 14.27, 95% CI 5.01–40.67; p < 0.001). According to tertile analyses, participants in the highest GLM7 tertile (T3) had substantially greater odds of developing GDM than those in the lowest tertile (T1) (crude OR 17.56, 95% CI 4.13–74.60; p < 0.001; Model IV OR 8.14, 95% CI 1.80–36.77; p = 0.006), whereas the intermediate tertile (T2) did not show a significant elevation. The models demonstrated a clear dose-response trend (p for trend < 0.001). The corresponding E-value (28.03) indicates that an unmeasured confounder would need to be very strongly associated with both GLM7 and GDM to fully explain the observed association. RCS analysis supported a linear dose-response between GLM7 and GDM risk (p for non-linearity = 0.266; Figure 2).

Sensitivity analyses

When GLM7 was modeled as a continuous variable, its positive relationship with GDM remained consistent across all sensitivity analyses (Model I OR 7.05, 95% CI 1.72–28.90, p=0.007; Model II OR 7.31, 95% CI 1.86–28.72, p=0.004; Model III OR 10.25, 95% CI 3.39–30.94, p < 0.001) (Table 3). In the tertile analyses, participants in the highest GLM7 tertile (T3) had the greatest risk of GDM compared with those in the lowest tertile (T1), reaching statistical significance in Model III (OR 5.61, 95% CI 1.19–26.35; p = 0.029), whereas the intermediate tertile (T2) was not significantly associated with GDM. A significant dose-response relationship was observed across the sensitivity models (p for trend = 0.046, 0.029 and 0.006 for Models I–III, respectively), supporting the robustness of the observed association between GLM7 and incident GDM.

Subgroup analysis

We investigated possible effect modification by conducting stratified analyses based on age, HOMA-IR, pre-pregnancy BMI, and HS. Within each stratum, models were adjusted for the same covariates except the stratification variable. The numbers of participants and GDM events for each subgroup are presented in Figure 3 and Supplementary Table S4. Because the number of GDM events within several subgroups was limited, the corresponding effect estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, the direction and magnitude of the association were generally consistent across all predefined subgroups. No statistically significant interactions were observed for any of the stratification variables (all p for interaction > 0.05), indicating no evidence of effect modification.

Subgroup analysis of the association between GLM7 and GDM risk.

ROC analysis

The discriminative performance of GLM7 for incident GDM was assessed by ROC analysis. GLM7 achieved an AUC of 0.839 (95% CI 0.767–0.912), indicating good discrimination within this cohort (Table 4 and Figure 4). Among the evaluated metabolic markers, GLM7 showed the highest observed AUC. In the unadjusted ROC analyses, pairwise DeLong comparisons demonstrated significantly higher AUCs for GLM7 than for HOMA-IR and FPG, whereas the differences compared with the TyG index, FLI, and HSI were not statistically significant. After covariate adjustment, GLM7 retained the highest numerical AUC (Supplementary Figure S2); however, none of the pairwise AUC differences reached statistical significance (all p > 0.05; Supplementary Table S5). The optimal GLM7 threshold determined by Youden's index was 6.932, corresponding to a sensitivity of 77.80% and a specificity of 78.20%. Because this threshold was derived and evaluated within the same cohort, it should be regarded as exploratory and requires external validation before clinical application.

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, higher first-trimester GLM7 levels were independently associated with subsequent GDM, with a fully adjusted OR of 14.27 (95% CI 5.01–40.67). The association exhibited a positive linear dose-response relationship and remained generally consistent across the subgroup and sensitivity analyses. GLM7 also demonstrated favorable discriminative performance within this cohort, with an AUC of 0.839. Collectively, these findings support further evaluation of the previously developed GLM7 index as a candidate composite metabolic marker for early GDM risk stratification. However, they should be interpreted as an initial pregnancy-specific evaluation of association and discrimination rather than as evidence supporting a validated clinical prediction tool.

Previous perinatal studies have reported inconsistent associations and variable discriminative performance for individual lipid markers, hepatic indices, and IR markers in relation to GDM (47–51). In the present cohort, women who subsequently developed GDM had higher levels of FPG, fasting insulin, and HOMA-IR, supporting the relevance of early disturbances in glucose metabolism and IR (52). Nevertheless, individual biomarkers may incompletely capture the complex metabolic alterations preceding GDM, which involve IR, adiposity, dysregulated lipid metabolism, and impaired glucose homeostasis (3, 6, 53, 54). These observations provide a rationale for evaluating composite indices that integrate multiple metabolic domains (12, 55, 56). Although GLM7 achieved the highest observed AUC, DeLong's test demonstrated statistically significant differences only compared with HOMA-IR and FPG, whereas no significant differences were observed compared with the TyG index, FLI, or HSI. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating the universal superiority of GLM7 over established composite metabolic indices (57). The slightly higher Youden index observed for FLI does not contradict the higher AUC of GLM7, because the Youden index reflects diagnostic performance at a single operating threshold, whereas the AUC summarizes discriminative performance across all possible thresholds.

The observed association is biologically plausible because GLM7 integrates anthropometric, glycemic, insulinemic, and lipid-related variables that reflect multiple metabolic pathways implicated in GDM development. During pregnancy, metabolic adaptations in the liver and adipose tissue contribute to changes in insulin sensitivity, hepatic glucose production, lipid mobilization, and circulating FFA concentrations (24, 58, 59). Excess hepatic fat accumulation and impaired insulin signaling may promote lipid overflow, systemic IR, and subsequent glucose intolerance. These metabolic disturbances may be further amplified by inflammatory and adipokine-mediated pathways (10, 11, 60–65). By combining age, pre-pregnancy BMI, FPG, fasting insulin, TG, LDL, and HDL, GLM7 may capture a broader spectrum of early metabolic alterations than any individual laboratory marker (66). Composite metabolic indices have been extensively investigated in relation to NAFLD, T2DM, and cardiometabolic multimorbidity in non-pregnant populations, but their application in early pregnancy remains relatively underexplored (17–20, 25). The present study therefore provides an initial independent evaluation of the previously developed GLM7 index in a pregnancy-specific population, with subsequent GDM as the study outcome.

The analyses treating GLM7 as a continuous variable, together with the trend and RCS analyses, consistently supported a positive linear association between GLM7 and subsequent GDM. Although the fully adjusted tertile analysis did not show a strictly monotonic increase across all exposure categories, the effect estimate for the intermediate tertile was imprecise, as reflected by its wide CI. Given that only 36 GDM events occurred, categorization of GLM7 further reduced the number of events within each exposure category, thereby increasing random variability and reducing statistical power. The apparent non-monotonic pattern observed across tertiles is more likely to reflect limited statistical power and random variation than a true departure from the overall association. Similarly, the subgroup analyses produced wider CIs because of the limited number of events within individual strata. These analyses were intended primarily to assess the consistency of the association rather than to provide precise stratum-specific estimates. No statistically significant interactions were observed for age, HOMA-IR, pre-pregnancy BMI, or HS, providing no evidence of meaningful effect modification.

The ROC findings should be interpreted with caution. The primary ROC analyses assessed the discriminative performance of individual biomarkers and composite indices rather than that of fully developed multivariable prediction models. Direct comparisons of GLM7 with individual biomarkers are inherently influenced by the greater amount of metabolic information incorporated into the seven-component GLM7 formula. Comparisons with the TyG index, FLI, HSI, and HOMA-IR are likewise influenced by differences in the number and types of variables included in each index. Thus, the higher observed AUC of GLM7 may partly reflect its broader metabolic composition rather than intrinsic superiority. Covariate-adjusted ROC analyses retained a broadly similar numerical ranking of the evaluated markers; however, none of the pairwise differences remained statistically significant after adjustment. This finding further supports a cautious interpretation of the comparative discriminative performance of GLM7. Moreover, this study was not designed to determine the incremental discriminative performance or clinical utility of GLM7. Larger independent studies are needed to determine whether GLM7 provides additional discrimination beyond established maternal characteristics, first-trimester FPG, and existing composite metabolic indices, and whether any improvement is accompanied by better calibration or greater clinical utility (67, 68).

An important methodological consideration is that FPG is one of the components of GLM7 and is independently associated with subsequent GDM. This does not constitute strict outcome circularity because GLM7 was calculated using measurements obtained at 10–14 weeks of gestation, whereas GDM was diagnosed later at 24–28 weeks using a standardized 75-g OGTT according to the IADPSG criteria. GLM7 and the GDM outcome were therefore assessed at different stages of pregnancy using distinct measurement procedures. Nevertheless, biological overlap remains unavoidable, and the inclusion of first-trimester FPG likely contributed to both the observed association and the discriminative performance of GLM7. To place this contribution into context, FPG alone yielded an AUC of 0.662 in this cohort, which was substantially lower than that of the complete GLM7 index (AUC 0.839), suggesting that the non-FPG components contribute additional metabolic information.

However, the estimated association (fully adjusted OR 14.27) likely reflects, at least in part, the contribution of the FPG component, and the relatively high AUC should not be interpreted as demonstrating discrimination fully independent of FPG. A recent methodological commentary by Wang and Huang (69) also questioned the inclusion of fasting glucose within GLM7 when evaluating diabetes-related outcomes because of potential overlap between the predictor and outcome. Although the present study examined future OGTT-defined GDM rather than concurrent diabetes, this concern remains relevant. Future studies should quantify the incremental contribution of GLM7 beyond first-trimester FPG and other established metabolic indices.

This study has several strengths. The prospective cohort design established a clear temporal sequence between first-trimester GLM7 assessment and subsequent GDM diagnosis. Covariate selection was informed by DAGs and prior evidence, while the number of adjusted covariates was restricted in view of the limited number of outcome events. Multicollinearity was minimal, with all VIF values below 1.2. Multiple sensitivity analyses, prespecified subgroup analyses, RCS modeling, and E-value analyses were also used to evaluate the robustness of the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational design precludes causal inference. Second, only 36 participants developed GDM, resulting in wide CIs and limited statistical precision, particularly in the tertile and subgroup analyses. Although the number of adjusted covariates was constrained according to the events-per-variable principle, residual overfitting and model instability cannot be excluded. Third, this study evaluated associations and discriminative performance but neither developed nor externally validated a clinical prediction model. Model calibration, internal validation, clinical net benefit, incremental predictive value, and decision-analytic performance were not assessed. Moreover, the GLM7 threshold of 6.932 was both derived and evaluated within the same cohort and may therefore represent an optimistic estimate. Accordingly, it should be regarded as exploratory rather than as an established clinical decision threshold. Fourth, the complete-case analysis excluded 41 of 623 otherwise eligible participants. Although the proportion of excluded participants was relatively small, comparison of the included and excluded participants showed that most baseline characteristics were similar between the two groups; however, excluded participants had modestly higher first-trimester FPG and FFA levels. Therefore, some degree of selection bias cannot be excluded if missingness was correlated with metabolic status or GDM risk. Because missing GDM outcomes could not be reliably imputed, multiple imputation was not performed. Fifth, the cohort consisted exclusively of Korean women with singleton pregnancies, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic populations and obstetric settings. Finally, GLM7 was calculated from single measurements of FPG and fasting insulin obtained during early pregnancy. Both biological and analytical variability may influence classification around the proposed threshold, and a single assessment cannot capture longitudinal metabolic changes throughout gestation. Therefore, external validation in larger, multicentre, multi-ethnic cohorts with repeated measurements is required before GLM7 or its proposed threshold can be considered for routine screening or individualized risk assessment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides an initial pregnancy-specific evaluation of the previously developed GLM7 index. Higher first-trimester GLM7 levels were independently associated with subsequent GDM and demonstrated favorable discrimination within this Korean cohort. These findings support further evaluation of GLM7 as a candidate composite marker for early GDM risk stratification. However, because this study did not develop or validate a clinical prediction model, external validation, assessment of calibration, incremental discriminatory value, clinical utility, and confirmation of the proposed GLM7 cut-off in independent populations are required before clinical implementation.
