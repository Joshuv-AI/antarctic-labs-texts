Patient-reported and caregiver-reported outcomes in refractory ascites secondary to cirrhosis: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yazan Haddadin, James Ryley, Benjaman To, Vasso Anagnostopoulou, Stephen Bremner, Lucia Macken, Sumita Verma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Refractory ascites (RA) secondary to advanced chronic liver disease (ACLD) results in poor health-related quality of life (HRQL), high symptom burden, and caregiver workload. Patient/caregiver-reported outcomes (PROs) remain uncharacterized in this cohort. This review summarises the available evidence on this topic.Methods:MEDLINE/Embase/Cochrane/PsycINFO/Web of Science/CINAHL were searched for articles that included PROs in RA secondary to ACLD. A structured narrative synthesis and a meta-analysis were undertaken.Results:Sixteen articles were included, comprising 1040 patients and 47 caregivers. Fourteen PRO measures were used, most commonly the Short Form-36 (SF-36) and the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ). Domain scores were pooled from 4 SF-36 and 3 CLDQ studies. Pooled domain scores were lower than those reported in stable outpatients with cirrhosis in 3/8 SF-36 domains: Social functioning (mean difference −18.8 [95% CI: −30.2 to −7.3]), Mental health (−17.9 [−33.5 to −2.3]), and Physical functioning (−13.6 [−24.5 to −2.7]); and 4/6 CLDQ domains: Abdominal symptoms (−1.5 [−2.3 to −0.7]), Activity (−0.9 [−1.4 to −0.5]), Worry (−1.2 [−2.1 to −0.3]), and Emotional function (−0.7 [−1.3 to −0.1]). Disease-specific tools had broader domain coverage and were more sensitive at picking up impairments in HRQL compared with generic ones. One symptom tool (the Ascites Questionnaire) had been validated in RA. The Short-Form Liver Disease Quality of Life questionnaire (SFLDQoL), the only HRQL measure validated in decompensated ACLD, was used once. Only 2 studies assessed informal caregivers.Conclusions:RA in ACLD detrimentally impacts HRQL across multiple domains. However, the lack of validated tools remains a barrier to assessing PROs and conducting well-designed RCTs in this cohort. This review highlights potential domains for the future development of such tools, which could drive equitable, patient-centered care in ACLD.Keywords: end-stage liver disease, health-related quality of life, patient-reported outcomes

INTRODUCTION

Refractory ascites (RA) is a challenging complication of advanced chronic liver disease (ACLD), with only a minority of patients being eligible for either liver transplantation (LTx) or a transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS).1,2,3 Transplant-free survival is estimated to be between 6 and 12 months, with mortality being as high as 80% at 5 years.4

The current standard of care for RA is large-volume paracentesis (LVP). This provides symptomatic relief but is not curative.1 The repeated build-up of ascites is associated with increasing symptom burden.5 To access hospital drainage, patients often have to navigate complex secondary care pathways, at the same time as having to come to terms with a life-limiting illness and the prospect of death.6

Beyond patients, informal caregivers of patients with ACLD are also affected by the burden of the disease.7,8 Studies demonstrate higher caregiver burden, lower health-related quality of life (HRQL), and higher incidence of psychological distress among caregivers compared with the general population, with ascites being a significant driver of all 3.7,8

Despite RA being recognized as a prompt to shift toward symptom-based management, timely linkage to palliative care (PC) remains suboptimal in ACLD.2,3 This denies patients and caregivers access to holistic care, which could alleviate symptoms and manage uncertainty associated with an incurable condition.9 This gap in clinical care is mirrored in research with a disproportionate focus on procedural outcomes and clinical endpoints. In a recent review of therapeutic interventions for RA, only 3 out of 77 studies looked at the impact on HRQL.10

Patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures are tools that measure symptoms, function, or well-being in relation to a health condition, from the perspective of patients, without interpretation by health-care professionals.11 These are arguably more relevant for a cohort approaching the end of life.12 The lack of validated PRO measures in ACLD, including RA, has been identified as an important barrier for the design and implementation of palliative clinical trials.13

Reviewing the current evidence on PROs in RA is a necessary first step toward understanding the lived experience of such patients and could provide the basis for developing the robust outcome measures needed to inform palliative interventions in this marginalized group.12

The aims of this systematic review and meta-analysis were therefore to:

summarize the quality of evidence around PROs in RA;

summarize the quality of evidence around PROs in RA;

examine the most commonly used PRO measures and the domains assessed in each; and

examine the most commonly used PRO measures and the domains assessed in each; and

explore the PRO domains most affected in RA and the impact of non-transplant interventions.

explore the PRO domains most affected in RA and the impact of non-transplant interventions.

METHODS

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplemental Appendix S1, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C490). Since it analysed publicly available secondary data, the review is exempt from formal Insitutional Review Board (IRB) approval. A detailed protocol was prospectively registered and published on PROSPERO (CRD42024432741).14

Search strategy and selection criteria

Six databases (MEDLINE/Embase/Cochrane/PsycINFO/Web of Science/CINAHL) were searched from their inception until May 20, 2026. A peer-reviewed search strategy framework was designed for MEDLINE and adapted for each database (Supplemental Appendix S2, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C490). The search terms followed the patient/intervention/comparator/outcome (PICO) framework. Due to the expected heterogeneity in study design, the “intervention” and “comparator” components of the search were left undefined to preserve the inclusivity of the search results.

Original articles (in English), irrespective of study design, that included adult patients (aged ≥18 y) with RA secondary to ACLD, providing data on patient/caregiver-reported outcomes were included. Studies that did not specifically mention RA were excluded. Authors of studies published after 2010 were contacted to provide further data if necessary. Reference articles of identified reviews were screened, and major journal websites were also hand-searched. Studies were classified as interventional versus observational and further grouped by the type of intervention.

Data extraction

Search results were combined and uploaded onto a systematic review platform, Rayyan.15 Duplicates were identified and manually removed. Two independent reviewers (Yazan Haddadin and James Ryley) screened titles, abstracts, and full texts against pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Data extraction forms were agreed a priori by 2 reviewers (Yazan Haddadin and Sumita Verma) and extracted by 1 (Yazan Haddadin). Extracted data were subsequently verified by 2 independent reviewers (Lucia Macken and Sumita Verma).

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was any reported PRO score at baseline and follow-up timepoints. Other variables assessed included study design, patient demographics, RA definition, cirrhosis etiology, prevalence of decompensation events, inclusion or exclusion criteria, primary study outcomes, presence of control groups, identified determinants of PROs, and treatment effect measures influencing PROs where available (eg, odds ratios, hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals, and/or p-values). If PRO scores were only provided in figures, authors were contacted to provide numerical scores, or a web tool was used to extract values of interest from high-quality graphs if applicable.16

Risk of bias assessments

Eligible studies were evaluated for risk of bias using the relevant Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist according to study type.17 All quality assessments were conducted independently by 2 reviewers (James Ryley and Yazan Haddadin). Disagreements were resolved by consensus and arbitrated by a third reviewer (Sumita Verma) if necessary. In anticipation of the paucity of data, no study was excluded based on the risk of bias assessment.

Data analysis

To provide a framework for the narrative synthesis, a list of PRO domains was adapted from the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) a priori.18 Identified PRO measures from the literature were deconstructed into their constituent items and mapped onto these domains. This process was carried out by 2 independent reviewers (Yazan Haddadin and Benjaman To), and disagreements were resolved through discussion. This methodology was adapted, with permission, from Singh et al.19

Where 3 or more studies reported the same PRO scores at baseline or used the same PRO in the assessment of the same intervention, a meta-analysis was carried out to provide an estimate of: (i) HRQL domain scores or (ii) treatment effect measures. Sample means and standard deviations were estimated from reported median/interquartile range (IQR) using established formulas.20,21 A random-effects meta-analysis was conducted in R statistical software (v4.5.3) using the metafor package (v4.8.0), and mean values were pooled using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) meta-analysis model.22,23 The I 2 statistic was used to quantify heterogeneity across studies, with values >75% suggesting high heterogeneity.24 No subgroup analysis was planned in the meta-analysis. Where published data for the same PRO instrument were available, pooled estimates for RA were compared with scores reported from cohorts with stable cirrhosis. Statistical comparisons were interpreted cautiously because of clinical and methodological differences between cohorts.

Risk of bias

The open-label design in all the RCTs and the lack of reporting of the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of PRO measures used were common sources of bias. The MCID was clearly reported in only one study.36 MCID is a key concept in PRO research and represents the smallest change in a score that could be perceived by patients as beneficial or harmful.41 Conclusions about the impact of interventions were frequently drawn despite the absence of MCID data. Other issues included small sample sizes and high rates of missingness and attrition. Detailed risk of bias assessments are provided in Figure 2. There was <10% disagreement between the 2 assessors (Yazan Haddadin and James Ryley), which was all resolved by consensus.

PRO tools

Across the 16 studies, 14 PRO measures were reported (Supplemental Appendix S4, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C490). The most frequently reported disease-specific HRQL tool was the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ)42 (n=5); the most frequently reported generic HRQL tool was the Short Form-36 (SF-36) questionnaire (n=6).43 Only one study used the Short Form Liver Disease Quality of Life (SFLDQoL) questionnaire,44 the only HRQL tool validated in decompensated ACLD (dACLD). The most frequently reported symptom-burden tools were the Ascites Questionnaire (Ascites-Q)36 (n=3) and the Ascites Symptom Inventory-7 (ASI-7)45 (n=3). The Ascites-Q was the only tool validated specifically in RA. Only 2 studies assessed informal caregivers, one recruiting caregiver-patient dyads in ACLD,37 the second focusing on patients with RA.35

Domain coverage and prevalence

The identified PRO tools yielded 122 unique items that mapped onto 15 domains across 4 broad categories. The coverage of each PRO tool against these domains is shown in Figure 3, whereas the prevalence of each domain in the literature is shown in Figure 4. Details of the mapping process are provided in Supplemental Appendix S5, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C490. The SFLDQoL44 had the greatest breadth, covering 12 domains. For tools assessing symptom burden, the Integrated Palliative care Outcome Scale (IPOS)46 had the broadest coverage (9 domains), followed by the Ascites-Q (7 domains).36 Domains, such as physical function and fatigue, were consistently assessed in the literature (domain prevalence 100%), whereas sleep, social isolation/companionship, sexual function, and cognitive function were under-reported (domain prevalence 13%–31%).

Health utility indices

Health utility indices (HUI) are numerical anchored at 0 (representing death) and 1 (representing perfect health) that reflect the health status of an individual.47 Scores may be negative for health states considered worse than death. The calculation of HUI often relies on population surveys that use specific PRO tools. However, only 2 studies reported HUIs for patients with RA (mean HUI 0.56–0.58);27,35 despite studies in the literature using higher estimates (0.6–0.7) when assessing the cost-effectiveness of interventions in RA secondary to ACLD.48,49,50,51

Meta-analysis

Domain scores at baseline were available in 4 out of 6 studies (67%) using the SF-36 tool27,37,38,40 and 3 out of 5 studies (60%) using the CLDQ.27,31,32 For both SF-36 and CLDQ, higher scores indicate better HRQL. Pooled SF-36 domain scores were, on average, lower than those reported in a stable outpatient cohort with cirrhosis52 in 3 out of 8 domains (Figure 5A): Social functioning (46.1 [95% CI 35.5–56.7] vs. 64.9 [95% CI 60.5–69.2]; mean difference (MD) −18.8 [95% CI −30.2 to −7.3]), Mental health (50.6 [95% CI 35.6–65.6] vs. 68.5 [95% CI 64.3–72.7]; MD −17.9 [95% CI −33.5 to −2.3]), and Physical functioning (37.0 [95% CI 27.3–46.8] vs. 50.6 [95% CI 45.8–55.5]; MD −13.6 [95% CI −24.5 to −2.7]). For all 3 domains, the point estimates of these mean differences exceeded the MCID: Social functioning (18.8 vs. 10.8 points), Mental health (17.9 vs. 7.3 points), and Physical functioning (13.6 vs. 7.3 points). Pooled CLDQ scores were on average lower than the same comparator cohort in 4 out of 6 domains (Figure 5B): Abdominal symptoms (3.5 [95% CI 2.7–4.2] vs. 5.0 [95% CI 4.7–5.2]; MD −1.5 [95% CI −2.3 to −0.7]), Activity (3.8 [95% CI 3.4–4.1] vs. 4.7 [95% CI 4.4–4.9]; MD −0.9 [95% CI −1.4 to −0.5]), Worry (3.5 [95% CI 2.6–4.3] vs. 4.7 [95% CI 4.4–5.0]; MD −1.2 [95% CI −2.1 to −0.3]), and Emotional function (4.1 [95% CI 3.5–4.6] vs. 4.7 [95% CI 4.5–5.0]; MD −0.7 [95% CI −1.3 to −0.1]). The point estimates of these MDs exceeded the MCID for all 4 domains: Abdominal symptoms (1.5 vs. 0.7 points), Activity (0.9 vs. 0.8 points), Worry (1.2 vs. 0.5 points), and Emotional function (0.7 vs. 0.5 points). Detailed methodology for the meta-analysis, and individual forest plots are provided in Supplemental Appendices S6 and S7, https://links.lww.com/HC9/C490.

DISCUSSION

This is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to focus on PROs in patients with RA secondary to ACLD. Sixteen studies were identified, reporting 14 distinct PRO measures in 1040 patients with RA. Our key findings demonstrate that HRQL in RA is substantially worse than in those with stable cirrhosis, with clinically important differences across multiple SF-36 and CLDQ domains. Most studies used tools that were not validated in RA; consequently, key domains relevant to the lived experience of patients, including sleep, social isolation, and cognition, were inconsistently captured across the literature. In addition, the primary outcomes were PROs in only 5 studies, and in none of the 6 RCTs identified. Together, these findings highlight a discrepancy between the patient-reported burden of disease and the limited extent to which trials were focused on addressing it.

Despite PROs having been recognized as important outcomes for almost 50 years, the published literature in RA due to ACLD remains limited. This is in stark contrast to other areas in hepatology where the evidence base is more established.53 Historically, there has been a disproportionate focus on clinical outcomes in this cohort. A recent review identified 47 studies reporting such outcomes following TIPS in RA,10 whereas our review identified only 2 reporting PROs for the same intervention (both published over 20 y ago).25,26 Our results also demonstrate the continued exclusion of RA patients with other ACLD complications, such as hepatic encephalopathy, HCC, and AKI, from clinical trials. This likely overestimates the HRQL of patients with RA, excludes those most likely to benefit from evidence-based palliative interventions, and limits the generalizability of the results. Recent reviews and society guidance have highlighted restrictive inclusion criteria as a barrier to palliative care research in ACLD.13,54

Our meta-analysis confirms a clinically meaningful, detrimental impact of RA on HRQL. Compared with a stable outpatient cirrhosis cohort, pooled estimates for patients with RA were lower in social functioning, mental health, and physical functioning on the SF-36, and in abdominal symptoms, activity, worry, and emotional function on the CLDQ; point estimates of these differences exceeded published MCIDs, indicating a signal towards clinically meaningful impairment.52 High LVP frequency and drainage volumes worsened symptom burden and HRQL scores, whereas relief of ascites, reduced abdominal distention, fullness, and back pain correlated with improved HRQL scores. The capacity to detect these effects, however, depended on the tools used. Disease-specific instruments were more sensitive at picking up impairments in HRQL compared with generic ones. This was evident in our meta-analysis, where RA impacted a higher proportion of domains on the disease-specific CLDQ compared with the generic SF-36 (67% vs. 38%). This was also seen in the ALFA pump RCT, which used both tools and found that CLDQ was more sensitive to intervention-related change than the SF-36.27

Although generic tools have some strengths in allowing for cross-disease comparisons, they are susceptible to “floor effects” in severely ill patients, where scores cluster at the lower boundary of the scales, thus reducing the tool’s ability to detect meaningful differences.55,56 For example, in a large international cohort of over 400 patients with RA, over half scored 0 (the lowest possible score out of 100) on the SF-36 “role physical” domain.38 Disease-specific tools, in contrast, are better designed to capture HRQL domains most relevant to patients, and are typically more responsive to disease progression, deterioration, or treatment-led effects.55 However, our results demonstrate that domain coverage varies between different disease-specific measures. The CLDQ, for example, which was the most commonly reported disease-specific tool in our review, has not been validated in decompensated cirrhosis and misses important domains relevant to RA, including sleep, cognitive function, social isolation, and companionship. The SFLDQoL, which is the only HRQL tool validated in dACLD, had the greatest breadth in our review and covered these important domains. Despite this, it was reported only in one study and was notably absent from a recent published review of available PROs in liver disease.35,53 The SFLDQoL is not without its limitations though; it is significantly longer than the CLDQ (72 vs. 29 items) and its validation cohort consisted of patients on the transplant waiting list, whereas the majority of patients with RA are ineligible for this potentially curative pathway.1 Taken together, our findings highlight the need for a robust instrument that is narrow enough to limit completion burden, yet broad enough to detect meaningful change from palliative interventions. Developing such a tool can take years,53 though our study has highlighted some domains of relevance to patients with ACLD and RA to include Physical and Social functioning, Mental health, Abdominal symptoms, Activity, Worry, and Emotional function.

Synthesizing the evidence around caregiver-reported outcomes in RA was not feasible, as only 2 studies formally assessed them.35,37 Nonetheless, both highlighted the negative impact of ACLD on the well-being of caregivers. Routine inclusion of caregivers in ACLD trials can improve our understanding of the societal impact of disease, inform study design of community-based interventions, and facilitate study recruitment and participant retention.6,13 A recently published landmark RCT, looking at the impact of a palliative care intervention on PROs in ACLD, was successful in the recruitment of >900 patients with 60% co-enrollment of caregivers. Retention at 3-months was high, at over 73%.57 Despite the significance of this study, unfortunately, we were unable to include it in our systematic review as data on RA were not collected.

Based on our review, no conclusion can be drawn about the impact of any specific RA intervention on PROs, as in most studies, these were secondary endpoints. The interpretation of findings was limited by small sample sizes, high attrition or early study termination, and the lack of comparison with MCIDs to establish whether results were clinically meaningful. Short-term improvements in HRQL domains and symptom burden were observed with the ALFA pump;27,28,29 however, durability and clinical significance were difficult to interpret due to the above-mentioned limitations. The TIPS data was undermined by the use of a non-validated PRO measure,25 and by inconsistent statistical methodology.26 The CTI trials were limited by early termination and differences in home visit scheduling32,33 which could have influenced symptoms and HRQL.58 The LTAD feasibility trial reported contradictory results across different PRO measures, though it was not powered to detect differences in PRO.35 This was likely due to small sample variation and lack of precision. Ongoing trials are looking at the role of interventions, including palliative LTAD and high-dose albumin infusion PROs in RA.59,60

Our results also have important implications for health economic analyses of interventions in RA. Only 2 studies reported a HUI in patients with RA, with mean values of 0.56 and 0.58.27,35 Previously published cost-effectiveness analyses of interventions, including TIPS, in this population have used estimates borrowed from other diseases (ranging between 0.6 and 0.7).48,49,50,51 Overinflated baseline HRQL for patients with RA would systematically underestimate the incremental benefit of any intervention that has the potential to improve HRQL, and increase the cost of any quality-adjusted life-year gained. Empirically derived HUIs from RA patients, generated alongside interventional trials, would improve the sensitivity analyses of cost-effectiveness studies.

A limitation of this review is that the random-effects meta-analysis model was conducted on a small number of studies (SF-36, n=4; CLDQ, n=3), which limits the reliability of the between-study variance estimate. Furthermore, a high I 2 statistic suggests PRO domain scores are context-specific and therefore indirect comparison in our meta-analysis should be interpreted cautiously. However, the results of these comparisons were corroborated by findings from the individual studies in the review. Another limitation was the process of mapping PRO tools across PROMIS-based domains, which involved an element of subjectivity, but the effect was mitigated by the use of 2 independent reviewers.

In conclusion, RA in ACLD is associated with significantly impaired PROs across multiple physical, social, and mental domains, with HRQL being substantially worse than in patients with stable cirrhosis. However, the absence of validated, disease-specific HRQL tools remains a barrier to assessing PROs in this cohort and to conducting well-designed RCTs. Our findings reveal a lack of consensus in this area of research and underscore the need for a shared framework and consistent approach to PROs in this cohort. Future research should prioritize the development of such tools in a collaborative effort between the hepatology community and patients/caregivers. This will help drive equitable, patient-centered care for patients and caregivers with RA due to ACLD.
