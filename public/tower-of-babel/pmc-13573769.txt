Functional Variation in the Feeding Apparatus of Charadriiform Shorebirds With Emphasis on Bill‐Tip Organs and Micro Tongue Tip Spines
Authors: Chieh‐Ting Lin, Daniel B. Thomas, Phil F. Battley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTShorebirds within Charadriiformes show substantial variation in where, what, and how they eat. Foraging is aided by a complex feeding apparatus that may include a bill‐tip organ with sensory pits and micro tongue tip spines. The morphology of these structures varies among species, but their evolutionary patterns have not been previously mapped. Here we present a comparative morphological analysis of 20 shorebird species across five families, using morphometric data from digital photographs and scanning electron microscopy to reconstruct the evolutionary history of bill‐tip organ morphology and micro tongue tip spines. While sensory pits occurred in all species, well‐developed bill‐tip organs for remote touch were characteristic of Scolopacidae, with pit number and bill length varying among species. Sensory pit numbers were best explained by a Brownian motion model of trait evolution, suggesting increases and decreases of pit numbers across time and high pit numbers in the most recent common ancestor of this clade. Species possessing tongue spines were generally smaller and exhibited a shorter distance between the tongue tip and mandible tip (e.g., the black‐fronted dotterel, Thinornis melanops), supporting a functional role in biofilm grazing. The presence of micro tongue tip spines was best explained by an equal‐rates model of trait evolution, with the trait evolving twice with no evidence of subsequent loss: once in the most recent common ancestor shared by calidrid sandpipers (Calidris spp.), shanks (Tringa spp.), the terek sandpiper (Xenus cinereus), and the ruddy turnstone (Arenaria interpres), and again in a plover lineage. The phylogenetic distribution of sensory pits and tongue spines showed functional adaptations to diverse foraging strategies. These findings provide new insights into the evolutionary diversification of the shorebird feeding apparatus.

Introduction

Shorebirds of the order Charadriiformes often forage in wetlands and mudflats and have developed a wide range of foraging strategies across dietary niches, reflected in variation in feeding apparatus morphology, such as bill length and shape, which are closely associated with particular diets (Baker 1979; Burton 1974; Colwell 2010). This inter‐ and intraspecific variation is related to foraging behaviors such as probing, pecking and scything (Johnston 2014; Pierce 1985; Rico‐Guevara et al. 2019), and allows different species or individuals to forage together in the same habitat, as resource partitioning reduces competition among shorebirds (Lourenço et al. 2016; Nebel and Thompson 2011; Ross 2018). Links between bill length and foraging behavior extend deep into shorebird evolutionary history (Moreno 1999). However, despite extensive research on bill size and shape, the evolutionary and functional relationships of sensory pits, bill‐tip organs, tongue spines, and other components of the shorebird feeding apparatus remain an underexplored aspect of morphology.

Bill length and curvature are mostly well described in birds (Burton 1974; Ferns and Siman 1994), whereas the morphology and evolution of bony sensory pits remain undescribed for many species. These pits are small cavities beneath the rhamphotheca that contain high densities of Herbst corpuscles and function as pressure detectors during foraging (Piersma et al. 1998; Rico‐Guevara et al. 2019). The bill‐tip organ, located in the distal or mid portions of the maxilla and mandible in some birds, consists of clusters of sensory pits and functions in vibration detection and “remote touch” during probing (Cunningham et al. 2013; du Toit 2022; du Toit et al. 2020, 2022, 2024; Nebel et al. 2005). This remote touch foraging mechanism allows birds to detect pressure gradients and reflected signals generated by prey hidden within sediment (Cunningham et al. 2010; Nebel et al. 2005; Piersma et al. 1998; van de Kam et al. 2004).

Bill‐tip organs are known from several shorebirds within Scolopacidae (Charadriiformes), ibises (Pelecaniformes: Threskiornithidae) and kiwi (Apterygiformes: Apterygidae), and show variation in sensory pit number and distribution across species (Cunningham et al. 2010; Cunningham et al. 2013; Schäfer and Schmitz 2016). Studies of bill‐tip organs in Scolopacidae have focused on Calidris where remote touch appears to be a primary detection mechanism (Nebel et al. 2005; Piersma et al. 1998). This variation may reflect foraging strategy, with birds possessing more sensory pits potentially spending more time in aquatic habitats or relying less on other feeding cues (Cunningham et al. 2010; Cunningham et al. 2013; Martin and Piersma 2009). Because sensory pits and bill‐tip organs occur in various forms among probe‐feeding birds across distantly related families, and are not present in all Charadriiformes, the bill‐tip organ has been suggested to represent an independent evolutionary adaptation to probing under specific ecological pressures (Cunningham et al. 2013; Kuhl et al. 2021; Martin and Piersma 2009; Massa et al. 2026). Comparative research on bill‐tip organs among different genera within Scolopacidae remains limited, as does understanding of the relationship between bill features and phylogeny.

In addition to bill morphology and bill‐tip organs involved in prey detection, the tongue plays an important role in food acquisition. As birds lack teeth, the tongue and palate function in food collection, manipulation, and swallowing, with variation in tongue morphology closely associated with dietary differences among birds (Erdoğan and Iwasaki 2014; Johnston 2014). The gross morphology of shorebird tongues includes diverse V‐shaped conical papillae that help prevent regurgitation during swallowing, elongated tongue bodies, and tongue tips typically composed of connective tissue and epidermal epithelium with an external keratinized layer (Erdoğan and Pérez 2015; Johnston 2014). These features are associated with variation in prey type and feeding ecology, as shorebirds primarily consume marine invertebrates, while some species also feed on terrestrial invertebrates, seeds, or fish (Hayman et al. 1986).

Beyond general tongue morphology, recent studies provide functional and mechanistic evidence that some smaller shorebirds, including western sandpipers (Calidris mauri), dunlins (Calidris alpina), red‐necked stints (Calidris ruficollis), and wrybills (Anarhynchus frontalis), use micro keratinized spines on their tongue tips to graze on biofilm to meet their high energy demands (Beninger and Elner 2020; Elner et al. 2005; Kuwae et al. 2012; Mathot et al. 2010; Withington 2015). Biofilm is a complex microbial layer commonly found on the surfaces of intertidal mudflats and sandflats (Passarelli et al. 2015). This feeding mode has been associated with surface pecking and surface‐tension transport of paste‐like biofilm from the bill tip into the oral cavity (Kuwae et al. 2008; Rubega and Obst 1993; Sutherland et al. 2000). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) provides high‐magnification images that enable detailed examination of the surface texture and fine‐scale morphology of tongue structures (Beninger and Elner 2020; Dunlap and Adaskaveg 1997; Elner et al. 2005; Withington 2015), yet SEM records of these structures in many shorebird species remain limited. Furthermore, the presence of tongue spines appears to be phylogenetically dependent based on findings from Kuwae et al. (2012).

In this study, we examine how phylogeny and foraging methods shape variation in feeding apparatus traits in charadriiform shorebirds through comparative morphological and phylogenetic analyses of the bill and tongue. Molecular phylogenies provide robust frameworks for evolutionary relationships among shorebirds, but morphological traits associated with feeding ecology may not always align with molecular patterns, potentially reflecting ecological specialization (Gibson and Baker 2012; Mayr 2011). Comparative morphological analyses within a phylogenetic framework are therefore important for understanding the evolutionary significance of feeding structures. Our study addresses these patterns through examination of sensory pits within bill‐tip organs across the genera Calidris, Tringa, Limosa, Xenus, Numenius, and Arenaria, the presence of micro tongue tip spines across species, and their associations with other traits such as bird mass, bill length, and tongue length.

To investigate the evolutionary pathways underlying these traits, alternative evolutionary models were applied within a phylogenetic framework (Burnham and Anderson 2002; Cavalli‐Sforza and Edwards 1967). For continuous traits, Brownian motion assumes that trait values evolve as a random walk over time along the phylogeny, with independent changes occurring along each branch (Cavalli‐Sforza and Edwards 1967; Thomas and Freckleton 2012), whereas Pagel's lambda scales the phylogenetic signal, reflecting the non‐random tendency of closely related species to resemble each other (Münkemüller et al. 2012; Pagel 1999). For discrete traits, the equal‐rates model assumes equal transition rates between trait states across the phylogeny, meaning traits are gained and lost at the same rate, whereas the all‐rates‐different model allows unequal transition rates among trait states (Pennell et al. 2014). By integrating morphological and phylogenetic analyses, this study provides insight into the links between feeding morphology and ecological function, with implications for understanding the evolution and conservation of shorebird diversity.

Material and Methods

Specimens

Forty individual birds (accidental casualties or birds found dead) from New Zealand and Australia were analyzed, comprising 14 species in the family Scolopacidae (suborder Scolopaci), one in Glareolidae (suborder Lari), three in Charadriidae, one in Recurvirostridae, and one in Haematopodidae (all suborder Charadrii). Taxonomy follows Kuhl et al. (2021)) (see Table S1 for specimen information, including common and scientific names). Frozen specimens were defrosted in a refrigerator for 24 h, after which the birds' heads were removed for dissection (Figure 1). All 40 individuals were included, although the maxilla of a little whimbrel (Numenius minutus) and the dentary of a red‐necked stint (Calidris ruficollis) were damaged during preparation.

Flow diagram showing the stepwise workflow for sample preparation, imaging and analysis of the shorebird feeding apparatus.

Digital Photography

Digital photographs of the head, bill, palate and tongue were taken using a mounted camera (Canon EOS Kiss X5 with a 50 or 100 mm lens depending on specimen size), with a scale included in each image as a reference. First, the right lateral view of the head with the bill was imaged to measure the straight lengths of maxilla and mandible with rhamphotheca. The upper and lower jaws were then dissected to expose the palate and tongue, after which the tongue was further separated from the mandible for detailed imaging. Images were taken of the ventral view of palates, the dorsal view of tongues within the mandible, and the dorsal and left lateral views of tongues, with a scale placed in the same plane for reference. At the end of the dissection, the tongue tip was removed for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) preparation. The remainder of the head was cleaned to obtain the skull by maceration in warm water below 50°C for approximately 1 week (Ajayi et al. 2016), followed by flushing to remove remaining tissue. Once cleaned, the bone was used for imaging the skull and bill‐tip organ using the mounted camera. Images from eight angles (dorsal, left dorsal lateral 45°, left lateral, left ventral lateral 45°, ventral, right ventral lateral 45°, right lateral, and right dorsal lateral 45°) of the premaxilla and dentary were used to measure bill‐tip organ length and count sensory pit numbers. The software ImageJ was used to measure lengths and count sensory pit numbers (Schneider et al. 2012). Measurements of the upper jaw included palate length, distance between the palate tip and conical papillae, conical papillae width, and choanal cleft length. Measurements of the lower jaw included the distance between the tongue tip and mandible tip, tongue length, distances between the tongue tip and papillary crest and between the papillary crest and the rear of the tongue, and papillary crest width and thickness (see Figures S1–S3). Bill‐tip organ lengths were measurable only in species within Scolopacidae. Sensory pit density was calculated as pit number divided by bill‐tip organ length.

Scanning Electron Microscopy

At the end of the dissection, the tongue tip was removed and soaked overnight at room temperature in primary fixative (Modified Karnovsky's fixative: 3% glutaraldehyde and 2% formaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.2) for SEM analysis. The fixative was removed the following day. The sample was then rinsed three times (10–15 min each) in phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.2) and dehydrated in an ascending ethanol series (25%, 50%, 75%, 95%, 100%), each for 10–15 min, followed by a final 100% ethanol wash for 1 h. The sample was further critical point dried using liquid CO2 as the critical point drying fluid and 100% ethanol as the intermediary (Polaron E3000 Series II critical point drying apparatus). Samples were mounted onto aluminum stubs using double‐sided tape and sputter‐coated with approximately 100 nm of gold (Baltec SCD 050 sputter coater). The mounted samples were then examined using an FEI Quanta 200 Environmental Scanning Electron Microscope at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV at an institutional microscopy facility. The tongues were not in optimal condition due to long‐term frozen storage between 2004 and 2014. However, several micro tongue tip spines were successfully preserved and observed using SEM despite concerns regarding specimen freshness.

Group Comparisons

Data normality of measured morphological variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and Tukey's Ladder of Powers was applied to transform non‐normal data where normality could be achieved (Royston 1995; Tukey 1977). Spearman's rank correlation was used to test comparisons involving non‐normal data, including relationships between bill‐tip organ length and maxilla and mandible straight length; sensory pit number and bill‐tip organ length of the premaxilla and dentary; sensory pit density and bill‐tip organ length of the premaxilla and dentary; and tongue length in relation to mandible straight length and the distance from the tongue tip to the mandible tip. To test for differences in bird mass, mandible length, and the distance from the tongue tip to the mandible tip between groups defined by the presence of micro tongue tip spines, t‐tests were applied to the transformed species averages of these variables. Additionally, data for micro tongue tip spines from Kuwae et al. (2012) and Withington (2015) were included to increase species coverage.

Phylogenetic Analyses

Specific traits of shorebirds, including total sensory pit numbers and the presence of micro tongue tip spines, were selected for phylogenetic signal tests. The operational taxonomic units (OTUs) corresponding to the study species were obtained from the BirdTree database (Jetz et al. 2012, 2014). A set of 100 phylogenetic trees containing these taxa was obtained from the database and used in subsequent phylogenetic analyses to account for phylogenetic uncertainty. Phylogenetic trees were visualized in R using the package ape (version 5.6‐2) and annotated using phytools (version 1.0‐1) (Paradis and Schliep 2019; Revell 2012). The resulting phylogenies were broadly consistent with relationships reported in previous studies based on multiple gene sequences and other taxonomic evidence (Christian et al. 1992; Gibson and Baker 2012; Mayr 2011).

Brownian motion and Pagel's lambda models were used to model continuous traits. These models were compared to determine the best‐fitting model of sensory pit number evolution using the R package MOTMOT (version 2.1.3) across a set of 100 phylogenetic trees, with Brownian motion set as the null model. Similarly, equal‐rates and all‐rates‐different models were used to model discrete trait evolution. These models were compared to identify the best‐fitting model for the presence of micro tongue tip spines using the R package geiger (version 2.0.10) (Pennell et al. 2014). Lambda transformations were also applied to assess the strength of phylogenetic signal (λ), with a model lacking phylogenetic signal set as the null hypothesis (Münkemüller et al. 2012; Pagel 1999). The best‐fitting evolutionary model was then used to estimate the probabilities of the presence or absence of tongue tip spines at each phylogenetic node through ancestral state reconstruction.

Results

Sensory Pits and Bill Length

Sensory pits were present in all species; however, species in Glareolidae, Charadriidae, Recurvirostridae, and Haematopodidae exhibited only a few minute foramina in their bill bones and lacked the bill‐tip organs comprising clusters of sensory pits characteristic of Scolopacidae (Figure 2). Among Scolopacidae, Calidris generally had the highest number of sensory pits (376–549 pits). Tringa had the second highest counts (241–375 pits), with values for the grey‐tailed tattler (T. brevipes; 241–244 pits) similar to those of the bar‐tailed godwit (Limosa lapponica) (201–250 pits). The ruddy turnstone (Arenaria interpres) had the lowest count (149 pits), with shallow sensory pits that appeared distinct from those of other species, and a rhamphotheca that was robust and firmly attached to the underlying bone (Figures 2 and 3).

Within Scolopacidae, birds with longer bill lengths had correspondingly longer bill‐tip organs in both the maxilla (Spearman's ρ = 0.46) and the mandible (Spearman's ρ = 0.51; Figure 4a,b). Both sensory pit number and density decreased with increasing bill‐tip organ length in the premaxilla (Spearman's ρ = −0.31 and −0.89, respectively; Figure 4c,e) and dentary (Spearman's ρ = −0.18 and −0.80, respectively; Figure 4d,f).

Despite having substantially longer bills, the bar‐tailed godwits exhibited bill‐tip organ lengths similar to those of the terek sandpiper (Xenus cinereus), the little whimbrel (Numenius minutus), A. interpres, and species of Calidris. However, despite their short bills and bill‐tip organs, Calidris had higher sensory pit numbers and densities than the other species. The red‐necked stints (C. ruficollis) exhibited the highest pit densities despite not having the greatest total number of pits (Figure 2). Additionally, within Calidris, pit density decreased with body size, with the highest densities observed in the smallest species (C. ruficollis, curlew sandpiper (C. ferruginea), and sharp‐tailed sandpiper (C. acuminata)) and the lowest density in the largest species, the great knot (C. tenuirostris). In contrast, Tringa had elongated bill‐tip organs occupying a greater proportion of the long (28.4–53.1 mm), rigid, dorsoventrally thick bill but with low pit densities (Figure 2). The common greenshank (T. nebularia) had the highest total pit numbers in Tringa, although pit density remained low. Within Tringa, pit number varied considerably and increased with bill‐tip organ length. The whimbrel (N. phaeopus) had a relatively long dentary bill‐tip organ, with sensory pits aligned along the elongated, curved mandible but at low density (see Figure S4). Within Numenius, despite the whimbrel having a much longer bill than the little whimbrel, they had similarly small numbers of sensory pits.

Tongue Structure

The tongues were enclosed within the mandibles. Birds with longer tongue lengths had correspondingly longer mandible lengths (Spearman's ρ = 0.83; Figure 5a) and greater distances from the tongue tip to the mandible tip (Spearman's ρ = 0.63; Figure 5b), with considerable interspecific variation. Although the South Island pied oystercatcher (Haematopus finschi) had a long bill, it had a very short tongue, resulting in a long distance between the tongue tip and mandible tip. Numenius species also had relatively short tongues compared with their bill lengths. In contrast, calidrid sandpipers (Calidris spp.) and terek sandpipers showed a range of tongue lengths, but all terminated within 1 cm of the bill tip.

Discussion

Sensory pit count and bill‐tip organ shape show substantial variation across shorebirds. These patterns suggest that pit number is not explained solely by foraging habitat and may also relate to prey type and foraging behavior. All species possess at least a few sensory pits in the bony premaxilla, indicating that even species lacking well‐developed bill‐tip organs with dense sensory pits may still use direct touch to detect prey (Heppleston 1970), although remote touch foraging facilitated by bill‐tip organs has been suggested to be more efficient (van de Kam et al. 2004). Notably, grey‐tailed tattlers (Tringa brevipes), terek sandpipers (Xenus cinereus), bar‐tailed godwits (Limosa lapponica), and whimbrels (Numenius phaeopus) all feed on crabs or polychaetes through remote‐touch probing and possess similar numbers of sensory pits (Duijns et al. 2013; Hockey and Douie 1995; Steed 2017), suggesting that remote‐touch probing may be associated with a characteristic range of sensory pit numbers. Foraging habitat has also been suggested to influence variation in sensory pit number (Cunningham et al. 2010; du Toit 2022). Little whimbrels (N. minutus) prefer inland terrestrial habitats and have low sensory pit numbers in their bill‐tip organs. Ruddy turnstones (Arenaria interpres) also have low sensory pit numbers (149 pits in our study and 130–140 reported by Massa et al. 2026), despite using their bills in diverse ways while foraging on tidal flats. However, substantial variation is also evident among aquatic species. For example, the sharp‐tailed sandpiper (Calidris acuminata) has 549 sensory pits, whereas X. cinereus has only 192–220 pits (140–160 reported by Massa et al. 2026). van de Kam et al. (2004) suggested that knots prefer moderately wet flats, where sediment conditions optimize pressure fields for remote touch foraging. Interestingly, the terrestrial North Island brown kiwi (Apteryx mantelli) also possesses numerous sensory pits (251), despite being distantly related to shorebirds, suggesting that high sensory pit numbers may represent a convergent adaptation for tactile foraging rather than a trait unique to shorebirds (Cunningham et al. 2013). It is also important to note that the number of mechanoreceptors embedded within each sensory pit is not fixed (Cunningham et al. 2013). For example, ibis species foraging in drier habitats have more Herbst corpuscles per sensory pit than species in more aquatic habitats, meaning that sensory pit number alone may not fully reflect tactile sensitivity (du Toit et al. 2022).

Most birds are thought to rely on multiple sensory cues for feeding. Some species, such as pied stilts (Himantopus leucocephalus formerly Himantopus himantopus), N. phaeopus, and shanks (Tringa spp.), have been suggested to rely more on tactile foraging during nocturnal feeding under low‐light conditions (Battley et al. 2003; Pierce 1985; Rojas et al. 1999; Turpie and Hockey 1993). However, some species with fewer sensory pits are still able to feed at night and have been suggested to rely more on visual cues (Martin and Piersma 2009; Rojas et al. 1999; Thomas et al. 2006). Overall, the relationship between sensory pit number and nocturnal feeding remains unclear. Sensory pit densities appear to be low in shanks that use scything during foraging. Scything is employed by some Tringa and members of Recurvirostridae (avocets and stilts) (Rico‐Guevara et al. 2019); however, Recurvirostridae lack bill‐tip organs (Schäfer and Schmitz 2016). This suggests that the prey capture mechanisms and diets of scything shanks and members of Recurvirostridae may differ, or that the bills of shanks serve multiple functions. Piscivorous shanks such as common greenshanks (T. nebularia) and marsh sandpipers (T. stagnatilis) feed on small fish and may use a ploughing foraging strategy, in which birds run rapidly forward in a line (Battley et al. 2003). An elongated sensory organ at the distal bill tip may enable detection of fish movement from multiple directions, facilitating herding and preventing prey from escaping. Finally, A. interpres possesses a short, wedge‐shaped, rigid bill with a low number of shallow sensory pits, previously considered absent (Schäfer and Schmitz 2016) but observed here, occupying a large proportion of bill length similar to that of shanks. This finding is consistent with du Toit et al. (2020), who considered the shallow sensory pits vestigial and unrelated to remote touch foraging. Nevertheless, its bill morphology enables diverse foraging behaviors, including flicking and pushing obstacles, probing crab burrows, surface pecking, smashing barnacles, and cutting gastropod adductor muscles (Hockey and Douie 1995; van de Kam et al. 2004; Whitfield 1990).

Generally, the gross structures of shorebird tongues and palates observed in this study are consistent with previous descriptions of other charadriiform shorebirds (Erdoğan and Pérez 2015; Johnston 2014). One notable finding of this study is the short tongue observed in the South Island pied oystercatcher (Haematopus finschi), a trait also reported in the Eurasian oystercatcher (H. ostralegus) (Hulscher 1982). This feature may be related to the robust bill of oystercatchers used for breaking bivalves, as a longer tongue could limit bill strength and thus reduce prying power (Dowding 2022). Similarly, Numenius species. also possess short tongues, which are considered a consequence of their heavily reinforced bills (Davidson et al. 1986).

SEM analyses demonstrated that micro tongue tip spines can be well preserved for many years when specimens are maintained under appropriate conditions. Nevertheless, specimen deterioration and limited sample sizes still prevented confirmation of their absence in some species. The small size of the tongue spines observed in the SEM images suggests a role in filtering or adhering to prey much smaller than typical benthic invertebrates (Beninger and Elner 2020). In addition to the species previously reported by Kuwae et al. (2012) and the wrybill (Anarhynchus frontalis) reported by Withington (2015), this study documents these spines for the first time in the black‐fronted dotterel (Thinornis melanops) and the curlew sandpiper (C. ferruginea). Combined with previous studies, these findings indicate that micro tongue tip spines are commonly present in calidrid sandpipers, shanks, X. cinereus, and A. interpres, but are absent in Limosa and Numenius. The occurrence of tongue tip spines in the species examined is consistent with the proposition of Kuwae et al. (2012) that these structures are more likely to be present in smaller shorebirds, which require high food intake to sustain their elevated metabolic rates, typically in species with a body mass no heavier than T. nebularia. However, the body masses of T. nebularia and great knot (C. tenuirostris) are similar to those of H. leucocephalus and N. minutus, which lack micro tongue tip spines, indicating that no clear body‐mass threshold distinguishes species with and without these structures. Furthermore, no micro tongue tip spines were observed in the small‐bodied red‐capped plover (Anarhynchus ruficapillus, formerly Charadrius ruficapillus) or the red‐kneed dotterel (Erythrogonys cinctus). The presence of micro tongue tip spines in one Anarhynchus species but not the other, together with their variable occurrence among plovers, suggests that factors beyond body mass likely influence their occurrence and that plover lineages warrant further investigation.

The mechanism of biofilm grazing using bristle‐like tongue tip spines has been suggested to be linked to surface pecking behavior and surface‐tension transport (Kuwae et al. 2008; Rubega and Obst 1993; Sutherland et al. 2000). This interpretation is consistent with tongue tip spines occurring in species with short‐ to medium‐length bills, but not in those with longer bills. Because surface pecking requires the tongue to contact the mud surface, the short gap between the tongue tip and mandible tip observed in some plovers, calidrid sandpipers, and X. cinereus may facilitate the trapping and transport of biofilm‐containing sediment. However, surface pecking is not exclusive to biofilm grazing and is also widely used by visually foraging species that lack tongue tip spines. In addition, our findings suggest another potential behavioral role for tongue tip spines during biofilm collection. There were species differences in the distribution of micro tongue spines, with those of the wood sandpiper (T. glareola) and X. cinereus occurring not only at the tongue tip but also along the elongated tongue body. As many shanks use scything foraging behavior, swinging their bills from side to side through shallow water or soft mud (Hockey and Douie 1995; Rico‐Guevara et al. 2019), spines distributed along the tongue body may help collect biofilm during this process. Similarly, A. frontalis has been suggested to use a tongue with a fringe of spines comparable to those described here when ingesting sediment and biofilm (Withington 2015). Moreover, if scything behavior is associated with biofilm feeding, the distance between the mandible tip and the tongue tip may be less critical than during pecking because the bill remains submerged. This may explain why Tringa exhibit slightly greater tongue tip–mandible tip distances than other species with tongue spines. However, similar to pecking, scything is also widely used by species lacking tongue tip spines. Finally, although our findings support a role for tongue spines in biofilm grazing, these structures may also serve additional functions.

The presence of sensory pits embedded in the bills of all birds examined in this study suggests that these structures were likely present in their most recent shared ancestor. In contrast, the clustering of these sensory pits into well‐developed bill‐tip organs appears to have occurred independently in multiple avian lineages, including the extant Scolopacidae, Threskiornithidae, and Apterygidae, as well as the extinct Lithornithidae from the Cretaceous (Cunningham et al. 2013; du Toit et al. 2020). This pattern suggests convergent evolution of these structures under similar selection pressures. Our results are consistent with this interpretation, as the best‐fitting Brownian motion model inferred that the large sensory pit numbers associated with the bill‐tip organ evolved in the most recent common ancestor of Scolopacidae (Thomas and Freckleton 2012). The observed taxonomic distribution of sensory pit numbers, with relatively small numbers in Limosa, Numenius, and Xenus, intermediate numbers in Tringa, and large numbers in Calidris, may reflect the accumulation of random trait variance expected under a Brownian motion model over evolutionary time (Cavalli‐Sforza and Edwards 1967). Bill morphology in Scolopacidae appears to have evolved relatively rapidly among shorebirds, likely reflecting foraging requirements, as indicated by their short phylogenetic branches (Moreno 1999). For example, the relatively small number of sensory pits in A. interpres may reflect a rapid evolutionary reduction associated with its specialized foraging strategies. Additionally, the high sensory pit number and density in the short‐billed red‐necked stint (C. ruficollis), compared with the lower number and density in C. tenuirostris, do not support the hypothesis of Moreno (1999) that tactile feeding coevolved only with long bill lengths.

The taxonomic distribution of tongue tip spines was likely absent in the ancestor shared most recently by charadriiform shorebirds and independently gained twice: once in a common ancestor of calidrid sandpipers, shanks, X. cinereus, and A. interpres, and again in an ancestor of some plovers (e.g., T. melanops). Tongue tip spines were never lost under the equal‐rates model. A strong phylogenetic signal indicates that the trait in descendant lineages is associated with their common ancestor, consistent with the concept of phylogenetic dependency described by Kuwae et al. (2012). The presence of tongue tip spines in additional plover species further supports this inference (Kuwae et al. 2012; Withington 2015). However, the possibility that sister clades share a derived trait cannot be excluded.

The distribution of these two morphological traits across taxa is broadly consistent with the phylogenetic tree derived from multiple gene sequences (Gibson and Baker 2012). Different evolutionary models best describe these traits, reflecting distinct evolutionary dynamics among them and suggesting that species' foraging strategies arise from complex interactions among multiple traits and their environments. The genera Calidris and Tringa have undergone relatively recent and rapid diversification, with most species being small‐ to medium‐sized Arctic migratory birds. Interspecific competition for food resources on the non‐breeding grounds is likely intense, and smaller species may have developed distinct foraging strategies compared with larger migratory birds to facilitate resource partitioning or to cope with variable ecological conditions encountered during migration (Kuwae et al. 2012; Lourenço et al. 2016). This relatively high diversification rate in Scolopacidae was noted by Moreno (1999), who also reported high diversification rates in avocets and stilts (Recurvirostrinae), but lower rates in plovers (Charadriinae).

While this dataset encompassed a broad range of shorebird diversity, it lacked species of medium to large body mass, such as the grey plover (Pluvialis squatarola) and Pacific golden plover (P. fulva). In addition, the present study primarily focused on species with bill‐tip organs from the family Scolopacidae, whereas several branches within the phylogeny of Charadriiformes remain comparatively understudied (Gibson and Baker 2012; Mayr 2011). Sensory pits on the bill tips of shorebirds vary in number, density, and distribution. Although this study proposes potential behavioral associations with these forms, the underlying mechanisms require further investigation. Similarly, the presence and morphology of keratinized tongue tip spines remain to be evaluated in several species, particularly the common greenshank and plovers, and the mechanisms linking these structures to diet and behavior across species remain to be elucidated. This study presents measurement methods, but further standardization and clear definitions of morphological features are required for future studies. For example, sensory pit counts for some species differed between our study and those reported by Massa et al. (2026), requiring independent verification to establish a more comprehensive data set. Furthermore, information on nocturnal foraging remains limited for some species. Although night‐time habitat selection is complex, these knowledge gaps may be addressed using advanced tracking, night‐vision, and acoustic recording technologies. Finally, understanding the association between the evolution of these feeding apparatus traits in shorebirds and ecological changes will require further research in genetics, functional morphology, paleontology, and behavioral ecology.

Conclusion

This study examines the morphological features and evolutionary history of the shorebird feeding apparatus within a phylogenetic framework. Considerable interspecific variation in sensory pit numbers within Scolopacidae was documented and compared with other bill features among species. Associations between micro tongue tip spines, smaller body size, and a reduced distance between the tongue tip and mandible tip support their role in biofilm grazing, as evidenced by SEM images across multiple species, including novel records in the black‐fronted dotterel (Thinornis melanops) and the curlew sandpiper (Calidris ferruginea). Evolutionary analyses suggest that sensory pit numbers diversified gradually and were likely present in high numbers in the most recent common ancestor of Scolopacidae, while micro tongue tip spines evolved independently within the lineages of calidrid sandpipers (Calidris spp.), shanks (Tringa spp.), the terek sandpiper (Xenus cinereus), and the ruddy turnstone (Arenaria interpres), as well as in some plover lineages. The taxonomic distribution of these traits indicates strong phylogenetic dependency and functional adaptations to specific foraging strategies. Together, these findings provide further evidence of the evolutionary diversification and complexity of shorebird feeding apparatus morphology, emphasizing the need for studies examining additional species and the relationship between behavior and morphology. This highlights the importance of biodiversity and habitat conservation for supporting diverse feeding niches.
