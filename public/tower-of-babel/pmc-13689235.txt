Rituximab and corticosteroids for M-protein-negative PGNMID: case report
Authors: Zhuolin Huang, Shuopeng Yang, Jun Cai, Fengying Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe report a case of a 69-year-old patient negative for serum M-protein who was diagnosed with proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits (PGNMID) via renal biopsy.Case presentationA 69-year-old male presented with hematuria, edema, and rapidly progressive renal failure. Serum M-protein was negative, but renal biopsy confirmed IgG3-κ PGNMID with intense C3 deposition and an MPGN-like pattern. Rituximab was initiated, but due to a pulmonary infection, only one of the planned rituximab doses was administered. After infection resolution, the patient’s renal function gradually improved (serum creatinine from 914 to 134 µmol/L) under long-term corticosteroid maintenance. Whether this improvement resulted from the single rituximab dose, prolonged corticosteroids, resolution of sepsis-associated AKI, or natural disease course remains uncertain. This case underscores that PGNMID, though rare, should be considered in seronegative patients with unexplained glomerulonephritis, and highlights the critical role of renal biopsy.ConclusionsPGNMID should be considered in rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, even when M-protein is negative. In this patient, a single reduced dose of rituximab combined with prolonged corticosteroids was associated with a favorable renal outcome, suggesting that a B-cell–directed, clone-directed approach may be a reasonable option worth further evaluation in seronegative PGNMID. Nonetheless, causality cannot be established from a single case, and the relative contributions of the individual therapies remain uncertain. Further studies are needed to define the optimal treatment for this rare entity.

Background

Proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits (PGNMID) is a rare clonally-driven glomerular disease under Monoclonal Gammopathy of Renal Significance (MGRS) [1]. Its pathogenesis involves glomerular deposition of a monoclonal immunoglobulin, leading to proliferation and basement membrane damage. Diagnosis requires a histopathological triad: a membranoproliferative or proliferative glomerulonephritis pattern (with or without membranous features) on light microscopy, monotypic Ig deposition on immunofluorescence, and non-organized granular deposits on electron microscopy. According to the 2025 RPS/IKMG consensus [2], PGNMID is classified under monoclonal immunoglobulin-associated glomerulonephritis with non-organized deposits. Clinically, patients often present with nephrotic syndrome, renal insufficiency, and hematuria. Notably, nearly 70% of patients lack a detectable circulating M-protein by immunofixation, and a clone is identified in only about one-third of cases, underscoring the need for renal biopsy [3]. Treatment lacks large-scale RCT evidence; current consensus has shifted from traditional immunosuppression toward clone-directed therapy [4]. However, challenges remain for seronegative patients regarding clonal origin inference and treatment intensity. This report describes a seronegative PGNMID case with rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, in which only one of two planned rituximab doses was administered, as the second was withheld due to pulmonary infection. The patient was maintained on long-term corticosteroids alone. Sustained renal improvement was observed, though the relative contributions of the single rituximab dose, prolonged corticosteroids, resolution of infection-associated AKI, and natural recovery remain uncertain. The aim is to enhance diagnostic awareness and discuss management challenges.

Case presentation

A 69-year-old male presented with a two-week history of gross hematuria, periorbital and lower limb edema, exertional dyspnea, and progressive oliguria (≈ 300 mL/day). Ten days prior, outside hospital evaluation showed rapidly deteriorating renal function (serum creatinine from 362.8 to 546 µmol/L over 5 days) with proteinuria (4+), hematuria (5856 RBCs/µL), and occult blood (3+). Supportive therapy was ineffective. Past history was unremarkable except for a 30 pack-year smoking history. On admission, blood pressure was 162/100 mmHg, BMI 23.3 kg/m², with periorbital and bilateral lower extremity pitting edema.

Admission laboratory data are shown in Table 1. Key findings: serum creatinine 550 µmol/L, nephrotic syndrome (proteinuria 10,277 mg/24 h with hypoalbuminemia). Autoimmune and viral serologies were negative; complement levels normal. Serum and urine immunofixation electrophoresis detected no monoclonal immunoglobulins. Renal ultrasound showed mildly enlarged kidneys.

Upon admission, symptomatic supportive therapy included blood pressure control, fluid intake restriction, and urine alkalization. On day 3, a right femoral venous catheter was placed and intermittent hemodialysis initiated. On days 5–6, chills and rigors during dialysis suggested catheter-related infection; the session was terminated, the catheter removed, and piperacillin/tazobactam 2.25 g q12h started.

Renal biopsy (Fig. 1) met the 2025 RPS/IKMG consensus criteria for PGNMID [2]. Light microscopy (8 glomeruli) showed an MPGN pattern with endocapillary hypercellularity and mesangial expansion, double contours, with global glomerulosclerosis in 2/8 and crescents in 4/8 glomeruli (3 large cellular and 1 small cellular). Immunofluorescence revealed intense granular C3 (3+) along capillary loops and mesangium, with monotypic IgG3 (2+) and kappa (2+) restriction; IgG1, IgG2, IgG4, and lambda were negative. C3 glomerulopathy was excluded because C3 staining did not exceed IgG3 by ≥ 2 orders of magnitude. IgA, IgM, C1q, fibrinogen, PLA2R, THSD7A, and AA amyloid were negative/trace. Electron microscopy confirmed non-organized granular electron-dense deposits in subendothelial and mesangial regions. Although serum cryoglobulins were not assessed, the absence of wire-loop structures, microthrombi, and organized substructures on light and electron microscopy argued against cryoglobulinemic glomerulonephritis. On immunofluorescence and electron microscopy, no immune-type deposits were identified along the tubular basement membranes or in the vessel walls (extraglomerular deposits absent), further supporting the diagnosis of PGNMID.

Serological and hematologic evaluation for a clonal disease showed negative serum/urine immunofixation, and a normal free light chain ratio despite elevated FLC levels. Bone marrow aspirate/biopsy revealed 2% plasma cells with normal morphology, normocellular marrow (≈ 40%), no ALIP or hotspots (Fig. 2). Multiparameter flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry (CD38, CD138, CD20, CD56, etc.) showed no evidence of plasma cell dyscrasia, lymphoma, or leukemia; Congo red negative. Manual plasma cell enumeration confirmed a polyclonal κ:λ ratio of 23:27. Overall, no overt clonal proliferation or hematologic malignancy was found. The patient then received intravenous methylprednisolone 40 mg/day for 4 days, followed by rituximab 500 mg on day 5, transitioning to oral methylprednisolone 20 mg/day. A single, reduced 500 mg dose of rituximab—rather than a standard regimen (375 mg/m² weekly for four doses, or 1 g on days 1 and 15)—was deliberately chosen to limit the immunosuppressive and infectious burden in this frail, elderly patient at high infection risk. A second 500 mg dose was planned two weeks later but was withheld owing to the pulmonary infection. At discharge, serum creatinine was 473 µmol/L and urine output 1,900 mL/day.

Following discharge from the initial hospitalization, the patient was maintained on oral methylprednisolone (20 mg/day) but did not undergo regular monitoring of serum creatinine or urine output. Two weeks later, he was readmitted for the planned second course of rituximab. At readmission, the patient still had hematuria and reported a decreased urine output (approximately 800 mL/day). Laboratory evaluation revealed a markedly elevated serum creatinine of 914 µmol/L compared with pre-discharge values, while the CD4 + count was mildly elevated at 1712 cells/µL (Laboratory findings are shown in Table 1). The acute deterioration in renal function was considered most likely attributable to the cessation of regular hemodialysis following previous discharge. A repeat kidney biopsy was recommended to clarify the etiology of the acute renal injury; however, the patient declined the procedure. On hospital day 10, the patient developed exertional dyspnea and cough without hemoptysis. Chest CT revealed pulmonary infiltrates consistent with infection. Blood cultures were negative, while sputum smears identified Gram-positive cocci (++) and Gram-positive bacilli (++). Respiratory viral PCR panel was negative. A diagnosis of bacterial pneumonia was established, and the planned rituximab infusion was consequently withheld. Methylprednisolone was simultaneously discontinued, and empiric antimicrobial therapy with piperacillin/tazobactam (2.25 g q12h) was initiated along with temporary central venous catheter placement for intermittent hemodialysis. By day 14 of antibiotic therapy, the patient’s dyspnea and cough had improved. Follow-up chest CT demonstrated significant resolution of the pulmonary infiltrates (Fig. 3). Serum creatinine decreased to 298 µmol/L, and urine output recovered to 1,900 mL/day. Given the improvement in both infection and renal function, hemodialysis was discontinued, and methylprednisolone was restarted at 16 mg/day. The patient was discharged on hospital day 37 with a serum creatinine of 360 µmol/L and a urine output of 1,200 mL/day. He was instructed to attend regular outpatient follow-up.

Following discharge, the patient was maintained on methylprednisolone monotherapy (16 mg/day) for approximately five months, at which point serum creatinine had decreased to 190 µmol/L, and the dose was reduced to 8 mg/day. Two months later, it was further tapered to 4 mg/day. At nine months post-discharge, serum creatinine had significantly decreased to 134 µmol/L. Urinalysis revealed trace hematuria and proteinuria (red blood cells 22/µL, protein 1+). Given the significant and stable recovery of renal function, corticosteroid therapy was discontinued.

Discussion and conclusions

This case of RPGN with IgG3-κ PGNMID serves as a reminder that this rare condition may present to any clinician. Three aspects merit discussion: (1) diagnostic challenges of seronegative PGNMID with an occult clone; (2) treatment rationale and complex factors underlying renal recovery; and (3) importance of infection prophylaxis and immunological monitoring during clone-directed therapy.

Diagnostic considerations

Monoclonal Gammopathy of Renal Significance (MGRS) is defined as a clonal lymphoproliferative disorder that produces a nephrotoxic monoclonal protein but does not meet criteria for treatment of a specific malignancy [5, 6].

Our case fulfilled the 2025 RPS/IKMG diagnostic criteria [2]: MPGN with crescents on LM, monotypic IgG3-κ restriction on IF, and non-organized granular deposits on EM. IgG3 restriction is particularly significant, as its potent activation of the classical complement pathway explains the intense C3 deposition and aggressive crescentic lesions observed.

Several differential diagnoses were carefully excluded. Infection-related and immune-complex glomerulonephritis were considered unlikely given the monotypic IgG3-κ restriction (rather than polyclonal staining) and the absence of subepithelial “hump-type” deposits. Lupus and other autoimmune nephritides were not supported by negative autoantibodies, normal complement, and the absence of a “full-house” immunofluorescence pattern. Membranous nephropathy was excluded by negative PLA2R and THSD7A staining. Amyloidosis and other monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition diseases were excluded by negative Congo red staining and the absence of linear tubular basement membrane or vascular deposits on immunofluorescence and electron microscopy.

Hematologic evaluation is critical for clone identification, but PGNMID frequently lacks detectable M-protein—up to 70% by immunofixation [3, 7, 8]. Our patient had negative serum/urine IFE. Although free light chain (FLC) levels were elevated, the FLC ratio was normal (1.05). In severe renal failure (eGFR 8.65 mL/min), impaired clearance of both κ and λ chains can normalize the ratio even when a clone exists. A dedicated renal reference range (0.37–3.1) has been proposed [11]; our patient’s ratio fell within this range, yet a cryptic clone cannot be excluded [6]. An alternative explanation is that pathogenic IgG3 is rapidly cleared and deposited in glomeruli, evading serologic detection [3]. High clinical suspicion thus mandated renal biopsy, which proved indispensable.

Pathogenesis of IgG3-κ PGNMID

PGNMID pathogenesis involves direct glomerular deposition of monoclonal immunoglobulins rather than classic immune complexes. The unique physicochemical properties of the M-protein—including protein misfolding and direct tissue binding—trigger local inflammatory responses leading to glomerular hypercellularity and proliferative glomerulonephritis [9]. IgG3 is overrepresented in PGNMID (65.6% of subtyped cases in Nasr [3]). This is attributable to its large molecular weight, strong positive charge, tendency for self-aggregation, and its superior capacity among IgG subclasses to activate the classical complement pathway, thereby recruiting neutrophils and driving proliferative lesions [3, 10]. Our biopsy findings (IgG3, C3, neutrophilic infiltration) align with this mechanism. IgG3-predominant PGNMID is associated with more aggressive histology (MPGN, crescents), warranting long-term follow-up despite favorable initial response [3, 10].

Additionally, PGNMID may not always arise from an autonomous tumorous clone; some cases [12] may be driven by external antigens (e.g., viral infections, solid tumors) that stimulate a restricted B-cell response.

Treatment rationale and interpretation of renal recovery

PGNMID treatment is not standardized but centers on clone-directed therapy [13, 14]. In serologically positive patients, IgM-associated disease (typically of B-cell or lymphoplasmacytic lineage) is generally treated with rituximab, whereas IgG/IgA types (typically of plasma cell lineage) are generally treated with plasma cell-directed agents such as bortezomib [15, 16]. In clone-negative cases such as ours, however, there is currently no consensus on whether clone-directed therapy is mandatory, or on whether therapy directed against B cells or against plasma cells should be preferred. However, the absence of a demonstrable plasma cell clone does not establish a B-cell origin: monoclonal IgG is most often produced by CD20-negative plasma cells, which are not directly targeted by anti-CD20 therapy. Rituximab plus corticosteroids was therefore chosen on pragmatic rather than definitively mechanistic grounds, as it has the largest published experience in PGNMID, can deplete the upstream CD20-positive B-cell pool that replenishes short-lived plasma cells, and was judged more tolerable than bortezomib in a frail patient with severe renal failure and high infection risk. We therefore elected rituximab plus corticosteroids, while recognizing that a hidden CD20 + B-cell clone was inferred rather than proven. Per the 2025 RPS/IKMG consensus [2], clone-directed therapy is preferred over traditional immunosuppression for MGRS-related PGNMID, and early use of rituximab was considered a reasonable empiric option given the aggressive MPGN with crescents. Nonetheless, the non-standard single low dose, together with the absence of post-treatment CD19+/CD20 + B-cell data and of quantitative proteinuria at remission, means that the biological and clinical effect of rituximab cannot be assessed in this case.

The post-treatment course was complex. After a single rituximab dose and corticosteroids, the patient initially improved. However, at readmission two weeks later for planned second rituximab, serum creatinine had risen to 914 µmol/L—most likely due to cessation of regular hemodialysis and lack of monitoring, though disease progression could not be excluded. A subsequent pulmonary infection may have further impaired renal function. Renal recovery after infection control and hemodialysis reinstitution suggested a significant role of reversible factors, but without a repeat biopsy, the relative contributions of disease activity versus superimposed insults remain uncertain.

Encouragingly, after nine months of gradual corticosteroid taper, serum creatinine declined to 134 µmol/L. However, this favorable outcome must be interpreted cautiously, as it likely reflects a combination of factors: resolution of sepsis-associated AKI, prolonged anti-inflammatory effect of corticosteroids, natural repair, and an unquantifiable contribution from the single rituximab dose. Their relative contributions cannot be determined from a single case. This does not advocate shortening standard clone-directed therapy; the outcome may reflect PGNMID heterogeneity rather than a generalizable treatment effect. Indeed, published cases report variable outcomes—remission with conservative management (Ji et al. [7]), superior results with clone-directed therapy (Gumber et al. [8]), and even spontaneous remission (van Kruijsdijk et al. [17]). These observations underscore the need for individualized treatment balancing therapeutic intensity against infection risks, especially in elderly comorbid patients.

Infection prophylaxis and immunological monitoring

Despite the favorable renal outcome, the infectious complication that altered therapy warrants reflection. Rituximab plus corticosteroids increases opportunistic infection risk, particularly Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP). KDIGO 2021 (Practice Point 1.8.4) recommends TMP-SMX prophylaxis in patients receiving such agents, especially with advanced age, renal impairment, or lymphopenia [18]. In this case, PJP prophylaxis was not given, and a severe pulmonary infection (Gram-positive pathogens) developed, forcing discontinuation of further rituximab. Although not PJP, the absence of prophylaxis left the patient vulnerable. Serial monitoring of CD19+/CD20 + B-cell counts, immunoglobulins, and CD4 + counts should be incorporated to guide therapy and identify high-risk patients. Future guidelines should address whether standardized infection prophylaxis and immunological monitoring improve safety in PGNMID and related MGRS entities.

Clinical learning points

Four key learning points emerge. First, seronegative PGNMID requires renal biopsy with IgG subclass and light chain restriction. Second, in severe renal failure, interpret the FLC ratio using the renal reference range (0.37–3.1); a normal ratio does not exclude a pathogenic clone, and advanced techniques (MASS-FIX, NGS) should be considered if conventional testing is negative. Third, treatment must be individualized, balancing clone-directed therapy intensity against infection risk, especially in elderly or immunocompromised patients. Fourth, per KDIGO 2021, PJP prophylaxis (TMP-SMX) should be considered when rituximab and corticosteroids are used together, and serial monitoring of CD19+/CD20 + B-cell counts and immunoglobulins is recommended to guide therapy and identify high-risk patients.

Limitations

This single case report has several limitations. First, the favorable outcome may not be generalizable. Second, the pulmonary infection confounds attribution of renal deterioration to disease progression, infection-related AKI, or hemodialysis cessation. Third, lack of post-treatment B-cell monitoring (CD19+/CD20+) precludes assessment of rituximab’s efficacy; renal recovery cannot be confidently attributed to rituximab over steroids or AKI resolution. Fourth, advanced molecular techniques (MASS-FIX, NGS) were not performed, leaving a cryptic clone possible. Fifth, “clinical remission” relies on serum creatinine and qualitative urinalysis only; no repeat biopsy, quantitative proteinuria, or serial albumin were obtained. Future studies should include serial B-cell monitoring (e.g., at 2, 4, 12, 24 weeks), quantitative proteinuria, and standardized infection prophylaxis.
