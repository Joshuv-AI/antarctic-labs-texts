Reservoir-enhanced segment anything model for subsurface diagnosis
Authors: Xiren Zhou, Shikang Liu, Xinyu Yan, Yizhan Fan, Xiangyu Wang, Yu Kang, Jian Cheng, Huanhuan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUrban roads and infrastructure, vital to city operations, face growing threats from subsurface anomalies like cracks and cavities. Ground Penetrating Radar effectively visualizes underground conditions using electromagnetic waves; however, accurate anomaly detection through this method remains challenging due to limited labeled data, varying subsurface conditions, and indistinct target boundaries. Although visually image-like, radar cross-sectional data fundamentally represent electromagnetic waves, with variations within and between waves critical for identifying anomalies. Addressing these, we propose the Reservoir-enhanced Segment Anything Model, a framework exploiting both visual discernibility and wave-changing properties of radar data. The model initially identifies visually apparent candidate anomaly regions and further refines them by analyzing anomaly-induced changes within and between electromagnetic waves in local radar scans, enabling precise and complete anomaly region extraction and category determination. Real-world experiments demonstrate that the model achieves high detection accuracy ( > 85%) and outperforms existing methods. Notably, it requires only minimal accessible non-target data, avoids intensive training, and supports both fully automatic operation and simple human interaction to enhance reliability. Our research provides a scalable, resource-efficient solution for rapid subsurface anomaly detection across diverse environments, improving urban safety monitoring while reducing manual effort and computational cost.Subject terms: Civil engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering

Introduction

As urbanization accelerates globally, rapid expansion of critical infrastructure increasingly encounters threats from subsurface hazards, such as cavities, soil loosening, and structural voids1. These underground anomalies pose significant risks, potentially leading to severe subsidence that endangers roads, buildings, and public safety2. In China alone, incomplete statistics indicate that over 1000 underground disasters occur annually, ranging from minor ground settlements to catastrophic collapses, which result in substantial economic losses and risks to human life3. Detecting and locating these hidden anomalies reliably and efficiently thus becomes critical.

Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR) has emerged as a crucial non-invasive geophysical tool for detecting underground facilities and anomalies4,5. As illustrated in Fig. 1, GPR emits high-frequency electromagnetic (EM) waves into the ground and captures their reflections6. The received signals along a measuring line are integrated into an image called B-scan. Interpreting B-scans allows us to infer the presence or absence of any potential subsurface targets. Nevertheless, with expanding detection, underground diagnosis requires precise identification and localization of anomaly regions in B-scan data, a laborious and time-consuming process when performed manually7,8.

Acquisition and construction of GPR B-scan data.

GPR refers to Ground Penetrating Radar, and EM waves denote electromagnetic waves. The antenna unit moves along the measuring line, emitting EM waves into the ground and receiving reflections from the subsurface. At each position, a waveform is recorded and converted into gray values representing wave amplitude. Stacking the gray values column by column yields the GPR B-scan data (shortened as B-scan), in which the horizontal axis follows the measuring line and the vertical axis represents the depth-related time window.

Traditional image or signal feature extraction methods assist in GPR B-scan data segmenting and categorizing9. Some studies follow a physics-informed pathway that integrates computational electromagnetics with sparsity-driven representations to extract discriminative scattering signatures from forward models10. Others refine hand-crafted image descriptors, such as Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG), by introducing phase- and shape-based features to detect hyperbola under noisy conditions11. Further developments emphasize multi-source feature fusion with supervised learning, leveraging diverse attributes and ensemble classifiers to strengthen target recognition and robustness12. Despite these advances, such approaches could struggle with the variability in subsurface media and anomalies due to differences in composition, size, and surrounding environment13.

Shifting from image processing, Deep Learning (DL) techniques, primarily Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have also been utilized to identify objects in GPR B-scan data14–16. Object detection frameworks, such as Faster R-CNN and YOLO variants, are trained on diverse types of B-scans to localize anomalies17,18. Some Transformer-based methods emphasize multi-branch frequency-scale extraction and shape-aware optimization to strengthen target detection19. Generative adversarial networks have also been explored for data augmentation20, as well as for direct target feature segmentation and shape delineation21. Nevertheless, DL approaches still face practical challenges. Firstly, there is a notable scarcity of multi-type labeled anomaly data, resulting in initial datasets heavily skewed toward abundant non-target samples (i.e., normal subsurface conditions without anomalies such as cavities or looseness). Secondly, the variability in underground conditions restricts the generalization capabilities of DL models, limiting their adaptability across diverse detection environments. Thirdly, the complexity and extensive parameterization of deep neural networks demand substantial computational resources, complicating efficient optimization when data availability is limited22. Apart from the above, end-to-end DL methods indiscriminately process large volumes of normal data prevalent in practical GPR surveys, thereby consuming excessive computational resources and processing time. The absence of efficient human intervention may also reduce the reliability and interpretability of anomaly detection outcomes.

Recent advances in foundational vision models, such as the Segment Anything Model (SAM)23, have shown potential in anomaly segmentation, eliminating the need for additional data collection or training for specific tasks. SAM segments visual objects supported with minimal and user-friendly manual prompts, suggesting promise for detecting visually discernible underground anomalies. It reduces manual effort while improving result reliability through human intervention24–27. Nonetheless, directly applying SAM in GPR B-scan data is hindered by the modality gap between optical images and EM waves. Despite being image-like as Fig. 1, B-scan data essentially represent EM waves. Although visually distinguishable, local variations caused by subsurface anomalies are expressed as dual-directional dynamic changes, both within and between these waves, manifesting as gradual waveform alterations rather than distinct object boundaries28. Consequently, SAM is prone to over-segmentation or incomplete anomaly delineation, underscoring a key challenge: the dual-directional changing information in B-scan data remains underutilized in existing frameworks29.

To address these challenges, we propose the Reservoir-enhanced Segment Anything Model (Res-SAM), a framework that combines reservoir computing with well-trained segmentation techniques to accurately identify and categorize anomalies in B-scan data. Res-SAM integrates visual discernibility of anomalies with the subtle changes within and between EM waves inherent in local B-scan data. It localizes candidate anomaly regions either with minimal user click prompts or in a fully automatic manner, where non-target areas are discarded and the remaining regions are retained. These candidate regions are then refined by analyzing waveform-changing information in local B-scan data via a specifically designed reservoir computing approach, enabling precise and complete anomaly extraction and categorization, with manageable computational cost. Reducing dependence on extensive labeled data, Res-SAM operates effectively using minimal, easily accessible non-target samples and avoids resource-intensive iterative training. Moreover, it incorporates intuitive human interaction in the click-guided mode, while also offering a hands-off solution in the fully automatic mode, enhancing practical reliability and interpretability. Experiments demonstrate that Res-SAM substantially outperforms existing approaches, highlighting its potential as a scalable, resource-efficient solution for rapid, accurate subsurface anomaly detection across diverse urban environments.

Results

Framework overview

Detecting underground anomalies using GPR faces practical challenges. First, anomaly involves unknowns, thus, there is a notable shortage of labeled anomaly data. Acquiring anomaly samples is costly, resulting in highly imbalanced datasets dominated by normal (non-target) samples. This imbalance severely restricts the training process of data-driven approaches. Second, the high variability of subsurface environments, including diverse soils and structures, leads to clear differences in EM wave characteristics. Such variability impedes the generalization capability, causing approaches trained on one scenario to likely perform poorly in others. Third, underground anomalies typically present as gradual changes in EM wave reflections rather than sharp, distinct boundaries. This gradual transition complicates precise anomaly localization, making conventional methods designed for visual images inadequate for wholly and accurately delineating anomaly boundaries in real-world applications.

The proposed Res-SAM leverages minimal non-target training data, readily obtainable from the detection area, and avoids computationally intensive training, enabling rapid deployment in unexplored environments. It synergizes visual discernibility with local dynamic variations in GPR B-scan data caused by underground anomalies. Recognizing that practical subsurface anomaly detection necessitates clearly delineating entire affected regions, Res-SAM provides rectangular outlines of anomalies along with their categorization. As depicted in Fig. 2, Res-SAM operates in two main phases: Feature Collection and Anomaly Detection.

Overview of the proposed Res-SAM framework.

Res-SAM refers to the proposed Reservoir-enhanced Segment Anything Model framework, and 2D-ESN refers to the Dual-Directional Echo State Network. Res-SAM includes two phases: (a) Feature Collection and (b) Anomaly Detection. In the Feature Collection phase, normal data patches are extracted from non-target B-scan data and fitted using 2D-ESN, with the resulting dynamic features stored in a feature bank. In the Anomaly Detection phase, SAM first identifies candidate anomaly regions. Local patches around each point in these regions are fitted using 2D-ESN, producing features that are compared with the feature bank, with anomaly-related patches identified and further merged into the final anomaly region.

In the Feature Collection phase, local patches are extracted from non-target B-scan data and fitted individually using the Dual-Directional Echo State Network (2D-ESN). Acknowledging the vertical continuity of EM waves and horizontal correlations due to the consistency of the subsurface medium, effective changing information exists both vertically and horizontally within B-scan data. 2D-ESN, a Reservoir Computing Network that incorporates two reservoirs in its hidden layer, connects each data point within the patch to its neighbors in both vertical and horizontal directions. Fitting each patch with 2D-ESN adequately captures the local data-inherent dual-directional changing information, resulting in a compact fitted readout model serving as dual-directional dynamic features or simply features of the original patch. These features, derived from patches on non-target data and denoted as normal features, collectively represent local changing information in non-target data and are stored in a Feature Bank.

In the Anomaly Detection phase, SAM is introduced to identify initial candidate anomaly regions, either guided by simple click prompts (typically single-digit number left- and right-mouse clicks) in the click-guided mode; or by segmenting the entire B-scan into coarse regions in the fully automatic mode, where each region is fitted via 2D-ESN with extracted dynamic feature compared against the feature bank to discard normal parts before further refinement. Anomalies in B-scan data often present indistinct boundaries due to EM wave reflection, diffraction, and attenuation, necessitating further refinement. To this end, we focus on local waveform-changing information within B-scan data caused by underground anomalies, aiming to precisely adjust the candidate region. Specifically, for each point within the candidate region, we extract a local patch centered on this point and fit this patch using 2D-ESN. The obtained dynamic features are compared with the normal features in the feature bank. Patches exhibiting clearly different dynamics (high anomaly likelihood) indicate anomalies; overlapping anomaly patches are then merged to precisely delineate anomaly regions. Owing to the similar changing information in B-scan data, similar subsurface structures result in comparable dynamic features, while those from different structures exhibit distinct variances, reflecting their distinct changing information along and among EM waves. Therefore, the confirmed anomaly regions undergo another round of 2D-ESN fitting to derive category-specific dynamic features, enabling anomaly categorization.

Dataset acquisition

The utilized GPR B-scan datasets include:

Real-world dataset: The dataset was collected using a GSSI GPR system with a 200MHz antenna over cement and asphalt road surfaces. Raw B-scan data underwent basic preprocessing, including removal of surface reflections, median filtering for noise suppression, and gain compensation for attenuation. The data were segmented into frames of two sizes (369 × 369 and 615 × 369 pixels), both with a fixed 70ns time window corresponding to about 4–6 m depth, while the two frame widths cover about 15 m and 25 m along the measuring line, respectively. The balanced dataset comprises 600 samples evenly distributed across six categories, with 100 samples per category: five anomaly types (cavities, cracks, looseness, manholes, and pipelines) and one non-target category.

Synthetic dataset: We constructed a 15 m (measuring line) × 6 m (depth) synthetic subsurface with soil-embedded targets, including cavities, cracks, and metal pipelines with varying sizes. A total of 450 B-scans (150 per anomaly type) were generated using gprMax31, a forward GPR solver that models dielectric permittivity and conductivity distributions and solves Maxwell’s equations using the finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method. Anomaly Region Identification

In subsurface anomaly detection, the primary step is to identify and localize anomalies in collected B-scan data, typically using a small set of non-target B-scans for initialization. We first validated Res-SAM on this task: for the real-world and open-source datasets, 20 non-target B-scans from each dataset were used, while for the synthetic dataset, 20 non-target samples (10 from each of the other two datasets) were selected. These initialization B-scans were used to build the feature bank, against which anomalies were detected in the remaining B-scans of each dataset. All validation experiments were conducted on a workstation with an Intel Core i7-14700KF CPU, 128 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090D GPU.

We start by validating the click-guided mode, where human interaction is involved. During the detection phase, click prompts are implemented in each B-scan data sample, where click prompts within the anomaly region serve as positive prompts (left-mouse click), and those outside are defined as negative prompts (right-mouse click). Figure 3 provides representative B-scan samples and corresponding anomaly detection results, where green and red dots represent positive and negative click prompts, respectively. The blue and red boxes indicate anomaly regions detected by SAM and Res-SAM. Notably, SAM tends to segment overly large or incomplete regions containing or intersecting with anomalies, but fails to provide precise and complete areas. In contrast, Res-SAM refines SAM’s results and provides a heatmap of anomaly likelihood, enabling accurate anomaly and boundary localization.

According to the outcomes and visual evidence, Res-SAM consistently outperforms competing methods across all three datasets and different prompt settings, achieving more precise and comprehensive anomaly identification. As represented by the real-world dataset, it attains an Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) of 0.888 and an F1-score of 92.8% in the 5/5 prompt settings. Even in the most challenging 3/1 case, Res-SAM maintains an AUC of 0.869 and an F1-score of 91.9%, highlighting strong generalization. This can be attributed to its ability to exploit both the visual discernibility and wave-collection properties of GPR B-scan data. In contrast, purely visual-based methods find anomalies with prompts but fail to extract the regions comprehensively and accurately, leading to substantial drops in AUC and F1 scores. Among these methods, SimpleClick performs best in the 5/3 setting with an AUC of 0.793 and an F1-score of 87.0%. However, as prompts decrease to 3/1, it suffers a sharp decline to 0.611 AUC and 72.8% F1-score. Similar performance drops occur in other competing methods, indicating the limitations of interactive segmentation under minimal prompt scenarios, whereas Res-SAM consistently excels. Comparable superiority is also observed on the open-source and synthetic datasets. In this context, our approach leverages both SAM to approximate anomaly locations and 2D-ESN to refine detection by assessing local changing information in B-scan data. Adequately capturing local dual-directional changing information via 2D-ESN fitting, features derived from abnormal patches clearly differ from the established feature bank, resulting in a substantially higher anomaly likelihood (evident as the heatmap in Fig. 3) and enabling precise refinement of anomaly regions.

We also experimented with the fully automatic mode (i.e., without any clicks). Existing studies on B-scan data do not address automatic anomaly detection on newly collected B-scans when only limited normal data are available. We assess Res-SAM against three types of state-of-the-art baselines that are capable of this task, despite not being specially designed for B-scan data, including: (i) Anomaly-synthesizing techniques: SimpleNet36 and Dual Subspace Re-projection (DSR)37. (ii) Data-reconstruction approaches: Adversarially Learned One-Class Classifier (ALOCC)38 and Interpolated Gaussian descriptor (IGD)39. (iii) Local feature comparison methods: PatchCore40 and Anomaly Detection using Position and Neighborhood Information (PNI)41.

Ablation study on the role of SAM

Our framework incorporates SAM as an initial anomaly detector that leverages the visual discernibility of subsurface targets on B-scans to filter out normal areas and propose compact candidate abnormal regions, reducing the search space for subsequent dynamic-feature analysis via 2D-ESN. To verify its effectiveness, an ablation comparison is conducted between Res-SAM and its SAM-ablated variant (w/o SAM) using anomalous B-scans. The SAM-ablated variant performs full-scanning on each B-scan, where 2D-ESN is applied to fit every patch for dynamic-feature extraction and subsequent analysis. On real-world, synthetic, and open-source datasets, the mean number of 2D-ESN fittings per sample of Res-SAM and this variant is compared to evaluate the computational savings brought by SAM.

As summarized in Fig. 5, incorporating SAM substantially reduces computational cost. In click-guided mode, under different prompt configurations, Res-SAM achieves a 47.9% -66.7% reduction in the mean 2D-ESN fitting counts against the w/o SAM variant, while even in fully automatic mode, the reduction remains 23.1% -27.3%. Combined with Fig. 3, SAM effectively removes a considerable portion of non-target normal parts, retaining a compact candidate abnormal region (blue box) and enabling it to be refined into a precision detection region (red box) with less 2D-ESN fitting. Notably, these results are obtained by detecting anomaly-included B-scans. For non-target B-scans, incorporating SAM quickly rules out anomalies, reducing full-scan 2D-ESN fittings to single digits: in fully automatic mode, only the few regions proposed by SAM need to be fitted for dynamic-feature analysis, while in click-guided mode, normal samples could be directly discarded without any 2D-ESN fitting. In both modes, the savings achieved by SAM are considerable. Overall, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of SAM and our key innovation of combining the visual discernibility and wave-changing properties of B-scans.

Normal data requirements

In Res-SAM, the normal data are collected from the same area under detection, or at most from areas with identical subsurface conditions to that area, ensuring that the constructed feature bank accurately reflects the local normal (non-target) waveform dynamics. To evaluate how the selection of normal B-scan data influences Res-SAM’s performance, we conduct a cross-environment experiment, in which normal data from different sources, i.e., the datasets, are used to derive feature banks, respectively, and are then paired with varying target datasets for anomaly detection. Specifically, three types of sources are used, including: normal samples from the real-world dataset; normal samples from the open-source dataset; and normal samples from each of the two datasets, denoted as the combined source. The real-world, open-source, and synthetic datasets are utilized for anomaly detection. Thus, all 3 normal data sources are cross-paired with the 3 target datasets, resulting in 9 conditions that can be divided into: Environment-consistent, the normal data are from the same dataset to be detected; Environment-mismatched, the normal data are not from the target dataset; Partially matched, some normal samples from the target dataset, but some are not.

From the results in Fig. 6, the environment-consistent condition (diagonal cases) yields the highest AUC. Conversely, in environment-mismatched cases, for instance, when using normal samples from the real-world dataset to detect anomalies in the open-source dataset, or vice versa, the AUC drops substantially by 0.112-0.161. This degradation arises since, under mismatched conditions, the feature bank’s stored dynamic features no longer validly represent the local waveform dynamics of target-free B-scan data in that environment; consequently, the distances computed between the compared features and the bank fail to reflect the reliable anomaly likelihood of the corresponding patches. On the other hand, when normal samples are partially matched, i.e., some from the same environment to be detected and some not (combined source), the AUC slightly decreases by 0.004-0.014, indicating that when encountering a relatively diverse and dynamic area, the normal data should also comprehensively reflect those on-site subsurface environments (i.e., normal subsurface conditions without any targets). Similarly, for the synthetic dataset, since its sample background comes from both real-world and open-source datasets, the combined source case performs considerably, while performance degrades under other conditions. These results highlight the importance of environmental consistency in selecting normal samples for Res-SAM.

Feature clustering for anomaly discrimination

Res-SAM employs 2D-ESN to capture dual-directional dynamics in B-scan data, incorporating two reservoirs to model the changing information in both the horizontal and vertical directions, thereby extracting dynamic features that reflect trace-to-trace continuity as well as variations along EM waves. Consequently, B-scans generated by similar subsurface structures yield consistent dynamic features, whereas those from different structures produce clearly distinguishable ones. The extracted features are subsequently grouped using clusters to categorize anomaly types in a one-shot process, avoiding offline iterative training. To evaluate the effectiveness of 2D-ESN-based anomaly clustering, we extract three categories of features for clustering comparison: (i) dynamic features obtained from 2D-ESN; (ii) statistical attribute features, comprising a set of amplitude-based statistics (mean, variance, and other common time-domain descriptors) denoted as Amplitude, a group of spectral statistics (spectral centroid, bandwidth, entropy, and frequency-domain energy) denoted as Spectral, and image texture features such as Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG)42 and Local Binary Pattern (LBP)43; and (iii) features extracted by deep-learning models, including VGG44, ResNet45, Inception46, and Vision Transformer (ViT)47 pre-trained on the ImageNet48 dataset without fine-tuning. These features are processed using three widely adopted clustering algorithms: K-Means49, Agglomerative Clustering (AC)50, and Fuzzy C-Means (FCM)51. The results demonstrate that, owing to 2D-ESN’s effective capture of dual-directional dynamics within B-scan data, Res-SAM excels in anomaly clustering and outperforms other feature extraction methods across all three clusters. Specifically, Res-SAM combined with Agglomerative Clustering achieves the best metrics, with an accuracy of 0.90, an Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) of 0.90, and a Normalized Mutual Info (NMI) of 0.88, indicating its ability to categorize most underground anomalies correctly. In contrast, traditional statistical attributes achieve at most 0.68 in any clustering metric, while pre-trained deep learning models fail to exceed 0.8. Visually, features derived from Res-SAM exhibit an obvious category separation effect in the projected space, whereas other methods show weaker or even chaotic distributions. This difference arises because the subsurface environment is inherently complex and non-uniform, with variations in layering, moisture content, and anomaly characteristics producing non-stationary waveform changes that statistical attributes struggle to capture. In addition, due to the existence of underground anomalies, distinctive dynamics of B-scan data arise from variations among and along the collected EM waves. Pre-trained DL networks optimized for visual features struggle to capture these characteristics effectively. Moreover, constructing diverse and accurately labeled training datasets for a specific target area is often impractical. Res-SAM addresses this challenge by focusing on data-inherent dynamics. By establishing connections in both vertical and horizontal directions within B-scan data, it comprehensively captures dual-directional dynamics, resulting in category-discriminative features that enhance anomaly clustering accuracy.

Discussion

With rapid urbanization and infrastructure expansion, subsurface hazards such as cavities, cracks, and structural voids increasingly pose severe risks to urban safety. Effective detection and accurate classification of these hidden anomalies are critical to preventing accidents and ensuring infrastructure stability. GPR has become a widely adopted non-destructive method for this purpose. However, efficient and reliable anomaly detection using GPR still faces several key challenges. First, labeled anomaly samples are scarce, making datasets highly imbalanced and dominated by non-target samples. Second, the high variability of subsurface environments results in substantial differences in electromagnetic wave characteristics, limiting the generalization ability of existing methods. Third, underground anomalies typically appear as gradual in EM wave reflections rather than distinct boundaries, complicating precise and complete localization via visual-based approaches.

This study introduces the Reservoir-enhanced Segment Anything Model (Res-SAM) framework, designed for efficient and precise detection and classification of subsurface anomalies in GPR B-scan data. Compared to conventional methods, Res-SAM combines the visual discernibility of target anomaly with its local wave changing information, providing complete and accurate anomaly regions. Importantly, Res-SAM supports both a click-guided mode and a fully automatic mode: in the former, minimal human click prompts substantially reduce manual labor while enhancing detection reliability; in the latter, candidate regions are generated automatically without user input, avoiding unnecessary operations on a large volume of easily distinguishable normal data. By capturing and distinguishing dynamic variations within and between EM waves in local B-scan data, Res-SAM further refines the identification, substantially improving the completeness and accuracy of output anomaly region. Experimental results clearly demonstrate that Res-SAM consistently outperforms existing state-of-the-art methods. In addition, Res-SAM maintains stability and accuracy even under minimal interactive prompts, attributed to its effective utilization of intrinsic dynamic changes within local anomaly-associated B-scan data. Furthermore, this study highlights the strong generalization capabilities of Res-SAM, which requires only minimal non-target B-scan data for initialization, facilitating rapid deployment in unexplored areas and effectively reducing development and application cycles. This adaptability underscores its potential practical value, particularly for urban subsurface infrastructure safety monitoring.

Our research also acknowledges certain limitations worth addressing in future studies. Specifically, while Res-SAM greatly reduces manual intervention, verifying anomaly detection results remains challenging. For anomalies not initially indicated by human prompts, it is essential to implement rapid yet comprehensive background verification processes that complement Res-SAM results. Efficient methods to automate this validation, potentially running in parallel or in the background, would be highly beneficial for practical deployment. Additionally, future work would also explore multi-source data fusion or joint diagnostics using additional sensor data that would further enhance the reliability and accuracy of subsurface anomaly detection.

Overall, the application results of Res-SAM in the field of subsurface anomaly detection are highly encouraging. It supports both human-in-the-loop and fully automatic modes, effectively reduces labeling costs and improves detection accuracy and efficiency, providing crucial technical support for ensuring the safety of urban infrastructure.

Thus, this Section begins with an overview of 2D-ESN, followed by explanations of the Feature Collection and Anomaly Detection phases.

Dual-directional echo state network (2D-ESN)

Similar to typical reservoir computing networks53–56, i.e., the Echo State Network (ESN)57, 2D-ESN comprises an input layer, a hidden layer, and an output layer. However, 2D-ESN incorporates two reservoirs within the hidden layer to effectively capture dynamics in both horizontal and vertical directions, establishing dual-directional correlations between data points. Each reservoir contains neurons interconnected through fixed and randomly determined connections.

The presence of underground anomalies leads to different intra-wave and inter-wave changing information in the collected B-scan data, that is, different dual-directional dynamics. Therefore, the dynamic features obtained by fitting the anomaly data using 2D-ESN markedly differ from those derived from normal data. Such feature discrepancy enables effective anomaly detection and clustering.

The patching and fitting processes are designed to thoroughly capture the local dual-directional dynamics of the non-target B-scan data, supporting the subsequent comparison of dynamics extracted from anomaly-associated local data.

Candidate region identification

Res-SAM provides two modes for candidate region identification: the click-guided or fully automatic mode. The presence of underground anomalies leads to changes along and among EM waves, resulting in visible grayscale variations in B-scan data. These visually detectable changes allow for the intuitive and rapid localization of the approximate anomaly region, which SAM, a well-trained segmentation approach, could efficiently handle. However, as previously mentioned, since B-scan data essentially collect and represent EM waves, the inherent wave properties such as reflection, diffraction, and attenuation mean that anomalies in GPR B-scan data often lack distinct boundaries.

In the click-guided mode, to address these indistinct boundaries, we recommend using click prompts, and further providing a rectangular region based on the SAM-segmented area. Given an input B-scan data sample g and a set of c click prompts C={(xi,yi)}i=1c, SAM provides an irregular approximate anomaly-associated region R~=SAM(g,C). However, practical subsurface anomaly detection necessitates displaying the entire anomaly-affected area, typically using rectangular outlines to entirely define these anomaly regions for further analysis. In the fully automatic mode, no click prompts are required. SAM first segments the entire B-scan into a set of coarse regions. Each region is then fitted with 2D-ESN to extract its dynamic features, which are compared with the feature bank to evaluate anomaly likelihood. Regions whose features are close to the normal features are discarded, while those showing clear differences are retained as potential anomaly regions (the discrimination process follows the same feature-distance-based method described in the Feature Discrimination and Patch Merging section). For each retained region, the smallest enclosing rectangular box is taken as its candidate anomaly region, in the same manner as in the click-guided mode.

Through the above, the candidate regions identified by SAM, either click-guided or automatically generated, provide a compact search space that reduces the unnecessary 2D-ESN fittings on non-target areas. In this way, SAM narrows down a considerable volume of B-scan data to only the most suspicious areas, while the subsequent dynamic feature analysis refines these regions to achieve precise boundary delineation and anomaly categorization.

Patch fitting for local dynamic feature

While changes in B-scan data due to underground anomalies are visually identifiable and could be generally depicted (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3), B-scan data fundamentally consists of wave collections. The variations in local dual-directional dynamics caused by these underground anomalies help describe the abnormal area more accurately.

To capture local dynamics, each point in the candidate region Rcan serves as the center for extracting a local patch of size X × Y, as Supplementary Fig. 2b shows, excluding patches that extend beyond boundaries. Each of these patches is then fitted via 2D-ESN to derive dynamic features, and all such features are accumulated into a set F.

Underground anomalies lead to distinct local changing information along and among EM waves, deriving dynamic features that set apart from those from non-target data. Consequently, features developed from these anomalous patches exhibit marked differences from those generated from normal data. Specifically, features derived from non-target data tend to cluster closely together, whereas those from anomalies are clearly distinct and positioned farther from the normal features.
