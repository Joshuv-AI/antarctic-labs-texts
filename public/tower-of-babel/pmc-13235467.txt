Hanta on the Hondius - public health and bioethical perspectives of the recent Andes hantavirus cruise ship outbreak
Authors: Sotirios Tsiodras, Effrossyni Gkrania-Klotsas, Marta Mora-Rillo, Nitin Gupta, Jan Felix Drexler, Pikka Jokelainen, Galadriel Pellejero-Sagastizabal, Aleksandra Barac, José-Ramón Paño-Pardo, François-Xavier Lescure, Martin P Grobusch
Published: 2026

Background

The outbreak of Andes hantavirus (ANDV) infection associated with a polar expedition vessel has been linked to at least eight confirmed cases and three deaths as of May 9th 2026 [[1], [2], [3]]. Following evacuation of three ill passengers at Cape Verde, the vessel arrived in Tenerife on May 9th 2026 under an agreement between the World Health Organization (WHO) and Spanish authorities within the framework of the IHR (2005) [3]. This editorial examines the public-health, regulatory, ethical, and cruise-industry implications of the event and highlights preparedness gaps relevant to future ship-related outbreaks.

Regulatory architecture for ship health inspections

International maritime-health governance is intended to prevent disease spread while minimizing unnecessary disruption to travel and trade. Relevant instruments include the IHR (2005), particularly Articles 19-28 [3]; the WHO Handbook for Inspection of Ships [4]; the EU SHIPSAN framework and European Manual for Hygiene Standards [5]; SHIPSAN inspection schedules and information systems [6]; and EU Regulation 2022/2371 on serious cross-border health threats [7].

Previous cruise outbreaks, including the Diamond Princess and Grand Princess COVID-19 events, exposed persistent ambiguity in flag-, port-, and coastal-state responsibilities, uneven port-health capacity, and operational difficulties involving evacuation and repatriation [8,9]. Maritime public-health implementation has historically been inconsistent. Only 39.6% of European authorities reported routine inspections on national voyages and 35.4% reported routine port-to-port communication [10]. A Spanish review found 22% of Maritime Declaration of Health forms non-compliant and 39% incomplete [11]. Although frameworks evolved through HEALTHY GATEWAYS, HEALTHY SAILING and SHIPSAN, the overall inspection footprint remains limited [6,12].

Challenges in initial coordination and response during the recent 2026 hantavirus event

The currently ongoing event highlights several challenges listed below that are associated with the nature and the evolution of such an event in a distant geographical location.

Person-to-person transmission

The person-to-person transmission of ANDV in a cruise ship raises issues of infection control and prevention that are extremely difficult to manage. Issues include the potential for super-spreading [13] and transmission before symptom onset [14]. Handling of potential exposure and follow-up of vulnerable individuals is particularly difficult, especially since passengers can dynamically embark and disembark, including in isolated ports, while the event is still evolving. The long incubation time of the virus also contributes to the difficulty.

Diagnostic, critical-care, surge-capacity, and deceased-at-sea limitations

Hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS) may require molecular diagnostic tools to diagnose quickly. Pathogen-specific protocols for shipboard viral haemorrhagic illness are absent and limited by the absence of point-of-care tests. Even if samples were sent to land, few clinical microbiology laboratories have rapid in-house diagnostics for New World hantaviruses [15]. Projecting from comparable VHFs, this lack of therapeutic infrastructure could result in high mortality differences between high- and low-resource settings for comparable VHFs [[15], [16], [17]]. Advanced life support and mechanical ventilation are unavailable on most expedition vessels, and ECMO support is only available in specific regional tertiary care centers. Surge capacity in terms of adequate health care personnel is especially important in an event occurring in a remote setting. Deceased-at-sea handling may present significant logistical and bioethical issues.

Biosafety without high-level isolation infrastructure

Standard viral hemorrhagic fever precautions require impermeable gowns, N95 respirators, face shields, double gloves, and negative-pressure rooms with 6-12 air changes per hour [18]. Cruise ships generally lack such infrastructure, raising uncertainty about achievable onboard biosafety.

Dynamic passenger populations and the repatriation gap

A significant challenge is the dynamic, continuously turning-over, passenger population that characterizes modern expedition itineraries - driven in part by commercial yield optimization toward multi-port embarkation/disembarkation models. This produces cohorts that are geographically and temporally fragmented across past, current, and future travelers. In 100- to 200-person expedition vessels with shared dining, lecture spaces, zodiac embarkations, and excursion groupings, every passenger has had plausible repeated proximity to others on board, placing exposure on a non-quantifiable risk continuum rather than in binary contact/non-contact categories. To our knowledge, no harmonized, binding international protocol exists for repatriation, post-disembarkation surveillance, or onward-travel restrictions of passengers from a vessel implicated in a high-consequence outbreak. During the recent outbreak a repatriation protocol was issued by WHO [19]. The IHR sets broad obligations [3]; the ILO Maritime Labour Convention establishes detailed crew repatriation rights but does not address public-health-driven repatriation in outbreaks [20]; SHIPSAN binds neither third-country flag, port, nor home states [5], [21]; and according to previous reports WHO cruise-ship outbreak guidance is situational and non-binding [8]. The result is bilateral, ad hoc negotiations involving chartered evacuations, embassies, charter insurers, and differing national quarantine policies.

Vector surveillance and ship-borne biosecurity

Rodent surveillance and integrated pest management on ships are less standardized compared to food and water hygiene protocols. An older study of 21 passenger ferries found that while no rats or mice were detected during active surveillance, three ferries had had rodents in the past, and the study emphasized that integrated pest control programs should include continuing monitoring for pest species in different ship locations [22]. The IHR 2005 implementation for maritime transport has focused primarily on hygiene standards, with ship sanitation certificates and inspections emphasizing food safety and water quality, while comprehensive vector and rodent surveillance protocols remain underdeveloped [23]. On another note, the establishment of new invasive species of vectors in remote areas is an important public health issue. Rattus norvegicus (brown rat) is the primary reservoir host for Seoul orthohantavirus (SEOV), which causes hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) in humans and has been reported worldwide in Asia, Europe, the Americas, and Africa [24]. Maritime traffic plays a critical role in SEOV dissemination [25]. SEOV has been detected in wild rats in numerous port cities including New Orleans and Hainan Province, China [26,27]. Studies in Africa demonstrate that seaports and imported rats facilitate Seoul virus circulation, with Africa exposed to SEOV introduction events through exponential growth in maritime trade [25]. On the other hand, Antarctica faces significant biosecurity threats from ship-borne introductions of invasive species like marine organisms and terrestrial invertebrates [28,29]. Ship traffic connects Antarctica directly to global ports, with vessels from South Atlantic and European ports being particularly important vectors [30]. Antarctic maritime traffic directly connects fragile ecosystems to global ports [30], and biosecurity incursions remain a major environmental concern [31].

Coastal-state port refusals: ships are not isolation facilities

Regional refusals of the cruise ship where the incident occurs illustrates how a coastal-state political decision can override IHR cooperation expectations when no binding mechanism for compelled humanitarian disembarkation exists. This, in our view, is the single most consequential public-health policy question raised by this event. It threatens to embed in maritime preparedness a principle that the public-health and cruise-industry literature has spent the past decade dismantling: the assumption that an outbreak-affected vessel can be safely isolated at sea while the underlying epidemic plays out. Ships lack negative-pressure isolation rooms, dedicated airflow, and the operational capacity to separate infected from non-infected populations. Crew-passenger interaction for routine ship operations - food and supply delivery, sanitation, medical care - cannot be eliminated, and shared ventilation and shared common spaces continue to function as transmission routes throughout any onboard quarantine. The COVID-19 maritime experience offers one well-documented illustration of this point: aboard the Diamond Princess, a 14-day onboard quarantine of approximately 3700 passengers and crew did not arrest transmission [8,9], and mathematical modelling showed that no single intervention or combination - symptom screening, viral testing, point-of-care diagnostics, telemedicine consultation, reduced numbers, restricted port visits - eliminated transmission risk at sea [32]. The response to the Grand Princess, by contrast, achieved better outcomes through rapid, structured disembarkation to land-based federal quarantine facilities under coordinated multi-agency support [33], establishing the now-accepted policy preference for shore-based isolation under appropriate IPC over prolonged shipboard confinement. The principle is general, not specific to a respiratory pandemic pathogen. Although ANDV is thought to transmit less efficiently than SARS-CoV-2, the underlying logic is the same: indefinite shipboard confinement of passengers and crew with limited critical-care capacity, on an expedition vessel where every person has had repeated proximity to others, is unlikely to arrest secondary transmission and could expose others on board to disproportionate harm. The proportionate, evidence-based response to a high-consequence maritime outbreak is structured disembarkation to land-based facilities under appropriate infection prevention and control. The lesson of earlier maritime events is the reason to receive the vessel under appropriate IPC supervision, not the reason to refuse it; we urge that this point is not lost in future outbreaks.

Bioethical considerations

The response to the current event additionally raises distinct bioethical questions. Public-health emergencies require deliberate balancing of population health, individual rights and autonomy, fair distribution of burdens and benefits, and duty of care to those serving in the response [[34], [35], [36]]. The situation can be even more complex in cases of emerging infectious diseases as legal precedent is missing by definition; the law should treat the affected passengers more like victims rather than vectors [37].

Decisions on quarantine, port-access denial, prolonged confinement, and disembarkation restrictions must satisfy the established principles of necessity, least-restrictive means, proportionality, and reciprocity [34,35] but also autonomy. ANDV does not transmit with the efficiency of common respiratory viruses; evidence for human-to-human spread is contested when shared rodent exposure is properly controlled for [13,38,39]. The port refusals must therefore be weighed against the proportionality of indefinitely confining 140-plus people on a vessel, against the IHR-derived duties of cooperation among State Parties, and against the empirical evidence that shipboard quarantine is itself counterproductive.

The dispersion of several passengers from Saint Helena, with confirmed cases now in Switzerland and established contacts in the Netherlands and South Africa, generates obvious tensions between public-health benefit and the privacy and self-determination of travelers that should retain their anonymity [40]. Proportional, purpose-limited data handling, transparent communication to affected persons, and clear, internationally agreed, data-retention policies are basic ethical requirements that should be embedded in maritime contact-tracing protocols rather than improvised under emergency conditions [35,36]. Similarly, the disembarkation of a passenger to Tristan da Cunha, a passenger who is now considered a probable case, presents an additional concern, as the island is only accessible by sea and only has limited medical facilities. A risk assessment of future disembarkations to remote islands that are, however, part of expedition cruises should balance risk to passengers with risk to others, including to local geographically populations.

HCPS can frequently and rapidly require advanced critical care, and the medical evacuation of one critically ill patient to South Africa illustrates that geography, nationality, and insurance - not pathophysiology - too often determine whether a passenger receives advanced interventions. Justice-based public-health ethics demands that maritime preparedness frameworks specify, prospectively, who has access to such care, who pays, and how scarce evacuation slots and advanced interventions capacity are allocated [34]. Crew members and the ship's physician are not passengers but workers under the ILO Maritime Labour Convention; they cannot disembark voluntarily, may have limited PPE meeting VHF standards, and depend entirely on shipboard infrastructure for any post-exposure care, with measurable infection risk including documented nosocomial ANDV transmission [13]. The principle of reciprocity should be explicit in maritime frameworks, encompassing paid sick leave, long-term sequelae monitoring, and family support if death occurs in service [36].

The current event includes deaths onboard, yet existing VHF mortality guidance is designed for land-based settings [18]; this creates a major gap in maritime preparedness, as infection-control requirements must be balanced with cultural, religious, and family expectations for dignified care of the deceased and the timely repatriation of remains across multiple countries.

Public-facing communication should be transparent, proportionate, multilingual, and updated as evidence emerges. WHO has assessed the global public-health risk as low and ECDC the European risk as very low; over-dramatization can fuel stigma toward travelers, indigenous communities, and sea travel, and can be instrumentalized to justify disproportionate coastal-state measures. Hantavirus events have historically been accompanied by stigma toward rural and indigenous communities; engagement with affected communities in Patagonia, rather than communication merely about them, is the ethical baseline. The psychosocial burden on quarantined passengers, on bereaved families notified across borders, and on crew remaining aboard a vessel after deaths and evacuations is well documented for prior cruise-ship quarantine events and is expected to be substantial [41]; the risk of moral harm to clinical staff on board cannot be ignored either and will need to be addressed in time. In the future multilingual psychosocial support may be an issue to discuss in similar events from the outset.

The cruise vessel ecosystem and lessons for maritime outbreaks

The event under discussion is properly understood within the cruise industry's transformation since the COVID-19 pandemic. Global ocean cruise passengers reached 31.7 million in 2024, exceeding the 2019 record of 29.7 million, and the orderbook of approximately 58 ships through 2030 is the largest construction pipeline in maritime history [42]. Demand has shifted toward smaller vessels and remote destinations, with luxury and expedition cruising - particularly polar - growing faster than the mass-market segment. The International Maritime Organization Polar Code (in force from 2017) and International Association of Classification Societies Polar Class designations prescribe ice-related and environmental requirements [43] but do not prescribe public-health architecture beyond minimum medical-facility provision. Such vessels are optimized for ice navigation, not for negative-pressure isolation, in-house molecular diagnostics, or surge medical capacity.

Naturalist and wildlife-focused expedition cruises involve repeated close ecological exposure through landings, lectures, photography excursions, and wildlife observation. The suspected exposure linked to a pre-embarkation bird-watching excursion exemplifies risks associated with these activities. Pre-travel risk communication should therefore reflect actual ecological hazards rather than generic gastrointestinal or respiratory guidance.

The clinical response is anchored, in the first hours and days, by the onboard medical team - often a single physician supported by one or two nurses or paramedics - expected to function simultaneously as primary-care physician, emergency physician, intensivist for early stabilization, occupational-health officer, and public-health officer [44]. On expedition vessels, additional skills (advanced trauma, intubation, zodiac landings carrying medical equipment over uneven terrain, multilingual communication) and current BLS/ACLS/ATLS/PALS certification are typically required [44]. The combination is rare; the cruise-ship physician at the time of an outbreak onset is alone, geographically isolated, and tasked with decisions that ashore would involve an entire IPC committee. The structural undersupply of physicians with this blend of expedition, emergency, and infectious-disease experience is itself a preparedness gap.

Maritime infectious-disease surveillance has historically been dominated by acute gastroenteritis (predominantly norovirus, occasionally Salmonella, Shigella, or Campylobacter from food-water cross-contamination) and influenza-like illness [5,10,21,45]. Norovirus protocols emphasizing voluntary cabin-based isolation, environmental disinfection, hand-hygiene reinforcement, and graded restrictions on self-service food operations are empirically validated for self-limiting enteric pathogens [45] but do not translate to ANDV-associated HCPS, which has a high case-fatality rate, requires advanced critical care unavailable at sea, carries documented (if low-efficiency) human-to-human transmission, and demands biosafety procedures beyond a routine cruise-ship medical department's capabilities.

Strengthening preparedness

Building on the foregoing, we propose the following coordinated actions, to be revisited as further data emerge.

First, integrate zoonotic pathogens, including hantaviruses, into routine syndromic surveillance and pre-departure clinical algorithms for expedition vessels operating in endemic regions, and tailor pathogen-specific risk communication for naturalist, birdwatcher, and educational cruise itineraries to the ecological exposures involved.

Second, invest in regional and reference laboratory capacity for serology, RT-PCR, and metagenomic sequencing, with pre-agreed specimen-transport protocols from remote maritime locations and standing agreements between cruise lines and reference laboratories.

Third, develop standardized maritime protocols for management of suspected viral haemorrhagic illness on board, including dedicated guidance on handling deceased patients at sea, on managing close contacts outside high-level isolation, on repatriation of asymptomatic but exposed passengers, and on the bioethical dimensions thereof.

Fourth, standardized vector and rodent surveillance for all long-itinerary expedition vessels that will cover a priority list of current and emerging pathogens should be discussed.

Fifth, for high-consequence pathogens identified at sea, the default response should be structured disembarkation to land-based facilities with appropriate isolation infrastructure, avoidance of disembarkation on remote destinations, not prolonged onboard quarantine; coastal-state agreements should anticipate this scenario, and IHR-derived obligations of cooperation should be reinforced to prevent ad hoc refusals of port access.

Sixth, establish clearer procedures for medical evacuation and pre-defined critical care e.g. ECMO referral pathways from remote regions, recognizing the documented value of structured contact tracing across sea, air, and land arrivals for high-consequence imported infections [40].

Seventh, in partnership with WHO, IMO, ILO, ECDC, and national authorities, support development of a pre-negotiated, harmonized international repatriation framework for outbreak-affected vessels addressing port-of-refuge designation, cost allocation, charter-flight mechanisms, post-disembarkation surveillance protocols calibrated to the relevant pathogen's incubation window, and ethical safeguards for those facing detention or isolation in unfamiliar jurisdictions. In the recent outbreak such guidance was recently produced [19].

Eighth, support broader adoption of maritime outbreak research and complementary technological tools - including point-of-care molecular diagnostics for common diseases and an agreement for advanced testing in case of similar emergencies. A culture of preparedness must include a culture of research-readiness for epidemiological, diagnostic and therapeutic response. In this context, clinical societies can contribute meaningfully not by running trials themselves, but by helping clinicians enter, contribute to, and benefit from research networks operating during similar outbreaks rather than after them. In the same context, newer technologies can be used in the future, including environmental sensors and Internet-of-Things-based vector surveillance and digital contact-tracing platforms for epidemiological surveillance or satellite-enabled telemedicine for real-time expert consultation on remote vessels, - recognizing that these tools augment rather than substitute for the structural measures already discussed.

Ninth, in parallel, support targeted training and capacity building, including expedition-vessel medical-officer accreditation in partnership with the International Maritime Health Association, port-health-officer training oriented to high-consequence pathogen recognition, structured crew training programs, and regular multi-agency simulation exercises that integrate ship medical teams, port authorities, evacuation services, receiving hospitals, and public-health agencies.

Tenth, address legal and economic dimensions that have, to date, been peripheral to maritime infectious-disease preparedness: enhancement of port state control specifically for expedition vessels in remote-area service; clearer professional liability frameworks for cruise-ship physicians and port-health officers operating under emergency conditions; and insurance and indemnity arrangements that incentivize rather than penalize enhanced preparedness investment by operators.

Eleventh, improve the operationalization of One Health collaboration across IHR State Parties simultaneously implicated in maritime events and engage actively with the ongoing IHR amendment process to ensure the maritime-specific challenges and gaps identified here.

Twelfth, given ANDV's potential for pre-symptomatic transmission, design itineraries, expeditions, and excursions that minimize exposure to the rodent reservoir.

The ESCMID Emerging Infections Subcommittee supports the idea that a mechanism could be established along with other partners to provide rapid-consultation for high-consequence pathogen events at sea, accessible by ship medical officers, port-health authorities, and clinicians receiving evacuated patients.

Conclusion

The recent cruise-ship hantavirus outbreak demonstrates how an uncommon zoonotic infection can generate major clinical, logistical, ethical, and public-health consequences when ecological exposure, confined living conditions, and rapid expansion of expedition tourism intersect. Two conclusions are central. First, preparedness for maritime outbreaks involving high-consequence zoonotic pathogens must be developed proactively rather than improvised during crises. Second, ships should not function as long-term isolation facilities. Evidence from multiple maritime outbreaks, including COVID-19 events, supports structured shore-based disembarkation under appropriate infection prevention and control as the proportionate response.
