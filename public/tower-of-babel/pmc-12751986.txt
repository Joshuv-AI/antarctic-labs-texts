Targeted suppression of CCR7/NF-κB signaling by apoptotic body-inspired colchicine nanovesicles halts atherosclerotic progression
Authors: Qi Chen, Yuan-Shu Peng, Qian Zhong, Yu-Bo Zhang, Liu-Bing Hu, Guan-Yan Zhang, Yi-Xian Xu, Pei-Na Dong, Si Shen, Jing-Hao Wang, Zhi-Guo Wang, Huan-Tian Zhang, Rong Zeng, Hao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtherosclerosis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disorder characterized by foam cell formation and persistent inflammation as central pathological drivers. Although colchicine (Col) exhibits potent anti-inflammatory activities, its clinical application is limited by a narrow therapeutic window. In the present study, we developed phosphatidylserine-exposing nanovesicles (Col@PSVs) that leverage the innate phagocytic capacity of macrophage-derived foam cells by presenting surface “eat-me” signals, thereby enabling targeted immune modulation. The synergistic collaboration between Col and PSVs allows low-dose Col to retain robust anti-inflammatory efficacy while mitigating dose-dependent toxicity. Mechanistically, Col@PSVs potently suppress CCR7-mediated NF-κB signaling activation in foam cells, leading to a marked downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokine and disruption of inflammatory cascades. In ApoE−/− AS mouse models, Col@PSVs treatment significantly improved plaque stability and attenuated disease progression. These findings highlight the pivotal role of the CCR7/NF-κB signaling pathway in AS-associated inflammation and present a translational nanotherapeutic strategy with the potential to overcome the clinical limitations of Col.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03840-x.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, Chronic inflammation, Colchicine, Apoptotic cell-derived microvesicles

Introduction

Atherosclerosis (AS) is a chronic vascular disease characterized by the accumulation of lipids and immune cells in medium and large arteries, ultimately leading to fatal cardiovascular events and stroke through arterial lumen narrowing and the rupture of unstable plaques [1–4]. The pathophysiological mechanisms of AS involve the extensive recruitment of monocytes to the damaged vascular endothelium, where they differentiate into macrophages. These macrophages subsequently engulf lipoproteins within the arterial intima, forming foam cells, a key pathological mechanism in AS development [5, 6]. Studies have shown that macrophages within AS plaques exhibit high heterogeneity, with pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages exacerbating local inflammation by releasing inflammatory cytokines and promoting foam cell formation. The accumulation of foam cells in the arterial intima further secretes inflammatory cytokines, triggering a chronic local inflammatory response that establishes a vicious cycle, accelerating plaque progression [7]. Chronic inflammation is considered a key driver of AS plaque progression [8–10]. Therefore, targeting inflammatory regulation has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for AS.

Colchicine (Col), a potent anti-inflammatory agent traditionally used to treat gouty arthritis and familial Mediterranean fever [11, 12], have demonstrated vasoprotective effects in preclinical and clinical studies [6, 13, 14]. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved Col for secondary prevention in patients with coronary artery disease, and it is increasingly being adopted in clinical practice [15, 16]. Studies have shown that Col exerts anti-atherosclerotic effects by inhibiting the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, thereby reducing the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 and attenuating macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses [17, 18]. However, Col’s narrow therapeutic window raises toxicity concerns [19], particularly at daily doses exceeds 0.5 mg or in patients with mild or significant renal/hepatic impairment [15], which limits its clinical application. Therefore, optimizing the administration route and minimizing the toxicity of Col are crucial for advancing its use in the management of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.

Macrophages not only play a central role in inflammation but also contribute to immune regulation by recognizing and clearing apoptotic cell-derived microvesicles (ApoMVs), which is crucial in the progression of various diseases [20–22]. Studies have shown that ApoMVs released by apoptotic cells can be efficiently recognized and phagocytosed by macrophages through surface “eat-me” signals, thereby modulating immune responses [23, 24]. Inspired by this mechanism, synthetic ApoMVs—such as phosphatidylserine (PS)-modified polymeric nanoparticles or liposomes—have been developed to mimic the anti-inflammatory and immunoregulatory functions of natural ApoMVs and have demonstrated therapeutic potential in various inflammation-related diseases [25, 26]. Our previous studies have also confirmed that PS-modified nanoparticles can regulate macrophage polarization, such as promoting M2 polarization to accelerate wound healing [27] or delivering the BRD4 inhibitor JQ1 to alleviate osteoarthritis [28]. During the progression of AS, macrophages within plaques exhibit impaired phagocytic capacity, leading to an accumulation of apoptotic cells and exacerbation of the pro-inflammatory response. The PS signaling pathway plays a crucial role in enhancing macrophage efferocytosis, thereby reducing the necrotic core, improving plaque stability, and alleviating inflammation. Nowadays, although PS functionalization has been considered as a universal approach to macrophage-targeting anti-inflammatory nanotherapy, it remains largely unknown how PS cooperate with potential clinical drugs for better therapeutic performance on AS and other inflammatory diseases.

Herein, we develop a novel apoptotic body-mimetic nanotherapeutics, Col loaded PS-exposing nanovesicles (Col@PSVs), targeting to synergistically modulate macrophage-derived foam cells in AS-related arterial plaque. Compared with normal macrophages, Col@PSVs exhibited enhanced recognition of foam cells, which may be attributed to the high expression of the PS receptor on foam cells. RNA-seq analysis and further experimental results demonstrated that Col@PSVs modulated the CCR7-mediated inflammatory pathway, reduced M1 polarization, and exhibited significant anti-inflammatory activity. Moreover, the synergistic interaction between Col and PSVs allowed low-dose Col to maintain potent anti-inflammatory effects, thereby mitigating Col-associated toxicity. These findings were further validated in the ApoE−/− AS mouse model, where Col@PSVs significantly stabilized atherosclerotic plaques and slowed disease progression without apparent toxicity. This study suggests that the PS-mediated CCR7/NF-κB axis may play an important role in AS-associated inflammation and provides a theoretical basis for the further design and clinical translation of PS signal-based liposomal therapeutic strategies (Fig. 1).

Results

Preparation and characterization of Col@PSVs

To overcome the relatively narrow therapeutic window of Col, Col was encapsulated into PS-functionalized nanovesicles (PSVs) to generate Col@PSVs (Fig. 2A). Based on relevant literature [28], a systematic evaluation was conducted to assess the anti-inflammatory effects of PS-containing liposomes with different molar ratios (7.5%, 15%, and 30%). Liposomes containing 15% PS exhibited the most significant inhibitory effect on inflammatory responses, indicating favorable immunomodulatory potential at this ratio Figure S1A, Supporting Information. This optimized formulation was subsequently used for the preparation of Col@PSVs in follow-up experiments. Both Col@PSVs and blank PSVs exhibited comparable hydrodynamic diameters (~ 100 nm), low polydispersity indices (PDI) of around 0.3, and similar negative zeta potentials, confirming uniform nanovesicles formation (Fig. 2B). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed spherical morphologies with a uniform size of approximately 100 nm in the dry-state (Fig. 2C). Surface PS modification was validated using annexin V-FITC binding, which detected PS signals on 98% of PSVs and Col@PSVs (Fig. 2D). This confirmed successful PS surface functionalization and minimal impact of Col loading on PS localization.

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading efficiency (DLE) in Col@PSVs were 92.4% ± 0.3% and 3.5% ± 0.3%, respectively. In addition, PSVs and Col@PSVs maintain good long-term physical stability at low temperatures (4 ℃) for at least 3 weeks (Fig. 2E). To investigate the potential impact of serum proteins on the macrophage targeting efficacy and biodistribution of Col@PSVs, we conducted a comprehensive study using a medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Notably, Col@PSVs exhibited stable particle size, zeta potential, and colloidal dispersion in the intrinsic medium over 48 h (Fig. 2F). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that over 95% of Col@PSVs displayed PS surface signals for up to 24 h (Figure S1B, Supporting Information), collectively demonstrating minimal interference from serum proteins on Col@PSVs properties. These results underscore the high colloidal stability of Col@PSVs, a prerequisite for effective in vivo biodistribution and targeted delivery to atherosclerotic lesions. The in vitro release behavior of free Col and Col@PSVs was determined using a UV spectrophotometer (Fig. 2G). Results showed that approximately 80% of free Col was released within 8 h, while only about 40% was released from Col@PSVs. After 12 h, free Col was completely released, whereas ~ 30% of the encapsulated Col remained retained within liposomes at 24 h, suggesting prolonged drug availability for sustained anti-inflammatory action.

The cytotoxicity and targeting ability of Col@PSVs in vitro

To evaluate the cytotoxicity of the formulations, Raw 264.7 and L929 cells were treated with Col, PSVs, or Col@PSVs for 48 h, respectively, and cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8 assay) (Figure S2A, B, Supporting Information). Notably, concentrations of Col exceeding 20 nM significantly reduced cell viability to below 80%. Meanwhile, we found that the physical mixture of Col and PSVs (Col + PSVs group) exhibited significantly higher cytotoxicity compared to the free Col group, blank PSVs group, and Col@PSVs group, suggesting that non-encapsulated physical mixtures may induce stronger toxic responses. In contrast, liposomal encapsulation of Col has the potential to reduce its cytotoxicity (Figure S1C, Supporting Information). Additionally, oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) stimulation induces oxidative stress, leading to increased in nitric oxide (NO) production by cells. In line with this, the inhibition of NO levels by Col was found to be concentration-dependent (Figure S2C, Supporting Information). Based on these findings and previous studies, a Col concentration of 10 nM was selected for subsequent in vitro experiments [29]. According to the ratio used in the preparation of liposomes (see the "Preparation of Col@PSVs" section), when the Col concentration is 10 nM in Col@PSVs, the PS concentration is 15 μM.

To investigate the intracellular accumulation of liposomes, a fluorescence probe, 1,1’-Dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-Tetramethylindodicarbocyanine, 4-Chlorobenzenesulfonate Salt (DiD), was encapsulated in Col@PSVs (DiD@Col@PSVs) and co-incubated with both normal macrophages and foam cells. The liposome content within the cells was quantified using confocal laser scanning microscopy (Figure S2F, Supporting Information) and flow cytometry analysis (Figure S2D, Supporting Information). Liposomes accumulation in activated macrophages was time-dependent, reaching its peak at 24 h. Normal macrophages also exhibited time-dependent accumulation of Col@PSVs, although the rate of accumulation in normal Raw 264.7 cells was significantly lower than in activated foam cells at every time point observed (Figure S2E, Supporting Information). These findings suggest that Col@PSVs preferentially accumulate in foam cells, highlighting their potential for targeted delivery and therapeutic effects in these cells.

The anti-inflammatory properties of Col@PSVs in foam cells

Foam cells serve as a critical in vitro model for studying AS [30]. In this study, a foam cell model was established by inducing cells with ox-LDL (50 μg/mL) for 48 h, followed by a series of treatments and analyses. Flow cytometry and immunofluorescence imaging revealed a significant increase in the expression of CD86, a marker of M1 macrophage polarization, in foam cells, which was effectively suppressed by Col@PSVs (Fig. 3A; Figure S3A–B, Supporting Information). As anticipated, the formation of lipid droplets (Bodipy/green) was markedly enhanced in foam cells. However, treatment with PSVs, Col, or Col@PSVs significantly reduced the accumulation of intracellular lipid droplets and foam cell formation, as assessed by Bodipy staining, which is commonly used to evaluate lipid droplet formation and distribution. Notably, the Col@PSVs group exhibited the most pronounced effect (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that Col@PSVs can effectively inhibit the formation of macrophage-derived foam cells, offering potential therapeutic implications for AS in vitro.

To evaluate the anti-inflammatory properties of Col@PSVs, supernatants and cell samples were collected for comprehensive analysis. To evaluate oxidative stress modulation, intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. ox-LDL-stimulated foam cells exhibited elevated ROS levels, whereas Col@PSVs treatment substantially reduced the ROS accumulation in living cells (Fig. 3C), suggesting their potent antioxidant activity in mitigating oxidative damage. Pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-18, and NO, were measured in the supernatants, revealing a marked increase in the ox-LDL-induced foam cells (model group) (Fig. 3D; Figure S3C—D, Supporting Information). However, pretreatment with PSVs, free Col, or Col@PSVs significantly attenuated these inflammatory markers, with Col@PSVs revealing the most robust suppression. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis of foam cell lysates further confirmed that Col@PSVs downregulated the transcriptional expression of TNF-α and IL-6 (Fig. 3E), highlighting their capacity to inhibit pro-inflammatory signaling pathways. To further examine whether Col@PSVs can regulate macrophage polarization, we analyzed the expression of pro-inflammatory macrophage markers in ox-LDL-induced foam cells. qPCR analysis revealed that Col@PSVs significantly suppressed mRNA levels of iNOS and CD86, key markers of pro-inflammatory (M1-like) macrophages (Fig. 3E). Western blot further showed that the protein expression of iNOS, NLRP3, and Cox-2 was markedly elevated in the untreated model group. However, treatment with PSVs, free Col, or Col@PSVs reduced their expression, with the Col@PSVs group exhibiting the most pronounced reduction (Fig. 3F). Collectively, these results underscore the ability of Col@PSVs to inhibit pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization, highlighting their therapeutic potential for attenuating inflammation in AS.

Effect of Col@PSVs on the formation of AS plaques

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of Col@PSVs in the formation of AS plaques in vivo, ApoE−/− mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) for 12 weeks to establish the atherosclerotic mouse model, followed by a series of treatments including PBS, PSVs, Col, and Col@PSVs injection via the tail vein for 8 weeks. All mice were euthanized, and serum and aortic tissue were collected for further analysis (Fig. 4A). Macrophage infiltration is a key indicator of tissue inflammatory in which a critical role in plaque formation and the expansion of the necrotic core [31, 32]. Herein, we did found that the expression of CD86 in the aortic root of mice was significantly reduced following Col@PSVs treatment (Fig. 4C - D). In accordance with this, we found that Col@PSVs exhibited a strong ability to regulate macrophage polarization, as demonstrated by the suppression of the levels of iNOS, NLRP3, and Cox-2 (Fig. 4E). Likewise, Col@PSVs treatment significantly inhibited the levels of serum pro-inflammatory factors including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-18 detected by ELISA kit (Fig. 4B).

Biosafety is a critical parameter for evaluating the clinical translation of nanodrug. The acute toxicity was assessed following a single intravenous injection of Col@PSVs, while chronic toxicity was evaluated through repeated injections over eight weeks. No significant differences in body weight were observed between the Col@PSVs-treated and PBS control group throughout the study period (Figure S4A, Supporting Information). Accordingly, histological analysis revealed no signs of tissue degeneration or visible damage in major organs (Figure S4D, Supporting Information). Similarly, serum levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum creatinine (CRE) remained within normal ranges, with no significant differences between groups (Figure S4E—H, Supporting Information). Col@PSVs exhibited significant anti-inflammatory properties led us to hypothesize that they may be a promising therapeutic approach for AS. Next, the in vivo targeting affinity of Col@PSVs to atherosclerotic plaques was assessed by intravenous injection with PBS or fluorescently loaded Col@PSVs Imaging results showed that the fluorescence intensity in vessels with plaque was significantly higher compared to normal vessels (Fig. 4F), indicating that Col@PSVs specifically target atherosclerotic plaques. Meanwhile, tissue distribution was evaluated 12 h after administration by examining excised organs, including heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys. Among these tissues, the liver showed the highest uptake of Col@PSVs (Figure S4I, J Supporting Information). This may be attributed to hepatic steatosis and the accumulation of inflammatory macrophages in the liver of HFD-fed mice. To further evaluate the targeting capability of Col@PSVs toward atherosclerotic plaques, DiD-labeled Col@PSVs were intravenously injected into AS mouse models for in vivo tracking. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a significant accumulation of M1 macrophages within the atherosclerotic lesions compared to normal mice. Co-localization analysis further confirmed that DiD@Col@PSVs were efficiently internalized by M1 macrophages, indicating the strong macrophage-targeting capability of this delivery system (Figure S5, Supporting Information).

Given that Col@PSVs nanosystems efficiently target and accumulate at the AS plaque, we then hypothesized that they could also deliver Col (at a dosage of 4 μg/kg) to the plaque. Consistent with previous reports, an obvious plaque formation was found in the aortic arch after HFD in the ApoE−/− mice, while treatment with Col@PSVs for 8 weeks significantly reduced its formation (Fig. 4G). Similarly, the en face micrographs of ORO-stained aortas showed that the ORO-positive area accounted for approximately 10.37% of the total aorta area in the model group, while the aortic lesion area was significantly reduced in the other three treatment groups (Fig. 4H). By measuring the levels of total cholesterol (TC) and triglyceride (TG) in mice, it was found that PSVs, Col, and Col@PSVs could reduce the level of lipid metabolism in vivo, yet the advantages of Col@PSVs group were not significant (Figure S4B, C, Supporting Information). Necrotic core area is closely related to plaque development and disease severity [31, 32]. Herein, H&E staining did indicate that the plaques of aortic roots in model group consisted of lipid-rich necrotic core. In contrast, the necrotic core decreased significantly in the PSVs, Col or Col@PSVs treatment groups, with the most pronounced reduction observed in in the Col@PSVs group (Fig. 4I, K). Masson staining showed that Col@PSVs increase collagen content around the plaques (Fig. 4J, L), resulted in an increase in fibrous cap thickness, which suggests that the plaque were becoming more stabilized after treatment. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Col@PSVs effectively inhibit the development of AS plaques and enhance their stability.

CCR7 is identified as a target of Col@PSVs

Next, we were interested to investigate the underlying mechanisms of Col@PSVs on AS. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, cell samples from the Model group (ox-LDL + PBS) and the Treated group (ox-LDL + Col@PSVs) were first subjected to RNA-seq analysis. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation between the two groups (Fig. 5B), and a total of 558 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 285 upregulated and 273 downregulated genes following Col@PSVs treatment, were identified. Enrichment analysis of the DEGs profile in the KEGG database demonstrated that these DEGs were predominantly associated with immune-related processes, with the Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway being the most significantly enriched (Fig. 5C - D). Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) showed that Col@PSVs treatment reduced the hyper-enrichment of the Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway in the foam cell model (Fig. 5E). Given the critical role of this pathway in immune regulation, we further validated key molecules within the pathway using qPCR, which confirmed the RNA-seq results (Fig. 5F).

Among these molecules, CCR7 has been previously shown to play a pivotal role in macrophage-mediated inflammation and immune regulation in coronary artery disease [33]. Luchtefeld et al. reported that CCR7 deficiency attenuated the development of atherosclerotic plaques in LDL−/− mice [34]. Based on this finding, molecular docking studies were conducted to investigate the potential interaction between CCR7 and Col using Autodock Vina software. The docking results revealed a binding energy of −7.3 kJ/mol, suggesting a stable and significant interaction between CCR7 and Col, which may provide further insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying AS progression (Fig. 6A). To further determine the interaction between Col@PSVs and CCR7, different concentrations of Col and Col@PSVs were individually applied to foam cells. qPCR results confirmed that both Col and Col@PSVs dose-dependently inhibited the expression of CCR7 mRNA, with Col@PSVs showing a more pronounced effect (Fig. 6B). Western blotting and immunofluorescence were used to verify the CCR7 expression in the cell model (Fig. 6C-F). Compared to the normal control group, the CCR7 expression was significantly elevated in the Model group, while treatment with Col@PSVs markedly suppressed CCR7 expression.

To investigate whether the expression of CCR7 is altered in individuals with AS, we analyzed 32 human atherosclerotic plaque samples and adjacent macroscopically intact tissues from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) dataset GSE43292 (Fig. 6G). The results revealed a significant increase in the CCR7 expression in human atherosclerotic plaques. Similarly, transcriptomic data from 104 tissue samples in the GEO dataset GSE100927 were analyzed to compare the CCR7 gene expression between atherosclerotic plaques in peripheral arteries (including carotid, femoral, and popliteal arteries) and normal arteries from deceased organ donors. The analysis revealed significantly elevated CCR7 expression in the atherosclerotic plaques compared to normal arterial tissue (Fig. 6H). Next, we sought to explore the role of CCR7 in atherosclerotic mice. Western blot analysis showed that the CCR7 expression was significantly increased in the aortic tissues of atherosclerotic mice, while treatment with Col@PSVs effectively inhibited the CCR7 expression (Fig. 6I). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining for CCR7 and the inflammatory macrophage marker CD86 revealed that Col@PSVs significantly reduced the expression of both markers (Fig. 6J). We also observed co-localization of CCR7 with CD86, suggesting that CCR7 may play a crucial role in macrophage polarization. Furthermore, ox-LDL was used to stimulate cells after CCR7 knockdown to investigate its effect on the expression of pro-inflammatory proteins. The results showed that CCR7 knockdown significantly reduced the expression levels of pro-inflammatory macrophage marker proteins in foam cells. Moreover, the combined treatment with Col@PSVs following CCR7 knockdown further suppressed the pro-inflammatory response, suggesting that Col@PSVs exert their anti-atherosclerotic effects, at least in part, by modulating CCR7 expression (Fig. 6K–L). Taken together, these findings indicate that CCR7 serves as a key mediator through which Col@PSVs influence the progression of AS.

Col@PSVs targets CCR7 for the treatment of atherosclerosis

Clinical studies have shown that, compared to non-atherosclerotic controls, the expression of CCL19, CCL21, and CCR7 is increased in atherosclerotic coronary plaques, and the elevated expression levels are positively correlated with the staging of coronary artery disease [35, 36]. To investigate the role of CCR7-mediated signaling pathways in AS, we measured the expression levels of CCR7 ligands (CCL19 and CCL21) in both in vitro cell models and in vivo mouse models using ELISA kits (Fig. 7A-B). The results showed that the secretion levels of CCL19 and CCL21 were significantly increased in the AS model. However, treatment with Col@PSVs significantly reduced the secretion of CCL19 and CCL21, suggesting that Col@PSVs may exert its anti-inflammatory effects by regulating the CCR7 signaling pathway.

Previous studies have demonstrated that CCR7 regulates the expression of inflammation-related genes by activating downstream signaling pathways, including NF-κB, PI3K/AKT, and MAPK (Fig. 7C) [37, 38]. To explore the regulatory effects of Col@PSVs on these pathways, we performed GSEA analysis using the RNA-seq data. The results indicated that Col@PSVs negatively regulates the all three pathways, with the NF-κB pathway showing the most significant modulation (Fig. 7D). In the NF-κB pathway, the IκB kinase (IKK) complex, particularly the IKKβ subunit, is considered the most critical initiator [39, 40]. We then analyzed the correlation between the gene encoding the IKKβ subunit (IKBKB) and CCR7 using the AS database GSE37824. Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed a significant association between IKBKB and CCR7 in atherosclerotic disease (Fig. 7E). The finding was further validated by immunofluorescence staining in mouse aortic root tissues (Fig. 7F). Subsequently, the Co-IP assay also confirmed a protein–protein interaction between CCR7 and IKKβ in the foam cell model group (Fig. 7G). Based on the above results, we hypothesized that Col@PSVs may exert its effects on atherosclerotic disease by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway through CCR7. AS expected Western blot analysis revealed significant activation of the NF-κB pathways in the AS model group, while Col@PSVs significantly inhibited the phosphorylation of NF-κB and IκBα both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 7H). These findings further confirmed the regulatory effect of Col@PSVs on the CCR7-mediated signaling pathway.

In summary, Col@PSVs may alleviate inflammatory responses by inhibiting CCR7-mediated NF-κB signaling activation, thereby exhibiting potential therapeutic value in the treatment of AS.

Discussion

Col is a first-line treatment for acute gout flare [41, 42]. In recent years, compelling evidence from several clinical trials (LoDoCo, LoDoCo2, COLCOT, and COPS) has suggested a significant reduction in the composite endpoint of cardiovascular mortality associated with the clinical application of Col [6, 43]. However, Col has a narrow therapeutic window, and its metabolism in vivo primarily depends on the hepatic CYP3A4 enzyme system and the P-glycoprotein (P-gp) transport system. After oral administration, Col is absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract and metabolized by hepatic CYP3A4, with a bioavailability of only approximately 25–50% [44]. The marked first-pass effect leads to considerable interindividual variability in plasma drug concentrations [45, 46]. When CYP3A4 or P-gp function is inhibited (e.g., by co-administration of clarithromycin, cyclosporine, or verapamil), Col clearance decreases, resulting in elevated plasma concentrations and an increased risk of systemic toxicity [47]. In addition, oral administration can cause high local drug concentrations in the gastrointestinal tract, which may disrupt microtubule function in intestinal epithelial cells and lead to common adverse reactions such as nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea [48]. Therefore, both the first-pass effect and excessive local exposure are key contributors to the enhanced toxicity of Col. Polypharmacy and related comorbidities, particularly renal dysfunction, are common in patients with AS [49], increasing the likelihood of Col-related adverse effects. Research has shown that a daily dose of only 0.5 mg of Col can have a positive impact on patients, whereas increasing the dose to 1 mg per day may counteract its beneficial effects on AS due to adverse reactions [50]. These limitations hinder clinical efficacy, and accurately delivering the drug to inflammatory sites remains a challenge [29]. The aim of this study was to develop a Col-based nanotherapeutic system to enhance the therapeutic efficacy, improve targeting efficiency, enable sustained drug release, and reduce systemic toxicity.

The selected concentration of Col is consistent with the minimum effective concentration reported in existing studies for the cellular model [29]. In the animal model, the therapeutic dose is less than one-tenth of previously reported doses [6, 29]. Both in vitro and in vivo studies utilize low concentrations of Col to investigate its therapeutic effects on AS. In the study, intravenous injection is prioritized for in vivo experiments, as this administration route allows for better control of drug exposure levels, enables rapid attainment of high plasma concentrations, and facilitates enhanced targeting efficiency. Moreover, it improves the controllability and reproducibility of therapeutic outcomes, which is advantageous for the preliminary evaluation of the formulation’s therapeutic potential. Notably, Col@PSVs demonstrated a specific binding affinity to aortic plaques, leading to reduced inflammation levels, inhibition of necrotic core expansion, and enhanced plaque stability, thereby delaying the progression of AS. Among all treatment groups, Col@PSVs demonstrated the most significant effect, likely due to the synergistic anti-inflammatory action of PS nanovesicles and Col. Therefore, low doses of Col may exert an optimal therapeutic effect in this context. Although the free Col group did not exhibit apparent toxicity in terms of safety, its therapeutic efficacy was limited. In contrast, the Col@PSVs group demonstrated markedly enhanced anti-inflammatory and pathological improvement effects under the same low-dose conditions, achieving efficient therapy at a reduced dosage and further supporting its potential application in the treatment of AS. However, treatment with Col@PSVs did not significantly affect the lipid levels in mice. This suggests that the inhibitory effect of these liposomes on AS may be more closely related to their targeted modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment within plaques, rather than influencing hepatic lipid metabolism. In future work, we plan to systematically evaluate the efficacy and toxicity differences among oral administration, intravenous free Col, and Col@PSVs formulations at different doses, to comprehensively verify the safety and translational potential of this delivery system.

To explore the potential molecular mechanisms of Col@PSVs in AS, RNA-seq analysis was performed to identify DEGs, followed by enrichment analysis. CCR7, a G protein-coupled receptor primarily expressed in immune cells and crucial for immune cell migration, was identified as a key molecule mediating the effects of Col@PSVs [33]. Previous studies have shown that CCR7 expression promotes M1 macrophage polarization, enhancing their pro-inflammatory properties [51, 52]. In this study, co-localization experiments in plaque tissues revealed that CCR7 co-localizes with pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages. In vitro foam cell models also demonstrated increased markers of pro-inflammatory macrophages and CCR7 expression, which were notably reduced following Col@PSVs treatment. Meanwhile, CCR7 knockdown significantly reduced the expression levels of pro-inflammatory macrophage marker proteins in foam cells. However, macrophages, as classical immune cells, possess strong phagocytic and clearance capabilities against exogenous substances, which leads to a certain degree of resistance to transfection agents such as siRNA, shRNA, plasmids, and viral vectors. In addition, their active lysosomal systems and innate immune responses accelerate the degradation of exogenous nucleic acids, thereby limiting transfection efficiency. These characteristics make it difficult to achieve complete CCR7 knockdown using CCR7 siRNA alone in macrophage-derived foam cells. On this basis, we further applied combined treatment with Col@PSVs and observed a more pronounced downregulation of CCR7 expression and associated inflammatory responses. These results suggest that Col@PSVs may exert a synergistic effect with CCR7 siRNA, enhancing the suppression of the CCR7 signaling pathway and thereby inhibiting inflammation-related signal transduction.

Clinical studies have reported that immunohistochemical analyses of AS samples at different stages show increased expression of CCL19, CCL21, and CCR7 in atherosclerotic coronary plaques compared to non-atherosclerotic controls. Moreover, the elevated expression levels are positively correlated with the stages of coronary lesions. The CCL19/CCL21-CCR7 axis is a novel homeostatic chemokine system that plays a pivotal role in the progression of AS [35, 36]. CCR7, upon binding to its ligands (CCL19 and CCL21), activates multiple signaling pathways, thereby mediating various intracellular targets and inducing cellular state changes [38]. Among these, the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways are closely associated with macrophage polarization and immune regulation, making them critical targets for AS treatment [53–56]. Based on the transcriptomic sequencing results of this study, Col@PSVs exerted the most significant inhibitory effect on the NF-κB pathway. Col@PSVs effectively reduce the expression levels of the CCL19/CCL21-CCR7 axis in both in vitro and in vivo models. Moreover, the inhibition of CCR7 expression by Col@PSVs is consistent with the suppression of NF-κB signaling pathways. These findings suggest that Col@PSVs may exert beneficial immunomodulatory effects by suppressing CCR7-mediated inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby offering potential therapeutic value in the treatment of AS. In this study, elevated CCR7 expression was confirmed in atherosclerotic lesions through analyses of cellular, animal, and clinical samples. Based on this observation, we designed Col@PSVs to downregulate CCR7 expression. The results demonstrated that Col@PSVs effectively reduced CCR7 levels and alleviated AS-related pathological changes. To further investigate whether CCR7 serves as a key functional target of Col@PSVs, CCR7 expression was silenced using siRNA in foam cells, which led to a marked reduction in pro-inflammatory responses. Although preliminary evidence supports the involvement of CCR7 in mediating the therapeutic effects of Col@PSVs, additional studies are required to determine whether CCR7 is the primary target. Specifically, future experiments involving CCR7 overexpression at the cellular or animal level will be essential to assess whether such manipulation can reverse the therapeutic effects of Col@PSVs. Moreover, to strengthen the rigor of our conclusions and enhance the mechanistic evidence, we plan to employ CCR7 knockout mouse models in future studies to further validate the reliability of the proposed pathway.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed apoptotic cell-mimicking nanovesicles (Col@PSVs) designed to specifically target macrophage-derived foam cells within atherosclerotic plaques by leveraging the macrophages’ ability to recognize ApoMVs. Col@PSVs partially inhibited the activation of the CCR7-mediated NF-κB signaling pathway, thereby attenuating inflammatory responses and potentially slowing the progression of AS. Therefore, the delivery of Col via apoptotic cell-mimicking liposomes may enhance its anti-inflammatory efficacy and provides a basis for further translational research.

Experimental section

Preparation of Col@PSVs

Col@PSVs were prepared through the combined method of thin-film hydration, ultrasonication, and extrusion [57]. In brief, soybean phosphatidylcholine (SPC), cholesterol (Chol), 1, 2-Dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-L-serine (PS) and Col with the optimized ratio (65 mol%: 20 mol%: 15 mol%: 0.01 mol%) were dissolve in 5 mL chloroform and methanol mixture (5:1, v/v). The organic solvent was removed by vacuum rotary evaporation. Then, the thin lipid film was obtained and further dried under vacuum for 4 h. The lipid film was then hydrated in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) buffer (pH 7.4) by vigorous shaking and vortex mixing for 30 min at 52 °C. The total lipid concentration was 10 mM. The solution was probe-sonicated (150 W) for 3 min under ice bath conditions, and the resulting solution was extruded through a 100 nm polycarbonate track-etch nanoporous membrane 4 times to acquire the Col@PSVs. Moreover, DiD-, IR820-, loaded Col@PSVs were prepared using the same procedure, PSVs were prepared as described above but without the addition of Col.

Characterization of Col@PSVs

The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and Zeta potential of PSVs and Col@PSVs were determined by Zetasizer (Nano-ZS, Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading efficiency (DLE) of Col were calculated by the equations: EE% = (the weight of total fed Col—the weight of unentrapped Col)/(the weight of total fed Col) × 100%, and DLE% = (the weight of total fed Col—the weight of unentrapped Col)/(the weight of total Col-loaded liposomes) × 100%, respectively. The content of Col was measured by UV–Vis spectrophotometry (Persee TU-1810SPC, China) at 243 nm [58]. The morphology of the liposomes was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) [59]. For TEM samples, liposomes were added onto the surface of copper mesh (230 mesh, round fields), stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid and dried under room temperature.The excess liquid was sucked away by filter paper, the TEM images were obtained on a JEM-1400 flash microscopy (JEOL, Japan).

Furthermore, we performed long-term physical stability tests for liposomal [60]. Freshly prepared liposome suspensions were stored at 4℃ for 3 weeks for testing. The Zeta potential, particle size, and PDI of the liposomes were also monitored. Meanwhile, to elucidate the in vivo macrophage targeting of Col@PSVs and the biodistribution affected by surface protein adsorption, we performed a comprehensive in vitro evaluation using medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Col@PSVs and 10% FBS were mixed at a 1:1 volume ratio and incubated at 37℃ at different time points (0, 0.5, 1, 4, 8, 12, 24, 36 and 48 h) [61]. Subsequently, Annexin V-FITC was co-incubated with Col@PSVs treated with 10% FBS, and the FITC fluorescence intensity on the liposome surface was measured by flow cytometry.

Detection of PS exposure on Col@PSVs

Annexin V-FITC (KGA105, Key GEN BioTECH) was used to further confirm the existence of PS on the surface of liposomes [62]. Briefly, PSVs or Col@PSVs liposomes with 0.5 μmol/mL total lipids were incubated with Annexin V-FITC in a binding buffer for 30 min, and PS-lacking liposomes were used as a control. Then, these samples were concentrated and separated from the Annexin-FITC suspension using 100-kDa molecular weight cutoff centrifugal filters (Millipore, USA). The samples were resuspended in 300 μL of PBS and then analyzed via flow cytometry (FACS) (BD, San Jose, CA).

Cell culture

Raw 264.7 cells (#CL-0190) and L929 cells (#CL-0137) were purchased from Procell. The cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% (v/v) FBS, 1% penicillin, and streptomycin at 37 °C with a 5% CO2 environment. Raw 264.7 cells were induced with ox-LDL (50 μg/mL) (IO1300-2 mg, Solarbio) for 48 h to construct macrophage-derived foam cells.

Cytotoxicity assay

Col, PSVs, and Col@PSVs cytotoxicity in Raw 264.7 and L929 cells was examined via CCK-8 assay. Briefly, these cells were plated overnight in 96-well plates, after which they were cultured for 48 h in the presence of a range of Col, PSVs, and Col@PSVs concentrations. A CCK-8 assay was then utilized to quantify viability based on standard protocols.

Analysis of the accumulation of Col@PSVs in cells

The uptake of DiD@Col@PSVs by foam cells was (Beckman, USA) and qualitatively analyzed via laser confocal microscope (Leica, Germany) and quantitatively analyzed by a flow cytometer. Briefly, Raw 264.7 cells (1 × 105/mL) were added to a 24-well plate containing cell crawling tablets, and the cells were then activated with ox-LDL. Subsequently, media was exchanged for media containing the desired DiD@Col@PSVs concentration. Following cultured for 0, 12, 24, 48, or 72 h, cells were rinsed using PBS and the nucleuses were stained with DAPI (1 μg/mL, Sigma). The uptake of DiD@Col@PSVs by normal Raw 264.7 cells was compared.

The therapy effect of Col@PSVs in vitro

Foam cell models were established by inducing Raw264.7 cells with ox-LDL. The cells were treated with Col (10 nM), PSVs (15 μM), or Col@PSVs (15 μM) in different experimental groups respectively. The culture supernatants were collected and the amount of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-18 were detected by ELISA kits following with the protocol (EMC102a, EMC004, Neobioscience)(EM1158, Fine Test). NO levels in samples were determined according to the instruction of kit (S0021, Beyotime). The cells were collected and analyzed by qPCR, Western blot, immunofluorescence and flow cytometry to verify the anti-inflammatory properties of Col@PSVs. Primer sequences and antibody information are listed in tables S1 and S2, respectively (Supporting Information). To quantify the ROS clearance ability of Col@PSVs, a DCFH-DA probe (1:1000) (G1706, Servicebio) was loaded after different cell treatments. The ROS levels were monitored by flow cytometry. Meanwhile, the formation of lipid droplets was observed by bodipy staining (MP03792, Molecular Probes).

Animals and animal groups

Our research protocol was reviewed and approved by the IACUC of Jinan University (Ethical approval number: IACUC-20221116–04). ApoE−/− mice of 6-week-old on a C57BL/6 J background (GemPharmatech) were fed under dedicated pathogen-free conditions and were randomly assigned to the experimental groups. The HFD (20% fat, 40% carbohydrate, 20% protein, and 1.25% cholesterol, D12108C) were fed for 12 weeks to induce AS. Then, ApoE−/− mice were randomized into 4 groups that were treated twice weekly for 8 weeks with PBS, PSVs, Col or Col@PSVs, while being maintained on HFD. The selected free Col dose was equivalent to that in the Col@PSVs. Similarly, the dose of PS in the group was comparable to that in PSVs. Normal C57BL/6 J mice were used as negative control.

Plaque targeting and biodistribution assay

The atherosclerotic ApoE−/− mice were injected intravenously with PBS and Col@PSVs containing the IR820 label, respectively. Normal WT mice were injected with Col@PSVs. After 12 h, aortas and vital organs were isolated for imaging under an IVIS imaging system (DPM-IVFM-NIR-Ⅱ).

In vivo safety evaluation of Col@PSVs

Blood samples and major organs such as heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney of ApoE−/− mice with different treatment were collected for in vivo safety analysis. Blood samples were evaluated with biochemical and hematological assay, and organs were prepared to paraffin sections for hematoxylin/eosin(H&E) staining.

The therapeutic effect of Col@PSVs in atherosclerotic mice

Mouse serum was collected to measure levels of inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-18) using ELISA kits. Meanwhile, lipid metabolism-related parameters, including triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) levels, were measured. Mouse aortic tissues were stained with Oil Red O to visualize atherosclerotic plaques and quantify plaque areas. Additionally, aortic roots were collected, fixed in 4% phosphate-buffered paraformaldehyde, and embedded in paraffin for sectioning. To assess the effect of Col@PSVs on pro-inflammatory macrophage infiltration, immunostaining for CD86 antibody was performed on the aortic root. H&E staining was used to examine the necrotic areas of the plaques. To assess the impact of Col@PSVs on the stability of atherosclerotic plaques, Masson staining was performed on aortic root sections. Furthermore, Western blot analysis was conducted to determine the expression levels of inflammation-related proteins in mouse aortic tissues.

RNA-seq analysis

To explore the therapeutic mechanism of Col@PSVs, we performed RNA-seq analysis on cell samples from model group and treatment group. RNA-seq analysis Total RNA extraction from cells was isolated using the TRIzol reagent (thermofisher), and RNA were quality-controlled by NanoDrop ND-1000 (NanoDrop, USA). cDNA was synthesized from the fragmented RNA by using Invitrogen SuperScriptTM II Reverse Transcriptase (cat. 1,896,649, CA, USA). RNA sequencing was conducted utilizing theIllumina Novaseq X Plus platform (Repugene Technology, Hangzhou, China). The data will be available at https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo.

RNA-seq analysis identified gene loci and their associated signaling pathways potentially involved in AS disease progression. These results were further validated in both in vivo and in vitro models through qPCR, Western blot, immunofluorescence and Co-IP.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least three times, and the data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for the total number of experiments. All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9 Software. Comparison between the two groups was conducted by using Student’s t-test. The significance of the differences among groups’ means was subjected to one-way ANOVA analysis, followed by Tukey’s test. To examine the influence of two categorical independent variables on one continuous dependent variable, two-way ANOVA was used. p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
