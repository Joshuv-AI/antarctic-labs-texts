Retinal Vascular Alterations in Chronic Non-Arteritic Anterior Ischemic Optic Neuropathy: A Dynamic Vessel Analyzer Study
Authors: Ludovica Gargiulo, Gemma Tremolada, Alessandra Criscuoli, Diletta Santarsiero, Davide Soranna, Gabriella Cammarata, Lisa Melzi, Paola Ciasca, Paolo Milani, Carolina Lombardi, Stefania Bianchi Marzoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNon-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) is a leading cause of sudden visual loss in older adults, often associated with vascular risk factors. The aim of this study was to investigate the retinal microvascular changes in chronic NAION and their relationship with systemic vascular conditions and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). This retrospective observational study included 31 patients with chronic NAION (≥6 months from onset) and 17 age-matched healthy controls. Retinal microvascular parameters were assessed using the Dynamic Vessel Analyzer (DVA), including static and dynamic vessel metrics. Data were correlated with systemic vascular risk factors and polysomnographic findings. Arterial dilation amplitude was significantly reduced in eyes affected by chronic NAION compared to both contralateral unaffected eyes and healthy controls (p < .01). Static analysis revealed a significantly lower arteriovenous ratio (AVR) in both affected and unaffected eyes compared to controls. No significant differences were observed in venous parameters. Arterial diameter and dilation amplitude were positively correlated with ganglion cell layer thickness thickness and visual field sensitivity. OSA was diagnosed in 51% of patients, but no significant association was found between OSA and DVA parameters. In chronic NAION, DVA demonstrated impaired retinal arterial function, likely reflecting secondary effects of optic nerve damage. Retinal venous parameters remained unaffected. Cerebrovascular risk appeared more strongly associated with systemic vascular comorbidities than NAION itself. OSA, even in mild form, may contribute to disease severity.

Introduction

Nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) is a common cause of acute unilateral visual loss associated with disc edema, typically occurring in individuals over 50 years of age. The pathogenesis of NAION remains unproven but is widely believed to be multifactorial.1 A predominant hypothesis involves compromised circulation within the short posterior ciliary arteries in anatomically crowded disk (“disc at risk”).2 A venous etiology due to transient small vessel insufficiency at the optic nerve head has also been proposed, although it remains unsubstantiated.3 While the association between NAION and systemic vascular risk factors is biologically plausible, definitive evidence is still lacking.4–10 In contrast, a link with obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) has been reported in substantial proportion of patients.11–13 The potential association between NAION and cerebrovascular events-possibly inferred from retinal microvascular changes- remains controversial.

The aim of this study was to investigate retinal microvasculature alterations in patients with chronic NAION using the Dynamic Vessel Analyzer (DVA) and to explore correlations with systemic vascular risk factors and OSA.

Methods

Subjects

This retrospective study reviewed medical records of patients diagnosed with NAION at the Neuro-Ophthalmology Center, IRCCS Istituto Auxologico, Milano between 2013 and 2023. Inclusion criteria were: (1) diagnosis of chronic NAION defined as disease duration >6 months; and (2) Completion of polysomnography (3) Attendance at routine follow-up. Exclusion criteria included presence of other ocular diseases, increased intraocular pressure, prior ocular surgery or incomplete data. Finally, 31 patients with chronic NAION met the eligibility criteria. The control group comprised 17 healthy age-matched individuals who had undergone a full neuro-ophthalmological evaluation.

Measurements

All subjects underwent the following evaluations: Humphrey visual field examination (HFA, Zeiss, 30–2 SITA-Standard), spectral domain OCT (OptoVue RTVue or Heidelberg Spectralis) and retinal vessel analysis with DVA (Imedos Inc., Germany).

The DVA employs a modified fundus camera (Zeiss FF450) and video recording unit to analyze vessel diameter changes during three flicker light stimulation cycles over 350 seconds.14 Data obtained included arterial and venous diameters (measurement units, MU), arterial and venous dilation (% change from baseline), and constriction (%), with validity scores reported for each recording. Static analysis, based on 50° fundus images centered on the optic disc, measured the following parameters: Central Retinal Artery Equivalent (CRAE), Central Retinal Vein Equivalent (CRVE) and the Arteriovenous Ratio (AVR = CRAE/CRVE) using the Visualis and VesselMap software. Cerebrovascular risk classification (low, intermediate, or high) was derived from AVR values according to criteria from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study.15,16

Vascular risk factors included diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension and dyslipidemia. OSA was classified by Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) as mild (5–15), moderate (15–30) or severe (>30).

Data considered for the analysis included: Best Corrected Visual Acuity (BCVA, Snellen), Mean Deviation (MD, dB), ganglion cell layer thickenss (GCCT, μm), artery and vein diameter (MU), artery and vein dilation amplitude (%) and Arteriovenous ratio (AVR). GCCT values were derived from ganglion cell complex measurements obtained with the Optovue RTVue OCT and from ganglion cell thickness measurements obtained with the Heidelberg Spectralis OCT. Values were expressed as the percentage reduction compared with the normative database of the respective instrument.

Data from both eyes were considered independently and categorized as Pathological Eye (PE, with chronic NAION) and Non-Pathological Eye (Non-PE, contralateral eye)

Ethical approval was obtained by the local ethics committees (37C302- SONNOOCU) and all procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki. Written Informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD or median [IQR], depending on distribution. Comparisons between two groups used t-tests or Wilcoxon tests; for three or more groups, ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied, followed by Holm-Sidak adjusted pairwise comparisons. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman coefficients. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), with significance set at p < .05.

Results

The chronic NAION group included 18 males and 13 females (mean age 59 years). Median interval from symptom onset to measurement was 13.5 months [IQR 5–41]. Twelve patients (39%) had bilateral NAION, and 19 (61%) had unilateral involvement. PE subgroup included 43 eyes; Non-PE 19 eyes. The healthy control group included 7 males, 10 females (mean age 58 years).

OCT was performed using OptoVue in 18 patients and 10 healthy controls, and Heidelberg Spectralis in 13 and 7 respectively.

Dyslipidemia was present in 6 patients (19%), hypertension in 13 (42%), and diabetes in 2 (6%). OSA was diagnosed in 16 patients (51%) classified as mild in 9 cases (56%), moderate in 6 (37%), and severe in 1 (6%).

Discussion

This study employed the DVA, a tool primarily used in research settings, to assess retinal vascular function in patients with chronic NAION. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation applying DVA to this patient population, offering a novel approach to characterizing microvascular dysfunction in optic neuropathies.

By enabling both dynamic and static analyses, the DVA provides a comprehensive evaluation of retinal circulation. The dynamic component measures endothelial function via retinal vascular response to flicker light stimulation, while the static analysis yields structural indices such as the arterio-venous ratio (AVR), a parameter validated as a marker of cerebrovascular risk marker in large epidemiologic studies such as the ARIC study.15,16

Our findings revealed a significant reduction in arterial dilation amplitude in chronic NAION-affected eyes, suggesting impaired endothelial responsiveness. This dysfunction likely reflects a secondary phenomenon, due to inner retinal atrophy resulting from retrograde degeneration following optic nerve ischemia. This interpretation is supported by significant correlations between reduced arterial diameter and thinning of the ganglion cell layer on OCT, as well as with visual field loss. These findings support the hypothesis that chronic NAION may lead to persistent, localized endothelial dysfunction in the retina, driven by structural neuronal loss.

In contrast, venous parameters did not differ significantly between patients and healthy controls, challenging the hypothesis of a venous contribution in chronic disease stage, and instead suggesting a predominantly arterial microvascular impairment.

Importantly, a significantly reduced AVR was observed not only in affected eyes but also in contralateral, unaffected eyes of patients with unilateral NAION. This bilateral involvement suggests a systemic predisposition to microvascular dysfunction, even in the absence of clinically apparent optic nerve damage. To our knowledge, such subclinical alterations have not previously been described in NAION and may point toward a broader vascular endophenotype in this patient group.

The relationship between NAION and cerebrovascular risk remains debate. Chu et al.17 reported a higher incidence of stroke in NAION patients, independent of comorbidities, whereas Foster et al.18 found no such association, attributing stroke risk to shared systemic vascular risk factors rather than to NAION itself. In our cohort, most patients exhibited a stroke risk profile based on DVA derived parameters, supporting the hypothesis that NAION may serve as a clinical marker of systemic microangiopathy rather than a direct cause of cerebrovascular events. This interpretation aligns with findings by Han et al.19 who demonstrated an association between NAION and cerebral small vessel disease.

OSA has been strongly linked to NAION, with prevalence rates up to 75% reported by Aptel et al.12 In our cohort, OSA was diagnosed in 51% of patients, confirming its frequent co-occurrence. However, we did not observe significant associations between OSA and DVA-derived vascular parameters. This contrast with findings by Tong et al.20 who reported reduced retinal arterial dilation with increasing OSA severity. The different distribution of OSA severity, which in most of our cases was mild (56%) may have masked the stronger correlation observed in cohorts with severe OSA. Nonetheless, we observed a consistent trend toward a greater ganglion cell layer thinning and more pronounced visual field loss in OSA-positive patients. This suggests that even less severe forms of OSA may contribute to optic nerve damage in NAION, potentially through mechanisms beyond nocturnal hypotension, such as chronic intermittent hypoxia. These findings challenge the notion that OSA contributes to NAION solely via impaired vascular autoregulation at the optic nerve head. Anatomical predisposition remains a critical factor, with a small, crowded optic disc, the so called “disc at risk, identified in 77% of our patients.

From a clinical perspective, our findings raise important considerations: endothelial dysfunction detected by DVA may serve as a biomarker of chronic ischemic retinal damage; contralateral AVR reduction may help to identify patients at risk before clinical NAION develops; routine screening for OSA, even in mild forms, may warranted in patients with NAION or disc at risk morphology.

This study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size may have limited the power to detect subtle associations. In addition, the use of two different OCT platforms, despite normalization strategies, may have introduced variability in ganglion cell layer thickness measurements. Future studies should include larger, multicenter cohorts and standardized imaging protocols to further elucidate the relationship among retinal vascular alterations, systemic vascular risk, and NAION pathophysiology.

Conclusions

This study provides novel evidence of retinal arterial endothelial dysfunction in patients with chronic NAION, as assessed by the DVA. The absence of venous abnormalities and the presence of bilateral AVR reduction suggest that NAION may reflect an underlying, systemic arterial dysregulation rather than an isolated ocular event. While the disease itself does not appear to confer additional cerebrovascular risk, the frequent co-occurrence of OSA, along with a trend toward more severe structural and functional damage, highlights its potential role as an aggravating factor. These findings underscore the importance of integrating retinal vascular analysis and sleep disorder screening into the clinical management of patients with NAION and “disc at risk” morphology.
