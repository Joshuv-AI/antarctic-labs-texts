Pseudomagnetic focusing of valley-polarized beams in strained graphene
Alina Mreńca-Kolasińska, Christophe De Beule, Jia-Tong Shi (bsmi施佳彤), Aitor Garcia-Ruiz (bsmi艾飛宇), Denis Kochan (bsmi可汗丹), Klaus Richter, Ming-Hao Liu (bsmi劉明豪)

Abstract
In addition to spin, the low-energy charge carriers in graphene have two inequivalent valley flavors 𝐊 \mathbf{K} and 𝐊 ′ \mathbf{K}^{\prime} related by time-reversal symmetry, which can potentially serve as carriers for additional information. One promising route of controlling the valley degree of freedom relies on strain-induced pseudomagnetic fields, which couple with opposite signs to the two valleys. In this work, we theoretically investigate quantum transport in strained graphene devices that host tailored pseudomagnetic field profiles for valley manipulation. We focus on experimentally feasible geometries and propose transverse pseudomagnetic focusing in a bent graphene ribbon as an efficient method for generating valley-polarized currents, even in presence of disorder. Our work highlights pseudomagneto-transport in graphene as a viable tool for valleytronic applications.

In addition, we also consider an S-shaped ribbon that results in a pseudomagnetic domain wall, leading to the formation of valley-chiral snake states. Both proposals display characteristic conductance oscillations that can be measured experimentally.

II.1
The model

Throughout this paper, we model the electronic properties of graphene with the tight-binding Hamiltonian describing electrons hopping between nearest-neighbor carbon
p_{z}
orbitals with amplitude
t_{mn}
in the presence of an onsite potential
U_{n}
due to applied gate voltages,

\hat{H}=-\sum\limits_{\left\langle{m,n}\right\rangle}t_{mn}\hat{c}_{m}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{n}+\sum\limits_{n}U_{n}\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{n},

(1)

where
\hat{c}_{n}^{\dagger}
(
\hat{c}_{n}
) are operators that create (destroy) an electron on the
n
-th site. In the presence of in-plane deformations, the original position of the
n
-th site
\mathbf{r}_{n}=(x_{n},y_{n})
becomes
\mathbf{R}_{n}=\mathbf{r}_{n}+\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{r}_{n})
where
\mathbf{u}(x,y)
is the in-plane (acoustic) displacement field
[
60
]
. These lattice deformations couple to the charge carriers by modifying the hopping amplitudes. Working in the central-force approximation, we use a phenomenological hopping function given by
[
50
]

t_{mn}=t_{0}\exp\left[-\beta\left(\frac{|\mathbf{R}_{m}-\mathbf{R}_{n}|}{a_{0}}-1\right)\right],

(2)

where
t_{0}=3\mathord{\thinspace\rm eV}
,
a_{0}=(4\sqrt{3})^{-1}\mathord{\thinspace\rm nm}
is the nearest-neighbor distance of pristine graphene, and we take
\beta=3.37
.

To make the transport simulations for mesoscopic strained graphene devices computationally feasible, we use the scaling method for unstrained graphene from Ref.
[
32
]
and its generalization to account for strain
[
31
]
. In this way, we can reduce the Hamiltonian matrix size, while leaving the long-wavelength physics near the charge neutrality point, that we are interested in, unchanged. Specifically, the generalized scaling transformation consists of properly scaling the lattice constant, hopping amplitude, and the displacement field, resulting in the following scaling laws:

\displaystyle a_{0}\rightarrow sa_{0}\ ,

\displaystyle t_{0}\rightarrow t_{0}/s\ ,

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}\rightarrow s\mathbf{u}\ ,

(3)

which leave the low-energy physics invariant as long as the scaled strain remains in the elastic regime
[
31
]
. Here, we use continuum elasticity to model the strain. Although this only accounts for acoustic displacements, the optical component describing relative motion between different sublattices is effectively included by a reduction factor that renormalizes the electron Grüneisen parameter
\beta

[
61
,
56
,
11
]
. Finally, in our quantum transport calculations, the main object of interest is the conductance from lead
l
to lead
k
, which is obtained from the Landauer formula
G_{kl}=2(e^{2}/h)T_{kl}
where the transmission function
T_{kl}
is calculated numerically with the wave-function-matching method within the linear response regime in the limit of zero temperature
[
68
,
26
]
.

Transverse pseudomagnetic focusing

So far, we have considered systems that realize TPMF for a uniform PMF. We have also investigated the influence of small deviations from uniformity, which can occur for other strain profiles. As an example, we consider triaxially-strained graphene (see SI for details
[
1
]
). Similarly as before, the local current densities maps reveal clear pseudomagnetic focusing of carriers, separated into two beams belonging to opposite valleys, which generate a valley-polarized beam that reaches the collector. Furthermore, at lower densities, the system is in the pseudo-Hall regime, and the current densities show two valley-chiral branches of edge states. Transport in the presence of a pseudomagnetic field is then compared with results obtained for a real magnetic field and without strain, where the edge current propagates in the same direction for both valleys. We conclude that as long as the local pseudomagnetic orbit does not change significantly along the focusing trajectory, deviations from a uniform PMF do not compromise the valley-polarized focusing current.

II.3
Pseudomagnetic snake states

In addition to TPMF, which requires a nearly uniform PMF, we also consider devices with spatially-varying PMF profiles. As an example, we perform two-terminal transport simulations for S-bent ribbons. This setup gives rise to a pseudomagnetic domain wall where the PMF changes sign along the transport direction. Pseudomagnetic snake states localized at the interface
[
34
,
51
,
57
]
then give rise to characteristic conductance oscillations. To achieve a highly non-uniform PMF in practice, we consider a spatially-varying bending strength by letting
u_{0}\rightarrow-u_{0}\tanh[x/(2d_{*})]
[see Eq. (
S1
)]. This smoothly interpolates between bending the ribbon inwards and outwards, resulting in the deformation and PMF shown in Fig.
4
(a). Hence, the PMF changes sign at

. In analogy to a bipolar junction in graphene in a uniform real magnetic field
[
34
,
51
,
57
]
, the reversal of the PMF for a uniform carrier density gives rise to a snake state bound at the interface. The difference is that the snake states arise here due to the alternating PMF, rather than the sign change of the quasiparticle charge.

II.4
Conclusions

In summary, we demonstrated how valley-polarized currents can be achieved by transverse pseudomagnetic focusing in a multi-terminal device made from a bent graphene ribbon. This setup is based on the valley-chiral skipping orbits which results in a spatial separation of valley-polarized beams. Hence, it is robust against moderately strong disorder. Although preferentially, the bending should be in the zigzag orientation which coincides with the
x
direction for our choice of coordinates to achieve a uniform pseudomagnetic field, as long as the local pseudomagnetic cyclotron radius does not vary significantly on the scale of the spacing between the injector and collector, the functionality of the system is not compromised by small deviations from a uniform pseudomagnetic field. This allows for more realistic deformation profiles that deviate from the fine-tuned displacement field (
S1
) for circular-arc bending
[
15
]
. Finally, we identified a fingerprint of pseudomagnetic snake states, localized at a domain wall where the pseudomagnetic field changes sign, in conductance oscillations for quantum transport across an S-shaped zigzag graphene ribbon.

Recent progress in engineering the strain profile in graphene systems
[
24
,
49
,
36
,
66
]
complemented by extensive theoretical predictions
[
8
]
may facilitate future experimental demonstration of our findings. Our work opens new avenues for applications of valley-based information processing in graphene straintronic devices.

S6
Role of edge roughness and scaling

Edge roughness is highly relevant to our proposed device due to its ability to induce intervalley scattering. To address the issue of the edge disorder, we performed several simulations in the armchair geometry shown in Fig. 2(e) of the main text, where atomic sites near the right boundary of the system were randomly removed with a probability of 0.4. We compared the effect of such roughness for the scaling factors
s=1,2,4
. The results are shown in Fig.
S6
. As a consequence of the edge disorder, the
\mathbf{K}
(
\mathbf{K^{\prime}}
) beam hitting the edge between the leads is not completely reflected to
\mathbf{K^{\prime}}
(
\mathbf{K}
) valley. Instead, scattering from the edge partially mixes valleys, so there is nonzero conductance beyond the first peak.
The second focusing peak becomes visible, albeit somewhat broadened due to the loss of specular reflection caused by the edge roughness.

Note that edge roughness on the lower (zigzag) edge can be detrimental to valley polarization since the beams reflected from
\mathbf{K}
to
\mathbf{K^{\prime}}
or vice versa may reach the collector lead. This can be avoided by placing a current drain at the lower side of the sample.

Here, we can also comment on the role of the scaling factor
s
. One must note that the effect of removing edge atoms varies in the scaled and unscaled lattices, therefore specific fluctuations vary for each
s
.
Nevertheless, the scaling factor does not affect the overall high valley polarization. This is because the pseudomagnetic field
B_{s}
steers the electrons belonging to the two valleys in the opposite directions.
