Convergence of spectral discretization for the flow of diffeomorphisms
Benedikt Wirth

Abstract
The Large Deformation Diffeomorphic Metric Mapping (LDDMM) or flow of diffeomorphism is a classical framework in the field of shape spaces and is widely applied in mathematical imaging and computational anatomy. Essentially, it equips a group of diffeomorphisms with a right-invariant Riemannian metric, which allows to compute (Riemannian) distances or interpolations between different deformations. The associated Euler–Lagrange equation of shortest interpolation paths is one of the standard examples of a partial differential equation that can be approached with Lie group theory (by interpreting it as a geodesic ordinary differential equation on the Lie group of diffeomorphisms). The particular group 𝒟 m \mathcal{D}^{m} of Sobolev diffeomorphisms is by now sufficiently understood to allow the analysis of geodesics and their numerical approximation. We prove convergence of a widely used Fourier-type space discretization of the geodesic equation. It is based on a regularity estimate, for which we also provide a new proof: Geodesics in 𝒟 m \mathcal{D}^{m} preserve any higher order Sobolev regularity of their initial velocity.

Introduction and main results

There are a number of partial differential equations (PDEs) that can be interpreted as a geodesic equation (the Euler–Lagrange equation satisfied by locally shortest paths) on an infinite-dimensional Lie group.
This viewpoint started with the seminal work by Arnol’d in the 1960s on hydrodynamics
[
1
]
,
who for instance interpreted the Euler equations of inviscid incompressible fluid flow as the geodesic equations on the Lie group of volume-preserving diffeomorphisms endowed with a right-invariant
L^{2}
-metric.
The great advantage of such a viewpoint is that it often makes these PDEs amenable to an analysis via ordinary differential equation (ODE) techniques
(corresponding sufficient conditions are e.g. given in
[
18
]
).
It is also exploited for numerics, e.g. by devising efficient, structure-preserving solvers based on Hamiltonian system integrators.
Other examples of PDEs that fit into this framework include Burgers’ equation, the Camassa-Holm equation, and the KdV equation, see
[
24
,
16
,
18
]
and the references therein.

Yet another classical prototype example is the so-called EPDiff equation

\dot{\rho}_{t}=-(\mathrm{div}(\rho_{t}\otimes v_{t})+(Dv_{t})^{T}\rho_{t})\qquad\text{with }v_{t}=\mathcal{R}\rho_{t}=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\rho_{t}

(1)

for
\mathcal{L}
a self-adjoint differential operator such as
\mathcal{L}=(1-\Delta)^{m}
with
m\geq 0
(
t
denotes time, the dot time differentiation, and
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{L}^{-1}
).
The close similarity to the Euler equations becomes apparent for
m=0
, in which case the equation reads
\dot{v}_{t}+\mathrm{div}(v_{t}\otimes v_{t})+\nabla\frac{|v_{t}|^{2}}{2}=0

(the incompressible Euler equations just differ by the additional incompressibility constraint
\mathrm{div}v_{t}=0
and the replacement of the internal energy
\frac{|v_{t}|^{2}}{2}
by the pressure
p
).
This EPDiff equation occurs as the Euler–Lagrange equation when trying to deform (or rather transport) a given image into another one by a time-dependent velocity field
v_{t}

with least possible energy
\int_{0}^{1}\langle\mathcal{L}v_{t},v_{t}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t
.
Therefore it is used a lot in computational anatomy, where medical images of a patient have to be mapped to an annotated template image
and where this framework for dealing with deformations is known as Large Deformation Diffeomorphic Metric Mapping (LDDMM).

This EPDiff equation actually turns out to be the geodesic equation on the group
\mathcal{D}^{m}
of diffeomorphisms (e.g. of the unit cube) of Sobolev regularity
m
, endowed with a right-invariant Riemannian Sobolev metric:
A geodesic (i.e. a locally shortest path)
t\mapsto\phi_{t}
of diffeomorphisms can be written as the so-called flow of a velocity field
v_{t}
, i.e. as solution of

\dot{\phi}_{t}=v_{t}\circ\phi_{t}

(in the language of fluid mechanics,
\phi_{t}
describes the motion in Lagrangian coordinates, while
v_{t}
is the Eulerian description of the motion),
and this velocity field
v_{t}
satisfies the EPDiff equation (where
\mathcal{L}
is related to the employed Riemannian metric
g
via
g_{\mathrm{id}}(v,w)=\langle\mathcal{L}v,w\rangle
).

The EPDiff equation represents a typical model setting for applying Lie group techniques to PDEs, see e.g.
[
28
,
24
]
.
One of the reasons is that by now the group of Sobolev diffeomorphisms and its geometric properties are quite well understood
[
24
,
7
,
12
]

and that the numerical implementation is straightforward.
In particular, (
1
) lends itself to a space discretization by truncated Fourier series:
For instance, the authors of
[
30
]
propose a scheme on the
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
in which
\rho_{t}
and
v_{t}
are approximated by bandlimited functions
P_{t}
and
V_{t}
(numerically represented by their finite Fourier series)
and both right-hand sides in (
1
) are essentially just truncated in Fourier space to satisfy the band limit, yielding a highly efficient code.
Their intuition is that the smoothing operator
\mathcal{R}
produces an effective bandlimit anyway.

The aim of this article is to prove that the above numerical space discretization from
[
30
]
converges if the initial data is regular enough:
We will show in
theorem

27
a more detailed version of the following.

Theorem 1

(Convergence of bandlimited EPDiff equation)
.

Let
m>1+\frac{d}{2}
and the metric on
\mathcal{D}^{m}
be induced by a Fourier multiplier
\mathcal{L}
.
If the initial velocity
v_{0}
has Sobolev regularity
m+k
for
k\geq 1
,
the numerical approximation of bandlimiting the right-hand sides in (
1
) converges to the true solution as the bandlimit
R
tends to infinity.
Moreover, for
k\geq 2
the error tends to zero at rate
R^{1-k}
.

(Note that numerically the convergence order seems to be better by one, so generically the convergence might be better.)
This shows that the earlier-mentioned intuition may be only partly correct: The smoothing operator
\mathcal{R}
alone might not produce a strong enough bandlimiting for the approximation to converge, but an additional, stronger smoothing of the initial condition might potentially be required.
(In fact,
[
30
]
additionally replaces differentiation by finite differences, but this only slightly simplifies the implementation.)
Following the theme that the underlying Lie group structure allows to use ODE techniques, the convergence is proven via Gronwall’s inequality.
This is made possible by a regularity result for geodesics in

,
sometimes known as “no-loss-no-gain (of regularity)”

[

[

In

15

Theorem 2

(Sobolev regularity preservation along geodesics)
.

Let
m>1+\frac{d}{2}
and the metric on
\mathcal{D}^{m}
be strong, twice differentiable, and induced by some
\mathcal{L}=B^{*}B+\bar{\mathcal{L}}
,
where

is continuous from

If the initial velocity

This result builds upon the knowledge acquired during recent years on

in particular the well-posedness of geodesics, the existence of shortest geodesics, and the rigorous derivation of the geodesic equation
[

7

12

For smooth Riemannian metrics it can be derived from the right-invariance of the metric and the resulting Riemannian exponential map:
Indeed, via the right-invariance, (higher order) differentiability of the exponential can be traded in for preservation of (higher order) Sobolev exponents

4

[

, Sec. 5.2]

However, that argument does not exploit that the derivatives of the exponential (such as

Our result

Zhang and Fletcher viewed their numerical approximation in
[

We will briefly discuss why an approximation via a Lie algebra of bandlimited functions with the original Lie bracket cannot exist.

None of the shown analysis applies to these settings, since almost nothing is so far known about the geometry of the associated group of diffeomorphisms:
Is it a manifold, and what (Banach) space

2
The Riemannian manifold of Sobolev diffeomorphisms

In this section we introduce all necessary notions and summarize what is known about the Riemannian manifold of Sobolev diffeomorphisms.
We consider diffeomorphisms on the
d
-dimensional flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, since the spectral discretization is devised for this setting.
However, statements analogous to the ones of this section also apply to other domains such as
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or smooth compact manifolds (in the latter case one has to work with charts).

Definition 3

(Group of Sobolev diffeomorphisms)
.

Let
m>\frac{d}{2}+1
.
The group of
Sobolev diffeomorphisms
(on the torus) of Sobolev regularity
m
is

\mathcal{D}^{m}=\{\phi\in H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{T}^{d})\,|\,\phi^{-1}\in H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{T}^{d}),\,\phi\text{ preserves orientation}\}

with group product
(\phi,\psi)\mapsto\phi\circ\psi
.
The subgroup formed by the connected component (in
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{T}^{d})
) of the identity is denoted
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
.

That
\mathcal{D}^{m}
actually forms a group follows from the regularity estimate

\|\psi\circ\phi\|_{H^{s}}\leq C\|\psi\|_{H^{s}}\qquad\text{for }0\leq s\leq m\text{ and all }\psi\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

(2)

with the constant
C
depending on
\phi\in\mathcal{D}^{m}

[
15
, Lemma 2.7]
.
Due to the continuous Sobolev embedding
H^{m}\hookrightarrow C^{1}
for
m>\frac{d}{2}+1
, the group
\mathcal{D}^{m}
is a subgroup of

\mathrm{Diff}^{1}=\{\phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{T}^{d})\,|\,\phi^{-1}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{T}^{d}),\,\phi\text{ preserves orientation}\},

the group of
C^{1}
diffeomorphisms of the torus.

We will only be concerned with
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
;
statements about other connected components
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{D}^{m}
(which do not form subgroups, though, for lack of the identity)
follow via the identification
\mathcal{C}=\phi\circ\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
for an arbitrary
\phi\in\mathcal{C}
.
At least in low dimensions, the decomposition into connected components is understood:
For
d=2
the quotient of the group under identifying elements from the same connected component, the so-called mapping class group of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
,
is given by
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})

[
11
, Thm. 2.5]
(in fact, this pertains from the group of homeomorphisms to the group of
C^{\infty}
-diffeomorphisms
[
11
, § 1.4]
).
For
d=3
the mapping class group for homeomorphisms on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
(and likely also for diffeomorphisms) is
\mathrm{SL}(3,\mathbb{Z})

[
14
]
;
for
d=4
little seems to be known, and for
d\geq 5
the mapping class groups are more complicated and also depend on whether homeomorphisms or diffeomorphisms are considered
[
13
, Thm. 4.1]
.

It is well-known that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
is a differential (
C^{\infty}
) manifold modelled over the Hilbert space
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})

[
24
]
.
In fact, this can be seen by interpreting
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
as the quotient space
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}=\{\phi:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\,|\,\phi-\mathrm{id}\in H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}),\,\det D\phi>0\}

upon which the group
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
acts by
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}\ni(z,\phi)\mapsto T_{z}\phi\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
with
(T_{z}\phi)(x)=\phi(x)+z
.
Essentially, taking the quotient just identifies the codomain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\mathbb{T}^{d}
by taking the modulus with respect to one.
Now due to
H^{m}\hookrightarrow C^{1}
, the set
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}-\mathrm{id}
is an open subset of
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and thus a differential (
C^{\infty}
) manifold modelled over that space.
Therefore, the quotient
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}/\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a smooth submersion by the Quotient Manifold Theorem;
it is actually even a smooth covering map
[
21
, Thm. 9.19]
so that
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
is a differential manifold modelled over the same Hilbert space.
The submersion is given by the map

\pi:\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}\to\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m},\ \pi(\phi)=p\circ\phi\quad\text{with}\quad p:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{T}^{d},\,p(x)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_{1}\text{ mod }1\\
\vdots\\
x_{d}\text{ mod }1\end{smallmatrix}\right).

The tangent space to
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
at any
\phi\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
is obviously given by

T_{\phi}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}=H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}).

From now on, we will denote by
\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}}
for
\phi\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
the smallest
H^{m}
-norm along its fibre,

\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}}=\min_{\psi\in\pi^{-1}(\phi)}\|\psi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}},

thus essentially we implicitly identify a Sobolev diffeomorphism
\phi
with an element of
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
.
Similarly, for any
\phi\in\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
and
f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
we will write

f\circ\phi\quad\text{for}\quad f\circ\pi(\phi).\qquad\text{and}\qquad f\circ\phi^{-1}\quad\text{for}\quad f\circ\pi(\phi)^{-1}.

Since
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
(and thus also
\mathcal{D}^{m}
) is a group as well as a manifold, it behaves in some aspects like a Lie group and is often formally treated like one.
However, inversion and left-multiplication are not smooth (not even differentiable), which is typically required for a Lie group
(such groups have been termed
half-Lie groups
in
[
19
,
22
]
).
In fact, denoting the group of homeomorphisms and
C^{\infty}
-diffeomorphisms on the torus by
\mathrm{Hom}
and
\mathrm{Diff}^{\infty}
, respectively,
by
[
6
, III, Exerc. §4, ¶7]
there cannot exist a Banach Lie group
G

such that
\mathrm{Diff}^{\infty}\subset G\subset\mathrm{Hom}
and the inclusions
\mathrm{Diff}^{\infty}\hookrightarrow G\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Hom}
are continuous homomorphisms.
In more detail, one can construct diffeomorphisms
\phi\in\mathrm{Diff}^{\infty}
that are arbitrarily close to
\mathrm{id}
, but do not belong to any one-parameter subgroup of
\mathrm{Diff}^{\infty}
;
thus they do not belong to a one-parameter subgroup of
G
either.
This, however, contradicts the local surjectivity of the Lie exponential map in Banach Lie groups.

The manifold
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
is equipped with a right-invariant Riemannian metric.

Definition 4

(Right-invariant Sobolev metric)
.

Let
(\!(\cdot,\cdot)\!)_{H^{m}}
be an inner product of
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
with induced norm
\vvvert\cdot\vvvert_{H^{m}}
equivalent to the standard
H^{m}
-norm, then

g_{\phi}(v,w)=(\!(v\circ\phi^{-1},w\circ\phi^{-1})\!)_{H^{m}}\qquad\text{for }\phi\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m},\,v,w\in T_{\phi}\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}

(3)

is the induced
right-invariant Riemannian Sobolev metric
on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
.
2
2

2

Note that in contrast to most standard textbooks on Riemannian geometry
we do not impose any smoothness requirements on a Riemannian metric, but simply take it to be a collection of inner products – one on each tangent space to the Riemannian manifold.
Depending on the choice of
(\!(\cdot,\cdot)\!)_{H^{m}}
, (
3
) will of course entail
some
regularity of the map
\phi\mapsto g_{\phi}
, but this is ignored for now.

The right-invariance
g_{\phi\circ\psi}(v\circ\psi,w\circ\psi)=g_{\phi}(v,w)
follows from the definition, the well-definedness of the metric follows from (
2
).
The metric is strong in the sense that it induces the topology of the tangent space;
sometimes also weak metrics (inducing a weaker topology) are considered in the literature, which we will come back to in
remark

17
.
We will denote the Riesz isomorphism associated with
(\!(\cdot,\cdot)\!)_{H^{m}}
by

\mathcal{R}:H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\to H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\qquad\text{with inverse }\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{R}^{-1}.

It is defined via the relation
\langle v,w\rangle=(\!(\mathcal{R}v,w)\!)_{H^{m}}
for all
v\in H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
w\in H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
and
\mathcal{L}
typically is a differential operator, e.g.
\mathcal{L}=(1-\Delta)^{m}
.

Any Riemannian metric induces a path energy and Riemannian distance.

Definition 5

(Path energy and Riemannian distance on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
)
.

The
Riemannian distance

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}}
on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
induced by the right-invariant Riemannian Sobolev metric is given by

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}}(\chi,\psi)^{2}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:[0,1]\to\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}\\
\phi_{0}=\chi,\,\phi_{1}=\psi\end{subarray}}E[\phi]

for the
path energy

E[\phi]=\int_{0}^{1}g_{\phi_{t}}(\dot{\phi}_{t},\dot{\phi}_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{1}(\!(\dot{\phi}_{t}\circ\phi_{t}^{-1},\dot{\phi}_{t}\circ\phi_{t}^{-1})\!)_{H^{m}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

It is a standard argument that the path energy
E
is an upper bound for the squared path length (with equality on paths that have unit speed)
[
17
, Prop. 1.8.7]
,
which is why
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}}
is a length metric.
Shortest connecting paths, i.e. shortest geodesics between two elements
\phi_{0},\phi_{1}\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
, are paths
\phi:[0,1]\to\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
that minimize
E[\phi]

among all paths with same end points
\phi_{0},\phi_{1}
.
Obviously, an equivalent characterization of geodesics is as solutions of the constrained problem

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:[0,1]\to\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}\\
v:[0,1]\to H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{1}(\!(v_{t},v_{t})\!)_{H^{m}}\,\mathrm{d}t\quad\text{such that }\dot{\phi}_{t}=v_{t}\circ\phi_{t}.

Definition 6

(Eulerian velocity and flow)
.

Let
\phi:[0,1]\to\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
,
v:[0,1]\to H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfy

\dot{\phi}_{t}=v_{t}\circ\phi_{t},

(4)

then the (time-dependent) vector field
v
is the
Eulerian velocity
field of the path
\phi
, while
\phi
is the
flow
of
v
.

The geodesic equation is the optimality condition for the minimization of
E
and thus a second order ODE on the manifold
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
(which is a PDE on
[0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}
).
On honest Lie groups with right-invariant metric, the geodesic ODE is invariant under right action of the group on itself so that by Noether’s Theorem it can be reduced to a first order ODE.
One can apply the same argument to formally derive such a first order geodesic equation on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
:
Let
\phi
be a geodesic path with velocity
v
and let
\eta\in C^{\infty}([0,1]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
be an infinitesimal perturbation with
\eta_{0}=\eta_{1}=0
.
Defining
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{t}=(\mathrm{id}+\varepsilon\eta_{t})\circ\phi_{t}
,
its velocity is given by
v^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\dot{\phi}^{\varepsilon}_{t}\circ(\phi^{\varepsilon}_{t})^{-1}=\left[(\mathrm{I}+\varepsilon D\eta_{t})v_{t}+\varepsilon\dot{\eta}_{t}\right]\circ(\mathrm{id}+\varepsilon\eta_{t})^{-1}
.
Assuming sufficient differentiability, the optimality conditions read

0=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}E[\phi^{\varepsilon}]\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{1}(\!(v^{\varepsilon}_{t},v^{\varepsilon}_{t})\!)_{H^{m}}\,\mathrm{d}t\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=\left.2\int_{0}^{1}\langle\mathcal{L}v^{\varepsilon}_{t},\tfrac{\mathrm{d}v^{\varepsilon}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}\varepsilon}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\\
=2\int_{0}^{1}\langle\mathcal{L}v_{t},D\eta_{t}v_{t}+\dot{\eta}_{t}-Dv_{t}\eta_{t}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t=2\int_{0}^{1}\langle\mathcal{L}v_{t},[v_{t},\eta_{t}]+\dot{\eta}_{t}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

(5)

for the Lie bracket

[v,w]=(Dw)v-(Dv)w.

The Lie bracket is usually written in terms of the adjoint representation
\mathrm{ad}_{v}w
:
Differentiating the conjugation isomorphism
\iota_{\phi}:\psi\mapsto\phi\circ\psi\circ\phi^{-1}
at
\psi=\mathrm{id}
yields the so-called adjoint map

\mathrm{Ad}_{\phi}=\partial_{\psi}\iota_{\phi}|_{\psi=\mathrm{id}}\qquad\text{with }\mathrm{Ad}_{\phi}w=(D\phi\,w)\circ\phi^{-1},

whose derivative with respect to
\phi
is known as the (Lie algebra) adjoint representation

\mathrm{ad}_{v}w=\partial_{\phi}\mathrm{Ad}_{\phi}(v)w|_{\phi=\mathrm{id}}=-[v,w].

Thus, after an integration by parts and applying the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations to (
5
) we arrive at the formal geodesic ODE

\dot{\rho}_{t}=-\mathrm{ad}_{v_{t}}^{*}\rho_{t}=-(\mathrm{div}(\rho_{t}\otimes v_{t})+(Dv_{t})^{T}\rho_{t})\qquad\text{for the momentum }\rho_{t}=\mathcal{L}v_{t}.

(6)

This first order ODE is known as the EPDiff or Euler–Poincaré equation
[
28
]
;
its integral yields
\rho
or equivalently
v
, from which then the geodesic path
\phi
can be calculated as the flow.
Note that the derivation was merely formal since along the way we illegally differentiated the group product of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}

(e.g.
\mathrm{Ad}_{\phi}
with
\phi\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
maps
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
only into
H^{m-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
).
As a consequence, due to
v_{t}\in H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\rho_{t}\in H^{-m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the right-hand side of (
6
) only lies in
H^{-m-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and the equation may not be well-defined.

With these preliminaries it turns out that for
m>\frac{d}{2}+1

1.

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
is a topological group (this can be checked directly, exploiting that the constant in (
2
) only depends on
\min_{x}\det D\phi(x)
and
\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}}
),

2.

the flow
\phi
(starting from
\phi_{0}=\mathrm{id}
) of any velocity
v\in L^{1}([0,1];H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}))
lies in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}

[
7
]
,

3.

paths of finite energy in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
exist between any two elements of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}

(since
\mathcal{D}^{m}
is open and locally connected in
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, its connected components, in particular
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
, are open; now connected open subsets of normed spaces are polygonally connected, and a polygon in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
, consisting of linear interpolation segments

, has finite path energy),

4.

shortest geodesics in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
exist between any two elements of
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}

[
29
]

(Trouvé showed this for the set of diffeomorphisms reachable via a path of finite energy, which by the previous two points turns out to be
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
),

5.

\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
with Riemannian distance
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}}
is complete as a metric space
[
29
]
,

6.

for the inner product
(\!(v,w)\!)_{H^{m}}=\langle\mathcal{L}v,w\rangle
with
\mathcal{L}=(1-\Delta)^{m}

or a more general differential operator of order
2m
with smooth coefficients the metric is smooth
[
24
, Thm. 4.1]
(this even holds for any elliptic invertible operator
\mathcal{L}\in\mathrm{OPS}_{1,0}^{2m}
of order
2m

[
2
, Thm. 4.16 & Thm. 5.1]
, see
[
10
,
3
]
for the Fourier multiplier case),
thus the geodesic ODE is locally uniquely solvable by classical differential geometry and ODE theory
[
17
, § 1.6]
,
its solution depends smoothly on the initial conditions,
and by the Inverse Function Theorem the Riemannian exponential map is a local diffeomorphism,

7.

for the same inner product,
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
is geodesically complete, i.e. geodesics can be extended for all times
(this is a consequence of the previous two points by
[
20
, Prop. 6.5]
),

8.

for an inner product
(\!(v,w)\!)_{H^{m}}=\langle\mathcal{L}v,w\rangle
with
\mathcal{L}=B^{*}B+\bar{\mathcal{L}}

for

a differential operator of order

with bounded coefficients and compact self-adjoint

, continuous into

,
geodesics satisfy a weak PDE, whose strong form is (

)
[

]
(cf.

).

In summary, with an appropriate inner product
(\!(\cdot,\cdot)\!)_{H^{m}}
, the group
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
is a metrically and geodesically complete Riemannian manifold
in which shortest geodesics between any two elements exist, which satisfy a weak PDE, whose strong form is (

).

3
Refined and new regularity estimates

In this section we give a regularity result for geodesics,
theorem

15
, that allows to prove convergence of the spectral space discretization.
Furthermore, in order to be able to provide the explicit dependence of the final error estimates on the initial condition
v_{0}
of the geodesic,
we revisit and refine in
lemmas

9
and
10
a few known regularity estimates for operations with Sobolev diffeomorphisms.
For completeness we also provide in
lemma

12
a sufficient condition for
l
-fold differentiability of the Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
,
since our convergence results require two- and threefold differentiability.
Again the results and proofs can readily be adapted from the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
to other domains such as
\mathbb{R}^{d}
or smooth compact manifolds.

We first recall a continuity result from
[
5
]
(there given for general Lipschitz domains and integrability exponents) for pointwise multiplication of Sobolev functions that we will frequently make use of.

Lemma 7

(Continuity of multiplication,
[
5
, Cor. 6.3, Thm. 7.4]
)
.

Let
0\leq s\leq r,m
with
r+m>s+\frac{d}{2}
, then pointwise multiplication is a continuous bilinear map
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times H^{r}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\to H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

We begin with an estimate for the composition with a small deformation (iterations of which will then yield estimates for general diffeomorphisms),
which refines the following statement from
[
15
]
(given there on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
instead of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, but the proof carries over).

Lemma 8

(Continuity of composition,
[
15
, Lemma 2.7]
)
.

Let
m>\frac{d}{2}+1
and
0\leq s\leq m
.
Then
H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\times\mathcal{D}^{m}\ni(f,\phi)\mapsto f\circ\phi\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is continuous.
Moreover, for any
m,M>0
there exists
C>0
such that
\det D\phi\geq m
and
\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}}\leq M
for
\phi\in\mathcal{D}^{m}
imply

\|f\circ\phi\|_{H^{s}}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{s}}\qquad\text{for all }f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

Our refinement consists in the precise control of the involved constant in terms of how close the diffeomorphism is to the identity.
Below we will abbreviate the closed norm ball in
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
of radius
\varepsilon>0
by

\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}=\{\psi\in H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\,|\,\|\psi\|_{H^{m}}\leq\varepsilon\}.

Lemma 9

(Regularity of composition)
.

Let
m>\frac{d}{2}+1
and
0\leq s\leq m
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
small enough such that
\mathrm{id}+\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
. There exists
C>0
such that

\|f\circ\phi\|_{H^{s}}\leq(1+C\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}})\|f\|_{H^{s}}\qquad\text{for all }\phi\in\mathrm{id}+\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(0)},\ f\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}).

Proof.

First note that by Sobolev embedding we have
\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{C^{1}}\leq c\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}}
for some constant
c>0
(which of course depends on the chosen inner product on
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
).
Thus, in particular
\|D\phi-\mathrm{I}\|_{C^{0}}\leq c\varepsilon
for all
\phi\in\mathrm{id}+\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}
,
and
\varepsilon
can indeed be chosen small enough such that
\det D\phi>0
everywhere and therefore
\mathrm{id}+\overline{B_{\varepsilon}(0)}\subset\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
.

The proof is by induction in
s
.
Without loss of generality we consider the standard norm
\|f\|_{H^{s}}^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{s}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|D^{j}f|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x
.
For
s=0
we have

\|f\circ\phi\|_{H^{0}}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f\circ\phi|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f|^{2}\det D(\phi^{-1})\,\mathrm{d}x\\
\leq\min_{x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{1}{\det D\phi(x)}\|f\|_{H^{0}}^{2}\leq[(1+cL\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}})\|f\|_{H^{0}}]^{2},

where
L
is the Lipschitz constant of the smooth map
A\mapsto\sqrt{1/\det A}
on the closed ball of radius
c\varepsilon
around the identity matrix
\mathrm{I}
.
Now let
s>0
.
We have
D(f\circ\phi)=Df\circ\phi\,D\phi
.
By the induction hypothesis
\|Df\circ\phi\|_{H^{s-1}}\leq(1+\kappa\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}})\|Df\|_{H^{s-1}}
for some
\kappa>0
.
Furthermore,

\|Df\circ\phi\,D\phi\|_{H^{s-1}}\leq\|Df\circ\phi\|_{H^{s-1}}+\|Df\circ\phi(D\phi-\mathrm{I})\|_{H^{s-1}}\\
\leq\|Df\circ\phi\|_{H^{s-1}}(1+\hat{K}\|D\phi-\mathrm{I}\|_{H^{m-1}})\leq\|Df\circ\phi\|_{H^{s-1}}(1+K\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}})

for some
\hat{K},K>0
by
[
15
, Lemma 2.3]
or
lemma

7
(essentially since
H^{m-1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is a Banach algebra).
In summary,

\displaystyle\,\quad\|f\circ\phi\|_{H^{s}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|f\circ\phi\|_{H^{0}}^{2}+\|D(f\circ\phi)\|_{H^{s-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+cL\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}})^{2}\|f\|_{H^{0}}^{2}+(1+K\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}})^{2}\|Df\circ\phi\|_{H^{s-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\!1\!+\!cL\|\phi\!-\!\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}}\!)^{2}\|f\|_{H^{0}}^{2}\!+\!(\!1\!+\!K\|\phi\!-\!\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}}\!)^{2}(\!1\!+\!\kappa\|\phi\!-\!\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}}\!)^{2}\|Df\|_{H^{s\!-\!1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1+C\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}})^{2}\|f\|_{H^{s}}^{2}

for some
C>0
(depending on
c,L,K,\kappa
and
\varepsilon
).
∎

Based on

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|^{2}}{|\phi^{-1}(x)-\phi^{-1}(y)|^{d+2\theta}}\det D(\phi^{-1})(x)\det D(\phi^{-1})(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq[(1+C\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}})\|f\|_{H^{\theta}}]^{2}

for
\theta\in(0,1)
and some
C>0
, exploiting
\det D(\phi^{-1})\leq 1+C\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}}
(cf. the proof of
lemma

9
) and
|\phi^{-1}(x)-\phi^{-1}(y)|\geq|x-y|/(1+L)

with
L\lesssim\|\phi-\mathrm{id}\|_{H^{m}}
the Lipschitz constant of
\phi-\mathrm{id}
(as can be readily seen after the change of variables
X=\phi^{-1}(x)
,
Y=\phi^{-1}(y)
).

4
Spectral space discretization and its properties

In this section we introduce the spectral space discretization of the geodesic equation (
6
) (a simplified variant of the one from
[
30
]
) and analyse its structure.
To this end we employ the semi-discrete Fourier transform or Fourier series transform

\hat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}f\exp(-2\pi i\xi\cdot x)\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{for }\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

of functions
f:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and of distributions on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
(for vector- or matrix-valued functions the Fourier transform is just applied componentwise).
Fixing a maximum frequency
R
, we introduce the Fourier series truncated at frequency
R
by

\widehat{f}^{R}(\xi)=\begin{cases}\hat{f}(\xi)&\text{if }|\xi|_{\infty}\leq R,\\
0&\text{else}\end{cases}

and denote the corresponding truncation operator as
\mathcal{T}_{R}
, defined via

\widehat{\mathcal{T}_{R}f}=\widehat{f}^{R}.

The consistency order of
\mathcal{T}_{R}
obviously depends on the Sobolev regularity.

Lemma 19

(Consistency order of truncated Fourier series)
.

For
k\geq l\geq 0
we have

\|\mathcal{T}_{R}f-f\|_{H^{l}}\lesssim R^{l-k}\|\mathcal{T}_{R}f-f\|_{H^{k}}\leq R^{l-k}\|f\|_{H^{k}}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we consider the norm
\|f\|_{H^{k}}^{2}=\langle f,(1+(\frac{-\Delta}{4\pi^{2}})^{k})f\rangle
on
H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and analogously on
H^{l}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
Let
f\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, then

\|\mathcal{T}_{R}f-f\|_{H^{l}}^{2}=\sum_{|\xi|_{\infty}>R}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+|\xi|^{2l})\leq\frac{1+R^{2l}}{1+R^{2k}}\sum_{|\xi|_{\infty}>R}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}(1+|\xi|^{2k})\lesssim R^{2(l-k)}\|\mathcal{T}_{R}f-f\|_{H^{k}}^{2}
.
∎

Analogously one obtains

\|\mathcal{T}_{R}f\|_{H^{k}}\lesssim R^{k-l}\|\mathcal{T}_{R}f\|_{H^{l}}\qquad\text{for }k\geq l\geq 0.

(8)

In case
f\in H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
has no additional regularity one only knows
\|\mathcal{T}_{R}f-f\|_{H^{k}}\to 0
as
R\to\infty
, but one cannot give a rate.
At least one obtains uniform convergence for a continuous family of functions
(below, recall that
{\mathcal{I}}
denotes the identity operator).

Lemma 20

(Uniform Fourier decay along a continuous path)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
w:[0,1]\to H^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
be continuous, then
\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})w_{t}\|_{H^{k}}\to 0
as
R\to\infty
, uniformly in
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

For a proof by contradiction assume there exists some
C>0
as well as times
t_{R}\in[0,1]
,
R\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})w_{t_{R}}\|_{H^{k}}>C
for all
R
.
By compactness of
[0,1]
the sequence
t_{R}
contains a converging subsequence with limit
t\in[0,1]
.
Since
\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{r})w_{t_{R}}\|_{H^{k}}>C
for all
r\leq R
we may assume without loss of generality that the whole sequence converges.
Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{r})w_{t}\|_{H^{k}}<C/2
,
then for
R>r
we have

C<\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})w_{t_{R}}\|_{H^{k}}\leq\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{r})w_{t_{R}}\|_{H^{k}}\mathop{\to}_{R\to\infty}\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{r})w_{t}\|_{H^{k}}<C/2,

where the limit follows from the continuity of
({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{r})
and
t\mapsto w_{t}
,
yielding the desired contradiction.
∎

The formal geodesic equation (
6
) in Fourier space reads

\dot{\hat{\rho}}_{t}=-((\hat{\rho}_{t}\otimes\hat{D})*\hat{v}_{t}+\hat{\rho}_{t}*(\hat{D}\cdot\hat{v}_{t})+(\hat{D}\otimes\hat{v}_{t})*\hat{\rho}_{t})\qquad\text{with }\hat{v}_{t}=\hat{\mathcal{R}}\hat{\rho}_{t}

or equivalently

\dot{\hat{v}}_{t}=-\hat{\mathcal{R}}((\hat{\rho}_{t}\otimes\hat{D})*\hat{v}_{t}+\hat{\rho}_{t}*(\hat{D}\cdot\hat{v}_{t})+(\hat{D}\otimes\hat{v}_{t})*\hat{\rho}_{t})\qquad\text{with }\quad\hat{\rho}_{t}=\hat{\mathcal{L}}\hat{v}_{t},

(9)

where
*
denotes discrete convolution (to be interpreted in the obvious way if a matrix field is convolved with a vector field),

\hat{D}(\xi)=2\pi i\xi
is the Fourier multiplier of differentiation,
and
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}=\hat{\mathcal{R}}^{-1}
are just the Riesz operator and its inverse expressed as operators on Fourier space.
In the following we will assume the Riesz operator and its inverse to be Fourier multipliers
so that the relation
v_{t}=\mathcal{R}\rho_{t}
between velocity and momentum remains after Fourier truncation,
\mathcal{T}_{R}v_{t}=\mathcal{R}\mathcal{T}_{R}\rho_{t}
.
Therefore,
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
and
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
may simply be interpreted as functions on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
in the above.

The spatial discretization of (
9
) is merely to replace the Fourier transforms of
v_{0}
and
\mathcal{R}
by their truncated versions, thus

\dot{\hat{V}}_{t}=-\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{R}((\hat{P}_{t}\otimes\hat{D})*\hat{V}_{t}+\hat{P}_{t}*(\hat{D}\cdot\hat{V}_{t})+(\hat{D}\otimes\hat{V}_{t})*\hat{P}_{t})\quad\text{ for }\quad\hat{P}_{t}=\hat{\mathcal{L}}\hat{V}_{t}

(10)

and
\hat{V}_{0}=\widehat{v}^{R}_{0}
, where we use capital letters
V
and
P
for the numerical approximation of
v
and
\rho
.
By construction,
\hat{V}_{t}
and
\hat{P}_{t}
have support on
Z_{d,R}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\,|\,|\xi|_{\infty}\leq R\}
.
This spectral discretization is particularly popular due to its computational efficiency.

Proposition 21

(Computational effort)
.

The right-hand side of (
10
) can be evaluated in
O(R^{d}\log R)
time.

Proof.

Since
\hat{P}_{t}
and
\hat{V}_{t}
are supported on
Z_{d,R}
,
all pointwise multiplications of
\hat{P}_{t}
,
\hat{V}_{t}
, and
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}^{R}
require
O(R^{d})
floating point operations, likewise the multiplications with
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
and
\hat{D}
.
It remains to compute convolutions of the form
\hat{f}*\hat{g}
with
\hat{f}
and
\hat{g}
supported on
Z_{d,R}
; to this end we extend
\hat{f}
and
\hat{g}
to
Z_{d,2R}
with zeros
and simply perform a circular convolution with subsequent truncation to
Z_{d,R}
.
Using the Fast Fourier Transform and the Discrete Convolution Theorem this requires
O(R^{d}\log R)
operations.
∎

The (space-discrete) ODE (
10
) is equivalently expressed as

\dot{P}_{t}=-\mathcal{T}_{R}{}[\mathrm{div}(P_{t}\otimes V_{t})+(DV_{t})^{T}P_{t}]=-\mathcal{T}_{R}\,\mathrm{ad}_{V_{t}}^{*}P_{t}\quad\text{ for }\quad V_{t}=\mathcal{R}P_{t}.

(11)

As already observed in
[
30
]
, this discrete approximation may be viewed as geodesic ODE in a finite-dimensional ‘‘approximate’’ Riemannian Lie group.
Here and in the following, ‘‘approximate’’ shall only refer to the fact that the associated bracket has a nonzero, but bounded Jacobiator,
i.e. the Jacobi identity is only satisfied up to a bounded error.
5
5

5

In the context of deformation theory for Lie groups and Lie algebras it seems
that closeness to a Lie algebra can sometimes be quantified by the smallness of the Jacobiator (the deviation from the Jacobi identity),
however, in our setting the Jacobiator is not small, but scales like
R^{2}
.

Proposition 22

(Approximate Riemannian Lie group geodesics)
.

The discrete approximation (
11
) is equivalent to the Euler–Poincaré equation

\dot{P}_{t}=-\widetilde{\mathrm{ad}}_{V_{t}}^{*}P_{t},\quad V_{t}=\mathcal{R}P_{t},

(12)

on
H^{m}_{R}=\mathcal{T}_{R}H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
with inner product induced by the canonical embedding
H^{m}_{R}\hookrightarrow H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})

and approximate Lie bracket
\widetilde{\mathrm{ad}}_{V}W=-[V,W]_{R}=-\mathcal{T}_{R}([V,W])
.

Proof.

To compare the solutions of (
12
) and (
11
) it obviously suffices to work with functions from
H^{m}_{R}
,
since the solution of (
11
) lies in this space.
Thus, let
W\in H^{m}_{R}
and let
P_{t},V_{t}\in H^{m}_{R}
solve (
12
), then

\langle\dot{P}_{t},W\rangle=-\langle P_{t},\widetilde{\mathrm{ad}}_{V_{t}}W\rangle=-\langle P_{t},\mathrm{ad}_{V_{t}}W\rangle=\langle-\mathrm{ad}_{V_{t}}^{*}P_{t},W\rangle.

Since this holds for arbitrary
W\in H^{m}_{R}
we have
\dot{P}_{t}=\mathcal{T}_{R}\dot{P}_{t}=-\mathcal{T}_{R}\,\mathrm{ad}_{V_{t}}^{*}P_{t}
, which coincides with (
11
).
∎

Remark 23

(Obstruction to finite-dimensional Lie group approximations)
.

The Lie bracket
[v,w]
satisfies the three characterizing properties of a Lie bracket:
bilinearity, antisymmetry, and the Jacobi identity.
However, as discussed before, it is not closed in
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, i.e.
v,w\in H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
does not imply
[v,w]\in H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
In contrast, the approximate Lie bracket
[V,W]_{R}
is closed in
H^{m}_{R}
,
but it is not a legitimate Lie bracket as it does not satisfy the Jacobi identity.
This is due to a principal obstruction observed by Omori in
[
26
]
:
There exists no Lie algebra
H^{m}_{R}
with finite Fourier support and correct Lie bracket.
Let us quickly adapt the argument from
[
26
]
to our setting, for simplicity restricting to dimension
d=1
:
Since the Lie bracket is closed, as soon as the Lie algebra
H^{m}_{R}
contains trigonometric functions of two different nonzero frequencies,
then by repeatedly applying the Lie bracket
[\cdot,\cdot]
one obtains trigonometric functions of arbitrarily high frequencies, which therefore must belong to
H^{m}_{R}
.
It does not help to drop the condition on
H^{m}_{R}
of finite Fourier support, either:
There cannot exist any Hilbert Lie algebra containing the constant function and trigonometric functions of arbitrarily high frequencies.
Indeed, due to
[[1,\sin(2\pi\xi x)],1]=[2\pi\xi\cos(2\pi\xi x),1]=(2\pi\xi)^{2}\sin(2\pi\xi x)
the Lie bracket cannot be bounded in any norm on
H^{m}_{R}
.
The only exception are the spaces
H^{m}_{R}=\mathrm{span}(1,\sin(2\pi\xi x),\cos(2\pi\xi x))
with exactly one frequency
\xi\in\mathbb{Z}
;
on those, the standard Lie bracket is closed so that
H^{m}_{R}
is a true Lie algebra.

By construction, the discrete approximation (
11
) is structure-preserving in that – just like for the original geodesic equation – the norm of the velocity is conserved.

Lemma 24

(Constant velocity geodesics)
.

The solution
V_{t}
to (
11
) conserves
\vvvert V_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}
over time.

Proof.

In fact, this holds for any equation of the form (
12
) with antisymmetric Lie bracket approximation,
i.e. with
\widetilde{\mathrm{ad}}_{V}W=-\widetilde{\mathrm{ad}}_{W}V
.
Indeed, let
P_{t},V_{t}
denote the solution of (
12
), then

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\vvvert V_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}^{2}}{2}=\langle V_{t},\dot{P}_{t}\rangle=-\langle\widetilde{\mathrm{ad}}_{V_{t}}V_{t},P_{t}\rangle=0.\qed

5
Convergence of spectral discretization

In this section we prove convergence of the spectral discretization as well as convergence rates depending on the geodesic regularity.
We begin with a regularity estimate for quadratic terms that occur in the geodesic equation.

Proposition 25

(Quadratic term regularity)
.

Let
m>\frac{d}{2}+1
and let
\mathcal{L}
satisfy the conditions from
lemma

12
for
l=1
.
Then

\|w^{T}D\mathcal{L}w\|_{H^{-m-1}}\lesssim\|w\|_{H^{m}}^{2}\qquad\text{for all }w\in H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Proof.

For simplicity we assume
B=a_{m}D^{m}
(additional lower derivatives are treated analogously).
It is straightforward to see
\partial_{x_{i}}B^{*}h=B^{*}\partial_{x_{i}}h+(D^{m})^{*}[(\partial_{x_{i}}a_{m}^{*})h]
for any function
h
,
where
a_{m}^{*}
is the adjoint of the tensor
a_{m}
.
Thus, for
g\in H^{m+1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
we have

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\!\!gw^{T}\partial_{x_{i}\!}\mathcal{L}w\,\mathrm{d}x\!=\!\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\!\!gw\cdot\partial_{x_{i}\!}(\bar{\mathcal{L}}w)\,\mathrm{d}x+\!\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\!\!\partial_{x_{i}\!}Bw\cdot B(gw)+D^{m}(gw)(\partial_{x_{i}\!}a_{m}^{*})\cdot Bw\,\mathrm{d}x.

The first term is clearly bounded in absolute value by a constant times
\|gw\|_{H^{m}}\|\partial_{x_{i}}\bar{\mathcal{L}}w\|_{H^{-m}}\lesssim\|w\|_{H^{m}}^{2}\|g\|_{H^{m}}
(recall that
H^{m}
is a Banach algebra).
Furthermore, by the product rule
B(gw)
equals
(Bw)g
plus a sum
S
of products of derivatives of
w
and
g
, each summand with
m
derivatives in total, at least one of them on
g
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\partial_{x_{i}}Bw\cdot B(gw)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}g\,\partial_{x_{i}}\tfrac{|Bw|^{2}}{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}S\cdot\partial_{x_{i}}Bw\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\tfrac{|Bw|^{2}}{2}\partial_{x_{i}}g\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}S\cdot\partial_{x_{i}}Bw\,\mathrm{d}x.

The first integral is bounded by
\|Bw\|_{H^{0}}^{2}\|\partial_{x_{i}}g\|_{W^{0,\infty}}\lesssim\|w\|_{H^{m}}^{2}\|g\|_{H^{m+1}}
,
the second by
\|\partial_{x_{i}}Bw\|_{H^{-1}}\|S\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\|w\|_{H^{m}}^{2}\|g\|_{H^{m+1}}
(using
lemma

7
).
Similarly,

\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}D^{m}(gw)(\partial_{x_{i}}a_{m}^{*})\cdot Bw\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\|Bw\|_{H^{0}}\|\partial_{x_{i}}a_{m}\|_{W^{0,\infty}}\|D^{m}(gw)\|_{H^{0}}\\
\lesssim\|w\|_{H^{m}}\|gw\|_{H^{m}}\lesssim\|w\|_{H^{m}}^{2}\|g\|_{H^{m}}.

Hence,
\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}gw^{T}\partial_{x_{i}}\mathcal{L}w\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\lesssim\|w\|_{H^{m}}^{2}\|g\|_{H^{m+1}}
for all
g\in H^{m+1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and all coordinates
i

so that the result follows from
w^{T}D\mathcal{L}w=\sum_{i=1}^{d}w^{T}\partial_{x_{i}}\mathcal{L}w
.
∎

A direct consequence is a regularity result for the right-hand side of the geodesic ODE (
6
).

Corollary 26

(
H^{m-1}
-regularity of velocity change)
.

Let
m\!>\!\frac{d}{2}\!+\!1
,
k\!\geq\!0
, let
\mathcal{L}
satisfy the conditions from
lemma

12
for
l=1
, and let
\rho
satisfy (
6
).
Then
\|\dot{\rho}_{t}\|_{H^{k-m-1}}\lesssim\|\rho_{t}\|_{H^{-m}}\|\rho_{t}\|_{H^{k-m}}
or equivalently
\|\dot{v}_{t}\|_{H^{m-1+k}}\lesssim\|v_{t}\|_{H^{m}}\|v_{t}\|_{H^{m+k}}
.

Proof.

For
w\in H^{m+1-k}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

\langle\dot{\rho}_{t},w\rangle=\langle\rho_{t},[v_{t},w]\rangle=\langle\rho_{t},Dw\,v_{t}\rangle-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\rho_{t}^{T}Dv_{t}\,w\,\mathrm{d}x\\
=\langle\rho_{t},Dw\,v_{t}\rangle+\langle\rho_{t},v_{t}\mathrm{div}w\rangle+\langle v_{t}^{T}D\rho_{t},w\rangle,

of which all summands can be bounded by
\|\rho_{t}\|_{H^{k-m}}\|v_{t}\|_{H^{m}}\|w\|_{H^{m+1-k}}

(using
lemma

7
and for
k=0

proposition

25
in the last summand).
∎

The convergence of the spectral discretization now follows via a typical Gronwall type ODE argument.

Theorem 27

(Convergence of geodesic approximation)
.

Let
m>\frac{d}{2}+1
and
\mathcal{L}
be a Fourier multiplier satisfying the conditions from
lemma

12
for
l=2
(so that geodesics are well-posed in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
).
Let
v_{t}
be the velocity of a geodesic in
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{id}}^{m}
and
V_{t}
the solution of (
12
) with
V_{0}=\mathcal{T}_{R}v_{0}
.

1.

If
v_{0}\in H^{m+1}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
\|V_{t}-v_{t}\|_{H^{m}}\to_{R\to\infty}0
uniformly in
t\in[0,1]
.

2.

If
v_{0}\in H^{m+k}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
k>1
, then

\|V_{t}-v_{t}\|_{H^{m}}\leq Ce^{C\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+1}}e^{C\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m}}}}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+k}}R^{1-k}

for all
t\in[0,1]
with a constant
C>0
independent of
v_{0}
.

Proof.

Extend
\widetilde{\mathrm{ad}}
to
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
\widetilde{\mathrm{ad}}=\mathcal{T}_{R}\mathrm{ad}
, which is still antisymmetric.
Let
e_{t}=V_{t}-v_{t}
.
We have

\dot{\rho}_{t}=-\mathrm{ad}_{v_{t}}^{*}\rho_{t},\qquad\dot{P}_{t}=-\mathcal{T}_{R}\widetilde{\mathrm{ad}}_{V_{t}}^{*}P_{t}.

We obtain

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}^{2}}{2}=\langle e_{t},\mathcal{L}\dot{e}_{t}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}\mathcal{L}\dot{e}_{t}\rangle+\langle({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})e_{t},({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})\mathcal{L}\dot{e}_{t}\rangle\\
=\langle\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t},\mathcal{L}\dot{e}_{t}\rangle+\langle({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t},({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})\dot{\rho}_{t}\rangle,

where we exploited
({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})e_{t}=-({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}
.
We estimate the first term as follows,

\langle\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t},\mathcal{L}\dot{e}_{t}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t},\mathrm{ad}_{v_{t}}^{*}\rho_{t}-\mathcal{T}_{R}\widetilde{\mathrm{ad}}_{V_{t}}^{*}P_{t}\rangle\\
=\langle[V_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}]_{R},P_{t}\rangle-\langle[v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}],\rho_{t}\rangle=\langle[V_{t}-v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}],P_{t}\rangle+\langle[v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}],P_{t}-\rho_{t}\rangle\\
=-\langle[({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}],P_{t}\rangle+(\!([v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}],\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t})\!)_{H^{m}}-(\!([v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}],({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t})\!)_{H^{m}},

exploiting
[\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}]=0
and therefore
[e_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}]=[({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})e_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}]=-[({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}]
.
Using that
H^{m}
is a Banach algebra as well as
lemma

19
and (
8
) we next estimate

\displaystyle\|[({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}]\|_{H^{m}}

\displaystyle=\|D\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}\,({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}-D({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\,\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}]\|_{H^{m}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m}}\|\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}+\|({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}\|\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}\|_{H^{m}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tfrac{\|({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}}{R}R\|\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}\|_{H^{m}}+\|({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}\|\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}\|_{H^{m}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}\|\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}\|_{H^{m}}.

Therefore, exploiting
lemma

24
we have

-\langle[({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}],P_{t}\rangle\leq\|[({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}]\|_{H^{m}}\|P_{t}\|_{H^{-m}}\\
\lesssim\|({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}\vvvert V_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}=\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}\vvvert V_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}}\|({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}\\
\leq\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}\vvvert v_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}}\|({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}.

Furthermore, an integration by parts yields

\displaystyle(\!([q,u],w)\!)_{H^{m}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(\mathcal{L}w)^{T}(Du\,q-Dq\,u)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathcal{L}w\cdot u\,\mathrm{div}q+u^{T}D(\mathcal{L}w)q+(\mathcal{L}w)^{T}Dq\,u\,\mathrm{d}x

for any
u\in H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
q,w\in H^{m+1}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Therefore we have

(\!([v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}],\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t})\!)_{H^{m}}\\
=-\!\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\!\!\!\mathcal{L}\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}\!\cdot\!\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}\mathrm{div}v_{t}\!+\!\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}^{T}D(\mathcal{L}\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t})v_{t}\!+\!(\mathcal{L}\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t})^{T}\!Dv_{t}\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}^{2}\|v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}

by
proposition

25
as well as

\displaystyle-(\!([v_{t},\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}],({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t})\!)_{H^{m}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\!\!\!\mathcal{L}({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\!\cdot\!\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}\mathrm{div}v_{t}\!+\!\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}^{T}D(\mathcal{L}({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t})v_{t}\!+\!(\mathcal{L}({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t})^{T}\!Dv_{t}\mathcal{T}_{R}e_{t}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}\|v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}.

Summarizing,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}^{2}}{2}\lesssim\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}\vvvert v_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}}\|({\mathcal{I}}\!-\!\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}+\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}^{2}\|v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}\\
+\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}\|v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}+\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})\dot{v}_{t}\|_{H^{m-1}},

(13)

where the last summand is an upper bound for
\langle({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t},({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})\dot{\rho}_{t}\rangle
.
(Note that if we had substituted
\langle({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t},({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})\dot{\rho}_{t}\rangle=\langle({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t},-\mathrm{ad}_{v_{t}}^{*}\rho_{t}\rangle=\langle[v_{t},({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}],\rho_{t}\rangle
,
the smallness of the term from two Fourier truncations rather than one would no longer be obvious.)
By assumption and
theorem

15
,
\|v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}
is uniformly bounded.
Furthermore, from
corollary

26
we see

\|\dot{v}_{t}\|_{H^{m-1}}\lesssim\vvvert v_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}^{2}=\vvvert v_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}}^{2}.

Since
t\mapsto v_{t}\in H^{m+1}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
is continuous by
theorem

15
,

\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}
tends to zero uniformly in
t
as
R\to\infty
due to
lemma

20
.
Since also
e_{0}\to 0
in
H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the Bihari–LaSalle inequality implies
e_{t}\to 0
as
R\to\infty
, uniformly in
t
, which proves the first claim.

For the second claim we collect the occurring powers of
R
; we start with

\vvvert e_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}}=\vvvert({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}}\lesssim R^{-k}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+k}}.

Furthermore, denoting by
\phi_{t}
the flow of
v_{t}
, we have

\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})v_{t}\|_{H^{m+1}}\lesssim R^{1-k}\|v_{t}\|_{H^{m+k}}\lesssim R^{1-k}\|\rho_{t}\|_{H^{-m+k}}=R^{1-k}\|\mathrm{Ad}_{\phi_{t}^{-1}}^{*}\rho_{0}\|_{H^{-m+k}}\\
\leq R^{1-k}\exp(C\|v\|_{L^{1}(I;H^{m})})\|\rho_{0}\|_{H^{-m+k}}=R^{1-k}\exp(C\vvvert v_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}})\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+k}},

where in the first step we used
lemma

19
,
in the second
\|\rho_{t}\|_{H^{-m+k}}=\|\mathcal{L}v_{t}\|_{H^{-m+k}}\lesssim\|v_{t}\|_{H^{m+k}}
,
in the third
theorem

15
,
in the fourth
proposition

10
(
3
) to estimate the operator norm of
\mathrm{Ad}_{\phi_{t}^{-1}}
,
and in the last step we used that the norm
\vvvert v_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}
of the velocity is constant along a geodesic.
Similarly,

\|({\mathcal{I}}-\mathcal{T}_{R})\dot{v}_{t}\|_{H^{m-1}}\lesssim R^{-k}\|\dot{v}_{t}\|_{H^{m+k-1}}\lesssim R^{-k}\|\rho_{t}\|_{H^{-m+k}}\vvvert v_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}\\
\leq R^{-k}\exp(C\vvvert v_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}})\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+k}}\vvvert v_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}=R^{-k}\exp(C\vvvert v_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}})\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+k}}\vvvert v_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}}

using
corollary

26
in the second step. In summary, (
13
) turns into

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+1}}\exp(2C\vvvert v_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}})\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\left(\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}^{2}+R^{1-k}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+k}}\sqrt{\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}^{2}}+R^{-2k+1}\tfrac{\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m}}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+k}}^{2}}{\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+1}}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+1}}\exp(2C\vvvert v_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}})\left(\vvvert e_{t}\vvvert_{H^{m}}^{2}+R^{2-2k}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+k}}^{2}\right)

via Young’s inequality,
which together with the estimate for
\vvvert e_{0}\vvvert_{H^{m}}
yields the desired estimate via the Gronwall inequality.
∎

Corollary 28

(Convergent scheme for generic regularity)
.

Let the conditions of
theorem

27
hold, and let
r\in o(\log R)
.
Given

, let
V^{r}\in\mathcal{T}_{R}H^{m}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the solution of (
12
) for initial condition
V^{r}_{0}=\mathcal{T}_{r}v_{0}
.
Then
\|V^{r}_{t}-v_{t}\|_{H^{m}}\to 0
uniformly in
t\in[0,1]
as
R\to\infty
.

Moreover, if
v_{0}\in H^{m+1}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the metric (
3
) is three times differentiable,
then
\|V^{r}_{t}-v_{t}\|_{H^{m}}\lesssim C(\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+1}})r
for all
t\in[0,1]
for a constant depending only on
\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m+1}}

Proof.

Denote by
v^{r}
the geodesic with initial velocity
v^{r}_{0}=V^{r}_{0}=\mathcal{T}_{r}v_{0}
.
We can estimate

\|V^{r}_{t}-v_{t}\|_{H^{m}}\leq\|V^{r}_{t}-v^{r}_{t}\|_{H^{m}}+\|v^{r}_{t}-v_{t}\|_{H^{m}}.

By

, for

\displaystyle\|V^{r}_{t}-v^{r}_{t}\|_{H^{m}}

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{C\|v_{0}^{r}\|_{H^{m+1}}e^{C\|v_{0}^{r}\|_{H^{m}}}}\|v_{0}^{r}\|_{H^{m+k}}R^{1-k}

Remark 29

(Convergence on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
.

The arguments hold verbatim also when replacing the domain
\mathbb{T}^{d}
by
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and interpreting
\mathcal{T}_{R}
as the truncation operator on continuous Fourier space.
However, this is of course of little numerical relevance since the resulting truncated ODE (
9
) is still infinite-dimensional.

In certain situations the geodesic ODE on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can actually be reduced to the geodesic ODE on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
Indeed, an integral of (
6
) with momentum
\rho
, velocity
v
and flow
\phi
is given by
\rho_{t}=\mathrm{Ad}_{\phi_{t}}^{*}\rho_{0}
,
from which one can quickly see that if
\rho_{0}
has compact support, then so does
\rho_{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]

[
23
, Sec. 4.3.1]
.
By rescaling and shifting coordinates we may assume
\rho_{t}
to stay supported within
[0,1)^{d}
.
Hence, by appropriately choosing the operator
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
(the Riesz isomorphism to be used on the torus; the subscript distinguishes it from the original operator
\mathcal{R}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
)
one obtains the identical evolution of
\rho_{t}
on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
as on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:
Operator
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
would be the composition of an extension (of
\rho_{t}
) from
[0,1)^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by zero
(in Fourier space this would mean to interpret the discrete Fourier sum as a sum of Dirac measures and convolving this measure with the Fourier transform
\hat{\chi}
of the characteristic function
\chi
of
[0,1)^{d}
),
an application of
\mathcal{R}
(which then yields
v_{t}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
),
a restriction (of
v_{t}
) to
[0,1)^{d}
(in Fourier space another convolution with
\hat{\chi}
),
and a reinterpretation as periodic function (by the Poisson summation formula, the discrete Fourier coefficients would just be the sampling of the continuous ones).
Unfortunately, our error analysis only applies in this setting if
\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}
is a Fourier multiplier, which for natural choices (such as
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{L}^{-1}
with
\mathcal{L}=(1-\Delta)^{m}
) is not the case.

For initial momentum of unbounded support the situation of course becomes even more involved.
For a numerical approximation one would need to periodize momentum or velocity (typically via the Poisson summation formula)
in addition to truncating in Fourier space and then analyze the error inflicted by both.
A possible direction would be to truncate
\rho_{0}
in space and investigate the resulting error,
after which in a second step the above reduction from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{T}^{d}
is applied.

For a numerical validation of
theorem

27
we solve the (space-discrete) geodesic ODE (
12
) on
[0,1]
for
R\in\{16,32,64,128\}

using an explicit
6
-stage Runge–Kutta method (the
5
th order consistent
6
-stage part of the Dormand–Prince method) with step size
2^{-15}

(by using different step sizes we checked that the error from the time discretization is negligibly small compared to the error from the spectral space discretization).
The result for
R=128
is taken as a substitute for the true solution, and errors for smaller
R
are computed with respect to that solution.
The experiments are performed in
d=2
space dimensions with
m=3
and the inner product
(\!(\cdot,\cdot)\!)_{H^{m}}
defined via the corresponding differential operator
\mathcal{L}=(1-\Delta)^{m}
.
To obtain initial conditions
v_{0}
of different Sobolev regularity we first define a complex-valued
H^{0}
-function
w

by choosing each Fourier coefficient
\hat{w}(\xi)
randomly and independently from
[0,1/\sqrt{1+|\xi|^{2}}/\log(2+|\xi|^{2})^{1/2+\epsilon}]
for
\epsilon=0.1
.
Via
\hat{u}(\xi)=\hat{w}(\xi)+\overline{\hat{w}(-\xi)}
we obtain a real-valued
H^{0}
-function
u
, and we simply set

\hat{v}_{0}(\xi)=\hat{u}(\xi)/(1+|\xi|^{2})^{s/2}

to obtain a
H^{s}
-regular initial condition
v_{0}
.

Figure

1
shows that the numerically observed convergence rates for different initial Sobolev regularity are better than the one of
theorem

27
by one order;
it may thus be that, generically (or at least for the above choice of initial conditions
v_{0}
), convergence rates are better, something we leave for future investigation.

Let us finally comment on why we restricted to operators
\mathcal{L}
that are Fourier multipliers:
First off, otherwise
P_{t}=\mathcal{L}V_{t}
for a bandlimited
V_{t}
will not be bandlimited so that one needs to approximate
\mathcal{L}
(by
\mathcal{T}_{R}\mathcal{L}
or in a different way) to obtain a working scheme.
However, central to our analysis was the estimate from
proposition

25
, and it is unclear how to approximate
\mathcal{L}
with a bandlimit while preserving such an estimate.
