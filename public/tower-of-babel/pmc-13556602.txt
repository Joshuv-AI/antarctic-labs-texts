870. Advancements in the Pharmacologic Treatment of Stuttering
Authors: G Maguire
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStuttering is a neuropsychiatric disorder classified in DSM. Stuttering has been reported throughout recorded human history and every language describes stuttering. The disorder typically begins in childhood yet now multiple etiologies have been postulated. Stuttering affects over 4% of children and persists to affect over 1% of all adults. In spite of its persistence and prevalence, no therapeutic has been approved by any regulatory agency and speech therapy techniques are limited by low sustained efficacy. Stuttering does share many similarities with other neuropsychiatric conditions such as Tourette Syndrome with ​age of onset usually in childhood, similar 4:1 male:female ratio and both ​related to dopamine dysregulated activity in the basal ganglia. Stuttering ​is associated with comorbid conditions such as ADHD, ​OCD and Social Anxiety and treatments targeting these symptoms can yield benefit in persons who stutterAims & Objectives1. Define the Demographics of Stuttering2. Describe ​common comorbid conditions associated with stuttering.3. Summarize Pharmacologic Treatment Strategies for Stuttering.MethodReview of animal model data, brain imaging and prior pharmacologic trials.ResultsCompounds that modify dopamine activity through D2 antagonism or partial agonism show efficacy in persons who stutter. Olanzapine remains the best studied compound with two well controlled trials showing efficacy in stuttering. Risperidone also has been shown to improve symptoms of stuttering and is associated with improved striatal activity. ​Investigational compounds such as gemlapodect and ecopipam ​require more investigation and a trend may be that younger patients may respond better.Discussion & ConclusionsStuttering is a neuropsychiatric condition that affects over one percent of our global population yet is largely ignored by our greater medical and scientific communities. Emerging evidence is revealing that dopamine antagonist/partial agonist medications show benefit for a significant percentage of persons who stutter. More research is needed especially around identifying biomarkers for response/nonresponse and in identifying an accurate metric to measure a defined clinical response over time.
