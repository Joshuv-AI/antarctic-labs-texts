Finiteness theorems for some representations of GL 3 {\rm GL}_{3}
Fatemehzahra Janbazi, Arul Shankar

Abstract
Let n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 be an integer and let K K be a number field with ring of integers 𝒪 K {\mathcal{O}}_{K} . We prove that the set of ternary n n -ic forms forms with coefficients in 𝒪 K {\mathcal{O}}_{K} and fixed nonzero discriminant, breaks up into finitely many GL 3 ​ ( 𝒪 K ) {\rm GL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K}) -orbits. This generalizes the result of Birch–Merriman [ 3 ] in the binary forms case. We also prove a similar finiteness result on the GL 3 ​ ( 𝒪 K ) {\rm GL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K}) -orbits of the 27 27 -dimensional representation of GL 3 {\rm GL}_{3} with highest weight ( 4 , 2 ) (4,2) .



1
Introduction

A classical result of Birch and Merriman
[
3
]
states that, for fixed
n
, the set of integral binary
n
-ic forms with a fixed nonzero discriminant breaks up into finitely many
{\rm GL}_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
-orbits. They also prove the analogous result over arbitrary number fields.
In this article, we consider the space of ternary
n
-ic forms.
For
n\geq 2
, and a ring
R
, let
V_{n}(R)
denote the set of ternary
n
-ic forms (i.e., homogeneous degree-
n
polynomials in three variables) with coefficients in
R
. The group
{\rm GL}_{3}(R)
acts on
V_{n}(R)
via linear change of variables:
\gamma\cdot f(x,y,z)=f((x,y,z)\cdot\gamma)
. When
n=2
, this is the (prehomogeneous) representation of
{\rm GL}_{3}(R)
on ternary quadratic forms. When
n=3
, the representation of
{\rm GL}_{3}(R)
acting on ternary cubic forms is no longer prehomogeneous, but is coregular - the ring of relative polynomial invariants is freely generated by two elements. As
n
grows, the invariant theory becomes very complicated, and explicit descriptions of the rings of invariants, much beyond Hilbert’s finite generation theorem, are unknown.

However, for all
n
, there is a classically known relative polynomial invariant for the action of
{\rm GL}_{3}(R)
on
V_{n}(R)
, namely, the discrimiant. We denote the discriminant by

\Delta_{n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[V_{n}]
; it is a degree-
3(n-1)^{2}
polynomial with integer coefficients. Then we prove the following result.

Theorem 1

Let
n\geq 2
be a fixed integer. Let
K/{\mathbb{Q}}
be a number field with ring of integers
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
, and let
0\neq D\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
be an algebraic integer. Then the set of elements in
V_{n}({\mathcal{O}}_{K})
with discriminant
D
breaks up into finitely many
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K})
-orbits.

The significance of the discriminant is as follows. Let
k
be a field, and let
f\in V_{n}(k)
be a nonzero element. We denote the curve cut out by
f
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
by
C_{f}:={\rm Proj}\;k[x,y,z]/(f)
. Then
C_{f}
is a smooth curve (of genus
(n-1)(n-2)/2
) if and only if
\Delta_{n}(f)
is nonzero. Let
K
be a number field, let
{\mathfrak{p}}
be a finite prime in
{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
, and let
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathfrak{p}}}
denote the ring of integers of
K_{{\mathfrak{p}}}
. We say that an element
f\in V_{n}({\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathfrak{p}}})
has
good reduction
at
\mathfrak{p}
if
\Delta_{n}(f)
is a unit in
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathfrak{p}}}
(equivalently, if
C_{\overline{f}}
is a smooth curve over
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathfrak{p}}}/{\mathfrak{p}}
, where
\overline{f}
denotes the reduction of
f
modulo
{\mathfrak{p}}
).
Given a finite set of places
S
of
K
, we denote the ring of
S
-integers in
K
by
{\mathcal{O}}_{K,S}
. Then we have the following result.

Theorem 2

Let
n\geq 2
be a fixed integer. Let
K/{\mathbb{Q}}
be a number field and let
S
be a finite set of places of
K
containing all the infinite places. Then the set of elements in
V_{n}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,S})
, with good reduction outside
S
, breaks up into finitely many
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,S})
-orbits.

We make the following remarks.

Remark 3

(1).
Our two main results are analogues of Theorems 1 and 2 from
[
3
]
, with binary forms replaced by ternary forms and
{\rm GL}_{2}
replaced with
{\rm GL}_{3}
. Our proof follows a somewhat similar structure, where we use Faltings’ theorem as a diophantine input instead of finiteness of the unit equation used by Birch–Merriman.

(2).
As in the case of binary forms, finiteness of the set of orbits when
all
the invariants are fixed follows by reduction theory methods (carried out in great generality by Borel–Harishchandra
[
4
]
). The novelty of the results of Birch–Merriman and ours is that only one particular invariant, namely the discriminant, needs to be fixed to ensure finiteness.

(3).
In our results, we consider
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K})
-orbits. However, finiteness also holds in Theorem
1
for
{\rm SL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K})
orbits, as will be clear from the proof. For Theorem
2
, finiteness does not hold for
{\rm SL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,S})
orbits. Indeed, if
f\in V_{n}(O_{K,S})
has good reduction outside
S
, and
q\in{\mathcal{O}}_{K}
is an element divisible only by primes in
S
, then
q^{k}f
also has good reduction outside
S
for all
k
. And these elements are all pairwise
{\rm SL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,S})
-inequivalent, since they have different disriminants.

Our next finiteness result concerns the irreducible representation
V_{2,2}
of
{\rm GL}_{3}
with highest weight
(4,2)
. For any ring
R
, the set of
R
-points of this representation is given by

V_{2,2}(R)=\bigl({\text{Sym}}^{2}(R^{3})\otimes{\text{Sym}}^{2}(R^{3})\bigr)/I,

where, if
{\text{Sym}}^{2}(R^{2})\otimes{\text{Sym}}^{2}(R^{2})
is written as bihomogeneous degree-
(2,2)
polynomials in
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
and
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
, then
I
is the ideal of all multiples of
\sum x_{i}z_{i}
. That is, we have

I=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}z_{i}\Bigr)\cdot k[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]\otimes k[z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}]\cap k[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]_{2}\otimes k[z_{i},z_{2},z_{3}]_{2}.

The action of
{\rm GL}_{3}(R)
on
V_{2,2}(R)
is given by

\gamma\cdot f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},z_{1},z_{1},z_{3})=f((x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\cdot\gamma,(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\cdot\delta(\gamma)),

where
\delta(\gamma)
denotes the cofactor matrix of
\gamma
.

Let
k
be a field of characteristic not equal to
2
. We explain in §2.2 that an elements
v
in
V_{2,2}(k)
cuts out a subvariety
X_{v}
in the flag variety
F_{k}=F(0,1,2;k)
. The two projection maps
F_{k}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
restrict to two maps
X_{v}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
. It is known that generically, this subvariety is a K3 surface and that the two maps are (degree-
2
) covering maps to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. We say that an element
v\in V_{2,2}(k)
is
generic
if
X_{v}
is a K3 surface in
F_{k}
, and the two maps
p_{1},p_{2}
from
X_{v}
to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
are covering maps. If
K
is a number field and
{\mathfrak{p}}
is a finite prime of
K
with norm relatively prime to
2
, we say that
v\in V_{2,2}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,{\mathfrak{p}}})
has
good reduction
at
{\mathfrak{p}}
if
\bar{v}\in V_{2,2}({\mathbb{F}}_{\mathfrak{p}})
is generic. We will also restrict ourselves to elements
v\in V_{2,2}({\mathcal{O}}_{K})
such that
X_{v}
has Picard rank
2
over the complex numbers. This last condition can be easily enforced by picking a prime
{\mathfrak{p}}
of
K
, and restricting to those elements
v\in V_{2,2}({\mathcal{O}}_{K})
such that the reduction
\bar{v}
of
v
satisfies
X_{\bar{v}}
having Picard rank
2
(see the discussion above Theorem 1 in
[
6
]
).

We then have the following result.

Theorem 4

Let
K/{\mathbb{Q}}
be a number field and let
S
be a finite set of places of
K
containing all the infinite places and all the places above
2
. Then the set of elements in
V_{2,2}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,S})
, with good reduction outside
S
, and such that
X_{v}
has Picard rank
2
, breaks up into finitely many
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,S})
-orbits.

We introduce a new framework allowing us to obtain finiteness results on orbits of representations from certain classical diophantine results: Let
V
be a finite dimensional representation of an algebraic group
G
, and let
\Delta\in{\mathbb{Z}}[V]
be a polynomial relative invariant for the action of
G
on
V
. Suppose for simplicity we are trying to prove that there are finitely many
G({\mathbb{Z}})
-orbits on
V({\mathbb{Z}})
with invariant
\Delta
fixed to be some nonzero integer. This can be done with the following four ingredients:

1.

For fields
k
, we require
G(k)
-orbits on
V(k)
to correspond to some geometric objects (say some sort of varieties over
k
). Moreover, we want
\Delta=0
to correspond to objects that are degenerate in some way.

2.

A diophantine input, which proves that the set of isomorphism classes of these objects over
{\mathbb{Q}}
with non-degenerate reduction outside a fixed finite set of places is finite.

3.

A geometric input, which proves that elements in
V({\mathbb{C}})
with the same invariant
\Delta
and corresponding to isomorphic geometric objects lie inside finitely many
G({\mathbb{C}})
-orbits.

4.

A geometry-of-numbers input, which proves that a
G({\mathbb{C}})
-equivalent set of elements in
V({\mathbb{Z}})
with fixed invariant
\Delta
must break up into finitely many
G({\mathbb{Z}})
-orbits.

Birch and Merriman’s proof of their main result is easy to write in this framework: nonzero binary
n
-ic forms with coefficients in
{\mathbb{Q}}
give rise to dimension-
0
degree-
n
subschemes of
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{\mathbb{Q}}
, yielding degree-
n
étale algebras over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Hermite’s theorem implies that the number of possibilities for these étale algebras is finite, once the discriminant of the binary
n
-ic forms has been fixed.
The finiteness of the unit equation gives us Step 3, namely, that there are finitely many
{\rm GL}_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
-equivalence classes (in fact
{\rm GL}_{2}(\overline{{\mathbb{Q}}})
-equivalence classes) on the set of binary
n
-ic forms with fixed discriminant and corresponding to the same étale algebra over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Indeed, the binary
n
-ic form factors over
\overline{{\mathbb{Q}}}
, and the action of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\overline{{\mathbb{Q}}})
can be used to move three of its roots to
0
,
1
, and
\infty
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}_{\overline{Q}}
. Since the discriminant is fixed, it is easy to see that the remaining roots have only finitely many possibilities – they correspond to solutions to a unit equation. Finally, Step 4 can be deduced from an application of Borel–Harish-Chandra
[
4
]
. (In fact Borel–Harish-Chandra can be used to obtain Step 4 whenever the following criteria apply: 1.
G
is a reductive group; 2. elements with
\Delta\neq 0
are stable (in the sense of GIT); and 3. the stabilizer in
G({\mathbb{C}})
of generic elements of
V({\mathbb{C}})
is finite.)

Our proof of Theorem
1
(on the representations
V_{n}
of
{\rm GL}_{3}
) is also easy to summarize in this framework. Assume that
n\geq 3
. (The case
n=1
is trivial, and the case
n=2
follows from the therory of quadratic forms.) For a field
k
, a
{\rm GL}_{3}(k)
-orbit on
V_{n}(k)
yields a degree-
n
curve in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
, which is smooth if and only if the discriminant is nonzero. Our diophantine input is Faltings’ theorem when
n\geq 4
and Siegel’s theorem on the finiteness of integral points on elliptic curves when
n=3
. It is easy to prove that for
n\geq 4
, two complex ternary
n
-ic forms forms cutting out isomorphic smooth curves in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
are
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{C}})
-equivalent, yielding Step 3. When
n=3
, we obtain Step 3 directly from the invariant theory of ternary cubic forms. Step 4 once again is an application of Borel–Harish-Chandra. To prove Theorem
2
only requires a small modification of this framework. The issue is that there are possibly infinitely many values for the discriminant of a ternary form with good reduction outside a fixed finite set of places. It is necessary to use the
{\mathbb{G}}_{m}
-action to cut this down to a finite set. The specific results required for this modified framework to work are proved in §3.2.

The proof of Theorem
4
proceeds as follows: The Borel–Weil realization of
V_{2,2}
implies that generic
{\rm GL}_{3}(k)
-orbits on
V_{2,2}(k)
yield K3 surfaces
X
in the flag variety
F_{k}
, along with two double coverings
X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
. For our diophantine input, we use Faltings’ theorem to obtain that the isomorphism classes of the two branch curves of these two maps to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
have only finitely many choices. Our main technical input is Step 3: we prove that the set of K3 surfaces
X
in
F_{\mathbb{C}}
such that the isomorphism classes of the branch covers of the two maps
X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
are fixed is finite. This is accomplished in §4.1. The rest of the proof follows identically to the proof of Theorem
2
.

2
The representations
V_{n}
and
V_{2,2}
of
{\rm GL}_{3}

In this section, we collect some preliminary results on the geometry and invariant theory of the representations
V_{n}
and
V_{2,2}
of
{\rm GL}_{3}
. Specifically, we associate curves to elements in these representations, and introduce polynomial invariants which determine smoothness of these geometric objects.

2.1
Plane curves corresponding to elements in
V_{n}

Let
R
be a ring. Recall that
V_{n}(R)
denotes the set of homogeneous degree-
n
polynomials in
R[x,y,z]
, and that
{\rm GL}_{3}(R)
acts on
V_{n}(R)
via linear change of variables. To any element
f\in V_{n}(R)
, we associate the scheme
C_{f}=C_{f,R}:={\rm Proj}(R[x,y,z]/(f)
. When
R=k
is a field, and
f\in V_{n}(k)
, and
C_{f,k}
is a smooth plane curve in
{\mathbb{P}}_{k}^{2}
of genus
(n-1)(n-2)/2
.

The
discriminant

\Delta=\Delta_{n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}[V_{n}]
is a
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})
-invariant on
V_{n}({\mathbb{Z}})
defined by

\Delta(f):=c_{n}\mathrm{R}\Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},\frac{\partial f}{\partial y},\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\Bigr),

(1)

where
\mathrm{R}
above denotes the resultant of the three partial derivatives of
f
, and
c_{n}
is a nonzero rational constant, unique up to sign, ensuring that the coefficients of
\Delta_{n}
are relatively prime. We collect the following well known properties of the discriminant polynomial.

(1)

The polynomial
\Delta_{n}
is irreducible and homogeneous of degree
3(n-1)^{2}
.

(2)

For a field
k
and
f\in V_{n}(k)
, we have that
C_{f,k}
is a smooth plane curve if and only if
\Delta_{n}(f)\neq 0
.

For a comprehensive treatment of discriminant polynomials, see
[
7
, Chapter 13.1 D]
.

2.2
The Borel–Weil realization of
V_{2,2}

The Borel–Weil theorem realizes finite dimensional irreducible representations of reductive groups as spaces of sections on certain line bundles on flag varieties. In this subsection, we describe this realization for the representation
V_{2,2}
of
{\rm GL}_{3}
.

For a field
k
, let
F_{k}=F(0,1,2,k)
denote the complete flag variety over
k
: we have

F(k)=\{(\ell,p):\ell\subset p\},

the set of pairs
(\ell,p)
, where
\ell\subset k^{3}
is a line going through the origin and
p\subset k^{3}
is a plane containing
\ell
. The action of
{\rm GL}_{3}(k)
on
k^{3}
yields an action of
{\rm GL}_{3}(k)
on
F(k)
.

Clearly
F_{k}
admits an embedding into the product of Grassmanians

F_{k}\hookrightarrow G(1,3)\times G(2,3),

where
G(m,n)
denotes the Grassmannian of
m
-dimensional subspaces of
n
-space over
k
. Both these Grassmannians are isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
, and so we obtain two projection maps

p_{1},p_{2}:F_{k}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}.

Thees projection maps send the pair
(\ell,p)
to
\ell
and to
p
, respectively. (Here we are using the fact that
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
is isomorphic to its dual.)

Consider the line bundle
\mathcal{L}
of the flag variety
F_{k}
, defined by

\mathcal{L}:=(p_{1})^{*}({\mathcal{O}}(2))\otimes(p_{2})^{*}({\mathcal{O}}(2)).

The Borel–Weil theorem realizes the representation
V_{2,2}(k)
of
{\rm GL}_{3}(k)
as the space of global sections of
\mathcal{L}
as follows.

We will parametrize lines in
k^{3}
going through the origin via points
[x_{1}:x_{2}:x_{3}]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{2}(k)
. We will parametrize planes in
k^{3}
going through the origin via points in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}(k)
, where
[z_{1}:z_{2}:z_{3}]\in{\mathbb{P}}^{2}(k)
corresponds to the plane given by the equation
\sum z_{i}x_{i}=0
. Therefore, the flag variety
F_{k}
is given by the hypersurface in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
cut out by the equation
\sum x_{i}z_{i}=0
. It is easy to check that the action of
{\rm GL}_{3}(k)
on
F(k)
descends from the following action of
{\rm GL}_{3}(k)
on
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}\times{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
:

\gamma\cdot([x_{1}:x_{2}:x_{3}],[z_{1}:z_{2}:z_{3}])=([x_{1}:x_{2}:x_{3}]\gamma,[z_{1}:z_{2}:z_{3}]\delta(\gamma)),

where
\delta(\gamma)=\det(\gamma)(\gamma^{-1})^{t}
denotes the cofactor matrix of
\gamma
.
In this setup,
H^{0}(F_{k},\mathcal{L})
is a quotient of the tensor product of the degree-
2
parts of the polynomial rings
k[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
and
k[z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}]
. Specifically, we have:

H^{0}(F_{k},\mathcal{L})=\frac{k[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]_{2}\otimes k[z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}]_{2}}{I},

where
I
is given by

I:=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}z_{i}\Bigr)\cap k[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]_{2}\otimes k[z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}]_{2}.

The group
\mathrm{GL}_{3}(k)
acts naturally on
H^{0}(F_{k},\mathcal{L})
. For
\gamma\in\mathrm{GL}_{3}(k)
and a bihomogeneous
(2,2)
polynomial
f(x_{i},z_{i})
, the action is given by:

\gamma\cdot f\big((x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\big)=f\big((x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\gamma,(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\delta(\gamma)\big).

To see that this is an action on
H^{0}(F_{k},\mathcal{L})
, it is only necessary to check that the ideal generated by
x_{1}z_{1}+x_{2}z_{2}+x_{3}z_{3}
is fixed by this action. This follows by noting that we have

\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}z_{i}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})^{t}=\frac{1}{\det(\gamma)}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\gamma((z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\delta(\gamma))^{t},

for every
\gamma\in{\rm GL}_{3}(k)
. The content of the Borel-Weil theorem is that under this action, we have an isomorphism of the
{\rm GL}_{3}(k)
-modules
H^{0}(F_{k},\mathcal{L})
and
V_{2,2}(k)
.

2.3
K3 surfaces corresponding to elements in
V_{2,2}(k)
, and their branch curves

Let
k
be a field with characteristic not
2
. Given
v\in V_{2,2}(k)
, we let
S_{v}
denote the surface in
F_{k}
cut out by the corresponding section of
\mathcal{L}
. It is well known that, generically,
S_{v}
is a K3 surface. Furthermore, this construction of
S_{v}
from
v
respects the two actions of
{\rm GL}_{3}(k)
on
F(k)
and
V_{2,2}(k)
. That is, for
\gamma\in{\rm GL}_{3}(k)
, we have
S_{\gamma\cdot v}=\gamma(S_{v})
.
Let
v\in V_{2,2}(k)
be an element which corresponds to the K3 surface
S_{v}
. Denote the branch curves of the two maps
p_{1}:S_{v}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
and
p_{2}:S_{v}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
by
C_{1,v}
and
C_{2,v}
. When both
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are covering maps,

C_{1,v}
and
C_{2,v}
are degree-
6
curves in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
. We have the following lemma (see, for example
[
8
, Example 1.3, iv]
).

Lemma 5

Let notation be as above. Assume that
p_{1}

(
resp.
p_{2})
are covering maps from
S_{v}
to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{k}
. Then
S_{v}
is smooth if and only if
C_{1,v}

(
resp.
C_{2,v})
is smooth.

We now construct two relative covariants for the representation
V_{2,2}
of
{\rm GL}_{3}
. These covariants are defined over
{\mathbb{Z}}
, and so we work in a general setting. Given a degree-
(2,2)
bihomogeneous polynomial
f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})
, with coefficients in a ring
R
, we consider it as a ternary quadratic form
Q_{z}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
in the
x_{i}
, whose coefficients are degree-
2
homogeneous polynomials in the
z_{i}
. We abuse notation, and use
Q_{z}
to denote the
3\times 3
symmetric Gram matrix associated to this quadratic form. Then we have

f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})Q_{z}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{t}.

Similarly, we may consider
f
as a ternary quadratic form
Q_{x}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})
in the
z_{i}
whose coefficients are degree-
2
homogeneous polynomials in the
x_{i}
. Once again, we use
Q_{x}
to also denote the Gram matrix associated to this quadratic form, and we have

f(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})Q_{x}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})^{t}.

Then our two covariants are given by

I_{x}(f):=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}){\rm Adj}(Q_{x})(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{t},\quad\quad I_{z}(f):=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}){\rm Adj}(Q_{z})(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})^{t},

where
{\rm Adj}(Q)
denotes the adjoint matrix of
Q
.
We next prove that these quantities are well defined, and in fact covariants.

Proposition 6

Let
R
be any ring. The maps

I_{x}:V_{2,2}(R)\to V_{6}(R)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad I_{z}:V_{2,2}(R)\to V_{6}(R)

are well defined. Moreover, we have

I_{x}(\gamma f)=\det(\gamma)^{2}\gamma(I_{x}(f)),\quad\quad I_{z}(\gamma f)=\delta(\gamma)(I_{z}(f)),

for all
\gamma\in{\rm GL}_{3}(R)
.

Proof:

To prove the first claim, we must check that
I_{x}(f)
only depends on the class of
f
in
V_{2,2}(R)
. That is,
I_{x}(f)=I_{x}(f+g)
for any
g
of the form
L(\sum_{i=1}^{3}x_{i}z_{i})
with
L
a degree-

3
Finiteness results for ternary forms

In this section, we prove Theorems
1
and
2
.

3.1
Ternary cubic forms

Let
K
be a number field. When
n=2
, our main theorems follow from the theory of quadratic forms. In this subsection, we consider the space
V_{3}
of ternary cubic forms. It is well known (see, for example,
[
2
]
) that the ring of polynomial invariants for the action of

on

is freely generated by two invariants, usually denoted by

and

. Here

has degree

and

has degree

, and the discriminant
\Delta=\Delta_{3}
is given by

\Delta(f):=\Delta(I(f),J(f)):=\frac{1}{27}(4I(f)^{2}-J(f)^{3}).

Let
K
be a number field, and let

be a finite set of places of

containing all the infinite places. Our main diophantine input for this subsection is Siegel’s theorem on the finiteness of

-integral points of elliptic curves.

Theorem 8
(
[
11
]
)

Let

be an elliptic curve over a number field

. Let

be a finite set of places of

, containing all the infinite places. Then the number of
S
-integral points on

is finite.

We will combine this diophantine input with the following well known application of the construction of fundamental domains for the action of
{\rm SL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,S})
on

(see works of Siegel
[
11
]
and Borel–Harish-Chandra’s
[
4
]
).

Theorem 9

Fix

. Let

and

be as above, and let

be an element of

which is stable in the sense of GIT. Then there are finitely many
{\rm SL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,S})
-orbits on
V_{n}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,S})\cap(\prod_{v\in S}{\rm SL}_{3}(K_{v})\cdot x)
.

The above result has the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 10

Let
T\subset V_{n}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,S})
be a subset of stable elements such that for
f_{1},f_{2}\in T
, we have
P(f_{1})=P(f_{2})
for every polynomial
{\rm SL}_{3}({\mathbb{Z}})
-invariant

. Then
T
breaks up into finitely many
{\rm SL}_{3}({\mathcal{O}}_{K,S})
-equivalence classes.

Proof:

Fix
n\geq 3
. Given Theorem
9
, it is only necessary to prove that for any place

, the set

breaks up into finitely many
{\rm SL}_{3}(K_{v})
-equivalence classes. Since every element in

is stable and has the same set of invariants, it follows that

lies within a single
{\rm SL}_{3}(\overline{K_{v}})
-orbit, say the orbit of some
x\in V_{n}(K_{v})
. Let
\Gamma
denote the stabilizer of

in
{\rm SL}_{3}(\overline{K_{v}})
, and note that
\Gamma
is a finite group.
It is well known (see for example,
[
10
, §I.5]
) that we have an injection

{\rm SL}_{3}(K_{v})\backslash\bigl({\rm SL}_{3}(\overline{K_{v}})\cdot x\cap V_{n}()\bigr)\to H^{1}(G_{\overline{K_{v}}/K_{v}},\Gamma).

Since there are finitely many fixed degree étale extensions of

, this Galois cohomology group is finite, yielding the result.

\Box

We are now ready to prove Theorems
1
and
2
in our case.

Proof of Theorems
1
and
2
for

:

Let

be nonzero. Then from Theorem

, we see that there are only finitely main pairs

. Theorem

now follows from a direct application of Corollary

, taking

to be the set of infinite valuations.

We turn to Theorem

S
be a finite set of places, containing all the infinite places. Let

be an element of

such that

has good reduction outside

\Delta(f)
is an element of

, an

\Box

Let

We are now ready to prove Theorem

Before we prove Theorem

, it will be convenient for us to set up some notation. Let

be

We denote the GIT quotient

. For a ring

as a subvariety, defined over

Let
K
be a number field, and let

be a finite set of places in

containing all the archimedean places. For any prime

of

which do not map to

(the point

The map

breaks up into finitely many
{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{C}})
-orbits.

A double plane over
{\mathbb{C}}
is a pair
(X,f)
, where
X
is a smooth surface over
{\mathbb{C}}
, and
f:X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
is a double cover. The branch curve of
p
will then be a smooth curve in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
. We say that two double planes
(X,f)
and
(X^{\prime},f^{\prime})
are
equivalent
if there exists an isomorphism
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
such that
f^{\prime}\circ\phi=f
. The following result is an immediate consequence of Catanese’s work on the Chisini conjecture
[
5
]
.

Proposition 15

Let
B
be a smooth curve in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Then there are finitely many equivalence classes of double planes over
{\mathbb{C}}
with branch curve
B
.

Proof:

Let
(X,f)
and
(X^{\prime},f^{\prime})
be two double covers of
\mathbb{P}^{2}
branched over the same curve
B
. Consider the map

\varphi^{\prime}=(f^{\prime})^{-1}\circ f:R\to R^{\prime},

where
R
and
R^{\prime}
denote the ramification divisors of
f
and
f^{\prime}
, respectively. Since
f|_{R}
and
f^{\prime}|_{R^{\prime}}
are both isomorphisms onto
B
, it follows that
\varphi^{\prime}
is an isomorphism.
Let
N_{R/X}
and
N_{R^{\prime}/X^{\prime}}
denote the normal bundles of
R
in
X
and
R^{\prime}
in
X^{\prime}
, respectively.
From
[
5
, Proposition 2.5]
, it follows that the element

\eta(X,X^{\prime})=N_{R/X}^{-1}\otimes(\varphi^{\prime})^{*}N_{R^{\prime}/X^{\prime}}

is always a
2
-torsion element of
\operatorname{Pic}(R)\cong{\rm Pic}(B)
. Moreover, the main theorem in
[
5
]
states that
\eta(X,X^{\prime})
is trivial if and only if
(X,f)
and
(X^{\prime},f^{\prime})
are equivalent as doubled planes.
These two results realize the set of equivalence classes of doubled planes over
{\mathbb{C}}
with branch curves
B
as a principal homogeneous space of a subgroup of
{\rm Pic}(B)[2]
. Since
{\rm Pic}(B)[2]
is finite, this completes the proof.

\Box

We apply the above result in our setting. Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be two smooth degree-
6
curves in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
. We say that two generic K3 surfaces
X
and
X^{\prime}
in
{\mathcal{F}}_{C_{1},C_{2}}
are
equivalent
if there exist elements
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\in{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{C}})
such that the pair of double planes
(X,\gamma_{i}\circ p_{i})
and
(X^{\prime},\gamma_{i}^{\prime}\circ p_{i})
are branched at
C_{i}
, and equivalent for

. Then we have the following immediate consequence of Proposition
15
:

Lemma 16

Fix two smooth degree-
6
curves
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
in
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
. Then the set
{\mathcal{F}}_{C_{1},C_{2}}
consists of finitely many equivalence classes.

Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be two equivalent generic K3 surfaces in
F_{\mathbb{C}}
. Then we obtain two isomorphisms
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}:X\to X^{\prime}
, coming from the two equivalences between the doubled planes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma_{1}\circ p_{1}}

by setting
\tau:=\phi_{1}^{-1}\circ\phi_{2}
. We next prove that in fact
\tau
has only finitely many possibilities. To this end, we begin with the following Lemma.

Lemma 17

Let
X
be a generic K3 surface in
F_{\mathbb{C}}
, and let
p_{1},p_{2}\colon X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
denote the two natural projection maps. Define divisor classes
H_{j}:=p_{j}^{*}\mathcal{O}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(1)
for
j=1,2
. Then:

(a)

The intersection pairing on
{\rm Pic}(X)
satisfies

H_{1}^{2}=H_{2}^{2}=2,\qquad H_{1}\cdot H_{2}=4.

(b)

The sublattice of
{\rm Pic}(X)
generated by
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
is isomorphic to
{\rm Pic}(F)
and has index dividing
4
in
{\rm Pic}(X)
.

Proof:

We begin with Part (a).
The intersection products in
X
can be calculated within the cohomology ring
H^{*}(F,{\mathbb{Z}})
of
F
, yielding

\begin{array}[]{rcl}H_{1}^{2}&=&({\mathcal{O}}_{F}(2,2),{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(1,0),{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(1,0));\\[7.22743pt]
H_{1}\cdot H_{2}&=&({\mathcal{O}}_{F}(2,2),{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(1,0),{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(0,1));\\[7.22743pt]
H_{2}^{2}&=&({\mathcal{O}}_{F}(2,2),{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(0,1),{\mathcal{O}}_{F}(0,1)).\end{array}

In particular, the value of these intersection numbers are independent of
X
, and are therefore as given by
[
12
, Proposition 2.6]
.

We move on to Part (b). Let
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
be generators of the lattice
{\rm Pic}(X)
. Define the integral quadratic form
f(x,y):=(xL_{1}+yL_{2})\cdot(xL_{1}+yL_{2})
given by the intersection pairing. Since
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
are also linearly independent elements in
{\rm Pic}(X)
, there exists a
2\times 2
matrix
\gamma
with integral entries such that

\gamma\cdot f(x,y)=(xH_{1}+yH_{2})\cdot(xH_{1}+yH_{2})=2(x^{2}+4xy+y^{2}).

Comparing discriminants on both sides and using the invariance of the discriminant under change of basis implies that
\det(\gamma)^{2}\mid 48
, and hence
\det(\gamma)\mid 4
.
This concludes the proof of the lemma.

\Box

We now have the following proposition.

Proposition 18

Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be two equivalent generic K3 surfaces in
F_{\mathbb{C}}
. Let
\tau\in{\rm Aut}(X)
be the corresponding automorphism of
X
. Then
\tau
has finitely many options.

Proof:

First note that the equivalence of
X
and
X^{\prime}
(with
\tau
being the associated automorphism) implies the existence of elements
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\gamma_{1}^{\prime},\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\in{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{C}})
and an isomorphism
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
such that

1.

The morphisms
\gamma_{1}\circ p_{1}
and
\gamma_{1}^{\prime}\circ p_{1}\circ\phi
from
X
to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
are the same, with branch curves
C_{1}
.

2.

The morphisms
\gamma_{2}\circ p_{2}
and
\gamma_{2}^{\prime}\circ p_{2}\circ\phi\circ\tau
from
X
to
{\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{\mathbb{C}}
are the same, with branch curve
C_{2}
.

For
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
H_{i}
denote the pullbacks
p_{i}^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(1)
on
X
, and let
H_{i}^{\prime}
denote the pullbacks
p_{i}^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(1)
on
X^{\prime}
. We have
\phi^{*}H_{1}^{\prime}
and
H_{1}
are linearly equivalent, and
\phi^{*}H_{2}^{\prime}
and
(\tau^{-1})^{*}H_{2}
are linearly equivalent. (This follows from the definitions of the maps, and Facts 1 and 2 above, in conjunction with the fact that for any
\gamma\in{\rm GL}_{3}({\mathbb{C}})
,
\gamma^{*}{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}_{2}}(1)
is linearly equivalent to
{\mathcal{O}}_{{\mathbb{P}}^{2}}(1)
.)

The automorphism
\tau
of
X
gives an action
\tau^{*}
on
{\rm Pic}(X)
, and moreover the kernel of the natural map
{\rm Aut}(X)\to{\rm Aut}({\rm Pic}(X))
is finite by
[
8
, Chapter 15, Theorem 2.6]
. The action of
\tau^{*}
on
{\rm Pic}(X)
is determined by the values of
\tau^{*}(H_{1})
and
\tau^{*}(H_{2})
. Therefore, it suffices to show that there are only finitely many possible possibilities for the pair
(\tau^{*}H_{1},\tau^{*}H_{2})
. We write

(\tau^{-1})^{*}H_{1}=a_{11}H_{1}+a_{12}H_{2},\quad(\tau^{-1})^{*}H_{2}=a_{21}H_{1}+a_{22}H_{2},

for values
a_{ij}\in{\mathbb{Q}}
. By Part (b) of Lemma
17
, we know that the matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq 2}
has determinant in
\pm\{1/4,1/2,1,2,4\}
and coefficients in
\frac{1}{4}{\mathbb{Z}}
. Moreover,
\tau
and
\tau^{-1}
, being automorphisms, preserve the intersection pairing on
{\rm Pic}(X)
. Consider the quadratic form
f(x,y):=(xH_{1}+yH_{2})\cdot(xH_{1}+yH_{2})
. We have
f(x,y)=2(x^{2}+4xy+y^{2})
by Part (a) of Lemma
17
, and
A
belongs to the orthogonal group of this quadratic form.

To further constrain the possible values of
A
, we proceed as follows. Let
L_{1}^{\prime}
and
L_{2}^{\prime}
generate
{\rm Pic}(X^{\prime})
. Since
\phi\colon X\to X^{\prime}
is an isomorphism, the pullbacks
L_{1}=\phi^{*}(L_{1}^{\prime})
and
L_{2}=\phi^{*}(L_{2}^{\prime})
generate
{\rm Pic}(X)
. By Lemma
17
(b), there are integral
2\times 2
matrices
M,M^{\prime}
with
|\det(M)|,|\det(M^{\prime})|\mid 4
such that

M\begin{pmatrix}L_{1}\\
L_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}\\
H_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad M^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}L_{1}^{\prime}\\
L_{2}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}^{\prime}\\
H_{2}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

Applying
\phi^{*}
to the second equation and combining with the first gives a matrix
N=M^{\prime}M^{-1}\in{\rm GL}_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})
such that

N\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}\\
H_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\phi^{*}\!\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}^{\prime}\\
H_{2}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}\\
(\tau^{-1})^{*}H_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Since we have

N=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix},\qquad m,n\in{\mathbb{Q}},

it follows that
a_{22}=\det(N)
has only finitely many possibilities. Combining this with the intersection pairing property
(\tau^{-1})^{*}H_{2}\cdot(\tau^{-1})^{*}H_{2}=2
, additionally gives
f(a_{21},a_{22})=2
. Therefore,
a_{21}
also has only finitely many possibilities.

Recall that
\det(A)
is constrained to lie in a finite set. Once
a_{21}
and
a_{22}
are fixed, we note that the condition
f(a_{11},a_{12})=2
(implied by the invariance of the intersection pairing under), constrains the pair
(a_{11},a_{12})
to also belong to a finite set. Indeed,
f(x,y)
is irreducible, and so the intersection of
f(x,y)=2
with any line has only finitely many solutions
(x,y)
. Therefore,
A
has only finitely many possibilities, and we are done.
