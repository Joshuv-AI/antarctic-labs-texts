Prognostic role of HSPs in human colorectal cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ling-Juan Zhang, Cai-Yun Zhang, Ming-Xin Li, Dan Zhang, Can Wang, Ming Zhang, Chang-E Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeat shock proteins (HSPs) play pivotal roles in cellular stress responses and are associated with the progression and treatment resistance of various cancers. They are also critically important in the occurrence, development, and prognosis of colorectal cancer (CRC). However, the specific prognostic value of HSPs in CRC remains controversial. This study aimed to conduct a meta-analysis to evaluate the prognostic significance of HSPs in CRC patients.MethodsLiterature searches were conducted in the PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, and China Biology Medicine (CBM) databases up to March 15, 2026. Data regarding the relationship between the expression of HSPs and survival outcomes were extracted. The pooled hazard ratio (HR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated.ResultsWe included 42 studies involving a total of 8,142 CRC patients. The results indicated that elevated levels of HSPs were associated with a poorer survival prognosis in CRC patients (HR = 1.78, 95% CI [1.48, 2.14]; p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis of overall survival revealed that high levels of HSP27 (HR = 1.72, 95% CI [1.23, 2.41]), HSP47 (HR = 2.43, 95% CI [1.82, 3.24]), HSP70 (HR = 2.14, 95% CI [1.74, 2.63]), and HSP90 (HR = 2.02, 95% CI [1.31, 3.14]) were significantly associated with a poorer survival prognosis in CRC patients(p < 0.05 for all comparisons).ConclusionThis meta-analysis highlights the significant role of HSPs in the prognostic evaluation of CRC patients. Elevated levels of HSP27, HSP47, HSP70, and HSP90 are identified as crucial biomarkers indicative of poor prognosis in CRC patients, with particularly noteworthy associations between high expression levels of HSP47 and HSP70 and adverse outcomes in CRC. While some heterogeneity was noted in the analysis, the results of the subgroup analysis reinforce the involvement of specific HSP family members in tumor progression. To further substantiate the independent prognostic value of HSPs and to investigate their potential as therapeutic targets for CRC, future studies should be rigorously designed, standardized, and conducted on a large scale.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-026-16125-9.Keywords: Heat shock proteins, Colorectal cancer, Prognosis, Survival rate, Meta-analysis

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most prevalent malignant tumor globally and is the second leading cause of cancer-related fatalities [1]. Although the majority of newly diagnosed cases and deaths have occurred in individuals over the age of 70, there has been a notable increase in incidence and mortality rates among adults under 50 across nearly all regions worldwide since the early 1990s [2]. Concurrently, with economic development and the Westernization of dietary and lifestyle habits, the burden of CRC is transitioning from high-income countries to low- and middle-income nations [3]. Furthermore, the global burden of CRC is projected to increase by 60% by 2030 [4].

In recent years, the treatment of CRC has progressively evolved toward a multidisciplinary comprehensive treatment model. This model integrates various approaches, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, which not only increase the quality of life of patients but also prolong their survival time [5]. However, approximately 20% of patients are diagnosed with metastasis, and up to 50% eventually develop metastatic CRC, which remains incurable in most cases [6]. Currently, commonly used clinical prognostic assessment indicators include tumor node metastasis (TNM) stage, degree of tumor differentiation, lymph node metastasis status, and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels. However, the predictive power of these indicators is limited and fails to fully capture the complexity of tumor biological behavior.

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) represent a structurally related and sequence-homologous family of proteins encoded by multiple gene families that are prevalent across a wide range of organisms from bacteria to humans. Under normal physiological conditions, these proteins are expressed at low basal levels; however, they are significantly induced under stress conditions, including high temperature, hypoxia, chemical toxins, or infection. Their primary function is to act as ‘molecular chaperones’, facilitating protein folding, assembly, transport, and degradation, thereby maintaining proteome stability. Currently, HSPs can be classified into six main families: HSP100s, HSP90s, HSP70s, HSP60s, HSP47s, and small heat shock proteins (sHSPs) [7, 8]. These proteins are highly expressed in tumors and play crucial roles in various stages of tumorigenesis and development. Different types of HSPs exhibit distinct distributions and functions during the progression of various tumors [9]. In recent years, numerous studies have reported on the expression of HSPs in CRC and their relationship with prognosis; however, the results have been inconsistent, and a systematic summary is lacking. Therefore, this study aims to comprehensively evaluate the prognostic value of HSPs family members in CRC patients through meta-analysis, providing evidence-based support for clinical practice.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines [10] and has been registered with PROSPERO (International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews, CRD420251136579). This analysis aimed to evaluate the impact of HSPs on survival endpoints in CRC patients. (The PRISMA checklist is provided in Supplementary Material 1)

Search strategy and selection criteria

This meta-analysis employed a systematic search strategy to identify relevant studies published between January 2010 and March 2026 across several databases, including PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang, and China Biology Medicine (CBM). This approach adhered to the Reporting Recommendations for Tumor Marker Prognostic Studies (REMARK) [11]. The search incorporated keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms such as Heat-shock proteins (HSPs) and colorectal neoplasms. The search strategy employed a combination of subject headings and free-text terms, with keywords integrated via Boolean operators: (“Heat-Shock Proteins” OR “HSP” OR “HSP*” )AND (“Colorectal Neoplasms” OR “Colorectal Cancer” OR “Colon cancer” OR “Rectal cancer” OR “CRC” )AND (“Prognosis” OR “Survival” OR “Prognostic” OR “Survival” OR “Outcome” OR “Mortality”). The syntax was tailored to the specific characteristics of each database (detailed search strategies are provided in Supplementary Material 2). Furthermore, we conducted a manual search for recommended references from systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and conference proceedings. All retrieved literature was managed using Zotero Version 8.0.4 (Corporation for Digital Scholarship, 2024) to remove duplicate entries. The search and screening process was independently conducted by three authors (Lingjuan Zhang, Caiyun Zhang, and Mingxin Li). In case of disagreements regarding study inclusion, a third party (Chang`e Gao) was consulted to reach a consensus.

Three researchers independently extracted the studies on the basis of the following inclusion criteria: (1) population: patients with CRC diagnosed through cytology or pathology; (2) exposure: detection of HSPs expression levels in tissue or plasma, with clearly defined criteria for high and low grouping; (3) study type: cohort studies (either prospective or retrospective), case-control studies, or observational data from randomized controlled trials that describe the relationship between clinicopathological parameters and HSPs expression levels; (4) outcome: the study must provide hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) with sufficient data, or Kaplan-Meier survival curves from which HR and 95% CI can be extracted. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) animal experiments or in vitro studies; (2) reviews, commentaries, letters, editorials, opinions, case reports, and expert opinions; (3) duplicate publications or studies with insufficient data; (4) studies that did not clearly distinguish between HSP subtypes; and (5) studies that lacked sufficient information to calculate HR and 95% CI.

Endpoints and subanalyses

The primary endpoint of this meta-analysis was overall survival (OS), with secondary outcomes including disease-free survival (DFS), relapse-free survival (RFS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and time to recurrence (TTR). OS was defined as the time from enrollment to death (from any cause) or the last follow-up; DFS was defined as the time from enrollment to disease recurrence or death (from any cause); RFS was defined as the time from enrollment to the first occurrence of tumor recurrence, metastasis, or death from any cause; DSS was defined as the time from enrollment to death due to the target cancer; TTR was defined as the time from enrollment to the first occurrence of tumor recurrence or metastasis. These endpoints were analyzed separately due to their distinct clinical meanings and potential heterogeneity. Perform pre-specified subgroup analyses to investigate specific factors influencing the overall estimates, such as differences between various subtypes of HSP.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two researchers (Caiyun Zhang and Mingxin Li) independently performed literature assessment and data extraction using a pre-designed spreadsheet. The extracted data included: Study characteristics (first author’s name, country, publication year, study design), study design features (study initiation time, follow-up duration, cutoff values, methods and samples used, calculation methods, outcome measures), participant-related data (sample size, gender, age, HSP subtypes, tumor location, tumor stage, treatment modalities). Extract HRs and 95% CIs related to prognosis, prioritizing multivariable-adjusted HRs, and conduct subgroup analyses by adjustment levels. For studies that only presented Kaplan - Meier curves, the online IPDfromKM tool [12] was utilized to obtain individual patient data (IPD). The data were processed and organized using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Meanwhile, SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) was employed to calculate the HR and 95% CI for OS or DFS of the corresponding studies. (For specific methods, please refer to Supplementary Material 3). In cases of discrepancies in data extraction, a third party (Chang’e Gao) was consulted to reach a consensus.

Quality assessment was conducted through independent evaluation by two reviewers (Dan Zhang and Can Wang) via the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) to assess the quality of the included studies. The evaluation criteria focused on three main aspects: population selection (4 points), comparability (2 points), and outcome measurement (3 points). Studies that achieved an NOS score of ≥ 7 were classified as high quality, whereas those with a score of ≤ 6 were deemed as low quality. Only studies judged to be of high quality (NOS score ≥ 7) were included in this meta-analysis. And resolve the discrepancies in the assessment through consultation with the third party (Ming Zhang) (detailed scoring results are provided in Supplementary Material 4).

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was conducted via Stata version 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The pooled estimates of HR and 95% CI were used to evaluate the relationships between HSPs expression and OS or DFS. Forest plots were employed to present the results of individual studies and the combined effect size. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed via the I² test ,τ² and Cochrane’s Q test; a random-effects model was applied when I²> 50%, indicating significant heterogeneity, whereas a fixed-effects model was used otherwise. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on HSP subtype, time of follow-up initiation, detection platform, sample type, sample size, tumor stage, treatment modality, and gender ratio. Publication bias was assessed using Begg’s and Egger’s tests. Sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding low-quality studies or individual studies to assess the stability of the results. p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics and quality of included studies

As shown in Fig. 1 (generated using the online tool PRISMA Flow Diagram [13]), the initial search yielded 849 relevant articles. After removing duplicate records and ineligible studies, 43 articles were retained and underwent full-text review based on the inclusion criteria. Finally, 31 articles comprising 42 cohorts were included in the study [14–44], involving 8,142 CRC patients from 3 prospective cohort studies and 39 retrospective cohort studies. Most patients underwent surgical treatment and adjuvant chemotherapy. The baseline characteristics of all included articles are presented in (Table 1). These studies were published between January 1, 2010 and March 15, 2026, including 28 English studies and 3 Chinese studies. Most studies evaluate baseline HSPs levels prior to chemotherapy. Among them, 22 studies used immunohistochemistry (IHC) to detect HSPs expression in tissues, 9 employed ELISA to measure serum HSPs levels, and 9 utilized PCR to examine HSPs mRNA expression. The included studies had NOS scores ranging from 7 to 9, all being high-quality studies.

Overall analysis of the relationship between HSPs and OS in CRC

This meta-analysis included 23 articles comprising 30 cohort studies to investigate the relationship between HSP expression levels and OS in CRC patients. As shown in Fig. 3, the pooled results revealed significant heterogeneity among studies (I²= 75.58%, p < 0.001). Using a random-effects model, we found that CRC patients with high HSP levels had shorter OS compared to those with low HSP levels (pooled HR = 1.78, 95% CI: [1.48, 2.14]; p<0.001). These findings suggest that high HSP levels are significantly associated with poor prognosis in CRC patients.

Forest plot indicating the association between HSPs expression and overall survival (OS) in human colorectal cancer

To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses based on HSP subtypes, detection methods, sample types, follow-up starting points, tumor stages, treatment regimens, sample sizes, and sex ratios. The specific grouping strategies for each subgroup are detailed in Supplementary Material 6. Differences between subgroups were compared using Q-tests. We found that different HSP subtypes (Qb = 56.50, p < 0.001), detection methods (Qb = 7.26, p = 0.03), follow-up starting times (Qb = 12.37, p = 0.01), and sample sizes (Qb = 14.02, p < 0.001) were the main sources of heterogeneity. Among HSP subtype subgroups, high expression of HSP27, HSP47, HSP70, and HSP90 was associated with shorter OS (all p < 0.05). Notably, HSP27 exhibited significant heterogeneity (I²= 69.16%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Further subgroup analyses were conducted for HSP27 based on detection methods, sample types, follow-up starting points, tumor stages, treatment regimens, sample sizes, and gender ratios. The heterogeneity of HSP27 was found to be primarily attributed to detection methods (Qb = 6.70, p = 0.01) and sample sizes (Qb = 8.23, p = 0.00) (Fig. 5).

Overall analysis of the relationship between HSPs and RFS, DFS, DSS, and TTR in CRC patients

This meta-analysis included 5 articles comprising 7 cohort studies that investigated the relationship between HSP expression levels and DFS in CRC patients. A total of 2 articles, including 2 cohort studies, examined the association between HSP expression levels and DSS in CRC patients. Three articles, involving 3 cohort studies, explored the correlation between HSP expression levels and RFS in CRC patients. One article, containing 2 cohort studies, studied the relationship between HSP expression levels and TTR in CRC patients. As shown in Fig. 6, CRC patients with high HSPs levels exhibited shorter DFS time compared to those with low HSPs levels (pooled HR = 1.99, 95% CI: [1.20, 3.31]; p = 0.01). This suggests that high HSPs expression is significantly associated with poorer disease-free survival in CRC patients. The pooled results of DFS, DSS and RFS groups showed significant heterogeneity among studies (I²>50%, p < 0.001). However, due to the limited number of included cohort studies, further meta-regression, subgroup and sensitivity analyses could not be performed on these results.

The prognostic relationship between HSP110 T17 deletion/ HSP110wt and MSI-type CRC

HSP110∆E9 is the first and only known HSPs mutation identified in cancer to date [45, 46]. It is systematically mutated in microsatellite instability (MSI) CRC cell lines and primary tumors, generated by exon 9 skipping due to a T17 single nucleotide repeat sequence located in intron 8 of HSP110, resulting in a truncated protein. The deletion of the HSP110 T17 microsatellite has been proven to be an effective marker for detecting MSI CRC [47]. Moreover, multiple studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between HSP110 levels and HSP110∆E9 expression or HSP110 T17 deletion [17, 21, 28]. Relevant studies have also focused on MSI-type CRC. Therefore, we separately analyzed the research on HSP T17 deletion and HSP110 wild-type (HSP110wt).

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

Publication bias for OS assessment was evaluated using Begg’s test and Egger’s test. Visual inspection of the funnel plot indicates that most studies on HSPs in CRC patients are concentrated in the upper right portion, and the funnel plot is asymmetrical (Fig. 9A), Begg’s test p = 0.0457,suggesting the possibility of publication bias. However, further detection using Egger’s linear regression method for small sample effects revealed no significant publication bias (p = 0.074) (Fig. 9B).

Discussion

Prognostic value of HSPs

HSPs are a class of evolutionarily highly conserved molecular chaperones that play a significant role in various biological processes, including the maintenance of intracellular protein homeostasis, stress responses, and signal transduction. The tumor microenvironment (TME) is often characterized by harsh conditions such as hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and oxidative stress, where the stress adaptability of HSPs becomes crucial for cancer cell survival [48, 49]. HSPs directly or indirectly promote malignant tumor progression and therapy resistance through multiple mechanisms, including: maintaining oncoprotein stability [50], modulating key pathways such as PI3K/Akt and STAT3 [51, [52], regulating cancer cell metabolism and mitochondrial function [53, 54], activating cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to foster an immunosuppressive TME [55], controlling cellular biological behaviors (e.g., epithelial-mesenchymal transition, migration, invasion) [56], and responding to shear stress [57]. Furthermore, within the TME, HSPs not only participate in protection against cellular stress but also play a central role in immune regulation. Primarily, as natural adjuvants, HSPs can induce tumor immune responses by binding to tumor antigen peptides and promoting the cross-presentation of these antigens [58, 59]. Secondly, HSPs can influence tumor immune surveillance and therapeutic efficacy by modulating the functions of immune cells such as T cells, dendritic cells (DCs), and macrophages. For instance, HSPs can induce the formation of immunosuppressive cells and secrete anti-inflammatory factors that inhibit anti-tumor immunity [60].Conversely, HSP-gp96 peptide complexes can activate DCs and cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), thereby inducing specific anti-tumor immune responses [61]. Furthermore, HSPs may also promote tumor immune escape by regulating immune checkpoints and utilizing exosome-mediated signal transduction [62, 63]. Consequently, HSPs not only facilitate tumor progression but also serve as potential immunomodulatory targets to activate anti-tumor immune responses. The HSPs family is involved in tumor proliferation, metastasis, immune evasion, and therapy resistance through its diverse structures and functions, which may also serve as prognostic markers and therapeutic targets.

A meta-analysis conducted by Ge et al. in 2018 reviewed the literature published between 1999 and 2017 on the association between HSPs and the prognosis of human gastrointestinal cancers (GICs). The analysis found that while HSPs expression did not significantly correlate with OS in GICs (HR = 1.17, 95% CI [0.90, 1.52], p = 0.25), it was significantly associated with poor OS in CRC patients (HR = 1.67, 95% CI [1.25, 2.24], p = 0.0006) [64]. This finding aligns with our research results, indicating that HSPs can serve as prognostic markers for CRC, where high expression correlates with a poor prognosis.

However, as stated in the Art of War by Sun Tzu: “There are no constant military dispositions, just as there is no constant shape to water.” Similarly, different HSPs members also exhibit distinct characteristics and diverse structural functions, playing a “double-edged sword” role in tumorigenesis through their trinity of molecular chaperoning, stress protection, and immune regulation. The TME of CRC constitutes a highly dynamic and heterogeneous ecosystem composed of various cell types, the extracellular matrix (ECM), and signaling molecules [65, 66]. Moreover, cancer-related proteins and dysregulated pathways vary across different regions of the colon [67].

Collectively, these factors shape the complex biological behaviors and therapeutic responses of CRC. Additionally, multiple factors—including individual diet, host metabolism, endocrine signaling, and dynamic interactions within the gut microbial network—can influence CRC development [68–71]. In summary, the role of different HSPs family members as prognostic biomarkers remains debatable, given their varied expression patterns across different CRC locations, developmental stages, population groups, and treatment regimens. For practical clinical application, further differentiation of HSP subtypes, combined with other biomarkers and individual patient characteristics, is essential for achieving more precise prognostic assessments.

HSP27

HSP27 is a small heat shock protein with a molecular weight of approximately 27 kDa. As a molecular chaperone, the core function of HSP27 is to assist other proteins in proper folding, maintain their stability, and prevent protein misfolding and denaturation during cellular stress (such as chemotherapy or heat shock) [72]. And it plays multiple roles in the occurrence, development, metastasis, and treatment of CRC. For instance, HSP27 can regulate tumor growth via the p38MAPK signaling pathway, and the inhibition of this pathway can significantly reduce tumor proliferation and invasion [73]. HSP27 interacts with long non-coding RNAs and can further influence the progression of CRC by regulating the expression of miRNAs [74]. Additionally, HSP27 plays a role in inhibiting ferroptosis to promote chemotherapy resistance in CRC [41]. Its high expression reduces the sensitivity of CRC cells to chemotherapeutic drugs such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU). Conversely, by inhibiting the expression of HSP27, the response of cancer cells to chemotherapeutic drugs can be significantly enhanced, and apoptosis can be promoted [75].

Although this meta-analysis suggests that patients with high HSP27 expression had significantly shorter OS, substantial heterogeneity was observed among studies (I²=69.16, p = 0.01), and multiple studies still indicated no significant association between HSP27 and CRC prognosis. Through further subgroup analyses, we found that detection methods and sample sizes might be potential sources of heterogeneity: the effect size measured by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (HR = 3.62) was much greater than that by immunohistochemistry (IHC) (HR = 1.45); small-sample studies (≤ 200 cases) tended to overestimate the hazard of HSP27 (HR = 3.03), while large-sample studies yielded relatively moderate results (HR = 1.28). Since the included studies did not provide sufficient outcome measures to differentiate between various CRC sites, we did not perform further subgroup analyses by tumor location. However, in the study by Elizabeth M Tweedle et al., it was found that in the cohort with poor prognosis, HSP27 served as an independent adverse prognostic marker specific to rectal cancer, with stable expression unaffected by neoadjuvant radiotherapy; however, a similar association was not observed in colon cancer. In the cohort with a better prognosis, there was no significant correlation between HSP27 expression and the prognosis of either rectal or colon cancer patients. The authors of the study suggested that the observed results may be attributed to a notably lower number of male patients in the poor prognosis group, the limited number of rectal cancer samples (n = 115), and the overall better prognosis, which may obscure the prognostic impact of HSP27 [31]. Additionally, the study by Bauer et al. identified HSP27 as an independent poor prognostic factor in left-sided colon cancer patients (HR = 1.624, 95% CI [1.085–2.43]) [26]. The differing significance of HSP27 based on tumor location may be attributed to the distinct genetic and molecular backgrounds of right- and left-sided colon cancers, as well as the closer genetic similarity between left-sided colon cancer and rectal cancer. For instance, the expression patterns of immune-related genes (IRGs) in right-sided colon cancer diverge from those in left-sided colon cancer, potentially influencing the immune microenvironment and treatment outcomes [76].

However, in contrast to the aforementioned two studies, the research conducted by Jan Hrudka et al. found no significant correlation between HSP27 expression and shortened OS (p = 0.2), and elevated expression of HSP27 in rectal cancer instead showed a trend towards better prognosis [24]. These contradictory results may stem from the varying male-to-female ratios across different study cohorts, necessitating further studies with larger sample sizes and more balanced gender ratios to clarify the associations. In summary, HSP27 may not serve as a reliable predictive marker in CRC. To increase the reliability of HSP27 in predicting the prognosis of CRC patients, it is recommended to evaluate it in combination with other markers. For example, the study by Wang et al. suggests that HSP27 and heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein K (hnRNP K) are independent poor prognostic biomarkers in CRC, and their combined detection can significantly improve the stratification of patient prognosis, potentially predicting the response to 5-FU chemotherapy [29]. Nevertheless, additional evidence is required to substantiate their reliability.

Chaperone DnaJ

The DNAJ protein, also known as heat shock protein 40 (HSP40), is a crucial member of the molecular chaperone family and plays a fundamental role in maintaining cellular homeostasis. The DNAJ protein family comprises numerous members, which can be primarily classified into three subfamilies—DNAJA, DNAJB, and DNAJC—based on their distinct domains [77]. Although this meta-analysis included only two studies investigating the association between DNAJ proteins and CRC prognosis, and the differing outcome measures precluded a combined assessment, multiple studies have demonstrated that several DNAJ members act as oncogenic factors in CRC. For instance, DNAJB6 promotes CRC cell invasion and lung metastasis through the IQGAP1/ERK signaling pathway, with its high expression in CRC independently associated with tumor invasion and poor prognosis [33]. DNAJA1 is highly expressed in CRC tissues and significantly correlated with serosal invasion, lymph node metastasis, and poor prognosis [30]. The expression level of DNAJC2 is significantly associated with tumor size, and its overexpression can accelerate cell proliferation [78]. DNAJ proteins also serve as important mediators of chemotherapy resistance in CRC. Studies have found that DNAJB8 can interact with TP53, inhibiting its ubiquitination and degradation, leading to the upregulation of the multidrug resistance gene 1 (MDR1) and thereby promoting resistance to oxaliplatin (L-OHP) in CRC [79]. The DNAJ/HSP40 protein family plays a crucial role in the development of CRC, making it a highly valuable prognostic biomarker and a potential target for a range of innovative therapeutic strategies (such as small molecule inhibitors, immunotherapy, etc.).

HSP47

HSP47 is an endoplasmic reticulum-resident protein with collagen-binding properties and is located on human chromosome 11q13.5, a “hotspot” region in various human cancers [80]. As a collagen-specific molecular chaperone, HSP47 facilitates collagen maturation and secretion, thereby influencing extracellular matrix remodeling, which plays a pivotal role in tumor invasion and metastasis [81]. Furthermore, HSP47 interacts with PHLPP1 (an AKT-specific phosphatase), reducing the stability of PHLPP1, thereby enhancing AKT phosphorylation and promoting tumor cell survival and chemotherapy resistance [22].

In this meta-analysis, the HR of all studies were greater than 1, indicating substantial effect sizes. This suggests highly consistent and robust evidence supporting HSP47 as a significant risk factor, with its elevated expression level reflecting a strong potential for hazard. Targeting HSP47 may simultaneously disrupt both the survival mechanisms of tumor cells and immune evasion strategies, rendering it an exceptionally attractive therapeutic target. The future development of HSP47 inhibitors is anticipated to offer new therapeutic hope for CRC patients with poor prognoses.

HSP60

HSP60 as a mitochondrial chaperone protein, plays a significant role in the occurrence, development, and prognosis of CRC. It is essential for maintaining mitochondrial protein homeostasis. Studies have demonstrated that the knockdown of HSP60 inhibits mTOR-mediated protein synthesis by disrupting mitochondrial protein homeostasis and activating the AMPK signaling pathway, thereby slowing the proliferation of CRC cells [82]. Additionally, long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) regulates VEGF-A expression through the miR-342-3p and HSP60 pathways, promoting angiogenesis and further driving the progression of CRC [83].

A study of metastatic CRC conducted by Vocka et al., it was found that elevated serum levels of HSP60 are associated with poorer prognosis and possess independent prognostic significance, particularly concerning liver metastasis burden [32]. This finding aligns with the previous research by Hamelin et al., who observed that serum HSP60 levels were significantly higher in CRC patients compared to the healthy control group, showing increased specificity in patients with advanced CRC [84]. Conversely, the findings of Kang et al. present a contrasting perspective: high expression of HSP60 in patient tissues correlates with improved prognosis, especially in advanced CRC. This finding was further substantiated by the correlation between the high HSP60 expression group and elevated p53 expression (multivariate analysis p = 0.033), indicating that HSP60 may facilitate tumor cell apoptosis through its interaction with p5315. The study by Karina Bauer et al. found that although HSP60 is highly expressed in tissues of most CRC cases, its relationship with prognosis remains unclear [26].

In summary, the prognostic value of HSP60 in CRC exhibits significant heterogeneity. This paradoxical phenomenon may arise from various factors, including differences in sample types (serum versus tissue), detection methods, and the tumor microenvironment. Serum HSP60 levels primarily reflect systemic inflammatory responses and tumor burden; thus, higher levels are associated with poor prognosis. In contrast, tissue HSP60 expression likely involves complex molecular interactions, such as influencing tumor cell fate by stabilizing or modulating p53 function. Its prognostic significance may be constrained by tumor stage, subcellular localization, and co-expression status with other molecules. Furthermore, the “cross-boundary” role of HSP60 between tumor cells and the immune microenvironment may contribute to its seemingly contradictory clinical significance across different research contexts. Future research should prioritize multicenter collaborations, standardized methodologies, and in-depth mechanistic studies to elucidate the true prognostic value of HSP60 in CRC and to establish a robust evidence-based foundation for its clinical application.

HSP70

HSP70 is one of the most extensively studied HSPs, which not only participates in the regulation of apoptosis in CRC but also influences the proliferation, migration, and invasion of tumor cells. Studies have shown that HSP70 inhibitors can significantly suppress the proliferation and migration of CRC cells [85, 86]. Furthermore, HSP70 plays a crucial role in immune regulation within CRC. Research indicates that exogenous HSP70 can activate anti-tumor immune responses, enhancing the activity of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer cells [87]. CRC-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) carrying HSP70 can enhance the phagocytic ability of macrophages by upregulating the expression of macrophage receptors, thereby promoting the clearance of tumor cells [88]. The expression level of HSP70 is closely related to the prognosis of CRC patients. A meta-analysis conducted by Gao et al. in 2021 reviewed the literature published between July 9, 1989, and July 9, 2020, concerning the association between HSP70 expression and CRC prognosis. These findings demonstrated that HSP70 overexpression was correlated with poorer OS (HR = 0.65,95% CI[0.52–0.78]) and DFS(HR = 0.77 ,95% CI[ 0.23–1.32], ps: the null line is at 0) in CRC patients [89]. The results of this meta-analysis are consistent with previous findings.

The studies included in this meta-analysis also suggest that high levels of serum HSP70 are most strongly associated with the risk of mortality in female patients under 70 years of age [27, 40]. It is speculated that this association may be related to the modulation of the protective function of estrogen by high serum HSP70 levels in premenopausal women [90]. Additionally, the combination of HSP70 with other indicators can enhance the accuracy of prognostic assessments: for instance, serum HSP70 combined with acute-phase proteins (CRPs, C1-INH) [27] or combined with serum Mortalin (Mortalin is a mitochondrial molecular chaperone protein, belonging to the HSP70 family, involved in mitochondrial protein import and folding, and has significant anti-apoptotic and pro-cancer effects) [14, 18], both of which have the potential for precise prediction.

Thus, HSP70 represents both a promising therapeutic target and a biomarker indicative of poor prognosis, suggesting that its complex regulatory network merits further investigation within the realm of translational medicine.

HSP90

HSP90 is a highly conserved molecular chaperone protein that is widely present across various organisms, from bacteria to mammals [91]. The HSP90 family comprises constitutively expressed HSP90β and its stress-inducible isoform HSP90α, along with other members such as tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated protein 1 (TRAP1) and glucose-regulated protein 94 (GRP94) [92]. Under normal conditions, HSP90 facilitates the proper folding of proteins, thereby preventing their misfolding and aggregation. In response to stressors such as heat, heavy metals, hypoxia, and acidosis, the expression of HSP90 is upregulated, which aids in cell survival. During tumorigenesis, the function of HSP90 is co-opted, allowing for malignant transformation and rapid somatic evolution, while also enabling cancer cells to tolerate imbalanced signaling [93]. Furthermore, HSP90 promotes the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process by activating the HIF-1α and NF-κB signaling pathways, which enhances the invasive and migratory capabilities of tumor cells [56]. Plasma levels of HSP90α are significantly elevated in CRC patients and are closely associated with tumor stage, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis [94].

Although the sensitivity of HSP90α as a standalone diagnostic marker is limited, its combination with traditional markers such as CEA and carbohydrate antigen 199(CA199) can significantly improve its diagnostic efficacy for early-stage CRC [95, 96]. HSP90 holds great potential as a therapeutic target for CRC. For example, HSP90 inhibitors can reverse deoxyribose nucleic acid (DNA) methylation and reactivate tumor suppressor genes [97]; additionally, the combination of HSP90 inhibitors with other treatment modalities (such as radiotherapy and chemotherapy) can significantly enhance anti-tumor effects [98]; Furthermore, HSP90 inhibitors can enhance the cytotoxic capacity of CD8+T cells, thereby exerting anti-tumor immune effects [99].

This meta-analysis suggests that patients with high HSP90 expression have significantly shorter OS. However, the prognostic significance of different HSP90 subtypes in CRC patients varies. Further clarification is still needed on the specific impact of different subtypes on prognosis, so as to provide more reliable evidence for prognostic stratification and the development of targeted therapy for colorectal cancer.

HSP110

HSP110 is strongly expressed in the majority of CRC tumors and is involved in the stabilization of oncoproteins, promoting cancer cell proliferation, metastasis, and poor prognosis [100]. HSP110 can promote the proliferation of CRC cells by activating the STAT3 signaling pathway [101]. Additionally, HSP110 plays a role in modulating the immune microenvironment, and its elevated expression can drive the differentiation of macrophages into pro-tumor, anti-inflammatory phenotypes [102]. Furthermore, HSP110 is significant in DNA damage repair, offers protection to tumor cells against chemotherapy-induced DNA damage [103], and is involved in chromatin remodeling and cellular stress responses [104]. Importantly, HSP110 presents a potential therapeutic target for CRC, with studies indicating that HSP110 inhibitors may effectively suppress tumors in CRC animal models [45]. Conversely, research has also demonstrated that the overexpression of HSP110 can enhance the immunogenicity of tumor antigens [105]. Meanwhile, the HSP110 protein, a close relative of the canonical HSP70 chaperone protein and an important nucleotide exchange factor (NEF) [106], functions as a molecular chaperone with anti-aggregation properties. It effectively present tumor-specific antigen peptides, activate CD8+T cells, and trigger anti-tumor immune responses [107, 108]. The amino acid sequence and expression pattern of HSP110 are highly conserved in humans, making it a potential tumor-associated antigen (TAA). Researchers have identified specific CTL epitopes derived from HSP110, that can induce specific T-cell responses in CRC patients with favorable safety profiles [109]. Additionally, an HSP110 peptide vaccine has been developed, and phase I clinical trials have demonstrated its ability to induce functional CTLs [110]. In addition, HSP110 may promote the differentiation of T cells, particularly towards Th1 or Th2 cells, through the IL2-MYC-IL2RA pathway, thereby enhancing the infiltration of CD8+T cells in the tumor region and inhibiting tumor invasion [111]. HSP110 plays a dual role in the CRC microenvironment, potentially driving the disease in completely opposite directions.

The HSP110 mutant, HSP110∆E9, lacks the substrate-binding domain of HSP110, resulting in the loss of its functions as an anti-aggregation chaperone and a nucleotide exchange factor for the HSP70 protein [20]. In the HCT116 subclone, when the HSP110 T17 deletion is ≥ 4 bp, the ratio of HSP110∆E9 to HSP110wt mRNA significantly increases. As a dominant-negative mutant, HSP110∆E9 can bind to HSP110 at a 1:1 ratio, disrupting the normal cellular localization of HSP110 and its interactions with other HSPs. This interference inhibits the chaperone effect and anti-apoptotic function of HSP110 in tumor cells, effectively acting as an endogenous inhibitor of HSP110 [36, 101]. Additionally, HSP110∆E9 is an aberrant form of HSP110 generated by mutations in MSI-type CRC. The HSP110 T17 microsatellite marker demonstrates high sensitivity and specificity in detecting MSI in CRC, outperforming the traditional pentaplex markers, thus providing a simpler and more economical method for MSI screening in CRC [47, 112]. Consequently, research on the association between HSP110 and prognosis has primarily concentrated on MSI CRC, with only one study indicating no significant association between HSP110 levels and the prognosis of CRC patients [24].

In studies examining the prognosis of MSI CRC patients, research by Dorard et al. And Collura et al. suggests that stage II-III tumor patients who received chemotherapy and exhibited a high deletion of HSP110 T17 (or high expression of HSP110ΔE9) demonstrated a significant 19% and 35% improvement in 5-year recurrence-free survival compared to those with smaller deletions [20, 36]. However, paradoxically, a 2014 study by Kim et al.found that while low expression of HSP110wt predicted a better prognosis, and was significantly correlated with the size of T17 deletion, the size of the HSP110 T17 deletion itself did not show significant prognostic value (p > 0.05) [21]. In their 2015 study, none of the HSP110wt-negative patients experienced recurrence or death; however, HSP110wt did not exhibit a significant association with the prognosis of MSI CRC patients [35]. Furthermore, the study by Oh et al. confirmed that HSP110wt was significantly correlated with the size of the HSP110 T17 deletion, but neither HSP110wt expression nor the size of HSP110 T17 deletion demonstrated prognostic significance (p > 0.05). Additionally, they established that the Dominant Intensity Score is the most effective method for evaluating HSP110wt IHC [17]. In a recent large-scale, multicenter prospective analysis conducted by Gaelle Tachon et al., the correlation between HSP110wt and HSP110 T17 deletion was reaffirmed, with the Dominant intensity score uniformly used to assess HSP110wt IHC. Furthermore, the authors concluded that HSP110 expression and the size of the HSP110 T17 deletion lack prognostic or predictive value regarding chemotherapy efficacy in MSI CRC [28].

This recurrent phenomenon of “significant correlation but insignificant prognostic value” profoundly illustrates the complexity of HSP110’s biological functions in MSI-type CRCr and highlights the limitations of current evaluation methods. Although multiple studies have confirmed a significant positive correlation between the expression level of HSP110wt and the size of T17 deletion, suggesting a close molecular mechanistic relationship, this association has not consistently translated into reliable clinical prognostic stratification. This indicates that relying solely on the size of the HSP110 T17 deletion or HSP110wt expression levels may be insufficient to fully capture its true role in tumorigenesis and progression. Furthermore, variations in detection methods (such as mRNA level assays versus IHC) and evaluation criteria (including the introduction of the Dominant Intensity Score) across different studies may have influenced the prognostic analysis results. Therefore, future research should move beyond single molecular markers, comprehensively consider different HSP110 isoforms, their interactions with the tumor microenvironment, and other synergistic molecular events, while also extending the study to all CRC patients to more accurately elucidate its clinical significance in CRC.

GRPs

Glucose-regulated proteins (GRP78, GRP94, GRP170, GRP75) are stress-inducible molecular chaperones located in the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria, classified within the HSPs family. They play a crucial role in tumorigenesis, progression, metastasis, and treatment resistance [113]. For instance, GRP78 regulates autophagy and cell survival through its interaction with the Protein kinase B (Akt/PKB/Rac) signaling pathway, thereby enhancing the anti-apoptotic capabilities of tumor cells [114]. Additionally, it promotes tumor cell invasion and migration by modulating the expression of matrix metalloproteinases MMP2 and MMP9 [115]. Furthermore, cell surface GRP78 can shield tumor cells from attacks by CTLs [116]. Conversely, GRP94 serves as an essential chaperone for toll-like receptors (TLRs) and major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) molecules, affecting both innate and adaptive immunity. When utilized as a tumor vaccine carrier, GRP94 can activate T-cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity [113, 117]. Both GRP78 and GRP94 function as endoplasmic reticulum (ER) chaperone proteins with dual roles in tumors: they can act as immune activation signals or as tumor-promoting factors [118]. These complex and sometimes contradictory roles may obscure their prognostic significance across various cancers. For instance, although GRP78 is highly homologous to the HSP70 family and gradually upregulated in expression during CRC progression, supporting its role in tumorigenesis, it is negatively correlated with lymphatic invasion. Patients with high expression levels of GRP78 have a slightly higher survival rate, although the difference was not statistically significant(HR = 0.733, 95% CI [0.466–1.154], p = 0.180)34. On the other hand, while high GRP94 expression is associated with larger tumor size and deeper invasion, patients with elevated GRP94 levels demonstrated a slightly higher survival rate (HR = 0.832, 95% CI [0.496–1.395], p = 0.485) [34], and it indicated a better prognosis in CRC patients younger than 65 years (p = 0.001) [38].

This ambiguity in prognostic significance reflects the complex and multidimensional regulatory networks of the glucose-regulated protein family in tumor biology. Unlike traditional HSPs that primarily function as “molecular chaperones” assisting in protein folding, molecules such as GRP78 and GRP94 assume more prominent roles as cellular stress sensors. Their functional outputs are highly dependent on subcellular localization, binding partners, and the specific context of the tumor microenvironment. Consequently, the differential distribution of these molecules across various subcellular compartments (e.g., the endoplasmic reticulum lumen, cell membrane surface, and extracellular secretion) and their “cross-boundary” functions between tumor cells and immune cells may elucidate the divergent clinical prognostic values observed in different studies. Future investigations into the functions of the GRP family should concentrate more precisely on their spatial distribution and interaction networks, rather than merely assessing their overall expression levels.

Heterogeneity sources and clinical significance

HSPs subtype differences

The HSPs family encompasses multiple subtypes, including HSP27, HSP47, HSP70, and HSP90, which are associated with poor prognosis in CRC. In contrast, HSP60, HSP110, and GRPs do not exhibit such associations; the DHAJ protein family may serve as potential biomarkers, but more research evidence is needed for validation. Notably, HSP47 and HSP70 serves as an independent poor prognostic factor for CRC. To enhance the accuracy of prognosis assessment, HSP27 and HSP70 can be combined with other indicators, while DNAJ protein, HSP90 and GRPs require further subtype differentiation. This underscores the necessity of clearly distinguishing between HSPs subtypes in clinical applications to avoid confusion, and when appropriate, to evaluate them in conjunction with other indicators. Furthermore, the limited research on HSP60 highlights the need for further investigation into its prognostic significance in CRC.

Differences in testing methods and samples

Subgroup analysis indicated that regardless of the detection method used, high HSP expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis. The strongest association was observed with PCR method (HR = 2.63). No significant differences were found among subgroups based on sample types. However, it should be noted that in studies of HSP60 and CRC prognosis, HSP60 expression levels in serum versus tissue showed completely opposite prognostic implications. Additionally, unlike serum HSP70 studies, the prognostic value of tissue HSP70 disappeared after stage adjustment [24], suggesting serum detection may possess greater independence. Therefore, in clinical practice, it is still necessary to differentiate the correlation between different specimens and detection methods with prognosis, and continuously improve the detection methods. For example, using the Dominant intensity score to evaluate HSP110wt IHC.

Sample size

Subgroup analysis suggested that the effect size in small-sample studies (≤ 200) was significantly larger than in large-sample studies (> 200), indicating potential publication bias. We conducted Begg’s and Egger’s tests for further verification. Visual inspection of Begg’s funnel plot revealed some asymmetry, and Begg’s test (p = 0.0457) suggested possible publication bias. However, Egger’s linear regression test (p = 0.074) did not show significant small-study effects. Meanwhile, sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the pooled OS estimate was robust. Taken together, this study may have mild publication bias, but its impact on the overall pooled effect size is likely limited.

Follow-up starting time

The subgroup analysis results suggest that when starting the calculation from the time point of “initial discharge,” HSP high expression has the most significant negative impact on survival (with a risk increase of more than sixfold). However, only one study used this time point as the starting follow-up, so the effect size may be overestimated, and further research is needed for verification.

Tumor location differences

The prognosis of CRC patients is closely linked to the location of the primary tumor. Studies indicate significant differences in clinical features, molecular biological characteristics, and treatment efficacy among right-sided colon cancer (RCC), left-sided colon cancer (LCC), and rectal cancer (RC) patients. These differences have a direct impact on patient survival rates and treatment responses. RCC generally presents a poorer prognosis, while LCC tends to have a relatively better prognosis, and the prognosis of RC falls between that of RCC and LCC [119–121]. Consequently, the site of the primary tumor should be considered in the clinical application of HSPs for CRC prognosis. However, few studies included in this meta-analysis had cohorts that assessed prognostic risk based on tumor location, thus precluding further subgroup analysis.

Gender differences

There is a significant gender disparity in the incidence of CRC, a phenomenon widely observed globally. Overall, men have a notably higher risk of developing CRC compared to women, with the male-to-female incidence ratio in the United States ranging between 1.07 and 1.62 [122]. Although the role of HSPs in tumor prognosis has been extensively studied, research on their gender differences remains relatively limited. Existing studies suggest that due to differences in hormone levels, immune system functions, and gene expression between males and females, there is a potential link between HSPs and gender-specific tumor biology [123, 124]. In CRC, the expression or genetic variations of certain HSPs, such as HSP27 and HSP70, may have differing prognostic implications across genders, thereby influencing tumor progression and patient outcomes. We used a male-to-female ratio of 1.5 as the cutoff value for subgroup analysis. However, our subgroup analysis results did not reveal a significant correlation between sex ratio and OS in CRC patients, suggesting that the gender disparity in incidence rates may not be directly driven by differential expression of HSPs. The underlying reason may be that tumor prognosis is influenced by multiple factors, including tumor stage, treatment response, lifestyle, and genetic background, which could potentially mask the sex-specific effects of HSPs. Furthermore, existing studies predominantly focus on individual HSP subtypes, whereas the HSP family members form a complex interaction network. The synergistic or antagonistic effects between different subtypes (e.g., HSP27 and HSP70) in male and female patients may not yet be fully elucidated. Future research should systematically elucidate the gender differences in HSPs expression profiles and their interactions with the tumor microenvironment and immune infiltration through large-scale prospective cohorts combined with multi-omics technologies, aiming to clarify the precise role of HSPs in gender-specific prognosis of CRC. Meanwhile, given the potential association between hormone levels and HSPs expression, exploring the intersection of sex hormone receptor signaling pathways with HSPs regulatory mechanisms may provide novel biological targets for developing gender-precision intervention strategies for CRC.

Tumor staging

Patient prognosis is highly dependent on tumor stage, with early diagnosis significantly improving survival rates, while advanced CRC has a poorer prognosis. Our subgroup analysis revealed no significant differences among different tumor stage groups, suggesting that the prognostic value of HSP is relatively consistent across patients with varying stages. Although no significant differences were observed among different tumor stage groups, this does not imply that HSP expression is unrelated to tumor progression, but rather more likely reflects its fundamental role in the initiation and development of CRC. As a molecular chaperone, HSP plays a central function in maintaining intracellular protein homeostasis, preventing abnormal protein aggregation, and assisting in the repair of damaged proteins. In the tumor microenvironment, cancer cells face various stress conditions such as hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and acidosis at both early and advanced stages, which may lead to sustained high expression of HSP to maintain their survival. Therefore, the prognostic value of HSP may reflect the overall stress state and adaptive capacity of tumor cells rather than merely tumor burden or invasion depth. Moreover, this relatively consistent prognostic value across different stages suggests that we should pay more attention to the biological behavior of tumors rather than relying solely on anatomical staging. Future research could further integrate HSP expression levels with other molecular markers (e.g., specific gene mutations) to construct more comprehensive prognostic prediction models. This will help identify the subgroup of patients who truly have a high risk of recurrence, regardless of their tumor stage, thereby enabling more precise individualized treatment.

Limitations

Our systematic review and meta-analysis provided valuable insights into the relationship between HSPs and CRC survival outcomes; however, several limitations warrant consideration.

Firstly, the included studies exhibited significant variations in experimental design, sample size, and detection methods, resulting in high heterogeneity and potential bias in our findings. Notably, the HSP110 subgroup demonstrated particularly pronounced heterogeneity. The studies in this subgroup were limited in number and reported diverse prognostic outcome measures: some focused on overall survival, others on disease-free survival, while others provided only cancer-specific survival or recurrence time. The follow-up duration varied substantially, ranging from 2 to 10 years. Additionally, some studies failed to provide directly extractable hazard ratios with corresponding confidence intervals, necessitating estimation from survival curves, which inevitably introduced additional measurement errors. Collectively, these factors contribute to significant methodological and clinical heterogeneity in this subgroup analysis. Forcing data pooling may yield biased summary results or misleading conclusions. Therefore, in accordance with the recommendations of the Cochrane Collaboration and PRISMA guidelines, we opted to conduct a descriptive systematic review rather than statistical pooling for the HSP110 subgroup in this meta-analysis, ensuring the scientific rigor of our research conclusions.

Secondly, the scope of some subgroup studies is overly limited. For instance, research on HSP110 has predominantly focused on MSI-type CRC, with study institutions primarily located in Northern Hemisphere countries and regions. This selection bias not only restricts the extrapolation of research conclusions but also hinders a comprehensive understanding of HSP as a prognostic marker for the general population. Specifically, MSI-type CRC exhibits significant differences from microsatellite stable (MSS)-type in terms of biological behavior, clinicopathological characteristics, and immune microenvironment. MSI-type patients generally have a better prognosis, respond more favorably to immunotherapy, and demonstrate a higher tumor mutation burden. Currently, there is insufficient evidence regarding whether the high expression of HSP110 in MSI-type patients has prognostic significance and whether this conclusion can be generalized to MSS-type patients, who constitute a larger proportion of CRC cases. Directly extrapolating research findings from MSI-type to the overall population may lead to an overestimation or underestimation of the actual prognostic value of HSP110. Meanwhile, research institutions are primarily concentrated in Northern Hemisphere countries and regions, such as Europe, North America, and parts of East Asia, while reports of related studies from the Southern Hemisphere, Africa, South America, and Southeast Asia are extremely rare. This geographical imbalance may introduce multidimensional confounding effects, including genetic backgrounds, environmental exposure factors, dietary habits, and healthcare resources. Genetic polymorphism variations among different ethnic groups, differences in molecular classification spectra of CRC, and heterogeneity in clinical diagnosis and treatment patterns across regions may all influence HSP expression levels and their association with prognosis. Current evidence primarily derives from Caucasian and East Asian populations, making it uncertain whether these conclusions are applicable to other ethnicities and regions. Moreover, the concentration of research institutions suggests potential publication bias—positive findings from developed countries in the Northern Hemisphere are more likely to be published, while negative or non-significant results from developing countries may be underreported due to journal preferences or language barriers. This “positive results bias” further exacerbates the limitations of the existing evidence system.

Third, further subclassification is necessary within different HSP families. For instance, the DNAJ protein family consists of numerous members that exhibit significant variations in domain composition, subcellular localization, tissue expression profiles, and biological functions. Simply aggregating members of the same family for analysis may obscure genuine biological associations and could lead to misleading conclusions with opposite directions. Similarly, although the HSP90 family has fewer members, there is functional divergence among its subtypes in tumors. In this meta-analysis, while the pooled results of HSP90 overall indicated that high expression was associated with poor prognosis, the included studies did not distinguish specific subtypes, making it impossible to determine which exact subtype drove this association. Furthermore, the interaction networks among different subfamilies remain unexplored.

Fourth, immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and CTLA-4 inhibitors, has revolutionized the treatment landscape for advanced CRC, especially in patients with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H). Under this new therapeutic paradigm, the prognostic significance of HSPs may undergo fundamental changes. Unfortunately, high-quality prognostic studies in the current era of immunotherapy remain relatively scarce. The data included in this meta-analysis were predominantly collected before 2015, when immunotherapy was not yet widely utilized in CRC, and patients primarily received treatments such as surgical resection and traditional chemoradiotherapy. In this treatment context, the high expression of HSPs, which reflects tumor aggressiveness, proliferative activity, and treatment resistance, was closely associated with poor prognosis. However, as immunotherapy gradually becomes the standard treatment for certain CRC patients, the clinical value of HSPs may dynamically evolve with the changing therapeutic landscape, making it challenging to directly extrapolate previous research conclusions to the era of immunotherapy. Future studies must urgently conduct stratified analyses based on different molecular subtypes to elucidate the specific roles of HSPs in various immunotherapy-sensitive subpopulations.

Finally, although the HSPs family demonstrates potential as biomarkers, challenges remain in clinical translation. For instance, as stress proteins, HSP levels may also elevate in benign inflammatory diseases (such as enteritis), which could compromise their specificity in early CRC screening. Additionally, the current lack of standardized HSP detection protocols (e.g., sample types, detection thresholds) may lead to heterogeneity across studies, while such heterogeneity further complicates statistical pooling. In this meta-analysis, some studies utilized tissue specimens for immunohistochemical staining, while others measured HSP levels in serum or plasma. These distinct sample types reflect fundamentally different biological implications—tissue expression indicates intrinsic characteristics of tumor cells, whereas circulating levels may be influenced by systemic inflammatory status, tumor burden, metabolism, and other factors. Secondly, regarding immunohistochemical interpretation criteria, significant variations existed in the positive thresholds adopted across studies. Some defined high expression by combining staining intensity scores (0–3) with positive cell percentages, others employed H-score semi-quantitative integration, and some simply dichotomized using median or mean values. This inconsistency in evaluation criteria directly compromised the comparability of “high expression” versus “low expression” population classifications. Furthermore, differences in antibody sources and clone numbers might lead to variations in staining sensitivity and specificity, further exacerbating inter-study heterogeneity.

Despite these limitations, all the studies included in our analysis had NOS scores greater than seven, indicating high-quality research. Furthermore, the sensitivity analysis of our findings suggests robustness, thereby reinforcing the reliability of our research conclusions.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis demonstrated that HSPs hold significant potential in the prognostic evaluation of CRC patients. Elevated levels of HSP27, HSP47, HSP70, and HSP90 serve as important biomarkers for poor prognosis in CRC, particularly where high expression of HSP47 and HSP70 are correlated with adverse outcomes. However, the clinical application of these biomarkers necessitates careful consideration of variations in HSP subtypes, tumor locations, detection methods. Meanwhile, given the limitations of using a single indicator to assess prognosis, future efforts should focus on constructing multidimensional prediction models. Integrating multi-omics data through algorithms such as machine learning may help improve the accuracy of prognostic evaluation. For instance, a research team has developed a CRC diagnostic model based on serum metabolomics and proteomics using machine learning, providing a new approach for non-invasive blood screening [125].

Future research should focus on the following aspects: (1) Advancing the standardization and normalization of HSPs detection by comparing the clinical utility of different detection methods. This includes specifying recommended antibody clone numbers, staining procedures, positive control settings, and interpretation systems (e.g., uniformly adopting H-score or Immunoreactive Score (IRS) scoring). Additionally, future studies should prioritize the collection of raw data to determine the optimal prognostic cut-off values for HSP expression levels through dose-response meta-analysis. This approach aims to address the current heterogeneity issues caused by inconsistent scoring standards (e.g., H-score, Allred) and detection platforms (IHC, qPCR), thereby promoting the standardization of clinical testing. (2) Large-sample, multicenter prospective studies should be conducted to address the limitations of current evidence. Given the insufficient sample sizes and homogeneous populations (e.g., HSP110 studies being primarily limited to MSI CRC patients) in current research on subtypes such as HSP60 and DNAJ proteins, future efforts should expand search scopes and incorporate more diverse population data to clarify the prognostic value of these subtypes across different CRC molecular subtypes (e.g., microsatellite stable cases). (3) Further investigation should be conducted to elucidate the prognostic value of HSPs in the context of immunotherapy. With the increasing application of immunotherapy in CRC, there is an urgent need to conduct prospective cohort studies involving advanced CRC patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors. These studies should systematically evaluate the association between HSP expression and objective response rate, progression-free survival, overall survival, and immune-related adverse reactions, exploring their potential as predictive biomarkers for immunotherapy efficacy.(4) Further investigation into the interactions between HSPs and other molecular characteristics is essential, along with mechanistic studies on HSP subtype specificity and the synergistic effects among family members. Given the numerous members and diverse functions of the HSP family, future research should concentrate on detailed examinations of specific HSP subtypes in CRC. Additionally, it is crucial to explore the synergistic or antagonistic effects among HSP family members, such as the interaction between HSP70 and HSP40, which influences the balance of protein folding and degradation, as well as their combined role in tumor cell stress adaptation and chemotherapy resistance. (5) Developing integrated predictive models based on multi-omics data is imperative. Single-level analyses are inadequate to fully elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of HSPs in CRC progression. Future research should systematically integrate HSPs with other clinicopathological features (such as CEA, carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA19-9), and TNM staging) alongside multi-omics data (including genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and epigenomic dimensions). By employing network analysis and systems biology approaches, researchers can construct HSP-related regulatory networks and signaling pathway maps. Utilizing large-scale sample data from public databases such as The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), combined with machine learning algorithms, will enable the identification of molecular subtypes associated with HSP expression. Furthermore, this approach will facilitate the exploration of sensitivity differences among various subtypes to targeted therapies, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, ultimately providing more precise individualized risk stratification and treatment guidance for CRC patients.

In summary, significant progress has been made in the research of HSPs as prognostic markers for CRC, but there remains a gap before clinical translation can be achieved. Only through multicenter collaboration, standardization efforts, multi-omics integration, and in-depth mechanistic studies can we truly unveil the actual value of HSPs in precision diagnosis and treatment of CRC, ultimately bringing tangible clinical benefits to patients.
