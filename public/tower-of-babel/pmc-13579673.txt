Mass casualty training for prehospital and emergency providers in Kigali, Rwanda: a quasi-experimental pre–post educational study
Authors: Oriane Longerstaey, Rafael Davis, William Mullen, Pascal Mugemangango, Kyle Denison Martin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA mass casualty incident (MCI) occurs when the number of patients exceeds available healthcare resources, placing significant strain on prehospital and facility-based emergency care systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). An effective response to MCIs requires coordinated systems, targeted training, and clear communication across all levels of care. Although Rwanda experiences both natural and man-made disasters, formal training in mass casualty management for healthcare workers remains limited.MethodsA two-part mass casualty training curriculum was developed for emergency physicians, nurses, paramedics, and community first responders in Kigali, Rwanda. The program combined asynchronous video-based modules with a half-day, simulation-based skills training. Participants completed a knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) assessment prior to training, immediately after the simulation, and at 3- and 6-month follow-up intervals. Pre- and post-training scores were analyzed to assess changes in knowledge, confidence, and practices using tests of statistical significance.ResultsA total of 41 participants representing multiple levels of training completed the program during 2022. Most participants were male and aged 25–44 years, with limited prior experience in mass casualty management. Mean test scores increased significantly following the simulation and remained higher than baseline at 3- and 6-month follow-up. Participants also demonstrated significant improvements in confidence across key MCI-related skills.ConclusionThis pilot study demonstrated that a multimodal mass casualty training curriculum was associated with improvements in knowledge and confidence among healthcare workers and community first responders in Kigali, Rwanda. These findings suggest the curriculum may be a promising approach to strengthening MCI preparedness in similar settings. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate its effectiveness and assess generalizability.

Introduction

A mass casualty incident (MCI) occurs when the number of patients exceeds available healthcare resources, though the threshold for what constitutes an MCI varies by setting. These can stem from accidents like motor vehicle collisions or disasters, both natural or man-made. When they occur, MCIs place significant strain on prehospital and facility-based emergency care systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs; 1). LMICs are disproportionately affected by disasters, yet much of the existing literature on MCI management originates from high-income settings, highlighting a gap in context-specific preparedness strategies [1]. An effective MCI response requires rapid adaptation to increased patient volume, higher acuity, and, in some cases, exposure to hazardous materials requiring decontamination. These complex demands necessitate coordinated systems, targeted training, and clear communication. Inadequate preparedness or breakdowns in response can lead to worsened patient outcomes and disruption of healthcare systems [2].

Prior studies demonstrate that MCI preparedness among hospitals globally remains suboptimal, with limited formal training among healthcare personnel [3]. For example, Corrigan et al. reported that only 59.3% of hospital staff in Australia had received prior education, and an even smaller proportion had participated in simulation-based training [4]. Similarly, Goniewicz et al. identified significant variability in MCI preparedness knowledge and skills among hospital staff in Poland [5]. In Yemen, Naser et al. found an absence of formal training programs, resulting in substantial gaps in preparedness [6]. In sub-Saharan Africa, emergency preparedness continues to evolve; Koka et al. noted ongoing opportunities to strengthen disaster response capacity in Tanzania [2]. Recognizing these gaps, the World Health Organization (WHO) initiated its first Mass Casualty Management Course in Ethiopia in 2021, bringing together participants from government, healthcare, and private sectors and demonstrating the perceived value of structured MCI training [7]. Similar efforts have been implemented elsewhere on the continent, including targeted prehospital training programs in Sierra Leone and trauma and MCI response training in Togo, which have contributed to broader capacity-building initiatives [8,9].

Rwanda faces a range of natural and man-made disasters, and the government has recognized the need to strengthen national disaster management capacity [10]. Recent regional and national events underscore this vulnerability. In 2022, a volcanic eruption in the neighboring eastern Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) led to mass population displacement affecting Rwanda, while a 6.2 magnitude earthquake in western Rwanda in 2008 resulted in 39 deaths and >600 injuries. Additionally, climate-related events such as flooding, droughts, and landslides are increasing in frequency and severity [11]. Despite these risks, there is no centralized trauma registry to quantify the burden of MCI, although Velin et al. conducted a media-based review to describe their epidemiology in Rwanda [12].

The Rwandan government has established a national disaster management framework but continues to prioritize strengthening capacity at the local level. Formal training in mass casualty management for healthcare providers remains limited, particularly across the continuum of care [8]. Kigali is served by multiple hospitals and a prehospital emergency system, the Service d’Aide Médicale d’Urgence (SAMU), established in 2007; however, providers within these systems have not received structured training specific to MCI response. Addressing this gap requires an interdisciplinary approach that includes first responders, prehospital personnel, and hospital-based providers, as demonstrated in training initiatives in Sierra Leone and Togo [8,9].

There have been many different educational initiatives for preparing healthcare workers to respond to disasters or MCIs, and existing programs vary widely in structure and content. Many combine didactic instruction with hands-on training. For example, Alim et al. implemented a program in Indonesia that integrated classroom-based teaching with disaster drills and demonstrated improvements through pre- and post-training evaluations [13]. Simulation-based training has also been increasingly supported as an effective approach to MCI education. Bentley et al. described a team-based simulated triage exercise that actively engaged learners and improved skill development across provider groups [14]. However, Much of the existing literature has focused on single cadres, particularly nursing staff. Noh et al. developed and evaluated a multimodal disaster simulation program for hospital nurses, emphasizing triage, incident command, and procedural skills [15]. Similarly, Mirzaei et al. examined disaster preparedness among nurses and identified improvements following targeted training interventions [16].

Overall, disaster preparedness initiatives are expanding globally, yet existing curricula remain heterogeneous and often tailored to specific contexts rather than standardized across settings. Despite demonstrated benefits of training interventions, most programs have focused on single cadres, with limited emphasis on interdisciplinary approaches that reflect the full continuum of emergency care. In Rwanda, formalized training in mass casualty management remains limited despite national efforts to strengthen disaster preparedness. There is a clear need for scalable, contextually relevant programs that engage providers across all levels of care, including community responders, prehospital personnel, and hospital-based clinicians.

This study was designed to address this gap by developing and implementing a low-cost, simulation-supported MCI training program tailored to the Rwandan context. The objective was to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of this interdisciplinary curriculum in improving knowledge, confidence, and preparedness among emergency care providers in a resource-limited setting.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was conducted as a collaborative effort between researchers from Brown University and healthcare practitioners at the University Teaching Hospital in Kigali (UTH-K). Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the University of Rwanda (No. 105/CMHS IRB/2023). Funding for the study was provided by the Brown Global Health Initiative Framework Grant and the Brown Center for Human Rights and Humanitarian Studies.

This was a single-center, quasi-experimental study conducted in Kigali, Rwanda, involving emergency care personnel across the full continuum of response. Participants were recruited from multiple organizations, including Healthy People Rwanda, the Rwandan Emergency Care Association (RECA), the SAMU, and emergency physicians and nurses from the UTH-K. Healthy People Rwanda is a youth-driven community organization focused on health promotion, including road safety and first-aid initiatives such as the First Aid Initiative Rwanda (FAIR) project, which provides first aid training to community members and educators [17]. RECA represents emergency physicians, residents, nurses, and prehospital providers and supports the development of emergency care systems in Rwanda [18]. SAMU serves as Kigali's prehospital emergency medical system with over 70 providers and eight ambulances [19]. Participants were recruited through these organizations on a voluntary basis.

The inclusion of participants ranging from community first responders to hospital-based clinicians was intended to reflect the interdisciplinary nature of mass casualty response, which requires coordination across all levels of care. Initial lifesaving interventions during MCIs often focus on trauma management and can be performed by individuals without formal medical training, while hospital-based care must adapt to resource constraints and altered triage priorities. This integrated approach was designed to strengthen system-level preparedness and coordination during MCI

Inclusion criteria included individuals aged 18 years or older who were serving as community first responders, paramedics, emergency physicians, or nurses. A total of 41 participants were recruited from the participating organizations and received compensation for their time. The sample size was determined based on feasibility, as this study was designed as a pilot evaluation.

Participants completed a pre-training assessment to establish baseline knowledge of mass casualty management. They then engaged in a series of four online modules over one month, covering core topics including an introduction to MCIs, incident command systems, the M-START triage algorithm, and basic hemorrhage control. These topics were selected based on a review of similar training initiatives [8,9,14,15]. The curriculum focused primarily on trauma-related MCIs, reflecting the local epidemiology where road traffic accidents and injury-related events are common drivers of mass casualty scenarios. Educational content was adapted from multiple sources, including Free Open Access Meducation (FOAMed) and other publicly available resources. Existing MCI command frameworks were reviewed and modified to align with local context and resource availability [19,20], while hemorrhage control instruction was based on Stop the Bleed principles [21]. Each module was followed by a brief knowledge assessment.

At the conclusion of the didactic component, participants attended a simulation-based training session designed to reinforce practical skills. Simulation scenarios were adapted from cases developed by Brown Emergency Medicine faculty and prior training models such as SIMWars [14]. The simulation was conducted over two half-day sessions, each involving of 20 participants. Participants practiced hemorrhage control skills such as applying pressure, packing wounds and placing tourniquets on foam rollers. In small groups, they applied triage rules to paper based scenarios. Finally, the group was split into two and participated in scenario based exercises using paper cutouts to practice resource placement in a physical space. One-half of the group performed the exercise and the other half observed and provided feedback. Scenarios included predefined critical actions to guide evaluation and discussion.

Participants completed the same assessment used at baseline immediately following the simulation session and again at 3- and 6-month follow-up intervals to evaluate changes in knowledge, attitudes, and practices.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected using Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices (KAP) surveys, a commonly used tool in public health to evaluate the impact of interventions [22]. The surveys assessed knowledge of key components of mass casualty management, including incident command, triage, and basic hemorrhage control. As prior studies using similar pre- and post-test designs have not published standardized instruments, the survey tool was developed by the study team based on the educational content of the training modules.

Participants completed the same survey at four time points: prior to training (baseline), immediately following the simulation session, and at 3- and 6-month follow-up intervals. Knowledge outcomes were measured using multiple-choice questions, while attitudes and practices were assessed using Likert-scale items evaluating participant confidence in key skills.

Data was collected electronically using Google Forms. Responses were de-identified and linked using unique participant codes. Limited demographic information, including age, sex, and level of training, was collected. All data were stored on password-protected devices with access restricted to study personnel. Data collection occurred between January 2022 and January 2023.

Data analysis

Analysis was performed using STATA statistical software (College Station, USA). Test scores obtained on the pre- and post-simulation studies were obtained by 2-sample t-test. Medians of attitude and practice scores were compared with the Mann Whitney U-Test.

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA software (College Station, USA). Differences in mean knowledge scores between pre- and post-training assessments were evaluated using two-sample t-tests. Changes in attitudes and practices, measured as median Likert-scale responses, were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Of the initial 41 participants, 29 completed the immediate post-training survey, 20 completed the 3-month follow-up, and 12 completed the 6-month evaluation. Participant characteristics remained similar across follow-up time points, with no statistically significant differences observed, except for a higher proportion of individuals with prior Stop the Bleed training among those who completed post-training assessments (Table 1). There was no missing data among completed surveys.

The mean knowledge score on the KAP assessment prior to training was 60.6% (range 30%–85%; Fig. 1). Immediately after the simulation, the mean score increased to 75.5% (range 50%–100%; p < 0.001). Scores declined slightly at follow-up, with a mean of 72.5% (range 50%–95%) at 3 months and 71.7% (range 45%–90%) at 6 months. This pattern was consistent across all participant subgroups. Perceived importance of MCI training remained high throughout the study period (82.9% at baseline and 91.7% at 6 months) and did not change significantly over time. In contrast, the proportion of participants who reported feeling prepared to control bleeding in an MCI increased significantly from 39% before training to 91.7% at 6 months (p < 0.05). There was no significant change in the proportion of participants who reported using these skills before and after training.

Discussion

In this study, 41 participants representing multiple levels of emergency care – including civilians, paramedics, nurses, and physicians – completed a multimodal training program for MCI response in Kigali, Rwanda. The curriculum combined asynchronous online modules with simulation-based training and was associated with significant improvements in both knowledge and confidence across key domains of MCI management. Knowledge scores increased substantially immediately after training and, although there was a modest decline over time, remained significantly higher than baseline at both 3- and 6-month follow-up. This pattern suggests that the intervention achieved durable learning gains, with partial knowledge decay that is consistent with prior findings in simulation-based education [24].

Participants also demonstrated marked improvements in confidence across multiple domains, including triage, leadership roles, and hemorrhage control skills. Notably, confidence in practical skills such as tourniquet application, direct pressure, and wound packing nearly doubled over the study period. These findings suggest that the inclusion of hands-on simulation was effective in reinforcing procedural skills and building confidence in high-acuity scenarios. While the perceived importance of MCI training remained high throughout the study period, the significant increase in participants’ reported preparedness to control bleeding highlights a meaningful shift from general awareness to actionable readiness. However, there was no change in reported use of these skills, which is likely reflective of the infrequent occurrence of MCIs rather than a lack of skill acquisition. In such contexts, simulation may serve as a critical surrogate for experience, allowing providers to maintain readiness for high-acuity, low-frequency events.

The sustained improvement in knowledge and confidence may be partially explained by the structure of the curriculum, which combined self-directed learning with experiential simulation. This approach aligns with educational frameworks that emphasize active learning and repetition as key drivers of retention [14]. Additionally, the interdisciplinary nature of the training – bringing together providers from the community, prehospital system, and hospital setting – may have enhanced system-level understanding and role clarity during MCIs. This is particularly relevant in low-resource settings, where coordinated responses across all levels of care are critical to effective MCI management.

These findings are consistent with prior studies demonstrating the benefits of MCI training in improving preparedness among healthcare providers [13]. However, most existing programs have focused on single cadres or primarily didactic instruction [8,9]. Studies from high-income countries, such as Australia and Poland, demonstrate the positive impact of formal disaster education, yet LMICs often lack such structured training opportunities [4,5]. In contrast, this study incorporated a simulation-based, interdisciplinary approach, which may better reflect real-world MCI response. In Rwanda, limited prior work has evaluated MCI training, with earlier efforts focusing primarily on triage exercises [23]. More recently, the World Health Organization has introduced standardized MCI training programs, though these often lack simulation components. The integration of simulation in this study may therefore represent an important advancement in training design, particularly for skill-based competencies.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was small, as this was designed as a pilot study to assess feasibility and preliminary effectiveness. Due to the limited sample size, subgroup analyses comparing outcomes across different provider cadres were not statistically powered and were not performed. Additional analyses comparing performance across provider groups and training components would provide further insight and should be explored in studies with larger sample sizes. Second, there was significant attrition over time, with only 12 participants completing the 6-month follow-up. This may be related to the financial incentive structure of the study, where participants were compensated for their time spent during the in-person training but not for completion of follow-up surveys. Although participant characteristics remained similar across time points, this loss to follow-up limits interpretation of long-term retention outcomes and may introduce response bias, as individuals who completed later surveys may have been more engaged or motivated. The higher proportion of participants with prior Stop the Bleed training among follow-up respondents may have also contributed to sustained performance. Additionally, outcomes related to confidence were self-reported and may be influenced by social desirability bias. Finally, the study was conducted in a single urban setting, which may limit generalizability to other regions.

Future studies should expand on these findings by including larger and more diverse participant populations, as well as multiple geographic regions within Rwanda and other low- and middle-income countries. In particular, larger studies would enable subgroup analyses comparing outcomes across different provider cadres, which may provide important insight into how training impacts learners with varying levels of clinical experience. Further work is also needed to evaluate objective measures of skill performance and to assess the impact of training on real-world clinical outcomes during mass casualty events. Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates that a low-cost, simulation-supported training program is both feasible and potentially effective in improving MCI preparedness. The use of accessible materials, such as printed resources and basic simulation equipment, supports the scalability of this approach in resource-limited settings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of implementing a multimodal MCI training curriculum in Rwanda. The program was associated with improvements in knowledge and confidence across key domains of MCI management, with evidence of sustained gains over time. By integrating online modules with simulation-based training, this approach offers a practical and adaptable model for strengthening disaster preparedness in resource-limited settings. Further studies are needed to validate these findings and assess the scalability and long-term impact of this curriculum in other contexts.
