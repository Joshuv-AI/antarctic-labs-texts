Coalgebras, bialgebras and Rota-Baxter algebras from shuffles of rooted forests
Pierre J. Clavier, Douglas Modesto

Abstract
We construct and study new generalisations to rooted trees and forests of some properties of shuffles of words. First, we build a coproduct on rooted trees which, together with their shuffle, endow them with bialgebra structure. We then caracterize the coproduct dual to the shuffle product of rooted forests and build a product on rooted trees to obtain the bialgebra dual to the shuffle bialgebra. We then characterize and enumerate primitive trees for the dual coproduct. Finally, using modified shuffles of rooted forests, we prove a property in the category of Rota-Baxter algebras.



Introduction

Forests, trees and words

Rooted trees and forests are a stepstone of combinatorics, and their various properties have been studied by generations of mathematicians. Not only are they essential in pure combinatorics (see for example
[
1
]
), but they are also appear in other domain of mathematics, and in particular number theory (see
[
2
,
3
]
) which is the motivation for this work. Let us also mention in passing that they are also crucial in Physics. They turned out for example to be at the heart of renormalisation theory (
[
4
,
5
]
), to play an essential role in the study of Schwinger-Dyson equations (
[
6
]
and also
[
7
]
for recent advances in this domain).

What will interest us here is that rooted trees and forests are a natural generalisation of words. As it turns out, words as well have many important properties that play important roles in combinatorics and number theory. For a set
\Omega
, we write
{\mathcal{W}}_{\Omega}
the vector space freely generated by words written in the alphabet
\Omega
. The starting point of our analysis is the shuffle of words, and its contracting counterparts, which are sometimes called quasi-shuffles, contracting shuffles or stuffles. The following definition is taken from
[
8
]
but is equivalent to other definition of shuffles, for example in
[
9
]
.

Definition 0.1
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field,
A
an algebra over
\mathbb{K}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
. The
\lambda
-shuffle product
on
{\mathcal{W}}_{A}
is defined recursively as follows:

1.

\emptyset\shuffle_{\lambda}\omega=\omega\shuffle_{\lambda}\emptyset=\omega
for any word
w\in{\mathcal{W}}_{A}
;

2.

for two non-empty words
\omega=w_{0}\sqcup\tilde{\omega}
and
\omega^{\prime}=w^{\prime}_{0}\sqcup\tilde{\omega^{\prime}}
in
{\mathcal{W}}_{A}
we set

\omega\shuffle_{\lambda}\omega^{\prime}=w_{0}\sqcup(\tilde{\omega}\shuffle_{\lambda}\omega^{\prime})+w_{0}^{\prime}\sqcup(\omega\shuffle_{\lambda}\tilde{\omega^{\prime}})+\lambda(w_{0}w^{\prime}_{0})\sqcup(\tilde{\omega}\shuffle_{\lambda}\tilde{\omega^{\prime}})

where
w_{0}w^{\prime}_{0}\in A
stands for the multiplication of
w_{0}
and
w_{0}^{\prime}
in
A
and
\sqcup
for the concatenation of words in
{\mathcal{W}}_{A}
.

It is a standard exercise to show that the
\shuffle_{\lambda}
are associative products for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
. If
A
is commutative, then the
\lambda
-shuffle are also commutative. Definition
0.1
still makes sense if
A
is only a semigroup. For
A
a set, we need to take
\lambda=0
. In the following, we will not differentiate these cases.

These products have many important properties which we now list. The first one is that one can actually build a coproduct which endows
{\mathcal{W}}_{A}
with a bialgebra structure. We refer the reader to one of the many excellent introductions to the theory of bialgebras and Hopf algebras (for example
[
10
]
) for full definition. For our purpose, let us simply say if
(B,m)
is a
\mathbb{K}-
algebra with product
m:B\otimes B\longrightarrow B
associative, we are looking for a
coproduct
map
\Delta:B\longrightarrow B\otimes B
which should be coassociative, and a
counit
map
\varepsilon:B\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
. These maps should have the following properties:

Content and main results

The
\lambda
-shuffle products of Definition
0.1
not only endow words with rich algebraic structures but also play important roles in the theory of multi zeta values (MZVs). These objects were generalised in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
to rooted trees and forests. In
[
3
]
, generalisations of
\lambda
-shuffles to rooted forests were built that play the same roles to generalised MZVs as usual
\lambda
-shuffles to MZVs. Since rooted trees and forests generalise words, our goal in this paper is to generalise the aformentioned properties of
\lambda
-shuffles of words to the shuffles products of rooted trees and forests.

We start Section
1
by introducing necessary combinatorial definitions and in particular the definition of
\lambda
-shuffles of rooted forests (Definition
1.2
). The associated coproduct is defined in Definition
1.6
. It is counital and coassociative (Propositin
1.7
). Our main result of this first section is Theorem
1.8
which states that the shuffles of rooted trees together with this coproduct endow the space of rooted trees with a bialgebra structure.

Section
2
is devoted to the study of structures dual to the shuffle of rooted forests. We give an inductive caracterisation of the coproduct dual to the shuffle of rooted forests which is stated in Theorem
2.1
, our first main result of this second section. We then introduce some further combinatorial constructions on rooted trees in Definition
2.3
, and in particular admissible families of vertices. The second main result of this second section is then Theorem
2.7
which provide a purely combinatorial description of the dual coproduct in terms of admissible families of vertices.

Moving on, we introduce two grafting products (Definition
2.8
) which are pre-Lie (Proposition
2.9
). The third important result of this section is that one of this pre-Lie product, together with the dual coproduct, endows the dual space of rooted trees with a bialgebra structure (Theorem
2.10
), which is the dual to the bialgebra structure of the aforementioned Theorem
1.8
. We finish this second section with a description of trees that are primitive for the dual coproduct (Theorem
2.14
). This description implies an inductive formula for the number of these primitive trees (Proposition
2.15
). This formula allows to easily compute the number of primitive trees with a low number of vertices: we give the values for trees up to 23 vertices, obtained with a standard laptop.

In section
3
, the final section of this paper, we look at the relationship between shuffles of trees and Rota-Baxter algebras. We first recall some notions of the theory of Rota-Baxter algebras, and a particular a universal property of the shuffle products of words. We introduce in Definition
3.3
new shuffles of rooted trees and forests. These are shown in our last important result, namely Theorem
3.6
, to have a property of universal type in the category of Rota-Baxter algebras.

1
Shuffle bialgebra

1.1
Shuffle of rooted forests

Let us start by recalling some classical definitions of (oriented) graphs theory. These definitions can be found in many introductions to the topic, for example
[
14
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

•

A
graph
is a pair of finite sets
G:=(V(G),E(G))
with
E(G)\subseteq V(G)\times V(G)
.
E(G)
is the set of edges of the graph and
V(G)
the set of vertices of the graph.

•

A
path
in a graph
G
is a finite sequence of elements of
V(G)
:
p=(v_{1},\cdots,v_{n})
such that for all
i\in[[1,n-1]]
,
(v_{i},v_{i+1})
is an edge of
G
.
By convention, there is always a path between a vertex and itself.

•

For a graph
G=(V(G);E(G))
, let
\preceq
be the binary relation on
V(G)
defined by:
v_{1}\preceq v_{2}
if, and only if, it exists a path from
v_{1}
to
v_{2}
. We also denote by
\succeq
the inverse relation. A
directed acyclic graph
(DAG for short) is a graph such that
(V(G),\preceq)
is a poset.

•

A
forest
is a DAG such that there is at most one path between two vertices. A
rooted forest
is a forest whose connected components each have a unique minimal element. These elements are called
roots
. A
rooted tree
is a connected rooted forest.

•

Let
\Omega
be a set. An
\Omega
-decorated rooted forest
is a rooted forest
F
together with a
decoration map

d:V(F)\mapsto\Omega
.

•

Two rooted forests
F
and
F^{\prime}
(resp. decorated rooted forests
(F,d_{F})
and
(F^{\prime},d_{F^{\prime}})
) are
isomorphic
if there exists a poset isomorphism
f_{V}:V(F)\longrightarrow V(F^{\prime})
(resp. and
d_{F}=d_{F^{\prime}}\circ f_{V}
).

As usual, we always consider isomorphism classes of rooted forests and therefore identify trees and forests with their classes and write
{\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}
the set of isomorphism classes of
\Omega
-decorated rooted forests and
{\mathcal{F}}
the set of isomorphism classes of undecorated rooted forests. Furthermore, when there is no need to specify the decoration map we simply write
F
for a decorated forest
(F,d)
. We also allow ourself to identify a vertex and its decoration in order to not have too heavy notations.

We write
\emptyset
the empty forest. Notice that it belongs to
{\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}
and set
{\mathcal{F}}^{+}_{\Omega}:={\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}\setminus\{\emptyset\}
. Finally, for a (decorated or not) rooted forest
F
, we write
|F|
the number of vertices of
F
(thus
|\emptyset|=0
). This degree induces a graduation of
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}
.

Our rooted forests are non-planar. This implies in particular that their concatenation is a commutative (and associative) product. We write
F_{1}\cdots F_{k}
the concatenation of the forests
F_{1},\cdots,F_{k}
. The empty forest is the unit of this product.

Further recall that for any
\omega\in\Omega
the
grafting operator

B_{+}^{\omega}:{\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}
is the linear operator that, to any rooted forest
F=T_{1}\cdots T_{k}
, associates the decorated tree obtained from
F
by adding a root decorated by
\omega
linked to each root of
T_{i}
for
i
going from
1
to
k
.

[

Definition 1.2
.

Let

\lambda

of two rooted forests

is defined recursively on

by

•

(and thus

), we set
\emptyset\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}\emptyset=\emptyset
.

•

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, assume the shuffle product of forests has been defined on every forests
f,f^{\prime}
such that
|f|+|f^{\prime}|\leq N
. Then for any two forests
F,F^{\prime}
such that
|F|+|F^{\prime}|=N+1
;

1.

If
F^{\prime}=\emptyset
(or
F=\emptyset
) set
F\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}\emptyset=\emptyset\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}F=F

2.

If
F

F^{\prime}
is not a tree, then we can write
F
and
f

(2)

.

3.

F=T=B_{+}^{a}(f)
and

are two nonempty trees, we set

T\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime}=B_{+}^{a}(f\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})+B_{+}^{a^{\prime}}(T\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}f^{\prime})+\lambda B_{+}^{a.a^{\prime}}(f\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}f^{\prime}).

(3)

This product is then extended by bilinearity to a product on
{\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}

1.2
Shuffle coproduct

Since the canonical injection
j_{\Omega}:{\mathcal{W}}_{\Omega}\hookrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}
send words to linear trees which are connected forests, we define a generalisation of the deconcatenation coproduct on rooted trees.

Definition 1.6
.

Define
\Delta:{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}
inductively on the number of vertices. First set
\Delta(\emptyset)=\emptyset\otimes\emptyset
. Assume that
\Delta
has been defined on all trees with
N
vertices and let
T
be a tree with
N+1
vertices. Then it exists
a\in\Omega
such that
T=B_{+}^{a}(F)
. We have two cases to consider:

•

If
F=t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}
set

\Delta(B_{+}^{a}(t))=(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)\Delta(t)+\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a}(t).

(4)

•

If
F=f\in\mathcal{F}_{\Omega}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
set

\Delta(B_{+}^{a}(f))=\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a}(f).

This map is then extended by linearity to a map
\Delta:{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}
.

This coproduct admits a simple graphical representation. Let the
trunk
of a non-empty rooted tree be the path from the root to the first vertex with at least two descendant. Then
\Delta(T)
is obtained by cutting the tree
T
below each vertex in the trunk. The lower parts are the first elements and the top parts are the second elements of
\Delta(T)=\sum_{(T)}T^{\prime}\otimes T^{\prime\prime}
, where we use Sweedler’s notation for the coproduct. In the case of a linear trees, the initial hypothesis
\Delta(\emptyset)=\emptyset\otimes\emptyset
brings one more cut above the unique leaf of the tree. In particular
\Delta
coincides with the deconcatenation coproduct on linear trees.

We first have that
\Delta
endow
{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}
with a coalgebra structure.

Proposition 1.7
.

(\mathcal{T}_{\Omega},\Delta)
is a noncocommutative, coassociative, counital coalgebra.

Proof.

The counity map
\varepsilon:{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}\longrightarrow\mathbb{K}
is given by the linear map which acts on rooted trees by
\varepsilon(\emptyset)=1
and
\varepsilon(T)=0
for any non-empty rooted tree
T
. This trivially fulfills the axioms of the counit (first diagramm of (
1
)).

It is obvious from the definition that if
T
is not empty and not a linear tree, then
\Delta(T)
is not symmetric, thus
\Delta
is non cocommutative. We are only left to prove that
\Delta
is coassociative.

We proceed by induction on
|T|
for
T\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
. If
|T|=0,
we have
T=\emptyset
and

(\Delta\otimes Id)\Delta(\emptyset)=\emptyset\otimes\emptyset\otimes\emptyset=(Id\otimes\Delta)\Delta(\emptyset).

Suppose now that
\Delta
is coassociative for all
t\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
with
|t|\leq N
and let
T\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
such that
|T|=N+1.
We have two cases, first if
T\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
be such that
T=B_{+}^{a}(f)
with
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\Omega}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{\Omega},
then

\Delta(T)=\emptyset\otimes T

and

(\Delta\otimes Id)\Delta(T)=\emptyset\otimes\emptyset\otimes T,

(Id\otimes\Delta)\Delta(T)=\emptyset\otimes\emptyset\otimes T.

Second, if
T=B_{+}^{a}(t)
with
t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}
, let
\Delta(t)=\sum_{(t)}t_{1}\otimes t_{2}.
Then we have

\Delta(T)=\sum_{(t)}B_{+}^{a}(t_{1})\otimes t_{2}+\emptyset\otimes T.

It follows that

(\Delta\otimes Id)\Delta(T)=\sum_{(t)}\left((B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)\Delta(t_{1})+\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a}(t_{1})\right)\otimes t_{2}+\emptyset\otimes\emptyset\otimes T.

Let us focus on the first term of this expression. We have

\sum_{(t)}\left((B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)\Delta(t_{1})\right)\otimes t_{2}=(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id\otimes Id)\sum_{(t)}\Delta(t_{1})\otimes t_{2}=(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id\otimes Id)(\Delta\otimes Id)\Delta(t).

Since
|t|=N
we can use the induction hypothesis: we have
(\Delta\otimes Id)\Delta(t)=(id\otimes\Delta)\Delta(t)
. Thus we obtain

\sum_{(t)}\left((B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)\Delta(t_{1})\right)\otimes t_{2}=(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id\otimes Id)\sum_{(t)}t_{1}\otimes\Delta(t_{2})=\sum_{(t)}B_{+}^{a}(t_{1})\otimes\Delta(t_{2}).

Thus we obtain

(\Delta\otimes Id)\Delta(T)=\sum_{(t)}\left(B_{+}^{a}(t_{1})\otimes\Delta(t_{2})+\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a}(t_{1})\otimes t_{2}\right)+\emptyset\otimes\emptyset\otimes T.

And on the other hand

\displaystyle(Id\otimes\Delta)\Delta(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(t)}(Id\otimes\Delta)\left(B_{+}^{a}(t_{1})\otimes t_{2}\right)+\emptyset\otimes\Delta(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(t)}B_{+}^{a}(t_{1})\otimes\Delta(t_{2})+\emptyset\otimes\left(\sum_{(t)}B_{+}^{a}(t_{1})\otimes t_{2}+\emptyset\otimes T\right)

\displaystyle=(\Delta\otimes Id)\Delta(T)

as expected. Therefore
\Delta
is coassociative.
∎

1.3
Shuffle bialgebra

Let us now state and prove the main result of this section. Notice that, as pointed out before, if
\lambda=0
, we can have
\Omega
to be a set without a semigroup structure in this Theorem (see Remark
1.3
).

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
(\Omega,.)
be a commutative semigroup. For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
,
(\mathcal{T}_{\Omega},\shuffle_{\lambda}^{T},\Delta)
is a commutative, non-associative, non-cocommutative and coassociative bialgebra.

Proof.

The various properties of the products and coproducts have been proven in
[
3
]
and in Proposition
1.7
above. Then compatibility of the counit with the product and of the unit with the coproduct are trivial to check. Thus we only need to prove that the coproduct is an morphism for the shuffle product (second diagram of (
1
)). We want to prove that, for any
\lambda
in
\mathbb{K}
and any rooted trees
T
and
T^{\prime}
one has

\Delta(T\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})=(\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda})\circ\tau_{23}\circ(\Delta\otimes\Delta)(T\otimes T^{\prime}).

(5)

We prove this by induction on
N=|T|+|T^{\prime}|
. If
N=0
we have
T=T^{\prime}=\emptyset
and a trivial computation gives

\Delta(\emptyset\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}\emptyset)=\emptyset\otimes\emptyset=(\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda})\circ\tau_{23}\circ(\Delta\otimes\Delta)(\emptyset\otimes\emptyset).

Assume now that Equation (
5
) holds for any
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
with
|t|+|t^{\prime}|\leq N,
and let
T,T^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}
such that
|T|+|T^{\prime}|=N+1.
We have various cases to consider:

If
T
(or
T^{\prime}
) is empty, then
\Delta(T\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}\emptyset)=\Delta(T)
and

(\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda})\circ\tau_{23}\circ(\Delta\otimes\Delta)(T\otimes\emptyset)=(\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda})\circ\tau_{23}\left(\sum_{(T)}T_{1}\otimes T_{2}\otimes\emptyset\otimes\emptyset\right)=\sum_{(T)}T_{1}\otimes T_{2}=\Delta(T)

and Equation (
5
) holds at rank
N+1
in this case as expected.

If
T
and
T^{\prime}
are both nonempty we can write
T=B_{+}^{a}(F)
and
T^{\prime}=B_{+}^{a}(F^{\prime})
and we have four subcases to consider depending on whether or not
F
and
F^{\prime}
are rooted trees.

1.

If
T=B_{+}^{a}(t)
and
T^{\prime}=B_{+}^{a^{\prime}}(t^{\prime})
with
t
and
t^{\prime}
in
{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}
, let
\Delta(t)=\sum_{(t)}t_{1}\otimes t_{2}
and
\Delta(t^{\prime})=\sum_{(t^{\prime})}t^{\prime}_{1}\otimes t^{\prime}_{2}.

We have then

T\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime}=B_{+}^{a}(t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})+B_{+}^{a^{\prime}}(T\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime})+\lambda B_{+}^{a.a^{\prime}}(t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime}).

By definition of the
\lambda
-huffle products of rooted forests, shuffles of rooted trees are linear combinations of rooted trees. Therefore

\displaystyle\Delta(t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})=(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)\Delta(t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})+\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a}(t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)(\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda})\circ\tau_{23}\left(\Delta(t)\otimes\Delta(T^{\prime})\right)+\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a}(t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})\quad\text{by the induction hypothesis}

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)(\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda})\circ\tau_{23}\left(\Delta(t)\otimes\left((B_{+}^{a^{\prime}}\otimes Id)\Delta(t^{\prime})+\emptyset\otimes T^{\prime}\right)\right)+\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a}(t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)(\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}\otimes\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda})\circ\tau_{23}\left(\sum_{(t),(t^{\prime})}t_{1}\otimes t_{2}\otimes B_{+}^{a^{\prime}}(t^{\prime}_{1})\otimes t^{\prime}_{2}+\sum_{(t)}t_{1}\otimes t_{2}\otimes\emptyset\otimes T^{\prime}\right)+\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a}(t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\penalty\

\displaystyle\sum_{(t),(t^{\prime})}B_{+}^{a}(t_{1}\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}B_{+}^{a^{\prime}}(t^{\prime}_{1}))\otimes(t_{2}\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime}_{2})+\sum_{(t)}B_{+}^{a}(t_{1})\otimes(t_{2}\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})+\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a}(t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime}).

Performing the same computation for the other two terms of
\Delta(T\shuffle_{\lambda}^{T}T^{\prime})
we find

\displaystyle\Delta(T\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})=\sum_{(t),(t^{\prime})}B_{+}^{a}(t_{1}\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}B_{+}^{a^{\prime}}(t^{\prime}_{1}))\otimes(t_{2}\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime}_{2})+\sum_{(t)}B_{+}^{a}(t_{1})\otimes(t_{2}\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})+\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a}(t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{(t),(t^{\prime})}B_{+}^{a^{\prime}}(B_{+}^{a}(t_{1})\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime}_{1})\otimes(t_{2}\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime}_{2})+\sum_{(t^{\prime})}B_{+}^{a^{\prime}}(t^{\prime}_{1})\otimes(T\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime}_{2})+\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a^{\prime}}(T\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\lambda\sum_{(t),(t^{\prime})}B_{+}^{a.a^{\prime}}(t_{1}\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime}_{1})\otimes(t_{2}\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime}_{2})+\lambda\emptyset\otimes B_{+}^{a.a^{\prime}}(t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime}).

Notice than in each of these computations, we could use the induction hypothesis since each of the trees appearing in
t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}T^{\prime}
,
T\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime}
and
t\shuffle^{T}_{\lambda}t^{\prime}
have at most
N
vertices. Putting the three summations over
(t)
and

2.1
The dual coproduct

Let
(A,m)
be a algebra freely generated as a vector space by a finite set
X
. Then
recall from
[
15
]
that for any such finite dimensional algebra the product
m
induces a coproduct
\Delta^{*}
on the dual
A^{*}
.
\Delta^{*}
is called the
dual coproduct
; that is to say “dual to
m
”. It is defined as follows: for a basis element
x\in X
, let us write
\langle x,.\rangle
the dual element in
A^{*}
defined on elements of the basis by
\langle x,y\rangle=\delta_{xy}
where
\delta_{xy}
is the Kronecker symbol. Extending the maps
\langle x,.\rangle
by linearity gives us a basis of
A^{*}
, and we define

\displaystyle\Delta^{*}:A^{*}

\displaystyle\to(A\otimes A)^{*}\simeq A^{*}\otimes A^{*}

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\Delta^{*}(f)

by

\langle\Delta^{*}(f),a\otimes b\rangle=\langle f,m(a\otimes b)\rangle,\quad a,b\in A\otimes A.

It is a standard exercise (see for example
[
10
]
) that
\Delta^{*}
is cocommutative (resp. coassociative) if, and only if,
m
is commutative (resp. associative).

In the case of rooted forest, we can use the fact that
{\mathcal{F}}
is graded by the number of vertices:
{\mathcal{F}}=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
with
{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
finite dimensional for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We work with the
graded dual

{\mathcal{F}}^{\circ}:=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{F}}_{n}^{*}
. This also work if the forests are decorated by a finite set
\Omega
, or if
\Omega
is such that each
({\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega})_{n}
is itself a graded, with each degree being finite dimensional. From now on, we will assume this to be true.

As in the generic case, a basis of
{\mathcal{F}}^{\circ}
is given by the the maps
\delta_{F}
on rooted forests by
\delta_{F}(F)=1
and
\delta_{F}(F^{\prime})=0
if
F\neq F^{\prime}
, and extended by linearity to a map on
{\mathcal{F}}
. Then
{\mathcal{F}}
and
{\mathcal{F}}^{\circ}
are clearly isomorphic as vector spaces and therefore, as a short hand notation, we will from now on write
F\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\circ}
instead of
\delta_{F}\in{\mathcal{F}}^{\circ}
. We use the same notations in the decorated case.

From now on,
\Delta^{*}
will be the coproduct dual to the shuffle of rooted forests. Recall also that for a rooted forest
F=T_{1}\dots T_{n}
and
I\subseteq[n]
we write
F_{I}:=\sqcup_{i\in I}T_{i}
. We prove a recursive formula for this coproduct, which for rooted trees is reminiscent of the Hochschild cocycle property of Connes-Kreimer coproduct (see for example
[
16
]
) which is important for applications of this coproduct to other domains, e.g. SPDEs (see for example
[
17
]
).

Theorem 2.1
.

We have
\Delta^{*}(\emptyset)=\emptyset\otimes\emptyset
, and for any
n\geq 2
, set
[n]_{i}:=[n]\setminus\{i\}
. Then for any non-connected forest
F=T_{1}\dots T_{n}

\Delta^{*}(F)=F\otimes\emptyset+\emptyset\otimes F+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\sqcup\otimes\sqcup)\circ\tau_{23}\left(\tilde{\Delta}^{*}(T_{i})\otimes\left(\sum_{I\subset[n]_{i}}\alpha_{I,n}F_{I}\otimes F_{[n]_{i}\setminus I}\right)\right)

(6)

with
\sqcup
the concatenation of rooted forests,
\tau_{23}
the usual flip defined by
\tau_{23}(a\otimes b\otimes c\otimes d)=a\otimes c\otimes b\otimes d
and

\alpha_{I,n}:=(|I|+1)(n-|I|).

For a tree
T=B_{+}^{a}(F)
we have

\Delta^{*}(T)=(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)(Id\otimes\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}})\Delta^{*}(F)+(Id\otimes B_{+}^{a})(\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\otimes Id)\Delta^{*}(F).

(7)

with
\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}:\mathcal{F}_{\Omega}\to\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
the projection to rooted trees parallely to non-connected rooted forests.

Proof.

First notice that
F_{1}\shuffle^{T}F_{2}=\emptyset\Longleftrightarrow F_{1}=F_{2}=\emptyset
. This proves
\Delta^{*}(\emptyset)=\emptyset\otimes\emptyset
.

Let us now prove Equation (
6
). For
F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}^{+}
we are looking for all forests
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
such that
\langle F,f_{1}\shuffle^{T}f_{2}\rangle\neq 0
. First observe that we have the trivial solutions
f_{1}=F
,
f_{2}=\emptyset
and
f_{1}=\emptyset
,
f_{2}=F
. These give us the terms
F\otimes\emptyset
and
\emptyset\otimes F
in Equation (
6
).

For the non-trivial solutions, let us set
f^{1}=t_{1}\cdots t_{p}
and
f^{2}=t^{\prime}_{1}\cdots t^{\prime}_{q}
. From Equation (
2
) of the definition of the shuffle of rooted forests we must have

. We must also have that one of the tree constituting
F
appears in

, for some

and

. The other trees of

must then be the other trees of
f^{1}
and
f^{2}
. Writing this rigorously be obtain

\exists i\in\{1,\cdots,n\},j\in\{1,\cdots,p\},k\in\{1,\cdots,q\}\text{ s.t. }\langle T_{i},t_{j}\shuffle t^{\prime}_{k}\rangle\neq 0\penalty\ \wedge\penalty\ \langle F_{[n]_{i}},f^{1}_{[p]_{j}}f^{2}_{[q]_{k}}\rangle\neq 0.

.

Let us turn our attention to the condition

other than the i-th

Thus, with the coefficients

2.

For
T
a rooted tree,
\Gamma\subseteq V(T)
is called
admissible
or an
admissible family
when we simultaneously have:

•

T_{\Gamma}
and
T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}
are both rooted trees;

•

for every node
v\in\Gamma
with
\text{fert}(v)\geq 2,
we have
\text{fert}(v)
in
T_{\Gamma}
to be equal to
\text{fert}(v)
in
T.

•

for every node
v\in V(T)\setminus\Gamma
with
\text{fert}(v)\geq 2,
we have
\text{fert}(v)
in
T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}
to be equal to
\text{fert}(v)
in
T.

3.

Let
T
be a rooted tree and
\Gamma\subseteq V(T)
. We define
contraction of
T
with respect to
\Gamma
to be the undecorated forest
\text{cont}_{\Gamma}(T)\in{\mathcal{T}}
obtained by contracting all the subtrees
S\subseteq T
such that

V(S)\subset\Gamma\text{ and }\nexists v\in{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}\text{ such that }root(S)<_{T}v

and contracting also all the subtrees
S\subseteq T
such that

V(S)\subseteq V(T)\setminus{\Gamma}\text{ and }\nexists v\in{\Gamma}\text{ such that }root(S)<_{T}v.

We also define the
contracted fertility
(relative to
\Gamma
) to be
1
if cont
{}_{\Gamma}(T)
is empty or reduced to only one vertex and

c_{\Gamma}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\text{cont}_{\Gamma}(T)\\
v\notin\ell(\text{cont}_{\Gamma}(T))\end{subarray}}\text{fert}(v)

otherwise.

Remark 2.4

We will give a combinatorial description of the coproduct
\Delta^{*}
using the following inductive formulas for the admissible families and contracted fertility.

Lemma 2.6
.

1.

For a tree
T=B_{+}^{a}(t)
with
t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}
, the admissible families of
T
are the sets

\Gamma=\tilde{\Gamma}\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma=\{a\}\cup\tilde{\Gamma}

with
\tilde{\Gamma}
an admissible family of
t
. In both cases the reduced fertility is given by
c_{\Gamma}=c_{\tilde{\Gamma}}
.

2.

For a tree
T=B_{+}^{a}(T_{1}\cdots T_{n})
with
n\geq 2
the admissible families of
T
are the sets

\Gamma=\Gamma_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma=\{a\}\cup\Gamma_{i}\cup V(T_{1}\cdots\widehat{T_{i}}\cdots T_{n})

some some
i\in[n]
and
\Gamma_{i}
an admissible family of
T_{i}
with
\Gamma_{i}\neq V(T_{i})
. In both cases the reduced fertility is given by
c_{\Gamma}=nc_{\Gamma_{i}}
.

Proof.

1.

By the definition of admissible families, we have that for a tree
T=B_{+}^{a}(t)
with
t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}
,
\Gamma\subseteq V(T)
is an admissible subfamily of
T
if, and only if,
\Gamma\cap V(t)
is an admissible family of
t
. Again by the definition of admissible families and since
t
is a tree, we have that admissible families of
T
might contain the root or not. In the former case, we find that admissible families of
T
are exactly the sets
\{a\}\cup\tilde{\Gamma}
with
\tilde{\Gamma}
an admissible family of
t
and in the later case that admissible families of
T
are exactly the sets
\tilde{\Gamma}
, again with
\tilde{\Gamma}
an admissible family of
t
.

In both of these cases, we see that the contraction of
T
with respect to
\Gamma
cont
{}_{\Gamma}(T)
is either cont
{}_{\tilde{\Gamma}}(t)
or
B_{+}(
cont
{}_{\tilde{\Gamma}}(t))
. In both cases we have
c_{\Gamma}=c_{\tilde{\Gamma}}
and thus the first point of the proof holds.

2.

For a tree
T=B_{+}^{a}(T_{1}\cdots T_{n})
with
n\geq 2
the two cases have to be considered separately. First, let
\Gamma\subseteq V(T)
be an admissible family of
T
which does not contains the root of
T
. Then since
T_{\Gamma}
must be a rooted tree, we have that
\Gamma
must contains vertices from at most one the the
T_{i}
. Thus in this case,
\Gamma
is an admissible family of
T
if, and only if, it is an admissible family of
T_{i}
which does not contain all the vertices of
T_{i}
(or otherwise the root has fertility
n-1
in
T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}
). In this case have
\Gamma=\Gamma_{i}
as claimed.

For the case where
\Gamma\subseteq V(T)
is an admissible family of
T
which does contains the root of
T
, we can conclude by observing that, according to the definition of admissible families, if
\Gamma
is an admissible family, then so is
V(T)\setminus\Gamma
. We can also observe directly that for
T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}
to be a rooted tree,
V(T)\setminus\Gamma
must contains vertices from at most one of the
T_{i}
since it does not contain the root, and that it must not contain all vertices of
T_{i}
otherwise the root of
T
has fertility one less in
T_{\Gamma}
than in
T
. Thus we have in this case exactly
\Gamma=\{a\}\cup\Gamma_{i}\cup V(T_{1}\cdots\widehat{T_{i}}\cdots T_{n})
with
\Gamma_{i}
an admissible family of
T_{i}
which does not contain all the vertices of
T_{i}
.

In both of these cases, to obtain
\text{cont}_{\Gamma}(T)
we must contract each of the
T_{j}
for
j\neq i
, in the first case as subset of
V(T)\setminus\Gamma
and in the second case as subset of
\Gamma
. Thus we have

\text{cont}_{\Gamma}(T)=B_{+}\left(\underbrace{\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}\cdots\penalty\ \begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\end{picture}}_{n-1\text{ times}}\text{cont}_{\Gamma_{i}}(T_{i})\right)

and therefore
c_{\Gamma}=nc_{\Gamma_{i}}
as claimed.

∎

We can now state and prove a combinatorial formula for the dual coproduct.

Theorem 2.7
.

The coproduct
\Delta^{*}:{\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}\otimes{\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}
dual to the shuffle of rooted forests
\shuffle^{T}
admits the following combinatorial description:

\Delta^{*}(T)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\subseteq V(T)\text{ admissible}\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma}\;T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}\otimes T_{\Gamma}

for any rooted tree
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}
and for any
F=T_{1}\cdots T_{n}
with
n\geq 2

\Delta^{*}(F)=F\otimes\emptyset+\emptyset\otimes F+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma_{i}\subseteq V(T_{i})\text{ admissible}\\
\emptyset\neq\Gamma_{i}\neq V(T_{i})\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma_{i}}\quad\sum_{I\subseteq[n]_{i}}\alpha_{I,n}\;(T_{i})_{V(T_{i})\setminus\Gamma_{i}}T_{I}\otimes(T_{i})_{\Gamma_{i}}T_{[n]_{i}\setminus I}

with once again
\alpha_{I,n}:=(|I|+1)(n-|I|)
.

Proof.

We prove this result by induction on
N=|F|
. If
N=0
, the result trivially holds since
\emptyset\subseteq V(\emptyset)
is the unique admissible family of the empty tree.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let us assume that the Theorem holds for all forests with
F
with
|F|\leq N
and let
T=B_{+}^{a}(F)
be a rooted tree of
N+1
vertices. Then by Equation (
7
) of Theorem
2.1
we have

\Delta^{*}(T)=\underbrace{(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)(Id\otimes\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}})\Delta^{*}(F)}_{=:A}+\underbrace{(Id\otimes B_{+}^{a})(\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\otimes Id)\Delta^{*}(F)}_{=:B}.

Then we have two cases to consider:

1.

If
F=t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}
then
|t|=N
and we can use the induction hypothesis to obtain
\Delta^{*}(t)
. Since for any
\tilde{\Gamma}
admissible family of
t
,
t_{V(t)\setminus\Gamma}
and
t_{\Gamma}
are rooted trees we have

A=(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\Gamma}\subseteq V(t)\\
\text{ admissible}\end{subarray}}c_{\tilde{\Gamma}}\;t_{V(t)\setminus\tilde{\Gamma}}\otimes t_{\tilde{\Gamma}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{\Gamma}\subseteq V(t)\\
\text{ admissible}\end{subarray}}c_{\tilde{\Gamma}}\;B_{+}^{a}\left(t_{V(t)\setminus\tilde{\Gamma}}\right)\otimes t_{\tilde{\Gamma}}.

Using the first point of Lemma
2.6
we see that the sum runs precisely on admissible families of
T
that do not contain the root. We have

A=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\subseteq V(T)\text{ admissible}\\
a\notin\Gamma\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma}\;B_{+}^{a}\left(t_{V(t)\setminus\Gamma}\right)\otimes t_{\Gamma}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\subseteq V(T)\text{ admissible}\\
a\notin\Gamma\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma}\;T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}\otimes T_{\Gamma}.

For the last equality, we have used that since
\Gamma\subseteq V(t)\Leftrightarrow a\notin\Gamma
we have
t_{\Gamma}=T_{\Gamma}
and
B_{+}^{a}\left(t_{V(t)\setminus\Gamma}\right)=T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}
.

Using exactly the same argument we obtain

B=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\subseteq V(T)\text{ admissible}\\
a\in\Gamma\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma}\;T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}\otimes T_{\Gamma}.

Therefore

\Delta^{*}(T)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\subseteq V(T)\text{ admissible}\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma}\;T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}\otimes T_{\Gamma}

as claimed and we have proved the formula for
\Delta^{*}(T)
at rank
|T|=N+1
in the case
T=B_{+}^{a}(t)
.

2.

Now if
F=T_{1}\cdots T_{n}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}\setminus{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}
, using the inductive formula (
7
) of Theorem
2.1
and the induction hypothesis we find

\displaystyle A=(B_{+}^{a}\otimes Id)(Id\otimes\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}})

\displaystyle\left(T_{1}\cdots T_{n}\otimes\emptyset+\emptyset\otimes T_{1}\cdots T_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma_{i}\subseteq V(T_{i})\\
\text{ admissible}\\
\emptyset\neq\Gamma_{i}\neq V(T_{i})\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma_{i}}\sum_{I\subseteq[n]_{i}}\alpha_{I,n}\;(T_{i})_{V(T_{i})\setminus\Gamma_{i}}T_{I}\otimes(T_{i})_{\Gamma_{i}}T_{[n]_{i}\setminus I}\right)

and

\displaystyle B=(Id\otimes B_{+}^{a})(\Pi_{\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\otimes Id)

\displaystyle\left(T_{1}\cdots T_{n}\otimes\emptyset+\emptyset\otimes T_{1}\cdots T_{n}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma_{i}\subseteq V(T_{i})\\
\text{ admissible}\\
\emptyset\neq\Gamma_{i}\neq V(T_{i})\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma_{i}}\sum_{I\subseteq[n]_{i}}\alpha_{I,n}\;(T_{i})_{V(T_{i})\setminus\Gamma_{i}}T_{I}\otimes(T_{i})_{\Gamma_{i}}T_{[n]_{i}\setminus I}\right).

Since by hypothesis
T_{1}\cdots T_{n}
is not a tree, the only on-vanishing terms in
A
are
T_{1}\cdots T_{n}\otimes\emptyset
and
I=[n]_{i}
. This implies
\alpha_{I,n}=n
and we obtain

A=T\otimes\emptyset+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma_{i}\subseteq V(T_{i})\\
\text{ admissible}\\
\emptyset\neq\Gamma_{i}\neq V(T_{i})\end{subarray}}nc_{\Gamma_{i}}B_{+}^{a}\left((T_{i})_{V(T_{i})\setminus\Gamma_{i}}T_{1}\cdots\widehat{T_{i}}\cdots T_{n}\right)\otimes(T_{i})_{\Gamma_{i}}

Using this time the second point of Lemma
2.6
we find that the two sums are precisely summing over non-empty admissible families of
T
that do not contain the root of
T
. Together with the term
T\otimes\emptyset
we obtain

A=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\subseteq V(T)\\
\text{ admissible}\\
a\notin\Gamma\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma}B_{+}^{a}\left((T_{i})_{V(T_{i})\setminus\Gamma_{i}}T_{1}\cdots\widehat{T_{i}}\cdots T_{n}\right)\otimes(T_{i})_{\Gamma}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\subseteq V(T)\\
\text{ admissible}\\
a\notin\Gamma\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma}T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}\otimes T_{\Gamma}.

For the last equality, we have used as before that since
\Gamma\subseteq V(T_{i})
we have
(T_{i})_{\Gamma}=T_{\Gamma}
and
B_{+}^{a}\left((T_{i})_{V(T_{i})\setminus\Gamma_{i}}T_{1}\cdots\widehat{T_{i}}\cdots T_{n}\right)=T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}
.

Using exactly the same argument we obtain

B=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\subseteq V(T)\text{ admissible}\\
a\in\Gamma\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma}\;T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}\otimes T_{\Gamma}.

Therefore

\Delta^{*}(T)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Gamma\subseteq V(T)\text{ admissible}\end{subarray}}c_{\Gamma}\;T_{V(T)\setminus\Gamma}\otimes T_{\Gamma}

as claimed and we have proved the formula for
\Delta^{*}(T)
at rank
|T|=N+1
in the case
T=B_{+}^{a}(T_{1}\cdots T_{n})
.

This covers the two possible cases and we have proven the formula for
\Delta^{*}(T)
.

We conclude the proof by noticing that for
F
a non-connected rooted forest with
N+1
vertices, the formula for
\Delta^{*}(F)
follows directly from the formula for
\Delta^{*}(T)
and the inductive formula of Theorem
2.1
for
\Delta^{*}(F)
. Therefore the Theorem holds for trees and forests with
N+1
vertices and, by induction, holds for every trees and forests.
∎

2.3
Dual bialgebra

We now endow
{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{+\infty}({\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega})_{n}
(where
({\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega})_{n}
) is the vector space freely generated by rooted trees with
n
vertices) with a bialgebra structure. Recall that for two trees
T
and
T^{\prime}
and
v\in V(T)
,
T\underset{v}{\curvearrowleft}T^{\prime}
is the tree obtained by grafting the root of
T^{\prime}
as a new direct descendant of
v
.

Definition 2.8
.

For any
T
and
T^{\prime}
in
{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}
set
\emptyset\lhd T^{\prime}=T^{\prime}
if
T=\emptyset
and, if
T\neq\emptyset
, set

T\lhd T^{\prime}:=\sum_{l\in\ell(T)}T\underset{l}{\curvearrowleft}T^{\prime}

where the sum runs over the set of leaves of
T
. We further define

T\unlhd T^{\prime}=\begin{cases}0,\quad\text{if}\quad|\ell(T)|>1\\
T\lhd T^{\prime},\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

These two maps are then extended by linearaity to product
\lhd,\unlhd:{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}\otimes{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}
.

These products are non commutative and non associative. However, they are two restrictions of the Grossman-Larson product introduced in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
so it should be no surprise that they are pre-Lie products. Since this result is just in passing, we simply refer the reader to
[
21
]
(although they were introduced earlier under a different name in
[
22
]
) for the definition of pre-Lie algebras, but they will be defined in the proof below.

Proposition 2.9
.

(\mathcal{T}^{\circ}_{\Omega},\unlhd)
is a right and a left pre-Lie algebra and
(\mathcal{T}^{\circ}_{\Omega},\lhd)
is a left pre-Lie algebra.

Proof.

We prove the two results separately.

For
\unlhd
, let
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}^{\circ}.
We first prove that it is a right pre-Lie algebra, i.e. we want to prove that for any trees
t_{1}
,
t_{2}
and
t_{3}
we have:

(t_{1}\unlhd t_{2})\unlhd t_{3}-t_{1}\unlhd(t_{2}\unlhd t_{3})=(t_{2}\unlhd t_{1})\unlhd t_{3}-t_{2}\unlhd(t_{1}\unlhd t_{3})

We have four cases to consider.

•

If
|\ell(t_{1})=|\ell(t_{2})|=|\ell(t_{3})|=1,
then the product
\unlhd
reduces to the concatenation of words. Thus it is associative and therefore pre-Lie.

•

If
|\ell(t_{1})|>1
, then

t_{1}\unlhd t_{2}=t_{1}\unlhd t_{3}=t_{1}\unlhd(t_{2}\unlhd t_{3})=0

and
|\ell(t_{2}\unlhd t_{1})|\geq|\ell(t_{1})|
therefore

(t_{2}\unlhd t_{1})\unlhd t_{3}=0

and the result follows.

•

The case
|\ell(t_{2})|>1
is similar to the previous one.

•

The last case
l(t_{3})>1
follows from the fact that

(t_{1}\unlhd t_{2})\unlhd t_{3}\;\text{and}\;t_{1}\unlhd(t_{2}\unlhd t_{3})

are the trees obtained by grafting
t_{3}
on top of
t_{2}
and
t_{2}
on top of
t_{1}
and hence are equal. The same argument follows for the other side of the pre-Lie identity.

The fact that
\unlhd
is also a left pre-Lie algebra is proven in the exact same way and we omit the details here.

For
\lhd
, we want to show
(t_{1}\lhd t_{2})\lhd t_{3}-t_{1}\lhd(t_{2}\lhd t_{3})=(t_{1}\lhd t_{3})\lhd t_{2}-t_{1}\lhd(t_{3}\lhd t_{2})
. This can be checked through a tedious verification of the arguments in the sums of each sides, but we give instead a simpler, more graphical argument. Notice that this is the usual argument to show that the Grossman-Larson product is pre-Lie.

First, observe that
(t_{1}\lhd t_{2})\lhd t_{3}
is a linear combination of two types of trees. Trees where
t_{3}
is grafted on a leaf of
t_{2}
which is itself grafted on a leaf of
t_{1}
; and trees where
t_{3}
and
t_{2}
are both grafted on leafs of
t_{1}
. But
t_{1}\lhd(t_{2}\lhd t_{3})
contains precisely the trees where trees where
t_{3}
is grafted on a leaf of
t_{2}
which is itself grafted on a leaf of
t_{1}
. Thus
(t_{1}\lhd t_{2})\lhd t_{3}-t_{1}\lhd(t_{2}\lhd t_{3})
contains only the trees where
t_{3}
and
t_{2}
are both grafted on leafs of
t_{1}
.

On the other hand, with the same argument but exchanging
t_{3}
and
t_{2}
we see that
(t_{1}\lhd t_{3})\lhd t_{2}-t_{1}\lhd(t_{3}\lhd t_{2})
contains also the trees where
t_{3}
and
t_{2}
are both grafted on leafs of
t_{1}
. Therefore the two sides of the equality are equal and
\lhd
is left pre-Lie.

∎

The next result is the main one of this subsection. It explains why the coproduct of Definition
1.6
is canonical, and it also implies that it is co-right pre-Lie.

Theorem 2.10
.

({\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ},\unlhd,\Delta^{*})
is the bialgebra dual to
(\mathcal{T}_{\Omega},\shuffle^{T},\Delta)
as bialgebra.

Proof.

One can directly check that the coproduct
\Delta^{*}
is a morphism for
\unlhd
, i.e. that

\Delta^{*}(T_{1}\unlhd T_{2})=(\unlhd\otimes\unlhd)\circ\tau_{23}\circ(\Delta^{*}\otimes\Delta^{*}(T_{1}\otimes T_{2}))

for any rooted trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. However, since by Theorem
1.8

(\mathcal{T}_{\Omega},\shuffle^{T},\Delta)
is a bialgebra, it is enough to prove that
\unlhd
is the product dual to the coproduct
\Delta
, i.e. that, for any trees
(T_{1},T_{2})\in({\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ})^{2}
we have

\langle T_{1}\unlhd T_{2},T\rangle=\langle T_{1}\otimes T_{2},\Delta(T)\rangle\penalty\ \forall T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}.

(9)

Let us assume that
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
and
T
are rooted trees. The general cases of
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
and
T
to be linear combinations is obtained by linearity. We have three cases to consider.

If
T_{1}=\emptyset
we have

\langle T_{1}\unlhd T_{2},T\rangle=\langle T_{2},T\rangle=\delta_{T_{2}T}

with
\delta
the Kronecker symbol. On the other hand, we then have
\langle T_{1}\otimes T_{2},\Delta(T)\rangle=\langle\emptyset\otimes T_{2},\Delta(T)\rangle
. The only possible non-vanishing term in this expression is
\emptyset\otimes T
. Thus we also obtain

\langle T_{1}\otimes T_{2},\Delta(T)\rangle=\delta_{T_{2}T}

and Equation (
9
) holds in the case
T_{1}=\emptyset
.

If
|\ell(T_{1})|=1
then
T_{1}
is a linear tree with
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
vertices. Then
T_{1}\unlhd T_{2}
is the tree obtained by grafting
T_{2}
on the unique leaf of
T_{1}
. On the other hand,
in this case, we have by definition of
\Delta
that
\langle T_{1}\otimes T_{2},\Delta(T)\rangle
is non-zero if,and only if, the trunk of
T
to be of length at least
n
, and the tree
T^{\prime}
obtained by removing the first
n
vertices of the trunk of
T
to be
T_{2}
. This is equivalent to saying that we have
T=T_{1}\underset{l}{\curvearrowleft}T_{2}=T_{1}\unlhd T_{2}
, with
l
the unique leaf of
T_{1}
. Therefore, in this case, Equation (
9
) holds as well.

Finally, if
|\ell(T_{1})\geq 2
then
\langle T_{1}\unlhd T_{2},T\rangle=0
for all
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}
. On the other hand, by definition of
\Delta
(Definition
1.6
) we have for any
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}

\Delta(T)=\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T}-1}\ell_{n}\otimes T^{\prime\prime}

with
N_{T}
the number of vertices in the trunk of
T
(thus
N_{T}\geq 1
since
T
is a tree) and
\ell_{n}
the linear tree with
n
vertices. The terms in the left of these tensors have always at most one leaf. Thus, if
T_{1}
has two or more leaves we have
\langle T_{1}\otimes T_{2},\Delta(T)\rangle=0
for any
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}
. Thus Equation (
9
) holds also in this case, and the Theorem is proven.

∎

2.4
Dual primitive trees

Let us now caracterize and enumerate the rooted trees that are primitive for the dual coproduct
\Delta^{*}
, i.e. the trees such that

\Delta^{*}(T)=T\otimes\emptyset+\emptyset\otimes T.

Remark 2.11
.

Notice that this is not the same as asking what are the primitive elements of
\Delta^{*}
. Linear combination of non primitive trees could a priori be primitive. Finding all primitive elements is much harder but less interesting for our purpose, since we are in the dual and since it is not clear that we have a Milnor-Moore theorem (
[
23
]
, see also
[
24
]
and
[
25
]
) for non associative Hopf algebras.

The caracterization of primitive rooted trees eventually reduces to the following two technical lemmas.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be two non-empty rooted trees, let
v\in V(T)
be a leaf of
T
and
T^{\prime}=B_{+}^{v^{\prime}}(F^{\prime}).
Then

\langle\Delta^{*}(T\underset{v}{\curvearrowleft}T^{\prime}),T\otimes T^{\prime}\rangle\neq 0.

Proof.

We prove this by induction on
N=|T|+|T^{\prime}|
. If
N=2
, then
T
and
T^{\prime}
both have only one vertex since both are non-empty, and
T\underset{v}{\curvearrowleft}T^{\prime}
is a linear tree with two vertices. Then the result follows from a direct computation.

Suppose that for some
N\geq 3
the statement holds for any rooted trees
t
and
t^{\prime}
such that
|t|+|t^{\prime}|<N
. Let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be two rooted trees such that
|T|+|T^{\prime}|=N.
If
|T|=1
then as before a direct computation with the inductive formula (
7
) for
\Delta^{*}(T\underset{v}{\curvearrowleft}T^{\prime})
.

If
|T|\geq 2
, write
T=B_{+}^{a}(T_{1}\dots T_{k})
and
T^{\prime}=B_{+}^{b}(T^{\prime}_{1}\dots T^{\prime}_{l})
and consider
v
a leaf of
T.
Then we have

T\shuffle^{T}T^{\prime}=B_{+}^{a}\left((T_{1}\dots T_{k})\shuffle^{T}T^{\prime}\right)+B_{+}^{b}\left(T\shuffle^{T}(T^{\prime}_{1}\dots T^{\prime}_{l})\right).

Since
|T|\geq 2
, we have that
v
is a leaf one of
T_{i},
for one
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}.
Then, since the shuffle
T_{i}\shuffle^{T}T^{\prime}
appears in
(T_{1}\dots T_{k})\shuffle^{T}T^{\prime},
and
|T_{i}|+|T^{\prime}|<N,
we can use the induction hypothesis to conclude that
T_{i}\otimes T^{\prime}
appears in the coproduct
\Delta^{*}(T_{i}\underset{v}{\curvearrowleft}T^{\prime})
. Going to the dual, this is equivalent to saying that
T_{i}\underset{v}{\curvearrowleft}T^{\prime}
appears in
T_{i}\shuffle^{T}T^{\prime}
. Therefore
T\underset{v}{\curvearrowleft}T^{\prime}
appears in
T\shuffle^{T}T^{\prime}
. Again going to the dual, this is equivalent to saying that
T\otimes T^{\prime}
appears in
\Delta^{*}(T\underset{v}{\curvearrowleft}T^{\prime})
, which is what we wanted to show.
∎

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
T,T^{\prime}
be non-empty rooted trees and
t\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}
such that
\langle t,T\shuffle T^{\prime}\rangle\neq 0
. Then there exists
v\in V(t)
such that
\text{fert}(t)=1.

Proof.

Let
T,T^{\prime}
be non-empty rooted trees and let us prove once again this result by induction on
N=|T|+|T^{\prime}|.
For
|T|+|T^{\prime}|=2
we have
|T|=|T^{\prime}|=1,
. Thus we can write
T=\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny a}\end{picture}
and
T^{\prime}=\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny b}\end{picture}
for some

. Then

T\shuffle^{T}T^{\prime}=\begin{picture}(-5.0,-1.0)\put(3.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(3.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{7.0}}\put(3.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\par\put(5.0,-1.0){\tiny a}\put(5.0,6.0){\tiny b}\end{picture}+\begin{picture}(-5.0,-1.0)\put(3.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(3.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{7.0}}\put(3.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\par\put(5.0,-1.0){\tiny b}\put(5.0,6.0){\tiny a}\end{picture}

and the result holds.

Assuming that for some
N\geq 3
the result holds for any
t,t^{\prime}
with
|t|+|t^{\prime}|<N,
let
T,T^{\prime}
such that
|T|+|T^{\prime}|=N.
We can write
T=B_{+}^{a}(F)
and
T^{\prime}=B_{+}^{b}(F^{\prime}),
in this case

T\shuffle^{T}T^{\prime}=B_{+}^{a}\left(F\shuffle^{T}T^{\prime}\right)+B_{+}^{b}\left(T\shuffle^{T}F^{\prime}\right).

In both expressions

and

we have shuffles of trees with less than N nodes. Therefore, by the induction hypothesis, a tree of each of the forests appearing in these product has vertility exactly one. Therefore it always exists a vertex in both

and

that has fertility exactly one and the lemma is proven by induction.
∎

We can now fully characterize the primitive trees for the dual coproduct
\Delta^{*}
.

Theorem 2.14
.

A tree is primitive for
\Delta^{*}
if, and only if, each of its vertex except the leafs has fertility at least two.

Proof.

(\implies)
Let
T
be a tree and
v\in V(T).
Suppose
fert(v)=1
and let
v^{\prime}
be the direct descendent of
v.
We can write
T=\tilde{T}\underset{v}{\curvearrowleft}\tilde{T^{\prime}}
where
\tilde{T^{\prime}}
has
v^{\prime}
as its root and
v
is a leaf of
\tilde{T}.
Therefore, from the Lemma
2.12
,
T
is not primitive.

(\impliedby)
Let
T\in{\mathcal{T}}_{\Omega}^{\circ}
be a tree and suppose that it’s not primitive for
\Delta^{*}.
Therefore, there exists two rooted trees
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
such that
\emptyset\neq t_{1}\neq T,

\emptyset\neq t_{2}\neq T
and

\langle T,t_{1}\shuffle^{T}t_{2}\rangle=\langle\Delta^{*}(T),t_{1}\otimes t_{2}\rangle\neq 0.

By Lemma
2.13
, each such tree in the set has at least one vertex with fertility one, hence the result holds.
∎

This Theorem allows us to deduce an inductive formula for the number of primitive trees with
n
vertices. Let
p_{n}
be the number of primitive trees for
\Delta^{*}
, not counting linear combinations. As stated above,
p_{n}
is a lower bound for the dimension of the space of primitive elements for
\Delta^{*}
.

Proposition 2.15
.

p_{n}
is given by the recursive formula
p_{0}=0
,
p_{1}=1
,
p_{2}=0
and, for
n\geq 2
:

p_{n+1}=\sum_{k=2}^{n}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}\geq\cdots\geq n_{k}\geq 1\\
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}=n\end{subarray}}\;\prod P_{n_{i}}

(10)

where
P_{n_{i}}=\binom{p_{n_{i}}+k_{n_{i}}-1}{k_{n_{i}}}
and
k_{n_{i}}
is the number of times
n_{i}
appears in the
k
-tuple
(n_{1},\dots,n_{k})
,and where the product is over each distinct
n_{i}
.

Proof.

The initial values of
p_{n}
are computed by enumeration of the primitive trees. For the inductive formula, let
n\geq 2
and
T=B_{+}(T_{1}\cdots T_{k})
be a primitive tree with
n+1
vertices. If
k=1
, the root has only one direct descendant, which is in contradiction with the previous characterisation of primitive trees. Thus we must have
k\geq 2
. Since
T
has
n+1
vertices and each of the
T_{i}
has at least one vertex, we must also have
k\leq n
.

Now set
n_{i}:=|V(T_{i})|
. For
T
to have
n+1
vertices, we must have
n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}=n
. Since a permutation of
T_{i}
does not change
T
, we have that
(n_{1},\cdots,n_{k})
must be a partition of the integer
n
(i.e. a finite unordered sequence whose terms sum to
n
). Up to a relabelling, these terms can be put in a decreasing order. Thus we obtain the summand of the second sum sign in Equation (
10
).

Notice that for each
T_{i}
, each of the internal vertices of
T_{i}
is an internal vertex of
T
, and thus has at least two descendants since

is primitive. Then, for each
n_{i},
consider
k_{n_{i}}
as the number of times
n_{i}
appears in the partition
n.
The number of different ways we can arrange

-combinations with repetitions on the set

vertices. It is a classic result of combinatorics (see for example

) that this number is

\binom{p_{n_{i}}+k_{n_{i}}-1}{k_{n_{i}}}.

The product over all those permutations for each

∎

Rooted trees and Rota-Baxter algebras

In this section,
A
will always denote an associative and commutative algebra over a field
\mathbb{K}
whose product we omit for readability.

3.1
Prerequisites on Rota-Baxter algebras

Rota-Baxter algebras (RBA for short) were introduced
[
11
]
and further developped in
[
12
]
and
[
27
]
among others.
We recall the needed definition and results of the theory.

Definition 3.1
.

1.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}.
A
Rota-Baxter algebra of weight
\lambda
(RBA of weight
\lambda
) is a pair
(R,P)
consisting of an algebra
R
and a linear operator
P:R\to R
that satisfies the equation

P(x)P(y)=P(xP(y))+P(P(x)y)+\lambda P(xy),\quad\forall x,y\in R.

2.

Let
(R_{1},P_{1})
and
(R_{2},P_{2})
be two Rota-Baxter algebras of the same weight
\lambda.
A
Rota-Baxter homomorphism
is an algebra homomorphism
\bar{f}:(R_{1},P_{1})\to(R_{2},P_{2})
such that

\bar{f}\circ P_{1}=P_{2}\circ\bar{f}.

Let
A
be an algebra. Recall that a free Rota-Baxter algebra of weight
\lambda
on
A
is an RBA
(R,P)
of weight
\lambda
and an algebra homomorphism
\phi:A\to R,
such that for any RBA
(R^{\prime},P^{\prime})
of weight
\lambda
and algebra homomorphism
f:A\to R^{\prime}
there is a unique Rota-Baxter algebra morphism
\bar{f}:(R,P)\to(R^{\prime},P^{\prime})
such that
f=\bar{f}\circ\phi
. Free RBA were described in
[
13
]
(see also
[
9
]
). We now recall this construction.

For an algebra
A
, recall that
{\mathcal{W}}_{A}
is the vector space freely generated by words written in the alphabet
A
. We denote the
\emptyset
the empty word which is an element of
{\mathcal{W}}_{A}
and by
\sqcup
the concatenation product on
{\mathcal{W}}_{A}
:

(a_{1}\cdots a_{p})\sqcup(b_{1}\cdots b_{q})=(a_{1}\cdots a_{p}b_{1}\cdots b_{q}).

We further endow
{\mathcal{W}}_{A}
with the product
\diamond_{\lambda}
defined by:

a\diamond_{\lambda}b=(a_{0}.b_{0})\sqcup(a^{\prime}\shuffle_{\lambda}b^{\prime})

where
a=a_{0}\sqcup a^{\prime},\;b=b_{0}\sqcup b^{\prime}
. As before,
a_{0}.b_{0}\in A
is the multiplication in
A
of
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
.

Further set
P_{A}:W_{A}\to W_{A}
to be

P_{A}(w)=(1_{A})\sqcup w

and consider
j_{A}:A\to{\mathcal{W}}_{A}
the canonical inclusion map. The result of
[
13
,
9
]
then reads:

Theorem 3.2
.

({\mathcal{W}}_{A},P_{A})
is a Rota-Baxter algebra of weight
\lambda
for the product
\diamond_{\lambda}
. Together with the map
j_{A},
it is the free commutative Rota-Baxter algebra on A of weight
\lambda.

3.2
Diamond products on forests

\lambda
-shuffles on forests were already introduced in Subsection
1.1
. Thus one could built
\diamond_{\lambda}
products on forests from these
\lambda
-shuffles on forests, but this construction does not have so much good properties. In order to take into account the concatenation product of rooted forests (which is very different from the concatenation of words, since the former is commutative while the later is not) we need modify the
\lambda
-shuffles of rooted forests. Recall that for a set
\Omega
, we set

to be the set of non-empty forests.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
A
be a commutative algebra over
\mathbb{K}
and set

on

et

on

•

Set
\emptyset\ast_{\lambda}\emptyset:=\emptyset

,

has been defined for any non empty forests
F
and
F^{\prime}
such that

, and that

has been defined for any forests
F
and

and

be two forests such that

If

.

•

If

and

•

Again if

\displaystyle\begin{picture}(-5.0,-1.0)\put(3.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(3.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{7.0}}\put(3.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\par\put(5.0,-1.0){\tiny a}\put(5.0,6.0){\tiny b}\end{picture}\diamond_{\lambda}\begin{picture}(-5.0,-1.0)\put(3.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(-0.65,0.0){$\vee$}\put(6.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(0.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(5.0,-2.0){\tiny c}\put(7.0,5.0){\tiny d}\put(-5.0,8.0){\tiny e}\end{picture}=\begin{picture}(-5.0,-1.0)\put(3.0,5.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(-0.65,5.0){$\vee$}\put(6.0,12.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(0.0,12.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(3.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(3.0,0.0){\line{0}{1}{5.0}}\put(6.0,-3.0){\tiny ac}\put(6.0,4.0){\tiny b}\put(9.0,12.0){\tiny d}\put(-5.0,12.0){\tiny e}\end{picture}+\frac{1}{2}\big(\begin{picture}(-5.0,-1.0)\put(3.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(-0.65,0.0){$\vee$}\put(6.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(0.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(0.0,14.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(0.0,7.0){\line{0}{1}{7.0}}\put(5.0,-2.0){\tiny ac}\put(9.0,5.0){\tiny e}\put(-5.0,5.0){\tiny d}\put(-5.0,12.0){\tiny b}\end{picture}+\begin{picture}(-5.0,-1.0)\put(3.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(-0.65,0.0){$\vee$}\put(6.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(0.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(0.0,14.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(0.0,7.0){\line{0}{1}{7.0}}\put(5.0,-2.0){\tiny ac}\put(9.0,5.0){\tiny d}\put(-5.0,5.0){\tiny e}\put(-5.0,12.0){\tiny b}\end{picture}\big)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\big(\begin{picture}(-5.0,-1.0)\put(3.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(-0.65,0.0){$\vee$}\put(6.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(0.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(5.0,-2.0){\tiny ac}\put(7.0,5.0){\tiny bd}\put(-5.0,8.0){\tiny e}\end{picture}+\begin{picture}(-5.0,-1.0)\put(3.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(-0.65,0.0){$\vee$}\put(6.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(0.0,7.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(5.0,-2.0){\tiny ac}\put(7.0,5.0){\tiny be}\put(-5.0,8.0){\tiny d}\end{picture}\big).

The main result of this Subsection is then

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
A
be a unitary commutative algebra over
\mathbb{K}
and let
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
. Then
({\mathcal{F}}_{A},\diamond_{\lambda},B_{+}^{1_{A}})
is a Rota-Baxter algebra of weight
\lambda
. Furthermore, for any commutative associative Rota-Baxter algebra
(R,P)
of weight
\lambda
and any algebra homomorphism
\varphi:A\to R
there is a Rota-Baxter algebra homomorphism
\bar{\varphi}:{\mathcal{F}}^{+}_{A}\to R
such that the following diagram commutes

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{({\mathcal{F}}^{+}_{A},B_{+}^{1_{A}})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{(R,P)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces j_{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bar{\varphi}}

(11)

where
j_{A}:A\to{\mathcal{T}}_{A}
is the natural embedding.

Furthermore,
\bar{\varphi}
is the unique such Rota-Baxter algebra morphism with this property which is also a morphism for the concatenation product of trees.

Proof.

Let us first prove that
({\mathcal{F}}^{+}_{A},\diamond_{\lambda},B_{+}^{1_{A}})
is a Rota-Baxter algebra of weight
\lambda
. Let
f=T_{1}\cdots T_{k}
and
f^{\prime}=t_{1}\cdots t_{n}
be two forests. Since the
T_{i}
and
t_{j}
are non empty we can write
T_{i}=B_{+}^{a_{i}}(f_{i})
and
t_{j}=B_{+}^{a^{\prime}_{j}}(f^{\prime}_{j})
. With a small abuse of notation we also write
\psi_{i}(f)=T_{1}\cdots\hat{T_{i}}\cdots T_{k}
and
\psi_{j}(f^{\prime})=t_{1}\cdots\hat{t_{j}}\cdots t_{k}
(where the hat means that the tree is omitted). Then

\displaystyle B_{+}^{1_{A}}(f)\diamond_{\lambda}B_{+}^{1_{A}}(f^{\prime})

\displaystyle=B_{+}^{1_{A}}(f\ast_{\lambda}f^{\prime})

\displaystyle=B_{+}^{1_{A}}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}B_{+}^{a_{i}}(f_{i}\ast_{\lambda}f^{\prime})\psi_{i}(f)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{+}^{a^{\prime}_{j}}(f\ast_{\lambda}f^{\prime}_{j})\psi_{j}(f^{\prime})+\lambda f\diamond_{\lambda}f^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=B_{+}^{1_{A}}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(T_{i}\diamond_{\lambda}B_{+}^{1_{A}}(f^{\prime})\right)\psi_{i}(f)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(B_{+}^{1_{A}}(f)\diamond_{\lambda}t_{j}\right)\psi_{j}(f^{\prime})+\lambda f\diamond_{\lambda}f^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=B_{+}^{1_{A}}\left(f\diamond_{\lambda}B_{+}^{1_{A}}(f^{\prime})+B_{+}^{1_{A}}(f)\diamond_{\lambda}f^{\prime}+\lambda f\diamond_{\lambda}f^{\prime}\right).

Thus
({\mathcal{F}}^{+}_{A},\diamond_{\lambda},B_{+}^{1_{A}})
is a Rota-Baxter algebra of weight
\lambda
as claimed.

To show the property
11
, let
(R,P)
be a Rota-Baxter algebra of weight
\lambda
and
\varphi:A\to R
be an algebra homomorphism. Define
\bar{\varphi}:(F_{A},B_{+}^{1_{A}})\to(R,P)
by induction on
|F|
.
When
n=1
we have
F=\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny a}\end{picture}
and

\bar{\varphi}(\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny a}\end{picture})=\bar{\varphi}(j_{A}(a)):=\varphi(a).

Assume now that for
N\geq 2
fixed,
\bar{\varphi}(f)
has been defined for all forest such that
|f|<N
and let
|F|=N.
We have two cases

•

If
F=B_{+}^{a}(F^{\prime})
with
|F^{\prime}|=N-1
we set

\bar{\varphi}(F)=\bar{\varphi}\left(B_{+}^{a}(F^{\prime})\right):=\varphi(a)P(\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime})).

•

If
F=T_{1}\dots T_{n}
with
n\geq 2
we set

\bar{\varphi}(F):=\bar{\varphi}(T_{1})\dots\bar{\varphi}(T_{n}).

Let us now show that
\bar{\varphi}
is indeed a Rota-Baxter homomorphism. For any
F\in{\mathcal{F}}_{A}^{+}
we have

\bar{\varphi}\left(B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F)\right)=\varphi(1_{A})P\left(\bar{\varphi(F)}\right)=P\left(\bar{\varphi(F)}\right)

where we have used the definition of
\bar{\varphi}
on trees and the fact that
\varphi
is an algebra morphism and therefore
\varphi(1_{A})=1_{R}
. Thus
\bar{\varphi}\circ B_{+}^{1_{A}}=P\circ\bar{\varphi}
as expected.

We should now show that
\bar{\varphi}
is an algebra morphism. For any non-empty forests
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
we prove
\bar{\varphi}(F_{1}\diamond F_{2})=\bar{\varphi}(F_{1})\bar{\varphi}(F_{2})
by induction on
|F_{1}|+|F_{2}|
. When
|F_{1}|+|F_{2}|=2
then
F_{1}=\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny a}\end{picture}
and
F_{2}=\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny b}\end{picture}
then

\bar{\varphi}(\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny a}\end{picture}\diamond_{\lambda}\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny b}\end{picture})=\bar{\varphi}(\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny ab}\end{picture})=\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)=\bar{\varphi}(\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny a}\end{picture})\bar{\varphi}(\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny b}\end{picture})

where we have used the definition of
\bar{\varphi}
and the fact that
\varphi
is an algebra morphism.

Now assume that for
N\geq 3
the result holds for
|F_{1}|+|F_{2}|<N
and let
F_{1},F_{2}
be non-empty forests such that
|F_{1}|+|F_{2}|=N.
We have two cases to consider:

•

If
F_{1}=T_{1}\dots T_{k}
and
F_{2}=t_{1}\cdots t_{n}
we have

\displaystyle\bar{\varphi}(F_{1}\diamond_{\lambda}F_{2})

\displaystyle=\bar{\varphi}\left(\frac{1}{kn}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big((T_{i}\diamond_{\lambda}t_{j})T_{1}\dots\widehat{T_{i}}\dots T_{k}t_{1}\dots\widehat{t_{j}}\dots t_{n}\big)\right)\quad\text{by definition of }\diamond_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kn}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bar{\varphi}(T_{i}\diamond_{\lambda}t_{j})\bar{\varphi}(T_{1})\dots\widehat{\bar{\varphi}(T_{i})}\dots\bar{\varphi}(T_{k})\bar{\varphi}(t_{1})\dots\widehat{\bar{\varphi}(t_{j})}\dots\bar{\varphi}(t_{n})\quad\text{by definition of }\bar{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{kn}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bar{\varphi}(T_{1})\dots\bar{\varphi}(T_{k})\bar{\varphi}(t_{1})\dots\bar{\varphi}(t_{n})\quad\text{by the induction the hypothesis}

\displaystyle=\bar{\varphi}(F_{1})\bar{\varphi}(F_{2})\quad\text{by definition of }\bar{\varphi}.

•

If however
F_{1}=B_{+}^{a}(F^{\prime}_{1})
and
F_{2}=B_{+}^{b}(F^{\prime}_{2})
, using the definitions of
\diamond_{\lambda}
and
\ast_{\lambda}
we can write

\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny ab}\end{picture}\diamond_{\lambda}\left(B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{1})\diamond_{\lambda}B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{2})\right)=\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny ab}\end{picture}\diamond_{\lambda}\left(B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F_{1}^{\prime}\ast_{\lambda}F_{2}^{\prime})\right)=B_{+}^{ab}(F_{1}^{\prime}\ast_{\lambda}F_{2}^{\prime})=F_{1}\diamond_{\lambda}F_{2}.

We then have

\displaystyle\bar{\varphi}(F_{1}\diamond_{\lambda}F_{2})

\displaystyle=\bar{\varphi}\Big(\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny ab}\end{picture}\diamond_{\lambda}\left(B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{1})\diamond_{\lambda}B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{2})\right)\Big)

\displaystyle=\bar{\varphi}\Big(\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny ab}\end{picture}\diamond_{\lambda}B_{+}^{1_{A}}\left(F^{\prime}_{1}\diamond_{\lambda}B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{2})+B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{1})\diamond_{\lambda}F^{\prime}_{2}+\lambda F^{\prime}_{1}\diamond_{\lambda}F^{\prime}_{2}\right)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt\llcorner\text{since }B^{1_{A}}_{+}\text{ is a RBO of weight }\lambda

\displaystyle=\bar{\varphi}\Big(B_{+}^{ab}\left(F^{\prime}_{1}\diamond_{\lambda}B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{2})+B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{1})\diamond_{\lambda}F^{\prime}_{2}+\lambda F^{\prime}_{1}\diamond_{\lambda}F^{\prime}_{2}\right)\Big)

\displaystyle=\varphi(ab)\bar{\varphi}\Big(B_{+}^{1_{A}}\left(F^{\prime}_{1}\diamond_{\lambda}B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{2})+B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{1})\diamond_{\lambda}F^{\prime}_{2}+\lambda F^{\prime}_{1}\diamond_{\lambda}F^{\prime}_{2}\right)\Big)\quad\text{by definition of }\bar{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\varphi(ab)P\Big(\bar{\varphi}\left(F^{\prime}_{1}\diamond_{\lambda}B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{2})+B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{1})\diamond_{\lambda}F^{\prime}_{2}+\lambda F^{\prime}_{1}\diamond_{\lambda}F^{\prime}_{2}\right)\Big)\quad\text{since }\bar{\varphi}\circ B_{+}^{1_{A}}=P\circ\bar{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\varphi(ab)P\Big(\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{1})\bar{\varphi}\left(B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{2})\right)+\bar{\varphi}\left(B_{+}^{1_{A}}(F^{\prime}_{1})\right)\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{2})+\lambda\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{1})\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt\llcorner\text{by the induction hypothesis}

\displaystyle=\varphi(ab)P\Big(\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{1})P\left(\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{2})\right)+P\left(\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{1})\right)\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{2})+\lambda\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{1})\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{2})\Big)\quad\text{since }\bar{\varphi}\circ B_{+}^{1_{A}}=P\circ\bar{\varphi}

\displaystyle=\varphi(ab)P\Big(\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{1})\Big)P\Big(\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{2})\Big)\quad\text{since }P\text{ is a RBO of weight }\lambda

\displaystyle=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)P\Big(\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{1})\Big)P\Big(\bar{\varphi}(F^{\prime}_{2})\Big)\quad\text{since }\varphi\text{ is an algebra morphism}

\displaystyle=\bar{\varphi}(F_{1})\bar{\varphi}(F_{2})

by definition of
\bar{\varphi}
and commutativity of
R
.

Therefore we
\bar{\varphi}
is an algebra morphism.

Finally, notice that by construction,
\bar{\varphi}
is an algebra morphism for the concatenation product of trees. Let us show by contradiction that it is the only morphism for this product that makes Diagram
11
commutes. Let
\psi:(\cal{F}^{+}_{A},B_{+}^{1_{A}})\to(R,P)
be a Rota-Baxter algebra morphism such that Diagram
11
and that is a homomorphism for the concatenation of trees and suppose
\psi\neq\bar{\varphi}
.

So it exists
f\in{\mathcal{F}}^{+}_{A}
such that
\psi(f)\neq\bar{\varphi}(f)
. Choose such an
f
with the less possible vertices and set
N:=|f|
. For any
F
with one vertex we have
F=\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny a}\end{picture}
and

\psi(F)=\psi(j_{A}(a))=\varphi(a)=\bar{\varphi}(\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny a}\end{picture}).

Thus we must have
N=|f|\geq 2
. Then we have two cases:

\bullet

if
f=B_{+}^{a}(\tilde{f}),
we write
\tilde{f}=t_{1}\dots t_{n},
then
\psi(t_{i})=\bar{\varphi}(t_{i})
for
i=1,\cdots,n
and

\psi(f)=\psi(\begin{picture}(-2.0,-1.0)\put(0.0,0.0){\circle*{2.0}}\put(2.0,-2.0){\tiny a}\end{picture}\diamond B_{+}^{1_{A}}(\tilde{f}))=\varphi(a)P(\psi(\tilde{f}))=\varphi(a)P(\psi(t_{1}\dots t_{n}))\\
=\varphi(a)P(\psi(t_{1})\dots\psi(t_{n}))=\varphi(a)P(\bar{\varphi}(t_{1})\dots\bar{\varphi}(t_{n}))=\bar{\varphi}(f);

which is a contradiction.

\bullet

if
f=t_{1}\cdots t_{n}
then again
\psi(t_{i})=\bar{\varphi}(t_{i})
for
i=1,\cdots,n
and since
\psi
and
\bar{\varphi}
are both algebra morphisms for the concatenation of rooted trees we find

\psi(f)=\psi(t_{1})\cdots\psi(t_{n})=\bar{\varphi}(t_{1})\cdots\bar{\varphi}(t_{n})=\bar{\varphi}(f).

Again, this is a contradiction. Therefore
\psi=\bar{\varphi}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Notice that the property
11
cannot be stated directly as a universal property since we need
R
to ba associative (otherwise
\bar{\varphi}
is ill-defined) but
\diamond_{\lambda}
is not. Thus we cannot say that
({\mathcal{F}}_{A},\diamond_{\lambda},B_{+}^{1_{A}})
is freely generated by
A
as an associative RBA since it does not belong to this category.
