ASO-functionalized gold nanoparticles for gene silencing in drug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa: a proof-of-concept study
Authors: Sarah Najah AlJanabi, Maroua Gdoura-Ben Amor, Wathiq Abbas Al-Draghi, Hanen Sellami, Olfa Frikha-Gargouri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present work aims at evaluating a novel therapeutic technique against clinical samples of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a multiple drug resistant bacterium by integrating gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) with antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) (ASO-AuNPs) to target and silence the blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes. The objectives were to evaluate the ability of ASO-AuNPs to silence resistance genes and reduce their expression to combat antibiotic resistance. Seventy P. aeruginosa clinical isolates collected from Iraqi hospitals were isolated, identified and their antibiotic resistance profiles were assessed. The blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes were detected by PCR. These genes were engineered through the use of ASO-AuNPs. Resistance to carbapenem and cephalosporin and quantitative blaVIM and blaCTX-M gene expression in P. aeruginosa were further studied before and after silencing. The blaVIM gene was found in 79.2% of carbapenem-resistant samples, while blaCTX-M was identified in 72.7% of cephalosporin-resistant samples. A significant reduction in antibiotic resistance was achieved following ASO-AuNPs application. Moreover, the gene silencing technique significantly decreased the expression of blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes. The mean fold change of the CTX-M gene was 15.92 before and 1.36 after gene silencing, that of the blaVIM gene was 0.63 and 0.51, respectively. These findings provide a proof-of-concept for the use of ASO-functionalized gold nanoparticles as a potential strategy for targeting antibiotic resistance genes in P. aeruginosa, with potential applications in managing resistant infections in healthcare settings.Keywords: Multi-drug resistant, Gold nanoparticles, blaVIM, blaCTX-M, Gene silencingSubject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Antibiotic resistance has become a critical public health issue, with the World Health Organization warning those common infections could become deadly because antibiotics no longer work effectively. One of the most troublesome pathogens contributing to this problem is Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a Gram-negative bacterium that developed substantial resistance to multiple antibiotics. This resistance is especially concerning in healthcare settings, where P. aeruginosa can cause life-threatening infections like pneumonia, urinary tract infections, wound infections, and sepsis, particularly among patients with a weakened immune system1. The bacterium is highly adaptable, surviving under hostile conditions, acquiring resistance genes, and forming biofilms that make it very difficult to treat2. Infections with P. aeruginosa in hospital settings, especially in intensive care units and burn wards, are frequently challenging to treat, often leading to longer hospital stays and higher mortality rates3. The acquisition of resistance genes such as blaVIM and blaCTX-M enables the bacterium to withstand carbapenems and third-generation cephalosporins, respectively, which are essential antibiotic classes for the treatment of severe infections. These resistance genes can spread rapidly within bacterial populations, limiting treatment options and presenting a serious challenge to healthcare facilities worldwide4,5. The spread of these genes has been increasing globally, especially in areas with high antibiotic use, and underscores the urgency of developing new treatment strategies6. Iraq, for example, has seen a notable rise in antibiotic-resistant infections in its major hospitals due to frequent antibiotic misuse, insufficient infection control, and a healthcare system strained by prolonged conflict7. With limited resources, outdated medical facilities, and a lack of training in antimicrobial stewardship, healthcare settings in Iraq have become particularly vulnerable to outbreaks of resistant P. aeruginosa infections8.

Studies in several Iraqi hospitals reveal that P. aeruginosa strains carrying blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes are common9, making infections very difficult to treat in intensive care units and burn units. In these high-risk environments, patients are more susceptible to secondary infections, and these resistant bacteria increase the risk of outbreaks within hospital wards10. P. aeruginosa infections are challenging due to the ability of the isolates to form biofilms which are clusters of bacteria surrounded by a self-produced protective layer. These biofilms protect the bacteria from immune responses and antibiotics, further complicating treatment efforts11. Biofilms commonly form on medical devices like catheters and ventilators, creating chronic infections in vulnerable patients, such as those with cystic fibrosis or severe burns. Biofilm-protected bacteria are often dormant, which makes them less susceptible to fast-acting antibiotics, adding another layer of difficulty to effective treatment12. Biofilms also enhance the transfer of resistance genes, such as blaVIM and blaCTX-M, between bacteria, allowing these genes to spread more efficiently through bacterial communities. For this reason, strategies that disrupt biofilms, in addition to combating free-living bacteria, are essential for controlling these infections. With the limited availability of effective treatments for major diseases such as cancer, antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) use has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach. The discovery of ASO as a powerful therapeutic tool has driven clinical research into various diseases and applications. ASO are chemical analogues of short single-stranded nucleic acid sequences modified to form stable oligomers. The sequence of these molecules is complementary to their target messenger RNA (mRNA). These oligonucleotides allow the modification of gene expression by binding to its complementary mRNA and inhibiting its translation into protein through the degradation of the ASO/RNA duplex and/or through steric blockage13. However, delivering the desired concentration of ASO to bacterial cells presents challenges which significantly hinders their application14. Among the various delivery approaches, gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) have gained particular attention due to their biocompatibility, ability to protect ASO from degradation, and tunable shape, size, and optical properties. Thus, researchers have explored the use of AuNPs to enhance ASO delivery to bacterial cells15. AuNPs improved the delivery of ASO and help it enter bacterial cells, providing a targeted approach for inhibiting resistance genes16. Despite their potential, research on ASO-AuNPs for bacterial infections remains in its early stages. Few studies have investigated the impact of ASO-AuNPs on the expression of resistance genes, highlighting the need for further exploration in this field. Therefore, this study aims to characterize the antibiotic resistance profiles of Iraqi P. aeruginosa isolates and evaluate, as a proof-of-concept, the ability of ASO-AuNP complexes to silence the key resistance genes blaVIM and blaCTX-M in the context of multidrug-resistant infections and rising antibiotic resistance.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and bacterial identification

From September 2021 until April 2022, a total of 150 clinical samples were collected from four tertiary hospitals in Iraq, located in Baghdad, Basra, Erbil, and Mosul. Samples were collected using appropriate sterile swabs and containers from patients with various infections, including respiratory tract infections, urinary tract infections, burn infections, and wound infections. The primary sources of clinical specimens included blood, urine, wound swabs, and sputum. The isolates were initially identified as P. aeruginosa using conventional microbiological methods, including Gram staining, oxidase and catalase tests, and pigment production. Gram-negative rod-shaped bacteria that tested positive for oxidase and catalase and produced green pigment (pyocyanin) on cetrimide agar were provisionally identified as P. aeruginosa. The identity of the isolates was confirmed with the VITEK−2 automated system (bioMérieux) using the VITEK−2 GN Card to guarantee accurate bacterial identification using detailed biochemical profiles. P. aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 control strain was used to ensure the quality of the identification process. The identification of the isolates followed the guidelines established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI).

Antibiotic resistance testing

The VITEK−2 automated system using the AST-N222 Card was used to evaluate the antibiotic resistance of P. aeruginosa strains. Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations (MICs) were determined for various antibiotics from bioMérieux, France. The European Committee for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing’s guidelines were used as a basis for the antibiotic susceptibility testing. A variety of antimicrobial agents, including penicillin and beta-lactamase inhibitors (e.g., piperacillin, ticarcillin, and ticarcillin/clavulanic acid), aminoglycosides (e.g., amikacin, gentamicin, amikacin, and tobramycin), fluoroquinolones (e.g., ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin), cephalosporins (e.g., ceftazidime and cefepime), polymyxins (colistin), and carbapenems (meropenem, imipenem, and meropenem) was investigated. The selection of antibiotics was determined according to their significance in treating P. aeruginosa infections, particularly in intensive care units and burn units. The MIC values were established using antibiotic susceptibility testing following CLSI guidelines, using broth microdilution techniques. Based on their MIC values, the isolates were categorized as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant using the CLSI breakpoints. Isolates exhibiting resistance to three classes of antibiotics were classified as multidrug-resistant (MDR), those resistant to all but one or two antibiotic classes were defined as extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and isolates resistant to all tested antibiotics were categorized as pan-drug-resistant (PDR). Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 reference strains were used as quality control strains to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the MIC testing.

Detection of blaVIM and blaCTX-M P. aeruginosa resistance genes by conventional PCR

DNA was extracted from P. aeruginosa isolates using the PrestoTM Mini gDNA Bacterial Kit (Geneaid, Taiwan) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The Master Mix AccuPower® PCR PreMix (Pioneer, Republic of Korea) was used to amplify the resistance genes in the PCR experiments. Specific primers designed using Primer3Plus software were used to amplify the blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes (Table 1). The 20 µL PCR mixture included 10 µL of Master Mix (2x), 5 µL of Nuclease Free Water, 3 µL of DNA (50 ng), and 1 µL of each of the Forward and Reverse primers (10 pmol/µL for each primer). The PCR was conducted in a thermal cycler with the following program: a denaturation step for 5 min at 95 °C, followed by 30 cycles of a denaturation step at 95 °C for 30 s, an annealing step at 57 °C and 59 °C for 30 s, respectively for blaVIM and blaCTX-M, an extension step at 72 °C for 45 s, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 7 min. Positive control strains containing blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes and a negative control containing nuclease-free water instead of template DNA were included in each PCR to confirm the amplification accuracy. Gel electrophoresis on 1.5% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide (Himedia, India) was used to evaluate the PCR results. DNA bands were viewed under UV light using a GelDoc system (Bio-Rad, USA). The 100 bp DNA ladder (TransGen, China) was used to determine the size of the amplified DNA fragments.

Gene silencing using ASO-AuNPs

Creation and design of ASO

The design and synthesis of the ASO molecules was performed by the Macrogen Company (USA). Specific ASO sequences that target specifically the blaVIM and blaCTX-M resistance genes were designed to complement the 5’ ends of the mRNA of these genes (Table 1).

Antimicrobial efficacy of gold nanoparticles against P. aeruginosa

This study utilized previously published protocol17 with minor modifications to determine the MIC. Gold nanoparticles (5–20 nm, Vira carbon nanomaterials, Iran) were serially diluted (1:2 ratio) in nutrient broth with concentrations varying from 100 to 0.18 ppm. 100 µL of gold nanoparticle suspension, 100 µL of diluted P. aeruginosa culture (104 CFU/mL), and 20 µL of Resazurin dye (Sigma, USA) (0.15 mg/mL) were introduced into each well of the microtiter plate. This latter was then incubated overnight at 37 °C. The antibacterial efficacy of gold nanoparticles on the bacteria was visually assessed by noting the color transition from pink (viable) to blue (non-viable).

The process of splicing ASO onto gold nanoparticles

An initial solution of 100 ppm AuNPs was made by combining 3 mL of AuNPs with 1 mL of 3 M NaCl. Using varying concentrations of NaCl (0.5–3 M), this mixture was serially diluted. To help the ASO bind and load onto the surface of the AuNPs, 4 ml of AuNPs-NaCl solution was mixed with 20 µL of ASO (100 pmol/L) and incubated at room temperature for 4 h. After that, 200 µL of varying NaCl concentrations (0.5–3 M) were added every 20 min, beginning with the lowest up to the maximum. The ASO-AuNPs complexes were purified in PBS by spinning at 12,000 rpm for 20 min to remove any unbound ASO. It should be noted that quantitative characterization of the ASO-AuNP conjugates, including loading efficiency, post-functionalization particle size distribution, and zeta potential measurements, was not performed in this study due to equipment limitations. Conjugation was confirmed qualitatively by gel electrophoresis with red dye staining. Furthermore, direct visualization of bacterial uptake of ASO-AuNP conjugates through fluorescence labeling or TEM imaging was not conducted, and intracellular delivery is therefore inferred from the observed functional outcomes.

Transfection of ASO-AuNPs complexes into bacterial cells for silencing blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes

For silencing the blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes, conjugated ASO was introduced to bacterial isolates. For that, P. aeruginosa grown on cetrimide agar plates were used to prepare bacterial suspensions in Brain Heart Infusion Broth (BHIB), which were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. 200 µL aliquot of the bacterial suspension, adjusted to 0.5 McFarland, was inoculated into the ASO-AuNPs solution. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. These conditions were identified as optimal for effective gene silencing. Bacteria were then isolated and inoculated on Mueller-Hinton agar media. Direct visualization of bacterial uptake of ASO-AuNP conjugates through fluorescence labeling or TEM imaging was not conducted, and intracellular delivery is therefore inferred from the observed functional outcomes.

Effect of gene silencing on antibiotic resistance patterns

In order to test the antibiotic susceptibility of the bacteria, they were isolated after transfection and then inoculated onto Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA). The disk diffusion method was used to determine antibiotic susceptibility in accordance with the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) standards. To determine whether bacteria were resistant, we examined carbapenems (imipenem and meropenem, 10 µg/tablet) and cephalosporins (cefepime and ceftazidime, 30 µg/tablet). The results were interpreted using the CLSI breakpoints based on the appearance of inhibition zone after 18–24 h of incubation at 37 °C.

Effect of gene silencing on blaVIM and blaCTX-M gene expression by real-time reverse transcription (RT)-PCR

The effectiveness of gene silencing was assessed by measuring the levels of blaVIM and blaCTX-M expression before and after being treated with ASO-AuNPs through quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR). RNA was extracted using the TransZol Up Plus RNA purification Kit (TransGen, China), and cDNA was synthesized using the ProtoScript® II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (NEB, USA). The ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR system was utilized to conduct qPCR using the SYBR Green GoTaq® qPCR master mix (Promega, USA) and the recA housekeeping gene as a reference. The PCR reaction mixture consisted of a total of 20 µL, comprising 5 µL qPCR Master Mix 2X, 1 µl of each primer (10 µM), 3 µL of cDNA template (50 ng), and nuclease-free water (qsp 20 µL). The primers employed in the RT-qPCR experiment for the blaCTX-M and blaVIM genes are shown in Table 1. The thermocycler settings were: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 58 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s. Melt curve analysis was performed at the end of the qPCR run by gradually increasing the temperature from 60 °C to 94 °C at 0.5 °C increments while continuously measuring fluorescence. The ΔΔCt method was used to determine the fold change in gene expression compared to untreated controls18. Normalization was carried out using the recA as the internal control gene.

Statistical methods

The SPSS version 24.0 (SPSS, Inc.) was used to analyze the data. In order to compare percentages, the Chi-square test was used. Continuous variables, such as MIC values and gene expression levels, were evaluated using either Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA, respectively. A p-value lower than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Regarding gender distribution, the percentage of males and females varied according to the infection source. The wound and respiratory tract infections were in higher proportions in male patients, while burns and otitis media were more prevalent in females.

Detection of blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes

In view of the substantial resistance detected against a broad spectrum of antibiotics, particularly carbapenems and cephalosporins, we hypothesized that this phenotype might be related to the presence of specific β-lactamase-encoding genes. For that, we aimed to investigate the contribution of the blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes, which are widely recognized as major contributors of resistance to carbapenems and extended-spectrum cephalosporins, respectively. The existence of blaVIM and blaCTX-M was verified in a significant proportion of the P. aeruginosa isolates through the PCR analysis yielding DNA fragments of 201 and 487 bp, respectively. In fact, blaCTX-M gene was detected in 72.9% (51/70) of the isolates, while blaVIM was detected in 64.3% (45/70) (Table 4). Among the carbapenem-resistant isolates, 79.2% (38/48) were found to be positive for blaVIM and 87.5% (42/48) were positive for blaCTX-M. However, the blaCTX-M gene was present in 72.7% (16/22) of cephalosporin-resistant isolates, while 77.3% (17/22) carried the blaVIM gene. A statistically significant differences were observed between carbapenem resistance and blaVIM (p = 0.0004) as well as with the blaCTX-M gene (p = 0.0002), and significant differences were also noted between the two genes (p = 0.0013).

Effect of gold nanoparticles on P. aeruginosa isolates

The antibacterial activity of AuNPs against P. aeruginosa isolates was evaluated to determine whether any observed effect was attributable to the AuNPs or to the ASO-AuNPs targeting the blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes. In fact, AuNPs alone were used as a control to verify the absence of intrinsic antibacterial activity at the tested concentrations. In contrast, ASO-AuNPs conjugates were employed to exhibit gene silencing in resistant isolates. This approach allowed us to attribute changes in antibiotic susceptibility specifically to the suppression of β-lactamase gene expression rather than to a nonspecific nanoparticle effect. The test performed on microtiter plate using various AuNP concentrations yielded wells showing pink color (Data not shown) indicating that these AuNPs at the tested concentrations do not have any antibacterial effect. The highest concentration of AuNPs (100 ppm) was used for the conjugation process of ASO onto AuNPs. Due to experimental constraints, only AuNPs alone were included as a control to evaluate nanoparticle-associated antibacterial effects. Additional controls, including ASO-only and scrambled ASO treatments, were not performed in this study.

Gene silencing through ASO-AuNPs

By the use of gene silencing mediated by ASO-AuNPs conjugates, it is expected that the expression of resistance genes will be significantly downregulated, leading to a reduction in β-lactamase activity and a consequent restoration of bacterial susceptibility to conventional antibiotics. Five multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates were selected as representative strains for preliminary functional validation of ASO–AuNP-mediated gene silencing, and were subsequently used to investigate antibiotic resistance patterns and the expression of blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes following gene silencing.

Effect of gene silencing on antibiotic resistance patterns

P. aeruginosa isolates were compared before and after gene silencing regarding the resistance to carbapenems and cephalosporins. Carbapenem- and cephalosporin-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates cultivated on agar plates showed the lack of inhibitory zones surrounding the antibiotic disk before gene silencing (Fig. 1). Conversely, the same isolates subjected to gene silencing demonstrated an expanded inhibition zone and appeared susceptible relative to standard MIC values as per CLSI criteria.

Effect of gene silencing on the expression of blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes

P. aeruginosa isolates were compared before and after gene silencing regarding the expression of the blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes. The application of ASO-AuNPs targeting these resistance genes resulted in a significant reduction in gene expression as shown in Fig. 2. A substantial decrease was observed particularly in the expression level of the blaCTX-M gene, for which the mean fold change decreased from 15.92 before treatment to 1.36 after treatment. The blaVIM gene showed a more modest but statistically significant reduction, with the mean fold change decreasing from 0.63 to 0.51 following treatment (p < 0.05). It should be noted that fold change values below 1.0 obtained by the ΔΔCt method indicate that the target gene is expressed at a lower level than the recA reference gene under baseline conditions, which is a biologically valid finding and does not reflect a normalization error. The decrease in blaVIM expression, although modest in absolute terms, is consistent with the observed phenotypic restoration of carbapenem susceptibility following ASO-AuNPs treatment, reinforcing the biological relevance of this result. However, it should be acknowledged that these gene expression data are derived from five clinical isolates and should therefore be interpreted with caution. While the observed changes in expression of both genes were statistically significant (p < 0.05) and corroborated by the phenotypic susceptibility data presented above, the limited sample size precludes firm conclusions regarding the broader therapeutic significance of ASO-AuNP-mediated gene silencing. Collectively, these findings support a preliminary proof-of-concept for ASO-AuNP-mediated gene silencing in multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa, and future studies with larger, independent clinical cohorts are warranted to establish robust and generalizable conclusions.

Discussion

P. aeruginosa has become an important cause of nosocomial infections and antibiotic resistance. It is one of the most prevalent pathogens in hospital environments, causing more than 50% of healthcare-acquired infections19. Infections caused by P. aeruginosa can be life-threatening when inadequate therapy is employed, particularly when MDR strains are involved. In fact, this microorganism is capable of rapidly developing resistance to antibiotics. Even though new antimicrobial drugs have been developed, the mortality rates due to P. aeruginosa remain high, in the range of 20–60%20. Since treating these infections is very difficult and has raised concerns such as reduced treatment choices and increased costs and mortality, it is currently considered a problem in health centers. One of the causes of antibiotic resistance is carbapenemase enzymes, which can be easily transferred to bacteria through the transport by integrons21. So, identifying these types of resistance and treating these patients as soon as possible can help control the infection. Routine surveillance studies are necessary to monitor antibiotic resistance trends in low-income countries where antibiotic selection pressure is likely to be high. Therefore, it was attempted to isolate these bacteria in Iraqi hospitals. 70 P. aeruginosa isolates were successfully isolated from 150 clinical samples yielding a percentage of isolation of 47% (70/150). The highest prevalence was observed in wound samples (67.7%) followed by urine samples (60.0%), and burn samples (47.4%). The high prevalence in urinary tract infections (UTIs) aligns with other findings22, who reported a similar prevalence in UTIs, particularly in hospitalized patients with indwelling catheters. Much lower percentages of isolation of P. aeruginosa were reported in the literature ranging from 13.2 to 32.8%23–27. The significant presence of P. aeruginosa in wound and burn samples emphasizes its role in nosocomial infections. These infections are often associated with surgical sites and burn units, where the bacterium can easily colonize and form biofilms, leading to persistent infections. The prevalence of P. aeruginosa in urinary tract infections (UTIs), burns, and wounds underscores its significant role in hospital-acquired infections. A study on burn wound infections in Isfahan, Iran, reported P. aeruginosa as the most prevalent isolates (48.7%) among Gram-negative bacteria, demonstrating high antibiotic resistance, particularly to ceftazidime and imipenem28. Another investigation in Baghdad hospitals highlighted P. aeruginosa in 14.1% of burns and wounds, often associated with multidrug resistance29. The study also found that P. aeruginosa isolates frequently colonized burn wounds due to their ability to form biofilms, complicating treatment and increasing morbidity.

The antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed that the resistance was highest for imipenem (71.4%), colistin (62.9%), ticarcillin (61.4%), and piperacillin (58.6%). These findings reflect a broader global trend of increasing resistance to carbapenems and beta-lactams, as documented previously30. In fact, high resistance to ticarcillin, ciprofloxacin, and piperacillin were reported30. The resistance to carbapenems, particularly imipenem and meropenem, is of particular concern. Carbapenems are often considered the last line of defense against MDR bacterial infections. The observed resistance levels suggest that P. aeruginosa has developed significant mechanisms to evade these critical antibiotics, complicating treatment options for infected patients. Our results align with those of other studies31 who reported a similar rise in carbapenem resistance among clinical isolates of P. aeruginosa. The results of resistance of 81 isolates of P. aeruginosa recovered from various clinical samples from hospitalized patients in Diyala hospitals in Iraq were reported and were higher than 50% except for imipenem (11.11%) and meropenem (23.45%)32. In that study, antibiotic susceptibility testing of the P. aeruginosa isolates showed that 30.8% of the isolates were MDR and that 23.45% of P. aeruginosa isolates were resistant to at least one of carbapenems32. Our results regarding imipenem resistance (68.57%) were in the range of those reported in China33 and in Egypt34 that were 73.3, and 78.3%, respectively. Much lower resistance rates of imipenem were reported in Assiut, Egypt (36.5%)26, in Saudi Arabia (38.57%)35, and in Iran (46%)36. These differences in the resistance results to imipenem in the various studies might be related to different antibiotic therapy protocols and detection of antibiotic susceptibility methods. Interestingly, our results showed relatively higher sensitivity rates to ticarcillin/clavulanic acid (90.0%), piperacillin/tazobactam (88.6%), ceftazidime (87.1%), cefepime (84.3%), amikacin (81.4%), indicating that these antibiotics remain effective against a subset of P. aeruginosa isolates. However, the potential for emerging resistance to these antibiotics cannot be ignored, as evidenced by recent reports of increasing colistin resistance in clinical settings37. The blaVIM gene produces enzymes that can degrade various β-lactam antibiotics, such as carbapenems conferring thus resistance by rendering these compounds ineffective. On the other hand, the blaCTX-M gene blocks the ability of cephalosporins and penicillin to fight bacteria by breaking their β-lactam ring, leading to resistance. Both genes are involved in the development of drug-resistant P. aeruginosa strains, making the treatment of severe infections more challenging. Since we detected notable resistance to various antibiotics, including carbapenems and cephalosporins, we aimed to investigate the involvement of blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes as key contributors to this resistance. The presence of blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes was found in a substantial proportion of the P. aeruginosa isolates. In fact, blaCTX-M gene was detected in 72.9% (51/70) of the isolates, while blaVIM was detected in 64.3% (45/70). The high prevalence of these genes in clinical isolates is consistent with the global trends38. These findings align with the global spread of extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) and carbapenemases, particularly in healthcare-associated infections. The detection of blaCTX-M in 72.9% of the isolates is particularly concerning, as it suggests that ESBL production is becoming increasingly common in P. aeruginosa, a trend that has also been observed in other Enterobacteriaceae30. The widespread dissemination of blaCTX-M and blaVIM genes among P. aeruginosa isolates poses a significant challenge to infection control. These genes are often carried on mobile genetic elements such as plasmids, which can facilitate their horizontal transfer between bacterial species. The simultaneous presence of blaCTX-M and blaVIM genes in P. aeruginosa substantially enhances antibiotic resistance in clinical environments. Multiplex PCR screening of 200 isolates revealed the presence of blaCTX-M and blaVIM among various resistance genes, highlighting the significance of molecular detection in elucidating resistance patterns39. The presence of blaCTX-M and blaVIM in colistin-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates from Iran was associated with multidrug resistance, attributed to the overexpression of efflux pump genes and the presence of additional beta-lactamase genes40. A separate Brazilian study identified blaCTX-M−2 in 2.8–6.1% of isolates, frequently coexisting with blaVIM, albeit at a low prevalence, indicating geographical variability in gene distribution41. Among ICU patients receiving mechanical ventilation, blaCTX-M was detected in 13.2% of the isolates, while blaVIM was present in 15.3%, illustrating the complexity of resistance in nosocomial infections42. A study conducted in Iran identified blaCTX-M in 45.83% of isolates highlighting the notable regional prevalence of this resistance mechanism43. These findings emphasize the essential role of blaCTX-M and blaVIM in facilitating resistance, underscoring the necessity for global surveillance and focused infection control strategies to mitigate their proliferation. This would help limit the spread of resistance within healthcare settings, particularly in environments where antibiotic use is high.

ASO technologies were developed as an innovative strategy to overcome multidrug resistance. Antisense therapeutics either exhibit direct antibacterial activity or suppress resistance mechanisms. The wide range of nucleotide analog chemistries, combined with multiple modes of action and diverse bacterial uptake strategies, creates high possibilities for incorporating ASO into the antimicrobial arsenal. In the present study, we studied the effect ASO-AuNPs complexes on the antibiotic resistance pattern and the expression of blaCTX-M and blaVIM genes before and after treatment. The antibacterial activity of AuNPs against P. aeruginosa isolates was evaluated as a control and showed the absence of intrinsic antibacterial activity at the tested concentrations. While ASO-AuNPs conjugates were employed to exhibit gene silencing in resistant isolates. Our results indicated that the application of ASO-AuNPs on P. aeruginosa multidrug-resistant isolates resulted in reduced antibiotic resistance and decreased expression of the blaVIM and blaCTX-M genes. This indicated the positive effect of ASO-AuNPs on the antibiotic-resistant isolates of P. aeruginosa. Following gene silencing, a marked decrease in antibiotic resistance to carbapenems and cephalosporins of P. aeruginosa isolates was observed. The reduction in resistance to critical antibiotics such as imipenem, meropenem, cefepime, and ceftazidime is particularly encouraging. These antibiotics are often used as last-resort treatments for serious infections caused by MDR P. aeruginosa44. Research on ASO-AuNPs for bacterial infections is still in early stages. The incorporation of ASO into AuNPs demonstrates potential for tackling issues like antibiotic resistance and illness management via gene silencing. However, only limited studies were conducted on the effect ASO-AuNPs on the expression of resistant genes. Our results are consistent with previous studies that have demonstrated the potential of ASO-AuNPs in downregulating resistance genes in bacterial pathogens and reduction in antibiotic resistance following gene silencing in various bacterial species. Garza-Cardenas et al.45 provide proof-of-concept evidence that ASO-AuNPs can reestablish the efficacy of beta-lactam antibiotics against resistant bacterial strains. In another study, liposome-encapsulated ASOs, targeting the oprM gene in P. aeruginosa, considerably decreased the MICs of piperacillin, ciprofloxacin, and levofloxacin among other antibiotics. The antibiotic efficiency was attributed to the suppression of an efflux pump subunit encoded by oprM46. The silencing of the mexA gene in P. aeruginosa using single strand siRNA sequence resulted in the silencing of mexA gene expression and the decrease of antibiotic resistance percentage except for cefazolin and ticarcillin/clavulanic acid47. Finally, enhancement in susceptibility to multiple beta-lactams was shown by Meng et al.48 who used a liposome-based ASO delivery system targeting the mecA gene in methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Staphylococcus epidermidis strains.

Overall, the present findings should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept demonstrating the feasibility of ASO-functionalized nanoparticles for modulating antibiotic resistance gene expression, rather than as a fully validated therapeutic strategy.

Study limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the functional gene silencing experiments were conducted on only five representative isolates, which limits the statistical power and generalizability of the findings; future studies should expand this to a larger, more diverse set of clinical isolates. Second, while AuNPs alone were confirmed to have no intrinsic antibacterial activity, a key limitation of this study is the absence of sequence-specific controls, including ASO-only and scrambled ASO treatments. This limitation precludes definitive discrimination between sequence-specific antisense effects and potential nonspecific contributions arising from delivery or stress responses. These critical controls will be incorporated in follow-up experiments to rigorously validate the specificity of the observed effects. Third, physicochemical characterization of ASO-AuNPs was limited to qualitative gel electrophoresis; key parameters such as loading efficiency, hydrodynamic size, and zeta potential were not measured, and bacterial uptake was not directly visualized. Future studies should address these aspects using dynamic light scattering, zeta potential analysis, and fluorescence or electron microscopy.

Finally, gene expression analysis was restricted to the two target genes blaVIM and blaCTX-M. No broader transcriptomic profiling was performed to confirm the absence of off-target silencing effects on other resistance-associated gene families. This represents an important limitation of the current study, and comprehensive transcriptomic validation are planned for future work.

Conclusions

The present study provides a preliminary proof-of-concept in vitro demonstration that ASO-AuNP conjugates can silence key antibiotic resistance genes blaVIM and blaCTX-M in multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa, resulting in reduced resistance to carbapenems and cephalosporins. However, given the limited number of isolates used for functional validation (n = 5), these results should be interpreted with caution and cannot be extrapolated to broad therapeutic conclusions at this stage. Future studies with larger and more diverse clinical cohorts, appropriate controls including scrambled ASO sequences, and in vivo validation are necessary before the therapeutic potential of this approach can be fully established.
