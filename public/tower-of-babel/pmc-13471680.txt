Ferroptosis-driven immune remodeling in heart failure: the central role of macrophage reprogramming and impaired efferocytosis
Authors: Haoyuan Zheng, Yanxiao Liang, Yuan Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProgressive myocardial remodeling in heart failure (HF) is sustained by unresolved inflammation and defective tissue repair. Ferroptosis, characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a critical source of cardiomyocyte death in failing hearts. Importantly, ferroptotic cells release damage-associated molecular patterns and oxidized phospholipids that alter macrophage activation states and perpetuate inflammatory signaling. Under physiological conditions, macrophage-mediated efferocytosis ensures rapid clearance of dying cells and promotes inflammation resolution. In HF, however, excessive cell death burden, redox imbalance, and metabolic stress collectively impair efferocytic capacity. The resulting accumulation of apoptotic and ferroptotic debris reinforces inflammatory activation and extracellular matrix deposition, generating a vicious cycle of injury and fibrosis. Here, we synthesize evidence supporting a ferroptosis-centered immune remodeling loop and define efferocytosis as a regulatory checkpoint in disease progression. We also evaluate emerging therapeutic strategies aimed at interrupting this cycle through iron modulation, antioxidant delivery, metabolic reprogramming, and bioengineered targeting systems. Introduction

Heart failure (HF), a terminal syndrome arising from multiple etiologies and convergent pathological pathways, continues to impose a substantial global burden of morbidity and mortality, which is further exacerbated by population aging and the rising prevalence of metabolic comorbidities [1, 2]. Although neurohormonal inhibition, device-based therapies, and novel pharmacological combinations have substantially improved symptoms and outcomes in selected patient populations, the natural history of HF, characterized by recurrent decompensation, cumulative structural remodeling, and progressive functional decline, often remains difficult to fundamentally reverse [3–5]. These observations suggest that current interventions mainly target the core components of neurohumoral and hemodynamic dysregulation, whereas certain forms of cellular injury, immune regulatory processes, and their interactive mechanisms, which critically shape the trajectory of remodeling, remain insufficiently understood [6]. Accordingly, developing a mechanistic analytical framework linking cardiomyocyte injury, alterations in the immune microenvironment, and failed tissue repair has become an important direction for advancing HF therapy from symptom control and delayed progression toward stratified and precision-based approaches [6].

Within this context, the concept of regulated cell death (RCD) provides a useful framework for understanding persistent cardiomyocyte loss and the amplification of pathological injury [7]. Among these modalities, ferroptosis has attracted particular attention because it is characterized by the convergence of iron homeostasis dysregulation and lipid peroxidation, ultimately leading to collapse of antioxidant defense systems and uncontrolled membrane lipid damage [8]. Evidence from multiple models of myocardial injury indicates that activation of ferroptosis-related molecular axes frequently coincides with cardiomyocyte dysfunction, enhanced inflammatory responses, and progressive deterioration of cardiac function, while interventions targeting key nodes within this pathway have shown protective effects in several studies [9, 10]. At the same time, previous reviews have emphasized that the strength of evidence for ferroptosis varies across disease stages, experimental models, and human HF subgroups, and that its relative contribution to overall pathophysiology still requires more systematic integration and validation [11]. Therefore, ferroptosis is more appropriately viewed as a form of cell death that may be amplified under specific metabolic and oxidative stress conditions; its relative importance across HF phenotypes and its stable coupling with immune clearance programs remain key questions that require stratified validation.

When the perspective shifts from intracellular mechanisms in cardiomyocytes to the tissue level, the immune microenvironment often determines whether injury is effectively resolved and whether repair can be successfully initiated [6, 12]. Macrophages, owing to their lineage diversity, functional plasticity, and role in clearing dying cells, occupy a central position in maintaining heart homeostasis and in injury responses [12]. Studies of efferocytosis further indicate that dead-cell clearance is not merely a passive process of “debris removal,” but an active immune program that promotes inflammation resolution and transitions toward tissue repair; conversely, when efferocytic efficiency declines, the persistence of cellular remnants and inflammatory signals is frequently associated with fibrotic propensity and progressive structural remodeling [13]. Taken together, this perspective leads to a more specific and testable integrative question: whether ferroptosis-associated oxidized lipids and disturbances in iron homeostasis can, in certain contexts, disrupt the macrophage recognition–engulfment–metabolic processing cascade, thereby increasing the risk of insufficient efferocytosis and impaired inflammation resolution [14].

At the therapeutic level, advances in medical-engineering integration and nanomedicine provide new technical opportunities to interrogate and modulate these complex processes [15]. Compared with conventional drug delivery strategies, engineered delivery systems offer distinct advantages in targeting, microenvironmental responsiveness, and coordinated regulation across multiple nodes [15, 16]. On this basis, the present study adopts the “ferroptosis signaling–macrophage state–efferocytic efficiency” axis as an evidence-organizing analytical framework. It delineates the molecular logic of ferroptosis in HF and evaluates the strength of the available evidence, then examines macrophage lineage diversity, phase-specific heterogeneity, and their potential metabolic constraints. It further reviews the key limiting steps underlying efferocytic dysregulation and, with explicit acknowledgment of current uncertainties and evidentiary gaps, summarizes the design principles, validation strategies, and translational challenges of medical-engineering-integrated nanotherapeutic interventions.

Core molecular features of ferroptosis: iron homeostasis dysregulation, lipid peroxidation, and collapse of the antioxidant system

Ferroptosis is regarded as a distinct form of RCD primarily because its triggering conditions and execution pathways exhibit high specificity through the coupled network of “iron homeostasis-lipid peroxidation-antioxidant defense” [17–19]. Unlike canonical death pathways in which protease cascades or membrane pore formation constitute terminal events, ferroptosis is better understood as a systemic failure of redox control: iron-dependent reactions drive the propagation of lipid peroxidation, whereas the abundance of peroxidizable membrane lipids and the loss of antioxidant capacity determine whether cells cross a cytotoxic threshold [8]. Current consensus frameworks generally describe ferroptosis as involving three interrelated processes: redistribution of iron metabolism that expands the labile iron pool (LIP), enrichment of membrane polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) that increases susceptibility to peroxidation, and collapse of antioxidant systems, particularly the glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4)-glutathione axis and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), which normally prevent the accumulation of toxic lipid peroxides [8, 17, 20]. These features make ferroptosis a useful framework for explaining how metabolic stress shifts membrane lipid homeostasis from a buffered and potentially reversible state toward irreversible oxidative injury. Although the core molecular logic of ferroptosis is well established in in vitro and animal models (Tier 1), whether it serves as a decisive driver of HF progression remains unresolved and requires stronger etiology-specific, stage-specific, and human stratification-based evidence. At present, its dominant role in human HF remains supported largely by correlative and inferential evidence (Tier 2/3) [11, 21].

In cardiovascular disease research, this framework is frequently invoked to explain how oxidative stress shifts from reversible injury to overt cardiomyocyte loss [9, 22, 23]. Evidence from models of ischemia-reperfusion injury, metabolic stress, and inflammation-associated myocardial damage consistently supports the core molecular logic of ferroptosis (Tier 1): lipid peroxidation accumulates, iron homeostasis-related molecules are altered in parallel with worsening cardiomyocyte injury, and interventions targeting lipid peroxidation or iron overload often confer cardioprotective effects [9, 24, 25]. However, the magnitude of ferroptosis contribution varies across models, and in chronic HF or human stratification studies its dominant pathogenic role remains insufficiently established (Tier 2/3), with much of the available evidence still limited to correlation and mechanistic extrapolation [11]. Accordingly, a more rigorous review framework should distinguish between the strong consensus regarding the core molecular logic of ferroptosis (Tier 1) and the still-unresolved question of its pathophysiological dominance in specific HF subtypes, which requires more systematic human evidence and phase-specific validation (Tier 2/3) [11, 26].

Manifestations of cardiomyocyte iron metabolism dysregulation across different types of HF

The etiological heterogeneity of HF means that disturbances in iron homeostasis are highly context dependent, making “aberrant iron metabolism” better understood as network reprogramming rather than alteration of a single parameter [27]. In ischemic injury and reperfusion settings, abrupt surges in oxidative stress and disruption of the mitochondrial respiratory chain are frequently described as major drivers of lipid peroxidation, with iron-related reactions acting mainly as amplifiers during the acute phase [9, 23]. In pressure overload and structural cardiomyopathy, chronic metabolic stress, microcirculatory impairment, and persistent inflammatory input may produce a slower but sustained drift in iron homeostasis, progressively shifting cardiomyocytes toward a ferroptosis-prone state over longer time scales [26, 28]. Diabetic and obesity-related cardiomyopathies may further couple lipid metabolic remodeling, reduced mitochondrial efficiency, and insufficient antioxidant substrates, thereby increasing susceptibility to membrane lipid peroxidation [29]. Collectively, different etiologies appear to perturb distinct nodes within the “iron-lipid-antioxidant” network, thereby increasing ferroptosis-related lipid peroxidation susceptibility in specific contexts rather than converging on a single obligatory terminal pathway. The relative contribution of this process should be defined by etiology-, stage-, and population-stratified evidence [17].

At the same time, abnormalities in iron metabolism should not be reduced to the simplistic view that “more iron is harmful” or “less iron is detrimental,” because toxicity risk is determined by the distribution of iron across cellular compartments and its binding states [18, 27]. Although iron deficiency and iron supplementation are well established in clinical HF management, current evidence indicates that systemic iron status and local tissue iron handling may show nonlinear relationships, creating a complex situation in which reduced bioavailable iron coexists with increased reactive iron risk [30–32]. Previous studies have shown that the hepcidin axis, ferritin and ferritinophagy, transferrin receptors, and ferroportin collectively shape local iron homeostasis, while also being closely intertwined with inflammatory signaling and metabolic hormone pathways [27, 33]. Accordingly, a more rigorous approach to discussing ferroptosis risk across HF subtypes is to focus on how iron homeostasis networks are reprogrammed and how such reprogramming shifts the balance between lipid peroxidation and antioxidant defense, thereby linking phenotypic heterogeneity to mechanistic thresholds [11].

Regulatory roles of GPX4, SLC7A11, and ACSL4 in Ferroptosis in HF

Among the most tractable mechanistic nodes, GPX4, SLC7A11, and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) are widely regarded as key regulatory interfaces within the ferroptosis network, representing peroxide detoxification capacity, glutathione precursor supply, and membrane lipid susceptibility, respectively [22]. Current reviews consistently indicate that collapse of the GPX4-glutathione system directly impairs lipid peroxide clearance, that SLC7A11-mediated cystine uptake determines substrate availability for glutathione synthesis, and that ACSL4 promotes the incorporation of PUFAs into membrane phospholipids, thereby increasing the pool of peroxidizable substrates at the source [34, 35]. Multiple studies in myocardial injury-related models have reported concurrent impairment of antioxidant defenses, enhanced membrane lipid peroxidation, and progressive cardiomyocyte injury, while modulation of these nodes has shown cardioprotective potential in different settings (Table S1). From this perspective, ferroptosis can be understood as the consequence of an imbalance between antioxidant clearance capacity and membrane lipid susceptibility, with these molecules serving as useful conceptual anchors for organizing evidence and designing interventional strategies.

At the same time, none of these factors should be framed as a single master switch determining disease trajectory, particularly given the still-limited stratified evidence from human HF. Their effects are constrained by upstream transcriptional programs, post-translational modifications, and broader metabolic fluxes [35, 36]. The literature further suggests that NRF2-associated antioxidant transcriptional networks, the ferritin-iron export system, and signaling pathways linked to mitochondrial stress responses can all modulate ferroptosis-related thresholds by influencing the GPX4/SLC7A11 axis or lipid metabolism [27, 34, 37]. In addition, evidence indicates that in chronic HF, prolonged single-target inhibition and compensatory reprogramming may introduce dose-window and safety limitations, underscoring the need to incorporate timing, cell type, and dosage into therapeutic design [38]. Taken together, this section is framed within a “mechanistic anchor plus evidence-strength stratification” approach: although these molecules constitute strong consensus anchors, their translational relevance across human HF subtypes still depends on more systematic omics-based stratification and functional validation.

Crosstalk between mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis signaling pathways

Cardiomyocytes are highly dependent on mitochondria, making this organelle a central point in discussions of ferroptosis. Mitochondria not only participate in essential processes such as iron-sulfur cluster and heme biosynthesis, but also regulate the dynamic balance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and clearance [39, 40]. Evidence from multiple HF-related studies indicates that reduced mitochondrial biogenesis, impaired substrate utilization, and decreased respiratory chain efficiency enhance oxidative stress and may synergistically increase susceptibility to lipid peroxidation. In turn, lipid peroxidation-induced damage to membrane structure and membrane protein function further disrupts mitochondrial membrane potential and energy transduction, thereby establishing a vicious cycle [41, 42]. From this perspective, mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis can be viewed, in certain contexts, as a mutually reinforcing damage-amplification system: mitochondrial status may influence how rapidly ferroptosis-related thresholds are reached, while accumulation of membrane lipid peroxidation can further destabilize mitochondrial homeostasis [43, 44]. Importantly, this concept of “mutual amplification” is better suited to describing the risk architecture of a coupled system than to implying that ferroptosis acts as a central driver at all stages of HF [11]. However, a major evidentiary gap remains, as systematic studies in human HF tissues are still lacking that can simultaneously resolve the temporal relationships among mitochondrial iron handling, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis markers, and establish clear interventional causal chains.

At the same time, available evidence suggests that crosstalk does not necessarily mean that mitochondria are always the initiating node. Some reviews regard mitochondria mainly as platforms for signal propagation and amplification, proposing that ferroptosis initiation depends more directly on membrane lipid substrates and the integrity of antioxidant defenses. Other studies suggest that abnormalities in mitochondrial iron handling may act as more proximal upstream drivers in specific contexts. However, this view remains inconsistent across models and is not yet supported by sufficiently direct causal evidence in human HF tissues [11, 43]. A more balanced synthesis, therefore, emphasizes that multilayered crosstalk and mutual amplification between mitochondria and ferroptosis represent a relatively strong consensus, whereas their relative contributions across different HF etiologies and disease stages require further clarification through integrated single-cell or spatial omics coupled with functional perturbation studies [45]. Accordingly, the mechanisms discussed in this section are better regarded as candidate pathways that may participate in, and potentially be amenable to intervention in, HF progression, rather than as a single dominant determinant of disease evolution (Fig. 1).

Functional heterogeneity of macrophages in the immune microenvironment of HF

Lineage differences between heart resident macrophages and monocyte-derived macrophages

Recognition of the heart as an immunologically “homeostatic organ” has underscored the central role of macrophages in maintaining myocardial homeostasis and orchestrating injury responses [46]. Current literature generally distinguishes two major origins of cardiac macrophages: embryonically established resident macrophages and monocyte-derived macrophages recruited after injury. Resident subsets are primarily associated with homeostatic clearance and tissue support, whereas monocyte-derived subsets more often contribute to inflammatory amplification, assume a substantial clearance burden during acute injury, and may become linked to fibrotic programs during chronic remodeling [12]. Crucially, these lineages may differ in their sensitivity and functional responses to ferroptosis-related stresses, such as iron overload and oxidized lipid accumulation, which may differentially affect their efferocytic capacity and roles in inflammation resolution.

Advances in experimental approaches have also reshaped understanding of lineage differences. Single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, and fate-mapping studies indicate that the traditional M1/M2 dichotomy does not adequately capture the continuous spectrum of cardiac macrophage states across temporal stages and spatial niches [6, 46]. At the tissue level, multiple studies suggest that programs related to phagocytic clearance, lysosomal processing, and metabolic adaptation are preferentially enriched in specific subsets, whereas under conditions of intense inflammatory pressure or oxidized lipid burden, these programs may become suppressed or dysregulated [14]. Consequently, macrophage lineage heterogeneity is not merely a taxonomic issue but also provides mechanistic insight into the heterogeneity of the HF immune microenvironment: the direction of remodeling in different individuals and at different stages may depend on which subsets predominate and on whether their clearance capacity is compromised by microenvironmental constraints (Fig. 2).

Lineage heterogeneity and functional continuum of macrophages in HF

Dynamic evolution of macrophage phenotypes across different stages of HF

In HF, the transition of macrophages from pro-inflammatory states toward resolution- and repair-associated programs is often hindered by persistent cell death burden and declining clearance efficiency, leaving them trapped in states that parallel progressive chronic remodeling [47, 48]. These phenotypic dynamics are constrained by microenvironmental inputs that define the boundaries of macrophage plasticity [49, 50]. Importantly, ferroptosis-associated danger signals, local oxidative stress, and metabolic perturbations may collectively shape the stability of macrophage states, increasing the likelihood that they become locked into non-resolving attractor states [51, 52].

Across the current literature, a relatively consistent observation is that elevated oxidized lipid burden and heightened metabolic stress often coincide with constrained resolution- and repair-associated macrophage programs in multiple models [47]. On this basis, a plausible mechanistic hypothesis is that persistent oxidized lipids and iron-related disturbances in the microenvironment may drive macrophages into a state of high metabolic stress, thereby impairing their transition toward resolution and repair. However, whether ferroptosis-related inputs constitute a major source of such stress, and whether they show sufficient spatiotemporal continuity and subset-specific correspondence in human HF tissues, remain unresolved because of the lack of systematic stratified human data and interventional causal evidence [11].

Functional reprogramming of macrophages driven by immunometabolic remodeling

Immunometabolism provides an integrative framework for understanding macrophage plasticity, as metabolic pathways not only supply energy and biosynthetic substrates but also shape transcriptional programs and effector functions through metabolic intermediates and redox states [53, 54]. Current literature and major reviews indicate that pro-inflammatory macrophage states are generally associated with a preference for glycolysis, reduced mitochondrial efficiency, and signaling through specific metabolic intermediates, whereas resolution- and repair-associated states depend more on oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation [55, 56]. Within the HF microenvironment, macrophages face dual metabolic constraints: insufficient local perfusion and acidosis limit oxidative metabolic efficiency, while an increased burden of dying cells and lipid handling raises the demands on phagocytic and lysosomal systems [57]. In this setting, immunometabolic reprogramming is better viewed as a key constraint on transitions between macrophage functional programs, influencing the likelihood and efficiency with which inflammatory responses shift toward resolution and repair, rather than acting as the sole driver of this process.

Available evidence provides relatively consistent directional support for the idea that metabolic programs regulate phagocytosis and inflammation resolution, although the dominance of specific metabolic nodes appears to vary across models [55]. Some studies suggest that oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage reduce phagocytic capacity and impair lysosomal degradation, whereas others identify bottlenecks in lipid-handling pathways as more critical determinants [54]. A more robust synthesis is therefore to emphasize the intermediate-level mechanism by which metabolic stress constrains switching between functional programs, while treating individual molecular nodes as stratified “potential regulatory interfaces” [57]. At the same time, distinctions in evidence strength should be maintained: although metabolism–function coupling is relatively well supported in model systems, its relevance to human HF stratification, spatiotemporal dynamics, and interventional causal chains remains insufficiently established. This framework also provides a logical bridge to engineered interventions. In principle, if nanodelivery systems or local microenvironmental modulation can relieve macrophage metabolic stress and alter substrate availability, they may coordinately influence polarization, efferocytosis, and inflammation resolution; however, this inference still requires cell type-specific functional validation before its translational relevance can be established.

Roles of macrophages in inflammatory amplification, fibrosis, and structural remodeling in HF

From the perspective of tissue remodeling, inflammatory amplification and fibrosis are better understood as successive phases of a shared process, with macrophage–fibroblast interactions representing a key axis linking the two [58, 59]. Multiple reviews indicate that macrophages promote fibroblast activation and extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition through inflammatory mediators, chemokine networks, and growth factors, while also influencing angiogenesis and tissue mechanical properties; conversely, increased matrix stiffness, altered stromal secretory profiles, and persistent hypoxia can feed back to reshape macrophage phenotypes, biasing them toward chronic inflammatory and pro-fibrotic programs [58]. Previous studies have shown that when clearance programs proceed efficiently, macrophages contribute to inflammation termination and tissue repair, thereby constraining remodeling within adaptive limits. In contrast, when clearance fails and danger signals persist, macrophages are more likely to sustain remodeling inertia [13, 60]. Overall, current evidence suggests that macrophages in HF function as a context-dependent double-edged sword, with their net effect determined by microenvironmental inputs and the success or failure of functional program switching [61].

When this framework is integrated with the ferroptosis–efferocytosis axis, a more systemic closed-loop model emerges. Ferroptosis may continuously generate oxidized lipids and iron-related perturbations that increase macrophage metabolic and clearance burdens while impairing efferocytic efficiency; the resulting persistence of cellular debris and inflammatory signaling can further promote fibroblast activation and matrix deposition; in turn, hypoxia and metabolic stress caused by matrix accumulation may heighten cardiomyocyte stress and susceptibility to ferroptosis, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop [14, 26]. It should be noted that although the concept of ferroptosis-driven suppression of macrophage efferocytosis is internally coherent and indirectly supported by multiple lines of evidence, direct spatiotemporal causal data in HF-specific contexts remain relatively limited.

Ferroptosis-induced macrophage responses and immunometabolic remodeling

Danger signals released by ferroptotic cardiomyocytes and their recognition by macrophages

When cardiomyocytes undergo membrane lipid damage and redox imbalance under ferroptotic stress, the signals they release are not merely terminal products of cell death, but composite inputs capable of persistently shaping immune response states [62]. Cross-disciplinary reviews consistently indicate that ferroptosis-associated microenvironments are enriched in oxidized phospholipids, lipid peroxidation-derived products, and iron-related molecular disturbances, all of which can function as danger signals that engage immune receptors and modulate cellular programs [63]. Evidence from diverse disease models suggests that these signals can alter antigen presentation, cytokine profiles, and phagocytic clearance efficiency, thereby exerting either pro-inflammatory or immunomodulatory effects depending on tissue context [64]. In the setting of HF, the key question is not whether such danger signals are present, but whether, under specific conditions, they can persistently impair macrophage clearance and resolution capacity and, together with other pathological drivers, sustain chronic inflammation and fibrosis [65].

From the perspective of recognition mechanisms, both the carrier forms and spatial presentation of these danger signals are critical. Soluble mediators, cellular debris, extracellular vesicles, and oxidized lipid particles may engage distinct receptor repertoires and intracellular pathways [66]. Efferocytosis depends on a dynamic balance between “eat-me” and “don’t-eat-me” signals, as well as sufficient post-engulfment lysosomal degradation capacity, and membrane lipid oxidation may disrupt surface labeling and membrane stability, placing subsets of dying cells into a gray zone of recognition and processing [65]. The literature also indicates that macrophages are highly sensitive to iron overload, with iron homeostasis itself functioning as a metabolic and redox signal that regulates polarization and functional output [27, 62]. Overall, ferroptosis-associated danger signals can be interpreted as composite inputs with dual potential for immune activation and interference with clearance. This property provides a plausible mechanistic interface for the mutual amplification of ferroptosis and immune inflammation, although their dominance, temporal dynamics, and spatial distribution in human HF tissues remain to be defined in greater detail (Fig. 3).

Multi-input model of ferroptosis-associated danger signals driving macrophage responses

Effects of Iron overload and lipid peroxidation on macrophage polarization and metabolic State

Macrophage polarization is better understood as a continuum of interconnected states rather than a simple binary classification. Within this dynamic network, the capacity of iron overload and lipid peroxidation to modulate state stability represents a mechanistic consensus (Tier 1) supported by direct causal data from in vitro and general immunological models [62]. Iron can enhance oxidative stress and induce iron-sequestration and antioxidant programs, whereas lipid peroxidation-derived products can influence transcriptional regulatory networks and membrane receptor function through signaling mechanisms [67]. Multiple reviews addressing iron homeostasis and macrophage function further indicate close coupling between iron-handling programs and inflammatory output, phagocytic capacity, and tissue repair propensity across diverse disease settings [62]. However, when extended to the HF microenvironment, the proposition that persistent ferroptosis-related inputs not only increase clearance burden but also drive macrophages into a high metabolic stress state—thereby impeding transitions from pro-inflammatory to resolving or reparative programs—still relies mainly on condition-dependent associations and inferential evidence (Tier 2/3) [26]. Direct spatiotemporal causal evidence in human failing hearts or in vivo cardiac models remains lacking.

Nevertheless, the net effects of iron overload and lipid peroxidation on polarization direction are not fully consistent across the literature, suggesting that these factors more likely define the boundaries of macrophage plasticity rather than acting as a unidirectional pro-inflammatory switch [68, 69]. Some studies suggest that macrophages can buffer iron stress through ferritin upregulation, enhanced iron export, and metabolic adaptation, thereby adopting tolerance-associated or immunosuppressive phenotypes under specific conditions, whereas others emphasize that sustained oxidative pressure and substrate overload are more likely to impair phagocytic and lysosomal function, thereby hindering inflammation resolution [70, 71]. Taken together, a more robust interpretation is that iron overload and lipid peroxidation increase the risk of macrophages becoming locked in non-resolving states, but the specific phenotypic outcome depends on concurrent signaling networks, disease stage, and subset composition, placing this regulatory logic within the inferential integration layer [62]. This uncertainty should not be viewed as a conceptual weakness; rather, it provides a strong rationale for multivalent and phase-stratified therapeutic strategies that distinguish between established mechanistic consensus and context-dependent functional outcomes.

Feedback effects of macrophage Iron handling on cardiomyocyte ferroptosis

At the level of cardiac tissue, iron homeostasis is determined not only by cardiomyocyte-intrinsic metabolism but also by spatial redistribution processes involving immune cells, among which macrophages have been identified as key regulators [27]. Studies of inflammation and tissue repair across organ systems show that macrophages actively participate in iron uptake, storage, and redistribution, and that their iron-handling strategies can markedly influence local exposure to reactive iron [72]. In the context of HF pathology, this implies that when cardiomyocytes are poised in states of oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation susceptibility, microenvironmental changes in iron reactivity may serve as external conditions that modulate the propagation of ferroptosis-related injury [17, 26, 67]. The literature further indicates that macrophages shape tissue-scale iron homeostasis by regulating pathways of iron uptake, sequestration, and export, and that distinct macrophage functional states display divergent iron metabolic profiles [27, 73].

Taken together, this process is best understood as a context-dependent regulatory mechanism: in some settings, macrophage iron handling may buffer cardiomyocyte ferroptosis risk, whereas in others it may amplify oxidized lipid-driven injury cascades [27].

From a feedback perspective, the influence of macrophage iron metabolism on cardiomyocyte ferroptosis is bidirectional and reflects the integrated state of iron-handling strategies and metabolic stress [27]. Multiple studies suggest that when macrophages enhance iron sequestration and restrict iron export, the local labile iron pool (LIP) may decline, theoretically reducing the likelihood of iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation propagation. Conversely, under sustained inflammatory stimulation or impaired iron-buffering capacity, enhanced iron export or insufficient sequestration may increase the risk that surrounding tissues are exposed to a pro-ferroptotic environment [72]. At the same time, available evidence indicates that macrophages themselves may undergo metabolic and antioxidant decline under conditions of high iron and oxidized lipid burden, thereby weakening their function as local “iron buffers” [74]. Overall, this process is more appropriately viewed as a context-dependent feedback loop whose net effect is jointly determined by HF stage, microenvironmental inputs, and macrophage subset composition.

Significance of ferroptosis-immune inflammation interactions in the progression of HF

During the long-term evolution of HF, inflammation is not an isolated event but is typically embedded in myocardial remodeling and functional decline in a low-grade and persistent manner [75]. Multiple cardiovascular reviews indicate that, under ideal conditions, injury-induced immune responses should gradually resolve after dying cells are efficiently cleared. However, in HF, this sequence of initiation, resolution, and termination is often disrupted, leading to a self-sustaining inflammatory state [76]. Integrating ferroptosis into this framework of immune dysregulation helps explain why certain inflammatory responses fail to extinguish spontaneously: evidence from multiple disease models suggests that ferroptosis-associated oxidized lipids and perturbations in iron homeostasis can, in specific contexts, continuously activate inflammatory sensing pathways and prolong the immune response window [26, 67]. On this basis, ferroptosis is better regarded as a signaling background that can, under permissive conditions, provide sustained pro-inflammatory input into the immune microenvironment, rather than as an autonomous or obligatory driver of inflammation [11].

The proposed “ferroptosis signaling–immune inflammation–secondary injury” framework integrates multiple observational findings into a testable mechanistic hypothesis. In diverse models, ferroptosis-related oxidized lipids and iron perturbations parallel increased inflammatory mediator release and immune cell recruitment. Conversely, inflammatory microenvironments may heighten cardiomyocyte susceptibility to ferroptosis by increasing oxidative stress, disturbing metabolic homeostasis, and altering iron distribution. The resulting cell death burden and clearance pressure can further constrain inflammation resolution, thereby establishing a cycle of mutual amplification [13, 67, 77]. Advancing this framework will require defining the spatiotemporal distribution of ferroptotic signals in human HF tissues, determining the specific contributions of distinct macrophage subsets, and demonstrating that targeted interventions can functionally decouple this loop [6, 26, 78].

Macrophage-mediated efferocytosis: mechanisms of dysregulation in HF

Molecular steps and regulatory networks of efferocytosis: recognition, engulfment, and lysosomal degradation

Efferocytosis occupies a pivotal position in tissue homeostasis by linking cell death to inflammation resolution, functioning as an active, programmatic process of immune regulation rather than a purely passive clearance mechanism [54, 79, 80]. While the core molecular steps of recognition, engulfment, and lysosomal degradation are well-established (Table S2), it is critical to emphasize that efferocytic efficiency remains highly dependent on macrophage metabolic state and organelle homeostasis. This inherent dependency explains its profound vulnerability under conditions of ferroptosis-related oxidative stress and metabolic dysregulation, which specifically impair this clearance program and hinder the transition of the local immune environment from an inflammatory to a reparative state [81, 82].

From the perspective of regulatory networks, efferocytic efficiency is not governed by any single molecule or pathway, but by the coordinated operation of multiple processes. Existing studies indicate that precise receptor-ligand matching between dying cells and macrophages, dynamic cytoskeletal remodeling during engulfment, lysosomal acidification and degradative capacity, and sustained energy and substrate supply can all become rate-limiting steps in successful efferocytosis [83]. Across diverse disease models, the literature further shows that even when initial engulfment occurs, defects in downstream lysosomal degradation or metabolic remodeling may lead to “incomplete efferocytosis,” resulting in persistence or even amplification of inflammatory signaling [84]. Overall, efferocytosis is best understood as a continuous, metabolically demanding process that is highly dependent on organelle homeostasis, which also explains its fragility under oxidative and metabolic stress.

Challenges imposed on the efferocytic system by increased cardiomyocyte death burden in HF

Unlike acute myocardial injury, HF progression is often accompanied by the continuous accumulation of cardiomyocyte death over time [85]. Studies in ischemic cardiomyopathy, pressure overload models, and metabolic cardiomyopathies indicate that cardiomyocyte loss can occur in a chronic and recurrent manner, exposing cardiac macrophages to sustained and high-intensity clearance demands [86]. Previous investigations have shown that when the death burden persistently exceeds the processing capacity of the efferocytic system, cellular debris accumulates and the risk of secondary inflammation rises markedly, thereby amplifying local immune activation [47]. Accordingly, the central issue for efferocytosis in HF is not whether it can be initiated, but whether it can maintain stable efficiency under prolonged stress [78].

At the same time, the increasing death burden rarely occurs in isolation and is usually accompanied by progressive deterioration of the tissue microenvironment. The literature indicates that HF progression is often associated with local hypoxia, tissue acidosis, increased oxidative stress, and restricted nutrient availability, all of which can impair the metabolic support and lysosomal function required for effective efferocytosis [71, 87]. From an integrated perspective, this condition represents a classic “supply-demand mismatch,” in which dying cells are continuously generated while the clearance capacity of the efferocytic system progressively declines. This imbalance provides an important conceptual basis for understanding why chronic inflammation is difficult to resolve and why structural myocardial remodeling continues to worsen. It also establishes the pathological background for the subsequent discussion of how ferroptosis may further amplify efferocytic stress.

Impairment of efferocytic function by oxidative stress, Iron overload, and energetic dysfunction

From a functional perspective, efferocytosis is an immune process that is highly dependent on energy supply and organelle integrity, rendering it particularly sensitive to redox balance and metabolic conditions [80]. Multiple studies across disease models indicate that oxidative stress disrupts the cytoskeletal remodeling and phagosome maturation required for macrophage engulfment, whereas iron overload and lipid peroxidation can directly impair lysosomal membrane stability and degradative efficiency [82, 88]. Previous investigations further show that in environments enriched in iron or oxidized lipids, macrophages may initiate phagocytosis but display marked limitations in degrading and metabolizing cellular debris, resulting in a state of “functional insufficiency of efferocytosis.” This phenomenon offers an important explanation for HF, in which phagocytic activity may persist while inflammation nonetheless fails to resolve [47].

Energetic dysfunction is also considered a major constraint on efferocytic capacity. Studies in immunometabolism indicate that engulfment, lysosomal acidification, and post-phagocytic metabolic remodeling all depend heavily on mitochondrial function and ATP availability, and the mitochondrial inefficiency and abnormal substrate utilization characteristic of the HF microenvironment may therefore prevent macrophages from sustaining effective efferocytosis over time [82]. Integrating current evidence, this mechanism can be interpreted in the HF setting as follows: oxidative stress, disruption of iron homeostasis, and metabolic dysfunction collectively create an “efferocytosis-inhibitory background” in which, even when death signals are prominent, clearance programs are unable to proceed to completion (Fig. 4).

The continuous process of efferocytosis and its limiting steps in HF

Pathological links between defective efferocytosis, persistent inflammation, and myocardial fibrosis

The consequences of impaired efferocytosis extend beyond the physical accumulation of dead-cell debris, with a more profound impact being the persistent disruption of inflammation-resolution programs [13]. Multiple reviews indicate that successful efferocytosis is normally accompanied by the release of anti-inflammatory signals and restoration of immune homeostasis, whereas defective efferocytosis allows pro-inflammatory mediators to persist, thereby providing sustained stimuli for fibroblast activation and ECM deposition [54, 79]. Across a range of cardiovascular disease models, reduced efferocytic efficiency has consistently been associated with greater fibrosis severity, a relationship that appears particularly relevant during the structural remodeling of HF [89].

When these observations are integrated with ferroptosis-related inputs and immune regulatory processes, a “closed-loop” framework can be proposed to organize current evidence and generate testable predictions. In this framework, ferroptosis-associated oxidized lipids and perturbations in iron homeostasis may, under certain conditions, increase both death burden and clearance difficulty; under concurrent metabolic and lysosomal constraints, the efferocytic system becomes more susceptible to functional decline; the resulting persistence of debris and inflammatory signaling then coincides with fibrotic activation; and fibrosis-associated hypoxia and metabolic stress may heighten cardiomyocyte stress and susceptibility to ferroptosis-related injury [13, 26, 65].

In light of current evidence, this closed-loop framework provides an integrative perspective on the “inflammation-fibrosis inertia” that characterizes HF progression, but it should not yet be regarded as a fully validated causal circuit. The strength of evidence across its different nodes remains uneven, and its stage dependence, cell type specificity, and actionable therapeutic windows still need to be defined through higher-resolution human studies and interventional evidence chains [6]. Accordingly, identifying the stages at which this framework is most prominent, the subsets that contribute most strongly, and the nodes that can be effectively decoupled remains an important goal for subsequent integrative analyses and testable predictions.

A systems-integrated model of the ferroptosis-macrophage-efferocytosis axis

System-level consequences and stage-specific differences of the axis

In the preceding sections, ferroptosis-related inputs, macrophage state changes, and constraints on efferocytic efficiency were discussed separately at the molecular and cellular levels. At the systems level, this axis is better conceptualized as a model of immune–metabolic–clearance coupling imbalance, which helps explain why inflammation tends to persist, resolution is difficult to achieve, and fibrotic inertia is readily maintained in HF, rather than serving as evidence for the dominance of any single mechanism (Fig. 5).

Systems-level analytical framework of the ferroptosis-macrophage-efferocytosis axis

To avoid conflating condition-dependent associations with universal causality, this chapter centers the framework around three testable questions. First, at which stages is this coupling imbalance most pronounced: during the high death-burden window of acute decompensation, or during the low-grade but persistent stress of the chronic phase? Second, which cellular subsets impose the major constraints at different stages, particularly regarding the relative contributions of resident and monocyte-derived macrophages to iron handling, inflammatory output, and resolution programs? Third, which nodes are most likely to represent intervenable breakpoints capable of functionally decoupling the axis, such as restoring resolution capacity or reducing ferroptosis-related susceptibility?

With respect to stage specificity, the literature suggests that during acute myocardial injury or decompensation, death events are more clustered and the immune system prioritizes rapid clearance and termination of inflammation, making efferocytic efficiency more directly linked to inflammation duration and functional recovery. By contrast, in chronic HF, this axis more often manifests as a low-grade but persistent background pressure, such that in microenvironments characterized by long-standing hypoxia, metabolic dysfunction, and established fibrosis, even partial restoration of clearance programs may have limited capacity to reduce the overall inflammatory burden [40, 90]. Consistently, in the chronic phase, this axis is better understood as an amplifying condition that sustains inflammation and inertia in structural remodeling rather than as a single or decisive driver.

From the perspective of testable prediction, this framework is more useful for generating stratified, phase-specific, and subset-oriented hypotheses than for making global judgments. For example, in subsets characterized by high oxidized lipid burden or abnormal iron handling, efferocytic constraints may serve as proximal bottlenecks limiting inflammation resolution. In contrast, in chronic contexts dominated by metabolic stress and hypoxia, metabolic and lysosomal capacities may become the principal limiting steps of clearance programs [62, 91]. These predictions still require validation through human spatiotemporal data and cell type-specific interventional studies.

Evidence gradients and validation breakpoints

Although the ferroptosis–macrophage–efferocytosis axis shows strong biological coherence at the conceptual level, the strength of evidence supporting its individual components remains uneven [11, 17]. When used as a systems-integration model, it is essential to distinguish clearly between elements that represent mechanistic consensus, those supported by condition-dependent associations, and those that remain inferential syntheses, so as to avoid directly extrapolating cross-model observations into HF-specific causal loops [92].

Based on the current literature, this axis can be divided into three evidence tiers. The first is the mechanistic consensus layer: the core molecular logic of ferroptosis, including iron homeostasis, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant defense, has been repeatedly validated across multiple myocardial injury and HF-related models, and interventions targeting key nodes have consistently shown protective trends, making this framework relatively robust [17, 26]. The second is the condition-dependent layer: ferroptosis-related inputs show convergent associations with macrophage metabolic stress, inflammatory output, and functional shifts across diverse disease contexts, but in HF-specific settings, much of the evidence remains correlative or mechanistically extrapolated [62]. The third tier is the “inferential integration layer”: reduced efferocytic efficiency is functionally linked to persistent inflammation, fibrosis, and adverse remodeling in cardiovascular models, but whether efferocytic limitation is directionally driven by ferroptosis-related inputs, and how this causal weight is partitioned between resident and monocyte-derived macrophage subsets, still lacks direct cell type-specific and phase-restricted validation [13, 14].

At present, the most important causal breakpoints converge on two key issues. First, it remains unclear whether ferroptosis-associated oxidized lipids and iron perturbations temporally precede and are sufficiently strong to directly constrain macrophage efferocytic capacity, rather than merely acting as background pressures that accompany declining clearance. Second, it is still unresolved whether the division of labor between resident and monocyte-derived macrophages in iron handling, inflammatory output, and efferocytic flux bottlenecks has been systematically defined in human HF tissues with adequate spatiotemporal resolution. This distinction is crucial for determining whether the axis can provide translatable stratified targets and actionable therapeutic windows [6]. Thus, although this axis already offers substantial integrative explanatory value, its key internal nodes must be strengthened through cell type-specific, phase-restricted interventions with clear functional readouts, so that current plausible associations can be advanced toward testable and translatable mechanistic causality.

Validation pathways and phase-specific strategies

When this systems framework is translated into intervention design, the priority is not to manipulate all nodes simultaneously, but to strengthen the evidence chain by defining an actionable minimal evidence package and advancing validation stepwise from weaker to stronger support. This approach helps avoid conclusions that appear promising yet prove irreproducible because of insufficient readouts or phase mismatches [92, 93]. Based on the current evidence base, a more robust validation pathway should satisfy three general criteria. First, selected targets should ideally lie at the intersection of multiple modules, so that a single intervention can influence at least two of the following: ferroptotic burden, macrophage stress, and efferocytic flux. Second, validation should prioritize functional endpoints over isolated molecular changes, including whether efferocytic flux is genuinely increased, whether inflammation resolution is restored, and whether structural remodeling and cardiac function improve in a coordinated manner. Third, intervention timing must explicitly distinguish acute high-burden windows from chronic low-grade stress backgrounds, with phase definition incorporated directly into study design and evaluation [92, 94].

Within this framework, constraints on efferocytic flux may represent a particularly efficient entry point, especially at the level of post-engulfment processing and metabolic remodeling. This node lies downstream of ferroptosis-related inputs while directly determining whether inflammation termination can proceed effectively [13, 54, 82]. By contrast, strategies that only suppress ferroptotic signaling or alter macrophage polarization in isolation are more likely to encounter compensatory reprogramming and narrowed safety windows in chronic HF. Their translational potential therefore depends on integration with phase-restricted delivery, cell type-specific targeting, and flux-based functional readouts [11, 94].

The value of engineered delivery systems and nanomedicine along this axis lies precisely in their ability to incorporate validation conditions into the intervention design itself. Tissue enrichment and cell-selective targeting can improve on-target engagement, microenvironment-responsive features can enhance phase alignment, and multi-node coordination may strengthen net effects within complex pathological networks. At the same time, engineering constraints provide measurable parameters for evaluating long-term safety and batch-to-batch consistency [93, 94].

Precision intervention strategies targeting the ferroptosis-efferocytosis axis from a medical-engineering integration perspective

To avoid a disconnect between engineering strategies and mechanistic reasoning, this chapter first proposes a mapping framework linking mechanistic breakpoints, engineering modules, and validation endpoints. The ferroptosis–macrophage–efferocytosis axis can be organized into three actionable interfaces: (i) cardiomyocyte ferroptotic burden and threshold, including iron homeostasis, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant defense; (ii) macrophage immunometabolism and functional switching; and (iii) efferocytic flux, including recognition, engulfment, and post-engulfment processing. Corresponding engineering modules include lesion enrichment and cell selectivity, microenvironment responsiveness and phase matching, as well as multi-node coordination coupled with causal evaluation, with validation endpoints aligned accordingly (Fig. 6).

Mapping of mechanistic breakpoints, engineering modules, and validation endpoints along the ferroptosis-efferocytosis axis

Challenges and design requirements imposed by the HF microenvironment

HF does not progress within a uniform and stable tissue setting, but rather within a highly heterogeneous and dynamically evolving myocardial microenvironment [95, 96]. Cardiovascular and immunological studies consistently show that the failing myocardium is characterized by hypoxia, tissue acidosis, increased oxidative stress, disrupted iron homeostasis, and dynamic changes in immune-cell composition and functional states [27, 97]. These features not only contribute to disease progression, but also strongly influence delivery efficiency, duration of action, and target responsiveness of therapeutic strategies [98]. Previous studies further indicate that even appropriately selected targets may show markedly reduced efficacy when local pathological constraints within the microenvironment are not adequately addressed [99].

From a therapeutic perspective, the multilayered regulatory nature of the ferroptosis–macrophage–efferocytosis axis limits the effectiveness of approaches that target only a single molecule or pathway in isolation. Available evidence suggests that intervention systems capable of responding to microenvironmental changes across pathological stages are more likely to improve efficacy while reducing the risk of excessive suppression of physiological immune functions [98]. In this sense, the complexity of the HF microenvironment provides a clear rationale for medical-engineering integration and underscores the need for engineered strategies in treating complex cardiovascular disease.

Engineering principles for targeting cardiomyocytes and macrophages

Within pathological axes involving coordinated interactions among multiple cell types, achieving cell type-specific and spatially resolved control is widely regarded as a key strategy for improving therapeutic specificity and safety [59]. The cardiovascular and immunoengineering literature emphasizes that cardiomyocytes and macrophages differ substantially in their functional roles, activation timing, and metabolic features during HF progression, making them central targets in intervention design [6, 100]. Previous studies have shown that systemic interventions lacking cell selectivity are often associated with greater off-target effects, thereby limiting long-term applicability and translational potential [94].

At the level of engineering design, multiple studies suggest that targeting strategies should integrate receptor expression profiles, tissue permeability, cellular uptake or phagocytic properties, and disease-stage differences of target cells. For example, the phagocytic activity and immunometabolic characteristics of macrophages provide intrinsic advantages for their use as delivery targets, whereas cardiomyocyte targeting depends more heavily on carrier enrichment within cardiac tissue and stable spatial localization [101].

Taken together, this design logic can be summarized as a principle of dual spatial and cellular constraint, which captures a common feature of existing engineered interventions in HF rather than a universally validated rule [102]. When integrated with the preceding mechanism-engineering framework, cardiomyocyte-targeted strategies align more directly with controlling ferroptotic burden and threshold, whereas macrophage-targeted strategies are better suited to modulating immunometabolic remodeling and improving efferocytic execution. In this way, the cell selectivity of delivery systems can be logically matched to specific mechanistic breakpoints [101].

Identification of key regulatory nodes in ferroptosis and efferocytosis through multi-omics approaches

With the development of systems biology, multi-omics technologies have become increasingly important for deciphering regulatory networks in complex diseases. Current evidence suggests that integrating single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics offers new opportunities to resolve cellular-state heterogeneity, pathway reprogramming, and metabolic remodeling within the HF immune microenvironment. Previous studies have shown that such approaches can systematically characterize differences among cell populations in iron metabolism, oxidative stress responses, and immune functions without relying on a single a priori hypothesis, thereby facilitating identification of spatiotemporally specific imbalances in ferroptosis and efferocytic capacity [103].

From the perspective of intervention design, comparative multi-omics analyses have revealed that regulators of iron homeostasis, pathways governing lipid peroxidation and antioxidant defense, and immunometabolic modules linked to phagocytic and lysosomal function often show more concentrated alterations in specific cell subsets or pathological stages [103]. As noted in the literature, such nodes are more likely than broadly expressed signaling pathways to represent functional bottlenecks under pathological conditions and may therefore offer greater regulatory leverage [27]. By integrating these omics-identified nodes with stage-specific features of HF, recent studies have begun to provide reverse-informative guidance for configuring functional modules in engineered intervention systems, rather than relying solely on empirically selected targets [104].

Taken together, multi-omics approaches provide a critical bridge between mechanistic analysis and engineered therapeutic design. On the one hand, they help define quantifiable candidate sets of “mechanistic breakpoints” within the ferroptosis–efferocytosis axis by identifying key regulatory nodes and their differential remodeling across cell populations. On the other hand, they inform target selection, functional combination, and phase alignment of engineered systems, for example by distinguishing acute high-burden peaks from chronic background stress, thereby improving consistency between intervention design and the spatiotemporal features of pathology.

Mechanism-guided construction of nanotechnology and engineered intervention systems

In complex cardiovascular disease, translating mechanistic insights into executable engineering principles is widely regarded as a key route to improving therapeutic efficiency and controllability [105, 106]. The nanomedicine and bioengineering literature indicates that advances in responsive materials, programmable delivery systems, and multifunctional carriers allow interventions to adapt dynamically to local microenvironmental changes [107]. Previous studies further suggest that systems engineered to respond to oxidative stress, iron overload, or inflammatory signals are, in principle, better aligned with the spatiotemporal features of HF pathology [94, 105].

By embedding the mechanistic features of the ferroptosis–macrophage–efferocytosis axis into engineering logic, a strategy of “mechanism-guided engineering” can be established for intervention development [101]. Based on current evidence, this approach may be understood as using ferroptotic burden, macrophage immunometabolic state, and efferocytic efficiency as functional input signals, thereby enabling coordinated multi-node regulation through engineered platforms rather than relying on single-target inhibition [101]. It should be noted that existing support for this strategy is largely derived from exploratory and preclinical studies, and that its long-term safety, regulatory precision, and clinical feasibility require systematic evaluation [105]. However, this design framework provides a clear and extensible theoretical backbone for the development of nanotherapeutics and precision delivery systems.

Advances in nanotherapeutic modulation of the ferroptosis-macrophage-efferocytosis axis

Targeting ferroptotic burden (Breakpoint 1): nanotherapeutic examples targeting cardiomyocyte ferroptosis

From the perspective of mechanism–engineering mapping, the examples in this section primarily correspond to the cardiomyocyte-level interface that regulates the ferroptotic threshold, namely the modulation of iron homeostasis, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant defense to reduce ferroptosis-associated injury burden. In HF-related ferroptosis interventions, a central challenge in drug design is how to enhance regulatory efficiency while preserving systemic safety [94]. Numerous cardiovascular studies have noted that conventional small-molecule antioxidants or iron chelators, when administered systemically, are limited by poor cardiac enrichment, short duration of action, and substantial off-target distribution [108]. Against this background, nanodelivery systems have been increasingly introduced to improve myocardial distribution and exposure stability of anti-ferroptotic agents through engineered design [109]. Previous studies have shown that certain nanocarriers, through passive accumulation or ligand-mediated targeting, can increase the local concentration of functional molecules in injured myocardium and thereby attenuate lipid peroxidation-associated injury in experimental models [94].

At the level of regulatory strategy, these nanotherapeutic approaches mainly focus on three modules: modulation of iron homeostasis, suppression of lipid peroxidation, and reinforcement of antioxidant defense systems. The literature reports that some nanoplatforms are designed to deliver iron chelators, radical scavengers, or modulators affecting GPX4 function to reduce the overall intensity of ferroptotic signaling [109]. Multiple studies in cellular and animal models suggest that, compared with free drugs, nanodelivery offers advantages in sustaining pharmacological effects and controlling local exposure [94, 109]. Taken together, these examples suggest that nanodelivery has clear theoretical potential to improve the efficiency of ferroptosis modulation, although its clinical relevance will ultimately depend on systematic evaluation of long-term carrier safety, biocompatibility, and release behavior.

Targeting macrophage stress (Breakpoint 2): nanotherapeutic strategies for modulating macrophage polarization and immunometabolism

The strategies discussed in this section primarily correspond to the macrophage-level interface governing immunometabolic state and functional program switching. Their aim is not simply to suppress inflammation, but to improve access to resolution- and repair-associated functions by reducing metabolic stress or reprogramming metabolic pathways. Modulation of the immune microenvironment has increasingly emerged as an important strategy for slowing HF progression. Studies in immunoengineering and nanomedicine indicate that the phagocytic behavior and metabolic plasticity of macrophages during systemic distribution make them especially prone to interacting with nanomaterials, thereby making them attractive targets for engineered interventions [110]. Previous work has shown that a wide range of nanomaterials can be readily taken up by macrophages during circulation or tissue accumulation, providing a practical basis for directed modulation of their functional states [111]. Building on this property, the literature has progressively explored the use of nanocarriers to deliver immunomodulatory agents to influence macrophage polarization and immunometabolic features [6, 59].

In terms of emerging trends, these nanointervention strategies tend to focus on attenuating pro-inflammatory signaling, reprogramming metabolic pathways, or alleviating oxidative stress burden [101]. Existing reports suggest that, with appropriate engineering design, nanoplatforms can modulate macrophage functional states to some extent and thereby indirectly alter their responses to cardiomyocyte death signals [101]. More broadly, this strategy may be understood as an indirect means of regulating the operating state of the ferroptosis–efferocytosis axis through immune modulation. However, current evidence remains largely exploratory and preclinical, and key issues, including cell specificity, stability, and long-term regulatory effects within the complex HF microenvironment, still require systematic validation.

Targeting efferocytic flux (Breakpoint 3): nanotechnological approaches to enhance efferocytosis and promote myocardial repair

The examples discussed in this section primarily correspond to the execution-efficiency interface of efferocytosis, including recognition and engulfment, post-engulfment lysosomal degradation, and metabolic support required for effective processing, thereby addressing the functional gap in which phagocytosis occurs but inflammation resolution remains insufficient (Table S3). Table S3 summarizes representative nanotechnological approaches across these interface levels, together with their mechanisms of action, major limitations, and remaining evidence gaps. As the importance of efferocytosis in HF has become increasingly recognized, several studies have begun to explore engineered strategies aimed at improving clearance of dying cells and enhancing inflammation-resolution capacity [112, 113]. Research in immune regulation and tissue repair indicates that timely removal of dead cells helps limit persistence of pro-inflammatory signals and creates a more favorable microenvironment for subsequent repair [13, 114]. Within this context, nanotechnological approaches have been introduced to enhance macrophage phagocytic function, support lysosomal activity, or improve energy and metabolic supply [112, 113, 115].

Previous studies have shown that certain nanomaterials are designed to interact with signaling pathways involved in phagocytosis or lysosomal function, thereby enhancing efferocytic efficiency at the model level [112, 116]. The literature also indicates that some engineered systems may indirectly facilitate efferocytosis by alleviating oxidative stress or providing metabolic support [117, 118]. Taken together, these strategies suggest, in principle, new opportunities to correct efferocytic imbalance. However, their durability, safety, and possible effects on physiological immune clearance in the setting of long-term and complex HF pathology still require more systematic evaluation [116, 119, 120]. Assessment of such combinatorial modes should also return to the “interface alignment” principle, rather than relying solely on tissue accumulation or phenotypic improvement as surrogates for mechanistic closure.

Advantages, limitations, and conceptual boundaries of multi-target synergistic nanotherapeutic strategies

Because the ferroptosis–macrophage–efferocytosis axis encompasses multiple interconnected pathological processes, the limitations of single-target interventions have become increasingly apparent. Numerous studies in nanomedicine indicate that advances in multifunctional carriers and co-delivery systems have made simultaneous modulation of several pathological nodes technically feasible [121, 122]. Previous investigations have shown that some nanoplatforms are engineered to co-deliver antioxidants, immunomodulatory agents, or metabolic regulators, thereby producing multilayered therapeutic effects in experimental models. Conceptually, such synergistic strategies are more consistent with the regulatory demands of systemic diseases (Fig. 7) [112, 116].

Hierarchical intervention layers and evidence status of nanotherapeutic strategies targeting the ferroptosis-efferocytosis axis

At the same time, the literature reveals several important limitations. Many nanointervention studies remain confined to a single pathological model or narrow temporal window, leaving their applicability across different HF etiologies, stages, and immune backgrounds insufficiently validated [123]. Prior work has further indicated that regulatory trends observed in acute injury models do not necessarily predict long-term performance in the context of chronic HF [94]. In addition, the criteria used to define “targeting success” vary substantially among studies. Some rely mainly on tissue accumulation or phenotypic improvement, whereas direct causal linkage to key nodes in ferroptosis, immunometabolism, or efferocytosis is often inadequately demonstrated [123]. Overall, these observations suggest that the field is transitioning from proof-of-concept toward mechanistic closure, and defining these conceptual boundaries is essential for a balanced assessment of the true potential and translational prospects of multi-target synergistic nanotherapeutic strategies.

Translational challenges and future perspectives

Safety, targeting, and reproducibility of nanotherapeutics in cardiovascular disease

As HF-related interventions increasingly move toward engineered and nanotechnology-based strategies, their clinical translatability has become a central research concern [94]. Reviews in the cardiovascular and nanomedicine fields emphasize that, compared with many other organ systems, the cardiovascular system is particularly sensitive to exogenous materials, and the long-term in vivo persistence of nanocarriers may entail risks of immune activation, organ accumulation, or interference with cardiac function [124]. Previous studies have further shown that some nanoplatforms performing well in short-term or single-dose models may exhibit unanticipated safety issues under prolonged dosing or increased exposure, a concern that is especially relevant in chronic HF [93, 94].

Beyond safety, stability and reproducibility of therapeutic outcomes represent equally important translational barriers. The literature indicates that many nanodelivery systems are evaluated under idealized pathological models, specific receptor-expression patterns, or relatively uniform physiological conditions, whereas in real-world clinical populations, interindividual genetic variation, disease heterogeneity, and concomitant medications may substantially alter biodistribution and biological effects [125, 126]. Prior work also suggests that even subtle batch-to-batch differences in physicochemical properties, such as particle size, surface charge, or surface modification, may be amplified into biologically meaningful variability [93]. Together, these considerations highlight the need for more rigorous quality-control systems, batch-consistency standards, and long-term safety-monitoring frameworks before nanotherapeutics can be advanced into clinical practice.

Challenges in the clinical validation of ferroptosis- and immune-targeted therapies

Introducing ferroptosis and immune regulation as therapeutic targets offers new opportunities for HF treatment, but their clinical validation is inherently complex [92]. Translational studies emphasize that ferroptosis-related signals are highly context dependent, with biological consequences that vary according to tissue type, disease stage, and metabolic state [62, 127]. Previous work has shown that certain iron-metabolic or antioxidant pathways may be protective in some pathological settings but disruptive to physiological adaptation or repair in others, making it difficult to define safe therapeutic windows and optimal intervention timing [11].

At the immune level, the dual functional roles of macrophages and their involvement in efferocytosis further complicate clinical validation. The literature indicates that immune modulation can attenuate inflammatory amplification, but if overapplied, may also impair host defense and tissue homeostasis [128, 129]. In addition, reliable clinical biomarkers that dynamically reflect ferroptosis intensity, efferocytic efficiency, or immunometabolic state are still lacking, which substantially limits patient stratification, efficacy assessment, and precise selection of intervention timing [130]. Taken together, these factors represent major hurdles that ferroptosis- and immune-targeted strategies must overcome as they move from mechanistic hypothesis to clinical validation [62].

Medical-engineering integration approaches as drivers of personalized and precision therapy in HF

HF is widely recognized as a highly heterogeneous clinical syndrome, with substantial interpatient variability in etiology, immune status, metabolic background, and disease trajectory [131, 132]. Multiple studies indicate that, in this setting, no single therapeutic strategy is likely to achieve stable and durable efficacy across broad patient populations [92]. By integrating mechanistic dissection, engineered delivery technologies, and data-driven analytical tools, the medical-engineering integration paradigm offers new opportunities for more refined patient stratification and therapy matching [6, 59]. Previous work has shown that combining multi-omics profiles, imaging features, and clinical phenotypes can help identify patient subsets in which ferroptosis, immune dysregulation, or impaired efferocytosis constitutes a dominant pathogenic driver [133, 134].

Looking forward, the literature suggests that precision therapy should not depend solely on increasingly complex drugs or carrier systems, but on establishing dynamic feedback among mechanism identification, target selection, and delivery strategy [133, 135]. In this sense, the medical-engineering integration framework can be viewed as a cross-scale model in which engineered systems improve the temporal and spatial precision of intervention, while systems biology and data analytics continuously refine the relevance and priority of therapeutic targets [135, 136]. This paradigm offers a practical and scalable pathway toward individualized regulation of the ferroptosis-macrophage-efferocytosis axis in HF.

Toward a new paradigm of closed-loop “mechanism-target-delivery” regulation in HF

As understanding of HF pathological complexity deepens, a growing body of research supports moving beyond linear intervention strategies toward system-level regulatory paradigms. The literature indicates that HF progression is driven by multiple interconnected pathological networks, and interventions directed at a single node or pathway are often insufficient to disrupt established positive feedback loops [47, 137]. Previous studies have shown that integrating forms of cell death, immune regulatory mechanisms, and engineered delivery strategies within a unified framework can provide a more comprehensive explanation for why interventions fail, show unstable efficacy, or exhibit strong phase dependence [135].

Based on current evidence, the “mechanism-target-delivery” closed-loop model emerges as an important theoretical anchor for future precision therapy in HF: mechanistic studies define dominant pathological drivers, target selection determines interventional priorities, and delivery strategies decide whether modulation occurs at the right time and in the right place [59, 138, 139]. This paradigm emphasizes cross-disciplinary integration rather than simple accumulation of individual technologies. At the same time, it should be recognized that the model currently functions primarily as an analytical framework for integrating multi-level evidence, and its clinical value will depend on continued advances in translational research and the establishment of standardized evaluation systems [93].

Although ferroptosis, immune regulation, and engineered delivery have increasingly converged as cross-cutting themes in HF research, the literature also indicates that current translational studies remain constrained by weaknesses in their evaluation frameworks. Previous investigations suggest that heterogeneity in endpoints and assessment criteria reduces comparability across studies; moreover, most work still focuses on improvement of individual pathological indices, with relatively limited attention to whether the ferroptosis–immune–efferocytosis axis has been collectively reshaped as a system [47, 93]. In light of the existing evidence, this gap reflects an incomplete transition from “single-metric validation” to “system-level effect assessment” in translational research.

Against this background, the value of the closed-loop regulatory paradigm lies not in delivering definitive causal conclusions, but in providing a testable and revisable systems-analytic perspective to guide future studies. Its clinical relevance should be progressively clarified through harmonized evaluation frameworks and long-term follow-up. From this perspective, the ferroptosis–macrophage–efferocytosis axis is regarded as a contextual framework for stratification and hypothesis generation, rather than as an a priori therapeutic priority. Whether it should be elevated to a primary intervention target must be determined by etiology, disease stage, and the strength of supporting evidence.

Conclusion

HF is a highly heterogeneous and progressive clinical syndrome in which persistent cardiomyocyte loss, chronic inflammation, and structural remodeling remain major barriers to disease reversal. Current evidence suggests that ferroptosis, macrophage functional remodeling, and impaired efferocytosis can be integrated into a coupled pathological axis that helps explain persistent inflammation and fibrotic progression in HF. However, this axis is regarded as a context-dependent regulatory framework than as a universally dominant therapeutic target, because its relative importance is likely shaped by HF etiology, disease stage, and immune-cell composition. Advances in medical-engineering integration and nanomedicine provide new opportunities to modulate this axis with greater spatial, temporal, and cellular precision. With stronger human validation, clearer biomarkers, and better alignment among mechanism identification, target selection, and delivery design, this framework may support future stratified and precision therapies for HF.
