1.
Introduction

For a real number
\beta>1
, consider the
\beta
-transformation

T_{\beta}(x):=\beta x\!\!\mod 1.

Given a number
t\in[0,1)
, we are interested in the
survivor set

K_{\beta}(t):=\left\{x\in[0,1):T_{\beta}^{n}(x)\geq t\ \forall n\geq 0\right\};

that is, the set of points whose forward orbit never enters the “hole”
[0,t)

1

Traditionally in open dynamical systems, one takes the hole to be open. But because here
0
is both an endpoint of the hole and an endpoint of the domain, it is more convenient to include this point with the hole; this affects the survivor set by at most countably many points.
.
Clearly, the set-valued function
t\mapsto K_{\beta}(t)
is non-increasing in
t
, and we may wish to study the
bifurcation set

(1.1)

\mathscr{E}_{\beta}:=\left\{t\in[0,1):K_{\beta}(t^{\prime})\neq K_{\beta}(t)~\forall t^{\prime}>t\right\}.

Kalle et al.
[
18
]
considered the case
\beta\in(1,2]
, and proved several properties of the set
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
(denoted by
E_{\beta}^{+}
in their paper). They furthermore showed that the dimension function
\eta_{\beta}:t\mapsto\dim_{H}K_{\beta}(t)
is a decreasing devil’s staircase. In particular, there is a point
t=\tau(\beta)
, called the
critical value
, at which
\eta_{\beta}(t)
first reaches the value zero. That is,

\tau(\beta):=\min\{t>0:\eta_{\beta}(t)=0\}.

In a previous paper
[
3
]
, the present authors determined this critical value
\tau(\beta)
for all
\beta\in(1,2]
.

The aim of the present article is to extend the results of
[
18
]
and
[
3
]
to all
\beta>1
. The primary difficulty in this generalization lies in the fact that for
\beta>2
one needs alphabets of size three or greater to understand the symbolic dynamics underlying the map
T_{\beta}
. The proofs in
[
18
]
and
[
3
]
relied heavily on the use of
Farey words
, which are words over the alphabet
\{0,1\}
that satisfy a certain balancing property. It is not a priori clear how one should extend these Farey words to larger alphabets. Doing this in the right way is the key to extending the above-mentioned results.

For the critical value
\tau(\beta)
, we state here first a general, qualitative result about the graph of
\beta\mapsto\tau(\beta)
. The precise calculation of
\tau(\beta)
splits into several cases and requires additional notation and concepts; we will present it in Section
3
.

Theorem 1.1
.

(i)

The function
\tau:\beta\mapsto\tau(\beta)
is left continuous on
(1,\infty)
with right-hand limits everywhere, but also has a countably infinite set of discontinuities;

(ii)

\tau
has no downward jumps;

(iii)

There is an open set
O\subseteq(1,\infty)
, whose complement
(1,\infty)\setminus O
has zero Hausdorff dimension, such that
\tau
is real-analytic, strictly convex and strictly decreasing on each connected component of
O
.

We remark that, as a consequence of (i), the dimension

is not jointly continuous in

was proved in

For the bifurcation set
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
, we prove the following:

Theorem 1.2
.

(i)

For any
\beta>1
,

\mathscr{E}_{\beta}=\left\{t\in[0,1):T_{\beta}^{n}(t)\geq t~\forall n\geq 0\right\}.

(ii)

For any
\beta>1
,

(iii)

For Lebesgue-almost every

(iv)

There is an uncountable set

{\mathscr{E}_{\beta}}
contains no isolated points if and only if
\beta\in E_{L}
.

1.1.
Related literature

The sets
K_{\beta}(t)
were first considered by Urbański
[
24
]
in the context of the doubling map (
\beta=2
). He proved that the function
\eta_{2}:t\mapsto\dim_{H}K_{2}(t)
is a decreasing devil’s staircase and the set-valued bifurcation set
\mathscr{E}_{2}
is equal to the
dimension bifurcation set

\mathscr{B}_{2}
, where

\mathscr{B}_{\beta}:=\left\{t\geq 0:\eta_{\beta}(t^{\prime})<\eta_{\beta}(t)~\forall t^{\prime}>t\right\},\qquad\beta>1.

Urbański showed furthermore that the critical value
\tau(2)=1/2
and that

\dim_{H}\big(\mathscr{E}_{2}\cap[t,1]\big)=\dim_{H}K_{2}(t)\qquad\forall\,t\in(0,1).

Kalle
et al.

[
18
]
conjectured that this identity should generalize to all
\beta\in(1,2]
, namely,

(1.2)

\dim_{H}\big(\mathscr{E}_{\beta}\cap[t,1]\big)=\dim_{H}K_{\beta}(t)\qquad\forall\,t\in(0,1).

Baker and Kong
[
6
]
verified (
1.2
) for the special case when
\beta
is the positive root of
x^{n+1}=x^{n}+x^{n-1}+\dots+x+1
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
. They also proved for such
\beta
the equality
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}=\mathscr{B}_{\beta}
. In our preprint
[
4
]
, we provide a proof of (
1.2
) for all
\beta\in(1,2]
, and show that, in constrast to the findings in
[
6
,
24
]
, the difference
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}\backslash\mathscr{B}_{\beta}
has positive Hausdorff dimension for Lebesgue-almost every
\beta>1
, but can also have any finite cardinality or be countably infinite, depending on
\beta
. Using the extended Farey words introduced in this article, these results may be extended to all
\beta>1
; this will be done in a separate paper.

The doubling map with an arbitrary hole
(a,b)\subseteq[0,1)
was considered by Glendinning and Sidorov
[
17
]
, and their work was partially extended to general
\beta
-transformations by Clark
[
10
]
. For
\beta\in(1,2]
, the symbolic survivor set corresponding to
K_{\beta}(t)
, which we introduce in Section
6
, can be put roughly in correspondence with the survivor set
K_{2}(a,b)
for the doubling map with a suitable choice of hole
(a,b)
; hence there is a dictionary between our results from
[
3
]
and those of Glendinning and Sidorov. Details of this relationship may be found in
[
4
]
. However, this correspondence breaks down for
\beta>2
: The symbolic dynamics of the “
k
-transformation”
T_{k}(x):=kx\mod 1
with an arbitrary hole for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 3}
, investigated by Agarwal
[
1
]
, are fundamentally different from those of the survivor set
K_{\beta}(t)
for
k-1<\beta\leq k
(even though the same alphabet is concerned), and there appears to be no direct link between the two problems.

The mathematical study of open dynamical systems, or dynamical systems with holes, was first proposed by Pianigiani and Yorke
[
20
]
. Since then, numerous papers and books have been written on the subject. For instance, Urbański
[
23
]
considered
C^{2}
-expanding, orientation-preserving circle maps with a hole
(0,t)
, and Dettmann
[
14
]
looked at asymptotics of circle maps with small holes. Besides the survivor set, another important object of study is the
escape rate
, i.e. the rate at which orbits of points enter the hole. Bunimovich and Yurchenko
[
8
]
showed that in general, the escape rate depends not only on the size, but also the position of the hole. That this is also true for the size of the survivor set follows from the work of Glendinning and Sidorov
[
17
]
. Other relevant works, some of which also address applications to billiards and mathematical physics, are the articles by Demers and Young
[
13
]
, Demers, Wright and Young
[
12
]
and Bruin, Demers and Todd
[
7
]
. The list is far from complete, of course. For a good overview of the subject, we refer to Chapter 8 of the book by Collet, Martínez and San Martín
[
11
]
.

Organization of the paper

This article is organized as follows. Section

, whose interiors collectively make up the set

. We decompose the complement of

, a countable collection of

and an

. Theorem

states that all these sets are small in terms of dimension. Theorem

then gives explicit descriptions for the critical value
\tau(\beta)
, depending on whether
\beta
lies in a basic interval, in

, in some

, or in
E_{\infty}
. Theorem

, whose somewhat tedious proof is essentially the same as in
[
3
]
and is therefore omitted, identifies the discontinuity points of

.

Before proving the main results, we summarize known facts about greedy and quasi-greedy expansions in Section

. Theorems

and

are proved in Sections
5
and
6
, respectively. Finally, Section

is devoted to the proof of Theorem

.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Greedy and quasi-greedy expansions

We begin by introducing important notation. First, for each
\beta>1
, we use the alphabet
A_{\beta}:=\{0,1,\dots,\lceil\beta\rceil-1\}
. Note that the alphabet depends on
\beta
: for instance, for
\beta\in(1,2]
we have
A_{\beta}=\{0,1\}
whereas for
\beta\in(2,3]
we have
A_{\beta}=\{0,1,2\}
. For a number
x\in[0,1)
,
b(x,\beta)
will denote the
greedy expansion
of
x
in base
\beta
over the alphabet
A_{\beta}
, defined as the lexicographically largest sequence
(c_{i})\in A_{\beta}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that

(2.1)

\pi_{\beta}((c_{i})):=((c_{i}))_{\beta}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{i}}{\beta^{i}}=x.

Likewise, for
x\in[0,1]
,
a(x,\beta)
denotes the
quasi-greedy expansion
of
x
in base
\beta
over
A_{\beta}
, defined as the lexicographically largest sequence
(c_{i})\in A_{\beta}^{\mathbb{N}}
that does not end in
0^{\infty}
and satisfies (
2.1
). We write
\alpha(\beta):=a(1,\beta)
. Note that
a(x,\beta)=b(x,\beta)
for all but countably many
x
.

2.2.
Farey words and extended Farey words

Throughout the paper, we use the following notation. First, for any finite word
\mathbf{w}=w_{1}\dots w_{m}
we write
\mathbf{w}^{+}:=w_{1}\dots w_{m-1}(w_{m}+1)
, and if
w_{m}>0
we write similarly
\mathbf{w}^{-}:=w_{1}\dots w_{m-1}(w_{m}-1)
. When
m=1
and
\mathbf{w}=w
, these definitions should be read as
\mathbf{w}^{+}=w+1
and
\mathbf{w}^{-}=w-1
. Furthermore, we denote by
\mathbb{L}(\mathbf{w})
the lexicographically largest cyclical permutation of
\mathbf{w}
, so for instance,
\mathbb{L}(10210)=21010
.

We next introduce the Farey words. First we recursively define a sequence of ordered sets
F_{n},n=0,1,2,\ldots
. Let
F_{0}=(0,1)
; and for
n\geq 0
the ordered set
F_{n+1}=(\mathbf{v}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{2^{n+1}+1})
is obtained from
F_{n}=(\mathbf{w}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{w}_{2^{n}+1})
by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbf{v}_{2i-1}=\mathbf{w}_{i}&\textrm{for}&1\leq i\leq 2^{n}+1,\\
\mathbf{v}_{2i}=\mathbf{w}_{i}\mathbf{w}_{i+1}&\textrm{for}&1\leq i\leq 2^{n}.\end{array}\right.

In other words,
F_{n+1}
is obtained from
F_{n}
by inserting for each
1\leq j\leq 2^{n}
the new word
\mathbf{w}_{j}\mathbf{w}_{j+1}
between the two neighboring words
\mathbf{w}_{j}
and
\mathbf{w}_{j+1}
. So,

\begin{split}&F_{1}=(0,01,1),\qquad F_{2}=(0,001,01,011,1),\\
F_{3}&=(0,0001,001,00101,01,01011,011,0111,1),\quad\dots\end{split}

Note that for each
n\geq 0
the ordered set
F_{n}
consists of
2^{n}+1
words which are listed from the left to the right in lexicographically increasing order.
We call
\mathbf{w}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{*}
a
Farey word
if
\mathbf{w}\in F_{n}
for some
n\geq 0
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}:=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}F_{n}
the set of all Farey words, and by
\mathcal{F}^{*}:=\mathcal{F}\backslash\{0,1\}
the set of all Farey words of length at least two. Finally, we set

\hat{\mathcal{F}}:=\mathcal{F}^{*}\cup\{1\}.

For analyzing the case
\beta\geq 2
, the extra Farey word 1 will play a critical role.

Next, we define a map
\theta
on finite words and infinite sequences that simply increments each coordinate by 1. Thus,

(2.2)

\theta(c_{1},\dots,c_{n}):=(c_{1}+1,\dots,c_{n}+1),\qquad\theta(c_{1},c_{2},\dots):=(c_{1}+1,c_{2}+1,\dots).

We define the
extended Farey set

\mathcal{F}_{e}
by

\mathcal{F}_{e}:=\{\theta^{k}(\mathbf{w}):\mathbf{w}\in\hat{\mathcal{F}},k=0,1,2,\dots\}.

Thus,
\mathcal{F}_{e}
contains all the Farey words except
0
, and in addition, it contains all words derived from such Farey words by incrementing all digits by the same amount. For example, applying
\theta
repeatedly to the Farey word
001
yields the words
112,223,334,\dots
in
\mathcal{F}_{e}
.

The following property is what makes the (extended) Farey words special (see, e.g.,
[
9
, Proposition 2.5]
):

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{s}}=s_{1}\ldots s_{m}\in\mathcal{F}_{e}
. Then

(i)

\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}})=s_{m}s_{m-1}\ldots s_{1}
.

(ii)

{{\mathbf{s}}^{-}}
is a palindrome; that is
s_{1}\ldots s_{m-1}(s_{m}-1)=(s_{m}-1)s_{m-1}s_{m-2}\ldots s_{1}
.

2.3.
The substitution operator

We now recall the substitution operator
\bullet
from
[
3
]
. Say a word
\mathbf{w}
is
Lyndon
if it is aperiodic and
\mathbf{w}
is the lexicographically smallest among all its cyclical permutations. In particular, any word consisting of a single digit is Lyndon. We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{e}
the set of all Lyndon words except
0
, and by
\mathcal{L}^{*}
the set of all Lyndon words in
\{0,1\}^{*}
of length at least 2. Then
\mathcal{F}^{*}\subset\mathcal{L}^{*}\subset\mathcal{L}_{e}
,
\mathcal{F}_{e}\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{e}
, and words in
\mathcal{L}_{e}
never end in the digit
0
.

Definition 2.2
.

For a Lyndon word
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathcal{L}_{e}
, we define the substitution map
\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}:\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\{\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
by

(2.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}(0^{k_{1}}1^{l_{1}}0^{k_{2}}1^{l_{2}}\dots)={\mathbf{s}}^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}})^{k_{1}-1}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}})^{+}{\mathbf{s}}^{l_{1}-1}{\mathbf{s}}^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}})^{k_{2}-1}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}})^{+}{\mathbf{s}}^{l_{2}-1}\dots,\\

where
1\leq k_{i},l_{i}\leq\infty
for all

.
We allow one of the exponents
k_{i}
or

to take the value

Example 2.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{s}}=01

. Then
{\mathbf{s}}^{-}=00

\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}})=10
, and

Note that we have not defined expressions such as

The next lemma collects some useful properties of the map
\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}
and the substitution operator
\bullet
. For the proofs, see
[

. Although the original proofs were given only for Lyndon words
{\mathbf{s}}
in
\{0,1\}^{*}
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathcal{L}^{*}
, it is easy to verify that all properties extend to
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathcal{L}_{e}
. In particular, the last property says that
\bullet
is associative. In the lemma and throughout the paper, the symbols
\prec
,
\succ
,
\preccurlyeq
and
\succcurlyeq
are used to indicate the lexicographical order on words and sequences, defined in the usual way.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathcal{L}_{e}
.

(i)

The map
\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}
is strictly increasing on
\left\{0,1\right\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

(ii)

For any word
{\mathbf{d}}=d_{1}\ldots d_{k}\in\{0,1\}^{*}
with
k\geq 2
, we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}({\mathbf{d}}^{-})=\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}({\mathbf{d}})^{-}&\textrm{if}&d_{k}=1,\\
\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}({\mathbf{d}}^{+})=\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}({\mathbf{d}})^{+}&\textrm{if}&d_{k}=0.\end{array}\right.

(iii)

For any two sequences
{\mathbf{c}},{\mathbf{d}}\in\left\{0,1\right\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we have the equivalences

\sigma^{n}({\mathbf{c}})\prec{\mathbf{d}}~\forall n\geq 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sigma^{n}(\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}({\mathbf{c}}))\prec\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}({\mathbf{d}})~\forall n\geq 0

and

\sigma^{n}({\mathbf{c}})\succ{\mathbf{d}}~\forall n\geq 0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sigma^{n}(\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}({\mathbf{c}}))\succ\Phi_{\mathbf{s}}({\mathbf{d}})~\forall n\geq 0.

(iv)

For any
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{L}^{*}
, we have
{\mathbf{s}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{L}_{e}
and
\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}})={\mathbf{s}}\bullet\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{r}})
.

(v)

For any
{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{L}_{e}
and
{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{t}}\in\mathcal{L}^{*}
, we have
({\mathbf{r}}\bullet{\mathbf{s}})\bullet{\mathbf{t}}={\mathbf{r}}\bullet({\mathbf{s}}\bullet{\mathbf{t}})
.

We now define the sets

\Lambda_{k}:=\{{\mathbf{s}}_{1}\bullet{\mathbf{s}}_{2}\bullet\dots\bullet{\mathbf{s}}_{k}:{\mathbf{s}}_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{e},{\mathbf{s}}_{2},\dots,s_{k}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}\},\qquad k=1,2,3,\dots,

and

(2.5)

4.
Properties of greedy and quasi-greedy expansions

We collect here some well-known characterizations and continuity properties of the greedy and quasi-greedy expansions. Since we are not working with a fixed alphabet, however, some extra care is needed with their statements.
The following property of
\alpha(\beta)
is well known (cf.
[
5
]

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

.

(i)

The restriction of the map
\beta\mapsto\alpha(\beta)
to
(k,k+1]
is an increasing bijection from
\beta\in(k,k+1]
to the set of sequences
\mathbf{a}=a_{1}a_{2}\dots\in\left\{0,1,\dots,k\right\}^{\mathbb{N}}
not ending with
0^{\infty}
such that

and

\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{a})\preccurlyeq\mathbf{a}\quad\forall n\geq 0.

(ii)

The map
\beta\mapsto\alpha(\beta)
is left continuous everywhere on
(k,k+1]
with respect to the order topology, and it is right continuous at
\beta_{0}\in(k,k+1)
if and only if
\alpha(\beta_{0})
is not periodic. Furthermore, if
\alpha(\beta_{0})=(a_{1}\ldots a_{m})^{\infty}
with minimal period
m
, then
\alpha(\beta)\searrow a_{1}\ldots a_{m}^{+}0^{\infty}
as
\beta\searrow\beta_{0}
.

The following result for greedy expansions was established by Parry

19

and de Vries and Komornik

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

. The map

is an increasing bijection from

to the set

\big\{\mathbf{z}\in A_{\beta}^{\mathbb{N}}:\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{z})\prec\alpha(\beta)~\forall n\geq 0\big\}.

Furthermore,

(i)

The map

is right-continuous everywhere in
[0,1)
with respect to the order topology in

;

(ii)

If

does not end with

, then the map
t\mapsto b(t,\beta)
is continuous at
t_{0}
;

(iii)

If

with

, then
b(t,\beta)\nearrow b_{1}\ldots b_{m}^{-}\alpha(\beta)
as

.

Lemma

has the following analogue for quasi-greedy expansions.

Lemma 4.3

Let
\beta>1
. The map

is an increasing bijection from

\big\{\mathbf{z}\in A_{\beta}^{\mathbb{N}}:\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{z})\preccurlyeq\alpha(\beta)~\forall n\geq 0\big\}.

Remark 6.1
.

For
{\mathbf{S}}\in\Lambda^{*}
, (ii) and (iii) were proved, respectively, in Proposition 5.1 and Remark 5.2 of

.

Recall that the survivor set
K_{\beta}(t)
consists of all
x\in[0,1)
whose orbit
\{T^{n}_{\beta}(x):n\geq 0\}
avoids the hole
[0,t)
. To describe the dimension of
K_{\beta}(t)
we introduce the topological entropy of a symbolic set. For a subset
X\subseteq\left\{0,1\right\}^{\mathbb{N}}
its
topological entropy

h_{top}(X)
is defined by

h_{top}(X):=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log N_{n}(X)}{n},

where
N_{n}(X)
denotes the number of words of length
n
occurring in sequences of
X
. The following result can be deduced from Raith
[
21
]
(see also
[
18
]
for the case
\beta\in(1,2]
).

Lemma 6.2
.

Given
\beta>1
and
t\in[0,1)
, the Hausdorff dimension of
K_{\beta}(t)
is given by

\dim_{H}K_{\beta}(t)=\frac{h_{top}(\mathbf{K}_{\beta}(t))}{\log\beta},

where

\mathbf{K}_{\beta}(t):=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in A_{\beta}^{\mathbb{N}}:b(t,\beta)\preccurlyeq\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{z})\prec\alpha(\beta)\ \forall n\geq 0\right\}.

Thus, it is sufficient to study the size of the symbolic survivor set
\mathbf{K}_{\beta}(t)
. The following lemma is crucial in this respect:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{S}}\in\Lambda
, and let
M\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
J^{\mathbf{S}}\subseteq[M,M+1)
. Then the set

(6.1)

\Gamma({\mathbf{S}}):=\{\mathbf{z}\in\{0,1,\dots,M\}^{\mathbb{N}}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\infty}\preccurlyeq\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{z})\preccurlyeq\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{S}})^{\infty}\ \forall n\geq 0\}

is countable.

This was first proved in
[
3
, Proposition 4.1]
for the case when
{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{s}}_{1}\bullet\dots\bullet{\mathbf{s}}_{n}
with each
{\mathbf{s}}_{i}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
(corresponding to

). A much shorter proof can be found in
[
4
, Lemma 7.3]
. The proof goes through without change when
{\mathbf{s}}_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{e}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
(i).

The proof is mostly the same as that of
[
3
, Theorem 2]
, so we only give a sketch.
Fix a basic interval
I^{\mathbf{S}}=[\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{S}},\beta_{*}^{\mathbf{S}}]
.
Take
\beta\in I^{\mathbf{S}}
, and let
t^{*}=({\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{\infty})_{\beta}
, where
{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{S}})=:a_{1}\ldots a_{m}
. Then

(6.2)

{\mathbf{A}}^{\infty}=\alpha(\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{S}})\preccurlyeq\alpha(\beta)\preccurlyeq\alpha(\beta_{*}^{\mathbf{S}})={\mathbf{A}}^{+}{\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{\infty}.

First we prove
\tau(\beta)\geq t^{*}
. We consider two cases: (a)
|{\mathbf{S}}|\geq 2
, and (b)
|{\mathbf{S}}|=1
.

(a) Suppose first that
|{\mathbf{S}}|=m\geq 2
. Let
j<m
be the integer such that
{\mathbf{S}}=a_{j+1}\ldots a_{m}a_{1}\ldots a_{j}
, and for
N\in\mathbb{N}
define
t_{N}:=\big(({\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{N}a_{1}\ldots a_{j})^{\infty}\big)_{\beta}
. It is not hard to verify that

\sigma^{n}\big(({\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{N}a_{1}\ldots a_{j})^{\infty}\big)=\sigma^{n}\left({\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{N+1}(a_{1}\ldots a_{j}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{N+1})^{\infty}\right)\prec{\mathbf{A}}^{\infty}\preccurlyeq\alpha(\beta)\qquad\forall n\geq 0.

So, by Lemma
4.2
we have
b(t_{N},\beta)=({\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{N}a_{1}\ldots a_{j})^{\infty}
. This implies that

\left\{{\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{N+1}a_{1}\ldots a_{j},{\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{N+2}a_{1}\ldots a_{j}\right\}^{\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbf{K}_{\beta}(t_{N}).

Hence,
h_{top}(\mathbf{K}_{\beta}(t_{N}))>0
, which by Lemma
6.2
implies that
\dim_{H}K_{\beta}(t_{N})>0
. Thus
\tau(\beta)\geq t_{N}
for all

. Since
t_{N}\nearrow t^{*}
as

, we conclude that
\tau(\beta)\geq t^{*}
.

(b) If
|{\mathbf{S}}|=1
, then
{\mathbf{S}}={\mathbf{A}}=k
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Without loss of generality we take
k=1
; the proof for bigger
k
is the same up to a renaming of the digits. Here
\alpha(\beta)\succcurlyeq 1^{\infty}
, and
{\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{\infty}=01^{\infty}
. Put
t_{N}:=((01^{N})^{\infty})_{\beta}
. Clearly,
b(t_{N},\beta)=(01^{N})^{\infty}
. Furthermore,
\mathbf{K}_{\beta}(t_{N})\supseteq\{01^{N+1},01^{N+2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, so
h_{top}(\mathbf{K}_{\beta}(t_{N}))>0
. We conclude as above.

Next we prove
\tau(\beta)\leq t^{*}
. From (

) we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\beta}(t^{*})

\displaystyle\subseteq\left\{\mathbf{z}\in A_{\beta}:{\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{\infty}\preccurlyeq\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{z})\prec{\mathbf{A}}^{+}{\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{\infty}~\forall n\geq 0\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in A_{\beta}:{\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{\infty}\preccurlyeq\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{z})\preccurlyeq{\mathbf{A}}^{\infty}~\forall n\geq 0\right\}=:\Gamma.

By Lemma
6.3
, the set
\Gamma({\mathbf{S}})=\left\{\mathbf{z}\in A_{\beta}:{\mathbf{S}}^{\infty}\preccurlyeq\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{z})\preccurlyeq{\mathbf{A}}^{\infty}~\forall n\geq 0\right\}
is countable. Since any sequence in

must end with
{\mathbf{S}}^{-}{\mathbf{A}}^{\infty}
,

is also countable, and so is
\mathbf{K}_{\beta}(t^{*})

\tau(\beta)\leq t^{*}
.
∎

For the next lemma, recall that
a(t,\beta)
and
b(t,\beta)
denote the quasi-greedy and greedy expansions of

in base
\beta
, respectively.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
, and
M<\beta<\beta^{\prime}\leq M+1
. Then:

(i)

a(\tau(\beta),\beta))\preccurlyeq{a(\tau(\beta^{\prime}),\beta^{\prime})}
;

(ii)

If
b(\tau(\beta),\beta)
is infinite, then also
b(\tau(\beta),\beta))\preccurlyeq b(\tau(\beta^{\prime}),\beta^{\prime})
.

Proof.

(i) Observe first that, since
\beta<\beta^{\prime}
,
a(\tau(\beta),\beta)
is in fact the quasi-greedy
\beta^{\prime}
-expansion of
(a(\tau(\beta),\beta))_{\beta^{\prime}}
. Furthermore, by Lemma
4.3
the map
t\mapsto a(t,\beta^{\prime})
is strictly increasing. Since
a(t,\beta^{\prime})=b(t,\beta^{\prime})
for all but countably many
t
, there is for each
\varepsilon>0
a number
t
with

(a(\tau(\beta),\beta))_{\beta^{\prime}}-\varepsilon<t<(a(\tau(\beta),\beta))_{\beta^{\prime}}

such that
b(t,\beta^{\prime})=a(t,\beta^{\prime})\prec a(\tau(\beta),\beta)
. Since the map
t\mapsto a(t,\beta)
is in addition left-continuous by Lemma
4.3
, there exists a point
t_{1}<\tau(\beta)
such that

b(t,\beta^{\prime})=a(t,\beta^{\prime})\prec b(t_{1},\beta)=a(t_{1},\beta)\prec a(\tau(\beta),\beta).

We then have (noting that
A_{\beta}=A_{\beta^{\prime}}=\{0,1,\dots,M\}
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{\beta^{\prime}}(t)

\displaystyle=\{\mathbf{z}\in A_{\beta^{\prime}}^{\mathbb{N}}:b(t,\beta^{\prime})\preccurlyeq\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{z})\prec\alpha(\beta^{\prime})\ \forall n\geq 0\}

\displaystyle\supseteq\{\mathbf{z}\in A_{\beta}^{\mathbb{N}}:b(t_{1},\beta)\preccurlyeq\sigma^{n}(\mathbf{z})\prec\alpha(\beta)\ \forall n\geq 0\}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\beta}(t_{1}).

Since
t_{1}<\tau(\beta)
, this shows, via Lemma
6.2
, that
\dim_{H}K_{\beta^{\prime}}(t)>0
. Hence
\tau(\beta^{\prime})\geq t
. Letting

. Therefore,

.

(ii) Since
b(\tau(\beta),\beta)
is infinite, the map
t\mapsto b(t,\beta)
is continuous at

. Thus, if

.

b(\tau(\beta),\beta)\preccurlyeq{\mathbf{s}}0^{\infty}
. As a result,

\tau(\beta)\leq({\mathbf{s}}0^{\infty})_{\beta}=(\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}})^{+}0^{\infty})_{\beta}-\frac{1}{\beta}<1-\frac{1}{\beta},

where the equality follows from Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
(iii).

Let
\beta\in E^{\mathbf{S}}
. Since
\dim_{B}E^{\mathbf{S}}=0
, the intervals
J^{{\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}}:{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathcal{F}^{*}
are dense in
J^{\mathbf{S}}\backslash I^{\mathbf{S}}
, hence there is a sequence
({\mathbf{r}}_{n})
of Farey words such that
\beta_{*}^{{\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}_{n}}\nearrow\beta
. Note that

\alpha\big(\beta_{*}^{{\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}_{n}}\big)=\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{+}({\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{\infty}=\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}\left(\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{+}{\mathbf{r}}_{n}^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{\infty}\right),

and by Theorem
3.4
(i),

b\left(\tau(\beta_{*}^{{\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}_{n}}),\beta_{*}^{{\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}_{n}}\right)=({\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{\infty}=\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}({\mathbf{r}}_{n}^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{\infty}).

Therefore, by Lemma
6.4
(ii),

(6.5)

b(\tau(\beta),\beta)\succcurlyeq\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}({\mathbf{r}}_{n}^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{\infty}).

Next, we claim that
\alpha(\beta)
is aperiodic. If
\beta<\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{S}}
, then
\hat{\beta}=\Psi_{\mathbf{S}}^{-1}(\beta)\in E
by (
5.2
). Thus
\alpha(\hat{\beta})
is aperiodic, and so
\alpha(\beta)=\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}(\alpha(\hat{\beta}))
is aperiodic as well. Otherwise, if
\beta=\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{S}}
, then
\alpha(\beta)=\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{S}})^{+}{\mathbf{S}}^{\infty}
, which is clearly aperiodic. The aperiodicity of
\alpha(\beta)
implies that the map
\gamma\mapsto\alpha(\gamma)
is continuous at
\beta
. Hence, we have, by the continuity of
\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}(1\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}(\alpha(\hat{\beta}))=\alpha(\beta)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha(\beta_{*}^{{\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}_{n}})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}\big(\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{+}{\mathbf{r}}_{n}^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{\infty}\big)=\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}\big(\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{+}{\mathbf{r}}_{n}^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{\infty}\big).

It follows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{+}{\mathbf{r}}_{n}^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{r}}_{n})^{\infty}=1\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots
. Since each
{\mathbf{r}}_{n}
is a Farey word and
|{\mathbf{r}}_{n}|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, this implies by Lemma
2.1
that
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbf{r}}_{n}=0\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots
. Hence, from (
6.5
) and the continuity of
\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}
we obtain

(6.6)

b(\tau(\beta),\beta)\succcurlyeq\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}(0\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots).

From here, we consider two cases:

Case 1.

\beta<\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{S}}
. Then
\hat{\beta}<2
, and so
0\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots
is a greedy
\hat{\beta}
-expansion by Lemma
4.2
, since
\sigma^{n}(0\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots)\prec\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\dots=\alpha(\hat{\beta})
for all
n\geq 0
, with strict inequality because
\alpha(\hat{\beta})
is not periodic. This implies
\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}(0\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots)
is also a greedy
\beta
-expansion. Hence, (
6.6
) implies

\tau(\beta)\geq\left(\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}(0\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots)\right)_{\beta}.

For the reverse inequality, note that there is a sequence
({\mathbf{r}}_{n}^{\prime})
of Farey words such that
\beta_{*}^{{\mathbf{S}}\bullet{\mathbf{r}}_{n}^{\prime}}\searrow\beta
. By the same reasoning as above but this time using Lemma
6.4
(i), we obtain

a(\tau(\beta),\beta)\preccurlyeq\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}(0\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots).

Letting
t^{*}:=\big(\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}(0\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots)\big)_{\beta}
, we have
a(t^{*},\beta)=b(t^{*},\beta)=\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}(0\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots)
. Hence
\tau(\beta)\leq t^{*}
.

Case 2.

\beta=\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{S}}
. Then
\hat{\beta}=2
, so
0\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots=01^{\infty}
, and (
6.6
) gives

b(\tau(\beta),\beta)\succcurlyeq\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}(01^{\infty})={\mathbf{S}}^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{S}})^{+}{\mathbf{S}}^{\infty}.

But since
\alpha(\beta)=\alpha(\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{S}})=\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{S}})^{+}{\mathbf{S}}^{\infty}
, this implies
b(\tau(\beta),\beta)\succcurlyeq{\mathbf{S}}0^{\infty}
. The reverse inequality follows from Lemma
6.6
. Thus,
b(\tau(\beta),\beta)={\mathbf{S}}0^{\infty}
and so

\tau(\beta)=({\mathbf{S}}0^{\infty})_{\beta}=\big({\mathbf{S}}^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{S}})^{+}{\mathbf{S}}^{\infty}\big)_{\beta}=\left(\Phi_{\mathbf{S}}(0\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}\dots)\right)_{\beta}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.4
(iv).

Put
{\mathbf{S}}_{n}:={\mathbf{s}}_{1}\bullet{\mathbf{s}}_{2}\bullet\dots\bullet{\mathbf{s}}_{n}
. Then
\beta\in J^{{\mathbf{S}}_{n}}
for each
n
, so by Lemma
6.6
,

{\mathbf{S}}_{n}^{-}\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{S}}_{n})^{\infty}\preccurlyeq b(\tau(\beta),\beta)\preccurlyeq{\mathbf{S}}_{n}0^{\infty}.

Letting
n\to\infty
gives
b(\tau(\beta),\beta)=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbf{S}}_{n}0^{\infty}
, from which the theorem follows.
∎

7.
The set-valued bifurcation set

Recall from the Introduction the
set-valued bifurcation set

\mathscr{E}_{\beta}:=\left\{t\in[0,1):K_{\beta}(t^{\prime})\neq K_{\beta}(t)~\forall t^{\prime}>t\right\}.

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
, we need a few lemmas.
The first one can be proved in the same way as
[
18
, Proposition 3.3]
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\beta>1
. If
t
is an isolated point of
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
, then its greedy expansion
b(t,\beta)
is periodic.

The next result can be deduced similarly to
[
18
, Proposition 3.10]
. Recall that for a Lyndon word
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathcal{L}_{e}
, the Lyndon interval generated by
{\mathbf{s}}
is
J^{{\mathbf{s}}}=[\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{s}},\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{s}}]
, where

\alpha(\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{s}})=\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}})^{\infty},\quad\alpha(\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{s}})=\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}})^{+}{\mathbf{s}}^{\infty}.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
J^{\mathbf{s}}=[\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{s}},\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{s}}]
be a Lyndon interval generated by
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathcal{L}_{e}
.

(i)

\sigma^{n}({\mathbf{s}}^{\infty})\prec\alpha(\beta)
for all
n\geq 0
if and only if
\beta>\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{s}}
;

(ii)

If
\beta\in(\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{s}},\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{s}}]
, then
({\mathbf{s}}^{\infty})_{\beta}
is isolated in
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
;

(iii)

If
\beta>\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{s}}
, then
({\mathbf{s}}^{\infty})_{\beta}
is not isolated in
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
.

Finally, we need the following property of
\alpha(\beta)
from
[
22
]
.

Lemma 7.3
.

For Lebesgue-almost every
\beta\in(1,\infty)
, the quasi-greedy expansion
\alpha(\beta)
contains arbitrarily long strings of consecutive zeros.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Statement (i) is proved in
[
4
, Lemma 3.1]
.
Statement (ii) follows by an easy adaptation of the proof of
[
18
, Proposition 2.7]
. In fact, the argument in that proof shows that
\dim_{H}(\mathscr{E}_{\beta}\cap[0,\delta])=1
for any
\delta>0
and
\beta>1
. This implies that
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
contains infinitely many accumulation points arbitrarily close to zero for every
\beta>1
. Hence, to prove (iii) it suffices to show that
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
contains infinitely many isolated points in any neighborhood of
0
for Lebesgue-almost all
\beta>1
.

Take
\beta>1
such that
\alpha(\beta)
contains arbitrarily long strings of consecutive zeros. By Lemma
7.3
it suffices to prove that
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
contains a sequence of isolated points decreasing to zero. Write

(7.1)

\alpha(\beta)={\mathbf{b}}_{1}0^{m_{1}}{\mathbf{b}}_{2}0^{m_{2}}\cdots{\mathbf{b}}_{k}0^{m_{k}}{\mathbf{b}}_{k+1}0^{m_{k+1}}\cdots,

where each
{\mathbf{b}}_{i}
contains no digit
0
, and each
m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}=\left\{1,2,\ldots\right\}
. Since
\alpha(\beta)
contains arbitrarily long strings of consecutive zeros, we have
\sup_{k}m_{k}=+\infty
.

Set
i_{0}=1
, and let
i_{1}>i_{0}
be the smallest index such that
m_{i_{1}}>m_{1}
. Then
m_{i_{1}}>m_{j}
for all
j<i_{1}
. Set

\mathbf{a}_{1}:={\mathbf{b}}_{1}0^{m_{1}}\ldots{\mathbf{b}}_{i_{1}}^{-},

and let
{\mathbf{s}}_{1}:=\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{a}_{1}):=
the lexicographically smallest cyclical permutation of
\mathbf{a}_{1}
. Since
\sigma^{n}(\alpha(\beta))\preccurlyeq\alpha(\beta)
for all
n\geq 0
, it follows that
\mathbf{a}_{1}
is not periodic, and so
{\mathbf{s}}_{1}
is Lyndon. Furthermore, by the definition of
i_{1}
it follows that
{\mathbf{s}}_{1}
begins with
0^{m_{1}}d_{1}
for some digit
d_{1}>0
. We claim that

\beta_{\ell}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{1}}<\beta<\beta_{r}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{1}},

where
[\beta_{\ell}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{1}},\beta_{r}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{1}}]
is the Lyndon interval generated by
{\mathbf{s}}_{1}
.
Note that
\alpha(\beta_{\ell}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{1}})=\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}}_{1})^{\infty}=\mathbf{a}_{1}^{\infty}
and
\alpha(\beta_{r}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{1}})=\mathbb{L}({\mathbf{s}}_{1})^{+}{\mathbf{s}}_{1}^{\infty}=\mathbf{a}_{1}^{+}{\mathbf{s}}_{1}^{\infty}
. By (
7.1
) and the fact that
m_{i_{1}}>m_{1}
it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}_{1}^{\infty}=({\mathbf{b}}_{1}0^{m_{1}}\ldots{\mathbf{b}}_{i_{1}}^{-})^{\infty}\prec\alpha(\beta)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{b}}_{1}0^{m_{1}}\ldots{\mathbf{b}}_{i_{1}}0^{m_{i_{1}}}\ldots

\displaystyle\prec{\mathbf{b}}_{1}0^{m_{1}}\ldots{\mathbf{b}}_{i_{1}}0^{m_{1}}10^{\infty}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{a}_{1}^{+}{\mathbf{s}}_{1}^{\infty}.

Thus,
\beta\in(\beta_{\ell}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{1}},\beta_{r}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{1}})
by Lemma
4.1
, establishing the claim. By Lemma
7.2
(ii) it follows that
({\mathbf{s}}_{1}^{\infty})_{\beta}
is isolated in
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
.

Proceeding inductively, suppose
i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}
have been chosen. Let
i_{k}>i_{k-1}
be the smallest index such that
m_{i_{k}}>m_{i_{k-1}}
. By induction,
m_{i_{k}}>m_{j}
for all
j<i_{k}
. Set
\mathbf{a}_{k}={\mathbf{b}}_{1}0^{m_{1}}\ldots{\mathbf{b}}_{i_{k}}^{-}
, and
{\mathbf{s}}_{k}:=\mathbb{S}(\mathbf{a}_{k})
. Then
{\mathbf{s}}_{k}
is Lyndon and begins with
0^{m_{i_{k-1}}}d_{k}
for some digit
d_{k}>0
. By the same argument as above we can show that
\beta\in(\beta_{\ell}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{k}},\beta_{r}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{k}})
, and thus
({\mathbf{s}}_{k}^{\infty})_{\beta}
is isolated in
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
.

This way we construct a sequence of Lyndon words
{\mathbf{s}}_{k}
,
k\geq 1
, such that
\beta\in(\beta_{\ell}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{k}},\beta_{r}^{{\mathbf{s}}_{k}})
and
({\mathbf{s}}_{k}^{\infty})_{\beta}
is isolated in
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
for all
k\geq 1
. Since
{\mathbf{s}}_{k}
begins with
0^{m_{i_{k-1}}}
and
m_{i_{k-1}}\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
, it follows that
({\mathbf{s}}_{k}^{\infty})_{\beta}\searrow 0
as
k\to\infty
. This proves (iii).

Finally we prove (iv). We will show that
{\mathscr{E}_{\beta}}
contains no isolated points if and only if
\beta\in E_{L}:=E\cup\left\{\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{s}}:{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathcal{F}_{e}\right\}
.
If
\beta\in(1,\infty)\setminus E_{L}
, then
\beta\in(\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{s}},\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{s}}]
for some
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathcal{F}_{e}
, so Lemma
7.2
(ii) implies that
({\mathbf{s}}^{\infty})_{\beta}
is an isolated point of
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
.

Conversely, take
\beta>1
and suppose
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
contains an isolated point
t
. Then by Lemma
7.1
the greedy expansion
b(t,\beta)
is periodic, say
b(t,\beta)=(b_{1}\ldots b_{m})^{\infty}
with
m
the minimal period. Since
t\in\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
, it follows from part (i) of the theorem and Lemma
4.2
that

(7.2)

(b_{1}\ldots b_{m})^{\infty}\preccurlyeq\sigma^{n}((b_{1}\ldots b_{m})^{\infty})\prec\alpha(\beta)\quad\textrm{for all }n\geq 0.

In particular,
{\mathbf{b}}:=b_{1}\ldots b_{m}
is a Lyndon word, hence it generates a Lyndon interval
J^{\mathbf{b}}=[\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{b}},\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{b}}]
. By assumption,
t=({\mathbf{b}}^{\infty})_{\beta}
is isolated in
\mathscr{E}_{\beta}
. Then by (
7.2
) and Lemma
7.2
(i) and (iii), it follows that
\beta\in(\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{b}},\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{b}}]
. As in the last paragraph of the proof of Lemma
5.2
, we conclude that there exists an
{\mathbf{s}}\in\mathcal{F}_{e}
such that
\beta\in(\beta_{\ell}^{\mathbf{s}},\beta_{r}^{\mathbf{s}}]
. This means
\beta\notin E_{L}
, completing the proof.
∎
