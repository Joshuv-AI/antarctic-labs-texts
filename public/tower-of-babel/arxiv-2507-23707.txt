Cellular, Cell-less, and Everything in Between: A Unified Framework for Utility Region Analysis in Wireless Networks
R. L. G. Cavalcante, T. Piotrowski, S. Stanczak, † {\dagger} Fraunhofer Heirinch Hertz Institute, Germany Nicolaus Copernicus University, Poland ♢ {\diamondsuit} Technical University of Berlin, Germany

Abstract
We introduce a unified framework for analyzing utility regions of wireless networks, with a focus on the signal-to-interference-noise-ratio (SINR) and achievable rate regions. The framework provides valuable insights into interference patterns of modern network architectures, such as cell-less and extremely large MIMO networks, and it generalizes existing characterizations of the weak Pareto boundary. A central contribution is the derivation of sufficient conditions that guarantee convexity of the utility regions. Convexity is an important property because it ensures that time sharing (or user grouping) cannot simultaneously increase the utility of all users when the network operates on the weak Pareto boundary. These sufficient conditions also have two key implications. First, they identify a family of (weighted) sum-rate maximization problems that are inherently convex without any variable transformations, thus paving the way for the development of efficient, provably optimal solvers for this family. Second, they provide a rigorous justification for formulating sum-rate maximization problems directly in terms of achievable rates, rather than SINR levels. Our theoretical insights also motivate an alternative to the concept of favorable propagation in the massive MIMO literature – one that explicitly accounts for self-interference and the beamforming strategy.

Introduction

In wireless networks with users competing for shared resources, system designers must balance fairness with overall network efficiency. To this end, the performance of each user is evaluated via a utility function, and this function guides resource allocation schemes to achieve the above balance. One of the main advantages of the max-min fairness criterion is that it often leads to optimization problems that, although nonconvex in general, can be solved with efficient fixed point algorithms
[

While time sharing allows for greater flexibility in improving utilities, it also introduces a challenging combinatorial aspect to resource allocation. This aspect undermines one of the primary benefits of the max-min fairness criterion without time sharing: the availability of efficient algorithms able to compute global optima. In contrast, operating points that maximize the sum utility (typically the achievable rates) cannot be improved via time sharing. However, sum-rate maximization is NP-hard in general

As discussed in the next subsection, the above challenges are eliminated if the feasible utility region is convex. In this scenario, time sharing is unnecessary in max-min utility optimization problems, and fast iterative algorithms that provably converge to global optima can potentially be devised for sum-rate maximization problems. However, to date, existing methods for identifying convex utility regions or tractable sum-rate maximization problems rely on assumptions that are difficult to satisfy in practice. They include equal interference-plus-noise for all users in the network

I-B

Main contributions

The first objective of the present study is to derive conditions that guarantee convexity of the SINR region, with and without power constraints. We also prove that our sufficient conditions for convexity of the SINR region imply convexity of the achievable rate region obtained by treating interference as noise. Our results cover a range of resource allocation problems in a unified way, including those described in
[
6
, Ch. 3, Ch. 7]
for cell-less networks, in
[
7
, Ch. 5.3.2]

[
8
, Theorem 7.1]
for massive MIMO systems, and in
[
13
, Ch. 5.6.3]
for traditional wireless networks, among others.

As direct consequences of our results, we identify specific problem instances where sum-rate maximization considering modern interference models can be posed as a convex optimization problem without any changes in variables, and where the performance of network configurations obtained by solving max-min problems cannot be further enhanced through time sharing. We also provide a strong justification for using directly the rates as the optimization variables in sum-rate optimization problems, rather than SINR levels. In addition, we resolve a conjecture in
[
13
, Sect. 5.4.4]
[
24
]
, derive an alternative parametrization of the weak Pareto boundary of the SINR and rate regions in terms of the spectral radius of nonlinear mappings, and introduce a new concept of channel compatibility among users. Our notion of compatibility overcomes limitations of the standard definition of favorable propagation in the massive MIMO literature.

I-C

Relation to existing studies

Convexity of the SINR region for systems with affine interference functions has been studied in
[
13
, Ch. 5]
[

]
. In those studies, convexity is only established if the interference matrices described later in Sect.

Convexity of rate regions in massive MIMO systems without symmetry assumptions has been investigated in
[
25
]
, but, unlike our results, the analysis is restricted to small systems with two users and simple power constraints.

The results that follow are also related to those in

Instead, our focus is on establishing sufficient conditions for the convexity of the entire SINR and rate regions of these modern interference models. By doing so, we identify a particular class of problems where the sum-rate maximization can be posed as a convex optimization problem. Notably, we do not impose symmetry on the interference patterns

II-B

Relating the conditional eigenvalue of standard interference mappings to the spectral radius of general interference mappings

In many resource allocation problems, the question of whether given utilities (as described in Sect.
I-A
) can be satisfied with power constraints involving monotone norms can be answered by computing conditional eigenvalues of standard interference mappings
[
23
, Proposition 5]
. However, handling conditional eigenvalues can be cumbersome because we need to keep track of both the mapping and the norm. To avoid this issue, we begin by linking the conditional eigenvalue of standard interference mappings to the spectral radius of corresponding general interference mappings (Proposition
1
). We then proceed to establish many connections between the spectral radius of these general interference mappings and monotone norms, culminating in a key result (Proposition
3
) from which convexity of the SINR region in wireless networks emerges as a corollary. This result allows us to leverage a substantial body of linear algebra literature to gain deeper insights into interference patterns in wireless systems, as detailed in the next sections. Before deriving these results, we first need to impose additional structure on the standard interference mappings under consideration.

A well-known property that is widely used in the wireless literature is that taking the pointwise minimum of a finite family of standard interference functions results in a standard interference function
[
22
]
. However, this property does not necessarily hold for the pointwise infimum of an infinite family of standard interference functions. Nevertheless, in wireless systems, the families have additional structure. For example, they are typically uniformly bounded away from zero because of the presence of noise (see
[
19
, Eq. (32)]
), and in such cases we can prove that the pointwise infimum retains the properties of standard interference functions, as demonstrated in the next lemma for a particular family of standard interference functions (concrete examples in the wireless domain are shown later in Sect.
III
).

.

Let
\mathcal{Y}

(5)

Proof.

Proposition 2

\|\cdot\|

(i) Immediate from the positive homogeneity of the spectral radius.

. Similarly, let

. Since

{T_{\|\cdot\|}}

7

\displaystyle(\forall{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N})~\|{\bm{x}}\|_{T_{\|\cdot\|}}:=\inf\{\gamma>0\mid(1/\gamma){\bm{x}}~\in S_{T_{\|\cdot\|}}\},

The result in Proposition

Definition 5

Achievable rate and SINR regions in the absence of power constraints

To study the feasible SINR and rate regions of the wireless model introduced above, let us start by ignoring the presence of the power constraint
\mathcal{P}
to relax key assumptions made in

The equality in (

\displaystyle{\bm{p}}\in\mathrm{Fix}({\mathrm{diag}(\bm{s})}T):=\{{\bm{p}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{++}^{N}~|~{\mathrm{diag}(\bm{s})}T({\bm{p}})={\bm{p}}\},

\mathcal{S}=\{{\bm{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{++}^{N}\mid\mathrm{Fix}(\mathrm{diag}({\bm{s}})T)\neq\emptyset\}\\

{\bm{D}}=e^{\bm{R}}-{\bm{I}}

{\bm{D}}=\mathrm{diag}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{N})

Proposition 4
.

Assume that the function
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}:{\bm{s}}\mapsto\rho({\mathrm{diag}(\bm{s})}T_{\infty})
is convex for a given standard interference mapping
T:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{++}^{N}
. Then the function

f:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}:{\bm{r}}\mapsto\rho((e^{\mathrm{diag}(\bm{r})}-{\bm{I}})T_{\infty})

is also convex.

Proof.

Recall that
[\cdot]_{+}
is the operator that sets the negative components of a vector or matrix to zero. Consider the function

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcl}h:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}&\to&{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\\
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})&\mapsto&\rho([\mathrm{diag}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})]_{+}T_{\infty}),\end{array}

The results in Fact
7
in the Supplemental Material imply that, for any two general interference mappings
G_{1}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}
and
G_{2}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}
such that
(\forall{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N})~G_{1}({\bm{x}})\leq G_{2}({\bm{x}})
, we have
\rho(G_{1})\leq\rho(G_{2})
. Therefore,
h
is monotone in the sense that
(\forall{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N})(\forall{\bm{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N})~{\bm{x}}\leq{\bm{y}}\Rightarrow h({\bm{x}})\leq h({\bm{y}})
. To prove that
h
is also convex, choose two vectors
({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and a scalar
\alpha\in~]0,1[
arbitrarily. We establish convexity of
h
as follows:

h(\alpha{\bm{x}}_{1}+(1-\alpha){\bm{x}}_{2})\leq h(\alpha[{\bm{x}}_{1}]_{+}+(1-\alpha)[{\bm{x}}_{2}]_{+})\\
\leq\alpha h([{\bm{x}}_{1}]_{+})+(1-\alpha)h([{\bm{x}}_{2}]_{+})=\alpha h({\bm{x}}_{1})+(1-\alpha)h({\bm{x}}_{2}),

(24)

where the first inequality is a consequence of monotonicity of
h
, and the second inequality follows from the assumption of convexity of
{\bm{s}}\mapsto\rho({\mathrm{diag}(\bm{s})}T_{\infty})
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}
. Therefore, we deduce from
[
40
, p. 86]
[
41

that the function
h\circ g:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, where

g:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{N}:(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\mapsto(e^{x_{1}}-1,\ldots,e^{x_{N}}-1),

is also convex because
h\circ g
is the composition of the monotonic convex function
h
and the function
g
, which is convex in each coordinate. Convexity of
h\circ g
implies convexity of the function
f
because
f
is the restriction of the convex function
h\circ g
to the nonnegative orthant.
∎

III-B

Achievable rate and SINR regions with power constraints

In the previous section, we discussed existing results proving that there exists at most one power vector

able to provide a given SINR requirement

20

of positive achievable SINR values with power constraints, we have to remove from the set

in (

corresponding to feasible SINR levels obtained with a power profile

, where
\mathcal{P}
is defined in (
17

\mathrm{Fix}({\mathrm{diag}(\bm{s})}T)
is nonempty is insufficient. If

{\bm{s}}

{\bm{p}}^{\star}\in\mathcal{P}

10
in the Supplemental Material we know that
{\bm{p}}^{\star}\in\mathrm{Fix}({\mathrm{diag}(\bm{s})}T)\neq\emptyset

and
\|\cdot\|

(25)

In turn, the set of achievable rates obtained by treating interference as noise is given by:

\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}:=\{{\bm{r}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{++}^{N}\mid\rho((e^{\mathrm{diag}(\bm{r})}-{\bm{I}}){T_{\|\cdot\|}})\leq 1\}.

(26)

We can now obtain a parametrization of the weak Pareto boundary of the SINR and rate regions in terms of the spectral radius of the general interference mapping

Proposition 5
.

Let
{\bm{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{++}^{N}

{\bm{s}}
lies on the weak Pareto boundary of

in (

\rho((e^{{\mathrm{diag}(\bm{r})}}-{\bm{I}}){T_{\|\cdot\|}})=1

Proof.

In this proof, we only consider the set

because the proof for the set

follows from the standard bijection
\text{rate}=\log(1+\text{SINR})

2
(ii) we know that

Remark 3

With

IV-A

Impact of self-interference on the convexity of the feasible rate and SINR regions

The diagonal entries of the interference matrix
{\bm{M}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N\times N}
defined in Example
4
corresponds to the so-called
self-interference
, which in many models in the massive MIMO and cell-less literature is caused by the lack of perfect channel state information. For Remark
2
to be applicable, self-interference must exist in all communication links
n\in\mathcal{N}
. The reason is that a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for
{\bm{M}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N\times N}
to be an inverse Z-matrix (or, equivalently, an inverse M-matrix, as mentioned above) is that all diagonal entries must be positive
[
30
, Theorem 2.9]
.

In contrast, Corollary
2
may be applicable even in the absence of self-interference in some links because of the presence of the additive matrices
({\bm{u}}{\bm{a}}_{n}^{t})_{n\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}
. However, their presence does not guarantee that
({\bm{M}}+{\bm{u}}{\bm{a}}_{n}^{t})_{n\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}
are inverse Z-matrices, which is the main assumption required in Corollary
2
. Nevertheless, as self-interference increases in every link, all feasible SINR and rate regions eventually become convex under the practical assumption that all links cause interference to each other, as proved below:

Corollary 3
.

Assume that the interference matrix
{\bm{M}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N\times N}
in Example
4
has positive off-diagonal entries, and it is replaced by
{\bm{S}}+{\bm{M}}
, where
{\bm{S}}\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{N\times N}
models the addition of self-interference to the system. Then there exists a nonnegative diagonal matrix
{\bm{S}}_{\mathrm{min}}\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{N\times N}
such that the sets
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{P}}
,
\mathcal{R}
, and
\overline{\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}}
are convex for every
{\bm{S}}\geq{\bm{S}}_{\mathrm{min}}
.

Proof.

Fact
2
in the Supplemental Material shows that there exists scalars
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{K+1})\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{K+1}
such that
(\alpha_{n}{\bm{I}}+{\bm{M}}+{\bm{u}}{\bm{a}}_{n}^{t})_{n\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}
and
\alpha_{K+1}{\bm{I}}+{\bm{M}}
are inverse M-matrices. It then follows from Fact
3
in the Supplemental Material that, for any
{\bm{S}}\in\mathcal{D}_{+}^{N\times N}
satisfying
{\bm{S}}\geq{\bm{S}}_{\mathrm{min}}:=\bar{\alpha}{\bm{I}}
, where
\bar{\alpha}:=\max\limits_{n\in\{1,\ldots,K+1\}}\alpha_{n}
, the matrices
{\bm{M}}+{\bm{S}}
,
{\bm{M}}+{\bm{S}}+{\bm{u}}{\bm{a}}_{1}^{t}
, …,
{\bm{M}}+{\bm{S}}+{\bm{u}}{\bm{a}}_{K}^{t}
are inverse M-matrices, hence also inverse Z-matrices because they are nonnegative (Fact
4
in the Supplemental Material). A direct application of Remark
2
, Corollary
1
, and Corollary
2
completes the proof.
∎

IV-B

Users offering compatible channels

Assume that two single-antenna users need to be served by a single basestation equipped with
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
antennas (we consider the general case soon). In the massive MIMO literature, a key concept indicating whether both users can be served simultaneously while maintaining good link performance is
favorable
or
approximately favorable propagation

[
7
, Ch. 7]
. More precisely, we say that the users offer
favorable propagation
if their channels
({\bm{h}}_{1},{\bm{h}}_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{L}\times\mathbb{C}^{L}
are such that
{\bm{h}}_{1}^{H}{\bm{h}}_{2}=0
, or that they offer
approximately favorable propagation
if
|{\bm{h}}_{1}^{H}{\bm{h}}_{2}|
is sufficiently small (possibly in a probabilistic sense for models such as those in Example
3
). It is often recommended to avoid scheduling users on the same resources when favorable propagation is absent
[
7
, p. 154]
, and this principle can be trivially generalized to systems with multiple users and basestations by considering the channels for each link pair. However, this system design recommendation overlooks key aspects of real systems, including, among others: (i) it does not take it account the presence of beamformers and the available transmit power; (ii) it ignores self-interference; (iii) many results proving approximate favorable propagation consider asymptotic regimes by letting the number of antennas diverge to infinity, but systems with a small number of antennas are common; (iii) users cannot be simply dropped – they need to be scheduled at some time slot considering some fairness criterion; (iv) and it lacks mathematical guarantees that users would not achieve better performance by being divided into smaller groups and served at different time slots.

The results in Sect.
III
provide a framework for defining a notion of user compatibility that overcomes the above drawbacks. Specifically, if the achievable rate region is convex, as discussed in Sect.
I-A
, we have a mathematical certificate that the network performance cannot be improved by splitting users into smaller groups and by serving these groups at different times,
irrespective of the fairness criterion being considered.
Therefore, the following definition is justified:

Definition 6
.

Consider the system model in Examples
3
and
4
. We say that the users offer
Z-compatible channels without power constraints
if the interference matrix
{\bm{M}}
is an inverse Z-matrix. Likewise, we say that the users offer Z-compatible channels with the power constraint
\mathcal{P}
in (
17
) if the matrices
({\bm{M}}+{\bm{ua}}_{n}^{t})_{n\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}
are inverse Z-matrices.

Corollaries
1
and
2
show that Z-compatibility guarantees convexity of the SINR and rate regions.

IV-C

Numerical aspects

A simple means to verify channel Z-compatibility across a group of users, in the sense of Definition
6
, is to invert the matrix
{\bm{M}}
or the matrices
({\bm{M}}+{\bm{u}}{\bm{a}}_{n}^{t})_{n\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}
(if they are invertible) and check whether the resulting matrices are Z-matrices (i.e., the off-diagonal entries are nonpositive). However, this direct approach based on matrix inverses becomes computationally expensive and numerically unstable in large systems. These issues can be mitigated by leveraging the vast literature on the identification of inverse Z-matrices or inverse M-matrices without actually computing any inverses, which is a well-studied problem with applications across different scientific and engineering domains
[
30
]
. Below, we explore some of these established results to reduce the computational burden of identifying users with Z-compatible or incompatible channels. For brevity, we focus on the interference matrix
{\bm{M}}
because the same principles apply to the matrices
({\bm{M}}+{\bm{u}}{\bm{a}}_{n}^{t})_{n\in\{1,\ldots,K\}}
without any changes.

If the interference matrix
{\bm{M}}
is of dimension
2\times 2
(i.e., we try to produce a certificate of channel Z-compatibility for two users), inverting such a small matrix may not pose serious challenges. Nevertheless, it is more efficient and numerically stable to apply the result stated in Fact
1
in the Supplemental Material. More specifically, an interference matrix
{\bm{M}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{2\times 2}
is an inverse Z-matrix if and only if its determinant
\mathrm{det}({\bm{M}})
satisfies
\mathrm{det}({\bm{M}})>0
. Unfortunately, this simple inequality is only a necessary condition for larger matrices
[
30
, Theorem 1.2]
. Nevertheless, all principal minors of inverse Z-matrices are positive
[
30
, Corollary 2.3.3]
, which in practice means that a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for Z-compatibility of all channels within a group of users is that any two channels are Z-compatible, which lead us to a simple rule for identifying users within a group
potentially
leading to a nonconvex SINR or rate region:

Remark 4
.

Consider the interference model in Examples
3
and
4
. Let
{\bm{M}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N\times N}
be the interference matrix of a network with
N
users. Given two users
(i,j)\in\mathcal{N}\times\mathcal{N}
, let
{\bm{M}}[i,j]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2\times 2}_{+}
be the reduced interference matrix obtained from
{\bm{M}}
by keeping only the
i
th and
j
th columns and the
i
th and
j
th rows. If
\mathrm{det}({\bm{M}}[i,j])\leq 0
, then the full matrix
{\bm{M}}
is not an inverse Z-matrix.

IV-E

The sum-rate maximization problem

In this final subsection of Sect.
IV
, we turn the attention to the interference model in (
14
) (or, equivalently (
16
)) in its full generality, with the models presented in Examples
3
and
4
serving as particular cases. In more detail, many scheduling and resource allocation schemes rely heavily on the sum-rate maximization problem, which is known to be NP-hard in general
[
14
]
. However, if
{T_{\|\cdot\|}}
in (
19
) is norm inducing, the closure
\overline{\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}}=\{{\bm{r}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{N}\mid\rho((e^{{\mathrm{diag}(\bm{r})}}-{\bm{I}}){T_{\|\cdot\|}})\leq 1\}
of the set of achievable rates
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}
in (
26
) is convex as shown in Proposition
6
, and
\overline{\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}}
can be equivalently expressed using a monotone norm as shown in (
28
). In this case, (weighted) sum-rate maximization can be posed as the following convex optimization problem, which opens up the possibility for deriving efficient solvers that provably converge to global optima (particular solvers will be considered elsewhere):

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rl}\text{maximize}&{\bm{w}}^{t}{\bm{r}}\\
\text{subject~to}&{\bm{r}}\in\overline{\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}}\end{array}

where
{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{++}^{N}
are the desired weights. Once a solution
{\bm{r}}^{\star}
to (
IV-E
) is obtained, assuming that
{\bm{r}}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{++}^{N}
, we can recover the optimal power allocation
{\bm{p}}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{++}^{N}
by computing the fixed point of the standard interference mapping
{\bm{D}}^{\star}T
in (
20
), where
{\bm{D}}^{\star}=e^{\mathrm{diag}(\bm{r^{\star}})}-{\bm{I}}
is the matrix of optimal SINR levels corresponding to the optimal rates
{\bm{r}}^{\star}
. In particular, the uniquely existing fixed point
{\bm{p}}^{\star}\in\mathrm{Fix}({\bm{D}}^{\star}T)
can be computed with the standard fixed point iterations
[
22
,
1
]
or with acceleration methods
[
39
]
. If
T
is also a positive concave mapping, convergence is guaranteed to be geometric in the standard Euclidean space
[
19
]
. If any components of
{\bm{r}}^{\star}
are zero, we can simply remove the corresponding users from the system and consider a fixed point problem with a reduced dimension.

It is worth emphasizing that the class of sum-rate maximization problems that can be reformulated as convex optimization problems does not necessarily require
{T_{\|\cdot\|}}
to be norm inducing, and the results from Sect.
III
offer analytical support for a straightforward guideline for designing sum-rate maximization algorithms:

Remark 5
.

In Proposition
6
, we have shown that convexity of the function
{\bm{s}}\mapsto\rho({\mathrm{diag}(\bm{s})}{T_{\|\cdot\|}})
implies convexity of the sets
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{P}}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}}
. However, the arguments cannot be reversed in general:
\overline{\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}}
can be convex even if neither the set
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{P}}}
nor the function
{\bm{s}}\mapsto\rho({\mathrm{diag}(\bm{s})}{T_{\|\cdot\|}})
is convex. This fact suggests to use the achievable rates directly as the optimization variables in sum-rate maximization problems, as done in (
IV-E
). The alternative approach of using SINR values
{\bm{s}}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{N})
as the optimization variables (or the power levels providing the required SINR values) and the sum-rate function
\sum_{n\in\mathcal{N}}\log(1+s_{n})
as the cost function may require the introduction of the set
\overline{{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathcal{P}}}
as a constraint, which, for the reasons mentioned above, is less likely to be convex compared to the set
\overline{\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{P}}}
. Therefore, we may lose the opportunity to exploit a potential hidden convexity of the sum-rate maximization problem, increasing the risk of iterative solvers converging to local rather than global optima, if convergence can indeed be guaranteed.

For the monotone norm

VI
Summary

In modern affine interference models that take self-interference into account, we have demonstrated that the SINR and rate regions are convex if the interference matrices are inverse Z-matrices. Moreover, we have established numerous connections between interference patterns in wireless networks and the extensive mathematical literature on these matrices to facilitate the identification of convex SINR and rate regions. Furthermore, we disproved a conjecture posed in
[
13
, Sect. 5.4.4]
[
24
]
, and we introduced a new concept of channel Z-compatibility for users that addresses certain limitations of previous notions of favorable propagation in the massive MIMO literature. Our findings also provide both numerical and theoretical support for formulating sum-rate maximization problems using rates, rather than SINR levels, as the optimization variables. By doing so, we increase the chance of uncovering hidden convexity in the problems.

While the aforementioned results involving inverse Z-matrices require interference models where beamformers remain unchanged if the transmit power changes, much of our developed theory also extends to scenarios where this assumption does not hold. In particular, we have derived simple characterizations of the SINR and rate regions using the concept of the spectral radius of nonlinear mappings. This approach simplifies notation and unlocks powerful machinery with a long history in mathematics, potentially enabling further extensions of the current results in future studies.
