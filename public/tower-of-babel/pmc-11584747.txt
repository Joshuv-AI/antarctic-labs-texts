Ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing to modify the quality characteristics of beef for aging
Authors: Lei Liu, Fengnan Niu, Yating Xiong, Peng Wang, Xiaoyu Lyu, Zongyun Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Meat products for elderly people are high in sodium content and hardness. Thus, the aim of this study is to explore how ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salting improves meat quality and determine the conditions that result in meat tenderness ideal for people aged 65–74 years. The ultrasound-assisted treatment of bovine hip muscle with non-sodium salt (CaCl2 and C6H10CaO6) was adopted, followed by sous vide (SV) cooking (65 ℃ for 8–12 h). Results showed that ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing can considerably reduce the shear force and hardness of beef (P < 0.05), promote the diffusion of Na+ (P < 0.05), and increase water content (P < 0.05), consistent with the water mobility results of low-field nuclear magnetic resonance. The tenderized effects were examined by Raman spectroscopy, which showed that the ordered α-helix and β-fold contents of the meat proteins decreased, and disordered β-turn and random coil contents increased after heat treatment with ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing. Scanning electron microscopy revealed that the degree of muscle fiber destruction by ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing combined with heat treatment was higher than that observed after traditional cooking with high amounts of sodium. With regard to tenderness, beef marinated with ultrasound-assisted C6H10CaO6 and subjected for 10 h was preferred by people aged 65–74 years. Ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing can improvements on simultaneously reduce the sodium content and enhance the tenderness of meat products for elderly people, and provides an ultrasonic scheme for meat processing using non-sodium salt.

Introduction

Elderly people are important to social groups, but their physiological health remains a cause of concern. The degree of muscle decay in elderly people increases with age, potentially posing a threat to their physiological health [1]. Meat is an important source of proteins, and a moderate increase in meat intake delays muscle loss in the elderly. Meat protein can promote muscle protein synthesis in the body, effectively counteracting the loss of skeletal muscles during muscle decay [2]. However, cured meat-based products and high salt content, are not “senior-friendly” and cannot be consumed by the elderly.

Curing is an important procedure in meat product processing and wet curing is one of the most common traditional curing methods. To date, sodium salt is mainly used in the curing of bulk meat products. It is one of the primary sources of salt in meat products [3]. The excessive intake of sodium salt induces hypertension, and cardiovascular diseases [4]. Cutler et al. [5] demonstrated that cardiovascular morbidity and mortality were reduced in the US population after blood pressure was decreased by the lowered dietary sodium. Therefore, low-sodium meat products that can satisfy the requirements of a healthy meat diet should be developed [6]. Calcium, potassium and magnesium salts (potassium chloride, calcium chloride, magnesium chloride, calcium lactate, and calcium citrate) commonly used in meat processing can partially replace salt because they have some properties that are similar to those of sodium salts. Using calcium salt to replace part of sodium salt is one of the effective ways to reduce sodium salt content [7]. It reduces the adverse effects of sodium salt to the human body and provides the calcium salt needed by the human body. The curing of meat-based products with low-sodium or non-sodium salts had not been explored. Ultrasonic treatment combined low-sodium curing (KCl instead of NaCl) can affect the physicochemical properties, sensory quality and microstructure of beef berk [8]. Low-salt meat (salt content < 2 %) is gaining considerable interest because of its low health risks, but improving its quality remains a technical challenge. Therefore, technical research is necessary [9], [10]. Ultrasound-assisted curing is a new form in the development of meat processing. Ultrasound is generated by waves with a vibration frequencies above the audible limit for humans (20 kHz) [11]. Ultrasound cavitation can change the microstructure of muscle tissues, loosens muscle fibers, improves the texture of meat products, and activates the activity of lysosomes, cathepsin, and calpain in meat [12]. Ultrasound-assisted curing not only tenderizes meat products, but also promotes the penetration of salt and enhances the curing effect of meat products.

Recent research on ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing for meat is gradually increasing, yet studies specifically focused on its use for aging remain limited. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to improve beef tenderness and low-sodium properties through ultrasound-assisted curing and sous vide (SV) cooking. The effects of these processes on texture, cooking loss, kinetic model of material diffusion and mass transfer were evaluated. The tenderness of meat was assessed on the basis of the bite force of the elderly and sensory evaluation. Our research will provide reference for the application of ultrasound technology to the preparation of healthy meat products for the elderly.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cattle rump was purchased from SUGUO supermarket (Nanjing, China). Salt (NaCl ≥ 99.1 g/100 g, food grade) was purchased from Jiangsu Salt Industry Group Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). Calcium chloride (CaCl2, food grade) was obtained from Hen nan Wanbang Industrial Co., Ltd. (Henan, China). Calcium lactate (C6H10CaO6, food grade) was purchased from Ruipu Bioengineering Co., Ltd. (Zhengzhou, Henan, China). Na+ and Ca2+ testing boxes were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Research Institute (Jiangsu, China). All other reagents were of analytical grade and used without purification.

Recruitment of elderly volunteers and testing of bite force

A total of 101 senior citizens (≥ 60 years old, Nanjing China) were recruited from two communities and four pension service institutions: Xiyuan Community of Nanjing Agricultural University, Kangdingli Community Service Center, Heyi Care Center, Ginkgo Tree Elderly Service Center in Pukou District, Ginkgo Tree Elderly Service Center in Jiangbei New District, and Zhuzhen Town Comprehensive Home Care Service Center. After elderly people without teeth or with severe tooth loss were excluded, 66 sets of statistically valid data were obtained. The bite force of teeth, particular in the front, left and right – back positions, was quantitatively characterized using a bite force tester (ISM003A, Haikang Baisi Health Technology Co., Ltd. Shen Zhen, China), which converted pressure from teeth into electrical signals. During testing, the elderly volunteers were asked to look straight ahead and keep the orbital ear plane parallel to the ground. Each volunteer bite the sensor with maximum force upon starting command. Bite force value was recorded in real time, and maximum bite force was obtained for analysis.

Meat pre-treatment

Each cattle rump was cut into 450 g pieces. The meat samples, which were vacuum-packed and stored at − 18 ℃, would run out within a week [13]. Before treatment, the meat was defrosted with circulating water in a container made from a foam tank, until the meat was completely defrosted. Excess juice was removed from meat with paper towel room temperature. After the fascia was removed, the beef rump was cut into 8 cm × 6 cm × 4 cm (200 ± 3 g).

Ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing design

According to the methods reported by Kang et al. [14], the ultrasonic parameters were set as follows: ultrasonic frequency, 25 kHz; ultrasonic power, 1200 W; ultrasonic time, 170 min, ultrasonic temperature, 10 ℃, and flesh-liquid ratio was 1:4. The added amounts of added NaCl, CaCl2, and C6H10CaO6 were calculated. The meat cured with 6 % NaCl solution was not subjected to ultrasound treatment and used as the control (D1). The three experiment groups, were cured with 4.8 % NaCl (JC1); 4.8 % NaCl and 1.2 % CaCl2 (JC2); 4.8 % NaCl and 1.2 % C6H10CaO6 (JC3). All experiments were performed in triplicate and replicated five times.

Shear force and texture profile analysis (TPA)

Shear force and TPA were measured. The control group was subjected to 6 % NaCl static curing treatment (SD1) and 6 % NaCl static curing treatment, and 35 g of beef was cut and heated to 75 ℃ (TD1). The experiment group were subjected to 4.8 % NaCl + 1.2 % CaCl2 + ultrasonic curing (SC1) and 4.8 % NaCl + 1.2 % C6H10CaO6 + ultrasonic curing (SC2). After 4.8 % NaCl + 1.2 % CaCl2 + ultrasonic curing, 35 g of beef was collected and cut, and the central temperature was heated to 75 ℃ (TC1). After salting with 4.8 % NaCl and 1.2 % C6H10CaO6 and ultrasonic curing, 35 g of beef was cut, and the central temperature was heated to 75 ℃ (TC2).

The central area of the cooked meat was collected. All the samples were cut into 0.5 cm × 1 cm pieces. The shear force for each sample was measured with a texture analyzer with equipped with a HDP/BSW probe. The test mode was compression, pre-test speed was 2 mm/s, test speed was 2 mm/s, post-test speed was 10 mm/s, mobile distance was 10 mm, and trigger force was 10 g.

The TPA was measured according to the method of Zou et al. [15] with corresponding modifications. A texture analyzer (TA.LB-WS1; Leibo Information Technology Co., Ltd, China) and a P/50 probe (36 mm stainless steel cylinder) were used. The test parameters were as follows: test speed of 2 mm/s, compression rate of 75 %, trigger force of 10 g, and residence time of 5 s. After cooling to running water, the meat samples (1 cm3) were placed on the center of the TPA plate for a two-cycle compression test. Hardness, elasticity, cohesiveness and chewiness were measured with the texture analyzer’s TPA at 25 ℃.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

An LF-NMR relaxation curve of each sample was recorded in a previous de-scription by Han et al. [16]. First, a sample (1.5 g) was placed into a cylindrical glass tube (15 mm in diameter) and inserted into an NMR probe. Then, a Niumag Benchtop (PQ001, Niumag Electric Co., Ltd. Shanghai, China) was operated at 18.2 MHz at 32 ℃. The CPMG sequence parameters used in the determination of raw meat samples (SD1, SC1 and SC2) were set as follows: SW = 100, RFD = 0.08, vTW = 4500, vNECH = 4000, and NS = 8. The parameters of the CPMG sequence used for the determination of cooked meat samples (TD1, TC1 and TC2) were set as follows: SW = 100, RFD = 0.08, TW = 4500, NECH = 4000, and NS = 8. The parameters of the CPMG sequences used for the determination of cooked meat samples (TD1, TC1 and TC2) were set as follows: SW = 100, RFD = 0.08, TW = 4000, NECH = 3500, and NS = 8. Each experiment had five parallel samples, and the measurements were repeated for three times. The multiple exponential model was fitted using Multi Exp Inv analysis Ver 4.0 (Niumag Electric Co., Ltd. Shanghai China).

Raman spectroscopy

The Raman spectra were obtained with a Raman spectrometer (Renishaw invia, UK). Each sample was tested for three times, and method described by Li-Chan et al. [17], [18] was used. The frequency of the instrument was corrected by using monocrystalline silicon. The 5 g of each sample was placed on a microscope plate, and 50 × telephoto lens was used to focus the laser to the solid sample on the slide. Raman spectroscopy was performed in the wavelength range of 400–3600 cm−1. The parameter settings were as follows: scanning, three times; integral time, 30 s; resolution was 2 cm −1. After the test, data were collected with LabSPEC6 software. The stretching vibration strength of phenylalanine ring at 1003 cm−1 was used as the internal standard for normalization. The strength did not change with the change in protein structure. Then, Peakfit V4.12 software was used for fitting and analysis of different secondary structures (α-helix, β-folding, β-corner, and random crimp contents).

Morphological observation of muscle fibers

Samples sections (1 mm thick) were observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HT7800, HITACH, Japan). The sections were fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde solution at 4 ℃ for more than 2 h. Then, a solution of 1 % osmium acid was fixed at 4 ℃ for 2 h. PBS was washed for 10 min three times. At 4 ℃, the samples were rinsed with 50 % acetone for 15 min; and then with 70 % acetone for 15 min. For dehydration, the samples were washed with 90 % acetone dehydration for 15 min and 100 % acetone for 15 min, two times. At room temperature, the samples immersed in 100 % acetone (EPON 812 embedding agent (1:1) for 2 h, then in 100 % acetone (EPON 813 embedding agent (1:1) for 2 h. At 30 ℃, the samples were embedded overnight with a pure embedding agent and then polymerized. The samples were polymerized, dehydrated, and cut into 60 nm-thick slices with an ultrathin slicer under the microscope. The sections were stained with uranium dioxide acetate and lead citrate. Images were obtained under the transmission microscope, and the ultrastructural characteristics of the muscle fibers before and after ultrasonic treatment were observed.

SV cooking experiment design

The D1, CC2 and CC3 group underwent ultrasound-assisted salt curing. After they were equally cut, 35 g of sample was collected. First, the samples were blanched in boiling water in an induction cooker at 2100 W for 3 min. Then, they were cooked at 65 ℃. According to the pre-experiment results, cooking time was set at 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12 h according to the pre-experiment results.

Effects of ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing and SV cooking on meat quality

Cooking loss

Cooking loss was determined using the method described by Chang et al. [12]. The initial weight of meat piece in the D1, CC2 and CC3 groups was denoted as M1. The weight of cooked meat was denoted as M2. According to the following formula (3), the cooking loss was calculated:(3)Cooking loss\%=M1-M2M1×100%

Shear force

The method of shear force testing is same as 2.5.2.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory evaluation performed according to the method described by Lorenzen et al. and Andersen et al. with appropriate modification [19], [20]. The optimal time point of SV cooking was determined using the D1, CC2 and CC3 groups. A total of 21 elderly volunteers aged 65–74 years were participated in the sensory evaluation of samples tenderness, and 15 valid data were obtained. The elderly volunteers underwent a brief sensory evaluation training before sensory evaluation. They performed sensory evaluation individually. According to the degree of satisfaction with meat products, they participants were divided into satisfactory and unsatisfactory groups.

Statistical analysis

All measurements were made in triplicate, and the obtained results were statistically analyzed using the SPSS 25.0 (SPSS INc., Chicago, USA) and Matlab R2009a. The significance of shear force, texture, material diffusion and mass transfer, cooking loss, tenderness (SV cooking experiment), and microstructure was determined through one-way analysis of variance. The kinetic analysis results of material diffusion and mass transfer were calculated. Differences between the data were considered significant at P values of < 0.05 or < 0.01. All measurements were conducted in triplicate, and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Graphs were made using Origin 8.0 software (Northampton, USA).

Results and discussion

Chewing ability analysis of elderly people (≥ 60 years old)

Chewing ability or disability impacts on food choices and nutrition received [21]. Fig. 1 shows the chewing ability of the elderly in different age groups. Participants over 75 years old had the smallest bite force among the age groups (3.4 kg), and the bite force of 65–74 years old participants was 14.29 % lower than that of the participants aged 60–64 years. The bite force of the participants over 75 years old showed 59.52 % decrease. The results suggested that meat products with different textures should be provided to older people depending on their chewing ability. For people aged over 75 years, lumpy meat products with strong fibrous texture are difficult to chew even after texture modification. People aged 65–74 years can eat meat pieces that are specially modified to have a soft texture, such as softy soy sauce beef [22]. Material diffusivity in beef subjected to ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing was analyzed using the method described by Kang et al. [25]. The mass transfer kinetics was analyzed using Fick's second law model. The diffusion coefficients of Na+, Ca2+, and water in ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing were calculated according to their determined content. In the diffusion dynamics analysis, ultrasound treatment and static curing were simplified. The meat sample were assumed to be thin cuboids. The material diffusion could be simplified to a unidirectional permeation model. In addition, the diffusion coefficient of Na+, Ca2+, and water diffusion coefficient did not change with treatment time at 10 ℃, and thus the shapes of the meat samples did not change during ultrasound treatment or static curing. The Na+, Ca2+, and water content of meat were evenly distributed in the initial stage of ultrasound treatment or static curing. Therefore, Fick's second law model could be described. LF-NMR water status of beef after ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing

The distribution of different status water (bound water, T20; immobilized water, T21; free water, T22) of meat and meat products is one of the important reference indices for measuring economic benefits and evaluating the quality of food products. When the ratio of free water is extremely high, the sensory and edible quality of meat will be seriously affected [29]. Fig. 2. shows the transverse relaxation time spectra of beef under different treatments. Four peaks were detected in each sample curve; T20 (0–1 ms), T21 (1–10 ms), T22 (30–80 ms) and T23 (100–400 ms). T21, T22 and T23 are the relaxation times of bound water, immobile water, and free water, respectively. Peak T22 had the largest signal intensity and peak area and is the main peak of the relaxation spectrum. The peak of the relaxation spectrum, indicated that the main form of water in all samples are immovable water. The peak of beef T22 after cooking tended to widen, mainly because of the change in myofibril. Heating can induce the contraction of myofibril, resulting in the drainage of water inside a myofibril. The peak position of the flowing water (T22) in the SC1 and SC2 groups did not easily shift after ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing. Compared with that in SD1, indicating that the degree of freedom and fluidity of water increased considerably. The possible reason was that ultrasonic treatment destroyed the myofibrillar proteins and connective tissues of beef [30]. In addition, Ca2+ can activate calcium-activating enzymes, degrade myocytoskeletal proteins, and render the structural proteins of muscle fibers unstable, thereby facilitating ultrasonic treatment [31]. The peak position of T22 markedly changed, but the peak area ratio was not affected by ultrasound and calcium ions, indicating that the content of immobile water did not change considerably. Given the strong correlation with this part of water and the water holding capacity of beef muscle tissues. Ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt salting treatment can maintain the original moisture in muscle fibers and will not affect the edible quality of beef. Secondary protein structure

The conformation of the protein main chain is determined by the characteristic peak of the amide I band(1600–1700 cm−1). The influences of ultrasonic and salting treatment on the structure of beef myofibrillar protein were investigated. The spectral curves of myofibrillar proteins in beef under different treatment conditions were determined by Raman spectroscopy, and the composition and relative content of the secondary structures of proteins, such as α-helices (1650–1660 cm−1), β-folds (1600–1640 cm−1) [32]. Fig. 3. depicts the relative content of myofibrillar proteins with secondary structure after baseline correction and normalization in different sonication and heat treatment groups (SD1, SC1, SC2, TD1, TC1 and TC2). The myofibrillar α-helix content of beef in the SC2 group decreased, the β-fold content of beef in the SC1 and SC2 groups increased, and the β-turn and irregular curling had no significant difference. The results show that ultrasonic curing or heating treatment lead to the transformation from α-helix to β-turn in different degrees. The possible reason was the unfolding of the protein molecules, and formation of β-turn and random coils [33]. In addition, many types of forces maintained the secondary structures of the proteins, α-helix and β-fold were mainly maintained by hydrogen bonds, and the hydrogen bond strength varied with secondary structure. Therefore, the ultrasonic curing treatment unlocked the molecular chains of the proteins and broke some hydrogen bonds, leading to the destruction of some of the ordered structures of α-helix and β-fold. In curing, salt ions, water, and temperature are used for protein, which change the hydrogen bonds within proteins, and eventually lead to a change in the secondary structure of beef protein and promote the tendering effect of low –sodium salt curing on beef. These results were similar to those reported by Wen et al. Cooking loss

The cooking loss rate reflects the water holding capacity of muscles, and is commonly used for evaluating the quality of meat products because it indicates the binding ability of proteins to water [36]. A low cooking loss value indicates good quality, particularly in terms of color, tenderness, flavor, nutritional value and aroma.

The cooking loss rate of ultrasound-assisted low-salt treatment in the D1, CC2, and CC3 groups at different cooking times are shown in Fig. 5. With the increase of cooking time, the cooking loss rate of D1 and CC3 groups increased gradually. Owing to the shrinking of myofibrillar structure. At a specific cooking time, the muscle fibrils of beef considerably shrunk, resulting in increased juice loss [37]. Interestingly, the cooking loss rate in the CC2 group decreased gradually. Especially at 11 and 12 h, the cooking loss rate in the CC2 group was significantly lower the rates in the D1 and CC3 groups (P < 0.05). Calcium salt solutions increase the ionic strength, and promote the interactions among hydrophilic proteins, causing the proteins to bind free water [24]. Shear force and sensory satisfaction degree of beef’s tenderness

Heating causes thermal changes in myofibrils and collagen. Myofibrillar degeneration causes meat to contract; thus, the tenderness of meat decreases, and juices is depleted. The degree of meat rejuvenation changes, and the cooking loss of meat is affected [38], [39]. As shown in Fig. 6(a), the effects of low temperature and slow cooking time on the tenderness of ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salted beef were further investigated by measuring the cooking time from 8 h to 12 h. The shear force values of groups D1, CC2 and CC3 decreased and then increased. These results indicated that heat had a dual effect on muscle tenderness and tenderized and hardened meat. Prolonged heating will cause the excessive denaturation of proteins, resulting in coagulation, agglutination, and shortening of proteins. The contraction of beef muscle fibers increased the shear force. The influence of heat treatment on muscle tenderness was related to the content of heat-insensitive proteins in muscle. Bertola et al. [40] found that connective tissues in muscles gradually changed into gelatin within a specific temperature range, thus increasing the muscle tenderness. However, the elastin in connective tissue is insensitive to heat, and thus meat remains tough even after cooking for long periods. The insensitivity of elastin in connective tissues to heat can cause meats to harden over long periods of cooking. In addition, the lowest shear force was observed in all groups at a cooking time of 10 h, indicating that the meat tenderness effect was good at a low temperature and slow cooking time of 10 h. Compared with D1 and CC2, CC3 had a lower shear force (P < 0.05). Ultrasound treatment combined with calcium lactate treatment effectively promoted the tenderizing effect of ultrasound combined with C6H10CaO6 and was better than ultrasound combined with CaCl2 under long time low temperature and slow cooking conditions. This suggests that the improvement in beef tenderness is the result of myofibrillar protein changes induced by C6H10CaO6 [41]. Fig. 6(b) shows that the highest satisfaction rate of group CC3 was 37.21 % compared with D1 and CC2, and the lowest dissatisfaction rate was 0. The tenderness of CC3 decreased after 10 h of low temperature slow cooking. Conclusions

The bite force of the elderly decreased. Our design conformed to the texture modification and low-sodium requirements of the elderly aged 65–74. The results showed that ultrasonic-assisted low-sodium salting can improve the tenderness and reduce the sodium content of beef. The edible quality of beef was improved by low temperature and slow cooking. In addition, sensory evaluation tests showed that after low temperature and slow cooking at 65 ℃ for 10 h, pickled beef subjected to 4.8 % sodium chloride and 1.2 % calcium lactate solution treatment combined with ultrasonic treatment was beneficial to people aged 65–74 years. Moreover, ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt curing changed the secondary structure of proteins from helical to folded and the partial transformation from an ordered structure to a disordered one was observed. TEM results showed that the muscle fiber structure of beef was loosened by ultrasound-assisted low-sodium salt. Through reasonable experimental design and rich test means, this work provides a feasible technical solution on meat food for muscle attenuation in the elderly.
