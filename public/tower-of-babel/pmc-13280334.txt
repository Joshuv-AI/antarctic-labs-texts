PIT-Net: Physics-informed transformer with differentiable diffusion constraints for quantitative photoacoustic tomography of deep tissues
Authors: Ying Fan, Yangkun Liu, Abdula Aji, Ting Feng, Dean Ta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuantitative photoacoustic tomography (QPAT) faces the ill-posed inverse problem of decoupling optical absorption from light fluence. Standard deep learning models often lack physical consistency and struggle to generalize in vivo. To address this, we propose PIT-Net, a physics-informed transformer framework for multi-wavelength QPAT. PIT-Net integrates a Swin Transformer backbone to extract multiscale, cross-spectral features with an embedded differentiable physics propagator (DPP). During the forward pass, the DPP solves the steady-state photon diffusion equation under Robin boundary conditions, coupled with an acoustic residual feedback loop to promote physical consistency. Trained entirely on synthetic data, PIT-Net demonstrates direct transferability to in vivo targets without fine-tuning. It achieves high quantitative accuracy in simulations (PSNR: 45.475 ± 3.599 dB; RMSE: (6.0 ± 2.7) × 10⁻³ ) and reconstructs high-resolution absorption images across physical phantoms and in vivo tissues. Longitudinal monitoring of murine tumors further validates PIT-Net as a robust, physically interpretable solution for QPAT.Keywords: Photoacoustic tomography, Quantitative imaging, Transformer, Physics-informed learning

Introduction

Photoacoustic tomography (PAT) has emerged as a premier hybrid imaging modality that effectively combines the high optical absorption contrast of optical imaging with the deep-tissue spatial resolution of ultrasonic detection [1], [2]. By bridging these physical domains, PAT offers molecular-level sensitivity alongside substantial tissue penetration depths [1], [3]. In clinical and preclinical research, PAT has demonstrated significant potential across diverse fields, including cancer screening, vascular mapping, and functional brain imaging [2], [4], [5]. Its distinct advantage lies in visualizing multiple endogenous chromophores, such as hemoglobin and lipids, which enables the extraction of rich functional and metabolic information at sub-millimeter resolution across multiple optical wavelengths [6].

Despite these capabilities, quantitative photoacoustic tomography (QPAT), which aims to accurately recover intrinsic optical absorption coefficients (μa), remains a formidable challenge [7], [8]. The difficulty arises because the measured initial photoacoustic pressure is inherently coupled to the local light fluence (Φ) [8]. In biological tissues, strong optical scattering causes the light fluence to exhibit complex, heterogeneous decay patterns [9], [10]. Consequently, decoupling the intrinsic absorption from the extrinsic light fluence constitutes a highly non-linear and ill-posed inverse problem. Conventional approaches often rely on simplified approximations that introduce quantification bias, or iterative schemes that are computationally prohibitive and highly sensitive to initialization [8], [11], [12].

Recently, deep learning has accelerated QPAT inversion by approximating these non-linear mappings in a data-driven manner [13], [14], [15]. While promising, standard supervised models face critical limitations. Operating primarily as "black boxes," these networks prioritize statistical fitting over governing physical laws, leading to physically inconsistent reconstructions. Furthermore, while early Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) attempt to mitigate this by adding physical equations as soft penalty terms in the loss function, this loose regularization often fails under severe domain shifts [13], [14], [16]. Models trained on synthetic datasets frequently collapse when applied to complex in vivo environments, as they cannot dynamically adapt to the unknown physical boundary conditions and complex anatomical geometries inherent in real-world biological tissues [17], [18].

To address these limitations, we present PIT-Net (Physics-Informed Transformer Network) as a deeply integrated architectural optimization for multi-wavelength QPAT reconstruction. The proposed framework integrates a multi-wavelength Swin Transformer V2 backbone with a spectrally-attentive feature pyramid decoder to capture multi-scale cross-spectral chromophore correlations. Rather than treating physics as a loose regularization penalty, differentiable light transport physics is embedded directly within the network's forward pass via a continuous diffusion solver. This solver dynamically calculates adaptive time-steps strictly derived from the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) stability criterion, mathematically ensuring numerical stability under varying gradients. Furthermore, a physics-driven feedback strategy is implemented to iteratively refine the estimated optical parameters by minimizing acoustic domain discrepancies, guiding the network toward physically consistent solutions. where D(r)=1/[3(μa+μs′)] is the diffusion coefficient, μs′ is the reduced scattering coefficient, and S(r,λ)represents the light source term. This equation describes light propagation in tissue and provides a physical constraint for deep-learning-based μa inversion.

The steady-state diffusion equation is a mathematically P1-approximation of the exact Radiative Transfer Equation (RTE). According to classical biomedical optics [20], [21], this approximation is highly accurate under the assumptions that the biological tissue is a scattering-dominated medium (μs′≫μa) and that the region of interest is at least one transport mean free path away from the directional light source. While Monte Carlo methods can precisely model photon transport without these assumptions, they are stochastic and fundamentally non-differentiable. In contrast, the continuous and deterministic nature of the diffusion equation makes it suited as a differentiable physical constraint for deep-learning-based μa inversion. The inverse problem aims to recover the intrinsic absorption map μa from the measured pressure sequence P=p0(⋅,λc)c=1C. To address the inherent ill-posedness and noise, we propose a physics-informed deep learning framework that synergizes data-driven denoising with explicit physical priors governing photon transport.

Network architecture

We propose PIT-Net, which integrates a hierarchical SwinV2 backbone, a spectrally-attentive dual-branch decoder, and a differentiable finite-difference solver (Fig. 1). The encoder utilizes a Swin Transformer V2 [22] backbone to efficiently capture long-range dependencies across the multi-wavelength input tensor P0∈RC×H×W, followed by a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN) [23] to aggregate multi-scale semantic information. To facilitate physics-guided disentanglement, the decoder bifurcates into two specific branches: an Absorption Branch for estimating μar and a Fluence Branch for predicting the initial light fluence ϕinit(r).

Architectural overview of the PIT-Net framework for deep-tissue quantitative photoacoustic tomography.

A critical innovation embedded within the decoder is the Spectral Attention Module (SAM) (Fig. 2), designed to exploit inter-channel correlations among distinct tissue chromophores. Since simple convolution cannot effectively model non-linear spectral dependencies, SAM employs a dual-channel pooling strategy. It aggregates global spectral statistics via both average-pooling and max-pooling to generate two distinct descriptors. These descriptors are processed by a shared Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) and fused via element-wise summation and Sigmoid activation to yield wavelength-specific attention weights. This mechanism dynamically recalibrates the feature maps, effectively emphasizing informative spectral bands while suppressing noise. Finally, distinct from methods that predict pixel-wise diffusion maps, we formulate the diffusion coefficients D, and source profile S as learnable, global physical vectors, allowing the network to robustly self-calibrate system constants during training.

Implementation details

The framework was implemented in PyTorch [26]and trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. We utilized the AdamW[27] optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1 × 10−4 and a one-cycle cosine annealing schedule over 200 epochs. To enhance generalizability, on-the-fly data augmentation, including rotation, scaling, flipping, and noise injection, was applied. Gradients were clipped at a norm of 0.5 to stabilize the differentiable physics gradients.

Performance evaluation

To evaluate the reconstruction performance of PIT-Net, we compared it against four representative data-driven models: CycleGAN [29], U-Net [30], U-Net++ [31], and a standard Vision Transformer (ViT) [32], Restormer [33], and a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) [34]. Additionally, a Physics-Informed U-Net (PI-UNet) was independently implemented as a baseline to represent the standard soft-constrained physics-informed neural network (PINN) paradigm. Restormer and DiT represent recent data-driven architectures, whereas U-Net and U-Net++ serve as standard benchmarks for medical image translation, and CycleGAN acts as an unsupervised reference. The implemented PI-UNet incorporates the steady-state diffusion PDE and Robin boundary conditions as soft-penalty terms in the loss function, providing a direct baseline to benchmark the efficacy of our deeply embedded physical constraints. Restormer and DiT represent recent data-driven architectures, while PI-UNet is included as a soft-constrained physics-informed baseline. Datasets

Simulation datasets

We first synthesized the spatial distribution of the absorption coefficient (μₐ) and employed the GPU-accelerated Monte Carlo eXtreme (MCX) framework [35] to simulate photon transport within the tissue, thereby calculating the optical fluence (Φ) and the initial acoustic pressure (P₀). Assuming the Grüneisen parameter is spatially constant and equal to 1, the initial acoustic pressure follows the relation P₀= μₐ·Φ. To simulate a wide-field illumination environment, four symmetrically distributed planar sources were arranged around the phantom to provide inward irradiation. A 5 × 5 grid was constructed within the phantom, containing randomly generated absorbers shaped as ellipses, rounded triangles, and rounded quadrilaterals. The dimensions and positions of these absorbers were randomized within defined ranges to enhance dataset diversity. The optical properties were configured as follows: a reduced scattering coefficient of μₛ′= 1 mm⁻¹ , an anisotropy factor of g = 0.9, and a background absorption coefficient of 0.005 mm⁻¹ . The region exterior to the tissue was modeled as air using a masking process. For each simulation, 109 photons were launched with a time gate of 5 ns to sufficiently capture the contribution of scattered photons. Subsequently, 2D acoustic wave propagation was simulated using the k-Wave toolbox [36]. The simulation domain measured 25.6 mm× 25.6 mm (corresponding to a 256 ×256 grid with a 0.1 mm pixel size) to match the spatial resolution of the experimental setup. Acoustic signals were acquired by an array of 256 sensors uniformly distributed along a 310°arc. The dataset comprises 5500 paired samples of P₀, μₐ, and Φ, which were partitioned into 3200, 800, and 1500 samples for training, validation, and independent testing, respectively. All data were normalized by their global maximum values, and the dataset generation was performed on a computing cluster equipped with eight NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs.

Experimental phantom datasets

The proposed method was validated through controlled liquid phantom experiments. We prepared two sets of dye solutions: one containing Indocyanine Green (ICG) at concentrations of 0.5, 1.25, and 2 μM, and another containing Methylene Blue (MB) at concentrations of 1.5, 4, and 5 μM. Both dyes were dispersed in a scattering background medium to achieve a controlled reduced scattering coefficient of μₛ' ≈ 1 mm⁻¹ . The ground truth absorption coefficients (μₐ) were determined based on the prepared molar concentrations and verified through dilution-based calculations. To ensure geometric consistency during imaging, custom-made containers were employed, with internal dimensions verified using digital calipers to an accuracy within ±0.1 mm. A total of two phantoms were constructed, and multi-wavelength data were acquired across the 660–950 nm range. To mitigate errors induced by photobleaching, the first two acquired frames from each sequence were selected as the test set for model evaluation.

In vivo datasets

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines (approval no. 2024-FAET−001). In this study, in vivo data were acquired from five 7-week-old healthy male athymic nude mice. Additionally, a longitudinal tumor study was conducted using five 6–7-week-old male athymic nude mice. For tumor induction, a 25 μL suspension containing approximately ∼1 × 10⁶ cells was injected into the tibia. To ensure animal welfare, the tumor size was maintained below 1.5 cm throughout the experiment. During the imaging sessions, mice were initially anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (0.01 mL/g body weight) and secured to a fixation plate. Anesthesia was subsequently maintained via a nose cone delivering 2% isoflurane, with a bite bar utilized to minimize motion artifacts. The water bath temperature was regulated at 36°C. Photoacoustic data were scanned across the abdominal and tumor regions with a step size of 0.5 mm, covering a wavelength range of 660–950 nm. For the tumor-bearing model, data were collected on days 1, 4, 7, 11, and 21 to monitor the developmental stages of the tumor.

PIT-net evaluation in in vivo datasets

To evaluate the preclinical potential of the proposed PIT-Net, we compared traditional PAT and QPAT using complex whole-body in vivo mouse models. The in vivo cohort in this experiment included 5 healthy mice and 5 tumor-bearing mice. Due to uneven light fluence distribution, traditional PAT produced inaccurate absorption representations in cross-sections with complex anatomy. For example, the image intensity of deep structures like the kidney and spinal cord was incorrectly estimated to be similar to superficial skin (indicated by the arrows), and some vascular regions lacked contrast with surrounding tissues (Fig. 8). In contrast, the quantitative absorption coefficient (μₐ) images provided by PIT-Net exhibited improved contrast, clearly distinguishing blood vessels and internal organs from background tissues. As shown in the first (P0) and second (μₐ) columns of Fig. 8, the contrast of the spine, arteries, veins, and kidneys in the μₐ images is higher than that in the P0 images. To quantify this structural fidelity, our CNR analysis, evaluated via the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, demonstrated a statistically significant improvement for PIT-Net (P < 0.001). Additionally, compared to data-driven architectures including CycleGAN, U-Net, U-Net++, Vision Transformer, PI-Unet, DiT, and Restormer, PIT-Net yielded more accurate μₐ distributions (Supplementary Material Fig S1). To evaluate predictive confidence in vivo, we performed Monte Carlo sampling-based uncertainty quantification (detailed in Supplementary Material). The resulting maps (Supplementary Figs. To further compare the capabilities of traditional PAT and QPAT, we applied linear spectral unmixing to the multi-wavelength estimates to calculate sO2 maps (Fig. 8 and Fig. 9c, d). The sO2 maps generated directly from P0 were affected by depth-dependent attenuation, leading to inaccurate sO2 estimations for deep vessels. Notably, in the vascular regions indicated by arrows in Fig. 8, the P0-based sO2 maps showed blurry boundaries and lower overall contrast. Conversely, the sO2 maps derived from the μₐ estimated by PIT-Net demonstrated enhanced distinguishability and clear tissue boundaries, with estimated sO2 values in vascular regions falling within a more physiologically realistic range.

We further evaluated the application of PIT-Net in tumor monitoring. As shown in Fig. 9c and d, the P0-based sO2 maps failed to show significant sO2 changes in the tumor region (black circles) during tumor growth. However, using the μₐ-based sO2 maps, we could clearly observe blood oxygen changes in the tumor region from day 1 to day 21. As shown in the quantitative analysis in Fig. 9e, f, the μₐ-based estimates detected early sO2 variations, showing statistically significant differences between day 1 and day 4, as well as between day 11 and day 21. In contrast, the initial P0-based estimates did not show statistical significance at these time points. The μₐ-derived sO2 maps revealed an overall downward trend in tumor oxygenation from day 1 to day 21, which is consistent with the physiological occurrence of hypoxia within the tissue during tumor growth. The longitudinal monitoring results for the remaining four tumor-bearing mice are provided in Supplementary Figs. S4–S7.

Ablation study

We conducted a progressive ablation study to evaluate the contribution of each core module in PIT-Net. Starting from the Full Model, we sequentially removed the Physical Refinement module, Light Transport module, Physics Constraint Loss, Attention Mechanism, and FPN. The results across three datasets are summarized in Table 2. The Full Model achieves the highest accuracy, reaching a PSNR of 48.738 dB and an RMSE of 4.1 × 10−3 on the Mouse dataset. Removing the Physical Refinement layer and the Light Transport module leads to a steady decline in performance, with the RMSE on the Mouse data increasing to 8.405 × 10−3. This confirms that explicit physical modeling is necessary for high-fidelity reconstruction. A more significant drop occurs when the Physics Constraint Loss is removed, as the RMSE rises to 12.343 × 10−3, highlighting the importance of using diffusion equation priors to guide the learning process. Performance further degrades as the Attention Mechanism and FPN are stripped away. Specifically, without the FPN, the PSNR on the Circle dataset falls to 26.947 dB, representing the lowest baseline in our study. These results demonstrate that while the FPN provides the structural foundation, the integration of physics-aware layers and constraints is what drives the model toward quantitative accuracy. To assess robustness against noise and domain gaps, we evaluated PIT-Net under varying noise levels and out-of-domain structures without fine-tuning (see Supplementary Material). First, PIT-Net maintains a higher PSNR at high noise levels than the baseline models (Table S2). Second, under structural mismatch, PIT-Net maintains a higher out-of-domain PSNR compared to the baseline, demonstrating domain generalization (Table S3).

Discussion

The primary challenge in QPAT is the non-linear attenuation of light fluence in scattering media. This makes decoupling the intrinsic absorption coefficient (μa) from the initial acoustic pressure (P0) a severely ill-posed inverse problem. Conventional model-based iterative methods are computationally demanding and highly sensitive to initialization [37]. Purely data-driven deep learning models offer a faster alternative. However, because of their "black-box" nature, they often produce artifacts in complex morphologies and lack physical consistency [38]. Our simulation and physical phantom experiments show that PIT-Net substantially outperforms baseline models such as U-Net, U-Net++, and Vision Transformer across a broad 660–950 nm spectral range. Notably, this boost in quantitative accuracy stems directly from embedding explicit physical laws into the data-driven feature extraction process.

Ablation studies shed light on the mechanics behind this performance. Removing the physics constraint loss and the light transport module causes a sharp spike in RMSE on the mouse dataset. This finding aligns with the consensus that spatial features alone, such as those from a FPN, cannot fully resolve highly non-linear optical inversion [39]. Unlike conventional PINNs that typically relegate physical equations to mere soft penalties in the loss function, PIT-Net explicitly embeds the steady-state photon diffusion equation directly into the forward pass. By pairing this with an adaptive numerical step size and Robin boundary conditions, the network essentially forces the output of physically plausible light fluence distributions in deep tissues. At the same time, the SAM extracts non-linear features from multi-wavelength data, while a physical refinement module uses acoustic measurement residuals for iterative feedback. Blending these mechanisms appears to largely resolve the physical inconsistencies typical of traditional "black-box" models, effectively minimizing quantitative bias.

A major bottleneck for deep learning in preclinical research is the domain shift from simulations to real in vivo environments [40]. PIT-Net was trained entirely on simulated data with extensive morphological variations. Yet, it translated to actual in vivo experiments without any domain-specific fine-tuning. In a validation cohort of 5 healthy and 5 tumor-bearing mice, the model demonstrated robust generalization capability across different biological structures. Traditional PAT images often suffer from contrast distortion in deep organs, like kidneys and deep blood vessels, because of light attenuation. In contrast, the μa images reconstructed by PIT-Net successfully restored the true optical contrast of these deep structures. During a 21-day longitudinal tumor study, the sO2 images, calculated from the accurately decoupled μa, captured the pathophysiological trend of gradual hypoxia in internal vessels as the tumor grew. Traditional P0 images missed this critical change due to light attenuation interference. These findings suggest that PIT-Net could serve as a highly sensitive quantitative tool for early detection and dynamic monitoring of the tumor microenvironment [41].

Recent physics-driven hybrid models, such as PIDP-qPAT [42], QOAT-Net [14], and EAP-Net [16], have advanced the field. These iterative methods achieve high accuracy by repeatedly solving forward and inverse models. However, they are computationally demanding during testing. They often require seconds to minutes to process a single frame. In contrast, PIT-Net is an end-to-end framework. It avoids the computational overhead of test-time optimization. As a result, PIT-Net achieves millisecond-level inference. This speed is suitable for real-time in vivo dynamic monitoring, while the embedded solvers maintain physical consistency.

Although pure data-driven baseline models, such as the standard U-Net, achieve reasonable reconstruction performance on synthetic datasets, they exhibit performance degradation when applied to in vivo measurements. This generalization gap stems from their tendency to overfit the idealized noise distributions and simplified acoustic forward models used during training. Real in vivo environments are complex, characterized by tissue heterogeneities, phase aberrations, and multi-path reflections, which introduce acoustic clutter and domain shifts. In contrast, the proposed PIT-Net addresses these limitations by integrating a DPP and an acoustic residual feedback mechanism. By enforcing the physical laws of wave propagation within the network architecture, the embedded physical model serves as a structural regularizer that stabilizes the ill-posed inversion process, filtering out non-physical artifacts and enabling microvascular reconstruction under in vivo conditions. However, despite its performance across synthetic and experimental validations, PIT-Net presents certain methodological limitations regarding the simplified physical forward model. In the current implementation of the DPP, the diffusion coefficient is approximated as a globally learnable constant per wavelength, though mathematically this parameter is spatially dependent and governed by local variations in both absorption and reduced scattering. This simplification assumes a relatively homogeneous scattering background to improve computational efficiency and stabilize the ill-posed inversion during network training. Yet, biological tissues exhibit optical heterogeneity, and the steady-state diffusion approximation becomes less accurate in localized low-scattering regimes, such as within major blood vessels. Consequently, enforcing a spatially uniform diffusion coefficient creates a physical mismatch between the theoretical propagator and real-world tissue optics, explaining the observed performance gap between synthetic datasets and in vivo scenarios. To address this limitation, future work will focus on developing models capable of predicting spatially resolved scattering maps to reduce this simulation-to-reality gap.

Conclusion

In this work, we present PIT-Net, an end-to-end framework for multi-wavelength QPAT. The method integrates a Swin Transformer with embedded physical models, including a differentiable physics propagator and an acoustic residual feedback mechanism. This design improves physical consistency and stabilizes inversion under ill-posed conditions. The model is trained on synthetic data and generalizes to experimental data. Across simulations, phantoms, and in vivo tissues, PIT-Net reconstructs high-resolution quantitative absorption images. These results demonstrate that combining physical constraints with deep models provides a robust and interpretable solution for QPAT.
