Drug-delivery oriented solubility enhancement of curcumin in choline chloride-based acidic deep eutectic solvents: thermodynamic analysis and MCF-7 cytotoxicity assessment
Authors: Mohammad Khorsandi, Hemayat Shekaari, Parisa Akbarzadeh Gondoghdi, Soheil Abbaspour-Ravasjani, Hamed Hamishehkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Curcumin is a bioactive compound with well-established chemopreventive and anticancer properties, but its pharmaceutical application is limited by extremely low aqueous solubility and poor formulation-dependent bioavailability. In this study, three choline chloride-based acidic deep eutectic solvents (ADESs), namely choline chloride/propionic acid (ChCl/Pro), choline chloride/acetic acid (ChCl/Ace), and choline chloride/lactic acid (ChCl/Lac), were systematically evaluated as solubilizing media for curcumin. The mole fraction solubility of curcumin was determined in aqueous ADES mixtures over 298.15–313.15 K at different ADES mass fractions. In all systems, solubility increased with both temperature and ADES content, and the overall solubilization efficiency followed the order ChCl/Pro > ChCl/Ace > ChCl/Lac. The highest solubility was obtained in neat ChCl/Pro at 313.15 K, where the mole fraction solubility reached 2.237 × 10–2, corresponding to an enhancement of more than 100,000-fold relative to water. The experimental data were correlated using the Jouyban-Acree, e-NRTL, and UNIQUAC models, with the Jouyban-Acree model showing the best overall performance. Thermodynamic analysis based on van't Hoff and Gibbs equations indicated that curcumin dissolution in the ADES-containing media is endothermic and predominantly enthalpy-driven. In vitro MTT assays on MCF-7 human breast cancer cells showed concentration-dependent cytotoxicity for the curcumin-loaded ADES systems, with IC50 values of 0.34 ± 0.01, 0.37 ± 0.01, and 0.42 ± 0.02 mg L−1 for ChCl/Lac, ChCl/Pro, and ChCl/Ace, respectively. Overall, choline chloride-based acidic DESs, particularly ChCl/Pro, markedly improve the solubility of curcumin and provide a promising solubilizing media for curcumin that may serve as a basis for further pharmaceutical formulation development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13065-026-01921-y.

Introduction

Curcumin (CUR) as showed in the Fig. 1 is the main phenolic compound in turmeric and has been studied for many years because of its anti‑inflammatory, antioxidant and anticancer effects. In spite of this broad pharmacological interest, curcumin has not yet led to many successful medicinal products [1]. The main reasons are its very low solubility in water, instability in physiological media and the fact that its bioavailability is strongly dependent on the formulation. For these reasons, improving the solubility and dissolution of curcumin is not a secondary issue, but a key step in the development of any drug delivery system based on this molecule [2–4] (Fig. 2).Fig. 1Molecular structure of CurcuminFig. 2 Cell Viability of the MCF-7 cell line dose–response curves of the mentioned ADESs-Curcumin mole fraction

According to the Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS), curcumin is usually placed in class II, which means that it has good permeability through biological membranes but poor solubility in aqueous media. For drugs in this class, the rate and extent of absorption after oral administration are often controlled by the dissolution step [5]. Because of this, different formulation strategies have been suggested to improve the solubility and apparent bioavailability of curcumin and other poorly water‑soluble drugs. Examples include adjustment of pH, the use of surfactants, inclusion complexes with cyclodextrins, preparation of amorphous solid dispersions, lipid‑based carriers, self‑emulsifying systems and supersaturating drug delivery systems. However, many of these techniques depend on organic solvents or excipients that may cause regulatory or safety concerns, or they require complex and expensive manufacturing processes. In contrast, co‑solvency is a simple and well‑known approach in industrial practice and is still one of the most widely used methods to increase the solubility of drugs in water [2, 6, 7].

During the last decade, deep eutectic solvents (DESs), and especially therapeutic deep eutectic systems (THEDES), have been introduced as new candidates for use as co‑solvents and formulation media [8, 9]. In a typical DES, a hydrogen bond acceptor such as choline chloride (ChCl) is mixed with a hydrogen bond donor such as an organic acid, alcohol or amide in a suitable molar ratio [9]. Strong hydrogen bonding between the components leads to a large decrease in melting point compared with the pure substances, and a liquid is obtained with adjustable polarity and solvation properties. If the components are pharmaceutically acceptable, these systems can work not only as solvents, but also as media that help to stabilize the active ingredient and sometimes even contribute to biological activity. Recent review papers show that THEDES are moving from a purely academic concept to practical tools that can improve dissolution, permeability and bioavailability of difficult drug molecules [10–12].

Curcumin has been used in several studies as a model compound to test DES and natural deep eutectic solvents (NADESs) in drug delivery applications. These investigations have reported that both hydrophilic and hydrophobic DESs and NADESs can strongly increase the solubility of curcumin, improve its physical and chemical stability and, in some cases, enhance its biological effects in cell culture [13]. More recently, choline chloride‑based DESs containing polyols as hydrogen bond donors have been shown to increase the solubility of curcumin by several orders of magnitude, and the experimental observations were supported by theoretical analyses of the interactions between the solvent and the drug. Altogether, these results point out that the solubility of curcumin is very sensitive to the exact composition of the DES, and they suggest that carefully designed DES/THEDES may be promising media for curcumin formulations. However, systematic studies on acidic ChCl‑based DESs as co‑solvents for curcumin, which also include thermodynamic modelling and cytotoxicity testing on cancer cells, are still limited [10, 14, 15].

An additional point that must be considered is the safety of DESs. Although the individual components of many DESs, such as choline chloride and simple organic acids, often have acceptable toxicological profiles, the behaviour of the combined system cannot be assumed to be harmless. Recent work on the cytotoxicity of choline chloride‑based DESs has shown that cell responses depend strongly on the particular combination of hydrogen bond acceptor and donor, their molar ratio and the concentration used [16]. These observations underline that formulation‑relevant toxicity studies are required before DESs can be used with confidence in pharmaceutical products, especially when they are combined with anticancer or chemopreventive agents like curcumin. In the context of DES‑based drug delivery, it is therefore important to link solubility and thermodynamic data with in vitro measurements of cell viability in the presence of both the DES and the active compound [17, 18].

On this background, the present work examines choline chloride‑based acidic deep eutectic solvents (ADESs) prepared with three simple and pharmaceutically relevant carboxylic acids: propionic acid (ChCl/Pro), acetic acid (ChCl/Ace) and lactic acid (ChCl/Lac). These mixtures use inexpensive, low‑molecular‑weight acids for which toxicological information is available [22, 23], and they offer different patterns of hydrogen bonding and ion–dipole interactions that can influence the solubility of curcumin. The aims of this study are: (i) to determine the solubility of curcumin in aqueous mixtures of these ADESs at different temperatures and DES mass fractions; (ii) to correlate the solubility data with the Jouyban–Acree (JAM), electrolyte Non‑Random Two‑Liquid (e‑NRTL) and UNIQUAC models and to obtain apparent thermodynamic parameters of dissolution (standard enthalpy, entropy and Gibbs free energy) using Gibbs and van’t Hoff relationships; and (iii) to evaluate the cytotoxicity of the ADESs in the presence of curcumin toward MCF‑7 human breast cancer cells using the MTT assay. By combining information on solubility, thermodynamics and biological effects, this work aims to clarify whether ChCl‑based acidic DESs, and in particular ChCl/Pro, can be considered not only as effective solubilizing agents, but also as realistic enabling media for ADES‑assisted delivery of curcumin and its chemopreventive use in breast cancer.

On the other hand, although curcumin solubilization in ChCl-based ADESs has been explored in several studies, a systematic comparison specifically targeting acidic hydrogen bond donors (HBDs) that integrates detailed thermodynamic modeling with in vitro cytotoxicity remains limited. The present work distinguishes itself by providing a comparative assessment of propionic, acetic, and lactic acid-based DESs to clarify how HBD structural features dictate the solvation microenvironment. Furthermore, by deriving activity coefficients through e-NRTL and UNIQUAC models and correlating these with a critical assessment of MCF-7 cell viability—while carefully distinguishing between the effects of the solvent vehicle and the loaded drug—this study provides a more nuanced mechanistic understanding of the interplay between curcumin solubility, thermodynamic driving forces, and biological response in acidic DES media.

Method and material

Materials

Curcumin, propionic acid, acetic acid, L‑( +)-lactic acid, absolute ethanol, choline chloride (ChCl), fetal bovine serum (FBS), RPMI‑1640 medium and thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT) were obtained from commercial suppliers and used as received without further purification. Choline chloride was supplied by Dae‑jung (Korea). Double‑distilled, deionized water was used throughout the experiments. A detailed list of all chemicals, their purities and suppliers is provided in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Preparation of acidic deep eutectic solvents (ADESs)

Acidic deep eutectic solvents based on choline chloride were prepared by mixing ChCl (hydrogen bond acceptor, HBA) with propionic acid, acetic acid or lactic acid (hydrogen bond donors, HBDs) at a molar ratio of 1:2 (ChCl:HBD). The weighed components were placed in a round‑bottom flask and heated with magnetic stirring at approximately 363 K until a clear, homogeneous and colorless liquid was obtained [18]. The resulting liquids were then dried under vacuum at room temperature to remove residual moisture. The water content of each ADES was determined by Karl–Fischer titration. The common features of ADESs employed in this research are summarized in Additional file 1: Table S2.

Solubility measurements

Prior to solubility measurements, a calibration curve for curcumin was established. Known amounts of curcumin were dissolved in a mixture of ethanol and distilled water, and the absorbance of the resulting solutions was recorded using a UV–vis spectrophotometer at 311 nm, corresponding to the maximum absorption wavelength of curcumin [19]. The calibration plot of absorbance versus concentration showed good linearity within the studied range. To confirm that the presence of ADESs did not interfere with the spectrophotometric determination, UV spectra of curcumin solutions containing ChCl/Pro, ChCl/Ace and ChCl/Lac were also recorded; no significant changes in the position or shape of the main absorption band were observed.

The solubility of curcumin in aqueous ADES mixtures was determined using the classical shaking‑flask method. Binary solutions of ADES and water with different mass fractions of ADES were prepared by accurately weighing the components on an analytical balance with a precision of ± 1×10⁻4 g. An excess amount of curcumin was then added to glass vials containing a known mass of the ADES–water mixture. The vials were tightly sealed, placed in a thermostated water bath and shaken for at least three days at the desired temperature (298.15–313.15 K), allowing the system to reach equilibrium. The temperature of the bath was controlled to within ± 0.01 K [20].

After equilibration, the suspensions were centrifuged to separate the undissolved solid from the saturated solution. The clear supernatant was carefully withdrawn and filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filter to remove any remaining solid particles. Aliquots of the filtrate were suitably diluted with an ethanol–water mixture and analyzed by UV–vis spectrophotometry at 311 nm using the previously constructed calibration curve to determine the concentration of dissolved curcumin. The mole fraction solubility of curcumin in each system was calculated from the composition of the saturated solution, using the molar masses and weight fractions of all components according to Eq. Cell culture

The human breast cancer cell line MCF‑7 was kindly provided by the Pastor Institute (Iran). Cells were cultured in RPMI‑1640 medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum under a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Cells were sub‑cultured at appropriate intervals to maintain them in the exponential growth phase. Cell morphology and confluence were routinely monitored using a Nikon Eclipse 80i inverted microscope [21].

MTT cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of the ADESs in the presence of curcumin toward MCF‑7 cells was evaluated using the MTT assay. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96‑well plates at an appropriate density and allowed to attach for 24–48 h under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2). A series of six concentrations of the test samples (ADESs containing curcumin, diluted in RPMI‑1640 medium) was prepared and added to the wells, while control wells received only culture medium [13, 17]. After 48 h of incubation, MTT solution was added to each well and the plates were incubated for an additional 4 h to allow the formation of purple formazan crystals by metabolically active cells [22].

The culture medium was then carefully removed, and the formazan crystals were dissolved by adding dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to each well. The plates were gently shaken in the dark for approximately 1 h to ensure complete dissolution. The optical density (OD) was measured using a microplate reader at 570–630 nm. Cell viability (%) was calculated as the ratio of the absorbance of treated wells to that of the control wells, multiplied by 100. The half‑maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), defined as the concentration of sample that reduces cell viability to 50%, was determined by fitting the concentration–response data using a non‑linear regression logistic model.

Correlation procedure

Thermodynamic modeling

The experimental solubility data of curcumin in the aqueous ADES systems were correlated using three complementary approaches: the electrolyte Non‑Random Two‑Liquid (e‑NRTL) model, the UNIQUAC model and the Jouyban–Acree (JAM) equation [23, 24]. These models were selected because they are widely used for describing non‑ideal behavior and solubility in mixed electrolyte and molecular solvent systems and provide both predictive capability and insight into solute–solvent interactions in pharmaceutical media.

In brief, e‑NRTL and UNIQUAC were applied to represent the activity coefficients of curcumin in the multicomponent ADES–water mixtures, while JAM was used as an empirical tool to correlate the solubility in binary solvent mixtures using a limited number of input data [25]. It is noteworthy that the melting temperature and enthalpy of fusion of curcumin were taken from the literature.

Thermodynamic models are powerful tools for predicting the solubility of medications in various solvents. This is important in the pharmaceutical industry, where scientists need to select the right solvents for drug development and manufacturing. Modern thermodynamic models are based on local composition theory, which accounts for the short-range order and nonrandom molecule orientations that result from variations in molecular size. To calculate the solubility of a solute in a solution at a given temperature, it is necessary to equate the activity of the solute in the saturated solution to its activity in the pure solid state. Jouyban-Acree model (JAM)

Accurate information about the thermophysical properties of solvent mixtures is essential in many fields and at various temperatures. However, it is not possible to experimentally measure all of these properties for all aqueous systems at all concentrations and temperatures [31, 32]. Therefore, the Jouyban–Acree model is one of the best models used to predict and correlate the solubility of a solute in solvent mixtures. This model was used to calculate the solubility of a solute in a binary solvent mixture, as shown in following equation [33]:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ \log X_{m,T} = w_{1} \log X_{1,T} + w_{2} \log X_{2,T} + \frac{{w_{1} w_{2} }}{T}\sum\limits_{i = 0}^{2} {J_{i} (w_{1} - w_{2} )^{i} } $$\end{document}

The Jouyban–Acree model can be used to predict the solubility of a solute in an aqueous mixture of two solvents, denoted by Xm. The model takes into account the weight fractions of the two solvents in the absence of the solute (f1 and f2), the mole fraction solubilities of the solute in the two neat solvents (X1 and X2), and the solvent–solvent and solute–solvent interactions for the binary solvent system (Ji). To determine the parameters of the Jouyban–Acree model, only two experimental solubility data points are needed for the solute in the two neat solvents, at the highest and lowest temperatures of interest.

More advanced predictive thermodynamic frameworks such as PC-SAFT (Perturbed-Chain Statistical Associating Fluid Theory) and COSMO-RS (Conductor-like Screening Model for Real Solvents) were not employed in the present study. These approaches, which can in principle predict solubility from molecular structure alone without experimental fitting, require accurate molecular parameters and quantum chemical input data that were not available for all components of the studied systems. Furthermore, the primary objective of this work was the correlation and comparative evaluation of experimental solubility data rather than purely predictive modeling. Nevertheless, PC-SAFT and COSMO-RS represent valuable directions for future work on DES-based pharmaceutical systems and are expected to provide additional mechanistic insight into solute–solvent interactions [2, 6].

Thermodynamic properties of dissolution

The apparent thermodynamic functions of curcumin dissolution in the ADES‑containing systems were derived from the temperature dependence of solubility using standard Gibbs and van’t Hoff relationships. Solubility data at several temperatures were used to construct van’t Hoff plots, from which the standard molar enthalpy of dissolution (ΔH°ₛₒₗ) was obtained, while the corresponding entropy (ΔS°ₛₒₗ) and Gibbs free energy (ΔG°ₛₒₗ) of dissolution were calculated in the usual way [34, 35]. The relative contributions of enthalpy and entropy to the driving force of dissolution were analyzed at a harmonic temperature of 305.55 K in order to discuss whether curcumin dissolution in the studied systems is predominantly enthalpy‑driven or entropy‑driven [35, 36]. Before interpreting the solubility trends, an important point should be considered. Curcumin is known to be chemically unstable in aqueous media and may undergo hydrolytic and oxidative degradation during prolonged exposure, especially in water-rich environments [43]. Therefore, the values measured in pure water and highly diluted ADES-water mixtures should be interpreted as apparent equilibrium concentrations under the present experimental conditions rather than ideal thermodynamic solubilities. In contrast, DES-rich mixtures are expected to provide a more protective microenvironment by reducing water activity and strengthening solute–solvent interactions, which may partially suppress curcumin degradation and improve the reliability of the measured solubility values [44, 45]. In all systems, solubility increased monotonically with both increasing temperature and increasing ADES content, which is the expected behavior for endothermic dissolution processes and has also been reported for curcumin in other DES and NADES media [3, 41, 46].

Among the studied co‑solvents, ChCl/Pro produced the most pronounced enhancement. At all investigated temperatures, the solubility of curcumin in ChCl/Pro–water mixtures exceeded that in pure water by more than 100,000‑fold at optimal DES fractions, corresponding to an increase of more than five orders of magnitude. The overall solubility order was: ChCl/Pro>ChCl/Ace>ChCl/Lac [41, 47].

When addressing the solubility of curcumin in pure water and highly aqueous mixtures (such as the diluted ChCl/Pro–water systems), a critical physicochemical limitation must be considered: curcumin is notoriously unstable and degrades rapidly in aqueous media. As comprehensively reported in the literature[48], curcumin undergoes rapid hydrolytic degradation in water, which significantly limits its concentration in the aqueous phase. Consequently, the extremely low solubility values measured in pure water and water-rich ADES mixtures during the extended equilibration period should be interpreted as apparent equilibrium concentrations, as they reflect a balance between dissolution and concurrent degradation. In contrast, at higher DES weight fractions, the significantly lower water activity and the strongly hydrogen-bonded DES microenvironment are expected to protect curcumin from rapid hydrolysis, rendering the measured solubility values more representative of true thermodynamic stability and highlighting the protective role of the ADES network.

To put the exceptional solubilization efficiency of the studied ADESs into perspective, it is useful to compare these results with statistical solubility data of curcumin in other conventional media. Literature data indicate that the aqueous solubility of curcumin is extremely poor, typically reported in the range of 11 ng/mL at room temperature. While conventional co-solvents such as ethanol–water mixtures or simple micellar systems (e.g., Tween 80 or SDS) can improve its solubility by 103 to 104-fold, the maximum solubility achieved in the neat ChCl/Pro system in this study represents an enhancement of more than 100,000-fold relative to pure water. This massive enhancement surpasses many traditional alcohol-water and surfactant-based systems, confirming that the specific interactions (such as strong multiple hydrogen bonds and ion–dipole interactions) provided by the acidic DES components offer a much more favorable solvation microenvironment for curcumin.

The observed solubility order, ChCl/Pro > ChCl/Ace > ChCl/Lac, indicates that curcumin solubilization in these media depends not only on general solvent polarity but also on the balance between specific intermolecular interactions and the internal structure of the DES-water network. Curcumin contains phenolic hydroxyl groups, methoxy substituents, and a β-diketone moiety, all of which can participate in hydrogen-bonding interactions with the DES components [3]. In addition, the ionic nature of choline chloride allows ion–dipole and electrostatic interactions that can further stabilize dissolved curcumin [43, 52]. Among the studied systems, ChCl/Pro appears to provide the most favorable balance between acidity, hydrophobic compatibility, and hydrogen-bonding ability, thereby generating a solvent environment better suited to accommodate the aromatic and weakly polar character of curcumin. In contrast, the lower performance of ChCl/Lac may be related to the more strongly associated and highly polar microstructure of the lactic acid-based system, which may reduce the availability of effective solvation sites for curcumin [47, 49].

Water also plays a dual role in these systems. At moderate amounts, water can reduce viscosity and improve mass transfer, thereby facilitating the dissolution process [50]. However, increasing water content progressively disrupts the hydrogen-bond network of the DES and weakens the specific solute–solvent interactions responsible for the high solubilization power of the concentrated eutectic phase [51]. Therefore, the increase in curcumin solubility with DES mass fraction likely reflects not only a composition-driven change in bulk polarity, but also the preservation of a more structured and interactive solvent environment at higher ADES contents. This interpretation is consistent with the marked decrease in the calculated activity coefficients of curcumin as the DES fraction increases.

The very large solubility enhancement observed in ADES‑containing media indicates that specific interactions between curcumin and the DES components dominate over the relatively weak dipole–dipole interactions present in water. Hydrogen bonding between the phenolic and β‑diketone groups of curcumin and the carboxylic acid HBDs, together with ion–dipole and electrostatic interactions involving the choline cation and chloride anion, can significantly lower the activity coefficient of curcumin in solution [52]. This interpretation is consistent with recent mechanistic studies on curcumin in choline chloride‑based NADES, where quantum‑chemical and spectroscopic analyses pointed to strong hetero‑molecular contacts with ChCl and the HBD as the main factors behind the elevated solubility [3, 46].

A comparison with previously reported media further highlights the relevance of the present findings. Curcumin is known to exhibit extremely low solubility in water, whereas conventional alcohol-water mixtures, surfactant-based systems, and micellar formulations can improve its apparent solubility by a factor of 103 to 104 [53, 54]. Nevertheless, the magnitude of enhancement observed here, particularly in the neat ChCl/Pro system (> 100,000-fold), exceeds that typically obtained in many conventional aqueous co-solvent systems. This suggests that the advantage of the present ADESs lies not merely in co-solvency, but in the combination of multiple stabilizing interactions provided by the eutectic microenvironment [3, 47].

Hydrogen bonding interactions

A molecular-level interpretation of the observed solubility trends requires consideration of the specific interactions between curcumin and the DES components. Curcumin exists predominantly in the enol tautomeric form in solution, in which the intramolecular hydrogen bond between the β-diketone carbonyl and the adjacent enol hydroxyl is partially disrupted upon interaction with external hydrogen-bond donors [55, 56]. The carboxylic acid HBDs present in the studied ADESs (propionic, acetic, and lactic acids) can act as strong intermolecular hydrogen-bond donors toward the carbonyl oxygen atoms of curcumin's β-diketone moiety, effectively competing with and breaking the intramolecular hydrogen bond. This disruption liberates additional hydrogen-bonding sites on curcumin and promotes stronger solute–solvent interactions. Simultaneously, curcumin's two phenolic hydroxyl groups can donate hydrogen bonds to the carboxylate oxygen of the HBD or to the chloride anion of choline chloride, further stabilizing the dissolved state. The choline cation ([(CH3)3N⁺CH2CH2OH]) contributes additional interactions through its terminal hydroxyl group, which can accept hydrogen bonds from curcumin's phenolic OH groups. These multiple, cooperative hydrogen-bonding interactions collectively reduce the activity coefficient of curcumin in the DES phase, as directly confirmed by the systematic decrease in γ1 values with increasing ADES content shown in Table 7 [41, 47].

Role of acidity in disrupting curcumin aggregation

The acidity of the DES medium plays a particularly important role in modulating curcumin's aggregation behavior. In neutral and mildly alkaline aqueous environments, curcumin readily forms π–π stacked dimers and higher-order aggregates through face-to-face interactions between its conjugated aromatic rings, which severely limit its effective dissolved concentration [54]. The acidic microenvironment generated by the carboxylic acid HBDs (estimated local pH approximately 3–5 depending on DES fraction and water content) can influence the protonation state of curcumin's phenolic groups and enol moiety, thereby altering the electronic density distribution across the conjugated system. This change in electron density can disrupt the complementary π-orbital overlap responsible for self-stacking, effectively reducing the thermodynamic driving force for aggregation [57]. Furthermore, the protonated form of curcumin's phenolic groups carries a partial positive charge that introduces electrostatic repulsion between adjacent curcumin molecules, further disfavoring aggregation. This aggregation-suppression mechanism may explain part of the very large solubility enhancement observed in DES-rich systems, beyond what can be accounted for by simple solvation effects alone [55, 56].

π–π and hydrophobic interactions

In addition to hydrogen bonding and electrostatic effects, π–π and hydrophobic interactions contribute to the stabilization of dissolved curcumin in the ADES microenvironment. The quaternary ammonium head group of the choline cation [(CH3)3N⁺] can engage in cation–π interactions with curcumin's electron-rich aromatic rings, providing an additional stabilizing force that is absent in conventional aqueous co-solvent systems [58]. This type of interaction has been identified as a significant contributor to the solubilization of aromatic hydrophobic compounds in choline-based ionic media, as supported by molecular dynamics simulations and NMR spectroscopic studies [59]. Furthermore, the alkyl side chains of the HBDs (ethyl group in propionic acid, methyl group in acetic acid) provide hydrophobic microdomains within the DES network that can accommodate curcumin's lipophilic regions through van der Waals and hydrophobic interactions. The larger ethyl group of propionic acid offers greater hydrophobic compatibility with curcumin's aromatic system compared to the shorter methyl group of acetic acid, which may contribute to the superior solubilizing performance of ChCl/Pro. In contrast, lactic acid's additional hydroxyl group increases the overall polarity of the DES network and reduces the extent of hydrophobic microdomains available for curcumin solvation, consistent with its lower solubilizing efficiency [60].

Cytotoxicity of ADES–curcumin formulations toward MCF‑7 cells

For pharmaceutical application, solubility enhancement must be balanced against the biological response to the solvent system [61]. Therefore, the cytotoxicity of the three ADESs in the presence of curcumin was evaluated in MCF‑7 human breast cancer cells using the MTT assay, following standard protocols for viability assessment [62, 63]. Cells were exposed to a series of concentrations of the DES–curcumin formulations, and IC50 values were obtained from non-linear fits of the concentration–response curves.

To enable a more rigorous interpretation of the biological data, the intrinsic cytotoxicity of the solvent vehicles was also evaluated by testing the corresponding blank ADESs under the same experimental conditions. In addition, cell-free MTT interference controls were included to exclude possible absorbance artifacts arising from the DES components. No significant interference with the MTT readout was observed under the tested conditions. These controls are important for DES-containing systems, where the vehicle itself may contribute to the overall biological response [64, 65].

The IC50 data summarized in Table 2 and the dose–response curves shown in Fig. 2 revealed the following order of cytotoxicity for the curcumin-containing ADES systems: ChCl/Lac > ChCl/Pro > ChCl/Ace, corresponding to lower IC50 values and therefore stronger growth-inhibitory effects. Thus, the lactic acid-based system exhibited the strongest reduction in MCF‑7 cell viability, whereas the acetic acid-based system was the least cytotoxic under comparable conditions. The blank ADESs also showed measurable and composition-dependent cytotoxicity, with IC50 values of 0.49 ± 0.01, 0.54 ± 0.01, and 0.61 ± 0.01 mg·L⁻1 for ChCl/Lac, ChCl/Pro, and ChCl/Ace, respectively. Upon incorporation of curcumin, the corresponding IC50 values decreased to 0.34 ± 0.01, 0.37 ± 0.01, and 0.42 ± 0.02 mg·L⁻1, respectively. This quantitative decrease confirms that the curcumin-loaded ADES systems produced a stronger reduction in MCF‑7 cell viability than the corresponding blank ADES vehicles at equivalent DES concentrations [66]. However, these data alone do not establish formal pharmacological synergy, since no combination index or isobologram analysis was performed. Therefore, the observed effect is more appropriately described as an enhanced antiproliferative response rather than a synergistic interaction.

To place these results in a broader formulation context, it is useful to compare them with previously reported curcumin-loaded delivery systems. Recent polymeric and nanostructured curcumin formulations tested against MCF‑7 cells have typically shown IC50 values in the range of approximately 0.5–5 μg/mL, depending on carrier composition, drug loading, and incubation conditions [67, 68]

In the present work, the IC50 values of the curcumin-containing ADES systems were in the range of 0.34–0.42 mg·L⁻1 (i.e., μg/mL), indicating that the antiproliferative activity achieved in these DES-based media is at least comparable to that reported for several polymeric systems. Although direct comparison between studies should be made cautiously because of differences in assay conditions, formulation design, and the way dose is expressed, this result nevertheless suggests that ADES-based solubilization can provide biologically relevant curcumin exposure in vitro [13].

Mechanistically, the enhanced antiproliferative response observed for the curcumin-loaded ADES systems is most plausibly related to the much higher dissolved concentration of curcumin delivered by the ADES media, which increases its apparent availability at the cell interface [12, 69]. In addition, the DES environment may help maintain curcumin in a more molecularly dispersed state, thereby reducing aggregation or precipitation in the aqueous biological medium and facilitating more effective contact with the cells [3]. A further contributing factor may be the ability of DES components to alter the local solvent–membrane environment and thereby influence the transport behavior of dissolved curcumin across the cell membrane, although this possibility remains inferential in the absence of direct uptake measurements [10, 70]. The known instability of curcumin in aqueous media also suggests that DES-rich microenvironments may partially protect the dissolved compound from rapid degradation, thereby prolonging its effective exposure during incubation.

These findings also show that ChCl-based acidic DESs should not be described as uniformly biocompatible or pharmaceutically acceptable without qualification. Although their individual components may be relatively safe, the toxicity profile of the final eutectic system is clearly formulation-dependent. In the present study, both the blank and curcumin-loaded systems followed the same toxicity order, with the lactic acid-based DES showing the highest cytotoxicity and the acetic acid-based system the lowest under comparable conditions [10, 12]. This composition-dependent variability is consistent with recent toxicological profiling of NADES and DES systems, which shows that acidic hydrogen bond donors can induce significant cellular stress and that DES toxicity cannot be inferred solely from the safety profile of the isolated components [17, 65, 70, 71]. Accordingly, the biological performance of DES-based curcumin systems should be interpreted as the result of both improved drug solubilization and the intrinsic composition-dependent effects of the solvent vehicle itself [72, 73].

From a formulation perspective, these findings indicate that ChCl-based acidic DESs can serve as effective solubilizing media for curcumin while also modulating the overall in vitro biological response, provided that their concentration is maintained within an acceptable biological window [12, 74, 75]. Among the studied systems, ChCl/Pro appears to offer the most favorable overall balance between strong solubilization performance and moderate cytotoxicity, whereas ChCl/Lac, despite its stronger antiproliferative effect, also exhibits higher vehicle-related toxicity. Although the present data are promising, further studies involving mechanistic cellular uptake analysis, blank-DES-normalized response assessment, and biorelevant or in vivo models will be required before these systems can be more directly linked to practical drug-delivery performance.

Thermodynamic parameters of curcumin dissolution

The temperature dependence of curcumin solubility in the ADES–water mixtures was further analyzed using van’t Hoff plots to obtain apparent thermodynamic parameters of dissolution. The plots of curcumin solubility data, lnx1 versus (1/T − 1/Thm) in the system based on the ChCl/Pro were presented in Fig. 7 and the standard molar enthalpy (ΔH°ₛₒₗ), entropy (ΔS°ₛₒₗ) and Gibbs free energy (ΔG°ₛₒₗ) of dissolution were calculated for each system and are reported in Table 8. In all three ADES‑containing media, ΔH°ₛₒₗ values were positive, indicating that curcumin dissolution is endothermic. The corresponding ΔS°ₛₒₗ values were also positive, consistent with an increase in system disorder upon transfer of curcumin from the crystalline solid to the solution phase. Analysis of ΔG°ₛₒₗ as a function of ADES mass fraction showed that the free energy of dissolution becomes more favorable as the DES content increases, which mirrors the strong solubilizing effect of the ADESs. To better understand the balance between enthalpic and entropic contributions, the enthalpic (ΔG°H) and entropic (ΔG°TS) components of ΔG°ₛₒₗ were evaluated at a harmonic temperature of 305.55 K. In all systems, the absolute magnitude of the enthalpic term was greater than that of the entropic term, indicating that the dissolution of curcumin in these media is predominantly enthalpy‑driven. This implies that the formation of favorable solute–DES interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonds and ion–dipole contacts) contributes more to the stabilization of dissolved curcumin than the entropic gain associated with increased randomness [77, 81].

Similar enthalpy‑dominated dissolution behavior has been reported for other hydrophobic drugs in DES‑based media and is usually interpreted as evidence that strong, specific solute–solvent interactions are the principal driving force for solubilization [69]. In the context of the present study, the combination of very large solubility enhancement, decreasing activity coefficients with increasing DES content, and positive but dominant enthalpic contributions strongly supports the conclusion that choline chloride‑based acidic DESs, particularly ChCl/Pro, act as highly interactive solvent environments for curcumin [77, 77].

Taken together, the solubility, cytotoxicity, modeling and thermodynamic results suggest that ChCl‑based ADESs can be considered not only as powerful solubilizing agents but also as realistic enabling media for DES‑assisted curcumin delivery, provided that their composition and dose are carefully optimized to balance efficacy and safety for breast cancer‑oriented applications [10, 12, 15].

Conclusion

This study addressed the critical limitation of curcumin’s extremely poor aqueous solubility by systematically investigating its dissolution behavior in aqueous mixtures of three choline chloride-based acidic deep eutectic solvents (ChCl/Pro, ChCl/Ace, and ChCl/Lac) over 298.15–313.15 K. In all systems, curcumin solubility increased with both temperature and DES mass fraction, following the order ChCl/Pro > ChCl/Ace > ChCl/Lac. The ChCl/Pro system exhibited the highest solubilization capacity, achieving more than a 100,000-fold enhancement relative to pure water.

Correlation analysis demonstrated that the Jouyban–Acree model provided the most accurate representation of the experimental data, while e-NRTL and UNIQUAC models reasonably captured the non-ideal behavior of the systems. Thermodynamic analysis based on van’t Hoff and Gibbs equations indicated that curcumin dissolution in the investigated ADES-containing media is endothermic and predominantly enthalpy-driven, consistent with the formation of favorable solute–solvent interactions.

In vitro cytotoxicity studies in MCF-7 cells revealed composition-dependent biological responses among the ADES systems, emphasizing that solubilization efficiency and cytotoxicity must be evaluated simultaneously. Overall, ChCl-based acidic DESs, particularly ChCl/Pro, represent effective solubilizing media for curcumin; future work should address biorelevant dissolution, stability, and in vivo pharmacokinetics before broader drug delivery claims can be made.
