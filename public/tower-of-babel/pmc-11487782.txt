Antimicrobial resistance and clonal relationships of Salmonella enterica Serovar Gallinarum biovar pullorum strains isolated in China based on whole genome sequencing
Authors: Lulu Cui, Peiyong Li, Qi Xu, Jiaqi Huang, Xiaoxue Gu, Mengze Song, Shuhong Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPullorum disease is a serious problem in many countries. Caused by Salmonella enterica serovar Gallinarum biovar Pullorum (S. Pullorum), it creates huge economic losses in the poultry industry. Although pullorum disease has been well-controlled in many developed countries, it is still a critical problem in developing countries. However, there is still a lack of information on S. Pullorum strains isolated from different regions and sources in China. The objective of this study was to supply the antimicrobial resistance patterns and clonal relationships of S. Pullorum from breeder chicken farms.MethodsIn this study, a total of 114 S. Pullorum strains recovered from 11 provinces and municipalities in China between 2020 and 2021 were selected. These 114 S. Pullorum strains were analyzed using whole genome sequencing (WGS). Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) was tested both by genotypic prediction using the WGS method and using disc diffusion to assess phenotypic AMR.ResultsThese 114 sequenced S. Pullorum strains were divided into three sequence types (STs), the dominant STs was ST92 (104/114). Further core genome multi-locus sequence typing analysis indicated that 114 S. Pullorum strains may have a close relationship, which could be clonally transmitted among different provinces and municipalities. Our results showed a close relationship between the S. Pullorum strains found in different regions, indicating these strains may have been transmitted in China a long time ago. Nearly all S. Pullorum strains 94.74% (n = 108) were resistant to at least one antimicrobial class, and 35.96% of the examined Salmonella strains were considered multiple drug resistant.ConclusionOverall, this study showed that S. Pullorum strains in China have a close genetic relationship in terms of antimicrobial resistance, suggesting widespread clonal transmission.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-024-03296-3.

Introduction

Salmonella is an important intracellular pathogen infecting animals or humans [1]. According to the difference in O, H, Vi antigens, there are more than 3000 identified Salmonella serotypes [2]. Salmonella enterica subspecies enterica serovar Gallinarum biovar Pullorum (S. Pullorum) can cause pullorum disease in poultry, an acute systemic disease that mainly infects poultry, especially young chicks [3]. Meanwhile, it can cause persistent or hidden infection in adult chickens, which is vertically transmitted to offspring [4]. Pullorum disease was identified in the nineteenth century and is associated with high mortality (up to 100%) and reductions in productivity in poultry [4]. Although pullorum disease has been well controlled in many developed countries, outbreaks persist among many countries in the world [4–6]. In China, Salmonella infection in chickens has been increasingly frequent and the main prevalent serovar was S. Pullorum, especially in native chicken breeds [7–9].

The overuse of antimicrobials in animal husbandry has led to increasingly serious antimicrobial resistance of bacteria [10, 11]. Recent studies have shown that in many countries, 50–80% of antimicrobials are used in food-production animals [10, 12]. It is reported that antimicrobials have been used to control Salmonella infections. Although the Chinese government is contributing to the reduction of antimicrobial agents used, antimicrobial treatment remains the mainstay of controlling S. pullorum in poultry [13]. The continued unnecessary use of antimicrobial agents has caused the development of bacterial resistance, which reduces drug efficacy and leads to treatment failure in animal husbandry [11, 14, 15]. Moreover, the appearance of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Salmonella strains has been increasingly observed worldwide [8, 16, 17]. Several studies report that pathogenic bacteria showed resistance to many critical antimicrobial classes, including β-lactams, quinolones, colistin, and 3rd- and 4th-generation cephalosporin [18–21].

Pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE), multi-locus sequence-based typing (MLST), and multi-locus variable-number tandem repeat analysis (MLVA) are useful methods for assessing the presence of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) [22–24]. However whole genome sequencing (WGS) may be more useful because it allows for the assessment of the core genome through SNP-based or MLST methods, which can determine evolutionary relationships. Besides, WGS can provide the antimicrobial resistance determinants and prediction of phenotypic antibiotic resistance spectrum [25, 26]. Thus, affordable WGS has been a molecular typing technology widely used to trace the transmission of Salmonella [27–29]. However, there are few studies regarding S. Pullorum of chicken origin in different provinces of China.

Poultry is the most important host of Salmonella, so this study aimed to investigate the antimicrobial resistance of S. Pullorum strains isolated from breeder chicken farms among 11 provinces or municipalities of China. Additionally, we analyzed the antimicrobial resistance and traced the genetic relationship of S. Pullorum among different farms. This study may help develop prevention and control strategies to control pullorum disease in the poultry industry.

Materials and methods

SalmonellaPullorum strains.

A total of 114 S. Pullorum (isolated between 2020 and 2021) were used in this study (Supplementary Table 1). They were isolated according to the Chinese national standard (GB 4789.4–2016) and previous studies [8, 30]. In short, each sample (i.e., 0.5 g water, 0.5 g stool, and 0.5 g tissues of chicken embryos) was inoculated in 4.5 ml Buffered Peptone Water (Haibo, Qingdao, Shandong, China) and incubated at 37 °C/8–12 h. Then a 0.5 ml culture was inoculated into 4.5 ml Selenite Cystine Broth (SC, Haibo, Qingdao, Shandong, China) and Tetrathionate Broth Base (TTB, Haibo, Qingdao, Shandong, China), respectively, and incubated at 37 °C/18–24 h. The culture of each SC and TTB broth was inoculated on a Xylose Lysine Desoxycholate Agar base (Haibo, Qingdao, Shandong, China) and incubated at 37 °C/48 h. Typical colonies were selected and confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays with primers designed for the fimW gene [8]. PCR was performed on isolates identified as S. Pullorum using the specific ipaJ gene for S. Pullorum. S. Pullorum (CVCC526) and S. Enteritidis (CVCC3377) were used as the positive controls.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The susceptibility of the studied strains to ten antimicrobials of six different classes was determined using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method according to the guidelines of the CLSI [31]. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922 was used as a quality control strain. The 10 antimicrobial agents (Binhe, Hangzhou, China) included: ampicillin (AMP, 10 µg), amoxicillin (AMX, 20 µg), tetracycline (TET, 30 µg), doxycycline (DOX, 30 µg), gentamicin (GM, 10 µg), streptomycin (STR, 10 µg), ofloxacin (OFX, 5 µg), sulfamethoxazole–trimethoprim (SXT, 25 µg), cefoxitin (FOX, 30 µg), and ceftazidime (CAZ, 10 µg).

Whole genome sequencing

Genomic DNAs of 114 Salmonella Pullorum strains were extracted by bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). The DNA quality was checked using the NanoDrop 1000 (Thermo, Rockford, IL, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genomic DNA was randomly fragmented to 350 bp by a sonicator (Covaris, USA). The DNA fragments were end-repaired, added A tailing and ligated at the 3′ end with paired-end adaptors (Illumina, USA). The Illumina Novaseq PE150 was then used for whole genome sequencing.

Gene annotation and analysis

The raw reads were uploaded to the EnteroBase Salmonella database (https://enterobase.warwick.ac.uk/), which provides the results of legacy multilocus sequence typing and core genome MLST (cgMLST). Meanwhile, the software SPAdes_3.12.0 was used to assemble draft genomes. We used the prokaryotic genome annotation website RAST (http://rast.nmpdr.org/) to obtain the basic information of the draft genome. The genome sequences were analyzed for plasmid replicons, resistance genes, and mutation sites using the CGE PlasmidFinder database, ResFinder database, ABRicate vs. 0.8, and RGI-CARD vs3.0. The quality control was shown in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

The chi-square test was performed using SPSS software version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A P value of < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Prevalence and geographical distribution of S. Pullorum MLST patterns

A total of 114 S. Pullorum isolates were obtained from different sources among 11 provinces or municipalities of China, from 2020 to 2021 (Fig. 1A and B). Our results showed that the 114 S. Pullorum isolates were identified as three STs: ST92 (n = 104), ST2152 (n = 5), and ST3717 (n = 5) (Fig. 1C). ST92 isolates were widely distributed in commercial embryos, parental embryos, and stool samples, while ST2151 and ST3717 isolates were only recovered from commercial embryo samples. Interestingly, ST92 isolates were found in all eleven regions, while ST2151 isolates were only observed in Guangdong province, and all five ST3717 strains were distributed in Anhui (Fig. 1D).

Antimicrobial resistance phenotype

The 114 S. Pullorum strains were less frequently resistant to quinolones (Fig. 4). The multidrug-resistant analysis showed that 94.74% (n = 108) of S. Pullorum strains were resistant to at least one antimicrobial class, and 71.05% (n = 81) of strains were resistant to two or more antimicrobial classes (Table 1). Moreover, 35.96% of the examined Salmonella strains (n = 41) were considered MDR. Our results also showed that 2.63% (n = 3) of strains were resistant to five antimicrobial classes.

The results showed that 95.7% (45/47), 93.6% (44/47), and 21.3% (10/47) of the strains isolated from 2020 were resistant to amoxicillin, ampicillin, and gentamycin, respectively, indicating that Salmonella strains isolated from 2020 showed a decreasing trend in resistance to these three antimicrobials compared to strains isolated from 2021. S. Pullorum strains showed an increasing trend resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and tetracycline from 2020 to 2021 (Fig. 5A). Notedly, 46 isolates (97.9%) from 2020 were resistant to at least one antimicrobial agent, whereas the rate was 91.0% (n = 61) in isolates from 2021. Regarding strains isolated from the top four provinces, the antimicrobial resistance of S. Pullorum isolates varied in different provinces and cities (Fig. 5B). Both located in the south of China, Jiangxi, and Guangdong provinces showed similar antimicrobial resistance, with isolates from both provinces having 100% resistance to AMX and AMP. Moreover, strains isolated from Jiangsu province presented higher levels of resistance compared with other sources: 82.8% of isolates were resistant to STR, TET, DOX, and SXT.

According to the sources, more than 70% of S. Pullorum strains isolated from stool were resistant to penicillins (AMX and AMP), aminoglycosides (STR and GM), and sulfonamides (SXT) (Fig. 5C). Meanwhile, despite the fewer numbers of isolates than other STs, it seems that ST3717 strains possessed the highest levels of resistance to the tested antimicrobial agents (Fig. 5D). The rate of MDR was significantly associated with the isolated regions of Salmonella strains (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Presence of antibiotic resistance genes

We used the online database to determine the presence of resistance genes and mutation sites of 114 S. pullorum strains (Fig. 6A). All 114 S. Pullorum strains harbored at least one antimicrobial resistance gene. Among them, our results showed that the carrier rates of the blaTEM-1B gene (100% V.S. 49%) - encoding resistance to β-lactamase and sul2 gene (100% V.S. 48%) - encoding resistance to sulfonamide in strains isolated from 2020 was much higher than in 2021. However, the positive detection rates of five genes in strains isolated in 2021 (tetracycline resistance gene tetA, disinfectant resistance gene qacE, sulfonamide resistance gene sul1, class I integron drfA17, and streptomycin resistance gene aadA5) was higher than strains isolated in 2020. Our findings also showed that these five resistance genes were detected and identified in 75 0.86% (22/29) of strains from Jiangsu province (Fig. 6B). The strains from other provinces mainly carried the β-lactamase resistance gene blaTEM-1B and sulfonamide resistance gene sul2. Resistance genes blaTEM-1B and sul2 were detected in 79 S. Pullorum strains recovered from different provinces, municipalities, and sample types. In addition, 13 resistance genes were detected in one strain isolated from Anhui Province, including the aminoglycoside integrons aac6-aph2, aac6-Ii, and ant6-Ia, the β-lactam resistance gene blaTEM-1B, the sulfonamide resistance genes dfrG and sul2, the lincomycin resistance genes ermB, lnuA/B/G, and lsaE, and the tetracycline resistance gene tet(L).

Plasmid replicons prediction

Plasmids were detected in all 114 S. Pullorum strains, of which the plasmid type ColpVC was detected among all tested strains (Fig. 7). IncQ1, IncN, and IncFIB (S) type replicons were only detected in 2021 S. Pullorum strains (Fig. 7A). Compared to the distributions between the isolation provinces or municipalities, our findings showed that strains isolated from Jiangsu province were dominated by plasmid replicons of ColpVC and IncN, while strains from the other 10 provinces or municipalities were mainly dominated by plasmid replicons of ColpVC and IncX1 (Fig. 7B). Additionally, the plasmid type IncX1 was detected in ST92 and ST2152. However, plasmid replicons IncN and IncQ1 were only distributed in ST92 (Fig. 7C). Meanwhile, MDR S. Pullorum strains were significantly associated with the presence of each plasmid replicon (P < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

Although pullorum disease can be controlled by biosecurity in the poultry industry, S. Pullorum is still persistent in the environment of farms and outbreaks are always reported in recent years [8, 32]. Therefore, investigating the genetic characterization of S. Pullorum will provide new insights into the core genome information of S. Pullorum. In this study, we investigated and compared the genomic characterization, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and plasmid replicons of S. Pullorum strains isolated from different provinces or municipalities in China from 2020 to 2021.

With the rapid development of whole genome sequencing, WGS can accurately predict the serotype types of Salmonella. The MLST patterns based on WGS showed that 104 out of 114 S. Pullorum strains were ST92, which was consistent with previous studies [33, 34], followed by ST2152, which was derived from ST92. Meanwhile, ST3717 was derived from ST2152. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on genetic and evolutionary relationships ST92, ST2152, and ST3717. Importantly, our findings also showed that the prevalence of S. Pullorum among different breeder chicken farms was associated with geographic distribution, which implies that these 114 S. Pullorum strains isolated from 11 different provinces or municipalities in 2020–2021 may have a close relationship.

Poultry is a widely consumed meat worldwide, and the indiscriminate use of antimicrobial agents in animal husbandry is conducive to accelerating the development of AMR in bacteria [17]. This study assessed the susceptibility to critically important antimicrobial classes of S. Pullorum strains isolated from 2020 to 2021. Among strains, the highest resistance was observed against ampicillin (69.3%) and streptomycin (67.54%), which was similar to reports of some developed or developing countries [35–37]. High resistance of S. Pullorum strains to sulfonamides and tetracyclines was also observed in this study, which was consistent with reports from different countries [38, 39]. This is not surprising as these antibiotics have been widely used in veterinary medicine for a long time [40, 41]. However, ampicillin is rarely used in the poultry industry, suggesting that the antimicrobial resistance may be related to cross-resistance [42]. Additionally, S. Pullorum strains were largely sensitive to cefoxitin, ofloxacin, and gentamicin, which may be due to the limited use of these antimicrobial agents among the studied chicken flocks.

MDR S. Pullorum isolates have strongly increased under the selective pressure of antimicrobials [43]. Notably, the rate of MDR strains in this study was 35.96% (41/114), which was lower than in Salmonella strains from southern China (57.79%) [44] and Sichuan province (68.38%) [42]. Of the isolates, whereas 25.5% (12/47) of S. Pullorum strains isolated in 2020 were MDR, by 2021 the MDR rate was 43.4% (29/67). S. Pullorum strains in the present study had lower levels of MDR compared to results found from S. Enteritidis or S. Indiana isolates in previous studies [11, 15, 45]. Meanwhile, although antimicrobial resistance was much lower than in previous studies [46, 47], resistance to quinolones and cephalosporins was also observed in this study. Gong et al. reported that the fluoroquinolone resistance rates of S. Pullorum strains developed an quickly increasing tendency in China [43]. The increasing resistance could be due to the presence of conjugative plasmids carrying resistance genes in the environment of poultry farms [48]. Hence, the use of these antimicrobials in the clinical treatment of Salmonella infection should be carefully done in order to slow the emergence of antimicrobial-resistant S. Pullorum in poultry.

The blaTEM-1B makes bacteria acquire resistance by hydrolyzing and inactivating penicillins among Gram-negative bacteria [49]. In this study, the 114 S. Pullorum strains were mainly resistant to penicillins and streptomycin. Among 80 penicillin resistance strains, 77 strains carried the β-lactams resistance gene blaTEM-1B, which may be a main reason for the development of resistance in Salmonella strains.

Tetracycline is always was used to prevent and treat Salmonella and Escherichia coli infections in the poultry industry. The most tetracycline-resistant mechanisms are the main active efflux and protection of the ribosome [33]. Tetracycline resistance is always mediated by tet(A), tet(B), tet(C), tet(D), and tet(G) in Salmonella spp [50]. In the current study, among tetracycline resistance strains, 22 out of 28 strains harbored a tet(A) gene, indicating that the tet(A) resistance gene may be the main cause of resistance to tetracycline among S. Pullorum strains isolated from different provinces and municipalities in China.

Bacteria resistant to aminoglycoside are mediated by various mechanisms, including alteration of the ribosomal binding sites, decreased uptake, decreased accumulation in bacteria, as well as the expression of enzymes that modify and inactivate aminoglycosides [50]. Among the 82 streptomycin resistance strains in this study, 24 strains carried related resistance genes, such as aadA5. The resistance gene aadA5 was initially discovered in the gene cassette of the class I integron [51]. Among the S. Pullorum strains in this study from 2020 to 2021, the detection rate of the aadA5 resistance gene was 18.4%, which did not increase significantly over time. However, 71.9% of S. Pullorum strains showed resistance to streptomycin. Although two strains of streptomycin resistance have detected resistance genes strA and strB, 70.7% of streptomycin-resistant strains did not carry any tested resistance gene in the current study. Resistant strains likely acquire streptomycin resistance through a variety of other mechanisms, such as the rpsL gene (encoding the ribosomal protein S12), and point mutations in 16s rRNA confer high levels of streptomycin resistance by preventing streptomycin binding to ribosomes [52, 53] or by inactivating mutations in the 16 S rRNA methyltransferase GidB [54].

Single point mutations at the Ser83 or Asp87 (i.e., Ser83 to Phe, or Asp87 to Tyr or Gly) in the gyrA gene of the quinolone resistance determining region (QRDR) are usually associated with a mechanism of fluoroquinolones resistance [55]. In this study, our results showed that the mutation occured in the gyrA gene among 113 S. Pullorum strains, which suggests that these tested S. Pullorum strains may have been completely resistant to fluoroquinolone antimicrobials.

Currently, Enterobacteriaceae have been reported to contain 27 Inc groups [56]. Meanwhile, plasmid replicon IncN was detected in Enterobacteriaceae of different origins and sources, which was similar to our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrated that the S. Pullorum strains isolated in breeder chicken farms among different provinces and municipalities of China in 2020–2021 had similar antimicrobial resistance patterns and plasmid presence characteristics. The core-genome SNP results showed that S. Pullorum strains isolated from different regions were genetically similar. Our data will contribute to better monitoring and prevention of pullorum disease.
