Ruptured Popliteal Artery Aneurysm Treated with Open Surgical Repair in a 91-Year-Old Woman: A Case Report
Authors: Shinichi Tanaka, Kiyoshi Tanaka, Jin Okazaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Popliteal artery aneurysms (PAAs) are the most common peripheral arterial aneurysms, although they remain relatively rare. Symptomatic PAAs generally present with limb ischemia due to distal embolization; however, rupture is extremely uncommon, and the optimal management has not yet been established. A 91-year-old woman presented with acute pain in the right leg and swelling of the popliteal fossa and posterior thigh. Computed tomography revealed a 45.1×44.7-mm right PAA. Emergency intervention was indicated, and open surgical repair via a posterior approach using a prosthetic graft was performed. Intraoperatively, a defect in the aneurysmal wall with active bleeding was identified, confirming rupture. The postoperative course was uneventful, and limb viability and graft patency were maintained for 3 years. Prosthetic graft interposition may be a feasible therapeutic option in carefully selected very elderly patients with ruptured PAA, even in the presence of limited distal runoff.

INTRODUCTION

Popliteal artery aneurysms (PAAs) are the most common type of peripheral arterial aneurysm, although they remain rare overall [1]. Some PAAs are identified incidentally, but more than half of patients present with symptoms such as acute lower limb ischemia or chronic limb-threatening ischemia at the time of diagnosis [2,3]. However, rupture of a PAA is extremely rare [4]. Herein, we report the case of a 91-year-old woman with a ruptured right PAA who was successfully treated with open surgical repair. The patient provided written informed consent for publication of the data and figures.

CASE

A 91-year-old woman presented with acute pain and swelling extending from the right popliteal fossa to the posterior thigh. She was a nonsmoker with a history of hypertension. The patient could ambulate independently without assistance prior to admission. Computed tomography (CT) performed at another hospital suggested a ruptured PAA, and the patient was transferred to our institution. Physical examination revealed subcutaneous ecchymosis and swelling in the posterior aspect of the thigh. CT demonstrated a 45.1×44.7-mm right PAA in the above-knee popliteal artery (Fig. 1). Severe arterial tortuosity was observed immediately above and below the aneurysm. Among the infrapopliteal arteries, only the peroneal artery remained patent. The anterior tibial artery was opacified at its origin but was occluded distally, likely because of atherosclerotic disease. No contralateral PAA was observed. CT screening performed at our institution revealed an incidental 48-mm left internal iliac artery (IIA) aneurysm (Fig. 2). Although CT did not show definite contrast extravasation from the PAA, the presence of subcutaneous ecchymosis, swelling extending from the right popliteal fossa to the posterior thigh, and acute pain at rest was considered highly suggestive of rupture or impending rupture. Therefore, emergency intervention was indicated, and open surgical repair under general anesthesia was performed based on the patient’s condition and the anatomical characteristics of the aneurysm.

For conduit and arterial assessment, preoperative CT was used to evaluate the superficial vein and arterial diameters. The ipsilateral great saphenous vein (GSV) measured 2.5-3.3 mm in diameter, without evidence of varicose changes. The ipsilateral small saphenous vein (SSV) measured 1.5-2.7 mm. Subcutaneous fat stranding extending from the popliteal region to the calf was observed, likely reflecting the hemorrhagic or inflammatory changes associated with the ruptured aneurysm. On the contralateral leg, neither the GSV nor the SSV demonstrated a diameter >2.0 mm on CT imaging.

The patient was initially placed in the supine position. A 20-cm segment of the ipsilateral GSV was harvested from the thigh. The distal superficial femoral artery was exposed and encircled with vessel loops for proximal control (corresponding to the level shown in Fig. 1C). Subsequently, the patient was placed in the prone position. A posterior, lazy S-shaped skin incision was made. The tibial nerve was identified, mobilized, and retracted laterally to avoid injury. After systemic heparinization, the distal superficial femoral artery was clamped to control antegrade flow. During dissection of the aneurysm sac, a focal wall defect with active bleeding was identified, establishing the diagnosis of a ruptured aneurysm. Despite adequate proximal control, substantial bleeding from distal branches persisted.

After resecting the aneurysm, interposition grafting using the ipsilateral GSV was initially attempted; however, the procedure was abandoned because of marked vein fragility, significant size mismatch with the native artery, and needle-hole tearing. Therefore, an 8-mm collagen-impregnated knitted polyester (Dacron) graft (Hemashield Gold Knitted Microvel Double Velour Graft, INTERVASCULAR SAS) was selected because it was immediately available at our institution and was considered the most appropriate prosthetic conduit in terms of size matching and handling characteristics in this emergency setting. A proximal anastomosis was constructed at the P1 segment (corresponding to the level shown in Fig. 1E) in an end-to-end configuration. Four interrupted stay sutures were placed, followed by continuous 5-0 polypropylene suturing. A distal anastomosis was created at the P2 segment (corresponding to the level shown in Fig. 1F) using circumferential continuous 5-0 polypropylene sutures. The total graft length was 60 mm. No distal embolic events occurred intraoperatively or postoperatively. The operative time was 4 hours, the estimated blood loss was 760 mL, and six units of red blood cells were transfused.

The hemoglobin level at admission was 9.6 g/dL (hematocrit, 28.7%). Immediately after surgery, the hemoglobin level was 9.9 g/dL (hematocrit, 27.3%). On postoperative days 1 and 2, hemoglobin levels were 9.6 g/dL and 9.0 g/dL, respectively. At discharge, the hemoglobin level had recovered to 9.7 g/dL (hematocrit, 30.7%). No additional postoperative transfusions were required.

CT performed on postoperative day 9 confirmed graft patency and demonstrated a mild diameter mismatch at the distal anastomotic site between the prosthetic graft and native popliteal artery (Fig. 3A). The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged 14 days postoperatively. Aspirin (100 mg/day) was initiated at discharge as single antiplatelet therapy. At the 1-year follow-up, duplex ultrasonography demonstrated an elevated peak systolic velocity at the distal anastomotic site, presumably related to the diameter mismatch between the prosthetic graft and native popliteal artery. Although the patient remained asymptomatic, clopidogrel (75 mg/day) was added, and dual antiplatelet therapy was initiated. Duplex ultrasonography performed 3 years postoperatively confirmed preserved antegrade graft flow (Fig. 3B). The peak systolic velocity at the distal anastomosis remained stable compared with that at the 1-year evaluation, indicating no stenosis progression. Primary graft patency was maintained, and the patient remained free of ischemic symptoms, including intermittent claudication.

Regarding the left IIA aneurysm, the maximum diameter at presentation was 48 mm (Fig. 2). Elective treatment was recommended after recovery from the PAA surgery; however, the patient and her family declined the intervention at that time. At the 6-month follow-up, the aneurysm had enlarged to 57 mm. Subsequently, endovascular treatment was performed, consisting of left IIA embolization followed by aneurysm exclusion using a left iliac artery stent graft. The procedure was completed under general anesthesia, with an operative time of approximately 1 hour. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged in a stable condition with independent ambulation.

DISCUSSION

PAAs have a reported prevalence of approximately 0.1%-1.0% in the general population, making them relatively rare [1]. PAA is often diagnosed after the onset of acute lower limb ischemia, and the reported major amputation rate is approximately 20%, even with therapeutic intervention [2,3]. Although rupture is a major concern in many aneurysmal diseases, rupture of a PAA is uncommon, making accurate initial diagnosis difficult. A recent study reported that ruptured PAA was correctly diagnosed initially in only 8 of 45 patients, half of whom had a prior diagnosis of PAA [4]. Sixty percent of patients with ruptured PAA were initially diagnosed with deep vein thrombosis or a Baker’s cyst [4]. The rarity of ruptured PAA has made it difficult to establish a clear treatment strategy, and no standardized recommendations have been established.

Previous studies have demonstrated the long-term durability of open surgical repair for asymptomatic and symptomatic PAAs [5,6]. Early- and mid-term outcomes of open repair with prosthetic grafts have also been reported to be favorable [7]. Several recent reports have described endovascular treatment of PAAs [8]. A meta-analysis comparing open and endovascular repairs for asymptomatic or symptomatic PAAs reported acceptable short-term outcomes with endovascular repair [9]. Endovascular repair is associated with a lower rate of wound complications and a shorter hospital stay [9]. The weighted mean primary and secondary patency rates of endovascular repair at 3 years were 68% and 80%, respectively [9]. However, open repair, particularly with an adequate GSV, remains the preferred option in suitable patients, especially when anatomical factors are unfavorable for endovascular repair [1,10].

In the present case, endovascular repair with a stent graft was initially considered because of the patient’s advanced age. However, open surgery was performed because of anatomical limitations. Severe arterial tortuosity was observed immediately above and below the PAA, and distal runoff was poor. Stent-graft placement raised concerns regarding graft occlusion and endoleak. In addition, the patient had no history of cancer, heart disease, or pulmonary disease, and cardiac function was well preserved. Therefore, open surgical repair under general anesthesia was considered acceptable. According to the Society for Vascular Surgery clinical practice guidelines, in asymptomatic patients with a life expectancy of ≥5 years, open repair should be considered first if an adequate GSV is available [1]. The definition of an adequate GSV has been extrapolated from peripheral artery disease studies and refers to a vein ≥3 mm in diameter without intraluminal stenosis or synechiae [1].

When an adequate single-segment GSV is unavailable or unsuitable, the choice of prosthetic conduit becomes a critical component of the surgical strategy. In addition to runoff quality and anatomical considerations, the graft material may also influence thrombogenicity, susceptibility to infection, and intraoperative hemostasis. Prosthetic graft materials commonly used for infrainguinal arterial reconstruction include expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE), woven Dacron, and knitted Dacron. Among these, ePTFE grafts are widely used, particularly heparin-bonded devices such as the Gore PROPATEN graft, which have demonstrated acceptable outcomes [1,11-13].

Kim et al. [14] evaluated the outcomes of below-knee prosthetic bypass in the absence of a suitable GSV and identified the associated risk factors in femoropopliteal occlusive disease. The main prosthetic conduit used in that study was a PTFE graft with external reinforcement [14]. At 3 years, the overall primary patency, secondary patency, and limb salvage rates were 58%, 70%, and 80%, respectively [14]. Tibial runoff was significantly associated with primary patency; therefore, careful evaluation of the outflow arteries is essential in decision making for below-knee prosthetic bypass and follow-up [14]. Another retrospective study reported outcomes of open repair of PAAs using prosthetic grafts (12% Dacron and 88% ePTFE; graft diameter, 6-8 mm). When at least two distal runoff vessels were present, the 3-year primary and secondary patency rates were 80% and 91%, respectively. In patients with a single tibial runoff vessel, the corresponding 3-year primary and secondary patency rates were 71% and 77%, respectively [7]. These findings underscore the impact of distal runoff quality on prosthetic graft patency.

Although retrospective comparisons between PTFE and Dacron grafts have been reported, definitive evidence demonstrating the superiority of one material over the other in femoropopliteal reconstruction remains lacking [15]. Similarly, direct comparative evidence demonstrating the superiority of heparin-bonded ePTFE grafts over Dacron grafts is currently unavailable. Moreover, data specifically addressing prosthetic conduit selection for ruptured PAAs are limited. A retrospective study of 45 patients with ruptured PAA reported that 38 underwent graft reconstruction [4]. Autologous vein grafts were used in 22 cases, prosthetic grafts in 14, and composite grafts in two cases [4]. However, the specific prosthetic materials were not described, and graft-specific patency rates were not reported [4]. Regarding infection, available reports indicate no significant difference in infection rates between ePTFE and Dacron grafts [16,17]. In the present emergency case that required immediate hemorrhage control, graft selection was individualized based on intraoperative findings, availability, and procedural familiarity. A collagen-impregnated knitted Dacron graft was selected to facilitate rapid hemostasis and operative efficiency.

As previously reported, this case highlights the importance of screening for concomitant aneurysms in patients with PAA. The incidence of PAA in patients with abdominal aortic aneurysm has been reported to range from 3% to 11% [1]. CT screening in the present patient revealed an isolated left IIA aneurysm. Within 6 months, the aneurysm showed rapid enlargement. Given the invasiveness of open repair, endovascular treatment of the IIA aneurysm was performed.

In summary, a 91-year-old woman with a ruptured right PAA was successfully treated with open surgical repair. Prosthetic graft interposition may be a feasible therapeutic option in carefully selected very elderly patients with ruptured PAA, particularly when rapid hemorrhage control is required. However, given the single-case nature of this report and the limited distal runoff, this treatment strategy should be generalized with caution.
