Spatial analysis of pollution source proximity to early childhood development centers in Gauteng
Authors: Yonela P. Mkunyana, Tahira Kootbodien, Renee Street
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Air pollution is a major public health concern, particularly for young children who are vulnerable to its adverse effects due to their developing respiratory and immune systems. This study assesses spatial exposure risks for early childhood development centers (ECDs) in Gauteng, South Africa, by analyzing their proximity to major pollution sources. Geographic information systems were used to conduct buffer analysis, kernel density estimation (KDE) and spatial autocorrelation analysis (Global Moran’s I) to identify pollution hotspots and potential clustering of ECDs located in high-risk areas. Statistically, the results showed significant spatial clustering of ECDs around pollution sources (Moran’s I > 0, p < 0.05). Using secondary data, results show that Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni have the highest number of ECDs in high-risk zones, despite Tshwane having the most pollution sources. This highlights that exposure is not solely determined by the number of pollution sources but is influenced by spatial distribution and urban density. Integration of Sentinel-5P satellite NO2 and SO2 data further validated the pollution exposure patterns, with elevated NO2 and SO2 concentrations observed along dense urban and industrial corridors, aligning with KDE-identified clusters. Sedibeng showed lower pollutant levels and more unexposed ECDs, suggesting that urban planning and source dispersion may mitigate exposure risks. A risk map developed by overlaying KDE outputs of pollution sources and ECDs highlighted critical hotspots requiring urgent intervention. Overall, the results suggest that pollution exposure is influenced more by population density and spatial distribution of pollution sources than by the sheer number of pollution sources. These findings underscore the need for targeted environmental health interventions in areas with high exposure risk. Moreover, established buffer zones around pollution sources can support safer spatial planning of ECDs.

Introduction

Air quality plays a pivotal role in public health, particularly for children, who are vulnerable to air pollutants. Globally, 92% of the population inhales toxic air, and in developing countries, nearly 98% of children under the age of five are exposed to air pollution (WHO, 2018). The World Health Organization (WHO) reported in 2019 that 99% of the world’s population live in areas that do not meet its air quality guidelines (WHO, 2024). Air pollution is associated with acute respiratory infections, asthma, and reduced lung function in children (Aithal et al., 2023; Bhuda et al., 2024). Young children are especially vulnerable due to their developing immune and metabolic systems, increased respiratory rates, and close proximity to ground-level pollutants. In 2021, air pollution caused over 700,000 deaths in children under five, accounting for 15% of global deaths in this age group (State of Global Air, 2024), highlighting the serious risk of long-term health impacts (WHO, 2018).

In South Africa, air pollution remains a significant public health concern. Gauteng Province, the country’s economic hub, is heavily affected by industrial pollution. The industrial corridor, stretching from Krugersdorp to Germiston including areas such as Vanderbijlpark, has historically experienced high pollution levels due to mining, smelting, and coal power generation (Khanyile & Fatti, 2017). The concentration of pollutants in atmospheric dust provides valuable insights into air quality and pollution sources (Tang et al., 2024). Understanding these pollution patterns is essential, particularly in urban areas where children may be exposed to air pollutants daily, and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) being a major contributor to premature deaths (Clean Air Fund, n.d.). In 2019, air pollution was responsible for approximately 25,800 premature deaths nationwide, making it the second leading health threat in the country (Clean Air Fund, n.d.). Furthermore, in 2021, ambient air pollution accounted for approximately 3365 deaths among children under 5 years old in South Africa (UNICEF, 2024). A South African study analysing the burden of disease attributable to ambient air pollution highlighted Gauteng Province as having the highest number of deaths linked to PM2.5 and ozone exposure in 2012 (Roomaney et al., 2022). During winter 2023, emissions in Johannesburg peaked at hazardous levels, with fine particulate matter (PM2.5) reaching an average of 72 µg per cubic meter—nearly five times the WHO’s 24-h guideline (Evans, 2023). This level of pollution poses significant health risks, particularly for children, the elderly, and those with pre-existing comorbid conditions (Evans, 2023).

Given the high pollution burden in Gauteng, understanding how environmental risks intersect with spaces where young children spend much of their day is critical. Early childhood development (ECD) centers, which provide foundational support for children’s lifelong learning, health, and wellbeing, represent one such environment. The location and accessibility of these ECD centers is influenced by various factors, including population density (Smith & Brown, 2019). Higher-density areas, such as the City of Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni, are likely to have a greater demand for ECD services due to the larger concentration of families with young children (Jones & Patel, 2021). This often leads to a higher number of ECD centers in urban areas but also raises concerns about overcrowding and resource allocation (Ngubane, 2020). In contrast, low-density areas like parts of Sedibeng and West Rand may have fewer ECD centers, limiting access for children in these regions (Mokoena, 2022). However, few studies have specifically used geospatial analysis to quantify and identify areas where young children are at higher risk due to proximity to pollution sources. To address this gap, this study aims to assess the spatial relationship between pollution sources and ECDs in Gauteng, South Africa, using satellite data as proxies for relative exposure, to determine the extent of potential exposure to environmental pollutants.

Materials and methods

Study area

‌This study was conducted in Gauteng Province, the smallest province in South Africa, which covers only 1.5% of the country’s total land area, approximately 18,182 km2 (Tsoriyo, 2024). Despite its size, Gauteng is the most densely populated province. According to the 2001 South African census, Gauteng had a population of 9,388,855, which grew to 12,272,263 by the 2011 census. By July 2022, Statistics South Africa estimated the population at 16,098,571. The steady rise from 9.4 million in 2001 to 16 million in 2022, reflects the significant increase in population density over 20 years.

The province consists of five district municipalities: Ekurhuleni, City of Johannesburg, City of Tshwane, West Rand, and Sedibeng (Fig. 1). Ekurhuleni (Germiston-administrative capital of Ekurhuleni, Springs, Alberton, Boksburg, Benoni) is a major industrial and commercial center. Similarly, Sedibeng (Vereeniging, Vanderbijlpark), and West Rand (Krugersdorp, Randfontein, Westonaria) district municipalities are key hubs for commerce, industry, and mining activities in the province. Gauteng’s mining landscape is dominated by the Witwatersrand Reef, a geological formation rich in gold deposits (GCRO, 2018). The Witwatersrand Gold Belt has historically been the backbone of South Africa’s gold mining industry and extends across major mining districts, including Johannesburg, Ekurhuleni, and the West Rand. While Gauteng is not a major coal producing province, the study area includes three coal-fired power plants, with two located in the City of Tshwane and one in Ekurhuleni municipality (Fig. 1). These industrial activities—mining, smelting, and coal power generation, were selected as major pollution sources in this study because they are major emitters of NO₂ and SO₂ and are strongly associated with adverse respiratory and developmental health outcomes especially in children (Clean Air Fund, n.d.).Fig. 1Map of Gauteng Province showing the spatial distribution of Early Childhood Development (ECD) centers and selected pollution sources (mines, smelters, and coal power plants)

Exposure risk mapping

Data acquisition

Data acquisition involved several key components. The locations of registered ECD centers in 2021 across Gauteng in 2021 were obtained from the publicly accessible list published by the Department of Basic Education (DoBE, 2021). Data on selected pollution sources, including locations of mines and smelters, were sourced from the Department of Mineral Resources and Energy (DMRE, 2024), while the locations of coal power plants were sourced from the Global Coal Plant Tracker Dashboard (Global Energy Monitor, 2024).

Mapping

To assess exposure risks, buffers were created around each pollution source to map proximity-based risk to ECD center. The proximity of each ECD center to these pollution sources was calculated, categorizing them into high (≤ 5 km), moderate (5–7 km), and low exposure (7–15 km) exposure zones. ECD centers located outside the 15 km buffer were considered to have minimal exposure to the select pollutions sources. These zones were generated to show gradients in pollutant dispersion and potential health risk. The classification of exposure zones was informed by both regulatory and scientific considerations. The Mine Health and Safety Act (Zvarivadza et al., 2024) specifies a minimum safety buffer of 100 m between mines and power lines to prevent physical hazards, but this is insufficient for assessing health-related air pollution exposure. Studies have shown that airborne pollutants such as fine particulate matter and heavy metals from industrial activities have the highest concentrations within 10 km of emission sources (National Research Council (US) Committee on Health Effects of Waste Incineration, 2019). Mahlangeni et al. (2024) further recommend a precautionary radius of approximately 20 km around coal power plants to protect surrounding communities from elevated air pollution levels.

Given the diversity of pollution sources in Gauteng—including mines, smelters, and coal power plants, we used buffers that balance proximity sensitivity across source types. The 5 km buffer represents areas of high potential exposure, capturing zones of elevated concentrations typically observed near pollution sources. The intermediate 5–7 km buffer enables assessment of pollutant dispersion dynamics between immediate and distant zones, while the ≥ 15 km buffer capture minimal exposure gradients. The 15 km threshold was chosen as a practical midpoint between the 10 km (for fine particulate matter) and ~ 20 km (for coal power plants) recommendations, ensuring a balanced approach applicable across multiple pollution types without favoring one over another.

The ECD center layer was overlayed and intersected with buffer layers to map ECD centers within each zone. Spatial join and buffer analysis were used to determine the number of ECDs in each zone (high-to-minimal exposure) per municipality. Due to overlapping buffers in highly populated areas, some ECDs may be double counted. As a result, we categorised zones of exposure as highly exposed (< 5 km to pollution source) and minimally exposed (> 15 km) per municipality. Moreover, studies suggest that airborne pollutants (e.g., fine particulate matter, heavy metals) tend to have the greatest concentration within 10 km of a source (National Research Council (US) Committee on Health Effects of Waste Incineration, 2019) as highly exposed ECDs within 5 km of a pollution source may need urgent interventions.

Spatial analysis

A spatial analysis was conducted using geographic information system (GIS) tools to map ECD locations, pollution sources, and exposure zones. Kernel density estimation (KDE) and Global Moran’s I statistics were applied to assess the spatial distribution of ECD centers in relation to pollution sources.

KDE is a non-parametric method for density estimation (Silverman, 1986). It calculates density by fitting a symmetrical kernel function (usually Gaussian) over each point and summing the overlapping surfaces. KDE was used to generate spatial density surfaces for both ECD centers and pollution sources, highlighting areas of high and low occurrences. A 5 km radius was applied to ECD KDE, while a 15 km radius was used for pollution source KDE to account for atmospheric dispersion patterns of NO2 and SO2 plumes, which extend beyond immediate point sources. The KDE outputs were overlaid to identify zones where high-density pollution sources coincided with high-density ECD centers, representing potential high-risk areas. The resulting risk map highlights areas where young children may be at greater exposure to environmental pollutants.

Atmospheric pollution concentration mapping

To complement proximity-based exposure analysis, atmospheric pollutant concentrations were acquired using Sentinel-5P TROPOMI satellite data. Daily tropospheric column concentrations of sulfur dioxide (SO2) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) acquired through Google Earth Engine (GEE), provided high-resolution atmospheric data in near real-time (NRT) format at level 3 (L3). The Sentinel-5P satellite has a temporal resolution of approximately one global revisit per day, allowing for near-daily monitoring of atmospheric pollutants. The spatial resolution of the TROPOMI sensor is approximately 3.5 × 7 km2 for NO2 and 7 × 7 km2 for SO2, providing fine-scale observations suitable for regional air quality assessments. The specific image collections used were the “COPERNICUS/S5P/NRTI/L3_NO2” and “COPERNICUS/S5P/NRTI/L3_SO2” products for the respective concentration maps over Gauteng. Both images used in the analysis represent the mean concentrations over the period from 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2021, computed by applying a.mean() reducer over the specified date range. The Google Earth Engine function calculates the pixel-wise average over the entire time range, so the image used in the analysis is not tied to one specific date. The images represent an average (composite) of daily NO2 & SO2 satellite observations collected between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2021, not a single snapshot. The raster images were exported and symbolized in ArcGIS to highlight areas of high and low concentration.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Stata SE 18. Descriptive statistics were first computed to NO₂ and SO₂ concentrations across pollution sources (coal power plants, mines, and smelters). Prior to inferential analysis, normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

The test indicated that NO₂ concentrations were not normally distributed (p = 0.0196), while SO2 concentrations followed a normal distribution (p = 0.1273). Therefore, different statistical approaches were applied for the two pollutants.

To examine the relationship between NO2 and SO2 concentrations, a Spearman’s rank correlation was performed, as this method does not assume normality. To compare concentrations of NO2 and SO2 among different pollution source types, a Kruskal–Wallis test was used for NO2, and a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for SO2 since it met normality assumptions. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

To assess the effect of proximity to pollution sources on air pollutant exposure, ECD centers were grouped based on their distance to the nearest pollution source: within 5 km and beyond 15 km. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare NO2 concentrations due to non-normal distribution, while a two-sample t-test was used for SO2.

Discussion

This study provides critical insights into potential pollution exposure at ECD centers across Gauteng Province. The results reveal that exposure levels are influenced by both the spatial distribution of pollution sources and population density, rather than the number of pollution sources within an area. In the City of Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni municipalities high exposure levels may be attributed to urban congestion, where ECDs are in close proximity to pollution sources. In contrast, the City of Tshwane, while having the highest pollution sources, showed relatively lower ECD exposure. This suggests that exposure risk is influenced more by population density and the spatial proximity of pollution sources to ECD centers, rather than the number of pollution sources within an area.

The spatial analysis highlights a concerning overlap between areas of high ECD center density and pollution hotspots, particularly in Johannesburg, Ekurhuleni, and parts of Tshwane. The observed spatial clustering-confirmed by Moran’s I-indicates that pollution exposure in Gauteng disproportionately affects specific communities. In South Africa, ECD centers encompass a wide range of socioeconomic backgrounds and fee structures, from low fee public and community-based centers to high-fee private institutions. In Gauteng, this diversity is particularly pronounced due to the province’s urban density and economic disparities. This variation is further shaped by proximity of many low socioeconomic communities to historic mining sites and industrial zones, which disproportionately exposes children attending these ECD centers to environmental risks compared to more affluent communities.

Gauteng’s municipalities show distinctive exposure characteristics. For example, Sedibeng, with a notably higher number of unexposed ECDs, appears to be a potential low-risk area, possibly due to lower population density and better urban planning that separates pollution sources from residential and childcare facilities. However, further investigation is needed in West Rand, where a significant proportion of ECDs are exposed despite the area having only a moderate number of pollution sources. This anomaly raises concerns and the need for further investigation into localized pollution dispersion mechanisms and potential hidden sources of emissions. The statistical results provide quantitative validation of these spatial trends, showing that the highest pollutant concentrations were consistently observed near industrial and traffic-related areas within Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni. These municipalities should therefore be prioritized for interventions.

In addition to the modelled exposure risks, satellite derived SO2 and NO2 concentrations provide further overlapping evidence of pollution exposure risks among ECDs in Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni. Thus, reinforcing the reliability of modeled patterns. The inclusion of air quality data strengthens the exposure assessment by demonstrating that pollution risks are spatially variable, even within municipalities. For example, certain parts of Tshwane, though having many pollution sources, showed lower pollutant concentration levels, suggesting better dispersion or lower emission intensity. These observations align with our findings that exposure risk is influenced more by spatial distribution and urban density than by number of pollution sources.

While previous studies in South Africa have examined air pollution exposure and its impact on public health (Rother et al., 2019)-particularly in urban areas, research specifically analysing the exposure of ECD centers to pollution sources in Gauteng Province remains limited. The review by Kalisa et al. (2023) emphasized the need for more air quality measurements in schools and intervention studies to protect children's health and reduce exposure in classrooms. Similarly, the findings of this study align with these concerns by demonstrating that pollution exposure is higher in densely populated areas, placing children in these environments at greater risk compared to those in less populated regions. Furthermore, this study emphasizes the spatial variability of pollution exposure, helping to identify pollution hotspots where children may be disproportionately affected. This geospatial approach strengthens the case for targeted interventions and policy measures aimed at mitigating air pollution exposure in vulnerable communities. The results resonate with global findings (Loftus et al., 2020; Zanobetti et al., 2024) showing that marginalized populations disproportionately experience higher exposure, where pollutant levels may exceed safe thresholds. Moreover, these findings support health-protective zoning for early-life environments in line with the World Health Organization Air Quality Guidelines (2021) and Clear the Air for Children policy (UNICEF, 2016), which both emphasize prioritizing pollution mitigation in child-centered spaces.

The integration of geospatial and statistical analyses in this study offers strong evidence that pollution exposure risks are spatially and socioeconomically patterned. ECDs located within 5 km from emission sources consistently experience significantly higher pollutant levels, suggesting the need for regulatory buffers or exclusion zones for new ECD facilities near major industries or highways. Implementing green buffer zones, stricter zoning laws, and improved air quality monitoring could substantially reduce risks for young children. However, this study has several limitations. Firstly, this study applied secondary environmental data sources and did not include direct field sampling or measurements of air, water, or soil quality. As a result, potential spatial or temporal variability in environmental exposures could not be directly validated. Secondly, only registered ECD centers were included, despite the awareness of various unregistered and informal ECDs across the study area. Thirdly, only selected pollution sites were used, and they do not represent all emission sources in the province. In addition, PM2.5 concentrations–an indicator of fine particulate pollution was not included in this study due to the unavailability of freely accessible, high-resolution satellite data comparable to Sentinel-5P SO2 and NO2 products. The omission of PM2.5 may therefore limit the completeness of the exposure risk assessment, as particulate matter often co-varies with other pollutants and has significant health implications for children. Lastly, while satellite-derived data provides valuable and extensive coverage, ground-based air quality monitoring would improve validation. It is important to compare the modelled risk map with the real pollution levels in exposed and unexposed areas (Gintamo et al., 2021). Nonetheless, the combination of geospatial mapping and statistical analysis enhances confidence in the findings. The statistically significant differences in NO2 and SO2 concentrations between ECDs within 5 km and beyond 15 km provide robust evidence that distance is a key exposure modifier. The convergence of satellite, spatial, and statistical evidence strengthens the case for integrated environmental health surveillance and targeted interventions in urbanized, high-density municipalities.

Conclusions

This study provides valuable insights into the spatial distribution of ECD center exposure to air pollution in Gauteng. The KDE revealed that Johannesburg and Ekurhuleni face the highest exposure risk, highlighting the need to prioritize these municipalities for targeted intervention. The findings suggest that pollution exposure is influenced not only by the number of pollution sources but also by population density and spatial distribution. Statistical comparison confirms that ECDs located within 5 km of pollution sources experience significantly higher NO2 and SO2 levels than those beyond 15 km, demonstrating a clear distance-exposure gradient. These results underscore the importance of integrating environmental risk mitigation into urban planning, particularly in densely populated urban centers.

Targeted interventions, policy reforms, and enhanced environmental monitoring are necessary to protect young children from the adverse effects of air pollution. The convergence of spatial and statistical evidence provides a strong scientific basis for immediate action to reduce exposure in early learning environments. Future research should incorporate real-time air quality monitoring, longitudinal environmental surveillance and assessments of the health outcomes among children attending ECD centers. Such studies will be key for long-term health monitoring and for strengthening the evidence base needed to inform sustainable policy and planning decisions.
