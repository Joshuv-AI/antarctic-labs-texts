A week in the life of the human brain reveals stable states punctuated by chaotic-like transitions
Authors: Maxwell B. Wang, Max G’Sell, James F. Castellano, R. Mark Richardson, Avniel Singh Ghuman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Critical real-world neurocognitive processes, such as settling into a conversation and neurophysiological fluctuations, vary over minutes-to-days in real-world environments. We harnessed simultaneous multi-electrode intracranial and video recordings in twenty people during a week of unconstrained, spontaneous behavior. Using dynamical deep learning algorithms, we found neurodynamics linked to circadian rhythm, heart rate, and multiple aspects of behavior (socializing, watching a screen, sleep depth, etc.). Transitioning between behaviors was associated with bursts of rapid, chaotic neural exploration that stabilized into new states. Despite this chaos, large-scale dynamics anchored to a stabilizing center manifold associated with neurophysiological and conscious states, with a central attractor involving default mode network activation. When perturbed by sleep deprivation, neural transitions were more chaotic and dynamics around the central attractor were suppressed, suggesting diminished neurodynamic control due to lack of sleep. These findings highlight how the brain chaotically transitions around a stabilizing equilibrium to balance dynamic exploration and stable equilibria during real-world behavior.

Introduction

Whether we are fatigued from attending a teleconference call or eager to read a book, whether we feel vibrant and ready to start our day or weary and winding down to sleep, many neurocognitive processes in our lives slowly fluctuate over minutes to hours to days in uncontrolled real-world environments. Behaviorally, we transition between tasks like interacting with devices and talking to friends over minutes-to-hours. Neurophysiologically, the interaction between someone’s brain and body is driven by hormones, sympathetic, and parasympathetic drivers related to processes like sleep, arousal, and circadian rhythms that fluctuate over a similar timescale. Yet, most of our understanding of human brain activity comes from well-controlled experiments (e.g., studying reactions to carefully chosen stimuli over milliseconds to seconds) or examining spontaneous neural activity from subjects “resting” inside a neuroimaging machine over relatively short timescales. Some studies have longitudinally analyzed brain state dynamics over longer timescales by repeatedly sampling a few minutes spread out over days to years using functional neuroimaging1–9. Some have examined recordings over days in limited settings, often by examining periodically taken snapshots of brain activity over days, showing that the brain forms identifiable neural states that can be linked to phenomenon such as neurophysiological correlates of depression phenotypes10,11 or a few circumscribed real-world behaviors12–15.

However, we know very little about how the brain continuously shifts and moves between neural states, especially outside of traditional experimental timescales and conditions. Here we leveraged chronic intracranial recordings in twenty neurosurgical participants (60–126 electrodes implanted per participant) undergoing treatment for epilepsy (Fig. 1A, Supplemental Figs. S1–3, Supplemental Table 1). We examined brain dynamics for between 75 and 283 h (near-continuous recordings across approximately 3–12 days). Using explainable deep learning and dynamical systems analyses (e.g., Koopman operators), we asked four major interrelated questions about the “kinematics” of the brain: First, how do individual areas of the brain change over time and are there reliable differences in how different anatomical regions do this? Second, what overall brain states emerge from these individual dynamics, how does the brain continuously change and fluctuate between those states, and how do they relate to behavior and physiology? Third, are there consistent stabilizing factors and anatomical drivers that anchor these dynamics? Finally, when the brain’s ability to form waking behavior is perturbed through sleep deprivation, how do these dynamics change?

Results

Functional parcels showed consistent fluctuations over days that could be linked to behavior and physiology with consistent anatomic trends governing their dynamics

Before studying how the whole brain evolves over the course of a week, we started by breaking it down into smaller parts and studying how those parts evolve over time. We used a data-driven approach to identify small groups of tightly connected electrodes that made up coherent functional brain parcels. Our first test was to validate our signal processing approach by testing whether we could link this activity to neuro-behaviorally relevant phenomena, namely real-world behavior and physiology.

After removing an hour before and after ictal (seizure) events as determined by the clinical team, we calculated the coherence between all pairs of electrodes in each participant every five seconds over five frequencies: theta (θ: 4–8 Hz), alpha (α: 8–12 Hz), low beta (βl: 14–20 Hz), high beta (βu: 20–30 Hz), and gamma (γ: 30–70 Hz)16. Electrodes were grouped into tightly connected, anatomically compact groups of electrodes, each a “parcel” of the brain (Fig. 1A) for regional analysis of brain dynamics. These parcels were chosen to optimize the overall modularity of the chosen subgroups (maximizing the connectivity within parcels while minimizing the connectivity between parcels) according to the Leiden algorithm17. In practice, parcels tended to be approximately two centimeters in size (compared with the average distance between electrodes, which was approximately six centimeters). After removing parcels and activity associated with participant seizure onset zones, we validated these data by assessing whether the neural activity from these parcels could be used to predict behavior and physiology. Here, activity was defined as the average coherence of the electrographic activity of a parcel, which ranged from zero to one and was meant to capture the activity of a parcel that was shared across it (see supplement for discussion). In practice, parcel coherence was correlated to the average power across parcels (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Specifically, we trained predictive classifiers to learn patterns of activity associated with behavior (defined broadly as either interacting with a digital device, socializing with family or friends, or manipulating a physical object) and physiology (defined as circadian rhythm and heart rate). For simplicity, for behavior, we started with linear classifiers trained on data from one day and then tested on a different day. For physiology, we trained on the first half of the week and tested on the second half. We found we could predict behavioral and physiological markers as shown in Supplementary Fig. S9 (average area-under-curve (AUC) for behavioral classification of 0.73, p = 0.03; average Spearman R of 0.28 for heart rate and 0.24 for time of day/circadian phase). More detailed analyses regarding consistent anatomical locations and more fine-grained analyses of behavior and physiology required nonlinear methods, which are explored later in Sections 4 and 5; this initial test was simply meant to validate our recording and analysis process.

After confirming that our processing pipeline could detect neurobehaviorally relevant phenomenon, we asked how individual parcels fluctuate throughout the week. In Fig. 1B-top, we plotted the activity of different parcels over the course of the week-long recording period. Fluctuations of this activity showed characteristic temporal scales that were repeated over different hours and days of data. We quantified this stability by how slowly each parcel’s autocorrelation decayed (timescale). Timescale differences between parcels were stable over time, indicating that parcels that fluctuated faster or slower would remain so throughout the week (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Differences between parcels were quantified over all participants by grouping parcels into six canonical networks (“default mode”, “dorsal attention”, “salience”, “somatomotor”, “control”, and “visual” as defined in ref. 18). Default mode parcels consistently showed higher autocorrelation magnitude and longer decay timescales across our participants, whereas salience and visual network parcels showed shorter timescales (Fig. 1B.3). These findings demonstrate a temporal hierarchy separating “fast” and “slow” regions of the brain. A temporal hierarchy, often measured using autocorrelation, has been hypothesized in the brain with transmodal systems, such as the default mode network, slowly integrating data from faster unimodal regions, such as the visual network, over seconds to minutes depending on the task1,2,19–21. Our results extend these findings to minutes-to-hours in a real-world setting during natural behavior.

Brain networks underwent bursts of rapid transitions that coincided with natural shifts in behavior

We next examined how the overall distribution of activity across the measured brain changed throughout the week. To expand from an individual parcel analysis to large-scale brain network activation, we first identified parcels and frequencies that covaried with each other using robust principal components analysis. We grouped these parcels and frequencies together into individual “network components”, each capturing a linear axis of neural activity across both anatomical space and frequency. We then asked how does the brain’s state move around in the high-dimensional space spanned by each network component’s activity? We started with the brain’s basic kinematics: overall position (which network components were active or inactive at each time window) and velocity (how quickly those network components changed over time). Specifically, velocity was defined as the Euclidean distance between the network activations of one-time window to the next in the principal component space (we avoid the term speed here due to its cognitive connotations).

Figure 2A.1 shows brain network activation patterns from one participant for the full week (position; top) and how quickly these activations changed between consecutive time windows (velocity; middle and bottom). We found that the brain alternated between stable states of relatively consistent network activations that lasted for minutes-to-hours and “bursts” of high-velocity, where the brain rapidly modulated its network activations (brain transitions) before stabilizing into a new configuration (brain state). Specifically, windows with high velocity tended to occur temporally adjacent or close to one another (bursts of high velocity) at significantly higher rates than if they occurred randomly via homogeneous Poisson process (Fig. 2A.2; Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, p < 0.05 in all 20 participants individually). There was also a significantly higher rate of long time periods with no high-velocity windows lasting for hundreds to thousands of seconds. This indicates the presence of “stable states” of neural activity: extended periods of time when brain activity is relatively consistent. To assess if these neural transitions related to behavioral transitions, across the video-annotated group, we marked times when participants began or ended one of the three behaviors annotated as described in the prior section. The results demonstrated that neural and behavioral transitions tended to coincide with one another moreso than if the neural and behavioral transitions were randomly distributed across time (Fig. 2B; p = 7e-5, paired t-test), validating that changepoints detected in the neural data are neurobehaviorally meaningful. Notably, roughly two-thirds of linked neural transitions preceded the associated behavioral change as shown in Supplementary Fig. S26. On average, neural transitions preceded behavioral transitions by roughly 16 s. The coarseness of the behavioral annotations means that each behavior involves multidimensional processes and there are likely behavioral or cognitive transitions not well captured by the behavioral annotations (such as a substantial transition in a conversation would not be captured as it is still socializing). Indeed, about a quarter of neural transitions were not within 30 s of an annotated behavioral transition on average. When the analysis is restricted to neural and behavioral transitions within 30 s of one another, neural transitions preceded behavioral transitions by an average of eight seconds.

This finding may be a generalization of the Bereitschaftspotential, or “readiness potential,” phenomenon, where neural activity starts to change roughly a second before a simple motor task is executed, thought to reflect the brain’s preparation23. Notably, in more complex and realistic experiments where participants are required to determine both the type and timing of movement, neural activity can change in predictable manners up to around five to ten seconds in advance of a decision24,25. These results (Fig. 2B) may be the extension of these findings to more realistic environments. Notably, the “tasks” in this study consisted of relatively complex behavior where even “social interaction” involved not just language and speech processes but also emotional and affective processes. The preparation that must occur in the brain before such a shift is likely more complex than what may be observed under traditional neurocognitive experiments.

Brain network transitions were circuitous, unpredictable, and chaotic

How does the brain change during neural transitions? We started with the priorly defined neural state-space: a representation of the brain in a high-dimensional Euclidean space where each axis marks the activation of a single brain network (the brain’s state in the principal components-defined space). A single time window forms a point in this space, a transitory burst (a series of consecutive time windows) becomes a trajectory. To assess these trajectories, we asked does the brain take relatively straight trajectories when transitioning between states or are brain trajectories more circuitous?

For visualization purposes, two transitory bursts from one participant are shown in Fig. 3A-left. Qualitatively, many transitions did not go directly from one state to another but rather took circuitous routes that explored many interim states before reaching their destination. Here, we use “explore” as a mathematical description of traversing interim states that fall off a direct path between a trajectory’s starting and ending point. We quantified this across participants by measuring the distance traversed by a trajectory (the trajectory’s length) and comparing it to its displacement (the straight-line distance between the starting and ending point). Specifically, distance was defined as the sum of the Euclidean distance between each time window to the next throughout the entire transition (path length), while displacement was defined as the Euclidean distance between the starting and ending states (straight line/shortest distance). Rather than taking the shortest distance between states, during these transitions, the brain would traverse a path that was 8.9 ± 1.19 (95% confidence interval across all participants). In comparison, during stable states, it would traverse paths that were 5.99 ± 2.47 times longer than the net displacement.

To provide context to these distance-to-displacement ratios, a Brownian motion (random diffusion) would have a ratio of 5.56. In other words, during transitions, the brain explores its state-space in a less efficient manner than a diffusive random walk. This is termed a “subdiffusive” exploration where instead of exploring new ground quickly like a standard random walk, a system will be temporarily trapped and thoroughly explore a local area before moving on, causing more circuitous transitions26. This can be due to structural limitations, for example, water flowing through a porous rock will follow subdiffusive geometry due to physical limitations in how water is able to flow27. Alternatively, it can be an intentional strategy to thoroughly explore local regions before moving on: Plasmodium sporozoite (malaria) parasites alternate between subdiffusive and superdiffusive exploration strategies when finding blood cells to latch onto28. Here we see the brain alternate between diffusive and subdiffusive strategies during stable states and transitory bursts.

Furthermore, the circuity of these transitions relates to the timing of behavioral changes. For each behaviorally related neural transition (restricted to cases where the neural and behavioral transition were within 30 s of one another), we calculated the time between the start of the neural transition (inward changes in the brain) and compared it to the start of the behavioral transition (outwardly observable changes in behavior). Transitions were divided into those with “late” behavioral changes (those where the time between the start of the neural transition and the behavioral transition fell within the top third of transitions for that participant) and those with “early” behavioral changes (those that fell within the bottom third). On average, “late” behavioral changes tended to lag 11 s or earlier behind the onset of the neural transition, while “early” behavioral changes tended to precede neural transitions by two or more seconds. We found that late behavioral changes tended to be associated with neural transitions with overly circuitous routes compared to early behavioral changes, which had comparatively more direct transitions (Fig. 3B, p = 0.027). This may be analogous to a real-world version of the Bereitschaftspotential24,25 wherein more complex (more circuitous) neural transitions are associated with longer neural preparation for the subsequent behavior.

While transitions were circuitous, were they consistent? For example, if the brain switched from state “A” to state “B” multiple times during a week, did it take a similar route each time? We compared transitions with the same start and end point to transitions with the same start point but different end points (Fig. 3C). If transition trajectories were repeated over the course of the week, the distance between repeated pairs of transitions (pairs of transitions that share the same starting and ending states) would remain small compared to the distance between diverging pairs of transitions (pairs of transitions that start in the same state but end up in differing ones). Instead, the first half of these paths displayed almost as much variability between bursts sharing a destination as between bursts with different destinations (Fig. 3C). Only when ~75% of the trajectory was complete did the variability between these groups diverge by a single standard deviation. Thus, even if two trajectories had the same start and end point, the first leg of the trajectories were typically very different from one other.

Given that transitions were circuitous and inconsistent, we hypothesized this may be because neural transitions are particularly sensitive to small perturbations of neurodynamics—e.g., chaotic. We tested this using 0–1 chaos tests, finding that neural chaoticity rose during these transitions (Fig. 3D; p = 2e-4, paired t-test). Furthermore, overly chaotic transitions were associated with a longer time between the start of the neural transition and the behavioral transition (Fig. 3D; p = 0.026, paired t-test).

We related these neural transitions to two currently existing hypotheses regarding how change can rapidly spread throughout the brain, namely self-organized criticality and metastability. First, we found that the size of these neural transitions (total distance of a transition as defined above) and the time between them followed power laws over minutes-to-hours (Fig. 3E; p = 0.024 to p = 7e-5 for likelihood tests of power law versus exponential or log-normal distribution, see “Methods” section “Neural dynamics undergo chaotic-like transitions that are associated with shifts in natural behavior” for statistical details). These power laws are often associated with chaotic and critical systems29,30 and are a focus of the “critical brain hypothesis31.” Criticality argues that many large, multi-component systems exhibit complex behavior when the system is held on a critical boundary between two self-amplifying regimes25. Maintaining a balance between inhibition and excitation is believed to hold the brain at such a boundary29,32. Small fluctuations on this boundary are then amplified into larger cascading bursts of transitory activity, which follow characteristic power law dynamics30,33,34. For neural processes, this is suggested to yield a balance between maintaining a stable representation of the brain’s current state while remaining flexible enough to rapidly adapt to environmental changes or changing behavioral/cognitive demands35. In artificial intelligence, criticality has been identified in efficient optimization of neural networks36.

These dynamics are also consistent with the metastable brain perspective, which hypothesizes that the brain temporarily stabilizes neural states far away from pre-existing attractors to perform specific tasks when necessary and leaves them when those tasks are completed37,38. This hypothesis argues the brain suppresses this stabilization routine when transitioning in and out of these states39. In Supplementary Fig. S14, we use simplified numerical models to demonstrate that while metastability-like concepts can allow the brain to hold consistent states that are not overwhelmed by stochastic noise, this stabilization makes performing state transitions difficult. Temporarily reducing stabilization within the system can result in faster state transitions, but it also makes the system uniquely susceptible to deflections during a transition, by stochastic noise and/or other drivers such as exploratory processes, potentially leading to fast-moving, chaotic, and circuitous state transitions such as we see here. These chaotic transitions are unlike what might be seen in a simple multi-attractor state model or if metastability-like processes were constantly activated. Prior literature has suggested that these times of transitions might be optimal times for modulation/intervention due to temporary lack of strong stabilization39.

Neural transitions that induce behavioral transitions do not occur in a simple, straightforward manner where the brain goes directly from one pattern of network activation to another, turning the old set “off”, and a new set “on.” Rather the brain rapidly explores several intermediate states in a chaotic, circuitous, and variable manner before stabilizing onto a new pattern of stable activity. The more chaotic and circuitous the transition—the more exploratory of the state space it is—the longer it takes for underlying changes in neural activity to result in outwardly observable shifts in behavior. This suggests that a cognitive concept, such as “how deliberatively versus automatically” we transition from one behavior to another, is associated with the degree of chaoticity and circuity in large-scale brain network dynamics. To better understand this phenomenon, we asked two major questions. First, are there any anatomical pathways that appear to drive or suppress these chaotic dynamics? Second, how do these metrics change when the brain is placed under a clinically relevant stressor such as sleep deprivation?

Neural dynamics predict behavior and are driven by a central attractor involving activation of the default mode network

While the brain’s transitions are chaotic and disordered, are there consistent controlling or driving forces behind them across participants? To answer this, we turned to dynamical systems analysis39 combined with neural networks. System dynamics, in and out of biology, are typically analyzed according to their critical points and their eigenvector dynamics. Critical points are areas in the system that draw in (stable “attractors”) or push out (unstable “repulsors”) the system’s dynamics39. We describe springs by how they stabilize towards their equilibrium point, sodium or glucose levels by their fluctuation relative to homeostatic set points. Eigenvectors are components of the system that share similar dynamical properties and trends39 and are used to describe how the system behaves around these critical points. Here, eigenvectors represent collections of brain networks whose activity tends to evolve together over time, attractor points represent configurations of these networks whose activity appears particularly temporally stable.

Due to the non-linear, complex, and chaotic dynamics seen in Fig. 3, we used data-driven, self-supervised deep recurrent neural networks (DNNs) and Koopman operators40 to identify these dynamic properties. While conventional dynamical systems analyses requires assumption of linear dynamics, DNN-Koopman operators are specifically designed to lift a non-linear system to a higher-dimensional latent space whose dynamics are organized linearly40, allowing for interpretability of their dynamics.

Using the model described in Fig. 4A and Supplementary Figs. S17 and S18, we learned a nonlinear representation (manifold) of these dynamics. Specifically, we trained a self-supervised neural network to project the original brain network activation profiles into a non-linear manifold where each axis represents a non-linear function of brain networks. These axes are defined to allow for neural dynamics to be captured linearly. This DNN-Koopman approach to building a self-supervised model from millions of datapoints over days creates a participant-level neurodynamic “foundation model” with interpretable dynamics. Additional machine learning models can be built on this foundation for additional tasks, such as classifying behavior and determining the neural correlates of those behaviors. The interpretable dynamics that arise from the DNN-Koopman approach to building a foundation model provides the opportunity to discover neurodynamic features, such as attractor points and important manifold dynamics. Learning this representation improved our capability to classify behavior relative to linear methods (Fig. 4B.1; p = 0.017, paired t-test). Physically manipulating an object consistently activated the dorsal attention, somatomotor, and salience networks. In Fig. 4C.1, we plot the flow diagram of the brain’s dynamics from one participant: how the brain’s state tends to change as a function of which networks are active/inactive, and their overall tendency is to drift towards a central attractor state. All participant flow diagrams are shown in Supplementary Fig. S23. At this attractor state, the brain consistently activated the default mode network while trending towards deactivating the visual network across participants (Fig. 4C.1; p = 0.01 and 0.018, respectively). There was also an increase in low frequency theta and alpha band activity and a suppression of higher-frequency high beta and gamma activity at the central attractor state (Fig. 4C.2; cutoff for this analysis was p < 0.05 after multiple comparisons correction).

Having identified a consistent “reference” point in the representation that was shared across participants, we next assessed how different behavioral and physiological states organize around this central attractor and whether there was any consistency in how neural dynamics appeared to be controlled around it.

We quantitatively verified the structure’s organization in two ways. First, we confirmed that the brain departed from the central attractor during times of active behavior compared to times of wakeful rest (Fig. 5A.3; p = 0.03, paired t-test). Second, we identified the structure’s primary dynamical axis. In dynamical systems analysis, a system’s temporal evolution can be decomposed into its constituent parts (eigendecomposition), one aspect being the “center manifold” which represents the most stable, long-lasting patterns in the system. We plotted the orientation of this center manifold in two participants in Fig. 5A.1 and found that it visually aligns as a “sleep-wake” axis that captures different stages of both sleep and waking behavior. We quantified this by separating the brain’s dynamics into two groups: how the brain moves along the center manifold (up and down the axis) and how it moves off it (perpendicular to the axis). Here we use the term “along the center manifold” to describe movement that is parallel to the center manifold, the eigenvector with the slowest eigenvalue. “Off-manifold” dynamics in comparison represents movement that is orthogonal to this eigenvector. We calculated the correlation of these dynamics to circadian phase (via time of day), heart rate (via EKG recordings), and heart rate variability (as shown in Supplementary Fig. S22). The brain’s position along the manifold was significantly more correlated to these physiological metrics than its position off of it (Fig. 5A.3, p = 0.002, paired t-tests; notably circadian rhythm and heart rate are associated with one another and are not entirely independent quantities).

We tested how specific behaviors and sleep stages organized along this manifold in Fig. 5B in the video-annotated group. Across participants, neural states along the center manifold consistently separated into different stages of wake and sleep, sorted by their level of outward orientation and depth of consciousness during sleep: outwardly active waking behavior, times when participants were awake but not outwardly active, shallow stages of sleep, and deeper stages of sleep (determined via sleep score classifier41). As the brain moved along this center manifold, it modulated an anatomically consistent set of networks involving the default mode, dorsal attention, and somatomotor networks (Fig. 5C; cutoff of p < 0.05, one-sample t-tests corrected for multiple comparisons in all cases). Different kinds of active waking behaviors (such as digital vs social interactions) did not separate along this axis, even though they separated in the wider DNN-Koopman space off the center manifold. Furthermore, movement orthogonal to the center manifold was comparatively rapid and chaotic compared to movement along it (Fig. 5D; p = 0.014, paired t-test). Given the known difficulties of analyzing geometries in nonlinear spaces42, to verify the generalizability of these results with different analytical approaches, we instead analyzed brain activity along the first principal component dimension of the Koopman feature space in Supplementary Fig. S24, and using conventional linear analyses without any nonlinear neural network component in Supplementary Fig. S25, finding similar results, albeit with diminished statistical power.

We next asked, are there kinematics that may help explain the hourglass-like shape? Specifically, neural activity appears to show large amounts of dynamic variability during both outwardly active waking behavior and deep sleep as shown by the width of the hourglass at its top and bottom. However, it appears to be narrowed around the central attractor. We quantified this by dividing each participant’s week-long brain activity into ten quantiles by their center manifold activity and calculating the average neural velocity and chaoticity within each quantile. Across the twenty participants, neural velocity was maximized at either end of the manifold (the hourglass’ top and bottom) and dampened in the middle near the central attractor (Fig. 5E.1; p = 0.006, paired t-test). Neural chaoticity was maximized during deep sleep, decreased as the brain approached the central attractor and stayed at those lower levels during waking states (Fig. 5E.2).

Taken together, deep sleep, shallow sleep, wakeful rest, and outwardly active waking behavior all differentiated from each other in non-trivial ways. Specifically, outwardly active waking behavior was marked by high center manifold activity, high neural velocity, and low chaos. Wakeful rest and shallow sleep were marked by center manifold activity close to the center attractor, low neural velocity, and low chaos. Deep sleep was marked by very low center manifold activity, high neural velocity, and high chaos.

Sleep deprivation affects both the stable and chaotic components of neural kinematics

How are these dynamics perturbed under adverse conditions, namely sleep deprivation? Earlier, we found that the delay between neural and behavioral transitions was predicted by how exploratory (chaotic and circuitous) the neural transitions were (Fig. 3B and D). What happens to these transitions following sleep deprivation would likely relate to what “drives” this chaoticity. If chaoticity and circuitousness is a metastability phenomenon where the brain constantly has to “control” noise/minor perturbations, but must temporarily remove this suppression to allow for fast transitions39, the chaoticity and circuitousness would increase if this stabilization process were impaired. Additionally, if this control phenomenon were disrupted, we would see that the brain’s capability to stabilize states far away from the central attractor to be impaired, shifting waking center manifold activity downwards. Alternatively, “neural fatigue” or tiredness may result in less dynamic exploration of the state-space, resulting in reduced chaoticity and circuitousness during transitions following sleep deprivation. Broadly, waking neural dynamics would look similar to wakeful rest or shallow sleep following sleep deprivation under a simple neural fatigue framework (Fig. 5B and E).

We identified eight participants who had a day where they were sleep deprived and another day where they were “relatively rested” at baseline (relevant sleep data, including number of hours slept and number of night-time seizures, shown in Supplementary Table 3). Of those eight, four were intentionally sleep deprived for clinical purposes, and the other four spontaneously had trouble sleeping in the hospital. The following results are reported with both groups combined, but all results remain statistically significant for both groups when analyzed separately.

To examine neural transitions when participants were comparatively sleep deprived, we collected all neural transitions during the first 12 h of waking activity in each sleep condition across the eight participants and calculated their circuitousness (ratio of distance to displacement) and chaoticity, as in Section 3. When participants were sleep-deprived, transitions were more circuitous and chaotic than while relatively rested (Fig. 6A; p = 4e-5). Specifically, distance traversed during a transition was significantly increased (p = 4e-5) while displacement between transition starting and ending states was not perturbed significantly (p = 0.23). We next tested how activation along the center manifold differed following sleep deprivation. We selected times when participants were awake and outwardly active and times of wakeful rest across the 12-h waking period for both days. Neural activation along the center manifold was closer to the central attractor during both conditions (Fig. 6B; p = 3e-4). Neural velocity also decreased following sleep deprivation (Fig. 6C).

Notably, sleep-deprived states did not resemble any normal wake or sleep stage. It had center manifold activity close to the attractor, low velocity, and high chaoticity. This combination differs from normal deep sleep, shallow sleep, or wakeful rest (Fig. 5). This suggests sleep deprivation does not simply “fatigue” the brain and make it “sleep-like” but rather forms a unique waking state with diminished control of its dynamics and chaoticity.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the expression, organization, and temporal dynamics—the kinematics—of neural and behavioral states during approximately a week of continuous natural human behavior. Brain dynamics consisted of long periods of relatively stable states punctuated by sharp bursts of dynamism—neural transitions where the brain would chaotically and circuitously explore many areas before settling into a destination. These bursts occurred when behavior changed, such as switching from looking at a smartphone to talking with a friend. The more chaotic and circuitous the neural transition, the longer it took for underlying neural changes to result in outwardly observable changes in behavior. Sleep deprivation increased the chaoticity and circuitousness of these transitions.

By projecting the data into a non-linear space using deep learning algorithms optimized for dynamical systems, we found that neurobehavioral states formed an hourglass-like structure around a dampened central attractor between wake and sleep. Behaviors clustered in predictable locations along this structure, allowing us to assess network activation patterns associated with specific behaviors, such as talking to a friend or watching a device. The hourglass’s central axis was formed by the “center manifold”, a nonlinear component of brain network dynamics consisting primarily of modulation of the default mode and dorsal attention networks, with outward active behavior forming one end of the axis and deep sleep on the other. Times when participants were awake but not outwardly interactive or in shallow sleep formed the central funnel. This funnel was maintained by a stabilizing central attractor involving low-frequency activation of the default mode network.

Neural activity around the central attractor was stabilized, showing significantly reduced velocity compared to both the waking and sleeping ends of the hourglass (Fig. 5E). It is not surprising that neural activity during wake and sleep is separable43, nor that attractors can be found when applying dynamical models to datasets. However, velocity suppression around an attractor is not trivially expected. Some dynamical systems, such as pendulums or springs, show high velocity around their central attractor (Supplementary Fig. S21). Systems with slow dynamics around an attractor, such as what is seen here for neurodynamics often display significant separation between a system’s slow versus fast dynamics44. When these “slow vs fast” systems approach their attractors, the fast dynamics stabilize first before the slow dynamics have reached equilibrium, causing slow dynamics to dominate around the attractor45. In biological neural circuits, slow vs fast dynamics have been argued to allow a circuit to balance between competing needs of flexibility and stability through two groups of dynamics that are coupled but distinct, without one dominating the other. For example, a computational study illustrated how neural circuit models can exploit slow vs. fast dynamics to quickly respond to new inputs while also slowly updating an internal memory state45. Here, in large-scale brain networks at timescales orders of magnitude longer than circuit-level models, we find slow center manifold dynamics relating to our physiology (such as circadian rhythm, heart rate, and state of consciousness) versus fast punctuated equilibrium dynamics relating to our behavior. These dynamics couple physiology and behavior together but also keep them neurologically distinct and balanced. The fact that sleep deprivation upsets this balance by causing the slow dynamics to dominate may indicate potential consequences for its disruption, given the known deleterious effects of sleep deprivation on cognition, behavior, and brain health.

These results suggest the brain must control or modulate its dynamics on both slow and fast timescales. On a fast timescale, the dynamics form a punctuated equilibrium of relatively stable, low velocity states to rapid, chaotic, and circuitous neural transitions in response to changing behavioral demands. While the brain keeps its chaoticity during waking behavior relatively low compared to deep sleep, during neural transitions, chaoticity spikes with large spikes being associated with delayed manifestation of outward behavioral changes. This supports aspects of metastability theory, which argues neural state stabilization is an active process rather than a passive consequence of multi-attractor states37. This framework hypothesizes that the brain uses this stabilization to maintain consistent neural states but suppresses it when rapid neural transitions are needed and increases it again once the desired state is reached39. This would explain the increase in chaoticity and circuitousness during neural transitions (see Supplementary Fig. S14). Sleep-deprivation may reduce control over this stabilization process, resulting in even more circuitous and chaotic transitions. Here, control refers to the brain’s capacity to modulate its chaoticity, not diminished cognitive control or task switching efficiency, though it is notable that both are impaired following sleep deprivation. On a slow timescale, active waking behavior-related states were organized “high” on the center manifold (Fig. 5A), requiring the dynamics to be pushed and temporarily stabilized away from the central attractor. Sleep deprivation perturbed this slow timescale control, diminishing the brain’ capacity to temporarily stabilize its dynamics away from the center attractor. In this fashion, sleep-deprived states were not simply “sleepy” or “rest-like” but rather formed a unique waking state with signs of diminished control of chaotic dynamics.

Slow versus fast dynamics, where the fast activity comes in bursts of rapid, chaotic behavior-related fluctuations, aligns with the idea of “punctuated equilibrium” from evolutionary biology and 1/f avalanches in cellular automata46,47. Evolutionary history does not show steady and gradual development, but rather alternates between periods of relative stability and transient bursts of rapid change46. These transitory periods are relatively disorganized and involve phylogenetic “explosions/explorations” that generate multiple species or variants that quickly undergo environmental selection22, generally in response to a large environmental change. Similarly, the explore-exploit tradeoff literature argues that many systems incentivized to adapt to changing environments will alternate between exploration-heavy strategies that search for new solutions and exploitation-focused stratagems that fine-tune a single one48. These systems must balance a short-term goal of optimizing known solutions (exploitation) versus the long-term benefits of searching for a potentially better solution (exploration). In the brain, different brain networks are specialized for different situations. When the brain encounters a situation not adequately addressed by its current network configuration, it may need to determine whether to continue with its current state or whether to find an entirely different configuration to switch into. Stable states punctuated by bursts of rapid exploration may indicate how the brain balances these two competing strategies. Many systems, from biological ones48 to artificial neural networks49 to evolutionary systems46 that need to optimize the explore-exploit balance display such punctuated equilibria, which has also been argued to be a hallmark of efficient group decision-making33. Furthermore, the point between stable and chaotic dynamics is commonly referred to as the “edge of chaos” in the self-organized criticality literature, which argues this point is a computationally efficient way of quickly distributing information across a system32,50.

These results also recontextualize the default mode network as a central attractor in the stabilizing slow regime, which is consistent with its relatively long autocorrelation (Fig. 1B) and low velocity at the center manifold (Fig. 5E). While previous work has shown that the default mode network can function as a slow integrator of neural activity from fast sensory networks2, here we extend that body of work to a much broader timescale across both waking and sleep, finding that it forms a central hub of stabilized, slow dynamics that governs the transition from sleep to wake.

These central attractors and the slow dynamics surrounding them are also a key component of the homeodynamics literature, which argues they represent how “set points” in biological systems shift over time51,52. For example, we have a resting heart rate of 70 beats per minute that has both fast inter-beat variability and slow oscillations according to physiological drivers53. Under that interpretation, “default mode network activation” may be akin to that resting point with central manifold activity representing its slow stabilized fluctuations. Since manifold dynamics involved broad-scale brain fluctuations, they are likely associated with fluctuations of many physiological and neuromodulatory systems of the body. For example, they likely interact with hormones or stimulants associated with circadian rhythms and arousal (Fig. 5A), such as melatonin, cortisol, or caffeine, all of which show impulse responses associated with stabilized processes. Stabilized fluctuations also offer potential explanations for undesirable rebounds from pharmacological agents we know can have a sharp effect on circadian-related physiology, such as caffeine, melatonin, or cortisol. For example, while melatonin has seen increasing usage in restoring circadian rhythms54, many people report an undesirable “rebound effect” where people will sleep deeply for a few hours after ingestion before sharply waking, unable to return to sleep55.

Rebound effects are identified elsewhere in stabilized physiological systems, such as rebound hypertension from short-acting blood pressure medication or rebound hyperglycemia after excessive insulin use56,57. Investigating these fluctuations using dynamical systems and artificial intelligence-guided analyses as we do here, has resulted in a burgeoning field of closed-loop controllers in diabetes and intraoperative blood pressure control58,59, an approach transferable to the brain under this framework. Notably, the non-linear dynamical systems methods used here, namely DNN-Koopman operators, have seen increasing use in control engineering60.

One other application of this work could be generalizing brain-computer interfaces (BCI) to real-world environments. Unlike controlled lab environments, everyday tasks are dynamic and unpredictable. Performance and usability degrades for BCI algorithms that do not adjust to changes in brain state when these states shift. By better understanding the ways our brains dynamically shift from one behavior to another in real-world, chaotic environments, we could potentially make BCI devices more robust and scalable for real-life applications as assistive devices or communication tools.

One consideration about this work is the sparse, heterogeneous coverage of electrodes across participants. The neural network-driven DNN-Koopman operators used here are very similar to non-linear variants of Luenberger observers that are designed to capture properties of a system even when only directly recording a subset of it. These methods make system-level estimations by converting large amounts of temporal data of a restricted view of a system into underlying estimates of the system’s overall state61. These observers use lengthy stretches of temporal data to estimate the dynamics of unmeasured regions through their influence on measured regions. This property of the algorithms used may explain the inter-participant reliability seen in these dynamics, such as anatomical consistency of behavior-related networks, central attractor, and center manifold dynamics, despite the sparse and heterogeneous measurements.

Another consideration is the relatively coarse behavioral measures used. The behaviors studied here are complex and multifaceted, such as “socializing”, which involves cognitive, affective, and emotional circuits, but the annotations are coarse and thus cannot parse these influences further. Despite this, we can still identify key brain-behavior relationships with these measures, such as links between neural and behavioral transitions. While capturing finer-tuned aspects of behavior is possible, likely requiring microphones and/or additional cameras (this study used cameras already present as standard of care and thus limited field of view and resolution), these present bioethical challenges as participant private and clinically sensitive moments are likely to be captured. Furthermore, additional video and audio recording would capture visitors and hospital employees who may be difficult to appropriately consent62 (see supplement for discussion of additional considerations). This consideration is also related to the use of autonomous dynamical systems analyses to analyze neural activity (e.g., we are only able to relate how the brain’s state at one time step relates to its state the prior time step without directly capturing environmental input to the brain). As a result, it is not possible to be entirely certain which of these dynamics were entirely self-generated versus driven by external forces. Additional work involving more fine-tuned recordings capturing the “input” to the brain: light, sound, touch, etc., would help to clarify this question.

This study demonstrates the potential for using a DNN-Koopman approach for using self-supervised learning leveraging millions of data points that can be collected via multiday neural recordings to build a neurodynamic foundation model to understand brain-behavior and brain-physiology relationships. We found that training such a model over unlabeled data can enhance the decoding performance of behavioral classifiers by identifying salient manifolds on which to project neural activity (Fig. 4B). With richer cognitive, behavioral, and physiological measurements, this approach can provide a detailed understanding of brain-body-behavior interrelationships. Understanding this interrelationship can advance the study of neurocognitive states during natural behavior and model how they are perturbed by environmental, pathological, pharmacological, and/or physiological factors. The inter-participant reliability suggests that approaches for building across individual foundation models may be possible. Such a cross-individual model may reduce the data needed for modeling new participants’ neurodynamics, provide avenues for individual differences, and could be leveraged to better understand and potentially treat pathology.

In summary, continuous human brain recordings over a week illustrate that brain networks transition between states via unpredictable, rapid, and chaotic trajectories. These trajectories appear to explore many possible brain states before stabilizing into local states that predictably correspond to behavior. Activation of the “default mode network” during wakeful rest serves as a central equilibrium point for the system. This equilibrium point, along with the central manifold that spans levels of outward orientation along a wake-sleep axis, governs slow, stable dynamics of the brain. Following sleep deprivation, the chaoticity and circuitousness of transitions increases, and the dynamics stay closer to the central equilibrium point, consistent with sleep deprivation impairing the brain’s capacity to control or modulate its dynamics. These results suggest that the complex dynamics, behaviors, and cognitions during real-world behavior are an emergent property63 of alternations over minutes-to-hours between stable exploitation of specialized local brain states and flexible exploration of the brain’s possible configurations during chaotic and circuitous state transitions.

Methods

Participants

Twenty participants (nine males, eleven females; mean age 40 years with a standard deviation of 11 years) had intracranial surface or depth electrodes implanted for the treatment of pharmacologically intractable epilepsy (Fig. 1A). Due to sample size, analyses were not segregated by gender. All participants gave informed consent to participate under research protocols approved by the University of Pittsburgh Institutional Review Board. Depth electrodes were produced by Ad-Tech Medical and PMT and were 0.86 and 0.8 mm in diameter, respectively. Grid electrodes were produced by PMT and were 4 mm in diameter. Eighteen participants had depth electrodes, two had grids. The main results of this paper, when studied on an individual participant-by-participant basis, did not differ significantly between those with depth electrodes and those with grids. Participant demographic and electrode placement details are summarized in Supplemental Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. S1–3.

Analysis overview

In summary, we collected (M3) and preprocessed chronic intracranial recordings continuously over multiple days, which we divided into five-second non-overlapping windows, and calculated the functional partial connectome of each window via all-to-all electrode coherence (M4). The electrodes were grouped into tightly connected parcels (M5) and the timescales (autocorrelation) of the parcels determined (M6). We then grouped parcels and frequencies into functional network components using Principal Components Analysis (M7) and studied how the overall mixture of all functional networks would change over time (M9–11). Finally, we learned a non-linear dynamical systems model of these networks using a deep recurrent neural network to find consistent anatomical drivers of these brain network dynamics (M12–15).

Artifacts were removed at multiple points in the analysis. Specifically, a comb filter was applied to remove line noise (M3). An hour before, during, and after all seizures were removed to eliminate ictal and peri-ictal activity (M4). Spatial regression was used to remove motion, respiratory, and cardiac artifacts (M4). ICA was used to remove large spike artifacts that sometimes occur due to disturbing the cables or connections (M4). Epileptogenic areas and activity that correlated with the activity in these regions was removed to eliminate interictal activity or other pathological activity (M4/5/8).

All statistical tests were performed as two-tailed tests.

Intracranial EEG data collection

Electrodes were localized via postoperative MRI or CT scans, coregistered to the preoperative MRI using Brainstorm64. Surface/grid electrodes were projected to the nearest point on the preoperative cortical surface, automatically parcellated via Freesurfer to correct for brainshift65. Electrode coordinates were then coregistered via surface-based transformations to the fsaverage template using Freesurfer cortical reconstructions. Intracranial electroencephalography data was collected using the Natus system at 1 kHz. Notch filters at 60 and 120 Hz were applied with a subsequent bandpass filter from 0.2 to 115 Hz.

Data preprocessing and artifact removal

The spatial autocorrelation between an electrode and all electrodes within 2 cm of it was measured and regressed out to remove both local and global artifacts66,67 (regression-based local rereferencing), including artifacts due to motion and current spread due to volume conduction (we chose this threshold because spatial autocorrelation had fallen off significantly by 2 cm). Assessment of the impact of alternative referencing schemes is described in Section “Parcellation”.

Neural activity an hour before and an hour after all seizures, whether electrographic or clinical, was removed before calculating coherence. A board-certified neurologist identified the seizure network in all but two participants, with those two participants having no recorded electrographic or clinical seizures during their stay in the hospital.

The data was then separated into five-second non-overlapping windows and coherence was computed over each window between all pairs of electrodes over five frequency bands: theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), low beta (14–20 Hz), high beta (20–30 Hz), and gamma (30–70 Hz). In summary, this generated five connectome structures every five seconds. This was performed using the scipy coherence function under default settings as of version 1.9.3.

Independent component analysis was then applied, and components were visually inspected for any artifacts, which were then removed. The main criteria for removal were independent components that possessed time course activations that were clearly non-neurological (these ranged from step-functions, near Dirac deltas, square pulses, or other very discrete, digital waveforms).

Parcellation

For each participant, we parcellated their electrodes into groups of tightly coherent electrodes. Note that at this stage of analysis, coherence is used as the distance measure for forming functional parcels and thus frequency bands are collapsed together to get a single parcellation scheme per participant. In all other analyses, the frequency bands are examined separately. Furthermore, later coherence between parcels with respect to frequency is examined, with methods that also allow for cross-frequency coupling. This use of coherence, both as a distance measure for local parcellation in modularity algorithms and for examining network-level effects, is similar to how resting-state correlations are used for both functional parcellation and connectomics in fMRI68. We utilized the Leiden algorithm to identify a single regional atlas that optimized graph modularity (a common metric in graph parcellation that calculates the strength of connections within parcels compared to the strength of connections between parcels) over the entire week-long period across all five frequency bands17.

We found that across participants, the similarity between the universal ground reference and spatial reference had an average Rand index of 0.92 and a standard deviation of 0.03. To ensure potentially pathological activity was removed from our analysis, we removed elements of the activity of non-seizure-related parcels that appeared to correlate to the activity of parcels within the seizure onset zone. More specifically, using linear regression, we regressed out anything that correlated with the coherence from seizure-related parcels from all other parcels of the brain.

We chose to analyze how this coherence varied throughout the week as a measure of the “coherent activity” within a parcel for two primary reasons. First, it captured the activity within a parcel that was shared across electrodes within it, capturing what the parcel was “doing” as a whole. Second, it was highly correlated to the electrographic power within that parcel (Spearman’s R 95% confidence interval across participants was [0.42, 0.59] as shown in Supplementary Fig. S4), indicating that in practice, coherence and electrographic parcel did not diverge dramatically from one another.

Autocorrelation stability (Fig. 1)

We tested whether the autocorrelation of each parcel’s coherence would show relatively consistent patterns of “fast” or “slow” rhythms throughout the week (Fig. 1). We split the entire roughly week-long time course for each participant into six-hour non-overlapping segments. After removing parcels associated with the seizure network, we then took the average coherence between electrodes within a single parcel for a single frequency band and then calculated its autocorrelation up to one hour in step sizes of five seconds. We tested whether parcels from different anatomical regions tended to have reliable differences in their autocorrelation across participants using linear mixed effect models. We assigned each parcel to one of the six canonical fMRI networks defined in ref. 18 (“default mode”, “dorsal attention”, “salience”, “somatomotor”, “control”, and “visual”) based on which network it had the largest number of electrodes in (two parcels from two participants had an equal number of electrodes in two separate networks and were excluded from this analysis). We used MATLAB’s fitlme (linear mixed effect model) to model each parcel’s autocorrelation parameters with the anatomical group as a fixed-effect and the participant as a random effect, allowing us to determine whether one anatomical group had a reliably higher autocorrelation parameter than the other across participants. We repeated this for all possible pairs of fMRI networks and used Benjamini–Hochberg multiple comparisons correction to identify pairs with significant differences (Fig. 1B.3).

Robust principal components analysis

Since many parcels tended to be highly colinear, we used a modified PCA protocol to reduce dimensionality and to also further identify parcels that covaried with the seizure network. We grouped parcels and frequencies that tended to covary together using random sample consensus PCA (RANSAC-PCA) on the parcel coherences. By taking the average intra-parcel coherence during each time window and frequency, we formed a (number of parcels × 5 frequency bands) by (number of time windows) 2D matrix, which we then reduced to a (number of components) by (number of time windows) matrix using the modified PCA protocol. This identifies parcels and frequencies that tend to strongly covary together that we denote as a single “network component” feature that captures within and cross-frequency relationships across parcels while also reducing the dimensionality of the original dataset to simplify further analyses.

The modified PCA protocol we used applies random sample consensus to avoid PCA’s susceptibility to noisy outliers by taking multiple small subsamples of the data and selecting one with the fewest number of outliers to train the model70. We generated 1000 subsamples, where in each subsample, we selected six 30-min segments of data from each day. This ensures that the PCA is robust to rare outliers and that the PCA produces principal components that are stable across days within a participant. Outliers were defined by calculating the Mahalanobis distance between each time window’s feature vector and each subsample’s distribution. In each participant, we found that these distances would take on clear bifurcations between relatively small distances and short “spikes” of extremely high distances (more than three standard deviations) away from the mean that typically lasted for a few minutes. We manually drew a cutoff for each participant that was approximately half the average Mahalanobis distance of these spikes. For each subsample, we calculated the number of outliers within the subsample and calculated PCA over the subsample with the fewest outliers. We utilized enough PCs to capture 90% of the variance in the dataset, generally resulting in 12–24 networks/PCs per participant.

The network component activation of a principal component was defined as the projection of the parcel coherences onto the principal component weights.

We also repeated the analysis detailed in Section “Autocorrelation stability” and Fig. 1 except on the network components rather than the parcels, finding that network components also showed consistent differences in their timescales (Supplementary Fig. S8).

Seizure network removal

When analyzing parcel dynamics (Fig. 1), we excluded all parcels with electrodes part of the seizure onset zone and early propagation as defined by a board-certified neurologist. For network component dynamics (Fig. 2 onwards), we first re-added these seizure-related areas before grouping parcels and frequencies into network components through robust PCA. We then removed any network components that were associated with the seizure network before analyzing their dynamics. The goal was to identify parcels whose activation strongly correlated with those of seizure-related areas and remove them from our analyses. More specifically, we calculated the dot product similarity between the absolute value of each principal component vector (normalized to a magnitude of one) and a binary vector that marked all electrodes that were part of the seizure network (also normalized to one). The similarity between these two vectors indicated how anatomically similar the driving factors of a principal component and the seizure network were to each other. A null distribution for this similarity was formed by randomly permuting the principal component vectors, and all principal component vectors that showed statistically significant similarity to the seizure network (p < 0.05) were removed from all further analyses. The similarity between each principal component and the seizure network are shown in Supplementary Fig. S7.

Network activation is tied to natural behavior and physiology

We tested whether we could identify combinations of network components that were associated with natural behavior. Participant behavior was manually annotated for three activities: watching a digital screen, socializing with someone else, or physically manipulating an object. These three behaviors were not mutually exclusive. We trained L1-regularized logistic classifiers using Python’s Sklearn toolbox to predict behavior by training on one day and testing on another (binary classifiers, was a participant performing the behavior or not). Training and testing days were separated by at least one day in between to avoid temporal adjacency confounders. Hyper parameterization was optimized on the training set using tenfold cross-validation. The AUC of the receiver-operator-characteristic of each network’s ability to classify the desired behavior was calculated on the test day. The AUC of the fit on the test dataset was calculated and then compared to a null distribution that was formed via permutation tests that temporally shifted each day’s network component activity forward or backwards by a uniform random number ranging from 0 to 4 h. This preserves the autocorrelation of the neural signals while eliminating any consistent association to behavior across days.

We also tested whether network components were connected to aspects of our participants’ physiology, namely, circadian rhythm and heart rate. Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) was used to identify a mixture of networks that matched a circadian sinusoid with a period of 24 h. The circadian sinusoid was defined as a1*cos(t/24 h) + a2*sin(t/24 h), where a1 and a2 are constants learned via CCA. CCA simultaneously tried to find a linear combination/weighting of network component activations to fit to this sinusoid.

The model was trained over the first half of the week and then tested on the second half through Spearman correlation (out of sample validation of correlation). The Spearman R of the fit on the test dataset was calculated and then once again compared to a null distribution of R that was formed via permutation tests that temporally shifted each day’s network component activity forward or backwards by a uniform random number ranging from 0 to 24 h.

Heart rate was assessed using collected EKG signals that were processed using HeartPy71. The instantaneous heart rate for any window was the average heart rate for a 30-s period centered on the window. Heart rate variability as shown in Supplementary Fig. S22 was calculated both as the “root mean square of successive differences” (how much the time between heart beats varied from one beat to another) and the PNN20 (percentage of consecutive heartbeats where the interval difference is greater than 20 milliseconds)71. L1-regularized regression was trained on the first half of the week to identify a mixture of parcels that predicted heart rate using Sklearn’s implementation (out of sample validation of regression). Hyperparameters were optimized on the training set using tenfold cross-validation. The quality of the fit was assessed on the remaining half of the week via Spearman correlation with a null distribution formed using the same permutation tests used for behavior and circadian rhythm to preserve both the autocorrelational properties of the heart rate and neural signals.

Network components show non-independent relationships that are well-preserved over days

Do pairs of networks interact with each other in a consistent manner across the week? For each pair of network components, we calculated the joint distribution between their activations on each day of data separately and asked whether these joint distributions were preserved across days and indicated significant non-independent and/or non-linear relationships. Notably, while our principal components implementation will group parcels with linear relationships together into network components, it will not capture non-linearities.

We tested whether network components had reliable interactions with each other by examining their joint distribution stability. For each pair of network components in a participant, we divided the total range of each component’s activation into 1000 discrete bins to generate a 1000 × 1000 grid covering the space of both components’ activation. Each time window was then assigned into one of these grid squares based on the activation of this network pair during that window. We calculated the empirical joint distribution of the network component pair over these bins for each day separately. We calculated the Bhattacharyaa distance between each day’s distribution to each other as well as the distance to the expected joint distribution if each network were independent but possessed the same marginal distribution (which we denote the expected independent distribution). The “effect size” metric shown in Supplemental Figs. S15B and S16 is the average distance between the real distributions on different days divided by the distance to the expected independent distribution. Using permutation testing (10 k trials), we established a null distribution for effect size if the networks were in fact independent by randomly drawing however many days of samples we had from the expected independent distribution and calculating the effect size over these draws. Statistical significance was then corrected for multiple comparisons across all possible pairs of joint network distributions using Benjamini–Hochberg correction.

Neural dynamics undergo chaotic-like transitions that are associated with shifts in natural behavior (Figs. 2 and 3)

We examined how the overall brain state (the status of all recorded networks in the brain) would change over time by dividing the week into “transitions”, periods when the brain was rapidly reconfiguring itself, and “states”, periods of time where the brain’s functional connectome appeared to be relatively stable.

In Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S10, we provide evidence that the brain falls into states and transitions by examining the “speed” of the brain. Speed was defined as how much the brain’s state changed between one five-second window and the next. More specifically, we took the vector of all network activations of each window (the parcel coherences projected into the network PCA space) and calculated the Euclidean distance between the network activation vector of one window and the next (the temporal derivative in the network PCA space).

We calculated the distribution of the time between windows that fell into the top 1% of speed across all participants and compared that distribution to Poisson distributions with λ = 0.01. The Poisson distribution captures what the expected time between high-speed windows would be in a memoryless process (non-autocorrelated speed). The results showed that windows with high speeds tended to cluster next to each other temporally (Supplemental Fig. S10). This finding supports the notion that brain network dynamics can be separated into times when the brain is relatively static (states), punctuated by bursts of time when the brain is quickly changing (transitions). Furthermore, we tested this between windows falling within the top 10% of velocity (λ = 0.1) and found the same result, thus the results are robust to the choice of λ. On an individual participant-level, we also used Kolomogorov–Smirnov tests to assess whether the time between high transition speed windows followed a Poisson distribution, rejecting the null hypothesis in all 20 participants (p < 0.05). These tests supported the idea that the “speed” of the brain was bursty and were divided into periods of high speed (transitions) and relatively slow speed (states).

In Figs. 2B and 3, we evaluated various properties of these transitory bursts. We identified neural transition bursts using binary segmentation on the time series of network activation vectors (change point detection), implemented via the Ruptures package in Python using default settings72. This algorithm starts by identifying the optimal place to position a single change point such that the network activations before the change point possess a maximally different distribution from the network activations after it, effectively dividing the week into two pieces. Binary segmentation then recursively subdivides the resulting pieces of time according to the same procedure, until no such change points can be found. Neural transition trajectories were then defined as the time around a change point possessing above average transition speed.

In Fig. 2B, we calculated the median time between neural and behavioral changepoints. Using nine participants with available high-quality video recordings throughout the week, we randomly selected two days to annotate for one of three natural behaviors based on what they were interacting with: a digital screen/device, another living creature (either human or, in one participant, a canine companion), or physically interacting with an object. For this analysis, we excluded times when participants were asleep. All annotations used for analyses in this manuscript were done by a single participant, however a second rater also annotated half of these annotations for validation. The Cohen’s Kappa for the consistency between these two annotations was 0.92, 0.83, and 0.85 for digital, social, and physical interactions, respectively.

In practice, if the participant was not doing one of these three behaviors, they were awake but not outwardly active (wakeful rest/presumably internally thoughtful). Behavioral changepoints were defined as anytime one of these three behaviors started or ended, including times when participants switched from one to another. We calculated the median time between neural and behavioral changepoints and found a null distribution for this metric using permutation testing.

Specifically, we temporally shifted the behavioral changepoints forward or backwards by a uniform random number ranging from 0 to 24 h, recalculating the median time difference between behavior and neural changepoints, and used 10,000 trials of this to form a null distribution of the expected time difference between behavioral and neural changepoints if there was no temporal relation between the two. We tested whether the real time difference between behavior changes and state changes was consistently smaller than the expected time difference using a paired t-test across participants.

In Fig. 3B, we repeat these statistics after separating transitions by how quickly or delayed the observed change in behavior was relative to the neural transition. We only used neural transitions that could confidently be linked to a specific change in behavior (time between neural transition and labeled change in behavior was less than 30 s) since some neural transitions were not associated with any obvious sign of change in behavior.

For each participant individually, we calculated the distribution of [time when behavior change occurred] minus [time when neural transition began]. We then took neural transitions falling in the top-third of this distribution and labeled them as “late behavior” neural transitions (neural transitions where changes in behavior lagged significantly behind the detected neural transition). Neural transitions in the bottom-third of the distribution were labeled as “early behavior” transitions. We chose this coarse definition as the exact time a neural transition or change in behavior occurs is dependent on the sensitivity of the criteria for detecting them. Thus, we wanted to choose a criterion with relatively strong separation between the two groups.

We calculated the average ratio between distance and displacement during neural transitions that fell into these two groups and used paired t-tests to assess whether there was a consistent difference in the two across participants.

In Fig. 3C, we examined the distance between the paths traversed by different transition trajectories. We calculated the distance between the start points of all trajectories in a participant and the distance between their end points. We found that the distribution of this distance was close to a Gaussian, which was not unexpected by the central limit theorem (since distance is computed over many dimensions and the sum of many random variables typically converges to a Gaussian). Two trajectories were considered to have similar starting or ending point if the distance between the points fell into the bottom 10% of trajectory pairs. We grouped trajectories into three groups: 1. trajectories with similar starting and ending point, 2. trajectories with similar starting points only, and 3. trajectories with similar ending points only. We calculated the average distance between trajectories that fell into each group as a function of how much of the trajectory had been completed. Specifically, we used linear interpolation to determine what was the brain state 5, 10, 15, 20, …, 95% of the way into each trajectory. We calculated the distance between brain states of the same percentage in each of the three groups. Figure 3C shows the distribution of these distances for a single participant and the effect size of the difference between these distances across all participants. Figure S11 shows the distribution of all participants individually. To determine the effect size, for each participant, we calculated the Cohen’s d between the distances between trajectories that start and end similarly to the distances between trajectories that start similarly but end differently. This measures the number of standard deviations that separate the distributions in the trajectory categories at different points along the trajectory. We then calculated the standard error of these Cohen’s d across all 20 subjects.

To account for the effect of somatomotor transients on this analysis, we removed parcels of the brain that overlapped with the somatomotor network and repeated the above analysis as shown in Figs. S12 and S13, finding no major difference in results.

In Fig. 3D-left, we studied whether these transitions influenced the chaoticity of the brain dynamics. Chaoticity was defined using the 0–1 chaos test using the protocol described in ref. 73 and was calculated over non-overlapping ten-minute segments. We calculated the chaoticity of each network component independently over each time segment. The chaoticity of the overall neural dynamics for a given time segment was defined as the median chaoticity of all network components. Segments during a transition were compared to segments not during transitions. In Fig. 3D-right, we compare the chaoticity during transitions with early vs late behavioral transitions as done in Fig. 3B.

In Fig. 3E, we analyzed the distribution of the transition size, which we defined as the net displacement of a transition and the time-between transitions. We fit power law exponents to these distributions using MATLAB’s nlinfit function with power laws defined as a1*frequency^(−a2) where a1 and a2 are learned.

We tested whether these distributions came from power law distributions using two methods from ref. 74. First, we used Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) tests to test whether we failed to reject the null hypothesis that the distributions plausibly came from power laws. We fit power law distributions to each participant’s transition size and time between transitions distributions separately and calculated the KS distance between the experimental distributions and their theoretical power law distributions. We formed a null distribution on these distances by drawing 10,000 random samples from the theoretical power law distribution, fitting a power law distribution to those samples, and then calculating the KS distance between the sampled distribution and the fitted one. If these distances were consistently lower than the distance between the real distribution and its estimated power law one, then we reject the null and conclude that the distribution did not come from a power law. 17/20 participants had transition size distributions that plausibly came from power law distributions (p > 0.05), and 20/20 participants had time-between transition distributions that plausibly came from power law distributions.

We then used likelihood comparison tests to see whether the transition size and time-between distributions were more likely to have come from power law, exponential, or log-normal distributions. We calculated the log-likelihood that each participant’s distributions came from each of the three categories. We used a Wilcoxon signed-rank test to assess whether the log-likelihood of power law distributions were higher than exponential and log-normal distributions across participants. Power law distributions were more likely than exponential (p = 0.007 for transition size and p = 4.8e-5 for time-between) and more likely than log-normal (p = 0.03 for transition size and p = 4.8e-5 for time-between).

Koopman operator dynamical systems analysis (Fig. 4A)

To determine whether these brain network dynamics possessed consistent anatomical and dynamical trends, we used dynamical systems eigendecomposition.

We used a deep recurrent neural network to learn a high-dimensional representation of the network activations where the brain’s dynamics could be captured using linear methods, a modern implementation of the original Koopman operator proposed in ref. 40. The full model, which is shown in Fig. S17 is an adaptation of the method presented in ref. 75 which uses deep neural networks to learn a nonlinear transformation between the original observables of a system onto their corresponding points on a new manifold where the evolution of the system can be captured by linear dynamical laws.

To describe a single forward-pass through the model, we start with xt, the network activation vector at time t (the intraparcel coherences projected onto the robust principal components) for a single participant. This vector was first fed into an encoder, a long short-term memory (LSTM) unit implemented in TensorFlow using default settings.

The output of the LSTM was the Koopman representation kt, a set of variables that summarized the current dynamical state of the brain up to time t. kt was chosen to have a dimensionality ten times as large as xt to allow it to serve as a nonlinear kernel. kt was then passed to two models during training. The first was a linear autoregression model with learnable parameters A, b that attempted to predict the next time step’s Koopman state as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\hat{k}}_{t+1}=A{k}_{t}+b$$\end{document}k^t+1=Akt+b. The autoregressive error of this is the squared loss between the predicted and actual Koopman state. In other words, how well does the information encoded in kt predict its own temporal evolution using linear methods?

We trained all models (f, d, A, b) simultaneously according to two loss functions. One of these loss functions asked whether the predicted Koopman state representation at time t + 1 was close to the actual Koopman state representation at that time. A single training step is described in Fig. S18.

We implemented these models using Python’s Tensorflow Adam optimizer under default settings. All networks used exponential activation functions and L-1 regularization.

Behavioral classification (Fig. 4B)

To test whether the Koopman state representation captured neurocognitively interesting information and whether behavior organized consistently along it, we used it to predict the participant’s behavior. Participant behavior was manually annotated for three activities: watching a digital screen, socializing with someone else, or physically manipulating an object. These three behaviors were not mutually exclusive. We trained L1-regularized logistic classifiers using Python’s Sklearn toolbox to predict behavior by training on one day and testing on another (binary classifiers, were they performing the behavior or not). Hyper parameterization was optimized on the training set using tenfold cross-validation. The AUC of the receiver-operator-characteristic of each network’s ability to classify the desired behavior was calculated.

To determine what brain networks were consistently associated with each behavioral state across participants, for each participant, we selected annotated time windows where the participant was doing a behavior of interest. Using the decoding model, we asked what brain networks were associated with the Koopman state representation of those time windows, forming a (networks × 1) vector for each time window. We averaged these vectors across all time windows associated with the behavior and projected them onto the six canonical fMRI networks. This projection was found by assigning each parcel in the brain to one of the six canonical fMRI networks, as done in Section “Autocorrelation stability”. Each brain network (a weighted linear sum of parcels found via PCA) then had an associated “weight” in each of the six canonical networks corresponding to the sum of the PCA-assigned weight of parcels belonging to that fMRI network. We calculated the dot product between each brain network’s weight along the six canonical networks (six canonical fMRI networks × number of brain networks matrix) and the average brain network activations associated with each behavioral state (number of brain networks × 1 vector), resulting in a six-by-one vector describing which fMRI networks were activated or deactivated during that behavioral state. We also repeated this procedure except for different stages of sleep (with sleep stages determined as described in Section “Neurocognitive states form an hourglass-like shape with a center manifold that separates stages of consciousness”), which is shown in Supplementary Fig. S20.

Using the temporal perturbation testing described in our behavioral changepoint testing (temporally shifting the data forwards or backwards by random amounts relative to the behavioral labels), we formed a null distribution for the activation of these six canonical fMRI networks. We then calculated the Cohen’s d effect size of the actual average network activation during a task compared to the null distribution with positive effect sizes indicating higher-than-random network activation. Across all participants, we then used one-sample t-tests to see if there was a consistent trend in these effect sizes across participants and used Benjamini–Hochberg for multiple comparisons correction (with the number of tests being 18 for three behavioral tasks over six networks).

Neural dynamics are driven by a homeostatic-like attractor at the default mode (Fig. 4C)

To identify the largest drivers of the brain’s dynamics, we started by calculating the critical points of the Koopman operator (A from Supplementary Fig. S17). In all participants, A was full rank and non-ill conditioned, leading to one found critical point (also known as a fixed or equilibrium point) of the system: inv(I-A)*b, where I is the identity matrix. We found that in all twenty participants, the real component of all eigenvalues had magnitude less than one, indicating this critical point was an attractor (brain states near the attractor tend to be pulled towards it) as shown in Supplementary Fig. S19. While dynamical systems may have multiple critical points, this single attractor found by the Koopman operator is conventionally thought to represent the “global” behavior of the system (e.g., a system with both an attractor and repeller will show eigenfunctions associated with the attractor if the system gravitates towards the attractor as time approaches infinity76). One caveat is that interpreting systems with more than one critical point using Koopman operators is an active area of investigation60.

To identify which areas of the brain were activated at this central attractor, we used the decoding model shown in Supplementary Fig. S17 to ask what original network activations were associated with this attractor state. We projected the resulting network activations onto the six canonical fMRI networks, as described in Section “Behavioral classification”, and averaged over frequency, giving us a six-by-one vector for each participant. We then subtracted each participant’s fMRI network activation vector by its mean to ask “which networks were activated or deactivated” relative to the rest of the brain. We used t-tests on each network activation across participants to see if any networks were consistently activated or deactivated. We used Benjamini–Hochberg for multiple comparisons correction. Participants that did not have electrodes in all six canonical networks were removed from this analysis. We then repeated this process except by averaging over networks, to ask if any frequencies were activated or inactivated.

Neurocognitive states form an hourglass-like shape with a center manifold that separates stages of consciousness (Fig. 5)

For visualization purposes, in Fig. 5A.1, we chose two participants for whom we could accurately predict all three behaviors and plotted their behavioral trajectories in Koopman space. First, we calculated a subspace where all three behaviors are separated based on the linear classifiers determined in the previous section. The first axis (Koopman subspace 1) was the found feature vector associated with digital screen usage. The second axis was the part of the socialization-associated feature vector that was orthogonal to the first axis. The third axis was the part of the physical manipulation-associated feature vector that was orthogonal to both the first and second axes. We then projected the Koopman state representation from the unmodeled second (testing) annotated day onto these three axes and plotted trajectories when the participants were partaking in each behavior along with times where the participant was not doing any of the behaviors.

To test whether outwardly active behavior departed from the attractor state relative to wakeful rest (Fig. 5A.2), for the nine participants with video annotations, we calculated the distance between each window’s Koopman state representation and that participant’s attractor state. We then averaged windows based on whether the participant was doing one of the three behaviors or awake but not doing any of the marked behaviors (which in practice meant sitting idly without obvious outward interactions). We used paired t-tests to ask whether the average active behavior to attractor distance was larger than the wakeful rest to attractor distance.

To quantitatively assess the organization of behavioral and physiological states around this “hourglass”, we used eigendecomposition of the Koopman operator, A. In dynamical systems, eigendecomposition is a method to “decompose” the system’s overall dynamics into separate components (eigenvectors) that each represent a set of building blocks (in this case brain networks) that tends to change together and can be analyzed as a single unit77. The overall dynamics of the system can be expressed as the sum of these components. For simplicity, we chose to focus on the component with the longest, most-stable dynamics in the system78—the center manifold—which is represented by the Koopman operator’s eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue (e.g., the eigenvalue closest to one since all eigenvalues were less than one). The center manifold encompasses a group of brain networks that tended to change together over time as the slowest identified dynamic in the brain.

We first asked whether the brain’s position along the center manifold aligned more with circadian rhythm compared to its position off the manifold by testing its correlation to a circadian sinusoid with a period of 24 h. This sinusoid was defined as a1*cos(t/24 h) + a2*sin(t/24 h) where a1 and a2 are constants. On-manifold dynamics was defined as the one-dimensional projection of each time window’s Koopman state onto the center manifold. Off-manifold dynamics were calculated as the (number of Koopman states minus 1)-dimensional projection of each time window’s state in the spaces orthogonal to the center manifold.

Using MATLAB’s fitlm function, we fit the circadian sinusoid (a1 and a2) to the brain’s projection along the center manifold. We then calculated the Pearson’s correlation of this fit for each participant separately. We repeated the same regression and correlation calculation over each dimension of the off-manifold dynamics separately, defining the overall correlation between off-manifold dynamics and time of day for a single participant as the median correlation across all off-manifold dimensions. We then used a paired t-test to determine whether there was a reliable difference in the correlation between on vs off-manifold dynamics to circadian time of day (Fig. 5A.3).

We also assessed the correlation between these on and off-manifold dynamics to heart rate. Heart rate was assessed using collected EKG signals that were processed using HeartPy71. The instantaneous heart rate for any window was the average heart rate for a 30-s period centered on the window. The Pearson correlation between heart rate and manifold dynamics and on and off-manifold dynamics was then calculated as described above.

After determining that the center manifold appeared to align with these physiological processes, we then asked: how do various behavioral and physiological states organize along the center manifold? What anatomical brain networks are associated with it? In Fig. 5B, we measure where neurocognitive states tended to form across participants along the center manifold. Using a sleep score classifier41 and partially validated by a trained neurologist (author JFC), we classified time windows associated with sleeping behavior into N1, N2, N3, and REM sleep. We then divided windows into the following groups: subtypes of actively outward waking behavior, waking rest, N1, N2, N3, and REM. The projection of each time window’s Koopman state along the center manifold was found by taking the dot product between each window’s Koopman state and the center manifold eigenvector. We calculated the mean projection of all time windows associated with each behavioral and physiological group with the attractor state defined as the zero point of the axis. Since the decision as to which end of an axis is “positive” or “negative” is arbitrary, we decided to define the end of the axis containing the center of active waking behavior as the positive end of the axis and observe where the other states formed naturally. We plotted the distribution of these state center projections in Fig. 5B. We used paired t-tests to ask whether different behavioral and physiological states had reliably different projections across participants with Benjamini–Hochberg used for multiple comparisons correction.

We then assessed what anatomical brain networks were activated or deactivated as the brain moved along this center manifold. For each participant, we calculated the Spearman’s correlation coefficient between each time window’s brain network activation and the location of that window’s projection along the center manifold. We then projected these correlations onto the six canonical fMRI networks and five frequency bands for each participant individually. To determine if certain fMRI networks were consistently correlated to the brain’s location along the center manifold across participants, we averaged each participant’s correlations across all frequency bands, resulting in a six by one vector describing the average correlation between each fMRI network and the center manifold state. We then used one-sampled t-tests to see if the correlations were reliably positive or negative with Benjamini–Hochberg multiple comparisons correction, with the results shown in Fig. 5C. To see if certain frequencies were reliably correlated, we instead averaged over fMRI networks and performed one-sampled t-tests on the average frequency correlations.

In Fig. 5D, we show the autocorrelation and 0–1 chaoticity of on and off-manifold dynamics. On-manifold dynamics were once again defined as the one-dimensional projection of each time window’s Koopman state onto the center manifold, with its autocorrelation and 0–1 chaoticity calculated across the week. Off-manifold dynamics were calculated as the (number of Koopman states minus 1)-dimensional projection of each time window’s state in the spaces orthogonal to the center manifold. The autocorrelation and 0–1 chaoticity were calculated over each dimension in this orthogonal space and then the median was calculated over each of these dimensions.

In Fig. 5E, we measure the velocity and chaoticity of neural dynamics the brain’s activation along the center manifold. For each participant, we divided their week-long brain activity into ten quantiles, from the lowest quantile of activity along the center to the highest and then calculated the average neural velocity within each quantile. For each quantile, we took the average velocity and 0–1 chaos test values from each participant and calculated the mean and standard error of these metrics across participants which is plotted in Fig. 5E. To test whether neural velocity was lower in the middle of the manifold compared to the edges, we used two paired t-tests: one comparing the middle (5th) quantile to the lowest (1st) quantile, and another comparing it to the highest (10th) quantile, both showing p < 0.01 across participants. Through the same paired t-tests, chaoticity showed lower values in the middle quantile compared to the lowest quantile (p < 0.01), but did not statistically differentiate itself from the highest quantile (p = 0.38).

Effect of sleep deprivation on neural kinematics

We tested whether the neural kinematics described above were perturbed in the sleep-deprived state. Inclusion criteria for the eight participants selected for this analysis are described in the main text and are summarized here: participants needed at least one day where they received three or fewer hours of sleep the night before and one day where they received six or more hours of sleep. For consistency, this was counted as any sleep between 10 pm and 6 am. We also required at least 12 h of continuous waking activity starting the morning after the sleep condition (6 am to 6 pm) with no sleep during that 12-h block. We then analyzed these 12-h blocks of waking activity to determine if neural kinematics were significantly perturbed between the sleep-deprived block and the well-rested block. Of these eight participants, four were intentionally sleep deprived for clinical purposes, four were unintentionally sleep deprived.

We first collected all neural transitions that occurred during these two 12-h periods, calculating the distance, displacement, and chaoticity of these transitions as described in Section “Neural dynamics undergo chaotic-like transitions that are associated with shifts in natural behavior”. We first calculated the autocorrelation of each metric for each participant separately (e.g., the Pearson correlation between each transition’s chaoticity and the following transition’s chaoticity). After verifying that all three metrics were non-autocorrelated (p > 0.05) across participants and could be treated as independent samples within each participant, we used mixed effect models to assess the impact of sleep deprivation on these metrics. Specifically, sleep deprivation was tested as the fixed effect and participant-level effects as random effects. These mixed effect models were tested across all eight participants and then in the intentional and unintentional sleep deprivation groups separately, maintaining statistical significance (p < 0.05) in all three cases.

We then tested whether the brain’s projection along the center manifold differed in the sleep deprivation group. We marked the two 12-h periods according to whether participants were awake and outwardly interactive with their environment (such as reading a book or browsing their phone) or if they were awake but not interacting with their environment (wakeful rest). Since these center manifold projections were autocorrelated, we first calculated the “effective number of independent samples” according to the procedure laid out in refs. 79,80. Specifically, we calculated the autocorrelation of the center manifold projection in each 12-h period for each participant independently. Autocorrelation was calculated at every lag (five-second window) until the autocorrelation turned negative as described in ref. 80. These autocorrelations were used to calculate how many effective samples of each behavior was shown during that 12-h period according to the procedure noted in refs. 79,80. We then used mixed-effect models on both behavioral groups separately, again with sleep deprivation as the fixed effect and participant-level effects as random effects to test whether sleep deprivation had a reliable impact on these center manifold projections using these corrected sample sizes. We then repeated these mixed effect models on the intentional and unintentional sleep deprivation groups independently, finding statistical significance (p < 0.05) in both cases.

We then repeated this procedure except for neural velocity over the 12-h period, again using the same mixed effect models with corrections for autocorrelation.

One of the main motivating factors for the use of mixed effect models in this analysis is their ability to summarize trends across multiple participants. While most participants displayed the overall trends shown throughout Fig. 6 (in most of the analyses, only one or two participants had statistics that went against the overall trend), in a few participants, there was significant variance in the observed metric. This may be due to a variety of factors that are difficult to elucidate ranging from individual differences in their functional dynamics, the varieties of the behaviors expressed, or differences in electrode placement. Ultimately, this statistical test is designed to summarize the trend of each participant’s individual statistics and determine if there is a population-level effect.
