Introduction

Let
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
be a set of integers. For
h\in\mathbb{N}
, write

hA\coloneq\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}:a_{i}\in A\}

to denote the
h

. Note that the

need not be distinct. Nathanson defined the set
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
of the possible sizes of these

-fold sumsets when

Definition 1.1

(
[

Let

h

\mathcal{R}(h,k)\coloneq\{|hA|:A\subset\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }|A|=k\}.

Nathanson
[

posed the problem of determining the exact values in
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
for arbitrary
h,k\in\mathbb{N}
. In this paper, we will determine the form of
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
for fixed
h\in\mathbb{N}
and large

, and for
h=3
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, specifically answering Nathanson’s Problems 8 and 9 in
[
Nat24
]
.

The first work related to
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
was by Erdős and Szemerédi
[

]
, who remarked that “it is easy to see that”
\mathcal{R}(2,k)=\left[2k-1,\binom{k+1}{2}\right]
for all

. Nathanson
[
Nat24
]
determined the exact form of
\mathcal{R}(h,3)
for any
h\in\mathbb{N}
. Nathanson’s recent program has involved finding specific values in
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
and studying the frequency with which certain sumset sizes show up, with particular attention to
k=4

[
Nat24
,
Nat25a
,
Nat25b
,
Nat25c
,
Nat25d
,
O’B25
]
. With fixed
h>2
and
k
increasing, the problem of determining
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
remained fully open.

For real numbers

and

, let

denote the interval of integers from
\mathopen{}\left\lceil a\right\rceil\mathclose{}
to
\lfloor b\rfloor
, including endpoints. It is not hard to show
[

that

(1.1)

\mathcal{R}(h,k)\subseteq\left[hk-h+1,\binom{h+k-1}{h}\right],

and that
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
includes the endpoints of this interval.
Let

. Nathanson
[

found a gap in this interval, showing that

for

. Schinina
[

extended this to find that there are no elements of

Theorem 1.4
.

Fix
h\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists a constant
k_{h}\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
k>k_{h}
, then

\mathcal{R}(h,k)=\left[hk-h+1,\binom{h+k-1}{h}\right]\setminus\Delta_{h,k}.

Remark 1.5
.

Tracking constants in our proof shows that one can take
k_{h}\leq 2^{20h^{2}}
. We have not attempted to optimize this quantity further because it appears to be quite far from the truth.

Numerical experiments for small
h
and
k
suggest that perhaps the conclusion of Theorem
1.4
holds for all
k>h
. This would be the best possible, since it sometimes fails for
k\leq h
; for example, when
k=3
and
h>3
, there are fewer elements in
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
. See Section
5.2
for a discussion of what happens when
k\leq h
.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Let
h\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
k>h
. Then

\mathcal{R}(h,k)=\left[hk-h+1,\binom{h+k-1}{h}\right]\setminus\Delta_{h,k}.

For any fixed
h
, Theorem
1.4
reduces Conjecture
1.6
to a finite problem, albeit a computationally intensive one. We are able to prove that
k_{3}=2
suffices, which implies Conjecture
1.6
when
h=3
; note that
\Delta_{3,k}=\{3k-1\}
.

Theorem 1.7
.

If
k>2
, then

\mathcal{R}(3,k)=\{3k-2\}\cup\left[3k,\binom{k+2}{3}\right].

In contrast with previous work, our proofs of Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.7
are nonconstructive; for a single desired sumset size, our proof does not give an explicit construction attaining it. Instead, we use continuity-type arguments to prove existence. We construct a sequence of sets
A_{i}
such that, even though the quantities
|hA_{i}|
vary non-monotonically, we can guarantee that the entire set
\{|hA_{i}|\}
is an interval. To study
|hA|
, we also study the distribution of the tuples
(|A|,|2A|,|3A|,\ldots,|hA|)
for various
k
-element sets
A
. Even though we do not characterize exactly which tuples are possible, we extract enough structural information to characterize the possible values of
|hA|
. This opens up fruitful directions for new research, to control these tuples more precisely. Another key part of our construction is combining dense sets, meaning sets with small doubling, and sparse sets, which look like Sidon sets or
B_{h}
sets, to get sets in the middle.

1.1.
Roadmap

In Section
2
, we prove Theorem
1.3
by employing an asymmetric form of Freiman’s
3k-3
theorem to control the sizes of sumsets from the diameter of sets. We then prove Theorem
1.7
before Theorem
1.4
because the proof of Theorem
1.7
contains simpler versions of some of the ideas in the proof of Theorem
1.4
. In Section
3
, we prove Theorem
1.7
using a nonconstructive inductive argument involving understanding the possible tuples
(|2A|,|3A|)
for all sets
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
with
|A|=k
. The bulk of the paper is in Section
4
, where we prove Theorem
1.4
. This is done using two separate constructions: In Section
4.2
, we construct sets
A
with smaller values of
|hA|
using a partially inductive construction; in Section
4.3
, we construct sets
A
with larger values of
|hA|
by combining dense sets that resemble arithmetic progressions with sparse sets that resemble geometric sequences. Section
5
discusses possible future directions of research and open problems.

Ruling out Numbers from

We begin with a definition of the diameter of a set

Definition 2.1

For

To prove Theorem

We now prove a lemma about about the sizes of sumsets of sets with larger diameter, which matches Lemma

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
h\geq 2
and
k\geq 2
. If
\mathrm{diam}(A)\geq 2k-3
, then

|hA|\geq 2hk-3h-k+3.

Proof.

We prove this lemma by induction on
h
. With
h=2
, the statement becomes, “If
\mathrm{diam}(A)\geq 2k-3
, then
|2A|\geq 3k-3.
” This is true by Freiman’s
3k-3

[
TV06
, Theorem 5.11]
or
[
Nat96
, Theorem 1.14]
).

Now, assume that the statement of the lemma is true for

, with
h\geq 3
. Let
B=(h-1)A
, where
\mathrm{diam}(A)\geq 2k-3
. The inductive hypothesis gives

|B|\geq 2(h-1)k-3(h-1)-k+3=2hk-3h-k+3+(3-2k).

Assume for the sake of contradiction that

(2.2)

|hA|<|B|+2k-3.

Since
|B|\geq|A|
,

|B|+2k-3=|A|+|B|+\min(|A|,|B|)-3.

Since

, an asymmetric form of Freiman’s

theorem due to Lev and Smeliansky implies that
A
is contained in an arithmetic progression of length at most

(see
[
LS95
]
or
[

This is a contradiction, since

, and the smallest arithmetic progression containing

. Therefore,

|hA|\geq|B|+2k-3\geq 2hk-3h-k+3.

So, by induction, we are done.

b_{i}\in O(b^{i})
. Let
\mathcal{P}(z)=1+p_{1}z+p_{2}z^{2}+p_{3}z^{3}+\cdots
. For
\eta\leq h
, we have

p_{\eta}=\sum_{\nu=0}^{\eta}a_{\nu}b_{\eta-\nu}.

The fastest growing term will be
a_{\eta}\in\Theta_{+}(a^{\eta})
. All other terms will be in
O(a^{\eta})\cdot\frac{b}{a}
, and will thus be dwarfed by
a_{\eta}
. Thus,
p_{\eta}\in\Theta_{+}(a^{\eta})
.

Now, assume the statement is true for
r-1
. Let

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(z)\coloneq\mathcal{F}(z)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}\mathcal{G}^{(j)}(z)\in\Theta_{+}[[az]]

by the inductive hypothesis. By repeating the argument of
r=1
on
\mathcal{P}(z)=\mathcal{P}^{\prime}(z)\mathcal{G}^{(r)}(z)
, we get that
\mathcal{P}(z)\in\Theta_{+}[[az]]
.
∎

4.3.3.
Sumset Sizes of
B_{j,\ell}
,
S_{m,\ell}
, and
T_{m,\ell}

We begin with our construction of dense sets
B_{j,\ell}
, which are given by the union of an arithmetic progression and an integer, and state their properties.

Definition 4.12
.

Let
\ell\geq 3
, and
s=(h-1)(\ell-2)+1
. Define the set
B_{j,\ell}\coloneq[0,\ell-2]\cup\{h(\ell-2)+2-j\}
for
j\in[1,s]
.

Lemma 4.13
.

For
\ell\geq 3
, the sets
B_{1,\ell},\ldots,B_{s,\ell}
satisfy the following:

(a)

0\leq|\eta B_{j-1,\ell}|-|\eta B_{j,\ell}|\leq h
for all
j\in[2,s]
and
\eta\in[2,h]
;

(b)

|\eta B_{j,\ell}|<h^{2}\ell
for all
j\in[1,s]
and
\eta\in[2,h]
;

(c)

\mathcal{F}_{B_{1,3}}(z)\equiv(1-z)^{-3}.

(d)

If
\ell>3
, then
\mathcal{F}_{B_{1,\ell}}(z)\equiv(1-z)^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{B_{s,\ell-1}}(z).

Proof.

Parts (a) and (b) follow immediately from
[
Nat96
, Theorem 1.11]
. Part (c) follows from
[
Nat96
, Theorem 1.11]
, using the binomial theorem to get
(1-z)^{-3}=\sum_{\eta=0}^{\infty}\binom{\eta+2}{2}z^{\eta}
. For part (d), let
d=h(\ell-2)+1
. Then
B_{s,\ell-1}=[0,\ell-2]
and
B_{1,\ell}=[0,\ell-2]\cup\{d\}
. In a similar way to (
3.1
),

(4.5)

\eta B_{1,\ell}=\bigcup_{\nu=0}^{\eta}\big((\eta-\nu)d+\nu B_{s,\ell-1}\big).

Let
\eta\in[1,h]
. Since
d>h(\ell-2)
, the sets on the right side of (
4.5
) are disjoint, so
|\eta B_{1,\ell}|=\sum_{\nu=0}^{\eta}|\nu B_{s,\ell-1}|
. Plugging in that
(1-z)^{-1}=(1+z+z^{2}+\cdots)
yields (d).
∎

Now, we define a set
S_{m,\ell}
, and bound its generating function
\mathcal{F}_{S_{m,\ell}}(z)
.

Definition 4.14
.

For
m\geq 2
and
\ell\geq 2
, define the set
S_{m,\ell}\coloneq\{0,1,m,m^{2},\ldots,m^{\ell-2}\}
.

Lemma 4.15
.

For
m\geq 2
and
\ell\geq 2
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{S_{m,\ell}}(z)=\frac{1-(\ell-2)z^{m}O[[\ell z]]}{(1-z)^{\ell}}.

Proof.

Let
\eta\in\mathbb{N}
. The elements in
\eta S_{m,\ell}
can be viewed as numbers
n
where
\lfloor\frac{n}{m^{\ell-2}}\rfloor
plus the sum of the base-
m
digits of
n\pmod{m^{\ell-2}}
is less than or equal to
\eta
. To construct such an
n
, construct
n
with
\lfloor\frac{n}{m^{\ell-2}}\rfloor
copies of
m^{\ell-2}
, and then use the base extension of
n\pmod{m^{\ell-2}}
in base
m
. This is a general construction of all elements
n
in
\eta S_{m,\ell}
.

This gives an expression of
|\eta S_{m,\ell}|
as the number of ways to put
\eta
balls in
\ell
boxes, where
\ell-2
of the boxes have a maximum number of
m-1
balls, and
2
of the boxes have no maximum. The
\ell-2
boxes correspond to the number of
1
’s,
m
’s,
\ldots
,
m^{\ell-3}
’s in the base-
m
expansion of
n
. These numbers are bounded between
0
and
m-1
. One infinite-capacity box corresponds to the number of
m^{\ell-2}
’s in
n
, and the other corresponds to the ‘overflow’, or the number of
0
’s in
n
. In total,
n
must be formed by exactly
\eta
balls, as it is a sum of
\eta
elements of
S_{m,\ell}
. This gives a generating function formula

\mathcal{F}_{S_{m,\ell}}(z)=(1+z+z^{2}+\cdots+z^{m-1})^{\ell-2}(1+z+z^{2}+\cdots)^{2}=\frac{(1-z^{m})^{\ell-2}}{(1-z)^{\ell}}.

Expanding the numerator
(1-z^{m})^{\ell-2}
out using the binomial theorem yields the desired result.
∎

Now, we will define a similar set
T_{m,\ell}
and prove a similar result about the generating function
\mathcal{F}_{T_{m,\ell}}
.

Definition 4.16
.

For
m\geq 2
and
\ell\geq 2
, define the set
T_{m,\ell}\coloneq\{1,m,m^{2},\ldots,m^{\ell-1}\}=S_{m,\ell+1}\setminus\{0\}.

Lemma 4.17
.

For
m\geq 2
and
\ell\geq 2
, we have

\mathcal{F}_{T_{m,\ell}}(z)=\frac{1-\binom{\ell-1}{2}z^{m+1}O[[\ell z]]}{(1-z)^{\ell}}.

Proof.

Let
\eta\in\mathbb{N}
. We will approximate
|\eta T_{m,\ell}|
by putting sums of elements of
T_{m,\ell}
into a standardized form. Say that a representation
n=a_{0}+a_{1}m+a_{2}m^{2}+\cdots+a_{\ell-1}m^{\ell-1}
is in
r
-
normal form
if
a_{0},\ldots,a_{\ell-2}
are in
[0,m-1]
and
a_{\ell-1}\geq 0
and
a_{0}+\cdots+a_{\ell-1}=r
. By taking the base-
m
expansion of
n
, every
n\in\mathbb{N}
can be written in normal form uniquely.

Each element
n\in\eta T_{m,\ell}
can be written as
n=a_{0}+a_{1}m+a_{2}m^{2}+\cdots+a_{\ell-1}m^{\ell-1}
, where
a_{i}\geq 0
and
a_{0}+\cdots+a_{\ell-1}=\eta
. This representation of
n
might not be unique, so we will put
n
into normal form.
If
a_{i}\geq m
for some
i<\ell-1
, we can decrement
a_{i}
by
m
and add
1
to
a_{i+1}
without changing the value of
n
; after this procedure then
a_{0}+\cdots+a_{\ell-1}=\eta-(m-1)
. Iterating this whenever possible, we will eventually get that
a_{0},\ldots,a_{\ell-2}
are in
[0,m-1]
and
a_{\ell-1}
is any positive integer; if we have done this
j
times, we get the sum
a_{0}+\cdots+a_{\ell-1}=\eta-j(m-1)
. Thus, any element
n\in\eta T_{m,\ell}
can be put into
\eta-j(m-1)
-normal form for some
j\geq 0
, and this form is unique.

Now, we reverse this process. Consider an element
n
in
\eta-j(m-1)
-normal form. To pull back
n
into
\eta T_{m,\ell}
, we need to decrement
a_{i}
by 1 and increment
a_{i-1}
by
m
for
j
different nonzero values of
i
. This is possible as long as we never reach a state where
a_{i}=0
for all
i>0
, which would mean
n
is represented as a sum of
\eta-j^{\prime}(m-1)
copies of 1. This will only happen when
n=\eta-j^{\prime}(m-1)
for
j^{\prime}\geq 1
. This rules out
\lfloor\frac{\eta}{m-1}\rfloor
numbers. Therefore,
|\eta T_{m,\ell}|
is equal to the number of elements
n
in
\eta-j(m-1)
-normal form for any
j\geq 0
, minus
\lfloor\frac{\eta}{m-1}\rfloor
.

We now create the generating function
\mathcal{F}_{T_{m,\ell}}
. Elements in
r
-normal form can be counted as the number of ways to put
r
balls in
\ell
boxes, where the number of balls in box
i
corresponds to
a_{i}
. There are
\ell-1
boxes which have a maximum of
m-1
balls, and
1
box with no maximum number of balls. Then, multiplying by
(1+z^{m-1}+z^{2(m-1)}+\cdots)
to account for elements in
\eta-j(m-1)
-normal form, and subtracting
\lfloor\frac{\eta}{m-1}\rfloor
, gives the generating function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{T_{m,\ell}}(z)

\displaystyle=(1+z+z^{2}+\cdots+z^{m-1})^{\ell-1}(1+z+z^{2}+\cdots)(1+z^{m-1}+z^{2(m-1)}+\cdots)-\sum_{\eta=0}^{\infty}\left\lfloor\frac{\eta}{m-1}\right\rfloor z^{\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-z^{m})^{\ell-1}}{(1-z)^{\ell}(1-z^{m-1})}-\sum_{\eta=0}^{\infty}\left\lfloor\frac{\eta}{m-1}\right\rfloor z^{\eta}.

We can then simplify

\sum_{\eta=0}^{\infty}\left\lfloor\frac{\eta}{m-1}\right\rfloor z^{\eta}=z^{m-1}(1+z+z^{2}+\cdots)(1+z^{m-1}+z^{2(m-1)}+\cdots)=\frac{z^{m-1}}{(1-z)(1-z^{m-1})}.

Plugging this into out formula for
\mathcal{F}_{T_{m,\ell}}(z)
gives

\mathcal{F}_{T_{m,\ell}}(z)=\frac{(1-z^{m})^{\ell-1}-z^{m-1}(1-z)^{\ell-1}}{(1-z)^{\ell}(1-z^{m-1})}=\frac{1-z^{m-1}-\binom{\ell-1}{2}z^{m+1}+\sum_{i=3}^{\infty}O(\ell^{i})z^{i+m-1}}{(1-z)^{\ell}(1-z^{m-1})}.

Expanding the left side of this out for
\ell=2
and the right side out for
\ell>2
yields the desired result.
∎

4.3.4.
Putting it All Together

We begin by defining a notion of a family of sets being connected.

Definition 4.18
.

A set of sets
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{r}\}
with all
|A_{i}|=k
is
connected
if the set of sumset sizes
h\mathcal{A}\coloneq\{|hA|:A\in\mathcal{A}\}
forms an interval.

Now, we prove that the set of all
A
, with
b
fixed and
j,s_{m},t_{m-1}
varying for
m\in[3,h]
, is connected.

Lemma 4.19
.

Fix
h\geq 4
. Fix
k
to be sufficiently large, fix
b\in[3,k-\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor]
, and let
c=k-b
. Let
B_{j}\coloneq B_{j,b}
. Define the family of sets

\mathcal{A}_{b}=\left\{A=B_{j}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{m=3}^{h}S_{m,s_{m}}\sqcup\bigsqcup_{m=2}^{h}T_{m,t_{m}}:\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[1,(h-1)(b-2)+1]\\
s_{m}\in[2,\lfloor c^{0.9}\rfloor]\textnormal{ if }m\in[3,h]\\
t_{m-1}\in[2,\lfloor c^{0.8}\rfloor]\textnormal{ if }m\in[3,h]\\
t_{h}=c-\sum_{m=3}^{h}(s_{m}+t_{m-1})\end{subarray}\right\}.

Then
\mathcal{A}_{b}
is connected.

Proof.

We will apply Lemma
4.5
with
d=2h-3
and

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{d})=(s_{h}-1,t_{h-1}-1,s_{h-1}-1,t_{h-2}-1,\ldots,s_{3}-1,t_{2}-1,j).

The values
n_{i}
are given in (
4.3
). So it will suffice to show that
|hA|
is nonincreasing in each entry
x_{i}
, that
\delta_{1}=1
, and that
\delta_{i}\leq\Delta_{i-1}
for
i\in[2,d]
.

Let
C=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{m=3}^{h}S_{m,s_{m}}\sqcup\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{m=2}^{h}T_{m,t_{m}}
. By Lemmas
4.15
and
4.17
, we have that

(4.6)

\mathcal{F}_{C}(z)=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{c}}\prod_{m=3}^{h}(1-(s_{m}-2)z^{m}O[[s_{m}z]])\cdot\prod_{m=2}^{h}\left(1-\binom{t_{m}-1}{2}z^{m+1}O[[t_{m}z]]\right).

By the binomial theorem,

(4.7)

\frac{1}{(1-z)^{c}}=\sum_{\eta=0}^{\infty}\binom{\eta+c-1}{\eta}z^{\eta}\in\Theta_{+}[[cz]].

We now prove the following two assertions about how changing one
s_{\mu}
or
t_{\mu}
while keeping all of the other
s_{m}
and
t_{m-1}
unchanged for
m\in[3,h-1]
will affect
\mathcal{F}_{C}(z)
.

(
\star
)

For
\mu\in[3,h]
, increasing
s_{\mu}
by 1 decreases
\mathcal{F}_{C}(z)
by an element of
z^{\mu}\Theta_{+}[[cz]]
.

(
\star\star
)

For
\mu\in[2,h-1]
, increasing
t_{\mu}
by 1 decreases
\mathcal{F}_{C}(z)
by an element of
(t_{\mu}-1)z^{\mu+1}\Theta_{+}[[cz]]
.

Notice that we can ignore the factor coming from
t_{h}
in (
4.6
) since it only affects powers of
z
greater than or equal to
h+1
, and also notice that each of the other factors in the products on the right side of (
4.6
) is in
O[[c^{0.9}z]]
.
To prove (
\star
) and (
\star\star
), we now use Lemma
4.11
to combine all of the factors in the products on the right side of (
4.6
). For (
\star
), increasing
s_{\mu}
by 1 to
s_{\mu}^{\prime}=s_{\mu}+1
will decrease
\mathcal{F}_{C}(z)
by an element of

\Theta_{+}[[cz]]((s_{\mu}^{\prime}-2)-(s_{\mu}-2))z^{\mu}O[[s_{\mu}z]]\prod_{m\in[3,h]\setminus\{\mu\}}O[[c^{0.9}z]]\cdot\prod_{m=2}^{h-1}O[[c^{0.9}z]]=z^{\mu}\Theta_{+}[[cz]].

For (
\star\star
), increasing
t_{\mu}
by 1 to
t_{\mu}^{\prime}=t_{\mu}+1
decreases
\mathcal{F}_{C}(z)
by an element of

\Theta_{+}[[cz]]\left(\binom{t_{\mu}^{\prime}-1}{2}-\binom{t_{\mu}-1}{2}\right)z^{\mu+1}O[[t_{\mu}z]]\prod_{m=3}^{h}O[[c^{0.9}z]]\cdot\prod_{m\in[2,h-1]\setminus\{\mu\}}O[[c^{0.9}z]]=(t_{\mu}-1)z^{\mu+1}\Theta_{+}[[cz]].

Since
A=B_{j}\sqcup C
, we have

(4.8)

hA=\sum_{\eta=0}^{h}|\eta C||(h-\eta)B_{j}|.

By (
\star
) and (
\star\star
) and Lemma
4.13
(a), we have that
|hA|
is nonincreasing in each entry
x_{i}
. From (
\star
) and (
4.8
), it is easy to check that the only term affected by increasing
s_{h}
by 1 will be
|hC|
, which will decrease by 1. Hence,
\delta_{1}=1
.

We now approximate
\delta_{i}
for
i\in[1,d]
and
\Delta_{i}
for
i\in[1,d-1]
, which we will use to prove that
\delta_{i}\leq\Delta_{i-1}
for
i\in[2,d]
, which implies the lemma. Throughout, we use Lemma
4.13
(b) to show that the terms with
\eta=h
and
\eta=h-1
dominate in (
4.8
).

Case 1:
i
is odd and
i<d
.
Let
x_{i}=s_{\mu}-1
for
\mu\in[3,h]
. By (
\star
), increasing
s_{\mu}
by 1 will decrease
\mathcal{F}_{C}(z)
by an element of
z^{\mu}\Theta_{+}[[cz]]
. By (
4.8
),

(4.9)

\delta_{i}=\sum_{\eta=\mu}^{h}\Theta_{+}(c^{\eta-\mu})|(h-\eta)B_{j}|=\Theta_{+}(c^{h-\mu})+b\Theta_{+}(c^{h-\mu-1}).

So, increasing
s_{\mu}
from
2
to
\lfloor c^{0.9}\rfloor
gives that

(4.10)

\Delta_{i}=\left(\lfloor c^{0.9}\rfloor-3\right)\sum_{\eta=\mu}^{h}\Theta_{+}(c^{\eta-\mu})|(h-\eta)B_{j}|=\Theta_{+}(c^{h-\mu+0.9})+b\Theta_{+}(c^{h-\mu-0.1}).

Case 2:
i
is even.
Let
x_{i}=t_{\mu}-1
for
\mu\in[2,h-1]
. By (
\star\star
), increasing
t_{\mu}
by 1 will decrease
\mathcal{F}_{C}(z)
by an element of
(t_{\mu}-1)z^{\mu+1}\Theta_{+}[[cz]]
. By (
4.8
),

(4.11)

\delta_{i}=\left(\lfloor c^{0.8}\rfloor-1\right)\sum_{\eta=\mu+1}^{h}\Theta_{+}(c^{\eta-\mu-1})|(h-\eta)B_{j}|=\Theta_{+}(c^{h-\mu-0.2})+b\Theta_{+}(c^{h-\mu-1.2}).

By (
\star\star
), increasing
t_{\mu}
from
2
to
\lfloor c^{0.8}\rfloor
will decrease
\mathcal{F}_{C}(z)
by an element of
\Theta_{+}(c^{1.6})z^{\mu+1}\Theta_{+}[[cz]]
. By (
4.8
),

(4.12)

\Delta_{i}=\sum_{\eta=\mu+1}^{h}\Theta_{+}(c^{\eta-\mu-1+1.6})|(h-\eta)B_{j}|\geq\Theta_{+}(c^{h-\mu+0.6}).

Case 3:
i=d
.
Here,
x_{d}=j
. By (
4.8
) and Lemma
4.13
(a),

(4.13)

\delta_{d}=\max_{j\in[1,(h-1)(b-2)+1]}\sum_{\eta=0}^{h-2}|\eta C|\big(|(h-\eta)B_{j-1}|-|(h-\eta)B_{j}|\big)\leq h\sum_{\eta=0}^{h-2}|\eta C|\leq\Theta_{+}(c^{h-2}).

Now, we can compare the formulas of (
4.9
), (
4.10
), (
4.11
), (
4.12
), and (
4.13
) to show
\delta_{i}\leq\Delta_{i-1}
for
i\in[2,d]
. With
i
being odd and less than
d
, let
x_{i}=s_{\nu}-1
and
x_{i-1}=t_{\nu}-1
. Then we are comparing (
4.9
) with
\mu=\nu
to (
4.12
) with
\mu=\nu
. Since
3\leq b\leq k-\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor
, and
c=k-b
, we have
c^{1.5}>b
. Hence,
\delta_{i}\leq\Delta_{i-1}
. With
i
being even, let
x_{i}=t_{\nu}-1
and
x_{i-1}=s_{\nu+1}-1
. Then we are comparing (
4.11
) with
\mu=\nu
to (
4.10
) with
\mu=\nu+1
, in which case it is clear that
\delta_{i}\leq\Delta_{i-1}
. With
i=d
, we are comparing (
4.13
) to (
4.12
) with
\mu=2
, so it is clear that
\delta_{d}\leq\Delta_{d-1}
.

So, we have checked all of the assumptions of Lemma
4.5
. Applying Lemma
4.5
yields the result.
∎

We are now ready to prove Proposition
4.2
by showing that the values of
hA
for
A\in\mathcal{A}_{b}
with
b
varying form an interval.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{b}
be as in Lemma
4.19
and recall that
c=k-b
. Define

\mathcal{A}\coloneq\bigcup_{b=3}^{k-\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor}\mathcal{A}_{b}.

By Lemma
4.19
,
\mathcal{A}_{b}
is connected. To show that
\mathcal{A}
is connected, it suffices to show that
h\mathcal{A}_{b}\cap h\mathcal{A}_{b-1}\neq\emptyset
for
b\in[4,k-\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor]
.

For any
b\in[3,k-\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor]
, define
A_{b}\in\mathcal{A}_{b}
by
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=(1,\ldots,1,1)
using (
4.1
) and (
4.2
). Let
A_{b-1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{b-1}
have
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=(1,\ldots,1,n_{d})
using (
4.1
) and (
4.2
). Then
A_{b}=B_{1,b}\sqcup C
and
A_{b-1}^{\prime}=B_{s,b-1}\sqcup C^{\prime}
where
\mathcal{F}_{C}(z)\equiv(1-z)^{-(k-b)}
and
\mathcal{F}_{C^{\prime}}(z)\equiv(1-z)^{-(k-b+1)}
by (
4.6
). By Lemma
4.13
(d), we also have
\mathcal{F}_{B_{1,b}}(z)\equiv(1-z)^{-1}\mathcal{F}_{B_{s,b-1}}(z)
. Hence,
\mathcal{F}_{A_{b}}(z)\equiv\mathcal{F}_{A_{b-1}^{\prime}}(z)
, so
|hA_{b}|=|hA_{b-1}^{\prime}|
. So
h\mathcal{A}_{b}\cap h\mathcal{A}_{b-1}\neq\emptyset
.

We now show that
\binom{h+k-1}{h}\in h\mathcal{A}_{3}
and that there is a value smaller than
\varepsilon k^{h}
in
h\mathcal{A}_{k-\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor}
. This will imply that the interval
h\mathcal{A}
contains the desired interval. Plugging into Lemma
4.13
(c) and (
4.6
) gives the formula

\mathcal{F}_{A_{3}}(z)\equiv(1-z)^{-(3-(k-3))}\equiv(1-z)^{-k}.

By the binomial theorem,
|hA_{3}|=\binom{h+k-1}{h}\in h\mathcal{A}_{3}
. Plugging into (
4.6
), we have that

(4.14)

\mathcal{F}_{A_{k-\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor}}(z)\equiv\frac{1}{(1-z)^{\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor}}\mathcal{F}_{B_{1,k-\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor}}(z).

By Lemma
4.13
(b),
|\eta B_{1,{k-\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor}}|\in O(k)
for all
1\leq\eta\leq h
. By (
4.7
), we also have
\frac{1}{(1-z)^{\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor}}\in\Theta_{+}[[k^{0.7}z]]
. Multiplying out (
4.14
) gives that
|hA_{k-\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor}|\in O(k^{0.7(h-1)+1})
. Since
0.7(h-1)+1<h
, we can get
|hA_{k-\lfloor k^{0.7}\rfloor}|<\varepsilon k^{h}
for any desired
\varepsilon>0
by making
k
large enough.
∎

5.1.
Working towards Conjecture
1.6

Let
k_{h}^{\prime}
be the
minimal
natural number such that for all

, we have

(5.1)

\mathcal{R}(h,k)=\left[hk-h+1,\binom{h+k-1}{h}\right]\setminus\Delta_{h,k}.

Then, Theorem
1.4
proves that
k_{h}^{\prime}
exists, and by Remark
1.5
,
k_{h}^{\prime}\leq 2^{20h^{2}}
. Conjecture
1.6
is that
k_{h}^{\prime}\leq h
. It would be interesting to try to improve the upper bound on
k_{h}^{\prime}
to exponential in
h
, or polynomial in

, in order to work towards Conjecture

. Or, if these bounds were improved for small

, it might be possible to explicitly compute that

) is always a half-open interval given by
[k_{h}^{\prime}+1,\infty)
.

Another natural follow-up would be to extend the inductive arguments from the proof of Theorem

to
h=4
or
h=5
. This could be approached using geometric pictures like Figure
4
. We have been unable to do this using the exact methods from Theorem

because it seems necessary to control

,
|3A|
, and

all at once.

for small

and

.

5.2.
What if
h\geq k
?

Let a “gap” in
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
be a number in
\left[hk-h+1,\binom{h+k-1}{h}\right]\setminus\mathcal{R}(h,k)
. For many pairs
(h,k)
with
h\geq k
, there appear to be gaps in
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
that are not in
\Delta_{h,k}
; in other words, (
5.1
) seems not to hold. We pose the problem of studying where all of these gaps are for certain values of
(h,k)
and proving that some of these other gaps exist. For example, one conjecture that would extend Lemma
2.2
and Lemma
2.3
is the following:

Conjecture 5.1
.

Let
h\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\geq 3
. If
|A|=k
and
\mathrm{diam}(A)=d
, then

|hA|\geq|h([0,k-2]\cup\{d\})|.

This conjecture is trivially tight if it is true. The value on the right side of Conjecture
5.1
is explicitly computed in terms of
h
and
k
in
[
Nat96
, Theorem 1.11]
(see Lemma
4.13
). Clearly,
|hA|\leq hd+1
. Conjecture
5.1
then implies that whenever
h>k+1
, there are gaps in
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
not in
\Delta_{h,k}
, disproving (
5.1
) for these values of
(h,k)
. One example of a gap implied by Conjecture
5.1
for any
h>k+1
is
2hk-3h+2
.

With
k=3
and
h\geq 4
, Nathanson
[
Nat24
, Theorem 9]
proved there are many gaps in
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
not in
\Delta_{h,k}
. Another example of a conjectured gap is that for
k=4
and
h\geq 4
,
5h\not\in\mathcal{R}(h,4)
. The only conjectured gaps outside
\Delta_{h,k}
we have found with
h\leq k+1
occur when
(h,k)\in\{(4,3),(4,4),(5,4)\}
.

When
h
is significantly larger than
k
, there are many more gaps in
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
than can be explained by Conjecture
5.1
. One could also conjecture the structure of more of these gaps, and try to prove that any of them exist.

5.3.
Restricted Sumsets

For
A\subset\mathbb{Z}
, define the
h
-fold restricted sumset of
A
by

\widehat{hA}\coloneq\{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{h}:a_{i}\in A\text{ and }a_{i}\neq a_{j}\text{ if }i\neq j\}.

Then, Nathanson defined
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(h,k)
analogously to
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
.

Definition 5.2
.

[
Nat24
]

Define the
range of cardinalities of
h
-fold restricted sumsets

\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(h,k)\coloneq\{|\widehat{hA}|:A\subset\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }|A|=k\}.

All of the questions asked or answered in this paper about
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
can be asked about
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(h,k)
, and one could study whether similar results to Theorems
1.3
,
1.4
, and
1.7
hold in this context.

5.4.
Working with Torsion

The results in this paper also hold with
\mathbb{Z}
replaced by any infinite torsion-free abelian group. We pose the problem of determining the form of
\mathcal{R}(h,k)
or
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(h,k)
in groups with torsion. One interesting candidate for such a group is
(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{n}
, with
m\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{1\}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. This seems interesting already when
m=2
.
