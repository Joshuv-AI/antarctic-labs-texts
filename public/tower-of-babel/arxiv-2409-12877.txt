Physics aware machine learning for micromagnetic energy minimization: recent algorithmic developments
Sebastian Schaffer, Thomas Schrefl, Harald Oezelt, Norbert J. Mauser, Lukas Exl

Abstract




Abstract.

In this work, we explore advanced machine learning techniques for minimizing Gibbs free energy in full 3D micromagnetic simulations. Building on Brown’s bounds for magnetostatic self-energy, we revisit their application in the context of variational formulations of the transmission problems for the scalar and vector potential. To overcome the computational challenges posed by whole-space integrals, we reformulate these bounds on a finite domain, making the method more efficient and scalable for numerical simulation. Our approach utilizes an alternating optimization scheme for joint minimization of Brown’s energy bounds and the Gibbs free energy. The Cayley transform is employed to rigorously enforce the unit norm constraint, while
R
-functions are used to impose essential boundary conditions in the computation of magnetostatic fields.
Our results highlight the potential of mesh-free Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and Extreme Learning Machines (ELMs) when integrated with hard constraints, providing highly accurate approximations. These methods exhibit competitive performance compared to traditional numerical approaches, showing significant promise in computing magnetostatic fields and the application for energy minimization, such as the computation of hysteresis curves. This work opens the path for future directions of research on more complex geometries, such as grain structure models, and the application to large scale problem settings which are intractable with traditional numerical methods.

Keywords.

micromagnetic energy minimization, magnetostatic energy bound, physics-informed neural network, boundary integral operator, extreme learning machine, vector potential

Introduction

Magnetostatics, a fundamental area of study in micromagnetics, focuses on understanding the behavior of magnetic fields and magnetization within materials. The minimization of magnetic energy, particularly Gibbs free energy, plays a critical role in determining stable magnetic configurations. Traditional numerical methods, such as the finite element method (FEM)
[
1
]
and finite difference methods (FDM)
[
2
]
, have long been used to solve magnetostatic problems, but these methods face challenges in efficiency and scalability, especially in three-dimensional domains with complex geometries and boundary conditions. In recent years, low-rank models were developed as a way to compress the high dimensional representation of the solution and perform computations in a reduced setting. Techniques such as multilinear low-rank tensor methods
[
3
]
and spectral decomposition for computing the eigenmode evolution of the effective field operator
[
4
,
5
]
exemplify these approaches. Kernel methods offer a way to compress magnetization data and capture magnetization dynamics within a low-dimensional latent space, enabling faster prediction of the system’s response to changes in the external magnetic field
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
. However, the supervised approach requires large amount of simulation data, which can be prohibitive.

Recently, there has been a growing interest in applying unsupervised Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs)
[
9
]
models to micromagnetic simulations, which provides promising alternatives to traditional numerical approaches and does not rely on precomputed data. PINNs have gained popularity for solving partial differential equations (PDEs) by embedding physical laws directly into the learning framework. Unlike conventional methods, they offer flexibility in the training process and can incorporate conditional parameters to learn a whole family of solutions
[
10
]
. This can be particularly useful for inverse modelling. A related method, Extreme Learning Machines (ELMs)
[
11
]
, allows for rapid training since only output parameters need to be optimized, offering computational efficiency while maintaining an accurate solution with a predetermined solution operator.
Hard-constrained PINNs and ELMs, incorporating R-functions
[
12
,
13
]
, extend these methods by exactly enforcing boundary conditions within the model architecture. This avoids the need for soft constraint formulations and penalty terms. These approaches are particularly valuable in magnetostatics, where the enforcement of boundary conditions is crucial to accurate simulations. We apply these methods for the computational expensive computation of magnetostatic fields.

One of the most challenging tasks in micromagnetic simulations is the minimization of the Gibbs free energy in full 3D systems. The main challenges lie in the costly computation of the magnetostatic fields and the enforcement of the unit norm constraint, which poses a difficult nonlinear optimization problem.
Brown’s energy bounds
[
14
]
offer theoretical limits for magnetostatic self-energy. However, in their plain form, they require the evaluation of whole space integrals, which is a challenging task for numerical simulations. Truncation approaches can be used, but they introduce an additional truncation error. Using the splitting ansatz proposed by Garcia-Cervera and Roma, we express these whole space integrals solely on the finite domain of the magnetic body. With this description, we can use an alternating scheme for the joint optimization of the energy bounds and magnetization without considering the exterior space.
To account for the unit norm constraint, penalty frameworks
[
15
]
and a hard constraint ansatz using the Cayley transform
[
16
]
have been proposed. Modern automatic differentiation (AD) frameworks allow the efficient computation of required differential operators and optimization of resulting objectives.

This article aims to explore the application of these modern machine learning methods, including PINNs and ELMs, in micromagnetic simulations, with a focus on the minimization of Gibbs free energy. By integrating traditional magnetostatic theory with state-of-the-art machine learning techniques, we aim to present new pathways for more efficient and scalable simulations.

Physics-Informed Neural Networks

Physics-informed machine learning and in particular a PINN is a novel approach to numerically solve a number of physical problems.
Contrary to the supervised learning task, in a physics-informed setting, there is only partial or no label information available and the objective is designed to minimize the residual of some governing algebraic or differential equation.

Due to the flexibility of neural networks, one can handle forward as well as inverse problems and integrate additional experimental or simulation data. PINNs have diverse applications in scientific and engineering domains such as fluid dynamics
[
17
,
18
]
, material science
[
19
]
, geophysics and climate modelling
[
20
]
.

For instance, consider a Poisson equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions on the domain
\Omega
with boundary
\partial\Omega

\displaystyle-\Delta u

\displaystyle=f\quad\,\text{in }\,\Omega

(1)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=g\quad\,\text{on }\,\partial\Omega.

One possibility to find an approximate solution to this problem is to use a neural network ansatz
u_{\bm{\omega}}=u(\,\cdot\,;\bm{\omega})
which is parameterized by
\bm{\omega}
and minimize the physics-informed objective

\mathcal{L}_{\text{PINN}}(\bm{\omega})=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}|\Delta u_{\bm{\omega}}(\bm{x})+f(\bm{x})|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}_{\mathcal{L}_{\text{PDE}}(\bm{\omega})}+\lambda\underbrace{\int_{\partial\Omega}|u_{\bm{\omega}}(\bm{x})-g(\bm{x})|^{2}\mathrm{d}s(\bm{x})}_{\mathcal{L}_{b}(\bm{\omega})},

(2)

where
\lambda
is a penalty parameter to account for boundary conditions.

Partial derivatives can be computed by AD either in forward or reverse mode. If the input is low-dimensional, forward-mode AD can be used efficiently. Hence, the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\omega}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{PINN}}
can be computed with forward AD followed by reverse mode, i.e.,
reverse-over-forward
.

Choosing the penalty parameter
\lambda
in (
2
) is not straightforward. One approach is to employ the squared penalty method, solving a series of optimization problems while gradually increasing the penalty parameter. However, while this may improve accuracy, it also significantly increases computational cost. Moreover, as the penalty parameter grows, the condition number of the Hessian worsens, leading to an ill-conditioned problem. Alternatively, instead of relying on the penalty formulation in (
2
), one could adopt an augmented Lagrangian approach to minimize the PDE residual
[
21
]
.

A priori and a posteriori error estimates of residual based minimization of linear PDEs, using PINNs with soft constraints, have been derived in
[
22
]
. This shows that a minimizer of (
2
) is actually a physically meaningful approximation to the true solution of the PDE.

Another example of PINNs would be the minimization of some energy functional with possible additional constraints in a penalty term, i.e.,

\displaystyle E(\bm{\omega})=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{E}\big(u_{\bm{\omega}}(\bm{x})\big)\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+\textrm{Penalty},

(3)

where
\mathcal{E}
is the energy density.

To compute the respective integrals of the objective function accurately, one could for instance choose some quadrature rule. For arbitrary geometries it is usually easier to use Monte Carlo integration and select a set of collocation points
\mathcal{S}=\{\bm{x}_{i}\,|\,i=1,\dots,N\}\subseteq\Omega
, drawn from some distribution, to approximate the integral. A comprehensive study of different sampling strategies, as well as residual based sampling algorithms, is given in
[
23
]
for various problem settings. It can be seen that quasi-random low discrepancy sequence leads to a lower relative error and importance sampling
[
24
]
further improves the performance and convergence of PINNs. However, this study does not take into account hard constraint PINNs (see section
2.1
), but it is expected that these results also hold.
For an energy functional as (
3
), where the energy density is non-zero, and the sampling distribution is non-uniform, importance sampling can be applied.

The main challenge is the minimization of the objective function. This is usually done with some variant of stochastic gradient descent, as well as second-order optimization methods like
L-BFGS
or
Trust-Region
. Further, hyperparameter optimization is a very challenging task for PINNs. It is not clear how to choose the perfect architecture to match the task at hand, and there is very little research regarding this issue
[
25
]
. Overparametrization of the model is usually required to gain accuracy, resulting in a more complicated optimization problem which becomes more ill-conditioned with a growing number of parameters.

2.1
Hard constraint formulation

An alternative approach to the soft constraint formulation, where the integrity of the solution is embedded in the governing objective, is the hard constraint formulation. Here, boundary conditions are strictly embedded into the architecture of the model. This transforms the optimization problem into an unconstrained one. For instance, in the case of problem (
1
), the model can be defined as.

u_{\bm{\omega}}(\bm{x})=\ell(\bm{x})\hat{u}_{\bm{\omega}}(\bm{x})+\tilde{g}(\bm{x}),

(4)

where

\ell(\bm{x})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0\quad\,\text{if}\,\,\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega\\
>0\quad\,\text{if}\,\,\bm{x}\in\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(5)

Here,
\hat{u}_{\bm{\omega}}
represents a neural network, while the function
\ell
serves as an indicator. There are various ways to construct
\ell
, but as highlighted in
[
13
]
, it is advantageous if
\ell
closely approximates the exact distance function near the boundary. The function
\tilde{g}
is designed to interpolate
g
at the boundary and to remain well-defined throughout the domain. It can be computed by means of transfinite interpolation
[
26
,
27
]
or trained with a neural network
[
28
]
. Instead of minimizing the full loss function as in (
2
), we focus solely on minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{PDE}}
, since
\mathcal{L}_{b}
is zero.

3
Physics-Informed Extreme Learning Machines

An Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) is a type of neural network that contains only a single hidden layer. The input data is randomly mapped onto a latent space, meaning that only the output layer requires training. Formally, for an input
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an ELM with
M
hidden nodes and a scalar output can be described as

u_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\beta_{i}\sigma_{i}(\bm{x})=\bm{\beta}^{T}\bm{z}(\bm{x}),

(6)

where
\bm{z}
represents the latent embedding of the hidden layer. For instance,
\sigma_{i}
can be given by

•

an affine map followed by a nonlinear activation
\sigma
, i.e.
\sigma_{i}(\bm{x})=\sigma(\bm{w}_{i}^{T}\bm{x}+b_{i})
, where the input weights
(\bm{w}_{i},b_{i})
are drawn from some distribution,

•

a radial basis function, centered around a point
\bm{w}_{i}\in\Omega
, i.e.
\sigma_{i}(\bm{x})=\exp(-\gamma\|\bm{x}-\bm{w}_{i}\|^{2})
with kernel parameter
\gamma
,

•

or a basis of a B-spline function, leading to a sparse linear regression problem.

If the mean squared error (MSE) is to be minimized for some training set
\mathcal{S}=\{(\bm{x}_{i},y_{i})\,|\,i=1,\dots,N\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
,

\mathrm{MSE}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\bm{\beta}^{T}\bm{z}(\bm{x}_{i})-y_{i})^{2},

(7)

the solution is given by the least squares problem

H\bm{\beta}=\bm{y},

(8)

with

\begin{aligned} H_{i,j}&=\sigma_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})\\
\bm{y}_{i}&=y_{i}\end{aligned}\qquad\text{ for }\;i=1,\dots,N\text{ and }j=1,\dots,M.

with the solution operator being the pseudoinverse
H^{\dagger}=(H^{T}H)^{-1}H^{T}
.
If the size of the system becomes restrictive, one can use an incremental method to train the ELM
[
29
]
.

For an overparameterized model, computing the pseudoinverse can quickly become an ill-conditioned problem. Adding a Ridge regression term
\mu\|\bm{\beta}\|^{2}
to (
7
) can be very helpful in these cases. The simplest way is by modifying the pseudoinverse, i.e., when computing the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)
H=USV^{T}
, with singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{M}
, the regularized solution operator is given by

H^{\dagger}_{\mu}=VS_{\mu}^{-1}U^{T},

(9)

where
S_{\mu}^{-1}
is diagonal with the
i
th
diagonal entry given by
S_{\mu,ii}^{-1}=\sigma_{i}/(\sigma_{i}^{2}+\mu)
.

3.1
Hard constraint ELMs

Another possibility is the usage of a hard-constraint ELM ansatz, as in (
4
). The indicator function
\ell
only acts on the activation functions and the ansatz becomes

u_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{x})=\ell(\bm{x})\hat{u}_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{x})+\tilde{g}(\bm{x})=\bm{\beta}^{T}\ell(\bm{x})\bm{z}(\bm{x})+\tilde{g}(\bm{x}).

(10)

For a Poisson equation, as in (
1
), we have

-\Delta(u_{\bm{\beta}}(\bm{x})+\tilde{g}(\bm{x}))=-\bm{\beta}^{T}\Delta\left(\ell(\bm{x})\bm{z}(\bm{x})\right)-\Delta\tilde{g}(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x}).

(11)

Minimization of the squared residual

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELM}}(\bm{\beta})=\int_{\Omega}|\bm{\beta}^{T}\Delta\left(\ell(\bm{x})\bm{z}(\bm{x})\right)+\Delta\tilde{g}(\bm{x})+f(\bm{x})|^{2}\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

(12)

is equivalent to the solution of the system

H\bm{\beta}=\bm{b},

(13)

with

\begin{aligned} H_{ij}&=-\int_{\Omega}\Delta(\ell(\bm{x})\sigma_{i}(\bm{x}))\Delta(\ell(\bm{x})\sigma_{j}(\bm{x}))\mathrm{d}\bm{x},\\
\bm{b}_{i}&=\int_{\Omega}f(\bm{x})\Delta(\ell(\bm{x})\sigma_{i}(\bm{x}))+\Delta\tilde{g}(\bm{x})\Delta(\ell(\bm{x})\sigma_{i}(\bm{x}))\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\end{aligned}\begin{aligned} \quad&\text{ for }\;i=1,\dots,M\\
&\text{ and }j=1,\dots,M.\end{aligned}

Proper numerical integration of all those integrals might be very costly. A Monte-Carlo integration of (
12
) is often easier with comparative performance. With the dataset of collocation points
\mathcal{S}=\{\bm{x}_{i}\,|\,i=1,\dots,N\}\subseteq\Omega
we get the objective

\mathcal{L}_{\text{ELM}}(\bm{\beta})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\bm{\beta}^{T}\Delta\left(\ell(\bm{x}_{i})\bm{z}(\bm{x}_{i})\right)+\Delta\tilde{g}(\bm{x}_{i})+f(\bm{x}_{i})|^{2}.

(14)

The minimizer of (
14
) is given by the solution to the least squares problem

A\bm{\beta}=\bm{b},

(15)

with

\begin{aligned} A_{ij}&=-\Delta\left(\ell(\bm{x}_{i})\sigma_{j}(\bm{x}_{i})\right),\\
\bm{b}_{i}&=f(\bm{x}_{i})+\Delta\tilde{g}(\bm{x}_{i})\end{aligned}\qquad\text{ for }\;i=1,\dots,N\text{ and }j=1,\dots,M.

If this system is not too large, the pseudoinverse
A^{\dagger}
defines the solution operator and can be precomputed using SVD. The best parameter
\bm{\beta}
for some right-hand side
f
is then given by a simple matrix multiplication. If the problem is ill conditioned, a Ridge regression term can be added. Further, for good performance and depending on the hidden layer activation, the input might be rescaled to
[-1,1]^{d}
.

3.1.1
Kernel perspective

The regularized solution operator can also be computed with the dual formulation, i.e.,

A^{\dagger}=(A^{T}A+\mu I)^{-1}A^{T}=A^{T}(AA^{T}+\mu I)^{-1},

(16)

by application of the push-through identity. This is equivalent to Kernel Ridge Regression (KRR)
[
30
]
with the kernel function given by

k(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2})=\Delta\left(\ell(\bm{x}_{1})\bm{z}(\bm{x}_{1})\right)^{T}\Delta\left(\ell(\bm{x}_{2})\bm{z}(\bm{x}_{2})\right).

(17)

Thus, the kernel matrix is given by
K=AA^{T}
.

There is a clear connection of ELMs and the low-rank KRR
[
8
,
7
,
31
]
. Consider a KRR with kernel function
a(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})=\ell(\bm{x}_{i})\psi(\bm{x}_{i})\cdot\ell(\bm{x}_{j})\psi(\bm{x}_{j})
, where
\psi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{\Omega},\bm{x}\mapsto\psi(\bm{x})
is a feature map which transforms the input to a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) and
\cdot
denotes the inner product in this space.
The regularized physics-informed loss is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\text{KRR}}(\bm{\xi})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\Delta\left(\ell(\bm{x}_{i})\psi(\bm{x}_{i})\right)\cdot\bm{\xi}+\Delta\tilde{g}(\bm{x}_{i})+f(\bm{x}_{i})|^{2}+\mu(\bm{\xi}\cdot\bm{\xi}).

(18)

Let
\psi_{\ell}(\bm{x})=\Delta\left(\ell(\bm{x})\psi(\bm{x})\right)
and denote
\Psi[\mathcal{S}]
as the matrix of size
N\times\mathrm{dim}(\mathcal{F}_{\Omega})
which holds the samples mapped to the RKHS

\Psi[\mathcal{S}]:=[\psi_{\ell}(\bm{x}_{1})|\dots|\psi_{\ell}(\bm{x}_{N})]^{T}.

(19)

The dual solution would then be given by

\bm{\xi}=\Psi[\mathcal{S}]^{T}(\Psi[\mathcal{S}]\cdot\Psi[\mathcal{S}]+\mu I)^{-1}\bm{b}=\Psi[\mathcal{S}]^{T}(G[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}]+\mu I)^{-1}\bm{b},

(20)

where
G[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}]
is the corresponding kernel matrix. If the kernel matrix
G[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}]
is now approximated with a low-rank version of rank
M
, i.e,

G[\mathcal{S},\mathcal{S}]\approx\widetilde{\Psi}[\mathcal{S}]\widetilde{\Psi}[\mathcal{S}]^{T}
with
\widetilde{\Psi}[\mathcal{S}]
being a low-rank matrix of size
N\times M
of
\Psi
, the solution operator has the same form as for the ELM ansatz.
The problem with this ansatz is the computation of the Laplace operator, since it is not clear what the corresponding kernel to
\Delta\left(\ell(\bm{x}_{i})\psi(\bm{x}_{i})\right)\Delta\left(\ell(\bm{x}_{j})\psi(\bm{x}_{j})\right)
should be. The ELM solves this by mapping the input directly onto a randomized low-rank RKHS. The feature space mapping
\widetilde{\Psi}[\mathcal{S}]
is directly given by the ELM’s
hidden layer output.

In our experiments, selecting for instance a radial basis activation function with
\ell(\bm{x})\sigma_{i}(\bm{x})=\ell(\bm{x})\exp(-\gamma\|\bm{x}-\bm{w}_{i}\|^{2})
with
\bm{w}_{i}\in\Omega
being the weight of the
i
th
node of the ELM, achieves similar performance to other activation functions, such as
tanh
or
gelu
with the advantage that the weights can be drawn from the domain and there is no intercept term required. For difficult functions, such as the approximation of a vortex state with vector potential (see Section
6.3
), this kind of ELM even outperforms ELMs with other activations. The disadvantage lies in the selection of the kernel parameter
\gamma
which requires hyperparameter tuning.

3.1.2
Neural operator perspective

The physics-informed ELM can also be seen as a kind of operator network, like Deep Operator Networks DeepONet
[
32
]
. A DeepONet
G
has the basic structure

G(\bm{x};\,u)=B(u)\cdot T(\bm{x}),

(21)

where
B
is the so-called branch network and
T
the trunk network. The branch network takes as input a function
u
(or discrete measurements of the function) and the trunk network takes a point from the input domain. The result is given by the inner product of the branch and the trunk. The DeepONet can the then be trained in a physics-informed setting for various input functions
u
, sampled from some function space for the goal to approximate the respective inverse operator. For an ELM and the objective (
14
), the branch is given by
B(f,g)=A^{\dagger}\bm{b}(f,g)
and the trunk by
T(\bm{x})=\ell(\bm{x})\bm{z}(\bm{x})
. The main difference is, that only a shallow network is applied and there is no complicated training procedure required for the determination of the solution operator. Pre-training a DeepONet might be a versatile alternative to ELMs. Especially, Graph Neural Networks
[
33
]
are of interest for the task of learning the solution operator
[
34
,
35
]
. The main challenge lies in the training of the model, which requires sampling from some function space. ELMs could be utilized for efficient sampling of such a space. Especially, training of the branch network can be challenging.

All in all, those seemingly simple ELM models are very powerful function approximators and are capable to solve quite complex problems with ease. For small scale problems, the solution can be computed without the need of the weak formulation, allowing a stable computation without preconditioning. However, for larger irregular domains, it is still questionable how to select the input weights properly. A Gradient Boosting technique could be applied to solve for consecutive small ELMs and the error information might be applied as information for the input weight distribution.

Magnetostatics with PINNs

Magnetostatics focuses on the study of magnetic fields in systems with steady currents. The fundamental equations governing magnetostatics are derived from Maxwell’s equations and describe how magnetic fields are generated by steady currents and magnetic materials. Solving these equations typically involves complex boundary conditions and interactions between magnetic domains, which has traditionally required numerical methods such as the finite difference (FDM) or finite element methods (FEM).

Recently, the advent of PINNs has introduced a novel approach to solving magnetostatic problems. PINNs can be used to model continuous magnetization configurations commonly used in micromagnetism
[
36
]
. This approach not only simplifies the computational process but also offers flexibility in incorporating additional conditional parameters such as exchange length. Moreover, as described in this chapter, they can efficiently handle the computationally intensive stray field problem by employing techniques like ELMs and splitting methods, further enhancing their applicability to magnetostatic simulations.

4.1
The magnetostatic self-energy

An important aspect of magnetostatics is the calculation of magnetostatic self-energy, which represents the energy stored in the magnetic field due to the magnetization of the material. This self-energy is a crucial factor in determining the stability and equilibrium configurations of magnetic systems, influencing how magnetic domains form and interact.

The magnetostatic self-energy or demagnetizing energy
E_{d}
of a magnet
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is usually computed via the stray field
\bm{H}_{d}=M_{s}\bm{h}_{d}
through
[
37
]

\displaystyle E_{d}(\bm{m})=-\frac{\mu_{0}M_{s}^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot\bm{h}_{d}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\frac{\mu_{0}M_{d}^{2}}{2}\,e_{d}(\bm{m}),

(22)

where
\bm{m}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{3}
is the magnetization configuration, which is non-zero in the bounded domain
\Omega
and zero in the exterior domain
\overline{\Omega}^{c}=\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
,
M_{s}
the saturation magnetization,
\mu_{0}
the vacuum permeability,
\bm{h}_{d}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{3}
the dimensionless stray field and
e_{d}
the reduced self-energy in dimensions of volume.

Accurate computation of the stray field is crucial for many numerical algorithms in micromagnetism. This step enables precise mathematical modeling of magnetic system behaviors, which is essential for designing highly efficient magnetic devices or permanent magnets
[
38
,
39
,
40
]
.

The stray field
\bm{h}_{d}=-\nabla\phi_{d}
is the solution of the whole space Poisson problem

\displaystyle\Delta\phi_{d}=\nabla\cdot\bm{m}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}

(23)

for the scalar potential
\phi_{d}
.
The Poisson equation (
23
) follows from the magnetostatic Maxwell’s equations,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bm{b}_{d}&\,=\bm{m}+\bm{h}_{d}\\
\nabla\cdot\bm{b}_{d}&\,=0\\
\nabla\times\bm{h}_{d}&\,=\bm{0},\end{aligned}

(24)

where
\bm{b}_{d}=\bm{B}_{d}/(M_{s}\mu_{0})
is the (dimensionless) magnetic induction (or magnetic flux density)
\bm{B}_{d}
. The fields
\bm{h}_{d}=\bm{h}_{d}(\bm{m})
and
\bm{b}_{d}=\bm{b}_{d}(\bm{m})
depend (linearly) on
\bm{m}
, but we omit this dependency for the sake of simpler notation.
The components
\bm{b}_{d}\cdot\bm{n}
and
\bm{h}_{d}\times\bm{n}
, with
\bm{n}
denoting the outward unit normal vector, are continuous across the surface of
\Omega

[
14
]
.
The fields
\bm{h}_{d}
and
\bm{b}_{d}
form a Helmholtz decomposition of the magnetization
\bm{m}
in whole space into a sum of an irrotational and solenoidal part
[
14
]
, which are
L^{2}
-orthogonal in whole space, that is,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bm{h}_{d}\cdot\bm{b}_{d}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=0.

(25)

Using this orthogonality and the relations
\bm{b}_{d}=\bm{m}+\bm{h}_{d}
in (
24
) one can express the demagnetizing energy (
22
) in the equivalent forms

\displaystyle e_{d}(\bm{m})

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot\bm{h}_{d}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{h}_{d}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(26)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\|\bm{m}\|^{2}-\bm{m}\cdot\bm{b}_{d}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\|\bm{m}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{b}_{d}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

where integration involving
\bm{m}
is over
\Omega
only and
\int_{\Omega}\|\bm{m}\|^{2}\,dx=V
the volume of
\Omega
.
Opposed to
\bm{h}_{d}
being the solution of (
23
), the magnetic induction
\bm{b}_{d}=\nabla\times\bm{A}_{d}
is the solution of the whole space vector Poisson equation

\displaystyle\nabla\times(\nabla\times\bm{A}_{d})=\nabla\times\bm{m}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}

(27)

for the vector potential
\bm{A}_{d}
. There is freedom of gauge for the vector potential, since one can add an arbitrary gradient field, i.e.,
\bm{b}_{d}=\nabla\times(\bm{A}_{d}+\nabla\Psi)
. Usually the
Coulomb gauge

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{A}_{d}=0

(28)

is chosen, which reduces (
27
) to

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{A}_{d}=-\nabla\times\bm{m}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(29)

Splitting the interior and the exterior part of the scalar potential,
\phi_{d}=\phi^{int}_{d}
in
\Omega
and
\phi_{d}=\phi^{ext}_{d}
in
\overline{\Omega}^{c}
, (
23
) can be formulated as a transmission problem
[
41
,
42
]

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi_{d}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\bm{m}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},

(30)

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi_{d}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\overline{\Omega}^{c},

\displaystyle[\phi_{d}]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle\left[D_{\bm{n}}\phi_{d}\right]

\displaystyle=-\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle\phi_{d}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\|\bm{x}\|}\right)

\displaystyle\text{as}\;\|\bm{x}\|\rightarrow\infty.

The jump conditions
[\,\cdot\,]
describe the difference of the exterior and interior behavior at the boundary of the magnet, i.e.,

\displaystyle[\phi_{d}]

\displaystyle=\left.(\phi^{ext}_{d}-\phi_{d}^{int})\right|_{\partial\Omega}

(31)

\displaystyle[D_{\bm{n}}\phi_{d}]

\displaystyle=\left.(D_{\bm{n}}\phi^{ext}_{d}-D_{\bm{n}}\phi^{int}_{d})\right|_{\partial\Omega}.

Further, the normal derivative is denoted by
D_{\bm{n}}
. For (
30
) to be well-posed, the boundary
\partial\Omega
needs to be Lipschitz continuous. For grain structure modeling, this is usually true.
In the same way, the Poisson equation of the vector potential (
29
) gives
[
42
]

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{A}_{d}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\times\bm{m}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega,

(32)

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{A}_{d}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}

\displaystyle\text{in }\overline{\Omega}^{c},

\displaystyle[\bm{A}_{d}]

\displaystyle=\bm{0}

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle\left[D_{\bm{n}}\bm{A}_{d}\right]

\displaystyle=-\bm{m}\times\bm{n}

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle(\bm{A}_{d})_{j}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\|\bm{x}\|}\right)

\displaystyle\text{as }\|\bm{x}\|\rightarrow\infty,\,j=1,2,3.

The transmission problems (
30
) or (
32
) can be solved in different ways numerically. Finite element methods usually solve the weak formulation for certain splitting of the potentials
[
43
,
44
,
1
,
45
]
, finite difference methods approximate the integral representations of the solution
[
2
]
,

\phi_{d}(\bm{x})=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{\nabla\cdot\bm{m}(\bm{y})}{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|}\mathrm{d}\bm{y}-\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\bm{m}(\bm{y})\cdot\bm{n}(\bm{y})}{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|}\mathrm{d}s(\bm{y})\right).

(33)

However, there are significant challenges in implementing this solution. Firstly, the computation of the convolution integral across the entire domain is highly resource-intensive. Secondly, obtaining the gradient of (
33
) is typically done with numerical differentiation, which can lead to discretization and round-off errors.
Recent neural network methods take a mixed approach
[
15
,
16
]
. In whatever way the solution is obtained, the computation of the
\bm{h}_{d}
-field (or
\bm{b}_{d}
-field) is usually the most time-consuming part in micromagnetic modelling due to its non-local nature
[
46
]
.

4.2
Brown’s energy bounds

In the 60s, W.F. Brown suggested simple sharp bounds for the magnetostatic energy only in terms of field variables with no dependency on
\bm{m}
and free from long-range interactions
[
14
,
37
]
. For any irrotational field
\bm{h}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{3}
and solenoidal field
\bm{b}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})^{3}
holds

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\|\bm{m}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{h}+\bm{m}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\,\leq\,e_{d}(\bm{m})\,\leq\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{b}-\bm{m}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(34)

The upper bound is derived by adding
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{b}_{d}-\bm{b}\|^{2}\,dx
to the energy, while the lower bound results from subtracting
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{h}_{d}-\bm{h}\|^{2}\,dx
from the energy
[
47
]
and using
\bm{b}_{d}=\bm{m}+\bm{h}_{d}
as well as orthogonality of irrotational and solenoidal fields (
25
).
In order to compute the energy as well as the respective stray field
\bm{h}_{d}
, one can maximize the lower bound with respect to a scalar potential
\phi
for the ansatz
\bm{h}=-\nabla\phi
. Similarly, minimizing the upper bound with
\bm{b}=\nabla\times\bm{A}
with a vector potential
\bm{A}
gives the respective magnetic induction
\bm{b}_{d}
in the optimum.

While those energy bounds have been shown to be applicable in a physics-informed setting
[
48
]
for 2d, in practice however, the integration over whole space in the bounds (
34
) causes problems. We will come back to this issue in Section
4.4
. For now, we will use these bounds to derive some basic results.

4.2.1
Weak formulation

A variational form of the transmission problem (
30
) involving the scalar potential is derived by utilizing partial integration and the jump condition
\left[D_{\bm{n}}{\phi_{d}}\right]=-\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}
at the boundary:

\displaystyle\text{For $\bm{m}\in\big(H^{1}(\Omega)\big)^{3}$, find $\phi_{d}\in X_{\phi}$ such that for all $\phi\in X_{\phi}$ there holds}

(35)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla\phi_{d}\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

where
X_{\phi}
is a suitable weighted Sobolev space
[
49
]
which incorporates the continuity across the boundary and the decay behavior.
There exists a unique solution of (
35
) in
X_{\phi}
. Solving the weak form (
35
) is equivalent to maximizing the following functional over
X_{\phi}

\displaystyle e_{\phi}(\phi;\,\bm{m})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\nabla\phi\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+2\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(36)

The variational formulation for the vector potential is derived similarly, using the jump condition
\left[D_{\bm{n}}{\bm{A}_{d}}\right]=-\bm{m}\times\bm{n}
:

\displaystyle\text{For $\bm{m}\in\big(H^{1}(\Omega)\big)^{3}$, find $\bm{A}_{d}\in X_{\bm{A}}$ such that for all $\bm{A}\in X_{\bm{A}}$ there holds}

(37)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla\bm{A}_{d}:\nabla\bm{A}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot(\nabla\times\bm{A})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

where
\nabla\bm{A}_{d}:\nabla\bm{A}=Tr[\nabla(\bm{A}_{d})^{T}\nabla\bm{A}]
denotes double contraction. Solving (
37
) is equivalent to minimizing

\displaystyle e_{\bm{A}}(\bm{A};\,\bm{m})=\int_{\Omega}\|\bm{m}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\nabla\bm{A}\|^{2}\,dx-2\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot(\nabla\times\bm{A})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(38)

over
X_{\bm{A}}
, where
\int_{\Omega}\|\bm{m}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
is a constant and
\|\nabla\bm{A}\|^{2}=\nabla\bm{A}:\nabla\bm{A}
.

The energy functionals (
36
) and (
38
) correspond to the lower and upper bound of (
34
)

e_{\phi}(\phi;\,\bm{m})\leq e_{d}(\bm{m})\leq e_{\bm{A}}(\bm{A};\,\bm{m}).

(39)

In numerical computations, we do not actually solve the potential equations in the infinite dimensional spaces above, but rather assume a (finite dimensional) approximation space
\widetilde{X}_{\phi}\subset X_{\phi}
.
Hence, assuming an exact solution
\widetilde{\phi}_{d}\in\widetilde{X}_{\phi}
with
\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{d}=-\nabla\widetilde{\phi}_{d}
of the corresponding weak form (
35
), that is, we solve the (Galerkin) problem:

\displaystyle\text{For $\bm{m}\in\big(H^{1}(\Omega)\big)^{3}$, find $\widetilde{\phi}_{d}\in\widetilde{X}_{\phi}$ such that for all $\phi\in\widetilde{X}_{\phi}$ there holds}

(40)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla\widetilde{\phi}_{d}\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

Equivalently we can maximize the energy functional (
36
) over
\widetilde{X}_{\phi}
,

\widetilde{\phi}_{d}=\arg\max_{\phi\in\widetilde{X}_{\phi}}e_{\phi}(\phi;\,\bm{m})

(41)

Applying this solution to (
36
) gives

\displaystyle e_{\phi}(\widetilde{\phi}_{d};\,\bm{m})=-\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{d}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(42)

Therefore, by using the
L^{2}
-orthogonality (
25
) and the first relation in (
24
)

|e_{d}(\bm{m})-e_{\phi}(\widetilde{\phi}_{d};\,\bm{m})|=|\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot(\bm{h}_{d}-\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}|=|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bm{h}_{d}\cdot(\bm{h}_{d}-\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{d})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}|

(43)

and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, the error of the self energy can be bound by

\frac{|e_{d}(\bm{m})-e_{\phi}(\widetilde{\phi}_{d};\,\bm{m})|}{\sqrt{e_{d}(\bm{m})}}\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{h}_{d}-\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{d}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(44)

With the same line of reasoning, for an exact solution
\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{d}
with
\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{d}=\nabla\times\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{d}
of (
37
) on a finite dimensional subspace
\widetilde{X}_{\bm{A}}\subset X_{\bm{A}}
, one finds

\frac{|e_{d}(\bm{m})-e_{\bm{A}}(\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{d};\,\bm{m})|}{\sqrt{\int_{\Omega}|\bm{m}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-e_{d}(\bm{m})}}\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\bm{b}_{d}-\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{d}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(45)

Therefore, the deviation of the energy values can be estimated by the
L^{2}
-error resulting from the respective field computation.

Further, since the exact solution on an approximate solution space of the weak form is in the infinite-dimensional spaces
X_{\phi}
and
X_{\bm{A}}
respectively, the self-energy for the scalar potential is underestimated while the energy for the vector potential is overestimated, i.e.,

-\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{h}}_{d}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\leq e_{\phi}(\phi_{d};\,\bm{m})=e_{d}(\bm{m})=e_{\bm{A}}(\bm{A}_{d};\,\bm{m})\leq\int_{\Omega}\|\bm{m}\|^{2}-\bm{m}\cdot\widetilde{\bm{b}}_{d}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(46)

However, our assumption of an exact solution of the approximate formulation is not realistic in numerical computations in general, where the whole space problem (
40
) will hardly hold exactly, and likewise (
42
). Hence, (
46
) might be violated from both sides.
Nevertheless, in this case where we assume only an approximation
\widetilde{\phi}\approx\widetilde{\phi}_{d}
and
\widetilde{\bm{A}}\approx\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{d}
, the lower and upper bounds are still guaranteed to hold,

e_{\phi}(\widetilde{\phi};\,\bm{m})\leq e_{d}(\bm{m})\leq e_{\bm{A}}(\widetilde{\bm{A}};\,\bm{m}).

(47)

For the evaluation of the self-energy, it is necessary to compute the stray field or the magnetic induction within the magnetic domain. However, this requires a solution to the whole space problem (
30
). One could for instance use a truncation approach and minimize the upper bound in (
34
). In
[
48
]
this was done in 2d. In 3d, however, it turned out to be much more challenging, and we were not able to verify this approach. This could be due to the jump conditions which are not automatically satisfied. Especially the jump in the normal derivative can not be satisfied with a simple neural network ansatz. An easier and more flexible approach is described in the following section and in section
5.4
.

4.3
Splitting ansatz of Garcia-Cervera and Roma

To find a solution to the whole space transmission problem (
30
), it can be expressed as two easier sub-problems which only require the solution within the magnetic domain. Therefore, the splitting ansatz of Garcia-Cervera and Roma is employed. It can be used for the scalar and the vector potential alike. For the scalar potential, it is split into two parts
\phi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}
with

\phi_{1}=\bigg\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\phi_{1}^{int}&\text{in}\;\Omega\\
\phi_{1}^{ext}&\text{in}\;\overline{\Omega}^{c}\\
\end{array},\;\phi_{2}=\bigg\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\phi_{2}^{int}&\text{in}\;\Omega\\
\phi_{2}^{ext}&\text{in}\;\overline{\Omega}^{c}\\
\end{array},

and
\phi_{1}^{int},\;\phi_{2}^{int}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\phi_{1}^{ext},\;\phi_{2}^{ext}\in H^{1}_{loc}(\overline{\Omega}^{c})
.

The component
\phi_{1}
satisfies the homogeneous Dirichlet Poisson problem, i.e.

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi_{1}^{int}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\bm{m}

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega,

(48)

\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{int}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\partial\Omega

\displaystyle\phi_{1}^{ext}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\overline{\Omega}^{c},

whereas the second component
\phi_{1}
fulfills the Laplace equation

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi_{2}^{int}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega,

(49)

\displaystyle-\Delta\phi_{2}^{ext}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\overline{\Omega}^{c},

\displaystyle[\phi_{2}]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle\left[D_{\bm{n}}\phi_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}+D_{\bm{n}}\phi_{1}^{int}

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle\phi_{2}^{ext}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\|\bm{x}\|}\right)

\displaystyle\text{as}\;\|\bm{x}\|\rightarrow\infty.

It is easy to verify that a solution to (
48
) and (
49
) satisfies the jump conditions in (
30
) since
[\phi_{1}]=0
and
[D_{\bm{n}}\phi_{1}]=-D_{\bm{n}}\phi_{1}^{int}
.

The solution of (
49
) can be expressed with the
single layer potential

\phi_{2}^{ext}(\bm{x})=\phi_{\nu}(\bm{x};\phi_{1},\bm{m}):=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{(\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}-D_{\bm{n}}\phi_{1}^{int})(\bm{y})}{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|}\,\mathrm{d}s(\bm{y}).

(50)

Likewise, the vector potential can be split in
\bm{A}=\bm{A}_{1}+\bm{A}_{2}
with

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{A}_{1}^{int}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\times\bm{m}

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega,

(51)

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{1}^{int}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\partial\Omega

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{1}^{ext}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\overline{\Omega}^{c},

and

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{A}_{2}^{int}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\Omega,

(52)

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{A}_{2}^{ext}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{in}\;\overline{\Omega}^{c},

\displaystyle[\bm{A}_{2}]

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle\left[D_{\bm{n}}\bm{A}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=-\bm{m}\times\bm{n}+D_{\bm{n}}\bm{A}_{1}^{int}

\displaystyle\text{on}\;\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle(\bm{A}_{2}^{ext})_{j}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\|\bm{x}\|}\right),\;j=1,2,3,

\displaystyle\text{as}\;\|\bm{x}\|\rightarrow\infty.

The solution of
\bm{A}_{2}^{ext}
is then given by

\bm{A}_{2}^{ext}(\bm{x})=\bm{A}_{\nu}(\bm{x};\bm{A}_{1}^{int},\bm{m}):=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{(\bm{m}\times\bm{n}-D_{\bm{n}}\bm{A}_{1}^{int})(\bm{y})}{\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|}\,\mathrm{d}s(\bm{y}).

(53)

With the single layer potential, the solution of the exterior domain can be projected onto the boundary. Only the interior domain
\Omega
needs to be considered and therefore, from now on, we dismiss the superscript
int
and
ext
.

To compute a solution to the stray field, one could proceed as follows. First, compute a solution
\phi_{1}
to (
48
). Second, use the retrieved solution to compute
\phi_{2}
with (
50
) and third, compute
\bm{h}_{d}=-\nabla(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})
.
The computation of the stray field with a hard constrained ELM ansatz is summarized in Algorithm

.

4.4
Evaluation of the energy bounds on the finite domain

For our numerical experiments, we are interested in the evaluation of the lower energy bound (
36
) and the upper energy bound (
38
).
In principle, a truncation approach could be used to evaluate the respective whole space integrals, but this still requires a very big domain outside the magnet and further introduces a truncation error. In the previous section, we used the splitting ansatz of Garcia-Cervera and Roma to derive a solution to the whole space transmission problems (
30
) and (
32
). This splitting ansatz can also be used for the computation of the energy bounds without considering the external domain
\overline{\Omega}^{c}
.

Two important properties can be used to derive a version of the lower bound (
34
) on the finite domain:

Lemma 1

(
[
42
]
)
.

Let
\phi_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\phi_{2}\in\left(H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{loc}(\overline{\Omega}^{c})\right)
solving the Laplace equation. Then we have

(i) The gradients
\nabla\phi_{1}
and
\nabla\phi_{2}
are
L^{2}
-orthogonal, that is,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{1}\cdot\nabla\phi_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=0.

(55)

(ii) Let
\phi_{2}
be as in (
50
), then the
L^{2}
-norm of
\nabla\phi_{2}
can be expressed as a double surface integral

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\nabla\phi_{2}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}-D_{\bm{n}}\phi_{1})\phi_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s(\bm{x}).

(56)

\square

Using the splitting of
Garcia-Cervera and Roma

\phi=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}
with
\phi_{1}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
\phi_{2}=\phi_{\nu}(\bm{x};\,\phi_{1},\bm{m})
being the single layer potential (
50
), as well as property
(i)
and
(ii)
from Lemma
1
and applying it to the energy functional (
36
) yields

e_{\phi}(\phi_{1};\,\bm{m})=-\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\phi_{1}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+2\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot(\nabla\phi_{1}+\nabla\phi_{2})\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}-D_{\bm{n}}\phi_{1})\cdot\phi_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s(\bm{x}).

(57)

Note that
\phi_{1}
does not satisfy the Dirichlet Poisson problem (
48
) here, but is a free function.
The surface integral can be transformed into a volume integral using the divergence theorem, together with property
(i)
,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}-D_{\bm{n}}\phi_{1})\cdot\phi_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s(\bm{x})=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot\bm{m})\phi_{2}+\bm{m}\cdot\nabla\phi_{2}-(\Delta\phi_{1})\phi_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(58)

Lemma
1
can be restated for the vector potential, consequently, the upper bound (
38
), with
\bm{A}_{2}=\bm{A}_{\nu}(\bm{x};\,\bm{A}_{1},\bm{m})
as in (
53
), can be written as

\displaystyle e_{\bm{A}}(\bm{A}_{1};\bm{m})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\|\bm{m}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\bm{A}_{1}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}-2\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot\big(\nabla\times(\bm{A}_{1}+\bm{A}_{2})\big)\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(59)

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{m}\times\bm{n}-D_{\bm{n}}\bm{A}_{1})\cdot\bm{A}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s(\bm{x}).

Again, it is possible to write the surface integral as volume integral like

\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{m}\times\bm{n}-D_{\bm{n}}\bm{A}_{1})\cdot\bm{A}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s(\bm{x})=-\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\times\bm{m})\cdot\bm{A}_{2}-\bm{m}\cdot\nabla\times\bm{A}_{2}+(\Delta\bm{A}_{1})\cdot\bm{A}_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(60)

Especially for numerical integration of the single layer potential it is useful not to integrate over the surface, but the volume instead since singularities are easier to control.

Further, for the unique solution of (
48
),
\phi_{d}=\phi_{1}+\phi_{2}
defines the unique solution of (
30
). The field
-\nabla(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})
defines the stray field
\bm{h}_{d}
, where the energy
e_{d}(\bm{m})=-\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot\bm{h}_{d}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}
can also be expressed as
[
42
]

\displaystyle e_{d}(\bm{m})=\int_{\Omega}\|\nabla\phi_{1}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+\int_{\partial\Omega}(\bm{m}\cdot\bm{n}-D_{\bm{n}}\phi_{1})\cdot\phi_{2}\,\mathrm{d}s(\bm{y}).

(61)

5
Micromagnetic Energy Minimization

Micromagnetic energy minimization aims to minimize the Gibbs free energy
[
36
]
of the magnet
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
which is given in reduced units as

\displaystyle e=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}e_{d}+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}-\bm{m}\cdot\bm{h}_{ext}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}_{e_{zee}}+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}Q(1-(\bm{m}\cdot\bm{a})^{2})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}_{e_{a}}+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{A}_{ex}\|\nabla\bm{m}\|^{2}_{F}\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}_{e_{ex}},

(62)

with the four fundamental energy terms: magnetostatic energy
e_{d}
as in (
26
), Zeeman energy
e_{zee}
, anisotropy energy
e_{a}
and exchange energy
e_{ex}
. The reduced energy has units
[e]=$\mathrm{m}^{3}$
. The energy functional in reduced units is derived by dividing the Gibbs free energy with the energy density
K_{m}:=\mu_{0}M_{s}^{2}
(
[\mu_{0}M_{s}^{2}]=$\mathrm{J}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{3}$
). The quantity
\mu_{0}=4\pi${10}^{-7}\text{\,}\mathrm{T}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{A}$
is the vacuum permeability and
M_{s}
(in units of
\mathrm{A}\mathrm{/}\mathrm{m}
) is the saturation magnetization. Therefore, the reduced uniaxial anisotropy constant is denoted with
Q:=K_{u}/K_{m}
with
K_{u}
being the uniaxial anisotropy constant and
\bm{a}
is the easy axis of the magnet. Further,
\widetilde{A}_{ex}:=A_{ex}/K_{m}
is the reduced exchange stiffness constant. One can for instance apply
LaBonte’s method
to minimize the energy and perform a curvilinear steepest descent optimization with the projected gradient of the energy to find a local minimum
[
50
]
, or variants of nonlinear conjugate gradient methods
[
51
,
52
]
. For a nonlinear PINN ansatz, optimization is more intricate.

5.1
Optimization of the Gibbs free energy functional

Let the magnetization be parameterized by the model
\widetilde{\bm{m}}(\;\cdot\;;\bm{\theta})
, it is possible to use AD to compute the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}e
of the energy functional with respect to the model parameters
\bm{\theta}
and use (stochastic) gradient descent to update the model parameters. Also higher order methods can be applied. However, two major problems arise. The first problem is the satisfaction of the unit norm constraint,
\|\bm{m}\|=1
, which will be explained later on. The second one arises from the non-local interaction due to the stray field.

When approximating the integral in (
62
), the integrand needs to be computed pointwise. The stray field is a function of the magnetization,
\bm{h}_{d}=\bm{h}_{d}(\;\cdot\;;\widetilde{\bm{m}},\bm{\theta})
, thus, computing the self energy for instance with

e_{d}(\bm{\theta})=-\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{\bm{m}}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})\cdot\bm{h}_{d}(\bm{x};\widetilde{\bm{m}},\bm{\theta})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(63)

requires AD through the stray field solver. This can be done with ease with modern AD libraries but comes with a big computational burden. Using the splitting ansatz of Garcia-Cervera and Roma in Section
4.3
,
\bm{h}_{d}=-\nabla(\phi_{1}+\phi_{2})
, and for instance a PINN to compute the approximate solution to
\widetilde{\phi}_{1}
and the single layer potential (
50
)
\widetilde{\phi}_{2}=\phi_{\nu}(\;\cdot\;;\widetilde{\phi}_{1},\widetilde{\bm{m}})
makes AD very expensive and prohibitive. If
\widetilde{\phi}_{1}
is computed via an optimization process, it would require implicit AD
[
53
]
of the solver. Further, AD of the single layer potential requires AD of the integral operator, which is expensive for large domains or grain structure models. To our luck, it is not necessary to compute the gradient of the stray field with respect to the model parameters
\bm{\theta}
, since the stray field is a linear function of the magnetization,
\bm{h}_{d}(\,\cdot\,;\widetilde{\bm{m}},\bm{\theta})=\bm{D}(\,\cdot\,)\widetilde{\bm{m}}(\,\cdot\,;\bm{\theta})
, with
\bm{D}
being the constant demagnetization tensor which is only dependent on the geometry. Hence, the partial derivative of
e_{d}
with respect to some parameter of the model
\bm{\theta}_{i}
is given by

\frac{\partial e_{d}}{\partial\bm{\theta}_{i}}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\theta}_{i}}\int_{\Omega}\widetilde{\bm{m}}\cdot\bm{D}\widetilde{\bm{m}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=-2\,\int_{\Omega}\bm{h}_{d}\frac{\partial\widetilde{\bm{m}}}{\partial\bm{\theta}_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(64)

Using a first order optimization method such as Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), only the gradient is required to update the model parameters. Therefore, one can compute the stray field pointwise, before evaluating the gradient
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}(e_{d}+e_{zee}+e_{a}+e_{ex})
. Note that the factor
\frac{1}{2}
is missing before the self energy term.
This approach results in Algorithm
2
, where

q(\bm{x},\bm{h};\bm{\theta})=-\widetilde{\bm{m}}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})\cdot\bm{h}-\widetilde{\bm{m}}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})\cdot\bm{h}_{ext}(\bm{x})+Q(1-(\widetilde{\bm{m}}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})\cdot\bm{a})^{2})+\widetilde{A}_{ex}\|\nabla\widetilde{\bm{m}}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})\|_{F}^{2}

(65)

is the corresponding integrand of
e_{d}+e_{zee}+e_{a}+e_{ex}
, and
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is the pointwise stray field. All other terms of (

\bm{b}_{d}
. The algorithm can be formulated analogous.

5.3
Cayley transform

Another possibility to account for the unit norm constraint is the utilization of the Cayley-transform
[
56
]
. This assures that the model output is given on the Lie algebra of the
SO(3)
rotation group, and this rotation can be applied to some initial magnetization
\bm{m}_{0}

[
16
]
. Specifically, we consider the isomorphism between
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and the Lie algebra
so(3)
of
3\times 3
skew symmetric matrices

\bm{p}=\begin{pmatrix}p_{1}\\
p_{2}\\
p_{3}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\hat{\bm{p}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-p_{3}&p_{2}\\
p_{3}&0&-p_{1}\\
-p_{2}&p_{1}&0\end{pmatrix},

(67)

and use the Cayley transform

\mathrm{cay}(\bm{p})=(I-\hat{\bm{p}})^{-1}(I+\hat{\bm{p}})

(68)

to describe the rotation of the initial magnetization. For some input
\bm{x}\in\Omega
,
a neural network
\mathcal{N}:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3},(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})\mapsto\mathcal{N}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})
with parameters
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and some initial magnetization
\bm{m}_{0}
, the hard constraint model takes the form

\widetilde{\bm{m}}(\bm{x},\bm{\theta})=\mathrm{cay}\left(\mathcal{N}(\bm{x};\bm{\theta})\right)\bm{m}_{0}(\bm{x}).

(69)

This architecture allows energy minimization of (
62

2
without the need of any framework for constrained optimization.

5.4
Optimization of the energy bounds on the finite domain

Up to this point, only first order optimization method were considered. If using second order optimizers, not only the gradient but also the objective value is important, and they need to be consistent. This can be problematic for implementation of second order methods, since one need to consider the objective
e=\frac{1}{2}e_{d}+e_{zee}+e_{a}+e_{ex}
but with gradients given by
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}(e_{d}+e_{zee}+e_{a}+e_{ex})
, assuming the stray field is independent of the magnetization and the gradient of the self energy is given by (
64
). In a first version, it was thought that the objective
e_{d}+e_{zee}+e_{a}+e_{ex}
can replace
e
and AD of the stray field can simply be prevented on a code level with the
JAX
directive
stop_gradient
. However, this does not work for second order methods, since the gradient corresponds to
e
but the value does not. This issue can probably easily be resolved on a code level, but the study on energy bounds opened a new viewpoint. It turns out, that the minimization of
\bar{e}=e_{d}+e_{zee}+e_{a}+e_{ex}
, without the factor
\frac{1}{2}
for the self energy, corresponds to minimization of the lower bound
e_{\phi}
in (
36
) with respect to the magnetization. In essence, this means that one can consider the value of
\bar{e}
instead of
e
. Therefore, in this section, the joint optimization of
\bm{m}
and the energy bounds is explained and results in a sophisticated alternating minimization algorithm.

Equation (
57
) and (
59
) give a computable formulation of the energy bounds on the finite domain and could be tackled, for instance, with Monte Carlo integration. However, we were not able to use those bounds directly for energy minimization in a physics-informed setting. It would require AD over the single layer potential, and minimization of the energy functional with PINNs does not seem to be numerically sound.

When training a physics-informed model like a PINN, in essence, we are only interested in the updates of the model parameters.
Considering again the lower bound (
36
) of the self energy

\displaystyle e_{\phi}(\phi;\,\bm{m})=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\|\nabla\phi\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}+2\int_{\Omega}\bm{m}\cdot\nabla\phi\,\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

The goal is the maximization of this functional with respect to the scalar potential
\phi

\bm{m}
. Hence, this leads to a min-max problem.
Computing the variational derivative with a variation

The proposed energy minimization method can be applied for the computation of a hysteresis curve. The hysteresis depends on the history of previous magnetization states as a function of the external field.
Incorporating the external field as conditional parameter can therefore be difficult and might lead to an optimization problem which is not well-posed. Moreover, a simple continuous neural network model cannot approximate discontinuities well and the energy functional needs to be properly minimized for each external field. Therefore, a better choice of optimization might be the application of a full batch second order optimizer such as the

method and increase/decrease the external field step-by-step.

To minimize grain structure models, one could use a separate small scale PINN to model the magnetization for each grain. Further, and ELM or PINN for each grain would be required to model scalar or vector potential. The training for the scalar or vector potential model can be performed in parallel on many computational units. The same holds true for the magnetization. Only computation of the single layer potential (
50

6.5.2
L-BFGS Optimizer

Although the trust region framework works reliably for energy minimization, the high computational cost to solve the subproblem is problematic. Also, if the parameters are already close to the optimum, the method struggles to find a good optimum in the flat energy landscape, leading to slow convergence. Therefore, line search methods, especially L-BFGS
[
55
]
, seem to be more suitable for this task. The same test was performed with an L-BFGS optimizer and the same ELM ansatz for scalar and vector potential. While for the trust region method, the scalar and vector potential was evaluated every iteration,
N_{\bm{m}}=20
L-BFGS iterations are performed before re-evaluating the potential. Figure
8
shows the result of the computation. The switching occurred at
h_{c}=-2.772
for the scalar potential ansatz and at
h_{c}=-2.802
for the vector potential, while the computation took only about
90\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
. This shows that also a positive definite Hessian approximation with line search can capture the switching. However, occasionally, the switching might not be completed after a single optimization step, which can cause the optimizer to stall since the history does not match. The information saved in the history of the previous iterates are a very poor approximation of the Hessian. This requires a restart of the optimizer, which is also necessary after the switching is completed.
The trust region method seems to be the better choice to compute the switching field accurately, whereas L-BFGS outperforms the trust region method in terms of computation time. This suggests that both optimizers could be applied together. This could be done by switching to the trust region scheme close to the switching point. Another possibility would be the usage of a sampled version of L-BFGS as in
[
60
]
which should eliminate this issue. It might also be possible to intelligently sample the respective objective function during line search.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we have explored the application of machine learning techniques, such as Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) and Extreme Learning Machines (ELMs), to address the challenges of magnetostatics, particularly in the context of Gibbs free energy minimization.
We present an alternating optimization method for the joint optimization of Brown’s energy bounds and the Gibbs free energy. Our method enforces the unit norm constraint via the Cayley transform on the model, removing the need for an expensive penalty framework. Additionally, building on the work of Garcia-Cervera and Roma, we can reformulate the energy bounds on the finite domain, which allows for more efficient computation of magnetostatic fields without having to account for external space.

While machine learning methods like PINNs and ELMs provide promising alternatives to traditional methods, several challenges remain. This includes hyperparameter tuning and efficient optimization of PINN models. We find, that the enforcement of essential boundary conditions via
R
-functions can result in highly accurate models which can compete with traditional numerical methods. Optimization of hard constraint PINNs can be challenging. Hard constraint ELMs, on the other hand, offer an easy and straightforward solution process.
In addition, we introduce the kernel and neural operator perspective for these cases, opening up novel algorithmic directions and applications.

We tested these methods for the computation of the magnetostatic fields and the demagnetization curve for simple domains.
Future research focuses on refining these methods and apply them to more complex geometries, such as grain structure models. Further, DeepONets are of particular interest for the fast prediction of magnetostatic fields.
