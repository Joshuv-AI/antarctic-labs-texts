Air-trapping bulla in the azygoesophageal recess as a “sentinel bulla” causing secondary spontaneous pneumothorax: A case report
Authors: Shinya Ito
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Successful surgical management of spontaneous pneumothorax usually depends on identifying the pleural fistula that is causing the air leak. However, identifying the exact location of the air leak preoperatively is typically impossible. A peculiar thin-walled bulla located in the azygoesophageal recess can cause pneumothorax in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). A 61-year-old man with a history of COPD presented with exertional dyspnea. Chest radiography revealed a right pneumothorax. Upon reviewing previous chest computed tomography images, we ascertained that the air-trapping bulla in the azygoesophageal recess had grown consistently over the previous 2 years. Persistent air leakage was observed after chest tube drainage. Video-assisted thoracoscopy revealed a thin-walled bulla with a fistula in the azygoesophageal recess. We encountered an expanding bulla in the azygoesophageal recess that eventually caused spontaneous pneumothorax. Definitive management guidelines for secondary spontaneous pneumothorax caused by azygoesophageal recess bullae with unique features remain elusive.

Introduction

Successful surgical management of spontaneous pneumothorax relies on the identification of the pleural fistula responsible for the air leak. However, identifying the exact location of the air leak preoperatively is typically impossible. When considering surgery in patients with secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP) and persistent air leak, risk of postoperative pulmonary complications is a concern in patients with emphysema. Moreover, hyperinflated emphysematous lungs can hinder identification of the fistula location, despite careful intraoperative inspection. The ERS/EACTS/ESTS clinical practice guidelines on adults with spontaneous pneumothorax suggest that factors should be considered such as age, comorbidities, type of underlying lung disease, performance status, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score and degree of emphysema on computed tomography (CT) [1]. Chest CT can sometimes lead to effective treatment of SSP by enabling clinicians to identify bullae with high potential for rupture that may cause spontaneous pneumothorax. Preoperative identification of the causative bullae as target lesions which could cause air leaks would benefit clinicians in their diagnostic and treatment processes. These bullae would serve as “sentinel” indicators and provide valuable information to facilitate correct diagnosis and good surgical outcomes. Herein, we report a case in which an expanding bulla located in the azygoesophageal recess (AER) was considered a “sentinel” bulla for SSP.

Case presentation

A 61-year-old man with a 40-pack-year smoking history was admitted to our hospital's emergency department with exertional dyspnea. The patient had a history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) that was treated with inhaled umeclidinium/vilanterol. Chest radiography revealed recurrent right spontaneous pneumothorax. A 20-Fr chest tube was positioned with the tip near the lung apex under suction (−10cmH20). Because the air leak ceased only in the supine position, a pneumothorax-causing ruptured bulla in the AER was suspected and confirmed by chest CT (Fig. 1). A review of the patient's previous chest CT images revealed that the bulla in the AER had expanded consistently over the previous 2 years (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The patient also had a history of Stanford type A aortic dissection and had undergone surgeries for aortic repair (Fig. 3A: ascending aortic replacement, Fig. 3B: total arch replacement). Before the current admission, descending thoracic aortic replacement was scheduled in approximately 1 month (Fig. 3C). In order to repair the persistent air leak and to lower the risk of pneumothorax recurrence, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) was performed and confirmed a thin-walled whitish bulla with a fistula originating from the right S6 segment (Fig. 4a, b). The bulla was resected after closing its neck with a loop suture (ENDOLOOP® Ligature; Johnson & Johnson, NJ, USA) (Fig. 4c), then covered with a polyglycolic acid sheet plus fibrin glue to prevent the recurrence of the ipsilateral pneumothorax. Three months later, surgery for the descending thoracic aortic aneurysm was rescheduled and safely performed via left thoracotomy with one-lung ventilation. Discussion

Although new imaging modalities for detecting air leaks in patients with pneumothorax have been developed [[2], [3], [4]], thoracic CT remains effective in presuming pneumothorax origins. The origin of the pneumothorax is critical to thoracic surgeons; however, identifying the exact location of an air leak before surgery is usually impossible. A peculiar bulla located in the AER causing SSP has been reported previously [[5], [6], [7]]. A pneumothorax-causing bulla located in the AER of patients with COPD is described as an air-trapping bulla originating from the dorsal part of the S6 segment of the right lower lobe, herniating beyond the thoracic spine into the AER and expanding caudally along the vertebrae, causing a pneumothorax. Air trapping may have led to the development of the bulla, eventually causing SSP. AER can be regarded as a half-closed space situated deep inside the thorax near the posterior mediastinum. Deep AER is believed to contribute to bulla formation and rupture in patients with SSP [6]. Air leak ceases only in the supine position despite being persistent; however, neither chest tube malfunction nor malpositioning can be excluded as causative factors. Air may be prevented from passing through the long bulla in the hollow region of the AER toward the fistula (usually on the tip of the AER bulla) by overcoming mechanical compression from the surrounding structures (lung, left atrium, esophagus, thoracic spine, chest wall, azygos vein, descending aorta), and the fistula is temporally sealed in the supine position. This phenomenon, together with the existence of an AER bulla detected using chest CT, helped the thoracic surgeons identify the AER bulla as the origin site for pneumothorax preoperatively. We have previously demonstrated that VATS is effective in treating SSP caused by a ruptured AER bulla with favorable long-term outcomes and concluded that the development of this specific air-trapping bulla causes spontaneous pneumothorax in patients with emphysema [7]. An AER bulla is identified by careful examination of chest CT scans. Multiplanar CT may help detect this unique bulla in the AER. However, if the AER bulla is collapsed when a pneumothorax occurs, it can mimic partial atelectasis in the right lower lobe (as shown/described in Fig. 1b). As a result, the causative bulla may be easily missed, even with a thin-slice chest CT scan. A “sentinel” bulla in the AER can serve as a useful diagnostic indicator among patients with SSP and help identify the culprit site (the true origin of an air leak). Takahashi et al. reported a case of right pneumothorax with a ruptured AER bulla in a patient who underwent left pneumonectomy [8]. A retrospective review of CT images revealed a substantial growth in the AER bulla originating from the right lower lobe for over 9 years. The authors suggested that excessive local overinflation of the lung parenchyma at the AER associated with a mediastinal shift and rotation after pneumonectomy cause the formation of such bullae. In the present case, a review of the patient's previous chest CT images showed steady growth of the AER bulla. At 27 months, the diameter of the AER bulla in the axial and coronal sections had increased by 2- and 4-fold, respectively (Fig. 3).

Intrinsic positive end-expiratory pressure and dynamic pulmonary hyperinflation (DPH) have been observed in patients with COPD. DPH is caused by air being trapped within the lungs after each breath owing to disequilibrium between the inhaled and exhaled volumes [9]. Mechanical ventilation during previous cardiothoracic surgeries (ascending aortic replacement and total arch replacement) may affect the development of AER bullae. Dynamic hyperinflation in patients with emphysematous bullae can result in life-threatening conditions, such as hemodynamic collapse or contralateral pneumothorax during one-lung ventilation [10]. In the present patient, we successfully repaired the fistula in the AER bulla, and consequently prevented recurrence (occurrence of contralateral pneumothorax) during cardiothoracic surgery via left thoracotomy. The key factors for ensuring favorable outcomes when treating AER bulla are identifying the bulla and its root of origin, inflating the collapsed bulla sufficiently to identify the exact point of air leakage, closing the neck of the bulla with a ligature at the base, gentle lung-protective surgical maneuvers considering the emphysematous changes in the background, and a ventilation strategy to maintain a low airway pressure. Due to the lack of evidence, a recommendation for or against early surgical intervention for the initial treatment of SSP remains elusive [1]. The optimal management strategy for incidentally detected expanding AER bullae remains unknown. Identifying the greatest risk for pneumothorax is difficult because predicting the probability of the inflated AER bulla leading to rupture is impossible. Sentinel surveillance of this AER bulla, including the implementation of diameter measurements in routine follow-up CT, may be an option for identifying risk. In addition, the measurement of bullous pressure using esophageal manometry may be helpful, as the bullae are usually situated in front of the esophagus. Given that pneumothorax can be potentially life-threatening in patients with COPD, surgical intervention for AER bulla as early definitive management could be offered in selected cases, considering pre-existing comorbidities. Data collection and further detailed analysis of clinical information from daily clinical practice among patients with SSP with an air-trapping bulla in the AER as the causative site are needed to establish definitive management guidelines in the future.

Conclusion

Identifying bullae which could act as sentinel indicators of pneumothorax is important for preoperative planning in cases with SSP. Recognizing AER bullae in patients with COPD as a cause of SSP may guide precision surgery without introducing pleurodesis to reduce pneumothorax recurrence.
