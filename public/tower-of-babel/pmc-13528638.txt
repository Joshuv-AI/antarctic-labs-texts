Internal carotid artery anterior transposition for internal jugular vein decompression: illustrative case
Authors: Kamran Aghayev, Zachary Comella, Frank D. Vrionis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Internal jugular vein (IJV) compression is a novel entity with serious pathological implications. The J3 segment of the IJV can be compressed between the transverse process of the atlas posteriorly and the styloid process (SP) of the temporal bone anteriorly, creating a “jugular nutcracker” stenosis.1 This condition presents with symptoms including tinnitus, headaches, visual disturbances, head pressure sensation, and cognitive complaints.

The pathophysiology involves obstruction of cranial venous outflow, leading to elevated intracranial pressure and reduction in brain perfusion. Clinically, IJV compression is similar to dural venous sinus stenosis. However, in cases of IJV compression the presence of a collateral extrajugular network (EJN) mitigates the effect of stenosis and results in milder symptomatology.

Although the vast majority of J3-IJV compressions occur due to bony compression, the presence of the internal carotid artery (ICA) near the IJV presents a potential risk. Cervical ICAs are known to make loops, coils, or kinks. Previously, midneck IJV (J2-IJV) compression by a tortuous ICA has been reported,2 but no case of surgically treated arterial J3-IJV compression at the styloid-C1 level has been published. We believe that this case will highlight a previously unknown entity and emphasize the treatment option.

Illustrative Case

A 49-year-old male presented with a several-year history of throbbing headaches in association with pulsatile tinnitus. Associated symptoms included visual disturbances (blurred vision) and cognitive performance decline. The symptoms had progressively worsened over the years, significantly impairing quality of life and mental health. Neurological examination, including cranial nerve evaluation, revealed no deficits. The patient was previously diagnosed with right transverse sinus stenosis for which he underwent endovascular stent placement. The intervention failed to relieve symptoms, which prompted further investigations. CT angiography revealed bilateral compression of the IJVs at the C1 level by loops of the ICA (Figs. 1A and 2A). The posterior surfaces of the IJVs were flattened against the C1TPs, indicating that posterior compression might contribute to overall stenosis. The posterior cervical EJN was very prominent, indicating that flow in the jugular basin was severely compromised (Fig. 3A). Surgical Intervention

After detailed discussion with the patient, he opted for surgical exploration and decompression on both sides. A vertical 4-cm incision starting at the tip of the mastoid process and extending over the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid (SCM) muscle was made. After identifying and securing the great auricular nerve in the subcutaneous tissue, the SCM muscle was dissected and retracted posteriorly. The posterior belly of the digastric muscle was found, dissected, and resected to increase access. The ICA and IJV were identified after cautious release of the carotid sheath. To increase the mobility of the IJV, the C1TP was resected posterior to the vein. This maneuver greatly increased IJV mobility and allowed posterior retraction during ICA dissection. An ICA loop was found between the SP and IJV, as expected. The SP was skeletonized by stripping away the muscles (stylohyoid, styloglossus, and stylopharyngeus), and an empty space was created just anterior to the SP to accommodate the ICA loop. The ICA was dissected for several centimeters, which allowed unrestricted motion, and then the loop was transposed anterior to the SP into the preformed space. A single 4-0 silk suture was passed through the ICA’s posterior wall adventitia. This suture was tied to the SP above the tip, so that the posterior wall of the ICA was juxtaposed to the anterior surface of the SP. Eventually, a 1- to 2-cm gap was formed between the ICA and IJV, allowing complete release from mechanical compression (Fig. 4). The same procedure was performed on the opposite side. Postoperative Course and Outcome

The patient experienced substantial resolution of all presenting symptoms in the immediate postoperative period and sustained this improvement at follow-up (8 months). Postoperative neurological examination was normal, including cranial nerve functions. At the last follow-up, he remained symptom free with no residual complaints.

CT angiography was performed on postoperative day 1 using the same protocol (timing, contrast amount, and administration site) as that for preoperative imaging to avoid artificial discrepancies. It demonstrated complete decompression of the IJVs, which resumed a normal oval configuration (Figs. 1B and 2B). In addition, the extensive posterior cervical collateral network disappeared, indicating that flow had been redirected to the IJVs (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

Observations

The IJV, critical for drainage of the brain and face, can be obstructed at multiple anatomical levels. J3-IJV compression between the C1TP and SP is an increasingly recognized entity.3,4 The present case represents a variation of external IJV stenosis where the compressing factor is arterial rather than skeletal. Compression of IJVs by tortuous ICAs has been previously reported.2 Interestingly, both patients in that report presented with headaches, similar to our case. Tortuous carotid arteries can cause pharyngeal compression and induce dysphagia. The treatment alternatives are either transposition or shortening of the artery via resection of the redundant segments.5–7

The pathophysiology of IJV stenosis involves impaired venous return, leading to increased intracranial pressure, reduced CSF absorption, and compromised cerebral perfusion. These mechanisms explain the symptomatology observed in our patient: pulsatile tinnitus, headaches, visual disturbances, and head pressure are typical symptoms of patients with increased intracranial pressure. Notably, the patient had been diagnosed with transverse sinus stenosis and treated with venous stenting without benefit. We hypothesize that the transverse sinus stenosis was a secondary phenomenon, a consequence of elevated intracranial pressure from the primary IJV obstruction. Cranial venous sinus narrowing due to elevated intracranial pressure is a very well-known phenomenon. It arises from external compression of dural sinus walls due to elevated CSF compressive force. Secondary stenosis of cranial venous sinuses may further contribute to intracranial hypertension via a self-reinforcing cascade. The presence of upstream narrowing in IJV stenosis is somewhat counterintuitive. Generally, venous stenosis/obstruction leads to upstream dilation and engorgement. The classical example is engorgement of the upstream veins in the case of thrombosis. Therefore, upstream venous stenosis is a unique phenomenon specific only to the closed intracranial compartment. This case emphasizes the importance of further investigation when transverse sinus stenosis is diagnosed, as clinicians should investigate downstream IJV stenosis to avoid unnecessary invasive procedures.

IJV compression by ICA is analogous to May-Thurner syndrome (MTS). In MTS, the left common iliac vein undergoes chronic compression by the overlying right common iliac artery against the lumbar spine.8–10 Similarly, in our patient, the ICA was compressing the IJV against the C1TP. MTS is treated with either endovascular intervention (venous stenting) or open surgical approaches (arterial transection and posterior transposition). In our case, endovascular IJV stenting was considered but rejected because it could have compressed the ICA against the SP. Another alternative treatment strategy could be shortening and straightening of the ICA as was previously done with pharyngeal compression. Transposition does not require arteriotomy and anastomosis. Manipulating the ICA close to the skull base is radically different from midcervical exposure where the surgeon has much more space. The field is limited anteriorly by the mandible and parotid gland, posteriorly by the mastoid process, and superiorly by the skull base. Additionally, the proximity of lower cranial nerves (IX–XII) further complicates the task. Inadvertent injury to the ICA may lead to massive bleeding and/or stroke. Therefore, transposition appears to be the safest procedure.

The choice of anchorage method seems to be a significant issue. Currently, there are no specific devices available for external carotid artery anchorage. Thus, the most obvious choice was the use of a nonresorbable suture tied to the tunica externa (adventitia) of the vascular wall. The adventitia is quite strong in extracranial arteries (unlike intracranial ones) and provides a sturdy fixation point. The suture does not pass through the lumen and is not exposed to flowing blood. The possibility of a thrombus and/or pseudoaneurysm formation is therefore eliminated. We chose silk suture for several reasons. First, it is very soft and pliable, allowing for easy manipulation and placement of the knot exactly as intended. Second, its woven structure and hydrophilic feature make knot slippage much less likely, which is essential for keeping the ICA in the intended position. Postoperative fibrosis, which inevitably fills the surgical cavity and newly created caroticojugular gap, is an additional barrier. Therefore, the silk anchor should work only for approximately 3 months, after which fibrotic tissue alone is strong enough to keep the vessels apart.

One of the hallmarks of IJV stenosis at the C1 level is the presence of a compensatory EJN. The EJN is a physiological high-resistance pathway for venous outflow in contrast to the low-resistance pathway provided by the IJVs. Physiologically, the IJVs collapse in the upright position and most of the blood flow is directed to the EJN. Although the EJN is a high-resistance pathway, the presence of gravitational pull in the erect position provides sufficient flow. In the supine position, the gravitational pull is absent, and flow is provided by low-resistance IJVs. This mechanism prevents overdrainage through the IJVs in the erect position in the presence of gravitational pull. The presence of a markedly engorged EJN in our patient on CT angiography (obtained in the supine position) is indirect evidence of IJV flow compromise. Interestingly, the collateral network flow nearly completely vanished immediately after IJV decompression. This phenomenon is also evident in patients undergoing IJV decompression due to atlantostyloid syndrome (personal experience, unpublished data). We believe that the presence or absence of the posterior cervical collateral network can be used as a marker of IJV stenosis and efficacy of decompression.

Lessons

This case demonstrates that anterior transposition of the ICA with fixation to the SP is a feasible and effective surgical approach for symptomatic IJV compression caused by arterial impingement at the C1-styloid level.

While this paper represents a single case and a larger experience is needed, the successful outcome suggests that arterial transposition should be considered as a therapeutic option in carefully selected patients with ICA-mediated IJV compression. This case also highlights the importance of recognizing secondary dural venous sinus narrowing as a potential consequence of primary IJV stenosis, and the need to address the underlying mechanical problem rather than treating upstream sequelae.

Another innovative aspect of this paper is the demonstration of a vast suboccipital venous collateral network. This network vanished on postoperative CT angiography and can be used as a marker of IJV stenosis and decompression.
