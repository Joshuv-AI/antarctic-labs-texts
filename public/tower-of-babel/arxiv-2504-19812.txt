Hyper-differential sensitivity analysis with respect to model discrepancy: Prior Distributions
Bart van Bloemen Waanders, Jixian Li, Timbwaoga A. J. Ouermi, Chris R. Johnson

Abstract
Hyper-differential sensitivity analysis with respect to model discrepancy was recently developed to enable uncertainty quantification for optimization problems. The approach consists of two primary steps: (i) Bayesian calibration of the discrepancy between high- and low-fidelity models, and (ii) propagating the model discrepancy uncertainty through the optimization problem. When high-fidelity model evaluations are limited, as is common in practice, the prior discrepancy distribution plays a crucial role in the uncertainty analysis. However, specification of this prior is challenging due to its mathematical complexity and many hyper-parameters. This article presents a novel approach to specify the prior distribution. Our approach consists of two parts: (1) an algorithmic initialization of the prior hyper-parameters that uses existing data to initialize a hyper-parameter estimate, and (2) a visualization framework to systematically explore properties of the prior and guide tuning of the hyper-parameters to ensure that the prior captures the appropriate range of uncertainty. We provide detailed mathematical analysis and a collection of numerical examples that elucidate properties of the prior that are crucial to ensure uncertainty quantification.

Introduction

Optimization constrained by computational models, such as partial differential equations, arises in a wide range of science and engineering applications. Although numerical optimization has a rich theoretical and algorithmic foundation, many problems remain unsolved due to the complexity of the computational models involved. For instance, state-of-the-art optimization algorithms require derivative information to efficiently traverse high-dimensional spaces. However, many complex computational models are developed with a focus on forward prediction and consequently fail to provide access to derivatives. Furthermore, derivative-based optimization algorithms still require many model evaluations to converge, which is prohibitive when each evaluation requires significant computational resources. Consequently, a common approach to enable computationally tractable optimization is to work with a lower-fidelity model that can provide derivative information and be evaluated more efficiently.

Hyper-differential sensitivity analysis with respect to model discrepancy was first developed in
[
1
]
to leverage a small number of high-fidelity model evaluations (without derivatives) to enhance an optimization solution computed using a low-fidelity model. Because high-fidelity model evaluations are limited,
[
2
]
extended the approach to facilitate uncertainty quantification through a Bayesian approach. Specifically, the model discrepancy, defined as the difference in the high- and low-fidelity models, is estimated using Bayesian calibration. The resulting posterior distribution is propagated through the optimization problem to characterize the most likely optimization solutions given the high-fidelity model evaluations. This framework leverages tools from numerical linear algebra to achieve computational scalability for high-dimensional optimization problems involving nonlinear partial differential equations; the computational complexity scales with rank rather than the native dimension of the problem.

The aforementioned works provide a computational framework to enable uncertainty quantification of the optimization solution. However, because the number of high-fidelity model evaluations are frequently limited, the model discrepancy calibration is ill-posed and consequently the quality of the uncertainty estimate depends heavily on the prior. Specifying this prior is difficult because the mathematical object being calibrated is an operator that maps from the optimization variable space to the state variable space. Moreover, the prior depends on many hyper-parameters, involving both the state and optimization variables, which require considerable expertise and manual tuning to determine appropriate values. This article focuses on specification of the prior discrepancy distribution and provides a pragmatic framework to facilitate analysis for complex systems.

Our contributions include:

\bullet

Proving that the prior discrepancy distribution is well-defined in the discretization refinement limit.

\bullet

Developing an algorithmic approach to initialize the prior hyper-parameters and thus reduce the difficultly of manual tuning of the prior.

\bullet

Proposing a visualization framework to facilitate analysis of the prior and guide any hyper-parameter modifications required after the algorithmic initialization.

\bullet

Demonstrating properties of the prior and providing insight through a hierarchy of numerical examples.

We consider optimization problems of the form

\displaystyle\min_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}J(S(z),z)

(2.1)

where

{\mathcal{Z}}

Solving (

2.1
Post-optimality sensitivity with respect to model discrepancy

To represent model discrepancy, the difference between
S({\bm{z}})
and
\tilde{S}({\bm{z}})
, in the optimization problem, we consider

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{z}}}\hskip 2.84526pt\mathcal{J}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}}):=J(\tilde{S}({\bm{z}})+{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{z}}),

(2.3)

where
{\delta}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{z}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}}
is an operator that we parameterize by
{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
and calibrate so that
{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}})\approx S({\bm{z}})-\tilde{S}({\bm{z}})
. To relate (
2.3
) to the low-fidelity optimization problem, we parameterize
{\delta}
so that
{\delta}({\bm{z}},\bm{0})=\bm{0}

\forall{\bm{z}}
. To facilitate our subsequent derivations, assume that
\mathcal{J}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}})
is twice continuously differentiable with respect to
({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}})
. Let
{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}
be a local minimizer of the low-fidelity optimization problem, which satisfies the first order optimality condition

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{z}}\mathcal{J}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},\bm{0})=\bm{0}.

(2.4)

Applying the Implicit Function Theorem to (
2.4
) implies that there exists an operator
{\bm{F}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{z}}
such that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{z}}\mathcal{J}({\bm{F}}({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{\theta}})=\bm{0}\qquad\forall{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{0}),

where
\mathcal{N}(\bm{0})
denotes a neighborhood of
\bm{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
. Assuming that the Hessian
\nabla_{{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}}\mathcal{J}({\bm{F}}({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{\theta}})
is positive definite
\forall{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathcal{N}(\bm{0})
, we can interpret
{\bm{F}}
as a mapping from the model discrepancy to the optimal solution. Furthermore, the Implicit Function Theorem provides the Jacobian of
{\bm{F}}
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{F}}(\bm{0})=-{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{B}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{z}\times n_{\theta}},

(2.5)

where
{\bm{H}}=\nabla_{{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}}\mathcal{J}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},\bm{0})
and
{\bm{B}}=\nabla_{{\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}}}\mathcal{J}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},\bm{0})
are second order derivatives of
\mathcal{J}
, evaluated at
({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},\bm{0})
. We refer to
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{F}}
as the post-optimality sensitivity operator, which has been the subject of different studies
[
30
,
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
.

{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}
has been computed, that
S
and
\tilde{S}
have been evaluated
N

\{{\bm{z}}_{\ell},S({\bm{z}}_{\ell})-\tilde{S}({\bm{z}}_{\ell})\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
, and that
{\bm{z}}_{1}={\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}

{\bm{z}}_{1}={\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}

. Hyper-differential sensitivity analysis with respect to model discrepancy is summarized as the two-step procedure:

(discrepancy calibration) determine

Since the number of high-fidelity model evaluations

’s such that the calibrated

matches the data. To incorporate prior information, and quantify uncertainty due to the data limitations, we pose the calibration step in a Bayesian framework. The result is a probabilistic description of

2

2.2
Model discrepancy parameterization

As a prerequisite to analyzing the prior distribution of
{\bm{\theta}}
, we recall the form of the discrepancy parameterization. Observe that the post-optimality sensitivity operator (
2.5
) depends on
{\bm{H}}=\nabla_{{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}}\mathcal{J}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},\bm{0})
and
{\bm{B}}=\nabla_{{\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}}}\mathcal{J}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},\bm{0})
. Since we assumed that
{\delta}({\bm{z}},\bm{0})=\bm{0}

\forall{\bm{z}}
,
{\bm{H}}
does not depend on the parameterization of
{\delta}
. Furthermore, we see that
{\bm{B}}
depends on the mixed
{\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}}
second derivative of
{\delta}
. Hence, it is sufficient to consider a parameterization that is bilinear in
({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}})
, or equivalently, an affine function of
{\bm{z}}
with coefficients parameterized by
{\bm{\theta}}
. Starting from a generic affine operator mapping between the Hilbert spaces
{\mathcal{Z}}_{h}
and
{\mathcal{U}}_{h}
, we arrive at the coordinate representation

\displaystyle{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}})={\bm{u}}_{0}({\bm{\theta}})+\mathbf{L}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{z}},

with

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}_{0}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{\theta}}_{0},\qquad\mathbf{L}({\bm{\theta}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bm{\theta}}_{1}^{T}\\
{\bm{\theta}}_{2}^{T}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{\theta}}_{n_{u}}^{T}\end{array}\right),\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bm{\theta}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}{\bm{\theta}}_{0}\\
{\bm{\theta}}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
{\bm{\theta}}_{n_{u}}\end{array}\right),

where
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}}
and
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{z}}
,
i=1,2,\dots,n_{u}
. We can equivalently express
{\delta}
as

\displaystyle{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bm{I}}_{n_{u}}&{\bm{I}}_{n_{u}}\otimes{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\end{array}\right){\bm{\theta}}

where
{\bm{I}}_{n_{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}\times n_{u}}
is the identity matrix. This Kronecker product representation (
2.2
) is crucial to enable computationally scalable linear algebra as it allows computation in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
to be done via computation in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{z}}

To complete our model discrepancy parameterization, we define an inner product on the affine operator space as the sum of a state space inner product on
{\bm{u}}_{0}({\bm{\theta}})
and a linear operator space inner product on
{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}
. This leads to endowing
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
with a
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{\theta}}

as

\displaystyle({\bm{\theta}},\bm{\vartheta})_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{\theta}}}=({\bm{u}}_{0}({\bm{\theta}}),{\bm{u}}_{0}(\bm{\vartheta}))_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}+(\mathbf{L}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}},\mathbf{L}(\bm{\vartheta}){\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}})_{\mathcal{L}({\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}},{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}})},

where the notation
({\bm{u}}_{1},{\bm{u}}_{2})_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}={\bm{u}}_{2}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}_{1}
is the
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}
weighted inner product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}}
and
({\bm{A}}_{1},{\bm{A}}_{2})_{\mathcal{L}({\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}},{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}})}=\text{Tr}_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}}({\bm{A}}_{2}^{*}{\bm{A}}_{1})=\text{Tr}_{2}({\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{A}}_{2}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{A}}_{1})
is the Schatten 2-norm on the space of linear operators whose domain and range inner products are weighted by
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}
and
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}
, respectively.
{\bm{A}}^{*}
denotes the adjoint of
{\bm{A}}
,
\text{Tr}_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}}
and
\text{Tr}_{2}
denotes the matrix trace in the
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}
and
\ell^{2}
inner products, respectively.

It follows that the inner product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
is defined by

({\bm{\theta}},\bm{\vartheta})_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{\theta}}}=\bm{\vartheta}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{\theta}}
, where

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}_{\bm{\theta}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}&\bm{0}\\

3
Bayesian discrepancy calibration

The discrepancy calibration step seeks to find
{\bm{\theta}}
such that
{\delta}({\bm{z}}_{\ell},{\bm{\theta}})\approx S({\bm{z}}_{\ell})-\tilde{S}({\bm{z}}_{\ell})
,
\ell=1,2,\dots,N
. For problems with large
n_{z}\geq N
, there exist many
{\bm{\theta}}
’s that interpolate the data, as stated in Theorem
3.1
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Given
N<n_{z}+1
data pairs
\{{\bm{z}}_{\ell},\bm{d}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
, where
\{{\bm{z}}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
are linearly independent, there is a vector
{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
and a subspace
\Theta_{\text{interp}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
of dimension
(n_{z}+1-N)n_{u}
such that
{\delta}({\bm{z}}_{\ell},{\bm{\theta}}^{\star}+{\bm{\theta}})=\bm{d}_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,2,\dots,N
, for any
{\bm{\theta}}\in\Theta_{\text{interp}}
.

A proof is given in
A
.

Theorem
3.1
is an unsurprising result that highlights the necessity of Bayesian calibration to weight the many potential parameters using prior knowledge. To illustrate Theorem
3.1
, consider an example where
S,\tilde{S}:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
generates data
\bm{d}_{1}=S({\bm{z}}_{1})-\tilde{S}({\bm{z}}_{1})=1
and
\bm{d}_{2}=S({\bm{z}}_{2})-\tilde{S}({\bm{z}}_{2})=2
for inputs
{\bm{z}}_{1}=(0,0)
and
{\bm{z}}_{2}=(1,0)
. Then

\displaystyle{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}})=\theta_{0}+\theta_{1}z_{1}+\theta_{2}z_{2},

with parameters
{\bm{\theta}}=(1,1,\theta_{2})
, interpolates the data
\{{\bm{z}}_{\ell},\bm{d}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{2}
for any
\theta_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The problem is that interpolation does not constrain the variation of
{\delta}
in directions that are not informed by the data. Specifically, the derivative of
{\delta}
in the direction
z_{2}
is uniformed and hence may be arbitrarily large or small. The prior is therefore critically important to inform the range of variation. Characteristic scales of the problem will be encoded in prior hyper-parameters, which will be analyzed in Section
4
where we provide a detailed approach to hyper-parameter specification.

3.1
Noise model

The noise model in Bayesian inverse problems is typically specified based on characteristics of the data collection process. For instance, the noise in observations may be characterized by a known probability distribution. However, in our context, the data is simulation output. Although we can fit the data perfectly (see Theorem
3.1
), we are fitting an affine operator
{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}})
to approximate
S({\bm{z}})-\tilde{S}({\bm{z}})
, which is a nonlinear operator, in general. The noise model reflects our belief about how well the affine map should fit the data. To enable a closed-form expression for posterior samples, we assume an additive Gaussian noise model with mean
\bm{0}
and covariance
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{d}}^{-1}=\alpha_{\bm{d}}{\bm{I}}_{N}\otimes{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}
, where
\alpha_{\bm{d}}>0
. In other words, the inverse of the noise covariance defines a data misfit norm in the state space. We interpret
\alpha_{\bm{d}}
as a threshold for how well the calibrated discrepancy should fit the data. As a default, we suggest taking
\alpha_{\bm{d}}=(0.001C_{\delta})^{2}
, where
C_{\delta}\in(0,\infty)
is defined as the average magnitude of the discrepancy data. This can be interpreted as a noise standard deviation that is
0.1\%
of the average data magnitude, thus ensuring that we fit the data well.

3.2
Prior distribution

Bayesian priors on function spaces has been the topic of considerable research
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
. However, development for priors defined on spaces of operators is less mature and has only received recent attention with the rise of operator learning
[
38
]
.
To leverage existing theory on the specification of function space priors, and to achieve computational efficiency via closed-form expressions for prior and posterior samples, we assume a Gaussian prior for
{\bm{\theta}}
. To simplify the exposition, we assume that the data is centered by computing the average magnitude of the discrepancy data
\{\bm{d}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
, denote it as
\overline{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and subtracting
\overline{d}
element wise from each
\bm{d}_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,2,\dots,N
. We abuse notation and use
\{\bm{d}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
to denote the shifted data.

Since the discrepancy data is centered, we assume a Gaussian prior for
{\bm{\theta}}
with mean zero and covariance matrix
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}\ \in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}\times n_{\theta}}
, which we will specify next. Recall that a mean zero Gaussian random vector has a probability density function proportional to
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\theta}}^{T}{\bm{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{\theta}}\right)
. Hence, the inverse covariance matrix, called the precision matrix, defines a weighted inner product such that small values of
{\bm{\theta}}^{T}{\bm{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{\theta}}
implies high probability. From this perspective, the precision matrix induces a norm penalizing deviation from prior belief.

To define
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}
, we introduce mean-zero Gaussian priors for the state and optimization variables, whose covariances are denoted as
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}\times n_{u}}
and
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{z}\times n_{z}}
, respectively. We will discuss the specification and interpretation of
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}
and
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}
later. To define a precision matrix for
{\bm{\theta}}
, we consider that
{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}})
is an affine operator and hence is characterized in terms of a point evaluation and slope (the linear operator
{\bm{L}}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}
). Since the post-optimality sensitivity analysis is local about
{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}
, we localize the weighted norm via

\displaystyle||{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}})||_{{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}+||\mathbf{L}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}||_{\mathcal{L}({\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}},{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}})}^{2},

(3.1)

where the Schatten 2-norm of
\mathbf{L}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}
has its domain and range weighted by
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}
and
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}
, respectively. The range’s inner product arises naturally as the precision matrix for the state space prior. The domain’s inner product may seem unintuitive. This choice of inner product ensures that prior discrepancy samples have adequate smoothness properties, which is necessary to ensure that the prior is well defined in the mesh refinement limit.

Thanks to our choices of the model discrepancy parameterization and inner products, we arrive at the expression

\displaystyle||{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}})||_{{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}+||\mathbf{L}({\bm{\theta}}){\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}||_{\mathcal{L}({\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}},{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}})}^{2}={\bm{\theta}}^{T}{\bm{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{\theta}},

where

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}&{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}\otimes{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\\
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}\otimes{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}&{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}\otimes({\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}+{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}})\end{array}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}\times n_{\theta}}.

The Kronecker products in (
3.2
) enable computation whose complexity scales like
\mathcal{O}(n_{u}+n_{z})
rather than
\mathcal{O}(n_{u}n_{z})
.

It follows that the covariance matrix is given by

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(1+{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}){\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}&{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\otimes-{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}\\
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\otimes-{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}&{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\otimes{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}\end{array}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}\times n_{\theta}}.

3.3
Trace class covariance operators

Since
{\mathcal{U}}
is infinite-dimensional and is discretized with the finite-dimensional subspace
{\mathcal{U}}_{h}
,
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}
should be defined as the discretization of a trace class covariance operator defined on
{\mathcal{U}}
. That is, in the mesh refinement limit,
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}
converges to a trace class covariance operator, and consequently,
\text{Tr}_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}({\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1})
, the
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}
weighted trace, converges to a real number. Similarly, when
{\mathcal{Z}}
is infinite-dimensional,
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}
should be the discretization of a trace class covariance operator so that
\text{Tr}_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}}({\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1})
converges to a real number in the limit as the discretization is refined. In practice, such trace class covariance operators are typically defined as the squared inverse of Laplacian-like differential operators
[
37
]
. To this end, let
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}\times n_{u}}
and
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{z}\times n_{z}}
denote the discretization of Laplacian-like differential operators and define the covariances

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}=\alpha_{\bm{u}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}=\alpha_{\bm{z}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1},

(3.6)

where
\alpha_{\bm{u}},\alpha_{\bm{z}}>0
are variance hyper-parameters. Defining the covariances in this way ensures that they are trace class and that samples may be computed efficiently. Theorem
3.2
demonstrates that
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}
is trace class in the discretization refinement limit, i.e., it is the discretization of a trace class covariance operator.

Theorem 3.2
.

If

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{h}\to{\mathcal{U}}}\text{Tr}_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}({\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim\limits_{{\mathcal{Z}}_{h}\to{\mathcal{Z}}}\text{Tr}_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}}({\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1})

exist and are finite, where the limit
{\mathcal{U}}_{h}\to{\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{Z}}_{h}\to{\mathcal{Z}}
indicates refinement of the discretization, and

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{h}\to{\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{Z}}_{h}\to{\mathcal{Z}}}{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}=\tilde{z}\in{\mathcal{Z}},

that is, the optimization solution has a well defined discretization refinement limit, then

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{{\mathcal{U}}_{h}\to{\mathcal{U}},{\mathcal{Z}}_{h}\to{\mathcal{Z}}}\text{Tr}_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1})

exists and is finite.

A proof is given in
B
.

The specific form of the operators
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}
and
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}
depends on the problem, most notably, if
{\bm{u}}
and
{\bm{z}}
are stationary or transient. We consider the definition of
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}
for these two scenarios and note that
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}
may be defined in an analogous fashion. If
{\mathcal{Z}}
is finite-dimensional, then
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}={\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}
is a reasonable default which leads to
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}=\alpha_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}
. We focus on a scalar-valued state noting that analogous derivations may be done component-wise for a vector-valued state.

Stationary problems

For stationary problems, the state space
{\mathcal{U}}
contains functions
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{s}
,
s=1,2,
or
3
. In this case, a Laplacian-like operator is given by
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}=\beta_{\bm{u}}{\bm{K}}_{\bm{u}}+{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}
, where
\beta_{\bm{u}}>0
and
{\bm{K}}_{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}\times n_{u}}
is the stiffness matrix
2
2

2

The
(i,j)
entry of the stiffness matrix given by the inner product of the gradients of the
i^{th}
and
j^{th}
basis functions.
. This corresponds to the discretization of the elliptic operator
-\beta_{\bm{u}}\Delta+I
, where
\Delta
and
I
are the Laplacian and identity operators, respectively.

Transient problems

For transient problems, the state space
{\mathcal{U}}
contains functions
f:\Omega\times[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{s}
,
s=1,2,
or
3
, and
T>0
is the final time. Assume that time is discretized at
n_{t}
nodes
0=t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots<t_{n_{t}}=T
and that space is discretized with
n_{s}
basis functions. Consequently, the state coordinate vector is
{\bm{u}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}}
,
n_{u}=n_{t}n_{s}
, with an ordering
{\bm{u}}=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{u}}_{1}^{T}&{\bm{u}}_{2}^{T}&\cdots&{\bm{u}}_{n_{t}}^{T}\end{pmatrix}^{T}
, where
{\bm{u}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{s}}
,
i=1,2,\dots,n_{t}
, are the spatial coordinates at each time step. It follows that the discretized inner product is weighted by
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}={\bm{M}}_{t}\otimes{\bm{M}}_{s}
, where
{\bm{M}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t}\times n_{t}}
is the mass matrix corresponding to piecewise linear basis functions (i.e., hat functions) defined on the time nodes and
{\bm{M}}_{s}
is the mass matrix corresponding to the spatial basis functions defined on
\Omega
.

The state covariance matrix is defined by
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}=\alpha_{\bm{u}}{\bm{W}}_{t}^{-1}\otimes{\bm{W}}_{s}^{-1}
, where
{\bm{W}}_{t}^{-1}
and
{\bm{W}}_{s}^{-1}
are covariance matrices corresponding to the temporal and spatial domains, respectively. As in the stationary case, we model
{\bm{W}}_{s}^{-1}={\bm{E}}_{s}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{s}{\bm{E}}_{s}^{-1}
, where
{\bm{E}}_{s}=\beta_{\bm{u}}{\bm{K}}_{s}+{\bm{M}}_{s}
,
{\bm{K}}_{s}
is the stiffness matrix corresponding to the spatial discretization. To define
{\bm{W}}_{t}^{-1}
, we consider the elliptic operator
{\bm{E}}_{t}=\beta_{t}{\bm{K}}_{t}+{\bm{M}}_{t}
on the temporal domain, where
\beta_{t}>0
and
{\bm{K}}_{t}
is the stiffness matrix corresponding to the time discretization. We defined
{\bm{W}}_{s}^{-1}
as square of
{\bm{E}}_{s}^{-1}
to ensure that the covariance is trace class for spatial dimensions
s=2
and
s=3
. However, squaring is not required in the temporal domain as
{\bm{E}}_{t}^{-1}
is a trace class operator since it is defined on an interval
[
37
]
. In many applications, the model discrepancy is small, or identically zero, at time

, and its magnitude increases as a function of time due to the compounding of errors. To model this, we consider a time dependent variance weighting
\bm{\alpha}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t}}
and define
{\bm{W}}_{t}^{-1}=\mathbf{D}(\bm{\alpha}_{t})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{E}}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{D}(\bm{\alpha}_{t})^{\frac{1}{2}}
, where
\mathbf{D}(\bm{\alpha}_{t})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t}\times n_{t}}
is the diagonal matrix whose diagonal is
\bm{\alpha}_{t}
. This provides the modeling flexibility to capture temporal trends in the discrepancy while preserving the computational efficiency of the space-time Kronecker product structure and elliptic solves. Note that the space-time Kronecker product
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}
can still be written in the form of (
3.6
) with
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}
corresponding to a Kronecker product of time and space operators.

3.4
Computing prior discrepancy samples

We seek to determine
{\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}\times n_{\theta}}
such that
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}={\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{T}
, and compute prior samples as
{\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}}
, where
\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
is sampled from a standard normal distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
. To this end, let
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}={\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}^{T}
and
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}={\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}^{T}
be factorizations and assume that matrix-vector products may be computed with
{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}
and
{\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}
. For instance, matrix-vector products with
{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}=\sqrt{\alpha_{\bm{u}}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
may be computed using Krylov subspace methods that require matrix-vector products
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{v}}
and
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{v}}
, respectively. It follows that we may decompose
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{-1}={\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}^{T}
with

\displaystyle{\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}&-{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}\otimes{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}\\
\bm{0}&{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}\otimes{\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}\end{array}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}\times n_{\theta}}.

To analyze samples from the prior discrepancy, let
\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
be sampled from a standard normal distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
and consider

\displaystyle{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bm{I}}_{n_{u}}&{\bm{I}}_{n_{u}}\otimes{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\end{array}\right){\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}&{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}\otimes({\bm{z}}-{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}})^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}\end{array}\right)\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle={\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}_{0}(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})+{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{L}}(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}}){\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}({\bm{z}}-{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}),

where
{\bm{u}}_{0}
and
{\bm{L}}
are defined in (
2.2

{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}=\sqrt{\alpha_{\bm{u}}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
{\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}=\sqrt{\alpha_{\bm{z}}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and exploiting properties of Gaussian distributions, we can equivalently express the samples as

\displaystyle{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})=\sqrt{\alpha_{\bm{u}}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{u}}^{(1)}+\sqrt{\alpha_{\bm{u}}}\sqrt{\alpha_{\bm{z}}}\sqrt{s_{\bm{z}}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{u}}^{(2)}

(3.10)

where
s_{\bm{z}}=({\bm{z}}-{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}})^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}({\bm{z}}-{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}})
and
\bm{\omega}_{\bm{u}}^{(1)},\bm{\omega}_{\bm{u}}^{(2)}
are independent samples from a standard normal distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{u}}
. In what follows, we use (

4
hyper-parameter specification

There are four prior hyper-parameters,
\alpha_{\bm{u}},\beta_{\bm{u}},\alpha_{\bm{z}}
, and
\beta_{\bm{z}}
, for stationary problems, and two additional hyper-parameters,
\bm{\alpha}_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
, for transient problems. If
Z
is finite-dimensional, we can assume
\beta_{\bm{z}}=0
. For generality, we will assume that
Z
is infinite-dimensional and include
\beta_{\bm{z}}
in our subsequent analysis. Although these hyper-parameters have physical interpretations to guide their specification, determining an appropriate value for them is nontrivial since they are embedded in blocks of a larger covariance that is defined on the space of operators. In this section, we present an approach that uses the data
\{{\bm{z}}_{\ell},\bm{d}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
to initialize the hyper-parameters and introduce a prior sample visualization framework. Visualization of the prior discrepancy is crucial to verify the hyper-parameter values generated by the algorithm-based initialization and adjust them as necessary. We suggest this algorithm-based initialization and prior sample visualization approach rather than a hierarchical Bayesian approach because the calibration data is limited.

4.1
Smoothness hyper-parameters

The hyper-parameters,
\beta_{\bm{z}},\beta_{\bm{u}}
, and
\beta_{t}
(for transient problems) scale the Laplacian in the optimization variable and state prior covariances, respectively, and consequently determine the smoothness of samples from the prior discrepancy. Since we are assuming a Gaussian prior, these smoothness hyper-parameters are related to the correlation length, defined as the distance at which we observe an empirical correlation of
0.1
in a Gaussian random field
[
39
,
40
]
. The values of
\beta_{\bm{u}}
and
\beta_{t}
should be specified to reflect the smoothness characteristics of the
S({\bm{z}})-\tilde{S}({\bm{z}})
. In some applications,
\beta_{\bm{u}}
and
\beta_{t}
may be selected based on physical knowledge such as knowing the length scales that are resolved by the high- and low-fidelity models. However, this information is already present in
\{\bm{d}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
. Accordingly, we estimate the spatial and temporal correlation lengths of
\bm{d}_{\ell}=S({\bm{z}}_{\ell})-\tilde{S}({\bm{z}}_{\ell})
,
\ell=1,2,\dots,N
, as described in
E
. For stationary problems,
\beta_{t}
is ignored. For transient problems, we separate
\{\bm{d}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
into space and time components to calculate the spatial and temporal correlation lengths,
\kappa_{\bm{u}}
and
\kappa_{t}
, separately. Specifically, each time snapshot is considered separately to compute
\kappa_{\bm{u}}^{i}
,
i=1,2,\dots,n_{t}N
, and define
\kappa_{\bm{u}}=\frac{1}{n_{t}N}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{t}N}\kappa_{\bm{u}}^{i}.
Similarly, the time series for each spatial node is considered separately to compute
\kappa_{t}^{j}
,
j=1,2,\dots,n_{s}N
, and define
\kappa_{t}=\frac{1}{n_{s}N}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{s}N}\kappa_{t}^{j}.
The smoothness hyper-parameters are specified as
\beta_{\bm{u}}=\kappa_{\bm{u}}^{2}/C_{s}
and
\beta_{t}=\kappa_{t}^{2}/4

[
40
]
, where
C_{s}
depends on the spatial dimension
s
of functions in
{\mathcal{U}}
. Specifically,
C_{1}=12
,
C_{2}=8
, and
C_{3}=4
. We compute
\beta_{\bm{z}}
in an analogous manner using the data
{\bm{z}}_{\ell}
,
\ell=1,2,\dots,N
. Note that
\beta_{t}=\kappa_{t}^{2}/4\neq\kappa_{t}^{2}/C_{1}
because the temporal operator is not squared.

4.2
Temporal variance weighting hyper-parameter

The temporal trend of the discrepancy magnitude is controlled by the hyper-parameter
\bm{\alpha}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t}}
. Note that if
\bm{\alpha}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t}}
is scaled by a constant
C
, then
\alpha_{\bm{u}}
may be scaled by
C^{-1}
to render the same prior covariance. Hence, we are not concerned about the norm of the vector
\bm{\alpha}_{t}
but rather the magnitude of its entries relative to one another. Note that all entries must be strictly positive since they specify variance.

Defining
\bm{\alpha}_{t}=\bm{e}
, the vector of
1
’s, will treat all time nodes equally (up to some boundary effects of the elliptic operator). However, the discrepancy data
\{\bm{d}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
provides temporal trends to inform the specification of
\bm{\alpha}_{t}
. To this end, we compute the spatial norm (squared) of
\{\bm{d}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t}n_{s}}
for each time step, yielding a dataset
\{\hat{\bm{d}}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t}}
, where the
i^{th}
entry of
\hat{\bm{d}}_{\ell}
is
\|\bm{d}_{\ell,i}\|_{{\bm{M}}_{s}}^{2}=\bm{d}_{\ell,i}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{s}\bm{d}_{\ell,i}
,
\bm{d}_{\ell,i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{s}}
denotes the time node
i
snapshot of
\bm{d}_{\ell}
. Note that we compute the norm squared since the variance is a squared quantity. Averaging the data and normalizing the average by its maximum (the vector infinity norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
) yields

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{t}=\frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N}\hat{\bm{d}}_{\ell}}{\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N}\hat{\bm{d}}_{\ell}\right\|_{\infty}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{t}},

which weights each time step with a value in
[0,1]
. To ensure sufficient expressivity of the prior, we inflate the variance by adding a constant
\epsilon_{t}>0
to define
\bm{\alpha}_{t}=\hat{\bm{\alpha}}_{t}+\epsilon_{t}\bm{e}
. We suggest
\epsilon_{t}=0.01
as a default meaning that the variance is inflated by
1\%
of the maximum variance magnitude.

4.3
State variance hyper-parameter

The prior discrepancy, evaluated at
{\bm{z}}={\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}
, is normally distributed with mean zero and covariance
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}
, see (
3.4
). Hence, the variance hyper-parameter
\alpha_{\bm{u}}
, which scales
{\bm{W}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}
, determines the magnitude of the prior model discrepancy at
{\bm{z}}={\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}
. We initialize
\alpha_{\bm{u}}
so that the expectation of the prior discrepancy norm, evaluated at
{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}
, equals the observed magnitude of the discrepancy data. Specifically, consider a prior sample
{\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}}
, where
\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}}
is sampled from a standard normal distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{\theta}}
. The discrepancy norm squared is

\displaystyle\|{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}={\bm{u}}_{0}(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})^{T}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}_{0}(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}}),

which is distributed as a weighted sum of chi-squared random variables, where the weights are defined by the eigenvalues of
{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega_{\bm{\theta}}}}\left[\|{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega_{\bm{\theta}}}}\left[{\bm{u}}_{0}(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})^{T}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{u}}_{0}(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\bm{u}}\text{Tr}_{2}({\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}^{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}^{\frac{1}{2}}).

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\bm{u}}\text{Tr}_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}({\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1})

Hence, we initialize the state variance hyper-parameter as

\displaystyle\alpha_{\bm{u}}=\frac{\|\bm{d}_{1}\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}}{\text{Tr}_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}({\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1})},

so that
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega_{\bm{\theta}}}}\left[\|{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{T}}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}\right]=\|\bm{d}_{1}\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}
. Note that the initialization of
\alpha_{\bm{u}}
depends only on
\bm{d}_{1}
and not
\{\bm{d}_{\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{N}
since the analysis is based on evaluating the discrepancy at
{\bm{z}}={\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}={\bm{z}}_{1}
.

4.4
Optimization variable variance hyper-parameter

The variance hyper-parameter
\alpha_{\bm{z}}
determines the rate at which the prior discrepancy varies with respect to changes in
{\bm{z}}
, i.e., it controls the magnitude of the Jacobian of the discrepancy with respect to
{\bm{z}}
. To specify
\alpha_{\bm{z}}

\Delta{\bm{z}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{z}}

\displaystyle{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}},\bm{T}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})-{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},\bm{T}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})={\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}{\bm{L}}(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}}){\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}},

which follows from (
3.4
). Theorem
4.1
provides a convenient expression for the expectation (over the prior distribution) of the discrepancy difference, normalized by the expected discrepancy at the low-fidelity optimization solution

Theorem 4.1

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\|(S({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}})-\tilde{S}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}}))-(S({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}})-\tilde{S}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}))\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}\right]

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega_{\bm{\theta}}}}\left[\|{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{L}}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}\right]=\|\bm{d}_{1}\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}
by our specification of
\alpha_{\bm{u}}
, and manipulating (
4.1
), yields the expression to initialize
\alpha_{\bm{z}}
as

\displaystyle\alpha_{\bm{z}}=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\|\bm{d}_{1}\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\Delta{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{z}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}}\right]}.

(4.4)

Next we discuss estimating the numerator and denominator of (
4.4
), thus providing an approximate initialization of
\alpha_{\bm{z}}
. Exact computation of (
4.4
) is not possible since
\gamma^{2}
involves the high-fidelity model, which can be accessed only sparingly.

Estimation of
\gamma^{2}

We leverage evaluations of
\tilde{S}
to capture characteristic scales that are relevant to provide an estimate of
\gamma^{2}
. For notational simplicity, let
\Delta S(\Delta{\bm{z}})=S({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}})-S({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}})
and
\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}})=\tilde{S}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}})-\tilde{S}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}})
and observe that manipulations of (
4.3
) yields

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[(\Delta S(\Delta{\bm{z}})-\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}}))^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}(\Delta S(\Delta{\bm{z}})-\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}}))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\|\Delta S(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}+\|\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}-2\Delta S(\Delta{\bm{z}})^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}})\right].

To facilitate a practical estimator for
\gamma^{2}
, we assume that
\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\|\Delta S(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\|\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}\right]
, that is, the high- and low-fidelity models have the same magnitude of sensitivity with respect to
{\bm{z}}
, on average. Then we have

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}=2\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\|\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}-\cos(\zeta)\|\Delta S(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}\|\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}\right],

(4.5)

where
\zeta
is the angle (in the
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}
inner product) between
\Delta S(\Delta{\bm{z}})
and
\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}})
. We cannot use (
4.5
) to compute
\gamma^{2}
because
\zeta
is an unknown that depends on the high-fidelity model
S
and varies with the input
\Delta{\bm{z}}
. However,
\cos(\zeta)\in(-1,1)
with
\cos(\zeta)=1
and
\cos(\zeta)=0
corresponding to the cases when
\Delta S(\Delta{\bm{z}})
and
\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}})
are aligned and orthogonal, respectively. Taking an intermediate value of
\cos(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}
, and assuming that

\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\|\Delta S(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}\|\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\|\Delta S(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}\right]\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\|\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}\right],

yields the approximation

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}\approx\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\|\Delta\tilde{S}(\Delta{\bm{z}})\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}}^{2}\right].

(4.6)

Then
\gamma^{2}
may be approximated via (
4.6
) using only low-fidelity model evaluations.

Estimation of
\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\Delta{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{z}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}}\right]

To estimate the denominator of (
4.4
), Theorem
4.2
demonstrates that the expectation may be expressed in a convenient form by leveraging the spectral decomposition of
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let

\displaystyle{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}={\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\bm{V}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{V}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}

(4.7)

denote the generalized eigenvalues decomposition of
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}
in the
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}
inner product, where the eigenvectors satisfies
\bm{V}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\bm{V}=\bm{I}
,
\bm{V}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{z}\times n_{z}}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Delta{\bm{z}}}\left[\Delta{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{z}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}}\right]=\|{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega}_{\bm{z}}}\left[\frac{\bm{\omega}_{\bm{z}}^{T}\bm{\Lambda}^{-4}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{z}}}{\bm{\omega}_{\bm{z}}^{T}\bm{\Lambda}^{-2}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{z}}}\right],

where
\bm{\omega}_{\bm{z}}
follows a standard normal distribution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{z}}
.

A proof is given in
D

Recall that
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}=\beta_{\bm{z}}{\bm{K}}_{\bm{z}}+{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}
, so

\displaystyle\beta_{\bm{z}}{\bm{v}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{K}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{v}}_{i}+{\bm{v}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{v}}_{i}=\lambda_{i}{\bm{v}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{v}}_{i}\implies\lambda_{i}=1+\beta_{\bm{z}}{\bm{v}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{K}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{v}}_{i}\geq 1.

Furthermore, the eigenvalues are known to increase quadratically, i.e.,
\lambda_{i}=\mathcal{O}(i^{2})
, since
{\bm{K}}_{\bm{z}}
is the discretization of the Laplacian. This implies that the integrand of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega}_{\bm{z}}}\left[\frac{\bm{\omega}_{\bm{z}}^{T}\bm{\Lambda}^{-4}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{z}}}{\bm{\omega}_{\bm{z}}^{T}\bm{\Lambda}^{-2}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{z}}}\right]

(4.8)

is strictly less than
1
and can be approximated by truncating the eigenvalue contributions since the numerator and denominator scale like
\lambda_{i}^{-4}=\mathcal{O}(i^{-8})
and
\lambda_{i}^{-2}=\mathcal{O}(i^{-4})
. Hence, we compute (
4.8
) via Monte Carlo estimation, which requires evaluating the leading eigenvalues of
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}
and generating standard normal random vectors whose dimension corresponds to the number of eigenvalues that are retained. For some problems, for instance those on a rectangular domain, the eigenvalues admit an analytic expression. Consequently, the Monte Carlo estimation may be computed at a negligible cost.

4.5
The role of visualization to verify or adjust the hyper-parameters

The algorithms presented above serve to initialize the prior hyper-parameters. However, the algorithms rely on many assumptions and approximations. Typically,
\alpha_{\bm{u}}
and
\alpha_{t}
can be initialized well since they do not require strong assumptions. On the other hand,
\beta_{\bm{u}},\beta_{\bm{z}},
and
\beta_{t}
rely on empirical correlation length estimation, which assumes that the data is sampled from a Gaussian random field. The most challenging hyper-parameter to initialize is
\alpha_{\bm{z}}
, which involves assumptions on the relationship between the high- and low-fidelity models and a crude estimation of
\gamma^{2}
.

Misspecification of the prior hyper-parameters will lead to inappropriate prior distributions that are either overly restrictive (i.e., only permitting small discrepancies) or highly uninformed (i.e., permitting such large discrepancies that the uncertainty analysis is not useful). Visualizing prior discrepancy samples is crucial to ensure that the hyper-parameters produce a physically meaningful prior and consequently a useful quantification of uncertainty. However, visualization of the discrepancy is challenging since it is an operator mapping from
{\mathcal{Z}}
to
{\mathcal{U}}
, which in general, are both function spaces. In the subsections below, we present an approach to curate prior sample data and visualize it in a way that guides verification and/or modification of the hyper-parameters determined by the initialization algorithms.

Prior sample data

Let
\{{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{Q}
denote samples from the prior parameter distribution. For each
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}
,
{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
is an operator mapping from
{\mathcal{Z}}
to
{\mathcal{U}}
and hence can be visualized only as input/output pairs since each
{\bm{z}}
and
{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
are functions. We center our analysis around the low-fidelity optimization solution
{\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}
and select optimization variable perturbations
\{\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{P}
to form a dataset

\displaystyle\{\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i}),{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})\}_{i=1,k=1}^{Q,P}.

(4.9)

The perturbations
\{\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{P}
are determined by seeking directions that yield the largest change in the prior discrepancy
{\delta}
. Specifically, we compute
\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}
’s that maximize the expected value of the Jacobian of
{\delta}
with respect to
{\bm{z}}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\theta}}\left[\nabla_{\bm{z}}{\delta}(\cdot,{\bm{\theta}})\right]
. In
F
, we demonstrate that the
\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}
’s should be taken as the leading eigenvectors of
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}
(computed in the
{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}
inner product). Observing from (
3.10
) that the magnitude of
{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})-{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
is proportional to the eigenvalue associated with the eigenvector
\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}
, and recalling that the leading eigenvalue of
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}
equals
1
, we determine the number of perturbations
P
by computing all eigenpairs whose eigenvalue is
\geq 0.1
, i.e., within one order of magnitude of the leading eigenvalue.

Since an adequate number of samples
\{{\bm{\theta}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{Q}
is required to characterize the prior distribution and an adequate number of perturbations
P
is required to characterize the sensitivity of
{\delta}
, we frequently have
Q=\mathcal{O}(10^{2})
and
P=\mathcal{O}(10^{2})
, or larger, which implies that the prior discrepancy dataset (
4.9
) consists of
\mathcal{O}(Q\cdot P)=\mathcal{O}(10^{4})
functions, each of which vary in space and/or time. Visualization of such data is challenging since only a small number of functions may be visualized simultaneously. An interactive framework is proposed to identify extreme cases in the dataset and render visualizations of select samples and perturbations.

Interactive visualization framework

To summarize the data in a way that
\mathcal{O}(10^{4})
samples may be visualized, we define two metrics and project the data onto a scatter plot where each sample is represented by the metric values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. We select metrics that reflect the interpretation of the hyper-parameters and hence enable visualizations that guide their specification. The temporal hyper-parameters
\bm{\alpha}_{t}
and
\beta_{t}
can be visualized by overlaying time series plots, as demonstrated Section
5.2
(see Figure
7
). We focus on two sets of visualizations relevant for two subsets of hyper-parameters,
\{\alpha_{\bm{u}},\beta_{\bm{u}}\}
and
\{\alpha_{\bm{z}},\beta_{\bm{z}}\}
. Visualization for
\{\alpha_{\bm{z}},\beta_{\bm{z}}\}
is demonstrated in Section
5.3
(see Figure
8
).

Recall from (
3.10
) that
{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
is directly proportional to
\sqrt{\alpha_{\bm{u}}}
and that its spatial smoothness properties are determined by
\beta_{\bm{u}}
. Inspired by this interpretation of
\alpha_{\bm{u}}
and
\beta_{\bm{u}}
, we use the maximum absolute value and correlation length of
{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
(which is a function defined on space and/or time) as metrics to project the data
\{{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{Q}
onto a 2D scatter plot, which allows for an assessment if the magnitude and smoothness of the prior samples
\{{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{Q}
are commensurate to characteristics of
S({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}})-\tilde{S}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}})
. Visualizing the fields
{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
for the extreme and average cases in the scatter plot reveals if
\alpha_{\bm{u}}
and
\beta_{\bm{u}}
have been specified correctly. Generally,
\beta_{\bm{u}}
should be analyzed first since
\alpha_{\bm{u}}
depends on it. Furthermore, there are potentially invalid assumptions made in our initialization of
\beta_{\bm{u}}
, whereas the initialization of
\alpha_{\bm{u}}
did not make assumptions on the data. If the visualizations identify misspecification of the hyper-parameters, the interpretation provided by (
3.10
) guides how the values should be modified. In particular, if the samples
\{{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{Q}
are too smooth (or non-smooth) then
\beta_{\bm{u}}
should be decreased (or increased) and if their magnitudes are too large (or small) then
\alpha_{\bm{u}}
should be decreased (or increased).

We assume that
\{\alpha_{\bm{u}},\beta_{\bm{u}}\}
have been specified properly based on of
\{{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{Q}
, and now consider analysis of
\{\alpha_{\bm{z}},\beta_{\bm{z}}\}
. Again recalling (
3.10
), observe that

\displaystyle{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})-{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})=\sqrt{\alpha_{\bm{u}}}\sqrt{\alpha_{\bm{z}}}\sqrt{s_{\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{u}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{u}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{u}}^{(2)},

(4.10)

where
s_{\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}}=\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}=\|{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}}\|_{{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}}^{2}
. We may interpret
\alpha_{\bm{z}}
and
\beta_{\bm{z}}
in (
4.10
) as controlling the rate of change when
{\delta}
is perturbed in the direction
\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}
. Note that
\beta_{\bm{z}}
is implicit in (
4.10
) since
\beta_{\bm{z}}
appears in
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}
, which enters (
4.10
) via
s_{\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}}
.

Notice that
\beta_{\bm{z}}
weights perturbations based on their smoothness properties. In particular, the eigenvectors of
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}
behave like sinusoidal functions and the eigenvalues are inversely proportional to the wave number. Hence, smooth (i.e., small wavenumber or low-frequency) eigenvectors correspond to larger eigenvalues. Furthermore, observe that
{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})-{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
is proportional to
\sqrt{s_{\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}}}
, which is proportional to the eigenvalue of
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}
associated with the eigenvector
\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}
. Hence, we interpret
\beta_{\bm{z}}
in terms of its influence on the spectrum of
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}
, which determines the response of
{\delta}
to high-frequency perturbations
\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}
. Large values of
\beta_{\bm{z}}
will result in
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}
having rapidly decaying eigenvalues so that
{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})\approx{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
for all but a small number of leading low-frequency perturbations. Conversely, taking small values of
\beta_{\bm{z}}
will result in
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}
having a “flat" spectrum (many eigenvalues close to
1
) and consequently
{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})-{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
will be large for high-frequency perturbations
\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}
. Visualizing the discrepancy response to low- and high-frequently perturbations provides the insight to verify or adjust
\beta_{\bm{z}}
. Assuming that
\beta_{\bm{z}}
is specified appropriately, observe from (
4.10
) that
\sqrt{\alpha_{\bm{z}}}
scales the magnitude of
{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})-{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
. Hence,
\alpha_{\bm{z}}
is verified or modified by linear scaling of
\sqrt{\alpha_{\bm{z}}}
based upon the magnitude of the discrepancy differences.

Given these observations about
\alpha_{\bm{z}}
and
\beta_{\bm{z}}
, we use the maximum absolute value of
{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})-{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
and the correlation length of
\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}
as metrics to project
\{\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})-{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})\}_{i=1,k=1}^{Q,P}
onto a 2D scatter plot. We select extreme points in the 2D scatter plot and visualize
\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k}
and
{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}}_{k},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})-{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},{\bm{\theta}}_{i})
to guide the verification or modification of
\alpha_{\bm{z}}
and
\beta_{\bm{z}}
, as demonstrated in Figure
8
.

5
Numerical results

This section illustrates our hyper-parameter specification framework and how the prior discrepancy depends on the hyper-parameters. We begin with a stationary problem in one spatial dimension for ease of explanation and visualization. Subsequently, we generalize to a transient problem in one spatial dimension to demonstrate the effect of the temporal variance hyper-parameter. Lastly, we consider a stationary problem in two spatial dimensions, where the hyper-parameter initialization algorithms produce a poor prior. We demonstrate the role of visualization to identify the issue and correct the initialization by adjusting the hyper-parameters.

6
Conclusion

Hyper-differential sensitivity analysis with respect to model discrepancy has considerable potential to enable approximate optimization with quantified uncertainties for many applications where currently only low-fidelity optimization is possible. However, to provide useful uncertainty estimates, it is crucial to specify the model discrepancy prior distribution appropriately. As was demonstrated in Subsection
5.1
, misspecification of the model discrepancy prior may lead to an optimal solution posterior distribution that is too narrow, thus giving unwarranted confidence in the solution. On the other hand, if the model discrepancy prior distribution is not sufficiently informative, then the posterior will have large uncertainty in directions not informed by the high-fidelity data. Consequently, propagating this posterior discrepancy through the optimization problem will yield large uncertainty in the optimization solution such that it fails to be useful.

Specifying the prior model discrepancy is challenging for large-scale and coupled systems due to the overwhelming volume of information that must be processed in order to formulate the prior appropriately. For instance, in a coupled system with five transient states in three spatial dimensions (e.g., a fluid flow model with three velocity components, a pressure component, and a temperature component), there are over 30 prior hyper-parameters that must be understood through visualization of many transient vector fields. This article provides a pragmatic approach to specify the prior through a combination of algorithmic initialization of the prior hyper-parameters and a visualization framework to understand their influence on the prior discrepancy. Importantly, our approach is scalable in both the computational complexity of the algorithmic initialization and in the visual complexity of the prior discrepancy analysis. Our mathematical analysis and numerical demonstrations coupled with visualization provide insight to guide hyper-parameter specification that would otherwise be intractable due to the volume of data generated from prior sampling.

Traditional Bayesian approaches to hyper-parameter selection may be considered as alternatives to our approach. For instance, choosing hyper-parameters that maximize the data likelihood. However, there are several important problem characteristics that motivate our approach. We focus on problems where the number of high-fidelity model evaluations
N
is small. In the case where
N=1
, there is no information in the data to inform
\alpha_{\bm{z}}
using data likelihood maximization. For
N>1
but much less than the dimension of
{\bm{z}}
, there will be many local extrema in the data likelihood maximization as a result of the hyper-parameters having confounding effects. On the other hand, for any
N\geq 1
, our initialization approach leverages the physical interpretation of the hyper-parameters to sequentially select the hyper-parameters. The only solution variability comes from estimating expected values. Hybrid approaches can also be explored. For example, our proposed method can be combined with data likelihood maximization to (i) reduce the number of local minima by fixing a subset of hyper-parameters, (ii) inform the prior distribution of the hyper-parameters, or (iii) serve as an initialization for the data likelihood optimization algorithm.

This article focused on Gaussian priors, which are reasonable given the characteristics of the mathematical object being estimated (a model discrepancy operator). However, we have not discussed enforcement of physics-based laws. For instance, if both the high- and low-fidelity models conserve mass, then the model discrepancy should also conserve mass. For physical laws that can be expressed as linear operators, the discrepancy may be parameterized as
\bm{C}{\delta}({\bm{z}},{\bm{\theta}})
, where
\bm{C}
is the linear operator enforcing the conservation law. Consequently, the proposed approach of prior hyper-parameter specification and discrepancy calibration may still be possible. However, many constraints may require the use of nonlinear operators or inequality constraints, which prohibit much of our analysis. Future work should explore which constraints can be imposed on the prior.

Lastly, we note that this work may provide a foundation for uncertainty quantification beyond our HDSA-MD framework. In particular, our approach to prior modeling may be useful in operator learning. Calibration of
{\delta}
is an example of learning an affine operator. Future work may explore extensions to nonlinear operator learning such as uncertainty quantification on a linearization of the learned operator, for instance, the output layer of a Neural Operator.

Appendix E
Empirical correlation length estimation

This appendix summarizes how the correlation length of a Gaussian random field is estimated from data. For generality, we assume that the data is denoted by
\bm{f}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, which corresponds to evaluations of a function
f:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
, where
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{s}
is a domain with either
s=1,2,
or
3
. This formulation is applicable for the state and optimization variables with dependence on time or space. Furthermore, we assume that nodal values are provided along with
\bm{f}
, i.e., the nodal values are contained in
\mathbf{X}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times s}
, so that the
i^{th}
component of
\bm{f}
corresponds to evaluating the function
f
for the
i^{th}
row of
\mathbf{X}
.

Since we are calibrating hyperparamters for a spatially homogenous Gaussian prior, we treat
\bm{f}
as a realization of a Gaussian random field. We seek to compute the correlation length
\kappa\in(0,\infty)
, which is defined as the distance for which the correlation between evaluations of
f
at points
\bm{x},\bm{y}
, such that
\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}=\kappa
, have a correlation of
0.1
.

To compute the correlation length from data, we use evaluations of
f
at various inputs
\bm{x},\bm{y}
to estimate the correlation for various distances
\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|_{2}
and estimate
\kappa
as the distance for which we achieve a correlation estimate of approximately
0.1
. To simplify this computation, we use the nodal data
\mathbf{X}
and function evaluation data
\bm{f}
to construct an interpolating function
\hat{f}
such that
\hat{f}\approx f
. We assume that the nodal data is sufficiently fine that interpolation error is negligible relative to others errors arising from assumptions and sampling.

Algorithm
1
outlines our approach to estimate the correlation length. We present the approach in dimension
s=1
on the domain
\Omega=(0,1)
for simplicity, but the algorithm generalizes to higher dimensions.

Algorithm 1

Correlation length estimation

1:

Input:
\bm{x},\bm{f}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

2:

Compute the mean
\overline{f}=\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}f_{i}

3:

Compute the variance
\sigma^{2}(f)=\frac{1}{m-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}(f_{i}-\overline{f})^{2}

4:

Construct an interpolating function
\hat{f}

5:

Set a distance increment
\Delta\kappa\in(0,1)

6:

Initialize the correlation
\rho=1

7:

Initialize the correlation length
\kappa=0

8:

while

\rho>0.1

do

9:

Increment
\kappa=\kappa+\Delta\kappa

10:

Compute
\bm{f}^{\kappa}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
by evaluating
\hat{f}
at
x_{i}+\Delta\kappa
,
i=1,2,\dots,m

11:

Compute the covariance
c_{\kappa}=\frac{1}{m-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}(f_{i}-\overline{f})(f_{i}^{\kappa}-\overline{f})

12:

Set the correlation
\rho=\frac{c_{\kappa}}{\sigma^{2}(f)}

13:

end

while

14:

Return: Correlation length
\kappa

15:

Note: For some
i
in lines 10-12,
x_{i}\pm\Delta\kappa\notin\Omega
, and hence evaluating
\hat{f}
is not appropriate. These datapoints are omitted and the normalization factor
m-1
in Line 12 is adjusted accordingly.

Appendix F
Determining perturbation directions

To determine the directions
\Delta{\bm{z}}
that maximize the prior discrepancy variation
{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}},\bm{T}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})-{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},\bm{T}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})
, observe that (
3.4
) implies that

\displaystyle{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}}+\Delta{\bm{z}},\bm{T}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})-{\delta}({\tilde{{\bm{z}}}},\bm{T}_{\bm{\theta}}\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}})={\bm{T}}_{\bm{u}}\bm{L}(\bm{\omega}_{\bm{\theta}}){\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}}.

We seek the perturbation directions
\Delta{\bm{z}}
that maximize
\|{\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}}\|_{2}^{2}
. Recalling that
{\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}={\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}}\|_{2}^{2}=\Delta{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}}.

Since
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}={\bm{V}}\bm{\Lambda}^{-1}{\bm{V}}^{T}
and
{\bm{V}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{V}}=\bm{I}
,

\displaystyle\|{\bm{T}}_{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}}\|_{2}^{2}=\Delta{\bm{z}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}{\bm{V}}\bm{\Lambda}^{-2}{\bm{V}}^{T}{\bm{M}}_{\bm{z}}\Delta{\bm{z}}.

Consequently, the maximum perturbation directions are given by the eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest eigenvalues of
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}
, or equivalently, the largest eigenvalues of
{\bm{E}}_{\bm{z}}^{-1}
.
