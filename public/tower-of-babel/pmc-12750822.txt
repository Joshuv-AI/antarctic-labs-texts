Uptake of COVID-19 vaccination and associated factors among patients attending oncology services at the Ocean Road Cancer Institute in Dar Es Salaam, Tanzania: insights from mixed design methods’ trajectory
Authors: David Makoko, Anthony Kapesa, Micheal Mugerwa, Mamsau Ngoma, Mark Mseti, Mary Lyimo, Lucy Kisaka, Ntuli A. Kapologwe, Jeremiah Seni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCOVID-19 vaccination campaigns have reduced disease severity and fatalities around the globe. Global data shows that 67.7% of the general population are vaccinated, and Tanzania has reported appealing findings of over 70% coverage among individuals over 18 years of age. However, a group-specific assessment of vaccination coverage is needed in sub-Saharan Africa. This study aimed at assessing the COVID-19 vaccination uptake and associated factors among cancer patients owing to their vulnerability.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study design was conducted in September 2022 at Ocean Road Cancer Institute (ORCI) in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. A multistage sampling approach was employed for quantitative data collection, combining stratified sampling to ensure the representation of cancer types and simple random sampling to select 479 cancer patients, and purposeful sampling was applied for qualitative data collection involving 26 patients. Data were analysed using the statistical software ‘R’ by logistic regression analysis to determine the association between socio-demographic, clinical, and Health Belief Model (HBM) variables against COVID-19 vaccine uptake among cancer patients.ResultsA total of 402 (79.5%) participants were female with a mean age (± standard deviation) of 48 ± 12.4 years, ranging from 18 to 83 years. Approximately 58.2% (278/479) of the participants in the quantitative research reported to be vaccinated against COVID-19, and among them, 79.5% were females. After controlling for other variables, having secondary school education or higher education (Adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.26, 95% CI 1.20–4.27; p = 0.011), perception of COVID-19 vaccine (aOR 8.86, 95% CI 2.84–32.2; p < 0.001), and perceived severity of COVID-19 (aOR 0.56, 95% CI 0.36–0.87; p = 0.010) were significantly associated with vaccine uptake. In the qualitative part, the findings suggest that individuals’ beliefs, perceptions, and external factors play a role in their decision to vaccinate.ConclusionsThe update of COVID-19 vaccine among cancer patients at ORCI was 58%; with patients’ higher education status, positive perception on the COVID-19 vaccine, and perceived disease severity being significantly associated with increased COVID-19 vaccination uptake. Public health interventions should leverage these identified factors to promote vaccine uptake by tailoring communication efforts to specific characteristics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11749-x.

Background

Coronaviruses encompass a broad group of viruses that cause spectrum of illnesses, including milder conditions akin to the common cold, as well as more serious diseases like Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) and Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS) to human beings and animals like camels, bats, and cattle [1]. Corona Virus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) is an infectious disease caused by a novel coronavirus called Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) [2]. The first case of the disease occurred in Hubei Province of the Republic of China in Wuhan City in November 2019 [3]. On 30th January 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) officially declared the outbreak a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, and then, on 11 March 2020, it elevated the situation to the status of a global pandemic [4, 5].

COVID-19 spreads through human-to-human via direct contact, droplet transmission, and airborne transmission. Close physical proximity increases the risk, as infected persons release respiratory droplets when talking, breathing, coughing, sneezing, or singing [2, 6].

Current COVID-19 prevention methods are social distancing (at least 1 m apart between individuals), wearing masks in public, practicing frequent hand hygiene, either by washing with soap and water or alcohol-based hand sanitizer, covering mouth and nose with bent elbow or tissue when coughing or sneezing, and getting COVID-19 vaccination [7, 8]. In the post-COVID-19 era, the availability, acceptance and universal roll-out of the SARS-CoV-2 vaccine remain pivotal for restoring life to its pre-pandemic normalcy. However, achieving herd immunity through vaccination programs largely relies on the public’s willingness to embrace and receive the vaccine [9]. It is crucial to direct the attention to vulnerable groups like people who are older than 60 years, those with underlying health conditions such as heart or lung diseases, diabetes, or people with cancer and other conditions that affect their immune system [10, 11]. Unless a patient has a history of allergy to a vaccine component, many specialist organizations, including the American Cancer Society, suggest that all cancer patients or those with a cancer history to be vaccinated against COVID-19 [12, 13]. Despite medical recommendations, the uptake of COVID-19 vaccinations among those suffering from cancer continues to be low. A survey of 435 cancer patients in India in 2021 discovered that only 20% were vaccinated for COVID-19, and 77% had vaccination reluctance; this was similar to 18.0% vaccination coverage among 3,470 participants in the general communities in eight regions across Tanzania. Fear of side effects, lack of confidence in the healthcare system, and the vaccine’s quick approval rate since the outbreak were the most popular reasons for rejecting or delaying the vaccine uptake [12, 14]. However, due to multipronged strategies made by a wide range of stakeholders, Tanzania made significant strides from vaccination hesitancy and extremely low vaccine uptake to joining COVID-19 Vaccines Global Access (COVAX) around mid-2021, and reaching vaccination coverage of approximately one-third of the population (and approximately 100% of vaccination to eligible population) by Dec 30, 2022 [15]. Identified critical research gaps were based on the availability of information on COVID-19 vaccination uptake exclusively in the general population and among health care workers [14, 16], and rumors on COVID-19 vaccination reluctance from social media and other audio-visual media [17]. Therefore, this study envisaged extending research to the vulnerable population of cancer patients in Tanzania by delineating the COVID-19 vaccination uptake, and thereby, complementing the existing global and national-level data on the general population which shows vaccination uptake of 67.7% and approximately 70.0%, respectively [18]. This study aimed at assessing the COVID-19 vaccination uptake and associated factors among cancer patients owing to their vulnerability and being little represented in the existing data in Tanzania and other African countries.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

A convergent mixed-method parallel design was applied, such as an analytical hospital-based cross-sectional study for quantitative data collection and an in-depth interview (IDIs) for qualitative data collection among cancer patients attending Ocean Road Cancer Institute (ORCI) in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania in September 2022.

The study was conducted at ORCI, which is located along the shores of the Indian Ocean and is one of the oldest health institutions in Tanzania formally established in 1895. The facility attends more than 55,000 individuals annually (including 28,000 cancer patients, 10,000 patients for cancer screening, and 12,000 non-cancer patients) [19].

The ORCI has ten specialized oncologists with an in-patient capacity of 270 beds. The institute estimates to attend 250 cancer patients on busy clinic days and 200 patients on ordinary clinic days per day. In Tanzania, there are four major centres for treating cancer namely ORCI, The Aga Khan Hospital – Dar es Salaam (AKH-D), Bugando Medical Centre (BMC), and Kilimanjaro Christian Medical Centre (KCMC). ORCI was selected because it is the largest cancer-specialized public referral hospital in the country with the highest attendance of cancer patients [19]. The study involved inpatients and outpatients attending oncology services at the ORCI. The units include chemotherapy, radiotherapy nursing, and palliative care.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The sample size for quantitative research was determined by using Cochran’s formula [20] by taking the proportion of hesitancy to the COVID-19 at 59.0% from a study conducted in India to oncologic patients [21], 95% confidence interval (CI) and a marginal error of 4.88% with an addition of 5% non-response rate. A multistage sampling approach was applied to ensure a representative and systematic selection of participants. In the first stage, stratified sampling was used to represent key cancer types, including cervical, breast, prostate, oesophagus, and other cancers. For qualitative, purposeful non-probability sampling was applied, and the exact number of people to be interviewed were determined when the saturation point was reached. Research assistants obtained the participants from the list of patients getting treatment on each specific day. The key characteristics used for purposeful sampling were centred around identifying individuals who represent the unique demographic and clinical profile of cancer. The sampling aimed to include participants with diverse attributes, such as varying cancer types, stages of cancer, age groups, educational backgrounds, and job statuses.

Inclusion criteria: (1) patients seeking oncology services at the ORCI who were 18 years of age or older and (2) patients who have been admitted as inpatients or seen as outpatients with any type of cancer. Exclusion criteria: involved patients with missing/incomplete clinical information based on variables of interest in the data collection tool for the quantitative component.

Data collection procedure

The data collection process for this study involved both quantitative and qualitative methods (see Appendices i & ii the supplemental files 1 and 2). A team of three research assistants carried out a data collection process, with two focusing on the quantitative aspects, and DM in collaboration with research assistants were dedicated to qualitative data. The quantitative team comprised of DM, medical doctor and pharmacist graduates, and for the qualitative data, DM and an experienced nurse with a master’s degree in health economics led the data collection process. To ensure a conducive and private environment for interviewees, the interviews were conducted in an ample space outdoor, allowing them to feel comfortable and ensuring confidentiality in their responses.

For the quantitative data collection, a translated questionnaire was used, which was administered using the Open Data Kit (ODK) software on mobile devices such as android phones, tablets, and computers. The questionnaire included metadata such as instance ID, device ID, start time, and end time, which are important for data cleaning. To avoid bias, duplication, and recurrence of data from one respondent, the patient file number was used as a unique identifier.

For qualitative data collection, IDI were conducted using a structured guiding tool to ensure consistency. The interviews were conducted in Swahili language and lasted for an average of 13 min each. Audio recordings were made with participants’ written consent, which was obtained before the interviews. Note-taking and participant observation complemented the recordings, capturing non-verbal cues and contextual insights. To ensure accuracy and comprehensiveness, the interviewer focused on conducting the interview while simultaneously taking brief, critical notes that were later expanded and cross-verified against the audio recordings. A back-and-forth translation process was carried out from Swahili to English to facilitate analysis.

Given the fact that data was collected using ODK software in a tablet and laptop for the quantitative part of the study, participants were fully informed about the study using an information sheet (a copy of which was also given to each participant) and were given the option to participate or withdraw from the study at any time. No compensation was provided to the study participants.

Data quality assurance

Prior to data collection, a pre-testing of the questionnaire was carried out at Muhimbili National Hospital, comprising 5.0% of the sample population. The purpose of this pre-test was to assess the internal consistency of the questionnaire. Acceptability was determined based on a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient exceeding 70.0%, which is commonly considered acceptable in psychometric evaluation [22]. The pre-test involved administering the tool to cancer patients at Muhimbili National Hospital, and adjustments were made based on feedback derived from the data collected during this phase.

Study variables

Dependent variables

The dependent variable of the study was vaccination status against COVID-19 which was operationalized as a binary variable (1 = “Yes” and 0 = “No”) based on whether the respondents had received any of the available COVID-19 vaccines (i.e., COVID-19 uptake). In this study, the term “vaccinated individuals’ referred to individuals who received at least one vaccine dose irrespective of the types of the COVID-19 vaccines.

Independent variables

The independent variables of the study were grouped into four main categories: socio-demographic characteristics, medical history of chronic diseases, general health behaviours, and perceptions and acceptance of the COVID-19 vaccine.

Data analysis procedure and statistical analysis

A convergent design method was used to analyse both quantitative and qualitative data in order to triangulate findings and increase the reliability and validity of the results. The quantitative data were obtained from ODK, an online data collection platform, and checked for consistency and completeness before being coded and exported to the ‘R Studio’ programming language for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and cross-tabulations, were used to summarize the data; chi-square, univariate, and multivariate logistic regression were performed to test the association between the dependent and independent variables. Measurements were done using odds ratios with their respective 95% confidence intervals. A comparison of the proportions of the females and males was conducted using a two-sample test of proportions to determine statistical difference in vaccination rates between the two groups. All variables with a p-value of ≤ 0.05 in the univariate logistic regression model were subjected to the multivariate analysis. The logistic regression analysis was calculated using the Wald test, with statistical significance association between dependent variables and independent variables defined as p-value of ≤ 0.05.

For the qualitative data collection, audio recordings of the interviews were transcribed, and all notes taken during the data collection process were reviewed, organized, and corrected to ensure the integrity of the data. The audio files were also translated into English. Structural Topic Modelling (STM) package in ‘R’ was used to identify key themes related to the acceptance and uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine among cancer patients attending oncology services at the ORCI in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. The ‘stm’ R package was used to analyse the open-ended text responses, and quotes with a theta score above 0.3 were extracted for further analysis [23]. A detailed thematic analysis was then conducted to allow a comprehensive exploration of the data content and underlying significance. The findings for both quantitative and qualitative data were triangulated in order to provide a comprehensive understanding of the factors that influenced the uptake of COVID-19 vaccination among cancer patients at the ORCI.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance for this study was obtained from the joint CUHAS/BMC Research Ethics and Review Committee (CREC) with certificate number CREC/606/2022. Permission to conduct the study at ORCI was also obtained from the executive director of ORCI through the Institutional Academics, Research, Publications, and Ethics Committee (IARPEC) with Reference 10/VOL.XXI/97-B and specific unit heads were informed. All interviewees were asked to provide informed consent before participating in the study. Confidentiality was maintained for all respondents throughout the study, from data collection to analysis, and respondents were free to decline to answer any questions that made them uncomfortable.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 505 out of 506 respondents participated in the study, yielding an impressive response rate of 99.8%. The majority of participants were female (402, 79.6%), and 94.85% took part in the quantitative component of the study. Participants ranged in age from 19 to 83 years, with a mean age of 48.05 years (SD ± 12.36). Notably, 96.63% of the respondents were aged 26 years and above. In terms of educational attainment, more than half of the respondents had completed primary education (42.97%) or secondary education and above (35.44%). Among those who disclosed their religious affiliations, Christians constituted the largest group, accounting for 51.69% of the sample. About two-thirds (62.97%) of the respondents were self-employed. Approximately 98.1% of the respondents reported being non-smokers; and 263 (54.9%) were engaging in regular exercises (Table 1).

COVID-19 vaccination uptake among cancer patients

Table 4 below on COVID-19 vaccination uptake among 479 individuals reveals that 58.0% of the respondents had received the COVID-19 vaccine. Among the vaccinated ones (N = 278), 43.2% had received the first dose, with only 6.5% having completed both doses. 50.4% of the vaccinated participants were uncertain on the vaccine received if it was the first dose or the second. Sinopharm and Janssen were mentioned most frequently, accounting for 23.4% and 21.9% of the responses, respectively. However, a significant portion (50.7%) could not recall the name of the vaccine they had received. Most participants (97.1%) reported taking whatever vaccine was available, indicating a lack of preference in their vaccine selection.

Among the unvaccinated individuals (N = 201), 59.2% expressed a greater inclination towards future vaccination. Notably, 21.4% reported that their decision remained unchanged, signifying hesitancy to take the vaccine. Most unvaccinated participants (93.0%) did not consult medical personnel, and among those who did, only a small percentage (1.0%) found the medical advice irrelevant. There was no statistical difference in COVID-19 vaccination rates between females and males [57.7% (221/383) versus 60.0% (57/96); p-value = 0.684].

Qualitative findings: exploring personal underlying beliefs and perspectives influencing COVID-19 vaccination uptake among oncology patients

Five themes emerged during the analysis, perceptions on the COVID-19 vaccine, motivating factors for vaccination among cancer patients, trust in information sources, barriers to and availability of the vaccine, and the influence of key figures.

Theme 1: perception of the COVID-19 vaccine

The data collected from the respondents revealed mixed feelings about the COVID-19 vaccine, with some expressing positive perceptions while others expressing negative perceptions. When asked about their perception of the COVID-19 vaccine, the respondents said:

“I advise my fellow patients that we get tested, get those vaccines, and adhere to guidelines… Let us pray that God blesses us so this pandemic does not continue” [KII1]. Another said, “My initial perspective was negative; later on, I realized it was helpful… Because many people were getting vaccinated, and some seemed confused, crazy, or overwhelmed… Our neighbour got vaccinated, and he was fine, but then he got sick. Then they went to give him more medicine or something to reduce the effects [of the vaccine]. He went to get an injection to reduce the severity of the medicine… Although I haven’t gotten vaccinated due to our illnesses [cancer], I am afraid” [KII2]. “Everyone is required to be vaccinated to protect themselves” [KII5].

Another said, “My initial perspective was negative; later on, I realized it was helpful… Because many people were getting vaccinated, and some seemed confused, crazy, or overwhelmed… Our neighbour got vaccinated, and he was fine, but then he got sick. The participants were also asked about their colleagues’ perceptions of the COVID-19 vaccine uptake. The following were the responses: “Actually, I don’t know about that [Motive drive to get vaccinated]; I am still sceptical because I see that there are many diseases, and I have been affected by HIV, and now this one [Cancer]. I am afraid that even if I get vaccinated, I may end up with the same problems” [KII10].

Another one was positive regarding the same issue: “Well, the way I think is that we should get vaccinated so that our bodies can function better… I haven’t heard much about it [the vaccine], but when I see someone getting vaccinated, I think that even if COVID-19 comes, it may not be strong because you have already received the vaccine.” [KII20].

The findings suggest that personal perceptions and experiences play a significant influence on the choice to get vaccinated, and this is influenced by exposure to (mis)information.

Theme 2: motivating factors for cancer patients to get COVID-19 vaccinated

Respondents said that they are vaccinated because of the chronic diseases (including cancer) they have or being pressured. “…I mean, they just feel pressured. For example, if you have cancer, it’s a complicated disease, and if COVID-19 comes along, you don’t know what will happen”. [KII7]. This aligns with the quantitative finding indicating a higher likelihood of vaccination among cancer patients. Another participant stated that reason to vaccinate against COVID-19 is a personal perception and confidence. “It is matter of a person and their fears… lack of confidence, there are people who believe ‘if I do this, it will help me’. But someone else may see it as insignificant” [KII4].

The qualitative interviews echo the quantitative association, providing a deeper understanding of why individuals with chronic diseases, such as cancer, might feel compelled to get vaccinated. The fear of the unknown and the perceived susceptibility expressed in qualitative responses align with the quantitative data showing higher vaccine uptake among cancer patients.

Theme 3: trust in the COVID-19 sources of information

Respondents expressed diverse perspectives on the trustworthiness of COVID-19 vaccination information sources. Some conveyed scepticism, linking misinformation to potential motives such as blood clotting and infertility. Others, however, expressed trust in those who have already been vaccinated, believing they haven’t encountered problems:

“… let me think a bit … you know, everyone has their own understanding. Someone may tell you, ‘oh do not get vaccinated, it is not necessary, people are just talking so we can get vaccinated,’ and each person has their own reasons. Now, someone else may say, ‘it is all lies, what are you doing? … everyone says their own thing, some say we should not get vaccinated and they are deceiving us. Now, maybe they want to prevent us from having children early…There are many challenges … I believed that if you get vaccinated, you protect yourself and use protective gears like masks to avoid getting infected. But there are people who don’t believe and say things like ‘no, do not get vaccinated, if you get vaccinated, your blood will clot.’ And if someone has already been told that their blood might clot, it means they are afraid they might die.” [KI12]. The response contradicted this “… I believe in people who have already been vaccinated and they say are not experiencing any problems” [KII21].

The above narrative resonates with the quantitative association between trust in information sources and vaccination uptake. The diverse opinions captured in qualitative interviews mirror the variations observed in the quantitative data. Individuals who trust information sources are more likely to get vaccinated, aligning with both sets of findings implying that trust in the sources of information plays a vital role in ensuring all people with cancer get vaccinated.

Theme 4: barriers to and availability of COVID-19 vaccines

Participants echoed the sentiment that the vaccines are readily available, they mentioned various accessible locations such as hospitals, cancer testing centres, and neighbourhood facilities. Also, participants highlighted the importance of proper education from medical personnel, emphasizing that misinformation could be a potential barrier to vaccine acceptance. “Vaccines are easily available. They have announced places where you can get vaccinated, like hospitals, places where cancer is tested, and even in our neighbourhood they were registering people who wanted to get vaccinated. You just have to decide and go and say, ‘I have decided to get this vaccine to protect myself…” [KII19]. The respondents said there are no barriers to get the COVID-19 vaccines “…there are obstacles. I think it depends on our doctors telling us how it is because it did not work before until they themselves tell us what happens when you get vaccinated. But if someone is treated like this and not educated properly, they might think it will cause them any more trouble when in reality they are being saved”. [KII10]

These narratives align with the quantitative findings emphasizing the perceived ease of access to COVID-19 vaccines. Both sources suggest that the availability of vaccines is a facilitating factor, contributing positively to vaccination uptake. The qualitative information adds a shade by underscoring the significance of health providers in overcoming potential barriers related to misinformation.

Theme 5: role of influential figures in COVID-19 vaccination

The qualitative findings also showed that religious leaders and healthcare providers have influence in people’s trust and decision to get the COVID-19 vaccine. “… Doctors [Healthcare providers] knows the disease very well, so when they tell us something, it is something they have already understood, they know its effects and benefits. So that is why they educate us, telling us to do this test, protect ourselves, and even the religious leaders, they have also gained understanding, and they tell us. Sometimes you find that people do not generally get such information in hospitals, but when they go to the churches, they get it, so we are grateful for that … the way they preach to us or give us seminars, we get understand …” [KII4]. About the influence of religious leaders “Exactly, because going to the religious leaders, people have faith, they will follow” [KII26].

The above findings complement the quantitative findings by providing rich context on how healthcare providers and religious leaders contribute to vaccine acceptance. Both data sources suggest that individuals who trust and are influenced by these figures are more likely to embrace COVID-19 vaccination.

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic is fading and life is resuming to normalcy with extensive lessons on the diverse mitigation strategies reflecting country-specific local context. Capitalizing on key vulnerable populations is currently of paramount importance not only to appropriately allocate limited resources but also maximizing favorable responsive actions to patients and the general public. This study found a significant uptake of COVID-19 vaccines (approximately 60.0%) among vulnerable cancer patients. This high prevalence is indicative of the success of vaccination campaigns and individuals’ willingness to engage in public health interventions. The convergence of both quantitative and qualitative findings reinforces this positive trend in the vaccine uptake [17, 24–26]. The findings also align with the strides made in Tanzania and Uganda within two years of rolling out their multifaced strategies to combat COVID-19, which showed the uptake of the COVID-19 vaccine being 53.4% and 49.7%, respectively [8, 14–16, 27, 28]. These findings shed light on the role of socio-demographic factors, health behaviors, vaccine perception and acceptance, and health beliefs in shaping individuals’ confidence in the vaccine as articulated in the following paragraphs [24, 25, 29, 30]. While in other previous studies, age, education level, and employment status were significant factors associated with vaccine confidence dispelling vaccine-related myths or misconceptions, and subsequently fostering vaccination uptake [21, 24, 31]. In this study, education emerged as a significant factor influencing vaccine uptake, with individuals having secondary education and above being more likely to receive the vaccine. This highlights the role of education in accessing information related to vaccination. Perceptions of COVID-19 vaccine benefits were also strongly associated vaccine uptake, emphasizing the importance of understanding positive outcomes in individuals’ decision-making processes. The findings are in line with the studies conducted Zambia, Uganda and in the United States of America which revealed that uptake of COVID-19 vaccine was higher among females, those with higher education levels, and those who had received other vaccines (flu and hepatitis) in the past [26, 29, 32, 33].

Confidence in the COVID-19 vaccine is a crucial aspect for the successful vaccination campaigns and public health interventions. The information and findings presented in this study contribute to our understanding of the factors that influence individuals’ confidence in the vaccine and their decision to get vaccinated. The results also revealed the influence of health beliefs and attitudes on vaccine confidence, Individuals who perceived the vaccine as important for their health, believed in the efficacy of vaccines, and recognized the benefits of vaccination to the community had more likelihood of accepting the COVID-19 vaccine. In contrast, individuals who expressed concerns about vaccine safety or doubted the necessity of vaccination for diseases with low prevalence demonstrated lower confidence in the vaccine [13].

External factors played a significant role in the vaccine confidence as well. Social support from leaders, friends and family members, as well as access to reliable information, were associated with higher vaccine acceptance in other studies. These highpoints the influence of social networks and availability of trustworthy information in fostering confidence in the vaccine [17, 34]. The trustworthy information from health care providers and religious leaders and was also reiterated in this study on breaking barriers and fostering COVID-19 vaccine uptake.

The qualitative findings underscored the deeply individualized nature of decision-making in vaccine acceptance among cancer patients. Personal understanding, fears, and confidence emerged as significant factors shaping these decisions. Many participants expressed that their decisions were rooted in experiential learning, with individuals often shifting perspectives after experiencing issues related to COVID-19. These insights highlight the need for tailored communication strategies that address individual concerns and fears, ultimately promoting confidence. When integrated with quantitative findings, these qualitative data provide a comprehensive understanding of the factors influencing vaccine decisions. This integration enhances the design of the targeted interventions and communication approaches, aiming to optimize vaccine acceptance among cancer patients at ORCI. Notably, these findings align with the studies conducted in Zambia and the United Kingdom on the COVID-19 vaccine perspective [8, 30, 35].

A critical takeaway is the importance of addressing the Five Cs–Confidence, Complacency, Communication, Convenience, and Context. Building trust in the COVID-19 vaccine requires public health efforts to provide accurate, transparent information and design messages that resonate with individual experiences. Tailored strategies can address specific concerns, mitigate vaccine hesitancy, and promote confidence in vaccination efforts. This approach is essential to achieving high coverage, minimizing the pandemic’s impact, and protecting vulnerable populations [13, 17, 31, 34].

The convergence of qualitative and quantitative findings highlights overlapping themes such as vaccine prevalence, factors influencing uptake, trust in information sources, and the role of influential figures. Qualitative insights enrich the understanding of statistical data, adding depth and context to the quantitative results. Together, they provide a holistic view of vaccine acceptance among cancer patients at ORCI and build on tangible evidence to prioritize this vulnerable group to the country-wide vaccination strategies and priorities.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This is the first study to delineate COVID-19 vaccine uptake and associated factors among vulnerable cancer patients in Tanzania, providing pivotal information on this patients’ population to refocus the country’s research agenda and specific interventions against COVID-19 pandemic at the ORCI and the Ministry of Health in Tanzania at large. Triangulating qualitative and quantitative approaches in this study was pertinent to provide in-depth dissection of the subject matter.

Despite commendable contributions, this study has some limitations; firstly, it was cross-sectional in design, and therefore, could not ascertain the study participants’ immunological responses following vaccination uptake. Secondly, this study was conducted in one tertiary center and therefore, generalization country-wide could be limited. Nevertheless, the study is unique in its design by involving a vulnerable and “marginalized” population of cancer patients in the COVID-19 vaccine studies globally; and the involvement of the largest cancer center and tertiary referral hub in Tanzania could partly cater for the “one-center limitation.”. We also acknowledge two limitations related to the study design i.e. due to the cross-sectional nature of the study, it was not possible to ascertain temporality of exposure-outcome; and the limitation related to the residual confounding; in spite of the various adjustments.

Conclusions

The update of COVID-19 vaccine among cancer patients at ORCI was approximately 58%. The patients’ higher education status, positive perception on the COVID-19 vaccine, and perceived disease severity were significantly associated with increased COVID-19 vaccination uptake. The multivariate analysis underscores the importance of Health Belief Model factors in influencing COVID-19 vaccination behavior. The findings offer valuable insights for integrating these targeted mitigations by the Ministry of Health specifically in the Tanzania Emergency Preparedness and Response Plan, and through the National Centre for public health campaigns to foster increase in the vaccine uptake among cancer patients.

Addressing misconceptions and fostering a better understanding of the benefits and severity of COVID-19 could potentially enhance vaccination uptake rates. However, further research is warranted to delve deeper into the mechanisms underlying these associations and to explore additional contextual factors that may also contribute to vaccination decision-making.
