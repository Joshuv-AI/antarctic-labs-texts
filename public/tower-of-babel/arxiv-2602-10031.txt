Position: Message-passing and spectral GNNs are two sides of the same coin
Antonis Vasileiou, Juan Cervino, Pascal Frossard, Charilaos I. Kanatsoulis, Christopher Morris, Michael T. Schaub, Pierre Vandergheynst, Zhiyang Wang, Guy Wolf, Ron Levie

Abstract
Graph neural networks (GNNs) are commonly divided into message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) and spectral graph neural networks, reflecting two largely separate research traditions in machine learning and signal processing. This paper argues that this divide is mostly artificial, hindering progress in the field. We propose a viewpoint in which both MPNNs and spectral GNNs are understood as different parametrizations of permutation-equivariant operators acting on graph signals. From this perspective, many popular architectures are equivalent in expressive power, while genuine gaps arise only in specific regimes. We further argue that MPNNs and spectral GNNs offer complementary strengths. That is, MPNNs provide a natural language for discrete structure and expressivity analysis using tools from logic and graph isomorphism research, while the spectral perspective provides principled tools for understanding smoothing, bottlenecks, stability, and community structure. Overall, we posit that progress in graph learning will be accelerated by clearly understanding the key similarities and differences between these two types of GNNs, and by working towards unifying these perspectives within a common theoretical and conceptual framework rather than treating them as competing paradigms.

Present work

2.1
Message-passing neural networks

Message-passing neural networks
(MPNNs) compute node- or graph-level representations in a permutation-equivariant manner. MPNNs iteratively update node representations by aggregating information from local neighborhoods.
Specifically, a generic MPNN updates node features

\!\!\!\!\bm{x}_{u}^{(t+1)}\!\coloneqq\!\textsf{UPD}_{t}\left(\bm{x}_{u}^{(t)},\textsf{AGG}_{t}\left(\{\!\!\{\bm{x}_{v}^{(t)}\mid v\in N_{G}(u)\}\!\!\}\right)\right),

(1)

\textsf{AGG}_{t}

\textsf{UPD}_{t}
is a parameterized update map, and
\bm{x}_{u}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}

The resulting computations are equivariant to permutations of node orderings and invariant to permutations of neighbors within each neighborhood. For a more extensive motivation and a derivation from symmetry considerations, see

D.1

2.2
Spectral GNNs

Let
G

N

\bm{X}

Common examples include the adjacency matrix
\bm{A}

Like MPNNs, spectral GNNs are built as a composition of layers, where each layer consists of a (linear) graph filter followed by a pointwise nonlinearity. A widely used class of such spectral layers is given by
functional-calculus filters
, which apply a learnable function to the spectrum of
\bm{S}

Let
\rho

For a predefined number of layers

\bm{X}^{(t+1)}\coloneqq\rho\left(H_{t}(\bm{S})\,\bm{X}^{(t)}+\bm{b}_{t}\right),\qquad t\in[L],

(2)

where,
H_{t}(\bm{S})\bm{X}^{(t)}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}^{(t)}\,H_{t}(\lambda_{i})^{\top}
,

Such architecture is called functional calculus spectral GNN, because it defines an operator-valued map

3.1
P1: Spectral GNNs and MPNNs are often equivalent but not the same

Although spectral GNNs and MPNNs originate from different principles, they are closely related in expressivity and approximation power under standard assumptions. Here we discuss these similarities and clarify where they begin to differ.

3.1.1
Spectral GNNs and MPNNs Are Derived From Separate Symmetries

Equivariant machine learning is the study of symmetries in data and architectures. Often, one would like to design neural networks that respect data symmetries.
Equivariant architectures
are models that respect these symmetries by their design. This approach typically leads to improved inductive bias and sample complexity, as no data or weights are wasted on learning the symmetries. Conceptually, MPNNs and spectral GNNs are derived from two different symmetries.

MPNNs From neighborhood permutations

An MPNN can be seen as an architecture that processes, in each layer, each neighborhood of the graph separately. The neighborhood multisets are represented as sequences. Since the same multiset can be ordered in different ways in a sequence, the network that processes the sequences should be
invariant to permutations
. Hence, MPNNs are seen as architectures derived from the permutation symmetry of each neighborhood.

Spectral GNNs From eigenbasis invariance

Each layer in a spectral GNN is a computation that maps the signal to the frequency domain, processes its spectral representation, and maps it back to the graph domain. The mapping to the spectral domain is done by projection upon the eigenvectors of the GSO, and the mapping back is done by taking a linear combination of the eigenvectors, scaled by the spectral coefficients (see
Appendix

C
for more details). However, the eigenvectors are not uniquely defined given a GSO. For example, for any eigenvector
\bm{v}_{j}
,
-\bm{v}_{j}
is also an eigenvector. Rather, only the eigenspaces are uniquely defined. Hence, a spectral GNN should be invariant to the choice of the eigenbasis. Definition (
2
) is one example of an eigenbasis invariant GNN.

Symmetry-preserving universal approximation properties of GNNs

The MPNN layer formulation in
Equation

1
is universal in that it can approximate any continuous permutation-invariant function on fixed-dimensional compact domains; see, e.g.,
(
116
;
111
)
. In this respect, MPNN layers are sufficiently expressive. In contrast, the spectral GNN layer in
Equation

2
does not enjoy a comparable universality guarantee, i.e., it constitutes one particular mechanism for enforcing eigenbasis symmetry, without being known to approximate all continuous functions that respect it.

To align with the theoretical guarantees of MPNNs, we argue that the community should seek spectral GNN architectures that simultaneously enforce eigenbasis symmetry and provide universal approximation guarantees. Initial progress in this direction appears in
(
61

3.1.2
MPNNs and spectral GNNs have similar expressivity properties

While MPNNs and spectral GNNs originate from different symmetries, they share several theoretical properties.
We focus here on expressivity; see
P4
for a discussion on generalization.

Polynomial GNNs are special MPNNs

When spectral frequency responses are restricted to polynomials (see
Equation

12
) and the GSO is chosen as the adjacency matrix, the resulting spectral layer can be implemented exactly by a linear MPNN with sum aggregation, where each polynomial degree corresponds to one message-passing step. See
Section

E.1
for a formal derivation, including bias terms and extensions to other commonly used GSOs such as graph Laplacians.

As a consequence, analyses of spectral GNNs that focus on polynomial filters often reduce to message-passing arguments and fail to exploit genuinely spectral properties. Such analyses, therefore, do not clarify when spectral methods can outperform MPNNs. We advocate studying more general spectral filters using tools from functional analysis, such as
operator Lipschitz continuity

(
2
;
66
)
.

The role of the GSO in spectral GNNs

The GSO is central to the expressivity of spectral GNNs. While commonly defined as an
N\times N
matrix encoding graph structure (e.g., with support on edges and the diagonal), this definition is overly permissive: without further constraints, GSOs may encode arbitrary global graph properties, undermining meaningful expressivity and complexity analyses.

1

1

For instance, a diagonal GSO encoding component isomorphism enables linear-time graph isomorphism tests.

To avoid such pathologies,

MPNNs and spectral GNNs have equivalent expressivity in terms of separation power

We compare MPNN and spectral GNN expressivity via graph separation using isomorphism tests that iteratively refine node labels based on local neighborhood structure and induce graph equivalence relations. A GNN class
\mathcal{F}
is said to match the separation power of a test if, for any pair of graphs distinguished by the test, there exists

This equivalence shows that, once artificially powerful GSOs are excluded, they have the same expressive power.

MPNNs and spectral GNNs have distinct but closely related universal approximation properties

Beyond graph separation, expressivity can be analyzed via universal approximation on fixed data distributions (see
Section

E.6
). If the spectra of the GSOs in the data are uniformly bounded, any spectral GNN can be approximated by a polynomial spectral GNN, because continuous frequency responses on compact intervals admit polynomial approximation. As MPNNs are realizable as polynomial spectral GNNs (

In contrast, for data distributions with unbounded GSO spectra, e.g., complete graphs, whose largest eigenvalue grows with graph size, polynomial spectral GNNs are not universal approximators of spectral GNNs, and consequently neither are MPNNs (see
Proposition

18

3.2
P2: MPNNs have unique benefits for discrete structure and enterprise settings

A key advantage of the message-passing perspective is that it aligns GNNs with the rich theory of discrete structures developed in graph theory, logic, and theoretical computer science. For example, MPNNs’ expressivity is most naturally characterized via graph isomorphism tests, most notably the
1
-WL test, and logical formalisms such as fragments of first-order logic
(

;
35
)
, which provide a principled way to reason about what structural properties an MPNN can and cannot capture

;

)
. Such results have led to a clear understanding of MPNNs’ limitations and to the principled development of more expressive architectures, overcoming the limitations of MPNNs, e.g., based on the

-dimensional Weisfeiler–Leman algorithm
(

, a more expressive generalization of the
1
-WL, subgraph and homomorphism counts
(

)
, or subgraph-based approaches
(

;

)
. This yields not only stronger empirical models, but also a clear theoretical hierarchy of expressivity, inherited from the corresponding Weisfeiler–Leman and logical hierarchies. That is, in this sense, MPNNs offer both a powerful practical modeling tool and a principled mathematical framework for understanding and extending the limits of graph representation learning. In addition, this viewpoint also directly sheds light on MPNNs’ and their more expressive architectures’ ability to approximate permutation-invariant and equivariant functions of graphs
(
30
;

)

In addition, MPNNs extend seamlessly to directed graphs

Furthermore, the specific local update mechanism of MPNNs has led to close alignment (graph) algorithms

(

and being able to approximate hard maximum constraint satisfaction problems

From a systems and deployment perspective, the explicit aggregation and update structure of MPNNs (see

3.3
P3: Spectral GNNs have unique benefits for smoothing, bottlenecks, and communities

To understand how information is smoothed, how it propagates through bottlenecks, and how it aligns with community structure in a graph, spectral GNNs provide a natural abstraction.
They enable (i) clean characterizations of the oversmoothing phenomenon as spectral contraction; (ii) remedies and explanations for oversquashing in terms of spectral gaps; and (iii) community-sensitive representations via eigenspaces that encode clusters.
Beyond interpretability, the spectral perspective also offers tunable, theoretically grounded design knobs rooted in spectral graph theory that can be leveraged to improve empirical performance.

Oversmoothing as spectral contraction

Oversmoothing limits the depth of GNNs and indicates that node embeddings contract toward a low-dimensional, low-frequency subspace as depth increases (See
Appendix

F
). It can be observed as the collapse of pairwise node-feature distances or decaying Dirichlet energy
(
59
;
12
;
87
)
.
Spectral filters often enforce non-amplifying assumptions that
\|h(\textbf{S})\|_{2}=\max_{\lambda}|h(\lambda)|=\gamma\leq 1
, which implies that after
t
layers, the spectral components decay geometrically as
\|\textbf{X}^{(t)}\|\leq\gamma^{t}\|\textbf{X}\|
. Therefore, repeated low‑pass filtering collapses features into the span of the slowest-decaying eigenvectors.

Compared to spatial formulations, the frequency response
h(\lambda)
makes the contraction rate and the role of the spectrum of the graph shift operator
\mathbf{S}
explicit and exposes depth vs. contraction trade-offs directly through the shape of
h(\lambda)
and the spectrum of
\mathbf{S}
.
This also allows for understanding effective remedies for oversmoothing, as discussed, e.g., in
87
;
24
;
91
. While spatial MPNNs can also be viewed as data-dependent low-pass operators, their effective spectrum is only implicitly defined by their local nonlinear aggregation and update functions, making these trade-offs harder to observe.

Oversquashing through bottlenecks

Oversquashing occurs when graph signals must traverse long distances through small cuts, creating structural bottlenecks for message passing
(
3
)
.
Deeper GNNs are then required to communicate across the cut, which exacerbates smoothing and attenuation.
This effect has been formalized via feature Jacobians as a measure of pairwise influence, especially between distant nodes
(
94
;
21
;
7
;
39
)
, i.e., smaller upper bounds on the Jacobian norm indicate weaker communication.
These bounds can be expressed through several measures such as balanced Forman curvature
(
94
)
, effective resistance
(
7
)
, or commute time
(
21
)
. Each of these measures captures how well (or bad) two nodes are connected by short, parallel paths.

In the spectral view, bottlenecks manifest, e.g., as low algebraic connectivity (the Laplacian’s second eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}
) or poor conductance as captured by Cheeger-type bounds
(
44
)
.
These provide computable proxies for oversquashing.
Practically, one can diagnose oversquashing via
\lambda_{2}
, pairwise Jacobian norms, or the histogram of eigenvalues together with the frequency response
h(\lambda)
.
Mitigation strategies for oversquashing follow naturally: (i) we can design filters whose passbands preserve mid frequencies to support long-range communication without excessive depth (e.g., resolvents
h(\lambda)=(1-\alpha)/(1-\alpha\lambda)
, rational filters such as ARMA/Cayley, or mild band-pass), or (ii) rewire the graph to modify the spectrum, e.g., by increasing
\lambda_{2}
or connecting high–effective-resistance pairs.
Thus, spectral analysis reduces oversquashing diagnosis and repair to shaping
h(\lambda)
and adjusting spectral properties of
\mathbf{S}
.

Community sensitivity from low‑frequency structure

On undirected graphs, community signals are those with small Dirichlet energy
(
100
)
. Under variance and orthogonality constraints, the minimizers are the Laplacian’s low‑frequency eigenvectors, so the low‑eigenspace encodes clusters. A spectral layer with a low‑pass frequency response function preserves precisely this subspace while damping high‑frequency noise, yielding sample‑efficient, stable behavior on assortative graphs. Crucially, the spectral lens gives explicit, tunable control of the passband—via a single scale parameter in heat kernels
h(\lambda)=e^{-\tau\lambda}
, a resolvent
h(\lambda)=(1+\beta\lambda)^{-1}
, or a short Chebyshev expansion to align the cutoff and slope with the observed label spectrum. MPNN models can approximate the same effect only indirectly through depth, normalization, and residual connections that induce an implicit low‑pass whose cutoff emerges from training and graph scaling, making it harder to tune and certify. The spectral formulation is basis‑invariant, provides clear diagnostics, and extends naturally to multi‑scale communities via mixtures of diffusions, offering a cleaner, more controllable route to community sensitivity.

3.4
P4: Nuances aside, MPNNs and Spectral provide similar perspectives. A dive into generalization, stability, and transferability

While there are differences between MPNNs and Spectral GNNs, we argue that the theoretical properties exhibited by both paradigms are similar in practice.
Indeed, when using the right tools, often an (almost) one-to-one mapping can be found between them.
To argue in favor of this position, we will analyze
stability
,
size transferability
, and
generalization
.

Stability and Size Transferability

In GNNs, stability refers to the property that small perturbations in the input lead to controlled changes in the output. Such perturbations may affect node features, edge weights, or the graph structure itself. This property is important given the presence of noise and changes in the distributions over time. In the spectral GNN literature, stability is typically analyzed at the level of graph filters, where operator-theoretic techniques are used to bound the sensitivity of functions of the graph shift operator to perturbations of the underlying graph
(
56
;
27
;
55
;
86
;
16
)
.
These analyses yield stability bounds that depend at most linearly on the (polynomial) filter degree or receptive field size, implying that increasing the range of interactions does not require additional nonlinearities.
Stability for MPNNs is usually studied in a layer-wise manner, tracking how perturbations propagate through successive neighborhood aggregation steps; as a result, existing bounds can grow exponentially with depth due to repeated compositions of nonlinear maps
(
18
)
. Importantly, this apparent discrepancy is largely driven by architectural conventions rather than a fundamental difference between MPNNs and spectral GNNs. If MPNNs are modified so that nonlinearities are applied only every
K
layers, separating aggregation from nonlinear transformation, the resulting stability behavior aligns closely with that of spectral GNNs using degree-
K
polynomial filters. Once differences in depth and nonlinearities are properly accounted for, the stability guarantees derived in both frameworks are consistent.

GNNs are defined by the architecture’s parameters, thus, by utilizing the same coefficients, GNNs can be deployed on graphs of different number of nodes. Related to
stability
is
size transferability
, which studies how the output of a GNN changes as the number of nodes in the graph grows.

In this field, spectral GNNs analyze graphs from the spectrum of the convolution
(
56
;
85
;
55
, e.g.,)
, whereas MPNN analyzes focus on the operator distances (e.g.,
53
, Graphop:
;
49
, similarity kernel:
; and

;
58
;
65
, graphons:
). In the case of spectral GNNs, the proofs typically rely on connecting the graph convolution with a continuous convolution through the spectrum of the filter
(
86
;
84
;
105
;
107
)
.

On the other hand, size transferability for MPNNs is typically derived utilizing operator theory – which as opposed to spectral convergence, does not require dense graphs models
(
53
)
.
To compare MPNNs acting on graphs of different sizes, the graph is viewed as an
operator
derived from the set of graphs considered. Given the dense nature of the graph models considered by the spectral theory, and the fact that MPNN theory allows for sparse graphs, there seems to be an upper hand for the MPNN theory.
But, when looking closer at the assumptions (Lipschitz, well behaved operators), and conclusions derived by the theories, there exists an almost one to one mapping between assumptions and the bounds are very similar.

Generalization

In machine learning, generalization refers to how well a model’s performance on the training data reflects its performance on unseen data. It is a key problem, as it relies on assessing the model’s ability to go beyond memorizing the training samples and to discover patterns and rules hidden in the data. For MPNNs, generalization analyses typically assume uniformly bounded node features
(
67
)
, normalized or degree-controlled aggregation schemes
(
81
;
65
)
, and Lipschitz-continuous message and update functions with uniformly bounded constants across layers
(
95
)
, or bounded coloring numbers
(
74
)
; see
(
96
)
for an overview. Under these assumptions, MPNNs define hypothesis classes consisting of functions that are Lipschitz continuous with respect to suitable (pseudo-)metrics defined on spaces of graphs.

As a consequence, generalization can be controlled by the capacity of the underlying graph space with respect to these metrics, for instance, through covering number bounds
(
57
;
95
)
, uniform approximation arguments
(
67
)
, or complexity measures such as VC dimension
(
74
)
or Rademacher
(
29
)

Spectral GNNs define hypotheses via linear operators of the form

\bm{L}_{G}
is the graph Laplacian. Generalization guarantees in this setting rely on analogous regularity assumptions, including uniform bounds on

(

)

(

)

(

3.5
P5: Spectral positional encodings (SPEs) are distinct tools that expand expressivity. GNNs can be used to generate them

Spectral positional encodings (SPEs) play a central role in modern graph Transformers
(
23
;
80
)
and in extending the expressive power of GNNs. They are often implicitly associated with spectral GNNs, since many widely used constructions rely on eigenvectors of GSOs such as the graph Laplacian or random-walk matrix. Concretely, let
\bm{S}=\bm{V}\bm{\Lambda}\bm{V}^{\top}
as in
Section

2
. Then the
i-
th row of
\bm{V}
or
\bm{V}\bm{\Lambda}
can be used as the PE for the
i
-th node of the graph,

\text{SPE}_{i}=\bm{V}[i,:]~\text{or SPE}_{i}=\bm{V}[i,:]\bm{\Lambda}
.
We argue that this association is conceptually misleading. SPEs constitute an input representation choice that is orthogonal to whether the backbone architecture is formulated as a message-passing or as a spectral operator. Consequently, a substantial fraction of the empirical gains attributed to certain GNN designs is driven by the choice of positional encodings (PEs) rather than by the parametrization of the graph convolution itself.

Without PEs or other feature augmentations, both MPNNs and spectral GNNs are subject to comparable expressivity limitations (
P1
). In contrast, PEs can inject global structural information or node identifiers that fundamentally change what a model can distinguish. This makes them a dominant factor in performance and generalization, and implies that comparisons between different GNN architectures can be easily confounded unless the PE mechanism is explicitly controlled. From a unifying perspective, PEs should therefore be treated as a separate and explicit design axis, alongside the choice of GNN backbone.

Limitations of SPEs

A central difficulty is that many commonly used SPEs fail to meet basic symmetry and stability requirements. Eigenvectors are defined only up to sign and, in the presence of repeated eigenvalues, up to arbitrary orthonormal transformations within eigenspaces. As a result, standard eigenvector-based PEs are not permutation equivariant and can change drastically under small graph perturbations, potentially harming generalization
(
19
)
. Recent work shows that these limitations can be addressed by enforcing invariance to sign flips and basis rotations, yielding permutation-equivariant and more stable SPEs
(
60
;
42
)
.

Generating SPEs with GNNs.

We claim that SPEs need not be introduced solely through explicit eigendecompositions, but can be implicitly generated by standard GNN architectures themselves. In particular, message-passing updates of the form
\bm{X}^{(l)}=\rho\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\bm{S}^{k}\bm{X}^{(l-1)}\bm{H}_{k}^{(l)}\right)
can be cast as a nonlinear function of eigenvectors
\bm{x}_{v}^{(l)}=\rho\left(\bm{W}^{T}\bm{V}[v,:]\right)

(
45
)
, showing that widely used architectures such as GCNs, GINs, and GraphSAGE implicitly construct spectral positional information (for more details see
Appendix

G
). Recent works
(
42
;
45
)
further demonstrate that spectral graph filters and MPNNs can be used to construct expressive, stable, and permutation-equivariant PEs. Together, these observations suggest a conceptual shift: rather than treating SPEs as a separate preprocessing step, GNNs can be viewed as learning data-dependent spectral representations whose form, expressiveness, and generalization behavior depend jointly on the initial node features
(
62
;
1
;
88
;
46
;
47
)
and the network parameters. We advocate a modular perspective in which one component of the model is responsible for constructing equivariant and stable positional information, while another, whether message-passing or spectral, uses this information for downstream prediction.

4
Alternative views

A direct alternative to our position is to posit that MPNN and spectral GNN designs should not be unified. A naïve reasoning for this view would be to claim that with enough effort, one approach should emerge as superior to the other. However, as discussed in
P2
and
P3
, each has its own benefits and use cases where it provides a more convenient starting point.

One could also posit that competition leads to vibrant discussion, which—even in adversarial form—is instrumental for progress that would not be achieved otherwise. However, as discussed in
P4
, the (somewhat artificial) divide between the two sides of the graph ML community creates a certain degree of duplication of results, derived with different techniques or nuances, yet ultimately supporting a similar understanding of GNN capabilities. A concrete example of a resulting downside is the preoccupation in the study of spectral GNNs with polynomial filters that mimic MPNN properties, as discussed in
P1
, rather than focusing on generalizing filter design beyond MPNN confines.

One could nevertheless argue that, even with the best of intentions, these approaches originate from disparate backgrounds and employ fundamentally different techniques that cannot be harmonized. However, the expressivity analysis in
P1
indicates that the function spaces parameterized by GNNs of both types have a significant overlap, sufficient to merit the effort to distill a common understanding of the similarities and differences.

Beyond these alternatives, focused on merits and feasibility, one could also comment on the prudence of pursuing such efforts. We believe the motivation presented here is sufficiently appealing, stemming from the need for coherent guidelines that ensure results are independent of parametrization and that identify the more fruitful avenues to pursue under the premise of a particular approach. However, for completeness, we include further discussion of this alternative perspective in
Appendix

B
.

5
Collaboration roadmap and vision

Moving forward
, we aim for a unified, operator-level theory of graph learning—spanning expressivity, generalization/stability, and optimization/convergence—that does not treat “spectral” and “spatial” approaches as fundamentally different objects, but as different parametrizations of the same core problem, namely learning permutation-equivariant operators for function approximation on graphs. As a roadmap, we propose: (i) adopting an operator-centric glossary as a “Rosetta stone” to reconcile terminology and make assumptions (e.g., about GSOs, bases, and PEs) directly comparable and understable across communities; (ii) building benchmarks with controlled GSOs that specifically probe oversmoothing/oversquashing, community detection, or directionality/link prediction, so we can empirically identify when one viewpoint offers genuine advantages; (iii) systematically exploring and analyzing hybrids—spectral filters with MPNN updates, learned GSOs under principled constraints, and spectral PEs inside MPNNs; and (iv) developing theory that matches the object of study, i.e., not collapsing spectral models into the polynomial/message-passing regime but focus on spectral analysis, thereby producing insights that are truly spectral yet still compatible with a common language.

Appendix B
Alternative views on the prudence and necessity of a unified framework

Our primary position here is that research into graph representation learning would benefit from prioritizing a formalism that unifies MPNN and spectral approaches to graph neural network derivation, essentially distilling the overlap where they are equivalent, especially in practical settings, while retaining their complementary advantages (both conceptually and algorithmically). Direct contrarian views to to our position were discussed in Sec.
4
, focusing on whether it is feasible or beneficial to unify these approaches into a harmonized framework.

On the other hand, a more indirect form of alternative views to our position would be to argue whether this should be a primary focus of the relevant research communities, regardless of whether they can or should be studied jointly. For example, positional encodings have garnered increasing interest in recent years, including the derivation of spectral ones as informative node features (or graph signals) to improve GNN performance, regardless of particular designs. However, as discussed in Sec.
3.5
, we regard these as independent of our view, and posit that their benefits do not detract from the importance of elucidating the relations between GNN designs. Furthermore, trained GNNs can also inform and contribute to data-driven formulation of positional encodings
(
42
;
45
;
14
, e.g.,)
, thus providing further motivation to pursue both avenues.

Similarly, one could assert that fundamentally, geometric deep learning aims to learn sequences of transformations that fully encode geometric information into node or graph features – whether done via filtration, message passing, or positional encoding – which can then be processed via task-relevant (often fully connected) readout layers. Hence, one could posit that the research focus should be devoted to data-driven learning of such transformations, e.g., via graph transformers and attention mechanisms, which would then be expected to be sufficiently adaptive to mimic any a priori design beneficial for a particular task. However, graph transformers are not easily trained, and often still work under particular GNN parametrization (i.e., in the spatial or spectral domain), and their study would benefit from unified understating of information propagation approaches over graphs. More generally, abstract encoding of information geometry is a challenging task, and pursuing a unified framework as we advocate here would promote deeper understanding of our ability to approach it with current deep learning tools.

Finally, it should be mentioned that, as is the case in various other ML fields or data types, one could argue that focused study of graphs in particular may soon become deprecated given the increasing success of LLMs in increasingly complex reasoning tasks. He find that a full discussion of this view is out of scope for this position paper. Nevertheless, we remark that so far, the demonstrated capabilities of such models (while impressive) do not reach a level where they can reliably and fully supersede more nuanced or tailored graph processing approaches, and do not nullify the importance of and interest in graph machine learning and geometric deep learning.

Norms.

For a vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the Euclidean norm and the infinity norm are defined as
\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\coloneq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
and

. For a matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}

\|\bm{M}\|_{F}\coloneq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}M_{ij}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\|\bm{M}\|_{2}\coloneq\sup_{\bm{x}\neq\bm{0}}\frac{\|\bm{M}\bm{x}\|_{2}}{\|\bm{x}\|_{2}}
and
\|\bm{M}\|_{\infty}\coloneq\max_{i\in[n]}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|M_{ij}|
.

Metric spaces and continuity.

A
metric space
is a pair
(\mathcal{X},d)
where
\mathcal{X}
is a set and
d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfies for all
x,y,z\in\mathcal{X}
:
(i)
d(x,y)=0
iff
x=y
,
(ii)
d(x,y)=d(y,x)
,
(iii)
d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)
.
Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be two metric spaces.
A function
f\colon(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
is
L
-Lipschitz
if

d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq L\,d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
. A function
f\colon(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
is
uniformly continuous
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that

d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))<\varepsilon
whenever
d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})<\delta
, for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
. Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces, and let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of functions
f\colon(X,d_{X})\to(Y,d_{Y})
. The family
\mathcal{F}
is said to be
equicontinuous
if for every
\varepsilon>0

there exists
\delta>0
such that

d_{Y}\bigl(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\bigr)<\varepsilon\quad\text{whenever}\quad d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})<\delta,

for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
and all
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

Remark 1
.

Uniform continuity can equivalently be characterized by the existence of a non-decreasing function
\omega\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
with
\omega(0)=0
and
\lim_{t\to 0}\omega(t)=0
such that
d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq\omega\big(d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})\big)
for all

L
-Lipschitz functions are uniformly continuous with
\omega(t)=Lt
.

Remark 2
.

Equivalently,
\mathcal{F}
is equicontinuous if there exists a non-decreasing function

\omega\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
with
\omega(0)=0
and
\lim_{t\to 0}\omega(t)=0

d_{Y}\bigl(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\bigr)\leq\omega\bigl(d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})\bigr)\quad\text{for all }x,x^{\prime}\in X\text{ and all }f\in\mathcal{F}.

MLPs

A multilayer perceptron (MLP) is a feedforward neural network obtained by composing affine maps with pointwise nonlinearities. Given an input
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
, an
L

\bm{x}^{(t+1)}=\rho\left(\bm{W}_{t}\bm{x}^{(t)}+\bm{b}_{t}\right)
for

,
where
\bm{W}_{t}
and
\bm{b}_{t}
are weight matrices and bias vectors, and

is a non linear function applied componentwise (e.g., ReLu, LeakyReLu, softmax).

Graph shift operators

Given a graph
G
, a

is any matrix
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
that in some sense respects the connectivity of the graph. Note that this is not a rigorous definition.
Some examples of self-adjoint GSOs are the
combinatorial Laplacian

\bm{L}_{\mathrm{comb}}=\bm{D}-\bm{A}
, the
symmetric normalzied Laplacian

\bm{L}_{\mathrm{sym}}=\bm{D}^{-1/2}\bm{L}_{\mathrm{comb}}\bm{D}^{-1/2}
, and the adjacency matrix itself
\bm{A}
. One can also define non-self-adjoint GSOs, like the
random walk Laplacian

\bm{L}_{\mathrm{rw}}=\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{L}_{\mathrm{comb}}
or the unitary
Cayley shift operator

\bm{C}=(\bm{L}-ic\bm{I})(\bm{L}+ic\bm{I})^{-1}
, where
\bm{L}
is any self-adjoint GSO and
c>0
.

Graph Fourier transform.

Any self-adjoint GSO
\bm{S}
has an orthonormal eigendecomposition

\bm{S}=\bm{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\bm{V}^{\top}
, where the columns
\bm{v}_{n}
of
\bm{V}
are the eigenvectors, interpreted as the Fourier modes, and
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is a diagonal matrix of the real eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}
, interpreted as the frequencies, or rates of oscillation of the Fourier modes. The
Graph Fourier Transform (GFT)
maps spatial signals
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
to their frequency coefficients

\mathcal{F}_{\bm{S}}(\bm{X})\coloneqq\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{X}.

The inverse GFT is given by
\bm{V}\bm{Y}
for frequency coefficients
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, and the Fourier inversion formula reads
\bm{X}=\bm{V}\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{X}
.

Appendix D
Derivation of MPNNs and spectral GNNs from
equivariant machine learning

This appendix provides a more detailed discussion of message passing neural networks (MPNNs) and spectral graph neural networks (GNNs), with a particular focus on their underlying symmetry principles and invariance requirements. We present two distinct conceptual motivations for these two classes of architectures: MPNNs arise naturally from permutation-equivariant processing of neighborhoods, while spectral GNNs are motivated by invariance with respect to the choice of eigenbases of graph operators. In addition, we discuss alternative viewpoints, modeling choices, and definitions that appear in the literature, highlighting both common formulations and less standard extensions. This extended discussion complements the concise definitions given in the main text and clarifies the scope of architectures considered in this work.

D.1
Definition of MPNNs

We present one way to conceptually derive MPNNs from symmetry-preserving design principles.
Given a graph
G
with node features
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
(assuming
V(G)=[N]
), consider the collection of neighborhoods
\{N_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
.
For each neighborhood
N_{i}
, define the multiset of neighbor features

\bm{Y}_{i}\coloneq\{\{\bm{X}_{j}\mid j\in N_{i}\}\}.

The collection
\{(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{i})\}_{i\in[N]}
can be viewed as a representation of the graph-feature pair
(G,\bm{X})
.
An MPNN layer updates the feature at vertex
i
as a function of
(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{i})
, shared across vertices.

Two symmetry requirements arise naturally:
(1) the ordering of vertices
[N]
is arbitrary, hence the overall mapping should be equivariant to permutations of vertices;
(2) within each neighborhood, the ordering of neighbors is arbitrary, hence the neighborhood map should be invariant to permutations of the multiset
\bm{Y}_{i}
.
Concretely, for any permutation
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{K}
with
K=|N_{i}|
, the neighborhood map
\psi
should satisfy

\psi(\bm{X}_{i},\sigma\bm{Y}_{i})=\psi(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{i}).

A classical construction for permutation-invariant multiset functions is given by the
DeepSets
formulation

\psi(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{i})=\phi\left(\bm{X}_{i},\sum_{\bm{y}\in\bm{Y}_{i}}\kappa(\bm{y})\right),

(3)

where
\kappa
and
\phi
are suitable neural networks.

This formulation directly leads to the standard class of message passing architectures commonly referred to as sum-aggregation MPNNs, which take the form

\bm{x}_{u}^{(t+1)}\coloneqq\phi_{t}\left(\bm{x}_{u}^{(t)},\sum_{v\in N_{G}(u)}\kappa_{t}(\bm{x}_{v}^{(t)})\right),

(4)

for all
u\in V(G)
and
t\in[L]
. Here,
\kappa_{t}
and
\phi_{t}
are learnable, parameterized functions, such as feed-forward neural networks.

Interpreting the sum as an aggregation function suggests a more general update–aggregate template,

\psi(\bm{X}_{i},\bm{Y}_{i})=\text{UPD}\left(\bm{X}_{i},\text{AGG}\bigl(\{\{\bm{y}\mid\bm{y}\in\bm{Y}_{i}\}\}\bigr)\right),

(5)

where
AGG
may be sum, mean, max, or other permutation-invariant aggregators.

Applying
Equation

5
at all nodes yields the standard MPNN update rule: at layer
t
, define
\bm{Y}_{i}^{(t)}=\{\{\bm{x}_{j}^{(t)}\mid j\in N_{i}\}\}
and set

\bm{x}_{i}^{(t+1)}=\psi_{t}(\bm{x}_{i}^{(t)},\bm{Y}_{i}^{(t)})
for a shared map
\psi_{t}
.

D.2
Definition of spectral GNNs

Let
\bm{S}
be a self-adjoint graph shift operator associated with
(G,\bm{X})
.
A spectral GNN layer is defined as a function that processes the signal
\bm{X}
in the spectral domain and maps the result back to the signal domain.
If
\bm{S}=\bm{V}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\bm{V}^{\top}
is an orthonormal eigendecomposition, one may consider the generic form

\psi(\bm{S},\bm{V},\bm{X})\coloneqq\bm{V}\,\hat{\psi}\left(\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{X}\right),

(6)

where
\hat{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times d^{\prime}}
acts on the spectral coefficients.

However,
Equation

6
is not sufficient to define a graph-consistent operation, because
\bm{S}
can admit multiple valid eigenbases:
eigenvectors are defined up to sign, and in the presence of repeated eigenvalues, any orthonormal basis of an eigenspace is admissible.
Therefore, for
\psi
to depend only on
(\bm{S},\bm{X})
(and not on arbitrary eigendecomposition choices), it should be invariant to the chosen eigenbasis.
Concretely, for any two eigenbases
\bm{V}
and
\bm{V}^{\prime}
of
\bm{S}
, we require

\forall\,\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}:\quad\psi(\bm{S},\bm{V},\bm{X})=\psi(\bm{S},\bm{V}^{\prime},\bm{X}).

(7)

A classical approach is to restrict
\hat{\psi}
to be diagonal in the eigenbasis.
In the one-dimensional case (
d=1
), this means

\hat{\psi}(\bm{Y})\coloneqq h(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\bm{Y},\qquad h(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})=\text{diag}\bigl(h(\lambda_{1}),\ldots,h(\lambda_{N})\bigr),

for a scalar function
h:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then
\psi(\bm{S},\bm{X})=h(\bm{S})\bm{X}=\bm{V}h(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\bm{V}^{\top}\bm{X}
.

In the case of higher-dimensional initial node features (often referred to as the multichannel setting in spectral GNNs) this construction naturally extends to

\forall\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d},\quad\psi(\bm{S},\bm{X})=H(\bm{S})\bm{X}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}\,H(\lambda_{i})^{\top},

(8)

where the frequency response is matrix-valued,
H:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times d}
. Such filters are called functional calculus filters since they define an operator-valued function of
\bm{S}
through its spectrum. Moreover,
Equation

8
satisfies the invariance requirement
Equation

7
: within each eigenspace, the sum of projectors
\sum_{i:\lambda_{i}=\lambda}\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}
is the orthogonal projector onto that eigenspace and is independent of the particular eigenbasis.

In practice, spectral GNNs are commonly built by stacking such spectral filters with trainable frequency responses, adding a bias, and applying a pointwise nonlinearity, recovering the spectral GNN formulation introduced in
Section

2
via
Equation

2
. A frequent choice is to parametrize
H
as a polynomial, which permits implementing
H(\bm{S})\bm{X}
directly in the spatial domain as a finite combination of powers of
\bm{S}
. This yields message-passing style implementations as analyzed in
Section

E.1
.

Alternative constructions

There are also alternative approaches to ensure eigenbasis invariance. For example, nonlinear spectral filters (NLSF)
(
61
)
are defined by

\psi(\bm{X})\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Big((\bm{v}_{n}\bm{v}_{n}^{\top}\bm{X})./|\bm{v}_{n}^{\top}\bm{X}|\Big)\,\psi_{n}\Big(|\bm{v}_{j}^{\top}\bm{X}|\Big)_{j=1}^{N},

(9)

where
\psi_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d^{\prime}}
is a trainable function,
|\bm{v}_{n}^{\top}\bm{X}|=(|\bm{v}_{n}^{\top}\bm{X}_{:,j}|)_{j=1}^{d}
, and
./
denotes elementwise division.

Here,
(\bm{v}_{n}\bm{v}_{n}^{\top}\bm{X})./|\bm{v}_{n}^{\top}\bm{X}|
is a consistent choice of the eigenvectors that depends only on the data, in case all eigenvalues have multiplicity 1. Indeed,
\bm{v}_{n}\bm{v}_{n}^{\top}
is the eigenspace projection and does not depend on the arbitrary choice of
\bm{v}_{n}
. Equation (
9
) has an extension for any eigenvalue multiplicities
(
61

Remark 6
.

If one allows spectral GNN architectures to use general activation functions—so that different layers and channels may employ different nonlinearities—then the implementation of an MPNN via a polynomial GNN becomes significantly simpler.

Next, we show that any MPNN with sum aggregation and general continuous message and update functions can be uniformly approximated by a polynomial GNN.

Theorem 7

E.3
MPNNs Expressivity via 1-WL

Graph isomorphism tests aim to decide whether two graphs are isomorphic, i.e., whether there exists a bijection
between their node sets that preserves adjacency.
Such tests typically proceed by iteratively refining node labels based on local graph structure, thereby inducing an equivalence relation on the space of graphs. A central example is the
1
-dimensional Weisfeiler–Lehman (
1
-WL) test (also known as color refinement)
The
1
-dimensional Weisfeiler–Lehman (
1
-WL) test, also known as color refinement, is an iterative graph isomorphism heuristic that assigns and refines node labels based on local neighborhood structure. Starting from an initial labeling
\ell^{(0)}:V(G)\to\Sigma
, where
\Sigma
is a countable set of labels, the test proceeds in rounds
t\geq 1
by updating each node label according to

\ell^{(t)}(v)=\text{HASH}\!\left(\ell^{(t-1)}(v),\{\{\ell^{(t-1)}(u)\mid u\in N_{G}(v)\}\}\right),

where
HASH
is an injective function on its arguments and
\{\{\cdot\}\}
denotes a multiset.
If the graphs are equipped with initial node features, the initialization
\ell^{(0)}
is taken to be any labeling that is consistent with these features, i.e., nodes with identical features receive the same initial label. Two graphs are declared non-isomorphic if the multisets of node labels differ at any iteration; otherwise, the test cannot distinguish them.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of GNN architectures, viewed as mappings from graphs to real-valued vectors, and let
\mathcal{T}
be a graph isomorphism test inducing an equivalence relation
\sim_{\mathcal{T}}
on graphs. We compare the expressive power of
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{T}
as follows:

•

\mathcal{F}
is not less expressive than
\mathcal{T}
if, for any graphs
G
and
H
with

G\not\sim_{\mathcal{T}}H
, there exists a GNN
\Phi\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\Phi(G)\neq\Phi(H)
.

•

\mathcal{F}
is not more expressive than
\mathcal{T}
if, for any graphs
G
and
H
with

G\sim_{\mathcal{T}}H
, we have
\Phi(G)=\Phi(H)
for all
\Phi\in\mathcal{F}
.

•

\mathcal{F}
is as expressive as
\mathcal{T}
if it is both not less expressive and not more
expressive than
\mathcal{T}
.

Below we state a central theorem connecting the expressive power of MPNNs as as defined in
Equation

10
with that of the
1
-WL test.

Theorem 8

(
(
75
;
112
)
)
.

Let any
L\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\text{GIN},L}
denotes the class of MPNNs whose layers are of the form of
Equation

10
, i.,e.,

\bm{x}_{i}^{(t)}=\phi^{(t)}\left(\bm{x}_{i}^{(t-1)},\sum_{j\in N(i)}\kappa^{(t)}(\bm{x}_{j}^{(t-1)})\right),

followed by a sum-pooling operator over final node feature to produce a graph-level representation. Then
\mathcal{F}_{\text{GIN},L}
is as expressive as the
1
-dimensional Weisfeiler–Lehman (
1
-WL) graph isomorphism test after
L
iterations

E.4
Aggregation graph shift operators

Since there is no formal mathematical definition that determines which operator constitutes a graph shift operator (GSO) of a given graph, one can construct exotic GSOs that allow spectral GNNs to break any given graph isomorphism test. Such constructions may encode global information directly into the operator, thereby artificially increasing the expressive power of the resulting spectral architecture.

To enable a meaningful comparison of the expressive power of MPNNs and spectral GNNs, we must exclude graph shift operators that explicitly encode global structural information. To this end, we restrict our attention to GSOs that can be computed from the adjacency matrix using only local operations. We refer to such operators as aggregation graph shift operators, defined below.

Definition 9

(Aggregation graph shift operator)
.

Let
G
be a graph with
N
nodes.
A matrix
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is called an
aggregation graph shift operator
if, for any signal
\bm{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the mapping

\bm{f}\longmapsto\;\bm{S}\bm{f}

can be implemented by a sum-aggregation message passing neural network with finitely many layers applied to the signal
\bm{f}
.

One can include an additional constraint on the definition of an aggregation GSO: that only entries such that
A_{i,j}\neq 0
can be nonzero in the GSO. However, even without this additional restriction, spectral GNNs based on aggregation GSOs are no more expressive than 1-WL, as it will become clear in
Section

E.5
. Since the additional restriction on the support of the GSO plays no role in the analysis, we omit it from the formal definition of aggregation GSO.

Although this definition may at first appear tailored to align MPNNs and spectral GNNs, we show below that all commonly used GSOs in the literature satisfy it. In particular, standard choices such as the adjacency matrix, the combinatorial and normalized graph Laplacians, as well as Cayley shift operators (via their Jacobi approximations), are all aggregation graph shift operators in
the sense of
Definition

9
.

Example 10
.

We give several examples of common graph shift operators that can be implemented by MPNNs with sum aggregation. Note that the signal
\bm{1}
can always be concatenated to
\bm{f}
by an MPNN. Indeed, as the first layer, just define
\phi^{(1)}(x,y)=(x,1)
.

•

The degree matrix
\bm{D}\coloneq\text{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{N})
, with
d_{i}=|N(i)|
, for
i\in[N]
can be implemented by a three-layer MPNN, as we show next.
Without loss of generality, assume
x_{i}^{(0)}=f_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
The mapping
\bm{f}\mapsto\bm{D}\bm{f}
can be implemented in three layers as follows:

x_{i}^{(1)}=\phi^{(1)}(f_{i},0)=(f_{i},1),

x_{i}^{(2)}=\phi^{(2)}\Bigl((f_{i},1),\sum_{j\in N(i)}(0,1)\Bigr)=\phi^{(2)}\bigl((f_{i},1),(0,d_{i})\bigr)=(d_{i},f_{i}),

x_{i}^{(3)}=\phi^{(3)}\bigl((d_{i},f_{i}),0\bigr)=d_{i}f_{i}=(\bm{D}\bm{f})_{i},

•

The combinatorial Laplacian
\bm{L}=\bm{D}-\bm{A}
can be implemented by a four-layer MPNN. Starting from
x_{i}^{(0)}=f_{i}
, the first three layers compute in parallel

x_{i}^{(1)}=(f_{i},1),\qquad x_{i}^{(3)}=\bigl((\bm{D}\bm{f})_{i},(\bm{A}\bm{f})_{i}\bigr),

where
(\bm{D}\bm{f})_{i}=d_{i}f_{i}
is obtained as in the previous example, and

(\bm{A}\bm{f})_{i}=\sum_{j\in N(i)}f_{j}

is computed by sum aggregation. The fourth layer applies a linear update function,

x_{i}^{(4)}=\phi^{(4)}\bigl((d_{i}f_{i},\,(\bm{A}\bm{f})_{i}),0\bigr)=d_{i}f_{i}-(\bm{A}\bm{f})_{i}=(\bm{L}\bm{f})_{i},

thereby implementing the combinatorial Laplacian.

•

The symmetric normalized Laplacian
\bm{L}_{\mathrm{sym}}=\bm{D}^{-1/2}\bm{L}\bm{D}^{-1/2}
can be implemented by a finite-depth MPNN. Starting from
x_{i}^{(0)}=f_{i}
, the degree signal
d_{i}
is first computed as above and used to form

x_{i}^{(1)}=(d_{i}^{-1/2}f_{i}),

via a pointwise transformation. Next, applying the construction for the combinatorial Laplacian yields

x_{i}^{(2)}=(\bm{L}(\bm{D}^{-1/2}\bm{f}))_{i}.

Finally, a further pointwise layer multiplies the result by
d_{i}^{-1/2}
,

x_{i}^{(3)}=d_{i}^{-1/2}(\bm{L}(\bm{D}^{-1/2}\bm{f}))_{i}=(\bm{L}_{\mathrm{sym}}\bm{f})_{i},

•

Cayley shift operators implemented via finite Jacobi approximations are aggregation graph shift operators.
The Jacobi approximation expresses the Cayley transform as a finite combination of repeated applications of a base graph shift operator, together with pointwise linear transformations. Since each application of the shift operator corresponds to a sum-aggregation message passing layer, the resulting mapping can be implemented by a finite-depth MPNN. Hence, Jacobi approximated Cayley shift operators satisfy
Definition

9
.

E.5
Expressivity of spectral GNNs with aggregation GSOs

In this section, we build on the results in
Section

E.1
,
Section

E.2
to analyze the expressive power of spectral GNNs. We show that spectral GNNs with aggregation GSOs and arbitrary continuous spectral filters cannot be more expressive
than the
1
-WL test. Moreover, we show that by using the adjacency matrix as the GSO together with polynomial filters of degree at most
1
, spectral GNNs can exactly match the expressive power of
1
-WL. The latter result follows from the fact that spectral GNNs of this type can uniformly approximate any MPNN of the form defined in
Equation

10
, combined with the equivalence between MPNNs and
1
-WL stated in
Theorem

8
.

We begin with the following lemma, which is the key ingredient for establishing an upper bound on the expressive power of spectral GNNs.

Lemma 11
.

Spectral GNNs with polynomial filters based on aggregation GSOs are special cases of message passing neural networks
with sum aggregation.

Proof.

Let
\bm{S}
be an aggregation GSO.
By the definition of aggregation GSO (see
Definition

9
), there exists a finite-depth message passing neural network with sum aggregation, denoted by
\mathrm{MPNN}_{\bm{S}}
, such that for every node signal
\bm{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

(\mathrm{MPNN}_{\bm{S}}(\bm{f}))_{i}=(\bm{S}\bm{f})_{i},

where
(\mathrm{MPNN}_{\bm{S}}(\bm{f}))_{i}
denotes the output of
\mathrm{MPNN}_{\bm{S}}
at node
i
.
Let
T_{\bm{S}}\in\mathbb{N}
denote the (finite) depth of
\mathrm{MPNN}_{\bm{S}}
.

Consider a polynomial spectral layer of degree
K\in\mathbb{N}
, omitting bias terms for simplicity:

\bm{h}=\rho\bigl(p(\bm{S})\bm{f}\bigr),\qquad p(\bm{S})=\sum_{k=0}^{K}a_{k}\bm{S}^{k},

where
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a pointwise nonlinearity.

Define
\bm{z}^{(0)}\coloneq\bm{f}
and recursively

\bm{z}^{(k)}\coloneq\mathrm{MPNN}_{\bm{S}}(\bm{z}^{(k-1)}),\qquad k=1,\dots,K.

By induction, we have
\bm{z}^{(k)}=\bm{S}^{k}\bm{f}
for all

.
This construction requires finite depth
KT_{\bm{S}}

Using feature concatenation, which can be realized by choosing update maps that ignore the aggregated argument, we maintain the channels

(\bm{z}^{(0)},\dots,\bm{z}^{(K)}).

We then apply a pointwise linear map to compute

\bm{u}\coloneq\sum_{k=0}^{K}a_{k}\bm{z}^{(k)}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}a_{k}\bm{S}^{k}\bm{f}=p(\bm{S})\bm{f}.

Finally, we apply the nonlinearity
\rho
by choosing the update map

\phi(u,y)=\rho(u),

so that the output satisfies
\bm{h}=\rho(\bm{u})=\rho(p(\bm{S})\bm{f})
.
Hence, the polynomial spectral layer can be implemented by a finite-depth MPNN with sum aggregation.
∎

We are now ready to characterize the expressive power of spectral GNNs with aggregation GSOs and polynomial filters.

Proposition 12
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{spec}}(\bm{S})

1

-WL. It remains to prove that

We now extend the previous result from polynomial spectral filters to general continuous frequency responses. This follows from the fact that continuous filters can be uniformly approximated by polynomial ones on the spectrum of the two graph shift operators.

Corollary 13

(Expressive power of functional-calculus spectral GNNs)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\bm{S})
denote the class of spectral GNNs defined via functional calculus, i.e.,networks whose layers are of the form
Equation

11

\bm{X}^{(t)}=\rho\Big(H_{t}(\bm{S})\bm{X}^{(t-1)}+\bm{b}^{(t)}\bm{1}^{\top}\Big),

followed by a sum-pooling operator over node features to produce a graph-level representation. Where each frequency response

H_{t}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}\times d_{t-1}}

\mathcal{F}_{\text{C}}(\bm{S})
is as expressive as
1
-WL.

We now formalize the approximation argument underlying the proof of
Corollary

13
. The key idea is that, since spectral GNNs apply continuous frequency responses to the spectrum of the graph shift operator, each such layer can be uniformly approximated by a polynomial spectral filter on the spectrum of
\bm{S}
. We first show that continuous filters admit uniform polynomial approximations on the spectrum, then we control how these perturbations propagate through the nonlinear layers of a spectral GNN. Finally, we state the main theorem characterizing the expressive power of spectral GNNs with aggregation GSOs and arbitrary continuous filters. We begin with the following three important lemmas.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\bm{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
be diagonalizable with real spectrum
\sigma(\bm{S})=\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}\}\subseteq[a,b]
. Let
H\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times d}
be continuous. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a matrix-valued polynomial
p(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{K}z^{k}\bm{C}_{k}
such that

\sup_{z\in[a,b]}\|H(z)-p(z)\|_{2}<\varepsilon.

In particular,

\max_{i\in[N]}\|H(\lambda_{i})-p(\lambda_{i})\|_{2}<\varepsilon.

Proof.

By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, each scalar-valued continuous function on
[a,b]
can be uniformly approximated by polynomials. Apply this entrywise to the matrix-valued function
H
to obtain a matrix-valued polynomial
p
such that
\sup_{z\in[a,b]}\|H(z)-p(z)\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
. Since all norms on a finite-dimensional space are equivalent, we may replace
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
by the
2
-norm, up to adjusting
\varepsilon
by a constant factor depending only on
d
and
d^{\prime}
. This yields the claimed bound in operator norm.
∎

Lemma 15
.

Let
\bm{S}
be symmetric with eigendecomposition
\bm{S}=\bm{V}\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N})\bm{V}^{\top}
.
Let
H,p:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times d}
be such that

\max_{i\in[N]}\|H(\lambda_{i})-p(\lambda_{i})\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon.

Then for matrix
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
,

\|H(\bm{S})\bm{X}-p(\bm{S})\bm{X}\|_{F}\leq\varepsilon\|\bm{X}\|_{F}.

Proof.

We have

H(\bm{S})\bm{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}\,H(\lambda_{i})^{\top},\qquad p(\bm{S})\bm{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}\,p(\lambda_{i})^{\top},

and thus

\big(H(\bm{S})-p(\bm{S})\big)\bm{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}\,\big(H(\lambda_{i})-p(\lambda_{i})\big)^{\top}.

Since the projectors
\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}
are orthogonal and sum to the identity, we obtain

\|\big(H(\bm{S})-p(\bm{S})\big)\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}\,\big(H(\lambda_{i})-p(\lambda_{i})\big)^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}.

Using
\|AB\|_{F}\leq\|A\|_{F}\|B\|_{\mathrm{op}}
and the assumption gives

\|\big(H(\bm{S})-p(\bm{S})\big)\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2}=\varepsilon^{2}\|\bm{X}\|_{F}^{2},

which proves the claim.
∎

Lemma 16
.

Let
\rho
be
L_{\rho}
-Lipschitz.
Consider two spectral layers with the same
\bm{S}
, weights, and biases, but different frequency responses
H
and
p
:

\bm{Y}=\rho\big(H(\bm{S})\bm{X}+\bm{b}\bm{1}^{\top}\big),\qquad\widetilde{\bm{Y}}=\rho\big(p(\bm{S})\bm{X}+\bm{b}\bm{1}^{\top}\big).

Then

\|\bm{Y}-\widetilde{\bm{Y}}\|_{F}\leq L_{\rho}\|\big(H(\bm{S})-p(\bm{S})\big)\bm{X}\|_{F}.

In particular, under the assumptions of
Lemma

15
,

\|\bm{Y}-\widetilde{\bm{Y}}\|_{F}\leq L_{\rho}\varepsilon\|\bm{X}\|_{F}.

Proof.

Since
\rho
is applied element-wise and is
L_{\rho}
-Lipschitz, it is
L_{\rho}
-Lipschitz with respect to the Frobenius norm:

\|\rho(\bm{U})-\rho(\bm{V})\|_{F}\leq L_{\rho}\|\bm{U}-\bm{V}\|_{F}.

Apply this with
\bm{U}=H(\bm{S})\bm{X}+\bm{b}\bm{1}^{\top}
and

\bm{V}=p(\bm{S})\bm{X}+\bm{b}\bm{1}^{\top}
, completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove

. The previous lemmas show that functional-calculus spectral] GNNs with continuous filters can be uniformly approximated by polynomial spectral GNNs, with controlled error across layers.

E.6
Expressivity via universal approximation

Expressivity via graph isomorphism test allows choosing a different GNN per pair of graphs. Hence, it does not model the expressivity of a fixed GNN on the whole data distribution. In this section, we propose an alternative definition of expressivity for fixed GNNs on data distributions. For simplicity, we focus on real-valued outputs, noting that the analysis extends directly to vector-valued settings.

Definition 17

(Expressivity via universal approximation)
.

Let
Q
be a probability distribution (e.g., over graphs with node features), and let

F_{1}
and
F_{2}
be families of real-valued functions defined on the support of
Q
.
We say that
F_{1}
is
not bounded by the expressivity of
F_{2}
on
Q
if there exist

f_{1}\in F_{1}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that, for every
f_{2}\in F_{2}
,

\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{q\sim Q}|f_{1}(q)-f_{2}(q)|>\varepsilon.

Equivalently,
F_{2}
is
not
a universal approximator of
F_{1}
on
Q
.

Proposition 18
.

Fix
L\geq 1
. There exists a data distribution
Q
over graphs with initial node features,
(G,\bm{X})
, such that
the family of
L
-layer polynomial-filter spectral GNNs with sum-pooling readout layer is not a universal approximator of the family of
L
-layer functional calculus spectral GNNs with sum-pooling readout layer on
Q
.

Proof.

We construct a data distribution
Q
and an
L
-layer functional-calculus spectral GNN whose

sum-pooled
input–output map cannot be uniformly approximated, in essential supremum over
Q
,
by any
L
-layer polynomial-filter spectral GNN (with ReLU activation and arbitrary biases) equipped with the same sum-pooling readout.

We begin by specifying the distribution
Q
. For each
n\geq 1
, let
G_{n}
be the complete graph on
n+1
vertices with adjacency matrix
\bm{A}_{n}
.
Its spectrum contains the eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}=n
, with associated eigenvector, the all ones vector
\bm{1}_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
Define

\bm{x}_{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n+1}\bm{1}_{n+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\bm{1}_{n+1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{n}=1.

We define
Q
by sampling
n\geq 1
with probability
\Pr(n)=2^{-n}
, setting
G=G_{n}
and
\bm{x}=\bm{x}_{n}
.
Then, for every
M>0
,
Q
assigns positive probability to graphs whose adjacency matrices have an eigenvalue
larger than
M
.

Consider an
L
-layer functional-calculus spectral GNN (as in
Equation

2
)
with identity activation and zero biases, and with the same frequency response at every layer,
H_{t}(\lambda)=f(\lambda)=\sin\lambda.

Since
\bm{x}_{n}
is an eigenvector of
\bm{A}_{n}
associated with
\lambda_{n}=n
, we have

f(\bm{A}_{n})^{L}\bm{x}_{n}=(\sin\lambda_{n})^{L}\bm{x}_{n}=(\sin n)^{L}\bm{x}_{n}.

Define the resulting output after the sum pooling as:

F(G,\bm{x})\coloneqq\bm{1}^{\top}f(\bm{A})^{L}\bm{x},

which is precisely the composition of the
L
spectral layers with the sum-pooling readout.
On the support of
Q
, i.e., this reduces to

F(G_{n},\bm{x}_{n})=\bm{1}_{n+1}^{\top}\big((\sin n)^{L}\bm{x}_{n}\big)=(\sin n)^{L}\bm{1}_{n+1}^{\top}\bm{x}_{n}=(\sin n)^{L}.

Now fix any
L
-layer polynomial-filter spectral GNN with ReLU activation and arbitrary biases, and equip it
with the same sum-pooling readout. Denote the resulting map by
P
.
When evaluating
P
on
(G_{n},\bm{x}_{n})
, the input
\bm{x}_{n}
lies entirely in the eigenspace of
\lambda_{n}=n
.
Moreover, each polynomial spectral layer acts diagonally in the eigenbasis (as in
Equation

2
),
so all spectral contributions corresponding to eigenvalues different from
\lambda_{n}
vanish on this input.
Consequently, the final sum-pooled output depends only on
\lambda_{n}
.
That is, there exists a function
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

P(G_{n},\bm{x}_{n})=\psi(\lambda_{n})=\psi(n)\qquad\text{for all }n\geq 1.

Furthermore, since the filters are polynomial and the only nonlinearity is ReLU, the function
\psi
is
piecewise-polynomial with finitely many pieces.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
.
Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be piecewise-polynomial with finitely many pieces. Then there exists
T\in\mathbb{R}

and a polynomial
q
such that
\psi(x)=q(x)
for all
x\geq T
. If
q
is non-constant, then
|q(n)|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, so for some integer
n>T
we have
|q(n)|\geq 2
.
Since
|(\sin n)^{L}|\leq 1
, it follows that

|(\sin n)^{L}-\psi(n)|=|(\sin n)^{L}-q(n)|\geq|q(n)|-1\geq 1>\varepsilon.

It remains to examine the case where
q\equiv c
, for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
. If
c>1
, then we define
\varepsilon=c-1>0
then since
|(\sin n)^{L}|\leq 1
for all
n
, we immediately have
|(\sin n)^{L}-c|\geq\varepsilon
for all
n
. Similarly for
c<-1

Assume now that
c\in[-1,1]
.
Since
1/(2\pi)
is irrational, Weyl’s equidistribution law (
(
52
)
)) implies that the sequence

\{n\bmod 2\pi\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is dense in
[0,2\pi)
. Because the
\sin
function is continuous and satisfies
\sin n=\sin(n\bmod 2\pi)
, it follows that the sequence
\{\sin n\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is dense in
[-1,1]
.

Consequently, the sequence
\{(\sin n)^{L}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is dense in
[-1,1]
if
L
is odd,
and dense in
[0,1]
if
L
is even. In either case, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
there exists an integer
n>T
such that

|(\sin n)^{L}-c|>\varepsilon.

Hence,

|F(G_{n},\bm{x}_{n})-P(G_{n},\bm{x}_{n})|=|(\sin n)^{L}-\psi(n)|>\varepsilon.

∎
