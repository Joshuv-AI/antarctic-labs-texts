Robustness of Constraint Automata for Description Logics with Concrete Domains
Stéphane Demri, Tianwen Gu

Abstract
Decidability or complexity issues about the consistency problem for description logics with concrete domains have already been analysed with tableaux-based or type elimination methods. Concrete domains in ontologies are essential to consider concrete objects and predefined relations. In this work, we expose an automata-based approach leading to the optimal upper bound ExpTime , that is designed by enriching the transitions with symbolic constraints. We show that the nonemptiness problem for such automata belongs to ExpTime if the concrete domains satisfy a few simple properties. Then, we provide a reduction from the consistency problem for ontologies, yielding ExpTime -membership. Thanks to the expressivity of constraint automata, the results are extended to additional ingredients such as inverse roles, functional role names and constraint assertions, while maintaining ExpTime -membership, which illustrates the robustness of the approach.

Introduction

Landmark results for description logics with concrete domains.

In description logics with concrete domains, the elements of
the interpretation domain are enriched with tuples of
values from the concrete domain,
see e.g.
[
3
,
27
,
28
,
25
,
5

,
enabling reasoning about concrete information.
The first decidability result for a large class of concrete domains

can be traced back to

-admissible concrete domains

Though this was a genuine breakthrough in understanding

satisfies the patchwork property

[

and
[

which corresponds to homomorphism

Furthermore,
constraint domain restrictions (a.k.a. CD-restrictions)

, Definition 6]
,
where

’s are binary predicates.
Moreover, no ABoxes in the consistency problem are handled in
[
25
]
.
Among the famous
concrete domains captured by
[
25
]
,
it is worth mentioning Allen’s interval algebra and
the region connection calculus
RCC8, see more details in
[
25
, Section 2]
.

Though the works
[
25
,
15
]
mainly focus on decidability,
a recent remarkable breakthrough was achieved in
[

]
,
which shows that the knowledge base consistency problem for

with

-admissible

, refining the decidability results; see also
[

]
.
This complexity result nicely extends
the type elimination algorithm for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}
that leads already to

,
based on the technique using elimination of Hintikka sets established

[

]
, see also
[

, Section 6.8]
,

[
29
, Section 2.3]

and
[

, Section 4.2]
.

Our motivations.

The well-known automata-based approach, see e.g.
[
14
,
36
,
37
]
,
consists of reducing logical problems to
automata-based problems to take advantage of results and decision procedures from
automata theory, see also
[
19
, Chapter 14]
.
Typically, a reduction is designed and one
relies on automata-based decision procedures.
Among the expected properties,
the reduction should be conceptually simple even if
showing its correctness requires some substantial work.
Ideally, the
computational
complexity of the automata-based target problems should be
well-understood, so that the reduction yields a tight upper bound
on the complexity of the logical problem.
Preferably, the reduction should lead to the optimal complexity for the logical
problem.

In this paper,
we would like to follow an automata-based approach for reasoning in
the description logics
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}
with concrete
domains
and regain the
ExpTime
-membership from
[
13
]

(see also the recent work
[
2
]
) based
on the approach
from
[
20
,
21
]
.
Type elimination and tableaux-based techniques are definitely worth being developed
but we would like to expose an alternative approach with
comprehensively developed formal methods that are equally accessible to a wide audience.
Our motivations definitely rest on the use of
concrete domains as
the automata-based approach for description logics (without concrete domains)
with automata over finite alphabets is already advocated in
[
9
, Section 3.2]
, see
also
[
4
]
and
[
6
, Section 3.6.1]
. This is the
research agenda we wish to pursue with
constraint
automata,
see also
[
20
]
.

Our contributions.

Our first task is to
introduce a class of constraint automata parameterised by concrete domains
that accept
infinite data trees
and the
scope of the constraints slightly extends the scope involved
in the constraint automata in
[
20
,
21
]
(Section
3
).
The constraints involved in the transitions have a simple and natural form
(constraints between siblings are allowed)
that
shall lead to a forthcoming smooth reduction from the consistency problem, though
technical difficulties still arise.
In order to get
k
-
ExpTime
-membership of the nonemptiness problem,
the concrete domains satisfy (C1.1) the completion property
[
18
,
11
]
(resp.
(C1.1.1) the amalgamation property and (C1.1.2)
homomorphism
\omega
-compactness
[
13
]
),
(C2) the arity of predicates is bounded,
(C3.
k
) the constraint
satisfaction problem for the concrete domain
\mathcal{D}

is in
k
-
ExpTime
and (C4) equality is part of the relations.
We provide a parameterised complexity analysis
that is helpful to characterise then the complexity of the consistency problem for the
description logic
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})
.
More precisely, we design a reduction to the nonemptiness
problem for Büchi tree automata,
known to be solved in
quadratic time, see e.g.
[
16
]
.
Along the paper, we are particularly interested in determining
which conditions are really needed for each part of our investigations.

Then, we design a reduction from the consistency problem for
the description logics

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})
with concrete domains
\mathcal{D}
satisfying the above conditions
into the nonemptiness problem
for constraint automata parameterised by
\mathcal{D}
, leading to
ExpTime
-membership if (C3.1) holds,
which is known to be optimal,
see e.g.
[
13
,
2
]
.
We mix standard ingredients to translate modal/temporal/description
logics into tree automata with a quite natural handling of CD-restrictions.
A few complications arise to handle
nominals (see e.g.
[
35
]
and the notion of guess
in
[
33
, Definition 6]
about the
\mu
-calculus
with converse, universality modality and nominals), and the fact that
concrete features admit partial interpretations.
It is notable that we allow slightly more general classes of concrete domains than those
in
[
13
]
(see Section
2.1
for a brief discussion about the
differences). By way of example, we allow more general concrete domain restrictions
and finiteness of the set
of relations is not always required.
Furthermore, we are a bit more liberal with the set of CD-restrictions, which allows us
to get rid of the condition JEPD (jointly exhaustive pairwise disjoint),
see e.g.
[
13
]
.
Often, we introduce notions that have counterparts
in
[
13
,
2
]
but tailored to our approach.

Constraint automata allow us to add ingredients
and to adapt smoothly the translation, for instance by
adding inverse roles while giving up the nominals
(as hinted possible in
[
13
, Section 5]
),
functional role names and predicate assertions
(see Section
2.3
and Section
5
).
The extension with inverse roles, though technically challenging, perfectly
illustrates the versatility of our approach.
In short,
we revisit the complexity results about description logics with concrete
domains from
[
13
,
2
]
by advocating an automata-based
approach, while slightly
refining some results (use of the completion property)
and establishing a few new ones (inverse roles).

Preliminaries: Towards the Consistency Problem for Ontologies

In this section, we introduce the concrete domains
\mathcal{D}
and the few assumptions
involved in this document. Then, we define the description logics

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})
parameterised by
the concrete domain
\mathcal{D}
.
After recalling the definition of the consistency problem for ontologies
(a.k.a. knowledge
bases), we conclude this section by presenting several additional features that
are handled in Section
5
, by taking advantage of our
modular automata-based approach.

2.1
Concrete Domains Under Scrutiny

A
concrete domain
is a relational structure

\mathcal{D}=(\mathbb{D},P_{1}^{\mathbb{D}},P_{2}^{\mathbb{D}},\dots),

where
\mathbb{D}
is a nonempty set and each

P_{i}^{\mathbb{D}}\subseteq\mathbb{D}^{k_{i}}

interprets a predicate symbol
P_{i}
of arity
k_{i}
.
Elements of
\mathbb{D}
are written
\mathbb{d}_{0},\mathbb{d}_{1},\ldots
.
Let
{\rm VAR}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots\}
be a countably infinite set of variables.
A
(Boolean) constraint
is an expression following the grammar below:

\Theta::=P(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\mid\lnot\Theta\mid\Theta\land\Theta\mid\Theta\lor\Theta,

with
P
a predicate symbol of arity
k
and
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\in{\rm VAR}
(repetitions allowed).
A
valuation
is a total function

\mathfrak{v}:{\rm VAR}\to\mathbb{D}
. Satisfaction of atomic formulas is defined by

\mathfrak{v}\models P(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{\;\Leftrightarrow\;}}

(\mathfrak{v}(v_{1}),\dots,\mathfrak{v}(v_{k}))\in P^{\mathbb{D}}
,
and extended to Boolean combinations in the usual way.
A
constraint system

S

is a set
of
literals of the form either
P(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
or
\neg P(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
.
We write
{\rm VAR}(S)
to denote the set of variables occurring
in

S
.
Given
X\subseteq{\rm VAR}
, we write
S|_{X}
to denote the subset
of
S
made of literals using only variables from the set
X
.
A valuation
\mathfrak{v}:{\rm VAR}\to\mathbb{D}
satisfies the system
S
,
written
\mathfrak{v}\models S
, if it satisfies every literal in
S
.
The
constraint satisfaction problem
for
\mathcal{D}
, denoted

\textsf{CSP}(\mathcal{D})
, takes as an input
a finite constraint system
S

and asks whether there is a valuation
\mathfrak{v}

such that
\mathfrak{v}\models S
.

In this document, we assume that the concrete domains satisfy a few properties
to guarantee at least decidability of the logical decision problems, and

k
-
ExpTime
-membership if possible.
The concrete domain
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the
completion
property

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{\;\Leftrightarrow\;}}

for every finite constraint system
S

and for every valuation

\mathfrak{v}:X\to\mathbb{D}

with
X\subseteq{\rm VAR}(S)
, if
\mathfrak{v}

satisfies the restriction

S|_{X}
and
S
is satisfiable, then there
is an extension
\mathfrak{v}^{\prime}:{\rm VAR}(S)\to\mathbb{D}
such that
\mathfrak{v}^{\prime}|_{X}=\mathfrak{v}

and
\mathfrak{v}^{\prime}\models S
.

A constraint system
S
is
complete
with respect to
some set
\mathcal{P}
of predicate symbols
and some set
X
of variables
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{\;\Leftrightarrow\;}}

no other predicate symbol or variable occurs in

S
and for all
P\in\mathcal{P}
of arity
k
and

v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\in X
, we have
either
P(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\in S
or
\neg P(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})\in S

(but not both).
A concrete domain

\mathcal{D}
satisfies the

amalgamation property

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{\;\Leftrightarrow\;}}

•

either
\mathcal{D}
has a finite set
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}
of predicate symbols
and for all finite constraint systems
S
,
S^{\prime}

such that
S|_{{\rm VAR}(S)\cap{\rm VAR}(S^{\prime})}=S^{\prime}|_{{\rm VAR}(S)\cap{\rm VAR}(S^{\prime})}

and
S|_{{\rm VAR}(S)\cap{\rm VAR}(S^{\prime})}
is complete w.r.t.

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\rm VAR}(S)\cap{\rm VAR}(S^{\prime})
,
we have
(\bigtriangleup)

S
and
S^{\prime}
are
satisfiable (separately) iff

S\cup S^{\prime}
is satisfiable,

•

or
\mathcal{D}
has an infinite set of predicate symbols and
for all finite sets
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{D}}
,
for all finite constraint systems
S
,
S^{\prime}
built over

\mathcal{P}
such that

S|_{{\rm VAR}(S)\cap{\rm VAR}(S^{\prime})}=S^{\prime}|_{{\rm VAR}(S)\cap{\rm VAR}(S^{\prime})}

and
S|_{{\rm VAR}(S)\cap{\rm VAR}(S^{\prime})}
is complete w.r.t.

\mathcal{P}
and
{\rm VAR}(S)\cap{\rm VAR}(S^{\prime})
,
we have (
\bigtriangleup
).

Similar definitions about amalgamation can be found
in
[
25
,
32
,
8
,
13
,
2
]
.
Note that the involved constraint systems are finite, and unlike
[
25
,
32
]
,
we do not assume completeness of the constraint systems,
see also the related notion of symbolic type in
Section
3.2
.
Finally,
a concrete domain

\mathcal{D}

is

homomorphism
\omega
-compact

(see e.g.
[
13
]
)

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{\;\Leftrightarrow\;}}

if every finite
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
of
a countable constraint system
S
is satisfiable,
then
S
itself is satisfiable.

We are ready to define the main properties satisfied by concrete domains
involved herein.

(C1)

The concrete domain
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the completion
property or (it satisfies the amalgamation property and is
homomorphism
\omega
-compact).
(from local to global)

(C2)

There is
k_{0}
such that all the relations in
\mathcal{D}
have arity
bounded by
k_{0}
.
(bounded arity)

(C3.
k
)

\textsf{CSP}(\mathcal{D})
is in
k
-
ExpTime
(
k\geq 1
). (
k
-
ExpTime
CSP problem)

(C4)

\mathcal{D}
contains the equality relation.
(equality in the concrete domain)

The condition (C4) is a bit less general than the condition JD (jointly diagonal)
from
[
13
,
8
]
but simpler to check.
The completion property has been already considered
in the literature
[
10
,
18
,
11
]
,
sometimes under the term “global consistency”,
see e.g.
[

, Definition 2.3]

and
[

]
.
By way of example, concrete domains satisfying the completion property
include
(\mathbb{R},<,=)
,

(\mathbb{Q},<,=)
and
(\mathbb{D},=)
for
some infinite set
\mathbb{D}
.
The concrete domain RCC8 with space regions in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
equipped with
topological relations between spatial regions
[
38
]

also satisfies the completion property,
see e.g.
[

, Section 2.1]
and
[

]
.
This applies also to the
Allen’s temporal concrete domain

\mathcal{D}_{A}=(I_{\mathbb{Q}},(R_{i})_{i\in[1,13]})
,
where
I_{\mathbb{Q}}
is the set of closed intervals
[r,r^{\prime}]\subseteq\mathbb{Q}

and
(R_{i})_{i\in[1,13]}
is the family of 13 Allen’s relations
[
1
]
,
see also
[
25
, Section 2]
. Observe that
RCC8 and Allen’s interval algebra also satisfy (C2), (C3.1) and (C4),
see e.g.
[
25
, Section 2]
.
The completion property being one alternative
in the condition (C1) is a natural condition that happens to
be sufficient for our needs.
However, this excludes
the concrete domain
(\mathbb{N},<,=)

that is nevertheless handled
in
[
15
,
23
,
24
,
21
]
.
Indeed,
\mathbb{N}
(resp.
\mathbb{Z}
) does not satisfy the completion property
and is not homomorphism

A simple property can be already established.

Lemma 1
.

If

\mathcal{D}
satisfies the completion property, then

The proof is in Appendix
A
.
The converse implication is not known to hold in general; that is,
whether the amalgamation property (or some variant) implies the completion property remains open.
More broadly, the exact relationships between the completion property, the amalgamation property,
and
\omega
-compactness are not fully understood.
By contrast with
[
13
,
2
]
,
observe that
the satisfaction of (C1), (C2), (C3.1) and (C4) allows the concrete domains to have an infinite
set of relations as in

.
The condition JEPD from
[

]
is
not required in our framework and (C4) implies JD (jointly diagonal)
from
[
13
]
.
Unlike the completion property in (C1), the second disjunct of (C1) is often assumed in the
literature,
see e.g.
[
25
,
32
]
and
the
ExpTime
-
\omega
-admissible concrete domains
in
[

,

]
.

2.2
Reasoning about Ontologies with Concrete Objects

In this section, we define
the description logic
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})
(over the concrete domain

\mathcal{D}
) defined as the description logic
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{D})

from
[
13
, Section 2]
(see also
[
2
]
)
except that
the assumptions about
\mathcal{D}
may differ and the concrete domain restrictions (a.k.a.
CD-restrictions) are defined in a slightly more general way.
Let

{\sf N}_{\mathbf{C}}=\{A,B,\ldots\}
,

{\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}=\{r,s,\ldots\}
,

{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}=\{a,b,c,\ldots\}
,
and
{\sf N}_{\mathbf{CF}}=\{f_{0},f_{1},\ldots\}

be countable sets of

concept names
,

role names
,

individual names

and

concrete features
, respectively.
Following the assumptions from
[
13
]
,
a
role path

p
is either a concrete feature
f

or the concatenation
r\cdot f
of a role name
r

and a concrete feature
f
.
The set of
complex concepts
in
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})
is defined as follows.

\displaystyle C\coloneqq{}\quad

\displaystyle A\mid\{\hskip-1.4457pta\hskip-1.4457pt\}\mid\top\mid\bot\mid\neg A\mid C\sqcap C\mid C\sqcup C\mid\exists r.C\mid\forall r.C

\displaystyle\mid\exists\ v_{1}:p_{1},\dots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \Theta(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\mid\forall\ v_{1}:p_{1},\dots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \Theta(v_{1},\dots,v_{k}),

where
A\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{C}}
,

a\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}
,

r\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}
,
and
\Theta(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
is a
\mathcal{D}
-constraint built over the
distinct

variables
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
(not necessarily an atomic formula).
The variables
v_{i}
bind values retrieved via the paths
p_{i}
.
The expression
\{\hskip-1.4457pta\hskip-1.4457pt\}
is known as a nominal and is interpreted by a singleton,
namely the one containing the interpretation of the individual name
a
.
For instance,
let
\mathcal{D}=(\mathbb{R},<,=)
, and suppose
\mathsf{Patient}\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{C}}
,

\mathsf{age}\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{CF}}
, and
\mathsf{hasBrother}\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}
.
The concept

\mathsf{Patient}\sqcap\forall\ v_{1}:\mathsf{age}.\ v_{1}<18

describes patients younger than 18, while

\exists\ v_{1}:\mathsf{age},\;v_{2}:\mathsf{hasBrother}\cdot\mathsf{age}.\ v_{2}<v_{1}

captures individuals older than at least one of their brothers.

A
general concept inclusion
(GCI) is an expression
of the form

C\sqsubseteq D
, where
C
,
D

are
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})

concepts. A
role assertion
(resp.
concept assertion
)
is an expression
of the form

(a,b):r

(resp.
a:C
).
An
ontology
is a pair
\mathcal{O}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A})
,
where
\mathcal{T}
is a finite set of GCIs and
\mathcal{A}
is a finite set of assertions.
As we shall provide complexity analyses, let us briefly describe how the size
of ontologies is defined.
The size of
an ontology
\mathcal{O}
, written
size(\mathcal{O})
, is defined
as the sum of the size of
\mathcal{T}
, written
size(\mathcal{T})
, and the size of
\mathcal{A}
,
written
size(\mathcal{A})
. The size of a TBox is
defined as the sum of the sizes of
its GCIs, whereas the size of an ABox is defined as the sum of the sizes of its
assertions. Assuming that the size of a concept
C
, written
size(C)
,
is defined as the sum between
the number of its subconcepts and the number of its subconstraints occurring in CD-restrictions,
the size of a GCI is the sum of the size of its two concepts.
Moreover, the size of a role assertion is defined as one,
and the size of a concept assertion is defined as the size of its concept.

An
interpretation

\mathcal{I}=(\Delta^{\mathcal{I}},\cdot^{\mathcal{I}})
consists of
a nonempty (interpretation) domain
\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
(its elements
are written
d,e,\ldots
)
and an interpretation function

\cdot^{\mathcal{I}}
such that

•

A^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
for all
A\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{C}}
;

r^{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\times\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
for all

r\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}
,

a^{\mathcal{I}}\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
for all
a\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}
.

•

f^{\mathcal{I}}:\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}\to\mathbb{D}
is a partial function, for
all
f\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{CF}}
.

•

Given
d\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
,

p^{\mathcal{I}}(d)
denotes either
\{f^{\mathcal{I}}(d)\}
for

p=f
or
\{f^{\mathcal{I}}(e)\mid(d,e)\in r^{\mathcal{I}}\}

for
p=r\cdot f
.
Such sets might be empty in case
f^{\mathcal{I}}
is undefined
for the involved elements of
\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
.

The semantics
below is standard, except CD-restrictions
admit arbitrary constraints, see also
[
32
, Definition 1]

3
Tree Global Constraint Automata

In this section, we introduce a class of tree constraint automata parameterised
by concrete domains
\mathcal{D}
that accept infinite data trees in which
every node is labelled by a finite tuple of data values from
\mathbb{D}
.
This is slightly
more general than the automata models from
[
20
]
,
not only because the definition does not assume a fixed concrete domain
but more importantly because constraints between siblings are allowed
unlike what is done in
[
20
,
21
]
.

3.1
Definitions

Unless otherwise stated, in the rest of this section, we assume that
the concrete domain
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the conditions (C1) and (C3.
k
)
for some
k\geq 1

(meaning that (C2) and (C4) are not needed).
We introduce the class of tree global constraint automata
that accept sets of labelled trees of the form

\mathbb{t}:[0,\mathtt{d}-1]^{*}\to\mathbf{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{D}^{\beta}

for some finite alphabet
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, for some
\mathtt{d}\geq 1
and
\beta\geq 0
.
The transition relation of such automata expresses constraints between the
\beta
values at
a node and the values at its children. The automaton is equipped with a Büchi
acceptance condition.
Formally,
a
tree global constraint automaton
(in short, TGCA) over
some concrete domain

\mathcal{D}

is a tuple

{\mathbb{A}}=(Q,\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathtt{d},\beta,Q_{in},\delta,F),

where:

•

Q
is a finite set of locations,

Q_{in}\subseteq Q

are initial locations,
F\subseteq Q
are accepting locations,

\mathbf{\Sigma}
is a finite alphabet;

•

\mathtt{d}\geq 1
is the branching degree, and
\beta\in\mathbb{N}
the number of
registers (a.k.a. variables);

•

\delta\subseteq Q\times\mathbf{\Sigma}\times{\rm Cons}(\beta,\mathtt{d})\times Q^{\mathtt{d}}
is the transition relation
where
{\rm Cons}(\beta,\mathtt{d})

denotes the set of
\mathcal{D}
-constraints built over
the distinguished registers in

{\rm REG}(\beta,\mathtt{d})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{x_{1},\dots,x_{\beta}\}\cup\{x_{j}^{i}\mid 1\leq j\leq\beta,\ 0\leq i<\mathtt{d}\}
.
The expression

x_{j}^{i}
refers to the
j
th register of the
i
th child.
Arbitrary registers in
{\rm REG}(\beta,\mathtt{d})
are also referred to by
y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},\ldots
.

Runs.

Let
\mathbb{t}:[0,\mathtt{d}-1]^{*}\to\mathbf{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{D}^{\beta}

be a full infinite
\mathtt{d}
-ary data
data tree, with
\mathbb{t}(n)=(\mathtt{a}_{n},\vec{z}_{n})

at each node
n
.
A

of
{\mathbb{A}}

on

is a mapping
\rho:[0,\mathtt{d}-1]^{*}\to\delta

that maps each node
n
to a transition

(q_{n},\mathtt{a}_{n},\Theta_{n},q_{n\cdot 0},\dots,q_{n\cdot(\mathtt{d}-1)})

such that:

{romanenumerate}

The source location of
\rho(n\cdot i)
is
q_{n\cdot i}

for all
0\leq i<\mathtt{d}
.

Let
\mathfrak{v}_{n}:{\rm REG}(\beta,\mathtt{d})\to\mathbb{D}
be the valuation defined by

\mathfrak{v}_{n}(x_{j})=\vec{z}_{n}[j]\text{ and }\mathfrak{v}_{n}(x_{j}^{i})=\vec{z}_{n\cdot i}[j],

for all
1\leq j\leq\beta,\ 0\leq i<\mathtt{d}
. Then
\mathfrak{v}_{n}\models\Theta_{n}
.

The source location of
\rho(\varepsilon)\text{ is in }Q_{in}
.

Given a path
\pi=j_{1}\cdot j_{2}\dots
in
\rho
starting from
\varepsilon
, we define

to be the set of locations that appear infinitely often as the source
locations of the transitions in
\rho(\varepsilon)\rho(j_{1})\rho(j_{2})\dots

A run

is

if all paths
\pi
in

\rho
starting from
\varepsilon
,
we have

We write

to denote the set of data trees

for which there exists

{\mathbb{A}}
on
\mathbb{t}
.
The
nonemptiness problem for TGCA
, written NE(TGCA), asks
whether
{\rm L}({\mathbb{A}})\neq\emptyset
for some TGCA

.
Herein, we use Büchi acceptance conditions but
it is easy to consider
other acceptance conditions, apart from the fact that

play no essential role in our investigations because we shall always assume that

(as in looping tree automata,

, Section 3.6.1]

3.2
Reduction to Nonemptiness for Büchi Tree Automata

To analyse the complexity of
the decision problem NE(TGCA),
we construct a reduction to the nonemptiness problem
for Büchi tree automata (BTA), known to be in
PTime

[
36
]
.
Büchi tree automata are
TGCA with
\beta=0

and no
\mathcal{D}
-constraints.
Given
a fixed TGCA

{\mathbb{A}}=(Q,\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathtt{d},\beta,Q_{in},\delta,F)
,
we write
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{A}}=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\}

to denote either the full set of predicate symbols of
\mathcal{D}

if finite, or the
finite
set of predicate symbols from
\mathcal{D}

occurring in
{\mathbb{A}}
.

Symbolic types.

To perform the reduction, we abstract
over concrete values in a TGCA run using
by

symbolic types
, following the standard notion of types in first-order languages,
see e.g.
[
31
, Section 3.1]
and
[
34
]
.
These are sets of literals that capture all atomic constraints relevant to the current node and its children.
A forthcoming notion of
projection compatibility ensures the coherence between parent and child types across the tree.
Let

\mathsf{Atoms}({\mathbb{A}})

be the set of atomic
formulae of the form

P(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})

for some
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\in{\rm REG}(\beta,\mathtt{d})

and
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{A}}
.
Similarly, let
\mathsf{Literals}({\mathbb{A}})

be the set of all literals over
\mathsf{Atoms}({\mathbb{A}})
,
i.e., atomic formulae or their negations.

A
symbolic type

\tau
is a
set

\tau\subseteq\mathsf{Literals}({\mathbb{A}})

such that
for every
\Theta\in\mathsf{Atoms}({\mathbb{A}})
, either
\Theta\in\tau
or

\neg\Theta\in\tau
, but not both. Let
\mathsf{STypes}({\mathbb{A}})
denote the set
of all symbolic types.
Symbolic types can be
obviously understood as constraints made of conjunctions of its elements
built over the registers in
{\rm REG}(\beta,\mathtt{d})
.
A symbolic type
\tau
is
satisfiable
if there is
a valuation

\mathfrak{v}:{\rm REG}(\beta,\mathtt{d})\to\mathbb{D}
such that
\mathfrak{v}\models\tau
.
Let
\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})
denote the set of all satisfiable symbolic types.
The properties of satisfiable types that we mainly use are stated in Lemma
2

where
\mathcal{D}\models\tau(\vec{z},\vec{z}_{0},\ldots,\vec{z}_{\mathtt{d}-1})

means that
\mathfrak{v}\models\bigwedge_{\Theta\in\tau}\Theta

for the valuation
\mathfrak{v}

such that
\mathfrak{v}(x_{j})=\vec{z}[j]

and
\mathfrak{v}(x_{j}^{i})=\vec{z}_{i}[j]
,
for all
1\leq j\leq\beta,\ 0\leq i<\mathtt{d}
.

Lemma 2

(Three properties about types)
.

(I) Let
\vec{z},\vec{z}_{0},\ldots,\vec{z}_{\mathtt{d}-1}\in\mathbb{D}^{\beta}
. There is a unique
satisfiable type

\tau\in\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})

such that
\mathcal{D}\models\tau(\vec{z},\vec{z}_{0},\ldots,\vec{z}_{\mathtt{d}-1})
.
(II) For every constraint
\Theta
built over
the predicate symbols in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{A}}

and the registers
{\rm REG}(\beta,\mathtt{d})
,
there is a disjunction

\tau_{1}\vee\cdots\vee\tau_{\gamma}
logically equivalent to
\Theta

and each
\tau_{i}
belongs to
\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})
(empty disjunction stands for
\perp
).
(III) For all distinct types
\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}\in\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})
,

\tau\wedge\tau^{\prime}
is not satisfiable.

The proof of Lemma
2
is by an easy verification
and can be found in
Appendix
B
.

We write
\tau\models\Theta
to denote that
for all valuations
\mathfrak{v}
,
if
\mathfrak{v}\models\tau
, then

\mathfrak{v}\models\Theta
.
If
\tau\in\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})
and
\Theta
satisfies
the properties of Lemma
2
(II), then
\tau\models\Theta

can be checked in polynomial time.
Let

\tau,\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{\mathtt{d}-1}\in\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})
.
We say that
\tau

is
projection-compatible
with
(\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{\mathtt{d}-1})

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{\;\Leftrightarrow\;}}
for each

i<\mathtt{d}
and every predicate symbol
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{A}}
of arity
k
,
and for all tuples
(j_{1},\dots,j_{k})\in[1,\beta]^{k}
, we have:

P(x_{j_{1}}^{i},\dots,x_{j_{k}}^{i})\in\tau

iff

P(x_{j_{1}},\dots,x_{j_{k}})\in\tau_{i}
.
We recall that in a type
\tau
, registers associated to the
values of the children nodes are also present. Hence, projection-compatibility
simply reflects the fact that the constraints for such registers
in
\tau
must be compatible with the constraints of the
registers in the children types.

Construction of Büchi tree automata.

We now define the Büchi tree automaton

{\mathbb{B}}=(Q^{\prime},\mathbf{\Sigma},\delta^{\prime},\mathtt{d},Q^{\prime}_{\mathsf{in}},F^{\prime})

that simulates the TGCA
{\mathbb{A}}
using symbolic types.
The basic idea consists in abstracting data values in
\mathbb{D}
by satisfiable
types from
\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})
. Moreover, the type at a node needs to be
compatible with the children’s types since a type expresses constraints between
the values at a node and those at its children. This is where
projection compatibility plays a crucial role. In this way, an infinite tree
accepted by the Büchi tree automaton can be viewed as an infinite constraint system
(each node has its own variables coming from
\beta
local registers)
for which satisfiability is required. Generally, additional conditions are needed
to guarantee satisfiability, see e.g.
[
23
,
24
,
20
]
.
In the case of concrete domains satisfying the condition (C1), importantly, this is all we need
as shown below. First, let us define
the Büchi tree automaton
{\mathbb{B}}
with the remarkably simple construction below.

•

Q^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}Q\times\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})
,

Q^{\prime}_{in}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}Q_{in}\times\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})
,

F^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}F\times\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})
,

•

\delta^{\prime}
contains the transition

((q,\tau),\mathtt{a},(q_{0},\tau_{0}),\dots,(q_{\mathtt{d}-1},\tau_{\mathtt{d}-1}))

if (a)

(q,\mathtt{a},\Theta,q_{0},\dots,q_{\mathtt{d}-1})\in\delta
,
(b)
\tau\models\Theta

and (c)

\tau
is projection-compatible with
(\tau_{0},\dots,\tau_{\mathtt{d}-1})
.

To build effectively
{\mathbb{B}}
from

{\mathbb{A}}
, we only need the decidability of
\textsf{CSP}(\mathcal{D})

to determine the satisfiable symbolic types.
We can establish that this construction preserves nonemptiness.
Again, apart from the decidability of
\textsf{CSP}(\mathcal{D})
, only the condition (C1) is required
to get the equivalence as the condition (C2) is mainly
relevant for computational purposes.

Lemma 3

(Soundness)

The proof does not require much about the

In its proof,
essential for our investigations
(see enlightening details in

),
each disjunct of

(C1) leads to a way to
construct the valuation
\mathfrak{v}
on which the data values

If
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the completion property,
then
the valuation

is
homomorphism

The nonemptiness problem for TGCA over

4
Constraint Automata for Checking Consistency

Below, we reduce the consistency problem for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})

to the nonemptiness problem for TGCA over
\mathcal{D}
.
Correctness of the reduction only requires that the concrete domain
\mathcal{D}

satisfies the condition (C4). As shown in Section
3
,
the translation from TGCA to BTA requires the satisfaction of (C1) and

\textsf{CSP}(\mathcal{D})
is decidable. In order to get
ExpTime
-membership of
the consistency problem for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})
, the condition
(C2) and (C3.1) are further assumed.

At this point, it is worth emphasizing that using the automata-based approach with
ABoxes
is not so common in the literature.
Indeed, several works dedicated to the consistency problem for description logics with concrete domains
consider only TBoxes.
see e.g.
[
4
,
25
,
15
,
23
]
,
most probably because this is much easier to conceive.
There is a notable exception with the recent works
[
13
,
2
]
.
Indeed, handling ABoxes means that the interpretations satisfying
ontologies should rather be viewed as forests and not only as simple trees.
Fortunately, forests can be encoded as trees as soon as it is possible
to manage the global information about the different trees of the forest
in a tree-like manner. Presently, the global information should include
which individual names belong to the interpretation of subconcepts,
but also to have access to the values returned by the concrete features
for the distinguished
elements
interpreting the individual names. Hopefully, this can be performed
thanks to additional registers in the constraint automata.
The need for some global information within the automata-based approach
in logical formalisms having syntactic objects such as
nominals or individual names can be found in the
works
[
4
, Section 6.3]
and
[
22
]
.

4.1
Preliminaries

Given an ontology
\mathcal{O}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A})
,
we shall build a TGCA
{\mathbb{A}}=(Q,\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathtt{d},\beta,Q_{in},\delta,F)
over
\mathcal{D}
,
such that

\mathcal{O}

is consistent iff
{\rm L}({\mathbb{A}})\neq\emptyset
.
This section is dedicated to prepare the definition of such a reduction.
More specifically, we briefly explain why the ontology
\mathcal{O}
can be
assumed to be in some normal form, and
we introduce several notions
that are helpful to design the constraint automaton
{\mathbb{A}}
.
Below, we introduce the notions of concept types (similar to Hintikka sets),
and (local/global/contextual) abstractions that are finite pieces
of information about elements in some interpretation domain

\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
. The construction of
{\mathbb{A}}

is finalised in Section
4.2

and the correctness
is postponed to Section
4.3
.

Normalisation.

A concept is in
negation normal form
(NNF)
if the negation operator for concepts
occurs only
in front of
concept names or nominals.
Every concept is logically
equivalent to a concept in NNF (and there is a simple
effective construction running in quadratic time).
In particular,

\neg(\mathcal{Q}\ v_{1}:p_{1},\dots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \Theta)

is logically equivalent to

\overline{\mathcal{Q}}\ v_{1}:p_{1},\dots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \neg\Theta
,
where
\mathcal{Q}\in\{\exists,\forall\}
,

\overline{\forall}=\exists
and
\overline{\exists}=\forall
.
This is valid since the constraints in CD-restrictions
are
closed under negations
.
Also, every GCI
C\sqsubseteq D
is equivalent to
\top\sqsubseteq\lnot C\sqcup D
.
Hence, we assume a fixed ontology

\mathcal{O}=(\mathcal{T},\mathcal{A})
such that
all the concepts are in NNF and the GCIs are of the form

\top\sqsubseteq C
.
This normalisation is standard and harmless for complexity.
We write

\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})

to denote the set of
NNF subconcepts
occurring in the ontology
\mathcal{O}

augmented with the concepts
\{\hskip-1.4457pta\hskip-1.4457pt\}
and
\neg\{\hskip-1.4457pta\hskip-1.4457pt\}

for all individual names
a
occurring
in
\mathcal{O}
in assertions and nominals (details omitted).
We introduce the following sets:

{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{a_{0},\dots,a_{\eta-1}\}
is the
set of
individual names occurring in assertions/nominals in
\mathcal{O}
,

{\sf N}_{\mathbf{CF}}(\mathcal{O})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{f_{1},\dots,f_{\alpha}\}
is the set of concrete
features
occurring in concepts
in
\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})

and

{\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}(\mathcal{O})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{r_{1},\dots,r_{\kappa}\}

is
the set of role names
occurring
in
\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})
.

Abstractions.

Consistency of the ontology
\mathcal{O}
asks for the existence
of an interpretation
\mathcal{I}
such that
\mathcal{I}\models\mathcal{O}
.
In the automata-based approach,
this reduces to the existence of an infinite data tree
\mathbb{t}

such that
\mathbb{t}\in{\rm L}({\mathbb{A}})
.
Such a witness tree
\mathbb{t}
represents an interpretation

\mathcal{I}
(nodes in
[0,\mathtt{d}-1]^{*}
roughly correspond to elements of
\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
),
so
{\rm L}({\mathbb{A}})
is designed to accept such representations.
An accepting run on
\mathbb{t}

thus ensures that
\mathbb{t}
, viewed as an
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})
interpretation,
satisfies
\mathcal{O}
.
In an accepting run
\rho
on
\mathbb{t}
, each node
n
is labelled by a transition

(q,\mathtt{a},\Theta,q_{0},\ldots,q_{\mathtt{d}-1})
,
where the location
q
contains some

finite symbolic abstraction
about the node
n

viewed as an element from the interpretation domain. This is
where
local, global and contextual abstractions
enter into play.

Given an element
d\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
for some
interpretation
\mathcal{I}
, the
local abstraction
of the element
d

in
\mathcal{I}
, written
locabs(d,\mathcal{I})
,
is a triple
(T,sl,act)
defined as follows.

•

T
is the
concept type
and

T\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{C\in\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})\mid d\in C^{\mathcal{I}}\}
,

•

act
is the
activity vector

in
\{\perp,\top\}^{\alpha}

such that
act[i]=\top

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{\;\Leftrightarrow\;}}

f_{i}^{\mathcal{I}}(d)
is defined, for all
i\in[1,\alpha]

(recall that concrete features
are interpreted by partial functions),

•

sl
is the set of
symbolic links

in
{\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}(\mathcal{O})\times{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})

such that

(r,a)\in sl

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{\;\Leftrightarrow\;}}

(d,a^{\mathcal{I}})\in r^{\mathcal{I}}
.

It is not immediate that this notion of local abstraction suffices,
but we shall show that it does.
Observe that the notion of local abstraction is parameterised by ontologies
but this is not emphasized in the notation.
Of course, in the forthcoming TGCA
{\mathbb{A}}
, we also
make use of the registers to encode the concrete features from interpretations
of
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})
.
By contrast, the
global abstractions
act as persistent
memories relevant for the whole interpretation.
Unsurprisingly, they assign to each individual
name in
{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})
a local abstraction;
due to a technical reason,
we can restrict these to just the concept type and activity vector.
Since runs of the one-way automaton
{\mathbb{A}}
cannot revisit nodes,
the global abstraction enables
to recover the local abstraction
of the individual names from
{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})

from any node.
We use a standard notion of
types
to represent locally consistent
sets of subconcepts,
similarly to Hintikka sets to prove completeness
of propositional logic for tableaux-style calculi,
see also
[
5
, Section 5.1.2]
.
The set of
concept types
,
denoted
\mathsf{CTypes}(\mathcal{O})
,
consists of all subsets
T\subseteq\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})
such that

1.

\bot\notin T
; for all concept names
A\in\mathsf{CTypes}(\mathcal{O})
, if
A\in T

then
\neg A\notin T
,

2.

for all
j\in[0,\eta-1]
,
either
\{\hskip-1.4457pta_{j}\hskip-1.4457pt\}\in T

or
\neg\{\hskip-1.4457pta_{j}\hskip-1.4457pt\}\in T
(but never both),

3.

if
C\sqcap D\in T
, then
C\in T
and
D\in T
;
if
C\sqcup D\in T
, then
C\in T
or
D\in T
,

4.

for every GCI

\top\sqsubseteq C\in\mathcal{T}
, we have
C\in T
.

An
anonymous
concept type
T

is such that

\{\neg\{\hskip-1.4457pta_{0}\hskip-1.4457pt\},\ldots,\neg\{\hskip-1.4457pta_{\eta-1}\hskip-1.4457pt\}\}\subseteq T
.
An
a
-type
T
is such that

\{\hskip-1.4457pta\hskip-1.4457pt\}\in T
and for
all
\{\hskip-1.4457ptb\hskip-1.4457pt\}
with
b\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})

distinct from
a
,
we have
\neg\{\hskip-1.4457ptb\hskip-1.4457pt\}\in T

(see e.g. the named states in
[
22
, Section 5]
and the named
types in
[
13
, Section 4]
).

An
activity vector

act

is a tuple in
\{\top,\bot\}^{\alpha}

where the
i
-th component
act[i]
indicates whether the concrete feature

f_{i}
(from
{\sf N}_{\mathbf{CF}}(\mathcal{O})
)
is defined (
\top
) or not (
\bot
).
The set of activity vectors is written
\mathsf{ACT}
.
Since the interpretation of each concrete feature is a partial function,
only the registers corresponding to positions where
act[i]=\top

represent meaningful feature values.
While the data tree assigns values to all registers uniformly,
entries with
act[i]=\bot
are
ignored when encoding
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})
interpretations.
A
symbolic link
is a pair

(r,a)\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}(\mathcal{O})\times{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})

intended to represent that
the current element is related to the
interpretation of
the individual name
a
via
the interpretation of the role name
r
.
The set
\mathsf{SLinks}
of symbolic link sets is the powerset

{\cal P}({\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}(\mathcal{O})\times{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O}))
.
A
local abstraction

\mathsf{LA}
is a triple in
\mathsf{CTypes}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathsf{SLinks}\times\mathsf{ACT}
.
A
global abstraction

\mathsf{GA}
is a function

\mathsf{GA}:{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathsf{CTypes}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathsf{ACT}
.
A location of the forthcoming automaton
{\mathbb{A}}
is structured
in such a way that it contains a local abstraction
\mathsf{LA}
(related
to the associated node) and a global abstraction
\mathsf{GA}
that records
a fixed knowledge about the individual names. Such pairs
are called
contextual abstractions

\mathsf{CA}=(\mathsf{GA},\mathsf{LA})
.

Branching degree
\mathtt{d}
.

We need to determine
a branching degree
\mathtt{d}
that allows us to satisfy all kinds
of existential restrictions.
Fortunately, the value
\mathtt{d}
shall be polynomial in the size
of
\mathcal{O}
.
To do so, we introduce the following values.

•

N_{\mathsf{ex}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}|\{\exists r.C\mid\exists r.C\in\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})\}|
.

•

N_{\mathsf{cd}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}|\{\exists\ v_{1}:p_{1},\dots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \Theta\mid\exists\ v_{1}:p_{1},\dots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \Theta\in\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})\}|
.

•

N_{\mathsf{var}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\max(\{0\}\cup\{k\mid\exists\ v_{1}:p_{1},\dots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \Theta\in\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})\})
.

The rationale to define
\mathtt{d}
is based on the following
requirements. For each
\exists r.C\in\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})
,
one needs one direction to have a witness individual.
Similarly, for each
\exists\ v_{1}:p_{1},\dots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \Theta\in\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})
,

k
directions are sufficient to get
k
witness values in
\mathbb{D}
.
Finally, the tree interpretation property for

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})
is based on the representation
of a forest such that each of its trees corresponds
to the unfolding of the interpretation from some individual name
in
{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})
. As this is encoded
at the root of the data tree accepted by
{\mathbb{A}}
,
the degree should be also at least
|{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})|
.
So, it makes sense to define
\mathtt{d}
as the value

\max\big\{N_{\mathsf{ex}}+N_{\mathsf{cd}}\times N_{\mathsf{var}},\ |{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})|\big\}
; the
forthcoming soundness proof
of Lemma
6

shall confirm this formally.

Correspondence between directions, role names
and restrictions.

Once the degree
\mathtt{d}
is defined, one needs to establish
a discipline to put in correspondence directions in
[0,\mathtt{d}-1]
,
role names
and restrictions. To do so, below, we define the maps
\lambda
and

dir
. Indeed, a standard approach consists in booking directions
in
[0,\mathtt{d}-1]
for each role name in
{\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}(\mathcal{O})
.
This is needed because the data trees have no labels on edges. Another
technique used in
[
9
, Section 3.2]
adds to the local
abstractions the role name of the incoming edge (unique thanks to the
tree structure).
Herein, the map
\lambda
takes care of
assigning directions to existential restrictions
and to role paths in existential CD-restrictions. By contrast,
the map
dir
is uniquely determined by

\lambda
and returns a set of directions among
[0,\mathtt{d}-1]

to each role name. Such a discipline is required
to construct the transitions in
{\mathbb{A}}
as one needs
to agree on the directions that are relevant for getting
witnesses of a given restriction.
We introduce the following sets.

•

\mathcal{E}_{r}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{\exists r_{i_{1}}.C_{1},\ldots,\exists r_{i_{N_{\mathsf{ex}}}}.C_{N_{\mathsf{ex}}}\}
contains all the existential restrictions from
\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})
.

•

\mathcal{E}_{\mathsf{cd}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{C^{\star}_{1},\ldots,C^{\star}_{N_{\mathsf{cd}}}\}

contains all the existential CD-restrictions from
\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})
.

We define the labeling domain as:

\mathcal{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{E}_{r}\cup\{(C^{\star}_{i},j)\mid 1\leq i\leq N_{\mathsf{cd}},\ 1\leq j\leq N_{\mathsf{var}}\}.

The global labeling function
\lambda:\mathcal{B}\to[0,\mathtt{d}-1]
, that is designed as a bijection,
is defined as:

•

\lambda(\exists r_{i_{k}}.C_{k})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}k-1
,
for
1\leq k\leq N_{\mathsf{ex}}
,

•

\lambda(C^{\star}_{i},j)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}N_{\mathsf{ex}}+(i{-}1)\times N_{\mathsf{var}}+(j{-}1)
, for
1\leq i\leq N_{\mathsf{cd}},\ 1\leq j\leq N_{\mathsf{var}}
.

The definition of the map
dir

is provided below.
Let
r\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}(\mathcal{O})
.
The set of
directions associated with the role name
r
is defined as:

{dir}(r)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\left\{\,i\in[0,\mathtt{d}{-}1]\;\middle|\;\begin{array}[]{l}\lambda^{-1}(i)=\exists r.C\text{ for some }C,\text{ or}\\
\lambda^{-1}(i)=(C^{\star},j)\text{ where }C^{\star}=\exists\ v_{1}{:}p_{1},\dots,v_{k}{:}p_{k}.\ \Theta,\\
\quad\text{with }1\leq j\leq k\text{ and }p_{j}=r\cdot f\end{array}\,\right\}.

Dimension
\beta
and
conventions about registers.

The forthcoming automaton
{\mathbb{A}}
accepts
infinite data trees
\mathbb{t}:[0,\mathtt{d}-1]\to\mathbf{\Sigma}\times\mathbb{D}^{\beta}
and it remains
to define
the dimension
\beta
before moving to
the final steps of the construction in Section
4.2
.
Since
{\sf N}_{\mathbf{CF}}(\mathcal{O})
contains
\alpha
concrete features,
we require at least
\alpha
registers.
Though global abstractions are persistent memories, this is not
sufficient to handle the values returned by the
concrete features for the individual names. This is useful
to consider the satisfaction of CD-restrictions requiring
as witness individuals those interpreting
some individual name in
{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})
.
That is why, we augment the dimension by

|{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})|\times\alpha
so that it is possible to carry all over the runs
the values of the concrete features for such individuals.
Consequently, we set

\beta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(\eta+1)\times\alpha
.
To use the registers in
{\rm REG}(\beta,\mathtt{d})
,
with
\beta
and
\mathtt{d}
defined above,
we follow conventions that happen to be handy and that diverge slightly
from the general notations introduced in Section
3
.
The distinguished registers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{\beta}

referring to values at the current node shall be represented by

x_{1},\ldots,x_{\alpha},x_{a_{0},1},\ldots,x_{a_{0},\alpha},\ldots,x_{a_{\eta-1},1},\ldots,x_{a_{\eta-1},\alpha}.

Hence, implicitly
x_{j}
refers to the value of the concrete feature

f_{j}
, if any. Similarly,
x_{a_{\ell},j}

refers to the value of
f_{j}
for the interpretation
of the individual name
a_{\ell}
.
Furthermore, we use
x_{j}^{i}
(resp.

x_{a_{\ell},j}^{i}
) to refer to the value
of
f_{j}
for the
i
th child
(resp. for the individual name
a_{\ell}
).
However, by construction of
{\mathbb{A}}
, we require
that
x_{a_{\ell},j}
is enforced to be equal to

x_{a_{\ell},j}^{i}
as such registers are dedicated
to store global values.

4.2
Definition of the Constraint Automaton
{\mathbb{A}}

Now, we define the TGCA
{\mathbb{A}}=(Q,\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathtt{d},\beta,Q_{in},\delta,F)
.
Each non-root node

n\in[0,\mathtt{d}-1]^{+}
in a run of the
automaton may represent an element of the interpretation domain, but this is not
systematic.
A node represents a domain element if its location is
not the designated
sink location
—a special marker
to identify
inactive
nodes.
In the active case, the location
encodes a contextual abstraction as previously defined.
By contrast, the root node
\varepsilon

does not correspond to any domain element.
It serves as a technical device to assemble a forest-shaped model into a single data tree:
its immediate children within
[0,\eta-1]

encode the interpretations
of the individual names in the ABox
(recall we can safely assume (UNA)).
The transitions are designed to enforce the semantical properties
of the description logic
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})

based on the contextual abstractions.

Below, we present the definition
of the constraint automaton
{\mathbb{A}}
based on
the definitions in Section
4.1
.

Set of locations.

The set
Q
is defined as
the set
\mathcal{CA}\cup\{\textcircled{$\varepsilon$},\square\}

and
F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}Q
,
where

•

\mathcal{CA}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathcal{GA}\times\mathcal{LA}
is the set
of
contextual abstractions
, where
\mathcal{GA}

is the set of
global abstractions
and
\mathcal{LA}
the set
of
local abstractions
,

•

\textcircled{$\varepsilon$}
is a distinguished location used only at the root (
root location
)
and
Q_{in}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\{\textcircled{$\varepsilon$}\}
,

•

\square
is a
sink location
used to label nodes
that represent no domain elements.

Alphabet.

The alphabet
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is
\{\mathtt{a}\}
;
the automaton
{\mathbb{A}}
does not rely on input symbols—only the structure
of the tree and the tuples in
\mathbb{D}^{\beta}
matter.

Transition relation.

Before providing the formal definition, let us briefly explain its main
principles.
Each non-root node in a run is either associated with a
contextual abstraction representing a candidate element of the interpretation domain,
or is labelled with the sink location
\square
.
If a node is associated with a contextual abstraction,
its concept type encodes the concepts it must belong to in any
corresponding interpretation: if a concept appears in the concept type of a
node, then the interpreted element is required to satisfy that concept
and this creates obligations (see the conditions for general transitions).
Role names are interpreted with the help of two kinds of links:
(i)
direct links
to
the children, where each direction in
[0,\mathtt{d}-1]

is possibly attached to a role name;
and (ii)
symbolic links
connecting a node
to the interpretation of individual names from the ABox. These links are used to
satisfy CD-restrictions, existential restrictions and value restrictions.

Constraints are evaluated using register values drawn from the current
node and its children (as determined by

\lambda
).
The current node stores its own concrete feature values via
the registers
x_{j}

and also the concrete feature values for the interpretation of the individual names
via the registers

x_{a,j}
.
These latter values
must remain
constant during the run, and this
is explicitly enforced
by the transition relation with the help of the constraints. Activity vectors determine
which concrete feature values are defined and
guarantee that only the defined values are referred to in the constraints.
The global abstraction—common to all nodes labelled
by non-sink locations—must remain constant too.
Formally,
the transition relation
\delta
is a subset of

Q\times\mathbf{\Sigma}\times{\rm Cons}(\beta,\mathtt{d})\times Q^{\mathtt{d}}

and contains three kinds of transitions.

(a) Padding transition.

There is a unique padding transition
\mathtt{t}_{\square}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(\square,\mathtt{a},\top,\square,\dots,\square)
.

(b) Root transitions.

From the root location
\textcircled{$\varepsilon$}
, the automaton performs an
initialization step by assigning contextual abstractions to the first

\eta
children, each of which represents the
interpretation of an individual name from the ABox. Each such contextual
abstraction is of the form
(\mathsf{GA}_{i},(T_{i},sl_{i},act_{i}))
.
For each individual name
a_{i}
, the local abstraction assigned to
the
i
-th child must match the corresponding entry in the global abstraction:
specifically, the concept type and activity vector must coincide with

\mathsf{GA}(a_{i})
. In addition, the value of each concrete feature

f_{j}
for
a_{i}
must be consistently stored in the register

x_{a_{i},j}
at each node, which is treated as a constant
throughout the run. This value originates from the local register
x_{j}

of the
i
-th child,
which represents the interpretation of
a_{i}
. Therefore, the consistency
condition
x_{a_{i},j}=x_{j}
must hold at that node.
These requirements
together form the
global information consistency check
.
Beyond this, the local abstraction must correctly reflect the ABox assertions
involving
a_{i}
.

Formally, root transitions are of the form

(\textcircled{$\varepsilon$},\mathtt{a},\Theta_{\mathsf{root}},\mathsf{CA}_{0},\ldots,\mathsf{CA}_{\eta-1},\square,\ldots,\square)
,
where each
\mathsf{CA}_{i}=(\mathsf{GA}_{i},(T_{i},sl_{i},act_{i}))

is a contextual abstraction and
T_{i}
is an

a_{i}
-type.
The constraint
\Theta_{\mathsf{root}}
is equal to the expression

\bigwedge_{i,i^{\prime}\in[0,\eta-1],j\in[1,\alpha],act_{a_{i}}[j]=\top}x_{j}^{i}=x_{a_{i},j}^{i^{\prime}}
.

The transition must also satisfy the following structural conditions:

\mathsf{GA}_{0}=\cdots=\mathsf{GA}_{\eta-1}
and
for all
i\in[0,\eta-1]
,

(T_{i},act_{i})=\mathsf{GA}(a_{i})
,

\{C\mid a_{i}:C\in\mathcal{A}\}\subseteq T_{i},
and

\{(r,b)\mid(a_{i},b):r\in\mathcal{A}\}\subseteq sl_{i}
.

From now on, for each individual name
a\in{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})
,
we let
\mathsf{GA}(a)=(T_{a},act_{a})
, where

T_{a}
denotes the concept type and
act_{a}

the activity vector associated to
a
via the global abstraction
\mathsf{GA}
.
This notation is well-defined due to the global information consistency check
performed by the root transition.

(c) General transitions.

From a location

\mathsf{CA}=(\mathsf{GA},(T,sl,act))
,
we need to find witness individuals for the existential restrictions
and CD–restrictions occurring in
the concept type

T
.
Each witness can be realized in two ways: either through a child,
determined via the branch labeling function
\lambda
,
or via a symbolic link in
sl
pointing to
an individual name.
As for value restrictions, the transition must verify that

all
possible witnesses satisfy the required concept or constraint.
For universal CD–restrictions, the constraint must
hold across all applicable combinations of registers extracted from
those directions and links.
General transitions also maintain some global information
and are of the form

\mathtt{t}=(\mathsf{CA},\mathtt{a},\Theta_{\mathtt{t}},q_{0},\dots,q_{\mathtt{d}-1})
,
where
\mathsf{CA}=(\mathsf{GA},(T,sl,act))

satisfies the conditions below.

1.

Existential restrictions.

For all
\exists r.C\in T
,
there is
(r,a)\in sl
such that
C\in T_{a}

or
i=\lambda(\exists r.C)
,

q_{i}
is of the form

(\mathsf{GA}_{i},(T_{i},sl_{i},act_{i}))

and
C\in T_{i}
.

2.

Value restrictions.

For all
\forall r.C\in T
,
for all
i\in{dir}(r)
with
q_{i}
of the form

(\mathsf{GA}_{i},(T_{i},sl_{i},act_{i}))
,
we have
C\in T_{i}

6
Conclusion

We have considered a class of concrete domains
\mathcal{D}
for which
the consistency problem for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})
can be reduced to
the nonemptiness problem for constraint automata parameterised
by
\mathcal{D}
, leading to the
ExpTime
-membership of the
consistency problem (Theorem
8
). This follows an automata-based approach,
whose versatility allowed us to add ingredients such as
inverse roles (while giving up nominals) and functional role names (Section
5
).
Furthermore, we carefully examined the conditions on the concrete domain
\mathcal{D}

to get the
ExpTime
upper bound. In particular, we have shown
that the nonemptiness problem for constraint automata
is in
ExpTime
whenever
\mathcal{D}
satisfies
the conditions (C1), (C2) and (C3.1) (Theorem
5
).
On the downside, the relationships between
the completion property, homomorphism
\omega
-compactness
and the amalgamation property are still to be characterised.
The decidability/complexity status of
the logics
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{D})
is left open (also evoked in
[
2
, Section 7]
for some
extension).

Appendix E
Complexity analysis for the nonemptiness problem for TGCA

Given a TGCA
{\mathbb{A}}
, let
us consider the parameters below.

•

m=|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{A}}|
.
As a consequence of the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{{\mathbb{A}}}
,
if
\mathcal{D}
has an infinite set of
relations,
m
is the number of predicate symbols occurring in

{\mathbb{A}}
.
Otherwise,
m
is the number of relations in
\mathcal{D}
.

•

\beta
is the number of registers, or equivalently the dimension
of the tuples of data values in data trees,

•

\mathtt{d}
is the branching degree of the trees,

•

k_{0}
is the maximum arity of predicates (fixed if (C2) holds true),

•

\mathtt{MCS}({\mathbb{A}})
is defined as the maximal size of
any
constraint
\Theta
appearing in a transition of
{\mathbb{A}}
.

Construction of types.

Each type is defined over the set
{\rm REG}(\beta,\mathtt{d})
,
containing
\beta(\mathtt{d}+1)
registers/variables. The number of atomic
formulae
over
{\rm REG}(\beta,\mathtt{d})
is bounded by:

|\mathsf{Atoms}({\mathbb{A}})|\leq m\times(\beta(\mathtt{d}+1))^{k_{0}}.

Each type corresponds to a complete assignment of truth values to these atoms,
hence:

|\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})|\leq|\mathsf{STypes}({\mathbb{A}})|=2^{|\mathsf{Atoms}({\mathbb{A}})|}\leq 2^{m\cdot(\beta(\mathtt{d}+1))^{k_{0}}}.

The satisfiability status of each type can be checked by invoking
a decision procedure dedicated to the constraint satisfaction problem

\textsf{CSP}(\mathcal{D})

on a constraint system of size

|\mathsf{Atoms}({\mathbb{A}})|
.
Let us suppose that
\textsf{CSP}(\mathcal{D})
can be solved in
time
\mathcal{O}(2^{\mathtt{p}_{1}(n)-n})

for some polynomial
\mathtt{p}_{1}
if (C3.1) is assumed.
The use of the polynomial
\mathtt{p}_{1}(n)-n
shall be convenient
for the final expression.
Each check
runs in time

\mathcal{O}(2^{\mathtt{p}_{1}(|\mathsf{Atoms}({\mathbb{A}})|)-|\mathsf{Atoms}({\mathbb{A}})|})
.
Thus, the total time for constructing the set
\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})
is:

2^{m\times(\beta(\mathtt{d}+1))^{k_{0}}}\times\mathcal{O}(2^{\mathtt{p}_{1}(m\times(\beta(\mathtt{d}+1))^{k_{0}})-m\times(\beta(\mathtt{d}+1))^{k_{0}}})=\mathcal{O}(2^{\mathtt{p}_{1}(m\times(\beta(\mathtt{d}+1))^{k_{0}})}).

(1)

Construction of the BTA.

The locations in the Büchi tree automaton
{\mathbb{B}}

are of the form
(q,\tau)

with
q\in Q
and
\tau\in\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})
.
So,

|Q^{\prime}|=|Q|\times|\mathsf{SatSTypes}({\mathbb{A}})|\leq|Q|\cdot 2^{m\times(\beta(\mathtt{d}+1))^{k_{0}}}.

A transition in the Büchi tree automaton is derived from a TGCA transition

(q,\mathtt{a},\Theta,q_{0},\dots,q_{\mathtt{d}-1})

and symbolic types

Appendix H
Complexity analysis about the nonemptiness test for the TGCA
{\mathbb{A}}

The size of the TGCA

{\mathbb{A}}

is bounded as follows:

•

Locations.

The set of locations
Q
consists of:

–

the special root location
\textcircled{$\varepsilon$}
,

–

the sink location
\square
,

–

all contextual abstractions
(\mathsf{GA},\mathsf{LA})
, where:

*

\mathsf{LA}=(T,sl,act)
, and

*

\mathsf{GA}:{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathsf{CTypes}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathsf{ACT}
.

The number of concept types is at most
2^{|\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})|}
.
The number of sets of symbolic links is at most

2^{|{\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}(\mathcal{O})|\cdot|{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})|}
.
The number of activity vectors is equal to

2^{\alpha}
with
\alpha=|{\sf N}_{\mathbf{CF}}(\mathcal{O})|
.
Consequently, we have

|Q|\in\mathcal{O}\left(\underbrace{|{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})|\cdot 2^{(|\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})|+\alpha)}}_{\text{global abstraction}}\cdot\underbrace{2^{|\mathsf{Sub}(\mathcal{O})|}}_{\text{local concept type}}\cdot\underbrace{2^{\alpha}}_{\text{local activity vector}}\cdot\underbrace{2^{|{\sf N}_{\mathbf{R}}(\mathcal{O})|\cdot|{\sf N}_{\mathbf{I}}(\mathcal{O})|}}_{\text{local symbolic links}}\right)

which is exponential in
size(\mathcal{O})
.
So there exists a polynomial
\mathtt{p}
such that

|Q|\in\mathcal{O}(2^{\mathtt{p}(size(\mathcal{O}))}).

•

Transitions.

Every transition is determined by a tuple of locations

(q,q_{0},\dots,q_{\mathtt{d}-1})

and a unique constraint
\Theta
computed from these locations as explained in
Section
4.2
. Hence the number of possible transitions is at most:

|\delta|\leq|Q|^{\mathtt{d}+1}
. Recall that
|\mathbf{\Sigma}|=1
.

•

Constraint size.

Each constraint
\Theta_{\mathtt{t}}
is the conjunction of:

–

CD–constraints
\Theta_{C}
for all
C\in\mathrm{CD}_{\exists}(T)\cup\mathrm{CD}_{\forall}(T)
.
For a CD–restriction of arity
k
, the corresponding constraint ranges over at most

|{\rm REG}(\beta,\mathtt{d})|^{k}

register tuples, hence is of size exponential in
k
. Since the value
k
is bounded
by a constant depending on the ontology, and the number of CD–restrictions is
linear in
size(\mathcal{O})
,
this part has size at most exponential in
size(\mathcal{O})

Handling constraint assertions

Beyond concept and role assertions, we can extend the ABox to
include assertions involving concrete features
and expressing relationships between concrete feature values of individual names.
Such assertions are called

, and have the form

is an arbitrary
\mathcal{D}
-constraint.
The constraint assertions of
the form

for some
k
-ary predicate

are called predicate assertions in the literature,
see e.g.
[
13
]
.
The
f_{i}
’s are concrete features and the
a_{j}
’s
are individual names. Such assertions can be handled in
[
13
, Section 4]
.
In particular, if the concrete domain
\mathcal{D}
is equipped with a family of unary
predicates
=_{\mathbb{d}}
interpreted by the singleton set
\{\mathbb{d}\}
,
then we can treat the special case of a
feature assertion
—that is, a statement
of the form
f(a)=\mathbb{d}
,
which asserts that the concrete
feature
f
takes the value
\mathbb{d}
on the interpretation of the individual
name
a
.

An interpretation
\mathcal{I}
satisfies the constraint assertion if

\mathcal{D}\models\Theta(f_{1}^{\mathcal{I}}(a_{1}^{\mathcal{I}}),\dots,f_{k}^{\mathcal{I}}(a_{k}^{\mathcal{I}})).

Encoding into the automaton.

To handle constraint assertions in the construction
of
{\mathbb{A}}
, we add to the
unique constraint
\Theta_{\mathsf{root}}
occurring only
in the root transitions,
constraints

\Theta(x_{a_{i_{1}},j_{1}},\dots,x_{a_{i_{k}},j_{k}})
.
Furthermore, for all

,
we require that
act_{a_{i_{\ell}}}[j_{\ell}]
is equal to

in order to ensure that

is defined for each individual name

.

Theorem 14
.

The consistency problem for
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{D})

is in
k
-
ExpTime
if
\mathcal{D}

.

Unlike the approach in
[
13
]
, our framework does not exclude concrete domains with infinitely many
predicate symbols such as

by relying on conditions (C1), (C2), (C3.1), and (C4).
This makes it possible to directly express arbitrary singleton equality predicates as feature assertions,
without requiring
the

condition on the concrete domain as done
for
[
13
, Theorem 11]
.
However, this assumes that
\mathcal{D}
includes unary predicates

In

thereby allowing singleton predicates to be simulated via isomorphic renaming. In contrast, our

\mathfrak{v}_{n}(x_{a_{j},\ell})=\vec{z}_{n}[\alpha\times(j+2)+\ell]

and for all
i<\mathtt{d}
,
\mathfrak{v}_{n}(x_{a_{j},\ell}^{i})=\vec{z}_{n}[\alpha\times(j+2)+\ell]
too (these last values are never
used).
Then

\mathfrak{v}_{n}\models\Theta_{n}
, where
\Theta_{n}

is the constraint of the transition
\rho(n)
.

If
\rho^{{\dagger}}(n)=\square
, then
\Theta_{n}=\top
and
the property obviously holds.
If
\rho^{{\dagger}}(n)=\textcircled{$\varepsilon$}
(i.e.
n=\varepsilon
),
then
\mathfrak{v}_{n}\models\Theta_{n}
for the same reason
as in the proof of Lemma
7
.

If
\rho^{{\dagger}}(n)
is of the
form
(\mathsf{PA}_{n},(T_{n},act_{n}))
,
with

\rho(n)=(\rho^{{\dagger}}(n),\mathtt{a},\Theta_{n},\rho^{{\dagger}}(n\cdot 0),\ldots,\rho^{{\dagger}}(n\cdot(\mathtt{d}-1)))
, then

\Theta_{n}
is equal to

\Theta_{\mathtt{t}}=\left(\bigwedge_{C\in\mathrm{CD}_{\exists}(T_{n})\cup\mathrm{CD}_{\forall}(T_{n})}\Theta_{C}\right)\wedge\left(\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[0,\mathtt{d}-1]\\
q_{n\cdot i}\neq\square\end{subarray}}\ \ \bigwedge_{j\in[1,\alpha],act_{n}[j]=\top}x_{j}^{\uparrow\ i}=x_{j}\right).

Let us show that
\mathfrak{v}_{n}\models\Theta_{\mathtt{t}}
.

•

Let
i\in[0,\mathtt{d}-1]
such that

\rho^{{\dagger}}(n\cdot i)\neq\square
,

j\in[1,\alpha]
, and
act_{n}[j]=\top
.
By definition of
\mathfrak{v}_{n}
,

\mathfrak{v}_{n}(x_{j})=\vec{z}_{n}[j]
.
By definition of
\mathfrak{v}_{n}
,

\mathfrak{v}_{n}(x_{j}^{\uparrow\ i})=\vec{z}_{n}[j]
too.

•

Let
C\in\mathrm{CD}_{\exists}(T_{n})
with

C=\exists\ v_{1}:p_{1},\dots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \Theta
.
Let us show that
\mathfrak{v}_{n}\models\bigvee_{(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\in Z_{1}\times\cdots\times Z_{k}}\Theta(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
.
We have seen earlier that we have picked
a tuple
(\mathbb{d}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{d}_{k})\in p_{1}^{\mathcal{I}}\times\cdots\times p_{k}^{\mathcal{I}}
such that

[v_{1}\mapsto\mathbb{d}_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\mapsto\mathbb{d}_{k}]\models\Theta,

from which
\rho^{{\dagger}}(n\cdot i)
is defined for all
i\in[0,\mathtt{d}-1]
.
For all
j\in[1,k]
such that

p_{j}
is of length two of the form

R_{j}^{\prime}\cdot f^{\prime}_{j}
,
there is
d_{j}\in\Delta^{\mathcal{I}}
such that

(\mathfrak{g}(n),d_{j})\in(R^{\prime}_{j})^{\mathcal{I}}
and

f_{j}^{\prime\mathcal{I}}(d_{j})=\mathbb{d}_{j}
.
For all
i^{\prime}
such that

\lambda^{-1}(i^{\prime})=(\exists\ v_{1}:p_{1},\ldots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \Theta,j)
for such
j
’s,

\mathfrak{g}(n\cdot i^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}d_{j}
and

\rho^{{\dagger}}(n\cdot i)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}(\mathsf{PA}_{i},locabs(d_{j},\mathcal{I}))
.
For all the other
i^{\prime}
,
\mathfrak{g}(n\cdot i^{\prime})
is undefined
and
\rho^{{\dagger}}(n\cdot i^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\square
.
Let us define a tuple
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\in Z_{1}\times\cdots\times Z_{k}

such that
(\mathfrak{v}_{n}(y_{1}),\ldots,\mathfrak{v}_{n}(y_{k}))=(\mathbb{d}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{d}_{k})
,
which allows us to conclude that

\mathfrak{v}_{n}\models\bigvee_{(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\in Z_{1}\times\cdots\times Z_{k}}\Theta(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
.

Let
\gamma\in[1,k]
.
If
p_{\gamma}=f_{\ell}
for some
\ell
, then
let us take
y_{\gamma}=x_{\ell}

and
\mathfrak{v}_{n}(x_{\ell})=\vec{z}_{n}[\ell]

by definition of
\mathfrak{v}_{n}
.
Moreover,
\mathbb{d}_{\gamma}=f_{\ell}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathfrak{g}(n))

by the semantics of
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{D})
.
By definition of
\vec{z}_{n}[\ell]
, we have

\vec{z}_{n}[\ell]=f_{\ell}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathfrak{g}(n))
.
Consequently,
\mathfrak{v}_{n}(y_{\gamma})=\mathbb{d}_{\gamma}
.
Finally, since
f_{\ell}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathfrak{g}(n))
is defined,

act_{n}[\ell]=\top
and
therefore
y_{\gamma}\in Z_{\gamma}
.

If
p_{\gamma}=R_{\ell^{\prime}}\cdot f_{\ell}

for some
\ell,\ell^{\prime}
, then we perform a case analysis to define

y_{\gamma}
. Let
m
be the parent of
n
.

Subcase
m\neq\varepsilon
:

If
(\mathfrak{g}(n),d_{\gamma})\in(R_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\prime})^{\mathcal{I}}
and
d_{\gamma}=\mathfrak{g}(m)
,
then
y_{\gamma}=x_{\ell}^{\uparrow}
.
Otherwise (
d_{\gamma}\neq\mathfrak{g}(m)
),

y_{\gamma}=x_{\ell}^{i}
with

\lambda^{-1}(i)=(C^{\star},\gamma)
.
Moreover,
\mathbb{d}_{\gamma}=f_{\ell}^{\mathcal{I}}(d_{\gamma})
by definition of

\mathbb{d}_{\gamma}
and

\mathfrak{g}(n\cdot i)=d_{\gamma}
by definition of
\mathfrak{g}
.
We have
\mathfrak{v}_{n}(x_{\ell}^{i})=\vec{z}_{n\cdot i}[\ell]

by definition of
\mathfrak{v}_{n}

and
\vec{z}_{n\cdot i}[\ell]=f_{\ell}^{\mathcal{I}}(\mathfrak{g}(n\cdot i))

by definition of
\vec{z}_{n\cdot i}
. Consequently,

\mathbb{d}_{\gamma}=\mathfrak{v}_{n}(y_{\gamma})
.
Since
\mathfrak{g}(n\cdot i)
is defined,

\rho^{{\dagger}}(n\cdot i)\neq\square
,

i\in dir(r_{\ell^{\prime}})
and
act_{n\cdot i}[\ell]=\top
,
we get
y_{\gamma}\in Z_{\gamma}
.

Subcase
m=\varepsilon
:

Suppose
n=a_{G}^{\mathcal{I}}

for some
G\in[0,\eta-1]
.
If
d_{\gamma}\in\{a_{0}^{\mathcal{I}},\ldots,a_{\eta-1}^{\mathcal{I}}\}
,
then let us take
y_{\gamma}=x_{a_{H},\ell}

for some
H
such that
d_{\gamma}=a_{H}^{\mathcal{I}}
.
Moreover,
\mathbb{d}_{\gamma}=f_{\ell}^{\mathcal{I}}(a_{H}^{\mathcal{I}})

by the semantics of
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{D})
.
By definition of
\mathfrak{v}_{n}
,

\mathfrak{v}_{n}(x_{a_{H},\ell})=\vec{z}_{n}[\alpha\times(H+2)+\ell]
.
By definition of
\vec{z}_{n}
,

\vec{z}_{n}[\alpha\times(H+2)+\ell]=f_{\ell}^{\mathcal{I}}(a_{H}^{\mathcal{I}})
.
Consequently,
\mathfrak{v}_{n}(y_{\gamma})=\mathbb{d}_{\gamma}
.
Since
(\mathfrak{g}(n),d_{\gamma})\in(R_{\ell^{\prime}})^{\mathcal{I}}
,

f_{\ell}^{\mathcal{I}}(d_{\gamma})
is defined
and
\mathfrak{f}(a_{H})[\ell]=\top
,
we get

(a_{G},a_{H}):R_{\ell^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{A}

and therefore
y_{\gamma}\in Z_{\gamma}
.
Otherwise (
d_{\gamma}\not\in\{a_{0}^{\mathcal{I}},\ldots,a_{\eta-1}^{\mathcal{I}}\}
), the treatment is exactly as
in the previous subcase.

This completes the case

C=\exists\ v_{1}:p_{1},\dots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \Theta

and the proof of

\mathfrak{v}_{n}\models\bigvee_{(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\in Z_{1}\times\cdots\times Z_{k}}\Theta(y_{1},\dots,y_{k}).

•

Let
C\in\mathrm{CD}_{\forall}(T_{n})
with

C=\forall\ v_{1}:p_{1},\dots,v_{k}:p_{k}.\ \Theta
.
In order to show that
\mathfrak{v}_{n}\models\bigwedge_{(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})\in Z_{1}\times\cdots\times Z_{k}}\Theta(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
, we proceed similarly to the
previous case by performing a case analysis on the elements in
the
Z_{\gamma}
’s. The details are omitted herein. ∎
