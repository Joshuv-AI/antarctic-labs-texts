Beyond “normative” loss: social context, loss integration, and adaptation to parental bereavement in midlife
Authors: Lior Baruch, Shay Ohayon, Einat Yehene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMidlife parental loss is often viewed as a normative transition, yet it remains socially underrecognized and under-investigated despite its significant psychological impact. Such perception fosters social disenfranchisement and may impede loss integration. This study investigated the psychological impact of midlife parental bereavement by comparing indicators of mental health and well-being, between bereaved and non-bereaved adults. Furthermore, it examined how social context influences grief intensity through the mediating role of loss integration. A sample of 423 adults aged 35–65 was categorized into dual-parental loss (n = 122), single-parental loss (n = 122), and non-bereaved (n = 179) groups. Measures included general psychological distress (PHQ-4), scales of happiness and subjective health, grief intensity (TRIG-PR), loss integration (ISLES-SF), and perceived social support and social recognition. Results showed no significant group differences in general psychological distress, happiness, health, or grief intensity. However, structural equation modeling showed that social recognition was positively associated with loss integration, which in turn predicted lower grief intensity (R² = 0.43). In contrast, socio-emotional support was directly associated with lower general psychological distress (R² = 0.09) but not with grief-specific outcomes. These findings underscore the distinct roles of social mechanisms in midlife bereavement and highlight the importance of social recognition in facilitating adaptive grief integration among this age group. The study advocates for community-based policies and therapeutic approaches that validate midlife bereavement during this unique developmental life stage.Keywords: Parental bereavement, Midlife transition, Social recognition, Loss integration, Disenfranchised grief

Introduction

Although the loss of a parent during midlife is often regarded as a natural and expected life event, this perception can obscure the significant emotional and psychological challenges it entails. Despite its normative timing, parental loss in midlife represents a major life transition that demands psychological adaptation and often involves shifts in family roles, caregiving responsibilities, and intergenerational identity [25, 32, 63].

While research has thoroughly documented the impact of parental loss in childhood and adolescence [39], studies examining bereavement during midlife and its psychological implications remain relatively limited and uneven. One reason may be its classification as a “natural” loss within the grief hierarchy [11], a framing that implies lower societal recognition and support. However, both Doka [11] and Stroebe and Schut [60] emphasize that even normative losses require attention to the social context in which grief unfolds, as social mechanisms can critically shape loss integration and psychological adaptation.

The current study addresses this gap by examining how midlife parental loss relates to general psychological distress and well-being, and by testing the role of social mechanisms in facilitating loss integration and adaptation among bereaved adult children.

Midlife as a transitional life stage

Midlife, typically defined as ages 40 to 60, is marked by increasing responsibilities and significant life transitions [29]. Despite mounting societal pressures, midlife remains underexplored in psychological research [27]. Adults often navigate demanding careers while providing care for both children and aging parents, managing physical and cognitive responsibilities [27]. This life stage is also marked by shifting family dynamics, including rising divorce rates among older adults. While divorce has declined among younger populations, adults aged 50 and older now account for over one-third of U.S. divorces [8]. Together with age-related health changes, these concurrent stressors may increase vulnerability to depression, anxiety, and emotional exhaustion [10, 19, 27].

The emotional impact of parental loss in midlife

Within this already burdened context, parental loss may be especially destabilizing, not only as another developmental stressor but as a loss that uniquely disrupts emotional equilibrium and identity continuity [39, 62, 63]. Its impact is evident even when the loss is anticipated and accompanied by pre-death grief [58]. Midlife bereaved adults report increased depressive symptoms, reduced self-esteem, diminished personal mastery, and declining physical health, often with long-lasting effects [32, 38]. The experience also heightens mortality awareness and existential reflection, prompting a reassessment of personal values, identity, and close relationships [26, 52, 63]. Gender differences in grief expression further shape emotional responses and coping strategies [23].

These processes reverberate across family systems, reshaping dynamics and roles [15], particularly when the bereaved must care for a surviving parent alongside other obligations [30, 59]. Such demands may delay grief, with emotional distress intensifying after the second parent’s death [39, 51]. In cases of dual-parental loss, the absence of generational anchoring can lead to emotional disconnection and renewed identity disruption, as the transition to “orphanhood” is often marked by compounded grief and psychological vulnerability [43].

The social context: support and recognition and their role in loss integration

Two influential social mechanisms - social-emotional support and social recognition - remain understudied in midlife parental loss, despite their crucial role in grief adaptation. Social-emotional support, defined as the perception of being understood, valued, and cared for, has long been linked to reduced psychological distress [9]. More than network size, emotional support predicts lower depression and anxiety and improved well-being after bereavement [33]. When bereaved individuals lack someone to listen or offer reassurance, grief symptoms often worsen. Moreover, support must align with the griever’s needs to be effective; mismatched or superficial responses can heighten emotional disconnection.

Social recognition, in contrast, refers to the societal validation of grief. Doka, [11] defined disenfranchised grief as mourning that lacks public legitimacy. Because midlife parental loss is viewed as a normative event, it is often symbolically acknowledged but not emotionally validated—leading to partial disenfranchisement [54]. This lack of recognition can obstruct loss-integration and increase distress [49, 64]. While emotional support stems from close relationships, social recognition reflects cultural norms that affirm grief’s legitimacy [7]. Its absence creates a paradox: midlife adults are expected to cope independently, even when their grief is profound [40, 53]. The grief hierarchy reinforces this by assigning lower social priority to “timely” losses such as parental death [11, 25].

Incorporating the loss into one’s self-concept, identity, and life narrative is central to psychological adaptation following bereavement and to regaining emotional balance [45]. Successful loss integration has been associated with reduced grief severity, lower depression and anxiety, and greater life satisfaction [18, 42]. However, this process depends not only on individual coping but also on external validation. Public rituals and symbolic validation provide critical scaffolding for the integration of loss [3, 47, 57]. More broadly, social recognition enhances emotional regulation, supports continuing bonds with the deceased, and reduces long-term grief [22, 46]. However, to the best of our knowledge these bereavement processes were rarely studied in the aftermath of parental loss in midlife.

The present study

Taken together, parental loss in midlife remains understudied, despite its potential psychological impact during an already demanding life stage. Although often viewed as a “natural” developmental loss, emerging literature suggests it carries distinct emotional burdens that may go socially unrecognized. To this end, the present study aims to address these gaps by: (1) examining the impact of midlife parental loss on mental health (general psychological distress, grief intensity) and well-being (global happiness and subjective physical health) indicators, by comparing non-bereaved adults, those who lost one parent, and those who lost both; (2) exploring how social-emotional support and social recognition mitigate psychological distress and grief intensity among bereaved adult children and the role loss integration play in this relationship. Findings may provide social recognition for this overlooked population and inform interventions to promote well-being in midlife. Our hypotheses were: (1) Individuals who experienced midlife parental loss would report greater general psychological general distress (GPD; PHQ-4) and lower global happiness and subjective physical health) than those who had not; (2) Individuals with dual parental loss would report higher grief intensity than those with single loss, though differences may vary due to limited existing research; (3) Higher social-emotional support and social recognition would be associated with greater loss integration, which in turn would predict lower general psychological distress and grief intensity.

Method

Participants and procedure

Midlife is commonly conceptualized as spanning ages 40–60; however, developmental research increasingly emphasizes the fluidity of this period and its overlap with both earlier and later adulthood [27]. Accordingly, the present study included a broader age range (35–65) to capture heterogeneity in midlife-related experiences. Participants were recruited between February and June 2024 during a period marked by war in Israel. Recruitment efforts targeted individuals using digital platforms, social media groups tailored to midlife adults and bereavement, and professional networks. Snowball sampling was also employed to ensure a diverse reflective sample. The study employed a cross-sectional, observational design and did not include experimental manipulations. Data were collected via Qualtrics. The 7–10 min survey included sociodemographic items and bereavement-specific questions (e.g., years since loss), followed by standardized questionnaires. IP tracking prevented duplicate responses, and electronic informed consent was obtained.

The sample was predominantly female (81.3%) and Jewish (97.6%), with a mean age of 51.81 (SD = 8.67). Most were married (70.2%), employed (86.3%), and highly educated (73.8% academic degrees). Participants were divided into: dual-parental loss (n = 122), single-parental loss (n = 122), and a non-bereaved control group (n = 179). Eligibility for bereaved groups required the loss to occur at age 35 or older, with at least one year elapsed since the most recent loss. Within the dual-loss group, 40 participants with incomplete grief questionnaire responses were excluded from grief-related analyses, resulting in a subsample of 82 for those analyses. Excluded participants were significantly older than those retained (M = 58.05 vs. M = 55.77 years), with no other significant demographic differences; age was therefore included as a covariate in all statistical models. Measures

Socio-demographic and background questionnaire

This section collected data on participants’ age, gender, marital status, education level, number of children, religiosity, and socioeconomic status. Exposure to prior traumatic life events was assessed using a checklist from which participants selected all events they had experienced (e.g., serious medical condition, job loss, bereavement, terror attack, physical assault). A count variable was created by summing the total number of events endorsed, with “none of the above” coded as 0; this variable is referred to as ‘number of past traumatic events’ in the analyses. Past psychiatric diagnoses were assessed via a checklist of common disorders (e.g., depression, anxiety, mood disorder, addiction), from which participants selected all that applied. A count variable was created by summing the total number of diagnoses endorsed, with “none of the above” coded as 0; this variable is referred to as ‘number of psychiatric diagnoses’ in the analyses. For bereaved participants, additional information was collected regarding parental loss specifics, including time since loss, which was measured in years elapsed since the most recent parental loss.

Well-being indicators

Two validated single-item scales adapted from Marks et al., [38] were used to assess emotional well-being and health perceptions. Global happiness was assessed with the item: ‘Taking things all together, how would you say things are these days?’ (1 = very unhappy to 7 = very happy). Subjective physical health was assessed with: ‘Compared to other people your age, how would you describe your health?’ (1 = very poor to 5 = excellent). These scales demonstrate high test-retest reliability (r = .86; [38]). The happiness item demonstrates strong associations with multi-item measures including the Oxford Happiness Inventory [1]. The health item has been widely used across survey research and has demonstrated validity as both a proxy for objective health status and as a perception of health [17]. More broadly, single-item measures of well-being have demonstrated adequate reliability and validity across diverse populations and contexts [2, 35].

General Psychological Distress (GPD)

GPD was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4; [34]), which combines two items for depressive symptoms (PHQ-2) and two for anxiety (GAD-2). Participants rated symptom frequency over the past two weeks on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all to 3 = nearly every day). A Hebrew version was used, adapted from validated translations of the PHQ-9 and GAD-7. Internal consistency was acceptable (Cronbach’s α = 0.69 for the depression subscale and α = 0.76 for the anxiety subscale), consistent with previous Israeli samples [4].

Grief intensity

Grief was measured using the Texas Revised Inventory of Grief–Present Scale (TRIG-PR; [14, 16]), a 13-item validated scale assessing responses such as acceptance and intrusive thoughts (e.g., “I still cry when I think of my parent who died”). Participants responded on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree). This study utilized the Hebrew version [37]. For participants with dual-parental loss, the TRIG-PR was completed for each parent and averaged. Internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.926).

Loss integration

The Integration of Stressful Life Experiences Scale-Short Form (ISLES-SF; [24]) assessed how individuals integrate loss into their identity (e.g., “I have made sense of this event”). Responses were recorded on a 5-point scale. The scale was translated into Hebrew and adapted by replacing “the event” with “the loss of my parent.” In this study, internal consistency was excellent (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.863).

Social recognition measure

This measure was specifically developed for the current study to assess perceived social acknowledgment of parental loss in midlife. Drawing on the conceptual framework of the Social Acknowledgment Questionnaire (SAQ; [36]) and informed by theoretical perspectives on disenfranchised grief [11], two items were designed to capture the unique context of midlife parental loss. Participants rated their agreement on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating lower perceived social recognition. The items included: “I feel that society expects me to quickly move on from the loss of my parent because of my age and life stage” and “My grief over my parent is often minimized or ignored by others due to the expectation that losing a parent in midlife is ‘natural’.” Internal consistency was satisfactory (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.702), and the high inter-item correlation provides preliminary evidence of convergent validity. Study-specific item development was necessitated by the absence of a validated instrument for this population; the SAQ targets trauma survivors and PTSD populations and does not adequately capture the disenfranchised grief experience of bereaved midlife adults. This approach is consistent with Hamama-Raz and Solomon [22], who similarly developed a purpose-built scale when no existing instrument fit their bereaved population.

Social-emotional support

A single-item measure [33] was assessed to evaluate perceived social-emotional support: “When you need someone to talk to about your problems or concerns, do you think your family, relatives, or friends are willing to listen?” Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all to 5 = very much). The measure was translated into Hebrew and adjusted to ensure conceptual equivalence while maintaining clarity and consistency. Prior research supports the validity of single-item approaches for assessing perceived social support, demonstrating strong correlations with the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS; [5, 56]).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 25, except for the structural equation modeling (SEM), which was performed using R 4.3.0 with the ‘lavaan’ package [55]. Preliminary analyses were conducted to evaluate sociodemographic and background differences across the three study groups using chi-square tests, independent sample t-tests, and one-way ANOVA. These analyses aimed to ensure group comparability, as a prerequisite for subsequent comparisons. To address the first and second hypotheses, which posit systematic differences in GPD (anxiety and depression) and well-being indicators (physical health and global happiness) across the three study groups, multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) while controlling for age, socioeconomic status and number of past traumatic events. For grief intensity, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted with the same covariates.

Addressing our third hypothesis, the analysis focused exclusively on participants who had experienced parental loss (N = 204). As no significant differences were found between the two bereavement groups, we combined the single-parent and dual-parent loss groups. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to assess the relationships between key study variables. Finally, path analyses using structural equation modeling (SEM) were performed to examine the mediation hypotheses. The conceptual model involved loss integration served as the mediator between each of two independent predictors - social-emotional support and social recognition - and two separate outcome variables: grief intensity and general psychological distress (GPD). Age, religiosity, socioeconomic status, number of children, number of past traumatic events, number of psychiatric diagnoses, and time since loss were included as covariates. We calculated the direct and indirect effects with 95% bootstrapped confidence intervals (CIs), which comprised 5000 random re-samples. Model`s goodness of fit was evaluated using standard fit indices: comparative fit index (CFI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR).

Results

To evaluate whether the sample size was adequate for the SEM mediation analysis, we conducted a post-hoc power analysis for the focal indirect effect using the semPower package for R. We found that the minimal standardized indirect effect that could be detected in the current sample with a power of 80% was 0.04.

Table 1 presents the distribution of sociodemographic variables across the three study groups. As Table 1 shows, significant differences emerged in age, socioeconomic status, and number of past traumatic events between the groups. Specifically, participants in the dual-loss group exhibited the highest mean age, aligning with the expected life-course patterns of parental loss, whereas those in the no-loss group were the youngest. Socioeconomic status also varied significantly across the groups. Additionally, the dual-loss group reported a higher number of past traumatic events than the single-loss and no-loss groups did.

Group differences in mental health and well-being indicators

Multivariate analysis of GPD, global happiness, and subjective physical health showed no statistically significant differences between the three study groups (Wilks’ λ = 0.99, F (6, 830) = 0.91, p = .490). Similarly, a comparison of grief intensity between the single- and dual-parental loss groups revealed no significant differences, and therefore were combined in subsequent analysis.

Structural equation model parameter estimates

As can be seen in Fig. 1 and Table 3, social recognition demonstrated a significant direct effect on grief intensity (β = 0.13, p = .020) and a significant indirect effect via loss integration (β = 0.10, p < .001). Given that higher scores on the social recognition scale indicate lower perceived recognition, these effects reflect that lower social recognition was associated with higher grief intensity both directly and through reduced loss integration. Social-emotional support, by contrast, showed no significant direct or indirect effect on grief intensity (β = 0.10, p = .110; indirect effect: β = -0.03, p = .375).

Concerning GPD, social-emotional support showed a significant direct negative effect (β = -0.16, p = .009), indicating that higher perceived social-emotional support was associated with lower psychological distress. Neither social recognition (direct effect: β = -0.02, p = .774; indirect effect: β = 0.03, p = .193) nor social-emotional support (indirect effect: β = -0.01, p = .504) showed significant effects on GPD via loss integration. Given the reverse coding of the social recognition scale, the non-significant effects indicate that variations in perceived recognition were not associated with psychological distress through loss integration.

Discussion

This study examined the impact of midlife parental loss on general psychological distress (GPD) and well-being, as well as how social mechanisms - support and recognition - affect adaptation to bereavement. By focusing on this rarely examined age group, the findings offer a nuanced understanding of the social mechanisms governing this life stage.

The effect of losing a parent on mental health and well-being

Results indicated no significant differences in GPD or well-being (happiness, physical health) between midlife adults who experienced parental loss and those who did not. Similarly, grief intensity did not differ significantly between single and dual parental-loss groups. While the limited literature addressing parental loss in midlife points to a more complex and heterogeneous picture [23], these findings extend and complicate prior work suggesting that midlife parental loss exacerbates psychological distress [38] and call into question whether the number of losses alone captures the complexity of grief at this life stage. This lack of differentiation suggests that the impact of bereavement may be shaped or obscured by developmental and contextual factors unique to midlife.

The Masking Effect of Midlife Stress Burden. A key explanation for these null findings is the high burden of stressors characteristic of midlife, including professional and financial responsibilities and family obligations, which diminish well-being regardless of bereavement [10, 19, 20, 30]. In our sample, one-third of participants were unmarried and nearly one-third reported multiple past traumatic events. This elevated baseline stress may obscure the specific psychological effects of parental loss.

Notably, 22% of our non-bereaved group were active caregivers, with 69.2% reporting their parent was “physically present but psychologically absent.” Among the bereaved, 60% provided care prior to death, with half describing a similar psychological absence. This form of ambiguous loss may elicit grief even in non-bereaved individuals, blurring distinctions between pre-death decline and the event of death itself.

Contextual Confound: The Ceiling Effect of National Trauma. Data collection (February–June 2024) occurred during the “Iron Swords” war following the October 7th, 2023 attacks on Israel. This pervasive national trauma likely functioned as a universal stressor, elevating baseline distress and creating a ceiling effect that obscured nuanced differences in personal bereavement. More broadly, collective trauma can simultaneously amplify personal grief while embedding it within shared national mourning, complicating the detection of grief-specific responses and blurring the boundaries between personal and collective loss [48]. As Hamama-Raz et al., [21] demonstrated, grief under collective trauma may be intensified but embedded within generalized distress, resulting in heterogeneous profiles rather than clear elevations in symptom severity. Thus, it becomes difficult to detect distinct grief-related patterns using broad psychological measures. This “bereavement overload” [61] makes isolating the incremental effect of personal loss difficult.

Taken together, these findings underscore the need for further research that directly compares bereaved and non-bereaved adults in midlife, using designs sensitive to caregiving histories, loss characteristics, and concurrent stress exposures, beyond what can be inferred from predominantly longitudinal approaches.

Conceptual model: social mechanisms, loss integration, and grief adaptation

Given the relative scarcity of research on parental loss in midlife [25], this study proposed a conceptual model emphasizing the role of social recognition in grief adaptation within an under-researched developmental and family life stage. Unlike bereavement earlier in life, midlife parental loss unfolds within established identities, careers, and social roles [27, 63], and within ongoing family responsibilities and intergenerational role expectations, rendering the social context particularly salient [40, 54].

Our findings differentiate the functions of social mechanisms: social recognition - defined as societal validation of grief - was associated with lower grief intensity both directly and indirectly through greater loss integration, whereas social-emotional support was linked only to general psychological distress, without mediation via loss integration. Higher levels of social recognition were strongly related to greater loss integration, which in turn predicted reduced grief intensity. These findings suggest that recognition operates not only as an emotional resource, but as a family-embedded process that facilitates identity reorganization following parental loss. Thus, when grief is socially validated, individuals more effectively integrate the loss into their identity [22, 41].

This context is particularly relevant in midlife, as societal expectations often prioritize self-sufficiency over emotional vulnerability [27], especially within family systems that rely on midlife adults as providers, caregivers, and stabilizing figures. Consequently, a lack of social recognition can cause internal conflict between emotional needs and societal roles, exacerbating isolation [11, 53]. Norms positioning midlife adults as pillars of stability often discourage open expression and delay integration, reinforcing the social environment’s role in shaping meaning and narrative [18]. In this way, framing parental loss in midlife as “normative” may inadvertently obscure its disruptive effects on family roles and psychological adaptation.

Our model expands previous research identifying loss integration as a key mediator of grief [42, 46] by providing empirical evidence for its relationship with social recognition. The extent to which grief is legitimized influences an individual’s capacity to process and integrate the experience [3, 47, 64, 67]. Importantly, this legitimization often occurs, or fails to occur, within family, workplace, and community contexts. Beyond lowering grief intensity, integration and meaning-making foster resilience across work, social belonging, and family domains [51–53].

The limits of emotional social support in bereavement

While social-emotional support improves general psychological distress (GPD), it does not appear to facilitate loss integration or reduce grief intensity. Findings show that emotional support neither correlates with social recognition nor mediates loss integration, underscoring a critical distinction between these mechanisms. While support reduces GPD, it lacks the specific validation necessary for grief processing, a function unique to social recognition [57].

This differentiation is vital for midlife populations, a group for whom family- and role-based transitions have been studied far less extensively than early-life bereavement [10]. Although social-emotional support protects against GPD, it is less effective for alleviating prolonged grief or facilitating identity-level integration [6, 57]. These results challenge conventional support-focused models by identifying social recognition as the primary predictor of integration. They further suggest that treating midlife parental loss as a routine or expected event may limit both research attention and intervention effectiveness. This aligns with research indicating that support alone cannot address the complexities of unacknowledged or minimized grief [12]. For grief to be integrated rather than suppressed, it must be legitimized within the individual’s social environment [11].

This distinction warrants further theoretical elaboration. Social-emotional support operates primarily at the interpersonal level — it reflects the perception of being heard, valued, and cared for within close relationships, and its effects are therefore most evident in reducing generalized psychological distress. Social recognition, by contrast, operates at a broader societal level, reflecting collective validation of the grief experience itself [36, 44]. Its effects are therefore grief-specific: when society legitimizes a loss, the bereaved individual is freed to engage in the identity reorganization and meaning-making processes central to loss integration [46]. Evidence from other bereaved populations supports this distinction - lack of social acknowledgment has been linked to complicated grief reactions [64] and secondary traumatic stress among professionals whose grief went unrecognized [13]. Together, these findings suggest that support and recognition are not interchangeable social resources but functionally distinct mechanisms, each targeting different dimensions of the bereavement experience.

Theoretical and clinical implications

These findings refine contemporary bereavement theory by clarifying that for midlife adults, social recognition is not merely an extension of support but a primary mechanism of adaptation [60]. From a family psychology perspective, recognition enables midlife adults to renegotiate intergenerational roles and family identity following parental loss. Because midlife is a stage defined by established social and family roles and expectations of resilience, societal validation is the specific lever that allows these “pillars of stability” to mitigate distress and integrate loss into their changing identity [67]. Without such validation, midlife grievers may suppress their emotional needs to meet the demands of their careers and families, significantly increasing the risk of prolonged grief and loneliness [12, 57].

Recognizing this distinction allows for a dual-focused approach to interventions tailored to the midlife transition. First, clinical treatment must identify disenfranchised grief in adults who, due to their life stage, may not present with conventional distress [11, 46]. Therapeutic modalities focusing on meaning reconstruction, such as narrative therapy, can facilitate loss integration by helping the individual renegotiate their identity after becoming a “midlife orphan.”

Second, community-based initiatives are pivotal for shifting the social context of mourning during the peak of one’s professional and family responsibilities. Workplaces can provide structural validation through explicit bereavement policies and flexible leave, which acknowledge that parental loss in midlife constitutes a significant family disruption rather than a routine event. At a broader level, increased research attention to this under-recognized developmental stage is needed, as normative framings may obscure meaningful variation in family adaptation and psychological outcomes. Ultimately, our study emphasizes that while socio-emotional support protects general well-being, it cannot substitute for the social recognition required to process the unique developmental and family transition of midlife parental loss.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its insights, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; longitudinal research is needed to track how social context and meaning-making influence bereavement over time. Additionally, the Social Recognition Measure was developed specifically for this study, as no validated instrument exists for this population and experience. As it captures social recognition as a global construct, future research should develop and validate a multidimensional instrument distinguishing recognition across different life domains (e.g., family, workplace, community), as these may exert distinct effects on grief adaptation.

Second, the sample was predominantly female, highly educated, and Jewish, which may limit generalizability. Female overrepresentation is common in bereavement convenience samples, likely reflecting women’s greater caregiving roles and psychological engagement with loss [28]; moreover, prior research suggests women may experience greater psychological impact following parental loss in adulthood [23, 32, 43], yet the present study was not designed to examine gender-specific adaptation patterns. Future research should explore cross-cultural variations in grief recognition and support and aim for more gender-balanced samples. Additionally, the study was conducted during wartime, a period of collective trauma that may have created a ceiling effect on distress levels, overshadowing personal bereavement [21, 66]. Research conducted during the Iron Swords War has shown that women in particular experienced heightened psychological vulnerability during this period [66], which may have further shaped the present findings. Furthermore, the study did not assess potentially relevant variables such as the quality of the relationship with the deceased parent prior to death or family relational dynamics, both of which may shape grief responses and loss integration in meaningful ways [26, 39]. Future research should incorporate these variables to better capture the complexity of midlife bereavement. Finally, this model should be tested against non-death losses, such as chronic caregiving for ill parents, which can yield psychological impacts comparable to bereavement [31, 50]. Understanding these overlapping complexities is essential for refining models of family adaptation to loss and for extending their relevance to midlife as a key developmental and family transition.
