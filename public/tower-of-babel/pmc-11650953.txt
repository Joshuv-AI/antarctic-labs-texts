INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic torsion is a rare condition, with only 1 previous case documented in veterinary medicine. 1 The report describes a 6‐month‐old male intact Cane Corso Mastiff who was presented to an emergency service for vomiting and lethargy. The dog was determined to have an acute abdomen on examination, and subsequent imaging (radiography and ultrasonography) revealed a mass lesion in the right abdomen and peritoneal effusion. Torsion of the left limb of the pancreas was identified during exploratory celiotomy and was surgically excised. The cause of the pancreatic torsion was not determined.

More reports of pancreatic torsion (also termed pancreatic volvulus) have been described in the human literature. In people, this condition is typically associated with a wandering spleen and an increased risk of torsion of the splenic pedicle. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 The normal anatomical location of the spleen in people is relatively constant as a result of several supporting ligaments. When these ligaments fail to develop or have increased laxity, the spleen can shift to atypical locations within the abdomen. The splenorenal ligament, which is not present in dogs, connects the tail of the pancreas to the hilum of the spleen and greater curvature of the stomach. 7 In rare instances, torsion of the spleen in people can cause a secondary torsion of the distal body and tail of the pancreas when it becomes twisted in the splenorenal ligament. 5

In human cases, pancreatic torsion is identified on abdominal imaging performed during evaluation of patients with splenic torsion. Changes that suggest torsion of the distal pancreas include enlargement with a heterogenous appearance on ultrasound and low attenuation on computed tomography (CT). 2 Treatment comprises derotation of the pancreas intraoperatively, returning it to its normal anatomical position. In cases where necrosis has occurred, distal pancreatectomy is indicated. 6

The current report describes the treatment of a unique cause of acute abdomen in a puppy. The systemic workup resulted in exploratory celiotomy and an ultimate diagnosis of pancreatic torsion, a condition relatively undescribed in veterinary medicine.

CASE SUMMARY

An 11‐week, 5‐day‐old intact female Alaskan Malamute weighing 10.2 kg was presented to a private practice emergency service for acute onset vomiting. The puppy vomited for the first time the previous night at 8 p.m. The vomitus was reported to contain food material. After the initial episode of vomiting the puppy became anorexic. The puppy vomited an additional 3–4 times overnight with these episodes containing only liquid. The last episode occurred at approximately 4 a.m. The puppy remained anorexic the next morning. No diarrhea, hematochezia, or melena was reported. The puppy was presented at 10 a.m. to the emergency hospital for evaluation.

The owners acquired the puppy 3 weeks before presentation, and no other pets were in the household. The puppy was being fed an unknown puppy dog food. Initial vaccinations were given by the breeder and continuation of the vaccination series with a veterinarian was scheduled for later that week.

On presentation, a temperature of 39.3°C (102.7°F), a pulse rate of 136/min, and a respiratory rate of 48/min were noted. A physical examination identified pain in the cranial abdomen upon palpation. The remainder of the physical examination was unremarkable. Due to the puppy's age, clinical signs, and only a partial vaccination status, a Parvovirus SNAPa test was performed, which was negative. A CBC and biochemistry panel (including albumin, globulins, alkaline phosphatase [ALP], alanine transaminase, BUN, creatinine, and glucose) with electrolytes (chloride, potassium, and sodium) revealed a thrombocytosis, leukocytosis with a left shift, and an increased ALP activity(Table 1).

A complete abdominal ultrasound was recommended and performed by a board‐certified radiologist. The reported abnormalities included a thickened descending colonic wall (up to 5‐mm thick), a mild amount of peritoneal effusion, and a well‐circumscribed lobulated structure with fluid in the left cranial abdomen. The structure was located cranial to the stomach and adjacent to the liver, measuring approximately 6.5 × 3.2 × 6 cm in size. A color Doppler ultrasound revealed absence of flow in this structure (Figures 1, 2, 3). The remainder of the abdominal organs were unremarkable. Based on these findings, pancreatic lobe torsion was suspected by the radiologist. Additional differentials included regional steatitis or an omental mass. Less likely differentials included liver lobe torsion or an inflammatory focus adhered to the gastric wall. After the ultrasound, emergency exploratory celiotomy was recommended. A sample of peritoneal effusion was not collected because results were not expected to alter this recommendation.

The puppy was placed under general anesthesia for surgical exploration. The puppy was positioned in dorsal recumbency and a routine abdominal midline approach was performed. The abdominal cavity contained a mild amount of serosanguinous effusion. Evidence of torsion with dark red, congested, edematous tissue measuring 7 × 3 cm in size was seen in the most distal aspect of the left limb of the pancreas. The remainder of the pancreas and abdomen appeared grossly normal. The distal aspect of the left limb of the pancreas was dissected from the mesoduodenum with electrosurgery. A Miller's knot ligature was placed at the edge of the torsion and normal pancreatic tissue using 3‐0 PDSb suture and the left limb of the pancreas was excised (Figure 4). No hemorrhage was noted at the site of ligation and the abdomen was lavaged before standard closure. Bupivacaine liposome injectable suspensionc was instilled into the soft tissues during closure. The pancreatic mass was submitted for histopathology. The puppy recovered from anesthesia without complication.

Excised congested pancreatic tissue that had evidence of torsion during exploratory celiotomy of a puppy with clinical signs of an acute abdomen. A scalpel blade is placed to the right of the tissue for size reference.

The puppy was hospitalized overnight for postoperative monitoring and supportive care. Treatments included IV fluids, maropitantd (1 mg/kg, IV, q 24 h), ondansetrone (0.4 mg/kg, IV, q 8 h), and a transdermal fentanyl patchf (12.5 µg/h). The next morning the puppy was eating and was well hydrated and comfortable. One day postoperatively the puppy was discharged from the hospital.

Histopathological evaluation of the excised tissue revealed pancreatic tissue with marked vascular congestion, interlobular edema, and acute hemorrhage throughout. Regionally extensive foci of exocrine lobule necrosis with fibrin exudate and infiltration by many neutrophils were noted. Some of the vessels had prominent fibrinoid necrosis. In addition, prominent fibrinopurulent peritonitis was noted. No preexistent condition or other abnormalities were identified.

Nine months after surgery, the dog was doing well but had a low body condition despite a good appetite. A Texas gastrointestinal panelg was performed to measure cobalamin, folate, and trypsin‐like immunoreactivity (TLI). All results were within normal limits (Table 2). Despite a normal TLI, pancreatic insufficiency was suspected because of the dog's body condition and a trial of pancreatic enzyme supplementation was recommended. At the last follow‐up, approximately 2 years after surgery, the dog no longer required pancreatic enzyme supplementation and has a slightly overconditioned body condition score.

DISCUSSION

Several similarities can be noted between this case and the previously reported case in veterinary medicine. 1 In both cases, an abdominal mass with peritoneal effusion was seen on diagnostic imaging. In‐house cytology of the effusion in the previous case report was inflammatory in nature without evidence of infectious agents or neoplasia. 1 In both instances, the condition occurred in a young, growing, larger breed of dog. Both cases also involved torsion of the left limb of the pancreas, which is thicker and shorter than the right limb. 1 No preexisting condition, history of trauma, or splenic torsion was reported in either case, which may indicate that the pathophysiology of pancreatic torsion in dogs involves spontaneous torsion without an apparent inciting cause. An association with the growth and development of the abdominal viscera in larger breed dogs could be considered. If supporting structures are not fully developed, or if developing viscera has more laxity, these could be risk factors for torsion. A larger case series would be required before any association with breed or age could be concluded.

Due to the paucity of data in veterinary medicine, human case reports were investigated to gain insight into the potential mechanisms of pancreatic torsion. As discussed, pancreatic torsion in human patients occurs when it becomes twisted in the splenorenal ligament secondary to splenic torsion. 5 Although splenic torsion is well documented in dogs 8 , the absence of the splenorenal ligament prevents secondary torsion of the pancreas. This likely contributes to the rarity of pancreatic torsion in dogs. In this case and the previous case report, no abnormalities of the spleen were observed during surgical exploration. Thus, the pathophysiology of pancreatic torsion in veterinary medicine remains unclear.

Abdominal ultrasound is very useful in cases of acute abdomen and provided vital information in this case. The results of this case and the previously reported case 1 during abdominal ultrasound were similar, with a mass lesion being the major finding. The presence of a mass lesion alone may coincide with other pancreatic pathologies, such as pancreatic abscesses, neoplasia, and occasionally acute pancreatitis. 9 However, in addition to the puppy's age making these differentials unlikely, the lack of blood flow demonstrated by color flow made pancreatic torsion the primary differential. The ability of ultrasonography to demonstrate lack of blood flow using color flow is valuable because only torsion would be expected to obstruct blood flow. A CT angiogram could also be used to identify compromised blood flow.

In this case, exploratory celiotomy was recommended because it allowed for confirmation and treatment of the suspected pancreatic torsion. Additional diagnostics that could have been considered before surgical exploration include sampling and analysis of the peritoneal effusion, cytology of the lobulated structure, and additional imaging with CT with contrast. These diagnostics were not recommended because of their uncertain diagnostic utility and were considered unlikely to alter the recommendation for an emergency exploratory celiotomy. Additionally, pursuing additional diagnostics would have delayed surgery and increased the potential for clinical decline. This could ultimately have made the puppy a less favorable anesthetic candidate and negatively impacted their overall prognosis.

After pancreatic torsion was confirmed intraoperatively, it was treated by surgical excision of the affected tissue. The left lobe of the pancreas normally begins at the pylorus and extends along the greater curvature of the stomach. 10 Torsion could cause this limb to shift cranially, between the stomach and liver, as seen on ultrasound. The histopathological findings of the excised tissue are expected secondary to passive congestion from torsion. No samples of the excised tissue were submitted for bacterial culture and sensitivity testing. This was not considered necessary because the surgical findings were consistent with a mechanical process (torsion) rather than a septic process. No bacterial agents were observed during histopathological examination. Treatment with partial pancreatectomy performed in this case differs from human cases of pancreatic torsion, which are most often treated by derotation without excision. In human patients, this is performed with concurrent treatment of the splenic torsion, which caused the torsion of the pancreas. Because no underlying cause of the pancreatic torsion was identified in this puppy's case, treatment with derotation alone would carry a risk of recurrence. Additionally, in cases of splenic torsion, derotation is contraindicated due to the risk of releasing sequestered blood, thrombi, microthrombi, free radicals, tumor necrosis factor, and other vasoactive compounds into circulation. 10 Derotation of an ischemic pancreas could carry similar risks. For these reasons, treatment with derotation alone would not have been recommended.

In veterinary medicine, complications documented after partial pancreatectomy include pancreatitis and issues with glycemic control. 11 The latter is associated with surgical treatment of insulinomas. At the time of this report, no information in the veterinary literature regarding pancreatic insufficiency after partial pancreatectomy was found. A human study investigated pancreatic function in people after distal pancreatectomy by measuring human stool elastase‐1 (HSE‐1) levels. 12 In the study, a group of the patients had a more extensive pancreatectomy performed, with the excised portion extending over or beyond the portal vein. A small subset of these patients was found to have low HSE‐1 levels after the surgery. Two years postoperatively, all patients in the study, however, had a normal HSE‐1 level. This data indicates that this subset of patients experienced a transient postoperative pancreatic insufficiency. Whether a similar phenomenon occurs in small animals that undergo partial pancreatectomy is unknown. It is possible that transient pancreatic insufficiency would explain the poor weight gain initially seen in this puppy, which later resolved. Caution must be taken, however, when extrapolating data from the human literature.

It must also be recognized that while TLI is the standard test for pancreatic insufficiency in dogs, a variety of tests are available in human medicine. Some of these include fecal elastase, coefficient of fat absorption, and C‐mixed triglyceride breath tests. 13 Each of these tests has limitations, especially after pancreatic resection. This makes diagnosis challenging and a combination of symptoms, nutritional status, and function tests must be used. Due to the difficulty of diagnosing pancreatic insufficiency, it is underrecognized and undertreated after pancreatic resection in people. 13 This may also be true in veterinary medicine because of the lack of routine testing after pancreatic resection and the constraint of having only 1 available standard test. Investigating the occurrence of pancreatic insufficiency in animals undergoing partial pancreatectomy would be an interesting area of research for future studies.

The current report documents the occurrence of pancreatic torsion in a puppy that was presented for emergency evaluation of an acute abdomen. Systematic investigation allowed for the diagnosis and treatment of a unique condition. Diagnostic imaging of the abdomen was crucial and highlighted its importance in cases of acute abdomen. Pancreatic torsion should be considered in cases where a mass effect is identified near the location of the pancreas. Pancreatic torsion may be successfully treated by partial pancreatectomy to excise the affected tissue; however, clinicians should be aware that pancreatic insufficiency may occur postoperatively in these animals.
