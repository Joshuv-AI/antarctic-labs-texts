Direct imaging of facet development during growth of single-crystal covalent organic frameworks
Authors: Qianxi Wang, Quan Wu, Yi He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Single-crystal covalent organic frameworks (COFs) have received increasing interest due to their well-defined structures and exceptional performance. However, the reaction pathways of the facet development during growth of single-crystal COFs remains unclear, limiting the regulation of their shapes and functions. We report the dynamic imaging of the facet development during single-crystal COF-300 microcrystal growth using in situ dark-field optical microscopy. Our observations reveal that the concentration of terephthalaldehyde (BDA) linkers not only influences the morphology of the primary COF-300 crystal seeds but also governs the facet evolution, leading to the generation of differently shaped COF-300 microcrystals. Mechanistic studies reveal that the observed shape evolution of COF-300 single crystals arises from distinct kinetically or thermodynamically controlled growth pathways, depending on the BDA concentration. Moreover, we uncover the shape-dependent deformation capacity after solvent uptake, demonstrating the impact of COF-300 crystal morphology on its performance. These discoveries elucidate the COF facet transition mechanism and offer guidance for the future design of single-crystal COFs.

INTRODUCTION

Controlling the morphology of crystals has been demonstrated to be an important avenue for tuning their properties and improving their performance in various applications (1–4). As a new class of crystalline polymers, covalent organic frameworks (COFs) with well-defined structures and permanent porosity have attracted tremendous attention in adsorption, catalysis, sensing, and other fields (5–11). Over the past decade, considerable progress has been achieved in the synthesis of a range of high-quality single-crystal COFs via reversible self-addition polymerization, imine-exchange strategy, and micelle-directed synthesis (12–16), particularly with respect to imine-linked single-crystal COFs. Recently, morphological tuning of COF single crystals (JUC-663) has been achieved using aniline as a modulator (17), and it has been found that the morphology of JUC-663 single crystals strongly influences their adsorption properties.

Density functional theory calculations show that aniline facilitates anisotropic competitive binding with specific facets, which controls the morphology of the COF single crystals (17). However, these speculations are based on the final structures and postreaction characterizations. The dynamic evolution of the crystal facet in single-crystal COFs during their growth is largely unknown due to technical challenges in directly tracking the structure and morphology changes under reaction conditions. A fundamental understanding of facet development is vital for better engineering the structure of single-crystal COFs and creating high-performance functional crystals.

In situ liquid-phase dark-field optical microscopy (DFM) combines the merits of high contrast, high sensitivity, high spatiotemporal resolution, and outstanding cost-effectiveness (18–20). This technique enables direct observation of single-crystal COF growth, providing a powerful tool to advance our knowledge regarding the crystal growth mechanism (21–24). Our previous work has found the coexistence of classical and nonclassical oriented-attachment growth pathways in COF microcrystals during the early stage of crystal formation, where no distinct facet development occurs (22). This limitation has hindered a fundamental understanding of facet evolution and the rational design of faceted COFs. In this study, we use in situ DFM to image the entire facet development pathways of COF-300 based on the condensation of tetra-(4-anilyl)methane (TAM) building blocks and terephthalaldehyde (BDA) linkers, a well-studied model system for single-crystal COF growth (25). Through in situ single-facet resolution observations and quantitative DFM image analysis, we uncover that the growth rates of both the {021} facet and the {010} facet show distinct variations with increasing BDA concentration, resulting in three different morphologies of single-crystal COF-300 such as quasi-tetragonal microprism, tetragonal bipyramid microprism, and tetragonal microbipyramid. The combination of real-time DFM imaging and theoretical analysis reveals that an increase in BDA concentration results in a transformation from a thermodynamically favored growth process to a kinetically preferred reaction pathway, which is responsible for generating distinct morphologies of single-crystal COF-300 (26–28). Last, we evaluate the differences in flexible deformation capacity among COF-300 single crystals with different shapes, highlighting the key role of morphology in regulating the inherent properties of COF crystals.

RESULTS

In situ observation of single-crystal COF-300 facet development

A fast imine-exchange strategy was selected for the growth of single-crystal COF-300 as a model reaction system (13). A precursor solution of BDA (12 mg) and TAM (20 mg) in 1,4-dioxane was prepared. The nucleation and growth of COF-300 single crystals are triggered by the injection of trifluoroacetic acid (CF3COOH, 100 μl, 6 M) as a catalyst and aniline (C6H5NH2, 81 μl, 10 equiv) as a modulator (13). At the early stage of the growth reaction, a burst of nucleation and growth occurs, leading to the formation of numerous microcrystal seeds that further grow into specifically shaped COF-300 single crystals (movie S1). In this scenario, COF-300 microcrystal seeds often tend to stabilize on specific facets as growth proceeds. To quantify the shape evolution of a COF-300 microcrystal seed, the propagation of different facets is dynamically tracked in situ and in real time. Because COF-300 crystallizes in the tetragonal space group I41/a (13, 25), the COF-300 microcrystal seeds are typically in tetragonal bipyramidal shape (Fig. 1A and fig. S1). A two-dimensional (2D) projection along the [201] viewing axis during facet development enables the measurements of distances from the crystal center to specific facets such as {021} and {010} for calculating the growth rates (29).

Time-sequential DFM images extracted from movie S1 clearly show how a 1.4-μm-sized tetragonal bipyramidal seed evolves into a COF-300 tetragonal bipyramidal microprism (Fig. 1B). At the onset of the shape evolution (t-t0= 0 min), tetragonal bipyramidal COF-300 seed is enclosed by {021} facets. As the COF-300 seed gradually grows, the {010} facets appear at t-t0 = 25 min (Fig. 1B). During the subsequent period from t-t0 = 25 min to t-t0 = 200 min, both {021} and {010} facets continue to grow, with a distinct growth rate of {021} > {010} (Fig. 1C). The growth rate (v) difference between {021} and {010} facets reaches its maximum at t-t0 = 60 min and subsequently decreases. Last, the growth of both {021} and {010} facets nearly stops, leading to the formation of single-crystal COF-300 tetragonal bipyramid microprisms (Fig. 1B). Since each {021} facet has a relatively faster growth rate than the {010} facet, the area fraction of the {021} facet eventually decreases as the COF-300 seed develops into a tetragonal bipyramidal microprism. To further confirm the observed morphology of the COF-300 single crystal, a high-resolution scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image was acquired, clearly revealing its tetragonal bipyramidal shape (fig. S2), consistent with the DFM observations. Meanwhile, a similar facet development process is observed during the growth of other tetragonal bipyramidal COF-300 seeds with varying initial sizes ranging from 0.965 to 1.153 μm (figs. S3 to S7).

To confirm the structure of the resulting COF-300 tetragonal bipyramidal microprisms, other complementary ex situ characterizations such as Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), powder x-ray diffraction (PXRD), and single-crystal x-ray diffraction (SCXRD) are performed. The characteristic C═N imine bonds in the COF-300 framework are verified by FTIR and XPS, where the C═N stretching vibration and binding energy are clearly found at 1614 cm−1 and 398.9 eV, respectively (figs. S8 and S9) (25). PXRD pattern and Pawley refinement identify the crystalline space group as tetragonal I41/a (no. 88) (fig. S10). On the basis of SCXRD analysis for a large-sized tetragonal bipyramidal COF-300 crystal that was prepared by extending the reaction time, two subfaces were indexed as {010} and their Friedel pairs when viewed along the {001} growth direction, and the other side faces were subsequently indexed as {100}, {021}, and their Friedel pairs or equivalent facets (fig. S11).

BDA concentration–dependent facet transition

Upon decreasing the BDA amount from 12 to 6 mg in the single-crystal growth solution, substantial changes are noted in the morphology and shape transition behavior of the resulting COF-300 seeds. As an example, Fig. 2 shows the growth trajectories of a tetragonal bipyramid prism-shaped COF-300 seed in the [100] viewing direction (movie S2). Under this reaction condition, the initial COF-300 crystal seed is a tetragonal bipyramidal microprism enclosed by {021} and {010} facets, with a size of 1.04 μm. As the reaction time prolongs, the COF-300 crystal becomes larger, and the distances from the crystal center to each facet increase (Fig. 2, B and C), proving the synchronous growth of {021} and {010} facets. Figure 2C illustrates that the {021} facet grows notably faster than the {010} facet, leading to a gradual decrease in the area fraction of the {021} facet. Ultimately, the {021} facet contributes a minor proportion to the single-crystal COF-300, yielding a COF-300 quasi-tetragonal microprism.

The same growth trajectories are also found in other COF-300 tetragonal bipyramidal prism crystal seeds as shown in figs. S12 to S16, confirming the generality of the obtained facet transition behavior. In addition, the yielded COF-300 quasi-tetragonal microprism single crystals are further characterized by SEM and PXRD (figs. S10 and S17). It is worth pointing out that the relative PXRD intensity of the (200) plane greatly increases in COF-300 quasi-tetragonal microprism compared to the COF-300 tetragonal bipyramidal microprism (figs. S10 and S17), which is expected for the quasi-tetragonal microprism-shaped COF-300.

On the contrary, when a relatively large amount of BDA (24 mg) is introduced into the single-crystal growth solution, we capture real-time DFM images showing a shape-preserved growth process for the COF-300 single crystals. The formed COF-300 seed exhibits a tetragonal bipyramidal morphology, and the 2D projection along the [201] viewing axis consists of eight {021} facets (Fig. 3A). Figure 3 (B and C) depicts time-lapsed DFM images, the corresponding crystal center–to–{021} facet distances, and growth rates. It can be seen that the single-crystal seed of COF-300 with a tetragonal bipyramidal shape is directed to form a COF-300 tetragonal microbipyramid enclosed by {021} facets via layer-by-layer growth. Likewise, this single-crystal growth behavior is further verified for other tetragonal bipyramidal COF-300 seeds (figs. S18 and S19). The crystallinity and morphology of the resulting COF-300 tetragonal microbipyramid are evidenced by PXRD and Pawley refinement and SEM (figs. S10 and S20).

On the basis of the above in situ and real-time DFM imaging results, we can conclude that BDA molecules play a vital role in the growth and facet transition of single-crystal COF-300. In general, the insufficient amount of BDA molecules in the growth solution cause an initial tetragonal bipyramidal prism seed exposed by {021} and {010} facets and lastly develops to a COF-300 quasi-tetragonal microprism with nearly covered by {010} facets. An appropriate increase of BDA to 12 mg leads to the generation of tetragonal bipyramidal seeds exposed by {021} facets, which gradually evolve into single-crystal COF-300 tetragonal bipyramidal microprisms enclosed by a mix of {021} and {010} facets. However, excess BDA molecules (e.g., 24 mg) strongly suppress the growth of {010} facets, inducing a shape-preserved growth to generate COF-300 tetragonal microbipyramid completely covered by {021} facets. Besides, the initial crystal growth rate is proportional to the amount (concentration) of BDA in the growth solution (Figs. 1C, 2C, and 3C). For instance, the initial crystal growth rate for {021} facets is determined to be 0.052 μm min−1 in the presence of 24 mg of BDA (Fig. 3C), which is 2.8 times higher than the rate upon the addition of 12 mg of BDA into the growth solution (Fig. 1C). As a result, BDA molecules act not only as reactants but also as morphology modulators.

Understanding the role of BDA in the facet evolution

To gain a better understanding of the key role of BDA in the facet evolution of single-crystal COF-300, surface energy (γ) calculations were performed for {010} and {021} facets of COF-300 (figs. S21 and S22). The surface energy of the {021} facet (γ021 = 81.73 mJ m−2) is higher than that of the {010} facet (γ010 = 49.98 mJ m−2) (Fig. 4A). Wulff construction plot is generated on the basis of surface energies (26, 27). As indicated in Fig. 4B, the predicted morphology of single-crystal COF-300 is a quasi-tetragonal microprism, suggesting that this morphology is the thermodynamically favored product.

Mechanistic investigation of BDA concentration–dominated facet development.

(A) The calculated surface energies of {021} and {010} facets. (B) Wulff constructions of COF-300 single crystal. Schematic illustration of the energy landscape: (C) the generation of thermodynamically and kinetically favored COF-300 products; (D) a series of sequential reactions featuring local and global minima.

The actual growth of COF-300 crystals from the condensation of BDA and TAM can be thermodynamically or kinetically controlled to yield thermodynamic and kinetic products (28, 30). In general, the activation energy barrier for the generation of thermodynamic products (E1) is larger than that for the formation of kinetic products (E2) (Fig. 4C) (28, 30, 31). Thus, the kinetically favored product is more readily formed at a relatively nonequilibrium state with a high reaction rate. On the basis of the surface energy minimization principle, the COF-300 quasi-tetragonal microprism is a typical thermodynamic product, obtained at a low concentration of BDA (6 mg) and accompanied by a low reaction rate (Fig. 4C). This process achieves an equilibrium and accumulates at the most stable state. In contrast, when the concentration of BDA is high (24 mg), the crystal growth rate increases markedly, preventing the system from reaching equilibrium and resulting in the formation of the kinetically favored product, the tetragonal bipyramidal COF-300 crystal (Fig. 4C). The different morphologies of COF-300 crystals should be better described as the products of a series of sequential reactions (30), which are in good agreement with DFM imaging results. The energy landscape shown in Fig. 4D provides a more appropriate illustration of thermodynamic versus kinetic control. Therefore, at an intermediate BDA concentration (12 mg), the kinetically favored tetragonal bipyramidal COF-300 crystal seeds gradually evolve into another kinetically preferred product (COF-300 tetragonal bipyramid microprism), corresponding to a local energy minimum (Fig. 4D) (30). Simultaneously, the thermodynamic and kinetic effects on the shape evolution of COF-300 single crystals are further verified by altering the amount of aniline as a modulator. Increasing the amount of aniline reduces the free BDA concentration and slows down the growth reaction (fig. S23). This slowdown leads to a transformation from kinetically favored tetragonal microbipyramids to thermodynamically favored quasi-tetragonal COF-300 microprisms (fig. S23).

Notably, the SEM images reveal deviations from the idealized models for quasi-tetragonal and bipyramidal prisms in the form of conical extremities rather than tetrahedral ones (fig. S2). These discrepancies can be attributed to the kinetically driven growth instability (32, 33). Because of the continuous monomer deposition and polymerization during the crystal growth process, a compressive stress is generated that reduces interface mobility. Stress relaxation occurs at the peak, whereas stress concentration occurs in the trough, resulting in the peak growing faster than the valley.

Deformation comparison of single-crystal COF-300 with different shapes

The resulting COF-300 single crystals with well-defined shapes, combined with the powerful imaging capability of DFM, allow us to precisely investigate the effect of crystal morphology on deformation behavior. Figure 5A and fig. S24 show that COF-300 crystals of different shapes, all exhibiting the sevenfold interpenetrated diamond topology (dia-7) and pore size identified by Rietveld analysis of PXRD patterns, have 1D straight channels along the [001] direction within a flexible framework. They can adapt upon interacting with solvents to form expanded phases (34, 35). As expected, all the single-crystal COF-300 with different shapes are transformed into shortened and deformed states upon inclusion of 6 M 1,4-dioxane or 6 M ethanol aqueous solution (Fig. 5B and fig. S25A). To quantitatively compare the deformation capacity of the three types of COF-300 single crystals, the variation in the relative aspect ratio (1-ΔL/D) before and after solvent uptake is measured. The statistically averaged values of 1-ΔL/D for different-shaped COF-300 single crystals upon the addition of 1,4-dioxane or ethanol aqueous solution are plotted in Fig. 5C and fig. S25B. These results illustrate that the deformation capacity of COF-300 single crystals with different shapes increases in the order of quasi-tetragonal microprism < tetragonal bipyramid microprism < tetragonal microbipyramid, which aligns well with the trend of the {021} facet area fraction in the crystals (Fig. 5D). This experimental phenomenon is attributed to the 1D channel of COF-300 along the [001] plane (36), with only the {021} facet accessible for solvent molecule adsorption. The increase in the area fraction of the {021} facet will strengthen the flexible deformation property for COF-300 single crystals. Bulk adsorption experiments were performed on COF-300 single crystals in 1,4-dioxane. PXRD analysis indicates that the 1,4-dioxane–solvated COF-300 single crystals—tetragonal bipyramid microprism, quasi-tetragonal microprism, and tetragonal microbipyramid—maintain their topologies, while their unit cells expand in the following order: tetragonal microbipyramid > tetragonal bipyramid microprism > quasi-tetragonal microprism (fig. S26). These findings further confirm the deformation capacity dominated by the {021} facet area fraction. Moreover, solid-state 13C cross-polarization magic angle spinning spectroscopy excludes the effect of defects on the structural deformation (fig. S27). These findings undoubtedly demonstrate that controlling the crystal morphology of COFs greatly influences their deformation capacity under identical crystal topology.

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, we have mapped the facet evolution behavior of single-crystal COF-300 frameworks in real time through in situ liquid-phase DFM. Visual quantification of the development of the {021} and {010} facets from time-resolved DFM images enables the analysis of BDA concentration–dependent dynamic facet evolution, resulting in three distinct shapes of COF-300 single crystals. DFM imaging and theoretical analysis collectively reveal that BDA uniquely serves as both a reactant and a morphology modulator, directing the formation of COF-300 single crystals with distinct shapes via thermodynamically or kinetically controlled growth pathways. Meanwhile, the greater deformation capacity of the tetragonal microbipyramidal COF-300 compared to other morphologies suggests that tuning the area fraction of specific facets offers an alternative to altering crystal topology for engineering framework flexibility. This discovery potentially leads to the development of more precise flexible COF single crystals. Furthermore, insights gained from our in situ DFM observations substantially advance the understanding of facet evolution during the growth of single-crystal COFs, paving the way for the rational design of high-performance COF crystals for various applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals

TAM (98%, Leyan Chemical), BDA (98%, Aladdin Reagent), aniline (99.5%, Macklin Reagent), trifluoroacetic acid (99%, Chendu Kelong Chemical Reagent), ethanol (99.7%, Chendu Kelong Chemical Reagent), and 1,4-dioxane (99.5%, Chengdu Kelong Chemical Reagent) were purchased and used directly without further purification. Deionized (DI) water with a resistivity of 18.2 megohm·cm was used throughout to prepare various solutions.

Fabrication of reaction microchamber

A reaction microchamber was specifically constructed for DFM imaging. Typically, a standard microscope glass slide (75 mm by 25 mm by 1 mm) was rinsed several times with ethanol and DI water in an ultrasonic bath. Then, the clean glass slide was combined with a coverslip (18 mm by 18 mm by 0.13 mm) to form a reaction microchamber.

Preparation of single-crystal COF-300 growth solution

Different amounts of BDA (6, 12, and 24 mg) were separately dissolved in 0.5 ml of 1,4-dioxane, followed by the addition of 81 μl of aniline and 100 μl of 6 M trifluoroacetic acid at room temperature (~25°C). After that, 20 mg of TAM, predissolved in 0.5 ml of 1,4-dioxane, was injected and mixed thoroughly.

Experimental procedure for in situ DFM imaging of single-crystal growth

The dark-field optical microscope was constructed on the basis of a BX53 optical microscope (Olympus, Japan) coupled with an oil-immersion dark-field condenser with a high numerical aperture (NA = 1.2 to 1.4), a dry 40× objective (NA = 0.65), and an HT2000CN color charge-coupled device camera with a 50-ms acquisition rate (Shanghai Hantu Optoelectronics Technology, China). In a typical experiment, 10 μl of the aforementioned single-crystal COF-300 growth solution was injected into the reaction microchamber, which was sealed with dimethyl silicone oil to avoid solvent evaporation. The microchamber was subsequently placed under a dark-field optical microscope for in situ imaging of COF-300 single-crystal growth, and DFM images were automatically recorded at various reaction time points. The resulting images were further analyzed using ImageJ software.

Single-crystal deformation experiment

Briefly, 2 mg of the single-crystal COF-300 with different shapes was dispersed into ethanol under an ultrasound condition. Subsequently, 8 μl of COF-300 ethanol dispersion was drop-cast onto a clean glass slide and allowed to dry naturally at room temperature. DFM images were recorded for single-crystal COF-300 samples on glass slides after exposure to DI water, 6 M ethanol aqueous solution, or 6 M 1,4-dioxane aqueous solution. The corresponding aspect ratios were then extracted using ImageJ software.

Theoretical calculation of surface energies

Theoretical calculations were performed using the Forcite module with the COMPASSIII force field. Electrostatic and van der Waals interactions were calculated using the atom-based method under ultrafine precision settings. Atomic charges were assigned via the force field method. All structures were fully optimized until the total energy and atomic forces converged to 10−5 kcal mol−1 and 10−3 kcal mol−1 Å−1, respectively. For surface energy calculations, {010} and {021} slab models with 1,4-dioxane solvent layer were established to investigate the effect of BDA on their surface energies.

Wulff construction for shape prediction

The shape prediction of single-crystal COF-300 was conducted using VESTA software based on its tetragonal I41/a space group (25). By taking advantage of the optimized unit cell parameters for COF-300 crystal [a = b = 19.92 Å, c = 9.04 Å, and α = β = γ = 90°], a primary unit cell was fabricated. The Miller indices (010) and (021), along with their corresponding surface energies calculated under different concentrations of BDA, were used as inputs to generate the Wulff constructions of COF-300 single crystals with various shapes.
