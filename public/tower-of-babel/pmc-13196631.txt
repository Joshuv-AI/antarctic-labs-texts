Preclinical safety evaluation of the dipeptidyl peptidase 3 inhibiting antibody Procizumab in rodents and non-human primates
Authors: Dirk van Lier, Magali Genest, Adrien Picod, Francesca Manicone, Bruno Garcia, Theo Ikenna Uba, Anika Schroeter, Karine Santos, Antoine Herpain, Alexis Nguyen, Feriel Azibani, Alexandre Mebazaa, Matthijs Kox, Peter Pickkers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe cytosolic protease dipeptidyl peptidase 3 (DPP3) is released into the circulation upon profound cellular stress. In the bloodstream, circulating DPP3 (cDPP3) rapidly degrades several important mediators of vascular tone. The cDPP3-antagonizing antibody Procizumab (PCZ) improved outcome in animal models of shock. This study aimed to establish the safety and tolerability of PCZ across relevant animal species. In the first study, mice received vehicle (n = 72), 37.5 (n = 114), 75 (n = 114), or 150 (n = 126) mg/kg of PCZ intravenously on days 1, 2, 13, and 14. In the second study, cynomolgus monkeys received vehicle (n = 10), 40 (n = 6), 120 (n = 6), or 350 (n = 10) mg/kg of PCZ intravenously on days 1, 2, 13 and 14. Toxicokinetic analyses demonstrated instant and dose-dependent peak concentrations. PCZ was eliminated relatively fast compared to other monoclonal antibodies and administration resulted in an instant reduction of cDPP3 enzyme activity, confirming the intended pharmacodynamic mechanism-of-action. Apart from fully reversible, non-dose-dependent increases of enzymes aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and creatinine kinase in monkeys, and decreased rarefaction of the cytoplasm of hepatocytes in treated mice compared to the control group, toxicologic analyses showed no test item-related effects. In both studies, no mortality, moribund condition or any other adverse histopathological findings were attributed to PCZ therapy. These findings demonstrate that intravenous, repeated administration of high doses of PCZ was well tolerated in mice and cynomolgus monkeys. Results of this study were followed by a Phase 1 safety assessment of PCZ in humans, with single-administration clinical doses of 3, 6, and 12 mg/kg (NCT06331884).KEYWORDS: Dipeptidyl peptidase 3, preclinical, procizumab

Introduction

Shock represents the clinical manifestation of circulatory failure, where oxygen delivery and cellular utilization are profoundly impaired.1 It affects roughly one-third of intensive care unit patients and is classified based on the primary causative pathophysiological mechanism as hypovolemic, cardiogenic, obstructive, or distributive shock.1 While this classic framework supports initial treatment strategies, it does not fully capture the diverse molecular mechanisms underlying shock development. Hybrid forms, such as cardiogenic-vasodilatory or vasodilatory-cardiogenic shock, acknowledge overlapping deficits in myocardial function and vascular tone, often triggered by mixed insults.2 Therapy remains centered on early hemodynamic support, such as fluids, vasopressors, inotropes, and mechanical organ support, yet major advances in prognosis have been limited.3 Thus, progress hinges on identifying biologically relevant pathways to guide targeted interventions.4

Dipeptidyl peptidase 3 (DPP3) is a ubiquitous cytosolic enzyme only present at low circulating concentrations in health. During shock, reduced tissue perfusion leads to profound cellular stress, in turn resulting in the release of DPP3 into the circulation.5–10 Once released into the bloodstream, circulating DPP3 (cDPP3) is involved in the degradation of several small peptides involved in the regulation of vascular tone, most notably angiotensin II.5,11–13 The degradation of angiotensin II by cDPP3 may aggravate the hemodynamic instability observed during shock, with additional cell death caused by tissue hypoperfusion leading to the release of more cDPP3 and unfavorable hemodynamic consequences in a vicious cycle.5 Indeed, in healthy mice, intravenous administration of DPP3 provokes a rapid deterioration of left ventricular function, as well as impaired kidney hemodynamics.7 Following cessation of DPP3 administration, both left ventricular function and cDPP3 activity returned to pre-infusion values within approximately 2 hours.7 In another murine study, DPP3 infusion nullified the blood pressure-increasing effects of angiotensin II infusion.14 Consequently, it is possible that inhibition of cDPP3 may have therapeutic potential in shock types with a vasodilatory component.

Antibodies that inhibit cDPP3’s enzymatic function have been developed. One of these antibodies, murine AK1967, which effectively and selectively inhibited DPP3‘s enzymatic activity in vitro, was subsequently humanized and termed Procizumab (PCZ, also referred to as AK1967 or Invobenitug (international nonproprietary name)).7 PCZ is directed against a conserved, surface-exposed loop in proximity to cDPP3’s active enzyme site.7 In healthy mice, PCZ significantly increased circulating concentrations of angiotensin II, III and IV.15 Furthermore, administration of PCZ improved left ventricular function, reduced cardiac oxidative stress and improved survival in non-clinical murine models of cardiogenic and septic shock.7,16 Finally, in an experimental pig model of septic shock, continuous PCZ administration was associated with reduced fluid and catecholamine requirement, less myocardial injury and cardiovascular inflammation, along with preserved angiotensin II signaling.17 In the study presented here, we assessed the safety and tolerability of PCZ in mice and cynomolgus monkeys, aimed at establishing a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) for subsequent Phase I testing in humans.

Materials and Methods

Animals and ethics

Mice and cynomolgus monkeys were identified as relevant species for non-clinical safety and toxicity testing. This is because mice and cynomolgus monkeys both express DPP3.11 Moreover, PCZ’s binding site is 100% conserved across these species and humans. Further, previous in vitro studies also demonstrated that PCZ inhibited murine, monkey and human DPP3, additionally supporting the selection of these species for toxicity testing (data not shown).

Animal experiments were performed according to applicable guidelines and regulations. All animals were kept under routine laboratory conditions, with temperature and humidity control, as well as 12-hour light/dark cycle conditions. Animals were housed in cages that conform to the Code of Practice for the Housing and Care of Animals Bred, Supplied or Used for Scientific Purposes (Home Office, 2014). Animals were weighed individually, and treatment was prepared individually for each animal. Both studies were conducted under OECD Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) conditions.

The repeated-dose toxicology and toxicokinetics study in mice was performed by Labcorp Early Development Laboratories Ltd (Huntingdon, United Kingdom) according to GLP standards. The study was approved by the Project License holder on behalf of The UK Home Office (Project License Number PP8548540). Healthy, naïve C57BL/6J mice (aged 9–12 weeks) were obtained from Envigo (United Kingdom). Mice were fed ad libitum with Certified rodent diet #2014C (Inotiv, Indianopolis, United States) and water. Male animals were individually housed, and female animals were housed up to three animals per cage.

The repeated-dose toxicology and toxicokinetics study in cynomolgus monkeys was also performed by Labcorp Early Development Laboratories Ltd (Huntingdon, United Kingdom) according to GLP standards. The study was approved by the Project License holder on behalf of The UK Home Office (Project License Number PP5384279). Healthy, naïve cynomolgus monkeys (aged 18–48 months) were obtained from BCM (Bioculture, Mauritius). Monkeys were fed Certified monkey pellets #5048 (LabDiets, Land O’Lakes, Arden Hills, United States) at least once daily, and drinking water was provided ad libitum. In addition, animals were given fruits and vegetables. Animals were socially housed by sex and treatment group, in groups of two, three or five animals per cage. Both studies met the criteria of ARRIVE 2.0 (Animals in Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments) guidelines.18

Study medication

Generation, screening, and development of the humanized DPP3 antibody AK1967 (PCZ) has been described previously.7 The antibody used in all experiments was provided by 4TEEN4 Pharmaceuticals GmbH (Hennigsdorf, Germany). Study medication was provided as stock solutions, stored at 2–8°C under temperature-controlled and restricted access conditions. For both animal studies, PCZ was produced by AGC Biologics (Chiba, Japan) under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) conditions.

For all animals that were administered PCZ, the drug was diluted to the required concentration with formulation buffer (10 mM Histidine-HCl, 250 mM Trehalose and 20 mM L-Methionine pH 6.5 buffer, which also served as vehicle), with the exception of the highest dose in mice (150 mg/kg), which was ready-to-use. In mice, the study drug was applied at a volume of 7.5 mL/kg, meaning PCZ concentrations varied between 0 (vehicle), 5, 10 and 20 mg/mL depending on the dose group. In monkeys, study medication was administered at a volume of 20 mL/kg, corresponding with PCZ concentrations of 0 (vehicle), 2, 6, and 17.5 mg/mL in the different dose groups. A dose formulation analysis confirmed the correctness of the applied drug formulations in both studies.

Toxicity studies

The toxicity studies were designed in line with regulatory recommendations and aimed at evaluating the toxicity profile after repeated dosing of PCZ. Three test item dose groups were included to investigate dose response. The selected dose levels were sufficiently high to cover clinical dose levels with respective safety margins. The dosing regimen (day 1, 2, 13 and 14) was selected to cover repeated dosing in a clinical setting with the shortest interval between dosing. Following standard rules for evaluation of toxicology studies and determination of a adversity,19,20 the likelihood of a relationship with PCZ, possible dose-dependency, correlation of findings from different endpoints providing evidence for impairment of functional capacity of an organ, severity of effects, potential for recovery and nonoccurrence in control animals were criteria used for determination of the NOAEL for each study.

Toxicity studies in mice

A previous less extensive (non-GLP) study was performed to assess the acute toxicity and establish a dose-range of PCZ in 288 C57BL/6J mice, of which the study protocol is summarized in the Supplementary Methods (murine dose range finding [DRF] study). Briefly, acute toxicity of PCZ was tested up to 150 mg/kg, administered on two consecutive days to mice, and all doses were found to be safe. In the present work, the toxicity of repeated administration of PCZ was assessed in 426 healthy C57BL/6J mice under GLP. Mice were randomly allocated to four groups: vehicle group, low (37.5 mg/kg), intermediate (75 mg/kg) and high-dose groups (150 mg/kg). Each group contained cohorts to cover: (1) toxicity evaluation until the end of the treatment period (n = 12/sex; main group), (2) toxicokinetic and DPP3 evaluation (n = 6/sex for the vehicle group and n = 33/sex for PCZ groups), and (3) cytokine analysis (n = 6/sex). In addition, the vehicle and high-dose group included animals to assess toxicity following 2-week of recovery after end of treatment (n = 6/sex; recovery group). Mice were administered either vehicle or the respective doses of PCZ by slow intravenous bolus administration via the tail vein over a 15-second period. Study drug was administered on days 1 and 2, as well as on days 13 and 14.

A graphical representation of all study procedures, frequency of measurements and endpoints of the murine study is provided in Figure 1. Animals were observed for signs of toxicity, which included assessment of general signs of illness, appearance, behavior, temperature, body weight, food consumption, injection site observations, ophthalmic examinations, modified Irwin tests,21 and mortality. Blood analyses included drug toxicokinetics, cDPP3 kinetics, hematology, clinical chemistry and plasma cytokines. Blood was withdrawn following induction of anesthesia with isoflurane. Animals were not allowed to recover from anesthesia after blood collection was completed, but were sacrificed through inhalation of carbon dioxide.

Additionally, 6 animals per late toxicology dose group were sampled at day 28 (14 days after final dosing). For toxicokinetic analyses, blood was withdrawn from the jugular vein or retro-orbital plexus into serum Monovettes (Sarstedt AG & Co. KG, Nümbrecht, Germany) on various timepoints before and after dosing of study medication (blood from 3 animals per timepoint, sex and dose, max. 2 samples per animal). On days 1 and 14, blood was withdrawn pre-dose, as well as 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 3, 6, and 24 hours after dosing. Additionally, animals in the recovery group were sampled 72, 144, 240, and 336 hours after administration of the final dose on day 14. Samples were allowed to clot for at least 30 minutes, followed by centrifugation and storage at −80°C until batch-wise analysis. PCZ concentrations were measured using a sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay developed and validated by Eurofins ADME, France, for mouse serum with a rabbit anti-PCZ antibody (custom production for 4TEEN4 by Agro-Bio, La Ferté-Saint-Aubin, France; Lot L650.20064.1) as capturing phase and an anti-human IgG (Fc-specific) peroxidase (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# A0170, RRID: AB_257868) for detection (assay lower limit of quantification = 20 ng/mL). Interference experiments in mouse samples spiked with excess DPP3 showed that excess DPP3 did not affect recovery of PCZ concentrations. Mean values from 3 animals per timepoint, sex and group were calculated and a toxicokinetic profile generated across all time points. Toxicokinetic animals were sacrificed the day after the final blood sampling.

For plasma cytokine analyses (IFN-y, IL-2, IL-5, KC/GRO, MCP-1, IL-12p-70, IL-1b, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, TNF), blood from 6 animals per dose group was withdrawn from the retro-orbital plexus into EDTA-containing vacutainers 2 hours after dosing on days 1 and 14. Samples were centrifuged within 60 minutes after collection and stored at −80°C until batch-wise analysis on a Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) sector imager system platform (MESO Sector S 600 MM, Meso Scale Discovery, Rockville, Maryland, USA). Animals in the cytokine group were sacrificed following completion of blood sampling.

For cDPP3 enzyme activity measurements, blood was withdrawn from the retro-orbital plexus into serum Monovettes on days 1, 0.5 hours and 24 hours after dosing, and day 14, 0.5 and 336 hours after dosing for 6 males and 6 females per dosing group. Blood from the control animals was collected 0.5 hours after dosing on day 1 and day 14. Samples were allowed to clot for at least 30 minutes, followed by centrifugation (at 2000 g for 10 minutes). All serum was harvested into aliquots and stored ultra-frozen until batch-wise analysis using a soluble activity assay. This method determines DPP3 enzymatic activity from the conversion of Arg-Arg-β-Naphthylamide to fluorescent β-Naphthylamine.7,22 Analysis was performed in microplate and read by a SpectraMax iD3, with excitation and emission wavelengths at 340 and 420 nm, respectively.

Blood samples for putative analysis of anti-drug-antibodies (ADAs) were collected via the orbital sinus on Day 1 pre-dose and 336 h after the last dose on Day 14. Blood was collected into serum separator tubes and samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for at least 30 min. Serum was harvested after centrifugation and stored at −80°C. Since evaluation of the toxicokinetic profile, biomarker and safety endpoints did not indicate formation of ADAs in this study, samples were not analyzed.

Toxicity studies in cynomolgus monkeys

A previous less extensive (non-GLP) study was performed to assess the acute toxicity and establish a dose range for PCZ in 16 cynomolgus monkeys, of which the study protocol is summarized in the Supplementary Methods (monkey DRF study). Briefly, the acute toxicity of PCZ was tested up to 400 mg/kg administered on two consecutive days in cynomolgus monkeys, doses which were found to be safe. In the present work, we aimed to assess both early and late toxicity, as well as the reversibility of PCZ effects under GLP in an animal model highly similar to humans. Thirty-two cynomolgus monkeys were randomly allocated to 4 groups: n = 10 for the vehicle and high-dose (350 mg/kg) groups, n = 6 for the low- and intermediate-dose groups (40 and 120 mg/kg). All groups contained an equal number of male and female animals. Monkeys were administered either vehicle or the respective doses of PCZ by means of a 2-hour intravenous infusion. The study drug was administered on days 1, 2, 13, and 14.

A graphical representation of all study procedures, frequency of measurements and endpoints of the monkey study is provided in Figure 2. Animals were observed for signs of toxicity, which included assessment of general signs of illness, appearance, injection site observations, body weight, food consumption, ophthalmic examination, electrocardiogram (ECG)-jacket telemetry, respiratory rate, blood pressure, physical examinations, behavior, stool consistency, and mortality. Blood analyses included drug toxicokinetics, hematology, clinical chemistry, coagulation, cytokines, complement activation, and cDPP3 activity. Urine analyses included urinalysis as well as microscopic examination of sediment.

Blood analyses included determinations of different hematology, serum chemistry and coagulation parameters (Figure 2). For that, blood was withdrawn from the femoral vein at baseline (prior to first dosing), 24 hours after completion of second dosing on day 14, as well as on day 28 in recovery group animals. Blood was collected in potassium-EDTA, lithium heparin and trisodium citrate vacutainers for determination of hematology (fibrinogen, hemoglobin, reticulocytes, thrombocytes, white blood cells), serum chemistry (total bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, gamma glutamyl transferase (yGT), aspartate aminotransferase [ASAT], ALAT, creatinine kinase [CK]) and coagulation (activated partial thromboplastin time, prothrombin time, fibrinogen) parameters, respectively. Analyses were performed on the same day of sampling (Cobas 6000 analyzer by Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland).

For toxicokinetic analyses, blood was withdrawn from the femoral vein into serum Monovettes (Sarstedt AG & Co. KG, Nümbrecht, Germany) on various timepoints before and after dosing of the study medication. On days 1, blood was withdrawn pre-dose, as well as 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8 and 24 hours after dosing to collect toxicokinetic data prior to the second dose. Additionally, blood was sampled on Day 14 in the main animals at the same at the same timepoints as on day 1. Furthermore, the recovery animals were sampled 48, 72, 120, and 168 hours after the last administration of study medication on day 14. Samples were allowed to clot for at least 30 minutes, followed by centrifugation and storage at −80°C until batch-wise analysis. PCZ concentrations were measured using a sandwich ELISA assay developed and validated by Eurofins ADME, France, for monkey serum (lower limit of quantification = 320 ng/mL). The capturing phase of this assay is based on Streptavidin and Biotin-DPP3 peptide to specifically immobilize PCZ from the serum sample. Detection of PCZ is performed by peroxidase-conjugated secondary anti-human IgG1 antibody (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA; Cat# A10648). Interference experiments in cynomolgus monkey samples spiked with excess DPP3 showed that excess DPP3 did not affect recovery of PCZ concentrations.

For plasma cytokine and complement analyses, blood was withdrawn from the femoral vein into lithium-heparin (for cytokines) -and EDTA (for complement) vacutainers 4 hours after dosing on days 1 and 14. Samples were centrifuged within 60 minutes after withdrawal and subsequently stored at −80°C until batch-wise analysis using a Luminex 200 platform (LX200, R&D Systems, Bio-Teche LTD, Barton, Abingdon, United Kingdom). Complement (C3a and factor Bb fragments) analysis was performed using a three-step immunoassay procedure according to manufacturer’s instruction (MicroVue C3a Plus EIA Kit; Quidel catalog number A032 and MicroVue BbPlus Fragment EIA Kit; Quidel, A027). As a first step, standards, controls, and diluted samples were added to a well plate coated with a murine monoclonal antibody to human C3a or Bb. After incubation and a washing step, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-C3 or anti-Bb was added followed by another incubation and washing step. After the addition of a chromogenic enzyme substrate, the wells were measured spectrophotometrically at 450 nm.

For cDPP3 enzyme activity measurements, blood was withdrawn from the femoral vein into serum Monovettes pre-dose, as well as on days 1 and 14 at 4 hours and 24 hours after dosing. Samples were allowed to clot for at least 30 minutes, followed by centrifugation at 2000 g for 10 min. All serum was harvested into aliquots and stored ultra-frozen until batch-wise analysis. As in the mice study, the method is a soluble activity assay determining the enzymatic activity of DPP3 in a sample by means of the conversion of Arg-Arg-β-Naphthylamide into the fluorescent β-Naphthylamine.7,22 Analysis was performed in microplates and read by SpectraMax iD3 with excitation and emission wavelengths at 340 and 420 nm, respectively.

Blood samples for putative analysis of ADAs were collected via the femoral vein at pre-dose, on Day 14 and at the end of the recovery period. Blood was collected into serum separator tubes and samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for at least 30 min. Serum was harvested after centrifugation and stored at −80°C. Since evaluation of the toxicokinetic profile, biomarker, and safety endpoints did not indicate formation of ADAs in this study, samples were not analyzed.

Animals were sacrificed one day after the last administration of the study drug (i.e., Day 15) with the exception of the recovery toxicity subgroup, of which animals were sacrificed two weeks after the last administration of the study drug (Day 28). All animals were sacrificed through intravenous injection of pentobarbitone after sedation with ketamine, followed by exsanguination. Pathologic examinations included macroscopic and histopathologic examinations of different organs, including assessment of the absolute and relative weight of major organs.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation, SD) or median [interquartile range, IQR], depending on data distribution. Group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and pairwise comparisons. Levene tests were performed prior to one-way ANOVAs to assess equality of variance between groups. If one-way ANOVAs yielded overall significance, Dunnett’s pos-hoc tests were subsequently performed. A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SAS package for Windows (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), version 9.4.

Results

Repeated dose toxicity in mice

Mortality, clinical findings

No PCZ-related deaths occurred during the study, while a total of six non-PCZ-related deaths occurred: one vehicle group male (day 14), two low-dose group males (days 13 and 14), one intermediate-dose group male (day 13), two high-dose group males (both on day 1). Deceased animals were either found dead, euthanized due to moribund condition, or died during bleeding or dosing procedures. No adverse clinical signs were observed prior to death for any of the deceased animals. At necropsy, no adverse macroscopic or microscopic findings were noted. No cause of death could be established. These early deaths are not considered to be related to treatment with PCZ, but likely related to stress.

No PCZ-related clinical signs were noted at any time during the study in any of the groups. Furthermore, no PCZ-related alterations in body weight, weight gain, food consumption, or body temperature were observed for all groups (Supplementary Table S1A-C).

Toxicokinetics

All PCZ drug concentration measurements performed in the control group were below the assay’s lower limit of quantification (20 ng/mL, data not shown). Cmax was observed at early timepoints in most PCZ-dosed animals, which was to be expected with the intravenous bolus administration strategy employed (Figure 3). After reaching Cmax, PCZ concentrations readily declined. The day 1 PCZ concentrations were used to explore possible drug accumulation by comparing Cmax and AUC values to those obtained after repeat dosing on day 14. As a consequence of the longer sampling regimen and terminal elimination profile obtained after dosing on day 14 (up until 336 hours in recovery animals), calculated elimination t1/2 values ranged from 107 to 133 hours in males and from 152 to 165 hours in females (Table 1). Sex ratios for AUC0–24 were above 2 on a total of three occasions, as a consequence, toxicokinetic evaluation was performed per sex and mean calculations for total dose groups were not performed. In contrast to AUC0–24, Cmax sex ratios were below 2 on all occasions. However, Cmax measurements displayed high variation, impeding clear interpretation of a sex-specific pharmacokinetic profile (Table 1). Mean concentration values of PCZ were measurable throughout the sampling timepoints until 24 h on day 1, and up until 336 hours after dosing at day 14 (Figure 3). Exposure, as assessed by PCZ Cmax values, increased in a dose-proportional manner between the dose groups of 37.5, 75 and 150 mg/kg, while AUC0–24 values were generally greater than dose proportional (between 4.99 and 13.4-fold when comparing low and high dose with a 4-fold increase between dose levels) (Table 1). Both Cmax and AUC0–24 values were similar on days 1 and 14, indicating no relevant accumulation of PCZ after repeated dosing in mice (Table 1).

Modified Irwin test

Administration of PCZ produced no effects on the behavioral and physiological state of animals in any dose group, as determined by Irwin tests (data not shown).

Hematology, clinical chemistry and cytokines

There were no PCZ-related effects observed on either hematology (Supplementary Table S2A) or clinical chemistry (Supplementary Table S2B). Cytokine analysis did not show PCZ-related effects for any group at any of the tested dose levels. The majority of samples for IFN-y, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, and IL-6 were below the lower limit of quantification in all groups at all timepoints (Supplementary Table S2C). While measurable concentrations of IL-10, KC/GRO, and TNF were present in more animals, these concentrations were either detected in only one sex, did not display a dose-dependent relationship with administered PCZ or had no clinically relevant magnitude of change compared to the control group (Supplementary Table S2C).

Necropsy and histopathology

Histopathological analysis revealed minimally to slightly decreased rarefaction of the cytoplasm of hepatocytes in the liver of male mice in the intermediate and high-dose groups compared to the control group (Supplementary Table S3A). As this finding correlated with a reduced absolute and relative liver weight in males of the high-dose group (Supplementary Table S3B), these findings were both considered as related to PCZ administration. However, they were not accompanied by other findings (e.g., inflammation or necrosis). Moreover, no accompanying changes in concentrations of biomarkers associated with hepatic function (i.e., increase in ALAT, ASAT, and bilirubin) were noted (Supplementary Table S2B). Finally, no histopathological findings were found in any animal of the recovery subgroup (assessed 14 days after final dosing), indicating the observed effects are reversible (Supplementary Table S3A).

Pharmacodynamics

PCZ administration led to an attenuation of cDPP3 activity compared to control group animals at all dose levels tested (Figure 4). Inhibition of cDPP3 activity was found at all investigated timepoints (0.5 and 24 hours after dosing at day 1, and 0.5 and 336 hours after dosing on day 14) (Figure 4). All dose groups showed enzyme activity inhibition of over 40% after the first dose, increasing to over 90% after receiving the final dose in the intermediate-dose group (Figure 4). In the highest dose group, cDPP3 activity was below the limit of quantification in over 50% of the samples (Figure 4). No linear dose-dependent trends for cDPP3 activity inhibition were observed in the different dose-groups (Figure 4).

Repeated dose toxicity in cynomolgus monkeys

Mortality and clinical findings

No deaths occurred during the entire study protocol. Post-dose observations possibly related to PCZ were observed in one female of the high-dose group. Symptoms were observed after dosing on day 14 and included transient decreased activity, piloerection, cold extremities, and pale appearance (gingiva). All the aforementioned symptoms were resolved by the next day. Injection site observations included bruising, abnormal purple coloring of the skin, swelling, as well as thickening of skin at intravenous access sites. These findings were observed equally across all dose groups, including vehicle.

Toxicokinetics

All PCZ drug concentration measurements performed in the control group were below the assays lower limit of quantification (Figure 5). Cmax was observed between 2 and 4 hours after start of infusion in most animals, corresponding with the continuous intravenous route of administration for a 2-hour duration. After cessation of intravenous infusion, PCZ concentrations readily declined from Cmax (Table 2). Similar to the murine study, day 1 PCZ concentrations were used to explore possible drug accumulation by comparing Cmax and AUC values to those obtained after day 14 repeated dosing. Elimination half-life values after day 14 dosing in the low and intermediate-dose groups were comparable, ranging between 4.46 and 7.49 hours (Table 2). Due to the longer sampling timepoints in the high-dose group, which included a recovery group, the half-life for 350 mg/kg could be calculated more accurately and was 42.8 h. The longer sampling duration allowed for a richer terminal elimination profile, resulting in the more accurate elimination half-life calculation. Sex ratios for both Cmax and AUC0–24 were below 2 on all occasions, suggesting no relevant sex differences in the toxicokinetic profile of PCZ. Consequently, mean calculations for total dose groups are also reported in Table 2.

Mean concentrations for PCZ were detectable 24 hours after study drug infusion on days 1 and 14 in all dose groups (Figure 5). For recovery group animals (highest dose group only), PCZ could be detected up until the last measurement timepoint 197 hours after start of study drug infusion on day 14. Exposure, as assessed by PCZ mean Cmax and AUC0–24 values, increased in a dose-proportional manner for low and intermediate doses (Table 2). For the highest dose, a weak trend toward a higher than dose-proportional increase (i.e., higher than the expected exposure ratio of 8.75) was observed between the low- and high-dose groups. When comparing exposure on days 1 and 14, there were no signs indicating accumulation of PCZ in cynomolgus monkeys (Table 2).

Behaviour

A functional observation battery of neurobehavioral investigations (observation of behavior, gait, posture, respiration, discharge, excretion, involuntary movements, appearance of skin, tail, eyes, pelage, activity) did not reveal any remarkable PCZ-related effects in any dose group (data not shown).

Hemodynamics, respiratory parameters and electrocardiography

Noninvasive blood pressure investigations performed during days 1 and 14 did not reveal any clinically relevant differences in systolic, diastolic and mean arterial pressure between different dose groups

Hematology and coagulation

Hematology and coagulation evaluation revealed increased reticulocyte counts and fibrinogen concentration on day 15 compared to pre-dose values in all dose groups (including vehicle) (Supplementary Table S5). Observed changes in reticulocyte counts and fibrinogen both tended to return to pre-dose values at the later measurement timepoints in recovery animals, demonstrating reversibility (Supplementary Table S5). All other differences observed in hematology and coagulation parameters were either minimal, inconsistent between sexes or reflected trends already present prior to dosing (Supplementary Table S5).

Clinical chemistry

Clinical chemistry evaluation demonstrated an increase in ALAT, ASAT, and CK in all groups at day 15 compared to pre-dose values (Table 3). While increases were also observed in the vehicle groups, increases in ASAT appeared less pronounced compared to animals in the active treatment groups (3-fold versus up to 10-fold) (Table 3). No dose-dependency of the increase in the aforementioned biomarkers could be distinguished, although individual variance of measurements was high (Table 3). All these findings showed complete signs of recovery, with mean biomarker levels at the end of recovery period being similar to those prior to dosing, indicating reversibility of observed effects (Table 3). Alkaline phosphatase and yGT activities were lower than pre-dose values on Day 15, but were not statistically different to the control. Mean values at the end of the recovery period were again comparable to pre-dose values. Total bilirubin concentrations were minimally increased at Day 15 in PCZ-treated animals and control males (Table 3). Total bilirubin concentrations decreased in 2 out of 4 male animals at the end of recovery period, demonstrating at least partial recovery (Table 3).

Urine analysis parameters

No PCZ-related effects were observed on any of the assessed urine analysis parameters, which included urine appearance, volume, glucose, ketones, specific gravity, bilirubin, PH, blood screening, protein and a microscopic examination of urine sediment (data not shown).

Cytokines and complement activation

No PCZ-related effects were observed on any of the investigated cytokines (Supplementary Figure S3A-B). A minimal increase in IL-6 was observed in PCZ-treated females only. Since the increase was not dose-dependent, occurred in only one sex and showed no consistent pattern between dosing days 1 and 14 and no correlation to any other clinical or hematological findings, it was not considered PCZ-related. There was also no clear effect of PCZ treatment on complement component C3a (Supplementary Figure S4A). A minor increase in Factor Bb fragments was observed on days 1 and 14 both in controls and PCZ-treated animals, but with maximum 30% increase compared to control group mean values and no clear dose dependency, this was not deemed to be clinically relevant (Supplementary Figure S4B).

Necropsy and histopathology

Statistically significant differences in organ weights compared with the control group were limited to lower body weight-relative thymus weights in females of the low- and high-dose groups (−34% and −38%, p < 0.05), as well as higher body weight-relative uterus/cervix weights in females of the low-dose group (177% p < 0.05). As no dose dependency was observed for these findings, they were not deemed to be of clinical relevance. Macroscopic findings were limited to findings at the injection site and included dark red perivenous discoloration and thickened perivascular tissue (data not shown). Subsequent histopathology only revealed hemorrhages and mixed inflammatory cell infiltrates at the injection site (data not shown). Both macroscopic and histopathologic findings were equally present in control and PCZ-treated animals, indicating that they result from the injection procedure (not test item-related).

Pharmacodynamics

Reduced cDPP3 activity compared to the control group was present in each active dose group at every assessed timepoint (Figure 6). Generally, the inter-individual variation of cDPP3 activity at baseline was high, while the number of animals per dose group was low (Figure 6). Consequently, data did not allow for a clear interpretation of dose-dependent differences in inhibition of cDPP3 activity (Figure 6). An increase in DPP3 activity in the control cynomolgus monkeys after start of the study compared to pre-dose is likely reflecting a stress-related reaction caused by the various handling procedures (e.g., dosing, blood sampling), which is not obvious in PCZ-treated animals due to the inhibitory activity of PCZ.

Discussion

In the current study, we investigated the safety and toxicokinetic profile of the cDPP3 inhibiting antibody, PCZ, in mice and cynomolgus monkeys during and after repeated dose-administrations over a period of 14 days. Based on our results, repeated intravenous treatment with PCZ appeared well-tolerated and safe up to the maximum tested doses in both species, i.e., 150 mg/kg in mice and 350 mg/kg in cynomolgus monkeys, which were identified as NOAEL, providing a 12-fold and 29-fold safety margin to the highest tested clinical dose of 12 mg/kg (NCT06331884).

Toxicology

Previously published (non-GLP) animal studies already investigated the efficacy of PCZ in healthy mice,15 as well as in various animal models of septic shock.7,16,17 No safety concerns for PCZ were observed in any of these studies, although only single administrations in the intended therapeutic range were investigated. In the current study, no mortality or moribund conditions could be attributed to repeated PCZ administration in the supratherapeutic dose range. Furthermore, no PCZ-related changes in body weight, behavior, vital signs, laboratory parameters, cytokines or histopathological findings were deemed to be adverse, either due to a lack of clinical signs, the absence of dose−response relationships, the absence of correlation between histopathology and functional tissue markers, and/or the overall low severity and/or reversibility of findings within the follow-up period.23,24 Noninvasive blood pressure, respiratory and electrocardiographic investigations did not reveal any clinically relevant differences between different dose groups. In line with previous pharmacological studies,7,13 inhibition of the low cDPP3 activity in the circulation of healthy animals is not expected to influence systemic hemodynamic parameters.

In individual cynomolgus monkeys, transient clinical signs (i.e., decreased activity, piloerection) were observed at higher doses (≥200 mg/kg), but only on isolated dosing days (i.e., these signs were not observed on the other dosing days in these animals). This is a common observation for monkeys systemically administered foreign proteins, and unlikely to be caused by PCZ.25 Moreover, as these clinical signs only occurred at dose levels ≥ 16-fold higher than the actual maximum clinical dose (12 mg/kg), they are unlikely to have clinical significance.

Fully reversible reductions in reticulocyte counts and increased fibrinogen concentrations were observed in all monkey dose groups, including control animals. These transient findings are often observed in safety studies implementing cynomolgus monkeys, as a consequence of the repeated blood sampling (i.e., blood loss) necessary for determination of all GLP-mandated toxicologic- and toxicokinetic parameters.25,26 Clinical chemistry analysis showed an increase in ALAT, ASAT and CK enzymes in PCZ-treated monkeys, when compared to pre-dose values. While these increases were also observed in vehicle-treated animals, they were more pronounced in PCZ-treated animals. There was no dose-dependency of the observed changes and they were transient, showing complete normalization. Furthermore, and most importantly, there were no histopathological changes in any tissue, e.g., muscle or liver, or differences in relative or absolute organ weight compared the vehicle group and no signs indicating an impairment of the functional capacity of the liver. These findings were thus not considered adverse. In mice, no changes in biomarker enzymes were observed, but histopathology revealed a non-adverse, decreased rarefaction of the cytoplasm of the hepatocytes consistent with decreased glycogen accumulation in mid- and high-dose treated male animals. Since the finding was of low severity, not accompanied by necrosis or extensive fibrosis and did not result in an increase of liver enzyme biomarkers in the blood of the animals, they were not considered adverse. Nevertheless, since in both studies liver-related findings occurred, the liver is potentially influenced by PCZ. Several theories could be put forward to explain these findings. Although large circulating proteins like monoclonal antibodies can be metabolized by many cell types, they are mainly degraded by the liver.27–29 Particularly, monoclonal antibodies are removed from the circulation through endocytosis by hepatocytes, either being degraded or recycled to the cell surface for secretion.27 The absence of dose−response relations of the observed liver enzyme kinetics does not correspond with drug-induced liver injury reactions that have been described after administration of other monoclonal antibodies.27,30 Moreover, the absence of both systemic cytokine and chemokine responses after PCZ administration support the absence of such an adverse drug reaction. As complexes of antibodies and their soluble target are known to be cleared via the liver,31 the transient liver findings might also represent an adaptive response to the large degree of ongoing hepatocellular protein clearance at supra-pharmacological dose levels. These may in turn result in saturation of clearance mechanisms already at the lowest tested dose. Apart from that, there is no known mechanism indicating that inhibition of cDPP3 may impact the liver.

To account for potential effects of PCZ in humans, mitigation strategies were included in the subsequent first-in-man safety study. A first mitigation strategy is represented by the proposed starting dose of 3 mg/kg, providing a large safety margin compared to the doses implemented in the monkey and mice studies, while it also represents a dose below the expected pharmacologically active range of 5–10 mg/kg.7,16 Frequent monitoring of potential alterations of liver function (i.e., alkaline phosphatase, ASAT, ALAT, γGT, and bilirubin) after dosing represents a second mitigation strategy.32 These biomarkers were monitored several times in the first 24 hours after dosing of study medication to accurately assess early toxicity.32 To capture late effects, as well as the potential for reversibility, these biomarkers were also assessed during several follow-up visits until 28 days after dosing, a time-window that takes the drug’s expected terminal half-life into account with a sufficient margin of error.

Toxicokinetics

Toxicokinetic analyses in both species demonstrated that peak concentrations were attained almost immediately after cessation of PCZ administration, and that they increased in a dose-dependent manner. Based on comparable Cmax and AUC0–24 after dosing on days 1 and 14, no relevant accumulation of PCZ appears to be present after repeated dosing. As the longer sampling regimen in the highest dosed animals in both mice and monkeys provides a more robust terminal elimination profile of PCZ, its elimination half-life is estimated at ~2–7 days. Interestingly, our findings indicate that PCZ was cleared from the circulation relatively fast in comparison to other clinically used monoclonal antibodies (between 11–30 days), as well as endogenous IgG antibodies (~21 days).33,34 This relatively short half-life might partly be explained by the interaction of PCZ with its target cDPP3.

Similar to an antibody’s pharmacodynamic properties, its pharmacokinetic characteristics can be influenced by the molecular properties of its binding partner.31,35 PCZ’s antigen, DPP3, while being a large protein as well, has a short circulating half-life of approximately 20–70 minutes.36 Although the mechanisms responsible for cDPP3’s rapid clearance from the circulation have not been investigated in detail, studies on the clearance kinetics of other enzymes suggests endocytosis in the liver followed by further processing in lysosomes.37 Based on the relatively short half-life values compared to other IgG antibodies, the rapid clearance of PCZ-DPP3 complexes from the circulation appears likely. Since PCZ was present in substantial molar excess over cDPP3 at the investigated dose levels, ranging from 29,000- to 429,000-fold depending on species and dose group, target-mediated clearance may have contributed to the observed toxicokinetic profile, but is unlikely to be the sole cause. Other saturable clearance pathways, such as hepatic elimination of PCZ or DPP3-PCZ complexes via mannose receptors, appear more plausible and are consistent with the liver-related findings observed in both species.38 The impact of higher cDPP3 concentrations on PCZ kinetics in patients with cardiogenic shock 6–8,10 is currently being further investigated in a dose-ranging clinical study (Procard 1b/2a, NCT06832722)

Pharmacodynamics

Administration of PCZ resulted in a marked attenuation of cDPP3 activity at all timepoints in both species, confirming that PCZ was pharmacologically active at the tested doses in both species. A clear dose dependency of PCZ’s inhibitory effect on cDPP3’s enzymatic activity could not be established. Overall, inhibitory effects appeared comparable between male and female animals, with minor variation likely being caused by a paucity of control samples representing baseline cDPP3 enzyme activity for each sex. The absence of a dose−response relationship could again be explained by the molar excess of PCZ compared to cDPP3, meaning maximum inhibitory concentrations were already approached at the lowest investigated dosages in both species, which should all be considered as supratherapeutic.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of repeated administration models, which allowed for the accurate assessment of drug accumulation in addition to characterizing toxicokinetic parameters. The inclusion of a recovery group in both studies allowed for the determination of longer-term adverse effects, as well as an estimate of the reversibility of adverse effects observed early on. Finally, the inclusion of two different species with >90% cDPP3 homology compared to humans further reduces the chance of having missed clinically relevant side-effects. Despite the inclusion of a recovery group, an important limitation of this study remains the duration of follow-up for mid- and low-dose groups, which was insufficient to reach definitive conclusions on PCZ half-life. However, estimates are robust enough to estimate the required duration of follow-up for the first-in-man study with an acceptable margin of error.

Conclusions

Repeated intravenous administration of Procizumab was well tolerated and safe in both mice and cynomolgus monkeys up to the maximum tested doses in both species. Toxicologic analyses did not identify a target organ of toxicity. However, based on intercurrent changes in biomarkers and histopathological liver findings, appropriate mitigation strategies were implemented during the phase 1 clinical testing in humans. In the toxicity studies, Procizumab administration resulted in an immediate reduction of cDPP3 enzyme activity, confirming the intended pharmacodynamic mechanism of action. Results of this study supported a subsequent Phase 1 safety assessment of Procizumab in humans, with a starting dose of 3 mg/kg and a maximum dose of 12 mg/kg (NCT06331884).
