Effects of dietary betaine as a potential choline substitute on growth performance, intestinal histomorphology, tissue malondialdehyde concentrations, and hepatic heat shock protein 70 expression in heat-stressed broilers
Authors: Engin Yenice, İsmail Yavaş, Neşe Nuray Toprak, Ali Anıl Çenesiz, Necmettin Ceylan, İbrahim Çiftci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the potential of dietary betaine to replace supplemental choline by comparing choline, betaine, or their combination and assessing their effects on growth performance, intestinal histomorphology, tissue malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations, and hepatic heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) gene expression in broilers exposed to cyclic heat stress (HS). A total of 768 one-day-old male Ross 308 broilers were randomly allocated to six dietary treatments with eight replicates of 16 birds each. Treatments consisted of a control diet without supplemental betaine or choline (CON); recommended choline supplementation (CH-REC; 700, 600, and 500 mg/kg during the starter, grower, and finisher periods, respectively) or equivalent betaine supplementation (BT-MATCH); high-dose choline (CH-HIGH; 1000 mg/kg); high-dose betaine (BT-HIGH; 1000 mg/kg); and combined betaine and choline supplementation (BTCH-MIX; 500 mg/kg each). Cyclic HS was applied from day 25 to 41 at 32 ± 1°C for 6 h daily. During the grower period, CH-REC increased feed intake compared with CON and CH-HIGH (P = 0.034), whereas all supplemented groups showed greater body weight gain (BWG) than CON (P = 0.038). Under HS, CH-REC, BT-MATCH, and BTCH-MIX improved BWG and feed conversion ratio (P < 0.05), whereas mortality did not differ significantly among dietary treatments. Carcass traits, abdominal fat, and liver weight were unaffected by dietary treatments. Dietary supplementation generally improved jejunal histomorphological characteristics, with the most pronounced responses observed in BT-MATCH (P < 0.05). Tissue MDA concentrations were reduced in most supplemented groups (P < 0.05), except in liver samples of CH-REC birds. Hepatic HSP70 gene expression increased in all supplemented groups except CH-REC (P < 0.001). Overall, these findings suggest that betaine may partially substitute for supplemental choline in corn–soybean meal-based diets under cyclic HS conditions. Choline-equivalent betaine supplementation (BT-MATCH) supported growth performance and favorable responses in jejunal histomorphology and tissue MDA concentrations, together with treatment-related differences in hepatic HSP70 gene expression, whereas higher betaine supplementation provided no additional benefit.

Introduction

Heat stress (HS) remains one of the major challenges facing modern poultry production, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions, and its impact is expected to increase with global warming (Onagbesan et al., 2023). Broiler chickens are highly susceptible to HS because of their feather-covered bodies, lack of sweat glands, and elevated metabolic activity, which leads them to dissipate excess body heat by increasing their respiratory rate. Under HS conditions, broilers typically decrease feed consumption to reduce metabolic heat generation, which consequently impairs feed efficiency, body weight gain, and survival rate (Nawaz et al., 2021). Heat stress also compromises intestinal integrity and nutrient absorption and promotes oxidative stress through excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby negatively affecting health and productivity (Aryal et al., 2025). Various nutritional strategies have been investigated over the years to reduce the adverse effects of HS in broilers (Mangan and Siwek, 2024). Among the nutritional approaches available, betaine and choline have received considerable attention because of their roles as osmolytes and methyl donors. These compounds, known as osmolytes, play an important role in maintaining cellular volume and balancing intracellular fluid levels (Ratriyanto and Mosenthin, 2018).

Betaine, a naturally occurring trimethylated glycine derivative, functions as an effective osmolyte owing to its zwitterionic structure. It helps maintain cellular hydration and ionic balance, protects intestinal epithelial cells and proteins from environmental stress, and reduces the energy required for ion transport (Abd El-Ghany and Babazadeh, 2022). Consequently, betaine may alleviate HS, enhance nutrient utilization and growth performance, improve carcass quality through modulation of lipid metabolism, reduce lipid peroxidation, and strengthen antioxidant defenses (Alirezaei et al., 2012; Abd El-Ghany and Babazadeh, 2022). In addition, as a methyl donor, betaine contributes to energy and protein metabolism and can partially replace dietary choline and methionine in poultry diets (Elsherbeni et al., 2025).

Choline, a precursor of betaine, can act as a source of organic osmolytes after being oxidized to betaine. However, this conversion is not fully efficient because it depends on a two-step mitochondrial process in liver and kidney tissues (Ratriyanto et al., 2009; Kumar et al., 2024). Accordingly, dietary betaine has been shown to increase hepatic betaine deposition more effectively than an equivalent molar amount of choline (Saarinen et al., 2001). Moreover, although both compounds serve as methyl donors, betaine can directly participate in the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine, whereas choline must first be converted to betaine before entering this pathway (Kidd et al., 1997; Abd El-Ghany and Babazadeh, 2022). Nevertheless, there is still no consensus regarding the extent to which betaine can replace choline, or vice versa, in broiler diets, as most studies involving choline have primarily focused on its methionine-sparing effects (Kpodo et al., 2015; Mahmoudi et al., 2018; Abd El-Ghany and Babazadeh, 2022; Gregg et al., 2023).

Although the beneficial effects of dietary betaine and choline supplementation on broiler performance under HS have been reported, direct comparisons of these two metabolically related methyl donors under identical experimental conditions remain scarce. In particular, comparative information on how different betaine- and choline-based supplementation strategies influence performance, intestinal, oxidative, and cellular stress-related responses under HS conditions remains limited. We hypothesized that betaine could provide physiological and productive responses comparable to those of choline and potentially exert complementary effects when combined with choline. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate different practical supplementation strategies of betaine and choline, administered alone or in combination, on the growth performance, jejunal histomorphology, oxidative markers, and cellular stress responses of broiler chickens exposed to cyclic heat stress. By directly comparing these two compounds within the same experimental framework, this study provides novel insights into their relative efficacy and highlights the potential of betaine as an alternative or complementary nutritional strategy under heat-stress conditions.

Material and methods

Birds and housing

A total of 768 one-day-old male Ross 308 chicks were reared in 48 floor pens (1.4 × 0.9 m each), each bedded with wood shavings and equipped with nipple drinkers and manually filled hanging plastic poultry feeders. Before the initiation of HS (0–24 d), all birds were reared under the standard environmental conditions recommended for their age in the Ross Broiler Management Manual (Aviagen, 2018). The lighting program and automatic heating, cooling, and ventilation systems were also managed in accordance with the recommendations of the same manual. Cyclic HS started when birds were 25 days old and consisted of 6 h at 32 ± 1°C during the daytime, approximately between 12.00 p.m. and 6.00 p.m., using an automated heater, followed by a 3 h gradual decrease to 25°C (Ribeiro et al., 2005). Birds were monitored at least three times daily for health and welfare throughout the experiment. Birds were allowed free access to mash diets and fresh water throughout the experiment. Animal handling and experimental procedures were performed under the approval of the Animal Ethics Committee of Ankara University (approval number: 2022-13-119).

Experimental design and diets

Experimental groups were formed using a randomized complete block design. A total of 768 one-day-old chicks were allocated to six dietary treatment groups, each consisting of eight replicates, with 16 chicks per pen. The dietary treatments were designed to compare different practical supplementation strategies of choline and betaine under cyclic heat-stress conditions. Six experimental diets were formulated: a basal control diet without supplemental choline or betaine (CON); a diet supplemented with commercially recommended, period-decreasing levels of choline (700, 600, and 500 mg/kg during the starter, grower, and finisher periods, respectively; CH-REC); a diet supplemented with betaine at levels matched to the corresponding choline doses (700, 600, and 500 mg/kg; BT-MATCH) to enable a direct comparison between the two methyl-donor compounds; a diet supplemented with a constant high level of choline throughout the production cycle (1000 mg/kg; CH-HIGH); a diet supplemented with a constant high level of betaine throughout the production cycle (1000 mg/kg; BT-HIGH); and a combined supplementation treatment containing 500 mg/kg betaine and 500 mg/kg choline (BTCH-MIX). The period-decreasing supplementation programs were selected to reflect practical feeding approaches commonly used in commercial broiler production. The supplemental choline levels used in the CH-REC treatment were selected considering the choline content of the basal diets and the choline recommendations of the NRC (1994) and Aviagen (2019) for Ross 308 broilers. In contrast, the constant high-dose treatments were included to determine whether maintaining elevated levels of choline or betaine throughout the rearing period could provide additional benefits under heat-stress conditions, where metabolic demands and nutrient requirements may be increased. The constant supplementation level of 1000 mg/kg was selected based on choline and betaine supplementation levels previously evaluated in broilers (Kpodo et al., 2020). Furthermore, 1000 mg/kg betaine is within the range previously evaluated in broilers under HS conditions (Liu et al., 2019; Ghasemi and Nari, 2020). The combined betaine–choline treatment was incorporated to evaluate the response to the simultaneous supplementation of both methyl-donor compounds. Because no established dietary recommendations are available for betaine in broiler chickens, the betaine inclusion levels were set equal to the corresponding supplemental choline levels. ActiBeet® 96 (Agrana Company, Vienna, Austria), used as a source of betaine, is a natural product obtained from sugar beet molasses via ion exclusion. ActiBeet® 96 contains 96% betaine (as betaine anhydrous) and an anticaking agent. Choline chloride (75%) was supplied by a local broiler integration company from a fresh tank batch and contained 560 000 mg choline/kg. Experimental diets based on corn and soybean meal were used during the starter (0–10 days), grower (11–24 days), and finisher (25–41 days) periods. The diets were formulated according to the nutrient recommendations of Ross 308 to meet or exceed those of Aviagen (2019) and National Research Council (1994) (Table 1). Proximate nutrient composition of the experimental diets and feed ingredients was determined using standard analytical procedures described by AOAC (2005). Total phosphorus concentrations of the experimental diets were also determined according to AOAC (2005). The amino acid profiles, phosphorus and calcium contents, and AMEn values of feed ingredients were estimated using near-infrared reflectance spectroscopy (NIRS; Evonik Nutrition & Care GmbH, Hanau-Wolfgang, Germany). Total choline levels of the basal diets were calculated according to the values reported for feed ingredients in the INRA feed tables (Sauvant et al., 2004). All micro-ingredients, including the betaine and choline supplements, were first mixed in a premix mixer and subsequently incorporated into the complete diet. Measurements of growth performance, carcass traits, abdominal fat, and liver

The body weights of the birds were measured on days 0, 10, 24, and 41. Feed intake (FI) was determined separately for the starter (0–10 days), grower (11–24 days), and finisher (25–41 days) periods. Based on these data, body weight gain (BWG) and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were calculated for each growth phase and for the overall experimental period (0–41 days). All measurements were recorded per pen for each replicate. Mortality was monitored daily, and corresponding corrections were applied in the calculation of FCR. Sixteen birds from each treatment (2 birds per pen) were randomly selected and subjected to an overnight fast of 8 h. After fasting, the birds were weighed and fitted with leg bands for individual identification. Birds were euthanized by severing the jugular vein and were allowed to bleed for approximately 2 min. Subsequently, the birds were immersed in hot water for approximately 30 s and the feathers were removed using a rotary defeathering machine. Viscera and abdominal fat were removed. Carcass, breast, drumstick, and thigh meat (bone-in and skin-on), abdominal fat, and liver were weighed and expressed as a fraction of individual live body weight (BW).

Jejunal histomorphology analysis

Among the birds used for carcass evaluation, one bird was randomly selected from each pen (8 birds per treatment) for laboratory analyses, including jejunal histomorphology, tissue MDA concentration, and hepatic HSP70 gene expression. For histomorphology analysis, approximately 5-cm sections were collected from the middle portion of the jejunum and gently rinsed in saline. Tissue samples were preserved in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 72 h at room temperature. Following standard histological processing, the samples were embedded in paraffin blocks. Sections of 4 μm thickness were cut with a microtome and mounted on glass slides. Histological images were captured using a Zeiss AxioLab.A1 light microscope equipped with a Zeiss AxioCam ICc 5 digital camera (Carl Zeiss AG, Germany). Morphometric measurements were performed using the ImageJ image analysis software (Laboratory for Optical and Computational Instrumentation, University of Wisconsin-Madison, Madison, WI, US). Jejunal histomorphology was evaluated by measuring villus height (VH), villus width (VW), crypt depth (CD), villus height: crypt depth ratio (VH/CD), villus surface area, and goblet cell number per jejunal villus according to the method described by Solis de los Santos et al. (2005).

Determinations of malondialdehyde concentrations in tissues

Liver and breast muscle tissues were homogenized in ice-cold physiological saline solution (10%, w/v) for 60 s, and subsequently disrupted using an ultrasonic cell grinder (Scientz-IID, NingBo Scientz Biotech Co., Ltd., Ningbo, Zhejiang, China) for 1 min with cycles of 1 s operation and 2 s intervals. The resulting homogenates were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected for malondialdehyde (MDA) analysis. MDA concentrations were determined using a commercial MDA assay kit (A003-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions and expressed as MDA content per mg of protein.

RNA extraction and HSP70 gene expression

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissue using TRIzol Reagent (Cat. No. 15596018; Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration was quantified with a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (Gene Company Ltd., Hong Kong, China). RNA integrity was assessed by denaturing agarose gel electrophoresis. Briefly, RNA samples were mixed with denaturing loading buffer, heated at 65°C for 10 min, and immediately chilled on ice before electrophoresis. Hepatic HSP70 gene expression was normalized to the GAPDH reference gene and expressed relative to the control (CON) as the mean fold difference (2-ΔΔCT) (Brown et al., 2018).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using a randomized complete block design with the General Linear Models (GLM) procedure of STATISTICA (Version 8.0; StatSoft Inc., Tulsa, OK, USA). Blocks were defined according to the spatial location of pens within the experimental house. Dietary treatment was considered a fixed effect, whereas block was included in the model as a random effect. Each pen was considered the experimental unit, with eight replicate pens per treatment. When treatment effects were statistically significant (P < 0.05), differences among treatment means were determined using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test. Mortality was assessed using Fisher’s exact test.

Growth performance, carcass traits, abdominal fat, and liver

Considering the periods before HS, no significant differences in growth parameters were observed among treatments during the starter period. All groups supplemented with betaine, choline, or their combination had higher BWG than the CON group (P = 0.038). The temperature during HS was 32 ± 1°C, whereas it was 25 ± 1°C during cooler hours, and relative humidity was 60 ± 5% during HS and 50 ± 5% during cooler hours. CH-HIGH and BT-HIGH did not provide greater growth-performance responses than CH-REC and BT-MATCH, respectively. Histomorphology parameters

Discussion

Growth performance before cyclic heat stress

Before HS exposure, birds receiving choline, betaine, or their combination had higher BWG during the grower period than the CON group, whereas FI was higher only in birds receiving CH-REC than in those fed CH-HIGH and the CON diet. Although FCR was not significantly affected, all supplemented groups showed numerically improved feed efficiency relative to CON. Previous studies conducted during periods without HS have reported inconsistent performance responses to choline and betaine supplementation. Some studies (Summers, 2013; Shakeri et al., 2019) reported that dietary choline or betaine supplementation did not affect broiler performance. Mahmoudi et al. (2018) also reported variable pre-HS performance responses to different levels and sources of methyl donors. In their first trial, different levels of choline (280 and 560 mg/kg) or betaine (320 and 640 mg/kg) did not affect FI, BWG, or FCR, whereas in their second trial, different combinations of choline and betaine affected FCR but not FI or BWG. However, the FCR response did not show a consistent dose-related pattern. Kpodo et al. (2020), who directly compared 500 and 1000 mg/kg of both choline and betaine, likewise found no significant effects of dietary treatment on FI or BWG during the pre-HS period. Although feed efficiency was affected by dietary treatment, increasing supplementation from 500 to 1000 mg/kg did not result in a consistent additional benefit. The greater BWG observed in birds receiving choline, betaine, or their combination in the present study may be associated with the distinct but metabolically related functions of these compounds. Their roles in methyl-group and lipid metabolism may support nutrient utilization and the availability of methionine for metabolic processes associated with protein synthesis and growth (Eklund et al., 2005; Zeisel and Blusztajn, 1994). Moreover, betaine supplementation may spare the energy otherwise expended during the conversion of choline to betaine, thereby supporting growth and maintenance in broilers (Singh et al., 2015).

Regarding the relative efficacy of choline and betaine, increasing the supplementation level to a constant 1000 mg/kg (BT-HIGH and CH-HIGH) did not provide additional improvements in growth performance compared with BT-MATCH and CH-REC during the pre-HS period. A similar lack of a consistent additional response to the higher supplementation level was reported by Kpodo et al. (2020), who directly compared 500 and 1000 mg/kg of both choline and betaine during the pre-HS period. Because the experimental diets met breeder recommendations for digestible methionine and methionine + cysteine, the potential for an additional growth response to higher methyl-donor supplementation may have been limited. Although choline and betaine have overlapping as well as distinct physiological roles, the performance responses observed with equivalent supplemental levels of betaine and choline during the pre-HS period were comparable. Similarly, El-Moniary et al. (2005), who compared choline and betaine on an equimolar basis, reported comparable growth performance between the two methyl-donor sources, while increasing dietary betaine to higher levels provided no additional performance benefit. Therefore, from a nutritional supplementation perspective, these findings support the potential of betaine as a choline substitute for maintaining growth performance during the pre-HS period, without implying complete physiological equivalence between the two compounds.

In the present study, the calculated choline contents of the basal diets were 1421 and 1322 mg/kg during the starter and grower phases, respectively. Although these values were approximately 300 mg/kg lower than the breeder recommendations for Ross 308 broilers, they remained above the NRC recommendations for birds of comparable age (National Research Council, 1994; Aviagen, 2019). The effects of supplemental choline provided above the levels naturally present in corn–soybean meal diets remain controversial because previous studies have reported inconsistent results. Gregg et al. (2022) reported that supplementing diets containing approximately 1200 mg/kg choline with an additional 400–2000 mg/kg choline had no significant effect on the growth performance of high-yielding broilers up to 32 days of age. The response to betaine, however, appears to depend on the amount of choline available in the diet. Dilger et al. (2007) demonstrated that betaine could partially spare supplemental choline when a minimum amount of choline was present, whereas betaine alone did not support growth in a choline-free diet. Consistent with this distinction, Emmert et al. (1996) found that supplemental choline improved growth performance in chicks fed a choline-deficient diet, whereas betaine or methionine did not. More recently, Kumar et al. (2024) showed that betaine supplementation could support performance when dietary choline and methionine were moderately reduced, but this response was not maintained with greater reductions. Together, these findings indicate that the nutritional relationship between choline and betaine depends on both supplementation level and methyl-donor source. Thus, part of the methyl-donor requirement supplied through choline may be spared by betaine supplementation, but this does not imply that betaine can replace the essential choline-specific functions (Dilger et al., 2007; Kumar et al., 2024). This may be because choline is preferentially utilized for essential physiological functions such as phospholipid and acetylcholine synthesis before becoming available for methyl-group metabolism (Zeisel and Blusztajn, 1994).

Growth performance during cyclic heat stress

Heat stress is known to impair broiler performance through a range of physiological disturbances, including increased respiratory activity, reduced feed intake, acid–base imbalance, and compromised intestinal function, which collectively can increase mortality and depress growth (Liu et al., 2019; Wasti et al., 2020; Nawaz et al., 2021). Under the cyclic HS conditions of the present study, FI was numerically lower in CON during the finisher period, although the differences were not significant, while over 0–41 d, CON had lower FI than all supplemented groups except CH-HIGH. This lower FI may have partly contributed to the poorer growth performance of CON birds, as reduced feed intake is a common adaptive response to HS that limits metabolic heat production (Kpodo et al., 2020; Abd El-Ghany and Babazadeh, 2022). During the HS period, CH-REC, BT-MATCH, and BTCH-MIX improved BWG and FCR relative to CON. Importantly, increasing either choline or betaine supplementation to 1000 mg/kg provided no additional performance advantage over CH-REC or BT-MATCH. CH-REC and BT-MATCH produced comparable BWG and FCR responses, suggesting similar efficacy of the two methyl-donor sources at the matched supplementation levels used during HS, while BTCH-MIX provided no additional benefit over either single-source strategy. Across 0–41 d, CH-REC and BT-MATCH improved both BWG and FCR relative to CON, whereas CH-HIGH improved only FCR and BT-HIGH only BWG; neither high-level treatment outperformed its corresponding lower-level strategy. Direct comparisons of choline and betaine under HS conditions are limited. Kpodo et al. (2020), who directly compared 500 and 1000 mg/kg of both choline and betaine, reported no consistent additional performance benefit from increasing either methyl-donor source from 500 to 1000 mg/kg. Mahmoudi et al. (2018) also reported variable performance responses when different levels of choline and betaine, alone or in combination, were evaluated under HS conditions, without a consistent dose-related pattern. Collectively, these findings indicate that performance responses were not simply dose-dependent and that neither higher supplementation levels nor combined supplementation necessarily provided additional benefits.

The beneficial responses observed may be attributed to the complementary physiological functions of betaine and choline. As methyl donors, these compounds may reduce the requirement for other dietary methyl-group sources, which may be particularly important under stress conditions when tissue repair and maintenance increase methyl-group demand (Kidd et al., 1997; Eklund et al., 2005). In addition, betaine acts as an organic osmolyte, while choline can contribute to osmoprotection following its oxidation to betaine, thereby helping to maintain cellular hydration and osmotic balance under hyperthermic conditions (Ratriyanto and Mosenthin, 2018). These physiological functions may contribute to the better growth performance observed in the supplemented groups under HS conditions. HS is known to induce endocrine and metabolic disturbances (Onagbesan et al., 2023), impair intestinal epithelial integrity, reduce digestive enzyme activity, and decrease the expression of nutrient transporters, ultimately compromising nutrient utilization (Yousefi et al., 2023). Supporting this view, Liu et al. (2019) reported that dietary betaine alleviated HS-induced reductions in growth performance and digestive capacity, likely through its osmoregulatory, methyl-donating, and antioxidant properties. Consistent with the present findings, dietary betaine supplementation under HS (500–2000 mg/kg) has been reported to improve BWG compared with the unsupplemented control (Liu et al., 2019; Ghasemi and Nari, 2020; Dadvar et al., 2022; Yousefi et al., 2023). In addition to growth performance, betaine has also been shown to enhance nutrient utilization under HS conditions. For example, Yousefi et al. (2023) reported that 1000 mg/kg dietary betaine improved nutrient digestibility in heat-stressed broilers. Improvements in FCR have likewise been reported with supplementation of 1000 mg/kg betaine in the diet (Ghasemi and Nari, 2020) or 1000 mg/L in drinking water (Al-Garadi et al., 2023) resulted in improved FCR compared with the control group. However, some studies have reported that neither HS exposure itself (Summers, 2013; Mahmoudi et al., 2018) nor dietary supplementation with betaine, choline, or both at 500–1000 mg/kg under HS conditions (Kpodo et al., 2015; Mahmoudi et al., 2018; Shakeri et al., 2019; Al-Garadi et al., 2023) had a significant effect on BWG. Likewise, dietary betaine or choline supplementation has been reported to have no significant effect on FI in heat-stressed broilers (Mahmoudi et al., 2018; Ghasemi and Nari, 2020; Al-Garadi et al., 2023; Yousefi et al., 2023). These inconsistencies among studies suggest that responses to betaine and choline supplementation under HS conditions may vary according to the severity and duration of HS, dietary composition, bird genotype, and management conditions.

Although mortality did not differ significantly among dietary treatments, all supplemented groups showed numerically lower mortality than CON during both the HS period and the overall experimental period, with the lowest values observed in CH-REC and BT-MATCH. Previous studies have reported variable effects of dietary supplementation on mortality in heat-stressed broilers. Under cyclic HS conditions, broilers exposed to 34°C for 8 h/day from hatch to slaughter age (Yousefi et al., 2023) or to 36°C for 4 h/day during 7–28 days of age (Dadvar et al., 2022) showed significantly reduced mortality when diets were supplemented with betaine (1000 mg/kg). In contrast, and consistent with the lack of significant differences among dietary treatments in the present study, broilers exposed to cyclic HS at 34°C for 6 h/day from 21 to 42 d of age showed no significant changes in mortality when supplemented with betaine or choline at 500 or 1000 mg/kg (Summers, 2013). Similarly, Mahmoudi et al. (2018) reported no effect on mortality in broilers exposed to 35°C for 12 h/day from 21 to 42 d of age when supplemented with betaine (320–640 mg/kg) or choline (280–560 mg/kg).

Carcass and cut yield, abdominal fat, and liver

In the present study, dietary supplementation with choline, betaine, or their combination had no significant effects on the relative weights of the carcass and its parts (breast, drumstick, and thigh) or on abdominal fat deposition under HS conditions. These findings are in agreement with Mahmoudi et al. (2018), who reported that supplementation with betaine (320 and 640 mg/kg) or choline (280 and 560 mg/kg), either alone or in combination, did not affect carcass and breast meat yield in heat-stressed broilers. Similarly, Liu et al. (2019) found that dietary betaine supplementation (0–2000 mg/kg) had no significant effect on the relative amount of abdominal fat under HS conditions. In contrast to the present findings, dietary betaine supplementation has been reported to improve carcass and breast meat yields in heat-stressed broilers (Liu et al., 2019; Dadvar et al., 2022). Furthermore, Dadvar et al. (2022) observed improvements in leg yield and carcass ratio together with a reduction in abdominal fat deposition. These inconsistent responses may partly reflect differences in dietary amino acid status and HS conditions among studies. Dietary methionine status may be particularly relevant, as some of the reported effects of betaine on breast muscle and abdominal fat deposition were observed in broilers fed methionine-deficient diets (McDevitt et al., 2000; Zhan et al., 2006). Under such conditions, the methyl-donor function of betaine may contribute to methionine sparing and thereby influence nutrient utilization and carcass composition. In addition, Zhan et al. (2006) associated the reduction in abdominal fat with changes in carnitine synthesis and lipolytic activity, suggesting that dietary conditions may influence the effects of betaine on lipid metabolism. HS itself can also alter amino acid and protein metabolism and reduce protein deposition in broilers (Qaid and Al-Garadi, 2021). Therefore, differences in dietary amino acid status and HS conditions among studies may partly account for the variable effects of betaine on carcass composition.

Although HS has been associated with hepatic oxidative stress, lipid accumulation, and inflammation (Liu et al., 2019; Chen et al., 2022), these alterations were not reflected in treatment-related changes in relative liver weight in the present study. The lack of differences in relative liver weight may be partly related to the well-balanced methionine and cysteine levels in the diets (Table 1), as methionine deficiency has been associated with increased hepatic oxidative stress (Khatlab et al., 2019). Mahmoudi et al. (2018) reported that the relative liver weight was significantly reduced by dietary betaine supplementation compared with choline supplementation; however, no significant change was observed when betaine and choline were supplemented together. The researchers emphasized that these findings were unexpected because they anticipated that choline, due to its role in hepatic lipid metabolism, would reduce both liver lipid accumulation and liver weight (Mahmoudi et al., 2018).

Jejunal histomorphology

The jejunal histomorphological characteristics observed in the CON group are consistent with those previously reported in broilers under HS conditions, including lower goblet cell number, VH, and VH/CD ratio and greater CD (Liu et al., 2019; Ahmad et al., 2022; Won et al., 2023). In broilers, HS may reduce intestinal blood flow through the redistribution of blood toward peripheral tissues and respiratory organs, leading to local hypoxia, oxidative stress, and inflammation that impair intestinal epithelial integrity and contribute to reduced VH and increased CD (Liu et al., 2019). HS also disrupts cellular water balance and membrane permeability, exposing enterocytes to osmotic stress (Ratriyanto and Mosenthin, 2018). HS in broilers triggers excessive ROS production that overwhelms antioxidant defenses, leading to oxidative damage to proteins, lipids, and DNA and consequently impairing intestinal integrity (Khatlab et al., 2019; Oke et al., 2024). Moreover, heat stress impairs intestinal barrier function, which can reduce the number of mucin-producing goblet cells and consequently compromise mucosal protection (Liu et al., 2019; Won et al., 2023).

The present findings suggest that dietary supplementation with choline, betaine, or their combination can support small intestinal morphology under HS conditions. At the matched supplementation levels, BT-MATCH resulted in greater villus height and VH/CD ratio than CH-REC, indicating a more favorable response to betaine than to choline for these specific jejunal morphological traits. Increasing either betaine or choline supplementation to 1000 mg/kg provided no additional improvement over BT-MATCH or CH-REC, respectively. The combined supplementation of choline and betaine did not provide additive or synergistic effects on jejunal morphology. This lack of an additional response may partly reflect the overlapping metabolic functions of choline and betaine, whereby combined supplementation may provide limited additional benefit when these functions are already adequately supported (Eklund et al., 2005; Dilger et al., 2007). In agreement with the present findings, Liu et al. (2019) and Yousefi et al. (2023) reported that dietary betaine supplementation increased jejunal VH and the VH/CD ratio in heat-stressed broilers. However, in contrast to the present study, those authors observed greater improvements at higher betaine inclusion levels (1000 or 2000 mg/kg), which may be related to the dietary concentrations of methionine, choline, or both. Conversely, Kpodo (2015) reported that HS applied after 21 days of age, as well as dietary supplementation with choline, betaine, or their combination, did not affect intestinal VH, CD, or VH/CD ratio in broilers. In the present study, in addition to favorable responses in villus morphology, dietary supplementation with choline, betaine, or their combination increased jejunal goblet cell numbers compared with the control group. Our findings are consistent with a previous study by Won et al. (2023), who reported increased goblet cell numbers following dietary supplementation with betaine, glycine, or their combination. Goblet cells produce mucins that form the protective mucus layer covering the intestinal surface and contribute to intestinal integrity, nutrient absorption, and immune homeostasis (Won et al., 2023). Accordingly, the higher goblet cell numbers observed in the supplemented groups may reflect better maintenance of the intestinal mucosal barrier under HS conditions.

The positive influence of betaine on intestinal morphology has been attributed to several complementary mechanisms. As a methyl-group donor, betaine may promote intestinal epithelial cell proliferation, while its osmoprotective properties may help stabilize the intestinal environment under stress conditions. Moreover, its antioxidant activity may reduce HS-induced oxidative damage (Wang et al., 2018). By contributing to osmotic balance and maintaining intestinal cell volume, betaine may support digestive enzyme secretion and nutrient uptake, consequently improving growth performance in broilers (Liu et al., 2019). Supporting this interpretation, Eklund et al. (2005) suggested that betaine helps preserve villus integrity, which may improve nutrient digestibility and absorptive capacity. The more favorable responses of BT-MATCH than CH-REC in villus height and VH/CD ratio may partly reflect differences in the metabolic utilization of betaine and choline. A possible explanation is that choline must first be oxidized to betaine, primarily in the liver and kidneys, before exerting osmoprotective and methyl-donor functions. This conversion requires metabolic energy and may become less efficient during HS. Indeed, the conversion of choline to betaine has been proposed as a rate-limiting process (Ratriyanto et al., 2009). Moreover, dietary choline is preferentially utilized for several essential physiological functions, including phosphatidylcholine synthesis for cell membrane maintenance, acetylcholine production for neurotransmission, and hepatic lipid metabolism (Mahmoudi et al., 2018). Consequently, a smaller proportion of supplemented choline may be available for conversion to betaine, which could explain its comparatively weaker effect on villus height and VH/CD ratio under HS conditions.

Tissue Malondialdehyde Concentrations and Hepatic HSP70 Gene Expression

Malondialdehyde, a major end-product of polyunsaturated fatty acid peroxidation, is widely used as a biomarker of oxidative stress and cellular damage. Elevated mitochondrial and plasma MDA concentrations in heat-stressed broilers have been associated with increased oxidative injury to cellular components (Khatlab et al., 2019; Yousefi et al., 2023). Previous studies investigating the effects of betaine supplementation under HS have produced inconsistent results. Yousefi et al. (2023) reported a significant reduction in blood MDA concentrations, while Yang et al. (2022) observed a numerical decrease in breast muscle MDA concentrations in betaine-supplemented broilers. Direct comparisons of choline and betaine with respect to MDA responses under HS conditions are more limited. Mahmoudi et al. (2018), who evaluated different dietary levels of choline and betaine as well as their combinations in heat-stressed broilers, found no significant differences in blood MDA concentrations among the dietary treatments. In the present study, all supplemented groups had lower breast muscle MDA concentrations than CON, whereas liver MDA was lower than CON in all supplemented groups except CH-REC. The responses among supplementation strategies also revealed tissue-specific differences. In breast muscle, MDA concentrations did not differ among the choline, betaine, and combined supplementation strategies or between supplementation levels. In the liver, however, BT-MATCH resulted in lower MDA concentrations than CH-REC, while CH-HIGH also reduced liver MDA relative to CH-REC. Increasing betaine supplementation from BT-MATCH to BT-HIGH provided no additional reduction, and BTCH-MIX did not further reduce liver MDA compared with the effective single-source treatments. The lower tissue MDA concentrations observed in the supplemented groups suggest that choline, betaine, or their combination may help limit lipid peroxidation under HS conditions. A possible biochemical basis for this response is their involvement in methyl-group metabolism and in metabolic pathways supplying substrates for glutathione (GSH) synthesis. Methionine residues are considered important endogenous antioxidants that contribute to the activity and stability of biological proteins (Khatlab et al., 2019). Beyond its role in protein synthesis, methionine serves as a precursor for cysteine through the transmethylation–transsulfuration pathway, which subsequently contributes to GSH synthesis (Swennen et al., 2011). Sbodio et al. (2019) further reported that nearly half of the cysteine generated via this pathway is utilized for GSH synthesis in hepatic cells. Moreover, Alirezaei et al. (2012) suggested that betaine may restore S-adenosylmethionine concentrations, thereby increasing the availability of substrates involved in GSH synthesis and enhancing cellular protection against ROS and other reactive metabolites. Collectively, these findings suggest a potential metabolic pathway through which choline and betaine may contribute to the lower MDA concentrations observed in the present study, although GSH and related antioxidant enzyme activities were not directly evaluated.

The lower effectiveness of the CH-REC group in reducing liver MDA concentrations compared with BT-MATCH and CH-HIGH may be related to differences in the metabolic utilization of betaine and choline, as well as to differences in choline supplementation level. Unlike betaine, which can directly donate a methyl group in the remethylation of homocysteine, choline must first be oxidized to betaine before its methyl groups become available through this pathway (Kidd et al., 1997; Eklund et al., 2005). Importantly, dietary choline supply can influence hepatic betaine availability in broilers. Saarinen et al. (2001) demonstrated that increasing dietary choline supplementation increased hepatic betaine concentrations in broiler chicks, although dietary betaine was more effective than choline in increasing the hepatic betaine pool. This dose-dependent relationship may be particularly relevant under HS conditions, because hepatic choline metabolism appears to be remodeled during thermal stress. Hubbard et al. (2018), using integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses in heat-stressed broilers, reported coordinated changes in hepatic choline metabolism consistent with redirection of choline toward betaine formation and maintenance of the hepatic betaine pool. Accordingly, the greater and sustained choline supply provided by CH-HIGH may have increased substrate availability for choline oxidation and thereby promoted greater metabolic flux toward endogenous betaine formation compared with CH-REC. This substrate-driven mechanism could partly explain why CH-HIGH reduced liver MDA concentrations whereas CH-REC did not. Greater endogenous betaine availability may, in turn, contribute to cellular homeostasis under HS through its methyl-donor and osmolyte functions (Ratriyanto and Mosenthin, 2018).

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) play an essential protective role at the cellular level, mainly through their molecular chaperone activity. These proteins help prevent irreversible aggregation of denatured proteins and contribute to maintaining cellular homeostasis under HS (Ahmad et al., 2022). Among the HSP family members, HSP70 is considered an important component of the cellular response to HS and has been widely used as a molecular marker of this response because of its role in protecting cells against stress-induced protein damage (Song and King, 2015; Surai et al., 2019). In the present study, the favorable effects of dietary choline and/or betaine supplementation on intestinal histomorphology and tissue MDA concentrations under HS conditions were accompanied by treatment-related differences in hepatic HSP70 gene expression. Hepatic HSP70 gene expression was upregulated in all supplemented groups compared with CON, except CH-REC. At the matched supplementation levels, HSP70 expression was higher in BT-MATCH than in CH-REC. Increasing choline supplementation from CH-REC to CH-HIGH was also associated with higher HSP70 expression, whereas increasing betaine supplementation from BT-MATCH to BT-HIGH produced no additional response. The highest HSP70 expression was observed in the BTCH-MIX group. However, the HSP70 response did not fully parallel the responses observed for growth performance, intestinal histomorphology, and tissue MDA concentrations across the dietary treatments, indicating that hepatic HSP70 expression and these response parameters did not follow a uniform pattern. This different response pattern may reflect the dynamic nature of HSP70 regulation during the cellular response to HS rather than a direct correspondence between the magnitude of HSP70 expression and the responses observed in other physiological parameters. In broilers, hepatic HSP70 mRNA and protein expression has been shown to change in a time-dependent manner during acute heat exposure (Gabriel et al., 1996), while greater hepatic HSP70 concentrations have also been associated with increased heat resistance following repeated thermal exposure (Givisiez et al., 1999). Thus, the present HSP70 findings support a treatment-related modulation of the cellular stress response, although the magnitude of HSP70 expression alone does not necessarily indicate the relative degree of protection provided by the different supplementation strategies. HSP70 expression has been linked to improved tolerance to HS through its cytoprotective activity and role in maintaining protein homeostasis during heat exposure (Ahmad et al., 2022). Betaine, through its multiple cellular protective functions, may contribute to maintaining protein integrity during HS by reducing heat-induced protein denaturation and facilitating the refolding of damaged proteins through HSP-mediated mechanisms. In addition, betaine has been reported to stabilize both chaperone structure and the refolded protein products by promoting protein dissociation during the refolding process (Diamant et al., 2001; Dangi et al., 2016). A study by Lian et al. (2020) reported that the protective effects of α-lipoic acid and arginine on gut integrity under HS conditions may be associated with increased HSP70 gene expression. Although this finding concerns intestinal rather than hepatic responses, it further illustrates the potential involvement of HSP70 in cellular adaptation to HS. This protein is thought to support epithelial barrier function by downregulating conventional protein kinase C activity, which consequently reduces the phosphorylation of myosin light chain proteins in the actin cytoskeleton.

Overall, the findings indicate that betaine can partially substitute for supplemental choline in corn–soybean meal-based diets of broilers exposed to cyclic HS. At matched supplementation levels, betaine supported growth performance comparable to choline while producing more favorable responses in specific jejunal histomorphological traits and liver MDA concentration. Increasing betaine supplementation to 1000 mg/kg provided no additional benefit over BT-MATCH, whereas higher choline supplementation produced some physiological responses not observed with CH-REC. Combined betaine and choline supplementation did not provide a clear overall advantage over the effective single-source strategies, despite producing the highest hepatic HSP70 expression. Thus, the responses depended on both methyl-donor source and supplementation level, and higher or combined supplementation was not consistently advantageous. These findings support appropriately supplemented betaine as a practical alternative to part of supplemental choline under cyclic HS conditions. Further studies across different dietary conditions, broiler genotypes, and HS models are needed to define optimal substitution levels and confirm their practical applicability.
