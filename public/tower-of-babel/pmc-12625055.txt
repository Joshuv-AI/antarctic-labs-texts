Biomechanical effects of aligner trimline design and intrusion protocol on mandibular anterior teeth: a finite element study
Authors: Rugan Su, Jun Sun, Song Li, Wei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study employed finite element analysis to evaluate the biomechanical effects of different aligner trimline designs (scalloped vs. high trimline) and intrusion methods (sequential vs. simultaneous) during mandibular anterior teeth intrusion.MethodsA finite element analysis model was developed to evaluate stress distribution and displacement patterns. Two trimline designs (scalloped and high trimline) and two intrusion protocols (sequential and simultaneous) were compared. Parameters measured included initial tooth displacement, root stress, periodontal ligament (PDL) stress, and stress concentration patterns.ResultsFinite element analysis demonstrated that sequential intrusion with scalloped trimline aligners produced greater initial displacement (0.062–0.108 mm in central incisors) and a more favorable stress distribution compared to simultaneous intrusion. The scalloped design showed superior performance by generating 13–40% lower root stress (0.053–0.060 MPa) compared to high trimline aligners. Stress concentration patterns differed notably between intrusion methods. Sequential intrusion localized stress at the lingual cervical third of the central incisors, whereas simultaneous intrusion shifted peak stress to the canines and increased PDL stress three-fold (0.005–0.006 MPa). Posterior anchorage control was also better preserved with sequential intrusion protocols.ConclusionThis study confirms that sequential intrusion using scalloped trimline aligners is biomechanically optimal for mandibular anterior intrusion. This method provides greater displacement efficiency and reduced stress on dental structures. The findings offer clinically relevant evidence supporting the preference for scalloped trimline designs over high trimlines. Moreover, sequential protocols are recommended over simultaneous intrusion in managing deep overbites with clear aligners. These results enable clinicians to make informed decisions regarding intrusion mechanics, potentially enhancing treatment outcomes and minimizing adverse effects.

Introduction

Clear aligners are digitally planned medical devices designed to correct malocclusions through controlled biomechanical force delivery. Treatment begins with intraoral scanning to obtain a digital dentition model, followed by virtual treatment setup using specialized software (e.g., ClinCheck®) that simulates staged tooth movements. Aligners are typically manufactured by thermoforming medical-grade polymer sheets over 3D-printed physical models; however, direct 3D printing with biocompatible resins has been increasingly adopted [1, 2]. Each aligner applies precisely calculated forces to achieve predetermined tooth displacement, often augmented by composite attachments to enhance force transmission and facilitate control over complex tooth movements [3].

Clear aligners have been shown to be highly effective for treating deep overbites, primarily by intruding the anterior teeth to open the bite [4]. Due to their full-coverage design, clear aligners apply forces closer to the center of resistance of the teeth, enabling nearly bodily movement [5, 6]. This characteristic provides clear aligners with a significant advantage in tooth intrusion [7, 8].

Clinical studies have reported intrusion efficiency of clear aligners ranging from 41.4% to 79.0% [9–11], achieving anterior tooth intrusion of 0.72–2.10 mm [3, 7]. In clinical practice, intrusion of mandibular anterior teeth is more common than that of maxillary anterior teeth, typically performed using either sequential or simultaneous intrusion methods [12]. When both canines and incisors require intrusion, sequential intrusion—intruding incisors first, followed by canines—demonstrates higher clinical efficiency.

The treatment efficacy of aligners relies on the integration of digital design, material properties, and biomechanical principles. Aligners feature various trimline designs, ranging from scalloped to straight edges, with certain designs extending beyond the gingival line and partially covering the gums. Previous research by Cowley et al. demonstrated that trimline design significantly influences treatment efficacy, with straight-edge designs associated with improved retention [13]. Additionally, extending the aligner’s edge improves force distribution around the cervical area [14].

The finite element method (FEM) is a computational engineering technique tailored to simulate individualized clinical conditions, with its predictions subject to validation through quantitative experimental data. This approach is extensively employed in biomechanical studies to assess initial displacement and stress distributions under applied loads. Linear elastic models, in particular, offer the advantage of computational efficiency while providing a physiologically plausible preliminary estimation of force distribution and biomechanical behavior [15]. In recent years, FEM has become an established methodology for modeling orthodontic tooth movement and analyzing associated biomechanical responses.

However, existing finite element studies on anterior tooth intrusion have primarily focused on the maxillary anterior teeth, while research specifically addressing mandibular anterior tooth intrusion remains limited. There is a notable lack of investigation into the intrusion patterns and the influence of aligner trimline design on intrusive movement in this context. In clinical practice, the treatment of most deep overbite cases requires coordinated intrusion of the mandibular anterior teeth to achieve bite opening. Intrusive movement of the lower anterior teeth represents a common and critical biomechanical objective in orthodontic treatment.

In this study, FEM simulations were conducted to assess the biomechanical effects of different aligner trimline designs and intrusion protocols on the mandibular anterior teeth. The analysis of the resulting biomechanical mechanisms aims to provide insights for optimizing clinical treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Model Reconstruction from Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT).

A three-dimensional (3D) anatomical model was reconstructed using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans acquired from a 25-year-old female patient exhibiting normal craniofacial morphology, physiologically positioned condyles, and a complete dentition. CBCT imaging was clinically indicated, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Stomatological Hospital, Capital Medical University (Approval No. CMUSH-IRB-KJ-PJ-2024-26). Written informed consent was obtained from the participant for both participation in the study and the use of her CBCT data.

The CBCT dataset, consisting of 600 axial slices at 0.300 mm intervals, was processed using Mimics 20.0 software (Materialise, Belgium) for preliminary segmentation and 3D reconstruction through the “Calculate 3D” function. Subsequent model refinement and surface optimization were performed using Geomagic Studio 2014 (Raindrop Geomagic, USA). To minimize the effects of lower incisor angulation on experimental outcomes, mandibular central incisors were adjusted to a 90° orientation during reconstruction. Final assembly of anatomical structures, including teeth, periodontal ligament (PDL), and alveolar bone, was completed with NX 1911 CAD software (Siemens, Germany), resulting in a computational model suitable for biomechanical analyses.

Development of the FEM

Model components were integrated using ANSYS Workbench 2019 (Ansys, Inc., Pennsylvania, USA) to construct a high-resolution FE model for simulations. Aligners were modeled with a uniform thickness of 0.38 mm, and the marginal and interdental regions featured smooth, rounded contours to avoid sharp edges. To create aligner models, mandibular central and lateral incisors were first intruded by 0.15 mm to their target positions. Tooth crown surfaces were then offset by 0.38 mm (matching aligner thickness), and Boolean operations were performed to finalize aligner models for mandibular anterior teeth intrusion simulations.

Two distinct aligner trimline designs were created to meet experimental requirements:Scalloped trimline, precisely following the gingival contour of the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to maximize biomechanical efficiency and minimize soft tissue irritation; andHigh trimline, extending 2 mm apically beyond the gingival margin to enhance anchorage control and force distribution during intrusion, as supported by previous biomechanical studies [16]. Boundary and contact conditions

The inferior boundary of the mandibular model was fully fixed to prevent displacement. Bonded contact conditions were defined between cancellous and cortical bone, the cortical bone-PDL interface, and between the PDL and tooth structure, allowing minimal frictionless sliding but preventing relative motion. Frictional contact with a coefficient of 0.2 was assigned for aligner-tooth interactions [17], and interproximal tooth contacts had a friction coefficient of 0.18 [18]. Potential contacts between maxillary and mandibular dentition were not included in this analysis.

Dental components were modeled as isotropic linear elastic materials with high elastic moduli, while PDL tissues were represented using hyperelastic material models to simulate their physiological deformation characteristics. Alveolar bone was approximated as a rigid structure for PDL stress analysis. Aligners were defined as isotropic and homogeneous materials (Table 1). Establishment of a coordinate system

An anatomical coordinate system was established by defining the occlusal plane using reference points at the incisal edges of the central incisors and the mesiobuccal cusp tips of the first molars. The X-axis (coronal plane) was oriented positively toward the mesial aspect, the Y-axis (sagittal plane) positively toward the lingual aspect, and the Z-axis (vertical plane) positively toward the gingival direction (Fig. 2).

Loading procedure

Sequential and simultaneous intrusion movements of mandibular anterior teeth (2–2 intrusion and 3–3 intrusion) were simulated. Clear aligners applied an intrusive force corresponding to a displacement of 0.15 mm along the long axis of the teeth. Reaction forces resulting from each intrusion method were calculated using ANSYS software after applying the preset displacement of 0.15 mm to the aligners [22]. Forces equal in magnitude to these reaction forces were then applied to the corresponding teeth to evaluate initial displacement, root stress, and PDL stress distribution.

In contrast, the 3–3 intrusion group exhibited greater initial displacement in posterior teeth compared to the sequential intrusion group (Table 3). This result suggests that simultaneous intrusion provides inferior posterior anchorage control. Displacement trends of mandible anterior teeth

The analysis of 3D displacement in mandibular anterior teeth indicated that the greatest displacement occurred along the Y-axis, suggesting pronounced lingual inclination during intrusion with clear aligners. During sequential intrusion, mandibular canines served as anchorage, partially counteracting vertical displacement and leading to relatively smaller vertical movement of anterior teeth. In contrast, simultaneous intrusion resulted in a smaller overall displacement magnitude, although intrusive forces applied to all three mandibular anterior teeth produced measurable initial displacement (Fig. 3). Additionally, aligners with scalloped trimlines consistently produced greater initial displacement compared to those with high trimlines under all experimental conditions.

Von-mises stress in roots of anterior teeth

Comparative analysis of root stress distribution in mandibular anterior teeth showed that trimline design had minimal impact on the location of maximum stress in mandibular central incisors. However, the intrusion method demonstrated marked differences: sequential intrusion concentrated maximum stress at the lingual cervical third of the root, whereas simultaneous intrusion distributed stress across both labial and lingual cervical halves. Neither trimline design nor intrusion method significantly altered stress distribution patterns in lateral incisors and canines. Nevertheless, characteristic patterns emerged: lateral incisors showed maximum stress at the labial cervical third, while canines exhibited stress concentration at the mesially biased lingual cervical third (Fig. 4).

Hydrostatic stress in PDLs

Under sequential intrusion conditions, peak stresses in the PDLs of mandibular anterior teeth were predominantly concentrated in the lingual PDL region of mandibular central incisors. In contrast, simultaneous intrusion demonstrated less distinct stress distribution patterns, although generally higher stress values were observed in the lingual PDL regions of all mandibular anterior teeth (Fig. 5). Notably, aligner trimline design exhibited no significant influence on stress distribution patterns within mandibular anterior tooth PDLs.

Analysis of PDL compressive stress during mandibular anterior intrusion (Fig. 5) revealed distinct biomechanical patterns. Sequential intrusion produced peak stress in central incisors (scalloped trimline: 0.00634 MPa; high trimline: 0.00793 MPa) with secondary stress in canines (0.00151–0.00251 MPa). In comparison, simultaneous intrusion significantly increased stress in canines (0.00529–0.006.00529.006 MPa), representing an approximate threefold increase. The trimline-associated stress variation (0.00159 MPa) observed in central incisors was statistically insignificant (p > 0.05) compared to protocol-dependent differences. Thus, the intrusion method was identified as the primary determinant of PDL stress magnitude in clear aligner therapy.

Discussion

This study provided a comprehensive biomechanical evaluation of different clear aligner trimline designs and their influence on mandibular anterior intrusion using 3D finite element analysis. This investigation systematically analyzed how distinct aligner configurations and intrusion strategies affected tooth displacement patterns and stress distribution within tooth roots and the PDL, thereby enhancing the current biomechanical understanding.

Intrusion protocols for mandibular anterior teeth

Intrusion of the mandibular anterior teeth is an important step for the treatment of deep overbite and Class II malocclusion [23]. During anterior teeth intrusion using conventional fixed appliances, intrusive forces are typically applied labial to the teeth’s center of resistance, often resulting in simultaneous labial inclination [24]. In contrast, clear aligners enclose labial, lingual, and incisal crown surfaces, enabling intrusive forces to act through or near the center of resistance. This theoretically facilitates true intrusion without undesirable tipping [25]. Moreover, clear aligners can simultaneously perform intrusion and retraction, especially in cases involving dental spacing [7]. During intrusive movement of mandibular anterior teeth using clear aligners, finite element analysis has indicated that uncontrolled forces frequently induce labiolingual tipping due to asymmetric stress distribution within the periodontal ligament. Introducing moderate interdental spacing creates biomechanically favourable conditions by enabling more uniform stress transmission along the dental roots, thereby facilitating true intrusion and reducing undesirable translational movements [26].

Clear aligner systems exhibit biomechanical limitations during mandibular anterior intrusion due to intrusive counterforces that may lead to aligner dislodgement, necessitating posterior anchorage reinforcement. Consistent with Jiang et al. [27], adverse movements including lingual inclination of incisors, mesial drift tendency, and canine extrusion were observed during sequential intrusion. These findings highlight the importance of optimized attachment designs to enhance anchorage control. Thus, strategic posterior attachments are recommended to counteract reaction forces and prevent unwanted tooth movements during intrusive clear aligner therapy.

The current findings demonstrated that during clear aligner therapy, sequential intrusion of mandibular anterior teeth resulted in greater initial displacement compared to simultaneous intrusion, indicating higher biomechanical efficiency. Given the technically demanding nature of simultaneous intrusion protocols, which require precise force control to avoid undesirable side effects, sequential intrusion is recommended when both canines and incisors require intrusion.

Biomechanical risks associated with intrusion of anterior teeth

During anterior tooth intrusion with clear aligners, inadequate control of intrusion direction or poor alveolar bone quality may result in unintended labial or lingual tooth movements, elevating the risk of bone fenestration and dehiscence [27]. Bone dehiscence is characterized by a V-shaped defect on the buccal or lingual aspects of alveolar bone, exposing the cervical third of the root. In contrast, bone fenestration refers to isolated alveolar defects that do not involve the alveolar ridge crest [28]. Since tooth movement with aligners is force-driven, a thorough understanding of the associated biomechanical mechanisms is essential for achieving controlled and safe clinical outcomes.

In the present study, the observed concentration of stress in the lingual cervical third to the midportion of the root of mandibular anterior teeth during intrusion is consistent with the findings reported by Fang et al. [29]. Their study similarly highlighted that intrusive movement of lower anterior teeth with clear aligners often results in unintended labiolingual tipping, potentially leading to alveolar bone resorption and root exposure. Furthermore, Fang et al. suggested that employing biomechanically optimized attachment designs, such as labial and lingual root-control ridges combined with lingual fossa excavation holes, can facilitate genuinely vertical intrusion, thereby enhancing mechanical control and reducing the risk of adverse outcomes.

However, our results contrast with the findings reported by Gupta et al. [30], who identified prominent stress concentrations in the apical region of maxillary incisors. This discrepancy highlights the influence of anatomical and methodological differences. Key distinctions include:

Therefore, clinicians should prioritize patient-specific biomechanical evaluation before treatment planning, particularly through detailed CBCT-based assessments of alveolar bone morphology and root positions. Utilizing optimized auxiliary features, such as precision attachments and customized force modules, can significantly improve directional control during intrusion. Ultimately, integrating finite element analysis simulations with clinical expertise may enhance the predictability of orthodontic outcomes while minimizing iatrogenic risks.

Influence of trimline design

The biomechanical performance of clear aligner systems is influenced by multiple factors, including material properties, aligner thickness, trimline design, and attachment configuration. Among these, trimline design plays a crucial role in controlling tooth movement. Specifically, a high trimline design has been shown to improve the predictability of tooth movements and may serve as an alternative to conventional attachments in certain clinical scenarios [16, 31]. For example, during maxillary expansion, high-trimline aligners effectively reduced molar buccal tipping [32]. These findings underline the importance of trimline selection in clear aligner therapy to optimize force delivery and minimize adverse effects.

Contrary to previous single-tooth studies, our 3D finite element analysis showed that high trimline aligners provided no significant benefit for mandibular anterior intrusion, while scalloped trimline designs generated greater initial displacement. This discrepancy with Tracersa et al.‘s in vitro findings may result from fundamental biomechanical differences associated with multi-tooth intrusion dynamics [33]. The extended gingival contact in high trimline designs introduces dynamic friction that counteracts intrusive forces, as supported by friction coefficient simulations in this study. These results, consistent with Henick and Blundell’s principles [34, 35], illustrate that trimline-gingival interactions significantly influence force transmission efficiency in multi-tooth movements, highlighting the need for optimized aligner designs in clinical intrusion protocols.

Limitations

This study employed finite element analysis to investigate the biomechanical behavior of various clear aligner designs during intrusion of mandibular anterior teeth. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the findings are constrained by the inherent simplifications of numerical simulations; thus, individual patient variations may produce significant differences in clinical responses, limiting the generalizability of the results. Second, the model assumed an ideal incisor position (IMPA = 90°) and did not account for proclined or retroclined tooth inclinations. Furthermore, the analysis was conducted under static conditions without simulating dynamic biomechanical changes occurring throughout treatment. Future studies should incorporate more diverse dental positions, simulate time-dependent mechanical responses, and validate these findings with large-scale clinical datasets.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the effectiveness of clear aligners for mandibular anterior intrusion depends on the treatment approach and appliance design. Sequential intrusion shows superior control and higher efficiency compared to simultaneous intrusion. The scalloped trimline design proves more advantageous than high trimline configurations, resulting in greater initial displacement and reduced root stress. These findings offer clinicians biomechanical evidence to optimize intrusion protocols in clear aligner therapy.
