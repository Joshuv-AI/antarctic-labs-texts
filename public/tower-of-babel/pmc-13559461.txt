Staple line coverage with suture fixation of polyglycolic acid patch can reduce postoperative air leakage
Authors: Yeon Soo Kim, Soorack Ryu
Published: 2026

We appreciate the interest and valuable comments by Wu et al. (1). Wu et al. stated that stapler technology and staple chamber design must be considered when interpreting the results of the study. They noted that the study lacked detailed information on cartridge models, staple configurations, and cartridge selection strategies. They also mentioned the possibility that air leakage decreased due to significant advancements in thoracoscopic stapling technology during the nearly 10-year study period.

As stated in the paper, only Medtronic products (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) were used in the study (2). All of the staples used were Tri-StapleTM purple staples (30, 45, and 60 mm). One change was made to the staple body used. Endo GIA manual bodies were used in the first half, and SigniaTM Powered bodies were used from August 2019 onwards (3). Therefore, it is unlikely that the type of staple used caused a difference in the results. However, differences in air leakage may have occurred as a result of using different staple bodies.

Group A underwent multiple suture fixation; Group B underwent single suture fixation; and Group C did not undergo suturing. There was no statistically significant difference in air leakage on the first day postoperatively depending on the type of body used. Therefore, it can be concluded that the likelihood of a difference in air leakage caused by the type of body used is low.

We compared surgeries performed after September 2019 that used both the Tri-Staple and the Signia Powered Bodies. Over a four-year period, 28 surgeries were performed in Group A and 12 in Group B. A statistically significant difference in air leakage on the first postoperative day was found between the two groups (P=0.024, Fisher’s exact test). Since the results are based on surgeries performed over the past four years using the same kind of staples and bodies, the differences in air leakage can be attributed to variations in surgical methods. Therefore, it can be concluded that multiple suture fixation significantly reduces air leakage compared to single suture fixation.

However, there are concerns about potential errors in the results due to significant imbalances in the distribution of pulmonary blebs among the study groups. The patients with multiple blebs used a statistically significantly higher number of staples compared to the single bleb in all three groups. On average, patients with multiple blebs used one additional staple than single bleb across all three groups. Furthermore, in Group C, air leakage occurred on the first postoperative day in 58% (34/58) of the multiple bleb group and 20% (3/15) of the single bleb group. Air leakage was statistically significantly higher in the multiple blebs group. However, Groups A and B showed no statistically significant difference in the first postoperative day air leakage based on bleb distribution. The authors conclude that reinforcing the staple line with sutures effectively reduces air leakage especially using multiple staples.

In Group C, which did not use sutures, the oldest surgical method in this study, air leakage lasting more than three days and more than five days occurred in 5.5% (4/73) and 2.7% (2/73), respectively. The time to chest tube removal was 2.4±1.07 days. These results are similar to those of two randomized trials, in which the patch coverage group showed air leakage lasting more than three and five days in 7.8% and 4.0% of cases, respectively. Chest tube removal times were 2.4±1.8 and 2.4±1.64 days (4,5). The results of Group C in this study are not inferior, and the results of Groups A and B can be considered relatively superior.

However, we acknowledge the limitations of this study. There may have been differences in surgical experience and postoperative management over the study’s approximately 10-year period. It is also possible that differences in surgical outcomes occurred due to changes in the type of staple bodies used during the study. In the authors’ study, fixing the polyglycolic acid patch with staple line using sutures reduced postoperative air leakage. This is believed to have led to improved reinforcement in areas considered highly susceptible to air leakage. Nevertheless, a well-planned prospective study is necessary to address the aforementioned concerns and uncertainties.
