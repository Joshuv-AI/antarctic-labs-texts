Rapid perfusion ultrasonography in the assessment of axillary venous catheter tip position: a prospective observational study
Authors: Niu Miao, Zhao Huiying, Qiu Xiaowen, Shen Peng, Shen Jiawei, Lv Jie, Zhou Gang, Zhu Fengxue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography in assessing the catheter tip position in axillary vein cannulation.MethodsThis prospective observational single-center study was conducted in a tertiary Grade A Hospital in China. Seventy adult patients who underwent successful right axillary vein catheterization were included. Patients were consecutively recruited based on eligibility for central venous catheterization via the axillary vein. Exclusion criteria included unsuccessful catheter placement, poor acoustic window for ultrasound, or known anatomical abnormalities. All enrolled participants completed the study. The primary outcome was the accuracy of saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography in determining catheter tip position, assessed through sensitivity, specificity, and agreement (kappa statistic) compared with chest radiography. The secondary outcome was the time required to complete each diagnostic method.ResultsThe sensitivity and specificity of saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography for determining the axillary vein catheter tip position were100% and 77.8%, respectively. The results were highly consistent with X-ray findings (Kappa = 0.859, P < 0.05). The mean operative time for ultrasonography was 5.6 min (95% CI: 5.3‒6.0), significantly shorter than that of X-ray (70.5 min; 95% CI: 66.3‒74.8).ConclusionsPhysiologic saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography is a highly sensitive and efficient method for assessing axillary vein catheter placement. It offers a faster alternative to X-ray examination, avoiding radiation exposure and contrast-related risks, making it suitable for widespread clinical application.Keywords: Ultrasonography, Axillary vein, Central venous cannulation, Critical care ultrasound

Introduction

Axillary venous catheterization is a critical procedure in the management of acutely ill patients, providing stable venous access for vasoactive drug infusion, hemodynamic monitoring, and intravenous nutrition [1, 2]. Accurate localization of the catheter tip is essential to ensure treatment efficacy and to prevent complications such as arrhythmias, infections, and thrombosis [3, 4]. While conventional X-rays offer accurate localization, they are time-consuming and involve radiation exposure, potentially delaying treatment and increasing patient risk [5]. As such, there is a growing clinical need for a rapid and accurate method to confirm catheter tip position without these drawbacks. The advent of bedside ultrasound offers new possibilities for solving this problem [6, 7]. However, further research and validation are required to establish the effectiveness of ultrasound-based methods in confirming catheter placement. Although conventional ultrasonography has shown better accuracy in some studies, its high cost and the possibility of allergic reactions triggered by contrast agents have limited its popularity in clinical settings [8]. Moreover, traditional studies of ultrasound judgment of catheter position have mostly focused on subclavian vein and internal jugular vein cannulation. With the growing adoption of axillary vein cannulation in intensive care units (ICUs), it is important to explore similar techniques that can be used for catheter tip verification in this context. Compared to subclavian and internal jugular vein catheterization, axillary vein catheterization involves greater variation in catheter placement depth due to the longer distance from the vein to the skin surface and differences in the operator’s needle insertion angle. Therefore, we need to identify a rapid and effective method to determine the position of the catheter tip during axillary vein catheterization.

In this study, we investigated the feasibility and clinical utility of saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography for assessing axillary venous catheter tip position, comparing its performance with that of conventional radiography. Our goal was to determine whether this approach could serve as a rapid, low-risk alternative for catheter verification after axillary venous catheterization.

Methods

Participants

In this study, we included 70 patients with acute or critical illnesses who underwent axillary venous cannulation in the Department of Critical Care Medicine at the People’s Hospital of Peking University between June 2023 and January 2025. Inclusion criteria were patients requiring central venous access via axillary vein cannulation. Exclusion criteria included superior vena cava syndrome, a recent history of cardiac surgery, existing medical devices in the superior vena cava, and pregnancy. All patients provided informed consent before the cannulation procedure, and the study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee.

The patients’ ages ranged from 29 to 90 years, with a mean age of 54.1 ± 15.2 years; heights ranged from 155 to 178 cm, with a mean of 166.2 ± 6.1 cm; and weights ranged from 50 to 90 kg, with a mean of 70.1 ± 9.2 kg; BMI ranged from 20.8 to 31.1 kg/m², with a mean of 25.3 ± 1.83 kg/m². In terms of sex ratio, 57.1% were male and 42.9% were female. The cannulation sites were located in the right axillary vein in 70patients (100%). In addition, the vertical distance from the skin to the axillary vein was 1.5–4.7 cm with a mean of 3.2 ± 0.8 cm.

Experimental design

In this study, we compared saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography with traditional bedside chest X-ray in evaluating axillary vein catheter tip position. First, perform an ultrasound-guided axillary vein catheterization procedure on the patient, using a standard 20 cm dual-lumen central venous catheter. After successful catheter placement, a linear ultrasound probe was used to confirm that the catheter had entered the axillary vein and to assess its trajectory. The ipsilateral internal jugular vein was also examined to rule out the occurrence of special circumstances, such as the catheter traveling in the opposite direction after entering the axillary vein or mistakenly entering the ipsilateral internal jugular vein. If an abnormal pathway or misplacement was identified, the catheter was repositioned and re-evaluated using a linear or phased array probe. If no misplacement was detected, a phased array probe was used to visualize the right atrium and right ventricle. If the catheter tip was seen in the right ventricle or right atrium, it was deemed too deep. If the central venous catheter tip was not visualized in the cardiac chambers, 10 mL of saline was rapidly injected through the main catheter lumen. Simultaneously, a phased array probe was applied to obtain an apical four-chamber view of the heart to observe whether high-echo turbulence could be detected in the right atrium within 2 s. If turbulence appeared within this timeframe, the catheter tip was considered to be correctly positioned in the superior vena cava (Figs. 1 and 2). A delayed or absent turbulence signal in the right side of the heart suggested catheter malposition. If the apical four-chamber view was unclear due to patient positioning or pulmonary obstruction, the subxiphoid four-chamber view was used to improve turbulence visualization in the right side of the heart. Concurrently, the radiology department was contacted to perform a bedside chest X-ray immediately after catheter placement to confirm the exact catheter tip position. The X-ray results served as the reference standard for comparison with the findings from saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0. Continuous variables with normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x ¯ ±s), while non-normally distributed data are expressed as median and interquartile range (M [P25, P75]). Categorical variables are presented as frequency and percentage (n [%]). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare operating time between the two methods, and the kappa statistic was used to assess agreement between the two methods for determining catheter tip position. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patient or public involvement was not sought for the design, conduct, or reporting of this study. The rationale for excluding patient or public involvement was that the research question was purely technical (e.g., diagnostic accuracy of a new imaging modality) and did not require direct input from patients or the public.

The study protocol was developed by a team of clinicians and researchers, without formal patient or public engagement. The authors acknowledge that this may limit the consideration of patient-centered outcomes, but the focus on objective diagnostic metrics was prioritized.

Results

A total of 70 patients who underwent successful right axillary vein cannulation were included. The catheter tip position was evaluated using both saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography and bedside chest radiography. Saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography identified the catheter tip in the superior vena cava (SVC) of the target vessel in 63 cases (90%) and in other locations of the non-target vessel in seven cases (10%). Chest radiography found the catheter tip in the SVC of the target in 61 cases (87.1%) and other locations of the non-target vessel in nine cases (12.9%). Good agreement was seen between the two methods in determining the central venous catheter tip position (kappa = 0.859, P < 0.05). Using bedside chest radiography as the gold standard, the sensitivity of saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography was100% (95% CI, 92.6%–100.0%), and specificity was 77.8% (95% CI, 40.2%–96.1%) in terms of time efficiency, saline infusion ultrasonography required 5.6 min (95% CI: 5.3‒6.0), significantly shorter than bedside chest radiography, which took 70.5 min (95% CI,66.3–74.8) (Fig. 4). This difference was significant (P < 0.01). These findings indicate that ultrasonography not only offers good accuracy in determining the sensitivity and specificity of the catheter tip position but also significantly outperforms conventional chest radiography in terms of operational efficiency.

Discussion

Accurate assessment of the central venous catheter tip position following venous cannulation is an important step in ensuring proper catheter function and avoiding complications [9]. Although traditional bedside chest radiography is the gold standard for assessing catheter position, it is complex and time-consuming and exposes patients to a certain dose of radiation [10]. Therefore, a rapid, effective, and safe method of assessment of catheter tip position has always been a clinical challenge, especially in acutely ill patients. With the widespread application of ultrasound technology in deep vein catheterization, many physicians have begun attempting ultrasound-guided axillary vein catheterization. Compared to ultrasound-guided subclavian vein catheterization, ultrasound-guided axillary vein catheterization carries a lower risk of complications such as pneumothorax. Even if an artery is accidentally punctured, compression hemostasis is more readily achievable. However, due to the relatively deep location of the axillary vein, significant individual variation in its course, and the frequent use of the in-plane approach for needle insertion, this technique demands higher operator proficiency. Consequently, the puncture success rate for ultrasound-guided axillary vein catheterization is relatively low; in this study, it was only 78.6%. Additionally, due to the longer distance between the target vessel and the skin surface for axillary vein access, and the varying needle insertion angles chosen by operators based on the specific vessel course, the depth of catheter placement can differ significantly. This increases the risk of incorrect catheter tip positioning. Therefore, it is necessary to develop an effective real-time method to determine the catheter tip position during axillary vein catheterization. Furthermore, suppose the catheter is incorrectly positioned on bedside chest radiography after routine placement. In that case, the skin protection film at the puncture point needs to be removed, sterilized, and repositioned, which increases the risk of infection and incurs additional costs. In this study, we explored the use of saline rapid infusion ultrasonography to directly determine the position of the catheter tip during axillary vein catheterization and used bedside chest radiography as a reference to validate the clinical value of this ultrasonographic technique by comparing the sensitivity, specificity, and operating time. The results showed strong agreement between bedside chest radiography and physiological saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography in terms of determining axillary vein catheter tip position (kappa = 0.859, P < 0.05). If chest radiography were used as the gold standard, the sensitivity of the saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography technique in determining the catheter tip position could be as high as 100% (95% CI 92.6%–100.0%), with a specificity of 77.8% (95% CI 40.2%–96.1%). The results of this study are consistent with those of previous studies that used ultrasonography to determine the location of internal jugular vein catheter tips [11, 12]. Compared to the average time taken for conventional X-ray examination, which was 70.5 min, saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography significantly reduced the time taken for catheter position determination, with an average time of only 5.6 min. This time advantage is important in clinical practice, especially in emergency and critical care, where the catheter position can be assessed more quickly, thereby accelerating therapeutic decisions and interventions. Moreover, ultrasonography is not dependent on radiation, which greatly reduces patient exposure, and is particularly suitable for pregnant women or patients who require repeated catheter position adjustments [13]. The use of saline as the perfusion medium in this study also eliminates the risk of allergic reactions associated with iodinated contrast media, enhancing procedural safety [14, 15].

However, ultrasound visualization techniques have limitations. First, the quality of the ultrasound image may be affected in some cases by the patient’s acoustic window and the operator’s experience. In some patients with obesity, subcutaneous emphysema, severe emphysema, or anatomical abnormalities, the ultrasound image may be blurred or fail to clearly and completely show all the cardiac chambers and other relevant structures, thus affecting the diagnostic accuracy [16, 17]. Second,[18, 19]. Nonetheless, saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography offers a reliable alternative for rapid, low-risk assessment of the axillary vein catheter tip location. Third, this study only assessed the catheter tip location following right axillary vein catheterization. Future research will further investigate ultrasound-based assessment of catheter tip location after left axillary vein catheterization.

Conclusions

In this study, the feasibility and utility of saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography as a catheter position assessment method were verified by comparing its sensitivity, specificity, and time efficiency to those of bedside chest radiography. Saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography not only provides efficient and accurate catheter position assessment but also reduces the risk of radiation exposure to patients, avoids the cumbersome step of re-adjusting the line due to catheter misplacement after axillary vein manipulation, avoids additional medical expenses, and reduces the risk of catheter-related infections. Thus, it represents a safe, cost-effective, and highly practical tool with broad application potential in clinical practice. Future studies should explore the applicability of saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography in different patient populations and clinical settings. Investigations comparing its effectiveness in different central venous access sites, evaluating long-term outcomes, and assessing inter-operator variability will further validate the utility of saline rapid perfusion ultrasonography.
