MicroRNA-661 Suppresses Proliferation and Migration of Pulmonary Artery Smooth Muscle Cells and Endothelial Cells by Targeting CCND2
Authors: Liyang Jiang, Aixiang Yang, Jun Liu, Jie Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a fatal cardiovascular disorder driven by pulmonary vascular remodeling, with high morbidity and mortality. Current therapies only alleviate symptoms and cannot reverse disease progression. Our previous study has demonstrated that microRNA-661 (miR-661) inhibits Human pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell (HPASMC) and Human pulmonary artery endothelial cell (HPAEC) proliferation, but its underlying molecular mechanism in PAH remains unclear. This study aimed to identify the target gene of miR-661 and elucidate its regulatory role in PAH pathogenesis, seeking novel therapeutic targets for this incurable disease.MethodsSerum samples from 12 PAH patients and 12 healthy volunteers were enrolled this research. Human pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (HPASMCs) and endothelial cells (HPAECs) were cultured under normoxic or hypoxic conditions. MiR-661 mimics/inhibitors and CCND2 plasmids were transfected into cells. RNA-seq screened differentially expressed genes, and dual-luciferase reporter assay verified the target binding of miR-661. RT-qPCR and Western blot detected gene/protein expression, while MTT, EdU, Transwell and flow cytometry assessed cell proliferation, migration and cycle progression.ResultsWe perform RNA-seq and luciferase assay and found that CCND2 is a direct miR-661 target that is repressed via 3’UTR binding. miR-661 is lower expressed while the expression of CCND2 is higher in PAH patient serum and hypoxic HPASMCs/HPAECs, showing a significant negative correlation. CCND2 overexpression promoted pulmonary artery cell cycle progression, proliferation and migration under hypoxia, while miR-661 overexpression reversed these effects. In addition, we found that Stattic, a specific inhibitor of STAT3 (an upstream regulatory protein of CCND2), could inhibit HPASMC cell proliferation and migration and this effect is enhanced by miR-661.DiscussionThis study clarifies that miR-661 suppresses HPASMC and HPAEC proliferation or migration by targeting CCND2, and the miR-661/CCND2 axis is a key regulatory pathway in PAH-related vascular remodeling. Hypoxia-induced miR-661 downregulation leads to CCND2 accumulation, driving abnormal pulmonary artery cell biological behaviors. miR-661/CCND2 axis regulates PAH progression, providing a potential therapeutic target.Keywords: pulmonary arterial hypertension, microRNA, CCND2, RNA-seq

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a dangerous disease, with a high disability rate and fatality rate. In addition, the incidence of PAH is currently on the rise. In the United States, 10.6 per 1 million adults are affected by PAH.1 The symptoms of PAH are nonspecific, and usually manifest as post-activity shortness of breath, fatigue, chest pain, chest tightness and palpitations, with lower limb edema, abdominal distension, poor appetite, diarrhea and liver pain.2

If left untreated, PAH can progress to right heart failure and can be fatal; even after treatment, 10–15% of PAH patients still die within 1 year.3,4 At present, the diagnostic guidelines for PAH patients are based on right heart catheterization at sea level and at rest, with pulmonary arterial pressure >20 mmHg, a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure of 15 mmHg or lower, and pulmonary vascular resistance of >2WU.5

At present, the treatment strategies for PAH are divided into the following: i) Relieving the symptoms and improving quality of life, eg, oxygen therapy, low-salt diet, diuretics, etc.; ii) targeting the underlying causes of PAH, as identifying and removing the underlying causes can effectively control the development of PAH; iii) nitric oxide inhalation therapy, which can dilate pulmonary blood vessels and reduce pulmonary vascular resistance; iv) drug therapy, drugs can be used to help dilate pulmonary arteries and reduce cardiac burden. Currently, drugs approved for use in PAH include drugs that enhance the nitric oxide-cyclic guanosine monophosphate biological pathway, eg, Adempas and Sildenafil, drugs that increase the effect of prostacyclin on receptors, eg, Prostacyclin and Epoprostenol, and drugs that antagonize the endothelin pathway, eg, Alisentan and Bosentan.6–8 Pulmonary vascular reconstruction surgery may also be considered for patients with severe pulmonary artery stenosis and poor expected cardiac function.9 However, these treatment strategies are all based on pathways related to endothelial dilation and vasoconstriction, and so far, no drugs have yet been proven to reverse the course of pulmonary hypertension; therefore, PAH is still considered an incurable disease and the development of targeted therapies is particularly important.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs/miRs) are a class of noncoding single-stranded RNA molecules with a length of ~22 nucleotides encoded by endogenous genes.10,11 miRNAs guide the RNA-induced silencing complex and, via specific base pairing with target mRNA, can lead to the degradation or translational inhibition of thetarget mRNA. miRNAs play an important role in gene regulation at the post-transcriptional and translational levels, thereby affecting some processes, such as cell proliferation, differentiation and migration.12–14 In recent years, miRNAs have gradually attracted widespread attention in the medical community due to their small molecular weight and extensive effects.15–17 miRNAs are closely related to various cardiovascular diseases, and more than a dozen miRNAs have been reported to be dysregulated in PAH samples from human patients and animal models. In our previous research (under review), we revealed that miR-661 was lowly expressed in PAH patients, and that it can inhibit the proliferation and migration of human pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (HPASMCs) under hypoxic conditions. Despite these findings, the specific molecular mechanism of miR-661 in PAH is poorly understood.

In this present study, we performed RNA-seq and identified cyclin D2 (CCND2) as a substrate of miR-661. Notably, miR-661 was found to repress CCND2 expression, with a significant negative correlation observed between miR-661 levels and CCND2 mRNA levels in PAH patients. In addition, CCND2-induced HPASMC proliferation and migration could be reversed by miR-661. We also identified that Stattic could facilitate miR-661-induced inhibition of HPASMC proliferation and migration. In this study, we revealed the mechanism of miR-661 and CCND2 in HPASMCs, and the expression levels of miR-661 and CCND2 in PAH patients, which may provide new targets for the diagnosis and treatment of PAH.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Samples

Serum samples from 12 PAH patients and 12 healthy volunteers were collected and stored in a refrigerator at −80°C. We adopted a 1:1 age- and sex-matched case-control study design. Patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) were individually matched to healthy control subjects based on age (± 2 years) and sex to minimize potential confounding effects. Enrolled patients were diagnosed with pulmonary arterial hypertension. Inclusion criteria: 1. Age>18 years old; 2. The subjects (or their legal representatives) have signed the informed consent form; 3. Diagnostic guidelines for pulmonary arterial hypertension in PAH patients: Right heart catheterization examination at sea level and resting state shows pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) ≥ 25 mmHg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 15mm Hg, and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) >3 WU. Exclusion criteria: 1. Under 18 years old; 2. Pulmonary hypertension secondary to left heart disease, chronic respiratory disease, hypoxia, chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH), or other known causes of pulmonary hypertension; 3. Pregnant and lactating women. This study is in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration, and all participants have signed informed consent forms, which have been approved by the Ethics Committee of Suzhou Hospital Affiliated to Nanjing Medical University (KL901382). Cell Culture

The human embryonic Kidney 293T cells (HEK293T), human pulmonary artery smooth muscle Cells (HPASMCs) and human pulmonary artery endothelial cells (HPAECs) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Cells were cultured in DMEM (Hyclone, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone, USA) at 37°C under normoxia (21% O2) or hypoxia (1% O2) in incubators maintaining a constant environment (5% CO2 balanced with N2) for 0–72 hours to subsequent functional and molecular assays.

Plasmids and Antibodies

The CCND2 plasmid was cloned from HEK293T cells (ATCC). The CCND2-Luc plasmid was synthesized by the Shanghai GenePharma company. Antibodies against PARP (cat. no. 13371-1-AP), GAPDH (cat. no. 10494-1-AP) and CCND2 (cat. no. 67048-1-Ig) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China) Antibodies against STAT3 (cat. no. 12640) and phosphorylated (p)-STAT3 (Tyr705) (cat. no. 9145S) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology, Inc. (Danvers, MA, USA). PX478 was purchased from Selleck (S7612).

Chemicals

Lipofectamine® 2000 was obtained from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Stattic was obtained from Selleck (Houston, Texas, USA).

Transfection and Hypoxic Treatment

The miR-negative control (miR-NC), and miR-661 mimics and inhibitor were purchased from Shanghai GenePharma company (Shanghai, China), and the sequences were as follows: The miRNA NC of mimics: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′. The mimics for miR-661: 5′-CUGCCUGGGUCUCUGGCCU-3′. The miRNA NC of inhibitor: 5′-CAGUACUUUUGUGUAGUACAA-3′. The inhibitor for miR-661: 5′-AGGCCAGAGACCCAGGCAG-3′. Cells were plated in 6-well plates and grown to a cell density of 50%, after which, RNA was transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 2000, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In addition, HPASMCs were exposed to hypoxia (1% O2 and 5% CO2) at 37°C for the hypoxia experiments.

3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-Yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium (MTT) Assay

HPASMCs were transfected with miR-661 mimics or CCND2 for 0, 24, 48 and 72 h, then seeded into 96-well plates (Corning, Acton, MA, USA) at a density of 5000 cells/well. To assess cell viability, MTT was added and incubated for 4 h. Following removal of the culture medium, 150 μL dimethyl sulfoxide was added to each well, and the absorbance was measured at 490 nm.

EdU Assay

HPASMCs were transfected with miR-661 mimics or CCND2 for 48 h, then seeded in 96-well plates (Corning) at a density of 5000 cells/well. The cells were incubated with 50 μM EdU for 2 h, then stained with Apollo and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), before being analyzed by fluorescence microscopy. After staining, five random non-overlapping fields were selected and counted by two independent researchers in a double-blind manner. The percentage of EdU-positive cells is calculated as the ratio of the number of EdU-positive cells (pink cells) to the total number of DAPI-stained cells (blue cells).

RNA Extraction and Reverse Transcription-Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HPASMCs using Trizol (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). RNA (1 μg) was reverse transcribed into complementary deoxyribose nucleic acid (cDNA) using an EasyScript® First-strand cDNA Synthesis kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The following primers were used for qPCR: miR-661, forward 5′-GTGCCTGGGTCTCTGGCCT-3′, reverse 5′-CGTCATGATGTTGCGTCACC-3’; U6, forward 5′-CTCGCTTCGGCAGCACA-3′, reverse 5′-AACGCTTCACGAATTTGCGT-3′; CCND2, forward 5′-CCCTACATGCGCAGAATGGT-3′, reverse 5′- GACCTCTTCTTCGCACTTCTGTTC-3′. qPCR was performed using Trans Start Top Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen, Beijing, China) and the Step One Plus Real-time PCR System.

RNA-Seq

HPASMCs were transfected with miR-661 NC or mimics with three independent biological replicates, and total RNA was isolated from the cells using Trizol (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) before being sent to Biotree Biotechnology Company (Shanghai, China) for RNA-seq analysis. Library construction and sequencing were conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads; raw data were processed using Biotree’s in-house quality control pipeline to remove low-quality reads (Q < 20) and adapter sequences, with the final clean data yield ≥ 6 Gb per sample that meets Biotree’s recommended read depth for mammalian transcriptome analysis. For bioinformatics analysis, clean reads were aligned to the human hg38 reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.2.1), and gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) using String Tie (v2.1.7). Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2, and genes with |log2 fold change| ≥ 1.0 and FDR < 0.05 (adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method) were defined as significantly differentially expressed genes. Batch effects were minimized by processing all samples in a single batch during library preparation and sequencing; principal component analysis (PCA) was further applied for data normalization, and no obvious batch clustering was observed, confirming the absence of batch effects. Additionally, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was carried out using cluster Profiler (v4.8.1), with adjusted P < 0.05 set as the significance threshold.

Luciferase Activity Assay

The binding site for miR-661 in the CCND2 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR) was inserted into the pGL6-TA-CP vector. Cells were co-transfected with the luciferase reporter CCND2 as well as miR-661 mimics; NC mimics were used as a control. A total of 48 h post-transfection, the cells were collected and the Dual-Glo system (Promega) was used to examine the luciferase activity.

Western Blot

Transfected cells were lysed with cell lysis buffer and centrifuge at 14,800 rpm, 4° C for 20 minutes. Determination of protein concentration using BCA colorimetric method (Thermo Scientific, USA). The extracted protein was separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel, and then transferred to the polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). After incubating the primary and secondary antibodies, protein blotting analysis was performed using ECL developer (Biological Industries, Israel).

Transwell Assay

HPASMCs at a density of 1×104 were placed in the upper chamber of 200 µL serum-free medium, and complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (500 μ L) Join the lower chamber. After incubation for 24 hours, migratory or invasive cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Observe and count cell numbers in five random fields under a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Five random non-overlapping fields were selected and counted by two independent researchers in a double-blind manner; automated image quantification software (ImageJ) was used for quantitative analysis to reduce human error.

Wound Healing Assay

HPASMCs were transfected with miR-661 mimics and/or CCND2 plasmids, inoculated into a 6-well culture plate and allowed to grow to 95% confluence. Subsequently, a sterile pipette tip was used to introduce similar sized wounds into the cell monolayers. The injured cell monolayers were washed with PBS three times and then cultured in a hypoxic environment in DMEM (Hyclone). Images of wound closure were captured after 48 h.

Flow Cytometric Assay

HPASMCs were transfected with miR-661 mimics and CCND2 plasmids in the presence or absence of hypoxia. Cells were then collected using trypsin and washed with PBS, after which, they were subjected to a cell cycle assay, using a cell cycle assay kit (cat. no. C1052; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistics

The Statistical Product and Service Solutions 22.0 program (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to statistically compare the data. Prior to formal statistical analysis, the Shapiro–Wilk Test was used to verify the normality of quantitative data, and the Levene’s Test was applied to assess variance homogeneity. Use Student’s t-test to analyze data that conforms to normal distribution and homogeneous variance criteria. If the data does not follow a normal distribution or has uneven variance, the Mann Whitney U-test is used. Correlation was made using Spearman test. In this study, each experiment was repeated three times (three independent biological replicates, each with three technical replicates) and the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. *P<0.05, **P<0.01, ***P<0.001.

Results

CCND2 is a Target of miR-661

Our previous research found that miR-661 was associated with the development of pulmonary arterial hypertension,18 however, the specific substrate remained elusive. To determine the specific substrate of miR-661, we performed an RNA-seq assay after transfecting HPASMCs with miR-661 or its control. As shown in Figure 1A, a heatmap of differentially expressed genes demonstrated that CCND2 was significantly downregulated by miR-661. CCND2 is a member of the cyclin family. Together with cyclin D1, cyclin D3 and cyclin E, it acts as a key cell cycle regulatory protein.19,20 Compared with CCND1 and CCND3, CCND2 exhibited the most pronounced changes in response to miR-661 (Table 2). Consistently, their RNA levels were negatively correlated in serum from PAH patients (Figure 1B). Moreover, according to the results of KEGG pathway (Figure 1C), we observed that post-transfection of miR-661 mimics into HPASMCs, the cell cycle pathway was significantly regulated, further indicating that CCND2 may be a target gene of miR-661.

Hypoxia Decreases miR-661 While Increasing CCND2 Expression in HPASMCs and HPAECs

To further explore the relationship between miR-661 and CCND2, we detected the expression levels of miR-661 and CCND2 in the serum of patients with PAH and healthy volunteers. As shown in Figure 2A, PAH patient serum contained lower miR-661 expression compared with that of the healthy volunteers, while an opposite expression trend was detected regarding CCND2; the results of statistical analysis showed the negative correlation between them. Abnormal proliferation of PASMCs in a hypoxic environment is associated with the pathogenesis of PAH. To validate these findings, HPASMCs and HPAECs were cultured under hypoxic conditions for different periods of time, followed by RT-qPCR and Western blotting (WB). We observed that miR-661 was downregulated in a time-dependent manner under hypoxic environment, while CCND2 was upregulated (Figure 2B–D). Therefore, these results confirmed a negative correlation between miR-661 and CCND2, and indicated that CCND2 may be a potential target of miR-661.

miR-661 Reduces CCND2 Expression

We predicted the target gene of miR-661 through the online database of TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org) and found that CCND2 may be a potential target of miR-661, with a binding structure free energy of −35.610001 kCal/mol, as predicted by miRanda v3.3a. After identifying the potential binding site between miR-661 and CCND2 (Figure 3A), we validated it through dual luciferase reporter gene detection experiments. The results showed that in HPASMCs or HPAECs, upregulation of miR-661 reduced the luciferase activity of the CCND2 3’UTR reporter plasmid (Figure 3B), while miR-661 binding site mutation had no significant effect on luciferase activity (Figure 3C and D). Moreover, the expression of CCND2 was reduced by miR-661 in HPASMCs and HPAECs under hypoxic condition (Figure 3E and F). Conversely, the inhibition of miR-661 promoted the expression of CCND2 (Figure 3E and F). Therefore, miR-661 binds to the 3’UTR of CCND2, which may lead to the degradation or translational inhibition of CCND2 mRNA, ultimately resulting in the downregulation of CCND2 expression.

miR-661 Attenuates the Effects of CCND2 on the Cell Cycle Progression, Proliferation and Migration of HPASMCs and HPAECs

To further elucidate the function of miR-661 and CCND2 in PAH, HPASMCs and HPAECs were transfected with miR-661 mimics, inhibitor or CCND2 plasmids, followed by culturing under hypoxia for 48 h. The effect of CCND2 on the cell cycle progression was analyzed by flow cytometry. As shown in Figure 4A, CCND2 promoted the cell cycle progression of HPASMCs, whereas miR-661 could abolish the effect of CCND2 on the cell cycle, and inhibition of miR-661 promoted cell cycle progression (Figure 5A), indicating miR-661 exerts an opposite regulatory effect on the cell cycle. Furthermore, MTT assay (Figures 4B and 5B), EdU staining assay (Figures 4C and 5C) and Transwell assay (Figures 4D and 5D) further demonstrated that CCND2 promoted HPASMC and HPAEC proliferation and migration, and these processes could be reversed by miR-661. Therefore, miR-661 inhibits HPASMC and HPAEC proliferation and migration by targeting CCND2.

Stattic Promotes miR-661-Mediated Inhibition of HPASMC Proliferation and Migration

Studies have reported that STAT3 functions as an upstream regulator of CCND2 and promotes its transcription.21 We speculated whether overexpression of miR-661 and inhibition of STAT3 could more significantly inhibit CCND2, and thus inhibit HPASMC proliferation under hypoxic conditions. To test our hypothesis, HPASMCs or HPAECs were transfected with miR-661 or treated with Stattic, a specific inhibitor of STAT3, followed by WB. As shown in Figure 6A, miR-661-induced inhibition of CCND2 was increased by Stattic. To further evaluate the role of the miR-661/CCND2 axis in cell apoptosis, the protein expression levels of cleaved-PARP, a representative hallmark of cell apoptosis, were measured. As shown in Figure 6A, Stattic induced cell apoptosis and this effect was further enhanced by miR-661. Moreover, Stattic facilitated miR-661-induced inhibition of HPASMC proliferation and migration (Figure 6B–F). Overall, these data suggested that Stattic could promote the effects of miR-661 on pulmonary artery cell proliferation and migration.

Discussion

PAH is a life-threatening syndrome associated with high mortality and significant economic burden.22–24 Our previous studies have suggested that miR-661 inhibits the proliferation and migration of HPASMCs under hypoxia; however, the mechanism remains elusive. The present study found that miR-661 inhibits the expression of CCND2, and indicated that the miR-661/CCND2 axis could be an ideal target for PAH treatment (Figure 7).

The schematic diagram of this study. Under the hypoxic condition, the level of miR-661 is decreased, thereby inducing the expression of CCND2. The accumulation of CCND2 facilitates cell mitosis process and finally induces PAH disease.

In recent years, microRNA (miRNA) have emerged as promising therapeutic targets for PAH due to their capacity to regulate multiple pathological pathways simultaneously.25 Accumulating evidence indicates that several key miRNAs, including miR-146a, miR-663, miR-191, and miR-371b-5p, participate in the progression of PAH by modulating endothelial dysfunction, abnormal proliferation of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells, vascular remodeling, and right ventricular fibrosis via TGF-β/Smad, BMPR2, and PI3K/AKT signaling axes.26–29 With the continuous advancement of multi-omics integration and AI modeling, miRNA research is gradually shifting from static profiling to dynamic simulation and mechanistic elucidation, these fundamental research findings have started to translate into clinical applications. For instance, circulating miRNAs, such as reduced plasma miR-20430 and elevated miR-21 expression31 have been closely associated with PAH clinical severity.

As a primate-specific miRNA, the sequence of miR-661 is highly conserved in humans. Multiple studies have demonstrated that miR-661 overexpression is implicated in the pathogenesis of various human diseases. It has been reported that miR-661 can induce oxidative stress, thereby promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition in non-metastatic DLD1 cells, which is associated with poor prognosis in Phase II Cholangiocarcinoma patients.14 On the other hand, miR-661 is upregulated in pancreatic duct adenocarcinoma, where it promotes cell proliferation.32 By contrast, researchers have demonstrated that miR-661 inhibits the motility, invasiveness, anchor-independent growth and tumorigenicity of invasive breast cancer cells by targeting MTA1.33

In our previous study, miR-661 could inhibit the proliferation of HPASMCs exposed to hypoxia, and in PAH patients, the expression of miR-661 was downregulated. These results suggested that miR-661 may be closely related to PAH. The effects of miR-661 on the pathogenesis of PAH has remained largely unknown. From the RNA-seqresults, we found that miR-661 is closely associated with cell cycle pathways, and consistent with this finding, CCND2 was shown to be significantly downregulated by miR-661.

The cell cycle can be divided into the intercellular phase (cell growth and DNA replication) and the mitotic phase (cell division). The progression of the cell cycle is controlled by different cyclins and corresponding cyclin-dependent kinases.34 Among them, CCND plays an important role in the G1/S phase of cells. Cyclin D has three main members, CCND1, CCND2 and CCND3; these three proteins have a high degree of homology, with 50–60% similarity in the coding region; however, their functions are differ significantly.35 Notably, the expression of CCND2 is regulated by some miRNAs. The long noncoding RNA SNHG1 interactions with miR-154-5p, reducing its ability to repress CCND2 expression and promoting colorectal cancer cell growth.36 Furthermore, overexpression of miR-2682-3p promotes osteosarcoma cell apoptosis by targeting CCND2, MMP8 and Myd88.37 CCND2 is closely related to the progression of PAH. Previous studies have shown that miR-29b suppresses cellular proliferation and promotes apoptosis of PASMCs through the inhibition of Mcl-1 and CCND2.38 In this study, we demonstrated that miR-661targets CCND2 3’UTR to inhibit its expression and regulate its effects on the proliferation of HPASMCs and HPAECs. miR-661 could downregulate the expression of CCND2, and this downregulation could be enhanced by STAT3 inhibition via Stattic.

In addition, our previous study found that miR-661 inhibited AKT/mTOR signaling by inhibiting TRIM29. AKT phosphorylates CCND2 degradation-related molecules (such as GSK-3β) and inhibits the phosphorylation of CCND2 by GSK-3β, thereby reducing the degradation rate of CCND2 by the ubiquitin-proteasome system and prolonging its protein half-life. Therefore, beyond directly targeting CCND2 to affect pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells, miR-661 may also indirectly inhibit cell proliferation and migration by regulating the AKT/mTOR/GSK-3β pathway through TRIM29.

Sotatercept is a fusion protein that can rebalance the bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 2 (BMPR II) and activator receptor II A (ActR II A) signaling pathways. Phase III clinical trials have shown that Sotatercept can reverse characteristic vascular remodeling in PAH.39 The development of drugs targeting PAH metabolic reprogramming is currently a research hotspot. Sodium dichloroacetate is a pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) activator that can reduce PDH phosphorylation by inhibiting pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase, activate PDH, and increase the ratio of glucose oxidation to glycolysis. It has shown inhibitory effects on cell proliferation, promotes cell apoptosis, enhances vasodilation, and reverses pulmonary vascular remodeling in various pH models.40 In the future, the potential function of miR-661 in regulating BMPR II/ActR II A and other signal balance or metabolic reprogramming can be further explored, and the interactive regulatory mechanism of miR-661 in pulmonary arterial hypertension can be further explored, providing theoretical and experimental basis for the development of PAH combination therapy strategies based on miR-661.

In the present study, we employed Stattic to inhibit STAT3, the upstream signaling molecule of CCND2, thereby reducing CCND2 expression and further potentiating the miR-661-mediated suppression of pulmonary artery cell proliferation and migration. However, given that Stattic itself exhibits pro-apoptotic effects, additional experimental and preclinical investigations are required to validate the safety profile and clinical translational potential of this inhibitor for the treatment of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH).

Our study has several limitations. First, all functional experiments in the present study were conducted in vitro on isolated HPASMCs and HPAECs; while we analyzed clinical serum samples from PAH patients and healthy volunteers to verify the expression patterns of miR-661 and CCND2, these analyses were restricted to the detection of molecular expression levels only without functional validation in human tissues or in vivo pathological models. Thus, additional well-constructed in vivo experiments are urgently required—including classic PAH animal models (eg, monocrotaline-induced, hypoxia-induced PAH models) and further validation in human PAH vascular tissue samples—to explore the regulatory mechanisms of the miR-661/CCND2 axis in the context of intact pulmonary vascular remodeling, and to confirm the functional relevance of our in vitro findings to the actual pathological process of PAH in humans and animals. Second, it is necessary to investigate the impact of miR-661 combined with commonly used PAH drugs in clinical practice on the progression of PAH, in order to achieve a doubling effect of combined treatment efficacy. Third, the exact role of the miR-661/CCND2 axis in PAH needs to be further validated in clinical samples.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides a novel theoretical basis for the translational potential of the miR-661/CCND2 axis in PAH diagnosis and treatment, while several critical issues need to be addressed for its clinical translation. In terms of delivery feasibility, the in vivo delivery of miR-661 remains a major challenge for its clinical translation. The main potential delivery strategies include non-viral delivery systems (eg, lipid nanoparticles, exosome-mediated delivery) and viral vectors are the main potential strategies, but subsequent studies need to optimize the delivery system to improve the targeting efficiency of miR-661 to pulmonary artery cells, enhance its stability in the circulatory system, and reduce systemic side effects. Regarding off-target effects, both miR-661 and its synergistic agent Stattic have potential off-target risks: miR-661 may non-specifically regulate other target genes beyond CCND2 in different tissues, and Stattic, as a pan-STAT3 inhibitor, may inhibit STAT3 signaling in normal tissues and cause unintended biological effects. Thus, the development of cell-type-specific targeting strategies (eg, pulmonary vascular cell-specific ligand-modified delivery systems, conditional STAT3 inhibitors) is essential to mitigate off-target risks and improve the therapeutic specificity. The miR-661/CCND2 axis exhibits dual potential in PAH: as a disease mediator, it is a key regulatory pathway driving hypoxia-induced pulmonary vascular remodeling, and its targeted intervention can reverse the abnormal proliferation and migration of pulmonary artery cells; as a diagnostic/prognostic biomarker, the significant negative correlation of miR-661 and CCND2 expression in PAH patient serum provides a potential non-invasive molecular marker, but its clinical application requires validation in large, multi-center clinical cohorts, establishment of standardized detection methods, and clarification of its diagnostic sensitivity and specificity for different PAH subtypes. In addition, the combination of the miR-661/CCND2 axis with existing PAH therapeutic targets (eg, BMPRII/ActRIIA, NO-cGMP pathway) (7–8) may provide a novel combination therapy strategy for PAH, and the synergistic effect of this combination warrants further in vivo and clinical research.

In conclusion, our work identified miR-661 as a transcription factor of CCND2, which inhibits the proliferation, migration and cell cycle progression of pulmonary artery cells by suppressing the expression of CCND2. These findings provide a new strategy for understanding the pathological changes and potential therapeutic targets of PAH.
