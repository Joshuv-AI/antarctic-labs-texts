The New Era of Respiratory Syncytial Virus Protection for Infants: A Mini Review of Current Tools for Immunoprophylaxis
Authors: Santtu Heinonen, Pekka Vartiainen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis mini review describes the history of infant respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) immunoprophylaxis and the latest advances in this area. It also reviews the efficacy, safety and impact of the recently introduced prophylactic strategies.MethodThis narrative review is based on a targeted review of published literature, with emphasis on pivotal clinical trials, regulatory documents and recent real‐world evidence.ResultsThree immunisation products for preventing RSV in infants have been approved since 2022. These are two long‐acting monoclonal antibodies, nirsevimab and clesrovimab, and a maternal RSV vaccine. The efficacy of these interventions has ranged from 49% to 76% against medically attended RSV‐associated lower respiratory tract infections and from 55% to 91% against hospitalisation due to RSV‐associated lower respiratory tract infection. The efficacy varied between the products and the duration of the follow‐up. Similar or higher effectiveness has been reported in post‐licensure, real‐world evidence studies. Overall, these interventions have been well tolerated, with favourable safety profiles.ConclusionThe newly introduced long‐acting monoclonal antibodies and the maternal RSV vaccine have proved highly effective and have demonstrated reassuring safety. They have already been implemented in a number of countries and have substantially reduced the RSV burden in infants.Keywords: antibody, clesrovimab, maternal, monoclonal, nirsevimab, respiratory syncytial virus, vaccine

Introduction

The respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) was first identified in 1957, and it was soon recognised as a major cause of lower respiratory tract infections in infants and young children. Global data showed that in 2019, RSV was associated with an estimated 3.6 million hospital admissions and 101 400 deaths in children under five years of age. More than 97% of the deaths occurred in low‐ and middle‐income countries [1]. While mortality has been low in high‐income countries, RSV has been recognised as the most common cause of hospitalisation in infants, with approximately 1%–2% of children in high‐income countries hospitalised due to RSV during their first year of life [2].

Clinical trials of the first RSV vaccine candidate, which was a formalin‐inactivated vaccine, were initiated in the early 1960s, only a few years after the discovery of the virus. However, the formalin‐inactivated RSV vaccine failed catastrophically, causing enhanced respiratory disease in infants infected with RSV, and leading to increased hospitalisation rates and three fatalities. The phenomenon of enhanced respiratory disease was exclusively observed in children who were RSV‐seronegative at the time of vaccination. It is believed to have resulted from non‐neutralising antibodies induced by the formalin‐inactivated vaccine and the formation of immune complexes that led to exaggerated immune responses [3].

The catastrophic failure of the first vaccine candidate halted RSV vaccine development for decades. In 1998, palivizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the RSV fusion (F) protein, became available for passive immunisation. However, its high cost and the need for monthly dosing throughout the RSV season limited its use to high‐risk infants during their first and second RSV seasons. Palivizumab was the first widely used monoclonal antibody that was used to prevent an infectious disease and remained the only RSV immunoprophylaxis available for over 20 years. Following the emergence of the novel immunoprophylactic interventions, manufacturing of palivizumab was discontinued at the end of 2025.

In 2013, a major breakthrough in RSV prophylaxis development was made when the structure of the RSV F protein in its prefusion conformation was discovered. The F protein plays a key role in viral entry, as it mediates the fusion of the viral envelope and the host cell membrane. Antibodies targeting the prefusion conformation of the F protein prevent viral entry into the host cell by locking the protein in its prefusion conformation. They were found to be more potent and have stronger neutralising effects than previously characterised RSV antibodies [4]. This discovery started a new era in the development of RSV immunoprophylactic products. Since 2022, three interventions for RSV prophylaxis for infants and young children have received marketing authorisation: Two long‐acting monoclonal antibodies and a maternal RSV vaccine. These are included in this minireview. In addition, three vaccines have been licensed for older adults and adults at increased risk for severe RSV disease, but these are outside the scope of this minireview.

Nirsevimab

Nirsevimab is a long‐acting monoclonal antibody that targets the RSV F protein and specifically the antigenic site Ø, which is presented exclusively on the prefusion conformation of the protein. In vitro experiments have shown that it is more potent and has better neutralisation capacity than palivizumab [5]. The half‐life of the molecule has been extended by targeted tyrosine–threonine–glutamic acid (YTE) modification of the fragment crystallizable (Fc) region of the antibody. By introducing three amino acid substitutions (M252Y, S254T and T256E) into the Fc domain, the half‐life of the antibody can be significantly extended to allow protective levels throughout one RSV season with a single intramuscular injection.

Nirsevimab was evaluated in a phase 2b randomized, controlled trial (RCT) of 1 453 preterm infants born at ≥ 29 weeks of gestation [6] and a phase 3 RCT of 3012 term and late preterm infants, born at ≥ 35 weeks of gestation [7, 8] (Table 1). During a five‐month follow‐up, nirsevimab had an efficacy of 70% and 76% against RSV‐associated lower respiratory tract infection (RSV‐LRTI) in preterm and term/late preterm infants, respectively. The efficacy against hospitalisation for RSV‐LRTI was 78% and 77% in preterm and term/late preterm infants, respectively [6, 7, 8]. A pragmatic, randomized, open‐label trial compared nirsevimab to no intervention in 8 053 full‐term and premature infants, born at ≥ 29 weeks of gestation. The efficacy was 83% in preventing hospitalisation for RSV‐LRTI [11]. An RCT compared nirsevimab to palivizumab in high‐risk infants, including those born preterm at < 29 weeks of gestation, infants with bronchopulmonary dysplasia and infants with congenital heart disease. The rates of RSV‐LRTI hospitalisations were low in both arms. RSV‐LRTI occurred in 4/616 (0.6%) of the infants who received nirsevimab and in 3/309 (1.0%) of the infants in the palivizumab arm. There were no significant differences between the interventions [12].

Nirsevimab received marketing authorisation from the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in 2022 for preventing RSV‐LRTIs in infants entering their first RSV season, with the indication extended in 2024 to children at increased risk for severe RSV diseases entering their second RSV season. It was also approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2023, including for high‐risk children entering their second RSV season. Nirsevimab has been introduced or recommended in many countries, including several European Union countries, the United States (US), Canada and Chile [13, 14, 15]. After its implementation, several real‐world evidence studies reported decreased rates of RSV hospitalisations and similar effectiveness to that observed in the pre‐licensure RCTs. These typically ranged from 70% to 90%, depending on the outcome and study design [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. In addition, similar effectiveness of approximately 80% against RSV‐related intensive care unit admissions and emergency department visits has been reported [16, 21].

Nirsevimab was well tolerated in the RCTs with low reactogenicity [6, 7, 12]. In the phase 3 trial, adverse events that were considered treatment‐related occurred in 25/1 998 (1.3%) of the nirsevimab recipients and 15/996 (1.5%) of the placebo recipients [8]. No safety concerns have been identified during the post‐marketing safety surveillance [22].

Clesrovimab

Clesrovimab is also a long‐acting monoclonal antibody that targets the RSV F protein. It targets the antigenic site IV, which is different from the site Ø targeted by nirsevimab. Site IV is accessible in both prefusion and postfusion forms of the F protein. In contrast, the site Ø targeted by nirsevimab is only accessible in the prefusion conformation. Similar to nirsevimab, clesrovimab also has extended half‐life based on the YTE modification of the Fc region of the antibody [23].

Clesrovimab was evaluated in a phase 2b/3 RCT that comprised both full‐term infants and premature infants born at ≥ 29 weeks of gestation. During a five‐month follow‐up, it showed 91% efficacy against hospitalisation due to RSV‐LRTI, and the efficacy against medically attended RSV‐LRTI ranged from 60% to 92%, depending on the severity of the outcome [9] (Table 1). Clesrovimab was compared to palivizumab in a phase 3 RCT of high‐risk infants, including premature infants of less than 29 weeks of gestation and infants with bronchopulmonary dysplasia or congenital heart disease. The incidence of RSV disease was similar in both groups [24]. Clesrovimab was well tolerated by both healthy and high‐risk infants with low levels of reactogenicity, and there were no differences in the numbers of adverse events.

Clesrovimab received marketing authorisation from the FDA in June 2025 and from the EMA in April 2026. It became available for clinical use ahead of the 2025–2026 RSV season in the US and is expected to be available in other parts of the world for the 2026–2027 season. There are no real‐world evidence reports available yet.

Maternal Vaccination

Maternal vaccination during pregnancy provides a way to protect infants through maternal‐derived antibodies that are transferred to the fetus during pregnancy. As the risk of severe RSV disease is highest during the first months of life, maternal immunisation offers an attractive strategy for early‐life RSV protection.

In 2023, a protein‐based bivalent RSV vaccine received marketing authorisation for pregnant women from both the FDA and the EMA, and it is currently the only RSV vaccine licensed for pregnant women. The vaccine also targets RSV F protein in its prefusion conformation. The pivotal phase 3 trial comprised 7 386 pregnant women vaccinated between 24 to 36 weeks of gestation. It showed that the efficacy of the vaccine against medically attended RSV‐LRTI was 58% after three months and 49% after six months of follow‐up. The efficacy against RSV‐related hospitalisations was 70% after three months and 55% after six months of follow‐up [10, 25] (Table 1). In post‐licensure, real‐world evidence studies, the effectiveness against RSV‐related hospitalisation ranged from 70% to 79%, depending on the study design and follow‐up time [20, 26, 27].

The vaccine was well tolerated. Local reactions were more common in the vaccine group, as expected. Injection site pain was the most common local reaction, and it was reported by 41% of the vaccine recipients and by 10% of the placebo group. No statistically significant differences were observed in systemic reactogenicity or other adverse events between the groups [10, 25].

Maternal Vaccination and the Risk of Preterm Birth

Even though the vaccine was considered safe and received regulatory approval, a statistically non‐significant imbalance in the rates of preterm birth at less than 37 weeks of gestation was observed between the groups in the pivotal phase 3 trial [28]. Preterm birth occurred in 206/3 656 (5.7%) of the deliveries in the vaccine group and in 172/3643 (4.7%) of the deliveries in the placebo group. The relative risk for preterm birth was 1.20, with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.98–1.46. The majority (91%) of the preterm births were late preterm at 34 + 0 to 36 + 6 weeks of gestation. The exploratory analyses showed no increased risk in the high‐income countries, but the difference was higher and statistically significant in non‐high‐income countries (relative risk 1.73, 95% CI 1.22–2.47). The study was conducted during the COVID‐19 pandemic, and the most prominent difference was detected in South Africa. The imbalance in preterm births was first detectable in South Africa in July 2021 when the Delta variant was the dominant strain and was most apparent during the last quarter of 2021 when the Omicron variant became the dominant strain.

Another protein‐based monovalent RSV prefusion F protein vaccine candidate was also evaluated in pregnant women in a large‐scale phase 3 trial. However, the trial enrollment was prematurely stopped due to a safety signal for an increased risk of preterm birth in vaccinated individuals. The development of the vaccine was later discontinued [29]. In the prematurely stopped trial, preterm birth, at less than 37 weeks of gestation, occurred in 237/3 494 (6.8%) of the infants in the vaccine group and in 86/1 739 (4.9%) of the infants in the placebo group, with an increased relative risk of 1.37 (95% CI 1.08–1.74). The increased risk was only observed in low‐income and middle‐income countries, similar to the observations in the trial of the licensed vaccine. In addition, the imbalance between the groups was restricted to a specific period from August to December 2021.

In both studies, the observed increase in preterm births was only seen in low‐ and middle‐income countries during a restricted time period in the second half of 2021. The mechanisms underlying the increased risk are unclear. Even if the imbalance in preterm births between the groups was limited to a certain period during the COVID‐19 pandemic, exploratory analyses revealed no clear associations with confirmed severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infections, and the impact of other maternal vaccinations administered during pregnancy was discrepant between the licensed vaccine and the non‐licensed vaccine candidate [28, 29]. As a safety measure, the licensed vaccine was recommended to pregnant women from the beginning of the 28th week of gestation in the United Kingdom (UK) [30] and from the beginning of the 32nd week of gestation in the US [31]. The vaccine is also subject to enhanced post‐marketing safety surveillance. No increase in preterm births has been observed in vaccinated individuals in the post‐licensure, real‐world evidence studies [32, 33, 34, 35].

Implementation of Infant RSV Prophylaxis

Following promising RCT results and regulatory approval of two long‐acting monoclonal antibodies and a maternal vaccine, several countries have implemented their infant RSV prophylaxis programs. In general, the programs have three key characteristics. The first is eligibility and whether all infants or just high‐risk infants are offered prophylaxis. The second is the product chosen and whether monoclonal antibody, maternal vaccine or both prophylaxes are offered. The third is the timing of the interventions and whether the immunisations are offered year‐round or as seasonal programs.

Monoclonal antibody strategies range from universal infant protection of all infants in their first RSV season to a risk‐based strategy that targets infants with pre‐specified, high‐risk conditions (e.g., prematurity, chronic lung disease, congenital heart disease or immunocompromise). The universal first‐season approach is well illustrated by the US recommendation of either maternal vaccine for all pregnant women between September and January [31], or one dose of long‐acting monoclonal antibody for all infants under eight months of age entering their first RSV season. In the US, nirsevimab is also recommended for infants with high‐risk conditions aged 8–19 months who are entering their second RSV season [36]. An assessment conducted by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control prior to the 2025–2026 RSV season reported that 19 European Union and European Economic Area countries had funded long‐acting monoclonal antibody programs. Of these, 16 were universal or near‐universal programs (e.g., Finland, Germany, Iceland, Spain and Sweden) and two countries reported having targeted programs for high‐risk infants (Latvia and Lithuania) [14].

A number of countries, including the UK, Argentina, Poland, Romania and Slovenia, had implemented fully funded national programs based on maternal vaccination [14, 26, 30]. In addition, some countries (e.g., Belgium, France, Greece and the US) offer both options, maternal vaccination or monoclonal antibody, based on parental preference. In some countries, monoclonal antibodies may be considered even if the mother received the maternal vaccine during pregnancy if the child has a high‐risk condition or if the vaccine was administered less than 14 days before the delivery [14].

Monoclonal antibodies have typically been offered seasonally to newborn infants born during the RSV season with a catch‐up campaign in some countries for eligible infants born before the onset of the RSV season. In Argentina, where maternal vaccine has been implemented as the primary infant RSV prophylaxis, it is offered seasonally to provide protection for infants expected to be born before or during the anticipated RSV season [26]. In contrast, the UK has adopted a year‐round universal maternal vaccination approach, where the RSV vaccine is offered to all pregnant women during, or soon after, 28 weeks of gestation [30]. While seasonal vaccination programs may appear to be more cost‐effective [37], a year‐round program could provide logistical advantages that outweigh the benefits of a seasonal program.

In summary, countries have adopted different policies by balancing effectiveness, safety, logistics, supply, cost‐effectiveness and local seasonality. Notably, some European countries have still not decided. Differences within European countries are further described elsewhere [14, 15], and an updated database of the RSV prophylaxis introduction status in different countries is provided by the International Vaccine Access Centre (IVAC) at https://view‐hub.org/vaccine/rsv [13].

Differences in RSV prevention strategies and their availability become striking when viewed from a global perspective. The burden of RSV disease is disproportionate, with the highest burden in low‐ and middle‐income countries, where 97% of RSV‐attributable deaths in children under 5 years of age occur [1]. Still, the high cost of these immunoprophylactic interventions means that, to date, universal RSV immunisation programs have been introduced mainly in high‐income countries [13, 38]. This has further exacerbated global inequities, as those with the best access to healthcare also have the best access to RSV immunoprophylactic products. Efforts are being made to ensure global and equal access to RSV immunisation, for example by Gavi, an international partnership to improve access to new vaccines for children living in economically less developed regions. In July 2025, Gavi decided to support and fund maternal RSV vaccination programs for eligible low‐ and middle‐income countries [38].

Impact of Early Life RSV Prevention on Later Respiratory Disease Burden

One concern related to early‐life RSV prevention has been whether preventing RSV during the first RSV season merely shifts the RSV‐related disease burden to the second year of life. However, real‐world data on nirsevimab suggests that this is not the case. Instead, children who received nirsevimab during their first RSV season continued to have a lower incidence of RSV‐related LRTI hospitalisation during their second RSV season compared to historical cohorts. The reduction in RSV‐LRTI was 85% during the first season and 55% in the second RSV season [17].

Several cohort studies have suggested that avoiding early life RSV infection is associated with a lower risk of recurrent wheezing and asthma later in childhood [39, 40]. However, previous RCTs of palivizumab did not demonstrate a reduction in later respiratory morbidity [41]. These negative results may also reflect heterogeneous study populations and the use of non‐specific respiratory outcomes in the trials. Large, high‐quality studies are needed to clarify whether RSV immunisation may have benefits beyond immediate protection against early‐life RSV disease.

RSV Vaccines for Older Children

Although infants have been the primary target group for RSV immunoprophylaxis, due to their high age‐specific disease burden, older children also experience a substantial burden of RSV‐related illness. In addition, preschool‐aged children play a significant role in the transmission of RSV within households [2]. Unfortunately, there is no vaccine available for children. Protein‐based vaccines are not considered the optimal platform for preschool‐aged children, due to the potential risk of vaccine‐associated enhanced respiratory disease in young RSV seronegative children. Promising results have been reported for live attenuated RSV vaccine candidates administered intranasally, which mimic natural infection and are therefore expected to be safe also in RSV‐seronegative children [42]. However, when the most promising live vaccine strain entered a phase 3 trial in children aged 6–22 months, the pre‐planned futility analysis showed lack of efficacy, and the study was prematurely discontinued. Adenovirus‐vectored and messenger RNA‐based RSV vaccine candidates have both been evaluated in preschool‐aged children in early‐phase clinical trials. However, the development of the adenovirus vector RSV vaccine has been discontinued, and the messenger RNA vaccine program in young children is also on hold, due to concerns of potential vaccine‐associated enhanced respiratory disease cases in the phase 1 trial [43, 44].

Conclusion

The recently approved RSV immunoprophylactic products have demonstrated high efficacy and have already transformed the burden of RSV infections in young infants. Countries that have implemented RSV prophylaxis have observed dramatic reductions in RSV hospitalisation rates. These have benefited not only the children who received immunisation but also healthcare systems by alleviating the substantial burden of RSV on paediatric hospitals and healthcare services.

Most countries that have introduced infant RSV prophylaxis offer universal or near‐universal programs targeting children entering their first RSV season. Further research is needed to better estimate the duration of protection and to identify high‐risk children who would benefit from long‐acting monoclonal antibodies also when entering their second RSV season. Studies assessing the impact of early‐life RSV immunisation on longer‐term respiratory outcomes would be of particular interest.

Despite these advances, current RSV immunisation strategies do not address the unequal global burden of RSV disease. Beyond price reductions, coordinated international efforts are essential to improve availability and ensure equitable access.
