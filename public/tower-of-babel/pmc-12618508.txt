A parcel-level cropland dataset with field shape indicators for Xizang (Tibet) from VHR remote sensing
Authors: Chuya Wang, Xiaoyan Wang, Yi Jiang, Shuaijun Liu, Fangyuan Jin, Zhangcheng Li, Lai Wei, Xiuchun Dong, Yuqiao Long, Si Wang, Ping Huang, Yimei Wang, Jin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-resolution cropland data are essential for regional agricultural research, particularly in Xizang, China, where fragmented landscapes and complex cropping systems require accurate monitoring of crop distribution, yield estimation, and land management planning. This study presents the first parcel-level cropland dataset for Xizang, generated by a deep learning-based field extraction model and high-resolution Google Earth imagery. Field validation of the dataset with 155 UAV samples showed 90% area matching accuracy, and cross-comparison with two existing cropland products of Xizang also showed strong linear correlations (R2 = 0.87 and 0.90). The dataset contains 1,072,110 individual parcels, totaling approximately 290,721.35 ha, mainly concentrated in the Three River Region (28°–31°N, 87°–92°E), with larger parcels (>0.5 ha) constituting the majority of cultivated area. Additionally, the field shape indicators are further calculated and provided, including main direction, regularity, and contiguity. This dataset offers critical insights into Xizang’s fine-scale cropland patterns and can serve as a foundation for parcel-level agricultural monitoring, land-use planning, and policy formulation.Subject terms: Agriculture, Geography

Background & Summary

Cropland represents one of the most vital land resources for human society, serving as the foundation for regional food supply and socio-economic stability1. In China, the combined pressures of climate change, rapid urbanization, and the increasing phenomena of farmland “non-agriculturalization” and “non-grainization” have rendered precise monitoring and management of cropland resources a strategic imperative2–4. An assessment of regional cropland quantity and spatial distribution constitutes a fundamental prerequisite for addressing these challenges5,6, with substantial implications for optimizing spatial land use and formulating food security policies7.

Numerous scholars have examined the patterns and drivers of cropland changes in China’s economically developed coastal regions8,9, and provinces like Henan10, Shandong11,12, and Heilongjiang13, which have high-quality farmland. These studies have revealed the spatial distribution of cropland in China’s primary grain-producing areas at a macro scale, mainly using data derived from remote sensing imagery such as SPOT, Landsat and MODIS, with spatial resolutions ranging from tens to hundreds of meters. However, in some fragmented landscape areas of China, the limited spatial resolution of the datasets may lead considerable mapping inaccuracies and uncertainties14,15, thereby constraining our understanding of cropland dynamics and distribution patterns.

The Xizang Autonomous Region (abbreviated as Xizang), situated on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, is one of the key pastoral areas in China. Due to its unique geographical location, high altitude, and complex terrain, agricultural activities are largely confined to river valleys, with cropland accounting for only about 0.2% of the region’s total area16. Nevertheless, given the heavy reliance of the Xizang people on locally grown crops, cropland still plays a crucial role in ensuring food security, increasing farmers’ incomes, and supporting poverty alleviation efforts17. Accelerated socio-economic development and population growth are driving rising land demands in Xizang, exacerbating the challenges of cropland utilization and protection18,19. However, conventional remote sensing land cover products (e.g., Landsat, MODIS) struggle to accurately capture Xizang’s fragmented agricultural landscapes, resulting in few of research on its cropland dynamics20–22. Recent studies have attempted manual visual interpretation using high-resolution Google Earth imagery to map Xizang’s cropland distribution16,23, which partially fills the data gap. Yet, such labor-intensive methods prove impractical for achieving parcel-scale (individual field) precision across extensive areas. Consequently, amid Xizang’s intricate mosaic of fragmented fields and heterogeneous crop spatial distributions, current datasets fail to meet the refined requirements of modern agricultural management, including well-facilitated farmland construction, crop classification and yield estimation, and monitoring of abandoned cropland.

Recent advancements in computing power and artificial intelligence have significantly enhanced the feasibility of automated cropland extraction from remote sensing imagery24. To address the region-specific demands and challenges in Xizang, this study implemented a dedicated deep learning network to extract cropland from high-resolution Google Earth imagery. We achieved precise field segmentation and delineation, producing Xizang’s parcel-scale cropland extent and boundary dataset. Furthermore, we calculated key shape indicators for all extracted parcels, providing robust support for land-use planning and agricultural management in Xizang. The results underwent rigorous ground-truth validation across over 30 major counties in Xizang, ensuring data reliability. This dataset enables detailed investigation of spatial distribution patterns of cropland parcels and serves as a foundational layer for crop mapping in fragmented agricultural landscapes.

Methods

Study area

Xizang (26°–36°N, 78°–99°E), located along China’s southwestern frontier (Fig. 1a), covers an extensive area with terrain descending from northwest to southeast and an average elevation exceeding 4,000 m. The region exhibits complex topography, characterized by four distinct geomorphological types: the Himalayan mountains, the northern plateau, the southern valleys, and the eastern alpine gorges. Climatic conditions are highly heterogeneous, with pronounced vertical zonation, ranging from subtropical monsoon humid climates to temperate plateau monsoon semi-humid and semi-arid climates25, with annual precipitation of about 400 mm26. Administratively, Xizang comprises seven prefecture-level divisions (Fig. 1b). The primary grain-producing areas are concentrated in Lhasa, Nyingchi, Qamdo, Shannan, and Shigatse within the southern valleys and eastern gorges, which are predominantly traversed by the Yarlung Tsangpo (Brahmaputra), Jinsha (Upper Yangtze), Lancang (Mekong), and Nu (Salween) rivers and their tributaries16,23. In contrast, the Nagqu and Ngari regions exhibit negligible agricultural activity. The cropping system in Xizang is diverse, dominated by highland barley (Hordeum vulgare L.), alongside wheat, rapeseed, maize, and vegetables. Croplands are characterized by fragmented field patterns and intricate spatial crop distributions.

Data preparation

While medium-resolution satellite imagery serves as a valuable data source for cropland mapping, its capability to delineate agricultural parcels in fragmented landscapes remains constrained. Compared to the expansive, homogeneous croplands typical of China’s plains, croplands in Xizang exhibit irregular geometries and heterogeneous cropping patterns, with inter-field widths often below 10 m (Fig. 1c). Very-high-resolution (VHR) satellite data can effectively address the crop heterogeneity across fragmented parcels in Xizang, preventing inter-parcel misclassification of differential crop types—thus establishing a critical foundation for subsequent cropland classification and yield estimation studies.

Most VHR imagery comes from commercial and military satellites, purchasing imagery covering the entire range of Xizang would be prohibitively expensive27,28. Based on the actual situation of the study area, we acquired the VHR optical imagery from the open-access Google Earth images (Zoom Level 17, about 2.15-m resolution) as the data source, which provides sufficient spatial granularity for precise parcel-level analysis. Google Earth provides free, large-scale RGB imagery covering the entire Tibetan Plateau by integrating images captured by various satellites (e.g. WorldView, QuickBird, GeoEye, and Pleiades etc.). It employs well-established sifting and preprocessing procedures, enabling rapid access to cloudless images and minimizing the impact of clouds on land feature extraction29. Many researchers have conducted VHR land cover mapping using Google Earth imagery30–32, demonstrating its feasibility. In this study, Google RGB images covering all major grain-producing regions of Xizang were downloaded via the open-source software QGIS. While most selected images are from 2022, the time range was expanded to 2019 for limited areas with frequent cloud cover to guarantee data quality and comprehensive coverage, yielding a total storage size of approximately 1 TB.

Field segmentation and extraction based on deep learning network

To accurately extract cropland parcels across extensive and fragmented landscapes, this study employed a Field Segmentation based on Domain Adversarial approach (FSDA)33, a deep learning network comprising three core modules (Fig. 2): (1) a parallel field boundary segmentation module, (2) a fine-grained adversarial domain adaptation module, and (3) a boundary connection and parcel refinement module. The parallel field boundary segmentation module integrates two leading semantic segmentation networks, UNet34 and DeepLabV3+35, establishing a dual-network framework that concurrently captures field texture and boundary features to achieve parcel-scale field segmentation. The fine-grained adversarial domain adaptation module incorporates transfer learning to enhance model robustness in unseen target domains36, ensuring reliable generalization under Xizang’s diverse natural conditions. The boundary connection and parcel refinement module applies morphological operators and rule-based edge linking to rectify discontinuities, fill voids, and eliminate misclassified non-cropland patches, thereby optimizing parcel topology. For implementation details of these modules, see ref. 33.

The FSDA algorithm framework.

For model training, we generated over 10,000 labeled pairs. Given the extensive spatial coverage and temporal variability of the imagery, which induces significant seasonal differences in cropland spectral characteristics, label production focused on representative areas encompassing croplands, buildings, mountains, and rivers. These regions were selected based on seasonal imagery, cultivation patterns, and terrain types. Field boundaries were vectorized following strict topological rules, with edge labels automatically derived from adjacency relationships. All data were standardized into 512 × 512 pixel image-label pairs for network input.

It can be seen that regardless of the terrain, cropland parcels and field roads are well separated, even for smaller parcels.

Calculation of field shape indicators

Field shape indicators are primarily used to characterize the geometric attributes of agricultural parcels, including main direction, regularity, and contiguity, which can support land-use planning and agricultural management37. The main direction of a field reflects its spatial alignment trend, aiding in the analysis of directional patterns in land utilization. In this study, we employed two methods for extracting field direction: principal component analysis (PCA) and bounding box. The PCA method identifies the main direction through linear transformation by projecting data onto the axis of maximum variance. For field parcels, this involves determining the best-fit line representing their dominant elongation direction. Data Records

Based on the methodology introduced above, we extracted parcel-level cropland data for Xizang and calculated field shape indicators. The vector data are stored in ESRI Shapefile format, which can be visualized and edited using software such as ArcGIS Pro, ArcMap, and QGIS. Field shape indicators are further recorded in Excel tables, with unique FIDs for data-parcel associations. The complete dataset is archived and available at figshare38 (accessible via link: 10.6084/m9.figshare.28955795). Details of the dataset are presented in Table 1.

Technical Validation

Field survey assess accuracy

To ensure data reliability, we conducted 155 field surveys across 35 major agricultural counties in Xizang between 2022–2024 using Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV). Based on the cropland area of each grain-producing county, a spatially stratified sampling strategy was adopted to design the sample plots, with their spatial distribution shown in Fig. 4a. High-resolution UAV imagery from these samples was manually digitized to establish ground-truth parcel boundaries, followed by comparison with results in this study (Fig. 4b,c). In total, 9,553 individual cropland parcels were obtained from the 155 UAV-based field surveys, serving as high-precision references for validating our parcel delineation results.

(a) Spatial distribution of 155 field survey sites; (b) Example of manually digitized cropland parcels from UAV imagery and (c) corresponding parcels extracted by this study in the same area; (d–i) Comparison samples between field-digitized parcels and extracted parcels in this study for different field morphologies.

As shown in Fig. 4d–i, our method achieved complete cropland detection, with precise morphological correspondence even for fragmented small parcels. For further accuracy assessment, we divided Xizang’s major grain-producing areas into three regions according to their geomorphological characteristics: Western Xizang (Shigatse), Central Xizang (Lhasa and Shannan) and Eastern Xizang (Qamdo and Nyingchi). These subregions represent three distinct cropland types (Fig. 3): Valley cropland (western region), Flat basin cropland (central region) and Slope cropland (eastern region). The number of survey plots in each region was 78, 40, and 37, respectively. Table 2 shows that the area ratios between our extracted parcels and the reference samples were approximately 89.9%, 89.2%, and 90.8% for the three regions, with an overall ratio of 89.8%. Considering the difference in image timeliness and the imperfections in manual digitization, this result is sufficient to show that the parcel-level cropland data obtained by our method has a fairly good quality.

Comparison with other products

In addition to comparing with manually digitized field boundaries from high-resolution UAV images, we conducted cross-validation with two existing farmland datasets specifically developed for Xizang. Both datasets were created through visual interpretation of high-resolution Google Earth satellite imagery; the first was produced by Wei et al.39, and the second was produced by Sang et al.23. Although the datasets have different reference years (Wei et al.: 2018; Sang et al.: 2020; this study: 2022), Xizang’s cropland distribution remains relatively stable over short timeframes. Accounting for spatial coverage differences among different datasets, we performed cross-validation specifically in the shared Three River Region of Xizang (Fig. 5a). To facilitate comparison of cropland area differences between datasets, we established a hexagonal grid system (each cell = 1000 km2) covering the entire Three River Region, totaling 67 grid cells. Spatial comparative analysis shows generally consistent cropland distribution patterns across all datasets, while our results provide the most detailed parcel-level delineation.

(a) Distribution of hexagonal grids in Three River Region and spatial comparison of datasets; The comparison and correlation of the area of the cropland in grids (b) between Wei et al.’s dataset and this study; (c) between Sang et al.’s dataset and this study.

Statistical analysis of cropland area within each grid revealed strong consistency between our results and both reference datasets, with linear R2 values of 0.87 and 0.90, respectively (Fig. 5b,c). In addition, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation, we also introduced a logarithmic-scale comparison, which demonstrates that our dataset maintains a stable proportional relationship with the reference datasets across both small and large cropland areas. Notably, our study generally yielded smaller cropland extents compared to previous manual interpretation-based studies. This discrepancy likely occurs because large-scale manual interpretation tends to merge contiguous farmland into single parcels, then narrow irrigation channels, field ridges, and farm roads were often included, and the labor-intensive nature of manual mapping may compromise detail precision. Our automated method overcomes these limitations by precisely delineating individual parcels, excluding non-cultivated linear features and maintaining consistent extraction standards.

Uncertainty analysis

Several uncertainties should be noted, primarily stemming from training samples and image quality. First, regarding training samples, the complex and heterogeneous landscapes of Xizang made it challenging to include all possible field characteristics (color, morphology, texture) in the training dataset. This limitation may lead to misclassification or incomplete segmentation of cropland parcels in areas not well-represented in the training samples. Second, concerning image quality, the high-resolution images used were not acquired simultaneously (spanning 2019–2022) and contain extensive mosaicking in some areas. These factors could result in omitted cropland parcels in certain regions.

Usage Notes

The fragmented landscapes and complex crop distribution patterns in Xizang make understanding current cropland utilization essential for regional agricultural sustainability. The dataset presented in this study significantly advances the fine-scale characterization of cropland distribution across Xizang, establishing an essential foundation for subsequent parcel-level crop classification and yield estimation research. Furthermore, these high-resolution data offer valuable references for key policy-oriented applications, including high-standard farmland construction, abandoned farmland monitoring, and agricultural land-use planning, thereby supporting evidence-based decision making for Xizang’s agriculture.

Notably, our method relies primarily on open-access high-resolution satellite imagery and field shape extraction algorithms, without depending on cadastral maps or statistical parcel data. Its successful application in Xizang, demonstrates both the robustness and adaptability of the approach. These conditions are, in many ways, analogous to those in conflict-affected or underdeveloped regions40, highlighting the potential of our method to support parcel-level cropland mapping in areas where official data are scarce or access is limited. Our approach and dataset also play an important role in global crop monitoring initiatives, such as GEO and JECAM, by facilitating the unification of parcel delineation. This is especially valuable in regions with intensive crop rotation, where annual re-identification of field boundaries (e.g., for winter wheat in Poland and South Africa) remains challenging for traditional manual interpretations41.

Details on the cropland dataset in Xizang

The dataset contains 1,072,110 individual parcels, totaling approximately 290,721.35 ha (hectare; 1 ha = 1 hm2). It enables detailed spatial analyses and assessments of cropland distribution patterns across Xizang. The croplands are primarily distributed in two main regions (Fig. 6a): 1) Southern Tibetan valleys, including the river valley areas of Lhasa, Shannan, and Shigatse, where relatively flat terrain and irrigation conditions are favorable for agriculture; 2) Southeastern Tibetan gorge region, primarily in Nyingchi and Chamdo, where the warmer and more humid climate allows for cultivated land to exist along narrow river valleys. Ecologically, the two regions contain multiple ecoregional units defined in the RESOLVE ecoregion dataset42 (e.g. the Yarlung Zanbo arid steppe, Eastern Himalayan alpine shrub and meadows, Northeast Himalayan subalpine conifer forests, Southeast Tibet shrublands and meadows, and Nujiang Langcang Gorge alpine conifer and mixed forests). Geographically, parcels are heavily concentrated in the Three River Region (28°–31°N, 87°–92°E) encompassing Shigatse, Lhasa and Shannan, while showing more dispersed distributions in Nyingchi and Qamdo.

(a) The spacial distribution of cropland in Xizang’s main grain-producing regions; Comparison of the (b) number and (c) area of cropland in major grain-producing cities in Xizang.

Moreover, the size-based classification facilitates comparative evaluations of cropland resources among different cities. In terms of parcel counts (Fig. 6b), Lhasa has the largest number of parcels (about 38% of Xizang’s total), followed by Shigatse (29%), Qamdo (21%), Shannan (9%) and Nyingchi (3%). However, the composition of parcel sizes varies across cities, Lhasa is dominated by small parcels (0.05–0.1 ha), while Shigatse and Qamdo contain relatively larger parcels (>5 ha). Overall, most parcels in all cities are smaller than 0.5 ha. For total cropland area by city (Fig. 6c), Shigatse has the largest share (about 39% of Xizang’s total), followed by Qamdo (27%), with Lhasa, Shannan and Nyingchi accounting for 16%, 12% and 6% respectively. In contrast to parcel counts, parcels larger than 0.5 ha constitute the major cropland area resources in Xizang.

The use of field shape indicators

This study present the first field shape indicators for parcel-level cropland in Xizang, which holds significant value for guiding future cropland planning and agricultural management. The result examples are shown in Fig. 7. Specifically, Fig. 7a displays the bounding boxes of the cropland parcels, representing the smallest-area rectangles that completely enclose the original shapes and indicating their basic geometric characteristics. Figure 7b displays contiguous zones of cropland generated through 10 m buffer merging, reflecting spatial connectivity patterns, with a total of 85,790 contiguous zones identified across the dataset, including a maximum contiguous area of approximately 4,413.69 ha.

Examples of the field shape indicators for cropland in Xizang. (a) Bounding box; (b) Contiguous zone; Main direction by (c) PCA method and (d) bounding box method; Regularity by (e) landscape ecology fractal dimension method and (f) bounding box ratio method.

The main direction of cropland parcels derived from both PCA and bounding box methods are presented in Fig. 7c,d, respectively. The results demonstrate generally consistent direction patterns between the two methods in the sample region. Each method exhibits distinct advantages and limitations: the bounding box method provides intuitive results for simple rectangular or regularly shaped parcels but disregards internal shape details and shows limited sensitivity to rotational variations, making it less suitable for irregular or complex shapes. In contrast, the PCA method better adapts to overall shape characteristics and remains unaffected by rotation, though it demonstrates higher sensitivity to noise and outliers. Hence, the user can choose the desired results according to the actual situation of the study area.

Figure 7e,f illustrate cropland regularity indicators calculated using the landscape ecology fractal dimension method and bounding box ratio method, respectively. These two methods differ in their calculation and value ranges (see Methods section), allowing users to select appropriate results based on specific research needs. The fractal dimension method effectively captures shape compactness and complexity through perimeter-area relationships, making it particularly suitable for evaluating overall morphological characteristics of intricate shapes. Conversely, the bounding box ratio method provides clearly bounded values (0–1) that facilitate straightforward interpretation, while excelling in assessing shape-rectangle similarity and performing well for elongated or directionally prominent parcels.
