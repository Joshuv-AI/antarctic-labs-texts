Correction and clarification on reported percentage error of estimated continuous cardiac output
Authors: Ryoichi Ochiai
Published: 2025

Dear Editor:

I am writing regarding the article by Romanelli et al. [1], “Estimating resting energy expenditure in critically ill patients: a retrospective exploratory comparison of predictive equations and Fick-derived Weir estimates in Italy,” published in Acute and Critical Care.

Estimated continuous cardiac output (esCCO) has been in clinical use worldwide for over a decade, and numerous clinical studies have evaluated its accuracy. In particular, comparisons with transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) have yielded variable results. In summary: (1) bias (the mean difference between the two methods) is generally small (≈ ±0.5 L/min in many reports); (2) limits of agreement are highly variable (≈ ±0.8 to ±2.9 L/min), depending on methods (left ventricular outflow tract vs. valve measurement, TTE vs. transesophageal echocardiography) and patient conditions; and (3) percentage error (PE) has been reported over a wide range (16%–60%), with typical clinical values around 25%–50%. Consequently, PEs often exceed the 30% threshold and fail to meet the interchangeability criterion.

In the current article, the authors cite references 40 and 41 and describe the PE of esCCO as “between 18% and 69%.” However, Joshi et al. [2] actually reported a PE of 46.29%, indicating that 69% is a typographical error requiring correction. In most comparisons of esCCO with thermodilution, PEs are close to 30%. For instance, Takakura et al. [3] reported PEs before and after mechanical ventilation of 26.2% and 31.1%, respectively, and this study was cited as reference 42 in the article. Moreover, Taniguchi et al. [4] evaluated pediatric patients using TTE, which is itself associated with large PEs. Thus, citing this study as supportive evidence for adult esCCO accuracy is inappropriate.

Finally, the article describes esCCO as “minimally invasive” or “less invasive”. Because esCCO relies solely on standard non-invasive parameters—electrocardiography, non-invasive blood pressure, and pulse oximetry—it should correctly be described as “non-invasive.”
