A Latent Principal Stratification Method to Address One-Sided Cluster and Individual Noncompliance in Cluster RCTs
Anthony Sisti, Ellen McCreedy, Roee Gutman, Anthony Sisti, Ellen McCreedy, Roee Gutman

Abstract
In pragmatic cluster randomized controlled trials (PCRCTs), the unit of randomization may be the healthcare provider. In these studies, noncompliance can occur at both the patient and cluster levels. Some studies measure cluster-level implementation using multiple continuous metrics while documenting individual binary compliance. The complier average causal effect estimates the intervention effects among individuals that comply with the assigned intervention. However, it does not account for compliance metrics at the cluster level. When compliance with the intervention is influenced by both providers and individuals, it can be scientifically beneficial to describe the effects of the intervention between all levels of compliance. We propose a Bayesian method for PCRCTs with one-sided binary noncompliance at the individual level and one-sided partial compliance at the cluster level. Our Bayesian model classifies clusters into latent compliance strata based on pretreatment characteristics, partial compliance status, and individual outcomes. Because compliance is only observed in the treatment arm, the method imputes unobserved compliance for control clusters and the individuals within them. This approach estimates finite and super-population estimands within strata defined by both cluster- and individual-level compliance. We apply this method to the METRIcAL trial, a multi-part, pragmatic cluster randomized trial evaluating the effects of a personalized music intervention on agitation in nursing home residents with dementia.

1
Introduction

Agitation and aggressive behaviors are common among cognitively impaired individuals who reside in nursing homes (NHs)
(
Zuidema et al. 2007
)
. These behaviors can decrease quality of life and enhance cognitive
decline
(
García-Alberca et al. 2019
;
Shin et al. 2005
)
while also being a major source of stress for NH staff
(
Karlsen et al. 2023
)
and other residents
(
Rosen et al. 2008
)
. Antipsychotic and mood-stabilizing medications are commonly used to reduce aggressive behaviors. However, these medications are associated with significant adverse events, such as falls and deaths [34]. Thus, it is important to examine the effects of non-pharmaceutical alternatives to mitigate aggressive behaviors and reduce antipsychotic use.

METRIcAL is a multi-stage, pragmatic, NH based, cluster randomized control trial (PCRCT)
that was implemented NHs across 4 corporate chains in the US
(
Mor 2021
)
. The trial evaluated the effects
of personalized music on the agitated behaviors and medication usage of nursing home residents
with moderate to severe dementia. Like many other pragmatic trials in patients with dementia
(
Allore et al. 2020
)
, METRIcAL included many individuals in the
active intervention arm who did not comply with the intervention.

Pragmatic randomized controlled trials (PRCTs) aim to estimate the effects of interventions in real-world settings where participants are subject to different degrees of compliance with the intervention
(
Patsopoulos 2011
)
. PRCT analyses commonly follow the intention-to- treat (ITT) principle, in which units are analyzed according to the trial arm to which they were originally assigned, regardless of their compliance status
(
Sheiner and Rubin 1995
;
Gupta 2011
;
Jo et al. 2008
)
. These estimands may not summarize the effects of receipt of the intervention, and may not provide insights into the effects of the intervention in settings with different patterns of compliance
(
Hernán and Robins 2017
)
. Naive methods to address non-compliance are the as-treated and per-protocol estimands. The as-treated estimands allocate units to the intervention that they received
(
Ellenberg 1996
)
, while per-protocol estimands consider only units who received their assigned intervention
(
Matilde Sanchez and Chen 2006
)
. Because these estimands consider samples that do not correspond to the original randomization design, they may result in biased estimates of the causal effects
(
Goldberg and Belitskaya-Levy 2014
)
.

When compliance status is binary, an estimand that preserves the randomization design is the complier average causal effect (CACE). This estimand describes the effects of an intervention among units that would comply with the intervention they are assigned to
(
Imbens and Rubin 1997
;
Frangakis 2002
)
. Because compliance is only observed under one intervention for each unit, additional assumptions are required to estimate the CACE. Under the monotonicity assumption, the exclusion restriction assumption, and individual-level randomization, instrumental variables (IV) methods can be used to estimate the CACE
(
Angrist et al. 1996
)
. Bayesian estimation procedures have shown improved operating characteristics compared to econometric IV techniques
(
Imbens and Rubin 1997
)
and can evaluate the impact of violations of the monotonicity and exclusion restrictions assumptions
(
Imbens and Rubin 1997
;
Hirano et al. 2000
)
. Another estimation procedure for the CACE relies on multiple imputation (MI) to explicitly impute the missing compliance status. In smaller sample sizes, this procedure was shown to have better operating characteristics than maximum likelihood and method of moments estimators
(
Taylor and Zhou 2009
)
. In cluster randomized designs,
(
Forastiere et al. 2016
)
describes a framework to estimate the CACE when there is individual-level noncompliance.
(
Imai et al. 2021
)
proposes a different method for two-stage randomized experiments that are subject to both interference and noncompliance at the individual-level. Bayesian methods have also been proposed to address individual-level noncompliance in cluster randomized designs
(
Frangakis et al. 2002
;
Taylor and Zhou 2011
;
Ohnishi and Sabbaghi 2022
)
. However, in pragmatic cluster randomized trials, where healthcare providers serve as the unit of randomization, noncompliance may arise at both the individual and cluster levels.

The principal stratification framework enables investigators to adjust for post-treatment variables to obtain valid causal effects estimands
(
Frangakis and Rubin 2002
)
. This framework is more flexible than the instrumental variable approach and can be used to define estimands that account for compliance at two levels. With binary compliance states at the cluster-level and the individual-level,
(
Schochet and Chiang 2011
)
defined 16 principal strata that summarize all combinations of compliance at both levels. The CACE is defined for the group of clusters and individuals that comply with their treatment assignments. To estimate this version of the CACE, a method of moments procedure under specific identifiability conditions is derived. An extension of this method considers a variety of identifying assumptions and proposes a different maximum likelihood estimation procedure
(
Sheng et al. 2019
)
.

In some studies, compliance to an intervention may be partial. For example, when individuals consume different portions of the assigned intervention dose
(
Jin and Rubin 2008
)
. In PCRCTs, partial compliance can also occur at the cluster-level.
(
Zhai and Gutman 2020
)
proposed a Bayesian MI procedure that imputes the unobserved partial compliance status of healthcare providers and the binary compliance status of patients to estimate the effects of video for advanced directives on healthcare utilization. This method relied on a single, discretized, compliance variable to define partial compliance levels at the cluster-level, and it did not specify a joint distribution for the outcomes and the compliance status.

We propose a new Bayesian method to analyze PCRCTs with one-sided binary noncompliance at the individual-level and one-sided partial compliance at the cluster-level. This method utilize a Bayesian mixture model to classify clusters into latent compliance strata based on their pre-treatment characteristics, their partial compliance status and individual-level outcomes. Because compliance is only observed in the treatment arm, the Bayesian model imputes the unobserved compliance levels for clusters and individuals assigned to the control arm. The procedure can estimate finite and super-population estimands within strata defined by a combination of the latent cluster-level compliance and individual-level compliance. We apply the procedure to analyze METRIcAL and find that residents who listened to the personalized music playlists and lived in NHs that were more likely to target agitated behaviors decreased their average antipsychotic use and increased their average agitated behaviors 4-month follow-up compared to those assigned to standard of care. However, these results were not statistically significant at the 5% nominal level.

2
Motivating Example: METRIcAL

METRIcAL is a PCRCT that examined the effects of a personalized music intervention on agitated behaviors and antipsychotic use among NH residents with dementia. The first stage of METRIcAL included 976 residents in 54 NH facilities. The average number of residents across facilities was 18.1 (SD = 1.5). Half of the facilities were assigned to the personalized music intervention and the other half were assigned to the control condition
(
Mor 2021
)
. Participants assigned to the personalized music intervention received iPods that included music that they listened to as young adults. The control condition was usual care, which could include the use of ambient or group music. In the first stage of METRIcAL, no statistically significant effects of personalized music intervention on agitated behaviors were observed
(
McCreedy et al. 2022
)
. One possible reason for the lack of significance may stem from lack of adherence of individuals to the assigned personalized music protocol. Only 71% of individuals in the intervention arm listened to the personalized music at least once, and the median minutes played per day was 22.1, which was less than the prescribed 30 minutes per day
(
McCreedy et al. 2022
)
.

2.1
Description of Data

Facility-level baseline characteristics were obtained from LTCfocus, a long term care database sponsored by the National Institute on Aging (1P01AG027296) through collaboration with Brown University. These characteristics included the previous year’s average daily census, racial and gender demographics, location of the facility within the US, the average age of residents, and the percent of self-ambulating residents. Participant level baseline characteristics were obtained from the Minimum Data Set (MDS)
(
CMS 2021
)
. The MDS contains comprehensive data on each participant’s health status and functional capabilities. These characteristics include demographics such as sex and age, co-morbid conditions such as depression, measures of functional status such as the Activities of Daily Living Scale (ADL)
(
Graf 2008
)
, measures of cognitive status such as the Cognitive Function Scale (CFS)
(
Thomas et al. 2017
)
and measures of dementia-related symptoms such as the Agitated and Reactive Behavior Scale (ARBS)
(
McCreedy et al. 2019
)
(Appendix Table A2). Study outcome measures including the Cohen-Mansfield Agitation Inventory (CMAI) and antipsychotic use were collected at baseline and four months after study initiation for each study participant. The CMAI is a standardized questionnaire that assesses agitation in elderly individuals. It measures 29 agitated behaviors, categorized as physically aggressive, physically non-aggressive, or verbally agitated. Each behavior is rated on a 7-point scale based on frequency, with higher scores indicating greater agitation
(
Cohen-Mansfield et al. 1989
)
. The antipsychotic use outcome indicates whether or not a resident received an antipsychotic medication in the past week, without indicating the number of days an antipsychotic was received, the dose received, or whether or not the administration was scheduled or delivered pro re nata, in response to agitated behavior. The trial team also collected information about the fidelity of the intervention. At the individual-level, the amount of personalized music played by each resident was recorded in the iPod’s metadata. At the NH level, investigators documented the proportion of nurse involvement, the proportion of unique songs loaded on the iPods for each resident, and the proportion of residents who were identified by NH-staff on a pretreatment survey as individuals whose behaviors they intended to target with the personalized music intervention. Detailed description of the trial design is reported in
(
McCreedy et al. 2021
)
.

Methods

3.1
Notation and Definitions

We consider a super-population of
I^{sp}
clusters, such that each cluster consists of
N_{i}
units, and the total population size is
N=\sum_{i=1}^{I^{sp}}N_{i}
, where
N
could be infinite. To study the effect of a binary intervention, a cluster randomized trial is conducted in a finite-sample with
I^{fp}
clusters indexed
i=1,\ldots,I^{fp}
, where
I^{fp}_{0}
is the number of clusters assigned to the control arm, and
I^{fp}_{1}=I^{fp}-I^{fp}_{0}
is the number of clusters assigned to the treatment arm. Cluster
i
comprises
n_{i}\leq N_{i}
units such that
n=\sum_{i=1}^{I^{fp}}n_{i}
denotes the total number of study participants. Let
W_{i}\in\{0,1\}
be a treatment assignment indicator that equals 0 if cluster
i
is assigned to the control condition and equals 1 if it is assigned to the active intervention. We define
\mathbf{W}=\{W_{1},\ldots,W_{I^{fp}}\}
as the treatment assignment vector for all
I^{fp}
clusters. In addition, let
\mathbf{C}_{i}(W_{i})=\{C_{i}^{1}(W_{i}),\ldots,C_{i}^{\ell}(W_{i})\}
denote a vector of
\ell
potential continuous compliance metrics that describe the implementation of the active intervention in cluster
i
when it is assigned to intervention
W_{i}
. We assume that clusters assigned to the control arm have no access to the active intervention so that
\mathbf{C}_{i}(0)=0
. For simplicity, we define
\mathbf{C}_{i}:=\mathbf{C}_{i}(1)
. For each cluster, we observe
P
covariates,
\mathbf{Z}_{i}=\{Z_{i}^{1},\ldots,Z_{i}^{P}\}
, that consists of variables recorded prior to the intervention being implemented. We assume that the values of
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
and
\mathbf{C}_{i}
follow a
K
component latent compliance strata mixture distribution. To identify the compliance stratum membership for each cluster, we introduce latent allocation variables
S_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
for
i\in\{1,\ldots,I^{fp}\}
. We define
\mathbf{C}=\left\{\mathbf{C}_{i}\right\}
and
\mathbf{S}=\left\{S_{i}\right\}
to be the cluster-level compliance and latent compliance strata vectors for all
I^{fp}
clusters, respectively

We define
D_{ij}(W_{i})\in\{0,1\}
to be an indicator that equals 1 when individual
j
in cluster
i
complies with the active intervention when assigned
W_{i}
, and 0 otherwise. Because individuals in control clusters have no access to the active intervention,
D_{ij}(0)=0\ \forall\ i,j
. We define
D_{ij}:=D_{ij}(1)
and designate an individual as a complier when
D_{ij}=1
. Let
\mathbf{Y}(0,\mathbf{D})=\left\{Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})\right\}
denote the set of potential outcomes for all
n
individuals under the control condition, and
\mathbf{Y}(1,\mathbf{D})=\left\{Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})\right\}
denote the set of potential outcomes for all
n
individuals under the active intervention. Similarly, we let
\mathbf{D}=\left\{D_{ij}\right\}
denote the set of individual compliance indicators. Lastly, for individual
j
in cluster
i
we record
M
pre-treatment covariates
\mathbf{X}_{ij}=\{X_{ij}^{1},\ldots,X_{ij}^{M}\}
.

We assume the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA)
(
Rubin 1990
)
for compliance status and the potential outcomes in the cluster randomized trial so that the observed and missing potential outcomes are

\displaystyle Y_{ij}^{obs}

\displaystyle=Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})\times W_{i}+Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})\times(1-W_{i})

\displaystyle Y_{ij}^{mis}

\displaystyle=Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})\times(1-W_{i})+Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})\times(W_{i}).

The sets of observed and missing compliance and variables are
\mathbf{D}^{obs}=\left\{D_{ij}|W_{i}=1\right\},\mathbf{D}^{mis}=\left\{D_{ij}|W_{i}=0\right\},\mathbf{C}^{obs}=\left\{\mathbf{\mathbf{C}}_{i}|W_{i}=1\right\}\text{ and }\mathbf{C}^{mis}=\left\{\mathbf{\mathbf{C}}_{i}|W_{i}=0\right\}
.

3.2
Principal Strata and Estimands

Causal estimands are statistics that are used to summarize the effects of an intervention across a population of interest
(
Gutman and Rubin 2015
;
Rubin 1978
)
. The population of interest may comprise only units in the sample, resulting in finite-sample estimands. Alternatively, the population can comprise all units in a target super-population, resulting in super-population estimands. The intention to treat (ITT) estimands summarize the effects of randomization across populations of interest
(
Imbens and Rubin 2016
)
. When individuals adhere with their assigned intervention ITT estimands can be used to infer the effects of the intervention. However, when trial participants do not comply with their assigned intervention, the ITT effect may not represent the effects of receiving the intervention
(
Imbens and Rubin 2016
)
. Possible finite and super-population estimands under the ITT principle are
\text{ITT}^{fp}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{ij}Y(1,D_{ij})-Y(0,D_{ij})
and
\text{ITT}^{sp}=E_{sp}[Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})-Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})]
,
where the expectation for
\text{ITT}^{sp}
is taken over the super-population.

Principal stratification estimands can be used to estimate the effects of receiving the intervention among certain sub-populations of participants.
(
Zhai and Gutman 2020
)
defined a set of principal strata estimands for a pragmatic CRCT using a scalar compliance measure at the cluster-level and a binary compliance indicator at the unit level. These estimands are based on principle strata that comprise of a discretized cluster-level continuous compliance metric, and within cluster-level compliance strata they include individual-level compliance strata.

With multiple continuous compliance metrics, we define principal strata estimands that stratify individuals by their cluster’s latent compliance class,
S_{i}
, and their individual compliance status,
D_{ij}
. A possible set of estimands describe the ITT effect among individuals in clusters that belong to latent compliance strata
k
are
\text{ITT}_{k}^{fp}=\frac{\sum_{ij:S_{i}=k}Y(1,D_{ij})-Y(0,D_{ij})}{\sum_{i}n_{i}*I(S_{i}=1)}
and
\text{ITT}_{k}^{sp}=E_{sp}[Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})-Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})|S_{i}=k]
, where
I(S_{i}=k)
is an indicator function that equals 1 if cluster
i
belongs to latent compliance strata
k
, and 0 otherwise.
A different estimand is the CACE, which describes the effects of the intervention among compliers regardless of their cluster’s compliance strata. This estimand is given by
\text{CACE}^{fp}=\frac{\sum_{ij:D_{ij}=1}Y(1,D_{ij})-Y(0,D_{ij})}{\sum_{ij}I(D_{ij}=1)}
and
\text{CACE}^{sp}=E_{sp}[Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})-Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})|D_{ij}=1]
.
Additionally, we can define the CACE within each latent compliance strata by

\text{CACE}_{k}^{fp}=\frac{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ij:\scriptscriptstyle D_{ij}=1,\\
\scriptscriptstyle S_{i}=k\end{subarray}}Y(1,D_{ij})-Y(0,D_{ij})}{\sum_{ij}I(D_{ij}=1)*I(S_{i}=1)}

and
\text{CACE}_{k}^{sp}=E_{sp}[Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})-Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})|D_{ij}=1,S_{i}=k]
.

To compare the effects of an intervention between cluster latent compliance strata, we can estimate contrasts between the
\text{ITT}_{k}
estimands. For example,
\text{ITT}_{1}^{sp}-\text{ITT}_{2}^{sp}
estimates the difference in being randomized to the intervention in compliance strata 1 compared to the same effect in compliance strata 2. Similarly,
\text{CACE}_{1}^{sp}-\text{CACE}_{2}^{sp}
estimates the difference between cluster-level compliance strata among compliers.

3.3
Bayesian Latent Class Model

3.3.1
Distributional Assumptions

We use a Bayesian approach to estimate the effects of the intervention within principal strata. Model-based estimation is a principled approach to incorporate restrictions on the joint distribution of observed variables and it allows for the inclusion of covariates in a flexible manner
(
Imbens and Rubin 2016
)
. Bayesian approaches also enable the assessment of model fit using posterior predictive checks to ensure sensible model specifications
(
Mattei et al. 2013
)
. The joint distribution of the variables described in Section
3.1
is

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{Y}(1,\mathbf{D}),\mathbf{Y}(0,\mathbf{D}),\mathbf{D},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Z,}\mathbf{X},\mathbf{S})\propto

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ P(\mathbf{Y}(1,\mathbf{D}),\mathbf{Y}(0,\mathbf{D})|\mathbf{D},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{\mathbf{S}})P(\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{\mathbf{S}})P(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{S})P(\mathbf{W}|\mathbf{Z})P(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{S}).

(1)

In a PCRCT, cluster treatment assignment is controlled by the investigators and is decided prior to study initiation. Thus, it is independent from the compliance status and the potential outcomes. Because treatment assignment is performed at the cluster level, it is commonly also independent from individual-level characteristics:

P(\mathbf{W}|\mathbf{Y}(1,\mathbf{D}),\mathbf{Y}(0,\mathbf{D}),\mathbf{D},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X})=P(\mathbf{W}|\mathbf{Z}).

Units are considered exchangeable when the joint distribution of their potential outcomes is the same regardless of their ordering
(
Bernardo 1996
)
. Cluster-level assignment to treatment inform whether clusters’ potential compliance metrics are observed, but does not inform their values. Similarly, within clusters, individual-level covariates, compliance status and the potential outcomes are not informed by their assignment to treatment. Thus, we make the following partial exchangeability assumptions.

Assumption 1
: Exchangeability

(a)

The compliance strata allocation variables,
S_{i}
, are exchangeable. Assuming the prior
P(\theta_{S})
and appealing to De-Finetti’s theorem
(
de Finetti 1992
)
,
P(\mathbf{S})\propto\int\prod_{i=1}^{I^{fp}}P(S_{i}|\theta_{S})P(\theta_{S})d\theta_{S}
.

(b)

The cluster compliance metrics,
\mathbf{C}_{i}
, and the cluster-level characteristics,
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
, are exchangeable given compliance strata
S_{i}
. Assuming the prior distribution
P(\theta_{CZ})
and appealing to De-Finetti’s theorem, we have

P(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Z}|\mathbf{S})\propto\int\prod_{i=1}^{I^{fp}}P(\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{Z}_{i}|S_{i},\theta_{CZ})P(\theta_{CZ})d\theta_{CZ}

(c)

The individual compliance status,
\mathbf{D}_{ij}
, is partially exchangeable within clusters conditional on
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
,
\mathbf{C}_{i}
,
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
, and
\mathbf{S}_{i}
. Assuming prior distribution
P(\theta_{D})
and appealing to De-Finetti’s theorem we have

P(\mathbf{D}|\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{S})\propto\int\prod_{i=1}^{I^{fp}}\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}P(D_{ij}|\theta_{D},\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{Z}_{i},\mathbf{X}_{ij},S_{i})P(\theta_{D})d\theta_{D}.

(d)

The potential outcomes are partially exchangeable within clusters conditional on
D_{ij}
,
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
,
\mathbf{C}_{i}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
. Assuming prior distribution
P(\theta_{Y})
and appealing to De-Finetti’s theorem we have

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{Y}(1,\mathbf{D}),\mathbf{Y}(0,\mathbf{D})|\mathbf{D},\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{S})\propto

\displaystyle\ \ \int\prod_{i=1}^{I^{fp}}\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}P(Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij}),Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})|\theta_{Y},D_{ij},\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{Z}_{i},\mathbf{X}_{ij},S_{i})P(\theta_{Y})d\theta_{Y}.

A commonly made assumption in randomized trials with noncompliance is the exclusion restriction assumption
(
Angrist et al. 1996
)
, which rules-out the effects of the assignment on the outcome for non-compliers. We assume a weaker version of this assumption that requires it to hold in distribution.

Assumption 2
: Stochastic exclusion restriction for individuals that do not comply with the active intervention
(
Imbens and Rubin 2016
)
. Formally,
P(Y_{ij}(1,0))=P(Y_{ij}(0,0))
.

This assumption is reasonable in PCRCTs with embedded interventions because the assignment of cluster to the active intervention should only influencee units’ outcomes if they receive the intervention.

We rely on three additional assumptions to simplify the parametric modeling of the joint distributions:

Assumption 3
: Conditional independence of individual compliance and potential outcomes from cluster’s characteristics. Formally,
P(D_{ij}|\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{Z}_{i},S_{i},\mathbf{X}_{ij},\theta_{D})=P(D_{ij}|S_{i},\mathbf{X}_{ij},\theta_{D})
and

P(Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij}),Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})|\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{Z}_{i},S_{i},D_{ij},\mathbf{X}_{ij},\theta_{Y})=P(Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij}),Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})|S_{i},D_{ij},\mathbf{X}_{ij},\theta_{Y}).

This assumption can be relaxed, but the latent cluster-level compliance is expected to encapsulate the cluster-level characteristics that influence individual-level compliance.

Assumption 4
: No contamination of imputation across treatments
(
Rubin 2008
)
. Formally,

Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})\perp Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})\ |\ S_{i},D_{ij},\mathbf{X}_{ij},\theta_{Y}
.

This assumption is commonly made by causal inference methods because the observed data does not include information on the relationship between the potential outcomes for the same individual
(
Hirano et al. 2000
;
Imbens and Rubin 2016
)
. When the units in a study are a random sample from a super-population, and the estimand is the average difference in
this super-population, this assumption has little inferential impact.
(
Hirano et al. 2000
;
Imbens and Rubin 2016
;
Rubin 1978
;
Imbens and Rubin 1997
)
.

Assumption 5
: Conditional independence of individual-level characteristics from cluster-level characteristics and compliance. Formally,
P(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{S})=P(\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{S}).

This assumption entails that given the latent compliance strata, individual-level populations do not depend on specific cluster-level characteristics or compliance metrics.

3.3.2
Parametric Model Specifications

Our estimation approach models the relationships between the outcomes, compliance status, cluster level covariates and unit level covariates under the assumptions in Section
3.3.1
. Because the compliance strata of facilities are unknown, we assume the joint distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{ij},\mathbf{C}_{i}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{i}
follows a
K
component mixture distribution, with allocation variable,
S_{i}
, indicating the compliance stratum of cluster
i
:

P(\mathbf{X}_{i},\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{Z}_{i},S_{i}|\theta_{S^{*}})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}P_{k}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})^{I(S_{i}=k)}\right]\text{MVN}(\mathbf{C}_{i},\mathbf{Z}_{i}|\boldsymbol{\mu}^{S}_{k},\Sigma)^{I(S_{i}=k)}\prod_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}^{I(S_{i}=k)},

(2)

where
\theta_{S^{*}}=\{\theta_{S},\theta_{CZ},\theta_{X}\}
, and MVN is the Multivariate Normal density function with mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{S}_{k}=\left\{\mu_{k}^{C_{1}},\ldots,\mu_{k}^{C_{\ell}},\mu_{k}^{Z_{1}},\ldots,\mu_{k}^{Z_{P}}\right\}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
.
P_{k}
is the distribution of individual-level covariates in mixture component
k
and
\pi_{k}
is the probability that a cluster belongs to latent compliance strata
k
. We define the set of parameters across all clusters as
\theta_{S}=\{\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{K}\}
and
\theta_{CZ}=\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{S}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{S}_{K},\Sigma\}
. The distribution of individual-level characteristics,
P_{k}
, can be modeled as either the empirical distribution across all observed individual-level covariate vectors,
\hat{F}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})
, or the empirical distribution across observed covariate vectors for individuals in clusters that belong to latent compliance strata
k
,
\hat{F}_{k}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})
. When the baseline covariates of trial participants are systematically different based on their cluster’s latent compliance strata,
\hat{F}_{k}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})
is preferred.

We model the conditional distribution of individual-level binary compliance indicators as

P(D_{ij}|S_{i},\mathbf{X}_{ij},\theta_{D})=\\
\prod_{k=1}^{K}[\text{probit}^{-1}(\mu_{k}^{D}+\mathbf{X}_{ij}\alpha_{k}+\phi_{i}^{D})^{D_{ij}}(1-\text{probit}^{-1}(\mu_{k}^{D}+\mathbf{X}_{ij}\alpha_{k}+\phi_{i}^{D}))^{1-D_{ij}}]^{I(S_{i}=k)},

(3)

where
\theta_{D}=\{\theta_{D_{1}},...,\theta_{D_{K}},\phi^{D}\}
,
\theta_{D_{k}}=\{\mu_{k}^{D},\alpha_{k}\}
and
\{\phi_{i}^{D}\}
are cluster-level effects. The conditional distribution for the potential outcomes is modeled as

\displaystyle P(Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij}),Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})|S_{i},D_{ij},\mathbf{X}_{ij},\theta_{Y})=

\displaystyle P(Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})|S_{i},D_{ij},\mathbf{X}_{ij},\theta_{Y})P(Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})|S_{i},D_{ij},\mathbf{X}_{ij},\theta_{Y})=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{K}[N(Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})|\mu_{k}^{Y}+\mathbf{X}_{ij}\beta_{0,k}+D_{ij}*(\delta_{1,k}+\mathbf{X}_{ij}\beta_{1,k})+\phi_{i}^{Y},\sigma_{k}^{2})\times

\displaystyle\ \ \ N(Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})|\mu_{k}^{Y}+\mathbf{X}_{ij}\beta_{0,k}+D_{ij}*\delta_{0,k}+\phi_{i}^{Y},\sigma_{k}^{2})]^{I(S_{i}=k)},

(4)

where
\theta_{Y}=\{\theta_{Y_{1}},\dots,\theta_{Y_{K}}\,,\phi^{Y}\}
,
\theta_{Y_{k}}=\{\mu_{k}^{Y},\beta_{1,k},\beta_{0,k},\delta_{1,k},\delta_{0,k},\sigma^{2}_{k}\}
and
\phi^{Y}=\{\phi^{Y}_{i}\}
are cluster-specific effects. For individuals in clusters that belong to latent compliance strata
k
,
\delta_{0,k}
represents the conditional difference in expected outcomes between compliers and noncompliers if assigned to control. Similarly,
\delta_{1,k}
represents the conditional difference in expected outcomes for compliers and noncompliers when assigned to the intervention. The vector-parameter
\beta_{1,k}
represents the interaction effects on the expected outcomes between individual-level compliance status and the individual’s observed covariates. We assume that
P(\phi_{i}^{D}|\tau_{D}^{2})=N(\phi_{i}^{D}|0,\tau_{D}^{2})
and
P(\phi_{i}^{Y}|\tau_{Y}^{2})=N(\phi_{i}^{Y}|0,\tau_{Y}^{2})
, which implies a common variance across the latent compliance strata. The vector parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\{\theta_{Y},\theta_{D},\theta_{S},\theta_{CZ},\theta_{X},\tau_{Y}^{2},\tau_{D}^{2}\}
comprises the set of parameters that define the distributions of all observable variables.

3.3.3
Estimation Procedures

A Bayesian approach for estimating finite-sample estimands,
\gamma^{fp}
, and super-population estimands,
\gamma^{sp}
, conditions on the observed data to calculate the posterior distribution,
P(\gamma|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W})
.
We describe one procedure to estimate the posterior distribution of
\gamma^{fp}
, and two procedures to estimate the posterior distribution of
\gamma^{sp}
under the assumptions in Sections
3.3.1
and
3.3.2
.

Procedure 1:
Finite-sample estimands are defined as functions of the observed data, missing data, and treatment assignment indicator. For example,

\text{ITT}^{fp}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{ij}Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})-Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})=\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{ij:W_{i}=1}\left[Y_{ij}^{obs}-Y_{ij}^{mis}\right]+\sum_{ij:W_{i}=0}\left[Y_{ij}^{mis}-Y_{ij}^{obs}\right]\right).

The posterior distribution of
\gamma^{fp}
is

\displaystyle P(\gamma^{fp}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W})

\displaystyle=\int\int\int\int P(\gamma^{fp}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Y}^{mis},\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{S},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W})\times

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ P(\mathbf{Y}^{mis},\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W})d\mathbf{Y}^{mis}d\mathbf{D}^{mis}d\mathbf{C}^{mis}d\mathbf{S}

\displaystyle=\int\int\int\int P(\gamma^{fp}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Y}^{mis},\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{S},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W})\times

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ P(\mathbf{Y}^{mis},\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X})d\mathbf{Y}^{mis}d\mathbf{D}^{mis}d\mathbf{C}^{mis}d\mathbf{S}.

(5)

Equation (
3.3.3
) shows that to estimate
\gamma^{fp}
we need to estimate
P(\mathbf{Y}^{mis},\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X})
because
P(\gamma^{fp}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Y}^{mis},\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{S},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{W})
is a degenerate distribution. Conditioning on
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,

\displaystyle P(\mathbf{Y}^{mis},\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X})=

\displaystyle\int_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}P(\mathbf{Y}^{mis},\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X})d\boldsymbol{\theta}

(6)

The posterior distribution,
P(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X})
, does not have an analytical form, so we developed a Gibbs sampling algorithm
(
Geman and Geman 1984
;
Imbens and Rubin 1997
)
to obtain posterior draws. Based on Equation (
3.3.3
), the algorithm for estimating
\gamma^{fp}
is

1.

Select initial values:
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}=\{\theta_{D}^{(0)},\theta_{Y}^{(0)},\theta_{S}^{(0)},\tau_{Y}^{2(0)},\tau_{D}^{2(0)}\}
.

2.

For
m
in
1,\dots,M
:

a)

Sample
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(m)}\sim(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X})
using the sampling procedure outlined in Appendix Section A1.

b)

Given
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(m)}
, draw

\{\mathbf{Y}^{mis(m)},\mathbf{D}^{mis(m)},\mathbf{C}^{mis(m)},\mathbf{S}\}
from

{P(\mathbf{Y}^{mis},\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(m)})}
using the procedure outlined in Appendix Section A2.

c)

Calculate
\hat{\gamma}^{fp(m)}
as a function of the observed values and
\{\mathbf{Y}^{mis(m)},\mathbf{D}^{mis(m)},\mathbf{C}^{mis(m)},\mathbf{S}\}
.

3.

Posterior intervals are obtained using percentiles of the posterior samples
\{\hat{\gamma}^{fp(1)},\dots,\hat{\gamma}^{fp(M)}\}
.

Procedure 3
: Some super-population estimands cannot be derived analytically as a function of model parameters. A different approach that approximates the posterior distribution of

\displaystyle\Omega_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(m)}}

4
Simulated Case Studies

We examine the operating characteristics of the proposed method using four simulated case studies. The first case study generates data according to the Bayesian models described in Section
3.3.2
, and case studies 2, 3 and 4 misspecifiy different parts of the model. The simulations consist of
I^{fp}=60
clusters that are partitioned into two latent compliance strata. Half of the
I^{fp}
clusters are assigned to the active intervention and half are assigned to the control condition. Each cluster comprises 20 individuals whose baseline covariates are resident age and Activities of Daily Living Scale (ADL)
(
Graf 2008
)
. The covariates’ values are randomly selected from stage 1 of METRIcAL. We estimate the posterior distributions of the super-population estimands described in Section
3.2
for each case study to examine the method’s performance and sensitivity to different misspecifications.

Cluster-level compliance metrics and characteristics are generated from a two-class Multivariate-Normal mixture distribution with equal probability of a cluster being in each class,

When the data generating mechanism for the potential outcomes includes interaction terms (Case Study 4), the method produces interval coverage that is slightly below nominal for
\text{CACE}^{sp}
, and the biases are larger than Case Study 1. However, the coverage of the posterior credible intervals for all estimands exceeds 0.90, with interval widths and RMSEs that are similar to Case Study 1.

5
Application to METRIcAL data

We apply the proposed method to METRIcAL and estimate the effects of the personalized music intervention on residents’ change in CMAI and antipsychotic usage from baseline to 4-month follow-up. We assume that NHs belong to one of two latent compliance strata (
K=2
) and consider one cluster-level characteristic and one cluster-level compliance metric. The cluster-level characteristic is the average daily census, which indicates the average number of residents who lived in a nursing home during the previous year. The cluster-level compliance metric is the clinical proportion which summarizes the proportion of enrolled residents who were identified by NH-staff on a pre-treatment survey as individuals whose behaviors they intended to target with the personalized music intervention. Appendix Table A3 describes the distributions of the average daily census and the clinical proportion for NHs assigned to the personalized music intervention. A NH resident is deemed a complier if they used the personalized music for at least 30 minutes a day on average during the 4 months after initiation. This was the target dosage described in the trial protocols
(
McCreedy et al. 2022
)
. Baseline covariates consisted of MDS variables and CMAI recorded at baseline (Table A2). We assume that

which allows for the distribution of individual-level covariates to differ between the latent compliance strata.

Posterior Predictive Checks

We implement a series of posterior predictive checks
(
Gelman et al. 1996
;
Rubin 1984
)
that use predictive discrepancy measures that were proposed by
Barnard et al. 2003
and implemented by
Mattei et al. 2013
to assess model fit. Following the notation in
Mattei et al. 2013
, let
\mathcal{D}_{w,k}^{\text{study}}=\{ij:S_{i}=k,D_{ij}=1\}
comprise the set individual-level compliers in the study data whose NH facilities are in compliance strata
k
and were assigned to intervention group
w
. Let
N^{\text{study}}_{w,k}
denote the number of individuals in
\mathcal{D}_{w,k}^{\text{study}}
, and let
\bar{Y}(w)^{\text{study}}_{k}
and
s^{2,\text{study}}_{w,k}
denote the sample mean and variance of the potential outcomes for individuals in
\mathcal{D}_{w,k}^{\text{study}}
, respectively. Possible posterior discrepancy measures are
SI_{k}^{\text{study}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\left|\bar{Y}(1)_{k}^{\text{study}}-\bar{Y}(0)_{k}^{\text{study}}\right|
,
NO_{k}^{\text{study}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\sqrt{\frac{s_{1,k}^{2,\text{study}}}{N_{1,k}^{\text{study}}}+\frac{s_{0,k}^{2,\text{study}}}{N_{0,k}^{\text{study}}}}

and
SINO_{k}^{\text{study}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=SI_{k}^{\text{study}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})/NO_{k}^{\text{study}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. These discrepancy measures assess broad features of signal, noise, and signal-to-noise
ratio within each estimated latent compliance strata
(
Barnard et al. 2003
)
.
Let
T^{\text{rep}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
denote the value of a discrepancy measure calculated on the posterior predictive distribution of the latent compliance strata, individual compliance, and potential outcomes using the posterior draws of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. Posterior predictive
p
-values (PPPV) assess the probability that
T^{\text{rep}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
would be as or more extreme as the observed statistic, denoted by
T^{\text{obs}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Formally,
\text{PPPV}=Pr(T^{\text{rep}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})>T^{\text{obs}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X})
. PPPVs that are close to 1 or 0 indicate that the model is not able to replicate
SI_{k}^{study}(\theta),NO_{k}^{study}(\theta)
and
SINO_{k}^{study}
that are observed in the study. Such values suggest that the proposed model has unintended influence on the estimated values of the estimands
(
Gelman et al. 1996
;
Rubin 1984
)
. The PPPVs for all discrepancy measures across latent compliance strata and potential outcomes are between 0.34 and 0.67 (Table

6
Discussion

We propose a Bayesian method to estimate the effects of interventions in pragmatic CRCTs when the randomization units are healthcare providers, and both the providers and individual levels are subject to one-sided noncompliance. This method defines a mixture model that classifies providers into compliance strata based on their characteristics and continuous compliance metrics. Binary individual-level compliance status is estimated using a probit regression model in the intervention arm. Using these compliance models we impute the
unobserved compliance metrics for providers assigned to control and binary compliance status for individuals receiving care from these providers. Using the imputed compliance status of clusters and individuals, we can estimate the effects of the intervention among individuals that comply with their assigned intervention within providers with similar levels of partial compliance.

In simulated case studies we display the method’s sensitivity to different data generating mechanisms. When the model is correctly specifies the estimates are relatively accurate and precise, which leads to nominal coverage. The procedure was also robust to misspecified data generating mechanisms, with approximate nominal coverage.

We apply the proposed Bayesian method to METRIcAL, a pragmatic CRCT that examines the effects of a personalized music intervention on the behaviors of nursing home residents with dementia in 54 nursing homes in the United States. We identified two latent compliance groups among NHs. The first group included lower-occupancy NHs with high proportions of individuals being targeted with the intervention. The second group included higher-occupancy NHs with a smaller proportions of individuals being targeted with the intervention. Point estimates suggest that, on average, residents in the intervention group had smaller reductions in CMAI scores from baseline compared to those receiving standard care. However, they also had lower estimated rates of antipsychotic use at four-month follow-up. Within NH compliance strata, higher-compliance NHs experienced an average decrease in CMAI scores, while lower-compliance NHs showed an increase. Among higher-compliance NHs, those receiving standard of care had a greater average CMAI reduction than those in the intervention group. In lower-compliance NHs, those receiving standard of care had a smaller increase than those in the intervention arm. In both compliance groups, the intervention arm showed a reduction in antipsychotic use, with a greater reduction in higher-compliance NHs. Similar trends were observed at the individual level, but the effects were more pronounced. Compliers were estimated to have a larger improvement in CMAI scores and a greater reduction in antipsychotic use compared to the overall population. The effects of the intervention on CMAI and antipsychotic use among NH in the higher compliance strata and those in the lower compliance strata are more pronounced among compliers then among the entire population.

Although none of the results were statistically significant at the 5% nominal level, the point estimates suggest an interesting trend. While the intervention was designed to reduce CMAI scores, our analysis indicates a smaller reduction in the intervention group than under the standard care. However, antipsychotic use was lower in the intervention group. Among higher-compliance facilities, the intervention was associated with a greater reduction in CMAI scores than in lower-compliance facilities, although this reduction was still smaller than the one in the standard care group. Estimated reductions in antipsychotic use were also larger in higher-compliance than in lower-compliance facilities. Lastly, compliers within high-compliance facilities experienced a greater reduction in antipsychotic use compared to the overall population and a notable decrease in CMAI scores, although this reduction in CMAI was still smaller than the reduction observed for compliers in standard care group.

A possible explanation for these findings is that in the standard care arm, individuals best suited for the intervention may have received antipsychotic medications to manage aggressive behaviors, while similar individuals in the intervention arm discontinued antipsychotic use. These medications may have a stronger effect on reducing CMAI scores than the personalized music intervention, which may lead to larger reductions or smaller increases in CMAI under standard of care. However, the intervention’s relative effectiveness appears to improve when both facilities and individuals comply. Because the results in METRIcAL are inconclusive, future research on music interventions for nursing home residents with ADRD should consider two key factors. First, potential adjustments to the intervention may be necessary to improve compliance. Second, while these interventions may not reduce aggressive behaviors as effectively as antipsychotic medications, they may offer a safer alternative because antipsychotic use has been linked to severe side effects among nursing home residents
(
Huybrechts et al. 2012
;
Chiu et al. 2015
;
Liperoti et al. 2017
)
.

In conclusion, the proposed Bayesian procedure provides a new methodology to analyze pragmatic CRCTs when the cluster and individual-levels contribute to noncompliance with the intervention. The procedure estimates the effects of the intervention among clusters with similar compliance and baseline characteristics, as well as among individuals who comply with the intervention when it is offered to them. We applied the method to a trial that examined the effects of personalized music intervention, and were able to obtain insights that were not observed otherwise. A potential extension would examine models that incorporate individual-level partial compliance.

We describe a Gibbs sampling algorithm to sample from the joint posterior
distribution of the parameters in the parametric model defined in
Section
3.3.2

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}

\displaystyle=\left\{\boldsymbol{\mu}^{S}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{S}_{K},\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{K},\Sigma,\mu_{1}^{D},\dots,\mu_{K}^{D},\alpha_{1}^{D},...,\alpha_{K}^{D},\tau_{D}^{2},\phi^{D},\right.

3.3.1
, we have,

P(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X})=\int\int\int P(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X})d\mathbf{D}^{mis}d\mathbf{C}^{mis}d\mathbf{S}

(15)

where
\mathbf{S}

\mathbf{D}^{mis}
and
\mathbf{C}^{mis}
are the unobserved compliance values for individuals and clusters assigned to the control. The term inside the integral in Equation (
15
) is the posterior distribution for
\boldsymbol{\theta}
augmented with
\mathbf{S}
,
\mathbf{C}^{mis}
and
\mathbf{D}^{mis}
.
This distribution is given by

\displaystyle P(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{Y}^{obs},\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle\propto P(\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\mathbf{Y}^{obs}|\mathbf{D}^{obs},\mathbf{X},\mathbf{S},\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\mathbf{D}^{mis},\mathbf{D}^{obs}|\mathbf{\mathbf{X}},\mathbf{S},\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\mathbf{C}^{mis},\mathbf{C}^{obs},\mathbf{Z}|\mathbf{S},\boldsymbol{\theta})P(\mathbf{S}|\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle\propto P(\boldsymbol{\theta})\prod_{k=1}^{K}\prod_{i:W_{i}=1}\prod_{j=1}^{n_{i}}N(Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})|\mu_{k}^{Y}+\mathbf{X}_{ij}\beta_{0,k}+D_{ij}*(\delta_{1,k}+\mathbf{X}_{ij}\beta_{1,k})+\phi_{i}^{Y},\sigma_{k}^{2})]^{I(S_{i}=k)}\times

Albert and Chib 1993

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{E_{sp}\left[D_{ij}|S_{i}=k\right]}\int_{\mathbf{X}_{ij}}\mathbf{X}_{ij}p_{ijk}\hat{F}_{k}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})d\mathbf{X}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{E_{sp}\left[D_{ij}|S_{i}=k\right]}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\mathbf{X}_{ij}p_{ijk},

\displaystyle E_{sp}\left[D_{ij}|S_{i}=k\right]

\displaystyle=E_{sp}\left[E_{sp}\left[D_{ij}|S_{i}=k,\mathbf{X}_{ij}\right]|S_{i}=k\right]

\displaystyle=E_{sp}\left[\text{probit}^{-1}\left(\left(\mathbf{X}_{ij}\alpha_{k}+\mu_{k}^{D}\right)/\sqrt{\tau_{D,k}^{2}+1}\right)|S_{i}=k\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\text{probit}^{-1}\left(\left(\mathbf{X}_{ij}\alpha_{k}+\mu_{k}^{D}\right)/\sqrt{\tau_{D,k}^{2}+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}.

Also,

\displaystyle E_{sp}\left[I(S_{i}=k)|D_{ij}=1\right]

\displaystyle=Pr(S_{i}=k|D_{ij}=1)

\displaystyle=\frac{P(D_{ij}=1|S_{i}=k)P(S_{i}=k)}{P(D_{ij}=1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}E_{sp}\left[D_{ij}|S_{i}=k\right]}{E_{sp}\left[D_{ij}\right]}

From previous derivation

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}}{E_{sp}\left[D_{ij}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}}{E_{sp}\left[E_{sp}\left[D_{ij}|S_{i}\right]\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}}{E_{sp}\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{S_{i}}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{S_{i}}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}}{E_{sp}\left[\sum_{s}I(S_{i}=s)\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijs}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}}{\sum_{s}\pi_{s}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{s}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijs}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle E_{sp}[Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})|D_{ij}=1]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}E_{sp}\left[I(S_{i}=k)|D_{ij}=1\right]\left(\mu_{k}^{Y}+E_{sp}\left[\mathbf{X}_{ij}|S_{i}=k,D_{ij}=1\right]\left(\beta_{0,k}+\beta_{1,k}\right)+\delta_{1,k}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}\left(\mu_{k}^{Y}+\frac{\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}\mathbf{X}_{ij}(\beta_{0,k}+\beta_{1,k})}{\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}}+\delta_{1,k}\right)}{\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}},

and

\displaystyle E_{sp}[Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})|D_{ij}=1]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}\left(\mu_{k}^{Y}+\frac{\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}\mathbf{X}_{ij}}{\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}}\beta_{0,k}+\delta_{0,k}\right)}{\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle E_{sp}[Y_{ij}(1,D_{ij})-Y_{ij}(0,D_{ij})|D_{ij}=1]

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}\left(\frac{\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\beta_{1,k}}{\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}}+\delta_{k}\right)}{\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\frac{1}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}n_{i}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}p_{ijk}}.
