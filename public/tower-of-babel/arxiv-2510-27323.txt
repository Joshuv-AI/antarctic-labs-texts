Abstract
. This paper establishes the strong convergence of solutions to stochastic differential equations (SDEs) and Volterra-type SDEs when approximated by compound Poisson processes. An explicit rate of convergence is derived. A key advantage of the compound Poisson approach over the classical Euler–Maruyama method is that it does not require the drift coefficient to be continuous in the time variable and can even accommodate singularities. Numerical experiments demonstrate the stability of our approach. Keywords : Compound Poisson approximation, Stochastic Volterra equation, Strong convergence



1.
Introduction and main results

Consider the stochastic differential equation (SDE) on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
:

\displaystyle X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}b(s,X_{s})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

(1.1)

where
\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and
b:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are Borel-measurable functions, and
W_{t}
is an
m
-dimensional standard Brownian motion defined on a complete filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0},\mathbb{P})
. We impose the following standard Lipschitz conditions on
\sigma
and
b
:

(H
0
)

There exists a function
\ell_{b}\in\cap_{T>0}L^{2}([0,T])
such that for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|b(t,x)-b(t,y)|}{|x-y|}+\sup_{x}\frac{|b(t,x)|}{1+|x|}\leqslant\ell_{b}(t),

(1.2)

where
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and there is a constant
\kappa_{\sigma}>0
, such that for all
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{\|\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(t,y)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}}{|x-y|}+\sup_{x}\frac{\|\sigma(t,x)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}}{1+|x|}\leqslant\kappa_{\sigma},

(1.3)

where
\|\sigma\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|\sigma_{ij}|^{2}
.

Under
(H
0
)
, it is well known that there is
a unique strong solution to the SDE (
1.1
); see, for instance,
[
11
]
.

To numerically approximate solutions of SDEs of the form (
1.1
), the Euler–Maruyama (EM) method is one of the most fundamental and widely used schemes. Given a deterministic time discretization
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\cdots
, the EM method constructs an approximate solution
\{Y_{t_{k}}\}
recursively: starting from
Y_{t_{0}}=X_{0}
, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle Y_{t_{k+1}}=Y_{t_{k}}+b(t_{k},Y_{t_{k}})(t_{k+1}-t_{k})+\sigma(t_{k},Y_{t_{k}})(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_{k}}).

This discretization extends the classical Euler method to the stochastic setting by approximating the Itô integral via Brownian increments.

The strong convergence of the EM method has been extensively studied. Under standard assumptions—namely, global Lipschitz continuity of the drift
b
and diffusion
\sigma
in space, along with
1/2
-Hölder continuity in time—the method achieves a strong convergence rate of
1/2
(see
[
8
]
). More recent work, such as
[
15
]
, has extended these results to weaker spatial regularity, while still requiring some temporal Hölder continuity of the coefficients.

Strong convergence has also been established under relaxed conditions on the drift. For example,
[
5
]
proved convergence under a one-sided Lipschitz condition for the drift, albeit with additional boundedness assumptions on the moments of the numerical solution. However, when the coefficients exhibit superlinear growth (e.g., polynomial or exponential), the classical EM scheme may fail to converge in
L^{p}
, as shown by counterexamples in
[
6
]
. This divergence has motivated the development of modified schemes. To address this issue,
[
7
]
introduced the tamed Euler–Maruyama method, which modifies the coefficients to control their growth and ensure strong convergence under non-globally Lipschitz conditions. This approach was further refined by
[
12
]
, who extended the taming framework to a broader class of SDEs with polynomial growth coefficients.
In addition to strong convergence, other convergence modes have been analyzed:
[
1
]
investigated weak convergence, while
[
4
]
established almost sure convergence and convergence in probability under suitable regularity assumptions.
Despite its simplicity, the convergence and stability of the EM method critically depend on the regularity and growth of the coefficients, motivating the use of enhanced schemes in practical applications involving stiff, non-globally Lipschitz, or highly nonlinear dynamics.

The compound Poisson approximation was introduced in
[
20
]
, where, under relatively weak assumptions, the author established weak convergence and convergence of invariant measures, and applied the framework to stochastic Navier–Stokes/Euler equations and distribution-dependent SDEs. However, strong convergence was proven only for ordinary differential equations (ODEs) of the form

\displaystyle X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(s,X_{s})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

In this paper, we extend the strong convergence analysis to the stochastic setting. Specifically, we prove strong convergence of the compound Poisson approximation for the SDE (
1.1
) and the stochastic Volterra equation (
1.7
), and we establish explicit convergence rates in both cases.

We now introduce the compound Poisson approximation for SDEs. Let
(T_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with a common exponential distribution of parameter 1, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}(T_{k}\geqslant t)=e^{-t},\quad t\geqslant 0,\quad k=1,2,\dots.

Define
S_{0}\equiv 0
, and for
n\geqslant 1
,

S_{n}:=S_{n-1}+T_{n},

and for
t\geqslant 0
,

{\mathcal{N}}_{t}:=\max\{n:S_{n}\leqslant t\}.

Then
{\mathcal{N}}_{t}
is a standard Poisson process with intensity 1, and
S_{n}
represents its jump times. Note that

{\mathbb{E}}T_{k}=1,\quad{\mathbb{E}}{\mathcal{N}}_{t}=t,\quad{\mathbb{E}}({\mathcal{N}}_{t}-t)^{2}=t.

For any given
\varepsilon>0
, define

{\mathcal{N}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon{\mathcal{N}}_{t/\varepsilon},\quad\widetilde{{\mathcal{N}}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}:={\mathcal{N}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}-t.

Then
{\mathcal{N}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}
is a Poisson process with jump size
\varepsilon
and intensity
1/\varepsilon
, and we have

{\mathcal{N}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\max\{n:S_{n}^{\varepsilon}\leqslant t\},

where
S_{n}^{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon S_{n}
and
T_{n}^{\varepsilon}:=S_{n}^{\varepsilon}-S_{n-1}^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon T_{n}
. Furthermore,
\widetilde{{\mathcal{N}}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}
is a martingale.

Note that

t\mapsto W_{{\mathcal{N}}^{\varepsilon}_{t}}=\sum_{k=1}^{{\mathcal{N}}_{t/\varepsilon}}(W_{k\varepsilon}-W_{(k-1)\varepsilon}).

Thus,
(W_{{\mathcal{N}}^{\varepsilon}_{t}})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a compound Poisson process with intensity measure
\nu_{\varepsilon}\sim N(0,\varepsilon I_{d})
, where
N(0,\varepsilon I_{d})
denotes the
d
-dimensional normal distribution with covariance matrix
\varepsilon I_{d}
; more precisely,

\displaystyle\nu_{\varepsilon}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)=(2\pi\varepsilon)^{-d/2}e^{-|z|^{2}/(2\varepsilon)}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z.

(1.4)

We now consider the following compound Poisson approximation of (
1.1
):

\displaystyle X_{t}^{\varepsilon}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s-}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{{\mathcal{N}}_{s}^{\varepsilon}}+\int_{0}^{t}b(s,X_{s-}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\mathcal{N}}_{s}^{\varepsilon}.

(1.5)

This equation can be written in fully discrete form as

X_{t}^{\varepsilon}=X_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{{\mathcal{N}}_{t/\varepsilon}}\left[\sigma(S^{\varepsilon}_{k},X_{S^{\varepsilon}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})(W_{k\varepsilon}-W_{(k-1)\varepsilon})+\varepsilon b(S^{\varepsilon}_{k},X_{S^{\varepsilon}_{k-1}}^{\varepsilon})\right].

This scheme can be viewed as a
random
time discretization method that can be readily implemented as a computer algorithm.

To state our main result, we impose the following continuity assumption on the time variable for
\sigma
.

(H
{}^{t}_{\sigma}
)

There exist constants
\alpha,\beta\in(0,1]
and
\kappa_{\tau}>0
such that for all
t,s\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\|\sigma(t,x)-\sigma(s,x)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}\leqslant\kappa_{\tau}|t^{\alpha}-s^{\alpha}|^{\beta}(1+|x|^{2}).

Our first main result establishes the strong convergence of the compound Poisson approximation for SDEs and provides the optimal convergence rate.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that
(H
0
)
and
(H
{}^{t}_{\sigma}
)
hold, and that the function
\ell_{b}
in (
1.2
) satisfies, for some
\gamma\in(0,1]
,

\sup_{0\leqslant s<t\leqslant T}\left(\frac{\int_{s}^{t}\ell_{b}(r)\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r}{(t-s)^{\gamma}}\right)\leqslant C_{T},\quad\forall T>0.

(1.6)

Then for any
T>0
and
X_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X_{t}^{\varepsilon}-X_{t}|^{2}\right)\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\gamma\wedge\frac{\beta}{2}}.

Remark 1.2
.

Suppose that for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}>0
and
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

|h(\theta,x)-h(\theta^{\prime},y)|\leqslant C(|\theta-\theta^{\prime}|^{1/2}+|x-y|).

Then for any
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
\sigma(t,x):=h(t^{\alpha},x)
satisfies

(H
{}^{t}_{\sigma}
)
. In this case,

{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X_{t}^{\varepsilon}-X_{t}|^{2}\right)\leqslant C\varepsilon^{{\gamma\wedge}\frac{1}{2}}.

This convergence rate is optimal, since

{\mathbb{E}}|W_{{\mathcal{N}}^{\varepsilon}_{t}}-W_{t}|^{4}=3{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{N}}^{\varepsilon}_{t}-t|^{2}=3\varepsilon t.

Next, we consider the following stochastic Volterra equation (SVE) with time singular kernels:

\displaystyle Y_{t}=Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(t,s,Y_{s})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}b(t,s,Y_{s})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

(1.7)

We impose the following assumptions on
\sigma
and
b
.

(H
{}^{\gamma}_{1}
)

Suppose there exist functions
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that for all
0\leqslant s<t<\infty
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{\|\sigma(t,s,x)-\sigma(t,s,y)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}}{|x-y|^{2}}+\sup_{x}\frac{\|\sigma(t,s,x)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}}{1+|x|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ell_{1}(t,s),

(1.8)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|b(t,s,x)-b(t,s,y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{2}}+\sup_{x}\frac{|b(t,s,x)|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ell_{2}(t,s),

(1.9)

and for any
T>0
, there exist constants
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
C>0
such that for all
t\in(0,T]
and
s\in(0,t\wedge 1)
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(\ell_{1}(t,r)+\ell_{2}(t,r)\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\leqslant C,\ \int_{0}^{s}\ell_{1}(t,r){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+\int_{t-s}^{t}\ell_{1}(t,r){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\leqslant Cs^{\gamma}.

(1.10)

(H
{}^{\gamma}_{2}
)

Suppose there exist functions
\ell_{3},\ell_{4}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that for all
t,t^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
0\leqslant s<t\wedge t^{\prime}

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\frac{\|\sigma(t^{\prime},s,x)-\sigma(t,s,x)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}}{1+|x|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ell_{3}(t^{\prime},t,s),

(1.11)

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\frac{|b(t^{\prime},s,x)-b(t,s,x)|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\ell_{4}(t^{\prime},t,s),

(1.12)

and for any
T>0
, there exist constants
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
C>0
such that for all
0\leqslant t<t^{\prime}\leqslant T
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(\ell_{3}(t^{\prime},t,s)+\ell_{4}(t^{\prime},t,s)\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\leqslant C|t-t^{\prime}|^{\gamma}.

(1.13)

(H
{}^{\gamma}_{3}
)

Suppose there exists a function
\ell_{5}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that for all
0\leqslant s,s^{\prime}<t
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\frac{\|\sigma(t,s,x)-\sigma(t,s^{\prime},x)\|^{2}_{\mathrm{HS}}}{1+|x|^{2}}\leqslant\ell_{5}(t,s,s^{\prime}),

(1.14)

and for any
T>0
, there exist constants
\gamma\in(0,1]
and
C>0
such that for all
t\leqslant T
and
0<\delta<1\wedge t/3
,

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{t_{\delta}}\ell_{5}(t,s,s_{\delta})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\leqslant C\delta^{\gamma},

(1.15)

where we define for any
\delta>0
and
s\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
,

\displaystyle s_{\delta}:=k\delta,\quad s\in[k\delta,(k+1)\delta),\text{ for }k\geqslant 0.

(1.16)

Under
(H
{}^{\gamma}_{1}
)
and
(H
{}^{\gamma}_{2}
)
, it is well known that there exists a unique solution to the SVE (
1.7
) (see
[
16
]
,
[
2
]
, and
[
19
]
). Although there is extensive literature on Euler–Maruyama schemes for SVEs, most existing results concern the case of non-singular kernels (see
[

]

).

]

For SVEs, the compound Poisson approximation of (

) is defined as

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}=Y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(t,s,Y_{s-}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{{\mathcal{N}}_{s}^{\varepsilon}}+\int_{0}^{t}b(t,s,Y_{s-}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\mathcal{N}}_{s}^{\varepsilon}.

Note that this equation can be written in the following fully discrete form:

Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}=Y_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{{\mathcal{N}}_{t/\varepsilon}}\left[\sigma(t,S_{k}^{\varepsilon},Y_{S_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}})(W_{k\varepsilon}-W_{(k-1)\varepsilon})+\varepsilon b(t,S_{k}^{\varepsilon},Y_{S_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}}^{\varepsilon})\right].

Our second main result establishes the strong convergence of the compound Poisson approximation for SVEs and provides an explicit rate of convergence.

Theorem 1.3
.

Under assumptions
(H
{}^{\gamma}_{1}
)
,

{}^{\gamma}_{2}

, and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}-Y_{t}|^{2}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\gamma/(2(2+\gamma))}.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We begin by preparing two technical lemmas.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\beta\geqslant 0
, there exists a constant
C=C(\alpha,\beta)>0
such that for any
\varepsilon>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|r^{\alpha}-(S_{k}^{\varepsilon})^{\alpha}|^{\beta}\lesssim_{C}(k\varepsilon)^{\alpha\beta}k^{-\beta/2},\quad r\in[(k-1)\varepsilon,k\varepsilon].

(2.1)

Proof.

Recalling that
S^{\varepsilon}_{k}=\varepsilon S_{k}
, without loss of generality we may assume
\varepsilon=1
and
k\geqslant 2
.
Noting that for
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
a,b>0
,

|a^{\alpha}-b^{\alpha}|\leqslant a^{\alpha-1}|a-b|,

we obtain, for some
C=C(\alpha,\beta)>0
and any
r\in[k-1,k]
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|r^{\alpha}-(S_{k})^{\alpha}|^{\beta}\leqslant r^{(\alpha-1)\beta}{\mathbb{E}}|r-S_{k}|^{\beta}\leqslant Ck^{(\alpha-1)\beta}(1+{\mathbb{E}}|k-S_{k}|^{\beta}).

(2.2)

Since
S_{k}-k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(T_{i}-1)
and
(T_{i}-1)_{i=1,\cdots,k}
are zero-mean i.i.d. random variables,
by Burkholder’s inequality of discrete martingale, we have

{\mathbb{E}}|k-S_{k}|^{\beta}\leqslant\left({\mathbb{E}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}(T_{i}-1)\right|^{\beta\vee 2}\right)^{\beta/(\beta\vee 2)}\lesssim\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\mathbb{E}}|T_{i}-1|^{2}\right)^{\beta/2}\lesssim k^{\beta/2}.

Substituting this into (
2.2
) yields the desired estimate.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

If we were to directly use
|a^{\alpha}-b^{\alpha}|\leqslant|a-b|^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
a,b>0
,
then we would only obtain

{\mathbb{E}}|r^{\alpha}-(S_{k})^{\alpha}|^{\beta}\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}|r-S_{k}|^{\alpha\beta}\lesssim k^{\alpha\beta/2},

which gives a worse rate than
k^{\alpha\beta-\beta/2}
.

Next, we provide an estimate for the difference between
{\mathcal{N}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}
and
t
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
p>0
, there exists a constant
C=C(p)
such that for all
t\in(0,\infty)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{N}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}-t|^{p}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{p/2}(t^{p/2}\vee t).

(2.3)

Proof.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, define

a_{n}(t):={\mathbb{E}}({\mathcal{N}}_{t}-t)^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(k-t)^{n}\frac{t^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-t}}{k!}.

Differentiating with respect to
t
, we obtain

a^{\prime}_{n}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(-n(k-t)^{n-1}\frac{t^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-t}}{k!}+(k-t)^{n+1}\frac{t^{k-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}}{k!}\right)=-na_{n-1}(t)+\frac{a_{n+1}(t)}{t}.

Noting that

a_{1}(t)=0,\quad a_{2}(t)=t,

by induction we conclude that

a_{2n}(t)=c_{n}t^{n}+c_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+c_{1}t,

where
c_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus,

a_{2n}(t)={\mathbb{E}}({\mathcal{N}}_{t}-t)^{2n}\leqslant C(n)t^{n},\quad t\geqslant 1.

For any
p\in(0,2n]
, Hölder’s inequality gives

{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{N}}_{t}-t|^{p}\leqslant({\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{N}}_{t}-t|^{2n})^{p/2n}\leqslant C(p)t^{p/2},\quad t\geqslant 1.

On the other hand, for
t\in[0,1]
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{N}}_{t}-t|^{p}\leqslant 2^{p-1}({\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{N}}_{t}|^{p}+t^{p})\lesssim t+t^{p}\leqslant 2t.

Hence, for
p>0
,

{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{N}}_{t}-t|^{p}\lesssim t^{p/2}\vee t.

Since
{\mathcal{N}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon{\mathcal{N}}_{t/\varepsilon}
, we finally obtain

{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathcal{N}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}-t|^{p}\lesssim\varepsilon^{p}\left(\left(\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)^{p/2}\vee\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}\right)\lesssim\varepsilon^{p/2}(t^{p/2}\vee t).

The proof is complete.
∎

We first recall the following standard moment estimates (see
[
10
]
).

Lemma 2.4
.

Under
(H
0
)
, for any
T>0
, there is a constant
C=C(\ell_{\beta},\kappa_{\sigma},d,T)>0
such that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X_{t}|^{2}\right)\lesssim_{C}1+{\mathbb{E}}|X_{0}|^{2},

(2.4)

and there exists another constant
C=C(\ell_{\beta},\kappa_{\sigma},d,T,X_{0})>0
such that for all
s,t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|X_{t}-X_{s}|^{2}\lesssim_{C}|t-s|^{(2\gamma)\wedge 1}.

(2.5)

Proof.

By Itô’s isometry, Hölder’s inequality and
(H
0
)
, for any
t\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|X_{s}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim{\mathbb{E}}|X_{0}|^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int^{t}_{0}\ell_{b}(s)(1+|X_{s}|){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int^{t}_{0}\|\sigma(s,X_{s})\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}|X_{0}|^{2}+\left(\int^{t}_{0}\ell^{2}_{b}(s){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\kappa_{\sigma}^{2}\right)\int_{0}^{t}{\mathbb{E}}(1+|X_{s}|)^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Gronwall’s inequality yields (
2.4
).

Similarly, for (
2.5
), Itô’s isometry gives

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|X_{t}-X_{s}|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim{\mathbb{E}}\left|\int^{t}_{s}b(r,X_{r}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right|^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\left|\int^{t}_{s}\sigma(r,X_{r}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{r}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int^{t}_{s}\ell_{b}(r)(1+|X_{r}|){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int^{t}_{s}\|\sigma(r,X_{r})\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\left(\int^{t}_{s}\ell_{b}(r){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r\right)^{2}+\kappa_{\sigma}^{2}|t-s|\right){\mathbb{E}}\left(1+\sup_{r\in[s,t]}|X_{r}|\right)^{2},

which together with (
2.4
) and (
1.6
) gives (
2.5
).
∎

Next, we establish a uniform moment estimate, which is essentially standard.

Lemma 2.5
.

Under
(H
0
)
, for any
X_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a constant

C=C(\ell_{\beta},\kappa_{\sigma},d,T)>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)\lesssim_{C}1+{\mathbb{E}}|X_{0}|^{2}.

(2.6)

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{\varepsilon}
be the associated Poisson random measure of
W_{{\mathcal{N}}_{\cdot}^{\varepsilon}}
, that is, for
t>0
and
E\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\varepsilon}([0,t],E):=\sum_{s\leqslant t}\mathbf{1}_{E}(\Delta W_{{\mathcal{N}}_{s}^{\varepsilon}})=\sum_{k\leqslant{\mathcal{N}}_{t}^{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon}\mathbf{1}_{E}(W_{k\varepsilon}-W_{(k-1)\varepsilon}).

(2.7)

The compensated Poisson random measure
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\varepsilon}
is defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\varepsilon}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)={\mathcal{H}}^{\varepsilon}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)-{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t\,\nu_{\varepsilon}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)/\varepsilon.

(2.8)

In terms of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}^{\varepsilon}
, the SDE (
1.5
) can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle X_{t}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(\sigma(s,X_{s-}^{\varepsilon})z+\varepsilon b(s,X_{s-}^{\varepsilon})){\mathcal{H}}^{\varepsilon}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)

By Doob’s maximal inequality, Itô’s isometry, and
(H
0
)
, for any

, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim{\mathbb{E}}|X_{0}|^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|b(s,X_{s}^{\varepsilon})|{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}|\sigma(s,X_{s}^{\varepsilon})z+\varepsilon b(s,X_{s}^{\varepsilon})|^{2}\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)}{\varepsilon}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}(1+{\mathbb{E}}|X_{s}^{\varepsilon}|^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}(\kappa_{\sigma}^{2}|z|^{2}+\ell_{b}^{2}(s)\varepsilon^{2})\frac{\nu_{\varepsilon}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}z)}{\varepsilon}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

By (

Gronwall’s inequality then completes the proof.

To prove strong convergence, we make the following decomposition:

\displaystyle X_{t}-X_{t}^{\varepsilon}

,
{\mathscr{I}}_{2}(t)

{\mathscr{I}}_{3}(t)
, and
{\mathscr{I}}_{4}(t)

Lemma 2.6
.

Under
(H
0
)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|{\mathscr{I}}_{1}(t)|^{2}\right)

Lemma 2.7
.

Under
(H
0
)

(H
{}_{\sigma}^{t}

Thus, by the definition of

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}_{2}(T)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\mathbf{1}}_{((k-1)\varepsilon,k\varepsilon]}(s)(\sigma(s,X_{s\wedge{\mathcal{N}}_{s-}^{\varepsilon}})-\sigma(S_{k}^{\varepsilon},X_{S_{k}^{\varepsilon}\wedge((k-1)\varepsilon)})){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}

\sum_{k=k_{1}}^{k_{2}}a_{k}:=-\sum_{k=k_{2}}^{k_{1}}a_{k}.

For

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|{\mathscr{I}}_{21}(T)|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{[T/\varepsilon]+1}\int_{(k-1)\varepsilon}^{k\varepsilon}\|\sigma(s,X_{s\wedge{\mathcal{N}}_{s}^{\varepsilon}})-\sigma(S_{k}^{\varepsilon},X_{S_{k}^{\varepsilon}\wedge((k-1)\varepsilon)})\|^{2}_{\rm HS}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right).

By
(H
0
)
and
(H
{}_{\sigma}^{t}
)

\displaystyle\delta_{k}^{\varepsilon}(s)\lesssim|s^{\alpha}-(S_{k}^{\varepsilon})^{\alpha}|^{\beta}(1+|X_{s\wedge{\mathcal{N}}_{s}^{\varepsilon}}|^{2})+|X_{s\wedge{\mathcal{N}}_{s}^{\varepsilon}}-X_{S_{k}^{\varepsilon}\wedge((k-1)\varepsilon)}|^{2}.

2.4

k\varepsilon\leqslant T

\displaystyle\|{\mathscr{I}}_{22}(T)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

In this section, we prove Theorem

. For Volterra-type equations, Doob’s maximal inequality cannot be applied directly, so the supremum over the time variable must be taken outside the expectation. We begin with the following key lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Under
(H
{}^{\gamma}_{1}
)
and
(H
{}^{\gamma}_{2}
)
, for any

such that

(3.2)

Proof.

(i) For

, applying Itô’s isometry, Hölder’s inequality, and
(H
{}^{\gamma}_{1}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|Y_{t}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\|\sigma(t,s,Y_{s})\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)+2t\,{\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|b(t,s,Y_{s})|^{2}\,{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right)

and (

19

(ii) For
s<t

{}^{\gamma}_{1}

Lemma 3.2
.

Under
(H
{}^{\gamma}_{1}
)

{}^{\gamma}_{2}

4.
Examples: fractional Brownian motion

We consider the fractional Brownian motion (fBm) with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
, i.e., the continuous, centered Gaussian process
(B_{t}^{H})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
with
B_{0}^{H}=0
a.s., and covariance kernel

R_{H}(t,s):=\operatorname{Cov}(B_{s}^{H},B_{t}^{H})=\frac{1}{2}(|s|^{2H}+|t|^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}).

When
H=1/2
, fBm coincides with standard Brownian motion for
t\geqslant 0
. When
H\neq 1/2
, fBm is not a semimartingale, so the usual stochastic calculus cannot be directly applied. For
t\geqslant 0
and
H\in(0,1/2)\cup(1/2,1)
, fBm admits the following representation (see
[
3
]
)

B_{t}^{H}=\int_{0}^{t}K_{H}(t,s){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s},\text{ a.s.,}

for some standard Brownian motion
(W_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
, where
K_{H}
is given by

K_{H}(t,s):=C_{H}\left((t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}+s^{H-\frac{1}{2}}F(t/s)\right){\mathbf{1}}_{\{s<t\}},

and

C_{H}:=\left(\frac{2H\Gamma(3/2-H)}{\Gamma(H+1/2)\Gamma(2-2H)}\right)^{1/2},\quad F(u):=\left(\frac{1}{2}-H\right)\int_{1}^{u}(r-1)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}(1-r^{H-\frac{1}{2}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r.

Here,
\Gamma
denotes the usual Gamma function.

We consider the stochastic Volterra equation with kernel
K_{H}
:

X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s})K_{H}(t,s){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}b(s,X_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

(4.1)

where
X_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, and
\sigma:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
b:\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
are Borel measurable functions.
The compound Poisson approximation of (
4.1
) is defined by

X_{t}^{\varepsilon}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,X_{s-}^{\varepsilon})K_{H}(t,s){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{{\mathcal{N}}_{s}^{\varepsilon}}+\int_{0}^{t}b(s,X_{s-}^{\varepsilon}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}{\mathcal{N}}_{s}^{\varepsilon}.

(4.2)

We verify the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
below, which leads to the following result:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
H\in(0,1/2)\cup(1/2,1)
.
Assume that
(H
0
)
and
(H
{}_{\sigma}^{t}
)
hold, with
\ell_{b}(s)\equiv\kappa_{b}
,
\alpha=1
, and
\beta\in(0,1]
.
Let
X_{t}
and
X_{t}^{\varepsilon}
be the solutions of (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), respectively. Then, for any
T>0
and
X_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1)
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}{\mathbb{E}}|X_{t}^{\varepsilon}-X_{t}|^{2}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\gamma/(2(2+\gamma))},

where

\gamma=\beta\wedge(H\wedge|1-2H|\wedge(2-2H)-\varepsilon^{\prime}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{H(0,1/3]\cup\{3/4\}\}}).

It is easy to verify that
(H
{}^{\gamma}_{1}
)
and
(H
{}^{\gamma}_{2}
)
hold. For
(H
{}^{\gamma}_{3}
)

Lemma 4.2
.

For any
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1)
and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
0<\delta<1\wedge t/3
,

\int_{\delta}^{t_{\delta}}\Big(s^{-\alpha}s_{\delta}^{-\beta}+(t-s)^{-\alpha}(t-s_{\delta})^{-\beta}\Big){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\lesssim_{C}\delta^{1-\alpha-\beta}+1-{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\alpha+\beta=1\}}\log\delta.

(4.3)

Proof.

Since
s-\delta\leqslant s_{\delta}\leqslant s
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{t_{\delta}}s^{-\alpha}s_{\delta}^{-\beta}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta^{-\beta}\int_{\delta}^{2\delta}s^{-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\int_{2\delta}^{t_{\delta}}(s-\delta)^{-\alpha-\beta}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\delta^{1-\alpha-\beta}+\frac{{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\alpha+\beta\neq 1\}}}{1-\alpha-\beta}\left((t_{\delta}-\delta)^{1-\alpha-\beta}-\delta^{1-\alpha-\beta}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\log(t_{\delta}-\delta)-\log\delta\right){\mathbf{1}}_{\{\alpha+\beta=1\}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{1-\alpha-\beta}+1-{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\alpha+\beta=1\}}\log\delta.

For
s\in(t-\delta,t_{\delta})
, since
s_{\delta}=t_{\delta}-\delta\leqslant t-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{t_{\delta}}(t-s)^{-\alpha}(t-s_{\delta})^{-\beta}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\delta}^{t-\delta}(t-s)^{-\alpha-\beta}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\delta^{-\beta}\int_{t-\delta}^{t_{\delta}}(t-s)^{-\alpha}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha-\beta}\left((t-\delta)^{1-\alpha-\beta}-\delta^{1-\alpha-\beta}\right){\mathbf{1}}_{\{\alpha+\beta\neq 1\}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\log(t-\delta)-\log\delta\right){\mathbf{1}}_{\{\alpha+\beta=1\}}+\frac{\delta^{-\beta}}{1-\alpha}\left(\delta^{1-\alpha}-(t-t_{\delta})^{1-\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{1-\alpha-\beta}+1-{\mathbf{1}}_{\{\alpha+\beta=1\}}\log\delta.

The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
H\in(0,1/2)
and
t>0
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for any
\delta<1\wedge t/3
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{t_{\delta}}|K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t,s_{\delta})|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\lesssim_{C}\delta^{(H-\varepsilon^{\prime})\wedge(1-2H)}.

(4.4)

Proof.

By the definition of
K_{H}
, we have

\displaystyle|K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t,s_{\delta})|

\displaystyle\leqslant|(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(t-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{1}{2}}|

\displaystyle\quad+|s^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-s_{\delta}^{H-\frac{1}{2}}|F\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)+s_{\delta}^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\left|F\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)-F\left(\frac{t}{s_{\delta}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant(s-s_{\delta})^{\frac{1}{2}-H}(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}(t-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+F(+\infty)(s-s_{\delta})^{\frac{1}{2}-H}s^{H-\frac{1}{2}}s_{\delta}^{H-\frac{1}{2}}+s_{\delta}^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\left|F\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)-F\left(\frac{t}{s_{\delta}}\right)\right|.

By Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left|F\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)-F\left(\frac{t}{s_{\delta}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\frac{t}{s}}^{\frac{t}{s_{\delta}}}(u-1)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}(1-u^{H-\frac{1}{2}}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}u

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}(u-1)^{\frac{2H-3}{2-H}}(1-u^{H-\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{2}{2-H}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u\right)^{\frac{2-H}{2}}\left(\frac{t}{s_{\delta}}-\frac{t}{s}\right)^{\frac{H}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Bigg(\int_{1}^{2}(u-1)^{\frac{2H-3}{2-H}}\left((u-1)u^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\frac{2}{2-H}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{2}^{\infty}(u-1)^{\frac{2H-3}{2-H}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u\Bigg)^{\frac{2-H}{2}}t^{\frac{H}{2}}(s-s_{\delta})^{\frac{H}{2}}(ss_{\delta})^{-\frac{H}{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\int_{1}^{2}(u-1)^{\frac{2H-1}{2-H}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u+\int_{2}^{\infty}(u-1)^{\frac{2H-3}{2-H}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u\right)^{\frac{2-H}{2}}(s-s_{\delta})^{\frac{H}{2}}(ss_{\delta})^{-\frac{H}{2}}.

Since
-2<\frac{2H-3}{2-H}<-1<\frac{2H-1}{2-H}<0
, we have

\left|F\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)-F\left(\frac{t}{s_{\delta}}\right)\right|\lesssim(s-s_{\delta})^{\frac{H}{2}}(ss_{\delta})^{-\frac{H}{2}}.

Hence,

|K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t,s_{\delta})|^{2}\lesssim\delta^{1-2H}\left(((t-s)(t-s_{\delta}))^{2H-1}+(ss_{\delta})^{2H-1}\right)+\delta^{H}s^{-H}s_{\delta}^{H-1}.

By (
4.3
), for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
, we have

\int_{\delta}^{t_{\delta}}|K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t,s_{\delta})|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\lesssim\delta^{2H}+\delta^{1-2H}-\delta^{1-2H}\log\delta{\mathbf{1}}_{\{H=1/4\}}-\delta^{H}\log\delta\lesssim\delta^{(H-\varepsilon^{\prime})\wedge(1-2H)}.

The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
H\in(1/2,1)
and
t>0
, there is a constant
C>0
such that for any
\delta<1\wedge t/3
and

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{t_{\delta}}|K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t,s_{\delta})|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\lesssim_{C}\delta^{(2H-1)\wedge(2-2H)}+{\mathbf{1}}_{\{H=3/4\}}\delta^{1/2-\varepsilon^{\prime}}.

By change of variable, for

From this representation, it follows that

\displaystyle|K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t,s_{\delta})|

\displaystyle\lesssim s^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\int_{s}^{t}\left|(r-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}-(r-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\right|r^{H-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\quad+\left|s^{\frac{1}{2}-H}-s_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\right|\int_{s}^{t}(r-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}r^{H-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\quad+s_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\int_{s_{\delta}}^{s}(r-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}r^{H-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\lesssim s^{\frac{1}{2}-H}(s-s_{\delta})^{\frac{3}{2}-H}\int_{s}^{t}(r-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}(r-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\quad+(s-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{1}{2}}s^{\frac{1}{2}-H}s_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\int_{s}^{t}(r-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\quad+s_{\delta}^{\frac{1}{2}-H}\int_{s_{\delta}}^{s}(r-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r.

Observe that

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}(r-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}(r-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant(s-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\int_{s}^{s+\delta}(r-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r+\int_{s+\delta}^{t}(r-s)^{2H-3}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\lesssim(s-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\delta^{H-\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\delta^{2H-2}-(t-s)^{2H-2}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2(s-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\delta^{H-\frac{1}{2}},

and

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}(r-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{H-\frac{1}{2}}\left((t-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(s-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\lesssim 1,

\displaystyle\int_{s_{\delta}}^{s}(r-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{3}{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{H-\frac{1}{2}}(s-s_{\delta})^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\delta^{H-\frac{1}{2}}.

Hence,

|K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t,s_{\delta})|^{2}\lesssim\delta^{2H-1}\left(s^{1-2H}+s_{\delta}^{1-2H}+(ss_{\delta})^{1-2H}\right).

By (

), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{t_{\delta}}|K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t,s_{\delta})|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{2H-1}(\delta^{2H}+1+\delta^{3-4H}-{\mathbf{1}}_{\{H=3/4\}}\log\delta)

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{(2H-1)\wedge(2-2H)}+{\mathbf{1}}_{\{H=3/4\}}\delta^{1/2-\varepsilon^{\prime}},

which completes the proof.
∎

\ell_{3}(t^{\prime},t,s)=\kappa_{\sigma}^{2}\left(K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t^{\prime},s)\right)^{2},\quad\ell_{4}(t^{\prime},t,s)=\kappa_{b}^{2},

so that
(
1.11
)
and
(
1.12
)
hold. In fact, by Itô’s isometry, we have for any
t<t^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t^{\prime},s)\right)^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left(K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t^{\prime},s)\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant R_{H}(t,t)-2R_{H}(t,t^{\prime})+R_{H}(t^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=|t-t^{\prime}|^{2H},

so that
(H
{}_{2}^{\gamma}
)
holds.
Moreover, by
(H
{}_{\sigma}^{t}
)
, we have

\displaystyle|\sigma

\displaystyle(s,x)K_{H}(t,s)-\sigma(s^{\prime},x)K_{H}(t,s^{\prime})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\sigma(s,x)-\sigma(s^{\prime},x)\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^{2}K_{H}(t,s)^{2}+|K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t,s^{\prime})|^{2}\|\sigma(s^{\prime},x)\|_{\rm{\mathrm{\tiny HS}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\kappa_{\tau}|s-s^{\prime}|^{\beta}K_{H}(t,s)^{2}+\kappa_{\sigma}^{2}|K_{H}(t,s)-K_{H}(t,s^{\prime})|^{2}\right)(1+|x|^{2})

\displaystyle=:\ell_{5}(t,s,s^{\prime})(1+|x|^{2}).

By (

), (

\displaystyle\int_{\delta}^{t_{\delta}}\ell_{5}(t,s,s_{\delta}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\lesssim\delta^{\beta}t^{(2H)\wedge(2-2H)}+\delta^{H\wedge|1-2H|\wedge(2-2H)-\varepsilon^{\prime}{\mathbf{1}}_{\{H\in(0,1/3],\{3/4\}\}}}.

Thus,
(H
{}_{3}^{\gamma}

5.
Numerical Experiments

In this section, we present two numerical experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed scheme. The results indicate that, compared with the classical Euler–Maruyama method, the Poisson approximation scheme provides a clear advantage when dealing with SDEs involving singular coefficients.

5.1.
Linear SDEs with Singular Time-Dependent Drifts

We first consider the

one-dimensional linear stochastic differential equation (SDE) with a singular time-dependent drift:

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=\mu(t)X_{t}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sigma(t)X_{t}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t}.

The exact solution of this equation is given by

X_{t}=X_{0}\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\left(\mu(s)-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma(s)^{2}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\int_{0}^{t}\!\!\sigma(s){\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}\right\}.

Consequently,

{\mathbb{E}}(X_{t})={\mathbb{E}}(X_{0})\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\mu(s){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right\},\qquad{\mathbb{E}}|X_{t}|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}|X_{0}|^{2}\exp\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\left(2\mu(s)+\sigma(s)^{2}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s\right\}.

In the numerical tests, we choose

\displaystyle\sigma(s)\equiv\sigma_{0},\quad\mu(s)=\begin{cases}\mu_{0}|s-s_{0}|^{-\alpha},&s\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2}),\\[4.0pt]
\mu_{1}|s-s_{1}|^{-\beta},&s\in[\tfrac{1}{2},1],\end{cases}

(5.1)

where
s_{0}\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
,
s_{1}\in(\tfrac{1}{2},1)
, and
\sigma_{0},\mu_{0},\mu_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Note that
\mu(s)
exhibits singularities at
s=s_{0}
and
s=s_{1}
, and is discontinuous at
s=0.5
in the sense that the left and right limits differ.

We compare the Poisson approximation and Euler–Maruyama schemes for the SDE defined in (
5.1
), using the parameters

\sigma_{0}=0.1,\quad\mu_{0}=0.3,\quad\mu_{1}=0.7,\quad s_{0}=0.4,\quad s_{1}=0.6,\quad\alpha=\beta=0.5.

5.2.

Next, we consider the linear stochastic Volterra equation

X_{t}=X_{0}+\mu\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha_{0}}|s-s_{0}|^{-\beta_{0}}X_{s}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\sqrt{\sigma}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha_{1}/2}|s-s_{1}|^{-\beta_{1}/2}X_{s}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s},

where
\alpha_{0},\beta_{0},\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},s_{0},s_{1}\in[0,1)
,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\sigma\geqslant 0
.

For
\alpha,\beta,a\in[0,1)
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}

(K^{\mu,a}_{\alpha,\beta}f)(t):=\mu\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}|s-a|^{-\beta}f(s){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,\quad f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}.

Lemma 5.1
.

The expectation of
X_{t}
admits the Neumann series representation

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(X_{t})={\mathbb{E}}(X_{0})\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[(K^{\mu,s_{0}}_{\alpha_{0},\beta_{0}})^{n}\mathbf{1}\right](t)\right).

(5.2)

If
\mu=0

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|X_{t}|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}|X_{0}|^{2}\left(1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[(K^{\sigma,s_{1}}_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}})^{n}\mathbf{1}\right](t)\right).

(5.3)

Proof.

From the definition of
X_{t}
,

{\mathbb{E}}(X_{t})={\mathbb{E}}(X_{0})+\mu\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha_{0}}|s-s_{0}|^{-\beta_{0}}{\mathbb{E}}(X_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

and similarly,

{\mathbb{E}}|X_{t}|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}|X_{0}|^{2}+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha_{1}}|s-s_{1}|^{-\beta_{1}}{\mathbb{E}}|X_{s}|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

The Neumann series representations (
5.2
)–(
5.3
) follow by iterating these integral identities.
∎
