Abstract
Across the developed world, there are growing calls to streamline and improve ever more complex income tax codes. Executing reform has proven difficult. Even when the desired outcomes of a reform are clear, the tools to design fitting reforms are lacking. To remedy this, we developed TaxSolver : a methodology to help policymakers realize optimal income tax reform. TaxSolver allows policymakers to focus solely on what they aim to achieve with a reform—like redistributing wealth, incentivizing labor market participation or reducing complexity— and the guarantees within which reform is acceptable—like limiting fluctuations in taxpayer incomes, protecting households from falling into poverty or shocks to overall tax revenue. Given these goals and fiscal guarantees, TaxSolver finds the optimal set of tax rules that satisfies all the criteria or shows that the set of demands are not mathematically feasible. We illustrate TaxSolver by reforming various simulated examples of tax codes, including some that reflect the complexity and size of a real-world tax system.



Evidence for Practice

1.

Most tax codes can be defined as a simple system of equations that make it possible to solve tax reform as (linear) optimization problem.

2.

Linear optimization allows policymakers to formulate both hard guarantees that the reform has to adhere to, as well as objectives that are maximized within these set constraints. This makes optimization a practical tool that is aligned with the reality of policy work.

3.

Our approach allows for a tax reform process that does not consist of ad-hoc tweaking of existing rules but reasons over the entire system and returns an optimal solution in a matter of hours rather than weeks.

4.

We illustrate our approach by reforming various example systems, as well as a system representing the complexity and scale of a real-world income tax code.

Introduction

To illustrate the problem this paper is trying to solve, we start with a brief anecdote about a civil servant named Jesse. Jesse works in the finance ministry of a developed country and is part of a special team tasked with proposing a new income tax reform. The main objectives are (i) simplifying a bloated income tax system and (ii) incentivizing labor market participation. Naturally, the reform has to be budget neutral and protect households against large income swings. The team optimistically starts mapping out the various tax rules in existence, but quickly realize the tax code is more difficult to navigate than they had expected. Tax rules discriminate on the basis of myriad characteristics: renter versus buyer, affluent versus those with minimal savings, high income versus low income, households with children versus those without, partnered versus single individuals, pensioners versus the young, and so on. The number of potential modifications appear limitless, and tweaking one rule often results in unexpected ripple effects elsewhere.

Having experienced the system’s complexity first hand, the team decides to start with simplification. They propose to merge the lowest two income brackets, providing a small tax revenue buffer. They also remove the top tax bracket—leading to a budget deficit—that further reduces complexity and increases labor incentives by slashing rates for high incomes. On analyzing the downstream effects of their initial reform, the team realizes a large proportion of low income taxpayers would fall into poverty due to increased lower end taxation. To remedy this, the team develops an income-dependent benefit. This benefit provides income support that falls with increasing income. By setting separate amounts for fiscal partners and single households, the benefit is less costly than reinstating the original lower bracket, reducing the budget deficit. As the team has now re-introduced complexity into the system, they merge the child benefit, formerly a separate rule, into this new benefit and dub it the “household composition income-dependent benefit”, or HCIDB. Reflecting on their work, Jesse would be the first to admit the reforms may not be perfect, but certainly progress. Little did Jesse know this was just the beginning.

The team’s initial proposal will go on to be dissected by government advisory bodies who might identify loopholes or faults with the original proposals. This cumbersome review process will likely show that some of the changes are less effective than originally hoped or conflict in unambiguous ways with existing rules
(
Lejour 2016a
)
. At the same time, intense public and political debate will likely ensue. Politicians, policy think tanks and rights groups alike will scrutinize the proposals
(
Alt et al. 2010a
)
. These political actors are faced with the same challenges in overseeing the system as Jesse and her team, and this will force them to focus on anecdotal examples or cling to tax rules out of principle. Many of these actors will also have conflicting goals to those of the original reform. The team will scramble to save as much of the original proposal under a barrage of amendments and criticism, while the independent review process starts lagging behind and ultimately becomes disconnected through the changes that arise from the political horsetrading. At the end of all this, if at all, the tax reform that does occur will be unrecognizable from the already sub-optimal proposal the team originally started with.

Some form of the above story has occurred across many of the realized and unrealized tax reform efforts in countries across the world. This fraught process has led to complex and suboptimal tax codes, often with unexpected quirks that have real impact. For example, the United Kingdom’s income tax code has a so-called 60%-tax trap where marginal pressure, the effective tax rate, becomes excessively high at incomes between 100,000 and 125,140 GBP, reducing work incentives and leading to workarounds like increased pension contributions
(
Dodwell et al. 2024a
)
. The Netherlands has marginal tax rates upwards of 80% for some middle-income households, far above what economic theory would prescribe
(
Quist 2015a
)
. Tax reforms in the US have inadvertently created ‘marriage penalties’ for certain demographic groups, where real income declines based on marital status
(
Crandall-Hollick & Greenstein 2025a
)
. In most cases, these ‘cliffs’ arose because well-intended reforms had unwanted consequences.

In short, most realized tax systems are a far cry from what optimal tax theory recommends
(
Feldstein 1976a
;
Diamond & Saez 2011a
)
. In fact, most tax systems and reforms are far removed from their own original intentions. How can this be? It is not due to a lack of ideas. There is a wealth of literature on optimal taxation theory. However, governments become stuck in an equilibrium far removed from any optimal form
(
Slemrod 2018a
)
. Key reasons why governments struggle to implement reform range from the inherent complexity of income tax codes, governmental capacity constraints, and the broader political economy challenge of building the coalitions that are required to enact reform
(
Tetlow et al. 2020a
;
Wilson 1999a
;
Bird 2014a
)
. This paper addresses a problem often overlooked in the academic literature but that underpins many of these issues: that designing politically viable reform proposals is simply very difficult.

The incomes of taxpayers are directly affected by complex tax and benefit schemes that a reformer might seek to simplify. Erring slightly in reform will lead to serious consequences for real people. Sharp decreases in tax revenues could also endanger the continuity of the government itself. Thus, designing income tax reform is a delicate, high-stakes balancing act with a staggering number of variables to consider. Most reform processes task civil servants and fiscal experts, like Jesse and her team, to dream up reforms to fulfill certain political aims. These experts design, to the best of their ability, reform proposals and assess whether those goals would actually be reached. This is a complex quantitative exercise of carefully modeling the fiscal outcomes for millions of households. We call this the ‘ad-hoc’ way of designing tax reform, and it suffers from two problems that often give rise to suboptimal reform processes:

•

Ad-hoc reform is hard.
In any complex tax system, the number of reform possibilities is close to infinite and there is currently no systematic way to effectively consider all possibilities and achieve the optimal reform for the stated political aims.

•

Ad-hoc reform is slow.
Designing and tweaking a proposal is time-consuming, making it impossible to quickly churn out new versions during a reform process.

We aim to address both problems at once by designing
TaxSolver
, a tool that can quickly generate optimal reforms given a set of ‘policy goals’ and ‘guarantees’. We do this by formulating tax reform as a constrained optimization problem. There are many attractive properties of constrained optimization. Optimization targets can easily be aligned with typical ambitions of tax reform, like budget neutrality or reduced complexity. Most importantly, any number of fiscal guarantees can be specified at policy-relevant levels. These include limiting budget shocks for individual households, incentivizing work and many more. Within the set constraints,
TaxSolver
guarantees mathematical optimality of the goal function.

Not only does
TaxSolver
relieve civil servants like Jesse from trial-and-error tinkering with the existing rules, it also fundamentally reorients the reform process. Instead of a slow process of designing an initial proposal followed by validation and political debate, the onus of debate is shifted towards what the goals and fiscal guarantees of the reform should be and
TaxSolver
can (i) verify whether a system satisfying such demands is possible and (ii) provide its optimal implementation. Finally, the natural ability to set hard constraints make it possible to design guarantees that are otherwise difficult to implement, like ensuring taxpayers or households do not fall into poverty due to the reform. We open source
TaxSolver
for all to use and improve.

2
The Good, The Bad, and The Ugly of income tax reform

Income tax codes fulfill many important functions in society. Income taxation represents a key pillar of government revenue that enable state expenditures, particularly in developed countries
(
OECD 2023a
;
Mikesell 1974a
)
. A well-defined income tax policy also stimulates economic growth by providing labor market incentives to workers by (dis)incentivizing the real return of additional hours of work
(
Van 2024a
;
Bae et al. 2012a
;
Eissa & Liebman 1996a
;
Gale & Samwick 2014a
)
. Many countries use the income tax code to incentivize other types of behavior as well
(
Tummers 2019a
)
, like switching to electrical vehicles, or to provide financial assistance to vulnerable groups through tax deductions, rebates, and benefits
(
Slemrod & Gillitzer 2013a
)
. All of the above make income tax codes an effective tool in shaping how society functions. This is what we call “The Good” of income tax.

The flip side is that poorly designed tax codes can disincentivize labor and hamper growth
(
Mirrlees 1971a
;
OECD 2010a
)
. Its execution can become costly and loopholes may arise that create or exacerbate inequality
(
Piketty & Saez 2007a
)
. Complex tax codes also have myriad unintended consequences. An opaque system leads to uncertainty, and can leave taxpayers with unexpected costs and the risk of poverty traps
(
Martin et al. 2024a
)
. A case in point is The Netherlands, where many income dependent benefits are never claimed by eligible taxpayers due to fears they may have to repay them at a latter point in time
(
Ministerie van Sociale Zaken en Werkgelegenheid 2023a
)
. The ‘benefit scandal’ that rocked The Netherlands and led to the eventual fall of the government in 2021 was in part a consequence of such income dependent benefits
(
Rijksoverheid 2021a
;
Peeters & Widlak 2023a
;
Tweede Kamer 2024a
)
. All the things that can go wrong are what we call “The Bad” of income tax.

Finally, “The Ugly” of income tax is the practical reality that most tax reforms bring out The Bad more than The Good. Governments face many practical, political and administrative barriers in enacting reform
(
Alt et al. 2010a
;
Lejour 2016a
)
. We claim that many of these barriers stem from two related issues described below.

The Coding Problem makes optimal reform challenging

Reforms, small and large, inherently suffer from what we call the ‘Coding problem’. Even when the goals of a tax reform are clear, it is difficult to optimally (re)form a system accordingly
(
James & Nobes 2009a
;
Mirrlees 1971a
;
Bird & Zolt 2004a
)
. Tax rules often interact in complex ways, and changes can have unexpected side-effects. An example is the common phenomenon where income-dependent benefits, meant to help low income taxpayers, also decrease labor incentives by increasing the effective tax rate experienced by those taxpayers
(
Dodwell et al. 2024a
;
Quist 2015a
)
.

The root cause of the Coding Problem is complexity. For any tax system, there is an effectively infinite set of reform proposals that can be considered. There is currently no way to systematically consider all possibilities
(
Gale 2001a
;
Slemrod & Gillitzer 2013a
)
. Civil servants often qualitatively pick a number of research directions and identify a handful candidates for thorough analysis. This leads to initial reform proposals that are often already suboptimal out of the gate with respect to the original goals with which they had been initiated.

Beyond the difficulty to formulate any reform, the lack of method to demonstrate that a proposal is optimal means there is always room for political doubt and debate as to the reform’s merits. As a result, political leaders may come back time and again looking for a ‘better’ answer to their question.

The time-consuming nature of reform leads to the Iteration Problem

Realizing an initial reform proposal is already time-consuming due to the Coding Problem. After that, an initial reform proposal typically goes on to be dissected and criticized by politicians, interest groups and independent advisory agencies
(
Slemrod 2018a
;
Tetlow et al. 2020a
;
Alt et al. 2010a
)
. This is not a problem in itself. However, when generating new results takes weeks or months, there is little room to effectively incorporate feedback into the reform proposal, leading to changes that lack objective merit. We call this the ‘Iteration Problem’.

The Iteration Problem is further amplified by the fact that political actors may have preferences that need not align with those of the original reform. Politicians and interest groups also tend to view certain tax rules as ‘political red lines’, and can be concerned with specific population groups or rules rather than the overall outcomes of the reform
(
Bierbrauer et al. 2021a
;
Alt et al. 2010a
)
.

Independent agencies are usually tasked with providing an unbiased assessment of the impact of a reform, and should inject objectivity into the political debate
(
Lienert 2013a
)
. However, such assessments also tend to be time-consuming and by the time such validation occurs, amendments to the reform will already be actively considered, further decoupling objectivity from the political debate
(
Auerbach 2009a
;
Slemrod & Gillitzer 2013a
)
. A lack of flexibility and speed in translating political demands into concrete reforms lies at the basis of the Iteration Problem and further harms effective reform.

A new approach to realizing optimal income tax reform

In sum, the Coding an Iteration problems combine in a way that leads to suboptimal reform (Figure
1
) and a general disconnect between the various stakeholders that play a role in the reform process
(
Peeters & Widlak 2023a
)
. Civil servants are tasked with the daunting task of initially navigating the Coding Problem. Their reform proposals are reviewed by independent government bodies while political debate ensues that typically demands changes to the original reform. The lingering uncertainty about the optimality of the original reform, combined with the inability to quickly adopt feedback leads to ‘horse-trading’ and reforms to be watered down or even shot down entirely.

Our hypothesis is that the reform process could be many times more effective if suitable tools were available that addressed the Coding Problem and did so in a way that would allow quick turnaround of new reform proposals in light of new political demands. Given such tools, a different political process could be imagined that starts with clearly stating the goals and guardrails for reform, rather than starting with a specific proposal. A political debate that is solely concerned with the objectives and conditions under which reform is agreeable. This ideal process is visualized in Figure
2
, including what is currently missing to realize it.

Such a workflow crucially hinges on the ability to actually translate goals and guarantees into a concrete reform and to do so quickly. In what follows, we propose such a methodology that can translate a set of goals and guardrails into reform proposals that guarantees optimality. If political leaders obtain new information in the process from independent reviews or the electorate, it is easy to create variations on the reform that show the consequences of setting different priorities. This allows political leaders to compare alternatives, visualize trade-offs and organize political coalitions around the preferred option, rather than having the process stall out.

3
Tax reform as constrained optimization problem

This section outlines the methodology underlying
TaxSolver
and represents our core contribution to enabling more efficient tax reform. We have chosen to focus on the rationale underlying our approach and defer technical details, formulas and proofs to the Appendix.

Term

Definition

Tax code

Complete set of tax rules applicable to taxpayers.

Taxpayer

An individual who is charged taxes within a country’s income tax code.

Input

A variable that is taxed (positively or negatively) within the tax code, such as income or capital gains.

Tax rule

A formula that takes an input and returns an absolute amount of taxes payable or receivable.

Absolute tax pressure

The absolute amount of taxes payable.

The increase or decrease in absolute tax pressure when the input increases by one unit.

The characteristics of a taxpayer that affect which types of tax rules are applicable to them.

We start this section with a couple of definitions that help us reason about a tax system and formulate reform as a constrained optimization problem. We start by defining an income ‘tax code’ as a set of ‘tax rules’. Each tax rule maps a ‘tax-relevant input’ (input, hereafter) to an ‘absolute tax pressure’ and a ‘marginal tax pressure’. An example tax rule could be an income tax bracket, with yearly income before tax as input. The absolute tax pressure is the amount of taxes payable or receivable due to this specific tax rule. The marginal tax pressure is the amount by which absolute tax pressure changes when the input increases by one unit (e.g. how much taxes have to be paid on 1 additional unit of income before tax). Finally, each taxpayer has ‘taxpayer characteristics’ that impact what tax rules are applicable to them. These definitions are listed in Table
1
.

Individual tax rules are often simple. What makes tax codes complex is the number of tax rules in operation and how their shape and eligibility is governed by taxpayer characteristics. That said, most tax systems across the globe consist of tax rules for which the following statements hold:

•

Tax rules are additive (additivity):
the absolute and marginal tax pressure of all individual tax rules applicable to a taxpayer add up to that taxpayer’s total absolute and marginal tax pressure.

•

Single input tax rules (scalar input):
tax rules have a single input on which the rule levies taxes.

•

Marginal pressure follows a step function (piecewise linearity):
marginal pressure is stable on intervals of the input, making input and absolute tax pressure piecewise linear.

•

Heterogeneity through taxpayer characteristics (heterogeneous):
the eligibility and exact formulation of a tax rule can depend on taxpayer characteristics.

We now proceed to illustrate that these characteristics hold for three archetype tax rules that cover the vast majority of tax rules in operation.

Benefits.

Benefits are another common tax rule that usually reflect a reduction in taxes paid. When there are no eligibility requirements the benefit is ‘universal’, although most benefits are tailored to specific population groups. Beyond eligibility, the benefit amount can depend on an input like one’s income before tax. Typically, the benefit reduces until it is ‘nullified’—i.e., there is no more reduction in absolute tax pressure. The benefit tax rule is thus a generalisation of tax brackets that include some lump sum transfer and a possible eligibility criterium.

Three examples of benefits are depicted in Figure

The middle inset shows a benefit that has no eligibility constraint and is available to all taxpayers. The benefit increases linearly with the amount of dependent children one has, which can be seen as one large bracket with children as input. This means the benefit can also be viewed as a bracket rule with a single rate. Finally, the right-most inset illustrates a benefit that is again universal, starts at a lump sum value, and is nullified over an interval. The height of the benefit and the interval on which it is nullified differs by group. Throughout these examples, the assumptions listed above are again satisfied.

Miscellaneous tax rules

Although most commonly encountered tax rules are variations of these archetypes, some are not and may violate the assumptions listed above. For example, a tax rule could be polynomial in its input. This violates the piecewise linearity assumption. There may also be tax rules that depend on multiple inputs, for example an income-dependent child benefit. For this specific example, one could view the number of dependent children as a taxpayer characteristic in which case the tax rule would constitute a conventional benefit with a single-input given a set number of children. Broadly speaking, we consider this broader set of miscellaneous tax rules beyond the scope of this paper (see Discussion).

The total tax code.

The key idea underlying our approach is that the above assumptions allow us to succinctly describe an entire tax code as a single formula. To do so, we first introduce the concept of a ‘tax group’. A tax group is a set of taxpayers who have exactly the same absolute and marginal tax pressure as long as they have the same set of inputs. Effectively, this means that all taxpayers in a single taxgroup have the same eligibility conditions for every tax rule.

The additivity assumption introduced earlier stated that the total tax pressure faced by some taxpayer is the sum over all individual tax rules. Within a tax group, we can then use the fact that if all tax rules are piecewise linear the sum over all tax rules is also piecewise linear (see Appendix). This means that the total tax code is also a piecewise linear function where the brackets of this tax function consists of the superset of all the cutoffs for each individual tax rule. This idea is illustrated in Figure
6
and is central to our approach to tax reform. Note that such piecewise functions exist for every input.

3.2
Tax reform as contrained optimization

When viewing the income tax code as a single piecewise linear function per tax group, as illustrated in Figure
6
, it becomes clear that there are two distinct sets of variables that determine how a tax code operates. The first are the cutoff points that determine the piecewise linear intervals that divide an input into brackets. The second are the rates levied in each interval combined with possible lump sum transfers. We call the set of cutoff points the ‘support’ of the tax system and we call the rates in each bracket and any lump sum transfers as the ‘rates’ of the system. Note that we define rates with respect to the entire support of someone’s tax function and not with respect to individual tax rules.

If we now turn to tax reform, we can think of a reformed system as one with an alternative set of rates than the current system. For simplicity, we can assume that the new system follows the same support as the old system, although others can be defined analogously. Now that we have explicated what tax reform looks like—finding some alternative set of rates that differ from the current rates—we can start defining the problem of finding an optimal reform as a constrained optimization problem.

A constrained optimization problem consists of three key parts. First, a set of ‘solver variables’ are defined that can be changed during optimization. Second, there are constraints that limit the solution space for the solver variables. Third, there is an objective function with which different choices for the solver variables can be compared.

For tax reform, we designate the rates in the system as the solver variables. During reform, different systems are assessed by changing the solver variables. We then constrain sets of values—solutions—through what we call ‘fiscal guarantees’. Fiscal guarantees are constraints to which a solyution
has to
abide to. For example, we may want to guarantee that a reform does not lead to large income fluctuations with respect to the current system. Finally, we define an objective, for example that reform should be as cheap as possible or as simple as possible within the feasible set of solutions. The user can also decide to omit the objective and simply assess whether there is any solution given a set of guarantees. The final elements required to enact reform are information on taxpayers like their inputs and current absolute tax pressure (the ‘data’), and a numeric optimization method (the ‘solver’).

. Beyond optimality, the core advantage of constrained optimization is its flexibility. Policy goals like budget neutrality or system complexity can easily be combined with explicit fiscal guarantees like protecting households against considerable income swings. Such hard constraints are usually not available to mean loss optimizing algorithms like those often encountered in e.g. supervised Machine Learning tasks that might be optimal in the aggregate, but could have extreme outcomes for certain taxpayers
(
Hastie et al. 2009a
)
.

Optimal reform of tax systems

To illustrate how these elements fit together, we first illustrate how we can recover an existing system’s rates using a constrained optimization approach. Assume we have a dataset on taxpayers that contains tax-relevant inputs like gross income, taxpayer characteristics like fiscal status, and observed tax pressures for the current system. For ease of representation, assume there is only one tax-relevant input and that there is only one tax group. Let’s also assume we know the support of the system.
5
5

5

Throughout this paper we leave the support out of the optimization and simply assume the reform follows the current system’s support. We return to strategies to include the system’s support into optimization in the discussion.
With this information, we can recover the current system’s rates exactly by defining each person’s tax as their complete tax function, setting the rates as the solver variables and adding fiscal guarantees that everyone’s absolute tax pressure in the reformed system
exactly equates
their tax pressure in the new system. As reform can only change the slopes in the tax function and we require that everyone pays exactly the same tax as before, the only feasible solution exactly follows the old system.

If we were to loosen these fiscal guarantees, for example allowing taxpayers to experience some changes to their taxes paid, alternative reforms with different rates become possible. When multiple candidate solutions exist, we can use a combination of objective functions and additional fiscal guarantees to realize optimal tax reform.

Setting fiscal guarantees for a reform

To go from recovering an existing system to optimal reform, we start by discussing different types of fiscal guarantees that may be desired.

Income constraints.

Fiscal guarantees regarding the taxes paid by taxpayers in the new system are typically the most important in defining the solution space. If income constraints are ‘tight’, like in the example earlier, the current system is the only feasible solution. When we relax income constraints, multiple systems might produce feasible solutions. For example, we might allow fluctuations in taxes paid of at most 5% or set absolute limits, like protecting taxpayers from falling below a pre-defined poverty line (see Appendix for details).

Marginal constraints.

Another example of a policy desire that can be expressed as a constraint could be to ensure that no taxpayer ever experiences prohibitively high marginal pressure. High marginal pressure is often an unwanted consequence of having multiple income-dependent benefits in conjunction with tax brackets. As taxpayers earn more, they enter higher tax brackets but also effectively lose income through reductions in benefits. This can severely reduce labor participation incentives. Since reform means finding an optimal set of rates given constraints, it is straightforward to limit the domain of these rates directly. We call these ‘marginal pressure constraints’ (see Appendix for details).

Budget constraints

A third type of frequently encountered policy concerns are the costs of a reform in terms of foregone tax revenue. We call these ‘budget constraints’ and they can be implemented analogous with the income constraints introduced above. The total tax revenue is simply the sum over all individuals’ taxes paid, which can be constrained (see Appendix for details).

We discuss other types of fiscal guarantees that may be appropriate for reform in the Appendix. These include constraints on the extent to which different taxpayers can deviate from one another due to a reform and can for example be used to ensure labor participation does not decrease.

Setting the objective for a reform

Whereas fiscal guarantees ensure that a reform will have certain properties, they otherwise do not place ‘value’ on different solutions that satisfy the set constraints. For example, there may be multiple reforms possible and one such reform might cost the state slightly more in foregone tax revenue, whereas another might put the burden on the taxpayer. Objective functions can further weigh the possible reforms. For example, we could set minimizing the loss in tax revenue as the objective. This means that among all reforms that satisfy the set constraints, the cheapest is selected. We could also incorporate some metric of complexity into the objective, which ensures the system is as simple as possible, or we could minimize marginal pressure to not just ensure the reform falls within the set marginal perssure constraints, but that it is in fact the lowest possible.

Setting the support, tax groups and tax rules for a reform

Throughout the above, it was assumed that the support of the system (the cutoffs used to divide inputs like income before tax into brackets) were given. Reforms can also be realized using alternative supports. For example, fewer cutoff points can be used to effectively simplify the solution by making the solver unable to utilize rates. Conversely, many more cutoffs can be used to allow for new brackets.

The same holds for the amount of tax groups that are available to reform the system. Rather than using the same tax groups as present in an existing system, policymakers can decide to reduce the extent to which taxpayers might experience different taxation.

Finally, it might be politically desirable to keep certain tax rules and reform the system net of these rules. This is also possible by setting certain solver variables to designated values instead of making them eligible for reform.

3.3
Example system reforms

To make the above concepts concrete, we provide a couple of examples that illustrate how reform goals could be set up.

Targeted tax break.

Assume the goal is to redistribute wealth to the middle 50% of incomes and we are willing to do this by foregoing (as little as possible) total tax revenue. Let’s also assume we only want to change the rates levied and keep the current system’s support. To do this, we could set (close to) tight income constraints for the lower and upper 25% of taxpayers by income. For the middle 50%, we would set a constraint that forces their income to be higher than in the current system. We could set the objective to minimizing the loss, which means the reform would be the cheapest possible given the above constraints.

Redistribution of wealth.

Instead of paying for an increase in net income of the middle 50% of incomes through a loss in tax revenue, we could also implement a redistribution of wealth. For example, we could set a (close to) tight budget constraint that ensures there is minimal change in overall tax revenues and allow the top 25% of incomes to forego income. In this case, the targeted tax break from before is paid for by the top earners.

Constraining marginal pressure.

Instead of realizing changes in taxpayer’s net income, we could also attempt to reduce the marginal pressure that is faced by individual taxpayers. To do this, we would set loose income constraints that provide some room for reform — like allowing income changes of up to 5% — in combination with marginal constraints limiting the marginal pressure anyone in the system is allowed to face.

Reducing complexity.

A final example of a real-world policy effort could be to reduce the complexity of the tax code with minimal bearing on taxpayer’s real income. This could be done by setting some complexity function as the objective function, or strongly reducing the support of the system to force
TaxSolver
to find a solution with fewer brackets or using fewer tax groups.

The mathematical details of the above, including the four example system reforms are given in the Appendix. In what follows, we illustrate the above concepts in practice using four simulated use cases including one based on a real-world system.

Example 3: A multiple rule, multiple tax group system

Goal of the example.
Expand reform across multiple tax groups and illustrate the ability to set fiscal guarantees at different levels of aggregation, like the household level.

In this third example, we split taxpayers along fiscal partnership status. Governments often use fiscal partnerships as a taxpayer characteristic to tailor tax rules. For example, in the Dutch income tax code the height and rate of healthcare benefits differ for fiscal partners as opposed to single taxpayers. Governments also tend to use fiscal partnerships—or ‘households’—as a relevant unit of analysis for tax reform. For example, governments may want a reform to limit variation in both personal as well as household income.

In this example we set income constraints at the household level. Fiscal partnerships receive a benefit amount of 2,250 for the household and singles receive an amount of 1,500. For the former, the benefit decreases to zero as combined household income increases from 30,000 EUR to 60,000 EUR. For the latter, the benefit decreases to zero as combined household income increases from 30,000 EUR to 40,000 EUR. For illustrative purposes, we further add a second taxpayer characteristic: whether someone is self-employed or in payroll employment (‘labor’ going forward). For the self-employed, we include a tax credit rule that provides them with tax free personal income up until 15,000 EUR.

5
Discussion

In this paper we presented
TaxSolver
, a tool to help turn policymakers realize effective income tax reforms.
TaxSolver
allows users to specify the goals of a tax reform and define fiscal guarantees to which the reform must abide. It then provides users with mathematically optimal reform in line with the set goals and guarantees. In doing so, it paves the way to fundamentally rethink how tax reform is realized. Rather than relying on ad-hoc tinkering that is then revised through political debate,
TaxSolver
enables political debate to focus solely on what the goals and guarantees of a reform should be and takes care of constructing a fitting reform.

As illustrated in our use cases,
TaxSolver
covers many of the typical desires in tax reform. These include encoding guarantees like limiting income shocks for taxpayers or budget shocks due to tax revenue loss. It also covers many different objectives, like capping marginal pressure, simplifying tax codes or finding the most budget effective alternative to an existing system.
TaxSolver
can generate reforms in a matter of hours, allowing many reforms covering different goals and guarantees to be compared. We showcased this in our final use case, where we provided multiple reforms using a simulated dataset from a real-world fiscal system that reduced both excessively high marginal pressure and system complexity.

The goal of this paper is to introduce the key methodological building blocks of
TaxSolver
and showcase its ability to realize meaningful reform. That said, we see a number of exciting avenues for future research that could further expand the potential of
TaxSolver
.

Expanding coverage.

Our current methodology supports most archetypical tax rules, but we recognize that there may be exceptions. Examples are many-input or non-linear tax rules. By open sourcing our tool we invite others to contribute and increase
TaxSolver
’s coverage of tax rules.

Dynamic bracketing.

TaxSolver
relies on the user to define a set of candidate cutoff points to bracket inputs like income before tax. Although our current implementation allows the user to supply many such cutoff points and lets
TaxSolver
decide which to use, we think that ‘dynamic bracketing,’ where the cutoff points are actively part of optimization, is worthwhile to explore. This would also allow the inclusion of input-reducing deductibles where the amount of the deductible becomes part of the optimization.

Multi-objective reform.

We recognize the need as well as the difficulty to weigh multiple objectives during reform. This was already illustrated in our final use case, where we combined a desire for a budget effective reform with one that removed complexity from the system. We used the number of non-zero rates in the system as proxy for complexity, but there may be other ways to capture complexity. More broadly, we identify the objective function to be an area of active development where many additional perspectives on tax reform could be incorporated. These could include considerations regarding the operational cost of reform and many more. The ability for policymakers to generate reform that cover multiple objectives and allows comparing reforms with different emphases would be an important next step.

Incorporate behavioral effects.

Our current implementation does not explicitly incorporate the expected behavioral effects of reform. For example, if a reform stimulates labor market participation it may increase tax revenue over time. This means a reform may (partly) pay for itself. Some of this intuition can be captured in the form of constraining the difference in net income between those out of the labor force and those in it (see Appendix). However, we do not yet explicitly encode expected macro trends as a result of reform.

Multi-step reform.

Finally, we developed
TaxSolver
with the explicit goal to assist politically viable reforms. For example, we allow existing tax rules to be kept outside of reform, as is sometimes a political demand for contentious tax rules. Another such functionality could be to support multi-step reform. It may not be politically feasible to enact optimal reform completely, because it is operationally infeasible to change many rules at once or there is limited political appetite for large-scale change. In such cases, it would be worthwhile to map out a multi-step reform where an optimal reform is reached through a number of intermittent reforms where each step is also optimal.

There are also limitations to
TaxSolver
in its current form. As noted above, our solution does not cover all tax rules. A more important limitation is that our approach requires a dataset of taxpayers that includes all relevant taxpayer characteristics, inputs and current taxes paid. In complex systems, assembling such a dataset can be challenging. However, a rich set is required such that fiscal guarantees cover the entire population, including niche cases, and that budget totals align with reality.

That said, we believe that
TaxSolver
can already help revolutionize real-world reform and that this can be badly needed. Many countries struggle to navigate the complexities of reform and this can lead to unwanted consequences. A case in point are the marginal pressures upwards of 80% faced by taxpayers in the Dutch system, but there are many more examples from across the globe. Given the central importance of taxation to the functioning of society, we hope that with
TaxSolver
we set the first steps to ensuring tax codes become forces for good instead of the well-intended but often self-defeating patchworks they sometimes become today.

All tax rules described above generally follow the assumptions listed in the main text of piecewise-linearity, single-input and heterogeneity. Using the fourth assumption of additivity, we can succinctly describe an entire tax code as a single formula. We use the concept of a ‘tax group’ to denote a set of taxpayers who have exactly the same absolute and marginal tax pressure as long as they have the same vector of inputs

Mathematically, we can then write the entire tax code for an individual

A.1.2
Constraints

After defining solver variables by including tax rules to the optimization problem, constraints can be added to limit the reform space.

Income constraints:

the most important constraint are individual or household income constraints. These can be defined as follows:

f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})\leq(1+\epsilon)y^{\prime}_{i},\quad\text{for i}=1,...,n.

(8)

Here,
f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})
represents the tax function for taxpayer
i
as defined in (
7
). The constraint setup above allows each individual’s tax pressure to change proportionally to their current tax pressure according to some
\epsilon
. If
\epsilon>0
we allow taxpayers to potentially pay
more
taxes than they were before. If
\epsilon<0
we guarantee that taxpayers only pay
less
taxes. The values for
\epsilon
can be customized for every taxpayer, which in turn allows very specific types of policy goals to be reached, like guaranteeing welfare transfers between taxpayers or providing assistance to specific groups. Income constraints can also be absolute, by simply replacing the right hand side of (
8
) with an absolute value. This can be useful to constrain taxpayers or households from falling into poverty.

Income constraints can ofcourse be grouped at any level of aggregation, like the household but also to target specific population groups. This simply requires summing both the left- and right hand sides across groups. Multiple constraints can be placed on the same individual or group, for example to set both upper and lower bounds on post-reform income.

Rate constraints:

this second type of constraint directly limits the solver variables in the optimization problem. For example, to limit marginal pressure at 50%, we can impose the additional
B
constraints:

\alpha_{b}\leq 0.5,\quad\forall b.

(9)

We call these ‘rate constraints’ that limit the marginal pressure taxpayers can experience. They can also be used to ensure progressivity of taxation (e.g.
\alpha_{b}\geq\alpha_{b-1}
) or enable other types of constraints on how tax rules look.

Budget constraints:

we might also want to constrain the loss (or gain) in tax revenue that is collected through the system between a reform and the current tax system. Note that although budget constraints are a variation of the income constraints introduced earlier, we specify them explicitly due to their policy relevance. By simply summing the tax functions of all taxpayers, we can then constrain this sum with respect to the old sum over taxes paid. This is illustrated below:

\sum_{i}[y^{\prime}_{i}-f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})]\leq C.

(10)

Here, the loss in total tax revenues is capped by some amount
C
.

Mirror constraints:

a last general type of constraint that allows for more specific restrictions on the solution space are ‘mirror constraints’. These serve the purpose of restricting the relative distance between two taxpayers. For example, policymakers may want to guarantee a certain difference in income between those working a minimum wage and those on benefits to keep labor participation incentives stable. These constraints can be added in the form:

f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})-f(x_{i^{\prime}},\mathcal{A})\leq C,

(11)

where
i^{\prime}
is the ‘mirror’ of
i
. They can also be used to limit the divergence in incomes between two taxpayers or households.

A.1.3
Objectives

Many objectives can be specified to weigh the value of different solutions. To prioritize budget efficiency, the left hand side of the budget constraint defined in (
10
) could be used. To prioritize complexity, a function on
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
could be defined. Generally, objective functions can become complex but can slow down runtime and limit optimality guarantees if they become non-linear
(
Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004a
)
.

A.1.4
Recovering and Reforming Tax Systems

Putting these elements together, we now illustrate how to recover an existing system’s rates,
\mathcal{A}
. Assume we have a dataset of
N
taxpayers that contains tax-relevant inputs, taxpayer characteristics and observed tax pressures for the current system. For ease of representation, there is only one tax-relevant input and only one tax group. The current system maps a vector of inputs
x
to a vector of taxes paid
y
, which are both
N\times 1
. The support of the system is
\Phi
. We can recover the current system’s rate parameters,
\mathcal{A}
, exactly by setting up the following optimization problem consisting of an objective and
N
‘tight’ income constraints:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle 0

(12)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})|_{\Phi}=y^{\prime}_{i},\quad\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

In this case, the objective function is set to 0 for illustrational purposes, such that the solver only attempts to find a solution that fits the constraints. This is done by varying
\mathcal{A}
such that the
N
constraints hold. Each of these
N
equations simply states that for every taxpayer the reformed system
f(\cdot)
with to-be-optimized rates
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
has to satisfy that the taxes paid per individual taxpayer in the reformed system are exactly equal to that in the old system. This means that if there were only two brackets in the tax function then each taxpayer’s tax function would be:
f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})=Z+\alpha_{1}g_{1}(x_{i})+g_{2}(x_{i}).
The optimization problem allows varying the rate parameters
\mathcal{A}=[Z,\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]
and our fiscal constraints mean it must do so in such a way that for every taxpayer the new tax pressure exactly equates to the old.

The unique solution to this problem is the original set of rates
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
, as long as there are sufficient observations (see below for the proof). This is also the reason why we did not have to specify an objective: we can simply look for the only feasible solution given the fiscal guarantees. We call this ‘recovering’ the existing system and it illustrates that the current system is in fact part of the solution space.

To find new tax systems requires loosening the income constraints such that new sets of rates
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
become feasible. For example, the four reforms described at the end of Section 3 in the main text are described below.

Targeted tax break:

to redistribute wealth to the middle 50% of incomes by foregoing tax revenue, but doing so in the cheapest way possible in terms of tax revenue can be setup as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}[y^{\prime}_{i}-f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})]

(13)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})|_{\Phi}\leq y^{\prime}_{i}(1+\epsilon),\quad\text{for}\quad i\in\quad[C_{\text{bottom\_quartile}},C_{\text{top\_quartile}}]

\displaystyle f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})|_{\Phi}\leq y^{\prime}_{i}(1+\delta),\quad\text{for}\quad i\in\quad[C_{\text{2nd\_quartile}},C_{\text{3rd\_quartile}}].

In this problem,
\epsilon
would be a small positive number allowing some income fluctuations for the bottom and top income quartiles.
\delta
would be a negative number, forcing tax pressure for the middle incomes to decrease.

Redistribution of wealth:

to pay for the above by allowing the top income to suffer some income loss can be set up as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle 0

(14)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})|_{\Phi}\leq y^{\prime}_{i}(1+\epsilon),\quad\text{for}\quad i\in\quad[C_{\text{bottom\_quartile}}]

\displaystyle f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})|_{\Phi}\leq y^{\prime}_{i}(1+\delta),\quad\text{for}\quad i\in\quad[C_{\text{2nd\_quartile}},C_{\text{3rd\_quartile}}]

\displaystyle f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})|_{\Phi}\leq y^{\prime}_{i}(1+\gamma),\quad\text{for}\quad i\in\quad[C_{\text{4th\_quartile}}]

\displaystyle\sum_{i}[y^{\prime}_{i}-f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})]\leq C.

Here,
\epsilon
is small and positive like before, ensuring the bottom incomes are not faced with a negative income shock.
\delta
is negative, indicating middle incomes receive reduced tax pressure. Finally,
\gamma
can be high to allow top incomes to suffer higher tax pressure. Instead of minimizing tax revenue loss, we cap it at
C
via the final constraint, which could be set to zero to guarantee budget neutrality.

Constraining marginal pressure:

Capping the marginal pressure that taxpayers experience while restraining the potential government loss to
C
in foregone tax revenue can be set up as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle 0

(15)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})|_{\Phi}\leq y^{\prime}_{i}(1+\epsilon),\quad\forall i

\displaystyle\alpha_{b,k}\leq 0.5,\quad\forall b,k

\displaystyle\sum_{i}[y^{\prime}_{i}-f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})]\leq C.

Restraining complexity could be done by setting non-tight income constraints and i) setting some complexity function as the objective function, or ii) strongly reducing the dimensions of
\Phi

TaxSolver
to find a solution in lower dimensionality. Both examples follow analogously from the above by either adding some function

into the objective, or modifying the parameter space of [
\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle M(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})|_{\Phi}\leq y^{\prime}_{i}(1+\epsilon),\quad\forall i

\displaystyle\alpha_{b,k}\leq 0.5,\quad\forall b,k

\displaystyle\sum_{i}[y^{\prime}_{i}-f(x_{i},\mathcal{A})]\leq C.

Proofs

The sum of a piecewise linear function is piecewise linear

Let
f(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
be piecewise linear functions on
\mathbb{R}
. Since
f
is piecewise linear, there exists a finite set of real numbers
\Phi_{f}=\{\phi_{x0},\phi_{x1},\dots,\phi_{xB}\}
such that
f
is linear on each interval between consecutive
x_{i}
. Similarly,
g
has a finite set
\Phi_{g}=\{\phi_{y0},\phi_{y1},\dots,\phi_{yB}\}
where it is linear on intervals between consecutive
y_{j}
.

Form a new set
\Phi
that contains all the endpoints from both functions:
\Phi=\{\Phi_{f},\Phi_{g}\}
. Sort
\Phi
in ascending order to get
\phi_{1}<\phi_{2}<\dots<\phi_{k}
. On each interval
[\phi_{i},\phi_{i+1}]
, both
f
and
g
are linear because
[\phi_{i},\phi_{i+1}]
does not contain any additional endpoints of
\Phi_{f}
or
\Phi_{g}
and there exist constants
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},d_{i}
such that for all
x\in[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
:
f(x)=a_{i}x+b_{i},\quad g(x)=c_{i}x+d_{i}
. The sum
h(x)=f(x)+g(x)
on
[s_{i},s_{i+1}]
is then
h(x)=f(x)+g(x)=(a_{i}x+b_{i})+(c_{i}x+d_{i})=(a_{i}+c_{i})x+(b_{i}+d_{i})
with derivative
a_{i}+c_{i}
which is also constant. Therefore,
h(\cdot)
is also piecewise linear.
\square

Setting tight income guarantees recovers the parameters of the current system

Assume
N
individuals, each with scalar input
x_{i}
and a true tax system given by
y_{i}=\sum_{b}\alpha_{b}g_{b}(x_{i},\Phi^{\star}_{k})
. Now assume we know the support of the system,
\Phi^{\star}_{k}
, and split the input
x_{i}
according to the bracketing function
g_{b}(\cdot,\Phi^{\star})
into
B
inputs
x_{i,b}
and set
y_{i}=\sum_{b}\alpha_{b}x_{i,b}
. If we then define tight income constraints, we get a system of
N
linear equations in
B
unknowns:
\textbf{y}=\text{X}\mathcal{A}
, where
y
is the
N\times 1
vector of outcomes,
X
is the
N\times K
matrix of inputs and
\mathcal{A}
the
B\times 1
vector of rates.

This matrix is of full rank if
N\geq B
and all columns are independent. The latter is the case per definition as the bracketing function splits the input into disjunct elements and the solution reduces to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}=(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}
.
\square

Representativeness.

Although our sample is reflective of a true system’s complexity, the data, and thus our reform, is not representative of the Dutch population in two important ways. First, our sample does not align with the Dutch population beyond simple univariate aggregates. For example, the overall proportion of fiscal partnerships aligns with the total population, as does the percentage of households living in a self-owned house. However, the interaction of the two is only representative if the two characteristics were completely independent from one another, which is unlikely. This limitation holds for all characteristics. In addition, we sample income distributions, asset distributions and number of children independently from other characteristics. This assumes that the distribution of e.g. the incomes of home owners is similar to those renting, which is likely also unrepresentative. Second, the most important input in the fiscal system --- pre-tax income --- is based on publicly available income percentiles. These do not contain aggregate statistics for the top income percentile and means we do not accurately represent the very top incomes.
11
11

11

The top income percentile is simply represented by “more than X EUR” in public statistics on personal incomes.
In sum, this case study accurately represents individual tax rules but does not represent macro outcomes, like total tax revenue.
