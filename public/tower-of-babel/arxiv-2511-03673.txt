OriFeel : Origami-Inspired Actuation for Force-Based Tactile Feedback on Ambient Surfaces
Shubham Rohal, Shijia Pan

Abstract
. People are constantly in touch with surfaces in their lives, such as a sofa, armrest, and table, making them natural tactile interfaces. Despite the recent advancements in shape-changing surfaces, current available solutions are often challenging to retrofit into ambient surfaces due to their bulky form factor or high power requirements. We present OriFeel , a foldable structure-enabled tactile feedback mechanism that leverages the structural properties of Miura-Ori fold to enable on-surface force actuation. The foldable structure allows the surfaces to provide perpendicular force via lateral actuation, resulting in a slim form factor that can be actuated via cable-based design using a servo motor. We evaluate the system with a real-world prototype and a user study. The user study shows that users can effectively distinguish multiple intensity levels.

1.
Introduction

Imagine the future ambient and interactive intelligence, where you can seamlessly interact with your home via touch – your sofa back can deliver remote hug or touch for immersive video calls, and your armrest can alert you about kitchen events via gentle “push" notification or interact via tactile effects.
The traditional vibration-based tactile surfaces lacks the ability to convey natural force-based feedback
(
1
)
.
Shape-changing display morphs the surface to present information in a spatial and intuitive way, which provides a promising direction towards our objective.
However, existing works on shape-changing surfaces, such as the pin array
(
7
;
18
)
or fluid pumps
(
13
;
6
)
, often utilizes an array of actuators to generate high-resolution haptic tactile feedback, resulting in large physical footprint and difficult to integrate into ambient environment, or high power consumption and difficult to scale to large surfaces.
Therefore, the research question we explore in this project is:
How can we enable force-based tactile feedback on ambient surfaces in a scalable and ubiquitous way?

We introduce an origami-inspired structural solution to surface tactile interaction, where the on-surface force actuation is achieve via folding movement of the structure.
Origami-based structures have been explored in sensor design
and robotics due to their ability to deliver coordinated motion distribution across a large area
(
21
)
.
We adopt Miura-ori fold in our design due to the following reasons.
First, it is a foldable structure that is
scalable
by repeating the pattern.
Second, since it can be expanded, it supports
ubiquitous
integration into ambient surfaces.
Finally, because it is foldable, it is naturally
actuatable
, suitable for tactile feedback.

However, integrating the Miura-Ori fold into ambient surfaces for interaction is not trivial and faces following challenges.
(C1) Although the paper origami is easy to fold, materials of ambient surfaces – such as sofa, armrest, and table – usually do not fold easily due to high bending stiffness
(
14
)
.
(C2) While the structure is foldable, the folding status may be unstable when the folding force is applied at inappropriate locations.
(C3) The actuation is indirect – the motor generates lateral pulling force and the structure converts it into vertical force/motion, which poses challenge for control.

To tackle these challenges, we design
OriFeel
, an origami-inspired tactile actuation mechanism. To enable the adaptation to ambient elastic surfaces, we adopt compliant mechanism at the folding line to enable desired structure response. To allow for stable and desired folding motion, we design a cable-based solution to enable expansion and contraction of the structure. Furthermore, to enable controlled actuation, we developed a firmware running on the embedded device to control the tactile feedback force.
We design and fabricate the prototype and conduct both system test and user study to evaluate
OriFeel
, and compared the system specs with other related work.
Our contributions are as follows.

•

We design a new mechanism for force-based tactile feedback on ambient surfaces by altering the surface structure with Miura-Ori fold.

•

We develop a cable-based control framework that works with this foldable structure to provide desired force feedback.

•

We fabricate a real-world prototype and conduct both system tests and a user study to evaluate our design.

2.
Related Work

OriFeel
is fundamentally a surface tactile device that provides force-based feedback via morphing. Therefore, we first discuss the comparison between different surface haptic solutions, followed by a specific comparison between shape-changing surface prior works. Finally, since we adopt the origami-inspired structure in our design, we also summarize the origami-based design in sensing and robotics.

2.1.
Vibration-based Surface Tactile Interface

There is substantial work on surface-based tactile interaction intended for a variety of applications,
such as remote interaction, immersive experience or new sense for visually impaired individuals.
With the advancements of smartphones and computers, touch screen become a common medium for interactions, providing visual feedback.
However, most of they cannot provide tactile feedback.
Some explore the electrovibration-based solutions to generate haptic feedback.
For example, TeslaTouch
(
2
)
uses an electrovibration layer on the touch screen to generate the electric pulses that alter the electric signal at the fingertip for tactile sensation.
Similarly, T-PaD
(
19
)
uses a thin piezo disk along with a glass disk to generate ultrasonic vibration and create a thin air turbulence layer that changes the friction perceived by the human touch.
These vibration based tactile feedback are often hard to be effectively integrated into soft surfaces such as sofa back or armrest.

2.2.
Shape-Changing Surfaces

The other category of surface tactile interfaces is shape-changing surfaces with a few major directions of work.

Pin array has been intensively explored to create a shape-changing display (refer to as 2.5D display) for applications such as remote interaction or immersive experiences.
For example, inFORM
(
7
)
and shapeCAD
(
18
)
are representatives of this design, with an array of pins being actuated by an array of actuator.
These solutions generally use an electromagnetic motor (AC/DC/stepper motors) to control each pin, producing strong force and precise height to produce expressive patterns for interaction.
However, this type of systems requires a high number of actuators and are often too bulky to be integration into ambient surfaces.

To counter the high actuator requirement of the pin array, pneumatic-based methods have been explored
(
,
)
.
These methods use air pressure and airflow or other liquids to change the shape of soft materials for tactile feedback.
However, the components required to generate high pressure or to generate airflow make these solutions bulky and difficult to implement in real world.
As an alternative, Schultz et al
(
16
)
use multiple embedded electroosmotic pumps (EEOP) of size few millimeters to create a haptic feedback display.
But the power requirement for such methods are still very high.

Shape Memory Alloy (SMA) is also used for achieving surface shape-changing, where SMA (mostly nitinol alloy) moves to a specified shape when high current is applied.
For example, Surflex
(
4
)
uses SMA to change the shape of tangible material, Besse et al
(
3
)
uses shape memory polymer to create complex array of actuators.
However, these methods require high power and generate heat up to
60^{\circ}
C in some cases, which makes them unsuitable for ubiquitous applications.

Tangible Origami Structure

Origami, meaning folding (ori) paper (gami), is an art form originate in Japan.
The structural properties of origami folds has been explored in many fields, such as structure engineering, space exploration, sensor construction, etc.

Origami-inspired design is also adopted in HCI.
For example, MiuraKit
(
6
)
, Foldio
(
12
)
and PaperTouch
(
22
)
explore origami-based fabrication toolkit or pipeline to enable tangible object interactions.
While there exist another set of work that uses origami for actuators.
For example, PneUI
(
20
)
, Miurakit
(
6
)
and aerorph
(
13
)
uses the soft changing material along with pneumatic pumps to generate programmable structure, that uses airflow to generate desired shape or folding.
However, these methods generally require high power.

3.1.
Compliant Design For Folding

Common ambient surfaces, such as a sofa, armrest, and table, are often elastic or rigid, and unlike paper, these materials are difficult to bend due to high bending stiffness.
Compliant mechanisms are often used as a structural solution to enable bending for these materials.
Our goal to use compliant mechanism design here is to enable the bending of Miura-Ori fold’s folding lines while maintaining the stability of the facets.
Our structural design intuition are twofolds: (1) A geometric discontinuity between two connected planes leads to stress concentration at the discontinuity, promoting localized deformation under bending loads ; (2) When two connected planes have different thicknesses, the rotational moment is concentrated at the thin horizontal plane
(
,
)
.
Therefore, we design the complaint mechanism for the folding line of the Miura-Ori fold as shown in Figure
4
A.
Two beams of thickness
T

\theta

These two beams are connected by a third thin beam of thickness
T/2
in horizontal orientation, marked by a thin box.
Together, they form a flexure joint-based compliant mechanism
(
10
)
.
When thick beams are subjected to the horizontal inward force, it forms a couple (moment), with inward force at the base and outward force at the top.

3.2.
Lateral Actuation for Vertical Force

Our design goal is to generate vertical force-based tactile feedback.
One of the advantages of using a foldable structure to realize this goal is the possibility of a slim form factor of the actuator, where the vertical motion/force is controlled by the lateral actuation.

Since the structure is symmetrical, we simplify the analysis to one half of the structure. We consider the force analysis of this setting is similar to the classic ladder problem
(
,
)
, as shown in Figure
4
B, and the top contact point as a roller joint on a frictionless wall, similar to
(
17
)
. We simplify the Miura-ori fold facet as a rigid beam.

We assume that there are a load
N
applied on top of the beam and a lateral force
F_{l}
applied at the center of the beam, and the structure reaches equilibrium.
Assuming that there is no deformation of the structure, the different forces acting on the structure are shown in Figure
4
C.
In the equilibrium state, the net force acting along
x
and
y
axes and the moment will be zero.
Therefore,
\sum F_{x}=F_{a}+F_{l}-R_{b}=0
, and
\sum F_{y}=R_{a}-mg-N=0
.

F_{a}
is the friction force at the base and can be defined as
F_{a}=\mu R_{a}
, where
\mu
is the friction coefficient.
Similarly, the moment
M
at the base is also zero,
\sum M=F_{l}(h/2)+mg(q/2)-R_{b}h+Nq=0
.
Considering the length
p
is constant, the value of

h=p\cos(\theta/2)
and
q=p\sin(\theta/2)

Substituting these values, we will get
\sum M=F_{l}(p/2)\cos(\theta/2)+mg(p/2)\sin(\theta/2)-(\mu(mg+N)+F_{l})p\cos(\theta/2)+Np\sin(\theta/2)=0

3.3.
Cable-based Control for Tactile Feedback

To realize lateral actuation, we adopt the cable-based control approach, where only cables are integrated into the foldable layer, as shown in Figure
6
.
This design has a few advantages.
First, it allows slim form factor of the actuatable structure with flexible actuator placement.
Second, it allows flexible actuation resolution – whether the cable is fixed to the facet determines whether the unit pattern is individually controlled.

We adapt the facet of the foldable structure by constructing two holes for cable embedding, as shown in Figure
6
.
The black rectangles notate the compliant mechanism design.
The tension force acting on the facet of the
OriFeel
foldable structure treats it like a lever with center at the top.
Increasing lever length can generate more rotational moment, but also make it susceptible to bending.
To prevent such a scenario,
OriFeel
structure includes cable embedding holes at the center of the facet.

The same hole-based cable embedding design allows for multiple foldable units to be controlled using a single cable.
This reduces the number of actuators required to control the
OriFeel
folding structure.
Figure
6
C shows an example of how multiple units can be actuated by one actuation cable.
Similarly, multiple cables controlling the equal structure units can be actuated by one actuator, further reducing the actuators required.
However, the force from the actuator gets distributed over more units, and it also limits the number of actuation patterns that can be generated.

4.
Implementation

This section presents the implementation details of our
OriFeel
prototype.
The prototype maintains a thin profile with 10 mm in heights (this does not include the height of the servo motor because its placement is flexible), and only require one actuator to actuate 130 mm
\times
180 mm area.
Figure
7
shows the main components of the
OriFeel
prototype across different layers: (1) foldable structure anchor, (2) elastic Miura-Ori fold structure, (3) supporting base, and (4) actuator consisting servo motor and gear extension.

4.1.
Foldable Structure Anchor

When the foldable module contract or expand, the individual unit patterns will be displayed from its original position due to the inter-connectivity between unit patterns.
Therefore, we adopt an anchor to keep the structure from moving.
The anchor structure is an umbrella shaped elastic plug, where the knob will be pressed through the foldable structure and the supporting base and the cap enhances the contact stability.
The anchor is made out of the same flexible material and it can deform multiple times without plastic deformation.

4.2.
Miura-Ori Fold Structure Fabrication

We use Shapr3D
(
15
)
modeling software to generate printable STL files for the Muira-Ori fold structure and fabricate both rigid and soft version.
The rigid structure is fabricated using Polylactic acid(PLA) plastic filament on an Fused Deposition Modeling(FDM) printer, while the elastic structure is fabricated using the Formlabs 50A resin
(
8
)
on Formlabs 4B printer
(
9
)
.
The resin presents material properties similar to silicone with a modulus of elasticity 1.8 MPa
(
8
)
.
Each facet is printed with two 2 millimeter holes separated by 5 millimeters at the center to enable cable embedding.

The printed foldable module consists of
3\times 4
unit pattern.
These 12 units are actuated and controlled with four cables with 1 in first row, 2 in second row, and 1 in third row in Figure
7
.
This configuration allows
OriFeel
to generate tactile force evenly across the units, which is discuss further in Section
5.3.3
.
The dimension of the prototype follows
x=22mm
and
\beta=70^{\circ}
.

4.3.
Supporting Base

The
OriFeel
foldable structure in our prototype sits on a supporting base, designed to approximate the surface onto which the
OriFeel
system is integrated.
The base plate is constructed with Polylactic Acid(PLA) plastic using Ender3 V3 3D printer
(
5
)
.
The base plate also contains openings for embedded cables to pass through and connect to the actuator.

4.4.
Actuator with Servo Motor and Gear Extension

The prototype is actuated by a servo motor placed underneath the supporting base with a wheel to reel the cables.
DS-3218 servo motor is used, which can generate up to 20 kg force
(
11
)
.
Since the servo motors can rotate only
180^{\circ}
, the amount of embedded cable it can pull is very limited.
As a result, a connecting wheel extension with larger circumference is used to increase effective length pulling.
When rotated clockwise the structure contracts, rotation in anticlockwise direction release the tension applied and structure expands.
The extension wheel is 4 centimeters in diameter and is constructed using PLA plastic filament on the FMD printer.

5.2.
System Tests

5.2.1.
Power Consumption

OriFeel
uses a servo motor to generate folding actuation, which is controlled using an Arduino Nano 33 microcontroller.
The servo motor in
OriFeel
operates at a 5V voltage and draws 1.9A of current, while the Arduino Nano 33 is powered through a 5V USB port, and its normal operation draws 0.025 A of current.
Therefore,
OriFeel
’s power consumption is 9.625 Watts.

5.2.2.
Latency

The actuation motor used by
OriFeel
prototype controls the speed of different actuation.
The actuation servo motor used by
OriFeel
prototype can generate
180^{\circ}
motion in 0.48 seconds.
Increasing the operation voltage of the servo motor actuator to 8.4 V can further reduce this time to 0.39 seconds.
However, this can increase the system’s power consumption from 9.625 Watts to 23.645 Watts.

5.5.
User Study

We further investigate the effectiveness of
OriFeel
system to convey distinguish the feedback to users through a User study.
For this we recruited 10 participants between the ages of 19 and 30 years, with 5 male and 5 female participants.

5.5.1.
Study Design

We asked the users to interact with the
OriFeel
prototype placed at three different locations: 1. Palm, 2. Forearm, 3. Back.
To generate the tactile sensation, the
OriFeel
prototype is actuated at 4 different actuation levels:
40^{\circ}
(1),
80^{\circ}
(2),
120^{\circ}
(3), and
160^{\circ}
(4).
Various combinations of four actuation levels are tested, and participants were asked the following questions:

1. Can you feel the difference between the two sensations?

2. If yes, then which sensation is stronger?

Goal of this user study is to establish if users can differentiate between different tactile patterns generated by
OriFeel
prototype.
The study also tests how feedback location on different part on body impact user’s ability to differentiate between different feedback combinations.

5.5.2.
Feedback Differentiation Analysis

Figure
11
shows the results on how accurately users are able to differentiate between different tactile actuation for all three locations on the body.
We observe that overall, users can effectively differentiate between different feedback combinations effectively,
except for feedback combinations 2-3 and 3-4.
This is because, as the structure contracts, the texture sensation on the skin changes
Some tactile actuation produces texture similar to each other, in this case texture 2-3 and 3-4.
However, every participant is able to differentiate between tactile actuation combination 2-4 effectively.

5.5.3.
Feedback location Analysis

We also evaluate how the user’s ability to differentiate two pattern changes when feedback is provided at different bodyparts.

We observe that across palm and forearm some participants are not able to distinguish between feedback combination 2-3 and 3-4.
However, across back all the participants are able to distinguish between feedback combination 2-3.
The feedback combination 3-4 are challenging to distinguish across all three bodyparts.
Despite this most of the participants are able to differentiate all six feedback combinations.
This shows
OriFeel
system can convey meaningful distinguishable information.

6.
Future Work

OriFeel
system presented in this paper demonstrate feasibility and potential to provide tactile feedback at multiple point on ambient surfaces with limited numbers of actuator points.
This section discusses the future directions we wish to explore.

6.1.
Rich Tactile Effects Control

OriFeel
system shows the possibility to actuate a larger area with less numbers of actuators because of the structure interconnectivity between unit patterns.
However, this interconnectivity also limits the systems’ ability to generate high resolution feedback.
Therefore, as the future work, we would like to explore the trade-offs between the hardware complexity and the richness of tactile effects the system can achieve.
We will work toward developing a control algorithm for multiple actuator control taking structure contraints into modeling.

6.2.
Integrating Sensing Capability with
OriFeel

This work explores the actuation of ambient surfaces without sensing capability.
To allow for bidirectional communication, we will explore integration sensing components to
OriFeel
. Since the actuation causing surface dimension change, we will explore the trade-off between the sensing capability and quality and actuation effects. Furthermore, miniaturazing the hardware with both sensing and actuation components will also be explored.

7.
Conclusion

We present
OriFeel
, an origami-inspired tactile feedback system.
We introduce a new actuation mechanism via foldable structure to enable ubiquitous and scalable design that can be integrated into ambient surfaces.
By adapting Miura-Ori fold structure with compliant michanism, we are able to implement foldable structure with both rigid and soft materials.
We design a cable based control solution to allow slim form factor.
We tested our system with various physical tests and a user study with 10 participants.
During the user study, the participants are effectively able to distinguish the tactile feedback generated by
OriFeel
.
