Exploratory analysis of regional factors influencing early childhood caries status in 5-year-old children: evidence from national oral health surveys of China, 2005–2015
Authors: Langxin Pan, Yangfan Peng, Xinzhu Zhou, Shuguo Zheng, Jian Liu, Xiangyu Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCurrently, regional disparities of early childhood caries (ECC) remained underexplored. This study aimed to investigate the associations of regional factors with temporal variations of ECC among 5-year-old children in China using data from the 3rd National Oral Health Survey of China (the 2005 survey) and the 4th National Oral Health Survey of China (the 2015 survey).MethodsPost-stratification-weighted data combining the two rounds of cross-sectional National Oral Health Surveys were used for establishing negative binomial regression models, with the number of decayed, missing and filled deciduous teeth (dmft) served as the outcome variable. Regional factors, including geographic region, per capita Gross Domestic Product (GDP), proportion of public health expenditure, dentists per million population, economic Gini coefficient and health Gini coefficient, were enrolled as explanatory variables. At individual level, sociodemographic characteristics, early childhood factors, oral health behaviors and parent-reported oral health status were added to the regression models as covariates. Sensitivity analyses were conducted on those whose mother finished the questionnaires.ResultsThe study sample included a total of 24,014 study participants (11,099 in the 2005 survey and 12,915 in the 2015 survey). Analysis revealed that in the 2005 survey, children in northeastern China showed significantly worse dental caries status compared to western regions, while provinces with higher GDP per capita values exhibited slightly poorer dental caries outcomes. In the 2015 survey, both northeastern and eastern regions demonstrated significantly higher childhood caries prevalence than western regions, with a positive correlation remaining in public health expenditure ratio and income Gini coefficient with caries status. Analyses of pooled survey data exhibited disparities between survey years, while geographic region and GDP per capita maintained statistical significance. Sensitivity analysis showed consistent trends.ConclusionDental caries of 5-year-old children in China was associated with regional factors such as geographic location, GDP per capita, public health expenditure ratio and income Gini coefficient under certain circumstances, highlighting the need for regionally differentiated policy-making and more balanced public health resource allocation in comprehensive ECC prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08576-0.Keywords: Early Childhood Caries (ECC), Regional factors, National oral health survey, Negative binomial regression model, China

Introduction

The high prevalence of early childhood caries (ECC) is a common phenomenon both in China and worldwide. ECC was nearly the most prevalent infectious disease in children, being five times more common than childhood asthma, four times more than early childhood obesity, and 20 times more than diabetes [1]. The results of 2015 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study revealed that dental caries of the primary dentition was the 12th most prevalent disease in all ages combined [2]. The prevalence of untreated caries in deciduous teeth peaked at 5-year-olds [3]. Socioeconomic development in China from 2005 to 2015 brought changes in dietary patterns and psychosocial factors, increasing the prevalence of ECC. According to the 4th National Oral Health Survey in 2015, caries prevalence among children aged 3, 4, and 5 years old was 50.8%, 63.6%, and 71.9% respectively in the official report, with mean scores of the number of decayed, missing and filled deciduous teeth (dmft) found as 2.28, 3.4, and 4.24, respectively. Among 5-year-olds, caries prevalence rose by 5.9% and the mean dmft score increased by 0.74 since 2005, with 95.8% of tooth decay remaining untreated, indicating considerable treatment needs and disease burden. Disparities were also observed between rural and urban children, with rural children exhibiting a higher caries prevalence of 73.4% compared to 70.4% in their urban counterparts [4, 5].

Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of regional factors in ECC status [6–10]. Variations in economic development, uneven distribution of dental care resources, and differing public health strategies between regions create distinct oral health landscapes. These disparities manifest through multiple mediating factors – particularly in household health awareness and availability of primary prevention programs. However, research on ECC among 5-year-old children in China has largely focused on individual-level risk factors, with limited attention to the mediating role of macro-environmental factors and regional heterogeneity. To address this gap, this study establishes a national decade-long database to analyze developmental trends and risk factors of ECC based on the national oral health surveys. Within a longitudinal framework, we examine the interactions between individual and regional-level influences.

Therefore, this study aimed to conduct an exploratory analysis of regional factors for temporal variations of ECC among 5-year-old children by comparing data from the 3rd and 4th National Oral Health Surveys of China.

Methods

Data source

This study used cross-sectional data from the 3rd (2005) and 4th (2015) National Oral Health Surveys of China, which were carried out with official authorization and ethic approval from the National Health Committee of the People’s Republic of China and the Chinese Stomatological Association in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants consented to attend the surveys. Ethical clearance and registration details (registry, trial registration number, and data of registration) were not required for the present study as it was a secondary analysis of anonymized data from existing national surveys and data modelling but not a clinical trial.

The 3rd National Oral Health Survey of China (abbreviated as “the 2005 survey”) and the 4th National Oral Health Survey of China (abbreviated as “the 2015 survey”) both used a multistage, stratified, equal proportion, random sampling methodology [4, 5]. The 2015 survey covered all 31 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities in the mainland China, while the 2005 survey covered 30 of them, except for Xizang Autonomous Region. For the 5-year-old age group, the sampling stages were: (1) provinces, autonomous regions or municipalities; (2) urban or rural areas; (3) streets or towns; (4) kindergartens. In the 2005 survey, in total 23,365 children aged 5 years old were recruited for oral health examination, but with the limitation of budgets, only about half of them, 12,692 (54.3%) children’s parents, were invited to answer the questionnaire. In the 2015 survey, 13,992 5-year-olds received oral health examination, with all of their parents responded to the questionnaire survey.

The procedures of oral health examination referred to official recommendation by the World Health Organization (WHO) as recorded in the 4th edition (published in 1997) [11] and the 5th edition (published in 2013) of Oral Health Surveys: Basic Methods [12], respectively. Diagnosis and recording codes for dental caries in primary dentition all followed the WHO criteria. Three examiners were designated per province for both surveys, resulting in a total of 90 examiners participating in the oral examinations for the 30 provinces, municipalities and autonomous regions of the mainland China involved in both surveys. Before the on-site investigations commenced, the National Survey Expert Group systematically trained all survey investigators in theoretical knowledge and standardized oral examination protocols. All examiners underwent standard consistency testing with calibration examiners, achieving Kappa values above 0.8 for caries diagnosis across all provinces, autonomous regions or municipalities. During field investigations, the National Survey Quality Control Group randomly rechecked 5% of participants’ oral health examination records. For the 5-year-old age group, the 2005 survey demonstrated a Kappa value of 0.94 for caries rechecks, while the 2015 survey showed a Kappa value of 0.95, indicating high consistency in examinations. The questionnaire survey, which details and preliminary findings were published in the official reports of the National Oral Health Surveys of China [4, 5], had covered children’s family demographics, early-life factors, oral health knowledge / attitudes / behaviors of children / guardians at survey time points, and guardians’ self-rated health perceptions of their children.

Variables selection

This study utilized dmft score for primary dentition as the outcome variable to assess caries status, calculated as the sum of decayed teeth (dt), missing teeth due to caries (mt), and filled teeth (ft). With factor screening, 16 potential individual-level explanatory variables across four main categories were included to serve as individual factors finally: sociodemographic characteristics (place of residence, sex, ethnic group, parental education level, and household income level), early childhood factors (birth weight, feeding patterns, and age of starting toothbrushing), oral health behaviors (daily toothbrushing frequency, sugar intake frequency, bedtime sugary intake, and last dental visit time), and parent-reported oral health status (dental pain experience in the past year, parent-rated oral health, parental oral health attitude, and parental oral health knowledge).

Regional factors potentially influencing caries status were selected based on previous research evidence with regards to similar disease patterns [13–15]. Finally, six regional indicators were included: geographic region, per capita Gross Domestic Product (GDP), proportion of public health expenditure, dentists per million population, economic Gini coefficient, and health Gini coefficient. According to the National Bureau of Statistics of People’s Republic of China [16], the 30 provinces, autonomous regions or municipalities involved in the present study were categorized into four regions: Western region (11 provinces, autonomous regions or municipalities): Inner Mongolia, Guangxi, Chongqing, Sichuan, Guizhou, Yunnan, Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai, Ningxia, and Xinjiang; Central region (6 provinces): Shanxi, Anhui, Jiangxi, Henan, Hubei, and Hunan; Northeast region (3 provinces): Liaoning, Jilin, and Heilongjiang; Eastern region (10 provinces or municipalities): Beijing, Tianjin, Hebei, Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Fujian, Shandong, Guangdong, and Hainan.

The per capita GDP data used in this study were sourced from the “Regional GDP” tables under the “National Economic Statistics” sections of the 2006 China Statistical Yearbook (2005 data) [17] and the 2016 China Statistical Yearbook (2015 data) [18]. Data for the proportion of public health expenditure were obtained from the 2006 China Statistical Yearbook (2005 data) and 2016 China Statistical Yearbook (2015 data). The distribution of oral health workforce sourced from the 2006 China Health Statistical Yearbook (reflecting 2002 provincial-level dentist distribution actually) [19] and the research report based on the 2015 survey [20]. Socioeconomic and health inequalities were reflected by 2005 and 2014 data from the research on human development index after inequality adjustment in Chinese provinces and regions which calculated provincial-level income and health Gini coefficients across six years (2003–2017) [21]. According to the paper, both of the income Gini coefficient and the health Gini coefficient are calculated using the direct computation method. The income Gini coefficient is derived from income data using the formula , Where yi and yj represent individual income values, n is the number of observations, and is the mean income. The health Gini coefficient is computed by applying the same direct method to health resource data, using the number of hospital beds per region as a proxy for health resource distribution [21].

Statistical analysis

Post-stratification hierarchical weighting method used in previous studies [22, 23] was employed to weight for provincial-level administrative units (30 strata), place of residence (urban or rural), sex (boy or girl) and ethnicity (Han ethnic group, or minorities), in line with the 5th National Demographic Census of China. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v26.0 and R software v4.4, with two-tailed tests and a significance threshold of P < 0.05. Given the over-dispersion characteristics of dmft score as the outcome variable, negative binomial regression models were fitted. Multilevel approaches were not adopted since the Interclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) among province-level administrative units was < 0.05 in the null model with dmft score serving as the outcome variable. Regression analysis and sensitivity assessments were conducted for the overall study population and the subgroup whose questionnaire was responded by their mothers.

In the 2005 survey, significant urban-rural disparities in caries status were observed (P < 0.001), with ethnic minorities demonstrating more severe caries than the Han ethnicity (P < 0.001). Caries status progressively worsened with decreasing parental education level (P < 0.001) and household income (P < 0.001). No significant difference in caries status was found between low-birth-weight children and their normal birth weight counterparts (P > 0.05). Significant differences in caries status existed across feeding practices (P < 0.001). Later initiation of toothbrushing correlated with worse caries status (P < 0.001). More pronounced caries experience was associated with: reduced daily toothbrushing frequency (P < 0.05), increased frequency of sugary food consumption (P < 0.001), higher frequency of pre-sleep sugary food intake (P < 0.001), and more recent last dental visit (P < 0.001). Increased frequency of toothache experiences in the past year (P < 0.001) and declines in self-rated oral health or oral health awareness level (P < 0.001) were associated with worse caries status. No significant differences were observed between other characteristic categories (P > 0.05).

As for the 2015 survey, most of the tendencies kept similar as above, whereas a few differences were exhibited as follows: no significant ethnic disparities in caries status (P > 0.05); although household income levels still demonstrated significant differences in caries status (P < 0.01), the gradient trend across income tiers weakened; significant differences persisted across toothbrushing frequency categories (P < 0.001) with diminished stepwise progression; oral health attitude levels showed significant differences (P < 0.01) without clear gradation; oral health awareness levels exhibited statistically significant differences (P < 0.001) but lacked complete stepwise characteristics.

The mean values, standard deviations and value ranges for regional factors were presented in Table 2. Results from negative binomial regression models using the study samples in the 2005 survey and the 2015 survey, respectively, were shown in Table 3. The 2005-survey adjusted model revealed regional-factor associations: the region, GDP per capita and dentists per million population correlated with caries status. The Northeast region showed significantly higher caries prevalence than the Western region (rate ratio (RR) = 1.63, P < 0.001). Higher level of socioeconomic development was associated with higher severity of caries (RR = 1.34, P < 0.001), whereas higher dentist density was weakly associated with lower caries risk (RR closer to 0.99, P < 0.001). No significant associations were found for public health expenditure ratio, income Gini coefficient, and health Gini coefficient (P > 0.05). On the other hand, the 2015-survey adjusted model exhibited different trend of regional-factor associations. Compared to the Western region, caries prevalence remained higher in the Northeast (RR = 1.74, P < 0.001) and the Eastern region (RR = 1.56, P < 0.001), however lower in the Central region (RR = 0.81, P < 0.001). The association between public health expenditure ratio and caries risk was significant (RR = 1.05, P < 0.05), while the income Gini coefficient showed similar trend (RR = 1.01, P < 0.01). Other regional factors showed non-significance (P > 0.05).

Discussion

This study reveals substantial heterogeneity and changes in the association between regional factors and dmft scores among Chinese 5-year-olds between the 2005 survey and the 2015 survey. The Northeast persistently demonstrated the highest risk of caries. Notably, the Eastern region underwent a significant transformation, shifting from a non-significant association in the 2005 survey to a significant risk factor in the 2015 survey. In contrast, living in the Central region was significantly associated with lower dmft scores in the 2015 survey.

Persistent gaps in oral health outcomes between different regions highlight the uneven nature of China’s rapid development. These disparities are not just economic, but reflect deeper and more structural issues [24]. Liao et al. examined ECC and contributing factors in 3-5-year-olds across eastern, central, and northwestern China [25]. Children in western regions had lower sweet food intake and higher exclusive breastfeeding rates (≥ 6 months), while eastern parents exhibited higher socioeconomic status and better oral health awareness. Geographic disparities persisted after adjustment, pointing to unaccounted regional variations such as diet, water fluoride, and health policies as potential influences on ECC prevalence.

The elevated caries risk in the Eastern region during the study period is likely linked to rapid urbanization and corresponding socioeconomic changes. As a leading economic zone, the region underwent a marked dietary shift, reflected in increased consumption of sugars, beverages, and ultra-processed snacks. These findings illustrate the complex ties between economic growth and oral health. Without targeted policy action, dietary changes and urban pressures can offset the advantages of economic advancement. As supported by broader literature, introducing taxes on sugary drinks, limiting junk food advertising, and expanding school fluoride initiatives could be helpful to counter these effects. On the other hand, the reduction in caries risk observed in the Central region may be associated with its balanced socioeconomic development, preservation of traditional dietary habits, and effective public health policies. The lower level of economic development may have helped avoid the rapid dietary transition seen in Eastern China. National oral health program was launched in 2008, initially offering pit and fissure sealants to children in central and western regions. The program was expanded in 2012 to include fluoride applications for preschool children. From 2014, the project was renamed as the National Oral Disease Comprehensive Intervention Program for Children and extended to all 31 provincial-level units in the Mainland China [26]. Despite improvements in oral health status of target groups, the project continues to face challenges related to limited local coverage and financial constraints.

The influence of economic development showed a weakened tendency and changed in magnitude and direction over time. GDP per capita emerged as a risk predictor in the 2005 survey; however, its effect was no longer statistically significant in the 2015 survey. Conversely, income inequality (Gini coefficient) emerged as a significant risk factor in the 2015 survey, suggesting socioeconomic disparities are a key driver of oral health inequalities. This evolution aligns with the theory of epidemiological transition, where the relationship between wealth and health changes as societies develop [27]. In the initial phases of economic growth, there may be an increase in purchasing power for sugary snacks, thereby elevating the risk of dental caries. However, at more advanced stages of economic development, these adverse effects are likely mitigated by positive factors, including enhanced oral health literacy, improved access to dental care, and increased investment in public health initiatives [28]. This finding emphasizes the crucial importance of implementing strong preventive oral health policies parallel to economic development.

A higher proportion of public health expenditure was associated with a slight increase in caries risk in the 2015 survey. This counterintuitive result suggests that general increases in health funding may not automatically benefit oral health. It is plausible that these funds were primarily allocated to tertiary hospital care or managing infectious and chronic diseases, rather than being directed toward targeted preventive oral health initiatives, reflecting a known gap in many health systems where oral health prevention is under-prioritized [29]. Alternatively, higher public health spending may serve as a proxy for regions facing greater overall health challenges. It should be noted, however, that this study did not have access to specific data on oral health expenditures; as a result, the use of general public health spending may not accurately reflect investments in oral care programs, which is a limitation in interpreting the observed association.

The findings concerning oral health resources reveal a complex picture. The dentist-to-population ratio showed a significant but minimal protective effect in the 2005 survey, which disappeared in the 2015 survey. This indicates that merely increasing the number of dental professionals, without a specific preventive focus, has limited population-level effectiveness. In China, the number of dentists per million people increased from 50 in the 2005 survey to 140 in the 2015 survey - still far below levels in high-income countries. The United Kingdom had 515 dentists per million people in 2020 [30, 31], and Japan had 830 [32, 33]. The shortage is even more pronounced in pediatric dentistry. In 2020, with a population of 253.38 million children aged 0–14, China had only 4,860 registered pediatric dental specialists - equivalent to 19.2 per million children [34]. This limited workforce was further constrained by structural gaps. Most primary hospitals in China lacked dedicated pediatric dental departments, relying instead on general dentists to manage children’s oral health needs. Thus, improving oral health outcomes among Chinese children requires a multipronged approach, not only expanding and specializing the dental workforce, but also implementing comprehensive prevention programs and enhancing health education to encourage timely and preventive use of services.

In 2020, the WHO set a global target of a 50% caries-free rate for 5-year-olds [35], which China currently falls far short of. In response, the Chinese government has introduced a series of policies emphasizing preventive care and oral health promotion. Key initiatives include the “Healthy China 2030” Plan and the “Healthy Oral Action Plan (2019–2025)”, which aim to integrate oral health into public health systems through school- and community-based interventions [36–38]. These policy measures align with several findings of this study. Community-based prevention, including using maternal and child health networks might overcome structural barriers of shortage of pediatric dentists and uneven resource distribution. However, their success depends on funding for preventive care, as highlighted by our findings on general health expenditure. Long-term success will require committed funding, effective implementation, and continuous monitoring of oral health outcomes, particularly for vulnerable groups.

Some limitations needed to be kept in mind when extrapolating the findings of this study. First, its cross-sectional design using two epidemiological surveys limited causal inference, primarily revealing potential associations rather than causation. Second, the selected regional factors, though reflecting some area-specific characteristics related to ECC, required refinement in relevance with oral health. Third, this study utilized databases from National Oral Health Surveys performed in 2005 and 2015, now a decade old. Nevertheless, given China’s gradual integration of oral health surveillance into the non-communicable disease (NCD) monitoring framework and the absence of newly released surveillance data, these remained the most comprehensive and temporally proximate national large-scale survey datasets currently accessible to researchers. As China transitioned from large-scale cross-sectional surveys to longitudinal chronic disease monitoring systems, future longitudinal studies could better elucidate caries progression and its interaction with regional contexts.

Conclusion

This study reveals significant regional disparities in dental caries among Chinese children, with persistent high risk in the Northeast and emerging risks in the East. Targeted public health policies, equitable resource allocation, and comprehensive preventive strategies are urgently needed to address these disparities and improve children’s oral health outcomes.
