Impact of Medical Crisis on the Competency of Surgical Conduct Regarding Departmental, Institutional and Regional Distribution in Korea: A National Insurance Claim Data Analysis
Authors: Jaeeun Song, Kyungmin Kang, Jungsook Cho, Woo-Jin Kim, Seonkyu Lee, Yujin Jeong, Hee Jeong Kim, In-Seob Lee, Jong Lyul Lee, Ji Hoon Kim, Geun Dong Lee, Sung-Hoon Kim, Sang Hoon Song, Tae Won Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn February 2024, a nationwide medical crisis erupted in South Korea following the mass resignation of over 90% of trainee physicians in protest against the government’s healthcare reform policy. Amid the ongoing crisis, the government initiated a series of restructuring activities aimed at concentrating high-complexity surgeries and critical care at tertiary hospitals, while redistributing low-complexity procedures to hospitals and clinics. However, the nationwide impact of the reform policy on high-complexity surgical practices, particularly in terms of institutional type, departmental specialty, and regional distribution, has not yet been assessed.MethodsUsing national insurance claims data from the Korean Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service, we analyzed 914 high-complexity surgical procedures across tertiary hospitals (n = 47), general hospitals (n = 331), and hospitals/clinics (n = 37,888). Claims data from February to July 2023 (pre-crisis) were compared with those from February to July 2024 (post-crisis), stratified by institutional type, medical specialty, and geographic region.ResultsAfter the medical crisis, overall high-complexity surgical claims decreased by 10% (P < 0.001), with a 19% decrease in tertiary hospitals (P < 0.001), 6% increase in general hospitals (P = 0.115), and 27% increase in hospitals/clinics (P < 0.001). The decreased capacity for oncological surgery in tertiary hospitals by 16% (P < 0.001) was partly compensated by increased volume in general hospitals by 23% (P = 0.001). In terms of medical specialty, shifts from tertiary to general hospital in cardiothoracic surgery (1%, P = 0.636), neurosurgery (1%, P = 0.765), and interventional radiology (0%, P = 0.186) were negligible. Oncological surgery in the capital region, which is home to a higher concentration of tertiary hospitals, decreased by 17%, while oncological surgery in around-capital and non-capital regions increased by 11% and 5%, respectively.ConclusionDuring the Korean medical crisis in 2024, nationwide surgical capacity and systems for maintaining competency were significantly compromised. Although some procedures were offset by redistribution to lower-tier institutions or regional hospitals, most high-complexity surgeries—particularly those requiring critical care—did not recover.

INTRODUCTION

In February 2024, a major medical crisis unfolded as residents and junior specialists across South Korea initiated a mass walkout, protesting against the government’s policy package because it included a unilateral plan to increase medical school admission quota by 67% annually.1 The government argued that this measure was necessary to address the shortage of doctors in underserved areas and meet the growing healthcare demands of an aging population. However, junior doctors and medical students strongly contended that the reform would not resolve the underlying issues in the healthcare system. They feared that the rapid expansion of medical school admissions would compromise the quality of medical education and inadequately address the lack of support for essential medical specialties such as emergency medicine and pediatrics.2 In particular, doctors who work in lifesaving specialties face a tangible threat of criminal prosecution when patients’ outcomes fail to meet the expectations of the patients’ families.3 Consequently, approximately 92% of the residents and fellows had resigned as of September 2024, and 95% of the medical students remained on a leave of absence as of March 2025. The absence of trainee physicians significantly disrupted operations at tertiary hospitals, and temporary measures—including the deployment of military doctors—proved insufficient in addressing the resulting workforce shortage.4

South Korea’s healthcare system is dominated by private providers and paid through a fee-for-service system by the national health insurance program. Physicians’ incomes are largely dependent on the volume of service they provide at a relatively low price decided by this program.2 Recently, the South Korean government initiated a healthcare reform aimed at centralizing critical care at tertiary hospitals and shifting general services to lower-tier institutions. As part of reimbursement guidelines introduced in November 2024, certain high-complexity surgeries were designated as pilot items for which tertiary hospitals are eligible to receive additional reimbursement.56 However, the impact of these measures remains unverified by empirical data. Despite the significance of this disruption, most studies have focused on individual institutions or medical specialties, with few offering a nationwide perspective on its systemic effects.7

This study aims to investigate the changes in surgical procedures before and after the healthcare disruption, using national insurance claim data. The analysis focuses on facility type, medical specialty, cancer type, and geographic region, with particular emphasis on shifts in surgical procedures by institutional level. Through this approach, we seek to assess whether the Korean healthcare system possesses sufficient resilience and redistribution capacity during unexpected nationwide crises.

METHODS

Study design

The database used in this study was provided by the Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (HIRA) in South Korea. National insurance claims data were extracted from the HIRA database, which includes both inpatient and outpatient services reimbursed under the Korean National Health Insurance program. The dataset covers tertiary hospitals (n = 47), general hospitals (n = 331), hospitals (n = 1,410), and clinics (n = 36,478) nationwide. The numbers of each category are slightly different in 2023 at 45, 331, 1,403, and 35,717, respectively. In this study, the volume of claims (defined as the number of procedures billed) for general anesthesia and high-complexity surgical services were analyzed, rather than the number of individual patients. To evaluate changes associated with the medical crisis that began on February 20, 2024, data were collected for 6 months from February to July in 2024. For comparative analysis, the corresponding period from the previous year (February to July 2023) was used as the baseline.

Using the HIRA database, 999 procedure codes comprising 995 surgical procedures and 4 anesthesia-related codes were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). High-complexity surgeries were defined based on the classifications outlined in the Ministry of Health and Welfare’s Guidelines for Supporting the Structural Reform of Tertiary Hospitals released in November 20246 and primarily included procedures involving the cardiovascular system, nervous system, musculoskeletal system, liver, lungs, and interventional techniques and cancer surgeries.8 Procedure codes marked as “inquiry period limitation” due to newly established or discontinued reimbursement (n = 32) as well as codes with no claims during either study period (n = 51) were excluded. Consequently, 914 procedures were included in the final analysis.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcome of this study was the change in procedural volumes before and after the medical crisis, based on healthcare facility type. National insurance claim counts were collected for each facility type—tertiary hospitals, general hospitals, and hospitals/clinics—and compared between the periods of February to July in 2023 and 2024 using data provided by the HIRA Open Data platform (https://opendata.hira.or.kr). Tertiary hospitals are high-complexity referral centers with a minimum of 300 beds, offering comprehensive and advanced medical care. They manage severe or complex conditions, perform high-risk surgeries, and provide treatment for rare and intractable diseases. General hospitals are institutions with at least 100 beds and a multidisciplinary team of specialists, including internal medicine and surgery. They are equipped to manage patients requiring hospitalization, surgical procedures, and other specialized medical services. Hospitals are facilities with a minimum of 30 beds, which deliver both inpatient and outpatient care, thus catering to a broader range of medical needs than clinics. Clinics primarily provide outpatient services for patients with mild symptoms or chronic conditions not requiring hospitalization.

The secondary outcome involved a detailed stratification of the data by medical specialty across 12 medical specialties (995 surgical codes classified by specialty) and by cancer type. For cancer-specific analysis, 6 major cancers designated as public health priorities in South Korea were included: stomach, colorectal, hepatic, breast, uterine, and lung cancers. For each cancer type, insurance claim volumes were further analyzed by geographic region categorized into 3 groups: 1) capital (Seoul) 2) around-capital (Gyeonggi and Incheon), and 3) non-capital regions (all areas outside Seoul, Gyeonggi, and Incheon).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to compare procedural volumes before and after the medical crisis, based on the number of insurance claims submitted under the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS). Claim volumes were aggregated and analyzed by facility type, medical specialty, cancer type, and region. In South Korea, medical institutions are officially classified into 4 categories based on the number of beds and range of medical services provided. However, hospitals and clinics were analyzed as a single group in the claims data analysis herein.

The relative proportions of claims across facility types (e.g., the share of tertiary hospitals vs. general hospitals vs. primary hospital/clinics) were also compared to assess shifts in institutional distribution of the surgical burden. Changes in absolute claim volumes between the 2 periods were calculated and expressed as percentages. These changes were then compared using 2-tailed paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

During the medical crisis, the number of high-complexity procedure claims decreased by 10% from 333,825 to 302,072 (P < 0.001). The total number of high-complexity procedures at tertiary hospitals decreased significantly by 19% from 213,185 to 172,013 (P < 0.001). Across all medical specialties, high-complexity claims decreased without exception. Furthermore, the total number of claims related to the 6 major cancers designated as national priorities in South Korea also declined, with an overall 4% reduction from 75,881 to 72,619 (P < 0.001). Table 1 illustrates the changes in high-complexity surgical claims by medical specialties and institution type. Across the 12 medical specialties analyzed, the volume of high-complexity procedure claims at tertiary hospitals decreased following the medical crisis without a corresponding statistically significant increase at general hospitals. This pattern was consistent across 9 of the 12 specialties, including cardiac surgery, neurosurgery, orthopedic surgery, ophthalmology, and others. Pain medicine was the only specialty that showed a statistically significant decrease in claims at both tertiary and general hospitals. The procedure included in the analysis was “Implantation, Change or Removal of Spine Neurostimulator Electrode” for pain blockade, which demonstrated a substantial decline in claim numbers. In contrast, urology and general surgery showed statistically significant increases at both general hospitals and hospitals/clinics, despite reductions at tertiary hospitals.

Following the medical crisis, the total number of claims decreased by 4% (P < 0.001); claims at tertiary hospitals dropped by 16% (P < 0.001), while claims at general hospitals increased by 23% (P = 0.001) and those at hospitals/clinics increased by 61% (P < 0.001), although the absolute volumes at hospitals/clinics remained relatively small. DISCUSSION

Following the 2024 medical crisis, there was a significant overall decline in high-complexity surgical claims in South Korea, especially at tertiary hospitals. While the volume of procedures decreased across all medical specialties at tertiary hospitals, most medical specialties did not exhibit a compensatory increase at general hospitals. Only urology and general surgery showed statistically significant increases at lower-tier institutions. Cancer-related procedures also demonstrated a shift, with overall reductions in claims at tertiary hospitals and increases at general and hospitals/clinics—most notably for breast cancer. Regionally, the capital region experienced the most substantial decline across all cancer types, whereas the around-capital region and non-capital regions showed stationary or increased volumes. These patterns suggest limited redistribution of surgical burden from tertiary to lower-tier institutions, raising concerns about the continuity of patient care and resilience of medical system during medical disruption. Taken together, the 2024 medical crisis in South Korea revealed the structural vulnerabilities of the healthcare system and its heavy reliance on trainee physicians for core clinical operations.9

These findings likely reflect the hierarchical dependency of South Korea’s surgical care delivery system. Tertiary hospitals—where high-complexity surgeries and cancer operations are typically concentrated—were the most affected by the crisis owing to shortages of residents and specialists. Lower-tier institutions may have lacked the capacity, resources, or specialized expertise to absorb complex cases ordinarily managed at tertiary centers. The inconsistent redistribution of surgical volume—more successful in certain medical specialties or cancer types—may be attributed to differences in procedural complexity, equipment requirements, and the availability of trained personnel in non-tertiary settings. Notably, among all cancer types, only breast cancer showed a clear compensatory increase in the capital and non-capital regions, while other cancers exhibited sharp declines in capital without sufficient redistribution elsewhere.

South Korea’s healthcare system, though based on a universal single-payer model, is characterized by heavy reliance on tertiary hospitals and high concentration of healthcare utilization in metropolitan areas, particularly the capital.10 The government operates a single-payer healthcare model through the NHIS, in which most services are delivered by private providers, and universal population coverage was ultimately achieved by 1989.11 Nevertheless, the system continues to face challenges such as inadequate reimbursement rates, substantial out-of-pocket costs, and weakened referral structure. Patients often bypass primary and secondary care facilities and seek treatment directly at tertiary hospitals, contributing to overcrowding and overutilization of high-tier healthcare facilities.

While the Ministry of Health and Welfare has reported on April 9, 2025 that tertiary hospitals are focusing on critical and rare diseases under the structural reform initiative—with increases in critical surgeries and admissions since the latter half of 2024—our findings reveal a different picture. Using national insurance claims data, we observed a notable decrease in high-complexity procedures at tertiary hospitals during the same period, which suggests that the structural transition may not have been uniformly implemented or effective during the early phase of the crisis. Although Kim et al.12 reported no significant increase in mortality during the 2024 resident strike, our study reveals a substantial decline in high-complexity surgical procedures, suggesting that the disruption in care may not be fully captured by short-term mortality data. Such disruptions could lead to worsening clinical conditions, delayed treatments, long-term complications, and poorer prognoses—outcomes that may not be immediately apparent but are nonetheless clinically significant.

While large-scale physician strikes have occurred previously in South Korea, prior studies found no significant increase in mortality or major disruption to patient care during those periods—most notably during the implementation of the drug-prescription-dispensing separation policy in 200013 and the 3-week resident strike in August 2020 opposing the government’s plan to increase the number of medical students.1415 In contrast, the 2024 medical crisis was marked by a substantial decrease in high-complexity surgical procedures, particularly at tertiary hospitals, without sufficient compensatory increases at general hospitals or hospitals/clinics. This suggests a greater systemic dependency on residents and a higher concentration of surgical burden at tertiary hospitals than in previous incidents. Although we did not analyze the impact of 2024 medical crisis on mortality, such crises might contribute to worse outcomes. For example, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), which constitutes approximately 80% of all lung cancer diagnoses, demonstrates particular susceptibility to the adverse effects of surgical delays.16 Time-to-surgery intervals exceeding 8 weeks from computed tomography diagnosis to surgical resection in early-stage NSCLC have been associated with a 19% relative increase in 5-year mortality rates. A 25% increase in 1-year disease recurrence rates has been documented under similar delayed conditions. For hepatocellular carcinoma, the effect of time-to-surgery on outcomes for curative-intent hepatectomy need to be considered in light of patient and tumor-specific factors.17 Long time-to-surgery was a risk factor for overall survival among patients with low tumor burden scores. Similar trends were observed relative to cancer-specific survival.

Our national analysis revealed significant declines in high-complexity surgeries at tertiary hospitals, contrary to government reports that suggested stable surgical volumes during the crisis. While breast cancer surgeries increased—especially in non-capital regions—more complex procedures, such as those for lung and hepatic cancers, declined sharply without sufficient redistribution. Similarly, vulnerable specialties such as cardiothoracic surgery and neurosurgery experienced marked reductions. These findings highlight critical blind spots in administrative reporting and underscore the importance of stratified, procedure-level analysis to uncover disparities in service delivery during healthcare disruptions. In contrast to the general pattern observed in other high-complexity surgeries, cardiothoracic surgeries demonstrated a markedly different trend. Despite a substantial 23% decrease in procedures at tertiary hospitals, there was minimal redistribution to other institutions. This limited mobility is likely attributable to the inherent complexity of these procedures, which require highly specialized surgical expertise, dedicated postoperative care, and integrated surgical systems. The essential infrastructure such as trained specialists, advanced surgical equipment, and intensive care units with dedicated staff and resources renders such procedures practically unfeasible at other facilities. It has been recently reported that the quality of care in surgery was similar during the absence of cardiothoracic residents.18 However, the structural and long-term viewpoint should be considered to achieve nationwide sustainability for such an important surgical delivery system.

Among the medical specialties analyzed, only urology and general surgery showed significantly increased volumes at general hospitals. The increase in urology appears to be because of nephrectomy for tumor removal and procedures related to testicular cancer.

In general surgery, we observed increased surgeries for gastric, colorectal, and breast cancer. Notably, breast cancer particularly influenced the overall volume, driven by increases in procedures such as mastectomy and axillary sentinel lymph node dissection for assessing metastasis involvement. These increases were especially prominent at lower-tier institutions in the areas surrounding the capital region. Among the 6 cancer types analyzed, breast cancer alone showed a statistically significant overall increase in procedural volume, despite reduction at tertiary hospitals. Importantly, procedure volumes rose by 35% at general hospitals and 62% at hospitals/clinics, suggesting that breast cancer care was more adaptable to redistribution than other cancer types. This trend may reflect the distinctive features of breast cancer treatment, including shorter hospital stays, highly standardized surgical techniques (e.g., mastectomy, sentinel lymph node dissection), and a well-established outpatient care system. Such characteristics likely contributed to the faster recovery and reallocation of breast cancer services during the medical crisis, especially in lower-tier institutions with limited critical care infrastructure. Additionally, regional differences were apparent. While Seoul experienced a 7% decrease in breast cancer procedures, around-capital regions and non-capital areas saw increases of 20% and 11%, respectively. This pattern suggests that regional hospitals outside Seoul may have played a critical role in absorbing surgical demand, especially in breast cancer cases, where procedures are more feasible outside tertiary settings. These findings imply that strengthening surgical capacity for breast cancer in regional hospitals could be a viable strategy to enhance system-wide resilience in future disruptions.

Notably, certain high-complexity surgery procedures that were declined at tertiary hospitals were not shifted to general hospitals as expected; however, they were moved to lower-tier hospital. For example, orthopedic procedures such as osteomyelitis debridement and prosthesis revision surgery showed increased claims in hospitals/clinics. Similarly, interventional radiology procedures, including percutaneous thrombectomy and embolization, shifted to hospitals. In urology, orchidopexy and ureteroneocystostomy also saw increased volumes at hospitals/clinics. Ophthalmologic procedures, including strabismus correction, orbital decompression, and intraocular foreign body removal, were more frequently claimed at hospitals/clinics rather than general hospitals. Notably, certain general surgery procedures, such as axillary sentinel lymph node dissection and benign parathyroidectomy, were also redistributed away from tertiary hospitals to hospitals and hospitals/clinics, without being adequately compensated for at general hospitals. These exceptional shifts at the procedural level may be partly influenced by the resignation of specialists at tertiary hospitals during the nationwide medical crisis, leading to workforce relocation to general hospitals. Additionally, the observed increase in interventional procedures may be associated with a decline in cancer surgeries at tertiary hospitals. These procedure-specific anomalies will be further examined in subsequent analyses.

This study represents the first nationwide quantitative analysis to examine the redistribution of high-complexity surgical care during a medical crisis in South Korea. By utilizing comprehensive claims data from the national health insurance system, it provides empirical evidence of the strain placed on tertiary hospitals and the limited capacity of general and lower-tier institutions to absorb complex surgical demands. These findings highlight the structural dependence on high-level institutions and underscore the need for systematic reforms to enhance resilience and continuity of essential surgical care. Contrary to government reports suggesting that surgical care remained unaffected during the medical crisis, our nationwide insurance claims-based analysis reveals a clear decline in high-complexity procedures—particularly within tertiary hospitals—without sufficient redistribution to lower-tier institutions. These findings challenge optimistic administrative narratives and underscore the need for data-driven policy evaluation to ensure continuity of surgical care during systemic disruption.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was based only on 995 high-complexity surgical procedures, as defined in the Ministry of Health and Welfare’s Guidelines for Supporting the Structural Reform of Tertiary Hospitals released in November 2024.5 These procedures represent only a subset of approximately 9,200 medical service codes currently used in the national health insurance system. Consequently, even high-complexity surgeries may have been excluded from the analysis. This limitation stems from the fact that the classification of all medical services, including surgical procedures, is highly granular, with procedure codes segmented according to factors such as the target, means, and action (WHO 3-axis framework).19 Therefore, functionally similar procedures may be coded differently and thus excluded from the defined list. Furthermore, this list was designed to guide reimbursement policies, not to serve as a comprehensive clinical classification of surgical difficulty. Second, in the analysis of cancer-related surgeries, although the ideal approach would involve linking surgical procedures to cancer diagnosis codes, our analysis was primarily based on procedural data. Consequently, procedure codes were not directly associated with diagnostic classifications such as the International Classification of Diseases. Therefore, the assignment of procedure codes to specific cancer types was performed based on criteria developed by the authors. Priority was given to procedures explicitly labeled with terms such as “cancer,” “malignant,” or “lymph node,” and we also included procedures not explicitly labeled as such but considered anatomically or clinically relevant (e.g., segmentectomy for lung cancer, lobectomy for hepatic cancer). In addition, although the use of robotic surgery has increased substantially in all types of cancer surgeries in recent years, these procedures are currently not covered by national health insurance and were therefore excluded from the analysis, which may cause underestimations in cancer surgery. Third, due to frequent reimbursement policy revisions, some procedures were reclassified. For example, the 2023 code R4423 (“simple oophorectomy”) was split into R4446 and R4447 in 2024 based on surgical technique—either open or laparoscopic.20 More importantly, the 995 procedures are highly heterogeneous in terms of clinical difficulty and resource needs. For example, the list lumps together procedures like heart transplants with more standardized laparoscopic surgeries, treating them as equivalent in the aggregate analysis. Fourth, in analyzing the nationwide medical crisis that erupted in February 2024, we included only the period from February to July—the latest month for which data were available at analysis—for comparison with previous years. Consequently, this study does not fully capture subsequent changes in medical service utilization patterns, which have yet to return to normal. To overcome such limitations, time-series analyses, such as interrupted time-series analysis, would allow for the robust modeling of long-term trends and seasonality, providing a more accurate counterfactual narrative of what would have happened without the crisis. Additionally, in-depth analysis that goes beyond reporting a drop in surgical volume and discusses its potential implications for patient health outcomes is needed. A decline in surgical procedures is a critical leading indicator of future adverse outcomes, such as cancer stage progression, increased morbidity, and reduced quality of life. Further studies are warranted to enable a more rigorous estimation of the causal impact of the walkout.

In conclusion, the 2024 medical crisis led to a substantial decline in high-complexity surgeries at tertiary hospitals in Korea, with only limited redistribution to general and lower-tier institutions. This emphasizes the healthcare system’s inherent structural dependence on tertiary care and limited capacity to redistribute high-complexity surgical demand during periods of disruption.
