Abstract
. 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is an emerging technique for photorealistic 3D scene rendering. However, rendering city-scale 3DGS scenes on mobile devices, e.g., your smartphones, remains a significant challenge due to the limited resources on mobile devices. A natural solution is to offload computation to the cloud; however, naively streaming rendered frames from the cloud to the client introduces high latency and requires bandwidth far beyond the capacity of current wireless networks. In this paper, we propose an effective solution to enable city-scale 3DGS rendering on mobile devices. Our key insight is that, under normal user motion, the number of newly visible Gaussians per second remains roughly constant. Leveraging this, we stream only the necessary Gaussians to the client. Specifically, on the cloud side, we propose asynchronous level-of-detail search to identify the necessary Gaussians for the client. On the client side, we accelerate rendering via a lookup table-based rasterization. Combined with holistic runtime optimizations, our system can deliver low-latency, city-scale 3DGS rendering on mobile devices. Compared to existing solutions, Voyager achieves over 100 × \times reduction on data transfer and up to 8.9 × \times speedup while retaining comparable rendering quality.

1.
Introduction

The field of 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)
(
Kerbl et al. 2023
;
Fan et al. 2024
;
Fang and Wang 2024
;
Girish et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2024b
;
Niemeyer et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Mallick et al. 2024
;
Gui et al. 2024
;
Radl et al. 2024
)
has made tremendous progress in recreating the 3D world with photo-realistic fidelity.
While 3DGS unlocks new possibilities and fuels our imagination, rendering city-scale 3DGS scenes on mobile devices, e.g., the smartphone in your pocket, remains a substantial challenge.
Primarily, there are some inherent limitations of mobile devices, such as limited compute resources and GPU memory.
Furthermore, as 3DGS models scale up, even with advanced model compression techniques
(
Fang and Wang 2024
;
Lee et al. 2024b
;
Papantonakis et al. 2024
)
, device storage can be drained quickly.
For example, a single scene like
Campus
from HierarchicalGS
(
Kerbl et al. 2024
)
already exceeds 80 GB in size.
These constraints make it infeasible to directly render existing large-scale 3DGS models on today’s mobile devices.

Given these limitations, a natural proposal arises: why not leverage the more powerful resources available in the cloud to overcome the constraints of mobile devices?
A straightforward design is to perform rendering entirely in the cloud and stream the rendered frames to the client.
However, this approach has two critical limitations.
First, it incurs high round-trip latency due to the strictly sequential pipeline.
Second, the approach demands unrealistic data communication, often surpassing the limits of current wireless communication technologies
(
Agiwal et al. 2016
;
Thompson et al. 2014
)
.
For instance, streaming 1080p images at 60 FPS requires roughly 2.8
\times 10^{3}
Mbps of bandwidth, which is at least one order of magnitude higher than the capabilities of wireless connections
(
Alsabah et al. 2021
)
.
Thus, to enable cloud-client collaborative rendering, an ideal 3DGS algorithm should carefully orchestrate the resources in the device and the cloud to jointly optimize end-to-end latency and data communication between these two.

Recall, 3DGS algorithms represent scenes using Gaussian ellipsoids, a.k.a. Gaussians.
By examining the state-of-the-art city-scale 3DGS methods
(
Kerbl et al. 2024
;
Ren et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2024
)
, these algorithms generally consist of two major stages:
level-of-detail (LoD) search
and
splatting
.
LoD search identifies the set of Gaussians at an appropriate LoD for rendering.
Splatting then renders these selected Gaussians on the canvas in a visually consistent order.
This stage adheres to the canonical 3DGS rendering pipeline
(
Kerbl et al. 2023
)
, which processes Gaussians from nearest to farthest.

To deploy such an algorithm between the client and the cloud, our key observation is that, as the user navigates through a 3D scene,
the number of newly visible Gaussians introduced by continuous viewpoint changes remains roughly constant
, assuming a general case of a smooth user motion.
By leveraging this property, our algorithm avoids streaming the final rendered images.
Instead, we send the intermediate data, i.e., Gaussians required for the splatting stage, to the client.
We show that our algorithm not only fits well within the wireless bandwidth constraints but also exhibits strong scalability, as the wireless bandwidth demand of our algorithm is not susceptible to resolution or frame rate increases.

On the cloud side, our rendering pipeline proactively performs LoD search and asynchronously sends the required Gaussians of upcoming frames to the client.
This way, we can effectively hide the latency of LoD search by overlapping it with the splatting stage on the client.
To further accelerate LoD search, we introduce two GPU-oriented optimizations.
First, we propose an efficient tree traversal algorithm that can be highly parallelized on GPUs to accelerate the LoD search process.
Second, we design a companion data structure that leverages temporal redundancy across consecutive tree traversals.
Rather than recomputing the tree traversal results from scratch, we can reuse previous traversal results to reduce the computation.
Compared against the previous LoD search algorithms, we achieve up to 6.5
\times
speedup with the same rendering quality.

On the client side, we optimize the rasterization process within the splatting stage, which has been identified as a main performance bottleneck in prior work
(
Wang et al. 2024b
;
Lee et al. 2024a
;
Huang et al. 2025
)
.
Our analysis shows that the primary source of slowdown during rasterization stems from the per-ray computation of Gaussian transparency.
This computation is inefficient due to the heterogeneous design of GPU compute units.
To accommodate this issue, we bypass the costly transparency computation by embedding a 128-byte precomputed lookup table (LUT) in GPU shared memory and approximating Gaussian transparency values instead.
Meanwhile, we design a runtime Gaussian management system that dynamically maintains the set of received Gaussians in GPU memory.
By removing irrelevant Gaussians, our system ensures that the client’s GPU memory usage always remains tight.

Finally, we introduce a contribution-aware compression to further reduce data communication between the cloud and the client.
Our experiments show that the primary contributor to communication overhead is the spherical harmonic (SH) coefficients of each Gaussian.
To address this, we propose a pruning technique that adaptively reduces the SH degrees of individual Gaussians based on their visual contribution.
By combining with off-the-shelf compression techniques, our method achieves 100
\times
communication reduction compared to directly streaming rendered frames.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

A parallel LoD search algorithm that exploits temporal coherence across frames to achieve 6.5
\times
speedup.

•

A runtime Gaussian management system that maintains a tight GPU memory footprint on the client side.

•

A GPU-efficient rasterization method that significantly improves rendering efficiency.

•

The first-of-its-kind framework that enables rendering city-scale 3DGS models on resource-constrained mobile devices, achieving up to 8.9
\times
speedup compared to prior works.

In contrast to these prior works, our framework focuses on large-scale 3DGS rendering. We jointly optimize both the LoD search and splatting stages, while addressing the latency and memory constraints that exist in today’s mobile GPUs.

Large-scale 3DGS

How to represent Level-of-Detail (LoD) efficiently is an issue existed broadly in neural rendering
(
Turki et al. 2023
;
Xu et al. 2023
;
Barron et al. 2023
)
.
Since 3DGS models are inherently point-based representations, various tree-based structures have been designed to manage large-scale scenes, such as Octree-GS
(
Ren et al. 2024
)
, CityGaussian
(
Liu et al. 2024
)
, and HierarchicalGS
(
Kerbl et al. 2024
)
.
These hierarchical structures organize Gaussian points to support scalable rendering.
However, the irregular tree traversals often introduce non-trivial runtime overhead.
In contrast, our LoD search is co-designed with GPU execution characteristics, such as the SIMT execution model, to achieve more efficient traversal and improved runtime performance.

Motivation

3.1.
Preliminary

The techniques proposed in this paper are generally applicable to all large-scale 3DGS algorithms.
However, we primarily use one large-scale 3DGS algorithm, HierarchicalGS
(
Kerbl et al. 2024
)
, as an example to demonstrate the effectiveness of our techniques.
Here, we introduce its data representation and pipeline.

HierarchicalGS introduces a hierarchical representation with two main purposes: first, it identifies the Gaussians inside the view frustum; second, it supports rendering at an appropriate level of detail.
HierarchicalGS stores all Gaussians in a hierarchical data structure called
LoD tree
, where each tree level represents a specific detail granularity (see Fig.
2
).
Each tree node contains a single Gaussian with an unfixed number of child nodes.
Gaussians in lower levels are generally smaller and provide finer granularity.

Fig.
2
gives an overview of the pipeline, which consists of two main stages:
LoD search
and
splatting
.
Each of these stages includes several sub-steps, which we describe as follows:

•

Cut Finding
: This step traverses the LoD tree from top to bottom. At each node, we assess if the projected dimension of the Gaussian is smaller than the predefined LoD (
\tau^{*}
), while the projected dimension of its parent node is larger. We then gather all the Gaussians that meet this criterion. Essentially, these Gaussians form a “cut” that separates the top and bottom of the LOD tree, as shown in Fig.
2
.

•

Weight Interpolation
: Each selected Gaussian then interpolates with its parent node to ensure a smooth transition across different LoDs. The interpolated Gaussians are inserted into the rendering queue in Fig.
2
.

•

Culling & Projection
: Once the cut is determined, the selected Gaussians are projected onto the image plane. Gaussians outside the view frustum or deemed irrelevant are culled.

•

Sorting
: The remaining Gaussians are then sorted by depth, from the nearest to the farthest.

•

Rasterization
: The final step contains two parts.
First, each pixel calculates the intersected transparency
\alpha_{i}
for each Gaussian. If
\alpha_{i}
is below a predefined threshold, the Gaussian is skipped for color blending. Otherwise, the Gaussian contributes to the final pixel color via weighted blending.

We identify two primary inefficiencies in cut finding.
First, existing methods primarily rely on exhaustive search to find the cut, as the inherent irregularity of the LoD tree makes it difficult to achieve efficient parallelism on modern GPUs.
Such a brute-force approach often leads to substantial redundant computation.
Our experiment shows that 92% of tree node accesses are unnecessary when the LoD is 6 (see Appendix).
Second, in real-time rendering scenarios, repeatedly performing tree traversal from the root node at every frame introduces considerable redundant computation.
We simulate a 60 FPS real-time rendering scenario and analyze the overlap in selected Gaussians across consecutive frames.
Experiments show that 99% of the selected Gaussians remain unchanged across frames when the LoD is 6 in Fig.
4
.
This indicates that a substantial amount of computation is wasted due to repetitive tree traversal.

While a few studies have addressed certain inefficiencies in rasterization, such as taming workload imbalance
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
and reducing warp divergence
(
Huang et al. 2025
)
, existing works have largely overlooked the most time-consuming operation in rasterization: computing the Gaussian transparency for each pixel.
Using Nvidia Nsight Compute
(
Nvidia 2025
)
, our profiling shows that this operation takes roughly 50% of the total rasterization time (see Appendix).
The root cause is the imbalanced compute units within modern GPUs.
Taking Nvidia GPUs as an example, most operations can be massively parallelized by CUDA cores.
However, the computation of Gaussian transparency requires exponential computation, which is executed by special functional units (SFUs).
Since CUDA cores vastly outnumber the SFUs (16:1)
(
Krashinsky et al. 2020
)
, calculating Gaussian transparency becomes a key bottleneck.

Methodology

To address the challenges in Sec.
3.2
, we introduce our framework,
Voyager
.

4.1
, then we explain the individual components in the following subsections.

4.1.
Overview

Fig.
5
gives an overview of our pipeline.
Overall, we perform LoD search and splatting asynchronously.
On the cloud side, we proactively predict future camera poses via linear extrapolation
(
Hou and Dey 2020
)
and perform LoD search for every
w
frames to reduce the communication pressure, where
w
is the frame window size.
Here,
w
is chosed to be 32.
The result from LoD search, the “cut” (see Fig.
2
), is then compressed and transmitted to the client.
Meanwhile, the client asynchronously receives the cut result.
Based on the existing Gaussians that the client has, the client performs splatting to render frames.
Our key here is that the pose prediction does not need to be exact to ensure the correctness of the rendering, because the nearby camera poses can share a similar “cut” result.

For LoD search, we treat the initial frame and subsequent frames separately.
To render the initial frame,
Voyager
performs a full LoD search, i.e., LoD tree traversal, in the cloud to compute the cut at the current camera pose.
Here, instead of using exhaustive search, we propose a
fully streaming LoD tree traversal
that is easily parallelizable on GPUs and avoids unnecessary tree node accesses.
The cut result is then compressed and transmitted to the client.

For subsequent frames,
Voyager
leverages the temporal correlation of cut results across adjacent frames and introduces a
temporal-aware LoD search
.
The motivation here is to leverage the previous cut result and avoid traversing the tree from the root.
Instead, we perform local search within relevant subtrees to update the cut for the current frame.
Further explanations of both fully streaming LoD tree traversal and temporal-aware LoD search are given in Sec.
4.2
.

Regarding the data communication of subsequent frames, although it is possible to transmit the complete cut result each time.
This method, nevertheless, would introduce a substantial communication overhead.
In Fig.
4
, we observe a significant overlap in cut results across consecutive frames.
Therefore, we only need to transmit the non-overlapped portion for the subsequent frames.
To achieve this, we introduce a
runtime Gaussian management
system that identifies and transmits only the necessary Gaussians (
\Delta
cut) required by the client.
This largely reduces the data transfer between the cloud and the client.
Sec.
4.3
explains how Gaussians are tracked and transmitted to ensure the client has all necessary data.

Once the client receives Gaussians from the cloud, the client decompresses the data and updates its local subgraph, which is a subset of the original LoD tree in the cloud that keeps track of Gaussians available on the client.
Note that, the subgraph update is co-designed with the Gaussian management system in the cloud so that both ends have a consistent view of which Gaussians reside on the client (Sec.
4.3
).
Then, the client checks which Gaussians are required for the current frame rendering and gathers those Gaussians into the rendering queue.
Lastly, the selected Gaussians will be processed by the subsequent stages, e.g., projection, rasterization, etc.
We further explain our efficient
LUT-based rasterization
in Sec.
4.4
.

4.2.
Temporal-Aware LoD Search

We first explain our fully-streaming LoD tree traversal for initial frame rendering.
Next, we describe how our temporal-aware LoD search can leverage previous cut results to accelerate LoD search.

Fig.
8
shows our fully-streaming LoD tree traversal.
The goal of our algorithm is to achieve high parallelism on GPUs while minimizing unnecessary tree node visits.
Rather than relying on the inherent parent-child relations in the original LoD tree (denoted by solid arrows), we augment the LoD tree with connections that enable tree traversal in breadth-first order (denoted by dashed arrows).

During the GPU execution, different GPU warps
1
1

1

A GPU warp is a group of GPU threads executed in a lockstep fashion.
are assigned the same amount of workload, i.e., a block of tree nodes.
Each block is then evenly distributed among threads within a warp to ensure a balanced workload across GPU threads.
Since each block of nodes is small enough to fully reside in GPU shared memory, our algorithm streamingly processes those tree nodes and avoids expensive global memory accesses.
The workload assignment is dynamically dispatched whenever a warp becomes available at runtime.
The tree traversal terminates once a clean cut separates the top and bottom of the LoD tree.
As illustrated in Fig.
8
, our algorithm processes only the green nodes while completely skipping the grey ones, thus avoiding redundant computation.

For tree traversal of subsequent frames, we introduce a temporal-aware LoD search.
Our algorithm first partitions the entire LoD tree into multiple subtrees offline, while preserving the hierarchical relationships within the LoD tree.
Fig.
8
highlights the individual subtrees in dashed blocks.
Here, we only show a two-level subtree partitioning for illustration purposes, our actual implementation applies multi-level partitioning to the LoD tree.

Given the cut result from the previous frame (highlighted in pink), our algorithm first identifies the subtrees to which each Gaussian in the cut belongs.
Fig.
8
highlights these subtrees in red.
Then, our algorithm only traverses those identified subtrees instead of all subtrees.
In GPU implementation, each subtree is assigned to a separate GPU warp for local subtree traversal.
The subtree partitioning is performed offline and guarantees that each subtree is approximately equal in size, ensuring balanced workload distribution across GPU warps.
If searching the local subtree cannot obtain the complete “cut” result, i.e., no clean cut for this substree, we then search its corresponding top-tree or subtrees to complete the cut finding.
Note that, the results from our temporal-aware LoD search are bit-accurate compared to those of the original full-tree traversal.

4.3.
Runtime Gaussian Management

To minimize the data transfer between the cloud and client,
Voyager
transfers only the necessary Gaussians to the client at runtime.
To enable this, our system should guarantee two key properties: 1) the cloud and client share a consistent view of which Gaussians are currently stored on the client; and 2) obsolete Gaussians should be removed from the client on the fly to alleviate memory pressure.
Now, we describe the system design on both sides separately.

On the cloud side, our system maintains a management table that tracks the set of Gaussians currently stored on the client (Fig.
5
).
For each Gaussian, a reuse window,
w_{r}
, is used to represent the number of frames since this Gaussian was last included in a cut result.
When a new cut result is generated via LoD search, the cloud iterates over all Gaussians in the cut result and updates those that do not exist in the management table.
Meanwhile, those newly encountered Gaussians are then assembled into a
\Delta
cut result and transmitted to the client.
To remove obsolete Gaussians, the cloud and client share a coherent reuse threshold, denoted
w_{r}^{*}
.
After the table is updated, both the cloud and client iterate through their tables and remove any Gaussians whose reuse window
w_{r}
exceeds the threshold
w_{r}^{*}
.

On the client side, Fig.
9
illustrates how the client manages its own local Gaussians.
After receiving a
\Delta
cut result, the client updates its local LoD subgraph with new Gaussians.
Instead of maintaining the entire LoD tree structure, the client uses a lightweight hash table to store and manage Gaussians.
To insert a Gaussian into the hash table, the client needs to update the entry for the Gaussian itself, as well as its associated parent and child nodes.
Each Gaussian entry includes attributes such as its parent ID, child IDs, spherical harmonic (SH) coefficients, and other necessary attributes for rendering.
After completing the insertion, the client performs the same removal process as the cloud to discard obsolete Gaussians whose reuse window exceeds
w_{r}^{*}
.
Finally, to render the next frame, the client iterates over the updated hash table and generates a rendering queue with the same criteria as in the canonical cut finding.

4.4.
LUT-Based Rasterization

In Sec.
3.2
, we show that the primary performance bottleneck in rasterization is the computation of Gaussian transparency, which accounts for over 50% of the total execution time.
This operation introduces significant latency because it relies on exponential computations.
Unlike common arithmetic operations, exponentials are not executed by the massive CUDA cores.
Instead, they are executed by SFUs, which are far fewer in number compared to CUDA cores (1:16).
This imbalance in hardware resources makes transparency computation a major bottleneck in rasterization.

To address this, we propose two simple yet effective optimizations to accelerate rasterization.
In canonical rasterization, the Gaussian transparency,
\alpha
, is computed by,

(1)

\alpha=\min(0.99,\theta_{i}\cdot e^{\rho}),

where
\rho
is the power decay of the current Gaussian-ray intersection and
\theta_{i}
is the opacity of the intersected Gaussian
i
.
Each individual
\alpha
is then compared against a transparency threshold
\alpha^{*}
to determine whether to proceed with the subsequent color blending.

Instead of computing the expensive exponential for every Gaussian-ray intersection, we refactor the transparency comparison as,

(2)

\log(\alpha^{*})-\log(\theta_{i})<\rho.

Since
\log(\theta_{i})
can be precomputed offline and stored for each Gaussian, we replace the original transparency check with our lightweight check,
\log(\alpha^{*})-\log(\theta_{i})<\rho
, before computing the transparency,
\theta_{i}\cdot e^{\rho}
.
This allows us to skip unnecessary exponent computations for Gaussians that are below the transparency threshold,
\alpha^{*}
.

Although our lightweight check reduces the number of exponential operations, it cannot eliminate them completely.
To remove these computations altogether, we introduce a lookup table (LUT) embedded in GPU shared memory.
The LUT can directly map
\rho
values to the corresponding exponential results,
e^{\rho}
, allowing us to obtain
e^{\rho}
via a memory lookup rather than invoking SFUs.

Fig. 10.
The distribution of
\rho
values across the value space,
[-5.55,0]
.

The next question is how to design such a LUT.
Our observation is that
\rho
values are uniformly distributed between the effective value space,
[-5.55,0]
, as shown in Fig.
10
.
Thus, we uniformly split the effective value space into
m
intervals. Here, we choose m to be 32.
a given
\rho
value can be directly indexed to this LUT by computing,

(3)

\text{index}=\left\lfloor\frac{\rho}{-5.55}\cdot m\right\rceil,

where
\lfloor\cdot\rceil
denotes rounding to the nearest integer.
The LUT indexing enables low-latency exponential approximation via a small shared memory.
The sensitivity of
m
is shown in Fig.
15
.

4.5.
Contribution-Aware Compression

The compression method in our framework needs to fulfill two key requirements:
1) it must support real-time performance, and 2) it must allow Gaussians to be compressed individually to support incremental transmission during real-time rendering.

To address this, we propose an element-wise compression method.
Following the approach of prior works
(
Papantonakis et al. 2024
;
Lee et al. 2024b
)
, we compress different Gaussian attributes independently.
For the most storage-intensive SH coefficients, our key observation is that Gaussians located at higher levels of the LoD tree have less detail and can thus be represented with fewer SH parameters.
Thus, we introduce a contribution-aware compression strategy that adaptively truncates the SH degree per Gaussian according to their LoD level, inspired by the method proposed in Papantonakis et al.
(
Papantonakis et al. 2024
)
.
Other properties, such as position and scale, which occupy only a small portion of the overall storage, are encoded using a 16-bit fixed-point representation to reduce memory footprint without compromising accuracy.

In terms of rendering speed,

-L

\times

and Fig.

6.
Conclusion

Human imagination is boundless.
When we use Gaussians to create virtual worlds, there should be no limits either!

Voyager
marks the first attempt toward enabling real-time city-scale Gaussian splatting on resource-constrained devices.
By harnessing the virtually unlimited resources in the cloud, we unleash the full potential of 3DGS and propose a novel cloud-client co-rendering framework that overcomes today’s wireless communication bottlenecks.
Overall,
Voyager
achieves over 100
\times
reduction on data transfer and up to 8.9
\times
speedup in large-scale scenes and 21.9
\times
speedup in small-scale scenes, all with comparable rendering quality.
