A network of parametrically driven silicon nitride mechanical membranes Preprint: APS/123-QED
Luis Mestre, Suyash Singh, Gabriel Margiani, Letizia Catalini, Alexander Eichler, Vincent Dumont

Abstract
Networks of nonlinear resonators offer a promising platform for analog computing and the emulation of complex systems. However, realizing such networks remains challenging, as it requires resonators with high quality factors, individual frequency tunability, and strong inter-resonator coupling. In this work, we present a system that meets all these criteria. Our system is based on metallized silicon nitride membranes that are coupled via their common substrate and controlled capacitively via electrodes. We demonstrate individual frequency tuning and strong parametric driving of each membrane. Notably, we tune membrane frequencies through avoided crossings and demonstrate tunability of the coupled membrane’s parametric response. This platform provides a scalable and controllable setting for exploring collective phenomena, dynamical phase transitions, nonlinear topology, and analog computing.

In this work, we present an electromechanical platform based on coupled silicon nitride membrane resonators on a single chip. Each resonator can be individually tuned in frequency by a few
\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
, features a quality factor up to
Q\sim 10^{4}
, and exhibits strong nearest-neighbor coupling, with the normal mode splitting comparable or larger than the membranes mechanical linewidth. The experimental platform is scalable, as many membranes can be fabricated on a single silicon substrate. We read out multiple mechanical resonators using a single laser interferometer, significantly reducing the experimental complexity. With this setup, we demonstrate a continuous crossover between a far detuned ‘resonator regime’, with each mode comprising mostly one bare membrane oscillating, to a ‘normal-mode regime’ where we observe strongly hybridized KPOs involving both membranes. The system can thus access various scenarios that have hitherto only been studied separately. In the future, this platform will allow us investigating many-body physics and computational architectures beyond the degenerate Ising system.

So far, we have shown that our system of membrane resonators has mechanical modes with high quality factors whose frequencies can be widely tuned and whose coupling is on the order of the total damping rate. We have also seen that these modes individually respond to parametric driving. Now, we combine all of these elements, demonstrating a network of KPOs that can be continuously tuned between two very different regimes: in the first regime, the original membrane resonators (1 and 2) are detuned, and the system response can be well understood in terms of parametric oscillations localized on either membrane. In the second regime, the resonators form hybridized modes and the parametric response involves both membranes simultaneously, generating a rich phase diagram of overlapping Arnold tongues.

When the membrane resonators are tuned into an avoided crossing, they become hybridized. Parametrically driving these normal modes results in a pattern of overlapping Arnold tongues
Heugel et al. 2022
. The Arnold tongues with lower and higher center frequency represent antisymmetric (
x_{1}-x_{2}
) and symmetric (
x_{1}+x_{2}
) parametric oscillation states, respectively, see Appendix
F

4
(a)(ii), a jump to the symmetric state occurs at approximately
202.35\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
, below which the zero-amplitude state becomes unstable and parametric oscillations start. A second jump is observed at

202.32\text{\,}\mathrm{kHz}
, well within the symmetric Arnold tongue.

To the left of this jump, the interplay between the nonlinearity and the resonator coupling destabilizes the symmetric state, forcing the system to jump to the antisymmetric state
Heugel et al. 2022
;
Ameye et al. 2025
. Those intricate patterns were previously only measured with electrical resonators whose quality factors were much lower than those of the mechanical modes reported here
Heugel et al. 2022
;
Álvarez et al. 2024
;
Margiani et al. 2025

and interstate statistical transitions

We emphasize that even though we only measure the amplitude and phase of membrane 1, we gain access to much information about the entire coupled system. In the detuned case in Fig.

Summary and outlook.—
We demonstrated a system of coupled nonlinear mechanical resonators based on silicon nitride membranes. Coupling between membranes can be designed via their separation. The platform combines high quality factors and large individual tuning of the resonator frequencies, offering access to distinct regimes of dynamics. In addition, we show that information about the entire network can be inferred from a measurement of just one membrane.

This platform is easily scalable. We envision that networks of six or nine membrane resonators can be probed without significant additional technical overhead, assuming that existing theory models
Ameye et al. 2025
appropriately describe the emerging phenomena. Beside the number of membranes, the quality factors of individual membranes can potentially be increased by orders of magnitudes using soft clamping
Tsaturyan et al. 2017
;
Mashaal et al. 2025
. This will greatly facilitate the study of activated fluctuations between the different states in such complex systems
Heugel et al. 2023a
. When detuning the constituent KPOs, the system could potentially mimic an asymmetric Ising model, which is central to the understanding of neural networks
Han et al. 2024
. Furthermore, we plan to use this platform as a testbed for Ising machines
Mohseni et al. 2022
, Hopfield networks
Hopfield 1982
, and the study of emergent nonlinear collective phenomena
Arenas et al. 2008
;
Scheffer et al. 2009
;
Scheffer et al. 2012
;
Liu et al. 2024
;
Grziwotz et al. 2023
;
Harris et al. 2024
;
Artime et al. 2024
, dynamical phases
Abrams and Strogatz 2004
;
Matheny et al. 2019
, and topology in nonlinear networks
Smirnova et al. 2020
;
Ravets et al. 2025
;
Villa et al. 2024
. With this, nanomechanical KPO networks will become a useful tool for tackling open questions in complex systems and artificial neural networks.

Appendix C
Capacitive driving

Here, we calculate the capacitive force
F_{\mathrm{c},i}
and the effective spring constant
k_{\mathrm{c},i}
induced on membrane
i
by applying a voltage
U_{i}
on the electrode beneath it.

Capacitance:
We consider the capacitance between two conducting plates of area
A=($450\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$)^{2}
, i.e. the membrane and electrode metallization area (neglecting the metallization hole of radius
50\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
), which are separated by a distance
d_{i}
. This leads to capacitance

\displaystyle C_{i}=\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{r}}\frac{A}{d_{i}},

(5)

with
\epsilon_{0}=8.85\times 10^{-12}
F/m the vacuum permittivity, and
\epsilon_{\mathrm{r}}=1
the relative permittivity of the surrounding medium (vacuum).

We need to take into account the fundamental membrane mode-shape
u(y,z)
in the
y
-
z
membrane plane, which is
Schmid et al. 2016

\displaystyle u(y,z)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi y}{L}\right)\sin\left(\frac{\pi z}{L}\right),

(6)

assuming clamped boundary conditions for the membrane and with
L=$450\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
the membrane lateral dimension. In this paper, we only consider the fundamental mode of each membrane, but a similar analysis can be done for higher order mechanical modes. The membrane-electrode separation is
d_{i}(y,z)\equiv d_{0}+u(y,z)x_{i}
, with
d_{0}
the distance between the membrane and the electrode, and
x_{i}
the membrane displacement at its center. The capacitance is then
Puglia et al. 2025

\displaystyle C_{i}=\int_{0}^{L}\int_{0}^{L}\frac{\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{r}}}{d_{0}+u(y,z)x_{i}}dydz.

(7)

Capacitive force:
The potential energy of the capacitor is
U_{\mathrm{pot},i}=-C_{i}U_{i}^{2}/2
for a voltage
U_{i}
applied to the membrane’s electrode, yielding an attractive force

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{c},i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial U_{\mathrm{pot},i}}{dx_{i}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial C_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}U_{i}^{2}

(8)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}U_{i}^{2}\int_{0}^{L}\int_{0}^{L}\frac{\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{r}}u(y,z)}{[d_{0}+u(y,z)x_{i}]^{2}}dydz.

(9)

Spring constant:
At the equilibrium position
x_{\mathrm{eq}}
, this force can be approximated as

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{c},i}(x_{i})\approx F_{\mathrm{c},i}(x_{\mathrm{eq}})+(x_{i}-x_{\mathrm{eq}})\left.\frac{\partial F_{\mathrm{c},i}}{\partial x_{i}}\right|_{x_{i}=x_{\mathrm{eq}}},

(10)

for
x_{i}\ll d_{0}
. When inserted in the equations of motion, the second term leads to a capacitive spring constant

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{c},i}

\displaystyle=-\left.\frac{\partial F_{\mathrm{c},i}}{\partial x_{i}}\right|_{x_{i}=x_{\mathrm{eq}}}

(11)

\displaystyle=-U_{i}^{2}\int_{0}^{L}\int_{0}^{L}\frac{\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{r}}u^{2}(y,z)}{[d_{0}+u(y,z)x_{\mathrm{eq}}]^{3}}dydz,

(12)

generating a spring softening. In the limit of small changes in equilibrium position,
x_{\mathrm{eq}}\ll d_{0}
, the spring constant becomes

\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{c},i}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{r}}}{d_{0}^{3}}U_{i}^{2}\int_{0}^{L}\int_{0}^{L}u^{2}(y,z)dydz

(13)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{r}}A}{4d_{0}^{3}}U_{i}^{2}.

(14)

Frequency shift:
As mentioned in the main text,
k_{\mathrm{c},i}
is an additional spring constant acting on the mechanical resonator, leading to an effective spring constant
k_{i}+k_{\mathrm{c},i}
and a shifted mechanical frequency
\Omega_{\mathrm{eff},i}=\sqrt{(k_{i}+k_{\mathrm{c},i})/m_{i}}
. The mechanical frequency shift is

\displaystyle\delta\Omega_{i}=\Omega_{\mathrm{eff},i}-\Omega_{i}=\sqrt{\frac{k_{i}+k_{\mathrm{c},i}}{m_{i}}}-\sqrt{\frac{k_{i}}{m_{i}}},

(15)

which, in the limit of
k_{\mathrm{c},i}\ll k_{i}
, reduces to

\displaystyle\delta\Omega_{i}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{k_{\mathrm{c},i}}{2m_{i}\Omega_{i}}=-\frac{\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{r}}A}{8m_{i}\Omega_{i}d_{0}^{3}}U_{i}^{2}.

(16)

Thus, the frequency of the resonator decreases quadratically with voltage
U_{i}
applied to the electrode underneath it.

Membrane-electrode distance:
Equation (
16
) provides a way of extracting the membrane-electrode distance
d_{0}
. Using the membrane area
A=$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}^{2}$
, the effective mass
m_{i}\approx$38\text{\,}\mathrm{ng}$
derived in Appendix
B
, and the prefactor
B\equiv-\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{r}}A/(8m_{i}\Omega_{i}d_{0}^{3})=2\pi\times$0.309\pm 1\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$
from the fit in Fig.
2
(b) relating the change in mechanical frequency for an applied voltage
\bar{U}_{i}
, we obtain
d_{0}\approx$13\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}$
. At that distance, the membrane-electrode capacitance is
C_{i}\approx$140\text{\,}\mathrm{fF}$
.

Equilibrium position:
We note that the first term, i.e.
F_{\mathrm{c},i}(0)
, in eq. (
10
) leads to a shift of the membrane’s equilibrium position to
x_{\mathrm{eq},i}
, which can cause an increased mechanical frequency (stiffening) due to the positive Duffing nonlinearity
\beta x_{i}^{3}
. To see this, we first find the equilibrium position
x_{\mathrm{eq},i}
where
F_{\mathrm{c},i}(0)-m_{i}\Omega_{i}^{2}=0
,

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{eq}}=-\frac{1}{2m_{i}\Omega_{i}^{2}}\frac{\partial C_{i}}{\partial x_{i}}U_{i}^{2}\approx-\frac{2\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{\mathrm{r}}A}{\pi^{2}m_{i}\Omega_{i}^{2}d_{0}^{2}}U_{i}^{2}

(17)

in the limit
x_{i}\ll d_{0}
, and using
\partial C_{i}/\partial x_{i}=4\epsilon_{0}\epsilon_{r}A/(\pi^{2}d_{0}^{2})
.

We can redefine
x_{i}
around this equilibrium position
x_{i}\rightarrow x_{\mathrm{eq},i}+x_{i}
, the Duffing term
\beta x_{i}^{3}
then becomes

\displaystyle\beta(x_{\mathrm{eq},i}+x_{i})^{3}=\beta x_{\mathrm{eq},i}^{3}+3\beta x_{\mathrm{eq},i}^{2}x_{i}+3\beta x_{\mathrm{eq},i}x_{i}^{2}+\beta x_{i}^{3}.

(18)

The first term
\beta x_{\mathrm{eq},i}^{3}
leads to a shift in equilibrium position, the second term
3\beta x_{\mathrm{eq},i}^{2}x_{i}
to a spring constant shift
3\beta x_{\mathrm{eq},i}^{2}
, the third term
3\beta x_{\mathrm{eq},i}x_{i}^{2}
to a static force and dynamics at
2\omega
for
x_{i}\sim\cos(\omega t)
, which gets filtered out by measuring with our lock-in at frequency
\omega

Eichler et al. 2013
and effectively leads to a renormalization of the Duffing nonlinearity
Kozinsky et al. 2006
;
Lifshitz and Cross 2008
, and the fourth term
3\beta x_{i}^{3}
to the usual Duffing nonlinearity.

In the main text, all measured amplitudes

\displaystyle=0.

(22)

\beta x_{i}^{3}=\beta x_{j}^{3}=0
and
\Gamma_{i}=\Gamma_{j}=0

\mathbf{x}=(x_{i},x_{j})^{\mathrm{T}}

\mathrm{T}

Appendix H

In order to measure coupled Arnold tongues, we need to carefully tune the parametric drive voltage
\tilde{U}_{2\omega,i}
applied each membrane. Since each membrane has a different DC voltage
\bar{U}_{i}
applied to it, and has a different quality factor
