Effect of post-weld friction stir processing on low-heat-input cold metal transfer (CMT) weld mechanical, microstructural and corrosion behaviors
Authors: Balram Yelamasetti, Y Krishnaiah, N Santhi Sree, Mohammed Zubairuddin, Chilakamarri L Aslesha, Harinadh Vemanaboina, Shahid Tamboli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the influence of post-weld friction stir processing (FSP) on 6-mm-thick AA6061 butt joints fabricated by low-heat-input cold metal transfer (CMT) welding using ER2219 filler. Weld integrity was evaluated using X-ray radiography, while microstructural features were examined by optical microscopy. Hardness was measured using Vickers micro-hardness testing, tensile properties were determined in accordance with ASTM E8, impact toughness was assessed by the Izod test, and corrosion behavior was characterized through potentiodynamic polarization in 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution. Friction stir processing produced a fine, equiaxed stir zone characterized by onion-ring flow patterns and clean, defect-free interfaces. The as-CMT joints exhibited an ultimate tensile strength of 216 MPa, a yield strength of 198 MPa, and an elongation of 12.6%. After applying friction stir processing (FSP), these values increased to 252 MPa, 215 MPa, and 23.8%, respectively, indicating substantial improvements in both strength and ductility. The Izod impact energy also increased markedly from 9.5 J in the as-CMT condition to 15.4 J after FSP, representing a 62% enhancement. The as-CMT sample displayed a lower corrosion current density than the CMT + FSP sample in 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution. Nevertheless, FSP resulted in notable improvements in strength, ductility, and hardness uniformity, whereas the as-CMT condition retained comparatively improved corrosion resistance, primarily due to its surface condition.Keywords: Aluminum alloys, Cold metal transfer, Friction stir processing, Mechanical properties and corrosion behaviourSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Aluminum alloys are widely employed in lightweight engineering applications because they offer low density together with high specific strength, corrosion resistance, good formability, and recyclability, allowing significant mass reduction while maintaining structural performance and economic viability1,2. Such attributes are especially beneficial in aerospace, automotive, rail, and marine applications, where weight reduction yields tangible gains in range, payload, and energy efficiency, and in pressure-bearing components demanding high stiffness, fatigue resistance, and manufacturability3. AA6061 is widely used in welded structural applications due to its favorable combination of weldability, corrosion resistance, and mechanical properties, making it well suited for thin-gauge, weight-efficient components in road vehicles, rail systems, and airframe sub-structures4,5. In fusion welding processes, filler-metal selection is a key factor influencing solidification characteristics, microstructural evolution, and the resulting in-service performance of the joint6. Cu-rich Al–Cu filler alloys, exemplified by ER2219, are especially relevant for high-duty welding applications because they offer elevated weld-metal strength and stable performance across a broad service-temperature range. These fillers are aerospace-qualified and have been extensively applied in propellant tanks and other high-load structural components7,8. Deploying ER2219 with 6xxx substrates provides a deliberate contrast in strengthening mechanisms and a rigorous basis for examining how weld-metal chemistry influences weldability, mechanical response, microstructure, and corrosion behavior in 6-mm AA6061 plate joints9,10.

Joining processes for aluminum alloys primarily fall into two categories: fusion and solid-state. Gas Tungsten Arc Welding (GTAW) offers excellent arc controllability and a clean weld pool, making it suitable for thin sections and critical root passes; however, deposition rate is modest, porosity control is demanding, and age-hardenable 6xxx alloys still exhibit heat-affected-zone (HAZ) softening8,11. Gadallah et al.12 studied dissimilar joints of AA5083–AA6082 by GTAW using ER4043 and ER5356; they reported solidification cracking and galvanic attack at the ER4043/5083 interface, while ER5356 produced defect-free welds with superior corrosion behavior. Naing et al.13 analyzed AA6082-T6 repair welds (ER4043) made by conventional versus pulsed TIG; pulsing reduced porosity and grain size in the weld metal and improved mechanical response, whereas HAZ over-ageing from repeated thermal cycles governed property loss. Alzahrani et al.14 compared ER5356, ER4043 and ER4047 in TIG-welded AA6082; welds made with ER5356 achieved the highest UTS (278 MPa) and weld efficiency (86%) with a harder weld zone, whereas ER4047 maximized impact toughness at lower strength linking filler chemistry to hardness profiles and fracture location.

Gas Metal Arc Welding (GMAW) provides higher productivity and stable penetration on plate and extrusions particularly with pulsed-spray transfer, and appropriate Si- or Mg-bearing filler wires help manage cracking and corrosion; nevertheless, the thermal cycle tends to widen the softened HAZ, so joint tensile performance is governed by HAZ properties rather than weld-metal strength15,16. Bi et al.17 demonstrated that dual-pulse MIG coupled with post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) substantially elevates tensile properties of AA6061 butt joints by refining the fusion-zone solidification structure and restoring precipitation hardening. The effect of double-pulse frequency (2–5 Hz) in MIG welding of Al–Mg–Si alloy joints and the role of post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) were investigated. Increasing the pulse frequency promoted fusion-zone refinement and the development of a more uniform equiaxed grain structure, leading to improved tensile strength. Subsequent PWHT further enhanced strength and hardness by re-precipitating strengthening phases and partially mitigating over-ageing in the heat-affected zone18. Cui et al.19 examined MIG-welded AA6082-T6 (4 mm) with ER5356 across a range of heat-input levels and correlated weld-pool morphology, microstructure, and properties; an intermediate heat input gave the best combination of UTS and ductility and coincided with a transition from dendritic to more cellular fusion-zone structure.

In contrast, Friction Stir Welding (FSW), a solid-state process, joins below the solidus, suppressing porosity and hot cracking while generating a recrystallized stir zone with refined grains, flatter hardness profiles, and more uniform tensile and fatigue response in 6xxx joints20–22. Arya et al.23 investigated the influence of tool rotation and welding speed on FSW of AA6061, correlating process windows with microstructure, mechanical response, and corrosion behavior; intermediate heat input produced a fine, equiaxed nugget and a flatter hardness profile, yielding higher tensile performance. Corrosion–fatigue behavior of friction stir welded AA6061 joints under low-cycle loading showed preferential crack initiation from corrosion pits in the HAZ/TMAZ, while the refined stir zone delayed early crack growth. The process, however, requires rigid fixturing and a backing anvil and results in an exit keyhole24. GTAW/GMAW provide broader geometric access but demand rigorous oxide removal, high-purity shielding, and precise heat-input control, and they remain susceptible to HAZ over-aging in precipitation-hardenable alloys25–27. Process selection should reflect component geometry, fixture capability, and the required levels of property uniformity and defect tolerance28,29. Singh et al.30 investigated dissimilar friction stir welded joints of 6082-T6 and 7050-T7 Aluminium alloys produced under low heat input conditions, followed by natural aging and high-temperature post-weld heat treatments. The study reported that these post-weld treatments altered weld-zone microstructure and precipitate characteristics, resulting in a notable recovery of mechanical properties relative to the as-welded state.

Cold Metal Transfer (CMT) has emerged as a low-heat-input fusion route for aluminum alloys that narrows the HAZ, suppresses spatter/porosity, and stabilizes bead geometry, yielding joint properties that often exceed conventional GTAW/GMAW and, in several studies, approach those of solid-state joints when parameters and post-treatments are optimized31,32. Yang et al.33 dissected the HAZ-softening mechanism in 6082-T6 CMT joints, showing a direct link between heat input and hardness loss/soft-zone width, and attributing strength reduction primarily to precipitate dissolution and over-ageing evidence that CMT mitigates, but does not eliminate, the precipitation sensitivity of 6xxx alloys. Yelamasetti et al.34 reported that post-weld heat treatment of 2014-T6 CMT welds increased tensile strength and micro-hardness relative to the as-welded condition, with microstructural evidence of recovery/precipitation strengthening underscoring that CMT’s low-heat-input fusion metallurgy responds well to modest thermal schedules.

Although numerous studies have investigated 6xxx series aluminum alloys welded by GTAW, GMAW, CMT, and FSW, the combined mechanical and corrosion performance of medium-thickness weldments remains insufficiently understood. In particular, research on 6-mm AA6061 joints produced using Cu-rich ER2219 filler is limited, and the effects of weld–substrate compositional mismatch on precipitation behavior, galvanic interactions, and localized softening are not yet well established35,36. Furthermore, integrated correlations linking microstructural evolution with mechanical response and electrochemical behavior are limited12. This study addresses these gaps by systematically evaluating the coupled influence of Cold Metal Transfer (CMT) welding and subsequent friction stir processing (FSP) on the structural integrity, mechanical strengthening, and corrosion characteristics of 6-mm AA6061–ER2219 joints, thereby establishing a process–structure–property framework relevant to industrial applications.

Materials and experimentation

Plates of AA6061-T6 with a thickness of 6 mm were used as the base material. Rectangular coupons of 120 × 75 mm were sectioned from mill stock by wire-EDM to avoid thermal distortion and edge work-hardening. The chemical composition of the base alloy (major alloying elements) is reported in Table 1, and the room-temperature mechanical properties are listed in Table 2. Welding was performed with ER2219 filler wire of 1.6 mm diameter; its principal alloying additions (Cu-rich Al–Cu) and nominal properties are also summarized in Table 2. Mating edges were prepared to a standard single-V groove with a 2 mm land (root face). Prior to welding, faying surfaces and adjacent heat-tint regions were degreased (acetone) and stainless-steel wire-brushed to remove oxide and contaminants. Bead-on-plate and full-penetration butt joints were produced using a FRONIUS TRANSPULS SYNERGIC 4000 (CMT) power source. Argon shielding was employed at a flow rate of 10 L min⁻1. Process inputs were kept constant across all specimens to enable iso-parametric comparisons. One of each pair of CMT-welded joints was subsequently processed by friction stir processing (FSP) restricted to the weld-bead surface. Friction stir processing was carried out using a single-pass approach along the weld centerline with a non-consumable H13 steel tool. The tool consisted of a cylindrical shoulder and a tapered pin with a pin height of 5.7 mm, while the shoulder diameter of 18 mm was maintained. Processing was performed using a conventional FSP machine, with the tool rotation direction aligned with the welding direction and a fixed tilt angle applied to ensure adequate material consolidation. The plunge depth was controlled to achieve full pin engagement within the weld region without excessive shoulder penetration. All processing was conducted on plates oriented along the rolling direction, and FSP was applied on one side only. The parameters used for both the techniques are listed in Table 3. These parameters were fixed from the literature review22 and trail runs. The schematic view of both the techniques are shown in Fig. 1. Characterization

All welds (as-CMT and CMT + FSP) were first screened by X-ray radiography test, and images were reviewed for porosity, lack of fusion, and linear indications prior to destructive testing. Plates were sectioned by wire-EDM, and coupons for microstructure, corrosion, tensile, micro-hardness, and impact were machined with the weld centered for transverse specimens, then edge-finished and solvent-cleaned. The 3D model view and sliced specimens from welded structures are shown in Figs. 3 and 4. Metallography followed ASTM E3: cross-sections normal to the weld were ground and polished to a colloidal-silica finish, then etched per ASTM E407 (Keller’s reagent); where grain contrast was required, Barker’s anodizing was applied and examined under polarized light. Optical micrographs were acquired across base metal to weld zone.

3D model of welded and testing coupons of (a) CMT and (b) FSP techniques.

Sliced welding coupons for (a) corrosion test, (b) Impact test, (c) microstructure/micro-hardness and (d) tensile test.

The CMT-welded and CMT + FSP 6 mm thick AA6061 plates were ground and polished to a uniform surface finish to eliminate the influence of FSP-induced roughness on the electrochemical measurements. Corrosion behavior was measured in 3.5 wt.% NaCl at room temperature using a three-electrode cell after OCP stabilization, with potentio-dynamic polarization and Tafel analysis to obtain E_corrossion and i_corrossion. Tensile tests followed ASTM E88 on a calibrated Universal Testing Machine (UTM) with an axial extensometer at an initial strain rate of 10⁻3 s⁻1; reported metrics include 0.2% YS, UTS, total elongation, and fracture location. Three samples were taken for tensile test as mentioned in Fig. 3 to get the standard deviation. Vickers micro-hardness mapping complied with ASTM E38410 using a light load and fixed dwell; traverses were taken from the base metal through the heat-affected zone, across the fusion/stir zone, and back through the opposite heat-affected zone into the base metal. Izod impact testing followed ASTM E2310 using V-notched specimens positioned to interrogate the target zone.

Results and discussions

X-ray radiography

Representative radiographs for the as-CMT and CMT + FSP joints show clean, continuous beads with uniform reinforcement and penetration. Within the method’s sensitivity, no volumetric indications and planar defects were detected; image quality indicators confirmed adequate contrast and resolution. The fusion line appears smooth and symmetric, evidencing stable arc behavior and proper mixing/dilution. For the FSP-processed specimens, the radiographs reveal no tunnels or voids along the processed track, indicating sound material consolidation. These findings validate the joints for subsequent mechanical, microstructural, and corrosion evaluations.

Microstructural studies

The macro- and microstructures of the CMT-welded AA6061–ER2219 joint are shown in Fig. 5, illustrating a well-formed, continuous bead with appropriate crown height and full penetration. The fusion zone (FZ) is symmetric about the centerline; the fusion line is smooth and continuous, and the HAZ is narrow and uniform. No macro-segregation, banding, or centerline solidification defects are evident; bead toe and root are free from visible lack-of-fusion. At optical scale, the FZ displays a fine cellular–dendritic α-Al matrix characteristic of Al–Cu weld metal, transitioning from columnar growth near the fusion boundary to a mixed cellular and equiaxed morphology toward the mid-thickness. The weld top (crown) and bottom (root) regions exhibit comparable morphology, indicating stable thermal fields and proper mixing/dilution. The HAZ adjacent to the fusion line shows slight grain coarsening with clean interfaces; no liquation cracking or oxide entrapment is observed in the etched condition. Overall, the CMT parameters yield a homogeneous weldment with uniform filler distribution34.

CMT AA6061 weldments (a) Macrostructure (1 µm), (b) HAZ (100 µm), (c) Interface (50 µm), (d) Top weld zone (100 µm) and (e) Bottom weld zone (50 µm).

After friction stir processing along the weld bead, the macrograph (Fig. 6) shows a smooth, flash-free processed track centered on the joint, with no tunnels or voids visible in cross-section and a continuous interface to the surrounding material. The stir zone (SZ) exhibits a fine, equiaxed grain structure produced by dynamic recrystallization, with characteristic onion-ring flow patterns beneath the shoulder. Compared with the as-welded fusion zone, second-phase constituents are broken up and redistributed, indicating chemical/mechanical homogenization of the Cu-rich weld metal into the Al–Mg–Si substrate. On both the advancing and retreating sides, a thermo-mechanically affected zone (TMAZ) composed of plastically deformed and rotated grains transitions smoothly into the heat-affected zone (HAZ), forming clean and defect-free interfaces. The crown and near-surface regions show the strongest refinement due to shoulder contact, while the root presents a milder TMAZ/SZ transition consistent with the tool-pin reach26. Overall, FSP eliminates porosity, levels compositional banding, and produces a more uniform, recrystallized structure across the prior weld, establishing a robust baseline for subsequent hardness, tensile, and corrosion assessments.

Corrosion studies

The Potential–log (I) polarization curves for similar AA6061 weldments in the CMT, CMT + FSP conditions are shown in Fig. 7, and Tafel-derived parameters are summarized in Table 4. The CMT weldment exhibits a relatively noble corrosion potential (E_corr = + 0.0646 V) with a very low corrosion current density (i_corr = 4.06 × 10⁻⁷ A·cm⁻2, ormalized to 1.0 cm2). Together with Rₚ = 1.90 × 104 Ω and Tafel slopes bₐ = 0.022 V·dec⁻1, b = 0.086 V·dec⁻1, this yields a corrosion rate of 0.004474 mm·y⁻1. Post-processing by FSP shifts the curve to a less noble potential (E_corr = + 0.0174 V) and increases the corrosion current density to 1.21 × 10⁻⁶ A·cm⁻2, with Rₚ = 2.04 × 104 Ω, bₐ = 0.149 V·dec⁻1, and b = 0.092 V·dec⁻1. The corresponding corrosion rate is 0.01368 mm·y⁻1. Relative to CMT, CMT + FSP is 47.2 mV more active (less noble E_corr) and displays a 3.0 × higher i_corr, giving a 3.06 × higher corrosion rate, even though Rₚ increases modestly by 7.4%. Consistent with the electrochemical parameters, the CMT + FSP curve lies at higher current density across the potential range, confirming a less favorable corrosion state. Microstructurally, FSP creates a fine, equi-axed surface layer and fragments/redistributes Cu-rich constituents, which increases the effective cathodic area and micro-galvanic activity; shoulder-induced surface work/relief further lowers the pitting threshold. Consequently, the as-CMT joint is more corrosion-resistant under the present surface preparation. Where FSP is needed for mechanical gains, fine polishing plus sealed anodizing is advised to recover corrosion performance24,39.

Tensile properties and Impact energy

The fracture specimens under tensile uni-axial loading and sudden loads are shown in Fig. 8, and the averaged metrics are listed in Table 5. The stress–strain plot of CMT and CMT + FSP welded specimens is shown in Fig. 9. The as-CMT joints achieved UTS 216, YS 198 MPa, and elongation 12.6%. Following FSP, all tensile measures increased to UTS 252 MPa, YS 215 MPa, and elongation 23.8% corresponding to gains of 16.7% (UTS), 8.6% (YS), and 89% (elongation). Engineering stress–strain traces showed an extended uniform-strain regime and later necking in the CMT + FSP condition, while fracture location shifted farther from the weld centerline. Scatter was low in both sets, with slightly larger SDs post-FSP attributable to minor variation in processed-track thickness. Consistent with the tensile response, Izod impact energy rose from 9.5 J (as-CMT) to 15.4 J (CMT + FSP), an increase of 62%; post-impact surfaces exhibited broader shear-lip formation and fewer faceted regions after FSP41. The processed joint achieved a UTS of 252 MPa compared to the base material value of 276 MPa, corresponding to a strength recovery (joint efficiency) of approximately 91%. The graphical representation of average tensile and impact properties of both the welded plates are shown in Fig. 10.

The as-CMT condition contains a cellular/dendritic fusion zone adjacent to a softened HAZ, encouraging early strain localization and notch sensitivity hence the lower UTS/YS, reduced elongation, and modest impact energy. FSP generates a fine, equiaxed stir zone, porosity, and homogenizes micro-hardness across the joint; these changes delay necking and crack initiation, directly explaining the observed increases in strength, ductility, and Izod energy.

Micro-hardness

Figure 11 shows the mid-thickness Vickers traverses across the joint, from base metal through the heat-affected zone to the weld centerline and back across the opposite heat-affected zone into the base metal. The base metal on both sides remains stable at 60 HV. Approaching the weld from the left, hardness rises and forms a plateau across the fusion region of the CMT joint at 100 HV; in the CMT + FSP condition this plateau is consistently higher by 5–8 HV through the processed band around the centerline. On the right-hand side, the CMT profile shows a distinct HAZ soft zone with a minimum near 76–80 HV before recovering to the BM level, whereas CMT + FSP lifts this trough to 84–88 HV and narrows its width, with visibly reduced point-to-point scatter. These results indicate that FSP elevates weld-region hardness and contracts the softened HAZ while leaving the BM unchanged. The uplift and homogenization are consistent with dynamic recrystallization and second-phase fragmentation/redistribution in the stir zone, which reduce heterogeneity inherited from fusion solidification and underpin the measured gains in tensile strength and impact energy20,42.

Conclusion

The following key-points are drawn from the current investigation on AA6061 similar weldments developed by Cold Metal Transfer (CMT) and post-processing with Friction Stir Processing (FSP);

The weld showed a well-formed fusion zone with smooth fusion lines and a narrow HAZ; after a single FSP pass, the joint developed a fine, equiaxed stir zone with clear onion-ring flow patterns, eliminated residual micro-voids, and exhibited cleaner, more continuous interfaces across the former fusion boundary.

With regard to the base metal, the joint efficiency increased from 70% for the CMT condition to 81% after combined CMT + FSP process.
