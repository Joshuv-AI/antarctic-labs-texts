Perspectives and experiences related to caring for older people among nursing students in a state university in Sri Lanka: a qualitative study
Authors: Chamodi Kaushalya Madhushani, Sarath Rathnayake
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo explore the perspectives and experiences related to caring for older people during clinical placement among undergraduate nursing students.DesignA qualitative exploratory study analysing individual interview data.SettingDepartment of Nursing, Faculty of Allied Health Sciences, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka.Participants: A purposive sample of 14 undergraduate nursing students.MethodSemi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted, audio-recorded and transcribed. Data were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis framework.ResultsFive themes emerged: (1) understanding ageing: The concept of ‘Age’, (2) Complexity of care: navigating challenges, (3) Care provision: rewards, growth and fulfilment, (4) Addressing challenges: barriers to support and (5) Reaching the horizon: enhancing geriatric education. Nursing students encountered both positive and negative experiences related to caring for older people. Positive experiences included increased self-awareness, learning opportunities, skill development and personal satisfaction. Negative experiences involved student-related and patient-related challenges, initial difficulties in providing care and inadequate support and mentoring during clinical placements.ConclusionDespite the complexities of caring for older people, nursing students are motivated to learn and provide the necessary care. This study highlights the need for quality care for older people, the importance of early geriatric education and the need for enhancing clinical supervision and mentoring.

This study provides rich, detailed and context-specific insights into nursing students’ perspectives and experiences related to caring for older people.

Introduction

According to the WHO,1 the population’s proportion of individuals aged 60 and over is growing. This growing ageing population worldwide suggests that providing care for older people is a global challenge.2 3 The ageing population has led to increasing demand for public health and social care services, raising concerns about the standard of care for older people.4 Therefore, there is a rising need for health professionals, especially nurses with specialised knowledge in gerontological nursing, to address the challenges posed by the expanding and complex ageing population.4 Furthermore, the attitudes of healthcare professionals towards older people can influence the quality of care they provide to this population, affecting their behaviour, interactions and the delivery of healthcare and other services.5

Nurses are the largest occupational group in healthcare and play an important role in delivering high-quality care for older people.5 Unfortunately, gerontological nursing is not regarded as a popular specialty in nursing and is frequently seen as challenging.6 Numerous studies indicate that nursing students often hold negative perceptions and attitudes in relation to providing care for older patients.79 Nursing students are the future workforce.7 The quality of nursing care for older people is significantly influenced by the education they receive.7 Their unfavourable attitudes of caring for older people are significantly shaped by their education.9 Therefore, nurse educators need to comprehend students’ experiences with older individuals to create effective educational strategies aimed at encouraging future nurses to choose geriatrics as a career choice.10

The identification and assessment of attitudes toward older people among younger groups have drawn more attention in recent years.11 Positive attitudes of clinical staff have a positive impact on students’ learning,11 and one’s attitudes, perspectives or perceptions can impact the quality of care provided.4 Student nurses’ attitudes and perceptions are important as they will care for older people in the future.12 According to the Social Cognitive Career Theory,13 student attitudes during clinical placements can shape their confidence and expectations about outcomes. This means that positive attitudes may encourage them to develop strong skills and even choose certain specialities, while negative attitudes can lower confidence and limit both career choices and clinical competence.13 Understanding nursing students’ perceptions and experiences can help identify gaps in their knowledge, attitudes and skills that may influence the quality of care.14 Such insights can inform curriculum planning, training and clinical support strategies, ultimately enhancing the preparedness of future nurses and improving care for older people.15

Nursing students’ perspectives towards older people are both positive and negative. For example, a recent systematic review reported that nursing students in Northern Ireland, Canada and Portugal expressed negative attitudes and discriminatory behaviours towards older people, whereas students in Turkey, Spain, the United Kingdom and Nigeria demonstrated more positive attitudes.16 Cultural norms, family structures and social values strongly shape how older people are viewed and treated. These findings underscore the potential influence of cultural and societal factors on nursing students’ attitudes towards older people.17 18 In the Sri Lankan context, the strong tradition of family caregiving and respect for older people may encourage nursing students to hold positive attitudes towards caring for older people.19 Yet another study examined staff and student nurses’ perceptions related to older people and reported positive perceptions.20 A study that examined the experiences of Canadian nursing students with older people found that students particularly enjoyed being with older people if they valued and appreciated the company of their grandparents and other older family members.10

In Sri Lanka, there are no well-established systems to care for older people living in the community or residential aged care facilities by registered nurses, even though these services must be given or overseen by registered nurses.21 Gerontological nursing is thus a crucial initiative to address the expanding nursing care needs of older people in Sri Lanka.21 The limited attention of health policymakers, nursing educators and leaders has hindered the advancement of gerontological nursing education in Sri Lanka.21 The multidisciplinary partnership between geriatrics and gerontology is crucial for Sri Lanka, as it can help in addressing the complex health and social care requirements of an ageing population22 and can facilitate specialised care provision for older people.22 Gerontological nursing is not yet essentially recognised as a central nursing competency in the Sri Lankan nursing education system.21

Most studies have employed a quantitative approach, while limited qualitative studies are available to examine nurse professionals, including nursing students’ perspectives and experiences related to the care of older people.3 6 Qualitative research is particularly valuable in this context as it provides rich, in-depth insights into individuals’ perspectives and experiences, which cannot be adequately captured through quantitative measures alone.23 Moreover, exploring student nurses’ perspectives, including their experiences, can directly inform curriculum development, guide policy decisions and improve the structuring of clinical placements to better prepare future nurses for caring for an ageing population.68 Therefore, this study aimed to explore the perspectives and experiences of undergraduate nursing students during their clinical placement in caring for older people at a public university in Sri Lanka.

Methodology

Design

This was a qualitative exploratory study. The qualitative exploratory approach enabled us to uncover new insights and themes that had not been previously explored, thereby providing a deeper understanding of the phenomenon of interest in this study.23 A qualitative exploratory study is well-suited for exploring complex and subjective experiences and perceptions.24 This qualitative approach offers deeper insights into the area studied and emphasises the need for thorough exploration of these perspectives25; therefore, the findings of this study can be used in designing curricula and planning policies related to geriatric and gerontological nursing.

This study is underpinned by an interpretivist/constructivist paradigm.26 This recognises reality as socially constructed and places individuals’ experiences, perceptions and meanings at the centre of understanding a phenomenon.26 This philosophical underpinning aligns with the study’s aim of exploring nursing students’ perspectives, experiences and meaning towards ageing and the care of older people and is suitable for examining the realities faced by students who provide care for older people.

Moreover, we followed the reflexive thematic analysis approach in this study, including study design, participant recruitment, interviewing techniques, data analysis and reporting,27 28 and this approach helped us to understand participants’ perspectives, experiences and needs29 related to providing care for older people. The details of the Reflexive Thematic Analysis Reporting Guidelines (RTARG) are presented in online supplemental file 1. Additionally, the reporting of results followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR), which are presented in online supplemental file 2.

Population, sample and sampling

The study population was undergraduate nursing students at the Faculty of Allied Health Sciences, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka. In this study, a purposive sample of nursing students was selected. We employed purposive sampling to select participants who could provide rich, informative data related to the care of older people. Students from four academic years were included to achieve maximum variation in the sample. Undergraduate students who had not yet attended clinical practice were excluded. Aligned with the reflective thematic analysis approach by Braun and Clarke,2729 the sample size was determined using researchers’ reflective judgement to collect comprehensive information that meets the objectives and requirements of the study, ensuring sufficient depth and diversity of perspectives for meaningful analysis. In this study, ‘researchers’ reflective judgement’ referred to a repeated and ongoing team process guided by Braun and Clarke’s reflective thematic approach to qualitative sampling.2729 As interviews were conducted, the research team regularly reviewed the data to assess whether the study objectives were being met and whether sufficient depth and diversity of perspectives had been achieved. Recruitment continued until the team reached a shared agreement that no new meaningful information was emerging, and the data were rich enough to meet the research objectives. The research team determined sufficient depth and diversity by considering the breadth of the research questions, the variety of participant experiences and the richness and quality of the interview data. Consistent with Braun and Clarke (2021) framework,28 data saturation was not the focus of this approach. Data collection was stopped after the team agreed that sufficient information had been gathered to address the study objectives. All students who consented to participate completed the interviews without dropping out.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were undergraduate nursing students who had completed at least one semester of clinical practice, had provided care for older people during their clinical placement and were willing to articulate their experiences related to caring for older people. Exclusion criteria included students who had no experience in providing care for older people during their clinical placements.

Data collection

Data collection was carried out in September and October 2023 by the first researcher. Two weeks prior to the interviews, an invitation was distributed through social media (WhatsApp) to all nursing students and was displayed at the department premises. In the next stage, inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied. All interviews were conducted in a quiet, private room within the faculty premises to ensure confidentiality and minimise interruptions. Only the participant and the interviewer were present during each interview. After providing written information and a verbal description of the study and the interview, written informed consent was obtained from each participant. Then, face-to-face interviews were conducted. The interviews were recorded with prior permission from the interviewees. Each interview lasted 30–40 min. Additionally, field notes were taken and reflective notes were maintained. In this study, a semi-structured interview guide (online supplemental file 3) was used to conduct interviews. It included 12 open-ended questions. The interview guide was developed based on the literature6 10 and reviewed by two experts with experience in qualitative research in nursing. Based on their feedback, the interview guide was modified. Additionally, the interview guide was pre-tested among four undergraduate nursing students.

Research team: The first author (CK) is a Demonstrator in Nursing with a BScN (Hons) qualification. This project was conducted as a requirement for the BScN (Hons) programme of the first author. The second author (SR) is a Senior Research Fellow in Health Informatics with experience in nursing, gerontology, dementia care, health informatics and qualitative methodology.

Data analysis

Each interview was transcribed verbatim. Data were analysed manually following the reflective thematic analysis approach.27 This approach included six phases: (i) familiarising with the data, (ii) generating initial codes, (iii) searching for themes, (iv) reviewing themes, (v) defining and naming themes and (vi) producing the report.27 30 Based on this approach, we followed the reflexive thematic analysis reporting guidelines (RTARG)31 and the 15-point checklist of criteria for good thematic analysis.31 Coding, theme development and mapping were conducted by two researchers using printed transcripts and reflective notes. After data analysis, a summary of the results was shared with participants. Participants were invited to review the summary, confirming whether the findings accurately reflected their views and experiences. The details of the analysis process are presented below.

Familiarisation with the data

The first researcher carefully listened to the audio recordings multiple times and transcribed them verbatim.30 32 The researcher reviewed transcripts and cross-checked with recordings for accuracy. Typographical errors were corrected, and key ideas were highlighted. This process helped to gain a holistic understanding of participants’ experiences.

Generating initial codes

The first researcher coded the transcripts, which were reviewed by a second researcher for reflexivity. The team collaboratively refined the codes. The data were coded using both inductive and deductive approaches. Deductive codes were derived from the research questions. Literature review supported both inductive and deductive coding.6 10 Inductive codes were developed directly from participants’ transcripts. Deductive codes were applied first to identify data relevant to our research aims, followed by inductive coding to capture new concepts emerging from the data. Semantic codes, which represent the explicit meaning of the text, were identified. Latent codes, though used less frequently, were applied to uncover underlying meanings, such as concepts, ideas or assumptions that support the surface-level content. Coded data were manually organised and managed.

Searching for themes

After coding was completed, similar codes were grouped together to form initial themes. Themes were developed inductively based on the data. To illustrate the connections between themes and subthemes, a thematic map was manually created. Table 1 includes the themes and subthemes with exemplary quotes.

Reviewing themes

The themes were reviewed against the entire dataset to ensure coherence, distinctiveness and alignment with the research objectives. Each theme was compared to all coded data and the full transcripts to check if it accurately reflected participants’ experiences. Two researchers independently assessed how well the themes aligned with the dataset and discussed any differences to reach consensus. Themes were refined, merged or renamed as needed to make sure that they represented the full range and depth of the data while addressing the research objectives. Some themes were merged and refined based on their relevance to the research objectives.

Defining and naming themes

Themes were clearly defined, named and interpreted to provide deeper meanings beyond participant responses.

Producing the report

A journal article and an abstract were drafted.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee, Faculty of Allied Health Sciences, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka (No: AHS/ERC/2023/074). The confidentiality and anonymity of the information were protected.

Findings: Themes and sub-themes

This study identified five key themes and their attended sub-themes. Table 2 shows the themes and sub-themes (table 1). Exemplary quotes are provided in online supplemental file 4 to enhance analytical transparency of this study.

Theme 1: Understanding ageing: The concept of ‘Age’

This theme provides insights into how nursing students define their perspectives on older people and health, offering a foundational understanding of their views on ageing. This theme reflects the different views that shape how people perceive care and ageing.

Defining old age: Biological to social

Most participants used chronological age to define older people, and the majority identified age 60 and over as an age limit for older people. Participant 11, a third-year nursing student stated, “As far as I am concerned, an old person is someone older with good knowledge, usually the age is more than 60” (P 11). They further stated that significant changes can be observed in old age which alter the external appearance, for example, changes in hair colour, skin and the appearance of the face. Participant 2, a second-year nursing student, stated, “In my opinion, an old person is someone who has weak energy, wrinkled skin, and grey hair” (P 2).

Participants further stated that their physical appearance could be changed due to the disease conditions and their nutritional level. Participant 1, a fourth-year nursing student stated, “Old people cannot be judged by appearance; they have wrinkled skin due to lack of nutrition or may be due to disease conditions, and we feel they are old” (P 1). However, some participants stated that older people could not be recognised by their external appearance. Participant 10, a third-year nursing student stated, “When I treat, I do not think that he is 65 years old. He may look like 40–50. Then, you will know by reading the BHT (bedhead ticket). There are people who died with beautiful hair” (P 10).

Chronicity and well-being

Most of the participants stated that older people had to live with chronicity, and chronic health problems were common among this population, for example, problems with vision, hearing

and difficulty in talking. Participant 8, a fourth-year nursing student stated, “It is very difficult for older people to do things for themselves. Their concentration is low. Their vision is poor. It is hard for them to think clearly. They find it difficult to hear, and they do not understand what is being said,” (P 8).

Additionally, they reported that older people were at risk of falls due to their physical weaknesses and diseases. Moreover, many participants stated that older people were at risk of developing psychological problems, for example, depression. Participant 13, a third-year nursing student stated, “When they are admitted to the hospital, they are very much stressed. They get depressed soon” (P 13). Moreover, participants reported that older people presented with impaired memory and poor environmental orientation. Participant 7, a third-year nursing student stated,“What is more, older people have psychological problems with age, so they may forget the time to take even a medicine. They may forget the time to take even a meal” (P 7). Additionally, some participants stated that older people experienced loneliness and had fewer social interactions and activities.

Age-related dependency

Participants stated that many older people were dependent, particularly on activities of daily living. For example, Participant 6, a first-year nursing student stated, “When getting old, that person becomes weaker in various aspects, both physical and mental. That means it is hard to get work done. They can not do it without someone’s help. It becomes impossible to walk. Some older people can not even eat or drink” (P 6). A few participants highlighted that older people lacked the energy to engage in activities. For instance, Participant 3, a second-year nursing student stated, “According to my own definition, an older person is a very weak one. They lack the energy to perform their own tasks. They need others’ support always” (P 3).

Healthcare seeking in old age

Participants viewed older people as a group seeking healthcare and services for minor illnesses, but they did not like to stay at the hospital. Participant 4, a fourth-year nursing student stated, “There are some people who are healthy even though they are old. They have come to take medicine for even a minor illness” (P 4). “Many older people do not like to stay in the hospital. Oh, when will you send me? Once, an older person asked me” (P 1).

Theme 2: Complexity of Care: Navigating Challenges

This theme explores the various challenges faced by nursing students, emphasising their attitudes, experiences and emotional responses. It highlights the complexity of providing care and the factors that contribute to stress and challenges faced by nursing students.

Sophistication of care

Participants stated that providing care for older people was more complex than for younger people. For example, one participant highlighted the complexity of geriatric nursing, which impacted the students’ desire to provide care for older people. “Caring for an old person is complex. I do not like geriatric nursing because of the complexity of care” (P 4). Participants stated that providing care for older people was difficult compared with other age groups, especially when they were unconscious, semiconscious, obese, bed-ridden and when they presented with psychiatric illnesses. For example, one participant stated that “Sometimes if they are bedridden, it is difficult to take care of them. When they pass stool, giving perineal care is very difficult” (P 4)

Ageism: From dependency to resistance

Most participants stated that some older people were over-dependent on others. For example, when they were hospitalised, they were over-dependent on student nurses to complete their day-to-day activities. “Older people are depending on others to do work. Some people are afraid of their illness. Therefore, many older people seek more support from us to do their daily activities” (P 4). Some participants stated that some older people were stubborn, especially when student nurses provided care. Furthermore, some participants reported that some older people became angry during care provision, and some older people showed resistance to care. “When we provided care for old people, there were very few people who scolded us. But there were a few people who got angry and asked why we were doing this?” (P 11).

Novice care challenges

Some participants highlighted that providing care for older people for the first time was challenging. “When I first saw an older patient in the ward in my first clinical placement, he was in bed, and the bystander should fulfil all his needs. I realised it was a challenge to provide care for older people” (P 3). Furthermore, some participants reported that student nurses initially hesitated and felt afraid when they provided care for the first time, but later became more confident. “At first, during the first year and second year, I was afraid to give even water to very old patients. I was afraid that they might aspirate. At the beginning, I was afraid because I did not know much about their conditions. As time passed, the fear of doing such a small thing disappeared” (P 4).

Obligation to interest

Although providing care for older people is challenging, some participants stated that student nurses were happy to work with older patients during their clinical placements. They stated that they wished to select geriatric nursing as a career choice. “Yes, I like it. There is a big chance for me to choose the Geriatric side” (P 6). Most participants reported that student nurses provided care for older people because it was their duty and responsibility as nurses. Participant 9, a fourth-year nursing student stated, “Older people have contributed to the country in many ways. Now, they are sick. Therefore, it is our duty to take care of them” (P 9). Furthermore, some participants stated that they did not want to choose geriatric nursing as their career choice and they provided care for older people without their interest. They further highlighted that they provided care because staff nurses asked them to do so. “I do not want to give mouth care for him. I can not stand near that patient due to the bad smell. The smell went through my head, and I felt dizzy. It was going to vomit. But I gave mouth care because the staff nurse asked to do” (P 2).

Caring with empathy and compassion

Some participants stated that older people expected love and affection from others; therefore, student nurses needed to provide care for older people with empathy, kindness and compassion. “Sometimes older people refuse to take medicine. I mean, I think it would be better if we could administer it with love and kindness, without being too harsh” (P 6). “When I see an old person, the first thing I always remember is my ill grandparents” (P 7).

Gaps in care: sadness and guilt

Participants stated that older people did not receive adequate care, especially in the activities of daily living. Some participants mentioned that there was a delay in the provision of care. “When wards are busy, there is a delay in providing care” (P 3). “Most of the staff nurses do not pay attention to older peoples’ basic needs” (P 8). Participants further reported that nurses paid poor attention to maintaining the privacy of older people. For example, one participant stated,“We need to think more about the privacy of patients. Privacy is not well maintained in wards. Age does not mean that they should not be respected” (P 7). They highlighted their feeling of sadness when they saw older people with chronic diseases, dying patients and helpless older people. “I felt sad. No one visits some older people, even their youngest child. They are helpless” (P 6).

Moreover, some participants expressed feelings of guilt when they were unable to provide quality nursing care to every patient in the same manner. “We cannot provide quality nursing care for each patient due to a lack of time and an increased number of patients in the wards. So, it makes me feel guilty” (P 4). Therefore, they stated that there is a need to improve the quality of nursing care for older people. “In the ward setting, providing quality nursing care for older people is limited; therefore, I think there needs to be an improvement in the quality of nursing care they receive” (P 11). Some participants stated that older people required reassurance and psychological support from staff, and that care should be based on the best interests of older people. One participant stated, “As student nurses, we can reassure older patients and help improve their health by doing so. This provides psychological support that aids in their recovery” (P 1). Participants highlighted that nurses and students needed to maintain good communication while caring for older people, as good communication promoted the recovery of the patients. “We need to understand them and get to know them better. Then, we have to build good communication with them and move forward” (P 9).

Theme 3: Care provision: Rewards, growth and fulfilment

This theme focuses on the positive outcomes and personal growth experienced by students, showing the rewards of providing care. This theme highlights the personal rewards that come with the challenges of caregiving.

Life lesson learnt

Participants reported that providing care for older people was an opportunity to gain insight into the realities of life. “In addition, it helps us realise the reality of life, as we are now truly experiencing ageing and associated issues” (P 4).

Some participants further stated that this understanding helped them provide effective care, especially when caring for grandparents at home. “They are valuable people in our lives. Providing care for them has taught us a valuable lesson. If there is a grandmother at home who was sick, now we know how to provide care for her” (P 10).

Shared wisdom

Participants stated that older people shared their life experiences with student nurses and offered advice based on those experiences, and this knowledge was very important to students’ lives. One participant stated, “Older people share their experiences with us. Then, we can learn several lessons from them. One example is the importance of working on time, and establishing good relationships with other staff members” (P 3). Some participants highlighted that older people who worked in health-related jobs provided valuable advice that was very important in clinical learning and career development. “There are some educated older people who did nursing, such as teachers and nurses. They give us advice on career development and motivate us to continue our studies” (P 7).

Learning and career growth

Most participants highlighted that providing care for older people offered them an opportunity to acquire new knowledge and hands-on skills related to providing care for older people. “As I see it, older people are a valuable resource for us to learn new disease conditions. We gain hands-on skills by providing care for them, which makes us more confident in our nursing skills” (P 13). Moreover, participants stated that providing care for older people was an opportunity to identify future career opportunities, as this area is in high demand globally. “With the current demand, it has been recognised as a demanding field. In the future, we might choose geriatrics because of this demand” (P 8).

Fulfilment through care

Participants reported that providing care for older people brought them a sense of self-satisfaction, particularly when older people expressed positive feedback on the care provided. “There was another patient who had not been bathed for a week. So, I bathed him. He said I had earned a lot of merits for that, which made me very happy and feel great” (P 10). Some participants reported experiencing genuine happiness when patients were discharged from the hospital after recovering from their illnesses. For example, one participant stated, “Helping an older patient brought me joy. I felt very happy when they were discharged from the hospital after their recovery” (P 1).

Theme 4: Addressing challenges: Barriers to support

This theme addresses the various challenges faced by students in clinical settings, highlighting the support systems and resources available to them. It highlights the critical factors that affect learning and the quality of patient care.

Nursing student hurdles

Some participants stated that they had inadequate knowledge and skills and were emotionally affected, which hindered their ability to provide effective care for older people. Participant 5, a first-year nursing student stated, “I went to help a friend with a procedure. Providing care for that grandmother was somewhat difficult. I did not know how to help my friend” (P 5). Additionally, some participants reported difficulties in communication with older people due to language barriers. “Most of the time, I do not understand what the tamil-speaking patients say” (P 2).

Patient-related barriers in care

A few participants highlighted that well-educated older people did not like to receive advanced care, for example, procedures such as cannulation and collecting blood samples by students. Sometimes, they asked too many questions to check the student nurses’ knowledge and skills. “Most of the time, when we went to care for well-educated older people, they refused us. When we introduced ourselves as student nurses, they preferred to be cared for by a staff nurse and often asked us many questions” (P 13). Moreover, another challenge faced by student nurses was obtaining consent for nursing procedures from older people, and some requested that such procedures be performed by a staff nurse. “We are student nurses. Then, they do not want to take injections from student nurses. They ask staff nurses to give injections” (P 3). Another main challenge faced by student nurses was communication problems that occurred while providing care for older people. “There are problems in communication with older people, meaning they do not understand what I am saying” (P 5).

Insufficient support from staff nurses

Participants reported that students provided care for older people under the supervision of staff nurses and received support from them. However, they emphasised that the level of support was not always adequate. Some students felt that they were required to manage complex situations with minimal guidance, which left them feeling uncertain and overwhelmed. One participant expressed this concern, stating, “It would be helpful to receive more support from staff nurses. Most of the time, staff nurses do not closely observe what we are doing. I believe they should provide more supervision and guidance instead of overlooking us” (P 7). “Most of the time, staff nurses do not have enough time to supervise us or teach us with their busy schedules” (P 9).

Resource limitations

Most participants reported that insufficient resources were one of the main challenges when providing care for older people. “The lack of resources in Sri Lanka’s health system is a problem. For example, you are going to insert a catheter. A recent experience was that you usually need an 18-gauge catheter, but the ward only had a 14-gauge catheter. This issue arises due to the lack of such resources” (P 3). “In our country, we do not have enough resources in the government setup. Such as sufficient beds, equipment, and medicines, among others. Sometimes, two patients were in the same bed. It was really difficult for the patients, especially for the older people” (P 2).

Theme 5: Reaching the horizon: Enhancing geriatric education

This is the final theme that explored the need for comprehensive geriatric education in the basic curriculum of undergraduate nursing students.

Bridging the gaps in the curriculum

Participants stated that students were interested in expanding their knowledge about older people’s care. They reported that there should be a separate geriatric module in the curriculum. “We still only have a few hours for older people care under an Adult Nursing module. There should be such a module. We do not have that” (P 7). In addition, participants suggested the need for increased clinical hours for geriatric care, including field visits to community care centres, aged care and rehabilitation centres. For example, one participant stated, “It would be beneficial to increase the clinical hours for geriatric care and provide more opportunities for practising procedures under new module. Arranging additional visits to community, aged care and rehabilitation centres would also be advantageous for the students” (P 11).

Participants highlighted that the department should provide basic knowledge about geriatric nursing from the beginning of the degree programme. For instance, one participant stated, “Geriatric nursing should be introduced from the first year” (P 3). They further stated the need for expanded knowledge on addressing the psychological problems of older people. One participant stated, “I think it would be helpful if we could spend more time on learning how to understand patients and how to approach their care. It would also be beneficial if we were taught strategies to support restoring patient’s psychological well-being” (P 6). A few participants reported that students needed to improve their hands-on nursing skills. In addition, participants reported that the department should teach some basic procedures necessary for geriatric care, such as nasogastric feeding, starting from the first year. Other learning needs, highlighted by students, included enhancing attitudes towards older people, developing soft skills such as kindness and patience, improving communication skills and learning to use new technology in gerontological care. Participant 12, a fourth-year nursing student stated, “I think it is better if we were taught more soft skills, such as how to maintain effective communication and how to be more kind and patient with difficult older people” (P 12).

Mentoring and emotional support

Participants stated that students struggled with their emotions while providing care. Another participant highlighted that students require education and support to manage their emotions before and during their clinical placements. “I sometimes get angry when I work with older people. Therefore, before sending us to the ward, we need training on how to manage our emotions. When we face challenges, there should be someone available to talk to” (P 2). Moreover, participants highlighted that students needed more clinical supervision and bedside teaching at the clinical placement. “I think if there is someone to supervise our work closely, we could address more issues. When we learn through practice without close supervision, staff occasionally point out our mistakes. We make errors without realising they are mistakes and continue repeating them, and by the time we notice, it is often too late. Therefore, close supervision would be very helpful” (P 5).

Discussion

This study provides insight into the perspectives and experiences of undergraduate nursing students regarding the care of older people. Understanding these perspectives is crucial as nursing students play a vital role in shaping the future of geriatric care. To our knowledge, this is the first study on nursing students’ perspectives and experiences related to caring for older people conducted in Sri Lanka. Findings from this study indicate that students define older people based on different attributes, including chronological age, physical appearance, chronic health conditions and levels of dependency. In line with a previous study, this study confirmed that the participants defined an older person using their chronological age, physical activity level and physical appearance.6 The present study further identified that students viewed that older people frequently experience a range of physical and psychological health issues, dependencies and disabilities compared with younger people. Participants commonly described older people prone to forgetfulness and mobility issues, which aligns with prior findings on age-related cognitive and physical decline.6 Ageing is not just about a person’s age in numbers but also includes their physical abilities and overall health. Consistent with previous studies,6 10 the present study reported that older people’s emotional fragility manifested as feelings of loneliness, sadness and depression. These emotional challenges can significantly impact older people’s overall well-being, emphasising the importance of holistic and compassionate geriatric care. Moreover, our study reported that older people were physically weak and dependent on others for their daily living activities, confirming the findings of previous research.6 Such dependency highlights the need for nursing students to develop both technical and empathetic caregiving skills to support this vulnerable population effectively.

This study further reports that geriatric nursing is a complex and challenging field, making the provision of effective care for older people challenging. Many nursing students find it difficult to meet the diverse needs of older patients, as their care often requires a combination of physical, emotional and psychological support. This result is consistent with the findings of a previous study.6 By highlighting these challenges, the findings suggest that clinical training programmes should offer structured opportunities for students to engage with older patients with complex geriatric problems under appropriate supervision.

Moreover, the majority of students reported negative experiences related to their first engagement with older people. This finding aligns with the findings of a recent study.20 Consistent with recent literature,9 this study also found that students felt afraid and shocked when providing care for older people for the first time. Consequently, these complexities need to be addressed through the curriculum when preparing students for the care of older people. However, consistent with a previous study,9 most participants in this study preferred to work with older people despite facing several challenges. These findings suggest that early clinical exposure, combined with supportive mentoring, can enhance students’ confidence and attitudes towards geriatric care.

Additionally, some students who perceived older people as boring/uninteresting stated that they provided care for older people as a part of their assigned duties without genuine interest. Their limited commitment to caring for older people reflects a challenge in preparing nurses for future geriatric nursing practice. This finding is confirmed by previous research.6 Similar to the findings of a recent study,20 this study also reported that staff members paid insufficient attention to older people when addressing their needs. Nursing students must possess strong empathy and maintain positive attitudes towards older people to provide high-quality nursing care, which in turn enhances their quality of life.6 33 Our study found that students viewed older patients as ‘their grandparents’, ‘an opportunity’ and ‘a duty’ to care for them, which confirms the findings of a previous study.6 These findings offer insight into diverse perspectives students hold towards older people and can inform nurse educators in the teaching and learning process in geriatric nursing. By incorporating these perspectives into activities, such as simulation exercises and reflective practice sessions, students can gain a deeper understanding of the experiences and needs of older people. This can help them build empathy and cultivate more positive attitudes and experiences in caring for older adults.

In line with the findings of recent studies,6 34 this study also confirmed that student nurses experienced challenges in communicating with older people. Effective communication is a crucial aspect of delivering high-quality nursing care; however, students often find it challenging to interact with older people. While some older people were shown to be outgoing and adept at initiating conversations, others remained quiet and disengaged from the students. Additionally, language barriers posed further challenges, limiting students’ ability to communicate effectively and build rapport with older people. Similar to a previous study,6 we found that students reported experiencing rejection from older patients and some older people preferred to be cared for by staff nurses rather than by student nurses. However, some older people preferred to receive care from student nurses and they were over-dependent on the students. A previous study supports this finding.6 In line with the findings of another study,11 this study also reported inadequate support received from staff nurses. Staff nurses play a crucial role in mentoring and guiding students during their clinical placements, and a lack of support can have a negative impact on students’ learning experiences and confidence.11 Insufficient supervision, guidance or encouragement from staff can make it difficult for students to navigate the challenges of geriatric nursing. Therefore, enhancing staff support in clinical settings is essential.11 These findings indicate that policies should emphasise providing structured mentoring programmes and clear guidelines for staff nurses. This would support nursing students in learning to provide care for older people and enhance their confidence and skills in geriatric nursing.

This study further found that students’ views on providing care for older people were affected by their curriculum. Increasing clinical hours allocated to geriatric care in the current context is essential to ensure that students receive more exposure and practical experience. This finding is consistent with findings from a previous study.9 The present study found that students require additional knowledge, skills and preparation to effectively manage older people with cognitive impairment and communication difficulties. This result is consistent with a previous study.20 Confirming the previous study’s findings,9 nursing students require adequate supervision and mentoring to enhance their knowledge, skills and adaptation in providing care for older people. Adequate guidance from experienced professionals is essential to help students develop both confidence and skills.9 This study further highlighted the need for more specific content on older people’s care in the curriculum, as well as the development of a comprehensive module on older people’s care within the basic nursing curriculum. Such a module would provide students with the essential knowledge and practical skills needed to deliver high-quality care to older people. This finding aligns with the findings from a previous study.9 These curriculum implications suggest that nurse educators and policymakers should collaborate to design geriatric modules that integrate clinical experience, theoretical knowledge and reflective practice.

In contrast to studies conducted in other countries,7 16 most students in our study reported positive attitudes towards working with older people despite initial fears and challenges. These findings suggest that cultural influences, such as strong family values, respect for older people and societal expectations regarding the care of older people,1618 shape students’ perceptions and willingness to engage in geriatric nursing practice. Therefore, this study offers unique insights into how nursing students in Sri Lanka perceive and engage with the care of older adults, highlighting the importance of considering social and cultural contexts in the design of nursing curricula and training programmes.

Students noted characteristics such as helplessness, over-talkativeness, fearfulness, stubbornness or emotional fragility in older people, reflecting ageist stereotypes held by students. However, it is important to emphasise that these characteristics are not limited to older people; any patient, irrespective of age, may exhibit such behaviours in a healthcare setting. Similarly, the descriptions provided by students reflect their perspectives and may be influenced by societal stereotypes about older people and ageing. Some of these perceptions could unintentionally reinforce negative stereotypes, and therefore, they must be interpreted with caution. Future research could explore perspectives across multiple universities, including those of postgraduate students and staff nurses, to better understand their perspectives on older people and care.

Limitations of the study

Although this study provides valuable insights, some limitations should be acknowledged. Since the study included students from only one university, the findings related to their experiences may reflect contextual factors unique to that setting, which limit the transferability of the results to other contexts. Additionally, we did not share the transcripts with the participants for member checking, adding another limitation to this study. However, we shared a summary of the results with the participants, allowing them to verify whether the findings accurately reflected their views and experiences.

Conclusion

Nursing students encountered both positive and negative experiences related to caring for older people. Increasing self-awareness, having learning opportunities, improving knowledge and skills, and having self-satisfaction are some of the positive experiences. Some negative experiences included student-related challenges and barriers (language problems, lack of skills and practice and students’ emotions), patient-related challenges and barriers (difficulty in obtaining consent from older people, over-talkative older people, lack of maintaining self-care and language problems), difficulties in providing care at the beginning of the clinical placement and poor support received from the staff. Although caring for older people is complex, nursing students are motivated to learn and provide the necessary care, aligning with their interests. This research highlights the need for enhancing the provision of quality care for older people and the importance of integrating early geriatric education into nursing education programmes, as well as improving clinical supervision and mentoring to address these challenges. Integrating geriatric nursing from the first year of the curriculum, introducing a comprehensive geriatric nursing module and planning clinical experiences with supervision and mentoring are recommended.
