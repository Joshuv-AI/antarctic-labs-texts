Contrastive language image pretraining for a cardiac magnetic resonance image embedding with zero-shot capabilities
Authors: Makiya Nakashima, Jielin Qiu, Peide Huang, Jihye Lee, Po-hao Chen, Richard Grimm, Christopher Nguyen, Byung-Hak Kim, Ding Zhao, Deborah Kwon, David Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVision-language models trained using self-supervised learning are crucial to reduce the dependency on large volumes of labeled data. However, conventional self-supervised approaches that rely on precise image-text pairing are not always feasible for cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) given its ability to visualize cardiac anatomy, physiology, and microstructure in a single exam. We present CMR-contrastive language image pretraining (CMR-CLIP), a vision language model which treats CMR images as videos to jointly learn embeddings between the images in the study and associated reports. The model is trained on a large dataset consisting of 11,028 studies performed at a single healthcare institution and evaluated on an internal test (N = 2,758) and external dataset (N = 428). CMR-CLIP achieves remarkable performance in real-world clinical tasks, achieving accuracies of 88.5% for non-ischemic cardiomyopathy, 88.0% for ischemic cardiomyopathy, 96.2% for cardiac amyloidosis, and 98.6% for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, potentially leading to more consistent diagnosis of cardiovascular disease.Subject terms: Magnetic resonance imaging, Translational research

Introduction

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) is a powerful but complex advanced cardiac imaging modality. CMR is able to visualize cardiac morphology, function, viability, tissue characteristics, and flow within a single exam. As such, CMR serves as the definitive diagnostic modality for several cardiac diseases including valvular pathologies, cardiomyopathies, pericardial disease, and aortic diseases1,2. However, interpretation and documentation time for each exam often takes greater than 40 min per study3 due to the large amount of information collected during a CMR exam. The complexity of interpretation also demands up to 2 years of additional training. There would be significant value in developing AI tools for CMR to ease interpretative burden.

Current work towards AI clinical decision support for CMR has been focused on narrow, task-specific models that rely on carefully curated datasets4,5. Such focal developments results in bottlenecks for development and deployment of AI applications6, particularly given the breadth of potential precision medicine use-cases7. Unfortunately, developing AI tools for CMR is difficult compared to other cardiac imaging modalities due to the order of magnitude lower volume of studies and limited number of expert readers. Self-supervised learning has been shown to reduce the need for large labeled datasets by implicitly learning a wide range of capabilities without explicit training8–11. Text-guided pretrained vision models using image-caption pairs such as Contrastive Language Image Pretraining (CLIP) or A Large-scale ImaGe and Noisy-text embedding (ALIGN) in the natural image domain have created robust embeddings which led to further improvements in multi-modality downstream tasks such as image captioning and image retrieval12–15.

These vision-language frameworks have been applied to biomedical data including chest X-ray, single view echocardiography, and pathology slides16–18 to improve domain-specific performance beyond generalist models. However, many of these data types are limited in data variety. For example, chest X-rays are usually limited to a single frontal and lateral view during an exam. Similarly, the work by Christensen et al. in echocardiography was limited to only 4 chamber view. In contrast, typical CMR study is comprised of over 1000 2D images with both temporal and volumetric relationships. Findings in CMR impressions rarely refer to features contained in single images (e.g. viability is often correlated with wall motion abnormalities or myocardial morphology), making it difficult to map individual sentences and images. This limits the efficacy of pretraining using pairs of singular image and text, akin to poor motion understanding of many generalist foundation models. In this work, we introduce CMR contrastive language image pre-training (CMR-CLIP), a vision-language foundation model for diverse diagnostic tasks for CMR interpretation. Unlike both generalists and other biomedical domain-specific models, which are trained using singular images or limited views, CMR-CLIP incorporates a wide variety of standard cardiac views and image types representative of morphology, function, and myocardial viability. CMR-CLIP is trained using a large, single health system CMR dataset with over 11,000 CMR studies across multiple vendors. This model is based on Frozen-In-Time19 framework, a web video-based foundational model trained to align the embeddings between video and the associated captions (Fig. 1). Consequently, we treat the various views of the heart and image types acquired during a CMR study as a sequence of images in video format. The framework is designed to learn from the entire CMR study without the need for manual identification of specific images relevant to particular diseases. The capabilities of the model were evaluated on a wide array of tasks required for accurate CMR interpretation, including classification of various cardiomyopathies, prediction of cardiac ejection fraction, text-to-image retrieval, image-to-image retrieval, CMR image segmentation, and CMR report generation.

Model Architecture.

The visual encoder processes sequences of CMR images and the text encoder processes the text from the “impression” section of the corresponding reports.

We demonstrate that the learned model holds immense potential for a wide variety of clinical applications beyond generalist foundation models or single image-based foundation models, and on par with some specialist models. Specifically, CMR-CLIP exceeded other models in identifying common pathologies such as myocardial fibrosis and left ventricular hypertrophy. It also exceeded other models in common computational tasks including retrieval of CMR studies or radiology reports and downstream disease classification tasks.

Results

CMR-CLIP is a vision embedding developed in self-supervised manner using 14,214 paired CMR studies and reports from 12,500 patients collected between 2008 and 2023 (Fig. 2). The cohort consists of 6305 females and 7909 males, and an age range is 54.1 ± 16.6. Additional details of general indications for CMRs, findings in the studies, and scanners used for imaging are included in Table 1. In total, the dataset included over 260,000 Cine videos of standard cardiac views including 4 chamber long axis (LAX), 2 chamber long axis (2ch), short axis (SAX), and 3 chamber long axis (3ch), and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) images. Clips of each individual cardiac view and image type from a single study were undersampled and concatenated together. 95% of CMR studies contained between 400–500 Cine and LGE images. For the paired reports, 95% of the impressions ranged from 30 to 150 words.

To evaluate the efficacy of the underlying representation of CMR-CLIP, we evaluate the model on common clinical tasks for CMR including disease classification, cardiac function analysis, and tissue characterization in the context of various data scaling levels. We do these analyses in various common foundation model settings; transfer learning setting, zero-shot setting, and few-shot setting. We further evaluate the capabilities of the model for cross-modality study retrieval to evaluate the alignment between CMR image and text. Finally, we compared the model’s diagnostic predictions against expert human readers and evaluated its ability to generate structured clinical reports.

CMR Interpretation without supervision

We first evaluated CMR-CLIP’s ability to perform zero-shot image classification in a wide variety of tasks important to CMR interpretation (Fig. 3a, b), starting with cardiac function and tissue characterization. For prompting, we used task-specific keywords derived from common clinical descriptors; for example, for the left ventricle (LV) dilation task, we used “left ventricle is dilated” and “left ventricle is enlarged in size.” The model achieved an average area under the curve (AUC) of 0.764 for the 7 common CMR findings, with each being AUC of 0.794 for overall LV dysfunction including wall motion abnormality and diastolic dysfunction, 0.734 for right ventricle (RV) dysfunction, 0.806 for LV dilation, 0.775 for RV dilation, 0.849 for LV hypertrophy, 0.680 for LGE and 0.709 for aortic dilation. The model also identified diseases commonly diagnosed with CMR with an average AUC of 0.794. Specifically, the model achieved AUCs of 0.896, 0.867, 0.682, and 0.732 for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), cardiac amyloidosis (CA), non-ischemic cardiomyopathy (NICM), and ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM), respectively.

On average, for the CMR findings task, CMR-CLIP outperformed OpenAI CLIP by 45.5% in the zero-shot setting. This large general domain model also takes similar sized video clips as CMR-CLIP, but failed to generalize to CMR specific tasks, with an overall AUC of only 0.525. CMR-CLIP outperformed the domain-specific static image based BiomedCLIP by 49.8%. These results demonstrate that although generalist models are trained with significantly more data, the information in CMR is significantly different from both natural images and more general biomedical data. On the internal test set, CMR-CLIP achieved an average of AUC 0.764 and VideoMAE-CLIP achieved an average of AUC 0.768, a difference of 0.6%. Few-shot classification

We further evaluate CMR-CLIP in prototypical learning few-shot classification, which demonstrates the efficacy of the underlying data representation for classification tasks with extremely low volumes of data (Fig. 3c, d). Prototypical learning attempts to identify a centroid using the K examples of each class within the hyperspace defined by the pre-trained representation of the underlying data (CMR-CLIP). New instances of data are then classified into each class based on the distance from the centroid. We trained the model using N = {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32} examples per class with support sets randomly selected from the training data. Each experiment was repeated 100 times for every value of N, and we report the average results.

As expected, AUCs rose and the variance in performance falls with increasing number of examples for each class. For instance, in the case of LV dysfunction, the AUC improved from 0.714 with 1-shot to 0.934 with 32-shot classifiers. Similar trends were observed for LV dilation and LV hypertrophy, where the AUCs rose from 0.698 and 0.720 at 1-shot to 0.911 and 0.921 at 32-shot, respectively. The variance was particularly high with 1- and 2-shot classifiers, which is unsurprising given that facets unrelated to disease can dominate the prototypes.

Similar to the zero-shot setting, CMR-CLIP outperformed other baseline foundation models significantly, beating OpenAI CLIP and BiomedCLIP by 35.7%, 32.6% respectively. Of note, CMR-CLIP achieves similar results using a single instance of data compared to other models 32 shot (Supplementry Tables 5, 6). Overall, these results demonstrate that CMR-CLIP requires less data to achieve the same level of classification performance compared to other foundation models. CMR-CLIP achieves 0.761 on internal data compared to 0.752 for VideoMAE-CLIP, and 0.691 versus 0.644 on external data.

Cross-modal study retrieval

One of the primary methods to evaluate the quality of data alignment is the retrieval task where the model is asked to rank the similarity of data against a query. Doing well in this task often means that the model can implicitly encode discriminative dimensions of the data. CMR-CLIP demonstrates remarkable retrieval performance across all diseases as shown in Fig. 2d, f. CMR-CLIP identified the paired modality within the top 5 ranked cases in 20.7% for text-to-video and 20.2% for video-to-text retrieval. By comparison, VideoMAE-CLIP achieves 11.7% on both tasks. OpenAI CLIP and BiomedCLIP models failed to show any ability in study retrieval, not exceeding random retrieval in any of the settings (Supplementry Table 7).

All model’s retrieval performance were further assessed using a text-to-video retrieval task on an external dataset comprising 428 studies sourced from a satellite hospital. Our model demonstrated a text-to-video retrieval score of 63.4 in the internal test sets and 56.1 in the external test sets @50 (Supplementry Table 8). The model performed better on the internal dataset, likely due to variations in scanner types and impression sentence styles within the external dataset.

Transferability to image classification

In order to assess the applicability of our trained model to downstream image classification tasks, we freeze the visual embeddings learned during pretraining and train a fully connected networks using the frozen embeddings. We treated each diagnosis as a separate binary classification task. The training and testing sets for our labeled Cardiomyopathies dataset were split into 70% and 30%, respectively. In our Cardiomyopathies dataset, the number of positive and negative samples were 1049 and 890 for NICM, 461 and 1478 for ICM, 161 and 1778 for CA, and 268 and 1671 for HCM. The results of the image classification are presented in Fig. 2g, h, with detailed results presented in Supplementry Table 9. CMR-CLIP achieves substantially higher AUCs compared to other embedding models, scoring 13% better than VideoMAE-CLIP embeddings. The models pretrained on general domain (OpenAI CLIP) did not generalize to CMR without fine-tuning on the CMR data, achieving an average AUC of only 0.707, compared to 0.961 trained using CMR-CLIP’s vision embeddings.

A fully-connected classifier trained using CMR-CLIP achieves an accuracy of 88.5% on NICM, 88.0% ICM, 96.2% on CA, and 98.6% on HCM (Supplementry Table 9). Calibration curves and Brier score for these experiments are also contained in the Supplementry Fig. 1.

The results outperform larger models trained on natural domain data by a substantial amount. This comparison highlights the need for domain-specific tuning to biomedical imaging data. We further assess the generalizability of our model using the public Automated Cardiac Disease Classification (ACDC) dataset, which only includes Cine SAX data. The binary-class classification results are presented in Fig. 2h. The model achieved macro AUC values of 0.934 for dilated cardiomyopathy, 0.997 for HCM, 0.985 for myocardial infarction, 0.987 for normal subjects, and 0.995 for abnormal RV.

We further compared the performance of the classification models in a cohort with final diagnosis in addition to the reader interpretation. Also included in the labels is reader diagnostic confidence. This cohort included CA, HCM, and other patients referred for evaluation for infiltrative cardiomyopathies. In this dataset, the readers correctly diagnosed amyloidosis in 62.9% of cases when they were strongly confident. When aggregating all mentions of amyloidosis, readers were found to be accurate in 77.3% of cases. CMR-CLIP reached an accuracy of 82.5% in this dataset. For HCM, both human readers and CMR-CLIP achieved an accuracy exceeded 89%. Finally, readers achieved only 53.1% for other class when confident and 70.3% when aggregating all mentions, compared to 87.5% achieved by CMR-CLIP (Fig. 3a). Proportion of correct diagnoses stratified by reported confidence level are detailed in Supplementry Table 104.

Performance on generative tasks

To evaluate CMR-CLIP’s utility beyond classification and retrieval, we implemented two approaches for automated report generation (Fig. 4). First, we designed a retrieval-based generation that leverages the learned embedding space to select the most relevant impression from the training set20,21. The images of each CMR study is encoded into embeddings by the frozen CMR-CLIP vision encoder as a key for the previously written report. When querying new studies, we compute the cosine similarity between the new study with the stored ones during training. The impression associated with the highest-scoring embedding is retrieved. Next, we also trained a Transformer-based AutoRegressive Generator for Text (CMR-TARGET) for end-to-end synthetic report generation (Fig. 3b). Comparison with individual image type embeddings

The performance of single image types, namely Cine, and LGE individually, was observed to be lower in comparison to that of multiple image types/views. Cine offers a dynamic view of the heart’s motion over time, while LGE captures the distribution of fibrosis in the heart. When both image types are included in the radiology report, it enhances the semantic alignment between image sequences and text reports by at least 16% improvement in retrieval metrics (Fig. 5a). The inclusion of 2ch and 3ch images provides more information about areas surrounding the aortic and mitral valves, respectively, in addition to the information provided by LAX and SAX views. This inclusion of left heart and valvular views enables better differentiation of certain diseases typically diagnosed using CMR, such as hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy.

Model optimization and stability

Miech et al.22 found that the number of input frames is crucial for the performance of retrieval systems. Our study’s findings indicate that increasing the number of input CMR images can enhance both retrieval and classification performance. Clinically, having more frames improves the ability to capture minute adverse changes to cardiac function during different points in the cardiac cycle23. Our study revealed that the 64 frame Cine and LGE configurations outperformed the 1, 4, 8, 16, and 32 frame settings (Fig. 5b). However, larger input sizes require more computational.

We further evaluate model stability by comparing embedding variability in response to different perturbations in the input data compared to average cosine distances between studies. This is intended to demonstrate that the developed models capture features important to disease detection but not sensitive to subtle changes in the input data. We focus on two kinds of input perturbations: changes in the included frames (linear shift and random swapping), and missing data (masking and random sub-sampling). The results of CMR-CLIP stability compared with other models are shown in Fig. 5c, where CMR-CLIP demonstrates the smallest change in perturbed embedding compared to other models. This is particularly noticeable compared to public foundation models where perturbations in the input data can exceed potential differences between studies. Changes in the included data such as linear shifts or random swapping has a lower impact on embedding compared to missing data. VideoMAE-CLIP exhibited significant sensitivity to both shifts which is substantially higher than that of other foundation models. In contrast CMR-CLIP greatly reduces the embedding model’s sensitivity to both shifts in the included frames and missingness of data.

Zero-shot classification performance across vendors and field strengths

CMR-CLIP demonstrated robust zero-shot generalizability across scanner vendors and field strengths (Supplementry Tables 13–16). On internal Philips data (N = 2726), AUCs of the findings ranged from 0.681-0.850, while a small internal Siemens subset (N = 32) yielded similar AUC (0.617–0.926) with greater variance. In an external Siemens cohort (N = 428), AUC (0.594–0.836) remained consistent, indicating resilience to domain shift. Stratification by field strength on the internal cohort showed comparable performance at 1.5 T (N = 1844; AUC 0.694–0.851) and 3.0 T (N = 914; AUC 0.652–0.854), and on the external cohort at 1.5 T (N = 421; AUC 0.605–0.830), with a very small external 3.0 T subgroup (N = 7) showing wider metric variability.

Discussion

In this work, we proposed a vision-language self-supervised learning framework for CMR trained using over a million individual images from over 10,000 unique studies. CMR-CLIP has longer spatial-temporal context window compared to other biomedical foundation models. Our results demonstrate CMR-CLIP achieves better performance in downstream applications compared to both more general biomedical foundation models, a large natural domain foundation model, and an alternative video-based foundation model. The learned representations exhibit potential utility in diverse clinical contexts, ranging from the creation of robust retrieval systems to the advancement of disease classification. Our work lays the foundation for future investigations exploring the integration of multimodal learning approaches in medical imaging, which may lead to more accurate diagnoses and improved patient outcomes.

CMR is an information dense imaging modality due to its ability to capture cardiac anatomy, function, and tissue characteristics. The average interpretation and documentation time for a CMR study can exceed 40 min3, compared to echocardiology and cardiac computed tomography. AI decision support tools to aid in CMR interpretation is particularly important to increasing access given the lengthy interpretation process3 and the lack of readers24. Current commercial tools to aid in interpretation are focused on automating biomarker extraction. Such tools, in combination with intelligently engineered data pipelines can drastically reduce CMR documentation times3. However, standard biomarkers are not adequate to accurately diagnose many cardiac diseases, particularly in the long tail of rare diseases25. These diseases (e.g. CA or Fabry disease) are frequently mis-diagnosed because subtle image features are not immediately apparent and not well differentiated in standard imaging biomarkers. Unfortunately, developing decision aids for such diseases is one of the more difficult tasks in healthcare because the total volume of CMR is 1/100th of that of other cardiac imaging modalities26,27, due in part to the logistics of operating a scan. This means access to CMR is primarily and largely limited to specialized cardiac care centers27. A foundation model developed specifically for CMR can have outsized influence in enabling AI development for such diseases by vastly lowering the volume of data necessary, instead relying on the emergent abilities encoded in the model during self-supervised training as shown by this work. Our broad clinical decision support tool also has the potential to improve completeness of reports by prepopulating structured or semi-structured reports. The generative aspect can also be used to draft impressions or possibly even full reports, reducing overall documentation time. Conventional image text learning provides cross supervision between image and textual domains. The core task which these learning paradigms perform is alignment between image and text embeddings. Zhang et al. proposed ConVIRT28, a contrastive image and text self-supervised learning framework similar to SimCLR29 for chest X-rays. Likewise, ConVIRT uses InfoNCE30 loss across image and text pairs. This multi-modality training exceeded fully supervised, end-to-end training using only 1% of the training data. Similarly, GLoRIA31, proposed by Huang et al. introduces a cross-attention layer in order to learn localized similarities both image and word subdomains. They report slightly improved results compared to ConVIRT, which may suggest the important of local representations in additional to the global representations trained using typical contrastive frameworks. However, both of these methods are dependent on pairs of single images and text rather than video clips.

The natural domain parallel to CMR-text pretraining is video-language pretraining, where most clips are not semantically well aligned with their corresponding text22,32. For example, “the basketball player makes a game winning shot” may have several seconds of additional gameplay and celebration for context. Miech et al. addressed this by minimizing video clip length while extending text to maximize the chance for alignment22. Similarly, Xu et al.33 varied the lengths of the video clips and enforcing overlapping clips to drive increased similarity between closely related clips. Such strategies can work for instructional videos or video captioning where there is still a strong assumption of alignment between the action and the text. However, this assumption cannot be applied to CMR reports where informative frames can come from disparate frames in the study.

Alternatively, Bush et al.34 and Bain et al.19 identified that single frames within videos contribute vast amounts of information. Thereby, they propose methods to explicitly identify single frames which correspond with the text. Specifically, Bush leverages an image-based embedding with self-attention mechanism to identify the most informative frame for any specific piece of text. Bain’s proposed solution is a space-time transformer-based encoder which can take both video and image jointly, robustly learning importance of both spatial features and temporal features. The benefit of such space-time strategies is that they minimize the need for well aligned video and text and also reduce the amount of data needed to train. However, these methods have not been applied to CMR where studies are a combination of 2D+time and 3D (and sometimes 4D) data.

Christensen et al. proposed EchoCLIP17, a vision-language foundation model for echocardiography that contains similar functional imaging to CMR. However, that work leveraged only a single image type (4 chamber). EchoPrime is a more recent update to the model which incorporates the large number of echocardiogram views35. That model achieved significantly better results in classification and retrieval performance compared to EchoCLIP. However, the work leveraged over 12 million studies which is orders of magnitude more data than possible for CMR given the large disparity in imaging volume. Zhang et al. proposed BiomedGPT36 which incorporates 3D biomedical imaging unlike prior biomedical foundation models. However, the model was trained using more general biomedical which contains very little CMR-like data.

There are significant avenues to explore with regard to CMR foundation models. First, CMR-CLIP contains only a fraction of the data used in a full protocol. Subsampling the data was a technological limitation. New methods in long-context learning and/or more efficient tokenization of both the reports and the images has the potential to yield better results. Incorporating other image types such as parametric mapping, perfusion imaging, or T2 weighted imaging would be needed for a comprehensive embedding of the study. The limited context window may have contributed to some of the lower accuracy such as aortic dilation, where CMR-CLIP does not include the necessary views to consistently and accurately identify this pathology. Second, The ACDC dataset used for public reproducibility is also limited both in volume and breadth of data, containing only SAX Cine images. This reflects a general trend in the lack of available public advanced cardiac imaging data. EchoNet-dynamic represents the largest publicly available dataset (10k echocardiograms), although it is similarly limited with respect to available view (only apical-4-chamber). Pace et al released a 3D CMR dataset for whole heart segmentation37 although it represents only patients with congenital heart disease and does not include standard 2D views and image types. Third, the LGE validation is based on documented patterns of LGE in the report. Such patterns are inconsistently documented38 and may not accurately reflect the pattern in the actual images.

In conclusion, our proposed foundation model for CMR, CMR-CLIP, was shown to have significant potential to aid in common cognitive tasks associated with CMR diagnostics. The potential applications of this framework are many-fold and far-reaching, including applications in content-based information retrieval, clinical decision support, and healthcare operations. We believe that it represents a significant step forward in the field of medical image analysis and clinical decision-making for CMR.

Methods

The Cleveland Clinic Institutional Review Board reviewed and approved the current study and waived the need to obtain informed consent due to minimum risk to patients (IRB #22-1213). All identifying data was stripped from image data and associated reports. Code to train the model and weights of the model are publicly available. Cleveland Clinic images and reports are not available for public use.

Data and data preprocessing and evaluation

Our dataset contains CMR images and cardiologists’ text reports of patients who underwent a CMR exam at a Cleveland Clinic Ohio (OH) site in both inpatient and outpatient settings between 2008 and 2022. Race and sex are patient self reported and extracted from the EHR. The total size of the initial cohort is 41,936 CMR studies, including a variety of indications according to standard clinical practices. CMR protocols are highly adaptable to both patient characteristics and the underlying pathology. For instance, patients with valvular disease may have additional studies that are sensitive to the flow of blood through the aorta. Alternatively, patients with ventricular dysfunction may have scans sensitive to myocardial tissue composition, such as fibrosis and edema. Therefore, similar to echocardiography, a single study is comprised of 50–100 short clips or images of various views and image types. We also included a separate cohort of CMR studies (N = 428) from adult patients in various Florida (FL) sites in our extended health system. All FL studies were done on separate scanners and interpreted by their own readers with no cross over. Characteristics of both OH and FL datasets are available in Table 1.

In this study, we focus on studies which target left ventricular diseases, which is one of the most common usages of CMR2. Studies were excluded if they failed to include all views (Fig. 2), Cine LAX and SAX or LGE LAX, SAX, 2ch, and 3ch. We also excluded any study in which the mid-slice Cine LAX and SAX series comprised fewer than 30 frames. Studies with LGE SAX stacks containing fewer than 10 slices or more than 15 slices were removed. We excluded studies which we were not able to retrieve a report for (e.g. report was saved in another system we could not access). In our image preprocessing pipeline, the mid-slice Cine LAX and Cine SAX sequences were treated as temporal series, with each frame representing time. By contrast, in the LGE SAX sequence each frame corresponded to a depth. For LGE LAX, 2ch, and 3ch views, we replicated each view so that its total frame count matched that of the LGE SAX stack. Finally, we concatenated all frames into a single continuous sequence and applied uniform sampling to extract 64 frames, thereby producing a fixed-length input (Fig. 6).

An example of the input data, where the number in parentheses indicates how many frames each modality–view pairing contributes.

In the mid-slice Cine long axis (LAX) and short axis (SAX), frames represents time; in LGE SAX, each frame corresponds to the depth dimension; and in LGE LAX, SAX, 2 chamber (2ch) and 3 chamber (3ch), views are duplicated so that each contributes the same number of frames as the LGE SAX stack.

For our internal dataset, the vast majority of cases were collected by Philips 1.5 T Achieva scanner or 3.0 T Ingenia scanner, while the external dataset was collected mostly on a Siemens Espree 1.5 T scanner. The two image types included in this study are Cine, which are 2D clips which capture motion in a single slice of the heart, and LGE, which are static 2D image which captures tissue viability. Multiple images of a single type are acquired at different slice locations to capture the whole volume of the heart. We included four standard CMR views, including (1) LAX, (2) SAX, (3) 2ch, and (4) 3ch. All images were normalized to an in-plane resolution of 1.5 × 1.5 mm2. Given each study amounts to over 1000 still-frames, we undersampled the Cine frames to minimize the total size of the model.

We focus on the impression section, which summarizes each CMR study’s key findings, differential diagnosis, and provides recommendations for plan of care. We used a rules-based parser which identified subsection heading to extract the impression section from the radiology report. This has a secondary impact of de-identifying the text data given patient identifiers (name, dates, ordering, and interpreting physicians, etc.) are contained in sections outside of the impression. The impressions section was then cleaned to remove formatting such as bullet points, numbered lists, and communication boilerplates. We purposefully preserved synonyms and abbreviations to increase text variation and embedding generalizability. Disease and clinical findings mentions in the impressions were extracted using a finetuned Large Language model Meta Artificial intelligence (LLaMA)39.

The data was split into training and test sets based on patient to prevent data leakage (splits illustrated in Fig. 2). 95% confidence intervals of metrics were found by bootstrapping. We further leveraged previously acquired manually annotated data for testing purposes. The first such dataset is a cohort of patients with known cardiomyopathy diagnosis (CM dataset). The CM dataset includes 1939 studies acquired on adult patients between 2008 and 2023. All patients were found to have either hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), cardiac amyloidosis (CA), undifferentiated non-ischemic cardiomyopathy (NICM), or ischemic cardiomyopathy (ICM). Final diagnosis was determined through manual review of a patient’s medical records following relevant guidelines. HCM was identified based on the CMR imaging biomarkers of left ventricular wall thickness >15 mm, absence of abnormal loading conditions, and absence of infiltrative cardiomyopathies40. CA was determined by positive cardiac pyrophosphate scan (PYP) or biopsy confirmed amyloidosis. NICM defined as any case of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, no evidence of ischemic process, and no specific heart failure etiology41. Definitive diagnosis of ICM was determined by ejection fraction <40% and either positive LGE or confirmed occlusion of a coronary artery42. Of this cohort, 1357 studies were placed in the training data, while 582 were held out for testing. For human vs AI evaluation cohort, there are 807 studies, comprises of final diagnosis of CA, HCM, and other conditions4. This dataset was identified as cases referred for evaluation of infiltrative disease from the documented indication for the exam. A subset of studies (N = 501) overlapped with the CM dataset. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training (70%) and test (30%) subsets. For the test set (N = 264), clinical reader confidence were manually reviewed4. We did not evaluate model performance across race or sex due to limited size of the testing data.

In addition to the Cleveland Clinic-produced datasets, we also leverage the public ACDC dataset43. It comprises of 150 clinical CMRs acquired at the University Hospital of Dijon, France on either a 1.5 T Siemens Area and 3.0 T Siemens Trio scanner. The dataset includes only Cine SAX images. We used the pathology labels included within the dataset, which include myocardial infarction with systolic heart failure, dilated cardiomyopathy, HCM, abnormal RV, and normal. The inclusion criteria and definitions for disease in this dataset are significantly different than the diagnostic guidelines for Cleveland Clinic cases, most importantly all cases in the ACDC are unequivocal diagnoses. Classes are evenly distributed (30 in each class), Due to the exclusion of certain cases that did not meet the inclusion criteria, the final class distribution was as follows: dilated cardiomyopathy (N = 29), HCM (N = 29), myocardial infarction (N = 27), normal (N = 25), and abnormal RV (N = 22). The dataset was split into training (70%) and testing (30%).

Zero-shot and few-shot experiments were conducted and evaluated using the labels extracted from impressions. The CM-dataset and ACDC dataset with disease specific labels were used for evaluating CMR-CLIP’s transfer capabilities. These labels were used to validate the model in downstream zero-shot and few-shot testing.

Model architecture and training

The detail of model parameters is shown in Supplementry Table 17. Our model was trained on 4x NVIDIA A100 GPUs. We adopt the approach introduced in Zhai et al. for image-text contrastive loss44. This loss assumes pairs of text and video data points in a batch are considered positive matches, while all other pairwise combinations in the batch are considered as negative samples. The model consists of separate video and text encoder. The video encoder is a modification of the Divided Space-Time attention model. As with most vision transformers (ViT), the video encoder is comprised of a stack of space-time transformer blocks. However, the residual connection between the block input and the temporal attention output was replaced with a residual connection between the block input and the spatial attention output. Each block applies temporal self-attention and then spatial self-attention sequentially to the output of the previous block. Finally, the video clip embedding is derived from the [CLS] token of the final block.

The text encoder component of our architecture is responsible for processing a sequence of tokenized words and producing a meaningful encoding that captures the semantic content of the input text. To achieve this, we employ a multi-layer bidirectional transformer encoder, which has demonstrated remarkable performance in a wide range of natural language processing tasks45,46. Specifically, we instantiate the text encoder using the Bio+ClinicalBERT model47, a domain-adapted BERT variant pre-trained on large-scale clinical notes (MIMIC-III). This enables the encoder to capture medical terminology and context without altering the underlying BERT architecture. At a high level, the text encoder processes the tokenized input sequence by iteratively transforming the embeddings of each token based on its context within the sentence, using a series of self-attention and feed-forward layers. The self-attention mechanism enables the model to attend to different parts of the input sequence when processing each token, while the feed-forward layers allow for nonlinear transformations of the learned representations. Moreover, the bidirectional nature of the encoder allows the model to incorporate information from both past and future tokens in the sequence, resulting in a more comprehensive encoding of the input text. To obtain the final text encoding, we apply mean-pooling over the final hidden states of all tokens using the attention mask to ignore padding to produce a single vector representation of the input sequence.

The video-to-text loss, Lv2t, computes the negative log probability of each video embedding matching its corresponding text embedding, relative to all other text embeddings in the batch, whereas the text-to-video loss, Lt2v, computes the negative log probability of each text embedding matching its corresponding video embedding, relative to all other video embeddings in the batch. By minimizing these losses, the model learns to generate embeddings that maximize the similarity between the corresponding video-text pairs and minimize the similarity between non-corresponding pairs. This approach allows the model to capture both the short-term and long-term temporal dynamics of the video and can effectively encode the information needed for downstream tasks such as video classification and retrieval. By sampling frames at different intervals during training and testing, our model can learn to recognize temporal patterns at different scales, resulting in robust and accurate representations of the video content. To generate patch embeddings, the patch embedding layer uses a 2D convolutional layer with a kernel and stride size equal to the target patch size P=16, with d=768 output channels (the chosen embedding dimension for the video encoder). The positional space and time embeddings have shapes M×d and N×d, respectively, where M is the maximum number of input video frames, and N is the maximum number of non-overlapping patches of size P within a frame (196 for a video resolution of 224×224. The [CLS] embedding has shape 1×d. Each space-time attention block includes norm layers, temporal and spatial self-attention layers, and a MLP layer, following the approach described in Bain et al. 19. To process spatio-temporal patches, the video clip input is divided into non-overlapping patches of size P×P, following the protocol in ViT and Timesformer48. The resulting patches x∈RM×N×3×P×P, where N=HW/P2, are passed through a 2D convolutional layer, and the output is flattened, generating a sequence of embeddings z∈RMN×D that is fed into the transformer. The size of the embeddings, D, depends on the number of kernels in the convolutional layer. This approach enables the model to identify the temporal and spatial position of each patch. Additionally, a learned [CLS] token is concatenated at the beginning of the sequence to produce the final visual embedding output of the transformer.

In order to establish a meaningful association between textual and visual information, it is imperative to first ensure that they are represented in a common feature space. To achieve this, both the textual and visual encodings are projected onto a shared dimension using separate linear layers. Subsequently, the similarity between these projected embeddings is computed by taking their dot product. This approach effectively enables the alignment of heterogeneous modalities, namely textual and visual, and facilitates their comparison in a manner that can be meaningfully interpreted by downstream tasks.

To enable a direct comparison with other video-based representation learners, we implemented the VideoMAE and CLIP pipeline on our CMR dataset following recent studies49–52 and refer to the result as VideoMAE-CLIP. VideoMAE-CLIP consists of two stages. We first pretrain a masked autoencoder on all 64 frames of MRI sequence, following the VideoMAE protocol49. In each training iteration, 90% of the spatiotemporal patches (tubes) are randomly masked, and the model learns to reconstruct them—forcing it to capture both cardiac motion and anatomical structure. Next, we take the pretrained video encoder and perform CLIP-style contrastive learning. As with our CMR-CLIP model, Bio+ClinicalBERT is used as the text encoder. We used the same training, validation, test set as in our CMR-CLIP experiments.
