Lurbinectedin sensitizes PD-L1 blockade therapy by activating STING-IFN signaling in small-cell lung cancer
Authors: Subhamoy Chakraborty, Utsav Sen, Kedwin Ventura, Vrinda Jethalia, Charles Coleman, Subhasree Sridhar, Avisek Banerjee, Hilal Ozakinci, Yazhini Mahendravarman, Konrad Snioch, Elisa de Stanchina, Misty D. Shields, Lewis E. Tomalin, Deniz Demircioglu, Theresa A. Boyle, Anna Tocheva, Dan Hasson, Triparna Sen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryLurbinectedin is an approved second-line treatment for small-cell lung cancer (SCLC). SCLC clinical trials combining lurbinectedin with PD-L1 blockade are currently ongoing. However, the immunomodulatory effects of lurbinectedin remain largely unknown. In this study, we demonstrate that lurbinectedin treatment activates the STING pathway, which increases interferon (IFN) signaling, pro-inflammatory chemokines, and major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) in SCLC models. Lurbinectedin treatment augments the anti-tumor immune response of PD-L1 blockade with significant tumor regression in first-line and maintenance settings in SCLC mouse models. In vivo, lurbinectedin treatment increases CD8+ T cells and M1 macrophages and decreases immunosuppressive M2 macrophages. STING and CD8 depletion reverses the anti-tumor response. Interestingly, our study shows that lurbinectedin treatment upregulates MHC-I/II genes and CD8 in SCLC clinical samples. We provide mechanistic insights into the effect of lurbinectedin on STING-mediated multimodal immune activation and demonstrate that lurbinectedin treatment represents a promising therapeutic strategy to potentiate the efficacy of immunotherapy in SCLC.

Introduction

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a high-grade neuroendocrine carcinoma, which accounts for approximately 10%–15% of all lung cancers.1 Approximately 70% of patients are metastatic at diagnosis (extensive stage) and have a median overall survival (OS) of less than 1 year.2 Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) plus chemotherapy, the standard-of-care frontline treatment of SCLC based on the results of the phase 3 ImPower1333 and CASPIAN4 trials, shows modest benefit in a minority of patients.

SCLC is characterized by an immunosuppressive phenotype with low levels of infiltrating T cells and reduced antigen presentation compared to non-SCLC.1 We and others have demonstrated that the suppression of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) represents a crucial mechanism of ICB resistance.1,5 However, most patients experience resistance to ICB through unknown mechanisms, and the limited benefits of ICB underscore the critical need to identify strategies that can enhance the anti-tumor response to ICB in SCLC.

Lurbinectedin, an RNA polymerase II (RNA Pol II) inhibitor, is approved as a second-line treatment for patients with SCLC.6,7 A phase 3 trial (IMforte-NCT05091567) is currently evaluating the effect of the anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) monoclonal antibody atezolizumab in combination with lurbinectedin compared with atezolizumab alone for first-line maintenance. We have previously shown that lurbinectedin significantly reduced tumor burden in SCLC patient-derived xenograft models and induced the expression of DNA damage response markers in SCLC models. However, the effect of lurbinectedin on immune subsets and the anti-tumor immune response when combined with an anti-PD-L1 antibody is still largely unknown.

In the current study, we investigated the effect of lurbinectedin treatment on immune subsets and whether lurbinectedin cooperates with anti-PD-L1 to induce a more meaningful durable anti-tumor efficacy in SCLC. We also investigated the immunomodulatory effect in lurbinectedin-treated SCLC patient samples. Our results indicated that multimodal immune activation was initiated by lurbinectedin treatment, which significantly augmented the anti-tumor immune response to PD-L1 blockade in SCLC.

Results

Lurbinectedin augments the anti-tumor immune response of PD-L1 blockade in SCLC mouse models

To study whether lurbinectedin potentiates the efficacy of PD-L1 ICB in SCLC, we used cells derived from immunocompetent mice with conditional loss of Rb1, Trp53, and Rbl2 (RPP) and Rb1, Trp53, and MYCT58A (RPM), broadly representing the ASCL1 and NEUROD1 SCLC subtypes, respectively.8,9 RPP or RPM subcutaneous tumor-bearing mice were randomized into four treatment arms: (1) vehicle, (2) anti-PD-L1 alone (300 μg/animal, intraperitoneal [i.p.], 1/7), (3) lurbinectedin alone (0.2 mg/kg, intravenous [i.v.], 1/7), and (4) combination of lurbinectedin and anti-PD-L1 (Figure 1A). In the RPP model, lurbinectedin treatment alone showed a significant anti-tumor effect, with a tumor volume of 452.2 mm3 on day 21 (66.9% reduction in tumor burden compared with the vehicle) (Figure 1B). Encouragingly, lurbinectedin in combination with anti-PD-L1 therapy reduced the tumor volume to an average of 83.5 mm3 on day 21 compared to the starting tumor volume of 127.83 mm3. Lurbinectedin in combination with anti-PD-L1 resulted in 93.9% regression in average tumor volume compared to the vehicle (Figure 1B). Most importantly, 11 out of 12 mice in the combination group showed a reduction in tumor volume compared to the baseline (day 0 tumor volume at the start of treatment), and 2 out of 12 mice showed complete tumor regression with no measurable tumor volume.

In RPM, lurbinectedin treatment alone showed a significant reduction in tumor growth, reaching an average tumor volume of 397.2 mm3 compared to 1,424.8 mm3 in vehicle treated on day 18 (72.17% reduction in tumor volume) (Figure 1C). While the combination treatment showed remarkable tumor regression reaching a volume of 130.18 mm3 on day 18, the average baseline tumor volume at the start of treatment was 161.82 mm3 (Figure 1C) indicating 90.86% of tumor regression on day 18 post treatment. Notably, in the RPM models, 3 out of 11 mice showed complete tumor regression on day 18 post treatment.

Body weight measurements (Figures 1D and 1E) in both RPP and RPM genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) showed no appreciable loss of body weight in lurbinectedin or lurbinectedin + anti-PD-L1 treatment groups (Table S1). Similarly, liver function enzymes (alanine transaminase [ALT] and serum creatinine level [SCL], Figures 1F and 1G) did not display significant changes between the conditions, confirming the safety of the treatment regimens.

Therefore, single-agent lurbinectedin treatment caused significant tumor regression and substantially enhanced the anti-tumor effect of PD-L1 blockade in two aggressive SCLC immunocompetent mouse models, representing the majority of the SCLC patient population.

Lurbinectedin alone or in combination with PD-L1 blockade modulates immune cell subsets in SCLC

Next, we assessed the effect of lurbinectedin, either as a single agent or in combination with PD-L1 blockade, on immune subsets in resected tumors. Multicolor flow cytometry analysis of T cells and antigen-presenting cells (Figures S1A–S1C) demonstrated that lurbinectedin treatment, either as a single agent or in combination with PD-L1 blockade, led to a significant increase in CD3+ total T cells (Figures 2A and 2D) and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (Figures 2B and 2E). Furthermore, lurbinectedin treatment, with or without anti-PD-L1 significantly increased CD69+ cells in both models (Figures 2C and 2F). CD69 is an early-activation marker expressed on cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells.10 Interestingly, programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)+/TIM3+-exhausted T cells and CD25+/FOXP3+ regulatory T cells, which have established immunosuppressive roles, were significantly decreased in both RPP (Figures 2G and 2H) and RPM (Figures 2J and 2K) models in lurbinectedin and lurbinectedin + anti-PD-L1 treatment. Pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages play an anti-tumor role, whereas anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages have been shown to play immunosuppressive roles in lung cancer.11 Interestingly, lurbinectedin treatment with or without PD-L1 blockade significantly increased the percentage of M1 macrophages in the RPP model (Figure 2M). Combination treatment with lurbinectedin and anti-PD-L1 resulted in a significant enhancement of M1 macrophages in the RPM models (Figure 2P). In contrast, lurbinectedin treatment, with or without PD-L1 blockade, significantly decreased the percentage of M2 macrophages in the RPP model (Figure 2N). No significant changes were observed in M2 macrophages in the RPM model (Figure 2Q). Finally, we observed a significant increase in interferon gamma (IFNγ)-positive T cells in response to lurbinectedin with or without anti-PD-L1 treatment in both the GEMMs (Figures 2O and 2R).

Together, our data showed that lurbinectedin treatment, either as a single agent or in combination with an anti-PD-L1 antibody, significantly enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration, enhanced the level of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, and decreased T cell exhaustion and levels of M2 macrophages in two immunocompetent mouse models of SCLC.

CD8+ T cells are essential for the anti-tumor immune responses induced by lurbinectedin treatment with or without PD-L1 blockade in SCLC

To assess whether the anti-tumor effect is mediated by CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, tumor-bearing mice (RPP) were treated with anti-PD-L1 or lurbinectedin ± anti-PD-L1 antibody in the presence of either control IgG or anti-CD8 antibody to immune-deplete CD8+ T cells. As expected, we observed a significant anti-tumor effect in the lurbinectedin and combination treatment groups in the IgG control cohort (Figure 2S). In contrast, CD8+ depletion substantially reduced the anti-tumor response to lurbinectedin or the lurbinectedin and anti-PD-L1 antibody combination treatment (Figure 2S). To validate CD8+ depletion in the in vivo models, we performed flow cytometry analysis to confirm the successful depletion of the intratumoral CD8+ T cell population in the RPP GEMMs (Figure 2T).

Moreover, tumor reduction after post-lurbinectedin treatment in RPP or RPM tumors grown in immunocompromised (nude) mice was lower than that in RPP or RPM tumors grown in immunocompetent mice (Figures S1D and S1E). This further demonstrates the role of an intact immune system in the lurbinectedin-mediated anti-tumor response in SCLC.

Collectively, our data demonstrate that lurbinectedin enhances the tumor regression of PD-L1 blockade in multiple in vivo models, at least in part, via a CD8+ T cell-mediated anti-tumor immune response.

Lurbinectedin treatment promotes T cell-mediated recognition and cytolysis in SCLC

We next investigated the effects of lurbinectedin on the antigen-specific cytotoxic T cell response in SCLC models and the functional consequences on MHC-I expression. We assessed T cell-mediated tumor cell killing alone, in response to lurbinectedin and the combination with PD-L1 antibody atezolizumab in human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-A∗02:01-expressing H69 SCLC cells.

We treated the H69 cells with 10 nM lurbinectedin or vehicle for 24 h. The cells were then pulsed with HLA-A∗02:01-restricted NY-ESO1 peptide antigen for 2 h and co-cultured with commercially available CD8+ T cells recognizing the HLA-A2-NY-ESO1 peptide.12 We measured total cell viability after 20 h of co-culture by CellTiter-Glo to assess CD8 T cell killing. While we found no significant changes in cell viability in any of the vehicle-treated groups compared to unpulsed tumor cells (Figure 2U), we observed a significant decrease in cell viability of peptide-pulsed H69 cells pre-treated with lurbinectedin for 24 h in the presence or absence of atezolizumab (Figures 2U and 2V). Notably, this response was MHC-I restricted since the addition of blocking anti-MHC-I antibody fully abrogated the observed reduction in cell viability (Figures 2U and 2V).

Together, these results further support our hypothesis that lurbinectedin treatment augments CD8 T cell-mediated cytolysis of SCLC tumors by increasing MHC-I expression since MHC-I repression can partially reverse the response.

Lurbinectedin treatment activates the cGAS-STING pathway and induces pro-inflammatory IFNs and chemokines in SCLC and other lung neuroendocrine models

Previous reports by our group and others demonstrated that DNA damage can activate the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway.13,14,15,16,17 To test whether the STING pathway was activated in SCLC models after post-lurbinectedin treatment, we treated SCLC cell lines and tumors with lurbinectedin, 10 nM for 8 and 16 h, and assessed STING pathway activation using transcriptomic and proteomic assays. We found that treatment with lurbinectedin led to activation of the STING pathway 8 and 16 h post treatment, including pSTING_S366, pTBK1_S172, cGAS, and pIRF3_S396 in multiple human (DMS114, H446, H196, H69, and H526) and murine-derived SCLC cell lines (PB115) (Figures 3A, S2A, and S2B). IRF3 has been previously identified as a transcription factor for type I IFN genes. As expected, lurbinectedin treatment caused significant increases in the mRNA expression of type I IFNs IFNα and IFNβ and type II IFNγ in murine (KP11) and human-derived SCLC cell lines (H446, H526, and H69) (Figures 3B, S2C, and S2D). CXCL10 and CCL5 are key mediators of CD8+ T lymphocyte chemotaxis, and CXCL10 and CCL5 overexpression is associated with the presence of CD8+ T lymphocytes in multiple cancers.14 Lurbinectedin treatment significantly upregulated CXCL10 and CCL5 expression in multiple SCLC cell lines (Figures 3B and S2D). We performed bulk RNA sequencing in three representative cell lines H69, H82, and H526 subtypes, with or without lurbinectedin treatment (10 nM, 24 h). Lurbinectedin treatment significantly increased the expression of CCL5, IRF3, IRF1, and IRF7 (Figures 3C and S3A). To confirm the upregulation of type I IFNs and chemokines at the protein level, we performed ELISA in cell lines pre- and post-lurbinectedin treatment. ELISA (Figure S3B) further demonstrated an increase in the secreted protein levels of IFNα, IFNβ, CCL5, and CXCL10 8 or 16 h post treatment with 10 nM lurbinectedin.

DNA damage-induced cytosolic aberrant DNA then can be detected by cGAS leading to direct activation of the STING pathway. We treated three SCLC cell lines (H446, H1048, and DMS114) with 10 nM lurbinectedin for 8 h and stained the cells with DAPI (to detect micronuclei) and co-stained with cGAS. We observed a significant increase in micronuclei formation upon lurbinectedin treatment in all three models (Figures 3D–3F [left panel], Figure S3C). More interestingly, lurbinectedin treatment increased cGAS co-localization with micronuclei in all treated models (Figures 3D–3F [right panel], Figure S3C). Our data demonstrate that lurbinectedin treatment increases micronuclei formation and recruits cGAS to the micronuclei, which leads to STING activation in SCLC models.

Next, to determine the role of STING in the upregulation of type I and type II IFNs and chemokines, we inhibited STING with pharmacological inhibitor H151 in multiple SCLC cell lines. As expected, STING inhibition significantly abrogated the increase in the mRNA expression of type I/II IFNs and CCL5 after lurbinectedin treatment in SCLC models (H446, DMS114, and KP11) (Figures 3G–3L, S2C, and S4A), supporting the critical role of STING in lurbinectedin-mediated upregulation of type I IFNs and chemokine genes in SCLC.

To expand the scope of lurbinectedin treatment to other neuroendocrine lung cancer models, H720 (atypical neuroendocrine carcinoid) and H661 (LCNEC) cells were treated with lurbinectedin. We investigated the sensitivity of H661 and H720 cells to graded concentrations of lurbinectedin ranging from 0.1 to 100 nM. Cell viability analysis demonstrated IC50 of 64.61 and 20.61 nM for H661 and H720, respectively (Figure S4B). We further treated the cells with 10 nM lurbinectedin for 8 and 16 h to investigate the effect of lurbinectedin on the induction of IFNs and chemokines in the H661 and H720 cell lines. Real-time PCR analysis showed an increase in the mRNA expression of IFNα, IFNβ, IFNγ, and CCL5 in H661 at 16 h lurbinectedin treatment, whereas 8 h treatment increased the expression of type I/II IFNs, CCL5, and CXCL10 in H720 cells (Figures S4C–S4F).

Type II IFN is a crucial regulator of PD-L1 expression. As we observed an increase in mRNA expression of IFNγ in our models, we next investigated the effect on PD-L1 expression. Western blot analysis of H211, H196, and H82 SCLC cell lines indicated a modest upregulation of both glycosylated (∼50 KD or kilodalton) as well as un-glycosylated bands (∼30–40 KD) (Figure S4G). Furthermore, in three SCLC lines (H69, H446, and H526), lurbinectedin treatment for 8H or 16H also increased the secreted level of PD-L1 (Figure S4H). Therefore, as shown in our previous study,14 lurbinectedin-mediated IFNγ induction (Figure S2C) may potentially increase PD-L1 in SCLC. However, further investigation is warranted to identify the biological implications of lurbinectedin-mediated IFNγ induction in SCLC.

In summary, our results demonstrate that lurbinectedin treatment leads to micronuclei formation, which directly recruits cGAS and activates the STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway in SCLC models. Lurbinectedin-mediated STING activation leads to the upregulation of type I and II IFNs (IFNα, IFNβ, and IFNγ) and pro-inflammatory chemokine CCL5 in SCLC.

Lurbinectedin treatment activates antigen presentation and induces intrinsic immunogenicity in a STING-dependent manner in SCLC

Next, we conducted transcriptomic analysis to determine the extent to which other immune-related genes (including those involved in antigen processing and presentation) were reactivated by lurbinectedin treatment. Notably, analyses of differentially expressed genes highlighted those of the endogenous peptide antigen presentation pathway, including HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C, and B2M (Figures S5A–S5C), and genes belonging to the immunological cell death (ICD) pathway, including HMGB1, ANXA1, CALR (calreticulin),18 were among the top upregulated genes after lurbinectedin treatment (Figures S5D–S5F). We further extended our investigation on the effect of lurbinectedin on MHC-I and ICD genes in two other neuroendocrine cell lines (H661 and H720). Real-time PCR analysis indicated a significant upregulation of HLA-A, HLA-B, and B2M genes in neuroendocrine cell lines (H661 and H720) in response to 8 or 16 h lurbinectedin treatment (Figures S5G and S5H). Real-time PCR analysis with the ICD genes confirmed that lurbinectedin treatment significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of the major regulators of the ICD pathway, including HMBG1, CALR, and ANXA1 in neuroendocrine cell lines (H661 and H720) (Figures S5G and S5H). The roles of ICD and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) in SCLC have not been studied previously, and the role of lurbinectedin treatment in activating ICD and DAMPs has not been explored. Therefore, we confirmed that lurbinectedin mediates these two crucial immune regulatory pathways.

To investigate the effect of lurbinectedin on MHC-I, we performed MHC-I flow cytometry in RPP and RPM tumors treated with lurbinectedin alone or in combination with anti-PD-L1. Previous reports demonstrated that pro-inflammatory type I and II IFNs can activate HLA genes.19 Therefore, we next performed pharmacological inhibition of STING in three SCLC models, DMS114, H446, and H196, and examined the role of STING in the lurbinectedin-mediated upregulation of HLA. Real-time PCR analysis confirmed that H151-mediated STING inhibition abrogated lurbinectedin-mediated upregulation of HLA-A, HLA-B, and B2M mRNA expression in all SCLC models (Figures 4B–4D).

Next, to confirm the surface level expression of MHC-I, we performed flow cytometry in DMS114, H446, H196, and H1048 cell lines. In agreement with increased mRNA expression, all four cell lines showed increased surface expression of MHC-I in after 10 nM lurbinectedin treatment for 24 h (Figure 4E). To establish the role of STING on MHC-I upregulation, we next treated DMS114, H446, H196, and H211 cells with STING inhibitor H151. Lurbinectedin-mediated MHC-I upregulation was successfully abrogated in the presence of STING inhibitor H151 (Figure 4F). Taken together, our results show that lurbinectedin can upregulate MHC-I in SCLC and that lurbinectedin-mediated STING activation may play a critical role in upregulating MHC-I in SCLC preclinical models.

To validate whether the transcriptional increase in ICD genes was also reflected in protein expression levels, we performed western blot analysis of HMGB1 and calreticulin in the PB115, H69, H211, H196, H446, and H526 cell lines pre- and post-lurbinectedin treatment for 8 and 16 h. Lurbinectedin treatment caused appreciable upregulation of HMGB1 and calreticulin proteins in murine and human-derived SCLC cell lines 8 or 16 h after 10 nM lurbinectedin treatment (Figures 4G, S5I, and S2A). HMGB1 functions as a DAMP after its release from the cells. So, to confirm HMGB1 release, we performed ELISA of the cell-free media in H211 and DMS114 cells treated with 10 nM lurbinectedin for 8 h. Our analysis indicates a significant upregulation in the secreted levels of HMGB1 in H211 and DMS114 cells in response to lurbinectedin (Figure 4H).

STING is a significant activator of the ICD pathway in cancer.20 Thus, we next investigated the role of STING in the lurbinectedin-mediated upregulation of ICD genes. We treated H446 and H196 cells with 10 nM lurbinectedin in the presence or absence of STING inhibitor H151. Real-time PCR confirmed a significant increase in the mRNA expression levels of HMGB1, ANXA1, and CALR post lurbinectedin treatment (Figures 4I and 4J). However, pharmacological inhibition of STING with H151 successfully reversed the STING-mediated transcriptional upregulation of the ICD genes in both cell lines.

To further validate the role of STING on downstream responses, we performed small interfering (siRNA)-mediated knockdown of TMEM173 (STING) (Figure S5J). First, we assessed the mRNA expression of type I/II IFNs and chemokines in STING knockdown cells compared to cells transfected with scrambled siRNA (Figure S5K). As expected, STING knockdown significantly decreased the mRNA expression of IFNα, IFNγ, CCL5, and CXCL10 in H446 cells and IFNα, IFNβ, IFNγ, and CXCL10 in H526 (Figure S5K).

Next, we used H69 cell line model to study ICD gene expressions upon lurbinectedin treatment with or without STING knockdown. Real-time PCR analysis confirmed that lurbinectedin treatment increased the expression of ICD genes HMGB1, ANXA1, and CALR in non-targeting control cells (Figure S5L). However, STING knockdown significantly abrogated the lurbinectedin-mediated activation of ICD genes in all SCLC models.

Therefore, our data collectively demonstrated the previously unexplored role of lurbinectedin in SCLC and other lung neuroendocrine models. We demonstrated that lurbinectedin treatment increased the expression of major regulators of antigen presentation and the ICD pathway in multiple SCLC cell lines and other neuroendocrine cell lines.

Lurbinectedin with anti-PD-L1 antibody-induced anti-tumor effect in the maintenance setting in SCLC

No preclinical studies have assessed the anti-tumor effect of lurbinectedin with or without PD-L1 blockade in maintenance settings and its downstream effects on immune subsets. Therefore, we investigated the effects of lurbinectedin in combination with an anti-PD-L1 antibody after treatment with chemotherapy (cisplatin and etoposide) and an anti-PD-L1 antibody in an in vivo immunocompetent SCLC model. Mirroring clinical management, we treated B6129F1 mice bearing flank RPP tumors with anti-PD-L1 antibody (300 μg/animal, once a week), cisplatin, and etoposide (3 mg/kg, three times a week) for three cycles. Following randomization, the mice received anti-PD-L1 antibody, lurbinectedin (0.2 mg/kg, 1 out of 7 days), or lurbinectedin plus anti-PD-L1 antibody (Figure 6A). Consistent with the clinical observations, tumor growth resumed rapidly with anti-PD-L1 antibody maintenance. The anti-PD-L1 antibody reached a tumor volume of 1,357.5 mm3 on day 55, showing limited efficacy of only ICB as a second-line treatment (Figure 6B). Encouragingly, single-agent lurbinectedin treatment significantly delayed the tumor growth (tumor volume reached 789 mm3 on day 55, showing 41.9% reduced tumor burden compared to the same day in the anti-PD-L1-only treated group) (Figure 6B). Most importantly, lurbinectedin treatment and PD-L1 blockade caused remarkable regression in tumor volume. The combination treatment reduced the tumor volume to an average of 356.9 mm3 on day 55 compared to anti-PD-L1 therapy, showing a 73.7% regression in tumor growth compared to the anti-PD-L1-treated group (Figure 6B). Mice treated with lurbinectedin, with or without anti-PD-L1 antibody, survived significantly longer than those treated with anti-PD-L1 antibody alone (Figure 6C). Discussion

SCLC continues to be one of the deadliest malignancies, and despite the introduction of ICBs, their therapeutic benefits remain limited. Here, we show that lurbinectedin launched STING pathway-mediated multimodal immune activation and, when combined with PD-L1 ICB, led to significantly higher anti-tumor efficacy in two immunocompetent SCLC models. We further showed that a combination of lurbinectedin treatment and PD-L1 blockade-induced CD8+ T cell infiltration decreased T cell exhaustion and modulated tumor-associated macrophages, resulting in durable anti-tumor efficacy in first-line and maintenance settings in SCLC. Finally, we provide evidence of lurbinectedin-mediated immune activation in SCLC clinical samples. Therefore, we demonstrate that the combination of lurbinectedin and PD-L1 blockade is clinically valuable in overcoming primary and adaptive resistance to ICB in SCLC.

Given the lack of an effective second-line therapy for extensive-stage SCLC,22 we investigated the efficacy of lurbinectedin with or without PD-L1 blockade in the maintenance setting. Lurbinectedin, as a single agent and when combined with PD-L1 blockade, has significantly superior anti-tumor efficacy compared to anti-PD-L1 alone. The anti-tumor effects were long term and durable, thus establishing the potential benefit of adding lurbinectedin with anti-PD-L1 in the maintenance setting or beyond for patients with SCLC. Lurbinectedin was dosed continuously in our study in both the first-line and maintenance settings and was well tolerated alone or when combined with anti-PD-L1, suggesting that combination approaches with lurbinectedin, even after prior treatment with chemoimmunotherapy, are feasible with ICB with non-overlapping toxicity. Loss or suppression of immune-active genes such as MHCs and a low number of infiltrating lymphocytes are thought to be associated with immunosuppression in SCLC.23,24 We demonstrated that the combination of lurbinectedin and an anti-PD-L1 antibody induced the recruitment of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, increased the number of M1 macrophages, and reduced T cell exhaustion in both mouse models. We established that the anti-tumor effect was mediated at least partly through the recruitment of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells.

Mechanistically, lurbinectedin treatment led to intratumoral activation of the innate immune cGAS-STING pathway. We have previously shown that the DNA damage response exacerbates cGAS-STING-mediated transcriptional upregulation of type I and II IFNs and the recruitment of pro-inflammatory chemokines in SCLC models, leading to increased recruitment of cytotoxic T cells.13,14,25 A recent report26 showed an increased association between cytosolic DNA-sensing pathways and immune scores, and with immune infiltration. Similarly, we demonstrate that lurbinectedin leads to micronuclei formation, recruitment of cGAS to the micronuclei, and activation of cytosolic DNA-sensing pathways in SCLC. Our data demonstrate that double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) derived from lurbinectedin treatment co-localizes with cGAS and may activate the STING pathway in SCLC. Phosphorylation of cGAS-STING leads to phosphorylation of IRF3, which translocates to the nucleus and transcriptionally upregulates the expression of type I and II IFNs.27 Previous reports on BRCA-deficient cancer showed the activation of the cGAS-STING innate immune pathway in CD8+ T cell recruitment in response to RNA Pol inhibition.28 Depletion of STING could abrogate the anti-tumor response in both mouse models and increase type I/II IFNs and the induction of pro-inflammatory chemokines mediated by lurbinectedin treatment. This establishes that the activation of the cGAS/STING pathway is crucial for the pro-inflammatory response mediated by lurbinectedin treatment.

We have previously shown that targeting other DDR proteins13,14,25 can also activate the cGAS/STING pathway, which is needed for the anti-tumor immune responses in SCLC. However, there are several upstream regulators and downstream effectors of the cGAS/STING pathway. For example, ecto-nucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 1 (ENPP1) is a crucial regulator of the cGAS-STING pathway. ENPP1 recognizes and degrades cGAMP, thereby suppressing the innate immune response.29 Different metabolites have also been suggested to regulate this innate immune response pathway. Guanosine diphosphate-mannose in combination with DNA-damaging agents can activate STING-dependent anti-tumor immunity.30 MRE11-RAD50-NBN complex binds to nucleosome fragments displacing cGAS from acidic-patch-mediated sequestration. This binding enables mobilization and activation of cGAS by dsDNA.31 A recent study also demonstrates that naked DNA in cytoplasm generated from genotoxic stress can activate cGAS.32 STING agonists are currently under clinical investigation in combination with chemotherapy or immunotherapy33 (NCT01057342) in SCLC, raising the question of whether the anti-tumor efficacy of lurbinectedin can be augmented by STING agonists, which could potentially be investigated in subsequent studies.

Presentation of peptides on MHC-I molecules to CD8+ T cells is a key mechanism of immune activation. Antigen presentation by MHC-I is an essential mechanism through which T cell-based immunotherapies can achieve efficacy and durability. In this study, we showed that lurbinectedin treatment significantly restored MHC-I cell surface expression, transcriptionally activated the antigen presentation pathway, and amplified tumoral immunogenicity. Upregulation of IFNs enhances the expression of HLA genes.34 We show that depletion of STING abrogated the lurbinectedin-mediated upregulation of HLA A/B/C and B2M mRNA expression and MHC-I surface expression. Our findings therefore suggest that STING activation might be a crucial component of lurbinectedin-mediated upregulation of MHC-I molecules. Therefore, lurbinectedin treatment is a viable approach for restoring MHC-I expression, attenuating immune suppression, and enhancing the sensitivity of SCLC to standard-of-care immunotherapy. Further, we demonstrated that lurbinectedin treatment leads to the transcriptional upregulation of ICD markers, such as HMGB1, calreticulin, and ANXA1. DAMPs associated with ICD, including HMGB1, calreticulin, ANXA1, type I IFNs, and ATP, are recognized by pattern recognition receptors and enhance the function of antigen-presenting cells, activate T cells, and increase the immunogenicity of tumor cells.35 Activation of the STING pathway induces the expression of several DAMPs.20 Similar to HLA-I expression, STING depletion abolished the lurbinectedin-mediated DAMP upregulation. Our data demonstrate the effects of lurbinectedin on ICD and the immune microenvironment.

In this study, we demonstrate the effect of lurbinectedin on cell-intrinsic and immune-related pathways. Lurbinectedin treatment increased the expression of several DNA damage response genes (including ATM, ATR, and RAD51 genes) in SCLC clinical samples. Interestingly, lurbinectedin treatment increased B cell, NK cell, and other antigen presentation markers in SCLC. Multiplex immunofluorescence showed an increase in CD8+ and CD4+ T cells post lurbinectedin treatment in SCLC patient samples. Interestingly, we observed an increase in co-stimulatory 4-1BB+ cells in primary tumor and lymph node metastasis post lurbinectedin treatment. 4-1BB activates a co-stimulatory response promoting CD8+ T cell expansion and survival. Furthermore, 4-1BB has been previously reported to be downregulated in SCLC contributing to its immune suppressive phenotype.21 Activation of MHC-I along with an increase in co-stimulatory signal transducer (4-1BB) provides strong evidence of T cell activation by lurbinectedin treatment in SCLC clinical samples. Despite several limitations, these results suggest that lurbinectedin can enhance anti-tumor immunity and may increase the opportunity to achieve durable responses to anti-PD-L1 in patients with SCLC. Hence, we believe that lurbinectedin-induced anti-tumor immune response in preclinical models will be translated in SCLC clinical trials. Encouragingly, a recent press release of the phase 3 IMforte trial (IMforte-NCT05091567) shows a statistically significant OS and progression-free survival benefit with lurbinectedin + PD-L1 antibody (atezolizumab) as a first-line maintenance therapy for extensive-stage SCLC treated with first-line chemoimmunotherapy.36 This is in agreement with our preclinical data that show the benefit of adding lurbinectedin + anti-PD-L1 antibody as a maintenance regimen in SCLC.

However, several inhibitors that have shown promise in preclinical studies have shown almost no benefit in SCLC clinical trials. The discrepancy of preclinical studies to human clinical trials may be due to the inherent biological differences between mouse and human tumors and technical differences in complex human clinical trial designs. The key hurdles of clinical translation of drugs from preclinical studies include1 biological differences,2 molecular differences altering drug effects,3 drug sequencing,4 lack of the genetic, transcriptomic, and epigenetic heterogeneity of human SCLC tumors in preclinical mouse models,5 and therapy-induced plasticity and heterogeneity of SCLC tumors.

Taken together, we demonstrated that lurbinectedin augments the anti-tumor immune response of PD-L1 blockade by invoking multimodal immune activation in SCLC. CD8+ T cell and STING are crucial for lurbinectedin-mediated anti-tumor immunity in SCLC. An increase in cytotoxic T cell lymphocytes and M1 macrophages, activation of STING-mediated MHC-I genes, and immunogenic cell death-related DAMPs indicate crucial mechanisms for improving immunogenicity. Importantly, we demonstrated that lurbinectedin alone or in combination with anti-PD-L1 caused tumor regression in the maintenance settings. Furthermore, STING activation launches multimodal immune activation with1 upregulation of IFNs,2 transcriptional activation and an increase in surface expression of MHC-I, and3 activation of ICD and DAMPs. Finally, we demonstrate that lurbinectedin treatment increases CD8+ T cells, MHC-I/II, and co-stimulatory receptors in SCLC clinical samples. Our study also provides strong preclinical and clinical evidence of key changes in the tumor immune microenvironment after lurbinectedin treatment, highlighting a mechanistic rationale for the combination of lurbinectedin and immunotherapy.

Limitations of the study

The limitations of our study are the relatively small number of clinical samples pre- and post-lurbinectedin treatment. This is mainly because most patients have metastatic disease, and biopsy after disease progression does not provide additional clinical benefits.

Next, our data suggest that lurbinectedin-induced dsDNA may activate cGAS, resulting in STING activation. However, a recent study suggested that histone-bound self-DNA generated from replication stress is unable to activate cGAS.32 Moreover, previous studies have shown that cGAS-STING pathway can be regulated by multiple mechanisms in cancer.29,37,38,39,40 Especially, we and others have shown that XPO1 inhibitions induce DNA damage repair and sensitize SCLC to chemotherapy. Though we found that lurbinectedin treatment did not uniformly modulate the expression of these regulators in preclinical models, the concrete mechanism by which lurbinectedin regulates XPO1 and the additional mechanism of lurbinectedin-mediated STING activation in SCLC needs additional investigation.

We further show that activation of the STING-IFN pathway may be a potential mechanism of lurbinectedin-mediated upregulation of MHC-I in SCLC as STING depletion in vivo could partially reverse the MHC-I induction. Paradoxically, unlike lurbinectedin, other inhibitors that induce DNA damage may not induce MHC-I expression in SCLC. Therefore, other potential mechanisms of lurbinectedin-induced antigen presentation in SCLC warrant further investigation.

Moreover, our in vivo studies and clinical sample analyses indicated a lurbinectedin-induced increase in M1 macrophages and MHC-I/II. These observations need further validation in a larger cohort of patient samples. Ongoing clinical studies investigating lurbinectedin and anti-PD-L1 will be able to provide the required clinical validation of our preclinical findings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture

Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (gibco, #A10491-01) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum, penicillin (100 U/mL), streptomycin (50 g/mL) and 2 mM L-Glutamine was used to culture SCLC cells that were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), and the European Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (ECACC) [cell line details have been provided in Table S2]. The cells were incubated in 5% Carbon Dioxide at 37°C and relative humidity above 90%. Cell lines were tested and authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling (DNA fingerprinting) and routinely tested for mycoplasma species before any experiments were performed. Generally, the cells were used within the first three to five passages for any experiments.

In vivo mouse models

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Tumors were measured twice weekly. Volumes were calculated as width 2 x length x 0.5. Body weights were monitored twice a week. Mice were euthanized when tumor volumes exceeded 10% of the average body weight, 2 cm in any dimension, or severe tumor ulceration. Linear mixed-effects regression models were used to estimate tumor growth rate in each treatment group and test for significant growth attenuation between treatment groups.

For the immune-compromised in vivo studies, 2×106 cells were mixed with a 1:1 mixture of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and Matrigel (#CB40234, Fisher) and were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of 6-week-old female nude mice obtained from ENVIGO (Boyertown, PA). Once the mean tumor volume reached 100–150 mm3, mice were randomized and treated with vehicle (5% glucose+0.5%HPMC, IV) or lurbinectedin (0.2 mg/kg, IV, 1/7).

For the studies with immune-competent mouse single-cell suspensions (2×106 RPP or RPM cells derived from GEMMs) were injected into 6-week-old female B6FVBF1/J mice (The Jackson Laboratory). Once mean tumor volumes reached 100–150 mm3, mice were randomized into groups and treated with vehicle (n = 9 for RPP, n = 10 for RPM) (5% glucose+0.5%HPMC, i.v.), anti-PD-L1 antibody (n = 9 for RPP, n = 10 for RPM) (300μg/animal, once weekly, i.p.), lurbinectedin (n = 8 for RPP, n = 10 for RPM) (0.2 mg/kg, i.v., once weekly), or combination of lurbinectedin and anti-PD-L1 antibody (n = 12 for RPP, n = 11 for RPM).

For the second line treatment therapy B6129F1 mice bearing flank RPP tumors were treated with anti-PD-L1 antibody (300 μg/animal, once a week, i.p.), cisplatin, and etoposide (3 mg/kg, three times a week) for three cycles. This treatment was followed by randomization into three groups. First group (n = 9) received anti-PD-L1 antibody (300 μg/animal, once a week, i.p.), lurbinectedin (n = 8) (0.2 mg/kg, one out of 7 days, i.v.), or lurbinectedin plus anti-PD-L1 antibody (n = 9). Mice were euthanized when we observed tumor volumes around 1400 mm3, consistent body weight loss greater than 10%, or severe tumor ulceration.

Clinical and rapid tissue donation samples

RNA-STEP data represents samples isolated before and after lurbinectedin treatment from one matched patient. Multiplex Immunofluorescence was performed from tissue samples isolated from the primary tumor site (lung) as well as metastatic sites (liver, lymph node) from 6 patients. Among the 6 patients, two patients did not receive lurbinectedin treatment, other four patients received lurbinectedin treatment in the clinic.

Tissue for this study was donated through the thoracic rapid tissue donation program at Moffitt.41 The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB, protocol 00000971; Advarra). Written informed consent was received prior to participation in this program which allows for communication with the next of kin after death and donation of tissue samples through an autopsy-like process as well as biobanking and research with clinical samples associated with the donor. Multiple post-lurbinectedin samples from primary tumor and metastatic site from 6 patients were included in this study. Matched clinical samples were available and sufficient from 7 patients for RNA STEP testing and from the patient described below for mIF testing.

The donor was a 71-year-old female who was a lifelong smoker and presented with hyponatremia. CT/PET scans showed multiple lymphadenopathies and a left lower lobe lung density (2 cm, SUV:4.8). A right supraclavicular lymph node biopsy was diagnosed with small cell carcinoma. She received etoposide, atezolizumab and carboplatin followed by radiation therapy with initial response. She had perceived radiologic progression versus therapy-related changes at 7 months after diagnosis and received Lurbinectedin for 2 months. Lurbinectedin was discontinued with the radiological changes considered more likely therapy-related changes, and she was feeling well at her next visit after discontinuing lurbinectedin. Later, she experienced disease progression with evolving liver failure and ultimately succumbed to the disease. Samples donated through the rapid tissue donation program revealed SCLC in both lungs, paratracheal and supraclavicular lymph nodes, and liver.

The right supraclavicular lymph node specimen collected at diagnosis and right lung specimen collected after death 13 months later (6 months after lurbinectedin therapy) were tested with a 7-marker multiplex immunofluorescence panel.

Method details

Drugs used in this study

Lurbinectedin (Selleckchem, #S9603) was obtained from Selleckchem and Jazz Pharmaceuticals. STING inhibitor H-151 was obtained from Selleckchem (Catalog No.S6652).

Flow cytometry analysis

Flow cytometry analysis was performed according to standard protocol. For in vitro studies, cells were incubated with TruFCX (for human cells) to block nonspecific binding. After blocking cells were stained stained (30 min) with fluorescent tagged anti-HLA-ABC antibody (Table S3) followed by washing. The data were obtained by LSRFortessa X-20 and was analyzed by FACSDiva software. For Murine tumors gentleMACS dissociator was used to prepare single-cell suspensions according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Single-cell suspensions were stained with anti-CD16/32 monoclonal antibody and fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (Table S3) for 30 min then fixed with 1% Paraformaldehyde. Data were obtained on a Cytek Aurora (Cytek, Bethesda, MD) flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo software (version 10.6, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ). Details of the flow antibodies have been provided in Table S3.

In-vivo STING knockdown study

The STING knocked-down cells were injected into RPP and RPM GEMM mice respectively to generate tumor. Once mean tumor volumes reached 100–150 mm3, mice were randomized into groups into 4 treatment arms: (1): vehicle (n = 5 for control, n = 6 for scr and n = 6 for STING KD), (2) PD-L1 alone (300 μg/animal, i.p., 1/7) (n = 5 for control, n = 6 for scr and n = 6 for STING KD) (3) only lurbinectedin (0.2 mg/kg, i.v.. 1/7 days) (n = 6 for control, n = 6 for scr and n = 6 for STING KD) (4) combination of lurbinectedin and PD-L1 administered concurrently (n = 6 for control, n = 6 for scr and n = 6 for STING KD).

T cell co-culture

H69 cells were plated at a concentration of 1 X106 cells per replicate. Next day H69 cells were then treated with either vehicle control (DMSO) or 10 nM lurbinectedin for 24h. In the meantime, NY-ESO-1 (SLLMWITQC)-specific CD8+ T cells (purchased from Charles River) were thawed in RPMI media supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% Pencillin/Streptamycin, 1mM sodium pyruvate and 1X non-essential amino acids and rested overnight. Post 24h vehicle or lurbinectedin treatment the H69 cells were washed with PBS and 50,000 cells were plated in 50 μL complete media in 96-well U-bottom plates. H69 cells was pulsed for 2h at 37C with NY-ESO-1 peptide at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL. Subsequently, H69 was washed twice without disturbing the cell pellet with complete media at 400g for 4mins. NY-ESO-1-specific CD8+ T cells were washed once with PBS and 50 μL of 50,000 cells was added to the H69 in complete media. 100 μL of 120 μg/mL Atezolizumab [final concentration = 60 μg/mL] or 20 μg/mL anti-MHC-I antibody [final concentration = 10 μg/mL] was added to the respective wells. All the remaining wells received 100 μL complete media alone. The wells were mixed using a multichannel pipette incubated for 20 h at 37C. After the incubation plate was centrifuged at 400g for 4 min and 150 μL of supernatants were discarded without disturbing the cell pellet. The cells were re-suspended with 50 μL more media. The cells and cell titer glo reagent was equilibrated at room temperature for 30 min. Equal volume of the cell titer glo reagent [1:1] was added to the cells, mixed well and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were then transferred to a 96-well opaque plate and luminescence reading was recorded.

Cell viability assay

CellTitre Glo kit was used to perform cell viability assay (Promega, #G7571). 2000 cells per well were plated on 96 well plates. 3-fold drug dilutions were made ranging 0nM–100nM concentration and cells were treated 24 h after seeding in triplicates. The cells were incubated for 24 h after treatment and CellTitre Glo substrate was added to the cells and luminesces was measured using BioTek synergy HTX multi-mode reader. The data was calculated using GraphPad Prism Ver. 9.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) to find Half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of Lurbinectedin.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Cell pellets were prepared freshly and were sent for RNA isolation, library preparation, and RNA sequencing to GENEWIZ. RNeasy Plus Universal mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used for total RNA extraction according to manufacturer’s protocol. NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) was used to prepare RNA seq. library for Illumina according to manufacturer’s protocol. Agilent TapeStation (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) was used to validate RNA samples and seq. libraries. Libraries were quantified by q-PCR (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA) as well as by Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA).

Libraries were loaded according to manufacturer’s protocol on Illumina HiSeq instrument (4000 or equivalent) following clustering on one flowcell lane. Using a 2x150bp paired end (PE) configuration samples were sequenced and HiSeq Control Software (HCS) was used to perform image analysis and base calling. Raw sequence data (.bclfiles) were converted into fastq files and de-multiplexed using Illumina’s bcl2fastq 2.17 software allowing one mismatch for index sequence identification.

Salmon v1.1.0 running on raw reads being mapped to 25-mer indexed mm-10 genome was used to quantify RNA-seq reads. Mapping validation (--validatemappings), bootstrapping with 30 re-samplings (--numBootstraps); sequence specific biases (--seqBias), coverage biases (--posBias) and GC biases corrections (--gcBias) were enabled apart from default settings. Differential gene expression and PCA analysis along with transcript per million (TPM) normalization were performed using Sleuth v0.30.0 in gene mode. The transcript to gene map was based on Ensembl 92.

Differential expression and functional analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using the gene level read counts and the DESeq2 (v1.28.1) R package42 Genes with less than 5 reads in total across all samples are filtered as inactive genes. Genes with adjusted p-value of less than 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change greater than 1 were considered as differentially expressed. P-value adjustment was done with DESeq2 using the Benjamin-Hochberg method.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier curve based on tumor regression data for labeled conditions was produced using survival R package (v3.2.11) to calculate survival and significance and R package survminer (v0.4.9) to plot curve. Statistical significance is calculated as a p-value using a log rank test.

Principal component analysis and heatmaps

Variance stabilizing transformation of the DESeq2 package was used to perform sample normalization. Principal component analysis and calculation of the Euclidean distances between each sample were done with 1000 most variable genes. Gene expression heatmaps show the z-scores of DESeq2 VST normalized gene-level read counts. The heatmaps are generated using all the DEGs identified across multiple comparisons to give an overview of the transcriptomic changes. The colored side bars of the heatmap represent whether the gene is differentially expressed at any of the comparisons stated in color bar names. These heatmaps depict z-scores of DESeq2 VST normalized gene-level read counts for the specified genes in each marker group. All visualizations are generated using plotly R package (v4.9.2.1) except for heatmaps.

R packages.R (v.4.1.0) is used to perform all bioinformatics analysis.

Western blot

Protein extraction was done in previously described method.43 Briefly, Cells were collected in ice-cold PBS and centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 3 min. Pellets were resuspended in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher, #89901) containing Sodium Fluoride and Phosphatase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher, #78446). The resuspended pellets were incubated on ice for 1 h with vortexing every 5 min. After an hour the cell lysates were centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube. This total protein extract was quantified using Coomassie Protein Assay reagent (Thermo Scientific, #1856209). Protein samples were prepared using Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad, #1610747) and 50μg proteins were run on 10–15% SDS-PAGE followed by wet-transfer on to 0.45 μm Immobilon-FL PVDF membrane (Millipore, #IPFL00010). The membrane was blocked using Blocker Casein (Thermo Scientific, #37532) in TBS (Fisher bioreagents, #BP2471-1) containing Tween 20 (Fisher bioreagents, #BP337-500) at room temperature for 1 h and was probed overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies [detailed list of western blot antibodies are provided in Table S4] diluted as 1:1000 in blocker Casein followed by washing with TBST. The blots were probed with secondary antibodies containing horseradish peroxidase enzyme diluted to 1:10000 ratio for 1 h at room temperature followed by washing with TBST. The blots were developed in iBright Western Blot Imaging Systems (Thermo Fisher) after incubating for 1 min in SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Scientific, #34577). Details of the western blot antibodies have been provided in Table S4.

Transfection of siRNAs

Silencer Select siRNAs (Invitrogen) for STING at a concentration of 10 nM was transfected into cells using lipofectamine RNAi-MAX (Invitrogen) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Silencer Select siRNA for Negative Control no.1 (AM4611, Invitrogen) was used as the scrambled control. Knockdown was confirmed by western blot.

RNA isolation and cDNA preparation

RNA isolation was performed in trizol method following manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, control and treated cells were centrifuged and collected in trizol (ambion by life technologies #15596026). 0.2 mL of 1-Bromo-3-chloropropane (Thermo Scientific, #106862500) was added per 1 mL of trizol. The cells were inverted 10 times and incubated on ice for 2 min followed by centrifugation for 15 min at 12000 g in 4°C. Aqueous phase was carefully transferred to new tubes and 0.5 mL of isopropanol was added per 1mL of trizol. The reaction was incubated at room temperature for 10 min followed by centrifugation at 12000g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded and the pellets were washed with 1 mL of 75% ethanol per 1mL of trizol and centrifuged at 7500 g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded. The pellets were air-dried at room temperature and dissolved in DEPC water. The RNA were incubated in water bath at 58°C for 12 min then snap chilled on ice. The concentration of RNA was measured using Thermo Scientific Nanodrop one. cDNA were prepared with 2–3 μg of RNA using Verso cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Scientific #AB-1452/B).

Q-PCR

Q-PCR was performed following 1:4 or 1:5 dilurions of cDNA using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (appliedbiosystems, #A25742) as per manufacturer’s protocol. Initial denaturation was performed at 95°C for 10 min and 40 cycles of denaturation temperature 95°C for 30 s, annealing temperature 60°C for 30 s and extension temperature of 72°C 30 s. Followed by melting curve analysis at 95°C for 15 s, 60°C 30 s and 95°C for 15 s. Primer list of the genes analyzed are detailed in Table S5.

Micronuclei assay

H446, DMS114 and H1048 cells were seeded in 8 chambered slides. Then the cells were treated with either vehicle [DMSO] or 10 nM lurbinectedin for 8h. Cytochalasin B (3 μg/mL) was added to the treated cell 4 h before the endpoint. Post-treatment, the media was removed and the slides were washed with DPBS twice. Then the cells were treated with weak hypotonic solution (0.075 M KCl/0.9% saline, 1:9) and incubated at 37°C for 5 min. Cells were then fixed with methanol: acetic acid = 3:1 solution. The cells were washed with DPBS, and then blocked with 1% BSA for 45 min at 37°C followed by 2 × 5 min of PBS washes. Then cells were incubated with cGAS primary and Lamin B1 antibody (1:300 dilution) overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber. Post-incubation cells were washed with PBS 2 × 5 min followed by incubation with secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488 Rabbit Monoclonal and Alexa Fluor 647 mouse Monoclonal) (1:500 dilution) for 1 h at 37°C in a humidified chamber followed by washing with PBS 2 X 5 min. The cells were then counterstained with counterstained with DAPI (2ug/ml) for 30 min at room temp. The stained cells were imaged with Leica STED 3X at 63X lens.

RNA STEP

Samples were tested with a 204 gene RNA Salah Targeted Expression Panel (STEP), which was designed with feedback elicited from Moffitt clinicians about which proteins or biomarkers would be most relevant for future clinical trials and validated using the nanoString platform in the Moffitt Cancer Center Advanced Diagnostics CLIA-certified laboratory, as described in a prior publication.44 Briefly, RNA was extracted from formalin fixed paraffin embedded (FFPE) tissue and processed with RNA STEP which has probes designed to target RNA transcripts from 204 specific genes. The nanoString platform digitally counts the mRNA transcripts in the samples. Each run includes a reference universal mRNA control derived from pooled human normal tissues (BioChain Institute, Inc., Newark, CA, Cat. No. R4234565) to ensure batch-to-batch consistency and normalized gene signals. Raw data files are processed and normalized using geNorm in the NanoString nSolver 4.0 advanced analysis software (https://university.nanostring.com/advanced-analysis-20-user-manual). The normalized log2 ratios are computed by subtracting the normalized log2 counts of the universal mRNA control genes from the log2 counts of the individual samples. In the RNA STEP validation, a log2 ratio of ≥2 was chosen as the criterion to define "high" expression.

Multiplex Immunofluorescence

Cell typing of each cell was then determined by the observation of positive marker status. Interacting cells were further examined by the presence of dual signals of two different cell types, such as CD3+ and CD4+, CD3+ and CD8+, and CD19+ and CD138+ dual positivity. The distribution of immune cells in the tumoral versus stromal compartments was assessed using PCK. Both H&E and PCK stained slides were evaluated by a pathologist. Cores were excluded if they contained non-tumoral epithelial cells with positive PCK staining or lacked PCK staining in tumoral cells.

FFPE tissue samples were immunostained using the AKOYA Biosciences OPAL TM 7-Color Automation IHC kit (Waltham, MA) on the BOND RX autostainer (Leica Biosystems, Vista, CA). The OPAL 7-color kit uses tyramide signal amplification (TSA)-conjugated to individual fluorophores to detect various targets within the multiplex assay.

Sections were baked at 65°C for 1 h then transferred to the BOND RX (Leica Biosystems). All subsequent steps (ex., deparaffinization, antigen retrieval) were performed using an automated OPAL IHC procedure (AKOYA). OPAL staining of each antigen occurred as follows: heat induced epitope retrieval (HIER) was achieved with EDTA pH 9.0 buffer for 20 min at 95°C before the slides were blocked with AKOYA blocking buffer for 10 min. Then slides were incubated with the first primary antibody at RT for 60 min followed by OPAL HRP polymer and one of the OPAL fluorophores during the final TSA step. Individual antibody complexes were stripped after each round of antigen detection with six rounds using the following antibodies: CD103 (Abcam, SP301 1:50), CD8 (Dako, C8/144B 1:50), TCF1/7 (CST C63D9 1:100), CD3 (DAKO, Rb poly, HIER-EDTA pH 9.0, 1:300, dye 540), MHCII (Dako, CR3/43 1:75), and CD69 (Abcam, EPR21814 1:250). After the final stripping step, DAPI counterstain was applied to the multiplexed slide and removed from BOND RX for cover slipping with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant (ThermoFisher Scientific). All slides were imaged with the Vectra3 Automated Quantitative Pathology Imaging System. Details about the multiplex immunofluorescence antibodies are listed in Table S6. To test for differential counts of immune marker-positive cells following Lurbinectedin treatment, we used a generalized linear mixed-effect model (GLMM) with a Poisson distribution, including total tumor or stroma cell count as an offset term to account for differences in cell counts across samples. The model included Time (Pre/Post) as a fixed effect and a random intercept across PatientID to account for repeated measures. The visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package in R (v3.4.2).

Quantitative image analysis

Multi-layer TIFF images were exported from InForm (AKOYA) and loaded into the HALO Image Analysis Platform version 3.6.4134 (Indica Labs, New Mexico) for quantitative image analysis. A classifier was trained to identify areas of tumor, stroma or non-tissue regions using random forest supervised machine learning. Pan-cytokeratin (PCK) was used to train tumor regions as a masking marker for tumor cells. The classifier was created and tested on various images in the image set. For the quantitative fluorescent phenotype analysis, the tissue was segmented into individual cells using the DAPI marker which stains cell nuclei. For each marker, a positivity threshold within the nucleus or cytoplasm was determined per marker based on visual intensity. After setting a positive fluorescent threshold for each staining marker, the entire image set was analyzed with the created algorithm. The generated data included positive cell counts for each fluorescent marker in the cytoplasm or nucleus, and percent of cells positive for the marker. The data was sorted by classification to include entire tissue, stroma or tumor regions. Along with the summary output, a per-cell analysis was exported to provide the marker status, classification, and fluorescent intensities for downstream bioinformatics analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

For in vivo experiments, to estimate the association between time and tumor-size for each treatment (Figures 1B, 1C, 2S, and 6B), we used linear mixed-effects regression models. These models included tumor-size as the dependent variable (Y), whilst including independent variables (X) Time (days) as a continuous variable and Treatment Group (Vehicle, PD-L1 etc), including interaction terms between Time and Treatment. To account for repeated measures (over time) from the same mouse, we included a random intercept and random slope across MouseID, assuming an auto-regressive (AR) covariance structure across time. Histograms, and QQ-plots were used to check that tumor-size and model residuals were approximately normally distributed, log transforming the data if necessary.

Quantitative rt-PCR, flow cytometry data and ELISA were expressed as means ± standard error of mean (SE) and tumor volume data in in vivo studies were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Paired two-tailed Students’ t-test was performed to compare mean values when the experimental design composed two individual groups. One-way ANOVA or the dunnett’s test were used for multiple group comparisons followed by students’ t-test (while comparing between two groups in a multiple group cohort) using GraphPad Prism Ver. 9.0 software to determine statistical significance among the biologically distinct groups/cells and p values of students’ t-test less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant (ns > 0.05, ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001). Data are expressed as the mean ± SEM.
