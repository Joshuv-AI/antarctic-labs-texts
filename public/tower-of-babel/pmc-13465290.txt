Development and initial validation of the digital health equity questionnaire for rural older adults
Authors: Qin Liu, Aifeire Abeibao, Na Meng, Mengsi Huang, Dilixiati Maimaitiyili, Hanyue Sun, Lei Kang, Shuping You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop a digital health equity questionnaire for rural elderly and test its reliability and validity.MethodsThe initial questionnaire items were based on the social ecological model. They were identified by systematically reviewing the literature and conducting semi-structured interviews. Delphi expert consultation was used to screen and revise the items, resulting in a pre-test questionnaire for digital health equity among rural older adults. A total of 206 rural older adults were selected to complete item analysis and exploratory factor analysis on the pre-test questionnaire. Another group of 227 rural older adults were selected to evaluate the reliability and validity of the formal questionnaire.ResultsThe developed Digital Health Equity Questionnaire for rural older adults includes five dimensions: individual level, interpersonal level, organizational level, community level, and policy level, with a total of 31 items. Exploratory factor analysis extracted 5 common factors, with a cumulative variance contribution of 75.120%. The results of the confirmatory factor analysis indicate that the overall fit of the constructed model is good: χ2/df = 2.19, TLI = 0.916, IFI = 0.903, CFI = 0.912, RMSEA = 0.064. The overall Cronbach’s α coefficient of the questionnaire is 0.903, the split-half reliability is 0.879, and the test-retest reliability after a two-week interval is 0.857.ConclusionThe Digital Health Equity Questionnaire for rural older adults has good reliability and validity and can be used as a standardized tool for the preliminary assessment of digital health equity among rural older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28085-7.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of digital health technologies has significantly transformed both the material and ideological aspects of people’s lives [1]. Digital health technologies such as smartphone applications and remote monitoring present novel opportunities for health interventions [2]. However, a critical issue in research is ensuring equitable access to these technologies across diverse populations [3]. According to the World Health Organization, health equity denotes the absence of unfair, avoidable, and remediable disparities in health conditions among individuals. Health equity is achieved when everyone can fully realize their health potential [4]. A subset of this concept, digital health equity, entails guaranteeing that all individuals can access digital health services regardless of age, income, or technical proficiency, ensuring no one is excluded due to lack of internet access or digital literacy [5]. While fostering technological innovation, it is essential to not only prevent the exacerbation of existing health disparities but also to effectively enhance the physical and mental health of all individuals [6]. Health equity aims to eliminate unnecessary, preventable, and unjust health disparities [7]. For practitioners engaged in developing, promoting, and implementing digital health equity interventions in real-world contexts, a systematic and comprehensive understanding of digital health equity is imperative [5, 8, 9]. Digital health equity is embedded within a complex social ecosystem that encompasses not only individual access to digital health tools but also structural factors at multiple levels, including interpersonal support, organizational services, community resources, and policy protections [10–12]. The degree to which individuals engage with digital health solutions is influenced by biases inherent in policymaking, healthcare models, and socio-cultural norms. These biases may be explicit, implicit, or structural [12–17], interacting across populations in ways that either exacerbate or mitigate health opportunity inequalities [18, 19]. Due to the multi-layered and complex nature of digital health equity, unidimensional measurement tools cannot comprehensively capture its essence. The Social Ecological Model (SEM) emphasizes multi-level interactions between individuals and their environments, providing an integrated theoretical framework for systematically analyzing determinants of digital health equity [20]. Utilizing this model, assessment tools can concurrently evaluate the comprehensive impact of multidimensional factors—including individual capabilities, social support, organizational services, community environments, and policy systems—on digital health equity among rural older adults, thereby avoiding simplistic attributions of health inequality to individual digital literacy deficits.

With the rapid development of digital technologies, particularly Internet-based technologies, the impact of digital technology on elderly health has garnered increasing attention [21, 22]. Firstly, digital technology enables the elderly to access extensive health-related information, which not only raises their health awareness but also encourages greater investment in health human capital [23, 24]. Additionally, digital platforms provide abundant medical service resources, facilitating timely diagnosis and treatment to improve health status [25]. Secondly, digital technology significantly expands the social networks of elderly individuals, offering increased social support and spiritual comfort [26–28]. However, rural residents face disadvantages compared to urban residents in accessing and utilizing digital technology, primarily due to insufficient digital infrastructure in rural areas and generally lower income levels among rural populations [29]. This disparity extends to digital health, creating inequities between urban and rural areas. By the end of 2022, the Internet penetration rate in rural China was 58.8%, compared to 83.1% in urban areas, highlighting a substantial digital divide that poses a fundamental barrier to digital health equity [30]. Urban areas benefit from superior information infrastructure and widespread 5G network access, resulting in preferential access to Internet information resources and services. Conversely, rural areas confront limited information channels, lower information quality, and inequities in the allocation and adaptability of digital health resources. Consequently, these disparities directly lead to unequal opportunities for urban and rural residents to access health services. Urban residents efficiently connect to medical resources via digital tools, rapidly develop scientific health awareness, and translate this into health-promoting behaviors. In contrast, rural residents often lack the ability to access or utilize digital health resources effectively, preventing timely receipt of authoritative health information and convenient digital health consultations. Ultimately, this results in disparities in the accessibility and effectiveness of health services between rural and urban populations, exacerbating existing urban-rural health equity imbalances.

Current domestic and international research has developed evaluation tools such as the Electronic Health Literacy Scale [31]ཤthe Digital Health Literacy Scale for Community-Dwelling Older Adults [32]ཤthe Health Information Literacy Scale [33], and the Media Health Literacy Scale [34], which provide important theoretical foundation for this study. However, these tools primarily assess individual-level health information acquisition, comprehension, and application abilities, and do not specifically measure digital health equity. No mature tool currently evaluates digital health equity explicitly. Conceptually, existing instruments focus on “literacy” or “ability” but omit the “equity” dimension; thus, they cannot measure perceived or objective digital health inequalities experienced by individuals nor reflect structural barriers faced by rural older adults in accessing digital health resources. It is important to recognize that digital health equity profoundly impacts elderly health outcomes. It influences not only the ability of older adults to leverage digital technology for health improvement but also the widening or narrowing of health disparities among elderly groups, directly relating to the ultimate realization of health equity. Therefore, despite the solid foundation established by existing scales, there remains a critical need for an assessment tool designed explicitly for older adults in rural China that integrates both “digital health” and “equity” aspects. The development of such a tool holds significant theoretical and practical value for accurately identifying health inequalities among rural older adults in the digital era, evaluating intervention effectiveness, and guiding health equity policy formulation.

Building on this background, the present study focuses on rural older adults in Xinjiang. Combining survey data on their health needs with the SEM as the theoretical framework, this study develops a digital health equity questionnaire tailored for this population. This tool offers a standardized measurement tool for investigating the current status of digital health equity, analyzing influencing factors, and comparing group differences. The findings can provide empirical evidence for health authorities at all levels to formulate age-appropriate digital health intervention policies, promote the targeted provision of digital health resources to grassroots rural areas, narrow the urban-rural digital health gap, and support the full implementation of the Healthy China and Digital Village strategies among rural older adults.

Methods

Subjects and methods

This study developed a novel Digital Health Equity Questionnaire for rural older adults; the complete English version is available in Supplementary Material 1. The study was conducted in Banfanggou Town, Urumqi County, Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region. Participants were recruited via convenience sampling. The questionnaire item pool was created based on the study results and a literature review. Initially, the draft of the questionnaire was finalized after two rounds of Delphi expert consultation, with experts from 11 provinces. Subsequently, a survey was administered to rural older adults in Banfanggou Town, Urumqi County. The collected data were analyzed to assess the questionnaire’s reliability and validity, leading to the development of the final version.

Theoretical framework and dimension presets

The development of this questionnaire strictly follows the SEM as the theoretical guiding framework. This model comprises five core dimensions: individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy levels. This model emphasizes that individual behavior and health outcomes are influenced by a multitude of factors at both micro and macro levels, providing a scientific theoretical perspective for a deeper analysis of digital health equity [20]. Specifically, at the individual level, digital literacy, health beliefs, and technology acceptance among rural older adults form the intrinsic foundation for their engagement in digital health [11]. At the interpersonal level, intergenerational support from family, neighborly assistance, and social network relationships directly affects their use of digital tools [35]. At the organizational and community levels, the digital service capabilities of grassroots healthcare institutions, information dissemination mechanisms of village organizations, and accessibility of community digital infrastructure determine whether this population can access appropriate health resources [36]. At the policy and societal levels, efforts to advance the digital village strategy, the scope of health equity policies, and structural disparities in urban-rural resource allocation fundamentally shape the digital health environment for rural older adults. Consequently, the SEM systematically reveals the multidimensional nature of digital health equity and aligns with the structural and hierarchical characteristics inherent in this concept, providing a solid theoretical foundation for developing the assessment tool in this study.

Phase 1: Creating search items

Using the Chinese search terms “rural, digital health; older adults, health equity; influencing factors” and the corresponding English keywords, databases including China National Knowledge Infrastructure, VIP Information, Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform, China Biomedical Literature Database, PubMed, PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science, and CINAHL were searched from the establishment of the database to May 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) research focused on digital health equity among older adults and related influencing factors; (2) studies published in Chinese or English. Exclusion criteria included: (1) duplicate publications; (2) inaccessible full texts or low-quality studies. A total of 5,613 articles were retrieved, with 75 articles retained for analysis after screening titles, abstracts, and full texts to construct the questionnaire item pool. Study quality was assessed using standardized tools. Cohort and case-control studies were evaluated with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), scored out of 9, with higher scores indicating better quality [37]. Cross-sectional studies were appraised using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) criteria, consisting of 11 items scored as “0” (no or unclear) or “1” (yes), with total scores ranging from 0 to 11. Scores of 8–11, 4–7, and 1–3 indicate high, moderate, and low quality, respectively [38]. Two researchers independently assessed all studies. Subsequently, discrepancies in scoring and items of disagreement were cross-checked, and consensus was reached through discussion or consultation with a third party when necessary.

From March to May 2024, 30 rural older adults were selected via convenience sampling for semi-structured interviews. The interviews were conducted in the respondents’ homes by investigators who had undergone standardized training, with each interview lasting approximately 20 ~ 30 min. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) continuous residence in Urumqi County for ≥ 1 year; (3) normal cognitive function with clear verbal expression; and (4) voluntary participation with informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) severe visual, hearing, or speech impairments confirmed by self-report, family reports, or investigator observation; (2) diagnosed with mental illness or severe cognitive impairment; (3) severe physical illnesses that prevented them from cooperating with the study. These criteria aim to ensure that respondents can provide meaningful and reliable answers relevant to the study’s topic, while covering as wide a range of older adults as possible across different ages, genders, and levels of digital usage experience to obtain diverse perspectives. Interviews were conducted by investigators who had undergone standardized training, and the following measures were taken to ensure objectivity: (1) a standardized semi-structured interview guide ensured uniform questioning; (2) investigators received training to avoid using leading or suggestive language; (3) investigators did not express personal opinions or judgments during the interviews; (4) with the informed consent of the participants, all interviews were fully audio-recorded and transcribed and verified by another researcher to ensure accurate and objective documentation. Data were analyzed using NVIVO 11.0, and a preliminary questionnaire item pool was developed based on the literature review and interviews. The questionnaire adopts a hierarchical structure with three levels: dimensions (first-level indicators), sub-dimensions (second-level indicators), and items.

Phase 2: Delphi method and content validity

Two rounds of expert consultations were conducted in June and July 2024. Experts were selected based on: (1) at least 10 years of professional experience in digital health, health equity, health policy, or digital literacy; (2) intermediate or higher professional title; (3) enthusiasm and willingness to participate in both rounds. Twenty-two experts from 11 provinces—including Liaoning, Henan, Guangdong, and Shandong—participated, covering expertise in digital health, health equity, health policy, and digital literacy.

The expert consultation questionnaire included: an introduction (research background and objectives), the main body (draft questionnaire), and expert information (personal details and familiarity with the questionnaire). The questionnaire was distributed and collected via email, with each round of consultation lasting two weeks. Experts rated the importance of each questionnaire item and provided suggestions for revisions or deletions. After two rounds of consultation, the experts reached a final consensus. A 5-point Likert scale was used, with each item rated on a scale from “not important” to “very important” (1 ~ 5 points). Items with an average score ≥ 4.0 and a coefficient of variation (CV) ≤ 0.25 were retained [39]. Items were revised based on feedback until consensus was reached.

Content validity was assessed using the Content Validity Index (CVI), including the Item-Level CVI (I-CVI) and Scale-Level CVI (S-CVI), the latter being the average of the former [26]. Criteria for acceptability were I-CVI ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.80 [40]. In the content validity assessment, in addition to rating the importance of each item, experts evaluated the items based on the following three aspects: (1) Relevance: Does the item accurately reflect the core essence of the dimension it measures? (2) Comprehensiveness: Does the set of items fully cover all aspects of the concept of digital health equity? (3) Understandability: Is the language of the item clear and concise, and does it align with the comprehension level of rural older adults? Experts provided revision suggestions for each item, and the research team revised the items based on expert feedback until all experts reached consensus on the items’ relevance, comprehensiveness, and clarity. In addition to content validity, the experts’ professional competence and the reliability of their opinions also influence the validity of the evaluation results; an authority coefficient was used to assess this. The authority coefficient (Cr) is calculated as Cr = (Ca + Cs)/2, where Ca represents the expert’s judgment basis coefficient, and Cs represents the expert’s familiarity with each indicator. If the expert authority coefficient exceeds 0.7, the authority is considered high, and the scores and recommendations provided by the experts are deemed more reliable [41]. Kendall’s W coefficient is used to assess the degree of agreement among expert opinions. The W value ranges from 0 to 1; the closer it is to 1, the better the agreement among expert opinions [42].

Phase 3: Psychometric testing

Sampling and data collection

The first round of the questionnaire survey took place from August to September 2024, and the second from October to November 2024 in Banfanggou Town, Urumqi County. Trained investigators administered surveys. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 60 years; residence in Urumqi County for ≥ 1 year; normal literacy and communication skills; ability to independently or with guidance complete the questionnaire; and voluntary informed consent. Exclusion criteria were as follows: individuals confirmed through self-report, family reports, or investigator observation to have severe visual, hearing, or language impairments; diagnosed with mental illness or severe cognitive impairment; or severe physical illnesses that prevent them from cooperating with the survey. Survey instruments include a general demographic questionnaire (e.g., age, gender, educational level, marital status, living arrangement, professional status, monthly income, internet usage, and network signal stability) and the finalized digital health equity questionnaire.

Data collection methods included paper-based and online questionnaires. Prior to the survey, researchers consulted village committees to understand the distribution of residents, mapped out household visit routes, and visited the homes of rural older adults to distribute paper questionnaires, interact directly with respondents, and provide assistance. For those unable to complete independently, one-on-one interviews were conducted without leading questions. All paper questionnaires were collected and verified on-site, with any questionnaires having more than 5% of items missing being excluded. Online questionnaires were distributed to villagers by researchers, accompanied by instructions for completion. Elderly participants with digital literacy completed the questionnaires independently, and researchers subsequently organized and screened the responses. Specifically, all questionnaire items were set as mandatory, and only one response was allowed per IP address. Within 24 h of questionnaire collection, data were entered into Excel and cross-checked by two researchers. Questionnaires were excluded if all items selected the same option, responses showed obvious patterns, or the completion time for the online questionnaire was less than 180 s [43].

The initial draft contained 37 items and considering factor analysis requirements (5–10 times the number of items), sample size was set between 185 and 370 [44]. Accounting for convenience sampling feasibility and potential invalid questionnaires, 230 questionnaires were distributed in the first round, with 224 returned and 206 valid (91.96% response rate). These valid samples were used for item analysis, reliability analysis, and exploratory factor analysis (EFA). In the second round, 251 questionnaires were distributed, 248 returned, and 227 valid (91.53% validity), used for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Medical University (Approval No.: XJYKDXR20231208001). Participants were informed about the study purpose, confidentiality, and voluntary participation before signing informed consent. Data collection was conducted anonymously; no personally identifiable information, such as names or ID numbers, was collected. All data were used solely for the analysis of this study, were securely stored by the researchers, and were not disclosed to third parties without the participants’ consent.

Data analysis

Prior to analysis, collected data were imported into Excel and coded. Analytical methods included item analysis, construct validity analysis, and reliability analysis, conducted using IBM SPSS 26.0 and AMOS 24.0 software. The sample was divided as follows: 206 valid questionnaires from the first survey round were used for item analysis, reliability analysis, and EFA; 227 valid questionnaires from the second round were used for CFA. The respondents in both rounds came from the same target population but constituted different samples to ensure the independence of the EFA and CFA.

Item Analysis: (1) Critical Ratio Method: The top 27% and bottom 27% of participants based on total questionnaire scores were designated as the high-scoring and low-scoring groups, respectively. An independent samples t-test was used to compare the groups, and items with p < 0.05 and a critical ratio greater than 3.00 were retained [45]; (2) Homogeneity Test: Items with a significant product-moment correlation coefficient (p < 0.05) and a high correlation coefficient (r > 0.40) were retained [39]; (3) Cronbach’s α Method: if removing a specific item significantly improved the internal consistency alpha coefficient of the dimension or the total score, that item was deleted [39]; (4) Commonalities and factor loadings: Retain items with a commonality value ≥ 0.20 and a factor loading ≥ 0.40 [46].

Structural Validity Analysis [47]: (1) Calculate the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy. A KMO value greater than 0.50 indicates that there is a certain degree of correlation among the item variables, satisfying the conditions for factor analysis. (2) Conduct Bartlett’s sphericity test. If the significance probability of the test is p < 0.05, factor analysis is appropriate. (3) EFA: Principal component analysis is used to extract factors, and varimax rotation was used for factor rotation. The number of factors is determined based on a comprehensive assessment of theoretical assumptions and statistical criteria. At the theoretical level, five dimensions were predefined based on the SEM. Statistically, the determination is based on the following criteria: the cumulative variance explained by the extracted factors exceeds 60%; each factor contains at least three items; the factors satisfy the criteria of the steepness plot and are easily named [40]. (4) To further validate the structural validity of the questionnaire, CFA was conducted on the 227 valid questionnaires from the second round of the survey. Model fit was evaluated using the following indicators: χ²/df < 5.0, and Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI) > 0.90, indicating good model fit. In addition, the factor loadings for each item should exceed 0.40, and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) should be less than 0.08, indicating acceptable model fit [41, 48]. At the same time, Modification Indices (MI) were used to assess the local fit of the model; if MI values were excessively high and theoretically significant, model modifications were considered [39].

Reliability Analysis: (1) Internal Consistency Reliability: Cronbach’s α coefficients were calculated for the total questionnaire and each dimension. A Cronbach’s α coefficient greater than 0.7 indicates that the questionnaire has good internal consistency reliability [49]. (2) Test-retest reliability: 20 rural older adults were randomly selected from the CFA sample to serve as the retest sample; they were surveyed again using the same questionnaire two weeks later. Reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC); a value > 0.70 indicates good reliability [50].

Results

Item analysis

A pool of questionnaire items was constructed based on the results of a literature review and qualitative interviews. The questionnaire adopted a hierarchical structure comprising three levels: dimensions, sub-dimensions, and items. Specifically, five dimensions (first-level indicators) were predefined based on the SEM. Under these five dimensions, 18 sub-dimensions (second-level indicators) were further developed to capture the specific connotations of each dimension. A total of 37 items were ultimately generated to operationalize the measurement of the aforementioned sub-dimensions, comprising 12 items at the individual level, 9 items at the interpersonal level, 6 items at the organizational level, 6 items at the community level, and 4 items at the policy level.

Delphi method

Across two rounds of Delphi consultation, 19 experts provided feedback. In the first round, 22 questionnaires were distributed and 19 were returned, yielding a response rate of 86.36%. In the second round, 19 questionnaires were distributed and 16 were returned, resulting in a response rate of 84.21%. See Table 1 for details.

The authority coefficient of the two rounds of experts is 0.922, indicating that the authority of the experts is high. Development of the preliminary questionnaire

Each round of evaluation was conducted by calculating the average importance scores and coefficients of variation for the secondary indicators and items. The questionnaire was revised based on a comprehensive review of these calculations, expert recommendations, a literature review, and research team discussions. Ultimately, the questionnaire comprised 5 primary indicators, 18 secondary indicators, and 33 items.

Item analysis

(1) Cutoff-point method: The sample was ranked by total score, with the top 27% classified as the high-score group and the bottom 27% as the low-score group, and an independent samples t-test was conducted. The results showed that the score differences between the high- and low-score groups were statistically significant for all items (t = 2.923 ~ 25.308, all p < 0.05), and 33 items were preliminarily retained. (2) Correlation Coefficient Method: The correlation coefficients between each item and the total questionnaire score were calculated. The results indicated that the correlation coefficients between all items and the total score were greater than 0.400 and p < 0.05, satisfying retention criteria. (3) Cronbach’s α: Item deletion analysis showed that after removing item D5 (“The village committee regularly promotes digital health services”), the questionnaire’s Cronbach’s α increased from 0.878 to 0.896, indicating that deleting this item improves internal consistency. Following discussions by the research team in conjunction with expert opinions, it was determined that this item conceptually overlapped with content under the organizational level (C2: “I have participated in digital device usage training courses organized in the village, such as learning to use electronic health records, electronic medical insurance, and online registration”). Consequently, 32 items were retained for further analysis. For details, see Supplementary Material 2.

Structural Validity

Exploratory factor analysis

In this study, EFA was conducted on 206 participants. The results showed a KMO value of 0.936 and a chi-square value of 6509.143 for Bartlett’s sphericity test (p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. Preliminary analysis identified five common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, each comprising at least three items, accounting for a cumulative variance of 73.117%. Combined with a steepness plot analysis, this suggested that retaining five factors was appropriate. These results align with the theoretically prescribed five dimensions, further supporting the applicability of the SEM in this study. Accordingly, the number of extracted factors was fixed at 5 for subsequent analysis. Items were screened, and the following items were sequentially excluded: (1) factor loadings below 0.4 or communality under 0.2 on their respective factors; (2) items with loadings that were too similar across dimensions and exhibited multiple loadings; (3) misalignment with the theoretical framework or interpretative difficulty; and (4) dimensions with fewer than three items. Following these criteria, item D1 (“My village has a stable internet signal, allowing me to use digital devices to browse health information smoothly”) was removed. Following discussion by the research team, this item was found to have a low factor loading (< 0.40) in the factor analysis, and its content leaned more toward infrastructure conditions, which did not fully align with the core connotations at the community level. A total of 31 items were ultimately retained, and factor analysis was conducted again. The results still yielded 5 common factors, with the cumulative variance explained increasing to 75.120%. The factor loadings of items within each dimension ranged from 0.444 to 0.927. See Table 3 for specific results.

Confirmatory factor analysis

Further screening via EFA resulted in the development of a digital health equity questionnaire for rural older adults, comprising 5 dimensions and 31 items (see Table 4). To validate the structural validity of the questionnaire, CFA was conducted on 227 participants using AMOS software. The results showed: χ²/df = 2.19, TLI = 0.916, IFI = 0.903, CFI = 0.912, and RMSEA = 0.064. Examination of modification indices revealed no recommendations to add paths or release error terms. Therefore, the model was not restructured, and the initial five-factor structure was retained. The loadings of each item on its corresponding factor ranged from 0.516 to 0.959. Among these, the item with the lowest loading was A3 (I am confident in my ability to obtain health information via the Internet), and the item with the highest loading was E4 (Current policies have ensured that rural older adults enjoy digital health services of the same quality as urban older adults). Based on these fit indices, the model demonstrates good fit and strong construct validity.

Digital health equity questionnaire for rural elderly

Questionnaire scoring method

The final digital health equity questionnaire for rural older adults comprises 5 dimensions and 31 items. Responses are measured using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“Strongly Disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly Agree”). Total scores range from 31 to 155 points, with each dimension score calculated as the sum of its constituent items. The dimensions and their corresponding item count and score ranges are: Individual Level (11 items, 11–55 points), Interpersonal Level (4 items, 4–20 points), Organizational Level (5 items, 5–25 points), Community Level (3 items, 3–15 points), and Policy Level (8 items, 8–40 points). Higher scores indicate greater digital health equity among rural older adults.

Intrinsic correlation test

The correlation coefficient between the dimensions of the questionnaire was 0.672 ~ 0.756; the correlation coefficient between the scores of each dimension and the total score was 0.649 ~ 0.699, and each dimension was positively correlated with the total scale, which was statistically significant (p < 0.001), as shown in Table 5.

Reliability analysis

Internal consistency reliability

The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale was 0.903, indicating excellent reliability. Dimension-specific Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.850 (individual level), 0.723 (interpersonal level), 0.766 (organizational level), 0.754 (community level), and 0.923 (policy level), demonstrating good internal consistency across all dimensions. The split-half reliability coefficient for the total scale score was 0.879. Split-half reliability coefficients for each dimension were 0.786(individual level), 0.792(interpersonal level), 0.728 (organizational level), 0.702(community level), and 0.878 (policy level), further confirming the questionnaire’s reliability.

Test-retest reliability

The results of the test-retest reliability analysis showed that the ICC for the total questionnaire was 0.857 (95% CI: 0.752–0.951). The ICC values for each dimension were as follows: individual level 0.791 (95% CI: 0.621–0.912), interpersonal level 0.766 (95% CI: 0.532–0.885), organizational level 0.810 (95% CI: 0.687–0.903), community level 0.773 (95% CI: 0.582–0.865), and policy level 0.782 (95% CI: 0.587–0.867), indicating good test-retest reliability across all dimensions.

Discussion

Digital health equity is a critical public health issue affecting the elderly [51], particularly in rural areas where digital resource distribution is uneven, and digital literacy varies significantly. Achieving digital health equity is of profound significance for narrowing the social gradient of health, ensuring that all people enjoy the dividends of digital health, and improving the efficiency and effectiveness of medical and health services brought about by digital health technologies [52]. Consequently, health policymakers and practitioners must scientifically assess the current state of digital health equity among the elderly to develop targeted interventions. Assessing digital health equity in rural older adults offers a comprehensive understanding of multidimensional disparities in technology access, usage ability, satisfaction with health information, and digital health service accessibility. It also identifies the interactive effects of structural barriers and individual factors. Therefore, guided by the SEM, this study developed a digital health equity questionnaire tailored to rural older adults across five levels: individual, interpersonal, community, organizational, and policy. The design of this questionnaire integrated theoretical foundations with expert input: on the one hand, through a systematic review of domestic and international research on digital health equity and based on the SEM, we comprehensively mapped out the connotations and multidimensional structure of digital health equity; on the other hand, following two rounds of Delphi expert consultations, the items underwent repeated revisions and optimizations to ensure that the questionnaire effectively covers the core dimensions of digital health equity among rural older adults. The questionnaire contains 31 items in 5 dimensions and has good reliability and validity. It can be used as an effective tool to evaluate the level of digital health equity of the rural older adults and provide a scientific basis for subsequent policy formulation and intervention practice.

The questionnaire exhibits good construct validity, with five factors identified through EFA aligning closely with SEM-based theoretical dimensions and explaining over 70% of cumulative variance. CFA further supported the factor structure, with fit indices meeting psychometric standards, indicating a well-fitting model. Reliability analysis results show that the questionnaire’s overall internal consistency reliability, split-half reliability, and test-retest reliability all meet acceptable psychometric standards. Although there are slight variations in the reliability coefficients across dimensions, all are above the recommended threshold of 0.70, indicating that the measurement results across different dimensions are stable and reliable.

During the questionnaire development, the SEM framework guided the process, and the items of relevant scales were used as a reference to ensure that the scale had a scientific theoretical basis. Nineteen experts from different fields across the country ensured the authority and standardization of the questionnaire. These experts, possessing extensive theoretical and practical experience in health equity and digital literacy, guaranteed the scale’s validity and comprehensiveness. Multiple item analysis methods were employed to rigorously screen questionnaire items, and the research team engaged in extensive discussions about the professional relevance of each item to maintain the questionnaire’s rigor.

To ensure the practicality of the questionnaire items, the following contents were mainly considered in the preparation process: (1) the content of the items is systematic and comprehensive. Based on the social ecological model, a five-dimensional evaluation framework of individual, interpersonal, organization, community and policy is constructed, which not only covers individual factors such as digital health skills, cognition and attitude, but also includes environmental indicators such as family support, age-appropriate conditions of medical institutions, and policy fairness perception, so as to realize the three-dimensional measurement of digital health equity problems of rural older adults; (2) Highlight the core concern of fairness, and accurately capture the subjective experience of rural older adults in the accessibility, quality and treatment of digital health services by setting differentiated items such as health equity perception, resource allocation fairness and policy fairness perception, so as to make up for the lack of health equity assessment in the context of digital divide in previous studies; (3) In view of the main obstacles faced by rural older adults in using digital health services, such as physiological function limitations and economic burden concerns, measurement items with situational adaptability are designed to improve the sensitivity and practical guidance value of the questionnaire in specific cultural regions. In addition, the language of the questionnaire is easy to understand and has good operability.

This study has the following limitations. First, due to time and geographic constraints, participants were exclusively recruited from Urumqi County, Xinjiang, using convenience sampling, which limits the sample’s representativeness and warrants caution when generalizing the findings. Second, data were collected via both online and paper-based questionnaires. Although standardized training and quality control measures minimized measurement errors, unobserved systematic differences between the two methods may persist. Third, as a preliminary validation study, this research conducted only preliminary tests of the questionnaire’s reliability and validity and did not assess measurement error or responsiveness. Future studies should examine the questionnaire’s responsiveness in longitudinal or intervention designs and calculate standard errors of measurement to more comprehensively evaluate measurement error. It is important to emphasize that questionnaire validation is an ongoing process. Future research should further examine the psychometric properties of the questionnaire in broader and more diverse rural contexts, employ longitudinal designs to assess test-retest reliability, and evaluate measurement invariance across subgroups such as gender and digital literacy levels to ensure its generalizability.

Conclusion

Based on the SEM, this study developed and validated a questionnaire on digital health equity for rural older adults across five levels—individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy—to comprehensively assess their perceptions of equity regarding access to digital health technologies, usage capabilities, and resource allocation. This approach aligns with the core principles of digital health equity, emphasizing multidimensional nesting and systemic interconnections. The questionnaire content closely reflects the current digital infrastructure in rural China, the actual barriers faced by older adults, and the characteristics of the primary healthcare system, demonstrating strong cultural relevance and practical applicability. Validation results confirm that the questionnaire has a clear structure, with reliability and validity indices meeting psychometric standards. It demonstrates good internal consistency, stability, and construct validity, making it an effective tool for assessing digital health equity among rural older adults. This instrument provides a scientific basis for primary healthcare institutions to identify service gaps, develop age-friendly digital health interventions, and promote targeted resource allocation.
