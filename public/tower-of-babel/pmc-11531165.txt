An inverted U-shaped relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout among village clinic doctors: the moderating effect of information and communication technology use
Authors: Lifang Zhou, Haiyuan Lv, Yuncong Yu, Jiaxian Shao, Yixuan Wu, Xiaona Li, Min Gao, Wenqiang Yin, Qianqian Yu, Zhongming Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVillage clinic doctors are an integral part of the rural healthcare workforce. With the advancement of science and technology and the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, the prevalence of remote work and the use of information and communication technologies (ICT) have increased significantly. However, these developments have also had an impact on the work family balance and mental well-being of village clinic doctors. The study aimed to explore how work-family conflict affected job burnout and what the role of ICT use was in this relationship.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study. Data were collected collected through an anonymous self-administered questionnaire survey conducted in December 2021 in Shandong Province, China. A total of 1,093 village clinic doctors participated in the survey. The data were analyzed using multiple linear regression and the PROCESS macro in SPSS.ResultsThe results showed that an inverted U-shaped relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout among village clinic doctors. Specifically, the squared term of work-family conflict had a negative coefficient (-0.324). The slope was positive (SL=2.291) at the low end of the X-range and negative (SH=-0.379) at the high end. The turning point occurred at X = 1.227(-2.309 ≤ X ≤ 1.812). Additionally, ICT use positively moderated between work-family conflict and job burnout (β1β4-β2β3 = 0.217, β4 > 0).ConclusionsThe work-family conflict has an inverted U-shaped effect on job burnout, while the use of ICT positively moderates this relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout. Policymakers should pay attention to the effects of ICT use and work-family conflict on job burnout among village clinic doctors.Keywords: Village clinic doctors, Work-family conflict, Job burnout, Information and Communication technology use

Introduction

Information and Communication Technology (ICT) refers to any device or technology that can collect, store, or transmit information [1]. With the development of science and technology, ICT has become part of modern life, including smartphones, social media, and email [2]. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, ICT has become increasingly important due to the need for people to work at home [3]. ICT has transformed communication (including organizational and doctor-patient communication) and working mode (such as work locations and service mode) [4]. With its help, employees can manage their work from almost anywhere and at any time [5]. Although ICT improves work efficiency, it also blurs the boundaries between work and family, creating a complex and nuanced impact on their relationship.

Work-family conflict is typically defined as a form of role conflict arising from the incompatibility between pressures from work and family roles. It includes both work-family conflict (where work demands disrupt family) and family-work conflict (where family demands disrupt work) [6]. This is a common phenomenon among healthcare workers [7]. When examining the relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout, most studies indicate that the former is a significant predictor of the latter [8]. According to resource conservation theory, when employees face work-family conflict, they experience a loss of psychological and physiological resources [9]. If measures are not taken to protect or replenish these resources in time, job burnout may occur [10]. In addition, studies have found that the widespread use of ICT forces employees to respond to job demands at any time, which can lead to negative emotions such as job burnout [11].

Job burnout refers to the state of physical, psychological and behavioral exhaustion caused by long working hours, heavy workload, and high work intensity, including emotional exhaustion, depersonalization and reduced personal accomplishment [12]. It can lead not only to mental health problems such as depression [13], anxiety, and even suicide [14], but also to physiological issues like chronic fatigue and headaches [15]. In addition, job burnout among doctors can lead to medical errors like missed or incorrect diagnosis, potentially endangering patient safety [16]. It has been identified as a serious public health problem [8].

Chinese village clinic doctors refer to medical workers who work in rural areas of China, whose function is to provide primary health care services to rural residents [17]. In recent years, the aging population in China’s rural areas has worsened, leading to an increased demand for healthcare services. However, the rural healthcare system faces challenges such as understaffing, which in turn increases the workload and psychological pressure on village clinic doctors [18]. Therefore, village clinic doctors are considered highly susceptible to job burnout, a problem that has attracted significant attention from researchers [19]. With the advancement of rural health informatization, ICT has become a crucial tool for village clinic doctors to manage tasks from their superiors and provide effective healthcare. Amid the COVID-19 pandemic, ICT has become the primary tool for village clinic doctors to receive and implement government mandates for epidemic prevention and control.

This study examines the impact of ICT use and work-family conflict on job burnout, as well as the potential moderating role of ICT. Currently, there is a lack of research specifically addressing these dynamics among village clinic doctors. Therefore, this study aims to fill this gap by investigating how work-family conflict contributes to job burnout, and analyzing the potential moderating role of ICT in this relationship.

Hypothesis development

Work-family conflict and job burnout

There is a nonlinear relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout. On the one hand, according to the theory of resource conservation, work-family conflict leads to significant resource depletion to village clinic doctors, which makes them more susceptible to psychological crises such as job burnout [20]. On the other hand, according to the adaptation theory, adaptation is a crucial feature of human evolution for survival. It help people recover their happiness, job satisfaction, and other emotions from external setbacks to their initial levels, enabling them to overcome psychological crises and adapt to new environments [21]. The adaptation of individuals’ responses to stressors has also been well-documented [22]. For example, Sandra’s study found a significant inverted U-shaped relationship between work-family conflict and stress [23]. Therefore, when village clinic doctors invest substantial time and energy into their work, resulting a ‘workaholic’ state, and have the ability to adapt to work pressure, the positive effects of work engagement can outweigh the negative effects of work-family conflict [24]. Job burnout caused by work-family conflict may have a downward trend. Accordingly, we hypothesized that there is a curvilinear relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout among village clinic doctors, leading to the formulation of Hypothesis H1.

Hypothesis 1 There is an inverted U-shaped relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout among village clinic doctors.

Hypothesis 1 There is an inverted U-shaped relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout among village clinic doctors.

The moderating role of ICT

There is a moderating effect of ICT on work-family conflict and job burnout. On the one hand, the flexibility and work overload associated with ICT blur the boundaries between work and family, exacerbating work-family conflicts [25, 26]. On the other hand, ICT is significantly associated with increased job burnout [27]. The use of ICT extends the working hours of village clinic doctors, making them more susceptible to job burnout. In summary, we propose that ICT has a positive moderating effect on the relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout, leading to the formulation of Hypothesis H2.

Methods

Participants and procedures

The data for this study were collected through a cross-sectional survey conducted in December 2021 in Shandong Province. The province is in the eastern part of China and ranks as the second most populous province in the country. Shandong Province currently comprises 16 prefecture-level cities, which are administrative divisions below the provincial level and above the county level. In 2022, Shandong’s population was 101.62 million. The rural population was 36.03 million, making up 35.46% of the total [28]. In 2021, the province had 52,940 village clinics and 71,765 village clinic doctors. On average, there were 1.36 doctors per clinic [29].

This study used a multi-stage stratified random sampling approach to select the study population. Within Shandong Province, 3 prefecture-level cities were randomly selected to represent high, medium, and low levels of economic development. Following the same principle, 3 counties were then randomly chosen within each selected city. Subsequently, 4–6 townships were randomly selected from each county, and 15 to 30 village clinic doctors were randomly chosen from each township. The researchers calculated the sample size using the following formula: n=[z2*p*(1-p)/e2]/[1+(z2*p*(1-p)/(e2*N))]. The relevant values were obtained based on recommendations from a standard textbook [30] and the data from the China Health Statistics Yearbook [31]. An estimated sample size of 1,053 participants was determined. To ensure adequate coverage, 1,200 questionnaires were distributed. Of these, 1,093 eligible questionnaires were collected, resulting in an effective response rate of 91.1%. The inclusion criteria for the study participants were: (1) village clinic doctors who were on duty during the survey and had been working for more than one year at the time of the survey, and (2) all participants were informed about the study and voluntarily agreed to participate. The survey was conducted by trained master’s and doctoral students who served as investigators. They organized the village clinic doctors to independently complete the survey questionnaire on-site. Before filling out the questionnaire, participants were informed about its anonymous nature and that the data would only be used for research purposes. All participants voluntarily agreed to participate. This study followed the STROBE cross-sectional checklist in preparing our report [32].

Measures

The measurement variables and survey questionnaire were designed based on the research hypothesis. The questionnaire comprised three sections: work-family conflict, job burnout, and ICT use. All items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

The Work-Family Conflict Scale, developed by Netemeyer and validated for the Chinese population [33], was utilized to evaluate work-family conflict among village clinic doctors. This scale comprised two dimensions: work affecting family and family affecting work, encompassing a total of 10 items (e.g., “Work demands affect your family life”). Cronbach’s of the scale was 0.863, with 0.933 for the work-family conflict dimension and 0.869 for the family-work conflict dimension.

The Job Burnout Scale, adapted by Li [34], was widely used in several studies and validated for the Chinese population [35, 36]. Based on these, our research team developed a job burnout scale for healthcare workers [37, 38]. The scale evaluated the degree of job burnout among village clinic doctors in this study. It comprised three dimensions: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment, encompassing a total of 15 items (e.g., “Work makes you feel physically and mentally exhausted”). Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.79.

The ICT scale utilized in this study, adapted from Gonzalez [39], was customized to evaluate work-related ICT usage among village clinic doctors in China. This scale necessitates village clinic doctors to self-assess their extent of ICT usage, encompassing three items (e.g., “Supervisors arrange work for you through online tools”). Cronbach’s α of the scale was 0.71.

According to previous studies, gender (1 = male, 2 = female), age (years), work tenure (years), marital status (1 = married, 2 = not in marriage), and education level (1 = primary schools and below, 2 = junior high school, 3 = senior high school, 4 = junior college, 5 = undergraduate and above) may have an impact on the research variables. Therefore, these variables were processed as control variables.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed in five steps using SPSS 23.0. First, Harman single factor scores was used to test common method bias for all scales [40]. Results showed that the factorial variance explained 27.358%, which was less than the critical value of 40%, indicating that there was no significant common method bias. Second, Z-scores were normalized for all variables to address potential multicollinearity in the squared terms [24]. Third, statistical descriptions and bivariate correlations were employed to examine the levels and interrelationships of key variables. Fourth, linear regression analysis, guided by the criteria established by Haans [41], was used to validate the hypothesized inverted-U relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout and to assess the moderating role of ICT within this relationship. This method is widely recognized for its application across multiple disciplines, underscoring its methodological rigor. Finally, the robustness of the moderating effects was tested using PROCESS model 1 in SPSS (Hayes, v4.1).

Demographic characteristics

A total of 1,093 village clinic doctors were included in the survey. Most of them were male, accounting for 62.1%; age ranged from 20 to 80, with an average age of 46.59 ± 7.82; the average cumulative working time was 25.13 ± 8.78 years; most of them were married, accounting for 96.1%, most of them had senior high school and junior college education, accounting for 54.8% and 36.5%.

According to Haans [41], three conditions need to be met simultaneously for an inverted U-shaped relationship between variables to hold. First, the coefficient of the squared independent variable must be negative. Second, the slope of the curve is positive when the independent variable is at its minimum value, and negative when the independent variable is at its maximum value. Third, the turning point of the curve (-β1/2β2) is located within the range of values for the independent variable.

First, verify the first condition. According to the results in Model 3 in Table 2, the coefficient for work-family conflict was positive (β1 = 0.795, P < 0.05) and the coefficient for the squared term of work-family conflict was negative (β2=-0.324, P < 0.05). Thus, the first condition is met.

Secondly, verify the second condition. The regression equation for work-family conflict (X) predicting job burnout (Y) is hypothesized as follows:

The slope of the curve is given by the equation S = β1 + 2β2X. According to Model 3, the coefficient β1 for work-family conflict is 0.795, the coefficient β2 for the squared term of work-family conflict is -0.324, and the standardized value range of X is from − 2.309 to 1.812. Therefore, when the independent variable (X) is at the low end of its range, the slope SL is 2.291. When the independent variable (X) is at the high end of its range, the slope SH=-0.379. So, the second condition is met.

Finally, verify the third condition. According to the properties of the quadratic function, the X-coordinate (-β1/2β2) of the turning point is 1.227. It is within the value range of the independent variable ([-2.309, 1.812]). Thus, the third condition is met. In conclusion, there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout among village clinic doctors. Hypothesis 1 is supported, as show in Table 3; Fig. 2.

Discussion

According to the results, there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout. This indicates that as work-family conflict increases among village clinic doctors, job burnout initially rises and then decreases. There is a turning point where job burnout peak, which should be noted by managers.

Work-family conflict occurs when individuals are unable to meet family needs due to their work roles. When work-family conflict is low, individuals may allocate time, energy and other resources from family to work, which interferes with fulfilling family responsibilities. At this stage, because the investment of resources is minimal, work rewards are also limited. Consequently, the negative emotions resulting from work-family conflict can outweigh the sense of accomplishment and professional identity gained from work rewards, leading to increased job burnout [42].

As job burnout continues to increase, a turning point is reached, marking the peak of burnout. After this point, even if an individual’s work involvement continues to rise, job burnout does not increase indefinitely and may begin to decrease. For managers, understanding this inverted U-shaped relationship and its turning point is crucial. By recognizing this, they can implement measures to help village clinic doctors transition smoothly into the phase after burnout peaks, ultimately boosting overall productivity and reducing turnover intentions [43].

When individuals put more resources from family into work, leading to a “workaholic” state, work-family conflict becomes more severe [44]. However, When work-family conflict exceeds a certain threshold, individuals may adapt to the pressure. At this stage, the high level of resource investment results in greater work rewards. As a result, the sense of achievement and professional identity gained from these rewards can outweigh the negative emotions caused by the conflict. In this case, work-family conflict can weaken job burnout.

ICT use plays a positive moderating role between work-family conflict and job burnout. This can be explained as: while using ICT appropriately can lessen the impact of work-family conflict on job burnout, it’s important to be aware that overusing ICT can make the impact of work-family conflict on job burnout worse. According to the view of technostress, on the one hand, the ever-increasing sophistication of ICTs creates challenges and pressures for managers and staff who use ICT [42]. On the other hand, while ICT makes work schedules and telecommuting more convenient, it also increases the likelihood of remote supervision, multitasking and, social isolation. All of these effects further blurred the boundaries between working hours and rest hours, and exacerbated work-family conflicts [45]. Therefore, excessive use of ICT may strengthen the work-family conflict, and then aggravate job burnout.

This study found that work-family conflict is a significant risk factor for burnout and that ICT overuse exacerbates the impact of work-family conflict on burnout. These findings can inform policy makers when developing measures in various aspects. At the social level, policymakers should advocate the establishment of a culture that supports work-family balance [46]. Through publicity and educational activities, the public’s awareness of the importance of work-family balance should be raised, and managers should be encouraged to take active measures to balance the family life of village clinic doctors. At the organizational level, policymakers can push managers to develop family-supportive policies. Managers should be concerned about the work status and family situation of village clinic doctors and provide them with the necessary support and resources to tide over the turning point of job burnout [43]. At the family level, spousal support is crucial for easing work-family conflict [46]. Community activities and family education can be used to enhance communication and understanding among family members and increase family support for work. At the individual level, managers should closely pay attention to the phenomenon that the rest time of village clinic doctors is encroached due to excessive use of ICT. This problem may intensify work-family conflict and increase the risk of job burnout accordingly [47]. Managers should optimize individual work assignments and workflows to protect the rest time of village clinic doctors [48]. Thus, they can minimize the indirect encroachment on village clinic doctors’ rest time caused by excessive use of ICT [49].

This study has its limitations. First, it is a cross-sectional study. Data was collected at only one point in time. This makes it hard to determine if the observed relationships are cause-and-effect. Future research could use longitudinal studies that track participants over time to better test these relationships. Second, the study was conducted in one province in Eastern China. The findings might not apply to other regions or countries. Future research should include more diverse samples from different areas and cultures to improve generalizability. Finally, the study took place during the COVID-19 pandemic, a period of increased ICT usage. This may have affected the results. Future studies could examine these relationships in a post-pandemic context or compare data from before and after the pandemic to understand changes in ICT usage and its impacts.

Conclusions

This study explored the relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout among village clinic doctors, and analyzed in depth the moderating effect of ICT use on this relationship. Findings: (1) Village clinic doctors’ work-family conflict has an inverted U-shaped effect on job burnout. (2) ICT use positively moderates the inverted U-shaped relationship between work-family conflict and job burnout among village clinic doctors. The results of this study provide new insights into the excessive use of ICT, work-family conflicts among village clinic doctors, and strategies for alleviating job burnout.
