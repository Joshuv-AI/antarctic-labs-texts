Social Intersectional Differences in Pulpal Clinical Consequences of Untreated Dental Caries among Brazilian Adolescents: Analysis of the SB Brasil 2023 Survey
Authors: Gabriela Scortegagna de Souza, Maria Laura Braccini Fagundes, Fernando Neves Hugo, Juliana Balbinot Hilgert, Jessye Melgarejo do Amaral Giordani, Orlando Luiz do Amaral Junior
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe aim of the study was to examine differences in the occurrence of consequences of untreated dental caries (pulpal involvement, ulceration, fistula, and abscess-PUFA) across intersectional social positions defined by sex, skin color, and household income among adolescents.MethodsThis study analyzed data from the Brazilian National Oral Health Survey (SB Brasil 2023), using a multistage stratified probability sampling design to represent adolescents aged 15–19 years. Social variables were combined into 8 intersectional groups based on sex, skin color, and family income. Outcomes included PUFA prevalence ≥1 and counts of its components. Survey-adjusted Poisson regression with robust variance and a three-way interaction term was applied.ResultsThe sample comprised 4,892 adolescents. Compared to high-income White males, prevalence was higher among low-income Black males (PR = 5.93; 95% CI: 2.60–13.51) and low-income Black females (PR = 4.06; 95% CI: 1.85–8.90). The predicted prevalence of PUFA ≥1 ranged from 4.0% (95% CI: 2.0–7.0) among high-income White males to 21.0% (95% CI: 11.0–32.0) among low-income Black males. Among females, prevalence estimates were 15.0% (95% CI: 9.0–20.0) in high-income Black, 16.0% (95% CI: 5.0–28.0) in low-income White, and 18.0% (95% CI: 12.0–24.0) in low-income Black individuals.ConclusionMarked differences in PUFA occurrence were observed across intersectional social positions, with the highest burden observed among low-income Black adolescents, particularly males for prevalence ratios, although females also presented high absolute prevalence. These findings reinforce the need for policies addressing structural social inequalities in oral health.

Introduction

In recent decades, social sciences have examined how forms of oppression and discrimination can be analyzed, measured, and understood within contemporary social contexts [1–3]. The intersectionality framework posits that dynamics of privilege and oppression vary by context, such that an individual may simultaneously occupy both advantaged and disadvantaged positions, thereby experiencing distinct forms of discrimination [1–3]. Consequently, categories such as race, class, and gender do not operate in isolation but rather as interconnected dimensions that structure social life in an integrated manner [4]. Within public health, this theoretical perspective has informed a research agenda aimed at understanding how intersecting systems of power and social stratification shape health and illness processes. It indicate the role of discrimination and persistent, often naturalized and structurally produced inequalities in generating and maintaining health inequities [2, 3, 5].

In studies on adolescent oral health, clinical indexes serve as tools for elucidating how social inequalities manifest in terms of oral health outcomes. The DMF index, which quantifies decayed, missing, and filled teeth, has traditionally measured lifetime caries experience. However, this index possesses limitations as it neither captures lesion severity nor reflects the clinical consequences of untreated disease. The PUFA index addresses these shortcomings by expanding the assessment of caries impact to include its more severe clinical manifestations, such as pulp involvement, ulceration, fistula, and abscess [6]. By doing so, this index extends beyond the biological dimensions of disease and suggests the social imprint of oral morbidity, emerging as a robust indicator of disparities in health access and care [7] and a relevant epidemiological metric for assessing the burden of untreated pulpal pathologies and the consequent need for clinical management, particularly endodontic treatment [6].

Previous research has consistently documented inequalities in the prevalence and severity of dental caries across individual social markers [7, 8]. However, limited attention has been given to how the combined distribution of sex, skin color, and income shapes the occurrence of the clinical consequences of untreated caries assessed by the PUFA index. Within a structural intersectionality framework, these social markers are conceptualized as interdependent axes of social stratification shaped by structural racism, sexism, and income inequality. As such, they should not be treated as standard confounders to be statistically controlled, but rather as core dimensions through which oral health inequalities are produced [9]. Examining these combined social positions may improve the identification of subgroups of adolescents experiencing a disproportionate burden of advanced untreated disease and contributes to ongoing efforts to incorporate structural determinants into oral epidemiology [6, 8, 10–12].

In this regard, analyses of national oral health surveys, such as the SB Brazil, offer valuable opportunities to gauge the magnitude of these issues and to explore the interplay of individual and contextual factors in shaping contemporary oral disease burden and dental care needs [8]. Health indices and indicators, therefore, constitute key tools for quantifying the distribution of health conditions across population subgroups, identifying social inequalities, and supporting the planning and prioritization of dental care delivery [13].

This study aimed to examine differences in the prevalence of the pulpal clinical consequences of untreated dental caries with the use of PUFA index and its individual components (pulpal involvement, ulceration, fistula, and abscess) across intersectional social positions defined by sex, skin color, and household income among Brazilian adolescents. The hypothesis posited that adolescents occupying intersections of social disadvantage, particularly those defined by low income, female participants and self-reported Black skin color, can suggest probabilities of pulpal involvement, ulceration, fistula, and abscess. This prediction rests on the premise that multiple, interdependent forms of social marginalization correspond to more severe manifestations of PUFA, while acknowledging that the patterning of disadvantage may vary across specific clinical outcomes and social positions.

Methodology

Study Design and Population

This study analyzed data from the 2023 Brazilian National Oral Health Survey (SB Brasil 2023), the most recent nationally representative epidemiological survey conducted by the Brazilian Ministry of Health. SB Brasil 2023 is part of a series of national oral health surveys initiated in 1986 and was designed to produce population-based estimates for multiple age groups, including adolescents. The survey protocol was approved by the National Research Ethics Committee (CONEP), and all procedures complied with ethical standards ensuring confidentiality and anonymity [14, 15].

SB Brasil 2023 employed a stratified multistage cluster sampling design, with census tracts as primary sampling units and households as secondary sampling units. For the 15–19-year age group, the number of households required in each census tract was defined to achieve the planned sample size for the stratum. The selection of sampling units was performed using probability proportional to size, based on the number of permanent private households. For the present analysis, adolescents aged 15–19 years who completed both the clinical examination and the questionnaire were included. Individuals with missing information on key variables or without clinical examination were excluded. This age group was selected because it represents a stage in which the cumulative consequences of untreated caries, as measured by the PUFA index, can be more reliably assessed within the SB Brasil 2023 survey design.

Dental examinations were conducted by calibrated dentists who completed standardized online training. Examiners were required to achieve at least substantial inter-examiner agreement (kappa ≥0.61) for all indices, including the PUFA index, prior to fieldwork. Sociodemographic characteristics, including sex, self-reported skin color, and household income, were collected using structured questionnaires according to the national protocol [14–16].

The complex survey design was incorporated into all analyses using information on primary sampling units, strata, and sampling weights as defined in the SB Brasil 2023 dataset. Sampling weights accounted for unequal selection probabilities and non-response, ensuring representativeness of the Brazilian population [14, 16].

Intersectional social positions were operationalized through the cross-classification of 3 social markers: sex (male, female); self-reported skin color (White, Black); and monthly household income (≤1 Brazilian minimum wage, >1 minimum wage). Skin color was classified according to the Brazilian context, with Black including individuals who self-identified as Black or Brown. Indigenous and Asian adolescents were excluded from the intersectional analyses because they are subject to distinct processes of racialization and social oppression that were beyond the scope of this study and because their inclusion would result in small and analytically unstable intersectional strata. Household income refers to the year 2022, when the national minimum wage was BRL 1,212.00 per month (equivalent to approximately USD 230.00 in 12/2022), and was used as a relative metric of socioeconomic position.

This approach yielded 8 mutually exclusive intersectional groups, each one conceptualized as a distinct social position rather than as ordered categories along a single socioeconomic gradient. This operationalization is consistent with descriptive quantitative intersectionality, which emphasizes the joint constitution of social positions through multiple, interdependent axes of social stratification [17]. This study was grounded in an intersectionality framework, which conceptualizes social categories such as sex, skin color, and socioeconomic position as mutually constitutive rather than independent determinants of health. Within this perspective, social positions are understood as jointly constructed through systems of power and stratification, rather than as separable exposures whose effects can be independently estimated or combined through interaction terms. Accordingly, a descriptive quantitative intersectional approach based on cross-classification was adopted, in which each combination of social markers was treated as a distinct social position. This analytical strategy prioritizes the description of outcome distributions across jointly defined social positions, in line with the study objective.

Guided by perspectives from critical social epidemiology and intersectionality theory, no additional covariates were included in the models. This analytical choice aimed to avoid conditioning on variables that may mediate, reflect or constitute social inequalities, and to describe how intersectional social positions are patterned in relation to clinical outcomes [18, 19].

Statistical Analysis

The software used was Stata 19.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Associations between intersectional social positions and PUFA outcomes were estimated using survey-adjusted Poisson regression models with robust variance estimation. Fully interacted models were specified by including a three-way interaction between sex, household income, and skin color, which simultaneously incorporates all corresponding main effects and two-way interactions. Although fully interacted models were specified, the purpose of including interaction terms was not to interpret interaction effects as independent parameters, but to allow the estimation of outcome measures for each intersectional social position. This approach is consistent with an intersectional framework in which social categories are not interpreted as separable exposures, but as jointly constitutive dimensions of social stratification. This modeling strategy allows the association of each social marker with the outcomes to vary across levels of the others, reflecting the interdependent nature of social positions as proposed by intersectionality theory.

For the binary outcome (PUFA ≥1), exponentiated coefficients from Poisson regression models with robust variance were interpreted as prevalence ratios. For count outcomes (pulpal involvement, ulceration, fistula, and abscess), exponentiated coefficients represent mean ratios, interpreted as the multiplicative change in the expected number of affected teeth. Poisson models with robust variance were preferred due to their consistency in estimating relative measures in cross-sectional studies, even under violations of the equidispersion assumption. This approach is widely recommended when the primary interest lies in comparing groups rather than fully specifying the outcome distribution. Relative estimates for each intersectional social position were derived from fully interacted models and expressed in relation to the most socially privileged group (male, household income >1 minimum wage, White), used exclusively as the analytical reference category. Due to the low frequency of some outcomes, particularly fistula and abscess, estimates for certain strata should be interpreted with caution as sparse data may affect precision.

To complement relative measures and to describe absolute differences in disease burden across intersectional strata, adjusted marginal predictions were obtained from the fitted Poisson models. For the binary outcome, margins represent the predicted prevalence of PUFA ≥1, whereas for the count outcomes they represent the predicted mean number of teeth affected by each PUFA component. All marginal estimates are model-based predictions that incorporate the complex survey design.

While relative measures require the specification of a reference group, marginal predictions do not rely on hierarchical contrasts. Each marginal estimate represents an absolute quantity for a specific intersectional social position, derived under the same statistical model and sampling structure. In line with intersectionality theory, which challenges the assumption of a neutral or normative reference group, the use of marginal predictions allows the simultaneous description of inequalities across all intersectional strata, emphasizing absolute differences in disease burden rather than comparisons anchored to a single baseline.

All analyses accounted for sampling weights, strata, and primary sampling units as defined in the SB Brasil 2023 survey. Standard errors and 95% confidence intervals were estimated using linearized variance estimation appropriate for multistage complex survey data. Results

The initial analytical sample comprised 8,041 adolescents with complete clinical examination data. Missing information was concentrated in household income and, to a lesser extent, skin color, both variables required to define the intersectional social positions. After excluding participants with incomplete information in these variables, the final analytical sample consisted of 4,892 adolescents (Fig. 1). Comparisons between included and excluded participants showed similar distributions of sex, skin color, and PUFA prevalence, suggesting limited evidence of selection bias resulting from the complete-case analysis (online suppl. Table S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553636).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. Alternative model specifications, including models excluding household income, models without interaction terms, binary outcome definitions, and models incorporating missing data as a separate category, yielded consistent directions and magnitudes of associations. In addition, negative binomial models produced estimates comparable to those obtained from Poisson models with robust variance. Descriptive analyses showed inequalities in PUFA outcomes across individual social markers (Table 1). Black adolescents had a higher prevalence of PUFA ≥1 (16.0%; 95% CI: 13.0–19.6) than White adolescents (6.9%; 95% CI: 5.0–9.3), as well as higher mean counts for all PUFA components. Adolescents in households with monthly income ≤1 minimum wage showed a higher prevalence of PUFA ≥1 (18.6%; 95% CI: 13.5–25.2) compared with those from higher income households (10.4%; 95% CI: 8.0–13.5), with the greater absolute difference observed for pulpal involvement (mean of 0.34 vs. 0.13 affected teeth). Differences by sex were modest.

The predicted probability of having PUFA≥1 ranged from 4% (95% CI: 2–7) among high-income White males to 21% (95% CI: 11–32) among low-income Black males. All low-income groups had predicted probabilities above 13%. The estimate for low-income White males was 13% (95% CI: 0–26). Confidence intervals for predicted prevalence were bounded at zero because prevalence cannot assume negative values. The wide confidence interval indicates substantial statistical imprecision due to sparse data in this subgroup.

For pulpal involvement, the highest predicted mean count was observed among low-income Black adolescents (0.37 teeth). For rarer conditions, including ulceration, fistula, and abscess, predicted mean counts were low across all groups but remained socially patterned. For ulceration, the highest predicted means were observed among Black adolescents from higher income households (0.06–0.07 teeth). Abscess showed a distinct pattern, with the highest predicted means among low-income White adolescents (0.02–0.03 teeth). For fistula and abscess, many predicted means were close to zero and their confidence intervals frequently included zero, indicating limited precision and low absolute occurrence.

Discussion

This study examined the intersections of sex, skin color, and household income in relation to the probability of presenting pulpal clinical repercussions of untreated dental caries. The results indicated that adolescents in intersectional positions characterized by low income, self-reported Black skin color, and female exhibited higher probabilities of pulp involvement, ulceration, fistula, and abscess. By using the PUFA index as the outcome, this study advances the literature by demonstrating how severe, irreversible clinical consequences of untreated dental caries, rather than cumulative caries experience, appear to be socially patterned during adolescence. The PUFA index captures visible pulpal involvement, fistulae, and abscesses, which represent advanced odontogenic infection and frequently necessitate endodontic treatment, and which remain invisible when traditional indices such as DMFT are used [6], These patterns are consistent with social inequalities that may arise from differential access to dental care, experiences of discrimination in healthcare settings, and the cumulative effects of socioeconomic adversity, although these mechanisms were not directly measured in the present study. Prior research in Brazil and other contexts has documented the concentration of untreated caries in populations exposed to socioeconomic disadvantage [20–22]. Studies of Brazilian adolescents have reported consistent social gradients in untreated caries, dental pain, and infection, with income and race as key factors. [8, 21]. The present findings align with this evidence and extend it by examining combined social markers, which may point it gradients not apparent when determinants are assessed in isolation. This approach is consistent with recommendations from social epidemiology to evaluate overlapping social dimensions and their influence on disease processes [23].

Although structural racism and discrimination were not directly assessed, the observed distribution of PUFA outcomes across intersectional social positions is compatible with mechanisms proposed by intersectionality theory and social epidemiology literature concerning the production and maintenance of health inequalities. These findings should, therefore, be interpreted as evidence of patterned social inequalities rather than direct evidence of specific causal mechanisms.

These findings have important implications for oral health equity. Interventions focused exclusively on individual behaviors have shown limited effectiveness in reducing the concentration of untreated dental caries among adolescents living in socially disadvantaged contexts [24–26]. Addressing oral health inequities, therefore, requires strategies that extend beyond the individual level, incorporating public policies and intersectoral actions aimed at the social conditions that generate and sustain unequal distributions of disease. [13, 27, 28]. In this context, strengthening primary health care may represent a relevant pathway to promote more equitable access to oral health services, including expanding coverage, improving the organization of care, and enhancing timely and resolutive interventions, particularly for populations experiencing overlapping social disadvantages [29]. From a global perspective, these findings add to the evidence suggesting that oral health inequalities may be shaped by intersecting social determinants across different contexts, and that intersectional approaches combined with predicted margins may help identify high-burden groups and inform equity-oriented strategies in diverse health systems [18].

An intersectional perspective further contributes to this debate by indicate that social disadvantage is not homogeneous. Inequalities are not only structured between privileged and disadvantaged groups, but also operate within socially marginalized populations through the interaction of multiple axes of oppression. Recognizing this heterogeneity is essential to avoid one-size-fits-all approaches and to inform equity-oriented policies capable of addressing the differentiated needs of adolescents positioned at distinct intersections of social inequality.

Adopting a common risk factor approach is pertinent as it recognizes that multiple health outcomes are shaped by shared social and commercial determinants [20]. Within this framework, integrating oral health into broader adolescent health policies, alongside efforts to address discriminatory processes, strengthen primary health care, and tackle common social and commercial determinants, may contribute to limiting the progression from untreated carious lesions to conditions captured by the PUFA index [30, 31]. Evidence from Brazilian studies suggests that intersectoral strategies combining health promotion, social protection, and organized access to care are associated with reductions in socially patterned inequalities in dental caries and related symptoms [32–34]. The present findings indicate that incorporating an intersectional perspective into such strategies may support the prioritization of adolescents [35].

This study has limitations. Regional variation was not examined, and contextual differences in the organization of care and socioeconomic conditions across Brazil may shape the distribution of untreated dental caries. However, the analysis was not designed to assess geographic heterogeneity. Self-reported skin color and household income may be subject to reporting biases, but these measures may reflect socially constructed positions as experienced in everyday life and are commonly used as proxies for structural inequalities in epidemiological research. The intersectional operationalization adopted here represents one strategy to empirically examine patterned social inequalities; however, systems of oppression are complex and cannot be fully captured by a single quantitative approach. Although cross-classification may simplify complex social processes, it does not constitute misclassification in the conventional epidemiological sense, but rather reflects an analytical strategy that prioritizes socially meaningful categories to describe patterned inequalities in population health. Alternative quantitative approaches, such as multilevel intersectional models or measures of additive interaction, may provide complementary insights. [19], although they rely on different conceptual assumptions, regarding the treatment of social categories as separable exposures and the decomposition of their joint effects into independent and interaction components. In contrast, the present analysis focused on describing how jointly constituted social positions are patterned in relation to oral health outcomes. In addition, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and residual confounding cannot be excluded. The low frequency of fistula and abscess outcomes resulted in sparse data across several intersectional strata, contributing to wide confidence intervals and reduced precision of some estimates. Online supplementary Table S2 illustrates the limited number of events observed in some groups. These findings should, therefore, be interpreted cautiously, particularly regarding the magnitude of relative differences. These findings should, therefore, be interpreted with caution, particularly regarding the magnitude of associations. In such contexts, large point estimates accompanied by imprecise intervals are expected and still indicate a markedly higher burden relative to the reference group. These considerations should be taken into account when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

These patterns likely reflect both socially patterned disease progression and unequal access to dental care. Although the hypothesis anticipated a particular burden among low-income Black females, the findings revealed a more nuanced pattern: pulpal involvement was primarily associated with low income, ulceration with Black skin color (regardless of sex), and abscess with low-income White females. While Black females presented high absolute prevalence, the highest relative differences were observed for low-income Black males. Together, these findings support the application of an intersectional perspective and indicate the need for public health strategies that address multiple, overlapping axes of social disadvantage.
