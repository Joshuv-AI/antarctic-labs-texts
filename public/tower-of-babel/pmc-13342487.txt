Mapping spatiotemporal variability of vegetation and climate sensitivity in northern Iran using google earth engine
Authors: Mehrdad Mehrjou, Hassan Ahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnderstanding the spatiotemporal dynamics of vegetation in response to climatic variability is critical for ecosystem monitoring and sustainable land management, particularly in ecologically sensitive regions. Northern Iran, specifically Guilan Province, with its humid subtropical climate and complex topography, is highly vulnerable to hydroclimatic fluctuations. However, comprehensive assessments integrating multi-source satellite data remain limited. This study aims to investigate spatiotemporal vegetation-climate interactions in Guilan Province from 2020 to 2024 using an integrated analytical framework developed within Google Earth Engine (GEE). The objectives are to quantify interannual changes in vegetation greenness (NDVI and EVI), assess relationships with land surface temperature (LST) and precipitation, and develop a novel Vegetation–Climate Sensitivity Index (VCSI) to evaluate ecological vulnerability. Methodology employed multi-source satellite datasets: Landsat 8-derived NDVI and EVI, MODIS MOD11A2 LST, and CHIRPS precipitation data. All datasets were harmonized to 1 km resolution and analyzed within GEE. The VCSI was constructed by integrating standardized vegetation indices with normalized precipitation and LST. Spatial autocorrelation analyses (Moran’s I, Getis-Ord Gi*) identified clustering patterns of vegetation-climate sensitivity. Results revealed marked interannual variability, with 2022 exhibiting the lowest NDVI/EVI and highest LST, indicating severe climatic stress, followed by partial ecosystem recovery in 2024. Strong positive correlations were found between vegetation indices and precipitation (r = 0.88–0.92), while negative correlations with LST (r = -0.62) confirmed thermal stress, particularly during summer (mean summer VCSI = 56.96). Lagged response analysis showed vegetation responded to precipitation with one- to two-month delays, highlighting soil moisture retention effects. The VCSI effectively captured spatial heterogeneity: high values (resilient zones) concentrated in forested highlands of the west and north, while low values (vulnerable zones) characterized agricultural lowlands and urbanized areas. Spatial autocorrelation confirmed strong clustering of ecosystem sensitivity (Global Moran’s I > 0.93, p < 0.001), with persistent hotspots in highland forests and coldspots in lowland anthropogenic landscapes, intensified during 2022 climatic stress. In conclusion, vegetation responses to climate variability in Guilan Province are spatially structured, temporally lagged, and highly sensitive to hydroclimatic drivers. The integrated GEE-based framework combining VCSI with spatial statistics provides a robust approach for mapping ecological vulnerability. Introduction

Understanding vegetation–climate interactions is essential for monitoring ecosystem health, guiding land-use management, and assessing the impacts of climate change, particularly in ecologically sensitive regions characterized by strong climatic gradients and complex topography. Satellite-derived vegetation indices, most notably the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and the Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), have been extensively applied to quantify vegetation greenness, biomass dynamics, and phenological variation across spatial and temporal scales1,2. NDVI effectively captures vegetation vigor through the spectral contrast between red and near-infrared reflectance; however, it is prone to saturation in densely vegetated environments. EVI partially overcomes this limitation by incorporating atmospheric and soil background corrections, thereby providing enhanced sensitivity in high-biomass regions3.

Vegetation dynamics are intrinsically linked to climatic forcing. Surface temperature and precipitation are widely recognized as dominant drivers of vegetation variability, influencing photosynthetic activity, water availability, and land–atmosphere energy exchange processes4,5. Land Surface Temperature (LST), in particular, serves as a critical proxy for surface energy balance and thermal stress, while precipitation regulates soil moisture conditions and vegetation productivity. The combined effects of thermal and hydrological variability often produce nonlinear, spatially heterogeneous, and temporally lagged vegetation responses, especially in regions with strong environmental gradients6,7.

Northern Iran, and Guilan Province in particular, represents a climatically and ecologically distinctive region. Located along the southern coast of the Caspian Sea, Guilan is characterized by high annual precipitation, dense forest and agricultural land cover, and pronounced altitudinal gradients over short spatial distances. These features render the region highly sensitive to both regional and global climate variability. Even relatively modest shifts in temperature or precipitation regimes can result in substantial changes in vegetation condition, spatial distribution, and ecosystem functioning8,9. Despite this sensitivity, comprehensive assessments of spatiotemporal vegetation–climate interactions in Guilan that integrate multi-source satellite data at appropriate spatial resolution remain limited10,11.

Recent literature in Iran and globally provides important context for this study. Studies in northern Iran using MODIS or Landsat-based NDVI and EVI have reported significant seasonal and interannual variability in vegetation, strongly linked to precipitation and temperature gradients12. These studies highlight the influence of topography and hydroclimatic variability on vegetation, but often focus on single indices or limited temporal periods. Globally, multi-index studies in humid subtropical and semi-arid regions demonstrate that integrating NDVI, EVI, LST, and precipitation improves understanding of vegetation–climate sensitivity, especially for capturing lagged and nonlinear responses13,14. However, most regional-scale applications remain descriptive, with limited attention to spatial autocorrelation or the combined effects of thermal and hydrological stress. These findings indicate a clear gap: while individual indices reveal important trends, a composite measure that accounts for multiple climate drivers is needed to more accurately map ecological vulnerability and resilience, particularly in the data-scarce and topographically complex landscapes of northern Iran15,16.

Recent advances in cloud-based geospatial platforms, particularly Google Earth Engine (GEE), have significantly expanded the capacity for large-scale, multi-temporal remote sensing analyses17. GEE enables efficient integration of long-term satellite archives with climate datasets, facilitating consistent analysis of vegetation and climate dynamics across space and time. Nevertheless, many existing GEE-based studies remain largely descriptive, often relying on simple correlation analyses and paying limited attention to spatial autocorrelation, lagged vegetation responses, and the heterogeneous intensity of vegetation–climate coupling18,19.

To address these gaps, recent literature has increasingly emphasized the value of composite or integrated satellite-based indices, which synthesize information from multiple biophysical variables to better capture ecosystem sensitivity and environmental stress under changing climatic conditions. Such composite indices have been shown to outperform single-variable indicators in identifying vulnerable ecological zones and complex vegetation–climate feedbacks. However, their application remains uneven across regional-scale studies, particularly in data-scarce or topographically complex environments20,21.

In this context, the present study develops an integrated analytical framework that combines vegetation indices, surface temperature, and precipitation data within the GEE environment to evaluate vegetation sensitivity to climatic variability in Guilan Province. NDVI and EVI derived from Landsat 8 imagery, LST from the MODIS MOD11A2 product, and precipitation data from the CHIRPS dataset are jointly analyzed over the period 2020–2024. Building upon recent advances in vegetation–climate coupling research, a composite Vegetation–Climate Sensitivity Index (VCSI) is constructed to quantify the combined influence of thermal and hydrological stress on vegetation dynamics12–14.

In addition, spatial statistical techniques are employed to explicitly capture the geographic structure of vegetation–climate interactions. Trend analysis and regression models are used to quantify temporal changes in NDVI, EVI, and LST, while correlation analyses explore both synchronous and lagged relationships with precipitation. A grid-based zonal framework is applied to reveal intra-provincial heterogeneity, and Global Moran’s I is calculated to assess spatial autocorrelation and identify clusters of high and low vegetation–climate sensitivity22,23.

Accordingly, the objectives of this study are threefold: (i) to quantify interannual and spatial changes in vegetation greenness (NDVI and EVI) and land surface temperature across Guilan Province from 2020 to 2024; (ii) to assess the strength and temporal lag of relationships between vegetation indices, surface temperature, and precipitation; and (iii) to develop and apply a Vegetation–Climate Sensitivity Index (VCSI) to evaluate spatial patterns of ecological vulnerability and resilience across different topographic zones.

The central hypothesis is that vegetation responses to climate variability in Guilan Province are spatially heterogeneous and temporally lagged, reflecting the combined influence of climatic gradients and anthropogenic land-use pressures. By testing this hypothesis, the study advances empirical understanding of vegetation–climate feedbacks in northern Iran and provides a transferable, cloud-based methodological framework for ecological monitoring in other climatically sensitive regions24,25.

Materials and methods

Overall framework

The present research was to carry out a multi-tier methodological workflow with multi-source satellite datasets, subjected to pre-processing and harmonization by way of the Google Earth Engine (GEE) environment, to analyze spatiotemporal patterns of interaction between vegetation and climate parameters. The following are the workflow phases: (1) satellite and climate dataset acquisition, (2) preprocessing and data quality assurance, (3) computations of vegetation and climatic indices, and (4) spatial-statistical and geostatistical analysis.The methodological approach adopted is summarized in a diagrammatic view, Fig. 1, where the stepwise elucidation leads from data collection to spatial interpretation of vegetation-climate sensitivity26,27.

Study area

Guilan province is situated in the northernmost point of the country of Iran, on the southern foothills bordering the Caspian Sea. The province covers an area of close to 14,000 km², along the latitudes of 36°33’−38°27’N and longitudes of 48°32’−50°36’E. Humid subtropical climate prevails with annual precipitation that usually exceeds 1,200 mm, with extremely high spatial gradients according to the topographic standpoint, from coastal plains to the foothills of the Alborz mountain range. Although Guilan Province is climatically characterized by high long-term mean annual precipitation, which commonly exceeds 1,200 mm based on multi-decadal meteorological records, this value represents a regional climatological average rather than the precipitation totals used in the present analysis. The varying climate gives rise to several microclimatic zones, which together with surface energy fluxes determine ultimately vegetation responses. Urbanization, deforestation, and agricultural intensification have increased sharply over recent decades, and with such land use changes, the conditions in the area face an increased vulnerability to climate variability28.

In this study, annual precipitation values were derived from the CHIRPS dataset and represent spatially averaged and temporally aggregated precipitation totals calculated specifically for the defined analysis period and spatial units. Therefore, the precipitation values reported in Table 1 reflect processed annual aggregates within the study framework and are not directly comparable to long-term climatological normals29,17.

Data sources

To investigate vegetation–climate interactions in Guilan Province, Iran, during the period 2020–2024, this study employed multi-source satellite and climate datasets processed entirely within the Google Earth Engine (GEE) cloud-based platform. Vegetation conditions were characterized using the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI), while thermal conditions were represented by daytime Land Surface Temperature (LST). Precipitation variability was incorporated using CHIRPS rainfall data to capture hydrological forcing33,34.

NDVI and EVI were derived from atmospherically corrected Landsat 8 surface reflectance data (LANDSAT/LC08/C02/T1_L2) at a native spatial resolution of 30 m. NDVI was calculated using red (Band 4) and near-infrared (Band 5) reflectance, whereas EVI incorporated blue (Band 2), red, and near-infrared bands to reduce atmospheric and soil background effects, particularly in dense vegetation regions3; Shammi, S. A., & Meng, 2021). All valid images within each year were cloud-masked and aggregated to annual mean composites to reduce noise and enhance interannual comparability35.

Land surface temperature data were obtained from the MODIS Terra MOD11A2 Version 6.1 product, which provides 8-day composite daytime LST at a spatial resolution of 1 km36. Raw digital values were converted from Kelvin to degrees Celsius using the standard scale factor (0.02) and offset (− 273.15), following official MODIS documentation37. Annual mean LST maps were generated for each year of the study period.

Precipitation data were sourced from the Climate Hazards Group InfraRed Precipitation with Station data (CHIRPS/DAILY), offering quasi-global rainfall estimates at approximately 5 km spatial resolution. Daily precipitation values were aggregated to monthly and annual totals to align with vegetation and temperature datasets. All datasets were spatially clipped to the administrative boundary of Guilan Province using a vector shapefile uploaded to GEE. A summary of the datasets is presented in Table 2.

Annual precipitation was calculated using the CHIRPS daily precipitation dataset by summing daily rainfall values over each calendar year for the period 2020–2024. The resulting annual precipitation layers were then spatially averaged across the study area (or grid-based analysis units) to obtain representative annual precipitation values. Consequently, the reported annual precipitation values correspond to spatially aggregated precipitation within the analytical framework rather than station-based or long-term climatological precipitation totals.

All raster datasets were resampled to a common spatial resolution of 1 km × 1 km using bilinear interpolation, aligned to the MODIS MOD11A2 grid, to ensure spatial consistency across vegetation, land surface temperature, and precipitation variables.

To analyze the spatial distribution and temporal variability of NDVI and EVI, both indices were classified into discrete categories representing distinct vegetation conditions. Classification of NDVI and EVI values was performed using the Natural Breaks (Jenks) method, which identifies groupings and patterns inherent in the data by minimizing within-class variance and maximizing between-class variance. The Jenks algorithm has been widely applied in vegetation and land cover studies to differentiate between vegetation density classes and to reduce classification bias caused by uneven data distribution (Jenks, 196338,39.

The classification procedure was implemented on annual and seasonal composite NDVI/EVI layers in Google Earth Engine, generating classes such as very low, low, moderate, high, and very high greenness based on the distribution of index values. This categorical approach facilitates comparing vegetation status across time and space, while enabling further analyses such as trend assessment and spatial clustering. Similar classification schemes have been used in multiple studies to interpret vegetation dynamics, including global assessments of greenness trends (Vidican, 2023) and regional analyses of vegetative response to climatic drivers5,40.

Data preprocessing and quality assurance

Given the heterogeneous spatial resolutions of the datasets (30 m for Landsat, 1 km for MODIS LST, and ~ 5 km for CHIRPS), a harmonization procedure was applied to ensure analytical consistency. All vegetation indices derived from Landsat imagery were resampled to a common spatial resolution of 1 km using bilinear interpolation to match the MODIS LST grid. CHIRPS precipitation data were also resampled to the same 1 km resolution to maintain spatial alignment across variables. Consequently, all subsequent analyses and outputs were produced at a final spatial resolution of 1 km.

Cloud and cloud-shadow contamination in Landsat imagery were removed using the CFMask algorithm and the pixel_qa quality flags41. Temporal aggregation was performed using mean reducers to generate monthly and annual composites, minimizing short-term anomalies and emphasizing seasonal and interannual patterns. Non-vegetated surfaces were excluded by masking pixels with NDVI values below 0.1, effectively removing water bodies, bare land, and highly urbanized areas (Hassan et al., 2007). To assess data stability, the coefficient of variation (CV) was calculated for each pixel across the study period, and pixels with CV values exceeding 0.25 were flagged as unstable and excluded from further analysis.

Land Surface Temperature (LST) in this study was obtained from the MODIS MOD11A2 product, which provides 8-day composite observations at 1 km spatial resolution. Although higher-resolution LST products derived from Landsat-8 are available, MODIS was chosen due to its continuous temporal coverage, which allows consistent monitoring of interannual and seasonal variations over the study period. The 16-day revisit cycle of Landsat-8, combined with frequent cloud contamination in humid regions such as Guilan, can result in significant data gaps that may affect trend and sensitivity analyses.

MODIS LST also offers integrated quality assurance and cloud-masked composites, ensuring reliable retrievals over large and heterogeneous landscapes. Additionally, the MODIS dataset is fully compatible with Google Earth Engine (GEE), facilitating efficient processing over the entire provincial scale without excessive computational demands. For the purposes of this study, the 1 km resolution is sufficient to capture broad spatial patterns of temperature and its interaction with vegetation, while aggregation to 0.02° grid cells ensures compatibility with NDVI, EVI, and precipitation data for the calculation of the Vegetation–Climate Sensitivity Index (VCSI)43.

This approach provides a robust, reproducible framework for analyzing LST patterns in relation to vegetation dynamics at the provincial scale, balancing temporal continuity, spatial coverage, and computational efficiency44.

Vegetation–climate sensitivity index (VCSI)

This index is actually a composite one that entails the multiplication of the standardized vegetation and climatic parameters in the Vegetation-Climate Sensitivity Index (VCSI), which is an entity conceived in the quantification of the ecological response to climate variation considered conjunctively:

VCSI = ((NDVI_std + EVI_std)/2) × P_norm × (1 − LST_norm).

Where NDVI_std and EVI_std are the normalized indices of vegetation, and P_norm and LST_norm are the normalized values of precipitation and LST, respectively45.

Higher values of VCSI show that vegetation is under high moisture and low temperature stresses, i.e., resilient systems, while lower values of VCSI show that vegetation is under thermal or drought stress, i.e., vulnerable systems.

Statistical and spatial analyses

Vegetation–climate relationships were examined using a combination of statistical and spatial analytical techniques. Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients were employed to assess synchronous and lagged associations between vegetation indices (NDVI and EVI) and climatic variables (precipitation and LST) at monthly and seasonal scales5. Lagged correlations with delays of one to three months were applied to capture delayed vegetation responses to climatic forcing. Temporal trends in NDVI, EVI, and LST were quantified using linear regression slope estimates to identify long-term changes. Spatial autocorrelation of VCSI values was assessed using Global Moran’s I to detect overall clustering patterns, while Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) were used to identify localized hotspots and cold spots of vegetation–climate sensitivity (Anselin, 199546;. All analyses were implemented within the GEE JavaScript API, ensuring reproducibility and scalability.

Validation and uncertainty assessment

Due to the absence of field-based validation data, an indirect validation strategy was adopted. MODIS-derived LST trends were compared with CHIRPS/DAILY reanalysis temperature data to assess thermal consistency at regional scale. Temporal patterns of NDVI and EVI were cross-referenced with published studies conducted under similar climatic and ecological conditions. Additionally, coefficient of variation analysis was used to identify and exclude unstable pixels, reducing uncertainty associated with sensor noise and anomalous observations. Collectively, these procedures enhance confidence in the robustness and reliability of the vegetation–climate assessments for Guilan Province33.

Results

Spatio-temporal vegetation dynamics based on NDVI and EVI (2020–2024)

Spatio-temporal vegetation dynamics in Guilan Province from 2020 to 2024 were analyzed using the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and the Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) derived from Landsat 8 imagery. Vegetation cover was classified into five density classes following Sonawane et al. (2017), providing a consistent framework for comparing vegetation conditions across both moderate and high biomass environments.

NDVI-based spatial patterns indicate pronounced interannual variability in vegetation greenness across the province (Fig. 2). NDVI values ranged from − 0.49 to 0.16, reflecting substantial spatial heterogeneity driven by topography, land use, and climatic gradients. Vegetation conditions were relatively favorable in 2021, whereas a noticeable decline in greenness was observed in 2022 and again in 2024, particularly within medium and medium-to-dense vegetation classes. These reductions suggest heightened sensitivity of intermediate vegetation types to short-term climatic variability, land-use change, or anthropogenic disturbance.

A comparative assessment of the two indices underscores their complementary analytical roles. NDVI captures interannual fluctuations and transitions among low to medium vegetation classes more clearly, making it effective for detecting vegetation degradation, recovery, and land-cover change. EVI, by contrast, exhibits smoother temporal behavior and greater robustness in areas with dense canopy cover, where NDVI tends to saturate. Together, these indices provide a more comprehensive representation of vegetation dynamics than either index alone.

Temporal trends in province-wide mean NDVI and EVI values further illustrate these contrasts (Fig. 4). While both indices exhibit broadly synchronous interannual variations, NDVI shows greater amplitude in year-to-year change, whereas EVI demonstrates more gradual temporal variation. This divergence suggests that short-term disturbances and phenological shifts are more strongly reflected in NDVI, whereas EVI emphasizes structural stability and long-term vegetation condition.

Overall, the combined use of NDVI and EVI enables a robust assessment of spatio-temporal vegetation dynamics in Guilan Province, capturing both short-term variability and long-term persistence of vegetation cover. Spatiotemporal patterns of land surface temperature and precipitation in Guilan Province

The spatial distribution of daytime land surface temperature (LST) in Guilan Province exhibits clear and persistent geographical contrasts over the study period (Fig. 5). Higher LST values are predominantly concentrated in lowland and agricultural areas, particularly across the Rasht metropolitan region and central plains, while lower temperatures are consistently observed in forested and mountainous zones.

Annual spatial distribution of daytime land surface temperature (LST) in Guilan Province.

These spatial patterns indicate the strong moderating role of vegetation cover and topography on surface thermal regimes. Areas characterized by dense vegetation and higher elevations maintain lower surface temperatures, whereas zones with reduced vegetation cover are associated with pronounced thermal hotspots, especially during the summers of 2022 and 2023. The spatial correspondence between elevated LST and areas of declining NDVI and EVI underscores the close coupling between vegetation dynamics and surface energy balance in the region.

Spatial analysis of annual precipitation reveals marked geographical gradients across Guilan Province (Fig. 6). Correlation and lagged relationships

The NDVI and EVI showed a great positive correlation with r = 0.95, confirming the consistency of vegetation greenness indicators. Moderate to strong correlations exist between both vegetation indices and precipitation (NDVI with r = 0.88; EVI with r = 0.92), whereas negative correlations with LST are weak to moderate (r = − 0.62).

Lagged correlation results showed that the response of greenery to precipitation was delayed by about one or two months, which can be explained in terms of retention of moisture in the soil and physiognomic adaptation to seasonal climate variability. This lag supports Zhang et al.5, and Wu et al., and4, who found delayed vegetation recovery after rainfall peaks in humid temperate zones (Fig. 7).

Datasets were collected for NDVI, EVI, LST, and precipitation between the season years 2020–2024, which will later be represented in Table 6 below for CV (coefficient of variation) analyses. Some fluctuations occurred during this period in LST (CV range: 36–43%); while variability reaches the peak of precipitation in 2021 (CV = 49.81%) and sharply drops in 2023 (CV = 24.55%). Thus, in general, 2022 was the most unstable year of the environment under review in terms of the dynamics of the vegetation. The analysis indicates that this area is vulnerable to climate variations and argues for diversity. This coincided with a period of high instability in climatic factors, indicating maximum stress on the vegetation. In contrast, the highest annual mean was observed in 2024, with a value of 102.62, showing a significant relative improvement in vegetation-climate synergy compared to previous years. The years 2020 (84.28) and 2021 (72.26) experienced intermediate values, while 2023 (64.89) marked the beginning of an upward trend towards recovery (Table 4).

The spatial pattern of the VCSI in the study area demonstrates a significant heterogeneity in the vegetation’s response to climatic conditions. According to the spatial distribution maps, the western and northern regions, which are mainly covered by dense and semi-dense forests, experience high VCSI values. This indicates a stronger synergy and better adaptation of these ecosystems to climatic fluctuations, possibly due to higher moisture storage capacity, controlled evapotranspiration, and greater stability of these biomes.

In contrast, the lowest VCSI values are clearly concentrated in low-lying and flat areas used for agriculture, as well as in urban and industrial zones. This pattern suggests that agricultural crops, which depend on human management (irrigation, planting and growing cycles), and urban green spaces are more sensitive and vulnerable to climatic stresses (such as reduced rainfall or increased temperatures), showing less synergy with the climate. This spatial distinction highlights the critical role of land cover type in moderating or amplifying the effects of climate change on the ecosystem.

Spatial autocorrelation of the VCSI (2020–2024)

Global Moran’s I results indicate a strong positive spatial autocorrelation of vegetation–climate sensitivity index (VCSI) across Guilan Province for the years 2020–2024 with values between 0.94 and 0.96 (p < 0.001). In areas similar to one another with respect to vegetation–climate sensitivity, the spatial clustering rather than random scattering follows. The strongest autocorrelation is met in 2022 (I = 0.963) and marks a period of climatic stress, indicating that spatial differentiation between the resilient (high-VCSI) and vulnerable (low-VCSI) ecosystems was intensified. In general, the high values of Moran’s I can indicate a stable spatial structure of vegetation–climate interaction systems largely imposed by topography, climate gradients, and land-use patterns in Guilan Province (Table 6).

Across the study period, the Gi* hotspot–coldspot pattern of vegetation–climate sensitivity (VCSI) exhibited marked interannual variability (Table 7). In 2020, the spatial structure was moderately polarized, with highly significant cold spots (99% confidence) covering 24.67% of the area and hot spots at the same confidence level accounting for 34.66%, while 29.85% of the region remained non-significant. In 2021, spatial structuring intensified, as 99% cold spots expanded to 35.57% and non-significant areas contracted to 15.27%, accompanied by a slight increase in highly significant hot spots (38.80%). In 2022, cold spot dominance weakened, with 99% cold spots declining to 23.73% and non-significant areas increasing to 31.07%, whereas 99% hot spots remained elevated (37.67%), indicating sustained vegetation–climate sensitivity under thermal stress. The pattern shifted in 2023, when cold spot expansion occurred mainly at lower confidence levels (95% and 90% reaching 8.09% and 4.21%, respectively), while highly significant hot spots decreased sharply to 20.61%, reflecting reduced clustering intensity and partial spatial diffusion. By 2024, a more balanced spatial configuration emerged, with 99% cold spots accounting for 32.43%, hot spots recovering to 25.02%, and non-significant areas stabilizing at 28.54%, suggesting partial normalization of spatial patterns following earlier climatic stress.

Discussion

Vegetation Response to Climatic Variability

During 2022–2023, interannual declines in vegetation indices were observed, driven by elevated surface temperatures and reduced precipitation. These findings support the central hypothesis that hydroclimatic variability is a principal determinant of vegetation dynamics in humid subtropical ecosystems. Similar responses have been documented in other regions; for instance, Hosen et al.47, reported considerable reductions in vegetation greenness due to drought and heat stress in Bangladesh, while Zeydan et al.48, observed analogous patterns in Turkey.

The enhanced stability of EVI relative to NDVI underscores its ability to capture canopy dynamics in dense forested areas while mitigating atmospheric noise and soil background effects. Partial vegetation recovery in 2024 is likely associated with increased rainfall and adaptive regrowth following previous climatic stress events. These observations align with recent research advocating multi-dimensional composite indices, such as Safaei et al.49, and Safaei et al.50,, which capture environmental stress more comprehensively than single-variable metrics. Similarly, Darvishi51, highlights the importance of integrating thermal and hydrological stressors to understand vegetation resilience under variable climatic conditions.

Land Surface Temperature and Vegetation Coupling

A consistent negative correlation was observed between LST and vegetation indices, reflecting the biophysical feedback between vegetation cover and surface energy balance. Dense vegetative cover induced lower surface temperatures due to evapotranspiration, whereas deforested and urbanized areas exhibited higher LST values. These findings are consistent with urban heat island studies in Southeast Asia and China52,53.

Peaks in LST during 2023 coincided with declines in vegetation, indicating that lowland ecosystems are particularly vulnerable to combined heat and drought stress. These results are in agreement with Solaimani and Darvishi54, which emphasizes the amplified effect of LST extremes on vegetation performance in subtropical environments.

Vegetation–Climate Interactions and Lagged Responses

Vegetation responses demonstrated clear lag effects with respect to precipitation, reflecting the accumulation of soil moisture rather than instantaneous rainfall events. This hysteresis suggests that soil water-holding capacity is a critical determinant of ecosystem resilience under dry conditions. Nonlinear relationships between NDVI and climatic drivers further indicate the complex feedback mechanisms between vegetation and climate, which linear models cannot adequately capture.

These findings corroborate recent studies highlighting delayed vegetation responses to hydroclimatic variability (Guan et al., 2017; Sharma et al., 2022;55, and demonstrate the value of integrated spatiotemporal approaches for capturing ecosystem dynamics.

Insights from the Vegetation–Climate Sensitivity Index (VCSI)

The VCSI provides an integrated assessment of ecological vulnerability and resilience across Guilan Province. High VCSI values were concentrated in forested uplands, which maintained stable vegetation–climate coupling, whereas low VCSI values were identified in agricultural lowlands and urbanized areas, reflecting elevated climatic and anthropogenic stress.

Composite indices such as VCSI improve upon traditional single-variable metrics by incorporating thermal, hydrological, and vegetative components into a unified measure. Comparative studies support this approach; for example49,50; demonstrated that integrated indices capture complex environmental stress gradients more effectively than NDVI or EVI alone. Similarly, Darvishi51, emphasizes that multi-dimensional metrics enable a more precise identification of vulnerable ecosystems and guide targeted land management interventions.

Comparative Context and Methodological Contributions

The methodological framework developed in this study represents a substantial advancement over previous linear approaches to vegetation–climate interaction analysis in regional studies4,5. By integrating the Vegetation–Climate Sensitivity Index (VCSI) with spatial autocorrelation analysis using Moran’s I, the framework captures both temporal and spatial heterogeneity in ecosystem responses, providing a more nuanced understanding of ecological sensitivity and vulnerability patterns across multiple spatial scales.

This integrated, cloud-based framework is highly transferable to other humid and subtropical regions facing similar hydroclimatic pressures. Moreover, it offers a scalable platform for future ecosystem monitoring, allowing incorporation of additional environmental layers such as soil moisture, evapotranspiration, or land-use stressors. By combining multi-source datasets, lagged response analysis, and spatial statistics, the approach provides actionable insights for land management and climate adaptation planning, enhancing the precision and relevance of ecological vulnerability assessments.

Spatial clustering patterns and hotspot dynamics

Spatial analyses using Local Moran’s I and Getis-Ord Gi* statistics revealed a strong spatial organization of ecological sensitivity in Guilan. High–high clusters and hotspots were concentrated in the northern and western forested highlands, indicative of strong resilience under favorable hydroclimatic and topographic conditions. Low–low clusters and coldspots were primarily located in central and southeastern plains, where agricultural intensification and urbanization compounded vegetation-climate stress.

The persistence of spatial patterns observed in 2022, along with partial recovery of hotspots in 2024, demonstrates stability in the province’s ecological structure, emphasizing the need for place-centered adaptation policies to enhance vegetation resilience in vulnerable lowland zones.

Limitations and future research

Despite the robust methodological framework and the use of widely adopted satellite-derived indicators (NDVI, EVI, LST, and precipitation), several limitations should be acknowledged, which also open avenues for future research.

First, this study relied primarily on single-variable spectral indices to characterize vegetation condition and surface thermal dynamics. Although NDVI and EVI are well-established and effective indicators of vegetation greenness and productivity, they may not fully capture the complexity of ecosystem responses under heterogeneous climatic and land-use conditions. Recent studies have demonstrated that composite or multi-dimensional satellite indices, which integrate vegetation, thermal, moisture, and surface reflectance information into a single metric, can provide a more comprehensive representation of environmental stress and ecosystem functioning (e.g., composite ecological or environmental quality indices) (e.g5.,; Rahmati et al., 2024). The exclusion of such composite indices constitutes a limitation of the present study and may have constrained the depth of environmental interpretation.

Second, the analysis focused on annual and seasonal aggregation of satellite products at a 1 km spatial resolution. While this scale is appropriate for regional-level assessments, it may mask fine-scale spatial variability, particularly in fragmented landscapes and heterogeneous land-use systems. Future research could benefit from incorporating higher-resolution satellite data or multi-scale approaches to better capture localized vegetation–climate–temperature interactions.

Third, although strong spatial and temporal associations were identified among NDVI, EVI, LST, and precipitation, the study remains largely correlational. The absence of ground-based ecological or meteorological observations limits the ability to fully disentangle causal mechanisms underlying the observed patterns. Integrating in situ measurements, land-use data, and socio-environmental variables would strengthen causal inference and improve interpretation of land–atmosphere feedbacks.

Future research should therefore prioritize the integration of composite satellite indices that combine multiple biophysical dimensions, as highlighted in recent remote sensing literature5,49, and explicitly introduce such indices in the conceptual framework and introduction of related studies. Additionally, expanding the methodological design to include multi-source data fusion, higher temporal resolution analyses, and advanced spatial modeling techniques would substantially enhance the assessment of ecosystem sensitivity and land degradation risks under ongoing climate variability.

Overall, addressing these limitations through methodological refinement and conceptual expansion will improve the clarity, robustness, and scientific impact of future investigations into vegetation–climate interactions and environmental change in Guilan Province and comparable regions.

Conclusion

This study presents a spatiotemporally dynamic integration of vegetation and climate indicators across Guilan Province, Northern Iran, entirely operationalized through the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform. By combining NDVI, EVI, LST, and CHIRPS precipitation data, interannual and spatial variability in vegetation productivity was assessed for the period 2020–2024. The development and application of the Vegetation–Climate Sensitivity Index (VCSI) allowed identification of ecologically sensitive zones and the integrated response of vegetation to hydroclimatic stressors across heterogeneous landscapes and multiple spatial scales.

The results indicate that rainfall is the primary driver of vegetation health and density, whereas LST exhibits an inverse relationship with vegetation indices. The year 2022 represented the most severe climatic stress, with low precipitation, elevated LST, and reduced vegetation quality, while partial recovery in 2024 highlights ecosystem resilience under favorable hydroclimatic conditions. These patterns underscore the delayed response of vegetation to soil moisture availability and canopy adjustments within humid subtropical systems, emphasizing the importance of multi-temporal and multi-index assessments.

The methodological contributions of this study include the operationalization of the VCSI, which integrates thermal, hydrological, and vegetative components into a comprehensive measure of ecological sensitivity, overcoming limitations of single-index approaches. Spatial autocorrelation analysis using Moran’s I identifies clustering and spatial heterogeneity in ecosystem responses, highlighting hotspots of resilience and vulnerability. Furthermore, the replicable cloud-based analytical workflow enables scalable, multi-temporal environmental assessments that can be adapted to other humid and subtropical regions for ecosystem monitoring.

From a theoretical and practical perspective, several implications emerge. GEE-based multi-source analyses allow near real-time monitoring of vegetation stress, drought, and land degradation, supporting climate adaptation strategies at regional scales. Prioritization of low VCSI areas for adaptive interventions -including reforestation, soil moisture conservation, and water-efficient agricultural practices- is critical, particularly in agricultural and urban lowland zones. The strong link between vegetation and temperature highlights the importance of preserving green infrastructure in rapidly urbanizing cities such as Rasht to mitigate urban heat island effects and regulate local climate. The spatially explicit VCSI framework also informs provincial-level land-use zoning, flood management, and biodiversity conservation by mapping at-risk ecosystems and guiding targeted management interventions.

While the current study effectively demonstrates the value of integrated, multi-source satellite data for ecological assessments, future research should incorporate higher-resolution datasets (e.g., Sentinel-2, CHIRPS/DAILY) and advanced modeling approaches, including machine learning, to enhance predictive capacity and long-term evaluation of ecosystem resilience under alternative climate scenarios.

Overall, this study provides a robust, transferable, and data-driven framework for assessing vegetation–climate interactions, offering a model for sustainable land and climate governance in Northern Iran and other climatologically sensitive regions worldwide. It demonstrates that systematic integration of multi-source satellite data in a cloud-based environment can significantly improve both accuracy and spatial resolution in ecological monitoring, thereby supporting evidence-based environmental management and policy-making.
