Cut-off phenomenon and asymptotic mixing for multivariate general linear processes
Gerardo Barrera, Michael A. Högele, Pauliina Ilmonen, Lauri Viitasaari

Abstract
. The small noise cut-off phenomenon in continuous time and space has been studied in the recent literature for the linear and non-linear stable Langevin dynamics with additive Lévy drivers - understood as abrupt thermalization of the system along a particular time scale to its dynamical equilibrium - both for the total variation distance and the Wasserstein distance. The main result of this article establishes sufficient conditions for the window and profile cut-off phenomenon, which are flexible enough to cover the renormalized (non-Markovian) Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process driven by fractional Brownian motion and a large class of Gaussian and non-Gaussian, homogeneous and non-homogeneous drivers with (possible) finite second moments. The sufficient conditions are stated both for the total variation distance and the Wasserstein distance. Important examples are the multidimensional fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, the empirical sampling process of a fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes driven by an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process and the inhomogeneous Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process arising in simulated annealing.

Introduction

The cut-off phenomenon - also known as abrupt convergence - describes an ergodic threshold phenomenon for a parameter dependent stochastic processes,
and goes back to the studies by D. Aldous, P. Diaconis and M. Shahshahani at the beginning of the 1980ies on strong stopping times of random walks on groups and card shuffling Markov models, see

Essentially, it describes the asymptotically ever sharper decay of the distance between the current state of a stochastic process and its limiting dynamical equilibrium along a specific time scale.
Recently, the cut-off phenomenon has been studied in different applied context including: stochastic simulations of chemical kinetics in
[
25
]
,
quantum Markov chains in
[

,
coagulation-fragmentation systems (Becker–Döring equations) in
[

,

[

deep limits for neural networks in
[

[

, and
viscous energy shell model in
[

In the sequel, let
\varepsilon>0
denote a complexity parameter.
For a given initial datum
x
we consider a continuous time stochastic process

X^{\varepsilon}:=(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x))_{t\geq 0}

which possesses a limiting measure
\mu^{\varepsilon}
as
t\to\infty
, and a renormalized family of distances or discrepancies
d=(d_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
on the set of probability measures over the respective state space of
X^{\varepsilon}
.
Then the family of processes
(X^{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon>0}
is said to exhibit a profile cut-off phenomenon if there exist a cut-off time scale
t_{\varepsilon}:=t_{\varepsilon}(x)
and time window
w_{\varepsilon}:=w_{\varepsilon}(x)>0
satisfying
t_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
,
w_{\varepsilon}=\textrm{o}(t_{\varepsilon})
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, and

(1.1)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}d_{\varepsilon}\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}(x),\mu^{\varepsilon}\right)=\mathcal{P}_{x}(r)\quad\textrm{ for any }\quad r\in\mathbb{R},

where
\mathcal{P}_{x}:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
is a continuous
function - the so-called
cut-off profile - such that

(1.2)

0=\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{x}(\rho)\leq\mathcal{P}_{x}(r)\leq\lim_{\rho\to-\infty}\mathcal{P}_{x}(\rho)=\mbox{diam}(d)\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad r\in\mathbb{R},

where
\mbox{diam}(d):=\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mbox{diam}(d_{\varepsilon})\in(0,\infty]
, with
\mbox{diam}(d_{\varepsilon})
denoting the diameter with respect to
d_{\varepsilon}
.

1.1

For an introduction to the field we refer to

In continuous state spaces and continuous time, the cut-off phenomenon was first studied for the small noise limit of
linear and non-linear multivariate overdamped Langevin equation driven by small Brownian motion

For more general Lévy drivers (including degenerate Brownian motion) the cut-off phenomenon has been established for finite and infinite dimensional stable systems, see

the use of the renormalized Wasserstein distance, which does not depend on this regularity, allows to include degenerate physical systems such as Jacobi chains of oscillators with isolated heat bath

All these systems have in common that due to the essentially Markovian structure combined with some compactness/tightness properties of the process, they exhibit a convergence to a dynamical equilibrium. To our knowledge the cut-off phenomenon of a fractional Brownian motion driver and hence continuous-time non-Markovian systems has not been established to date.
By means of specific examples and using coupling techniques it is argued in
[
38

that the cut-off phenomenon is also valid even for systems without any Markovian structure.
The novelty of this article is to establish the cut-off phenomenon for a large class of families of non-Markovian processes with a stable linear component.

One of the simplest such systems is a small noise Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process driven by fractional Brownian motion, introduced and studied in
[
28
]

[
49

and the references therein. On top of the case of the fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, we consider generalized Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes driven by non-Markovian and non-Gaussian drivers. These cover essentially all stationary processes in continuous time,
see
[

for the extension to multivariate setting. For reader’s convenience, in Section

More generally, our main results given in Section

state the following.
For a general class of multivariate linear processes
(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x))_{t\geq 0}
such that for each

satisfies

(1.3)

where
\varepsilon>0

is Routh–Hurwitz stable and

,

In addition, for

being the total variation distance or the Wasserstein distance of order

\lim_{t\to\infty}d_{0}\left(\frac{S_{t}}{\sigma_{t}},Z\right)=0

for some sub-exponential (possibly constant) deterministic scale
(\sigma_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and a random variable
Z
taking values on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We aim to show the cut-off phenomenon in the sense of the existence of some cut-off time scale
t_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0

w_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{o}(t_{\varepsilon})
as
\varepsilon\to 0

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}d_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{X_{t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}(x)}{\sigma_{t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}},Z\right)=\mathcal{P}_{x}(r)\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad r\in\mathbb{R}

for

d_{\varepsilon}
being the total variation distance or the
(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\varepsilon}})-
renormalized Wasserstein distance of order
p\geq 1
.
In Section
4
, we showcase the applicability of our results by giving several new examples that have not yet been considered in the literature. In addition, if
S_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}\mathrm{d}D_{s}
and
(D_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a Brownian motion or a discontinuous Lévy process, we recover some important known cases. Our new examples show that the empirical sample mean process of a sequence of fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes exhibits a cut-off phenomenon as the sample size increases. We also show that Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes with general stationary increment drivers in
L^{2}
and the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process driven by another centered Gaussian Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process can be treated by applying our results. Moreover, we apply the results to the case of inhomogeneous stochastic convolution
S
that arise naturally in simulated annealing context. Finally, we treat the case of the non-centered Ornstein–Uhlenbeck drivers by considering a particular nontrivial rescaling of the process
S
. These examples illustrate the wide generality of our results.

While it comes not as a surprise that different distances measure different details of discrepancy, we stress two distinctive features of the total variation distance and the Wasserstein distance:
The total variation distance enjoys the highly non-linear, zero-homogeneity structure that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(cX,cY)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(X,Y)
for any
c\neq 0
(Lemma
B.1
(ii)).
Even the linear shifts of a random vector by a sequence of random vectors with discrete laws which converge in distribution are in general discontinuous under the total variation
distance
[
14
, Lemma 1.17]
. Therefore, its topology is finer than the one by convergence in distribution.
In our main results it therefore captures certain features of the driving noise in the cut-off profile such as the variance, see
formula (
2.12
). As expected, due to the zero-homogeneity no renormalization of the process
S
is needed. On the other hand, the Wasserstein distance, is more aligned with the linear structure via the so-called shift-additivity property,
\mathcal{W}_{p}(v+X,X)=\|v\|
,
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
deterministic vector and
p\geq 1
,
see Lemma
C.1
(iii). Here the particular features of the noise essentially cancel out and the profile is given by the norm of the matrix exponential of our linear process, which implies that the cut-off profile is universal in the sense that it does not retain any properties of the driving noise but only of the deterministic linear part. Moreover, the distance has to be renormalized by the noise intensity
\varepsilon
in order to exhibit a cut-off phenomenon.

B
and Appendix

we gather the most relevant properties of the total variation and the Wasserstein distance.

2.
Univariate fractional
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

In this section we explain our main results for reader’s convenience in the paradigmatic example of the univariate fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.
For a given
\varepsilon>0
, let
(X^{\varepsilon,H}_{t}(x))_{t\geq 0}
be the solution of the
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process driven by fractional Brownian motion (for short OUfBM)
given by

(2.1)

\mathrm{d}X^{\varepsilon,H}_{t}=-\lambda X^{\varepsilon,H}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\varepsilon\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{t}\quad\textrm{ for }\quad t\geq 0,\quad\textrm{ and }\quad X^{\varepsilon,H}_{0}=x,

where
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
x\neq 0
, is a deterministic initial datum,

\lambda>0
, and the driven noise

(B^{H}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a fractional Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}
with Hurst exponent

H\in(0,1)
.
We recall that
(B^{H}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a continuous-time zero-mean Gaussian process starting at zero almost surely, having stationary increments, and whose covariance function is given by

(2.2)

\mathbb{E}\left[B^{H}_{s}B^{H}_{t}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left(t^{2H}+s^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H}\right)\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad s,\,t\geq 0.

For
H=1/2
the classical Brownian motion is recovered and then its increments are independent. However, for
H\neq 1/2
, the increments of
(B^{H}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are not independent.
By the variation of constants formula we have the path-wise solution of (
2.1
)

X^{\varepsilon,H}_{t}(x)=e^{-\lambda t}x+\varepsilon e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{s}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad t\geq 0.

We define the stationary process
U^{H}:=(U^{H}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
by

U^{H}_{t}:=e^{-\lambda t}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{s}\quad\textrm{ for }\quad t\geq 0.

Note that
U^{H}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}U^{H}_{0}
, where

\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution, and that
U^{H}_{0}
has mean zero.
Then we denote the covariance of
U^{H}
by
\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(t)
, that is,

\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(t)=\mathbb{E}[U^{H}_{0}U^{H}_{t}]
,
t\geq 0
.
We then have the following representation of the solution

(2.3)

\begin{split}X^{\varepsilon,H}_{t}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}&e^{-\lambda t}(x-\varepsilon U^{H}_{0})+\varepsilon U^{H}_{t}=e^{-\lambda t}x+\varepsilon\left(U^{H}_{t}-e^{-\lambda t}U^{H}_{0}\right)\quad\textrm{ for }\quad t\geq 0.\end{split}

For
m\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma^{2}>0
we denote the Normal distribution with mean
m
and variance
\sigma^{2}
by

\mathcal{N}(m,\sigma^{2})
.

Lemma 2.1

(Ergodicity)
.

Let
H\in(0,1)
be fixed.
For all
t>0
, the random variable
X^{\varepsilon,H}_{t}(x)
has Normal distribution with mean

(2.4)

m_{t}(x):=e^{-\lambda t}x

and variance

(2.5)

\sigma^{2}(t;\varepsilon,H)=\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)+e^{-2\lambda t}\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)-2e^{-\lambda t}\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(t)\right),

where

(2.6)

\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)=\mathbb{E}[U^{2}_{0}]=\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda s}s^{2H}\mathrm{d}s=\frac{\lambda^{-2H}\Gamma(2H+1)}{2},

and
\Gamma
denotes the usual Gamma function.
Moreover, as
t\to\infty
the law of the random variable
X^{\varepsilon,H}_{t}(x)
converges (in the total variation distance and in the Wasserstein distance of any order
p\geq 1
) to a random variable

X^{\varepsilon,H}_{\infty}
that has Normal distribution with zero mean and variance
\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)
.

In particular, for
H=1/2
it follows that

\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)=1/(2\lambda)
and

\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(t)=\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)e^{-\lambda t}
,
t\geq 0
, while for
H\neq 1/2
the autocorrelation function

\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(t)
is given as special function,
see Theorem 2.3 in
[
28
]
, which has behavior

\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(t)\sim t^{2H-2}
as
t
tends to infinity.

In what follows, we study the sharp behavior of the discrepancy

(2.7)

\mathrm{d}^{\varepsilon,H}_{\mathrm{TV}}(t;x):=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X^{\varepsilon,H}_{t}(x),X^{\varepsilon,H}_{\infty}\right),\quad t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R},

where
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the total variation distance. We refer to Appendix
B
for the definition and basic properties of the total variation distance.
The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 2.2

(Cut-off convergence in the total variation distance)
.

Let
H\in(0,1)
be fixed and
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
x\neq 0
, be fixed.
For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)

define

(2.8)

t_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right).

Then for any
(w_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}
such that
w_{\varepsilon}\to w
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
with

it follows that

(2.9)

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{d}^{\varepsilon,H}_{\mathrm{TV}}(t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon};x)=\mathcal{P}^{H}_{\mathrm{TV}}(r),\quad r\in\mathbb{R},

where

(2.10)

\mathcal{P}^{H}_{\mathrm{TV}}(r):=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(e^{-\lambda rw}x,\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)\right),\,\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)\right)\right),\quad r\in\mathbb{R}.

In addition, for
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
the following limits holds true:

(2.11)

\lim\limits_{r\to-\infty}\mathcal{P}^{H}_{\mathrm{TV}}(r)=1\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\lim\limits_{r\to\infty}\mathcal{P}^{H}_{\mathrm{TV}}(r)=0.

Remark 2.3

(Total variation profile in terms of the error function)
.

Using Item (i) in Lemma B.2 in Appendix B of
[
19
]

and Item (ii) in Lemma
B.1

we obtain that, for every

,

(2.12)

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}^{H}_{\mathrm{TV}}(r)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mathcal{R}_{U}(0)}}e^{-\lambda rw}|x|}e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}\mathrm{d}y,\end{split}

which with the help of Dominated Convergence Theorem yields (

Proof of Theorem
2.2
(Cut-off convergence in the total variation distance).

By Lemma
2.1
and Item (ii) in Lemma
B.1

we have

(2.13)

\begin{split}\mathrm{d}^{\varepsilon,H}_{\mathrm{TV}}(t;x)&=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X^{\varepsilon,H}_{t}(x),X^{\varepsilon,H}_{\infty}\right)\\
&=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(e^{-\lambda t}x,\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)+e^{-2\lambda t}\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)-2e^{-\lambda t}\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(t)\right)\right),\,\mathcal{N}\left(0,\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)\right)\right)\\

The preceding equality with the help of Lemma B.4 in Appendix B of
[

∎

\mathrm{d}^{\varepsilon,H}_{\mathcal{W}_{p}}(t;x):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{W}_{p}\left(X^{\varepsilon,H}_{t}(x),X^{\varepsilon,H}_{\infty}\right),\quad t\geq 0

,

)
.

Let

be fixed and
x\in\mathbb{R}
be fixed.
For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)

define

as in (

).
Assume that
p\geq 1
.
For any
(w_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}
such that

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{d}^{\varepsilon,H}_{\mathcal{W}_{p}}(t_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon};x)=\mathcal{P}^{H}_{\mathcal{W}_{p}}(r),\quad r\in\mathbb{R},

\mathcal{P}^{H}_{\mathcal{W}_{p}}(r):=\mathcal{W}_{p}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(e^{-\lambda rw}x,\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)\right),\,\mathcal{N}\left(0,\mathcal{R}_{U^{H}}(0)\right)\right),\quad r\in\mathbb{R}.

(Wasserstein profile for

3.
Cut-off convergence for general linear processes in law

In this section, we establish the small noise cut-off phenomenon in the total variation distance and
in the Wassertein distance of order
p\geq 1

for general linear process in law of the type (
3.1
) given below.
In the sequel,
m
denotes a fixed positive integer,

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard inner product for
\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
and

\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
Moreover, we always assume that
-\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is
a Routh–Hurwitz matrix. That is, all the eigenvalues of
-\Lambda
have negative real parts.
For each
\varepsilon>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

we consider the following linear continuous-time stochastic process
X^{\varepsilon,x}:=(X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x))_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
in law defined by

(3.1)

X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}e^{-\Lambda t}x+\varepsilon S_{t}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad t\geq 0,

where
S=(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an arbitrary continuous-time stochastic process taking values on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and satisfying
\mathbb{E}[S_{t}]=0_{m}
for all
t\geq 0
, with
0_{m}
denoting the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
For classes of examples of processes
S
we refer to Section
4
.
We observe that

\mathbb{E}[X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)]=e^{-\Lambda t}x
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
t\geq 0
.
For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0_{m}\}
we study the
asymptotic behavior of
e^{-\Lambda t}x
for
t\gg 1
.

Lemma 3.1

(Hartman–Grobman asymptotics)
.

Let
-\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
be a Routh–Hurwitz matrix. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0_{m}\}
there exist

(1)

a positive rate

,

(2)

size and multiplicity parameters

such that

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\left\|\frac{e^{\lambda t}}{t^{\ell-1}}e^{-\Lambda t}x-v(t;x)\right\|=0,

where

(3.3)

v(t;x):=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{*}}e^{\mathsf{i}\theta_{j}t}v_{j}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad t\geq 0.

Moreover, the following holds true:

(3.4)

0<\liminf\limits_{t\to\infty}\|v(t;x)\|\leq\limsup\limits_{t\to\infty}\|v(t;x)\|<\infty.

A version of this lemma is established as Lemma B.1 in Appendix B of

[

]
, the result was proven under an additional coercivity assumption for
\Lambda
. That is, in
[
18

it is assumed that there exists

for all

.
However, inspecting the proof line by line one can easily see that the authors only use the fact that the matrix

is a Routh–Hurwitz matrix.

Lemma

states roughly that the rate of the long-term dynamics is determined by
\lambda
, the total number and the size (in the sense of geometric multiplicity) of those Jordan blocks of
\Lambda
with eigenvalues which have precisely the (smallest) real part

, while the bounded limiting object is determined exclusively by the angular velocities, that is the imaginary parts of the aforementioned eigenvalues.
Also, we emphasize that the angular velocities
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m_{*}}
take values in
\mathbb{R}
instead of taking values on
[0,2\pi)
, due to the fact that the map
t\mapsto e^{\mathsf{i}\theta t}
is not

-periodic for
\theta\not\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Compared to the results given in
[
18
]
, the present setting of Lemma

allows to include more examples of interest such as Jacobi chains of oscillators, the Brownian gyrator, or more general linear stable networks, see for instance
[

11
]

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0_{m}\}

we define the

-limit set of

given in (

) by

\begin{split}\omega(x):&=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\textrm{$v$ is an accumulation point of }(v(t;x))_{t\geq 0}\right\},\end{split}

that is,
v\in\omega(x)
if and only if
there exists a sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

as

.
Note that

\omega(x)\neq\emptyset

and the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem.
Moreover,

due to the lower bound in (

).
We point out that for

, we have

,

,
\theta_{1}=\ldots\theta_{m_{*}}=0
,
\lambda=\lambda_{1}
and

.

In the sequel, we define the cut-off time scale.
Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0_{m}\}
be fixed.
For any

set

\begin{split}t^{*}_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)\quad\textrm{ and then define }\quad t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}:=t^{*}_{\varepsilon}+\left(\frac{\ell-1}{\lambda}\right)\ln(\lambda t^{*}_{\varepsilon})-\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\left(\sigma_{t^{*}_{\varepsilon}}\right),\end{split}

where the

3.1.
Multivariate cut-off phenomenon in the total variation distance

The first main result in this subsection states sufficient conditions for the window cut-off in the total variation distance.

Theorem 3.2

(Window cut-off convergence in the total variation distance)
.

Assume that there exists a deterministic function
\sigma:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
and a random vector
Z
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}

satisfying

(3.9)

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\frac{S_{t}}{\sigma_{t}},Z\right)=0.

In addition, assume that
for any
r>0
it follows that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\sigma_{t+r}}=1
.
Then for any
(w_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}
such that
w_{\varepsilon}\to w
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
with
w>0
it follows that

(3.10)

\begin{split}\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\frac{X^{\varepsilon}_{t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}(x)}{\varepsilon\sigma_{t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}},Z\right)&=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\lambda^{1-\ell}e^{-\lambda rw}\check{v}(x)+Z,Z\right),\\
\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\frac{X^{\varepsilon}_{t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}(x)}{\varepsilon\sigma_{t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}},Z\right)&=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\lambda^{1-\ell}e^{-\lambda rw}\hat{v}(x)+Z,Z\right)\end{split}

for some
\check{v}(x),\hat{v}(x)\in\omega(x)
, where
\omega(x)
is given in (
3.5
) and
t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}

is defined in (
3.8
).
In addition, the following limits holds true:

(3.11)

\begin{split}\lim_{r\to-\infty}\liminf\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\frac{X^{\varepsilon}_{t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}(x)}{\varepsilon\sigma_{t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}},Z\right)&=1,\\
\lim_{r\to\infty}\limsup\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\frac{X^{\varepsilon}_{t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}(x)}{\varepsilon\sigma_{t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}},Z\right)&=0.\end{split}

Remark 3.3

(Growth of the function
\sigma=(\sigma_{t})_{t\geq 0}
at infinity)
.

Since for any
r>0
we assume
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\sigma(t+r)}{\sigma(t)}=1
, it is not hard to see that
\sigma\circ\ln
is a slowly varying function at infinity.
By the Karamata Representation Theorem
we have that

\sigma_{t}=\mathrm{o}(e^{ct})
as

for any
c>0
.

The second main result in this subsection states a characterization of profile cut-off in the total variation distance in terms of the geometric shape of the
\omega
-limit set
\omega(x)
.

Theorem 3.4

(Profile cut-off convergence in the total variation distance)
.

Suppose the assumptions of Theorem
3.2
hold true and recall the notation.
Then the following statements are equivalent. The limit

(3.12)

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\frac{X^{\varepsilon}_{t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}(x)}{\varepsilon\sigma_{t^{\mathsf{cut}}_{\varepsilon}+r\cdot w_{\varepsilon}}},Z\right)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\lambda^{1-\ell}e^{-\lambda rw}v(x)+Z,Z\right)\quad\textrm{ holds true}

for any element

,
if and only if, for any fixed positive
\rho
the map

(3.13)

\omega(x)\ni v\mapsto\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\rho v+Z,Z)\quad\textrm{ is constant.}

4.
Examples

4.1.
Linear equations and convolution processes
S

Let
D:=(D_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous-time stochastic process on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
(a.k.a. the driver process) such that the stochastic convolution
S:=(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
given by

(4.1)

S_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}\mathrm{d}D_{s}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad t\geq 0,

is component-wise well-defined (in an appropriate sense). Since for all
t\geq 0
the components of the map
s\mapsto e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}
are of bounded variation, it is well-known that the integral
in (
4.1
) is well-defined provided that the noise
D
has a continuous version. We also note that by using the smoothness of
s\mapsto e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}
, the integral can be defined through integration by parts formula. That is,

S_{t}=D_{t}-e^{-\Lambda t}D_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}D_{s}\mathrm{d}s,

where the integral is just the classical Lebesgue integral. Finally, note that if
D
has jumps, one can always consider the continuous part of
D
and the jumps separately.
Note that throughout the article we assume that

\mathbb{E}[S_{t}]=0_{m}
for all
t\geq 0
, but more general cases can be handled by shifting with
\mathbb{E}[D_{t}]
(and having suitable properties for the mean function).

4.2.
Recovering some existing results

Recently, the cut-off phenomenon has been established for linear and non-linear Langevin equations
for
\varepsilon
-small Brownian and Lévy noise in total variation distance and the Wasserstein-
p
distance in a series of articles
[
17
,
18
,
20
,
14
,
11
,
15
,
13
,
16
]
.
We briefly review how the window cut-off results in those papers can be recovered for linear drift (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck type processes) by the verification of
Condition (
3.9
) in
Theorem
3.4
and Condition (
3.14
) in Theorem
3.5
.
In
[
17
]
the authors establish the window/profile cut-off phenomenon for scalar non-linear Langevin equations driven by small Brownian motion in the total variation distance via a linearization procedure, Girsanov’s Theorem, and explicit Gaussian formulas for the invariant measure. As a by product, profile cut-off is shown for the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process in total variation due to explicit formulas for the total variation.
In
[
18
]
these results are generalized to the multivariate Langevin dynamics with coercive non-linearity and additive non-degenerate Brownian motion.
The proof method boils down to an even more sophisticated linearization procedure, at the core of which, the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process exhibits a window cut-off phenomenon in total variation distance, which then allows an asymptotic expansion of the limiting measure in
\varepsilon
by explicit formulas for the total variation distance between multivariate normal distributions.
In
[
20
]
the authors study the multivariate Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process driven by a
\varepsilon
-small non-degenerate Lévy process. Here several new features emerge. Note that for
D_{t}=L_{t}
a Lévy process, we have by path-wise integration by parts and the fact that stationarity and the independence of the increments imply that the reflected process

\widetilde{L}_{r}=L_{t}-L_{t-r}
has the same finite-dimensional laws as
L_{r}
, that

\displaystyle S_{t}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\Lambda u}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{L}_{u}.

This can be carried out by standard calculations, see for
instance
[
10
, Formula (2.11)]
. Consequently,
Condition (
3.9
) reads for
\sigma_{t}\equiv 1
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(S_{t},Z)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\Lambda u}\mathrm{d}L_{u},\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\Lambda u}\mathrm{d}L_{u}\right),

which is satisfied whenever the laws of
S_{t}
and
Z
are absolutely continuous w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure. However, the smoothness of
Z
is not always guaranteed for (possibly degenerate) Lévy drivers, since the total variation distance is in general discontinuous under discrete approximations of absolutely continuous laws, see
[
14
, Lemma 1.17]
. Consequently, the authors code sufficient conditions for the underlying smoothness assumption in Condition (H) there, which is stated in terms of the integrability of the characteristic function
\widehat{S}_{t}
of
S_{t}
and the uniform convergence of the
tail integrals
\int_{|\lambda|>R}|\widehat{S}_{t}(\lambda)|\mathrm{d}\lambda
to
0
as
R\to\infty
.
In
[
14
]
these results have been established in a long case study to non-linear Langevin equations driven by a class of Lévy process of infinite intensity, called strongly locally layered stable processes, satisfying a so-called equator condition. However, the rather strong condition of the pole at the origin of the Lévy measure of order
\alpha>\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}
, which appears as a sufficient condition to ensure the respective smoothness, seems difficult to verify.

On the other hand, for a Lévy driver
D=L
Condition (
3.14
) for the Wasserstein-
p
distance is easier to establish, even in the case of a degenerate dispersion matrix in front of the driver, since it only depends on the moments of
X^{\varepsilon}
, which coincide with those of
L
and do not vary over time.
A particular role is played by the recently discovered shift-additivity property
[
11
, Lemma 2.2]
of the Wasserstein-
p
distance, since it reduces the complexity of calculations considerably.
As a consequence, in
[
11
, Subsection 4.2.4]
the authors establish window cut-off for the degenerate system of a linear oscillator under subcritical damping and for linear chains of oscillators with heat-baths at outer extremes. These results are generalized to linear infinite dimensional systems, like the heat and the damped wave equation
in
[
13
]
, and very recently to energy shell lattice models
in
[
16
]
. In
[
15
]
the authors show how to generalize the preceding results to non-linear Langevin equations with strongly coercive drift in the Wasserstein-
p
distance.

4.3.
Average process from sampling a fractional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes

For a given
N\in\mathbb{N}
we consider an i.i.d. sampling array of processes

(X^{H,j})_{j=1,\ldots,N}
. More precisely, each

X^{H,j}=(X^{H,j}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is an independent copy of

X^{H}_{t}(x)=e^{-\lambda t}x+e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\mathrm{d}B^{H}_{s}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad t\geq 0,

where
(B^{H}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are fractional Brownian motions with fixed Hurst index
H\in(0,1)
.
We now consider the
average process
S^{H,N}:=(S^{H,N}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
given by
S^{H,N}_{t}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}X^{H,j}_{t}
,
t\geq 0
. We note that

\mathrm{d}S^{H,N}_{t}(x)=-\lambda S^{H,N}_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B^{H}_{s}}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad t\geq 0,\qquad S^{H,N}_{0}=x,

where
(\widetilde{B^{H}_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
is a fractional Brownian motion of index
H
. Here the complexity parameter is
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{N}:=1/\sqrt{N}
.
Hence, the assumptions of Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
2.4
(for all
p\geq 1
) are valid for
(S^{H,N}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with cut-off time scale
t_{\varepsilon_{N}}=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\ln(N)
. The standard Brownian driver (
H=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
) has been studied in
[
7
,
41
]
.
The same remains true if the fractional Brownian motions
B^{H}
are replaced with some other suitable driving noises, cf. subsections below.

4.4.
Generalized Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes

Suppose that the noise
D
has stationary increments with variance function
V(t)
. Then by the arguments of the proof
of
[
60
, Theorem 1]
the process

(4.2)

U_{t}=e^{-\Lambda t}\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}\mathrm{d}D_{s}\quad\mathrm{for}\quad t\geq 0

is a well-defined stationary process. In fact, it follows from elementary change of variables that
U
is stationary whenever
D
has stationary increments, provided that for all
t\geq 0
the integral
\int_{-\infty}^{t}e^{\Lambda s}\mathrm{d}D_{s}
is well-defined, which follows since
-\Lambda
is Routh–Hurwitz.
Note that for
S_{t}
defined by

S_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}\mathrm{d}D_{s}\quad\textrm{ for all }\quad t\geq 0,

we have the relation

(4.3)

S_{t}=e^{-\Lambda t}U_{0}+U_{t}\quad\mathrm{for}\quad t\geq 0.

Now

\mathrm{Var}(S_{t})=\mathbb{E}[S_{t}S_{t}^{*}]=\mathcal{R}_{U}(0)+e^{-\Lambda t}\mathcal{R}_{U}(0)e^{-\Lambda^{*}t}+e^{-\Lambda t}\mathcal{R}_{U}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{U}^{*}(t)e^{-\Lambda^{*}t},

where
\mathcal{R}_{U}(t)
is the covariance function of
U
given by
\mathcal{R}_{U}(t)=\mathbb{[}U_{0}U_{t}^{*}]
.
For
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)
defined by (
3.1
), we have the link

X^{\varepsilon}_{t}(x)=e^{-\Lambda t}(x_{0}-\varepsilon U_{0})+\varepsilon U_{t}\quad\mathrm{for}\quad t\geq 0.

We then immediately obtain the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have
S_{t}-U_{t}\to 0
almost surely and in
L^{p}(\Omega)
for any
p\geq 1
such that
U\in L^{p}(\Omega)
. In particular, the statements of Theorem
3.5
and Theorem
3.6
are true with
\sigma_{t}\equiv 1
and
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}\mu\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}U_{0}
.

Proof.

The claimed convergence follows directly
from (
4.3
), and the statements related to convergence in the Wasserstein distance follow from the fact that convergence in
\mathcal{W}_{p}
is equivalent to the convergence of
p
-th absolute moments together with weak convergence.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Note that the above proposition essentially covers all square integrable stationary processes. Indeed,
by
[
60
, Theorem 1]
a continuous-time process
U
is stationary if and only if it is of the
form (
4.2
) for some positive definite
\Lambda
which implies that
-\Lambda
is Routh–Hurwitz.

Remark 4.3
.

Note that by the square integrability of
D
, we can always choose any
p\in[1,2]
, while for higher values
p>2
one needs the existence of
p
-moments for
p>2
.

Remark 4.4
.

While the above result provides weak convergence and convergence in
L^{p}
(and hence convergence in the Wasserstein distance of order
p
), these are not sufficient to obtain convergence in the total variation distance, but instead one needs to impose additional assumptions. For example, if the underlying random variables admit densities
f_{S_{t}}
and
f_{Z}
, then
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(S_{t},Z)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
if and only if
f_{S_{t}}-f_{Z}\to 0
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Indeed, from Scheffe’s inequality we can deduce

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(S_{t},Z)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f_{S_{t}}(x)-f_{Z}(x)|\mathrm{d}x.

Another possibility to obtain convergence in the total variation distance is to lean on Malliavin calculus, whenever the associated randomness is generated by Gaussian objects, see
[
52
]
. In particular, the convergence in Proposition
4.1
takes place in the total variation distance as well whenever
S
belongs to a finite sum of so-called Wiener chaoses,
see
[
51
]
and the references therein. As a particular example, one could consider the Rosenblatt process as the driver
G
, see
e.g.
[
55
]
for details on the Rosenblatt process. Indeed, as the Rosenblatt process lives in the second Wiener chaos, we also have the convergence in the total variation distance, and the statements of Theorem
3.2
, Theorem
3.4
, Theorem
3.5
, and Theorem
3.6
, are true with any
p\geq 1
.
Lastly, we point out that total variation convergence can be obtained via uniform integrability of the characteristic functions, see Proposition A.3 and Proposition A.5 in Appendix A
of
[
9
]
.

4.5.
Iterated Gaussian Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes

Let
S
be given as the stochastic convolution

S_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}\mathrm{d}D_{s}\quad\mathrm{for}\quad t\geq 0,

with centered, continuous, and stationary driver noise
D=(D_{s})_{s\geq 0}
. Then, again
by
[
60
, Theorem 1]
, we can write
D
as the solution to

\mathrm{d}D_{s}=-\Theta D_{s}\mathrm{d}s+\mathrm{d}G_{s}

for some stationary increment process
G
. As such,
S
can be viewed as the iterated Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. In the Gaussian case we have the following result.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose
D
is a centered, continuous, stationary Gaussian process with covariance matrix
\mathcal{R}_{D}(s)=\mathbb{E}[D_{s}D_{0}^{*}]
. Then as
t
tends to infinity it follows that
S_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}\mathrm{d}D_{s}\to\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
in the total variation distance and in the Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{p}
for any
p\geq 1
, where

(4.4)

\begin{split}\Sigma&=\mathcal{R}_{D}(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{R}_{D}(s)e^{-\Lambda^{*}s}\Lambda^{*}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Lambda e^{-\Lambda s}\mathcal{R}_{D}(-s)\mathrm{d}s\\
&\quad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Lambda e^{-\Lambda s}\mathcal{R}_{D}(s-v)e^{-\Lambda^{*}v}\Lambda^{*}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}v.\end{split}

In particular, the statements of
Theorem
3.2
,Theorem
3.4
, Theorem
3.5
, and Theorem
3.6
are true with
\sigma_{t}\equiv 1
and
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
.

Proof.

Using integration by parts, we obtain

S_{t}=D_{t}-e^{-\Lambda t}D_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda^{-\Lambda(t-s)}D_{s}\mathrm{d}s.

Now by Gaussianity, it suffices to show the convergence of the covariance matrix. Moreover, since
e^{-\Lambda t}D_{0}\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, it suffices to consider

\widetilde{S}_{t}=D_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda^{-\Lambda(t-s)}D_{s}\mathrm{d}s.

For this we obtain by direct computations that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{S}_{t}\widetilde{S}_{t}^{*}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[D_{t}D_{t}^{*}]+\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[D_{t}D_{s}^{*}]e^{-\Lambda^{*}(t-s)}\Lambda^{*}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}\mathbb{E}[D_{s}D_{t}^{*}]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda e^{-\Lambda s}\mathbb{E}[D_{s}D_{v}^{*}]e^{-\Lambda^{*}v}\Lambda^{*}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{D}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{R}_{D}(t-s)e^{-\Lambda^{*}(t-s)}\Lambda^{*}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda e^{-\Lambda(t-s)}\mathcal{R}_{D}(s-t)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\Lambda e^{-\Lambda s}\mathcal{R}_{D}(s-v)e^{-\Lambda^{*}v}\Lambda^{*}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{R}_{D}(0)+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{R}_{D}(s)e^{-\Lambda^{*}s}\Lambda^{*}\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{\infty}\Lambda e^{-\Lambda s}\mathcal{R}_{D}(-s)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Lambda e^{-\Lambda s}\mathcal{R}_{D}(s-v)e^{-\Lambda^{*}v}\Lambda^{*}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}v=:\Sigma,

as
t
tends to infinity,
where the last convergence can be obtained by using change of variables. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.6
.

Note that this result generalizes beyond Gaussian processes
D
. However, one then has to analyze the convergence of both terms in
\widetilde{S}_{t}
more carefully. Indeed, while the covariance always converge to
\Sigma
, the weak convergence does not automatically follow since now both terms contribute to the limit.

4.6.
Non-homogeneous example

In this subsection, we study linear non-homogeneous process of the type

X^{\varepsilon}_{t}=e^{-\lambda t}x+\varepsilon e^{-\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\tau(s)\mathrm{d}B_{s},\quad t\geq 0,\,x\neq 0,

where
\tau:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a continuous deterministic function and
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard Brownian motion.
By Itô’s isometry we have

X^{\varepsilon}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}(e^{-\lambda t}x,e^{-2\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{2\lambda s}(\tau(s))^{2}\mathrm{d}s)
. Provided that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\tau(t)=\tau(\infty)\in(0,\infty)
, L’Hôpital’s rule implies

e^{-2\lambda t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{2\lambda s}(\tau(s))^{2}\mathrm{d}s\to\frac{\tau^{2}(\infty)}{2\lambda}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad t\to\infty.

From this, it follows that the statements of Theorem
3.2
, Theorem
3.4
, Theorem
3.5
, and Theorem
3.6
are true with
\sigma_{t}\equiv 1
and
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\tau^{2}(\infty)}{2\lambda}\right)
.

4.7.
Ex-centric integrated Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

In this subsection, we study linear equations driven by Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes.
It serves as a model for the position of a linear oscillator with linear friction perturbed by a Brownian or a symmetric
\alpha
-stable noise, see
[
35
]
. This example yields a meaningful example, where (
3.9
)
and (
3.14
) are satisfied with a non-constant scale
\sigma_{t}\not\equiv 1
.

Let
Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}=y+\int_{0}^{t}X^{\varepsilon}_{s}\mathrm{d}s
,
t\geq 0
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
be the integrated Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process driven by

X^{\varepsilon}_{s}=e^{-\lambda s}x+\varepsilon e^{-\lambda s}\int_{0}^{s}e^{\lambda r}\mathrm{d}B_{r},\quad s\geq 0,\,x\neq 0,

where
\lambda>0
and
\varepsilon>0
.
Using the stochastic Fubini Theorem we obtain that

(4.5)

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}\left(e^{-\lambda s}\int_{0}^{s}e^{\lambda r}\mathrm{d}B_{r}\right)\mathrm{d}s&=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(1-e^{-\lambda(t-r)}\right)\mathrm{d}B_{r}.\end{split}

By the Itô isometry we obtain

Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\left(y+\frac{1}{\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda t})x,\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\left(t-\frac{2}{\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda t})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}(1-e^{-2\lambda t})\right)\right).

Define
Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}:=\frac{Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}-(y+x/\lambda)}{\sqrt{t}}
and
note that

Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}
has law

Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\left(-\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{\sqrt{t}}x,\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\left(1-\frac{2}{\lambda t}(1-e^{-\lambda t})+\frac{1}{2\lambda t}(1-e^{-2\lambda t})\right)\right)

and its limiting law as
t\to\infty
is given by

Z^{\varepsilon}_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right)
. Hence the hypotheses of Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.6
(for all
p\geq 1
) are valid for
S_{t}=Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}-(y+x/\lambda)
and nontrivial
\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{t}
.
If we replace the driver
B
of
X^{\varepsilon}
by a symmetric
\alpha
-stable driver
L
,
\alpha\in(1,2)
we see that

Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}y+\frac{1}{\lambda}(1-e^{-\lambda t})x+\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}(1-e^{-\lambda(t-r)})\mathrm{d}L_{r},

where the preceding stochastic integral has an
\alpha
-stable law whose characteristic function is given by

(4.6)

\psi(z)=\exp\left(-|z|^{\alpha}C_{\alpha}\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha}}{\lambda^{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{t}(1-e^{-\lambda r})^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}r\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{R},

where
C_{\alpha}
is a positive constant, see (1.11) and (1.13) in
[
39
]
.
By L’Hôpital’s rule we have

\lim\limits_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}(1-e^{-\lambda r})^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}r=1
.
For
Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}:=\frac{Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}-(y+x/\lambda)}{t^{1/\alpha}}
we obtain

(4.7)

Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}-\frac{1}{\lambda}\frac{e^{-\lambda t}}{t^{1/\alpha}}x+\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda t^{1/\alpha}}\int_{0}^{t}(1-e^{-\lambda(t-r)})\mathrm{d}L_{r}

and its limiting law is given by
Z^{\varepsilon}_{\infty}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{Law}}}{{=}}\varepsilon L_{1}
,
where
L_{1}
has characteristic function

(4.8)

\phi(z)=\exp\left(-|z|^{\alpha}\frac{C_{\alpha}}{\lambda^{\alpha}}\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{R}.

Whence the hypotheses of Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.6
(for
1\leq p<\alpha
) are valid for
S_{t}=Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}-(y+x/\lambda)
and
\sigma_{t}=t^{1/\alpha}
.

Remark 4.7
.

By more tedious computations and using integration by parts, the above results generalize to the case of more general noises
D
instead of only Brownian noises
W
or
\alpha
-stable noises

Appendix B
Basic general properties of the total variation distance

In this section, we recall the definition of the total variation distance.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
be a measurable space and let

\mathfrak{P}:=\{\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]:\mathbb{P}\textrm{ is a probability measure}\}.

The total variation distance
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}:\mathfrak{P}\times\mathfrak{P}\to[0,1]
is defined by

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}):=\sup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}|\mathbb{P}_{1}(F)-\mathbb{P}_{2}(F)|\textrm{ for any }\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}\in\mathfrak{P}.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and
let
X_{1}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
X_{2}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be two random vectors having distribution
\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}}
, respectively.
Then the total variation distance between the law of
X_{1}
and the law of
X_{2}
is given by

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}},\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}})=\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})}|\mathbb{P}(X_{1}\in B)-\mathbb{P}(X_{2}\in B)|,

where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
denotes the Borelian
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
In conscious abuse of notation and for short-hand notation, we write
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(X_{1},X_{2})
instead of
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}},\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}})
.
The following lemma states the basic properties of the total variation distance.
In the absolute continuous case, the proofs of Item (i) and Item (ii) can be found in
Lemma A.1 in Appendix A of
[
20
]
or Theorem 5.2 in
[
29
]
for the general case.
Item (iii) follows from the continuity of the shift operator in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
and Item (iv) follows from a standard approximation argument.

Lemma B.1

(Basic properties of the total variation distance)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, and let
X_{1}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
X_{2}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be two random vectors.
Denote the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
by

\|\cdot\|
.
Then the following holds true.

(i)

Translation invariant: for any deterministic vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
it follows that

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(v_{1}+X_{1},v_{2}+X_{2})=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}((v_{1}-v_{2})+X_{1},X_{2})=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(X_{1},(v_{2}-v_{1})+X_{2}).

(ii)

Zero-homogeneity:
for any non-zero constant
c
it follows that

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(cX_{1},cX_{2})=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(X_{1},X_{2}).

(iii)

Continuity at the shift: for any function of deterministic vectors
(v_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
such that
\|v_{\varepsilon}-v\|\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}

for some
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
it follows that

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(v_{\varepsilon}+X_{1},X_{1})=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(v+X_{1},X_{1}).

(iv)

Displacement at infinity: for any function of deterministic vectors
(v_{\varepsilon})_{\varepsilon}
such that
\|v_{\varepsilon}\|\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}

it follows that

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(v_{\varepsilon}+X_{1},X_{1})=1.

Appendix C
Basic general properties of the Wasserstein distance

In this section, we recall the definition of Wasserstein distances.
We continue with the notation introduced in Appendix
B
.
Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
be the product
\sigma
-algebra.
We say that a probability measure
\nu:\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\otimes\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})\to[0,1]
is a coupling between the probabilities
\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}}
if and only if for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
we have

\nu(B\times\mathbb{R}^{m})=\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}(B)\textrm{ and }\nu(\mathbb{R}^{m}\times B)=\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}}(B).

We then denote the set of all coupling between
\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}}
by
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}},\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}})
.
In addition, assume that

X_{1}
and
X_{2}
have
finite absolute
p
-th moments for some
p\geq 1
. More precisely,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\|u\|^{p}\,\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}(\mathrm{d}u)<\infty
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\|u\|^{p}\,\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}}(\mathrm{d}u)<\infty
,
where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We define the
Wasserstein distance of order
p
between
\mathbb{P}_{X_{1}}
and
\mathbb{P}_{X_{2}}

(C.1)

Lemma C.1

(Basic properties of the Wasserstein distance of order
p\geq 1
)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, and let
X_{1}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
X_{2}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be two random vectors
having finite absolute
p
-th moments for some
p\geq 1
.
Then the following holds true.

(i)

Translation invariant:
for any deterministic vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
it follows that

\mathcal{W}_{p}(v_{1}+X_{1},v_{2}+X_{2})=\mathcal{W}_{p}((v_{1}-v_{2})+X_{1},X_{2})=\mathcal{W}_{p}(X_{1},(v_{2}-v_{1})+X_{2}).

(ii)

One-homogeneity:
for any non-zero constant
c
it follows that

\mathcal{W}_{p}(cX_{1},cX_{2})=|c|\,\mathcal{W}_{p}(X_{1},X_{2}).

(iii)

Shift-additivity:
for any deterministic vector
v_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
it follows that

\mathcal{W}_{p}(v_{1}+X_{1},X_{1})=\|v_{1}\|.

(iv)

Shift and scaling continuity:
for any deterministic vectors
v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and matrices
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
it follows that

\mathcal{W}_{p}(v_{1}+M_{1}X_{1},v_{2}+M_{2}X_{1})\leq\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|+\|M_{1}-M_{2}\|_{2}(\mathbb{E}[\|X_{1}\|^{p}])^{1/p},

where for
C=(c_{j,k})_{j,k\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
we set
\|C\|_{2}:=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}c^{2}_{j,k}\right)^{1/2}
.
