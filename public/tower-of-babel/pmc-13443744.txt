Maximum power point tracking implementation and solar tracker energization challenges in off-grid battery-less PVRO desalination plant
Authors: Mazdak Ebadi, Mohammad Amin Bahramian, Mohammad Khalili, Negin Abbasi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper proposes a Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT) algorithm for an off-grid, battery-less photovoltaic system that powers a solar desalination plant, including parallel reverse osmosis (RO) modules. The open circuit voltage and surface temperature of the PV array are first measured. Then the MP point voltage (VMP) is estimated. The PV voltage is estimated before each RO module is activated. So, the RO units are activated sequentially, before reaching the Maximum-Power-Point (MPP). A single-axis solar tracker is integrated to boost power generation capacity. The solar tracker, controlled by a calendar-based relay, does not require a radiation sensor. As the battery has no role in the steady operation of the system, it is replaced with pre-designed capacitors to handle the start-up inrush current of the tracker’s motor in the absence of a battery. A two-stage return algorithm is proposed for the solar tracker to return the panels to their initial direction without the need for a battery. So the entire system works without a battery backup. Experimental results from a 1100-watt solar system powering ten RO modules with a total demand of 860 watts demonstrate that the simultaneous implementation of the proposed MPPT and the hourly-operating solar tracker can enhance water desalination capacity by 71%.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54907-0.Keywords: Solar desalination, Single-axis sun-tracker, Maximum power point tracking, Off-grid battery-less photovoltaicSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

The quest for sustainable and efficient water desalination methods has led to various innovative approaches, with a growing focus on harnessing renewable energy sources. One significant challenge with traditional lead-acid batteries in photovoltaic (PV) systems is their limited lifespan, high maintenance requirements, and environmental impact. Consequently, researchers have been exploring battery-less, standalone photovoltaic systems for desalination, as highlighted in1. Abbasi et al.2 evaluated a battery-less single-phase PV desalination system incorporating RO + HD technology, demonstrating stable performance without an energy-saver. This system’s design included PV panels, a DC-DC converter, a DC-AC inverter, and a reverse osmosis unit. Similarly, Zhao et al.3 introduced a portable solar evaporator for seawater desalination and wastewater purification, while Hu et al.4 explored the potential of a distillation desalination system powered by solar and ocean thermal energy.

Solar tracking systems have emerged as powerful tools to boost PV efficiency, offering a range of innovative solutions. From semi-continuous single-axis trackers that significantly reduce energy consumption5 to advanced agricultural applications with reverse tracking for improved light distribution6, these systems demonstrate versatility. In osmotic power, real-time maximum power point trackers optimize energy output7, while offshore floating PV systems with dual-axis trackers and tension leg platforms enhance stability and energy generation in marine environments8. Zhu et al.9 developed a sensorless single-axis tracker based on Earth-Sun geometry and solar radiation prediction, while Kuttybay et al.10 highlighted the efficiency of timing table-based trackers over LDR sensor-based systems. Batayneh et al.11 proposed a discrete single-axis tracker with predetermined azimuth adjustments. For large-scale desalination plants, optimizing PV panel orientation and inclination is critical to maximize energy yield. Simulation-based studies, such as those conducted for the PV installation at the Al-Hoceima desalination plant, provide valuable insights into achieving optimal configurations for desalination energy needs12.

The field of water treatment has witnessed various technological advancements. Yadav et al.13 conducted a comprehensive analysis of a concentrated solar-PV-thermal integrated system for power, cooling, and desalination. Saidi et al.14 experimentally validated a solar-powered humidification-dehumidification desalination system, achieving improved performance with integrated air and water collectors. Multi-effect distillation, as explored by Annamalai and Kannappan15, offers high water recovery and energy efficiency for seawater desalination. In agricultural contexts, portable solar-powered electrochemical desalination systems have emerged as sustainable solutions, with innovations like the self-powered electrochemical deionization (ECDI) system providing clean water to remote communities without reliance on external power grids16.

The synergy between solar tracking and desalination technologies has proven instrumental in improving system efficiency. Maximum Power Point Tracking (MPPT), widely used in grid-connected PV systems, enhances energy extraction, while Kelley et al17. proposed a single-axis tracker with concentrator mirrors to boost reverse osmosis output. Finnerty et al.18 suggested interfacial solar vapor generation for off-grid desalination in small-island communities. Numerous off-grid standalone water desalination systems are being proposed, employing various technologies such as membrane-based desalination, vapor generation, and integrated air and water collectors14,19,20. A notable advancement is the integration of desalination with photovoltaic-thermal (PV/T) systems, as exemplified by the hybrid PV/T-pressure retarded osmosis-solid oxide fuel cell (PRO-SOFC) system, which recovers waste heat from PV panels to enhance energy efficiency and ensure a continuous power supply for desalination processes21. Moreover, innovative off-grid systems powered by marine currents offer dual functionality, producing both desalinated water and hydrogen, thus broadening the scope of renewable energy applications in water treatment22. Additionally, energy management innovations, such as super-capacitor-based charge pumps, stabilize battery-less off-grid PV systems, improving their reliability and efficiency for desalination applications23. Furthermore, ongoing research into control strategies and optimization techniques continues to refine the performance of these systems under diverse operational conditions24. Also, integrating different solar technologies like inclined and tubular solar stills in desalination systems is another solution to increase treatment efficiency25.

Recent studies confirm tracking enhances off-grid desalination performance: algorithmic power optimization in battery-less PV-driven systems improved ion removal efficiency26, while mechanical solar-tracking evaporators significantly boosted freshwater yield under variable irradiance27. Experimental validation of tracked parabolic concentrators coupled to solar stills demonstrated > 40% productivity gains, supporting sensorless orientation strategies for instantaneous power maximization28. Comprehensive reviews of solar-RO integration further underscore that dynamic panel positioning—whether algorithmic, calendar-based, or geometry-driven—is critical for stabilizing output in storage-free configurations29, directly motivating the proposed sensor-minimal MPPT and two-stage return tracker for battery-less PV-RO operation. In general, solar trackers and MPPT methods have not been investigated in off-grid PVRO desalination plants, especially in battery-less systems.

This paper proposes a new MPPT algorithm to implement in an off-grid Reverse-Osmosis-based PV system (PVRO). As mentioned in the literature, all the MPPT algorithms are used in grid-tied PV systems. In the proposed method, the MP point power is first estimated based on the open circuit voltage (OCV) of the PV. Then RO modules run sequentially. The PV voltage is measured every time to estimate reaching the MP point. So the maximum possible number of RO modules is activated and run for the next working hour.

Following this, the paper investigates the challenges of implementing a single-axis solar tracker on a battery-less off-grid system. Typically, a battery is hired to handle the inrush current of the tracker system motor’s start-up. But as the battery has no role in the steady operation of the system, it is replaced with two pre-designed capacitors with much longer lifespan, of which the capacity design has been fully investigated in this paper. But as the energy saving of the capacitor is limited, the operation of the solar tracker needs the power from the PV array. The challenge here is to return to the initial position at the end of the day. This paper proposes rotating backward in two phases: one at the end of each working day, which rotates the PV array back to mid-day orientation, and the other at the beginning of the next working day to align the PV array with the sun’s radiation direction. This way, the tracker system’s motor can return the structure to its original direction with the energy available from the panels. Furthermore, while existing MPPT methods make sense in grid-connected photovoltaic systems, the proposed method maximizes the PV panels’ power generation in an off-grid system. As a result, the water purification rate is also maximized.

In comparison30, demonstrated a single-axis tracker with an inclined solar still, achieving a 26% yield increase through thermal desalination based on radiation measurements, whereas this paper integrates a calendar-based relay tracker and a sensor-minimal MPPT (using only panel voltage and temperature) to enable battery-less PVRO operation, resulting in a 71% capacity improvement. Modelling PVRO performance with insolation-optimized tracking (single/dual-axis) for permeate gains presented in31. The system under study uses battery backup and is hired for electro-pump start-up operation. In24, a concentrated PV-thermal poly-generation system with Rankine and ejector cycles has been proposed, optimizing exergy and refrigerant choice, yet without PVRO-specific battery-less management. In25, integration of tubular and inclined solar stills with parabolic tracking and pumping for thermal desalination has been proposed, whereas this paper’s PV-driven parallel RO modules emphasize off-grid resilience32. evaluated PVRO economics using manual tracking and cooling, but did not address automated MPPT or battery elimination—challenges resolved in this paper’s proposed system to enhance true off-grid viability.

In the following section, Sect. 2 introduces the parts of the system under study. Then, the proposed MPPT and solar tracking technique are described in detail. Section 3 gives the results of experimental tests on the system and discusses them. The results comparison with the literature is presented at the end, before the conclusion.

System under study

Figure 1 illustrates the general scheme of an off-grid solar-powered, battery-less desalination system. To supply electrical parts of the treatment system, the PV output is first regulated using a DC/DC converter, then converted to AC voltage using an inverter. Both the inverter and the DC/DC converter are controlled by a closed-loop control of the constant voltage type. The HF DC/DC converter is used as a pre-voltage regulator for the inverter. In larger scale systems, the excess power may lead to overvoltage on DC bus. A resistive overvoltage suppressor can be hired as a protective device in such case. The electrical parts include a water pump, power supply adapters for reverse osmosis (RO) modules, an electric motor for the solar tracker system, and a power supply for the control electronics of the set. Except for the induction motor used in the tracker system, the other electrical components of this system require low steady-state currents and do not require a high initial transient current during start-up. But the motor used in the tracker system, which must rotate the entire array of panels toward the sun every 60 min, draws current several times its nominal current during start-up. Due to the absence of a battery as an energy storage element, starting such a motor will result in a voltage drop in the PV and, consequently, instability in the system. For this reason, two electrolytic capacitors with pre-designed capacities have been installed before and after the DC/DC converter to supply the initial energy required by the motor during start-up.

In this case, the MPPT algorithm will start over from the beginning.

Solar tracking system

This section explains how the solar tracker system works. It has two phases: moving forward to follow the sun during the day and moving backward at the end of the day to get back to where it started. It is worth noting that a calendar-based relay is utilized to predict the times of sunrise and sunset. This is the same relay used to activate the street lighting network, which can estimate the sunrise and sunset moments.

Moving backward

As previously mentioned, in the lack of an energy storage device, rotating backward to the starting orientation for the next working day will be the main challenge of this system. This paper proposes a two-phase process to overcome this challenge. First is 50% rotating back to the midday position, at the last sunny hour of the day. The second phase is fully returning to the starting orientation at the first hour after sunrise on the next working day.

It should be noted that moving against the orientation of sunlight causes a decrease in voltage and energy available from the panels. During the reversal process, it is assumed that the panels can give the tracker motor enough start-up energy. Thus, the only concern is ensuring the supply of energy to the electric motor during steady-state movement along the way.

Characteristics and performance of RO modules

To maximize the use of solar energy produced by the PV, two types of RO modules are used in this system: 100-watt modules with a purified water production flow rate of about 60 L/h, and 40-watt/20-watt modules with a purified water production flow rate of about 20 L/h and 8 L/h. In total, there are eight 100-watt modules and two 40-watt/20-watt modules in the system, which are activated with an optimal arrangement every hour, depending on the instantaneous power of the PV panels. The feed water entering the system had a total dissolved solids (TDS) of about 1000 mg/liter and was free of suspended particles. The only pre-treatment stage in this system was the use of a 5-micron polypropylene filter, which is installed in the inlet flow path to prevent possible suspended particles from entering the membranes and to mechanically protect them. Performance of the proposed MPPT

To evaluate the performance of the proposed MPPT in different radiation and temperature conditions, the power generation in the system under study is investigated for a full working day.

According to the time required at the beginning and end of the working day for the tracker system to rotate back, the start and end times of the system were 08:00 and 16:10. One of the important assumptions in this research is that the panels are almost clean when used. Also, the area where the research was conducted is in the middle of Iran, and the summer air temperature does not exceed 40 degrees Celsius. The altitude of the research site is 1750 m above sea level. Tables A and B in the Appendix present the maximum power output from the system at various hours of the day, accounting for temperature variations on the panel surface and the angle of solar radiation. To record the power extracted from the panels, their output voltage and current were recorded using a voltmeter and an ammeter. The output power delivered to the RO modules was obtained by adding the rated power of the active modules. Also, to record the angle of sunlight on the panels, an indicator perpendicular to the surface of the panels was used, and by measuring the length and width of its shadow on the panels, the horizontal and vertical angles of sunlight on the panels were obtained exactly.

In another test to evaluate the performance of the system under partial shade conditions, while the system is in stable operation, part of the panels are covered.

Figure 6 illustrates the dynamic response of the proposed system under a partial shading condition. The system behavior can be divided into four distinct intervals. Before the occurrence of shading, the system operates under steady-state conditions at approximately 495 W. The MPPT algorithm maintains the operating point at the maximum power point, resulting in stable voltage and current waveforms. At the instant when partial shading occurs, a sudden reduction in irradiance causes an abrupt drop in the available PV power. This leads to a transient disturbance in the DC-link voltage and converter current. Due to the mismatch introduced between the RO modules, the system experiences instability, and all RO modules are switched off.

Electrical performance under partial shading. CH1: Inverter Current, CH2: DC-Link Current, CH3: PV Array Voltage, CH4: DC-Link Voltage.

During the subsequent transient interval, oscillatory behavior is observed as the control system attempts to reinitialize. The voltage and current waveforms exhibit fluctuations due to the interaction between the converter dynamics and the changing PV characteristics under partial shading conditions. After reactivation of the MPPT algorithm, the RO modules are sequentially brought back online. The controller searches for the MPP, and the system gradually stabilizes to establish a new steady-state operating condition at approximately 335 W. The total recovery time from the shading occurrence to the new steady-state condition is approximately 14 s. These results demonstrate that the proposed MPPT strategy effectively manages partial shading conditions and ensures stable operation after transient disturbances.

As explained earlier, the return of the system to the initial direction to get ready for the next working day is done in two phases. Phase one is to return against the direction of the sun to the midday position, which takes place about an hour before sunset. The second phase takes place in the morning of the next day, when the system starts moving from the midday position and goes in the direction of the sun’s radiation, then runs the MPPT. In the meantime, the first phase is more challenging because the rotation is against the direction of the sun’s radiation. Figure 8 shows the performance of the system in reverse circulation at 16:49, which is 29 min before full sunset. The entire first phase of the return took about 40 s. The terminal voltage of the panels slowly dropped from 132.6 to 128.5 volts due to moving away from the sun, while the DC link voltage remained well fixed at 48 volts by the DC/DC converter. The supply power of 70 watts for the motor was well provided until the end of the rotation of the panels, and there was no instability in the DC-link voltage. It should be noted that according to Table B in the Appendix, the PMPP at this hour for the PV array fixed in the midday orientation is 100 watts, which is enough to feed the motor.

Electrical efficiency

Figure 9a shows that the total converter loss varies from 65 to 96 watts, ranging from no-load to full-load conditions. Based on the results, the electrical efficiency of the system under study — defined as the ratio of the total power received by the RO modules to the power received from the solar panels (the product of voltage and current) — has been calculated at different operating points. The results seen in Fig. 9b show that for a loading of at least 300 watts, the minimum efficiency is 77%, and for loading above 650 watts, the system reaches its best electrical efficiency of 88%.

(a) Power dissipation and (b) electrical efficiency (PV generation/RO load).

It is notable that since the inverter is not grid-tied, the instantaneous PV power output must be equal to the total consumption of the RO modules plus the internal losses of the converters. For this reason, the power consumption of the modules is always slightly less than the PV output, the difference being used for system losses. On the other hand, due to the nature of the step-wise changes in the power consumption of the RO modules, the final power consumption may be slightly lower than the nominal capacity of the PMP, which means that this amount of PV power is practically unused, but with an increase in the number of RO modules, the percentage of unused power will be negligible.

Water treatment efficiency

To investigate the effect of using the proposed MPPT and the solar tracker on the water desalination capacity of the system under study, the amount of power supplied to the RO modules during a full working day in two different seasons was measured—one sunny day in September and another in February. The results presented in Fig. 10a and b show that the system had 8 and 9 working hours, respectively, starting from about 60 min after sunrise and continuing until 30–45 min before sunset.

As can be seen in Fig. 10a, the power generated at the beginning and end of the day in the system equipped with a solar tracker oriented to the sun direction is twice the power generated in the photovoltaic system with panels fixed in the midday position. However, this difference reaches zero in the middle hours of the day. For the results taken in February, the effect of the tracker system on production capacity at the beginning and end of the day was more than 50%.

To evaluate the performance of the proposed MPPT, it is important to clarify that without MPPT, a larger and integrated RO unit with a fixed capacity should be used. In this way, if the power that can be received from the photovoltaic system is higher than the power of the RO unit, the water treatment system will be activated and inactive at other times. For example, if an integrated unit with a nominal power capacity of 400 W is used, on a sunny day in September (Fig. 10a), the photovoltaic system without a solar tracker can run it for 7 h, and in tests in February (Fig. 10b) for 8 h. It should be noted that the better performance of the system in February can be related to the lower air temperature and better efficiency of the panels.

On the other hand, the analysis of Fig. 10 shows that by adding the solar tracker to the system without MPPT, the system is capable of operating an integrated RO unit with a rated power of 400 W throughout the entire working day—8 h in September and 9 h in February. In this way, for the MPPT-free system that uses an integrated RO unit with a fixed nominal power of 400 watts, the use of the solar tracker system compared to the fixed panels in the midday position increases the number of working hours and the daily capacity of the water treatment system by more than 14% in the summer months. Figure 10 shows that during the first hour of the working day, the production power in the system increased from 460 watts in September with a solar tracker to 600 watts in February, and from 260 watts without a solar tracker in September to 400 watts in February. However, the midday production power remained constant at 740 watts in both cases. Consequently, the integrated RO module, with a capacity of 400 watts, absorbed 3200 watts with a solar tracker and 2800 watts without it during the September tests. In February, at the peak of the cold season, these values increased to 3600 watts with the tracker and 3200 watts without it. The results clearly show the positive effect of the solar tracker on the performance of the system under study, especially in the summer tests.

To check the effect of the proposed MPPT, instead of an integrated RO unit, the proposed system containing 10 small parallel modules activated with the MPPT is used. In this way, as the sun rises, more RO modules are activated every hour. The total energy received by the RO modules during a working day in September was 4480 W-hours, and in February, 5235 W-hours. Implementing the solar tracker improves these numbers to 5365 and 5665 W-hours, respectively. In this way, the system using parallel RO modules and the proposed MPPT energy intake, which is directly proportional to the daily water desalination capacity, shows an increase of 60% and 63%, respectively, in September and February tests compared to the integrated RO unit. This increase reaches 79% and 71% with the addition of the solar tracker system. Table 5 compares some major results with the literature.

The comparative analysis in Table 4 highlights the significant advancements of the proposed system compared to existing solar desalination technologies. Conventional solar stills, such as the nanofluid-based still24, tubular still25, and inclined still30, achieve efficiencies of 64.17%, 35.6%, and 25.4%, respectively, utilizing dual-axis, parabolic concentrators, and single-axis trackers. PV-RO systems31 and32 demonstrate further improvements, with dual-axis tracking achieving a 62% gain over fixed panels, single-axis trackers yielding a 43% improvement, and a manual 3-point system achieving a 60% permeate recovery. However, these systems typically rely on large battery packs, limiting their feasibility in off-grid applications. In contrast, the proposed PV-RO system eliminates battery dependency by integrating a novel MPPT algorithm and a calendar-based single-axis solar tracker, coupled with a two-stage return algorithm using electrolytic capacitors to manage inrush current. Experimental results demonstrate that without MPPT, a fixed-capacity 400 W RO unit absorbs 2800–3200 W-hours of energy in summer tests, improving by over 14% with the addition of a solar tracker. However, with the proposed MPPT and parallel RO configuration, energy intake increases to 4480 and 5235 W-hours in September and February, respectively, further rising to 5365 and 5665 W-hours with the solar tracker, achieving water treatment efficiencies of 63% and 71%. These results demonstrate that the proposed system outperforms conventional technologies by optimizing energy utilization, enhancing desalination capacity, and addressing key challenges for off-grid, battery-less applications, making it a reliable and cost-effective solution for sustainable water desalination.

Conclusion

In this paper, a method for implementing RO desalination on a battery-less photovoltaic system, isolated from the power grid, is presented. It analyzes the challenges facing the simultaneous implementation of an MPPT algorithm and a solar tracker on such a system and provides a solution for each. The proposed MPPT algorithm includes the use of parallel RO modules with low capacities. Once an hour, depending on the estimated maximum PV power, the maximum possible modules are activated. In this method, the estimation of the MPP is done by measuring the no-load voltage at the PV terminals and does not require a solar radiation and electric current sensor. The experimental results obtained show that the proposed MPPT increases the water desalination capacity up to 63% compared to a large RO module with a fixed capacity. Considering that the RO modules in this method are energized sequentially, their startup does not create a significant electrical transient. However, the use of a solar tracking system that has a larger motor causes the challenge of electrical instability of the system due to the high starting current. On the other hand, due to the lack of batteries, this system will face another challenge in returning to the initial position at the end of the working day. This paper has presented an innovative method including the use of high-capacity capacitors and a two-phase algorithm to return PV panels to the starting orientation. Experimental results indicate that the simultaneous utilization of a solar tracker and MPPT increases the water desalination capacity by 71% compared to an integrated RO module operating in a fixed position. Although the proposed method significantly improves the desalination capacity, its implementation requires several small, independent RO modules operating in parallel. In other words, it is not possible to implement the proposed method on a large, integrated RO unit. Although the proposed method is simple, robust, and practical, and its implementation is possible with minimal facilities, some of the coefficients in some equations depend on the results of laboratory tests and should be taken on a case-by-case basis. On the other hand, some environmental conditions, like partial shade, dirty panels, or high temperatures, may affect the results. But designing a new generation of battery-less off-grid PV- Desalination makes the proposed system eco-friendly and more sustainable. The use of lithium-ion batteries or super-capacitor packs can be other solutions to improve this system from a research perspective. Developing the proposed method to integrate units and other desalination technologies could be a future horizon for this research.
