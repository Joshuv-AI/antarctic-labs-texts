Everolimus Attenuates Salicylate-Induced Tinnitus-Like Behavior and Auditory Cortical Hyperexcitability
Authors: Boya Fan, Yixuan Liu, Wanxin Cao, Tao Li, Mengqin Li, Ke Zhang, Furong Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTinnitus is an auditory perception without external stimuli, driven by maladaptive neuroplasticity. This study investigated whether targeting the mTOR pathway with everolimus (EVE) could ameliorate pathological synaptic marker alterations, neurochemical imbalance, and neural hypersynchrony in the primary auditory cortex (A1) using a sodium salicylate (SS)-induced acute tinnitus rat model.MethodsAdult rats were divided into Control, Tinnitus, and Tinnitus+EVE groups. Behavioral phenotypes were assessed using gap prepulse inhibition of the acoustic startle (GPIAS) test. In vivo multi-channel electrophysiology was used to measure spontaneous firing rates (SFR) and local field potentials (LFPs), focusing on gamma-band power and theta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling (PAC) in A1. Molecular changes were quantified via Western blotting, immunofluorescence, immunohistochemistry, and targeted LC-MS/MS neurotransmitter profiling.ResultsSalicylate-induced tinnitus-like gap-detection deficits were mitigated by systemic EVE administration. Rats with tinnitus-like behavior displayed A1 hyperexcitability, reflected by elevated spontaneous firing rates and heightened c-Fos expression. Their network activity was disrupted, showing increased gamma-band power and significantly strengthened theta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling. At the molecular level, the mTOR pathway was activated, and the synaptic proteins BDNF and PSD95 were upregulated. Neurochemically, the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate accumulated significantly. Everolimus administration suppressed these abnormalities, attenuating the aberrant firing rates, pathological theta-gamma coupling, and the excessive synaptic protein and glutamate levels.ConclusionTargeted mTOR inhibition attenuates salicylate-induced tinnitus-like behavior by modulating maladaptive synaptic plasticity markers and mitigating pathological network hypersynchrony in A1. These findings suggest the mTOR cascade as a potential target for regulating cortical oscillatory and synaptic homeostasis in auditory phantom perception models.Keywords: tinnitus, auditory cortex, synaptic plasticity, everolimus, electrophysiology

Introduction

Tinnitus, the perception of sound in the absence of an external acoustic stimulus, affects a significant proportion of the global population and frequently leads to severe impairments in quality of life, including sleep disturbances, anxiety, and depression.1,2 Despite its high prevalence, the precise pathophysiological mechanisms underlying tinnitus remain elusive, and there are currently no FDA-approved pharmacological treatments targeting its core mechanisms.3,4 Accumulating evidence indicates that tinnitus is not merely a peripheral auditory disorder but is fundamentally driven by maladaptive neuroplasticity and aberrant neural activity within the central auditory system.5–7 Specifically, the primary auditory cortex (A1) has been identified as a critical locus for the generation and maintenance of tinnitus perception, characterized not only by neuronal hyperactivity and altered synaptic transmission but also by disrupted macroscopic network oscillations.8,9

High doses of sodium salicylate (SS), established at a dose of 350 mg/kg (i.p.), have been widely used to reliably induce transient tinnitus-like behavior in animal models, offering a platform for exploring the neural correlates of abnormal auditory perception.10–14 Within this framework, A1 has been identified as a key region exhibiting pronounced pathophysiological alterations.15 At the single-neuron level, studies have reported changes such as elevated spontaneous firing rates and reorganization of tonotopic maps.16 At the network level, aberrant oscillatory dynamics have emerged as candidate electrophysiological signatures potentially linked to the tinnitus-like percept, with particular attention being directed towards enhanced gamma-band activity and altered cross-frequency coupling.17,18

Molecules involved in synaptic plasticity have also been implicated in tinnitus-related cortical changes. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95), both essential for maintaining synaptic structural integrity and functional efficacy, have been shown to undergo activity-dependent regulation in various neurological and psychiatric conditions.19 In models of chronic pain, epilepsy, and stress-related disorders, alterations in these synaptic markers have been associated with maladaptive plasticity and aberrant network synchrony.20–22 Given the parallels between these conditions and tinnitus in terms of neural hyperexcitability and network reorganization, it is plausible that similar molecular events at the synaptic level may also be engaged in the A1 following SS administration. However, the nature and direction of these changes in the context of an acute tinnitus model, as well as their precise contribution to the perceptual phenomenon, remain to be fully elucidated. Whether these maladaptive plastic changes and oscillatory dysregulations in the A1 are governed by specific upstream signaling cascades remains an open question. The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway functions as a central hub regulating protein synthesis, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal excitability. Dysregulation of mTOR has been implicated in various neurological disorders characterized by pathological neuroplasticity, such as epilepsy and neuropathic pain.23 Importantly, mTOR activation not only modulates the expression of synaptic proteins like PSD95 and BDNF but also influences the balance of excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitter metabolism, which is fundamental to maintaining stable cortical rhythms.24,25 An excessive accumulation of principal excitatory neurotransmitters, such as glutamate, can severely disrupt the local microenvironment and drive cortical hyperexcitability; yet, the neurochemical profile of the A1 during SS-induced acute tinnitus-like states has not been systematically characterized. Everolimus (RAD001), an FDA-approved rapamycin analog, represents a clinically viable tool for inhibiting the mTOR pathway.26 It was developed as an improved derivative of rapamycin, offering superior pharmacokinetic properties that include higher oral bioavailability, faster time to steady-state concentration, and a shorter half-life that allows for more flexible dose adjustment.27,28 In addition, its favorable safety profile have already been demonstrated in clinical trials for neurological conditions such as tuberous sclerosis complex-associated epilepsy.29 These attributes position everolimus as a particularly compelling candidate for probing the mechanistic involvement of mTOR signaling in tinnitus-related cortical pathology. We hypothesized that if hyperactivation of the mTOR cascade contributes to the tinnitus-related pathological plasticity and metabolic imbalance in the A1, targeted inhibition by everolimus could ameliorate these deleterious changes.

However, the activation state of the mTOR pathway in the A1 during acute tinnitus-like states remains unknown, and no studies have yet investigated whether targeted mTOR inhibition can attenuate SS-induced neurophysiological and molecular abnormalities. To this end, the present study aimed to investigate whether everolimus intervention could ameliorate the functional and molecular pathologies in the A1 using an SS-induced acute tinnitus rat model. We sought to determine if everolimus could effectively suppress SS-induced neuronal hyperexcitability, modulate aberrant neural network oscillations, and ameliorate the dysregulation of neurotransmitters and synaptic plasticity markers. By integrating behavioral assessments, in vivo multi-channel electrophysiology, targeted neurochemical profiling, and molecular biology, this study provides a multi-dimensional elucidation of the pharmacological potential of mTOR inhibition in mitigating acute tinnitus-like abnormalities.

Methods

Animals and Experimental Design

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (8–10 weeks old, 280–350 g) were procured from the Experimental Animal Center of Peking University Health Science Center. Only male rats were utilized in this study to eliminate the potential confounding effects of the estrous cycle on cortical excitability and baseline synaptic protein expression. Upon arrival, the animals were housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled facility and allowed a 1-week acclimatization period to minimize transportation stress. Following acclimatization, rats designated for behavioral testing were transitioned to a reversed 12-h light/dark cycle (lights off at 08:00, lights on at 20:00) for 1 week prior to the experiments to ensure that behavioral assessments were conducted during their active phase. All animals had ad libitum access to standard laboratory chow and water. All experiments were approved by the Medical Science Research Ethics Committee of Peking University Third Hospital (Approval No. A2023106) and were performed in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, as well as the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines. To ensure animal welfare, humane endpoints were predefined, and the general condition, alertness, and body weight of the animals were monitored daily to exclude any non-specific systemic toxicity induced by the pharmacological treatments. To minimize suffering, appropriate perioperative analgesia and deep anesthesia were strictly administered prior to surgical procedures and tissue harvesting, with precise protocols detailed in subsequent sections. Prior to group allocation, baseline health and general responsiveness were assessed; animals exhibiting signs of middle ear infection or baseline behavioral anomalies were predefined to be excluded. Animals were randomly allocated into three experimental groups (Control, Tinnitus, and Tinnitus+EVE) using a random number sequence. Although investigators were not blinded to group allocation during the in vivo interventions, potential subjective bias was minimized by employing automated data acquisition systems for behavioral testing and utilizing standardized, script-based computational protocols for electrophysiological data extraction and histological quantifications.

To establish the acute tinnitus model, sodium salicylate (SS) was dissolved in normal saline (NS) to yield a 10% (w/v) solution. Everolimus (EVE, HY-10218, MedChemExpress, USA) was initially dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 50 mg/mL stock solution. For in vivo administration, this stock was subsequently diluted with a vehicle mixture containing Tween-80 (2% v/v) and PEG400 (10% v/v), and then brought to the final volume with NS to achieve the desired concentration, ensuring the final DMSO concentration remained within a safe limit for systemic injection. Animals in the Tinnitus and Tinnitus+EVE groups received a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of SS (350 mg/kg), a dosage well-established to robustly induce tinnitus-like behaviors.30–32 For the pharmacological intervention, the Tinnitus+EVE group was administered a single dose of EVE (10 mg/kg, i.p.) 30 minutes following the SS injection. This specific dosage was chosen based on its established neuromodulatory efficacy in central nervous system models.33,34 The 30-minute interval was selected to target the critical temporal window for rapid synaptic remodeling. Previous neuropsychopharmacological studies have shown that a 30-minute interval after i.p. administration can allow central target engagement to modulate rapid neuroplasticity and aberrant kinase signaling, particularly involving the mTOR/BDNF pathways.35,36 Therefore, administering EVE 30 minutes after the salicylate challenge allows the pharmacological agent to efficiently intercept acute molecular alterations prior to the peak of cortical hyperexcitability. Behavioral, electrophysiological, and molecular assessments were subsequently performed 2 hours post-EVE administration (ie, 2.5 hours post-SS). This specific temporal window was selected to align with the peak pharmacological action of high-dose SS (typically 2–3 hours post-injection) and to allow the early EVE intervention to effectively counteract the pathological escalation of mTOR-dependent network alterations. To ensure temporal alignment across all cohorts, rats in the Control and Tinnitus groups received matched volumes of the corresponding vehicle at the exact equivalent time points. Following the respective treatments, separate cohorts of animals were utilized for behavioral assessments, in vivo multi-channel electrophysiological recordings, and subsequent molecular or morphological analyses. GPIAS Behavioral Assessment and Peripheral Hearing Evaluation

Tinnitus-like behavior was assessed using the gap prepulse inhibition of the acoustic startle reflex (GPIAS) paradigm. The experimental procedure was conducted in a noise-shielded box (ZS-ZJT, ZS Dichuang Co., Beijing, China). Each rat was restrained in a breathable, transparent polycarbonate acoustic holder installed on a plexiglass base containing a sensitive piezoelectric sensor. The output from the sensor was connected to an A/D converter on an RP2 real-time processor (ZS Dichuang Co., Beijing, China).

Rats were tested under background sounds of 16, 12, and 6 kHz (60 dB sound pressure level [SPL]) at 2 hours following the pharmacological intervention. During the testing trials, a startling stimulus (115 dB SPL, 20 ms duration) was embedded in the background sound. To suppress intense startle reflexes, a 50 ms silent gap was inserted into the continuous background noise prior to the startle stimulus in gap trials. Higher GPIAS values indicate better gap detection, whereas reduced GPIAS reflects impaired gap detection consistent with tinnitus-like behavior. Animals were divided into three groups (n = 12 rats per group): the Control group received saline plus vehicle, the Tinnitus group was used to assess salicylate-induced tinnitus-like behavioral deficits, and the Tinnitus+EVE group was used to evaluate the pharmacological effect of the single-dose EVE intervention.

To further verify whether peripheral hearing thresholds were affected, auditory brainstem response (ABR) audiometry was conducted at discrete frequencies of 8 and 16 kHz in a separate subset of rats (n = 6 per group). Detailed procedures and parameters for ABR recordings are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Surgical Preparation and Microelectrode Array Implantation

For in vivo electrophysiological experiments, induction of anesthesia was achieved using 3% isoflurane, and the animals were subsequently secured in a stereotaxic frame (RWD, Shenzhen, China). During the surgical procedure, anesthesia was maintained with 1–2% isoflurane via a specialized nose cone. Body temperature was regulated, and ophthalmic erythromycin ointment was applied to prevent corneal dehydration. After standard surgical preparation and disinfection of the scalp, a midline incision was made to expose the skull. The primary auditory cortex (A1) was identified using the following stereotaxic coordinates relative to bregma: AP −5.16 mm, ML 6.85 mm, and DV −4.50 mm. A unilateral craniotomy was carefully performed over the target region. A 16-channel tetrode microelectrode array (KedouBC, China) was precisely lowered into the A1 using a stereotaxic micromanipulator. Once the array contacted the cortical surface, it was slowly advanced to the target depth. The exposed brain tissue was protected with sterile water-soluble lubricant and semi-solid paraffin, and the entire electrode assembly, along with the ground and reference cerebellar screws, was permanently anchored to the skull using dental acrylic. Animals were allowed a full 7-day recovery period prior to electrophysiological recordings. All surgical procedures described above were performed on eight rats per group for subsequent electrophysiological recordings.

In vivo Electrophysiological Recording and Data Processing

Extracellular neural signals from the A1 were captured using an Intan Technologies recording system. To remove alternating current (AC) line noise artifacts, a 50 Hz notch filter was applied to the recordings. The raw wideband signals were continuously sampled at 20,000 Hz, digitized, and stored for offline processing in MATLAB. During the analysis phase, the raw traces were filtered into two distinct components: high-frequency spike activities (> 300 Hz) and low-frequency local field potentials (LFPs, < 200 Hz).

Single-Unit Sorting and Spontaneous Firing Analysis

To isolate single-unit activities, the high-frequency signals underwent principal component analysis (PCA) followed by unsupervised clustering using KlustaKwik software. Extracted single units were classified into putative pyramidal neurons (spike width > 0.5 ms, baseline firing rate < 10 Hz) and putative interneurons (spike width < 0.4 ms, baseline firing rate > 10 Hz) based on their action potential waveforms and intrinsic firing properties. Given the critical role of excitatory neuroplasticity in tinnitus pathogenesis, our quantitative analysis was specifically focused on putative pyramidal neurons. For each isolated unit, the spontaneous firing rate (SFR) was calculated by dividing the total number of spikes by the recording duration, and the mean SFR per animal was computed for subsequent statistical comparisons. To avoid pseudoreplication, the SFRs of all putative pyramidal neurons recorded from the same animal were averaged, and this animal-level mean (n = 8 rats per group) was utilized for all subsequent statistical comparisons.

Local Field Potential (LFP) Preprocessing and Network Oscillation Analysis

To evaluate the macroscopic network dynamics within the primary auditory cortex (A1), comprehensive frequency-domain, time-frequency, and cross-frequency coupling analyses were performed on resting-state LFPs. All raw single-channel LFP data (sampled at 1250 Hz) were preprocessed using a second-order Butterworth band-stop filter at 50 Hz to eliminate line noise artifacts prior to subsequent spectral calculations. For time-frequency visualization, short-time Fourier transform (STFT) was applied to the preprocessed LFPs. To generate high-resolution continuous dynamic spectrograms, the STFT was performed using a 1-second Hann window with 90% overlap and zero-padding. The absolute spectral power was computed and subsequently converted to a logarithmic decibel scale (10×log10(Power)). The resulting dynamic spectrograms allowed for the visual tracking of frequency-specific power fluctuations over the recording period within the 1–100 Hz range. For overall group-level comparisons, the power spectral density (PSD) was quantified. The absolute spectral power (dB) was extracted across standard frequency bands, with a specific statistical focus on the gamma band (30–100 Hz). The resulting band-specific powers were visualized using boxplots with superimposed individual data points representing individual animals.

Phase-Amplitude Coupling (PAC) Analysis To investigate the hierarchical coordination of neural oscillations and quantify the cross-frequency coupling between the low-frequency phase and high-frequency amplitude of LFPs, we calculated the Modulation Index (MI) as described by Tort et al37

To specifically isolate theta-gamma coupling, the preprocessed LFPs were filtered into a series of theta phase frequencies theta phase frequencies () ranging from 4 to 12 Hz (step size: 0.5 Hz) and gamma amplitude frequencies () ranging from 30 to 100 Hz (step size: 2 Hz). Filtering was performed using second-order Butterworth bandpass filters. The bandwidths were set to ± 1 Hz for the phase filtering and ± 15 Hz for the amplitude filtering. The wide bandwidth for amplitude filtering is crucial to capture the sidebands produced by the modulating phase frequency. Subsequently, the standard Hilbert transform was applied to the filtered signals to extract the instantaneous phase time series (φ(t)) and the instantaneous amplitude envelope ((t)). The phase values [-π, π) were evenly divided into n = 18 bins. The mean amplitude of (t) corresponding to the phase values within each bin was computed and normalized by the sum across all bins, yielding a composite amplitude probability distribution . To statistically evaluate the overall theta-gamma coupling strength, the mean MI value within the predefined theta (4–12 Hz) and gamma (30–100 Hz) coordinate window was extracted for each recording epoch.

Nissl Staining and Electrode Site Verification

Upon completion of the in vivo electrophysiological recordings, the animals were deeply anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of 20% urethane at a dose of 1.2 g/kg and transcardially perfused with pre-warmed 0.9% normal saline, followed immediately by pre-chilled 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate buffer. The brains were carefully extracted, post-fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C, and subsequently cryoprotected by sequential immersion in 20% and 30% sucrose solutions until they sank. The brain tissues containing the primary auditory cortex (A1) were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound, and coronal sections were obtained using a cryostat microtome. To reconstruct and verify the anatomical locations of the recording electrode tracks, these sections were subjected to Nissl staining. Briefly, the slides were stained with Cresyl violet solution, rinsed in distilled water, dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped. The exact locations of the electrode tracks within the A1 were carefully examined and mapped under an optical microscope. Only the electrophysiological data obtained from recording sites confirmed by Nissl staining to be within the target A1 region were included in the final analysis.

Western Blotting

Following deep anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) and rapid decapitation, primary auditory cortex (A1) tissues were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, Cat. R0010, China) supplemented with protease (Solarbio, Cat. P6730) and phosphatase inhibitors (Solarbio, Cat. P1260). Following centrifugation at 11,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, the protein concentrations of the collected supernatants were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (NCM Biotech, Cat. WB6501, China). Equal amounts of protein samples were separated on precast Bis-Tris (4–20%) gradient gels (ABclonal, Cat. BRK0030, China) and electrophoretically transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature. Subsequently, they were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against phospho-mTOR (Ser2448) (ABclonal, Cat. AP1413), mTOR (ABclonal, Cat. A2445), BDNF (ABclonal, Cat. A4873), PSD95 (Abcam, Cat. ab18258), and Vinculin (ServiceBio, Cat. GB150068) as a loading control. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Target protein levels were normalized to Vinculin or their respective total proteins. All groups consisted of n = 6 rats for Western blot analysis.

Targeted Metabolomics Analysis of Neurotransmitters

The concentrations of neurotransmitters and their metabolites in the primary auditory cortex (A1) were quantified using a targeted metabolomics approach based on liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Briefly, under deep anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg), rats were decapitated, and 50 mg (± 2.5 mg) of brain tissue was immediately harvested and homogenized in 500 μL of −20 °C pre-chilled 70% methanol/water. The mixture was vortexed for 3 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred and kept at −20 °C for 30 min, followed by a secondary centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to ensure protein precipitation. The resulting supernatant was analyzed using an ExionLC™ AD system coupled with a QTRAP® 6500+ mass spectrometer. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column (1.8 µm, 100 mm×2.1 mm). The mobile phases consisted of water (A) and acetonitrile (B), both containing 0.1% formic acid. Quantitative analysis was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, with optimized declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) for each target metabolite. Absolute concentrations (ng/g) were calculated based on established standard curves. This targeted neurotransmitter metabolomics was conducted on n = 6 rats per group (further details regarding these metabolic profiles are provided in the Supplementary Materials).

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence (IF) staining, rats were deeply anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 50 mg/kg and transcardially perfused with warm 0.9% saline, followed by ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for fixation. The brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA, cryoprotected by sequential immersion in 20% and 30% sucrose solutions, and cut into 40 μm thick coronal sections using a cryostat. These sections were processed using a free-floating staining method, beginning with a 1-hour blocking step at room temperature in a solution containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), 0.3% Triton X-100, and normal goat serum to minimize non-specific binding. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with a cocktail of primary antibodies, including rabbit anti-c-Fos (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. 2250S) and mouse anti-NeuN (Abcam, Cat. ab104224). Following thorough washing, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature in the dark, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images of the primary auditory cortex (A1) were captured using an Olympus FV4000 confocal laser scanning microscope, with all acquisition parameters kept constant across experimental groups. The density of c-Fos/NeuN double-positive neurons was quantified by an investigator blinded to the treatment groups using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of BDNF, brain sections were initially incubated in 3% H2O2 for 15 minutes at room temperature to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. After washing with PBS, the sections were blocked with a solution containing 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and normal goat serum for 1 hour to prevent non-specific binding. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C with a primary antibody against BDNF (Servicebio, Cat. GB15559). Following primary antibody incubation and subsequent washing, the sections were treated with a biotinylated secondary antibody and an avidin-biotin-peroxidase complex (ABC kit) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen. The sections were then dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped for microscopic examination. Images of the primary auditory cortex (A1) were captured using an optical microscope, and the expression levels of BDNF were quantified based on the histological score (H-score) using ImageJ software by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups. For in situ morphological analyses, including immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry, a cohort of six rats per group was employed to account for spatial heterogeneity.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R software (R version 4.4.0). Quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was evaluated with Levene’s test. For datasets involving all three experimental groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to evaluate overall differences, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple group comparisons. For the exploratory neurotransmitter metabolomic profiling, overall ANOVA P-values were further adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure to correct for multiple testing. In accordance with our a priori experimental design, statistical analyses were otherwise strictly restricted to planned pairwise comparisons to address specific hypotheses. Specifically, A1 glutamate levels were evaluated as an independent a priori primary target based on its established upstream role. The comparison between the Control and Tinnitus groups served to validate the successful induction of the disease model, while the comparison between the Tinnitus and Tinnitus+EVE groups was conducted to evaluate the pharmacological efficacy of the intervention. For normally distributed data with equal variances, statistical significance for these specific predefined pairs was determined using independent two-sample t-tests. When the data failed to meet the assumptions of normality, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U-test was employed. To avoid pseudoreplication, the experimental unit was defined as the individual animal across all experiments. For data nested within animals such as single-unit spontaneous firing rates, multiple observations from the same subject were first averaged at the animal level prior to applying inferential statistics. A two-tailed P value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Everolimus Partially Attenuated GPIAS Deficits in Tinnitus Rats

To evaluate the behavioral evidence of tinnitus, the gap prepulse inhibition of the acoustic startle (GPIAS) test was conducted under 16 kHz, 12 kHz, and 6 kHz background noise conditions. Across all tested frequencies, the Tinnitus group exhibited a profound suppression of gap detection compared to the Control group, confirming the successful induction of tinnitus-like behavioral deficits (Figure 2). Specifically, at the 16 kHz background frequency, the GPIAS index dropped precipitously from 63.90 ± 3.90% in the Control group to 1.47 ± 3.36% in the Tinnitus group (P < 0.0001). Following systemic intervention, this deficit was significantly mitigated, with the GPIAS index in the Tinnitus+EVE group recovering to 31.51 ± 4.90% (P < 0.0001 vs Tinnitus). A similar pathological trend and pharmacological reversal were observed at 12 kHz, where the Tinnitus group demonstrated a markedly impaired GPIAS index relative to Controls (5.71 ± 3.23% vs 48.56 ± 4.72%; P < 0.0001). EVE administration effectively mitigated this impairment, elevating the behavioral performance metric to 31.16 ± 4.28% (P < 0.05 vs Tinnitus). Furthermore, under the 6 kHz condition, gap inhibition was severely degraded in tinnitus animals (6.74 ± 4.31%) compared to the healthy baseline (45.52 ± 5.26%; P < 0.0001). Following multiple-comparison adjustments, treatment with EVE yielded a trend toward rescue at this frequency, bringing the GPIAS index up to 21.72 ± 5.07% (P = 0.093 vs Tinnitus). These behavioral data demonstrate that our animal model exhibits persistent, broadband gap-detection deficits, and importantly, systemic EVE treatment confers a significant, frequency-dependent mitigation of these tinnitus-linked behavioral impairments.

Furthermore, to rule out the potential confounding effect of peripheral hearing loss, ABR audiometry was performed across 8 and 16 kHz. As detailed in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Figure 1), high-dose SS induced mild threshold shifts, whereas EVE administration partially mitigated these peripheral alterations, suggesting that the behavioral recovery was unlikely to be solely attributable to peripheral hearing loss. Daily welfare monitoring revealed no adverse drug-induced changes in body weight, or general alertness following systemic EVE administration. Together, these combined observations help rule out the possibility that the behavioral recovery was merely an artifact of non-specific systemic toxicity, lethargy, or peripheral hearing impairment.

Everolimus Mitigates Neuronal Hyperactivity and Pathological Neural Oscillations in the Primary Auditory Cortex of Tinnitus Rats

To investigate the electrophysiological correlates of tinnitus and evaluate the modulatory effects of everolimus (EVE), we performed in vivo multi-channel recordings in the primary auditory cortex (A1; Figure 3A) across the experimental groups (n = 8 rats per group). First, we analyzed the spontaneous firing rate (SFR) of isolated single units to assess baseline neuronal excitability. In total, we isolated 1,306 putative pyramidal neurons across the groups (388 from Control, 354 from Tinnitus, and 564 from Tinnitus+EVE). By comparing the animal-level averages, extracellular recordings revealed significant hyperactivity in A1 neurons following tinnitus induction. The average SFR in the Tinnitus group (3.25 ± 0.12 Hz) was significantly higher than that in the Control group (2.53 ± 0.10 Hz; P < 0.001). Systemic EVE administration attenuated this pathological hyperactivity, significantly shifting the mean firing rate in the Tinnitus+EVE group (2.56 ± 0.12 Hz) back toward Control levels (P = 0.002 vs Tinnitus; Figure 3B). Beyond single-unit activity, we examined local field potentials (LFPs) to assess population-level neural network oscillations. Continuous time-frequency spectrograms (STFT) and power spectral density (PSD) mapping demonstrated a pronounced alteration in the spectral composition of A1 in tinnitus rats (Figure 3C and E–G). Quantitative analysis of absolute power highlighted a significant elevation specifically in the gamma band (30–100 Hz) for the Tinnitus group (20.77 ± 0.77 dB) compared to the Control group (14.68 ± 0.93 dB; P < 0.001). EVE administration ameliorated this high-frequency oscillatory anomaly, significantly reducing gamma band power to 16.34 ± 0.58 dB (P < 0.001 vs Tinnitus; Figure 3D). Furthermore, to explore the fcross-frequency interactions within the auditory cortex, we computed the phase-amplitude coupling (PAC) between low-frequency theta phase and high-frequency gamma amplitude (Figure 3H–J). The modulation index (MI) summary revealed that tinnitus triggered an aberrant increase in theta-gamma coupling strength (Tinnitus: 0.473×10−3 ± 0.052×10−3) relative to the Control group (Control: 0.112×10−3 ± 0.007×10−3). Everolimus Inhibits mTOR Pathway Overactivation and Attenuates Alterations in Synaptic Plasticity Markers and Glutamate Levels in the Auditory Cortex

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of everolimus (EVE) on acute tinnitus-like deficits, we assessed the activation state of the mTOR signaling pathway, the expression of key synaptic plasticity proteins, and the local neurochemical environment in the primary auditory cortex (A1). Our Western blot results revealed that while the total mTOR protein expression remained unchanged across all experimental groups, the ratio of phosphorylated mTOR to total mTOR (p-mTOR/mTOR) was significantly elevated in the Tinnitus group compared to the Control group (P < 0.05) (Figure 4). Following the administration of EVE, this aberrant overactivation was effectively suppressed, as evidenced by a significant reduction in the p-mTOR/mTOR ratio in the Tinnitus+EVE group relative to the untreated Tinnitus group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, we evaluated the expression of PSD95 and BDNF, two crucial markers of synaptic structure and functional plasticity. Consistent with the observed mTOR hyperactivation, the protein levels of both PSD95 (P < 0.01) and BDNF (P < 0.05) were significantly upregulated in the A1 of rats in the Tinnitus group compared to controls.

To further evaluate the excitatory-inhibitory metabolic balance within the A1, we performed targeted neurotransmitter metabolomics using an LC-MS/MS approach. As our primary predefined target within this comprehensive panel, A1 glutamate levels were significantly elevated in the Tinnitus group, paralleling the synaptic protein overexpression. Systemic EVE intervention mitigated these pathological changes, significantly attenuating the elevated levels of PSD95, BDNF, and glutamate. Furthermore, an exploratory quantitative analysis of the extended neurotransmitter profile revealed broader metabolic alterations associated with the tinnitus state. These findings were subjected to multiple-testing corrections and are detailed in the hierarchical clustering heatmaps and comprehensive statistical summaries (Supplementary Figure 2 and Supplementary Table 1).

These molecular and metabolic findings collectively indicate that everolimus effectively dampens the hyperactive mTOR signaling cascade, mitigates the disruption of synaptic plasticity-related proteins, and attenuates the imbalance within the excitatory neurotransmitter microenvironment in the auditory cortex during acute tinnitus state.

Everolimus Suppresses Tinnitus-Induced Neuronal Hyperactivity and Abnormal Synaptic Plasticity in the Primary Auditory Cortex

To determine whether mTOR inhibition by everolimus (EVE) could attenuate the neuronal hyperexcitability associated with acute tinnitus, we initially evaluated the expression of c-Fos, an immediate early gene and a well-established marker for neuronal activation, in the primary auditory cortex (A1). Double immunofluorescence staining was performed to co-localize c-Fos with the mature neuronal marker NeuN (Figure 5). As illustrated in representative images (Figure 5A), c-Fos immunoreactivity (green) was predominantly localized within the nuclei of NeuN-positive neurons (magenta) in the A1. Quantitative analysis revealed a low basal level of c-Fos expression in the Control group. Following the induction of tinnitus by sodium salicylate, the number of c-Fos positive cells was significantly increased in the Tinnitus group compared to the controls (P < 0.001), indicating neuronal hyperactivation. However, intervention with EVE significantly mitigated this aberrant neural activation, as evidenced by a substantial reduction in the number of c-Fos positive cells in the Tinnitus+EVE group relative to the untreated Tinnitus group (P < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we provide multi-dimensional evidence indicating that the mTOR signaling pathway is strongly associated with the pathogenesis of acute tinnitus, and that its targeted pharmacological inhibition by everolimus effectively attenuates tinnitus-related abnormalities at behavioral, electrophysiological, neurochemical, and molecular levels. By integrating GPIAS assessments, in vivo multi-channel electrophysiological recordings in the A1, and comprehensive molecular analyses, we revealed that systemic administration of SS reliably induces gap-detection deficits consistent with tinnitus-like behavior in rats. Accompanying these behavioral manifestations, we observed pronounced neuronal hyperactivity in the A1, reflected by significantly elevated spontaneous firing rates, alongside hypersynchronous network oscillatory dynamics characterized by aberrantly increased gamma-band power and altered cross-frequency coupling. These network-level dysfunctions coincided with mTOR hyperactivation, as evidenced by elevated mTOR phosphorylation in the A1, and were paralleled by a marked upregulation of key synaptic plasticity markers, including BDNF and PSD95. This coupling between mTOR overactivation, synaptic protein overexpression, and cortical hyperexcitability suggests a mechanistic cascade in which SS-driven mTOR activation may contribute to maladaptive synaptic remodeling, ultimately facilitating the aberrant neural synchrony that underlies the tinnitus percept. Importantly, systemic administration of everolimus effectively mitigated these pathological alterations: it improved behavioral gap-detection performance in a frequency-dependent manner, attenuated the aberrant neuronal firing rates and oscillatory dynamics in the A1, and suppressed the mTOR-mediated upregulation of BDNF and PSD95. Together, these findings support a strong pathophysiological association between the mTOR signaling cascade and SS-induced acute tinnitus pathology, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic target for tinnitus intervention.

The GPIAS test serves as a well-established behavioral paradigm for detecting the presence of a tinnitus percept in animal models, and its validity has been corroborated across multiple tinnitus induction methods, including noise trauma and salicylate administration.32,38–40 Our behavioral data demonstrated that SS administration induced severe gap-detection deficits across a broad frequency spectrum including 6, 12, and 16 kHz in the Tinnitus group, consistent with prior reports showing that high-dose salicylate disrupts temporal acoustic processing in a frequency-nonspecific manner.41,42 The pharmacological effect of everolimus exhibited a frequency-dependent profile. While systemic EVE administration significantly mitigated gap-detection deficits at higher background frequencies (16 and 12 kHz), it showed a trend toward recovery at 6 kHz, although it did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.093). This differential sensitivity may suggest that higher-frequency auditory circuits are more susceptible to early pharmacological intervention, or it could reflect a specific tonotopic gradient in mTOR pathway activation during acute tinnitus. The significant, albeit partial, behavioral improvement following pharmacological mTOR inhibition provided the initial phenotypic validation in this context. To elucidate the neural correlates underlying this behavioral manifestation, we examined the extracellular electrophysiological profiles of the A1. Consistent with the behavioral impairment, single-unit recordings revealed a pathological elevation in the spontaneous firing rate of A1 neurons. This finding aligns with previous studies documenting salicylate-induced neuronal hyperexcitability in the auditory cortex and subcortical structures.43,44 More broadly, elevated spontaneous firing has been recognized as a hallmark of the central gain adaptation that follows peripheral deafferentation, and has been similarly reported in noise-induced tinnitus models.18,45,46 This cellular hyperexcitability was further corroborated by morphological and molecular evidence, including the enhanced co-localization of the immediate early protein c-Fos with NeuN-positive neurons, reflecting neuronal activation within the A1 circuit. Activity-dependent c-Fos upregulation in the auditory cortex has previously been reported following salicylate treatment,47,48 as well as in models of acoustic trauma, which validates our experimental paradigm and confirms that the A1 is a primary locus of abnormally elevated neuronal engagement during tinnitus generation. Our extracellular recordings provide objective evidence against the possibility of non-specific systemic sedation or generalized central nervous system depression induced by EVE. If the behavioral improvements were merely a secondary artifact of lethargy or systemic neural suppression, a widespread suppression of baseline cortical activity well below normal physiological levels would be expected. Instead, EVE administration specifically normalized the SS-induced elevation in spontaneous firing rates and high-frequency gamma power back to normal Control baseline levels. This restoration of cortical activity toward Control levels supports the interpretation that the observed behavioral improvement was associated with attenuation of tinnitus-related central hyperactivity rather than a nonspecific systemic side effect.

Beyond individual neuronal hyperactivity, our local field potential analysis unveiled complex population-level oscillatory dysregulations. Time-frequency analysis and power spectral density quantification revealed a striking elevation in absolute gamma-band power ranging from 30 to 100 Hz in the Tinnitus group. High-frequency gamma oscillations are fundamentally linked to local cortical processing, attention, and the excitation-inhibition balance. Enhanced gamma activity has emerged as a commonly reported electrophysiological feature of the tinnitus state, having been documented not only in salicylate models42 but also in noise-exposed animals and in human tinnitus patients using magnetoencephalography.49–51 Furthermore, PAC analysis demonstrated a pathological strengthening of theta-gamma coupling, measured by the Modulation Index. This excessive cross-frequency coupling suggests that low-frequency theta rhythms were abnormally coupled to high-frequency gamma cortical processing, representing a hallmark of maladaptive hypersynchrony often observed in auditory phantom perceptions. Aberrant theta-gamma coupling has been similarly reported in the auditory cortex of tinnitus patients,52 as well as in other states of pathological cortical hyperexcitability such as epilepsy, reinforcing the interpretation that tinnitus and epilepsy may share overlapping network mechanisms.10,53,54 Systemic everolimus significantly mitigated this aberrant theta-gamma interaction and attenuated the elevated gamma power, indicating that mTOR inhibition not only dampens single-unit hyperactivity but also ameliorates the disruption of macroscopic rhythmic coordination across the A1 network.

The potential mechanistic link between mTOR signaling and these electrophysiological aberrations may lie in the maladaptive structural and functional remodeling of synapses. Our molecular data showed that the network hypersynchrony in the Tinnitus group coincided with significant hyperactivation of the mTOR pathway. This is consistent with the established role of mTOR as a master regulator of activity-dependent protein synthesis at synapses.24,55,56 In neurological disorders characterized by pathological hyperexcitability, including epilepsy and neuropathic pain, mTOR hyperactivation has been shown to drive the overexpression of plasticity-related proteins that promote aberrant circuit strengthening.57,58 This cascade activation subsequently coincided with the dramatic upregulation of critical synaptic plasticity proteins, specifically BDNF and PSD95. BDNF and PSD95 are structural cornerstones of excitatory synapses; their overexpression facilitates aberrant synaptic strengthening and likely shifts the cortical excitation-inhibition balance toward hyperexcitability. BDNF upregulation has been implicated in promoting neuronal hyperexcitability in models of epilepsy and chronic pain, while increased PSD95 expression has been associated with enhanced excitatory synaptogenesis and spine stabilization.59–62 BDNF also serves as a biomarker of stress-related responses, where acute stress elevates BDNF levels.63 In the context of tinnitus, elevated BDNF expression has also been reported in the cochlear nucleus and inferior colliculus following salicylate or noise exposure,64,65 suggesting that mTOR-driven synaptic protein overexpression may be a shared pathological feature across multiple nodes of the central auditory pathway. By suppressing mTOR phosphorylation, everolimus effectively downregulated BDNF and PSD95 expression and reduced the abnormal elevation of glutamate concentrations back to near-baseline levels. This integrated intervention attenuated the pathological synaptic reorganization and neurotransmitter dyshomeostasis, which coincided with the mitigation of hyperactive local field potential gamma oscillations and theta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling. The regulation between mTOR activity and synaptic protein expression observed here echoes findings in models of autism spectrum disorder66 and tuberous sclerosis,67 where mTOR inhibitors counteract synaptic protein dyshomeostasis and ameliorate network-level dysfunction.

An additional mechanistic implication of our findings is that mTOR signaling may serve as a potential converging point linking neuronal activity, synaptic protein synthesis, neurotransmitter metabolism, and network synchronization within the auditory cortex. Previous studies have demonstrated that excessive neuronal firing can activate the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway through activity-dependent calcium influx and neurotrophin signaling, thereby promoting local translation of synaptic proteins required for long-term potentiation and structural plasticity.23,55 Enhanced expression of proteins such as BDNF and PSD95 can further increase excitatory synaptic efficacy, creating a positive feedback loop that amplifies cortical excitability.23 In this framework, salicylate-induced tinnitus may represent a central pathological state characterized by increased activation of the cortical mTOR pathway, potentially contributing to maladaptive synaptic strengthening and network hypersynchronization. The mitigation of both molecular and electrophysiological abnormalities by everolimus supports this possibility and suggests that mTOR may serve as a relevant mediating node in the central neural network underlying aberrant auditory perception.

While chronic tinnitus models better mimic the clinical condition, the acute sodium salicylate model is valuable for investigating the early events associated with maladaptive neuroplasticity. By assessing the auditory cortex at this specific acute time window, we were able to characterize early alterations in mTOR signaling and the concomitant network hypersynchrony before complex brain-wide compensatory mechanisms obscure these fundamental changes. This approach is conceptually analogous to the use of acute seizure models to uncover the initiating molecular events of epileptogenesis.68,69 Future studies utilizing chronic tinnitus models and multi-dose pharmacological paradigms are warranted to evaluate the long-term translational potential of everolimus, as well as to determine whether sustained mTOR inhibition can durably prevent the consolidation of the tinnitus percept. Our findings support a pathological axis that spans from mTOR-associated synaptic alterations to network hypersynchrony and tinnitus-like behavioral deficits, highlighting targeted mTOR inhibition as a potent strategy for restoring auditory cortical homeostasis. These results not only converge with prior observations of mTOR-dependent synaptopathy in models of epilepsy and chronic pain, but also extend the scope of mTOR-mediated pathology to the auditory domain, where it may serve as a common molecular substrate linking peripheral insult to central perceptual dysfunction.70–74

From a translational perspective, the identification of mTOR as a therapeutic target is attractive because several mTOR inhibitors, including everolimus and rapamycin, have already been approved for clinical use. However, as noted in the broader context of neuropharmacology, the clinical translation of systemic mTOR inhibition must be approached with caution. Pharmacological agents may produce effects that differ substantially between acute experimental animal models and human patients receiving prolonged systemic treatment, potentially leading to complex and sometimes paradoxical clinical outcomes. Therefore, the adverse effect profile and limitations of everolimus must be carefully considered before proposing its clinical repurposing for tinnitus. Moreover, accumulating evidence suggests that dysregulated mTOR signaling contributes to a broad spectrum of neurological disorders characterized by aberrant neuronal excitability and maladaptive plasticity, including epilepsy, neuropathic pain, autism spectrum disorders, and neurodegenerative diseases.75–77 The present findings extend this concept to tinnitus and raise the possibility that mTOR-dependent synaptic dysregulation may represent a shared pathological mechanism across multiple disorders involving phantom perception and network hyperactivity.

Despite the strengths of the present study, certain limitations should be noted. First, although our findings were obtained in an acute salicylate-induced tinnitus model and may not fully capture the pathology of chronic tinnitus, this model was chosen for a specific reason. While acoustic trauma models better mimic chronic clinical states, the salicylate-induced acute model offers a distinct advantage for revealing the initial molecular cascades driving cortical hyperexcitability. The rapid, synchronized, and reversible onset of tinnitus-like perception after salicylate administration creates a useful window for examining early events such as acute mTOR hyperactivation and immediate early gene expression, before long-term compensatory remodeling begins. This makes the model especially suited to our goal of intercepting early pathological molecular triggers. Second, lacking an everolimus-only control group, it remains challenging to completely distinguish the drug’s tinnitus-specific mitigating effects from its baseline modulatory impact on normal auditory cortical function. Third, while our molecular investigation deliberately focused on BDNF and PSD95 due to their direct relevance to auditory synaptic plasticity and network hyperexcitability, classical downstream translational regulators of the mTOR cascade, such as p70S6K and 4E-BP1, were not assessed in parallel, as our current design prioritized the downstream functional effectors of synaptic remodeling over the intermediate translational machinery. Consequently, our findings indicate a strong pathophysiological association rather than a fully characterized causal signaling sequence. Additionally, direct morphological evidence of synaptic structural remodeling was not evaluated. Furthermore, although our supplementary Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR) audiometry confirmed acute peripheral threshold shifts induced by high-dose salicylate (Supplementary Figure 1), this peripheral alteration remains an inherent confounding factor in the acute SS model.78–80 Consequently, we cannot completely uncouple the observed central tinnitus-generating mechanisms in the primary auditory cortex from the secondary effects of acute peripheral auditory dysfunction. Finally, while everolimus shows clinical promise in otology,81 for example in managing NF2-related vestibular schwannoma, its tumor-controlling properties must be distinguished from direct anti-tinnitus effects. Future studies employing chronic tinnitus models, localized targeted delivery approaches, and comprehensive molecular profiling are warranted to validate these findings and improve their translational potential.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study provides converging behavioral, electrophysiological, neurochemical, and molecular evidence suggesting that mTOR signaling pathway hyperactivation in the primary auditory cortex is strongly associated with sodium salicylate-induced acute tinnitus-like deficits. The Tinnitus group exhibited behavioral deficits, accompanied by neuronal hyperexcitability, enhanced gamma-band oscillations, aberrant theta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling, and neurotransmitter dyshomeostasis in the A1. Mechanistically, these network-level dysfunctions coincided with the upregulation of synaptic plasticity proteins, including BDNF and PSD95. Pharmacological inhibition of mTOR by everolimus effectively attenuated these behavioral, electrophysiological, neurochemical, and molecular abnormalities. Taken together, our findings delineate a potential pathological axis linking mTOR-associated synaptic alterations to cortical network hypersynchrony and tinnitus-like behaviors, and highlight mTOR inhibition as a promising strategy for alleviating auditory cortical dyshomeostasis. Future studies are warranted to evaluate the long-term translational potential of this approach in both acute and chronic tinnitus models.
