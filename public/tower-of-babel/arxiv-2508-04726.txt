Subset Sum in Near-Linear Pseudopolynomial Time and Polynomial Space
Thejas Radhika Sajith

Abstract
Given a multiset A = { a 1 , … , a n } A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\} of positive integers and a target integer t t , the SubsetSum problem asks if there is a subset of A A that sums to t t . Bellman’s [1957] classical dynamic programming algorithm runs in O ⁡ ( n ​ t ) O(nt) time and O ⁡ ( t ) O(t) space. Since then, there have been multiple improvements in both time and space complexity. Notably, Bringmann [SODA 2017] uses a two-step color-coding technique to obtain a randomized algorithm that runs in O ~ ​ ( n + t ) \tilde{O}(n+t) time and O ~ ​ ( t ) \tilde{O}(t) space. On the other hand, there are polynomial space algorithms – for example, Jin, Vyas and Williams [SODA 2021] build upon the algorithm given by Bringmann, using a clever algebraic trick first seen in Kane’s Logspace algorithm, to obtain an O ~ ​ ( n ​ t ) \tilde{O}(nt) time and O ~ ​ ( log ⁡ ( n ​ t ) ) \tilde{O}(\log(nt)) space algorithm. A natural question, asked by Jin et al. is if there is an O ~ ​ ( n + t ) \tilde{O}(n+t) time algorithm running in poly ( n , log ⁡ t ) (n,\log t) space. Another natural question is whether it is possible to construct a deterministic polynomial space algorithm with time complexity comparable to that of Bellman’s. In this paper, we answer both questions affirmatively. We build on the framework given by Jin et al., using a multipoint evaluation-based approach to speed up a bottleneck step in their algorithm. We construct: • a deterministic algorithm that runs in O ~ ​ ( n ​ t ) \tilde{O}(nt) time and O ~ ​ ( n ​ log 2 ​ t ) \tilde{O}(n\log^{2}t) space • a randomized algorithm that runs in O ~ ​ ( n + t ) \tilde{O}(n+t) time and O ~ ​ ( n 2 + n ​ log 2 ​ t ) \tilde{O}(n^{2}+n\log^{2}t) space.

Interestingly,

This leads us to two natural open questions, the first of which was explicitly posed by Jin et al.

Preliminaries

Consider the polynomial
A(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1+x^{a_{i}})
associated with the
SubsetSum
instance
(A=\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\},t)
. Expanding the polynomial out, we see that the coefficient of
x^{t}
is nonzero precisely when
t
is a subsum of
A
. The following trick, first seen in
[
Kan10
]
can be used to check if the coefficient of
x^{t}
is nonzero.

Lemma
1.1
.

[
Kan10
]
Let
\mathbb{F}_{p}
be the finite field of order
p
, for some prime
p
. Given an integer polynomial
f(x)
of degree
d
less than
p
, and
0<t\leq d
, define
c_{t}
to be the coefficient of
x^{t}
. Then:

-c_{t}\bmod p=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}}x^{p-1-t}f(x).

Thus, the sum is zero if and only if
p\mid c_{t}
.

Proof.

Let
g
be a generator for the cyclic group
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
. Consider the following sum, for some integer
a
:

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}}x^{a}=\begin{cases}(p-1)\cdot 1=-1,&\text{if }(p-1)\mid a\\
\sum\limits_{i=0}^{p-2}(g^{a})^{i}=\dfrac{g^{a(p-1)}-1}{g^{a}-1}=0,&\text{if }(p-1)\nmid a\end{cases}

Then, if
f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}c_{i}x^{i}
, we have:

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}}x^{p-1-t}f(x)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}}x^{p-1-t}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{d}c_{j}x^{j}\right)=-c_{t}\bmod p

∎

Such computations can be done in low-space and are thus especially useful in this context. Both of our low-space algorithms have a similar high-level structure: choose a ‘large enough’ prime
q
, an appropriate polynomial
p(x)
encoding the
SubsetSum
instance, and compute the sum described above to extract the required coefficient. Instead of computing the sum term-by-term, we may employ the following technique:

Lemma
1.2
.

(Multipoint Evaluation)
Let
R
be a commutative ring with unity. Given a polynomial
f(x)\in R[x]
of degree
d=O(n)
, where
n=2^{k}
, and evaluation points
u_{0},\cdots,u_{n-1}
, one can compute
f(u_{0}),\cdots,f(u_{n-1})
in
O(n\polylog n)
time and space.

Proof.

(Sketch, see
[
vzGG13
]
, Section 10.1 for a more detailed proof.)

Precompute and store the polynomials

M_{i,j}(x)=\prod_{l=0}^{2^{i}-1}(x-u_{j\cdot 2^{i}+l}),

for
0\leq i\leq k
,
0\leq j\leq 2^{k-i}-1
. Doing this carefully, as shown in the reference, takes
\tilde{O}(n)
time and space.

Then, compute
f_{0}(x)=f(x)\bmod(x-u_{0})\cdots(x-u_{n/2-1})
and
f_{1}(x)=f(x)\bmod(x-u_{n/2})\cdots(x-u_{n-1})
. Notice that
f
agrees with
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
on the first and last
n/2
points respectively. We can now recurse, asking for
\{f_{0}(u_{0}),\cdots,f_{0}(u_{n/2})\}
and
\{f_{1}(u_{n/2+1}),\cdots,f_{1}(u_{n-1})\}
. Notice that the polynomials that appear as dividends in the recursion are precisely the polynomials
M_{i,j}
computed earlier. Note that
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
have degree at most
n/2
.

We obtain the following recurrence for the time complexity:

T(n)=2T(n/2)+O(D(n))

where
D(n)
is the time taken to perform the divisions. Since
D(n)=O(n\polylog n)
, we have
T(n)=O(n\polylog n)
, as well.
∎

There are two obstacles to applying multipoint evaluation directly. First, the polynomials that we consider have degree
\Omega(t)
, and thus storing the polynomials
M_{i,j}
takes too much space. Second, computing remainders usually requires FFT, which again takes
\Omega(t)
space. However, by ordering the evaluation points carefully, one can avoid the pre-computation step and compute remainders quickly and in low-space. The following lemma will help us do so.

Lemma
1.3
.

Let
q
be a prime power, and write
q-1=2^{k}r
, where
r
is odd. Let
g
be a generator of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
and define the coset
H=g^{s+j}\langle\left(g^{r}\right)^{2^{i}}\rangle
, where
0\leq s<r
,
0\leq j<2^{i}
and
0\leq i<k
. Then, the polynomial
p_{H}(x)
defined as

p_{H}(x)=\prod_{h\in H}(x-h)

simplifies to a binomial:

p_{H}(x)=x^{2^{k-i}}-\left(g^{s+j}\right)^{2^{k-i}}.

Proof.

The degrees of the polynomials on the LHS and the RHS match, since
|H|=2^{k-i}
. It then suffices to check that for each
h\in H
,
h^{2^{k-i}}=\left(g^{s+j}\right)^{2^{k-i}}
, which holds since each element of
\left(g^{-(s+j)}\right)H
has order dividing
2^{k-i}
.
∎

When we order the points using the above lemma, we shall see that it becomes natural to minimize
r
. The following result will prove useful:

Theorem 1.4
.

(Prime Number Theorem for Arithmetic Progressions
[
MV07
, Section 11.3]
)

Let
a
and
d
be coprime integers. Define
\pi(x;d,a)
to be the number of primes less than
x
that are congruent to
a\pmod{d}
. Then:

\pi(x;d,a)=\frac{1}{\phi(d)}\cdot\frac{x}{\ln x}+E(x;a,d),

where
E(x;a,d)
is an error term satisfying
E(x;a,d)=o(\pi(x;d,a))
. Here,
\phi
denotes Euler’s totient function.

Definition 1.5
.

A polynomial
f(x)
is called
n-sparse
if at most n of its coefficients are nonzero.

Definition 1.6
.

A polynomial
f(x)
is called
\Pi
-n-sparse
it can be written as a product
f(x)=f_{1}(x)\cdots f_{l}(x)
, where each
f_{i}(x)
is
k_{i}
-sparse, and
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{l}\leq n
. We call such a factorization a
presentation
of
f
.

We may analogously define polynomials that are
\Sigma\Pi
-n-sparse or
\Pi\Sigma\Pi
-n-sparse. Such polynomials of degree at most
d
can be stored in
O(n\log d)
space, provided that each coefficient in each presentation polynomial is
\tilde{O}(d)
. This is because storing each coefficient-exponent pair takes
O(\log d)
space, and at most
n
such pairs need to be stored.

Remark.
Suppose that we have a
\Pi
-n-sparse polynomial
p(x)
with presentation
p_{1}(x)\cdots p_{l}(x)
and we need to compute
p(x)\bmod p_{H}(x)
for some coset
H
as described above. Instead of computing the remainder using a standard method, we use the sparsity of
p(x)
and the fact that
p_{H}(x)
only has two terms, and replace each monomial
x^{a}
in each
p_{i}(x)
by its image modulo
p_{H}(x)
, which is itself a monomial. Computing a remainder polynomial as such takes
O(n\log d)
time and space. Similar arguments hold for
\Sigma\Pi
-n-sparse or
\Pi\Sigma\Pi
-n-sparse polynomials.
All remainders in both algorithms are computed as described above.

We use the techniques described above to obtain the following theorems:

Theorem 1.7
.

There is a deterministic
\tilde{O}(nt)
time,
\tilde{O}(n\log^{2}t)
space algorithm for
SubsetSum
.

Theorem 1.8
.

There is a randomized
\tilde{O}(n+t)
time,
\tilde{O}(n^{2}+n\log^{2}t)
space algorithm for
SubsetSum
.

The algorithm in Theorem
1.8
is Monte Carlo with one-sided error, in that it returns YES on a valid instance with probability
1-\frac{1}{\Omega(n+t)}
, and always returns NO on an invalid instance.

Deterministic Algorithm

We prove Theorem
1.7
, providing a simple deterministic algorithm. The novel ideas carry over neatly to the randomized algorithm.

We start by looking at the polynomial
A(x)=(1+x^{a_{1}})\cdots(1+x^{a_{n}})
. Note that this polynomial is
\Pi
-
O(n)
-sparse, and that when expanded, each coefficient in the polynomial is bounded from above by
nt
.

Claim 2.1
.

There is a prime
q\in(nt,nt\log nt]
, such that
q-1=2^{k}r
, where
r
is odd and at most
q^{1/2}
.

Proof.

Let
2^{k}
be the smallest power of two larger than
(nt\log nt)^{1/2}
. We then estimate the number of primes that are 1 (
\bmod\;2^{k}
) and lie in the range
(nt,nt\log nt]
using the theorem stated above. We have, for large enough
n
and
t
:

\displaystyle\pi(nt\log nt;2^{k},1)-\pi(nt;2^{k},1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\left[\frac{nt\log nt}{\log(nt\log nt)}-\frac{nt}{\log nt}\right]+E\left(nt\log nt,2^{k},1\right)-E\left(nt,2^{k},1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\left[\frac{nt\log nt}{\log(nt\log nt)}-\frac{2nt}{\log nt}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{nt}{2^{k-1}}\left[\frac{\log nt}{\log(nt\log nt)}-\frac{2}{\log nt}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{nt}{\log nt}\right)^{1/2}>1

where the first inequality follows from the fact that
E(x;a,d)=o(\pi(x;d,a))
, and the remaining follow from simple bounding. Thus, there is a prime of the form
r\cdot 2^{k}+1
in the interval
(nt,nt\log nt]
, where
r\leq(nt\log nt)^{1/2}\leq 2^{k}
. Thus,
q-1=2^{k}r\geq r^{2}
, giving us a prime with the required bound.
∎

We may run through the interval
(nt,nt\log nt]
and find such a prime in
\tilde{O}(nt)
time and
\tilde{O}(\polylog nt)
space deterministically. Simply check each number in the range for primality using the AKS primality test
[
AKS04
]
, and for each prime, successively divide by two to obtain the corresponding value of
r
, and accept if
r<q^{1/2}
. Upon obtaining such a prime
q

g
for
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
can be found in
\tilde{O}(nt)
time and
\tilde{O}(\polylog nt)
space, simply by iterating through each element of the group and using binary exponentiation to find the order. Write
q-1=2^{k}r
, as above. Note that
q=\tilde{\Theta}(nt)
We now assume that
n\leq t
; otherwise, we may use Bellman’s textbook (deterministic) algorithm that now runs in
\tilde{O}(nt)
time and
\tilde{O}(t)=\tilde{O}(n)
space.

Consider the polynomial
f(x)=A(x)x^{q-1-t}
, having degree
d=\tilde{\Theta}(nt)
. Note that the coefficient
c_{t}
of
x^{t}
in
A(x)
can be bounded as such:
c_{t}\leq nt<q
. Thus,
c_{t}=0
if and only if
\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}f(x)=0
. Let
\langle g\rangle
be the multiplicative group of the field and
l=\left\lfloor\log n\right\rfloor
.

We may now use Lemma
1.1
to isolate the required coefficient, using multipoint evaluation to compute the sum quickly. The obstacles discussed earlier are no longer relevant, since precomputation is not required at all, and we may compute remainders without using FFT, since

Claim 2.2
.

The algorithm
FastSumComputation
(
f(x)
,
\langle g\rangle
) returns
\sum_{x\in\langle g\rangle}f(x)
.

Proof.

The proof follows from an argument similar to that of the proof of the multipoint evaluation lemma. The only major difference is that we first divide the evaluation into
r
batches, instead of the usual binary split. It thus suffices to show the correctness of
FastBinaryCosetSum
on each batch.

We show the correctness of
FastBinaryCosetSum
(
p(x)
,
g^{s+j}\langle\left(g^{r}\right)^{2^{i}}\rangle
,
currentSum
) inductively. When
i=k-l
, the correctness follows from that of the multipoint evaluation lemma. For the inductive step, assume correctness for smaller values of
i
. Observe that
H\sqcup K=g^{s+j}\langle\left(g^{r}\right)^{2^{i}}\rangle
, and that
p(x)
and
p_{0}(x)
agree on
H
while
p(x)
and
p_{1}(x)
agree on
K
. It follows that the sum over
g^{s+j}\langle\left(g^{r}\right)^{2^{i}}\rangle
is correct as well.
∎

Claim 2.3
.

The algorithm
FastSumComputation
(
f(x)
,
\langle g\rangle
) runs in
\tilde{O}(nt)
time.

Proof.

We split the sum into
r
batches, and computing the remainder polynomial
f_{H}
for each batch takes
O(n\log d)
time, giving a total runtime of
O(nr\log d)
.

For a particular batch, the time taken to compute
FastBinaryCosetSum
(
f_{H}(x)
,
g^{s}\langle g^{r}\rangle
,
sum
) can be computed by looking at the corresponding recursion tree. The (binary) tree has depth
k-l
and so has
O(2^{k}/n)
nodes. Each leaf corresponds to a standard instance of multipoint evaluation on
n
points, and thus the time taken at each leaf is
O(n\polylog n)
. At each internal node, we spend
O(n\log d)
time computing the two remainder polynomials. Thus, the total time taken is:
O(\frac{2^{k}}{n}\cdot n\log d+\frac{2^{k}}{n}\cdot n\polylog n)=O(2^{k}(\log d+\polylog n))
.

Thus, the time taken across all batches is:
O(nr\log d+2^{k}(\log d+\polylog n)r)=O(nr\log d+q(\log d+\polylog n))
. Combined with the fact that
r<q^{1/2}
and
q=\tilde{\Theta}(d)
, the runtime is
\tilde{O}(nd^{1/2}+d)
. Since
d=\tilde{\Theta}(nt)
and
n\leq t
, the final runtime is
\tilde{O}(n^{3/2}t^{1/2}+nt)=\tilde{O}(nt)
.
∎

Claim 2.4
.

The algorithm
FastSumComputation
(
f(x)
,
\langle g\rangle
) runs in
\tilde{O}(n\log^{2}t)
space.

Proof.

We shall find the space used by FastBinaryCosetSum using induction. At depth
k-l
in the recursion tree, computing the sum using standard multipoint evaluation takes
O(n\polylog n)
space. Assume that at depth
i+1
, the space used is
O((k-l-i-1)n\log q+n\polylog n)
. At depth
i
, the space used by FastBinaryCosetSum(

,

,

) is
O(n\log q+(k-l-i-1)n\log q+n\polylog n)=O((k-l-i)n\log q+n\polylog n)
, where the the first term comes from storing the remainder polynomials and the second term comes from the space used by the recursive calls made. We may reuse the space used by each recursive call. Thus, FastBinaryCosetSum(
f_{H}(x)
,
g^{s}\langle g^{r}\rangle
,
sum
) takes
O(n\polylog n+(k-l)n\log q)=O(n\polylog n+n\log^{2}q)
space.

We split the computation in FastSumComputation(

,

) into

, as required.
∎

We may compute the required sum using the algorithm given above in the case

Randomized Near-Linear Time Algorithm

We use the techniques developed in the previous section, along with a clever two-step color-coding-based construction seen first in
[

]

]

[

Lemma
3.1
.

There exists a randomized algorithm that, on input a
SubsetSum
instance
(A,t)
, returns a polynomial
\tilde{A}(x)
of degree at most
2t\log^{3}n
, such that:

•

If
t
is a valid target, the coefficient of
x^{t}
in
\tilde{A}(x)
is nonzero with probability
1-\frac{1}{\Omega(n+t)}
;

•

If
t
is not a valid target, then the coefficient is zero with probability
1
.

Furthermore, the polynomial
\tilde{A}(x)
can be computed in
O(n\polylog t)
time and
\tilde{O}(n\log t)
space.

Proof.

(Very rough sketch, the construction is explained in greater detail in the
Appendix
)

The idea is to construct the polynomial
A(x)
and
collapse
certain terms in the product randomly. Suppose
t
is a valid target and
R
is a subset of
A
that sums to
t
. Call the elements of
R

relevant
. The idea is to partition
A
randomly, ensuring with high probability that no two relevant elements end up in the same partition. Suppose such a partition is
A=\bigsqcup_{i}T_{i}
. We may then construct the polynomial
\tilde{A}(x)=\prod_{i}(1+\sum_{a\in T_{i}}x^{a})
. Note that the coefficient of
x^{t}
is nonzero with high probability, since it is unlikely that any two relevant items lie in the same
T_{i}
. Note that such partitions cannot give us false positives, since we only limit the possible subsums by partitioning.

In particular, it is important to note that the polynomial
\tilde{A}(x)
is
\Pi\Sigma\Pi
-
\tilde{O}(n)
-sparse and has a presentation in which each monomial has coefficients from
\{0,1\}
. Expanding the polynomial out, we see that the coefficients are
\tilde{O}(t)
.
∎

We define
d=2t\log^{3}n
, and let
c_{t}
denote the coefficient of
x^{t}
in
\tilde{A}(x)
. Note that
c_{t}
is at most the number of subsets of
A
that add to
t
, which in turn is at most
2^{n}
. Then, the number of primes larger than
d
that divide
c_{t}
is bounded from above by
\frac{n}{\log d}
.

We may assume that
n\leq\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{d/\log d}
; otherwise, we may use the near-linear time algorithm given by Bringmann
[
Bri17
]
, which now runs in
\tilde{O}(n+t)
time and
\tilde{O}(t)=\tilde{O}(n^{2})
space.

Claim 3.2
.

There are at least
3n/\log d
primes
q\in(d,d\log d]
, such that
q-1=2^{k}r
, where
r<q^{1/2}
and
r
is odd.

Proof.

Let
2^{k}
be the smallest power of two larger than
(d\log d)^{1/2}
. We shall now estimate the number of primes that are 1 (
\bmod\;2^{k}
) and lie in the range
(d,d\log d]
, again using the theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions.

\displaystyle\pi(d\log d;2^{k},1)-\pi(d;2^{k},1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\left[\frac{d\log d}{\log(d\log d)}-\frac{d}{\log d}\right]+E\left(d\log d,2^{k},1\right)-E\left(d,2^{k},1\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\left[\frac{d\log d}{\log(d\log d)}-\frac{2d}{\log d}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{2^{k-1}\cdot\log d}\left[\frac{\log d}{1+\frac{\log\log d}{\log d}}-2\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d^{1/2}}{\sqrt{\log d}\log d}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3n}{\log d}

where the first inequality again follows from
E(x;d,a)=o(\pi(x;d,a))
and the third from the fact that
n\leq\frac{1}{3}\sqrt{d/\log d}
. Then, if
q
is such a prime, we may write
q-1=2^{k}r
, where
r\leq\frac{q}{2^{k}}\leq q^{1/2}
.
∎

Now consider the following algorithm:

Input:

if there exists a subset summing to

,
False
otherwise

if

Compute the polynomial
\tilde{A}(x)

Choose a prime

g

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}

Repeat the last two steps
\log(n+t)

Algorithm 3

LinearLowSpaceSubsetSum
(
A
,
t
)

Suppose
t
is a valid target. For
LinearLowSpaceSubsetSum
(

,

to divide

, which happens with probability
(\frac{2}{3})^{\log(n+t)}=\frac{1}{\Omega(n+t)}
, or for
c_{t}
to be zero, which happens with probability
\frac{1}{\Omega(n+t)}
. The union bound tells us that the final error probability is
1-\frac{1}{\Omega(n+t)}
.

On the other hand, the algorithm is always correct when
t
is not a valid target, since the sum computed is always zero.

Claim 3.3
.

The algorithm
LinearLowSpaceSubsetSum
(
A
,

\tilde{O}(n+t)
time and
\tilde{O}(n^{2}+n\log^{2}t)

Proof.

Computing the polynomial
\tilde{A}(x)
takes
O(n\polylog t)
time and
\tilde{O}(n\log t)
space. As before, we use the AKS primality test to find the primes of the required form in near-linear time and low space. Since the polynomial
\tilde{A}(x)x^{q-1-t}
is
\Pi\Sigma\Pi
-
\tilde{O}(n)

.
∎

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we presented two new algorithms for
SubsetSum
that speed up previous low-space approaches. We presented a deterministic algorithm that runs in
\tilde{O}(nt)
time and
\tilde{O}(n\log^{2}t)
space along with a near-linear
\tilde{O}(n+t)
time randomized algorithm that uses only
\tilde{O}(n^{2}+n\log^{2}t)
space. Our key contribution was to use a divide-and-conquer approach to speed up the computation of the sum of a polynomial over the multiplicative group of a finite field. An important point is that our contributions do not use randomness at all. Any algorithm for
SubsetSum
that uses Kane’s summation trick can be sped up using the techniques shown here.

We conclude with some open problems. Jin et al. present an algorithm running in
\tilde{O}(nt)
time
O(\log nt)
space. Is there a time-space tradeoff algorithm that interpolates between their algorithm and the
\tilde{O}(n+t)
time
\tilde{O}(n^{2}+n\log t)
space randomized algorithm presented here? Is there a way to construct a deterministic polynomial space algorithm that runs in
O(\sqrt{n}t)
time, giving a polynomial space variant of the algorithm given in Koiliaris & Xu
[
KX19
]
? Can the space used by the randomized algorithm given in Theorem
1.8
be improved to
O(\log(nt))
?

Appendix

We summarize the construction of the polynomial in Lemma
3.1
, taken from
[
JVW21
]
. They first construct a hash family with the following properties:

Lemma
4.1
.

There is an
efficiently invertible
hash family
\mathcal{H}=\{h:[n]\xrightarrow{}[m]\}
, for all
0\leq m\leq n
, with the following
load-balancing
property: given a set
S\subset[n],|S|=m
, with high probability,
|S\cap h^{-1}(\{i\})|\leq C\log n
, for each
1\leq i\leq m
and some
C>0
depending only on
\mathcal{H}
, where
h
is chosen uniformly at random from the hash family. A hash function can be specified using a seed of length
\tilde{O}(\log n)
.

Intuitively, the hash family distributes each subset of size
m
over the image with very few collisions. We now give the algorithm to construct the polynomial:

Layer Splitting:
Split the multiset
A
into
\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
layers as such:
L_{i}=(t/2^{i},\,t/2^{i-1}]\cap A
,
\forall\,1\leq i\leq\lceil\log n\rceil-1
and
L_{\lceil\log n\rceil}=A\setminus\left(L_{1}\cup\cdots\cup L_{\lceil\log n\rceil-1}\right)
.

Two-Layer Partition:
Call a subset
R
of
A

relevant
if the sum of the elements in
R
is
t
. Note that the
i
th layer contains at most
2^{i}
relevant elements. We now split this layer using a random hash function
h:[|L_{i}|]\xrightarrow{}[2^{i}]
from the hash family shown above. Thus, we partition
L_{i}=S_{1}\sqcup S_{2}\cdots\sqcup S_{2^{i}}
, and the load-balancing property tells us that each
S_{j}
contains at most
O(\log n)
relevant elements. We extract each
S_{j}
using the
efficient invertibilty
of the hash family. Let
r=\left\lceil\log n\right\rceil
. We now partition each
S_{j}
into
r^{2}
disjoint sets
T_{1},\cdots,T_{r^{2}}
by placing each element of
S_{j}
into a set
T_{i}
, where
i
is chosen uniformly at random. A simple counting argument shows that the relevant elements in
S_{j}
all go into different sets in the partition with probability at least
3/4
.

Thus, the polynomial
\prod_{i=1}^{r^{2}}(1+\sum_{a\in T_{i}}x^{a})
contains the monomial
x^{c}
, where
c
is the sum of the relevant items in
S_{j}
, with probability at least
3/4
. We repeat this last step
\Theta(r)
times and add all the polynomials up. This gives a polynomial of the form
\sum_{\Theta(r)}\prod_{i=1}^{r^{2}}(1+\sum_{a\in T_{i}}x^{a})
, that contains the monomial
x^{c}
with probability at least
1-1/n^{O(1)}
. Such a procedure associates a polynomial with each
S_{j}
. We now multiply all such polynomials together, giving us a polynomial
\tilde{A}(x)=\prod_{L_{i}}\prod_{j}\sum_{r}\prod_{i=1}^{r^{2}}(1+\sum_{a\in T_{i}}x^{a})
.

A careful argument shows that this polynomial contains a monomial of the form
x^{t}
with probability
1-1/n^{O(1)}
, if
t
is a valid target. Note that such a polynomial can only contain monomials with exponents corresponding to subsums. Thus, if
t
is not a valid target, the polynomial shown above will not contain the monomial
x^{t}
.

It can be shown that the algorithm described above can be implemented in
O(n\polylog t)
time and
\tilde{O}(n\log t)
space. Note that each element of the set
A
can appear as an exponent in
\tilde{A}(x)
at most
O(\log n)
times (this happens because of the repetition done to boost the probability from
3/4
to
1-1/n
). This shows us that the polynomial constructed is
\Pi\Sigma\Pi
-
\tilde{O}(n)
-sparse. Bounding the degree gives us that the polynomial
\tilde{A}(x)
has degree at most
2(\log^{3}n)\max A\leq 2(\log^{3}n)t
.
