Hoang Khang Phan, Nhat Tan Le

Abstract
. Psychological stress is a widespread issue that significantly impacts student well-being and academic performance. Effective remote stress recognition is crucial, yet existing methods often rely on wearable devices or GPS-based clustering techniques that pose privacy risks. In this study, we introduce a novel, end-to-end privacy-enhanced framework for semantic location encoding using a self-hosted OSM engine and an LLM-bootstrapped static map. We rigorously quantify the privacy-utility trade-off and demonstrate (via LOSO validation) that our Privacy-Aware (PA) model achieves performance statistically indistinguishable from a non-private model, proving that utility does not require sacrificing privacy. Feature importance analysis highlights that recreational activity time, working time, and travel time play a significant role in stress recognition.



1.
Introduction

In a fast-paced society, stress, which stems from heightened professional, academic, and social demands, increasingly afflicts individuals, manifesting as diminished productivity and posing risks to well-being and longevity.
In 2018, over 70% of people reported having felt so stressed that they have been overwhelmed or unable to cope in a YouGov survey
(
3
)
.
Notably, in 2024, 75% of high school students feel constant stress
(
32
)
. Additionally, stress contributes to a 55% loss of workdays globally, equivalent to 17 million working days, and costs the US industry over 300 billion dollars annually
(
31
)
.
Hence, a precise and accessible stress recognition tool is crucial for early detection, improving work performance, and overall health. This highlights an urgent need for accessible and responsible AI-driven tools that can help identify early signs of distress without compromising user privacy.

Smartphones, ubiquitous in modern life, also hold potential for ongoing stress management
(
2
;
35
;
37
;
11
)
, demonstrating successful stress detection using smartphone sensor data.
Despite limitations in capturing certain biometric data, smartphones provide valuable insights into user behavior, such as movement, conversation, sleep quality, and individuals’ activity. However, the analysis of location and mobility-based features has been relatively underexplored due to challenges in real-time prediction, users’ privacy, and data processing.

Location data, an easy-to-interpret data type, offers valuable insights into human activity patterns
(
15
;
27
)
-such as recreational time, work hours, and travel duration-that can reflect an individual’s emotional state
(
37
)
. For instance, a vacation may be indicated by reduced time at home or at work and increased leisure or shopping activities, suggesting efforts to mitigate stress. Therefore, a detailed analysis of GPS data can be highly beneficial for understanding psychological stress in individuals.

Nevertheless, the privacy aspect of GPS-involved systems was underdeveloped. Existing methods for effective remote stress recognition often depended on GPS-based clustering techniques
(
35
;
24
)
that posed privacy risks.

To address this gap, this study introduces a GPS encoding method designed to transform raw location data into behaviorally meaningful and privacy-aware features, all without storing identifiable coordinates. In contrast to conventional approaches that store sensitive location clusters, this method transforms GPS data into less identifiable, categorical location types that are related to daily life, such as time spent at home, school, or recreational areas, and analyzes their correlation with stress.

In this work, we aim to:

(1)

Extract high-level, privacy-enhanced location categories from GPS data-such as home, school, and recreation-that reflect students’ daily lives.

(2)

Leverage these extracted features with machine learning techniques to predict daily stress, allowing the study to quantify the privacy-utility trade-off in GPS-based stress recognition.

2.1.
Stress Recognition

Traditional Machine Learning Approach

The StudentLife dataset
(
34
)
, a comprehensive collection of smartphone-derived data, provides a valuable foundation for examining student behavior and its impact on academic outcomes. Wang et al. leveraged this dataset to explore the correlation between daily living patterns and student learning, extending beyond traditional academic metrics. Upon the StudentLife research, many studies
(
35
;
37
;
24
)
leverage various mobile sensing data for their application, such as conversation, activity, etc., for mental health assessments. These research used RAPIDS
(
33
)
, which framework built for feature extraction from all resources from mobile sensing data as acceleration, conversation, location, etc, as the backbone for their application. However, this approach raises concerns about the effective utilization of mobile sensing data, privacy threats, and transparency for mental health assessment applications.

Furthermore, most prior works in mobile sensing for mental health recognition used DBSCAN for localizing frequent locations, but present notable limitations. Density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN)
(
12
)
, while effective in identifying clusters of prolonged presence, inherently discards data related to less frequent activities, such as recreational outings or travel. This results in a substantial loss of potentially valuable information about less time-consuming and random activities as recreation or traveling.

Deep Learning Approach

The state-of-the-art, Deep Spatio-Temporal Graph Neural Network (STGNN) approach has demonstrated strength in handling GPS data. For instance,
14
showed that using only raw GPS data, an STGNN improved stress classification by 3%, increasing the accuracy and F1 score from the 58% and 55% achieved by traditional GCN methods. However, such deep learning approaches present challenges, including a lack of model transparency and, more importantly, significant privacy concerns due to their reliance on precise location coordinates.

2.2.
Privacy-preserving Methods and Limitations

While robust privacy-preserving methods are well-established for data types like speech
(
1
)
, text
(
28
)
, and facial images
(
9
)
, their application to location data presents unique challenges. Formal techniques, such as differential privacy
(
36
)
for location-based applications, provide mathematical guarantees by altering the original data by adding noise or transform the original data by mathematics functions, which often associate with a high cost to data utility. In the context of behavioral modeling, such an approach can alter the data so significantly that it obscures the subtle nuances of a person’s location experience, which in turn affects the explainability of the application.

Another important method for protecting location data is obfuscation (or spatial cloaking)
(
39
)
, where coordinates are intentionally made less precise to enhance user privacy. However, this approach still involves storing users’ sensitive location data, which poses a significant privacy risk.

Federated Learning
(
22
)
, a decentralized approach to machine learning deployment, is another noteworthy technique for enhancing user privacy. In this framework, edge devices (e.g., smartphones, personal computers, etc.) participate in model training alongside a central server. The devices locally train the model on their data and then send only the updated weights-subject to predefined constraints-to the server, which aggregates them to update the global model. The updated global model is then distributed back to the participating devices. This process ensures that users’ raw data never leaves their devices, thereby offering strong privacy protection.

Nevertheless, recent studies have shown that attackers can exploit vulnerabilities in this setup. For instance, they may reverse-engineer model weights to reconstruct sensitive data (reconstruction attacks)
(
40
;
13
)
, or poison the model during training (model poisoning attacks)
(
38
)
. These threats highlight persistent privacy and model safety risks for users despite the advantages of federated learning.

These limitations of existing methods highlight the need for a more granular framework specifically designed for the complexities of location information.
19
provide such a framework by identifying three key aspects of location privacy: identity, spatial, and temporal. Adopting this multi-faceted view allows for the development of more targeted privacy-enhancing solutions that can better balance data protection with the utility required for specific applications.

Guided by these structured understanding, our work develops a location encoding method (PA) that directly addresses the challenges outlined by
19
’s framework. Instead of applying a uniform privacy mechanism, our approach, which is detailed in Subsection
3.6
, is specifically designed to mitigate spatial and temporal privacy risks. By transforming raw coordinates into meaningful categories and analyzing aggregated time-based features, we preserve the essential behavioral signals for our model while enhancing the privacy of the user’s location data.

Methodology

3.1.
Dataset

The StudentLife dataset
(
34
)
was used for feature mining and insight analysis in this study.
This dataset, gathered in 2014, utilized an Android app to consistently track various data streams, including GPS, conversation, activity recognition, etc., alongside monitoring the mental health status, deadlines, etc., of the participants. The dataset comprises information from 48 students over a 10-week academic term at Dartmouth College.
In addition, notwithstanding the age of the data, the StudentLife dataset continues to serve as a canonical benchmark in computational behavior recognition
(
14
;
21
)
.

This research specifically focuses on utilizing subsets of the dataset, namely the GPS (recorded in 20-minute intervals) and academic information for analysis and exploration.

In this work, time, longitude, and latitude from the GPS data stream from StudentLife research
(
34
)
were utilized as the source for GPS data. Apart from that, we also used 3 sets of StudentLife academic-based data streams, shown below, to enrich the feature set:

•

Class stream: contains the classes of each volunteer in the examined semester.

•

Class info stream: contains the information of each class: class name, location, and period.

•

Deadline stream: contains the deadlines of each day for every volunteer.

Finally, we used the EMA stress response dataset for labeling the stress status of the students.
In this dataset, the stress label is collected at 5 levels: a little stressed, definitely stressed, stressed out, feeling good, and feeling great, and labeled from 1 to 5, respectively.
However, this label does not show a linear progression between the stress levels and the label. Hence, we employ label transformation, which transforms labels from 1 to 5 to represent feeling great, feeling good, a little stressed, definitely stressed, and stressed out.

Following that, for some days, receive multiple stress reports, the final stress report will be the median of the report values. After that, a binary stress level was employed by assigning a stress level of less than 3 to no stress (0) and stress otherwise (as suggested in
(
14
;
6
)
). Ultimately, we received 1131 records from the StudentLife dataset.

3.2.
Location encoding method

The influence of environmental context on human emotional states is well-documented, with studies indicating that exposure to natural environments, such as parks and green spaces, often elicits positive affective responses, while confined or stressful environments, like workplaces, can contribute to heightened stress levels
(
25
;
18
)
. This phenomenon is attributed, in part, to the activation of the autonomic nervous system in response to perceived environmental cues.

In this research, to quantify the relationship between student location patterns and their potential impact on well-being, our method is designed to transform raw GPS coordinates into semantic features in a privacy-aware, two-step process:

First, raw coordinates are mapped to address types and numbers. The implementation of this step involves a self-hosted reverse-geocoding engine using a pre-downloaded OpenStreetMap (OSM)
(
26
)
database. This architecture ensures no identifiable GPS data is transmitted to a third party server. Then the coordinates is being sent to self-hosted server for reverse geocoding. As a result, we collected 103 distinct location types and identities. This location identity, which is used to extract the number of locations visited, is made by concatenating the address number and address type.

Second, these 103 granular location types are classified into key life-related categories. LLMs, which have been shown to be powerful and highly accurate (up to 92%) for recognizing location function
(
20
)
, offer a valuable capability for this categorization, especially in real-world scenarios where the system encounters previously unseen cases. Our method implements this as a static, pre-defined map (e.g., ’dormitory’
\rightarrow
’home’), which runs locally and requires no external API calls, thus enhancing system privacy
(
5
)
. To develop this map, we employed the public Gemini 2.5 LLM
(
30
)
as a bootstrapping tool, selected for its clear privacy policy. We used the zero-shot prompt: "Please classify
location_type
into one of the following groups: home, school, shop, workplace, recreation, travel, and others. Respond with only the group name". To validate the LLM’s output, we created a ground truth by manually labeling all 103 types. The LLM’s classification achieved a 90% zero-shot accuracy against this ground truth, ensuring the reliability and ethical grounding of the static map used in our research.

3.3.1.
Location-based features

As the environment plays a pivotal role in human feeling, we then calculate the total time spent and standard deviation by each participant in each location function (see Section
3.2
), during defined daytime (6:00 AM to 6:00 PM), nighttime (6:00 PM to 6:00 AM), and only total time spent in the whole day (24 hours) period for location-function-based features. To quantify time spent outside of primary academic and domestic environments, we compute the cumulative time spent in all categories, excluding ’home’ and ’school.’ To assess the balance between academic and personal life, we calculate the difference between daytime and nighttime durations spent at home and school, as expressed in equation (
1
):

(1)

feature\_time_{d\_vs\_n}=feature\_time_{d}-feature\_time_{n}

where feature represents either ’home’ or ’school’ and d and n represent day and night, respectively. Ultimately, 38 location-function-based features were extracted.

To quantify mobility patterns, 3 location-address-based features were extracted. Firstly, we collected location address numbers to determine the number of unique locations visited via the total number of different address identities. Furthermore, we defined "repetition" as the number of instances a participant visits the same address identity (e.g., 10 Apartment) within a specified timeframe. Mobility activity repetition metrics will be calculated on both a daily and weekly basis.

3.3.2.
Academic-based features

Academic stressors, such as demanding class schedules and impending deadlines, are prevalent in student life, often leading to heightened levels of exhaustion and stress. Therefore, this study aims to quantify the relationship between class schedules, attendance patterns, deadline proximity, and student stress.

In this research, we first extracted class schedule data from class information and the class dataset. Attendance rates were then calculated by comparing scheduled class times with time spent in academic locations, as determined by GPS data (refer to Section
3.3.1
for details on location data processing). An attendance rate below 0.7 was defined as an indicator of potential class absenteeism. We then examined instances of potential class absenteeism across five temporal windows - the day of, the day after, two days after, three days after, and seven days after the scheduled class - to assess the temporal impact of absenteeism on student stress.

Furthermore, we extracted deadline information from course syllabi to quantify the proximity of upcoming assignments. We assessed the status of deadlines on the day of data collection and then examined the three days preceding the deadline (three, two, and one day prior) to analyze the escalating impact of deadline proximity on student stress.

3.3.3.
Time-based features

Time is an omnipresent factor in human life, influencing behavior and thought patterns in profound ways. For example, individuals often experience a sense of anticipation as Friday or holiday weeks approach
(
29
)
, while feelings of fatigue and resignation can become more pronounced as Monday comes to a close
(
4
)
.

Recognizing these temporal variations, we have incorporated two key features into our analysis-the "date in week" and the "week index." These features enable us to capture the dynamic role of time in shaping student mental stress behavior across an academic semester. By accounting for both the specific day of the week and the position within the semester, our approach allows for a nuanced understanding of how academic pressures affect mental well-being during periods such as midterms, finals, and weekends.

3.4.
Classification model

In this study, we employed two ensemble learning models, Random Forest and XGBoost with random search , to predict daily stress levels.

•

Random Forest (RF) is a learning model that, according to the Gini impurity factor, combines decision trees, and the decision is based on the Gini coefficient. The result of the RF model is the average of the results from the decision trees within it. It aggregates results from multiple decision trees to create a model with low bias and variance
(
7
)
. In this study, base RF was tuned with
n_estimators=70
.

•

XGBoost (XGB) is an optimized distributed gradient boosting library designed to be highly efficient, flexible, and portable
(
10
)
. It implements machine learning algorithms under the Gradient Boosting framework. XGBoost provides a parallel tree boosting that solves many data science problems in a fast and accurate way. This model was set with
eta=0.2
and

.

3.5.
Threat modeling

In ubiquitous computing systems, the risk of data being exploited by malicious actors is a primary concern for user privacy. This section presents a simulation to quantify the privacy-preservation guarantees of our encoding strategy against a re-identification attack.

In this work, we define a threat model where an attacker has gained access to the database. The attacker’s objective is to re-identify specific users from the supposedly anonymized data. We simulate three scenarios based on the attacker’s prior knowledge, corresponding to the amount of data they possess:

(1)

Rich Knowledge: The attacker has a large dataset of known user behavior (80% train, 20% test).

(2)

Moderate Knowledge: The attacker has a balanced dataset (50% train, 50% test).

(3)

Limited Knowledge: The attacker has very little prior data (20% train, 80% test).

To evaluate the risk, we compare the re-identification accuracy across three feature sets:

(1)

Privacy-Aware (PA): Our proposed privacy-enhanced model, which removes the high-risk repetition-based and academic-based features.

(2)

All-Features (AF): The full feature set, which includes the daily and weekly location repetition features identified as a privacy risk.

(3)

Raw GPS Baseline: A set of five simple statistical features (mean, max, min, standard deviation, and inter-quartile range) extracted directly from the volunteers’ daily latitude and longitude coordinates.

For each of the three "knowledge" scenarios, we trained an XGBoost classifier to predict the user’s ID. The model’s accuracy on the test set represents the success rate of the attack. A lower accuracy score indicates a more robust defense against re-identification and, therefore, a stronger privacy guarantee.

3.6.
Evaluation settings

After feature extraction, two feature sets were examined for students’ stress recognition, including PA and AF (see table
8
).

These feature sets were evaluated by accuracy (Acc) and F1 score (F1) in three conditions: 75:25 random split, 10-fold cross-validation, and LOSO validation. In each condition, we acknowledge an imbalance between the positive and negative stress classes. Consequently, the data will be balanced via the Edited Nearest Neighbour Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTEENN)
(
16
)
for a 75:25 random split and 10-fold cross-validation, and Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE)
(
8
)
for LOSO validation. In addition, due to some participants having little data, the test person in the LOSO technique must have more than 10 days of data for a fair result in LOSO validation.

5.
Discussion

A key contribution of this research is the introduction of a novel GPS encoding and LLM-assisted feature extraction pipeline, designed to enhance stress recognition while being capable of mitigating re-identification attacks. This approach significantly improves upon existing methods, outperforming the GPS only state-of-the-art
(
14
)
by 6% in F1 score and accuracy. Critically, our study also proposes a pathway for leveraging location characteristics to advance stress recognition research in a privacy-conscious manner. By focusing on inherent location-based features, this work paves the way for future studies that prioritize user privacy and the generalizability aspect in mental well-being detection.

5.1.
Behavioral Insights on Stress Patterns

Our visualization reveals that the happy student spends more time on recreation tasks in comparison with their counterparts. Particularly, the non-stressed individuals often go out for 2 hours for recreation, which is 50% more than those who suffer from stress (see Figure

More importantly, figure
5
shows that stressed students were rarely observed at the workplace, whereas their happier counterparts consistently spent about one hour there during a typical study week. We hypothesize that the workplace may serve as more than just a professional setting-it could also function as a social hub where individuals have greater opportunities to interact and form meaningful connections. These interactions likely facilitate the sharing of emotions and the development of empathy, which in turn can contribute to improved mood and overall well-being. The positive social dynamics observed in the workplace may act as a buffer against stress, promoting both emotional expression and mutual support among students. This observation opens intriguing avenues for future research. Specifically, further studies could employ both qualitative and quantitative methods to explore how social interactions in work settings influence stress levels, emotional regulation, and mental health outcomes within academic environments.

5.2.
Social Impact

Rising academic and professional pressures in the modern world are leading to an increase in mental health challenges, reflecting a worldwide issue where stress negatively impacts both individual well-being and overall productivity. Our work can be applied to address this by allowing users to identify early indicators of mental distress. This would empower students to understand more about themselves. In addition, such a tool will allow educators to intervene effectively and provide crucial assistance to students who are struggling.

For students experiencing stress, our application can provide deeper insights into the specific causes of their difficulties. A student may, for example, be struggling to balance work, studies, and their personal life. By leveraging the invaluable transparency machine learning algorithm provides, the student can better understand which factors might be contributing to the issue.

For educators, we advocate for using this information to foster support among students. For example, administrators could use insights into student stress levels to organize timely and helpful activities, such as workshops or social events where students can de-stress and share their experiences. This approach uses the data to build a supportive community and relieve pressure, fulfilling the technology’s intended purpose.

By contrast to this potential, there is a significant risk if the application. A university administration, for instance, could misapply stress-level data in a punitive manner, such as flagging students as "at-risk." This could trigger a cycle of mandatory, and potentially unwelcome, meetings that could paradoxically increase the very stress the system was designed to detect.

5.3.
Limitation

Due to the scarcity of open-source datasets containing raw GPS trajectories required for our baseline comparison, the StudentLife dataset
(
34
)
, which was collected in 2014, was chosen as a benchmark dataset. Although this dataset is a foundational benchmark for mobile sensing research, the intervening decade has seen some shifts in student behavior, technology integration, and lifestyle patterns. Consequently, while our findings on the privacy-utility trade-off are methodologically robust, the specific behavioral insights-such as the correlations between stress and time spent at work or recreation-may be temporally bound and not fully representative of current student experiences.

In addition, although our most accurate model demonstrates strong stress recognition capabilities, its reliance on repetition-based features introduces a significant privacy risk. The exposure of these features could allow an adversary to re-identify a student by correlating their frequent visits to specific types of locations, making it possible to infer their personal schedule even without access to raw GPS coordinates.

For instance, an attacker who knows "Student X" has a unique weekly schedule-such as a part-time job at a campus office and regular volunteering-could analyze features that count daily and weekly location repetitions. This pattern creates a unique digital fingerprint, allowing the attacker to successfully re-identify Student X and compromise their privacy.

To address this vulnerability, we introduced a privacy-aware (PA) model that effectively limits re-identification by completely removing these vulnerable features. In the "Student X" example, the PA model discards the features that track visit frequency. An attacker would now only see the total duration Student X spent in the "workplace" and "recreation" categories over the week.

This aggregated data is far less unique, as many other students might spend a similar amount of time in these categories, but with different, irregular patterns. Without the frequency data, Student X’s unique schedule becomes indistinguishable from the general behavior of other participants, thus preserving their anonymity.

While this enhancement comes with a slight trade-off-a 1% decrease in both accuracy and F1-score in random sampling scenarios, there are zero statistically significant trade-off for KFold and LOSO. Furthermore, our method also offers a robust and viable alternative for stronger privacy protection (see Figure
2
and Table
2
).

Another limitation of our approach lies in the classification of location types. Given the inherently ambiguous function of many locations, our single-label classification poses a potential challenge. For example, a single coffee shop could be a venue for recreation, a remote workplace, or simply a place for dining. This multifunctional characteristic means our system may not capture the user’s true context.

While this work provides a complete algorithmic framework for privacy-aware stress recognition from data-at-rest, we have also identified the critical path to solving end-to-end privacy. Future work should aim for a more precise grouping of location functions by aligning both the location type and temporal patterns. A more in-depth analysis could, for instance, use the time of day or day of the week to infer whether a visit to a location is for work-related activities or leisure. More importantly, future studies should also explore deeper in on-device LLM, such as Llama or Qwen, to further enhance users’ privacy, location encoding accuracy, and edge-case handling.

6.
Conclusion

In this paper, we present an approach to enhance privacy and compares the performance of stress recognition between the proposed and the traditional method. Our methodology incorporates a GPS encoding strategy, which transforms GPS coordinates into more private location features, thereby ensuring individual privacy. Additionally, we leverage the capabilities of Gemini 2.5, a Large Language Model (LLM), to categorize these location features into meaningful categories of home, school, recreational activity, shopping, working, travel, and others, which are crucial for assessing students’ daily stress status.

Our experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach, achieving a 64% F1 score and 67% accuracy for the PA setting for the stress level prediction task while decrease top 1 re-identification accuracy by 5 to 20% to AF scenario. In addition, the LOSO scenarios demonstrate the robust generalizability of this approach. On entirely unseen subjects, our Privacy-Aware (PA) model achieved an F1-score of 70%, a performance statistically indistinguishable from the substantially less private AF model (71% F1). This not only underscores the potential of GPS data in stress recognition but also highlights the importance of the comprehensive utilization of limited data sources and users’ privacy.
