Concept inconsistency in dermoscopic concept bottleneck models: a rough-set analysis of the Derm7pt dataset
Authors: Gonzalo Nápoles, Isel Grau, Yamisleydi Salgueiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConcept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) are devoted to melanoma classification route predictions through a clinically grounded concept layer, which binds interpretability to concept-label consistency. When a dataset contains concept-level inconsistencies, identical concept profiles mapped to conflicting diagnosis labels create an unresolvable bottleneck that imposes a hard ceiling on achievable accuracy. In this paper, we apply rough set theory to the Derm7pt dermoscopy benchmark and characterize, for the first time, the full extent and clinical structure of this inconsistency. Among 305 unique concept profiles formed by the dermoscopic criteria of the 7-point melanoma checklist, 50 (16.4%) are inconsistent and span 306 images (30.3% of the dataset). This yields a theoretical accuracy ceiling of 92.1% for any hard CBM trained on the full raw data, where over half of all melanoma images carry concept signatures belonging to the boundary region. This disproportionate fraction shows that the checklist concepts are less discriminative for melanoma than for non-melanoma lesions. We characterize the conflict-severity distribution and identify the clinical features most responsible for boundary ambiguity. Two filtering strategies are proposed to remove images with inconsistent concept signatures, both producing a benchmark that we refer to as Derm7pt+. The symmetric strategy yields a fully consistent subset of 705 images, while the asymmetric strategy retains all melanoma images and removes only the conflicting non-melanoma counterparts, producing 841 images. We evaluate a hard CBM across 19 backbone architectures from the EfficientNet, DenseNet, ResNet, and Wide ResNet families on both Derm7pt+ variants. Under symmetric filtering, DenseNet-169 achieves the best test macro F1 of 0.80± 0.01, and EfficientNet-B4 leads under asymmetric filtering with a test macro F1 of 0.82± 0.01. Macro-averaged concept accuracy remains moderate yet stable across all configurations, and this level of concept prediction suffices to produce solid label macro F1 scores. This shows that annotation noise rather than backbone capacity is the binding constraint on bottleneck quality. We further apply the Sparseness-Optimized Feature Importance (SOFI) explainer to the true positive melanoma predictions of the best-performing model on the symmetric Derm7pt+. We found that irregular dots and globules drives every melanoma prediction, while irregular streaks, atypical pigment network, and present blue-whitish veil form a consistent secondary tier.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) were introduced by Koh et al.1 as a class of two-stage neural architectures that route all predictions through an intermediate layer of human-interpretable concepts before producing a final label. In their original formulation, the backbone first maps the raw input to a vector of concept predictions, and a downstream predictor maps that concept vector to the output class. This design admits two principal variants: soft CBMs, in which the label predictor receives continuous probability scores over each concept, and hard CBMs, in which those scores are discretized into binary or categorical assignments before being passed downstream2. While soft CBMs achieve higher task accuracy, they suffer from concept leakage, a phenomenon in which the label predictor implicitly encodes information beyond the intended concept values3. Hard CBMs are resilient to leakage by construction since the bottleneck is a stop-gradient discrete tensor, so the label predictor can only access information that a clinician would observe by reading the concept assignments. Both variants support test-time human interventions, where an expert corrects a mispredicted concept, and the updated prediction propagates forward through the label head, a capability that has been shown to improve accuracy substantially4. Extensions of the original framework include Concept Embedding Models5, which go beyond the accuracy-vs-interpretability trade-off by learning high-dimensional concept representations. Similarly, Post-hoc Concept Bottleneck Models6 retrofit the bottleneck onto already-trained networks by transferring concepts from other datasets without requiring concept-annotated training data. More recent variants, such as Label-free CBMs7 or the UCBM architecture8, automate concept set construction using vision-language models or latent concept discovery, respectively. These architectures are part of a broader effort to reconcile the accuracy of deep neural networks with the transparency demands of symbolic and logic-based reasoning9. The medical imaging domain10,11, and dermoscopy in particular, has become a natural testbed for these architectures, given the availability of structured clinical vocabularies that map directly onto concept bottlenecks and the need for structured explanations.

In dermatology, deep neural networks have achieved accuracy comparable to board-certified dermatologists in skin lesion classification 12–14, yet their black-box nature limits clinical adoption15. A systematic review of XAI in skin cancer recognition 16 found that the overwhelming majority of published studies rely on post-hoc saliency methods, with inherently interpretable approaches remaining substantially underrepresented. Widely adopted post-hoc saliency methods, such as LIME17, SHAP18, or Grad-CAM19 and its variants20–22, produce feature attributions that identify which regions of an image drove a particular prediction. While these methods can highlight diagnostically relevant skin regions, they neither name nor constrain the clinical concepts used in the decision, so a clinician cannot verify whether the model reasoned in terms of recognized dermoscopic criteria. Moreover, they are frequently rejected as solutions to the transparency problem in clinical settings due to the risk of introducing confirmation bias16. CBMs address this gap by grounding predictions in dermoscopic criteria that dermatologists use in practice, such as pigment network morphology, vascular patterns, and the presence of a blue-whitish veil. Lucieri et al.23 showed that deep classifiers trained on dermoscopic images encode disease-related concepts in their latent space in a manner consistent with dermatological descriptions. Recent work has extended this to inherently interpretable frameworks, showing that CBMs trained on curated concept annotations can be applied to skin lesion diagnosis without sacrificing classification performance24. Moreover, concept-grounded approaches yield explanations that post-hoc saliency methods cannot guarantee25. When concept-grounded explanations are provided alongside predictions, clinicians’ diagnostic accuracy, confidence, and trust in the system increase compared to support from a standard non-explainable AI26.

In terms of data, several dermoscopic image datasets have been released to support automated skin lesion classification. The PH dataset27 provides 200 annotated images of melanocytic lesions with expert segmentations, clinical diagnoses, and dermoscopic criteria. HAM1000028 supplies 10,015 multi-source dermatoscopic images across seven diagnostic categories and has become the most widely used benchmark for pigmented lesion classifiers. The BCN20000 dataset29 extends the diversity of available data with approximately 19,000 dermoscopic images from Hospital Clínic Barcelona, covering challenging lesions such as those in nail and mucosal locations, and was a primary contributor to the ISIC 2019 challenge training set30. The ISIC 2020 challenge introduced a patient-centric collection of over 33,000 images31 that explicitly encodes multiple lesions per patient to support the ugly-duckling diagnostic heuristic. More recently, DERM1234532 released 12,345 images spanning 40 fine-grained subclasses, providing a detailed taxonomic coverage currently available in the open literature. Among all these resources, the 7-Point Checklist dataset, Derm7pt33, occupies a unique position. Its 1,011 paired clinical and dermoscopic images are annotated not only with diagnosis labels but also with the seven dermoscopic criteria of the 7-point melanoma checklist. It allows for more interpretable model predictions through the inclusion of intermediate semantic attributes34. This makes Derm7pt the natural choice for developing and evaluating concept-bottleneck classifiers in dermoscopy, as highlighted in recent concept-based work26. However, this dataset is far from perfect.

Annotation noise and inter-observer variability are recognized sources of fragility in concept-based models. Collins et al.35 studied human uncertainty in the context of concept-based models and showed that prior work in this space typically assumes that annotators are always certain and correct, an assumption that fails in real medical settings. Their results indicate that training with uncertain concept labels helps mitigate the weaknesses of concept-based systems when handling ambiguous interventions. Park et al.36 provided the first systematic study of noisy concept annotations in CBMs and showed that even moderate corruption simultaneously degrades prediction performance, interpretability, and intervention effectiveness, with the damage concentrated in a small subset of susceptible concepts. These findings establish that concept-level noise is not merely an imprecision but a structural threat to the core guarantees of the bottleneck. On the clinical side, inter-observer agreement on dermoscopic features is well documented as limited. Rodriguez-Lomba et al.37 reported that most dermoscopic features result in only fair to moderate Fleiss kappa values among five experienced dermoscopists, and Polesie et al.38 found that agreement for melanoma features ranges from slight to substantial across seven independent dermatologists. Our study differs from these lines of work in a specific and complementary way. Rather than treating annotation noise as a stochastic or distributional problem, we characterize concept inconsistency using a mathematical formalism for modeling uncertainty.

In this paper, we propose a formal analysis of concept-level inconsistency in the Derm7pt dataset and its consequences for hard CBMs trained on dermoscopic data. Using rough set theory39,40, we characterize the full extent of the inconsistency and derive a provable theoretical accuracy ceiling of 92.1% for any classifier depending exclusively on hard concept representations. The bound is tight and holds for any pure CBM variant regardless of backbone architecture. A particularly concerning finding is that 136 of the 252 melanoma images (54.0%) carry concept signatures that belong to the boundary region, far exceeding the overall boundary fraction of 30.3%. The ramification of this fact is that the seven checklist concepts are less discriminative for melanoma than for non-melanoma lesions. The concepts most responsible for this boundary ambiguity are irregular dots and globules and irregular streaks, which appear in 79% and 39% of images whose concept signatures belong to inconsistent profiles compared to 29% and 19% of images with consistent concept signatures. To correct the inconsistency, we propose and discuss two filtering strategies that both yield a filtered benchmark, which we refer to as Derm7pt+. The symmetric strategy removes all images with inconsistent concept signatures to produce a fully consistent subset of 705 images with no hard accuracy ceiling. The asymmetric strategy retains all melanoma images whose concept signatures belong to inconsistent profiles while removing only the conflicting non-melanoma counterparts, yielding 841 images and preserving sensitivity at the cost of partial residual inconsistency. We then evaluate a hard CBM across 19 backbone architectures from the EfficientNet, DenseNet, ResNet, and Wide ResNet families on both Derm7pt+ variants to inspect their predictive difficulty. Under symmetric filtering, DenseNet-169 achieves the best test macro F1 of 0.80± 0.01, and EfficientNet-B4 leads under asymmetric filtering with a test macro F1 of 0.82± 0.01. Finally, we apply the Sparseness-Optimized Feature Importance (SOFI)41–43 explainer to the true positive melanoma predictions of the best-performing model, producing a ranked analysis of which concept states most drive the melanoma diagnosis. The SOFI analysis reveals that the concept states driving melanoma predictions are the same ones most responsible for boundary ambiguity in the dataset.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section Dataset analysis presents the theoretical and statistical analysis of concept inconsistency in Derm7pt using rough set theory, including the derivation of the accuracy ceiling theorem and the characterization of the boundary region. Section Addressing inconsistency describes and evaluates two filtering strategies to remove inconsistencies, resulting in the construction of the filtered Derm7pt+ benchmark1. Section Hard concept bottleneck model details the hard CBM architecture used for numerical validation, the data preparation pipeline, and the empirical evaluation across 19 CNN-based backbone architectures. Section Concept-level explanations presents a concept-level explanation analysis of the best-performing model using the SOFI explainer. Section Concluding remarks concludes with a summary of findings, limitations, and directions for future work.

Dataset analysis

This section conducts a rough set analysis of the Derm7pt dataset to characterize concept-level inconsistency and its implications for hard CBM architectures. First, we derive a provable accuracy ceiling for any classifier operating exclusively on hard concepts and the whole dataset. Second, we quantify the extent of inconsistency across profiles and images, and identify the dermoscopic criteria most responsible for boundary ambiguity.

Theoretical analysis

Hard CBMs determine the class labels from a set of concepts that describe the input images. In the context of the Derm7pt dataset, these concepts are the seven dermoscopic criteria, each taking a discrete value. Two images that share the same concept signature are deemed indistinguishable from the classifier’s perspective since they represent the same input. Rough set theory39 formalizes this notion through equivalence classes, each defined as the set of all images that share an identical concept pattern. Within an equivalence class, a classifier that operates on concepts alone must assign every image the same diagnosis. We say that two images are inconsistent when they belong to the same equivalence class but carry different diagnosis labels. In other words, the same concept signature maps to both melanoma and non-melanoma in the data. The set of all images involved in at least one such conflict is called the boundary region. For images in the boundary region, a concept-based classifier receives identical inputs, yet the ground-truth assigns them different diagnosis labels. It must therefore assign both the same prediction, so some of them will be misclassified 44, regardless of the training procedure or model capacity.

Remark

The theoretical accuracy ceiling is a tight upper bound that applies to any hard CBM determining class labels exclusively from the seven dermoscopic concepts. This bound is computed globally, treating the entire dataset as a single pool before any partitioning is performed. The local ceiling will vary across training, validation, and test splits depending on how images with inconsistent concept signatures are distributed among them. A split concentrating such images in the training set imposes a tighter local ceiling on training accuracy while leaving the test ceiling relatively relaxed.

Dataset inconsistency undermines the semantic validity of concept attributions. The CBM requires that the composition be well-defined, where is the predicted concept vector (the values predicted by the model for each attribute in C) and h maps concept vectors to a diagnosis. Inconsistency violates this requirement since there exist pairs that carry the same concept signature, i.e., , yet receive conflicting labels , making h undefined on that concept signature at the concept level.

When the same concept vector is paired with conflicting labels, the label classifier faces an irresolvable contradiction and cannot learn a consistent concept-to-diagnosis mapping. Predictions for those concept signatures become arbitrary, which degrades both accuracy and the reliability of the explanations the CBM is designed to provide. Soft CBMs face the same contradiction, but the continuous nature of their concept scores creates an unintended escape route. The backbone can adjust those scores to encode discriminative information about the image that has no correspondence to the defined dermoscopic criteria. The label classifier then exploits that encoded information to resolve conflicts that would otherwise be irresolvable. The concept scores are a mixture of dermoscopic concepts and latent image features that were never part of the clinical vocabulary, so it becomes impossible to determine which component drives any given prediction. Hard CBMs eliminate this ambiguity by forcing concept activations to pass through a hard threshold before reaching the classifier.

Addressing inconsistency

We acknowledge that inconsistency in concept annotations is an inherent reality of real-world medical datasets. It often arises from inter-observer variability, ambiguous lesion morphology, and the inherent subjectivity of dermoscopic interpretation. However, images with inconsistent concept signatures should not be used as training data for CBMs, since exposing the model to contradictory supervision undermines the core guarantee that the concept bottleneck functions as a well-defined mapping. The appropriate choice is to train on concept-consistent data and allow the model to generalize to real-world cases at inference time. Several strategies exist to address concept inconsistency at the dataset level. Reannotation by domain experts is the most solid option, but it is costly, requires specialized dermoscopic knowledge, and risks introducing new disagreements. Expanding the concept set could break existing inconsistencies by making previously indistinguishable concept signatures separable, but it requires the new annotations to be collected exhaustively across the entire dataset. A third direction is to remove the images with inconsistent concept signatures from the dataset. In this section, we explore two strategies that follow this direction, both yielding a filtered benchmark that we refer to as Derm7pt+.

Filtering strategies

The existence of means that any CBM trained on the raw dataset will be exposed to contradictory supervision where the same concept prediction vector is paired with both d=1 and d=0 labels. In this subsection, we will discuss two filtering strategies grounded in the framework of presumably correct decision sets44 to remove images with inconsistent concept signatures from the dataset. Note that we do not seek to remove difficult instances from the dataset, but those that are impossible to classify by a CBM that determines the class labels solely from the concept heads.

Asymmetric filtering gives |U''| = 841 (-16.8%). All 252 melanoma images are retained, and the class ratio improves slightly to 1:2.3. Since the non-melanoma counterparts of every boundary profile are removed, each formerly inconsistent profile in U'' contains only melanoma images and is therefore consistent by construction. Of the 50 formerly inconsistent profiles, 23 had non-melanoma as the original majority class, meaning the retained melanoma images represent the minority signal of those profiles. A classifier trained on U'' will therefore learn to predict melanoma for concept signatures whose empirical prior in the raw data actually favors non-melanoma. For rigorous interpretability benchmarking using hard CBM implementations, symmetric removal (U') is preferred since it yields a dataset with genuine concept consistency, where every profile carries a single diagnosis label regardless of filtering. For clinical screening applications, asymmetric removal (U'') is preferred since it retains all melanoma training signal and preserves sensitivity, though at the cost of inverted supervision for 23 profiles whose non-melanoma prior is suppressed. Neither strategy is universally optimal since both come at the cost of reducing the available training pool, so the filtering choice should be documented explicitly in any publication using Derm7pt+ for CBM evaluation.

The filtered datasets are more consistent in the rough set sense, which is the property hard CBMs require to function as well-defined mappings. This does not imply that the filtered datasets are clinically superior or that the removed images are less diagnostically relevant than those retained. No evidence in this study suggests that images in are easier or harder to classify based on their visual features. Nor is there evidence that they are more or less clinically meaningful than images in . The causes of the observed inconsistency cannot be determined from the data alone and fall outside the scope of this paper. For example, Fig. 4 shows two visually distinct images that share an identical concept signature yet carry different diagnoses. This case is more suggestive of problematic annotations than of genuine visual ambiguity, but it cannot be generalized to the full boundary region. Some inconsistent profiles may involve lesions that are genuinely indistinguishable at the concept level and produce identical concept signatures for different diagnoses.

Beyond filtering the concept annotations to remove inconsistent patterns, we manually cropped all images to remove border artifacts and better center the lesion within the frame. All images were saved at 384 × 384 pixels. In principle, a CNN backbone trained with enough samples and epochs would learn to distinguish diagnostically relevant regions from noise at the image borders. However, both filtering strategies substantially reduce the number of available training samples, implying that longer training schedules risk overfitting rather than improving generalization. This motivated our choice of a conservative training setup when building CBM baselines, as described in the next section.

Hard concept bottleneck model

We now build and evaluate a hard CBM to assess how well concept-based classifiers perform on Derm7pt+ under both filtering strategies. For completeness, we also evaluate the same pipeline on the unfiltered Derm7pt dataset alongside the filtered variants. As the analysis in Section Dataset analysis makes clear, training a CBM on a dataset containing images with inconsistent concept signatures exposes the model to contradictory signals that undermine the core guarantee of the bottleneck; the unfiltered results should therefore be interpreted as a reference point rather than a competitive baseline.

Data preparation

Experiments are conducted on three dataset variants: the unfiltered Derm7pt, the asymmetric Derm7pt+, and the symmetric Derm7pt+. The unfiltered variant uses the raw 1,011 images without any removal and serves as a reference point that exposes the model to contradictory concept supervision. The asymmetric variant applies the filtering defined in Eq. (10), retaining all 252 melanoma images and removing only the 170 non-melanoma images whose concept signatures belong to inconsistent profiles, yielding |U''| = 841 images with . The symmetric variant applies the filtering defined in Equation (9), restricting the dataset to by removing all images whose concept signatures belong to a profile in regardless of label, yielding |U'| = 705 images with and genuine concept consistency.

The Derm7pt benchmark provides predefined training, validation, and test splits that are disjoint at the case level by construction33, where each case corresponds to a unique lesion. For each filtered variant, the removal operation is applied within each split independently, using the boundary profiles identified from the full dataset. It is also worth noting that no case is redistributed across splits, and no resplitting is performed. Case-level disjointness is therefore fully inherited from the original benchmark design and is not affected by either filtering strategy.

Training images undergo a stochastic augmentation pipeline comprising random resized cropping (scale ∈ [0.75, 1.0], output size 224 × 224), horizontal and vertical flips, random rotation up to , random affine transforms (translation up to 10%, shear up to , applied with probability 0.5), color jitter (brightness 0.4, contrast 0.4, saturation 0.3, hue 0.1), random grayscale conversion (probability 0.05), and a randomly selected sharpening, Gaussian blur, or identity transform. Validation and test images are resized to 224 × 224 and normalized only, using ImageNet statistics μ = (0.485, 0.456, 0.406) and σ = (0.229, 0.224, 0.225). The backbone , concept heads , and label classifier are trained jointly in a single stage, avoiding the sequential two-stage procedure of the original CBM formulation. However, joint training introduces the risk of label leakage where gradient signals from the label loss could propagate back through and distort the concept representations. To prevent this, the gradient flow through the hard bottleneck is severed by treating as a stop-gradient tensor during the backward pass, , so that the label classifier loss does not reach the backbone or the concept heads. As a result, and are updated exclusively by the concept supervision signal, preserving the semantic alignment of the bottleneck without sacrificing training efficiency. Empirical results

We evaluate 19 backbone architectures spanning four families. The EfficientNet family covers B0 through B7 (eight models)47. DenseNet-121, 161, 169, and 201 (four models)48, ResNet-18, 34, 50, 101, and 152 (five models)49, and Wide ResNet-50-2 and 101-2 (two models)50 complete the set. All backbones are initialized with ImageNet pretrained weights. The fraction of tunable backbone layers, the batch size, and the learning rate were selected by grid search on the validation set over the ranges , , and , respectively. The remaining hyperparameters were fixed after preliminary experimentation. Weight decay was set to for the backbone, with dropout rate 0.3 and hidden dimension 128 for the label classifier. Training used cosine annealing with and loss balancing coefficients . All models are trained for up to 20 epochs, as longer training produces overfitting even when early stopping is applied. The best checkpoint is defined as the one that achieves the highest macro-averaged F1 on the validation label set, and it is used for test set evaluation. Each architecture is trained with five independent random seeds, and all reported metrics represent the mean and standard deviation computed over these runs. Additionally, we implement SoftCBM as a baseline with a richer concept representation, where the argmax in the concept bottleneck is replaced with a softmax function. Both HardCBM and SoftCBM variants are evaluated under all three filtering strategies.

Label performance is measured by macro-averaged F1 as the primary criterion, since the test sets remain class-imbalanced after filtering, and accuracy can be misleading under imbalance. Weighted accuracy, macro sensitivity, melanoma-specific sensitivity, macro specificity, and AUROC are reported as secondary metrics. For concept prediction, we report the macro accuracy and the per-concept accuracy scores, without assigning differential weight to any single one. The most effective backbone for label prediction is the one that simultaneously achieves the highest test macro F1 and the smallest standard deviation across seeds. When two backbones reach the same rounded mean F1, the one with the lower standard deviation is preferred, as it indicates more consistent generalization.

Per-concept accuracy provides a descriptive measure of the backbone’s difficulty in recognizing each dermoscopic concept. However, the discriminatory power of a concept for diagnosis is not tightly coupled to its prediction accuracy. A backbone might often fail to recognize a given concept, yet the classifier might not rely on that concept when determining the diagnosis label. The numerical results support this rationale, since macro F1 label scores exceed macro-averaged concept accuracy across all backbones and dataset variants. This gap suggests that detailed extraction of individual dermoscopic concepts from images is not a prerequisite for solid diagnostic accuracy.

Concept-level explanations

Next, we resort to the SOFI explainer41–43 to characterize which dermoscopic concepts drive the predictions of EfficientNet-B5, the best-performing backbone on the symmetric Derm7pt+. We focus exclusively on the symmetric dataset because its perfect quality of classification γ (C,d) = 1.0 guarantees that every concept profile maps unambiguously to a single diagnosis label. SOFI is an optimization-based approach suitable for concept intervention analysis that seeks a concept ordering , a permutation of C, that maximizes explanation sparsity while remaining faithful to . Concluding remarks

We presented Derm7pt+, a concept-consistent dermoscopy benchmark derived from Derm7pt through rough set analysis and symmetric filtering of all images with inconsistent concept signatures. The dataset guarantees perfect quality of classification, meaning every concept profile maps unambiguously to a single diagnosis label, removing the hard accuracy ceiling that afflicts any classifier operating exclusively on hard concepts. Alongside the dataset, we presented a hard CBM architecture that trains the backbone, concept heads, and label classifier jointly while severing gradient flow through the hard bottleneck via a stop-gradient operation, which prevents label leakage into the concept representations.

The inconsistency analysis revealed that irregular dots and globules and irregular streaks are most responsible for boundary ambiguity, yet both are also among the strongest melanoma indicators. This duality explains why inconsistency concentrates in the concept subspace with the highest melanoma prevalence. The most ambiguous profiles combine these attributes with a blue-whitish veil or an atypical pigment network, producing concept signatures with no discriminative power. On the unfiltered Derm7pt, the best-performing backbone in test macro F1 shows M-Sensitivity substantially lower than all other metrics, a direct consequence of contradictory concept supervision. This gap narrows progressively as filtering becomes more aggressive, and the best model under symmetric filtering produces the strongest profile across all six label metrics, with M-Sensitivity reaching its highest upper bound across all three datasets. At the attribute level, blue-whitish veil is the most accurately predicted concept across all backbones, while pigmentation and dots and globules are the hardest to predict, a difficulty that traces directly to their overrepresentation in inconsistent profiles. Macro-averaged concept accuracy remains stable regardless of backbone or filtering strategy, yet label macro F1 varies more widely across the same architectures. This dissociation shows that the label classifier draws diagnostic signal from the joint pattern of concept assignments rather than from individual attribute correctness, and confirms that annotation noise is the binding constraint on bottleneck quality. In terms of concept-based explanations derived from the best-performing model trained with the symmetric Derm7pt dataset, the SOFI analysis reveals that the concept states driving melanoma predictions are the same ones most responsible for boundary ambiguity.

It should be stressed that none of the three datasets explored in this paper is unconditionally preferable. The unfiltered Derm7pt is not a sound training resource for hard CBMs where the focus is interpretability. Concept-level inconsistency makes the bottleneck mapping ill-defined and imposes a hard ceiling on what any concept-only classifier can achieve. Asymmetric Derm7pt+ embeds the clinical prior that false negatives carry a higher cost than false positives, an assumption that may not hold in all deployment contexts. Symmetric Derm7pt+ is the most suitable dataset for building hard CBMs, though at the cost of removing 306 images and 54.0% of melanoma cases. Future work will study the clinical validity of learned concept attributions and their utility for actionable explanations in screening settings.
