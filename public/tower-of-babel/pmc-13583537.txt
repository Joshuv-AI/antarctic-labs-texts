Oxygen Consumption Rate‐Defined Phases Couple Metabolism to Matrix Dynamics in Chondrocyte‐Mesenchymal Stromal Cell Co‐Culture
Authors: Zhiyao Ma, Liam McEachern, Xiaoyi Lan, David Xinzheyang Li, Aahil A Ansari, Ivan Au, Madeline Barker, Melanie Kunze, Aillette Mulet‐Sierra, Adetola B Adesida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChondrocytes and mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) metabolism shapes cartilage matrix quality, but cartilage engineering lacks a non‐destructive, time‐resolved readout that links oxygen uses to matrix assembly and mechanics under standard culture conditions. We continuously recorded oxygen‐consumption rate (OCR) in nasal chondrocyte (NC), MSC, and NC–MSC co‐culture pellets cultured for 27 days under normoxia, and integrated OCR trajectories with time‐resolved gene expression, matrix histology, glycosaminoglycan (GAG)/DNA, and unconfined compression mechanics across defined NC:MSC ratios (monocultures; 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, 1:3). OCR trajectories were reproducibly tri‐phasic—(I) condensation/priming (Days 0–9), (II) differentiation/matrix synthesis (Days 9–21/24), and (III) maturation/remodeling (Days 24–27)—and strongly composition dependent. NC‐rich mixtures exhibited an earlier hypoxic tone with transient HIF‐1α, accelerated SOX9 followed by ACAN and COL2A1 induction, and marked GAG synergy peaking at 3:1. MSC‐rich mixtures sustained late respiration with higher PGC‐1α, elevated COL10A1 and MMP13, and achieved the highest equilibrium modulus at 1:3 despite lower GAG/DNA. An OCR downshift near Day 24 marked metabolic settling in most groups, whereas 1:3 pellets maintained or increased respiration, consistent with continued oxidative remodeling. Correlation analyses linked OCR features to hyaline anabolism in NC‐rich pellets and to remodeling/hypertrophy in MSC‐rich pellets, indicating ratio‐specific coordination between respiratory and matrix‐associated signatures. These findings support OCR monitoring as a sensitive, non‐destructive process metric associated with chondrogenic stage transitions, provide guidance for selecting NC‐rich ratios to maximize hyaline fidelity and GAG productivity, and MSC‐rich ratios to increase stiffness while managing hypertrophic risk, and offer a generalizable framework for in‐process bioenergetic control across tissue‐engineering contexts.Keywords: bioenergetics, HIF‐1α, metabolic profile, oxygen consumption rate, PGC‐1α

Introduction

Articular cartilage, a specialized connective tissue covering the ends of bones in synovial joints, exists in a low‐oxygen microenvironment [1]. Its structural and functional attributes are tuned for sustained mechanical loading, yet its intrinsic repair capacity is minimal because it is avascular, aneural, lacks lymphatic drainage, and is sparsely cellular [2]; if injury is left untreated, this often culminates in early osteoarthritis (OA).

Cell‐based therapeutic options aim to stimulate cells to produce the biomechanically functional extracellular matrix (ECM) of cartilage. Bone marrow–derived mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) are an attractive cell source for cartilage repair [3], but their low abundance necessitates extensive monolayer expansion, which compromises chondrogenic potential [4, 5], and they are at risk of forming pre‐hypertrophic chondrocytes with a propensity to calcify in vivo [6, 7, 8]. Co‐culture of chondrocytes with MSC [9, 10, 11] mitigates these liabilities by combining chondrocyte lineage stability with MSC trophic support, improving matrix deposition and restraining hypertrophy through indirect [12, 13, 14, 15, 16] and direct [9, 17] synergistic mechanisms. Using nasal chondrocytes (NCs) as an ectopic chondrocyte source circumvents many limitations of articular chondrocytes: NCs exhibit higher and more reproducible chondrogenic capacity [18, 19], independent of donor age [20], and generate cartilage with mechanical characteristics similar to articular cartilage [21, 22].

Across cartilage development, homeostasis, and degeneration, oxygen availability and cellular metabolism are central regulators. Chondrocytes rely predominantly on glycolysis yet retain mitochondrial capacity, and the glycolysis–oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) balance shifts with developmental stage and culture context [23, 24]. Hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α (HIF‐1α) preserves the chondrogenic phenotype by coordinating glucose transport, glycolytic flux, and survival in low oxygen [25], whereas dysregulated signaling or mitochondrial dysfunction can accelerate matrix loss [26]. MSCs also undergo metabolic reprogramming during lineage commitment: Proliferating MSCs engage in mixed OXPHOS and glycolytic metabolism, and chondrogenic induction is accompanied by a shift in bioenergetics whose direction and timing depend on cues and culture conditions. Previous work emphasizes that tuning the OXPHOS–glycolysis balance can modulate MSC chondrogenic potential and hypertrophic risk, underscoring the need for time‐resolved metabolic readouts during differentiation [27, 28].

Despite extensive work on culture conditions, most studies treat oxygen as a static parameter and sample metabolism sparsely. This obscures cellular stage transitions and limits the ability to link bioenergetic state to tissue‐level outcomes. The challenge is compounded in direct NC–MSC co‐culture, where paracrine and contact‐mediated interactions are ratio‐dependent and mechanistically diverse, and likely reprogram metabolic demands and fate trajectories [29]. A process‐level, non‐destructive readout is therefore needed to track metabolic dynamics and translate them into actionable design rules for cartilage engineering.

Oxygen‐consumption rate (OCR) provides a non‐destructive readout of oxygen use [30]. We reasoned that OCR trajectories encode the evolving energetic demands of chondrogenesis and remodeling. In this view, the NC:MSC ratio should produce distinct, phase‐structured OCR patterns that correspond to specific molecular and tissue outcomes. If these trajectories can be quantified and interpreted prospectively, OCR could function as a process metric for engineered cartilage, enabling early prediction and control of composition and mechanics. We therefore hypothesize that varying the NC:MSC ratio produces distinct OCR trends that correspond to unique metabolic signatures and, in turn, to specific tissue‐level outcomes. To test this hypothesis, we integrated 27‐day, 15‐min resolution OCR monitoring with time‐resolved gene profiling, histology/immunofluorescence, day 27 GAG/DNA quantification with synergy analysis, and mechanical testing across defined NC:MSC ratios to elucidate how oxygen‐consumption dynamics relate to chondrogenic capacity within engineered pellets.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

All procedures followed relevant guidelines and regulations and were approved by the University of Alberta Health Research Ethics Board—Biomedical Panel (Study ID: Pro00018778).

Isolation and Expansion of NC and MSC

Human nasal chondrocytes (NC) were isolated from nasal septal cartilage obtained from three female donors (ages 24, 25, and 26) and cryopreserved at the end of passage 1 (P1). Mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC) were isolated from the bone marrow of one female donor (age 41, Figure S1) and cryopreserved at P1. Nasal chondrocytes (NC). Septal cartilage was digested for 22 h in 0.15% (w/v) collagenase Type II (Worthington, #LS004176). Released cells were plated in T‐75 tissue‐culture flasks (Sarstedt, Germany) for 48 h to recover, then detached and reseeded at 1 × 104 cells/cm2. Cells were expanded for 1 week under normoxia (21% O2, 5% CO2, ~95% humidity) in high‐glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat‐inactivated FBS, 10 mM HEPES, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 2 mM L‐glutamine (PSG; Life Technologies, ON, Canada), 5 ng/mL FGF‐2 (Neuromics, USA; cat. PR80001), and 1 ng/mL TGF‐β1 (ProSpec, cat. CYT‐716) to reach P1. NC were cryopreserved at P1 and, for experiments, thawed and expanded to P2 under the same conditions.

Bone marrow mononuclear cells (MNC) were isolated using Histopaque‐1077 (Sigma) per manufacturer instructions. MNC were plated in T‐75 flasks to enable adherent MSC population within the MNC to develop and proliferate for 1 week to P1 under hypoxia (3% O2, 5% CO2, ~95% humidity) in α‐MEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Sigma‐Aldrich, MO), PSG, and 5 ng/mL FGF‐2. For experiments, MSC were thawed and expanded to P2 under the same conditions.

Formation and Culture of Cell Pellet

At P2, the single MSC donor (F41) was paired separately with each NC donor to create three biologically independent donor pairs:

Pair 1: NC(F24) + MSC(F41), Pair 2: NC(F26) + MSC(F41), Pair 3: NC(F25) + MSC(F41).

Within each donor pair, pellets were prepared and analyzed across seven groups: NC monoculture, MSC monoculture, and NC:MSC co‐cultures at 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3. Each pellet contained 0.5 × 106 total cells. Pellets were transferred to 96‐well, round‐bottom plates (Sarstedt, Germany) and cultured individually for 27 days in a defined serum‐free chondrogenic medium supplemented with 10 ng/mL TGF‐β3 under normoxia. Medium was changed every 3 days.

Measurement of Oxygen Consumption Rate (OCR)

For each donor pair and group, 2–3 pellets were randomly selected and placed on a single 96‐well round‐bottom plate fitted with a 32‐sensor Resipher oxygen‐sensing lid (Lucid Scientific, USA). Pellets were assigned to wells available on the Resipher lid without positional randomization. OCR was recorded every 15 min throughout the 27‐day culture. Data were analyzed using the Resipher web application (Lucid Scientific), and values are reported as fmol/mm2/s (as exported by the platform) Figure S3.

RT‐qPCR

For each donor pair, 2 pellets per group were collected on days 6, 9, 12, 15, 21, and 27 to assess expression of ACAN, COL1A2, COL2A1, COL10A1, SOX9, LOX, PPARγ, HIF‐1α, HIF‐2α, VEGFA, RUNX2, ADAMTS5, MMP13, and PGC‐1α. Total RNA was extracted and purified using the PuroSPIN Total RNA Purification Kit (Luna Nanotech, Canada) according to the manufacturer's instructions, reverse‐transcribed to cDNA, and quantified by RT‐qPCR. Expression was normalized to RPL13A, β2M, and YWHAZ and reported using the 2−ΔCT method.

GAG and DNA Biochemical Assay

Biochemical assays measured the total glycosaminoglycan (GAG) and DNA levels after a 27‐day culture period. For each donor pair, 2 pellets per group were digested overnight with proteinase K (Sigma‐Aldrich, United States, #P2308) at 56°C. The GAG levels were quantified using a 1,9‐dimethyl methylene blue assay (DMMB, Sigma‐Aldrich, United States, #341088), using chondroitin sulfate (Sigma‐Aldrich, United States, #C8529) as the standard. DNA levels were determined using a CyQuant cell proliferation assay kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, United States, #C7026), employing various dilutions of bacteriophage λ DNA as the standard. Histology and Immunofluorescence

On each gene‐expression collection day, two additional pellets from Pair 1 were processed for histology. Pellets were fixed in 10% (v/v) neutral‐buffered formalin (Anachemia) overnight at 4°C, washed in PBS, dehydrated, and paraffin‐embedded. Blocks were sectioned at 5 μm on a microtome (Leica Biosystems). To visualize sulfated GAG deposition, sections were stained with Safranin‐O (Sigma‐Aldrich, #S2255‐25G), Fast Green FCF for collagen (Sigma‐Aldrich, #F7258‐25G), and Hematoxylin (Sigma‐Aldrich, #MHS32‐1 L) for cell nuclei visualization. For immunofluorescence, sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies to Type I collagen (rabbit, Cedarlane #CL50111AP‐1, 1:200), Type II collagen (mouse, DSHB #II‐II6B3, 1:200), and Type X collagen (rabbit, Abcam #ab58632, 1:100), followed by goat anti‐rabbit (Abcam #ab150080, 1:200) and goat anti‐mouse (Abcam #ab150117, 1:200) secondary antibodies and DAPI (Cedarlane). Images were captured on a Nikon Eclipse Ti‐S microscope (10× objective).

The obtained histological images were quantified through a systematic process using a self‐developed algorithm. Quantitative measurements included background subtraction to minimize nonspecific staining artifacts, calculation of the total pixel intensity for marker expression, and counting of nuclei within the section area. The quantification result was expressed as the average pixel intensity per cell for collagen and total intensity for GAG. For each ratio group at each time point, three sections taken from the same pellet were independently quantified for each stain. The three section‐level values were then averaged to yield a single per‐pellet value reported for that group and time point. Bern scores were calculated as described by Grogan et al. [31]. Three independent evaluators, unaware of group and time point, scored all pellets, and the mean Bern score per pellet was used for analysis.

Mechanical Testing

At the end of the 27‐day culture period, 3 pellets per group per donor pair were selected for mechanical testing. The MicroTester G2 from CellScale Biomaterials Testing (Ontario, Canada) was used to perform a stepwise stress‐relaxation test to evaluate the mechanical properties of the pellets. Force was recorded over time, and stress was calculated by normalizing force to the cross‐sectional area of the construct. The peak modulus and equilibrium modulus were defined as the slopes of the peak and equilibrium strain–stress curves, respectively. These slopes were determined through linear regression analysis of the best‐fit regions of the curves.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. When data met assumptions of normality, repeated‐measures one‐way ANOVA with Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test with individual variances computed for each comparison. When normality was not satisfied, a non‐parametric one‐way analysis with Dunn's multiple comparisons test was used. Correlations were evaluated using Pearson's correlation and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Ratio‐Controlled Oxygen Consumption Rate With an End‐Stage Shift

We monitored oxygen consumption rate (OCR) for nasal chondrocytes (NC), mesenchymal stromal cells (MSC), and co‐cultures at NC to MSC ratios of 1:3, 1:2, 1:1, 2:1, and 3:1 across 27 days (Figure 1). With medium exchanges every 72 h, all groups showed a consistent pattern: OCR dropped immediately, recovered rapidly within 8–12 h, and then gradually increased until the next exchange (Figure 1a).

Trends in oxygen consumption rate (OCR) throughout pellet culture. (a) Average dynamic OCR trajectories for NC monoculture, MSC monoculture, and NC:MSC co‐cultures (3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, 1:3) over 27 days of static culture for three donor pairs. Medium was exchanged every 72 h. OCR was recorded every 15 min with a Resipher oxygen‐sensing lid and reported as fmol/mm2/s. (b) Zoomed view, Days 0–6. (c) Zoomed view, Days 21–27. (d) Average OCR for each group within every 3‐day medium‐change window. n = 2–3.

During Days 0–3 all groups showed an early increase in OCR as pellets formed (Figure 1b). From Days 3 to 9 the three‐day mean within each medium change cycle either held steady or dipped slightly relative to Days 0–3 for several groups, after which it increased again. From Days 3 to 24 the three‐day means generally rose across cycles. Co‐culture groups reached their highest three‐day mean OCR in the window from Days 21 to 24. A clear composition effect was evident for co‐culture groups. Within most windows, a higher NC fraction was associated with higher OCR, with significant differences among groups from Days 3 to 6 and from Days 21 to 24 (Figure 1d).

At approximately Day 24, OCR behavior diverged: most groups declined after the last medium change—NC‐rich pellets already falling beforehand—whereas 1:3 NC:MSC kept increasing and finished with the highest OCR (170.4 fmol/mm2/s), followed by the 3:1 group (129.9 fmol/mm2/s) (Figure 1c,d). Across the experiment, NC monocultures maintained higher OCR than MSC monocultures, which were the lowest throughout. Both monocultures peaked between Days 18 and 21 and then tapered toward the end. Due to large donor variability, only the difference between NC monoculture and 1:3 NC:MSC co‐culture in the Days 21–24 window and between 1:2 and 1:1 NC:MSC in the Days 3–6 window were statistically significant. These respiratory dynamics frame the subsequent tissue level changes.

NC Fraction Drives Cartilage‐Like Matrix Assembly and Spatial Maturation

We next evaluated cartilage‐like matrix formation and the spatiotemporal distribution of collagens I, II, and X as well as glycosaminoglycans, GAG, by immunofluorescence and Safranin O staining at Days 6, 9, 12, 15, 21, and 27 (Figure 2). Across conditions, pellet cross‐sections generally enlarged with time, with the NC monoculture showing the most pronounced growth by Day 27; MSC monoculture pellets remained comparatively small, and co‐cultures were intermediate.

Representative histology/immunofluorescence of pellet sections from donor pair I stained for (a) collagen I, (b) collagen II, (c) Safranin‐O (sulfated GAG), and (d) collagen X across all groups at Days 6, 9, 12, 15, 21, and 27. Scale bar, 0.5 mm.

Collagen I was detectable by Day 6 in all groups and remained modest through Day 15, followed by a clear rise from Days 21 to 27. Signal localization was strongest at the periphery early in culture and progressively filled the interior with time (Figure 2a). Quantification confirmed this trajectory. Per cell collagen I intensity increased sharply after Day 15 and reached the highest values at Day 27 (Figure 3a).

Quantitative assessment of cartilage phenotype development and endpoint readouts. (a) Time‐course quantification from histological sections of collagens I, II, X (average pixel intensity per cell) and GAG (Safranin‐O total intensity) at Days 6, 9, 12, 15, 21, and 27; three sections per pellet were averaged to yield a per‐pellet value. (b) Day‐27 biochemical assays of GAG and DNA and the GAG synergy coefficient. (c) Day‐27 mechanical properties from stepwise unconfined compression with stress relaxation, reported as peak and equilibrium moduli.

Collagen II deposition depended on the NC fraction. NC monoculture displayed detectable signal as early as Day 6, whereas MSC monoculture showed little to no collagen II until about Day 21. Co‐cultures increased in a ratio‐dependent manner such that a higher NC fraction produced earlier and stronger collagen II signal (Figures 2b and 3a). Spatially, collagen II mirrored collagen I. It appeared first as a peripheral ring and then extended centrally as culture progressed (Figure 2b). GAG accumulation increased with time and tracked closely with collagen II, with stronger staining in NC‐rich groups and weaker staining in MSC monoculture. The most intense signal was evident on Day 27. On Day 27, the NC monoculture had the highest GAG signal overall. Among the co‐cultures, the 1:3 NC to MSC group was the strongest (Figures 2c and 3a). The Bern score showed a similar temporal trend; however, differences between groups (except for MSC monoculture) gradually decreased over time, and by Day 27, all groups had comparable scores, with MSC monoculture remaining lower (Figure S2). Collagen X signal was consistently low relative to collagens I and II and showed only a modest increase from Days 21 to 27. By Day 27, MSC monoculture exhibited the highest per cell collagen X, co‐cultures were generally intermediate, and NC monoculture was the lowest. Overall, collagen X tended to scale with increasing MSC fraction (Figures 2d, 3a). These spatial and temporal patterns motivated end‐of‐culture biochemical and mechanical analyses.

Co‐Culture Yields GAG Synergy While MSC Maximizes Mechanical Property

At Day 27 we quantified total glycosaminoglycan (GAG) and DNA, computed the GAG to DNA ratio, and calculated a synergy coefficient (SC) for GAG production (Figure 3b). NC monocultures, on average, contained over 2‐fold more GAG, DNA, and GAG/DNA than MSC monocultures. Across co‐cultures, total GAG generally increased with the NC fraction, approaching NC levels; the 3:1 group slightly exceeded NC on average (67.2 μg vs. 61.9 μg). GAG/DNA showed a similar overall upward trend with increasing NC content, though not strictly monotonic. DNA in co‐cultures fell within a relatively narrow range (~4–6 μg) without a strong composition‐dependent trend. All co‐culture groups except for 1:3 NC:MSC had higher DNA compared to NC monoculture. All co‐culture groups had SC > 1, indicating synergistic interaction for GAG deposition. SC values were modest (1–1.3) for most ratios, whereas the 3:1 group showed the highest mean SC (~1.75) and the greatest variability (Figure 3b).

Peak and equilibrium moduli exhibited the opposite composition‐dependence to GAG metrics: moduli tended to decrease as the NC fraction increased (Figure 3c). The 1:3 NC:MSC group showed the highest equilibrium modulus (147.5 kPa) and peak modulus (699.2 kPa). Ratios with greater NC content (> 1:1) yielded lower moduli, and the 2:1 group displayed a wider spread across replicates. Both monocultures had lower moduli than most co‐cultures, with NC showing a higher modulus than MSC. Only the 1:3 and 2:1 NC:MSC groups showed statistically significant differences for equilibrium modulus. These biochemical and mechanical trends led us to examine gene expression dynamics across the culture period.

NC Accelerates the Hyaline Gene Program and MSC Promotes Remodeling

Across the 27‐day culture, the NC to MSC ratio governed both the timing and magnitude of chondrogenic induction (Figure 4). SOX9 peaked early (Days 6–9) in NC monocultures and NC‐rich pellets (≥ 50% NC) and then declined. In MSC‐rich pellets and MSC monocultures, SOX9 rose later and to lower levels. For example, the 1:3 NC:MSC group peaked at Day 21 with a 1.4‐fold lower maximum than the 3:1 group, indicating weaker, delayed induction as MSC fraction increased. Downstream hyaline cartilage matrix genes ACAN and COL2A1 largely paralleled SOX9 and scaled with NC fraction (NC mono > co‐cultures > MSC mono). Both showed a biphasic pattern: an early rise from Days 9 to 12 that was most evident in NC‐rich pellets and a second increase near Day 21 that was more pronounced in MSC‐rich pellets, which is consistent with their delayed SOX9 peak. In contrast, expression of the fibrocartilage‐associated gene COL1A2 increased with MSC fraction and with time in all groups, especially after Day 15. MSC monoculture rose sharply from Days 12 to 21 and ended about 7‐fold higher than baseline. The cross‐linking enzyme LOX also scaled with NC fraction but displayed a distinct temporal profile: In NC‐rich pellets, LOX peaked at Day 6 and then declined. In the 3:1 NC:MSC group, the Day 27 value was about 3‐fold lower than the Day 6 value. In MSC‐rich pellets, the trajectory was comparatively flat with a modest rise in the 1:3 group through Day 21.

Metabolic and hypoxia/angiogenic‐associated markers reinforced these trends. PPARγ was highest early in MSC‐rich pellets and fell as chondrogenesis progressed. PGC‐1α, a marker of mitochondrial biogenesis, separated the cohorts: NC‐rich pellets showed a low‐amplitude transient peak at about Day 12 that returned to baseline, whereas MSC‐rich pellets exhibited about 3‐fold higher levels and a later peak near Day 21, coincident with their delayed SOX9 peak. By Days 21 to 27, PGC‐1α subsided toward baseline in all groups. HIF‐1α showed a uniform transient peak at Day 9 in NC‐containing pellets. The NC monoculture reached about 3‐fold the Day 6 value and the 3:1 group reached about 2‐fold the Day 6 value, with modest differences among those groups. MSC monocultures were markedly lower, dropping about 2‐fold by Day 9 and remaining at approximately half the level observed in NC‐containing pellets thereafter. HIF‐2α was low overall, decreased to Day 15, and then stabilized, with slightly higher levels as the NC fraction increased. VEGFA, which is regulated by HIF‐1α and HIF‐2α, was higher in NC‐rich pellets, peaked at Day 6, and declined across all groups to similar levels by Day 27; MSC monocultures remained lowest throughout.

Hypertrophy and remodeling favored MSC‐rich conditions. RUNX2 peaked around Day 9 across groups, with the highest values in 1:3 and 1:2 pellets, then fell toward Day 12 and was maintained with modest fluctuations. COL10A1 increased monotonically from minimal levels at Day 6 with a clear fraction dependence, such that a higher MSC fraction produced higher expression. Group separation was evident by Day 15. By Day 21 the 1:3 group exceeded the 3:1 group by about 3‐fold and was 20‐fold higher than the NC monoculture at Day 27. ADAMTS5 expression started highest at Day 6 in all groups, dropped earlier in NC‐rich pellets around Day 9 and later in MSC‐rich pellets, most notably the 1:3 group around Day 15. By Day 21, NC‐rich groups were about 3‐fold lower than MSC‐rich groups, with differences narrowing by Day 27. MMP13 showed a clear delay: NC‐rich pellets exhibited modest early peaks around Day 9 followed by decline, whereas MSC‐rich pellets peaked at Day 21; at that time 1:2 and 1:3 clustered about 2‐fold above the other groups. By Day 27, groups partially converged but retained a fraction‐dependent ordering.

Composition Shapes Multi‐Module Coupling of Matrix Programs, Hypoxia/Angiogenesis, and Oxidative Metabolism

To visualize how markers covary within each culture group and to compare patterns across groups, we computed Pearson correlation matrices (Figure 5) and the corresponding p value maps (Figure S3, Data S1). Markers were organized into six functional modules as follows: ECM anabolism including ACAN, SOX9, COL1A2, COL2A1, and LOX; regulatory bridge marker PPARγ; hypertrophic differentiation including COL10A1 and RUNX2; matrix catabolism including MMP13 and ADAMTS5; hypoxia and angiogenic response including HIF‐1α, HIF‐2α, and VEGFA; oxidative metabolism including PGC‐1α and OCR. Culture time is displayed as a design variable.

Heatmaps for correlation matrices of selected markers. For each pellet group (NC, MSC, and co‐cultures at 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2, 1:3), Pearson correlation matrices were computed across time‐course data for 16 variables in modules: ECM anabolism (ACAN, SOX9, COL1A2, COL2A1, LOX), regulatory bridge (PPARγ), hypertrophic differentiation (COL10A1, RUNX2), matrix catabolism (MMP13, ADAMTS5), hypoxia/angiogenic response (HIF1α, HIF2α, VEGFA), and oxidative metabolism/process readouts (PGC‐1α, OCR), with culture time included as a design variable. Heatmaps show Pearson r.

In MSC the ECM module showed cohesive and significant positive correlations. Each pair was significantly positively correlated except for pairs that included SOX9. COL10A1 also correlated positively with ECM markers other than SOX9 with statistical significance, whereas ADAMTS5 showed negative correlations with the ECM panel with significant negatives for LOX and COL1A2. In NC few pairwise correlations reached significance. In both monocultures oxygen consumption rate did not show significant associations with other markers.

In co‐culture groups, as the MSC fraction increases, the within ECM positive correlations strengthen and approach the MSC pattern, with COL1A2 again anchoring these effects. PPARγ behaves as a regulatory bridge whose magnitude of association increases with MSC content. In the MSC‐rich 1:3 condition, PPARγ shows strong and significant negative correlations with COL2A1, COL10A1, OCR, and time, and shows a significant positive correlation with ADAMTS5. In the NC‐rich 3:1 condition, PPARγ is weakly positive or near zero and not statistically supported. The hypoxia and angiogenic response module shows the opposite trend. In the NC‐rich 3:1 condition, it correlates positively with ECM and catabolic markers, and HIF‐2α and VEGFA decline with time with negative and significant associations. These relations progressively attenuate with higher MSC fractions, with VEGFA remaining significantly positively correlated with SOX9 and LOX in 1:3 NC:MSC.

Oxygen metabolism marker PGC‐1α displays generally weak and mostly not significant positive correlations, with isolated significant pairs such as PGC‐1α with HIF‐1α in 3:1 NC:MSC and with RUNX2 in 2:1 NC:MSC. By contrast, OCR shows the clearest cross‐module pattern in the extreme ratios. In 3:1 NC:MSC, OCR is significantly negatively correlated with SOX9, LOX, HIF‐2α, and VEGFA and is significantly positively correlated with COL2A1 and COL10A1. In 1:3 NC:MSC, OCR exhibits broad and significant associations with many markers in the same directions, while significant negative correlations with the hypoxia response are not observed. This indicates that respiratory activity adapts with the remodeling program across mixtures.

Discussion

This study identifies the nasal chondrocyte (NC): mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) ratio as a primary cell‐physiological control parameter that coordinates proliferation, gene‐program timing, matrix assembly and remodeling, cellular metabolism, and mechanical function in 3D pellets. By integrating continuous oxygen consumption rate (OCR) measurement with expression of chondrogenic, metabolic, and hypertrophic markers, histology/immunofluorescence, and end‐point biochemical and mechanical readouts, we define three OCR‐based metabolic phases that couple HIF‐1α‐associated glycolytic priming and PGC‐1α–linked respiratory signatures to specific matrix programs. NC‐rich mixtures accelerate a hyaline‐like chondrogenic program with GAG synergy, whereas MSC‐rich mixtures bias toward matrix remodeling and hypertrophic features and reach higher stiffness despite lower GAG per DNA. Thus, different NC:MSC ratios produced distinct patterns of metabolic–ECM coordination, suggesting that bioenergetic trajectories may provide indicators of cartilage matrix development and quality. NC and MSC exhibit distinct baseline bioenergetics that shape their roles in cartilage engineering: NCs are adapted to low oxygen and rely predominantly on glycolysis [23, 24], whereas differentiating MSCs have a more flexible metabolic profile, often upregulating mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative phosphorylation [27, 32, 33]. In direct co‐culture, complementary strengths are integrated via paracrine signals, cell–cell contact, and matrix‐mediated feedback [29, 34], in a ratio dependent manner that steers downstream processes such as proliferation, chondrogenic induction, matrix synthesis, and remodeling. Together OCR trajectories, molecular timing, and matrix patterns delineate three metabolic stages over 27 days: (I) condensation and priming (Days 0–9), (II) differentiation and matrix synthesis (Days 9–21/24), and (III) maturation and remodeling (Days 24–27).

Based on our results and previous computational models, we observed that immediately after mixing, pellets undergo rapid condensation with a brief oxidative burst that covers the energy demand of cell adhesion and cytoskeletal reorganization. As condensation proceeds, oxygen is consumed faster than it is replenished, establishing a pericellular oxygen gradient and a hypoxic core [35]. This transition prompts a shift toward glycolysis, aligning the engineered niche with the metabolic profile of native cartilage. HIF‐1α, a key regulator in the hypoxia‐inducible factor pathway [25], functions as the central regulator of this transition: an early, transient increase is most pronounced when NCs are present and aligns with an early VEGFA peak, consistent with hypoxic adaptation and anabolic priming rather than catabolic activation [36, 37]. In MSC‐rich cultures, PPARγ elevation early in culture marks phenotypic plasticity and lineage gating [38]; its decline with progression indicates commitment toward the chondrogenic program. In parallel, SOX9 engages earlier and at higher levels in NC‐rich pellets, initiating ACAN and COL2A1, while LOX exhibits a transient early rise consistent with the transient increase of HIF‐1α and initiation of collagen cross‐link formation. Histologically, matrix appears in a peripheral‐to‐central pattern, earlier and more robust with higher NC content. A brief RUNX2 rise around Day 9, most evident in MSC‐rich pellets, suggests an intrinsic hypertrophic bias early in commitment [39]. Increasing NC content dampens this signal and limits premature hypertrophy. Taken together, in Phase I, condensation leads to oxygen drawdown that triggers HIF‐1α–mediated glycolytic priming; in NC‐rich mixtures this manifests as early SOX9 engagement and hyaline‐like matrix initiation, whereas in MSC‐rich pellets it is predominantly expressed as early commitment with delayed and reduced SOX9, underscoring the central role of the NC:MSC ratio in setting the downstream trajectory.

The mid phase is defined by matrix biosynthesis as the primary energetic task. Collagen II fibrillogenesis and sulfated GAG assembly are highly energy demanding [40, 41], oxidative phosphorylation becomes increasingly important; at the same time, glycolysis remains engaged to sustain chondrogenic gene expression. The result is a hybrid metabolic state—rising mitochondrial respiration with persistent glycolytic support—that aligns with the OCR trajectory and observed continued HIF‐1α expression accompanying the chondrogenic phenotype, although the present data do not establish a direct role in suppressing hypertrophy. Transcriptionally, the network coordinates depending on composition. SOX9 still defines the hyaline lineage, with earlier and stronger engagement as the NC fraction increases, mirrored by ACAN and COL2A1. PGC‐1α, known to enhance oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial biogenesis, and the oxygen consumption rate [42, 43], separates the co‐culture cohorts: modest and transient in NC‐rich pellets, but higher and later in MSC‐rich groups, consistent with a larger mitochondrial contribution as MSC differentiate. PPARγ expression is reduced with chondrogenic differentiation of MSC indicative of lineage commitment. Features associated with early matrix remodeling emerge more prominently with higher MSC content, including rising MMP13 and ADAMTS5, foreshadowing architectural remodeling that will dominate the endpoint, Phase III [44]. Functionally, NC‐rich pellets appear to allocate more of the mid‐phase energy to hyaline‐like matrix accumulation, whereas MSC‐rich pellets devote a greater portion into differentiation and early remodeling. For this stage, the NC:MSC ratio tunes the balance between matrix production and early matrix remodeling, while HIF‐1α supported continuous glycolysis and PGC‐1α linked mitochondrial activity jointly power the differentiation and ECM accumulation.

As differentiation consolidates, OCR exhibits a downshift around Day 24, reaching the final stage. Most mixtures show a decline after the final medium change, and NC‐rich pellets begin falling even earlier, consistent with metabolic settling as hyaline‐like matrix stabilizes and oxidative demand subsides [45]. In contrast, MSC‐rich 1:3 maintains an elevated OCR late in culture, indicating ongoing oxidative work characteristic of active remodeling. The partial decoupling between sustained respiration and the Day 27 decrease in PGC‐1α transcript suggests a lag between mitochondrial function and single‐time‐point gene readouts, or post‐transcriptional regulation.

By the endpoint, many markers converge across ratios as MSC‐derived cells adopt a chondrocyte‐like state; however, the hypertrophic module remains a clear exception. COL10A1 separates strongly by ratio, accompanied by MMP13 and ADAMTS5 indicating matrix turnover and endochondral drift in MSC‐dominant conditions [44, 46]. The combination of increased oxidative drive and rising hypertrophy and catabolic markers indicates a hypertrophic trajectory in engineered cartilage and has been linked to calcification and vascular invasion in vivo when unmanaged [7]. Increasing NC content was associated with lower hypertrophic and catabolic marker expression, earlier SOX9 induction, and a modest HIF‐1α signal; however, the mechanisms underlying these relationships remain to be determined. Despite the hypertrophic risk when MSC dominates, including MSC confers tangible benefits. Co‐cultures generally exhibit higher DNA content than NC monoculture, and GAG synergy is present across ratios. These gains are consistent with MSC trophic support and their capacity to augment matrix productivity, provided the fraction is tuned to limit hypertrophic drift.

The endpoint mechanical phenotype underscores that stiffness does not scale merely with total GAG; instead, it depends on ECM composition and organization. GAGs contribute to load support when interfaced with a robust collagen network—particularly type II collagen—that constrains swelling [47, 48]. If GAG deposition outpaces collagen accumulation and maturation, the GAG‐to‐collagen ratio becomes abnormally high and the scaffold is under‐reinforced, limiting gains in modulus [49]. Cross‐link density and fibril architecture are equally critical; enzymatic cross‐linking must keep pace with deposition to translate composition into load‐bearing structure [50]. In our study, LOX showed an early rise but did not escalate with the same tempo as matrix accumulation. Spatial inhomogeneity, common in pellets as a dense peripheral shell and softer core due to diffusion limits, further depresses bulk stiffness despite high average GAG. Prior studies show that improving matrix uniformity by reducing construct thickness or adding nutrient channels enhances mechanical properties without large changes in overall GAG or collagen [51]. In our 27‐day pellets, these organizational factors likely explain why MSC‐rich 1:3 achieved the highest moduli despite lower GAG per DNA, whereas NC‐rich mixtures, though compositionally hyaline‐like, settled into lower OCR and modest stiffness at this endpoint.

Limitations of this study include donor variability, geometry‐dependent diffusion in pellets, and reliance on bulk measures that mask micro‐regional differences, and the absence of longitudinal normalization of pellet‐level OCR to DNA content or viable cell number because of the limited pellet number. In addition, glycolytic activity was not directly assessed; therefore, interpretations concerning glycolysis remain hypothetical. Future work should pair OCR with spatially resolved imaging and mechanics, quantify collagen cross‐link density and fibril architecture, and test hypertrophy‐modulating regimens to preserve mechanical gains while maintaining hyaline fidelity. Single‐cell analyses could resolve NC–MSC interaction modes and clarify how metabolic programs are wired into lineage control.

Conclusions

We profiled oxygen consumption rate (OCR) during chondrogenic induction of human NC–MSC co‐cultures and showed that OCR resolves three metabolic phases in which cellular metabolism, gene expression, and matrix processes reorganize. NC‐rich mixtures favor earlier chondrogenic induction and hyaline‐like matrix accumulation, whereas MSC‐rich mixtures favor late oxidative remodeling that improves stiffness but increases hypertrophic risk. Adjusting the NC:MSC ratio and oxygen conditions, and tracking OCR as a process metric, enables targeted control of engineered cartilage composition and mechanics throughout the culture timeline. From a cell‐physiology perspective, these findings link HIF‐1α‐associated glycolytic priming and PGC‐1α–linked oxidative reserve to distinct matrix programs and mechanical outcomes. From a translational standpoint, they suggest that OCR trajectories may serve as a non‐destructive in‐process biomarker of graft quality and hypertrophic risk before implantation, providing practical guidance for optimizing chondrocyte–MSC co‐culture protocols for cartilage repair.
