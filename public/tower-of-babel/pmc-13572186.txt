Grafting of Md5 gC onto herpesvirus of turkeys confers full protection against very virulent Marek's disease virus
Authors: Panpan Yang, Minghe Xu, Muran Xu, Yanfang Cong, Mengjia Xiang, Dongdong Yang, Baiyu Wang, Jianli Li, Jun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Marek's disease virus (MDV) virulence has progressively compromised the efficacy of current vaccines, including herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT). Glycoprotein C (gC) is conserved among mardiviruses and essential for horizontal transmission, but its immunogenic potential remains underexploited. We developed a “viral grafting” strategy by precisely replacing the HVT gC gene with that of very virulent MDV (vvMDV) strain Md5, generating a recombinant virus rHVT‑gC/Md5. The rHVT‑gC/Md5 replicated similarly to parental HVT in chicken embryo fibroblasts and stably expressed Md5‑gC. Specific-pathogen-free chickens immunized subcutaneously at 1 day old with 2,000 PFU of rHVT‑gC/Md5 and challenged at 6 days old with 500 PFU of vvMDV Md5 showed full protection: 0% mortality, 0% tumor incidence, and a protection index of 100, compared with 28% mortality, 36% tumor incidence, and a protection index of 35 for HVT. rHVT‑gC/Md5 markedly reduced viral loads to near‑undetectable levels in all organs, preserved body weight gain, and maintained bursal and thymic architecture (indices >0.7). Importantly, rHVT‑gC/Md5 markedly reduced viral shedding in feather follicles compared with HVT-vaccinated and unvaccinated groups, indicating reduced potential for horizontal transmission. Mechanistically, rHVT‑gC/Md5 induced gC‑specific T‑cell proliferation, elevated CD8⁺ T‑cell responses, and a Th1‑biased cytokine profile with 11‑fold higher IFN‑γ expression in the thymus versus HVT, while suppressing proinflammatory cytokines. These results establish viral grafting as a novel vaccine design strategy and identify gC as a critical protective antigen against vvMDV.

Introduction

Marek's disease (MD) is a highly contagious lymphoproliferative disorder of chickens caused by Marek's disease virus (MDV), an alphaherpesvirus of the genus Mardivirus (Calnek, 2001). Despite decades of successful vaccination, MD continues to impose substantial economic burdens on the global poultry industry due to the relentless evolution of MDV field strains toward increased virulence (Witter, 1997, 2001). The emergence of very virulent (vv) and very virulent plus (vv+) MDV strains has progressively eroded the protective efficacy of first-generation vaccines, necessitating the development of improved vaccination strategies (Gimeno, 2008; Boodhoo et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2025).

The herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT), classified as Meleagrid alphaherpesvirus 1, was isolated from healthy turkeys and introduced as an MD vaccine in the early 1970s (Witter et al., 1970; Okazaki et al., 1970). HVT is non-pathogenic in chickens, provides robust protection when administered at day of age, and has been widely used both alone and in combination with other serotypes. However, contemporary vvMDV strains can break through HVT-induced immunity, and field evidence indicates that the protective capacity of current vaccines is finite (Baigent et al., 2006; Liu et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2025). Improving HVT efficacy therefore represents an urgent priority for sustaining MD control.

Glycoprotein C (gC) is a conserved alphaherpesvirus glycoprotein with multifunctional roles in viral biology. It mediates initial attachment to cellular heparan sulfate proteoglycans (Mettenleiter et al., 1990; Osterrieder, 1999), participates in virus egress, and contributes to immune evasion by binding complement component C3 (Eisenberg et al., 1987; Huemer et al., 1993). For avian herpesviruses, gC is dispensable for replication in cultured cells but absolutely required for horizontal transmission in chickens, indicating its critical function in natural infection (Jarosinski et al., 2007; Jarosinski and Osterrieder, 2010; Vega-Rodriguez et al., 2021). Importantly, MDV gC can functionally compensate for HVT gC in restoring horizontal transmission in turkeys, demonstrating conserved function across species (Luo et al., 2025). This functional conservation raises the question of whether gC might also contribute to protective immunity—a possibility that has received limited attention. Beyond these traditional roles, early studies using reticuloendotheliosis virus-transformed chicken cell lines demonstrated that MDV glycoproteins, including gC, are recognized by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) from MD-resistant chicken lines (Omar and Schat, 1996; Markowski-Grimsrud and Schat, 2002). Glycoprotein C-expressing target cells were lysed by syngeneic MDV-sensitized splenocytes from genetically resistant chickens, suggesting that gC contains CTL epitopes that may contribute to protective immunity (Markowski-Grimsrud and Schat, 2002). These observations provide a rationale for exploring gC as an immunogen in vaccine design.

In this study, we introduce a novel strategy termed "viral grafting", in which the glycoprotein C (gC) gene of a very virulent Marek's disease virus (vvMDV) strain Md5 is precisely substituted into the HVT vaccine backbone. We hypothesized that replacing the endogenous HVT gC with the gC of vvMDV Md5 would enhance protective immunity against very virulent MDV challenge without compromising vaccine replication fitness, thereby providing superior protection compared to conventional HVT vaccines.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Ethics and Animal Welfare Committee of Henan Agricultural University (Permit number: HNND2025031004, approved on March 10, 2025). Procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Cells, viruses, plasmids and bacteria

Primary chicken embryo fibroblasts (CEFs) were prepared from 9-day-old specific-pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated chicken eggs (Beijing Boehringer Ingelheim, Beijing, China) and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) for growth and 2% FBS for maintenance. The HVT FC-126 strain and vvMDV strain Md5 were propagated and titrated in CEFs using standard procedures. All viruses were stored in liquid nitrogen until use.

The full-length infectious HVT FC-126 strain bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) clone pHVT-BAC and the pEPkan-S plasmid containing kanamycin resistance gene cassette (Kan) were generously provided by Professor Benedikt Kaufer (Freie Universität Berlin, Germany). The pEPkan-S plasmid served as template for Kan amplification. Escherichia coli strain GS1783, which carries chromosomally encoded inducible Red recombinase and I-SceI endonuclease, was generously provided by Professor Gregory A. Smith (Northwestern University, USA) and used for all genetic manipulations. Plasmid pMD18-gC/Md5 containing glycoprotein C gene of Md5 was generated in our lab.

Construction of recombinant HVT-BAC-gC/Md5

For the purpose of generating a recombinant HVT virus carrying the gC gene from vvMDV strain Md5, a two-step Red recombination technology was utilized. By this method, the open reading frame of the HVT gC was precisely substituted with that of Md5. The Kan cassette containing an I-SceI site and 50 bp homology arms corresponding to the Md5 gC gene was amplified by PCR using the pEPkan-S plasmid as template and primers Kan-F/R (Table 1). The amplified fragment was inserted into the NdeI site of pMD18-gC/Md5 to generate donor plasmid pMD18-Kan-gC/Md5. Then a gC-Kan fragment containing the selection marker flanked by 50 bp sequences homologous to HVT gC flanking regions was amplified using plasmid pMD18-Kan-gC/Md5 as template and primers gC-F/R (Table 1). The purified amplicon was electroporated into GS1783 cells harboring pHVT-BAC that had been induced at 42°C to express Red recombinase. First recombination at the HVT gC locus generated intermediate BAC clone pHVT-BAC-Kan-gC/Md5. Subsequent induction with L-arabinose (32°C for 1 h) and temperature shift to 42°C activated I-SceI and Red recombinase expression, excising the kanamycin cassette to yield the final infectious clone pHVT-BAC-gC/Md5. Recombinant virus rHVT-gC/Md5 was rescued by transfecting purified pHVT-BAC-gC/Md5 DNA into primary CEFs using Lipofectamine 3000 (ThermoFisher, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Transfected cells were monitored daily for cytopathic effects, and rescued virus was harvested, plaque-purified three times, and amplified for subsequent experiments.

Identification of recombinant virus rHVT-gC/Md5

CEFs grown on BeyoGold™ 35mm Confocal Dishes (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were infected with either 100 plaque forming unit (PFU) of rHVT-gC/Md5 or parental HVT. At 72 h post-infection, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 30 min at 37°C. Primary antibody incubation was performed for 60 min at 37°C using a mixture of chicken anti-HVT positive serum (1:100 dilution) and rabbit anti-Md5-gC (26aa-106aa) polyclonal antibody (1:100 dilution). After washing with PBS, secondary antibodies, FITC-conjugated rabbit anti-chicken IgY (1:500) and Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500; Proteintech, China), were applied for 60 min at 37°C. Following final washes, fluorescence signals were visualized using a ZEISS Airyscan confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany).

For virus titration, rHVT-gC/Md5 and HVT were serially diluted 10-fold (10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁷) and inoculated onto CEF monolayers in 24-well plates with three replicates per dilution. After 120 h of incubation, plaques were counted in wells containing 5–10 plaques. For growth kinetics, CEFs in 6-well plates were infected with 100 PFU of each virus. At 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, and 144 h post-infection, infected cells were harvested by trypsinization, serially diluted, and co-cultivated with fresh CEFs. Plaques were enumerated 5 days later. Two independent experiments were performed, each with triplicate samples.

Animal experiment design

To evaluate the safety and immune protective efficacy of the recombinant virus rHVT-gC/Md5 in SPF chickens, a total of 140 one-day-old SPF chickens were randomly allocated into four groups. Groups 1 (rHVT-gC/Md5, n=40) and 2 (HVT, n=40) were immunized subcutaneously in the neck with 2000 PFU of the respective vaccines. Group 3 (unvaccinated challenged control, n=40) and Group 4 (uninfected control, n=20) received 100 μL of DMEM. At 6 days of age (5 days post-immunization), all birds except those in the uninfected control group were challenged subcutaneously with 500 PFU of the very virulent MDV strain Md5. This 5-day interval was chosen based on the known rapid onset of HVT‑induced immunity, which is detectable as early as 3‑5 days post‑vaccination (Witter et al., 1970; Okazaki et al., 1970). The birds were monitored daily for growth status, clinical symptoms, morbidity, and mortality throughout the 12-week experimental period. The Protection Index (PI) for each vaccine was calculated based on the incidence rate in each group using the following formula: PI = (Morbidity rate in challenged control group - Morbidity rate in immunized group) / Morbidity rate in challenged control group × 100%.

Body weights of the chickens were recorded weekly starting from the first week post-Md5 challenge. Every two weeks, three SPF chickens from each group were euthanized for pathological examination. Various tissues were observed for gross lesions. The bursa of Fabricius and thymus were collected and weighed to evaluate atrophy using the Bursa/Body Weight Index (BBIX) and Thymus/Body Weight Index (TBIX). These indices were calculated as follows: BBIX = (Bursa weight/Body weight of immunized group) / (Bursa weight/Body weight of healthy control group); TBIX = (Thymus weight/Body weight of immunized group) / (Thymus weight/Body weight of healthy control group). The BBIX and TBIX values were compared between the experimental groups and the healthy control group, where a value < 0.7 indicated tissue atrophy and a value > 0.7 indicated normal tissue morphology. These indices are established parameters for evaluating MDV-induced immunosuppression and vaccine efficacy (Calnek, 2001; Gimeno, 2008).

Viral loads were determined in samples of liver, heart, spleen, kidneys, bursa, and thymus collected from chickens that died during the experiment or were euthanized every two weeks. Additionally, these tissue samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, routinely processed through dehydration and paraffin embedding, and sectioned at 4 µm thickness. The resulting sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological evaluation.

Detection of viral shedding in feather follicles

To monitor Md5 shedding following challenge, feather follicle samples were collected at 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9 weeks post‑challenge. At each time point, three chickens per group were euthanized, and wing feather tips were plucked. The feather follicle at the root was excised, placed into a 2 mL Eppendorf tube containing 1 mL of phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS), and vigorously shaken for 5 min. Subsequently, 200 μL of the supernatant was used for DNA extraction using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Quantitative real‑time PCR (qPCR) with SYBR Green I detection was performed on a CFX96 Real‑Time PCR Detection System (Bio‑Rad, California, USA) using primers Meq‑qF/R (Table 1) to determine the fold change in Md5 DNA levels relative to the uninfected control group. Each sample was tested in triplicate. The 20 μL reaction mixture contained 10 μL of 2 × ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China), 0.5 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μmol/L), 100 ng of DNA template, and nuclease‑free water to a final volume of 20 μL. Thermal cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 15 s. The Meq gene signal was normalized to chicken glyceraldehyde‑3‑phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal reference, and relative fold changes were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method [21]. ΔCT = CT (target) – CT (GAPDH); ΔΔCT = ΔCT (immunized) – ΔCT (control); relative expression = 2-ΔΔCT. All samples were run in triplicates.

Quantification of vvMDV Md5 viral loads in tissues

For viral load detection in tissues, DNA was extracted from 100 mg samples of liver, heart, spleen, kidneys, bursa of Fabricius, and thymus collected at various time points using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). The viral load of Md5 in the tissues was determined by SYBR Green I quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, California, USA) using primers Meq-qF/R (Table 1). The Meq gene was used as the target indicator for the presence of Md5. A standard curve was generated using ten-fold serial dilutions of the plasmid standard pMD18-Meq (constructed by cloning the Md5 Meq gene into the pMD18-T vector), ranging from 10⁹ to 10³ copies/μL, to quantify the copy number of the Meq gene and subsequently determine the viral load in each organ.

The qPCR reaction (total volume 20 μL) consisted of: 10 μL of 2 × ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China), 0.5 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μmol/L), and 2 μL of tissue DNA template. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of amplification (95°C for 10 seconds, 60°C for 15 seconds). The final viral load was calculated as the number of copies per milligram of tissue sample, and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. The limit of detection (LOD) for the assay was 50 copies/mg tissue, and the limit of quantification (LOQ) was 100 copies/mg tissue.

Cytokine expression analysis by qRT-PCR

To assess the effects of rHVT-gC/Md5 immunization on the expression of inflammation-related genes, IL-2, IL-6, CCL4 and IFN-γ, in immune organs during the acute mortality phase, spleen and thymus were collected from birds that died or were euthanized at week 7 post-challenge. Total RNA was extracted using the RNAprep Pure Tissue Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). One microgram of RNA was reverse transcribed using Oligo dT and random primers in ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix Kit (Toyobo, Japan) according to the manufacturer's protocol. One microliter of cDNA was used as template to perform real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China) on a CFX96 system. Primers for chicken IL-6, IL-2, CCL4, IFN-γ, and GAPDH (internal control) are listed in Table 1. Each reaction contained 10 μL 2 × ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix, 0.4 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μmol/L), 2 μL cDNA, and nuclease-free water to 20 μL. Cycling conditions were: 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s. Gene expression levels were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). All samples were run in triplicates.

T lymphocyte subset and proliferation analysis

Splenic lymphocytes were isolated using Chicken Spleen Lymphocyte Separation Medium (TBDscience, Tianjin, China), and peripheral blood lymphocytes were isolated using Chicken Peripheral Blood Lymphocyte Separation Medium (TBDscience, Tianjin, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. At weeks 7, 9, and 11 post-challenge, lymphocytes from each group were counted, and 5 × 10⁶ cells were used for staining. Cells were washed with BD Perm/Wash buffer (BD Biosciences, USA) and incubated with 0.25 μg each of mouse anti-chicken CD8α-PACBLU, mouse anti-chicken CD4-PE, and mouse anti-chicken CD3-APC monoclonal antibodies (Southern Biotech, USA) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. After incubation, cells were centrifuged at 500 × g for 3 min, washed with 2 mL Perm/Wash buffer, and fixed in 0.5 mL 4% paraformaldehyde. Stained cells were analyzed using a BD FACSAria III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, USA).

At week 9 post-challenge, splenic and peripheral blood lymphocytes were isolated as described above. Cells were cultured overnight in RPMI 1640 complete medium, collected by centrifugation at 250 × g for 5 min, resuspended in fresh medium, and counted. Cell concentration was adjusted to 5 × 10⁷ cells/mL in PBS for labeling. CFDA-SE (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was dissolved and added to cell suspensions at a final concentration of 5 μmol/L. Cells were gently vortexed and incubated at 37°C for 10 min in the dark. The reaction was stopped by adding RPMI 1640 complete medium, followed by centrifugation at 450 × g for 5 min. Washing was repeated once, and cells were resuspended at 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL. One hundred microliters (1 × 10⁶ cells) were added to each well of a 96-well plate.

Cells from each group were stimulated with: (1) purified Md5 gC protein (1 μg/well), (2) ConA (1 μg/well, positive control; Beyotime, Shanghai, China), or (3) PBS (negative control). After 24, 48, and 72 h of stimulation, cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and fixed in 0.5 mL 4% paraformaldehyde, respectively. Proliferation was analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSAria III) as CFDA-SE dilution (decreased fluorescence intensity indicates cell division).

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 6 software. Values are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Datasets including in vitro replication characteristics of the recombinant virus, body weight, immune organ indices (BBIX and TBIX), viral loads in various tissues, feather follicles and T cell subset proportions were subjected to two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. The relative expression levels of cytokines in different organs were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, with significance levels indicated as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Construction and in vitro characterization of rHVT-gC/Md5

The gC gene from vvMDV strain Md5 was precisely substituted into the HVT vaccine backbone using two-step Red recombination (Fig. 1). The recombinant virus, rHVT-gC/Md5, was successfully rescued in CEFs, and plaque morphology was indistinguishable from parental HVT (Fig. 2A). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed stable expression of Md5-gC in infected cells, while parental HVT-infected cells only expressed HVT antigens (Fig. 2B). Multistep growth kinetics showed comparable viral titers between rHVT-gC/Md5 and HVT over 144 h, indicating that grafting did not compromise replication in vitro. Both rHVT-gC/Md5 and HVT exhibited progressive increases in viral titers from 24 to 120 hours post-infection, reaching 1.04 × 10⁵ PFU/mL and 1.18 × 10⁵ PFU/mL, respectively (Fig. 2C). rHVT-gC/Md5 confers complete protection against lethal vvMDV challenge

To evaluate protective efficacy, one-day-old SPF chickens were immunized subcutaneously with 2000 PFU of rHVT-gC/Md5, parental HVT, or DMEM (mock), then challenged at 6 days of age with 500 PFU of vvMDV strain Md5 and monitored for 12 weeks. Unvaccinated birds experienced 52% mortality (13/25); HVT vaccination reduced mortality to 28% (7/25). Remarkably, all rHVT-gC/Md5-immunized chickens survived the challenge with 0% mortality and 0% tumor incidence, while HVT-vaccinated birds showed 28% mortality and 36% tumor incidence, and unvaccinated birds exhibited 52% mortality and 60% tumor incidence. Discussion

In this study, we introduce "viral grafting", a strategy of precisely replacing a viral gene with its counterpart from a very virulent strain, as a novel approach to enhance vaccine efficacy. Grafting vvMDV gC onto HVT generated a recombinant virus that provides full protection against lethal challenge, as evidenced by 0% mortality, 0% tumor incidence, undetectable viral load in all organs, preserved immune architecture, and robust gC-specific cellular immunity. This level of efficacy substantially exceeds that of parental HVT and, to our knowledge, represents the highest protection index reported for any serotype 3 based vaccine against contemporary very virulent MDV under the specific challenge conditions used here (500 PFU of vvMDV Md5 strain). For comparison, protection indices of 30‑40 are typically reported for HVT against the same challenge model (Baigent et al., 2006), and recombinant HVT vectors expressing foreign antigens show comparable or slightly enhanced efficacy under similar conditions (Morgan et al., 1993; Darteil et al., 1995).

This study introduces a conceptually distinct approach to vaccine improvement, which we term “viral grafting.” It is important to distinguish this strategy from conventional recombinant vector vaccines. Traditional approaches typically involve inserting foreign antigen genes—such as infectious bursal disease virus VP2 or Newcastle disease virus F protein—into non‑essential regions of the herpesvirus of turkeys (HVT) genome, thereby generating a vector that co‑expresses its own antigens and the foreign immunogen (Morgan et al., 1993; Darteil et al., 1995). While effective, such insertions can impose potential biological costs, including genetic instability over serial passages, possible interference with viral replication fitness, or unintended alterations in immunogenicity. In contrast, viral grafting does not add any foreign genetic material. Instead, it precisely replaces an existing gene of the vaccine virus—in this case, the endogenous HVT gC—with its counterpart from a very virulent strain (Md5 gC). The resulting recombinant virus thus expresses only its own gene products, albeit with an optimized antigenic match to contemporary challenge viruses, and retains the complete native gene repertoire of the vaccine backbone. By avoiding the introduction of exogenous sequences, this strategy is more likely to preserve the favorable growth characteristics, safety profile, and genetic stability of the parental HVT vaccine. Importantly, the enhanced protection observed with rHVT‑gC/Md5 (0% mortality, 0% tumors) therefore arises not from adding an extra immunogen, but from improving the antigenic fidelity of an existing protective antigen. As such, viral grafting represents a new paradigm for enhancing live herpesvirus vaccines, particularly applicable to viral genes that are functionally conserved across strains and capable of eliciting protective cellular immunity. The strategy leverages two key observations. First, gC is functionally conserved among mardiviruses, with MDV gC capable of fully complementing HVT gC function (Jarosinski et al., 2007; Jarosinski and Osterrieder, 2010; Vega-Rodriguez et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2025); this explains why grafting does not impair viral fitness in vitro (Fig. 2C). Second, gC is immunologically relevant. Early studies demonstrated MDV gC recognition by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) from genetically resistant chickens(Omar and Schat, 1996; Markowski-Grimsrud and Schat, 2002). Our data now provide direct evidence: the grafted virus induces gC-specific T cell proliferation (Fig. 10) and elevated CD8+ T cell responses (Fig. 9), identifying gC as a critical protective antigen.

The complete protection achieved, characterized by functional elimination of detectable viral load (Fig. 7), suggests that gC grafting accomplishes more than simple antigen addition. The strong IFN-γ response (Fig. 8) and gC-specific memory T cells (Fig. 10) indicate robust Th1 biased cellular immunity, which is likely responsible for rapid viral clearance. This aligns with emerging "rational vaccine design" principles emphasizing antigenic match between vaccine strains and circulating field isolates (Iwasaki and Omer, 2020).

The protection index of 100 achieved by rHVT-gC/Md5 exceeds that typically reported for HVT alone against vvMDV challenge, which ranges from 30 to 60 depending on challenge strain and conditions (Baigent et al., 2006). It also compares favorably with recombinant HVT vectors expressing foreign antigens such as Newcastle disease virus F protein or infectious bursal disease virus VP2(Morgan et al., 1993; Darteil et al., 1995). Notably, our approach achieves this without introducing any foreign genetic material, simply by optimizing an existing viral gene. The elimination of detectable viral load is particularly striking. Conventional MD vaccines prevent disease but do not prevent infection or viral replication (Haq et al., 2012). The near complete viral clearance achieved by rHVT-gC/Md5 suggests a qualitatively different immune response, likely mediated by the gC-specific CD8+ T cells we documented (Fig. 9, Fig. 10). This may have important implications for field performance, as reduced viral shedding could decrease horizontal transmission and slow the emergence of vaccine resistant strains.

Several lines of evidence converge to establish gC as a critical protective antigen. First, gC-specific T cell proliferation was observed only in rHVT-gC/Md5 immunized birds (Fig. 10), demonstrating that grafting generated immune recognition of the grafted antigen. Second, CD8+ T cell responses were significantly enhanced in grafted virus immunized birds (Fig. 9), consistent with CTL mediated immunity. Third, the strong IFN-γ response (Fig. 8) indicates Th1 polarization, which is optimal for antiviral defense. Fourth, suppression of virus driven inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-2, and CCL4, suggests that rapid viral clearance prevents the immune dysregulation characteristic of MDV infection (Parvizi et al., 2010). Notably, the contrasting patterns observed between these inflammatory mediators and IFN-γ highlight the ability of rHVT-gC/Md5 to simultaneously suppress pathological inflammation while promoting protective cellular immunity.

Glycoprotein C is conserved throughout the Alphaherpesvirinae subfamily, with homologous proteins in herpes simplex virus, pseudorabies virus, equine herpesvirus, and bovine herpesvirus (Mettenleiter et al., 1990; Osterrieder, 1999). In several of these viruses, gC has been shown to contain CTL epitopes recognized during natural infection (Banks and Rouse, 1992; Ober et al., 1998). The grafting strategy may therefore prove broadly applicable for enhancing other herpesvirus vaccines. For example, replacing gC in pseudorabies virus vaccine strains with that of contemporary field isolates, or optimizing bovine herpesvirus vaccines by grafting gC from virulent strains, could represent promising extensions of this approach.

However, several questions remain unanswered. The precise epitopes within gC responsible for protective immunity require mapping. The duration of protection beyond 12 weeks should be confirmed, although MD vaccines typically confer lifelong immunity. Efficacy against other vvMDV and vv+MDV strains, including recent field isolates, must be evaluated. The potential for reduced horizontal transmission in vaccinated flocks, suggested by undetectable viral loads, merits direct testing in contact transmission experiments. In addition, although the recombinant virus was genetically stable during in vitro passages, its long‑term genetic stability and safety during serial in vivo passages have not been formally demonstrated. Finally, commercial scale production, stability, and field performance under typical husbandry conditions will determine practical utility.

In conclusion, we demonstrate that viral grafting of vvMDV gC onto HVT generates a recombinant vaccine conferring full protection against lethal challenge. This work establishes gC as a critical protective antigen through the induction of gC- specific T cell immunity and Th1 biased cytokine responses, and introduces a novel platform for next generation herpesvirus vaccines. The rHVT-gC/Md5 construct represents a promising vaccine candidate for controlling evolving very virulent MDV strains and warrants accelerated evaluation for commercial application.
