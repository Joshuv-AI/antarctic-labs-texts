DeepSAP: improved RNA-seq alignment by integrating transcriptome guidance with transformer-based splice junction scoring
Authors: Fadel Berakdar, Thomas D Wu, Tong Zhu, Mehrzad Samadi, Pankaj Vats
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvancements in high-throughput sequencing have revolutionized transcriptomics, enabling insights into gene expression, splicing, and fusions. However, RNA-seq analysis remains challenging due to complex splice junctions, multi-mapped reads, and chimeric events. We present DeepSAP, which improves RNA-seq alignment by integrating GSNAP’s transcriptome-guided genomic alignment with transformer-based splice-junction scoring. This synergy enhances splice-junction detection, indel identification, and resolution of complex splicing patterns. On the Baruzzo human simulated benchmark across complexities, DeepSAP achieves the highest mean F1-score for splice junction detection, outperforming DRAGEN, novoSplice, STAR, HISAT2, and Subjunc. DeepSAP captures intricate sequence patterns surrounding splice donor and acceptor sites, advancing RNA-seq analysis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04100-3.

Background

High-throughput sequencing has revolutionized our understanding of molecular biology, with RNA-seq allowing us to explore the transcriptome in unprecedented detail. RNA-seq enables us to quantify differential gene expression under various conditions or treatments, as well as identify both known and novel transcripts. Novel transcripts can reveal gene fusions, alternative splicing events, and non-coding RNA that may have been overlooked by previous methodologies. However, achieving such insights into gene regulation and function depends critically on accurate read alignment. RNA-seq protocols produce short fragments with read lengths of 100–200 base pairs, either from both ends (paired-end) or from one end (single-end) of a cDNA fragment. Aligning short reads to the genome is challenging due to sequencing errors, as well as issues unique to RNA-seq, including intronic regions, alternative splicing, incomplete reference transcriptomes, non-coding RNA, pseudogenes, ambiguous splicing patterns, and RNA editing. In particular, as RNA-seq is applied to individual transcriptomes, which have shown surprising variability [1], it is becoming increasingly important to detect such personalized variations accurately.

Inherent complexities in RNA-seq data necessitate the use of specialized alignment algorithms and tools designed to address their unique characteristics, making RNA-seq alignment significantly more challenging than DNA-seq. Although current RNA-seq alignment programs can handle most reads effectively, especially when they are consistent with a reference transcriptome, biologically significant phenomena remain problematic, particularly reads containing insertions or deletions (indels), reads spanning cryptic splice junctions, and those originating from genomic regions with single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) or mutations. Another major issue is multi-mapped reads, which can align to multiple genomic locations with similar confidence scores. To ensure accurate results, aligners must consider all possible genomic locations during the alignment process.

To address these challenges, a variety of splice-aware alignment tools have been developed over the years. The first category includes alignment-based RNA-seq tools like GSNAP [2], STAR [3] and HISAT2 [4]. These programs perform a complete alignment between reads and the genomic reference, producing a Binary Alignment/Map (BAM) file as output, which contains base-level alignment and junction calls, making them ideal for comprehensive RNA-seq analysis. In contrast, alignment-free tools, such as Salmon [5] and Kallisto [6], function by matching k-mers of reads to a transcriptomic reference without performing a complete alignment. Although these approaches significantly accelerate the process and can generally provide sufficient accuracy for transcript quantification, alignment-free methods are relatively ineffective for detecting mismatches, indels, novel isoforms, or alternative splicing events, and may underperform compared with traditional alignment tools on small low-abundance genes and non-coding RNA.

The performance of RNA-seq alignment tools has been evaluated previously in published benchmarking studies. Notably, a study conducted by Baruzzo et al. [7] performed an exhaustive benchmarking analysis of 14 widely used RNA-seq aligners, utilizing simulated datasets derived from annotated transcriptomes of two different species, human and malaria (Plasmodium falciparum). The study evaluated the performance of each tool over multiple criteria, including base-level accuracy, read-level alignment, and splice junction detection. The study generated simulated datasets at three progressively increasing levels of complexity (T1, T2, and T3) for both human and malaria, designed to emulate realistic biological variation and sequencing noise. T1 represents the least complex condition, with low polymorphism rates (0.001 substitutions, 0.0001 indels) and a sequencing error rate of 0.005, roughly corresponding to mapping high-quality RNA-seq reads to a corresponding reference genome. T2 introduces moderate polymorphism (0.005 substitutions, 0.002 indels) and an error rate of 0.01, approximating data quality observed in well-characterized model organisms. T3 represents the most challenging situation, with high polymorphism (0.03 substitutions, 0.005 indels) and an error rate of 0.02, intended to mimic highly divergent or noisy sequencing data such as those encountered in complex oncology samples. The results revealed significant differences in the performance of aligners, particularly in their proficiency at detecting indels, mismatches, and splice junctions. In the study conducted by Baruzzo et al., as well as in a separate study by Grant et al. [8], GSNAP outperformed other aligners on both read- and base-level benchmarks. Nevertheless, all aligners demonstrated areas for improvement, particularly at the T3 level of alignment complexity.

To further improve alignment accuracy and overcome existing limitations, we have developed the Deep Splice Alignment Program (DeepSAP). This framework introduces two novel methodologies: (1) Transcriptome-Guided Genomic Alignment (TGGA), as a novel capability implemented in GSNAP and (2) a Transformer-based Splice Junction Scoring (TSJS) module, which uses state-of-the-art deep learning methods to evaluate splice junctions in alignment output (Fig. 1). The TGGA feature leverages a given transcriptome (such as those used by alignment-free methods) to allow for a relatively straightforward alignment of reads to known transcripts, while retaining the capacity for full genomic alignment to accommodate novel splice phenomena. The concept of combining transcriptome and genome information for alignment was explored previously in the RNA-seq Unified Mapper (RUM) [8]. However, successful implementation of the idea is challenging, which our work has addressed. Specifically, although aligning to a transcriptome is much simpler than aligning to a genome because intron gaps do not need to be considered, a read can still potentially contain mismatches and indels compared with its transcript of origin. Therefore, we have found that several methods are required to perform transcriptome alignment accurately and efficiently. In our implementation, TGGA in GSNAP employs three methods in successive order: exact search, prevalence search, and extension search, until a satisfactory alignment is obtained (Fig. 1). Another challenge in implementation involves the criterion for a satisfactory transcript alignment, before performing a more time-consuming genomic alignment. A genomic alignment is necessary when a read does not come from a transcript in the reference transcriptome, or deviates significantly from the annotated transcript. For paired-end reads, our implementation of TGGA searches for a concordant alignment of the two ends to the same transcript; for single-end reads, TGGA in GSNAP looks for an alignment with 95% or more of the nucleotides in the read matching the transcript. If a satisfactory transcriptome alignment cannot be found, then GSNAP attempts to find an alignment to the genome using its standard methods, which are already among the most accurate, thereby enhancing the overall accuracy of TGGA.

Overview of the DeepSAP workflow. DeepSAP utilizes the TGGA methodology in GSNAP initially to align short-read RNA-seq data. The TGGA aligner employs three methods for transcriptome alignment (exact search, prevalence search, and extension search) to find a satisfactory solution. Otherwise, GSNAP resorts to genomic alignment. In the next phase, it incorporates Transformer-based Splice Junction Scoring (TSJS) which uses the fine-tuned DNABERT MS150 transformer model to enhance splice junction detection. This second stage analyzes all alignments generated by TGGA GSNAP for each query read, recalibrates the MAPQ (mapping quality) scores for multi-mapped reads and applies soft clipping for splice junctions that exhibit either low transformer scores or flanking bases with suboptimal Phred quality scores

Building upon the improvements in alignment accuracy using TGGA, we have also integrated state-of-the-art large language models (LLMs), built on transformer-based architectures, to improve RNA-seq alignment accuracy through the enhanced evaluation of splice junctions. Our methodology leverages transformers [9], which excel at capturing long-range dependencies and complex sequence patterns. Transformers are specifically designed to process and understand sequences by focusing on relationships between elements using a mechanism called “attention”. This enables them to effectively capture context and relationships within data. LLMs, such as GPT and BERT, are large-scale implementations of transformers trained on massive datasets. While originally developed for natural language processing tasks like generating human-like text, answering questions, and translating languages, transformers have also shown tremendous potential in genomics. Models such as DNABERT [10], DNABERT2 [11], and Nucleotide Transformer [12] highlight the adaptability of transformers to biological sequence analysis.

Recently, deep learning methods have been applied to improve the accuracy of splice site prediction, starting with SpliceAI [13], which used a deep convolutional neural network architecture, whereas another deep learning method Splam [14] uses deep residual neural networks. Subsequently, many researchers have shown that such neural network models are outperformed by transformer-based models; notable examples include SpliceTransformer [15], Transformer-45k [16], and TrASPr [17].

Most deep learning approaches for splice site prediction have been applied at the genome level, trying to identify splice junctions across the genome, which may have been inferred from summaries of genomic read alignments. For example, SpliceAI is designed to infer splice junctions in both normal and mutated genomic contexts, while tools such as Splam perform as a post-processing filter on a set of splice junctions to improve the quality of downstream transcriptome assemblies. In contrast, our work diverges fundamentally from genome-level analysis to consider issues at the read level, by focusing on the accuracy of splice junctions in individual reads from a given sample. Applying splice site scoring at the level of individual reads offers significant advantages, given that many downstream analyses depend on a sufficient number of read alignments that support the detection of splice junctions as well as SNPs and indels.

In this paper, we present and evaluate our implementation of TGGA and TSJS in the DeepSAP workflow. We show that the two components are synergistic, with TGGA providing high sensitivity in alignment, whereas TSJS provides high specificity. The efficacy of our approach is demonstrated through comprehensive benchmarking, comparing DeepSAP with state-of-the-art aligners, and quantifying the benefits of integrating sensitive alignment with transformer-based splice junction scoring.

Results

Fine-tuning of transformer models for splice site scoring

To optimize transformer models for RNA-seq data, we fine-tuned pre-trained transformers that were initially trained on extensive genomic datasets. We evaluated various transformer models, including DNABERT k = 6-mer [10], DNABERT2 [11], and the 2.5B multi-species Nucleotide Transformer (NT) [12].

These models were fine-tuned on labeled donor and acceptor site sequences, as well as negative examples. Negative examples were generated randomly from the genome by selecting pairs of genomic positions based on the following criteria: (1) they do not overlap with known donor or acceptor sites, (2) they are located within a predefined distance range from each other corresponding to a maximum allowed intron length, and (3) the negative dataset was created such that the percentage of canonical splicing motifs (GT-AG) matched the proportion observed in the true (positive) examples. The motif sequences of true acceptor and donor sites were kept in the center as input for the transformer models. Input sequence lengths surrounding each splice site were fixed per dataset at 90, 150, 200, or 400 bases. Separate models were generated using multiple sources of splice junctions: (1) GRCh38 NCBI RefSeq assembly (GCF_000001405.40), (2) GRCh38 GENCODE release 44, (3) malaria ASM276v2, and (4) a multi-species dataset that included splice junctions from Homo sapiens, Arabidopsis thaliana, Zea mays, Xenopus tropicalis, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Drosophila melanogaster, Caenorhabditis elegans, Rattus norvegicus, Mus musculus, Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium berghei, and Plasmodium knowlesi.

The fine-tuning datasets could therefore be categorized by species and sequence length: RefSeq90, RefSeq150, RefSeq200, and RefSeq400 for human RefSeq data; GENCODE150 and GENCODE400 for GENCODE data; Malaria90, Malaria150, Malaria200, and Malaria400 for malaria data; and MS150 for a multi-species dataset. Each dataset was shuffled randomly and split into training (70%), testing (15%), and validation (15%) sets, ensuring no data leakage between them. The validation set remained completely unseen during training and testing. The number of fine-tuning examples increased as sequence length decreased, given that the exon and intron lengths had to exceed half the window size. For instance, RefSeq90 included 630,424 sequences, whereas RefSeq400 contained 58,760. These extensive datasets facilitated the effective fine-tuning and evaluation of transformer models for splice junction prediction.

We evaluated our fine-tuned models using the validation dataset to measure their effectiveness in identifying splice sites. Their performance was assessed using several metrics: loss function, which measures the deviation of the model’s predictions from the correct answers (lower loss is better); MCC (Matthews correlation coefficient), which indicates the overall accuracy of the model, even with imbalanced data; F1-score, which balances precision and recall to show how well the model avoids false predictions; recall, which indicates how many true splice sites the model detected; accuracy, which represents the overall percentage of correct predictions; and precision, which measures the proportion of correctly identified splice sites. These metrics helped us evaluate the reliability of each model in identifying complex splice patterns in genetic data.

Our results demonstrated improved performance when using RefSeq datasets, achieving the highest MCC across all fine-tuned transformer models (Fig. 2A). Notably, the DNABERT model consistently outperformed DNABERT2, regardless of sequence length or genome annotation. For instance, the MCC measurements showed: DNABERT RefSeq150 at 0.9655, DNABERT2 RefSeq150 at 0.9394, and NT at 0.9628. Detailed results of recall, precision, F1-scores, and accuracy are provided in Additional file 1: Fig. S1.

To identify an optimal DNABERT configuration, we performed a hyperparameter ablation study on RefSeq150 across k-mer sizes (k=4,5,6), learning rates (LR=1×10-5, 3×10-5, 6×10-5) and batch sizes (BS=8, 16, 32, 64, 128) (Fig. 2B and Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Our search selected DNABERT parameters of k=4, BS =32, and LR =6×10-5 as the best-performing setting. We then compared splice-site probability distributions across splice signals between the default configuration and the optimized model, both fine-tuned on the multi-species dataset (Fig. 2C and Additional file 1: Fig. S3). The default model showed probability distributions for non-canonical splice signals that had slightly higher densities near probabilities of 1.0, suggesting improved robustness on rare splicing signals. Based on this analysis, we hereafter refer to the default DNABERT model fine-tuned on the multi-species dataset with window size 150 and default hyperparameters (k=6, BS =8, LR =3×10-5) as DNABERT MS150, and we used this model as the transformer component in the DeepSAP workflow.

Our analysis revealed that the multi-species DNABERT MS150 demonstrated a stronger ability than other models, including the SpliceAI model, to discriminate correct from incorrect splice junctions, clustering positive examples closer to a score of 100 and negative splice junction examples closer to 0 (Fig. 2F and Additional file 1: Fig. S4A). This advantage was particularly evident in the malaria datasets, where the multi-species model outperformed even the malaria-specific model in differentiating between true donor and acceptor sites (Fig. 2G).

Moreover, attention maps generated by the DNABERT MS150 model reveal that at true donor and acceptor splice sites that contain canonical GT or AG dinucleotides, respectively, the model assigned high attention weights to 6-mers up to 75 base pairs upstream of donor splice sites and both upstream and downstream of acceptor splice sites. These maps suggest that, among potential splice sites with canonical dinucleotides, exon content is highly critical for predicting donor splices, perhaps due to differing codon usage between coding regions in exons and non-coding regions in introns. For acceptor splice sites, the upstream region may contain sequences critical for the spliceosome, such as the branch point sequence. In contrast, attention weights around negative examples in general were significantly lower than those of positive examples (Fig. 2D, E), implying that the local context around the splice site also plays an important part in prediction. The attention maps demonstrate the advantage of long-range models in transformers, compared with local models such as MaxEnt [18], which look at only 3 to 9 base pairs surrounding each splice site. Our finding is consistent with other studies [13] that have found that using relatively long regions is favorable for splice site prediction.

Although exon and intron lengths were not used as inputs to the transformer scoring function, we observed a notable relationship between predicted splice site probabilities and these lengths (Fig. 2H, I). Specifically, intron lengths exhibited a bimodal distribution, whereas exon lengths followed a unimodal distribution, consistent with established biological patterns. The bimodal distribution of intron lengths is well-documented in the literature and has been attributed to evolutionary mechanisms that favor distinct classes of introns [19]. Furthermore, the performance of DNABERT on the RefSeq400 dataset demonstrates its ability to handle complex splicing motifs more effectively than SpliceAI400 (the model using 400-bp context) [13] (Fig. 2J). Notably, DNABERT achieved high accuracy in predicting rarer biotypes and splicing signals across different species. These findings underscore the adaptability and robustness of DNABERT in capturing intricate splicing patterns.

Benchmarking RNA-seq aligners

We evaluated the performance of DeepSAP using the Baruzzo [7] and SimBA [20] simulated datasets against several state-of-the-art RNA-seq aligners, including DRAGENTM v4.0.3 (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA), novoSplice v0.8.4 [21], STAR v2.7.10a [3], HISAT2 v2.2.1 [4], and Subjunc v2.0.1 [22]. The SimBA dataset was generated by a set of tools designed to generate realistic simulated RNA-seq datasets [20]. All aligners were benchmarked using their default settings, and performance was evaluated in terms of precision and recall on three levels of alignment: read, base, and junction, using the same metrics from the original study by Baruzzo. At the read level, a read was considered correctly mapped if at least one base from the entire read aligned to its true genomic origin. At the base level, metrics provided a more granular view by evaluating the fate of each nucleotide, distinguishing correctly matched bases from insertions, deletions, and skipped intronic positions. Here, “skipped bases” included reference bases spanned by intronic gaps in a spliced alignment (i.e., the length of N operations), and were therefore distinct from junction-level counts. At the junction level, metrics captured how accurately splice sites were identified, scoring the number of correct splice boundary events rather than the number of bases inserted, deleted, or included in an intron.

DeepSAP demonstrated exceptional performance, yielding high precision and recall values across the majority of evaluated metrics (Fig. 3A). A notable exception was observed in the detection of insertions and deletions (indels), which presented a greater analytical challenge and resulted in comparatively lower accuracy. The performance of DRAGEN and novoSplice was also highly competitive, with their results on the T1 and T2 datasets being largely comparable to those of DeepSAP. However, DeepSAP outperformed DRAGEN and novoSplice on the most complex T3 datasets.

Figure 3B provides a detailed breakdown of base-level alignment metrics and illustrates DeepSAP’s exceptional accuracy and robustness, particularly when applied to high-complexity datasets. While other top-performing alignment algorithms such as DRAGEN and novoSplice demonstrated performance parity with DeepSAP on the less complex T1 and T2 datasets, DeepSAP consistently maintained the highest percentage of correctly aligned bases (represented by green bars) when evaluated on the most challenging T3 datasets. To establish statistical measures for these observations, we conducted a comprehensive bootstrap analysis utilizing 500 replicates (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A). The bootstrapping methodology confirmed that the performance characteristics observed across individual replicates demonstrate statistical robustness. The tight 95% confidence intervals associated with DeepSAP performance, even when evaluated on the most challenging T3 datasets, substantiate the reliability of its high accuracy metrics and demonstrate minimal performance variance across complex scenarios.

Focusing on the most challenging event detection tasks, Fig. 3C displays the aggregated F1-scores for detecting splice junctions, deletions, and insertions on the human datasets across the three complexities (T1, T2, T3) and replicates (R1, R2, R3). The F1-score metric, which balances precision and recall, revealed a clear performance hierarchy among the aligners. DeepSAP, DRAGEN, and novoSplice were among the best performers, achieving the highest F1-scores across all three categories. DeepSAP, in particular, was consistently the top performer especially for junction detection where it showed a clear advantage over all other tools with a range between 0.939 and 0.988. The tight confidence intervals for DeepSAP also indicate its highly consistent performance. In contrast, STAR, HISAT2, and Subjunc exhibited significantly lower F1-scores, indicating a poorer balance between precision and recall for these critical metrics. Detailed results for all datasets (human and malaria), including junction, skipping, insertion and deletion events, can be found in Additional file 2: Tables S1–S6.

A statistical comparison of the best performing aligners, DeepSAP and DRAGEN, on the most challenging T3R1 dataset (Fig. 3D) highlights their unique strengths and performance profiles. Violin plots illustrate the distribution of per-pair performance scores, with reported means and statistical significance determined by the two-sample Z-test described in the methods. The results reveal a critical distinction in performance strategy. While both aligners demonstrated exceptionally high precision, the most substantial performance gap was in recall. DeepSAP consistently achieved significantly higher recall across junction, deletion, and insertion variants compared to DRAGEN (p-value < 1E-308 for all three). For instance, DeepSAP achieved a mean deletion recall of 0.616 compared to DRAGEN’s 0.471. This indicates that on challenging data with high polymorphism and error rates, DeepSAP’s primary advantage derived from its outstanding sensitivity, or recall, without compromising its high level of precision. Comparison with other aligners across different datasets is provided in Additional file 1: Fig. S5B.

The generalizability of these results was confirmed using the SimBA RNA-seq benchmark suite [20]. Under similar levels of sequence complexity, the DeepSAP again demonstrated robust and consistently high performance (Additional file 1: Fig. S6).

Finally, to more clearly characterize splice junction detection under controlled ground truth, we quantified false negatives (missed true junctions) and false positives (spurious junction calls) in simulated Baruzzo and SimBA datasets for human and malaria across increasing complexity levels (Additional file 1: Fig. S7). In this setting, DeepSAP, which combines the GSNAP-TGGA aligner with TSJS, was benchmarked against GSNAP (without and with TGGA enabled), DRAGEN, and STAR, as well as TSJS-augmented versions of DRAGEN and STAR. Our results show that the TGGA algorithm substantially reduces false negatives, reflecting a marked gain in splice-site sensitivity, but at the cost of an increased number of false positives. The TSJS module counterbalances this effect by filtering out low-confidence junctions and thereby restoring higher specificity. When applied to other aligners, the TSJS module likewise reduces false positives by suppressing spurious junction calls, indicating that it provides a broadly beneficial post-processing step.

Detection of complex splice junctions

We evaluated the performance of various aligners in detecting splice junctions using both simulated and real-world datasets, resulting in scatterplots that compare splice junction counts from each aligner against the ground truth from the Baruzzo simulated datasets. Our results (Fig. 4A and Additional file 1: Fig. S8) revealed significant variability among aligners, particularly in the Baruzzo T3 dataset. Among the evaluated methods, DeepSAP demonstrated the closest agreement to the ground truth, as evidenced by the Theil–Sen slope, which indicated the strongest concordance along the main diagonal. Additionally, pairwise scatterplots comparing the aligners (Fig. 4B) revealed that DeepSAP demonstrated strong correlations with both DRAGEN and novoSplice in terms of known and novel splice junction counts. Notably, DeepSAP identified a greater number of splice junctions compared to Subjunc, STAR, and HISAT2, as illustrated in (Fig. 4A, B).

Our methodology achieved significantly higher T3 accuracy compared with previous benchmarks on these challenging datasets. For example, we highlight such an improvement in the ANK3 gene (Fig. 4C–E), which contains simulated mismatches and insertions near both donor and acceptor splice sites and was detected with the highest sensitivity by DeepSAP and novoSplice.

These plots show strong Pearson correlations with highly significant p-values when comparing DeepSAP with DRAGEN and novoSplice for both known and novel splice junctions. In the publicly available pHGG sample SRR5280319, for example, our workflow identifies a novel splicing event involving a retained intron in the SMARCC2 gene (Fig. 5B), which was missed by other aligners and not found in a previous survey of alternate splicing in high-grade gliomas using STAR [23]. This splice junction was characterized by multiple mismatches near its splice sites (Fig. 5C, D), explaining why it was observed only by DeepSAP.

Another real-world example was observed in myelodysplastic syndrome sample SRR6781226 for the FAM72A gene (Additional file 1: Fig. S9E), which plays a crucial role in regulating DNA damage repair and maintaining genome stability [24–26]. In our analysis, complex splice junctions supported by annotated ENCODE transcripts were identified by DeepSAP, DRAGEN, and novoSplice, but missed by other aligners. For instance, the splice junction in transcript ENST00000470041.5 was identified with coverage of 12, 14, and 14 reads by DeepSAP, DRAGEN, and novoSplice, respectively, while remaining undetected by other aligners. Similarly, the splice junction in transcript ENST00000367129.6 was detected with coverage of 5, 4, and 4 reads by DeepSAP, DRAGEN, and novoSplice, respectively, and was missed by other aligners.

The sensitivity of DeepSAP allowed it to detect complex, non-canonical splice junctions. For example, DeepSAP successfully identified a novel splice junction in the FOXA1 locus within a prostate cancer sample (SRR10354196) from the PRJNA579899 project [27]. This finding highlights DeepSAP’s effectiveness in uncovering previously unrecognized splicing events in cancer genomics. As visualized using sashimi plots (Fig. 5E), DeepSAP successfully identified this novel junction while the widely-used aligner STAR failed to detect it. Notably, STAR’s failure occurred even when using parameters previously tuned for high sensitivity [28]. The difficulty arises due to the rare ’GC–AG’ dinucleotide pair at the donor and acceptor sites, further complicated by two mismatches (wildtype GG to variant AA) near the donor site, as confirmed by the read pileup (Fig. 5F, G). To understand the transformer model’s prediction mechanism in a non-canonical novel splice junction with high coverage, we investigated the splice site probabilities using the DNABERT MS150 model (Fig. 5H). The analysis revealed that while the reference donor site with the ’GC’ signal had a very low probability (0.085), incorporating the GG-to-AA mismatches from the supporting reads increased this probability to 0.214. To confirm the model’s behavior, we tested a hypothetical sequence where the non-canonical ’GC’ was replaced with a canonical ’GT’ (with the GG-to-AA mismatches retained), which yielded a near-perfect probability of 0.992. However, when we changed only the donor splice site without incorporating the GG-to-AA mismatches, then the donor site probability dropped to 0.409. These findings highlight DeepSAP’s ability to integrate both canonical signals and supporting sequence evidence, enabling accurate interpretation of complex, novel splice junctions.

Detection of insertions and deletions

Another application of RNA-seq alignment is to identify indels, which can play a role in various diseases, including cancer. To evaluate the performance of different aligners in detecting indels, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of indel detection using the Baruzzo [7] simulated datasets and a standard pipeline based on VarScan (Fig. 6A). The resulting F1-scores showed that DeepSAP consistently delivered high and stable performance in detecting both insertions and deletions with performance that was matched closely by that of DRAGEN and novoSplice; all three tools demonstrated robust F1-scores, typically exceeding 0.75, and low variability across replicates. While all aligners were affected by increasing dataset complexity, DeepSAP, DRAGEN, and novoSplice maintained high F1-scores even on the most challenging T3 datasets.

Discussion

The improved performance of DeepSAP appears to derive from two key factors. First, it takes advantage of domain knowledge in the form of known transcripts through our implementation of TGGA in GSNAP. Domain knowledge about known splice sites has been used routinely by several aligners, including GSNAP, that allow the user to specify a database of known splice sites. However, TGGA leverages additional domain knowledge in the form of paired splice site information, or introns between corresponding donor and acceptor splice sites. In contrast, when only individual splice sites are provided, an aligner may still not be able to determine the matching splice site. Transcriptome alignment also simplifies the use of domain knowledge about splice sites, since alignment to a transcript is relatively straightforward in the absence of intron gaps. The process of remapping a transcriptome alignment to the genome is also generally straightforward. Nevertheless, GSNAP retains the ability to handle novel transcripts not present in the given transcriptome. If a transcriptome alignment is not adequate, GSNAP then aligns it to the genome using conventional splice-aware alignment methods that do allow for intron gaps. This dual approach yields higher accuracy and efficiency, especially at the ends of reads, where a genomic alignment may have insufficient evidence to determine the location of an intron, whereas known transcripts can often indicate its precise endpoints.

Second, DeepSAP utilizes fine-tuned transformer models specifically designed for splice junction scoring, capturing intricate sequence patterns around splice donor and acceptor sites to significantly enhance splice junction detection accuracy. Our analysis demonstrates that the DNABERT model consistently outperforms both DNABERT2 and Nucleotide Transformer 2.5B models across diverse species and datasets, with particularly notable performance improvements by using multi-species fine-tuning datasets such as MS150, while attention maps further reveal the critical role of distant exon and intron content in predicting splice sites accurately.

The methods of TGGA and transformer-based models are highly complementary. TGGA, as implemented in GSNAP, demonstrates high sensitivity in identifying introns, particularly at the ends of reads. Although this sensitivity can result in an increased rate of false positives, the transformer model is able to accurately detect false splice sites, thereby enhancing overall accuracy of alignment. The importance of splice site models has been recognized by GSNAP since its inception, relying on splice site scoring using the MaxEnt model [18], which examines only a handful of nucleotides near the splice site. In contrast, modern transformer-based models provide superior accuracy over local splice site models in distinguishing true splice sites from false ones, offering a significant improvement over traditional approaches.

While aligners generally performed well on the human and malaria T1 and T2 datasets, the T3 datasets presented the greatest challenges, revealing significant differences in accuracy across aligners. The performance trends across various benchmarking metrics in the Baruzzo datasets indicate that as dataset complexity increases or species of origin shifts, aligner accuracy can vary significantly. Notably, DeepSAP stands out for its consistent accuracy across these conditions, demonstrating a strong capability in handling complex splice junctions and transcriptomics variations in both human and malaria RNA-seq datasets. Although T3 dataset-level complexity is uncommon, it is frequently observed in cancer samples with elevated mutation burdens, particularly in metastatic cancers, mismatch repair-deficient tumors, and melanomas. These malignancies often demonstrate sequence variations comparable to the T3 dataset across their entire genomic landscape. Furthermore, while real-world samples may not exhibit overall T3-level complexity genome-wide, specific transcripts of interest within these samples may reach complexity levels comparable to those observed in the T3 dataset.

Although this work focuses on the alignment step, the proposed improvements have important implications for downstream RNA-seq analyses, in which read alignments underpin biological inference and enable more sensitive and specific detection of transcriptional events, particularly for transcript quantification, which depends on the accurate assignment of reads to genomic loci. For transcript isoform analysis, TGGA further facilitates isoform-level expression estimation by reporting, for each read, the set of compatible transcripts, which can be directly used by expectation maximization (EM) based methods. Beyond expression, improved alignment accuracy is expected to enhance the detection of mismatches, insertions, deletions, and anomalous splice junctions, for which sensitivity (recall) is critical and false positives can be mitigated by cumulative evidence from multiple reads. In our benchmarks, DeepSAP achieves higher recall for insertions, deletions, and splice junctions than other aligners, and DeepSAP and DRAGEN exhibit consistently better overall alignment quality on the challenging T3 datasets. Since cancer is characterized by the most complex genomic mutations and transcriptional changes, DeepSAP should be particularly useful for identifying novel transcriptional phenomena in tumor samples.

In addition, we believe that the accuracy of RNA-seq alignment will become increasingly important for personalized and individualized analyses of transcriptomes. In the past, analysis with existing aligners has been useful in exploring the shared, common repertoire of the transcriptome in the human population. But as sequencing becomes cheaper and more widely used, the next frontier will be to explore novel transcript phenomena in the presence of individual sequence variations and indels, where corroborating information from other individuals will be absent, and whose presence can be revealed only with the sensitivity and specificity provided by advances in RNA-seq alignment such as DeepSAP.

For future work, after validating our strategy with the DeepSAP workflow, we are motivated to work on improving its speed, through approaches that include our own expertise in adapting and accelerating aligners by utilizing graphics processing units (GPUs). Another approach may include incorporating the transformer model to inform the alignment process rather than applying it as a post-processing step, which may also improve overall accuracy.

Conclusions

DeepSAP provides a significant advancement in RNA-seq alignment, addressing long-standing challenges in detecting splice junctions, identifying indels, and resolving complex splicing patterns. Its improved performance derives from its innovative integration of transcript-guided genomic alignment with transformer-based deep learning models. In simulated datasets, DeepSAP consistently ranked as the top performer in recall and precision for read-level and base-level metrics, and the highest sensitivity in detecting splice junctions compared with widely used aligners like STAR, HISAT2, DRAGEN, novoSplice and Subjunc. The ability of improved alignment to help discover complex splice phenomena was also demonstrated in several examples from tumor samples.

Methods

Transcriptome-guided genomic alignment

To leverage the domain knowledge contained in known transcripts, we have developed the methodology of TGGA, and implemented it in GSNAP. Essentially, TGGA first aligns a given read to a transcriptome and, if such an alignment is unsuccessful, it subsequently attempts to align the read to the genome using conventional splice-aware alignment methods that accommodate intron gaps. Although this idea seems relatively straightforward, a successful implementation depends upon the development of specific algorithms for (1) performing accurate and efficient transcriptome alignment, (2) determining whether to accept transcriptome-based alignments or proceed to genome-based alignment, and (3) converting transcriptome alignments to genomic alignments, resolving instances where multiple transcript alignments are plausible but yield differing results when mapped onto the genome. The conversion of transcriptome alignments to genomic ones is what distinguishes a transcriptome alignment from transcriptome-guided genomic alignment.

Before we can align RNA-seq reads using TGGA, we need to pre-process both the transcriptome and genome. We assume that a transcriptome is available as exon coordinates on a given genome, and such sequences are readily available in GFF or GTF formats from standard genome assemblies hosted by sites like NCBI and Ensembl. Alternatively, if transcript sequences are available without their genomic coordinates, these coordinates can be obtained by aligning the transcripts to a given genome using long-read RNA aligners such as GMAP.

For subsequent alignment, the transcriptome is pre-processed using the standard hash table format that GSNAP uses for genomes, with k-mers tabulated every 1 bp, in contrast to the default 3 bp used by GSNAP for large genomes. By default, the k-mer size for both the transcriptome and genome hash tables is 15 bp. Additionally, the transcriptome sequence is stored in a linear format, similar to that used by GSNAP for genomes, with two bits for each nucleotide. As with GSNAP, the high bits and low bits are stored in separate arrays, and the bits are organized in 128-bit words to allow efficient SIMD (Single Instruction, Multiple Data) operations to count and identify mismatches [29]. The genomic coordinates for each exon–exon boundary in each transcript are stored to facilitate the conversion of transcriptome alignments to genomic alignments.

The genome is also pre-processed to generate a hash table for GSNAP, with k-mers at 3-bp intervals, and the nucleotide sequence in a linear format. A recent improvement in GSNAP involves using regional suffix arrays, where each array covers a non-overlapping 65,536-bp region of the genome. These arrays borrow upon the idea of local indexing introduced by HISAT, although their implementation is based upon the Burrows-Wheeler Transform and FM index, rather than suffix arrays. This region size was chosen to be represented by a suffix array with 16-bit elements, enabling the interrogation of one, two, or three regions to search for introns up to 200,000 bp, as typically observed in human transcripts. These regional suffix arrays help identify introns at the ends of reads during genomic alignment. Since introns are not expected in transcriptome alignments, regional suffix arrays are not necessary for alignment to the transcriptome. Once a transcriptome and corresponding genome have been pre-processed, RNA-seq reads can be aligned to them. Even though alignment to the transcriptome avoids the issue of intron gaps, it must still be able to handle mismatches and potentially small indels. In our implementation, alignment to the transcriptome therefore involves three methods, each one tried in succession until a sufficiently good alignment is found. Sufficiency is determined by a threshold (default 5%) of mismatches or unaligned bases in the alignment between the read and the transcriptome. For paired-end reads, both ends must align to the same transcript in the same orientation for the paired-end alignment to be considered sufficient. One advantage of transcriptome alignment is that it removes the possibility of alignment gaps due to introns, and so alignments are generally expected to extend to the ends of the read, making it possible to have an accurate evaluation of the correctness of an alignment based on unaligned bases.

The first transcriptome alignment method is called exact search, which is intended to be fast and handle the vast majority of reads. In this method, the first and last k-mer in the read are looked up in the hash table. Since the hash table is constructed from transcriptome k-mers in their forward orientations (or sense direction) only, the first and last k-mers of the reverse complement of the read are also looked up. Each hash table lookup yields a list of positions in the transcriptome for the corresponding k-mer. We can therefore find exact alignments to the genome, or even alignments with mismatches between the end k-mers by taking the intersection of the two lists of positions, adjusting for their expected distance in the transcript from the distance between the k-mers in the read. For each candidate position in the intersection, we then compare the read nucleotides against the transcript nucleotides to determine the number of matches and mismatches. Intersections are performed efficiently using a modified version of a SIMD algorithm developed elsewhere [29].

The second method is an exhaustive “prevalence search” that identifies transcriptome positions with the highest number of matching k-mers from a read. It counts matches by looking up each read k-mer in a hash table, retrieving the list of transcriptome positions for that k-mer, and converting those positions into diagonals by subtracting the k-mer’s offset within the read; k-mers belonging to the same alignment share the same diagonal. This adjustment is performed separately for forward k-mers (assuming a sense-strand alignment) and for reverse-complement k-mers (assuming an antisense-strand alignment). All diagonals are then globally sorted, and a multi-way merge is used to aggregate and count k-mers per diagonal–transcriptome position pair. Our current implementation performs this global sort via a multi-way merge that leverages a SIMD-optimized merge primitive, though we are exploring more efficient strategies. The diagonals with the largest k-mer counts indicate the best candidate alignment segments. For reads containing an indel, the dominant diagonal typically corresponds to the longer aligned segment, while a nearby diagonal with fewer matches reflects the shorter segment across the indel. Thus, this exhaustive method yields one dominant segment, or a small set of segments in the indel case, that closely approximates the read’s alignment to a given transcript.

For paired-end reads, concordance is used as the next criterion. Both exact search and prevalence search, if needed, yield diagonals in the transcriptome for each read, which is likely to contain the transcript that is most likely to represent the origin of the paired-end read. Therefore, we can further constrain the set of candidate transcripts by looking for diagonals from the two ends of the paired-end read that match the same transcript. This procedure is linear in the total number of diagonals because exact and prevalence searches are designed to generate diagonals in ascending order, and therefore their corresponding transcripts are also ordered. Candidate pairs of diagonals are then refined by tallying their matches and mismatches to the corresponding transcript, and converting transcript alignments to genomic ones. For computing matches and mismatches, we use the representation of the transcriptome with two bits per nucleotide, but with the high and low bits represented in separate arrays of 128-bit registers that allow for SIMD operations.

Alignments from TGGA, whether they come from transcriptome-based or genome-based methods, are reported in genomic coordinates. Therefore, a continuous alignment to a transcript may correspond to one or more exons in genome coordinates. The conversion of transcript coordinates to genome coordinates is performed using the precomputed data structure that contains the genomic coordinates of each exon–exon boundary in each transcript. The conversion process is also able to incorporate indels in the transcript alignment into corresponding indels in the genomic alignment, taking care to handle indels near splice sites (i.e., within 3 bp) by finding the most plausible location for both the intron and the splice site. For example, a deletion near a splice site could potentially indicate a different splice site.

For single-end reads, if exact search yields one or more candidates, each candidate is refined by tallying its matches and mismatches against its transcript, and converted to a genomic alignment. If exact search does not yield any candidates, then prevalence search is used to identify potential candidates for processing. For paired-end reads, having both ends deriving from the same transcript is a sufficient condition for terminating the transcriptome alignment, whereas for single-end reads, a sufficiency criterion of 5% or fewer mismatches is used instead.

If a sufficient transcriptome alignment is not found, the third method is called extension search, which essentially follows the idea of seed-and-extend. In this method, seed k-mers are selected starting from both ends of the read, and are tried in succession inward, with each seed evaluated by its longest matching extension in the transcriptome. Seeds are extended iteratively in a breadth-first algorithm. When an extension ends for a given seed, we assume that a single-nucleotide mismatch was most likely responsible, and we therefore resume the exploration of seeds for that branch in the search by skipping one position in the read. In a breadth-first algorithm, each branch in the search corresponds to the same number of continuous segments that match perfectly to the transcriptome. Search terminates when the segment reaches the other end of the read, and therefore the breadth-first search attempts to find solutions with the fewest number of matching segments. Because the algorithm is greedy, it is not guaranteed to find the optimal solution in terms of matching segments. Nevertheless, this algorithm works well when the exact method fails, because it handles cases where a mismatch occurs in either the first or last k-mer of the read, or when a read contains an indel. The extension method will therefore also yield a series of segments that approximate the read alignment.

As before, approximate alignments yielded by the extension method are subsequently refined by procedures that identify the precise position of indels to minimize the number of mismatches. Resulting alignments from all methods are evaluated to see whether they satisfy the condition for ending the alignment process, or whether genomic alignment is called for. The condition for a sufficient paired-end read is finding a concordant alignment on the same transcript, whereas the condition for a single-end read is finding an alignment where 95% or more of the nucleotides on the read match those of a known transcript. Genomic alignment is especially necessary for reads corresponding to novel genes or isoforms that are not represented in the transcriptome.

If the transcriptome-based methods do not find an alignment that is considered sufficient, then genome-based methods are used. These genome-based methods are similar to those for the transcriptome, corresponding to exact, extension, and exhaustive approaches to produce approximate alignments. The exhaustive method organizes diagonals according to regions of the read where they are found, and then performs dynamic programming to find a chain of regions and corresponding diagonals. For paired-end reads, concordance is also used as a way to filter candidates before refining them, where concordance is determined by an expected genomic distance between the two ends of the paired-end read. If a concordant paired-end alignment cannot be found, a further search is made from the segments on each read to find any segments on the other read within a specified concordant distance, and an approximate alignment is constructed from those segments.

Approximate alignments are processed by procedures that both extend and refine the alignments. Extensions are found using both the genomic hash table and regional suffix arrays, which look for nucleotide sequences from the read that match the genome within a specified maximum indel or intron distance. Suffix arrays are searched using an exact method, which allows for no mismatches, or an exhaustive method using 4-mers, which does allow for mismatches. The extension methods can often yield multiple candidates for extending the alignment. These multiple candidates are handled differently depending on whether the unaligned end is on the inside or outside portion of a paired-end read (for a single-end read, both ends are considered to be on the outside). For the outside portion, if one candidate is clearly better in terms of a smaller intron length, that solution is used; otherwise the end is left unaligned. For the inside portion of a paired-end read, the candidates for one end are constrained by the genomic location of the other read. Among the allowable candidates, if one candidate is close enough to the other read, then that candidate is favored because we expect paired-end reads to come from a fragment with a relatively well-defined insert length. Procedures that refine approximate alignments use a splice model from MaxEnt to locate precise splice positions. Donor and acceptor splice junctions that appear at different positions in a read suggest the possibility of an indel, and such cases are handled by a procedure that solves for both a splice and nearby indel.

Fine-tuning transformer models

Traditional approaches to splice site prediction have relied on maximum entropy modeling [18], machine learning [30, 31], and, more recently, convolutional neural networks [13, 32, 33]. These methods, however, face challenges in capturing the complex sequence patterns underlying splicing mechanisms [34, 35]. With the advancement of deep learning, genomics research is being revolutionized, introducing powerful new tools for analyzing genetic data. Among these advances, transformer-based LLM have shown particular promise in decoding the complexities of splice site detection.

The transformer architecture is a groundbreaking neural network model for natural language processing (NLP) [9]. Unlike traditional sequential models, transformers use self-attention mechanisms to process input sequences in parallel, capturing long-range dependencies between words. This architecture includes encoder and decoder components, employs multi-head attention, and position-wise feed-forward networks. The transformer achieved state-of-the-art performance on various NLP tasks, offering scalability and parallelization advantages. It marked a significant shift in the field, making it a foundational model in NLP and machine learning. In this study, we explored the application of several cutting-edge transformer-based DNA foundation models, including DNABERT [10], DNABERT2 [11], and the Nucleotide Transformer [12], to the challenging task of splice site prediction.

DNABERT is a BERT-based [36] transformer model pre-trained on DNA sequences from the human reference genome. The sequences are tokenized into overlapping k-mers of lengths 3, 4, 5, or 6, with special tokens added at the beginning and end of each sequence, with maximum sequence length of 512 bases. DNABERT was pre-trained for 120,000 steps using a batch size of 2,000. During the first 100,000 steps, approximately 15% of the k-mers in each sequence were masked, as they could be trivially inferred from the neighboring k-mers. In the final 20,000 steps, the masking rate was increased to 20% [10].

Building on the foundation of DNABERT, DNABERT2 introduces several key innovations to further improve genomic sequence modeling, using the same transformer encoder architecture but replacing k-mer tokenization with Byte Pair Encoding (BPE), a statistics-based data compression algorithm that constructs tokens by iteratively merging the most frequent co-occurring genome segments in the corpus. Additionally, DNABERT2 incorporates several recent advances in deep learning to enhance the model’s efficiency and performance: (i) Attention with Linear Biases (ALiBi) [37] replaces learned positional embeddings, allowing the model to overcome input length limitations. (ii) FlashAttention [38], which speeds up the attention mechanism. (iii) Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [39], used during fine-tuning to enable parameter-efficient training. DNABERT2 was pre-trained on a multi-species genome dataset containing 32.49 billion nucleotide bases, covering genomes from 135 species [11].

Complementing these advances, the Nucleotide Transformer represents a collection of transformer-based DNA language models that have learned general nucleotide sequence representations from 6kb unannotated genomic sequences, considering sequences of nucleotides as sentences and non-overlapping 6-mers as words. Pre-trained NT uses varying parameter sizes and datasets. (i) a 500 million parameter model pre-trained on sequences extracted from the human reference genome, (ii) a 500 million and (iii) a 2.5 billion parameter model both pre-trained on 3,202 genetically diverse human genomes, and (iv) a 2.5 billion parameter model based on 850 species including 11 model organisms. We chose the latest 2.5B parameters multi-species model to be tuned on classifying the splice junction sites since that model showed the highest normalized mean of MCC performance among the NT models for this specific downstream task [12].

For tuning DNABERT 6-mer, DNABERT2, and the 2.5B multi-species NT, we opted to generate our own splice-site datasets to ensure transparency, customizability, reusability, and randomness. Each dataset consisted of three files: train.csv for model tuning, dev.csv for evaluation during tuning, and test.csv for final validation, ensuring no data leakage between the files. Each of these CSV files contained (sequence, label) data points. Sequences were DNA sequences of specific lengths (90, 150, 200, 400) in which the splice donor or acceptor sites were in the center of the sequence. Labels indicated whether the sequence was donor (label = 0), acceptor (label = 1), or none (label = 2). The negative data points were generated by (i) masking the genome around the annotated splice donor and acceptor sites, and (ii) mimicking splice junctions with varying intron sizes and splicing signals. The datasets were generated from (i) GRCh38 NCBI RefSeq assembly GCF_000001405.40 using transcripts with biotypes mRNA, lnc_RNA and transcript, (ii) GRCh38 GENCODE release 44 using all the annotated transcripts regardless of the biotype and (iii) malaria ASM276v2. Furthermore, we tuned DNABERT using splice junctions with a window size of 150 from Arabidopsis thaliana, Zea mays, Xenopus tropicalis, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Drosophila melanogaster, Caenorhabditis elegans, Rattus norvegicus, Mus musculus, Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium vivax, Plasmodium berghei, Plasmodium knowlesi. The generated fine-tuning datasets are named as RefSeq90, RefSeq150, RefSeq200, RefSeq400, GENCODE150, GENCODE400 and MultiSpecies150.

The TSJS component performs two passes over the given BAM file. In the first pass, it identifies splice junctions in spliced reads, creating labeled sequences for processing by the transformer model. In the second pass, it applies the transformer-based splice junction score to achieve two objectives: (i) soft-clipping novel splice junctions with low transformer score or low Phred quality, and (ii) adjusting MAPQ values for multi-mapped reads containing splice junctions.

The TSJS component eliminates low-probability splice junctions using soft-clipping. It parses the Compact Idiosyncratic Gapped Alignment Report (CIGAR) string for each spliced alignment to identify introns (N) and enumerate splice junctions. For every junction, DeepSAP uses the DNABERT MS150 model to predict donor and acceptor splice-site probabilities, and computes the left and right anchor support (number of aligned read bases flanking the junction) as well as the mean Phred quality of the anchor bases. Novel junctions are flagged as low-confidence (“bad”) and soft-clipped when any of the following thresholds are not met: minimum DNABERT MS150 junction score (min_novel_score = 50), minimum anchor length (min_overhang_bases_novel = 14), minimum mean anchor Phred (min_average_qual = 20; Baruzzo reads are provided as FASTA and therefore lack base-quality scores), and minimum intron length (min_novel_intron_size = 20). Novel introns shorter than min_novel_intron_size are treated as implausible splice events and are converted from an intron operation (N) to a deletion-like event during scoring and penalization.

To resolve multi-mapping through score-based pairing and MAPQ reassignment in reads with multiple candidate alignments, the TSJS component re-ranks hits using a read-level score that combines (i) alignment evidence from the CIGAR and (ii) transformer-derived junction confidence. Base alignment scoring uses match_reward = 12 per matched base (M/=), mismatch_penalty = 30 per mismatch (X), and gap penalties of gap_open_penalty = 20 plus gap_ext_penalty = 5 per base for indels (I/D). Junction evidence is incorporated by aggregating per-junction DNABERT scores, applying a penalty to novel junctions (novel_junction_penalty = 30) and a reward to annotated junctions (annotated_junction_reward = 99). Benchmarking study with simulated datasets

We conducted a large-scale RNA-seq alignment benchmarking study using simulated datasets from two origins. First, datasets were obtained from Baruzzo et al. [7], which include simulated RNA-seq datasets from human and malaria. For both organisms, the data spans three complexity levels (T1, T2, and T3), each represented by three independent replicates. The least complex dataset, T1, contains a low polymorphism rate (0.001 substitution, 0.0001 indels, with a 0.005 sequencing error rate) followed by T2, which contains a moderate polymorphism rate (0.005 substitution, 0.002 indels, and 0.01 sequencing error rate). Finally, T3 is the most complex dataset in the study with polymorphism rate (0.03 substitution, 0.005 indels, and 0.02 sequencing error rate). Each complexity level has three replicates. Secondly, we also simulated datasets using the SimBA tools suite [20] with similar levels of complexities of the previous datasets and using the same genomes and annotations. The twelve datasets were then aligned using state-of-the-art RNA-seq aligners including DeepSAP with TGGA GSNAP v2024–06–24 and DNABERT MS150, Illumina’s DRAGENTM v4.0.3, novoSplice v0.8.4 [21], STAR v2.7.10a [3], HISAT2 v2.2.1 [4], and Subjunc v2.0.1 [22]. Full details of aligners’ command-line arguments are listed in Additional file 2: Table S7. To assess the performance of the RNA-seq aligners, we compared the alignment output for each aligner with the known ground truth provided by the simulators. For Baruzzo datasets, we used the original methods to calculate different levels of benchmarking metrics as introduced in the Baruzzo study: (i) Base-level: every nucleotide of every read was scored as aligned correctly when its CIGAR code and genomic coordinate matched the simulator’s ground truth uniquely; otherwise it was counted as mis-aligned, unaligned, or ambiguously aligned. Precision was calculated as (correctly aligned bases)/(uniquely aligned bases) and Recall as (correctly aligned bases)/(total bases). By comparing the BAM file with the ground truth for specific CIGAR operations, the Baruzzo benchmarking script also obtained insertion, deletion and skip (intronic ‘N’) precision and recall. (ii) Read-level: a read was considered correct when it aligned uniquely and at least one of its bases occupied the true genomic position; all other uniquely mapped reads were classified as incorrect, while multi-mappers were counted as ambiguously aligned. Read-level precision and recall were computed analogously to the base-level formulas. (iii) Junction-level: for each read crossing an intron, the donor and acceptor coordinates were extracted; a junction was scored correct only if both ends matched the truth set. To rigorously compare the performance of different RNA-seq aligners, we performed a statistical analysis on the aggregated results from each Baruzzo dataset by analyzing benchmarking results on pair-level; each dataset consists of 10 million pairs of reads. For each aligner and performance metric, we first calculated an overall binomial proportion by summing the total number of successful outcomes (k) and the total number of events (n) across all reads. For example, for insertion recall, k is the total number of correctly identified insertions (true positives, TP), and n is the total number of true insertions present in the ground truth (TP + false negatives, FN). For insertion precision, while k remains the number of true-positive insertions, n becomes the total number of insertions called by the aligner (TP + false positives, FP).

We calculated a 95% confidence interval for each proportion using the Jeffreys interval method, which is known for its robust performance, especially for proportions approaching 0 or 1. The statistical significance of the performance difference between DeepSAP and every other aligner was determined using a two-sample Z-test for proportions. The standard error (SE) for each aligner’s score was first derived from its 95% confidence interval. The Z-score was then computed based on the difference between the two aligners’ proportions (μ1, μ2) and their respective standard errors (SE1, SE2) using the formula:

Two-sided p-values were then derived from the Z-scores to assess significance. For data visualization, we generated violin plots to illustrate the distribution of per-pair performance scores. These plots were overlaid with box plots to indicate the median and interquartile range, and a distinct marker was used to highlight the mean performance for each aligner.

To increase statistical power in the Baruzzo benchmark, we also created 500 paired bootstrap replicates for every data set. A single evaluation on the full 20 million reads yields only one point estimate per metric, whereas bootstrapping converts that single value into an empirical distribution, from which we can quantify uncertainty and perform robust statistical tests.

Bootstrap replicate generation procedure was performed as follows:

Per-Pair Benchmarking

Each original BAM file was first benchmarked on a read-pair basis using the Baruzzo script to obtain a comprehensive table of per-pair metrics (base-, read-, and junction-level).

Replicate Generation (500 × 10M pairs)

From these per-pair tables, we drew 500 bootstrap subsets of 10 million read pairs with replacement, using a seeded random number generator for reproducibility (numpy.default_rng(seed = 777 + replicate_id)). Crucially, the same integer index of pairs was re-used for every aligner in a given dataset, creating perfectly paired replicates.

Per-Replicate Metric Calculation

For each of the 500 bootstrap samples, summary metrics were calculated. Absolute counts (e.g., “bases_aligned_correct”) were summed across the 10 million pairs, and performance ratios (e.g., precision) were then re-calculated from these aggregate sums to yield a single, robust metric value for that replicate.

Confidence Intervals

For every aligner and metric, we reported the mean of the 500 replicate values and the percentile-bootstrap 95% confidence interval, defined as CI95 = [Q0.025, Q0.975].

Paired Significance Testing (DeepSAP versus other aligners)

To translate the 500 bootstrap resamples into a formal hypothesis test, we treated each bootstrap replicate as a matched-pair observation. For every metric, we formed a vector of paired differences, Δi=xiDeepSAP-xiOther aligner for i=1…500, which captures the algorithmic differences after all sampling noise cancels out. The resulting distribution of differences (Δ) was used to derive: (i) a non-parametric 95% confidence interval on the difference (the 2.5% and 97.5% empirical quantiles of Δ), and (ii) an exact, two-sided empirical p-value. Raw p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (α=0.05).

For the datasets generated using SimBA, we employed the accompanying benchmarking script named BenchCT to analyze two levels of events: mapping-level and junction-level. Additionally, we only took into account Sequence Alignment/Map (SAM) records that are uniquely mapped with a MAPQ score of equal to or greater than 5. The BenchCT output contains true-positive, false-positive and false-negative numbers for aligning the reads correctly in addition to identifying splice junctions. Detection of complex splice junctions

To closely evaluate how well the RNA-seq aligners in our benchmark measured splice junction expression compared to the ground truth, we analyzed the Baruzzo human and malaria simulated datasets. Splice junction counts were calculated by processing the sorted aligned BAM files from each aligner using featureCounts [40] with the following options: (-J -O -Q 0 -t exon -g gene_id -p -M), which enabled the inclusion of overlapping features and multi-mapped reads, as well as proper handling of paired-end data.

Detected junctions were then compared with the ground truth using scatterplots generated in Python with the matplotlib library. For aligner-to-truth comparisons, we applied the Theil–Sen robust linear estimator using the theilslopes function from SciPy with default parameters. For pairwise aligner comparisons, we used the Pearson correlation coefficient via SciPy’s pearsonr function to assess similarity in junction detection profiles, reporting both correlation values and associated p-values. These analyses revealed each aligner’s ability to recover complex splicing patterns, with particular emphasis on the challenging T3 dataset, which includes simulated indels and complex splice junctions.

To complement our analysis on simulated datasets, and to identify differences between aligners, we extended our evaluation to real RNA-seq data using samples from a glioma study by Siddaway et al [23]. However, in the absence of a definitive ground truth for splice junctions in real datasets, DeepSAP’s splice junction detection performance was assessed by comparing its results with those of other aligners using concordance scatterplots. To estimate the abundance of splice junctions, FeatureCounts [40] was employed on each aligner’s sorted BAM files, with the following options: (-J -O -Q 0 -t exon -g gene_id -p -M) allowing both overlapping features and multi-mapped reads. The concordance of known and novel splice junctions across aligners was then assessed by visualizing them in scatterplots.

These scatterplots (Fig. 5A) highlight DeepSAP’s concordance with other aligners in splice junction detection. Points near the diagonal represent junctions with similar counts to those reported by DeepSAP for the same site. Points along the line x = 1 correspond to junctions uniquely identified by DeepSAP, while points along y = 1 represent junctions detected exclusively by other aligners. Counts are shown on a log scale using log(count + 1) to accommodate a wide range of values while avoiding issues with zero counts.

To ensure reproducibility, the entire analysis was managed within a Snakemake [41] pipeline, streamlining the workflow across aligners and handling all alignment and analysis steps within a reproducible framework.

Detection of insertions and deletions

To assess the ability of DeepSAP to detect indels in RNA-seq data, we compared its performance against several widely used RNA-seq aligners using the simulated datasets from Baruzzo et al. [7]. We used all aligners from the benchmarking study with their respective default settings to ensure compatibility with indel detection and accurate splice junction mapping.

To identify indels from each aligner’s output BAM file, we implemented a standard variant calling and indel normalization workflow. Aligned BAM files were first converted into pileup format using samtools mpileup, subsequently, small insertions and deletions were detected using VarScan’s mpileup2indel function with default parameters. To enable consistent and fair comparison of variant calls across aligners, we applied bcftools norm with parameters (-m -any) to left-align and normalize multi-allelic indels, followed by bcftools sort to ensure proper Variant Call Format (VCF) record ordering. Finally, we used the bcftools isec option to compute intersections between aligner-derived VCFs and the ground truth provided by Baruzzo et al. [7]. This pipeline ensured alignment-independent variant comparison by enforcing consistent indel representation across aligners. Performance was quantified using true positives, false positives, and false negatives, with percentages reported for each metric to reflect each tool’s ability to accurately recover indels and minimize erroneous calls.
