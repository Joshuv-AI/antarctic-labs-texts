On convergence structures in infinite graphs
Paulo Magalhães Junior, Renan M. Mezabarba, Rodrigo S. Monteiro

Abstract
. It is well known that a graph admits a natural closure operator defined on its vertex set, which also corresponds to a pretopology. In this paper, we describe the classical pretopology on a graph in terms of nets, with the aim of relating combinatorial properties to convergence properties.

During the decades of 1920–1940, graph theory was largely focused on finite objects,

In 1936, König

Later, in 1964, Halin

and high connectivity. But earlier, in 1931, Hans Freudenthal

Topological methods are widely used in infinite combinatorics, since classical combinatorial approaches and finite algorithms are generally not sufficient to deal with the complexity of infinite graphs. In a graph, or in its end space, it is possible to consider several natural topologies, depending on the structural aspects one wishes to emphasize, see

In many situations, it is difficult to work with these topologies, even though they are natural. However, from a categorical perspective, an even more natural structure in a graph emerges: a
convergence

In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, many results in analysis and topology were formulated in terms of sequences. However, it soon became clear that in spaces that are not first-countable, sequences are not sufficient to capture all convergence phenomena.
The concept of a net was first introduced by E. H. Moore and Herman L. Smith
[

Proof.

4.
Remarks and future works

Here we have only considered convergence at the vertices of a graph. However, there are many interesting combinatorial properties concerning edges, so it is also important to define convergence for edges. A natural approach to this is to use the line graph. The
line
graph of
G
, denoted by
L(G)
, is the graph with
E(G)
as vertex set, in which two vertices are adjacent if and only if they have an end in common in
G
. The concept of thorn graphs was proposed by Gutman and different applications have been studied by many others, see
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
and
[
12
]
. Could this convergence be a way to characterize edge-connectivity and to deal with edge decompositions through convergence systems?

A next step would be to consider a convergence on the end space of a graph
G
that is not the usual one but still retains some known properties. If
X\subseteq V(G)
is finite and
\omega\in\Omega(G)
, there is a unique component of
G-X
that contains a tail of every ray in
\omega

C(X,\omega)
.
If
C
is any component of
G-X
, we write
\Omega(X,\omega)
for the set of ends
\omega^{\prime}
of
G
with
C(X,\omega^{\prime})=C(X,\omega)
. The collection of all sets
\Omega(X,\omega)
with
X\subseteq V
finite and
\omega
an arbitrary end form a basis for the usual topology on
\Omega(G)
. Then a net
\varphi\in\textsc{Nets}(\Omega(G))
converges usually to and end
\omega
if for every finite set
F\subseteq V(G)
there is
d\in\hbox{dom}(\varphi)
such that
\varphi[d^{\uparrow}]\subseteq\Omega(F,\omega)

\Omega(G)

\omega\in\Omega(G)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
we denote by

the set of ends that admit a representative for which there are at least

\omega
. we say that a net
\varphi\in\textsc{Nets}(\Omega(G))
converges to
\omega\in\Omega(G)
if for every

there is

such that

. Thus, a net of ends converges if it is possible to obtain a sufficiently large connection from some point onwards. Notice that this convergence is strictly stronger than the usual one and is also topological. Moreover, is topological, it remains zero-dimensional and, unlike the usual one, it is first countable. In this case, a graph being locally finite does not imply compactness. Which known results in the literature still hold, and which new ones can we obtain? Moreover, can the existing results in the literature be proved more simply using nets?
