Temperature-sensitive liquid embolic agent transarterial chemoembolization versus drug-eluting bead transarterial chemoembolization for BCLC Stage B/C hepatocellular carcinoma: a multicenter real-world study and spatial transcriptomics profiling (CHANCE 2515)
Authors: Qingyun Xie, Ying Yang, Sinan Xie, Xiaojuan Yang, Fengwei Gao, Weili Qi, Hu Liu, Yuanjun Liu, Jiran Deng, Xianguo Liu, Yi Zhou, Xin Zhao, Kangyi Jiang, Tianyang Mao, Xiuyong Liao, Ruihong Dai, Yinghao Lyu, Yunshi Cai, Kunlin Xie, Hong Wu, Tian Lan, Chang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransarterial chemoembolization (TACE) remains a cornerstone therapy for intermediate-to-advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC); however, the optimal embolic platform remains uncertain. This multicenter, retrospective, real-world study (Clinical trial registration number: ChiCTR2500113198) conducted across China compared a novel temperature-sensitive liquid embolic agent, TempSLE-TACE (T-TACE), with conventional drug-eluting bead TACE (D-TACE) in 328 patients with Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage B/C HCC. Following inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), T-TACE achieved significantly superior objective response rates (ORRs) compared with D-TACE according to both RECIST 1.1 criteria (54.11% vs. 27.78%, P < 0.001) and mRECIST criteria (73.56% vs. 54.93%, P = 0.002). T-TACE was additionally associated with significantly prolonged progression-free survival (median PFS: 12.0 vs. 9.0 months; HR = 0.67, P = 0.001) and overall survival (median OS, 24.0 vs. 15.0 months; HR = 0.49, P < 0.001). Moreover, T-TACE demonstrated a favorable safety profile, with lower incidences of hepatic and gastrointestinal toxicities, including any-grade alanine aminotransferase elevation and hyperbilirubinemia. Subgroup analyses further demonstrated consistent OS, PFS, and ORR benefits across major clinical subgroups, with effect sizes remaining significantly favorable in high-risk populations, including advanced portal vein tumor thrombosis type Vp4, baseline AFP > 1000 ng/mL, and PIVKA-II > 2000 mAU/mL. Exploratory histo-molecular and spatial transcriptomic analyses suggested that T-TACE may promote immune microenvironment remodeling through enhanced Th17-cell infiltration and CD8⁺ T-cell activation, whereas incomplete embolization after D-TACE was more frequently associated with residual intermediate-state tumor cells and an immunosuppressive microenvironment. Collectively, these findings provide preliminary evidence supporting T-TACE as a promising real-world therapeutic strategy for intermediate-to-advanced HCC.Subject terms: Gastrointestinal cancer, Cancer therapy

Introduction

Liver cancer is the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with HCC accounting for approximately 90% of primary liver malignancies.1 For patients with Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) stage 0 and A HCC, liver resection and transplantation are uniformly recommended as curative treatment options by major international guidelines.2–5 In contrast, the substantial heterogeneity in tumor burden and underlying hepatic function among patients with BCLC stage B (intermediate) and stage C (advanced) HCC renders therapeutic decision-making highly complex in real-world clinical practice.6,7 TACE remains the standard treatment for intermediate-stage HCC5 and is also widely employed in the advanced-stage setting as part of combination strategies incorporating systemic therapy. The therapeutic efficacy of TACE is critically dependent on the properties of the embolic agent. An ideal embolic material should achieve rapid and complete occlusion of tumor-feeding arteries while enabling sustained local delivery of chemotherapeutic agents.8 However, the lipophilic nature of lipiodol may permit systemic diffusion and toxicity,9 whereas the non-uniform structure and biodegradability of gelatin sponge compromise mid- to long-term embolic stability.8 Although drug-eluting bead TACE (DEB-TACE; hereinafter D-TACE) overcomes certain limitations of conventional TACE, the restricted microsphere size range confines embolization predominantly to higher-order tumor vasculature, potentially leading to non-target vessel embolization and iatrogenic injury to adjacent healthy tissue.10Accordingly, there is a critical clinical need for embolic materials capable of achieving precise drug delivery, durable distal tumor vessel occlusion, and real-time intraprocedural visualization.

TempSLE, a temperature-sensitive liquid embolic agent characterized by a unique sol-to-gel phase-transition mechanism, has previously demonstrated clinical utility in cerebrovascular interventions, including arteriovenous malformations and aneurysms,11 benign prostatic hyperplasia treatment,12 and hemostatic applications. Unlike conventional microsphere-based embolic agents, TempSLE maintains low viscosity at room temperature, facilitating microcatheter delivery, and undergoes rapid thermal gelation at physiological temperatures, thereby enabling embolization of distal tumor microvasculature below 100 μm. In addition, its intrinsic radiopacity permits real-time intraprocedural visualization, whereas its polymer matrix supports sustained local drug release (Fig.1a–d and Supplementary Materials).

To evaluate tumor control achieved through vascular embolization, we established an orthotopic mouse model of hepatocellular carcinoma (Hepa1-6) and performed a series of experiments (Fig. 1e). As illustrated in Fig. 1f, a 1 mL insulin syringe was used for precise left portal vein puncture during the embolization procedure. Upon completion of embolization, gross examination of the harvested livers revealed marked ischemic areas on the surface of the left liver lobe in both the TempSLE (Fig. 1g) and DEBs (Fig. 1h) groups, with target tumors effectively occluded. To dynamically monitor tumor growth inhibition, in vivo fluorescence imaging was performed before intervention (Day 0) and at Day 1 and Day 5 post-embolization (Fig. 1i). Compared with the negative control (NC) group, both the TempSLE and DEBs groups exhibited substantially reduced fluorescence intensity over time, indicating decreased tumor burden. Histopathological analysis (Fig. 1j) demonstrated satisfactory distribution of embolic agents within tumor vasculature at both Day 1 and Day 5 post-intervention. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed necrotic changes in tumor morphology. Ki-67 immunohistochemical staining and quantitative analysis (Fig. 1l) further confirmed that tumor cell proliferative activity was significantly suppressed in both treatment groups compared with the NC group, where active proliferation was observed. Quantitative tracking of tumor volume changes (Fig. 1k) corroborated these findings, showing that tumor growth was effectively inhibited in both the TempSLE and DEBs groups, in contrast to rapid tumor progression in the control group. Collectively, these results demonstrate that both TempSLE and DEBs can effectively disrupt tumor blood supply and significantly suppress the growth and proliferation of orthotopic liver tumors.

Nevertheless, clinical evidence regarding the application of TempSLE in HCC remains limited, and direct comparative data evaluating the long-term efficacy and safety of TempSLE-TACE (T-TACE) versus established D-TACE in real-world settings are lacking.13 Given the distinct “liquid penetration–phase transition–controlled release” mechanism demonstrated in our preclinical model, we hypothesized that T-TACE may provide superior therapeutic benefit through more complete devascularization and subsequent modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment. To investigate this hypothesis, we conducted this national multicenter, retrospective, real-world study integrating intention-to-treat (ITT) clinical outcomes with post-treatment histopathologic analyses and exploratory spatial transcriptomic profiling to characterize the biological features associated with differential treatment responses between these two TACE modalities.

Results

Patient selection and cohort construction

The patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 2a, b. Among 546 patients initially screened, 218 were excluded according to predefined criteria, including secondary malignancies, receipt of potentially confounding locoregional therapies, incomplete key clinical data, and non-HCC-related death within 30 days after the procedure. The remaining 328 patients were included in the intention-to-treat (ITT) cohort, including 41 patients categorized as early loss to follow-up. All patients underwent epirubicin-based TACE and were assigned to either the TempSLE-TACE (T-TACE) group (n = 117) or the drug-eluting bead TACE (D-TACE) group (n = 211). Comparative analyses of the final study cohort excluding patients with early loss to follow-up (n = 287) were additionally performed as sensitivity analyses and are presented in Supplementary Figs. 1, 2 and Supplementary Tables 1–2. Systemic therapy consisted of either monotherapy or combination regimens as described in the Methods section. Following inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), the weighted ITT cohort comprised 113.91 patients in the T-TACE group and 210.44 patients in the D-TACE group.

Baseline patient characteristics

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the unadjusted cohort are summarized in Table 1. Before statistical adjustment, significant imbalances were observed between the T-TACE and D-TACE groups across several baseline covariates (P < 0.05 and absolute standardized mean difference [SMD] > 0.1). Specifically, significant intergroup differences were identified in maximum tumor diameter (P = 0.007), number of HCC lesions (P = 0.015), proportion of patients exceeding the “Up-to-Seven” criteria (P = 0.015), and distribution of combined systemic therapy regimens (P = 0.034) (Fig. 2c, d, blue dots), suggesting substantial baseline selection bias. After IPTW adjustment, these imbalances were effectively minimized, and no statistically significant differences remained across recorded baseline covariates. Absolute SMD values for nearly all variables included in the propensity model were reduced below the predefined threshold of 0.1, indicating satisfactory covariate balance (Fig. 2c, red dots). Consistent findings were observed in the sensitivity analysis using 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM), which generated well-balanced cohorts comprising 93 patients per group without significant baseline differences, further supporting the robustness of the adjustment strategy (Table 1). Detailed characteristics of the final sensitivity analysis cohort are provided in Suppl. Table 1.

Tumor response

Treatment efficacy was evaluated according to both RECIST 1.1 and mRECIST criteria following IPTW and PSM adjustment (Fig. 3b–e). According to RECIST 1.1, the ITT T-TACE cohort demonstrated a significantly higher objective response rate (ORR) than the D-TACE cohort after both IPTW adjustment (54.11% vs. 27.78%, P < 0.001) and PSM adjustment (46.24% vs. 29.03%, P = 0.015). This superiority was primarily attributable to a markedly increased proportion of partial responses (PRs) in the T-TACE group (IPTW: 54.11% vs. 27.06%, P < 0.001; PSM: 46.24% vs. 29.03%, P = 0.015). Disease control rates (DCRs) were also significantly higher in the IPTW-adjusted T-TACE cohort (84.02% vs. 72.79%, P = 0.026).

Evaluation according to mRECIST criteria demonstrated similar findings. In the IPTW-adjusted cohort, T-TACE achieved significantly higher ORR (73.56% vs. 54.93%, P = 0.002) and Partial response (PR) rates (69.36% vs. 49.93%, P = 0.002) compared with D-TACE. Although statistical significance was attenuated after 1:1 PSM, T-TACE continued to demonstrate numerically higher ORR (65.59% vs. 59.14%, P = 0.364) and PR rates (63.44% vs. 56.99%, P = 0.369), whereas DCRs were identical between groups (82.80% in both cohorts). Collectively, these findings suggest that T-TACE provides superior antitumor activity and promotes more extensive treatment-related tumor necrosis compared with conventional D-TACE. Analyses of tumor response within the pre-matched cohort are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Individual patient-level response dynamics in the ITT cohort are further illustrated in Fig. 3 through waterfall plots depicting target lesion size changes according to RECIST 1.1 and mRECIST, as well as swimmer plots summarizing treatment sequencing, TACE session frequency, and response duration over time. Detailed inter-observer agreement analyses for RECIST 1.1 and mRECIST assessments, demonstrating substantial agreement between reviewers, are provided in Suppl. Table 4.

Treatment safety

The safety profiles of T-TACE and D-TACE were systematically evaluated using adverse events (AEs) graded according to CTCAE version 5.0, with analyses performed following both IPTW and PSM adjustment (Table 1). After IPTW adjustment, the D-TACE group demonstrated significantly higher incidences of any-grade diarrhea (33.17% vs. 21.37%, P = 0.045), alanine aminotransferase elevation (P < 0.001), and hyperbilirubinemia (P < 0.001), as well as higher rates of grade ≥ 3 hyperbilirubinemia (P = 0.012) and hypoalbuminemia (P < 0.001). Within the PSM cohort, the T-TACE group demonstrated an incidence of any-grade leukopenia (31.18% vs. 16.13%, P = 0.025). Conversely, the D-TACE group continued to exhibit significantly higher rates of any-grade alanine aminotransferase elevation (41.94% vs. 23.66%, P = 0.012), any-grade hyperbilirubinemia (43.01% vs. 17.20%, P < 0.001), and grade ≥3 severe hypoalbuminemia (9.68% vs. 0.00%, P = 0.006). Overall, both treatment modalities demonstrated manageable safety profiles; however, T-TACE was associated with comparatively more favorable hepatic and gastrointestinal toxicity profiles. Safety outcomes for the pre-matched cohort are summarized in Suppl. Table 5.

Long-term survival outcomes and subgroup analysis

In addition to superior tumor response outcomes, T-TACE was associated with significant improvements in long-term survival compared with D-TACE (Fig. 4c–h). In the unadjusted cohort, median overall survival (OS) was significantly prolonged in the T-TACE group relative to the D-TACE group (21 vs. 15 months; HR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.44–0.75, P < 0.001; Fig. 4c). This survival advantage remained stable following adjustment for baseline confounding variables. After IPTW adjustment, median OS was 24 months in the T-TACE cohort compared with 15 months in the D-TACE cohort (HR = 0.49, P < 0.001). Similar findings were observed in the PSM cohort, in which median OS was 20 months versus 16 months, respectively (HR = 0.58, P = 0.001; Fig. 4d, e). Progression-free survival (PFS) analyses demonstrated consistent superiority of T-TACE across all analytical cohorts. Median PFS was significantly prolonged in the unadjusted cohort (12 vs. 9 months; HR = 0.71, P = 0.005; Fig. 4f), IPTW-adjusted cohort (12 vs. 9 months; HR = 0.67, P = 0.001), and PSM cohort (11 vs. 9 months; HR = 0.68, P = 0.010; Fig. 4g, h).

COX analysis

Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards analyses were performed in the ITT cohort before and after IPTW and PSM adjustment. To identify baseline prognostic factors, variables with P < 0.1 in the univariate Cox analysis were included in the multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. As shown in Fig. 4i–j, macrovascular invasion, including PVTT and hepatic vein tumor thrombosis (HVTT), together with impaired hepatic reserve reflected by advanced Child-Pugh classification and cirrhosis, represented the strongest independent adverse prognostic factors for survival outcomes following TACE. In contrast, combined systemic therapy incorporating targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors was independently associated with improved OS and PFS. Spatial transcriptomics profiling

To further explore the biological characteristics of intermediate-state tumor cells in the D-TACE group and to compare immune-cell infiltration patterns between treatment groups, exploratory 10x Visium HD spatial transcriptomic profiling was performed using one representative pathologic specimen from each group that passed quality-control criteria. Given the constraints associated with clinical specimen quality control, our analytical results are presented as preliminary hypotheses.16 cellular clusters were found in D-TACE and 15 in T-TACE, grouped into six cell types: B cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, hepatocytes, myeloid cells, and T/NK cells (Suppl. Fig. 13a, b; Fig. 6a–d). Analyses focused on necrotic and surviving tumor regions, and T/NK cells near tumors (Suppl. Fig. 13c, d). To investigate potential differences in tumor necrosis-associated biological processes between embolization modalities, transcriptomic analyses comparing necrotic and surviving tumor regions were performed. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses suggested that T-TACE-associated necrosis showed enrichment in ferroptosis-related pathways, while D-TACE-associated death was linked to oxidative phosphorylation. Direct comparison indicated higher ferroptosis signatures in T-TACE, suggesting potentially greater tumoricidal activity (Suppl. Fig. 13e–h; Supplementary Fig. 14a, b).

We next compared transcriptomic features between intermediate-state and surviving tumor regions within the D-TACE specimen. GO and KEGG analyses suggested that intermediate-state tumor cells exhibited altered metabolic and oxidative stress-related processes under conditions of incomplete embolization together with increased expression of HCC-associated genes (Supplementary Fig. 14c–e). In addition, several signaling pathways associated with tumor survival and treatment resistance appeared relatively enriched, including the PI3K/Akt/mTOR,14–17 MAPK, and FoxO signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 14d–f). These findings may suggest that intermediate-state tumor cells emerging after incomplete embolization possess biological features associated with enhanced survival and therapeutic resistance.

We subsequently compared the immune microenvironmental characteristics between the T-TACE and D-TACE specimens. GO enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes within T/NK-cell populations demonstrated enrichment in extracellular matrix organization, cytokine-mediated signaling pathways, cellular response processes, and regulation of apoptosis (Fig. 6e). KEGG analyses further identified enrichment of the PI3K-Akt, Relaxin, ECM-receptor interaction, MAPK, IL-17, TNF, and Toll-like receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 6f). Previous studies have shown that activation of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway promotes the transition of T cells toward a hyperproliferative and hypermetabolic effector phenotype.18 Relaxin signaling has been reported to upregulate IFN-γ and IL-12 expression, thereby enhancing cytotoxic T-cell activity and reducing immunosuppressive signaling.19 In addition, activation of the ECM-receptor interaction pathway is closely associated with immune activation,20 whereas MAPK signaling plays a central role in T-cell development, differentiation, and immune responses.21 IL-17 and TNF are key immune cytokines involved in HCC-associated immune regulation and tolerance, while Toll-like receptors participate in both innate and adaptive immune activation.22 Collectively, these transcriptomic findings suggested that T/NK-cell populations in the T-TACE specimen exhibited features consistent with a relatively immune-activated state (Fig. 6f).

Finally, T/NK-cell populations were further classified into nine subclusters using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis (Fig. 6g, h). Compared with the D-TACE specimen, subcluster 3 demonstrated increased abundance in the T-TACE specimen (Fig. 6g, h) and was annotated as a Th17-cell population based on spatial transcriptomic analysis (Supplementary Fig. 15). Th17 cells represent a subset of CD4⁺ helper T lymphocytes capable of promoting CD8+ cytotoxic T-cell activation through IL-17-mediated signaling. The increased infiltration of Th17-associated cells observed in the T-TACE specimen therefore suggested enhanced anti-tumor immune activity within the T-TACE-associated tumor microenvironment.23

Discussion

Conventional embolic agents in HCC interventions have inherent limitations. Lipiodol’s lipophilic properties can cause diffusion beyond the target and systemic toxicity, while gelatin sponge particles’ biodegradability and irregular structure affect embolic durability. Drug-eluting microspheres are limited by particle size, leading to proximal embolization and risks of non-target vessel occlusion and tissue injury.24–26 Radiopaque DEBs were subsequently developed to improve intraprocedural visualization and post-embolization assessment by overcoming the limited radiographic visibility of conventional microspheres. Nevertheless, despite their enhanced radiopacity, these agents remain inherently restricted by their spherical geometry, which primarily limits embolization to proximal or intermediate-order feeding arteries and may not achieve the distal microvascular penetration required for complete tumor devascularization.27,28 Similarly, calibrated microspheres were designed to target specific vessel diameters; however, in clinical practice, they may encounter unpredictable intravascular aggregation and “bridging” effects within tortuous tumor vessels, potentially resulting in incomplete embolization or premature proximal stasis.29,30 The TempSLE platform, developed to address limitations via temperature-responsive phase-transition properties, maintains low viscosity at room temperature for smooth microcatheter delivery and rapidly gels at physiological temperatures for distal tumor embolization. Its intrinsic radiopacity enables real-time visualization without contrast agents, while its polymer matrix encapsulates chemotherapeutics for sustained local release up to 9 days.13 These features may enhance embolization completeness and procedural precision and safety.

T-TACE was associated with significantly improved ORR, PFS, and OS compared with D-TACE according to both RECIST 1.1 and mRECIST criteria, with findings remaining consistent after IPTW and PSM. Given the relatively balanced distribution of combined systemic therapy regimens between groups after adjustment, these findings suggest that the observed therapeutic differences were not solely attributable to systemic treatment heterogeneity. Following IPTW adjustment, the ORR in the T-TACE group was 54.11% compared with 27.78% in the D-TACE group according to RECIST 1.1, and 73.56% versus 54.93%, respectively, according to mRECIST. However, the superior ORR observed with T-TACE did not directly translate into higher conversion-to-resection rates. This discrepancy likely reflects the complexity of real-world multidisciplinary treatment patterns. Most patients in the T-TACE cohort were treated at municipal interventional centers rather than tertiary teaching hospitals (Suppl. Table 11), where formal multidisciplinary collaboration between interventional radiology and hepatobiliary surgery services may be less standardized. Consequently, some patients who achieved sufficient downstaging for potential resection did not ultimately undergo surgery because of broader clinical considerations or patient preference. Furthermore, complete response (CR) rates according to mRECIST remained modest and did not differ significantly between groups (4.20% vs. 4.99%, P = 0.752). This finding was likely influenced by the high baseline tumor burden of the study population, as more than 80% of patients exceeded the “Up-to-Seven” criteria and fewer than 50% received TACE combined with systemic therapy. Compared with prospective clinical trials31,32 conducted under highly selective enrollment conditions, including the study by Cheng et al.28 reporting a CR rate of 7% and the CHANCE2005/CARES-005 trial29 reporting a CR rate of 16%, real-world cohorts inherently demonstrate greater heterogeneity in treatment adherence and regimen consistency. Within this context, the CR rates observed in the present study remain clinically reasonable. Subgroup analyses further demonstrated the stability and broad applicability of the therapeutic benefit associated with T-TACE. Improvements in survival and tumor response remained directionally consistent across all major clinical subgroups without significant interaction effects. Notably, effect sizes remained strongly favorable for T-TACE in high-risk populations characterized by advanced tumor burden, including patients with PVTT type Vp4, baseline AFP > 1000 ng/mL, or PIVKA-II > 2000 mAU/mL, suggesting that the antitumor advantages of T-TACE may persist even in biologically aggressive disease settings.

The integration of TACE with systemic therapy has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for intermediate-to-advanced HCC. In the phase III EMERALD-1 trial,33 the combination of TACE with durvalumab and bevacizumab achieved a median PFS of 15.0 months. Similarly, Cheng et al.31 reported a median PFS of 17.9 months in a phase II study, whereas Teng et al. demonstrated a median PFS of 10.8 months in the CHANCE2005/CARES-005 trial.32 In comparison, the T-TACE strategy evaluated in the present study achieved a median PFS of 13.0 months and a median OS of 26.0 months in the IPTW-adjusted cohort, yielding survival outcomes broadly comparable to those reported in recent combination trials. Furthermore, the ORRs observed in the T-TACE cohort were similarly comparable to those reported in these contemporary studies.31–33 Notably, the present real-world cohort demonstrated high consistency in treatment sequencing, as all patients underwent systemic therapy initiation only after TACE and recovery of liver function, typically within 2–14 days after the procedure. This sequential “TACE-first, then systemic therapy” strategy parallels the treatment paradigms adopted in the aforementioned clinical trials.31–33 Several biological mechanisms may support this sequence. TACE induces local tumor devascularization and ischemic necrosis, resulting in the release of tumor-associated antigens, promotion of immunogenic cell death, and upregulation of PD-L1 and VEGF expression. Subsequent administration of anti-angiogenic agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors may therefore target this post-embolization microenvironment, potentially reversing immunosuppression and enhancing antitumor immune responses. Nevertheless, the alternative strategy of administering systemic therapy before TACE also possesses a strong theoretical basis. According to the vessel normalization hypothesis, upfront anti-angiogenic therapy may remodel abnormal tumor vasculature, alleviate tumor hypoxia, and improve the efficacy and drug-delivery efficiency of subsequent TACE procedures. In addition, pre-TACE systemic therapy may reduce tumor burden, thereby permitting lower embolic doses during subsequent TACE and improving preservation of non-tumorous hepatic parenchyma. In this real-world setting, landmark analyses showed that the PFS benefit attenuated over time. This was likely driven by the highly individualized subsequent systemic modifications or crossover therapies among long-term survivors. This therapeutic heterogeneity inevitably diluted direct PFS variance in a retrospective context, while the independent, long-term OS advantage of T-TACE remained robust and continuous.

The present study also systematically compared the safety profiles of T-TACE and D-TACE using both IPTW and PSM analyses. Although the overall incidence of adverse events (AEs) was generally comparable between groups, D-TACE was associated with significantly higher rates of several specific toxicities, including diarrhea (33.17% vs. 21.37%, P = 0.045), alanine aminotransferase elevation (46.53% vs. 21.74%, P < 0.001), and hyperbilirubinemia (45.57% vs. 17.01%, P < 0.001). In addition, the D-TACE group demonstrated higher incidences of grade ≥ 3 hyperbilirubinemia (6.25% vs. 0.00%, P = 0.012) and hypoalbuminemia (8.67% vs. 0.82%, P < 0.001). These differences may reflect the distinct embolic characteristics of the two materials. Because D-TACE primarily induces proximal embolization owing to microsphere size limitations, it may increase the risk of non-target hepatic ischemia. In contrast, TempSLE enables distal microvascular embolization through temperature-triggered phase transition, while its intrinsic radiopacity permits real-time procedural visualization and catheter repositioning, thereby improving embolization precision and potentially reducing injury to non-tumorous liver tissue. These characteristics may plausibly contribute to the lower incidence of hepatic and gastrointestinal toxicity observed in the T-TACE cohort. Nevertheless, these safety findings should be interpreted cautiously. Given the retrospective multicenter design of the study, complete capture of all treatment-related adverse events was not feasible, and the incidence of minor or transient complications may have been underestimated.

Histopathologic analyses of conversion-resection specimens from the TempSLE cohort revealed two prominent features: complete occlusion of arterial lumina within tumor-feeding arterioles surrounding HCC lesions and deep intratumoral penetration with sustained retention of embolic material within tumor nests. Collectively, these findings suggest a dual antitumor mechanism involving both vascular blockade and direct sequestration of embolic material within the tumor bed, potentially limiting tumor-cell dissemination into hepatic sinusoids and reducing micrometastatic potential. In contrast, D-TACE specimens more frequently demonstrated incomplete arterial occlusion, likely reflecting the non-uniform distribution and embolization characteristics of microspheres. Correspondingly, H&E-stained sections demonstrated patchy necrosis after D-TACE, whereas TempSLE-treated tumors exhibited more homogeneous coagulative necrosis. These histopathologic differences closely paralleled the degree of arterial occlusion achieved by each modality and may partly explain the superior tumor response and long-term outcomes observed in the T-TACE cohort.

Compared with D-TACE-treated tumors, T-TACE specimens demonstrated increased infiltration of CD8⁺ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, reduced PD-L1 expression, increased Caspase-3-positive apoptotic tumor cells, and lower Ki-67 proliferation indices. These findings suggest that TempSLE may attenuate tumor-associated immune tolerance through induction of a hypoxic microenvironment associated with enhanced tumor-antigen release and activation of antigen-presenting cells. In addition, multiplex immunofluorescence analyses demonstrated increased CD8⁺ T-cell accumulation and enhanced spatial co-localization at the tumor-necrosis interface in the T-TACE group. This spatial distribution pattern may reflect TempSLE-associated ischemic stress and immunogenic cell death accompanied by the release of damage-associated molecular patterns, including high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), calreticulin, and ATP.34 Within the hypoxic–ischemic microenvironment induced by T-TACE, residual tumor cells may undergo metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis, resulting in lactic acid accumulation and microenvironmental acidification. Such metabolic alterations may suppress regulatory T-cell differentiation through mTORC1-related mechanisms while enhancing CD8⁺ T-cell oxidative phosphorylation through the AMPK–PGC1α axis,35 thereby potentially promoting sustained antitumor immune activation. In contrast, incomplete embolization after D-TACE may produce heterogeneous microenvironmental conditions that limit the durability of immune activation.

To further explore the biological features associated with T-TACE efficacy and characterize intermediate-state tumor cells identified in D-TACE specimens, exploratory spatial transcriptomic profiling was performed. Four principal differences were observed between the two modalities. First, necrotic signatures differed substantially, with T-TACE-associated tumor regions demonstrating enrichment of ferroptosis-related pathways, whereas D-TACE-associated regions demonstrated enhanced oxidative phosphorylation signatures. Second, intermediate-state tumor cells identified in the D-TACE specimen demonstrated partial apoptotic features while potentially retaining greater malignant potential and relative resistance to embolization. Third, T/NK-cell populations in the T-TACE specimen demonstrated transcriptional features suggestive of enhanced anti-apoptotic capacity and cytokine secretion. Fourth, Th17-cell populations appeared relatively enriched within the T/NK-cell compartment of the T-TACE specimen and may contribute to enhanced CD8⁺ T-cell-mediated antitumor activity. Collectively, these exploratory spatial transcriptomic analyses provide cell-level insights while preserving tissue architecture and may help characterize biological differences underlying the distinct treatment responses observed between T-TACE and D-TACE.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the retrospective, non-randomized design and individualized treatment allocation may have introduced residual confounding from unmeasured variables, including operator familiarity, institutional preferences, device availability, and patient socioeconomic factors, which could not be fully corrected through stabilized IPTW or PSM. In addition, the retrospective collection of adverse-event data may have resulted in underestimation of low-grade toxicities. Second, the external validity of the findings may be limited because the study cohort was derived exclusively from high-volume Chinese tertiary centers, predominantly consisted of patients with HBV-related HCC, and utilized systemic therapy regimens reflective of Chinese clinical practice, which differ from Western treatment paradigms where HCV- and NASH-related HCC predominate. Moreover, TempSLE currently holds only Class III medical device registration approval in China and has not yet received CE marking or FDA clearance, limiting the immediate applicability of these findings to Western clinical settings. Third, the concurrent use of systemic therapy may partially confound differences attributable specifically to locoregional treatment effects. Fourth, given the limited sample size for spatial transcriptomic profiling, the findings regarding molecular mechanisms should be interpreted as exploratory, serving primarily to generate hypotheses or identify potential pathways for further validation. Finally, although the present findings support the efficacy of the “TACE-first, then systemic therapy” strategy, alternative sequencing approaches were not evaluated in this study. Future prospective, multicenter randomized controlled trials are therefore needed to define the optimal sequencing strategy for combination therapies intermediate-to-advanced HCC.

This multicenter, real-world study demonstrated that T-TACE using the novel TempSLE embolic agent was associated with superior clinical efficacy and a more favorable safety profile compared with D-TACE in patients with BCLC stage B or C HCC. After rigorous adjustment for baseline confounding factors, T-TACE achieved significantly improved tumor response and overall survival outcomes. Exploratory pathologic and spatial transcriptomic analyses further suggested that these therapeutic advantages may be associated with more complete distal microvascular embolization, ferroptosis-related tumor injury, and modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment. Collectively, these findings provide preliminary clinical and mechanistic evidence supporting T-TACE as a promising and effective locoregional treatment strategy for intermediate-to-advanced HCC.

Materials and methods

Orthotopic liver tumor model

To establish the biological rationale for the potential clinical advantages of TempSLE, a murine Hepa 1-6 orthotopic liver tumor model was generated in C57BL/6 mice. Following successful tumor establishment (Day 0), animals were randomly assigned to the negative control (NC), DEB, or TempSLE treatment groups. Under direct visualization, the portal vein was carefully punctured using a 1-mL insulin syringe for administration of the designated embolic materials. Successful embolization was macroscopically confirmed by ischemic discoloration of the left hepatic lobe (Fig. 1f–h). Tumor progression was serially monitored using an in vivo fluorescence imaging system (IVIS). Radiant efficiency was quantified on Days 0, 1, and 5 to evaluate viable tumor burden and growth kinetics (Fig. 1i–k). Tumor tissues were harvested on Days 1 and 5 after treatment for H&E staining to evaluate tissue morphology and embolic distribution, as well as immunohistochemical staining for Ki-67 to assess cellular proliferative activity using 4-μm tissue sections (Fig. 1j–l). Quantitative data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3 per group), and statistical comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA (Fig. 1l).

Study design

This multicenter retrospective cohort study included patients with BCLC stage B or C HCC who underwent treatment with either T-TACE or D-TACE at seven tertiary medical centers in China (Supplementary Table 11). The study protocol and data collection procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of West China Hospital, Sichuan University. The IRB approval covered the entire data collection and follow-up period. The study was registered at www.chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2500113198). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to enrollment.

Patient selection

Eligible patients were required to meet all of the following criteria: confirmed HCC diagnosis according to the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) and American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidelines36,37 based on histopathologic or clinical/imaging criteria; classification as BCLC stage B (intermediate) or stage C (advanced); presence of at least one measurable lesion according to mRECIST38 and RECIST 1.139 criteria; initiation of first-line TACE treatment (either T-TACE or D-TACE) between January 2020 and January 2023 with completion of at least two cycles of the assigned regimen; and availability of complete baseline clinical and radiographic data as specified in Supplementary Methods. All patient data from the seven participating tertiary medical centers were collected consecutively.

Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: active malignancy other than HCC, with the exception of cured basal cell carcinoma of the skin or cervical carcinoma in situ; receipt of other locoregional therapies, including radiotherapy or ablation, prior to the first scheduled efficacy assessment (6–8 weeks after initial TACE), which could confound target-lesion evaluation; absence of key baseline data required for outcome assessment; death within 90 days after the procedure clearly adjudicated as unrelated to HCC progression or TACE-related complications; or unavailable survival status without intermediary follow-up records (“Unclassifiable Survival”). This study was reported in accordance with the STROCSS guidelines.40

Interventions

TACE procedures were performed by five qualified teams, each conducting over 400 annually, following the Chinese College of Interventionalists’ algorithm. For T-TACE, TempSLE (Beijing Guanhe Medical Technology Co., Ltd.; model GH-TempSLE-5 mL) loaded with 60–80 mg epirubicin was administered via right femoral artery access using the Seldinger technique. Tumor vascularity was evaluated with DSA (GE Innova 4100), and superselective microcatheterization was performed. The mixture was infused at 0.5 mL/min until flow stasis, with stable microcatheter positioning to avoid non-target embolization. Arterioportal shunts were managed with gelatin sponge particles and lipiodol if needed. Catheters were flushed with heparinized saline to prevent thrombosis. Embolization endpoints were confirmed by angiographic stasis, and postprocedural DSA verified devascularization. This protocol integrated hemodynamic assessment and adaptive vascular management (Fig. 7). D-TACE followed the same framework. (details provided in Supplementary Methods).

All systemic therapies were initiated after recovery of liver function following the initial TACE procedure and were determined by multidisciplinary team (MDT) consensus according to the CSCO Clinical Practice Guidelines for Primary Liver Cancer.41 For patients receiving TACE combined with PD-1 inhibitors, TKIs, or PD-1 plus TKI therapy, agents including pembrolizumab, camrelizumab, sintilimab, tislelizumab, and ramucirumab were selected according to guideline recommendations and drug availability in China. All PD-1 inhibitors were administered at standard doses and schedules, with anti-PD-1 therapy initiated at least 5 days after the initial TACE procedure. TKIs, including sorafenib, lenvatinib, regorafenib, and apatinib, were also administered at standard doses, with all oral agents initiated within 2 weeks after TACE. Systemic therapies were continued until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. Detailed protocols regarding treatment administration, dose modification, and discontinuation are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Assessment of tumor response

Preprocedural evaluation included contrast-enhanced liver CT or MRI within 1 week before each TACE session. Post-treatment follow-up was performed according to institutional protocols, with an initial radiographic assessment conducted 6–8 weeks after TACE, followed by serial evaluations every 8–12 weeks during the first 2 years and continuing until data censoring on November 1, 2025. Tumor response was evaluated using contrast-enhanced CT or MRI according to RECIST 1.139 and mRECIST38 criteria. All imaging studies were independently reviewed by two experienced radiologists blinded to clinical outcomes. Response categories included Complete Response(CR), PR, Stable Disease (SD), and Progressive Disease (PD). ORR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving CR or PR, whereas DCR was defined as the proportion achieving CR, PR, or SD. Tumor response represented the best overall response observed during the entire study period. All patients underwent evaluable imaging at protocol-specified time points, and missing assessments were handled using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method.

Variables and outcome measures

To preserve the ITT principle, no patients were excluded from the primary survival analysis because of early loss to follow-up. Collected variables included demographic, clinical, and oncologic characteristics. Demographic and clinical variables comprised age, sex, BMI, hepatitis status, ECOG performance status, liver cirrhosis,42 ascites grade,43 clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH),44 Charlson comorbidity index (CCI),45 and Child-Pugh score. Tumor-related variables included AFP, PIVKA-II levels, maximum tumor diameter, BCLC5 and CNLC46 stage, PVTT classification,47 HVTT classification,48 (defined in Supplementary Methods.) number of HCC lesions, status beyond the “up-to-seven” criteria,49 receipt of combined systemic therapy, and conversion to resection (defined in Supplementary Methods.). The primary endpoints were OS, defined as the interval from the initial TACE procedure to death or last clinical follow-up, and PFS, defined as the interval to radiographic progression according to RECIST 1.1 or mRECIST criteria, or death. Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of treatment effects between T-TACE and D-TACE across clinically relevant baseline covariates for ORR, PFS, and OS. Interaction testing was performed using logistic regression or Cox proportional hazards models to determine whether treatment effects differed significantly among subgroups. A P-interaction value > 0.05 was considered indicative of no significant interaction. Secondary endpoints included ORR, DCR, and adverse events graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0 (NCI-CTCAE v5.0).50

Histo-molecular analysis

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor-normal interface specimens from propensity score-matched patients (T-TACE/D-TACE, n = 5 each); underwent multimodal histo-molecular analyses. H&E staining, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and multiplex immunofluorescence (MIF) were performed to assess embolic-vascular congruence, necrosis patterns, and the tumor immune microenvironment. Detailed staining protocols, antibody information, and quantitative analyses are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Spatial gene expression profiling was performed using the Visium HD Spatial Gene Expression platform (10x Genomics), which enables single-cell-level spatial resolution using contiguous 2 × 2 µm barcoded capture areas. RNA quality of FFPE tissue was assessed using the DV200 metric, with a threshold of > 30% required for inclusion. Preliminary spatial transcriptomic profiling was performed on two independent samples, with DV200 values of 53% and 59%, respectively. According to the Visium HD FFPE Tissue Preparation Handbook (CG000684), 5 μm FFPE sections were mounted on histology slides, dried at 42 °C for 3 h, dehydrated overnight, and incubated at 60 °C for 30 min before deparaffinization, H&E staining, and imaging. Probe hybridization, ligation, slide processing, probe release, extension, and library preparation were performed according to the Visium HD Spatial Gene Expression Reagent Kits User Guide (CG000685). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using paired-end reads.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). Continuous and categorical variables were compared using appropriate tests, including Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test. To minimize confounding, a propensity score-based IPTW approach was applied, with covariate balance evaluated using SMDs, where an absolute SMD < 0.1 indicated adequate balance. As a sensitivity analysis, 1:1 PSM was additionally performed. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using log-rank tests, with hazard ratios (HRs) calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression models. Treatment efficacy and AEs were compared using appropriate statistical methods. Additional details regarding the propensity score model, weighting procedures, balance diagnostics, interaction analyses, and landmark analyses are provided in Supplementary Methods.
