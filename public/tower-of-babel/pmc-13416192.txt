Decision‐Making About Abortion in an Abortion‐Restricted Setting: A Qualitative Study of Young Women in Namibia
Authors: Ndinohokwe F Mukerenge, Julia M Tjandja, Filippine N Nakakuwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsUnsafe abortion is one of the leading causes of maternal death in low‐ and middle‐income countries. In Namibia, restrictive abortion legislation limits young women's reproductive autonomy and contributes to unsafe abortion practices. Despite high teenage pregnancy rates and documented barriers to reproductive health services, little is known about factors influencing abortion decision‐making among young women in this restrictive context. This study explored abortion decision‐making among young women admitted with abortion‐related complications at a referral hospital in Kavango East region, Namibia.MethodsWe employed a qualitative exploratory descriptive design. In‐depth individual interviews were conducted with 15 young women (aged 16–24 years) admitted with abortion‐related complications at a referral hospital in Kavango East region, Namibia, during 2022–2023. Participants were purposively sampled for diversity in age, educational status, and pregnancy history. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's (2006) six‐phase approach. The study received ethical approval from the University of Namibia Research Ethics Committee.ResultsParticipants' ages ranged from 16 to 24 years; 10/15 were currently attending school. and 8/15 had previous pregnancies. Abortion decision‐making was highly influenced by personal and interpersonal factors operating within a restrictive legal and social context. Key themes included: (1) fear of rejection by parents and community; (2) lack of social and financial support; (3) desire to pursue education; and (4) expectations to bear children within marriage. Young women demonstrated an understanding of abortion risks yet felt they had limited alternatives. Participants expressed mixed views on abortion legalization, with concerns about reproductive autonomy, social discrimination, and future fertility.ConclusionsYoung women's abortion decisions are shaped by complex internal factors (personal aspirations, fears) and external factors (social stigma, lack of support, restrictive policies). Understanding these factors is critical for developing youth‐centered reproductive health policies and services. Our findings support the need for policy reform to make safe abortion services available and accessible to young women in Namibia, potentially reducing unsafe abortion‐related morbidity and mortality in this vulnerable population.Keywords: abortion decision‐making, Namibia, qualitative research, restrictive abortion laws, unsafe abortion, young women

Introduction

Between 2015 and 2019, an estimated 73 million induced abortions occurred globally each year, representing approximately 39 per 1000 women aged 15–49 [1]. Approximately 45% of these abortions (35 million annually) are classified as unsafe, performed by individuals lacking necessary skills, in environments not meeting minimal medical standards, or both [2]. Unsafe abortion contributes to an estimated 4.7%–13.2% of maternal deaths globally, with the highest burden in low‐ and middle‐income countries where legal restrictions limit access to safe services [3]. The WHO (2022) defines unsafe abortion as a procedure of terminating a pregnancy performed by persons lacking the necessary skills, in an environment not conforming with minimal medical standards, or both.

In Namibia, abortion is restricted by the Abortion and Sterilization Act No. 2 of 1975, inherited from South Africa at independence. This Act prescribes three conditions under which abortion can be performed by a medical practitioner only: (1) serious threat to the woman's physical or mental health; (2) serious risk of fetal abnormality; or (3) pregnancy resulting from rape or incest. All procedures must be performed by a medical practitioner, with no specified gestational age cut‐off. Abortions performed outside these conditions are illegal and constitute a criminal offense, potentially punishable by imprisonment [4].

From a person‐centered perspective, young women's unmet need for safe abortion services reflects broader failures in reproductive autonomy, bodily integrity, and access to comprehensive sexual and reproductive health care. When young women cannot access safe abortion services, they face increased risks of morbidity, mortality, educational disruption, and economic hardship, undermining their life trajectories and wellbeing [5]; WHO, 2022). Understanding young women's lived experiences and decision‐making processes is essential for developing policies and services that respect their autonomy and protect their health.

In Namibia, where approximately 90% of the population identifies as Christian, religious beliefs significantly influence abortion attitudes and contribute to stigma, shaping both individual decision‐making and community responses to abortion‐seeking behavior [6]. Young women in Namibia face particular vulnerabilities regarding unsafe abortion and reproductive health access. Despite an overall contraceptive prevalence of approximately 58% among women of reproductive age, adolescents and young women experience significant barriers to contraceptive access, including provider bias, lack of confidentiality, limited method choice, and geographic disparities [7, 8]. The most common contraceptive methods are injectables (65.1%) and oral contraceptive pills (20.6%), both requiring consistent service access and having typical‐use failure rates higher than those of long‐acting reversible contraceptives [9]. The teenage pregnancy rate in Namibia remains high, with approximately 20% of girls aged 15‐19 having experienced pregnancy [10]. Recent data show an increase in teenage pregnancies from 13,552 in 2019 to 14,983 in 2020, a 10.6% increase attributed in part to COVID‐19 pandemic lockdown restrictions and school closures (Namibia MoHSS, 2021). Many unintended pregnancies among young women end in unsafe abortion due to restrictive legislation, social stigma, and fear of parental or community rejection. However, limited research has explored young women's lived experiences of abortion decision‐making in this restrictive context, particularly the complex interplay of individual, interpersonal, and structural factors that shape their choices.

According to the Namibia Ministry of Health and Social Services [11], approximately 7000 cases of spontaneous abortions (miscarriages) were recorded in health facilities annually. However, data on intentional abortion are scarce, as many procedures occur outside formal health systems due to legal restrictions. The actual number of induced abortions, particularly unsafe procedures, is likely substantially higher than reported figures. Understanding the factors that influence young women's abortion decisions is essential for developing appropriate policies, services, and interventions that respect their autonomy and protect their health. This study addresses this gap by exploring abortion decision‐making among young women admitted with abortion‐related complications at a referral hospital in Namibia. We employed a socio‐ecological framework [12] to examine factors at multiple levels: individual (personal beliefs, aspirations, knowledge), interpersonal (partner, family, peers), community (stigma, social norms), and structural (legal restrictions, health system barriers).

Methods

Study Design

We employed a qualitative exploratory descriptive design to explore abortion decision‐making among young women in Namibia. This approach was appropriate for gaining an in‐depth understanding of participants' lived experiences, perspectives, and the contextual factors influencing their decisions [13].

Interview Guide Development

The semi‐structured interview guide was developed through a multi‐step process informed by: (1) a literature review of abortion decision‐making factors in similar restrictive contexts; (2) the socio‐ecological framework guiding the study; and (3) consultation with reproductive health experts, clinicians, and counselors working with young women in Namibia. The guide explored five key domains: (a) pregnancy discovery and initial reactions; (b) decision‐making process and timeline; (c) factors influencing the decision (individual, interpersonal, structural); (d) information sources and support systems; and (e) perceptions of abortion legislation and access. The guide was pilot‐tested with two young women (not included in the final sample) admitted with similar conditions and refined based on their feedback and the research team's reflections.

Study Setting

The study was conducted at a tertiary referral hospital in a North‐Eastern region of Namibia. This hospital serves as the main referral center for the region and admits young women with abortion‐related complications from across the region.

Sampling and Participants

We used purposive sampling to recruit young women aged 15–24 years admitted to the gynecology ward with abortion‐related complications (spontaneous or induced abortion). Purposive sampling allowed us to select information‐rich cases and ensure diversity across key characteristics relevant to abortion decision‐making. We specifically sampled for variation in: (1) age (adolescents 15–19 years vs. young adults 20–24 years); (2) educational status (currently in school vs. out of school); (3) pregnancy history (first pregnancy vs. previous pregnancies); and (4) type of abortion complication (spontaneous vs. suspected induced abortion, based on clinical presentation). Potential participants were identified through daily ward rounds in collaboration with nursing staff. The second author (JMT) approached eligible young women, explained the study purpose and procedures, and invited them to participate. Those who expressed interest received detailed information about confidentiality, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw.

We conducted 15 in‐depth interviews over a period of 3 months (June–August 2022). We reached informational adequacy—the point at which sufficient data were collected to address our research questions and new interviews yielded minimal new insights regarding the main themes [14]. We acknowledge that our sample size is modest and may not represent full theoretical saturation across all potential dimensions of the phenomenon, as some methodologists suggest 40‐60 interviews may be needed for comprehensive saturation [15]. However, given the sensitive nature of the topic, the vulnerability of the population, and the rich, in‐depth nature of the data collected, this sample provided sufficient information power to meet our exploratory study objectives [14].

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Namibia Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. SON 45/2022). All participants provided written informed consent. For participants under 18 years, assent was obtained from the minor and consent from a parent/guardian as per the Child Care and Protection Act No. 3 of 2015 [16]. Confidentiality was maintained throughout the study, and participants were assured that their participation would not affect their medical care. Interviews were conducted in private spaces within the hospital to ensure privacy. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequences.

Data Collection

Data were collected through in‐depth individual face‐to‐face semi‐structured interviews conducted by the second author (JMT) in the hospital ward. Interviews were conducted in English or Rukwangali (local language), based on participant preference, and lasted between 30 and 60 min. All interviews were audio‐recorded with participant permission. The interview guide covered topics including pregnancy discovery, decision‐making process, influencing factors, information sources, support systems, and perceptions of abortion legislation. Field notes were taken during and immediately after interviews to capture non‐verbal cues, contextual observations, and initial impressions.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke's [17] six‐phase approach: (1) familiarization with data; (2) generating initial codes; (3) searching for themes; (4) reviewing themes; (5) defining and naming themes; and (6) producing the report. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcriber familiar with both English and Rukwangali. The first author verified all transcripts against audio recordings for accuracy. Transcripts in Rukwangali were translated into English, with back‐translation performed by an independent bilingual researcher to ensure accuracy.

We developed the codebook using a hybrid deductive–inductive approach. Initial deductive codes were derived from the socio‐ecological framework and literature review (e.g., ‘parental influence,’ ‘educational aspirations,’ ‘legal barriers,’ ‘partner involvement’). Through iterative, line‐by‐line reading of transcripts, we inductively identified additional codes emerging from the data (e.g., ‘marriage expectations,’ ‘fear of community gossip,’ ‘information gaps,’ ‘self‐blame’).

Two researchers (NFM and JMT) independently coded three transcripts, compared codes, discussed discrepancies, and refined code definitions and inclusion criteria. This process was repeated with three additional transcripts until inter‐coder agreement was achieved (Cohen's kappa > 0.80). The remaining transcripts were divided between the two coders, with regular meetings to discuss emerging patterns and ensure consistency. The final codebook included 47 codes organized into 6 major themes and 18 sub‐themes. Coded data were organized using Atlas. ti (version 9.0). We used constant comparison to identify patterns, variations, and relationships within and across cases. Themes were reviewed against the entire dataset to ensure internal coherence and external distinctiveness. The research team met regularly to discuss interpretations, consider alternative explanations, and refine themes.

Rigor and Trustworthiness

We employed several strategies to enhance trustworthiness [18]. Credibility was enhanced through prolonged engagement with participants, peer debriefing among the research team, and member checking. Following initial analysis and theme development, we offered participants the opportunity to review their interview transcripts for accuracy and provide feedback. Contact information was available for seven participants who had consented to follow‐up. Three participants reviewed their transcripts and confirmed the accuracy of their statements, with no requests for changes. This process occurred after initial coding but before finalizing themes, allowing participants to validate our interpretations. Transferability was supported by providing thick descriptions of the context, participants, and findings. Dependability was ensured through maintaining an audit trail of all analytical decisions. Confirmability was enhanced through reflexive journaling and team discussions to examine researcher biases and assumptions.

Thematic Findings

Our analysis identified six major themes that illuminate the complex factors influencing young women's abortion decision‐making in Namibia's restrictive context. These themes operate at individual, interpersonal, community, and structural levels, consistent with our socio‐ecological framework.

Theme 1: Fear of Social Consequences

Fear of social consequences emerged as the most powerful driver of abortion decision‐making, operating primarily through anticipated parental rejection and community stigma. This fear reflects the intersection of economic dependence, cultural expectations, and social surveillance that characterizes young women's lives in this context.

Parental Rejection and Economic Vulnerability

Young women described intense anxiety about disappointing parents, losing financial support, and facing punishment or expulsion from home. This fear was particularly acute among those still in school and economically dependent on parents. As one 17‐year‐old student explained:

I was so scared my parents would find out. My father always said if I get pregnant while in school, he will chase me away and stop paying my school fees. Where would I go? I have no money, no job. I felt I had no choice but to try to end it [the pregnancy].

(P03, 17 years, G2P0A1, in school, suspected induced abortion)

This narrative illustrates how young women's economic dependence on parents fundamentally constrains their reproductive autonomy. The threat of losing educational and financial support creates a coercive environment in which abortion, even through unsafe means, appears as the only viable option to maintain parental support and continue education.

Several participants echoed this sentiment, with 9 of 10 school‐attending participants citing fear of parental reaction as a primary factor in their decision‐making. The two participants who had disclosed pregnancies to parents before complications occurred both described initial anger and disappointment, validating others' fears:

My mother cried and shouted at me. She said I have brought shame to the family and ruined my future. She didn't speak to me for 3 days.

(P07, 19 years, G1P0, in school, spontaneous abortion)

Community Stigma and Social Surveillance

Beyond parental rejection, participants described pervasive community stigma and social surveillance that intensified their desire for secrecy. In small, close‐knit communities, pregnancy outside marriage—particularly among students—becomes public knowledge quickly and carries lasting social consequences:

Everyone will know. They will gossip about you, call you names, say you are spoiled. Even after you finish school, people remember and judge you. Some girls who got pregnant, people still talk about them years later.

(P11, 22 years, G2P1, out of school, suspected induced abortion)

This quote reveals how social stigma operates not only in the immediate term but also creates lasting reputational damage that can affect young women's future marriage prospects, employment opportunities, and social standing. The permanence of stigma in participants' communities amplifies the perceived necessity of concealing pregnancy through abortion.

Eight of 15 participants specifically mentioned fear of ‘gossip’ or ‘what people will say’ as influencing their decisions, indicating the powerful role of community surveillance in shaping reproductive behavior. This finding aligns with the socio‐ecological framework's community‐level factors, demonstrating how social norms and collective attitudes constrain individual choices.

Theme 2: Educational Aspirations and Future Plans

The desire to pursue education emerged as a central factor in abortion decision‐making, particularly among participants currently in school. Young women viewed pregnancy as incompatible with educational goals and perceived abortion as necessary to protect their futures.

Participants described education as their pathway to economic independence, better employment, and improved life circumstances. Pregnancy represented a direct threat to these aspirations:

I want to finish my studies and get a good job. If I have a baby now, everything will stop. I will have to drop out, stay at home. My dreams will be finished. I had to do something.

(P12, 22 years, G1P0, in school tertiary, suspected induced abortion)

School is my only chance to have a better life. My parents are struggling. If I finish school, I can help them and help myself. A baby now would destroy everything I've worked for.

(P05, 19 years, G2P1, in school Grade 12, suspected induced abortion)

These narratives reveal how young women's abortion decisions are forward‐looking, motivated by aspirations for social mobility and economic security. The choice to seek abortion reflects not irresponsibility but rather rational calculation about life opportunities in contexts where single motherhood severely limits educational and economic prospects.

Notably, all 10 participants currently in school cited educational concerns as a primary factor, compared to only 1 of 5 out‐of‐school participants. This pattern suggests that educational enrollment intensifies the perceived incompatibility between pregnancy and future plans, making abortion appear more necessary to protect educational investments.

Theme 3: Partner and Relationship Dynamics

Partner involvement, or lack thereof, significantly influenced abortion decision‐making. Participants described varied partner responses, ranging from supportive to abandonment, with most experiencing limited partner support during decision‐making and in dealing with complications.

Partner Denial and Abandonment

The most common pattern was the partner's denial of responsibility and subsequent abandonment. Participants described how partners initially involved in the relationship distanced themselves upon learning of the pregnancy:

When I told him I'm pregnant, he said it's not his. He said I must have been with other men. Then he stopped answering my calls. I was alone.

(P02, 17 years, G1P0, in school, suspected induced abortion)

He said he's not ready for a baby. He said I should ‘take care of it’ but he didn't help me or give me money. After I came to the hospital, he hasn't visited me once.

(P08, 20 years, G2P1, out of school, suspected induced abortion)

This pattern of partner denial and abandonment left young women to navigate pregnancy and abortion decisions alone, without emotional or financial support. The absence of partner support compounded other pressures (parental, economic, educational) and increased young women's sense of desperation.

Pressure to Terminate

Some participants described explicit pressure from partners to terminate pregnancies. This pressure took various forms, including threats to end the relationship, refusal of financial support for childcare, and provision of abortion methods or contacts:

He said if I keep the baby, he will leave me. He said he's still young, not ready to be a father. He gave me some tablets and told me to take them.

(P06, 19 years, G3P1A1, out of school cohabiting, suspected induced abortion)

These accounts illustrate how power imbalances in relationships can coerce young women toward abortion decisions they might not otherwise make. While some participants expressed ambivalence about terminating pregnancies, partner pressure, combined with economic dependence and fear of abandonment, constrained their decision‐making autonomy.

Theme 4: Limited Knowledge and Information Gaps

Participants demonstrated significant gaps in knowledge about contraception, pregnancy, abortion methods, and legal abortion access. These information gaps contributed to unintended pregnancies and influenced how young women sought to terminate pregnancies.

Contraceptive Knowledge and Misconceptions

Several participants reported limited understanding of contraceptive methods, effectiveness, and proper use. Common misconceptions included beliefs about when pregnancy could occur and how contraceptives work:

I didn't know I could get pregnant the first time. People said the first time is safe.

(P01, 16 years, G1P0, in school, spontaneous abortion)

I was using family planning [injectables] before, but I stopped because I heard it makes you not have babies in the future. Then I got pregnant.

(P11, 22 years, G2P1, out of school, suspected induced abortion)

These knowledge gaps reflect inadequacies in comprehensive sexuality education and youth‐friendly contraceptive counseling. Young women's decisions about contraceptive use were influenced by misinformation from peers and community members rather than accurate information from healthcare providers.

Information about Abortion Methods and Risks

When faced with unintended pregnancies, participants sought information about abortion methods from informal sources, primarily friends and internet searches. The information obtained was often inaccurate, incomplete, and focused on methods rather than risks:

My friend told me about tablets you can buy. She said they work and it's easy. She didn't tell me about the pain and bleeding. I didn't know it could be dangerous.

(P03, 17 years, G2P0A1, in school, suspected induced abortion)

I searched on the internet. I found information about herbs and things to drink. I tried some of them. When the bleeding started, I got scared.

(P13, 23 years, G2P0A1, in school tertiary, suspected induced abortion)

The reliance on informal information sources reflects young women's inability to access accurate information through formal healthcare channels. Fear of judgment, concerns about confidentiality, and lack of awareness about legal abortion grounds prevented participants from seeking information from healthcare providers.

Lack of Awareness about Legal Abortion Access

Most participants were unaware that abortion is legal under certain circumstances in Namibia. This lack of awareness meant that even participants whose situations might have qualified for legal abortion did not attempt to access services through formal healthcare channels:

I didn't know you could get abortion at the hospital. I thought all abortion is illegal. That's why I tried to do it myself.

(P09, 21 years, G1P0, in school tertiary, suspected induced abortion)

This finding highlights a critical gap between legal provisions and public awareness. Even where legal abortion is theoretically available, lack of information about legal grounds and how to access services effectively renders the law meaningless for many young women.

Theme 5: Cultural Expectations and Marriage Norms

Cultural expectations about marriage, childbearing, and family formation strongly influenced abortion decision‐making. Participants described how norms prescribing childbearing within marriage created pressure to terminate pregnancies that occurred outside this context.

In our culture, you must be married before you have children. If you have a baby without a husband, people say you are not respecting yourself. Your family is ashamed.

(P14, 23 years, G3P2, out of school cohabiting, suspected induced abortion)

I want to have children, but when I'm married. Not now when I'm alone. Children should have a father who is there, who is married to the mother.

(P15, 24 years, G1P0, in school tertiary, spontaneous abortion)

These cultural expectations created a temporal framework for acceptable childbearing; children should come after marriage, not before. Pregnancy outside this framework was seen as shameful and socially unacceptable, creating pressure to conceal or terminate such pregnancies.

For participants in unstable relationships or with partners unwilling to marry, cultural norms intensified the perceived impossibility of continuing pregnancies. The social stigma of single motherhood, combined with economic challenges and limited partner support, made abortion appear as the only socially viable option.

Theme 6: Perceptions of Abortion and Legal Restrictions

Participants held complex and sometimes contradictory views about abortion, reflecting tensions between personal experiences, religious teachings, social norms, and pragmatic realities. While many expressed moral ambivalence about abortion, they also articulated an understanding of why women seek abortions and frustration with legal restrictions.

Moral Ambivalence and Religious Beliefs

Several participants described internal conflict between religious teachings that condemn abortion and their own situations that made abortion seem necessary:

I know abortion is wrong. In church they say it's a sin, it's killing. But what else could I do? I had no choice. I pray God will forgive me.

(P04, 18 years, G1P0, in school, spontaneous abortion)

This moral ambivalence reflects the influence of religious discourse on young women's abortion experiences. Even as participants felt compelled to seek abortion by their circumstances, religious teachings created feelings of guilt and moral distress that compounded the physical and emotional challenges of abortion.

Views on Abortion Legalization

Participants expressed mixed views on whether abortion should be legalized more broadly. Some supported maintaining restrictions, citing religious or moral concerns. Others argued for liberalization, emphasizing women's rights to choose and the dangers of illegal abortion:

I think abortion should be allowed. It's our bodies, our lives. If a woman is not ready, she should be able to decide. The law should not force us.

(P11, 22 years, G2P1, out of school, suspected induced abortion)

Maybe abortion should be legal in some cases, like if the girl is still in school or if she was raped. But not for everyone, because people might use it wrongly.

(P07, 19 years, G1P0, in school, spontaneous abortion)

These divergent views reflect the complexity of abortion attitudes and the influence of competing frameworks, rights‐based perspectives emphasizing autonomy versus moral frameworks emphasizing protection of fetal life.

Desire for Reproductive Autonomy and Choice

Several participants expressed frustration with limited reproductive choices and articulated a desire for autonomy in abortion decision‐making, reflecting awareness that their reproductive options were constrained by law, not only by social factors. While some participants accepted legal restrictions as legitimate, others questioned why women could not make these decisions themselves:

I think women should be able to choose. It's our bodies, our lives. If I'm not ready to have a baby, why should the law force me? The law doesn't help me raise the child or pay for school.

(P11, 22 years, G2P1, out of school, suspected induced abortion)

If it was my choice, I would want to decide for myself when I'm ready to have a baby. Not the law, not my parents—me. But we don't have that choice here.

(P14, 23 years, G3P2, out of school cohabiting, suspected induced abortion)

These sentiments reflect an unmet need for abortion on request and highlight young women's desire for reproductive autonomy. Participants recognized the disconnect between their lived realities, in which continuing pregnancy would have severe consequences and legal frameworks that did not account for their circumstances. This finding underscores the importance of person‐centered approaches to abortion policy that respect women's decision‐making capacity and lived experiences.

This qualitative study explored abortion decision‐making among young women in Namibia's restrictive legal context. Our findings reveal that young women's decisions are shaped by complex, interconnected factors operating at individual, interpersonal, and structural levels. Fear of social consequences, particularly parental rejection and community stigma, emerged as the primary driver, often outweighing health and safety considerations. Educational aspirations and cultural expectations to bear children within marriage further influenced decision‐making. Critically, young women demonstrated awareness of abortion risks yet felt they had limited alternatives given their economic dependence, social vulnerability, and the restrictive legal environment. These findings illuminate the profound impact of restrictive abortion policies on young women's reproductive autonomy, health‐seeking behaviors, and well‐being.

Our findings align with and extend recent evidence on the health impacts of restrictive abortion laws. De Londras et al.'s (2022) synthesis of legal and health evidence demonstrates that grounds‐based abortion laws, such as Namibia's restrict access, reduce service quality, and infringe on pregnant people's human rights [5]. Their review concludes that such restrictions lead to worse health outcomes and that abortion on request better supports access, quality, and rights than grounds‐based approaches. Our study provides empirical support for these conclusions by documenting how Namibia's restrictive framework shapes young women's decision‐making in ways that increase health risks. Participants' accounts reveal that legal restrictions do not prevent abortion‐seeking but rather push young women toward unsafe methods, delay care‐seeking, and compound health risks with social and economic vulnerabilities. This pattern, where legal barriers drive unsafe abortion rather than preventing abortion, is well‐documented globally and underscores the public health imperative for law reform (WHO, 2022 [19]).

The fear of parental rejection and community stigma identified in our study aligns with findings from Ghana [20, 21], Ethiopia [22], and other sub‐Saharan African contexts [23]. However, our study extends this literature by documenting how these social factors interact with restrictive legal frameworks and limited contraceptive access to create conditions in which young women feel abortion is their only option, even when they must pursue it through unsafe means.

The high unintended pregnancy rate among study participants, despite available contraception, reflects documented barriers to contraceptive access for young women in Namibia. While overall contraceptive prevalence is approximately 58% among reproductive‐age women, adolescents and young women face specific access barriers including provider bias, lack of confidentiality, limited method choice, and poor adolescent‐friendly service delivery [7, 8]. The most common contraceptive methods in Namibia are injectables (65.1%) and oral contraceptive pills (20.6%), both of which require consistent service access and have typical‐use failure rates higher than those of long‐acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) (Namibia MoHSS, 2014). Young women may face particular challenges with consistent method use due to stigma when accessing services, concerns about side effects, and service delivery gaps. Additionally, LARCs, which have the lowest failure rates, are less available to adolescents and young women due to provider concerns and limited training [7]. These factors contribute to contraceptive discontinuation and method failure, resulting in unintended pregnancies that, in the context of restrictive abortion laws and social stigma, may lead to unsafe abortion‐seeking. Improving contraceptive access and choice for young women, particularly through youth‐friendly services and expanded LARC availability, is essential for reducing unintended pregnancy and unsafe abortion in this population.

Our findings also highlight the critical role of partner dynamics in abortion decision‐making. The pattern of partner denial and abandonment documented in our study reflects broader gender inequalities and power imbalances in relationships. Young women bore the primary consequences of unintended pregnancy, social stigma, educational disruption, and economic hardship while partners often evaded responsibility. This gendered burden of reproductive consequences underscores the need for interventions that engage men and boys in reproductive health and promote equitable partnership and shared responsibility.

Policy and Practice Implications

Our findings have several important implications for policy and practice in Namibia. First, they support the case for abortion law reform. The evidence that restrictive laws drive young women toward unsafe abortion, combined with international evidence that grounds‐based restrictions worsen health outcomes [5], provides a strong rationale for liberalizing Namibia's abortion law. Enabling abortion on request, at least in the first trimester, would align Namibia with international human rights standards and evidence‐based public health practice (WHO, 2022 [19]).

Second, even within the current legal framework, improvements are needed in: (1) comprehensive sexuality education that includes accurate information about contraception, pregnancy options, and safe abortion access; (2) youth‐friendly contraceptive services that ensure confidentiality, offer diverse methods including LARCs, and train providers in non‐judgmental care; (3) post‐abortion care that is accessible, high‐quality, and includes contraceptive counseling; and (4) community‐level interventions to reduce stigma and improve social support for young women facing unintended pregnancy.

Third, our findings highlight the need for interventions that address the structural factors constraining young women's reproductive autonomy, including economic dependence on parents, conditional educational support, and limited livelihood options. Policies that enable young mothers to continue education and programs that provide economic support may reduce the pressure young women face to seek abortion to maintain parental support and educational opportunities.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. We employed rigorous qualitative methods with a clear analytical framework, enhancing the credibility and transferability of findings. Our sample included diversity in age, educational status, and pregnancy history, allowing exploration of variation in experiences. The sensitive, in‐depth interview approach enabled participants to share experiences that are highly stigmatized and often concealed.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our sample size (n = 15), while providing rich data, may not capture the full range of experiences and may limit transferability to other regions of Namibia or similar contexts. Second, participants were recruited from one hospital, potentially missing experiences of young women who did not seek facility‐based care or who accessed care in other settings. Third, the sensitive nature of induced abortion and its legal status may have led some participants to withhold information or misrepresent their experiences, despite assurances of confidentiality. Fourth, we did not interview partners, parents, or healthcare providers, limiting our understanding of interpersonal and health system dynamics. Future research should include larger, multi‐site samples and diverse stakeholder perspectives to provide a more comprehensive understanding of abortion decision‐making in Namibia.

Conclusions

Young women's abortion decisions in Namibia are shaped by complex, interconnected factors at individual, interpersonal, community, and structural levels. Restrictive abortion laws, combined with social stigma, limited contraceptive access, and economic vulnerabilities, create conditions in which young women feel compelled to seek abortion through unsafe means. Our findings support the need for comprehensive policy and programmatic responses, including abortion law reform, improved contraceptive access, youth‐friendly services, and interventions to address the social and economic factors constraining young women's reproductive autonomy. Ultimately, protecting young women's health and rights requires moving beyond restrictive legal frameworks toward person‐centered approaches that respect women's decision‐making capacity and provide safe, accessible reproductive health services.
