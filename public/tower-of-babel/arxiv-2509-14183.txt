Index Date Imputation For Survival Outcomes for Externally Controlled Trials
Quentin Le Coent, Gary L. Rosner, Mei-Cheng Wang, Chen Hu

Abstract
Summary Externally controlled trials (ECTs) compare outcomes between a single-arm trial and external controls drawn from sources such as historical trials, registries, or observational studies. In survival analysis, a major challenge arises when the time origin (index date) differs across groups, for example when treatment initiation occurs after a delay in the single-arm trial but is undefined in the external controls. This misalignment can bias treatment effect estimates and distort causal interpretation. We propose a novel statistical method, Index Date Imputation (IDI), that imputes comparable index dates for external control patients using the estimated distribution of treatment initiation times from the single-arm cohort. To address population-level confounding, IDI is combined with propensity score weighting or matching, yielding balanced and temporally aligned cohorts for survival comparison. We detail diagnostics for covariate balance and truncation bias, and evaluate performance via extensive simulations. Applying IDI to a randomized oncology trial, we demonstrate that the method recovers the known treatment effect despite artificial index date misalignment. IDI provides a principled framework for time-to-event analyses in ECTs and is broadly applicable in oncology and rare disease settings.



1
Introduction

Externally controlled single-arm trials (ECTs) have gained increasing traction in the evaluation of therapeutic interventions for rare diseases and oncology
(
Hatswell et al. 2016
;
Goring et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
. ECTs are attractive, particularly when randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are infeasible due to small patient populations, ethical concerns, or logistical barriers. In these designs, outcomes from a nonrandomized treatment cohort are compared to an external control arm constructed from historical clinical trials, disease registries, or real-world data.
Approaches using historical information to strenghten clinical trials have been proposed, especially in the Bayesian framework
(
Viele et al. 2014
;
Ibrahim et al. 2015
)
.
When the primary endpoint is time to some event such as overall survival or progression-free survival—ECTs offer a potentially viable path to inference—but introduce critical methodological challenges due to their observational nature that must be carefully accounted for
(
Jahanshahi et al. 2021
)
.

Among these challenges, misalignment of the index date—defined as the time origin from which event times are measured—has emerged as a major source of bias. When external sources of comparative data are used, patients in the single-arm trial initiate treatment after a sequence of diagnostic or clinical eligibility steps, while external controls are indexed at an earlier milestone (e.g., diagnosis or relapse). As a result, treated patients must survive the time between the external cohort’s index date and treatment initiation, creating a period of guaranteed survival known as immortal time. If not properly accounted for, this asymmetry leads to overestimation of treatment benefit and invalid causal inference.

These concerns are not merely theoretical. In October 2022, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) convened an Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee (ODAC) meeting to review a biologics license application for 131I-omburtamab, a radiolabeled antibody for pediatric patients with neuroblastoma metastatic to the central nervous system. The application was supported by a single-arm trial with overall survival compared to an external control cohort from a German registry. FDA reviewers identified serious limitations in the comparability of the cohorts, such as the differences in post-relapse therapies and treatment era. Moreover, they highlighted the problematic choice of index date: survival in the treatment arm was measured from drug initiation, while in the external control it was measured from relapse. This discrepancy introduced potential immortal time bias, contributing to the agency’s conclusion that a treatment effect could not be reliably established.

Motivated by such regulatory and methodological challenges, this manuscript proposes a novel statistical method to mitigate index-date bias in ECTs with time-to-event endpoints. We begin by formalizing the inferential problem and defining the target estimand. We then describe the proposed approach and its theoretical underpinnings, including conditions for unbiasedness and practical considerations for implementation. To evaluate performance, we conduct simulation studies under varying degrees of index date misalignment and censoring. Finally, we apply the method to a real-world clinical trial example to illustrate its utility and limitations. While grounded in applications from oncology, the method is broadly relevant to rare disease settings where ECTs are increasingly considered a regulatory and clinical necessity.

2
Background and Related Work

2.1
Timeline Misalignment and Bias in Single-Arm vs External Comparisons

Externally controlled trials (ECTs) are increasingly used in settings where randomized trials are infeasible. In such designs, a single-arm trial is conducted and compared with an external control cohort constructed from historical trials, observational studies, or disease registries. While this structure offers logistical and ethical advantages, it introduces key asymmetries in data availability and time origin definitions across the two groups.

In a typical single-arm trial, patients initiate the investigational treatment after completing prior therapy and meeting protocol-defined eligibility criteria. Enrollment generally occurs shortly before the start of the new treatment (step 2). Retrospectively, the patient’s clinical history—including the date of initial diagnosis (step 1)—is reconstructed. Thus, survival outcomes in the single-arm cohort are often analyzed from treatment initiation forward, using it as the index date. Only those who survive to receive the investigational treatment are included, resulting in left truncation.

In contrast, external control patients are usually observed from the time of initial diagnosis, and by definition, they never receive the experimental treatment. The timing of a comparable “treatment initiation” does not exist for these individuals. Survival is measured from diagnosis, and the covariates available often reflect an earlier disease state. This fundamental misalignment—between retrospective time origin in the single-arm trial and prospective follow-up in the external control—introduces immortal time bias and threatens the validity of naïve comparisons.

This structure is visualized in Figure
1
. The top panel shows the sampling framework: external control patients represent an unbiased sample from population 0, while single-arm patients are a left-truncated sample from population 1, conditional on surviving long enough and meeting trial eligibility. The bottom panel outlines the available data in each group: single-arm patients contribute observed diagnosis and treatment dates and survival from both origins; external controls contribute diagnosis dates and survival thereafter but lack information on any potential treatment initiation.

These structural differences pose two primary challenges. First, survival is anchored to different time origins across groups, making direct comparison invalid. Second, there may be confounding due to systematic differences between the populations at baseline. This manuscript introduces a method to address both issues in the estimation of survival treatment effects using externally controlled comparisons.

2.2
Approaches to Address Index Date Bias in Survival Analysis

Several analytic strategies have been proposed to handle misalignment in time origins and mitigate immortal time bias in externally controlled trials with time-to-event endpoints. Each method reflects a trade-off between simplicity, validity, and the estimand targeted.

One common strategy is
left-truncation
, where survival is measured from a common baseline date such as diagnosis, and patients in the single-arm cohort enter the study with late entry at the time they initiate the new treatment
(
Wang et al. 1993
;
Kalbfleisch & Prentice 2002
)
. This approach ensures a well-defined and clinically meaningful time origin for all patients, but it does not estimate the effect of treatment from the initiation time onward. Rather, it estimates an effect conditional on having survived and received treatment, and thus does not address the marginal treatment effect from the point of actual intervention.

Landmark analysis
restricts analysis to patients who survive beyond a fixed time point (the landmark), allowing survival comparisons to be anchored at a shared index date
(
Anderson et al. 1983
;
Dafni 2011
)
. This method is easy to implement and interpret but discards all events occurring before the landmark time, which may lead to biased estimates and loss of efficiency. Moreover, the choice of the landmark is often arbitrary and may not align with clinically meaningful decision points
(
Morgan 2019
)
.

The
time-dependent Cox model
treats treatment status as a time-varying covariate, modeling the hazard function with respect to time since diagnosis
(
Fisher & Lin 1999
)
. This model can adjust for treatment initiation when observed in both groups but is generally infeasible in externally controlled settings where (new) treatment initiation is only defined for single-arm patients.

Another strategy, known as
Prescription-Time Distribution Matching (PTDM)
, was originally proposed to address immortal time bias in pharmacoepidemiologic studies
(
Zhou et al. 2005
)
. PTDM involves sampling pseudo-treatment initiation times for external control patients from the observed empirical distribution of treatment initiation times in the single-arm trial. Patients in the control cohort who are censored before their assigned pseudo-date are excluded from the analysis. This method aims to mimic the survival risk sets observed in the single-arm trial and mitigate immortal time bias that would otherwise arise from misaligned index dates.

While PTDM offers an intuitive and simple adjustment, it suffers from several important limitations. First, PTDM samples pesudo-index dates directly from the observed initiation times in the single-arm group but does not account for the fact that these observations are left-truncated, meaning that only patients who survived beyond their initiation time are represented. As a result, the observed distribution differs from the true underlying distribution in the target population. Second, exclusion of censored controls prior to their pseudo-index date can induce selection bias and reduce generalizability.
Third, the randomness inherent in sampling index dates introduces non-reproducibility and variability into the estimates. Finally, it lacks a formal causal or statistical framework, complicating inference and uncertainty quantification. These drawbacks motivate more principled alternatives for aligning time origins in externally controlled survival comparisons.

Overall, while these methods offer partial solutions, none simultaneously addresses both the structural misalignment in time origins and the confounding between external and treated populations in a unified, principled manner. This motivates the development of our proposed method.

2.3
Limitations of Existing Methods and Motivation for Index Date Imputation (IDI)

Despite their intuitive appeal, existing methods fall short in simultaneously addressing index date misalignment and population-level confounding in externally controlled trials. For instance, left-truncation approaches assume that treatment initiation times are observed in both groups, which is rarely the case in external control data. Landmark analysis imposes arbitrary time cutoffs and discards early events, limiting both efficiency and generalizability. Time-dependent Cox models require treatment status to be observed longitudinally for all patients and focus on conditional effects, making them unsuitable for marginal survival comparisons when treatment initiation is undefined in the control group.

To overcome these challenges, we propose a new approach—
Index Date Imputation (IDI)
—that combines the strengths of empirical alignment and causal inference. IDI uses a model-based strategy to impute pseudo-treatment initiation times in the external control group, informed by baseline covariates and the initiation time distribution in the treated cohort. This enables individual-level risk set alignment while preserving reproducibility and accommodating censoring. By integrating inverse probability weighting to adjust for population confounding, the IDI approach provides valid estimation of marginal survival functions from treatment initiation within a well-defined target population.

The method thus offers a unified solution to two central problems in externally controlled comparisons: time origin misalignment and baseline imbalance. In the following section, we formally describe the IDI framework and its implementation.

We aim to estimate the marginal survival effect of the experimental treatment from the initiation time onward, within the target population
G=1

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{1}(t)&\equiv\mathbb{P}(T-R>t\mid T>R,G=1,Z=1),\\

(1)

h(t)=\frac{h_{1}(t)}{h_{0}(t)},\quad\text{where}\quad h_{z}(t)=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log S_{z}(t).

G=1

4
Methods

4.1
Overview

We propose the Index Date Imputation (IDI) framework, designed to jointly address time origin misalignment and baseline confounding in externally controlled single-arm trials. Our approach combines risk-set alignment via pseudo-index dates with propensity score methods to enable valid estimation of marginal survival treatment effects.

The procedure consists of four main components:

1.

Confounding and truncation adjustment:
Reweight the single-arm trial due to left truncation and the external control group to balance baseline covariates relative to the single-arm group;

2.

Index date imputation:
Assign pseudo-treatment initiation times to external controls to mimic left-truncation in the treated group;

3.

Survival analysis:
Estimate the marginal treatment effect using weighted Cox regression on aligned survival times;

4.

Bootstrap inference:
Repeat the procedure across resampled datasets to average over stochastic imputations and estimate uncertainty.

Because treatment initiation is delayed in the single-arm group, only those surviving to that time are observed—a classical left-truncation scenario. Direct propensity score modeling on the pooled data would thus produce biased estimates. We correct for this by weighting single-arm patients by the inverse of their truncation probability, estimated from observed data. This enables valid estimation of group membership probabilities, which in turn support inverse probability weighting or matching.

4.2
Step 1: Confounding and Truncation Adjustment via Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW)

To estimate a marginal average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), we model the probability of single-arm participation conditional on covariates
X
. Let
G\in\{0,1\}
denote population membership and assume:

A1)

X
captures all confounders of the treatment-survival relationship;

A2)

The truncation time
R
is independent of
X
.

Under A1, the propensity score is defined as:

\mathbb{P}\left(G=1\mid X\right)=\frac{e^{\beta X}}{1+e^{\beta X}}.

(3)

To correct for the bias induced by left-truncation in the single-arm group, we estimate each single-arm individual’s truncation probability:

\mathbb{P}(T>R\mid X)=\int\mathbb{P}(T>r\mid X)\,\mathrm{d}F_{R}(r),

where
F_{R}
denotes the (untruncated) cumulative distribution function of treatment initiation times in the target population.

Assumption A2 allows us to estimate
F_{R}
from observed single-arm data via a weighted empirical distribution:

\hat{F}_{R}(r)=\frac{\sum\hat{S}_{T}^{-1}(r_{i};G=1,Z=1)I(r_{i}\leq r)}{\sum\hat{S}_{T}^{-1}(r_{i};G=1,Z=1)},

(4)

with
\hat{S}_{T}
a left-truncated Kaplan-Meier estimator. The nonparametric estimator
\hat{F}_{R}(r)
possesses the property as the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator of
F_{R}(r)
, as discussed in
(
Wang 1991
)
.

The conditional survival
\mathbb{P}(T>r\mid X)
is estimated using a Cox model with Breslow baseline hazard:

\hat{\mathbb{P}}(T>r\mid X)=\exp\left(-\hat{\Lambda}_{0}(r)\cdot e^{\hat{\theta}^{\top}X}\right),\quad\hat{\Lambda}_{0}(r)=\sum_{T_{i}\leq r}\frac{\delta_{i}}{\sum_{j\in R(T_{i})}e^{\hat{\theta}^{\top}X_{j}}},

where
R(T_{i})
denotes the set of individuals at risk at
T_{i}
.
Integrating over
\hat{F}_{R}
, we obtain truncation-adjusted weights,
\zeta_{i}
, for single-arm subjects:

\zeta_{i}=\hat{\mathbb{P}}(T>R\mid X_{i})^{-1}=\left[\int\hat{\mathbb{P}}(T>r\mid X_{i})\,\mathrm{d}\hat{F}_{R}(r)\right]^{-1}.

The weights
\zeta_{i}
are used to weight the single-arm patients in the logistic regression for estimating
\hat{\beta}
, the maximum-likelhood estimator of the parameter
\beta
in the logistic regression model given in Equation
3
. In this regression model, the external-control patients are given a weight
\zeta_{i}=1
.
Once the estimator
\hat{\beta}
of
\beta
is obtained, the final ATT weights are given by,

w_{i}=\begin{cases}1\ \mathrm{if}\ G_{i}=1\\
\frac{\hat{p}_{i}}{1-\hat{p}_{i}}\ \mathrm{if}\ G_{i}=0\ \mathrm{where}\ \hat{p}_{i}=\frac{e^{\hat{\beta}X_{i}}}{1+e^{\hat{\beta}X_{i}}},\end{cases}

(5)

4.3
Step 2: Index Date Imputation in the External Control Group

To emulate left-truncation in the external controls, we impute pseudo-treatment initiation times from the estimated distribution
\hat{F}_{R}
. For each control subject
j=1,\dots,n_{0}
:

1.

Draw
\hat{R}_{j}\sim\hat{F}_{R}
;

2.

Compute adjusted survival time:
Y_{j}-\hat{R}_{j}
;

3.

Retain the subject if
Y_{j}>\hat{R}_{j}
, mimicking truncation.

This aligns the external control risk sets with those observed in the treated group. Unlike PTDM, which samples directly from observed (biased) initiation times, our approach uses an estimated distribution accounting for truncation bias.

4.4
Step 3: Estimating the Marginal Treatment Effect

With aligned index dates and covariate balance achieved, we estimate the treatment effect using a weighted Cox proportional hazards model:

\lambda(t\mid Z)=\lambda_{0}(t)\cdot\exp(\gamma Z),

where
Z\in\{0,1\}
is the treatment indicator.

The survival time is defined from the treatment initiation point:

•

Single-arm patients:
Y_{i}=T_{i}-R_{i}
,
\delta=I(T_{i}\leq C_{i})
;

•

External control patients:
Y_{i}=T_{i}-\hat{R_{i}}
,
\delta=I(T_{i}\leq C_{i})
.

Under assumptions and correct model specification, the estimate
\hat{\gamma}
consistently estimates the log hazard ratio comparing
S_{1}(t)
and
S_{0}(t)
as defined in Equation
2
.

4.5
Step 4: Bootstrap Averaging for Inference

To account for the stochasticity in index date assignment, we apply nonparametric bootstrap resampling (with replacement)
(
Mooney et al. 1993
)
:

1.

For
b=1,\dots,B
, resample both groups with replacement;

2.

Recompute weights, impute index dates, and re-estimate
\gamma_{b}
;

3.

Use the empirical distribution of
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{B}
for inference.

This procedure yields an empirical distribution
\{\hat{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\hat{\gamma}_{B}\}
, from which we derive point estimates, standard errors, and confidence intervals—e.g., using the percentile method.

4.6
Summary and Flowchart of the IDI Approach

Figure
2
provides a schematic overview of the four-step IDI procedure. The workflow begins by addressing truncation and covariate imbalance via inverse probability weighting (Step 1), followed by risk-set alignment using pseudo-index dates in the control group (Step 2). A marginal treatment effect is then estimated via weighted survival analysis (Step 3). Finally, the full process is embedded within a bootstrap loop to stabilize inference across imputed datasets (Step 4).

The bootstrap step plays a crucial role in averaging out the randomness introduced by the imputation of index dates and ensuring reproducibility. While larger values of
B
improve precision, even moderate values (e.g.,
B=100
) typically yield stable results in practice.

4.7
Alternative to Weighting: Propensity Score Matching

As an alternative to weighting, 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching based on estimated propensity scores may be used to construct a matched subset of external controls. After matching, Step 2 (IDI) is applied as before. All matched subjects are assigned unit weights in the outcome model.

4.8
Diagnostics

The IDI framework integrates two key components: propensity score (PS) adjustment and index date imputation. Diagnostic tools are important to evaluate the adequacy of each component separately and to guide potential model refinement.

For the PS adjustment, a commonly used diagnostic is the standardized mean difference (SMD) between the single-arm and external control groups for each covariate included in the propensity score model
(
Austin 2008
;
Belitser et al. 2011
;
Ali et al. 2014
)
. The SMD for a covariate
X
is defined as:

\operatorname{SMD}(X)=\frac{\bar{X}_{1}-\bar{X}_{0}}{s_{p}},

(6)

where
\bar{X}_{1}
and
\bar{X}_{0}
are the sample means of
X
in the single-arm and external control groups, respectively. The pooled standard deviation
s_{p}
is given by:

s_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{(n_{1}-1)s_{1}^{2}+(n_{0}-1)s_{0}^{2}}{n_{1}+n_{0}-2}},

where
s_{1}^{2}
and
s_{0}^{2}
are the sample variances of
X
in the single-arm and external control groups, respectively.

When PS weighting is used, both the sample means and variances should be computed using the PS weights
w_{i}
. Specifically, for each group
g\in\{0,1\}
, we compute:

\displaystyle\bar{X}_{wg}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}w_{ig}X_{ig}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}w_{ig}},

\displaystyle s_{wg}^{2}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}w_{ig}(X_{ig}-\bar{X}_{wg})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}w_{ig}}.

Graphical comparisons of SMDs before and after PS adjustment can provide intuitive assessments of covariate balance. An example is shown in the top panel of Figure 1 in Section A of the Supplementary Materials. In practice, an SMD threshold of 0.1 is often used as a heuristic for acceptable covariate balance
(
Austin 2009
)
. If this threshold is not met, it may be necessary to revise the PS model (e.g., by including interaction terms or transforming variables).

For the index date imputation component, the key quantity is the estimated cumulative distribution function
\hat{F}_{R}(\cdot)
, which characterizes the distribution of index times
R
in the untruncated single-arm population. This estimator is used to generate imputed index dates for external control patients.

Although a direct comparison between
\hat{F}_{R}(\cdot)
and the empirical distribution of observed index times in the single-arm group is not meaningful (due to left truncation), we can compare
\hat{F}_{R}(\cdot\mid T>R)
, the truncated version of the estimator, with the empirical distribution of observed
R
values.

Using
\hat{S}_{T}
, the estimated survival distribution from diagnosis in the single-arm population, the truncated distribution of
R
, conditional on
T\geq R
, can be estimated by:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hat{F}_{R}(r\mid T\geq R)&=\frac{\hat{\mathbb{P}}(R\leq r,R<T)}{\hat{\mathbb{P}}(R<T)},\\
\text{where}\quad\hat{\mathbb{P}}(R<T)&=\int_{R}\hat{S}_{T}(r)\,\mathrm{d}\hat{F}_{R}(r),\\
\hat{\mathbb{P}}(R\leq r,R<T)&=-\int_{T}\hat{F}_{R}(\min(r,t))\,\mathrm{d}\hat{S}_{T}(t).\end{split}

(7)

A Q–Q plot comparing the estimated truncated distribution
\hat{F}_{R}(\cdot\mid T\geq R)
with the empirical distribution of observed index times in the single-arm group can be used to assess model fit. An example is shown in the bottom panel of Figure 1 in Section A of the Supplementary Materials. Discrepancies in this plot may suggest the need to re-evaluate the estimation of
\hat{F}_{R}
, for example, by modifying the underlying survival model or truncation adjustment.

5.1
Data Generation

We simulated data for two groups: a single-arm trial group (
G=1
) with
n_{1}=200
patients and an external control group (
G=0
) with
n_{0}=800
patients. Each subject’s
i
baseline covariates
X_{i}=(X_{i1},X_{i2})
include a binary variable
X_{i1}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.5)
and a continuous variable
X_{i2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.3^{2})
.

Group membership
G
was assigned using the logistic model:

\operatorname{logit}\left[\mathbb{P}(G_{i}=1\mid X_{i})\right]=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}X_{i1}+\beta_{2}X_{i2},

with
\beta_{0}=-0.5
,
\beta_{1}=0.3
, and
\beta_{2}=-0.2
.
We considered two settings for the distribution of the truncation time
R_{i}
in the single-arm group:

1.

Covariate independence:

R_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}[0,2]
;

2.

Covariate dependence:

R_{i}
drawn from an exponential distribution with hazard

\lambda_{R}(r\mid X_{i})=\lambda_{0,R}\exp(\beta_{R,1}X_{1i}+\beta_{R,2}X_{2i}),

where
\lambda_{0,R}=0.5
,
\beta_{R,1}=-2
, and
\beta_{R,2}=-2
.

Survival time from diagnosis,
T_{i}
, was generated from a piecewise exponential distribution for treated patients (
Z_{i}=1
) with hazard:

\lambda_{T}(t\mid X_{i},R_{i},Z_{i}=1)=\lambda_{0}\exp\left(\gamma^{\top}X_{i}+\alpha\cdot\mathbb{I}(t\geq R_{i})\right),

so that the treatment effect is introduced at time
R_{i}
and controlled by
\alpha
on the log-hazard scale.
Patients with
T_{i}\leq R_{i}
were excluded from the single-arm dataset, mimicking left-truncation. Censoring time was set as
C_{i}=R_{i}+W_{i}
, with
W_{i}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(0.3)
.
For external controls (
G=0
),
R
was considered missing and survival was generated from:

\lambda_{T}(t\mid X,Z=0)=\lambda_{0}\exp(\gamma^{\top}X),

with censoring
C\sim\mathrm{Exp}(0.3)
. The same baseline hazard
\lambda_{0}
was used in both groups.
An illustration of the baseline hazard function for patients in the single-arm and external control is provided in Figure 2 in Section A of the supplementary material. Also provided in this figure is the marginal survival functions of both groups without any adjustment for confounding or truncation in the no treatment effect case (
\alpha=0
).

Evaluated Methods.

We compared the following analysis strategies:

•

Naïve Cox model:
Uses diagnosis as the index date without adjusting for truncation or confounding.

•

PS Matching + IDI:
First estimates propensity scores (accounting for truncation weights), matches control patients 1:1 to single-arm patients, and applies IDI on the matched set.

•

PS Weighting + IDI:
Similar to the above, but uses inverse probability weights rather than matching.

5.3
Sensitivity Analyses

The validity of the IDI approach relies on the assumption of no unmeasured confounding—specifically, that all covariates influencing both population membership and survival are included in the vector
X
. To evaluate the robustness of our method to violations of this assumption, we conducted a sensitivity analysis incorporating an unobserved confounder.

For each patient in both the single-arm and external control groups, we generated a latent variable
\omega\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
that was excluded from the adjustment set but influenced both group assignment
G
and the survival time
T
. The level of unmeasured confounding is governed by the variance
\sigma^{2}
, with higher values indicating stronger confounding. Specifically,
\omega
was included as an additive term in the logistic model for group membership (modifying Equation
3
) and in the log-linear model for the hazard function of
T
.

We simulated datasets with
n_{1}=200
and
n_{0}=800
under varying levels of unmeasured confounding (
\sigma=0.3
and
\sigma=1
) and treatment effects (
\alpha=0,-0.5,-1
). Results are summarized in Table
3
.

When unmeasured confounding was modest (
\sigma=0.3
), the bias of the IDI-based estimators remained limited, and coverage rates were only slightly attenuated. However, for stronger confounding (
\sigma=1
), substantial bias emerged in both the matching and weighting implementations of IDI. Absolute bias ranged from 0.076 to 0.169 across the scenarios. Although the estimated standard errors were relatively unaffected, the increase in bias led to notable undercoverage of the 95% confidence intervals, indicating a loss of inferential validity under substantial unmeasured confounding.

These findings reinforce that, like other propensity score–based methods, the performance of IDI depends critically on the validity of the conditional exchangeability assumption.

Emulating an Externally Controlled Trial from ECOG-2108

We applied the proposed IDI approach to the ECOG-ACRIN E2108 trial [NCT01242800]
(
Khan et al. 2022
)
, a randomized phase III study that investigated whether early local therapy (ELT), compared to continued optimal systemic therapy (OST), improves overall survival in women with de novo metastatic breast cancer.

The trial employed a two-step design. In the first step, all enrolled patients received standardized systemic therapy for 16 to 32 weeks, guided by National Comprehensive Cancer Center Network (NCCN) guidelines, according to age, menopausal status, metastatic sites, and tumor marker profile of the primary tumor. Those whose disease did not progress were then randomized 1:1 to ELT (surgery and radiotherapy; Arm B) or continued systemic therapy (Arm A). The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), measured from the time of randomization to death or censoring. The trial found no statistically significant difference in OS between the two arms (3-year OS: 68.4% for ELT vs. 67.9% for OST; log-rank
p=0.57
).

Because ECOG-2108 was a randomized controlled trial, its two-step design lends itself to evaluating methods for externally controlled comparisons. In particular, the randomization time (used as the index date for OS) is not typically available in observational data, where patients remain on systemic therapy continuously. This makes ECOG-2108 a useful case for emulating an ECT design in which index date misalignment is an issue.

To mimic an ECT, we treated Arm B (ELT) as the “single-arm trial” and Arm A (OST) as the “external control.” The randomization time
R
—observed for Arm B—was used to define survival from treatment initiation. For Arm A patients,
R
was treated as unobserved and imputed using the IDI procedure based on the empirical distribution
\hat{F}_{R}
estimated from Arm B.

Eligibility Considerations.

The ECOG-2108 dataset also includes a third group: patients who were ineligible for randomization due to disease progression. Because progression time was not provided in the available dataset, we excluded these patients from the analysis. Including them would violate the comparability assumption and introduce outcome-dependent selection bias that cannot be addressed by IDI or propensity score methods.

Covariate Adjustment.

Although the randomized design ensures covariate balance between Arms A and B, we performed IDI both with and without propensity score adjustment for completeness. Baseline covariates used in adjustment were age, race, and disease stage. We restricted the analysis to patients with complete data on survival time and these covariates, resulting in a final sample of 122 patients in Arm A and 115 patients in Arm B (out of 131 and 125, respectively).

Implementation.

The IDI procedure followed the steps outlined in Section
4
. The distribution
\hat{F}_{R}
of treatment initiation times was estimated using Arm B, accounting for left truncation. A Cox model for
T\mid X
was fit in Arm B to estimate truncation probabilities for inverse weighting. These weights were used in a logistic regression to estimate ATT weights as in Equation
5
, where Arm B patients received inverse-truncation weights and Arm A patients received unit weights.

Next, we imputed pseudo-index dates
\hat{R}
for Arm A patients by sampling from
\hat{F}_{R}
. Patients with
T-\hat{R}>0
were retained, mirroring the truncation condition in Arm B. A weighted Cox model was then fit using survival from the imputed or observed index date and treatment assignment as the sole covariate.

Results.

Figure
5
summarizes the results. The top panel reproduces the original ECOG-2108 randomized comparison, showing a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.863 (95% CI: 0.598-1.246) based on survival from randomization. The middle panel shows a naïve ECT-style analysis that uses baseline (enrollment) as the index date for Arm A and randomization for Arm B. This naive analysis yielded an attenuated HR of 0.940 (95% CI: 0.651-1.358), illustrating the bias introduced by index date misalignment.

The bottom panel shows results using IDI, both with and without propensity score weighting. IDI with PS weighting yielded an HR of 0.882 (95% CI: 0.610-1.277), closely matching the RCT estimate (0.863). IDI without weighting produced a similar result (HR = 0.862 [95% CI: 0.597-1.245]). These findings suggest that IDI can recover unbiased estimates of the marginal treatment effect, even when index dates are unobserved in the external control group.

Interpretation.

In this application, PS adjustment is not strictly necessary due to the randomized design. Nonetheless, the fact that IDI recovers the benchmark result provides a compelling empirical validation. One nuance is that patients in both arms of the original RCT were eligible only if progression had not occurred at randomization (i.e.,
P>R
). In our emulated ECT design, IDI imposes a truncation condition
T>\hat{R}
on external controls. This results in a “double truncation” condition (
P>R
and
T>\hat{R}
) in the imputed group, potentially inducing selection bias.

However, in this dataset, the bias appears minimal. This may be due to the original progression-based eligibility (
P>R
) being stricter than
T>\hat{R}
, making the latter condition non-restrictive for most external control patients. Nonetheless, we note that this feature is application-specific and may not generalize.

Discussion

In this article, we proposed a method for handling index date misalignment in externally controlled single-arm trials using Index Date Imputation (IDI), in combination with propensity score (PS) methods. The IDI approach is tailored to time-to-event analyses where the treatment initiation time is unobserved in the external control group. It estimates the distribution of truncation times from the single-arm cohort and uses this distribution to impute pseudo-index dates for external control patients.

A key assumption underlying IDI is that the index date distribution is shared across treatment groups. This is appropriate when targeting the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT), as external controls are reweighted to represent the single-arm population. For the average treatment effect (ATE), however, the assumption becomes more stringent and potentially less tenable, as it would require the unobserved index date distribution in the untreated group to match that of the treated. Adapting IDI for ATE estimation would likely require additional modeling or external information.

Another critical assumption is the absence of unmeasured confounding, which underpins all PS-based adjustment methods. Our simulations show that IDI is sensitive to violations of this assumption, with increasing bias observed under stronger unmeasured confounding (Table
3
). While sensitivity analysis
(
Groenwold et al. 2010
;
Cinelli & Hazlett 2020
)
or instrumental variable methods
(
Martens et al. 2006
;
Hernán & Robins 2006
)
may partially address this issue, they cannot fully compensate for omitted confounders without additional identifying assumptions.
While matching simplifies interpretation and avoids instability from extreme weights, it may reduce sample size and efficiency, particularly if covariate overlap is limited.

A strength of the proposed framework is its compatibility with standard survival analysis techniques. Once index dates are imputed and the groups balanced, classical methods such as the Cox proportional hazards model, Kaplan-Meier estimator, or restricted mean survival time (RMST)
(
Royston & Parmar 2013
)
can be applied to estimate marginal treatment effects. While this paper focused on Cox modeling, the IDI framework accommodates a wide range of analytic strategies.

In contrast to earlier methods such as the pseudo-time-dependent model (PTDM)
(
Zhou et al. 2005
)
, which samples index dates from the observed treated group without adjusting for truncation bias, IDI uses a corrected estimate of the full index time distribution and explicitly applies a truncation condition to external controls. PTDM lacks formal justification for marginal comparisons and may introduce selection bias by relying on a truncated distribution. Our approach addresses these limitations and improves both theoretical soundness and empirical performance, as shown in simulations and the ECOG-2108 application.

Despite its strengths, IDI has two notable limitations. First, it introduces randomness through imputation of pseudo-index dates. While this randomness is mitigated using nonparametric bootstrap, which both stabilizes estimates and enables inference (e.g., confidence intervals via the percentile method), the stochastic nature of the process may hinder reproducibility compared to fully analytic estimators.

Second, imputing index dates can result in the exclusion of external control patients whose follow-up ends before their assigned date (i.e.,
T<\hat{R}
). This truncation reduces effective sample sizes and may reduce power. The extent of loss depends on the distribution of
R
and censoring patterns in the control group.

These limitations arise from IDI’s patient-level implementation. A potential alternative is a population-level approach that combines (i) an estimate of survival from baseline in the external control group with (ii) the estimated distribution
\hat{F}_{R}
from the single-arm group. Such a method could produce marginal survival estimates from treatment initiation without discarding patients or introducing simulation-based randomness. Developing and validating such an approach is a subject of future research.

The IDI procedure aims to achieve two goals: (1) impute a meaningful index date for external controls, and (2) apply a comparable truncation condition (
T>R
) so that patients included in both groups are aligned in terms of survival risk. However, in practice, truncation is based on the observed follow-up time
Y=\min(T,C)
. For the estimator
\hat{F}_{R}
defined in Equation
4
to be unbiased, we require that censoring occurs after the truncation time with probability one—i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(C>R)=1
. If this condition fails, then the observed truncation becomes
Y>R
rather than
T>R
, potentially biasing
\hat{F}_{R}
.

This mismatch introduces asymmetry: in the single-arm group, true truncation is based on
T>R
; in the external control group, we only observe and can enforce
Y>R
. If censoring depends on baseline covariates, this difference may induce covariate imbalance between groups post-truncation. Addressing this issue would require either modifying the estimator
\hat{F}_{R}
or developing alternative methods that explicitly account for censoring.

We have introduced IDI as a principled approach to align index dates and address truncation bias in externally controlled trials. Combined with propensity score methods, it provides a flexible and modular framework that can yield valid marginal survival comparisons under plausible assumptions.

To assess the goodness of the propensity score adjustment we suggested using a graphical approach based the standardized mean difference but other approaches such as the prognostic score–based measures could be used
(
Stuart et al. 2013
)
.

While the motivating application and data come from oncology, the proposed approach is applicable in other areas of clinical research. It may be particularly useful in rare diseases or in therapeutic settings involving complex, multi-phase treatment regimens—such as stem cell transplantation, long induction therapies, or eligibility-driven adaptive treatments—where delayed treatment initiation creates time-origin misalignment in external control analyses.

Future work will focus on addressing two key limitations of the current framework: removing the stochasticity introduced by patient-level index date sampling, and avoiding the sample size reduction caused by truncation-based exclusion. We also aim to refine the estimator
\hat{F}_{R}
to accommodate censoring that may occur before truncation and covariate information, further improving robustness in real-world applications.
