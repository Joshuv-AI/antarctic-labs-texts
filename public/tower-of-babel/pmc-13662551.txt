Beyond the Favorable-Risk Label: Sequential Measurable Residual Disease and KIT Variant Status in Pediatric Core-Binding Factor Acute Myeloid Leukemia
Authors: Bohyun Kim
Published: 2026

Core-binding factor acute myeloid leukemia (CBF-AML), defined by RUNX1::RUNX1T1 and CBFB::MYH11, is classified as a favorable-risk leukemia in childhood. However, a favorable prognosis does not guarantee a good outcome: the 10-yr cumulative incidence of relapse is 25% for t(8;21) and 35% for inv(16), corresponding to RUNX1:RUNX1T1 and CBFB:MYH11 at the molecular level, respectively [1]. Both the 5th edition of the WHO Classification of Haematolymphoid Tumors (WHO-HAEM5) and the International Consensus Classification (ICC) classify these fusions as AML defined by specific genetic variants, enabling diagnosis based on genetic findings rather than morphology alone. WHO-HAEM5 removed the blast threshold, whereas the ICC retains a ≥10% requirement [2, 3]. One principle applies directly to the study by Yeh et al. [4]: the fusion transcript status is a stable diagnostic marker, whereas cooperating variants and treatment-response kinetics are risk modifiers that laboratories should track over time.

Yeh et al. [4] analyzed 112 children with CBF-AML (84 with RUNX1::RUNX1T1 and 28 with CBFB::MYH11) treated at 12 centers in Taiwan over 23 years. Measurable residual disease (MRD) was quantified in bone marrow by quantitative reverse transcriptase PCR at six timepoints from diagnosis to the end of therapy, expressed both as the log reduction (LR) from the diagnostic level and as normalized copy number (NCN) per 105 ABL1 transcripts. Three findings are noteworthy. First, MRD at the end of induction—the timepoint at which most pediatric AML protocols pivot—carried no prognostic information for RUNX1::RUNX1T1 AML, whereas ≥3 LR at the end of the second consolidation and ≥4 LR at the end of consolidation (EOC) predicted superior relapse-free and overall survival. Second, LR and NCN provided complementary prognostic information; the subgroup with <4 LR and NCN >500 at EOC had particularly poor outcomes. Third, KIT variants were present in 36.8% of patients, and KIT D816 variants were associated with inferior survival—yet patients with a KIT variant who reached ≥4 LR at EOC had favorable outcomes. The genotype set the baseline risk, and the molecular kinetics were revised accordingly.

Neither WHO-HAEM5 nor ICC uses KIT status to define or subclassify CBF-AML, and the European LeukemiaNet 2022 genetic risk classification retains CBF-AML in the favorable-risk category, irrespective of KIT variant status [5]. This approach maintains diagnostic stability but provides little information on disease course after diagnosis. Genetic features beyond the defining lesion carry prognostic value. Incorporating myelodysplasia-related gene variants reassigns a substantial fraction of Korean patients with AML between the two classifications [6], as does considering the TP53 status [7], and applying these features to identical cohorts shifts the prognostic weight of a minority of cases [8]. Similarly, in CBF-AML, diagnostic markers shared across classification systems may not indicate the same prognosis. The laboratory plays an essential role in quantitative risk stratification.

Quantitative stratification by MRD relies on assay characteristics, such as amplification efficiency, calibration, analytical sensitivity, and sample adequacy, all of which should be confirmed before an LR is treated as a decision threshold. Yeh et al. [4] used an in-house assay developed according to the Europe Against Cancer program [9], with duplicate independent cDNA syntheses, high- and low-level and non-template controls in every run, and a validated analytical sensitivity of 1×10–5. Critically, only samples yielding >105 ABL1 copies were considered assessable, considering that sample adequacy is a mandatory analytical requirement.

Reporting both LR and NCN provides complementary clinical insights. LR measures the decrease relative to the baseline tumor burden of a patient and is clinically intuitive but depends heavily on the quality of the diagnostic sample and varies between laboratories. The NCN provides a standardized assay-anchored measurement that is comparable across laboratories, although it relies on reference gene normalization. Yeh et al. [4] observed discordance between these two metrics for some patients, indicating that they refined the risk assessment when considered together. A molecular MRD report should include the quantified fusion transcript, NCN and control gene copy number, LR relative to diagnosis, the assay’s limits of detection and quantification, and a sample adequacy statement. The importance of standardized reporting was evident when comparing flow cytometry and next-generation sequencing for MRD in pediatric B-lymphoblastic leukemia [10] and in a nationwide Korean survey of flow-cytometric immunophenotyping that revealed considerable interlaboratory variation [11]. The message is consistent: MRD testing should follow validated and standardized methods where possible.

The study has several limitations, some of which were acknowledged by Yeh et al. The findings were far more consistent in RUNX1::RUNX1T1 AML than they were in CBFB::MYH11 AML, for which only 28 patients were included, all of whom assessed at EOC remained in remission, leaving no survival comparison possible. The proposed thresholds therefore apply primarily to RUNX1::RUNX1T1 AML. Because molecular-clearance kinetics depend as much on the chemotherapy protocol as they do on the leukemia itself, these cutoffs may not directly apply to other regimens. In addition, Sanger sequencing of KIT exons 8, 11, and 17 missed small subclonal populations, variants in other exons, or co-occurring variants in other genes. Finally, the LR cutoffs were both derived and evaluated in the same cohort, which can overestimate their discriminative performance; external validation in independent cohorts is required before adoption.

These issues define the research agenda. Whether more sensitive methods (such as ultrasensitive next-generation sequencing or digital PCR) can detect small subclones early enough to change treatment and whether newer tools, such as RNA sequencing (which can resolve the fusion landscape of acute leukemia in a single assay [12]), add anything beyond fusion-transcript quantitative PCR can be answered in prospective studies where the treatment protocol and the MRD method are standardized together.

The practical implications are clear. Quantitative fusion-transcript assays should be validated according to established analytical standards before clinical use, and the results should be reported as LR and NCN together with an adequacy statement. Monitoring timepoints should be determined according to the treatment protocol, and the molecular report should present the coexisting-variant genotype together with sequential MRD results, as neither can be interpreted alone. Ultimately, what matters is the treatment decision: MRD at the EOC may eventually help identify children who would benefit from treatment intensification and those who could safely avoid it. Classification, whether under WHO-HAEM5 or ICC, indicates the disease. It is the laboratory, measurement, and reporting that show what the disease is doing.
