A Shared Chemokine Signature of Triglyceride-Rich Lipoproteins and lipoprotein(a)
Authors: Mira Mousa, Jeffrey Kroon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglyceride-rich lipoproteins/remnants (TRL), and lipoprotein(a) (Lp[a]) are causal drivers of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (CVD), yet they do not appear to act through identical biological pathways.1 TRL/remnants and Lp(a) may be several-fold more atherogenic per particle than LDL, raising an important biological question: what distinguishes one atherogenic apolipoprotein B (apoB)-containing particle from another? In this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, Björnson et al2 show that TRL/remnants and Lp(a) converge on a circulating chemokine signature that is largely absent with LDL. The findings suggest that the biological distinction between atherogenic lipoproteins may depend not only on the cholesterol they carry, but also on the inflammatory signals generated by their composition and metabolism, thereby identifying a candidate inflammatory pathway that may help explain their differential atherogenicity.

TRL and Lp(a) Share a Common Chemokine Response

Björnson et al2 integrated Olink proteomic data from 2,920 plasma proteins with Mendelian randomization (MR) in more than 46,000 UK Biobank participants to test the effects of genetically predicted TRL/remnants, LDL, and Lp(a) exposure on circulating proteins. MR identified effects of TRL/remnants exposure on 432 proteins, Lp(a) on 111, and LDL on only 58. Proteins upregulated by TRLs and Lp(a) mapped to chemokine signaling and leukocyte chemotaxis, whereas the LDL-associated proteomic profile was related to lipid transport and sterol metabolism. Per 10 mg/dL of apoB, both particles raised CXCL5, CXCL6, CXCL13, CCL17, CCL23, and CCL27 by more than 20%. In clinical terms, a 1.5 mmol/L increase in plasma triglyceride corresponded to a 10%-30% rise in these chemokines, far exceeding the effects on the traditional inflammatory mediators interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-6 (each approximately 5% and statistically nonsignificant after correction). A composite chemokine score was statistically consistent with mediating approximately 40% of the association between TRL/remnant cholesterol and CVD, compared with 13% for IL-6 and 5% for C-reactive protein, suggesting chemokines may therefore represent a previously underappreciated component of lipoprotein-driven inflammation. Concordant effect sizes in the independent Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis provide external support for these associations.

The important observation, therefore, is not simply that TRL/remnants and Lp(a) are associated with inflammation. Rather, 2 structurally distinct lipoprotein classes converge on a shared chemokine program, whereas LDL does not. This distinction extends the concept of lipoprotein-associated inflammation beyond the IL-1β/IL-6/C-reactive protein axis3 and points to pathways that govern leukocyte recruitment and organization within vascular tissue. CXCL5 and CXCL6 signal through CXCR2 and participate in neutrophil recruitment; CXCL13-CXCR5 signaling contributes to B-cell trafficking and organization of lymphoid structures; and CCL17 has been implicated in T-cell regulation in atherosclerosis.4 Thus, the observed signature may reflect more than a nonspecific increase in systemic inflammation. It raises the possibility that TRL/remnants and Lp(a) preferentially engage specific programs of immune-cell recruitment and vascular inflammation.

Why Should TRL/Remnants and Lp(a) Converge?

The convergence is mechanistically intriguing because TRL/remnants and Lp(a) have little obvious structural similarity beyond their shared apoB content. TRL particles are triglyceride-rich and apoB-based; Lp(a) is an LDL-like particle with apo(a) covalently bound to it and is a major carrier of oxidized phospholipids (OxPLs). OxPL on Lp(a) activate arterial endothelial cells and promote transendothelial monocyte migration,5 and Lp(a)-enriched diacylglycerols and lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) can drive monocyte inflammation, in part via the NLRP3 inflammasome.6 During lipolysis, TRL/remnants generate partially hydrolyzed lipid species with potentially similar bioactivity.7,8 These shared minor lipid cargos, rather than apoB itself, support a mechanistic hypothesis for the convergence on chemokine induction. Either could act on endothelial, vascular, or leukocyte targets to induce chemokine production. Particle size may compound this: unlike large chylomicrons and large VLDL, which are too big to enter the arterial intima, remnants are small enough to cross and be retained beneath the endothelium, where their cargo can act locally.1 This explanation remains speculative and requires direct, particle-resolved lipidomic and functional testing. The findings shift the emphasis from cholesterol quantity to particle composition and metabolism as determinants of the inflammatory information that a lipoprotein delivers to the vessel wall.

Mediator or Marker, and What Comes Next

The MR analysis supports a causal effect of genetically determined lipoprotein exposure on circulating chemokines but does not establish that circulating chemokines themselves cause atherosclerotic CVD. Consistent with this, protein quantitative trait locus analysis demonstrates that chemokine-specific variants lost their CVD association after adjustment for TRL/Lp(a) levels, consistent with chemokine activity lying downstream of lipoprotein exposure. The relevant pathogenic signal may therefore reside in local chemokine activity within the arterial wall rather than in circulating concentrations, as atherosclerosis develops in the wall where chemokines act through highly localized gradients. Plasma levels may be an imperfect surrogate for the biologically relevant signal. The mediation analysis is based on observational data and an assumed linear causal chain from exposure to mediator to outcome; the authors appropriately acknowledge that it can support, but not prove, causality. The estimate is also imprecise, and more than half of the TRL/remnant-CVD association remains unexplained, indicating that chemokine signaling may account for only part of it. The primary genetic analyses were conducted predominantly on individuals of European ancestry. Although concordant effect sizes in the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis cohort support the lipoprotein-chemokine associations, the generalizability of the mediation findings to other ancestries and to individuals with established CVD or on lipid-lowering therapy remains untested.

The immediate priority is to identify the molecular trigger shared by TRL and Lp(a). Targeted lipidomic and cell-based experiments could test whether OxPLs, LPA, diacylglycerols, or other (minor) lipid species are responsible, extending approaches already applied to Lp(a)-induced CXCL8 release.8 The second priority is to determine whether the chemokine response is a mediator of atherosclerosis rather than a biomarker of exposure. Genetic colocalization at shared TRL/Lp(a)-chemokine loci (eg, LPA, APOC3, ANGPTL3), combined with multivariable and network MR, could distinguish shared from distinct causal variants, separate TRL- and Lp(a)-specific effects, and establish a causal pathway from lipoproteins through chemokines to CVD. Drug-target MR using cis-instruments at chemokine receptor genes (CXCR2, CXCR3, CXCR5, CCR4) would better emulate pharmacologic blockade than ligand-level instruments alone, prioritizing specific receptors for development. Clinically, the identification of specific, druggable chemokine axes identifies candidate adjunctive antiatherogenic strategies that may complement, rather than replace apoB- and Lp(a)-directed therapies now in development.4 Whether selective inhibition of these pathways can reduce vascular risk without compromising host defense remains unknown. The critical next step is determining whether pharmacologic reduction of TRL/remnants or Lp(a), for example, with apoC-III or ANGPTL3 antisense agents, or emerging Lp(a)-lowering therapies, reduces this chemokine signature and whether the impact of this reduction translates into reduced cardiovascular events. Because TRL elevation is common in the population, whereas high Lp(a) is rare, nonlinear MR approaches could define threshold effects and clinically actionable cutpoints for each lipoprotein. Together, these studies could determine whether the chemokine signature is simply a marker of lipoprotein exposure or a modifiable component of atherosclerotic disease.

Conclusions

Björnson et al2 identify a genetically anchored chemokine signature shared by 2 structurally distinct, highly atherogenic lipoproteins but absent in LDL. The findings do not yet demonstrate that chemokines account for the greater atherogenicity of TRL/remnants and Lp(a), nor that circulating chemokines themselves should be therapeutic targets. They do, however, challenge the assumption that all atherogenic apoB-containing particles communicate the same biological information to the artery. The key next step is to establish whether this circulating chemokine signature reflects local arterial chemokine activity or identifies a causal and safely targetable pathway in lipoprotein-driven atherosclerosis.
