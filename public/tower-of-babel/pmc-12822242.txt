Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS)-augmented calcicoptosis by calcium-based nanoparticles for enhanced immunogenic cell death induction in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Yang Gao, Ziting Xu, Yingshan Gao, Li Zhang, Yu Liang, Qiuyu Li, Minyi Liu, Haibo Lan, Yanyan Zhang, Bingxia Zhao, Yingjia Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) shows limited therapeutic efficacy due to the absence of effective targeted treatment options, with its immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment further diminishing the effectiveness of immunotherapy. Intracellular Ca²⁺ homeostasis plays a critical role in cancer progression, yet the efficacy of traditional inorganic nanoparticles in inducing calcicoptosis through Ca²⁺ release is constrained by poor water solubility, stability, and delivery efficiency. To address these challenges, we developed novel nanoparticles (NPs) by coordinating Ca²⁺ with the natural bioactive compound glycyrrhizic acid to create a multifunctional carrier (GC NPs), which was further loaded with the therapeutic natural compound curcumin (CUR) to form GC@CUR NPs. Through synergistic effects of Ca²⁺ overload and CUR-mediated regulation, these NPs significantly promote Ca²⁺ accumulation in mitochondria, triggering mitochondrial dysfunction and efficiently inducing calcicoptosis. Additionally, low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS)-mediated targeted release markedly improved antitumor effects. Importantly, this strategy effectively induces immunogenic cell death (ICD) in TNBC, thereby significantly boosting antitumor immune responses. This study provides an innovative and efficient strategy for integrating chemotherapy and immunotherapy to transform TNBC tumors into immunoresponsive ones, offering new possibilities for comprehensive TNBC treatment.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03914-w.Keywords: Calcicoptosis, Immunogenic cell death, Mitochondrial calcium ion overload, Curcumin, Low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS)

Introduction

Breast cancer is currently the most prevalent cancer among women and one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality in this population [1]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is the most aggressive and heterogeneous subtype of breast cancer. Due to the lack of effective targeted therapies, conventional chemotherapy remains the primary treatment modality for TNBC, resulting in limited therapeutic efficacy [2–4]. Although immunotherapy has shown efficacy in certain tumor types, its effectiveness in TNBC remains controversial [5, 6]. To date, immunotherapy using immune checkpoint inhibitors has not demonstrated improvements in overall survival for TNBC patients [7, 8]. This ineffectiveness may be attributed to TNBC being an immunosuppressive tumor with limited sensitivity to immunotherapy, which restricts the patient population that can benefit from such treatments [9–11]. Currently, emerging nanotherapeutic strategies for TNBC include the delivery of STING agonists or nanovaccines to amplify antitumor immune responses [12], as well as photo- or sonodynamic approaches that generate reactive species locally within tumors to enhance immunotherapy efficacy [13]. However, STING-based strategies still raise concerns about systemic inflammation, while optical activation is limited by tissue penetration depth. Therefore, it is imperative to develop novel therapeutic strategies using nanomaterials to reprogram the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Recently, a series of studies have systematically demonstrated the feasibility of modulating dendritic cell activity and remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment through a series of nanoplatform-based strategies, thereby achieving markedly enhanced antitumor immunotherapy outcomes [14–17]. These studies provide valuable insights into the potential of nanomaterial-based approaches for reprogramming the immunosuppressive microenvironment of TNBC.

Among the numerous ions in the human body, calcium ions (Ca²⁺), as the most abundant metal element, are critically regulated to maintain essential cellular functions [18, 19]. Abnormal Ca²⁺ accumulation in tumor cells disrupts mitochondrial function, leading to an excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Under oxidative stress, mitochondria further accumulate Ca²⁺, creating a vicious cycle that ultimately results in tumor cell calcicoptosis [20, 21]. This mode of cell death differs from classical forms of cell death, such as apoptosis or ferroptosis, and primarily results from calcium overload and mitochondrial dysfunction [22]. Moreover, calcicoptosis can induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), thereby recruiting and activating dendritic cells (DCs), enhancing the infiltration and activity of tumor-specific cytotoxic T cells, and significantly boosting antitumor immune responses against TNBC [23–25]. Therefore, modulating mitochondrial Ca²⁺ concentrations represents a highly promising strategy for cancer therapy.

Given the therapeutic potential of calcicoptosis, providing exogenous Ca²⁺ is a critical approach to realizing this strategy. Currently, exogenous Ca²⁺ is typically derived from inorganic nanomaterials, such as calcium carbonate (CaCO3), calcium peroxide (CaO2), and calcium phosphate (CaP) nanoparticles [26–28]. However, their water solubility, stability, and delivery efficiency require further optimization [29, 30]. To enhance biocompatibility, modifications with other metal elements or non-metal nanoparticles are often employed, but these increase the potential toxicity risks of calcium-based nanomaterials [31, 32]. In contrast, natural bioactive compounds, with their abundant availability and excellent biocompatibility, offer new possibilities for reducing the toxicity risks of nanomaterials. Glycyrrhizic acid (GA), the primary bioactive component extracted from licorice root, has been certified as safe by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [33]. Its amphiphilic molecular structure, consisting of two hydrophilic glucuronic acid residues and a hydrophobic aglycone, confers surface activity in aqueous solutions [34]. This property significantly enhances the bioavailability of lipophilic drugs, providing a new direction for optimizing calcium-based nanomaterials.

However, solely relying on exogenous Ca²⁺ for inducing calcicoptosis remains insufficient due to intracellular buffering systems that expel excess Ca²⁺ or store it in the endoplasmic reticulum [35, 36]. Therefore, a synergistic strategy combining exogenous Ca²⁺ delivery with the induction of endogenous calcium overload holds greater promise. Curcumin, a natural compound extracted from turmeric rhizomes, acts as a calcium channel inhibitor, blocking Ca²⁺ efflux and significantly increasing intracellular Ca²⁺ concentrations [37–39]. However, its hydrophobicity and low bioavailability limit its clinical application, necessitating high doses that may increase the risk of side effects [40]. To overcome these limitations, nanodrug delivery systems can encapsulate curcumin to enhance its solubility and bioavailability, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy and reducing toxicity risks.

One of the primary advantages of using nanodrug delivery systems is their ability to leverage the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect for drug delivery to tumors. However, high interstitial pressure and dense extracellular matrix at the tumor site may impede the release efficiency of nanodrugs [41]. Without controlled targeted release mechanisms, drugs may distribute systemically, exposing healthy tissues to high concentrations of drugs or nanoparticles, thereby increasing the risk of systemic toxicity [42, 43]. Unlike traditional methods that utilize microbubble-induced cavitation [44], recent studies have shown that low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (LIPUS) can significantly enhance the targeted release efficiency of drugs from nanoparticles carriers through non-cavitation effects, such as acoustic radiation force (ARF) and acoustic streaming [45–47]. Implementing LIPUS technology for targeted release of nanoparticles at the tumor site not only reduces metabolic exposure but also improves drug release efficiency in the target region, thereby optimizing therapeutic outcomes. Due to the pronounced heterogeneity in the spatial distribution of biochemical features across different tumor types, pH- or ROS-responsive release systems sometimes exhibit unpredictable and poorly controllable drug release behavior [48, 49]. In contrast, LIPUS enables precise regulation of both the rate and the spatial location of drug release by adjusting acoustic intensity, pulse frequency, and irradiation duration, thereby markedly enhancing the targeting specificity and safety of drug delivery [50, 51]. Therefore, the incorporation of LIPUS offers a distinct advantage for achieving safe, precise, and controllable tumor-targeted drug release.

Herein, we utilized the natural bioactive compound GA and Ca²⁺ to fabricate GC NPs, and incorporated curcumin (CUR), a plant-derived compound, as a regulator of intracellular calcium homeostasis, to construct GC@CUR NPs. Upon entering tumor cells, the nanoparticles release substantial amounts of Ca²⁺ and CUR. CUR inhibits calcium efflux, synergizing with exogenous Ca²⁺ to rapidly elevate cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ concentrations, triggering mitochondrial calcium overload and impairing mitochondrial function, ultimately inducing calcicoptosis. Concurrently, this process activates ICD, releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which enhance the infiltration and activation of tumor-specific cytotoxic T cells, eliciting a more effective antitumor immune response against TNBC. Furthermore, ultrasound targeting the tumor region enhances the release of CUR, amplifying their antitumor effects. In summary, this approach induces calcicoptosis in TNBC while promoting immune activation, thereby enhancing the efficacy of combined chemotherapy and immunotherapy (Fig. 1).

Results

Synthesis and characterization of GC@CUR NPs

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the preparation of GC@CUR NPs involves two main steps: the synthesis of GC NPs using glycyrrhizic acid and Ca²⁺, followed by the loading of CUR to form GC@CUR NPs. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that the GC@CUR NPs exhibit a monodisperse, spherical morphology with a uniform size of approximately 87.61 ± 6.5 nm (Fig. 2A). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements further confirmed a hydrodynamic size of 114.1 ± 1.47 nm and a polydispersity index of 0.031 (Fig. 2B). The difference between DLS and TEM is expected due to the presence of the hydration layer. To evaluate the dispersion and stability of GC@CUR NPs, their hydrodynamic size and zeta potential were monitored in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) over 10 days, showing no statistically significant changes (Fig. 2C). These results demonstrate the excellent stability of GC@CUR NPs. Additionally, the stability and dispersity of GC NPs were evaluated (Supplementary Figure S1). The hydrodynamic diameter of GC NPs was determined to be 80.54 ± 2.38 nm, which increased slightly upon curcumin loading but remained stable. The zeta potential shifted from − 21.08 ± 0.68 mV for GC NPs to -33.52 ± 1.95 mV for GC@CUR NPs, likely due to the non-covalent incorporation of curcumin via hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding. To confirm the successful incorporation of Ca²⁺ and CUR, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis detected Ca 2p₁/₂ and Ca 2p₃/₂ peaks at approximately 351.1 eV and 347.4 eV, respectively (Fig. 2D and E). Additionally, UV-vis absorption spectroscopy of GC@CUR NPs exhibited characteristic peaks at 252 nm and 424 nm, respectively. These peaks correspond to glycyrrhizic acid (GA) at 250 nm and CUR at 425 nm, showing a shift that may be attributed to peak position changes induced by the NPs structure (Fig. 2F). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading efficiency (LE) of CUR, determined using UV-vis standard curves (Fig. 2G), were 77.90 ± 2.9% and 23.07 ± 2.2%, respectively. In addition, the ability of US to trigger drug release from GC@CUR NPs was investigated in vitro. After US exposure, the fluorescence intensity of CUR significantly increased, indicating that US irradiation enhanced CUR release [52] (Fig. 2H). The drug release behavior of GC@CUR NPs was investigated under tumor-relevant pH (6.5), physiological pH (7.4) (Fig. 2I), and in medium containing 10% FBS (Supplementary Figure S2). The results demonstrated that at pH 6.5, the cumulative release of CUR reached 78.26 ± 1.19% at 24 h post-US stimulation, significantly higher than the 41.63 ± 3.80% observed without US stimulation. These findings suggest that US effectively triggers drug release from GC@CUR NPs, enhancing the therapeutic potential of GC@CUR NPs in tumor microenvironments.

In vitro uptake and cytotoxicity assessment

To determine the optimal US intensity, the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was used to assess the effects of varying acoustic intensities on 4T1 cell viability under consistent US parameters (1.0 MHz, 60% duty cycle, 1 min). The results showed that at 0.6 W/cm², 4T1 cells maintained cell membrane integrity and basic biological functions, whereas higher intensities caused irreversible damage (Supplementary Figure S3). This finding provided a critical reference for selecting US conditions in subsequent experiments. Next, the cytotoxicity of GC@CUR NPs was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. The results demonstrated that GC@CUR NPs significantly inhibited 4T1 cell proliferation in a concentration-dependent manner, with cell viability decreasing as the concentration increased. Compared to the GC@CUR NPs group at equivalent concentrations, the GC@CUR NPs + US group exhibited greater cytotoxicity (Fig. 3A and B and Supplementary Figure S4). This synergistic effect suggests that US enhances drug release from GC@CUR NPs, thereby improving their ability to kill 4T1 cells. To further investigate the potential risks of GC@CUR NPs and their combination with US on normal cells, CCK-8 assays were conducted using 3T3 fibroblasts, a representative normal cell line (Supplementary Figure S5). The results revealed that GC@CUR NPs exhibited significantly lower cytotoxicity against 3T3 cells compared to 4T1 cancer cells. Specifically, at the same concentration (20 µg/mL), the cell viability of 3T3 cells treated with GC@CUR NPs alone was approximately 84.6%, whereas that of 4T1 cells was 50.2%. When combined with US, the viability of 3T3 cells was 72.3%, compared to only 29.0% for 4T1 cells. These findings indicate that both GC@CUR NPs alone and in combination with ultrasound have minimal impact on normal cell viability, demonstrating favorable selectivity for cancer cells. This reduced cytotoxicity toward normal cells suggests that the proposed therapeutic approach holds significant potential for targeted cancer treatment while exerting limited adverse effects on healthy tissues. Moreover, given the high dependence of cardiomyocytes and neurons on calcium signaling, we will further evaluate potential effects on the cardiac and nervous systems in future studies to ensure safety.

To investigate cellular uptake of GC@CUR NPs, which is critical for tumor suppression, fluorescence microscopy (FM) and flow cytometry (FCM) were used to track CUR fluorescence (Supplementary Figure S6). In treated cells, clear CUR fluorescence signals were observed, confirming efficient uptake of GC@CUR NPs. Moreover, compared to cells treated with GC@CUR NPs or CUR alone, the GC@CUR NPs + US group exhibited significantly stronger fluorescence intensity. This indicates that US markedly enhances CUR release efficiency, thereby increasing its intracellular accumulation. FCM analysis further confirmed these findings.

Detection and analysis of Ca2+ and ROS in vitro

To investigate the dynamics of intracellular Ca²⁺ levels following GC@CUR NPs uptake, Fluo-4 AM probes were used to track and quantify Ca²⁺ concentrations. As shown in Fig. 3C and D, and Supplementary Figure S7, neither the GC nor CUR groups exhibited significant Ca²⁺ accumulation compared to the control group. In contrast, the GC@CUR NPs group displayed substantial Ca²⁺ accumulation, indicating that the synergistic interaction between CUR and Ca²⁺ effectively disrupts the cell’s ability to regulate intracellular Ca²⁺ levels. Furthermore, to explore the impact of US on Ca²⁺ dynamics, we compared the GC@CUR NPs + US group with the GC@CUR NPs group. The results revealed significantly higher Ca²⁺ accumulation in the GC@CUR NPs + US group, demonstrating that US accelerates intracellular Ca²⁺ influx. Given the substantial Ca²⁺ accumulation induced by GC@CUR NPs + US, we hypothesized that elevated Ca²⁺ levels might enter mitochondria and disrupt mitochondrial Ca²⁺ homeostasis. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was used to examine the distribution of Ca²⁺ in mitochondria, with MitoRed (red fluorescence) specifically labeling mitochondria, DAPI (blue fluorescence) staining the nucleus, and green fluorescent Ca²⁺ indicating its distribution. As shown in Fig. 3E, the control group showed minimal Ca²⁺ accumulation with a Manders’ colocalization coefficient (MCC) of 0.390 ± 0.006, whereas the GC@CUR NPs + US group exhibited significant overlap between Ca²⁺ distribution and mitochondrial locations with the MCC of 0.863 ± 0.027, confirming that Ca²⁺ generated by GC@CUR NPs + US effectively accumulates in mitochondria.

Since elevated intracellular Ca²⁺ levels are often associated with oxidative stress, we used DCFH-DA probes to measure ROS production via FM and FCM. As shown in Supplementary Figure S8, the control group displayed weak green fluorescence, whereas the GC@CUR NPs group exhibited bright green fluorescence, indicating that the synergistic effect of Ca²⁺ and CUR induces significant ROS production through calcium overload. Notably, ROS fluorescence was even higher in the GC@CUR NPs + US group, suggesting that US exacerbates calcium overload and further enhances ROS production. Given that mitochondrial Ca²⁺ accumulation may disrupt mitochondrial Ca²⁺ homeostasis, we used the MitoSOX probe (red fluorescence) to detect mitochondrial ROS (mtROS). Consistent with the trend of cellular ROS, the GC@CUR NPs + US group showed strong red mitochondrial signals, with FCM analysis revealing fluorescence intensities approximately 2.2 times and 4.3 times higher than those of the GC@CUR NPs and control groups, respectively (Fig. 3F and G, and Supplementary Figure S9). These findings indicate that mitochondrial Ca²⁺ imbalance leads to excessive mtROS production, exacerbating cellular redox imbalance. To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic efficacy of GC@CUR NPs, we employed 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO), a ROS scavenger, and 1,2-bis(o-aminophenoxy)ethane-N, N,N’,N’-tetraacetic acid acetoxymethyl ester (BAPTA-AM), a Ca²⁺ chelator, to confirm the dependence on ROS generation and Ca²⁺ overload. The results demonstrated that both inhibitors significantly reduced the ROS and Ca²⁺ overload induced by GC@CUR NPs in 4T1 cells (Supplementary Figure S10 and S11). These findings confirmed that ROS generation and Ca²⁺ overload are critical mediators of the cell death induced by GC@CUR NPs.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and calcicoptosis induced by GC@CUR NPs

Excessive intracellular ROS can trigger oxidative stress, leading to mitochondria-mediated cellular damage [20]. Given the strong correlation between elevated Ca²⁺ levels and mitochondrial ROS production, we further investigated the mitochondrial dysfunction induced by GC@CUR NPs + US. Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP, ΔΨm) is a key biomarker of mitochondrial function, and its loss may indicate changes in mitochondrial membrane permeability [53]. Here, MMP changes were assessed using JC-1 staining. At high MMP, JC-1 accumulates in the mitochondrial matrix, forming red-fluorescent aggregates. Conversely, at low MMP, JC-1 exists as green-fluorescent monomers. As shown in Fig. 4A and the corresponding fluorescence quantification data (Supplementary Figure S12), the ratio of red-fluorescent aggregates to green-fluorescent monomers in the GC@CUR NPs group was significantly reduced, indicating that Ca²⁺ and CUR synergistically exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction. Notably, in the GC@CUR NPs + US group, red-fluorescent aggregates were nearly undetectable, exhibiting the lowest ratio among all groups. This suggests that US further intensifies the loss of MMP, markedly aggravating mitochondrial dysfunction. To further quantify these observations, FCM analysis revealed that 92.20 ± 0.52% of cells in the GC@CUR NPs + US group exhibited a negative MMP state, confirming the enhanced damage to MMP caused by US-assisted treatment (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, mitochondrial distribution was assessed using MitoTracker Red staining (Fig. 4C and D). As expected, the GC@CUR NPs group showed a significantly lower mitochondrial count compared to the control group, indicating that GC@CUR NPs compromise mitochondrial structural integrity. In the GC@CUR NPs + US group, mitochondrial fluorescence signals were further diminished, with the lowest mitochondrial count, reflecting severe mitochondrial damage.

To explore the ultimate impact of GC@CUR NPs + US on cell fate, apoptosis was evaluated using FCM (Fig. 4E and F). The results showed that GC@CUR NPs treatment significantly increased mortality to approximately 35.40 ± 3.0%. With US exposure, the mortality rate in the GC@CUR NPs + US group rose markedly to 74.76 ± 1.91%. These findings demonstrate that GC@CUR NPs + US effectively induces mitochondrial Ca²⁺ overload in 4T1 cells, leading to excessive ROS production, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ultimately triggering calcicoptosis. In addition, we employed the calcium chelator BAPTA-AM to alleviate intracellular Ca²⁺ overload and used the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 to evaluate the contribution of ferroptosis to 4T1 cell death induced by GC@CUR NPs treatment. The experiments showed that Fer-1 failed to restore cell viability, whereas BAPTA-AM reduced cell death, confirming the critical role of Ca²⁺ overload in calcicoptosis (Supplementary Figure S13).

In vitro ICD-mediated by GC@CUR NPs

Calcium-based nanoparticles delivery platforms can synergistically combine calcium overload therapy with immunotherapy. Based on this, we hypothesized that calcicoptosis induced by GC@CUR NPs + US could trigger immune responses by releasing DAMPs, such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP), high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and calreticulin (CRT). To verify this, CLSM was used to assess the expression of CRT and HMGB1, two representative DAMPs. The results showed that GC@CUR NPs + US treatment significantly increased CRT fluorescence, with an intensity approximately 5.34 times higher than that of the control group (Fig. 5A and C). This indicates that intracellular Ca²⁺ overload promotes CRT translocation from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the cell membrane, markedly increasing surface CRT exposure, which serves as an “eat-me” signal to stimulate DC phagocytosis [54]. In contrast, HMGB1 fluorescence was reduced in cells treated with GC@CUR NPs, and even more significantly diminished in the GC@CUR NPs + US group, with an intensity approximately 0.36 times that of the control group (Fig. 5B and D), suggesting nuclear-to-cytoplasmic translocation and subsequent extracellular release of HMGB1, which acts as a proinflammatory signal to activate DCs. To further validate the extracellular release of HMGB1, we performed an ELISA to quantify HMGB1 levels in the supernatant of GC@CUR NPs + US group at various time points (Supplementary Figure S14). The results demonstrated that HMGB1 release increased progressively over time, reaching a peak at 24 h. In addition, Extracellular ATP levels, a key hallmark of ICD induction, were quantified to further validate the immunogenic effects. Compared to the control group, ATP levels in the GC@CUR NPs and GC@CUR NPs + US groups increased by approximately 5.23 and 6.33 times, respectively (Supplementary Figure S15), indicating robust extracellular ATP secretion that functions as a chemoattractant to recruit and activate DCs. These findings demonstrate that GC@CUR NPs + US effectively induces ICD in 4T1 cells by releasing DAMPs, with CRT promoting phagocytosis, HMGB1 enhancing proinflammatory signaling, and ATP facilitating DC recruitment and activation [55]. Consequently, we hypothesized that GC@CUR NPs + US could enhance DC maturation, thereby promoting robust anti-tumor immune responses (Fig. 5E and F). FCM analysis revealed that while CUR or GC@CUR NPs alone increased the proportion of mature DCs, the GC@CUR NPs + US group exhibited the highest proportion of mature DCs (CD11c⁺CD80⁺CD86⁺), reaching 25.9 ± 3.5%. Collectively, GC@CUR NPs + US induces DC maturation by enhancing CRT surface exposure, HMGB1 extracellular release, and ATP secretion, thereby activating DCs through distinct yet complementary immunostimulatory mechanisms.

In vivo biodistribution, US-guided drug release, and biosafety

To investigate the accumulation and distribution of GC@CUR NPs at tumor sites in mice, we evaluated their ability to effectively enrich in tumors. For this purpose, CUR was replaced with IR780, a strongly fluorescent dye, to form GC@IR780 NPs. In vivo imaging system (IVIS) analysis revealed a significantly elevated fluorescence signal in tumors 24 h after intravenous injection of GC@IR780 NPs (Fig. 6A and B). Consequently, in subsequent in vivo imaging and anti-tumor studies, US was applied to the tumor site 24 h post-administration. To comprehensively assess the biodistribution of GC NPs, mice were euthanized 24 h post-injection, and ex vivo fluorescence imaging was performed on major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) and tumor tissues. The results showed that fluorescence intensity in tumor tissues was significantly higher than in other organs (Supplementary Figure S16). Notably, the kidneys and liver, as metabolic organs, exhibited mild fluorescence, while other organs (heart and lungs) displayed relatively weak signals. These findings collectively demonstrate that GC NPs enable effective drug accumulation in tumors.

To determine whether US enhances drug accumulation at tumor sites, we first evaluated the fluorescence intensity of GC@IR780 NPs under US stimulation in vitro. As shown in Supplementary Figure S17, US treatment significantly increased the fluorescence intensity of GC@IR780 NPs, indicating that US promotes the release of IR780 from NPs in vitro. This effect was further validated in vivo, where the fluorescence signal in the right tumor (without US) remained unchanged, suggesting limited release of GC@IR780 NPs without external stimulation. In contrast, the left tumor, treated with US, exhibited a significantly enhanced fluorescence signal, confirming that US effectively promotes the release of NP-loaded drugs in vivo (Supplementary Figure S18). To quantify the impact of US on the in vivo release of GC@CUR NPs, the concentrations of CUR and Ca²⁺ in tumor tissues were analyzed with and without US treatment. As shown in Supplementary Figure S19, US treatment significantly increased CUR accumulation in tumors by approximately 1.36 times compared to the non-US-treated group. Similarly, ICP-MS analysis revealed that US treatment enhanced Ca²⁺ accumulation in tumors by approximately 1.22 times compared to the non-US-treated group (Supplementary Figure S20), further underscoring the critical role of US in enhancing drug and ion release. Additionally, biodistribution analysis using ICP-MS quantified Ca²⁺ levels in tumors and major organs (liver, spleen, kidney, heart, and lung), demonstrating the highest Ca²⁺ accumulation in tumors (Supplementary Figure S21). Prior to tumor suppression experiments, an additional experiment was conducted including an US-only group to ensure that US alone does not induce tumor cell killing or tissue damage. The results showed no statistically significant differences in tumor volume between the control and US-only groups, confirming the safety of the applied US parameters (Supplementary Figure S22).

Additionally, a hemolysis assay was conducted to assess the biocompatibility of GC@CUR NPs. The results revealed no significant hemolytic activity across all tested concentrations, with a hemolysis rate of only approximately 1.70% at a high concentration of 200 µg/mL (Supplementary Figure S23), confirming the excellent hemocompatibility of GC@CUR NPs. Finally, the biosafety of GC@CUR NPs + US was evaluated. Compared to the control group, the GC@CUR NPs + US group showed no significant changes in alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CRE), or blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels, indicating no adverse effects on liver or kidney function (Supplementary Figure S24). Histological analysis of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) using H&E staining further confirmed no apparent toxicity or adverse effects from GC@CUR NPs + US treatment (Supplementary Figure S25). Collectively, these results demonstrate the enhanced drug and ion delivery facilitated by US, alongside the favorable biocompatibility and biosafety of GC@CUR NPs + US in vivo.

In vivo antitumor effects

To further investigate the anti-tumor efficacy of GC@CUR NPs + US in vivo, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were treated according to an established protocol. During the treatment period, all groups exhibited a slight increase in body weight (Fig. 6E), indicating that GC@CUR NPs at the experimental dose caused no significant systemic toxicity and demonstrated favorable biosafety. Post-treatment tumor morphology observations were consistent with tumor volume measurements (Fig. 6C and D). Compared to the control group, the GC NPs and CUR groups showed moderate tumor suppression, while the GC@CUR NPs group exhibited significantly greater inhibition of tumor growth than either the GC NPs or CUR group alone. This suggested that the synergistic induction of Ca²⁺ overload by Ca²⁺ and CUR enhanced tumor suppression. Most notably, the GC@CUR NPs + US group achieved the strongest anti-tumor effect, with a tumor growth inhibition rate of 93.03%. These results highlight the potent in vivo anti-tumor efficacy of GC@CUR NPs + US.

H&E staining revealed densely packed and morphologically intact tumor cells in the control group, whereas the GC@CUR NPs + US group displayed a reduced cell count and the most severe cellular damage. Immunofluorescence staining for Ki67 and TUNEL confirmed this trend, showing significantly suppressed proliferation and markedly increased apoptosis in 4T1 cells in the GC@CUR NPs + US group (Fig. 6F). Collectively, these findings underscore the success of GC@CUR NPs + US in inhibiting tumor growth. In addition, to further validate the involvement of mitochondrial dysfunction related to calcium overload in vivo, we performed immunofluorescence staining for cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (COXIV), a marker of mitochondrial structural integrity, in tumor tissues. The results showed a reduced and disorganized COXIV signal in GC@CUR + US group compared to the control group, indicating mitochondrial damage and dysfunction consistent with calcicoptosis (Supplementary Figure S26).

In vivo ICD and related tumor immune responses mediated by GC@CUR NPs

Building on the excellent anti-tumor efficacy observed in vivo and the effective induction of ICD at the cellular level, we further investigated the ability of GC@CUR NPs combined with US to trigger immune responses in vivo. Immunofluorescence analysis of tumor tissue sections revealed significantly increased CRT exposure in the GC@CUR NPs + US group (Fig. 7A). CRT translocation from the endoplasmic reticulum to the cell membrane enables binding to specific receptors on dendritic cells DCs, thereby promoting DC phagocytic activity and subsequent maturation. To assess DC maturation, FCM analysis was performed, demonstrating that the GC@CUR NPs + US group exhibited significantly higher DC maturation compared to other groups (Fig. 7B and C). Mature DCs present antigens to T cells, initiating adaptive immune responses characterized by the production of tumor-specific T lymphocytes (CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells). FCM analysis of spleen cells showed that the GC@CUR NPs + US group had 1.81-fold and 2.35-fold increases in CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell populations, respectively, compared to the GC@CUR NPs group, reflecting stronger immune activation (Fig. 7D and E, and 7F). To confirm whether T cells were effectively recruited to the tumor site, immunofluorescence staining was used to evaluate the infiltration of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells in tumor tissues. As expected, the GC@CUR NPs + US group exhibited the highest infiltration levels of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 7G and Supplementary Figure S27), indicating enhanced recruitment and proliferation of these immune cells at the tumor site, likely due to US-mediated enhanced drug release and more effective DAMP exposure, which activates local immune responses. Furthermore, we analyzed the expression of forkhead box P3 (Foxp3), a marker of regulatory T cells (Tregs), in tumor tissues from treated mice. The results revealed a significant reduction in Foxp3 expression in the GC@CUR + US group compared to the control group, (Supplementary Figure S28). This reduction suggests that GC@CUR + US treatment may suppress immunosuppressive Treg activity, thereby enhancing anti-tumor immune responses in the tumor microenvironment.

(G) Representative immunofluorescence images of CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues of each treatment group. Scale bar: 100 μm. The level of (H) IFN-, (I) IL-6 and (J) TNF-α in different treatment groups. Representative images from three biologically independent samples are shown. (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001)

Finally, to evaluate immune-mediated tumor-killing effects, levels of typical pro-inflammatory cytokines secreted by mature DCs and activated T cells were measured. As shown in Fig. 7H and I, and 7J, serum levels of IFN-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α in the GC@CUR NPs + US group were significantly higher than in other groups. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GC@CUR NPs + US effectively triggers ICD in vivo, eliciting robust anti-tumor immune responses.

Conclusion

To address the challenges of limited therapeutic efficacy and immunosuppressive TME in TNBC, we designed and developed a novel NP based on natural bioactive compounds, named GC@CUR NPs. This platform significantly improves TNBC treatment outcomes by synergistically inducing calcicoptosis and activating immune responses. Furthermore, by LIPUS for targeted drug release, GC@CUR NPs optimize drug delivery efficiency at tumor sites, enhancing both anti-tumor efficacy and immunogenicity. In conclusion, the combination of a natural bioactive compound-based NP with LIPUS technology not only demonstrates excellent biosafety but also amplifies therapeutic effects, providing new insights and robust experimental evidence for precision TNBC therapy.

Materials and methods

Materials

Calcium chloride (CaCl2) and glycyrrhizic acid (GA) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). Curcumin (CUR) was purchased from Acmec Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK8) was purchased from Biosharp (Beijing, China). Dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), Fluo-4AM, JC-1 Detection Kit, ATP Assay Kit, and Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). RPMI-1640 medium, 0.05% trypsin-EDTA, fetal bovine serum (FBS) and Penicillin-Streptomycin were purchased from Gibco (USA). CRT and HMGB1 antibodies were acquired from Cell Signaling Technology (USA). Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (Alexa Fluor® 647) were purchased from Abcam (UK), FITC-conjugated anti-mouse CD80 antibody, APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 antibody, PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD11c antibody, APC-Cy7-conjugated anti-mouse CD45 antibody, FITC-conjugated anti-mouse CD3 antibody, PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD4 antibody, and APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD8 antibody, were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA).

Cell lines and animals

The 4T1 mouse breast cancer cell line was obtained from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (China), with its use approved by the Laboratory Animal Management Committee of Nanfang Hospital (Approval No. IACUC-LAC-20240415-003). 4T1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin, and streptomycin. The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, 1% O₂, and 94% N₂. Female BALB/c mice (4–6 weeks old) were sourced from the Animal Laboratory of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China).

Synthesis of GC@CUR NPs

The preparation of GC@CUR NPs was adapted and modified from previous studies [56]. To synthesize GC NPs, 0.6 g of GA and 0.2 g of CaCl₂ were separately dissolved in 10 mL of double-distilled water. The GA solution was added dropwise to the CaCl₂ solution under magnetic stirring until the mixture turned milky white, indicating the formation of GC NPs. The resulting solution was centrifuged, washed three times with double-distilled water, and the precipitate was lyophilized in a vacuum freeze dryer for subsequent use. To prepare GC@CUR NPs, GC powder (2 mg/mL) was dissolved in PBS, and CUR (0.8 mg/mL) was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol. The GC and CUR solutions were mixed dropwise and stirred for 24 h. The organic solvent was then removed by rotary evaporation, and the product was hydrated for 30 min. The final GC@CUR NPs solution was collected by centrifugation and stored at 4 °C in the dark.

Characterization

Morphological and elemental analyses of GC@CUR NPs were conducted using various analytical techniques. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging was performed with a JEOL JEM-2100 F TEM. Elemental calcium content was quantified using an Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometer (ICP-OES). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was carried out on a Thermo ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer. Hydrodynamic size and surface charge were determined using a Zetasizer Nano ZS-90 (Malvern, UK). Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectra were recorded with a Shimadzu UV-vis spectrophotometer. Drug loading efficiency (LE) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) were calculated using the following formulas: LE (%) = (weight of encapsulated CUR / weight of GC@CUR NPs) × 100; EE (%) = (weight of encapsulated CUR / weight of feeding CUR) × 100.

Ultrasound experimental parameters

For in vitro experiments, US was applied at an intensity of 0.6 W/cm², frequency of 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, and 1-minute duration, administered at the 4th hour of treatment. For in vivo experiments, US was applied at an intensity of 1.5 W/cm², frequency of 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, and 2-minute duration, administered 24 h post-injection.

In vitro drug release

To evaluate the drug release profile of GC@CUR NPs, suspensions (1 mg/mL) were prepared with and without US treatment (1.5 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 2 min duration, applied at 0 h). These suspensions were enclosed in dialysis bags and immersed in 30 mL of PBS with continuous agitation at room temperature. At designated time points (2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, and 30 h), 4 mL of PBS was sampled to quantify CUR content, and an equivalent volume of fresh PBS was replenished after each sampling.

To further investigate the effect of US on CUR release, fluorescence changes were examined under different conditions. Briefly, a thin-walled polypropylene tube containing 0.5 mL of GC@CUR NPs suspension was placed in the focal zone of a US transducer. Before and after US treatment (1.5 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 2 min duration), fluorescence spectra of the samples were measured in the range of 500–700 nm using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (RF-6000, Shimadzu, Japan).

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

To investigate the impact of US on 4T1 cell damage, cells were treated with US at varying intensities (1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1 min duration, applied at the 4th hour). Cell viability was subsequently measured using the CCK-8 assay.

The cytotoxic effects of GC@CUR NPs on 4T1 cells were evaluated by seeding cells at a standard density in 96-well plates and culturing them under controlled conditions (5% CO₂, 1% O₂, 94% N₂, 37 °C). After cell adherence, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing GC@CUR NPs. Following a 24-hour exposure, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay.

In vitro cellular uptake

In

4T1 cells were incubated for 6 h with PBS, CUR (20 µg/mL), GC@CUR NPs, or GC@CUR NPs + US (0.6 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1 min duration, applied at the 4th hour). Fluorescence intensity was subsequently measured using flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa™ Cell Analyzer, USA) and fluorescence microscopy (Nikon Eclipse Ti-U, Tokyo, Japan).

Intracellular calcium ion content assessment

Intracellular free calcium ion concentrations in 4T1 cells were quantified using the Fluo-4 AM probe. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and subjected to the following treatments: (1) PBS; (2) GC NPs ([Ca]: 10 µg/mL); (3) CUR (20 µg/mL); (4) GC@CUR NPs (CUR: 20 µg/mL); (5) GC@CUR NPs + US; (0.6 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1 min duration, applied at the 4th hour). After a 6-hour incubation, cells were stained with Fluo-4 AM and analyzed for fluorescence signals indicative of calcium ion levels using fluorescence microscopy. Fluorescence intensity was further quantified by flow cytometry.

To assess mitochondrial calcium ion, 4T1 cells were treated with PBS, GC NPs, CUR, GC@CUR NPs, or GC@CUR NPs + US for 6 h and subsequently stained with Fluo-4 AM, DAPI, and MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Beyotime, China). Fluo-4 AM fluorescence was visualized using CLSM.

Intracellular ROS analysis

4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with PBS, GC NPs, CUR, GC@CUR NPs, or GC@CUR NPs + US (0.6 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1 min duration, applied at the 4th hour). After a 6-hour incubation, cells were stained with DCFH-DA probe for 30 min in the dark. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and fluorescence intensity was quantified by flow cytometry.

Mitochondrial superoxide detection

4T1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured at 37 °C under controlled conditions (5% CO₂, 1% O₂, 94% N₂). After adherence, cells were treated with PBS, GC NPs, CUR, GC@CUR NPs, or GC@CUR NPs + US (0.6 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1 min duration, applied at the 4th hour) for 6h . Following incubation, cells were stained with MitoSOX Red probe (Beyotime, China) to assess mitochondrial superoxide levels. Fluorescence signals were visualized using a CLSM and quantified by flow cytometry.

Assessment of mitochondrial function

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), a key indicator of mitochondrial function, was assessed using the JC-1 detection kit. 4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with PBS, GC NPs, CUR, GC@CUR NPs, or GC@CUR NPs + US (0.6 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1 min duration, applied at the 4th hour). After a 24-hour incubation, cells were stained with JC-1 and analyzed for fluorescence signals using CLSM and flow cytometry.

To evaluate mitochondrial distribution, 4T1 cells were subjected to the same treatments (PBS, GC NPs, CUR, GC@CUR NPs, or GC@CUR NPs + US) for 24 h and subsequently stained with DAPI and MitoTracker Red CMXRos (Beyotime, China). Following washing with PBS, mitochondrial distribution was visualized using CLSM.

Analysis of cell apoptosis

4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h under controlled conditions (5% CO₂, 1% O₂, 94% N₂, 37 °C). Cells were then treated with PBS, GC NPs, CUR, GC@CUR NPs, or GC@CUR NPs + US (0.6 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1 min duration, applied at the 4th hour). After a 24-hour incubation, cell apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin V-FITC/PI staining kit and analyzed by flow cytometry.

In vitro CRT exposure and HMGB-1 release

4T1 cells were seeded in confocal dishes and cultured overnight under controlled conditions (5% CO₂, 1% O₂, 94% N₂, 37 °C). Cells were then treated with PBS, GC NPs, CUR, GC@CUR NPs, or GC@CUR NPs + US (0.6 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1 min duration, applied at the 4th hour) for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and blocked with 5% BSA. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies against CRT and HMGB1 at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies and DAPI staining. Fluorescence images were acquired using CLSM.

Assessment of extracellular ATP level

To quantify extracellular ATP levels, 4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h under controlled conditions (5% CO₂, 1% O₂, 94% N₂, 37 °C). Cells were then treated with PBS, GC NPs, CUR, GC@CUR NPs, or GC@CUR NPs + US (0.6 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1 min duration, applied at the 4th hour) for 24 h. The supernatant was collected and analyzed for extracellular ATP levels using an ATP assay kit.

In vitro DCs maturation detection

BMDCs were generated from bone marrow cells isolated from the femurs and tibias of BALB/c mice. The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 20 ng/mL GM-CSF, and 10 ng/mL IL-4 for 7 days to induce differentiation into immature BMDCs. 4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with PBS, GC NPs, CUR, GC@CUR NPs, or GC@CUR NPs + US (0.6 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 1 min duration, applied at the 4th hour). After a 24-hour incubation, the supernatant from treated 4T1 cells was collected and co-incubated with BMDCs for 24 h. BMDCs were then stained with anti-CD11c, anti-CD80, and anti-CD86 antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and analyzed for maturation by flow cytometry.

The biodistribution of GC@CUR in vivo

To investigate the in vivo biodistribution of GC@CUR NPs, in vivo imaging system (IVIS) was employed to track their distribution in a 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse model. Due to its strong fluorescence properties, the dye IR780 was used as a substitute for CUR in GC@CUR NPs. In vivo imaging was performed at 0-, 3-, 6-, 12-, 24-, and 48-hours post-administration. For ex vivo biodistribution analysis, tumor-bearing mice were euthanized 24 h after injection, and major organs and tumors were collected and imaged.

The effect of US in promoting drug accumulation in vivo

4T1 cancer cells were injected into both flanks of BALB/c mice (n = 3) to establish a bilateral tumor model. After 24 h, GC@IR780 NPs was administered via tail vein injection. Subsequently, the left tumor was treated with US (1.5 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 2 min), while the right tumor served as an untreated control. IVIS was then performed to evaluate drug distribution in the tumor tissues.

A single dose of GC@CUR NPs was administered via tail vein injection in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice model. For the US-treated group, US with the same parameters as above was applied 24 h post-injection. Tumors were then excised, homogenized, and extracted with methanol overnight. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and its fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader. The CUR content in the tumor tissues was calculated based on a standard curve of CUR fluorescence intensity.

In vivo biosafety analysis

The in vivo biosafety of GC@CUR NPs was evaluated by measuring liver function markers (ALT and AST) and kidney function markers (BUN and CREA) in serum from treated mice. Additionally, major organs (heart, lungs, liver, spleen, and kidneys) were harvested and examined using H&E staining for histopathological analysis.

Hemolysis assay

To assess hemocompatibility, a 200 µL red blood cell suspension was mixed with varying concentrations of GC@CUR NPs in PBS and incubated at 37 °C for 6 h. After centrifugation, the supernatants were collected and analyzed for hemoglobin release at 542 nm using UV-Vis spectrophotometry.

In vivo antitumor efficacy

4T1 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 4): (1) PBS, (2) GC NPs, (3) CUR, (4) GC@CUR NPs, and (5) GC@CUR NPs + US. Each group received tail vein injections on days 1, 4, 7, and 10, with a CUR equivalent dose of 10.0 mg/kg. Specifically, in the GC@CUR + US group, US treatment (1.5 W/cm², 1.0 MHz, 50% duty cycle, 2 min) was applied 24 h after each GC@CUR injection. Tumor dimensions and body weight were monitored every 3 days, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: volume = length × (width²) / 2. On day 12, mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and processed for histological analysis, including H&E staining and immunofluorescence (TUNEL and Ki-67 assays).

Evaluation of immunomodulatory effects in vivo

4T1 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into five groups, as described previously. On day 9 post-injection, spleens were collected, and single-cell suspensions were prepared. These cells were stained with anti-CD86, anti-CD11c, and anti-CD80 to assess the frequency of mature DCs, and with anti-CD45, anti-CD3, anti-CD4, and anti-CD8 to evaluate the frequency of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells using flow cytometry.

Concurrently, tumor sections were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and cut into slices for immunofluorescence staining. The staining targeted CRT and CD4+ and CD8+ T cells to detect immune infiltration within the tumor microenvironment.

In vivo cytokine detection

Serum was collected from 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, previously divided into five treatment groups as described earlier, for cytokine analysis. The concentrations of IFN-γ, IL-6, and TNF-α were measured using an ELISA kit (Meimian, Jiangsu, China) to evaluate the immune response.

Statistical analysis

The student’s T test and the one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to analyze the significance using Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software). P values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. ns indicates P>0.05; * indicates P<0.05; ** indicates P<0.01; *** indicates P<0.001.
