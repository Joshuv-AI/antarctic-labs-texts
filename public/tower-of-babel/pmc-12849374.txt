Cooperation between architectural C2H2 proteins in CP190 recruitment to Drosophila regulatory elements
Authors: Oksana Maksimenko, Natalia Klimenko, Ghadeer Salloum, Anastasia Umnova, Olga Kyrchanova, Larisa Melnikova, Pavel Georgiev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCTCF (CCCTC-binding factor) is the best-studied architectural protein that is highly conserved among animals, including Drosophila and mammals. In Drosophila, CTCF is involved in the organization of functional promoters and insulators, in cooperation with many other architectural proteins, including Su(Hw) and Pita. These proteins, like many other architectural proteins, interact with CP190, which, along with its partners, recruits transcriptional complexes to target promoters. This study was conducted to investigate the cooperation between architectural proteins in the recruitment of CP190 to regulatory elements.ResultsWe generated transgenic flies expressing double and triple mutants of dCTCF, Su(Hw), and Pita that cannot interact with CP190. The single mutants are fully viable; however, few triple mutants develop into adults. In the triple mutant, although the CP190 concentration is reduced, the level of gene transcription remains unaltered, suggesting that co-expression of the three mutant proteins is responsible for CP190 instability. ChIP-seq analysis showed that CP190 is required for dCTCF–chromatin binding. In the triple mutant, CP190 demonstrates an almost complete loss of association with the promoters and insulators to which the tested architectural proteins bind; however, these regulatory elements were found to retain their activity.ConclusionsArchitectural proteins cooperate to recruit CP190 to regulatory elements and determine its stability. Despite the important role of CP190 in transcriptional regulation, its functions may be partially performed by partner proteins.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13072-025-00656-9.

Introduction

The eukaryotic genome exhibits a complex spatial organization critical for the proper regulation of nuclear processes, including transcription, replication, recombination, and DNA repair [1–4]. In mammals, the CTCF protein plays a key role in the formation of chromatin architecture [5, 6]. Based on its structural organization, CTCF can be classified into one of the largest groups of DNA-binding transcription factors (С2H2 proteins), containing five or more C2H2 domains in a cluster that determine highly specific binding to 12–18 bp motifs on DNA [7].

The hypothesis postulating the existence of a large class of architectural C2H2 proteins sharing common properties is well-supported by studies of C2H2 proteins, including a CTCF homologue (dCTCF), in the model organism Drosophila [8]. The most well-characterized architectural C2H2 proteins, apart from dCTCF, are Su(Hw) and Pita, which can support long-distance interactions and are involved in insulator formation [9–14]. The main similarity between dCTCF, Su(Hw), and Pita is the presence of a cluster of 9–11 C2H2 domains; a portion of the C2H2 domains in each protein determines specific binding to long motifs [10, 15]. However, these C2H2 proteins exhibit significant differences in their structural organization. Pita contains a structured N-terminal dimerization domain (zinc finger-associated domain; ZAD) involved in maintaining specific long-distance interactions [16, 17]. Similar ZADs have been found in approximately one hundred Drosophila C2H2 proteins, five of which (ZIPIC, Zw5, Mzfp1, ZAF1, and M1BP) have been shown to function as architectural C2H2 proteins [16, 18–22]. dCTCF contains an unstructured N-terminal dimerization domain that is important for its recruitment to chromatin [23]. Finally, the terminal unstructured domains of the Su(Hw) protein do not participate in homodimerization; thus, how Su(Hw) maintains specific long-distance interactions remains unclear [24–26].

Pita and dCTCF preferentially bind to gene promoters or introns whereas most Su(Hw) sites are located in intergenic regions [9, 10, 14, 16, 27–30]. Unlike Pita and dCTCF, when bound to promoters, Su(Hw) acts as a transcriptional repressor [31–33]. The most evident cooperative activity of dCTCF, Su(Hw), and Pita as architectural C2H2 proteins is their role in establishing boundaries between regulatory domains in the Bithorax complex (BX-C) (Fig. 1A). The BX-C comprises three highly conserved homeotic genes, Ubx, abd-A, and Abd-B, which encode transcription factors that determine the identity of the last thoracic and all abdominal segments of the fly [8, 34]. Pita, dCTCF, and Su(Hw) bind to boundaries that separate regulatory domains and ensure their autonomous activity [35, 36]. For instance, dCTCF inactivation disrupts proper Abd-B expression [11, 14, 37]. Similarly, mammalian CTCF is required for the independent regulation of Hox genes, underscoring an evolutionarily conserved role in homeotic gene regulation [38–41].

A key feature of a large group of architectural C2H2 proteins, including dCTCF, Su(Hw), Pita, ZIPIC, M1BP, Mzfp1, and Opbp, is their ability to interact with CP190 through their unstructured domains [13, 14, 19, 42, 43]. The CP190 protein has an N-terminal BTB/POZ domain that forms homodimers and several adjacent unstructured domains that collectively interact in various combinations with C2H2 architectural proteins [44]. C2H2 proteins are responsible for recruiting CP190 to regulatory regions on chromosomes. Furthermore, CP190 is required for the efficient binding of at least Su(Hw) and dCTCF to some their chromatin sites [11, 27, 45, 46].

CP190 is essential for Drosophila development, as cp190-null mutants die during pupal and pharate adult stages [47, 48]. In the absence of maternal and zygotic CP190 protein, mortality occurs at the embryonic and early larval stages, indicating an important role of CP190 in the early stages of Drosophila development [49]. CP190 is involved in long-range interactions between regulatory elements and insulator function in embryos [13, 24, 49–51]. In particular, CP190 is recruited to architectural C2H2 proteins located at the boundaries of the BX-C regulatory domains and participates in the organization of their autonomous functioning [35]. CP190 in cooperation with Chromator and Z4 proteins localizes predominantly to housekeeping gene promoters, where they facilitate the recruitment of complexes that modify chromatin and regulate transcription [52–58].

Finally, the CP190 protein participates in the formation of nuclear speckles, referred to as insulator bodies, in which dCTCF, Su(Hw), and Mod(mdg4) proteins have also been found [45, 59–62]. At present, the functional role of insulator bodies remains unknown. According to one hypothesis, insulator bodies form a reserve of architectural C2H2 proteins that are placed on newly synthesized DNA during its replication [59]. By participating in the formation of insulator bodies and protein complexes, CP190 may increase the efficiency of architectural C2H2 protein binding to chromatin. For example, RNAi-mediated inactivation of CP190 in S2 cells has been shown to significantly reduce Su(Hw) binding to its chromatin sites [27].

We previously found that deletions in dCTCF, Pita, and Su(Hw) proteins that disrupt interaction with CP190 do not significantly affect the functional activity of these proteins [13, 14, 43]. This finding can be explained by the hypothesis that all regulatory elements are formed by several architectural proteins that can simultaneously recruit CP190. To test this hypothesis, we generated transgenic Drosophila lines bearing mutations in two or three genes (dCTCF, Pita, and Su(Hw)) encoding variants unable to interact with CP190. We examined how chromatin binding of a dCTCF protein unable to interact with CP190 would be impaired and how CP190 binding would be altered in lines with mutant dCTCF expressed in various combinations with mutant Pita and Su(Hw).

Results

Generation of transgenic lines expressing mutant variants of dCTCF, Su(Hw), and Pita proteins deficient in CP190 interaction

The first goal of this study was to determine whether a phenotypic effect would be observed when combining mutations that disrupt the interaction of dCTCF, Pita, and Su(Hw) proteins with CP190. In a previous study, we generated the ctcf allele expressing the mutant dCTCF∆705–733-HA protein, which lacks the CP190-interacting region (Fig. 1B; Additional File 1: Fig.S1) [14]. Flies homozygous for this mutation (renamed CTCFΔCP) are fertile and exhibit normal viability (Fig. 1C) [14].

The su(Hw)∆114 mutation (indicated as su(Hw)∆CP in this work) was obtained through a deletion in the N-terminal region (88–202 aa) that interacts with the CP190 protein (Fig. 1B; Additional File 1: Fig.S1) [24]. Homozygous su(Hw)∆CP flies are viable, fertile, and exhibit no visible phenotypic defects.

To obtain the pitaΔCP1 mutation, the transgene expressing PitaΔCP1 (deletion of the 220–232 aa region that is critical for CP190 interaction [28] was inserted into the pita1attP platform, in which a 261 bp of pita including the first exon were deleted and replaced with attP site (Fig. 1B; Additional File 1: Fig.S1) [63]. While the null pita1attP mutation causes embryonic lethality, pitaΔCP1 homozygous flies exhibit normal viability and fertility and have no visible phenotypic defects.

Next, we combined CTCF∆CP with pitaΔCP1 and su(Hw)∆CP pairwise and across both lines. The combinations of su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP and pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP reduced the viability of the flies by ~ 50%, with lethality occurring at the larval/pupal stages (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, the lifespan of these flies was reduced to approximately 3 days. The triple mutant, pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP, died predominantly during larval/pupal stages, with rare deaths occurring within 1 day post-eclosion. These results demonstrate strong genetic interactions between CTCFΔCP, su(Hw)ΔCP, and pitaΔCP1 mutations.

In the BX-C (Fig. 1A), most known boundaries contain 1–2 binding sites for the dCTCF [8] protein. However, two well-characterized boundaries, Mcp and Fub, also have binding sites for the Pita and Su(Hw) proteins. Mcp, which separates the regulatory domains controlling abd-A and Abd-B expression, exhibits closely spaced (9 bp) binding sites for dCTCF and Pita [64]. Both proteins are required for Mcp activity, although additional unknown factors likely contribute [35]. The Fub boundary separating the regulatory regions of the Ubx and abd-A genes comprises two redundant modules, each of which can function independently. The proximal module contains binding sites for the architectural proteins Mzfp1, Pita, and Su(Hw), whereas the distal module contains binding sites for Su(Hw) and dCTCF [19, 35].

CTCFΔCP males exhibited a near-normal phenotype; in females, weak deformation of the A7 segment was observed, along with several abnormal bristles on the A8 tergite (Fig. 1D). These findings indicate that the expression of homeotic genes in the BX-C was not seriously impaired. Approximately 50% of the CTCFΔCP males exhibited various small pigmented spots on the A4 segment, suggesting that mild impairment of the Mcp boundary function results in minor ectopic activation of Abd-B. In contrast, all pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP homozygous males displayed pigmented spots on the A4 tergite, reflecting stabilization of Mcp dysfunction. In addition, the combination pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP resulted in sterility, despite no visible genital changes. The su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP combination did not result in synergistic enhancement of the CTCFΔCP mutant phenotype (Fig. 1D); however, it caused sterility.

The rare survivors among the triple mutants (pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP) did not demonstrate additional phenotypic changes in comparison with pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP flies (Fig. 1D). Taken together, these results suggest that the boundaries retain significant functionality, likely due to architectural protein redundancy.

dCTCF, Su(Hw), and Pita are stably associated with their genomic sites during Drosophila development

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the chromatin binding of dCTCFΔCP and CP190 in double- and triple-mutant backgrounds (pitaΔCP1, su(Hw) ΔCP, CTCFΔCP). For chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) analysis, we used third-instar larvae to minimize maternal effects, since homozygotes for pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP can only be obtained from heterozygous pitaΔCP1/pitaΔCP1; su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP /TM6,GFP parents.

As the chromatin binding of dCTCF, Su(Hw), and Pita at the third-instar larval stage remains uncharacterized, we performed genome-wide ChIP-seq to map dCTCFwt-HA, Pita, and Su(Hw) binding. Chromatin was isolated from third-instar larvae of the CTCFwt line, which expresses C-terminally 3xHA-tagged dCTCF protein (dCTCFwt-HA). ChIP was conducted using antibodies against the 3xHA epitope (dCTCFwt-HA), the C-terminal unstructured region of Pita [28], or the N-terminal region of Su(Hw) [65], followed by Illumina high-throughput sequencing. Only peaks overlapping DNA binding motifs for the respective proteins were considered.

Regarding dCTCFwt-HA, we identified 432 motif-containing peaks. Among these, 386 and 422 overlapped with previously reported embryonic (n = 581) and adult (n = 548) dCTCF-HA peaks, respectively (Fig. 2A). Embryo-specific “unique” sites exhibited weak or absent dCTCF binding in larvae and adults (Additional File 1: Fig. S2). These embryonic-unique sites showed reduced chromatin accessibility [66, 67] in adult tissues (whole-body ATAC-seq; median fold change [FC] = 0.58 and 0.63) compared with those at sites conserved across all three stages, while accessibility in early embryos (nc8-nc10, nc11-nc13, whole embryo ATAC-seq) remained comparable (median FC = 0.83–0.96; Mann–Whitney comparison with each adult FC resulted in p < 1 × 10− 16; Additional File 1: Fig. S3). In contrast, larval- and adult-unique peaks displayed only minor signal reductions at other stages, suggesting their uniqueness likely reflects technical variation (Fig. 2A). Overall, these results indicate that dCTCF binding is largely stable at motif-containing regions throughout development, with embryonic-specific sites localized to genomic regions that form accessible chromatin only in early embryos.

Regarding Pita, 663 larval motif-containing peaks were identified (Fig. 2B). The comparison with embryonic [16] and adult [28] datasets revealed substantial overlap, with < 10% stage-unique peaks. These “unique” peaks exhibited reduced but detectable signals at other stages, further supporting the developmental stability of Pita binding (Additional File 1: Fig. S2).

Su(Hw) exhibited the highest number of motif-associated sites (2661 larval peaks; Fig. 2C). Embryonic ChIP-Seq data [68] identified 2325 motif-containing peaks, with stage-specific “unique” peaks likely attributable to technical variability, consistent with prior reports of Su(Hw) stability across development (Additional File 1: Fig. S2) [10, 27, 30]. Finally, we identified 4220 CP190 larval binding sites (Fig. 2D), with stage-unique peaks showing enrichment across developmental stages.

In our study of the colocalization of dCTCF, Su(Hw), Pita, and CP190, we revealed distinct patterns for all proteins (Fig. 3A). Consistent with previous reports [10, 27], ~ 60% of Su(Hw)-only sites (non-overlapping with dCTCF/Pita) localized to intergenic regions/introns. Among the Su(Hw) sites that colocalize with CP190 (40%), the proportion of those located in promoters increased. In contrast, 65% of Pita-only sites colocalized with CP190, predominantly in promoters (Fig. 3A). More than half of dCTCF-only sites (59%) colocalized with CP190, largely at promoters. Co-binding of any two architectural proteins (dCTCF, Pita, Su(Hw)) almost always coincided with CP190. All three proteins co-occupied eight genomic regions, including the Fub boundary in the BX-C.

We further analyzed the overlap of the binding regions of the studied C2H2-containing architectural proteins (M1BP, Mzfp1, and ZIPIC) that target housekeeping gene promoters (Fig. 3B) [9, 16, 19, 21, 70]. It is known that these C2Н2 proteins interact with the unstructured N-terminal regions of CP190 [44]. Su(Hw) binding regions show almost no colocalization with M1BP, Mzfp1, and ZIPIC sites, suggesting binding to different groups of promoters. Indeed, Su(Hw) was found preferentially in promoters that are expressed in specific tissues of Drosophila, and the proportion of housekeeping gene-associated promoters was approximately 20% (Fig. 3С). In contrast, approximately 54% of Pita + CP190 sites were found to colocalize with M1BP, Mzfp1, or ZIPIC (Fig. 3B). Strikingly, no colocalization was observed with Pita-only sites. Similar results were obtained with dCTCF. Approximately 34% of dCTCF + CP190 sites were found to colocalize with M1BP, Mzfp1, or ZIPIC, whereas no colocalization was observed for dCTCF-only sites. As expected, CP190-associated regions were predominantly coincided with housekeeping gene promoters (Fig. 3С).

Taken together, our results are consistent with previous observations that Pita and dCTCF proteins, along with the Su(Hw) protein, are involved in organizing different groups of promoters. The Pita and dCTCF proteins exhibit two chromatin-binding variations—in cooperation with other architectural C2H2 proteins and CP190, primarily in the region of housekeeping gene promoters, and independently from known C2H2 proteins and CP190. The obtained results are also consistent with the hypothesis that CP190 collaborates with distinct architectural C2H2 proteins in the organization of regulatory elements.

The stability and efficiency of dCTCFΔCP binding to chromatin are reduced upon the expression of mutant proteins Su(Hw)ΔCP and PitaΔCP1 that cannot interact with CP190

To investigate genome-wide dCTCF and CP190 binding, we performed ChIP-seq using anti-HA (dCTCF) and anti-CP190 antibodies on chromatin isolated from third-instar larvae homozygous for the following genotypes: CTCFwt, CTCFΔCP, pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP, su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP, pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP.

Both dCTCFΔCP-HA and dCTCFwt-HA proteins were expressed under endogenous promoter control. We first assessed how disrupting CP190 interactions in mutant architectural proteins affects the association of dCTCFΔCP with its binding sites. In contrast to prior findings in adult flies, dCTCFΔCP exhibited slightly reduced chromatin binding at CP190-associated sites in CTCFΔCP larvae compared with that observed with CTCFwt (Mann–Whitney test, p = 2 × 10− 16, Fig. 4, Additional File 1: Fig. S4). The binding efficiency of dCTCF in pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP larvae was only slightly reduced compared with that observed in CTCFΔCP larvae (p = 0.06) at dCTCF/CP190 and dCTCF/Pita/CP190 co-occupied sites. Taken together, these results show that CP190 is positively involved in dCTCF binding to chromatin; however, the simultaneous inability of Pita and dCTCF mutants to interact with CP190 only weakly affects dCTCFΔCP binding to genomic regions co-occupied by dCTCF and Pita.

In su(Hw) ΔCP CTCFΔCP larvae, dCTCF binding was significantly reduced at all CP190-associated sites, with the most pronounced reduction observed at dCTCF + Su(Hw) co-occupied sites (p = 2 × 10− 16 vs. CTCFΔCP) (Fig. 4). Further reduction occurred in pitaΔCP1 su(Hw) ΔCP CTCFΔCP larvae across all CP190-associated sites (p = 8 × 10− 10 vs. su(Hw) ΔCP CTCFΔCP).

The strong reduction in dCTCF binding to dCTCF/CP190 co-occupied sites in su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP and pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP larvae might be explained by the negative effect of su(Hw)ΔCP on the expression of CTCFΔCP and CP190. No peaks for the Pita, Su(Hw), dCTCF, and CP190 proteins were found in the regulatory regions of the CTCF and cp190 genes. Furthermoe, RT–PCR showed that CTCF gene transcription levels were similar across CTCFwt, CTCFΔCP, pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP, su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP, and pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP larvae (Fig. 5A). Transcription of the cp190 gene was slightly reduced only in pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP larvae. These results indicate that transcription of the cp190 and СTCF genes remains largely unchanged in larvae regardless of the mutant background. Surprisingly, Western blot analysis revealed significantly reduced dCTCFΔCP and CP190 levels in pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP larvae (Fig. 5B).

Thus, simultaneous expression of dCTCFΔCP, PitaΔCP1, and Su(Hw)ΔCP cooperatively reduces the stability of the CTCFΔCP and CP190 protein in larvae. This finding explains the significant reduction in dCTCF binding efficiency at all sites in pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP larvae.

CP190 stability and chromatin association are severely impaired in triple-mutant pitaΔCP1su(Hw)ΔCPCTCFΔCP larvae

We next analyzed CP190 binding in larvae with distinct mutant backgrounds (Fig. 6, Additional File 1: Fig.S5). In CTCFΔCPand pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP mutants, CP190 binding was slightly reduced at dCTCF and dCTCF + Pita co-occupied sites, at comparable levels (Mann–Whitney test, p = 2 × 10− 16 for CTCFΔCP vs. CTCFwt, p = 0.43 for CTCFΔCP vs. pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP, Fig. 6). This finding aligns with the observed reduction in dCTCFΔCP binding at the same genomic sites (Fig. 4). Notably, CP190 binding remained unchanged at Pita-only sites in the pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP mutants, indicating that disruption of the Pita–CP190 interaction does not impair CP190 recruitment to Pita or dCTCF + Pita sites. Overall, diminished CP190 occupancy correlated with weakened dCTCFΔCP binding.

This resilience likely reflects redundancy in boundary mechanisms, where other factors compensate for reduced CP190 recruitment.

Insulator body formation is not affected in pitaΔCP1su(Hw)ΔCPCTCFΔCP larvae

Previous studies have demonstrated that CP190 is required for the inclusion of the Su(Hw) and dCTCF proteins into nuclear speckles, also referred to as insulator bodies [45, 59, 61, 62]. We propose that recruitment of C2H2 proteins to insulator bodies preserves them from degradation and facilitates further binding to chromatin. In an attempt to explain the severe loss of stability and chromatin binding of dCTCF and CP190 in pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP larvae, we hypothesized that simultaneous inactivation of Pita, Su(Hw), and dCTCF interactions with CP190 destabilizes speckle formation. To test this hypothesis, we performed fluorescence immunostaining on imaginal discs from pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP and pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP lines. In these lines, Pita and Su(Hw) are tagged with a 3xFLAG epitope for sensitive detection. The pitawt line expressed Pitawt-FLAG [28], serving as a positive control for native speckle assembly. In the pitawt control, Pita-FLAG formed 1–3 large nuclear speckles per nucleus that completely colocalized with CP190 (Fig. 8), showing for the first time that Pita enters insulator bodies, like Su(Hw) and dCTCF. During mitosis, the Pita-associated speckles disappeared, and CP190 began to localize to centrosomes. Similar dynamics were observed in pitaΔCP1 and pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP mutants: speckles containing CP190 and Pita/Su(Hw) were observed in interphase, and association with CP190 was observed only with centrosomes during mitosis. Thus, PitaΔCP1and Su(Hw)ΔCP can enter nuclear speckles without direct interaction with CP190. In general, no noticeable disturbances in speckle formation were detected in the mutant lines, as previously demonstrated following CP190 protein inactivation [59, 61].

Significantly reducing CP190–promoter binding has no apparent effect on the expression of the housekeeping genes in triple-mutant pitaΔCP1su(Hw)ΔCPCTCFΔCP larvae

We analyzed differential gene expression across mutant lines to determine whether combinatorial mutations affect transcriptional activity at promoters associated with dCTCF, Su(Hw), or Pita. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified through RNA-seq: 396 in CTCFΔCP, 3712 in pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP, 4953 in su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP, and 1212 in pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP (triple mutant). Notably, the DEG count did not scale with mutation complexity, suggesting non-additive effects.

As shown in Fig. 9, while ChIP-seq signals in promoters decreased progressively with mutation accumulation (log2FC histograms shifted downward on the x-axis), gene expression remained largely unperturbed (log2FC histograms centered at near-zero on the y-axis, with few outliers). Changes in CP190 binding strength across mutants showed no correlation with expression changes when compared with the wild type (Spearman correlation, p > 0.15). Similarly, absolute expression changes lacked correlation (Spearman correlation, p > 0.05). We repeated the analysis using housekeeping genes alone to rule out confounding effects from developmental delays or stage-specific transcriptional noise, and the results remained consistent. In particular, no difference was observed in the expression of the cp190 and CTCF genes in the CTCFΔCP, su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP, and pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP lines, confirming our findings that the decrease in protein expression occurs at the post-transcriptional level (Additional File 1: Fig. S6). RNA-seq analysis does not accurately detect minor changes in gene expression. However, we can state that no significant changes in gene expression were observed, which indicates that the reduction of CP190 protein at promoters does not lead to a noticeable decrease in their activity.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the genome-wide distribution of three key architectural C2H2 proteins—Su(Hw), Pita, and dCTCF. Our findings confirm that these proteins stably bind to their sites in open chromatin regions at different Drosophila stages, though their genomic distributions and functional roles diverge significantly. The Su(Hw) protein exhibits the broadest binding repertoire, occupying ~ 4-fold more sites than Pita or dCTCF, predominantly in intergenic regions. Previously, Su(Hw) has been demonstrated to function as a transcriptional repressor of neuronal gene promoters in female ovaries [31, 32]. This repression is mediated by its C‑terminal domain, which also enables insulator activity [32]. One of the functions of Su(Hw) is likely the complete inactivation of promoters in cells in which the corresponding gene should not be expressed. In contrast, Pita and dCTCF are frequently localized near transcription start sites in housekeeping gene promoters, together with other architectural C2H2 proteins (M1BP, Mzfp1, and ZIPIC) that also recruit CP190. It can be speculated that architectural C2H2 proteins form open chromatin at promoters and recruit transcriptional complexes, including CP190, that are necessary for the formation of active promoters. Since only a small subset of C2H2 proteins have been studied, how many C2H2 proteins are sufficient to generate an active promoter remains unknown.

We have previously demonstrated that the Pita protein binds stably to insulators (e.g., Mcp, Fub) with minimal dependence on adjacent dCTCF or Su(Hw) sites [19, 35, 64]. The presence of the N-terminal ZAD domain, which forms exceptionally stable tetramers [71], may explain the high efficiency of protein binding to chromatin. In contrast, dCTCF and Su(Hw) binding depends on Pita co-occupancy, explaining why РitaΔCP1 retains chromatin association and CP190 recruitment via partner C2H2 proteins. It has been previously shown that the dСTCF and Su(Hw) binding efficiency at CP190-dependent chromatin sites is largely determined by CP190 [11, 27, 45]. Consistent with this, we found that the removal of the CP190-binding region (dCTCFΔCP) only partially impairs dCTCF binding at CP190-associated sites, but significant CP190 inactivation disrupts dCTCF binding to a much greater extent. This stronger effect is likely due to the simultaneous reducing binding of other CP190-interacting C2H2 proteins, which collectively stabilize dCTCF at these sites. Conversely, reduced binding of C2H2 proteins to chromatin leads to a more pronounced reduction in CP190 levels at the same sites.

It is likely that the efficient binding of dCTCF and Su(Hw) to chromatin sites requires prior interaction of these proteins with CP190, which may occur in speckles. Mod(mdg4) and CP190 proteins are thought to be extensively sumoylated and form the speckle core through liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS) via multivalent interactions [45, 59, 61]. Su(Hw), Pita, dCTCF, and other architectural C2H2 proteins are then recruited into speckles through specific interactions with CP190 and Mod(mdg4) isoforms. We found that PitaΔCP1 and Su(Hw)ΔCP continue to enter speckles despite their lack of CP190 interaction, likely through interactions with Mod(mdg4) isoforms alone.

The triple mutant pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP caused a global reduction in CP190 and dCTCF binding, even at Pita-associated chromatin regions. This finding could be explained by the destabilization of CP190 and dCTCFΔCP proteins resulting from РitaΔCP1 and Su(Hw)ΔCP co-expression. It can be speculated that РitaΔCP1 and Su(Hw)ΔCP continue to complex with CP190, despite the deletion of the CP190-interacting domains, through interactions with other proteins, such as Mod(mdg4) isoforms. As a result, РitaΔCP1, Su(Hw)ΔCP, and CTCFΔCP bind CP190 in partially disorganized complexes that are recognized by the ubiquitination system and degraded. This hypothesis may explain the significant decrease in CP190 and dCTCF observed in pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP larvae in our Western blot analyses.

Furthermore, we found that gene promoter activity remained largely unaffected in pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP mutants, despite reduced CP190 binding. This robustness is likely explained by redundancy in the organization of active promoters. CP190 collaborates with Z4 and Chromator to organize a platform that recruits transcriptional machinery [54, 72–75], and these factors may compensate for the loss of CP190 by directly interacting with architectural C2H2 proteins. The ability of Z4, Chromator, and other proteins to partially compensate the functions of CP190 likely plays an important role in stable and efficient housekeeping gene promoter regulation. This redundancy is consistent with the survival of CP190 null mutants until the pharate adult stage [49], which can be explained by the presence of maternal CP190 protein. This protein is likely sufficient to support embryonic development – the period when CP190 is most critical [51]. In contrast, CP190-deficient embryos, which lack both maternal and zygotic CP190 [49, 51], fail to develop beyond the early larval stage, underscoring the essential role of CP190 in embryogenesis. CP190 helps organize regulatory boundaries, thereby ensuring the proper spatial and temporal expression of developmental genes [49, 51]. Furthermore, CP190 may be necessary for the efficient recruitment of architectural C2H2 proteins, such as dCTCF and Su(Hw), to nascent chromatin during the rapid cell divisions in early embryogenesis.

Conclusion

This study provides additional support for the hypothesis that functional promoters and insulators are formed by groups of architectural C2H2 proteins that cooperatively recruit CP190 to their regulatory elements. Simultaneous РitaΔCP1, Su(Hw)ΔCP, and CTCFΔCP expression results in CP190 destabilization and a significant reduction in its binding to all chromatin sites. Thus, proper interaction between architectural C2H2 proteins and CP190 is essential for their stability. CP190 binding was greatly reduced in triple mutants, primarily at promoters containing dCTCF and Su(Hw) binding sites. However, the significant decrease in CP190 binding to promoters did not affect their transcriptional activity, suggesting that other proteins may at least partially substitute CP190 functions at promoters. Similar resistance to decreased CP190 binding was observed at BX-C insulators. Previous studies have demonstrated that CP190 plays a role in Su(Hw) localization in speckles. However, РitaΔCP1 and Su(Hw)ΔCP mutants lacking the ability to interact with CP190 also enter speckles. This finding suggests that other proteins, possibly Mod(mdg4) isoforms, replace CP190 in recruiting mutant proteins to speckles. Thus, CP190 exhibits diverse but partially redundant functions in maintaining the functional activity of architectural C2H2 proteins.

Fly crosses and transgenic lines

Drosophila strains were grown at 25 °C under standard culture conditions. CTCFΔCP – ctcf gene with the deletion of 705–733 aa in encoding region, fused with an 3 × HA-tag at the C-terminus, inserted in the attP site of the CTCFattP platform (3 L:7356496.7354324, according FlyBase r6.53; Dmel) [14], has no markers, fertile in homozygous, for crossing was balanced on TM3,GFP. pitaΔCP1 – Pita cDNA with the deletion of 220–232 aa, fused with 3×FLAG, under control of the Ubi-p63E promoter [28], integrated in pita1attP platform (2R: 23554440.23554180, according FlyBase r6.53; Dmel), has mini-white reporter, fertile in homozygous, balanced on CyO. su(Hw)ΔCP – Su(Hw) cDNA with the deletion of nucleotides corresponding to 88–202 aa, fused with 3×FLAG, under control of the Ubi-p63E promoter, integrated in Δsu(Hw)dsRed attP [24] (3R:14308153.14304792, according FlyBase r6.53; Dmel), has intron-less yellow gene as a reporter, sterile in homozygous, balanced on TM3,GFP. Combination of the CTCFΔCP with su(Hw)ΔCP was obtained by recombination followed by locking by TM3,GFP balancer and then saved on balancer as CTCFΔCP su(Hw)ΔCP /TM3,GFP. Combinations of the CTCFΔCP or CTCFΔCP su(Hw)ΔCP with pitaΔCP1 –were obtained using CyO/If; TM3,GFP/+ double balancer line and then saved on balancers as pitaΔCP1 /CyO; CTCFΔCP su(Hw)ΔCP /TM3,GFP. All lines were validated by PCR analysis. Fertility was determined by the ability of males or females to produce offspring when crossed with males or females from wt or y1w1118 line.

Details of the cloning procedures, primers, and plasmids used for plasmid construction are available upon request.

Cuticle preparation

Adult flies were collected in 1,5 ml tubes and stored in 70% ethanol for at least 1 day. The ethanol was then replaced with 10% KOH, and the flies were heated at 70 °C for 1 h. After heating, the flies were washed twice with dH2O and heated again in dH2O for 45 min. The digested flies were then washed with 70% ethanol and stored in 70% ethanol. The abdomen cuticles were cut from the rest of the digested fly using fine tweezers and an insulin syringe needle and placed in a drop of glycerol on a glass slide. The abdomens were then cut longitudinally on the dorsal side through all the tergites with the syringe. Some cuts were made between the tergites to ensure that the cuticle lay flat on the slide. The cuticles were then flattened with a coverslip. Photographs in the bright or dark field were taken on a Nikon SMZ18 stereomicroscope using Nikon DS-Ri2 digital camera, processed with ImageJ 1.50c4 and Fiji bundle 2.0.0-rc-46.

Antibodies

The antibodies used were as follows: affinity purified rat anti-CP190 [9]; affinity purified rabbit anti-Pita [9]; affinity purified rabbit anti-Su(Hw) [76]; rabbit anti-HA antibodies (Proteintech); rabbit anti-FLAG antibodies (Proteintech); mouse monoclonal anti-lamin Dm0, clone ADL84.12 (#ADL84.12, DSHB, USA).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Chromatin was prepared from third-instar larvae. 500 milligrams of larvae was ground in a mortar in liquid nitrogen and resuspended in 20 mL of buffer A (15 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.6, 60 mM KCl, 15 mM NaCl, 13 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM EGTA, 0.15 mM spermine, 0.5 mM spermidine, 0.5% NP-40, and 0.5 mM DTT) supplemented with 0.5 mM PMSF and Calbiochem Cocktail V. The suspension was then homogenized in a Potter and Dounce homogenizer with a tight pestle, filtered through a 100 μm Nylon Cell Strainer (Miltenyi Biotec, USA), and cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Cross-linking was stopped by adding glycine to a final concentration of 125 mM. The nuclei were washed with three 10-mL portions of wash buffer (15 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.6, 60 mM KCl, 15 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM EGTA, 0.1% NP-40, and protease inhibitors) and one 5-mL portion of nuclear lysis basic buffer (15 mM HEPES, pH 7.6, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM EGTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5 mM DTT, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, and protease inhibitors) and resuspended in 1 mL of nuclear lysis buffer (15 mM HEPES, pH 7.6, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM EGTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5 mM DTT, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.5% SLS, 0.1% SDS, and protease inhibitors).

The suspension of nuclei was sonicated in a Covaris ME220 focused-ultrasonicator (40 alternating 15-s ON and 45-s OFF intervals, peak power 75, duty % factor 25), and 50-µL aliquots were used to test the extent of sonication and measure the DNA concentration. Debris was removed by centrifugation at 14,000 x g, 4 °C, for 10 min, and chromatin was pre-cleared with Protein G Dynabeads (Invitrogen, USA). Corresponding antibodies were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with 20 µL aliquots of Protein G (rat anti-CP190, 1:300; rat anti-Su(Hw), 1:200; rat non-specific IgG) or Protein A (rabbit anti-HA, 1:500; rabbit anti-Pita, 1:200; rabbit non-specific IgG) Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) mixed with 200 µL of PBST. Then antibody–Dynabead complexes were washed and equilibrated in nuclear lysis buffer. Chromatin samples containing 10–20 µg of DNA equivalent in 200 µL nuclear lysis buffer (2 µL aliquots of pre-cleared chromatin as input material) were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibody–Dynabead complexes. After 3 rounds of washing with lysis buffer supplemented with 500 mM NaCl and TE buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8; 1 mM EDTA), the DNA was eluted with elution buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0; 1 mM EDTA, 1% SDS), the cross-links were reversed, and the precipitated DNA was extracted using a ChIP DNA Clean &Concentrator kit (Zymo Research, USA).

The ChIP-seq libraries were prepared with the NEBNext®_Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep kit per the manufacturer’s instructions. Amplified libraries were quantified using fluorometry with DS-11 (DeNovix, USA) and a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, USA). Diluted libraries were clustered on a pair-read flowcell and sequenced using a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, USA).

ChIP-seq data processing and sequence analysis

ChIP-seq analysis was performed for 15 dCTCF, Pita, Su(Hw) and CP190 proteins. The number of samples and replicates for each protein are summarized in the Additional File 2.

For all larvae CTCF and CP190 samples except pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP we have obtained 4 biological replicates with two independent flies collection procedures to avoid differences which might occur due to dynamic changes in the transcription regulation during the larvae stage. At the same time there was no opportunity to obtain pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP larvae flies in 4 replicates because of their low viability, therefore only 2 replicates with one collection procedure are provided for these samples. Reads were processed as described previously [77], the main steps were trimming Illumina adapters with cutadapt [78], low quality ends were trimmed with sickle (https://github.com/najoshi/sickle) (with -q 20 option)), alignment with bowtie2 [79] against version dm6 of the Drosophila melanogaster genome, filtration of PCR duplicates and non-unique mapping with picard (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) and blacklist filtration with bedtools [80]. Then reproducible peaks calling was conducted against corresponding preimmune controls with IDR pipeline (https://sites.google.com/site/anshulkundaje/projects/idr). A soft p value threshold for MACS2 [81] in IDR of 0.01, the IDR reproducibility threshold was set to 0.05 for true replicates and 0.01 for pseudoreplicates. For conditions with 4 biological replicates before IDR the pairs of replicates from different collection procedures were merged with each other (.bam files). After that two obtained .bam files were passed to the IDR pipeline. All samples showed good reproducibility (the rescue ratio or/and the self consistency ratio were < 2).

Public data were obtained as .fastq files and processed with the same procedures as newly generated data. In case of single-end public data, bowtie (with -m 1 option) was used instead of bowtie2. In case of 1 biological replicate in public data binding sites were determined using MACS2 with p value threshold 1 × 10-5 instead of IDR.

De novo motif discovery was performed using ChIPMunk [82, 83]. For motif discovery the top-200 peaks per sample were narrowed to ± 200 bp around the summit, motif length was set to 15. Peak sets with motif sites were identified using sarus (https://github.com/VorontsovIE/sarus) with p value threshold 1 × 10-4. ChIP-seq coverage tracks were obtained using deepTools [84] bamCoverage and bamCompare functions, with a bin width of 50 and 10 bps and RPKM normalization or readCount scaling and log2 operation (in case of bamCompare).

Downstream analysis was performed in R, version 4.2.2 [85]. Peak annotation was performed using ChIPseeker [86] and GenomicRanges [87] packages; promoter segments were considered as ± 200 bp from the TSS. Analysis of the overlapping peaks was performed with ChIPpeakAnno [88] and visualised using the UpSetR package [89]. Heatmaps showing ChIP-Seq signal in binding sites were produced using deepTools [84] computeMatrix and plotHeatmap functions.

RT-PCR

Total RNA was isolated from ten third-instar larvae using the TRI reagent (Molecular Research Center, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was treated with two units of Turbo DNase I (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 30 min at 37 °C to eliminate genomic DNA. The synthesis of cDNA was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using 1 µg of RNA, 100 U of EpiScript reverse transcriptase (LGC Biosearch Technologies, UK), and 50 pM of oligo(dT) as a primer. The amounts of specific cDNA fragments were quantified by real-time PCR. At least three independent measurements were made for each RNA sample. Relative levels of mRNA expression were calculated in the linear amplification range by calibration to a standard genomic DNA curve to account for differences in primer efficiencies. Individual expression values were normalized with reference to RpL32 mRNA.

Protein extraction

Twenty third-instar larvae were cooled in liquid nitrogen, homogenized for 30 s with a pestle in 200 µL of extraction buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 5% glycerol, 10 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 1 mM DTT, 5 mM PMSF, and 1:100 Calbiochem Complete Protease Inhibitor Cocktails VII and V) and incubated on ice for 10 min. The suspension was sonicated in a Bioruptor (Diagenode, USA) for 3 min on setting H, 15 s ON/45 sec OFF. Then 4x SDS-PAGE sample buffer was added to the homogenate. Extracts were incubated for 10 min at 100 °C, centrifuged at 16,000 x g for 5 min, and loaded on a 6% SDS-PAGE gel.

RNA-seq

Total RNA was isolated from larva using the TRI reagent (Molecular Research Center, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. mRNA was isolated using oligo(dT) Magnetic beads (NEB, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA-Seq libraries were prepared with the NEBNext® Ultra RNA Library Prep, NEB, USA) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Amplified libraries were quantified using fluorometry with DS-11 (DeNovix, USA) and a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, USA). Diluted libraries were clustered on a pair-read flowcell and sequenced using a NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, USA).

RNA-Seq was performed for 5 larvae samples: wt, CTCFΔCP, su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP, pitaΔCP1 CTCFΔCP and pitaΔCP1 su(Hw)ΔCP CTCFΔCP. Adapters, poly-A t ails and low-quality ends were trimmed as described above for ChIP-Seq data. The processed reads were aligned to the version dm6 of the Drosophila melanogaster genome using the splice-aware STAR algorithm [90]. The gene read counts were calculated using the STAR quantMode option.

Further analysis was performed in R version 4.2.2 [85]. Firstly, the read counts were normalised using the TMM algorithm [91] after discarding the reads with multiple mapping (multimapping), those overlapping with more than one gene (ambiguous), not overlapping with genes (noFeature), unmapped read (unmapped), mapped on ribosomal RNA, and genes with expression more than 1 CPM in less than 2 sample. Then, the differentially expressed genes for each lne with mutation against wt line were identified using the estimateDisp, glmQLFit and glmQLFTest functions from the edgeR package [92] with 0.05 FDR significance threshold.

Dissection and immunostaining of Drosophila imaginal discs

Dissection of imaginal discs (wing, leg, haltere, eye-antennal) from third instar Drosophila melanogaster larvae was performed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The tissues were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 20 min, followed by a washing step (three times for 10 min each) with washing solution (PBS with 1% BSA, 0.1% Triton X-100, and 0.03% sodium azide).

For immunostaining, discs were incubated in a blocking/permeabilization solution (PBS with 1% BSA, 0.3% Triton X-100, and 0.03% sodium azide) for 2 h. Incubation with primary antibodies—rat anti-CP190 and rabbit anti-FLAG—diluted 1:200 in washing solution, was performed overnight at 4 °C.

Secondary antibodies—anti-rat Alexa Fluor 647 and anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific)—were applied at a 1:625 dilution for 2 h.

Each antibody incubation was followed by a washing step (three times for 20 min each). Finally, discs were mounted in Vectashield (Vector Laboratories, United States) and imaged using a Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope. Images were processed using ImageJ.
