Real-life clinical validation of artificial intelligence-assisted echocardiography for aortic root and left ventricular outflow tract measurements against human readers and cardiac magnetic resonance
Authors: Patrycja Mołek-Dziadosz, Oleksandra Bondarchuk, Aleksandra Woźniak, Maria Królikowska, Agnieszka Mirek, Anna Furman-Niedziejko, Karol Mazur, Elżbieta Ostrowska-Kaim, Karolina Golińska-Grzybała, Aleksander Siniarski, Joanna Szachowicz-Jaworska, Tomasz Miszalski-Jamka, Radosław Rychlak, Marc R Dweck, Jadwiga Nessler, Andrzej Gackowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsArtificial intelligence (AI) algorithms may improve echocardiographic measurement standardization, but direct validation against cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the agreement between AI-assisted transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), expert manual TTE, and CMR as a gold standard for aortic root and left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) diameters.Methods and resultsOut of 183 patients with analysable TTE and CMR recordings performed within 7 days, 169 had complete measurements of LVOT, sinotubular junction (STJ), and sinus of Valsalva (SoV) by AI and were included in the primary analysis. Automated AI measurements were obtained using the US2.ai platform and compared with those of expert echocardiographers and CMR. Agreement was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) and Bland–Altman analysis. Agreement between Expert 1 and CMR, and Expert 2 and CMR, yielded ICCs: 0.63 (95% CI: 0.50–0.73) and 0.51 (95% CI: 0.36–0.65) for LVOT, 0.71 (95% CI: 0.63–0.78) and 0.82 (95% CI: 0.73–0.87) for STJ, and 0.85 (95% CI: 0.79–0.89) to 0.85 (95% CI: 0.78–0.89) for SoV, respectively. Artificial intelligence–derived measurements demonstrated comparable agreement with CMR: ICC 0.70 (95% CI: 0.61–0.77) for LVOT, 0.77 (95% 0.70–0.83) for STJ, and 0.77 (95% 0.70–0.83) for SoV. Clinically significant differences (≥2 mm for LVOT and ≥4 mm for STJ and SoV) between AI and CMR measurements were observed in 36.7% of LVOT, 20% of STJ, and 11.8% of SoV measurements.ConclusionArtificial intelligence–assisted echocardiography showed comparable agreement for aortic root and LVOT measurements, but physician oversight remains necessary.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Aortic root, Left ventricular outflow, Echocardiography, Cardiac magnetic resonance

Introduction

Accurate measurement of the aortic root and left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) plays a crucial role in the diagnosis, surveillance, and procedural planning of aortic and valvular heart disease.1–3 Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) remains the first-line modality for aortic-related measurements; however, it is susceptible to variability related to image quality, off-axis planes, and calliper placement.3, 4 In contrast, cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) provides highly precise measurements and is often considered a reference standard.4–6

Artificial intelligence (AI)–driven tools have demonstrated the feasibility of fully automated 2D TTE-based assessment of aortic dimensions.7 Karužas et al.7 showed in 58 retrospectively evaluated examinations that AI software successfully generated measurements of the aortic annulus, sinus of Valsalva (SoV), sinotubular junction (STJ), and proximal ascending aorta in more than 90% cases. Moreover, Krishna et al.8 evaluated patients across the full clinical spectrum of aortic stenosis and assessed aortic root and LVOT dimensions using TTE, interpreted by both an AI algorithm and expert cardiologists, with measurements compared with computed tomography (CT) as the reference standard. Moderate agreement and correlation were observed between expert- and AI–derived TTE measurements and CT across all aortic root dimensions, supporting the concordance and reliability of echocardiographic measurements relative to CT.

Although variability between expert echocardiographers and AI has been described, rigorous validation against CMR—the reference standard for aortic geometry—remains limited. Consequently, inter-modality bias and clinical interchangeability are not yet fully established. We hypothesize that AI–assisted echocardiography demonstrates agreement with CMR comparable to expert readers and may help standardize key aortic root and LVOT measurements in routine practice.

Methods

Study population

In this retrospective study, we evaluated 313 consecutive adult patients hospitalized at the Department of Coronary Artery Disease and Heart Failure between 2015 and 2024, who underwent CMR within 7 days before or after TTE assessment to minimize potential physiological changes. The principal indications for CMR referral included suspected myocarditis, diagnostic work-up of heart failure, and morphological or functional evaluation of cardiomyopathies. Patients were excluded from the study based on the following criteria: unstable clinical condition, prior valve replacement or repair, absence of corresponding imaging data available to expert readers (TTE and/or CMR) or a lack of an adequate parasternal long-axis (PLAX) cine view, or very poor image quality precluding measurements by both the AI software and human expert readers. The primary analysis was performed in 169 patients with complete echocardiographic and CMR recordings available for comparative assessment and with complete AI-derived measurements of all three anatomical structures (LVOT, SoV, and STJ). Secondary analysis, including 14 patients with incomplete measurements done by AI, was also performed. The detailed patient selection process, including inclusion and exclusion criteria, is summarized in the study flowchart (Figure 1).

Baseline demographic data included age, sex, body height, and body weight. Recorded comorbidities included history of hypertension, myocardial infarction, percutaneous coronary intervention, atrial fibrillation (AF), and ventricular arrhythmias. Cardiac rhythm at the time of TTE was recorded as sinus rhythm or AF. Relevant valvular lesions—including mitral and tricuspid regurgitation, aortic stenosis and regurgitation, and mitral stenosis—were graded according to their severity (moderate vs. severe). The prevalence of cardiomyopathy subtypes, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, dilated cardiomyopathy, and arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, was also reported.

The study was performed at St. John Paul II Hospital, Kraków, Poland, and received approval from the local Ethics Committee (reference number 118.6120.203.2023). All procedures complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Echocardiography

Retrospective evaluation of echocardiographic examinations was carried out on DICOM-format recordings performed using recordings obtained on four echocardiography machines in routine clinical practice. Examinations had been performed by experienced cardiologists or fellows with at least one year of training, according to standard clinical protocols. Imaging was conducted using EPIQ CVx or Affinity 75 ultrasound platforms (Philips Healthcare, Andover, Massachusetts, USA), with ECG gating. Each cine loop comprised three consecutive cardiac cycles. All recordings were stored in the institutional PACS archive and later reanalysed in a blinded manner.

Artificial intelligence-based echocardiographic image analysis

All echocardiographic datasets were analysed using the Us2.ai software (US2.v2 version, Singapore). Echocardiographic measurements in the study were performed fully automatically using US2.ai software, which, after uploading the entire echo study, independently identifies the PLAX view from the available sequences without requiring manual selection by the investigators. In studies with multiple PLAX views, the US2.ai algorithm automatically processes all available clips, eliminating the need for selection and ensuring complete automation of the analysis. The software generated mean measurements of the LVOT, SoV, and STJ, thereby enabling a comprehensive morphometric assessment of these aortic structures. Measurements were obtained at end-diastole, perpendicular to the proximal aortic axis, at the levels of the SoV and STJ, and at end-systole, below the leaflet insertion, for the LVOT. End-diastole was defined visually as the frame demonstrating the largest left ventricular dimension immediately prior to aortic valve opening. The SoV was defined as the maximal diameter of the aortic root bulb, and the STJ was measured at the interface between the SoV and the proximal ascending aorta. In accordance with current echocardiographic guideline recommendations, the SoV and STJ diameters were obtained using the leading-edge (external-to-internal) convention, whereas the LVOT diameter was measured using the inner-edge (internal-to-internal) convention.9 Each discrepancy was classified according to its potential clinical impact using absolute differences between AI and CMR reference values. While a ≥4 mm threshold was applied for the SoV and STJ based on the 2024 guidelines for the management of peripheral arterial and aortic diseases promoting a 4 mm change as a significant difference in longitudinal follow-up, a stricter ≥2 mm threshold was applied for the LVOT. This narrower cut-off was chosen because the LVOT diameter is squared in the calculation of cross-sectional area; thus, minor errors of ≥2 mm introduce mathematically compounded, clinically critical discrepancies in stroke volume, aortic valve area calculations, and valvular regurgitation grading.1

Manual assessment of the aortic root and left ventricular outflow tract

Manual assessment of the aortic root and LVOT was performed by two independent experienced echocardiographers to ensure unbiased evaluation. Both experts were blinded to the CMR and AI results. The aortic root and LVOT were assessed in the PLAX view. The detailed measurement technique, including timing within the cardiac cycle and edge-to-edge conventions, is described above. Visual quality control by one echocardiography expert was performed in each case to assess recording quality, identify reasons for substantial errors, or exclusions from the study. Image quality for each examination was graded on a three-point scale based on structure visibility: poor, moderate, or good.

Cardiac magnetic resonance

Cardiac magnetic resonance examinations were performed using a 1.5 Tesla system (Magnetom Avanto, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a phased-array coil. The imaging protocol included standard cine acquisitions with steady-state free precession sequences with a slice thickness of 8 mm. Acquisitions were performed in short-axis and long-axis views, including three-chamber long-axis view used for evaluation of the LVOT, SoV, and STJ (syngo.via software, version VB40, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) in accordance with current EACVI recommendations.10

Measurements were obtained by a cardiologist trained in CMR. The SoV and the STJ were measured at end-diastole, while the LVOT was measured at end-systole, using the inner-to-inner edge convention to ensure consistency. The LVOT diameter was measured just below the aortic annulus at the level of cusp insertion. The SoV was assessed at its maximum dimension, perpendicular to the axis of the ascending aorta. The STJ was quantified at the transition from the SoV to the tubular portion of the ascending aorta. Cut-off values for the comparative analysis were defined based on SoV diameter < 35 mm vs. ≥35 mm as assessed by CMR. The 35 mm threshold was data driven and corresponded to the quartile-based cut-off separating the third quartile (Q3) from the remaining data.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using dedicated software packages (SPSS Statistics, version 25.0.0.2, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR), following evaluation of distributional assumptions with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were summarized as absolute frequencies and corresponding percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate, based on sample size and distributional characteristics.

Agreement between measurement methods (AI, echocardiography expert, and CMR) was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; two-way random effects model, absolute agreement), with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and Pearson correlation coefficients (r). Measurement dispersion was quantified using Bland–Altman analyses, reporting bias, and IQR. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare measurement differences among image quality strata. A two-tailed P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Feasibility

Of the initial cohort of 313 patients, 183 cases were analysable by both AI and human readers (Figure 1). Of these, 169 had complete AI-derived measurements for all three anatomical structures (LVOT, STJ, and SoV) and were included in the primary analysis. Among these 169 patients, echocardiographic image quality was classified as good in 64 patients (37.9%), moderate in 74 patients (43.8%), and poor in 31 patients (18.3%). Additional 14 patients had partial AI measurements (one or two structures), with corresponding echocardiographic and CMR data available for comparison. However, in 12 of 14 cases (85.7%), image quality was very poor, and the measurements were considered only borderline feasible by expert readers. Given the limited reliability of these assessments, this subgroup was excluded from the primary analysis. Results of agreement analyses including this subgroup are presented separately. The remaining 130 patients were excluded for the following reasons: absence of corresponding imaging data available to expert readers (echocardiography and/or CMR), lack of an adequate PLAX cine view in the available dataset in 124 cases, very poor image quality precluding measurements by both AI and human readers in four cases, and availability of transoesophageal echocardiography only in two cases.

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the patients are presented in Table 1. Briefly, the study population consisted of 169 adults with a median age of 52 years [IQR: 39.0–66.5]. The median body weight was 75.0 kg [IQR: 65.5–84.0], and the median height was 170.0 cm [164.0–176.0]. The most prevalent cardiovascular risk factor was arterial hypertension, observed in 46% of the cohort. A history of AF was documented in 16% of participants, and AF during the examination was recorded in 8%. On CMR, the median LVOT diameter was 22 mm [IQR: 20–24], the STJ diameter was 26 mm [IQR: 23–28], and the SoV diameter was 32 mm [IQR: 29–35]. Patients with a larger SoV diameter (≥35 mm) on CMR were predominantly men, taller, and heavier. They also more frequently had moderate MR compared with the remaining patients.

Inter-reader echo experts’ left ventricular outflow tract and aortic root assessment

Agreement between Echo Expert 1 and Expert 2 for LVOT and STJ measurements was moderate and strong for SoV measurements. For LVOT, the ICC was 0.71 (95% CI: 0.63–0.78) with a median bias of 0.0 mm [IQR: −1.0–1.0], and the correlation coefficient was 0.71 (P < 0.001). For STJ, the ICC was 0.73 (95% CI: 0.65–0.79), the median bias was −1.0 mm [IQR: −3.0–0.0], and the correlation coefficient was 0.73 (P < 0.001). The SoV assessment showed an ICC of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.88–0.93), a median bias of 0.0 mm [IQR: −1.0–1.0], and an r of 0.91 (P < 0.001).

Inter-modality left ventricular outflow tract and aortic root assessment (artificial intelligence vs. echo expert and cardiovascular magnetic resonance)

Artificial intelligence-based measurements showed comparable agreement with CMR and the Echo Experts. For LVOT, AI achieved an ICC of 0.70 (95% CI: 0.61–0.77) with a median bias of −1.00 mm [IQR: −2.48–0.04] and r = 0.70 (P < 0.001) vs. CMR (Figure 2A). Sinotubular junction measurements by AI had an ICC of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.70–0.83), a median bias of 1.82 mm [IQR: 0.01–3.44], and r = 0.77 (P < 0.001) vs. CMR (Figure 2B). The SoV assessment showed an ICC of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.70–0.83), a median bias of 0.04 mm [IQR: −1.43–1.57], and r = 0.77 (P < 0.001) (Figure 2C).

Compared with AI-derived measurements, Echo Expert 1 showed a moderate agreement for LVOT (ICC 0.63, 95% CI: 0.50–0.73), with a median bias of 1.46 mm [IQR: −0.60–1.54] and r = 0.65 (P < 0.001). For STJ, agreement was moderate to good (ICC 0.71, 95% CI: 0.63–0.78), with a median bias of 2.36 mm [IQR: −2.79 to 0.46] and r = 0.75 (P < 0.001). For SoV, Echo Expert 1 demonstrated good agreement (ICC 0.85, 95% CI: 0.79–0.89), with a median bias of 1.73 mm [IQR: −1.80–0.81] and a strong correlation (r = 0.85; P < 0.001) with CMR.

In comparisons between Echo Expert 2 and AI, the LVOT agreement was lower (ICC = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.36–0.65), with a median bias of 1.70 mm [IQR: −0.88–1.82] and r = 0.53 (P < 0.001). For STJ, the agreement improved to good (ICC = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.73–0.87), with a median bias of 1.88 mm [IQR: −1.22–1.75] and r = 0.82 (P < 0.001). For SoV, the agreement was also good (ICC = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.78–0.89), with a median bias of 1.68 mm [IQR: −1.72–0.58] and r = 0.85 (P < 0.001).

Secondary subanalysis including patients with incomplete artificial intelligence measurements

A secondary subanalysis was performed, including the additional 14 patients in whom AI successfully generated measurements for only one or two anatomical structures, yielding a combined cohort of 183 patients (Table 2). Compared with the primary analysis restricted to the 169 patients with complete AI-derived measurements, the overall pattern of agreement remained largely unchanged. Correlation coefficients and ICC values for AI vs. CMR, as well as for expert readers vs. CMR for STJ and SoV measurements, were minimally affected by the inclusion of these patients. However, a modest reduction in agreement metrics was observed for parameters most susceptible to image quality limitations, particularly at the LVOT level. This finding is consistent with the markedly poorer echocardiographic image quality in the incomplete-measurement subgroup, in which 85.7% of examinations were classified as very poor or borderline feasible.

Prevalence of significant measurement discordance in the primary population with complete artificial intelligence measurements

Significant discrepancies (difference ≥ 2 mm for LVOT and ≥4 mm for STJ and SoV) between AI and CMR were observed in 36.7% (n = 62) of LVOT, 20% (n = 34) of STJ, and 11.8% (n = 20) of SoV measurements, using thresholds of ≥2 mm for LVOT and ≥4 mm for STJ and SoV. Discrepancies were classified according to their potential clinical impact based on absolute differences between AI and CMR reference values: differences of <2 mm for LVOT and <4 mm for STJ and SoV were considered not clinically significant, whereas differences of ≥2 mm for LVOT and ≥4 mm for STJ and SoV were considered clinically significant. Four principal categories of AI–CMR measurement discrepancies were identified after a visual review of all paired diameters (LVOT, STJ, and SoV). These included (i) measurements obtained in the incorrect cardiac phase, such as STJ/SOV measured during systole with opened cusps or LVOT captured in diastole with closed cusps (Figure 4A and D); (ii) measurements obtained partially or wholly together with a closely related anatomical structure (Figure 4B and C); and (iii) measurements labelled as ‘poor quality or uncertain’, where lumen borders were indistinct because of motion or insufficient contrast (Figure 4E and F).

Automated AI-based echocardiographic measurements demonstrated structure-dependent error rates across the evaluated aortic root components. For the LVOT (n = 169), measurement errors occurred in 62 cases (36.7%), predominantly due to incorrect cardiac phase selection (13.6%), followed by partial or anatomically confounded measurements (11.8%) and poor image quality (10.6%). For the SoV (n = 169), errors were identified in 20 cases (11.8%), with poor image quality being the most frequent cause (5.9%), followed by partial or anatomically confounded measurements (3.5%) and incorrect phase selection (2.4%). The highest error rate was observed for STJ measurements (n = 169), with 34 erroneous measurements (20.0%), largely attributable to inadequate image quality (18.2%), whereas errors related to incorrect phase selection were uncommon (1.8%), and no anatomically confounded measurements were observed.

Discussion

This study addresses a critical gap in the validation of AI-driven echocardiographic measurements by providing the first systematic head-to-head comparison of automated aortic root and LVOT assessment against CMR as a reference standard. We demonstrated that a fully automated, AI-based quantitative assessment achieves performance comparable to CMR and human experts. Furthermore, our findings indicate that automated metrics are most reliable when the algorithm successfully generates a complete set of measurements across all three anatomical structures. Nonetheless, given the potential for significant clinical discordance in individual patients due to phase misidentification or suboptimal image quality, rigorous human physician oversight remains mandatory.

The observed interobserver concordance among expert echocardiographers is consistent with previously reported operator-dependent variability in aortic root measurements, a phenomenon that has long been recognized as a contributor to diagnostic uncertainty and heterogeneity in longitudinal surveillance recommendations.11 Manual measurements are particularly vulnerable to subtle differences in calliper placement, cardiac cycle timing, and imaging plane angulation, with reported variability ranging from 2 to 4 mm for the aortic root.1 The clinical implications extend beyond reproducibility; in the context of aortopathy surveillance, a measurement discrepancy of 3–5 mm can alter the recommended timing of surgical referral and transcatheter aortic valve replacement planning.12

Our analysis showed that fully automated AI-derived measurements agree well with CMR across all three parameters and fall within the range observed for experienced human observers. In the primary cohort, AI–CMR agreement for LVOT was comparable to Expert 1 and slightly better than Expert 2. However, this performance depended on complete data availability, as shown by the secondary subanalysis in which the inclusion of 14 patients with incomplete AI measurements due to poor local image quality reduced agreement for LVOT, while expert-to-CMR agreement remained unchanged. These findings highlight a key limitation of current AI systems: they perform well under optimal conditions but lack the flexibility of human experts in borderline-quality cases. This limitation applied only to borderline frames, whereas most exclusions were due to technical or protocol-related issues unrelated to the AI software. The need for human oversight is further supported by the frequency of clinically significant discordance, which ranged from 11.8% for SoV to 36.7% for LVOT. These errors were mainly driven by phase misidentification and tracking artefacts. By contrast, the AI system’s ability to analyse all available PLAX acquisitions and select optimal frames may improve reproducibility and reduce imaging obliquity compared with manual assessments based on subjective frame selection.13 The standardization of calliper placement and cardiac-cycle synchronization may also reduce random variability and thereby lower inter-modality discrepancies14–17

Precise aortic root and LVOT measurements underpin critical clinical decisions in structural heart disease and aortopathy management. Nevertheless, TTE remains the first-line screening modality.1 Enhancing echocardiographic diagnostic accuracy in appropriately selected patients may reduce the need for TEE or CT while maintaining diagnostic certainty.18 Our demonstration of the high concordance between AI–derived echocardiographic measurements and CMR suggests that standardized, AI–assisted echocardiographic quantification may constitute a reliable modality for initial cardiovascular assessment. Similarly, in aortopathy surveillance —whether for bicuspid aortic valve, Marfan syndrome, or other connective tissue disorders—measurement reproducibility is paramount for detecting subtle progression over time and determining surgical timing according to guideline-specified diameter thresholds.1 The reduced inter-modality variability achieved by AI vs. CMR (median bias of ∼1.0 mm for all measurements) approaches the level of precision necessary for reliable longitudinal surveillance, a consideration of particular clinical relevance given that aortic expansion rates on the order of 0.5–1.0 mm per year may warrant evaluation for potential surgical intervention.19

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a retrospective, single-centred study using one AI platform, so external validation across different populations, vendors, and workflows is needed. Second, although CMR served as the reference standard, measurements were obtained in an apical three-chamber–analogous long-axis view rather than by full 3D multiplanar reconstruction, which may be less accurate for elliptical structures and less comparable with studies using orthogonal 3D planes. In addition, LVOT measurements from the CMR three-chamber view may vary if the slice is not perfectly aligned with the LVOT long axis, reflecting a limitation of standard multimodality practice. Third, residual variability remains even in CMR, and surgical or autopsy correlation would represent the ultimate reference in selected cases. Fourth, excluding patients with prior valve surgery or very poor image quality limits assessment of AI performance in the most challenging cases. Fifth, spectrum bias may be present because our cohort was referred for CMR for specific indications and may not fully reflect patients undergoing routine aortic assessment, such as those with isolated aortic stenosis or bicuspid aortic valve. Finally, differences in timing and edge-detection conventions between echocardiography and CMR may have introduced systematic offsets; however, CMR was used as the reference standard, and our aim was to identify the echocardiographic approach with the closest agreement.

Conclusions

Artificial intelligence–assisted echocardiography showed comparable agreement with expert measurements and CMR in analysable studies. However, physician review remains necessary because AI may still produce clinically relevant errors, most often in suboptimal image quality. As evidence from outcome trials accumulates and implementation frameworks mature, AI-augmented echocardiography has the potential to improve the precision, operational efficiency, and temporal responsiveness of structural heart disease evaluation, thereby optimizing clinical decision-making.
