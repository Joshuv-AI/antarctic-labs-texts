Coaching beyond gender: a mixed-methods study of relational quality and mental toughness in adolescent female basketball players in Turkey
Authors: Nuri Karabulut, Yasin Akinci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoach athlete relational quality plays a pivotal role in adolescent athlete development, yet few studies have examined its association with mental toughness through the lens of coach gender in non-Western contexts. This study explored whether relational quality statistically mediates the link between coach gender and mental toughness in adolescent female basketball players in Turkey, using a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design grounded in Self-Determination Theory and Social Role Theory.MethodsA total of 366 female adolescent basketball players (aged 12–18) from 22 teams completed validated Turkish versions of the Sports Mental Toughness Questionnaire–Youth (SMTQ-Y) and the Coach–Athlete Relationship Questionnaire–Child (CART-Q-C). Multi-group confirmatory factor analysis confirmed scalar measurement invariance across coach gender and age groups. Quantitative analyses employed Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression with cluster-robust standard errors to account for team-level nesting. A subsample of 24 athletes participated in semi-structured interviews, thematically analysed to contextualize statistical patterns.ResultsFemale-coached athletes reported significantly higher mental toughness and relational quality (Cohen’s d = 0.59–0.64). Regression analyses revealed that relational quality was the strongest predictor of mental toughness (β = 0.51, p < .001). Mediation analysis confirmed that relational quality statistically mediated the association between coach gender and mental toughness (indirect effect B = 0.216, 95% CI [0.121, 0.311]). Qualitative findings identified three mechanisms, relational empowerment, gender-congruent role modelling, and emotional atonement, that helped explain this mediation.ConclusionThe findings suggest that female coaches are statistically associated with enhanced relational environments, which in turn appear to co-occur with greater mental toughness in adolescent female athletes. These patterns underscore the need for culturally sensitive coach education programs that emphasize relational competence and support female leadership in youth sport systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03893-5.Keywords: Adolescence, Coach athlete relationship, Gender, Mental toughness, Mixed-methods, Sports psychology

Introduction

The coach athlete dyad is increasingly recognized as a multifaceted social system that significantly influences adolescents’ sport experiences, especially regarding motivation, self-perception, and psychological adaptation. During adolescence, a period marked by identity exploration, peer comparison, and heightened sensitivity to feedback, the relational environment provided by coaches can either facilitate or inhibit young athletes’ capacity to manage stress, remain engaged, and derive psychological benefits from sport participation [1]. In this developmental window, relational quality has emerged as a robust conceptual framework for understanding the interpersonal processes that contribute to athlete outcomes. Commonly defined through the 3Cs which are closeness, commitment, and complementarity, relational quality is most frequently measured with the Coach Athlete Relationship Questionnaire (CART-Q), a psychometrically sound instrument validated across a variety of settings, age groups, and cultural backgrounds [2–4]. Parallel to this, the construct of mental toughness, a multidimensional capacity involving confidence, constancy, and emotional control, has gained prominence in youth sport psychology. Although historically regarded as a relatively stable personality trait, emerging perspectives conceptualize mental toughness as partially plastic, shaped by contextual and relational inputs such as coaching behaviour, perceived emotional support, and motivational climate [5–7]. Notably, cross-sectional research with adolescent and young adult athletes increasingly reveals positive associations between perceived relational support from coaches and indicators of psychological functioning, including resilience, persistence, and performance-related self-efficacy.

The evolution and importance of mixed-methods research in sport psychology

Mixed-methods research (MMR) has gained substantial traction within sport psychology due to its ability to bridge the gap between statistically generalizable insights and richly contextualized experiences [8, 9]. Especially in domains like coach-athlete relationships, where psychosocial mechanisms are deeply embedded in cultural and interpersonal contexts, MMR offers a powerful framework to triangulate data sources and strengthen inference validity [10]. In the present study, a sequential explanatory MMR design is employed to integrate quantitative analysis of relational quality and mental toughness with qualitative inquiry into athletes’ lived experiences [11, 12]. This approach facilitates both breadth and depth in understanding how gendered coaching dynamics manifest in the Turkish youth sport context.

Theoretical framework

The present investigation is anchored in two complementary theories. Self-Determination Theory (SDT) posits that optimal psychological functioning arises when individuals experience autonomy, competence, and relatedness [13]. In sport, autonomy-supportive coaching environments have been linked with higher psychological well-being, resilience, and adaptive coping [14, 15]. These relationships are mediated by perceived interpersonal support, making relational quality a key variable in SDT-informed models [16, 17].

Complementing this, Social Role Theory (SRT) explains how gendered socialization shapes expectations of leadership and interpersonal behaviour [18]. In sport settings, especially those with entrenched patriarchal norms, athletes may interpret coaching behaviours through gendered schemas that influence perceived warmth, competence, and legitimacy [19, 20]. These perceptual biases can shape relational dynamics and psychological outcomes, particularly in cultures where female coaches are underrepresented [21].

Coach gender, relational quality, and adolescent athletes

Empirical studies indicate that female athletes often report stronger perceptions of emotional safety, trust, and communication with female coaches, though findings vary by context [22, 23]. These relational patterns may be shaped by congruent gender-role identification, increased empathic atonement, or reduced perceived threat of stereotyping [24, 25]. However, coach gender may also serve as a proxy for unmeasured variables, such as interpersonal skill, communication style, or coaching philosophy [26, 27]. Thus, claims of mediation must consider alternative explanations and the possibility that relational quality co-varies with contextual or personal attributes beyond gender.

Adolescence further complicates this dynamic. As a period of identity consolidation and increasing autonomy, adolescent athletes are particularly sensitive to interpersonal feedback and social validation [28, 29]. SDT-informed coaching interventions in this stage have demonstrated significant impacts on mental toughness, persistence, and stress management [30, 31]. Relational climates that foster emotional communication and mutual respect are therefore crucial for psychological development during this sensitive window [32].

Cross-cultural considerations and current gap: the Turkish context

Much of the existing literature on coach–athlete relationships and mental toughness derives from Western, individualistic societies, limiting the generalizability of findings to collectivist or patriarchal cultures [33, 34]. In Turkey, where traditional gender norms and hierarchical family structures persist, female leadership in sport often challenges prevailing cultural scripts [35, 36]. Concepts such as “abla” (older sister) and “abi” (older brother) reflect familial metaphor frameworks that shape how adolescent athletes interpret authority and relational closeness in sport [37].

Despite these culturally specific dynamics, few studies have examined how coach gender intersects with relational quality and mental toughness in Turkish youth sport. This gap is notable given both the underrepresentation of female coaches and the sociocultural importance of gendered role modelling [38]. Addressing this void, the current study utilizes culturally validated measures [39, 40] and incorporates a theoretically grounded mixed-methods approach to elucidate these interrelations.

Hypotheses and planned tests

Guided by SDT and SRT, the following hypotheses were tested:

Measurement invariance across gender and age groups was assessed using multi-group CFA to ensure valid group comparisons [41]. Quantitative analyses employed MANOVA and regression with cluster-robust standard errors to account for the nested structure of the data [42]. Mediation was tested using a bootstrapped procedure with covariates [43]. The subsequent qualitative phase sought to probe the plausibility of these statistical associations, explore alternative mechanisms, and contextualize findings through athletes’ narrative experiences.

In interpreting results, causal claims are avoided, and mediation is framed as a statistical association consistent with, but not determinative of, an explanatory process. The integrated design enhances theoretical and ecological validity while responding to calls for culturally informed sport psychology research [34, 44].

Methods

Study design

This study employed a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design [9], integrating quantitative and qualitative data to examine associations among coach gender, coach–athlete relational quality, and mental toughness in adolescent female athletes in Turkey. The design was specifically chosen to first identify statistical patterns and subsequently explore contextually grounded mechanisms using qualitative inquiry (see Supplementary File S1).

Participants and sampling

A total of 366 adolescent female basketball players (Mage = 15.1, SD = 1.8; range = 12–18) were recruited from 22 club and school teams across three provinces. Of 410 athletes invited, 366 completed the survey, resulting in a response rate of 89.3%. Participants were recruited through a nested sampling strategy: a total of 35 teams were initially contacted, 22 agreed to participate (63%), and these 22 teams provided access to the full sample of enrolled athletes. The sample was nested within 22 teams, coached by 18 coaches (10 female, 8 male), with four coaches managing multiple teams. Team size ranged from 12 to 21 athletes (M = 16.6).

Coach gender was recorded via federation registration and verified by team officials. No non-binary identifications were reported. Descriptive statistics for coach qualifications, experience, and team level (school vs. club) are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. This nested recruitment structure required statistical handling of clustering effects to ensure accurate estimation of standard errors and prevent Type I error inflation. Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Usak University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 885-885-25). All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legal guardians (see Supplementary File S5).

Measures and psychometric properties

Sports Mental Toughness Questionnaire – Youth (SMTQ-Y): This 14-item measure assesses confidence, constancy, and control using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Never to 5 = Always). Scores were computed as means. The Turkish version [40] demonstrated strong reliability (α = 0.84) and CFA-supported structure (χ²/df = 2.18, CFI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.056). To enhance transparency, a representative item from the Confidence subscale is: “I believe I can cope with anything my sport throws at me.”

Coach–Athlete Relationship Questionnaire – Child Version (CART-Q-C): This 11-item scale evaluates closeness, commitment, and complementarity on a 5-point Likert scale. Total scores were calculated as item means. The Turkish adaptation [39] had excellent reliability (α = 0.91) and strong CFA fit indices (CFI = 0.97, RMSEA = 0.053). To enhance transparency, a representative item from the Closeness subscale is: “I like my coach.”

Measurement invariance

Multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA) tested measurement invariance for both scales across coach gender and athlete age group (12–14, 15–16, 17–18). Scalar invariance was established (ΔCFI < 0.01; ΔRMSEA < 0.015), supporting valid group comparisons [41].

Quantitative analysis procedures

All quantitative analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28, supplemented by the PROCESS macro [43]. Prior to conducting inferential analyses, intra-class correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated to assess clustering by team. ICCs for the SMTQ-Y and CART-Q-C were − 0.0071 and 0.0134, respectively, suggesting negligible intra-team dependence. Despite the low clustering, all regression and mediation models were estimated using cluster-robust standard errors (CRSE) to provide conservative estimates and account for potential intra-team dependencies. To examine group differences, multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to test whether mental toughness and relational quality varied as a function of coach gender and athlete age. To identify predictors of mental toughness, a series of multiple regression models were estimated with relational quality as the main predictor, while controlling for athlete age and weekly training load. To assess whether relational quality statistically mediated the association between coach gender and mental toughness, mediation analysis was conducted using bootstrapped indirect effects with 5,000 resamples. All mediation models incorporated CRSE and included relevant covariates to control for potential confounding effects.

Missing data treatment

Missing values were less than 2% per variable. Little’s MCAR test yielded a non-significant result (p > .10), supporting the assumption that data were Missing Completely at Random (MCAR). Accordingly, listwise deletion was applied. Sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation produced consistent patterns and did not alter substantive conclusions.

Qualitative phase: participants and sampling

A purposive subsample of 24 athletes was selected to ensure variation across coach gender, coach age, and athlete developmental stage. Selection was based on CART-Q-C and SMTQ-Y scores to capture diverse experiences. Interviews lasted 30–45 min and were conducted by female graduate researchers experienced in adolescent sport psychology (see Supplementary File S1).

Data collection and analysis

Interviews followed a semi-structured protocol. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Turkish and translated into English by bilingual experts. Thematic analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s [45] six-phase method, using a hybrid inductive-deductive approach within NVivo 14.

Two trained coders independently analysed transcripts. Intercoder reliability was excellent (κ = 0.87), with discrepancies resolved through discussion or third-party arbitration. Saturation was reached at the 21st interview.

A detailed codebook and thematic map are available in Supplementary Files S2 and S3. Integration of findings across methods was displayed using a joint table linking statistical results to narrative themes and quotes (see Supplementary File S4).

Data Availability

All anonymized data, syntax files, and supplementary materials (interview guide, codebook, joint display) are publicly accessible via the Science Data Bank (10.57760/sciencedb.29824). Interview transcripts are not shared to protect participant confidentiality.

Multivariate and univariate analyses

A two-way MANOVA was conducted to examine the main and interaction effects of coach gender (female vs. male) and athlete age group (12–14, 15–16, 17–18) on two dependent variables: mental toughness and relational quality. The multivariate test revealed significant main effects for coach gender (Wilks’ Λ = 0.867, F(2, 359) = 27.42, p < .001, partial η² = 0.13), indicating that athletes with female coaches scored significantly higher on the combined dependent variables. Age group also had a statistically significant multivariate effect (Wilks’ Λ = 0.968, F(4, 718) = 2.89, p = .022, partial η² = 0.02), while the interaction between coach gender and age group was not significant.

Follow-up ANOVAs for each dependent variable are reported in Table 3. Athletes coached by women reported significantly greater mental toughness (F = 52.18, p < .001, η² = 0.13, 95% CI [0.07, 0.18]) and relational quality (F = 48.72, p < .001, η² = 0.12, 95% CI [0.06, 0.17]) compared to those coached by men. Age group differences emerged only for relational quality (F = 4.12, p = .017, η² = 0.02), with older adolescents reporting slightly higher levels. No significant coach gender × age group interaction effects were found.

Sensitivity analyses

Although the interaction effect between coach gender and age group was statistically non-significant, descriptive statistics for mental toughness and relational quality stratified by both variables are provided in Supplementary Table S4 to aid interpretation of developmental patterns across subgroups.

We conducted robustness checks by disaggregating the CART-Q-C into its three subscales (closeness, commitment, complementarity) and testing each as a separate mediator. All models supported the mediation hypothesis, with relational closeness showing the strongest indirect effect. Additional covariates, including coach age and qualifications, did not substantially alter the mediation pattern.

Qualitative findings

The qualitative phase included 24 female adolescent basketball players, purposively selected to maximise diversity in coach gender, coach age, and athlete age group. This sample size was adequate to identify consistent patterns complementing the quantitative findings. However, the cross-sectional, single-interview design limited the ability to capture longitudinal changes in coach–athlete relationships. Future research employing multi-wave designs is recommended to examine relational dynamics across developmental stages.

Thematic analysis

Thematic analysis revealed three overarching themes that illuminate the quantitative findings: (1) Relational Empowerment, (2) Gender-Congruent Role Modelling and Identification, and (3) Emotional Communication and Empathy. These themes are presented below with illustrative quotes.

Theme 1: relational empowerment

This theme captures how athletes’ perceptions of their coaches’ trust and support directly influenced their sense of confidence and autonomy. Many athletes described a direct link between feeling trusted by their coach and their own self-belief.

—“When my coach trusts me, I trust myself. That’s when I play my best. It’s not about yelling, it’s about knowing she believes in me.” (P15, age 16, female coach).

— “He pushes me hard, but he also tells me he knows I can do it. That makes me feel powerful on the court.” (P21, age 17, male coach).

Theme 2: gender-congruent role modelling and identification

This theme highlights the unique impact of female coaches as role models for female athletes. Many participants expressed a sense of shared understanding and identification with their female coaches, which they felt contributed to a more open and supportive relationship.

— “Seeing a woman in charge, who is strong and knows the game, it makes me think I can be like that too. It’s inspiring.” (P08, age 14, female coach).

— “She understands what it’s like to be a girl in sports. We can talk about things I wouldn’t feel comfortable discussing with a male coach.” (P12, age 15, female coach).

Theme 3: emotional communication and empathy

This theme focuses on the perceived differences in communication styles between male and female coaches. Athletes often described female coaches as more empathetic and emotionally attuned, which fostered a sense of psychological safety.

— “She can tell when I’m having a bad day, and she’ll talk to me about it. It’s not just about basketball; She cares about me as a person.” (P05, age 13, female coach).

— “My male coach is a great tactician, but he’s not as approachable. It’s harder to talk to him about personal stuff.” (P18, age 17, male coach).

Discussion

This study examined the combined effects of coach gender and athlete age on the mental toughness and coach–athlete relational quality of adolescent female basketball players in Turkey, using a sequential explanatory mixed-methods design. Quantitative analyses, robust to clustering effects (CRSE), revealed that athletes coached by women appear to co-occur with higher mental toughness and greater relational closeness, commitment, and complementarity compared to those coached by men. Furthermore, older athletes (17–18 years) tended to score higher on both psychological and relational measures than younger athletes, although the interaction between coach gender and age group was not statistically significant. Regression analyses indicated that relational quality was a significant predictor of mental toughness, suggesting that the interpersonal environment is statistically linked linked to athletes’ psychological resilience.

The qualitative phase provided rich insights into these patterns. Athletes often described female coaches as empathetic, approachable, and communicative, qualities that they linked to feeling understood and supported in both sport and personal domains. Conversely, male coaches were sometimes characterized as more authoritative and less emotionally expressive, leading some athletes to perceive relational distance. These perceptions were not uniform, however; a minority of participants described male coaches as highly supportive and skilled in cultivating mental toughness, demonstrating the diversity of relational experiences in youth sport. Integrating the two strands of data, this study underscores the central role of coach–athlete relationship quality in being statistically associated with mental toughness among adolescent female athletes, with coach gender appearing to function as an important, though not exclusive, determinant of relational dynamics.

The findings are interpreted through the lenses of Self-Determination Theory [13], which emphasizes the fulfilment of basic psychological needs, and Social Role Theory [18], which considers how gendered expectations shape interpersonal behaviour. Importantly, the Turkish context where gender norms in sport are evolving but still constrained by traditional expectations, provides a culturally specific perspective that both aligns with and diverges from existing Western and Eastern literature.

Quantitative findings and their practical implications

H1: Female athletes with female coaches will report higher levels of mental toughness than those with male coaches.

H1: Female athletes with female coaches will report higher levels of mental toughness than those with male coaches.

Our findings indicated a significant effect of coach gender on mental toughness, with a partial eta squared (η²) of 0.13. This effect size, while not exceptionally large, suggests that coach gender is statistically associated with a meaningful portion of the variance in mental toughness among adolescent female athletes. In practical terms, this means that female athletes coached by women are linked to a higher prevalence of mental toughness that can be attributed to their coach’s gender, compared to those coached by men. This is a substantively important finding, as even small to medium effect sizes can have considerable real-world implications in applied settings like youth sport. For instance, a 13% difference in mental toughness could translate into tangible performance advantages, such as improved coping under pressure, greater persistence in challenging situations, and enhanced self-confidence during competition. From a practical standpoint, these results underscore the importance of supporting female coaches in youth sports, not only for gender equity but also for cultivating optimal psychological development in female athletes. The consistent advantage observed for female-coached athletes suggests that coaching education programs should emphasize relational and emotionally attuned coaching styles, which appear to be more prevalent among female coaches and highly beneficial for adolescent girls. Specifically, coach development workshops could include modules on active listening, empathetic communication, and creating a psychologically safe environment, drawing on best practices from female coaches.

The qualitative data deepened this understanding, providing rich narratives that illuminated the mechanisms behind the quantitative findings. Many athletes described female coaches as “someone who understands both my game and my emotions” (P12, age 16), emphasizing emotional resonance as a factor in feeling psychologically strong during competition. This quote highlights how the perceived empathy and understanding from female coaches are linked to an athlete’s sense of psychological safety and confidence, which are core components of mental toughness. Another participant stated, “When I make a mistake, she talks to me, not at me. It makes me want to fight harder next time” (P08, age 14). This powerful testimonial illustrates how autonomy-supportive communication, often observed in female coaches, is associated with intrinsic motivation and resilience, rather than fear of failure. These narratives echo the findings of Voelker et al. [46], who noted that the relational warmth of female role models is linked to both sport-specific and general self-efficacy in adolescent girls.

However, a small number of participants reported equally high mental toughness under male coaches. One athlete explained, “My male coach is tough but fair; he pushes me to my limit, and that’s how I’ve become stronger” (P21, age 17). This suggests that certain male coaches, perhaps those who integrate emotional support with high performance standards, can also support resilience, aligning with the work of [47], who found that relational quality, rather than gender per se, is the critical determinant of athlete outcomes. The qualitative data here provides a crucial nuance: while female coaches generally exhibit traits statistically associated with mental toughness, some male coaches also possess these qualities, demonstrating that effective coaching transcends gender and is rooted in specific relational behaviours. This highlights the need for coaching development programs to focus on cultivating these beneficial behaviours regardless of the coach’s gender.

Culturally, these findings reflect both convergence and divergence with global patterns. In Western contexts, female coaches often have to navigate stereotypes questioning their technical competence [48], yet when given equal authority, their relational approach can be particularly effective for female athletes. In Eastern contexts, especially in collectivist cultures such as Japan and South Korea, relational harmony is highly valued regardless of coach gender, which may partly explain why gender effects are less pronounced in some studies [49]. In Turkey, where women in coaching roles remain underrepresented, female coaches may serve as both skill developers and symbolic figures of empowerment, enhancing athletes’ identification and commitment. The concept of “abi-abla” (older brother-older sister) relationship, prevalent in Turkish culture, might explain the perceived closeness and trust with female coaches, as they often embody a nurturing and guiding figure beyond just a coach. This cultural nuance suggests that relational quality in Turkey may be deeply intertwined with traditional family and community values, where respect and emotional connection are paramount. The findings suggest that the socio-cultural dynamics in Turkey appear to amplify the positive impact of female coaches, making their presence even more critical for the holistic development of female adolescent athletes.

H2: Older athletes will report higher mental toughness and relational quality scores than younger athletes.

H2: Older athletes will report higher mental toughness and relational quality scores than younger athletes.

The data supported H2, with the 17–18 age group outperforming younger athletes across both mental toughness and relational quality measures. This is consistent with developmental research indicating that older adolescents are linked to more advanced coping mechanisms, self-regulation skills, and social perspective-taking abilities [50]. Similar patterns have been documented in Western contexts; for example, Gucciardi et al. [5] found that mental toughness in Australian athletes is associated with increased age and competitive experience. In China, Tian et al. [51] reported that older adolescent athletes demonstrated greater resilience partly due to increased exposure to high-stakes competitions.

The qualitative themes reinforced these trends. Older athletes often spoke about drawing on past experiences to manage pressure. As one participant noted, “When I was younger, I’d panic after a bad play. Now I focus on the next point, I’ve learned not to carry mistakes” (P17, age 18). This quote vividly illustrates the development of coping strategies and emotional regulation with age and experience. Relationally, older athletes described more nuanced interactions with their coaches, with one stating, “We can talk as adults now, not just as player and coach” (P04, age 17). This suggests a shift towards more collaborative and mature coach-athlete relationships as athletes age, cultivating a deeper sense of relatedness and competence.

However, some younger athletes expressed strong relational bonds despite lower mental toughness scores, suggesting that relational quality may develop earlier than certain psychological skills. One 14-year-old athlete reflected, “I feel close to my coach, but I’m still learning how to stay calm under pressure” (P10). This aligns with work by Crust and Clough [52], who argue that while social connectedness can be established early, the translation of that connection into resilience often requires accumulated sport experience. This qualitative insight is crucial for understanding the developmental trajectory of mental toughness, indicating that while strong relationships are foundational, the full manifestation of mental toughness requires time and varied experiences.

From a cultural perspective, the Turkish context may amplify these age effects. In many Turkish sports settings, senior athletes often receive more individual attention and leadership responsibilities, which could accelerate their psychological development. This contrasts with some Western developmental models that emphasize equal opportunity and rotational leadership across age groups [53]. The hierarchical structure often observed in Turkish sports clubs, where older athletes are looked up to and given more responsibility, might contribute to their accelerated development of mental toughness and relational maturity. This cultural emphasis on seniority and respect within teams can provide a unique environment for the cultivation of mental toughness and leadership skills among older adolescent athletes in Turkey, potentially explaining the stronger age effects observed in this study compared to some Western counterparts.

H3: Relational quality (closeness, commitment, complementarity) will positively predict mental toughness.

H3: Relational quality (closeness, commitment, complementarity) will positively predict mental toughness.

Regression analyses confirmed H3, showing that higher relational quality is statistically associated with greater mental toughness. This finding is strongly supported by prior work across contexts. In the UK, Norman [54] demonstrated that athletes who perceived higher relational quality with their coaches reported greater psychological resilience during high-pressure tournaments. Similarly, in South Korea, Kim et al. [55] found that relational quality statistically mediated the relationship between coaching behaviours and athlete well-being, underscoring its central role across cultural contexts.

Qualitatively, the theme of “relational empowerment” captured how athletes interpreted this link. One participant remarked, “When my coach trusts me, I trust myself. That’s when I play my best” (P02, age 15). This quote directly links the coach\‘s trust to the athlete’s self-efficacy and performance, highlighting the profound impact of relational quality on mental toughness. Another explained, “We understand each other without words, it makes me believe I can handle anything” (P19, age 17). This illustrates the deep level of understanding and connection that is associated with a sense of control and confidence, key aspects of mental toughness. These experiences align with the basic psychological need for relatedness outlined in Self-Determination Theory, which is known to support intrinsic motivation and resilience [14].

Yet, divergent experiences were also evident. A participant with low relational quality reported, “I respect my coach, but we don’t talk much outside of practice. Sometimes I feel on my own during tough games” (P14, age 16). This reflects findings by Nicholls and Perry [56], who noted that the absence of relational support can be linked to increased stress and reduced coping efficacy. This contrasting narrative further emphasizes the critical role of relational quality; its absence can lead to feelings of isolation and reduced mental fortitude during challenging moments.

Culturally, the relational–toughness link in this study resonates with literature from both collectivist and individualist societies, but the mechanisms may differ. In Western contexts, relational quality often supports autonomy and individual goal pursuit [17], while in Eastern and Middle Eastern contexts, relational quality may also fulfil culturally specific expectations for social harmony and mutual obligation [57]. In the Turkish setting, where hierarchical respect and personal trust coexist, high-quality relationships may uniquely blend performance support with socio-emotional care. This dual emphasis on performance and socio-emotional well-being, rooted in cultural values, makes the coach-athlete relationship a particularly potent factor in developing mental toughness. The “abi-abla” dynamic, for instance, fosters a sense of familial bond and trust, which can significantly enhance the impact of relational quality on mental toughness by providing a secure base for athletes to develop resilience and self-efficacy within a culturally familiar framework.

Discussion of unexpected findings

While our quantitative analysis did not reveal a statistically significant interaction effect between coach gender and athlete age group on mental toughness or relational quality (p > .05), the qualitative data provided some nuanced insights that might explain this lack of interaction. It appears that while female coaches are generally associated with higher relational quality and mental toughness, the specific mechanisms and their salience might vary across age groups. For instance, younger athletes (12–14) might be more sensitive to the nurturing and empathetic communication styles often exhibited by female coaches, while older athletes (17–18) might benefit more from the role modelling and empowerment aspects. The absence of a significant interaction in the quantitative data could be due to the complex interplay of these factors, where different aspects of the coach-athlete relationship become prominent at different developmental stages, effectively balancing out any clear interaction effect. Future research with larger samples and more granular age group distinctions, potentially employing longitudinal designs, could further explore these subtle developmental shifts in the impact of coach gender.

Limitations and future research

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration and provide avenues for future research. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the relationships between coach gender, relational quality, and mental toughness. Longitudinal studies are needed to track the development of these constructs over time and establish causality. Second, the reliance on self-report measures, while common in sport psychology, may be subject to social desirability bias. Future research could incorporate multi-informant approaches (e.g., coach reports, peer ratings) or observational methods to triangulate findings. Third, the sample was drawn exclusively from female adolescent basketball players in Turkey, limiting the generalizability of findings to other sports, genders, age groups, or cultural contexts. Future studies should replicate this research across diverse populations, including male athletes, different sports, and other non-Western cultures, to explore the universality and cultural specificity of these dynamics. Fourth, while efforts were made to ensure sample diversity, the voluntary participation and specific recruitment networks might introduce some selection bias. Future research could employ more randomized sampling strategies where feasible. Finally, the study focused on coach gender as a primary factor; however, other coach characteristics (e.g., coaching experience, personality, coaching philosophy) could also influence relational quality and mental toughness and should be explored in future investigations.

Practical applications and recommendations

Based on our findings, we propose several actionable recommendations for coaches, sport organizations, and coach education programs, particularly within the Turkish context:

Promote Female Coaches: Given the positive statistical association between female coaches and higher mental toughness and relational quality in female athletes, sport organizations should actively recruit, train, and retain more female coaches. Mentorship programs for aspiring female coaches and initiatives to challenge gender stereotypes in coaching roles are crucial. Specifically, national sports federations in Turkey should develop targeted policies and funding mechanisms to increase the representation of female coaches at all levels of youth sport, recognizing their potential cultural and developmental impact.
