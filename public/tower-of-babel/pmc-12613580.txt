Hyperuricemia-induced kidney injury: a narrative review of mechanisms and therapeutic advances
Authors: Yang Zhang, Fanyi He, Xuezhi Yu, Tiantian Li, Li Zhou, Baochun Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Uric acid (UA) is the final product of purine metabolism in the human body. Excessive accumulation of UA leads to hyperuricemia (HUA) and the deposition of UA crystals in tissues, including the joints and kidneys. HUA has been identified as an independent risk factor for kidney disease. Although the association between HUA and kidney damage has been long established, the underlying pathological and physiological molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. This article reviews the latest advances in the mechanisms of kidney injury caused by HUA, particularly in the context of inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, renal fibrosis, and imbalanced composition of the intestinal microbiota. Additionally, we discuss the advances in the treatment of HUA, aiming to provide valuable references for the study of HUA-induced kidney diseases and therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

Uric acid (2,6,8-trioxypurine, molecular formula C5H4N4O3; UA) is the final product of endogenous and exogenous purine metabolism, primarily synthesized in the liver [1]. Hyperuricemia (HUA) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by increased serum UA levels, arises from an imbalance between UA production and excretion. Intrinsic causes of HUA include overproduction of UA due to increased purine metabolism and impaired renal or intestinal UA excretion, often linked to genetic predispositions or renal tubular dysfunction [2]. Liver damage, such as that seen in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease or hepatitis, can exacerbate HUA by impairing hepatic UA metabolism or altering xanthine oxidase activity, potentially leading to further increases in circulating UA levels [3]. Extensive clinical studies have demonstrated that HUA serves as a fundamental pathological condition associated with certain metabolic diseases, including chronic kidney disease (CKD), gout, cardiovascular disease, and hyperglycemia [4–6]. Notably, HUA shares a bidirectional relationship with hyperglycemia, as elevated UA levels can induce insulin resistance, while hyperglycemia may enhance UA production through increased purine catabolism [7]. HUA has also been identified as an independent risk factor for the development and progression of CKD [8, 9]. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms of HUA-related kidney injury and advances in therapeutic interventions is crucial for preventing and delaying the onset of CKD. In this review, we focus on the potential mechanisms of HUA-induced kidney injury and summarize recent advances in improving renal damage. These insights may provide a useful reference for future research on HUA-induced kidney injury and the development of potential therapeutic strategies.

Pathomechanisms in hyperuricemia-induced kidney injury

Hyperuricemia (HUA) -induced kidney damage involves complex mechanisms, including urate crystal deposition, inflammatory reactions, endothelial dysfunction, renal fibrosis, and dysbiosis of the gut microbiota [10–12]. Among these, the deposition of urate crystals is the most direct form of injury, as they form stones in renal tissues, obstructing the ureters and leading to renal impairment [13]. Additionally, HUA may exacerbate renal damage through various mechanisms such as activating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, and increasing oxidative stress [14, 15]. It is worth noting that although HUA has become an important risk factor for chronic kidney disease (CKD), its early diagnosis and treatment still pose challenges. Therefore, a thorough exploration of the mechanisms by which HUA causes kidney damage and its impact on CKD is crucial for the development of new treatment strategies.

Hyperinflammation and oxidative stress

Studies have shown that increased UA levels in HUA are an independent risk factor for early-stage renal dysfunction in CKD [16]. The solubility of UA under normal physiological conditions (pH 7.5, 37 °C) is 380 µmol/L, with the majority of UA existing in an ionized and soluble form [17–19]. However, in HUA, when UA concentration exceeds 420 µmol/L, the saturation threshold for monosodium urate (MSU) crystals in the serum is surpassed [19]. This leads to the abundant formation and deposition of MSU crystals, which can accumulate in the kidneys and other tissues, causing tissue damage. This process results in tubular injury, glomerulosclerosis, and interstitial fibrosis, culminating in gouty nephropathy [4, 20]. The underlying mechanisms primarily involve dysregulated activation of the innate immune system and oxidative stress [4].

The innate immune system has evolved as a natural defense mechanism in the human body. NOD-like receptors (NLRs), a class of pattern recognition receptors, are widely distributed in the cytoplasm and form a crucial component of the innate immune system. Dysregulation of NLRs can trigger inflammatory responses and contribute to the development of autoimmune diseases [4, 13]. In 2006, Martinon et al. [21] demonstrated that MSU crystals, formed in HUA, induce gouty inflammation through the activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome. The NLRP3 inflammasome comprises NLRP3 protein, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), and pro-cysteinyl aspartate specific proteinase-1 (pro-caspase-1). It is widely expressed in subsets of granulocytes, macrophages, T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, and other immune cells [22, 23]. Upon activation, NLRP3 protein interacts with ASC through PYD, and the CARD domain of ASC recruits pro-caspase-1, which facilitates the assembly of NLRP3 inflammasome and activation of caspase-1, ultimately triggering the release of inflammatory cytokines IL-1 and IL-18, amplifying the inflammatory response [24]. In vitro studies have shown that MSU crystals can activate NLRP3 inflammasome in monocytes and murine peritoneal macrophages, leading tothe secretion of IL-1 and IL-18 [21]. Additionally, research has demonstrated that MSU crystals increase the expression of intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM1) and induce adhesion between human renal mesangial cells and human monocytes, thereby triggering monocyte-driven inflammation. This process involves both the TLR2/4-p38-ICAM1 pathway and the TLR2/4-independent ASC-p38-ICAM1 axis [25]. Furthermore, MSU crystals also directly stimulate the activation of the T cell receptor complex by increasing phosphorylation of the zeta chain. Simultaneously, MSU crystals promote the expression of the T cell proliferation transcription factor c-Myc, leading to T cell proliferation [26]. In line with these cellular responses, gouty nephropathy manifests as both glomerular and tubular injury [27]. Tubular damage is often more prominent due to the preferential deposition of MSU crystals, but mesangial cell activation and glomerulosclerosis have also been observed, highlighting that multiple renal compartments are susceptible to injury [28]. In this context, the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and associated inflammatory pathways in various renal cell types (mesangial cells, tubular epithelial cells, and infiltrating immune cells) may contribute to the progression of renal injury. In summary, the innate immune system is integral to MSU crystal-induced kidney injury in HUA.

In a normal physiological state, UA is considered an effective endogenous antioxidant. However, in HUA, when UA levels become excessively high, soluble UA exhibits pro-oxidant effects and increases the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [29]. Oxidative stress induced by elevated soluble UA levels in HUA has been shown to directly cause kidney damage and can act as an independent risk factor for CKD [30]. MSU crystals, formed when UA concentrations exceed the saturation threshold in HUA, can also exacerbate oxidative stress by activating inflammatory pathways, such as the NLRP3 inflammasome, which further amplify ROS production. Oxidative stress is characterized by the excessive generation of ROS or an increase in pro-oxidants, leading to direct damage to cellular membranes, DNA, proteins, and organelles by reactive oxygen species such as superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroxyl radicals, ultimately causing cellular dysfunction and cell death [31]. Mitochondria, often referred to as cellular powerhouses, are the primary site of ROS production within cells [31, 32]. The kidneys, which are highly oxygen-dependent, play a critical role in providing energy for various cellular functions, including metabolism, hemodynamics, acid-base and electrolyte balance, nutrient reabsorption, and maintenance of fluid homeostasis [33, 34]. Renal tubules are rich in mitochondria, and mitochondrial damage consequently results in tubular dysfunction [14]. Jiang et al. [35] reported that mitochondrial dysfunction and organelle stress suppress adenosine triphosphate (ATP) generation and increase ROS levels, leading to renal impairment. Zhang et al. [36] found in an in vitro study that elevated soluble UA induces oxidative stress in renal mast cells, resulting in mitochondrial damage. Cristóbal-García et al. [37] demonstrated in an in vivo study that long-term HUA, driven by elevated soluble UA, damages renal tubules in rat by disrupting mitochondrial structure and function. While MSU crystals primarily contribute to gouty nephropathy through inflammatory mechanisms, their role in promoting oxidative stress via NLRP3 activation may synergize with soluble UA-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in HUA. In conclusion, maintaining renal mitochondrial homeostasis represents a key therapeutic target for oxidative stress-related kidney injury [34]. Endothelial dysfunction

Endothelial dysfunction refers to the alteration of functional characteristics of the vascular endothelial layer, such aschanges in blood coagulation status, regulation of vascular growth, and remodeling [38, 39]. It is considered a key factor in the pathophysiology of cardiovascular and renal diseases [38, 39]. Endothelial cells secrete various vasoactive substances, including vasodilators (e.g., nitric oxide (NO), prostacyclin, endothelium-derived hyperpolarizing factor) and vasoconstrictors (e.g., endothelin-1, thromboxane A2, angiotensin II) [40, 41]. HUA-induced endothelial dysfunction primarily disrupts endothelium-dependent vasodilation, due to impaired production or reduced bioavailability of NO. Elevated UA in HUA increases ROS production, binds directly to NO, and reduces endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity, leading to increased peroxynitrite (ONOO−) formation, which causes DNA damage, cell death, and lipid peroxidation [42–44]. Growing evidence suggests that UA downregulates NO production and eNOS activity, thereby reducing the bioavailability of NO [45–47]. Studies in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and rat models have confirmed that elevated UA in HUA dose-dependently decreases NO production and eNOS activity [43, 45, 46, 48–50]. Moreover, UA activate the high mobility group box 1/receptor for advanced glycation end products (HMGB1/RAGE) pathway, promoting nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) activation and inflammatory cytokine expression, further exacerbating endothelial dysfunction [51]. These studies shed light on the mechanisms underlying HUA-induced endothelial dysfunction. The mechanism of HUA-induced endothelial dysfunction is shown in Fig. 2.

Renal fibrosis

Renal fibrosis represents a key outcome of CKD progression, characterized by the excessive accumulation and growth of fibrous connective tissue in the kidneys. It is primarily manifested as glomerulosclerosis and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, leading to the loss of functional renal units, a progressive decline in kidney function, and ultimately, end-stage renal diseases (ESRDs) [52, 53]. Clinical studies have shown that patients with HUA present with more severe renal dysfunction and pathological changes, particularly segmental glomerulosclerosis and tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis [54]. Renal interstitial fibrosis involves the activation of myofibroblasts and accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM), often driven by inflammatory responses and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [55, 56]. In hyperuricemic nephropathy, elevated UA levels induce EMT, a critical process that transforms epithelial cells into myofibroblasts, thereby promoting renal fibrosis through increased ECM production [57–60].

Elevated UA in HUA downregulates the mRNA expression of the EMT marker E-cadherin, reducing its production and simultaneously promoting its degradation [61–63]. These changes in E-cadherin expression play an important role in renal tubular cell EMT and renal fibrosis [61–63]. In 2013, Ryu et al. [63] proposed a new mechanism for HUA-induced phenotypic transition in renal tubular cells. They found that UA in HUA activates Snail and Slug, inducing EMT by reducing E-cadherin synthesis and increasing its degradation [63]. Subsequently, Kang et al. [61] investigated EMT in renal tubular cells cultured with elevated UA in HUA and reached similar conclusions to Ryu et al., but with more detailed findings. Their study demonstrated that elevated UA in HUA promotes the expression of Snail and Slug, which are transcriptional repressors of E-cadherin, leading to reduced E-cadherin mRNA levels and production [61]. Simultaneously, elevated UA in HUA facilitated E-cadherin degradation through ubiquitination [61]. Additionally, Kang et al. [61] observed decreased E-cadherin expression and increased alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression in the kidneys of hyperuricemic rats, providing strong evidence for the significant role of EMT in HUA-induced renal fibrosis.

Further in-depth research has revealed that multiple upstream signaling pathways regulate HUA-induced EMT, including the NLRP3 inflammasome, PI3K/Akt, oxidative stress, and ERK1/2. Romero et al. [64] found increased expression of NLRP3/ASC in the renal epithelium of rats under HUA with elevated UA levels, suggesting that elevated UA in HUA can promote renal EMT by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome. In another study, the review by Zhang and Wang [65] pointed out that the NLRP3 inflammasome regulates inflammatory responses and is associated with myofibroblast differentiation during renal fibrosis. The PI3K/Akt pathway has also been reported to be involved in HUA-induced EMT in renal tubular cells. Xiong et al. [66] detected activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway in NRK-52E cells (a normal rat kidney cell line) cultured with high UA and observed significant morphological changes accompanied by increased α-SMA expression and decreased E-cadherin expression. Kang et al. [61, 67] reported that elevated UA levels induce phenotypic transition in renal tubular epithelial cells and vascular endothelial cells through oxidative stress, leading to renal interstitial fibrosis. Additionally, elevated UA levels in HUA activate the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), upregulate angiotensin II, promote vasoconstriction and vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) proliferation, resulting in thickening of the blood vessel wall, luminal occlusion, renal arteriopathy, renal hypoperfusion, and accelerated tubulointerstitial inflammation and fibrosis [15]. These vascular changes contribute to the development of hypertension, a key driver of kidney disease progression. HUA promotes hypertension through multiple mechanisms, including endothelial dysfunction, reduced NO bioavailability, and RAAS activation [68–70]. Specifically, elevated UA levels in HUA impair endothelial function by reducing NO production, which leads to impaired vasodilation and increased vascular resistance [68]. Concurrently, HUA-induced RAAS activation upregulates angiotensin II, further promoting vasoconstriction and sodium retention, which elevate blood pressure [70]. Clinical and experimental studies have demonstrated that HUA is an independent risk factor for hypertension, with evidence suggesting a causal relationship, as lowering UA levels with xanthine oxidase inhibitors, such as allopurinol, can attenuate blood pressure elevations in hyperuricemic models [71, 72]. Moreover, previous studies have confirmed in rats that HUA stimulates the intrarenal renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), leading to glomerular hypertension and interstitial fibrosis [73]. Zha et al. [62] found that culturing NRK-52E cells (rat proximal tubular epithelial cells) with UA led to increased expression of α-SMA, decreased E-cadherin expression, and thus promoted EMT, which was importantly accompanied by increased production of endothelin-1 (ET-1), activation of extracellular-regulated protein kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2), and upregulation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase 4 (NOX4). Based on these findings, the authors suggested that elevated UA in HUA may induce EMT in renal tubular epithelial cells through NADPH oxidase-mediated ERK1/2 activation and subsequent ET-1 release.

The above studies demonstrate that elevated UA levels can induce renal fibrosis by regulating cell EMT, the NLRP3 inflammasome, PI3K/Akt, oxidative stress, and ERK1/2. Additionally, renal fibrosis is accompanied by persistent expression of specific genes, including Wnt and Notch, that are critical for renal fibrosis development [74, 75]. Gut microbiota dysbiosis

The gut microbiota is the most important and complex microbial ecosystem in the human body, playing a crucial role in the gut-kidney axis and is vital for maintaining overall health, especially kidney health [11, 76]. Approximately one-third of UA is excreted through the intestine, where it may be metabolized by specific gut microbes into various degradation products, such as allantoin, ammonia, and carbon dioxide. These microbial metabolites may exert local or systemic effects on host physiology, including renal function [77, 78]. A recent study suggests a close relationship between the newly discovered pathogenesis of HUA and the imbalance of the gut microbiota [11, 79]. Significant differences in gut microbiota composition exist between HUA patients and healthy individuals [10, 80]. In HUA models, increases in certain genera of bacteria may be positively correlated with elevated serum UA levels [81]. In the feces of HUA patients, the abundance of some dominant bacteria, such as Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes, significantly increases, while Archaea and archaeal polysaccharides decrease markedly [82]. Yu Y et al. found reductions in the abundance of Clostridia, Desulfovibrionaceae, Ruminococcus, and Lactobacillus in hyperuricemic rats [83]. Moreover, when feces from hyperuricemic mice are transplanted into normal mice, the UA levels in the recipient mice increase [84]. Several studies indicate an imbalance in the gut microbiota of HUA patients [82, 83, 85]. More importantly, there is a bidirectional relationship between these gut microbiota changes and HUA. On the one hand, reducing UA levels improves this imbalance in the gut microbiota [83]. On the other hand, a recent time-resolved study by Liu et al. demonstrated that gut microbiota dysbiosis and impairment of the intestinal barrier occurred in the early stages of HUA modeling, even prior to significant elevation of serum UA levels [86]. This suggests that microbial alterations may not merely be a consequence of HUA but could act as a predisposing factor that contributes to disease onset and progression. Furthermore, gut dysbiosis may lead to the accumulation of uremic toxins such as p-cresyl sulfate and indoxyl sulfate, which originate from microbial metabolism and have been shown to aggravate kidney injury [87]. Thus, current evidence strongly supports a correlation between microbial dysbiosis, gut barrier dysfunction, and HUA development, but further mechanistic and interventional studies are needed to establish causality.

Treatment targets for UA-induced kidney injury

The treatment of HUA has always been a focus of clinical research, with existing strategies mainly divided into non-pharmacological and pharmacological interventions. Non-pharmacological treatments primarily focus on restricting purine-rich diets, but their effectiveness in lowering UA levels is limited, often necessitating drug regulation by patients. Currently, mainstream drugs include UA synthesis inhibitors (such as allopurinol) and uricosuric agents (such as probenecid), which can effectively reduce serum UA (SUA) levels. Clinical trials have demonstrated that Allopurinol does not effectively affect the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in patients with stage 3/4 CKD [88]. However, long-term use may lead to gastrointestinal reactions, allergies, or hepatorenal toxicity, restricting their clinical application [12, 89, 90]. Therefore, researchers are gradually shifting their focus towards molecular target interventions, such as targeted regulation of renal urate transport proteins, the NLRP3 pathway, the PI3K/AKT pathway, and the development of natural UA-lowering pathways. This includes modulating the gut microbiota, optimizing transporter protein function, and utilizing probiotics/prebiotics as intervention methods [12, 91–93], see Table 1. In summary, developing efficient and side-effect-free methods to reduce UA concentrations holds significant clinical importance.

Targeted therapy for lowering UA

Studies have shown that the deposition of MSU crystals can be reversed. When serum UA levels decrease below the saturation point and inflammation is alleviated, MSU crystals can dissolve [94]. Research has demonstrated that the reduction rate of gout stones’ diameter is twice as rapid when serum UA levels are below 4 mg/dL compared to levels above 5 mg/dL [95]. Therefore, lowering UA levels is an effective approach for treating HUA-induced kidney damage. Currently, there are two main classes of drugs used clinically to lower blood UA: xanthine oxidase inhibitors (XOI) that inhibit UA synthesis, including allopurinol and febuxostat, and drugs that enhance UA excretion (e.g., probenecid). However, these drugs possess specific limitations. Allopurinol is metabolized to active metabolites in the liver and subsequently excreted by the kidneys. When renal function is impaired, allopurinol can accumulate in the body, thereby increasing the risk of drug toxicity [95]. Probenecid can induce hepatic steatosis and is not suitable for patients with renal insufficiency [13, 96]. Therefore, it is necessary to explore additional therapeutic targets or low-toxicity alternatives that effectively address UA-induced kidney damage.

Molecular target intervention strategies

Extensive research has demonstrated the significant role of NLRP3 in UA-induced kidney damage, where UA can activate NLRP3, stimulating the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18, leading to severe renal injury [4, 97]. Therefore, it may be inferred that inhibiting NLRP3 may reduce renal inflammation and alleviate kidney damage. Zhou et al. [98] pointed out that Shizhifang can inhibit UA-induced pyroptosis of tubular epithelial cell by targeting NLRP3, suppressing tubular inflammation, and effectively preventing and halting the progression of UA-related kidney injury. Zhang et al. [99] found that Simiao San has a therapeutic effect in relieving HUA and renal inflammation, which may be related to its inhibitory effect on NLRP3 inflammasome. Cui et al. [100] discovered that mice with HUA exhibited renal dysfunction, fibrosis, and inflammation. In contrast, Phloretin significantly improved renal injury in mice with HUA, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in the kidneys, and inhibited the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as NLRP3 [100]. In vitro experiments further demonstrated that Phloretin inhibited NLRP3 pathway activation in HUA-induced HK-2 cells [100]. Cui et al. [101] found that chloroquine (CQ) significantly inhibited the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome, reduced the levels of NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, and IL-1β in mice with hyperuricemic nephropathy, and improved renal function. These findings suggest that NLRP3 can serve as a therapeutic target for HUA-induced inflammatory kidney injury, and inhibiting its expression may improve renal damage. Additionally, autophagy, as an important process in CKD, has been found to negatively regulate NLRP3 inflammasome [102, 103]. Studies have shown that inducing autophagy promotes the degradation of NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, inhibiting the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome [103]. Therefore, inducing autophagy may also have a beneficial effect on improving kidney damage. Wang YJ et al. found that Pterostilbene has a nephroprotective effect in HUA-induced nephropathy. Further investigation of its mechanism revealed that Pterostilbene inhibits TGF-β-mediated NLRP3 inflammasome activation and EMT by inducing autophagy [104].

Research has shown that approximately 90% of patients with HUA have impaired renal UA excretion function [105]. The renal UA excretion function relies on the expression and activity of various UA transport proteins [106, 107]. ATP-binding cassette transporter G2 (ABCG2) is widely present in the kidneys and intestines and has been identified as a high-capacity UA transporter that significantly promotes UA excretion in renal tubular epithelial cells [108]. Dysfunctions of ABCG2 have been associated with serum UA levels and the risk of gout/HUA [109, 110]. Glucose transporter member 9 (GLUT9) is a basolateral and apical membrane transporter protein in the proximal tubule epithelial cells of the kidneys, mediating the reabsorption of UA in the kidneys. Deficiency of this protein directly affects UA transport [106, 109]. In addition, urate transporter 1 (URAT1), organic cation transporter 2 (OCT2, SLC22A2), and organic anion transporters (OATs) are also crucial for the absorption, distribution, and excretion of UA in the kidneys [96]. These findings suggest that modulating these renal UA transport proteins may help counteract HUA and protect the kidneys. Clinically available drugs such as benzbromarone and probenecid can promote UA excretion [111]. Benzbromarone, as a first-line medication for improving UA excretion, is an effective inhibitor of URAT1 and can promote UA excretion by inhibiting the reabsorption process, ultimately maintaining renal function without significant changes in eGFR. However, its use is associated with side effects such as hepatic steatosis and has certain limitations for patients with renal impairment [96]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find candidate drugs with lower toxicity and higher efficacy for improving UA excretion and treating HUA. The recent clinical study by Tanabe et al. confirmed that dotinurad may have good tolerability in patients with HUA and may achieve efficacy comparable to current standard treatments in patients with stage 3/4 CKD [112]. Yang B et al. found that naringenin dose-dependently reduced serum UA levels in hyperuricemic mice and improved renal dysfunction [113]. In their study, naringenin decreased the expression of GLUT9 by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and increased the expression of ABCG2 by enhancing the abundance of PDZK1 [113]. Furthermore, naringenin inhibited the TLR4/NF-κB signaling pathway, suppressing the release of inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α both in vivo and in vitro. This suggests that naringenin can promote renal UA excretion and alleviate inflammation in the kidneys [113]. Wang S et al. investigated the protective effects and mechanisms of Smilax glabra Roxb. (SGF) in a rat model of urate nephropathy induced by a high-purine diet [114]. In their study, SGF significantly inhibited oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions in the kidneys and reversed UA-induced kidney damage. Further research revealed that SGF treatment significantly enhanced UA excretion, which was associated with increased expression of ABCG2, OAT1, OCT2, and organic cation/carnitine transporter 2 (OCTN2) [114]. Based on these findings, the authors proposed that SGF could improve renal oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions in urate nephropathy rats by promoting UA excretion [114]. The above results indicate that UA transporters play a crucial role in the absorption, distribution, and excretion of UA and that by regulating the expression of UA transporters and promoting UA excretion, they can also improve kidney damage.

Emerging therapeutic targets

The kidney is the main pathway for UA excretion, with approximately 2/3 of UA excreted into the urine through the renal pathway, while the remaining 1/3 of UA is excreted into the intestines and further metabolized by intestinal bacteria [12]. Clinical studies have found significant differences in the fecal microbiota composition between gout and HUA patients compared to the normal population [82, 115]. In HUA animal models, the abundance of certain genera of bacteria is notably reduced, such as Prevotella, Dehalobacterium, Ruminococcus, and Lactobacillus [83, 116, 117]. Additionally, several transport proteins that promote UA secretion have been identified in different segments of the intestines, with the elimination of UA induced by these transport proteins extensively studied [118]. This suggests that the intestinal microbiota may serve as a new target for alleviating and treating HUA and related diseases. In recent years, strategies for regulating the intestinal microbiota have gradually become a research hotspot in the field of HUA treatment due to their favorable safety characteristics [119]. As two primary forms of microbial intervention, probiotics and prebiotics demonstrate unique therapeutic potential through multi-pathway intervention mechanisms [120].

Probiotics are composed of yeast or live bacteria with various health benefits, and have made significant contributions to regulating the intestinal microbiota [120, 121]. Among these probiotics, the group represented by the Lactobacillus genus effectively regulates intestinal microbial balance and is a major category in improving HUA [121, 122]. It has been reported that the Lactobacillus genus plays a key role in the expression regulation of the UA degradation enzyme system (including uricase, allantoinase, etc.), effectively reducing serum UA levels by catalyzing the biological conversion of UA to urea [121, 123]. Furthermore, research indicates that Lactobacillus can also reduce SUA levels and the incidence of HUA by decreasing the absorption of purines in the intestines [124]. Some foods rich in Lactobacillus, such as a strain of Lactobacillus in Chinese sour cabbage (L. plantarum strain DM9218) and Lactobacillus plantarum strain WCFS1, with 99% similarity, can be used as probiotics to prevent HUA by assimilating purine nucleosides (adenosine and guanosine) [125]. Clinical observations have shown increased abundance of Bifidobacterium and decreased abundance of Clostridium and Bacteroides in the gut microbiota of gout patients [82]. Similarly, Bifidobacterium and Clostridium are reduced in HUA rat models but are restored by supplementation with tuna meat oligopeptides (TMOP) [89]. Moreover, phenotypes of HUA and kidney inflammation were alleviated after transplanting the fecal microbiota of TMOP-treated mice, suggesting that the beneficial effects of TMOP on HUA are at least partially mediated by the gut microbiota [89]. Therefore, using natural products rich in probiotics or even fecal transplantation to regulate the gut microbiota and maintain intestinal homeostasis may become a new intestinal microbiota therapy for HUA.

Represented by polyphenols, peptides, and plant bioactive ingredients, prebiotics can intervene in the process of HUA through multiple pathways such as regulating intestinal microbial homeostasis, restoring intestinal epithelial barrier function, and inhibiting inflammatory signaling pathways [120]. Studies have found that these substances can not only improve UA excretion disorders but also suppress the development of chronic inflammation by modulating the gut microbiota [84]. For instance, intervention with Simiao decoction in a gouty arthritis model significantly reduces the abundance of pro-inflammatory bacteria Bifidobacterium, and can alleviate HUA by inhibiting UA generation through the gut microbiota-lipopolysaccharide-TLRs/NF-κB axis [126]. In terms of UA metabolism regulation, the intestinal tract, as the second major excretory pathway after the kidneys, plays a crucial role through its transport protein system (including ABCG2, URAT-1, etc.). Studies have shown that ABCG2, as the primary intestinal UA transporter, significantly increases the risk of HUA when its activity is reduced. Many herbs and plant compounds can enhance the activity of ABCG2 in the intestines, promote UA excretion, thereby reducing serum UA levels, and exert prebiotic effects in the prevention and treatment of HUA. For example, plants of the Dendrobium genus [127], berberine [128], resveratrol [129], inulin [130], Punicalagin [105], among others, have been confirmed to have such regulatory effects. The prebiotic effects of these natural products stem from their multidimensional regulation of the gut microbiota: on one hand, they improve gut environment by reshaping microbial balance, and on the other hand, they maintain mucosal barrier integrity by coordinating the proliferation dynamics of intestinal epithelial cells. Moreover, their anti-inflammatory properties can alleviate the interference of chronic inflammation with UA metabolism, while enhancing UA excretion by increasing UA transcription factors.

Treatment targets for UA-induced kidney injury

Conclusion

In summary, HUA can affect kidney function through mechanisms such as abnormal activation of the innate immune response, oxidative stress, induction of endothelial dysfunction, renal fibrosis, tubular EMT, and gut microbiota imbalance. Furthermore, strategies such as reducing UA levels, inhibiting NLRP3 expression, promoting UA excretion, and improving gut microbiota are effective approaches to reversing UA-induced kidney injury. Taken together, this review summarizes current understanding that may serve as a reference for identifying potential therapeutic targets in HUA-related kidney disease.
