Rubik’s Abstract Polytopes
Giovanni Luca Marchetti

Abstract
We generalize the Rubik’s cube, together with its group of configurations, to any abstract regular polytope. After discussing general aspects, we study the Rubik’s simplex of arbitrary dimension and provide a complete description of the associated group. We sketch an analogous argument for the Rubik’s hypercube as well.

1
Introduction

The
Rubik’s cube
is a mechanical puzzle invented by Ernő Rubik in
1974
. It has since become an iconic brain-teaser due to its overwhelming difficulty disguised by an elementary geometric premise. Indeed, the puzzle consists of a cube partitioned into colored pieces that can be permuted by rotating its faces. Starting from an arbitrary configuration, the goal is to reach the initial one characterized by monochromatic faces. There are, however, more than
43
quintillions of possible configurations that are connected via face rotations. Planning a solution is, therefore, a surprisingly challenging task.

Throughout the years, the Rubik’s cube has inspired a plethora of variations and generalizations – see
[
6
]
for an overview. For example, similar physical puzzles have seen light in alternative shapes, such as the Platonic solids (Figure
1
). The Rubik’s dodecahedron and tetrahedron have been commercialized under the names
Megaminx
and
Halpern-Meier Pyramid
respectively. Even further, higher-dimensional analogues of the Rubik’s cube – the Rubik’s
hypercubes
– have been simulated via software
[
7
]
and solved (Figure
2
, right).

From a mathematical perspective, the core interesting aspect of the Rubik’s cube and of similar puzzles is that the set of possible configurations forms a group. The latter has face rotations as generators and the initial configuration as the identity element. Solving the puzzle can be rephrased as finding a path to the identity in the Cayley graph associated to the generators. The groups of the Rubik’s cube and of its analogues manifest intricate structures that have been described and extensively studied
[
3
,
1
]
. However, each such group is typically discussed separately, raising the need for a unified mathematical treatment.

Figure 2:
One the left, the Rubik’s hyperbolic triangular tiling. On the right, the Rubik’s
4
-dimensional hypercube projected to the
3
-dimensional space.

In this work, we generalize the Rubik’s puzzle, together with the associated group, to arbitrary
regular polytopes
. To this end, we rely on the language of
abstract
polytopes in the sense of
[
5
]
. This elegant combinatorial formalism allows us to formulate a general theory that is applicable to polytopes in arbitrary dimensions and in arbitrary geometries. Along the way, we derive constrains on the Rubik’s group of a regular polytope generalizing the classical ones for the Rubik’s cube, and directly compute the group of Rubik’s polygons and Rubik’s hosohedra. We then focus on the case of the simplex in arbitrary dimension. In order to describe the associated group, we rely on an inductive argument involving the group of the facets, which are lower-dimensional simplices. Finally, in the last section we argue that the group of the Rubik’s hypercube can be derived similarly since its facets are lower-dimensional hypercubes as well. The same result for the hypercube has been obtained with a similar inductive argument in
[
8
]
.

1.1
Notation and Conventions

We will make extensive use of permutations and symmetric groups. To this end, we denote by
{\rm Sym}_{k}
the symmetric group consisting of permutations of the finite set
\{1,\cdots,k\}
. We follow the functional convention and write the composition product in
{\rm Sym}_{k}
as
(\pi_{1}\pi_{2})(i)=\pi_{1}(\pi_{2}(i))
. Thus, all the expressions involving permutations have to be read from right to left. We denote by
{\rm sign}\colon{\rm Sym}_{k}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
the sign homomorphism and by
{\rm Alt}_{k}
its kernel, i.e., the alternating group consisting of even permutations.

The Rubik’s Construction

We now introduce the core construction of the present work. Consider an
n
-dimensional abstract regular polytope
(\mathcal{P},\preceq)
.

Definition 5
.

The
Rubik’s construction

{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})
of
\mathcal{P}
is the set of pairs
(F,G)
where
F,G\not=F_{-1},F_{n}
are faces such that
F\preceq G
.

In other words,
{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})
coincides with the ordering
\preceq
seen as a binary relation on
\mathcal{P}\setminus\{F_{-1},F_{n}\}
. This represents an abstraction of the physical Rubik’s cube puzzle. A pair
(F,G)\in{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})
such that
G
is a facet abstracts a colored piece of the puzzle:
F
represents the location of the piece (vertex, edge etc.), while
G
represents the color. Based on this, for an element
(F,G)\in{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})
, we refer to the components
F
and
G
as its
location
and its
color
, respectively. Differently from the physical puzzle, our definition includes colors of lower rank. This is done purely for the sake of elegance: since an automorphism of a polytope is determined by its action on facets, constraining
G
to be a facet would result in an equivalent theory. We now describe the moves that can be performed on
{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})
.

Definition 6
.

Let
H\in\mathcal{P}
be a facet and
\phi\in\Gamma^{+}(H/F_{-1})
be a rotation. By considering
\phi
as an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}
stabilizing
H
via the isomorphism
\Gamma^{+}(\mathcal{P},H)\simeq\Gamma^{+}(H/F_{-1})
, define the

\mu_{\phi}
associated to
\phi
as the permutation of
{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})
given by:

\mu_{\phi}(F,G)=\begin{cases}(\phi(F),\phi(G))&{\rm if}\ F\preceq H,\\
(F,G)&{\rm otherwise.}\end{cases}

(3)

The
Rubik’s group

\Gamma{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})
is the permutation group on
{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})
generated by the moves
\mu_{\phi}
as
H
varies among the facets of
\mathcal{P}
and
\phi
varies among the rotations stabilizing
H
.

\phi\in\Gamma^{+}(F/F_{-1})

Rubik’s groups can be interpreted as a combinatorial analogue of

4

. In this picture, regular polytopes
\mathcal{P}
are thought as ‘discrete manifolds’, whose tangent space at

is represented by the set

. The action by the move
\mu_{\phi}
mimics the parallel transport of vectors tangent to a facet

by part of a rotation
\phi
of that facet. Therefore, the Rubik’s group is reminiscent of the holonomy group of the tangent bundle of a Riemannian manifold.

For each direct factor of Equation

Our strategy is to first show that the canonical characters of the direct factors do not depend on the choice of coordinates. To see this, fix two system of coordinates whose isomorphisms we denote by

and a face

3.2
On the Non-Rotational Version

So far, we have defined the Rubik’s group via rotational automorphisms, which is in line with the physical Rubik’s puzzle. It is of course possible to define a non-rotational version
\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(\mathcal{P})
of the Rubik’s group by allowing
\phi
in Definition
6
to range among arbitrary automorphisms in
\Gamma(H/F_{-1})
. The wreath product representation from Section
3.1

as well as Proposition
3.2
hold for
\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(\mathcal{P})
, albeit without the rotational constraint on vertices in Equation
5
.

We start by computing the non-rotational Rubik’s group of the simplest regular polytopes, i.e., the polygons
P_{k}
from Example
2.1
. Note that since facets of polygons are segments with no non-trivial rotations,
\Gamma{\rm R}(P_{k})
is trivial instead. The following result describes the group of Rubik’s polygons completely.

Proposition 3.3
.

There is an isomorphism

\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(P_{k})\simeq\begin{cases}{\rm Sym}_{k}&{\rm if}\ k{\rm\ is\ even}\\
{\rm Ker}(\chi)&{\rm if}\ k{\rm\ is\ odd}\end{cases}

(8)

where
\chi\colon C_{2}\wr{\rm Sym}_{k}\rightarrow C_{2}
denotes the canonical character.

Proof.

Let
F_{1},\cdots,F_{k}
and
G_{1},\cdots,G_{k}
be the vertices and edges of
P_{k}
listed in their cyclic order, meaning that
F_{i}\preceq G_{i}\succeq F_{i+1}
for all
i
. We can then define a system of coordinates on
P_{k}
by setting
\mathcal{A}_{0}=F_{n}/F_{1}
and by identifying
G_{i-1},G_{i}\in F_{n}/F_{i}
with
G_{k},G_{1}\in\mathcal{A}_{0}
respectively. The construction in Section
3.1
provides then an embedding
\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(P_{k})\hookrightarrow C_{2}\wr{\rm Sym}_{k}
where
C_{2}\simeq\Gamma(\mathcal{A}_{0})
is the cyclic group of order two. Since
G_{i}/F_{-1}
is a segment for all

, there is a unique non-trivial automorphism
\phi_{i}\in\Gamma(P_{k},G_{i})\simeq C_{2}
fixing
G_{i}
. The move
\mu_{\phi_{i}}\in\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(P_{k})
corresponds to the element

where

is a transposition and the

of

is non-trivial only for

. Since transpositions generate the symmetric group, the projection
\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(P_{k})\rightarrow{\rm Sym}_{k}
is surjective. We now distinguish the two cases.

Case 1
:
k
is even. In this case, the elements
(\tau,\sigma)\in C_{2}\wr{\rm Sym}_{k}
lying in the image of
\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(P_{k})
satisfy the following condition:
\tau^{j}\in C_{2}
coincides with
\sigma^{-1}(j)-j
modulo
2
. Since
k
is even, this condition defines a subgroup of
C_{2}\wr{\rm Sym}_{k}
and it is immediate to verify that it is satisfied by the moves
\mu_{\phi_{i}}
. This means that
\tau
is determined by
\sigma
, which in turn implies that the projection
\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(P_{k})\rightarrow{\rm Sym}_{k}
is an isomorphism, as desired.

Case 2
:
k
is odd. Given
i
, consider the element of
\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(P_{k})
defined by the following product of

moves:

\mu=\mu_{\phi_{i+1}}\cdots\mu_{\phi_{k+i-3}}\mu_{\phi_{k+i-2}}\mu_{\phi_{k+i-1}}\cdots\mu_{\phi_{i+1}}\mu_{\phi_{i}}

(9)

where all the indices are modulo
k

\mu
goes back and forth through all the moves in their cyclic order. It is immediate to check that
\mu
corresponds to
(\tau,\sigma)\in C_{2}\wr{\rm Sym}_{k}
, where
\sigma=1
and
\tau^{j}
is non-trivial only for

. These elements generate all the
(\tau,1)

. Together with the fact that the projection
\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(P_{k})\rightarrow{\rm Sym}_{k}
is surjective, this shows that the image of
\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(P_{k})
in
C_{2}\wr{\rm Sym}_{k}
is
{\rm Ker}(\chi)
, as desired.
∎

We now draw a connection between the rotational and the non-rotational version of the Rubik’s group. To this end, we will make use of hosotopes (Example

), and in particular of the group isomorphism given by Equation

in terms of the non-rotational Rubik’s group of

.

Proposition 3.4
.

There is a group embedding:

\Gamma{\rm R}(\oslash(\mathcal{P}))\hookrightarrow\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(\mathcal{P})\times\Gamma(\mathcal{P}),

(10)

with the projection
\Gamma{\rm R}(\oslash(\mathcal{P}))\rightarrow\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(\mathcal{P})
being surjective. If
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
is generated by stabilizers of facets of
\mathcal{P}
, then the projection
\Gamma{\rm R}(\oslash(\mathcal{P}))\rightarrow\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
is surjective as well.

Proof.

Recall that
\oslash(\mathcal{P})=\mathcal{P}\sqcup\{V_{1},V_{2}\}
. In particular, for a face
F\in\mathcal{P}
the interval
F_{n}/F
is the same when
F
is seen as a face of
\mathcal{P}
or of
\oslash(\mathcal{P})
. On the other hand, if

then there is an isomorphism
F_{n}/F\simeq\mathcal{P}
. This implies that there exists a decomposition

{\rm R}(\oslash(\mathcal{P}))\simeq{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})\sqcup\mathcal{P}\sqcup\mathcal{P}.

(11)

Now pick a move
\mu_{\phi}\in\Gamma{\rm R}(\oslash(\mathcal{P}))
as in Definition
6
associated to a rotation
\phi\in\Gamma^{+}(\oslash(\mathcal{P}),F)
, where
F
is a facet of
\oslash(\mathcal{P})
. Then
F
is a facet of
\mathcal{P}
as well,

restricts on to an automorphism
\hat{\phi}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{P},F)
and
\mu_{\phi}
restricts to
\mu_{\hat{\phi}}
on
{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})
. Moreover,

fixes both

and

if and only if
\hat{\phi}\in\Gamma^{+}(\mathcal{P},F)
, in which case

restricts to
\hat{\phi}
on both the copies of
\mathcal{P}
in Equation
11

\hat{\phi}\not\in\Gamma^{+}(\mathcal{P},F)
then

swaps

and
V_{2}
, and induces the isomorphism
\hat{\phi}
between the two copies of
\mathcal{P}
. This implies that
\hat{\phi}
carries all the information of how
\mu_{\phi}
acts on
\mathcal{P}\sqcup\mathcal{P}
. By associating
\mu_{\phi}\mapsto(\mu_{\hat{\phi}},\hat{\phi})
we obtain a group embedding as in Equation

\Gamma(\mathcal{P},F)

due to Equation

, the projection
\Gamma{\rm R}(\oslash(\mathcal{P}))\rightarrow\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(\mathcal{P})
is surjective. The last claim is immediate by construction, since

always fixes a facet of
\mathcal{P}
.
∎

Next, we completely characterize the Rubik’s group of hosotopes of dimension
3

the hosohedron associated to the

Proposition 3.5
.

There is an isomorphism

\Gamma{\rm R}(\oslash_{k})\simeq\begin{cases}{\rm Sym}_{k}\times C_{k}&{\rm if}\ k{\rm\ is\ even}\\
{\rm Ker}(\chi)\times C_{k}&{\rm if}\ k{\rm\ is\ odd}\end{cases}

(12)

where
C_{k}\simeq\Gamma^{+}(P_{k})
denotes the cyclic group of order
k
and

denotes the canonical character.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.4
there is an embedding
\Gamma{\rm R}(\oslash_{k})\hookrightarrow\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(P_{k})\times D_{2k}
, where

denotes the dihedral group, with the projection to

being surjective. Note that non-trivial moves in

correspond to non-rotational automorphisms in

and to transpositions in

. This means that we can replace

with

and obtain an embedding
\Gamma{\rm R}(\oslash_{k})\hookrightarrow\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(P_{k})\times C_{k}
. In what follows, we will adhere to the convention from the proof of Proposition

and denote by

and
G_{1},\cdots,G_{k}
the vertices and edges of

in their cyclic order. These correspond to the edges and facets of
\oslash_{k}
as well. We will additionally denote by
\phi_{i}\in\Gamma^{+}(\oslash_{k},G_{i})
the only non-trivial rotation fixing

and by
\hat{\phi}_{i}\in\Gamma(P_{k},G_{i})

Case 1
:

\Gamma{\rm R}^{-}(\oslash_{k})

Case 2
:

is odd. Given

∎

3.3
Extending Moves from Facets

In this section, we describe a procedure to extend moves of the Rubik’s group of a facet of a polytope to moves of the Rubik’s group of the whole polytope. Since facets have rank
n-1
, this can be exploited for arguments and constructions that are inductive with respect to the dimension
n

F
of
\mathcal{P}
and a facet
G
of

, which is a ridge (i.e., of rank

) when seen as a face
\mathcal{P}
. By the diamond property there is a unique facet

of

such that

. We extend the move

to the move

. Note that inclusion gives a natural embedding

. With an additional technical assumption, the following result guarantees that the move extension is coherent with the embedding.

Proposition 3.6
.

Suppose that any two facets of
\mathcal{P}
have at most one incident ridge and pick

and
\phi
as above. Then the move
\mu_{\phi^{\prime}}
, seen as a permutation of
{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})
, restricts on
{\rm R}(F/F_{-1})
to

. In other words, the following diagram commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\rm R}(\mathcal{P})}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{{\rm R}(F/F_{-1})}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{\phi^{\prime}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{\phi}}

Since by hypothesis

incident to both

and

, every face incident to both

and

4
The Rubik’s Simplex

In this section, we study the Rubik’s group for a classical regular polytope in arbitrary dimension: the
simplex
. We start by recalling the definition of the latter.

Definition 8
.

For
n\geq 0
, the
n
-dimensional

\triangle^{n}
is the abstract polytope whose faces are the subsets of
\{0,\cdots,n\}
ordered by inclusion.

For a face
F\in\triangle^{n}
of cardinality
r+1
, its rank is given by
r
. The simplex has thus
\binom{n+1}{r+1}
faces of rank
r
, where
\binom{\cdot}{\cdot}
denotes the binomial coefficient. There are natural isomorphisms
F/F_{-1}\simeq\triangle^{r}
and
F_{n}/F\simeq\triangle^{n-r-1}
given by restricting subsets. The group of automorphisms of the simplex can easily be characterized as the symmetric group
\Gamma(\triangle^{n})\simeq{\rm Sym}_{n+1}
, naturally acting on
\triangle^{n}
by permuting subsets. In particular, the simplex is a regular polytope. By choosing the flag
\emptyset\prec\{0\}\prec\{0,1\}\prec\cdots\prec\{0,\cdots,n\}
, the automorphism
\rho_{j}
from Section
2
corresponds to the transposition
(j\ \ j+1)
and thus the rotation subgroup is isomorphic to the alternating group
\Gamma^{+}(\triangle^{n})\simeq{\rm Alt}_{n+1}
. For the rest of the section, we assume
n\geq 3
.

A Rubik’s tetrahedron
{\rm R}(\triangle^{3})
(left) together with a move in
\Gamma{\rm R}(\triangle^{3})

Fix a system of coordinates on
\triangle^{n}
. By the results of Section

,
\Gamma{\rm R}(\triangle^{n})
embeds into

\left({\rm Alt}_{n}\wr{\rm Sym}_{n+1}\right)\times\prod_{1\leq i<n-1}{\rm Sym}_{n-i}\wr{\rm Sym}_{n_{i}}

(14)

where

. We denote a typical element of Equation

by
(\tau_{i},\sigma_{i})_{0\leq i\leq n-2}
where:

•

\tau_{0}\in{\rm Alt}_{n}^{n+1}
.

•

\tau_{i}\in{\rm Sym}_{n-i}^{n_{i}}
for

.

•

\sigma_{i}\in{\rm Sym}_{n_{i}}
for all

Moreover, we denote the components of each

by

\Gamma{\rm R}(\triangle^{r})\simeq\Gamma{\rm R}(H/H_{-1})\hookrightarrow\Gamma{\rm R}(\triangle^{n})

(15)

given by extending a permutation

4.1
Fundamental Result for Rubik’s Simplices

The main result of this section is the following complete characterization of the group of the Rubik’s simplex.

Proposition 4.1
.

The Rubik’s group of the simplex
\Gamma{\rm R}(\triangle^{n})
consists exactly of the elements
(\tau_{i},\sigma_{i})_{0\leq i\leq n-2}
satisfying:

•

\prod_{1\leq j\leq n+1}\tau_{0}^{j}\in{\rm Alt}_{n}^{\prime}
.

•

\prod_{1\leq j\leq n_{i}}\tau_{i}^{j}\in{\rm Alt}_{n-i}
for
i>0
.

•

\sigma_{i}\in{\rm Alt}_{n_{i}}
for all
i
.

Before proving the result, recall that the commutator subgroup
{\rm Alt}_{n}^{\prime}
is trivial for
n=3
, is the Klein group for
n=4
and coincides with the whole
{\rm Alt}_{n}
for
n>4
, in which case the first condition is vacuous.

The proof of Proposition
4.1
will be divided in several steps organized in a series of lemmas. We begin by showing that the conditions are indeed satisfied by the elements in the embedding.

Lemma 4.2
.

The elements in
\Gamma{\rm R}(\triangle^{n})
satisfy the conditions in Proposition
4.1
.

Proof.

The first two conditions are a direct consequence of Proposition
3.2
since
{\rm Sym}_{n}^{\prime}={\rm Alt}_{n}
. Regarding the third condition, we will show the following stronger statement: an even permutation in
{\rm Alt}_{k}
for some
k
induces an even permutation of subsets of
\{1,\cdots,k\}
of fixed cardinality
h<k
. From here, it is immediate that all the moves (see Definition
6
) induce even
\sigma_{i}
’s. In order to deduce the statement, consider a transposition
(i\ j)\in{\rm Sym}_{k}
. Its induced permutation on subsets fixes the ones that contain both
i
and
j
and the ones that contain neither of them. Moreover, it induces a transposition on the subsets that contain
i
but not
j
by replacing
i
with
j
. Thus, the induced permutation is a product of
\binom{k-2}{h-1}
transpositions. This number is independent of
i
and
j
, meaning that an even permutation in
{\rm Alt}_{k}
induces a product of an even number of permutations that are either all even or all odd. In either case, the induced permutation is even, as desired.
∎

What is left to show is the converse of the lemma above. This involves factorizing any element as in Proposition
4.1
into a product of moves in the sense of Definition
6
. We will construct such a factorization by induction on the dimension
n
. In other words, we will describe a recursive algorithm for ‘solving’ the puzzle represented by the
n

\triangle^{n}
, which are
(n-1)
-dimensional simplices, and then relying on the extension of moves from Section
3.3
to conclude. One subtlety is that elements in

whose location is a ridge are unaffected by moves extended from facets, and they have to be addressed separately in the proof. We start by proving the base of the induction.

Lemma 4.3
.

Proposition
4.1
is true for
n=3
i.e., for the tetrahedron.

Proof.

This is a well-known fact
[
3
]
and be even checked by exhaustion in a computer-assisted manner since
|\Gamma{\rm R}(\triangle^{3})|=3^{4}\cdot 4!\cdot 2^{6}\cdot 6!\ /\ 24=3732480
.
∎

We now prove a technical result enabling the reduction to facets, which will be the key step of the inductive argument.

Lemma 4.4
.

Pick three distinct elements

, such that the locations

Since

Lemma 4.7
.

Pick two distinct faces

and four facets
G_{i}^{j}
,

Proof.

Since

\alpha\in\{0,\cdots,n\}
such that
\alpha\in F_{1}\setminus F_{2}
. Consider the facet
H=\{0,\cdots,n\}\setminus\{\alpha\}

G_{2}^{1}
and

has to be different from

G_{2}^{1}
. It is straightforward to find

.

For any

5
The Rubik’s Hypercube

We finally focus on the higher-dimensional analogue of the classical Rubik’s cube. First, we introduce the abstract hypercube.

Definition 9
.

For
n\geq 0
, the
n
-dimensional
hypercube
is the abstract polytope whose faces are given by

\square^{n}=\{-1,0,1\}^{n}\sqcup\{F_{-1}\},

(20)

where
F_{-1}
is a formal element, e.g.,
F_{-1}=\emptyset
. We denote the
i
-th component of
F\in\square^{n}\setminus\{F_{-1}\}
as
F(i)
. The ordering on the hypercube is then defined as follows:
G\preceq F
if for every
i
,
F(i)\not=0
implies
G(i)=F(i)
.

For a face
F\in\square^{n}\setminus\{F_{-1}\}
, define
V(F)=\{i\ |\ F(i)=0\}
. The rank of
F
is then given by the cardinality of
V(F)
. There are thus
2^{n-r}\binom{n}{r}
faces of rank
r
. If
F
has rank
r
then there are natural isomorphisms
F/F_{-1}\simeq\square^{r}
. Associating to
G\succeq F
the set
V(G)\setminus V(F)
defines moreover an isomorphism
F_{n}/F\simeq\triangle^{n-r-1}
. The group of automorphisms of the hypercube can easily be characterized as the wreath product
\Gamma(\square^{n})\simeq C_{2}\wr{\rm Sym}_{n}
, where
C_{2}=\{\pm 1\}
is the cyclic group of order two. The action by an element
(\lambda,\pi)\in C_{2}\wr{\rm Sym}_{n}
on a face
F
can be described as follows:
\pi
permutes the components of
F
, while
\lambda_{i}\in C_{2}
determines whether the sign of
F(i)
gets flipped after the permutation. Now fix the flag
F_{-1}\prec\cdots\prec F_{n}
where
F_{i}=(1,\cdots,1,0,\cdots,0)
. Then the automorphism
\rho_{j}
from Section
2
corresponds to
(\lambda,\pi)
, where
\lambda=1
and
\pi=(n-j\ \ n-j+1)
for
j\not=0
, while
\pi=1
and
\lambda=(1,\cdots,1,-1)
for
\rho_{0}
. It follows that the hypercube is a regular polytope and that the rotation subgroup
\Gamma^{+}(\square^{n})
consists of automorphisms satisfying:

\prod_{1\leq j\leq n}\lambda_{i}={\rm sign}(\pi).

(21)

Fix a system of coordinates on
\square^{n}
. By the results of Section
3.1
,
\Gamma{\rm R}(\square^{n})
embeds into

\left({\rm Alt}_{n}\wr{\rm Sym}_{2^{n}}\right)\times\prod_{1\leq i<n-1}{\rm Sym}_{n-i}\wr{\rm Sym}_{n_{i}}

(22)

where
n_{i}=2^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}
. Similarly to Section
4
, we denote a typical element of Equation
22
by
(\tau_{i},\sigma_{i})_{0\leq i\leq n-2}
. Before discussing the complete description of the group of the Rubik’s hypercube, we introduce a comparison homomorphism with the Rubik’s simplex.

5.1
From Rubik’s Simplices to Hypercubes

The group of the
(n-1)
-dimensional Rubik’s simplex can be embedded into the one of the
n
-dimensional Rubik’s hypercube via a peculiar homomorphism. In order to construct the latter, pick
\alpha\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
and consider the facet
H=\{1,\cdots,n\}\setminus\{\alpha\}\in\triangle^{n-1}
, as well as the facets
H_{+},H_{-}\in\square^{n}
with all vanishing entries except for the one at index
\alpha
, which is set to
1
and
-1
respectively. Note that a rotation
\phi\in\Gamma^{+}(\triangle^{n-1},H)\simeq{\rm Alt}_{n-1}
induces a rotation in
\Gamma^{+}(\square^{n},H_{+})=\Gamma^{+}(\square^{n},H_{-})
, which in turn defines two moves
\mu_{+},\mu_{-}\in\Gamma{\rm R}(\square^{n})
. These moves commute since no face of
\square^{n}
(different from
F_{-1}
and
F_{n})
is adjacent to both
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
. As
\alpha
and
\phi
vary, it is straightforward to check that the association
\mu_{\phi}\mapsto\mu_{+}\mu_{-}=\mu_{-}\mu_{+}
extends to a group embedding

\Theta\colon\Gamma{\rm R}(\triangle^{n-1})\hookrightarrow\Gamma{\rm R}(\square^{n}).

(23)

Explicitly, for
\mu=(\tau_{i},\sigma_{i})_{1\leq i<n-1}\in\Gamma{\rm R}(\triangle^{n-1})
(notation according to Section
4
),
\Theta(\mu)
sends
(F,G)\in{\rm R}(\square^{n})
to
(F^{\prime},G^{\prime})
, defined as follows. Assuming the identification
F_{n}/F\simeq\triangle^{n-r-1}
, where
r
is the rank of
F
, we have
G^{\prime}=\tau_{r-1}^{j}(G)
, where
j
is the index corresponding to
V(F)\in\triangle^{n-1}
. Moreover,
V(F^{\prime})=\sigma_{r-1}(V(F))
while the non-vanishing entries of
F^{\prime}
are obtained by permuting the ones of
F
via
\tau_{n-2}^{j}
.

It is worth mentioning that the embedding described in this section can be generalized to
power polytopes
– see
[
5
]
, Chapter
8
. The power of a polytope is a construction generalizing the relation between the hypercube and the simplex. The definition of
\Theta
extends almost verbatim to an embedding of the Rubik’s group of a polytope into the Rubik’s group of its power. However, for the sake of conciseness, we will not discuss further details here.

5.2
Fundamental Result for Rubik’s Hypercubes

We now provide a complete characterization of the Rubik’s group of the hypercube. As mentioned in Section
1
, the same result has been obtained in
[
8
]
with a similar inductive argument.

Proposition 5.1
.

The Rubik’s group of the hypercube
\Gamma{\rm R}(\square^{n})
consists exactly of the elements
(\tau_{i},\sigma_{i})_{0\leq i\leq n-2}
satisfying:

•

\prod_{1\leq j\leq n+1}\tau_{0}^{j}\in{\rm Alt}_{n}^{\prime}
.

•

\prod_{1\leq j\leq n_{i}}\tau_{i}^{j}\in{\rm Alt}_{n-i}
for
i>0
.

•

\sigma_{i}\in{\rm Alt}_{n_{i}}
for
i<n-3
.

•

{\rm sign}(\sigma_{n-2})={\rm sign}(\sigma_{n-3})
.

We begin by showing the following.

Lemma 5.2
.

The elements in
\Gamma{\rm R}(\square^{n})
satisfy the conditions in Proposition
5.1
.

Proof.

Note that the first two conditions are a direct consequence of Proposition
3.2
since
{\rm Sym}_{n}^{\prime}={\rm Alt}_{n}
. In order to address the third and fourth condition, we will study the sign of the permutation
\sigma_{r}\in{\rm Sym}_{k_{r}}
of faces of rank
r<k
of
\square^{k}
induced by an automorphism
(\lambda,\pi)\in C_{2}\wr{\rm Sym}_{k}\simeq\Gamma(\square^{k})
for a given
k
. Suppose first that
\lambda=1
and
\pi=(i\ j)
is a transposition. Then
\sigma_{r}
swaps pairs of faces
F
such that
F(i)\not=F(j)
and fixes the other ones. There are

2^{k-r}\left(\frac{1}{2}\binom{k-2}{r}+2\binom{k-2}{r-1}\right)

(24)

such faces for
r<k-1
while there are
4
for
r=k-1
. Therefore,
\sigma_{r}
factorizes into half as many transpositions. Suppose now that
\pi=1
while
\lambda_{i}=1
for all
i
except for some
j
. Then
\sigma_{r}
swaps pairs of faces
F
such that
F(j)\not=0
and fixes the other ones. There are

2^{k-r}\binom{k-1}{r}

(25)

such faces, and thus
\sigma_{r}
factorizes into half as many transpositions. Note that half of both Equation
24
and
25
is even for
r<k-2
. Moreover, for
r=k-2
half of Equation
24
is odd while half of Equation
25
is even, and the opposite is true for
r=k-1
. All this implies that if Equation
21
holds for an arbitrary automorphism
(\lambda,\pi)
then
\sigma_{r}\in{\rm Alt}_{k_{r}}
for
r<k-2
, while
{\rm sign}(\sigma_{k-1})={\rm sign}(\sigma_{k-2})
. The desired third and fourth condition follow by applying the latter fact to the moves from Definition
6
with
k=n-1
.
∎

The converse of the above result can be proven by induction on
n
, analogously to the case of the Rubik’s simplex (Proposition
4.1
). The case of the Rubik’s hypercube is extremely similar, with a few additional details. Even further, the embedding described in Section
5.1
can be exploited for recycling the results from Section
4.1
and extend them to the hypercube. For the sake of simplicity, we briefly outline the main additional subtleties that arise. For a proof complete of all the details, we refer the reader to
[
8
]
. To begin with, Proposition
5.1
is well-known for the Rubik’s cube (i.e.,
n=3
), which is analogous to Lemma
4.3
. Next, Lemma
4.4
and Lemma
4.7
can be extended to the Rubik’s cube via the aforementioned embedding. The only difference is that, as an additional step, it is necessary to flip the sign at a given index of the involved locations, which is straightforward. From here, the analogue of Lemma
4.6
and Lemma
4.8
can be derived by induction with the same argument based on commutators. Lastly, we are left with discussing the ridges of the Rubik’s hypercube. Due to the fourth condition in Proposition
5.1
, it is necessary to show that any transposition of ridges can be realized in
\Gamma{\rm R}(\square^{n})
. Similarly to Lemma
4.5
, this can be achieved by bringing two given ridges to a common location and reducing to the case
n=3
.

As a consequence of Proposition
5.1
, the number of possible configurations of the Rubik’s hypercube is:

|\Gamma{\rm R}(\square^{n})|=\frac{1}{c_{n}2^{2^{n}+2(n-2)}}\prod_{0\leq i<n-1}(n-i)!^{2^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}}\left(2^{n-i}\binom{n}{i}\right)!,

(26)

where:

c_{n}=|{\rm Alt}_{n}/{\rm Alt}_{n}^{\prime}|=\begin{cases}3&n=3,4,\\
1&n\geq 5.\end{cases}

(27)
