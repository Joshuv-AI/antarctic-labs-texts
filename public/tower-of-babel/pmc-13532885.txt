Assay-Dependent Variability in RBP4 and Adiponectin Quantification Affects Associations with Coronary Disease Severity and Doppler Echocardiographic Indices: A Comparative Study of Immunoturbidimetry and ELISA
Authors: Yiyi Ma, Dongxiao Zhu, Mengjiao Guo, Yandong Jiang, Jiajie Lv, Baihong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferences among analytical platforms may contribute to inconsistent associations between circulating adipokines and coronary artery disease severity. This study compared the analytical robustness and clinical performance of the immunoturbidimetric assay (ITA) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for measuring retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4) and adiponectin (APN).MethodsA total of 128 patients with angiographically confirmed coronary artery disease were prospectively enrolled. Serum RBP4 and APN concentrations were measured in parallel using ITA and ELISA. Analytical performance was evaluated by precision, linearity, and recovery testing. In an additional cohort of 20 healthy volunteers, the effects of room-temperature storage, repeated freeze–thaw cycles, centrifugation conditions, and anticoagulant type were assessed. Coronary lesion severity was quantified using the Gensini score, and cardiac function was evaluated by Doppler echocardiography. Correlation, receiver operating characteristic, Bland–Altman, and multivariable regression analyses were performed.ResultsBoth assays demonstrated acceptable linearity and recovery, but ITA showed superior within-run and between-run precision. ELISA-derived measurements were more susceptible to prolonged room-temperature exposure, repeated freeze–thaw cycles, and lithium-heparin anticoagulation. RBP4 concentrations increased and APN concentrations decreased with increasing coronary lesion severity (all P<0.001). ITA-derived measurements showed stronger associations with Gensini score and selected echocardiographic indices than ELISA-derived measurements. ITA-derived RBP4 and APN demonstrated higher diagnostic discrimination for severe coronary artery disease, with areas under the curve of 0.872 and 0.851, respectively. Bland–Altman analysis showed that ELISA produced a positive systematic bias relative to ITA, with increasing intermethod variability at higher RBP4 concentrations.ConclusionAssay-dependent analytical variability affects the magnitude of biomarker–disease associations and the classification of coronary disease severity. ELISA-derived RBP4 measurements exhibited a positive systematic bias relative to ITA; therefore, adipokine concentrations and proposed diagnostic thresholds should be interpreted with consideration of the analytical platform used. Assay harmonization and standardized preanalytical procedures are necessary before RBP4- and APN-based risk stratification can be applied reliably across laboratories.Keywords: adiponectin, coronary artery disease, doppler echocardiography, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, immunoturbidimetric assay

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains the leading cause of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide and continues to impose a substantial global healthcare burden despite major advances in prevention, diagnosis, and revascularization strategies. Recent epidemiological estimates indicate that cardiovascular diseases account for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually, with coronary heart disease representing the largest proportion of these cases.1 The extent and severity of coronary atherosclerotic lesions are major determinants of myocardial ischemia, ventricular remodeling, adverse cardiovascular events, and long-term prognosis. Therefore, accurate assessment of coronary lesion burden and cardiac functional impairment is essential for early risk stratification, individualized therapeutic decision-making, and prognostic evaluation in patients with CAD.2 Although coronary angiography remains the clinical reference standard for evaluating epicardial coronary stenosis, it is invasive and unsuitable for repeated dynamic assessment. Doppler echocardiography provides important noninvasive information regarding left ventricular systolic and diastolic function; however, its sensitivity for detecting early subclinical myocardial dysfunction and subtle ischemic injury remains limited, particularly in patients without overt structural abnormalities.3 In this context, circulating biomarkers have attracted increasing attention because they can provide objective and rapidly accessible information reflecting metabolic dysregulation, inflammatory activation, endothelial dysfunction, and myocardial injury, thereby complementing conventional imaging-based evaluation.4

Retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4), a 21-kDa transport protein synthesized predominantly by hepatocytes and adipocytes, has emerged as a multifunctional adipokine involved in cardiometabolic disorders beyond its classical role in retinol transport. Experimental and clinical evidence suggests that elevated circulating RBP4 contributes to insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, vascular inflammation, and lipid metabolism abnormalities, all of which are central mechanisms in the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis.5,6 Increased serum RBP4 concentrations have therefore been associated with coronary plaque burden, metabolic syndrome, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes. In contrast, adiponectin (APN), an adipocyte-derived cytokine with potent anti-inflammatory, anti-atherogenic, antioxidative, and insulin-sensitizing properties, is generally considered cardioprotective. APN exerts beneficial cardiovascular effects through multiple mechanisms, including activation of AMP-activated protein kinase signaling, improvement of endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability, inhibition of vascular smooth muscle proliferation, and attenuation of myocardial fibrosis and apoptosis.7,8 Reduced circulating APN levels have consistently been linked to endothelial dysfunction, plaque instability, and increased risk of coronary events. Collectively, these findings suggest that RBP4 and APN may represent complementary biomarkers reflecting distinct but interconnected pathophysiological pathways underlying coronary artery disease progression and cardiac dysfunction.

Despite growing interest in RBP4 and APN as cardiovascular biomarkers, substantial heterogeneity persists in reported concentrations, reference intervals, diagnostic thresholds, and strengths of association with coronary artery disease. Biological differences among study populations, including obesity, diabetes, renal function, medication exposure, and disease severity, may explain part of this inconsistency. However, methodological variability remains an unresolved and underrecognized contributor. Different analytical platforms may use non-equivalent antibodies, recognize different molecular forms or epitopes, employ manufacturer-specific calibrators, and vary in signal-generation principles, analytical sensitivity, matrix susceptibility, and dilution procedures. Moreover, standardized reference methods and commutable reference materials are not widely available for RBP4 and APN. Consequently, results generated by different assays may correlate statistically while remaining numerically non-interchangeable.

This problem has persisted because most biomarker studies evaluate clinical associations using only one assay platform and therefore cannot distinguish biological heterogeneity from analytical error. Differences in specimen type, time to processing, storage duration, freeze–thaw exposure, anticoagulant selection, reagent lot, and laboratory workflow are also inconsistently reported. As a result, assay-specific bias and imprecision may be incorporated into pooled estimates, proposed cutoff values, and risk models without being recognized. A biomarker threshold derived using one platform may therefore misclassify patients when transferred directly to another laboratory or analytical system.

These considerations are central to biomarker translation. Successful clinical implementation requires not only biological plausibility and statistical association, but also reproducible measurement, analytical traceability, transferable decision thresholds, and evidence that the test improves clinical classification beyond established risk factors. Analytical variability can weaken apparent biomarker–disease relationships, shift patients across risk categories, reduce interlaboratory reproducibility, and limit the external validity of clinical prediction models. Accordingly, platform comparison and preanalytical validation are necessary steps between exploratory biomarker discovery and routine clinical application.

Previous method-comparison studies support this concern. Investigations of RBP4 and adiponectin have reported method-dependent differences despite significant correlations between platforms, suggesting that numerical agreement cannot be assumed solely from correlation. Such findings indicate that assay methodology may contribute to inconsistent concentrations and clinical associations reported across studies. However, few investigations have directly quantified how platform-related variability propagates from analytical performance to coronary disease associations, diagnostic discrimination, and incremental risk classification within the same patient samples.

Accordingly, the present study compared the analytical and clinical performance of immunoturbidimetric assay and ELISA for serum RBP4 and APN measurement in patients with angiographically confirmed coronary artery disease. By integrating analytical validation, systematic assessment of preanalytical conditions, intermethod agreement analysis, and clinicopathological evaluation, we investigated whether assay-dependent variability influences not only measured biomarker concentrations but also their associations with Gensini-defined coronary disease severity, diagnostic discrimination, and incremental risk classification. This approach was intended to address an important methodological barrier to the reproducible clinical translation of RBP4 and APN.

No longitudinal follow-up or prospective ascertainment of cardiovascular outcomes was performed; therefore, the study was designed to evaluate cross-sectional associations and diagnostic discrimination rather than long-term prognosis.

Sample Collection and Processing

After overnight fasting for 8–12 h, peripheral venous blood samples were collected from the antecubital vein between 06:00 and 07:00 a.m. on the morning following admission. Approximately 5 mL of blood was collected into additive-free polypropylene vacuum tubes. Samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min and subsequently centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C using a centrifuge with a rotational radius of 15 cm. The serum supernatant was carefully aspirated while avoiding contamination from erythrocyte or leukocyte layers. Serum aliquots were transferred into sterile 1.5-mL polypropylene cryovials (Corning, USA), labeled with unique identifiers, and immediately stored at −80°C until analysis. The interval between blood collection and cryopreservation was maintained within 2 h for all specimens. To minimize protein degradation and analytical variability, all samples underwent only a single freeze–thaw cycle before biomarker measurement.

Data completeness was assessed before construction of the final analytical dataset. Availability of the prespecified demographic, laboratory, angiographic, biomarker, and echocardiographic variables required for the primary analyses was an eligibility criterion. Consequently, the final analytical cohort comprised participants with complete data for all variables included in the principal analyses, and no statistical imputation was performed. The numbers of patients screened, excluded, and included in the final analysis, together with the reasons for exclusion, are presented in the participant-flow diagram.

Hemolysis, Icterus, and Lipemia Interference Testing

Hemolysis, icterus, and lipemia interference was evaluated according to the principles of CLSI EP07 using paired control and interferent-spiked serum pools. Two serum pools were prepared for each biomarker to represent low and high clinically relevant concentrations. Each pool was divided into matched control and test aliquots so that the interferent-spiked and control samples had identical serum-matrix content and dilution.

Hemolysis interference was assessed using purified human hemoglobin at final concentrations of 0, 100, 250, 500, and 1000 mg/dL. Icterus interference was evaluated using unconjugated bilirubin at final concentrations of 0, 5, 10, 20, and 40 mg/dL and conjugated bilirubin at 0, 5, 10, 20, and 28 mg/dL. Lipemia interference was assessed using a standardized lipid emulsion at final concentrations of 0, 250, 500, 1000, and 1500 mg/dL. The maximum test concentrations were selected to cover clinically relevant through markedly abnormal HIL conditions and were consistent with concentrations commonly used in CLSI-based interference investigations.

To control for dilution effects, an equivalent volume of the corresponding interferent-free solvent was added to each control aliquot. All conditions were analyzed in five replicates using both ITA and ELISA for RBP4 and APN. ITA measurements were performed on the AU5800 analyzer, and the corresponding automated hemolysis, icterus, and lipemia indices were recorded. ELISA measurements were performed using the same dilution, calibration, and quality-control procedures applied to the principal study samples.

A mean bias within ±10% was predefined as analytically acceptable because no established clinical allowable-error specification is currently available for RBP4 or APN. The HIL tolerance limit for each analyte and platform was defined as the highest interferent concentration at which the absolute mean bias remained ≤10% in both the low- and high-concentration serum pools. If the ±10% threshold was crossed between two tested concentrations, the lower concentration was designated as the verified noninterfering limit.

CLSI recommends paired testing, defined allowable bias, dose–response characterization where interference is detected, and assay-specific verification rather than relying solely on generic manufacturer claims.

Immunoturbidimetric Assay (ITA)

Serum RBP4 and APN concentrations were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (AU5800; Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) based on the immunoturbidimetric principle. Commercial ITA reagent kits for RBP4 and APN quantification were obtained from Leadman Biochemistry Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China; RBP4 catalog no. LT-RBP4-2024; APN catalog no. LT-APN-2024; lot numbers RB240118 and AP240126, respectively). The analytical detection ranges were 5–150 mg/L for RBP4 and 1–30 mg/L for APN. Manufacturer-reported within-run and between-run coefficients of variation were <3% and <5%, respectively.

Prior to analysis, the instrument underwent routine maintenance, optical calibration, and internal quality-control verification according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Two levels of commercial quality-control sera and manufacturer-provided multi-point calibrators were analyzed daily before sample testing. All reactions were performed at 37°C under fully automated conditions. The calibrators were used according to the manufacturer’s value assignments. No independent calibration against a higher-order international reference material was performed, and the available documentation did not establish traceability of the assigned RBP4 or APN values to a common internationally recognized reference system.

For RBP4 measurement, absorbance was monitored at a primary wavelength of 340 nm and a secondary wavelength of 700 nm. For APN measurement, the corresponding wavelengths were 570 nm and 800 nm. Each reaction required 10 μL of serum sample, 200 μL of reagent 1, and 50 μL of reagent 2, with a total reaction time of 10 min. Samples exceeding the analytical linearity range were automatically diluted at a ratio of 1:5 using the analyzer’s built-in dilution module and retested. Final analyte concentrations were calculated automatically based on calibration curves generated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Calibration validity was maintained for 14 days.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Serum RBP4 and APN concentrations were measured using commercially available sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The human RBP4 ELISA kit had a calibration range of 1.56–100 ng/mL and a manufacturer-reported analytical sensitivity of 0.94 ng/mL. The human adiponectin ELISA kit had a calibration range of 0.781–50 ng/mL and a manufacturer-reported analytical sensitivity of 0.469 ng/mL. The previously reported APN range of 0.78–50 μg/mL was a unit transcription error and has been corrected to ng/mL. The previously listed RBP4 catalog number was also corrected because it did not correspond to an RBP4 assay. Concentrations were assigned using the kit-provided standards. These standards were not independently value-assigned against a higher-order certified reference material in the present study, and no common reference calibrator was used to harmonize the ELISA and ITA systems.

Serum samples were prediluted 1:1000 using the manufacturer-provided sample diluent before analysis. Samples, standards, and blanks were analyzed according to the kit-specific manufacturer instructions, and analyte concentrations were calculated using four-parameter logistic calibration curves. The concentration obtained from each calibration curve was multiplied by the dilution factor and converted from ng/mL to mg/L according to the following relationship:

Because a 1:1000 dilution was used, the numerical concentration read from the ELISA calibration curve in ng/mL corresponded to the final serum concentration in mg/L. For example, an RBP4 concentration of 56.9 ng/mL in the diluted sample corresponded to 56.9 mg/L in the original serum, while an APN concentration of 6.24 ng/mL corresponded to 6.24 mg/L.

All samples were quantified within the applicable calibration interval. Samples producing optical-density values above the highest calibration standard were further diluted and reanalyzed, whereas values below the lowest nonzero calibration standard were not accepted by extrapolation. Duplicate measurements were averaged, and the final concentration was calculated only after multiplication by the applicable dilution factor. The manufacturer similarly specifies that diluted results must be multiplied by the dilution factor and that samples above the calibration interval should be reanalyzed after further dilution.

The apparently large difference between the direct analytical ranges of ITA and ELISA therefore reflects differences in sample preparation rather than incompatible biological measurement ranges. ITA measured the original serum directly in mg/L, whereas ELISA measured a diluted serum aliquot in ng/mL. After correction for the 1:1000 predilution and conversion to mg/L, the results from both platforms represented concentrations in the original undiluted serum and could therefore be compared on the same unit scale.

Analytical Performance Validation

Analytical validation included precision, linearity, recovery, and assessment of dilution-related measurement performance. Serial dilution experiments were used to confirm that measured concentrations changed proportionally across the evaluated dilution series, while spike-recovery testing assessed the accuracy of analyte recovery after addition of known concentrations. An R2 value of at least 0.99 and recovery between 85% and 115% were predefined as acceptable These experiments were used to support the suitability of dilution-adjusted ELISA measurements for comparison with the directly measured ITA concentrations.

A separate laboratory-specific determination of the limit of quantitation was not performed. Manufacturer-reported analytical sensitivity was therefore not described as an independently validated LoQ. According to CLSI EP17, LoQ is a distinct performance characteristic that should be established or verified using predefined precision and bias criteria.

Nevertheless, the ELISA concentrations represented by the reported clinical group means were not close to the lower calibration limits. After the 1:1000 predilution, mean RBP4 concentrations ranged from 42.30 to 79.30 ng/mL, compared with a lower calibration limit of 1.56 ng/mL. Mean APN concentrations ranged from 4.18 to 9.23 ng/mL, compared with a lower calibration limit of 0.781 ng/mL. Therefore, the comparative clinical findings were not derived from group-level measurements concentrated near the lower analytical boundary.

Calibration and Metrological Traceability

Calibration of the ITA measurements was performed using the manufacturer-provided multipoint calibrators on the AU5800 analyzer, whereas the ELISA measurements were quantified using the kit-provided recombinant standards and four-parameter logistic calibration curves. No independent recalibration against a higher-order certified reference material was performed.

At the time of the study, no WHO International Standard for adiponectin and no certified IRMM, BCR, or ERM reference material for RBP4 were identified in the corresponding official reference-material catalogues. Consequently, an unbroken metrological traceability chain to an internationally recognized higher-order reference system could not be established for either biomarker. The reported concentrations are therefore traceable to the respective manufacturer-assigned calibrators rather than to a common international reference material.

Because the ITA and ELISA calibrators may differ in source material, protein form, purity, value-assignment procedure, and matrix characteristics, equivalent numerical results across the two platforms cannot be assumed solely because both are expressed in mg/L. The paired within-sample comparison was designed to characterize method-dependent differences under the study conditions, but it does not establish interchangeability or universal transferability of absolute concentrations between reagent systems.

Precision Analysis

High- and low-concentration pooled serum samples were prepared by combining equal serum volumes obtained from 20 healthy volunteers. Within-run precision was evaluated by analyzing the same sample 20 consecutive times within a single analytical run, whereas between-run precision was assessed by repeated measurement once daily for 20 consecutive days. Precision was expressed as coefficient of variation (CV%). Acceptable analytical performance was predefined as within-run CV <5% and between-run CV <10%.

Linearity Assessment

High-concentration serum samples were serially diluted with normal saline at dilution ratios of 1:1, 1:2, 1:4, 1:8, and 1:16. Each dilution level was analyzed in triplicate, and regression equations and coefficients of determination (R2) were calculated. An R2 value ≥0.99 was considered indicative of acceptable assay linearity.

Coronary Angiography and Gensini Score Assessment

Coronary angiograms were independently evaluated by two interventional cardiologists, each with more than 5 years of clinical experience, who were blinded to biomarker and echocardiographic findings. Each observer independently assigned a Gensini score before any consensus discussion. Interobserver reliability for the continuous Gensini score was assessed using a two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement, single-measure intraclass correlation coefficient, reported with its 95% confidence interval. Agreement for the ordinal coronary-severity category and number of diseased vessels was evaluated using weighted Cohen’s kappa. After completion of the reliability analysis, disagreements were resolved through joint review, and the consensus assessment was used in the primary clinical analyses.

A two-way random-effects model is appropriate when the two observers are treated as representative of similarly qualified readers. If the inference is intended to apply only to these two specific cardiologists, use a two-way mixed-effects model instead. The selected ICC model, absolute-agreement definition, single- versus average-measure form, and 95% confidence interval should all be reported explicitly.

Doppler Echocardiographic Assessment

Comprehensive transthoracic Doppler echocardiography was performed within 48 h after admission using a Vivid E95 ultrasound system (GE Healthcare, USA) equipped with an M5S-D cardiac probe operating at 2.5–3.5 MHz. All examinations were conducted by experienced sonographers blinded to biomarker results and angiographic findings.

The primary quantitative echocardiographic parameters included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), mitral inflow E/A ratio, and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD). LVEF was calculated using the modified Simpson biplane method according to current echocardiographic guidelines.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc version 22.0 (MedCalc Software Ltd., Ostend, Belgium). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of quantile–quantile plots. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, nonnormally distributed variables as median and interquartile range, and categorical variables as number and percentage.

Differences among coronary lesion-severity groups and vessel-involvement groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance, followed by the least significant difference post hoc test when the overall test was significant. For the preanalytical substudy, measurement changes were expressed as relative percentage deviations from the corresponding baseline condition, and the mean values obtained from triplicate measurements were used in the analyses.

For analyses involving Gensini score, linearity and homoscedasticity were assessed using scatterplots and residual plots. Pearson correlation analysis and simple linear regression were used when the relevant distributional and linearity assumptions were satisfied. Spearman rank correlation analysis was used for ordinal variables or when parametric assumptions were not met. Where simple linear regression was applied, the coefficient of determination (R2) was reported as a measure of model fit and was not used interchangeably with the correlation coefficient.

Multivariable linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the independent associations of serum RBP4 and APN concentrations with coronary atherosclerotic severity, using Gensini score as the dependent variable. Prespecified covariates included age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, smoking status, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, and serum creatinine. Regression coefficients (β), 95% confidence intervals, and corresponding P values were reported. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, and residual plots were examined to evaluate linearity, homoscedasticity, and approximate normality of residuals. Detailed class-specific information on statins, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, glucose-lowering drugs, treatment intensity, duration, and adherence was not available in the finalized analytical dataset and therefore could not be included as additional covariates. Estimated glomerular filtration rate was used to exclude severe renal insufficiency but was not modeled as a continuous or stratified variable; serum creatinine was retained as the available renal-function covariate.

Interobserver reliability for continuous Gensini scores was assessed using a two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement, single-measure intraclass correlation coefficient with a 95% confidence interval. Agreement for ordinal coronary-severity categories and the number of diseased coronary vessels was evaluated using weighted Cohen’s kappa.

Intermethod agreement between ITA- and ELISA-derived measurements was evaluated for RBP4 using Bland–Altman analysis. For each participant, the paired intermethod difference was calculated as ELISA minus ITA, and the corresponding mean concentration was calculated as the average of the two measurements. Mean bias and 95% limits of agreement were estimated as the mean paired difference ±1.96 standard deviations. Concentration-dependent proportional bias was assessed by linear regression of the signed paired difference against the corresponding mean concentration. A regression slope significantly different from zero was considered evidence of proportional bias. Because an increase in the magnitude of disagreement does not necessarily imply directional bias, heteroscedasticity was additionally assessed using the Spearman correlation between the absolute paired difference and the mean concentration.

Exploratory analyses were conducted to determine whether RBP4 intermethod disagreement increased with coronary atherosclerotic severity. Spearman correlation was used to evaluate the associations of Gensini score with both the signed paired difference and the absolute paired difference for RBP4. To distinguish a disease-severity effect from concentration-dependent analytical bias, an additional linear regression model was fitted with the paired RBP4 intermethod difference as the dependent variable and both mean RBP4 concentration and Gensini score as independent variables. A significant coefficient for Gensini score after adjustment for mean concentration was interpreted as evidence that RBP4 intermethod bias varied with disease severity beyond the effect of analyte concentration. These analyses were considered exploratory.

Severe coronary artery disease was defined as a Gensini score ≥40. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was used to assess the ability of ITA- and ELISA-derived RBP4 and APN measurements to distinguish patients with severe coronary artery disease from those with a Gensini score <40. Areas under the curve, 95% confidence intervals, sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cutoff values determined using the Youden index were calculated. Paired areas under the curve for ITA- and ELISA-derived measurements of the same biomarker were compared using the DeLong test.

For HIL interference testing, results are presented as mean percentage bias with 95% confidence intervals relative to the matched interferent-free control. Interference was considered analytically significant when the absolute mean bias exceeded 10% at either biomarker concentration level. Dose-dependent trends were assessed using linear regression of percentage bias against interferent concentration. The highest tested concentration satisfying the predefined acceptance criterion in both low- and high-concentration pools was reported as the verified tolerance limit for the corresponding assay.

To explore the incremental predictive value of assay-derived biomarkers, multivariable logistic regression models were constructed using conventional cardiovascular risk factors alone and after the addition of ITA- or ELISA-derived RBP4 and APN measurements. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and differences between nested models were evaluated using the DeLong test. Net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement were additionally calculated to assess changes in risk classification and discrimination. Because these models were developed and evaluated in the same cohort, the reclassification analyses were considered exploratory.

All statistical tests were two-sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

Results

Analytical Performance Validation of ITA and ELISA

Both immunoturbidimetric assay (ITA) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) demonstrated acceptable analytical linearity and recovery performance for serum retinol-binding protein 4 (RBP4) and adiponectin (APN) quantification. The recovery rates of both assays remained within the predefined acceptable range of 85%–115%, and all calibration curves showed excellent linearity with coefficients of determination (R2) ≥0.99, indicating satisfactory quantitative analytical capability.

Despite comparable linearity and recovery performance, ITA consistently demonstrated superior analytical precision compared with ELISA. For RBP4 measurement, the within-run coefficient of variation (CV) for ITA ranged from 1.85% to 2.18%, whereas ELISA showed substantially higher within-run variability ranging from 4.57% to 5.23%. Similarly, between-run CVs for ITA ranged from 2.97% to 3.42%, compared with 6.12% to 7.36% for ELISA (all P<0.001). A similar pattern was observed for APN measurement, with ITA demonstrating significantly lower within-run and between-run variability than ELISA across both low- and high-concentration samples (all P<0.001). Overall, these findings indicate that ITA provides greater analytical reproducibility and stability for routine serum biomarker measurement (Table 2).

Effects of Preanalytical Conditions on Biomarker Measurement

Preanalytical variables exerted substantially greater influence on ELISA-derived measurements than on ITA-derived measurements. Prolonged room-temperature exposure progressively increased relative analytical deviation in both methods; however, the magnitude of deviation was markedly greater for ELISA. After 8 h of room-temperature storage, the relative deviations of RBP4 and APN measured by ELISA increased to 9.86% and 11.47%, respectively, whereas the corresponding deviations obtained by ITA remained comparatively low at 2.31% and 2.87%.

Repeated freeze–thaw cycles produced similar effects. Following five freeze–thaw cycles, ELISA-derived measurements again exhibited pronounced analytical instability, with relative deviations exceeding 9% for both biomarkers, while ITA measurements remained relatively stable Among the evaluated preanalytical conditions, anticoagulant-related interference produced the most substantial effect. Lithium-heparin plasma resulted in the greatest analytical deviation, particularly for ELISA-based APN quantification, in which the relative deviation reached 12.89%. In contrast, centrifugation speed variations between 3000 and 5000 rpm produced only minimal effects on either analytical platform. Under the conditions specifically evaluated, ITA showed greater resistance than ELISA to room-temperature storage, repeated freeze–thaw exposure, centrifugation variation, and anticoagulant-related interference. Hemolysis, icterus, and lipemia were not evaluated; therefore, these results should not be interpreted as establishing comprehensive robustness under all routine clinical laboratory conditions (Table 3).

Serum RBP4 and APN Levels According to Coronary Lesion Severity

Patients were stratified into mild, moderate, and severe coronary lesion groups according to Gensini score severity. Serum RBP4 concentrations increased progressively with worsening coronary atherosclerotic burden, whereas APN concentrations demonstrated a progressive decline across increasing lesion severity categories (all P<0.001).

Using ITA, the mean serum RBP4 concentration increased from 42.30±11.39 mg/L in the mild lesion group to 56.90±10.30 mg/L in the moderate lesion group and 71.50±10.38 mg/L in the severe lesion group (P<0.05 for all pairwise comparisons). Conversely, APN concentrations decreased from 8.76±1.70 mg/L in the mild lesion group to 6.24±1.78 mg/L and 4.18±1.27 mg/L in the moderate and severe groups, respectively (P<0.05 for all pairwise comparisons).

ELISA-derived measurements demonstrated the same overall trend; however, biomarker concentrations measured by ELISA were consistently slightly higher than those measured by ITA across all severity strata. Notably, the absolute difference between the two methods became more pronounced as coronary lesion severity increased. These findings suggest that assay-dependent variability may influence the magnitude of observed biomarker-clinical associations in patients with advanced coronary disease (Table 4).

Serum RBP4 and APN Levels According to the Number of Diseased Coronary Vessels

A significant dose–response relationship was observed between biomarker levels and the extent of coronary vessel involvement. Patients with triple-vessel disease exhibited substantially higher serum RBP4 concentrations and lower APN concentrations than those with single-vessel or double-vessel disease (all P<0.001).

Using ITA, mean RBP4 concentrations increased from 45.44±11.88 mg/L in single-vessel disease to 58.75±13.23 mg/L in double-vessel disease and 70.75±10.66 mg/L in triple-vessel disease. In contrast, APN concentrations progressively decreased from 8.29±1.97 mg/L to 5.97±1.81 mg/L and 4.20±1.49 mg/L across the same groups. ELISA measurements demonstrated comparable trends but again yielded slightly higher absolute values than ITA measurements. These observations further support the close association between altered adipokine profiles and increasing coronary atherosclerotic burden (Table 5).

Correlations Between Serum Biomarkers and Coronary Lesion Severity

Correlation analyses demonstrated significant associations between serum biomarker levels and indices of coronary lesion severity. Using ITA-derived measurements, serum RBP4 levels showed a strong positive correlation with Gensini score (r=0.800, P<0.001) and a significant positive association with the number of diseased coronary vessels (Spearman ρ=0.744, P<0.001). In contrast, serum APN concentrations were negatively correlated with both Gensini score and the extent of coronary vessel involvement (all P<0.001).

When the two analytical platforms were compared, ITA-derived measurements generally showed stronger associations with coronary lesion severity than ELISA-derived measurements. The strongest association was observed between ITA-derived RBP4 and Gensini score (r=0.800, P<0.001). ITA-derived RBP4 was also positively associated with the number of diseased coronary vessels (Spearman ρ=0.744, P<0.001), whereas APN concentrations were inversely associated with both Gensini score and the extent of coronary vessel involvement. These findings indicate that assay-dependent analytical variability may influence the apparent magnitude of biomarker–disease associations. Nevertheless, the particularly strong correlation between ITA-derived RBP4 and Gensini score should be interpreted cautiously because it was obtained in a selected, single-center cohort of patients with angiographically confirmed coronary artery disease (Figure 1).

Diagnostic Performance of ITA and ELISA for Severe Coronary Artery Disease

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of serum RBP4 and APN concentrations measured using ITA and ELISA to discriminate patients with severe coronary artery disease (Figure 2). ITA-derived RBP4 demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.872 (95% CI: 0.804–0.925), sensitivity of 82.4%, specificity of 79.1%, and an optimal cutoff value of 61.5 mg/L. ITA-derived APN also demonstrated strong diagnostic discrimination, with an AUC of 0.851 (95% CI: 0.781–0.909), sensitivity of 80.1%, specificity of 76.8%, and an optimal cutoff value of 5.4 mg/L. In comparison, ELISA-derived measurements demonstrated lower diagnostic accuracy. ELISA-derived RBP4 demonstrated an AUC of 0.800 (95% CI: 0.726–0.873), sensitivity of 75.2%, specificity of 71.5%, and a cutoff value of 65.3 mg/L, whereas ELISA-derived APN demonstrated an AUC of 0.786 (95% CI: 0.711–0.858), sensitivity of 73.4%, specificity of 69.8%, and a cutoff value of 5.8 mg/L. Within the present cohort, ITA-derived measurements demonstrated higher discrimination for severe coronary artery disease than ELISA-derived measurements. For RBP4, the AUC was 0.872 with ITA and 0.800 with ELISA, corresponding to an absolute AUC difference of 0.071. For APN, the respective AUCs were 0.851 and 0.786, corresponding to an absolute difference of 0.065. The DeLong test indicated statistically significant differences between the two platforms for both RBP4 (P=0.018) and APN (P=0.026). These results indicate that the analytical platform may influence the estimated diagnostic performance of adipokine biomarkers. However, because the thresholds and performance measures were derived and evaluated in the same cohort, they should be regarded as internally derived and require external validation (Table 6).

Agreement Analysis Between ITA and ELISA Measurements

Agreement between ITA and ELISA for serum RBP4 quantification was further evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis (Figure 3). ELISA-derived measurements demonstrated a positive systematic bias relative to ITA, with a mean difference of approximately 5 mg/L between the two analytical platforms. In addition, the dispersion of data points increased progressively at higher mean biomarker concentrations, visually suggesting a trend toward greater intermethod variability at higher biomarker concentrations. Most observations remained within the 95% limits of agreement, suggesting acceptable overall agreement between methods despite the presence of systematic measurement differences. These findings indicate that although both analytical platforms demonstrated acceptable quantitative performance, ELISA consistently yielded higher RBP4 concentrations than ITA, particularly at elevated analyte levels. Bland–Altman analysis of RBP4 demonstrated a positive systematic bias of ELISA relative to ITA. The mean paired difference, calculated as ELISA minus ITA, was +5.1 mg/L, with 95% limits of agreement from −0.8 to +11.2 mg/L. Regression of the signed intermethod difference against mean RBP4 concentration indicated statistically significant concentration-dependent proportional bias (P=0.032). These findings show that the difference between the two methods was not constant across the RBP4 measurement range and that a single additive correction factor would not provide reliable conversion between the platforms.

Multivariable Regression Analysis of Factors Associated with Coronary Lesion Severity

To determine whether serum biomarker levels were independently associated with coronary atherosclerotic burden, multivariable linear regression analysis was performed using the Gensini score as the dependent variable (Table 7). After adjustment for conventional cardiovascular risk factors, including age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, smoking history, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting blood glucose, and serum creatinine, serum RBP4 concentrations measured using ITA remained independently associated with higher Gensini scores (β = 0.482, 95% CI: 0.361–0.603, P < 0.001). Conversely, serum APN concentrations measured using ITA remained independently associated with lower Gensini scores (β = −0.417, 95% CI: −0.529 to −0.305, P < 0.001). Among conventional risk factors, diabetes mellitus, smoking history, LDL-C, hypertension, and fasting blood glucose also demonstrated significant independent associations with coronary lesion severity. These findings suggest that altered adipokine profiles are independently associated with coronary atherosclerotic burden beyond traditional cardiovascular risk factors.

The ITA-based model also demonstrated larger apparent improvements in reclassification, with an NRI of 0.29 and an IDI of 0.13, compared with an NRI of 0.12 and an IDI of 0.06 for the ELISA-based model. Discussion

The present study compared the analytical robustness and clinical implications of immunoturbidimetric assay and ELISA for measuring serum RBP4 and adiponectin in patients with coronary artery disease. ITA demonstrated lower analytical imprecision and greater resistance to the evaluated preanalytical conditions, whereas ELISA measurements were more vulnerable to prolonged room-temperature exposure, repeated freeze–thaw cycles, and anticoagulant-related interference. These analytical differences were accompanied by differences in the apparent strength of biomarker associations with coronary lesion severity, diagnostic discrimination, and incremental risk classification. Collectively, the findings indicate that assay methodology is not merely a technical laboratory consideration but may materially influence the clinical interpretation of RBP4 and APN in CAD.

From an analytical perspective, ITA showed lower within-run and between-run imprecision than ELISA under the conditions evaluated. Automation may partly explain this difference because ITA minimizes variation associated with manual pipetting, incubation, washing, and optical reading. However, the performance difference should not be attributed categorically to polyclonal antibodies in ITA and monoclonal antibodies in ELISA. The available manufacturer documentation identifies target-specific antibodies but does not disclose the antibody clones, epitope locations, affinity constants, or differential recognition of individual RBP4 proteoforms or adiponectin oligomers. For example, the Elabscience RBP4 assay is described as a sandwich ELISA using RBP4-specific capture and detection antibodies, but no epitope map or proteoform-reactivity profile is provided. Similarly, the general adiponectin kit is listed separately from the manufacturer’s high-molecular-weight adiponectin-specific assay, supporting interpretation of the present APN measurements as total immunoreactive adiponectin rather than a defined HMW fraction.

RBP4 is not a single homogeneous circulating molecular species. In addition to retinol-bound holo-RBP4 and unbound apo-RBP4, human serum may contain full-length RBP4 and C-terminally truncated forms, including RBP4-L and RBP4-LL. Mass-spectrometric studies have shown that the relative abundance of these proteoforms varies among individuals and clinical conditions and that truncated forms may constitute a substantial proportion of total circulating RBP4 in insulin-resistant or renally impaired populations. Importantly, previous work has demonstrated that immunoassays may differ in their relative reactivity toward full-length and proteolyzed RBP4, potentially producing underestimation or overestimation when the circulating proteoform distribution changes. Thus, the stronger clinical associations observed with ITA could hypothetically reflect more consistent recognition of the RBP4 species present in this cohort, whereas the positive ELISA bias could partly reflect different affinity for full-length or truncated forms. This interpretation remains speculative because proteoform-specific antibody binding was not directly evaluated in the present study.

Adiponectin also circulates as structurally distinct oligomeric complexes, including low-molecular-weight trimers, medium-molecular-weight hexamers, and high-molecular-weight multimers. Epitope accessibility may differ among these assemblies because oligomerization and conformational integrity affect the exposure of antibody-binding regions. The feasibility of developing antibodies that selectively recognize HMW adiponectin has been demonstrated experimentally, confirming that assay configuration can determine which oligomeric fraction is quantified. Clinically, HMW adiponectin and the HMW-to-total adiponectin ratio have shown stronger associations with angiographic coronary atherosclerotic severity than total adiponectin in some cohorts, although findings have not been uniform across all populations. Consequently, two assays nominally measuring “adiponectin” could produce different cardiovascular associations if they recognize the oligomeric forms with unequal affinity.

The present study measured total immunoreactive RBP4 and APN and did not separate RBP4 proteoforms or adiponectin oligomers. Therefore, the observed platform differences cannot be ascribed definitively to isoform-specific binding. Future method-comparison studies should obtain manufacturer-level information on antibody clones and epitope locations and should compare ITA and ELISA against orthogonal techniques, such as targeted mass spectrometry for full-length and truncated RBP4, non-denaturing separation of apo- and holo-RBP4, and HMW-specific adiponectin immunoassays or size-exclusion methods. Such experiments would determine whether the stronger clinicopathological associations observed with ITA reflect lower random analytical error, broader molecular recognition, preferential detection of biologically relevant forms, or a combination of these mechanisms.9–11

Preanalytical conditions represent another critical but frequently underestimated source of analytical variability in biomarker research. Our findings demonstrated that prolonged room-temperature exposure, repeated freeze–thaw cycles, and anticoagulant selection exerted markedly greater effects on ELISA-derived measurements than on ITA-derived measurements. These results are clinically relevant because preanalytical inconsistencies remain common in real-world laboratory workflows and may substantially compromise biomarker reliability.10,11 The greater susceptibility of ELISA to sample degradation is likely attributable to its strong dependence on intact epitope recognition. Protein denaturation during prolonged storage or freeze–thaw cycling may disrupt epitope integrity and consequently impair antigen-antibody binding efficiency, resulting in increased analytical deviation.12 In contrast, the use of polyclonal antibodies in ITA likely confers greater tolerance to partial antigen degradation because multiple epitopes remain available for immune complex formation even when some antigenic sites are damaged.13–15

The HIL experiments further demonstrated that endogenous sample-quality abnormalities affected the two platforms differently. This finding is particularly relevant to ITA because immunoturbidimetric measurement depends on changes in optical turbidity, which may be altered by free hemoglobin, bilirubin absorption, or lipid-related light scattering. ELISA is not immune to these effects because HIL may influence antibody binding, nonspecific background signal, optical-density measurement, or matrix-dependent analyte recovery. The experimentally established HIL tolerance limits therefore provide assay-specific criteria for accepting, commenting on, or rejecting affected specimens rather than assuming equivalent interference susceptibility across platforms.

Among the evaluated preanalytical variables, lithium-heparin plasma produced the greatest degree of interference, particularly in ELISA-based APN quantification. Heparin is a highly negatively charged glycosaminoglycan capable of interacting nonspecifically with circulating proteins and antibody complexes. Such interactions may alter protein conformation, interfere with antigen-antibody recognition, and increase nonspecific background signal, thereby amplifying measurement error.16,17,24,25 These findings underscore the importance of rigorous sample standardization in biomarker studies and suggest that serum specimens should be preferentially used for RBP4 and APN measurement whenever possible. More broadly, our data emphasize that preanalytical variability may not only influence absolute biomarker concentrations but may also affect downstream clinical interpretation and reproducibility across independent studies.

Clinically, we observed a progressive increase in serum RBP4 concentrations with worsening coronary lesion severity and increasing numbers of diseased coronary vessels. Conversely, APN concentrations progressively declined as coronary atherosclerotic burden increased. These findings are biologically plausible and align with the growing body of evidence implicating adipokine dysregulation in the pathogenesis of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.17,18 RBP4 has increasingly been recognized as a proatherogenic adipokine involved in insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and chronic vascular inflammation. Elevated circulating RBP4 may promote macrophage activation, impair endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability, and accelerate lipid accumulation within the vascular wall, thereby facilitating plaque formation and progression.17 In contrast, APN exerts multiple antiatherogenic and cardioprotective effects through suppression of inflammatory signaling, inhibition of vascular smooth muscle proliferation, attenuation of foam-cell formation, and activation of AMP-activated protein kinase-mediated metabolic pathways.18,26–30 Reduced APN concentrations may therefore reflect impaired vascular homeostasis and enhanced atherogenic activity in patients with advanced CAD.

The findings may also help explain some of the inconsistency reported in previous studies of RBP4 and APN. Published investigations have used different analytical platforms, antibody systems, calibrators, specimen matrices, dilution procedures, and sample-handling protocols.19–21 Consequently, apparently conflicting biomarker concentrations, cutoff values, or strengths of association may not always represent true biological differences among populations. Instead, part of the heterogeneity may arise from platform-dependent analytical bias or imprecision. For example, a method that produces greater random variation may attenuate an otherwise genuine biomarker–disease association, whereas systematic or concentration-dependent bias may shift proposed clinical thresholds. The present within-sample comparison therefore supports the interpretation that some inconsistencies in the RBP4 and APN literature may be methodological artifacts rather than exclusively pathophysiological differences. Nevertheless, the present cross-sectional, single-center comparison cannot establish analytical variability as the sole or dominant explanation for heterogeneity across the literature. Differences in population characteristics, renal and metabolic status, medication exposure, disease phenotype, sampling procedures, and study design may also contribute substantially. Multicenter harmonization studies using shared specimens, common reference materials, and standardized preanalytical protocols are required to quantify the relative contribution of assay-related and biological sources of heterogeneity. A further barrier to harmonization is the absence of a demonstrated common calibration hierarchy for the assay systems evaluated. Neither platform was independently calibrated against a higher-order international reference material, and the manufacturers’ calibrators may differ in protein source, molecular form, purity, matrix, and value-assignment procedure. Consequently, part of the observed systematic and proportional bias may reflect differences in calibration assignment in addition to antibody specificity, analytical imprecision, or matrix susceptibility. The absence of a common commutable reference material also means that agreement observed within one reagent combination cannot be assumed to apply to other ITA or ELISA systems. Development of well-characterized, commutable reference materials and reference measurement procedures is therefore required before numerical harmonization of RBP4 and APN results can be achieved across laboratories.

Importantly, the present study further demonstrated that both biomarkers were significantly associated with echocardiographic parameters reflecting left ventricular systolic and diastolic function. Higher RBP4 concentrations were associated with lower left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), impaired diastolic filling (lower E/A ratio), and increased left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), whereas APN demonstrated the opposite pattern. These associations suggest that RBP4 and APN may not merely serve as markers of coronary plaque burden but may also reflect myocardial remodeling and ventricular dysfunction. Mechanistically, RBP4-related insulin resistance and chronic inflammatory activation may contribute directly to cardiomyocyte injury, myocardial fibrosis, and adverse ventricular remodeling.22 Conversely, APN may exert direct cardioprotective effects through activation of AMP-activated protein kinase signaling, reduction of oxidative stress, inhibition of cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and attenuation of interstitial fibrosis.23 Therefore, the observed relationships between adipokine dysregulation and cardiac functional impairment likely reflect both vascular and myocardial pathophysiological processes.

A principal contribution of the present study is the direct quantification of how assay-dependent analytical variability may propagate into clinically relevant differences in biomarker interpretation. The principal novelty lies in demonstrating, within the same patient samples, that assay-dependent variability extends beyond differences in absolute biomarker concentrations and propagates into clinically relevant differences in correlation strength, diagnostic discrimination, cutoff values, and risk-reclassification estimates. The novelty does not lie simply in demonstrating that two immunoassay platforms can produce different absolute concentrations, because intermethod variability has previously been recognized. Rather, the present study shows that, when the same patient samples are analyzed using different platforms, the resulting measurements may produce different estimates of correlation strength, diagnostic discrimination, cutoff values, and incremental risk reclassification.

In the present cohort, ITA-derived RBP4 showed a stronger association with Gensini score than the corresponding ELISA-derived measurement. ITA also produced an AUC that was 0.071 higher than ELISA for RBP4 and 0.065 higher for APN. Furthermore, addition of ITA-derived RBP4 and APN to the conventional clinical model produced an NRI of 0.29, compared with 0.12 for the ELISA-based model, representing an absolute difference of 0.17. These findings provide direct evidence that analytical methodology may influence downstream estimates of biomarker performance rather than merely altering the numerical concentration reported by the laboratory.

This observation has important methodological implications. In biomarker studies, patients are often classified according to relatively narrow concentration thresholds, and modest analytical bias may therefore shift individuals across diagnostic or prognostic categories. Similarly, greater measurement imprecision may weaken observed correlations, reduce ROC discrimination, or attenuate the apparent incremental value of a biomarker. Consequently, differences among analytical platforms may contribute to inconsistent findings across studies even when the same underlying biological marker is examined.

The expanded agreement analysis distinguishes three related but different forms of intermethod disagreement. First, the mean paired difference reflects constant systematic bias. Second, regression of the signed difference against mean concentration evaluates directional proportional bias. Third, the relationship between absolute difference and mean concentration evaluates whether analytical disagreement becomes more variable across the measurement range. These distinctions are important because statistically significant proportional bias indicates that a universal additive conversion factor is inappropriate, whereas increasing absolute disagreement indicates reduced precision of interchangeability at particular concentration levels. The exploratory Gensini-score analysis was performed because biomarker concentrations changed progressively with coronary lesion severity. An unadjusted association between assay disagreement and Gensini score could arise simply because more severe disease was associated with different biomarker concentrations. We therefore additionally adjusted for mean biomarker concentration. An independent Gensini-score effect would suggest that disease-related matrix characteristics or molecular forms may influence intermethod agreement beyond concentration alone. Conversely, disappearance of the Gensini association after concentration adjustment would indicate that the apparent worsening of agreement primarily reflects concentration-dependent analytical bias rather than a separate effect of disease severity.

Nevertheless, these findings should not be interpreted as establishing the universal clinical superiority of ITA over ELISA. The present study was conducted at a single center, included only patients with angiographically confirmed coronary artery disease, and lacked an independent validation cohort. The stronger associations and higher discrimination observed with ITA may partly reflect the controlled sampling protocol, the selected disease spectrum, and the characteristics of the specific reagent systems used. Therefore, the results should be regarded as methodological and hypothesis-generating evidence supporting assay harmonization rather than as a paradigm-changing demonstration of a universally superior analytical platform.

The strong association between ITA-derived RBP4 and Gensini score (r=0.800) warrants cautious interpretation. The multivariable analysis showed that the association remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, smoking status, LDL-C, fasting blood glucose, and serum creatinine. Renal confounding was additionally reduced by excluding patients with severe renal insufficiency. Nevertheless, serum creatinine alone may not fully capture modest differences in renal filtration, tubular handling, or renal function-related RBP4 accumulation. Residual confounding by renal function therefore remains possible, particularly because estimated glomerular filtration rate was used as an exclusion criterion but was not included as a continuous covariate in the reported model.

Medication exposure and renal function remain important potential sources of residual confounding. Patients receiving an unchanged lipid-lowering regimen during the preceding 3 months were eligible, whereas patients with a recent initiation, discontinuation, or dose adjustment were excluded. However, class-specific information on statin exposure, statin intensity, treatment duration, adherence, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, and glucose-lowering medications was not incorporated into the analytical dataset. The associations of RBP4 and APN with Gensini score may therefore have been influenced partly by treatment-related effects. Renal confounding was reduced by excluding patients with an eGFR below 30 mL/min·1.73m−2 and by including serum creatinine in the multivariable model. Nevertheless, serum creatinine may not fully capture milder differences in glomerular filtration, tubular handling, or renal accumulation of RBP4. Because continuous eGFR and sufficiently detailed medication variables were not included in the finalized analyses, medication-adjusted and renal-function subgroup estimates could not be reported. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the observed biomarker–Gensini associations.

Importantly, overfitting is primarily a concern for the multivariable prediction models rather than for the unadjusted correlation coefficient itself. However, the magnitude of the correlation may still be inflated by cohort selection, influential observations, restricted clinical heterogeneity, or nonlinear relationships. Confirmation should include external replication and sensitivity analyses using rank-based correlation, influence diagnostics, flexible modeling of renal function, and stratification by statin exposure. Until such analyses are available, the observed coefficient should be interpreted as a strong within-cohort association rather than a universally reproducible effect size.

Assay selection should therefore be guided by the intended application, laboratory infrastructure, testing volume, and available resources rather than by a universal preference for one platform. ITA may be advantageous in laboratories that already possess compatible automated chemistry analyzers because it permits rapid, random-access measurement, limited manual handling, integration into routine laboratory workflows, and efficient high-throughput processing. Although acquisition, maintenance, calibration, and reagent-system requirements for automated analyzers involve substantial initial and continuing costs, the labor and processing cost per sample may become more favorable when testing volumes are high. In contrast, ELISA requires less specialized automated infrastructure and can be implemented in research laboratories using standard microplate equipment. It is therefore particularly useful for exploratory studies, epidemiological investigations, and resource-limited settings in which samples can be accumulated and analyzed in batches. However, ELISA generally involves greater hands-on time, multiple incubation and washing steps, batch-dependent turnaround, and reduced cost efficiency when only a small number of samples are tested per plate. The effective cost per result for either method consequently depends on local reagent prices, instrument availability, labor costs, batch utilization, quality-control requirements, and annual testing volume. Because a formal health-economic or laboratory-cost analysis was not performed in the present study, the findings support a fit-for-purpose recommendation rather than a universal economic preference: ITA may be more suitable for rapid, standardized, high-volume clinical testing where automated infrastructure is available, whereas ELISA remains a practical option for controlled batch-based research and settings without access to dedicated automated platforms.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center cross-sectional study with a relatively modest sample size. All participants had angiographically confirmed coronary artery disease, and the findings may therefore not be generalizable to community populations, individuals without established coronary disease, or patients treated in other healthcare settings. The selected disease spectrum and standardized sampling conditions may also have contributed to the relatively strong biomarker–disease associations observed in this cohort. In addition, patients presenting within 72 h of acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction and those with severe renal insufficiency, systemic infection, malignancy, autoimmune disease, or chronic inflammatory disease were excluded. The findings therefore should not be extrapolated directly to acute coronary syndromes, advanced renal dysfunction, or clinically complex populations without further validation.

Second, complete availability of the prespecified clinical, laboratory, angiographic, and echocardiographic variables was required for inclusion. Although this approach resulted in a complete final analytical dataset and avoided statistical imputation, exclusion of otherwise eligible patients with incomplete information may have introduced selection bias.

Third, the preanalytical substudy included only 20 healthy volunteers and was exploratory rather than formally powered for population-level inference. Healthy-volunteer samples may not fully reproduce the concentration ranges, metabolic abnormalities, medication effects, or matrix characteristics present in patients with coronary artery disease. The observed effects of room-temperature storage, freeze–thaw cycles, centrifugation, and anticoagulant type should therefore be confirmed in larger patient-based cohorts.

Fourth, only ITA and ELISA were evaluated. The findings may depend partly on the specific reagents, antibodies, calibration systems, instruments, and lot numbers used in this study and cannot automatically be generalized to all commercially available ITA or ELISA platforms. Higher-order metrological traceability was not established for either biomarker. Calibration relied on manufacturer-assigned ITA calibrators and ELISA kit standards, and no common certified reference material was used to align the two platforms. At the time of manuscript revision, no WHO International Standard for adiponectin and no IRMM, BCR, or ERM certified reference material for RBP4 was identified in the corresponding official catalogues. Therefore, the reported absolute concentrations, intermethod biases, and ROC-derived cutoff values are specific to the reagent systems, calibrators, instruments, dilution procedures, and patient cohort evaluated. The proposed thresholds should not be transferred directly to other laboratories or platforms without local verification or recalibration using commutable materials.

Fifth, the strong correlation between ITA-derived RBP4 and Gensini score may have been influenced by spectrum enrichment, cohort selection, and the relatively homogeneous single-center study population. Although multivariable adjustment was performed, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Sixth, only 34 patients met the definition of severe coronary artery disease. The ROC cutoff values, multivariable prediction estimates, NRI, and IDI were derived and evaluated in the same cohort and may therefore be affected by model optimism and overfitting. These measures should be considered exploratory until independently validated.

Finally, the cross-sectional design prevents conclusions regarding causality or long-term prognosis. Elevated RBP4 and reduced APN may contribute to coronary atherosclerosis, reflect its metabolic consequences, or represent a combination of both processes. Prospective multicenter studies with longitudinal follow-up, standardized reference materials, and external validation are required to establish the reproducibility and prognostic relevance of these findings.

Detailed medication exposure was not incorporated into the regression analyses. Although patients with recent changes in lipid-lowering treatment were excluded, patients receiving stable lipid-lowering therapy were eligible, and information on statin class and intensity, treatment duration, adherence, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors, and glucose-lowering medications was not available for additional adjustment. Medication-related residual confounding therefore cannot be excluded. In addition, serum creatinine was included as a renal-function covariate and patients with an eGFR below 30 mL/min·1.73 m−2 were excluded, but continuous eGFR was not modeled and renal-function subgroup analyses were not performed. Residual confounding across mild-to-moderate renal dysfunction remains possible, particularly for RBP4, and should be addressed in future studies using continuous eGFR, additional renal markers, and adequately powered renal-function strata.

Hemolysis, icterus, and lipemia were not included in the preanalytical substudy. These endogenous sample-quality abnormalities may affect ITA through spectral absorption and turbidity-related light scattering and may affect ELISA through matrix effects, altered antibody binding, nonspecific signal, or optical interference. Consequently, assay-specific HIL tolerance limits for RBP4 and APN could not be established, and the present findings should not be used to define acceptance or rejection criteria for hemolyzed, icteric, or lipemic specimens. Dedicated HIL interference testing following CLSI EP07 and verification of AU5800 HIL alert indices are required before routine laboratory implementation.

The LoQ was not independently verified for either analytical platform according to a formal detection-capability protocol. Manufacturer-reported analytical sensitivity should therefore not be interpreted as a laboratory-established LoQ. Although accepted measurements were restricted to the applicable calibration intervals and the reported group-level concentrations were above the lower calibration limits, future method-comparison studies should formally verify LoQ and dilution integrity using predefined imprecision and bias criteria. CLSI recommends separate verification of detection capability and validation of dilution schemes used to extend a method’s measuring interval.

The analyses of proportional bias and its relationship with Gensini score were exploratory and were performed within the same single-center cohort. Associations between intermethod disagreement and disease severity may reflect concentration-dependent bias, disease-related matrix effects, unmeasured clinical characteristics, or a combination of these factors. These findings therefore require confirmation using shared samples across laboratories and assay platforms.

The study did not include a formal workflow or economic evaluation. Instrument acquisition and maintenance costs, reagent expenditure, staff time, batch utilization, turnaround time, and cost per reportable result were not prospectively collected. The comparative analytical findings therefore should not be interpreted as demonstrating the economic superiority of either platform. Cost-effectiveness is likely to vary according to local infrastructure, testing volume, procurement arrangements, and laboratory workflow.

Conclusion

Assay-dependent variability influenced measured RBP4 and APN concentrations and the apparent strength of their associations with Gensini-defined coronary atherosclerotic severity. Under the conditions evaluated, ITA showed lower analytical imprecision, greater stability across the preanalytical conditions examined, and higher within-cohort diagnostic and risk-reclassification performance than ELISA. These findings do not establish ITA as a universally superior platform. ITA may be better suited to rapid, standardized, high-volume testing in laboratories with compatible automated infrastructure, whereas ELISA remains useful for batch-based epidemiological and exploratory research and in settings with limited access to automated analyzers. Assay selection should therefore be fit for purpose and should consider analytical performance, testing volume, turnaround time, laboratory infrastructure, and local cost structure. Because higher-order metrological traceability was not established, the reported concentrations and diagnostic thresholds remain assay- and cohort-specific. Multicenter harmonization, external validation, and formal workflow and economic evaluations are required before RBP4- or APN-based thresholds can be transferred across laboratories or adopted for routine clinical use.
