Qualitative and flow cytometric evaluations of goat’s frozen semen in Mito-TEMPO supplemented plant-based extender
Authors: Fatemeh Zarei, Nader Asadzadeh, Hassan Khamisabadi, Hoda Javaheri Barfourooshi, Ali Hatefi, Reza Masoudi, Mohsen Sharafi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCryopreservation of buck spermatozoa is an efficient strategy to improve qualified sperm cells for artificial insemination; however, this process potentially decreases the qualitative characteristics of post-thawed sperm. Therefore, this research aimed to evaluate the influence of the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant “Mito-TEMPO” on buck sperm quality during the cryopreservation process in a plant-based extender.ResultsThis study indicated that the dosages 10 and 100 µM Mito-TEMPO presented higher (P ≤ 0.05) percentages of total motility, progressive motility, membrane integrity, mitochondrial activity, acrosome integrity, and viability while showing lower (P ≤ 0.05) late apoptosis, DNA fragmentation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) concentration, and lipid peroxidation compared to the control group. Different treatments of Mito-TEMPO indicated no effect (P > 0.05) on sperm cells with abnormal morphology.ConclusionsIt can be concluded that this mitochondria-targeted antioxidant may be able to preserve the qualitative parameters in the post-thawed buck spermatozoa.

Background

Sperm freezing has been known as an essential strategy for the long-term preservation of sperm cells. This strategy is capable of conserving gene pools, and has developed the desired reproductive merits through artificial insemination; however, this process leads to physical and chemical damages to sperm, which considerably reduces the fertility potential [1]. The polyunsaturated fatty acid content of sperm is susceptible to lipid peroxidation due to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) during the freeze-thawing process [2], resulting in alterations to the plasma membrane and reduced fertility [3]. Although sperm and seminal plasma contain endogenous antioxidants that naturally neutralize ROS, their levels are insufficient under artificial conditions; therefore, exogenous antioxidants must be supplemented to the extender to support sperm preservation [3].

The mitochondria-targeted antioxidant “Mito-TEMPO” has been indicated as a powerful cell-permeable ROS scavenger. It consists of Tempo and tri-phenyl-phosphonium (TPP+). Tempo mimics superoxide dismutase activity and dismutases superoxide in the catalytic cycle, while TPP is a membrane-permeant cation [4]. This combination provides a mitochondria-targeted component that effectively scavenges superoxide. Mito-TEMPO also protects cells against oxidative damages in some human pathologies [5, 6]. Mito-TEMPO also preserved the quality of human post-thawed spermatozoa after cryopreservation [7].

The ingredients of the extender, on the other hand, are effective in preserving the quality of the post-thawed spermatozoa [1]. Soybean lecithin (SL) has been reported to show an extracellular cryoprotective effect and has been successfully used in semen cryopreservation [8]. It could be recognized as a potent alternative for egg yolk because of egg yolk issues such as microbial contamination, agglutination, and early induced acrosome reaction [8]. Therefore, using SL could be helpful to remove the risks of egg yolk in ram sperm cryopreservation extenders.

By now, no study has yet been conducted to evaluate the effect of Mito-TEMPO on goat semen quality cryopreserved in SL-based extender. Therefore, this research was performed to evaluate the supplementing effects of SL-based freezing medium with Mito-TEMPO on the sperm qualitative traits, such as motility, membrane functionality, abnormal morphology, mitochondrial activity, acrosome integrity, viability, apoptosis status, lipid peroxidation, DNA fragmentation, and ROS concentration of buck post-thawed semen.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

The Chemicals, mentioned in the “Materials and methods” section, were provided from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany) and Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA) companies.

Semen collection and freezing procedure

The semen samples were collected via artificial vagina twice a week from five healthy mature Saanen bucks (3–4 years) in six replicates. All procedures regarding the maintenance of the mentioned bucks were in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines. The samples were collected in the breeding season (September), and the temperature was 30–35 °C. Samples were evaluated and included in the experiment if the following parameters were observed: volume of 1–2 mL; sperm concentration of 3 × 109 spermatozoa/ml, total motility ≥ 70%, and normal morphology ≥ 85%. Buck semen samples were then pooled due to eliminating individual differences. The samples were pooled with equal concentrations of semen from each goat at the same time. The cryopreservation extender was composed of glycerol (5% v/v), fructose (1.26 g/100 mL), citric acid (1.64 g/100 mL), soybean lecithin (1.5% w/v), Tris (3.07 g/100 mL), streptomycin (1 mg), and penicillin (100 IU). The pH was 6.8, and the osmolality without glycerol was 425 mOsm/kg water [9]. Semen samples were diluted with extender (ratio: 1:10; final concentration: 400 × 106 cell/ml) containing various concentrations of Mito-TEMPO as follows: extender without Mito-TEMPO (MT0), extender with 1 µM Mito-TEMPO (MT1), extender with 10 µM Mito-TEMPO (MT10), extender with 100 µM Mito-TEMPO (MT100), and extender with 1000 µM Mito-TEMPO (MT1000). Diluted buck semen samples were gently cooled at 5 °C for 120 min and then loaded into 0.25 ml French straws (Biovet, L’Agile, France) to obtain 50 × 106 spermatozoa/straw. The straws were then exposed to static nitrogen vapor (-70 °C) for 10 min, plunged into liquid nitrogen (LN2), and stored in LN2 until thawing time. For quality evaluation, the samples were thawed in water (37 °C) for 30 s.

Quality evaluation of post-thawed semen

Motility and velocity parameters

Sperm class analysis software (Version 5.1; Microptic, Barcelona, Spain) evaluated the sperm motility parameters [10]. In this evaluation, semen samples were diluted with PBS buffer (1:7) and incubated at 35 °C for 5 min. 5 µL of diluted sample was then put on the pre-warmed (38 °C) chamber slide (Leja 4, 20 μm height, Leja Products, Luzernestraat B.V., Holland). At least six fields which were contained a minimum of 400 spermatozoa, were evaluated for each sample under a phase contrast microscope at 37 °C and 100 × magnification. The following recorded items such as: total motility (TM, %), progressive motility (PM, %), average path velocity (VAP, m/s), straight-line velocity (VSL, m/s), curvilinear velocity (VCL, m/s), and linearity (LIN, %).

Membrane functionality

Hypo-osmotic swelling test (HOST) recorded sperm samples with the integrated membranes [11]. HOST depends on the resistance of the sperm membrane to stressful status in a hypo-osmotic medium. In this assessment, 5 µL of the semen sample was mixed with 50 µl hypo-osmotic solution (57.6 mM fructose and 19.2 mM sodium citrate, 100 mOsm/L) and incubated for 20 min. Then, 300 spermatozoa were analyzed under a phase-contrast microscope (×400 magnification), and samples were recorded with swollen and non-swollen tails as sperm cells with integrated and non-integrated membranes, respectively.

Sperm morphology

Hancock solution was applied to investigate sperm abnormal morphology in the post-thawed condition. Therefore, a drop of the thawed sample was placed in an Eppendorf tube containing 1 µL of Hancock solution [150 mL sodium saline solution, 500 ml double-distilled water, 62.5 mL formalin (37%), and 150 mL buffer solution] [12]. Then, 300 spermatozoa were counted under a phase-contrast microscope (CKX41, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan; × 1000 magnification; oil immersion), and the percentages of abnormal sperm cells (detached heads, acrosome and caput abnormalities, abnormal mid-pieces, the presence of cytoplasmic droplets, and tail defects) were measured.

Acrosome integrity

Pisum Sativum Agglutinin (PSA) measured sperm acrosome integrity [13]. Five µL semen sample was combined with 100 µL ethanol and after 20 min, 10 µL of the suspended sample was added to 30 µL of PSA on the glass slide. 200 spermatozoa were then evaluated on a slide by fluorescent microscope (BX51, Olympus) with the fluorescent illumination and a FITC filter with ×400 magnification. Spermatozoa with and without green heads were considered as the intact acrosomes and disrupted/damaged acrosomes, respectively.

Lipid peroxidation

As an indicator of lipid peroxidation, the Malondialdehyde (MDA) level was detected via reacting with thiobarbituric acid [14]. The MDA level was determined using absorption with a standard curve of MDA equivalent created via the acid-catalyzed hydrolysis of 1, 1, 3, 3-tetramethoxypropane. In this regard, 1 mL of the diluted semen sample (400 × 106 sperm cells/ml) was added to 1 mL of cold 20% (w/v) tri-chloroacetic acid to allow the proteins of the sample to precipitate. The precipitate was then pelleted under centrifuging (960 × g for 15 min), and 1 mL of the supernatant was incubated with 1 mL of 0.67% (w/v) thiobarbituric acid into a boiling water bath at 100 °C for 10 min. Following cooling, the absorbance was determined via a spectrophotometer (UV-1200, Shimadzu, Japan) at 532 nm. All MDA concentrations were indicated as nmol/ml.

Flow cytometry evaluation of semen parameters

A FACSCalibur (Becton Dickinson, San Khosoz, CA, USA) flow cytometer set was applied to investigate the mitochondrial activity, apoptosis status, DNA fragmentation, and ROS concentration [15]. At least 10,000 spermatozoa were evaluated per assay at a flow rate of 100 cells/s. The spermatozoa population was gated under 90° and forward-angle light scatter to eliminate aggregates and debris. The wavelength of excitation was 488 nm, which was provided by an argon laser. Green fluorescence (Rh123, Annexin V, and DCF) was detected on the FL1 detector with a 530/30 nm band-pass filter, and red fluorescence (PI) was detected on the FL2 detector with a 585/42 nm band-pass filter. As a sign of fragmentation, Single-stranded DNA emits a red fluorescence that is detected at a 670 band pass filter (FL3). Recorded data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Treestar, Inc., San Carlos, CA).

Mitochondrial activity

To assess mitochondrial activity, 10 µL of Rhodamin-123 solution (Rh123, 0.01 mg/ml; Invitrogen TM, Eugene, OR, USA) was supplemented to 300 µL of diluted semen and then incubated in the dark room for 20 min. After that, sperm suspension was centrifuged at 500 × g for 3 min and again re-suspended in 500 µL Tris buffer. 10 µL of propidium iodide (PI, 1.0 µg/mL) was then supplemented to the buck sperm suspension. Sperm cells with the positive signal for Rh123 and the negative signal for PI (Rh123+/PI-) were recorded as spermatozoa with active mitochondria [16].

Apoptosis status

Annexin V-FITC kit (IQP, Groningen, The Netherlands) and PI were used to assess phosphatidyl serine externalization as an index of apoptosis status in spermatozoa [16]. The samples were washed in calcium buffer and adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 106 spermatozoa/ml, followed by the supplementation of 10 µL Annexin V–FITC (0.01 µg/mL) to 100 µL of the sperm suspension, subsequently incubated for 20 min at room temperature 22 °C. Afterward, 10 µL of PI was supplemented to the sperm suspension and then incubated at 22 °C for at least 10 min. This suspension was the investigated via flow cytometric method, and sperm subpopulations were classified into 4 groups: (1) viable cells were negative for both Annexin V and PI (A-/PI-); (2) early apoptotic cells were positive for Annexin V but negative for PI (A+/PI-); (3) late apoptotic cells were positive for both Annexin V and PI (A+/PI+); and (4) necrotic cells were negative for Annexin V and positive for PI (A-/PI+).

DNA fragmentation

The Sperm Chromatin Structure Assay (SCSA) was used to measure sperm DNA damage by the flow cytometric analysis [17]. A PBS-diluted aliquot (1 × 106 per ml) of washed spermatozoa was made, and the cell suspension was consequently treated by an acid detergent solution that contained 0.1% Triton X-100, 0.15 mol/l NaCl, and 0.08 M HCl for 40 s, and then stained by a 6 µg/L purified acridine orange in the phosphate-citrate buffer. Acridine orange, when associated with single-stranded DNA, emits a red fluorescence which is detected at a 670-band pass filter (Fl-3). The abnormal spermatozoa percentage was obtained from the DFI frequency dot plot, which is calculated from the ratio of red and total (red plus green) fluorescence intensity [18].

ROS concentration

Dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) evaluated the intracellular H2O2 content as an index of ROS concentration [19]. Semen samples were washed with PBS to adjust the concentration of 3–5 × 106 spermatozoa/ml. DCFH-DA (25 µM) was supplemented to the sperm suspension and then incubated at 25° C for 40 min. After that, the suspension was re-washed, and the supernatant was removed. PBS was added to readjust the same sperm concentration (3–5 × 106 spermatozoa/mL) at 25° C (1000 rpm for 8 min) and 2 µL of PI (PI, 1.0 µg/mL) was supplemented to the semen samples before flow cytometric investigation.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using Proc GLM procedures of the SAS 9.1 program (SAS Institute, version 9.1, 2002, Cary, NC, USA). Tukey’s test was applied to find out the statistical differences between the various experimental groups. The analyzed results were presented as mean with their standard error of the mean (SEM) for all groups for each investigated parameter. There were considered to be mean differences when there was a P ≤ 0.05.

Discussion

Supplementation of freezing extenders with antioxidants is an effective approach to conserve the quality of spermatozoa after the freeze-thawing process [20]. Polyunsaturated fatty acids in sperm membrane make spermatozoa prone to cryo-damage that decreases post-thawed sperm quality [3]. Therefore, using exogenous antioxidants in the freezing medium could be an efficient strategy to improve spermatozoa performance against cryo-damages.

Mito-TEMPO is a powerful mitochondria-targeted antioxidant that specifically accumulates in the mitochondrial matrix because of its positive charge. It exerts the targeted antioxidant impact via inhibiting or eliminating lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial oxygen-free radical production. It is also able to regulate antioxidant-related enzyme activities in the cells [21]. In this study, the use of Mito-TEMPO resulted in higher TM, PM, membrane functionality, mitochondrial activity, acrosome integrity, and viability, as well as lower lipid peroxidation, late apoptotic changes, DNA fragmentation, and ROS concentration in the buck post-thawed spermatozoa. The results are in agreement with other studies, which presented the ROS-scavenging ability of Mito-TEMPO in ram [22].

As presented in the results, the fresh semen group showed the best quality compared to the other groups. This is clear, and we didn’t need to discuss it here because the fresh semen samples did not face to cold shock [16]. However, among the cryopreserved semen samples, MT10 and MT100 were the best and presented higher quality than the others. According to the results in Tables 1 and 2, and 4, MT1 presented a similar quality to MT10 in terms of total motility, membrane functionality, and DNA fragmentation, so it showed that the lower concentration of Mito-TEMPO had some positive effect, but it was insufficient. While the higher concentration of Mito-TEMPO had no beneficial effect on the quality of buck semen, as it was recorded, there was no difference between MT0 and MT1000. It would be mentioned that the higher concentration of Mito-TEMPO neutralizes its positive effects and decreases the quality parameters [11].

The freezing process induces several changes in sperm acrosome and calcium channels, which is termed cryo-capacitation [23, 24]. However, an increase in the apoptotic factors, such as phosphatidylserine externalizations during the freezing-thawing process, can induce acrosome reactivity [25]. The supplementation of 10 and 100 µM Mito-TEMPO created a greater acrosome integrity, which is indicative of the beneficial effect of Mito-TEMPO on acrosome integrity. The impact of Mito-TEMPO on acrosome integrity during the freezing process was in agreement with other research results in the ram [16] that presented supplementing Mito-TEMPO in the freezing medium can be efficient in protecting acrosome integrity following the freeze-thawing process.

Mitochondria supply energy for spermatozoa through oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthase [26], but the thermal shocks and ROS have adverse effects on the mitochondrial activity, resulting in ATP transport impairment [27]. An imbalance between the formation and removal of free radicals induces oxidative stress, resulting in DNA damage and apoptotic changes [28, 29]. Intense oxidative stress causes mitochondrial fission, which elicits mitochondria-dynamic response [30], aggregation [31], and dysfunctions [32]. An increase in mitochondrial fission most likely decreases ATP production, which causes induced mitochondrial-derived apoptosis [33]. As observed in this study, optimum concentrations of Mito-TEMPO preserved sperm mitochondrial activity, so this phenomenon probably improved other mitochondria-relevant processes such as motility, viability, and DNA integrity. Mitochondrial dysfunction induces the nuclear translocation of apoptosis factors like endonuclease G [34], which increases the formation of the pores in the outer mitochondrial membrane and consequently mitochondrial permeability transition by rupture of the outer membrane due to matrix swelling [35]. Mito-TEMPO prevents mitochondrial BAX translocation [36], making it a potentially useful strategy for protecting mitochondrial activity and viability during the freeze-thawing process. Mito-TEMPO also preserves mitochondrial activity and reduces stress-induced apoptosis and necrosis [37] by reducing superoxide levels. It maintains the electron transport chain and phospholipid bilayer membrane stability [21, 38]. DNA integrity and mitochondrial activity correlate with the cell-qualitative parameters and fertility potential of post-thawed sperm cells [39]. The reported mechanisms are in agreement with the results of the current study, which presented the helpful effects of Mito-TEMPO in the reduction of DNA fragmentation, apoptotic changes, and ROS concentration. The similar effects were observed when buck’s semen samples were cryopreserved in MitoQ-supplemented freezing extender [40].

In this study, Mito-TEMPO did not indicate the effect on the rate of sperm with abnormal morphology because morphology is a relevant feature to spermatogenesis [41]. The results of morphology evaluation are in agreement with other studies, which recorded the independence of sperm morphology from the freeze-thawing process in the ram [16].

In this study, SL was supplemented to the freezing extenders for evaluating the effect of supplementing SL-based extender with Mito-TEMPO on buck sperm quality. The use of extenders based on SL has recently been increased due to several problems of animal-origin extenders [1]. In previous studies [1, 42], SL showed several beneficial effects against cryo-damages because it can adhere to the sperm plasma membrane and preserve sperm cells against ROS [42]. Moreover, egg yolk (EY)-based extenders have several problems, such as agglutination, standardization, and contamination, that lead to an increase in the demand for the replacement of EY in extenders [1]. In some studies, higher protective effects of SL have been reported when compared to EY [43] (Emamverdi M, Zhandi M 2013), while others have indicated equal effects between SL and EY [42]. Moreover, Tabarez et al. (2020) reported that SL, as a potential alternative to powdered egg yolk for buck sperm cryopreservation, does not protect them from mitochondrial damage [44].

Conclusion

In this study, supplementing 10 and 100 µM Mito-TEMPO to buck plant-based cryopreservation medium preserved the quality of post-thawed spermatozoa. Therefore, it can be concluded that the supplementation of Mito-TEMPO to the SL-based freezing medium could be an effective strategy to preserve buck post-thawed semen quality for reproductive programs. Further research could be helpful in elucidating the mechanisms underlying the protective role of Mito-TEMPO in buck semen freezing in SL-based freezing media.
