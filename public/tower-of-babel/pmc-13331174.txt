When recanalization is not reperfusion: The no-reflow challenge in stroke
Authors: Matija Zupan, Senta Frol
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Impaired microvascular reperfusion despite successful macrovascular recanalization has emerged as a critical determinant of poor outcomes after technically successful endovascular treatment (EVT) in large vessel occlusion (LVO) acute ischemic stroke (AIS). The recent review by Qiao et al., provides an important and timely synthesis of a key limitation of modern reperfusion therapy: The persistent mismatch between angiographic “success” and meaningful tissue salvage.[10] By shifting the focus from macrovascular patency toward tissue-level perfusion, this review strengthens the conceptual foundation for a more biologically relevant definition of reperfusion success.

Incomplete tissue-level reperfusion can occur despite successful EVT, and appears to be driven by at least two distinct mechanisms: territorial hypoperfusion from distal emboli and microvascular failure within the ischemic core often conceptualized as true no-reflow.[10] This distinction is clinically critical, as it highlights that restoration of proximal flow is necessary but frequently insufficient for neurological recovery. Indeed, futile recanalization remains a major unresolved challenge in AIS, suggesting that downstream microvascular dysfunction may be a decisive determinant of final infarct growth and functional outcome.

Mechanistically, the no-reflow phenomenon reflects dysfunction of the neurovascular unit, including endothelial swelling, capillary compression from edema, microvascular obstruction by thromboemboli and platelet–leukocyte aggregates, and pericyte-mediated capillary constriction.[12] These processes result in persistent hypoperfusion within territories that appear angiographically reperfused. Although no-reflow has been extensively described in experimental models, translation into routine clinical identification remains difficult, largely due to variability in imaging methodology and lack of standardized diagnostic criteria. Perfusion imaging using computed tomography or magnetic resonance perfusion, and contrast-free arterial spin labeling, may allow detection of persistent regional cerebral blood flow reduction after EVT. However, without harmonized thresholds and systematic serial imaging, the true prevalence and clinical impact of no-reflow remain uncertain. Qiao et al., convincingly argue that this diagnostic ambiguity has contributed to inconsistent terminology and wide variation in reported rates of incomplete tissue-level reperfusion.[10]

Beyond its prognostic value, no-reflow is increasingly recognized as a potentially modifiable therapeutic target. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that impaired microvascular reperfusion despite successful recanalization is common and strongly associated with poor neurological outcome and increased complications.[9] Collectively, these findings reinforce the notion that the “angiographic ceiling” of EVT has been reached in a subset of patients, and that additional strategies aimed at microvascular rescue are needed.

In this context, adjunctive intra-arterial (IA) thrombolysis following EVT has re-emerged as a promising approach. While EVT effectively restores proximal vessel patency, it does not directly address distal thrombi or microvascular obstruction. IA thrombolysis may therefore improve downstream reperfusion by dissolving residual distal emboli and fibrin-rich microthrombi. Importantly, recent clinical data suggest that this strategy can improve functional outcomes without a clear increase in symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sICH). A meta-analysis of randomized evidence by Gonçalves et al., found that IA tenecteplase administered after successful EVT improved excellent functional outcomes while not increasing mortality or hemorrhagic risk.[3] While the PROACT II trial showed improved recanalization and outcomes with IA thrombolysis,[2] several recent randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have rigorously evaluated adjunctive IA thrombolysis after successful EVT for LVO AIS. The CHOICE trial demonstrated that adjunct IA alteplase significantly increased the proportion of patients achieving an excellent 90-day outcome (modified Rankin Scale [mRS]: 0–1: 59.0% vs. 40.4% with placebo; adjusted risk difference ~18%) compared with placebo, albeit with limited sample size and early termination that tempered definitive interpretation.[11] The ANGEL-TNK trial extended this approach using IA tenecteplase, showing a higher rate of excellent 90-day functional outcome (40.5% vs. 26.4%; relative risk 1.44; p = 0.02) without increased sICH or mortality, though secondary outcomes did not uniformly corroborate the primary endpoint.[7] In the PEARL trial, IA alteplase delivered after successful EVT similarly improved 90-day freedom from disability (44.8% vs. 30.2%; adjusted risk ratio 1.45; p = 0.01) compared with standard care, with numerically higher rates of any intracranial hemorrhage and mortality that did not reach statistical significance.[13] Most recently, the CHOICE 2 trial demonstrated a 15% absolute increase in excellent functional outcomes (57.5% vs. 42.9%; number needed to treat ≈7) with IA alteplase and a reduction in persistent hypoperfusion on imaging, without a significant increase in sICH, although all-cause mortality was numerically higher in the lytic arm.[1] Collectively, these studies suggest that adjunctive IA lytic therapy following technically successful EVT may augment microvascular reperfusion and improve functional outcomes. However, limitations remain, including heterogeneous thrombolytic agents and doses, mixed statistical support across secondary endpoints, early trial termination (CHOICE), and imbalances in safety end points (PEARL, CHOICE-2). Larger, confirmatory phase III trials with standardized protocols and long-term safety assessments are required before revising guideline recommendations.

Qiao et al., appropriately highlight that most RCTs lacked systematic perfusion imaging, limiting mechanistic interpretation.[10] In particular, the greater treatment effect observed in patients with incomplete angiographic reperfusion (eTICI 2b) suggests that IA thrombolysis may primarily target distal emboli rather than true microvascular no-reflow. Thus, although pharmacological reperfusion appears beneficial, future studies should incorporate early and serial perfusion imaging to disentangle whether observed clinical gains reflect improved territorial perfusion, reversal of microvascular dysfunction, or both. This distinction is essential, because microvascular no-reflow may require fundamentally different interventions targeting pericyte constriction, inflammatory cascades, endothelial injury, or blood–brain barrier disruption rather than fibrinolysis alone.

Further enhancement of fibrinolysis may be achievable by combining thrombolytics with agents such as eptifibatide, argatroban, factor XI/XII inhibitors, a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with a thrombospondin type 1 motif, member 13 (ADAMTS-13), or anti–von Willebrand factor aptamers,[8] although hemorrhagic risk must be carefully considered. A recently presented ATTRACTION trial evaluated whether adjunctive platelet inhibition can mitigate futile recanalization after technically successful EVT.[6] In this large multicenter RCT, the investigators included 1,380 patients from China with anterior-circulation LVO achieving successful reperfusion, randomized to tirofiban (IA bolus followed by 24-h intravenous infusion) versus placebo. Adjunct tirofiban significantly increased functional 90-day independence (mRS: 0–2: 49.3% vs. 43.3%; adjusted risk ratio 1.15, 95% CI: 1.03–1.27; p = 0.01), supporting the concept that post-EVT microvascular platelet-mediated no-reflow or early re-occlusion may be clinically modifiable. However, sICH was numerically higher (11.9% vs. 9.4%) despite being reported as not statistically different, underscoring the narrow safety margin of intensified antiplatelet therapy in reperfused brain tissue. In addition, generalizability beyond the Chinese EVT population, interaction with periprocedural antithrombotic regimens, and the absence of mechanistic imaging endpoints limit immediate extrapolation. Nonetheless, ATTRACTION provides important randomized evidence that post-thrombectomy glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibition may improve functional recovery without an overt mortality penalty, warranting confirmatory trials and refined risk stratification.

Despite strong and consistent neuroprotective effects in experimental ischemia models, where cooling reduces metabolism, excitotoxicity, and infarct growth, clinical translation of hypothermia as a targeted neuroprotection into acute stroke care has been challenging.[14] While multiple small human studies have shown that cooling strategies (systemic or selective) are feasible and generally safe, they have not demonstrated convincing consistent improvements in functional outcomes in AIS patients; moreover, slower cooling methods have been particularly difficult to implement and have been associated with higher complication rates such as pneumonia and shivering.[14] However, in a recently presented CHILL-ART trial from China, 262 patients were randomized to EVT plus IA cold saline infusion versus room-temperature saline, demonstrating a statistically significant improvement in 90-day functional independence (mRS: 0–2: 54.7% vs. 39.8%; adjusted risk ratio 1.36, 95% CI: 1.05–1.76), without excess sICH (7.0% vs. 9.0%) or mortality (13.3% vs. 18.0%), suggesting a plausible neuroprotective adjunct at the time of reperfusion.[4] In contrast, the similarly sized FOCUS trial, also from China (n = 258), found no improvement in global disability (adjusted common odds ratio 1.16, 95% CI 0.75–1.79), yet reported a significant reduction in any intracranial hemorrhage at 24 h (adjusted risk difference −0.174, 95% CI: −0.288 to −0.059), with no difference in sICH or mortality.[5] Taken together, these studies reinforce the feasibility and apparent procedural safety of IA cooling, but the mixed functional efficacy, despite overlapping designs, populations, and endpoints, including limited generalizability to other populations, raises concerns about reproducibility, sensitivity to cooling protocols (dose/temperature/duration), and the potential for chance findings in moderately sized trials. Definitive adoption will require larger confirmatory trials with standardized hypothermia delivery metrics and mechanistic imaging correlates to determine whether observed hemorrhage reduction translates into consistent patient-centered benefit.

No-reflow could also be integrated into the evolving ischemic core paradigm. Traditionally regarded as irreversibly infarcted, diffusion-weighted imaging lesions may partially reverse with rapid reperfusion, supporting the concept that the “core” includes heterogeneous tissue with variable viability.[12] Within this framework, persistent microvascular hypoperfusion may drive secondary infarct expansion even after successful macrovascular recanalization. This reinforces the clinical relevance of no-reflow as an active biological process rather than merely an epiphenomenon of completed infarction.

In conclusion, Qiao et al., provide a highly valuable synthesis that advances an important paradigm shift in AIS care: From angiographic recanalization toward tissue-level reperfusion as the true therapeutic goal.[10] Adjunctive IA thrombolysis, particularly with tenecteplase, represents a biologically plausible strategy to bridge the gap between technical success and functional recovery. Certain adjunct antiplatelet agents (e.g., tirofiban) may improve functional outcomes after successful recanalization, and targeted neuroprotection with selective hypothermia may show additional clinical benefit. Nevertheless, future trials should incorporate standardized perfusion imaging endpoints to identify mechanistic subtypes of incomplete reperfusion and guide individualized treatment approaches.
