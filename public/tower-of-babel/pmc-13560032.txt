Dynamic changes and roles of LYVE-1 during lipopolysaccharide-induced ALI/ARDS in mice
Authors: Shuntaro Matsunaga, Shinnosuke Takamori, Shen Lin, Chen Man, Yukie Mizuta, Sayoko Narahara, Takahito Kawano, Masaharu Murata, Tomohiko Akahoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•LYVE-1 expression dynamically changes in acute lung injury.•LYVE-1 expression exceeds basal levels during the resolution phase.•Pharmacologic LYVE-1 blockade exacerbates lung injury in LPS-induced ARDS.•M2 macrophages express LYVE-1 during the ARDS resolution phase.Keywords: LYVE-1, Acute lung injury, ARDS, M2 macrophage

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is characterized by severe hypoxemia due to non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema with excessive alveolar-capillary permeability and poor prognosis [[1], [2], [3]]. ARDS is caused by acute lung injury (ALI), which presents as dysregulated inflammation, alveolar-capillary barrier dysfunction, impaired alveolar fluid clearance, and oxidative stress [4]. To date, no effective pharmacotherapy has been successful in treating ARDS [5,6] and most studies have focused on suppressing inflammation during the acute exudative phase of ARDS [[7], [8], [9]].

Recent studies have shown that efficient pulmonary lymphatic drainage is crucial for resolving inflammation in ARDS [10]; however, the underlying molecular mechanisms remained unexplored.

Lymphatic vessel endothelial hyaluronan receptor 1 (LYVE-1) is a homolog of CD44, the main receptor for hyaluronan (HA) in the lymphatic vessel endothelium, and is a widely used marker for distinguishing blood and lymphatic vessels [11]. During the exudative phase of ARDS, fluid, protein, and HA accumulation in the interstitium impairs alveolar ventilation. In the long term, hyaline membrane formation is implicated in lung fibrosis and irreversible lung dysfunction [12]. LYVE-1 is a pivotal functional protein in the lymphatic duct that plays important roles not only in the clearance of alveolar fluid, including HA, and lymphatic drainage system, but also in leukocyte trafficking system [13]. Furthermore, LYVE-1 expression is not limited to lymphatic vessels, but extends to diverse cell types, including hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells, Kupffer cells in the liver [12], and vascular endothelial cells in the lung [14]. Recently, Kieu et al. reported that the main subset of LYVE-1⁺ cells is M2-type macrophages expressing CD163, an M2 marker, and may play an important role in wound healing and tissue remodeling in dental pulp [15]. Taken together, these results suggest that LYVE-1 plays crucial roles in fluid clearance, lymphangiogenesis, and tissue repair. However, the expression pattern of LYVE-1 in ALI/ARDS has not been thoroughly elucidated. Therefore, we investigated the expression dynamics and physiological roles of LYVE-1 in a mice model of ARDS.

Materials and methods

LPS-induced ARDS model mice

Male C57BL/6NJ mice (8–10 weeks old, weighing 23–28 g) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory Japan, Inc. (Yokohama, Japan). Mice were housed in a controlled environment (22 ± 2°C; 12-h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. The animals were randomly assigned to either the LPS-treated or sham control group. LPS-treated mice were sacrificed 6, 12, 24, or 48 h after intratracheal instillation (n = 5 per time point), whereas sham mice received phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) alone (n = 5).

For lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Escherichia coli O111:B4; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) administration, mice were anesthetized with 1–2% isoflurane and intubated via the trachea using the outer sheath of a 22G indwelling needle (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). LPS was dissolved in 50 µL of PBS and administered intratracheally at a dose of 5 mg/kg using a microsprayer (Model KN-34700-1; Natsume Seisakusho Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Sham animals received an equivalent volume of PBS. At each designated time point, the mice were euthanized and lung tissues were harvested for subsequent biochemical analyses.

Histopathological analysis

Lung tissues were fixed with 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 5 μm-thick sections, which were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) using standard methods. A pathologist blindly scored the lung injury objectively according to the following criteria: alveolar congestion, hemorrhage, neutrophil infiltration into the airspace or vessel wall, and thickness of the alveolar wall/hyaline membrane formation. Each category was graded on a 4-point scale: 0 = no injury, 1 = injury up to 25% of the field, 2 = injury up to 50% of the field, 3 = injury up to 75% of the field, and 4 = diffuse injury. The average value of each parameter was calculated and used as the Lung Injury Score [16,17].

Immunohistochemistry

The lung sections were deparaffinized and LYVE-1 immunostaining was performed. Sections were then washed with PBS for 5 min and heated in Tris-EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (pH 9.0, 95°C, 20 min). Sections were then soaked in PBS containing 3% skim milk for 10 min. LYVE-1 antibody (52811R; Bioss Inc., Woburn, MA, USA) diluted to 5 µg/mL in 3% skim milk in PBS was added and incubated overnight at 4°C. Stained sections were washed with PBS three times for 5 min and soaked in 0.3% hydrogen peroxide-methanol solution for 30 min to remove endogenous peroxidase activity. After washing with PBS, Simple Stain MAX-PO (G) (Nichirei Biosciences Inc.) was added dropwise and allowed to react for 30 min. The cells were then washed with PBS and incubated for 2–10 min with DAB substrate solution (DAKO, Glostrup, Denmark) until an appropriate staining intensity was achieved. After washing with distilled water, counterstaining (Meyer's homemade hematoxylin), dehydration, clearing, and mounting with a water-insoluble mounting medium (Matsunami Glass Ind., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) were performed. Stained sections were visualized using Axio Scan Z1 and Zen (Carl Zeiss AG. Ltd., Thornwood, NY, USA). The left lungs from mice in each time group (sham, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h after intratracheal LPS administration; n = 5 per group) were analyzed. A total of 10 random microscopic fields were selected from each lung. Using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), regions exhibiting staining intensity comparable to that of LYVE-1–positive lymphatic vessels were defined as positive areas. The percentage of LYVE-1–positive area relative to total lung tissue area was calculated for each field, and the mean value from 10 fields was used for statistical analysis.

Immunofluorescence

The harvested mouse lungs were intratracheally administered 1 mL of the O.C.T. compound diluted in physiological saline (1:2) to preserve tissue morphology, followed by embedding in O.C.T. compound and freezing. Frozen tissue was then sectioned at a thickness of 8 µm at -25°C. The sections were then dried and washed with PBS. Subsequently, permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After washing, the tissue sections were blocked for 10 min using Blocking One Histo (Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan). Following this, sections were incubated with anti-LYVE-1 (52811R; Bioss Inc., Woburn, MA, USA) antibody diluted to 5 µg/mL in Blocking One Histo (× 20; Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan) at 4°C overnight. After another washing step, sections were incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L Alexa Fluor® 488 (Abcam plc, Cambridge, UK) secondary antibody diluted to 4 µg/mL in PBS containing 1% BSA for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the tissue sections were washed, mounted using ProLong® Gold Antifade Reagent with DAPI (Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Danvers, MA, USA), and observed under a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X700; Keyence Corporation, Osaka, Japan). For multiplex staining, during the overnight incubation at 4°C with the primary antibodies, Alexa Fluor® 594 anti-Nos2 (iNOS) antibody (BioLegend, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) diluted to 1 µg/mL and CoraLite®594-conjugated arginase-1 monoclonal antibody (Proteintech Group, Inc., Rosemont, IL, USA) diluted to 20 µg/mL were added. Images were acquired using a 20 × objective lens at a field area of approximately 0.16 mm² per high-power field (HPF). One random field was selected from each individual mouse for evaluation, with a total of six mice analyzed (n = 6). The numbers of iNOS-positive cells and ARG-1-positive cells were quantified, and the percentages of double-positive cells for iNOS and LYVE-1, as well as ARG-1 and LYVE-1, were assessed. Double-positive cell rates were evaluated in the 6-hour group for iNOS and in the 24-hour group for ARG-1, as positive cells predominantly appeared at those respective time points.

Western blot analysis

Lung tissues and cultured macrophages were dissected or collected, and homogenized in Pierce™ RIPA Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing cOmplete™ EDTA-free protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich Corporation, St. Louis, MO, USA). The lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C and supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using the Protein Quantification Kit-Rapid (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). Equal amounts of proteins (30 µg) were separated on SDS-PAGE (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan) and transferred to a membrane using the Trans-Blot Turbo Transfer System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA). The membranes were then blocked with Every blocking buffer (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) and immunoblotted with the following primary antibodies at 4°C overnight: mouse anti-GAPDH antibody (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Inc., Danvers, MA, USA); mouse anti-LYVE-1 antibody (1:1000; Bioss Inc., Woburn, MA, USA); mouse anti-VEGF-C, HRP Conjugated antibody (1:1000; Bioss Inc., Woburn, MA, USA). Membranes were then stained with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated rabbit IgG (1:5000; VECTOR Laboratories, Inc., Burlingame, CA, USA) and chemiluminescence was visualized using the ECL advance kit (GE Healthcare Biosciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA). The images were digitized and the intensity of each band was quantified using densitometric scanning with the ImageJ software.

Hyaluronan quantification

Lung tissues were homogenized and 2 mL of digestion buffer was added per 100 mg tissue. The digestion buffer was composed of 200 μg/mL proteinase K (Kanto Chemical Co., Inc., Tokyo, Japan), 0.1 M NaCl, 0.01 M Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 10 mM EDTA, and 0.5% SDS. The samples were incubated at 55 °C for 12 h. To remove nucleic acids, DNase (100 μg/mL) and RNase (100 μg/mL) were added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Following enzymatic digestion, 500 μL of each sample was mixed with an equal volume of phenol-chloroform, vortexed, and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. From the aqueous phase, 300 μL was carefully collected and mixed with 600 μL of pre-chilled ethanol, followed by overnight incubation at -20 °C. The samples were then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to precipitate hyaluronan. The resulting pellets were resuspended in 200 μL of distilled water, and 100 μL aliquots were mixed with 1 mL Alcian blue solution (pH 2.5; Nacalai Tesque, Inc., Kyoto, Japan). The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. Each pellet was gently washed with 1 mL of distilled water to avoid dislodging and the wash solution was removed. The final pellets were dissolved in 500 μL of 50% sulfuric acid, and 200 μL of each sample was transferred to a 96-well plate. The absorbance was measured at 600 nm using a microplate reader.

For standard curve preparation, sodium hyaluronate (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan) was used to prepare standard solutions at concentrations of 2500, 1000, 500, 250, and 0 μg/mL (100 μL each). Standards were processed similar to the samples using Alcian blue solution to generate a calibration curve.

LYVE-1 neutralization

To evaluate the role of LYVE-1 during the resolution phase following intratracheal LPS administration, LPS (5 mg/kg) dissolved in 50 µL of PBS was administered intratracheally to induce lung injury. 12 hours after LPS administration, 10 µg of the neutralizing anti-LYVE-1 antibody (Clone: 64R; Cosmo Bio Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) dissolved in 20 µL PBS was injected via the tail vein. The control group received PBS (20 µL) alone. Lung tissues were collected 24 hours after LPS stimulation and subjected to HE staining and hyaluronan quantification. The survival rate in each group was compared.

Cell culture/M1/M2 macrophage polarization

The murine macrophage cell line, RAW264.7 was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Osaka, Japan) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 10% penicillin/streptomycin (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan). To generate M1 macrophages, RAW264.7 cells were incubated in DMEM with LPS (50 ng/mL) for 3 days. To generate M2 macrophages, RAW264.7 cells were incubated in DMEM with MCSF (100 ng/mL; BioLegend, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), dexamethasone (5 × 10−7 M; Fujifilm Wako Chemicals Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan), and IL-4 (10 ng/mL; PeproTech, Inc., Cranbury, NJ, USA) for 3 days. Five randomly selected microscopic fields were analyzed (0.03 mm² per field). The cultured cells were also used for Western blotting analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Differences among groups were evaluated using the Mann-Whitney U test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Results

LYVE-1 expression is decreased at 6 h after LPS-induced ARDS and enhanced at 12 h during the repair process

Lung tissues collected at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h after intratracheal LPS (5 mg/kg) administration were subjected to HE staining. Inflammatory changes were most prominent in the 6-hour group and showed a time-dependent improvement (Fig. 1A). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that multiple cell types, including the alveolar epithelial cells, vascular endothelium, and macrophages, were positive for LYVE-1. Visual examination further demonstrated a reduction in LYVE-1 expression in the lungs at 6 h post-LPS administration (Fig. 1B). While lymphatic vessel structures were predominantly stained, staining was also observed in a subset of mononuclear cells and alveolar epithelial cells. Additionally, Lung Injury Score revealed that the 6-hour group had the highest degree of lung injury compared to the sham group. Over time, the lung injury improved at 48 h post-LPS administration and was comparable to the sham group (Fig. 1C). Image analysis of LYVE-1–positive areas in the lung tissue revealed a significant reduction in the 6-hour group compared with the sham group, followed by a marked increase in the 12-hour group. Thereafter, LYVE-1 expression gradually returned toward baseline levels, reaching a steady-state level by 48 hours (Fig. 1D). Similarly, western blot analysis demonstrated that LYVE-1 expression was significantly decreased in the 6-hour group, markedly increased in the 12-hour group, and then progressively returned to baseline by 48 hours (Fig. 1E). Tissue hyaluronan levels significantly increased at 6 h after lung injury and then decreased at 12, 24, and 48 h (Fig. 1F).

LYVE-1 neutralization worsened lung injury and survival rate

Lung specimens from mice treated with LPS followed by LYVE-1 neutralizing antibodies revealed that inflammatory changes were more severe in the group treated with anti-LYVE-1 antibodies than in LPS alone and sham groups (Fig. 2A). Additionally, the anti-LYVE-1 antibody treatment group had significantly higher lung injury scores (Fig. 2B) and HA levels (Fig. 2C). The survival rates in the anti-LYVE-1 and LPS treatment groups were 50% and 100%, respectively (Fig. 2D).

M2 macrophages predominantly appeared at 24 and 48 h after LPS administration and expressed LYVE-1

Fluorescence immunostaining of the lung tissues and distributional changes in M1 and M2 macrophages at various time points following intratracheal LPS administration, including sham, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h groups are shown in Fig. 3A and B. LYVE-1 (green), iNOS (red), and DAPI (blue) staining is shown in Fig. 3A. A few iNOS-positive M1 macrophages were observed in the 6- and 12-hour groups (Fig. 3A). In the sections stained for LYVE-1 (green), ARG-1 (red), and DAPI (blue), ARG-1 positive M2 macrophages were detected in the sham, 24-hour, and 48-hour groups. Additionally, M2 macrophages were confirmed to be double-positive for LYVE-1 (Fig. 3B). The number of iNOS-positive macrophages per high-power field (HPF) was counted (n = 6), revealing a significant increase in iNOS-positive M1 macrophages in the 6- and 12-hour groups compared to the sham group (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the number of ARG-1-positive macrophages per HPF was significantly reduced in the 6- and 12-hour groups; however, the number increased above baseline levels in the 24- and 48-hour groups (Fig. 3D). The number of LYVE-1 expressing cells was assessed in each field of view in the 6-hour group, where iNOS-positive macrophages were most abundant, and in the 24-hour group, where ARG-1-positive macrophages were most prevalent (n = 6). Results indicated that ARG-1-positive M2 macrophages co-expressed LYVE-1 (Fig. 3E).

In vitro cultured M2 macrophages express LYVE-1 and VEGF-C

Our in vitro findings on M1 and M2 macrophages were consistent with those of the in vivo experiments. Fluorescence immunostaining demonstrated that unstimulated RAW264.7 cells showed no detectable expression of LYVE-1, iNOS, or ARG-1 (Fig. 4A). After polarization, LYVE-1 expression was observed predominantly in ARG-1-positive M2 macrophages, whereas M1 macrophages remained negative for LYVE-1 (Fig. 4B, C). Analysis of five randomly selected microscopic fields (field area: 0.03 mm² per field) demonstrated that nearly all M2 macrophages were positive for LYVE-1, whereas M1 macrophages were predominantly negative. Furthermore, western blotting results demonstrated significantly higher expression of LYVE-1 (p < 0.0001) and VEGF-C (p < 0.0001) in M2 macrophages than M1 macrophages (Fig. 4C, D).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate LYVE-1 expression in lungs of LPS-induced pulmonary injury mice model. LYVE-1 expression was significantly reduced at 6 h after LPS administration and was restored at 12, 24, and 48 h. Interestingly, LYVE-1 expression initially decreased, followed by a rebound increase, and subsequently returned to a steady-state level comparable to baseline. This suggests that LYVE-1 expression dynamics is an active response to injury. Consistent with our findings, Zheng et al. reported that LYVE-1 mRNA level increases in mice lungs 4 and 8 h post-LPS injection, whereas LYVE-1 protein levels are increased 48 h after the treatment [14]. Conversely, Zhang et al. reported that LPS stimulation decreases LYVE-1 expression in the lungs [18]. However, the expression dynamics of LYVE-1 protein following LPS administration is not yet been studied. Therefore, we performed a detailed analysis of LYVE-1 expression over time in ALI/ARDS mice model. Our results confirmed that LYVE-1 is expressed not only in lymphatic vessels but also in a subset of macrophages and the vascular endothelium of the lung. Moreover, our study showed that direct inhibition of LYVE-1 using a neutralizing antibody aggravated LPS-induced lung injury and decreased the survival rate in mice. This suggests that LYVE-1 induction after injury is necessary for the healing process in the lungs. It is reported that HA is a major component of the extracellular matrix and is involved in several inflammatory processes [19]. Tissue injury triggers the degradation of high-molecular weight HA (HMW-HA) into low-molecular weight HA (LMW-HA) fragments, which act as potent damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). These HA fragments engage pattern recognition receptors, such as Toll-like receptors 2 and 4 (TLR2/TLR4) and CD44, leading to the activation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways [20]. Subsequent downstream signaling promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, CXCL1, and CXCL2, thereby orchestrating neutrophil recruitment from the circulation to the injured tissue [21]. Infiltrating neutrophils release reactive oxygen species (ROS) and proteolytic enzymes such as elastase, which further degrade HA and perpetuate a self-amplifying inflammatory cascade [22]. Since LYVE-1 is responsible for the uptake of HA from the tissue matrix [23,24], our study suggests that increased LYVE-1 expression may control inflammation via enhanced HA uptake. Consistent with the overall findings, tissue HA levels in this study also increased during the inflammatory phase, followed by a time-dependent decrease. Additionally, LYVE-1 blockade exacerbated the lung injury accompanied by HA accumulation. Besides its role in HA metabolism, efficient lymphatic drainage is crucial for the clearance of apoptotic and necrotic inflammatory cells. Thus, lymphatic architecture and LYVE-1 expression during the inflammatory phase are considered pivotal determinants for the timely resolution of inflammation [25].

Furthermore, studies have reported that LYVE-1 expressing macrophages maintain arterial tone through HA-mediated regulation of smooth muscle cell collagen, and the loss of arterial resident LYVE-1 expressing macrophages exacerbates atherosclerosis [26,27]. We confirmed that LYVE-1 expression in the vascular endothelium and macrophages decreases during the inflammatory phase and enhanced during the repair process. The appearance of LYVE-1-positive macrophages in the lungs during the inflammatory resolution phase, as observed in the present study, has not been previously reported. These macrophages may contribute to the maintenance of lung structure in cooperation with LYVE-1-positive vascular endothelial cells. Although not in inflammatory settings, previous studies have reported the presence of LYVE-1-positive M2-like tumor-associated macrophages, which contribute to tumor progression by interacting with lymphatic vessels [20,28,29]. Conversion from M1 to M2 phenotype is beneficial and important for recovery in humans with ARDS as well as in experimental ARDS models [30,31]. Recent studies suggest that LYVE-1, associated with M2 macrophages, plays a role in facilitating drainage and immune cell trafficking during the inflammatory resolution phase [24,32]. We confirmed that M2 macrophages express VEGF-C, an upstream regulator of LYVE-1, suggesting that M2 macrophages may contribute to tissue repair and lymphangiogenesis by promoting LYVE-1 expression [33]. In any case, it has now become clear that LYVE-1 expression is not exclusively restricted to lymphatic endothelial cells, as traditionally assumed [14,34].

Future studies should focus on the physiological roles of LYVE-1, a protein which may potentially serve as a target for the pharmacological treatment of lung injury. LYVE-1, which is expressed in the vascular endothelium and M2 macrophages, has been suggested to play a pivotal role during the ARDS resolution phase, not only through regulating HA drainage but also via other mechanisms [25,26,32]. In any case, the identification of factors that regulate LYVE-1 expression, including those that increase or decrease its levels, remains incomplete. Further elucidation of the molecular cascades and regulatory pathways involved represents an important subject for future investigation.
