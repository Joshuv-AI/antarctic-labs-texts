Antimicrobial resistance profiling of Salmonella and Escherichia coli isolates from conventional poultry farms in Hossana Town, Central Ethiopia
Authors: Habtamu Endale Tilahun, Debele Abdeta Efa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is an increasing One Health threat posing serious challenges to both human and animal health and the health of the environment they share. The highly growing livestock production with high rate of antimicrobial use and misuse is very important source of the emergence and spread of AMR mainly in association with foodborne zoonotic pathogens, such as Salmonella and Escherichia coli O157:H7. A cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2023 to May 2024 in conventional poultry farms in Hossana Town, Central Ethiopia, to assess the AMR profiles of Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 using the disc diffusion method. A total of 27 Salmonella and 20 E. coli O157:H7 isolates were recovered from 228 samples (200 cloacal swabs and 28 postmortem samples). All Salmonella isolates exhibited resistance to ampicillin, with high levels of resistance against sulfamethoxazole (85.1%), cefoxitin (85.1%), and tetracycline (77.8%). Similarly, E. coli O157:H7 isolates demonstrated complete resistance to ampicillin and high (90%) resistance to cefotaxime and tetracycline. Both pathogens showed significant sensitivity to gentamicin and azithromycin. Multidrug resistance (MDR) was identified in all Salmonella isolates and in 80% of the E. coli O157:H7 isolates. This finding reveals the presence of high level of AMR in Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 isolates from poultry farms and that the poultry production in the study has undeniable contribution for the emergence and spread of AMR. This investigation underscore the importance of further investigations into the factors contributing to resistance, as well as the development of integrated AMR surveillance systems. Molecular investigations are recommended to confirm the mechanisms of resistance and to inform policy decisions and implement evidence based interventions.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR), defined as the ineffectiveness of conventional antimicrobials in treating infections, poses a significant threat to modern medicine, public health, and the achievement of sustainable development goals [1]. It is a growing global threat that undermines the effectiveness of modern medicine and contributes significantly to high morbidity and mortality rate. In 2019, an estimated 1.27 million deaths were directly attributable to resistant bacterial infections, highlighting the urgent need for coordinated interventions [2]. Although AMR arises naturally, the misuse and overuse of antimicrobials in both humans and animals has accelerated its emergence and spread [3]. AMR diminishes the effectiveness of antimicrobial treatments and often results in higher costs, increased incidence, and greater severity of infections. Scientific evidence currently shows that the indiscriminate use of antimicrobials in veterinary medicine has contributed to the development of resistant bacteria that infect humans, especially strains of Enterococcus spp., Campylobacter spp., Salmonella spp., and E. coli. From the One Health perspective, resistance to cephalosporins exemplifies the significant impact antibiotics have on both animal and human health. A major concern regarding the use of antimicrobials in animals, whether for treatment or prevention, is the potential spread of AMR [4].

The rising prevalence of AMR in both human and veterinary medicine raises serious concerns about the irresponsible use of these medications. In low- and middle-income countries, including Ethiopia, antimicrobials are often accessible without prescription and used indiscriminately in poultry farms to manage infections, improve productivity, and compensate for poor husbandry conditions [5, 6]. The utilization of antimicrobials in animal production is widespread globally, driven by an unprecedented increase in the consumption of animal proteins. It is reported that approximately 8,164,662 kg of antimicrobials are used worldwide each year, with 70% of this usage being non-therapeutic [7]. In Africa, around 3,558 tons of antimicrobials were employed in animal production in 2020 [8]. In many countries, antimicrobials are also used for non-therapeutic purposes, such as enhancing feed efficiency and promoting growth in livestock [9, 10]. Currently, a significant proportion (about 80%) of animals and birds involved in food production receive medication for either part or the majority of their lives [11, 12].

According to [13–16] livestock producers in various regions of Ethiopia use antimicrobials inappropriately and irresponsibly, both therapeutically and non-therapeutically. In different scales of poultry production, including conventional farms [17], antimicrobials are widely employed for prevention (prophylaxis) and treatment of infections [18, 19]; for growth promotion [19–21]; in some areas to enhance egg production in layers [9, 22, 23]; and to compensate for the inadequate hygienic conditions [24, 25]. This misuse contributes to the emergence of AMR in both harmless and pathogenic bacteria (e.g., E. coli and Salmonella), which in turn contributes to global AMR in humans and animals. The detection of antimicrobial resistance in Enterobacteriaceae, such as E. coli and Salmonella, highlights the occurrence of drug-resistant strains in poultry farm environments and suggests their potential role as indicator organisms for monitoring AMR [26]. Antimicrobial-resistant Salmonella and E. coli in poultry can render chemotherapy for associated diseases ineffective, resulting in significant financial losses in the sector. Furthermore, these resistant strains can cause infections in humans that do not respond to antimicrobial treatment, leading to increased morbidity and mortality, as they are zoonotic [27, 28].

Drug-resistant and multi-drug-resistant pathogens originating from poultry can pose significant health risks to humans through the food chain. This can occur by consuming meat or eggs contaminated with resistant pathogens or genes [29, 30]; as well as through direct contact with diseased or healthy poultry that shed these pathogens and their environments [31, 32]. The β-lactamase (ESBL) genes found in Enterobacteriaceae isolated from human blood cultures are similar to those identified in human rectal swab samples and most of those isolated from retail raw chicken meat. This suggests that AMR developed in animal farming has a critical impact on public health [33, 34]. Additionally, resistant bacteria can reach humans through untreated poultry excreta used as manure in gardens, particularly via fresh vegetables [35, 36].

E. coli O157, and Salmonella species (including S. Typhi, S. Typhimurium, and S. Paratyphi) are the most prevalent foodborne zoonotic pathogens associated with animal products [37, 38]. AMR in bacteria from the Enterobacteriaceae family, such as E. coli and Salmonella, indicates the presence of drug-resistant strains within the community [26]. Studies conducted in various regions of Ethiopia [7, 39–43] have shown that poultry production is a significant hotspot for antimicrobial-resistant E. coli and Salmonella. These studies reported that both E. coli and Salmonella exhibited resistance to various antimicrobials commonly used in human and animal health care. Poultry production in Ethiopia has been steadily growing in recent years, with both large-scale and small-scale poultry farms contributing to the overall production. The emergence and spread of AMR in poultry production centers is a growing global concern that poses a significant threat to poultry health and production and public health owing to the failure of chemotherapy. Despite this, there is no published research data showing the AMR profile of Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 in conventional poultry farms in Hossana town and surroundings, central Ethiopia. Thus, current study was aimed in investigating the AMR profiles of Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 isolates in conventional poultry farms in Hossana Town, Central Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Description of study area

The investigation was conducted in Hossana Town, Central Ethiopia, from October 2023 to May 2024. Hossana Town, the regional administrative center of Central Ethiopia, comprises three sub-cities and eight kebeles (the smallest governmental administrative units). It is located 232 km northwest of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. Geographically, the town is situated at a latitude of 7° 22’ N and a longitude of 37° 28’ E. Topographically, its altitude ranges from 1,560 m to 2,688 m above mean sea level (m.s.l.). The mean annual temperature and precipitation are 21 °C and 750.9 mm, respectively [44] (Fig. 1).

Study design

A cross-sectional study utilizing both simple random and purposive sampling was conducted to isolate, identify, and characterize the phenotypic resistance patterns of Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 from conventional poultry farms between October 2023 and May 2024. The number of samples allocated to each farm was proportional to the stocking size. Cloacal swabs were collected from both clinically sick and apparently healthy chickens during visits to each farm, ensuring that a single farm was not revisited within a short period. To distinguish sampled chickens from non-sampled ones, a double marking was applied to their legs and back plumes using a permanent marker. Additionally, postmortem samples from freshly deceased chickens were collected through ongoing communication with farm owners or managers during farm visits.

Study animals

For the current investigation, chickens raised in both conventional private and governmental poultry farms located in Hossana town, Central Ethiopia, were included. The study encompassed apparently healthy, clinically sick, and freshly dead layers and broilers of both sexes and two age groups, including young (≤ 6 months) and adult (> 6 months) [45].

Sampling technique and sample transportation

A total of 228 samples were collected for the current investigation, comprising 200 cloacal swab samples from live chickens and 28 postmortem samples from freshly dead chickens. Of the 200 cloacal swabs, 130 samples were obtained from apparently healthy chickens, while 70 were taken from clinically sick chickens. The postmortem samples included a total of 28 organ samples, consisting of 7 livers, 7 lungs, 7 ovaries, and 7 kidneys. During the sampling process, apparently healthy chickens were randomly selected from each farm, whereas clinically sick chickens those exhibiting clinical signs such as diarrhea, matted feathers around the vent, ruffled feathers, swollen navels, and reduced appetite were purposefully sampled, along with freshly deceased chickens. For the cloacal swabs, the wings of the chickens were held with one hand while their heads were positioned downward to expose their caudal regions. A sterile cotton swab, moistened with buffered peptone water, was then inserted into the cloaca and rotated several times.

The swabs were immediately transferred to test tubes containing 10 ml of buffered peptone water (BPW) (HiMedia™, M1494I Pvt. Ltd., India) and stored in an icebox after appropriately labeling all required information, as outlined by [46]. For the postmortem samples, freshly deceased chickens exhibiting the aforementioned symptoms were collected from the participating farms and promptly transported to the Wachemo University Veterinary Laboratory for necropsy. The necropsy was conducted following the procedures established by [47], and visceral organs, including the kidneys, liver, and ovaries, were aseptically sampled in sterile universal plastic bottles containing BPW. The samples were then transported in an icebox to the Wachemo University Medical Microbiology Laboratory, where bacteriological procedures were performed on the same day.

Those farms volunteered to provide informed consent and were willing to cooperate fully with the study protocols, which included granting access to farm records and permitting sample collection. Farms those raise chicken regularly were included to prevent discontinuation of sampling and employed an intensive management system. Farms whose owners were unwilling to cooperate with the complete study protocol and did not permit access to the farm at the designated times were excluded. Additionally, farms practicing extensive and semi-intensive management systems, as well as small-scale and backyard farms, were not included in the study.

Bacteriological methods

Bacterial isolation and identification and isolate preservation

Isolation and identification of E. coli O157:H7

In the current investigation, E. coli O157:H7 was isolated and identified using standard bacteriological and biochemical tests. The buffered peptone water (BPW) with cloacal swabs was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, a bacterial suspension was streaked onto MacConkey Sorbitol Agar (HiCrome™, M1340, Pvt. Ltd., India), which serves as a selective and differential medium for E. coli O157:H7, and incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. The following day, colorless colonies were sub-cultured onto MacConkey agar (HiMedia™, M081, Pvt. Ltd., India) and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h to obtain pure colonies, which were then stored on a nutrient agar slant at 4 °C. The isolates were further characterized through biochemical tests, including Triple Sugar Iron (TSI) agar (Huankai Microbial, HCM 014, Sci. & Tech. Co., Ltd., China), indole production (HiMedia™, R008, Pvt. Ltd., India), and the sulfide indole motility (SIM) test (HiMedia™, M180, Pvt. Ltd., India), as described by [48]. Each organ sample was macerated using a pestle and mortar, mixed thoroughly with 10 ml of BPW, and incubated at 37 °C overnight. A loopful of the culture suspension was then streaked onto MacConkey Sorbitol Agar and incubated aerobically for 24 h at 37 °C. The same procedures used for the cloacal swabs were followed, as indicated by [49].

Isolation and identification of Salmonella

The cloacal swabs in the BPW were incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h to promote bacterial recovery. Then, 1 ml of the pre-enriched sample was introduced to Rappaport-Vassiliadis (RV) broth (HiMedia™, M1467 Pvt. Ltd., India) and incubated at 42 °C for 24 h to afford Salmonella selective enrichment over other competitive microbes. A loopful of inoculant from RV was grown on the Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar (Biomrk, B819, Pvt. Ltd., India)/Salmonella-Shigella (SS) agar (Biomark, B361, Pvt. Ltd., India) and incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h and the plates were observed for red (XLD agar) and colorless (in SS agar) colonies with blackened centers typical features of Salmonella [50]. Suspicious Salmonella colonies from XLD/SS were sub-cultured on the MacConkey. Afterward, colorless colonies on the MacConkey were aseptically picked and stored on a nutrient agar slant for further biochemical characterization [51]. Then, these isolates were screened by biochemical tests including triple sugar iron, indole production test and Simon’s Citrate test and SIM test (Merck Millipore, S 8539, Sigma Aldrich, Germany). Organ samples were crushed by gentle maceration by pestle and mortar and one gram of minced tissues was mixed distinctly with 10 ml BPW and incubated at 37 °C for 18–24 h to promote bacterial growth. After that, the same procedures adopted for cloacal swabs in this section were performed [50]. Once the bacteria were isolated, the isolates from confirmed colonies were inoculated in the 1.5 ml volume nutrient broth (NB) and incubated for six hours at 37 °C. Then, 0.5 ml of NB containing colony suspension was transferred into the sterile serum screw vials. Next, 0.5 ml of 40% glycerin (HiMedia™, M1494I Pvt. Ltd., India), already prepared in distilled water by autoclaving was added by pasture pipette making the volume 1 ml. After that, preserved at −70 °C temperature until serotyping and an antibiogram evaluation was performed [52].

Serological test

The isolated Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 strains were serotyped at the Wachemo University Microbiology Laboratory based on O (somatic) and H (flagellar) antigens, following the protocol established by White-Kauffmann-LeMinor and the antigenic formula designated by [53] as previously adopted by [54, 55]. Additionally, the user guidelines provided by the kit manufacturers were adhered to. The antisera utilized in this study included S. Typhimurium (1,4 [5],,12:i:1,2), S. Gallinarum, and S. Pullorum (O1,9,12), S. Enteritidis (1,9,12:g, m), S. Dublin (1,9,12[Vi]: g,p) (SSI Diagnostica A/S, Herredsvejen 2, 3400 Hillerod, Denmark), and E. coli (157:7) (BD Difco™, Fisher Scientific, BD 221545, Gothenburg, Sweden). The Salmonella serovars were designated according to their agglutination reactions with the antisera used; those that were positive for the Salmonella polyvalent antiserum but not reactive with all available antisera were interpreted as other serovars. Since S. Gallinarum and S. Pullorum share the same genetic structure, biochemical tests, including dulcitol fermentation and ornithine decarboxylation, were employed to differentiate between them. Isolates that fermented dulcitol but did not decarboxylate ornithine were identified as S. Gallinarum, while those that did not ferment dulcitol but did decarboxylate ornithine were classified as S. Pullorum, as adopted by [56, 57]. For E. coli, the target isolate was the O157:H7 strain, which was confirmed serologically by slide agglutination, as previously implemented by [58] using monovalent O157 and H7 antisera. Isolates that exhibited agglutination in both the O157 and H7 monovalent antisera were confirmed as E. coli O157:H7, based on the kit manufacturers’ guidelines.

Antibiogram of the isolated bacteria

Phenotypically confirmed isolates of both E. coli O157:H7 and Salmonella isolates were subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing using the disc diffusion method (Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion). A bacterial suspension was prepared by transferring 2–4 colonies grown overnight on nutrient agar to a glass tube containing 5 ml of sterile normal saline using a sterile inoculating loop. The suspension was vortexed and visually matched to a 0.5 McFarland standard for turbidity [59]. Subsequently, the isolates in the suspension were spread onto Mueller-Hinton agar using a sterile cotton swab, ensuring the entire surface of the medium was covered to promote uniform growth. After allowing the agar to dry for 30 min at room temperature and checking for excess moisture, discs impregnated with specific concentrations of antimicrobials were placed on the bacterial lawn and incubated at 37 °C for 18 to 24 h. The antimicrobial agents were selected based on their widespread use in veterinary and human medicine in Ethiopia, their categorization as critically or highly important antimicrobials by WHO/WOAH, and the availability of standardized discs recommended by CLSI for susceptibility testing.

For this investigation, 16 antimicrobial agents (Table 1) commonly used in both veterinary and human healthcare were tested. These includes, penicillins (ampicillin (10 µg), amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (30 µg), sulbactam/Ampicillin (30 µg)); cephalosporins (cefotaxime (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), cefoxitin (30 µg)); phenicols (chloramphenicol (30 µg)); carbapenems (meropenem (10 µg)); fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin (10 µg)), folate pathway inhibitors (sulfamethoxazole (30 µg), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (10 µg), trimethoprim (5 µg)); aminoglycosides (streptomycin (10 µg), gentamicin (10 µg)); tetracyclines (tetracycline (30 µg), oxytetracycline (30 µg)) and macrolide (erythromycin (15 µg)). Afterward, the bacteria were interpreted as sensitive, intermediate, or resistant against applied antimicrobials by measuring the inhibition zones in millimeters using a ruler by adopting the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute’s cut points [59]. This antibiogram study was conducted in the Wachemo University medical microbiology laboratory. Those resistant to at least one drug of three or more antimicrobial classes were classified as MDR [60].

Ethical approval and consent of participation

Ethical approval for the current study was obtained from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Addis Ababa University, College of Veterinary Medicine and Agriculture (Reference number VM/ERC/02/10/162024). Additionally informed consent of participation has been obtained from the farm owners, ensuring that their rights were respected by clearly explaining the purpose of the study and the potential impact on their animals in understandable language. For illiterate farm owners, the informed consent of participation was obtained from their parents or representatives. During the sampling of live chickens, all stringent hygiene, safety, and welfare measures were implemented.

Data management and statistical analysis

Data collected from the laboratory investigation was entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet and analyzed using STATA version 14.0 for Windows (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA). The frequencies of resistance, intermediate susceptibility, and susceptibility of isolates to the tested antimicrobials were computed. Before data analysis, a normality test was conducted graphically using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Subsequently, the rate of antimicrobial resistance of the isolates against each drug was determined by computing a frequency table. The multiple antimicrobial resistance (MAR) index was identified and interpreted using the formula developed by Krumperman (1983), expressed as a/b, where ‘a’ represents the number of antimicrobials to which the test isolates exhibited resistance, and ‘b’ denotes the total number of antimicrobials tested. Isolates with a MAR index ≥ 0.2 were considered to originate from environments where many antimicrobials are frequently used, indicating a high risk of contamination [61]. The antimicrobial resistance (R) score was calculated using the model referenced by [62]. A score of 0, 0.5, and 1 was assigned to each isolate showing susceptibility, intermediate susceptibility, and resistance, respectively, to specific antimicrobial agents. The R score was defined by calculating the ratio of the sum of scores to the total number of antimicrobial agents tested; isolates with R scores of 0 and 1 were designated as pan drug-susceptible and pan drug-resistant, respectively. Concurrent resistance to different antimicrobial agents was assessed using the Spearman pairwise correlation test. Differences in MAR and R scores based on chicken type and bacterial genus were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Additionally, Spearman rank correlation was employed to identify parallel resistance to the antimicrobials.

Results

Bacterial isolation and identification

In this investigation, from a total of 228; 200 cloacal swabs and 28 organ samples, 47 (20.61%) were positive for both Salmonella 27 (11.84%) and E. coli O157:H7 20 (8.77%). Among the 20 E. coli O157:H7 isolates, 15 (75.00%) were from cloacal swabs and 5 (25.00%) were from postmortem samples. Of the 27 Salmonella isolates, 19 (70.37%) were from cloacal swabs and 8 (29.63%) were from postmortem samples. Among 15 E. coli O157:H7 and 19 Salmonella cloacal swab isolates, 10 (66.7%) and 15 (78.95%) were isolated from healthy-looking chicken and 5 (33.3%) and 4 (21.05%) from diseased chicken, respectively.

Serological test

A total of 29 bacterial isolates were identified as Salmonella through bacteriological and biochemical tests. From this, 27 isolates were confirmed as Salmonella and serotyped to different serovars by serological slide agglutination test. Up on serological test, Salmonella serogroup D serovars S. Enteritidis, S. Gallinarum, S. Pullorum, and S. Dublin and serogroup B S. Typhimurium and unknown serotype (other) with respective frequencies of 9(33.3%), 5(18.5%), 3(11.1%), 1(3.7%) and 7(25.9%) and 2(10.5%) were identified. Among the Salmonella serovars, S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium most frequent. Regarding E. coli, interestingly all the isolates (20) identified in bacterial culture and biochemical tests were serologically confirmed as E. coli O157:H7.

Antibiogram profile of Salmonella isolates

In the current investigation, all 27 Salmonella isolates exhibited resistance to ≥ 5 antimicrobial agents tested at different rates. The highest rate of resistance was recorded against ampicillin 27 (100%) followed by sulfamethoxazole and cefoxitin 23(85.1%), tetracycline 21(77.8%), trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole 20(74.1%), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid 18(66.6%), ceftriaxone and streptomycin 15(55.6%), ciprofloxacin 14(51.9%), ampicillin/sulbactam 13(48.1%), trimethoprim 12(44.4%) and cefotaxime 11(40.7%). Higher susceptibility was recorded to the remaining antimicrobial agents meropenem 22(81.48%), gentamicin 18(66.67), chloramphenicol 25(92.59%), and azithromycin 22(81.48%). Regarding the class of antimicrobials tested, higher resistance level was recorded in penicillin groups (100% in AMP) followed by folate synthesis inhibitors (85.19% to sulfamethoxazole) and cephalosporins (74.07% to cefoxitin). Conversely, good invitro antimicrobial activity was documented in antimicrobial classes including carbapenem (81.48% to meropenem), macrolides (81.48% to azitromycin), aminoglycosides (66.67% to gentamicin), phenicol (66.67% to chloramphenicol) and quinolones (48.15% to ciprofloxacin) (Table 2).

Species-level antimicrobial resistance of Salmonella isolates

Out of 9 S. Enteritidis isolates, 9 (100%) exhibited resistance against ampicillin, cefoxitin and sulfamethoxazole. From tested isolates, 7(77.8%) were resistant to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, 3(44.4%) were resistant against ampicillin/sulbactam 6(66.7%) were resistant to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and streptomycin, 5(55.6%) have shown non-susceptibility to ceftriaxone, tetracycline and ciprofloxacin. The majority 7(77.8%), 6(66.7%) and 5(55.6%) of the S. Enteritidis were susceptible to azithromycin, meropenem and chloramphenicol and gentamicin, respectively. Of the 5 S. Discussion

Today, the emergence of mono-drug and multi-drug resistant bacteria in poultry production poses a significant threat to poultry productivity and public health worldwide [63]. This issue is exacerbated by the fact that poultry is one of the fastest-growing sources of meat globally, with an international annual growth rate of 5% [64]. Determining the antimicrobial resistance patterns of Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 in poultry farms is crucial for implementing effective prevention strategies to mitigate the impact of antimicrobial-resistant Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 on poultry health, production, and environmental and public health [65]. In the current investigation, out of a total of 228 samples comprising 200 cloacal swabs and 28 organ samples 47 (20.61%) were found to be positive for both Salmonella 11.84% (27/228) and E. coli 8.77% (20/228).

The prevalence of Salmonella was also lower than the report of [41] 19.0% (39/205) from East Shoa Ethiopia [66], 24.3%, (131/539) in Central Ethiopia [55], 39% (78/200) Mansoura city, Egypt and [67] 31.25% (35/112) from Saudi Arabia and. It was higher than the previous report by [68] 2.65% from Jimma Town, South Western Ethiopia [69], 2.65% from Debre Zeit and Modjo, Ethiopia and [70] (9.27%) from Kafa zone, Southwest Ethiopia. The prevalence of E. coli O157:H7 in current study was higher than the report of [71] 6.5% in Dessie and Kombolcha towns, Ethiopia. From these E. coli O157:H7 isolates, 75% (15/20) were from cloacal swabs and 25% (5/20) were from postmortem samples. Of the 27 (11.84%) Salmonella isolates, 70.37% (19/27) were from cloacal swabs and 29.63% (8/27) were from postmortem samples. From 15 E. coli O157:H7 and 19 Salmonella cloacal swab isolates, 10 (66.7%) and 15 (78.95%) were isolated from healthy-looking chicken and 5 (33.3%) and 4 (21.05%) were from diseased chicken, respectively. Relatively higher frequency of these bacteria in healthy-looking chickens than from diseased further exacerbate the effect of the AMR on the poultry health and production, environmental and public health [72, 73]. This is due to that this apparently healthy chicken harboring drug-resistant Salmonella were left unchecked and not treated with alternative antimicrobials or not isolated from the production. In addition, as people contact with chickens while husbandry and use their products directly and indirectly without caution as the chickens are considered healthy, they contract this drug-resistant bacteria from the chickens.

The overall AMR rate of the present study was similar to the finding of [74] from Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, China and of [70] from Kafa zone, Southwest Ethiopia who stated all the Salmonella isolates were resistant to at least one drug but higher than the report of [75] who reported 73.8% overall resistance from Mumbai city, India. In another study [68], from Jimma Town, South Western Ethiopia reported the antimicrobial resistance rate of Salmonella as 90.09%, which is lower than the current finding. This variation in the antimicrobial resistance rate of Salmonella reported from different study sites might be attributed to the difference in sample type, poultry producers’ practice of antimicrobial use (irrational vs. rational use), and farm management (as good and hygienic management reduces infection and subsequent antimicrobial use). It may also be attributed to the poultry production system whether it was mostly antimicrobial dependent or more organic production and the type of antimicrobial agents commonly used for the chicken therapeutically or non-therapeutically in each study site.

In the current investigation, highest resistance rate was recorded against penicillins (ampicillin) followed by sulfonamides (sulfamethoxazole) and cephalosporins (cefoxitin), tetracyclines (tetracycline), potentiated penicillins (amoxicillin-clavulanic acid), and streptomycin, quinolones (ciprofloxacin), trimethoprim, carbapenems (meropenem), aminoglycosides (gentamicin), phenicols (chloramphenicol) and only one isolate was resistant to macrolides (azithromycin). Both Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 isolates showed higher resistance to penicillins, third-generation cephalosporins, tetracycline, and folate synthesis inhibitors. This higher resistance rate to penicillins, tetracyclines and folate synthesis inhibitors may be owing to the frequent use of these antimicrobials in veterinary medicine as documented by [76] from Bishoftu, Central Ethiopia and [77] from Rift Valley areas of Ethiopia. Higher resistance against antimicrobials infrequently used in poultry production may be attributed to possible antimicrobial cross-resistance in bacteria resistant to one drug may also become non susceptible to another as stated by [78]. Furthermore, it may also be associated with cross-resistance arising from the use of sanitizers even if the drug was not common on the farm [79]. In previous study [80], found cross resistance between the antimicrobials and biocides. This is hardly shocking considering how widely available, inexpensive, and prescription-free these antimicrobials were in use in poultry.

All Salmonella isolates exhibited resistance against ampicillin which was similar to the report of [70] from Kafa zone, Southwest Ethiopia. Almost the same pattern, 97.8%, 85.2%, 81.8%, 70%, 45% and 28.8% was reported by [81] from Southern Ethiopia; [82] from Debre Zeit and Addis Ababa, Ethiopia; [7] from Jimma University poultry farm, Southwest Ethiopia; [69] from Debre Zeit and Modjo, Ethiopia; [83] from middle Anatolia, Turkey, and [84] from Alage, Ziway and Shashemene, Ethiopia, respectively. Of the total tested Salmonella isolates, (66.6%) from which (44.4%) were cloacal swab isolates and (22.2%) postmortem isolates and (48.1%) were found resistant to the β-lactams and β-lactamase inhibitors combinations amoxicillin-clavulanic acid and ampicillin-sulbactam, respectively. This was lower than the report of [85] 91.7% to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid and 75% to ampicillin-sulbactam; and [7] from Southwest Ethiopia who recorded 54.5% of the tested Salmonella were resistant both amoxicillin-clavulanic acid and ampicillin-sulbactam. On the other hand [69], documented a lower 24.0% resistance rate against amoxicillin-clavulanic acid in Debre Zeit and Modjo, Ethiopia; [67] from Saudi Arabia 11.4% against ampicillin-sulbactam and 45.7% to amoxicillin-clavulanic. In addition [86] documented a resistance rate of 45.0% to amoxicillin-clavulanic and 23.0% to ampicillin-sulbactam. This varied resistance rate to amoxicillin-clavulanic and ampicillin-sulbactam may be owing to the reasons stated in previous paragraph of this manuscript. In addition to this, it may also due to the variation in consumption rate and frequency of this antimicrobial agents in farms of each study site.

With regard to the cephalosporins, Salmonella exhibited higher resistance to cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, and cefoxitin. The resistance rate of Salmonella to ceftriaxone in the current study was lower than the report of [87] (81.3%), but higher than the resistance rate (36.4%) recorded by [7] and [85] who recorded (16.7%). In another study [88], documented 6.4%, and 5.1% resistance rates of Salmonella against cefoxitin and cefotaxime, respectively, which was lower than the resistance pattern recorded in the current investigation. Before five years [89], from Korea reported that none of the 57 Salmonella isolates exhibited resistance to third-generation cephalosporin and cefoxitin. This higher resistance rate observed in the present study than the previous findings reported several years ago instigates that the resistance to these drugs in poultry production is increasing over time. In addition to this [90], documented that through time Salmonella has established broad resistance to some cephalosporins. WHO [6] categorized Enterobacterales resistant to third-generation cephalosporins as the critical pathogens. Thus, Salmonella isolated in this study falls under this category of pathogens. Pathogens under this group are considered as those that cause the maximum menace to public health owing to restricted treatment opportunities, high ailment burden and snowballing trends in ABR, with few or no promising candidates in the therapeutic pipeline.

Particularly the third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins are critically important antimicrobials for treating infections brought on by Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 in both humans and animals [91]. In spite of this, third-generation cephalosporin-resistant isolates of Salmonella have been recorded to be emerging quickly all over the world recently including current studies. This is a grave health risk since cephalosporins are clinically very critical drugs to treat different infections in humans and instigate that Salmonella in poultry production is producing extended spectrum β lactamases conferring resistance against this antimicrobial class. other authors [92] stated by ceasing the application of antimicrobial agents in this group in poultry production, it is possible to counter back the emergence and spread of extended-spectrum cephalosporin-resistant Salmonella. They recorded a steady decline in occurrence of Salmonella extended spectrum cephalosporin resistance after the intentional cessation of ceftiofur use by the Japanese poultry industry. The findings of present study necessitate enactment of the same strategies for these antimicrobial class in current study area.

In an attempt made to identify the resistance of Salmonella against aminoglycosides, about (55.6%) and (11.1%) exhibited resistance to streptomycin and gentamicin, respectively. The majority (66.7%) and (44.4%) of the Salmonella were found susceptible to gentamicin and streptomycin, respectively. Other authors [93], from the East Coast of Peninsular Malaysia and [70] from Kafa, Ethiopia reported zero and (19%) and (32.14%) resistance against streptomycin, respectively. In addition [69], recorded a lower (30.0%) rate in Debre Zeit and Modjo, Ethiopia.

Conversely [86] and [85], documented a higher (28%) and (66.7%) resistance rate than the current study. As stated formerly in this manuscript, this varied resistance rate may be ascribed to variation in the usage of these antimicrobials in each study site. Within the context of One Health, Salmonella resistant to aminoglycosides represents a serious risk because of their potential effects on the health of people, animals, and the environment. As aminoglycosides are frequently used to treat bacterial infections, including Salmonella infections in both humans and animals.

In relation to the folate synthesis inhibitors, about (85.1%), (74.1%) and (44.4%) of total Salmonella isolates exhibited resistance against sulfamethoxazole, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim, respectively. The resistance against trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole was lower than the report of [81] (100%) resistance in poultry production in Southern Ethiopia. In contrast [94], documented zero rate resistance to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and trimethoprim in Adama and Modjo towns, Ethiopia. Also [70] from Kafa, Ethiopia reported lower (46.42%) resistance than in the current study. Other authors [85] and [67] recorded (70%) and (68.6%) resistance to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. As stated, in the previous portion of this manuscript, sulfa drugs and their combinations are one of the most frequently used drugs by the poultry producers of current study site. This could be the possible contributing factor for the occurrence of relatively higher resistance rate in present study than some other reports.

Regarding quinolones (ciprofloxacin) above half of Salmonella isolates exhibited resistance, which was almost similar to the resistance rate documented by [95] and higher than the previous report of [81] (31.1%) in poultry industry southern Ethiopia. Conversely [96], reported higher (82.86%) resistance from northern India. Ciprofloxacin is the frontline antimicrobial for salmonellosis in both animals and human beings, but increasing resistance in current and other studies in poultry production instigates its therapeutic futility in the future. As poultry production is the main source of foodborne salmonellosis in humans, the existence of Salmonella resistant to first-line antimicrobials further fuels the public health hazard of the bacteria. According to [90], ciprofloxacin resistant Salmonella is responsible for 33,000 morbidities annually in the United States. A high incidence of Salmonella resistant to fluoroquinolones has been documented even after some of these antimicrobials were taken out of production, which poses a serious risk to the safety of poultry products and public health.

In an attempt made to determine the serotype level antimicrobial resistance pattern of Salmonella, the most frequent and public health important isolates S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurum exhibited high resistance to the most drugs tested. S. Enteritidis was resistant to ampicillin, cefoxitine and sulfamethoxazole, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole and streptomycin, and ceftriaxone, tetracycline and ciprofloxacin. The majority of the S. Enteritidis were susceptible to azithromycin, meropenem and chloramphenicol and Gentamicin, respectively, which was in line with the report of [97] from Central Ethiopia. S. Typhimurium exhibited complete susceptibility to chloramphenicol, azithromycin and Gentamicin and the majority were susceptible to meropenem. In contrast, before 14 years [98], from Shaanxi, China reported none of the S. Typhimurium and S. Enteritidis isolates were resistant to trimethoprim, gentamicin, tetracycline, and amoxicillin/clavulanic acid were found.

Conversely [99], reported that S. Typhimurium exhibited the highest resistance against ciprofloxacin and streptomycin then to tetracycline and gentamicin, ampicillin and amoxicillin-clavulanate. At the same time S. Enteritidis, showed the highest nonsusceptibility against streptomycin then to ciprofloxacin, tetracycline, and gentamicin. In another study by [100] 95.3% and 72.7% of the S. Enteritidis were resistant to ampicillin and tetracycline respectively which was almost higher resistance rate as in this study. Nontyphoidal Salmonella resistant to the fluoroquinolones are ranked under high priority pathogens which are notably difficult to cure and contribute significantly to the burden of disease, exhibit rising resistance patterns, difficult to prevent, highly transmissible, and for which there are limited treatment alternatives.

This relatively higher resistance rate along with higher isolation rate of S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium in the poultry, which is growing hotspot for AMR is an alarming finding necessitating effective control strategies in poultry production and associated food chains. It is documented that S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium are the most frequent serotypes that acclimate to diverse hosts and transmission niches and they have noteworthy epidemiological significance across the globe. Poultry products are well-thought-out important reservoirs of many S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium which can reach the human being through chicken product consumption [55, 101]. The higher resistance of poultry-specific serovars S. Gallinarum and S. pullorum in the current study reveal their health, productivity and welfare hazards on the poultry. From S. pullorum tested, all and 66.7% exhibited non-susceptibility to ampicillin, cefoxitine and sulfamethoxazole, and amoxicillin/clavulanic, ampicillin/sulbactam, cefotaxime, streptomycin and tetracycline, respectively. Interestingly, all and 80% of S. pullorum were susceptible to azitomycin and ciprofloxacin, meropenem, cefotaxime and gentamicin, respectively. The single isolate S. Dublin exhibited resistance to ampicillin, ceftriaxone, sulfamethoxazole, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, trimethoprim and tetracycline.

Regarding the antibiogram of E. coli O157:H7, all the isolates were found resistant to at least three antimicrobials tested with highest resistance rate against ampicillin followed by cefotaxime and tetracycline, cefoxitin and trimethoprim, sulfamethoxazole, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid and ceftriaxone, ampicillin-sulbactam, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, streptomycin and meropenem. Interestingly, all the E. coli O157:H7 isolates were susceptible to azithromycin. In line with current findings [102], from Ohio State America reported, 100% resistance to ampicillin. Conversely [43], from eastern Ethiopia and [93] East Coast of Peninsular Malaysia reported the highest (96.15%) and (100%) resistance to azithromycin, respectively.

In another study by [103] from Addis Ababa chicken slaughterhouses the highest resistance rate recorded was 47.5% against streptomycin and zero resistance to gentamicin and ciprofloxacin. In the same study, the lowest (2.5%) and (3.1%) resistance was observed against chloramphenicol and sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, respectively. This varied resistance rate among different studies could be owing to the difference in the sample type, health status and overall management of source chicken in each site. According to [104, 105], poultry and their products are the most important source and dissemination vehicle for mono-drug and multidrug resistant E. coli O157:H7 and associated public or one health hazards. This demonstrates that a higher antimicrobial resistance rate with a higher isolation rate of E. coli O157:H7 in chicken is of great concern as it reaches human beings through direct and indirect contact and the food chain.

In relation to the penicillin groups of the tested antimicrobials, like our finding different studies have reported a high resistance rate of E. coli O157:H7 against ampicillin [43], (92.30%) [106], (62%) [107], (77.78%) [93], (87.5%) [108], (60.0%) and [103] (100%). This high degree of resistance demonstrates that resistance by E. coli O157:H7 in poultry production is rendering ampicillin therapeutically futile for the E. coli O157:H7 infected animals and humans as the resistance can disseminate and circulate within one health interface. Almost the same (51%) and lower (3.3) resistance to amoxicillin-sulbactam and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid was documented by [106] and [109] in unspecified strains of E. coli, respectively. Conversely [110], documented higher (83%) resistance to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid. These all instigates that, E. coli was developing resistance to the penicillin groups in an upward fashion making use of these antimicrobials therapeutically futile.

In relation to the tested cephalosporins, E. coli O157:H7 exhibited a high level of resistance with which highest resistance recorded in cefotaxime. A slightly higher resistance (84.61%) than the current result against cefoxitin was reported by [43]. The resistance pattern of E. coli O157:H7 to ceftriaxone is almost in agreement with the report of [107] (55.56%). In another study by [111] 28.2% resistance to ceftriaxone was documented which is relatively lower than the current. As compared to the result of this study, relatively lower resistance rate to cefotaxime (21.3%) and ceftriaxone (18.2%) was also reported by [109] from China and cefoxitin (12.6%), ceftriaxone (13.8%) and cefotaxime (17.2%) by [39] from Gondar City, Northwest Ethiopia in non-serotyped E. coli. It is obvious that third-generation cephalosporins are the highest priority and critically important beta-lactam antimicrobial commonly used for the chemotherapy of infections caused by Gram-negative bacteria E. coli [112], ominously the resistance is rendering it challenging over time. Furthermore, the multidrug resistance in these bacteria further bottleneck the therapeutic opportunities.

As the antibiogram result revealed, more than half of E. coli O157:H7 isolates exhibited resistance against tested folate synthesis inhibitor/sulfonamide antimicrobial agents. This was lower than the previous report of [102], 100% against trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole [113] from Zaria, Nigeria 85.7% against trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and of [109] 78.9% to sulfamethoxazole and 77.8% to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole. Conversely [43], documented that most (92.30%) of the test isolates were susceptible to trimethoprim. The resistance rate (78%) against trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole documented by [110] was notably higher than in the present study. This demonstrates there was a high level of resistance to sulfonamides even if it was varied in region and time of the study.

Among the tested antimicrobials, aminoglycosides, carbapenems, phenicol, quinolone and macrolides class showed good invitro effect on the E. coli O157:H7 in which above half of the test isolates were susceptible. This finding corroborates the previous report of [43] who documented higher susceptibility to ciprofloxacin (100%), chloramphenicol (96.15%) gentamicin (88.46%) and streptomycin (65.38%). Conversely, the same author and [93] reported higher (96.15%) and (100%) resistance against erythromycin, respectively. In addition [102], noted that all (100%) isolates were susceptible to chloramphenicol, ciprofloxacin and meropenem. In contrast, this author reported lower susceptibility to gentamicin (6.7%) and streptomycin (46%). Carbapenems are the drug of choice to treat severe infections with ESBL Enterobacteriaceae [114], current finding indicates interesting invitro activity of these critical drugs against E. coli O157:H7 but there is resistance.

In an attempt at MDR determination, all the Salmonella isolates possessed multidrug resistance to a minimum of 3 and maximum of 8 antimicrobial classes out of 9 antimicrobial groups tested. This finding corroborates the report of [41, 85, 102] who documented MDR in all (100%) of the Salmonella isolates. In addition [81], reported all (100%) the Salmonella isolated from the poultry industry of Southern Ethiopia were multidrug resistant. In contrast [67], recorded MDR in 65% of the isolates which is lower than in the current study. MDR recorded in the present study was higher than the MDR Salmonella isolates human (82.9%) reported by [115].

All the Salmonella isolates exhibited MDR against antimicrobial classes understudy, most (44.4%) were multidrug resistant to six antimicrobial classes. From the E. coli O157:H7 isolates, 80% exhibited MDR, to maximum of seven antimicrobial classes tested. Other authors [43, 108] and [109] reported high levels of MDR 92.30%, 71.1% and 83%, respectively. The remaining 20% (4/20) were not multidrug-resistant. According to [116] MDR bacterial infections are the leading cause of death. The most recent antimicrobial resistance report reveals that over half of the bacterial species commonly linked to fatal diseases caused by multidrug resistance are Gram-negative bacteria, including S. enterica, and E. coli. These bacteria accounted for nearly three million deaths globally in 2019, which is equivalent to roughly 60% of all MDR-related deaths. MDR microorganisms have posed a risk to public health, particularly. In this spectacle, a higher level of MDR was documented in both bacteria isolated from the main hotspot and vehicle poultry production distributing it to the public as well as the one health interface. This necessitates timely intervention to tackle further rise MDR to the serious level as the poultry production is becoming most common protein source and income source with higher and uncontrolled use of antimicrobials.

The result this investigation revealed that multidrug resistance was widespread in Salmonella and very common in E. coli O157:H7, with concerning serotype-specific resistance patterns: S. Enteritidis and S. Typhimurium exhibited complete resistance to ampicillin and high levels of reduced susceptibility to β-lactams, sulfonamides, tetracyclines, and fluoroquinolones. These results align with global data indicating that these zoonotic serotypes serve as major reservoirs of ESBL and plasmid-mediated resistance genes in poultry [117]. Such resistance undermines primary treatments for invasive non-typhoidal Salmonella infections and complicates poultry disease management. Additionally, most isolates showed high MAR indices (> 0.2), indicating ongoing antimicrobial selection pressure on farms [118]. The presence of resistance to critically important drugs including cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and even a carbapenem in one isolate raises concerns about the potential transfer of resistance genes to human pathogens [119].

Additionally, poultry-adapted serotypes such as S. Gallinarum and S. Pullorum also demonstrated multidrug resistance, posing threats to animal health and productivity. The identification of resistance to critically important antibiotics, including a carbapenem in one isolate, serves as an early warning of emerging resistance. These serotype-specific multidrug resistance patterns highlight the critical need for molecular surveillance, responsible antimicrobial use in poultry farming, and One Health–oriented strategies to prevent the spread of high-risk resistant clones between animals and humans [120].

In the current study, a higher MAR index (the ratio of the number of antimicrobial agents to which the bacteria were resistant to the total number of antimicrobials against which the bacteria were tested) was documented for both bacteria. All the Salmonella and 95% of E. coli O157:H7 isolates exhibited a MAR index higher than 0.2. This result was in line with the report of [121] who recorded a MAR index ≥ 0.2 in 86.8% of isolates and [55] who recorded a MAR index ≥ 0.2 in 94% of E. coli isolates. Similarly [122] reported that 100% of the Salmonella isolates MAR index value higher than 0.2. This MAR index value higher than 0.2 suggests these isolates were identified from farms where there is a high menace of bacterial contamination where there was frequent higher level antimicrobial consumption and higher antimicrobial abuse [123]. The MAR index of Salmonella ranges from 0.31 to 0.75 with an average of 0.53 and that of E. coli O157:H7 was recorded as 0.19 to 0.55 with an average of 0.35. Nevertheless, the difference in MAR between the two genera of the bacteria under investigation was statistically non-significant (P-value = 0.8610, X2 = 0.031, d.f. 1) (P-value=). This result in conjunction with the result of MDR pattern instigates, Salmonella was exhibiting higher antimicrobial resistance than E. coli O157:H7. Interestingly, one isolate from broiler chicken exhibited MAR index (0.19) lower than 0.2 indicating antimicrobial use in the farm from which the isolate was isolated was infrequent and rational [124].

In converse [93], documented a higher MAR index for E. coli O157:H7 than for Salmonella. MAR index of bacteria isolated from broiler chicken ranges from 0.19 to 0.56 with an average of 0.38 and for layers was from 0.31 to 0.75 with an average of 0.53. The highest MAR index 0.75 was recorded for bacteria isolated from layer chicken. This result shows the MAR index was higher in bacteria isolated from layer chickens, suggesting that there was higher and more frequent consumption of antimicrobials in layer farms than in broiler farms. The difference in MAR index between broiler and layer type of chicken was statistically significant (P = 0.000, X2 = 9.559). In contrary [125], MAR index was significantly lower bacteria isolated from in layers. Since these bacteria were resistant to multiple antimicrobials and isolated from the areas where there was high antimicrobial abuse, infection by this bacteria pose greater challenge in treating as stated by [126].

Regarding the R score, most (78.72%) of the bacterial isolates under investigation exhibited R score ≥ 0.5 (high/very high AMR) and the remaining 22.28% showed R score lower than 0.5 (low/very low AMR). This was lower than the R score recorded by [125] who recorded 0.5 to 0.7 in all isolates in selected districts of Nepal. About 81.48% of the Salmonella isolates exhibited high/very high AMR and 85.0% of E. coli O157:H7 isolates were revealed high/very high AMR (R score ≥ 0.5). From both genera of bacteria, the highest R score (0.78) was documented in E. coli O157:H7. A statistically significant (P = 0.002, X2 = 9.204) difference was observed between the two genera of bacteria under study. A higher (85.2%) R score ≥ 0.5 (high/very high AMR) was recorded in bacteria isolated from layer than from broiler chicken (69.23%) and the difference in R score between the two types of chicken was statistically nonsignificant (P = 0.2032, X2 = 1.619). In contrast [125], reported a highly significant difference in R score with chicken type. The result suggests that a majority (78.72%) of bacterial isolates displayed high/very high levels of antimicrobial resistance, particularly in E. coli O157:H7 strains compared to Salmonella strains.

R scores are crucial in guiding clinical decision-making, especially in cases where drug-resistant infections are prevalent. Quantifying resistance levels helps to inform healthcare providers to tailor treatment regimens to ensure optimal patient outcomes and minimize the spread of resistant pathogens within healthcare settings and communities. In addition to this, in combatting AMR, the R score serves as a quantitative indicator of the level of AMR exhibited by pathogens to specific antimicrobial agents, aiding healthcare professionals in selecting appropriate treatment strategies and combating drug-resistant infections effectively.

In the current study, concurrent phenotypic resistance was observed to different antimicrobials including cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, meropenem and amoxicillin-sulbactam, cefoxitin, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, and meropenem; sulfamethoxazole, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, amoxicillin, ciprofloxacin and sulfamethoxazole, gentamicin and azithromycin. Concurrent resistance against the antimicrobials of the same class could be due that they share the same mechanism of action but unrelated classes may be due to cross selection effect or plasmid carriage of different resistance genes [127, 128]. In line to this study [28, 125, 129], reported concurrent resistance to the antimicrobials of the same and different classes. The resistance correlation between amoxicillin-sulbactam and cefotaxime, cefoxitin and meropenem, chloramphenicol and ciprofloxacin, sulfamethoxazole and meropenem, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim and ceftriaxone, and azithromycin and amoxicillin-sulbactam was significantly strong (P < 0.05).

The strongest correlation was recorded between cefoxitin and meropenem (rs = 0.4847). In another study, the strongest correlation was documented between chloramphenicol and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole resistance (rs = 0.60) [130]. In addition [131] reported a strong correlation in E. coli O157:H7 resistance to gentamicin and ciprofloxacin. Conversely, a significant (P < 0.05) negative correlation was observed between trimethoprim and cefoxitin, tetracycline and ceftriaxone, and azithromycin and meropenem resistance. This means that, as the Salmonella and E. coli O157:H7 attain resistance to trimethoprim, tetracycline and azithromycin, they become more susceptible to cefoxitin, azithromycin and azithromycin and vice versa, respectively. This may be attributed fact that on some occasions, there is bacterial hyper-susceptibility in which strains resistant to certain antimicrobial agents become more susceptible than the parental strain [132].

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it employed a cross-sectional design, providing only a snapshot of resistance patterns and unable to capture temporal changes or trends. Second, sampling was limited to poultry farms in Hossana, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to other regions of Ethiopia. Third, only phenotypic methods were used; molecular characterization of resistance genes and mobile genetic elements was not conducted due to resource constraints, limiting deeper insights into resistance mechanisms and potential transmission dynamics. Fourth, data on actual antimicrobial usage at the farm level were not collected, restricting causal inferences about the drivers of resistance. Finally, the relatively modest sample size may have reduced the power to detect less common resistance patterns or rare serovars. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable baseline data that can inform future molecular research and guide national AMR surveillance initiatives.

Conclusion and recommendations

Antimicrobial resistance is reported to emerge in poultry production increasingly and distribute to the environment, other animals and human through occupational contact, improper farm west management and food chain across the globe. This study indicated a high prevalence of antimicrobial-resistant E. coli O157:H7 and Salmonella in conventional broiler farms in Hossana Town, where all the Salmonella and the majority of E. coli O157:H7 isolates were multidrug resistant. The widespread resistance to commonly prescribed antibiotics such as ampicillin, cephalosporins, and tetracycline indicates the urgent need for improved antimicrobial stewardship in poultry production. This investigation implicate that poultry production is one of the important hotspot for the emergence of antimicrobial resistant zoonotic pathogens with possibility of transmission to the One Health framework. This study acknowledges the resistance pattern was not evaluated molecularly. Thus, implementation of effective, incessant and targeted monitoring and regulations on irrational antimicrobial dispensing and use along with collaborative and multilevel stewardship campaign. Awaring of poultry producers about the rational antimicrobial use, proper management of the pharmaceuticals in the farm and while discarding and AMR and its preventive methods. Further molecular characterization of resistance genes is needed to guide effective AMR mitigation.
