Empirical vulnerability strata in under-five mortality in Cameroon: Evidence from DHS data
Authors: Aoudou Njingouo Mounchingam, Vissého Adjiwanou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite substantial declines in under-five mortality in sub-Saharan Africa, child survival remains marked by persistent and socially structured inequalities. Variable-centered survival models document average effects of risk factors but provide limited insight into how multiple disadvantages combine to generate heterogeneous mortality risks. This study aims to identify and characterize empirical configurations of vulnerability in under-five mortality in Cameroon. Using pooled data from the 2011 and 2018 Cameroon Demographic and Health Surveys, we analyze time to death before age 59 months. A random survival forest is used as an exploratory survival-based tool to derive individual risk scores, which serve to stratify children into ordered mortality risk strata and then summarized using weighted Kaplan-Meier estimates. Three distinct mortality risk strata emerge. The highest-risk stratum, with an estimated probability of death before age five of 27.8%, concentrates overlapping disadvantages, including low birth weight, short birth intervals, limited maternal and partner education, lack of antenatal care, home delivery, low media exposure, and constrained maternal decision-making autonomy. These configurations are observed consistently across survey waves, indicating persistent social structuring of mortality risk. Under-five mortality in Cameroon remains sharply stratified along cumulative social, reproductive, and gender-related dimensions. Inequalities in child survival are produced not by isolated risk factors but by socially embedded configurations of disadvantage. This study contributes to demonstrate how survival-based machine learning can be combined with conventional statistical tools to reveal socially interpretable strata of vulnerability, shifting attention from marginal covariate effects to empirical configurations of cumulative mortality risk.Keywords: Under-five mortality, Risk stratification, Random survival forest, Kaplan-meier ;Cameroon

Introduction

Over the past two decades, the under-five mortality rate worldwide has declined by about 52%, the result of long-term governmental and international collaboration. These advances, driven by effective life-saving interventions, have enabled millions of children to survive and develop healthily. However, the last data from the United Nations Inter-Agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation (UN IGME) indicate that this progress is slowing. In 2023, approximately 4.8 million children under-five still died from largely preventable causes and the highest national under-five mortality rates are found in sub-Saharan Africa (UN IGME, 2025). This region accounts for nearly half of the global burden of under-five mortality, while representing only about 17% of the world's population (UNICEF, 2022; UNICEF, WHO, World Bank Group and United Nations, 2018). Under-five mortality therefore remains a major public health issue, as levels and trends in child mortality are widely regarded as key indicators of population health, socioeconomic development, and health system performance (Reidpath & Allotey, 2003; Wang et al., 2014).

Cameroon reflects these broader regional challenges. Although under-five mortality declined from 144 to 80 deaths per 1000 live births between 2004 and 2018, it remains well above the Sustainable Development Goal target of 25 deaths per 1000 live births by 2030 (Institut National de la Statistique et UNICEF WHO World Bank Group and United Nations, 2018, September 13; Institut National de la Statistique (INS) & ICF, 2020). Beyond national averages, child survival remains marked by pronounced social inequalities. Previous research shows that under-five mortality in Cameroon, as elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa, is strongly associated with demographic, socioeconomic, health-related, and gender-related factors whose effects often interact in complex ways (Adjiwanou & Engdaw, 2017; Debere & Adjiwanou, 2024; Mounchingam & Adjiwanou, 2025). Understanding how these factors jointly structure mortality risk is therefore essential for both sociodemographic research and policies aimed at improving child survival.

Under-five child mortality has long been recognized as a central indicator of population health and social development (Akoto, 1987; Boco, 2011; Reidpath & Allotey, 2003). Beyond its immediate biomedical determinants, it reflects the unequal distribution of material resources, health-related knowledge, and access to protective environments across social groups (Adjiwanou & LeGrand, 2014; Braverman-Bronstein et al., 2023; Mounchingam & Adjiwanou, 2025). Classical demographic frameworks demography emphasize that early-life survival chances are deeply embedded in broader systems of social stratification operating through households, communities, and institutions (Mosley & Chen, 1984). Likewise, demographic research has long recognized that mortality risks are heterogeneous across individuals and social contexts. Frailty models and multilevel survival models were developed to account for this heterogeneity through unobserved individual and contextual variability (Hougaard, 1995; Sastry, 1997), building on the concept of frailty introduced by Vaupel et al. (1979) and subsequent work on mortality selection (Vaupel & Yashin, 1985). Although these approaches established heterogeneity as a central feature of mortality dynamics, they generally treat it as an unobserved statistical component rather than as an empirical phenomenon characterized through observable configurations of social conditions.

This perspective is particularly relevant in sub-Saharan Africa, where child survival is shaped by the interaction of multiple social, demographic, and contextual dimensions (Aboagye et al., 2022; Debere & Adjiwanou, 2024; Kravdal, 2018; Mounchingam & Adjiwanou, 2025; Yaya et al., 2020). Most empirical studies have relied on the analytical framework proposed by Mosley and Chen (1984). This framework conceptualizes child mortality as the product of a limited number of proximate determinants through which distal socioeconomic and contextual factors operate. Empirical applications have most often relied on variable-centered analytical strategies, estimating average effects of individual predictors or, more rarely, specific interaction terms (Debere & Adjiwanou, 2024). While these studies have identified robust correlates of child mortality, they provide limited insight into how multiple disadvantages combine within individuals and households to generate differentiated levels of mortality risk. In practice, vulnerability rarely results from a single adverse characteristic but rather from the accumulation of reproductive constraints, limited parental resources, restricted access to health services, and adverse community environments.

Among these dimensions, gender constitutes an important component of cumulative disadvantage. Gender stratification shapes women's access to education, economic resources, decision-making power, and health care, thereby influencing maternal health behaviours and children's survival (Adjiwanou & LeGrand, 2014; Boehmer & Williamson, 1996; Shen & Williamson, 1997). Empirical studies consistently show that maternal education, decision-making autonomy, and attitudes toward gender-based violence are closely associated with under-five mortality (Birhanie et al., 2025; Mounchingam & Adjiwanou, 2025; Yaya et al., 2020). Community-level gender norms further reinforce these inequalities by shaping local health environments and women's access to collective resources (Aboagye et al., 2022; Sossa, 2019). Together, these findings suggest that under-five mortality is produced through socially embedded configurations of disadvantage rather than isolated risk factors.

Despite broad recognition of mortality heterogeneity and cumulative social disadvantage, most empirical studies continue to adopt variable-centered approaches that estimate the average effects of individual covariates. Such approaches provide limited information on how multiple characteristics combine to position children along differentiated gradients of mortality risk. They rarely identify empirically distinct configurations of vulnerability or quantify the levels of mortality associated with them. Addressing this gap requires an analytical perspective capable of characterizing mortality risk as the joint expression of multiple dimensions of social disadvantage rather than the sum of isolated covariate effects.

In response to these limitations, person-centered approaches offer a complementary framework for characterizing individuals based on configurations of coexisting characteristics (Laursen & Hoff, 2006; Miconi et al., 2024). Rather than focusing on the marginal contribution of individual predictors, these approaches seek to identify how combinations of characteristics position individuals along differentiated risk gradients. Conceptually, this perspective is consistent with ecosocial theory, which views health inequalities as the embodied consequences of socially structured exposures accumulated over the life course (Krieger, 2001). Recent population health studies have increasingly adopted person-centered approaches to identify empirical profiles of vulnerability and resilience (Kamal et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2016; Van Hulst et al., 2015; Wong et al., 2018). From this perspective, vulnerability profiles can be interpreted as observable manifestations of the heterogeneity long recognized in demographic research but rarely characterized empirically.

Survival analysis provides a natural framework for such an approach because it characterizes individuals according to their cumulative risk of experiencing an event over time. Building on this tradition, recent methodological developments integrating machine learning into survival analysis have shown that survival-based risk scores can be used to stratify individuals into ordered groups with distinct survival trajectories, while avoiding strong assumptions about the functional form of covariate effects or their interactions (Ishwaran et al., 2008; Li et al., 2024). Although these methods have largely been developed for prediction, they also provide a flexible way of deriving empirical gradients of mortality risk.

Building on these developments, the present study adopts a person-centered analytical perspective to examine under-five mortality in Cameroon. Rather than estimating only the average effects of individual predictors, we aim to identify empirically grounded vulnerability strata that summarize how multiple social characteristics jointly structure mortality risk across early childhood. We use survival-based machine learning as an exploratory tool to derive a continuous mortality risk gradient, which is subsequently partitioned into ordered strata representing distinct levels of cumulative vulnerability. These strata do not correspond to latent classes or causal typologies. Instead, they represent empirical groupings along a continuous gradient of mortality risk and should therefore be interpreted as descriptive representations of cumulative vulnerability rather than distinct causal entities. The analysis addresses three research questions: (1) What distinct strata of under-five mortality can be empirically identified in Cameroon? (2) How are these strata socially structured? (3) What levels of cumulative mortality risk are associated with each stratum?

This study makes three contributions to research on under-five mortality in sub-Saharan Africa. First, it advances a person-centered perspective by identifying empirically derived and socially structured vulnerability strata, thereby complementing conventional analyses based on average covariate effects. Second, it illustrates how machine learning-based survival models can be integrated into demographic research as exploratory tools for empirical risk stratification. By relaxing assumptions of proportional hazards and linearity (Ishwaran et al., 2008; Ishwaran & Kogalur, 2025), this approach provides a flexible representation of heterogeneity in mortality risk while remaining anchored in substantive sociodemographic interpretation. Third, using pooled DHS data from 2011 to 2018, the study examines whether these empirically derived vulnerability strata display similar patterns of social structuring across survey waves.

Cameroon provides a particularly relevant setting for examining socially structured vulnerability in under-five mortality due to its pronounced socioeconomic heterogeneity and persistent community- and gender-based inequalities (Ministère de la promotion de la femme et de la famille& Institut National de la Statistique, 2024; InstitutNational de laStatistique(INS)ICF, 2012; National institute of statistics, 2020; United States Department of state, 2020). The analysis draws on pooled data from the 2011 and 2018 DHS, allowing the identification of a common set of empirical vulnerability strata across survey waves while strengthening statistical power and ensuring temporal comparability (Schmidt & Elkasabi, 2022).

Methods

Data

This study draws on data from the two most recent nationally household surveys conducted in Cameroon: the 2011 Demographic and Health Survey-Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (DHS-MICS) and the 2018 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS). The 2011 survey was implemented by the National Institute of Statistics and the Ministry of Economy, Planning, and Regional Development, in collaboration with the Ministry of Public Health, ICF International, UNICEF, and the Pasteur Center of Cameroon. The 2018 DHS was carried out by the National Institute of Statistics in partnership with the Ministry of Public Health and ICF International. Both surveys employed a two-stage stratified probability sampling design and provide comprehensive information on population health, household characteristics, and women's reproductive histories. These data are particularly well suited for the analysis of under-five mortality, as they include complete birth histories that allow for the construction of time-to-event measures for child survival. Detailed descriptions of the sampling procedures and survey methodology are available in the corresponding final reports (Institut National de la Statistique & ICF, 2012;Institut National de la Statistique & ICF, 2020).

The pooled dataset includes information on 21,465 children born in the five years preceding each survey (11,732 in 2011 and 9733 in 2018). Of these, 1646 (998 in 2011 and 648 in 2018) children died before reaching age five, while the remaining observations correspond to children who were still alive at the time of the survey and are treated as right-censored. Pooling the two survey waves increases the number of observed death events and improves the precision of estimates across social groups, while preserving the nationally representative nature of each survey (Schmidt & Elkasabi, 2022). All analyses are conducted using the full sample. Descriptive statistics and estimates of cumulative mortality within each stratum account for survey weights, stratification, and clustering. In contrast, survival models are estimated without weights and are used exclusively for empirical stratification purposes, namely to construct a relative ordering of individuals along a mortality risk gradient. Population representativeness is ensured at the estimation stage through weighted Kaplan-Meier survival curves.

Variables

Dependent variable

The outcome of interest is under-five mortality, modeled using a time-to-event framework. For each child, the survival time is defined as the age in months at death for children who died before reaching age five, or the age in months at the time of the survey for children who were still alive. Children who survived beyond the survey date or reached age 59 months without experiencing the event are treated as right-censored. An event indicator equal to 1 denotes death before age five, while 0 indicates right-censoring.

Independent variables

The selection of covariates is guided by existing research on child mortality in sub-Saharan Africa and data availability in the DHS (Argoze, 2021; Boco, 2011; Mounchingam & Adjiwanou, 2025; Sossa, 2019). Variables are grouped into four domains.

Child-related characteristics include sex (female, male), previous birth interval (less than 24 months, 24 months or more, first birth), birth order (first birth, second or third, fourth or higher), and birth weight (low, normal, high).

Mother-related characteristics include educational attainment (no education, primary, secondary, higher), employment status (not employed, employed), attitudes toward domestic violence (not favorable, favorable), decision-making autonomy (none, low, high), mother's age (under 20, 20-29, 30-39, 40 or older), religion (Catholic, Protestant, Muslim, other Christian, animist/other), ethnic group (Bamileke, Beti–Bassa–Mbam, East, North-West/South-West, Northern, other), level of media exposure (none, low, medium, high), number of antenatal care visits (none, 1-4, 5 or more), and place of delivery (home, health facility).

Household-related characteristics include household wealth status (poor, middle, rich), partner's educational attainment (no education, primary, secondary, higher), sex of the household head (male, female), and household size (2-3, 4-6, 7 or more members).

Contextual characteristics capture the broader environment of residence and are constructed by aggregating individual-level information at the survey cluster level. These include place of residence (urban, rural), the proportion of women exposed to mass media, the proportion of households classified as wealthy, and the proportion of women with at least secondary education within the cluster.

Software and statistical packages

All statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software. Survey-weighted analyses accounting for the complex DHS sampling design were performed using the survey package (Lumley, 2024). Missing covariate values were imputed using the missForest package (Stekhoven & Bühlmann, 2012). Cox proportional hazards models were estimated using the survival package (Therneau, 2024), whereas random survival forest models were implemented using the randomForestSRC package (Ishwaran & Kogalur, 2025). Kaplan-Meier survival curves were produced using the survminer package (Kassambara et al., 2025).

Statistical analysis

We first describe the study sample by reporting weighted distributions of all variables while accounting for the complex sampling design of the DHS. Because data from two DHS waves were pooled, sampling weights were re-normalized at the combined sample level to preserve the representativeness of each survey while avoiding disproportionate influence of any single wave. This re-normalization followed established DHS recommendations for pooled analyses (Zethof et al., 2023). In addition, all models include an indicator for survey year to account for potential period effects between the 2011 and 2018 surveys. Missing values on covariates were imputed using a random forest-based imputation approach, which is well suited for handling mixed categorical and continuous variables (Stekhoven & Bühlmann, 2012). Overall, 4.9% of all covariate values were missing and were imputed using this procedure. Missing data affected 38.7% of children, with most missingness arising from birth weight.

To examine under-five mortality as a time-to-event process, we estimated two complementary survival models. First, we fitted a Cox proportional hazards model as a benchmark approach commonly used in studies of child mortality (Cox, 1972). The Cox model allows for flexible baseline hazards and provides interpretable estimates of relative risks but relies on the proportional hazards assumption, which may be violated when risk factors interact in complex ways. Second, we estimated a random survival forest (RSF) model as a flexible, non-parametric approach to survival analysis. RSF relaxes the proportional hazards assumption and automatically accounts for nonlinear effects and interactions among covariates, providing a more robust framework for revealing heterogeneity in mortality risk (Nasejje & Mwambi, 2017). In this study, the RSF was used exclusively as an exploratory tool to derive an empirical ordering of children according to their mortality risk and to define risk strata.

Cox proportional hazards model

Cox proportional hazards models are first estimated as a benchmark commonly used on child mortality. The model specifies the hazard of death at age t as a function of a baseline hazard and a vector of covariates describing child, maternal, household, and contextual characteristics. The Cox model is semi-parametric, as it leaves the baseline hazard unspecified and estimates covariate effects through partial likelihood (Cox, 1972). Estimation relies on comparing individuals who experience the event at a given age t with those still at risk immediately before t. However, age at death is measured in months, which induces a discrete time scale with a non-negligible number of tied event times, corresponding to children dying in the same month. Although the exact partial likelihood method is theoretically appropriate under such conditions (Therneau & Grambsch, 2001), the combination of large risk sets and multiple tied events at certain time points makes it computationally infeasible in practice. We therefore use Efron's approximation, which has been shown to provide accurate estimates in the presence of many ties and is generally preferred to Breslow's method in such contexts (Allison, 2010; Z. Li et al., 2023).

Alongside these considerations, a key assumption of the model is proportional hazards, implying that relative differences in mortality risk between individuals remain constant over time. This assumption is assessed using tests based on Schoenfeld residuals (Schoenfeld, 1982). Based on the estimated coefficients and baseline survival function, individual survival functions and cumulative probabilities of death before a given age are derived.

Random survival forest

To explore heterogeneity in under-five mortality beyond proportional hazards assumptions, we estimate RSF model. RSF extends tree-based ensemble methods to right-censored time-to-event data by recursively partitioning the sample into subgroups with distinct survival experiences using log-rank splitting rules (Ishwaran et al., 2008). Unlike the Cox proportional hazards model, the log-rank splitting rule used in RSF does not require maximizing a partial likelihood. Consequently, tied event times are handled directly through the computation of the log-rank statistic during tree construction, and no approximation such as Efron's method is required. By aggregating information across many trees, RSF yields individual-level survival functions without imposing assumptions on the functional form of covariate effects or their interactions.

In this study, RSF is used as an exploratory tool that natively accounts for nonlinear relationships and complex interactions between child, mother, household, and contextual characteristics. Model estimation is implemented using the randomForestSRC package in R (Ishwaran & Kogalur, 2025). Unlike Cox regression, RSF does not rely on the proportional hazards assumption, making it particularly suitable for uncovering heterogeneity in mortality risk. Technical details on RSF estimation are provided in supplementary material.

Because the objective of the study is empirical stratification rather than prediction, model performance metrics are not emphasized.

Identification of under-five mortality risk strata

To characterize under-five mortality risk strata, individuals are first ranked according to their model-based mortality risk scores, derived from the survival models. The risk ordering is summarized at the horizon of 59 months, which captures the full span of early childhood and provides a comprehensive basis for stratification. Based on this ordering, children are grouped into ordered risk strata (Li et al., 2024). Risk thresholds were defined to ensure both meaningful differentiation of vulnerability and adequate sample size within each stratum. This approach does not aim to classify children into discrete latent groups but rather to summarize a continuous gradient of vulnerability into interpretable strata. Each stratum therefore represents a distinct level of cumulative exposure to socially structured mortality risk, derived empirically from observed survival trajectories. Within each risk strata, dominant characteristics are identified by comparing the distribution of covariate modalities within the strata to their distribution in the overall sample. Modalities that are overrepresented in each strata are interpreted as defining features of that risk strata. This descriptive profiling strategy allows for a substantive interpretation of socially structured configurations of mortality risk without implying causal mechanisms.

For each risk strata, the cumulative probability of death before age five is estimated non-parametrically using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, accounting for right censoring and survey design. The cumulative mortality function is defined as Fˆk(t)=1−Sˆk(t), where Sˆk(t) denotes the Kaplan–Meier estimate of the survival function within strata k. Confidence intervals are derived using Greenwood's variance estimator (Sawyer, 2003).

Marked social differentials characterize maternal and household conditions. More than one quarter of mothers have no education, while about one third have completed secondary education or higher. Although most mothers report being employed, nearly one third indicate no participation in household decision-making. Mothers are predominantly aged 20-39 years and mainly identify as Christian or Muslim. Finally, socioeconomic and contextual indicators further reveal substantial heterogeneity. Approximately 45% of children were born into poor households, and 57% reside in rural areas. More than one third of births occurred at home, and fewer than one third of mothers attended five or more antenatal visits. Community-level indicators point to wide variation in media exposure, household wealth, and female educational attainment.

Bivariate associations between covariates and child survival were examined using Log-rank tests. With the exception of the sex of the household head, all covariates show statistically significant associations with child survival. Detailed distributions by covariate are reported in supplementary material. However, these descriptive associations offer limited insight into how multiple social and demographic factors combine to shape mortality risk. To move beyond isolated associations, we next estimate multivariate survival models that provide the basis for empirical risk stratification.

Cox proportional hazards model

Tests based on Schoenfeld residuals indicate widespread violations of the proportional hazards assumption. The global test strongly rejects proportionality (χ2 = 172.35, p < 0.001), and departures are observed for most child, maternal, household, and community covariates. Despite these limitations, the Cox model highlights several well-documented differentials in under-five mortality. Lower risks are associated with longer birth intervals, higher maternal education, higher birth weight, and increased use of antenatal care, while higher risks are observed for higher-order births. However, many associations attenuate or lose statistical significance once jointly adjusted (see supplementary material for more details).

Importantly, these violations imply that a Cox-based linear risk score would be unstable across age, potentially misleading and therefore unsuitable for empirical stratification purposes based on a risk score, as the relative contribution of covariates varies over time. Moreover, the Cox model imposes a linear and additive structure on covariate effects, which limits its ability to capture the complex, non-linear, and cumulative configurations of risk that motivate our person-centered approach. Taken together, the extensive violations of the proportional hazards assumption and the attenuation of many adjusted associations suggest that a more flexible modeling approach is required to capture the heterogeneity and cumulative nature of under-five mortality risk. We therefore turn to a random survival forest approach to derive an empirical risk ordering that does not rely on proportionality, linearity or additivity assumptions.

Empirical stratification of under-five mortality risk though RSF

Distribution of the RSF-based mortality risk score

Using the RSF model, we derived for each child a cumulative risk score summarizing the estimated probability of death before age 59 months (see supplementary material for more details about estimation process). This score provides a continuous ordering of children along a gradient of cumulative mortality risk. The distribution of the RSF-based risk score exhibits substantial heterogeneity across individuals, indicating wide dispersion in vulnerability to under-five mortality that is not captured by average covariate effects alone. Because the RSF was trained to capture relative differences in survival trajectories, the resulting score is interpreted as a relative risk index rather than as a calibrated population-level probability of death. Its primary function is to rank children according to their position along a continuum of mortality risk, thereby enabling the construction of empirical risk strata. Additional details on the construction and internal validation of the RSF risk score, including out-of-bag error estimation, model tuning, and variable importance measures, are provided in the supplementary material (Figures S.1-S.3). Figure S.3 summarizes the relative contribution of each covariate to the empirical construction of the mortality risk score.

Identification of empirical mortality risk strata

Ranking children according to the RSF-based cumulative risk score at 59 months reveals a continuous gradient of under-five mortality risk. To summarize this gradient in an interpretable and reproducible manner, the continuous risk score was initially partitioned into quartiles. This quantile-based categorization provides a simple, non-parametric descriptive framework for summarizing a continuous risk gradient without assuming the existence of natural clusters or latent classes. Similar quantile-based stratifications are commonly used in epidemiological and population health research to facilitate the interpretation of continuous risk gradients (Riyahifar et al., 2025). The resulting quartiles were then examined with respect to their survival trajectories, cumulative mortality, social and demographic composition. Because the first and second quartiles exhibited highly similar survival patterns, mortality levels, and social profiles, they were combined into a single low-risk stratum, whereas the third and fourth quartiles were retained as the intermediate- and high-risk strata, respectively.

Table 2 presents observed under-five mortality across the empirically defined risk strata, together with non-parametric estimates of the cumulative probability of death before age 59 months. The results reveal a differentiated mortality risk. Children in the low-risk stratum, who represent slightly more than half of the sample (51.3%), experience very low mortality levels, with a cumulative probability of death before age five of 2.1% (95% CI: 1.8-2.5). In contrast, mortality rises sharply in the intermediate-risk stratum, where children face a cumulative probability of death of 6.3% (95% CI: 5.5-7.2). The high-risk stratum exhibits dramatically elevated mortality: although it comprises only one quarter of the sample (25.0%), children in this group experience a cumulative probability of death before age five of 27.8% (95% CI: 26.3-29.3).

Social and demographic structure of mortality risk strata

Fig. 2 summarizes the social and demographic composition of the risk strata by highlighting characteristics that are overrepresented within each group relative to their prevalence in the full sample. The results reveal sharply differentiated and internally coherent configurations of vulnerability across strata. Children in the low-risk stratum are predominantly embedded in socially advantaged contexts. This group is characterized by a strong overrepresentation of higher maternal and partner education, higher maternal mass media exposure, as well as favorable household and community conditions. Children in this stratum are more likely to belong to wealthy households and to reside in communities with higher average levels of wealth and female education. Health-related characteristics are also prominent. Higher birth weight and a higher number of antenatal visits are all overrepresented in this group. The low-risk stratum further includes a higher proportion of urban residents and women with higher decision-making autonomy. Together, these features describe a configuration of cumulative advantage in which social, demographic, and health-related resources co-occur and translate into very low levels of under-five mortality.

The intermediate-risk stratum displays a mixed configuration combining selected socioeconomic resources with pronounced biological and reproductive vulnerabilities. Despite these advantages, children in the intermediate-risk stratum are disproportionately exposed to several high-risk early-life conditions. Low birth weight is overrepresented, alongside short preceding birth intervals and a higher prevalence of young motherhood. Limited use of maternal health services is also evident, as reflected by the overrepresentation of mothers reporting no antenatal visits. In addition, children in this stratum are more likely to live in households with a middle level of economic resources. This stratum highlights a form of partial vulnerability in which educational and informational resources coexist with adverse biological and reproductive conditions that substantially elevate mortality risk. The intermediate-risk stratum thus occupies a distinct position along the mortality continuum, illustrating that the presence of certain protective social characteristics does not fully offset early-life health disadvantages.

The high-risk stratum exhibits the most pronounced concentration of social, reproductive, and biological disadvantages. Compared to the intermediate-risk group, adverse early-life conditions are not only more prevalent but substantially amplified. Low birth weight is strongly overrepresented and appears at markedly higher levels than in the intermediate stratum, indicating severe early biological vulnerability. This stratum is also characterized by a strong overrepresentation of short preceding birth intervals, reflecting closely spaced fertility and limited maternal recovery between births. Maternal and partner education are consistently low, with no education being overrepresented for both parents, pointing to restricted access to health-related knowledge and resources. Health service utilization is particularly limited: mothers in the high-risk stratum are disproportionately likely to report no antenatal care and to give birth at home rather than in health facilities. In addition, women without any decision-making autonomy in the household are overrepresented, underscoring structural constraints in household power relations that may further restrict timely health-seeking behavior for both mothers and children. Taken together, these characteristics define a configuration of compounded vulnerability, in which biological risk factors, constrained reproductive conditions, and entrenched social disadvantage reinforce one another.

Unlike the intermediate-risk stratum, where some socioeconomic resources coexist with health-related vulnerabilities, the high-risk stratum reflects a convergence of disadvantages across multiple dimensions, corresponding to the highest observed probability of death before age five. Furthermore, although certain modalities are overrepresented in several risk strata, each stratum is distinguished by the relative extent of this overrepresentation. Thus, the strata are distinguished primarily by differences in the extent of overrepresentation rather than by mutually exclusive factors.

For more details on the representation gap of variable modalities for each profile, see supplementary material.

Finally, a comparison of risk strata between the 2011 and 2018 DHS waves reveals a strong persistence in the social structuring of under-five mortality similar to that presented above (see Appendix Fig A.1 and A.2). In both surveys, high-risk strata are consistently characterized by the accumulation of reproductive, educational, and health disadvantages, including low birth weight, short birth intervals, lack of maternal and partner education, absence of antenatal care, home delivery, and limited maternal decision-making autonomy. Conversely, low-risk strata in both waves concentrate protective resources related to parental education, access to health services, media exposure, and more favorable community contexts.

While the relative prominence of specific characteristics varies across survey waves, the overall configuration of vulnerability remains remarkably stable. These results indicate that under-five mortality in Cameroon is shaped by enduring and socially structured patterns of cumulative disadvantage, which persist over time despite changes in aggregate mortality levels and contextual conditions.

Discussion

This study adopted a person-centered approach to examine under-five mortality in Cameroon by identifying empirical strata of cumulative vulnerability using random survival forests. Rather than estimating the average contribution of individual determinants, the analysis characterized how multiple social, reproductive, biological, and contextual disadvantages combine to produce differentiated mortality risks. Three distinct strata emerged, with children in the highest-risk stratum experiencing an estimated probability of death before age five of 27.8%, compared with only 2.1% among children in the lowest-risk stratum. These findings demonstrate that under-five mortality in Cameroon is strongly socially stratified and that mortality inequalities are better understood as configurations of cumulative disadvantage than as the independent effects of isolated risk factors. Furthermore, despite a decline in mortality among children under five between 2011 and 2018, we found that the social structure of vulnerability across strata remained remarkably stable across survey waves, suggesting that progress in child survival occurred without fundamentally altering the distribution of mortality risk across social groups.

The characteristics defining the high-risk stratum are highly consistent with previous research on child mortality in Cameroon and sub-Saharan Africa. Earlier studies have shown that low birth weight, short preceding birth intervals, limited maternal education, inadequate antenatal care, and poor household socioeconomic conditions are among the strongest correlates of under-five mortality (Adjiwanou & Engdaw, 2017; Debere & Adjiwanou, 2024; Kravdal, 2018; Mounchingam & Adjiwanou, 2025; Yaya et al., 2020). Our findings reinforce this evidence while extending it in an important way. Rather than demonstrating that these factors independently increase mortality risk, the present study shows that they are overrepresented within the empirical strata associated with the highest mortality risk. This suggests that elevated mortality is socially structured around populations in which multiple disadvantages are more prevalent. This also supports the study by Kamal et al. (2020) on Indian states with high under-five mortality. These authors demonstrated that infant mortality can be divided into distinct empirical subgroups, defined by the cumulative and synergistic combination of modifiable risk factors.

The analysis also highlights the central role of gender-related inequalities in structuring child survival. The overrepresentation of women with limited decision-making autonomy, low educational attainment, and restricted access to maternal healthcare within the highest-risk stratum is consistent with previous studies emphasizing that women's social position influences children's survival through health-seeking behaviours and access to protective resources (Adjiwanou & LeGrand, 2014; Boehmer & Williamson, 1996; Shen & Williamson, 1997). Likewise, recent studies conducted in sub-Saharan Africa have shown that maternal autonomy, education, and attitudes toward gender norms are closely associated with under-five mortality (Birhanie et al., 2025; Mounchingam & Adjiwanou, 2025; Yaya et al., 2020). Our findings suggest that these gender-related dimensions coexist within broader patterns of cumulative social disadvantage. They are also consistent with ecosocial theory (Krieger, 2001), which views health inequalities as the embodiment of socially structured conditions. From this perspective, these results reflect distinct distributions of social and demographic characteristics associated with different levels of mortality risk.

Another important finding concerns the intermediate-risk stratum. Although children in this group benefit from relatively favorable educational and informational resources, they remain characterized by adverse biological and reproductive conditions, particularly low birth weight, short birth intervals, young motherhood, and limited antenatal care. This configuration illustrates that educational resources alone may not be sufficient to offset early-life biological vulnerabilities. Such findings are consistent with previous demographic research showing that child survival results from interactions between socioeconomic resources and reproductive conditions rather than from either dimension considered independently (Debere & Adjiwanou, 2024; Kravdal, 2018).

Beyond substantive findings, this study also contributes methodologically. Most previous studies on under-five mortality have relied on variable-centered regression models that estimate average covariate effects (Debere & Adjiwanou, 2024; Mounchingam & Adjiwanou, 2025). While these approaches have been fundamental for identifying major determinants of child mortality, they provide limited insight into the cumulative organization of vulnerability across individuals. By combining random survival forests with Kaplan-Meier estimation, our approach makes mortality heterogeneity empirically observable through socially interpretable risk strata. This perspective complements, rather than replaces, conventional regression approaches by shifting attention from isolated predictors to configurations of co-occurring disadvantages.

This perspective is also consistent with demographic theories emphasizing heterogeneity in mortality processes. Frailty models and multilevel survival models have long recognized that mortality risks differ substantially across individuals and social environments (Hougaard, 1995; Sastry, 1997; Vaupel et al., 1979; Vaupel & Yashin, 1985). However, such approaches generally represent heterogeneity through unobserved statistical components. The present analysis demonstrates that an important part of this heterogeneity can be characterized empirically through observable combinations of reproductive, socioeconomic, gender-related, and contextual characteristics. In this respect, the identified strata provide a representation of the socially structured vulnerability underlying mortality inequalities.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis relies on retrospective birth histories, which may be subject to recall bias and misreporting, particularly for early neonatal deaths. Because mortality in the high-risk stratum is heavily concentrated during the neonatal period, any underreporting or misdating of these early deaths could have a proportionally greater impact on the estimated mortality level for this stratum. Second, although the stratification reveals patterns of cumulative vulnerability, the resulting strata are not causal typologies and should not be interpreted as identifying mechanisms of effect. Future research could extend this approach by examining transitions between risk strata over time with real longitudinal data, mapping the spatial distribution of vulnerability strata using DHS GPS cluster coordinates, and integrating contextual variables at finer spatial scales beyond the cluster-level proxies used in the present study. Applying similar stratification strategies to other health outcomes or age groups could also help assess the generalizability of person-centered survival approaches in demographic research.

Conclusion

This study identified three empirical strata of under-five mortality risk in Cameroon using a person-centered approach based on survival analysis. The results revealed marked differences in mortality across these strata, with the estimated probability of death before age five increasing from 2.1% in the low-risk stratum to 27.8% in the high-risk stratum. The identified strata were characterized by distinct social, reproductive, and healthcare categories. The highest-risk stratum was distinguished by the overrepresentation of children with low birth weight and short birth intervals, as well as by the overrepresentation of mothers and households characterized by lower educational attainment, limited use of antenatal care, home delivery, reduced media exposure, and lower decision-making autonomy. These findings indicate that under-five mortality in Cameroon is socially stratified and that the populations facing the greatest mortality risk are also those in which multiple disadvantages are disproportionately represented.

Methodologically, this study demonstrates that survival-based machine learning can be used as an exploratory tool to derive empirically interpretable mortality risk strata. Rather than replacing conventional regression models, this approach complements variable-centered analyses by providing a population-level representation of heterogeneity in mortality risk.

From a public health perspective, these findings suggest that interventions aimed at reducing under-five mortality should consider the concentration of disadvantages within high-risk population strata rather than addressing individual risk factors in isolation. Future research could examine the stability of these empirical vulnerability strata in other national settings and assess their usefulness for identifying populations that may benefit most from integrated maternal and child health intervention.
