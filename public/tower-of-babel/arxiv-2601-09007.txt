Global polynomial-time estimation in statistical nonlinear inverse problems via generalized stability
Sven Wang

Abstract
Non-linear statistical inverse problems pose major challenges both for statistical analysis and computation. Likelihood-based estimators typically lead to non-convex and possibly multimodal optimization landscapes, and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods may mix exponentially slowly. We propose a class of computationally tractable estimators—plug-in and PDE-penalized M-estimators—for inverse problems defined through operator equations of the form L f ​ u = g L_{f}u=g , where f f is the unknown parameter and u u is the observed solution. The key idea is to replace the exact PDE constraint by a weakly enforced relaxation, yielding conditionally convex and, in many PDE examples, nested quadratic optimization problems that avoid evaluating the forward map G ⁡ ( f ) G(f) and do not require PDE solvers. For prototypical non-linear inverse problems arising from elliptic PDEs, including the Darcy flow model L f u = ∇ ⋅ ( f ∇ u ) L_{f}u=\nabla\!\cdot(f\nabla u) and a steady-state Schrödinger model, we prove that these estimators attain the best currently known statistical convergence rates while being globally computable in polynomial time. In the Darcy model, we obtain an explicit sub-quadratic o ⁡ ( N 2 ) o(N^{2}) arithmetic runtime bound for estimating f f from N N noisy samples. Our analysis is based on new generalized stability estimates, extending classical stability beyond the range of the forward operator, combined with tools from nonparametric M-estimation. We also derive adaptive rates for the Darcy problem, providing a blueprint for designing provably polynomial-time statistical algorithms for a broad class of non-linear inverse problems. Our estimators also provide principled warm-start initializations for polynomial-time Bayesian computation.

1.1
Generalized M-estimators

Before giving an overview of the main results, let us explain the intuition underlying the proposed estimation procedures, which we shall refer to as
generalized M-estimators
. The estimators can be motivated using two very simple ideas for modifying the non-convex (possibly intractable) likelihood landscape (
2
). These ideas consist of (i) the
augmentation of the parameter
and (ii) a certain
weak formulation
of the PDE constraint defining the map
\mathcal{G}
. We will consider forward maps
\mathcal{G}
which are defined implicitly by an operator equation

L_{f}[u]=g\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ +\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{boundary conditions},

(3)

where
g
is some known source term and
(L_{f}:f\in\mathcal{F})
represents a family of (differential) operators. Suppose that for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{G}(f)
is given as the unique solution
u
of (
3
). This setting includes many prototypical parameter inference problems for PDEs—such as the Darcy flow problem where
L_{f}u=\nabla\cdot(f\nabla u)
and the steady-state Schrödinger model where
L_{f}u=\Delta u/2-fu
, as well as several other non-linear PDE examples such as the McKean-Vlasov and reaction-diffusion equations discussed further below.

In place of considering the likelihood landscape
f\mapsto\ell_{N}(f)
, we instead decouple the regression function
u
(observed solution of the PDE) from the statistical parameter
f
, and to link the pair
(u,f)
via an additional term in the objective.
A first (naive) application of this principle would result in a minimization problem

\text{minimize}\penalty\ \penalty\ J(f,u)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}[Y_{i}-u(X_{i})]^{2}+\lambda^{2}\|u-\mathcal{G}(f)\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}+\mathcal{R}(u,f).

(4)

Here and below,
\lambda>0
is some regularisation parameter and
\mathcal{R}
represents some (e.g. Tikhonov-type) complexity penalty. The
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
-norm was chosen here in resemblance of the information-theoretic Kullback-Leibler divergence
KL(f_{1},f_{2})\approx\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{G}(f_{1})-\mathcal{G}(f_{2})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
(with equality holding for uniformly sampled
X_{i}
and
\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,1)
). The decoupling permits to further modify the ‘PDE penalty’
\|u-\mathcal{G}(f)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
, which operates on the level of the regression functions, to
|\!|\!|L_{f}u-g|\!|\!|
for some suitable norm
|\!|\!|\cdot|\!|\!|
.

Method I: PDE-penalized M-estimator.

\text{minimize}\penalty\ \penalty\ J(f,u)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-u(X_{i}))^{2}+\lambda^{2}|\!|\!|L_{f}u-g|\!|\!|^{2}+\mathcal{R}(u,f).

(5)

The idea of minimizing a PDE penalty to circumvent potentially costly evaluations of PDE solvers has appeared in key methodologies, notably in physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)
[
50
]
, so-called ‘all-at-once’ formulations of inverse problems
[
31
]
, optimal control
[
62
]
and PDE-constrained regression
[
26
]
and ‘calming’ techniques in nonparametric statistics
[
57
]
, see below for more discussion.

The second estimation procedure studied in this paper is given by yet another simplification of (
5
), which we term
plug-in M-estimation
. Here one further decomposes the minimization task into two nested problems:

Method II: Plug-in M-estimator.

\begin{split}\hat{u}_{N}\in\argmin_{u\in\mathcal{U}}J_{1}(u),&\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ J_{1}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-u(X_{i}))^{2}+\mathcal{R}_{1}(u),\\
\hat{f}_{N}\in\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}}J_{2}(f),&\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ J_{2}(f):=\lambda^{2}|\!|\!|L_{f}\hat{u}_{N}-g|\!|\!|^{2}+\mathcal{R}_{2}(f).\end{split}

(6)

One can, of course, think of the the plug-in M-estimator entirely independently of the above motivation. In fact, the ‘plug-in principle’ of first performing a pure regression or ‘data smoothing’ step then an inversion step has appeared in the literature, see the paper
[
7
]
for linear inverse problems as well as the paper
[
32
]
discussed futher below. The key point is that the non-linear inversion itself is formulated as a computationally tractable
minimization of the PDE-constraint
. In light of this, our motivation provided above gives a unified way of thinking about Methods I and II as
generalized M-estimators
. We also note that the preliminary estimator
\hat{u}_{N}
of
\mathcal{G}(f_{0})
need not necessarily arise from a penalized least squares method, but could arise from other nonparametric regression methods.

The formulations (
5
) and (
6
) are computationally attractive from several points of view. The first obvious reason is that the minimizations of (
5
)-(
6
) no longer require evaluations of
\mathcal{G}(f)
(which likelihood-based methods require), circumventing possibly expensive access to PDE solvers. Secondly, in many key examples such as the Darcy and Schrödinger models,
(f,u)\mapsto L_{f}u
is bi-linear, whence the objective (
5
) is
conditionally quadratic
. The plug-in method (
6
) becomes a nested quadratic problem. The third reason relates to the range of functions
f
over which optimization is permitted. Often the map
\mathcal{G}(f)
—which involves the
inversion
of the differential operator
L_{f}
—is only well-defined on a non-linearly constrained subset
\mathcal{F}
(e.g., non-negative functions). In contrast, the differential operator
L_{f}u
is typically well-defined for
arbitrary
sufficiently smooth functions
f
, which permits formulating (
5
) as an unconstrained minimization problem. This will be convenient in the non-linear problems considered here, see Sections
1.2
and
2
.

. One can similarly also give polynomial-time guarantees for the computation of Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) estimators, but we refrain from doing so here.

1.5
Discussion and comparison with related literature

1.5.1
Statistical convergence guarantees

There have been several lines of work investigating the statistical convergence properties of regularization-based and Bayesian inference procedures in non-linear inverse problems. The papers
[
42
]
and
[
39
]
gave general recipes for deriving convergence rates for MAP estimators as well as posterior contraction rates with Gaussian priors, see also
[
1
,
22
,
67
]
for related results. Another line of work has investigated the nonparametric
Bernstein-von Mises
phenomenon, aiming to establish asymptotic normality of the posterior distribution; we refer to
[
47
,
40
]
as well as
[
43
]
for more references. While such results have been well-understood in the Schrödinger equation model, in the Darcy model fundamental difficulties arise relating to the range properties of the ‘information operator’
D\mathcal{G}_{f_{0}}^{*}D\mathcal{G}_{f_{0}}

[
43
]
. The best known convergence rates derived in the above-mentioned works, for the Darcy model, are given by

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}]&\lesssim N^{-\frac{2(\alpha-1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{(inverse problem)},\\
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathcal{G}(\hat{f}_{N})-\mathcal{G}(f_{0})\|_{L^{2}}^{2}]&\lesssim N^{-\frac{2(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{(forward problem)},\end{split}

where
\alpha
represents the smoothness of
f_{0}
. For the forward problem, the rate can be shown to be minimax-optimal
[
42
]
. These rates are matched by all the methods proposed in the current paper—see the Theorems
1.1
and
2.1
,
2.4
below. For the Schrödinger model, our methods achieve minimax-optimal convergence rates both for the forward and the inverse problem, see Theorem
2.9
below.

The paper
[
32
]
pursues a related plug-in principle in a Bayesian framework: it first derives posterior contraction for forward quantities (in particular
u
and its derivatives) and then recovers
f
via an explicit inversion step based on classical numerical ideas
[
53
,
54
]
, which in practice amounts to solving a transport PDE. Their development largely focuses on
d=2
and hinges on structural assumptions such as
\mathcal{G}(f_{0})
lying in an iterated eigenspace of the prior covariance operator. In contrast, our approach avoids explicit inversion via transport/characteristics: we formulate the recovery of
f
through a PDE-penalty and leverage generalized stability, which yields nested quadratic optimization problems. This yields explicit, non-asymptotic
o(N^{2})
runtime guarantees for the discretized estimators (cf. Theorem
2.4
), whereas obtaining comparable end-to-end complexity guarantees for transport-based inversion would require a separate analysis of the full numerical pipeline with hyperbolic PDEs.

1.5.2
Existing computational guarantees and MCMC mixing times

There has been an extensive literature developing on high-dimensional Bayesian computation in recent years. In particular, there has been a surge of interest in high-dimensional log-concave sampling
[
16
,
18
]
and infinite-dimensional mixing of MCMC algorithms
[
23
]
. However, in the present context non-linear inverse problems, the ‘Gibbs measure’
\Pi(\cdot|Z^{(N)})
is non-convex and computational guarantees have remained a core challenge. As discussed in Section
1.4
, a line of work starting from the paper
[
46
]
(see
[
2
,
8
,
43
]
) has derived several theoretical computational guarantees for Langevin-type Markov Chains upon warm-start. In the Schrödinger model, even a global polynomial-time computation guarantee was shown in
[
46
]
; albeit using completely different proof techniques from ours. The techniques there rely on so-called ‘gradient stability’ arguments for the linerization
D\mathcal{G}
of the forward operator to assert local log-concavity of the expected likelihood function, and then utilize concentration-of-measure arguments from empirical process theory
[
59
]
to assert the local log-concavity of
\ell_{N}
with high probability. While this approach achieved the first
qualitative
polynomial-time results, it seemed rather difficult to make the exponents in the runtime guarantees explicit. Moreover, the computational feasibility seems to be reliant on the occurrence of a high-probability event, which may only have significant probability for large
N
. Thirdly, due to the aforementioned concentration-of-measure arguments, extremely strong regularity assumptions (e.g.,
d\leq 3
and
\alpha\geq 22
) are required—this can be seen in our polynomial-time MCMC Theorem
2.7
, which builds upon those references. One of the main goals of this work was to relax those stringent assumptions. In line with this, the computational runtime of our statistical estimators have explicit (sub-quadratic) exponents and is
deterministic
, taking a step towards more practical algorithmic guarantees. However, it is not yet clear whether our estimators can achieve (semi-parametrically) efficient uncertainty quantification
[
66
,
43
]
, see Section
4
for more comments.

We also mention here the classical regularization-based literature which has considered (penalized) least squares schemes—typically in a context of deterministic noise, see, e.g.
[
27
,
41
,
24
,
25
,
30
]
. Similarly to the statistical setting here, the algorithmic guarantees developed in this literature are typically limited by the non-convexity of the involved least-squares functionals, and are thus limited to local ‘warm-start’ guarantees; we refer to
[
30
]
for an overview.

1.5.3
PINN, PDE-regularized methods, ‘all-at-once’ formulations

The idea of penalizing the deviation from the ‘physics’ prescribed by the defining equation
L_{f}=g
is, by now, common in many key methodologies in applied mathematics and scientific computing. Related to our Method I (
5
) are the ‘all-at-once’ formulations of inverse problems
[
31
]
which also utilize the idea of augmenting the optimization space. The most notable recent methodology using this are physics-informed neural networks (PINN)
[
51
,
17
]
. While these methodologies were initially aimed at solving (potentially high-dimensional) PDEs, they have recently also been extended to the solution of inverse problems, we refer the reader to
[
15
]
for an overview. Previous to PINNs, similar ideas have appeared e.g. in optimal control of PDEs
[
62
]
and in PDE-regularized regression
[
56
]
, albeit usually not targeting inverse problems. For the above methodologies, some statistical consistency theory has gradually developed over recent years—see, e.g.,
[
3
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
37
]
. However, at least for PINN-type methodologies, the question of rigorous statistical-algorithmic guarantees yet seems out of reach—due to the highly non-linear neural network parameterization of functional parameters there—and is a highly ambitious research future direction.

1.5.4
Non-linear PDEs

We conclude the introduction with a brief discussion of the applicability of our theory to even more complicated non-linear inverse problems. Methodologically, our methods can be applied to any inverse problem given by
\mathcal{L}_{f}[u]=g
, including the case when the defining PDE is
non-linear
, in the sense that
L_{f}
is a non-linear differential operator. In cases where the mapping
(f,u)\mapsto L_{f}u
is still linear in the
f
-variable, both steps of the plug-in estimator constitute quadratic optimisation problems, and thus are computationally tractable. This includes the case of inferring the interaction potential in McKean-Vlasov equations
[
44
]
, and inferring the non-linear ‘reaction function’ in reaction-diffusion type equations arising in physics, chemistry and biology. In these models, traditional likelihood-based and Bayes methods are even more expensive that in the elliptic PDE examples discussed here, and it would be of significant interest to derive statistical guarantees via generalized stability estimates for plug-in methods in these non-linear models. In contrast, there is a class of non-linear inverse problems where the parameter
f
to be inferred is not defined on the same spatial (or spatio-temporal) domain as the regression measurements, whence it seems challenging to directly apply our ideas. Notably, this includes inference problems on the initial condition in data assimilation of (deterministic) dynamical systems
[
45
,
48
]
and non-linear X-ray transform problems
[
49
]
with boundary measurements.

Some notation

We fix some notation used throughout. For an open set
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote by

the standard
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
–Sobolev spaces of functions with square-integrable
weak derivatives up to order
\alpha
. Moreover,
C(\mathcal{O})
and

denote the spaces of continuous functions on

\mathcal{O}
and
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
, respectively, equipped with the supremum norm.
For integers
k\geq 1
,

denotes the space of
k

differentiable functions for which the supremum norm of all partial derivatives up to order

is finite,

\|u\|_{C^{k}(\mathcal{O})}:=\max_{|\beta|\leq k}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{O}}|\partial^{\beta}u(x)|.

For non-integer
\alpha>0
,

denotes the usual Hölder spaces. Finally, we write
\nabla
,

, and

for the gradient, divergence, and Laplacian operators.

2
Main results for PDE models

This section contains our main findings for two prototypical PDE model examples, the
Darcy problem
and the
steady-state Schrödinger equation
. In both cases, the forward map
\mathcal{G}
arises as a parameter-to-solution operator of an elliptic PDE where the infinite-dimensional statistical parameter space
\mathcal{F}
is assumed to consist of Sobolev-regular functions. Throughout, let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote a bounded, smooth (open) domain with smooth boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}
, assumed without loss of generality to have unit Lebesgue measure,
\text{vol}(\mathcal{O})=1
. For integer
\alpha>0
,
f_{min}>0
and
R>0
, we denote

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}(\alpha,f_{min},R):=\big\{f\in H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O}):f\geq f_{min},\penalty\ \|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})}\leq R\big\}.

(9)

In the Darcy model, the lower bound
f_{min}>0
ensures uniform ellipticity, while the smoothness level
\alpha
will determine the convergence rates achieved. The ensuing theory requires
\alpha>d/2
in order to guarantee sufficient regularity for the PDE analysis—in this case, the Sobolev embedding

H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow C^{\eta}(\mathcal{O})
for any
\eta\in(0,\alpha-d/2)

ensures that functions in
\mathcal{F}
admit pointwise representatives. Throughout this section, we consider the random design regression model

Y_{i}=\mathcal{G}(f)(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},\qquad X_{i}\sim^{i.i.d.}\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{O}),\quad\varepsilon_{i}\sim^{i.i.d.}N(0,1),\quad 1\leq i\leq N,

(10)

where
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
and
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
are independent. While our techniques extend to more general sampling distributions
X_{i}\sim P_{X}

and to broader noise classes (e.g. sub-Gaussian
\varepsilon_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
),
we restrict attention to the present setting for clarity.

Guided by the intuition developed in Section
1.1
,
we establish theoretical guarantees for both the PDE-penalized M-estimators defined in (
5
) and the corresponding plug-in estimators from (
6
); see Theorem
2.1
.
A central result is Theorem
2.4
, which shows that a high-dimensional wavelet discretization of the plug-in estimator attains the best currently known statistical convergence rates while remaining computable in sub-quadratic time. Further computational implications of these results, including polynomial-time
sampling guarantees, are discussed in Section
2.3
, while adaptive rates are derived in Section
2.4
. These results focus on the Darcy model. Analogous statements for the steady-state Schrödinger equation are discussed more briefly, due to space constraints, in Section
2.5
.

2.1
Darcy flow: infinite-dimensional estimators

Let
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
be fixed. For any conductivity
f:\mathcal{O}\to(0,\infty)

satisfying
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{O}}f(x)>0
and
f\in C^{1+\eta}(\bar{\mathcal{O}})

for some
\eta>0
, the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}\nabla\cdot(f\nabla u)=g&\text{in }\mathcal{O},\\
u=0&\text{on }\partial\mathcal{O},\end{cases}

(11)

admits a unique classical solution
u_{f}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{O})\cap C(\bar{\mathcal{O}})

by standard elliptic theory
[
20
, Theorem 6.14]
.
We denote by
\mathcal{G}(f)=u_{f}
the associated parameter-to-solution map. Whenever
\alpha>d/2+1
and
f\in\mathcal{F}(\alpha,f_{\min},R)
,
the Sobolev embedding

H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow C^{1+\eta}(\mathcal{O})
for

\eta\in(0,\alpha-d/2-1)

ensures that
\mathcal{G}(f)
is well-defined for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

The following theorem considers two estimation procedures for the Darcy model.
The first is the PDE-penalized M-estimator introduced in (
5
), with suitably chosen Tikhonov penalties. Writing
L_{f}u=\nabla\cdot(f\nabla u)
, we define the PDE-penalized M-estimator as any measurable minimizer of the functional

\begin{split}J(u,f)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[Y_{i}-u(X_{i})\big]^{2}+\lambda_{N}^{2}\|L_{f}u-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}+\mu_{N}^{2}\Big(\|u\|_{H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}+\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}\Big),\end{split}

(12)

over
(u,f)\in H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O})\times H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})
,
with regularization parameters

\lambda_{N}=N^{-\frac{2}{2(\alpha+1)+d}},\qquad\mu_{N}=N^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}.

(13)

The second estimator is the two-stage plug-in M-estimator from (
6
). Specifically, it is given by

\begin{split}\hat{u}_{N}&\in\argmin_{u\in H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O})}\Big\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big[Y_{i}-u(X_{i})\big]^{2}+\mu_{N}^{2}\|u\|_{H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}\Big\},\\
\hat{f}_{N}&\in\argmin_{f\in H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})}\Big\{\|\nabla\cdot(f\nabla\hat{u}_{N})-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}\Big\},\end{split}

(14)

where
\mu_{N}
is as in (
13
) and
\nu_{N}=N^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}.
Note that in (
14
), both optimization problems are strictly convex (in fact, quadratic) and therefore admit unique
solutions.

Importantly, both formulations allow the estimator of
u_{0}=\mathcal{G}(f_{0})

to lie outside the range of the forward operator
\mathcal{G}
, and they are formulated as unconstrained problems over the
linear spaces

H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})
and
H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O})
, respectively, in particular neither enforcing the positivity constraint of
f>0
nor the range constraint
u\in\{\mathcal{G}(f):f\in\mathcal{F}\}
. A priori, it is not evident that such a procedure should achieve the same statistical guarantees as likelihood-based approaches

[
42
,
43
,
22
]
.
As explained in Section
1.3
, this is owing to the generalized
stability properties of the Darcy problem which we will state below in Lemma
2.2
. We now state the main convergence result for both estimators, which will be proven in Section
B
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
and suppose
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a smooth, bounded domain. Let
\alpha>d/2+1
and
\alpha\geq 3
. Fix constants
f_{min}>0,\penalty\ R>0
. Suppose that the data (
10
) arise from some
f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}(\alpha,f_{min},R)
and let
(\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N})
denote either the generalized M-estimator from (
12
) or the plug-in M-estimator from (
14
). There exists
C>0
only depending on
\mathcal{O},d,\alpha,f_{min},R
such that for all
N\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f_{0}}^{N}\big[\|\hat{u}_{N}-\mathcal{G}(f_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq CN^{-\frac{2(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}

(15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{f_{0}}^{N}\big[\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq CN^{-\frac{2(\alpha-1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}.

(16)

Two convergence rates are established in Theorem
2.1
.
The first concerns the forward problem of estimating
\mathcal{G}(f_{0})

and coincides with the minimax rate for estimating
(\alpha+1)
-smooth regression
functions. It is shown in
[
42
]
that this rate is optimal, in a minimax sense,
over the model class
\{u_{f}:f\in\mathcal{F}(\alpha,f_{\min},R)\}
. The second rate addresses the inverse problem of recovering the conductivity
f_{0}
.
At a heuristic level, this rate corresponds to the minimax estimation rate in a
2
-ill-posed inverse problem where the observed signal is
\alpha+1
-smooth. Indeed, the inverse map
\mathcal{G}^{-1}:u\mapsto f
is known to
satisfy a
2
-ill-posed stability estimate: for sufficiently smooth and uniformly
elliptic coefficients
f_{1},f_{2}
, one has

\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\;\lesssim\;\|u_{f_{1}}-u_{f_{2}}\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{O})},

see, for instance, Lemma 24 in
[
42
]
. Similar stability estimates appear in
earlier works, such as
[
53
]
for
L^{\infty}
–
C^{2}
bounds and

[
33
]
for one-dimensional
L^{2}
estimates. The central analytical insight underlying
Theorem
2.1
is that suitable stability estimates continue to hold
even when the PDE constraint is satisfied only approximately. The following lemma makes this
generalized stability
phenomenon precise.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth, bounded domain and let
f_{min},g_{min}>0
. Suppose that for
i\in\{1,2\}
, we have functions

u_{i}\in H^{2}(\mathcal{O}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ f_{i}\in C^{1}(\mathcal{O}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ g_{i}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O})\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{with}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \nabla\cdot(f_{i}\nabla u_{i})=g_{i}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{on}\penalty\ \mathcal{O}.

Assume that the first triplet
(u_{1},f_{1},g_{1})
additionally satisfies
u_{1}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{O})\cap C(\bar{\mathcal{O}})
,
g_{1}\in C(\mathcal{O})
and

\inf_{x\in\mathcal{O}}f_{1}(x)\geq f_{min}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \inf_{x\in\mathcal{O}}g_{1}(x)\geq g_{min},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ u_{1}|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}=0.

Then, for every
B>0
there exists a constant
C>0
solely depending on
f_{min},g_{min},\mathcal{O}
and
B
such that whenever
\|f_{1}\|_{C^{1}}\vee\|u_{1}\|_{C^{2}}\leq B
, it holds that

\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq C\big(\|f_{2}\|_{C^{1}}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{H^{2}}+\|g_{1}-g_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\big).

A key feature of Lemma
2.2
is the
asymmetry
of its hypotheses. The main structural assumptions (uniform ellipticity, Dirichlet condition, and lower bound on
g_{1}
) are imposed only on the reference triplet
(u_{1},f_{1},g_{1})
, while
(u_{2},f_{2},g_{2})
is unrestricted apart from basic regularity. Due to boundedness properties of the forward map
\mathcal{G}
, it turns out that the constant
B\geq\|f_{1}\|_{C^{1}}\vee\|u_{f_{1}}\|_{C^{2}}
can be chosen uniformly over triplets
g_{1}=g
,
u_{1}=\mathcal{G}(f_{0})
and
f_{1}=f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
with
\alpha>d/2+1
representing the data-generating ground truth. Moreover, we note that the proof of Theorem
1.1
in fact yields stronger (sub-Gaussian) concentration inequalities for suitable
penalized empirical risk functionals—see the proof for details.

2.2
Darcy flow: high-dimensional estimator

In this section, we present the high-dimensional estimator which underlies Theorem
1.1
. The estimator arises from a high-dimensional wavelet frame discretization of the infinite-dimensional plug-in scheme (
14
). Wavelets are chosen over alternative representation systems, such as Dirichlet Laplacian eigenfunctions, in order to accurately approximate functions in
H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O})
up to the boundary
\partial\mathcal{O}
. The main result of this section is Theorem
2.4
, asserting that the estimator is computable in sub-quadratic runtime, while possessing the same statistical convergence properties as its infinite-dimensional counterpart from Theorem
2.1
.

Before stating our main results, we clarify the following about the computation model used in this section.

Remark 2.3
(Computation model).

The explicit complexity bounds in Theorem
2.4
count floating-point operations associated with matrix–vector products and linear system solves. We assume oracle access to point evaluations and
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
inner products of wavelet basis functions and their derivatives, which may be precomputed to high precision. Moreover, we upper bound computational costs by that of dense linear algebra; exploiting additional sparse structure of
\Phi
or
\Psi
could further reduce runtime, but this is not pursued here. This computation model refines previously used notions of computation time, such as MCMC iteration complexity used in
[
46
]
as well as in Section
2.3
below, which presume precise oracle access to evaluations
\mathcal{G}(f)
of the forward map.

Wavelet frames and preliminaries

We introduce some notation and basic facts about the wavelet frames which will be used throughout. For a comprehensive treatment of this theory, we refer the reader to
[
61
]
. As before, let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote a smooth, bounded domain. For some
u>\alpha+1
, we denote by

\big\{\phi_{lk}:l\geq 0,1\leq k\leq N_{l}\big\},

a wavelet frame of
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
consisting of wavelets with smoothness at least
C^{u}(\mathcal{O})
in the sense of Definition 5.25 in
[
61
]
; the existence of such a wavelet system is ensured by Theorem 5.51 of
[
61
]
. At ‘resolution level’
l\geq 0
, there are at most
N_{l}\lesssim 2^{ld}
elements. Given any multi-scale vector

v=(v_{lk}:l\geq 0,\penalty\ 1\leq k\leq N_{l}),

we can then define the following sequence norms

\|v\|_{h^{\alpha}}^{2}:=\sum_{l\geq 0}\sum_{k\leq N_{l}}2^{2l\alpha}v_{lk}^{2},\penalty\ \penalty\ \alpha\geq 0,

with
h^{\alpha}
denoting the space of all sequences where the norm is finite. A key property of the above wavelet frame is that
\|\cdot\|_{h^{\alpha}}
characterizes the classical Sobolev norms. Indeed, for any
f\in H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})
, it holds that

\inf_{v}\|v\|_{h^{\alpha}}\simeq\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})},

(17)

where the infimum runs over all admissible representations
f=\sum_{l,k}v_{lk}\phi_{lk}
, see Theorem 5.51 of
[
61
]
(with
p=q=2
). The infimum on the left hand side is necessary since the
(\phi_{lk})
may not form a basis, whence the representation of
f
may not be unique. However, there exists a bounded and linear operator
S:H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})\to h^{\alpha}
which selects a representation such that

\|S(f)\|_{h^{\alpha}}\simeq\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ f=\sum_{l,k}S(f)_{lk}\phi_{lk},

see Theorem 5.51 in
[
61
]
. We denote the selection operator for the
(l,k)
-th coefficient by

S_{lk}:H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathbb{R},\penalty\ \penalty\ S_{lk}(f)=S(f)_{lk}.

Throughout, we fix one such selection operator. We also need finite-dimensional truncations of the above wavelet spaces. For resolution level
J\geq 1
, we denote the subspace with frequencies up to level
J
by

L^{2}_{J}(\mathcal{O})=\text{span}(\phi_{lk},\penalty\ l\leq J,\penalty\ k\leq N_{l})\subseteq L^{2}(\mathcal{O}),

which has dimension

\text{dim}(L^{2}_{J}(\mathcal{O}))\leq p_{J}:=\sum_{l\leq J}N_{l}\lesssim 2^{Jd},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ J\geq 1.

In slight abuse of notation, we will write
v\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}
for the Euclidean space of finite-resolution multi-scale vectors, and

\|v\|_{h^{\alpha}_{J}}^{2}:=\sum_{l\leq J,k\leq N_{l}}2^{2l\alpha}v_{lk}^{2},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \alpha\geq 0,\penalty\ J\geq 1,\penalty\ v\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}.

2.2.1
Construction of
\hat{u}_{N}

Let
\mu_{N}=N^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}
denote the minimax rate of estimation for
(\alpha+1)
-smooth functions. At sample size
N\geq 1
, we choose the resolution level
J=J_{N}
such that

2^{Jd}\simeq N\mu_{N}^{2}=N^{\frac{d}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}.

This choice balances the approximation error of the wavelet projection with the stochastic error of estimating
p_{J}\asymp 2^{Jd}
coefficients for an
\alpha+1
-regular function. The estimator
\hat{u}_{N}
will be given as

\hat{u}_{N}:=\sum_{l\leq J,k\leq N_{l}}\hat{\eta}_{lk}\phi_{lk}\in H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O}),

(18)

where
\hat{\eta}_{N}
is the (unique) minimizer of the quadratic functional

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(Y_{i}-\sum_{l\leq J,k\leq N_{l}}\eta_{lk}\,\phi_{lk}(X_{i})\Big)^{2}+\mu_{N}^{2}\|\eta\|_{h^{\alpha+1}_{J}}^{2}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{over}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \eta\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}.

In matrix form, we can write this as a high-dimensional penalized least squares problem

\hat{\eta}_{N}=\argmin_{\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\big\|Y-\Phi\eta\big\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{2}+\mu_{N}^{2}\eta^{T}\Lambda^{\alpha+1}\eta\Big),

(19)

where
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}
denotes the Euclidean norm,
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p_{J}}
is the ‘design matrix’ with entries
\Phi_{i,(l,k)}=\phi_{lk}(X_{i})

and
\Lambda
is the
p_{J}\times p_{J}
diagonal matrix representing the
h^{\alpha+1}
-norms,

\Lambda:=\text{diag}\big(1\dots\underbrace{2^{2l}\dots 2^{2l}}_{N_{l}\penalty\ \text{times}}\dots 2^{2J}\dots 2^{2J}\big).

(20)

From the formulation, one sees immediately that (
19
) indeed has a unique minimizer
\hat{\eta}_{N}
—since
\Lambda
is strictly positive definite—given by

\hat{\eta}_{N}=\big(\Phi^{T}\Phi+N\mu_{N}^{2}\Lambda^{\alpha+1}\big)^{-1}\Phi^{T}Y.

(21)

Since
p_{J}\asymp 2^{Jd}\asymp N\mu_{N}^{2}=N^{\frac{d}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}
,
the cost of forming
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
and solving the resulting linear system involving the inversion of the inversion of
(\Phi^{T}\Phi+\mu_{N}^{2}\Lambda^{\alpha+1})
(assuming dense linear algebra) is bounded by

O(Np_{J}^{2}+p_{J}^{3})=O\big(N^{1+\frac{2d}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}+N^{\frac{3d}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}\big)=O\big(N^{1+\frac{2d}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}\big),

the computational cost of the remaining matrix-vector operations are of smaller order.

2.2.2
Construction of
\hat{f}_{N}

Conditional on
\hat{u}_{N}
, the plug-in estimation step for calculating
\hat{f}_{N}
is again given as a basis expansion

\hat{f}_{N}:=\sum_{l\leq J,k\leq N_{l}}\hat{\theta}_{lk}\phi_{lk},

(22)

where
\hat{\theta}_{N}
is the solution to the following high-dimensional penalized least squares problem. With
J=J_{N}
chosen as before, we write
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}
for the frame coefficients of the source
g
,

\gamma_{lk}=S_{lk}(g),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\penalty\ l\leq J,\penalty\ k\leq N_{l}.

The estimator
\hat{\theta}_{N}
is then given as

\hat{\theta}_{N}=\argmin_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}}\|\gamma-\Psi\theta\|_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\theta^{T}\Lambda^{\alpha}\theta,

where
\nu_{N}=N^{-\frac{(\alpha-1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}
is chosen as in the infinite-dimensional case,
\Lambda
is given by (
20
), and the matrix
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}\times p_{J}}
is given by the coefficients

\begin{split}\Psi_{(lk),(l^{\prime}k^{\prime})}&=S_{l^{\prime}k^{\prime}}\big(\nabla\cdot(\phi_{lk}\nabla\hat{u}_{N})\big)\\
&=S_{l^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\nabla\phi_{lk}\cdot\nabla\hat{u}_{N})+S_{l^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\phi_{lk}\Delta\hat{u}_{N})\\
&=\sum_{m\leq J,j\leq N_{m}}(\hat{\eta}_{N})_{mj}\big[S_{l^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\nabla\phi_{lk}\cdot\nabla\phi_{mj})+S_{l^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\phi_{lk}\Delta\phi_{mj})\big].\end{split}

Here,
\hat{\eta}_{N}
is the estimator given by (
21
). Note that constructing
\Psi
requires access to wavelet-frame coefficients of products of basis functions and their derivatives, such as

S_{l^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\nabla\phi_{lk}\cdot\nabla\phi_{mj})
and
S_{l^{\prime}k^{\prime}}(\phi_{lk}\Delta\phi_{mj})
;
we treat these as available oracle quantities (or as precomputable offline to high precision) within our computation model. Given
\Psi
,
\hat{\theta}_{N}
possesses a closed-form expression

\hat{\theta}_{N}=\big(\Psi^{T}\Psi+\nu_{N}^{2}\Lambda^{\alpha}\big)^{-1}\Psi^{T}\gamma,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \hat{\theta}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}.

(23)

Theorem 2.4
.

Consider the Darcy flow problem on a bounded, smooth domain
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with smooth and positive
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
. Suppose that
\alpha>d/2+1
and
f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}(\alpha,f_{min},R)
for some
f_{min},R>0
. There exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
depending at most on
\alpha,d,\mathcal{O},f_{min},R
and an exponent
\kappa=\kappa(d,\alpha)<2
such that for all
N\geq 1
the estimators
\hat{\eta}_{N},\hat{u}_{N},\hat{\theta}_{N},\hat{f}_{N}
defined by (
18
)-(
23
) satisfy the convergence rates

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{f_{0}}^{N}[\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}]\leq C_{1}N^{-\frac{2(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{E}_{f_{0}}^{N}[\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}]\leq C_{1}N^{-\frac{2(\alpha-1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}},\end{split}

(24)

and the high-dimensional vectors
\hat{\eta}_{N},\hat{\theta}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}
are computable within
C_{2}N^{\kappa}
floating-point operations.

Theorem
2.4
in particular implies Theorem
1.1
. In fact, the explicit runtime exponent is given by

\kappa=\max\Big\{1+\frac{2d}{2(\alpha+1)+d},\frac{3d}{2(\alpha+1)+d}\Big\}=1+\frac{2d}{2(\alpha+1)+d}

Since
\alpha>d/2+1
, we have
\kappa<2
, yielding a uniformly sub-quadratic computational complexity. In particular, while the statistical convergence rates deteriorate with increasing physical dimension
d
, achieving the (in the forward problem, minimax-optimal) rates does not incur a super-quadratic computational cost.

2.3
Polynomial-time Bayesian computation

We now state two results about polynomial-time computational guarantees for high-dimensional posterior sampling. The notion of polynomial-time computation used below differs from that
employed in Section
2.2
. The Bayesian literature typically measures computational complexity
in terms of the number of iterations required for an MCMC algorithm to reach a prescribed accuracy, under the assumption that each iteration possesses oracle access to evaluations of the forward map
\mathcal{G}
(and potentially its derivatives). Accordingly, the polynomial-time guarantees in this section focus on iteration complexity rather than total arithmetic cost.

We briefly recall a standard Bayesian nonparametric formulation for the Darcy
model and related nonlinear inverse problems, as considered for instance in

[
46
,
43
]
. For
k\geq 1
, let
(e_{k},\lambda_{k})\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O})\times(0,\infty)
denote the
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
-orthonormal eigen-basis of the negative Dirichlet Laplacian on
\mathcal{O}
. Following
[
42
,
43
,
1
]
, we parameterize non-negative
diffusivities through truncated series expansions in this basis.
Specifically, for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
define

f_{\theta}:=\Phi\circ F_{\theta},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{with}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ F_{\theta}(x):=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\theta_{k}e_{k}(x).

Here,
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to(f_{min},\infty)
is a smooth and strictly increasing ‘link function’ satisfying
\Phi^{\prime}>0
and with bounded derivatives of all orders,
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\Phi^{(k)}|<\infty
, as well as
\inf_{x\geq z}\Phi^{\prime}(z)>0
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
. We place a Gaussian diagonal prior on
\theta
of the form

\Pi=N(0,N^{-\frac{d}{2\alpha+d}}\Sigma),\qquad\Sigma=\text{diag}(\lambda_{j}^{-\alpha}:1\leq j\leq D)\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}.

Denoting the data by
Z^{(N)}=((Y_{i},X_{i}):1\leq i\leq N)
, the posterior density is, up to a multiplicative constant, given by

\pi(\theta|Z^{(N)})\propto\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}[Y_{i}-\mathcal{G}(f_{\theta})(X_{i})]^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\theta\Big),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \theta\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

(25)

Let
\theta_{0}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}),\penalty\ \theta_{0}^{(D)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
respectively denote the series coefficients of
F_{0}
as well as their truncated counterpart;

\theta_{0,k}=\langle F_{0},e_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \theta_{0}^{(D)}=(\theta_{0,k}:1\leq k\leq D).

Let
\tilde{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})
denote the spectrally defined Sobolev spaces arising from the Dirichlet Laplacian

\tilde{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O}):=\Big\{f=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}e_{k}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for}\penalty\ \penalty\ \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}^{2}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}<\infty\Big\}.

The following assumption encapsulates the regularity assumptions required in Section 5.3 of
[
43
]
, where the
localized
sampling problem for the Darcy posterior distribution (
25
) is studied using a ‘gradient stability’ approach akin to
[
46
]
.

Assumption 2.5
.

Let
f_{0}=\Phi\circ F_{0}
for some function
F_{0}\in\tilde{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})
for some
\alpha\geq 22
, where
d\geq 3
and
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a bounded, smooth domain. For
N\geq 1
let the dimension be chosen as
D=D_{N}:=aN^{\frac{d}{2\alpha+d}}
, for some
a>0
.

The high smoothness requirement
\alpha\geq 22
is inherited from

[
43
]
and is not intrinsic to our approach. In Theorem 5.3.5 of that reference, it is shown that under Assumption
2.5
, the posterior density
\pi(\theta|Z^{(}{N})
satisfies the following two properties with probability tending to
1
under the law of
Z^{(N)}
. Firstly, it is log-concave on a region of size
\{\theta:\|\theta-\theta_{0,D}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}\leq r\}
with
r=cD^{-8/d}
, with
c>0
a small enough constant. Secondly,
\Pi(\cdot|Z^{(N)})
concentrates the overwhelming majority of its mass in this region. Thus, a sufficient condition (asymptotically as
N\to\infty
) for successful warm-start initialisation is to find a point
\vartheta_{init}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
in polynomial-time such that with probability tending to
1
as
N\to\infty
,
\|\vartheta_{init}-\theta_{0,D}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}\leq D^{-8/d}/\log N.

Theorem 2.6
.

Consider the Darcy problem under Assumption
2.5
. Then, there exists an estimator polynomial-time computable estimator
\vartheta_{init}=\vartheta_{init}(Z^{(N)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
such that

\mathbb{P}_{f_{0}}^{N}\big(\|\vartheta_{init}-\theta_{0,D}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}\leq D^{-8/d}/\log N\big)\to 1
as
N\to\infty
.

The proof of Theorem
2.6
is given in Section
B.3
. It turns out that
\alpha\geq 12
is in fact sufficient to achieve a convergence rate of
D^{-8/d}/\log N
. As a consequence of Theorem
2.6
together with
[
43
, Theorem 5.3.6]
, it follows that the high-dimensional posterior mean is computable via MCMC within polynomially many iterations; this is the subject of our next result. For ‘Gaussian innovations’
\xi_{k}\sim^{i.i.d.}N(0,I_{D\times D})
, we define the following time-discretized Langevin-type MCMC algorithm on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
with step-size
\delta>0
,

\begin{cases}\vartheta_{0}&=\vartheta_{init},\\
\vartheta_{k+1}&=\vartheta_{k}+\delta[\nabla\tilde{\ell}_{N}(\vartheta_{k})+\Sigma^{-1}\vartheta_{k}]+\sqrt{2\gamma}\xi_{k+1},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ k\geq 0,\end{cases}

(26)

where
\tilde{\ell}_{N}
is the globally log-concave ‘surrogate likelihood’ function constructed in
[
43
, eq. (5.8)]
which coincides with the true log-likelihood in a neighborhood of

\vartheta_{init}
, see also
[
46
]
for a detailed construction. Denoting the posterior mean by

\bar{\theta}_{N}=\bar{\theta}_{N}(Z^{(N)}):=\mathbb{E}[\theta|Z^{(N)}]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}\theta d\pi(\theta|Z^{(N)})\in\mathbb{R}^{D},

we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.7
.

Suppose Assumption
2.5
holds. Fix
P>0
. For any
N\geq 1
let
D=D_{N}
be chosen as above and let
\rho=\rho_{N}\geq N^{-P}
denote the desired precision level. Then, there is some suitably chosen step size
\delta=\delta(N,D,\varepsilon)
and exponent
\gamma>0
such that after polynomially many
J=O(N^{\gamma})
iterations, the ergodic average

\bar{\theta}^{MCMC}_{J}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{k=J_{in}}^{J+J_{in}}\vartheta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}

of the MCMC chain
(\vartheta_{k}:k\geq 0)
from (
26
) satisfies that

\mathbb{P}\big(\|\bar{\theta}^{MCMC}_{J}-\mathbb{E}[\theta|Z^{(N)}]\|_{\mathbb{R}^{D}}\leq\rho_{N}\big)\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}1.

Here
\mathbb{P}
denotes the product probability of the law of the data
P^{N}_{0}
and the randomness of the MCMC chain
(\vartheta_{k}:k\geq 0)
.

2.4
Adaptation to unknown smoothness

We conclude this section by presenting an adaptive version of the plug-in M-estimator,
which adapts to the unknown smoothness of the ground truth.
As it turns out, the achievable convergence rates are governed by the smoothness of

u_{0}=\mathcal{G}(f_{0})
rather than that of
f_{0}
itself. Our next theorem is formulated in the infinite-dimensional setting; a corresponding
high-dimensional version could be developed analogously to the constructions in
Section
2.2
. For simplicity, and in order to remain compatible with the PDE theory employed
throughout, we restrict attention to integer-valued smoothness indices. We define the adaptive plug-in estimation procedure as follows. For integers
a,b\geq 0
, we write
[a:b]=\{a,a+1,\dots,b\}
.

1.

Form the preliminary estimator

(\hat{\beta},\hat{u}_{N})\in\argmin_{\beta\in[\beta_{\min}:\beta_{\max}],\penalty\ u\in H^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})}\frac{1}{N}\|Y-u(X)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}^{2}+\mu_{N,\beta}^{2}\big(\|u\|_{H^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}+A^{2}\big),

(27)

for a suitable constant
A>0
, and with
\mu_{N,\beta}:=N^{-\frac{\beta}{2\beta+d}}
.

2.

With regularisation parameter
\nu_{N,\hat{\beta}}:=N^{-\frac{\hat{\beta}-2}{2\hat{\beta}+d}}
, compute the plug-in estimate

\hat{f}_{N}\in\argmin_{f\in H^{\alpha_{\min}}(\mathcal{O})}\|L_{f}\hat{u}_{N}-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}+\nu_{N,\hat{\beta}}^{2}\|f\|_{H^{\alpha_{\min}}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}.

(28)

We emphasize that adaptation is achieved entirely through the preliminary estimator

\hat{u}_{N}
in Step 1, while the inverse step in (
28
) is performed
over a fixed Sobolev space
H^{\alpha_{\min}}(\mathcal{O})
, with adaptation entering
only through the data-driven choice of the regularization parameter
\nu_{N,\hat{\beta}}
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
(\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N})
be the estimators defined in
(
27
)–(
28
).
Let
\alpha_{\min}>d/2+1
and suppose that
f_{0}\in H^{\alpha_{\min}}(\mathcal{O})
.
Let
2\leq\beta_{\min}<\beta_{\max}<\infty
be integers, and define

\beta_{0}:=\max\big\{\beta\in[\beta_{\min}:\beta_{\max}]:u_{0}\in H^{\beta}(\mathcal{O})\big\}.

Then there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{f_{0}}^{N}(\hat{\beta}\geq\beta_{0})

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}1,

(29)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{f_{0}}^{N}\big(\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\geq C_{1}N^{-\frac{\beta_{0}}{2\beta_{0}+d}}\big)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0,

(30)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{f_{0}}^{N}\big(\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\geq C_{2}N^{-\frac{\beta_{0}-2}{2\beta_{0}+d}}\big)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0.

(31)

The constant
C_{1}
may be chosen uniformly over classes

\{f_{0}:\|\mathcal{G}(f_{0})\|_{H^{\beta_{0}}(\mathcal{O})}\leq R\}
, while
C_{2}
may be chosen
uniformly over

\{f_{0}:\|\mathcal{G}(f_{0})\|_{H^{\beta_{0}}(\mathcal{O})}\leq R,\ \|f_{0}\|_{C^{1}(\mathcal{O})}\leq R\}
.

The proof of Theorem
2.8
can be found in Section
B.4
. Theorem
2.8
shows that the procedure
(
27
)–(
28
) adapts to the unknown smoothness of

u_{0}=\mathcal{G}(f_{0})
rather than that of
f_{0}
. The adaptive procedure is formulated in terms of nonparametric M-estimation,
primarily for ease of exposition.
Alternative adaptation strategies, such as Lepskii-type methods
[
34
]
,
could potentially yield comparable results. An advantage of the present approach is that Step 1 simultaneously produces an estimate of the smoothness index, leading to adaptation with respect to Sobolev losses
H^{\gamma}(\mathcal{O})
for all
\gamma\in[0,\beta_{0}]
in particular for the
H^{2}(\mathcal{O})
-norm relevant in the generalized stability estimates. To the best of our knowledge, the only previous adaptive rates in the Darcy model
were those
[
22
]
, where adaptation is achieved for the forward problem but not for the diffusivity
f_{0}
, see Theorems 8-10 in
[
22
]
. Note that adaptation is possible here without incurring additional logarithmic factors in the convergence rate, due to the
L^{2}
-loss function employed here, cf. Chapter 8 in
[
21
]
.

2.5
Results for the Schrödinger model

We now turn to our second main PDE example, an inverse problem for the steady-state
Schrödinger equation studied, for instance, in
[
47
,
46
,
1
]
.
The main result of this section is Theorem
2.9
, which establishes
minimax-optimal convergence rates for both the PDE-penalized and plug-in M-estimators. The forward map
\mathcal{G}(f)
is defined through the unique solution
u=u_{f}
of

\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\Delta u-fu=0&\textnormal{on }\mathcal{O},\\
u=g&\textnormal{on }\partial\mathcal{O},\end{cases}

(32)

where
g\in C^{\infty}(\partial\mathcal{O})
is a known strictly positive boundary datum.
Whenever
f\geq 0
and
f\in C^{\eta}(\mathcal{O})
for some
\eta>0
, elliptic theory yields a unique
classical solution
u\in C^{2+\eta}(\mathcal{O})\cap C(\overline{\mathcal{O}})
.
See
[
12
]
for a comprehensive PDE theory of Schrödinger-type equations and
[
4
]

for the occurrence of this problem in photoacoustic tomography (PAT).
For
f:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
, write

L_{f}u:=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u-fu.

We consider the following two estimation procedures, which are direct analogues of those
introduced for the Darcy model. Define, for
(u,f)\in H^{\alpha+2}(\mathcal{O})\times H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})
, the objective

J(u,f):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(Y_{i}-u(X_{i})\bigr)^{2}+\lambda_{N}^{2}\bigl\|L_{f}u-g\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}+\mu_{N}^{2}\Bigl(\|u\|_{H^{\alpha+2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}+\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}\Bigr).

(33)

The PDE-penalized M-estimator is defined as any measurable minimizer

(\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N})\in\argmin_{(u,f)\in H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O})\times H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})}J(u,f),

(34)

with regularization parameters

\lambda_{N}=N^{-\frac{2}{2(\alpha+2)+d}},\qquad\mu_{N}=N^{-\frac{\alpha+2}{2(\alpha+2)+d}}.

Alternatively, the plug-in M-estimator is defined via the two-stage procedure

\hat{u}_{N}=\argmin_{u\in H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O})}\left\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(Y_{i}-u(X_{i})\bigr)^{2}+\mu_{N}^{2}\|u\|_{H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}\right\},

(35)

followed by

\hat{f}_{N}=\argmin_{f\in H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})}\left\{\bigl\|L_{f}\hat{u}_{N}-g\bigr\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}\right\},

(36)

where
\mu_{N}=N^{-\frac{\alpha+2}{2(\alpha+2)+d}}
,
\nu_{N}=N^{-\frac{\alpha}{2(\alpha+2)+d}}
.

To avoid excessive technicalities, we derive the following convergence result in high probability rather than mean squared error.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
d\geq 1
and suppose
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a smooth, bounded domain.
Let
\alpha>d/2+1
and
\alpha\geq 3
, and fix constants
f_{\min}\geq 0
and
R>0
.
Suppose that the data (
10
) arise from some

f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}(\alpha,f_{\min},R)
, and let
(\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N})
denote
either the PDE-penalized M-estimator defined in
(
34
) or the plug-in M-estimator defined in
(
35
)–(
36
).
There exists
C>0
depending only on
\mathcal{O},d,\alpha,f_{\min},R
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{f_{0}}^{N}\Big(\|\hat{u}_{N}-\mathcal{G}(f_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}\geq CN^{-\frac{2(\alpha+2)}{2(\alpha+2)+d}}\Big)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0,

(37)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{f_{0}}^{N}\Big(\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}\geq CN^{-\frac{2\alpha}{2(\alpha+2)+d}}\Big)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}0.

(38)

The proof is given in Section
B.5
. Like for the Darcy model, a key ingredient to the proof is that the Schrödinger problem satisfies the following generalized stability estimate.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a smooth, bounded domain and let
c_{\min}>0
.
Suppose that for
i\in\{1,2\}
the functions

u_{i}\in H^{2}(\mathcal{O})
,
f_{i}\in C(\mathcal{O})
and
h_{i}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
satisfy

L_{f_{1}}u_{1}=h_{1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad L_{f_{2}}u_{2}=h_{2}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{O}.

Assume moreover that
(u_{1},f_{1},h_{1})
satisfies
u_{1}\in C^{2}(\mathcal{O})
and
h_{1}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})
, and that

\inf_{x\in\mathcal{O},\,i=1,2}u_{i}(x)\geq c_{\min}.

Then there exists a constant
C>0
, depending only on
c_{\min}
and upper bounds on

\|u_{1}\|_{C^{2}(\mathcal{O})}
and
\|h_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}
, such that

\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\leq C\big(\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{H^{2}(\mathcal{O})}+\|h_{1}-h_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\big).

Using the same techniques as in the case of Darcy flow, one could also derive analogous results for a high-dimensional discretization of the Schrödinger problem computable in sub-quadratic time, as well as results on adaptive estimation. Since these results would closely parallel the results in Sections
2.2
-
2.4
, we do not pursue this extension here.

3
An abstract theorem for generalized M-estimators

We now formulate a general result on convergence rates of ‘augmented M-estimators’ building on classical ideas developed in
[
63
]
, used throughout the proofs. The formulation is intentionally
slightly technical in order to allow maximum flexibility in its application to the
various estimation procedures considered in this paper.

Let
d\geq 1
and let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Borel measurable, non-empty subset. Let
X_{i}\in\mathcal{O}
be the design points at which we are given regression measurements

Y_{i}=u_{0}(X_{i})+\varepsilon_{i},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ i=1,...,N,

where
u_{0}:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
is some regression function and
\varepsilon_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are independent Gaussian measurement errors with variance at most
\sigma^{2}>0
,

\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,\sigma_{i}^{2}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \sup_{1\leq i\leq N}\sigma_{i}\leq\sigma.

The result in this section will not make any assumption regarding whether the
X_{i}
are deterministic or random.

We consider M-estimators in two variables
\eta\in H
and
\theta\in\Theta
, where
\eta\in H
should be interpreted as the variable which parameterizes the regression functions, and
\theta\in\Theta
is another statistical parameter of interest. Throughout we fix some parameterization of regression functions on
\mathcal{O}
,

\eta\mapsto u_{\eta}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for all}\penalty\ \penalty\ \eta\in H,\penalty\ \penalty\ u_{\eta}:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}.

We will consider the minimization of functionals of the form

J:H\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ J(\eta,\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(Y_{i}-u_{\eta}(X_{i}))^{2}+\mathcal{R}(\eta,\theta),

where
\mathcal{R}:H\times\Theta\to[0,\infty)
is some non-negative penalty term, which be arbitrary in the context of this section. Throughout, let us assume that minimizers
(\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta})
of
J
exist, and that there exists a choice of
(\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta})
which is a measurable function of the data
(Y_{i},X_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
, which in the settings relevant to our theorems above, one may verify using well-known measurable selection theorems, e.g.
[
11
]
. We denote the empirical norm by

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}h(X_{i})^{2}=\|h\|_{N}^{2},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ h:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}.

For any
(\eta,\theta)\in H\times\Theta
and function
u:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the following ‘discrepancy functional’

\tau^{2}((\eta,\theta),u):=\|u_{\eta}-u\|^{2}_{N}+\mathcal{R}(\eta,\theta).

Using this discrepancy functional, we introduce the following localized classes of regression functions. For any ‘centering’ element
\eta^{*}:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
and
R>0
, let

\mathcal{U}(\eta^{*},R):=\big\{u=u_{\eta}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for some}\penalty\ \penalty\ (\eta,\theta)\in H\times\Theta\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{with}\penalty\ \penalty\ \tau^{2}((\eta,\theta),u_{\eta^{*}})\leq R^{2}\big\}.

Intuitively,
\mathcal{U}(\eta^{*},R)
is the set of all the regression functions
u_{\eta}
represented by parameter pairs
(\eta,\theta)
which are ‘close to’ the reference point
\eta^{*}
, as measured by the
\tau^{2}
functional. We may think of
\eta^{*}
as the ‘best approximation’ of the ground truth. The metric entropy integral of
\mathcal{U}(\eta^{*},R)
with respect to the empirical norm is denoted by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\eta^{*},R):=

\displaystyle\sigma R+\int_{0}^{\sigma R}H^{1/2}(\rho,\mathcal{U}(\eta^{*},R),\sigma\|\cdot\|_{N})d\rho.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
(\eta^{*},\theta^{*})\in H\times\Theta
an arbitrary element, suppose that

\displaystyle\Psi(R)\geq\mathcal{I}(\eta^{*},R)

(39)

is an upper bound such that
R\mapsto\Psi(R)/R^{2}
is non-increasing. There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for all such
(\eta^{*},\theta^{*})
and
\Psi(\cdot)
, any
N\geq 1,\delta_{N}>0
with

\sqrt{N}\delta_{N}^{2}\geq C\Psi(\delta_{N}),

and any
R\geq\delta_{N}
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}\big(\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u_{0})\geq 2\big(\tau^{2}((\eta^{*},\theta^{*}),u_{0})+R^{2})\big)\leq C\exp(-NR^{2}/C).

(40)

Moreover, we have the following moment bound for all
k\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\tau^{2k}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u_{0})\big]\leq C_{k}\Big(\tau^{2k}((\eta^{*},\theta^{*}),u_{0})+\delta_{N}^{2k}+\frac{\delta_{N}^{2k-2}\sigma^{2}}{N}\Big),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{some}\penalty\ C_{k}>0.

(41)

The proof, presented in Section
C
, generalizes classical empirical process arguments for penalized least squares estimators, in particular
[
63
]
, to the present augmented parameter setting. The key idea is a localization argument based on the discrepancy functional
\tau^{2}
,
combined with concentration inequalities for Gaussian processes indexed by the localized
class
\mathcal{U}(\eta^{*},R)
.
The monotonicity assumption on
\Psi(R)/R^{2}
then allows one to use a standard ‘peeling argument’.

Remark 3.2
.

At the expense of additional technicalities, our results could be extended to the case of
independent sub-Gaussian noise variables, satisfying for some
\sigma>0

\mathbb{E}\big[e^{\lambda\varepsilon_{i}}\big]\leq\exp(\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}/2),\qquad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R},\ \forall i\geq 1.

(42)

In this case, the Borell–Sudakov–Tsirelson inequality used in the proof can be replaced
by a corresponding tail inequality for sub-Gaussian processes; see, for instance,
Corollary 8.3 in
[
63
]
. We do not pursue this extension here.

Remark 3.3
(White Noise).

A concentration result analogous to Theorem
3.1
can also be formulated in a white noise observation model, which is also commonly considered in the literature, see, e.g.,
[
42
,
32
]
. Here,
u_{0}
may belong to a general separable Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
and one observes a single realization of the Gaussian process

Y_{N}=u_{0}+N^{-1/2}\mathbb{W},

where
\mathbb{W}
is an isonormal centered white noise process indexed by
\mathbb{H}
, with covariance
\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{W}(g)\mathbb{W}(h)]=\langle g,h\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}
. In this case, one considers a parameterization
\eta\mapsto u_{\eta}\in\mathbb{H}
, and the objective function is given by

(\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta})\in\argmin_{(\eta,\theta)\in H\times\Theta}-2\langle Y_{N},u_{\eta}\rangle+\|u_{\eta}\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}+\mathcal{R}(\eta,\theta).

The proof proceeds along essentially the same lines as in the discrete-design case, with the
\|\cdot\|
-norm replacing the empirical norm
\|\cdot\|_{N}
throughout. Since the applications in this paper
concern the discretized case of finitely many
N\geq 1
measurements—for which the question of computational complexity is most natural—we do not pursue the white noise formulation further here.

4
Further remarks and future directions

Boundary and other penalties

Our objective does not include an explicit penalty enforcing boundary conditions.
In physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), by contrast, boundary mismatch terms are often incorporated in the loss function to promote adherence to the PDE constraint
[
50
]
.
For the PDE-based inverse problems studied in this paper, this turns out not to be necessary: the generalized stability estimates established below show that interior information alone already controls the parameter error, even when the PDE constraint is only enforced weakly.
That said, in other inverse problems or for different operator equations, additional penalties—including boundary terms or other structural regularizers—may be useful, and our framework readily allows such extensions.

Uncertainty quantification (UQ)

A natural question is whether the present non-likelihood-based methods can deliver not only optimal convergence rates, but also finer notions of optimality relevant for statistical uncertainty quantification (UQ), such as semiparametric efficiency in the Cramér–Rao sense
[
66
]
. Concretely, one may ask whether estimators based on our surrogate objectives can achieve the smallest possible asymptotic variance for regular linear functionals of the form

\sqrt{N}\,\langle\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0},\psi\rangle,

for suitable test functions
\psi
. In settings where a Bernstein–von Mises theorem holds, Bayesian procedures are known to be asymptotically optimal for such functionals. In the Darcy inverse problem, however,
\sqrt{N}
-consistent estimation is not attainable along arbitrary smooth directions
\psi
, due to range restrictions induced by the forward map
\mathcal{G}
; see
[
43
]
and Chapter 25 of
[
66
]
. The objective functions studied here are based on tractable surrogates of the likelihood and are therefore no longer directly linked to the Fisher information operator
D\mathcal{G}_{f_{0}}^{\ast}D\mathcal{G}_{f_{0}}
. As a consequence, while they yield optimal rates, they may in principle lead to a suboptimal asymptotic covariance structure. A detailed analysis of asymptotic distributions and efficiency—including limiting laws for
\hat{u}_{N}
and
\hat{f}_{N}
—is beyond the scope of this paper and constitutes an interesting direction for future work. We also refer to
[
57
]
for related results on limiting distributions of M-estimators.

A
Auxiliary PDE results

We present two key analytical properties for our main PDE examples, the Darcy model and the Schrödinger model. The properties needed are: firstly, ‘boundedness’ properties for the forward map
\mathcal{G}
between suitable (Sobolev) smoothness balls and secondly,
generalized stability estimates
for
\mathcal{G}^{-1}
discussed above. Note that the boundedness requirement for the forward map
\mathcal{G}
is weaker than what is required in convergence proofs for Bayes and MAP estimators
[
42
,
22
,
43
]
, where local Lipschitz estimates for
\mathcal{G}
are required. The technical reason why we do not require such Lipschitz estimates is that the regularity of
u
is explicitly penalized.

Next, we turn to the proof of the generalized stability property for the Darcy model, which we restate for the convenience of the reader.

See
2.2

We divide the proof into three steps. Our proof builds on the approach of
[
42
, Lemma 24]
using elliptic PDE theory.

Step 1

For
f_{1},\penalty\ f_{2}
as in the hypotheses, write
h=f_{1}-f_{2}
. By assumption,
h
satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot(h\nabla u_{1})

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot(f_{1}\nabla u_{1})-\nabla\cdot(f_{2}\nabla u_{2})-\nabla\cdot(f_{2}\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2}))

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot(f_{2}\nabla(u_{2}-u_{1}))+(g_{1}-g_{2}).

The
L^{2}
-norm of the right hand side can further be upper bounded by

\|\nabla\cdot(f_{2}\nabla(u_{1}-u_{2}))\|_{L^{2}}+\|g_{1}-g_{2}\|_{L^{2}}\leq 2\|f_{2}\|_{C^{1}}\|u_{2}-u_{1}\|_{H^{2}}+\|g_{1}-g_{2}\|_{L^{2}}.

(43)

Step 2: Multiplier identity via Green’s formula

The overall goal is to lower bound the first term of the left hand side of (
43
). To this end, we prove the following identity: For
v=e^{-\lambda u_{1}}h
(where
\lambda>0
is chosen later, for now arbitrary),

\langle\nabla\cdot(h\nabla u_{1}),he^{-\lambda u_{1}}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\langle\frac{1}{2}\Delta u_{1}+\lambda\|\nabla u_{1}\|^{2},v^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\mathcal{O}}v^{2}\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial n},

(44)

where
n=n(x)
denotes the outward normal vector on
x\in\partial\mathcal{O}
. Note that since
\|u_{1}\|_{C^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\leq B
and
f_{1}-f_{2}\in C^{1}(\mathcal{O})
(with finite
C^{1}(\mathcal{O})
-norm), the functions
v
,
u_{1}
and
\nabla u_{1}
are Lipschitz on
\mathcal{O}
and admit a unique continuous extension to
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
; the boundary term is understood in this sense. To see (
44
), we use Green’s identity (integration by parts) to obtain

\displaystyle\langle\Delta u_{1},v^{2}\rangle+\langle\nabla u_{1},\nabla(v^{2})\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{O}}\nabla\cdot(v^{2}\nabla u_{1})=\int_{\partial\mathcal{O}}\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial n}v^{2},

and then also

\langle\Delta u_{1},v^{2}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla u_{1},\nabla(v^{2})\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle\Delta u_{1},v^{2}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\mathcal{O}}\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial n}v^{2}.

Moreover, by the definition
v=e^{-\lambda u_{1}}h
, we have

\nabla v=e^{-\lambda u_{1}}\nabla h-\lambda e^{-\lambda u_{1}}h\nabla u_{1}=e^{-\lambda u_{1}}(\nabla h-\lambda h\nabla u_{1}).

Since
\nabla(v^{2})=2v\nabla v
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla u_{1},\nabla(v^{2})\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla u_{1},v\nabla v\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\Big\langle\nabla u_{1},\;ve^{-\lambda u_{1}}(\nabla h-\lambda h\nabla u_{1})\Big\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle\nabla h\cdot\nabla u_{1},\;ve^{-\lambda u_{1}}\rangle_{L^{2}}-\lambda\langle\|\nabla u_{1}\|^{2},\;v^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}.

Using the previous equations and
he^{-2\lambda u_{1}}=ve^{-\lambda u_{1}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\cdot(h\nabla u_{1}),he^{-2\lambda u_{1}}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle h\Delta u_{1}+\nabla h\cdot\nabla u_{1},he^{-2\lambda u_{1}}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle h\Delta u_{1},he^{-2\lambda u_{1}}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla u_{1},\nabla(v^{2})\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle\lambda\|\nabla u_{1}\|^{2},v^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta u_{1},v^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\langle\nabla u_{1},\nabla(v^{2})\rangle_{L^{2}}+\langle\lambda\|\nabla u_{1}\|^{2},v^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle=\langle\frac{1}{2}\Delta u_{1}+\lambda\|\nabla u_{1}\|^{2},v^{2}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\mathcal{O}}v^{2}\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial n}.

Step 3: Lower bound for (
44
)

We show that the right-hand side of (
44
) is bounded from below by a positive multiple of
\|h\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}
, for a suitable choice of
\lambda=\lambda(g_{\min},B)>0
in the definition of
v
.

(i) Positivity of the boundary term.

Since
g_{1}\geq g_{\min}>0
and
u_{1}|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}=0
, the strong maximum principle implies that

u_{1}(x)<0,\qquad x\in\mathcal{O}.

(45)

Hence, by the Hopf boundary point lemma (see, e.g.,
[
20
, Lemma 3.4]
), we have

\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial n}(x)>0,\qquad x\in\partial\mathcal{O},

(46)

and consequently,

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\mathcal{O}}v^{2}\frac{\partial u_{1}}{\partial n}\,\geq 0.

(47)

(ii) Pointwise lower bound on the multiplier.

Recall that
u_{1}
solves
\nabla\cdot(f_{1}\nabla u_{1})=g_{1}
, hence

g_{1}(x)=f_{1}(x)\Delta u_{1}(x)+\nabla f_{1}(x)\cdot\nabla u_{1}(x),\qquad x\in\mathcal{O}.

(48)

Using
f_{1}\geq f_{\min}
and
\|\nabla f_{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\|f_{1}\|_{C^{1}}\leq B
, we infer that for every
x\in\mathcal{O}
,

g_{\min}\leq f_{1}(x)\Delta u_{1}(x)+\|\nabla f_{1}\|_{\infty}\|\nabla u_{1}(x)\|\leq B\Delta u_{1}(x)+B\|\nabla u_{1}(x)\|.

Hence, for every
x\in\mathcal{O}
, at least one of the following two inequalities holds:

\Delta u_{1}(x)\geq\frac{g_{\min}}{2B}\qquad\text{or}\qquad\|\nabla u_{1}(x)\|\geq\frac{g_{\min}}{2B}.

(49)

Since
\|u_{1}\|_{C^{2}}\leq B
, we have
\|\Delta u_{1}\|_{\infty}\leq c(B)
for some
c(B)<\infty
. Therefore, choosing
\lambda=\lambda(g_{\min},B)>0
sufficiently large, we obtain the pointwise lower bound

\frac{1}{2}\Delta u_{1}(x)+\lambda\|\nabla u_{1}(x)\|^{2}\geq c_{0},\qquad x\in\mathcal{O},

(50)

for some constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(g_{\min},B)>0
.

(iii) Conclusion.

Combining (
44
), (
47
) and (
50
) yields

\big\langle\nabla\cdot(h\nabla u_{1}),\,he^{-2\lambda u_{1}}\big\rangle_{L^{2}}\;\geq\;c_{0}\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2}.

On the other hand, by Cauchy–Schwarz and the bound
\|e^{-2\lambda u_{1}}\|_{\infty}\leq\exp(2\lambda B)<\infty
,

\big|\big\langle\nabla\cdot(h\nabla u_{1}),\,he^{-2\lambda u_{1}}\big\rangle_{L^{2}}\big|\leq\|\nabla\cdot(h\nabla u_{1})\|_{L^{2}}\,\|he^{-2\lambda u_{1}}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\exp(2\lambda B)\|\nabla\cdot(h\nabla u_{1})\|_{L^{2}}\,\|h\|_{L^{2}}.

Therefore,

\|\nabla\cdot(h\nabla u_{1})\|_{L^{2}}\,\|h\|_{L^{2}}\;\geq\;c\,\|v\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}^{2},

(51)

for some
c=c(g_{\min},f_{\min},B,\mathcal{O},d)>0
. Since
u_{1}
is bounded and
v=e^{-\lambda u_{1}}h
, we have
\|v\|_{L^{2}}\asymp\|h\|_{L^{2}}
with constants depending only on
\lambda
and
B
. Inserting this into (
51
) gives

\|\nabla\cdot(h\nabla u_{1})\|_{L^{2}}\;\gtrsim\;\|h\|_{L^{2}},

and the claimed stability estimate now follows by combining this bound with (
43
).
∎

-norm in place of the empirical norm. Define the
L^{2}
-counterpart of the
\tau
-functional by

\mathcal{T}^{2}((u,f),u_{0})=\|u-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mathcal{R}(u,f).

Take
K\geq L_{0}^{2}
and
K\geq 16
large enough, and consider the event

A_{N}=\big\{\mathcal{T}^{2}((\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N}),u_{0})\geq K^{2}\mu_{N}^{2},\penalty\ \tau^{2}((\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N}),u_{0})\leq K\mu_{N}^{2}\big\}.

On that event, we have
\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}\leq\sqrt{K}
, and choosing
L_{0}\geq M\geq\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}
large enough without loss of generality, we have

\begin{split}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}&\geq\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}-\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{N}^{2}\\
&=\mathcal{T}^{2}((\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N}),u_{0})-\tau^{2}((\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N}),u_{0})\\
&\geq(K^{2}-K)\mu_{N}^{2}\geq K^{2}\mu_{N}^{2}/4\geq K\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{N}^{2}/4,\end{split}

and at the same time

\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}\leq L_{0}+\sqrt{K}\leq 2\sqrt{K}.

Thus, it holds that

A_{N}\subseteq\Big\{\exists v\in H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O}):\|v\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}\leq 2\sqrt{K},\ \|v\|_{L^{2}}\geq K\mu_{N}/2,\ \|v\|_{L^{2}}\geq\sqrt{K}\|v\|_{N}/2\Big\}.

Since
\alpha+1>d/2
, Sobolev embedding yields that for some
M_{K}>0
,
\sup_{w:\|w\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}\leq 2\sqrt{K}}\|w\|_{\infty}\leq M_{K}\lesssim\sqrt{K}
. Using Lemma
D.1
with
R=K\mu_{N}/2
and
M=M_{K}\asymp\sqrt{K}
(noting that
\sqrt{K}/2\geq 2
), it follows that for some constant
C>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}(A_{N})\leq 2\exp(-N\mu_{N}^{2}K/C),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ K>0\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{large enough}.

Combining this with the concentration result for the empirical norm (
55
), we conclude that for some
C>0
and all
K\geq K_{0}
large enough,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}\big(\mathcal{T}^{2}((\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N}),u_{0})\geq K^{2}\mu_{N}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}\big(A_{N}\big)+\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}\big(\tau^{2}((\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N}),u_{0})\geq K\mu_{N}^{2}\big)

\displaystyle\leq C\exp(-N\mu_{N}^{2}K/C).

To obtain the corresponding moment bounds, let us write for simplicity

Z=\mathcal{T}^{2}((\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N}),u_{0})
. From the previous displayed inequality, it follows that we have

\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}(Z^{k}\geq t)=\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}(Z\geq t^{1/k})\lesssim\exp\big(-N\mu_{N}t^{1/2k}/C\big),\qquad\forall k\geq 1,\ \forall t\geq(K_{0}\mu_{N})^{2k}.

Using Lemma E.2 with
\gamma=1/(2k)
,
L=N\mu_{N}/C
and
a=(K_{0}\mu_{N})^{2k}

(note that
La^{\gamma}=(N\mu_{N}/C)\cdot(K_{0}\mu_{N})\simeq K_{0}N\mu_{N}^{2}\geq 1
since
K_{0}\geq 1
),
we deduce that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[Z^{k}\big]&\lesssim(K_{0}\mu_{N})^{2k}+\int_{(K_{0}\mu_{N})^{2k}}^{\infty}\exp\big(-N\mu_{N}t^{1/2k}/C\big)\,dt\\
&\lesssim\mu_{N}^{2k}+\frac{\mu_{N}^{2k(1-\frac{1}{2k})}}{N\mu_{N}}\exp\big(-K_{0}N\mu_{N}^{2}/C\big)\\
&\leq\mu_{N}^{2k}+\frac{\mu_{N}^{2k-2}}{N}=O(\mu_{N}^{2k}),\end{split}

where we used in the last step that
\mu_{N}=o(\sqrt{N})
.

Step 3: Convergence rate for the inverse problem

Using the definitions of
\mathcal{R}
and of
\mathcal{T}^{2}
, the preceding bound implies that for any
k\geq 1
, there exists
C_{k}>0
such that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}[\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2k}]+\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}[\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{2k}]&\leq C_{k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall N\geq 1,\end{split}

(56)

as well as

\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}[\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2k}]\leq C_{k}\mu_{N}^{2k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall N\geq 1.

(57)

A standard Sobolev interpolation argument gives

\begin{split}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}&\lesssim\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+1}}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{\frac{2}{\alpha+1}}\\
&\lesssim\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+1}}\Big(\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{\frac{2}{\alpha+1}}+\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{\frac{2}{\alpha+1}}\Big).\end{split}

(58)

We next verify that we may utilize Lemma
2.2
with
(u_{1},f_{1},g_{1})=(u_{0},f_{0},g)
and
(u_{2},f_{2},g_{2})=(\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N},L_{\hat{f}_{N}}\hat{u}_{N})
. Since
f_{0}\in\mathcal{F}(\alpha,f_{\min},R)
, we have
f_{0}\geq f_{\min}>0
on
\mathcal{O}
and, by Sobolev embedding

H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow C^{1}(\overline{\mathcal{O}})
(recall
\alpha>d/2+1
), also

\|f_{0}\|_{C^{1}(\mathcal{O})}\lesssim\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})}\leq R
.
Moreover, by Lemma
A.1
the corresponding solution
u_{0}=\mathcal{G}(f_{0})
satisfies

u_{0}\in H^{\alpha+1}(\mathcal{O})\subseteq C^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{O}})
and
\|u_{0}\|_{C^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\leq B
for some

B=B(\alpha,f_{\min},R,\mathcal{O})
.
Finally, the boundary condition
u_{0}|_{\partial\mathcal{O}}=0
holds by definition of
\mathcal{G}
. Consequently, Lemma
2.2
yields

\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{C^{1}}^{2}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|L_{\hat{f}_{N}}\hat{u}_{N}-g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=:I+II

We separately bound the expectation of the two terms. For the second term, we obtain by the definition of
\mathcal{R}
and the choice of
\lambda_{N}
from (
13
) that

\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}[II]\lesssim\lambda_{N}^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}[\mathcal{R}(\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N})]\lesssim\lambda_{N}^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}[\mathcal{T}^{2}((\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N}),u_{0})]\lesssim N^{-\frac{2(\alpha-1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}.

Since
\alpha>d/2+1
, Sobolev embedding yields
H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow C^{1}(\overline{\mathcal{O}})
. By (
56
), it thus holds that
\sup_{N\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{C^{1}(\mathcal{O})}^{2k}<\infty
for every
k\geq 1
. Using this, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, (
58
) and (
56
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}[I]

\displaystyle\leq\big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{C^{1}}^{4}\big)^{1/2}\big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{4}\big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{C^{1}}^{4}\big)^{1/2}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4(\alpha-1)}{\alpha+1}}\Big(\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{\frac{8}{\alpha+1}}+\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{\frac{8}{\alpha+1}}\Big)\Big]\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\Big[\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4(\alpha-1)}{\alpha+1}}\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{\frac{8}{\alpha+1}}\Big]\Big)^{1/2}+\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{\frac{4}{\alpha+1}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4(\alpha-1)}{\alpha+1}}\Big)^{1/2}

(59)

\displaystyle=:I_{a}+I_{b}.

Term
I_{b}
is estimated using Jensen’s inequality and (
57
),

I_{b}\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\Big[\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{4}\Big]^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2(\alpha+1)}}\lesssim\mu_{N}^{\frac{2(\alpha-1)}{\alpha+1}}\lesssim N^{-\frac{2(\alpha-1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}.

For term
I_{a}
, we use Hölder’s inequality
\|vw\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|v\|_{L^{p}}\|w\|_{L^{q}}
with

p=\frac{2(\alpha+1)}{\alpha-1},\penalty\ \penalty\ q=\alpha+1,\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{with}\penalty\ \penalty\ \frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1/2,

as well as (
56
)-(
57
) to obtain

I_{a}\lesssim\big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{4}\big)^{\frac{\alpha-1}{2(\alpha+1)}}\big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{4}\big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}\lesssim\mu_{N}^{\frac{2(\alpha-1)}{\alpha+1}}\lesssim N^{-\frac{2(\alpha-1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}.

Putting everything together, we obtain that

E_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big]=O(N^{-\frac{2\alpha-2}{2\alpha+2+d}}),

which finishes the proof of the convergence rate for the PDE-penalized M-estimator.

B.1.2
Plug-in estimator

We now prove the analogous convergence guarantee for the plug-in estimator.

Step 1: Convergence of
\hat{u}_{N}

We again employ Theorem
3.1
, this time with a single parameter
\eta=u\in H^{\alpha+1}
. [Formally we may take
\Theta=\{0\}
such that the minimization problem is independent of
\theta
.] The regularization functional is given by

\mathcal{R}(u)={\mu}_{N}^{2}\|u\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{2}.

Define the functional

\tau^{2}(\hat{u}_{N},u_{0}):=\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{N}^{2}+\mu_{N}^{2}\|u\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{2},

and set
u^{*}=u_{0}
. By the definition of
\mathcal{R}
, it holds that

\mathcal{U}(\eta^{*},R)\subseteq\{u:\|u\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}\leq R/\mu_{N}\},\penalty\ \penalty\ R>0.

Thus, the same arguments (
53
)-(
54
) as in the proof for the generalized M-estimator give that the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
are fulfilled with
\Psi(R):=C_{1}R\mu_{N}^{-\frac{d}{2(\alpha+1)}}
, and with
\delta_{N}:=C_{2}N^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}
, for
C_{1},C_{2}>0
large enough. This implies that we have the concentration inequality

\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}\Big(\tau^{2}(\hat{u}_{N},u_{0})\geq L^{2}N^{-\frac{2(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}\Big)\leq C_{3}\exp\big(-L^{2}N^{\frac{d}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}/C_{3}\big),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ L\geq L_{0},

(60)

where
L_{0}>0
is some large enough constant, as well as the moment bound

\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\tau^{2k}(\hat{u}_{N},u_{0})\big]\lesssim\delta_{N}^{2k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{any}\penalty\ k\geq 1.

The preceding bound extends also to the
L^{2}(\mathcal{O})
-norm. Arguing just as in the proof for the PDE-penalized M-estimator, defining

\mathcal{T}^{2}(\hat{u}_{N},u_{0})=:\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mu_{N}^{2}\|u\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{2},

and application of Lemma
D.1
yields that for all
K\geq K_{0}
large enough,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}\big(\mathcal{T}^{2}(\hat{u}_{N},u_{0})\geq K^{2}N^{-\frac{2(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}\big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\exp(-KN^{\frac{d}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}/C),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\mathcal{T}^{2k}(\hat{u}_{N},u_{0})\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu_{N}^{2k}=N^{-\frac{2k(\alpha+1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}},

which implies in particular that for some
C_{k}>0
,

\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2k}\big]\leq C_{k}\mu_{N}^{2k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{2k}\big]\leq C_{k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall N\geq 1.

(61)

Step 2: Convergence of
\hat{f}_{N}

By definition of
\hat{f}_{N}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\cdot(\hat{f}_{N}\nabla\hat{u}_{N})-g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\cdot(f_{0}\nabla\hat{u}_{N})-g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\cdot(f_{0}\nabla(\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}))\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|f_{0}\|_{C^{1}}^{2}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}.

Note that the right hand side is always finite, since
\hat{u}_{N}\in H^{\alpha+1}
and
\alpha\geq 1
by assumption. The preceding inequality in particular implies that

\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}\lesssim 1+\nu_{N}^{-2}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}.

As in the proof of the PDE-penalized estimator, the hypotheses of the generalized stability estimate from Lemma
2.2

are satisfied with
(u_{1},f_{1},g_{1})=(u_{0},f_{0},g)
, since
f_{0}\geq f_{\min}
and
u_{0}\in C^{2}(\overline{\mathcal{O}})

with
\|f_{0}\|_{C^{1}}\vee\|u_{0}\|_{C^{2}}\leq B
. Using Lemma
2.2
, the Sobolev embedding
H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow C^{1}(\overline{\mathcal{O}})
(since
\alpha>d/2+1
), as well as Cauchy-Schwarz, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{C^{1}}^{2}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\cdot(\hat{f}_{N}\nabla\hat{u}_{N})-g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[(1+\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{C^{1}}^{2})\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[(1+\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2})\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[1+\eta_{N}^{-4}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{4}\big]\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{4}\big]\Big)^{1/2}.

(62)

Using the same interpolation argument following (
59
) as well as the moment bounds in (
61
), we can now estimate

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{4}\big]\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\Big[\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4(\alpha-1)}{\alpha+1}}\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{\frac{8}{\alpha+1}}\Big]\Big)^{1/2}+\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{\frac{4}{\alpha+1}}\Big(\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{4(\alpha-1)}{\alpha+1}}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\lesssim N^{-\frac{2(\alpha-1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}=\nu_{N}^{2}.

Plugging the above into both factors on the right hand side of (
62
) yields that

\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big]\lesssim N^{-\frac{2(\alpha-1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}},

which completes the proof of theorem.
∎

B.2
Proof of Theorem
2.4

Step 1: Convergence of
\hat{u}_{N}

satisfying

]

\begin{split}Z_{i}&=(u_{0}-u_{0,J})^{2}(X_{i})-\|u_{0}-u_{0,J}\|_{L^{2}}^{2},\\

By arguing as in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, this implies in particular the moment bounds

\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2k}\big]\leq C_{k}\mu_{N}^{2k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{2k}\big]\leq C_{k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall N\geq 1.

(65)

Now define the
L^{2}
-counterpart of the
\tau^{2}
-functional,

\mathcal{T}^{2}(\eta,u_{0})=\|u_{0}-u_{\eta}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\mu_{N}^{2}\|\eta\|_{h^{\alpha+1}}^{2}.

The same application of Lemma

as in Step 2 of the proof of Theorem

gives that for all

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}\big(\mathcal{T}^{2}((\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N}),u_{0})\geq K^{2}\mu_{N}^{2}\big)\leq C\exp(-N\mu_{N}^{2}K/C),

as well as, for some

\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2k}\big]\leq C_{k}\mu_{N}^{2k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}^{2k}\big]\leq C_{k},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall N\geq 1.

(66)

Step 2: Convergence of
\hat{f}_{N}

Let
\theta_{0}=S(f_{0})\in h^{\alpha}
and
\theta_{0,J}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}
denote the frame coefficients (and their truncated counterpart) of
f_{0}
, and let

f_{0,J}:=\sum_{l\leq J,k\leq N_{l}}(\theta_{0,J})_{lk}\phi_{lk}\in L^{2}_{J}(\mathcal{O}).

Then we have that

\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})}\simeq\|\theta_{0}\|_{h^{\alpha}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \|f_{0}-f_{0,J}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\lesssim 2^{-2J\alpha}\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2},\penalty\ \penalty\ \|f_{0}-f_{0,J}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq 2^{-2J(\alpha-1)}\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}.

By definition of
\hat{\theta}_{N}
, we have the ‘basic inequality’

\begin{split}\|\Psi\hat{\theta}_{N}-\gamma\|_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|\hat{\theta}_{N}\|_{h^{\alpha}}^{2}\leq\|\Psi\theta_{0,J}-\gamma\|_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|\theta_{0,J}\|_{h^{\alpha}}^{2}.\end{split}

(67)

The second term on the right hand side is upper bounded by

\nu_{N}^{2}\|\theta_{0,J}\|_{h^{\alpha}}^{2}\leq\nu_{N}^{2}\|\theta_{0}\|_{h^{\alpha}}^{2}\simeq\nu_{N}^{2}\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}\lesssim N^{-\frac{2(\alpha-1)}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}.

For the first term, let us denote the coefficient selection operator up to level
J
by

S^{(J)}:H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}},

and let us also introduce the associated ‘pseudo-projection operator’

P^{(J)}:L^{2}(\mathcal{O})\to L^{2}_{J}(\mathcal{O}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ P^{(J)}(f)=\sum_{l\leq J,k}S(f)_{lk}\phi_{lk}.

We then estimate

\begin{split}\|\Psi\theta_{0,J}-\gamma\|_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}}&=\|S^{(J)}(\nabla\cdot(f_{0,J}\nabla\hat{u}_{N})-g)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}}\\
&\leq\|S(\nabla\cdot(f_{0,J}\nabla\hat{u}_{N})-g)\|_{\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N})}\\
&\simeq\|\nabla\cdot(f_{0,J}\nabla\hat{u}_{N})-g\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\\
\end{split}

Using
L_{f_{0}}u_{0}=g
, the
C^{2}(\mathcal{O})
-bound from Lemma
A.1
, and recalling that
2^{-J(\alpha-1)}\simeq\nu_{N}=N^{-\frac{\alpha-1}{2(\alpha+1)+d}}
, we further estimate this by

\begin{split}\|\nabla\cdot(f_{0,J}\nabla\hat{u}_{N})&-\nabla\cdot(f_{0}\nabla u_{0})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\\
&\leq\|\nabla\cdot(f_{0,J}\nabla(\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}))\|_{L^{2}}+\|\nabla\cdot((f_{0,J}-f_{0})\nabla u_{0})\|_{L^{2}}\\
&\lesssim\|f_{0,J}\|_{C^{1}}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}+\|f_{0,J}-f_{0}\|_{H^{1}}\|u_{0}\|_{C^{2}}\\
&\lesssim\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}+2^{-J(\alpha-1)}\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}}\\
&\lesssim\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}+\nu_{N}.\end{split}

Using the previous steps, the fact that
H^{\alpha-1}
is a multiplication algebra satisfying the inequality

\|vw\|_{H^{\alpha-1}}\lesssim\|v\|_{H^{\alpha-1}}\|w\|_{H^{\alpha-1}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall v,w\in H^{\alpha-1}.

as well as the Sobolev interpolation inequality, we now conclude that

\begin{split}\|L_{\hat{f}_{N}}\hat{u}_{N}-g\|_{L^{2}}&\lesssim\|S^{(J)}(L_{\hat{f}_{N}}\hat{u}_{N}-g)\|_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}}+2^{-J(\alpha-1)}\|L_{\hat{f}}\hat{u}_{N}-g\|_{H^{\alpha-1}}\\
&=\|\Psi\hat{\theta}_{N}-\gamma\|_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}}+2^{-J(\alpha-1)}\|L_{\hat{f}}\hat{u}_{N}-g\|_{H^{\alpha-1}}\\
&\lesssim\|\Psi\hat{\theta}_{N}-\gamma\|_{\mathbb{R}^{p_{J}}}+\nu_{N}\big(\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}+\|g\|_{H^{\alpha-1}}\big)\\
&\lesssim\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}+\nu_{N}\big(1+\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}\big)\\
&\lesssim\big(1+\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}+\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}\big)\Big(\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+1}}+\nu_{N}\Big).\end{split}

To further estimate
\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}
, note that the above arguments and the ‘basic inequality’ (
67
) also imply

\nu_{N}^{2}\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}\leq\|\nabla\cdot(f_{0,J}\nabla\hat{u}_{N})-g\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|f_{0,J}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}\lesssim\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}.

An application of the generalized stability Lemma
2.2
with the triples
(f_{0},u_{0},g)
and
(\hat{f},\hat{u}_{N},L_{\hat{f}_{N}}\hat{u}_{N})
, which is justified since
f_{0},u_{0},g
satisfy the required ‘ellipticity’ conditions, and putting everything together, we obtain that

\begin{split}\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}&\lesssim\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{C^{1}}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}+\|L_{\hat{f}_{N}}\hat{u}_{N}-g\|_{L^{2}}\\
&\lesssim\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}\\
&\qquad+\big(1+\|\hat{u}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha+1}}\big)\big(1+\nu_{N}^{-1}\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}\big)\Big(\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+1}}+\nu_{N}\Big)\\
&=:I+II.\end{split}

Then, using the moment bounds (
66
), we may perform an argument on each of the terms
I
and
II
, akin to the argument in Step 2 of the proof for the infinite-dimensional case in Section
B.1.2
following the display (
61
), to conclude the proof and obtain that

\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\big]\lesssim\nu_{N}^{2},

which completes the proof of Theorem
2.4
. ∎

B.3
Proof of Theorem

Let
\hat{f}_{N}
denote the plug-in estimator from Theorem
2.4

In particular, setting

\Phi

\sup_{\|f\|_{H^{\alpha}}\leq L,\penalty\ f\geq f_{min}+\rho/2}\|\Phi^{-1}\circ f\|_{H^{\alpha}}\leq M<\infty

Finally we define
\theta_{init}

D

(e_{k}:k\geq 1)

\theta_{init,k}=\langle\hat{F}_{N},e_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}}\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall 1\leq k\leq D,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \theta_{init}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}.

\inf_{\partial\mathcal{O}}g\geq g_{\min}
, a Feynman–Kac argument implies a uniform positivity bound
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{O}}u_{f}(x)\geq g_{\min}e^{-c\|f\|_{\infty}}>0
for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
, see display (5.36) in
[
42
]
. In particular, it holds that
u_{\min}>0
. Thus

\mathbb{P}_{f_{0}}^{N}(\inf_{x\in\mathcal{O}}\hat{u}_{N}\geq u_{\min}/2)\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}1.

By the definition of
\hat{f}_{N}
, it holds that

\begin{split}\|L_{\hat{f}_{N}}\hat{u}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}&\leq\|L_{f_{0}}\hat{u}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}\\
&\leq\|L_{f_{0}}\hat{u}_{N}-L_{f_{0}}u_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}\\
&\lesssim\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\nu_{N}^{2}\|f_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}}^{2}\\
&\lesssim\nu_{N}^{2},\end{split}

which in particular also yields that for
\beta\in(0,\alpha-d/2)
,
\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{C^{\beta}}\lesssim\|\hat{f}_{N}\|_{H^{\alpha}}\leq C^{\prime\prime}
for some constant
C^{\prime\prime}
, on the event
A_{N}
. Thus, an application of the generalized stability estimate from Lemma
2.10
with the first triplet consisting of
(u_{0},f_{0},0)
and the second triplet consisting of
(\hat{u}_{N},\hat{f}_{N},L_{\hat{f}_{N}}\hat{u}_{N})
yields that on the event
A_{N}
,

\|\hat{f}_{N}-f_{0}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\|\hat{u}_{N}-u_{0}\|_{H^{2}}+\|L_{\hat{f}_{N}}\hat{u}_{N}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\nu_{N},

which proves the second claim.
∎

C
Proof of Theorem
3.1

The proof works by adapting ideas from
[
63
]
to the current setting with ‘augmented parameters’
(\eta,\theta)
. Fix any
(\theta^{*},\eta^{*})
and let us write

\hat{u}:=u_{\hat{\eta}},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ u^{*}:=u_{\eta^{*}}.

By the definition of
(\hat{\eta}_{N},\hat{\theta}_{N})
, it holds that

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(Y_{i}-u_{\hat{\eta}}(X_{i}))^{2}+\mathcal{R}(\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta})\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(Y_{i}-u^{*}(X_{i}))^{2}+\mathcal{R}(\eta^{*},\theta^{*}),

which is the same as

\begin{split}\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u_{0})-\tau^{2}((\eta^{*},\theta^{*}),u_{0})\leq\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}(\hat{u}(X_{i})-u^{*}(X_{i})).\end{split}

(70)

Next, we note that the functional
\tau^{2}
satisfies the following ‘triangle-type’ inequality in the
\eta
-variable: For any
\eta,\eta^{\prime},\eta^{\prime\prime}\in H
with corresponding regression functions
u,u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime}
and any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
, using the inequality
\|a-b\|_{N}^{2}\leq 2\|a-c\|_{N}^{2}+2\|c-b\|_{N}^{2}

for the empirical norm and the non-negativity of the penalty

\mathcal{R}\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\tau^{2}((\eta,\theta),u_{0})

\displaystyle\leq 2\tau^{2}((\eta,\theta),u^{\prime})+2\tau^{2}((\eta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime}),u_{0}),

\displaystyle\tau^{2}((\eta,\theta),u^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\leq 2\tau^{2}((\eta,\theta),u^{\prime})+2\tau^{2}((\eta^{\prime\prime},\theta^{\prime}),u^{\prime}),

which implies

\displaystyle\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u^{*})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u_{0})-\tau^{2}((\eta^{*},\theta^{*}),u_{0}),

(71)

\displaystyle\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u^{*})-\tau^{2}((\eta^{*},\theta^{*}),u_{0}).

(72)

Denote the event whose probability we want to bound by

A_{N}:=\big\{\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u_{0})\geq 2\big(\tau^{2}((\eta^{*},\theta^{*}),u_{0})+R^{2})\big\}.

(73)

On
A_{N}
, using (
71
), it holds that

\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u^{*})\geq R^{2}.

Moreover, by the definition of
A_{N}
and using (
72
), we obtain

\displaystyle\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u_{0})-\tau^{2}((\eta^{*},\theta^{*}),u_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u_{0})+\frac{2}{3}\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u_{0})-\tau^{2}((\eta^{*},\theta^{*}),u_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{3}\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u_{0})+\frac{1}{3}\tau^{2}((\eta^{*},\theta^{*}),u_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{6}\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u^{*}).

In light of (
70
), this further implies

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}(\hat{u}(X_{i})-u^{*}(X_{i}))\geq\frac{1}{12}\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u^{*}).

The preceding argument allow us to employ ‘slicing’ to upper bound the probability of
A_{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}(A_{N})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\penalty\ \mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}\big(A_{N}\penalty\ \text{and}\penalty\ \tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u^{*})\in\big[2^{2(s-1)}R^{2},2^{2s}R^{2}\big]\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}\Big(\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}(\eta^{*},2^{s}R)}\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}(u(X_{i})-u^{*}(X_{i}))\Big|\geq\frac{1}{48}\sqrt{N}2^{2s}R^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}P_{s}

We write
\mathcal{U}_{s}=\mathcal{U}(\eta^{*},2^{s}R)
. For each
s\geq 1
, conditional on the design points
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
, the process

X_{u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\varepsilon_{i}(u(X_{i})-u^{*}(X_{i})),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ u\in\mathcal{U}_{s},

is centered Gaussian with covariance metric

d^{2}(u,u^{\prime})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{2}(u(X_{i})-u^{\prime}(X_{i}))^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\|u-u^{\prime}\|_{N}^{2},

with respect to which the set
\mathcal{U}_{s}
has diameter

D_{s}:=\sup_{u,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}_{s}}d(u,u^{\prime})\leq 2\sigma 2^{s}R.

Dudley’s inequality for suprema of sub-Gaussian processes (see, e.g., Theorems 2.3.6 and 2.3.7 in
[
21
]
or Theorem 2.2.4 in
[
65
]
) gives that for any
v\in\mathcal{U}_{s}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\big[\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{s}}\big|X_{u}\big|\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}|X_{v}|+4\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{D_{s}/2}\sqrt{H(\mathcal{U}_{s},\sigma\|\cdot\|_{N},\rho)}d\rho

\displaystyle\leq\sigma 2^{s}R+4\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{\sigma 2^{s}R}\sqrt{H(\mathcal{U}_{s},\sigma\|\cdot\|_{N},\rho)}d\rho

\displaystyle\leq 4\sqrt{2}\Psi(2^{s}R)\leq\frac{1}{96}\sqrt{N}2^{2s}R^{2},

where we can ensure the last inequality by choosing the constant
C>0
in the hypothesis (
39
) large enough. Using this and the Borell-Sudakov-Tsirelson inequality (see Theorem 2.5.8 in
[
21
]
), noting that the one-dimensional variances are upper bounded by

\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{s}}\text{Var}(X_{u})\leq\sigma^{2}2^{2s}R^{2}
,

\displaystyle P_{s}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}\Big(\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{s}}\big|X_{u}\big|-\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}_{s}}\big|X_{u}\big|\geq\frac{1}{96}\sqrt{N}2^{2s}R^{2}\Big)\leq\exp\Big(-\frac{N2^{2s}R^{2}}{2\cdot 96^{2}\sigma^{2}}\Big).

Summing the previous inequalities gives

P_{0}^{N}(A_{N})\leq\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}P_{s}\leq C\exp\big(-NR^{2}/C\sigma^{2}\Big),

where we also used that under the hypothesis (
39
), it holds that
\sqrt{N}\delta_{N}^{2}\geq C\sigma\delta_{N}
such that
NR^{2}/(2*96^{2}\sigma^{2})
can be bounded away from zero by some universal constant for any
R\geq\delta_{N}
. This proves (
40
).

To show (
41
) for
k=1
, we integrate the concentration inequality (
40
), as in Lemma 2.2 of
[
64
]
. Let us write
\hat{\tau}^{2}=\tau^{2}((\hat{\eta},\hat{\theta}),u_{0})
. For
\delta_{N}
satisfying (
39
), let

t_{N}^{*}=2(\tau^{2}((\eta^{*},\theta^{*}),u_{0})+\delta_{N}^{2}).

Then

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}[\hat{\tau}^{2}]&\leq t_{N}^{*}+\int_{t_{N}^{*}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}(\hat{\tau}^{2}\geq t)dt\leq t_{N}^{*}+C\int_{\delta_{N}}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{Nu^{2}}{C\sigma^{2}}\Big)du^{2}\\
&\leq t_{N}^{*}+\frac{C^{\prime}\sigma^{2}}{N}\exp\Big(-\frac{N\delta_{N}^{2}}{C\sigma^{2}}\Big)\leq t_{N}^{*}+\frac{C^{\prime}\sigma^{2}}{N},\end{split}

which proves the claim.

We now show the claim for higher moments of
\hat{\tau}
. For integer
k\geq 2
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{0}^{N}[\hat{\tau}^{2k}]&\leq(t_{N}^{*})^{k}+\int_{(t_{N}^{*})^{k}}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{0}^{N}(\hat{\tau}^{2}\geq t^{1/k})dt\leq(t_{N}^{*})^{k}+C\int_{\delta_{N}^{2k}}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{Nt^{1/k}}{C\sigma^{2}}\Big)dt\end{split}

(74)

Since
\Psi(R)\geq\sigma R
, the assumption
\sqrt{N}\delta_{N}^{2}\geq C\Psi(\delta_{N})

implies
N\delta_{N}^{2}/(C\sigma^{2})\geq 1
, so that Lemma
D.2

applies with
\gamma=1/k
,
a=\delta_{N}^{2k}
and
L=N/(C\sigma^{2})
. Using that lemma, we obtain that

\begin{split}\int_{\delta_{N}^{2k}}^{\infty}\exp\Big(-\frac{Nt^{1/k}}{C\sigma^{2}}\Big)dt\lesssim\frac{\sigma^{2}\delta_{N}^{2k-2}}{N}\exp\Big(-\frac{N\delta_{N}^{2}}{C\sigma^{2}}\Big)\leq\frac{\sigma^{2}\delta_{N}^{2k-2}}{N},\end{split}

as desired. ∎

D
Two auxiliary lemmas

D.1
Concentration of the empirical norm

Lemma D.1
(Concentration of empirical norm).

Let
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be some open subset with finite Lebesgue measure, and let
X_{i}\sim^{i.i.d.}\text{Unif}(\mathcal{O})
. Let
\mathcal{U}
be a class of real-valued functions on
\mathcal{O}
with uniform upper bound

\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}=:M<\infty,

where
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{O})}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{O}}|u(x)|
. There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for any
(\delta_{N}:N\geq 1)
satisfying

N\delta_{N}^{2}\geq CM^{2}H(\mathcal{U},\|\cdot\|_{\infty},\delta_{N}),

(75)

and any
R\geq C(\delta_{N}+M/\sqrt{N})
, it holds that

\mathbb{P}\Big(\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\geq 2\|u\|_{N}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for some}\penalty\ \penalty\ u\in\mathcal{U}\penalty\ \text{with}\penalty\ \|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\geq R\Big)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{NR^{2}}{CM^{2}}\right).

Lemma
D.1
is a direct specialization of Lemma A.5 in
[
52
]
to the case of uniform design
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{O})
and the function class
\mathcal{U}
with uniform envelope
M=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\|u\|_{\infty}
.
∎

For classes
\mathcal{U}
which belong to a sufficiently regular Sobolev ball

\mathcal{U}\subseteq\{u\in H^{\alpha}(\mathcal{O}):\|u\|_{H^{\alpha}}\leq M\},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \alpha>d/2,

the preceding lemma implies the following. By virtue of the Sobolev embedding
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}\lesssim\|\cdot\|_{H^{\alpha}}
and using the entropy bound
H(\mathcal{U},\|\cdot\|_{\infty},\delta_{N})\lesssim(M/\delta_{N})^{d/\alpha}
(e.g.
[
60
, Thm. 4.10.3]
), we see that the condition (
75
) is fulfilled for

\delta_{N}\gtrsim MN^{-\frac{\alpha}{2\alpha+d}},

where
\gtrsim
denotes a constant which is independent of
M,N
. It follows that for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
,

\mathbb{P}\Big(\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\geq 2\|u\|_{N}\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{for some}\penalty\ \penalty\ \|u\|_{H^{\alpha}}\leq M,\penalty\ \penalty\ \|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O})}\geq R\Big)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{NR^{2}}{C^{\prime}M^{2}}\right),

with some constant
C^{\prime}
.

D.2
A basic tail inequality

The following tail bound is used repeatedly in our proofs.

Lemma D.2
.

For any
\gamma\in(0,1)
, there exists a constant
C_{\gamma}>0
such that for all

L,a>0
with
La^{\gamma}\geq 1
,

\int_{a}^{\infty}e^{-Lt^{\gamma}}\,dt\leq C_{\gamma}\frac{a^{1-\gamma}}{L}\,e^{-La^{\gamma}}.

One sees this via substituting
u=Lt^{\gamma}
, so that

t=\left(\frac{u}{L}\right)^{1/\gamma},\qquad dt=\frac{1}{\gamma}L^{-1/\gamma}u^{\frac{1}{\gamma}-1}\,du.

Then

\int_{a}^{\infty}e^{-Lt^{\gamma}}\,dt=\frac{1}{\gamma}L^{-1/\gamma}\int_{La^{\gamma}}^{\infty}u^{\frac{1}{\gamma}-1}e^{-u}\,du=\frac{1}{\gamma}L^{-1/\gamma}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1}{\gamma},\,La^{\gamma}\right),

where
\Gamma
is the upper incomplete gamma function,

\Gamma(s,z)=\int_{z}^{\infty}u^{s-1}e^{-u}\,du.

For fixed
s>0
, the tail of
\Gamma
satisfies

\Gamma(s,z)\leq C_{s}\,z^{s-1}e^{-z}\qquad\text{for }z\geq 1.

Applying this with
s=\frac{1}{\gamma}
and
z=La^{\gamma}
, one obtains

\int_{a}^{\infty}e^{-Lt^{\gamma}}\,dt\leq C_{\gamma}\,\frac{a^{1-\gamma}}{L}\,e^{-La^{\gamma}},

with
C_{\gamma}>0
depending only on
\gamma
.
∎
