Introduction

Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are rare and highly heterogeneous malignant tumors that originate from neuroendocrine cells. Their most common locations are the lungs, pancreas, rectum, appendix, and small intestine. They are rare in head and neck region. Laryngeal neuroendocrine neoplasms (LNENs) account for less than 1 % of all laryngeal tumors, and the majority of them occur in the supraglottic region [1].

The symptoms of LNEN, such as pharyngeal paresthesia, hoarseness and dysphagia, vary depending on the location and size of the tumors. Their treatment and prognoses depend on the specific pathological type; therefore, different classifications have been developed. While many pathological types of laryngeal neuroendocrine tumors have been proposed in the past, the previously accepted classifications are typical carcinoid, atypical carcinoid, small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma and large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma. As LNENs are being better understood, their classification has evolved. According to the 2022 WHO classification of Head and Neck Tumors, epithelial neuroendocrine neoplasms are subtyped as well-differentiated and poorly differentiated epithelial neuroendocrine neoplasms; well-differentiated epithelial neuroendocrine neoplasms are termed neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) and are graded as G1 NETs, G2 NETs and G3 NETs. Poorly differentiated epithelial neuroendocrine neoplasms are termed neuroendocrine carcinomas and are further classified into small-cell and large-cell neuroendocrine carcinomas [2].G1 NETs and G2 NETs correspond to typical carcinoid and atypical carcinoid tumors respectively.

A G2 NET of the larynx is a type of LNEN that is red and lumpy and is occasionally misdiagnosed as a hemangioma. We report the case of a patient who underwent several surgeries before being diagnosed as a G2 NET. We introduce the diagnosis and treatment of a patient with a G2 NET of the larynx, and we hope that our experience can increase surgeons' understanding of LNEN.

Our work has been reported in line with the SCARE Guidelines 2023 criteria [3].

Case presentation

A 67-year-old man presented to our Otolaryngology Department after experiencing pharyngeal paresthesia for 3 years. He had smoked 20 cigarettes a day for approximately 50 years but stopped smoking approximately 1 year ago. He had undergone two surgeries on his larynx. The first operation was performed at another hospital in June 2021. The results of the histopathological examination were unknown, but the electronic laryngoscopy results were similar to those of a laryngeal hemangioma (Fig. 1A). The second operation was performed at our hospital in October 2022, and preoperative electronic laryngoscopy revealed a light pink, lobular mass on the medial wall of the right aryepiglottic fold (Fig. 1B). Biopsy revealed atypical cell hyperplasia, considering the patient's preference and the existing examination results, we performed local excision alone. Postoperative pathology revealed a laryngeal neuroendocrine neoplasm, the specific pathological type was uncertain, and immunohistochemistry was recommended for this patient, but the patient did not agree for economic reasons. We returned to this admission, the rest of his medical, surgical, family, and social history were noncontributory. Blood counts, liver and kidney function were within normal limits. Electronic laryngoscopy revealed a spherical, bright red mass located on the medial wall of the right aryepiglottic fold (Fig. 1C). There was no obvious abnormality in the lung computed tomography. Cervical magnetic resonance revealed a spherical mass on the medial wall of the right aryepiglottic fold and multiple lymph nodes on both sides of the jaw and neck. Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance of the neck revealed no enhancement in the mass, but the right aryepiglottic fold was mildly enhanced (Fig. 2). On the basis of the above examinations and his previous surgical history, a clinical diagnosis of laryngeal neuroendocrine neoplasm was made initially.

MR revealed a spherical mass on the medial wall of the right aryepiglottic fold, contrast-enhanced MR revealed no enhancement in the mass, but the right aryepiglottic fold was mildly enhanced.

Then, we performed partial laryngectomy and neck lymph node dissection for this patient. No malignancies found in the safety margin during the operation. Pathology and immunohistochemistry revealed the tumor to be a well-differentiated epithelial neuroendocrine tumor, Grade 2. No cervical lymph node metastasis. Consequently, the diagnosis was modified to a G2 neuroendocrine tumor of the larynx. The patient was discharged from the hospital after the operation and was followed up regularly. At the 3-month postoperative follow-up, we performed electronic laryngoscopy to evaluate the patient's condition and found no signs of recurrence. At the 1-year follow-up after the operation, the patient's condition was good, and there was no sign of recurrence.

Discussion

Laryngeal G2 NET lesions are macroscopically present as dark red polypoid or nodular masses located on the laryngeal surface of the epiglottis and aryepiglottic fold, and the surface is still smooth, which is very different from the cauliflower-like or ulcer-like growth pattern of common malignant tumors such as laryngeal cancer, so they are easily misdiagnosed as benign tumors such as hemangiomas.

The disease is mainly diagnosed via a biopsy with immunohistochemical studies and electron microscopic investigations. Specific pathologic identification is highly important for determining the correct treatment and prognosis. However, it is difficult to make a definite diagnosis via only light microscopy. In this case, the patient underwent a biopsy before the second operation, but the pathologic examination revealed atypical cell hyperplasia, and a definite diagnosis was not reached. This may be related to the superficiality of the pathological materials used and the small sample size. Considering the patient's preference and the existing examination results, we performed local excision alone. Postoperative pathological examination revealed a laryngeal neuroendocrine neoplasm, and immunohistochemistry was recommended to clarify the classification. The patient refused immunohistochemistry for economic reasons. It was not until after the third operation that immunohistochemistry was performed to clarify the classification. The limitation of this case was that the pathology was unknown after the first operation, and the patient did not undergo immunohistochemistry after the second operation. If the pathology after the first operation is known, the second operation may be different. If the patient had immunohistochemistry after the second operation, we might have intervened earlier. To some extent, these can lead to recurrence.

Laryngeal atypical carcinoids are highly invasive and prone to metastasis. At present, no specific and comprehensive treatment protocol exists for laryngeal atypical carcinoids [4]. The recommended treatment for atypical carcinoid tumors is radical surgical resection with cervical lymph node dissection. Selective neck dissection should be performed even in patients with N0 tumors because of the high incidence of both early cervical metastasis and subsequent involvement of the cervical lymph nodes [5]. In this case, although the specific pathological type was not clear, according to the second operation, the pathology was a neuroendocrine neoplasm. We considered the local recurrence of NETs and the existing examination results; thus, partial laryngectomy with cervical lymph node dissection was performed. Postoperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy were not performed. Because atypical carcinoids are not responsive to chemotherapy, the role of radiotherapy is controversial. Tom P. van der Laan et al. conducted a meta-analysis of 163 reports of atypical laryngeal carcinoids and revealed that, compared with patients who underwent surgery, patients who received initial radiotherapy had lower disease-specific survival (DSS) rates, whereas patients who received postoperative radiotherapy had worse DSS [6]. However, a multicenter study from the United States has shown that radiotherapy combined with other methods is valuable for treating locally advanced or metastatic AC of the lung [7].

Regardless of adequate treatment, most patients will experience recurrence (62.5 %) after 5 years of follow-up, and many of them will have distant metastasis (69.4 %). Extending the follow-up period to 10 years revealed a further decrease in disease-specific survival (DSS) compared with the 5-year DSS. Notably, recurrence may occur after the traditional 5-year follow-up period [6]. Therefore, long-term follow-up (10 years or more) is strongly advised for these patients [8].

Conclusion

The recommended treatment for G2 Neuroendocrine Tumor (G2 NET) of the larynx is partial laryngectomy and neck lymph node dissection, local resection is easy to relapse. The diagnosis depends on immunohistochemistry; it is difficult to make a definite diagnosis via only light microscopy.
