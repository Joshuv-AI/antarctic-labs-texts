Monoallelic SH2B1 Variants in Neurodevelopmental Disorders: Phenotypic Expansion and Functional Characterization of Rare Variants
Authors: Xin Xu, Gege Yuan, Bixia Zheng, Chunli Wang, Wei Zhou, Songming Huang, Senjie Du, Yikang He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground SH2B1 encodes an adaptor protein involved in metabolic regulation and body‐weight control. Although SH2B1 haploinsufficiency is a recognized contributor to obesity in 16p11.2 deletion syndrome, the relationship between rare monoallelic SH2B1 variants and neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) remains poorly characterized.MethodsWe analyzed genetic and clinical data from a cohort of individuals with NDDs. The identified rare SH2B1 variants were evaluated using bioinformatic tools and structural modeling. Functional consequences were assessed in vitro through analyses of protein expression, subcellular localization, and extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK) pathway activation via EGF stimulation assays.ResultsFive individuals carrying rare monoallelic SH2B1 variants presented with syndromic NDDs, including developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, dysmorphic features, and hyperphagia. Whole‐exome sequencing identified five heterozygous SH2B1 variants, including three missense variants (c.52C>G, p.Leu18Val; c.696A>T, p.Arg232Ser; c.1141C>T, p.Pro381Ser) and two truncating variants (c.90_91delTG, p.Cys30Ter; c.121_130dup, p.Arg44LeufsTer30); four of these arose de novo, whereas c.696A>T (p.Arg232Ser) was maternally inherited. The individual carrying the maternally inherited p.Arg232Ser variant presented with elevated BMI and hyperphagia. In overexpression assays, truncating variants showed reduced protein expression and abnormal subcellular localization, whereas missense variants retained comparable protein levels but showed increased EGF‐induced ERK phosphorylation in vitro, suggesting altered ERK signaling responses.ConclusionsThis study further delineates the clinical spectrum associated with rare monoallelic SH2B1 variants in individuals with NDDs. Functional analyses suggest variant‐specific effects on protein expression, subcellular localization, and ERK signaling responses. These findings support the consideration of SH2B1 in genetic analyses of NDDs, particularly in individuals with co‐occurring metabolic abnormalities.Keywords: monoallelic variants, neurodevelopmental disorder, SH2B1 gene

Introduction

The 220‐kb distal 16p11.2 deletion, which encompasses multiple genes including SH2B1, is a well‐established copy‐number variant (CNV) associated with severe early‐onset obesity and neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) (Maillard et al. 2015; Rein and Yan 2020; Chung et al. 2021). Affected individuals exhibit a range of phenotypes, including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), intellectual disability (ID), and epilepsy (Shinawi et al. 2010). Evidence indicates that the increased risk of early‐onset obesity and type 2 diabetes in this syndrome is largely attributable to haploinsufficiency of SH2B1 (Bochukova et al. 2010; Hanssen et al. 2023).

SH2B1 (Src homology 2 [SH2] B adaptor protein 1; OMIM: 608937) encodes a multidomain adaptor protein that is widely expressed in the central nervous system and metabolic tissues (Rui 2014). It plays a critical role in regulating energy homeostasis and cognitive function, primarily through its predominant β isoform (Argetsinger et al. 2023). Specifically, SH2B1 promotes leptin and insulin signaling by binding via its SH2 domain to phosphorylated tyrosines in Janus kinase 2 and the insulin receptor (Morris et al. 2010). Furthermore, it activates the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway within the hypothalamic‐dorsal raphe circuit to suppress appetite and enhance energy expenditure (Li et al. 2024).

The contribution of SH2B1 to the 16p11.2 deletion phenotype has been further demonstrated in animal models. Global Sh2b1 knockout mice develop key metabolic features of the syndrome, including obesity, insulin resistance, and hepatic steatosis (Ren et al. 2007), while neuron‐specific restoration of SH2B1 rescues these defects, supporting its role in neurometabolic integration. In addition, SH2B1 modulates hippocampal extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK) signaling during neurodevelopment and influences cognitive functions such as working memory and fluid intelligence (Du et al. 2023). This is particularly relevant because the ERK signaling pathway is a central regulator of neurodevelopment, and its dysregulation has been implicated in multiple NDDs.

Genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) have implicated SH2B1 polymorphisms in both metabolic traits and cognitive phenotypes (Volckmar et al. 2012). Furthermore, loss‐of‐function variants in SH2B1 have been associated with neurobehavioral abnormalities in obesity cohorts (Doche et al. 2012). However, the genotypic and phenotypic spectrum of rare monoallelic SH2B1 variants in NDDs remains poorly characterized. Moreover, unlike the well‐documented haploinsufficiency mechanism underlying 16p11.2 deletions, the mechanisms by which such monoallelic variants may contribute to NDDs have not been systematically investigated.

In this study, we characterized five individuals carrying rare monoallelic SH2B1 variants identified in an NDD cohort. Clinical phenotyping revealed diverse neurodevelopmental features, which may help delineate the phenotypic spectrum associated with rare monoallelic SH2B1 variants. We further analyzed the genotypic and phenotypic characteristics of these cases and explored the potential functional consequences of the identified variants through structural modeling and in vitro functional assays.

Materials and Methods

Patients and Ethics Approval

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using an institutional WES repository comprising 5066 families of individuals with NDDs who had been evaluated at the Children's Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Wang et al. 2023). Although this repository was partly described in our previous cohort‐level study of NDDs with microcephaly, SH2B1‐related cases and the functional consequences of SH2B1 variants were not systematically analyzed in that report. From this cohort, five patients carrying monoallelic SH2B1 variants were identified. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children's Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (No. 202508022‐1). Written informed consent for publication of clinical information, genetic data, and images was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants.

Clinical information was retrospectively collected from medical records and genetic testing reports, including developmental milestones, speech and language abilities, behavioral features, neurological examination findings, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings, anthropometric measurements, and dysmorphic features. Developmental delay, ID, ASD, attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) or hyperactivity, speech delay, and dysmorphic features were assessed by trained rehabilitation therapists based on clinical history, physical examination, and standardized clinical assessments. ASD‐related features were evaluated using the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS), Autism Behavior Checklist (ABC), and Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS). ADHD‐related features were evaluated based on DSM‐5 diagnostic criteria, supported by clinical observation and auditory–visual attention (AVA) testing. For CARS, total scores range from 15 to 60; scores below 30 indicate the non‐autistic range, scores of 30–36.5 indicate mild‐to‐moderate ASD symptoms, and scores ≥ 37 indicate severe ASD symptoms. The severity of ID was determined according to available intelligence quotient (IQ) or developmental assessment results, together with clinical evaluation, and was classified as mild, moderate, severe, or profound, generally corresponding to IQ ranges of approximately 50–69, 35–49, 20–34, and < 20, respectively. Clinical features were also mapped to Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms when available.

Whole‐Exome Sequencing

Exome enrichment was performed using the xGen Exome Research Panel v2.0 (Integrated DNA Technologies, Iowa, USA), followed by paired‐end sequencing (2 × 100 bp) on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform. Raw reads were processed using fastp with a base‐quality threshold of Q20 and aligned to the human reference genome GRCh37/hg19 using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA v0.5.9‐r16). Single‐nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions or deletions (indels) were called using HaplotypeCaller in the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK v4.1.4.1). Sample‐level sequencing quality‐control metrics were retrieved from archived QC reports for all five probands and summarized as cohort‐wide ranges. Across the five probands, target‐region coverage ranged from 99.70% to 99.90%, with 99.20%–99.50%, 98.80%–99.20%, and 98.40%–98.90% of target bases covered at depths of > 10×, > 20×, and > 30×, respectively. The Q20 and Q30 rates ranged from 98% to 99% and from 93% to 98%, respectively. At the five SH2B1 variant sites, total read depths ranged from 29× to 149×. The two small indels, c.90_91delTG and c.121_130dup, were supported by 21 of 55 reads and 23 of 58 reads, respectively. A sample‐level mean coverage of ≥ 20× was used as a quality‐control criterion.

Variants with an allele frequency greater than 1% in public databases, including gnomAD, the 1000 Genomes Project, dbSNP, ExAC, and ESP, were excluded. The potential effects of missense variants were evaluated using PROVEAN, SIFT, PolyPhen‐2 (HDIV and HVAR), MutationTaster, M‐CAP, CADD, and REVEL. Computational ACMG/AMP criteria (Richards et al. 2015) were applied only when multiple validated in silico tools provided concordant predictions; neither PP3 nor BP4 was applied when the predictions were discordant. Rare coding and canonical splice‐site variants were prioritized according to variant type, inheritance pattern, segregation status, gene‐disease association, and consistency with the clinical phenotype.

WES‐based CNV analysis was performed using CANOE, CNVnator, DeviCNV, and ExomeDepth. Candidate CNVs were annotated using the Database of Genomic Variants and evaluated with reference to ClinGen dosage‐sensitivity data and DECIPHER. No additional pathogenic or likely pathogenic SNVs/indels or clinically relevant CNVs, including 16p11.2 deletions involving SH2B1, were identified in the five probands within the detection limits of WES. All five SH2B1 variants, including c.90_91delTG and c.121_130dup, were independently confirmed by Sanger sequencing using target‐specific primers (Table S1).

Structural Modeling Analysis

The full‐length amino acid sequences of wild‐type SH2B1 and the three missense variants, p.Leu18Val, p.Arg232Ser, and p.Pro381Ser, were submitted separately to the SWISS‐MODEL workspace for structural modeling (Waterhouse et al. 2018). For all four sequences, the top‐ranked model was generated using the AlphaFold DB model of human SH2B1 (template ID Q9NRF2.1.A, chain A; AlphaFold v2), with full‐length coverage of residues 1–756. The target–template sequence identity was 100.00% for the wild‐type sequence and 99.87% for each mutant sequence, and the global model quality estimate (GMQE) was 0.57 for all models. QMEANDisCo global and QMEAN Z‐scores were not computed by SWISS‐MODEL because the selected template originated from the AlphaFold Database. The resulting models were visualized using PyMOL, and the local hydrogen‐bonding environments surrounding residues 18, 232, and 381 were compared qualitatively. These model‐based comparisons were considered hypothesis‐generating and were not interpreted as direct evidence of altered protein stability or pathogenicity.

Plasmid Vector Construction

The cDNA of human SH2B1 was cloned into the pcDNA3.1–3 × FLAG eukaryotic expression vector using the ClonExpress Entry One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, C112‐01). The variants (p.Leu18Val, p.Cys30Ter, p.Arg44LeufsTer30, p.Arg232Ser, p.Pro381Ser) were introduced into the wild‐type construct using PCR‐based site‐directed mutagenesis with the Mut Express II Fast Mutagenesis Kit V2 (Vazyme, C214‐01) and gene‐specific primers. All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Cell Culture, Transfection, and EGF Stimulation

HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. At 70%–80% confluence, cells were transiently transfected with plasmids encoding either wild‐type SH2B1β or the indicated mutants using polyethyleneimine (PEI) at a DNA:PEI ratio of 1:3 (w/w). After 4–6 h, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. For EGF stimulation assays, transfected cells were serum‐starved for 18 h and then treated with 30 ng/mL recombinant human EGF (Beyotime, P01133). Cells were harvested under basal conditions and at 5 and 15 min after stimulation for subsequent immunoblotting analysis.

Western Blotting

Protein lysates were extracted from HEK293T cells using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, P0012). Equal amounts of protein, 30 μg per lane, were separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti‐FLAG (1:1000; Abcam, ab49763), anti‐GAPDH (1:5000; Proteintech, 10494‐1‐AP), anti‐phospho‐ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204; 1:1000; Proteintech, 28733‐1‐AP), and anti‐ERK1/2 (1:1000; Proteintech, 11257‐1‐AP). After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000; Beyotime, A0208/A0216) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) substrate and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system. Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software (Bio‐Rad), and densitometric analysis was further performed using Fiji ImageJ.

Confocal Microscopy

For subcellular localization analysis, HEK293T cells were seeded on glass coverslips and transfected with FLAG‐tagged SH2B1 constructs for 48 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‐100 for 10 min at room temperature. After blocking with 5% BSA, the cells were incubated with a mouse anti‐FLAG primary antibody (1:1000; Abcam, ab49763) overnight at 4°C. After three washes with PBS, the cells were incubated with a FITC‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse secondary antibody (1:500; Beyotime, A0568) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal microscope with a 63 × oil‐immersion objective. Fluorophores were excited using laser lines at 405 nm for DAPI and 488 nm for FITC.

Statistical Analysis

Clinical and genetic findings were summarized descriptively. Quantitative data from Western blot experiments are presented as the mean ± SEM from three independent biological replicates. Each biological replicate represented an independent cell culture, transfection, treatment, protein extraction, and Western blot experiment. Band intensities were quantified using Image Lab software (Bio‐Rad) and Fiji ImageJ. FLAG‐SH2B1 signals were normalized to GAPDH for protein expression analysis, whereas p‐ERK1/2 signals were normalized to total ERK1/2 for ERK signaling analysis. Normalized values were expressed as fold changes relative to the corresponding control group within the same experiment.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 10.0. For comparisons between WT and multiple SH2B1 mutants in protein expression assays, one‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple‐comparison test was used. For EGF stimulation assays involving multiple constructs and time points, two‐way ANOVA followed by Sidak's multiple‐comparison test was used for comparisons between NC and WT at the corresponding time points in Figure 1A. Two‐way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple‐comparison test was used to compare each mutant construct with WT at the corresponding time points in Figure 1B. Results

Identification and Characterization of Five Rare SH2B1 Variants in an NDD Cohort

From a cohort of individuals diagnosed with NDDs at the Children's Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, we identified five unrelated probands carrying rare heterozygous variants in SH2B1 (GenBank: NM_001387430.1) (Figure 2A). Their clinical and genetic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. All five probands exhibited speech delay, and three were either nonverbal or had severely limited verbal output. Global developmental delay (GDD) and ID were present in all cases (5/5), with severity ranging from mild to severe. ASD was diagnosed in three probands (3/5; Individuals 1, 3, and 5), and ADHD or prominent hyperactivity was reported in all five (5/5). Hypotonia was observed in Individuals 1 and 5. Additional neurological features included structural brain abnormalities on MRI, such as enlarged lateral ventricles, mega cisterna magna, and widened left temporal extra‐axial spaces. Dysmorphic facial features, including bushy eyebrows, low‐set ears, a long philtrum, and a thin or tent‐shaped upper lip were observed in three probands (Individuals 2, 4, and 5) (Figure 2B).

Notably, Individual 4 was a male aged 12 years and 10 months who presented with obesity (BMI 27.5 kg/m2, +3.2 SD) and hyperphagia. He also had mild ID, hydronephrosis, strabismus, and hepatic steatosis (Figure 2C). His fasting glucose and insulin levels were 4.51 mmol/L and 15.8 mU/L, respectively, yielding a calculated HOMA‐IR of 3.17. Liver function testing showed mild elevations in alanine aminotransferase and γ‐glutamyl transferase levels (46 U/L and 26 U/L, respectively), whereas aspartate aminotransferase, bilirubin, and albumin levels were within the reference ranges. WES identified a maternally inherited missense variant in SH2B1 (c.696A>T; p.Arg232Ser). Although his birth weight and early childhood weight were within normal limits, hyperphagia and rapid weight gain emerged during puberty. His mother, who carried the same variant, was overweight (BMI 25.6 kg/m2) but did not show cognitive impairment. His father did not carry the p.Arg232Ser variant, confirming maternal inheritance; however, paternal metabolic laboratory testing was not performed. In contrast, the other four probands, aged 4–8 years, had normal BMI values (15.9–17.4 kg/m2) and showed no signs of hyperphagia.

Genetic Landscape of Identified and Previously Reported SH2B1 Variants

Whole‐exome sequencing analysis identified five heterozygous SH2B1 variants in our cohort, comprising three missense variants (c.52C>G, p.Leu18Val; c.696A>T, p.Arg232Ser; c.1141C>T, p.Pro381Ser) and two truncating variants (c.90_91delTG, p.Cys30Ter; c.121_130dup, p.Arg44LeufsTer30). Among these, four occurred de novo, whereas the c.696A>T (p.Arg232Ser) variant was maternally inherited. Notably, p.Leu18Val was observed once among 155,424 alleles in gnomAD v2.1.1, corresponding to an allele frequency of approximately 6.4 × 10−6 (Table 1). According to ACMG/AMP criteria, the two truncating variants were classified as pathogenic, whereas the three missense variants were conservatively classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS). Detailed variant‐level classifications and supporting criteria are summarized in Table S2. No additional pathogenic or likely pathogenic SNVs/indels or clinically relevant CNVs, including 16p11.2 deletions, were identified in these five probands within the detection limits of WES. These variants are distributed across different functional domains of the SH2B1 protein. Specifically, the three missense variants are located upstream of the N‐terminal pleckstrin homology (PH) domain (p.Leu18Val), within the region overlapping the reported nuclear export sequence and between the key hydrophobic residues Leu231 and Leu233 (p.Arg232Ser), and in the region between the PH and Src homology 2 (SH2) domains (p.Pro381Ser) (Figure 3A). The two truncating variants (p.Cys30Ter and p.Arg44LeufsTer30) lie within the dimerization domain (DD) and are predicted to introduce premature termination codons, which may trigger nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay (NMD) at the endogenous transcript level. However, endogenous NMD was not directly assessed in this study.

SH2B1 appears to be highly constrained for loss‐of‐function (LoF) variation, as reflected by a probability of LoF intolerance (pLI) score of 1.0 in gnomAD v4.1.0. In contrast, its missense Z‐score of 0.8 is below the threshold of 3.09 for high missense intolerance (Harrison et al. 2019), suggesting greater tolerance for missense variation in the general population. Notably, the missense variants identified in this study cluster in specific regions and show missense tolerance ratio (MTR) scores suggestive of mild intolerance (Figure 3B). Furthermore, all affected residues are evolutionarily conserved (Figure 3C), supporting their potential functional relevance.

To explore the potential structural consequences of the identified missense variants, the SWISS‐MODEL‐generated structures were qualitatively compared using PyMOL. The modeled p.Arg232Ser structure showed a difference in the predicted hydrogen‐bonding interaction between residue 232 and Glu299 compared with the wild‐type structure. Local differences in the predicted hydrogen‐bonding environments were also observed for p.Leu18Val and p.Pro381Ser (Figure 3D). These model‐based observations are hypothesis‐generating and do not independently demonstrate altered protein stability or pathogenicity.

To date, multiple rare SH2B1 variants have been reported in the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD) and published studies (Doche et al. 2012; Pearce et al. 2014; Li et al. 2016; Flores et al. 2019; Fonseca et al. 2022) (Table 2). These variants are distributed across the gene, and no clear genotype–phenotype correlation has been established for specific protein domains and clinical features. Obesity appears to be more prominent in adolescent and adult patients, with body mass index (BMI) often exceeding 30 kg/m2 in individuals aged 13 years or older. However, markedly elevated BMI values have also been reported in some younger children, including one aged 4.7 years, suggesting that metabolic abnormalities may emerge during childhood in a subset of individuals carrying rare SH2B1 variants. Neurodevelopmental and behavioral features, including developmental speech or learning difficulties, ASD, ADHD, aggression, anxiety, and social withdrawal, have also been reported in some individuals. However, because several previous reports were based primarily on obesity‐ or metabolism‐focused cohorts, neurodevelopmental phenotypes were not systematically assessed in all cases. Therefore, the variants summarized in Table 2 should not be interpreted as established NDD‐causing alleles. Long‐term follow‐up combining metabolic surveillance with neurodevelopmental and behavioral assessment may help clarify the phenotypic spectrum associated with rare SH2B1 variants.

Functional Effects of SH2B1 Variant Overexpression

The SH2B1 gene produces four alternatively spliced isoforms, among which SH2B1β is the most extensively studied because of its important role in cellular signaling pathways. All five variants identified in this study are located N‐terminal to the SH2 domain. To investigate their functional consequences, we generated FLAG‐tagged expression constructs encoding wild‐type (WT) SH2B1β, two truncating variants (p.Cys30Ter and p.Arg44LeufsTer30), and three missense variants (p.Leu18Val, p.Arg232Ser, and p.Pro381Ser). These constructs were transiently expressed in HEK293T cells.

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that WT SH2B1β was predominantly distributed in the cytoplasm and at the plasma membrane. The three missense variants, including p.Arg232Ser, showed no gross alteration in steady‐state subcellular localization compared with WT under the conditions examined. Thus, although p.Arg232Ser overlaps the reported nuclear export sequence, it did not produce detectable nuclear enrichment in this qualitative assay. In contrast, the severely truncated p.Cys30Ter and p.Arg44LeufsTer30 proteins showed a diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic distribution with reduced membrane‐associated staining (Figure 4A). These truncating variants also resulted in markedly reduced protein expression (Figure 4B,C; Figure S1), whereas the missense variants did not significantly affect SH2B1β protein levels.

The two truncating variants are predicted to introduce premature termination codons and may trigger NMD at the endogenous transcript level. Because endogenous NMD was not directly assessed in this study, subsequent signaling analyses focused on the three missense variants. Recent evidence suggests that Sh2b1 deletion in the mouse hippocampus leads to hyperactivation of the ERK signaling pathway and is associated with deficits in fluid intelligence (Du et al. 2023). Although SH2B1 is most commonly associated with leptin and insulin signaling in metabolic contexts, we used EGF stimulation to assess ERK activation for methodological reasons. EGF robustly and reproducibly activates the RTK–RAS–MAPK/ERK pathway in HEK293T cells, which have high transfection efficiency and consistent responsiveness, making them well suited for standardized signaling assays. We therefore assessed ERK phosphorylation in HEK293T cells expressing WT or mutant SH2B1β under basal conditions and after EGF stimulation. Compared with empty vector controls, overexpression of WT SH2B1β mildly reduced ERK phosphorylation after EGF stimulation (Figure 1A), consistent with its role as a negative regulator of ERK signaling. All three missense variants showed increased EGF‐induced ERK phosphorylation compared with WT (Figure 1B), suggesting altered regulation of ERK signaling in this assay. Together, these findings indicate that the missense variants may affect ERK signaling responses in a heterologous overexpression system, although further studies are needed to determine their mechanistic relevance in patients.

Discussion

Although SH2B1 variants have been associated with obesity risk, their phenotypic spectrum within NDD cohorts remains poorly characterized. In this study, we identified five rare SH2B1 variants in individuals presenting with NDDs, including GDD, ID, ASD, facial dysmorphism, and hyperphagia. SH2B1 is highly intolerant to protein‐disrupting variants, consistent with clinical reports linking SH2B1 haploinsufficiency to 16p11.2 deletion syndrome, which is characterized by severe obesity and neurodevelopmental impairment. These findings suggest that rare monoallelic SH2B1 variants may be associated with heterogeneous neurodevelopmental manifestations.

Compared with previous obesity‐ or metabolism‐focused cohorts, our cases were identified from an NDD cohort and showed prominent neurodevelopmental and behavioral features, including speech delay, ID, ASD, ADHD, or hyperactivity, and dysmorphic features. The presence of hyperphagia and obesity in one adolescent further suggests that metabolic manifestations may emerge later in development. These findings expand the phenotypic spectrum associated with rare monoallelic SH2B1 variants and support the assessment of both neurodevelopmental and metabolic features in affected individuals.

In addition, the present study builds on an institutional WES repository that was partly described in our previous cohort‐level analysis of NDDs with microcephaly. Whereas the previous work mainly assessed the diagnostic yield of WES and prioritized candidate genes, the current study provides a gene‐focused analysis of rare monoallelic SH2B1 variants by integrating clinical characterization, structural modeling, and functional assays.

Among the five variants, p.Arg232Ser was associated with the most prominent obesity phenotype in this cohort. The maternally inherited p.Arg232Ser variant warrants cautious interpretation. Although the proband presented with hyperphagia, obesity, and mild ID, his mother, who carried the same variant, was overweight but showed no clear neurodevelopmental phenotype. This intrafamilial difference may reflect variable expressivity, reduced penetrance, or additional modifying factors. Accordingly, p.Arg232Ser was conservatively classified as a variant of uncertain significance, and additional cases are needed to clarify its clinical significance. The p.Arg232Ser variant overlaps the reported nuclear export sequence of SH2B1β and lies near the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain. However, it did not cause detectable nuclear enrichment in our qualitative confocal assay, suggesting that nuclear export was not completely disrupted, although subtle effects on nucleocytoplasmic shuttling cannot be excluded. The increased EGF‐induced ERK phosphorylation observed for p.Arg232Ser may therefore represent a more readily detectable functional consequence in this system. In contrast, the diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic distribution of p.Cys30Ter and p.Arg44LeufsTer30 may reflect loss of membrane retention and passive nuclear access rather than canonical NLS‐ or NES‐mediated trafficking. The PH domain of SH2B1 has been implicated in energy balance and glucose homeostasis. Previous studies have reported elevated homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) scores in individuals with variants adjacent to the PH domain, such as G238C, R270Q, and M388V (Flores et al. 2019). Animal studies have shown that homozygous deletions affecting the SH2B1 PH domain lead to obesity, insulin resistance, and glucose intolerance. This is consistent with the pronounced hyperphagia and elevated BMI observed in the pediatric carrier of the p.Arg232Ser variant in our study.

The p.Leu18Val variant warrants cautious interpretation because leucine‐to‐valine is a conservative substitution and the variant is present at a very low frequency in gnomAD. Nevertheless, it occurred de novo at an evolutionarily conserved residue and was associated with increased EGF‐induced ERK phosphorylation in our overexpression assay. Given the limitations of this heterologous system, p.Leu18Val remains classified as a VUS.

ERK signaling plays a critical role in synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory (Peng et al. 2010). Its dysregulation has been implicated in multiple NDDs, including RASopathies and certain forms of ID (Cui et al. 2008; Samuels et al. 2009; Motta et al. 2020). Furthermore, many genes associated with ASD risk converge on ERK‐related signaling cascades, suggesting that ERK pathway dysregulation may represent a common mechanism underlying neurodevelopmental impairment (Gazestani et al. 2019). For instance, mice with 16p11.2 deletion exhibit anxiety‐like behaviors and memory deficits accompanied by elevated ERK activity during neurogenesis. These abnormalities can be partially ameliorated by ERK inhibitors in adult animals (Pucilowska et al. 2015, 2018). Consistent with this concept, Du et al. showed that Sh2b1 knockout in hippocampal CA1 GABAergic interneurons enhanced ERK phosphorylation and impaired fluid intelligence‐related behaviors, which could be partially rescued by local ERK pathway inhibition (Du et al. 2023).

In our study, overexpression of SH2B1 missense variants in HEK293T cells increased EGF‐induced ERK1/2 phosphorylation, suggesting that these variants may affect ERK signaling responses in vitro. Because this assay used a heterologous overexpression system with EGF stimulation, future studies in neuronal or metabolically relevant models are needed to clarify the role of SH2B1‐dependent ERK regulation in neurodevelopmental and metabolic phenotypes. In addition, brain MRI was not performed in Individuals 2 and 4, limiting the assessment of potential structural brain abnormalities and the completeness of clinical characterization in these cases.

Conclusion

This study further delineates the phenotypic spectrum associated with rare monoallelic SH2B1 variants in individuals with NDDs through genetic and clinical characterization of five cases. We identified five rare SH2B1 variants and evaluated their potential functional consequences using bioinformatic predictions and in vitro functional assays. These analyses revealed variant‐specific effects on protein expression, subcellular localization, and ERK signaling responses. Our findings support the consideration of SH2B1 in genetic analyses of NDDs, particularly in individuals with co‐occurring hyperphagia or obesity, and suggest that altered ERK signaling may represent a potential mechanism warranting further investigation.
