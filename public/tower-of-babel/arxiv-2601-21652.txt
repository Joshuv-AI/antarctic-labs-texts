Improved Approximations for Dial-a-Ride Problems
Jingyang Zhao, Mingyu Xiao

Abstract
The multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem (mDaRP) is a fundamental vehicle routing problem with pickups and deliveries, widely applicable in ride-sharing, economics, and transportation. Given a set of n n locations, h h vehicles of identical capacity λ \lambda located at various depots, and m m ride requests each defined by a source and a destination, the goal is to plan non-preemptive routes that serve all requests while minimizing the total travel distance, ensuring that no vehicle carries more than λ \lambda passengers at any time. The best-known approximation ratio for the mDaRP remains 𝒪 ⁡ ( λ ​ log ⁡ m ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\lambda}\log m) . We propose two simple algorithms: the first achieves the same approximation ratio of 𝒪 ⁡ ( λ ​ log ⁡ m ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\lambda}\log m) with improved running time, and the second attains an approximation ratio of 𝒪 ⁡ ( m λ ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\frac{m}{\lambda}}) . A combination of them yields an approximation ratio of 𝒪 ⁡ ( n 4 ​ log 1 2 ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt[4]{n}\log^{\frac{1}{2}}n) under m = Θ ⁡ ( n ) m=\Theta(n) . Moreover, for the case m ≫ n m\gg n , by extending our algorithms, we derive an 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n\log n}) -approximation algorithm, which also improves the current best-known approximation ratio of 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ log 2 ​ n ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}\log^{2}n) for the classic (single-vehicle) DaRP, obtained by Gupta et al. (ACM Trans. Algorithms, 2010).



1
Introduction

In the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem
(
Luo et al., 2023
)
, we are given a complete graph
G=(V,E,w)
with
|V|=n
locations, a weight function
w:E\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a set of
h
identical vehicles
K\subseteq V
, and a (multi-)set of
m
requests
\mathcal{R}=\{(s_{i},t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}\subseteq V\times V
.
All vehicles have the same capacity of
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
The goal is to plan routes for vehicles to satisfy all requests, while minimizing the total travel distance. Each vehicle is not required to return to its starting point after completing its assigned requests.
Moreover, for each vehicle, the number of requests served on its route must never exceed its capacity at any point in time.
We assume that the edge-weight function
w
is a
metric
, satisfying symmetry and the triangle inequality; that is, for any
a,b,c\in V
,
w(a,a)=0
and
w(a,c)=w(c,a)\leq w(a,b)+w(b,c)
.
When
h=1
, the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem is known as the classic dial-a-ride problem
(
Stein, 1978
,
Psaraftis, 1980
,
Cordeau, 2006
,
Ho et al., 2018
)
.

In the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem, each request
(s_{i},t_{i})
corresponds to an item that needs to be transported from the source
s_{i}
to the designated destination
t_{i}
.
Based on the properties of requests, two versions are commonly considered in the literature: the
preemptive
version and the
non-preemptive
version.
In the preemptive version, after picking up an item at its source, the vehicle is allowed to temporarily drop off the item at some intermediate vertices before delivering it to its destination.
In the non-preemptive version, once the vehicle picks up an item at its source, it must carry the item until it reaches its destination without any intermediate drop-offs.

As in the previous studies
(
Gupta et al., 2010
,
Luo et al., 2023
)
, we study the non-preemptive multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem.

1.1
Related Work

We briefly review approximation algorithms for the dial-a-ride problem and the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem, respectively.

DaPR.

When
\lambda=1
, the dial-a-ride problem is also known as the stacker-crane problem
(
Frederickson et al., 1976
,
Frederickson et al., 1978
)
.
Furthermore, if the source and destination of each request are the same, the stacker-crane problem further reduces to the traveling salesman problem.
For the traveling salesman problem,
Christofides, 2022
proposed a
\frac{3}{2}
-approximation algorithm.
Recently,
Karlin et al., 2021
,
Karlin et al., 2023
improved the ratio to
\frac{3}{2}-10^{-36}
.
For the stacker-crane problem,
Frederickson et al., 1976
,
Frederickson et al., 1978
proposed a well-known
\frac{9}{5}
-approximation algorithm, which remains the best-known to date.

When all requested objects are identical, the dial-a-ride problem becomes the
\lambda
-delivery traveling salesman problem
(
Anily and Bramel, 1999
,
Chalasani and Motwani, 1999
,
Charikar et al., 2001
)
.
Furthermore, when all request sources coincide with the vehicle depot, it becomes the well-known capacitated vehicle routing problem
(
Dantzig and Ramser, 1959
)
.
Approximation algorithms for the capacitated vehicle routing problem can be found in
(
Haimovich and Kan, 1985
,
Altinkemer and Gavish, 1987
,
Blauth et al., 2023
,
Friggstad et al., 2022
)
.
Although the dial-a-ride problem is a fundamental vehicle routing problem in Operations Research
(
Ho et al., 2018
)
, research on approximation algorithms is relatively limited.

When
\lambda\geq 1
, the
\frac{9}{5}
-approximation algorithm for the case
\lambda=1

(
Frederickson et al., 1978
)
directly implies an
\mathcal{O}(\lambda)
-approximation algorithm.

Charikar and Raghavachari, 1998
were the first to propose novel approximation algorithms.
For the non-preemptive case, they obtain an approximation ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\lambda}\log n\log\log n)
.
For the preemptive case, they obtain an approximation ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\log n\log\log n)
.
Their approaches are based on the
tree-embedding
technique in
(
Bartal, 1998
)
, which randomly embeds general metric spaces into tree metrics with an expected distortion of
\mathcal{O}(\log n\log\log n)
.
This distortion bound was later improved to
\mathcal{O}(\log n)

(
Fakcharoenphol et al., 2004
)
, which is asymptotically tight.
Hence, the corresponding approximation ratios improve to
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\lambda}\log n)
for the non-preemptive case, and
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
for the preemptive case.

Later,
Gupta et al., 2007
,
Gupta et al., 2010
introduced the
\lambda
-Steiner forest
technique
(
Segev and Segev, 2010
)
, which greedily covers all requests using
minimum-ratio

\lambda
-Steiner forests, as the greedy algorithm for the
\lambda
-set cover problem
(
Williamson and Shmoys, 2011
)
.
They mainly focused on the non-preemptive case and achieved an approximation ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\min\{\sqrt{\lambda},\sqrt{n}\}\cdot\log^{2}n)
.
They also showed that their techniques extend to more general dial-a-ride problem settings, including cases where some requests require more than one unit of vehicle capacity.

Approximation algorithms for the non-preemptive dial-a-ride problem on paths have also been studied.

Krumke et al., 2000
proposed a 3-approximation algorithm, which was later improved to
2.5
by
Hu, 2009
.

In the literature, there exist few results on the inapproximability for the dial-a-ride problem.
For the traveling salesman problem,
Karpinski et al., 2015
proved that there is no
\frac{123}{122}
-approximation algorithm unless P=NP.
Although the non-preemptive dial-a-ride problem appears unlikely to admit an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-approximation ratio, the traveling salesman problem lower bound remains the best hardness result.
For the preemptive dial-a-ride problem,
Gørtz, 2006
proved a super-constant lower bound of
\Omega(\min\{\log^{\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon}n,\lambda^{1-\varepsilon}\})
for any constant
\varepsilon>0
under the assumption that all problems in NP cannot be solved by randomized algorithms with expected running time
\mathcal{O}(n^{\text{polylog}\ n})
.

multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem.

The multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem is a natural generalization of the dial-a-ride problem.
For the non-preemptive multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem,
Luo et al., 2023
proposed an
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\lambda}\log m)
-approximation algorithm that runs in
\mathcal{O}(m^{3}\log\lambda+m^{2}\lambda^{2}\log\lambda)
time and performs well in practice.
Unlike previous approximation algorithms for the dial-a-ride problem, their approach uses an
iterative pairing
strategy based on minimum-weight matching, grouping requests before planning routes.

For the preemptive multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem,
Gørtz et al., 2009
,
Gørtz et al., 2015
showed that an
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
-approximation algorithm can be obtained by extending the
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
-approximation algorithm for the preemptive dial-a-ride problem
(
Charikar and Raghavachari, 1998
,
Fakcharoenphol et al., 2004
)
.

The multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem has also been studied under the
makespan
objective, which aims to minimize the maximum travel distance among all vehicles.
For the preemptive case,
Gørtz et al., 2009
,
Gørtz et al., 2015
proposed an
\mathcal{O}(\log^{3}n)
-approximation algorithm, and an
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
-approximation algorithm for the case
\lambda=\infty
.
They also showed that for special classes of metrics induced by graphs excluding any fixed minor (e.g., planar metrics), these ratios can be improved to
\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}n)
and
\mathcal{O}(1)
, respectively.
Moreover, they proposed an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-approximation algorithm for the case
\lambda=1
, i.e., multi-vehicle stacker-crane problem, which even computes a non-preemptive solution.

1.2
Our results

For the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem, we design new approximation algorithms.
The contributions are summarized as follows.

1.

We first propose two simple approximation algorithms that are particularly practical when
m=\Theta(n)
.
The first achieves an approximation ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\lambda}\log m)
with a running time of
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
, whereas the second achieves a ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\frac{m}{\lambda}})
with a running time of
\mathcal{O}(m^{2}\log m)
.
Therefore, the combination of these two algorithms yields a ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\min\{\sqrt{\lambda}\log m,\sqrt{\frac{m}{\lambda}}\})
, which is at most
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt[4]{n}\log^{\frac{1}{2}}n)
when
m=\Theta(n)
and thus breaks the
barrier

\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
when
m=o(n^{2})
.
Note that for the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem, the previous algorithm
(
Luo et al., 2023
)
is relatively complex
and achieves a ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\lambda}\log m)
with a running time of
\mathcal{O}(m^{3}\log\lambda+m^{2}\lambda^{2}\log\lambda)
.

2.

Although we may have
m,\lambda\gg n
, there are at most
n(n-1)
number of distinct requests. Building upon our two previous algorithms and the techniques from
(
Gupta et al., 2010
)
, we give our third algorithm for the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem, which achieves a ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n\log n})
. Notably, this result improves upon the best-known ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}\log^{2}n)
for the dial-a-ride problem
(
Gupta et al., 2007
,
Gupta et al., 2010
)
, which has remained unchanged for nearly two decades.

Our algorithms are based on new structural properties that are also valuable for related dial-a-ride problems.
Specifically, the first algorithm is built on a simple metric graph in which each request is represented by a single vertex, thereby enabling us to use techniques from the well-known capacitated vehicle routing problem (recall that in which, each request involves a single location).
The second algorithm uses a simple yet elegant decomposition of a Steiner forest.
Although these ideas are surprisingly simple, they yield strong theoretical guarantees through careful analysis.

Preliminaries

Let
G=(V,E,w)
denote the input complete graph with
|V|=n
and edge-weight function
w:E\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
We call
G
a
metric graph
if
w
satisfies the metric properties defined earlier. In this case,
G
can also be viewed as a
metric space

(V,w)
.
There is a (multi-)set of
m
requests
\mathcal{R}=\{(s_{i},t_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}\subseteq V\times V
. Each request
\{s_{i},t_{i}\}\in\mathcal{R}
corresponds to an item that needs to be picked up from the source
s_{i}
and delivered to the destination
t_{i}
.
We let
X=\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and
Y=\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
denote the sets of all sources and destinations, respectively.
Furthermore, there is a set of
h
vehicles, each with the same capacity
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
Their initial locations are given by
K=\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{h}\subseteq V
.

We assume that every vertex in
V
is either an initial location of a vehicle or the source or destination of a request; otherwise, since
G
is a metric graph, such a vertex can be removed.
For ease of analyzing the running time of our algorithms, we also assume
m=\Omega(n)
, following
(
Luo et al., 2023
)
.
Under this assumption, for example, the running time may be expressed simply as
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
; otherwise, it would be
\mathcal{O}(\max\{n^{2},m^{2}\})
, since reading the input alone already requires
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time.
Moreover, for the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem, we assume, at the cost of losing a factor of 2, that each location in
V
hosts at most one vehicle, i.e.,
h\leq n
. This is because if a vertex hosts multiple vehicles, their routes can be served by a single vehicle with a total weight at most twice the original, by the triangle inequality. Hence, for the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem, we have

h\leq n=\mathcal{O}(m).

(1)

We introduce some notations on (multi-)graphs. Let
G^{\prime}=(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},w^{\prime})
be a (multi-)graph.
In what follows, we work with multi-edge sets, where the union of two edge sets is taken with multiplicities.

For any edge set
E_{s}\subseteq E^{\prime}
, define
w^{\prime}(E_{s})\coloneqq\sum_{e\in E_{s}}w^{\prime}(e)
.
For any subgraph
G_{s}
of
G^{\prime}
, let
V^{\prime}(G_{s})
and
E^{\prime}(G_{s})
denote its vertex set and edge set, respectively.
For any vertex set
V_{s}\subseteq V^{\prime}
, let
G^{\prime}[V_{s}]
denote the subgraph of
G^{\prime}
induced by
V_{s}
.

A
walk

W=v_{1}v_{2}\dots v_{s}
in
G^{\prime}
is a sequence of vertices (vertices may repeat), corresponding to the edge set
E^{\prime}(W)\coloneqq\{v_{1}v_{2},v_{2}v_{3},\dots,v_{s-1}v_{s}\}
.
A
tour

T=v_{1}v_{2}\dots v_{s}
is similarly a sequence of vertices, but with the edge set
E^{\prime}(T)\coloneqq\{v_{1}v_{2},v_{2}v_{3},\dots,v_{s}v_{1}\}
.
The weight of a walk
W
(resp., tour
T
) is
w^{\prime}(W)\coloneqq w^{\prime}(E^{\prime}(W))
(resp.,
w^{\prime}(T)\coloneqq w^{\prime}(E^{\prime}(T))
).
Note that a sequence of vertices can represent either a walk or a tour, depending on whether the first and last vertices are connected; the context will always clarify which applies.
Moreover, a tour
T
is a
simple tour
if it contains no repeated vertices, and a
traveling salesman problem tour
in
G^{\prime}
if additionally
|E^{\prime}(T)|=|V^{\prime}|
.

Given a walk or tour
{\dots xyz\dots}
in
G^{\prime}
, a new walk or tour
{\dots xz\dots}
can be obtained by skipping the vertex
y
. This operation is called
shortcutting
the vertex
y
or the edges
xy
and
yz
. Since the weight function
w^{\prime}
is a metric, the new walk or tour has a non-increasing weight by the triangle inequality.

For any integer
p\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, let
[p]\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots,p\}
.
Consider a tour
os_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\dots s_{i_{p}}
visiting
p
sources of requests in
\{(s_{i_{j}},t_{i_{j}})\mid j\in[p]\}
and the corresponding tour
ot_{i_{1}}t_{i_{2}}\dots t_{i_{p}}
visiting the
p
destinations, where
o
denotes an
optional
vehicle.
Such a pair of tours, which share the same permutation of indices
i_{1}i_{2}\dots i_{p}
, is called
consistent
.
More generally, if the index sequences for the sources and destinations are both permutations of
\{i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{p}\}
(not necessarily identical), we call them
weakly consistent
.
Whenever
o
appears in one tour of a (weakly) consistent pair, it must also appear in the other.

Similarly, let
\mathcal{T}_{s}
and
\mathcal{T}_{t}
be two sets of tours, where every tour in
\mathcal{T}_{s}
consists of no destinations and every tour in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
consists of no sources.
We call these sets (weakly) consistent if, for every tour in
\mathcal{T}_{s}
, there exists a tour in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
that is (weakly) consistent with it, and vice versa.
In this case, we must have
|\mathcal{T}_{s}|=|\mathcal{T}_{t}|
.

A walk is
simple
if it contains no repeated vertices; such a walk is called a
path
.
Two paths, or two sets of paths, are said to be (weakly) consistent in the same manner as tours, with the same requirement on the presence of an optional vehicle
o
.
(For paths, if
o
exists, we require that the paths start from
o
.)
For instance, the tours
os_{1}s_{2}
and
ot_{1}t_{2}
are consistent;
the paths
s_{1}os_{2}
and
t_{1}ot_{2}
are not (weakly) consistent.
For any two sequences
T_{1}=p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{i}
and
T_{2}=q_{1}q_{2}\dots q_{j}
, we use
T_{1}\circ T_{2}
(resp.,
T_{1}\bullet T_{2}
) to denote the sequence obtained by concatenating
T_{2}
to
T_{1}
(resp., removing all elements of
T_{2}
from
T_{1}
).
For a sequence
T=p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{i}
, we let
|T|=i
.
For instance, if
T_{1}=12134
and
T_{2}=1216
, then
T_{1}\circ T_{2}=121341216
,
T_{1}\bullet T_{2}=34
, and
|T_{1}|=5
.

In the following, we denote by
OPT
the weight of an optimal solution to the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem.

3
Two Simple Algorithms for the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem

In this section, we propose two algorithms, denoted by
Alg.1
and
Alg.2
.
Recall that
h\leq n
and
n=\mathcal{O}(m)
by (
1
), and that
X=\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
,
Y=\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, and
K=\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{h}
by definition.
For convenience, we assume in this section that all vertices in
X
,
Y
, and
K
are pairwise disjoint because we can consider the same vertex as two distinct vertices with zero distance.
Then
V=X\cup Y\cup K
and
n=h+2m=\Theta(m)
.

3.1
The First Algorithm

Our first algorithm, denoted by
Alg.1
, is inspired by the well-known iterated tour partition algorithm for the CVRP
(
Haimovich and Kan, 1985
)
.
The iterated tour partition works as follows: (1) compute an
\alpha
-approximate traveling salesman problem tour in
G
; (2) partition this tour into the desired path fragments; and (3) transform each fragment into a feasible tour.
Despite its simplicity, iterated tour partition achieves a strong approximation guarantee of
\alpha+2
for the CVRP
(
Altinkemer and Gavish, 1987
)
.
Surprisingly, it has not been explored for approximation algorithms in the dial-a-ride problem setting.

We begin with the dial-a-ride problem, and later show how to extend the method to the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem with minor modifications.

To modify iterated tour partition to work for the dial-a-ride problem, we note that instead of finding an approximate traveling salesman problem tour in
G
as (1) in iterated tour partition, we may find two approximate traveling salesman problem tours
T_{s}
in
G[X\cup K]
and
T_{t}
in
G[Y\cup K]
since each request involves two locations.
Moreover, we may ensure that these two traveling salesman problem tours are consistent, say
T_{s}=os_{i_{1}}s_{i_{2}}\dots s_{i_{m}}
and
T_{t}=ot_{i_{1}}t_{i_{2}}\dots t_{i_{m}}
.
This is important, as for any fragment
B^{i}_{s}=s_{i_{j}}s_{i_{j+1}}\dots s_{i_{j+j^{\prime}}}
from
T_{s}
, there always exists a fragment
B^{i}_{t}=t_{i_{j}}t_{i_{j+1}}\dots t_{i_{j+j^{\prime}}}
from
T_{t}
that is consistent with
B^{i}_{s}
.
Then, as (2) in iterated tour partition, we construct a set of fragments
\mathcal{B}_{s}
(resp.,
\mathcal{B}_{t}
) by partitioning
T_{s}
(resp.,
T_{t}
). Note that each fragment is a path, and we may require that
\mathcal{B}_{s}
is consistent with
\mathcal{B}_{t}
.
Last, as (3) in iterated tour partition, we transform each pair of consistent fragments in
\mathcal{B}_{s}\cup\mathcal{B}_{t}
into a tour. This can be done by doubling all edges in the pair of consistent fragments, adding two copies of the minimum-weight edge connecting these fragments, and then taking shortcuts.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be the set of tours obtained.
Traversing the traveling salesman problem tour in
T_{s}
together with the tours in
\mathcal{T}
yields a feasible dial-a-ride problem solution.

To extend the above idea to the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem, we seek two consistent sets of tours:
\mathcal{T}_{s}
in
G[X\cup K]
and
\mathcal{T}_{t}
in
G[Y\cup K]
, such that
(a) each source in
X
(resp., destination in
Y
) appears in exactly one tour in
\mathcal{T}_{s}
(resp.,
\mathcal{T}_{t}
);
(b) each vehicle in
K
appears in exactly one tour in
\mathcal{T}_{s}
and in exactly one tour in
\mathcal{T}_{t}
;
(c) each tour in
\mathcal{T}_{s}\cup\mathcal{T}_{t}
contains exactly one vehicle.
Given any pair of consistent tours from
\mathcal{T}_{s}\cup\mathcal{T}_{t}
, we can apply the same procedure as in the dial-a-ride problem to construct a route that serves all requests in the pair
(see
Alg.1
in Algorithm
1
).

3.1.1
Compute Two Consistent Sets of Tours

To compute the consistent sets of tours
\mathcal{T}_{s}
in
G[X\cup K]
and
\mathcal{T}_{t}
in
G[Y\cup K]
, we employ an approximate solution to the multi-depot TSP (multi-depot traveling salesman problem)
(
Xu et al., 2011
)
.

First, we construct a new complete graph
H=(V_{H},E_{H},\widetilde{w})
, where
V_{H}=V^{1}_{H}\cup V^{2}_{H}
,
{V^{1}_{H}}=\{k_{1},\dots,k_{h}\}
, and
{V^{2}_{H}}=\{l_{1},\dots,l_{m}\}
. Each vertex
k_{i}\in V^{1}_{H}
refers to a vehicle
o_{i}\in K
, and each vertex
l_{j}\in V^{2}_{H}
refers to a request
(s_{j},t_{j})\in\mathcal{R}
.
For any
x,y\in V_{H}
, we define the weight function
\widetilde{w}
as follows:

\widetilde{w}(y,x)=\widetilde{w}(x,y)\quad\text{ and }\quad\widetilde{w}(x,y)=\begin{cases}2w(o_{i},o_{j}),&x=k_{i}\in V^{1}_{H},y=k_{j}\in V^{1}_{H},\\
w(o_{i},s_{j})+w(o_{i},t_{j}),&x=k_{i}\in V^{1}_{H},y=l_{j}\in V^{2}_{H},\\
w(s_{i},s_{j})+w(t_{i},t_{j}),&x=l_{i}\in V^{2}_{H},y=l_{j}\in V^{2}_{H}.\end{cases}

(2)

Note that
\widetilde{w}
remains a metric.
By regarding the vertices in
V^{1}_{H}
as
depots
, the graph
H
forms a valid instance of the multi-depot traveling salesman problem, where the objective is to compute a set of
multi-depot traveling salesman problem tours
minimizing the total weight, subject to the conditions that: (1) each vertex in
V_{H}
is contained in exactly one tour, and (2) each tour contains exactly one depot in
V^{1}_{H}
. Note that a tour may consist of a single depot only.

Since

.

Every permutation on

Based on the above lemma, we first prove the following structural property.

Lemma 3

Given any two metric spaces

where
T^{*}_{s}
(resp.,

(resp.,
M_{t}

Proof.

Suppose

T^{*}_{t}={t_{p_{1}}t_{p_{2}}\dots t_{p_{r}}}

p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{r}

\{1,\dots,r\}
.
Since

is a traveling salesman problem tour in
M_{t}
, we assume w.l.o.g. that

.
To prove the lemma, it suffices to prove that there exists a traveling salesman problem tour
T={q_{1}q_{2}\dots q_{r}}
in
M_{st}
, where
q_{1}q_{2}\dots q_{r}
is a permutation on
\{1,\dots,r\}

w_{s}(T_{s})+w_{t}(T_{t})\leq 2\sqrt{r-1}(w_{s}(T^{*}_{s})+w_{t}(T^{*}_{t}))
, where
T_{s}={s_{q_{1}}s_{q_{2}}\dots s_{q_{r}}}
and
T_{t}={t_{q_{1}}t_{q_{2}}\dots t_{q_{r}}}
.

We propose an algorithm to construct such a permutation.
The main idea is as follows.

The traveling salesman problem tours
T^{*}_{s}
and
T^{*}_{t}
correspond to the permutations

and
p_{1}\dots p_{r}
, respectively.
By Lemma

, the sequence

contains either an increasing or decreasing subsequence of length at least

.
By traveling along

and

and

corresponding to this subsequence, and by the triangle inequality,

w_{s}(T^{\prime}_{s})+w_{t}(T^{\prime}_{t})\leq w_{s}(T^{*}_{s})+w_{t}(T^{*}_{t}).

Then, we remove the elements of this subsequence from

and repeat the process on the remaining sequence, obtaining another pair of consistent tours. By iterating this procedure and combining all such tours, we eventually obtain two consistent traveling salesman problem tours corresponding to the same permutation.

We next recall another structural property of the dial-a-ride problem.

Lemma 4

(
Gupta et al., 2010
)
.

Given any instance of the dial-a-ride problem, there exists a feasible walk
W
satisfying the following conditions: (1)
W
can be partitioned into a set of segments
\{W_{1},W_{2},\dots,W_{N}\}
, where in each
W_{i}
the vehicle serves a subset
\mathcal{R}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
of at most
\lambda
requests such that
W_{i}
is a path that first picks up each; (2) the weight of
W
is at most
\mathcal{O}(\log m)
times that of an optimal solution, i.e.,
w(W)\leq\mathcal{O}(\log m)\cdot\mbox{OPT}
.

Lemma
4
guarantees the existence of a solution satisfying the
batch delivery
property whose total weight is at most
\mathcal{O}(\log m)\cdot\mbox{OPT}
, where this property means the vehicle serves requests in batches, each consisting of picking up at most
\lambda
requests first, followed by delivering all of them before proceeding to the next batch.
As shown in
(
Luo et al., 2023
)
, this structural property extends trivially to the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem.

3.2
The Second Algorithm

Our second algorithm, denoted by
Alg.2
, is motivated by the following observation. When
\lambda=\Theta(m)
,
Alg.1
attains its worst-case approximation ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{m}\log m)
. In this regime, however, an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-approximation for the dial-a-ride problem can be obtained through a simple approach.

We begin by computing an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-approximate traveling salesman problem tour
T_{s}
(resp.,
T_{t}
) in
G[X\cup K]
(resp.,
G[Y\cup K]
).
Clearly,
w(T_{s})+w(T_{t})=\mathcal{O}(1)\cdot\mbox{OPT}
. Traversing the concatenated tour
T_{s}\circ T_{t}
once allows the vehicle to serve
\lambda
requests. Since
\lambda=\Theta(m)
, all requests can be served by traversing
T_{s}\circ T_{t}
at most
\left\lceil\frac{m}{\lambda}\right\rceil=\mathcal{O}(1)
times.
Hence, the weight of the resulting route is at most
\mathcal{O}(1)\cdot(w(T_{s})+w(T_{t}))=\mathcal{O}(1)\cdot\mbox{OPT}
.
Therefore, when
\lambda
is sufficiently large, there exists an approximation algorithm for the dial-a-ride problem that outperforms
Alg.1
. However, the above method may yield at best an
\mathcal{O}(\frac{m}{\lambda})
-approximation in general. In
Alg.2
, we employ a more refined approach to achieve an improved approximation ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\frac{m}{\lambda}})
for the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem.

In
Alg.2
, we begin by grouping requests via a
Steiner forest
in
G[X\cup Y]
that covers all vertices in
X\cup Y
, ensuring that the source and destination of each request lie within the same tree.
The task of finding such a minimum-weight forest is the classical
Steiner forest problem
.
Using the well-known primal-dual algorithm of
(
Goemans and Williamson, 1995
)
, we obtain a
2
-approximate Steiner forest
\mathcal{F}
.

We then transform
\mathcal{F}
into a set of walks
\mathcal{W}
with the following properties:
(a) each walk contains exactly one vehicle;
(b) each vehicle appears in exactly one walk (a walk may contain a single vehicle only);
(c) for every request, both its source and destination appear in the same walk.

To achieve this, we double all edges in
\mathcal{F}
, add an
\mathcal{O}(1)
-approximate multi-depot traveling salesman problem solution
\mathcal{T}
in
G[X\cup K]
with the depots given by
K
, which together forms a set of connected components, and then, for each component, perform shortcutting to obtain a simple walk.

Select an arbitrary walk
W\in\mathcal{W}
and let
o_{W}
denote its vehicle. Let
N_{W}
be the number of requests contained in
W
.
Define
W_{s}
(resp.,
W_{t}
) as the walk obtained from
W
by shortcutting all destinations (resp., sources) and the vehicle
o_{W}
.
For instance, if
W=os_{1}t_{4}s_{7}s_{4}t_{1}t_{7}
, then we have
N_{W}=3
,
W_{s}=s_{1}s_{7}s_{4}
and
W_{t}=t_{4}t_{7}t_{1}
.

As in
Alg.1
, we partition
W_{s}
and
W_{t}
simultaneously to form two sets of fragments
\mathcal{B}_{s}
and
\mathcal{B}_{t}
, where each fragment in
\mathcal{B}_{s}
(resp.,
\mathcal{B}_{t}
) contains at most
\lambda
sources (resp., destinations), but we use a new
decomposition
method.
These fragments are then combined to form a feasible route for
o_{W}
serving all
N_{W}
requests in
W
.

We first describe how to use the decomposition method to construct
\mathcal{B}_{s}
and
\mathcal{B}_{t}
.
We consider two cases.

Case 1:
N_{W}\leq\lambda
.
In this case, since
W_{s}
(resp.,
W_{t}
) contains at most
\lambda
sources (resp., destinations), we simply set
\mathcal{B}_{s}=\{W_{s}\}
and
\mathcal{B}_{t}=\{W_{t}\}
.

Case 2:
N_{W}>\lambda

We may assume that
N_{W}
is divisible by
\lambda
and that
\frac{N_{W}}{\lambda}
is the square of some integer.
The reason is as follows.
If
N_{W}
does not satisfy these two properties, we can express it as
N_{W}=\lambda\cdot N_{0}^{2}+N^{\prime}
for some integers
N_{0}
and
N^{\prime}
such that
\lambda\cdot N_{0}^{2}<N_{W}<\lambda\cdot(N_{0}+1)^{2}
. We then add
\lambda\cdot(N_{0}+1)^{2}-N_{W}

requests to obtain a new walk
\widetilde{W}

W

The resulting walk
\widetilde{W}
has
N_{\widetilde{W}}=\lambda\cdot(N_{0}+1)^{2}
and thus meets the two desired properties.
Since dummy requests can be served at zero weight, the new instance is equivalent to the original.
Moreover, since
\lambda\cdot(N_{0}+1)^{2}-N_{W}\leq\lambda\cdot(2N_{0}+1)<3\lambda\cdot N_{0}^{2}\leq 3N_{W}

We then partition

Lemma 10
.

Consider a weighted multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem instance
I

\lambda
, where each request size
N_{i}
satisfies
N_{i}\leq\lambda
. Let
J

(s^{*}_{i},t^{*}_{i})

N_{i}

N_{i}
unit requests.
Suppose
J
admits a solution consisting of
r

\{W_{1},\dots,W_{r}\}
with total weight
\mbox{SOL}\coloneq\sum_{j=1}^{r}w(W_{j})
.
Then, there exists a polynomial-time computable feasible solution for

3\cdot\mbox{SOL}
.

Proof.

For each route
W_{j}
, construct a line (i.e., a path)
L_{j}
by “unfolding”
W_{j}
from its root as in
(
Gupta et al., 2010
)

W_{j}
becomes one edge appended to
L_{j}
with the same weight. Hence,
w(L_{j})=w(W_{j})
.
For any request
i
, i.e.,
(s^{*}_{i},t^{*}_{i})
, the unweighted solution serves its
N_{i}
unit copies, each traversing a segment from
s_{i}
to
t_{i}
on some route
W_{j}
.
Therefore, request
i
induces a collection of
N_{i}
segments distributed among the lines
\{L_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}
.
Let
N_{i}^{j}
denote the number of segments corresponding to request
i
on
L_{j}
, and for each edge
e\in L_{j}
, let
N_{i}^{j,e}
denote the number of these segments that contain
e
.
Let
N_{e}^{j}\coloneq\sum_{i}N_{i}^{j,e}
denote the total number of unweighted requests carried by
W_{j}
when traversing edge
e
.
Since the unweighted solution is feasible, we must have
N_{e}^{j}\leq\lambda
for all
j
and
e\in L_{j}
.

Random assignment.

We randomly construct a weighted multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem instance as follows: for each request
i
, choose uniformly at random one of its
N_{i}
segments and assign the entire size
N_{i}
to that segment.
For any edge
e\in L_{j}
,
\mathbb{P}\left[\text{request }i\text{ chooses a segment containing }e\right]=\frac{N_{i}^{j,e}}{N_{i}}
, so the random total size on
e
is

\sum_{i}N_{i}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\text{request }i\text{ chooses a segment containing }e\}
,
and hence
\mathbb{E}[\text{size on }e]=\sum_{i}N_{i}\cdot\frac{N_{i}^{j,e}}{N_{i}}=\sum_{i}N_{i}^{j,e}=N_{e}^{j}\leq\lambda
.
Therefore, the expected flow lower bound on
L_{j}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{flowLB}(L_{j})]=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sum_{e\in L_{j}}w(e)\cdot\mathbb{E}[\text{size on }e]\leq\sum_{e\in L_{j}}w(e)=w(W_{j})
.
Summing over all
j
yields
\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{flowLB}(L_{j})]\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}w(W_{j})=\mbox{SOL}
.

Derandomization by minimum-weight segment selection and trimming.

For each request
i
, let its
N_{i}
candidate segments have weights
w_{i,1},\dots,w_{i,N_{i}}
.
If we assign the entire size
N_{i}
to one segment of weight
w^{\prime}
, then its contribution to the flow lower bound equals

\frac{N_{i}}{\lambda}\cdot w^{\prime}
.
Under the uniform random choice, the expected contribution equals

\frac{N_{i}}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{N_{i}}w_{i,i^{\prime}}
.
Hence, choosing the segment with weight
w_{i}\coloneq\min_{i^{\prime}\in[N_{i}]}w_{i,i^{\prime}}
yields a contribution

\frac{N_{i}}{\lambda}\cdot w_{i}\leq\frac{N_{i}}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{1}{N_{i}}\sum_{i^{\prime}}^{N_{i}}w_{i,i^{\prime}}
,
which is no larger than the random expectation.
Summing over all requests (and lines) gives a deterministic assignment with
\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathrm{flowLB}(L_{j})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{flowLB}(L_{j})]\leq\mbox{SOL}
.

After this assignment, the tail of a line

may contain edges not covered by any chosen segment.

w(\widetilde{L}_{j})
. Thus,

\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathrm{SteinerLB}(\widetilde{L}_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{r}w(\widetilde{L}_{j})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{r}w(L_{j})=\mbox{SOL}
.

Approximation on each line and lifting.

By
(
Gupta et al., 2010
)
, the 3-approximation algorithm for the dial-a-ride problem on lines
(
Krumke et al., 2000
)
extends to weighted requests and is guaranteed
w.r.t. the preemptive flow and Steiner lower bounds. Applying this algorithm independently on each
\widetilde{L}_{j}
yields a feasible route of weight at most

3\cdot\max\left\{\mathrm{flowLB}(\widetilde{L}_{j}),\mathrm{SteinerLB}(\widetilde{L}_{j})\right\}
.
Summing over
j
and using
\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathrm{flowLB}(\widetilde{L}_{j})\leq\mbox{SOL}
and
\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathrm{SteinerLB}(\widetilde{L}_{j})\leq\mbox{SOL}
, the total weight is at most
3\cdot\mbox{SOL}
.
Since each
\widetilde{L}_{j}
is a subpath of
L_{j}
, which in turn was obtained by unfolding
W_{j}
, mapping these routes back to the original metric yields a solution to the weighted multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem instance
I
while preserving the total weight.
All steps run in polynomial time.

Therefore, from the unweighted multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem solution with total weight
SOL
, we obtain a weighted multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem solution of weight at most
3\cdot\mbox{SOL}
in polynomial time.
∎

Corollary 2
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that computes a solution to the weighted multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem instance
\widehat{I}_{w}
with weight at most
O\left(\sqrt{n\log n}\right)\cdot\mbox{OPT}(\widehat{I}_{w})
.

Proof.

By (
19
) and Lemma
10
, there is a polynomial-time algorithm that computes a solution to the weighted multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem instance
\widetilde{I}_{w}
with weight at most
O\left(\sqrt{n\log n}\right)\cdot\mbox{OPT}(\widetilde{I}_{w})
.

Since
\widehat{I}_{w}
and
\widetilde{I}_{w}
are equivalent encodings of the same instance, the same solution also applies to
\widehat{I}_{w}
, with weight at most
O\left(\sqrt{n\log n}\right)\cdot\mbox{OPT}(\widetilde{I}_{w})=O\left(\sqrt{n\log n}\right)\cdot\mbox{OPT}(\widehat{I}_{w})
.
∎

Lemma 11
.

There is a polynomial-time algorithm that computes a solution to the unweighted multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem instance
I_{u}
with weight at most
O\left(\sqrt{n\log n}\right)\cdot\mbox{OPT}(I_{u})\leq O\left(\sqrt{n\log n}\right)\cdot\mbox{OPT}
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\mbox{OPT}(I_{u})\leq\mbox{OPT}
by the triangle inequality.
Moreover, the solution to the weighted instance
\widehat{I}_{w}
from Corollary
2
is also a feasible solution for
I_{u}
with the same weight. Thus, it remains to show
\mbox{OPT}(\widehat{I}_{w})\leq\mathcal{O}(1)\cdot\mbox{OPT}(I_{u})
.

Let
I_{w}
denote the weighted multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem instance corresponding to
I_{u}
.
By Lemma
10
, there exists a solution
\mathcal{W}=\{W_{1},\dots,W_{r}\}
to
I_{w}
with weight at most
3\cdot\mbox{OPT}(I_{u})
, i.e.,
\mbox{OPT}(I_{w})\leq 3\cdot\mbox{OPT}(I_{u})
.
Since
\widehat{I}_{w}
rescales capacities and request sizes, the same routes
\mathcal{W}
may not be directly feasible.

Recall that
\lambda\gg\alpha
and
N_{i}\leq\widehat{N}_{i}\ll\widehat{\lambda}
.
Therefore, by definition, we have

\widehat{\lambda}=\left\lfloor\alpha\right\rfloor\cdot\gamma=\left\lfloor\alpha\right\rfloor\cdot\frac{\lambda}{\alpha}\geq\lambda-\frac{\lambda}{\alpha}\gg\frac{\lambda}{2}
and

\widehat{N}_{i}=\left\lceil\frac{N_{i}}{\gamma}\right\rceil\cdot\gamma\leq N_{i}+\gamma\leq 2N_{i}\ll\widehat{\lambda}
.
Hence, each
\widehat{N}_{i}
is at most twice
N_{i}
, while
\widehat{\lambda}
is at least
\frac{\lambda}{2}
.
In particular, if the vehicles in
\widehat{I}_{w}
had capacity
4\widehat{\lambda}\geq 2\lambda
, the routes
\mathcal{W}
would be feasible.

Since
N_{i}\ll\widehat{\lambda}
, we may assume that
n
is sufficiently large so that
\max_{i}N_{i}\leq\frac{\widehat{\lambda}}{3}
.
Consequently, for each vehicle route
W_{i}\in\mathcal{W}
, we can use up to
4+\left\lceil\frac{(4-1)\cdot\max_{i}N_{i}}{\widehat{\lambda}}\right\rceil=5
vehicles with capacity
\widehat{\lambda}
simultaneously to serve all requests assigned to
W_{i}
.
The reason is as follows. A vehicle with capacity
4\widehat{\lambda}
can simulate the route
W_{i}
.
If we instead use four vehicles each with capacity
\widehat{\lambda}
, then at most
4-1=3
requests are split across two vehicles.
Since
\max_{i}N_{i}\leq\frac{\widehat{\lambda}}{3}
, these requests can be handled by one more vehicle.
Hence, to realize the same service with only a single vehicle of capacity
\widehat{\lambda}
, we may let it traverse
W_{i}
up to five times.
By the triangle inequality, this increases the total route weight to at most
(2\cdot 5-1)\cdot w(W_{i})=9\cdot w(W_{i})
.

Therefore, there exists a solution to
\widehat{I}_{w}
with weight at most
9\cdot\mbox{OPT}(I_{w})
, i.e.,
\mbox{OPT}(\widehat{I}_{w})\leq 9\cdot\mbox{OPT}(I_{w})
. Since
\mbox{OPT}(I_{w})\leq 3\cdot\mbox{OPT}(I_{u})
, we conclude that
\mbox{OPT}(\widehat{I}_{w})\leq 27\cdot\mbox{OPT}(I_{u})
, as required.
∎

Combining (
17
), (
18
), and Lemma
11
, we immediately obtain the following result.

Theorem 4
.

For the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem, there exists a polynomial-time
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{n\log n}\right)
-approximation algorithm.

5
Conclusion

This paper investigates approximation algorithms for the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem. We propose an algorithm that achieves an approximation ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n\log n})
, even improving upon the previous best-known ratio of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n}\log^{2}n)
for the classic (single-vehicle) dial-a-ride problem. Our results are derived from new structural properties of the problem, which allow us to leverage techniques from the well-known capacitated vehicle routing problem. We believe that these insights may also be applicable to other variants of the dial-a-ride problem. A key direction for future research is to determine whether the
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
-approximation barrier can be surpassed.
