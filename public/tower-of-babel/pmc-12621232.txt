Oculomotor nerve palsy caused by arterial displacement due to subarachnoid hemorrhage from a ruptured duplicated middle cerebral artery aneurysm: illustrative case
Authors: Yuta Koketsu, Takafumi Tanei, Masaki Sumitomo, Akino Kawamura, Eiji Tachibana, Ryuta Saito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDA duplicated middle cerebral artery (DMCA) is a rare variant of the middle cerebral artery (MCA), arising between the anterior choroidal artery and distal end of the internal carotid artery. Despite its rarity, DMCA is clinically significant due to its association with aneurysm formation and high risk of rupture. No case of oculomotor nerve palsy related to DMCA aneurysm has been reported.OBSERVATIONSA 49-year-old woman presented with sudden severe headache (Hunt and Kosnik grade III) and left pupil dilation. Imaging showed a diffuse, left predominant subarachnoid hemorrhage and four aneurysms. Based on the hematoma location and clinical findings, the left DMCA aneurysm was suspected as the rupture point. She underwent left frontotemporal craniotomy with clipping of three aneurysms. Intraoperative evaluation revealed that the DMCA was displaced by perianeurysmal hematoma, compressing the left oculomotor nerve. After aneurysmal neck clipping, removal of the hematoma surrounding the DMCA was performed. Duplicated middle cerebral artery (DMCA) is one of the anomalies of the middle cerebral artery (MCA), along with accessory MCA, fenestration of the MCA, duplicated origin of the MCA, and twig-like MCA.1 DMCA arises from the internal carotid artery (ICA), typically between the origin of the anterior choroidal artery (AChA) and the distal end of the ICA, and is distinct from accessory MCA, which originates from the anterior cerebral artery (ACA). The DMCA supplies cortical territories typically served by the temporopolar, anterior temporal, and middle temporal arteries.2 It has a reported incidence rate of 0.7%–2.9% in autopsy studies and 0.24%–0.34% in angiographic series.3 Despite its relatively low incidence, DMCA has significant clinical implications, since it is often associated with aneurysm formation and presents a high risk of rupture.4 Here, we describe a case of a ruptured DMCA aneurysm presenting with oculomotor nerve palsy. The cranial nerve palsy was caused by the perianeurysmal hematoma, which indirectly compressed the oculomotor nerve by causing displacement of the DMCA. We also discuss the anatomical and clinical features of the DMCA and its aneurysms.

Illustrative Case

A 49-year-old woman presented with severe headache of sudden onset and was transported to our hospital by ambulance. She had a history of hypertension and no relevant family history. On arrival, her Glasgow Coma Scale score was 14 (E3V5M6), indicating mild impairment of consciousness. Her left pupil was dilated and unresponsive to light, while her right pupil was 3 mm in diameter with a preserved light reflex. She exhibited no diplopia, extraocular dysmotility, or limb paralysis. CT imaging revealed diffuse and slightly left dominant subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) (Fig. 1A and B). CT angiography showed bilateral MCA aneurysms and two aneurysms in the left ICA (Fig. 1C and D). Since the resolution of the CT angiograms was poor and the aneurysms were small, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was subsequently performed, which allowed clear identification of the aneurysms and the vessels branching from them (Fig. 2A–F). DSA revealed four aneurysms: bilateral MCA aneurysms, a left AChA aneurysm, and a left ICA aneurysm at the origin of an artery located distal to the AChA. The artery originating from between the AChA and above the ICA bifurcation was identified as a DMCA (Fig. 2D and E). The left DMCA aneurysm measured 2.8 mm in maximum diameter and had an irregular shape. Based on the location of the subarachnoid hematoma, the irregular shape of the aneurysm, and the dilated left pupil, rupture of the left DMCA aneurysm was suspected to be the cause of her symptoms.

An emergency left frontotemporal craniotomy was immediately performed, with clipping of the necks of the three left-sided aneurysms, leaving the right MCA aneurysm unclipped. Intraoperative evaluation revealed that the DMCA was displaced by a perianeurysmal subarachnoid hematoma, and that the displaced artery compressed the left oculomotor nerve (Fig. 3A). The left DMCA aneurysm was clipped using a slightly curved Sugita no. 99 mini clip (Mizuho Corporation). Thereafter, the hematoma around the DMCA aneurysm was removed, which reduced the deviation of the DMCA, thereby relieving the pressure on the oculomotor nerve (Fig. 3B). While applying the clip, we encountered an unexpected rerupture of the aneurysm. Hemostasis was achieved by direct clipping of the aneurysm, confirming that the SAH originated from rupture of the left DMCA aneurysm. Next, the left AChA aneurysm was clipped using a Sugita II no. 81 mini clip (Fig. 3C). Finally, the left MCA aneurysm was clipped using two Sugita II no. 2 clips and a Sugita II no. 87 mini clip (Fig. 3D). Complete occlusion of these aneurysms and patency of the left DMCA, AChA, and MCA were confirmed by intraoperative Doppler ultrasonography and a surgical microscope integrated with indocyanine green video angiography.

The thin arrow indicates the ruptured aneurysm and the arrowheads the DMCA. B: A mini clip was applied for the DMCA aneurysm (arrow). The DMCA (arrowheads) course improved, becoming more straight. C: A mini clip was applied for the AChA (arrow) aneurysm (arrowhead). D: Standard and mini clips were applied for the MCA aneurysm (arrows).

Postoperative CT angiography showed obliteration of the three clipped aneurysms (Fig. 4A). Ozagrel sodium and fasudil hydrochloride were administered intravenously during the period of cerebral vasospasm postoperatively. However, MRI performed the day after surgery revealed a high signal intensity area localized to the cortex at the left temporal tip on diffusion-weighted imaging (Fig. 4B). Since this was the area supplied by the DMCA, the hypoperfusion was thought to be due to cerebral vasospasm resulting from mechanical stimulation of the DMCA during surgery. Fortunately, the patient did not experience any apparent new neurological deficits, and left pupillary dilation returned to normal within a few days after surgery. Three weeks postoperatively, the patient was discharged home without any neurological deficits. Three years after the surgery, CT angiography showed no recurrence of the three clipped aneurysms or enlargement of the remaining small right MCA aneurysm (Fig. 4C). MRI revealed that the postoperative ischemic area in the left anterior temporal lobe had become a permanent cerebral infarction (Fig. 4D).

Discussion

Observations

DMCA was first described in 1962 under the term “accessory middle cerebral artery.”5 Since then, confusion has persisted regarding the two terminologies. However, based on a classification proposed in 1973, it is now widely accepted that arteries originating from the ICA are termed as DMCAs, and arteries originating from the ACA are termed as accessory MCAs.6 The DMCA is an artery that arises from the ICA between the origin of the AChA and the terminal bifurcation of the ICA, and it typically courses anteriorly at a sharp angle into the sylvian fissure or runs parallel to the horizontal segment of the main MCA.7 The perfusion territory of the DMCA is thought to include the anterior to middle portions or, occasionally, the entire temporal lobe.2 Based on its cortical distribution, the DMCA is considered to correspond either to a direct branch of the main MCA trunk or to an early temporal branch of the MCA. In the former case, its perfusion may extend across the entire temporal lobe. In the latter case, it perfuses to the anterior to middle portions of the temporal lobe.8

The DMCA is divided into two subtypes based on its site of origin.9 Type A DMCAs arise from the very tip of the ICA together with the ACA, giving the appearance of an early bifurcation. Type A DMCAs have a diameter comparable to that of the main MCA trunk and give rise to perforating arteries that supply the basal ganglia. On the other hand, type B DMCAs originate from the ICA between the AChA and the top of the ICA bifurcation and are considered an anomalous early manifestation of an MCA branch. Type B DMCAs have a smaller diameter than that of the main MCA trunk and have no perforators.6 In the present case, the location of the vascular origin, vascular diameter, and blood perfusion area corresponded to a type B DMCA.

To date, 49 cases of DMCA aneurysms have been reported.4,9–21 A review of these reports reveals several characteristic features. First, most DMCA aneurysms arise from type B DMCAs. It is hypothesized that type B DMCAs are exposed to higher hemodynamic stress due to the sharper angle between the ICA and DMCA compared with type A DMCAs.7 Type A DMCAs typically course parallel to the main MCA trunk, while type B vessels follow a curved trajectory toward the temporal lobe, forming a more acute separation angle.8 It was previously reported that only sharply curved DMCAs that coursed to the temporal lobe were associated with aneurysms originating from the origin of this artery. The DMCA observed in the present case also demonstrated a sharp separation angle and was likely under higher hemodynamic stress. Second, previous studies have reported that aneurysms associated with type B DMCAs have a high risk of rupture even if they are of small size.4 Third, almost half of the reported cases of DMCA aneurysms are associated with multiple aneurysms.22 This is also thought to be caused by hemodynamic stress, which contributes to the formation of aneurysms by inducing localized endothelial injury and weakening of the vessel wall. However, in the present case, since there was also a contralateral aneurysm that was not related to hemodynamic stress, certain genetic factors might also be involved. Because of their high risk of rupture, aggressive treatment strategies, such as surgical clipping or endovascular intervention, are recommended for DMCA aneurysms.4

Oculomotor nerve palsy with ruptured aneurysms has been reported to occur due to direct aneurysmal compression, direct injury from bleeding, jet flow from bleeding, or irritation from subarachnoid hematoma.23–28 In the present case, intraoperative findings indicated that the oculomotor nerve palsy was caused by compression by the displaced DMCA, rather than by direct compression by the aneurysm. This mechanism differs from the typical mechanism seen in posterior communicating artery (Pcom) aneurysms, in which the oculomotor nerve palsy usually results from direct compression by the aneurysm itself. Pcom aneurysms are well known to cause oculomotor nerve palsy, particularly when they protrude inferolaterally from the ICA,29 since the oculomotor nerve usually runs parallel to the Pcom artery along its inferolateral side.30 As far as we could determine from the literature, all reported DMCA aneurysms were found to protrude laterally, likely due to their anatomical origins.4,9–17 However, since the DMCA arises more distally than the Pcom artery, direct compression of the oculomotor nerve by a DMCA aneurysm is generally considered unlikely. Although there has been a report of oculomotor nerve palsy associated with a tortuous DMCA in the presence of other lesions, such as a parietal convexity meningioma and an anterior communicating artery aneurysm, the mechanism in this case was presumed based solely on imaging findings rather than confirmed intraoperatively.31 In contrast, our case is distinctive in that intraoperative observation clearly demonstrated direct compression of the oculomotor nerve by the displaced DMCA.

Lessons

In patients with SAH and multiple aneurysms, accurate preoperative identification of the ruptured lesion is essential. The presence of oculomotor nerve palsy is an important diagnostic indicator of the possible offender.27 The present case demonstrated that oculomotor nerve palsy following rupture of a DMCA aneurysm can occur due to compression by the displaced DMCA, even without direct compression by the aneurysm itself. Even aneurysms located distally, such as those at the DMCA origin, might contribute to cranial nerve palsy when accompanied by the mass effect of hemorrhage. Awareness of this mechanism is important in accurately identifying the ruptured aneurysm, particularly in patients with multiple aneurysms.
