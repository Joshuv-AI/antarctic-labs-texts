Targeted inhibition of the BDNF/AKT/mTOR pathway in the inferior colliculus ameliorates salicylate-induced tinnitus in rats
Authors: Jiachuan Lin, Jiaqian Wu, Yingxin Wang, Yan Huo, Jianning Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTinnitus involves peripheral auditory damage and aberrant central plasticity, with dysregulated BDNF signaling linking injury to compensation. Yet, direct evidence connecting BDNF/AKT/mTOR pathway activation to behavioral phenotypes is limited. This study examined BDNF/AKT/mTOR expression in the inferior colliculus (IC) of sodium salicylate (SS)-induced tinnitus rats and assessed therapeutic inhibition. Rats received intraperitoneal SS, showing decreased GPIAS ratios, elevated ABR thresholds, and upregulated BDNF/TrkB/AKT/mTOR phosphorylation in the IC—changes associated with tinnitus-like behavior. Bilateral IC microinjection of BDNF siRNA or systemic rapamycin revealed that siRNA suppressed BDNF/TrkB and ameliorated tinnitus without improving hearing, while rapamycin reduced mTOR phosphorylation and partially alleviated behavior. These results first demonstrate that BDNF/AKT/mTOR upregulation in the IC contributes to tinnitus in SS rats, and its inhibition improves behavioral manifestations without reversing peripheral hearing loss.

Introduction

Tinnitus refers to the perception of sound in the absence of an external acoustic source.1 It affects approximately 14% of the global adult population, with 20% of affected individuals experiencing comorbid anxiety and depression, imposing a significant socioeconomic burden.2,3 Although its pathological mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated, the predominant hypothesis posits that tinnitus arises from interactions between peripheral auditory damage and compensatory central plasticity.4,5 Peripheral auditory insults (e.g., cochlear hair cell dysfunction) trigger neuronal hyperexcitability in the central auditory pathway, including the inferior colliculus (IC), thalamus, and auditory cortex.6,7,8 This hyperexcitability is closely associated with enhanced glutamatergic signaling, reduced γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic inhibition, and neuroinflammation.9,10 In recent years, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and its role in regulating synaptic plasticity have become an increasing focus in tinnitus research.11,12

BDNF activates tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) and downstream PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, playing a pivotal role in neuronal survival, synaptic remodeling, and excitation-inhibition balance.13,14,15 In auditory injury models, noise exposure or ototoxic agents induce aberrant BDNF upregulation in the spiral ganglion and IC, enhancing synaptic transmission efficiency and promoting central neuronal hyperexcitability.16,17 Notably, hyperactivation of the AKT/mTOR pathway—a hub integrating metabolic and trophic signals—facilitates synthesis of synaptic proteins (e.g., PSD-95, synapsin I), potentiates glutamatergic synaptic efficacy, and suppresses GABAergic interneuron activity, thereby disrupting neural network excitation-inhibition equilibrium.18,19 This imbalance is strongly linked to tinnitus chronicity and shares commonality with pathologies like epilepsy and neurodegeneration.20,21 Furthermore, recent evidence indicates that microglial activation amplifies neuroinflammation via a BDNF-TNF-α positive feedback loop, suggesting that glia-neuron crosstalk sustains central hyperexcitability in tinnitus.22,23

The mTOR pathway is a master regulator of protein synthesis-dependent synaptic plasticity, which is critical for neural circuit adaptation in the central auditory system.24 Although mTOR also functions in the peripheral auditory system, its role in central auditory plasticity—and particularly its dysregulation in conditions such as tinnitus—has attracted increasing attention.25,26 Pharmacological inhibition of mTOR with rapamycin can alleviate certain auditory pathologies, but it remains unclear whether this therapeutic effect involves normalizing maladaptive plasticity within central hubs like the inferior colliculus.27

However, direct evidence linking BDNF/AKT/mTOR pathway activation to tinnitus-like behavioral phenotypes is lacking, and whether targeting this pathway can reverse tinnitus-associated neural plasticity remains unverified. This study presents the first systematic analysis of the temporal dynamics of the BDNF/AKT/mTOR pathway in the IC of rats treated with sodium salicylate (SS) over a period of 1–7 days. The experimental timeline and design are summarized in Figure 1. By combining BDNF small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated gene silencing and pharmacological mTOR inhibition with rapamycin, we aimed to elucidate the dynamic regulatory mechanisms of the BDNF-TrkB axis and its downstream cascades in central neuronal hyperexcitability during tinnitus. Sodium salicylate induces tinnitus and upregulates the BDNF/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in the rat inferior colliculus

Temporal development of sodium salicylate-induced changes in PPI and GPIAS

Baseline testing revealed no significant differences in PPI inhibition ratios among groups (control, SS1, SS3, SS5, SS7; p > 0.88), indicating comparable baseline auditory processing and startle reflexes across all animals. Following daily intraperitoneal administration of SS (300 mg/kg/d), PPI inhibition ratios remained stable across all time points (days 1, 3, 5, 7) within experimental groups and compared to controls (p > 0.98). No significant main effect of time or group × time interaction was observed (Figure 2A). Tinnitus-like behavior was evaluated using the gap-prepulse inhibition of the acoustic startle reflex (GPIAS) paradigm. Baseline GPIAS inhibition ratios showed no intergroup differences (p = 0.99), confirming the absence of spontaneous tinnitus phenotypes. SS administration induced significant time-dependent alterations (Figure 2B): Compared to the control group, all experimental groups (SS1, SS3, SS5, SS7) exhibited significantly reduced GPIAS inhibition ratios (p < 0.001). The most pronounced suppression occurred on day 5 (21.61 ± 15.57%, mean ± SD). However, cross-group comparison across treatment durations (SS1–SS7) revealed no statistically significant differences (p > 0.59), suggesting a potential plateau effect in the behavioral manifestation of tinnitus. These findings confirm that SS successfully induced pathological alterations within the central auditory pathway, exhibiting characteristic behavioral signatures consistent with established tinnitus animal models.29

Assessment of sodium salicylate-induced ABR threshold shifts

To evaluate the effects of SS on auditory function, auditory brainstem response (ABR) thresholds were measured. Baseline testing revealed stable thresholds within normal hearing limits (<40 dB SPL) across all groups (4–32 kHz frequency range). Following SS administration (300 mg/kg/d), experimental groups exhibited significant threshold elevations at all tested frequencies (4, 8, 12, 16, 24, and 32 kHz) compared to the control group (p < 0.01) (Figure 2C). Notably, although the SS1 group demonstrated numerically lower mean thresholds than longer-treatment groups, no statistically significant differences were observed across treatment durations (SS1–SS7, p > 0.47). This pattern suggests SS-induced impairment of cochlear afferent nerve function occurs without progressive worsening over the 7-day treatment period.

Sodium salicylate upregulates BDNF/TrkB pathway expression in the inferior colliculus

Western blot analysis was performed to assess alterations in BDNF/TrkB pathway expression within the inferior colliculus of SS-treated rats (Figures 3A and 3B). Key findings include (Figures 3C and 3D) (1) compared to the control group, BDNF protein expression was significantly elevated in the inferior colliculus of the SS3, SS5, and SS7 groups (p < 0.01), while no significant change was observed in the SS1 group (p = 0.24). (2) TrkB protein expression was significantly upregulated across all SS treatment durations (SS1–SS7 groups; p < 0.05), with no significant differences observed between the SS5 and SS7 groups (p = 0.89). These data demonstrate that intraperitoneal SS administration (300 mg/kg) activates the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway in the inferior colliculus. Temporal analysis revealed distinct expression kinetics: BDNF levels peaked at day 5 and subsequently stabilized, whereas TrkB expression progressively increased, reaching maximal levels at day 7. Assessment using the GPIAS paradigm showed no intergroup differences in tinnitus-related behavioral indices before intervention (p > 0.05), confirming the absence of spontaneous tinnitus in all animals at baseline. Post-intervention comparisons revealed (Figure 5B): compared to the control group, the SS group exhibited a significantly decreased GPIAS inhibition ratio (p < 0.05), confirming the successful establishment of a tinnitus-like behavioral model. Compared to the SS group, both the BDNF siRNA group and the rapamycin (RAP) group showed a significant recovery in GPIAS inhibition ratio (p < 0.05). Importantly, the GPIAS inhibition ratio in the BDNF siRNA group and the RAP group was also significantly higher than that in the sham group (p < 0.05) and the control siRNA group (p < 0.05). No significant differences were observed among the SS, sham, and control siRNA groups (p > 0.05). These results indicate that the amelioration of tinnitus-like behavior observed in the BDNF siRNA and RAP groups cannot be attributed to the surgical procedure itself or to non-specific effects of siRNA administration. Both gene silencing targeting the BDNF signaling pathway (siRNA) and mTOR inhibition (rapamycin) effectively reversed SS-induced tinnitus-like behavioral abnormalities in rats.

Changes in ABR thresholds in rats before and after treatment

To investigate the impact of inhibiting the BDNF signaling pathway expression on auditory function, ABR thresholds were assessed in rats before and after treatment. The results demonstrated that baseline ABR thresholds for all tested frequencies were below 40 dB SPL in all animals prior to intervention. Post-intervention intergroup comparison analysis revealed (Figure 5C) (1) compared to the control group, the SS group, sham group, control siRNA group, BDNF siRNA group, and RAP group exhibited significantly elevated ABR thresholds at all tested frequencies (p < 0.05). (2) Intergroup comparisons among these intervention groups (SS, sham, control siRNA, BDNF siRNA, RAP) revealed no statistically significant differences in ABR thresholds (p > 0.05). These findings collectively indicate that neither the surgical procedure itself, non-specific siRNA administration, BDNF siRNA nor rapamycin administration significantly reversed the SS-induced hearing deficits across all tested frequencies.

Discussion

Contemporary research on tinnitus has begun to elucidate the central remodeling mechanisms involved, including hyperactivation of the BDNF signaling pathway, neuroinflammation, and glia-mediated synaptic plasticity alterations. Nevertheless, targeted therapeutic strategies addressing this complex pathophysiology remain limited. This study evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of BDNF siRNA and rapamycin against SS-induced tinnitus in rats and explored their underlying molecular mechanisms. Behavioral assessments revealed that both interventions ameliorated SS-induced tinnitus-like behaviors. Regarding auditory function, while SS induced broad-spectrum hearing impairment, neither BDNF siRNA nor rapamycin significantly improved ABR thresholds. Molecular analyses demonstrated that BDNF siRNA suppressed BDNF/TrkB expression and downstream AKT/mTOR phosphorylation, whereas rapamycin primarily inhibited mTOR phosphorylation without significantly affecting BDNF expression. Importantly, sham surgery and scrambled siRNA control groups confirmed that neither the stereotaxic procedure nor non-specific siRNA effects contributed to the observed behavioral or molecular phenotypes.

Critical role of neural network remodeling in the inferior colliculus in tinnitus pathogenesis

This study provides the first systematic evidence that SS drives tinnitus development through activation of the BDNF/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in the inferior colliculus. As a key integrative hub of the auditory system, the involvement of the IC in tinnitus pathogenesis is supported by multiple lines of evidence: elevated neuronal spontaneous firing rates, persistent resting membrane potential depolarization, and attenuation of GABAergic inhibitory synaptic transmission have all been closely associated with tinnitus-like behavioral phenotypes in animal models.30,31,32,33 Notably, we observed sustained activation of the BDNF/TrkB signaling pathway in the IC following SS intervention. This finding complements recent reports of BDNF upregulation in the auditory cortex of SS-induced tinnitus models, suggesting that distinct auditory nuclei may contribute to tinnitus pathology through differential neurotrophic factor regulatory networks.16

The bidirectional projection properties of neural circuits indicate that the IC plays a pivotal role in relaying peripheral auditory injury signals to central structures.34 We observed that SS-induced elevation of ABR thresholds (indicating reduced peripheral auditory input) occurred concurrently with tinnitus development. Paradoxically, specific inhibition of BDNF signaling in the IC significantly ameliorated tinnitus-like behaviors in rats. This seemingly paradoxical phenomenon suggests that tinnitus manifestation may arise from a dynamic imbalance between central compensatory mechanisms and diminished peripheral input.35 Specifically, SS competitively inhibits prestin function in cochlear outer hair cells, leading to reduced cochlear amplification.8,36 This peripheral deficit subsequently triggers compensatory remodeling within the central auditory system. Such remodeling manifests as BDNF-mediated enhancement of glutamatergic synaptic transmission and mTOR-driven increases in synaptic protein synthesis.18,19 When this compensatory response pathologically exceeds physiological boundaries, it may generate spontaneous neural oscillations, ultimately resulting in tinnitus perception.

Molecular mechanisms underlying BDNF-TrkB signaling axis regulation of synaptic dysfunction

Our experimental data demonstrate that SS intervention induced sustained activation of TrkB receptors during the early phase, while functional BDNF protein expression exhibited delayed upregulation, peaking on day 5. This temporal disparity may be attributed to ligand-independent TrkB activation pathways and the involvement of glial-derived BDNF secretion in later-stage neural remodeling.12,37 The TrkB receptor can be transactivated by other signaling pathways in the absence of BDNF, initiating downstream cascades.38 Furthermore, increased receptor expression may itself elevate basal TrkB activity, as high-density receptors can undergo spontaneous dimerization and phosphorylation.39 In tinnitus models, the early salicylate-induced hyperexcitability of auditory centers could trigger this ligand-independent activation. These mechanisms collectively explain the early upregulation of TrkB signaling and establish a basis for the subsequent rise and sustained activation of BDNF. Notably, BDNF siRNA intervention not only effectively downregulated both BDNF and its receptor TrkB but also significantly suppressed downstream AKT/mTOR pathway activation. This confirms a top-down hierarchical regulatory cascade mediated by the BDNF/TrkB pathway. Aberrant BDNF-TrkB signaling likely disrupts neural network homeostasis in the IC by facilitating the trafficking of AMPA receptor subunit GluA1 to the postsynaptic membrane, thereby enhancing glutamatergic synaptic transmission efficiency; activating mTORC1 to promote increased synthesis of the postsynaptic density protein PSD-95, resulting in persistent and potentially aberrant synaptic structural remodeling.18,19,40

Dynamic regulation characteristics of the AKT/mTOR signaling cascade

This study revealed that SS-induced mTOR phosphorylation (p-mTOR, Ser2448) exhibited rapid early activation, peaking at day 5. This temporal pattern likely reflects distinct phases of cellular pathway engagement: the initial activation phase primarily stems from a rapid response of the mTORC1 complex within neurons, accelerating synaptic protein synthesis through enhanced ribosome biogenesis; whereas the subsequent stabilization phase may involve sustained activation of the mTORC2 complex in microglia, promoting the release of inflammatory cytokines.18,41 Notably, while rapamycin effectively suppressed mTORC1 activity, it demonstrated limited efficacy in regulating AKT phosphorylation (p-AKT, Ser473). This may be attributed to its inability to block BDNF-TrkB-mediated upstream PI3K signaling.42,43 Research indicates that upregulated BDNF in tinnitus animal models may promote pathology by enhancing synaptic plasticity in auditory cortex neurons, a process potentially facilitated by mTOR pathway activation.16,17 Furthermore, mTOR signaling may contribute to tinnitus pathogenesis by modulating neuroinflammation and oxidative stress.41 In the present study, mTOR phosphorylation remained significantly elevated at day 7 post-intervention, suggesting its potential involvement in chronic tinnitus mechanisms through the maintenance of heightened neuronal metabolic activity. The observed upregulation dynamics of the BDNF/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway closely corresponded with behavioral manifestations of tinnitus, supporting the concept that tinnitus development represents a dynamic maladaptive plasticity process.

Central plasticity mechanisms underlying tinnitus attenuation via modulation of the BDNF/AKT/mTOR pathway

Stereotaxic delivery of BDNF siRNA into the IC effectively reduced BDNF expression and significantly attenuated SS-induced tinnitus-like behaviors in rats. This indicates that BDNF within the IC plays a pivotal role in tinnitus pathogenesis, wherein its downregulation may alleviate symptoms by suppressing neuronal hyperexcitability or aberrant neural activity. Prior studies have demonstrated that salicylates induce acute excitotoxic injury via potentiation of glutamatergic signaling at cochlear NMDA receptors, concurrently triggering neural plasticity alterations in central auditory structures (e.g., inferior colliculus and auditory cortex)—a key mechanism in tinnitus generation.30,44 Notably, despite significant behavioral improvements following BDNF siRNA and rapamycin interventions, neither treatment altered ABR thresholds. This suggests the predominant involvement of the BDNF/AKT/mTOR pathway in central auditory plasticity rather than peripheral auditory function.

BDNF’s role differs between central and peripheral auditory systems. Centrally, BDNF primarily modulates synaptic plasticity through TrkB receptors, facilitating neural network reorganization in response to auditory insult or environmental changes.17,45 Peripherally, it sustains survival and function of spiral ganglion neurons and inner hair cells during injury repair.46 This dichotomy likely explains why BDNF silencing preferentially affects central plasticity while minimally impacting peripheral auditory restoration.

We observed upregulated BDNF expression and enhanced AKT/mTOR phosphorylation in the IC of SS-induced tinnitus models, suggesting central auditory hyperexcitability. Rapamycin, a selective mTOR inhibitor, modulates neural plasticity by suppressing mTOR activation.47 mTORC1’s established role in driving protein translation—particularly of synaptic components like PSD-95 and glutamate receptor subunits—is central to neural plasticity. In our model, rapamycin selectively reduced p-mTOR(S2448), a marker of mTORC1 activity, without altering upstream BDNF/TrkB/p-AKT signaling. This dissociation implies that salicylate-induced hyperactivity may converge upon and be maintained by mTORC1-dependent translational machinery. Elevated levels of PSD-95 and GluA1 in the auditory cortex and inferior colliculus have been associated with behavioral phenotypes in models of tinnitus and hyperacusis. As an mTORC1 inhibitor, rapamycin attenuates such pathological synaptic protein synthesis. Consequently, we propose that the behavioral improvement observed with rapamycin likely resulted from normalizing the salicylate-enhanced, mTORC1-driven synthesis of key excitatory synaptic proteins in inferior colliculus neurons, which restored excitatory-inhibitory balance. Its therapeutic mechanisms may involve (1) normalization of neuronal hyperactivity in the IC and (2) inhibition of maladaptive neuroplasticity. These findings provide novel experimental evidence for tinnitus pathogenesis and highlight targeted modulation of the BDNF/AKT/mTOR pathway as a promising therapeutic strategy.

Translational implications and future directions

This study demonstrates that targeting the BDNF/AKT/mTOR pathway effectively alleviates behavioral manifestations of tinnitus; yet, translating this approach into clinical practice remains challenging. The required intracerebral siRNA injection is invasive and clinically impractical, necessitating the exploration of more feasible delivery methods. We found that systemic rapamycin administration partially reverses tinnitus symptoms, indicating that modulating central signaling through systemic treatment is a viable strategy. Future research could develop more specific and potent mTORC1 inhibitors or antagonists targeting the BDNF/TrkB axis for tinnitus therapy. Small-molecule TrkB antagonists, for instance, have suppressed neurotrophic signaling in models of other neurological diseases and warrant investigation for tinnitus intervention.48 Potential side effects from long-term mTOR inhibition must also be considered. Chronic use of rapamycin analogs may cause adverse effects including immunosuppression, impaired wound healing, and metabolic disorders.49 Consequently, designing chronic tinnitus treatments requires a careful balance between efficacy and risk, where intermittent dosing could help mitigate side effects. In summary, while our work identifies a novel molecular target for tinnitus, subsequent studies must prioritize resolving drug delivery and safety issues to advance this strategy toward clinical application.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. First, behavioral detection of tinnitus relied on the GPIAS under broadband background noise. This paradigm assumes the salicylate-induced tinnitus percept is broadband or spectrally complex. Consequently, it may be less sensitive to highly frequency-specific, tonal tinnitus. Future studies using multiple narrowband noise backgrounds or direct neural coding analyses could better resolve the frequency topology of this phantom percept. Second, although we included a scrambled siRNA control to account for sequence-independent effects, off-target silencing remains a formal possibility inherent to siRNA approaches. More specific genetic evidence could be obtained in future studies using knockout models. Third, this study employed the SS model, which is well-established for dissecting central plasticity mechanisms but does not recapitulate noise-induced cochlear damage—the most common clinical etiology of tinnitus. Validation in noise-exposure models is therefore required to confirm the translational relevance of these findings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

A total of 66 male Wistar rats (8 weeks old, 205.12 ± 10.88 g) were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Certificate No. 20220004046897). Animals were housed in SPF barrier facilities at the Experimental Animal Center of Yueyang Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Environmental conditions were maintained at 20°C–25°C, 40–70% relative humidity, with a 12-h light/dark cycle. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All interventions were administered daily at 10:00 a.m. The experimental protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. YYLAC-2024-242).

Method details

Experimental design

In the initial phase, following an adaptive feeding period, 30 rats were randomly allocated into five groups (Figure 1A): a control group (Control, n = (6) receiving intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of 0.9% physiological saline (3 mL/kg/d) for 7 consecutive days; four experimental groups administered 10% sodium salicylate solution (300 mg/kg/d, i.p.) for 1 day (SS1, n = 6), 3 days (SS3, n = 6), 5 days (SS5, n = 6), or 7 days (SS7, n = 6). Following completion of the treatment regimen, the rat tinnitus model was evaluated using prepulse inhibition (PPI) and gap-prepulse inhibition of the acoustic startle reflex (GPIAS), auditory function was assessed via auditory brainstem response (ABR), and the animals were subsequently euthanized.

Following determination of the optimal modeling duration, 36 rats were randomly divided into six groups (Figure 1B). The control group (Control, n = (6) received i.p. injections of 0.9% sodium chloride solution (3 mL/kg/d). The sodium salicylate group (SS, n = 6), sham surgery group (Sham, n = 6), scrambled siRNA control group (Control siRNA, n = 6), BDNF small interfering RNA group (BDNF siRNA, n = 6), and rapamycin group (RAP, n = (6) all received i.p. injections of 10% sodium salicylate (300 mg/kg/d). Additionally, all surgically treated groups (Sham, Control siRNA, and BDNF siRNA) underwent bilateral stereotaxic surgery on days 4 and 5 of SS treatment. For the BDNF siRNA group, BDNF siRNA (400 nM per side, cat. no. sc-42121, Santa Cruz) was microinjected into the inferior colliculus. The Control siRNA group received an identical volume and concentration of a control scrambled siRNA (400 nM per side, cat. no. sc-36869, Santa Cruz), which is a non-targeting 19–25 nt siRNA designed as a negative control. The Sham group underwent the complete surgical procedure, including needle insertion, but no solution was injected. The RAP group was concurrently administered i.p. injections of rapamycin solution (cat. no. S1039, Selleck; 2 mg/kg) daily during sodium salicylate administration. Following the intervention period, the rat tinnitus model and auditory function were evaluated, and the animals were then euthanized.

Behavioral test of tinnitus

Tinnitus behavior was evaluated using GPIAS and PPI paradigms in an SR-LAB startle response system (San Diego Instruments, USA). Rats were placed individually in a sound-attenuating chamber (Figure 1E) equipped with a piezoelectric transducer to measure startle amplitude (Figure 1F) elicited by a 115 dB white noise burst (20 m s duration).

For PPI testing, following a 3 min acclimation in silence, rats were presented with two randomized conditions (10 trials each): (1) startle stimulus (115 dB SPL, 20 m s broadband noise) alone (amplitude recorded as V1), and (2) a prepulse stimulus (65 dB SPL, 50 m s broadband noise) followed 100 m s later by the startle stimulus (amplitude recorded as V2), with the PPI inhibition ratio calculated as [(V1-V2)/V1]×100%. For GPIAS testing, after a 5 min rest period, continuous 65 dB SPL background noise was introduced; following a 3 min noise acclimation, two randomized conditions were presented (30 trials each): (1) startle stimulus (115 dB SPL, 20 m s broadband noise) delivered in continuous background noise (amplitude recorded as V3), and (2) startle stimulus delivered 100 m s after a 50-ms silent gap inserted into the background noise (amplitude recorded as V4), with the GPIAS inhibition ratio calculated as [(V3-V4)/V3]×100%. Inter-trial intervals exceeded 30 s for all paradigms to minimize habituation effects.

Auditory brainstem response

ABR thresholds were recorded using a TDT RZ6 BioAmp Processor (Tucker-Davis Technologies, USA). Rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/kg, i.p.) and placed in a sound-attenuated, temperature-controlled chamber (37°C). Subdermal needle electrodes were positioned as follows: active electrode at the vertex, reference electrode posterior to the contralateral pinna, and ground electrode on the back (impedance <1 kΩ). Pure tone stimuli (frequencies: 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32 kHz) were generated by the TDT system and delivered via a free-field speaker positioned 10 cm in front of the rat’s head (Figure 1G). Stimuli began at 80 dB SPL and decreased in 5 dB steps (Figure 1H). The threshold was defined as the lowest intensity at which a reproducible wave II could be identified following 1024 signal averages.

Stereotaxic surgery and microinjection

Rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg) and secured within a stereotaxic apparatus (Puxin, Shanghai, China). Utilizing coordinates from a rat brain stereotaxic atlas,28 the IC target site was identified as: 8.3 mm posterior to bregma, ±1.5 mm lateral to the midline, and 4.5 mm ventral to the skull surface (Figure 1C). Following aseptic preparation of the surgical field, a midline scalp incision was made. The periosteum was bluntly dissected to expose the skull. A microsurgical drill (XSZ-4, Guangdian, Shanghai, China) was used to create a burr hole at the designated coordinates. Guided by the stereotaxic arm, a microsyringe was vertically lowered to the target depth. BDNF siRNA solution (cat. no. sc-42121, Santa Cruz, a pool of three to five target-specific 19–25 nt siRNAs designed to knockdown gene expression) was bilaterally infused into the IC at a volume of 1 μL per side (Figure 1D). The Control siRNA group received an identical volume and concentration of a control scrambled siRNA (cat. no. sc-36869, Santa Cruz, a non-targeting 19–25 nt siRNA designed as a negative control). The Sham group underwent the complete surgical procedure, including needle insertion to the target coordinates, but no solution was injected. The needle was left in situ for 2 min post-injection to allow diffusion. The incision was sutured in layers and the surgical site was disinfected. Postoperatively, animals were monitored for respiratory status, body temperature, and anesthetic recovery. Rats exhibiting no signs of neurological impairment were returned to their home cages for recovery.

The rationale for naked siRNA delivery refers to the direct administration of a solution containing uncomplexed siRNA sequences into the target site. Certain tissues, such as the eye, central nervous system (CNS), and lung, are capable of efficiently absorbing naked siRNA.50,51,52 Previous studies have successfully employed naked siRNA injections into the nervous system to silence target protein expression.53,54 Consequently, this study utilized naked injection of BDNF siRNA or control siRNA into the IC to achieve localized suppression of BDNF protein expression within this brain region. To mitigate potential in vivo degradation of the siRNA, a dose of 400 nM per side was administered, with the siRNA dissolved in RNase-free water. The scrambled siRNA (sc-(36869) is a proprietary sequence (not disclosed by Santa Cruz), unmodified 19–25 nt duplex, FITC-conjugated. Both siRNAs were dissolved in RNase-free water at 400 nM per side.

A separate cohort (n = (3) received FITC-conjugated scrambled siRNA injection. Weak residual fluorescence at the expected IC site (Figure S1) provides supportive, not definitive, evidence for targeting accuracy.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence Staining was performed as previously described.55 Experimental rats were deeply anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital sodium (40 mg/kg). Transcardial perfusion was performed by sequentially infusing 50 mL of physiological saline followed by 100 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) through the left ventricle. Following brain extraction, whole brains were immersed in 4% PFA and fixed at room temperature for 48 h. Tissues were then sequentially dehydrated through a graded sucrose series (10%, 20%, and 40%) until they sank to the bottom. The IC region was identified according to a rat brain atlas.28 After removing excess tissue anterior and posterior to the IC, the samples were embedded in OCT compound (cat. no. MM1501, Sakura) and stored at −80°C until use. Coronal sections (8 μm thick) were serially cut using a cryostat (CM3050S, Leica, Germany) at −20°C. Coronal sections containing the IC were selected. For imaging and analysis, the central nucleus of the IC was identified based on anatomical landmarks and sampled consistently across all animals. Sections were air-dried at room temperature for 30 min, rehydrated in distilled water, and then washed three times (5 min each) in phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST). Permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, followed by additional PBST washes. After blocking with a solution of 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 10% normal goat serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature, sections were incubated with the primary antibody (BDNF, 1:500 dilution, cat. no. ab108319, Abcam) at room temperature for 2 h. Following PBST washes, sections were incubated with the secondary antibody (1:500 dilution, cat. no. ab150073, Abcam) for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (cat. no. 28718-90-3, Solarbio). Sections were mounted with glycergel mounting medium and coverslipped. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (DM6000B, Leica, Germany), with consistent imaging parameters maintained across all experimental groups.

Western blot

Western blotting was performed as previously described.56 Following rapid euthanasia of anesthetized experimental rats, brain tissue was promptly excised. The inferior colliculus was isolated according to a rat brain atlas and stored at −80°C. Tissue samples were homogenized in lysis buffer and centrifuged; the supernatant was collected for protein concentration determination using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Protein samples were denatured by heating at 95°C for 5 min. Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) resolving gels (8% or 12%, depending on the target protein molecular weight) were prepared. Proteins were separated by electrophoresis under constant voltage (80 V) and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes under constant current (340 mA) in an ice bath. Membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: BDNF (1:1000, cat. no. ab108319, Abcam), TrkB (1:800, cat. no. ab187041, Abcam), AKT (1:1000, cat. no. 9272, Cell Signaling Technology), p-AKT (Ser473, 1:1000, cat. no. 9271, Cell Signaling Technology), mTOR (1:1000, cat. no. 2983 T, Cell Signaling Technology), p-mTOR (Ser2448, 1:500, cat. no. 2971 S, Cell Signaling Technology), and β-actin (1:5000, cat. no. AC026, ABclonal). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000, cat. no. D110058, Sangon Biotech) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent. Band intensity (gray values) was quantified using ImageJ software. Three independent replicates were performed for each experiment.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Experimental data were statistically analyzed and visualized using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Data were initially assessed for normality and homogeneity of variance. For data involving two independent variables (e.g., treatment group and time/frequency), two-way analysis of variance (two-Way ANOVA) followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was performed. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.
