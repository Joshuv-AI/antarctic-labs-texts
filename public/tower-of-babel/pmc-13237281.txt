Top 10 ground challenges in microsystems and nanoengineering
Authors: Microsystems & Nanoengineering
Published: 2026

Microsystems and Nanoengineering—a cutting-edge discipline integrating microfabrication, nanotechnology, and interdisciplinary system design—boasts pivotal strategic value. Over the past two decades, explorations into the research and development of microdevices and integrated systems with high precision, low power consumption, and multifunctional characteristics have experienced an explosive growth. Technological innovations in this field have provided core support for humanity to tackle numerous critical challenges, leaving a profound imprint on areas ranging from intelligent medical sensing and industrial precision detection to aerospace miniaturized equipment, as well as hardware innovations in next-generation information technology.

Like all academic fields in a period of rapid development, microsystems and nanoengineering still face a multitude of unresolved bottleneck issues. Fortunately, the number of research teams and engineering forces dedicated to exploring its underlying principles, breaking through manufacturing bottlenecks, and expanding application boundaries is larger than ever before.

To commemorate the 10th anniversary of the journal’s founding, Microsystems & Nanoengineering has launched a global survey targeting scholars and industry experts in this field. The initiative aims to identify the most critical and innovative core scientific issues and technical challenges that must be addressed to promote the large-scale application and cross-domain integration of microsystems and nanoengineering.

Under micro- and nano-systems Engineering, how to break through the key bottleneck of design complexity and manufacturing yield via AI-augmented methodologies?

A central challenge in micro- and nano-systems engineering lies in overcoming the critical bottlenecks of escalating design complexity and constrained manufacturing efficiency. The core research question focuses on how AI-augmented methodologies can fundamentally transform the traditional, often sequential and experience-dependent, design-fabrication-operation cycle into an integrated, data-driven, and adaptive process.

As device complexity increases—driven by demands for multi-functionality and miniaturization—traditional trial-and-error and physics-only simulations become prohibitively time-consuming and costly. They struggle to navigate vast design spaces and accurately predict real-world manufacturing outcomes, leading to suboptimal performance, low yield, and extended development cycles. This urgency is amplified by emerging applications in personalized healthcare, IoT, and advanced communications, which require highly customized and reliable microsystems.

To fundamentally address current bottlenecks in advanced device development, the research must focus on pioneering multi-modal AI architectures designed to seamlessly integrate heterogeneous data streams—from foundational materials science and high-fidelity multi-physics simulation to real-time manufacturing process feeds. A primary strategic thrust involves establishing dynamic digital twins that enable comprehensive closed-loop optimization across the entire “design-fabricate-characterize” lifecycle. A core application involves employing advanced machine learning to precisely model the highly intricate, non-linear dependencies between fabrication inputs and final device characteristics, thereby facilitating the autonomous, iterative refinement of both manufacturing workflows and architectural specifications. This integrated paradigm facilitates true autonomous design exploration under complex, multi-objective constraints and ensures real-time control over fabrication processes. Success in these pivotal areas will dramatically accelerate R&D timelines, substantially elevate production yield and reliability, and ultimately unlock a new generation of high-performance, intelligent devices—ranging from adaptive sensors to smart implants—promising profound technological and socioeconomic transformations.

How can advances in biohybrid microsystem technologies and their fabrication be applied to micro- nanorobotics, precision medicine, and targeted therapies?

The past few decades have seen an extraordinary transformation in how we understand and treat disease. As medicine increasingly shifts toward precision—tailoring treatments to the individual characteristics of each patient—the need for equally precise tools has never been greater. Enter biohybrid systems: a field that is redefining what is possible at the smallest scales of intervention in different fields of science and technology. With the advent of biofabrication methods and spurred by additive manufacturing, newer methods that combine the use of biological cells with synthetic materials are being developed. The development of these newer methods that use highly sensitive cells has significantly increased over the past 5-10 years, and hybrid systems that combine metals, inorganic oxides, magnetic materials, synthetic polymers with natural biological polymers and cells have been developed.

Micro- and nanorobots, typically ranging from micrometers to nanometers in size, are engineered to perform tasks within the human body that were once the realm of science fiction. These miniature machines can navigate complex biological environments, deliver therapeutic agents directly to diseased cells, perform minimally invasive procedures, or even assist in real-time diagnostics. Their potential impact spans across oncology, neurology, cardiology, and infectious diseases, offering new pathways for treatment with reduced side effects and improved outcomes. What makes this field particularly exciting is its interdisciplinary nature, including the integration of new technologies that combine biological and synthetic materials, such as biohybrid systems. Yet, significant challenges remain in bringing biohybrid systems into micro robotics: biofabrication methods, biocompatibility, precise control in vivo, scalable manufacturing, and clinical translation are all active areas of research.

Applications of these systems are mainly in the biomedical space, with regenerative medicine, human augmentation, and organ replacement being the main focus. Environmental applications and industrial applications in biorobotics and in sensing systems are also being considered. The impact of these newly emerging fabrication methods with applications will introduce dramatic changes in the social and economic fabric of society.

In nanobiosensor-based global health diagnostics, how to balance the relationship between ultra-sensitivity and affordability to achieve equitable healthcare access?

Global health stands as one of the most critical challenges of the 21st century, encompassing not only individual well-being but also the environmental and socioeconomic systems that sustain it. Equitable access to sustainable diagnostic technologies remains a foundation of effective healthcare worldwide. However, conventional laboratory-based diagnostics, though accurate and well-established, are often costly, resource-intensive, and dependent on skilled personnel, factors that limit their accessibility, especially in low-resource settings where disease burden is highest. Focusing on advanced nanobiosensors, which are biosensing platforms optimized through nanotechnology and renewable materials, serves as a pathway to explore next-generation, sustainable global health diagnostic technologies. The core research question focuses on how to design nanobiosensor systems that balance ultra-sensitivity, affordability, and scalability, ensuring diagnostic equity and sustainability across diverse healthcare contexts.

Addressing global diagnostic inequities requires overcoming key technological and socioeconomic bottlenecks. Traditional diagnostics face inherent constraints in portability, cost-effectiveness, and real-time data connectivity, limiting their deployment at the point of care (POC). The REASSURED framework (Real-time connectivity, Ease of specimen collection, Affordable, Sensitive, Specific, User-friendly, Rapid and robust, Equipment-free, and Deliverable to end-users) outlines essential criteria for next-generation diagnostic tools but remains challenging to fully realize due to gaps in sustainable materials, device integration, and circular design. Moreover, pandemic preparedness and personalized medicine demand rapid, scalable, and environmentally responsible diagnostic solutions. These challenges highlight the urgency of developing nanobiosensors that merge high analytical performance with eco-design principles and digital health integration, thereby addressing both health and sustainability imperatives.

To overcome these bottlenecks, research must advance in the integration of renewable nanomaterials (such as graphene, quantum dots, and nanostructured polymers) with sustainable fabrication technologies and digital diagnostic platforms. Such convergence enables portable, affordable, and highly sensitive biosensing systems that fulfill REASSURED principles. By promoting circularity, affordability, and scalability, advanced nanobiosensors can revolutionize global diagnostics, enabling early disease detection, real-time health monitoring, and rapid epidemic response. Beyond medical impact, their widespread adoption can stimulate socioeconomic development through technology transfer, spin-off creation, and innovation ecosystem strengthening, ultimately driving progress toward sustainable, equitable, and digitally connected healthcare for all.

How to balance the relationship between atomic-scale manufacturing and cross-scale domain control technology to achieve high-performance MEMS devices?

A combination of micro and nano manufacturing methods has become a fundamental technology for creating high-performance MEMS devices for healthcare, computing, navigation, and industrial automation applications. The performance of traditional MEMS devices relies heavily on “top-to-bottom” and “bottom-to-top” fabrication techniques. However, micro-nano fabrication precision is approaching the physical limits even with the use of scanning probes and electron beams, enabling single-atom manipulation.

During micro-nano fabrication, factors including thermal fluctuations, stochastic defect nucleation, and process disturbances significantly degrade the performance of MEMS devices. “How to maintain and transfer atomic-level precision throughout the manufacturing process” has become a major challenge for high-performance planar and non-planar MEMS devices. Future research will focus on active suppression of atomic-scale defects, atomic-level smoothing control of interfacial abruptness, among other strategies.

Several critical challenges urgently need to be overcome: (1) establishing physical models of precision attenuation to quantify key sources of precision loss; (2) developing novel in-situ real-time correction methods through energy-field intervention, enabling “closed-loop manufacturing” for dynamic compensation of precision deviations; (3) creating novel intrinsic “precision-friendly” material systems to sustain atomic-level accuracy in manufacturing, including advanced 3D printing techniques. These interventions are expected to drive the development of high-performance MEMS devices for applications in healthcare, quantum technology, computing, navigation, and industrial automation in the “post-Moore era”.

In the field of MEMS physical sensors, how to break through the limitations of single-physical-effect and achieve a performance leap through multi-physics coupling?

In the field of MEMS physical sensors, the core research question is how to significantly enhance sensor performance via multi-physics coupling—especially as current devices (e.g., magnetic and accelerometer sensors) face performance bottlenecks due to their heavy reliance on a single physical effect.

It is quite necessary to carry out this research. Existing MEMS physical sensors, relying solely on single physical effects like piezoresistive or capacitive effects, have limited performance. Each physical effect, such as piezoresistive, capacitive, optical, thermal, and magnetic effects, provides unique information about the measured quantity. In a single-effect sensor, only one type of information is utilized. However, when multiple physical effects are coupled, different aspects of the measured object can be sensed simultaneously. Moreover, although this topic has been discussed since the early days of MEMS research, there is still a lack of in-depth practical results. This highlights the urgency and practical significance of the research.

In response to the above research bottlenecks, the cutting-edge research direction can focus on integrating multi-physics coupling mechanisms, including optical, electrical, thermal, and magnetic aspects, into the structural design of MEMS, and strengthening heterogeneous integration and interface control. It is expected that the sensor accuracy can be improved by an order of magnitude, and the power consumption can be reduced by more than 50%. This will provide high-performance and low-power core components for high-end applications such as autonomous driving, UAV navigation systems, and biomedical sensing.

In implantable wireless neural modulation, how to address the energy and bidirectional signal communication to meet the demands of completely impassive closed-loop neuro modulation in vivo?

The core of fully passive closed-loop neural modulation lies in how to get rid of batteries and wires and establish an intelligent system that can self-power, self-sense, and self-regulate. Traditional implantable neural modulation devices rely on battery power, which has problems such as limited lifespan, the need for surgical replacement, large size, and high infection risk. At the same time, existing systems are mostly open-loop control and lack the ability to provide real-time feedback on neural signals, making it difficult to achieve precise and dynamic intervention. Therefore, researching how to achieve a closed-loop neural modulation system that does not require an external energy supply and has real-time sensing and regulation capabilities has become a core challenge that needs to be urgently broken through in the current field of neural engineering.

Conducting research on fully passive closed-loop neural modulation has significant clinical and scientific significance. With the continuous increase in the number of patients with neurological diseases, such as Parkinson’s disease, epilepsy, and depression, traditional drug treatment and open-loop electrical stimulation therapy are no longer able to meet the needs of individualized and precise treatment. Closed-loop systems can dynamically adjust stimulation parameters based on real-time changes in neural activity, improving therapeutic effects and reducing side effects. However, existing closed-loop systems rely on batteries and complex circuits, limiting their feasibility and safety for long-term implantation. Therefore, developing a battery-free, long-term stable, and self-adaptive regulation-capable fully passive neural modulation technology can not only reduce medical costs and risks but also promote the development of neural science research and brain-computer interface technology.

To break through the existing technical bottlenecks, future research should focus on three major directions: energy self-sufficiency, signal sensing, and intelligent regulation. On the one hand, piezoelectric, magnetoelectric, or thermoelectric materials can be used to convert tiny mechanical movements, magnetic field changes, or temperature differences in the body into electrical energy to achieve self-powering of the system. On the other hand, high-sensitivity nanosensors can be used to collect neural electrical signals in real time, and combined with ultra-low power signal processing circuits to achieve local signal analysis and feedback. Ultimately, a smart microsystem based on the collaborative design of materials, devices, and algorithms should be constructed to achieve truly fully passive closed-loop regulation. Once this technology matures, it can be widely applied in the treatment of neurological diseases and provide key technical support for brain-computer interfaces, artificial nerves, and intelligent prosthetics, ushering in a new era of self-driven and self-evolving neural modulation.

In Organ-on-Chip Systems, how to balance the precision modeling of micro-nano systems with real-time biosensing integration to achieve functional organoid reconstruction?

Organoids have emerged as indispensable models for studying human development, disease mechanisms, and drug responses. However, their functionality, stability, and reproducibility remain inadequate, limiting their translational potential. Organ-on-Chip (OoC) technologies offer transformative platforms that integrate micro- and nano-engineering, biomaterials, and biosensing strategies. A critical scientific question arises in OoC about how the precision modeling of micro-nano systems can be effectively balanced with real-time biosensing integration to achieve functional organoid reconstruction. Addressing this challenge requires cross-disciplinary innovation spanning micro-nano fabrication, biomimetic modeling, and intelligent sensing.

Despite recent progress, several bottlenecks have hindered the advancement of organoid functional reconstruction. Current organoid models often fail to accurately reproduce the vascularization, immune microenvironment, and inter-organ communication, which are crucial for physiological relevance. Furthermore, the lack of real-time and high-fidelity biosensing limits our ability to monitor dynamic biochemical and biophysical processes within organoids. At the technological level, achieving precise regulation of microenvironmental parameters, including mechanical cues, chemical gradients, and cellular organization, requires sub-micron level control and reliable sensor integration. These limitations highlight the urgent need for developing intelligent OoC platforms that can simultaneously model, sense, and feedback regulate to bridge the gap between structural reconstruction and functional maturation.

Future efforts should focus on three key directions: (1) enhancing organoid reconstruction by integrating vascular and immune components within OoC platforms; (2) developing multi-organ interaction systems to simulate complex physiological networks; and (3) advancing multimodal biosensing and AI-assisted data analysis for continuous, real-time monitoring. Technological breakthroughs in nanoscale biological interface regulation, precision microenvironment engineering, and intelligent control algorithms will enable dynamic feedback between organoid behavior and device response. Ultimately, the integration of OoC micro-nano system technologies will accelerate new drug discovery, improve clinical predictability, and open up new fields in personalized and regenerative medicine, delivering both scientific and socioeconomic impact.

In artificial intelligence applications, how can fluidic memristors address the issues of low performance and poor integrability to meet the requirements of neuromorphic computing chips?

With the rapid development and widespread deployment of artificial intelligence, the demand for computing power continues to rise exponentially. However, constrained by the von Neumann bottleneck, the computational speed and energy efficiency of conventional architectures are approaching their theoretical limits. By contrast, the human brain achieves remarkable computational capability and learning efficiency through densely interconnected neuronal networks operating at ultralow energy consumption. Nanofluidic ionic devices, which process and propagate signals through ion transport in aqueous media, emulate the essential operating principles of biological synapses. Compared with solid-state memristors, fluidic ionic memristors offer distinct advantages, including high noise tolerance, versatile encoding modalities, biocompatibility, and responsiveness to chemical stimuli. Recent progress has shown that diverse nanofluidic ionic memristors can emulate various synaptic and neural behaviors.

Despite these advances, nanofluidic ionic memristors are still at an emerging stage and face several challenges that impede their deployment in neuromorphic computing. First, the intrinsically slower ion mobility, relative to electronic carriers, limits the device response speed. Second, the diffusion nature of ions leads to a short memory retention time of fluidic memristors. Third, scalable and reliable strategies for the large-scale integration of complex nanofluidic devices remain underdeveloped, representing a critical bottleneck for constructing functional neuromorphic circuits.

Addressing these issues requires deeper insight into ion diffusion, equilibrium, and transport phenomena under nanoscale confinement, thereby informing the rational engineering of channel geometries and interfacial chemistries to optimize memristive behavior of fluidic devices. The feasible strategies include: (1) engineering biomimetic ion channels, where nanoscale confinement and conjugated sites can promote efficient ion transport; (2) constructing ångström-scale channels and implementing surface functionalization to promote the formation of Bjerrum polyelectrolytes and strengthen ion adsorption, thereby prolonging memory retention time; (3) building programmable fluidic memristors using ion gels, alongside the development of multi-material micro/nano-additive manufacturing technologies to enable the monolithic integration of ion-gel channels, micro-reservoirs, and micro-electrodes, ultimately facilitating the construction of nanofluidic neuromorphic computing chips.

In heterogeneous integration, how can micro/nanoscale thermo-mechanical stress co-design resolve the conflict between extreme integration density and reliability assurance?

Heterogeneous integration, which vertically stacks and interconnects chips made with different processes and materials, has become a key technology pathway for extending system performance beyond the limits of Moore’s Law. Yet its practical implementation faces a fundamental research challenge: how to achieve thermo-mechanical reliability co-design at the micro/nanoscale under strong multi-physics coupling. Severe mismatches in coefficients of thermal expansion (CTE) among dissimilar materials lead to interfacial delamination and warpage; stress concentration makes interconnect structures such as through-silicon vias (TSVs) and micro-bumps highly susceptible to fatigue fracture; and rapidly increasing heat flux density in 3D architectures imposes stringent thermal-management requirements. These interacting factors make it difficult to simultaneously optimize structural integrity, thermal performance, and long-term system reliability, forming the core scientific problem of this research field.

Addressing this problem is urgently necessary because existing approaches remain limited in their predictive accuracy, scalability, and integration with design workflows. Current multi-physics simulation tools struggle to capture the coupled thermal, mechanical, and interfacial phenomena at micro/nanoscale dimensions. Although hybrid bonding and other advanced interconnect technologies have entered industrial adoption, their reliability still depends strongly on process variations and material heterogeneity. Meanwhile, atomistic-scale studies of interfacial transport and mechanical behavior have produced valuable insights yet remain insufficient for guiding multiscale system-level design. These gaps represent critical bottlenecks: insufficient understanding of stress evolution, inadequate reliability evaluation methodologies, and the absence of unified co-design frameworks that can balance performance, manufacturability, and lifetime under complex operating conditions.

To break through these bottlenecks, future research should advance in three interconnected directions. First, the microstructural design of novel thermal interface materials and active cooling technologies can enable directional and adaptive heat management. Second, key manufacturing processes—particularly hybrid bonding—require further optimization to achieve higher yield, reduced cost, and improved structural robustness. Third, integrating AI with multiscale, multi-physics modeling will enable intelligent layout and reliability co-design, while innovations in stress-adaptive structures and engineered interfaces can fundamentally enhance durability. Together, these advances will establish standardized frameworks for reliability assessment, unlocking the full performance potential of advanced packaging technologies and supporting the growth of artificial intelligence, high-performance computing, and next-generation communications.

Under the trend of On-Chip photonic integration, how to break through the key bottleneck of electrically pumped lasing at the nanoscale via low-dimensional materials and plasmonic cavities?

As an emerging frontier in photonics, nanolaser technology represents a paradigm shift in light generation and control through its capacity to confine optical fields at subwavelength dimensions. The nanoscale optical cavities surmount the fundamental barrier of the optical diffraction limit while simultaneously unlocking unprecedented opportunities in quantum information processing and on-chip photonic manipulation. This disruptive technology is poised to become the cornerstone of next-generation photonics, particularly for high-density optoelectronic chips and ultra-fast optical communications. This proposal addresses a central challenge: achieving electrically pumped, low-threshold nanolasers that are compatible with photonic circuits to bridge the gap between physical mechanisms research and practical applications.

The pressing need for this research stems from fundamental limitations in current nanolaser architectures. Although significant progress has been made in demonstrating room-temperature lasing under optical excitation, the transition to efficient electrical pumping remains hindered by multiple physical constraints. These include inefficient carrier injection mechanisms, challenging thermal dissipation requirements, and inadequate optical mode confinement at nanoscale dimensions. Furthermore, existing nanolaser systems typically exhibit excessive power consumption and limited compatibility with mobile platform specifications. These technical barriers obstruct the development of energy-efficient, high-speed optical communication networks, which underscores the critical demand for innovative approaches in both material systems and resonant cavity designs.

To address these challenges, research priorities should center on the synergistic integration of novel low-dimensional gain materials with advanced plasmonic confinement structures. Particularly promising is the development of hybrid cavity systems through deliberate symmetry engineering. By strategically modifying the structural symmetry in plasmonic-photonic resonant cavities, researchers can achieve precise control over lasing characteristics, including polarization states and radiation patterns—capabilities indispensable for practical photonic integration. This methodology, when combined with the exceptional optical gain and charge carrier confinement properties of low-dimensional semiconductors, enables extreme subwavelength light compression while maintaining efficient electrical injection characteristics. The resulting architectural solutions are anticipated to demonstrate low-threshold lasing operation with high emission efficiency while facilitating the implementation of densely integrated, power-efficient nanolaser chips. These advances will drive the development of photonic information systems and next-generation computing, enabling fully integrated platforms that harness nanoscale light sources.
