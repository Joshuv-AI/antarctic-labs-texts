CALM: Class-Conditional Sparse Attention Vectors for Large Audio-Language Models
Videet Mehta, Liming Wang, Hilde Kuehne, Rogerio Feris, James R. Glass, M. Jehanzeb Mirza

Abstract
Large audio-language models (LALMs) exhibit strong zero-shot capabilities in multiple downstream tasks, such as audio question answering (AQA) and abstract reasoning; however, these models still lag behind specialized models for certain discriminative tasks (e.g., audio classification). Recent studies show that sparse subsets of attention heads within an LALM can serve as strong discriminative feature extractors for downstream tasks such as classification via simple voting schemes. However, these methods assign uniform weights to all selected heads, implicitly assuming that each head contributes equally across all semantic categories. In this work, we propose C lass-Conditional Sparse A ttention Vectors for Large Audio- L anguage M odels (CALM), a few-shot classification method that learns class-dependent importance weights over attention heads. This formulation allows individual heads to specialize in distinct semantic categories and to contribute to ensemble predictions proportionally to their estimated reliability. Experiments on multiple few-shot audio and audiovisual classification benchmarks and tasks demonstrate that our method consistently outperforms state-of-the-art uniform voting-based approaches by up to 14.52%, 1.53%, 8.35% absolute gains for audio classification, audio-visual classification, and spoofing detection respectively. Introduction

Large audio-language models (LALMs), such as Qwen2-Audio
9
and Qwen2.5-Omni
44
, have demonstrated impressive zero-shot and few-shot capabilities across a wide range of audio understanding tasks
9
;
44
. These models leverage large-scale pretraining on paired audio-text data and demonstrate strong generalization without requiring task-specific fine-tuning
33
;
23
;
26
.

Despite their strong generative and reasoning capabilities, effectively re-purposing LALMs for discriminative audio classification remains an open challenge. Specialized audio classifiers often outperform prompt-based approaches but require substantial labeled data and task-specific training pipelines
14
;
43
;
13
;
17
.
In contrast, directly leveraging frozen LALMs for classification offers several advantages, including reduced training costs
18
, lower sample complexity in few-shot settings, and a unified model that supports both generative and discriminative tasks without retraining
45
.

Recent work has shown that frozen generative models can be transformed into strong discriminative classifiers by extracting intermediate representations rather than relying on prompt-based predictions
28
;
22
;
48
;
19
;
21
;
39
.
In particular, Sparse Attention Vectors (SAVs)
28
demonstrate that a small subset of attention heads in a frozen audio-language model can serve as effective feature extractors for few-shot classification.
Their proposed algorithm selects the most discriminative heads and aggregates their predictions through majority voting. SAVs significantly narrow the performance gap between generative models and specialized discriminative models without requiring finetuning on labeled data.

In this paper, we extend this line of work to the audio and audio-visual domains. Compared to vision or text, audio classification presents unique challenges, including the presence of multiple overlapping acoustic events
12
;
36
, higher intra- and inter-class variability, and temporal ambiguity in semantic cues. These characteristics make it especially unlikely that a single uniform aggregation strategy over attention heads is optimal across all classes.
Although this is highly effective, SAVs rely on a key simplifying assumption that we aim to disprove via empirical results: all selected attention heads are treated as
equally reliable
across all classes. By contrast, prior research on interpretability and representation learning shows that attention heads exhibit
functional specialization
, often responding with varying activation levels depending on the type of semantic patterns or sensory stimuli
1
;
24
;
27
;
41
. This observation exposes a limitation of uniform voting-based approaches: they fail to account for class-dependence and relative head-reliabilities
32
. At inference time, uniform aggregation allows weak or noisy heads to influence predictions for classes where they lack in discriminative power.
To address this limitation, we propose
C
lass-Conditional Sparse
A
ttention Vectors for
Large Audio-
L
anguage
M
odels (CALM), a principled extension of SAVs that models attention heads as
class-conditional experts
rather than uniformly reliable voters. CALM estimates class-specific reliability scores for each selected attention head using few-shot data. This enables heads to contribute to predictions in proportion to their estimated confidence. These scores are computed using a margin-based confidence measure and run during inference with a weighted voting scheme.
We note that our CALM operates directly on the frozen LALM with no fine-tuning and introduces minimal computational overhead.
Our contributions are threefold:

1.

We introduce CALM, a probabilistically weighted framework that models attention heads as class-conditional experts.

2.

We present the first application of attention vectors as discriminative classifiers to audio and audio-visual classification despite modality-specific challenges.

3.

We demonstrate consistent improvements over uniform voting-based SAVs on multiple benchmarks and tasks, including ESC-50, VGGSound, AudioSet, and LA-Spoof.

Related Work

Our work is related to large language models and their variants for various downstream tasks, as well as methods that study the internal representations of these models.
In the following, we provide a brief overview of these lines of work.

Generative Models for Audio Classification.

Prior work in audio classification have explored generative model paradigms. Several approaches treat classification as a controllable generation problem, prompting large generative models to directly output class labels via prompt engineering or few-shot demonstrations to guide the model
38
;
16
;
3
;
6
.
Although effective in some settings, these approaches are sensitive to prompting strategies such as CoT or soft-prompting
29
;
34
;
35
and may underperform compared to specialized discriminative classifiers or zero-shot adaptation
8
;
11
.
Another line of work leverages generative likelihoods over candidate labels, using their output probabilities from LLMs as proxies for classification confidence
25
;
20
. Beyond prompting, instruction tuning adapts generative models to classification by fine-tuning on labeled audio or multi-modal datasets
7
;
4
. Preference-based alignment methods such as DPO further optimize model outputs using curated reward
30
. Despite good performance, both approaches require additional supervision and sacrifice the finetuning-free flexibility of frozen foundation models. A complementary direction explores test-time adaptation, where models update parameters during inference time to better fit the task distribution
37
;
46
.

Internal Representation of LALMs.

A complementary line of work repurposes the
internal representations
of frozen language models as features for downstream prediction tasks
2
. This avoids reliance on decoding time prompting. In audio, representation-based classifiers can be formed by comparing audio embeddings to class text embeddings in a shared embedding space
38
. Several recent works argue that task-relevant information can be encoded directly in hidden-states during in-context learning to strengthen downstream classification tasks
19
;
22
.
Interpretability studies show that attention heads exhibit non-uniform functional and semantic specialization, implying that different heads can be informative for different concepts or classes

;
40
;
1
;

.
Building on this idea, Sparse Attention Vectors (SAVs)
28
leverage this structure by selecting a small subset of attention heads from a frozen generative model and using them to serve as effective feature extractors for downstream classification.
However, SAVs uniform aggregation implicitly assumes that selected heads are equally reliable across all classes, despite evidence of class- and concept-dependent specialization
24
;
1
.
Although effective, uniform aggregation allows weak or noisy heads to influence predictions for classes where they lack in discriminative power.
In our work we estimate class-specific reliability scores for each selected attention head using few-shot data. This enables heads to contribute to predictions in proportion to their estimated confidence.
Extensive empirical evidence shows that our probabilistic voting scheme outperforms the naive uniform voting proposed by SAVs on multiple benchmarks.

Methods

Suppose we have a
C
-way classification problem: given an audio waveform or audio-visual clip
x
, we want to predict its class
c
. Further, we assume a small few-shot training dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}=\{(x_{i},c_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
with labels
c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}=\{1,\dots,C\}
and
n_{c}

c

n_{c}

\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}=\{(x_{i},c_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{test}}}
. Using these data, we would like to build a classifier by leveraging the internal representation of a frozen large audio(visual)-language model (LALM). To this end, we first prompt the LALM to generate a response, and extract the attention vector corresponding to the final token of the response for each attention head
j\in{1,\dots,K}
, denoted as
h_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. These vectors are then used as features to our classifier.
Using these features, we attempt to build a finetuning-free classifier by (i) constructing per-class centroids in each head space and (ii) aggregating head level evidence into the final prediction.

2
provides an overview of our method.

Under this framework, we first recap SAVs
28
and its applicability to audio and audio-visual settings (Section
3.1
).
Then, we introduce our main method,
CALM
, which generalizes SAVs by learning
reliability weights
for head aggregation and non-uniform voting (Section
3.2
).
CALM also supports a self-supervised extension via pseudo-labeling (Section
3.3
).

3.1
Recap: Sparse Attention Vectors

We establish SAVs, the baseline approach that motivates our key innovations
28
. The method first builds class prototypes per head and then selects the most discriminative heads.

Per-head Centroids.

For each class
c
and head
j
, SAVs computes a class centroid by averaging head features over the training set.

\mu_{c}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i:c_{i}=c}h_{j}(x_{i}).

(1)

The compatibility score between a query
x
with class
c
in head
j
is computed via cosine-similarity:

s_{j}(x,c)=\frac{h_{j}(x)^{\top}\mu_{c}^{(j)}}{\lVert h_{j}(x)\rVert_{2}\,\lVert\mu_{c}^{(j)}\rVert_{2}}.

(2)

Top-
k
head selection.

Previous literature mentions that not all heads are useful for downstream tasks. SAVs removes these heads by estimating a head’s discriminative quality using nearest-centroid classification on the training set and selecting the top-
k
heads. Concretely, head
j
predicts

\hat{y}^{(j)}(x)=\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\;\;s_{j}(x,c),

(3)

and heads are ranked by accuracy over
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
. The selected set is denoted
\mathcal{H}_{\text{SAV}}
.

Majority Voting (Uniform Aggregation).

At inference time, SAVs aggregates head predictions by uniform voting:

\hat{y}(x_{\text{test}})=\arg\max_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{SAV}}}\mathbf{1}\left[\hat{y}^{(j)}(x_{\text{test}})=c\right].

(4)

SAVs assumes that once a head is "good", it is equally reliable as
all other top-
k
heads across
all classes
, an assumption we relax in CALM.

3.2
CALM

The key insight of CALM is to model attention heads as
class-specific experts
by learning how reliably each head discriminates each class.
Instead of uniform voting (Eq. (
3
)), CALM learns reliability weights and performs a weighted ensemble of votes. This preserves the finetuning-free nature of SAV-style classifiers while enabling fine-grained credit assignment to heads.

Head Posteriors.

Provided we have the per-head class centroids from Eq.
1
and the similarity scores from
Eq.
2
, we convert each head’s score into a probability distribution over classes.

p_{c}^{(j)}(x)=\frac{\exp\left(s_{j}(x,c)/\tau_{p}\right)}{\sum_{c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\exp\left(s_{j}(x,c^{\prime})/\tau_{p}\right)},

(5)

for some hyperparameter
\tau_{p}>0.

3.2.1
CALM-Global: Head Weighting

As an intermediate step, we first formulate the idea that heads are equally performant across classes but the top-
k
heads have different strengths. We first estimate a single reliability score per head by measuring its margin-based confidence. For an example
(x_{i},\phantom{.}c)
, we define the clamped margin as

m^{(j)}(x,c)=\max\Bigl(0,p_{c}^{(j)}(x)-\max_{c^{\prime}\neq c}\;\;p_{c^{\prime}}^{(j)}(x)\Bigr).

(6)

The clamp ensures that incorrect or low-confidence cases contribute zero, so reliability reflects positive evidence rather than penalizing heads for mistakes.

3.2.2
Class-Conditional Head Weighting

Our main algorithm improves over both SAVs and global weighting exploiting the idea that attention heads often specialize.
After calculating the marginals in Eq. (
6
), we compute a class-specific reliability score by averaging the margin when treating
c
as the target class:

r_{c}^{(j)}=\frac{1}{n_{c}}\sum_{i;c_{i}=c}m^{(j)}(x_{i},c).

(11)

Compared to SAVs, which ranks head using a single global accuracy score,
r_{c}^{(j)}
provides a reliability estimate based on confidence per class. This lets each class identify the heads that most consistently separates it from its closest confounders.

Similar to SAVs, we select the top-
k
heads. However, we do this
per class
and take the softmax to get the final weight distribution across all heads and classes. Let the top-
k
heads for class
c
be
\mathcal{H}^{c}_{\text{CALM}}
,

w_{c}^{(j)}=\begin{cases}\frac{\exp(r_{c}^{(j)}/\tau_{w})}{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{c}_{\text{CALM}}}\exp(r_{c}^{(j^{\prime})}/\tau_{w})},&j\in\mathcal{H}^{c}_{\text{CALM}},\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(12)

for some hyperparameter
\tau_{w}>0.
This generalizes SAVs single shared top-
k

p_{c}(x_{\text{test}})=\sum_{j=1}^{K}w_{c}^{(j)}p_{c}^{(j)}(x_{\text{test}}),

(13)

3.3
Self-supervised CALM

To enable our approach in settings without ground-truth labels, we generate pseudo-labels from the frozen LALM using a simple self-consistency filter.
Given an unlabeled dataset

\mathcal{D}_{\text{unlabeled}}=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
we obtain
M
stochastic predictions

\{\hat{c}_{i}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{M}

by decoding with temperatures

\tau\sim\mathcal{U}(1,2.5)
.
We assign a pseudo-label by majority vote, and retain only examples where at least
50\%
of rollouts agree, which filters out noise and low-confidence samples for stronger centroids. The resulting pseudo-labeled set
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\text{train}}={(x_{i},\tilde{c}_{i})}
is then used in replacement for
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
to run SAV or CALM on.

4
Experiments and Results

Here, we evaluate our proposed method on a range of audio and audio-visual classification benchmarks.
First, we describe the implementation details, models used, datasets, and evaluation protocols. We then discuss our hyper-parameter selection and analyze the impact of head sparsity and class-specific weighting through ablations.

4.1
Implementation Details

All experiments are implemented in PyTorch and run on NVIDIA A6000 GPUs. We use frozen pretrained models from the Qwen family
9
;
44
.

Qwen-2-Audio

9
is an audio-language model in which an audio encoder feeds into the language model. The language model consists of 32 Transformer layers with 32 attention heads per layer (1024 heads total), each with a hidden dimension of 128. We extract attention head activations from the language model layers rather than the audio encoder to capture cross-modal audio–text interactions.

Qwen-2.5-Omni

44
is a multi-modal model supporting audio, image, video, and speech inputs. As with Qwen-2-Audio, we extract attention activations exclusively from the language tower.
To find the best values of
\tau_{p}
and
\tau_{w}
, we ran a hyperparameter sweep across all
\tau_{p},\tau_{w}\in[0.001,0.03,0.1,1,2]
. We use the following hyperparameters:
\tau_{p}=0.03,\tau_{w}=0.5
. For AudioSet, we use
\tau_{p}=0.001,\tau_{w}=0.5
.
For ESC, VGG Sound, and Audioset, an example prompt is:
What caption does the given audio belong to? A. washing_machine B. toilet_flush C. car_horn D. wind
, while for spoofing detection, an example prompt is:
Is this audio bonafide or spoofed? A. bonafide B. spoofed
.

4.2
Datasets

We primarily evaluate our approach on audio classification benchmarks, and further extend it to audio–visual recognition and audio spoofing detection. Across all datasets, we follow the input formatting protocol of SAV
28
, casting each task into a multiple-choice question-answering formulation suitable for LALMs.

ESC-50

31
is a standard environmental sound classification benchmark consisting of 2,000 five-second audio clips across 50 classes. We sample class-balanced subsets according to the desired shot setting and frame the task as multiple-choice audio classification.

VGGSound

5
is a large-scale audio–visual dataset containing over 200k videos spanning 309 sound event classes. Compared to ESC-50, it exhibits substantially greater semantic and acoustic diversity. We adopt the same multiple-choice formulation and use both audio and video modalities for audio–visual experiments.

AudioSet

15
is a large-scale ontology-based audio–visual dataset with 527 non-mutually exclusive sound event classes. Due to its multi-label nature, we decompose each audio clip into independent classification queries, one per ground-truth label, and report strict
exact-match
accuracy at the clip level.

ASVspoof

42
is a dataset used for spoofing detection.
To evaluate robustness beyond natural sound recognition, we study
spoofing detection
using the LA (Logical Access) subset of ASVspoof 2019. This task involves distinguishing bona-fide speech from synthetic audio generated via speech synthesis and voice conversion methods. Given the class imbalance, we report Macro-F1 as the primary evaluation metric
47
.

4.3
Results

4.3.1
Main Results

Our primary results are shown in Table
1
. We report classification performance across a variety of audio classification benchmarks using frozen LALMs, SAVs, and CALM. We notice that CALM almost always outperforms uniform voting-based SAVs.
On ESC-50, both SAVs and CALM achieve near-saturated accuracy, with Qwen2.5 outperforming SAV implementation by 0.25%.
On VGGSound, CALM improves over SAVs by +2.5% in Qwen2-Audio and +0.7% in Qwen2.5-Omni.
The largest gains appear on Audioset, where CALM outperform SAVs by up to +14.5% in Qwen2-Audio.
This large gain highlights the advantage of CALM in tackling challenging classification tasks such as Audioset, which contains 527 classes. With large variability, global head selection is brittle to unspecialized heads, while class-conditioned experts are much more robust.
In audio-visual classification with Qwen2.5-Omni, we notice a +1.5% increase from SAVs and another +20.31% increase from zero-shot inference.
On spoofing detection, CALM achieve the best Macro-F1 score on Qwen2.5-Omni but drops in performance with the audio-only model. A likely cause is high-variance in the centroids and overfitting the reliability weights to the two classes. These generally positive gains highlight the benefit of class-specific head specialization in large-scale question-answering settings.
Similarly, we observe improvements in performance by using CALM in the self-supervised setting with our pseudo-labeling approach from Section
3.3
. This robustness is driven by quality-controlled pseudo-labels. The majority-agreement filtering removes low-confidence examples, leading to more accurate class centroids and more stable head-reliability estimates.

4.3.2
Class-Specific Head Specialization

For a better understanding of the contribution of different attention heads, Figure
3
visualizes per-class weight survival functions for representative VGGSound classes. Under uniform SAV aggregation, the distribution corresponds to one where all selected heads are assigned identical weight
\frac{1}{k}
, as shown by the dashed lines. This behavior reflects the fact that uniform aggregation enforces equal contribution.
In contrast, CALM learns class-conditional, non-uniform weights. The resulting survival function exhibits two behaviors. First, there is a strong class-specific specialization as the survival function curves are different for all the classes. Second, some of the distributions with the heavier tails indicate that a small subset of heads receive substantially larger weights while some other contribute minimally.
Additionally, we report the index of the highest-weighted attention head for each class,

\arg\max_{j}(w_{c}^{(j)})
, to analyze where the most influential heads are typically located. We notice that peaks tend to cluster in later layers (16-28) (Figure
6
), suggesting that strong knowledge of audio classification is concentrated in these layers. There are also some specific heads that are the strongest across many classes indicating the presence of strong, general heads alongside class-specific experts.
Altogether, these observations suggest LALMs contain certain heads that work as class-specific experts while others behave more like generalized feature discriminators. This supports the design choice of class-conditional weighting with sparsity.

4.3.3
Ablations

We conduct several ablations to enforce the algorithmic design choices in CALM.

Local vs. Global Weighting.

First, we compare our class-conditional, locally weighted CALM against our previously discussed globally weighted variant in which each head is assigned a single reliability score shared across all classes. Table
2
and Figure
4
show globally weighting each head results in under-performance in VGGSound (-1.13%) while improving performance in LA-Spoof dataset (+7.97%).
The higher performance with global weighting in spoofing detection points to overfitting with individual weights for spoofed and bona-fide samples. For this binary classification task, our ablation shows that a small set of consistently reliable heads generalizes well across both classes.

Design Choices.

Table
3
shows that both cosine similarity and margin-based reliability contribute to CALM’s performance on VGGSound. Removing cosine similarity and switching with unnormalized dot product consistently reduced accuracy. This indicates that scale variance in attention heads across shots introduce noise into centroids. Additionally, eliminating margin-based reliability led to drop in performance as well, showing weighting heads by its discriminability is important. Together, these ablations demonstrate all our design components are necessary for CALM’s effectiveness.

5
Conclusion

We have presented
CALM
, a finetuning-free approach for few-shot audio and audiovisual classification.
CALM treats attention heads in frozen LALMs as
class-conditional experts
.
CALM better aligns with previous findings of strong attention-head semantic, functional specialization and enables fine-grained credit assignment across classes.
Empirically, CALM outperforms uniform voting-based sparse attention vectors across many benchmarks.
Our ablations validate our algorithmic decisions such as vector normalization and margin-based weighting. We further demonstrate that CALM operates well in self-supervised settings via pseudo-labeling and confidence-based filtering.
Overall our extensive empirical analysis shows that attention heads are strong class-specific discriminative classifiers.
This allows for a powerful mechanism to convert LALMs into competitive discriminative audio classifiers without retraining.

Limitations

CALM represents a new method of finding expert heads for discriminative classifiers. This class-conditional head reliability can lead to overfitting in extremely low-shot and noisy datasets. Additionally, CALM introduces temperature hyperparameters
(\tau_{p},\tau_{w})
whose optimal values are dataset-dependent. This requires empirical sweeps for best performance. Moreover, the method may suffer from hallucinations, producing misleading predictions, particularly for complex audio containing multiple overlapping acoustic events. Developing effective mitigation strategies for such hallucinations remains an open challenge. Finally, our evaluation primarily focuses on audio and limited audiovisual benchmarks, which we hope to focus on, in future work.
