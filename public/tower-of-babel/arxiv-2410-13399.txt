Heisenberg scaling based on population coding
Masahito Hayashi

Abstract
We study Heisenberg scaling of quantum metrology in the viewpoint of population coding. Although Fisher information has been used for a figure of merit to characterize Heisenberg scaling in quantum metrology, several studies pointed out it does not work as a figure of merit because it does not reflect the global structure. As an alternative figure of merit, we propose the mutual information, which connects the number of distinguishable elements of the parameter space in the viewpoint of population coding. We show that several unitary models achieve Heisenberg scaling in this context.



keywords
Heisenberg scaling; quantum metrology; group symmetry; mutual information; Schur duality

2
State estimation

To see the standard scaling for the mutual information,
we consider state estimation, i.e.,
we focus on a
d
-parameter state family

\{\rho_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}

on the Hilbert space
{\cal H}_{A}
, where

\Theta
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We consider a Bayesian prior
\mu
on
\Theta
.
We denote the set of density matrices on
{\cal H}
by

{\cal S}({\cal H})
.
We denote the classical system
\Theta
by
B
.
We consider
n
-copy state
\rho_{\theta}^{\otimes n}
.
We have classical-quantum state

\displaystyle\rho_{AB}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{\Theta}\rho_{\theta}^{\otimes n}\otimes|\theta\rangle\langle\theta|\mu(d\theta).

(1)

We focus on the mutual information between
A
and
B
, which is given as

\displaystyle I(A;B)

\displaystyle=S(\rho_{A})-\int_{\Theta}S(\rho_{\theta}^{\otimes n})\mu(d\theta),

\displaystyle=\int_{\Theta}D(\rho_{\theta}^{\otimes n}\|\rho_{A})\mu(d\theta),

(2)

where
S(\rho)
expresses the von Neumann entropy

-\mathrm{Tr}\rho\log\rho
of
\rho
,
and
D(\rho\|\sigma)
expresses the quantum relative entropy

\mathrm{Tr}\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)
.

When all densities are commutative, i.e., the model is classical, the references

[
31
,
32
]
showed that

\displaystyle I(A;B)=\frac{d}{2}\log n+O(1).

(3)

When our model
\{\rho_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}

is the full model on
t
-dimensional system
and
\mu
is invariant for unitary action,
the reference
[
33
]
showed that

\displaystyle I(A;B)=\frac{t^{2}-1}{2}\log n+O(1).

(4)

Since the number of parameters of the full model is
t^{2}-1
,
(
4
) can be considered as a generalization of
(
3
).
When a model satisfies a certain condition,
using the result by
[
36
]
,
we can show

\displaystyle I(A;B)=\frac{d}{2}\log n+o(\log n).

(5)

That is, the leading term is
the number of parameters times

\frac{1}{2}\log n
.

We back to the spirit of population coding, and focus on the number

N(\{\rho^{\otimes n}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta},\epsilon)

of distinguishable states among
\{\rho^{\otimes n}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}

with the average decoding error probability
\epsilon>0
.
As shown in
[
44
, (4.32)]
, using Fano inequality, we can
evaluate this number as

\displaystyle\log N(\{\rho^{\otimes n}_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta},\epsilon)\leq\frac{\log 2+I(A;B)}{1-\epsilon}.

(6)

That is, the relations (
3
), (
4
), and (
5
)
give upper bounds of this number.

In estimation theory, it is also important to clarify
the distance between the estimate
\theta_{guess}

and the true value
\theta_{true}

when we exclude a certain error probability
\epsilon>0
;

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{R}\{R|{\rm Pr}(\|\theta_{guess}-\theta_{true}\|>R)\leq\epsilon\},

(7)

where
\|x\|\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{j}|x_{j}|
.
In the state estimation,
the normalized difference

\sqrt{n}(\theta_{guess}-\theta_{true})
.
asymptotically obeys a Gaussian distribution,
which is called local asymptotic normality
[
36
]
.
This means that the above value
R_{\epsilon}
scales
O(n^{-1/2})
and its coefficient is asymptotically determined by the covariance of

\sqrt{n}(\theta_{guess}-\theta_{true})
.
This relation is the reason why
we consider the minimum covariance
in the parameter estimation in classical statistics.
Since this value scales as the square of variance,
we can consider that
the value
R_{\epsilon}
scales
O(n^{-1})
in the Heisenberg scaling.
That is, when we focus on
R_{\epsilon}
,
the Heisenberg scaling can be considered as
the case when the value
R_{\epsilon}
scales
O(n^{-1})
.
However, under the Heisenberg scaling,
the variance
n(\theta_{guess}-\theta_{true})

does not asymptotically obey a Gaussian distribution.
That is, its asymptotic distribution is not fixed.
Therefore,
the coefficient of
R_{\epsilon}
in the scale
O(n^{-1})

cannot be asymptotically determined by the covariance of

{n}(\theta_{guess}-\theta_{true})
.
The minimization of the variance does not have the same important role under the Heisenberg scaling as under the usual scaling.

In the following, for simplicity, we consider the one-parameter case,
and assume that the parameter set is given as an interval
[a,b]
.
In this case,

\lfloor(b-a)/(2R_{\epsilon})\rfloor
elements of
[a,b]

can be distinguished within the error probability
\epsilon
.
This relation between
R_{\epsilon}
and the number of distinguished elements can be extended to the higher dimensional case.
That is, the number of distinguished elements
is meaningful in the parameter estimation
even under the Heisenberg scaling.

3
General problem formulation

3.1
General description

Let
G
be a compact group and
\mu
be the Haar measure of
G
.
We focus on a unitary representation
f
of
G
over a
finite-dimensional Hilbert space.
For this aim, we choose an input state.
We assume that any entangled input state with
any reference system is available.
To consider this problem, we denote the set of the labels of
irreducible representations of
G
by
\hat{G}
.
Let
{\cal U}_{\lambda}
be the irreducible representation space
identified by
\lambda\in\hat{G}
, and

d_{\lambda}
be its dimension.

We also define the twirling operation for the group
G
as

\displaystyle{\cal T}_{G}(\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{G}f(g^{\prime})\rho f(g^{\prime})^{\dagger}\mu(dg^{\prime}).

(8)

We denote the set of irreducible representations appearing in
f

by
\hat{G}_{f}
.
We decompose the representation system
{\cal H}_{A}
as follows.

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{A}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}{\cal U}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n_{\lambda}},

(9)

where

n_{\lambda}
expresses the multiplicity of the representation
space
{\cal U}_{\lambda}
.

When a reference system
\mathbb{C}^{l}
is available, we have

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{Al}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\cal H}_{A}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{l}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}{\cal U}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{ln_{\lambda}}.

(10)

However, when the input state is a pure state,
the orbit is restricted to the following space by choosing
a suitable subspace
\mathbb{C}^{\min(d_{\lambda},ln_{\lambda})}

of
\mathbb{C}^{ln_{\lambda}}
.
That is, our representation space can be considered as follows.

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}{\cal U}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{\min(d_{\lambda},ln_{\lambda})}.

(11)

When
l\geq d_{\lambda}/n_{\lambda}
for any
\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}
,
our representation is given as

\displaystyle{\cal H}_{AR}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}{\cal U}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{\lambda}}.

(12)

In the following, we consider the above case.
We denote the projection to
{\cal U}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{\lambda}}

by
P_{\lambda}
.

3.2
Mutual information

We choose an input state

\rho

and a distribution
P
on the system

{\cal B}=G
, where
the random choice of
{\cal B}
is denoted by the random variable
B
.
So, we have a classical-quantum state

\sum_{g\in{\cal X}}P(g)f(g)\rho f(g)^{\dagger}\otimes|g\rangle\langle g|
.
We focus on the mutual information
I(Al;B)
or
(AR;B)
, which depends on
\rho
and
P
.
When
\rho
is decomposed as
\sum_{j}q_{j}\rho_{j}
,
the mutual information is evaluated as

\displaystyle I(Al;B)[P,\rho]

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=

\displaystyle S\Big(\sum_{g^{\prime}\in{\cal X}}P(g^{\prime})f(g^{\prime})\rho f(g^{\prime})^{\dagger}\Big)

\displaystyle-\sum_{g\in{\cal X}}P(g)S\Big(f(g)\rho f(g)^{\dagger}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in{\cal X}}P(g)

\displaystyle\cdot D\Big(f(g)\rho f(g)^{\dagger}\Big\|\sum_{g^{\prime}\in{\cal X}}P(g^{\prime})f(g^{\prime})\rho f(g^{\prime})^{\dagger}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j}q_{j}\sum_{g\in{\cal X}}P(g)

\displaystyle\cdot D\Big(f(g)\rho_{j}f(g)^{\dagger}\Big\|\sum_{g^{\prime}\in{\cal X}}P(g^{\prime})f(g^{\prime})\rho_{j}f(g^{\prime})^{\dagger}\Big).

(13)

The final inequality follows from the joint convexity of relative entropy.
Since any mixed state can be written as a mixture of pure states,
to maximize the mutual information, we can restrict the input state as an input pure state

|\psi\rangle=\oplus_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}\sqrt{p_{\lambda}}|\psi_{\lambda}\rangle
.
In this case,
since the von Neumann entropy of a pure state is zero,
the mutual information is given as the von Neumann entropy of the mixture of the final states.

\displaystyle I(Al;B)[P,|\psi\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S\Big(\sum_{g^{\prime}\in{\cal X}}P(g^{\prime})f(g^{\prime})|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|f(g^{\prime})^{\dagger}\Big).

(14)

We consider the following optimization problem.

\displaystyle I(G)_{Al}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{|\psi\rangle}\max_{P}I(Al;B)[P,|\psi\rangle].

(15)

As shown in
[
45
]
,
due to the convexity of von Neumann entropy
the maximum is attained by the Haar measure
\mu
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\max_{P}I(Al;B)[P,|\psi\rangle]=

\displaystyle I(Al;B)[\mu,|\psi\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S({\cal T}_{G}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)),

(16)

which implies

\displaystyle I(G)_{Al}=\max_{|\psi\rangle}S({\cal T}_{G}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)).

(17)

Here,
we set
|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle
to be a pure state

{\cal U}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{\min(d_{\lambda},ln_{\lambda})}
such that

\mathrm{Tr}_{{\cal U}_{\lambda}}|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle\langle\phi_{\lambda}|

is the completely mixed state on

\mathbb{C}^{\min(d_{\lambda},ln_{\lambda})}
.
Such a state
|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle
is called
the maximally entangled state on

{\cal U}_{\lambda}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{\min(d_{\lambda},ln_{\lambda})}
.

When
|\psi\rangle
is the state

|\phi(p)\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}\sqrt{p_{\lambda}}|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle
, where

p
is a distribution on
\hat{G}_{f}
,
we have

\displaystyle S({\cal T}_{G}(|\phi(p)\rangle\langle\phi(p)|))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S\Big(\sum_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}p_{\lambda}\frac{P_{\lambda}}{d_{\lambda}\min(n_{\lambda},d_{\lambda})}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S(p)+\sum_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}p_{\lambda}\log(d_{\lambda}\min(n_{\lambda},d_{\lambda})).

(18)

Since the dimension of support of

{\cal T}_{G}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)
is upper bounded by

\sum_{\lambda\in\hat{G}}d_{\lambda}\min(n_{\lambda},d_{\lambda})
,
we have

\displaystyle I(X;Al)[\mu,|\psi\rangle]=S({\cal T}_{G}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle R(G)_{Al}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\log\Big(\sum_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}d_{\lambda}\min(ln_{\lambda},d_{\lambda})\Big).

(19)

The equality holds
when the input state is

|\phi(p)\rangle
and

p_{\lambda}=\frac{d_{\lambda}\min(ln_{\lambda},d_{\lambda})}{\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\hat{G}_{f}}d_{\lambda^{\prime}}\min(ln_{\lambda^{\prime}},d_{\lambda^{\prime}})}
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle I(G)_{Al}=\log\Big(\sum_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}d_{\lambda}\min(ln_{\lambda},d_{\lambda})\Big).

(20)

Overall, the optimal input state is

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}\sqrt{\frac{d_{\lambda}\min(ln_{\lambda},d_{\lambda})}{\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\hat{G}_{f}}d_{\lambda^{\prime}}\min(ln_{\lambda^{\prime}},d_{\lambda^{\prime}})}}|\phi_{\lambda}\rangle.

(21)

In particular, when
l
satisfies the condition
l\geq d_{\lambda}/n_{\lambda}
for any
\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}
,

\displaystyle I(G)_{AR}=I(G)_{Al}=R(G)_{AR}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\log\Big(\sum_{\lambda\in\hat{G}_{f}}d_{\lambda}^{2}\Big).

(22)

3.3
Number of distinguishable elements

Next, we consider how many elements in
G
can be distinguished
via the above process.
When the input state is
\rho
, we denote the number of distinguishable states among
\{f(g)\rho f(g)^{\dagger}\}_{g\in G}

with the average decoding error
\epsilon>0
by
M_{Al}(\rho,\epsilon)
.
That is,

\displaystyle M_{Al}(\rho,\epsilon)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=

\displaystyle\max\left\{M\middle|\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathrm{Tr}f(g_{j})\rho f(g_{j})^{\dagger}\Pi_{j}\\
\geq 1-\epsilon\end{array}\right\},

where
the maximum in (
3.3
) is taken with respect to

{M,g_{1},\ldots,g_{M}\in G,\{\Pi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}}
.
We consider the following optimization problems.

\displaystyle M_{\epsilon}(G)_{Al}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{\rho}M_{Al}(\rho,\epsilon).

(25)

The number of distinguishable elements
is related to the error radius of the estimation.
We consider a distance
D

distance on
G
satisfying

D(g_{1},g_{2})=D(gg_{1},gg_{2})=D(g_{1}g,g_{2}g)
for
g,g_{1},g_{2}\in G
.
As the unitary version of (
7
),
we focus on the following value.

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{R}\{R|{\rm Pr}(D(g_{guess},g_{true})>R)\leq\epsilon\}.

(26)

To evaluate the above value, we introduce

\displaystyle B(R)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu_{H}(\{g|D(g,g_{0})\leq R\}).

(27)

Then, we have

\displaystyle M_{\epsilon}(G)_{Al}B(R_{\epsilon})\leq 1

(28)

That is, using
M_{\epsilon}(G)_{Al}
, we have the following upper bound for
R_{\epsilon}
;

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon}\leq B^{-1}(M_{\epsilon}(G)_{Al}^{-1}).

(29)

In addition,
when
\{g_{j}\}
are equally spaced, i.e.,
the subset
\{g_{j}\}
forms a discrete subgroup of
G
,
the maximum value (
3.3
) is achieved with a certain
\epsilon>0
.
Given a discrete subgroup
\Omega\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{g_{j}\}\subset G
,
we define

B(\Omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu_{H}(\{g\in G|\min_{g}D(g^{\prime},g)=D(e,g)\})
.
When
|\Omega|=M_{\epsilon}(G)_{Al}
,
we have
an upper bound of
R_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle R_{\epsilon}\leq R(\Omega)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=

\displaystyle\min_{g\in G}\{D(e,g)|\exists g^{\prime}\in\Omega,D(e,g)=D(g^{\prime},g)\}.

(30)

However,
it is not so easy to find a relation between
a variance-type of error and
R_{\epsilon}
.
Indeed, in the case of state estimation,
the limiting distribution of the variable

\sqrt{n}(\theta_{guess}-\theta_{true})

asymptotically obeys the Gaussian distribution,
the asymptotic variance derives the asymptotic behavior of
R_{\epsilon}
.
However, in the unitary estimation,
the asymptotic normality does not hold
[
46
]
.
Hence, we have no useful relation between
a variance-type of error and
R_{\epsilon}
.

To maximize the mutual information, we can restrict the input state as an input pure state
|\psi\rangle

because a mixed input state can be simulated as a randomized choice of input pure states.
In this case,
M_{Al}(|\psi\rangle,\epsilon)
can be evaluated by using
the Rényi entropy

S_{\alpha}\Big(\sum_{g^{\prime}\in{\cal X}}P(g^{\prime})f(g^{\prime})|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|f(g^{\prime})^{\dagger}\Big)

of the mixture of the final states, where

S_{\alpha}(\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathrm{Tr}\rho^{\alpha}
with
\alpha\neq 1
.
Using the results
[
47
, (6)]
and
[
44
, (4.67)]
, we have

\displaystyle\log M(|\psi\rangle,\epsilon)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\max_{P}S_{\alpha}\Big(\sum_{g^{\prime}\in{\cal X}}P(g^{\prime})f(g^{\prime})|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|f(g^{\prime})^{\dagger}\Big)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\alpha-1}(\log 2-\log\epsilon)

(31)

\displaystyle\log M(|\psi\rangle,\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{P}S_{\beta}\Big(\sum_{g^{\prime}\in{\cal X}}P(g^{\prime})f(g^{\prime})|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|f(g^{\prime})^{\dagger}\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\beta-1}\log(1-\epsilon)

(32)

for
0<\beta<1
and
1<\alpha\leq 2
.
For its detailed derivation, see Appendix
A
.
The above maximum is attained when
P
is the Haar measure
\mu
.
Since the dimension of support of

{\cal T}_{G}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)
is upper bounded by

\sum_{\lambda\in\hat{G}}d_{\lambda}\min(n_{\lambda},d_{\lambda})
,
we have

\displaystyle S_{\alpha}({\cal T}_{G}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|))\leq R(G)_{Al}.

(33)

The equality holds
when the input state is
|\phi\rangle
.
Thus, choosing the best choice
\alpha=2
and taking the limit

\beta\to+0
, we have

\displaystyle R(G)_{Al}-(\log 2-\log\epsilon)\leq\log M_{\epsilon}(G)_{Al}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle R(G)_{Al}-\log(1-\epsilon).

(34)

In particular, we have

\displaystyle R(G)_{AR}-(\log 2-\log\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\log M_{\epsilon}(G)_{AR}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\log R(G)_{AR}-\log(1-\epsilon).

(35)

Therefore, when
1>\epsilon>0
is fixed,

\log R(G)_{AR}
is the dominant term.

Since we have (

\displaystyle\log\Big(\sum_{\lambda^{\prime}\in Y_{t}^{n}}d_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{2}\Big)\geq(t^{2}-1)\log n+O(1).

We choose a positive real number

such that
\frac{n}{a_{n}t}
is an integer.
We define the subset

as

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{n}(a_{n})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=

\displaystyle\Big\{\lambda\Big|\frac{n}{a_{n}t}\leq\lambda_{k+1}-\lambda_{k}\hbox{ for }k=1,\ldots,t-1\Big\}.

\displaystyle d_{\lambda}=

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq t}\frac{k-j+\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j}}{k-j}

\displaystyle\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq t}\frac{n}{a_{n}t^{2}}=\big(\frac{n}{a_{n}t^{2}}\big)^{\frac{(t-1)t}{2}}.

7
Conclusion

We have derived general formulas for
the mutual information
and the logarithm of the number of distinguishable elements
when a unitary group representation is given.
We have proposed the above quantities
as a figure of merit to address the population coding with group representation
because these quantities reflect the global information structure unlike Fisher information.
Then, we have applied these general formulas
to the case with multi-phase estimation and multiple applications of
\mathop{\rm SU}\,(t)
.
As the results, we have revealed that
the optimal strategy realized the twice value of the
standard case for these two quantities, which can be considered as Heisenberg scaling.
We have also shown that
the optimal strategies for maximizing Fisher information
have much smaller values for these quantities.
This fact shows the advantage of our figure of merit over Fisher information.
