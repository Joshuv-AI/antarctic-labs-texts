Mosquito diversity and orthoflavivirus detection in Khao Yai National Park, Thailand
Authors: Chalida Sri-in, Itsarapong Voraphab, Pairwa Teangchatapun, Jidapa Boonum, Noppawan Chamnansilp, Muchima Smittakorn, Suchansa Thanee, Wittawat Wechtaisong, Aung Aung, Sonthaya Tiawsirisup
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKhao Yai National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site in Thailand, hosts diverse ecosystems that sustain a wide variety of mosquito species. This study was designed to characterize mosquito diversity across an altitudinal gradient in Khao Yai National Park and detect orthoflaviviruses in mosquitoes. Mosquitoes were collected monthly over two years (2023–2024) from eight locations ranging from 479 to 1290 m above sea level. A total of 1041 mosquitoes, representing 36 species from five genera, were identified through morphological and molecular analyses. Culex was among the most diverse mosquito genera in the national park, accounting for 16 out of 36 identified species. Orthoflavivirus detection using polymerase chain reaction and sequencing analysis revealed the presence of Japanese encephalitis virus and Tembusu virus in Culex tritaeniorhynchus and dengue virus in Aedes aegypti. Species richness, Shannon diversity index, and abundance patterns suggested reduced mosquito assemblage complexity at higher elevations, although this relationship was descriptive and not strictly monotonic. This pattern is consistent with previous observations suggesting that altitude may influence mosquito distribution. In addition, variations in temperature, humidity, and rainfall were observed alongside changes in mosquito abundance, suggesting that these environmental factors may contribute to shaping mosquito population dynamics. This study provides baseline data that may support ongoing surveillance and future risk assessment of mosquito-borne diseases in Khao Yai National Park and similar ecosystems.Keywords: Altitude, Climate, Khao Yai National Park, Mosquito, Orthoflavivirus, Vector-borne disease

Introduction

Khao Yai National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site in Thailand, encompasses a mountainous area of ∼2168 km2, spanning four provinces: Saraburi, Nakhon Ratchasima, Prachinburi, and Nakhon Nayok. More than 80% of Khao Yai National Park is covered by evergreen and semievergreen forests, much of which consists of tall, high-quality primary forests (IUCN, 2005, 2025). The park represents the only mountainous section in the region, with elevations of 100–1351 m above sea level (masl). Its primary rainforest, comprising dry evergreen forest (200–400 masl), mixed deciduous forest (400–600 masl), tropical rainforest (600–1000 masl), montane rainforest (> 1000 masl), and secondary forest, constitutes the majority of the park’s forested area (IUCN, 2005, 2025). The annual monsoon, which brings strong rainfall during the season, significantly impacts the park. The average annual temperature is 21 °C, and the average relative humidity is 66%. Due to its vast forested area and complex ecological diversity, Khao Yai National Park serves as an important watershed for five major rivers in Thailand and provides a habitat for many globally endangered species. Among its rich biodiversity, mosquitoes represent one of the most abundant insects.

Mosquitoes can reproduce rapidly due to their short life cycles, especially in tropical regions such as Thailand. They are among the primary vectors of arboviruses in the country and pose significant public health risks when environmental conditions are favorable. The blood-sucking behavior of the female mosquito spreads infections to both humans and animals. In Thailand, the genera Aedes, Culex, and Anopheles are among the principal mosquito genera involved in the transmission of mosquito-borne pathogens (Wiwatanaratanabutr, 2013; Harbach and Wilkerson, 2023; Firmansyah et al., 2025). Both Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus are commonly found in Thailand and serve as important vectors of pathogens worldwide. Their geographical range is expected to expand even further (Kraemer et al., 2015). Several major mosquito-borne arboviral diseases are caused by viruses of the genus Orthoflavivirus (family Flaviviridae), including dengue virus (DENV), West Nile virus (WNV), yellow fever virus, Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), Zika virus (ZIKV), and Tembusu virus (TMUV) (Postler et al., 2023). Symptoms of orthoflavivirus infections range from asymptomatic cases to severe and sometimes fatal diseases, including hemorrhagic fever, shock syndrome, encephalitis, paralysis, congenital defects, and hepatic failure. The threat of emerging orthoflaviviruses continues to be a significant public health concern (Pierson and Diamond, 2020).

According to data from the Department of Disease Control (DDC) Weekly Disease Forecast and Disease Surveillance of Thailand (Report 506), in 2022, DENV infection had the highest morbidity rate among mosquito-borne diseases, with 45,145 reported cases and an incidence rate of 66.97 per 100,000 population (DDC, 2023). Moreover, there was a 4.5-fold increase in reported cases in 2022 compared with 2021, with a higher patient number than the median for 5 years prior (DDC, 2023). Despite this substantial national burden, up-to-date information on the diversity of mosquitoes and their roles as vectors, particularly in high-traffic ecological settings such as national parks, remains limited. This gap constrains effective surveillance and management of mosquito-borne orthoflaviviruses. Khao Yai National Park recorded the highest number of visitors among Thailand’s national parks, with up to 1,428,765 visitors from October 2021 to September 2022 (DNP, 2022). However, data specifically addressing the diversity and potential roles of mosquitoes as disease vectors in this park remain scarce, highlighting a critical gap in site-specific risk assessment.

Climate change has been suggested to influence mosquito distribution, including potential shifts to higher altitudes (Colón-González et al., 2021). This underscores the need for research on mosquito diversity across elevational gradients and associated climatic variables. This study aims to explore and assess the diversity and significance of mosquito populations in Khao Yai National Park as potential orthoflavivirus vectors in correlation with elevation gradients and climatic factors. Mosquitoes were collected monthly over 24 months (2023–2024) from multiple sites within the park and identified at the species level using both morphological and molecular analyses. The presence of viruses in the mosquitoes was examined to assess the potential role of mosquitoes as orthoflavivirus vectors. Associations among mosquito abundance, elevation, and climatic factors were explored descriptively. Our results provide baseline evidence for future risk assessments because climate-driven shifts in temperature, humidity, and rainfall may change mosquito population dynamics and increase the suitability of higher-altitude areas for the emergence of vector-borne disease.

Materials and methods

Mosquito collection

Mosquitoes were collected monthly from January 2023 to December 2024 at eight sites across the Khao Yai National Park, Thailand, representing a range of elevations (Table 1, Fig. 1). Sampling sites included forested and semi-disturbed environments associated with tourist activity and wildlife presence, providing heterogeneous ecological settings for mosquito surveillance. This study was conducted in collaboration with staff from the Khao Yai National Park, who performed mosquito collections and transported specimens to the Parasitology Unit, Department of Veterinary Pathology, Faculty of Veterinary Science, Chulalongkorn University. Adult mosquitoes were collected during both daytime and nighttime. This study focused exclusively on adult mosquito collection; no larval or pupal sampling was performed, as the objective was to assess adult mosquito diversity and detect orthoflaviviruses in field-collected specimens. Daytime collections were conducted using dip nets targeting mosquitoes resting in vegetation, shaded areas, and natural resting habitats. Nighttime collections targeted host-seeking mosquitoes using CDC Miniature Light Traps operated from late afternoon through the following morning (approximately 15:00 to 09:00 h). These complementary collection methods were used to capture both diurnal and nocturnal mosquito species and to maximize species representation. Collected specimens were immediately transferred into 50-ml screw-cap tubes and stored using a triple-containment system consisting of sealed tubes, ziplock bags, and rigid outer containers. Samples were maintained on dry ice during transport to the laboratory to preserve nucleic acid integrity for downstream molecular analyses.

Mosquito identification

Morphological identification

All specimens were examined under a stereomicroscope for genus- and/or species-level identification and sex determination using illustrated morphological keys (Rattanarithikul et al., 2005). Only adult mosquitoes were included in this study; no immature stages were collected or analyzed.

Molecular identification

To confirm species identity, representative samples were subjected to molecular analysis. Total RNA was extracted (see Section 2.4), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized and used as a template for PCR amplification of a 710-bp fragment of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (cox1) gene using primers described by Folmer et al. (1994). PCR reactions were performed in a total volume of 25 μl containing 12.5 μl of KOD One PCR Master Mix Blue (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan), 0.75 μl of each primer (10 μM), 2 μl of cDNA template (or distilled water as a negative control), and 9 μl of distilled water. Thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 52 °C for 30 s, and 68 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 68 °C for 7 min (Li et al., 2020). Amplified products of the expected size were excised from agarose gels and purified using a GenePHlow Gel/PCR Cleanup Kit (Geneaid Biotech Ltd., New Taipei City, Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Sample processing and pooling

To facilitate downstream analyses, adult mosquitoes were pooled (1–10 individuals per pool) following the approach described by Sri-in et al. (2024). Pools were organized based on species, sex, collection site, and collection date. Pooling was performed after morphological identification to ensure taxonomic consistency within each pool.

RNA extraction and complementary DNA synthesis

Mosquito pools were homogenized in 0.1 ml of TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using a rotor-stator homogenizer at room temperature. Homogenates were centrifuged at 3000× g and 4 °C for 5 min to obtain a 10% suspension.

Total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol and eluted in UltraPure DNase/RNase-Free Distilled Water (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using Maxime RT PreMix (Random Primer) (iNtRON Biotechnology, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea) with 250 ng of total RNA in a final volume of 20 μl. The reaction conditions were as follows: 45 °C for 60 min for cDNA synthesis, followed by enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 5 min. cDNA samples were stored at −20 °C until further use.

Virus detection

Viral detection was performed using PCR targeting a conserved region of the nonstructural protein 5 (NS5) gene of orthoflaviviruses. The primer set used enables detection of a broad range of flaviviruses, including dengue virus (DENV), Zika virus (ZIKV), Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), West Nile virus (WNV), yellow fever virus, Tembusu virus (TMUV), and mosquito-specific flaviviruses. PCR amplification targeted a 1018-bp fragment using primers described by Daidoji et al. (2021). Each 25 μl reaction mixture contained 12.5 μl of KOD One PCR Master Mix Blue, 0.75 μl of each primer (10 μM), 2 μl of cDNA template (or distilled water as a negative control), and 9 μl of distilled water. Thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min, followed by 45 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 68 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 68 °C for 7 min. PCR products of the expected size were excised and purified using a GenePHlow Gel/PCR Kit.

Sequence and phylogenetic analyses

Purified PCR products were sequenced using a commercial service (U2Bio, Bangkok, Thailand). The obtained nucleotide sequences were compared with reference sequences available in the GenBank database using the nucleotide Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLASTn) (Altschul et al., 1990). Sequence alignment and trimming were performed using the ClustalW algorithm (Thompson et al., 1994) implemented in MEGA X (Kumar et al., 2018). The aligned sequences were manually inspected and trimmed to equal lengths prior to downstream analyses.

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using the best-fit nucleotide substitution model selected based on the Bayesian Information Criterion in MEGA X (Kumar et al., 2018). Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the maximum likelihood method with 1000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA X (Kumar et al., 2018). Reference sequences for comparative analyses were retrieved from GenBank and VectorBase.

Results

Mosquito identification and phylogenetic analysis

NCBI BLASTn was used to analyze the cox1 sequences generated from 490 pool samples (1041 mosquitoes). All cox1 sequences generated were aligned using MEGA X, and analyses were conducted using BLASTn and DnaSP6 (Rozas et al., 2017). BLASTn and haplotype analyses identified 44 haplotypes across five genera and 36 species (Table 2). Haplotype sequences were submitted to the GenBank database (PX466332-PX466377) and used to construct a phylogenetic tree for comparisons with other mosquito species registered in GenBank. The phylogenetic tree was generated using the maximum likelihood method and the general time reversible model (Nei and Kumar, 2000) (Fig. 2). This analysis involved 161 cox1 sequences; the final dataset contained 639 positions. Of the 44 haplotypes, the genus Culex was the most diverse (16 species), followed by Anopheles (9 species), Aedes (6 species), Mansonia (3 species), and Armigeres (2 species) (Fig. 2).

Detection of orthoflaviviruses in the collected mosquitoes

The overall pool positivity rate for the 490 pooled samples (1041 mosquitoes) was 0.61% (3/490). Of the total samples, one Ae. aegypti sample collected from the Visitor Center in August 2023 tested positive for DENV, whereas Cx. tritaeniorhynchus samples collected from the Lam Ta Khong Campsite in February 2023 and January 2024 tested positive for JEV and TMUV, respectively. Of the 490 pooled samples, the PCR technique employing pan-flavivirus primers indicated the presence of three Orthoflavivirus sequences. These were arranged into three nucleotide sequence types (ntSTs): ntST#1 (PX471057) showing a 100% match with JEV (GenBank: JN711459); ntST#2 (PX471056) showing a 100% match with TMUV (GenBank: AB110489); and ntST#3 (PX466320) showing a 100% match with DENV (GenBank: JF730050) (Table 3).

Mosquito collection

Abundance of mosquito species in Khao Yai National Park

Over 24 months from January 2023 to December 2024, a total of 1041 mosquitoes representing 36 species across five genera were collected from eight sampling sites spanning different elevations in Khao Yai National Park (Fig. 1). The mosquito assemblage was dominated by a few species, with Cx. gelidus, Cx. tritaeniorhynchus, and Ar. subalbatus accounting for the largest proportions of the total catch, whereas the remaining species were present at relatively low abundances (Table 4).

Intermediate patterns were observed at two sites, the Haew Narok Protection Unit Office and the Visitor Center, where relatively high species richness was accompanied by moderate diversity, suggesting uneven species dominance within the community. Differences in species composition were also apparent among sites, with dominant taxa varying across locations, indicating localized ecological structuring of mosquito populations.

Overall, mosquito assemblages in Khao Yai National Park exhibited pronounced spatial heterogeneity, with both species richness and diversity generally declining with increasing elevation. The overall Shannon diversity index (H′ = 2.328) indicates a moderately diverse mosquito community at the park level, reflecting the combined influence of species richness and relative abundance on community structure. This pattern is consistent with environmental constraints at higher elevations, including lower temperatures and reduced availability of suitable breeding habitats.

Influence of climate on the abundance of collected mosquito populations

The study examined climatic factors, including temperature, relative humidity, and rainfall, at Khao Yai National Park from January 2023 to December 2024. Highest average temperature of 27.1 °C was recorded in April; lowest average temperature of 21.7 °C was recorded in January (Fig. 9A). Highest average relative humidity of 86.35% was recorded in June; lowest average relative humidity of 74.98% was recorded in January (Fig. 9B). Highest average rainfall of 404.5 mm was recorded in September; lowest average monthly rainfall of 0.30 mm was recorded in December (Fig. 9C). Additionally, the association between the climate and abundance of mosquitoes collected by light trap was determined. The relationships between mosquito abundance and climatic variables were explored descriptively based on monthly variations in average temperature, relative humidity, and rainfall between 2023 and 2024. Mosquito abundance appeared to increase during periods of higher temperature, relative humidity, and rainfall (Fig. 9). These observations suggest potential associations between environmental variables and mosquito abundance; however, no formal statistical analysis was performed, and these relationships should be interpreted with caution.

Effect of elevation on the abundance of collected mosquito populations

Mosquito abundance differed across the elevation gradient (Table 6). The highest abundance was recorded at the Haew Narok Protection Unit Office (479 masl), accounting for 34.68% of the total collection, whereas the lowest abundance was observed at Khao Khaew Viewpoint (1290 masl), representing 1.83% of the total collection. Overall, abundance was relatively higher at lower-elevation sites, although this pattern was not strictly monotonic (Fig. 10). These observations suggest a potential association between elevation and mosquito abundance; however, as this analysis is descriptive, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Additional environmental and site-specific factors are also likely to influence the observed patterns.

Discussion

Mosquitoes are important disease vectors, and their diversity and distribution influence disease transmission dynamics. In this study, mosquitoes were surveyed through monthly collections over 2 years (2023–2024) in Khao Yai National Park. A total of 36 mosquito species belonging to five genera (Aedes, Anopheles, Armigeres, Culex, and Mansonia) were identified. The most abundant species were Cx. gelidus, Cx. tritaeniorhynchus, and Ar. subalbatus. Culex was the most species-rich genus, accounting for 16 out of the 36 identified species. Overall, the mosquito assemblage in Khao Yai National Park was moderately diverse, although dominated by a few abundant species. This pattern reflects uneven species abundances, with one dominant species and several co-occurring species contributing to reduced evenness. The dominance of Cx. gelidus likely contributed to this pattern, slightly lowering overall community evenness. This finding is consistent with previous reports from Doi Inthanon National Park, Thailand, where Culex spp. were dominant across multiple habitat types (Srisuka et al., 2022). The widespread occurrence of Culex spp. in Thailand is likely associated with their ecological adaptability, broad host range, and diverse breeding habitats, particularly under tropical climatic conditions characterized by high temperature and humidity (Prummongkol et al., 2012; Thongsripong et al., 2013; Laojun et al., 2025).

In this study, three orthoflaviviruses were detected in mosquito samples, including Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), Tembusu virus (TMUV), and dengue virus (DENV). JEV and TMUV were detected in Cx. tritaeniorhynchus pools, highlighting the potential role of this species in virus transmission. The opportunistic feeding behavior of Culex spp., which includes birds, wildlife, livestock, and humans, likely facilitates their role as bridge vectors and supports the maintenance and circulation of these viruses in natural and peri-domestic environments (Prummongkol et al., 2012; Laojun et al., 2025). Culex spp. are important vectors of several orthoflaviviruses, including JEV, WNV, TMUV, and Culex flavivirus (Lalubin et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2013). JEV was detected in Cx. tritaeniorhynchus samples collected in February 2023, whereas TMUV was detected in Cx. tritaeniorhynchus samples collected in January 2024, both from Lam Ta Khong Campsite. This site is located in a forested area near a headwater branch of the Lam Ta Khong River and provides suitable ecological conditions, including the presence of wildlife hosts such as birds, wild boars, and deer, which may enhance local virus transmission dynamics. According to the WHO reports, Thailand recorded 15 and 8 human cases of JEV infection in 2023 and 2024, respectively (WHO, 2025). Humans are incidental dead-end hosts and may become infected when in proximity to amplifying hosts, particularly wading birds and pigs (Solomon et al., 2000; Wang and Liang, 2015).

TMUV is a mosquito-borne orthoflavivirus that primarily infects poultry, notably ducks. In recent years, TMUV has been detected across Thailand and occasionally in ducks (Thontiravong et al., 2015), mosquitoes (Sri-in et al., 2025), and non-bird hosts such as bottlenose dolphins (Piewbang et al., 2025), raising One Health concerns. Entomological surveillance programmes in northern Thailand have detected TMUV RNA in Culex spp., indicating that local mosquito populations can carry the virus and maintain transmission outside farm settings. These studies include phylogenetic analysis linking mosquito isolates to poultry strains (Hamel et al., 2023). Surveillance programmes typically include sampling of feces, swabs, and sera from wild and migratory birds to detect viral RNA or antibodies; migratory flyways are suspected routes for long-distance TMUV dispersal into China, Thailand, and Taiwan (Fang et al., 2023). Our findings, together with previous studies, indicate that surveillance of JEV and TMUV should not focus solely on poultry and migratory birds, as these viruses are maintained within complex enzootic transmission cycles involving mosquitoes, pigs, and a broader range of vertebrate hosts (van den Hurk et al., 2009; Pearce et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2020).

Aedes aegypti in a pool collected in August 2023 (rainy season) from the Visitor Center were positive for DENV serotype 2. DENV is a mosquito-borne orthoflavivirus that causes dengue fever, dengue hemorrhagic fever, and dengue shock syndrome and is a major public health problem in tropical and subtropical regions worldwide (Bhatt et al., 2013). DENV comprises four antigenically distinct serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4) that share approximately 65% amino acid sequence similarity, and each serotype contains multiple genotypes that vary by region (Xiao et al., 2018; Harapan et al., 2020). Immunity acquired against one serotype confers only transient cross-protection and may increase the risk of severe disease during secondary infection (Parra-González et al., 2025). All four serotypes circulate in Thailand, although their prevalence varies by region and over time (Sahavechaphan et al., 2024). In Thailand, dengue incidence typically peaks during the rainy season (June-September), when increased rainfall creates abundant breeding sites for mosquitoes, particularly Ae. aegypti and Ae. albopictus (Sahavechaphan et al., 2024). According to the 2024 Epidemiological Surveillance Report, 102,927 laboratory-confirmed dengue cases, including 84 deaths (case fatality rate of 0.08%), were reported. In comparison, 155,920 laboratory-confirmed cases, including 175 deaths (case fatality rate of 0.11%), were reported from week 1 to week 50 in 2023 (WHO, 2024). Although DENV cases and deaths decreased in 2024 compared with 2023, economic and human losses remain substantial, underscoring the continued public health burden in Thailand.

Regarding the influence of altitude on mosquito abundance, our field data showed that mosquito abundance was generally higher at lower elevations. However, this pattern was not strictly monotonic, suggesting that factors beyond elevation, such as habitat characteristics, microclimate, and human activity, also contribute to shaping mosquito distribution. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported that mosquito abundance and vector-borne disease risk tend to decline with increasing altitude. For example, in the Western Highlands of Kenya, each 10 m increase in altitude was associated with c.5% decrease in the odds of Plasmodium falciparum infection (Oduma et al., 2023). Similarly, lower egg counts of Aedes spp. have been reported at elevations above 500 masl compared with lower elevations (Silva et al., 2024), and both Aedes spp. and Culex spp. have been shown to decrease significantly with increasing altitude (Asigua et al., 2017). These patterns are likely driven, at least in part, by temperature gradients, as ambient temperature decreases with increasing elevation.

Climatic factors, including temperature, relative humidity, and rainfall, appeared to influence mosquito abundance. In this study, mosquito abundance tended to increase with higher temperatures, which is consistent with previous studies identifying temperature as a key driver of mosquito development and survival (Naish et al., 2014). Within optimal ranges, higher temperatures can shorten larval and pupal development time, accelerate adult emergence, increase biting rates, and reduce the extrinsic incubation period of pathogens. However, extreme temperatures may negatively affect mosquito survival and activity (Naish et al., 2014; Ezihe et al., 2017).

Similarly, higher relative humidity coincided with greater mosquito abundance, potentially due to reduced desiccation and enhanced survival and activity. Mosquitoes are particularly sensitive to low humidity because of their high surface area-to-volume ratio, which increases water loss; thus, higher humidity may support longer survival and increased reproductive success (Bai et al., 2013; Fu et al., 2024). Rainfall may also contribute by increasing the availability of breeding habitats, and previous studies have reported higher mosquito abundance during or shortly after the rainy season (Shaman and Day, 2005; Bello et al., 2025; Panigrahi et al., 2025). In the present study, peak mosquito abundance was observed in June, corresponding to the period following the onset of rainfall in Thailand (Limsakul, 2020). However, as no formal statistical analyses were conducted, these observations should be interpreted cautiously, and further studies incorporating quantitative analyses are warranted to confirm these relationships.

Notably, orthoflavivirus-positive mosquitoes were detected during the study period, indicating the presence of potential transmission cycles within the study area. These findings highlight the importance of continued surveillance and monitoring of mosquito-borne pathogens across different ecological settings.

Despite repeated sampling over 24 months using daytime resting collections and overnight CDC light traps in a forested national park, the number of collected mosquitoes remained modest relative to the spatiotemporal scale of the survey. Accordingly, the dataset is better suited for baseline surveillance and descriptive biodiversity assessments than for robust inference on environmental drivers. Several methodological limitations should be considered. Random sampling of outdoor resting mosquito populations is inherently difficult, and the absence of sampling across all life stages (eggs, larvae, pupae, and adults) may have limited the completeness of species detection. In addition, passive collection methods targeting outdoor resting mosquitoes may not adequately represent the local mosquito community and may under-sample species with different resting or host-seeking behaviors. As emphasized by Silver (2008), no single sampling method is sufficient to characterize mosquito populations comprehensively, as each approach is subject to inherent bias. Sampling strategies should therefore be aligned with study objectives, ecological context, and mosquito behavior. Consistent with this, integrating complementary sampling approaches and applying appropriate analytical frameworks are essential for generating robust and interpretable data (Jalilian et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings highlight the need for further research and the development of adaptive surveillance and public health strategies capable of capturing spatiotemporal variability in vector populations.

Conclusions

This study provides baseline data on mosquito diversity and orthoflavivirus detection in Khao Yai National Park. The observed patterns suggest potential associations between mosquito abundance and environmental factors, including elevation and climatic variables. These findings contribute to an improved understanding of mosquito ecology in forested ecosystems and may support future surveillance and risk assessment efforts. Orthoflaviviruses, including Japanese encephalitis virus, Tembusu virus, and dengue virus, were detected in field-collected mosquitoes, indicating ongoing viral circulation in the study area. The observed distribution patterns suggest that mosquito abundance tended to be higher at lower elevations; however, these findings should be interpreted with caution, given the descriptive nature of the study. Further studies incorporating more comprehensive sampling across life stages and more robust analytical approaches are needed to better elucidate the drivers of mosquito population dynamics and to refine assessments of vector-borne disease risk in this and similar ecosystems.
