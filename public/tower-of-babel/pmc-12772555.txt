Resilience-building in palliative care professionals: scoping review
Authors: Suwanan Yongpraderm, Patcharanat Inpithuk, Itthipon Wongprom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractResilience-building in palliative care professionals: scoping reviewBackgroundBurnout, demoralisation and compassion fatigue are common among palliative care professionals. Practising palliative care necessitates a quality of resilience in order to ensure constant and optimal patient care. However, there is no universal approach to prevent burnout or raise resilience among palliative care professionals. This study aims to provide an overview of interventions and explore their effectiveness in building resilience in palliative care professionals.MethodsThe search was conducted in four databases: MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO and Cochrane Database. Two independent investigators reviewed eligibility, with conflicts resolved by a third investigator. One reviewer performed data extraction, later reviewed by a second investigator. All eligible studies were manually re-reviewed. Quantitative and qualitative data synthesis were conducted.ResultsThe search initially revealed 11 470 potentially eligible citations. 12 studies were included in the analysis. Most studies had a small number of participants. The studies varied in sample size, interventions and assessment tools, making it challenging to identify the most effective resilience-building interventions. However, our analysis revealed commonly found elements among these interventions: five essential elements (regularity, self-care, mindfulness, reflective practice and cognitive–behavioural therapy) and three supporting elements (peer support, educational sessions and organisational support).DiscussionWhile the effectiveness of specific interventions remains inconclusive, this review highlights essential and supporting elements that should be considered in designing resilience-building programmes for palliative care professionals. Future research should focus on developing assessment tools specific to palliative care, conducting well-designed studies, and creating replicable, standardised interventions.Keywords: Palliative Care, Psychological care, Patient Care Team, Education and training

Introduction

Burnout, demoralisation and compassion fatigue are common among palliative care professionals including physicians, nurses, social workers, allied health professionals and chaplains.1 The nature of work demands palliative care professionals to be immersed in death and dying routinely; palliative care professionals usually witness the terminal period of their patient’s lives.2 An effective way to recover from stressful, emotional experiences is to develop resilience.3 4 Resilience is essential in palliative care not only to protect the mental health of palliative care professionals but also to ensure that they can maintain a high quality of compassionate care despite the emotional demands of their work.5 6 This is particularly important because the cumulative stress and emotional exposure in palliative care can lead to detrimental effects, including burnout, compassion fatigue and decreased job satisfaction. By developing resilience, palliative care professionals are better equipped to handle these stressors, reduce the risk of burnout and continue to provide effective care.2 7 8

Resilience, in the context of this review, is an ability to effectively cope with and adjust to challenging or adverse life situations by demonstrating mental, emotional and behavioural flexibility in response to both external and internal demands.9 This concept of resilience is closely related to but distinct from well-being and professional satisfaction, which refer more broadly to an individual’s overall mental and emotional state. Conversely, burnout and stress can be viewed as challenges or adverse outcomes that resilience can help mitigate. A study showed that resilience and coping skills positively impact students’ ability to manage daily stressors.10 Another molecular adaptation study argued that resilience might serve as a protective factor against neurovascular changes in the blood–brain barrier.11 Furthermore, many studies support and recommend that understanding and practising resilience will improve coping with stress.8 12 Building resilience is therefore essential for both the well-being of palliative care professionals and for sustaining the workforce in palliative care, a field where skilled professionals are critically needed to support patients and families facing end-of-life situations. Without sufficient resilience, palliative care professionals are at greater risk of leaving the field, which could have negative implications for the quality and availability of palliative care services. Hence, practising palliative care necessitates resilience to maintain consistent, optimal patient care.13 14

Although resilience and clinician’s well-being are frequently discussed, burnout remains a significant problem. Literature emphasised the significance of burnout among palliative care professionals.15,17 One study showed that one-third of Canadian palliative care physicians have burnout problems.7 Various interventions have been proposed to tackle burnout or compassion fatigue in palliative care professionals. Educational sessions about self-care or reflective practice are commonly implemented.2 16 Some institutions integrate these sessions into their informal curricula.18 19 Others use mindfulness-oriented activities or experience-sharing sessions as strategies to foster resilience. However, little is known about effective measures to prevent burnout or enhance resilience among palliative care professionals. Therefore, this study aims to provide an overview of what is known about interventions that build resilience in palliative care professionals. By synthesising the available evidence, this review seeks to identify effective strategies and highlight gaps in knowledge to inform future research and practice in this critical area.

Methods

This study is a scoping review that follows the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) protocol.20 A PRISMA-ScR checklist was shown in online supplemental appendix A. The study is guided by the question, ‘What interventions can build resilience in palliative care professionals?’. Interventions are defined as any activities provided to the target population. Palliative care professionals include professionals and clinical learners across various disciplines. This study is retrospectively registered on the Open Science Framework, and the full registration can be accessed online (https://osf.io/8hbtj).

Eligibility criteria

For this review, ‘palliative care professionals’ refers to licensed, accredited or qualified healthcare workers involved in palliative care, including physicians, nurses, social workers, allied health professionals and chaplains. The articles were included for screening based on these criteria: (1) the study involved at least one intervention with an intended or observed impact on resilience-building for healthcare workers, regardless of the type of intervention; (2) participants are healthcare workers in palliative care, both cancer and non-cancer; (3) there is an outcome assessment. No additional exclusion criteria were applied.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the second week of September 2023. An updated search was performed in November 2024. The following databases were searched: MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL and The Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. The search strategy included keywords and controlled vocabulary terms related to resilience and palliative care professionals. Keywords used in the search are listed in online supplemental appendix B. No date restrictions were applied.

Screening and data charting

All citations were imported into the Windows PC-based reference manager Zotero, and subsequently transferred to COVIDENCE for further review. The abstracts and titles were screened independently by two investigators for eligibility. Studies that did not meet the three inclusion criteria were excluded. Then, the full-text versions of the studies were retrieved, and their eligibility was reviewed independently. Conflicts were resolved through discussion with a third investigator. All eligible studies were manually re-reviewed and analysed further.

Data items

Data items included study characteristics (eg, first author, year of publication), population details (eg, profession, years of experience, specific roles within palliative care) and intervention/exposure characteristics (eg, types of resilience-building activities such as mindfulness programmes, educational sessions, peer support systems, duration, frequency and delivery methods). Comparison groups, when applicable, included placebo groups, standard care or no intervention. Outcomes measured the impact of interventions on resilience using any validated tools, as well as measures of stress, quality of life, and other relevant psychological and physiological indicators. The study design variable included the methodological framework, ranging from randomised controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies to pre-post studies.

Synthesis

We employed both quantitative and qualitative data analysis methods to synthesise findings from the included studies. Quantitative data from the included studies were extracted and organised in tables to facilitate comparison. Key variables such as study design, sample size, intervention type, duration, frequency and outcomes were documented. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the characteristics of the included studies, including the number of studies, participants and intervention types. Qualitative data from the included studies were later analysed to identify common themes and patterns related to resilience-building interventions. Two investigators, SY and IW, analysed these data independently, and conflicts were resolved by investigator triangulation, PI.

Variety of interventions and outcomes

The studies included in this review employed a wide range of interventions. Due to the heterogeneity of the interventions and the lack of direct comparative studies, it is difficult to compare one set of interventions to another. Essential elements

Five similar patterns are seen in the interventions implemented in the included studies. These patterns or themes reflect some characteristics of the provided interventions.

Regularity

As observed in the included studies, resilience programmes were often implemented continuously at regular intervals, such as monthly or weekly.21,2426 Some studies mentioned providing remuneration or offering protected time to participate in their programmes.21 22 25 Others used reminders to notify participants when new resources were available or to inform them of meeting schedules, ensuring continuous support.23 24

Self-care

Physical exhaustion undoubtedly affects healthcare workers’ mental well-being and decision-making. Therefore, focusing on self-care is frequently mentioned in many studies. Some studies included self-care topics in their educational sessions,22 25 while others highlighted their importance.24 26 Physical self-care includes regular meals, adequate rest and sleep, short breaks and routine exercise. Mental self-care is also discussed and can be supported by training in coping skills, meditation, evaluating stressors and debriefing.2426,28 Another study employed art and reflective exercises to enable participants to visualise their self-care practice.31

Mindfulness

Many studies mentioned mindfulness as a means of raising awareness through practices such as body awareness, breathing and compassion-oriented meditation.21,2326 28 Some employed activities aimed at facilitating relaxation, diverting attention from stress and enhancing control.23 27

Reflective practice

Reflection and reflective practice are common among palliative care professionals, often used to guide patients and support their families when facing challenges. Many studies in this review included reflective practice or transformative learning concepts in their programmes for exploring thoughts and feelings, identifying expectations and needs, recognising stressors and quality of life, assessing self-value and self-validation, and discovering coping patterns.21 23 30 31 Others employed writing or journaling to facilitate reflection.28 29 32 Additionally, reflective practice from others is beneficial for therapeutic feedback and validation experiences.24

Cognitive–behavioural therapy

Some techniques based on this principle were frequently mentioned in the studies, such as positive psychology, acceptance and commitment therapy, Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy and Defusion.23 26 28 29 For example, writing plans and chosen paths yield a pause time to connect, self-appreciation, and a better understanding of both others and our work. One study did not describe in detail but explicitly stated using CBT in the programme.26

Supporting elements

There are three similar patterns observed in the interventions implemented in the included studies. These patterns or themes reflect how the interventions were provided. These three supporting elements, educational sessions, peer support and organisational support, are essential in building resilience.

Providing educational sessions

Ten studies provided interventions through educational programmes such as courses, sessions and group workshops.21,2325 These educational programmes were well-structured, with clear objectives and expected outcomes. The programmes typically aimed to reduce stress, address trauma and equip participants with new skills. The interventions in these programmes primarily revolve around discussing the number of sessions, their duration, frequency and whether facilitators are involved. Therefore, attention was given to both the delivery platforms and the content being delivered simultaneously.

Peer support

Many included studies used interventions based on collaborative work with peers or other supporters, including group discussions, one-to-one sessions with teachers and group workshops.21,2325 26 28 These strategies aim to cultivate a supportive work environment, with peer-based interventions specifically fostering social support among colleagues. For example, the Buddy System study demonstrated that structured peer support could increase compassion and resilience scores. Consequently, the authors suggested that understanding the principles of therapeutic feedback is beneficial for creating a supportive workplace.24

Organisation support resources

In most of the studies included, various forms of organisational support were observed for the establishment and implementation of support programmes. Some studies mentioned remuneration or providing protected time to participate in the programmes.2225 26 28,31 These regularity and resource allocations are more likely to be achieved with support from organisational levels, which may include funding, education or the provision of necessary facilitators such as experts and therapists.21,2327

Discussion

We conducted a comprehensive scoping review on building resilience in palliative care professionals. The result yielded six interventional studies. They often mention elements contributing to resilience, which can be categorised as essential elements and supporting elements. We propose a model derived from our analysis to depict building resilience processes, as shown in figure 2.

Model approach to building resilience.

In the model, the essential elements are the interventions or strategies often implemented to build resilience. These elements include regularity, mindfulness, CBT, reflective practice and self-care. They could be viewed as palliative care professionals’ intrinsic resources or ‘ingredients’. Another vital facet is the supporting elements, which include peer support, educational sessions and organisational support. Metaphorically, these supporting elements catalyse or transform the aforementioned ‘ingredients’ into the final product: resilience. This proposed model raises two important questions: what should be included in resilience-building programmes, and how should they be delivered? For example, to achieve a mindfulness-based resilience-building programme, it is imperative to have organisational support, such as allocating protected time for the activity and providing relevant educational resources. Another example involves integrating reflective practices with peer support techniques. Nonetheless, while this model highlights commonly used elements for building resilience among palliative care professionals, it does not imply that all elements are necessary, nor does it clarify if combining elements (eg, mindfulness and CBT) is more effective than using them individually. Further research could help clarify how these elements best support resilience in palliative care professionals.

Our analysis is in concordance with other studies. The literature suggests a systematic approach to providing resilience-building programmes using reflective practice and emphasises the essential role of organisational support, which could lead to workforce-related cost reductions.33,35 Other research also highlights the importance of both individual and organisational factors, similar to our proposed model’s essential and supporting elements.36 Furthermore, a study on medical learners in other disciplines also called for the systematic implementation of meaningful interventions to prevent burnout and promote resilience.37

While standardised interventions provide a structured approach to resilience-building, it is essential to consider the potential need for individualised approaches. Given the diverse backgrounds, experiences and personal coping mechanisms of palliative care professionals, resilience-building may benefit from flexible, tailored interventions that account for individual needs and preferences.

Limitation

There are some limitations to this scoping review. The search could be more comprehensive if other terms, such as satisfaction or well-being, were included. Nonetheless, to preserve the intended meaning, it is challenging to encompass all the commonly used interchangeable terms associated with resilience in the search process. Additionally, data synthesis may provide different insights if there were studies with more robust methodologies, such as controlled trials and larger sample sizes.

Recommendation

Strategies promoting resilience in palliative care professionals should be grounded in the best available evidence. Organisations providing palliative care or relevant authorities should focus on a systematic approach to building resilience among learners and professionals. Intervention options should be carefully selected from those based on educational theory, such as reflective practice or CBT. While this study focused specifically on resilience-building interventions within palliative care, further studies could explore resilience strategies that have proven effective in other fields, such as nursing or emergency services, to see if they may benefit palliative care professionals. Examining these approaches could help address gaps and broaden the range of resilience-supportive strategies in this field. Finally, future research should prioritise the development of assessment tools specific to palliative care, conduct more well-designed studies, and create replicable, standardised interventions.

Conclusions

Resilience is viewed as a quality that can alleviate burnout, prevent compassion fatigue and decrease burdens. Given the nature of palliative care, which routinely involves dealing with death and dying, it is important for palliative care professionals to strengthen and sustain their resilience over time. Many programmes and interventions have been implemented and studied to build resilience, but no evidence-based standardised programmes have been established yet. Although this scoping review cannot conclude the effectiveness of these interventions or identify the best approaches, it does identify and propose five essential elements (regularity, self-care, mindfulness, reflective practice and CBT) and three supporting elements (peer support, educational sessions and organisational support) that should be included in resilience-building programmes. Palliative care organisations must emphasise the importance of resilience and make concerted efforts to promote it among palliative care learners and professionals.
