Leveraging clinical decision support to improve depression screening and follow-up: insights from a quality improvement case study
Authors: Dongwook Kim, Andre Peri, Natalie Marker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe primary objective was to evaluate the impact of clinical decision support (CDS) tool integration into primary care visits on depression screening and follow-up rates and to assess whether CDS use improves adherence to Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) guidelines for depression screening and follow-up.DesignThis quality improvement evaluation study employed quantitative and qualitative components conducted in parallel to provide complementary insights. Modified Poisson regression with generalised estimating equation (GEE) was used to assess the association between CDS tool use and meeting HRSA criteria for depression screening and follow-up. In addition, semi-structured interviews explored perspectives on the implementation and utility of CDS tools.SettingThis study was conducted at a federally qualified health centre in Minnesota.ParticipantThe dataset included 12 338 patient encounters attributed to 8647 unique patients, covering 2 years of data. Five care providers were recruited through purposive sampling for the semi-structured interviews.ResultCDS use was significantly associated with an increased likelihood of meeting HRSA depression screening and follow-up criteria (relative risk 1.44, 95% CI 1.34 to 1.55; p<0.001). Qualitative findings suggested that while providers found CDS tools useful, workflow challenges and human-centred practices shaped their effectiveness.ConclusionIntegrating CDS tools into primary care workflows can enhance adherence to depression screening and follow-up guidelines. However, their effectiveness relies on supportive person-centred approaches, including collaboration and previsit preparation. These findings highlight the need for a balanced approach that integrates technological interventions with human interaction to enhance clinical practices. Future research should investigate how CDS tools are used in practice, address barriers to their adoption and develop strategies to promote their broader use while fostering continued learning among providers.

Introduction

Depression is often the most prevalent diagnosis in psychiatric syndromes in the USA, significantly impacting public health.1 The economic cost of depression in 2010, including both direct costs and indirect costs such as loss of productivity, is estimated to have reached approximately US$210 billion.2 Individuals with severe and persistent mental illness, among whom depression is a common comorbidity or primary diagnosis, experience disproportionately worse health outcomes. For instance, they have a life expectancy 10–20 years shorter than the general population, in part due to the high burden of depression.3 4 This is particularly significant as depression is one of the most prevalent diagnoses within psychiatric syndromes and a common accompanying condition to chronic health conditions that can exacerbate overall morbidity and mortality.5 6 Recognising this, incorporating depression screening into primary care visits has been advocated as part of a whole-person approach to healthcare.7 8

Depression screening in primary care has the potential to improve depression recognition and to decrease existing care disparities.9 The United States Preventive Services Task Force advises depression screening for all adults, including those who are pregnant or post partum and the older adults.10 To effectively leverage the advantages of early depression screening and follow-up, and to reduce related health disparities, it has been noted that individuals with positive depression screening results should receive further evaluation and, when necessary, have access to evidence-based treatments.8 11

To facilitate standardised reporting and enhance the quality of care, federally funded health centres are required to submit data through the uniform data system (UDS)—a comprehensive dataset that includes patient demographics, services provided, clinical processes and health outcomes.12 The UDS allows for the tracking of clinical quality measures and health outcomes, enabling policymakers, health administrators and health centres to make data-driven decisions regarding programme development and resource allocation.12

With respect to depression screening and follow-up, the UDS quality measure reports the percentage of patients aged 12 years and older who (a) were screened for depression using a standardised tool and, if the screening was positive, (b) had a documented follow-up plan.12 However, despite the availability of computer systems like electronic health records (EHRs) to capture this information, challenges remain in clinical practice. EHR systems often present usability issues such as information overload, unnecessary hard stops and inappropriate default settings, which can hinder efficient documentation and decision-making.1315 To address these challenges and enhance clinical workflows, many healthcare settings have adopted clinical decision support systems (CDSSs).16

Clinical decision support: literature review

CDSSs encompass a range of tools and systems aimed at improving healthcare delivery by offering clinicians, staff, patients and other individuals with evidence-based knowledge and tailored information within the clinical workflow.17 18 While CDSSs are applied across many conditions, including asthma, prenatal care and chronic disease management,19 20 this study focuses specifically on their role in depression screening and follow-up. Within the broader framework of CDSSs, clinical decision support (CDS) tools play a significant role in supporting clinical decisions pertaining to depression screening and follow-up. CDS tools assist in identifying patients at risk for depression who might otherwise go unscreened by alerting practitioners to gaps in care and suggesting appropriate screenings based on patient-specific factors, such as their medical history and current symptoms.21 CDS tools can ensure that more patients receive necessary evaluations and interventions.21 Additionally, CDS tools promote the standardisation of care by ensuring that screenings are conducted using validated, standardised tools, which is vital for generating reliable and consistent results across diverse patient populations.12 Furthermore, these tools encourage healthcare providers to perform screenings during patient visits, ultimately increasing overall depression screening rates.12 CDS tools have the potential to improve patient outcomes by offering evidence-based recommendations and reminders. They are designed to increase the chances that patients who screen positive for depression receive timely and appropriate follow-up care.12

While the potential benefits of CDS tools are well-recognised, previous studies provide further insights into their practical applications and limitations. Gard and Wessel22 highlighted that well-designed CDS systems can improve provider performance and patient health outcomes, especially when they incorporate features such as integration into clinical workflows, actionable guidance and timely prompts to support decision-making. However, the study also noted limitations, as it did not provide evidence demonstrating the effectiveness of these tools in improving adherence to asthma guidelines. Mahabir et al,23 through a pre-implementation and post-implementation analysis using data from a New York hospital, demonstrated that embedding structured Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)-2 and PHQ-9 forms into EHR workflows and implementing CDS alerts can enhance depression screening and follow-up care within a patient-centred medical home model. Lin et al24 underscore the importance of incorporating family health history (FHH) into CDS systems as a key component of risk assessment for various prenatal conditions. They argue that these systems use FHH data to offer clinicians valuable insights and actionable recommendations. Prior literature has indeed shown a gap in direct empirical evidence demonstrating the impact of CDS on patient care or outcomes. Most existing studies tend to descriptively outline the potential benefits of integrating CDS into clinical workflows, such as improved risk assessment,24 enhanced decision-making22 and personalised patient management.23 However, they often fall short of providing concrete data-driven evidence that links the use of CDS tools to measurable improvements in provider performance or patient outcomes.

Study purpose

This evaluative study seeks to answer three key questions. First, this study examines how the use of CDS tools varies across clinician types, providing a descriptive illustration of these differences. Second, it assesses whether screening and follow-up rates differ significantly between patients treated by providers who used CDS tools integrated into an EHR system and those treated by providers who did not use such tools. Third, it examines the relationship between the use of CDS and the likelihood of meeting HRSA guidelines for depression screening and follow-up.

Methods

Study design

This study adopts a quality improvement approach and follows the Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (V.2.0) guidelines.25 The present study applies an ex-post outcome evaluation framework26 to investigate the impact of the programme 2 years following its conclusion. It was conducted as a descriptive study with parallel quantitative and qualitative approaches. While the quantitative and qualitative strands were implemented independently, their findings were interpreted together to provide complementary perspectives on CDS implementation.

Setting

The study was conducted at a federally qualified health centre (FQHC) in Hennepin County, Minnesota, which is affiliated with the University of Minnesota. By March 2024, Minnesota is home to 16 FQHCs—essential safety net providers for vulnerable populations including people living with disability, with severe mental illnesses, migrants, the homeless and victims of domestic violence and human trafficking.21 27

Intervention description

Aligned with HRSA’s UDS guidelines, the Community-University Health Care Center (CUHCC), an FQHC in Minnesota, initiated a CDSS project in February 2022. The intervention sought to enhance depression screening and follow-up rates for clinically appropriate patients aged 12 years and older through the implementation of CDS tools. The initiative included three key components: (1) creating and integrating a computer-mediated CDS tool called ‘SMART Phrases’ into the EHR system, (2) providing training sessions for care providers and (3) conducting 4-week check-ins with primary care providers who adopted the CDS tools, all over an 8-week project period. The tools were designed to prompt clinical care teams by integrating the standardised PHQ-9 within the EHRs for depression screening, symptom monitoring and follow-up. These dynamic CDS tools (SMART Phrases) alert providers to patients who may require screening and streamline documentation processes, allowing for the rapid capture and organisation of patient data during encounters. By prepopulating relevant clinical information based on patient history and displaying it in a single user-friendly interface, the CDS tools expedite clinical workflows, reduce administrative burden and enables providers to focus more on patient interaction rather than on extensive documentation. Additionally, the system allows for timely follow-up by flagging necessary assessments and referrals based on individual patient needs, ensuring comprehensive care delivery.

Data collection and retrieval

Quantitative

The data analysis team involves the members of CUHCC’s Quality Team who took responsibility to generate datasets. The quantitative data were extracted and compiled from the OCHIN Epic EHR system, which stores, retrieves and manages patient health information collected during patient encounters and triage documentation for all patients.

The dataset 1 consisted of two components, both derived from CDS tools that embedded within the OCHIN Epic to enhance depression screening and follow-up in compliance with HRSA UDS guidelines. The first component, the meaningful use/UDS (MUUDS) SMART Phrase dataset, included 539 encounters involving 41 providers and 443 unique patients (identified by medical record number (MRN)). The second component, the service area (SA) 124 SMART Phrase dataset, containing 315 encounters with 10 providers and 164 unique patients (MRN). SMART Phrases, which are predefined text templates within the EHR, function as part of the agency’s CDS system to streamline clinical documentation and ensure that providers accurately record required screening and follow-up procedures. Among these, 24 MRNs were present in both the SA 124 and MUUDS datasets. Rather than reducing the total number of cases (607) by removing duplicates, the authors chose to adopt a ‘Keep Duplicates as They Are’ approach, as the matched MRNs represented encounters involving different providers. Consequently, the combined total of unique cases, including multiple encounters with the same patients, was finalised at 607 for our analytic sample. This dataset was restricted to patients aged 12 years and older who: (a) were screened for depression using a CDS tool and (b) had a documented follow-up plan if the screening result was positive. Dataset 1 was primarily designed to capture provider-level variables (eg, provider ID, clinician title, provider type, department, provider sex) for the purpose of analysing CDS utilisation patterns.

The merged dataset integrates the first dataset with the larger UDS Table dataset, containing 12 338 encounters. Dataset 2 included patient-level records such as age, sex, race, language and the HRSA-defined depression screening outcome (met, not met, expected, excluded). It incorporates de-identified provider IDs, depression screenings and follow-ups using CDS tools (coded as yes=1, no=0), and additional demographic variables such as age, sex, race and language. The second dataset identified seven unmatched MRNs from the first dataset, leaving the total CDS count at 600. The UDS Table dataset served as the anchor for the merged dataset.

Qualitative

Key informant interviews were conducted to capture nuanced and diverse perspectives from medical professionals. Data collection followed a structured interview format. A tailored interview guide was developed specifically for healthcare providers. All interviews were held via Zoom Workplace with five primary care providers, ensuring privacy by not recording the sessions. Instead, the lead author took detailed notes during and immediately after each interview, as creating verbatim transcripts was not feasible. To enhance accuracy and rigour, notes were reviewed collaboratively among team members and discussed to resolve discrepancies before coding and analysis. On average, each interview lasted approximately 20 min.

Data analysis

The quantitative analysis is based on patients’ health records and involved two datasets. The dataset 1 includes unique patient cases with applied CDS (n=607) from January 2022 to December 2023. This dataset was then merged with a larger secondary clinical dataset, ‘UDS Table’ (n=12 338), using patients’ MRN as the common identifier (dataset 2). Patients aged 12 years and older with at least one eligible visit during the measurement period were included for analysis.

For analytic purposes, depression screening outcomes were dichotomised into two categories: (1) met, defined as patients who screened negative for depression or those who screened positive and had a documented follow-up plan on the date of the visit or within 2 days after and (2) not met, defined as patients who were not screened when required or who screened positive without a documented follow-up plan within the same timeframe. Patients falling into the ‘excepted’ (eg, refusal or emergent visit) and ‘excluded’ (eg, telephone visit only, pre-existing bipolar disorder) categories as specified in HRSA’s UDS manual12 were omitted from the regression analysis.

Descriptive statistics were conducted to present the distribution of demographic characteristics of the sample, CDS use and patient outcome in accordance with HRSA’s guidelines on depression screening and follow-up. Bivariate analyses were performed to test whether significant differences in patient outcome by patient demographic profiles and CDS use were presented. To investigate potential disparities in CDS utilisation across different provider types, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. Finally, because the unit of analysis was the encounter rather than the patient, repeated observations from the same patient (MRN) were possible. To account for potential intrapatient correlation, we fitted a generalised estimating equation (GEE) model with a Poisson distribution and log link, clustering on MRN. This approach allowed us to estimate population-averaged effects and report relative risks (RRs). All statistical analyses were performed using R Studio (R V.4.4.1), with a 95% CI set as the threshold for statistical significance.

On the qualitative side, this social inquiry aims to provide an in-depth insight into the care providers’ experiences and perspectives on depression screening and follow-up using CDS tools. This helps with developing culturally adaptive and evidence-informed interventions for our client population.28 The interview guide was collaboratively developed by the study team. The lead author conducted all five interviews via Zoom Workplace and simultaneously took detailed notes including exemplary quotes during and immediately after each session. We conducted a thematic analysis29 of the interview notes to uncover key and recurring themes related to the interviewees’ perspectives. A purposive sampling method was used to effectively capture the lived experiences of primary care providers with CDS and its integration into depression screening and follow-up practices. The lead author carried out the initial coding and theme development, which were subsequently reviewed and discussed with the study team to strengthen analytic rigour. The interpretation of meaning and subsequent conclusions were collaboratively developed by the authors. This process continued until consensus was reached among the evaluation team through repeated, in-context review of transcript materials. We resolved disagreement in interpretations through discussions.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

The study population (n=12 338) was diverse, with the largest groups identifying as black/African American (35%) and Hispanic/Latino (30%). White (18%) and Asian (12%) patients also comprised notable proportions, while American Indian/Alaska Natives (AIAN) and multiracial groups together accounted for <5%. The average age was 41 years (SD=28), and just over half were female (55%). During the 2-year study period, 4.9% of patients had CDS tools incorporated into their care (table 1).

Bivariate analysis indicated that age, race, primary language and CDS utilisation were significantly associated with HRSA-defined screening and follow-up outcomes, whereas sex was not (table 1). Specifically, older age, non-English language and identifying as black/African American or AIAN were associated with lower adherence, whereas Asian race and CDS utilisation were associated with higher adherence to HRSA depression screening and follow-up guidelines.

Addressing the first analytical objective and using dataset 1, we observed that CDS usage varied noticeably by provider type over the observation period. As shown in figure 1, the distribution of CDS-using patients per provider was highly skewed. Most provider groups had a median of one patient with CDS recorded, but spread differed considerably. Residents (median=1.0, IQR 1.0–2.0, maximum=12) and pharmacists (median=1.0, IQR 1.0–2.0, maximum=8) demonstrated the widest range, indicating that some individuals in these groups applied CDS far more frequently than their peers. Finally, we examined the relationship between patient demographic factors and CDS utilisation in relation to the RR of meeting HRSA’s requirements for depression screening and follow-up. A modified Poisson regression with GEEs (figure 2) was used to examine the association between demographic factors, provider’s use of CDS and the likelihood of meeting HRSA guidelines for depression screening and follow-up among an analytic sample of 8765 observations. Holding all other factors constant, age was a significant factor, with all age groups demonstrating lower likelihood of meeting HRSA guidelines compared with the reference group of patients under 18 years of age. Older adults aged 55 years and above had the lowest RR of adherence (RR 0.58, 95% CI 0.54 to 0.63, p<0.001).

Sex was not significantly associated with adherence to HRSA depression screening and follow-up guidelines. Male patients had slightly lower RR compared with females (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.00, p=0.051), but this difference did not reach statistical significance. Language barriers were significantly associated with lower RR of compliance, as non-English-speaking patients were less likely to meet HRSA guidelines than their English-speaking counterparts (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.84 to 0.94, p<0.001). Race was also significantly associated with compliance. Compared with white reference patient group, AIAN patients had a significantly decreased probability of meeting HRSA guidelines (RR 0.79, 95% CI 0.67 to 0.94, p=0.006). Black/African American patients also had a reduced likelihood of adherence (RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.85 to 0.98, p=0.009). In contrast, Asian patients had a significantly increased likelihood of being screened by care providers in adherence to HRSA guidelines (RR 1.10, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.20, p=0.03), while Hispanic/Latino and multiracial patients did not demonstrate significant differences compared with the reference group. The results address the study’s objective of examining disparities in guideline adherence by demographic characteristics.

Lastly, the use of CDS tools was a strong factor in meeting HRSA guidelines. After controlling for other covariates, patients treated by providers who used CDS tools had a significantly higher likelihood of compliance compared with those treated by providers who did not integrate CDS tools, with their likelihood being approximately 1.4 times greater (RR 1.44, 95% CI 1.34 to 1.55, p<0.001). This finding directly supports the study aim by confirming that CDS use is associated with improved adherence to HRSA depression screening and follow-up.

In the qualitative analysis, insights were drawn from interviews (n=5) who all acknowledged the high prevalence of depression among clients. Each participant emphasised the significance of maintaining human elements in the workflow, noting that tasks such as PHQ-9 screenings were frequently handled by medical assistants, suggesting a preference for human interactions over computer-mediated tools in certain aspects of patient care. Opinions on the effectiveness of CDS tools were mixed among the participants. Some primary care providers expressed scepticism about the benefits of these tools, questioning their positive influence on depression screening processes. In contrast, other providers considered the CDS tools indispensable for guiding clinical decisions. One interviewee explicitly criticised the CDS systems, describing them as ‘burden’ and ‘extra tasks’ that added to their workload due to the time-consuming nature of inputting information. These diverse perspectives highlight the complex dynamics and varied acceptance of health informatics, underlining the need for balancing technological integration with human-centric approaches in clinical workflows. Discussion

Our study revealed that demographic factors and the use of CDS tools influenced HRSA guidelines adherence for depression screening and follow-up, suggesting that certain client groups, particularly older adults, non-English speakers and people of colour, may face disparities. While our study examines adherence on a per-encounter basis and incorporates multiple factors, including CDS use, our findings are broadly consistent with prior research.

Regarding language, our results align with studies showing that Spanish-speaking patients are screened at lower rates than English-speaking counterparts. Interpreter-assisted visits require additional time, often leading providers to prioritise acute or chronic disease management over preventive care such as depression screening.30 Our qualitative findings reinforce this, highlighting the competing demands faced by providers during patient encounters. Our model did not include an interaction term between race/ethnicity and primary language; therefore, we cannot isolate effects for specific race–language combinations (eg, English-speaking Hispanic patients), which remains an area for future research.

For sex differences, although prior studies have found that males are less likely to be screened than females,31 32 our study did not identify a statistically significant association between sex and adherence to HRSA depression screening and follow-up guidelines. This suggests that, within our sample, sex-based disparities may be less pronounced, although the trend was consistent with existing literature.

Regarding age, our findings mirror prior research showing lower screening and follow-up rates among older adults.31 This may reflect misconceptions that depressive symptoms are a natural part of ageing and the tendency for clinicians to prioritise comorbid physical conditions over mental health needs.33

Finally, concerning racial and ethnic disparities, our study’s findings are consistent with existing literature, highlighting lower depression screening rates among minority populations.34 Providers may unintentionally deprioritise depression screening for racial and ethnic minorities due to high burdens of chronic illness, and time-limited appointments may cause clinicians to focus on immediate health concerns instead of preventive mental healthcare.34 35

This study found that CDS plays a significant role in ensuring compliance with depression screening and follow-up outcomes as guided by HRSA. However, a key challenge identified is the low utilisation of CDS tools across the clinic, with usage accounting for <5% of total encounters in 2022 and 2023. According to internal CUHCC quality monitoring data, the clinic’s overall depression screening and follow-up rate was 48% in 2022 and 45% in 2023, reflecting broader institutional efforts. However, this study specifically examines the association between CDS usage in depression screening and follow-up and the RR of a patient being seen by a provider using CDS tools meeting HRSA standards. This limited adoption may be particularly relevant in primary care settings, where providers may not have the time or resources to conduct comprehensive screenings for every patient. Qualitative findings further highlight that primary care staff at FQHCs encounter multiple barriers to CDS implementation, including depression screening during interpreted visits and competing priorities of patient engagement and assessment. In this way, the two strands provide complementary perspectives: while the quantitative analysis demonstrates a positive association between CDS use and adherence to HRSA guidelines, it does not explain why overall adoption of CDS tools remained so limited. The qualitative findings help fill this gap by identifying the practical challenges and provider perceptions underlying low utilisation. Together, these insights enrich our interpretation of CDS implementation and point to areas for improvement. The findings align with the previous literature. In their evaluation study, Gard and Wessel22 posit that while CDS tools demonstrated positive usability across several principles, clinician users reported challenges in integrating these tools into their workflow due to the length of forms.

Based on a critical evaluation and analysis of the project aimed at improving depression screening and follow-up guided by HRSA recommendations, several conclusions can be drawn. First, the data show that CDS usage during depression screening and monitoring varied across clinician titles. Instead, the analysis suggests that effective depression screening might be more influenced by comprehensive previsit planning and iterative communication among healthcare professionals. These factors likely play a pivotal role in facilitating successful depression screening and follow-up practices, underscoring the importance of a holistic approach to patient care over the isolated use of technological aids. While there were some correlations observed between the use of depression screening and follow-up applications and the likelihood of patients seen for a provider visit meeting HRSA depression screening and follow-up guidelines, overall usage rates remain very low.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the available variables capturing both provider and patient characteristics were limited, restricting our ability to control for potential confounding factors and thereby affecting the explanatory power of our findings. Second, due to operational and logistical constraints, we were only able to interview a small number of providers, with an average interview duration of <25 min. This limitation arose because interviews were conducted during office hours, which often overlapped with patient encounters and other professional commitments, limiting participants’ availability. Third, because interviews were not audio-recorded, analysis relied on detailed notes. Given the brevity of the interviews (typically under 20 min) and the research team’s familiarity with the clinical context, note-based analysis was deemed appropriate and efficient. To enhance trustworthiness, notes were immediately reviewed and discussed among team members. Nonetheless, the absence of verbatim transcripts may have limited the depth of qualitative analysis in capturing subtle nuances. Fourth, the quantitative analysis was conducted using data from a single FQHC in Minnesota. Because the sample was drawn from one site using a non-probability approach, the findings cannot be assumed to be representative of other FQHCs or healthcare settings. Lastly, as a single case study, this study provides valuable insights into the use of CDS and its relationship with adherence to depression screening and follow-up guidelines, but its findings are best interpreted as context-dependent rather than universally generalisable.

Conclusion

Depression screening remains underperformed in primary care settings. Coordinated interventions and brief screening instruments are imperative to improve the recognition of depression.36 While our quantitative findings indicate that CDS use improves adherence to HRSA guidelines in depression screening and follow-up, qualitative findings reveal barriers and limited uptake. Collectively, our findings suggest that integrating CDS tools into primary care workflows can be valuable but must be supported by comprehensive training and organisational change to be fully effective in clinical practice.22 As demonstrated in this case study, health centres can leverage internal assessments to refine CDS implementation and better integrate it into the clinical workflows. It is worth pursuing future research, which explores the extent to which care staff conduct depression screening and follow-up, as well as how CDS can enhance both the efficiency and effectiveness of these processes. Moreover, discussions should centre around strategies to increase CDS adoption among care providers, particularly in light of the underwhelming usage rates. Beyond provider-centred implementation, future studies should also prioritise capturing patients’ perspectives and needs to gain a more nuanced understanding of their experiences with depression screening and follow-up.
