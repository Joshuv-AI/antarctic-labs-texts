Structural dissection of CD38 antigen engagement by CAR binders and rational affinity tuning
Authors: Zelin Cheng, Liangminghui Zhang, Ze Liang, Qianping Huang, Ziwei Tang, Xinyu Shi, Lili Liu, Guang Yang, Lei Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy uses synthetic receptors to direct T cells to target and lyse cancer cells. CD38 is a multifunctional ectoenzyme involved in immunomodulation and a therapeutic target in hematological malignancies. Here, we report structural and functional characterization of two CD38-targeting binders, RP02 and 028, revealing distinct mechanisms of epitope engagement and enzymatic inhibition. Crystal structures demonstrate that RP02 binds the N-lobe of CD38 via VH-mediated interactions, while 028 spans both N- and C-lobes, inducing allosteric inhibition. Alanine scanning identified critical residues for affinity tuning. Functional assays showed 028 potently inhibits CD38’s cyclase activity, whereas RP02 has minimal effect, correlating with 028’s occlusion of the catalytic pocket via η6 loop-mediated dimerization. Further, CAR-T cells engineered with affinity-attenuated 028R103G exhibited reduced fratricide while retaining cytotoxicity against CD38+ tumors. Our work delineates structure-guided strategies to optimize CD38-targeted therapeutics by balancing affinity, inhibition, and cellular selectivity.Subject areas: natural sciences, biological sciences, biochemistry

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) are synthetic receptors that reprogram immune effector cells to recognize specific antigens, enabling targeted antitumor responses.1 These transmembrane constructs typically consist of an extracellular single-chain variable fragment (scFv) that binds a tumor-associated surface antigen, linked to intracellular signaling domains designed to activate T cell effector functions (Figure 1A).1,2,3 CARs targeting the plasma cell marker CD38 have emerged as promising therapies for multiple myeloma and other hematologic malignancies.4,5 CD38-directed CAR therapy represents a paradigm shift in treating refractory plasma cell dyscrasias.6 Based on current clinical research dynamics and database statistics (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov), CD38 has indeed emerged as a highly regarded hotspot target in CAR-T therapy, ranking second only to established targets such as CD19 and BCMA in research activity, while no CD38-specific CAR-T products are yet FDA-approved.

Clinically, autologous T cells are engineered to express CARs ex vivo before reinfusion into patients.7 A persistent challenge in CAR development is optimizing scFv affinity and specificity.3,8 High-affinity binders may cause on-target/off-tumor toxicity by engaging antigen on healthy tissues (e.g., hematopoietic precursors),9,10,11 whereas low-affinity binders fail to trigger sufficient T cell activation.12,13,14 For CD38, highly expressed on malignant plasma cells, affinity tuning is particularly critical due to its broad expression on immune subsets.9 Recent studies demonstrate that moderate-affinity anti-CD38 CARs enhance tumor selectivity while reducing fratricide and exhaustion.15 Additionally, trogocytosis (antigen transfer from target to effector cells) exacerbates CAR-T dysfunction tumors by promoting fratricidal killing, with antigen density and scFv affinity modulating this process.11,16,17,18

The RP02 antibody, originating from a screening campaign utilizing CD38 antigen density-based panning, was engineered into CAR-T cells that demonstrated selective cytotoxicity against tumor cells with high CD38 expression.15 In contrast, the 028 antibody, despite its potent antitumor activity, lacks the ability to discriminate between high and low CD38-expressing cells. This lack of selectivity results in on-target off-tumor effects against normal cells, such as PBMCs.15,19 Here, we elucidate the structural basis of CD38 recognition by two novel binders, RP02 and 028, through X-ray crystallography. RP02 engages the N-lobe with minimal enzymatic inhibition, whereas 028 spans both lobes, mimicking Clade III antibodies such as Isatuximab to allosterically disrupt catalysis. We further identify critical binding residues via mutagenesis and demonstrate that affinity tuning of 028 enables the generation of CAR-T cells with reduced self-lysis while preserving antitumor activity. Our findings provide a framework for designing next-generation CD38 therapeutics with tailored functional properties.

Results

Structures of RP02 and 028 in complex with soluble CD38

To determine structures of the CD38-RP02 and CD38-028 complexes, we expressed and purified both binders alongside soluble CD38 (sCD38) in Expi293 cells. RP02 was retained as an scFv to preserve binding integrity, whereas 028 was converted to a Fab fragment, increasing its molecular mass from ∼25 to ∼50 kDa to facilitate crystallization.

We first characterized the CD38-RP02complex (Figure S1A) and CD38-028 (Figure S1C) via preparative size exchange chromatography (SEC). Diffraction-quality crystals of both complexes were ultimately obtained through extensive screening (Figures S1B and S1D) and finally resolved to 2.72 Å and 3.15 Å, respectively (Table S1). Structural analysis revealed both binders engage the membrane-distal region of the CD38 ectodomain, distal to its catalytic center (Figure 1A). Upon binding, RP02 buried 552.0 Å2 of surface area (BSA) (Figures 1B and 1C). Structural alignment further demonstrated a 90° clockwise rotation of 028 relative to RP02 on the CD38 ectodomain when viewed extracellularly (Figure 1D). In contrast, 028 exhibited a larger total BSA (649.6 Å2), comprising 373.2 Å2 from its heavy chain and 171.7 Å2 from its light chain (Figure 1E). To compare the binding modes of RP02 and 028, the CD38 protein of the CD38-RP02 complex was superimposed onto the CD38-028 complex. Although they recognize different epitopes on CD38, RP02 and 028 binding to CD38 exhibited a steric collision with overlap between RP02-VL and 028-VH (Figure S2). Overall, RP02 primarily targets the N-lobe of CD38, whereas 028 recognizes a broader epitope spanning both the N- and C-lobes.

Detailed interactions between CD38 and RP02 or 028

CD38-RP02 complex demonstrated VH-mediated anchoring through HFW1/HFW2/CDRH3 loops, while VL established contacts via CDRL3 (Figures 2A–2E and S3A). Specifically, VL domain residues engage CD38 helix α1 (residues 149–162) through CDRL3 (Figure 2B), whereas VH residues bind loop η1 (residues 195–207) via HFW1/HFW2/CDRH3 and η3 (residues 260–272) through CDRH3 (Figures 2C–2E). Intriguingly, R78 of CD38 exhibits a notable binding mode, forming extensive hydrogen bonds with surrounding residues and four pairs of salt bridges (R78 [NE] with E51 [OE1] and [OE2] of RP02; R78 [NH2] with E51 [OE1] and [OE2] of RP02) (Figure 2D; Table S2). Moreover, the side chain of R78 also engages in cation-π, NH-π, and π-π interactions with the aromatic rings of the W34HFW1 and W92LFW3 side chains (Figure 2E). The single mutation R78G in CD38 completely abolishes binding to RP02 at the tested concentrations (12.5–200 nM; Figure 2F).

Similar to RP02, 028 primarily engages CD38 through VH domain interactions, with CDRH3 dominating the binding interface across three key structural elements η1 (residue 95); η3 (residue 202); η4 (residues 116–120) (Figure 2G). Briefly, S30CDRH1 interacts with the main-chain carbonyl group of P95η1. VL domain contributions were limited to CDRL1 contacting η3 (residue 202) and η5 (residue 233–234) (Figures 2G and S3B). In detail, the side-chain NH2 group of R30CDRL1 donates hydrogen bonds to the main-chain carbonyl oxygen atoms of E233η3 and K234η3 within CD38’s η3 β-sheet (Figure 2H). Additionally, the benzopyrrole NH2 group of W32CDRL1 hydrogen-bonds with D202η3, which concurrently forms a salt bridge with the guanidinium group of R103CDRH3 (Figure 2H). Within the η4 β-sheet of CD38, the side chains of T116 and N120 form hydrogen bonds with the main-chain carbonyl groups of P100CDRH3 and D104CDRH3, respectively, while C119η4 engages E102CDRH3 via main-chain interaction (Figure 2I). Specific interaction details are provided in Table S3.

Affinity tuning of RP02 and 028

Based on the CD38-RP02 structure, we designed seven RP02 mutants in which residues W34, E99, Y101, Y102, Y103, or Y104 were mutated to alanine or glycine, compared with wide-type RP02 (Figures 3A–3H). Among these, W34 was identified as a critical residue for RP02 binding (Figure 3B). This finding aligns with our structural analysis, which revealed that W34 contacts R78 of CD38 via cation-π, NH-π, or π-π interactions (Figure 2E). We reasoned that mutating Y101 and Y103 to alanine would substantially disrupt each tyrosine’s interactions with CD38 (Figures 3E and 3G), without significantly perturbing the overall protein backbone geometry (Figures 2C and 2D). In contrast, the E51A, E99A, and Y102A mutations only slightly altered binding affinities (Figures 3C, 3D, and 3F).

On the other side, through alanine-scanning mutagenesis of residues involved in CD38-028 binding, we substituted key interacting residues with alanine or glycine. The W32A mutation maintained wild-type (WT) affinity (mean KD = 0.73 nM vs. WT 0.21 nM), whereas R103G and R30A substitutions reduced mean binding affinity to nanomolar levels (R103G: 1.58 nM; R30A: 1.74 nM, respectively) (Figures 3I–3L). These observations highlight the differential contributions of individual residues to the CD38-028 interaction (Figures 2G–2I), validating the feasibility of affinity tuning through rational mutagenesis.

Enzymatic activity

CD38 is an ecto-enzyme with both cyclase and hydrolase activity.19,20,21 It catalyzes the conversion of NAD+ or NADP+ into the Ca2+-mobilizing metabolites cADPR, ADPR, or NAADP.21 Furthermore, binding of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) to CD38 has been shown to modulate its cyclase and hydrolase activities.22,23 According to prior reports, Isatuximab potently inhibited both the cyclase and hydrolase activities of CD38, whereas Daratumumab showed no detectable inhibition.23,24

We evaluated the inhibitory effects of antibodies RP02 (as scFv-Fc) and 028 (as IgG1) against the ADP-ribosyl cyclase activity of human CD38 in vitro, using NGD as a surrogate substrate for NAD. Over the 30-min reaction period, RP02 exhibited negligible inhibitory activity at stoichiometric ratios (RP02:CD38) ranging from 0 to 0.5 (Figure 4A). Inhibitory effects became apparent only when the stoichiometric ratio exceeded 4. It is noteworthy that at a ratio of 40, the reaction ceased almost completely within 10 min (Figure 4A). In contrast, antibody 028 potently inhibited the enzymatic activity of 5 nmol/L recombinant CD38, achieving near-complete inhibition at 200 and 20 nmol/L (Figure 4B). A human IgG1 isotype control antibody showed no measurable inhibition in these assays (Figure 4C). The inhibitory effect was comparable to the significant inhibition induced by HexaBody-CD38 (a hexameric assembly based on antibody 028) in the literature.25 In experiments where the reaction time was extended to 60 min, we measured substrate generation at 10, 20, 30, and 60-min time points. The inhibition curves generated against antibody concentration showed that the inhibitory effect of RP02 became gradually apparent over time (Figure S4A). In contrast, antibody 028 exhibited a complete inhibition curve at all four time points, with an IC50 below 10 nM (Figure S4B). To obtain a saturated inhibition curve for RP02, a reaction time of 120 min was ultimately selected. Therefore, the IC50 values of hIgG1, RP02, 028, and a reported CD38 inhibitor 78c (as positive control)26 were further evaluated at this time point (Figure 4D). The IC50 of RP02 was determined to be approximately 22.7 nM, while those of 028 and 78c were 2.1 nM and 26.9 nM, respectively (Figure 4D). Notably, the IC50 for 028 was consistent with the value measured in the earlier time course (Figure S4B).

Structural basis for enzymatic activity inhibition by RP02

Structural superposition of CD38-RP02 with the CD38-NAD complex reveals a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 1.24 Å for overall Cαs of CD38, indicating maintenance of an open conformation in the substrate-binding pocket (Figure 5A). The catalytic and substrate-binding residues (W125, E146, W189, and E226) occupy conserved positions (Figure 5B). Notably, conformational rearrangements of the catalytic and substrate-binding residues occur in response to RP02 binding, as opposed to substrate binding, evidenced by the formation of D219-R177 electrostatic pair (Figure 5B). By presenting adjacent symmetry mates, we found that two neighboring CD38 molecules form a highly suspicious compact dimer mediated by η6 loop-mediated dimerization (Figures 5C and 5D). Electron density maps revealed unambiguous density in the catalytic pocket that was definitively assigned to residues 244HGGREDSR251 of the η6 loop (β5-α8 connector in the C-lobe) from a symmetry-related molecule, rather than bound ligands, following iterative refinement (Figure 5E). The η6 loop completely occupies the substrate-binding pocket, engaging W176 and key substrate-binding residues W125 and W189, thereby sterically blocking substrate access (Figure 5F). 247RED249 of CD38′ η6 loop forms extensive interactions with surrounding residues W125CD38, T221CD38, and W189CD38 for R247CD38’, W176CD38 for E248CD38’ and R177CD38 for D249 CD38’ (Figures 5E and 5F).

CD38 oligomerization has been well characterized, with two core structural models established. First, noncovalent homodimers on murine B cell surfaces rely on the cytoplasmic region and α1-helix (e.g., residue G68 in the α1-helix) for stability, which is preserved by polyoxyethylene detergents (NP-40, Triton X-100) rather than interdisulfide bonds.27 These dimers regulate CD38’s plasma membrane half-life but are dispensable for its NAD+ glycohydrolase activity. Second, tetramers in mouse CD38 form via a “dimer-of-dimers” mechanism: the type I interface (α1-helix interactions) forms the core dimer, while type II/III interfaces (involving α2-helix, α4-helix, α9-helix, and β5-strand/α8-helix loops) assemble core dimers into tetramers.28 Tetramerization is critical for catalytic activity and membrane raft association, with glycosylation preventing excessive aggregation via the type IV interface. Notably, neither model identifies the η6 loop as a dimerization mediator—prior interfaces depend on α-helices and specific inter-domain loops, not the η6 loop. This suggests the η6 loop-mediated dimer represents a novel interaction mode, potentially differing in functional relevance (e.g., catalytic regulation, membrane localization) from previously described α-helix/loop-dependent oligomers. Whether this η6 loop interface is conserved across species or restricted to specific CD38 isoforms requires further investigation.

Molecular dynamics simulations reveal impaired dimer stability due to interfacial mutation

To elucidate the structural impact of key interfacial mutations of the η6 loop, we performed 100-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations comparing WT CD38 with the R247A/E248A/D249A triple-mutant dimer. The simulations revealed that, although the WT dimer exhibited a higher absolute RMSD, its fluctuation amplitude was markedly lower than that of the mutant, indicating a more compact and conformationally stable assembly (Figure S5A). This stability was further reflected in smaller fluctuations in the inter-subunit distance over time, particularly between 20 and 60 ns, whereas the mutant displayed pronounced variability consistent with impaired dimer integrity (Figure S5B). While the overall compactness—assessed by radius of gyration—remained similar between the two (Figure S5C), the mutant showed a clear reduction in the number of interfacial hydrogen bonds (Figure S5D), directly weakening subunit interactions. Visualization of the dimer interface confirmed that this reduction stemmed from the disruption of a specific network of hydrogen-bonding residue pairs, which are abundant in the WT but drastically diminished in the mutant (Figures S6A and S6B).

Binding free energy calculations quantitatively supported these observations: The mutant dimer exhibited a less favorable inter-subunit affinity (−54.02 kcal/mol) compared to the WT (−61.82 kcal/mol) (Table S4). Together, these data demonstrate that the R247A/E248A/D249A mutations disrupt critical hydrogen-bond networks at the dimer interface, thereby reducing structural stability and subunit binding affinity—a molecular insight that correlates with the attenuated function observed in the corresponding CAR-T variant.

Structural basis for enzymatic activity inhibition by 028

Concurrently with our investigation, the co-crystal structure of 028-Fab bound to CD38 was serendipitously reported (Figures 6A and 6C).25 Independent validation confirms the structural integrity of the CD38-028 binding interface. As reported by Hiemstra and colleagues, the CD38-028 antibody complex structure resembles the enzyme’s catalytically competent state (Figure 6A). This is supported by the structural superposition with NAD+-bound CD38, which shows that the catalytic pocket in the reported 028-bound structure can fully accommodate the NAD+ molecule (Figure 6B).25 The RMSD between the NAD+-bound CD38 and the reported 028-bound structure is 1.13 Å. In comparison, the superposition of our 028-bound structure with NAD+-bound CD38 yields an RMSD of 1.59 Å. Thereby, we have identified a structural state where the catalytic pocket is disrupted (Figure 6C). Initial destabilization of the α4 helix triggers a substantial conformational rearrangement in the α5-β2 connecting loop, induced by steric occupancy of a chlorine atom (Figure 6C). This results in the closure of the catalytic pocket (Figure 6D). The alignment of CD38 structures showed a moderate backbone divergence (Cα RMSD = 1.63 Å, residues 49–283), primarily arising from conformational flexibility in three functionally important motifs (Figure 6E). The observed 7.8 Å Cα movement of I128 in the α4-β2 connecting loop results in the occlusion of the substrate entry channel (Figure 6F). Our structural analysis suggests that 028 binding allosterically perturbs the catalytic pocket, resulting in substrate-binding site destabilization.

Comparison of the binding modes of CD38-targeting antibodies

Structural analysis of CD38-antibody complexes revealed three distinct epitope clusters, enabling classification into functional clades (Figures S7 and S8). Firstly, Clade I antibodies (FLT004, E2-RecA, and RP02) engage the N-lobe through membrane-proximal interactions, while Clade II members (Daratumumab, B6-D9) exclusively bind the C-lobe with Daratumumab’s distinctive binding pose, suggesting steric blockade of substrate access.24,29,30 Notably, Clade III antibodies (Isatuximab, 028) span both lobes, forming an extensive interface that induces the allosteric inhibition of the enzymatic pocket, a structural advantage explaining their superior (80–90%) activity suppression compared to the partial (40–60%) inhibition seen with Clade I/II agents.23

This universal targeting of dorsum epitopes distal to the catalytic site likely reflects evolutionary constraints against direct catalytic interference, though it creates an unexpected limitation in current therapeutic development: Despite diverse origins, all clinically developed CD38 antibodies converge on just three epitope hotspots, leaving potentially valuable targeting opportunities unexplored, particularly at the catalytic pocket itself and oligomerization interfaces. The structural clustering observed suggests that future antibody engineering should prioritize either combining Clade I + II mechanisms to mimic Clade III efficacy while minimizing resistance risks, or breaking from tradition by developing first-in-class antibodies targeting CD38’s underexplored functional sites.

Characterization of CAR T cells incorporating 028 binder mutants

To overcome adenosine-mediated tumor immunosuppression, we developed an affinity-optimized CAR based on the 028 binder that inhibits CD38 enzymatic activity while preserving tumor selectivity.6,31 A key challenge in such therapies is CAR-T cell fratricide due to shared antigen expression, which current strategies address through antigen knockout, multi-antigen sensing CARs, or transient expression platforms.32,33,34,35,36 Advances in in vivo CAR-T delivery, design, and protocols have improved clinical feasibility.37,38,39 Building on this, we propose that precision affinity tuning of our CD38-targeting CAR can concurrently enable tumor-selective recognition via antigen density discrimination, prevent fratricide through optimized activation thresholds, and support sustained in vivo persistence—critical features for effective adoptive cell therapy.

Through our mutational screen, we identified candidate 028 derivatives (028R103G and 028S30A) with attenuated affinity for CD38 relative to the WT binder (Figures 3I–3L). Initial characterization of CAR-T cells incorporating RP02, WT 028, and its mutants was performed following T cell activation (Figures S9 and S10). Hereafter, we refer to the WT construct as 028 CAR-T and the affinity-attenuated mutant as R103 CAR-T; data for 028S30A are omitted due to suboptimal performance across assays. Flow cytometry confirmed the surface expression of CD38 and the CAR itself. R103 CAR-T exhibited a higher transduction efficiency (75.5%) than 028 CAR-T (53.1%) (Figure 7A, left; Figures S10A and S10C). Notably, while 028 CAR-T showed substantial CD38 shedding, R103 CAR-T maintained high CD38 surface levels, comparable to Mock and RP02 controls (Figure 7A, right). Furthermore, R103 CAR-T displayed higher CD69 expression (74.5% vs. 67% for 028) (Figures S10B and S10D), indicating a distinct early activation profile.

Longitudinal immunophenotyping over 12 days revealed that the enhanced early activation of R103 CAR-T was coupled with reduced apoptosis during the first week post-transduction (Figures S11A–S11D). By day 12, however, differences in CD25 and CD69 expression, as well as apoptosis levels (assessed by Annexin V/7-AAD), had equilibrated between the two groups, suggesting a convergence in phenotypic states over extended culture.

To evaluate functional potency, we assessed cytotoxicity against target cells with varying CD38 densities: RPMI 8226 (multiple myeloma, CD38+), Daudi (Burkitt lymphoma, CD38+), and K562 (CML, CD38−) (Figure S12A). This analysis revealed that the CD38 antigen density threshold required to trigger cytolysis differed between 028 and R103 CAR-T, indicating that affinity tuning directly shapes the antigen-sensitivity window of CAR-T cells (Figures 7B–7D).

A critical challenge for CD38-directed CAR-T therapy is fratricide due to shared antigen expression on effector T cells. Following co-culture of different groups of CAR-T or mock-T cells with PBMCs, the proportion of CD3-positive cells in the 028 group decreased (76.8% versus almost 98% of other groups), while the percentage of CD38 and CD3 double-positive cells remained unchanged, suggesting potential fratricide. In contrast, our previous report indicated that the CD38 and CD3 double-positive population was almost undetectable.15 A possible explanation is that in the earlier experiment, PBMCs were irradiated to arrest proliferation, whereas in the current study, irradiation was not performed. No significant differences were observed in the other CAR-T groups (r103 and rp02) or the mock-T group. (Figure 7E), confirming that affinity attenuation reduces self-lysis. A quantitative viability assay (CAR-T cells co-cultured with irradiated CD38+ T cells) confirmed severe fratricide in 028 CAR-T cells (32 ± 4% viability) versus preserved viability in R103 CAR-T cells (78 ± 6%), comparable to controls.

Discussion

Our study delineates the structural basis of CD38 recognition by two distinct CAR binders, RP02 and 028, and demonstrates the utility of structure-guided affinity tuning for optimizing CD38-targeted CAR T cell therapy. The divergent binding modes of these binders, RP02’s N-lobe engagement and 028’s cross-lobe interaction, translate to profound differences in enzymatic inhibition and CAR T cell functional outcomes, offering critical insights for next-generation therapeutic design.

RP02 is a Clade I CD38 antibody that binds the N-lobe primarily via residue R78, minimally perturbing the catalytic pocket, similar to other Clade I antibodies such as FTL004 and E2-RecA.29,30 Structural analysis shows that RP02 does not block the substrate-binding site; rather, at high concentrations, its bivalency induces dimerization that leads to time-dependent enzymatic inhibition, a mechanism analogous to allosteric regulation in other CD38 complexes.23 Deep mutational scanning confirms the essential role of R78, as an R78G mutation completely abolishes binding, mirroring resistance mechanisms observed in CD19 CAR-T therapy.40 This finding suggests that epitope-based editing at CD38-R78G could be explored as a strategy to generate universal off-the-shelf immunotherapies, building on the principle successfully applied to CD45 to evade CAR-T recognition while preserving protein function.41

In contrast, antibody 028 belongs to Clade III, binding both the N- and C-lobes of CD38 to induce allosteric inhibition, similar to Isatuximab.23 Its binding induces a conformational change that occludes the substrate entry channel—distinct from the direct active-site blockade used by Clade II antibodies such as daratumumab.24 028 exhibits potent cyclase inhibition (IC50 = 2.1 nM), consistent with the characteristically high (80–90%) activity suppression of Clade III antibodies, outperforming Clade I/II agents (40–60%). Our identification of R103 as a key affinity-determining residue enabled the engineering of the affinity-tuned variant 028R103G. The mutant exhibits intermediate affinity and balanced selectivity: It avoids the fratricide associated with high-affinity binding on CD38-expressing T cells, while maintaining cytotoxicity against high CD38-expressing tumors such as RPMI 8226 and Daudi cells. This principle, whereby single HCDR3 mutations modulate off-rate and alter binding energy, as demonstrated for CD19 and CD229 or EGFR binders, underpins the design of lower-affinity CAR T cells, which achieve enhanced tumor selectivity, reduced off-tumor toxicity, and decreased trogocytosis-mediated antigen loss.14,40,41

Structural analysis delineates that CD38 antibodies segregate into three functional clades, with all agents developed for clinical and preclinical application clustering within just three epitope hotspots.23,24,25,29,30 This pattern of epitope conservation parallels that observed for CD19, where therapeutic binders such as FMC63 and SJ25C1 also target overlapping regions.40 This epitope convergence likely arises from evolutionary constraints favoring membrane-distal recognition, yet it also increases susceptibility to antigen escape, a growing concern in CD38-targeted therapies.42 Our work suggests this limitation could be addressed by combining Clade I and II binding mechanisms or targeting novel, underexplored epitopes, a strategy akin to that proposed for bispecific CARs engaging both CD19 and CD20 or CD22.43,44,45

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the in vitro characterization of CAR T cells should be complemented by in vivo models to evaluate persistence and antitumor efficacy in a physiological context. Second, the role of CD38 dimerization in enzymatic inhibition requires further investigation, as η6 loop-mediated oligomerization may contribute to both on-target efficacy and off-tumor toxicity. Third, we focused on two binders, and expanding to a broader panel of CD38-targeted antibodies could reveal additional structural determinants of affinity and inhibition. Despite these limitations, our work provides a framework for structure-guided optimization of CD38-targeted therapeutics, which can be extended to other CAR targets such as HER2 and mesothelin.

In summary, we demonstrate that rational affinity attenuation, as achieved with the R103 mutation, can overcome fratricide in CD38-targeting CAR-T cells. Our integrated structural and functional analysis reveals how this strategy decouples antitumor potency from self-reactivity by raising the activation threshold, thereby preserving T cell function and viability. This work validates affinity-based tuning as a critical strategy to enhance the therapeutic window of lineage-targeted CAR-T therapies and provides a framework for targeting multifunctional antigens such as CD38.

Limitations of the study

Functional evaluation of CAR-T cells has been primarily confined to in vitro experiments. Although the R103G mutation reduces homologous killing while maintaining antitumor activity, these findings require validation in animal models, as the in vivo tumor microenvironment may influence therapeutic efficacy and persistence. We observed η6-ring-mediated dimerization in the CD38-RP02 crystal structure and hypothesized its role in time-dependent enzyme inhibition. However, this interface formed by crystal symmetry requires validation under physiological membrane conditions. Our alanine scanning targeted only a limited set of interface residues, potentially overlooking other affinity-modulating sites. Comprehensive approaches, such as deep mutation scanning, may identify superior variants. As an early proof-of-concept study, clinical translation requires systematic off-target assessment, long-term safety and persistence studies, scalable manufacturing processes, and careful evaluation of CD38’s broad expression across multiple immune cell types.

Experimental model and study participant details

All cell lines have been authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination.

No animals or human participants were involved in this study.

Cell culture

HEK293T (human embryonic kidney cells with SV40 T-antigen) were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, #10270106) and 2 mM L-glutamine at 37°C under 5% CO2. Cells were passaged every 3-4 days at 80–90% confluency using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (ThermoFisher, #25200056) and seeded at 2.5-5×104 cells/cm. Expi293 cells (ThermoFisher, #A14527) were maintained in serum-free Expi293 Expression Medium (Gibco, #A1435101) in baffled shake flasks (Corning, #444024303) at 37°C, 8% CO2, with 125 rpm orbital agitation. Cell density was kept at 0.5-5×106 cells/mL, and viability >95% was ensured via trypan blue exclusion. For transfection, cells were diluted to 2.0×106 cells/mL and transfected using linear 25 kDa polyethylenimine (PEI; Polysciences, #24765-1) at a DNA: PEI ratio of 1:2.5 (w/w).

RPMI-8226-luci, Daudi-luci, Raji-luci, or K562-luci cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, and 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol, as previously.15 Cultures were maintained at densities of 0.2-1×106 cells/mL in T-25 flasks and passaged every 2-3 days by 1:5 dilution in fresh medium.

Method details

Protein expression and purification of recombinant CD38

The method of expression and purification of non-glycosylated extracelluar domain of CD38 (ngCD38ecd) was modified from previously published paper.23 Briefly, human CD38 extracellular domain R45-I300 (UniProt: P28907) with N100D, N164A, N209D and N219D mutation for preventing potential N-glycosylation, followed by a hexahistidine tag in the C-terminus was produced by transient transfection of expression vector pFUSE-hIgG1-Fc1 (InvivoGen, # pfuse-hg1fc1) into Exp293F cells for 3 days. The supernatant was harvested and loaded onto a Ni Sepharose excel column equilibrated with PBS supplemented with 5 mM Imidazole. The column was washed with the same buffer and the bound protein was eluted with 500 mM Imidazole in PBS. The elution fractions containing ngCD38ecd were pooled and dialyzed against 50 mM sodium acetate buffer at pH 4.05. The dialyzed sample was loaded onto a SP Sepharose HP column (Cytiva) equilibrated with the same buffer. The column was eluted with a linear salt gradient (0-1 M NaCl). Fractions containing ngCD38ecd were pooled and purified on Superdex 200 (Cytiva) followed by SAS-PAGE evaluation.

An expression plasmid encoding amino acids 44-300 of human CD38, fused to a C-terminal hexahistidine (His6) tag, was constructed. The recombinant CD38-His protein was expressed in HEK-293T cells via transient transfection using PEI. The culture supernatant was subsequently purified by immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) using Ni-Excel resin (Cytiva). Protein concentration was determined by measuring absorbance at 280 nm (A280), with the molar extinction coefficient calculated using the ExPASy ProtParam tool.

Protein expression and purification of antibodies and fragments

The gene encoding anti-CD38 antibody RP02 in the form of scFv was also cloned into the vector pFUSE-hIgG1-Fc1 (InvivoGen, #pfuse-hg1fc1) in frame and downstream of a signal peptide (MYRMQLLSCIALSLALVTNS from the human IL2 gene) followed by Fc domain in the C-terminus. Then, the plasmid was transiently transfected in Exp293F cells for 4 days and the supernatant was harvested by centrifuge. Finally, RP02 (scFv-Fc) was purified by Protein A affinity chromatography (Cytiva). The column was washed with PBS to remove unbound contaminants, and the target protein was eluted using a 0.1 M glycine-HCl buffer (pH 2.5). The eluate was immediately neutralized and concentrated, with the buffer exchanged into PBS for storage.

To produce the full-length IgG format of antibody 028, its heavy and light chains were individually cloned into vector pFUSE-hIgG1-Fc1. The resulting plasmids were then co-transfected into Expi293F cells for transient expression. The antibody was subsequently harvested from the supernatant and purified via Protein A affinity chromatography, following a methodology analogous to that described above.

scFv and Fab fragments were prepared by digesting scFv-Fc or IgG antibodies with papain. Briefly, purified antibody was incubated with papain enzyme in the presence of reducing agents (e.g., cysteine or DTT) to activate protease activity. The reaction was typically carried out at 37°C for 1-4 hours in a buffer containing EDTA (e.g., PBS pH 7.4), followed by termination with iodoacetamide alkylation. Fc fragments and undigested IgG were removed by Protein A affinity chromatography to obtain purified scFv or Fab fragments.

Crystallization

The 1:1.2 mixture of purified ngCD38ecd and the RP02-scFv was incubated for 1 h at 4°C, and further purified by gel filtration chromatography using a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva). Gel-filtration fractions containing the complex of ngCD38ecd and RP02-scFv were concentrated to 15 mg/mL in 20 mM Tris, pH 8.0, and 50 mM NaCl. A similar procedure was applied to the preparation of the ngCD38ecd and 028-Fab complex for crystallization.

Crystals of CD38-RP02 and CD38-028 were grown by the hanging-drop vapor-diffusion method at 20°C within one week, with a well solution containing (0.1 M Bis-Tris, pH 6.5, 0.2 M lithium sulfate, and 25% PEG 3350) and (0.2 M lithium nitrate, and 20% PEG3350), respectively. Before X-ray diffraction experiment, crystals were cryoprotected by brief immersion in a solution containing corresponding mother liquid plus 20% glycerol.

Data collection and structure determination

X-ray diffraction data for the CD38-RP02 and CD38-028 complex were collected at 100 K on beamLine BL02U1 at SSRF, then integrated and scaled using HKL3000.46 The structure of CD38-RP02 was determined by molecular replacement in Phenix,47 employing the CD38 structure from the CD38-SAR650984 Fab complex (PDB code 4CMH) and an antibody model generated by Swiss Model as search models.23,50 The structure of the CD38-028 complex was similarly determined using the SAR650984 Fab and CD38 structures from the CD38-SAR650984 Fab complex as search models.23 Due to the intrinsic flexibility of the elbow region of an antibody Fab, the variable and constant regions within the Fab of the search model were disassembled during molecular replacement. After phasing by molecular replacement, the electron density corresponding to the binding complex of CD38-antibody was obvious. Iterative model building and structure refinement were conducted using COOT and PHENIX.47,48 The figures of the structures were generated using PyMol.49 Statistics for data collection and structure refinement are presented in Table S1. The atomic coordinates and structure factors for the structure of the binding complex of CD38 and RP02 or 028 have been deposited in Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org), under the accession code 8ZYE and 9VXL.

Binding affinity measurement using surface plasmon resonance

The CD38 extracellular domain (CD38ecd) is immobilized onto a flow cell (Fc) of a CM5 sensor chip (Cytiva, # BR100530) via amine coupling on Biacore 8K (Cytiva), while a reference flow cell (Rc) is prepared with activated/blocked dextran only. Analyte samples (RP02 mutants) at various concentrations are sequentially flowed over both the CD38ecd surface (Fc) and the reference surface (Rc). Real-time sensorgrams are recorded to monitor association and dissociation phases. Sensorgrams undergo reference subtraction (Fc-Rc) and buffer blank subtraction. The processed data is globally fitted to a 1:1 binding model using analysis software (Biacore Evaluation Software) to determine the association rate constant (ka), dissociation rate constant (kd), and equilibrium dissociation constant (KD = kd / ka). Strictly defined regeneration conditions are applied to remove bound analyte and regenerate the chip surface between cycles. This method enables rapid and quantitative determination of binding affinity and kinetics between analytes and immobilized CD38ecd.

Binding affinity determination using Biolayer Interferometry

Target binding affinity of human CD38-specific antibodies was determined by Biolayer Interferometry (BLI) on an Octet instrument (FortéBio) while shaking at 1,000 RPM at RT. Ni-NTA biosensors immobilized His-tagged CD38 (20 μg/mL). Antibody affinity (028 wt and mutants) was measured via association (300 s)/dissociation (300 s) in kinetic buffer. Data were globally fitted to a 1:1 model.

CD38 enzymatic activity assay

CD38 is a multifunctional enzyme that catalyzes the metabolism of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) and cyclic adenosine diphosphate ribose (cADPR). Due to the rapid hydrolysis of cADPR, an alternative enzymatic assay was employed using nicotinamide guanine dinucleotide (NGD+) as a substrate instead of NAD+. NGD+ is converted by CD38 into cyclic GDP-ribose (cGDPR) and nicotinamide, followed by the slow hydrolysis of cGDPR to GDPR, as previously reported.51 This slower kinetics allows for the accumulation of cGDPR, enabling real-time monitoring of enzymatic activity via fluorescence detection. To evaluate the inhibitory effects of anti-CD38 antibodies, recombinant CD38 (5 nM) was preincubated with varying concentrations of antibodies (0.2, 2, 20, or 200 nM respectively) for 15 minutes at room temperature (RT) in a black 96-well plate containing reaction buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.0). The enzymatic reaction was initiated by adding NGD+ (Sigma Aldrich) to a final concentration of 80 μM. The plate was incubated in the dark at RT, and cGDPR production was monitored over time by measuring fluorescence (excitation: 300 nm, emission: 410 nm). Three independent experiments were performed, each with duplicate technical replicates. Background fluorescence, determined from wells lacking CD38-His, was subtracted from all measurements. The background-corrected fluorescence (in relative fluorescence units, RFU) was plotted against reaction time for each antibody concentration.

CAR-T cell generation

The second-generation CAR was designed with an anti-CD38 scFv (RP02, 028, or mutants), a CD8α hinge and transmembrane domain (residues 137–209, UniProt: P01732), and intracellular 4-1BB-CD3ζ signaling domains (CD137, residues 214–255, UniProt: Q07011; residues 52–164, UniProt: P20963). The construct included a C-terminal myc-tag (sequence: EQKLISEEDL) for detection and was cloned into a lentiviral vector under the EF-1α promoter. Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells using psPAX2 and pMD2.G plasmids (Addgene, #12260 and #122590) with PEI transfection, harvested after 48–72 h, concentrated by ultracentrifugation, and stored at -80°C. PBMCs were enriched for CD3+ T cells (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-050-101), activated with anti-CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (1:1 ratio, Thermo Fisher, #11161D) in X-VIVO 15 (Lonza, #04-418Q), 10% FBS, and 100 IU/mL IL-2 (novoprotein, #C013), while transduced at MOI 5-10 with 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich, #H9268) via spinoculation (1,000 g, 90 min). After 48-72 h culture, Dynabeads were removed and cells expanded for 7-14 days in IL-2. CAR expression (>30%) was verified by flow cytometry (fluorophore-labeled anti-myc antibody), with functional validation through cytotoxicity.

Analysis of CD38 and CAR expression by flow cytometry

T cells transduced with distinct CAR constructs were harvested and washed twice with cold FACS buffer (e.g., PBS containing 1% BSA or FBS). For cell surface staining, approximately 1×10ˆ6 cells per sample were resuspended in 100 μL FACS buffer and incubated with antibody mixture containing FITC-conjugated anti-human CD38 antibody (BioLegend, #303503) and Myc-Tag (9B11) Mouse mAb (PE Conjugate, Cell Signaling, #3739S) or PE-conjugated Rabbit monoclonal Anti-(G4S)n Linker antibody (SAILY-BIO, #XY-Ab001-100T-PE) at the manufacturer's recommended dilution (or a predetermined optimal dilution, e.g., 1:100) for 30 minutes at 4°C in the dark. Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with cold FACS buffer to remove unbound antibodies, resuspended in a fixed volume of buffer, and analyzed on a flow cytometer (Beckman, CytoFLEX). Data shown are representative of three independent experiments using PBMCs from different healthy donors.

Assessment of CAR-T cell fratricide activity

To assess CAR-mediated fratricide activity against normal T cells, a co-culture assay was established. Briefly, non-transduced allogeneic T cells from healthy donors were isolated. The prepared CAR-T cells (effectors) were co-cultured with the irradiated target T cells at various effector-to-target (E:T=4:1) ratios. After 72 hours, cells were harvested and stained with anti-CD38-PE (BioLegend, #303505) and anti-CD8-Pacific Blue (BioLegend, # 344718) antibodies for 30 minutes at 4°C to distinguish effector from target populations. For flow cytometric analysis, a sequential gating strategy was employed: lymphocytes were first gated based on forward and side scatter, followed by doublet exclusion. The target T cell population was defined as CD8-PE-/CD38-FITC+.

CAR-T cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxic activity of CAR-T cells was evaluated using a sensitive luciferase-based reporter system. Target cell lines (RPMI8226-luci, Daudi-luci and K562-luci) stably transfected with luciferase reporter genes were plated at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates. Cells were maintained in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) under standard culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2).

Three experimental groups were tested: (1) Non-transduced T cells (Mock control), (2) 028 CAR-T cells, (3) R103 CAR-T cells and (4) RP02 CAR-T cells. For the co-culture assays, effector cells were incubated with target cells at the specified E:T ratios, with three replicates per condition. Following incubation period of 12 hours, cytotoxicity was quantified by measuring luciferase activity.

For signal detection, 0.75 mg/mL D-luciferin potassium salt (Yeasen, # 11404ES80) was added as substrate, and luminescence was immediately measured using an EnSpire Multimode Plate Reader (PerkinElmer). The relative luminescence units (RLU) were recorded and normalized to control wells to calculate specific lysis percentages.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation setup and execution

All MD simulations of the wild-type (WT) dimeric CD38 protein and its R247A/E248A/D249A triple mutant were performed using the GROMACS 2024 software suite with the CHARMM36 force field. The TIP3P explicit water model was employed to solvate the protein systems, and periodic boundary conditions were applied in all spatial dimensions.

The simulation workflow consisted of four sequential stages: energy minimization, heating, equilibration, and production simulation. First, heavy atoms of the protein were restrained, and energy minimization of the water molecules was conducted over 10,000 steps, combining 5,000 steps of steepest descent followed by 5,000 steps of conjugate gradient algorithm. After removing the restraints, the entire system underwent an additional 10,000-step energy optimization to eliminate steric clashes and stabilize initial configurations.

Subsequently, the system was gradually heated from 0 K to 300 K over 50 ps. Equilibration was carried out in the NPT ensemble for 50 ps to maintain a constant temperature of 300 K and pressure of 1 atm. Finally, production MD simulations were run for 100 ns in the NPT ensemble with a time step of 2 fs. Trajectory data were saved every 20 ps for subsequent structural and energetic analyses.

Structural and energetic analysis

Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) was calculated after least-squares fitting of the protein backbone to quantify conformational fluctuations over the simulation timeframe. The radius of gyration (Rg) was computed to assess the overall compactness of the dimeric structures. The center-of-mass distance between the two subunits was monitored to evaluate inter-subunit spatial dynamics. Interfacial hydrogen bonds between the two subunits were identified and counted using standard geometric criteria (donor-acceptor distance ≤ 3.5 Å and donor-hydrogen-acceptor angle ≥ 120).

Inter-subunit binding free energies for both the WT and mutant dimers were decomposed into key components (van der Waals, electrostatic, polar solvation, and nonpolar solvation energies) using the molecular mechanics/generalized Born surface area (MM/GBSA) approach, with average values and standard deviations calculated across the equilibrated trajectory segments. Interfacial interacting residues were identified based on proximity and hydrogen-bonding interactions within the dimer interface.

Structural visualization of antigen-antibody interfaces using PyMOL

The antigen-antibody complex was visualized in PyMOL (v2.5 or above).49 The antigen (chain A) was displayed as a solid surface and colored light gray (gray90). The antibody (chains H and L) was shown in cartoon representation and colored by chain. To define the epitope, all antigen residues within 5 Å of the antibody were selected and highlighted as a red molecular surface. Complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of the antibody were either automatically annotated using PyMOL’s built-in antibody detection (command fab) or manually selected based on Kabat/Chothia numbering and displayed as sticks with distinct colors. The view was optimized with ambient light, shadows disabled, and a white background. Final images were rendered with ray tracing for publication.52

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and data reproducibility

All experiments were independently replicated ≥3 times (biological replicates: distinct cell batches or primary T cells from different donors; technical replicates ≥2 per biological replicate). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0: IC50 values via non-linear regression; group differences via two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test (p < 0.05 = significant). Crystallographic data were validated with PHENIX and MolProbity, meeting standard resolution criteria. Raw data (FCS files, sensorgrams, diffraction images) are available upon request for reproducibility.
