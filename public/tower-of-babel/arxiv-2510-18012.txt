Abstract
Let 𝒟 = { D 0 , … , D n − 1 } \mathcal{D}=\{D_{0},\ldots,D_{n-1}\} be a set of n n topological disks in the plane and let 𝒜 := 𝒜 ⁡ ( 𝒟 ) \mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}) be the arrangement induced by 𝒟 \mathcal{D} . For two disks D i , D j ∈ 𝒟 D_{i},D_{j}\in\mathcal{D} , let Δ i ​ j \Delta_{ij} be the number of connected components of D i ∩ D j D_{i}\cap D_{j} , and let Δ := max i , j ⁡ Δ i ​ j \Delta:=\max_{i,j}\Delta_{ij} . We show that the diameter of 𝒢 ∗ \mathcal{G}^{*} , the dual graph of 𝒜 \mathcal{A} , can be bounded as a function of n n and Δ \Delta . Thus, any two points in the plane can be connected by a Jordan curve that crosses the disk boundaries a number of times bounded by a function of n n and Δ \Delta . In particular, for the case of two disks we prove that the diameter of 𝒢 ∗ \mathcal{G}^{*} is at most max ⁡ { 2 , 2 ​ Δ } \max\{2,2\Delta\} and this bound is tight. For the general case of n > 2 n>2 disks, we show that the diameter of 𝒢 ∗ \mathcal{G}^{*} is at most 2 ​ n ​ ( Δ + 1 ) n ⁡ ( n − 1 ) / 2 ​ min ⁡ { n , Δ + 1 } 2n(\Delta+1)^{n(n-1)/2}\min\{n,\Delta+1\} . Introduction

The
arrangement

\mathcal{A}(S)
defined by a set
S
of objects in the plane is the subdivision of the plane into faces, edges, and vertices induced by (the boundaries of) the objects.
Arrangements in the plane and in higher dimensions play a fundamental role in computational geometry, since many geometric problems can be transformed into problems on arrangements.
As a result, the
combinatorial complexity
of arrangements—the total number of vertices, edges, and faces (and higher-dimensional features)—induced by various types of objects has been studied extensively.
These studies concern the complexity of complete arrangements as well as the complexity of various substructures: single cells, many cells, zones, levels, and so on; see the book by Sharir and Agarwal
[
7
]
or the surveys by Agarwal and Sharir
[
1
]
or Halperin and Sharir
[
5
]
.
The known results on arrangements typically deal with constant-complexity objects such as lines, circles, or other objects whose boundaries intersect a bounded number of times. This is not surprising, as the complexity of the arrangement induced by some set of objects obviously cannot be bounded if we do not put a bound on the number of intersections between the boundaries of any two objects in the set.
In this paper we are interested in whether anything meaningful can be said about complexity of an arrangement if the number of boundary intersections of any two objects in the arrangement is not necessarily bounded.

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{D_{0},\ldots,D_{n-1}\}
be a set of
n
topological disks in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
To simplify the presentation we will refer to the objects in
\mathcal{D}
as
disks
, but the reader should keep on mind that the boundaries of the disks in
\mathcal{D}
are arbitrary closed Jordan curves.
In particular, the boundaries
\partial D_{i}
and
\partial D_{j}
of two disks
D_{i},D_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
can intersect arbitrarily many times.
We will assume that the disks in
\mathcal{D}
are in
general position
. More precisely, we assume that
\partial D_{i}\cap\partial D_{j}
consists of finitely many points, that two boundaries properly cross every time they intersect, i.e., there are no tangencies, and that no three disk boundaries have a point in common.

Let
\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})
be the arrangement induced by
\mathcal{D}
.

Let
\Delta_{ij}
denote the number of connected components of the intersection
D_{i}\cap D_{j}
of a pair of objects
D_{i},D_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
, and define
\Delta:=\max_{i,j}\Delta_{ij}
.
We refer to
\Delta
as the
overlap number
of
\mathcal{D}
.

Note that the complexity of
\mathcal{A}
cannot be bounded as a function of
n
and
\Delta
.
Indeed, even for
n=2
and
\Delta=1
, the number of faces in
\mathcal{A}
can be arbitrarily large, as illustrated in Figure
1
(i).

Figure 1
:
(i) A set of two topological disks with
\Delta=1
can induce an arrangement with arbitrarily many faces.
(ii) A set of three topological disks and the dual graph of their arrangement. The arrangement
has four maximal faces, whose dual nodes are shown in black.
The edges incident to the node corresponding to the unbounded face are
only drawn partially, to avoid cluttering the figure.
If the blue disk is
D_{0}
, the red disk is
D_{1}
, and the green disk is
D_{2}
,
then
\Delta_{01}=1
and
\Delta_{02}=\Delta_{12}=3
, so
\Delta=3
.

But are there perhaps other quantities describing how complex the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
can be, yet be bounded in terms of
n
and
\Delta
?

The first quantity we will study is the diameter of the dual graph of the arrangement.
The
dual graph
of the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
is the graph
\mathcal{G}^{*}:=\mathcal{G}^{*}(\mathcal{A})
whose nodes correspond to the faces in
\mathcal{A}
and two nodes in
\mathcal{G}^{*}
are adjacent if and only if the corresponding faces have at least one edge in common; see Fig.
1
(ii).
The
diameter
of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is the maximum hop-distance between any two nodes in
\mathcal{G}^{*}
.

The second quantity of interest is the number of
maximal faces
in
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
ply
of a face in
\mathcal{A}
be the number of disks in
\mathcal{D}
that contain the face.
Two faces are called
neighbors
in
\mathcal{A}
if they have at least one common edge, i.e., if their corresponding nodes in
\mathcal{G}^{*}
are adjacent.
A face is called
maximal
if its ply is larger than the ply of any of its neighboring faces.

Our results.

Let
\diam(\mathcal{G})
denote the diameter of a graph
\mathcal{G}
, and let
\mu(\mathcal{A})
denote the number of maximal faces of an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
. Furthermore, define

\displaystyle\xi(n,\Delta)

\displaystyle\coloneq\max_{\mathcal{D}}\{\diam(\mathcal{G}^{*}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}))\}

and

\displaystyle\mu(n,\Delta)

\displaystyle\coloneq\max_{\mathcal{D}}\{\mu(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}))\},

with the maximum taken over all sets
\mathcal{D}
of
n
topological disks with overlap number
\Delta
in general position.
We first consider the case of two disks. Bounding
\mu(\mathcal{A})
is trivial for
n=2
, since
\mu(\mathcal{A})=\Delta

if
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
intersect, and
\mu(\mathcal{A})=2
if
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
are disjoint. Thus,
\mu(2,\Delta)=\max\{2,\Delta\}
.
Bounding
\xi(2,\Delta)
is less straightforward. We study this problem in Section
2
,
where we prove the following tight bound on the maximum possible diameter of
\mathcal{G}^{*}(\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}))
in terms of
\Delta
.

Theorem 1
.

\xi(2,\Delta)=\max\{2,2\Delta\}
.

In Section
3
we then turn our attention to the much more
challenging case of general
n
. Here, we first prove that
\mu(n,\Delta)
is bounded.

Theorem 2
.

For any
n>2
, we have
\mu(n,\Delta)\leqslant n(\Delta+1)^{n(n-1)/2}
.

Finally, we show that the diameter of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is bounded in terms of
n
and
\Delta
.

Theorem 3
.

For any
n>2
, we have
\xi(n,\Delta)\leqslant 2n\cdot\min\{n,\Delta+1\}\cdot(\Delta+1)^{n(n-1)/2}
.

Related work.

The problem of bounding the diameter of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is equivalent to the following question.
For two points
s,t\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
not lying on the boundary of any disk in
\mathcal{D}
, let
d(s,t)
be the minimum number of times one needs to cross a disk boundary when going from
s
to
t
.
Then, the question is: what is the maximum value of
d(s,t)
over all such pairs
s
and
t
?
This is related to the concept of
barrier coverage
in sensor networks, which aims to ensure that all possible paths through a surveillance domain will intersect the boundary of a sensor region at least once.
Cardei and Wu
[
4
]
and Meguerdichian
et al.

[
6
]
provide comprehensive overviews of work on this topic.
The notion of barrier coverage closest to our work is the one introduced by Bereg and Kirkpatrick
[
3
]
, who define the
thickness
of a barrier as the minimum number of sensor region intersections of a path between any two regions.

A substantial difference between the paper by Bereg and Kirkpatrick and our paper is the way of counting the number of crossings of an
st
-path with the region boundaries:
they count a boundary crossing only if the curve enters a region for the first time,
while we count every boundary crossing.
Cabello
et al.

[
2
]
study the related problem of
computing, for a given set of line segments and two points
s
and
t
, the minimum
number of segments crossed by an
st
-path, but they also count each segment at most once.

The case of two disks

In this section we will prove Theorem

.
We first prove the upper bound — that is, we show that the diameter of the dual graph
\mathcal{G}^{*}
of an arrangement of two topological disks is at most
\max\{2,2\Delta\}

The upper bound.

It is easy to verify that
\diam(\mathcal{G}^{*})=2
when
\Delta=0
or
\Delta=1

Let

Observe that any edge in

We call a path in

For any two purple

Since

The general case of
n
disks

We now consider the case of general
n
, which turns out to be significantly harder
than the case of two disks. The main difficulty lies in bounding the number
of maximal faces; for two disks this number is simply the overlap
number
\Delta
(assuming the disks are not disjoint), but for general

it
is not even clear that the number of maximal faces is bounded. Our main effort
lies in proving that this is the case (Section

). Once we have shown that, establishing
a bound on the diameter of the dual graph of the arrangement of disks is relatively easy (Section
3.2
).

For an elementary interval

, define

\Lambda(I)\coloneqq\{C\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{D}):I\subseteq C\}

to be the
component set
of
I
.
Furthermore, we define
f(I)
to be the face of
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})
that
is incident to
I
and lies inside
D_{0}
. We call
f(I)
a
boundary face

(with respect to the fixed disk
D_{0}
). Now consider two elementary intervals
I,I^{\prime}
.
We will show that if
\Lambda(I)=\Lambda(I^{\prime})
and
f(I)
and
f(I^{\prime})
are both maximal,
then
f(I)=f(I^{\prime})
. Thus the number of maximal boundary faces is bounded by the
number of distinct component sets that can occur, showing that the number of maximal
faces is bounded. We need the following lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Let
I,I^{\prime}
be two elementary intervals such that
\Lambda(I)=\Lambda(I^{\prime})
and let
D_{i},D_{j}\neq D_{0}
be distinct disks
that both contain
I
and
I^{\prime}
. Then there is a curve
\gamma_{ij}\subset D_{0}\cap D_{i}\cap D_{j}

that connects
I
to
I^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
p
be a point in the interior of
I
and let
p^{\prime}

be a point in the interior of
I^{\prime}
.
Because
\Lambda(I)=\Lambda(I^{\prime})
, the faces
f(I)
and
f(I^{\prime})
must be contained in the same
component of
D_{0}\cap D_{i}
. Hence, there is a curve
\gamma_{i}\subset D_{0}\cap D_{i}

connecting
p
to
p^{\prime}
. Similarly, there is a curve
\gamma_{j}\subset D_{0}\cap D_{j}

connecting
p
to
p^{\prime}
. Finally, because
I
and
I^{\prime}
are contained in the same component
of
D_{i}\cap D_{j}
there is a curve
\gamma^{*}\subset D_{i}\cap D_{j}
connecting
p
to
p^{\prime}
.
The curve
\gamma^{*}
may lie partially outside
D_{0}
.
Every time
\gamma^{*}
exits
D_{0}
, it must re-enter
D_{0}
at some point on
\partial D_{0}
.
Thus we can replace the portions of
\gamma^{*}
that lie outside
D_{0}
by
paths that follow
\partial D_{0}
; see Fig.
6
(i). We denote the rerouted curve by
\gamma^{\prime}
.
By construction,
\gamma^{\prime}
connects
p
to
p^{\prime}
and is contained in
D_{0}
.
We claim that
\gamma^{\prime}
is also contained in
D_{i}
and in
D_{j}
.
Thus, we can take
\gamma_{ij}:=\gamma^{\prime}
to prove the lemma.

:
(i) The curve
\gamma^{*}
is rerouted to obtain a curve
\gamma^{\prime}
(shown in orange)
staying inside
D_{0}
.
(ii) The left and right envelope (shown in orange) of a set of curves (shown in green).

Next we prove our claim that
\gamma^{\prime}
is contained in
D_{i}
;
the proof that it is contained in
D_{j}
is analogous.
Consider the closed curve
\gamma_{i}\cup\gamma^{*}
, which is contained in
D_{i}
.
Since
D_{i}
is a topological disk, the region
R^{*}
enclosed by the (possibly self-intersecting)
curve
\gamma_{i}\cup\gamma^{*}
must be contained in
D_{i}
.
Note that the region
R^{\prime}
enclosed by
\gamma_{i}\cup\gamma^{\prime}
is contained in
R^{*}
;
indeed, the replacement of
\gamma
by
\gamma^{\prime}
removes the portions of
R^{*}

that lie outside
D_{0}
. Thus,
R^{\prime}
and, hence,
\gamma^{\prime}
, are contained in
D_{i}
,
as claimed.
∎

We can now prove that any two elementary intervals with the same component set
that both bound a maximal boundary face, must in fact bound the same face.

Lemma 7
.

Let
I,I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
be two elementary intervals with
\Lambda(I)=\Lambda(I^{\prime})
and such
that the boundary faces
f(I)
and
f(I^{\prime})
are both maximal. Then
f(I)=f(I^{\prime})
.

Proof.

If
I
and
I^{\prime}
are contained in a single disk
D_{i}\neq D_{0}
, then the
lemma is easily seen to hold. Indeed, since
\Lambda(I)=\Lambda(I^{\prime})
, we know
that
I
and
I^{\prime}
are contained in the same component of
D_{0}\cap D_{i}
,
and so there is a curve
\gamma_{i}
connecting
I
to
I^{\prime}
that stays inside this component.
The curve
\gamma_{i}
cannot intersect the boundary of any disk
D_{j}\not\in\{D_{0},D_{i}\}
, since the first time

\gamma_{i}
would intersect such a boundary it would enter the corresponding disk
D_{j}
—this is true
because
D_{j}
does not contain
I
—which contradicts the maximality of
f(I)
.
So from now on we assume that
I
and
I^{\prime}
are contained in at least two disks,
in addition to
D_{0}
.
Recall that each component
C\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{D})
is a connected component of the
intersection
D_{i}\cap D_{j}
defined by some pair
D_{i},D_{j}\in\mathcal{D}
. Now consider
a disk
D_{i}\neq D_{0}
, and define
\Lambda_{i}(I)\subset\Lambda(I)
to be the

set of components
for which
D_{i}
is one of the defining
disks. In other words,

\Lambda_{i}(I)\coloneqq\{C\in\Lambda(I):\mbox{ $C$ is a component of $D_{i}\cap D_{j}$ for some $j\neq i$\lx@text@rbrace}\,.

For each component
C\in\Lambda_{i}(I)
of
D_{i}\cap D_{j}
there is
a curve
\gamma_{ij}\subset D_{0}\cap D_{i}\cap D_{j}
that connects
I
to
I^{\prime}
, by Lemma
6
.
Fix one such curve for each pair
(i,j)
, and
let
\Gamma_{i}
denote the collection of all these curves
\gamma_{ij}
.
Note that any two sets
\Gamma_{i},\Gamma_{j}
have a curve in
common, namely
\gamma_{ij}
.
The part of
\partial D_{0}
not covered by
I
or
I^{\prime}
consists of
two intervals. Let
p_{\mathrm{left}}
be a point in the interior of one of these intervals and

p_{\mathrm{right}}
to be a point in the interior of the other interval.
We use the points
p_{\mathrm{left}}
and
p_{\mathrm{right}}
to define the
left and right envelope of a given collection
\Gamma

of curves connecting
I
to
I^{\prime}
, as follows. Consider the arrangement

\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{A}(\Gamma\cup\{\partial D_{0}\})
defined by the curves in
\Gamma
and
\partial D_{0}
within
D_{0}
.
Let
f_{\mathrm{left}}
be the face of
\mathcal{A}
containing
p_{\mathrm{left}}
, and
let
f_{\mathrm{left}}
be the face of
\mathcal{A}
containing
p_{\mathrm{left}}
.
Then the
left envelope
of
\Gamma
, denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma)
,
is the part of the boundary of
f_{\mathrm{left}}
lying strictly inside
D_{0}
. Similarly,
the
right envelope

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma)
is
is the part of the boundary of
f_{\mathrm{right}}
lying strictly inside
D_{0}
;
see Fig.
6
(ii) for an illustration.
Now consider the left and right envelopes
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma_{i})

of the sets
\Gamma_{i}
defined above. Let
R_{i}
be the region in
D_{0}
between

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma_{i})
. Since
D_{i}
is a topological disk,
and all curves in
\Gamma_{i}
as well as
I
and
I^{\prime}
are contained in
D_{i}
, we know that
R_{i}\subset D_{i}
.
Let

B_{\mathrm{left}}:=\{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i}):I\subset D_{i}\}

denote the set of left envelopes, and define

Z_{\mathrm{left}}:=\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(B_{\mathrm{left}})

to be the
right
envelope of the left envelopes.
Similarly, define
Z_{\mathrm{right}}
to be the left envelope of the set

B_{\mathrm{right}}:=\{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma_{i}):I\subset D_{i}\}

of right envelopes. We will now consider the relative position of

Z_{\mathrm{left}}
and
Z_{\mathrm{right}}
to prove that there must be a curve
\gamma^{*}

connecting
I
to
I^{\prime}
that lies in the common intersection of the disks
D_{i}

that contain
I
. In other words, we can go from
I
to
I^{\prime}
via

\gamma^{*}
without leaving any of the disks containing
I^{\prime}
. Now,
if
f(I)
is a maximal face, then
\gamma^{*}
cannot enter any disk,
thus proving that
f(I)=f(I^{\prime})
. Hence, to prove the lemma, it remains
to argue the existence of
\gamma^{*}
.
We consider three cases. (In these cases, the terms
left
and
right

are defined using the points
p_{\mathrm{left}}
and
p_{\mathrm{right}}
, as before.
For instance,
Z_{\mathrm{left}}
lying completely to the left of
Z_{\mathrm{right}}

means that
Z_{\mathrm{left}}
lies in the region bounded by
Z_{\mathrm{right}}
and
the part of
\partial D_{0}
that contains
p_{\mathrm{left}}
.)

•

Case I:
Z_{\mathrm{left}}
lies completely to the left of
Z_{\mathrm{right}}
.

Let
\gamma^{*}
be any curve between
Z_{\mathrm{left}}
and
Z_{\mathrm{right}}
,
and consider any disk
D_{i}
containing
I
.
By definition of
Z_{\mathrm{left}}
, the curve
\gamma^{*}
lies to the right of all
left envelopes. In particular,
\gamma^{*}
lies to the right of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})
.
Similarly,
\gamma^{*}
lies to the left of all
right envelopes, and so
\gamma^{*}
lies to the left of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma_{i})
.
Hence,
\gamma^{*}\subset R_{i}\subset D_{i}
. Since this holds for any
disk
D_{i}
containing
I
, we conclude that
\gamma^{*}
has the desired properties.

•

Case II:
Z_{\mathrm{left}}
lies completely to the right of
Z_{\mathrm{right}}
.

We claim that this cannot happen. To see this, let
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})

define the endpoint of
Z_{\mathrm{left}}
on
I
, and let
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma_{j})
define the endpoint of
Z_{\mathrm{right}}
on
I
.
Then
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})
lies to the right of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma_{j})

along
I
. But this is not possible because there is a curve,

\gamma_{ij}
, that is a curve in
\Gamma_{i}
and in
\Gamma_{j}
.
Thus along
I
the leftmost curve of
\Gamma_{i}
cannot lie to the right
of the rightmost curve in
\Gamma_{j}
.

•

Case III:
Z_{\mathrm{left}}
crosses
Z_{\mathrm{right}}
.

We will prove that this cannot happen either. To this end, let
x

be an crossing point of
Z_{\mathrm{left}}
and
Z_{\mathrm{right}}
. Let

Z_{\mathrm{left}}
and
Z_{\mathrm{right}}
be defined by
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma_{j})

at the point
x
, respectively, so that
x\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})\cap\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma_{j})
.
Note that the endpoint of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})
on
I
must lie to the left
of the endpoint of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma_{j})
on
I
; this follows by the same
reasoning as in Case II. The curve
\gamma_{ij}\in\Gamma_{i}\cap\Gamma_{j}

starts between
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma_{j})
on
I
, and by
definition it reaches
I^{\prime}
while staying inside
R_{i}\cap R_{j}
.
But this is impossible if
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})
and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{right}}(\Gamma_{j})
cross,
because then
\gamma_{ij}
must cross at least one of these curves,
say
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})
, in order to reach
I^{\prime}
. This is a contradiction, because
if
\gamma_{ij}
crosses
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})
for the first time, it would enter the
part of
D_{0}
to the left of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\Gamma_{i})
, which contradicts the definition
of
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{left}}(\gamma_{i})
since
\gamma_{ij}\in\Gamma_{i}
.
∎

We are now in a position to prove Theorem
2
, which states that
\mu(\mathcal{A})
,
the number of maximal faces in the arrangement induced by a set

\mathcal{D}
of
n
topological disks with overlap number
\Delta
, is bounded by

n(\Delta+1)^{n(n-1)/2}
.
To this end, observe that the number of maximal boundary faces of a fixed
disk
D_{i}\in\mathcal{D}
is bounded by the number of distinct component sets
\Lambda(I)
, by Lemma
7
. Also observe that a component set
\Lambda(I)
contains at
most one component from each set
\mathcal{C}(D_{j},D_{k})
, and that
|\mathcal{C}(D_{j},D_{k})|\leqslant\Delta

by the definition of
\Delta
. Hence,
the number of different component sets is bounded by
(\Delta+1)^{n(n-1)/2}
.
Multiplying this by
n
, the number of different choices for the fixed disk
D_{i}
,
we obtain the desired bound of
n(\Delta+1)^{n(n-1)/2}
on the number of maximal faces.

3.2
The proof of Theorem
3

Using the bound on the number of maximal faces in the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, we now obtain a bound on the diameter of the dual graph
\mathcal{G}^{*}
of
\mathcal{A}
.
Recall that the
ply
of a face
f
in the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
, denoted by
\ply(f)
,
is the total number of disks in
\mathcal{D}
that contain
f
.
Define
p_{\max}:=\max_{f}\ply(f)
to be the maximum ply over all faces in the arrangement
\mathcal{A}
.
For a node
u
in the dual graph
\mathcal{G}^{*}
, we define
\ply(u):=\ply(f_{u})
, where
f_{u}
is the face in
\mathcal{A}
corresponding to
u
.
The next lemma relates
\mu(\mathcal{A})
, the number of maximal faces in
\mathcal{A}
, to
\diam(\mathcal{G}^{*})
, the diameter of the dual graph
\mathcal{G}^{*}
of
\mathcal{A}
.
Combining the lemma with Theorem
2
proves Theorem
3
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arrangement induced by a set
\mathcal{D}
of
n
disks, and let
\mathcal{G}^{*}
be the dual graph of
\mathcal{A}
.
Then
\diam(\mathcal{G}^{*})\leqslant 2p_{\max}\cdot\mu(\mathcal{A})\leqslant 2n\cdot\mu(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

Let
V
be the set of nodes of
\mathcal{G}^{*}
, and let
M=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{t}\}
be the subset of nodes from
V
corresponding to maximal faces in
\mathcal{A}
.
We say that a node
u\in V
has a
monotone path
to a node
v_{i}\in M
if there is a path
u=w_{1},\ldots,w_{s}=v_{i}
in
\mathcal{G}^{*}
such that
\ply(w_{j+1})=\ply(w_{j})+1
for all
1\leqslant j<s
.
Observe that a node
v_{i}\in M
has a monotone path to itself and to no other node
v_{j}\in M
.
We partition
V
into subsets
V_{1},\ldots,V_{t}
, one for each node
v_{i}\in M
, where we define

V_{i}:=\{u\in V:\mbox{$u$ has a monotone path to $v_{i}$ but not to any $v_{j}$ with $j<i$}\}.

Note that each node
u\in V
is assigned to a set
V_{i}
, because from
u
we can greedily move to faces of higher ply until we reach a maximal face.
Also note that
v_{i}\in V_{i}
for all
i
.
Now, let
\overline{\mathcal{G}^{*}}
be the graph obtained from
\mathcal{G}^{*}
by contracting each
subgraph
\mathcal{G}^{*}[V_{i}]
to a single node.
We make the following two observations.

(i)

\overline{\mathcal{G}^{*}}
is a connected graph (because
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is obviously connected) with
\mu(\mathcal{A})
nodes. Hence, the distance between any two nodes
V_{i},V_{j}
in
\overline{\mathcal{G}^{*}}
is at most
\mu(\mathcal{A})-1
.

(ii)

Let
v_{i},v_{j}\in M
be such that the nodes in
\overline{\mathcal{G}^{*}}
corresponding to the sets
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
are adjacent. Then, the distance
d(v_{i},v_{j})
between the nodes
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
in
\mathcal{G}^{*}
is at most
\ply(v_{i})+\ply(v_{j})
. To see this, let
w_{i}\in V_{i}
and
w_{j}\in V_{j}
be adjacent nodes in
\mathcal{G}^{*}
. Such nodes must exist because
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
are adjacent in
\overline{\mathcal{G}^{*}}
. From
w_{i}
we can walk to
v_{i}
in
\ply(v_{i})-\ply(w_{i})
hops and from
w_{j}
we can walk to
v_{j}
in
\ply(v_{j})-\ply(w_{j})
hops. Hence,

d(v_{i},v_{j})\leqslant\big(\ply(v_{i})-\ply(w_{i})\big)+\big(\ply(v_{j})-\ply(w_{j})\big)+1\leqslant\ply(v_{i})+\ply(v_{j}),

where the last inequality follows because
\ply(w_{i})
and
\ply(w_{j})
cannot both be zero.

Now, let
u,u^{\prime}
be two arbitrary nodes in
V
.
Let
i,j
be such that
u\in V_{i}
and
u^{\prime}\in V_{j}
.
We can walk from
u
to
v_{i}
in
\ply(v_{i})-\ply(u)\leqslant p_{\max}
hops, and we can walk from
u^{\prime}
to
v_{j}
in
\ply(v_{j})-\ply(u^{\prime})\leqslant p_{\max}
hops.
Moreover, by combining the two observations above, we see that there is a path from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
in
\mathcal{G}^{*}
consisting of
(\mu(\mathcal{A})-1)\cdot 2p_{\max}
edges.
Hence,
d(u,u^{\prime})\leqslant 2p_{\max}\cdot\mu(\mathcal{A})
.
It remains to observe that
p_{\max}\leqslant n
, which we trivially have.
∎

4
Concluding Remarks and Discussion

In this paper, we derive bounds on the diameter of the dual graph of an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
of disks in terms of the number of disks,
n
, and the maximum number of intersection components between any two disks,
\Delta
. For an arrangement containing only two disks, we establish a sharp linear bound of
\max\{2,2\Delta\}
. Such upper bound is equivalent to a bound on the number of crossings of any minimum-crossing
st
-curve in arrangements of two disks. For an arrangement
\mathcal{A}
of
n
disks, we bound both the diameter of the dual graph and the number of maximal faces as a function of
n
and
\Delta
. The bound on the number of maximal faces
\mu(\mathcal{A})
is
n(\Delta+1)^{n(n-1)/2}
. Therefore the upper bound on the diameter of the dual graph of arrangements with
n
disks is
2n(\Delta+1)^{n(n-1)/2}\min\{n,\Delta+1\}
, which corresponds to the bound for the number of crossings of any minimum-crossing
st
-curve.
We end this paper with an open problem: Is it possible to derive an upper bound on
\mu(\mathcal{A})
which is polynomial in
n
and
\Delta
? Our best example has
\Theta(n^{2}\Delta)
maximal faces, see Fig.
7
. We conjecture that this bound for
\mu(\mathcal{A})
is tight up to a constant factor.
