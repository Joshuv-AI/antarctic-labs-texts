Connect Kits for Family Action: Intervention Development and Exploratory Data Analysis of Proximal Outcomes
Authors: Kelli A. Komro, Juli R. Skinner, Emily Ivanich, Caroline M. Barry, Ashna Jagtiani, Bethany J. Livingston, Sierra L. Talavera-Brown, Hannah LaBounty, Ashley N. Gassaway, Melvin D. Livingston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We designed a home-based family intervention to address risk of substance use among adolescents in rural and Native American communities. Through a Tribal-University partnership, study aims were to develop, implement and explore effects on proximal risk and protective factors. The study was conducted in or near the Cherokee Nation Reservation in northeastern Oklahoma and was a cluster-randomized trial with 10 high schools randomly assigned to intervention and 10 to delayed intervention group. The study involved a cohort of 919 tenth-grade students, surveyed approximately every six months over three years. Self-reported measures assessed the intervention’s impact on proximal outcomes linked to receipt of specific intervention components. Results indicate successful altering of norms, increased risk perceptions and parental disapproval of substance use. No improvements were found for parent/caregiver support and monitoring, friend support, or anxiety and depressive symptoms. The intervention’s cultural relevance and community involvement were key strengths, particularly in addressing health disparities in rural and Native populations. Despite some limitations, such as unchanged perceptions of social support, the findings suggest that culturally tailored, family-centered interventions can effectively reduce risk factors and increase protective factors. The study contributes to the limited literature on family-focused prevention for Indigenous youth and underscores the importance of community partnerships and culturally inclusive approaches in public health practice.

Introduction

Rural and Native American communities continue to confront the impacts of intergenerational trauma and social inequities, which have contributed to disproportionately high rates of deaths of despair, including drug overdose and suicide among adolescents (Ivanich et al., 2021; Komro, 2018). Native Americans ages 10–24 experience suicide rates over 2.5 times higher than the national average (CDC, 2024), and overdose deaths occur at significantly higher rates compared to their non-Native peers (Friedman et 2022). Rurality further compounds the problem for reservation-based youth due to limited access to care and knowledge of available resources (Hirsch & Cukrowicz, 2014). Despite significant challenges, rural and Native communities have led the way in mitigating harms through evidence-based, culturally appropriate prevention and clinical services. Communities like those throughout the Cherokee Nation in northeastern Oklahoma have been at the forefront of these efforts, demonstrating a commitment to preventive care and culturally grounded approaches (Bark, 2023, 2024; Cherokee Nation Behavioral Health, 2024).

Family-level interventions in reservation-based settings, while rare relative to individual-level approaches (Blue Bird Jernigan et al., 2020), have emerged as a vital tool to address harms associated with substance use. Interventions like the Iowa Strengthening Families Program have shown promise when adapted for Indigenous contexts (Ivanich et al., 2020; Tuitt et al., 2023). Indigenous-led, culturally grounded interventions are called for in Native health promotion efforts (Walters et al., 2020), while community-driven, multilevel strategies are needed to address broader social determinants of health (Blue Bird Jernigan et al., 2020).

In response, the “Prevention Trial in the Cherokee Nation,” led by Cherokee Nation Behavioral Health (CNBH) and university-based public health scientists, focuses on opioid and other drug prevention among older adolescents attending high schools in underserved small-town areas within and near the Cherokee Nation Reservation (Komro et al., 2022). The team, having collaborated for over ten years, integrated cultural practices with evidence-based prevention strategies, community partnerships, and tribal oversight in developing and implementing preventive interventions (Komro et al., 2023). Tribal oversight is a critical component of the trial, with the Cherokee Nation Institutional Review Board (CNIRB) serving as the single IRB, ensuring that all research activities comply with tribal governance and oversight requirements.

The current trial, which began in 2019, expanded upon a previous alcohol use prevention trial that successfully reduced alcohol and other drug use among youth through school-based brief intervention and community organizing (Komro et al., 2017; Livingston et al., 2018; Wagenaar et al., 2018). The new trial was in the planning phase as the world faced the COVID-19 pandemic. The pandemic had profound impacts on society, including significant loss of life, strained healthcare systems, and closures of work-places, schools, and other public spaces. As with most societal functions, initiating and successfully implementing a community-based prevention trial during such a turbulent period required rapid adaptations to the implementation approach. One successful adaptation we made was shifting approaches from a community organizing intervention to developing a new home-based family empowerment intervention.

The overall trial results found that a combined school-based computerized brief intervention with the new home-based family intervention reduced the frequency of substance use among students in rural public high schools on or near the Cherokee Nation Reservation (Komro et al., 2025). Study participants included a cohort of 919 tenth-grade students followed over three years. At baseline the mean age was 15.5; about one-third self-identified as American Indian only, American Indian and another race/ethnicity, or White only; and 74% received free or reduced-price lunch. By the end of 12th grade, the intervention group had significantly lower frequency of alcohol use, binge drinking, cannabis use and prescription opioid misuse compared to the control group (Komro et al., 2025). However, few statistically significant differences were found between the groups in the overall trajectories of summary scales for the hypothesized proximal outcomes. Therefore, we aimed to conduct detailed exploratory data analysis to understand the direct effect of each kit on specific, targeted proximal outcomes. The objectives of this paper are to: (1) describe the new home-based family intervention using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist (Hoffmann et al., 2014), and (2) present process evaluation results, including parent/caregiver and student feedback, and an exploration of the intervention’s effect on individual proximal outcomes.

Methods

Intervention

Our initial proposed prevention plans included a replication and expansion of our community organizing intervention, Communities Mobilizing for Change on Alcohol (CMCA) (Wagenaar et al., 2018), to address growing concerns about vaping, the proliferation of medical cannabis dispensaries, and the opioid epidemic (Komro et al., 2022). However, in response to pandemic-related challenges and our decision to engage remote, rural communities, we transitioned to a new prevention plan that focused on families. While encompassing similar goals, empowerment and action orientation as CMCA, the newly adapted intervention was delivered directly to homes via mail and through schools. The ultimate goal was the reduction of substance use among youth, which was achieved (Komro et al., 2025). Proximal goals include empowering individuals to make healthy changes, including fostering self-efficacy and a healthy lifestyle, reducing access to alcohol and other drugs, enhancing social connections, and supporting drug-free norms.

The new family intervention, Connect Kits for Family Action, was developed through a partnership between CNBH and university-based public health scientists. The intervention design team included three members from CNBH and five university-based members, one of Native American ancestry (Komro et al., 2023). The intervention team met weekly for one hour to plan and design the intervention, with two team members dedicated solely to the development of intervention materials. Intervention design was based on CMCA (Wagenaar et al., 2018), previous family intervention components (Brody et al., 2010, 2012, 2021; Komro et al., 2006; Stigler et al., 2006), clinical and lived expertise of team members, feedback from a Cherokee Nation youth group, and our conceptual guiding framework. Given the diversity of families within and near the Cherokee Nation Reservation and of students attending Oklahoma public schools, we designed materials and activities to be flexible for families to match their family and cultural practices and norms. We designed Native American and Cherokee-specific culturally appropriate options and examples throughout the kits.

Conceptual framework

Our conceptual framework guided the selection of intervention objectives and measures and was based on merging socio-ecological and risk and protective frameworks (Hawkins et al., 1992; Keyes et al., 2014; Komro et al., 2016; Wagenaar & Perry, 1994) with an Indigenous relational worldview perspective (Blackstock, 2019; Cross, 2007). This framework acknowledges that Indigenous health and well-being are deeply rooted in epistemological understandings that differ from Western worldviews. Figure 1 displays our integrated conceptual framework, which is meant to highlight dynamic, multi-level, inter-relationships between contextual (i.e, societal, community, social) and intra- and inter-level (i.e., mind, body, spirit) factors that influence health and wellbeing as it relates to abstinence or initiation and escalation of substance misuse during adolescence into young adulthood (Livingston, Barry, Jagtiani, et al., 2023). Specifically, the family intervention was developed to enhance protective and health-promoting influences within the family and peer social context (norms, social support, safe environment), including intra- and inter-level factors that influence a healthy mind (norms, self-efficacy, mental wellbeing) and spirit (social support, future aspirations) that would support physical wellbeing through the prevention of alcohol and other substance misuse.

Intervention implementation

The Connect Kits for Family Action were distributed directly to parents/caregivers. Schools either provided caregiver contact information for direct mailing or facilitated the delivery of kits. Each kit included an introductory card outlining the content and goals, along with interactive activities tailored to family values. Kits were distributed over three years, on average every 3 months, with this structured approach aimed at enhancing family connections, creating safe environments, and supporting community engagement, ultimately fostering a supportive ecosystem for adolescent development.

Feedback collected in the final year indicated high engagement and perceived helpfulness. First, the majority of kits were delivered to families, with less than 4% returned as undeliverable following the first mailing. On the final student survey at the end of 12th grade, 37% of students remembered receiving the Connect Kits in the mail. Of those, 71% rated the kits as “a little helpful” to “extremely helpful.” Caregivers were invited to provide feedback via a brief survey available at a school event and included in the final kit. Of 45 completed surveys, 84% of caregivers remembered receiving kits in the mail and 80% remembered using pieces from the kits. Among those, 100% rated the kits as “a little helpful” to “extremely helpful.” Caregivers were asked to describe the one piece that they used the most and the most frequent responses included: family games, conversation cards, recipes, meal plan menu board, locks for cabinets and/or lock bags for prescription drugs, and summer monitoring sticky notes. Proximal outcomes

Participants

The study cohort comprised older adolescents (10th-grade students at baseline, mean age 15.5 years) from high schools located in low-resource, sparsely populated rural areas within or adjacent to the 14 counties that constitute the Cherokee Nation Reservation (Komro et al., 2022). Nearly half self-identified as female (49%) or male (48%), with 4% declining to answer. Participants’ self-identified race/ethnicity was primarily American Indian (AI) and White (AI only n = 29.2%, White only n = 35.5%, AI and White n = 22.5%, and other n = 12.7%).

Out of 919 students enrolled at baseline, 465 were in the schools assigned to the intervention condition, while 454 were in schools allocated to the delayed-program condition. Baseline assessments revealed no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of demographics, substance use, depressive symptoms, and 12 out of 13 proximal outcomes.

Measures

Proximal outcome measures included multiple items capturing social support, social normative disapproval beliefs, perceived access to drugs, normative estimates, self-efficacy, perceptions of risk, career goals, confidence to succeed, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 8-item Patient Health Questionnaire depression scale (PHQ-8), a validated measure of current depression in the general population (Kroenke et al., 2009). Anxiety symptoms were measured with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7), which has demonstrated acceptable specificity and sensitivity, and can differentiate between mild and moderate generalized anxiety disorder among adolescents (Mossman et al., 2017). Detailed descriptions of survey measures have been previously documented (Livingston, Barry, Jagtiani, et al., 2023).

Survey procedure

The consent and assent procedures for parents/guardians and students were approved by the Cherokee Nation Institutional Review Board (IRB), which provided trial oversight under a Reliance Agreement with Emory University. In the fall of 2021, a baseline survey was administered to the 10th-grade cohort across all 20 study high schools (n = 919, response rate 87%). Surveys were administered in classrooms by research staff using students’ personal computers or project iPads. A personalized link to the Qualtrics survey was emailed to each student the morning of each survey. Five follow-up surveys were conducted approximately every six months over three years (response rates: spring 2022, 89%; fall 2022, 88%; spring 2023, 85%; fall 2023, 83%; spring 2024, 83%). Response rates did not differ by study condition. The family intervention commenced in the spring of 2022 and continued through the spring of 2024.

Analysis

To explore the intervention’s effect on individual proximal outcomes measured in the subsequent survey conducted one to nine months after kit distribution, we implemented independence estimating equations (IEEs) for the participant-average effects using generalized estimating equations (GEEs) with an independence working correlation structure. Independence estimating equations may be preferred over typical compound symmetry GEEs when cluster size is informative (Kahan et al., 2023). In our case, if larger schools see differential effects relative to smaller schools, than the typical compound symmetry approach would yield biased estimates while the independence approach would still yield consistent estimates. As a sensitivity analysis, we re-estimated all linear models using the compound symmetry approach, finding similar results with one exception (significant reductions in the PHQ-8 following kit #4), which was the result of unstable intraclass correlations that were estimated as negative artificially inflating our effective sample size. Best practice in these scenarios is to constrain the ICC at zero, lending further credence to the independence approach.

The final model used was: g(yt) = β0 + β1Int + Xi + Zs where g() represents the link function for outcome y at the relevant follow-up timepoint, Int is an indicator for the intervention group, Xi is a series of individual-level baseline covariates which includes age, gender, race, and food insecurity, and Zs is the series of school-level covariates used in the constrained randomization which includes percent American Indian enrollment, school absenteeism rate, percent of students receiving free/reduced-price lunch, rate of substance use incidents reported by the school, four-year graduation rates, class size, whether the community contained a medical cannabis dispensary, whether the community had a city police department, and the mean number of community-based mental health counselors. For anxiety and depressive symptoms, linear regressions were used. For all other outcomes, ordinal logistic regressions were used where β1 was transformed and expressed as odds ratios.

Results

Each kit was designed to target specific proximal determinants outlined in the conceptual framework (e.g., norms, access, support, self-efficacy, future orientation), and Table 2 presents the corresponding kit-specific outcomes measured in the subsequent youth survey, conducted one to nine months after kit distribution. All kits included fact-based infographics to inform families of the risks associated with youth substance use.

Four of the eight kits emphasized facts (kits 1, 2, 3, and 5) with a goal to increase parent/caregiver disapproval of youth substance use. Results showed that youths’ perceptions of parent/caregiver disapproval of alcohol use and prescription opioid misuse increased, as well as disapproval of cannabis use following the first three kits.

The first two kits emphasized the importance of fostering family connections and spending quality time together, yet following these kits, there were no differences in reports of parent/caregiver support and monitoring between the intervention and control groups. Kits 5 and 7 also included components to increase monitoring and support, yet results showed no differences between the intervention and control groups following the kits.

Kit 3 focused on the safe storage of alcohol and other drugs at home. Following kit 3, youths’ perception of easy access to cannabis in the home was reduced among the intervention group. There were no differences in perception of ease of access to alcohol or prescription opioids.

Kit 4 focused on mental health, yet there were no differences in anxiety or depressive symptoms among the intervention and control groups following the distribution of the kit.

Kit 5 focused on safe celebrations and strategies for youth to deal with risky situations. Besides increased parent/caregiver disapproval among the intervention group, self-efficacy to refuse alcohol/drugs increased and normative estimates of cannabis decreased. There were no differences in normative estimates of alcohol or prescription opioid misuse, although they were lower among the intervention group.

Kit 6 was a supplemental kit focused on media literacy and awareness of medical cannabis dispensary advertisement messaging, previously described in detail (Livingston, Barry, Walker, et al., 2023). Results show increased disapproval among youth of youth alcohol and cannabis use, as well as increased risk of harms from youth cannabis use.

Kits 7 and 8 focused activities for a healthy transition to young adulthood. Kit 7 emphasized planning for the future and goal setting. Following the kit, youth in the intervention group reported increases in five of the six items measuring career goals and confidence to succeed. The focus of Kit 8 was on friendships and social connections. Following the kit, confidence to succeed was higher among the intervention group, yet no differences in perceived friend support were observed.

Discussion

Connect Kits for Family Action were theoretically informed to strengthen protective factors and prevent risk factors for substance use and designed to alter individual and social norms around substance use, enhance familial and peer connections, create safe environments, and improve mental health. This detailed analysis revealed significant differences in multiple proximal outcomes between the intervention and control groups, offering valuable insights for tailoring future prevention efforts, especially for rural and Native populations.

All kits included educational components using infographics to share facts regarding risks, protections, and harms associated with youth substance use. Overall, the infographics were successful in increasing youths’ perceptions of caregiver disapproval of use. One kit emphasized reducing home access (kit 3) and significantly reduced perceptions of access to cannabis in the home, while the pattern of lowering access to alcohol and prescription opioids in the home was consistent, they were not statistically significant. Kit 6 reinforced the protection of reduced access to cannabis in the home, as this kit focused on cannabis advertising, media literacy, and community action and resulted in increased disapproval of use and increased risk of harm. The kit that focused on safe celebrations and managing risky situations (kit 5) was effective in increasing youth’s refusal self-efficacy and perceptions of caregiver disapproval of alcohol and prescription opioids, but not cannabis. However, it was successful in decreasing cannabis normative estimates. It did not change perceptions of caregiver support or monitoring, which had been one focus of the kit. Kits 7 and 8 focused on healthy transition to young adulthood and were successful in increasing career goals and confidence to succeed. While kit 8 provided strategies for peer support and friendship, in the short term it did not shift perceptions of support from friends.

We did not find significant differences between study conditions in all proximal factors, most notably perceived support from caregivers and friends. This may be due to the short/varied timeframe between delivery of kits and measurement, actual usage of the kits, and precision of measurements on the student survey. We were unable to measure family engagement, so it is difficult to assess whether the lack of detecting improvement in parent/caregiver support might be due to limited engagement, inadequate measurement, or limitations of intervention components. Second, some proximal outcomes (e.g., friend support) may require longer exposure or social network change beyond the assessment window. Mental health outcomes were not a primary intervention target, and the brief kit-based content may have been insufficient to observe improvements in anxiety and depressive symptoms. However, most proximal items trended in the hypothesized direction, i.e. higher protective effects in the intervention group which may have had a cumulative effect over time in influencing less risky behaviors and overall substance use reduction results in the intervention group. These detailed analyses are valuable to guide revisions to improve and strengthen intervention components to further address important proximal factors that did not show improvement.

The success of the Connect Kits for Family Action is an important addition to a very limited number of evidence-based family programs for Indigenous youth. A recent systematic review of substance use prevention for Indigenous youth found that, despite the importance of family and community in Indigenous cultures, only one-third of prevention programs included in their review were community- or family-based, and among those families were engaged through one-off workshops or pamphlets and none evaluated the family component specifically (Snijder et al., 2020). The Connect Kit for Family Action multi-year program, developed in partnership with Indigenous colleagues, offers a feasible, acceptable, and culturally inclusive family program implemented within homes by family members when convenient, offering an ideal format for remote and rural communities.

In addition to the program’s innovation, the study has noteworthy strengths, most importantly the high level of community involvement and cultural relevance made possible by the partnership between Cherokee Nation and university-based prevention scientists. Culturally grounded intervention development is a key strength in addressing health disparities in rural and Native American communities (Komro et al., 2023; Snijder et al., 2020; Whitesell et al., 2020). The collaboration between CNBH and university-based scientists exemplifies a Tribal-University partnership; community-driven research that respects Indigenous traditions, sovereignty, and governance, evident through the Cherokee Nation IRB’s oversight. The intervention includes options for family-specific tailoring, expanding generalizability, and relevance to other communities. The intervention was well-received by caregivers and youth. Researchers demonstrated flexibility and innovation by shifting the intervention from a community-organizing approach to a home-based, family-centered intervention in response to COVID-related challenges. This adaptation likely increased reach during a time of crisis, ensuring continuity in preventive efforts.

There are limitations that are also important to consider. As noted, there were no changes in several proximal outcomes, including caregiver and friend support, mental health, and reduced access to alcohol and prescription opioids in the home. While the intervention was effective on many outcomes, it does not strongly influence all theorized mediators, limiting its comprehensiveness in addressing the broader social and psychological contributors to substance use. However, results from the detailed analyses can be used to revise and improve components of the intervention. The study relied on voluntary engagement of the intervention by the families and self-reported data to assess engagement. Despite providing a $10 gift card incentive, caregiver feedback was limited by a low response rate (~10%), likely due to passive recruitment through mailed materials and broader declines in survey participation following the COVID-19 pandemic. Accordingly, caregiver feedback is presented as descriptive rather than representative of all participating families. However, of those 80% reported using the kits, and all of them rated the kits as helpful. On the endpoint student survey, only 37% of the students remembered the kits. This may be a branding issue, as few kits were returned as undeliverable. Future implementation efforts may explore digital adaptations of the Connect Kits to enhance scalability, support real-time engagement measurement, and facilitate tailoring and dissemination across diverse rural and Indigenous communities. Self-report may be influenced by recall and social desirability bias, particularly for substance use behaviors; however, concurrent randomized comparison across groups reduces risk of differential bias. Finally, while the study covers a three-year period, the long-term sustainability of the intervention’s effects on substance use and proximal outcomes remains unknown. Future studies could benefit from extended follow-up to determine whether desired changes in proximal outcomes and substance use persist into adulthood.

The multi-component preventive intervention, including Connect Kits for Family Action, effectively reduced substance use among high school students, with lower rates of alcohol, binge drinking, cannabis, and prescription opioid misuse in the intervention group (Komro et al., 2025). The kits were designed to address implementation challenges during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Pandemic-related changes in adolescent substance use were heterogeneous, with some studies documenting reductions in prevalence possibly due to reduced peer interaction and others reporting increased frequency and coping-motivated use among existing users. Because intervention and control schools were exposed to the same pandemic context, the randomized design mitigates potential confounding to provide rigorous evidence of prevention’s effectiveness. The kits, designed to strengthen protective factors and prevent risk factors, successfully altered norms, created more protective home environments, and increased career goals and confidence. The intervention increased perceptions of caregiver disapproval, and specific kits reduced home access to cannabis, and increased disapproval and perceived harm of substance use. Kits focusing on safe celebrations improved refusal self-efficacy and caregiver disapproval, while those aimed at transitions to adulthood boosted career goals and confidence to succeed. Despite some limitations, such as unchanged perceptions of support and improvement in mental health, the intervention’s cultural relevance and community involvement, particularly through the Cherokee Nation partnership, were key strengths. Significant differences in proximal outcomes between intervention and control groups suggest effective tailoring for rural and Native populations. Adaptations for COVID-19 increased reach, and future studies should explore long-term effects and further refine intervention components.
