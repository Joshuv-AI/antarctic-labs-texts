Responsible AI UK: priorities for delivering England’s health and life sciences plans
Authors: Findlay Macaskill, Matea Deliu, Ilias Zapantis, Lydia Jidkov, Peter Mcculloch, Aislinn Bergin, Ian Abbs, Prokar Dasgupta
Published: 2026

In 2025, the UK government published two major policy documents shaping healthcare and innovation in England: the ‘10-year plan: ‘Fit for the Future’ and the ‘Life Sciences Sector Plan’. Together, they articulate a vision for a digital, data-enabled health system using artificial intelligence (AI).1 2

Responsible AI (RAi) UK is a UK Research and Innovation (UKRI)-funded organisation established to advance responsible research and innovation in AI. The RAi Health and Social Care Working Group brings together senior leaders from academia, clinical practice, the National Health Service, industry and regulators. Its remit is to provide independent, multidisciplinary expertise on the development, evaluation and implementation of AI technologies.

This editorial responds to both plans, welcoming their direction but emphasising that success depends on execution, not just ambition. Drawing on our collective expertise, we highlight four priority areas critical for responsible, equitable and sustainable AI adoption in health and social care.

Priority 1: infrastructure for AI and data readiness

AI adoption remains constrained by inadequate digital infrastructure. Some healthcare organisations continue to rely on paper records, face internet disruption, and 45% of National Health Service (NHS) services lack a digital pathway, still relying on paper-based administrative processes.3 4 The 10-year plan does not specify the investment needed. Targeted funding and regional support is required. Without these, AI could widen existing inequalities between organisations and regions.

Interoperability remains critical. Dominance of several electronic health record providers may prohibit integration of novel AI products. Mandated open standards for data exchange (eg, Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) and Observational Medica Outcomes Partnership (OMOP)) and published application programming interfaces are essential to enable innovation while avoiding vendor lock-in.

The creation of a ‘single patient record’ has clear benefits of continuity and transparency but only if the basic infrastructure is right first. Large-scale data initiatives, including the proposed ‘Health Data Research Service’, must prioritise data quality, standardisation and governance. Poorly structured or biased data will undermine the investment in this.

Priority 2: improve innovation of AI to realise real-world value

Both reports advocate reforming the medical technology approvals process, including a proposed single entry point for innovators.1 2 The RAi Working Group responded to a government consultation on a market-approval pathway, arguing that it should incorporate responsible research and innovation principles.5 This means shifting from technology-led to problem-focused development, prioritising patient benefit and early adoption. This approach is outlined in the Life Sciences Sector plan, with National Institute for Health and Care Research governance revised to require a dual mandate for health and growth, ensuring that publicly funded innovation demonstrates patient benefit and economic value.2 NHS priorities can influence technology efforts through National Institute for Health and Care Excellence’s Early Value Assessments, which support and guide the use of immature technologies. Despite challenges, engaging patients and the public is essential, and transparent explanations of benefits, risks and limitations are crucial for trust.

Regulatory sandboxes like the Medicine and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency’s AI Airlock offer opportunities to improve innovation. These controlled environments allow testing technologies using real-world scenarios while managing risk. Early industry collaboration should use existing reporting frameworks, such as the IDEAL framework, DECIDE-AI and FUTURE-AI guidelines.6–8

Priority 3: responsible evaluation, adoption and oversight

Both documents set out the creation of an ‘Innovator Passport’, an assessed once and accepted everywhere approach. However, evaluation must extend beyond clinical and cost-effectiveness to capture broader real-world value, including environmental impact, efficiency, workforce impact and equity.

Current variation in local NHS evaluation creates uncertainty for innovators. National adoption of a standard, holistic, life-cycle based evaluation pathway is needed. Projects like ASSESS-DHT and AdSoLve address these gaps by developing appropriate evaluation frameworks and metrics for complex models. AdSoLve develops mitigating solutions for large language models in healthcare and new evaluation packages, both pertinent given the recent UK Government-OpenAI partnership.6 7

Implementation should take a systems approach, as the value that AI products return will depend on the infrastructure or service model they are deployed within. Datasets used for development and validation should be scrutinised for bias, under-representation and lack of diversity, with transparent reporting of limitations and exclusions, in keeping with standards.8 To aid institutions, we welcome the ‘NHS AI Strategic roadmap’ which should deliver national policy around governance, implementation and post-implementation monitoring that can be used locally, assuring that local deployment is equitable and ethical and robust monitoring measures are installed.

Priority 4: people, trust and system-wide capability

AI implementation is ultimately a sociotechnical challenge. Workforce readiness remains a major barrier, with variable AI literacy and limited formal training. Clinicians increasingly report consultations requiring them to understand and explain AI-generated diagnoses, highlighting the need for professional preparedness.

Education and training must be multilevel: foundation AI literacy for all staff, intermediate training for those using AI tools directly, and advanced understanding for implementation leaders and specialist roles such as clinical safety officers. Professional bodies should formally incorporate AI into continuing professional development and curricula. Simulation-based training offers a safe, effective way to develop skills.

The spreading of innovation equitably requires coordinated networks to prevent innovation from remaining within the ‘Global Institutes’.1 Our RAi NHS Champion network can be best placed here. The RAi Champion network consists of NHS healthcare professionals who have experience in AI transformation and who advocate for best practices. They can act as leaders and advocates for effective and responsible adoption of AI within the NHS.

Patient involvement and digital inclusion are equally critical. While the 10-year plan places patients at the centre of digital transformation, ‘digital by default’ models risk excluding those without access, affordability and literacy. The ‘Digital Inclusion Action Plan’ must be a priority to ensure quality of healthcare is not determined by level of digital literacy.9 Alongside this, we need to ensure our services and pathways for patients still include traditional methods of care as digital needs to be seen as an enabler.

Conclusion

The UK Government’s health and life sciences plans articulate a clear ambition for digital transformation, but delivery will depend on sustained attention to infrastructure, innovation pathways, evaluation, workforce capability and public trust. Without this, there is a real risk that AI will amplify rather than reduce existing inequalities.

The Responsible AI UK Health and Social Care Working Group exists to support this delivery phase. Convening stakeholders and developing and communicating best practice throughout the NHS. Our future work focuses on defining responsible AI adoption, supporting workforce development and ensuring we maintain clinician, patient and public trust throughout the adoption phase.

The challenge now is not whether AI will shape the future of healthcare, but how it will be implemented responsibly, equitably and effectively.
