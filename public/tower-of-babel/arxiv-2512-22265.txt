Representations of the symmetry groups of infinite crystals Preprint: AIP/123-QED
Bachir Bekka, Christian Brouder

Abstract
We investigate the representations of the symmetry groups of infinite crystals. Crystal symmetries are usually described as the finite symmetry group of a finite crystal with periodic boundary conditions, for which the Brillouin zone is a finite set of points. However, to deal with the continuous crystal momentum 𝐤 {\mathbf{k}} required to discuss the continuity, singularity or analyticity of band energies ϵ n ​ ( 𝐤 ) \epsilon_{n}({\mathbf{k}}) and Bloch states ψ 𝐤 \psi_{\mathbf{k}} , we need to consider infinite crystals. The symmetry groups of infinite crystals belong to the category of infinite non-compact groups, for which many standard tools of group theory break down. For example, character theory is no longer available for these groups and we use harmonic analysis to build the group algebra, the regular representation, the induction of irreducible representations of the crystallographic group from projective representations of the point groups and the decomposition of a representation into its irreducible parts. We deal with magnetic and non-magnetic groups in arbitrary dimensions. In the last part of the paper, we discuss Mackey’s restriction of an induced representation to a subgroup, the tensor product of induced representations and the symmetric and antisymmetric squares of induced representations.

Introduction

Space groups and their representations are essential
tools for the theory of electrons, phonons and magnons in solids,
as well as for the description of the crystallographic and
magnetic structures of materials.
Space groups are thoroughly studied and widely used in condensed matter calculations,
many textbooks are devoted to
them

computer packages and web sites (e.g.

) describe their properties.
Still, space groups face a fundamental open problem that we want to solve in the present paper.

The properties of a crystalline sample, which is a finite object,
are most often investigated by representing it either as an infinite crystal or as
a finite crystal with Born-von Kàrmàn boundary conditions
17
linking opposite sides of the crystal
(that we call a
toric crystal
because it is topologically equivalent to a torus).
In solid-state physics, both infinite and toric crystals are used,
in spite of the differences between the two models
51
.

On the one hand, an infinite crystal has to be used when the wavevector
{\mathbf{k}}
varies continuously,
for example to study the continuity of an energy band
E_{n}({\mathbf{k}})
with respect to

{\mathbf{k}}
or to know whether the Wannier functions can be
exponentially localized
25
.
On the other hand, a (finite) toric crystal is used to investigate the symmetry properties
of a crystal, because the symmetry group of a toric crystal is finite and
powerful techniques are available to work with finite groups. However, the Brillouin zone
of a toric crystal is a finite set of points and no continuity or analyticity
questions can be addressed. As a concrete consequence,
Bloch functions computed at a finite number of points of the Brillouin
zone pick up random phases from one point to the other
and making these phases “consistent” is an additional work
that has not to be done in an infinite crystal.
Moreover, it is necessary to address the problem of the influence of the size
of the torus (i.e. the number of unit cells in each direction
for which the toric crystal is periodic) and to prove
the validity of the thermodynamic limit (i.e. the limit
where the size of the torus tends to infinity) for the observables of interest.

In many papers, even by the most distinguised authors, both points of views are
intermingled without proper justification. For instance
the landmark paper of solid-state physics by Bouckaert, Smoluchowski and
Wigner
18
uses both results on irreducible representations
of space groups derived for a finite toric crystal
121

and a continuity argument requiring an infinite crystal.

The present paper aims at providing tools and proofs to deal consistently with
crystal symmetries and continuous wavevectors, i.e. to work with infinite crystals.
The main difficulty is that many problems break loose
when we go from finite to infinite groups: an infinite group can have
infinite-dimensional irreducible representations (as for
the familiar Lorentz group), its collection of irreducible representations can be
so wild that it is not classifiable modulo equivalence
(
10
, Thm. 8.F.3)
, the regular representation
might not be a (generalized) sum of irreducible representations
in a
unique
way
(
10
, Thm. 1.G.11)

and a finite-dimensional representation is
is not necessarily equivalent to a unitary representation
120
,
to name just a few of them.

A further difference specific to the case of crystallographic groups
has to be considered: if
{\mathbf{k}}
is a vector of the Brillouin zone and

{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}
a lattice vector, we can
define
f(n)=e^{in{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}}
for any (signed) integer
n
.
We can see that
f(n)=e^{2\pi i\alpha n}
,
where
\alpha
is a rational number when the crystallographic group is finite
while
\alpha
can be an irrational number when the group is infinite.
In the first case
f(n)
is periodic, it takes a finite number of values and
each value is reached an infinite number of times. In the second
case
f(n)
takes an infinite number of different values and each value
is reached only once. This ergodic behavior considerably complicates
the discussion of the characters of irreducible representations, which
become
almost-period
functions in the sense of von Neumann
131
.
As a consequence of these differences between finite and infinite groups, it is not clear
whether results derived for finite toric crystals are still valid
for infinite crystals.

As we shall see, the main technical difference between finite and infinite groups
is the necessity to use tools from analysis (topology, integrals, distributions, etc.)
instead of finite sums. This beautiful mixture of
group theory and functional analysis is called
harmonic analysis

(see Ref.
102
for a history of the subject).

We not only want to provide a mathematically correct description
of the irreducible representations of infinite crystallographic groups
in arbitrary dimension, but we also wish to
provide safe tools that solid-state physicists can use to investigate infinite crystals.
We deal with crystallographic groups in arbitrary dimension
because crystallographic groups of dimensions higher than 3
(i.e. superspace crystallography) are used
to describe quasicrystals, incommensurately modulated crystals
73

as well as incommensurate magnetic structures
116
.
The extension of results on 3-dimensional space groups to arbitrary dimensions is not always
trivial because some papers use the fact that space groups are solvable
121

and this is no longer true for dimensions
n\geq 4
.

Crystallographic groups were thoroughly studied by mathematicians because
they are related to Hilbert’s 18th problem and the solution of any Hilbert’s problem
brings eternal fame to its solver. However, irreducible representations of
crystallographic groups are not well known in the mathematical literature and we have
recourse to the more general (but highly complex) theory of locally compact groups,
for which approachable textbooks are now available
78
;
10
;
58
;
59
.
Still, the gap between the terminology used in physics and that used in mathematical group theory
(modules, orbifolds, imprimitivity, short exact sequence, Mackey machine, etc.)
is wide and this paper is partly devoted to the translation of these terms into notions commonly
used in physics.

Magnetic crystallographic groups in general dimensions received much less attention
and there are very few works on the representation of
locally compact groups by unitary and antiunitary operators
108
.
Several results are presented at the end of the paper.

In the last section of the paper, we present the generalization
to crystallographic groups of several useful tools:
Mackey’s restriction of an induced representation to a subgroup, the tensor product
of induced representations and the symmetric and
antisymmetric squares of induced representations.

Irreducible representations are implicitly unitary and often called
irreps

for short. The Hilbert spaces considered in this paper are implicitly complex and separable.

II
Crystallographic groups

A crystal is a pattern of atoms and ions which is regularly repeated in all directions.
The pattern of atoms can be a single atom, or a molecule that can be as large as a protein.
In
n
dimensions, the repetition is ensured by symmetry operations denoted by
\{R,{\mathbf{t}}\}

consisting of
a translation by a vector

represented
1

The condition

it conserves the distance between points, which means that it transports

The product of two symmetry operations is defined by

\{R,{\mathbf{t}}\}
is
\{R^{-1},-R^{-1}{\mathbf{t}}\}
and the unit element is

\{{\mathrm{Id}},0\}

A crystallographic group

is a group of symmetry operations which is discrete, in the sense
that the distance between copies of the pattern cannot be infinitesimally small

III
Irreducible representations of the group
T
of translations

The translation group
T
of a crystallographic group
G
has a
very simple structure since, as we shall see, it is isomorphic the commutative
group
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
made of
n
copies of the
group
{\mathbb{Z}}
of (signed) integers where the group product is
the addition of integers. In spite of this simplicity, the construction of the
regular representation of
T
, of its group algebra, of its irreducible
representations and the nature of its characters will require some work
because
T
is an infinite group.

Another (smaller) difficulty is that, although
T
is mathematically similar to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
,
it is physically different from it because the fundamental translations
{\mathbf{a}}_{i}
of
the Bravais lattice have definite lengths related to the distances between atoms in the
crystal. As a consequence, the irreducible representations of
T
will be described
by vectors in the Brillouin zone and the results in the mathematical literature must
be adapted to this situation. We start by defining the Brillouin zone in
n
dimensions.

III.1
Irreducible representations of
T
and the Brillouin zone

Wigner showed
136
that quantum physics describes symmetry
by unitary (or anti-unitary) operators
\rho
acting on some Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
.
In the case of finite toric crystals,
{\mathcal{H}}
is a finite-dimensional vector space
but we now need to deal with possibly infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.

Irreducible unitary representations of commutative finite groups are one-dimensional
and the same is true for commutative locally compact infinite groups
(
59
, Proposition 3.12)

and, being one-dimensional, a representation can be identified
with its character, denoted by
\chi

(
59
, Proposition 3.13)
.

We now relate the product of elements of
T
to the addition
of elements of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
. If
t=\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}
and
t^{\prime}=\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}^{\prime}\}
are elements of
T
,
then
t\cdot t^{\prime}=\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}^{\prime}\}
,
where
{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(m_{i}+m^{\prime}_{i}){\mathbf{a}}_{i}
.
We see that the group
T
with the product
t\cdot t^{\prime}

is isomorphic to the additive group
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
Mathematicians have shown that the
irreducible representations of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
are indexed
by
n
-tuples
(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})
and
take the form
(
58
, Corollary 10.11)

\displaystyle\chi_{(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})}\big((m_{1},\dots,m_{n})\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\imath(m_{1}\theta_{1}+\dots+m_{n}\theta_{n})},

where each
\theta_{i}
runs over the one-torus
\{\theta_{i}\in[-\pi,\pi[\}
with periodic boundary conditions.

To adapt this result to the physics notation, we rewrite the
representations as
\chi_{\mathbf{k}}({\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}})=e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}}
,
where we changed the sign before
\imath
and where

{\mathbf{k}}
belongs to the (first) Brillouin zone of
T

that we construct as follows.

We start by introducing the reciprocal lattice
\Lambda^{*}
of
\Lambda
as the set

\displaystyle\Lambda^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}n_{i}{\mathbf{b}}_{i}|n_{i}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\},

where the reciprocal basis vectors are defined by
{\mathbf{a}}_{i}\cdot{\mathbf{b}}_{j}=2\pi\delta_{ij}
.
By construction, the product
{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}
is an integer multiple of
2\pi

whenever
{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}
belongs to
\Lambda
and
{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}
belongs to
\Lambda^{*}
.
This can also be interpreted by saying that
\chi_{{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}}=\chi_{\mathbf{k}}
,
so that two
{\mathbf{k}}
denote the same irreducible representation if they differ
by an element of
\Lambda^{*}
. This leads us to identify irreducible representations
with vectors in the Brillouin zone
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
, which is defined as the set of
points closer to the origin of
\Lambda^{*}

than to any other point of
\Lambda^{*}
:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{\Omega}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n};||{\mathbf{k}}||\leq||{\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}||\text{ for all }{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\in\Lambda^{*}\}.

This can also be written

\displaystyle{\mathrm{\Omega}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n};-\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}||^{2}\leq{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\leq\frac{1}{2}||{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}||^{2}\text{ for all }{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\in\Lambda^{*}\}.

We observe that
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
is a convex set and that if
{\mathbf{k}}
is
in the interior of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
(i.e. such that
||{\mathbf{k}}||<||{\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}||
for all
{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\not=0
),
it is the unique reference to an irreducible representation, whereas
if
{\mathbf{k}}
lies on the surface of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
(i.e. if

||{\mathbf{k}}||=||{\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}||
for some
{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\not=0
), then
{\mathbf{k}}
is on the plane
bissecting the vector
{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}
and it refers to the
same irreducible representation as
{\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}

which is also on the surface of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
, on the plane
bissecting the vector
-{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}
. Therefore, both planes
must be identified and we recover the topology of an
n
-dimensional torus
of the mathematical description.

III.2
The Brillouin zone as a group

The mathematical literature makes use of the fact that the Brillouin zone is
itself a group
2
.
The “product” of two elements
{\mathbf{k}}
and
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}
of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
is
derived from the product of representations:

\chi_{\mathbf{k}}({\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}})\chi_{{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}({\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}})=\chi_{{\mathbf{k}}\bullet{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}}({\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}})
with

{\mathbf{k}}\bullet{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}={\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}
, where
{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\in\Lambda^{*}
is chosen so
that
{\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}
is in
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
.
This is again a group with an infinite number of elements
but it is now a compact group: the volume
|{\mathrm{\Omega}}|
of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

is finite and can
be calculated explicitly as
|{\mathrm{\Omega}}|=|\det B|
,
where
B
is the
n\times n
matrix whose
columns are
{\mathbf{b}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{b}}_{n}
.

Compact groups such as
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
have almost
all the nice properties of finite groups
122
.
The main difference is that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}f(g)

is  replaced  by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\mathrm{\Omega}}|}\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}f({\mathbf{k}})d{\mathbf{k}}.

The irreducible representations of
the compact commutative group
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

are indexed by
{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\in\Lambda
and
their values are

\chi_{{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}}({\mathbf{k}})=e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}}
.
This relation between the groups
T
and
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

leads to a generalization of the Fourier transform, as
we shall see below.

In the same way as the Bravais lattice
\Lambda
can be
considered as a group whose dual is the group
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
,
the reciprocal lattice
\Lambda^{*}
can be
seen as a group and its (compact) dual group is the unit cell
C

with periodic boundary conditions. If
A
denotes the
n\times n
matrix whose
columns are
{\mathbf{a}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{a}}_{n}
, then
the volume
|C|
of the unit cell of
\Lambda
is
|C|=|\det A|

and the relation
A^{T}B=(2\pi){\mathrm{Id}}
between
A
and
B
implies

\det A\det B=(2\pi)^{n}
and

|{\mathrm{\Omega}}|=(2\pi)^{n}/|C|
.

III.3
Group algebra

When
G
is a finite group, it is very useful to define the
group algebra

A=\{\sum_{g}a_{g}g\}
, where all
a_{g}\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
This algebra enables us to define the sum of two elements

a=\sum_{g}a_{g}g
and
b=\sum_{g}b_{g}g
of
A
by
a+b=\sum_{g}(a_{g}+b_{g})

and the product of an element of
a
by a complex number
\lambda

as
\lambda a=\sum_{g}(\lambda a_{g})g
. The group product
gives us an algebra product:

\displaystyle a\cdot b

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(\sum_{g_{1}}a_{g_{1}}g_{1}\big)\cdot\big(\sum_{g_{2}}b_{g_{2}}g_{2}\big)=\sum_{g_{1}g_{2}}a_{g_{1}}b_{g_{2}}(g_{1}\cdot g_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{g}\big(\sum_{h}a_{gh^{-1}}b_{h}\big)g.

The group algebra enables us to define the regular representation,
projection operators, irreducible idempotents, etc.
94
.
Moreover, a representation
(V,\rho)
of
G
becomes a
representation
3

of
A
defined by
\rho(a)=\sum_{g}a_{g}\rho(g)
.

From the mathematical point of view, it is more
convenient to replace the algebra
A
whose elements are
a=\sum_{g}a_{g}g

by the algebra of functions
f:G\to{\mathbb{C}}
defined by

f(g)=a_{g}
. The sum of elements of the algebra

and its product by a complex number

become the sum of the corresponding functions and its product by
\lambda
.
The product

in the algebra

becomes the convolution product of functions

\displaystyle(f\star f^{\prime})(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in G}f(gh^{-1})f^{\prime}(h).

(1)

Finally, the representation
(V,\rho)
of
G
induces
the representation
\rho(f)=\sum_{g}f(g)\rho(g)
.

For an infinite group such as
T
or
G
, the infinite sum in the convolution
product Eq. (

To deal with this problem we define, for any function

, its
(possibly infinite)

-norm as

\displaystyle||f||_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\sum_{g\in G}|f(g)|^{p}\Big)^{1/p},

(2)

that we extend to

.
The
sequence space

\ell^{p}(G)
is the space of functions
such that
||f||_{p}
is finite. It is a Banach space
(i.e. the vector space is complete for the topology
induced by the metric
||\cdot||_{p}
).
In the case of crystallographic groups
G
, we have

\ell^{p}(G)\subset\ell^{q}(G)
whenever
1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty

.

Two sequence spaces will be particularly useful:
\ell^{2}(G)

because it is a Hilbert space and
\ell^{1}(G)
because it can
be considered as the group algebra of

.

The space
\ell^{2}(G)
is a Hilbert space
(
58
, Thm. 5.45)

when equipped with the scalar product

\displaystyle\langle f,f^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}\overline{f(g)}f^{\prime}(g).

We consider now the Brillouin zone group
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

and functions from
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
to
{\mathbb{C}}
.
Since the Brillouin zone is seen as an
n
-dimensional torus,
these functions are periodic:

f({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}})=f({\mathbf{k}})
for every
{\mathbf{k}}
of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
and
every
{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}
of the reciprocal lattice.
Bloch states
\psi_{n,{\mathbf{k}}}(\mathbf{r})
are examples of these functions
(for each
\mathbf{r}
). The

-seminorm of a measurable function

on

is

\displaystyle||f||_{p}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{|{\mathrm{\Omega}}|}\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}d{\mathbf{k}}|f({\mathbf{k}})|^{p}\Big)^{1/p},

and the function space
L^{p}({\mathrm{\Omega}})
is the set of functions
for which

Again,

III.4
Fourier transformation of

and
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

The Fourier transformation can be generalized to
commutative locally compact groups
(
58

.
In our case, it creates a link between the translation group

the Brillouin zone group
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

If

\displaystyle{\tilde{f}}({\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(f)({\mathbf{k}})=\sum_{{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\in\Lambda}e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}}f({\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}),

When the sum is well defined,

The inverse Fourier transformation is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{-1}({\tilde{f}})({\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{n}})

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\mathrm{\Omega}}|}\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}d{\mathbf{k}}e^{\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{n}}}{\tilde{f}}({\mathbf{k}}),

({\mathcal{F}}^{-1}\circ{\mathcal{F}})(f)=f

In particular, if we consider the function

a useful identity to investigate the band-structure or phonons

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\mathrm{\Omega}}|}\int_{{\mathrm{\Omega}}}e^{\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{n}}}d{\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{{\mathbf{n}},0}.

(4)

By working with the reciprocal lattice group
\Lambda^{*}
and the unit cell group

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|C|}\int_{C}d\mathbf{r}e^{\imath{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{m}}\cdot\mathbf{r}}

Various function spaces are useful to work with Fourier transformation

We only mention

A last application of the Fourier transformation

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\in\Lambda^{*}}e^{\imath{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}d\mathbf{r}e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{r}}f(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\in\Lambda^{*}}e^{\imath{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\cdot\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|C|\sum_{{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\in\Lambda}\delta(\mathbf{r}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}),

and its companion

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\in\Lambda}e^{\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}}

\displaystyle|{\mathrm{\Omega}}|\sum_{{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\in\Lambda^{*}}\delta({\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}).

IV
Representations of crystallographic groups

In this section we describe the general properties of
the unitary representations of crystallographic groups
and we start with the description of some properties of
crystallographic groups.

IV.1
Properties of crystallographic groups

We first consider the conjugacy classes in crystallographic groups.
If
g=\{R,{\mathbf{t}}\}
and
g^{\prime}=\{R^{\prime},{\mathbf{t}}^{\prime}\}

are two operations of a crystallographic group
G
, the conjugation of

g^{\prime}
by
g
is

\displaystyle g\cdot g^{\prime}\cdot g^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{R,{\mathbf{t}}\}\cdot\{R^{\prime},{\mathbf{t}}^{\prime}\}\cdot\{R^{-1},-R^{-1}{\mathbf{t}}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{RR^{\prime}R^{-1},-RR^{\prime}R^{-1}{\mathbf{t}}+R{\mathbf{t}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{t}}\}.

We first check that if
g^{\prime}=\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}
is in
T
, then

g\cdot g^{\prime}\cdot g^{-1}=\{{\mathrm{Id}},R{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}
is in
T
and
T
is an invariant subgroup of
G
.
As a consequence, there is a quotient group
G/T
.
To describe it, we determine when
g=\{R,{\mathbf{t}}_{R}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}
and
g^{\prime}=\{R^{\prime},{\mathbf{t}}_{R^{\prime}}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}^{\prime}\}
belong to the same coset in
G/T
.
We have
g^{\prime}g^{-1}=\{R^{\prime}R^{-1},-R^{\prime}R^{-1}({\mathbf{t}}_{R}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}})+{\mathbf{t}}_{R^{\prime}}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}^{\prime}
; since
{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}

and
-{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}^{\prime}
are in
\Lambda,
, it follows that
g^{\prime}g^{-1}
is in
T
if and only if
R^{\prime}=R
.
In other words, two elements of
G
are in the same coset
iff they have the same
R
and their translation parts differ by
an element of
\Lambda
.
Therefore, the number of cosets is equal to the number of elements of
G_{0}
,
which is finite. In other words, the index
[G{:}T]
of
T
in
G
is finite.

IV.2
The group algebra

For any crystallographic group
G
, the
space
\ell^{1}(G)
equipped with the convolution product
is an algebra
4
, called the
group algebra of
G

This algebra has a unit:
\delta_{g,{e}}
, where
{e}
is the unit of
G
.

It will be useful to define the space
\ell^{\infty}(G)
of

bounded functions
from
G
to
{\mathbb{C}}
, which is the set
of functions
\chi:G\to{\mathbb{C}}
for which there is a number

M
such that
|\chi(g)|<M
for all
g\in G
.
The importance of this set comes from the fact that the
matrix coefficients of unitary representations of
G
belong to
\ell^{\infty}(G)
.
Boundedness implies that there is a well-defined bilinear coupling between
f
in
\ell^{1}(G)
and

\chi
in
\ell^{\infty}(G)
by
(
58
, Def. 5.21)
:

\displaystyle(f,\chi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}f(g)\chi(g),

which is non-degenerate: if
(f,\chi)=0
for all
\chi

then
f=0
and if
(f,\chi)=0
for all
f

then
\chi=0

(
58
, Thm. 5.51)
.
This coupling also defines a norm-preserving isomorphism
between
\ell^{\infty}(G)
and the dual of
\ell^{1}(G)

as well as a norm-preserving injective map
between
\ell^{1}(G)
and the dual of

\ell^{\infty}(G)

(
58
, Thm. 5.51)
.

The group algebra of the Brillouin zone group
\Omega
is more complicated.
A natural candidate for the group algebra seems to be
L^{1}({\mathrm{\Omega}})

with the convolution product

\displaystyle(f\star f^{\prime})({\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\mathrm{\Omega}}|}\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}d\mathbf{q}f({\mathbf{k}}-\mathbf{q})f^{\prime}(\mathbf{q}).

However, the unit of this algebra would be

|\Omega|\delta({\mathbf{k}})
,
where
\delta({\mathbf{k}})
is the Dirac delta distribution,
which is not in
L^{1}({\mathrm{\Omega}})
.

Therefore, we extend the group algebra to
the space of measures

(see
(
58

for a
detailed definition). This extension enables us to include
the Dirac delta distribution and the unit of the algebra

M^{1}({\mathrm{\Omega}})
.
A maybe more familiar way to define

is to start
from
{\mathcal{C}}({\mathrm{\Omega}})
, the space of functions from

{\mathrm{\Omega}}
to
{\mathbb{C}}
, continuous for the topology of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
defined
from the standard metric
||\cdot||
by

d({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})=\min_{{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{m}}\in\Lambda^{*}}||{\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{m}}||
.
The space

can be seen as the dual
{\mathcal{C}}({\mathrm{\Omega}})^{\prime}

of

\lambda:{\mathcal{C}}({\mathrm{\Omega}})\to{\mathbb{C}}

It is a Banach space for the norm

||\lambda||=\sup\{|\lambda(f),f\in{\mathcal{C}}({\mathrm{\Omega}}),||f||\leq 1\}

(this is a consequence of Ref.
(
58

IV.3
The regular representation

For a finite group
G
, the group algebra
is made of elements
a=\sum_{g}a_{g}g
with
a_{g}\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
The
left regular representation
of
h\in G
is defined on this algebra by

\displaystyle\lambda_{G}(h)\big(\sum_{g}a_{g}g\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{g}a_{g}(h\cdot g)=\sum_{g}a_{h^{-1}\cdot g}\,g.

If we consider now the group algebra seen as
the algebra of functions
f:G\to{\mathbb{C}}
,
the (left) regular representation of the group algebra
transforms the function
f

into the function
\lambda_{G}(h)f
defined by

\big(\lambda_{G}(h)f)(g)=f(h^{-1}\cdot g)
,
where
g
and
h
are in
G
.

In the case of a crystallographic group
G
, we can no longer use
arbitrary functions because
G
is infinite and the correct space for the regular representation
turns out to be
\ell^{1}(G)
acting on the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(G)
. For any

f\in\ell^{2}(G)
we can write again
(
59
, Section 3.3)

\big(\lambda_{G}(h)f)(g)=f(h^{-1}\cdot g)
.
So we can extend the left regular representation
from
G
to the group algebra
\ell^{1}(G)
and a function
f
of
\ell^{1}(G)

is represented on the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(G)

by the convolution product
(
59
, Def. 3.15)

\displaystyle\lambda_{G}(f)v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\star v,

for every
v\in\ell^{2}(G)
.

IV.4
Irreducible representations

We must be more precise in the definition of irreducible
representations of infinite groups.
A unitary representation
({\mathcal{H}},\rho)
of a locally compact group
G

is said to be
reducible
if there is a non-zero
closed proper subspace
V
of
{\mathcal{H}}

such that
\rho(g)V\subset V
for every
g\in G

(if
V
is not closed, it can be replaced by its
closure
(
59
, Prop. 3.5)
).
A representation is
irreducible
if it is not reducible.
Note that Schur’s lemma is still valid for unitary representations of
locally compact groups
(
10
, Prop.1.A.11)
.

Two irreducible representations
({\mathcal{H}}_{1},\rho_{1})
and

({\mathcal{H}}_{2},\rho_{2})
are considered equivalent if
there exists a unitary operator
U
from
{\mathcal{H}}_{1}

to
{\mathcal{H}}_{2}

such that
\rho_{1}(g)=U^{\dagger}\rho_{2}(g)U
for all
g\in G
.
We denote by
{\widehat{G}}
the set of equivalence classes of
irreducible representations of
G
.

Irreducible representations of locally compact groups can be of
type I, II or III
10
. The precise definition of
these three types would bring us too far and suffice it to say that
representations of type I are relatively nice while those of type II and III can be quite wild.
Elmar Thoma proved a theorem
128

that turns out to be crucial for our work:
a discrete group
G
has a commutative
invariant subgroup
T
with a finite index if and only if
all unitary representations of
G
are of type I;
in this case, all irreducible unitary representations of
G

are finite dimensional and their dimensions
cannot be larger than the index.

Crystallographic groups
G
correspond to the
hypothesis of Thoma’s theorem because they
are discrete groups with a commutative
invariant subgroup (the group
T
of translations)
and the index of
T
is the number of elements

|G_{0}|
of the point group
G_{0}
of
G
.
Since this number is finite, all representations
of
G
are of type I and the irreducible
representations of
G
have dimension
smaller or equal to
|G_{0}|
.
This result will enable us to link the representations
of infinite crystals to those of finite toric crystals.

The second central result we need is that,
when all representations of a group are of type I,
there is a canonical decomposition into irreducible
representations
(
10
, Thm. 6.D.7)
.
We shall see that any irreducible
representation
\rho
of a crystallographic group
G
is entirely
determined by a vector
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathrm{\Omega_{0}}}
and
a projective representation
\tau
of
G_{0}({\mathbf{k}})

and we denote it
\rho_{{\mathbf{k}}\tau}
instead of
\rho
.

With this notation, for every representation
({\mathcal{H}},\sigma)

of
G
in a (separable) Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
,
there exist a
unique
set of (possibly infinite) integers
\{m_{{\mathbf{k}},\tau}\}

and a positive measure
\mu
on
{\mathrm{\Omega_{0}}}

(i. e. the kernel of a positive distribution)
such that:

\displaystyle\sigma(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{\oplus}_{{\mathrm{\Omega_{0}}}}\mu({\mathbf{k}})d{\mathbf{k}}\bigoplus_{\tau}m_{{\mathbf{k}},\tau}\rho_{{\mathbf{k}},\tau}(g),

(8)

where
{\mathrm{\Omega_{0}}}
is the irreducible Brillouin zone
{\mathrm{\Omega}}/G_{0}
,

\tau
runs over the small representations of
G({\mathbf{k}})

(or equivalently the irreducible projective representations of
G_{0}({\mathbf{k}})
),

\rho_{{\mathbf{k}},\tau}
is the representation of
G
induced from
the representation
\tau
and

m_{{\mathbf{k}},\tau}
is the number of times the representation
\rho_{{\mathbf{k}},\tau}

appears in
\sigma
.
The notation
\bigoplus_{\tau}
and
\int^{\oplus}
stands for
direct sum and direct integral.

We can separate the translation part and the rotation part in the usual way:

\displaystyle\sigma\big(\{R,{\mathbf{t}}_{R}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int^{\oplus}_{{\mathrm{\Omega_{0}}}}\mu({\mathbf{k}})d{\mathbf{k}}e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}}\bigoplus_{\tau}m_{{\mathbf{k}},\tau}\rho_{{\mathbf{k}},\tau}\big(\{R,{\mathbf{t}}_{R}\}\big).

This decomposition is unique.
Note that when a representation is not of type
I, several decompositions exist with inequivalent
irreducible representations
(
10
, Thm. 1.G.11)
,
a situation that would be very awkward for quantum
physics applications.

IV.5
Decomposition of the regular represention
and Plancherel theorem

As an example of the decomposition an infinite dimensional
representation into irreducible representations, we discuss
the decomposition of the regular representation introduced
in section
IV.3
.

For any
f\in\ell^{1}(G)
and any irreducible representation

we define the matrix

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\mathbf{k}}\tau}(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}f(g)\sigma_{{\mathbf{k}}\tau}(g),

and its Hilbert-Schmidt norm

Then,
(
82
, Thm. 4.4)
gives us the Plancherel formula

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}|f(g)|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\mathrm{\Omega_{0}}}|}\int_{{\mathrm{\Omega_{0}}}}d{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\tau}\dim\tau\,||\sigma_{{\mathbf{k}}\tau}(f)||^{2},

where
\tau
runs over the projective representations of

,

|{\mathrm{\Omega_{0}}}|=|{\mathrm{\Omega}}|/|G_{0}|

Brillouin zone
and

Correspondingly, the regular representation

of

the following decomposition

\displaystyle\lambda_{G}(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\mathrm{\Omega_{0}}}|}\int_{{\mathrm{\Omega_{0}}}}d{\mathbf{k}}\bigoplus_{\tau}\big(\sigma_{{\mathbf{k}}\tau}(g)\otimes{\mathrm{Id}}_{\dim\tau}\big).

V.1
Projection-valued measures

We first recall that a measure is a way to quantify the size of sets.
Intuitively, the measure
of a group of disjoint objects is the sum of the measures of each object
and the measure of nothing is zero.
In mathematical parlance, if
X
is a set and
\Sigma
a collection of
subsets
5

of
X
, then
\mu:\Sigma\to[0,+\infty]
is a measure if:
i)
\mu(\emptyset)=0
and ii) if
(E_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a (countable) collection
of disjoint sets in
\Sigma
(i.e.
E_{i}\cap E_{j}=\emptyset
if
i\not=j
), then

\displaystyle\mu\Big(\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\mu(E_{i}).

Besides, an orthogonal projection on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}

is a linear map
P:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
such that

P^{2}=P
and
P^{\dagger}=P
.

From these definitions the following concept of
a
projection-valued measure
was created
(
59
, Def. 2.22)
.

Definition 1
.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
denote a Hibert space,
X
a set and

\Sigma
a collection of subsets of
X
.
A
projection-valued measure

P
is a map from
\Sigma

to the set of orthogonal projections on
{\mathcal{H}}
satisfying

1.

P(\emptyset)=0
and
P(X)={\mathrm{Id}}

2.

For all countable collection
\{E_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of pairwise
disjoint sets in
\Sigma
and for every
v\in{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle P\Big(\bigcup_{i\in I}E_{i}\Big)v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}P(E_{i})v.

3.

For any
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
in
\Sigma

\displaystyle P(E_{1}\cap E_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(E_{1})P(E_{2}).

Familiar examples of projection-valued measures are the spectral
projections onto the eigenspaces corresponding to a set of eigenvalues.
In quantum physics, the projection valued-measured corresponding to
the Hamiltonian
H
and the set of
energies

\displaystyle P(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{e_{\min}\leq e_{n}\leq e_{\max}}|\phi_{n}\rangle\langle\phi_{n}|,

It is often convenient to write this projection-valued measure as

\displaystyle P(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\gamma}\frac{dz}{z-H},

(where

.

Projection-valued measures were also used to investigate the localizability of
quantum mechanical systems

64

Projection-valued measures describe pure quantum states
and their generalization to mixed states

\displaystyle\sigma\big(({\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{n}})\big)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|{\mathrm{\Omega}}|}\int_{\mathrm{\Omega}}e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{n}}}P({\mathbf{k}})d{\mathbf{k}}.

Moreover, these projections are covariant:

\displaystyle\sigma\big((R,{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{n}})\big)P({\mathbf{k}})\sigma\big((R,{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{n}})\big)^{-1}

\displaystyle P(R^{-1}{\mathbf{k}}).

Finally, if

Projections in

V.2
Action of
G
on
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

To be able to use the mathematical results that we need,
we must show that the (left) action of the crystallographic group

G
on the Brillouin zone (or more precisely on the
characters of
T
) is
nice enough
.
We first describe this action and in the next section we
define what we mean by “nice enough”.

The left action of an element
g
of
G
on
a character
\chi
of
T
is the character

g\triangleright\chi
of
T
defined by

(g\triangleright\chi)(t)=\chi(g^{-1}tg)
.
We need
g^{-1}tg
and not
gtg^{-1}
to ensure that

g^{\prime}\triangleright(g\triangleright\chi)=(g^{\prime}g)\triangleright\chi
.
For a crystallographic group operation, we have

\displaystyle\big(\{R,{\mathbf{t}}\}\triangleright\chi_{{\mathbf{k}}}\big)\big(\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{{\mathbf{k}}}\Big(\{R^{-1},-R^{-1}{\mathbf{t}}\}\cdot\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\cdot\{R,{\mathbf{t}}\}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{{\mathbf{k}}}\big(\{{\mathrm{Id}},R^{-1}{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\big).

But

\displaystyle\chi_{{\mathbf{k}}}\big(\{{\mathrm{Id}},R^{-1}{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(R^{-1}{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}})}=e^{-\imath(R{\mathbf{k}})\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}}=\chi_{R{\mathbf{k}}}\big(\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\big).

In other words
\{R,{\mathbf{t}}\}\triangleright\chi_{{\mathbf{k}}}=\chi_{R{\mathbf{k}}}
.
In solid-state physics, we rather consider the related action of
G
on
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

defined by
\{R,{\mathbf{t}}\}\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}=R{\mathbf{k}}
.
This leads us to the following familiar concepts of the theory of space groups:

1.

the
little group
6
of
{\mathbf{k}}
, that we denote by
G({\mathbf{k}})=\{\{R,{\mathbf{t}}\}\in G;R{\mathbf{k}}={\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\text{ for some }{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\in\Lambda^{*}\}

2.

the
star of
{\mathbf{k}}
, also called the
orbit of
{\mathbf{k}}
, which is the set

{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbf{k}}=\{R{\mathbf{k}};\text{ for all }\{R,{\mathbf{t}}\}\in G\}
, also denoted by
\star{\mathbf{k}}
.

In the mathematical literature, the little group and the orbit are built by acting
on irreducible representations of
T
instead of on
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

and the condition is
R{\mathbf{k}}={\mathbf{k}}
instead of
R{\mathbf{k}}={\mathbf{k}}+{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}

but the two are equivalent when
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
is assumed to be periodic.

The general theorem asserts that, if the action of
G
on
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
is nice enough,
every irreducible representation of
G
arises as an induced representation
of some irreducible representation of
G({\mathbf{k}})
for some
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathrm{\Omega}}
.
The technical translation of “nice enough” is that the action of

G
on
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
must be
regular
.
A frustrating aspect of the application of mathematical theorems to physics is
that the hypotheses of the theorems must be checked.
This is the purpose of the next section.

V.3
Regularity of the action

The action of a crystallographic group
G
on the Brillouin zone
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

is regular if the following
two conditions hold
(
59
, Def. 7.6)
:

1.

The space of orbits of the action of
G
on
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
is countably separated:
there is a countable family
(E_{j})
of

G
-invariant Borel subsets of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
such that each orbit
{\mathcal{O}}

is the intersection of all
E_{j}
that contain
{\mathcal{O}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{O}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcap\{E_{j};{\mathcal{O}}\subset E_{j}\}

(14)

2.

For every
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathrm{\Omega}}
, the map
G/G({\mathbf{k}})\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbf{k}}
given
by
g\cdot G({\mathbf{k}})\mapsto g\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}
is a homeomorphism.

The purpose of this section is to show that the action of
a crystallographic group
G
on its Brillouin zone
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

is regular. But we first give a familiar example of an
ergodic (i.e. non-regular) action.

V.3.1
Regularity versus ergodicity

The action of an infinite group can
have an unexpected behavior, called ergodicity.
A simple example of ergodicity is given by the action
of the additive group of signed integers
{\mathbb{Z}}
(seen as a one-dimensional crystal) on the unit circle

C
by

\displaystyle m\triangleright\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\cos(\theta+2\pi m\alpha)\\
\sin(\theta+2\pi m\alpha)\end{array}\right),

where
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
If
\alpha
is a rational number
\alpha=p/q
, then the orbit of a point
M
of
C
under the action
of
{\mathbb{Z}}
is a (finite) set of
q
points (assuming
p
and
q
to be relatively prime)
and the action of
{\mathbb{Z}}
on
C
is regular.
If
\alpha
is irrational, then the orbit of any point
M
of
C
under the action of
{\mathbb{Z}}

is an infinite set of points which is dense in
C
: for any point
M^{\prime}
of
C

and any
\epsilon>0
, there is a
m\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
||m\triangleright M-M^{\prime}||<\epsilon
.
Kirillov
(
81
, p. 118)
gives other simple examples of groups with ergodic action.

Such an ergodic behavior can be very desirable, for instance
in statistical physics
101
, but this
not what we need in crystallography because we would not
be able to define the irreducible Brillouin zone, as we shall see.
Therefore, we prove now that the action of
G

on
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
is regular.

V.3.2
Topology of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

As a first step, we must determine the proper topology
of the Brillouin zone.
According to the general theory
(
58
, Corollary. 10.7)
,
the set
\widehat{N}
of characters of a commutative locally compact group
N

(i.e. the
Pontryagin dual
of
N
) is a commutative locally compact group
for the pointwise product of characters
(i.e.
(\chi\cdot\chi^{\prime})(g)=\chi(g)\chi^{\prime}(g)
for every
g
in
N
)
where
\widehat{N}
is equipped with the compact-open topology
(i.e. the topology of uniform convergence on compact sets).
For countable discrete groups, this means that
a sequence
\{\chi_{n}\}
of characters of
N
converges
to the character
\chi
iff for any
g
in
G

and any
\epsilon>0
, there is an integer
n_{0}
such that

|\chi_{n}(g)-\chi(g)|<\epsilon
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
For the translation group
T
, this condition becomes

|e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}_{n}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}}-e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}}|<\epsilon

for all
n\geq n_{0}
.
By using

|e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}_{n}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}}-e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}}|=|e^{-\imath({\mathbf{k}}_{n}-{\mathbf{k}})\cdot{\mathbf{t}}}-1|

and
2|\alpha|/\pi\leq|e^{-\imath\alpha}-1|\leq|\alpha|
for
|\alpha|\leq\pi
,
we see that, if
{\mathbf{k}}
is in the interior of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
,
the compact-open topology on
\widehat{T}
is equivalent to
the metric topology on
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
, where the sequence

\{{\mathbf{k}}_{n}\}
converges to
{\mathbf{k}}
iff, for every
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, there
is an integer
n_{0}
such that
||{\mathbf{k}}_{n}-{\mathbf{k}}||<\epsilon^{\prime}
whenever
n>n_{0}
.
To be able to encompass points
{\mathbf{k}}
on the surface of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
, we must take
the periodic property of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
into account and
replace the distance
||{\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}||
by
the distance
d({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})=\min_{{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{m}}\in\Lambda^{*}}||{\mathbf{k}}-{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}+{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{m}}||
.

V.3.3
Countably-separated orbits

Having identified the proper topology on
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
, we
use the fact that
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
has
a countable family of open sets
19
that we
build now.
Let
\{{\mathbf{k}}_{n}\}
be a countable set of points of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

which is dense in
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
. For example, this can be
the set of points of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
whose coordinates are rational numbers.
We define the balls
U_{n,m}=\{{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathrm{\Omega}};d({\mathbf{k}},{\mathbf{k}}_{n})<1/m\}
,
where
d
is the distance defined in the previous section.
These balls are a countable base of the topology of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
:
for any
{\mathbf{k}}
in
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
and any open set
U
of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
containing

{\mathbf{k}}
, there is a ball
U_{n,m}
such that

{\mathbf{k}}\in U_{n,m}\subset U
.

Let
g_{1},\dots,g_{q}
be representatives of the cosets of
T
in
G
,
where
q={[}G{:}T{]}
and let

E_{n,m}=\{g_{i}\triangleright{\mathbf{k}};i=1,\dots,q\text{ and }{\mathbf{k}}\in U_{n,m}\}
,
where we recall that
g\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}=R{\mathbf{k}}
with

R
the rotation part of
g
.
Since the action of
g
on
{\mathbf{k}}
does not involve the translation part of

,
we see that the orbit of the action of

on

is

. In other words

\displaystyle G\triangleright U_{n,m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{g\triangleright{\mathbf{k}};g\in G\,\,\text{and}\,\,{\mathbf{k}}\in U_{n,m}\}=E_{n,m}.

The set
E_{n,m}
is
G
-invariant because it is the orbit of

.
It is also a Borel set. Indeed, for every

,
the set

E_{i}=\{g_{i}\triangleright{\mathbf{k}};{\mathbf{k}}\in U_{n,m}\}

is a Borel set, since it is the image of the Borel set

under the homomorphism
{\mathbf{k}}\mapsto g_{i}\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}

and
E_{n,m}=\bigcup_{1}^{q}E_{i}
is a finite union of Borel sets.

We prove now that the collection

separates the different orbits of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
.
Let
{\mathbf{k}}
and
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}
be two points of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
such that

G\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}\not=G\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}
. Since orbits
are either identical or disjoint, this means that

g_{i}\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}\not=g_{j}\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}
for every

and
j

in
\{1,\dots,q\}
and there is a minimal distance

between the set

\{g_{i}\triangleright{\mathbf{k}};i=1,\dots,q\}

and the set
\{g_{i}\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime};i=1,\dots,q\}
.
Of course, we allow
g_{i}\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}=g_{j}\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}

for some

.
By choosing
m>2/\epsilon
and

such that

and

{\mathbf{k}}\in U_{n,m}
, we see that

(G\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime})\cap E_{n,m}=\emptyset

and

separates the orbit of
{\mathbf{k}}

from the orbit of
{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}
.

To conclude that we have indeed condition (i),
we use the fact that any orbit

is included in the intersection of the

’s containing

{\mathcal{O}}
. Then, for any orbit

{\mathcal{O}}^{\prime}=G\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}^{\prime}
in this intersection,
we just proved that if

is not included
in
{\mathcal{O}}
, then there is a

that
separates it form

V.3.4
Irreducible Brillouin zone

A first consequence of condition (i) is that
we can define an
irreducible Brillouin zone
{\mathrm{\Omega}}/G={\mathrm{\Omega}}/G_{0}
.
Indeed, it follows from (i) that
we can find a set of representatives for
{\mathrm{\Omega}}/G

which is a Borel subset of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
.

We saw that, for
g=\{R,{\mathbf{t}}\}
in
G
,

g\triangleright\chi_{{\mathbf{k}}}=\chi_{R{\mathbf{k}}}
,
so that only the rotation part
R
of
g

acts on
\chi_{\mathbf{k}}
. Since
R
runs over
G_{0}

when
g
runs over
G
, the quotient of the Brillouin
zone by
G
is the irreducible Brillouin zone
{\mathrm{\Omega}}/G_{0}
,
which is a convex subset of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
such that

{\mathrm{\Omega}}
is the union of
R\triangleright({\mathrm{\Omega}}/G_{0})

where
R
runs over
G_{0}
and the volume
of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}/G_{0}
is the volume of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}

divided by the number of elements of
G_{0}
.
In mathematical parlance, irreducible Brillouin zones
are called

and their construction is
gently described in Ref.
142

Irreducible Brillouin zones are widely used in
solid-state physics because they reduce the
computing time of band-structure calculations
(see Refs.
123
;
77

for nice pictures of irreducible Brillouin zones).

If regularity condition
(i)
were not satisfied,

{\mathrm{\Omega}}/G
would not be a measurable subset of
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
.

V.3.5
Proof of condition (ii)

To show that condition (ii) holds for crystallographic groups,
we first recall that the quotient
G/H

of a group
G
by its subgroup
H
,
is the set of the left cosets of
H
in
G
.

The translation group
T
is clearly a subgroup of
G({\mathbf{k}})
.
Since the index
[G{:}T]
of

in
G
is finite,
the index
[G({\mathbf{k}}){:}T]
is also finite and it divides
[G{:}T]
.
If we denote by
p=[G{:}G({\mathbf{k}})]
the index of
H=G({\mathbf{k}})
in
G
,
the quotient of
G
by
H
is
G/H=\{g_{1}\cdot H,\dots,g_{p}\cdot H\}

and we can define a map
\varphi:G/H\to{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbf{k}}
by

\varphi(g_{i}\cdot H)=g_{i}\triangleright{\mathbf{k}}=R_{i}{\mathbf{k}}
, where

R_{i}
is the rotation part of
g_{i}
.
Define the action of
G
on
G/H

by
g\triangleright(g_{i}\cdot H)=g_{j}\cdot H
,
where
g_{j}
is the representative of
g\cdot g_{i}

(i.e. there is a
h\in H
such that
g\cdot g_{i}=g_{j}\cdot h
).
Then we see that the second condition is satisfied since

\varphi
is a bijection between finite sets.

We are now ready to reap the fruits of our efforts.

V.4
Mackey’s imprimitivity theorems

Mackey’s work on the irreducible representations
of certain locally compact groups is a true masterpiece of mathematics
130
.

Although the concept of imprimitivity is probably unknown to
most physicists, it has a very ancient and distinguished origin since
it can be traced back to Evariste Galois in the 1830’
as
groupe non primitif

110
;
23
.
The concept was then more clearly defined
by Camille Jordan
(
76
, p. 34)
. It was translated into
german by Dyck in 1883
48
either as
Imprimitivität

(
48
, p. 83)

or
Nichtprimitivität

(
48
, p. 86)

and stabilized as
imprimitive Gruppe
by Frobenius in 1895

.
Burnside translated it into
imprimitive group
in 1897
(
29
, p. 193)
.
The relevance of these concepts for locally compact groups was
stressed by Mackey
96
.

We now present a theorem which is equivalent to step (vi) showing that
all irreducible representations of
G
can be obtained by the construction.
We first recall the definition of a representation
\sigma
of
G

induced from a representation
\rho
of
G({\mathbf{k}})
.
Let
g_{j}
with
j=1,\dots,|G/G({\mathbf{k}})|
be a complete set of representatives
of the cosets of
G({\mathbf{k}})
in
G
and let

\displaystyle\sigma(g)_{ir,js}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in G({\mathbf{k}})}\rho(h)_{rs}\delta_{h,g_{i}gg_{j}^{-1}}.

(16)

Then,
\sigma
is a representation of
G
called the representation of
G

induced from the representation
\rho
of
G({\mathbf{k}})
. The following
theorems describe the nature of these induced representations.

The first theorem
(
59
, Thms. 7.4)

states that, since the action of
G
on
{\mathrm{\Omega}}
is regular,
any unitary irreducible representation of
G

can be obtained by induction from an irreducible
representation of a little group
G({\mathbf{k}})
.

Let
G
be a crystallographic group and
T
its subgroup of translations.
For every unitary irreducible representation
({\mathcal{H}}_{\sigma},\sigma)
of

G
, there is unique orbit
{\mathcal{O}}
and, for any
{\mathbf{k}}

satisfying
{\mathcal{O}}_{\mathbf{k}}={\mathcal{O}}
, there is a unitary irreducible

({\mathcal{H}}_{\rho},\rho)
of
G({\mathbf{k}})

such that

is equivalent to
the representation of
G
induced from
the representation

.

As in the case of finite space groups

we see that the irreducible representations of

come from the
small representations

({\mathcal{H}}_{\rho},\rho)

of some little group

, which are
representations such that, for any

\rho(t)=\chi_{\mathbf{k}}(t){\mathrm{Id}}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{\rho}}
.

We just showed that each irreducible representation of

can be obtained from an
irreducible representation of some

. Now we deal with
step (v) by proving that each representation induced from a small representation of
a little group is irreducible. This is the
second fundamental theorem
(

which holds again because the action of

on

is regular:

Let
G
be a crystallographic group and
T
its subgroup of translations.
For any
{\mathbf{k}}\in{\mathrm{\Omega}}
and for any small representation

({\mathcal{H}}_{\rho},\rho)
of
G({\mathbf{k}})

of
G
induced from

representation of
G
.

V.4.1
Constructing the irreps of the little groups

We used a rather heavy mathematical machinery to prove
that all the irreps of a crystallographic group
G
can be obtained form
irreps of
G({\mathbf{k}})
for some
{\mathbf{k}}
.
This may seem to be a small progress because

G({\mathbf{k}})
is also a crystallographic group with an infinite number of irreps.
Moreover,
G({\mathbf{k}})=G
when
{\mathbf{k}}=0
.
However the crucial point is that we need only the small
representations of
G({\mathbf{k}})
and there are
only a
finite
number of them.

Since
T
is an invariant subgroup of
G({\mathbf{k}})
,
we can obtain representations of
G({\mathbf{k}})
by induction
from representations
\chi_{\mathbf{k}}
of
T
.
We must show that these induced representations are
small representations and that all small representations
can be obtained by such an induction.
In the finite group case, this is done by using
the Frobenius reciprocity theorem
(
80
, Section 14.4)
.

In locally compact groups the Frobenius reciprocity theorem is not always valid,
but it turns out that it is valid for countable discrete groups such as
crystallographic groups
98
.
Therefore, the proof of the finite group case can
be followed (but see Ref.
100
for a proof in the context of
locally compact groups) and the small representations of

G({\mathbf{k}})
are built in terms of projective
representations of the point group
G_{0}({\mathbf{k}})=G({\mathbf{k}})/T
, which is called
the

of
{\mathbf{k}}
.
Note that
G_{0}({\mathbf{k}})
is a subgroup of the point group
G_{0}
of
G

because
T
is an invariant subgroup of
G({\mathbf{k}})
.

The cogroup
G_{0}({\mathbf{k}})
is the set of elements
R
of
G_{0}

such that

for some
{\mathbf{k}}_{\mathbf{n}}\in\Lambda^{*}
.
Let
(V,\rho)
be a small representation of

.
We first notice that

is entirely determined on

because,
by definition of small representations, in any basis of

\displaystyle\rho\big(\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathbf{k}}\big(\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\big){\mathrm{Id}}_{d}=e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}}{\mathrm{Id}}_{d},

where

.

On the other hand, we know that all elements of

can
be written
\{R,{\mathbf{t}}_{R}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}
for some
{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\in\Lambda

From
\{R,{\mathbf{t}}_{R}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}=\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\cdot\{R,{\mathbf{t}}_{R}\}
,
we can write

\displaystyle\rho\big(\{R,{\mathbf{t}}_{R}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho\big(\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\big)\rho\big(\{R,{\mathbf{t}}_{R}\}\big)=e^{-\imath{\mathbf{k}}\cdot{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}}\rho\big(\{R,{\mathbf{t}}_{R}\}\big).

(17)

Therefore,

is entirely determined if we know

\rho\big(\{R,{\mathbf{t}}_{R}\}\big)
for each

.
Choose a collection of representatives

with

,
where

are the elements of the group

.
Every

of

can be written

To determine

we compute

\displaystyle g_{i}\cdot g_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{R_{i}R_{j},R_{i}{\mathbf{t}}_{j}+{\mathbf{t}}_{i}\}.

Let

in

. There is a unique

\displaystyle g_{i}\cdot g_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{R_{i}R_{j},R_{i}{\mathbf{t}}_{j}+{\mathbf{t}}_{i}\}=\{R_{k},{\mathbf{t}}_{k}+{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}=\{{\mathrm{Id}},{\mathbf{t}}_{\mathbf{m}}\}\cdot g_{k}.

V.4.2
A happy conclusion

We gave an example of the fact that the action of

on
the circle can be ergodic if an irrational number is involved.
By construction, the wavevectors of a finite toric crystal
are fractions of the reciprocal basis vectors. It could have been
feared that the action of a crystallographic group on
a wavevector involving irrational numbers might have
been ergodic. We showed that this is not the case.

By using the regularity of the action of a crystallographic group

and
the Mackey machine, we showed that, for every wavevector

as for the case of a finite toric crystal (for which

are rational numbers).
We also showed that all the irreducible representations of

are obtained by this construction.
Therefore, the happy conclusion of our work is that
the worst behaviors of infinite groups do not take
place in crystallographic groups and the construction of their
irreducible representations is similar to that of
finite toric crystals.

Corepresentations of magnetic groups

We recall that a magnetic group

in

If

(resp.

(resp.

of index 2 (

translationgleich

and

is a subgroup of

of index 2 (

is then said to be a

klassengleich

(

.

Since
G_{+}
is a subgroup of
G
of index 2,

is a left coset
G_{-}=rG_{+}
for any

The quotient

is a group containing two elements: the identity

representing the elements of

and the element
\xi
representing the
elements of
G_{-}
. There is a homomorphism from

to

(

. In particular, since

is a group,
the inverse of
\xi
can only be
\xi
and
\xi^{2}=1
. As a consequence
of the homomorphism,
(ru_{1})(ru_{2})\in G_{+}
for any
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
in
G_{+}
. In particular
r^{2}\in G_{+}
.

Let us define antiunitary operators.
An operator
O:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathcal{H}}
acting on a Hilbert space

is antilinear
if
O(v_{1}+v_{2})=O(v_{1})+O(v_{2})
and

O(\lambda v)=\overline{\lambda}O(v)
,
for every

,
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
in
{\mathcal{H}}
and
\lambda
in
{\mathbb{C}}
, where
\overline{\lambda}

is the complex conjugate of

.
The adjoint
O^{\dagger}
of the antilinear operator

is defined by

\langle v_{1},Ov_{2}\rangle=\langle v_{2},O^{\dagger}v_{1}\rangle
.
Then, an operator
O
is antiunitary if it is antilinear
and
O^{\dagger}O=OO^{\dagger}={\mathrm{Id}}
.

A corepresentation of
({\mathcal{H}},\sigma)
of

is a homomorphism
\sigma
of

into the group of

such that

is unitary for every

and antiunitary for every

.
The corepresentation

{\mathcal{H}}
and
\{0\}
are the only closed
\sigma(G)
-invariant subspaces of

.

VI.2
Corepresentation of
G
from representation of
G_{+}

We show now that an irreducible corepresentation of
G
can be expressed in terms of
a representation of the subgroup
G_{+}
.
Let
({\mathcal{H}},\sigma)

be an irreducible corepresentation of
G
.
The restriction
\sigma|_{G_{+}}
of
\sigma
to
G_{+}
is
a unitary representation of the crystallographic group
G_{+}
.
If we could find a
\sigma(G_{+})
-invariant subspace
V
of
{\mathcal{H}}

such that the representation of the crystallographic group

G_{+}
defined on
V
is irreducible, we could proceed
with the analysis of
\sigma.
However, for an infinite group
G
, a subgroup

H
and an irreducible representation
({\mathcal{H}},\sigma)
of

it may happen that such a subspace

does not exist.

For instance, let
G
be the countable discrete group
\left\{g_{ab}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb{Q},a\neq 0\right\}

and fix a non-trivial unitary character
\delta
of
\mathbb{Q};
the representation
\sigma
of

on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\})

defined by
\sigma(g_{ab})f(x)=\delta(bx)f(ax)
is irreducible (see
(
10
, Rem.3.C.5)
).
The subgroup
H=\left\{g_{a0}:a\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}\right\}

is a subgroup of

but there is no subspace of

which is invariant and irreducible under

\sigma(H)
.
Indeed, as

is commutative, such a subspace

would have to be one-dimensional
and there would be a non-zero function

and a unitary character
\chi
of

such that
\sigma(g_{a0})f=\chi(g_{a0})f

As a consequence, the function
|f|
would satisfy

for every

but the zero function is the only dilation invariant function in

Coming back to our irreducible corepresentation
({\mathcal{H}},\sigma)
of the magnetic
G

it has been shown

(see also Wigner

is finite dimensional) that

is irreducible or
\sigma|_{G_{+}}
is a direct sum of two
irreps of
G_{+}.

As we know that the irreps of a crystallographic group are finite dimensional,
we see that there is a finite dimensional
\sigma(G_{+})
-invariant subspace

which is irreducible under
G_{+}
.

Let
\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{d}
be a basis of
V
.
The action of
G_{+}
on
V

is written

\displaystyle u\triangleright\psi_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\rho(u)_{ji}\psi_{j}.

For an arbitrary
r
in
G_{-}
, set
\psi^{\prime}_{i}=r\triangleright\psi_{i}
.
The action of an element

of
G_{+}

\displaystyle u\triangleright\psi^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(ur)\triangleright\psi_{i}=r(r^{-1}ur)\triangleright\psi_{i}.

Since

\displaystyle u\triangleright\psi^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r\triangleright\sum_{j}\rho(r^{-1}ur)_{ji}\psi_{j}=\sum_{j}\overline{\rho(r^{-1}ur)_{ji}}\,\,r\triangleright\psi_{j}

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\overline{\rho(r^{-1}ur)_{ji}}\psi^{\prime}_{j},

Denote

It is easy to see that

From now on, we can follow without change the argument
given by Wigner

;

VI.3
Classification of corepresentations

Let
\sigma

in the restriction

We classify

There are three cases

1.

If

Wigner showed that

.

(a)

(first type for
(

(
20

M\overline{M}=+\rho(r^{2})
,
then the irreducible corepresention

is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho(u),

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho(ar^{-1})M,

its representation space is
V=W
and the dimension of
\sigma
is the same as the dimension of
\rho
.

(b)

(second type for
(
136
, p. 344)
, type (b) for
(
20

, the
irreducible corepresentation

is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\rho(u)&0\\

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}(a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\pm\rho(ar^{-1})M\\
\rho(ar^{-1})M&0\end{array}\right),

2.

(third type for
(

An important conclusion is that all irreducible corepresentations of

The permutation group reduction of tensor powers of corepresentations

VII
Induced representation theorems for crystallographic groups

Induced representations are a standard tool of quantum physics
and books are essentially devoted to their enumeration
87

and to their applications in the elecron theory of solids, for
the description of defects in crystals and
second-order phase transitions,
as well as in phonon spectroscopy and
magnetism
8
;
52
;
53
.

As a simple example of induced representations,
consider the computation of molecular orbitals
of methane
140
;
141
.
The methane molecule (CH
4
) has the symmetry group
T_{d}

while each hydrogen atom has only the symmetry subgroup
C_{3v}
.
The irreducible representation describing the atomic orbitals of H
can generate orbitals of the whole molecule
by induction of an irreducible representation of

C_{3v}
to a representation of
T_{d}
.
Similarly, the irreducible representations of
C_{3v}
describing the
vibration of a hydrogen atom can be used to induce
a representation of
T_{d}
describing the vibration of all the hydrogen atoms in
the methane molecule.

In this section we discuss several operations that can be
carried out with induced representations : restriction of an induced
representation to a subgroup, tensor product of induced representations,
symmetric and antisymmetric squares of induced representations.

As a word of caution it must be stressed that,
although induced representations of locally compact groups
are well defined, the induced representation of
more general infinite groups from a unitary representation
of a subgroup does not always exist, and when it does
it can be not uniquely defined
86
;
85
.

VII.1
Double cosets

In section
V.4
we considered crystallographic groups

G
and subgroups
H
of
G
of finite index
[G{:}H]
.
In the present section we consider general sugroups
H

that can be finite, for example of the finite symmetry group (i.e. stabilizer) of a particular
site of the crystal, or infinite, for example
the infinite subgroup of
H
that stabilizes a plane in

In both cases, the index of the subgroup is infinite
and we must deal with an infinite
set of coset representatives
\{g_{j}\}_{j\in J}=G/H
where

is
a countable set. The choice of

in each coset must
be explicit for practical applications
(and to avoid the Axiom of choice).
We still have the decomposition

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{j\in J}g_{j}H.

Since this section relies on double cosets, we first define this concept.
Let
G
be a group,
H
and

two subgroups of

.
For each

(or

Two elements

The set of all double cosets is denoted by

a countable set and

is a

of the double coset

The group

is the disjoint union of its double cosets

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{j\in J}Hg_{j}K.

Again, for practical applications, it is
useful to explicitly determine the representatives

, as done for halite in

Double cosets are a bit like cosets but do not share all their
properties: different double cosets can have different numbers
of elements, which do not necessarily divide the order of

In this section, the subgroup

This is because there is an obvious bijection between

The structure of such product of groups can be seen

This provides an explicit description of double cosets

For example, let

be a complete set of representatives of the

\displaystyle g_{j}Kg_{j}^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{p\in P}K_{j}h_{p}.

Then,

\displaystyle Hg_{j}K

\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{p\in P}Hh_{p}g_{j},

VII.2
Mackey’s restriction formula

Mackey’s restriction theorem
97
gives a precise solution to the following
problem. Consider a group
G
, two subgroups

H
and
K
and a representation
({\mathcal{H}}_{\rho},\rho)
of
K
.
If
\sigma
is the representation of
G
induced from
\rho
,
what is the result of the restriction of
\sigma
to the subgroup
H
?
For example, if we consider a space group
G
and
an atom at (Wyckoff) positions
(x)
, we can build
an orbital of the crystal by induction from an orbital
of the atom. This is a classical problem of solid-state physics
that has been discussed from the group-theoretical point of view
since the early sixties
88
;
36

(see Ref.
52
for more details).
In general we start from an atomic orbital
which is an irrep of the symmetry point group (i.e. stabiliser)
K

of position
(x)
and induce a representation of
G

by the usual formula. This infinite-dimensional representation
can be expanded into irreducible representations of
G
, but
Mackey’s restriction formula enables us to avoid this step.
Now we wonder how this induced representation is seen from another
atom at (Wyckoff) positions
(y)
with symmetry point group
H
.
In mathematical terms, we induce a representation
\sigma
of
G
from the representation

\rho
of
K
and we compute the restriction of
\sigma
to
H
.

Mackey’s restriction formula
precisely describes
this situation for finite groups
97
and
then for some locally compact groups
98
.
Fortunately, crystallographic groups satisfy the conditions of
validity of Ref.
98
because
two arbitrary subgroups
H
and
K
of a crystallographic group
G
are
discretely related

in the sense of section 7 of Mackey’s paper
98
since

G
, being a countable set, is a countable union of
double cosets
H\backslash G/K
.

To state Mackey’s restriction formula,
let
\{g_{j}\}_{j\in J}
, where
J
is a countable set,
be a complete set of representatives
of the double cosets
H\backslash G/K
, so that

G=\cup_{j\in J}Hg_{j}K
. Define the sugroups
K_{j}=g_{j}Kg_{j}^{-1}\cap H

of
H
. If
({\mathcal{H}},\rho)
is a representation of
K
, then

\rho_{j}(k)=\rho(g_{j}^{-1}kg_{j})
, for

, is a representation of
K_{j}
.
If
\sigma
is the representation induced from
\rho
to
G
,
Mackey’s restriction formula
(
98
, Thm. 7.1)
states that the restriction of
\sigma
to
H
is

\displaystyle({\mathrm{Ind}}_{K}^{G}\rho)|_{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma|_{H}=\bigoplus_{j\in J}{\mathrm{Ind}}_{K_{j}}^{H}\rho_{j}.

We describe more explicitly
{\mathrm{Ind}}_{K_{j}}^{H}\rho_{j}
. Since
K_{j}
is a subgroup
of
H
, we can write
H=\cup_{i}h_{i}K_{j}
, where
\{h_{1},\dots,h_{q}\}
is a complete
set of representatives of the cosets of
K_{j}
in
H
.
For
k
an element of
K_{j}
, we know that
g_{j}^{-1}kg_{j}
is an element
of
K
and
\rho_{j}(k)=\rho(g_{j}^{-1}kg_{j})
.
By definition (
16
) of induced representations,
if
\tau_{j}={\mathrm{Ind}}_{K_{j}}^{H}\rho_{j}
, then

\displaystyle\big(\tau_{j}(h)\big)_{mr,ns}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in K_{j}}\rho_{j}(k)_{rs}\delta_{k,h_{m}hh_{n}^{-1}}.

Mackey’s restriction formula was used in
concrete physical applications

;
52
.

As an illustration of Mackey’s restriction formula,

Halite is a ionic crystal made of ions Na

VII.4
Mackey’s decomposition formulas

For the investigation of selection rules and Clebsch-Gordan coefficients,
it is necessary to have a precise description of the tensor
product of two irreducible representations. We observed that
irreducible representations of crystallographic groups can always
be induced from irreducible representations of subgroups.
Therefore, it is useful to describe the tensor product
of induced representations.

As a preparation for Mackey’s decomposition formula,
we start with a simpler situation. If
H
is a closed subgroup of a locally compact group
G
,
then for arbitrary representations
\rho
of
G
and
\tau
of
H

the following relation holds
(
78
, Thm. 2.58)

\displaystyle\rho\otimes{\mathrm{Ind}}_{H}^{G}\tau

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ind}}_{H}^{G}(\rho|_{H}\otimes\tau).

We want to determine what happens when
\rho
itself is an
induced representation, in other words, we want to decompose
the tensor product of two induced representations
in terms of induced representations.

As we shall see, this problem was solved by
Mackey’s decomposition formula

for finite groups
97
and for some infinite groups
98
, under the
same conditions as for Mackey’s restriction formula. Therefore,
this formula holds for crystallographic groups.
Mackey’s decomposition formula was used (without proof)
by Bradley
22
to determine
selection rules and by Patricia Gard
61

and Rhoda Berenson
12

to compute Clebsch-Gordan coefficients of space-group representations. Since then,
Mackey’s method is commonly used because of the simplification
it brings to the understanding and calculation of
Clebsch-Gordan coefficients
129
;
43
;
44
;
45
;
50
.

Let
G
be a crystallographic group and
H
and
K
two subgroups of
G
.
Let
g_{j}
, with
j\in J\subset{\mathbb{N}}
(where
J
can be infinite)
be a complete set of representatives
of the double cosets
H\backslash G/K
, so that

G=\cup_{j\in J}Hg_{j}K
. Define again the sugroups
K_{j}=g_{j}Kg_{j}^{-1}\cap H

of
H
.
If
({\mathcal{H}},\rho)
is a representation of
K
, then

\rho_{j}(k)=\rho(g_{j}^{-1}kg_{j})
, for
k\in K_{j}
, is a representation of
K_{j}
.
If
\sigma
is a representation of
H
,
then Mackey’s decomposition formula states that
(
98
, Thm. 7.2)

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ind}}_{H}^{G}\sigma\otimes{\mathrm{Ind}}_{K}^{G}\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{j\in J}{\mathrm{Ind}}_{K_{j}}^{G}\big(\sigma|_{K_{j}}\otimes\rho_{j}\big).

In words, the left hand side of this formula
is the tensor product of the representations of
G
induced
from the representation
\sigma
of
H
and the
representation
\rho
of
K
.
For the right hand side, we consider the subgroup
K_{j}
of

H
, we make the tensor product
of the restriction of
\sigma
to
K_{j}
by the
representation
\rho_{j}(k)=\rho(g_{j}^{-1}kg_{j})
of
K_{j}
,
we induce a representation of
G
from this tensor
product, and we sum over
j
.

For future reference, we write the case where
K=H
and
\sigma=\rho
:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ind}}_{H}^{G}\rho\otimes{\mathrm{Ind}}_{H}^{G}\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{j\in J}{\mathrm{Ind}}_{H_{j}}^{G}\big(\rho|_{H_{j}}\otimes\rho_{j}\big),

where
H_{j}=g_{j}Hg_{j}^{-1}\cap H
and

\rho_{j}(h)=\rho(g_{j}^{-1}hg_{j})
is a representation of
H_{j}
.

Bradley
22
made a detailed application of Mackey’s decomposition formula
for the irreducible representations of space group
P23

(N
∘
195 in Ref.
68
).
Wawrzycki
132
extended these formulas to the case of Krein spaces
(a generalization of Hilbert space useful in quantum field theory

).

VII.5
Mackey’s decomposition of symmetric and antisymmetric squares

Symmetric and antisymmetric squares of representations have
many applications in quantum physics.
The most common ones come from the fact that antisymmetric squares describe
fermions (e.g. coupled electrons) while symmetric squares
describe bosons (e.g. coupled phonons).
For example, they were used
to investigate Cooper pairs

;
141

or unconventional gaps
126

superconductivity
111

and soft phonon modes in antiferromagnets

Bradley describes

transitions between an initial and a final states when the final state of
is related to the initial state by time
reversal (see also
(
20
, Sect. 4.8)
).
Finally, a group
G
is said to be multiplicity-free when
the decomposition of the tensor product of two irreducible representations of
G

does not contain any irreducile representation of
G
more than once
(simply reducible groups listed by Luan
93

multiplicity-free).
Dealing with non multiplicity-free groups is possible

30

keeping track of this multiplicity generates a cluttering of indices.
Fortunately,

and all three-dimensional crystallographic point groups
are multiplicity free, except for the tetrahedral groups
T
and
T_{h}

for which the square of the irrep
T
(not to be confused with the group
T
)
contains twice the irrep
T

(
7

\displaystyle T\otimes T

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A\oplus{}^{1}E\oplus{}^{1}E\oplus T\oplus T.

These two

can be uniquely specified
by requiring that one is in the symmetric square of
T

and the other in its antisymmetric square.
Bradley discussed the case of finite groups and we extend his
work to infinite crystallographic groups.

In the case of a
finite

Mackey proved a decomposition formula for
the symmetric and antisymmetric squares of an induced representation of
G

as a sum of induced representations
(
99

(see also
(
42

The case of classical groups was treated by Magaard et al

At the end of his paper, Mackey writes that he intends
to discuss the case of infinite groups in “a subsequent paper" which,
as far as we know, was never published.

We prove now that his formulas remain valid for crystallographic
groups and, more generally, for discrete countable groups.
We prove this in detail to illustrate the kind of work that
has to be done to go from a finite-group property to
its infinite-group counterpart.
Let

VIII
Conclusion

This paper is a contribution to the formulation of the physics of
infinite crystals, avoiding periodic boundary conditions and questions
about the validity of the thermodynamic limit.
Our purpose was to close the gap between the description of the
irreducible representations of the symmetry group of (finite) toric crystals
and the use of infinite crystals required
to investigate the analytical properties of eigenstates and eigenenergies
of band structure calculations.
We presented here the properties of irreducible representations
of infinite crystals that are similar to those
of (finite) toric crystals.

The present work can be extended in several directions: by investigating
more general groups or by studying other properties of group representations.
For example, spin-space groups were first considered by Brinkman
and Elliott
24
and they
play an important role in the study of electronic band structure
118

of magnon band topology
38
and altermagnetism.
Spin-space groups (in dimension 3) were recently enumerated
34
;
75
;
138
.
By generalizing the case of 3-dimensional spin-space groups
91

we can define spin-space groups in dimension
n
as any subgroup of the direct product

G\times SO(n)\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
, where
G
is a crystallographic group
and
SO(n)\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
acts on the spin-variable. Since
SO(n)
is a
compact group, the derivation of the irreducible representations of
spin-space groups for infinite crystals does not
meet additional difficulty. Note that
SO(n)\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}

can be replaced by any compact group, for instance
U(n)
.
Our results on magnetic space groups can also be
extended to colored space groups
89

and the related Mackay groups
95
;
72
.
It is possible to define induced corepresentations of magnetic
groups
52
;
117
. It would be interesting to
extend Mackey’s restriction and decomposition formula to that case.

The results of the present paper may give the impression that
infinite crystals are very much like finite toric crystals
and that the presence of an infinite number of elements
and of irrational-valued coordinates in the Brillouin
zone is harmless.
This is however not the case for objects such as the characters of these representations,
which are no longer periodic functions but become ergodic
as in the example of
f(n)=e^{2\pi i\alpha n}
in the introduction.
Since character theory is such a powerful tool for finite groups,
it would be interesting to investigate how much of it can
be adapted to infinite crystallographic groups.
We intend to discuss this question in a forthcoming publication.
