Positive psychological capital and antenatal depression: the chain-mediating roles of social support and maternal health literacy in rural China
Authors: Shi-Yun Wang, Dan Luo, Yi-Qi Lai, Xing Huang, Rong Wang, Chao-Yue Zhai, Wei-Cai Zeng, Yan-Qiong Ouyang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntenatal depression (AND) is a common complication during pregnancy, particularly in rural China, where access to mental health resources is limited. This study aimed to examine the relationship between positive psychological capital (PsyCap) and AND, and to explore the mediating roles of maternal health literacy (MHL) and social support (SS).MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 1,683 pregnant women from seven rural regions in China. Participants completed validated instruments measuring PsyCap, SS, MHL, and AND. Data were analyzed using Pearson correlations, hierarchical regression, and structural equation modeling. Mediation effects were tested with 5,000 samples bootstrapping.ResultsA total of 51.1% of participants screened positive for AND (M = 9.91). Positive psychological capital was significantly associated with lower depression scores (β = -0.32, p < .01). Maternal health literacy (β = -0.13, p < .01) and social support (β = -0.22, p < .01) each partially mediated this relationship. Additionally, a mediation model suggested that higher PsyCap was associated with higher MHL and greater SS, and associated with lower depressive symptoms. The final model explained 28.0% of the variance in depression scores.ConclusionsPositive psychological capital is negatively associated with antenatal depression, and this association is partially explained by higher maternal health literacy and social support. These findings highlight the potential importance of focusing on internal psychological resources, maternal health literacy, and social support in rural maternal mental health research. The results suggest that future interventions for AND in rural areas could explore community-based and digital approaches to improve maternal health literacy and social support.Trial registrationThe study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committees of Wuhan University (No. WHU-LFMD-IRB2023069) and Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University, Zhuhai ([2023] 2023ZH0092).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03595-y.Keywords: Antenatal depression, Positive psychological capital, Chain-mediated effect, Rural pregnant women

Background

Antenatal depression, defined as depression occurring during pregnancy, is a prevalent complication with significant consequences for mothers, children, families, and society [1]. The global prevalence of perinatal depression is high, with rates reaching up to 28.5% [2]. In China, the prevalence of antenatal depression (AND) ranges from approximately 10% to 30%, with an average rate of 19.7% and an increasing trend over the past decade [3]. Representing a global statistic, between 32 and 57% of antenatal depression is not detected early due to the overlap of its symptoms with normal pregnancy changes, and only a fraction of affected women receive adequate psychological treatment [4, 5]. Antenatal depression is associated with negative outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and long-term neurodevelopmental problems in children [6, 7].

Pregnant women in rural areas face a heightened risk of mental health issues. Studies have reported prevalence rates of 18.2% in the second trimester and 20.1% during the third trimester in rural China [8]. This elevated risk is largely due to the disproportionate impact of adverse social determinants of health in rural settings [9]. Factors such as socioeconomic status, education, income, and geographic region have been found to be strongly related to maternal mental health outcomes and may partly explain differences in prevalence across populations [10]. Moreover, China has pronounced regional disparities in economic development, with rural women in less developed areas often experiencing greater social and healthcare disadvantages [11].

Given these disparities, rural women face disproportionate social, economic, and healthcare disadvantages that heighten vulnerability to poor mental health outcomes. Addressing antenatal depression in rural areas is therefore crucial for reducing inequities and improving maternal and child health at a population level.

Psychological capital, social support, and maternal health literacy as factors influencing antenatal depression in rural areas

The psychological well-being of pregnant women, especially in rural areas, is influenced by a complex interplay of various factors, including psychological capital, social support, and maternal health literacy. Positive psychological capital (PsyCap) refers to an individual’s positive psychological state, comprised of optimism, hope, resilience, and self-efficacy, which can be developed through training [12, 13]. Positive psychological capital serves as a key internal resource that helps buffer stress and unlock individual potential [14]. Pregnant women with higher PsyCap are better equipped to cope with the psychological, physiological, and social changes during pregnancy, thereby reducing negative emotions and preventing adverse outcomes [15]. Although PsyCap has been linked to depression, research focusing on maternal PsyCap—especially among rural women—remains limited [16].

Social support is widely recognized as a key factor in safeguarding mental health during pregnancy [17]. In rural China, where limited access to social support networks is a common challenge, studies have shown that pregnant women with lower levels of social support are more likely to experience negative mental health outcomes, such as depression and anxiety [18]. Moreover, lower socioeconomic status and family instability, which are prevalent in these regions, further heighten the vulnerability of pregnant women, contributing to poorer mental health [19]. Effective social support provides both informational and emotional assistance, which helps pregnant women better understand and manage the health challenges they face during pregnancy [20]. It also improves their ability to acquire and apply health knowledge, thus enhancing maternal health literacy and reducing the risk of prenatal depression [21].

Maternal health literacy (MHL), defined as the ability to access, comprehend, and apply health-related information, significantly affects a woman's psychological adaptation to pregnancy [21]. Women with higher MHL are better equipped to understand and use the information provided by their support networks, translating it into beneficial health behaviors and emotional coping strategies [22, 23]. As a result, women with higher health literacy can more effectively communicate with family and friends, thereby reducing stress and anxiety during pregnancy [24]. Low health literacy may prevent pregnant women from fully understanding or applying the information received from their support networks, leading to increased psychological burden and a higher risk of antenatal depression [25].

During pregnancy, adequate social support provides both emotional and informational resources, while higher health literacy enables women to effectively process this information and adopt healthy coping mechanisms to manage pregnancy-related stress. The interaction of these factors helps enhance self-efficacy and psychological resilience, core components of positive psychological capital, thereby reducing the likelihood of depressive symptoms [26]. This effect is especially pronounced in rural areas, where limited social support and maternal health literacy may leave pregnant women more vulnerable to psychological distress. In rural areas, where social support and maternal health literacy are often constrained, the interactions between these factors can further exacerbate the vulnerability of pregnant women to perinatal mental health issues [27]. Therefore, understanding the relationships between SS, MHL, and PsyCap is essential for developing contextually relevant interventions aimed at improving maternal well-being in underserved communities, yet little is known about how these factors interact within an integrated framework, particularly among rural pregnant women.

Potential mediating roles of social support and maternal health literacy

Although previous studies have examined the individual effects of PsyCap, SS, and MHL on mental health, few have integrated these variables into a unified model, particularly among pregnant women in rural China [16, 25, 28]. Understanding the combined mechanisms of these factors may reveal more effective pathways to reduce antenatal depression in underserved populations. Positive psychological capital is recognized as a key psychological asset that enhances emotional regulation and coping with stress [29]. Empirical evidence suggests that higher PsyCap improves perceived social support by facilitating positive interpersonal interactions and increasing optimism toward help-seeking [12]. PsyCap has also been linked to better health behaviors and more active information-seeking, which may contribute to higher levels of maternal health literacy [30]. In addition, social support provides informational, emotional, and instrumental resources that can improve women’s ability to acquire, understand, and use health information, thereby enhancing maternal health literacy. Both social support and maternal health literacy have independently been linked to fewer depressive symptoms in perinatal populations [31]. These findings suggest that SS and MHL not only protect against depression on their own but may also function as mediators linking PsyCap to maternal mental health outcomes.

Critical Resource Theory (CRT) provides the conceptual framework for this study [12], emphasizing that key internal resources enable individuals to activate and sustain external and behavioral resources, forming a positive cycle of resilience and well-being [32]. In this study, psychological capital is the core internal resource, while social support and maternal health literacy are external and behavioral resources. According to CRT, higher PsyCap is expected to enhance the use of SS and MHL, which are critical for managing stress and preventing antenatal depression. In addition, the Conservation of Resources (COR) Theory further supports the proposed model by clarifying the dynamic interaction between resource investment, acquisition, and psychological outcomes [29]. COR Theory posits that individuals are motivated to acquire, protect, and accumulate valuable resources, and that stress arises when these resources are threatened, lost, or insufficiently gained following investment [29]. Psychological resources such as PsyCap not only buffer against resource loss but also facilitate the acquisition of additional resources, including social support and health literacy. These, in turn, contribute to improved psychological outcomes, such as reduced antenatal depression. Complementing these perspectives, Social Support Theory emphasizes the protective effects of support, and Stress and Coping Theory suggests that effectively utilizing resources can reduce negative psychological outcomes [33, 34]. Together, these theories provide a comprehensive rationale for hypothesizing that PsyCap influences AND through SS and MHL.

The present study

Therefore, the present study proposes a mediation model in which PsyCap influences AND directly and indirectly through SS and MHL. Specifically, PsyCap may increase SS, which in turn enhances MHL, ultimately leading to fewer depressive symptoms. The following hypotheses were formulated: (1) Maternal PsyCap, SS, and MHL are each negatively associated with AND. (2) SS mediates the relationship between PsyCap and AND. (3) MHL mediates the relationship between PsyCap and AND. (4) MHL and SS jointly form a chain mediating the pathway linking PsyCap to AND.

Methodology

Participants and recruitment

The target population of this study consisted of pregnant women residing in rural areas of China. The inclusion criteria for participants are as follows: (1) rural residency and long-term habitation in the area (defined according to the 2006 Regulations on the Statistical Division of Urban and Rural Areas by the National Bureau of Statistics of China, where “rural” refers to areas outside of officially designated urban regions, typically agricultural and less densely populated); (2) clinically confirmed pregnancy; (3) age 18 years or older; (4) minimum education level of junior high school; (5) able to independently complete the questionnaire via smartphone.

A convenience sampling method was employed. Participants were recruited from rural areas in seven regions of China (Northeast, East, North, Central, South, Southwest, and Northwest). This regional coverage was intended to enhance geographic diversity and improve the generalizability of findings to rural communities nationwide. In each region, at least a level II or higher hospital was selected (a regional general hospital with at least 100 inpatient beds that provides comprehensive medical and preventive services and serves as a technical center for a county or district). Pregnant women were recruited in the outpatient department to complete an online questionnaire from December 2023 to February 2024.

Eligible pregnant women were approached while waiting for outpatient services and were invited to participate by trained research assistants in each hospital. Before participation, two trained researchers explained the study’s purpose, procedures, risks, and benefits to the participants. Written informed consent was obtained, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. Confidentiality of personal information was strictly maintained. Subsequently, participants who agreed to participate were provided with a QR code link to an online questionnaire survey platform called "Wenjuanxing". The participants were asked to scan the QR code using their smartphones, which led them to the electronic version of the survey. Each participant independently completed the survey, and the research team assisted with requests. Each participant completed the survey in approximately 15–20 min.

A total of 2,128 pregnant women met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria and were initially enrolled. Among the eligible participants, 445 were excluded for the following reasons: incomplete questionnaires (n = 262), contradictory responses (e.g., reporting no complications while selecting “severe complications”) (n = 105), and illogical or patterned responses (e.g., identical answers throughout) (n = 78). The final sample comprised 1,683 valid responses. Participants completed a sociodemographic information form, the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), Positive Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PPQ), Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), and Perinatal Maternal Health Literacy Scale.

Pilot testing and questionnaire refinement

A pilot test was conducted with 60 eligible pregnant women to evaluate the clarity and comprehensibility of the questionnaire items. Minor wording adjustments were made based on participant feedback. These pilot participants were not included in the main study sample.

Measures

Sociodemographic data

A demographic questionnaire was developed based on a literature review. Items included age, residence, occupation, education, marital status, per capita monthly household income, conception type, pregnancy stage, and health-related characteristics. (See Appendix A.)

Positive Psychological Capital Questionnaire (PPQ)

This questionnaire was developed by Luthans [12], and the Chinese version was developed by Zhang [35]. The PPQ includes 26 items measuring optimism (e.g., “I always see the positive side of things”), hope (e.g., “I pursue my goals with confidence”), self-efficacy (e.g., “I always accomplish tasks successfully”), and resilience (e.g., “I recover quickly after setbacks”). Five reverse-scored items (items 8, 10, 12, 14, and 25) help control for response bias. Responses use a 7-point Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 26 to 182. Higher scores indicate greater PsyCap. The overall scale showed high internal consistency (α = 0.927). Subscale Cronbach’s alphas were 0.883 for self-efficacy, 0.796 for resilience, 0.865 for hope, and 0.887 for optimism.

Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)

The Chinese version of the EPDS, translated by Wang, Guo [36], assesses perinatal depressive symptoms. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0–3), with a total score of 10 or above indicating clinically significant depressive symptoms. Sample items include statements such as “I have been anxious or worried for no good reason” and “I have felt sad or miserable.” This scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in Chinese perinatal populations [37]. Higher total scores on the EPDS indicate more severe depressive symptoms, with a typical cutoff of 10 or higher suggesting clinically significant symptoms. Cronbach’s α in this study was 0.890.

Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS)

The Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS) was developed by Xiao [38] and is widely used in Chinese populations to assess levels of social support. It contains 10 items, with items 1–5 and 8–10 scored from 1 to 4. Sample questions include “How many close friends or relatives do you have from whom you can get support?” “When you are in trouble, how often do you seek help from others?” Higher scores reflect greater perceived and received social support. The total score is calculated by summing the item scores across the three dimensions. In this study, Cronbach’s α for the total scale was 0.819. Cronbach’s alphas of the subscales in this study were 0.799 for subjective support, 0.710 for objective support, and 0.713 for support utilization.

Perinatal Maternal Health Literacy Scale (PMHLS)

The Perinatal Maternal Health Literacy Scale was developed by Wang [39]. The scale includes functional health literacy (e.g., “Newborns may experience physiological weight loss within the first week of birth”), communication health literacy (e.g., “I actively seek maternal health information from medical professionals”), and critical health literacy (e.g., “I critically evaluate the reliability of online maternal health information”). A total score of ≥ 204 (80% of 255) indicates adequate health literacy. Four reverse-scored items [6, 14, 20, 23] were recoded before analysis to ensure consistency. Total scores range from 0 to 255, with higher scores indicating greater health literacy. The scale has demonstrated good psychometric properties in previous studies. In this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.934, with subscale alphas of 0.894 for functional health literacy, 0.896 for communication health literacy, and 0.899 for critical health literacy.

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using SPSS 24.0 and AMOS 25.0. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and the four core constructs. Standardized scores were used in correlation and regression analyses. Pearson correlation analysis examined bivariate associations among PsyCap, SS, MHL, and AND. To test the conceptual model, we employed a combination of hierarchical regression analysis and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), each serving complementary purposes. Hierarchical regression, conducted in SPSS, assessed the predictive effects of PsyCap, SS, and MHL on AND while controlling for relevant covariates. Structural Equation Modeling was conducted in AMOS 25.0 to examine latent relationships among constructs. As all scales have been previously validated in Chinese populations and are widely used, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was not performed. The structural model was estimated directly, and model fit was evaluated using multiple indices: chi-square/degrees of freedom (2/df), GFI, AGFI, NFI, RFI, IFI, TLI, CFI, and RMSEA. Mediation effects of SS and MHL in the relationship between PsyCap and AND were tested using the Bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

Common method biases tests

Harman’s single-factor test was used to assess common method bias. A total of 97 items from the four core scales were included in the exploratory factor analysis. The results identified 19 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. The largest single factor explained 17.449% of the variance, which is well below the 40% threshold recommended by Podsakoff, MacKenzie [40]. This indicates that common method bias is unlikely to significantly affect the interpretation of the findings.

Descriptive statistics

A total of 2,128 questionnaires were distributed online, of which 1,683 were valid, yielding a valid response rate of 79.09%. Detailed demographic data are presented in Appendix B. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 50 years (M = 29.87, SD = 4.60). In terms of regional distribution, 21.81% were from East China (n = 367), 15.51% from South China (n = 261), 20.14% from Central China (n = 339), 13.90% from North China (n = 234), 18.95% from Southwest China (n = 319), 5.35% from Northeast China (n = 90), and 4.34% from Northwest China (n = 73). By pregnancy trimester, 27.81% were in the first (n = 468), 44.62% in the second (n = 751), and 27.57% in the third trimester (n = 464). The majority had a college education or below (n = 896, 53.24%), and 66.13% (n = 1113) had received pre-pregnancy healthcare.

Bivariate correlations

Descriptive statistics of key variables

The average AND scores were 9.91 ± 5.90 (range = 0–27). About 51.1% (n = 843) of participants met the clinical cutoff for depression (≥ 10 points). The average score for PsyCap was 122.89 ± 24.48 (range = 35–180), while the mean total score for social support was 40.49 ± 8.41 (range = 12–59). The average score for maternal health literacy was 191.65 ± 24.88 (range = 99–244).

Demographic differences in measured outcomes

The Chi-square tests and one-way ANOVA were used to examine demographic differences in study variables (see Appendix C). Results showed significant differences in AND, PsyCap, SS, and MHL across regions and income levels (p < 0.001). Participants from Southwest China reported the highest prevalence of AND (61%), as well as significantly higher depression scores (M = 12.48, SD = 7.08) and lower levels of PsyCap (M = 105.67, SD = 26.30), SS (M = 36.06, SD = 10.33), and MHL (M = 184.04, SD = 30.39). Construction of structural equation model and testing of mediation effect

The structural equation model showed a good overall fit to the data (χ2/df = 4.72, GFI = 0.981, CFI = 0.971, TLI = 0.958, RMSEA = 0.047), all within the recommended thresholds. As presented in Table 3 and Fig. 3, PsyCap showed a significant positive association with maternal health literacy (β = 0.34, z = 10.29, p < 0.01) and social support (β = 0.30, z = 8.53, p < 0.01). In turn, MHL positively predicted SS (β = 0.35, z = 9.42, p < 0.01). Regarding antenatal depression, PsyCap (β = −0.29, z = −10.21, p < 0.01), MHL (β = −0.10, z = −3.44, p < 0.01), and SS (β = −0.29, z = −8.18, p < 0.01) showed significant negative associations. These path coefficients suggest that higher PsyCap is associated with lower levels of AND, with MHL and SS potentially functioning as independent mediators. Moreover, the sequential pathway is consistent with a potential chain mediation effect. Taken together, these findings are consistent with Hypotheses 1 through 4.

Discussion

Prevalence of antenatal depression and its associated demographic factors

This study investigated the role of social support and maternal health literacy on the association between positive psychological capital and antenatal depression in 1,623 pregnant rural Chinese women. The average EPDS score was 9.91 ± 5.90, and 51.1% of participants screened positive for depression (EPDS ≥ 10), a rate notably higher than the national average of 10–30% reported in previous studies [41].

Several factors may explain this high prevalence. One key factor is that all participants were from rural areas, where lower education levels may reduce emotional regulation and coping skills. Consistent with findings by Nisar, Yin [3], women with less education showed higher depression rates, likely due to limited cognitive and self-management abilities. Economic hardship, especially in underdeveloped southwestern regions, contributes to increased risk. For instance, chronic financial stress and social disadvantage are known triggers for psychological distress in rural women [42]. Additionally, rural areas often lack mental health resources, and both residents and providers tend to prioritize physical over mental health [43]. Moreover, traditional gender norms intensify emotional burden. Rural women may feel pressure to prioritize family needs, avoid male providers, and experience anxiety due to persistent son-preference ideologies [44]. Further, physical discomfort during pregnancy (e.g., fatigue, nausea) may further increase vulnerability to depression [1]. Lastly, methodological differences in depression screening tools may partially explain the high rates. These findings underscore the urgent need for early detection and culturally tailored mental health interventions in rural China.

Relationship between psychological capital, social support, maternal health literacy, and antenatal depression among rural pregnant women

The average PsyCap score among participants was 122.89 ± 24.48, indicating a moderate level. PsyCap was negatively associated with AND and served as a significant direct predictor, consistent with earlier research [16, 45]. Psychological capital enables pregnant women to maintain an optimistic attitude under pressure, enhances resilience, and promotes adaptive coping strategies such as seeking social support [46]. These strengths reduce emotional distress and help prevent depression during pregnancy [47].

The mean MHL score was 191.65 ± 24.88, indicating a generally low level. A significant negative correlation between MHL and AND was observed. Low MHL may limit women's ability to recognize symptoms, seek professional care, or navigate support systems, increasing vulnerability to depression [48]. Similar issues have been reported not only in China but also in other countries and regions [49–51]. The trend of inequitable health service utilization among rural, remote, and socioeconomically disadvantaged mothers is a global concern, which may be attributed to healthcare personnel shortages and the need to travel long distances to access maternal services. This reflects a widespread challenge of inadequate health facility coverage in underserved areas. Despite unprecedented advancements in educational opportunities and access to information, MHL remains low worldwide [52–54]. In China, a considerable proportion of pregnant and postpartum women may face increased health risks due to low levels of health literacy [55]. Studies from rural regions in western and southern China indicate that pregnant women exhibit only moderate levels of MHL, particularly those from low-income households [48, 56]. Research has shown that higher MHL enhances self-efficacy and support-seeking behavior, thereby reducing depressive symptoms [57]. Improving health literacy can empower pregnant women to engage in effective self-care and emotional regulation. Higher MHL enables pregnant women to access health information, recognize emotional distress, and seek help from healthcare providers. Maternal health professionals should guide women in developing appropriate attitudes toward health information, while also considering their socio-cultural and economic contexts. A comprehensive support system should be established to enhance access to reliable health information and ensure effective health education, ultimately improving maternal health outcomes.

The average social support score was 40.49 ± 8.41, similar to findings in other rural studies but lower than urban averages [58, 59]. Higher social support was linked to lower AND, affirming its role as a protective factor. Support from family, friends, and the community—whether emotional or material—helps reduce psychological stress [60–63]. Women who actively express their concerns, seek support from family, friends, and organizations, and participate in group activities can effectively alleviate AND [64]. Perceptions of support are particularly important, as they influence help-seeking behaviour, which in turn affects mental health outcomes. Barriers such as stigma and limited social networks may prevent rural women from seeking help, which leads to insufficient social support, including emotional, economic, informational, and evaluative support [65]. Intervention strategies should focus on enhancing both access to and utilization of social support [66].

The mediating role of maternal health literacy and social support

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first national study to confirm that MHL and SS independently and jointly mediate the relationship between PsyCap and AND. According to Critical Resource Theory, psychological well-being depends on the interaction of internal and external resources [32]. Our findings extend this theory by suggesting that PsyCap is associated with lower levels of depression and may be indirectly related to well-being through MHL and SS, illustrating a resource chain in which internal strengths activate and reinforce external resources. The Conservation of Resources (COR) Theory further complements this explanation, emphasizing that individuals strive to acquire and safeguard valuable resources, while resource depletion leads to stress. In this framework, PsyCap functions as a key psychological resource that not only buffers against potential losses but also fosters the accumulation of additional resources, such as MHL and SS, which jointly mitigate depressive symptoms. In line with Social Support Theory, higher PsyCap enables women to better perceive and mobilize social support, which in turn contributes to lower depression levels, particularly in rural areas with limited healthcare resources. Consistent with Stress and Coping Theory, PsyCap also enhances maternal health literacy, equipping women with the knowledge and skills to effectively use social support when facing stress. Together, these results clarify how internal psychological strengths interact with behavioral and external resources to reduce antenatal depression.

Maternal health literacy plays a partial mediating role in the relationship between PsyCap and AND in rural pregnant women. High PsyCap promotes proactive health behaviors, such as information seeking, trust in medical professionals, and engagement in antenatal education, which improve MHL and thereby reduce the likelihood of depression [67, 68]. This effect is particularly salient in rural China, where psychological empowerment is often necessary to overcome institutional and informational barriers [69]. Additionally, SS was also identified as a mediator. Individuals with greater PsyCap tend to maintain closer relationships, request assistance more effectively, and perceive greater emotional and instrumental support [70, 71]. Pregnant women with higher PsyCap are more likely to seek help, maintain stronger interpersonal relationships, and perceive greater emotional and instrumental support. In the Chinese rural setting, strong PsyCap may drive community engagement and family communication, indirectly enhancing emotional stability.

Importantly, the results also confirmed a chain mediation pathway: PsyCap → MHL → SS → AND. This mechanism indicates that PsyCap may be related to greater access to knowledge and interpersonal interaction, which could contribute to lower depression risk [45]. Higher PsyCap was associated with greater knowledge-seeking and health literacy, which were in turn linked to better engagement with social networks and support systems.

Regional differences and AND

Since the economic level has been emphasized in maternal mental health and a significant difference has been found between per capita monthly household income and AND, the relationship between them was not clear. The seven regions of China have different economic development levels. The National Bureau of Statistics of China reported in 2023 that eastern China has the highest gross regional domestic product (GRDP) [72]. However, the lowest level and detection rate of AND were found in rural areas of northeast China (M = 8.48 ± 4.36; 35.56%). These disparities reflect not only imbalances in the supply of medical and health services (MHSS), but also broader sociocultural and structural determinants [73]. For instance, the high prevalence of AND in southwest China may be influenced by limited healthcare access, but also by socioeconomic stress, low mental health literacy, cultural stigma surrounding psychiatric conditions, and differences in local health policies or screening implementation [58]. These multidimensional factors highlight the need for region-specific strategies in maternal mental health promotion [74]. Mental health interventions for pregnant women should be tailored according to each region’s unique economic, social, and cultural context, rather than relying on a uniform national approach.

Implications for intervention

Pregnancy involves physiological and social changes that increase vulnerability to depression. In rural areas, limited infrastructure and healthcare access worsen this risk. This study highlights the importance of early intervention, suggesting that boosting PsyCap, MHL, and SS could serve as an effective prevention framework. The results show that PsyCap is crucial in enhancing mental well-being during pregnancy. Emerging evidence indicates that PsyCap can be enhanced through mindfulness-based practice, group counseling grounded in positive psychology, and the STress-And-coping suppoRT (START) intervention in various populations [33, 75, 76]. These approaches foster resilience, optimism, and self-efficacy, which are crucial for coping with pregnancy-related stressors. The PsyCap-enhancing programs targeting rural populations could adapt these strategies into culturally appropriate, low-cost formats, such as short mindfulness sessions during prenatal visits or group-based workshops delivered by trained nurses.

Since PsyCap influences depression through health literacy and social support, future intervention designs should not only enhance PsyCap directly, but also incorporate components that promote MHL and SS. Therefore, the PsyCap-enhancing strategies may include mobile health (mHealth) platforms and community-based peer support groups. Research has shown that mHealth platforms can increase Maternal and Child Health (MCH) services utilization in rural health facilities and enhance maternal health literacy [77, 78]. This can improve women’s understanding of prenatal care and empower them to take a more active role in their health management. Additionally, community-based peer support groups can provide critical social support, fostering networks of women who share experiences and offer encouragement. Peer support has been linked to reduced feelings of isolation and stigma, key barriers to mental health help-seeking in rural populations [74, 79, 80].

Additionally, capacity-building efforts should focus on training local healthcare workers in basic psychotherapy, PsyCap-enhancing counseling skills, and culturally sensitive communication. Local health providers could better identify and support women at risk of antenatal depression. In addition, partnerships with local governments and non-governmental organizations can help establish and sustain psychological counseling units within rural clinics. A system centered on improving “PsyCap + MHL + SS” should include (1) structured maternal mental health education sessions, (2) digital or offline resources tailored to local language and literacy levels, and (3) family-inclusive interventions to enhance support from spouses and elders. By localizing interventions and adapting delivery modes, this framework offers a scalable solution for alleviating AND in underserved populations.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Second, although participants were recruited from seven rural regions, sample sizes were uneven. Northeast and Northwest China had smaller samples, limiting regional comparisons. Moreover, each region includes multiple provinces with potentially substantial intra-regional variation; thus, the sampling strategy—selecting one or more hospitals per region—may not sufficiently capture provincial-level differences. As such, the regional representativeness of the sample should be interpreted with caution, and the current findings may not fully generalize to all provinces within each region. Third, the association between PsyCap and AND may be much more complex and complicated than the models developed in this study. According to the society ecosystems theory, the three factors that were examined affected at the micro and meso levels. Future research could focus on the macroscopic level.

In addition to the above, future research should expand the current model in several key areas. While this study confirmed the mediating roles of MHL and SS, these factors only partially explained the link between PsyCap and AND, suggesting that other mediators, such as perceived stress, coping styles, or family functioning [62, 74]. Moreover, as this study relied solely on quantitative data, it could not capture the nuanced ways in which women perceive and utilize social support or health information in their daily lives. Qualitative approaches would be valuable for uncovering perspectives that remain hidden in survey data. Third, future studies are encouraged to examine regional differences in the availability, perception, and impact of social support, especially among socioeconomically disadvantaged or geographically remote populations. Finally, future intervention studies should consider designing and evaluating culturally sensitive, context-specific programs that focus on enhancing PsyCap, strengthening social support, and improving MHL, thereby promoting maternal mental health outcomes more effectively.

Despite these limitations, this is the first nationwide study focusing on rural pregnant women through a psychological capital framework. It demonstrates that PsyCap directly and indirectly affects antenatal depression through both MHL and SS, offering insights into how internal and external factors interact in shaping maternal mental health in underserved settings.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant associations between PsyCap and AND among 1,623 rural Chinese pregnant women. The findings reveal that low levels of MHL and SS contribute to the high prevalence of AND in rural areas. PsyCap showed a direct negative association with AND, and indirect associations through MHL and SS, as well as their chain mediating effect. The results suggest that interventions for perinatal depression in rural areas should incorporate community-based and digital approaches to enhance maternal health literacy and social support. However, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits causal inferences, and future research should address regional disparities and explore more complex interactions at the macroscopic level.
