Evaluation of the Association between Smoking and Tonsillolith Characteristics Using Cone-Beam Computed Tomography
Authors: Ali Rasat, Sevcihan Günen Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTonsilloliths are calcified concretions within tonsillar crypts whose relationship with smoking remains poorly understood. This study investigated the association between smoking and tonsillolith characteristics using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT).Subject and MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study evaluated 416 dental CBCT scans obtained between 2021 and 2023. Scans were evaluated for the presence, size, number, laterality, and anatomical localization of tonsilloliths by a single oral and maxillofacial radiologist, with a second radiologist consulted in cases of diagnostic uncertainty. Demographic characteristics and smoking status were obtained from medical records. Statistical analyses included the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, independent-samples t test or Mann-Whitney U test, and one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction (α = 0.05).ResultsTonsilloliths were detected in 29.6% of participants (123/416). Smoking was not significantly associated with tonsillolith prevalence, size, or number (p > 0.05). Among patients with tonsilloliths, smokers showed significantly higher frequencies of unilateral (p = 0.041) and lingual (p = 0.036) tonsilloliths than non-smokers. Age was significantly associated with both prevalence and size (p < 0.001 and p = 0.037, respectively), with higher prevalence in older groups. No significant sex differences were found.ConclusionSmoking was not associated with the overall prevalence, size, or number of tonsilloliths, but unilateral and lingual tonsilloliths were more frequent among smokers. Age was the strongest factor associated with tonsillolith formation, exerting a greater influence than smoking. Further prospective, multicenter studies with detailed smoking exposure data are needed to validate these findings.

Introduction

Soft tissue calcifications in the head and neck region may primarily result from physiological or pathological mineralization caused by irregular calcium phosphate deposits, known as heterotopic calcification [1]. The tonsils are lymphoid tissues located on the lateral walls of the oropharynx on the right and left sides. Tonsilloliths are calcified concretions that form within the tonsillar crypts, often associated with chronic inflammation, accumulation of debris, bacteria, and epithelial cells [2]. Although rare in children, they are frequently observed in adults. Small ones are asymptomatic; large ones can cause symptoms such as pain, difficulty swallowing, foreign body sensation, and halitosis [3, 4]. Tonsilloliths may be incidentally detected during examination of a panoramic radiograph taken for any purpose, where they usually appear as radiopaque areas superimposed on the mid-ramus region [5]. Information about the shape, number, size, and location of tonsilloliths can be determined using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). Tonsilloliths are more common in adults and in individuals with recurrent chronic tonsillitis as repeated inflammation facilitates the accumulation of debris in the tonsillar crypts. Their etiology is multifactorial and may be associated with upper respiratory tract inflammation, dry mouth, changes in the microbial flora, insufficient saliva secretion, and poor oral hygiene [6–8]. Smoking causes chronic hypoxia in vascular structures by reducing oxygen utilization and increasing carbon monoxide levels, while also altering oral flora and decreasing saliva flow [9, 10].

Although tonsilloliths are frequently detected as incidental findings in adult CBCT examinations, the influence of smoking on their prevalence and radiological characteristics has not been clearly defined, and the available evidence regarding this association remains limited and largely indirect. To our knowledge, no previous study has evaluated the effect of smoking on both the prevalence and localization patterns of tonsilloliths using CBCT. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between smoking and tonsillolith prevalence, size, number, laterality, and anatomical localization.

Subject and Methods

Sample Size Calculation

An a priori sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.4; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) based on the primary objective of comparing the prevalence of tonsilloliths between smokers and non-smokers. Assuming a medium effect size (w = 0.50), a two-tailed α level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.95, the minimum required sample size was 210 participants.

Study Design

This retrospective study examined CBCT images taken for different dental indications, such as implant planning, extraction of impacted teeth, and orthognathic surgery, at the Akdeniz University Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, between 2021 and 2023. A total of 439 CBCT images were evaluated for eligibility. Only CBCT images that captured the relevant anatomical regions and exhibited optimal diagnostic quality were included; scans encompassing the oropharynx and nasopharynx were reviewed for calcifications within the tonsillar region. Poor-quality images or scans with an unsuitable field of view for detecting tonsilloliths were excluded. Of the images evaluated, 23 did not meet the inclusion criteria and were excluded. After data verification, the final analytic sample comprised 416 patients, with ages ranging from 18 to 82 years (mean 44.3 ± 12.5 years). For descriptive and comparative purposes, the study population was divided into four age groups (18–35, 36–50, 51–65, and ≥66 years) to ensure adequate sample size within each category and to facilitate comparison with previous studies that use similar age bands.

All CBCT images were obtained using the Veraview X800 (J. Morita Mfg. Corp., Kyoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s standard parameters (FOV: 15 × 14 cm; 4.8 mA; 99 kVp; 35.8 s exposure). Scans were evaluated in 1-mm-thick coronal, axial, and sagittal slices using i-Dixel 2.0 three-dimensional imaging software (J. Morita, Kyoto, Japan) on a 22-inch full HD (1,920 × 1,080) monitor in a dimly lit room, following standard positioning protocols. Only images with a 15 × 14 cm region of interest were used to ensure standardization. For each identified tonsillolith, age group (18–35, 36–50, 51–65, ≥66 years), sex, laterality, size (mm), number (single or multiple), smoking status, daily cigarette consumption (0–10, 11–20, ≥21), and anatomical localization (palatine/lingual/both) were recorded (Fig. 1). Smoking status and daily cigarette consumption data were obtained from patients' medical records. The maximum diameter of each tonsillolith was measured in the plane in which it appeared largest, using axial, coronal, and sagittal reconstructions.

Observer and Reliability Assessment

All CBCT scans were primarily evaluated by a single oral and maxillofacial radiologist (A.R.) with 5 years of experience in CBCT interpretation, who was blinded to the patients’ smoking status throughout image assessment. A second experienced oral and maxillofacial radiologist (S.G.Y., 15 years of experience) was consulted only in cases of diagnostic uncertainty to reach a consensus diagnosis. Therefore, the image assessment was not performed independently by two observers, and interobserver reliability was not calculated.

To assess intraobserver reliability, 40 randomly selected CBCT scans (9.6% of the total sample) were re-evaluated by the same observer after a 2-week washout period, with a maximum of 20 patients assessed per day under standardized viewing conditions. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated for categorical variables (presence, laterality, and localization), and the intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated for the continuous variable (size). Intraobserver agreement was excellent (κ = 0.96, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.91–1.00 for presence; intraclass correlation coefficient = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.97 for size).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). A significance level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed) was applied for all tests. Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance using Levene’s test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and where appropriate, 95% CIs were calculated.

For comparative analyses between groups, different statistical tests were applied depending on the type and distribution of variables. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when expected cell frequencies were less than five. Continuous variables comparing two groups were analyzed using the independent samples t test for normally distributed data or the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was used for normally distributed variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables. When overall tests involving multiple groups (such as age categories or smoking intensity) were statistically significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni correction to control for type I error inflation.

Results

A total of 416 cases were included in the study. Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 82 years, with a mean age of 44.3 ± 12.5 years. The distribution across age categories was 18–35 years (n = 108, 26.0%), 36–50 years (n = 154, 37.0%), 51–65 years (n = 119, 28.7%), and ≥66 years (n = 35, 8.4%). A total of 47.3% (n = 197) of the cases were male, and 52.7% (n = 219) were female participants. Tonsilloliths were detected in 123 cases (29.6%), while 293 cases (70.4%) had no tonsilloliths. Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The prevalence of tonsilloliths was 31.3% (n = 62/198) in the group of smokers and 28.0% (n = 61/218) in the group of non-smokers, as shown in Figure 2. This difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.505). The correlation between smoking intensity (daily cigarette consumption groups: 0–10, 11–20, ≥21) and the occurrence of tonsilloliths was not statistically significant (p = 0.713). Therefore, smoking or daily amount did not affect the overall prevalence of tonsilloliths.

In 123 cases with tonsilloliths, the maximum diameter of the stones was 2.14 ± 1.19 mm on average. There was no significant difference in size between smokers and non-smokers (2.23 ± 1.08) (p = 0.644). Similarly, no significant difference was found when comparing groups based on the number of cigarettes smoked per day (p = 0.323). In terms of the number of tonsilloliths, 54.5% of cases contained a single tonsillolith, while 45.5% contained multiple tonsilloliths. No significant relationship was found between smoking status (p = 0.323) and number of cigarettes smoked (p = 0.701) in terms of number distribution.

Regarding anatomical localization, 15.1% of the participants (n = 63) had lingual tonsilloliths, 8.9% (n = 37) had palatine tonsilloliths, and 5.5% (n = 23) had both lingual and palatine tonsilloliths. Among individuals with tonsilloliths, the distribution of tonsillolith localization (palatine/lingual/palatine-lingual) differed significantly between smokers and non-smokers (p = 0.036). Specifically, lingual tonsilloliths were more common in smokers (n = 36, 58.1% of smokers with tonsilloliths) compared to non-smokers (n = 27, 42.6% of non-smokers with tonsilloliths). Conversely, palatine tonsilloliths were more frequent in non-smokers (n = 25, 39.3%) than in smokers (n = 12, 19.4%). Furthermore, laterality analysis showed a statistically significant difference between smokers and non-smokers (p = 0.041). Unilateral tonsilloliths were significantly more common in the smoking group (n = 47, 75.8%) compared to the non-smoking group, as shown in Table 2 (n = 35, 55.7%).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the relationship between the presence and characteristics of tonsilloliths (size, number, laterality, and anatomical localization) and demographic and behavioral factors, including age, sex, and smoking (smoking status and daily cigarette consumption). The findings indicated that both the presence and size of tonsilloliths were significantly associated with age (p < 0.001 and p = 0.037, respectively), with the highest prevalence (56.8%) and the largest mean size (2.74 mm) observed in the ≥66-year age group. Smoking was not associated with the overall prevalence, size, or number of tonsilloliths. However, exploratory subgroup analyses showed that smokers exhibited significantly higher frequencies of unilateral tonsilloliths (p = 0.041) and lingual tonsilloliths (p = 0.036) than non-smokers. No significant association was observed between sex and the presence or size of tonsilloliths. These findings suggest that smoking may influence the anatomical distribution of tonsilloliths rather than their overall occurrence.

Although smoking does not appear to affect the overall prevalence of tonsilloliths, our findings indicate that unilateral and lingual tonsilloliths are more common in smokers, and the localization distribution is significantly different, with lingual tonsilloliths being more frequent in smokers and palatine tonsilloliths in non-smokers. Cigarette smoke causes chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and an impaired local immune response in the oropharyngeal mucosa [12]. Histopathological studies have shown that the tonsillar crypt epithelium of smokers exhibits basement membrane disruption, cellular degeneration, and superficial erosions. These findings indicate a loss of mucosal integrity, which is thought to contribute to tonsillolith formation. Such epithelial alterations may facilitate the retention of cellular debris and bacterial colonization within the crypts, thereby promoting conditions favorable for subsequent calcification. One possible explanation for the higher frequency of lingual tonsilloliths among smokers is greater exposure of the lingual tonsillar tissue to cigarette smoke and heat because of its anatomical proximity to the tongue base, potentially resulting in more pronounced local irritation and chronic inflammation. Similarly, the increased frequency of unilateral tonsilloliths in smokers may be interpreted in light of habitual smoking behavior, such as the preferred position of the cigarette within the lips and the resulting direction of smoke flow, which could lead to asymmetric exposure of the tonsillar tissues to tobacco smoke. Alternatively, pre-existing anatomical asymmetries in crypt architecture between the two tonsils may render one side inherently more susceptible to debris retention and calcification, with smoking acting as an aggravating rather than a primary causative factor. Because smoking-side preference, hand dominance, and other behavioral variables were not assessed in the present retrospective study, these explanations remain hypothetical and should be investigated in future well-designed prospective studies.

Our study revealed that the presence and size of tonsilloliths showed a statistically significant relationship with age. The findings of the present study are consistent with the existing literature. Takahashi et al. [3], in their evaluation of computed tomography (CT) images from 2,873 consecutive patients, reported that the prevalence of palatine tonsilloliths increased significantly after the age of 40, with the highest prevalence observed in the 50–69-year age group. Similarly, Oda et al. [13], in their analysis of 482 paired CT and panoramic radiographs, demonstrated a significant correlation between age and the detection of tonsilloliths and reported that the detection rate was significantly higher in individuals older than 40 years than in those younger than 40 years. Likewise, Kalabalık et al. [14], who evaluated 399 CBCT scans from a Turkish population in the Aegean region, found a positive correlation between age and the number of tonsilloliths and showed that tonsillar calcifications were significantly more prevalent in individuals aged 41 years and older, whereas they were significantly less common in those aged 20 years or younger. More recently, Yasan et al. [7] reported that the mean age of patients with tonsilloliths (42.39 ± 16.03 years) was significantly higher than that of patients without tonsilloliths (37.50 ± 15.56 years). Taken together, these consistent findings strongly suggest that tonsillolith formation is a progressive, age-dependent process.

Our analysis showed no statistically significant difference between the sexes in the presence (p = 0.515) or size (p = 0.443) of tonsilloliths. Although some studies report no significant difference between the sexes [13, 15, 16], other studies indicate that the prevalence significantly differs between the sexes [11, 17]. Tonsilloliths were detected in 31.4% of the males and 27.8% of the females included in our study. These conflicting results support the generally accepted view that the etiology of tonsilloliths is determined primarily by local factors (crypt morphology, debris accumulation, inflammation) rather than biological sex alone.

A further point warranting discussion is the radiographic differential diagnosis of tonsilloliths. Not all radiopaque foci detected in the tonsillar region represent tonsilloliths; phleboliths and calcified carotid plaques may exhibit a similar radiographic appearance on CBCT and should be differentiated based on their characteristic morphology and anatomical location [18]. Rarely, unusually large calcified masses in this region may represent giant tonsilloliths presenting as symptomatic oropharyngeal masses and may be difficult to distinguish clinically from a neoplastic process until histopathological confirmation is obtained [19]. Awareness of these possibilities is particularly important in smokers, who have an increased risk of vascular calcification; therefore, incidental calcifications detected on CBCT in this population should be interpreted with careful radiographic assessment.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it used dental CBCT rather than medical CT. Although CBCT provides high spatial resolution for imaging mineralized structures, its limited soft-tissue contrast resolution may reduce the sensitivity for detecting small or minimally calcified tonsilloliths. Consistent with this, published estimates of tonsillolith prevalence appear to vary not only according to the study population but also according to the imaging modality used. CBCT-based studies have generally reported prevalence rates ranging from 18.6% to 33.2% [2, 14, 16, 20], whereas studies using medical CT have reported prevalence rates between 24.6% and 46.1% [3, 7, 13, 17, 21] (Table 3). Therefore, the overall prevalence observed in our study (29.6%) may, at least in part, reflect the lower detection sensitivity of CBCT compared with medical CT rather than true differences in the underlying population. In addition, because CBCT provides limited soft-tissue contrast [22], precise differentiation between palatine and lingual tonsillar tissues may be less reliable than with medical CT. Furthermore, due to the retrospective design of the present study, it was not possible to evaluate more granular aspects of smoking behavior, such as the side of lip contact, the direction of smoke flow, or the depth of inhalation. Another limitation of the present study is that the laterality of unilateral tonsilloliths (right versus left side) was not recorded. Consequently, no conclusions could be drawn regarding a possible relationship between the preferred position of the cigarette within the lips, the direction of smoke flow, and the side of tonsillolith occurrence. Future prospective studies should systematically document, particularly in patients with unilateral tonsilloliths, the side of lesion involvement (right or left) together with smoking-related behavioral variables, such as the preferred position of the cigarette within the lips and the direction of smoke flow, using patient questionnaires and clinical examinations. This approach may help clarify the potential role of smoking behavior in the lateralized distribution of tonsilloliths. The frequently reported asymmetric distribution of tonsilloliths in the literature [17] suggests that localized factors, such as the direction of smoke flow, may influence debris retention within the tonsillar crypts. However, this hypothesis requires validation through well-designed prospective studies.

Conclusion

This study found no significant association between smoking status and the overall prevalence, size, or number of tonsilloliths. However, smoking was significantly associated with unilateral involvement and a higher frequency of lingual tonsilloliths, suggesting that local effects of smoking may influence the anatomical distribution of tonsilloliths rather than their overall occurrence. Given the retrospective design and the methodological limitations of the present study, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future prospective studies incorporating detailed smoking-exposure parameters, potential confounding variables, and multimodal imaging or biological inflammatory markers are warranted to clarify the role of tobacco exposure in the pathogenesis and localization of tonsilloliths.
