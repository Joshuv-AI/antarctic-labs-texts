Controlling Mastitis in Cows: Development of an Evaluation Methodology for Determining the Coverage Efficacy of Nozzle Configurations of an Automatic Teat Spraying System
Authors: Can Tezel, Gokhan Bayar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAutomatic post‐milking teat spraying systems are widely used in dairy farming to improve udder hygiene and reduce the risk of mastitis. However, these systems often fail to achieve homogeneous coverage of the outer teat surface due to limited adjustability in nozzle positions and the number of nozzles.ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop a novel methodology to evaluate the homogeneity of spray coverage on the outer teat surface and to determine the coverage efficiency of different nozzle configurations used in automatic teat spraying systems.Study DesignExperimental and simulation‐based study using a laboratory test setup and a 3D modelling framework.MethodsThe proposed methodology integrates a geometry‐based modelling structure, an image‐processing‐based evaluation system and a grid‐algorithm‐based assessment model. A simulation environment and an experimental setup were developed to evaluate the approach. Experiments were conducted using four life‐sized rubber cow teats whose positions and orientations could be independently adjusted to simulate different teat and nozzle conditions. The developed model was also integrated into a 3D cow prototype. Spray coverage was evaluated for nozzle configurations commonly used in practice (one, two and four nozzles).ResultsThe results indicated that spray coverage increased with the number of nozzles. According to the image‐processing‐based algorithm, the coverage rates for one‐, two‐ and four‐nozzle configurations were 55.7%, 57.1% and 59.7%, respectively. Using the grid‐based algorithm, the corresponding coverage values were 68.5%, 71.8% and 84.6%. The findings demonstrate that increasing the number of nozzles significantly improves the coverage performance of automatic teat spraying systems.Main LimitationsThe study was conducted using rubber teat models under controlled experimental conditions, which may not fully represent the variability encountered in real dairy farm environments.ConclusionsThe proposed methodology provides a systematic and quantitative framework for evaluating spray coverage and optimizing nozzle configurations in automatic teat spraying systems. The approach can support the design and improvement of more effective teat disinfection systems in dairy farming.

Introduction

Bovine mastitis is one of the most prevalent and economically significant diseases affecting dairy cattle worldwide. The disease is characterized by inflammation of the mammary gland and udder tissue and is primarily caused by bacterial infections, although fungi, viruses and physical injuries may also contribute to its occurrence. Mastitis leads to substantial economic losses in dairy production due to decreased milk yield, deterioration of milk quality, increased veterinary treatment costs and premature culling of affected animals. The disease occurs in both clinical and subclinical forms, with subclinical mastitis being particularly problematic because it often progresses without visible symptoms and is therefore difficult to detect. Gonçalves et al. (2018) investigated the effect of different pathogens on subclinical mastitis by comparing healthy and infected mammary quarters in 146 lactating cows and reported significant reductions in milk yield associated with infected quarters. Similarly, Down et al. (2013) developed a stochastic Monte Carlo model to analyse treatment strategies and identified transmission risk, bacteriological cure rate, culling costs and milk yield loss as major contributors to the economic burden of clinical mastitis.

Maintaining proper udder and teat hygiene is therefore essential for preventing mastitis and ensuring optimal milk production. The bovine udder consists of four mammary glands, commonly referred to as quarters, which are highly susceptible to infection during the milking process. The teat canal serves as the primary entry point for pathogens into the mammary gland. Healthy teats are characterized by smooth skin, intact teat ends and a properly closing teat canal after milking. Several studies have emphasized that udder cleanliness and teat condition play an important role in mastitis prevention. Manzi et al. (2012) reported that cows with rough teat ends and poor udder cleanliness were more susceptible to intramammary infections. In addition, Dohmen et al. (2010) highlighted that increased somatic cell counts in automatic milking systems may indicate hygiene deficiencies within the milking process. Penry (2018) compared mastitis control factors between automatic and conventional milking methods, highlighting the unique challenges and solutions for automatic milking systems (AMS). Breen (2019) emphasized that disinfection plays a crucial role in maintaining udder and teat health. Gleeson et al. (2018) noted that pre‐milking disinfection had no significant benefit in preventing mastitis. Teat disinfectants are applied through dipping, manual spraying, or automatic spraying. Canny (2022) evaluated an automatic teat sprayer system's ability to eliminate bacteria on Holstein cow teats, underscoring its potential to improve hygiene practices. Benavides et al. (2020) showed that contemporary automated spraying systems, including the DeLaval VMS V300 and TSR, achieve teat coverage levels similar to those obtained with dipping methods. To ensure effective disinfection, it is essential that the entire surface of the teat is fully covered.

Post‐milking teat disinfection, commonly referred to as post‐dipping, is widely recognized as one of the most effective practices for controlling mastitis in dairy herds. This procedure is applied immediately after milking in order to eliminate pathogens that may have been transferred to the teat surface during milking and to reduce bacterial penetration while the teat canal remains temporarily open. Previous studies have demonstrated that post‐milking teat disinfection significantly reduces the occurrence of intramammary infections and somatic cell counts. Williamson and Lacy‐Hulbert (2013) reported that consistent application of post‐milking disinfectants contributes substantially to mastitis control programs. Common disinfectants used in post‐dipping formulations include iodine, chlorhexidine and chlorine dioxide, each providing antimicrobial activity against different pathogens. More recently, alternative disinfectant formulations have also been investigated. Aiemsaard et al. (2023) evaluated an essential oil‐based spray formulation and reported that citronella exhibited strong antibacterial activity against mastitis‐causing pathogens. Martins et al. (2016) demonstrated that a high free iodine barrier teat disinfectant effectively prevented new intramammary infections and clinical mastitis cases. Verdier‐Metz et al. (2022) further showed that water‐in‐oil emulsion post‐milking products can limit the growth of harmful bacteria such as Pseudomonas and Staphylococcus.

Teat disinfectants can be applied using several methods, including teat dipping, manual spraying and automatic spraying systems. Teat dipping generally provides reliable coverage of the teat surface; however, it is labour‐intensive and less suitable for modern dairy farms utilizing automatic milking systems. Manual spraying methods may reduce labour requirements but often result in inconsistent disinfectant application due to operator variability. Consequently, automatic teat spraying systems have been developed to improve application consistency and reduce labour requirements. Hiley et al. (2018) compared dipping and spraying methods and reported that dipping requires more manual labour, whereas automated spraying provides more consistent application with reduced labour input. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of spray‐based systems depends strongly on spray duration, nozzle configuration and the resulting coverage of the teat surface. Pocknee et al. (2018) reported that increasing spray duration improved teat coverage in automatic systems, whereas Ohnstad et al. (2018) investigated teat coverage characteristics using dip cups. Inadequate coverage remains a critical concern, as untreated areas on the teat surface may allow pathogens to survive and increase the risk of intramammary infections. Edmonson (2011) reported that insufficient coverage from a single‐nozzle automatic sprayer resulted in increased herd somatic cell counts due to infections caused by pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus.

Automatic teat spraying systems currently available in the market typically employ one, two or four stationary nozzles. However, these systems must accommodate natural variations in teat position and orientation among cows. Differences in teat angles and spatial positions may result in incomplete coverage of certain areas of the teat surface, particularly the outer sides of the teats, potentially increasing the risk of infection. Despite the increasing use of automatic teat spraying systems in modern dairy farms, the influence of teat orientation, spatial position and nozzle configuration on the coverage of the teat surface has not been systematically investigated.

This staged research approach, involving simulation and controlled experimental validation prior to live animal testing, is consistent with ethical research practices aimed at reducing unnecessary animal experimentation during the early phases of technological development.

Therefore, the main objective of this study is to evaluate teat surface coverage in automatic spraying systems under varying teat positions and orientations. Specifically, this study aims to (1) develop a geometrical model to analyse teat coverage under different spatial configurations, (2) determine the optimal number of spray nozzles required to maximize coverage of the teat surface and (3) validate the proposed model through both simulation and experimental analysis. To achieve these objectives, a geometrical modelling framework was developed and integrated into a simulation environment. An experimental setup was constructed to evaluate the coverage performance of an automatic teat spraying system using four life‐sized rubber Holstein cow teats with adjustable positions and orientations. The model was also tested on a three‐dimensional life‐sized cow prototype. In addition, an image processing technique was developed to quantify teat fluid coverage area, and a novel grid‐based algorithm was implemented to evaluate the homogeneity of the coverage distribution. The details of the proposed models, simulation framework, experimental setup and obtained results are presented in the following sections of this article.

Design and Construction of the Experimental Setup and the Experimental Studies

An experimental setup teat spraying system is designed (Figure 18—left) and manufactured (Figure 18—right). The dimensions of the system designed are arranged as 1020 × 680 × 700 mm3 (length × width × height). The setup includes four teats, each with a diameter of 27 mm and a length of 70 mm. A nozzle bed with multiple holes is also designed and constructed to test the different number of nozzles. The nozzle locations are same as with the locations shown in Figure 8.

Experimental setup. (Left) 3D CAD design of the experimental teat sprayer system: isometric (a), front (b) and isometric top (c) views of the designed system. (Right) Sprayer system manufactured: isometric (a), front (b) and isometric top (c) views of the designed system.

The upper grid plate, 3D printed from PLA, is supported via 40 × 80 sigma profiles. This grid has square holes which allow to change the positions of the teats and their subassemblies. Each teat subassembly consists of three 3D printed PLA parts (Figure 19—left). The lower plate, which is used to hold the teat, is connected to the upper plate via 2 mm ropes. One rope is connected to the lower teat plate aligned with the y‐axis, and the other rope is aligned with the x‐axis. This arrangement allows the teat to be angled by pulling the ropes from one side. The angle of the teat is measured using a digital tilt sensor (Figure 19—right). The top square part sits on the grid plate for locking the teat plate and its design allows for easy removal and repositioning within the grid.

(Left) Teat and its subassemblies. (Right) Digital tilt sensor used for measuring the teat directional (orientation) angle.

The nozzles used in the experimental studies have full cone shapes with the spraying angles of 60. Each nozzle can be able to provide 1 L/min spraying fluid at 3 bar spray pumping pressure.

The flowchart of the sprayer system structure constructed is shown in Figure 20. The fluid is drawn from a tank and pumped by a process pump into a diaphragm accumulator at 3 bar pressure. During the pumping process, a solenoid valve remains closed. Once the diaphragm accumulator is filled and the system is prepared for spraying, the solenoid valve is opened and closed rapidly. The duration for the valve's openings and closings is managed by a Programming Logic controller (PLC). PLC is also responsible for controlling the pump.

Flowchart of the sprayer system structure constructed. Blue and black‐coloured‐lines show the fluid flow and control signal, respectively. The grey‐coloured‐line indicates the air flow. Right side of the image illustrates the different nozzle configurations for different number of nozzles.

The fluid input is distributed among the nozzle(s). In order to obtain the fluid coverage areas, the spraying fluid is created via a mixed fluid involving water with blue‐liquid‐food‐colouring. The mixed fluid is prepared at a ratio of 20 mL of food colouring per 2 L of water; therefore, 2.02 L (approximately 2 L is taken in the calculations) spraying fluid is used in the experimental studies. To collect and obtain the amount of the fluid sprayed, straw paper is used. The outer surface of each teat is wrapped via straw paper which is precisely cut into dimensions of 84 × 70 mm2. Once the model‐teats are prepared, the spraying system is automatically activated. One of the results of the teats sprayed is demonstrated in Figure 21.

Teats sprayed with water mixed with blue‐liquid‐food‐colouring.

It is arranged in the experimental studies that in all cases the system consistently delivers 10 mL of fluid with a valve opening time of 0.3 s (spray duration time). This value is experimentally confirmed for each nozzle configuration by using a measurement cup with ∓0.2 mL tolerance. In the studies, 30 experiments (10 for each configuration) are conducted using the calibrated measuring cups. In each spraying time of 0.3 s, 10, 5 and 2.5 mL fluid are sprayed in the case of one nozzle, two and four nozzles configurations, respectively. This shows that the spraying duration necessarily differs in each case. In order to reach 10 mL amount of spraying fluid in each experiment, one spraying, two sprayings and four sprayings are performed for one nozzle, two and four nozzles configurations, respectively.

Note that it is assumed that the fluid drawn from a tank and pumped using the process pump through a diaphragm accumulator works at a constant pressure of 3 bar. In reality, this value may not be constant due to the environmental conditions. In the experimental studies conducted, it is observed that even if the environmental conditions are not constant, the release of fluid in each spray time is between 9.8 and 10.2 mL, which may have a slight effect on the atomization quality of the spray.

The spraying quality depends largely on the nozzle selection (types, spraying angle, etc.) and the working parameters of the spray system (flow rate, pressure, solenoid valve opening time, etc.). The selection of the spraying system module and parameters was guided by several practical constraints. First, it is adhered to industry standards and regulations for dairy farm equipment, which specify requirements for components that contact milk or are used in the dairy environment. For instance, specialized stainless steel components, resistant to lactic acid degradation, are required for certain applications. Second, the selection is influenced by the availability of components from established manufacturers who comply with these standards. The specific nozzle type is chosen to provide droplets large enough to effectively wet the teat surface while minimizing overspray. Additionally, it is constrained by the equipment accessible through the industry partner and regional supply chain limitations. Within these constraints, the system parameters (pressure, angle, flow rate and solenoid timing) are optimized according to the manufacturer guidelines and preliminary testing to achieve the most effective coverage while maintaining practical implementation feasibility.

An image processing algorithm based on Python image recognition library is developed to obtain the fluid coverage area percentage on the straw paper (Figure 22b). It can be observed that although the fluid coverage when the system is used with four‐nozzle configuration is less than that with two nozzles, the result is much more homogeneous and therefore more desirable. In order to quantify this homogeneity, a grid‐based algorithm is developed. In this algorithm, the paper surface is divided into a 20 × 20 grid. Each cell created is independently evaluated based on its wetness percentage. There is no information in the literature about a threshold value of the wetness percentage of a teat surface. In the cow farms, many interviews were done by the experienced workers responsible for cow milking. The real‐time spraying processes were also observed in these farms. The information gained from the field was used in the spraying experiments so that a numerical value was obtained about what percentage of the teat surface should be wet for the teat surface to be considered wet. The farm and laboratory studies show that when the wetness percentage of a grid cell exceeds a threshold of 10%, the cell can be considered wet (covered with spraying fluid); otherwise, it can be considered dry (uncovered with spraying fluid). Figure 22c shows the results of the grid‐based algorithm applied to the system with four and two nozzles, respectively.

Top figures: four nozzle configuration. Bottom images: two nozzle configuration. (a) Sprayed straw paper. (b) Image processing algorithm developed to determine the fluid coverage percentage on the straw paper. (c) Application of the grid‐based algorithm to the sprayed straw paper.

It can be observed that the algorithm has accurately identified the dry grids (Figure 22c). The two‐nozzle configuration results in a lower wetness percentage in the grid algorithm compared to the four‐nozzle configuration. This is due to the large dry areas present in the two‐nozzle test.

In order to show the performance of the system developed and evaluate the system accuracy with respect to different positions and orientation angles of the teats, four different case studies are conducted. In each case, the experiments (Videos S1 and S2) are carried out for different teat locations and teat orientation angles using one, two and four nozzles.

In ‘Case Study‐1’, the teat locations and orientation angles shown in Table 2 are used. Figure 23 presents the setup of the system with the respective teat locations and angles. Figure 24 shows the wet straw papers covered on each teat for different number of nozzles; one, two and four nozzles. The image processing algorithm gives the results (Figure 24) for ‘Case Study‐1’ that the fluid coverages (wet areas) are 50.76%, 59.93% and 61.17% for one, two and four nozzles, respectively. Analysis and Discussion

In this study, a methodology combining image processing and grid‐based approaches was developed to evaluate the coverage efficacy of nozzle configurations in an automatic teat spraying system. This framework provides several advantages, including quantitative assessment of spray distribution and homogeneity, while also presenting certain limitations that should be addressed in future investigations. The literature review indicates a limited number of studies that directly evaluate teat spray coverage through experimental or simulation‐based approaches. This gap underscores the novelty and relevance of the current work, which establishes baseline data and a methodological approach for future comparative studies in this practical area of teat spraying.

The grid‐based method partitions the spray region into discrete grids and evaluates positions based on geometric constraints. The accuracy of this method depends on grid resolution: Finer grids yield more precise placement but require higher computational resources, whereas coarser grids reduce computational cost at the potential expense of optimal nozzle positioning. The percentage of wet surface is used to quantify droplet deposition, and uniformity is a key performance indicator that will be integrated into future analyses to optimize the number and placement of nozzles.

Rubber teats were employed in the experimental setup because they are widely used for feeding newborn calves and approximate the outer geometry, surface softness and flexibility of adult dairy cow teats. However, these models cannot perfectly replicate the physical and biological properties of real teats, particularly surface roughness and temperature, which can influence droplet adhesion. Future studies on live animals will address these factors to validate the experimental findings under realistic conditions. Additional limitations include closely positioned or touching teats, large teat orientation angles (greater than 45) or atypical udder shapes, which may hinder complete coverage and reduce the accuracy of the proposed model.

The maximum number of nozzles in this study was limited to four, reflecting common industry practices in commercial automatic teat spraying systems. The results confirm that while four nozzles outperform one‐ or two‐nozzle configurations, complete coverage is not always achieved, highlighting the potential benefit of additional nozzles or dynamic nozzle positioning. Implementing closed‐loop control mechanisms to adjust nozzle orientation based on teat geometry could further enhance coverage and reduce blind spots.

Simulation and experimental results demonstrated the efficacy of the proposed evaluation framework. Image‐processing‐based algorithms and grid‐based homogeneity assessments provided consistent quantitative measurements of spray coverage. These results establish a robust baseline for evaluating more complex configurations in future work. From a practical perspective, improving the effectiveness of automatic teat spraying systems may enhance post‐milking disinfection, contributing to mastitis prevention and overall herd health management in automated dairy operations.

Collectively, these findings provide a robust basis for evaluating and optimizing automatic teat spraying systems, highlighting both the potential and the limitations of current nozzle configurations. This sets the stage for concluding remarks and practical recommendations.

Although this study focused on simulation and controlled experimental validation, these approaches represent essential preliminary steps in the development of technological systems for livestock management. Simulation‐based optimization enables researchers to evaluate multiple system configurations while minimizing unnecessary animal use during early stage development. Once optimal configurations are determined, future studies should validate system performance under real farm conditions using live animals. Such validation will provide further insights into the practical effectiveness of the system and its potential contribution to precision livestock farming.

The insights gained from both simulation and experimental evaluations directly inform the conclusions regarding optimal nozzle configurations and future implementation strategies.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the coverage performance of different nozzle configurations in an automatic teat spraying system for post‐milking disinfection. Building on the simulation and experimental insights discussed above, a framework incorporating geometric modelling, image processing and a grid‐based homogeneity assessment was developed to quantify spray distribution and evaluate the effectiveness of one‐, two‐ and four‐nozzle configurations.

Results demonstrate that nozzle configuration strongly influences coverage efficacy. The four‐nozzle arrangement consistently provided the highest coverage and minimized blind spots, whereas one‐ and two‐nozzle configurations exhibited incomplete coverage, leaving dry regions on portions of the teat surface. These findings highlight the importance of nozzle placement, orientation and potential dynamic adjustment through closed‐loop control mechanisms to optimize spray uniformity.

From a practical standpoint, optimizing automatic teat spraying systems can enhance post‐milking disinfection and contribute to mastitis prevention, supporting overall dairy herd health management in automated farming operations. The proposed framework also establishes baseline data and a methodological foundation for evaluating and refining future teat spraying systems.

Future work will focus on validating the system under real farm conditions using live cows with varying teat geometries and orientations. The performance of the spraying system will be assessed during natural cow movements, and further optimization of nozzle configurations, fluid flow rates and coverage uniformity will be explored. This staged research approach is consistent with ethical research practices, reducing unnecessary animal experimentation during early phases of technological development and supporting the advancement of precision livestock farming.
