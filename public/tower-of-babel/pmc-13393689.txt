The effect of haptic virtual reality simulation training before clinical endodontic practice on student performance and perception
Authors: Emine Şimşek, Selimcan Satar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreclinical education plays a critical role in helping dental students transform theoretical knowledge into psychomotor skills and prepare for clinical practice. In endodontics, access cavity preparation is one of the most fundamental yet technically sensitive procedures, requiring manual precision, anatomical understanding, and self-confidence. Haptic virtual reality simulation (HVRS) has emerged as a promising educational tool that may enhance students’ technical skills and preparedness before treating real patients.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of HVRS training provided during the preclinical period on dental students’ performance in endodontic access cavity preparation on real patients, and to examine students’ perceptions of this training.Materials and methodsThirty fourth-year dental students who would be treating patients for the first time in the endodontics clinic were included in the study. The students were randomly assigned to either an experimental group or a control group. The experimental group (n = 15) received HVRS training before clinical practice, whereas the control group (n = 15) followed only the routine training process. All students performed endodontic access cavity preparation on single-rooted permanent teeth in real patients. Clinical performance was evaluated according to criteria related to access cavity preparation and was analyzed in terms of cavity score and cavity classification. In addition, procedure time was recorded in seconds. The opinions of the students in the experimental group regarding HVRS training were assessed using a 10-item Likert-type questionnaire. The data were analyzed statistically.ResultsThe mean cavity score of the experimental group was significantly higher than that of the control group (p < 0.05). In terms of procedure time, the experimental group also completed the procedure in a significantly shorter time than the control group (p < 0.05). Regarding cavity classification, no poor performance was observed in the experimental group, and the proportions of moderate and good performance were higher than those in the control group; however, the difference between the groups was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Questionnaire results showed that students generally found HVRS training useful and expressed a desire for HVRS to be used in other endodontic procedures and incorporated into the curriculum.ConclusionThe results of this study indicate that preclinical HVRS training positively affected students’ endodontic access cavity preparation performance on patients. Higher cavity scores, shorter procedure times, and positive student feedback suggest that HVRS is an effective complementary educational tool that supports clinical skill development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09577-x.

Background

Dentistry education is a multifaceted process that comprises theoretical instruction, preclinical skills training, and clinical practice [1]. Within this process, preclinical education is considered a critical stage in which students acquire the necessary knowledge, practical skills, psychomotor coordination, and self-confidence before performing procedures on real patients [2]. Therefore, the quality of preclinical education plays a decisive role in students’ clinical success.

In traditional preclinical dental education, phantom heads, artificial teeth, extracted natural teeth, and 3D-printed teeth are widely used [3]. Although these methods support the development of basic manual skills, they have several limitations, including difficulties in obtaining natural teeth, infection control and ethical concerns, the cost of artificial teeth, limited anatomical variability, inability to fully replicate the real clinical feel, and lack of standardization in assessment [4]. Therefore, interest in innovative methods that are more standardized, repeatable, and allow objective evaluation has increased in endodontic education. In this context, 3D-printed teeth have gained importance as an alternative or complementary approach in preclinical endodontic education [5]. These models offer advantages in terms of standardization, accessibility, and anatomical reproducibility, allowing students to practice on samples with similar levels of difficulty [6, 7]. However, differences in hardness and radiopacity between resin material and natural dentin, the inability to fully reproduce internal canal anatomy, and standardization problems in production protocols remain the main limitations of these models; therefore, further improvement of materials and production processes is required for their more widespread use in routine education [5].

In recent years, advances in digital technologies have increased the use of new educational tools, such as haptic virtual reality simulators (HVRS), in dental education. HVRS provides tactile feedback during virtual tooth preparation, allowing users to distinguish between different structures such as enamel, dentin, and pulp chamber perforation [8]. By combining three-dimensional visualization with tactile feedback, these systems offer students a realistic, interactive, and repeatable learning environment [9]. Thus, students can perceive differences in the hardness of dental tissues, repeat procedures in a safe environment, and receive immediate feedback, thereby improving their technical skills, spatial awareness, hand–eye coordination, and decision-making abilities [10]. In addition, HVRS may contribute to reducing environmental waste by decreasing the number of artificial teeth used.

In this study, SIMtoCARE Dente (SIMtoCARE B.V., Vreeland, the Netherlands) was used as the preclinical training tool. SIMtoCARE Dente is a dental simulator developed for dental education that combines virtual reality/mixed reality with high-fidelity haptic feedback. The system offers various training modules, including manual skill development, operative dentistry, prosthetic preparations, and endodontics, and also allows applications for endodontic access cavity preparation. When the literature is reviewed, studies using the SIMtoCARE Dente system have generally reported positive outcomes and favourable student feedback in preclinical dental education [11, 12]. Based on these studies, the SIMtoCARE Dente system was selected in the present study as a suitable haptic simulation tool for endodontic access cavity preparation training.

Preclinical dental training models, including natural teeth, artificial teeth, 3D-printed models, phantom head exercises, and virtual/haptic simulation systems, should be regarded not only as technical training tools but also as structured learning environments grounded in experiential learning, deliberate practice, and simulation-based education principles [13]. These educational models allow students to practice repeatedly in controlled settings, develop their clinical skills progressively, and gain experience safely before treating real patients [14]. In this context, endodontics stands out as one of the most technically and cognitively challenging areas of undergraduate dental education [15]. The complex anatomy of the root canal system, the need for accurate orientation, and the irreversible nature of treatment make this field even more difficult for students. One of the first and most critical stages of endodontic treatment, access cavity preparation, requires accurate and controlled entry into the pulp chamber [16]. Errors during this procedure, such as excessive removal of tooth structure, perforation, inadequate cavity design, or failure to locate canal orifices, may negatively affect treatment success. Therefore, access cavity preparation requires not only theoretical knowledge but also fine motor skills, three-dimensional anatomical perception, and controlled hand movements [17]. From the students’ perspective, performing an endodontic procedure on a real patient for the first time is often associated with anxiety, stress, and lack of confidence [18]. Especially during the transition to clinical practice in the fourth year, students must simultaneously demonstrate technical competence and ensure patient safety. This situation may affect both psychomotor performance and decision-making. For this reason, contemporary dental education places increasing importance not only on providing knowledge and technical skills but also on preparing students emotionally and cognitively for clinical practice. Determining to what extent preclinical training methods contribute to students’ first real-patient applications is therefore of great importance for educational planning. In this context, HVRS may offer a useful contribution to endodontic education.

Current findings in the literature on endodontic access cavity preparation training indicate that these systems show promising results in terms of both student performance and student acceptance [19, 20]. However, the majority of studies in the literature have been conducted in preclinical settings [21, 22]. This highlights the need to assess not only the contribution of haptic virtual reality simulation to laboratory performance, but also its translation into clinical performance, while considering factors such as the technical characteristics of the simulator used, students’ level of training and clinical experience, the duration of simulation training, and the different stages of endodontic treatment (access cavity preparation, location of canal orifices, canal shaping, irrigation, and obturation).

In the literature, only a limited number of studies have investigated the effects of haptic virtual reality simulators on real-patient applications [23, 24]. Sánchez-Herrera et al. trained fifth-year dental students on access cavity preparation in the maxillary first premolar (#14) using the Simodont haptic simulator, and subsequently evaluated their access cavity preparation performance and procedure-related anxiety during clinical endodontic treatment of maxillary first premolars (#14 and #24) in real patients [23]. Usta et al. provided preclinical training to fourth-year dental students using the VirTeaSy Dental virtual reality haptic simulator and the Dental EndoMaster mobile application, and then assessed students’ manual competence, stress levels, and self-confidence during access cavity preparation in maxillary incisors diagnosed with chronic apical periodontitis [24]. Unlike these clinical studies, the present study used SIMtoCARE Dente as the preclinical training tool; in addition, the evaluation was based on endodontic access cavity preparations performed by students using an indirect technique on maxillary anterior teeth (maxillary central incisors, lateral incisors, and canines), namely the central incisors, lateral incisors, and canines. In this respect, the study aimed to make an original contribution to the literature by focusing both on a different haptic simulator system and on a specific tooth group and application approach (indirect technique). Accordingly, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of haptic training on clinical performance by comparing the endodontic access cavity preparation performance of dental students who received HVRS training before treating patients with that of students who began clinical patient care without receiving this training. The secondary aim of this study was to assess students’ perceptions and opinions regarding HVRS training in terms of its usefulness, realism, and inclusion in the preclinical curriculum. The study hypothesis was that preclinical HVRS training would significantly improve students’ performance in endodontic access cavity preparation on real patients and would make a positive contribution to the preparation process before clinical practice.

Methods

Ethical approval and sample size calculation

This study was approved by the Mersin University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (protocol no. 2026/078, dated 04.02.2026). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before the clinical procedures. Based on the sample size of a previously conducted similar study [23] and the recommended sample size for pilot studies [25], a power analysis was performed using GPower software (GPower 3.1; Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany). Accordingly, an a priori power analysis was conducted for an independent samples t-test to evaluate the difference between the means of two independent groups. Under a two-tailed hypothesis, with an effect size of d = 1, a significance level of α = 0.05, a power of (1 − β) = 0.80, and an allocation ratio of 1:1 between groups, a total of 28 participants, with 14 participants in each group, was determined to be sufficient. To prevent possible data loss and sample attrition during the study process, 15 students were included in each group, and the study was conducted with a total of 30 students.

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a prospective, randomized, controlled, parallel-group educational intervention study to evaluate the effect of HVRS training on dental students’ performance in endodontic access cavity preparation in the clinical setting. The study was conducted with fourth-year dental students who were going to treat patients for the first time in the endodontics clinical practice course at the faculty of dentistry. Before entering clinical practice, all students had completed the conventional endodontic training routinely implemented at our faculty, which included both theoretical education and practical preclinical training. This training constitutes the common foundational educational framework for all undergraduate students and begins in the second year. In the second year, students receive theoretical instruction in endodontics throughout the academic year and simultaneously perform preclinical root canal treatment procedures on natural teeth. In the third year, theoretical instruction continues for an additional year; in parallel, students perform root canal treatment procedures on natural teeth mounted on phantom heads. This process is not limited to practical training alone; students are also regularly monitored through examinations and structured assessments in terms of both their theoretical knowledge and preclinical skills. Their practical performance is graded according to predetermined criteria, allowing their knowledge level, manual skills, and preclinical competence to be evaluated systematically. Therefore, all students included in the study had completed the same two-year intensive training process before entering clinical practice, including theoretical courses, structured preclinical training carried out on natural teeth and phantom heads, as well as the same assessment and grading procedures. Students who had successfully completed the third-year preclinical endodontic course and achieved a minimum preclinical course grade of 75/100 were eligible for inclusion in the study. This criterion was used to ensure that all participants had reached a comparable level of theoretical knowledge, manual competence, and preclinical performance before entering clinical practice. Written informed consent was obtained from all students who agreed to participate in the study. Participation was voluntary, and students were clearly informed that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any stage. All participant data were evaluated in accordance with confidentiality principles and used solely for scientific purposes. A total of 40 students who met the eligibility criteria were initially assessed for inclusion in the study. However, 10 students were excluded because they were unwilling to participate, were unable to comply with the study schedule, were absent on clinical practice days, failed to complete the study protocol, or did not meet the appropriate patient/tooth criteria for evaluation. Consequently, the study was completed with 30 fourth-year dental students, with a mean age of 22.47 ± 1.03 years; 16 were female and 14 were male. The inclusion criteria were as follows:

Allocation of participants to groups

After eligibility assessment and before the intervention, the 30 students included in the study were randomly allocated to either the experimental group or the control group in a 1:1 ratio. Randomization was performed using an online randomization tool (randomizer.org), which generated a random allocation sequence assigning 15 students to the experimental group and 15 students to the control group. The random allocation sequence was generated and held by an independent researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment, clinical training, or outcome assessment. Allocation concealment was maintained by keeping the allocation sequence inaccessible to the researchers involved in eligibility assessment and participant enrollment until enrollment had been completed. Group assignments were revealed only after eligibility assessment and informed consent had been obtained. After allocation, the experimental group received pre-training with HVRS before clinical practice, whereas the control group continued with the routine preclinical/clinical training process only. The evaluators responsible for clinical performance assessment were not involved in the generation of the allocation sequence or in assigning participants to groups.

Training protocol and clinical procedure

After the theoretical training, students in the control group were taken directly to clinical practice without receiving any haptic virtual reality simulator training. To ensure clinical comparability and standardization between the groups, cases were selected from teeth with a similar level of endodontic difficulty. The study included permanent, single-rooted, caries-free teeth indicated for root canal treatment, with no anatomical variation, no canal or pulp chamber calcification or pulp stones, no crown destruction or excessive loss of tooth structure, and suitable for Class I access cavity preparation. To provide a more controlled and comparable assessment during students’ first clinical experience, patients with maxillary central incisors, lateral incisors, and canines indicated for endodontic treatment, in which access cavities could be prepared using the indirect technique, were preferred. Eligible teeth and patients were selected by two researchers according to the predefined inclusion criteria. After eligible patients were identified, they were randomly assigned to students by the clinical secretary within the routine clinical workflow, without considering whether the students were in the experimental or control group. Thus, no selection or direction was made by the researchers during patient allocation between the groups. Students in the control group performed indirect access cavity preparation on a total of 15 permanent single-rooted teeth in patients presenting to the endodontics clinic, including 6 central incisors, 5 lateral incisors, and 4 canines (Table 4). The students completed the access cavity preparation under faculty supervision in accordance with standard clinical protocols, and the procedure time for each student was recorded in seconds using a stopwatch. This time measurement was performed to evaluate the students’ procedural efficiency during clinical practice.

Students in the experimental group received a standardized haptic simulator training program using SIMtoCARE Dente (SIMtoCARE B.V., Vreeland, the Netherlands) located at Mersin University Faculty of Dentistry before starting clinical practice. The training lasted 2 h per day for 5 days, for a total of 10 h, and was conducted by a single instructor according to a standard protocol. The total 10-hour training period was planned as an adequate introductory training duration to allow students to adapt to the haptic simulator, repeatedly practice the steps of endodontic access cavity preparation, and improve their hand–eye coordination, bur control, depth perception, and basic manual skills. In addition, this duration was determined by considering its feasibility within the preclinical education program and its ability to provide students with sufficient repetition in a controlled environment before proceeding to clinical practice. During the HVRS training, all procedures were performed under the supervision of a faculty member/researcher, and each access cavity preparation was evaluated by the instructor. At the end of the 10-hour training period, the students’ ability to perform the basic steps of access cavity preparation was assessed through instructor observation, and in the verbal feedback obtained from the students, all students stated that they felt competent in performing access cavity preparation on the haptic simulator. The main objective of this haptic training was to develop students’ skills in endodontic access cavity preparation on single-rooted teeth requiring Class I cavity preparation. The training content mainly focused on endodontic access cavity preparation in single-rooted teeth located in the maxilla. As in clinical practice, a diamond round bur was used at the beginning of access cavity preparation, followed by a safe-ended tapered bur. When students reached the canal entrance, the simulator provided immediate evaluation of whether the access cavity had been prepared appropriately through an on-screen validation image and system alert. The main objectives of the training included correct localization of the access cavity, bur control, differentiation between enamel and dentin tissues, controlled and accurate entry into the pulp chamber, creation of an appropriate cavity form allowing visualization of the canal orifice, and avoidance of unnecessary removal of tooth structure. Through the tactile feedback provided by the haptic simulator, the training aimed to improve not only students’ visual perception but also their manual and spatial skills (Fig. 1).

After completing the haptic simulation training, students in the experimental group also proceeded to chairside clinical practice under conditions comparable to those of the control group. In this context, as in the control group, the students performed indirect access cavity preparation on a total of 15 permanent single-rooted teeth (6 central incisors, 5 lateral incisors, and 4 canines) indicated for root canal treatment in patients presenting to the endodontics clinic (Table 4). During the clinical procedures, the same instruments and materials used in the control group were also used in the experimental group, and all procedures were carried out under faculty supervision and according to the same clinical standards. Furthermore, as in the control group, the time required for access cavity preparation in the experimental group was measured with a stopwatch and recorded in seconds.

Evaluation of endodontic access cavity preparation

The endodontic access cavity preparations performed by the students were evaluated independently by two specialist endodontists using predetermined scoring criteria. The evaluators were not involved in the random allocation process. However, complete blinding of the evaluators to group allocation could not be ensured due to the nature of the educational intervention and the clinical setting. In addition, one of the evaluators had been involved in the HVRS training process; therefore, the performance assessment was not considered fully blinded. To reduce potential assessment bias, both evaluators scored the access cavity preparations independently according to the same standardized rubric, and inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa analysis.

The evaluation criteria were developed in accordance with the fundamental principles of endodontic access cavity preparation, relevant literature [26], and current endodontic educational resources [16]. The assessment was carried out based on the five parameters presented in Fig. 2 for access cavity preparation. Similar structured assessment approaches have been used in previous haptic endodontic training studies [12, 19, 20, 23]. For each parameter, 1 point was assigned if the criterion was present in the clinical application, and 0 points if it was absent. The scores obtained from all parameters were summed to calculate the total access cavity preparation score for each case. Based on the total score, the quality of access cavity preparation was classified into three categories:0–1 points: poor access cavity preparation2–3 points: moderate access cavity preparation4–5 points: high (good) access cavity preparation

Questionnaire and subjective evaluation

The questionnaire was administered only to students in the experimental group after completion of both the HVRS training and the clinical application [19, 20, 23]. The questionnaire used in this study was developed by the authors in line with the aims of the present study, taking into account the relevant literature and the educational objectives of haptic virtual reality simulation in preclinical dental education. The questionnaire items were designed to evaluate the effects of HVRS training on students’ perceptions of technical skills, anatomical understanding, clinical self-confidence, stress management, and integration into the curriculum. In this context, the questionnaire specifically covered bur control, conservative cavity preparation, locating canal orifices, prevention of iatrogenic errors, better understanding of tooth anatomy, and self-confidence during clinical practice. In addition, it included students’ opinions regarding the use of HVRS in other endodontic procedures and its incorporation into the endodontic education curriculum. In this respect, the questionnaire provided a multidimensional content structure that assessed the psychomotor, cognitive, and affective contributions of HVRS together. The items were structured using a 5-point Likert scale, allowing participants to rate each statement as “strongly disagree,” “disagree,” “neutral,” “agree,” or “strongly agree.” To ensure content validity, the questionnaire form was evaluated in terms of clarity, relevance, and comprehensiveness by two endodontists who were not involved in the study, and the items were revised in accordance with their expert feedback. Before implementation, a pilot test was conducted with a small group of students to assess the clarity and comprehensibility of the items, and minor modifications were made where necessary. In its final form, the questionnaire consisted of 10 items, as presented in Table 5, and was distributed electronically to the students in the experimental group via Google Forms (Google LLC, USA). The data obtained from the questionnaire were analyzed to reveal students’ perceptions of HVRS and its contribution to their preparation for clinical practice.

Inter-rater reliability

In this study, the scoring of prepared endodontic access cavities was performed independently by two specialist dentists. Both evaluators assessed the samples separately according to the predetermined scoring criteria. To determine the consistency and reliability of the evaluations, Cohen’s kappa analysis was applied. The mean κ value was 0.93 (range: 0.86–0.98), indicating an exceptionally high level of agreement between the evaluators and confirming that the scoring procedure was reliable and consistent.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (min–max) for quantitative variables, and as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the data were found not to be normally distributed. Since the data were not normally distributed, the Mann–Whitney U test was used to analyze cavity scores and cavity preparation time. The Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test was used for the analysis of cavity categories. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to assess inter-researcher agreement. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated to assess the internal consistency of the questionnaire. A Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.70 or higher was considered an acceptable level of reliability. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Endodontic access cavity results

When the experimental and control groups were compared in terms of cavity score, the mean score of the experimental group (3.47 ± 0.83) was found to be higher than that of the control group (2.73 ± 0.88). Statistical analysis revealed that the difference between the groups was significant (Z = -2.052, p = 0.040). This finding indicates that the cavity score values in the experimental group were significantly higher than those in the control group (Table 1) (Fig. 3). No major procedural complications, such as perforation, damage to the pulpal floor, excessive removal of tooth structure, or any event preventing completion of the access cavity preparation, were observed in either group during the clinical procedures. Therefore, perforations and other major procedural errors were not analyzed as separate outcome measures.

Questionnaire results

The questionnaire results showed that the participants generally had positive views regarding HVRS training. In particular, high response rates were obtained for the statements that HVRS training increased confidence during access cavity preparation in the clinic (agree, 46.7%), reduced procedure time (agree, 46.7%), helped canal orifices to be located more easily (agree, 60.0%), and contributed to the prevention of iatrogenic errors (agree, 53.3%). In addition, the majority of participants indicated that they would like HVRS to be used not only for access cavity preparation but also for other endodontic procedures such as canal shaping, irrigation, and obturation (strongly agree, 46.7%), and stated that this training should be incorporated into the endodontics curriculum before clinical procedures (strongly agree, 46.7%). However, regarding the contribution of HVRS to understanding the anatomical challenges encountered in the clinic, the highest response rates were observed for agree (40.0%) and neutral (40.0%). Overall, the findings indicate that HVRS was perceived by students as a useful educational tool that supports clinical training (Table 5) (Fig. 5). When the internal consistency of the questionnaire was evaluated, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated as 0.98, indicating a high level of internal consistency.

Discussion

Preclinical education plays a critical role in enabling dental students to transform theoretical knowledge into psychomotor skills before clinical practice [1, 27]. In this process, haptic virtual simulation systems stand out as innovative tools that can be integrated into preclinical education by providing a controlled, repeatable, and safe learning environment. These systems have been reported to support students’ preparation before clinical practice, particularly in precise procedures such as endodontic access cavity preparation. However, the current literature indicates that these systems cannot completely replace traditional phantom-based preclinical training, but rather may be used as a complementary educational tool [28]. A review of the current literature shows that clinical studies evaluating the effects of haptic virtual simulation systems on real-patient applications are limited, indicating the need for further research in this field [23, 24]. For this reason, by comparing the access cavity preparation performance of students who received haptic training (experimental group) with those who did not (control group), we aimed to determine the contribution of haptic training to clinical skill development and to evaluate the extent of its effectiveness.

The hypothesis of this study was that preclinical HVRS training would improve students’ performance in endodontic access cavity preparation on real patients and make a positive contribution to the preparation process before clinical practice. In line with the findings obtained, this hypothesis was accepted. The higher scores observed in students who received haptic training, together with positive student feedback, suggest that HVRS may play a supportive role as an educational tool in the development of clinical skills and preparation for clinical practice.

When the experimental and control groups were compared in terms of cavity score, the mean score of the experimental group was higher than that of the control group, and this difference was found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05). This finding, consistent with previous studies, suggests that HVRS training may improve endodontic access cavity preparation performance and support clinical skill development [23, 24]. Similarly to the present study, the clinical studies conducted by Sánchez-Herrera et al. [23] and Usta et al. [24] reported that dental students trained with haptic virtual reality simulation achieved higher performance scores in endodontic access cavity preparation compared with the control groups.

Although no statistically significant difference was found between the groups in terms of cavity classification (p > 0.05), the distribution in favor of the experimental group is noteworthy. The absence of the poor classification and the higher proportion of moderate/good categories in the experimental group suggest that preclinical HVRS training may have supported students’ clinical performance. The lack of a significant difference may be related to the limited sample size, insufficient duration of haptic training, and the broad categorical structure of cavity classification. While cavity scores may be more sensitive in detecting small performance differences, classification reflects clinical competence at a more general level. Therefore, future studies with larger sample sizes, longer training periods, and more objective evaluation criteria are needed to clarify the effect of haptic training on cavity classification and clinical competence.

When evaluated in terms of procedural time, the experimental group completed the access cavity preparation in a shorter time than the control group, and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05). This finding indicates that preclinical HVRS training may positively affect not only students’ cavity preparation performance but also their procedural efficiency. The results are consistent with the findings of the randomized controlled clinical study conducted by Usta et al. in 2025. In that study, students trained with a haptic virtual reality simulator were also reported to complete access cavity preparation in significantly less time during clinical procedures compared with the control group [24].

In the present study, the higher cavity scores and shorter procedural times observed among students who received HVRS training may be explained not only by the development of motor skills, but also by cognitive and behavioral mechanisms involved in the learning process. From the perspective of cognitive load theory, during the transition from conventional preclinical training to the clinical environment, students are required to perform multiple mental tasks simultaneously, such as recognizing anatomical structures, recalling procedural steps, controlling instruments, and managing the fear of making errors [29]. HVRS training may have reduced this cognitive load by allowing students to practice access cavity preparation repeatedly in a controlled environment before treating real patients. However, it should also be considered that the direct transfer of skills acquired in a virtual environment to real patient conditions may be limited. The clinical setting includes several variables that cannot be fully represented in a simulation environment, such as patient movement, saliva control, restricted visibility, the presence of soft tissues, anxiety management, and anatomical variations [17]. Therefore, the effect of HVRS training should be interpreted not as a complete replacement for conventional clinical training, but rather as a preparatory approach that may shorten students’ learning curve and help them feel more prepared for their initial clinical procedures.

Survey findings showed that students perceived HVRS training as a useful and supportive method for clinical endodontic education. Most students reported that HVRS increased their self-confidence, shortened procedure time, facilitated the localization of canal orifices, and contributed to the prevention of iatrogenic errors. These findings suggest that HVRS may support not only technical skill development but also students’ readiness for clinical practice, which is consistent with previous studies [23, 24]. When clinical performance and student perceptions after HVRS training were evaluated together, this method appeared to be supportive particularly in terms of procedure time, access cavity preparation quality, and readiness for clinical practice. The shorter procedure time in the experimental group compared with the control group, together with the finding that 80% of students reported that HVRS shortened their procedure time in the clinic, suggests that this training may contribute to procedural efficiency. In addition, the higher cavity scores observed in the experimental group, the greater number of students in the “good” category, and the absence of students in the “poor” category suggest that HVRS training may support the quality of access cavity preparation and reduce the risk of low performance. These findings are consistent with the students’ reports that they were able to prepare more conservative access cavities after HVRS training. However, only 40% of the students stated that HVRS helped them understand anatomical difficulties encountered in the clinic, indicating that this method alone may not be sufficient for teaching complex anatomical variations and clinical challenges. Although most students had a positive attitude toward integrating HVRS training into the endodontic curriculum, several implementation-related barriers should be considered. System cost, technical infrastructure requirements, faculty training, and accessibility issues may limit its routine use in dental education [17]. Therefore, HVRS should be evaluated not only in terms of educational effectiveness, but also in terms of feasibility, sustainability, and cost-benefit balance. In addition, the transfer of skills acquired in the simulation environment to real clinical practice, their long-term retention, and the influence of patient-related variables remain controversial [14]. Clinical factors such as mouth opening, saliva control, patient movement, anxiety, pain management, radiographic interpretation, tooth position, presence of restorations, pulp chamber size, and variations in root canal anatomy should be considered when interpreting the contribution of HVRS training to clinical performance [7].

Although some studies have reported that haptic virtual reality training may positively affect the clinical and preclinical performance of dental students [23, 24], other studies have shown that this effect is not always evident or that HVRS does not provide superiority over conventional training methods [11, 30, 31]. The inconsistent findings in the literature may be attributed to differences in simulator technology, the realism of haptic feedback, training duration, procedural difficulty, students’ previous experience, and assessment methods.

The clinical significance of this study may be considered in relation to the shorter time required by students who received HVRS training to complete access cavity preparation, which could improve patient comfort, reduce fatigue associated with prolonged mouth opening, and facilitate more efficient appointment management. Although an increase in cavity preparation scores was observed in the experimental group, the lack of a significant difference in cavity classification limits the interpretation of this improvement as a direct indicator of clinical competence. Additionally, the experimental group received an additional 10-hour structured HVRS training, suggesting that the higher scores may reflect not only the effect of the simulator but also the combined influence of extended practice and repeated training. Therefore, HVRS training should be considered a complementary approach that supports more controlled and time-efficient procedures and facilitates the transition from preclinical to clinical practice, rather than a method that independently improves clinical success.

This study has several limitations. First, the limited sample size may have reduced the generalizability of the findings and may also have contributed to the inability to detect statistically significant differences in some comparisons. In addition, if the duration of the haptic training had been longer, the effect of the training on clinical performance might have been demonstrated more clearly. Therefore, future studies with larger sample sizes and longer HVRS training periods are needed to support these findings. Second, it should be considered that some of the criteria used in the performance assessment, particularly the quality scoring of access cavity preparations, may include a certain degree of subjectivity because they were based on evaluator judgment. Although standardized evaluation criteria were used, subjective interpretation cannot be completely eliminated. Therefore, the use of more objective and digital measurement methods in future studies may increase the reliability of the assessment. Third, the performance assessment in this study reflected only the students’ immediate clinical performance. The effect of HVRS training on the retention of acquired skills or its contribution to long-term clinical performance was not evaluated. Therefore, future studies should include follow-up assessments to determine whether the skills gained through haptic training are maintained over time. Fourth, this study evaluated only endodontic access cavity preparation performance on real patients. The effect of HVRS training on more advanced endodontic procedures, such as canal shaping, irrigation, and obturation, was not assessed. Therefore, future studies should separately investigate the effects of HVRS training on different endodontic procedures. Fifth, another limitation is that the evaluation was restricted to specific tooth groups. Since different tooth types and anatomical characteristics may influence the difficulty level of access cavity preparation, studies including different tooth groups in both the maxilla and mandible may provide more comprehensive information. Sixth, patient- and environment-related variables during clinical practice could not be fully controlled. Factors such as patient anxiety, mouth opening, oral anatomical variations, saliva control, visibility, and similar clinical conditions may have influenced students’ performance. Since these variables cannot be completely eliminated in a real clinical setting, this should be considered when interpreting the findings. Seventh, the student perception survey was administered only to the experimental group, which may also be considered a limitation. This approach was preferred because the main aim of the study was to evaluate the perceptions of students who had received HVRS training, and the control group had no experience with the haptic system. However, the absence of comparable perception data from the control group limits the comparison of student attitudes between groups. In addition, the questionnaire was developed specifically for the purposes of this study and was not a previously validated standardized instrument. Although expert review, pilot testing for clarity and comprehensibility, and internal consistency analysis using Cronbach’s alpha were performed, more comprehensive psychometric validation, such as factor analysis and test–retest reliability assessment, was not conducted. Therefore, the questionnaire findings should be interpreted as descriptive perception data rather than as results obtained from a fully validated measurement scale. Eighth, another important limitation of this study is the potential training-process-related bias arising from the additional 10-hour structured HVRS training received by the experimental group. Since the control group did not receive an equivalent amount of additional practice time or an alternative training activity, it is not possible to clearly distinguish whether the performance improvement observed in the experimental group resulted specifically from the use of HVRS or from the additional training time, increased repetition, and structured practice process. This may limit the interpretation of the specific effect of the intervention and may introduce a risk of training-process-related bias. Future studies should minimize this potential bias by providing the control group with additional training or standardized practice of similar duration and intensity, so that the only difference between groups is the educational method used. Ninth, the single-center design of the study, the relatively homogeneous student population, and the use of only the SIMtoCARE Dente system may limit the generalizability of the findings to different institutions, student groups, and other haptic simulator systems. Therefore, future studies should be conducted in multiple centers, with larger and more diverse student populations, and should compare different haptic simulator systems to better evaluate the external validity of the findings. Finally, due to the nature of the educational intervention and because one of the evaluators was also involved in delivering the haptic simulator training, complete blinding of the evaluators to group allocation could not be achieved. Therefore, the performance assessments were not conducted under fully blinded conditions, which may have introduced a risk of assessment bias. In future studies, it is recommended to use independent evaluators who are not involved in the training process and who are blinded to group allocation in order to improve the methodological rigor and reliability of performance assessment. In addition, studies designed with larger sample sizes, longer training periods, different tooth groups and endodontic procedures, long-term follow-up assessments, and more objective measurement methods would allow the true contribution of HVRS training to endodontic education to be evaluated more comprehensively and reliably.

Conclusions

The results of this study indicate that HVRS, used in the preclinical phase before clinical practice, may positively affect undergraduate dental students’ performance in endodontic access cavity preparation on patients. The higher cavity scores, shorter procedure times, and positive student feedback observed in the haptic training group suggest that HVRS may be an effective preparatory educational tool that supports clinical skill development. However, considering the reported limitations in tactile realism, HVRS should not be regarded as a replacement for conventional preclinical education, but rather as a complementary tool that supports it.
