Development, Factor Structure, and Preliminary Psychometric Evaluation of the Rhinitis Medicamentosa Questionnaire (RMQ) Among Topical Nasal Decongestant Users
Authors: Lili Lakatos, Attila Velich, Zsolt Demetrovics, Andrea Czakó, Ildikó Danis, Szabolcs Szentpéteri, József Rácz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRhinitis medicamentosa (RM) is associated with prolonged topical nasal decongestant (TND) use and may involve dependency‐like behavioral patterns extending beyond nasal obstruction alone. Existing sinonasal patient‐reported outcome measures do not specifically assess these behavioral features. This study aimed to develop the Rhinitis Medicamentosa Questionnaire (RMQ) and perform a preliminary psychometric evaluation in individuals reporting TND use.MethodsInstrument development followed Waltz's four‐stage framework and was informed by Griffiths' addiction model and DSM‐5‐TR substance use disorder criteria. An initial 50‐item pool underwent face and content evaluation involving patients and a multidisciplinary expert panel, resulting in a 37‐item version. A total of 390 Hungarian adults reporting TND use within the previous 12 months completed the online questionnaire. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA; n = 210) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA; n = 180) were performed on non‐overlapping subsamples. Internal consistency, preliminary test–retest reliability, and score distribution were assessed.ResultsThe final RMQ consisted of 16 items across four factors: Overuse, Salience, Tolerance, and Withdrawal. CFA demonstrated good model fit (CFI = 0.975, TLI = 0.970, RMSEA = 0.055). Internal consistency was high across all subscales (Cronbach's α = 0.872–0.944), and preliminary test–retest reliability was favorable (ICC = 0.92). Higher RMQ scores were associated with more frequent and prolonged self‐reported TND use. An exploratory sample‐derived threshold was identified for higher dependency‐related burden within the study population.ConclusionThe RMQ is a newly developed multidimensional questionnaire designed to assess dependency‐related behavioral patterns associated with prolonged TND use. This preliminary study supports its internal structure, reliability, and interpretability. Further validation in clinically confirmed RM populations and international settings is required.

Introduction

Nasal blockage is a prevalent symptom in the general population, affecting 1 in 10 people daily [1], leading many individuals to turn to over‐the‐counter decongestant nasal sprays for relief. Rhinitis medicamentosa (RM) is a clinical condition associated with prolonged and excessive use of topical nasal decongestants (TND) [2, 3]. It is characterized by a “vicious cycle” where rebound mucosal congestion necessitates increasingly frequent reapplication [4, 5]. Over time, patients develop tolerance, requiring escalating doses to achieve relief. Emerging evidence suggests that prolonged TND use may involve behavioral and dependency‐like features extending beyond nasal obstruction alone [6], aligning RM with the framework of behavioral addictions.

To date, several research approaches have attempted to establish diagnostic criteria for RM. These have primarily focused on the duration of use ranging from 3 days to 4 weeks [7, 8, 9, 10], the presence of characteristic histological alterations in the nasal mucosa [10, 11, 12], or macroscopic changes of the nasal mucosa visible during physical inspection [4, 13, 14]. However, none of these methods provide a definitive differential diagnosis, as similar pathological findings are frequently observed in allergic rhinitis or other forms of drug‐induced rhinitis. Standardized diagnostic criteria for RM remain unestablished, largely due to conflicting pathophysiological theories and the lack of consensus on the diagnostic criteria.

Standardized QoL questionnaires support patient‐centered care by identifying unmet needs, evaluating treatment effects beyond symptom reduction, and guiding individualized care planning and outcome monitoring [15]. In rhinology, the most widely utilized tools include the 22‐item Sinonasal Outcome Test (SNOT‐22) [16], Nasal Obstruction Symptom Evaluation (NOSE) [17], Rhinoconjunctivitis Quality of Life Questionnaire (RQLQ) [18], Visual Analogue Scale of Nasal Obstruction (VAS) [19], and Total Nasal Symptom Score (TNSS) [20]. Although these tools effectively assess sinonasal symptoms and QoL impairment, they do not specifically capture dependency‐related behavioral patterns associated with prolonged TND use.

The literature has seen the extensive development of validated scales for various behavioral addictions, such as internet [21], online gaming [22], gambling [23], exercise [24], and mobile phone addiction [25], as well as workaholism [26]. Diagnostic frameworks for these conditions have evolved from Griffiths' components model of addiction [27] to the current DSM‐5‐TR criteria (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision) [28]. While the DSM‐5‐TR typically employs nine criteria for behavioral addictions, there is a significant theoretical overlap with Griffiths' six‐element approach—comprising salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict, and relapse. These core components provide a useful theoretical framework for examining dependency‐related behavioral features associated with prolonged TND use. A structured assessment of dependency‐related experiences may support future research and improve characterization of prolonged TND use.

Consequently, there is a need for a specialized instrument capable of assessing dependency‐related symptoms and behavioral patterns associated with prolonged TND use. The present study aimed to develop the Rhinitis Medicamentosa Questionnaire (RMQ) and perform a preliminary psychometric evaluation of its measurement properties in a Hungarian‐speaking population.

Methods

Study Design

This study was conducted following the four‐stage framework (2.1.1‐4) proposed by Waltz et al. [29], and a preliminary psychometric evaluation was performed and reported in accordance with the COSMIN guidelines [30].

Selection of a Conceptual Model

The conceptual framework for this study was established through an integrative review of existing literature on RM e.g., [31, 32, 33, 34]. Given that RM exhibits characteristics analogous to addictive disorders, the conceptual framework was informed by Griffiths' component model of addiction and DSM‐5‐TR substance use disorder criteria.

Determination of Measurement Objectives

The measurement objectives focused on dependency‐related behavioral dimensions associated with prolonged TND use. The modality was established as a self‐report instrument utilizing a four‐point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Agree, and 4 = Strongly agree), chosen to reduce central tendency bias and encourage directional responses regarding dependency‐related experiences. As the instrument was intentionally designed without negatively worded items, reverse scoring was not required. Total scores were calculated by summing individual item ratings.

Development of a Blueprint

A content specification table was constructed to define addiction as the core construct and its components as constituent elements. This ensured balanced representation across all dimensions by allocating an appropriate number of items to each addiction component.

Instrument Construction

An initial pool of 50 items was generated by the research team based on previous concept analysis codes [6], covering behaviors, thoughts, and emotions related to the phenomenon.

Content Validity

It was evaluated using qualitative and quantitative methods involving a multidisciplinary expert panel of 8 specialists in otorhinolaryngology (n = 3), psychiatry (n = 2), addiction psychology (n = 2), and instrument development (n = 1). During the qualitative phase, the panel evaluated item wording, grammar, scaling, and conceptual allocation. Quantitative content validity was assessed using the content validity ratio (CVR) and the item‐content validity index (I‐CVI). For the CVR, the eight experts rated each item as ‘essential,’ “useful but not essential,” or “not essential.” Following Lawshe's criteria for an eight‐member panel [35], a minimum CVR value of 0.75 was required for item retention; items below this threshold were revised or excluded. For CVI assessment, experts rated item relevance on a four‐point Likert scale, with I‐CVI values ≥ 0.78 considered acceptable [36]. Chance agreement was adjusted using modified Cohen's kappa coefficients, with values κ ≥ 0.74 indicating excellent agreement [37]. Based on these indices and expert feedback, 37 items were retained from the initial pool of 50.

Face Validity

Face validity was evaluated using a mixed‐methods approach with 10 individuals with self‐reported prolonged TND use. Qualitative assessment used face‐to‐face interviews to identify difficulties, ambiguities, and issues with item appropriateness. Feedback was analyzed descriptively, and the research team discussed suggested modifications. For the quantitative assessment, participants rated item importance on a five‐point Likert scale (1: “Not important” to 5: “Completely important”). An item impact score was calculated by multiplying the frequency of high importance ratings (%) by the mean importance score. Items exceeding the 1.5 threshold were retained [37].

Participants and Data Collection

Data were collected using an online, self‐administered questionnaire accessible via QR code. A total of 390 individuals completed the survey between March and October 2025. Eligibility criteria included age ≥ 18 years and self‐reported use of decongestant nasal sprays or drops within the preceding 12 months. No physician‐confirmed diagnosis of RM was required for participation. Access to the questionnaire was restricted to individuals meeting these criteria. QR codes were distributed via printed flyers in outpatient clinics and pharmacies, and the survey was also promoted on social media platforms (e.g., Facebook community groups for local residents). The recruitment strategy was designed to maximize accessibility to individuals with prolonged TND use; however, it may have preferentially captured more symptomatic or health‐conscious users.

Statistical Analysis

The structure of the instrument was assessed using exploratory (EFA) and confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) on non‐overlapping random subsamples. The initial sample was partitioned sequentially by completion order. EFA was conducted on the first subsample (n = 210) using the 37‐item pool in IBM SPSS Statistics version 31.0. Data were screened for missing values and outliers; normality was verified via skewness (±2) and kurtosis (±3). Sampling adequacy was evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure and Bartlett's test of sphericity. Items with communalities < 0.40 were considered for removal [38]. Factor extraction was performed using principal axis factoring, with factors retained based on eigenvalues greater than 1. Direct oblimin rotation was applied, assuming correlated latent constructs. Retention criteria included factor loadings ≥ 0.40, minimal cross‐loadings, and conceptual relevance. Item reduction was guided by psychometric performance, conceptual relevance, redundancy, and clarity of interpretation.

The factor structure identified through EFA was subsequently evaluated using CFA with maximum likelihood estimation on a separate subsample (n = 180) in AMOS version 31.0 to verify the fit of the 16‐item model. Model fit was assessed using the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Relative Fit index (RFI) and Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), with values ≥ 0.95 indicating good model fit, as well as the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), with value ≤ 0.08 considered indicative of adequate model fit [38].

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha, with values ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable. Preliminary temporal stability was explored using test–retest data obtained from a subsample of 20 participants who completed the questionnaire twice over a 2‐to‐4‐week interval. Relative stability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; two‐way random‐effects model, absolute agreement), while absolute stability was quantified using the standard error of measurement (SEM).

As no established RM‐specific comparator instrument currently exists, convergent and discriminant validity analyses could not be performed within the scope of the present study.

Ethical Considerations

The Ethics Committee of Semmelweis University of Medical Sciences, Budapest, Hungary, approved this study (code: BM/5088‐1/2025). Prior to participation, all respondents were informed about the study objectives, assured of confidentiality, and provided electronic informed consent via the digital interface before completing the questionnaire.

Content validity: The original questionnaire included 50 items. During face validity evaluation, four items were omitted, and seven items were revised based on participant feedback. In the content validity assessment, seven items were removed due to overlap or CVR values below 0.75, and an additional two items were excluded because their I‐CVI values were less than 0.78. Consequently, the number of items was reduced to 37, yielding a Scale‐level Content Validity Index (S‐CVI) of 0.91. Modified Cohen's kappa coefficients for the retained items ranged from 0.76 to 1.00, indicating acceptable content validity.

Normality testing: Although the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test indicated deviation from normality, skewness and kurtosis values were within acceptable limits, supporting approximate normality of the score distribution.

Missing values: The dataset was screened for missing values and outliers, and none were identified.

Item analysis: Of 37 items, six were removed because their inter‐item correlation coefficients exceeded 0.70, indicating item overlap. The corrected item–total correlations for the remaining items were all above 0.30, which is considered acceptable for retention.

Factorability: The KMO statistic was 0.930, and the Bartlett's test of sphericity was statistically significant (approx. χ 2 = 2693.9, df = 120, p < 0.001), confirming sampling adequacy and appropriateness for factor analysis.

Structure of the Instrument and Item Deletion: EFA was conducted on a n = 210 subsample iteratively, starting with 31 items. Items with communalities below 0.40, substantial cross‐loadings, or limited coherence with the emerging factor structure were removed stepwise, and the model was re‐run after each deletion. Across successive iterations, 4, 3, 2, 2, and 4 items were excluded, resulting in a final 16‐item model. Factor retention was based on eigenvalues > 1, scree plot inspection, and theoretical interpretability. Final communalities ranged from 0.555 to 0.925. The final solution comprised 16 items loading on four factors, which were interpreted based on item content and labeled as Overuse (5 items), Salience (4 items), Tolerance (3 items), and Withdrawal (4 items). Each factor explained more than 5% of the variance, with the four factors together accounting for 78% of the total variance. Factor loadings were presented in Table 2.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis

The 16‐item, four‐factor model derived from EFA was tested as a latent‐variable structure using CFA on a N = 180 subsample. The model demonstrated a good fit to the data across multiple indices: χ 2/df = 1.549, RFI = 0.919, TLI = 0.970, CFI = 0.975, RMSEA = 0.055, and PCLOSE = 0.292. All factor loadings were statistically significant and exceeded the minimum threshold of 0.40, with minimal cross‐loadings. Strong positive correlations were observed among the factors, with coefficients ranging from 0.66 to 0.77, indicating substantial interrelatedness without multicollinearity (Figure 1).

Scoring

Items were scored on a scale of 1–4, resulting in a possible total score ranging from 16 to 64. For the 16 items, the mean total score of the respondents was 37.0 (SD = 12.9). A preliminary sample‐derived threshold of 48 points was set to indicate higher levels of addiction‐like features within the present sample, corresponding to the 75th percentile of the sample distribution. Notably, this threshold exceeded the sample mean by more than the minimal detectable change (MDC = 10.11). To explore the interpretability of this preliminary threshold, a one‐way ANOVA was conducted. Participants in the higher‐score group (RMQ ≥ 48) reported significantly higher daily frequency of nasal spray use (M = 2.64, SD = 1.19) compared to those below the cut‐off (M = 1.73, SD = 0.92), F(1, 388) = 60.02, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.13. Similarly, the duration of nasal spray use was significantly greater in the higher‐score group (M = 4.20, SD = 1.28) than in the lower‐score group (M = 3.44, SD = 1.73), F(1, 388) = 15.66, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.04. Both variables were assessed on 5‐point ordinal scales; means therefore represent scale scores rather than absolute units.

In the absence of an external diagnostic reference, this percentile‐based threshold was chosen to identify individuals with the highest symptom burden in the examined sample. These findings provide preliminary support for the interpretability of higher RMQ scores, as elevated scores were associated with more frequent and prolonged TND use. However, this threshold should not be interpreted as a clinically validated diagnostic cut‐off and requires further evaluation in physician‐confirmed RM populations.

Discussion

This study developed the Rhinitis Medicamentosa Questionnaire (RMQ), a 16‐item, multidimensional instrument designed to assess dependency‐related behavioral patterns and functional burden associated with prolonged TND use and to provide preliminary psychometric evaluation of its structure and reliability. The RMQ demonstrated promising preliminary psychometric properties, including a stable four‐factor structure, satisfactory item performance, and good internal consistency, supporting its use as an exploratory instrument for characterizing TND overuse‐related experiences.

The primary aim was to develop a brief questionnaire capable of capturing behavioral features of prolonged TND use in addition to symptom burden. Such an instrument may be useful in outpatient settings to support the identification of individuals with potentially problematic use patterns who may benefit from closer clinical follow‐up or more comprehensive management strategies beyond standard treatment strategies (e.g., cessation of TND, intranasal corticosteroids). Importantly, the RMQ is not intended as a standalone diagnostic tool for RM, but rather as a structured method for quantifying dependency‐like behavioral features within this patient population. A secondary exploratory aim was to examine whether score distributions could support preliminary categorization of higher versus lower dependency‐related burden within the study sample. In addition, the RMQ may provide a standardized framework for future research by enabling more consistent characterization of TND use patterns and improving comparability across studies.

A key methodological challenge in the development of the RMQ was the absence of a previously validated instrument specifically targeting dependency‐like behavioral features in TND overuse. Many existing rhinological questionnaires [16, 17, 18, 19] focusing on nasal breathing and nasal symptoms fail to adequately capture the functional impairment experienced by individuals with RM, while generic QoL instruments [39, 40] are too broad to clearly attribute QoL deterioration specifically to TND use. Accordingly, the RMQ was designed to characterize dependency‐related behavioral patterns associated with prolonged TND use. Item development was informed by prior qualitative research [6] and guided by Griffiths' component model of addiction and DSM‐5‐TR substance use disorder criteria, acknowledging the substantial conceptual overlap between these frameworks. Multiple items were initially generated for each criterion to support psychometric refinement.

EFA yielded a four‐factor structure, with the primary factor (“Overuse”) accounting for the largest proportion of variance and encompassing excessive use, efforts to control, craving, and continued use despite harmful consequences. “Tolerance” and “Withdrawal” emerged as distinct factors, corresponding to DSM‐5 criteria Nos. 10 and 11, respectively. Consistent with qualitative findings, social and interpersonal conflicts appeared less prominent in TND abuse, and related items were excluded during analysis. The fourth factor, termed “Salience”, reflected preoccupation with use and integration into daily routines, in line with Griffiths' conceptualization.

Participant recruitment prioritized broad accessibility to maximize sample size and statistical robustness; therefore, all individuals who had used any TND within the previous year were eligible to complete the questionnaire. Consequently, not all respondents were regular users or met the criteria for nasal spray abuse. However, the sample was predominantly composed of long‐term users, with 50.8% reporting TND use for more than 5 years and 70.8% indicating use for at least 1 year. The sample included a broad spectrum of TND users, with a substantial proportion of long‐term users, allowing exploration of score distribution within a real‐world, self‐selected population. However, the self‐selected nature of the sample may have introduced participation bias.

In the absence of an external diagnostic reference, a preliminary sample‐derived threshold (75th percentile, 48 points) was used to explore potential differentiation of higher dependency‐related burden within the study population. This threshold should be interpreted as an exploratory categorization rather than a clinically validated cut‐off. Higher RMQ scores were associated with increased self‐reported frequency and duration of TND use, providing preliminary evidence of criterion‐related interpretability.

Limitations

All measures were self‐reported, which may introduce recall and social desirability bias. As no previously available instrument specifically designed to assess nasal spray dependence was available, concurrent validity could not be assessed. The RMQ was developed in Hungarian, and all participants completed this version. The English translation followed a structured process including AI‐assisted forward translation, native‐speaker proofreading, professional back‐translation, and final review of the Hungarian back‐translated version. Further studies are needed to validate the psychometric properties of the English version. A key limitation is that no clinically confirmed RM cohort was included, therefore diagnostic accuracy and clinically validated cut‐off values could not be established. Future research should therefore validate the scale in clinical populations where formal diagnosis can serve as a reference standard. The study population consisted predominantly of women (81%), which may reflect differential willingness to participate in online health surveys rather than the epidemiology of RM. Because RM has not consistently been shown to exhibit a female predominance, the present findings should be generalized cautiously until replicated in more demographically balanced populations.

Conclusion

The RMQ is a brief, multidimensional patient‐reported questionnaire designed to assess dependency‐related behavioral patterns and functional burden associated with prolonged TND use. The present study offers preliminary evidence for its factor structure, reliability and interpretability, supporting potential utility as an exploratory tool for characterizing TND overuse‐related experiences.

Its concise format may facilitate use in research settings and future clinical studies aimed at evaluating patterns of problematic TND use. However, it is not intended as a standalone diagnostic instrument, and further validation in clinically confirmed populations and cross‐cultural settings is required to establish its full psychometric properties and determine its potential clinical relevance.
