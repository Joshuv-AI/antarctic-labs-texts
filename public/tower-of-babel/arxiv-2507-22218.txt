Abstract
Many political science theories relate to latent variables, but such quantities cannot be observed directly and must instead be estimated from data with inherent uncertainty. In regression models, when a variable is measured with error, its slope coefficient is known to be biased toward zero. We show how measurement error interacts with unique aspects of latent variable estimation, identification restrictions in particular, and demonstrate how common error adjustment strategies can worsen bias. We introduce a method for adjusting coefficients on latent predictors, which reduces bias and typically increases the magnitude of estimated coefficients, often dramatically. We illustrate these dynamics using several different estimation strategies for the latent predictors. Corrected estimates using our proposed method show stronger relationships—sometimes up to 50% larger—than those from naive regression. Our findings highlight the importance of considering measurement error in latent predictors and the inadequacy of many commonly used approaches for dealing with this issue. Introduction

Many key concepts in political science, such as political knowledge, ideology, and democracy, are fundamentally unobservable and can only be estimated based on sets of observed indicators. A large body of research relies on measures of these latent variables as predictors in regression models to test theories and make empirical claims. For example, studies have employed proxies for political knowledge to explain patterns of political participation
(
Carpini & Keeter 1996
)
, used estimates of individuals’ ideology to predict vote choice and other political behaviors
(
Tausanovitch & Warshaw 2013
;
Jessee 2009
)
, and used indices of democracy to assess the relationship between democratic institutions and economic growth
(
Acemoglu et al. 2019
)
or the risk of conflict
(
Hegre et al. 2001
)
.

Because these latent variables must be estimated based on observed indicators, these measurements necessarily will include error. Latent variables also have no inherent scale, which means that researchers must impose identifying restrictions to pin these measurements down. This, combined with measurement error, produces coefficient estimates that are biased in different ways as compared to the standard classical measurement error case with observable variables. While the problems of measurement error and attenuation bias are widely recognized
(
Carroll et al. 2006
;
Benoit et al. 2009
;
Pancost & Schaller 2022
;
Fong & Tyler 2021
)
, the additional challenges posed by latent predictors are often underemphasized in applied work. Indeed, in an original survey of all articles published in the three top substantive political science journals (
APSR, AJPS, JOP
as well as
Political Analysis
) between 2000 and 2023, we find that while a growing number of articles mention latent variables or measurement error, a small and relatively constant number of papers discuss the two in combination (see Appendix VII).

In this paper, we examine the issues associated with using estimates of latent variables as predictors and present methods to mitigate the resulting biases under specific assumptions. We build on previous work, including
Achen 1975
and
Ansolabehere et al. 2008
, while deriving important new results and introducing new tools that account for key features of latent variables and their estimation. We show that two commonly used techniques for adjusting regression estimates with latent predictors can and often do make the problem worse, sometimes dramatically so. Most notably, the method of composition
(
Treier & Jackman 2008
)
, which is the most prominent method used in this area, actually makes the attenuation bias due to measurement error
worse
than it would be if the measurement error had simply been ignored altogether.

We propose a corrected estimator based on adjusted split-sample linear regression and instrumental variables (IV) approaches that can reduce bias and yield consistent estimates of the regression coefficients under reasonable assumptions. Our approach can be used with a wide range of measurement models, including simple additive or averaging strategies, factor analytic approaches, or machine learning techniques, and does not require simultaneous estimation of the measurement models and regression model, making its application more straightforward for applied researchers than full joint estimation of the latent traits and regression models as well as other approaches (e.g.,
Blackwell et al. 2017
;
Schofield 2015
).

We illustrate these methods with several applications, showing that correcting estimates for measurement error can sometimes result in substantively large changes, in some cases up to 50% in magnitude. But in other cases, measurement error may be mild enough that these corrections are smaller, perhaps even negligible. We also present theory and simulation results that demonstrate that while the naive OLS estimator (ignoring measurement error in the latent predictor) is biased toward zero and previously used methods for addressing measurement error can result in even more severely biased estimates, our corrected estimator dramatically reduces bias and produces estimates very close to those obtained using the true latent predictor values in simulations. Finally, we provide open-source software for implementing these methods, helping applied researchers account for measurement error when using latent predictors in practice.

1
Latent Variable Estimation and Identification

Our use of the term
latent
is meant to indicate not only that we do not have a true measure of the variable, but also that the variable is fundamentally unobservable and lacks an objective scale. Variables such as knowledge or ideology do not have objective scales. The values of these variables can be measured indirectly by examining indicators such as the number of factual questions a respondent answers correctly in the case of knowledge or the number of policy questions a respondent gives the conservative response to in the case of ideology. Of course, we could define the variable of interest in these cases as the proportion of questions answered correctly (or the proportion of responses given in the conservative direction), which would mean that we do, in fact, have the variable itself measured. But it is likely that we actually care about some true underlying level of knowledge or of liberal-conservative ideology, and that the responses to these indicators are useful because they give us (imperfect) information about each respondent’s true value on these latent scales.

To put it more formally, we use the term
latent variable
to refer to a variable that is not directly observable, can only be estimated based on a set of stochastic indicators, and that does not have an objectively defined scale, so therefore must be measured on a scale that is defined by one or more identifying restrictions. Using these sorts of variables as predictors in regression models presents additional complications above and beyond the well-known issues with measurement error in (non-latent) predictors, as we discuss below.

We do not consider here the important issue of whether a measurement strategy for a latent trait is theoretically related to the concept of interest (e.g.,
Morucci et al. 2025

We note that some other work has used the term
latent
to refer to variables that have objective scales, but which, in some applications, may not be directly observed. For example, a person’s annual income may be considered latent if it is assumed that we do not have a measure of its exact value, but we instead have a variable that is measured with error. In this case, the scale of income is objective (presumably measured in dollars per year), even though it cannot be directly measured with perfect precision. These types of variables are not what we refer to here as latent, and in fact, they correspond to the standard measurement error setup with observable predictors.

Before concluding, we re-emphasize that, while data-rescaling is usually not a source of bias (see preceding reference to the Maximum Likelihood invariance principle), in this case, the dynamics are different because the scaling factors are data- and measurement error-dependent.

Below, we briefly outline the additional complications that arise when using a predictor that is measured with error, is latent, and therefore has no objective scale. The key issue is that some scale must be assumed for the latent trait (i.e., we must pin down the variable’s meaning), both for the true unobserved variable
X
and for the observed but measured with error variable,
\hat{X}
.

A commonly used identification restriction is that the true latent variable
X
has mean 0 and variance 1, which identifies the scale in the common one-dimensional setting, except for direction (sign flips).
3
3

3

See
Rivers 2003

The direction of the scale is typically specified as well, with a corresponding identifying restriction imposed, e.g., that higher values of an ideology scale mean that someone is more conservative. In our discussion here, we adopt the typical identification restriction, assuming that the true latent variable
X
has a mean of 0 and a variance of 1. This is without loss of generality since the scale is arbitrary, similar to how temperature could be measured using the Celsius or Fahrenheit scale. The mean 0, variance 1 identification restriction has the advantage of making the latent variable’s scale essentially a
z
-score, indicating how many standard deviations above or below the mean any value is, which can aid interpretability. The results below generally apply similarly to other possible identification restrictions. To see this, note that in the one-dimensional case for most ideal point or factor analytic models, using a different identification restriction will simply impose an affine transformation on the estimated latent traits.

i

•

•

\hat{X}
is equal to
\tilde{X}
minus the sample mean of
\tilde{X}

\tilde{X}
.

In the standard (non-latent variable) measurement error setup with an observable predictor, we would observe
\tilde{X}

X

\tilde{X}

\tilde{X}

X
. In the case of a latent predictor, not only do we not observe the true variable
X
but we also do not observe
\tilde{X}
. Instead, we must use the identified and transformed
\hat{X}
as our predictor, which is rescaled to have the same variance as the true
X
. We can think of
\hat{X}
as formed by taking the untransformed estimates
\tilde{X}
and subtracting off the sample mean and dividing by the sample standard deviation. Note that we do not typically know what the variance of
\tilde{X}
is without knowing the variance of the measurement error
\sigma^{2}_{U}
, so we cannot know what the scale of
\tilde{X}
would be.

To put it differently, when estimating a latent variable based on a set of indicators, researchers need to define a scale for the true latent trait that we are interested in, but also need to identify the estimates of that latent trait (i.e., pin them down to a scale). A common thing to do is for researchers to think of these two sets of identification restrictions as being the same – if we think of the true latent variable
X
as having mean 0 and variance 1, then we impose these identifying restrictions so that our point estimates of the latent traits have mean 0 and variance 1. But when a variable is measured with ‘‘white noise’’ error, the measurements will have a larger variance than the true variable.

4

4

It would be possible to identify estimates to have variance equal to the assumed variance of the true
X
plus the variance of the measurement error
\sigma^{2}_{U}
, but this would obviously require knowing or estimating
\sigma^{2}_{U}
. Our proposed estimators below are, in fact, equivalent to estimating
\sigma^{2}_{U}
, identifying estimates in this way, then imposing corrections based on attenuation in the standard (observable, not latent, predictor) setting.
In the latent case, because both the true

and estimates of

2
Attenuation Bias in the Standard and Latent Predictor Cases

We consider a bivariate (for now) linear regression model predicting some dependent variable
Y
with the true latent predictor
X
or, alternatively, with
\tilde{X}
or with
\hat{X}
, under standard linear model assumptions, assuming that the error term in the linear regression is independent of the measurement error,
U
. We use the following notation:

•

\beta_{X}
, our central parameter of interest, is the true slope coefficient on the (true) latent variable
X
in the bivariate linear regression predicting
Y

•

\hat{\beta}_{X}
is the least squares estimator of
\beta_{X}
if we were to use the
true

X
(measured without error) as our predictor

•

\hat{\beta}_{\tilde{X}}
is the least squares estimator of
\beta_{X}
if we were to use the
un-transformed estimated
latent trait
\tilde{X}
as our predictor

•

\hat{\beta}_{\hat{X}}
is the least squares estimator of
\beta_{X}
if we were to use the
transformed (i.e., identified)
latent trait estimates
\hat{X}
as our predictor

We call
\hat{\beta}_{\hat{X}}
the
naive estimator
of the slope since it ignores measurement error in the latent predictor and instead treats point estimates
\hat{X}
as if they were the true values of the predictor. As mentioned above, in the latent predictor case we generally do not observe either the true latent variable
X
or the un-transformed estimates
\tilde{X}
, so our discussions of
\hat{\beta}_{X}
and
\hat{\beta}_{\tilde{X}}
will be for purposes of illustration and comparison to
\hat{\beta}_{\hat{X}}
and to the corrected estimators we propose below.

If we were able to use the true latent variable
X
as our predictor, then the standard linear regression assumptions would ensure that our estimator
\beta_{X}
of the slope coefficient is unbiased. Of course, the fact that the predictor is latent means that we can never observe it directly, and hence we can never measure it without error.

If, by contrast, we were to use the measured with error predictor
\tilde{X}
, which is on the same scale as the true latent variable
X
, as our predictor, then the slope coefficient in a bivariate regression would have the standard measurement error attenuation factor with

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{\tilde{X}}]=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{X}}{\sigma^{2}_{X}+\sigma^{2}_{U}}\;\beta_{X}=\frac{1}{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}\;\beta_{X}=\lambda\;\beta_{X}

(2)

since the identifying assumptions for
X
include its true values having variance 1. Here
\lambda\equiv\nicefrac{{1}}{{(1+\sigma^{2}_{U})}}
is called the attenuation factor. For example, if on the true latent scale, the measurement error variance were equal to the variance of the latent trait itself (i.e.,
\sigma_{U}^{2}=1
then the attenuation factor would be
\frac{1}{1+1}=\frac{1}{2}
and the estimated coefficient when using
\tilde{X}
as the predictor would be half of the size of the true coefficient in expectation.

The standard (observable rather than latent variable) measurement error setup involves observing
\tilde{X}
. But when
X
is a latent variable,
\tilde{X}
is typically not obtainable since, in choosing the scale on which we identify the estimates of our latent trait, we are imposing a transformation that changes the scale on which the variable is estimated. In other words, if we think of the true scale as being identified as having mean 0 and standard deviation 1, then we obtain estimates of the latent traits that are equal to the true latent traits plus some measurement error, then we standardize these noisy estimates to have mean 0 and standard deviation 1, we are shrinking the scale of the measured with error estimates so they have the same variance as the true
X
. Thus, when we use
\hat{X}
we are using a transformed predictor that has been multiplied by the reciprocal of the sample standard deviation of
\tilde{X}
(we’ll ignore subtracting off the sample mean which doesn’t change the estimated slope coefficient, but only changes the intercept which isn’t our focus here). Note that the sample variance of
\tilde{X}
is
\sigma^{2}_{\tilde{X}}=\sigma^{2}_{X}+\sigma^{2}_{U}=1+\sigma^{2}_{U}
so that
\hat{X}=\frac{\tilde{X}}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}}
and hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{\hat{X}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\beta_{\tilde{X}}]\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}\,=\,\frac{1}{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}\beta_{X}\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}}\beta_{X},

(3)

meaning that while measurement error in this latent predictor setting still results in attenuation (note that
0<\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}}<1
whenever
0<\sigma^{2}_{U}<\infty
) the degree of attenuation is different from the standard observable predictor setting.

Figure 2:
Attenuation factor as a function of measurement error variance for observable and latent predictors.

Figure
2
shows the attenuation factor for the bivariate regression slope coefficient estimate as a function of the measurement error variance
\sigma_{U}^{2}
for the cases of observable and latent predictors. In both cases, it is assumed that the true predictor has variance 1 (equivalently, one can think of the variance of the measurement error as being the ratio of the variance of the measurement error to the variance of the true predictor). When there is no measurement error, the attenuation factor is 1 in both cases. As measurement error increases, both attenuation factors drop below 1, meaning that the expected value of the slope coefficient estimate is smaller in magnitude than the true parameter value. But in the latent setting, the scale of the measured-with-error predictor is transformed by the identifying restriction to have variance 1 — the same variance as the true variable measured without error. This rescaling, which involves dividing the predictor by a positive number greater than 1, thus multiplies the estimated slope coefficient by the same number, making it larger in magnitude and thereby mitigating, but not eliminating, the attenuation bias.

It follows from the different attenuation factors for the latent predictor setup we consider here that applying standard corrections for attenuation based on estimates of measurement error will over-adjust the coefficient, resulting in estimates that are inflated, i.e., biased away from zero. In other words, if one corrects for an attenuation factor of
\frac{1}{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}
when the true attenuation factor is
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}}
then this would be an
over
-correction resulting in amplification bias in the estimated slope coefficient.

3
The Method of Composition: Impact on Bias

Previous work in political science and elsewhere has considered how to appropriately propagate the uncertainty in estimates of latent predictors into estimated slope coefficients in regression models. While some of this work has estimated the latent variable and regression models jointly, e.g., using full Bayesian MCMC or related methods, much of this work has employed an approach based on the method of composition (MOC)
(
Tanner 2012
, p. 52)
. In a prominent article that introduced the specific variant of MOC commonly used in political science and more broadly,
Treier & Jackman 2008
estimate countries’ levels of democracy, a latent trait, using Bayesian MCMC; then, for each iteration of the sampler for the latent traits, they estimate a regression predicting the risk of civil war with level of democracy and other factors, where level of democracy is set to the iteration’s sampled value of the latent traits. Many high-impact works have used MOC with the goal of appropriately accounting for the uncertainty in latent predictors
(see e.g.
Bernhard et al. 2020
;
Caughey & Warshaw 2018
;
Caughey & Warshaw 2019
;
Jessee 2010
;
Kastellec et al. 2015
;
Lax et al. 2019
)
. The Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem) project even provides, in addition to point estimates of their democracy measures, a series of posterior samples so that researchers can employ MOC in subsequent models
(
Pemstein et al. 2018
)
.
5
5

5

Note, however, that V-Dem’s use of MOC to combine individual measures into more general indices and account for the resulting uncertainty
(
McMann et al. 2016
)
does not necessarily suffer from the issues we identify in using MOC to fit second stage regression models with latent predictors.
In a recent
APSR Letter
,
Tai et al. 2024
criticize previous work on the relationship between public support for democracy and regime survival, arguing that MOC should be used to incorporate the uncertainty in estimates of the latent predictors.

Although it shares a name with a general method of sampling from a distribution, MOC for latent predictors, as advocated by
Treier & Jackman 2008
and as commonly used in the subsequent latent traits literature, uses slightly different steps. The general MOC, used to sample from the density for some random variable
y
, first samples
x^{\prime}
from the density for some other random variable
g(x)
, then samples
y
from
f(y|x^{\prime})
. As applied to the latent predictor situation with a matrix of indicators
W
from which we can estimate the latent trait
X
according to some statistical model, MOC proceeds according to the following steps for a set of draws
t=1,\ldots,T
:

1.

Sample
X^{(t)}
from
p(X|W)
according to chosen measurement model

2.

Fit a regression predicting
Y
with
X^{(t)}
, noting the coefficient point estimate
\hat{\beta}^{(t)}
and its accompanying variance-covariance matrix
\hat{V}^{(t)}

3.

sample
\beta^{(t)}
from multivariate normal distribution with mean vector
\hat{\beta}^{(t)}
and variance-covariance matrix
\hat{V}^{(t)}

Proponents of MOC have argued that the samples
\beta^{(t)}
, typically treated as if they were draws from
p(\beta|W,Y)
, provide a set of draws from a distribution for the slope coefficient that appropriately accounts for the uncertainty in the latent predictor
X
.

MOC is typically motivated by the concern that simply using point estimates of the latent trait as a predictor may result in overly precise findings and that the uncertainty in the latent trait estimates should be taken into account in the estimation, with their uncertainty allowed to “propagate” into the regression level of the model. But as discussed above, the primary concern when estimating regression models using a predictor measured with error is attenuation bias, which results in slope coefficient estimates being pulled toward zero on average for the predictor measured with error. Furthermore, in the simple linear regression case, although standard errors can be biased either upwards or downwards, this is counteracted by the coefficient attenuation, with the overall result that
t
-statistics are biased toward zero.
6
6

6

See
Pischke 2007
, p. 3 for a derivation of this
t
-statistic bias.
This means that overconfidence is not a problem that needs to be remedied in this situation.

MOC and related approaches average over a set of estimates, each using separate draws from the posterior distribution for the latent variable given the indicator data. Each of these individual draws will typically have
even more
random variation than do the point estimates of the latent traits. To see this, note that the point estimates are themselves equal to the true latent traits plus some measurement error, and the individual draws from the posterior distribution for
X
given
W
can be thought of as being randomly distributed around the point estimates. i.e.,
X^{(t)}=\hat{X}+\xi
where
\hat{X}
is the point estimate (here likely the posterior mean for
X
given
W
) and
\xi
is an additional source of mean-zero randomness relative to just using the point estimates for
X
. MOC thus averages over a series of even further attenuated estimates as compared to using the point estimates of the latent traits themselves. The attenuation factor with MOC is now based on a measurement error variance that is the sum of the measurement error variance from the traditional case in Equation
3
and the variance of the conditional posterior for the latent traits
X
. In other words, applying MOC is analogous to increasing the measurement error from
\sigma^{2}_{u}
to
\sigma^{2}_{u}+\sigma^{2}_{\xi}
for each draw of the sampler, then averaging over these more biased values.

Seen in this light, it is unsurprising that many applications of MOC have estimated slope coefficients on latent predictors to be even closer to zero than those from applications using ordinary regression based on the point estimates of the latent trait. In other words, MOC generally makes attenuation bias worse (often much worse) than it would be if one had simply ignored measurement error altogether.

Although the idea of fully accounting for the uncertainty in latent predictors may sound appealing, MOC’s main impact is to exacerbate the key problem in these situations, which is slope estimates that are biased toward zero. Note that although MOC, as commonly used in the latent predictor setting, seems similar to a full joint Bayesian modeling strategy, it first samples a value
X^{(t)}
from
p(X|W)
and then samples from
p(\beta,\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}|Y,X^{(t)})
. Importantly,
Y
is not conditioned on in the sampling of
X^{(t)}
as it would be in a full joint setup.
7
7

7

Treier & Jackman 2008
note this and argue that MOC relies on the assumption that
p(X|Z,Y)=p(X|Z)
, i.e., that
X
is independent of
Y
conditional on
Z
, which they note might not be plausible.
Because of this, and more importantly, because this version of MOC tends to exacerbate, rather than ameliorate, the main problem of attenuation, we should seek other solutions for the challenges that arise when employing latent regression predictors.

4
Correcting Regression Coefficients with Latent Predictors

One approach to dealing with the specific type of attenuation that arises in the latent predictor case is to fit a standard linear regression using the latent trait estimates (
\hat{X}
) as the predictor and then correct the slope estimate based on the attenuation factor from Equation
3
above. To do this, we need a way to estimate
\sigma^{2}_{U}
—the measurement error variance. Importantly, this is measurement error variance on the scale of the true latent trait
X
, not the transformed (standardized) scale for the estimates of the latent trait that we can observe.

One way to obtain information about
\sigma^{2}_{U}
is by having multiple measures of the latent trait
X
for one or more observations. If these measures tend to give very similar values for each observation, then
\sigma^{2}_{U}
is likely to be small, whereas if there are large differences between the measures,
\sigma^{2}_{U}
is likely to be larger. In the case of estimating latent traits based on multiple indicators, we can typically obtain multiple measures by partitioning the indicators into multiple sets. For example, imagine we had 10 indicators for a latent trait
X
. We could use 5 of the indicators to obtain estimates
\hat{X}_{1}
and use the other 5 to obtain estimates
\hat{X}_{2}
. If we split the indicators evenly, it may be reasonable to assume that the variance of the measurement errors for each of these two sets of estimates is the same.
8
8

8

In the case of an odd number of indicators, we can consider all partitions in which the number of items in the two sets differs by one.

Following the notation above, we will denote two (unobserved) estimates of the latent trait on the same scale as the true latent trait itself as
\tilde{X}_{1}=X+U_{1}
and
\tilde{X}_{2}=X+U_{2}
where we assume that measurement errors
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
each have mean zero and the same variance
\sigma^{2}_{U}
and are independent of each other and of
X
. We also define
\hat{X}_{1}
and
\hat{X}_{2}
as the corresponding standardized estimates, which we can observe if we assume they have a mean of 0 and a variance of 1. To estimate the attenuation factor, we can first recognize that even without knowing
\tilde{X}_{1}
or
\tilde{X}_{2}
, the correlation between
\tilde{X}_{1}
and
\tilde{X}_{1}
must be the same as the correlation between the observable estimates
\hat{X}_{1}
and
\hat{X}_{2}
since these are simply affine transformations of each other. We can then show that

\displaystyle\text{Cor}(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2})

\displaystyle=\text{Cor}(\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2})=\frac{\text{Cov}(\tilde{X}_{1},\tilde{X}_{2})}{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{\tilde{X}_{1}}\sigma^{2}_{\tilde{X}_{2}}}}

(4)

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{Cov}(X+U_{1},X+U_{2})}{\sqrt{(\sigma^{2}_{X}+\sigma^{2}_{U})^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{Cov}(X,X)+\text{Cov}(X,U_{2})+\text{Cov}(U_{1},X)+\text{Cov}(U_{1},U_{2})}{\sigma^{2}_{X}+\sigma^{2}_{U}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{X}+0+0+0}{\sigma^{2}_{X}+\sigma^{2}_{U}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{X}}{\sigma^{2}_{X}+\sigma^{2}_{U}}=\lambda

where, as in Equation
2
above,
\lambda
is the attenuation factor in the standard (non-latent) bivariate linear regression case where the predictor is measured with classical error and, with a slight abuse of notation,
\sigma^{2}_{U}\equiv\textrm{Var}{(U_{1})}=\textrm{Var}{(U_{2})}
. In our setup,
\sigma^{2}_{X}=1
by identification so that
\lambda=\nicefrac{{1}}{{(1+\sigma^{2}_{U})}}
.

This, along with Equation
3
above, suggests using the square root of the sample correlation between the two replicate measures of
X
in order to correct for attenuation in the estimated slope coefficient,
\beta_{X}
.

9

9

In some latent variable estimation situations, our split-half approach to estimating measurement error variance may not be appropriate. For example, if indicators are designed to tap different aspects of a latent variable rather than with internal consistency in mind—perhaps even being chosen to have lower correlations with each other (e.g.,
Gosling et al. 2003

Noting that in our latent variable predictor setup the attenuation factor for the slope coefficient is
\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}}
rather than the
\frac{1}{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}
from the standard observable variable case, we could estimate the standard linear regression coefficient using
\hat{X}_{1}
, as our predictor to obtain
\hat{\beta}_{\hat{X}}
and then correct this estimate to obtain

\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\text{OLS},\hat{X}_{1}}=\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS},\hat{X}_{1}}}{\sqrt{\text{Cor}(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2})}}

(5)

or, using
\hat{X}_{2}
as our predictor,

\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\text{OLS},\hat{X}_{2}}=\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS},\hat{X}_{2}}}{\sqrt{\text{Cor}(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2})}}

(6)

since we have no reason to privilege either of the two sets of estimates.

10

10

Note that we cannot use
\hat{X}
—the point estimates of the latent trait using all of the indicators—as our predictor here since we have an estimate of the correction factor based on the amount of measurement error in
\hat{X}_{1}
and
\hat{X}_{2}
, not in

which is estimated based on all of the available indicators rather than one partition of them.
Here and below, we use the asterisk superscript to denote corrected estimators.

We can also average over these two estimators
\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\text{OLS},\hat{X}_{1}}
and
\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\text{OLS},\hat{X}_{2}}
which has the benefit of reducing variability from the choice of which partition to use.
11
11

11

Our averaging approach, and in particular the equivalent averaged instrumental variables (IV) approach we describe in the next section, has something in common with that the strategy proposed in
Gillen et al. 2019
.
Additionally, this averaging strategy has the benefit of mitigating concerns about violations of the assumption that
\sigma^{2}_{U_{1}}=\sigma^{2}_{U_{2}}
, since if such a violation results in our correction factor being too small then flipping the sets indicators would produce a correction factor that is too large and vice-versa. As we show in Appendix I, the bias induced when
\sigma^{2}_{U_{1}}\neq\sigma^{2}_{U_{2}}
in practice seems likely to be quite small, if not negligible, over plausible ranges of
\sigma^{2}_{U_{1}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{U_{2}}
when the estimator is averaged across these two values.

Finally, because there are typically multiple ways to partition the indicators into two equally sized sets, we can define our corrected estimator
\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\text{OLS}}
as the median over all such partitions, where we first average the estimators within each partition between
\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\text{OLS},\hat{X}_{1}}
and
\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\text{OLS},\hat{X}_{2}}
. This helps guard against the influence of one or more outlying estimates for certain partitions of the indicators, producing more stable estimates.

An advantage of this corrected OLS approach with a correlation-based adjustment is its relative simplicity and ease of implementation, requiring only the ability to split the indicators used to estimate the latent variable into two sets, estimate the latent trait separately using each of the sets of indicators, and calculate the correlation between the two estimates. This means that our approach can be used with virtually any method of estimating a latent trait, from simple additive or averaging approaches that do not specify a formal model to parametric models or advanced ML or AI approaches. In the following section, we introduce a more flexible corrected estimator for latent predictors.

Instrumental Variables Correction for
Measurement

Error with Latent Predictors

One common method for correcting for measurement error in a predictor is instrumental variables (IV). Although its use today is often motivated by causal identification, IV can also, under certain assumptions, produce consistent estimates of slope coefficients when a predictor is measured with error.
12
12

12

Although we focus on measurement error here, note that in a causal framework, IV can be used to address measurement error as well as simultaneity and omitted variable bias under suitable assumptions (see
Pancost & Schaller 2022
).
In the standard observable predictor setting, if
X
is measured with error following the setup above and we seek to estimate the slope coefficient on
X
in a linear regression predicting some other variable
Y
, we can correct for the resulting attenuation bias by leveraging an instrument
Z
with the following properties: (i)
\textrm{Cov}(X,Z)\neq 0
; (ii)
\textrm{Cov}(Z,U)=0
; (iii)
\textrm{Cov}(Z,\varepsilon)=0
. In other words, a valid instrument in this setting must be correlated with the true predictor
X
and be uncorrelated with both the measurement error in
X
and the error term in the regression predicting
Y
with
X
.

Following
Angrist & Pischke 2009
, the two-stage least squares (2SLS) IV estimator of the slope if we were able to observe
\tilde{X}
could be written as

\hat{\beta}_{\textrm{IV},\tilde{X}}=\frac{\hat{\beta}_{Y\sim Z}}{\hat{\beta}_{\tilde{X}\sim Z}}=\frac{\nicefrac{{\textrm{Cov}(Y,Z)}}{{\textrm{Var}(Z)}}}{\nicefrac{{\textrm{Cov}(\tilde{X},Z)}}{{\textrm{Var}(Z)}}}=\frac{\textrm{Cov}(X\beta+\varepsilon,Z)}{\textrm{Cov}(X+U,Z)}=\frac{\beta\textrm{Cov}(X,Z)}{\textrm{Cov}(X,Z)}

(7)

Since by assumption
\textrm{Cov}(Z,\varepsilon)=0
and
\textrm{Cov}(Z,U)=0
and hence it follows that in this observable predictor setup

\operatornamewithlimits{plim}\hat{\beta}_{\textrm{IV},\tilde{X}}=\beta_{X}

(8)

In the latent predictor case, we can obtain a suitable instrumental variable by splitting the indicators used to estimate the latent variable into two sets and estimating the latent trait separately using each of these two sets of indicators, calling these estimates
\hat{X}_{1}
and
\hat{X}_{2}
as above. We can then use
\hat{X}_{1}
as an instrument for
\hat{X}_{2}
or vice-versa. Because these are both measures of the same latent trait, they should be highly correlated with each other (which we can also check empirically) and with the true latent trait
X
.
13
13

13

In the latent predictor case, different indicators indicators of the same latent trait, and especially scales constructed based on multiple indicators, are typically highly correlated with each other
(see e.g.
Ansolabehere et al. 2008
)
, suggesting that the weak instruments problem should rarely be severe in practice.
We may also assume that the instrument is independent of the measurement error, i.e., that
\hat{X}_{2}\perp\!\!\!\perp U_{1}\equiv(X-\hat{X}_{1})
.

In contrast to the IV setup with an observable predictor, our estimates
\hat{X}_{1}
and
\hat{X}_{2}
are not on the same scale as
X
itself. If we had
\tilde{X}_{1}=X+U_{1}
to use as our predictor, then the IV results in Equations
7
and
8
would hold. In our case, however, we use as our predictor
\hat{X}_{1}=\frac{\tilde{X}_{1}}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}}
, i.e. a rescaled version of
\tilde{X}
since it is standardized by dividing by
\sigma_{\tilde{X}}=\sqrt{1+\sigma_{U}^{2}}
.
14
14

14

Note that this is a slight abuse of notation since here
U
is the measurement error in the latent trait estimates based on the first half of the indicators. In other words, what is being referred to as
\tilde{X}
(and also
\hat{X}
) has changed to now refer to the latent trait estimates based on half of the indicators.
This implies that our IV estimate using
\hat{X}
will have the following relation with
\hat{\beta}_{\textrm{IV},\tilde{X}}

\hat{\beta}_{\textrm{IV},\hat{X}_{1}\mid\hat{X}_{2}}=\hat{\beta}_{\textrm{IV},\tilde{X}_{1}\mid\tilde{X}_{2}}\sqrt{1+\sigma_{U}^{2}}

(9)

and hence

\operatornamewithlimits{plim}\hat{\beta}_{\textrm{IV},\hat{X}_{1}\mid\hat{X}_{2}}=\beta\sqrt{1+\sigma_{U}^{2}},

(10)

so that using IV to correct for measurement error in this latent predictor setting actually
over
-corrects for the attenuation bias in standard linear regression, resulting in coefficient estimates that are inflated in magnitude on average since
\sqrt{1+\sigma_{U}^{2}}>1
whenever
\sigma_{U}^{2}>0
, i.e., whenever there is any measurement error in the predictor. Note that here, we are using the notation
A\mid B
to indicate that
A
is our predictor and
B
is our instrumental variable.

This implies a further correction that can be accomplished by multiplying the IV estimate by the reciprocal of this factor, so that

\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\textrm{IV},\hat{X}_{1}\mid\hat{X}_{2}}=\frac{\hat{\beta}_{\textrm{IV},\hat{X}_{1}\mid\hat{X}_{2}}}{\sqrt{1+\sigma_{U}^{2}}}=\hat{\beta}_{\textrm{IV},\hat{X}_{1}\mid\hat{X}_{2}}\sqrt{\lambda}.

(11)

With this correction in place,

\operatornamewithlimits{plim}\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\textrm{IV},\hat{X}_{1}\mid\hat{X}_{2}}=\beta

(12)

implying that by adjusting the split-half IV estimate to account for the identifying restriction used for the estimated latent traits, which itself is based on the measurement error variance
\sigma^{2}_{U}
, we can obtain a consistent estimate of the linear regression slope coefficient on the true latent
X
predicting
Y
.

Of course, the correction used in
\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\textrm{IV},\hat{X}_{1}\mid\hat{X}_{2}}
is based on the variance of the measurement error (on the true latent variable’s scale)
\sigma_{U}^{2}
which we do not typically know. As discussed in the previous section, however, we use these replicate measures,
\hat{X}_{1}
and
\hat{X}_{2}
, to learn about
\sigma^{2}_{U}
and, consequently, about
\lambda
. Since the correlation between these replicate measurements estimates
\lambda
, we can calculate our corrected estimator as the product of the IV slope estimate and the square root of the correlation:

\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\textrm{IV},\hat{X}_{1}\mid\hat{X}_{2}}=\hat{\beta}_{\textrm{IV},\hat{X}_{1}\mid\hat{X}_{2}}\;\sqrt{\text{Cor}(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2})}.

(13)

Another way of understanding the corrected IV estimator is to note that if we transformed our measures
\hat{X}_{1}
and
\hat{X}_{2}
from having variance 1 to having variance
1+\sigma^{2}_{U}
, which would effectively mean we are in the standard measurement error setup, then the standard IV estimator would provide consistent estimates.
15
15

15

We actually would not need to apply this transformation to whichever one is used as the instrument, since any affine transformation of the instrument will cancel out in the expression for the IV estimator.
A convenient way to do this is to estimate
\sigma^{2}_{U}
with
[\nicefrac{{1}}{{\text{Cor}(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2})}}-1]
, then note that since we want the variance of our predictor to be
1+\sigma^{2}_{U}
we can multiply it by
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\text{Cor}(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2})}}}
and use this transformed predictor in a standard IV analysis.
16
16

16

Since the ratio of the uncorrected OLS and uncorrected IV estimators provides information about
\lambda
(see
Pancost & Schaller 2022
), yet another equivalent estimator can be constructed based on the square root of the product of the baseline OLS and uncorrected IV estimators using the partitioned
\hat{X}_{1}
and
\hat{X}_{2}
. Since this approach is less intuitive, we do not discuss it here.

Obviously, multiplying the predictor by
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\text{Cor}(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2})}}}
has the result of dividing the estimated slope coefficient by the same factor, which is equivalent to multiplying it by
\sqrt{\text{Cor}(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2})}
as in Equation
13
above.

As above, we may want to calculate our corrected IV estimator over all equally sized partitions of the indicators for estimating
\hat{X}_{1}
and
\hat{X}_{2}
. For each such partition take the average of the corrected estimator using
\hat{X}_{1}
to instrument for
\hat{X}_{2}
and the one using
\hat{X}_{2}
to instrument for
\hat{X}_{1}
, then choose as our estimator the median of all such partition averages. In fact, this estimator, which we call
\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\text{IV}}
, is equivalent to
\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}^{*}
(see Appendix II) In other words, the correlation-adjusted OLS estimator and the correlation-adjusted IV estimator are equivalent and below we refer to them together as the corrected estimator.

Despite the equivalence shown between
\hat{\beta}_{\text{OLS}}^{*}
and
\hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}}^{*}
in the bivariate case, one advantage of the IV approach is the ease with which it can be extended to multivariate regression. If one predictor is measured with error, we may either have other ‘‘control’’ variables or the predictor measured with error may itself be a control variable in which case adjusting for this may be important to prevent bias in the other coefficient(s) of interest.
17
17

17

Note in the latter case we may not care about appropriately adjusting the IV coefficient on the predictor that is measured with error.
We may even have multiple predictors measured with error, and believe that we have instruments that are suitable to address this. In both the OLS and IV cases, we employ the bootstrap to assess uncertainty, as this row-level resampling allows us to incorporate uncertainty not only in the coefficient estimate but also in the correction factor itself.

Illustrative Application: Political Knowledge Predicting Duty to Vote

We begin with an example using data from the American National Election Study’s 2024 Time Series Study in order to illustrate our methods, including how our corrected estimator compares to the naive estimator and other approaches. Our latent predictor is political knowledge, which is estimated based on a four-question battery including questions on federal spending, the length of Senate terms, and party control of the House and Senate.
18
18

18

The full text of these items is listed in Appendix IV.
. Many latent traits, such as political knowledge, are often estimated with a small number of indicators in practice. Here, we take the simple approach of estimating the political knowledge level of each respondent with the number of items answered correctly, which we then standardize to have mean 0 and variance 1 within the sample.
19
19

19

To simplify the analysis, we drop all observations with missing values on any of the indicators or the dependent variable, leaving 3,059 of the original 3,349 observations.
This standardization makes comparison easier when looking at estimates based on different numbers of indicators as well as different estimation methods, and it also matches the identification restriction used in our derivations above. In applications like this, researchers likely care less about the proportion of correct answers on the specific questions used in the survey and care more about the underlying levels of relative political knowledge held by each respondent, which the question responses provide information about. Given this, identifying the measurement scale through standardization seems a natural approach.

This application demonstrates how our corrected estimators can be applied to any method of estimating a latent trait, including sophisticated models (e.g., item response, factor analytic, etc.) or simpler additive or averaging strategies. Our dependent variable is the response to a 7-point scale question about whether each respondent views voting as a duty or a choice, where we orient the scale so that “Very strongly a duty” is 7 and “Very strongly a choice” is 1. We make no claims here about disentangling the causal relationships between these variables, but only intend this example to illustrate the results of different estimation approaches in commonly encountered situations where latent predictors are estimated based on a small number of indicators.

Naive Estimation

We start by fitting a standard linear regression predicting vote duty with estimated knowledge. Here, we are using the point estimates of this latent trait as our predictor without making any corrections to our estimates. In addition to using all four indicators for this estimation, we also separately estimate political knowledge using each possible combination of the four indicators to explore the dynamics of basing our estimates of the latent trait on different numbers of indicators. There are
2^{4}-1=15
such possibilities since we ignore the case where none of the four questions is included.

Each grey point in Figure
3
plots the point estimate of the slope coefficient from a standard linear regression predicting our dependent variable with estimated knowledge, where knowledge is estimated using a given set of between 1 and 4 of the indicators. The black dots show the average of these OLS slope estimates when using a given number of indicators. We observe that as more items are used to estimate political knowledge, the estimated slope coefficient on political knowledge tends to be higher, which is unsurprising given that a measure using more indicators tends to have less measurement error and, consequently, produce less attenuation than one based on fewer indicators.

The estimate obtained by using all four indicators can be thought of as the naive OLS estimate, since if one were to ignore measurement error, one would presumably use all available indicators to estimate the latent trait, then regress the dependent variable on the point estimates for the latent trait without any further adjustment. This strategy estimates the slope to be 0.42, as indicated in the figure by the horizontal dashed line.

Corrected Estimator

We can also create two separate measures of knowledge by partitioning the four indicators into two sets, each including two of the knowledge items. For example, we could define
\hat{X}_{1}
as the standardized number of correct answers that a respondent obtains on the first two knowledge questions and define
\hat{X}_{2}
as the standardized number of correct answers that a respondent obtains on the third and fourth knowledge questions. The sample correlation between these two estimates in this case would be 0.25, suggesting a relatively large amount of measurement error. We could then use
\hat{X}_{1}
as an instrument for
\hat{X}_{2}
(or vice-versa), correcting the resulting slope estimate by multiplying it by
\sqrt{\text{Cor}(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2})}
as in Equation
9
.

The horizontal dotted line in Figure
3
shows the value of our corrected estimator
\hat{\beta}_{\text{IV}}^{*}
, which takes the median of the within-partition averages of the corrected IV estimators over all possible partitions of the four items into two sets of two items. As shown above, this estimator is equivalent to the corrected OLS estimator
\hat{\beta}^{*}_{\text{OLS}}
. This corrected estimate is 0.62, which is nearly 50% larger than the baseline (uncorrected) OLS estimate, showing how accounting for measurement error can yield substantively meaningful changes in our estimates. Note also that weak instruments do not appear to be a problem, given the high correlation between the two sets of latent trait estimates. The
F
-statistic from the first stage averaged well over 200 across all possible partitions, with the smallest
F

Had we used the standard (uncorrected) IV estimator (not shown in the figure) we would have estimated the slope as 1.27 on average across these partitions of the indicators, which is more than double the value of corrected estimate.

20

This illustrates the high degree of amplification bias—estimating the slope as being
larger

Item Response Modeling and Method of Composition

We could also have used an item response model to estimate political knowledge in this application instead of the simple additive strategy. This would entail obtaining point estimates (e.g., maximum likelihood parameter values or posterior means) of the political knowledge levels for each respondent, then using those as predictors in either baseline OLS regressions or IV regressions, as above, to which we could apply appropriate corrections.

Using a common item response model to estimate our latent traits here produces baseline OLS estimate of the slope of 0.45 (quite similar to the 0.42 using the standardized proportion correct over the set of 4 items).
21
21

21

Here we use the ideal function in the
pscl
R package to estimate latent traits.
Using our corrected estimator with the item response model estimates, we estimate the slope as 0.67, which is slightly higher than the corrected estimate using an additive measure rather than the item response model. Estimating an item response model rather than using simple aggregation methods, such as the total number correct, may be expected to provide a more precise estimate of the latent trait, which could account for the slightly larger estimates obtained for both the standard OLS and corrected IV approaches.

When using the latent traits model, we can also apply MOC, which is the primary solution proposed in the political science latent traits modeling literature for accounting for the measurement error inherent in latent predictors. Note that MOC requires some sort of model for estimating the latent trait since we need to be able to obtain draws from the posterior distribution for the latent traits or something similar (e.g., some likelihood-based distribution), so it cannot be applied to simple latent trait estimation strategies like adding or averaging that do not have an explicit model.

But as discussed above, MOC averages over a set of draws from the posterior distribution for the latent traits given the indicators, with each of these draws being an even noisier measure of the true latent trait than the baseline strategy of using the point estimates (e.g., posterior means or maximum likelihood point estimates) for the latent traits as our predictor. In our political knowledge example, MOC yields a point estimate of 0.20, as shown by the solid horizontal line in Figure
3
. This is less than half the size of the unadjusted OLS approach (which is itself known to be attenuated) and is less than one-third the size of our corrected estimate. This is consistent with most previous applications of MOC in the literature, which tend to produce coefficient estimates that are smaller in magnitude than the (presumably already attenuated) naive estimates treating latent trait estimates as known and illustrates how, rather than helping, MOC actually exacerbates the central problem of attenuation bias that arises when using latent predictors in regression models.

This example also suggests that the specific method used to estimate a latent trait (e.g., simple aggregation or averaging versus item response models) may often matter much less for the resulting slope coefficient estimates than does the method of adjusting (or not adjusting) for the inherent measurement error in the latent trait estimates. While there are only minor differences when switching from the simpler aggregation approach to using an item response model here, estimates differed by nearly 50% when moving from naive OLS to our corrected estimator, and the differences were even more dramatic for MOC and unadjusted IV.

Simulation: The Role of Sample Size & Dimensionality

In this section, we examine how estimates of slope coefficients on latent predictors change under various estimation strategies as we vary the number of indicators and the number of observations in a dataset. The simulation results presented here provide insight into how our method compares to simply ignoring measurement error, using an uncorrected IV approach, and using MOC under various situations. We also assess how these approaches perform when estimating the latent trait based on larger or smaller numbers of indicators, illustrating when our corrected estimators are likely to result in the largest changes.

We assume that a researcher seeks to estimate a latent trait based on a set of binary indicators using an item response model–a widely used measurement strategy in political science and more broadly. Formally, we assume that a set of

8
Additional Applications

Up to this point, we have provided some theoretical background, an illustrative example of our proposed method, and generated further evidence through simulation. In the political knowledge application, our proposed correction resulted in very large changes to the estimated slope, suggesting that naive linear regression ignoring measurement error produced a high degree of attenuation bias. In the ideology example that motivated our simulations in Section
7
, the corrected estimator produced much smaller changes when the latent trait was estimated using all 30 indicators, but when using fewer indicators to estimate the latent trait, as is more common in the literature, our proposed correction produced substantively large changes.

But how significant are these measurement error dynamics in practice when attempting to answer different substantive social science research questions? Here we turn to two more substantive applications—one drawn from comparative politics, another from the realm of text analysis—illustrating how our method can be used to assess whether measurement error in a given application appears to bias estimates to a meaningful degree. Both of these examples also use measurement models for the latent traits that do not have full underlying statistical models. This means that while our corrected estimation approach can be used, other methods, such as MO or full joint estimation, would not work with these measurement strategies.

9
Discussion

The derivations and analyses above illustrate the unique challenges encountered when estimating regression models involving a latent variable as a predictor. We demonstrate that using standard regression models with a latent predictor can yield highly attenuated estimates, whereas MOC, which is likely the most prominent method for correction in political science, typically exacerbates these biases. Standard IV corrections can result in estimates that are even more biased in the opposite direction. We introduce a split-sample IV setup that can provide consistent estimates of the slope coefficient of interest, provided that the typically overlooked consequences of rescaling the estimated latent traits to impose identification restrictions are recognized and accounted for. Our method reduces bias, often dramatically, and performs similarly to more cumbersome approaches such as full joint estimation of the latent traits and regression models. In contrast to other approaches, our method can also be used with any estimation strategy for the latent trait, including those without explicit statistical models.

Applied researchers using latent predictors should check standard estimates against our corrected estimates. In some cases, we may expect attenuation bias to be negligible. For example, estimates of legislator ideology based on roll call voting, which often use hundreds of indicators, tend to be estimated extremely precisely, at least conditional on the assumed measurement models
(
Lewis & Poole 2004
)
. NOMINATE legislator ideology scores estimated separately with the first and second sessions of the 117th U.S. Senate are correlated at 0.99, implying that naive OLS regressions using NOMINATE scores would be attenuated by less than one percent. Similarly, the example from Section
8.1
above of democracy predicting economic growth shows less than a 1% change in the estimated coefficient after using our corrected estimator.

The estimated coefficients on other latent predictors may show non-trivial, albeit not huge, changes when applying our corrected methods. For example,
Ansolabehere et al. 2008
finds that correlations between various issue scales when estimated from the two halves of ANES items range from 0.61 to 0.84. This implies that naive OLS slope estimates in these situations may need to be adjusted upwards in magnitude by between 9 and 28 percent. As shown in Section
8.2
above, correcting estimates of the relationship between Congressional speech sentiment and subsequent incumbent vote share produces smaller, but still substantively meaningful changes of around 14% in magnitude.

But in many applications for latent predictors, attenuation bias may be much more severe. It is quite common for latent traits to be estimated based on a very small number of indicators. The political knowledge example presented above demonstrates that correcting naive regression estimates using our proposed method yields a roughly 50% increase in the estimated slope coefficient. Furthermore, our simulations in Section
7
demonstrate that when using 10-20 policy questions to estimate respondents’ ideology, our corrected estimators can result in more than 10-25% increases in the magnitude of estimates compared to naive estimation, and when using 6 indicators, correcting estimates can increase the magnitude of estimates by over one third.

There are several avenues for building on our results. For example, constructing optimal partitions of indicators for our corrected IV approach could minimize the variability of estimates. We also plan to explore whether the latent variable case, with its multiple indicators that can be used to construct various sets of IVs, may allow for useful over-identification tests, perhaps of the assumptions necessary for the consistent estimation of the slope coefficient of interest. Additionally, under specific latent traits estimation strategies (e.g., Rasch-type models), it may be possible to derive results based on the standard errors of latent trait estimates so that corrections can be applied similarly to our corrected approach above, but using all items together to estimate the coefficient rather than partitioning them into two sets.

Finally, we hope to expand our focus on bivariate linear models to encompass other regression frameworks (e.g., GLMs) as well as multivariate models in which the latent predictor may be the central variable of interest or may be of interest as a control variable.

Appendix I: Evaluating the Sensitivity of the Corrected Estimator to Violations of
\sigma^{2}_{U_{1}}=\sigma^{2}_{U_{2}}

In our main derivations in the paper, particularly in showing how the correlation between the two sets of latent traits estimates can be used to estimate the measurement error variance, we assumed that the measurement error variance was the same for both of our replicate measures
\hat{X}_{1}
and
\hat{X}_{2}
which were obtained by partitioning our indicators into two equally sized sets and estimating the latent trait separately based on each of the two sets. Although this may be roughly true given that each set of estimates is based on the same number of indicators (or nearly the same number in the case of an odd number of indicators), it is unlikely that these variances will be exactly the same in practice.

Note, for example, that different indicators may contain different amounts of information about the latent trait. To use an example from educational testing, a question that every single student answers incorrectly does not give us
any
information about the relative intelligence of the test takers. For example, even if half of test takers answer a question correctly, their responses may not correlate with overall performance—correct answers might reflect regional familiarity with question content or other non-intelligence factors. And, some highly informative indicators may discriminate very strongly between those with low and high levels of the latent trait.

To the extent that the values of
\sigma^{2}_{U_{1}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{U_{2}}
differ, our estimate of
\lambda
based on
\text{Cor}(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2})
and hence the implied correction factor applied to the standard OLS or IV estimators will be incorrect which will cause our corrected estimators to be biased. But how large is this bias likely to be in practice if such assumption violations arise? In other words, how different must
\sigma^{2}_{U_{1}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{U_{2}}
be to meaningfully affect the ultimate estimates of
\beta_{X}
that our corrected estimators produce? …and how much are these possible biases mitigated by our strategy of averaging over slope estimates from each of the different partitions of indicators into two sets? In this section, we explore these questions by deriving an expression for the degree to which estimates are affected by given values of the two variances and discussing the implications for our estimators in practice.

Recall that in the main text (Equation
3

\hat{X}
, then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{\hat{X}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\beta_{\tilde{X}}]\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}\,=\,\frac{1}{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}\beta_{X}\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}}\beta_{X}.

\text{Cor}(\hat{X}_{1},\hat{X}_{2})=\frac{1}{1+\sigma^{2}_{U}}

But for the correlation between the two latent traits estimates
\hat{X}_{1}

\hat{X}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{Cov}(X+U_{1},X+U_{2})}{\sqrt{(\sigma^{2}_{X}+\sigma^{2}_{U_{1}})(\sigma^{2}_{X}+\sigma^{2}_{U_{2}})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{Cov}(X,X)+\text{Cov}(X,U_{2})+\text{Cov}(U_{1},X)+\text{Cov}(U_{1},U_{2})}{\sqrt{(\sigma^{2}_{X}+\sigma^{2}_{U_{1}})(\sigma^{2}_{X}+\sigma^{2}_{U_{2}})}}

but instead observe the set of 30 survey questions (indicators) from which we estimate
X
. In other words, we use
estimated
ideal points
\hat{X}
as our predictor rather than the true ideal points without any correction or adjustment. As expected, the coefficient estimates are generally smaller in magnitude, with the average estimate falling just below 0.38. The RMSE is more than double that of the known
X
case.

The third pane of Figure
A.III.1
shows estimates from the standard split-sample IV approach without any corrections for the latent variable case. In these simulations, after simulating the matrix of indicators, we split the 30 indicators into the first 15 and the second 15, estimating ideal points for all respondents separately using each of these two sets. This produces
\hat{X_{1}}
and
\hat{X_{2}}
, two separate measures. We then use two-stage least squares, using
X_{1}
as an instrument for
X_{2}
in the model predicting presidential vote. In the standard case of measurement error in an observable (as opposed to latent) predictor, this IV setup can yield consistent estimates of the slope coefficient. But as shown above, when applied to the latent case, it will
over
-correct for the attenuation bias, producing amplification bias. This is illustrated in the figure, where the average estimate is 0.43; the RMSE is nearly 4 times as large as in the known
X
case (nearly double the value with uncorrected OLS and estimated
X
).

Finally, the bottom pane of Figure
A.III.1
shows split-sample IV estimates corrected using the correlation between
\hat{X}_{1}
and
\hat{X}_{2}
as described in Equation
13
. These estimates are quite similar to those using the true
X
, but with a very slightly higher bias and RMSE. This suggests the feasibility of estimating
\beta_{X}
with the corrected IV approach in the more realistic case where we cannot use the true
X
to estimate the measurement error variance of the split-half estimates.

In the last two applications (both corrected IV estimates), this simulation suggests favorable bias dynamics. (Appendix I discusses the possibility of a very enduring small amount of downward finite sample bias.)

This example illustrates the attenuation bias induced in estimates of a bivariate linear regression slope by using a noisy measure of a predictor. In our application, the measure is based on a relatively large number (30) of indicators, resulting in a relatively good signal-to-noise ratio. In cases where fewer indicators or less informative indicators are used, the attenuation bias will be larger, as seen below. It is also shown that while the standard IV approach to dealing with measurement error produces
over
-correction, it is possible to correct for this and obtain slope estimates that are nearly unbiased, with variance barely higher than if the true predictor were known without error.

A.III.1.3
Power Analysis

Figure
A.III.2
shows that, across all the simulated settings examined, our corrected procedure retains close to nominal coverage while confidence intervals from the uncorrected OLS and IV estimators frequently fail to contain the true parameter value (0.4) even at moderate sample sizes. With the correct approach, as the number of indicators (
M
) grows, measurement error in the latent predictor diminishes, allowing the corrected estimator’s coverage to stay near nominal levels. In contrast, the uncorrected methods exhibit persistently lower coverage, particularly in smaller
M
regimes. In this way, statistical inference is improved with the corrections described here.

Figure A.III.2:
Power results varying
N
and
M
.

A closer look at the small-
M
regime reveals that even the corrected approach can show mildly reduced coverage there, reflecting finite-sample bias when estimating the latent factor from very little data. One possibility is that, in this low-information setting, random variation in the latent variable estimates via the bootstrap is insufficient to reflect true sampling uncertainty. Another possibility is the presence of small but lingering finite sample bias in this low-information case. Nonetheless, once
N
and
M
become sufficiently large, this small-
M
artifact subsides; the corrected procedure once again not only outperforms the uncorrected OLS or IV, but also yields inferences approaching nominal levels.

A.III.1.4
Comparison with Bayesian Strategies

Researchers have increasingly turned to Bayesian strategies for inference regarding latent traits. We examine three such approaches: (1) method of composition, (2) multiple over-imputation, and (3) joint factor and outcome models. We compare these strategies using the same data-generating process outlined in the simulation section (see Section
7
).

The method of composition approach draws from the posterior distribution of the latent trait estimates
(
Treier & Jackman 2008
;
Tanner 2012
)
. We first fit the outcome OLS model to each posterior draw of the latent traits. We then draw random samples from the resulting likelihood distribution, using draws from a Multivariate Gaussian centered at the point estimate and with a heteroskedasticity-consistent variance-covariance matrix. See
Treier & Jackman 2008
for details (especially the right panel of p. 215).

Multiple imputation, described in
Rubin 1988
and refined in
Blackwell et al. 2017
for the joint outcome and measurement error modeling case, involves generating multiple plausible values for the latent traits using mean and variances from an initial MCMC step as priors—analyzing each imputed dataset separately before combining results.

In the joint modeling strategy, we fit a Bayesian model that simultaneously uses both outcome and observed factor information to generate estimates of the latent quantities of interest. This approach generates a Bayesian posterior over the coefficient estimates. Again, draws of the latent trait,
X
, are rescaled so that the posterior mean takes on a standard deviation of one.

Figure A.III.3:
Various approaches, compared against the approaches analyzed in the main text.

Figure
A.III.3
presents the results for these approaches. We observe that none of the Bayesian approaches outperform the sample splitting adjustment methods proposed in this paper. These results suggest that Bayesian modeling strategies do not necessarily solve the problems observed in this problem. The Bayesian posterior draws are noisier than the posterior mean estimate, hence increasing the degree of measurement error. Both the joint Bayesian model and multiple imputations face questions around how to perform standardization, whether that be within or across posterior draws.

In contrast, simple OLS estimates based on sample splitting, adjusted for measurement error as proposed in this paper, provide a compelling alternative. Beyond improvements in RMSE, this approach does not require fitting a larger-scale Bayesian model, thereby avoiding the increased computational costs associated with generating uncorrelated draws from the posterior distribution.

Appendix V: A
lpme
Package Tutorial

lpme
implements a bootstrap analysis for latent variable models with measurement error correction. We average over
nPartition
split sample partitions:

# install.packages("devtools")
devtools::install_github("cjerzak/lpme/lpme-software")

# Generate data
Yobs <- rnorm(1000)
ObservablesMat <- matrix(sample(c(0, 1), 1000 * 10,
replace = TRUE),
ncol = 10)

# Latent error correction method, with partitioning and bootstrap
results <- lpme::lpme(
Yobs,
ObservablesMat,
nBoot = 32L,
nPartition = 10L,
bootBasis = 1:length(Yobs),
ReturnIntermediaries = TRUE
)

# Print the corrected IV coefficient and its standard error
print(c(results$Corrected_IVRegCoef, results$Corrected_IVRegSE))

Appendix VII: Measurement Error and Latent Predictors in the Political Science Literature

To assess the state of the political science literature regarding measurement error in latent predictors, we conducted an original survey of all articles published in the three top substantive political science journals (
APSR, AJPS, JOP
) as well as
Political Analysis (PA)
between 2000 and 2023. We observe in Figure
A.VII.1
that the overall percentage of articles mentioning latent variables and, especially, measurement error is increasing, while the number of published works mentioning both measurement error and latent variables has remained relatively constant throughout the study period. This survey of recent political science articles demonstrates that while many researchers are aware of and concerned about measurement error, the full extent of its impact on analyses involving latent variables is, perhaps, not yet widely recognized or addressed in applied work.
