Prevalence of pre-existing neutralizing antibodies to AAV5 and AAV8 in patients with Wilson’s disease
Authors: Zi-Wei Zheng, Hong Liu, Ling-Yu Guo, Lu-Na Fan, Chuan Liu, Fu-Rong Li, Ran Zhou, Lin Qu, Rou-Min Wang, Wan-Qing Xu, Guo-Min Yang, Biao Dong, Yi Dong, Zhi-Ying Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWilson’s disease (WD) is an autosomal recessive copper metabolism disorder. AAV-based gene therapy is promising but hindered by pre-existing neutralizing antibodies (NAbs), with no region-specific data on AAV5 and AAV8 NAbs in WD patients. This study aimed to address this gap. We investigated AAV5 and AAV8 NAb seroprevalence and dynamics in a cohort of Chinese WD patients via a cell-based transduction inhibition assay. Results showed that seroprevalence of AAV8 (58.52%) was higher than that of AAV5 (44.89%), with AAV8 NT50 titers 4.6-fold higher (p < 0.001). Seroprevalence increased with age, and AAV5 and AAV8 NAbs were strongly correlated (r = 0.848, p < 0.001) with no AAV5-only positivity. Longitudinal data revealed stable serostatus (3.8% seroconversion, no seroreversion) and no significant associations with other clinical parameters. The p.I1148T variant of ATP7B correlated with higher NAb titers. These findings provide epidemiological insights into pre-existing immunity to AAV vectors in WD patients and may help inform vector selection considerations for future gene therapy studies. Early intervention and personalized strategies may improve therapeutic accessibility. This study provides critical data for AAV-ATP7B trial design in Chinese WD patients.Keywords: Wilson’s disease, adeno-associated virus, neutralizing antibodies, gene therapy, seroprevalence

Introduction

Wilson’s disease (WD) is an autosomal recessive disorder of copper metabolism caused by pathogenic variants in the ATP7B gene, which disrupts copper homeostasis and leads to toxic accumulation in the liver, brain, and other organs.1,2 The global prevalence of WD ranges from 1/2,600 to 1/30000, with a carrier frequency of approximately 1/90.3,4 Without timely intervention, the disease progresses to liver failure, irreversible neurological deficits, and eventual death. Current therapies, including copper-chelating agents and zinc salts, require lifelong adherence and are associated with significant side effects (e.g., nephrotoxicity, neurological deterioration), underscoring the unmet need for curative therapies. Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene therapy has emerged as a transformative approach for WD: three AAV-ATP7B candidates are currently in phase 1/2 trials, and at least four preclinical programs are poised for investigational new drug (IND) filing globally (Table S1).

A critical translational barrier for AAV gene therapy is pre-existing neutralizing antibodies (NAbs), which can ablate vector transduction. Reported NAb seroprevalence varies by AAV serotype (30% for AAV5 to 60% for AAV2/8) and is primarily derived from non-WD cohorts (healthy volunteers or patients with other diseases).5 Seroprevalence also differs substantially across geographic regions and ethnicities, with up to 60% of adults carrying NAbs against common AAV serotypes6—these antibodies neutralize vectors, block transduction, and render gene therapy ineffective. In WD, chronic liver inflammation and immune activation may further increase NAb prevalence compared to healthy populations. Preclinical and early clinical evidence suggests that NAbs against AAV8 and AAV9 (serotypes advanced in WD trials) may exclude 30%–50% of patients.7

Notably, the reported seroprevalence of anti-AAV NAbs varies considerably across different populations, influenced by factors such as ethnicity, geographic region, and underlying disease status. For instance, one study of 888 healthy volunteers found AAV2 NAbs to be the most prevalent, followed by AAV1, AAV7, and AAV8.8 Another investigation of 552 human samples reported high rates of NAbs against AAV5 (63.9%) and AAV8 (60.4%).9 Some research results showed that the seropositivity was 38% for AAV8.10 Chinese healthy subjects exhibit higher seroprevalence for AAV2 (96.6%) and AAV8 (82.0%) than AAV5 (40.2%).11 In hemophilia A/B patients, AAV8 NAb seroprevalence ranges from 23% to 70%, and AAV5 ranges from 6% to 66%,7 in cystic fibrosis pediatric patients, AAV2/5/6 NAb seroprevalence is 4%–15%.12 A systematic review of published data shows an AAV5 NAb positivity rate of 35% in hemophilia B patients (NCT03061201)13 and 32% in MMA (methymalonic acidemia) patients (NCT00078078).14 However, direct comparisons across studies are limited by heterogeneous geographic regions and assay methods.

For WD, limited seroprevalence data and chronic hepatic immune activation suggest distinct NAb profiles compared to general populations. This represents a critical knowledge gap for WD patients, despite active preclinical AAV gene therapy programs. AAV8, AAV5, and AAV9 are the main serotypes currently used in WD gene therapy, and we specifically selected AAV5 and AAV8 for this study based on three core rationales: First, both serotypes exhibit strong liver tropism—since the liver is the key target organ for WD gene therapy (correcting hepatic copper metabolism disorders is the core of curative treatment). AAV8 demonstrates exceptional liver transduction efficiency (90%–95% hepatocyte transduction) with 3–4-fold higher efficiency than AAV2.15 AAV5 exhibits robust liver targeting in primate studies with distinct immunological profiles,16 while AAV9 is more prominent in cross blood-brain barrier (BBB) transduction and is mainly used for improving neurological symptoms in WD, with liver transduction efficiency inferior to AAV5 and AAV8.17 Second, AAV8 and AAV5 have been widely deployed in global WD gene therapy pipelines: AAV8 has entered phase 1/2 clinical trials (e.g., VTX-801),18 and AAV5 is in preclinical development—their seroprevalence data in WD patients are thus directly relevant to clinical translation. Third, existing NAb data for these two serotypes are mostly derived from healthy populations or other diseases, and their immunogenicity in WD patients with chronic liver inflammation remains unclear; in contrast.

Against this backdrop, we conducted the first head-to-head comparison of AAV5 and AAV8 NAbs in a large single-center cohort of Chinese WD patients (n = 176). Additionally, 26 patients underwent paired sampling a median interval of 31 months apart, providing the first longitudinal insight into NAb serostability in WD. These data inform an evidence-based vector-selection algorithm for upcoming AAV-ATP7B trials, bridging bench-to-bedside translation.

Results

Clinical characteristics

Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and genetic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Among 176 WD patients, 94 (53.4%) were male. The mean age at onset (AAO) was 15.0 ± 9.7 years (range: 2.0–53.0 years; median: 14.0 years), and the mean current age was 25.5 ± 11.1 years (range: 3.0–59.0 years; median: 24.5 years). The mean disease duration was 10.3 ± 8.4 years (range: 1 day–36.0 years; median: 9.0 years). The median course of disease was 9.0 years, with the 25th and 75th percentiles being 3.0 and 14.0 years, respectively. Imaging confirmed liver cirrhosis in 34 patients (19.3%) and splenomegaly in 63 patients (35.8%); 12 (19.0%) of those with splenomegaly had undergone splenectomy.

Age-stratified analysis showed no consistent trend in NT50 levels for AAV5 and AAV8 (AAV5: p = 0.5300; AAV8: p = 0.6164), but seroprevalence increased with age. Specifically, AAV5 and AAV8 seroprevalence was 33% in children (≤12 years) and 50% in adolescents (13–18 years). Among adult subgroups, AAV8 seroprevalence ranged from 58% to 100%, and AAV5 ranged from 47% to 66% (Figure 1B). Age stratification was significantly correlated with AAV5 (r = 0.971, p = 0.025) and AAV8 (r = 0.868, p = 0.001) seroprevalence (Figure S2). Across all age groups, AAV8 consistently exhibited higher seroprevalence and antibody titers than AAV5. These findings indicate that age may serve as a potential influencing factor for the distribution of AAV serotypes in the study cohort.

Compared with previously reported data from healthy Chinese individuals using a similar methodology, the seroprevalence of AAV8 in our WD cohort (58.52%) appeared lower than that reported as 82.0%, while AAV5 seroprevalence (44.89%) was slightly higher than the 40.2% observed in the healthy population.11 However, direct comparisons are limited by differences in assay sensitivity and specific cohort demographics.

AAV5 seroprevalence was slightly higher in males (45.7%) than females (43.9%), while AAV8 seroprevalence was higher in females (61.0%) than males (56.4%); however, these gender differences were not statistically significant. Besides, male patients had marginally higher AAV5 NT50 titers (22.41 vs. 15.89; p = 0.077) and AAV8 titers (97.34 vs. 78.56; p = 0.212), but these differences were also non-significant (Table 2).

Correlation between AAV5 and AAV8

Co-distribution analysis of AAV5 and AAV8 serotypes revealed a high degree of overlap: 44.89% of patients were positive for both serotypes, 41.48% were negative for both, and 13.64% were positive only for AAV8 (Figure 2A). Notably, no patients were exclusively positive for AAV5 (Table S3). We further investigated the relationship between AAV5 and AAV8 NAb levels and clinical parameters (age, sex, AAO, and disease duration) using correlation heatmap analysis (Figure 2C). The results showed a strong positive correlation between AAV5 and AAV8 NAb levels (r = 0.848, p < 0.001). This indicates that AAV5-positive patients are almost universally AAV8 positive, with roughly proportional antibody levels (Figure 2B), likely reflecting broad, cross-reactive pan-AAV immunity at higher titers.

However, a critical evaluation of the specific serostatus revealed a distinctly discordant subpopulation that highlights the independent clinical utility of AAV5. Although AAV5 positivity strongly predicts AAV8 positivity, the reverse is not strictly true. Specifically, the 13.64% of the total cohort who were positive only for AAV8 represent approximately 23.5% of the entire AAV8-positive patient population (calculated as 13.64%/[13.64% + 44.32%]). In our paired analysis (Figure S1), this subpopulation exhibited clear seropositivity to AAV8 but remained strictly seronegative for AAV5 (NT50 < 1:5).

The serotype conversion dynamics of 26 patients

A longitudinal observational study evaluated AAV5 and AAV8 NAb serostatus dynamics in 26 WD patients (Figure 3A). Serum samples were collected at two time points, with a median interval of 31 months (range: 8–59 months). Among these 26 patients, 15 (57.7%) were male and 11 (42.3%) were female (Table S4). AAV5 seroprevalence increased slightly from 50.0% to 53.8%, while AAV8 seroprevalence rose from 53.8% to 57.7% between the two samplings (Figure 3B). No significant differences in AAV NAb titers were observed between the two time points (p > 0.05). Notably, the serostatus remained stable in most patients, with no seroreversion (from positive to negative) observed for AAV5 or AAV8 during follow-up (Figure 3C). For AAV5: 13 (50.0%) patients were seropositive at baseline, 12 (46.2%) remained consistently seronegative, and 1 (3.8%) experienced seroconversion (patient 14: first titer <1:5; second titer: 29.49). For AAV8: 14 (53.9%) patients were seropositive at baseline, 11 (42.3%) remained consistently seronegative, and 1 (3.8%) experienced seroconversion (patient 5: first titer <1:5; second titer: 8.38). Consistently seronegative patients had significantly different ages at onset and diagnosis compared to consistently seropositive patients (all p < 0.05). This discrepancy is unlikely due to differences in pre- and post-treatment follow-up but instead reflects the previously noted association between age and seroprevalence (Figure 3D).

Among patients treated with D-penicillamine (DPA, n = 13), AAV5-seropositive cases increased from 8 to 9 (1 seroconversion), while AAV8-seropositive cases remained stable at 9. In the dimercaptosuccinic acid group (n = 9), 3 cases maintained AAV5 seropositivity and AAV8-seropositive cases increased from 3 to 4 (1 seroconversion). In the zinc gluconate group (n = 3), 2 cases remained seropositive for both AAV5 and AAV8 throughout follow-up (1 patient was untreated). Overall, the seroprevalence for either AAV was modest across all treatment groups, and no distinct treatment-associated trend was observed (AAV5: p = 0.237, AAV8: p = 0.420). Additionally, no significant associations were found between clinical parameters and AAV5 and AAV8 seroconversion (all p > 0.05, Table S5).

Discussion

This study used a standardized cell-based TI assay to characterize anti-AAV5 and AAV8 NAb profiles in the Chinese WD patients, providing preliminary epidemiological observations to inform future gene therapy design for this understudied population. Previous studies have shown that NAb prevalence increased with age and was higher in females, participants of Asian ethnicity, and participants in cancer trials.9 Against this backdrop of broad variability, the present study provides crucial data in the under-characterized WD population. Our study found AAV5 NAb seroprevalence of 44.89% and AAV8 NAb seroprevalence of 58.52% in WD patients—exceeding rates reported in other gene therapy trials. Additionally, AAV8 NT50 titers were 4.6-fold higher than AAV5. These findings highlight a distinct pre-existing immunity profile in WD, which may reflect disease-specific immune activation and has direct implications for the design of AAV-based gene therapies in this population.

The persistent hepatic inflammation and dysregulated immune microenvironment characteristic of WD may modulate immune responsiveness to AAV vectors.19 Interestingly, our data revealed significantly elevated NT50 values in splenectomized patients (n = 12). This phenomenon may be explained by several immunological mechanisms. First, the absence of splenic filtration may alter antigen kinetics, allowing naturally occurring AAV particles to persist longer in systemic circulation. Second, the loss of the spleen may redirect circulating antigens to the bone marrow—a primary site for the generation of long-lived plasma cells (LLPCs)—thereby potentiating high-affinity NAb production. Finally, the recovery of peripheral leukocyte counts following the alleviation of hypersplenism might intensify humoral responses to environmental antigens. While these insights are compelling, the small sub-sample size limits definitive conclusions.

The observed increase in the prevalence of anti-AAV NAbs with increasing age is consistent with prior observations. Age-stratified analysis revealed lower AAV5 and AAV8 seropositivity rates in pediatric WD patients, providing direct support for the “early therapeutic windowˮ hypothesis. This concept aligns closely with clinical experience using AAV9 in spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), where early intervention—before robust humoral immunity to AAV develops—improves transduction efficiency and treatment safety.20 These findings emphasize the importance of early gene therapy feasibility assessments in pediatric WD patients and provide insights into optimal intervention timing. However, applying liver-directed AAV therapy in pediatric populations presents a critical dilemma: the natural proliferation of hepatocytes during pediatric liver growth can severely limit long-term therapeutic durability. Because AAV vectors remain largely as non-integrating episomes, rapid cell division inevitably leads to the dilution and gradual loss of vector genomes. Balancing the advantage of a low-NAb therapeutic window against the risk of waning transgene expression remains a key challenge in optimizing pediatric gene therapy strategies for WD.

No significant correlations were found between NAb levels and clinical parameters (age, gender, AAO, and disease duration), suggesting AAV antibody production is not directly linked to WD severity. However, phenotypic differences in seroprevalence were observed: hepatic onset patients had higher AAV8 seroprevalence, while mixed onset patients had higher AAV5 seroprevalence. However, these results require validation in larger cohorts due to small sample sizes for some phenotypes.

The strong positive correlation between AAV5 and AAV8 NAb levels, characterized by a 44.89% co-positivity rate and an absence of AAV5-only positivity, understandably raises questions about cross-reactivity (Figure S1). Regarding methodology, we ensured the reliability of our comparative data by strictly optimizing the multiplicity of infection (MOI) for each serotype to achieve comparable baseline signals. The assay sensitivities were highly harmonized, providing a robust and consistent basis for direct comparison without technical bias.

While this high co-prevalence suggests the presence of broadly cross-reactive antibodies, our paired discordance analysis reveals a critical clinical nuance: 23.5% of AAV8-positive individuals remained entirely seronegative for AAV5 (Figure S1). Biologically, this distinct serological independence aligns with AAV5’s pronounced structural divergence, which typically minimizes universal cross-reactivity. We acknowledge that without specific cross-neutralization validation (e.g., pre-incubation with empty capsids), we cannot definitively rule out that some co-positive signals arise from shared epitopes. Therefore, future studies must incorporate strict cross-neutralization validation to isolate serotype-specific signals. Nevertheless, the persistence of a ∼21% AAV5-seronegative window among AAV8-positive patients, coupled with recent clinical evidence suggesting that AAV5-based therapies can remain efficacious even in the presence of pre-existing NAbs, firmly preserves its potential clinical advantage as a robust alternative vector.

Longitudinal follow-up showed stable serostatus in most patients, with 3.8% seroconversion and modest median titer declines over time (consistent with gradual humoral immunity waning). No associations between NAb status/titers and clinical/genetic/hepatic parameters were found, indicating pre-existing AAV5 immunity is not directly influenced by WD pathophysiology or treatment. These findings have important implications for the development of AAV5-based gene therapies, as stable pre-existing immunity may impact vector transduction efficiency and therapeutic outcomes in this patient population.

It is imperative to emphasize that AAV8 remains the current “gold standard” for liver-directed gene therapy due to its superior transduction efficiency and accumulated clinical safety data in WD. While AAV5 demonstrates a lower seroprevalence (44.89% vs. 58.52%) and may increase patient eligibility, its clinical superiority remains speculative without head-to-head quantitative efficacy data demonstrating comparable copper metabolism normalization.

Based on these preliminary epidemiological observations, we propose the following considerations for future WD gene therapy development: (1) priority population: AAV5 NAb-negative individuals <18 years old without liver transplantation; (2) vector selection: while AAV8 remains the benchmark for potency, AAV5’s lower seroprevalence (44.89% vs. 58.52%) suggests it may expand the eligible patient pool in China, provided its biological efficacy is validated; (3) vector engineering: employ capsid protein engineering techniques, such as site-directed mutagenesis at key epitopes (e.g., Y445F, T491V), to reduce recognition by NAbs.21,22 However, it is critical to note that engineered capsids represent novel antigens; thus, the seroprevalence data for wild-type AAV5 presented here may not directly apply to modified vectors. Furthermore, while immunoadsorption is a theoretical pre-treatment strategy to deplete circulating antibodies, its clinical feasibility is severely constrained by high costs, invasiveness, and the high risk of rapid antibody rebound following apheresis; (4) pre-treatment strategy: for NAb-positive patients, consider immunoadsorption to deplete circulating antibodies-a strategy supported by preclinical evidence223; and (5) post-treatment monitoring: assess NAb titers every 2 years to detect seroconversion and prevent therapeutic loss.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size (n = 176) and single-center nature may introduce selection bias, and these findings primarily reflect the immunological landscape of Chinese WD patients; thus, extrapolating these observations globally requires caution due to regional and environmental biases. Notably, the absence of a synchronized healthy control cohort run on the same assay platform limits our ability to definitively determine whether the observed NAb profiles are uniquely specific to WD pathophysiology or reflect broader regional exposures. While we contrasted our results with published literature, future studies should prioritize age- and region-matched controls to refine these disease-specific insights. Second, the use of sodium butyrate (an HDAC inhibitor) as a transduction enhancer, while necessary for signal detection, may artificially boost transgene expression and potentially lead to an underestimation of true NAb positivity rates by masking low-affinity inhibitory effects. Additionally, we could not evaluate the potential impact of hypergammaglobulinemia on our results. Routine IgG testing is not standard practice for WD at baseline, and the limited volume of our archived serum samples precluded retrospective testing after AAV5 and AAV8 NAb assessments. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that undetected elevated IgG levels might have led to an overestimation of NAb titers in some patients, highlighting the need for systematic immunological profiling in future prospective cohorts. To address these gaps, future studies should enroll larger cohorts (≥500 participants) with extended follow-up (≥5 years) and integrate single-cell RNA sequencing for personalized immune risk stratification. Such efforts will further guide precise vector selection and the global development of AAV gene therapy for WD.

In summary, this study provides the first comprehensive analysis of pre-existing NAbs against AAV5 and AAV8 in a Chinese WD cohort. The data demonstrate substantial seroprevalence of anti-AAV antibodies, with higher prevalence and titers observed for AAV8 compared with AAV5. These findings provide important epidemiological information that may inform the design of future AAV-based gene therapy trials in WD. However, vector selection for clinical applications should consider not only seroprevalence but also transduction efficiency, safety profiles, and long-term therapeutic durability.

Methods

Patients and samples

From August 2018 to December 2024, 176 consecutive WD patients were enrolled: all were initially diagnosed per established criteria and confirmed to carry biallelic pathogenic ATP7B variants.24 Twenty-six patients agreed to repeat sampling post-diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included: coexisting autoimmune or immunodeficiency disorders; blood product transfusion within 3 months; acute infection within 1 month; immunosuppressive therapy (except low-dose regimens post-transplant with documented details); and poor-quality serum samples (hemolysis or lipemia). Baseline total serum IgG levels were measured upon initial admission and confirmed to be within the normal range for all recruited patients.

Peripheral blood samples were collected in sodium heparin tubes, centrifuged at 1,000–2,000 × g at 4°C for 10 min, and serum was aliquoted (1 mL) and stored at −80°C (mean storage duration: 0.96 years; range: 0.5–7 years) until analysis. Serum was thawed on wet ice, with one aliquot used for AAV5 and AAV8 NAb detection. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (approval number: 2015-048, 2019-032), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal guardians.

Data collection and definitions

Clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized case report form, including (1) demographics (age and sex); (2) immune-relevant factors (liver cirrhosis, exposure to chelating agents such as D-penicillamine [DPA]); (3) splenic manifestations (splenomegaly and splenectomy history); and (4) copper-related laboratory indices (ceruloplasmin levels, 24-h urine copper excretion). These variables were selected based on their hypothesized roles in immune modulation, NAb induction, or associations with disease severity/treatment response.

Cell culture, transduction inhibition assay, and NAb detection

AAV vector construction and production

Recombinant AAV5-CB-Luc and AAV8-CB-Luc vectors, encoding a luciferase reporter gene under the control of the CB promoter, were produced by triple-plasmid co-transfection in HEK293 cells. The vectors were purified using sequential affinity chromatography and anion-exchange chromatography. To accurately assess vector purity and capsid composition, analytical ultracentrifugation (AUC) was performed. The analysis revealed that for AAV5-CB-Luc, the composition was 80.97% full capsids and 16.02% empty capsids (with 3.01% other components). For AAV8-CB-Luc, the composition was 71.84% full capsids, 11.82% partial capsids, and 11.21% empty capsids. The low proportion of empty capsids (16.02% and 11.21%, respectively) confirmed that their potential decoy effect in the neutralizing antibody assays was minimized (Table S6).

Cell culture and transduction inhibition assay

NAbs against AAV5 and AAV8 were detected using cell-based transduction inhibition (TI) assays. HeLa cells were used for the AAV5 TI assay, while 293 T cells were used for the AAV8 TI assay (Figure S3). NAbs against AAV5 and AAV8 were detected using cell-based TI assays. This cell line pairing was selected based on pre-experimental optimization and our previously validated protocol for AAV NAb detection.25 Cells were seeded at 30,000 cells/well in 96-well plates.

Serum samples were initially diluted at a minimum required dilution (MRD) of 1:5 and incubated with AAV5-CB-Luc or AAV8-CB-Luc vectors for 1 h at room temperature. The mixtures were then added to cells at optimized MOIs of 5,500 vector genomes (vg)/cell for AAV5 and 1,000 vg/cell for AAV8 (Figure S4). After 24 h of incubation, luciferase activity was measured using a luminescence microplate reader.

Under these conditions, the relative light unit (RLU) values corresponding to 100% transduction were approximately 30,000–40,000 for both serotypes, ensuring that signals were within the dynamic range and significantly above background levels. Sodium butyrate was used during assay development and applied consistently to enhance transduction efficiency (Figure S5).

Assay optimization and linearity validation

MOIs were optimized by assessing the relationship between RLU values and vector dose. AAV5-CB-Luc and AAV8-CB-Luc vectors were evaluated across MOI ranges of 0–7,000 and 0–5,000, respectively, demonstrating dose-dependent increases in transduction signals. Assay linearity was validated using serial dilutions of anti-AAV5 and anti-AAV8 monoclonal antibodies. TI exhibited a linear relationship with the logarithm of antibody concentration, with R2 values of 0.98 for AAV5 and 0.96 for AAV8 (Figure S6). Assay sensitivity, defined as the antibody concentration achieving 50% inhibition, was 111.08 ng/mL for AAV5 and 114.47 ng/mL for AAV8.

Controls and assay cut point

Mouse monoclonal anti-AAV5 antibody (clone ADK5b, PROGEN Biotechnik) and anti-AAV8 antibody (clone ADK8, PROGEN Biotechnik) were used as positive controls. These antibodies specifically recognize intact AAV particles without cross-reactivity between serotypes. Serial dilutions (800, 200, 50, and 12.5 ng/mL) were used to assess assay performance. Negative controls consisted of pooled serum from healthy donors (n > 10) confirmed to be negative for AAV5 or AAV8 NAbs. This pooled serum was also used as the diluent to minimize matrix effects. The assay cut point (ACP) for NAb positivity was defined as ≥50% TI.

NAb titer calculation

Samples testing positive in the screening assay were further evaluated using a serial dilution series (1:5, 1:20, 1:80, 1:320, 1:1280, and 1:5120). The NT50 value was defined as the reciprocal serum dilution that achieved 50% TI. NT50 values were calculated by linear interpolation using the two data points immediately above and below the 50% inhibition threshold. Assay specificity was further confirmed by the absence of cross-reactivity between anti-AAV5 and anti-AAV8 antibodies in the corresponding heterologous serotype assays.26

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient baseline characteristics. Between-group comparisons for continuous variables were performed using Student’s t test (for normally distributed data with equal variance) or Mann-Whitney U test (for non-normally distributed data or data with heterogeneous variance). Paired t tests were used to analyze longitudinal changes in antibody levels in the 26 patients with repeated sampling. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages, %), and between-group associations were assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test, continuity correction, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Correlations between continuous variables were analyzed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (normally distributed data) or Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (non-normally distributed data). Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of NAb positivity. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).
