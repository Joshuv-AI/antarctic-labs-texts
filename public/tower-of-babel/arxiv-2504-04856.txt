Approach to optimal quantum transport via states over time
Matt Hoogsteder-Riera, John Calsamiglia, Andreas Winter

Abstract
We approach the problem of constructing a quantum analogue of the immensely fruitful classical transport cost theory of Monge from a new angle. Going back to the original motivations, by which the transport is a bilinear function of a mass distribution (without loss of generality a probability density) and a transport plan (a stochastic kernel), we explore the quantum version where the mass distribution is generalised to a density matrix, and the transport plan to a completely positive and trace preserving map. These two data are naturally integrated into their Jordan product, which is called state over time (“stote”), and the transport cost is postulated to be a linear function of it. We explore the properties of this transport cost, as well as the optimal transport cost between two given states (simply the minimum cost over all suitable transport plans). After that, we analyse in considerable detail the case of unitary invariant cost, for which we can calculate many costs analytically. These findings suggest that our quantum transport cost is qualitatively different from Monge’s classical transport.

1.1
Mathematical preliminaries

The Choi and Jamiołkowski isomorphisms
[
24
,
12
]
are a relation between CPTP maps from
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
to
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
and bipartite states on the joint state space
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
. These isomorphisms are relevant to this work because they allow us to treat maps as operators, allowing for operations that would not be possible otherwise. In more general terms, these isomorphisms allow us to translate complete positivity of a map to positivity of an operator, with the latter usually being much easier to check. We also make use of this fact in

3

Theorem 1.1

(Choi isomorphism
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\rightarrow\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
be a linear map and

the non-normalised maximally entangled vector on the composite system of
A
and a copy

of it. The Choi operator, defined as

C_{\mathcal{E}}=\left(\operatorname{id}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{E}\right)\left(\outerproduct{\Phi_+}{\Phi_+}\right)\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}),

(1.2)

recovers the action of
\mathcal{E}
as

\mathcal{E}(x)=\Tr_{A}\left[(x^{T}\otimes\openone_{B})C_{\mathcal{E}}\right].

(1.3)

Moreover,

is completely positive if and only if
C_{\mathcal{E}}
is positive semi-definite and
\mathcal{E}
is trace preserving if and only if
\;\Tr_{B}\left[C_{\mathcal{E}}\right]=\openone_{A}
.

Let

the swap operator on the joint system

recovers the action of
\mathcal{E}
as

\mathcal{E}(x)=\Tr_{A}\left[(x\otimes\openone_{B})J_{\mathcal{E}}\right].

(1.5)

Moreover,
\mathcal{E}
is completely positive if and only if
\;J_{\mathcal{E}}^{T_{A}}
is positive

2

2

For simplicity we usually use positive instead of the more rigorous positive semi-definite.
and
\mathcal{E}

\Tr_{B}\left[J_{\mathcal{E}}\right]=\openone_{A}
.

It is clear from the theorems that if
C
and

are Choi and Jamiołkowski operators associated to the same CPTP map, then

J=C^{T_{A}}.

(1.6)

These theorems clarify how states and channels relate and how the two isomorphisms relate to each other. Each has its own advantages: the Choi matrix is manifestly positive, which is convenient when positivity must be checked explicitly, while the Jamiołkowski form is basis-independent. In the Choi construction one uses a maximally entangled state and the transpose on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
. Both of those steps depend on a choice of basis, so the Choi matrix is basis-dependent. In contrast, using the Jamiołkowski form avoids that dependence. Moreover, the swap operator plays a useful computational role in the Jamiołkowski picture, facilitating manipulations like exchanging tensor factors or expressing compositions more neatly.

We use the notation
C
and
J
for Choi and Jamiołkowski operators, respectively, throughout this work. Moreover, we generally refer to Choi or Jamiołkowski operators associated to CPTP map. Because our work deals with finite dimensional systems only, we also use the terms Choi and Jamiołkowski matrix interchangeably with operator. We usually use the Jamiołkowski operator, but the Choi operator will sometimes be more convenient to use in some circumstances. We always assume that the transpose is taken with respect to the canonical basis, or whichever privileged basis the problem presents. The basis dependence of the Choi matrix is only a problem when dealing with practical cases and never for general theoretical derivations.

Throughout the paper we use
A^{T}
for the transpose of a matrix
A
,
\overline{A}
for the element-wise complex conjugate, and
A^{*}
for the conjugate transpose. With this notation,
A^{*}=\overline{A}^{T}
.

2
Jordan product motivation and properties

In this section we want to motivate our choice of coupling for the quantum optimal transport, since this choice is what differentiates our approach from the rest. The main observation was done in the previous section: a classical joint probability distribution can be interpreted as both a correlation function of a composite system and a map for the evolution over time of a single subsystem. The first interpretation can be generalised to quantum systems with bipartite states, as seen in, for example,
[
18
,
8
]
. We choose the other interpretation, that of a stochastic map on a system. In the context of quantum mechanics this corresponds to quantum channels acting on a system. As we explain in the following, these questions on how to write states encoding the correlations of an input system with its output in quantum mechanics had been asked before in the context of quantum foundations.

The concept of
states over time
as introduced in
[
23
]
is based on previous
attempts to formalise causal correlations in quantum theory, see
[
35
,
17
]

and references within
[
23
]
. Its main motivation is to find an operator in the tensor
product of the state spaces associated to two time points, which would capture the
process transforming the initial state to the final one.

For this purpose, several properties have been put forward as desirables for a state over time: as a function of the initial state and the quantum channel it should be Hermitian preserving, bilinear in the two arguments, it should contain the classical case, reproduce the initial and final states as marginals, and, finally, keep the composability of channels. Through the later works of Fullwood and Parzygnat
[
19
,
21
,
20
]
and
[
36
]
, we know that the Jordan product (also called symmetric bloom or Fullwood-Parzygnat state over time function in the literature) is the unique function that fulfils a slightly stronger set of axioms
[
36
]
that imply the properties above (that had been proposed in
[
23
]
).

The set of desiderata introduced in the paper are, in our opinion, well suited to the study of quantum optimal transport. Notably, the bilinearity, associativity (which translates to clear composability of the channels) and clear reduction to the classical case. Hermiticity and preservation of marginal states are useful mathematically as they allow us to define the problem as an SDP. Other approaches mentioned in
[
23
]
, the LS state over time and the W state over time fail to fulfil bilinearity and composability; and preservation of the classical limit, respectively. That said, other works
[
14
,
18
]
have approached this problem using the LS state over time, even if not explicitly acknowledged in the manuscripts, with some success. Our approach takes the generalised FJV state over time (from
[
23
]
) due to its nice properties not shared with other states over time.

In the context of quantum optimal transport, a state over time gives an initial state and a process (in the form of a CPTP map). As previously mentioned, this is similar to how in classical (optimal) transport a coupling
\pi(x,y)
gives an initial state and final states (the marginals) and a process through the conditional probability formula
\pi(y|x)=\pi(x,y)/\pi(x)
. In this sense, a classical joint probability distribution acts as both a joint state in space and time, something that does not happen for quantum joint states. In this work we
take the state over time interpretation of a classical joint probability distribution and
extend this to the quantum case.

The Jordan product is a defined as one half of the anti-commutator: given two operators
A,B
, we denote
A\star B:=\frac{1}{2}\{A,B\}=\frac{1}{2}\left(AB+BA\right)

[
28
,
29
,
30
,
27
]
.
In the context of transport maps, this operation has several desirable properties, as previously
mentioned here and further expanded upon in
[
23
]
.
Moreover, while the Jordan product is not associative in general, it was shown in
[
19
]

to fulfil the associative property for products of matrices of the form

A_{01}\equiv A_{01}\otimes\openone_{23}
,

B_{12}\equiv B_{12}\otimes\openone_{03}
and

C_{23}\equiv\openone_{01}\otimes C_{23}

A_{01}\star(B_{12}\star C_{23})=(A_{01}\star B_{12})\star C_{23}
.
This form of product is what we encounter in our formalism, see Subsection
2.2
for the details on the associativity.

is a stochastic map since these coefficients do not affect the system, as
N_{i\leftarrow j}q_{j}=0
regardless of the chosen value.

We will also be interested in the cone generated by a set of states over time,
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{B})
. A cone is a subset of a real vector space that is closed under product by positive constant and convex combination.
We consider the cone and not the convex hull because we are interested in studying the positivity of the quantum transport cost, and the cone gives us a better framework to study it later.
The normalisation condition is linear and therefore much easier to deal with.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B}
be finite dimensional Hilbert spaces and
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{B})
the set of Jamiołkowski matrices between these two spaces.
The cone of states over time is

\begin{split}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathcal{H}_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{B})&=\mathrm{cone}\left(\left\{\rho\star J|\,J\in\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{B})\text{ and }\rho\in S(\mathcal{H}_{A})\right\}\right)\\
&=\mathrm{cone}\left(\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{B})\right)=\mathrm{cone}\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\rho\in S(\mathcal{H}_{A})\\
\sigma\in S(\mathcal{H}_{B})\end{subarray}}\mathcal{Q}(\rho,\sigma)\right).\end{split}

(2.16)

2.2
States over multiple times

If instead of a single channel we have
n
channels in sequence we can write the space of states over time associated to this process as a recursive hierarchy:

Definition 2.7
.

Consider Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{i}
,
i\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{0}:\cdots:\mathcal{H}_{n})&=\left(\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{0}:\cdots:\mathcal{H}_{n-1})\otimes\openone_{n}\right)\star\left(\bigotimes_{j=0}^{n-2}\openone_{j}\otimes\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{H}_{n-1}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{n})\right)\\
&\equiv\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{0}:\cdots:\mathcal{H}_{n-1})\star\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{H}_{n-1}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{n}).\end{split}

(2.17)

By taking partial traces on specific subsystems we can ‘forget’ about the state of the system at that slot, that is that
\Tr_{i}\left[\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{0}:\cdots:\mathcal{H}_{n})\right]=\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{0}:\cdots:\mathcal{H}_{i-1}:\mathcal{H}_{i+1}:\cdots:\mathcal{H}_{n})
. This is true because given two Jamiołkowski matrices
J_{i-1,i},\,J_{i,i+1}
we can construct a Jamiołkowski matrix
\tilde{J}_{i-1,i+1}=\Tr_{i}\left[\left(J_{i-1,i}\otimes\openone_{i+1}\right)\star\left(\openone_{i-1}\otimes J_{i,i+1}\right)\right]
such that the associated channels fulfil
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}_{i-1,i+1}=\mathcal{E}_{i,i+1}\circ\mathcal{E}_{i-1,i}

[
11
]
.

Given a state over
n
times, we can also reconstruct the initial and all instantaneous states of the system, as well as the CPTP maps linking different times. Indeed, by tracing out every subsystem except a consecutive pair, we can reduce the problem to the two-time scenario and use
Theorem

2.3
.

3
Quantum transport cost and optimal transport

Before introducing the optimal transport cost between two states, it is useful to define the cost of transporting a single state through a given quantum channel.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{A}
,
\mathcal{H}_{B}
be Hilbert spaces, let
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
be a quantum state, and let
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
be a quantum channel.
For a given cost matrix
K\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
, we define the
quantum transport cost
of
\rho
under
\mathcal{E}
as

\displaystyle\kappa(\rho,\mathcal{E})=\Tr\left[KQ_{\rho,\mathcal{E}}\right],

(3.1)

where
Q_{\rho,\mathcal{E}}=\rho\star J_{\mathcal{E}}
denotes the state over time (or
coupling
) associated with sending
\rho
through the channel
\mathcal{E}
, with Jamiołkowski operator
J_{\mathcal{E}}
.

This quantity measures the expected cost of transporting the state
\rho
from system
A
to system
B
when the process is fixed to be
\mathcal{E}
.
In general, there may exist many channels
\mathcal{E}
that transform
\rho
into a target state
\sigma
.
The
quantum optimal transport cost
identifies, among all such admissible transformations, one that achieves the smallest possible cost:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{A,B})
and
K\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
a Hermitian operator. The quantum transport cost with cost matrix
K
between
\rho
and
\sigma
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)=\min_{\mathcal{E}\,|\,Q_{\rho,\mathcal{E}}\in\mathcal{Q}(\rho,\sigma)}\kappa(\rho,\mathcal{E})=\min_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}(\rho,\sigma)}\Tr[KQ].

(3.2)

Sometimes, when it is clear from the context, we refer to this quantity as just the cost, with the implication that it is quantum, optimal and transport based. Importantly, this definition explicitly depends on the choice of cost matrix
K
, which serves as the analogue of the cost function in classical optimal transport. The relevance of
\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)
and the properties it inherits are determined by this choice.

As we will discuss later in
Section

4
, the choice of cost matrix
K
determines the properties of the transport cost. In particular, we are interested in the properties that define a distance (cost
0
for identical inputs, positivity, triangle inequality and symmetry) as well as some generically useful properties in quantum information (additivity, joint convexity). Ideally, we would be able to find cost matrices
K
with good properties, notably the weaker ones: cost
0
for identical inputs and positivity, that allows us to encode some physical property we are interested in considering in a distinguishability measure. In this regard, transport costs differ from traditional distinguishability measures that rely purely on the vector space structure of the states.

Note that the quantum optimal transport cost can be calculated with an SDP
[
7
,
54
]
. Let, again,
K
be some cost matrix and
\rho
and
\sigma
states. Then the SDP associated to finding the quantum optimal transport cost between
\rho
and
\sigma
with cost matrix
K
is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{J}&\quad\quad\;\Tr\left[(K\star\rho)J\right]\\
\text{s.t.}&\quad\>\left\{\begin{aligned} \Tr_{B}J&=\openone\\
\Tr_{A}\left[\rho J\right]&=\sigma\\
J^{T_{A}}&\geq 0\end{aligned}\right..\end{split}

(3.3)

This form can provide some problems computationally due to the partial transpose in the positivity condition. To move these partial transposes to the constants of the problem, we present the SDP with the optimisation over the channel being in Choi form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\min_{C}&\quad\quad\;\Tr\left[(K\star\rho)^{T_{A}}C\right]\\
\text{s.t.}&\quad\>\left\{\begin{aligned} \Tr_{B}C&=\openone\\
\Tr_{A}\left[\rho^{T}C\right]&=\sigma\\
C&\geq 0\end{aligned}\right..\end{split}

(3.4)

and its dual

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{Y_{1},Y_{2}}&\quad\quad\;\Tr\left[Y_{1}\right]+\Tr\left[\sigma Y_{2}\right]\\
\text{s.t.}&\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} Y_{1}\otimes\openone+\rho^{T}\otimes Y_{2}&\leq(K\star\rho)^{T_{A}}\\
Y_{1},Y_{2}&\text{ Hermitian}\end{aligned}\right..\end{split}

(3.5)

The formulation of the primal in Choi form allows us to see the relation between the primal and dual problems. We use the notation of
[
54
]
. In this notation, the primal SDP is given by the triple
(\Phi(X),A,B)=((\Tr_{B}\left[X\right],\Tr_{A}\left[\rho^{T}X\right]),-(K\star\rho)^{T_{A}},(\openone,\sigma))
. Then
\Phi^{*}((Y_{1},Y_{2}))=Y_{1}\otimes\openone+\rho^{T}\otimes Y_{2}
.

The primal expression of the SDP further shows the connection between the coupling and the channel. In fact, the coupling is only implicitly in the SDP through
\Tr\left[(K\star\rho)J\right]=\Tr\left[K(\rho\star J)\right]=\Tr\left[KQ\right]
. We use the Jamiołkowski matrix in the SDP instead of the coupling in the SDP because it is unclear how the couplings can be characterised through semidefinite expressions.

4
Properties

In classical probabilistic settings, it is easy and of supreme interest
to use transport costs to define metrics on probability distributions.
Recall that a metric on a set
\Omega
is a function
D:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

(the distance) that satisfies the following properties for all
x,y,z\in\Omega
:

\displaystyle D(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq 0

(4.1)

\displaystyle D(x,y)

\displaystyle=D(y,x),

(4.2)

\displaystyle D(x,y)

\displaystyle=0\text{ if and only if }x=y,

(4.3)

\displaystyle D(x,z)

\displaystyle\leq D(x,y)+D(y,z).

(4.4)

Note that not all properties described here are equal. While symmetry and the triangle inequality can be nice, a function without these properties can still be a divergence, a very important and useful concept in information theory
[
41
,
48
]
. Thus, finding conditions of
K
for which the associated quantum optimal transport cost fulfils these properties is an important question.

The main goal of this section is to study sufficient conditions on
K
for which the associated cost has desirable properties. This is the main theoretical framework and general results of our approach to quantum optimal transport. A better characterisation of which cost matrices induce certain properties in the associated cost would allow us to define physically relevant costs with desirable properties.
Additionally, we examine general mathematical properties of interest in this context, namely convexity properties of the cost under ensemble input states as well as additivity/multiplicity under tensor products.

In the next section, we will use these results to focus on particular choices of cost functions under further physically relevant constraints, namely unitary invariance of the cost.

4.1
Cost of identity

For a quantum transport cost to be reasonable we want that the cost associated with the identity channel is
0
, regardless of the input state.
We could ask that there exists a state over time such that the cost is
0
if
\rho=\sigma
, like in
Eq.

4.4
, but physically it makes sense to ask that doing nothing results in zero cost.
The following result characterises the cost matrices that fulfil this property.

Proposition 4.1
.

Given a finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, a cost matrix
K
assigns cost 0 to the identity map (with any input) if and only if

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr_{B}\left[\mathcal{S}\star K\right]=0,\end{split}

(4.5)

where
\mathcal{S}
is the swap operator.

Proof.

The Jamiołkowski operator associated to the identity channel is the swap operator
\mathcal{S}
, clearly from
Section

1.1
:
J_{\mathrm{id}}=(\mathrm{id}\otimes\mathrm{id})(\mathcal{S})=\mathcal{S}
. Now, let
K
be a matrix such that
\kappa(\rho,\operatorname{id})=\Tr\left[(\rho\star\mathcal{S})K\right]=0

\,\forall\rho\geq 0
. We can transform the left-hand side in the following way, using the definition of the Jordan product, the cyclic property of the trace and properties of partial traces:

\displaystyle\Tr\left[((\rho\otimes\openone)\star\mathcal{S})K\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[((\rho\otimes\openone)\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{S}(\rho\otimes\openone))K\right]=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[(\rho\otimes\openone)(\mathcal{S}K+K\mathcal{S})\right]

(4.6)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left[\rho\Tr_{B}\left[\mathcal{S}\star K\right]\right]=0.

(4.7)

The set of positive matrices generates the whole space
[
46
,
2
]
, therefore this is equivalent to saying that the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product of
\Tr_{B}\left[\mathcal{S}\star K\right]
with all other elements is 0.
Therefore
\Tr_{B}\left[\mathcal{S}\star K\right]=0
.
The converse is immediate, so the proof is done.
∎

4.2
Positivity

A natural requirement for a cost matrix
K
is that the associated quantum optimal transport cost be nonnegative,
i.e.

\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)\geq 0
for all
\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B})
.
Note that in the definition of the cost matrix
K
is always Hermitian. Because
K
and the states over time
Q\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{B})
are Hermitian the cost is a real number. Moreover, since
\Tr\left[KQ\right]=\langle Q,K\rangle_{HS}
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{HS}
denotes the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, the
k
for which the cost is positive is the dual cone to
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{B})
with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. We have no closed-form characterisation of this dual cone, but we can provide some partial results.
Throughout this section, whenever we refer to duality, we mean it with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product.

We will be working with the cone generated by the set of states over time:
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathcal{H}_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{B})=\mathrm{cone}\left(\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{B})\right)
, as seen in
Definition

2.6
.

Remark 4.2
.

The cone can also be obtained by adding an ancillary system
R
and considering the CPTP maps that take inputs in the composite system
AR
. More precisely, consider finite dimensional Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{A},\mathcal{H}_{B},\mathcal{H}_{R}
and the following: let

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{t}p_{t}\rho_{t}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\quad\left\{\mathcal{E}^{A\rightarrow B}_{t}\right\},

(4.8)

where
\mathcal{E}^{A\rightarrow B}_{t}
are quantum channels from
\mathcal{H}_{A}
to
\mathcal{H}_{B}
.
Now consider the extension to a conditional quantum channel and the following extended state

\displaystyle\rho^{AR}=\sum_{t}p_{t}\rho^{A}_{t}\otimes\outerproduct{t}{t}^{R},\quad\mathcal{E}^{AR\rightarrow B}(X_{A}\otimes X_{R})=\sum_{t}\mathcal{E}_{t}^{A\rightarrow B}(X_{A})\expectationvalue{X_R}{t},

(4.9)

where the definition of
\mathcal{E}^{AR\rightarrow B}
is extended to general elements by linearity. The Jamiołkowski operator associated to

is

First, we want to characterise the dual cone to the set of Jamiołkowski matrices
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}_{B})^{*}

Definition 4.3
.

Let
X\subseteq V
be a subset of a real inner product vector space
V
. The dual cone to
X
with respect to the inner product is a cone in
V
, denoted by
X^{*}
, and defined as

X^{*}=\left\{v\in V\,|\,\left\langle v|x\right\rangle\geq 0\,\forall x\in X\right\}.

(4.12)

In the context of this work,
V
is the Hermitian subset of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
equipped with the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product. Note that with no further requirements for
X
,
X^{*}
fulfils the definition of cone.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
I
be an index set and
\left\{\mathcal{C}_{i}\right\}_{i\in I}
a set of convex cones. Then

\displaystyle\bigcap_{i\in I}\mathcal{C}_{i}^{*}=\left(\sum_{i\in I}\mathcal{C}_{i}\right)^{*}.

(4.13)

Proof.

We can show the equality directly. Let
x\in\bigcap_{i\in I}\mathcal{C}_{i}^{*}
. Then,

\displaystyle\begin{split}x\in\mathcal{C}_{i}^{*}\;\forall i\in I\quad&\Leftrightarrow\quad\innerproduct{x}{c_i}\geq 0\;\forall c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}\;\forall i\in I\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\sum_{i\in I}\innerproduct{x}{c_i}\geq 0\;\forall c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}\\
&\Leftrightarrow\quad\innerproduct{x}{\sum_{i\in I}c_i}\geq 0\;\forall c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad x\in\left(\sum_{i\in I}\mathcal{C}_{i}\right)^{*}.\end{split}

(4.14)

∎

Recall that a convex cone
C\subseteq V
is called
pointed
if for all
nonzero
x\in C
,
-x\notin C
.

Lemma 4.5
.

A cone
C
is pointed if and only if there exists an element
f
in the dual space
such that
f(x)>0
for all nonzero
x\in C
.

Proof.

Let
x,-x\in C
and
f\in C^{*}
such that
f(x)>0
for all nonzero
x\in C
.
Because
f
is linear
f(-x)=-f(x)<0
, which is a contradiction.

Conversely, let
C
be pointed, then
(C\setminus\{0\})\cap((-C)\setminus\{0\})=\emptyset
.
By the Hahn-Banach Theorem (as formulated in
[
39
, Theorem 7.8.4]
), there exists a linear map such that
f(x)>0
for all
x\in C\setminus\{0\}
.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Consider the convex cone
\mathcal{C}_{2}=\{C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})\;|\;\Tr_{B}\left[C\right]\propto_{\mathbb{C}}\openone\}
. Its dual is

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}^{*}=\left\{A\otimes\openone\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})\;|\;A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\openone\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{B}),\;\Tr\left[A\right]=0\right\}.

(4.15)

Proof.

Let us call this set
\mathcal{A}=\left\{A\otimes\openone\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})\;|\;A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\;\Tr\left[A\right]=0\right\}
. The following calculation shows that
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{2}^{*}
: let
A\otimes\openone\in\mathcal{A}
and
C\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
, then:

\displaystyle\Tr\left[\left(A\otimes\openone\right)C\right]=\Tr_{A}\left[\Tr_{B}\left[\left(A\otimes\openone\right)C\right]\right]=\Tr\left[A\Tr_{B}\left[\openone C\right]\right]=z\Tr\left[A\openone\right]=0.

(4.16)

To see that they are equal, note that
\mathcal{C}_{2}
(and thus the orthogonal
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{*}

[
2
]
) and
\mathcal{A}
are real subspaces of the real vector space
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
. We will calculate the dimension of each and see they are the same. The real dimension of
\mathcal{A}
is just the real dimension of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
minus the dimension subtracted by the two real (one complex) linear conditions
\Tr\left[A\right]=0
. That is

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{A}=\dim\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})-2=2d_{A}^{2}-2.

(4.17)

To find the dimension of
\mathcal{C}^{*}_{2}
, we first find the dimension of
\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Recall that this set is defined by the condition
\Tr_{B}\left[C\right]\propto_{\mathbb{C}}\openone
. This corresponds to
2d_{A}(d_{A}-1)
equations (real and imaginary parts of non diagonal terms equal to 0) plus

. That is because the condition is proportionality, not equality, so we first fix the real and imaginary components of the first diagonal element and then every other diagonal element will have to have the same real and imaginary components, for a total of

. Thus the dimension is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dim\mathcal{C}_{2}&=\dim\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})-2d_{A}(d_{A}-1)-2d_{A}+2\\
&=\dim\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})-2d_{A}^{2}+2d_{A}-2d_{A}+2\\
&=\dim\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})-2d_{A}^{2}+2.\end{split}

(4.18)

The dimension of the orthogonal complement is the dimension of the total space minus this, thus

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{C}^{*}_{2}=\dim\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})-\dim\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})+2d_{A}^{2}-2=2d_{A}^{2}-2.

(4.19)

Since this two sets

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{*}_{2}=\mathcal{A}=\left\{A\otimes\openone\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})\;|\;A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\;\Tr\left[A\right]=0\right\}.

(4.20)

∎

The partial transpose map, denoted here by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr_{A}\left[T_{A}(K)C\right]&=\Tr_{A}\left[K\;T_{A}(C)\right]\\

Proof.

Recall that the transpose is a basis dependent operation. These two properties are trivial to check if we expand the equations in a product basis that includes the basis over which we are transposing. Alternatively, we can use tensor network notation

First, we can show using

Proposition 4.10
.

The cone of states over time

Proof.

First, we show that

The properties of pointed and spanning are such that if the primal cone has one, the dual has the other

4.3
Symmetry

We call an optimal quantum cost symmetric if

\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)=\mathcal{K}(\sigma,\rho),\quad\forall\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{A,B}).

(4.48)

A naive guess or first thought might suggest that symmetry of the cost matrix under input–output exchange, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}K\mathcal{S}=K
, guarantees symmetry of the associated cost. We will see that this not the case. To understand this, we first address another natural question, namely whether the set of states over time itself is symmetric. In other words, we ask whether swapping the input and output space in the coupling always produces a valid state over time for the reversed pair of states, that is, whether
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{Q}(\rho,\sigma)\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{Q}(\sigma,\rho)
.

There is a clear asymmetry in the case when, for example,
\rho
is full rank and
\sigma
is pure, where a single channel that brings
\rho
to
\sigma
exists. Despite that, as seen in
Theorem

2.3
, if the input state is not full rank a single state over time can correspond to multiple channels. Therefore, in the case where
\sigma
is pure, it could be that the single channel that brings
\rho
to
\sigma
inverts into all the channels that bring
\sigma
to
\rho
.

In the following example we perform specific calculations for the case where both states are qubits. We observe, in examples
iii), iv)
, that some states over time cannot be time inverted, thus strongly suggesting that
\mathcal{S}K\mathcal{S}=K
is not a sufficient condition on the cost matrix to give rise to symmetry of the cost.

, with

\sigma_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1+p&p-1\\
p-1&1-p\end{bmatrix},\quad\sigma_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1-p&1-p\\
1-p&1+p\end{bmatrix}.

(4.59)

This Jamiołkowski matrix corresponds to another measure-and-prepare map, this time with non-orthogonal measurements since
\Tr\left[\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}\right]=p(1-p)
.

The example shows a type of channels where the symmetry of the set of states over time is broken: channels which reduce the coherence of the input states. In
Section

5
we discuss an example where this asymmetry is numerically shown in the cost function, rather than the set of couplings, acting as a proof that
K(\rho,\sigma)
is not a symmetric function for symmetric cost matrices.

4.4
Triangle inequality

We will state a condition for the fulfilment of the triangle inequality. For this purpose, we consider
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{B}:\mathcal{H}_{C})
from
Definition

2.7
and its dual,
\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{B}:\mathcal{H}_{C})^{*}
. This dual provides us with a cone with respect to which we can define a partial order for cost matrices:

Theorem 4.16
.

Consider Hilbert subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}
,
\mathcal{H}_{B}
and
\mathcal{H}_{C}
. The inequality

\mathcal{K}_{AB}(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})+\mathcal{K}_{BC}(\rho_{B},\rho_{C})\geq\mathcal{K}_{AC}(\rho_{A},\rho_{C})

(4.60)

will be fulfilled for all
\rho_{i}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{i})
,
i\in\left\{A,B,C\right\}
if the cost matrices fulfil the following identity:

\displaystyle K_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C}+\openone_{A}\otimes K_{BC}-K_{AC}\otimes\openone_{B}\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}_{A}:\mathcal{H}_{B}:\mathcal{H}_{C})^{*}.

(4.61)

Proof.

Consider first an admissible Jamiołkowski matrix in systems
AB
and a cost matrix
K_{AB}
. Because
\openone_{B}=\Tr_{C}\left[J_{BC}\right]
for any admissible Jamiołkowski matrix in systems
BC
, and the partial associativity of the Jordan product
[
23
,
17
]
we can rewrite the cost as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr\left[K_{AB}(\rho\star J_{AB})\right]&=\Tr\left[K_{AB}((\rho\star J_{AB})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes\Tr_{C}\left[J_{BC}\right]))\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[(K_{AB}\star(\rho\star J_{AB}))(\openone_{A}\otimes\Tr_{C}\left[J_{BC}\right])\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[((K_{AB}\star(\rho\star J_{AB}))\otimes\openone_{C})(\openone_{A}\otimes J_{BC})\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[(K_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})((\rho\star(J_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C}))\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J_{BC}))\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[\left(K_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C}\right)(\rho\star((J_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J_{BC})))\right].\end{split}

(4.62)

Similarly, because if a channel yields
\sigma
as the image of
\rho
its Jamiołkowski matrix will fulfil
\Tr_{A}\left[\rho\star J_{AB}\right]=\sigma
we can operate the cost for any admissible Jamiołkowski matrices and cost
K_{BC}
as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr\left[K_{BC}(\sigma\star J_{BC})\right]&=\Tr\left[K_{BC}((\Tr_{A}\left[\rho\star J_{AB}\right]\otimes\openone_{C})\star J_{BC})\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[(J_{BC}\star K_{BC})(\Tr_{A}\left[\rho\star J_{AB}\right]\otimes\openone_{C})\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[((\openone_{A}\otimes J_{BC})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes K_{BC}))(\rho\star J_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[(\openone_{A}\otimes K_{BC})((\rho\star(J_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C}))\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J_{BC}))\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[(\openone_{A}\otimes K_{BC})(\rho\star((J_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J_{BC})))\right].\end{split}

(4.63)

Finally, for systems
AC
, consider the link product
[
11
]
of any two Jamiołkowski matrices as the Jamiołkowski matrix of
AC
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr\left[K_{AC}(\rho\star J_{AC})\right]&=\Tr\left[K_{AC}(\rho\star\Tr_{B}\left[(J_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J_{BC})\right])\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[(K_{AC}\star\rho)\Tr_{B}\left[(J_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J_{BC})\right]\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[((K_{AC}\otimes\openone_{B})\star\rho)((J_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J_{BC}))\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[(K_{AC}\otimes\openone_{B})(\rho\star((J_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J_{BC})))\right].\end{split}

(4.64)

Let
K^{\prime}=K_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C}+\openone_{A}\otimes K_{BC}-K_{AC}\otimes\openone_{B}
. With these 3 equalities in hand, we can consider 3 optimal Jamiołkowski matrices, indicated by the superindex
o
, for the costs
\mathcal{K}_{AB}
and
\mathcal{K}_{BC}
and
\mathcal{K}_{AC}
. Then by using the previous expressions we can show that:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{K}_{AC}=&\Tr\left[K_{AC}(\rho\star J^{o}_{AC})\right]\leq\Tr\left[K_{AC}(\rho\star J_{AC})\right]\\
=&\Tr\left[K_{AC}\otimes\openone_{B}(\rho\star((J^{o}_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J^{o}_{BC})))\right]\\
=&\Tr\left[(K_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C}+\openone_{A}\otimes K_{BC}-K^{\prime})(\rho\star((J^{o}_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J^{o}_{BC})))\right]\\
=&\Tr\left[(\openone_{A}\otimes K_{BC})(\rho\star((J^{o}_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J^{o}_{BC})))\right]\\
+&\Tr\left[\left(K_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C}\right)(\rho\star((J^{o}_{AB}\otimes\openone_{C})\star(\openone_{A}\otimes J^{o}_{BC})))\right]\\
-&\Tr\left[K^{\prime}\left(\rho\star\left(J_{AB}^{o}\star J_{BC}^{o}\right)\right)\right]\\
=&\Tr\left[K_{AB}(\rho\star J^{o}_{AB})\right]+\Tr\left[K_{BC}(\sigma\star J^{o}_{BC})\right]-\Tr\left[K^{\prime}\left(\rho\star\left(J_{AB}^{o}\star J_{BC}^{o}\right)\right)\right]\\
=&\mathcal{K}_{AB}+\mathcal{K}_{BC}-\Tr\left[K^{\prime}\left(\rho\star\left(J_{AB}^{o}\star J_{BC}^{o}\right)\right)\right].\end{split}

(4.65)

Finally, because
K^{\prime}
is in the dual of
\mathcal{Q}_{3}
,
\Tr\left[K^{\prime}\left(\rho\star\left(J_{AB}^{o}\star J_{BC}^{o}\right)\right)\right]\geq 0
and therefore
\mathcal{K}_{AB}+\mathcal{K}_{BC}\geq\mathcal{K}_{AC}
.
∎

For the sake of completeness, this general statement can be converted to a statement about
the triangle inequality for the cost
\mathcal{K}
associated to a given cost matrix
K
:

Corollary 4.17
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space,
K\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H})
and
\mathcal{K}
the quantum optimal cost associated to
K
.
Then
\mathcal{K}
fulfils the triangle inequality if

K\otimes\openone_{3}+\openone_{1}\otimes K-K\otimes\openone_{2}\in\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{H}:\mathcal{H})^{*},

(4.66)

where the subindices indicate different copies of the same Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

4.5
Structural properties of the transport cost

We would like to prove subadditivity of the cost for both inputs, unfortunately we can only see it for the second input. The following proposition shows this result as well as two consequences of the triangle inequality.

Proposition 4.18
.

Let
p_{x}
be a probability distribution. The optimal quantum cost fulfils the following:

i)

Convexity in the second argument:
\mathcal{K}\left(\rho,\sum_{x}p_{x}\sigma_{x}\right)\leq\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}\left(\rho,\sigma_{x}\right).

Moreover, if the triangle inequality is fulfilled:

ii)

\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}(\rho_{x},\sigma)\leq\mathcal{K}\left(\sum_{x}p_{x}\rho_{x},\sigma\right)+\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}\left(\rho_{x},\sum_{x^{\prime}}p_{x^{\prime}}\rho_{x^{\prime}}\right)
.

iii)

\mathcal{K}\left(\sum_{x}p_{x}\rho_{x},\sigma\right)\leq\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}\left(\rho_{x},\sigma\right)+\sum_{x^{\prime}}p_{x^{\prime}}\mathcal{K}(\sum_{x}p_{x}\rho_{x},\rho_{x^{\prime}}).

Proof.

Consider optimal Jamiołkowski matrices
J_{o}
for
\left(\rho,\sum_{x}p_{x}\sigma_{x}\right)
and
J_{x}
for
\left(\rho,\sigma_{x}\right)
. Note that
J_{\Sigma}=\sum_{x}p_{x}J_{x}
is a Jamiołkowski matrix with an associated channel that fulfils
\mathcal{E}_{J_{\Sigma}}(\rho)=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sigma_{x}.
Thus,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{K}\left(\rho,\sum_{x}p_{x}\sigma_{x}\right)&=\Tr\left[K\left(\rho\star J_{o}\right)\right]\leq\Tr\left[K\left(\rho\star J_{\Sigma}\right)\right]=\Tr\left[K\left(\rho\star\left(\sum_{x}p_{x}J_{x}\right)\right)\right]\\
&=\sum_{x}p_{x}\Tr\left[K\left(\rho\star J_{x}\right)\right]=\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma_{x}),\end{split}

(4.67)

where we used the bilinearity of the Jordan product
[
23
]
and the linearity of the trace.

The second property is a direct consequence of the triangle inequality:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}\left(\rho_{x},\sigma\right)&\leq\sum_{x}p_{x}\left(\mathcal{K}\left(\rho_{x},\sum_{x^{\prime}}p_{x^{\prime}}\rho_{x^{\prime}}\right)+\mathcal{K}\left(\sum_{x^{\prime}}p_{x^{\prime}}\rho_{x^{\prime}},\sigma\right)\right)\\
&=\mathcal{K}\left(\sum_{x}p_{x}\rho_{x},\sigma\right)+\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}\left(\rho_{x},\sum_{x^{\prime}}p_{x^{\prime}}\rho_{x^{\prime}}\right).\end{split}

(4.68)

Similarly, we can show the third property. Let
\rho=\sum_{x}p_{x}\rho_{x}
. Then,
\forall\;x

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{x}&\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)\leq p_{x}\mathcal{K}(\rho,\rho_{x})+p_{x}\mathcal{K}(\rho_{x},\sigma)\\
\Rightarrow\quad\sum_{x}p_{x}&\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}(\rho,\rho_{x})+\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}(\rho_{x},\sigma)\\
\Rightarrow\quad&\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}(\rho,\rho_{x})+\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}(\rho_{x},\sigma),\end{split}

(4.69)

where we first used the triangle inequality and then we added all the inequalities together.
∎

Remark 4.19
.

A similar proof does not work for convexity on the first input and joint subadditivity because of the following. We will use subadditivity on the first input as an example. Let
J_{o}
be the optimal Jamiołkowski matrix for
\left(\sum_{x}p_{x}\rho_{x},\sigma\right)
. Starting on the left hand side we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}{\left(\sum_{x}p_{x}\rho_{x},\sigma\right)}=\Tr\left[K\left(\sum_{x}p_{x}\rho_{x}\star J_{o}\right)\right]=\sum_{x}p_{x}\Tr\left[K\left(\rho_{x}\star J_{o}\right)\right].

(4.70)

At this point we can observe that the channel associated to
J_{o}
does not necessarily have output
\sigma
for each
\rho_{x}
(unless
\sigma
is pure) and we can not upper bound the associated cost with anything defined with the optimal channels for the pairs
(\rho_{x},\sigma)
. In contrast, in the proof of
Proposition

4.18
it was possible to define the joint channel
J_{\Sigma}
because we could send
\rho
to each element
\sigma_{x}
of the ensemble
\{(p_{x},\sigma_{x})\}
and that would in total define a channel that sends
\rho
to
\sigma
.

Moreover, we can show joint convexity is false in general. Let
\sigma_{x}=\mathcal{E}_{J_{o}}(\rho_{x})
and observe that letting
\sigma=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sigma_{x}
in the previous equation we obtain:

\mathcal{K}{\left(\sum_{x}p_{x}\rho_{x},\sum_{x}p_{x}\sigma_{x}\right)}\geq\sum_{x}p_{x}\mathcal{K}(\rho_{x},\sigma_{x}).

(4.71)

This joint concavity is not general in the sense that we have the relation only for
\sigma_{x}=\mathcal{E}_{J_{o}}(\rho_{x})
, where the ensemble
\{(p_{x},\rho_{x})\}
can be arbitrarily chosen, but the channel must be the one associated to the optimal Jamiołkowski matrix for
\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)
.

For the next proposition, we consider bipartite initial and final spaces, and for simplicity we assume them to be the same:
\mathcal{H}_{A}=\mathcal{H}_{B}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}
. In this setting we want to study how the cost for product states relates to related costs in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
for different configurations of the joint cost matrix.

Proposition 4.20
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{i}
be Hilbert spaces and
\rho_{i},\sigma_{i}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}_{i})
with
i=1,2
. Let
K_{12}
be a cost matrix in
\mathcal{B}((\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2})^{\otimes 2})
, and
K_{i}
be cost matrices in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}_{i})
. Then the optimal transport cost of
\mathcal{K}\left(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}\right)
fulfils the following:

i)

If
K_{12}=K_{1}\otimes K_{2}
, then
\mathcal{K}(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2})\leq\mathcal{K}(\rho_{1},\sigma_{1})\mathcal{K}(\rho_{2},\sigma_{2})
.

ii)

If
K_{12}=K_{1}\otimes\openone_{2}+\openone_{1}\otimes K_{2}
, then
\mathcal{K}(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2})\leq\mathcal{K}(\rho_{1},\sigma_{1})+\mathcal{K}(\rho_{2},\sigma_{2}).

Proof.

Objects in different subsystems commute and
J_{1}\otimes J_{2}
is admissible for
\left(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}\right)
if
J_{i}
is admissible for
\rho_{i},\sigma_{i}
,
i=1,2
. To show the first inequality, consider two optimal Jamiołkowski matrices
J_{1}^{o},J_{2}^{o}
that optimise the costs between
\rho_{i},\sigma_{i}
with cost matrix
K_{i}
,
i=1,2
. Then,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{K}(\rho_{1},\sigma_{1})&\cdot\mathcal{K}(\rho_{2},\sigma_{2})=\Tr\left[K_{1}\left(\rho_{1}\star J_{1}^{o}\right)\right]\cdot\Tr\left[K_{2}\left(\rho_{2}\star J_{2}^{o}\right)\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[\left(K_{1}\left(\rho_{1}\star J_{1}^{o}\right)\right)\otimes\left(K_{2}\left(\rho_{2}\star J_{2}^{o}\right)\right)\right]=\Tr\left[\left(K_{1}\otimes K_{2}\right)\left(\left(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\right)\star\left(J_{1}^{o}\otimes J_{2}^{o}\right)\right)\right]\\
&\geq\mathcal{K}(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}).\end{split}

(4.72)

For the second inequality, consider the same Jamiołkowski matrices as before. Then

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{K}(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2},\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2})&=\Tr\left[K_{12}\left(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\right)\star J_{12}^{o}\right]\\
&\leq\Tr\left[\left(K_{1}\otimes\openone_{2}+\openone_{1}\otimes K_{2}\right)\left(\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}\right)\star\left(J_{1}^{o}\otimes J_{2}^{o}\right)\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[K_{1}\rho_{1}\star J_{1}^{o}\right]\Tr\left[\rho_{2}\star J_{2}^{o}\right]+\Tr\left[\rho_{1}\star J_{1}^{o}\right]\Tr\left[K_{2}\rho_{2}\star J_{2}^{o}\right]\\
&=\mathcal{K}(\rho_{1},\sigma_{1})+\mathcal{K}(\rho_{2},\sigma_{2}).\end{split}

(4.73)

∎

5
Unitary invariant cost

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space of dimension
d
. Here we consider
cost matrices which yield unitary invariant quantum optimal costs, that is

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)=\mathcal{K}(U\rho U^{*},U\sigma U^{*})\quad\forall U\in U(d),\,\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}).

(5.1)

This follows automatically if the cost matrix is invariant under simultaneous
unitary transformations of both systems:

K=(U\otimes U)K(U^{*}\otimes U^{*})\quad\forall U\in U(d).

(5.2)

In this section, we consider the case where the cost is unitarily invariant (UI), meaning it remains unchanged under a common unitary transformation of both the input and output states:

\displaystyle\kappa(\rho,\mathcal{E})=\kappa(U\rho U^{*},\mathcal{U}\circ\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}^{-1})\quad\forall U\in U(d),\,\rho,\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{H}).

(5.3)

where we have defined the unitary channel
\mathcal{U}(X)=UXU^{*}
, and
we denote by
U(d)
the group of unitary operators acting on the Hilbert space, of dimension
d
. We show the relation between the maps and states in the following commutative diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U\rho U^{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U\sigma U^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{E}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{U}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{U}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{U}\circ\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}^{-1}}

Throughout this section, we show that unitary invariance fully determines the cost matrix up to a single parameter. Later, we compute the cost for several relevant quantum channels, and derive a simplified optimisation problem to compute the cost
\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)
valid for arbitrary states within the UI setting. In
Proposition

5.10
, we obtain analytic expressions for the optimal cost in the special case of commuting states.
Section

5.2
explores the striking modification of the cost induced by embedding the problem into a higher-dimensional Hilbert space, and study its asymptotic behaviour. Finally, in
Example

5.5
, we present an example that illustrates several noteworthy features.

The following proposition shows that there is a single cost matrix (up to positive scaling) with the UI property:

Proposition 5.1
.

The only cost matrices that belong to the dual to the cone of states over time,
assign cost
0
to the identity channel according to
Proposition

4.1

and commute with unitaries of the form
U\otimes U
are positive multiples of

K_{0}=d\openone-\mathcal{S}.

(5.4)

Proof.

First, let us prove that if
J
is the Jamiołkowski matrix whose channel takes
\rho
to
\sigma=\mathcal{E}(\rho)
,
then the Jamiołkowski matrix
corresponding to the associated channel

\mathcal{E}_{U}^{\prime}=\mathcal{U}\circ\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{U}^{-1}
which maps
U\rho U^{*}
to
U\sigma U^{*}
, is given
by
J_{U}^{\prime}=(U\otimes U)J(U^{*}\otimes U^{*})
.

For this purpose we start from the definition in
Eq.

1.4

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{U}^{\prime}&=\left(\operatorname{id}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{U}^{\prime}\right)\left(\mathcal{S}\right)=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\mathcal{U})\circ(\operatorname{id}\otimes\mathcal{E})\circ(\operatorname{id}\otimes\mathcal{U}^{-1})(\mathcal{S})\\
&=(\openone\otimes U)\,(\operatorname{id}\otimes\mathcal{E})\!\left((\openone\otimes U^{*})\mathcal{S}(\openone\otimes U)\right)(\openone\otimes U)^{*}\end{split}

(5.5)

Recall that the swap operator satisfies
\mathcal{S}(X\otimes Y)\mathcal{S}=Y\otimes X
, i.e.

(I\otimes V)\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}(V\otimes I)
. Using this relation twice we can write

(\openone\otimes U^{*})\mathcal{S}(\openone\otimes U)=\mathcal{S}(U^{*}\otimes\openone)(\openone\otimes U)=\mathcal{S}(\openone\otimes U)(U^{*}\otimes\openone)=(U\otimes\openone)\mathcal{S}(U^{*}\otimes\openone)
, and thereby pull the unitaries
out of the action of the map
\mathcal{E}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{U}^{\prime}&=(\openone\otimes U)(U\otimes\openone)\,(\operatorname{id}\otimes\mathcal{E})(\mathcal{S})\,(U^{*}\otimes\openone)(\openone\otimes U^{*})\\
&=(U\otimes U)\,J\,(U^{*}\otimes U^{*}).\end{split}

(5.6)

Now we can impose the UI of the cost function,
\kappa(\rho,\mathcal{E})=\kappa(U\rho U^{*},\mathcal{E}_{U}^{\prime})
,
to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr\left[K_{0}\left(\rho\star J\right)\right]&=\Tr\left[K_{0}\left(\left(U\rho U^{*}\right)\star\left((U\otimes U)J(U^{*}\otimes U^{*})\right)\right)\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[(U\otimes U)K_{0}(U^{*}\otimes U^{*})(\rho\star J)\right].\end{split}

(5.7)

We require this equality to hold for all choices of input states and channels, i.e. for all elements of the set of states over time
Q=\rho\star J
. Since this set is spanning
(
Proposition

4.10
), the scalar equality
Eq.

5.7
translates into the operator equality
K_{0}=(U\otimes U)K_{0}(U^{*}\otimes U^{*})
or equivalently
\left[U\otimes U,K_{0}\right]=0
.
This must hold for all unitaries.

From the representation theory of
GL(d)
, and because the set of unitaries generates the whole of
GL(d)
as an algebra, whose operations leave the commutator invariant, the only elements with this property are the symmetric and antisymmetric projectors. The vector space generated by these two projectors also has
\{\openone,\mathcal{S}\}
as a basis
[
56
]
. Therefore
K_{0}=a(b\openone-\mathcal{S})
with real
a
and
b
, to preserve Hermiticity.

We can now impose the second condition,
Proposition

4.1
:

\displaystyle 0=\Tr_{B}\left[\mathcal{S}\star K_{0}\right]=a\Tr_{B}\left[\mathcal{S}\star(b\openone-\mathcal{S})\right]=a\Tr_{B}\left[b\mathcal{S}-\openone\right]=a(b-d)\openone.

(5.8)

We find that
b=d
. Finally, we see that the positivity of the cost requires
a
to be positive:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr\left[K_{0}(\rho\star J)\right]&=a\Tr\left[(d\openone-\mathcal{S})(\rho\star J)\right]\\
&=ad\Tr\left[\rho\star J\right]-a\Tr\left[\mathcal{S}(\rho\star J)\right]\\
&=a\left[d-\Tr\left[\rho(\mathcal{S}\star J)\right]\right].\end{split}

(5.9)

The last term can be upper bounded by switching to the Choi matrix representation of the problem using
Lemma

4.7
. Recall that
\mathcal{S}^{T_{A}}=\outerproduct{\Phi_+}{\Phi_+}
, where
\ket{\Phi_+}=\sum_{i}\ket{ii}
is the unnormalised maximally entangled state and is such that
\Tr\left[\outerproduct{\Phi_+}{\Phi_+}\right]=d
. Then

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr\left[\rho(\mathcal{S}\star J)\right]=&\Tr\left[{\mathcal{S}^{T_{A}}}^{T_{A}}(\rho\star J)\right]=\Tr\left[\outerproduct{\Phi_+}{\Phi_+}(\rho\star J)^{T_{A}}\right]=\expectationvalue{\rho^T\star J^{T_A}}{\Phi_+}\\
=&\expectationvalue{\rho^T\star C}{\Phi_+}\leq d\Tr\left[\rho^{T}\star C\right]=d.\end{split}

(5.10)

Thus we have that
a
times a positive constant has to be positive, therefore
a
is positive.
∎

We will also use the normalised version of
K_{0}
:
\tilde{K}_{0}=\openone-\frac{1}{d}\mathcal{S}
, so that the maximum achievable cost is 1. With this specific cost matrix, there are some simple ways to write the cost associated to a channel depending on which representation of the channel we take. These forms will be useful later.

Remark 5.2
.

Let
\rho
be a state in a finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and the cost matrix
\tilde{K}_{0}=\openone-\frac{1}{d}\mathcal{S}
. Consider a channel
\mathcal{E}
with associated Jamiołkowski and Choi matrices
J
,
C
, respectively and Kraus representation
\{E_{k}\}
. Moreover, let
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}
for some basis
\{\ket{i}\}
,
\ket{\rho}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\ket{ii}
its vectorized form and
\ket{\Phi_+}=\sum_{i}\ket{ii}
is the unnormalised maximally entangled state. Then

\displaystyle\kappa(\rho,\mathcal{E})=\Tr\left[\tilde{K}_{0}(\rho\star J)\right]

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{d}\expectationvalue{\rho^T\star C}{\Phi_+}

(5.11)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{d}\mathfrak{Re}(\bra{\rho}C\ket{\Phi_+})

(5.12)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{ij}\frac{p_{i}+p_{j}}{2}\bra{i}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j}

(5.13)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}{p_{i}}\sum_{j}\mathfrak{Re}(\bra{i}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j})

(5.14)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k}\mathfrak{Re}\left(\Tr\left[E_{k}^{*}\right]\Tr\left[E_{k}\rho\right]\right).

(5.15)

Proof.

The term
1
in every equation comes from the trace of the states over time with the identity, which is always one because the partial trace of a state over time is a state. The other part is associated to
\Tr\left[\mathcal{S}(\rho\star J)\right]
, and we will focus on that.

Eq.

5.11
and
Eq.

5.12
are a direct consequence of
Lemma

4.7
, recalling that
\mathcal{S}^{T_{A}}=\outerproduct{\Phi_+}{\Phi_+}
.

Eq.

5.13
comes from the definition of the Jamiołkowski matrix,
J=\sum_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{j}{i})
and
Theorem

2.3
, which shows that in the product basis of the diagonal basis of
\rho
,
\rho\star J=\sum_{ij}\frac{p_{i}+p_{j}}{2}\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{j}{i})
. Then we define the swap operator in this product basis,
\mathcal{S}=\sum_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\outerproduct{i'}{j'}\otimes\outerproduct{j'}{i'}
and calculate
\Tr\left[S(\rho\star J)\right]
:

\begin{split}\Tr\left[S(\rho\star J)\right]&=\Tr\left[\sum_{ij}\frac{p_{i}+p_{j}}{2}\sum_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\left(\outerproduct{i'}{j'}\otimes\outerproduct{j'}{i'}\right)\left(\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{j}{i})\right)\right]\\
&=\sum_{ij}\frac{p_{i}+p_{j}}{2}\sum_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\delta_{ij^{\prime}}\delta_{ji^{\prime}}\bra{i'}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{j}{i})\ket{j'}=\sum_{ij}\frac{p_{i}+p_{j}}{2}\bra{i}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j}.\end{split}

(5.16)

Finally, for
Eq.

5.15
consider
J
written as a function of the Kraus operators:

J=(\operatorname{id}\otimes\mathcal{E})(\mathcal{S})=\sum_{k}(\openone\otimes E_{k})\mathcal{S}(\openone\otimes E_{k}^{*})=\sum_{k}(E_{k}^{*}\otimes E_{k})\mathcal{S}.

(5.17)

Then we add
\rho
and the
\mathcal{S}
from the cost:

\begin{split}\Tr&\left[\mathcal{S}(\rho\star J)\right]=\sum_{k}\Tr\left[\mathcal{S}\left(\rho\star(E_{k}^{*}\otimes E_{k})\mathcal{S}\right)\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\left(\Tr\left[\mathcal{S}\left(\rho(E_{k}^{*}\otimes E_{k})\mathcal{S}\right)\right]+\Tr\left[\mathcal{S}\left((E_{k}^{*}\otimes E_{k})\mathcal{S}\rho\right)\right]\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\left(\Tr\left[\rho E_{k}^{*}\otimes E_{k}\right]+\Tr\left[E_{k}\rho\otimes E_{k}^{*}\right]\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}\left(\Tr\left[\rho E_{k}^{*}\right]\Tr\left[E_{k}\right]+h.c.\right)\\
&=\sum_{k}\mathfrak{Re}\left(\Tr\left[E_{k}^{*}\right]\Tr\left[\rho E_{k}\right]\right).\end{split}

(5.18)

This concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

It is well known that a single channel can have multiple Kraus representations
[
40
, Theorem 8.2]
. By
Eq.

5.15
, for every Kraus representation of a channel

1-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k}\mathfrak{Re}\left(\Tr\left[E_{k}^{*}\right]\Tr\left[\rho E_{k}\right]\right)=\Tr\left[S(\rho\star J)\right],

(5.19)

and the Jamiołkowski matrix is unique, therefore different Kraus representations of the same channel have the same associated cost.

We can also show this explicitly. If two Kraus representations
\{E_{i}\}
,
\{F_{j}\}
give rise to the same quantum channel, then there exists a unitary
U=(U_{ij})
such that
E_{i}=\sum_{j}u_{ij}F_{j}

[
40
]
. Then

\begin{split}\sum_{i}\mathfrak{Re}\left(\Tr\left[E_{i}^{*}\right]\Tr\left[\rho E_{i}\right]\right)&=\sum_{i}\mathfrak{Re}\left(\Tr\left[\sum_{j}\bar{U}_{ij}F_{j}^{*}\right]\Tr\left[\rho\sum_{j^{\prime}}U_{ij^{\prime}}F_{j^{\prime}}\right]\right)\lx@nobreakspace\\
&=\sum_{jj^{\prime}}\left(\sum_{i}U_{ij^{\prime}}\bar{U}_{ij}\right)\mathfrak{Re}\left(\Tr\left[F_{j}^{*}\right]\Tr\left[\rho F_{j^{\prime}}\right]\right)\\
&=\sum_{jj^{\prime}}\left(U^{T}\left(U^{T}\right)^{*}\right)_{j^{\prime}j}\mathfrak{Re}\left(\Tr\left[F_{j}^{*}\right]\Tr\left[\rho F_{j^{\prime}}\right]\right)\\
&=\sum_{jj^{\prime}}\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\mathfrak{Re}\left(\Tr\left[F_{j}^{*}\right]\Tr\left[\rho F_{j^{\prime}}\right]\right)=\sum_{j}\mathfrak{Re}\left(\Tr\left[F_{j}^{*}\right]\Tr\left[\rho F_{j}\right]\right).\end{split}

(5.20)

In the following examples we calculate the cost associated to two important channels using the unitary invariant cost matrix.
Example

5.5
in particular allows for some observations on the properties of the unitary invariant cost.

Example 5.4

(The replacement channel)
.

Consider
\mathcal{E}(x)=(\Tr x)\sigma
. Then the associated Jamiołkowski matrix is
\openone\otimes\sigma=\sigma_{B}
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\kappa(\rho,\mathcal{E}_{R})=\Tr\left[(d\openone-\mathcal{S})\rho_{A}\star\sigma_{B}\right]=&d-\Tr\left[\rho\sigma\right]\geq d-1,\end{split}

(5.21)

where the last inequality can be seen, for example, using the trace and operator norms of

and

\Tr\left[\rho\sigma\right]\leq\norm{\rho}_{tr}\norm{\sigma}_{op}\leq 1.

Example 5.5

(Unitary channels)
.

Consider now the class of unitary channels:
\mathcal{E}_{U}(x)=UxU^{*}
, where
U
is a unitary operator. The associated Jamiołkowski matrix is
J_{U}=U_{B}\mathcal{S}U_{B}^{*}
, where
U_{B}=\openone\otimes U
. We can use

5.15
in

5.2
to calculate the cost, since the Kraus operators associated to a Unitary channel are just
\{U\}
. This cost is then

\displaystyle\begin{split}\kappa(\rho,\mathcal{E}_{U})=&\Tr\left[(\rho_{A}\star(U_{B}\mathcal{S}U_{B}^{*}))(d\openone-\mathcal{S})\right]=d-\mathfrak{Re}\left(\Tr\left[U^{*}\right]\Tr\left[\rho U\right]\right)\end{split}

(5.22)

We can find an expression for the optimal
U
when
\rho
and
\sigma
are pure states. Due to the unitary invariance, w.l.o.g.
\rho=\outerproduct{0}{0}
and
\sigma=\outerproduct{\varphi}{\varphi}
where
\ket{\varphi}=\alpha\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\ket{1}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The optimal unitary (in terms of maximising its trace) will leave
\left<\{\ket{0},\ket{1}\}\right>
invariant and have 1 in the diagonal elements outside this subspace. Therefore the optimal (i.e. largest) value is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Re}\left(\Tr\left[U^{*}\right]\Tr\left[\rho U\right]\right)=\alpha(d-2+2\alpha)

(5.23)

with associated cost

\mathcal{K}_{U}(\rho,\sigma)=d-\alpha(d-2+2\alpha)=d(1-\alpha)+2\alpha(1-\alpha)=(1-\alpha)(d+2\alpha).

(5.24)

Note that this optimum value is influenced by the action of the unitary on an invariant subspace orthogonal to the subspace where our state evolves; in particular it depends on its dimension. We will later address this further and show how we can remove this dependency in the limit when
d\rightarrow\infty
.

If we now further restrict the problem to
d=2
, then this becomes
2(1-\alpha)(1+\alpha)=2(1-\alpha^{2})=2(1-\absolutevalue{\braket{0}{\varphi}}^{2})=2T(\outerproduct{0}{0},\outerproduct{\varphi}{\varphi})^{2}
, where
T
is the trace distance. Since it is the square of a distance, it cannot be a distance, as it violates the triangle inequality.

In the limit of high

this quantity approximately becomes

, which, for small angles is
d(1-\cos\theta)\approx\frac{d}{2}\theta^{2}
, which is again the square of a distance.

Importantly, the second example computes the optimal unitary cost, which can then be compared to the optimal cost over all channels. In

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\rho
,
\sigma
have joint support
\mathcal{H}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{S}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\bot}
. Then there exists an optimal channel of the unitary invariant quantum optimal transport
\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)
such that its associated Kraus operators are of the form

E=E_{S}\oplus c\Pi_{\bot},

(5.25)

where
\Pi_{\bot}
is the projector on the orthogonal, or embedding Hilbert space,
\mathcal{H}_{\bot}
. Therefore, the optimal channel acts as the identity channel on the embedding Hilbert space.

Proof.

We start by noting that our optimization problem (
3.3
) remains invariant under unitaries that act non-trivially only outside the joint support of the input and output states, i.e. they are of the form
(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})
, where
\Pi_{S}
is the projector onto
\mathcal{H}_{S}
and
U_{\bot}
are unitaries on
\mathcal{H}_{\bot}
(see
[
49
]
for a general discussion of SDP under symmetries).We show that the cost and constraints of the problem remain invariant under unitaries of this form: Given a valid Jamiołkowski matrix
J
, we can construct a new Jamiołkowski matrix
J_{U_{\bot}}=(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})J(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*})
that has the same cost and satisfies the same constraints as
J
from (
3.3
):

\begin{split}\Tr\left[(\tilde{K}_{0}\star\rho)J_{U_{\bot}}\right]&=\Tr\left[(\tilde{K}_{0}\star\rho)((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}))J((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*}))\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*}))(\tilde{K}_{0}\star\rho)((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}))J\right]\\
&=\Tr\left[(\tilde{K}_{0}\star\rho)J\right],\end{split}

(5.26)

where we have used the unitary invariance of the cost
\tilde{K}_{0}
and the fact that
\rho
and
\sigma
commute with
U_{\bot}
. Similarly, the constraints of the problem are also invariant:

\displaystyle\Tr_{A}\left[\rho J_{U_{\bot}}\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr_{A}\left[\rho((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}))J((\Pi_{S}\oplus U^{*}_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*}))\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr_{A}\left[((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*})\otimes\openone)\rho((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}))J(\openone\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*}))\right]

(5.27)

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\Tr_{A}\left[\rho J\right](\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*})=(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\sigma(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*})=\sigma,

\displaystyle\Tr_{B}\left[J_{U_{\bot}}\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr_{B}\left[((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}))J((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*}))\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr_{B}\left[(\openone\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*}))((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}))J((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*})\otimes\openone)\right]

(5.28)

\displaystyle=(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\Tr_{B}\left[J\right](\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*})=(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\openone(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*})=\openone.

Due to the linearity of the cost and constraints, it follows that a twirled matrix
J^{\prime}=\int dU_{\bot}J_{U_{\bot}}
is also a valid Jamiołkowski matrix, as it represents a convex combination of valid Jamiołkowski matrices.
That is, for any
J
we can construct a twirled version
J^{\prime}
that retains the same cost. Hence, without loss of generality, we can optimize our cost over the set of Jamiołkowski matrices satisfying the symmetry condition:
[(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}),J]=0
.

Since we wish to identify optimal Kraus operators we will work with the Choi matrix
C
: the canonical Kraus representation can be readily obtained from
C=\sum_{E}\outerproduct{\Kr}{\Kr}
, where the (unnormalized) eigenstates are
\ket{\Kr}=\sum_{rs}E_{rs}\ket{rs}
are the vectorised form of the Kraus operator
\sum_{rs}E_{rs}\outerproduct{r}{s}
. In addition, we can use a basis for the partial transpose such that the Choi matrices inherit the symmetry of the problem. In particular, we consider a basis such that
\Pi_{S}
is diagonal which will preserve the direct sum structure of
\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}
. In such a basis, the Choi matrix inherits the symmetry of the problem as follows:

\displaystyle C^{\prime}

\displaystyle=J^{\prime T_{A}}=\int dU_{\bot}J_{U_{\bot}}^{T_{A}}=\int dU_{\bot}((\Pi_{S}\oplus\bar{U}_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}))J^{T_{A}}((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{T})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*}))

\displaystyle=\int dU_{\bot}((\Pi_{S}\oplus\bar{U}_{\bot})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}))C((\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{T})\otimes(\Pi_{S}\oplus U_{\bot}^{*}))

(5.29)

where in the second equality we have used the properties of the partial trace given in Lemma
4.7
.
In order to ease the notation we will use Latin letters to label the

\mathcal{H}_{\bot}

taking

The only non-zero terms can be written as

\displaystyle C_{S}

\displaystyle:=\int dU_{\bot}(\bar{U}_{\bot}\otimes U_{\bot})C(U_{\bot}^{T}\otimes U_{\bot}^{*})=a\Pi_{\bot}\otimes\Pi_{\bot}+b\outerproduct{\Phi_\bot}{\Phi_\bot}.

(5.35)

where

is the projector onto
\mathcal{H}_{\bot}
and

is the maximally entangled state in
\mathcal{H}_{\bot}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\bot}
. In Eqs. (

-

group integral
[

\int dUU_{\alpha\beta}\bar{U}_{\nu\mu}=\frac{1}{d}\delta_{\alpha\nu}\delta_{\beta\mu}

We next show that the optimization of the cost when the input and output are embedded in a larger Hilbert space can be written in terms of a quantum map acting only on the joint support of input and output.

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}
and
\sigma
have joint support
\mathcal{H}_{S}\subseteq\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{S}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\bot}
;
with orthonormal basis
B_{S}=\left\{\ket{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
\mathcal{H}_{S}
and
B_{\bot}=\left\{\ket{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha=1}^{d_{\bot}}
of
\mathcal{H}_{\bot}
, and
d=n+d_{\bot}
.
The optimal unitarily invariant cost is

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{d}\max_{\{E_{k}\}}\left(\mathfrak{Re}\left(\sum_{k}\Tr\left[E_{k}\rho\right]\Tr\left[E_{k}^{*}\right]\right)+d_{\bot}\sqrt{\sum_{k}\absolutevalue{\Tr(\Kr_k \rho)}^{2}}\right)

(5.38)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{d}\max_{\mathcal{E}}\left(\mathfrak{Re}\left(\sum_{ij}p_{i}\bra{i}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j}\right)+d_{\bot}\sqrt{\sum_{ij}p_{i}p_{j}\bra{i}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j}}\right),

(5.39)

where the maximisation is over CPTP maps
\mathcal{E}(\bullet)=\sum_{k}E_{k}\bullet E_{k}^{*}
on

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
s.t.
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\sigma
.
Equivalently, we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)=1-\frac{1}{d}

\displaystyle\max_{C_{S}}\left(\mathfrak{Re}(\bra{\rho\vphantom{\Phi_S}}C_{S}\ket{\Phi_S})+d_{\bot}\sqrt{\bra{\rho}C_{S}\ket{\rho}}\right)

(5.40)

\displaystyle\phantom{===}\text{s.t. }C_{S}\geq 0,\ \Tr_{B}C_{S}=\openone_{S},\ \Tr\left[\rho^{T}C_{S}\right]=\sigma,

where
\ket{\Phi_S}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ket{ii}
,

\ket{\Phi_\bot}=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d_{\bot}}\ket{\alpha\alpha}
, and the input is written in vectorized form
\ket{\rho}=\sum_{ii}p_{i}\ket{ii}
.

Proof.

From
Lemma

5.6
, we can take Kraus operators to be of the form
E_{k}={E_{k}}_{S}\oplus c_{k}\Pi_{\bot}
, such that
\left\{{E_{k}}_{S}\right\}
is a set of Kraus operators restricted to the support
\mathcal{H}_{S}
and
c_{k}
form a unit vector. The non-trivial part of the cost, starting from
Eq.

5.15
, then is

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Re}\left(\sum_{k}\Tr\left[\rho E_{k}\right]\Tr\left[E_{k}^{*}\right]\right)=\mathfrak{Re}\left(\sum_{k}\Tr\left[\rho{E_{k}}_{S}\right]\Tr\left[{E_{k}}_{S}^{*}\right]+d_{\bot}\sum_{k}c_{k}^{*}\Tr\left[\rho{E_{k}}_{S}\right]\right).

(5.41)

We can remove the real part on the second term due to the phase freedom of each
c_{k}
with respect to
{E_{k}}_{S}
. This freedom will allow us to tune the phase of
c_{k}
in each Kraus operator such that
c_{k}^{*}\Tr\left[\rho{E_{k}}_{S}\right]=\absolutevalue{c_k}\absolutevalue{\Tr\cwich{\rho{\Kr_k}_S}}
, which is the maximum achievable real part.

Now fix a set of Kraus operators on the support
\left\{{E_{k}}_{S}\right\}
. Consider the vectors
\mathbf{u}=(c_{k})
and
\mathbf{v}=(\Tr\left[\rho{E_{k}}_{S}\right])
. With this notation we are maximising the inner product between
\mathbf{v}
and a unit vector
\mathbf{u}
. The Cauchy-Schwartz inequality states that
\absolutevalue{\braket{\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{v}}}\leq\norm{\mathbf{u}}\norm{\mathbf{v}}=\norm{\mathbf{v}}
and that the inequality is tight if and only if
\mathbf{v}
and
\mathbf{u}
are linearly dependent. Therefore the non-trivial part of the cost is

\mathfrak{Re}\left(\sum_{k}\Tr\left[\rho{E_{k}}_{S}\right]\Tr\left[{E_{k}}_{S}^{*}\right]+d_{\bot}\sqrt{\sum_{k}\absolutevalue{\Tr\cwich{\rho{\Kr_k}_S}}^{2}}\right),

(5.42)

where
\sqrt{\sum_{k}\absolutevalue{\Tr\cwich{\rho\Kr_k}}^{2}}=\sqrt{\innerproduct{\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}}}
is the norm of
\mathbf{v}
and
c_{k}=\frac{\Tr\left[\rho{E_{k}}_{S}\right]}{\sqrt{\sum_{k^{\prime}}\absolutevalue{\Tr\cwich{\rho{\Kr_{k'}}_S}}^{2}}}
. Finally, we can take the maximum over all admissible channels to obtain the optimal channel. The equivalent equations
Eq.

5.39
and
Eq.

5.40
follow immediately from the general expressions
Eq.

5.14
and
Eq.

5.12
, respectively.
∎

Proposition 5.8
.

Consider two pure states in a
d
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
,
w.l.o.g.
\rho=\outerproduct{0}{0}
and

\sigma=\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}

where
\ket{\psi}=\alpha\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\ket{1}

with
\alpha\geq 0
. Let
\tilde{K}_{0}
be the cost matrix. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)=(1-\alpha)(d+2\alpha)

(5.43)

and the optimal channel is given by conjugation with the unitary

U=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&-\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\\
\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}&\alpha\end{bmatrix}\oplus\openone_{d-2}.

(5.44)

Proof.

Let
\left\{E_{k}\right\}
be an admissible set of Kraus operators for
\rho
and
\sigma
. Because
\sigma
is pure,
E_{k}\rho E_{k}^{*}=p_{k}\sigma
, with
p_{k}
a probability distribution. From
Lemma

5.6
and
Theorem

5.7
, we can write
E_{k}
as follows

E_{k}=\sqrt{p_{k}}\left[\begin{tabular}[]{c | c}\begin{tabular}[]{c c}$\alpha$&$\gamma_{k}$\\
$\beta$&$\eta_{k}$\end{tabular}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&$\Pi_{\bot}$\\
\end{tabular}\right],

(5.45)

for some
\gamma_{k},\eta_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
and where we defined
\beta=\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\geq 0
.
The trace-preserving condition
\sum_{k}E_{k}^{*}E_{k}=\mathbb{I}
gives, from the lower-right entry of the
2\times 2
block,

\sum_{k}p_{k}\left(\absolutevalue{\gamma_k}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\eta_k}^{2}\right)=1,

(5.46)

and, from the off-diagonal entry,

\sum_{k}p_{k}\left(\alpha\gamma_{k}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\eta_{k}\right)=0.

(5.47)

Taking real parts, we define

\gamma_{R}:=\sum_{k}p_{k}\,\mathfrak{Re}(\gamma_{k}),\qquad\eta_{R}:=\sum_{k}p_{k}\,\mathfrak{Re}(\eta_{k}).

(5.48)

So that

\alpha\gamma_{R}+\beta\eta_{R}=0.

(5.49)

For the cost we want to maximise, from
Theorem

5.7
,

\begin{split}\mathfrak{Re}\left(\sum_{k}\Tr\left[{E_{k}}_{S}\rho\right]\Tr\left[{E_{k}}_{S}^{*}\right]\right)+d_{\bot}\sqrt{\sum_{k}\absolutevalue{\Tr({\Kr_k}_S \rho)}^{2}}&=\mathfrak{Re}\left(\sum_{k}p_{k}\alpha\left(\alpha+\bar{\eta}_{k}\right)+d_{\bot}\sqrt{\sum_{k}p_{k}\alpha^{2}}\right)\\
&=f(\alpha,d_{\bot})+\alpha\eta_{R},\end{split}

(5.50)

where
f(\alpha,d_{\bot})=\alpha(\alpha+d_{\bot})
is a function of
\alpha
and
d_{\bot}
only, and therefore is a constant in the optimisation. Since
\alpha
is positive, thus the optimisation reduces to maximising
\eta_{R}
defined in (
5.48
) subject to (
5.46
) and (
5.47
).
Since
x\mapsto x^{2}
is convex, Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\gamma_{R}^{2}+\eta_{R}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k}p_{k}\,\mathfrak{Re}(\gamma_{k})\right)^{2}+\left(\sum_{k}p_{k}\,\mathfrak{Re}(\eta_{k})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k}p_{k}\left[(\mathfrak{Re}(\gamma_{k}))^{2}+(\mathfrak{Re}(\eta_{k}))^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{k}p_{k}\left(\absolutevalue{\gamma_k}^{2}+\absolutevalue{\eta_k}^{2}\right)=1,

(5.51)

where the last equality follows from the trace-preserving condition (
5.46
).
Combining this with (
5.47
), which for for
\alpha>0
reads,

\gamma_{R}=-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\eta_{R},

(5.52)

we get

\left(1+\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\right)\eta_{R}^{2}=\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\eta_{R}^{2}\leq 1\;\Rightarrow\;\eta_{R}\leq\alpha.

(5.53)

For
\alpha=0
, (
5.47
) gives
\eta_{R}=0
, so the bound holds in all cases.
Therefore,

f(\alpha,d_{\bot})+\alpha\eta_{R}\leq f(\alpha,d_{\bot})+\alpha^{2}=\alpha(2\alpha+d_{\bot})

(5.54)

Hence,

\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)=d-(f(\alpha,d_{\bot})+\alpha\eta_{R})\leq d-\alpha(2\alpha+d-2)=(1-\alpha)(d+2\alpha)

(5.55)

As seen in
Example

5.5
, this bound is achieved by the unitary

U=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-\beta\\
\beta&\alpha\end{pmatrix}\oplus\Pi_{\bot},

(5.56)

for which
U\ket{0}=\ket{\psi}
and
\eta_{R}=\alpha
, completing the proof.

∎

5.1
Commuting density matrices

In this section we continue the study of the unitary invariant cost problem by adding an extra constraint, that is that the states commute:
[\rho,\sigma]=0
. First we show that the cost between arbitrary states can be bounded by the cost between the first states and the pinching of the second in the basis of the first, which will yield commuting states. Then we see that the optimal quantum transport cost between commuting states can be analytically calculated and that the optimal map is purely quantum. We also bound the cost between commuting states given by classical maps and show it is much larger than the general quantum cost in general.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space,
\rho,\,\sigma\in S(\mathcal{H})
with
\rho
diagonal in the basis
\{\ket{i}\}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
the pinching map in this basis,
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}(x)=\sum_{i}\expectationvalue{x}{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be the quantum optimal transport cost associated to the unitary invariant cost matrix
K_{0}
. Then

\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)\geq\mathcal{K}(\rho,\mathcal{E}_{\rho}(\sigma)).

(5.57)

Proof.

Let
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}
and consider the Choi matrix
C
associated to a channel
\mathcal{E}
such that
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\sigma
. This matrix will be
C=\left(\sum_{ij}\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\right)
. The diagonal elements of this matrix, which need to be positive, are
\expectationvalue{\cE(\ketbra{i})}{j}
and fulfil

\sum_{j}\expectationvalue{\cE(\ketbra{i})}{j}=\Tr\left[\openone\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{i})\right]=1.

(5.58)

Thus these form a classical stochastic map
p(j|i)=\expectationvalue{\cE(\ketbra{i})}{j}
. Also note that because
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\sigma
the Choi matrix must fulfil
\sigma=\Tr_{A}\left[\rho C\right]=\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{i})
. We can apply the pinching
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
to this equation to obtain

\mathcal{E}_{\rho}(\sigma)=\sum_{i}p_{i}\mathcal{E}_{\rho}\left(\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{i})\right)=\sum_{ij}p_{i}\expectationvalue{\cE(\ketbra{i})}{j}\outerproduct{j}{j}=\sum_{ij}p_{i}p(j|i)\outerproduct{j}{j},

(5.59)

which will be useful later.

We can now bound the cost associated to each channel with an expression of the associated classical stochastic map. Note that
\bra{i}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j}
are the off diagonal elements of
C
that complete a
2\times 2
minor with
\expectationvalue{\cE(\ketbra{i})}{i}
and
\expectationvalue{\cE(\ketbra{j})}{j}
. Therefore,

\mathfrak{Re}\left(\bra{i}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j}\right)\leq\sqrt{\expectationvalue{\cE(\ketbra{i})}{i}\expectationvalue{\cE(\ketbra{j})}{j}}=\sqrt{p(i|i)p(j|j)}.

(5.60)

Finally, with
Eq.

5.60
we can calculate the bound on the non-trivial part of the cost associated to an admissible Choi matrix. Let
\ket{\Phi_+}=\sum_{i}{\ket{ii}}
again be the unnormalised maximally mixed state, then:

\begin{split}\Tr&\left[\outerproduct{\Phi_+}{\Phi_+}(\rho\star C)\right]=\sum_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}ij}\bra{i'i'}\frac{p_{i}+p_{j}}{2}\left(\outerproduct{i}{j}\otimes\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\right)\ket{j'j'}\\
&=\sum_{ij}\frac{p_{i}+p_{j}}{2}\bra{i}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j}=\sum_{i}p_{i}\expectationvalue{\cE(\ketbra{i})}{i}+\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{p_{i}+p_{j}}{2}\bra{i}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j}\\
&=\sum_{i}p_{i}\expectationvalue{\cE(\ketbra{i})}{i}+\sum_{i<j}\left(p_{i}+p_{j}\right)\mathfrak{Re}\left(\bra{i}\mathcal{E}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j}\right)\\
&\leq\sum_{i}p_{i}p(i|i)+\sum_{i<j}\left(p_{i}+p_{j}\right)\sqrt{p(i|i)p(j|j)}=\sum_{i}p_{i}p(i|i)+\sum_{i\neq j}p_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)p(j|j)}.\end{split}

(5.61)

Fix an admissible channel between
\rho
and
\sigma
and its associated classical stochastic map
p(j|i)
. Let
C_{p}=\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}+\sum_{i\neq j}p(j|i)\outerproduct{ij}{ij}
with
\ket{\phi}=\sum_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)}\ket{ii}
. This is clearly positive and

\displaystyle\Tr_{B}\left[C_{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr_{B}\left[\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}+\sum_{i\neq j}p(j|i)\outerproduct{ij}{ij}\right]=\sum_{i}p(i|i)\outerproduct{i}{i}+\sum_{i\neq j}p(j|i)\outerproduct{i}{i}=\openone,

(5.62)

\displaystyle\Tr_{A}\left[\rho^{T}C_{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr_{A}\left[\sum_{k}p_{k}(\outerproduct{k}{k}\otimes\openone)\left(\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}+\sum_{i\neq j}p(j|i)\outerproduct{ij}{ij}\right)\right]

(5.63)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}p_{i}p(i|i)\outerproduct{i}{i}+\sum_{i\neq j}p_{i}p(j|i)\outerproduct{j}{j}=\sum_{ij}p(j|i)p_{i}\outerproduct{j}{j}=\mathcal{E}_{\rho}(\sigma),

(5.64)

where the last equality was seen in
Eq.

5.59
. Therefore
C_{p}
is a Choi matrix with associated channel such that
\mathcal{E}_{C_{p}}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}_{\rho}(\sigma)
. The elements
\bra{i}\mathcal{E}_{C_{p}}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j}
are

\displaystyle\bra{i}\mathcal{E}_{C_{p}}(\outerproduct{i}{j})\ket{j}

\displaystyle=\bra{i}\Tr_{A}\left[(\outerproduct{j}{i}\otimes\openone)\left(\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}+\sum_{i^{\prime}\neq j^{\prime}}p(j^{\prime}|i^{\prime})\outerproduct{i'j'}{i'j'}\right)\right]\ket{j}

(5.65)

\displaystyle=\bra{i}\Tr_{A}\left[(\outerproduct{j}{i}\otimes\openone)\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}\right]\ket{j}=\sqrt{p(i|i)p(j|j)},

(5.66)

which is the tight version of
Eq.

5.60
. This means the bound (
5.61
) can be made tight for every admissible classical stochastic map between
\rho
and
\sigma
in the problem between
\rho
and
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}(\sigma)
by choosing the adequate channel
C_{p}
. In particular, we can tighten this bound in the problem between
\rho
and
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}(\sigma)
for a classical stochastic map associated to an optimal channel between
\rho
and
\sigma
, thus yielding

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle\geq d-\sum_{i}p_{i}p(i|i)+\sum_{i\neq j}p_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)p(j|j)}=\Tr\left[K_{0}(\rho\star C_{p}^{T_{A}})\right]\geq\mathcal{K}(\rho,\mathcal{E}_{\rho}(\sigma)),

(5.67)

finalising the proof.
∎

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
\rho
and
\sigma
commute. In a common diagonal basis they can be written as
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}
,
\sigma=\sum_{i}q_{i}\outerproduct{i}{i}
. Let
\tilde{K}_{0}
be the cost matrix. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{1}{d}\left(d-\sum_{ij}p_{i}\sqrt{\min\{1,\tfrac{q_{i}}{p_{i}}\}\min\{1,\tfrac{q_{j}}{p_{j}}\}}\right)

(5.68)

Proof.

We have seen in the proof of
Proposition

5.9
that for every admissible channel there is an associated stochastic map and that for each stochastic map with an associated channel there is a channel that makes
Eq.

5.61
tight. Therefore the problem is equivalent to the following optimisation over classical stochastic maps:

\displaystyle\min_{p(j|i)}d-\sum_{i}p_{i}p(i|i)-\sum_{i\neq j}p_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)p(j|j)},

(5.69)

such that
q_{j}=\sum_{i}p(j|i)p_{i}
. Note that only the diagonal terms of the classical stochastic map contribute to the cost and we want to maximise them. This is equivalent to the well known problem of writing the total variation distance as a classical optimal transport problem (see
[
53
, Section 6]
, also
[
50
, Example 4.14]
for a detailed explanation of the total variation distance as a classical transport problem). We succinctly give the solution to this problem for completion.

The maximum value of
0\leq p(i|i)\leq 1
subject to
q_{i}=\sum_{j}p(i|j)p_{j}\geq p(i|i)p_{i}
, is
p(i|i)=\frac{q_{i}}{p_{i}}\leq 1
if
p_{i}\geq q_{i}
and
p(i|i)=1
if
p_{i}<q_{i}
, or more succinctly

p(i|i)=\min\{1,\frac{q_{i}}{p_{i}}\}.

(5.70)

This will maximise the amount of weight the map leaves in place. If
p_{i}=0
then
q_{i}/p_{i}
is not well defined. In this case we take the maximum value
p(i|i)=1
, as this value appears in the cost and we want to have as large as possible for the optimisation, but it does not appear in the constraint (it is multiplied by
p_{i}=0
).

To finalise we need to find the values of the terms
p(j|i)
for
i\neq j
. If
p(i|i)=1
, then
p(j|i)=0
for
i\neq j
, as the sum of these positive terms is 1. When
p(i|i)<1
, given that the terms
p(j|i)
,
i\neq j
, do not contribute to the cost, the optimal transport plan
p(j|i)
can take a variety of values as long as the admissibility of the overall coupling, given by the condition
q_{j}=\sum_{i}p(j|i)p_{i}
, is preserved.
∎

Remark 5.11
.

The optimal channel associated to the unitary invariant quantum optimal transport problem between commuting states with common basis
\left\{\ket{i}\right\}
is, as we have seen, in Choi matrix form:

C=\outerproduct{\phi}{\phi}+\sum_{i\neq j}p(j|i)\outerproduct{ij}{ij},

(5.71)

with
\ket{\phi}=\sum_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)}\ket{ii}
and
p(i|i)
as previously defined in
Eq.

5.70
, which has rank at most
d^{2}-d+1
.

We can further study the structure of these maps by looking at their Kraus matrices. We have the unnormalised eigenvectors of the Choi matrix:
\{\ket{\Kr_k}\}=\{\sum_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)}\ket{ii};\allowbreak\sqrt{p(j|i)}\ket{ij},\;i\neq j\}
, such that
C=\sum_{k}\outerproduct{\Kr_k}{\Kr_k}
. The associated Kraus matrices are the un-vectorised elements:

\{E_{k}\}=\left\{\sum_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)}\outerproduct{i}{i};\sqrt{p(j|i)}\outerproduct{j}{i},\;i\neq j\right\}.

(5.72)

These Kraus matrices have the characteristic of not being able to generate coherence, but, in the case of
\sum_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)}\outerproduct{i}{i}
, not completely destroy it. In the context of the theory of quantum coherence as a resource, these matrices are incoherent operations (IO), but not strictly incoherent (SIO)
[
47
]
.

Remark 5.12
.

If
\rho
and
\sigma
commute, we can consider the case where we restrict our channels to classical channels to see how it relates to known classical distances and whether classical maps are optimal in the quantum setting. A quantum map will be classical if its Jamiołkowski matrix is diagonal in a product basis. W.l.o.g.
\rho
and
\sigma
are diagonal in the computational basis and the Jamiołkowski matrix be diagonal in the product of canonical basis. As seen in
Remark

2.5
a state over time in this case will be of the form
Q=\sum_{ij}p_{ij}\outerproduct{ij}{ij}
, with
p_{ij}
a joint probability distribution, that is a classical coupling. It is immediate to see that the associated cost with cost matrix
\tilde{K}_{0}=\openone-\frac{1}{d}\mathcal{S}
is related to the total variation distance as follows:
\Tr\left[(\openone-\frac{1}{d}\mathcal{S})Q\right]=\sum_{ij}p_{ij}-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}p_{ii}=1-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}p_{ii}=1-\frac{1}{d}+\frac{1}{d}\frac{1}{2}|\rho-\sigma|\geq 1-\frac{1}{d}
.
7
7

7

We slightly abuse notation here be denoting the classical probability distribution associated to the diagonal of states
\rho,\sigma
in the canonical basis by
\rho,\sigma
.
We obtain the cost in
Proposition

5.10
without the term
-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i\neq j}p_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)p(j|j)}
. The fact that this cost is larger than
1-\frac{1}{d}
shows that a large gap can exist between the cost associated to classical channels and the optimal quantum cost, which can go to zero by definition.

5.2
Limit
d\rightarrow\infty

With the analytical formula of the particular case of commuting states for the normalised cost matrix
\tilde{K}_{0}=\openone-\frac{1}{d}\mathcal{S}
, we can consider what happens if we embed our finite dimensional states into a larger
d
dimension system and then take the limit
d\rightarrow\infty
. First, let us rewrite
Eq.

5.68
. We can split the sum into a sum over
i
and a sum over
j
as

\frac{1}{d}\left(d-\sum_{i}p_{i}p(i|i)-\sum_{i\neq j}p_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)p(j|j)}\right)=\frac{1}{d}\left(d-\left(\sum_{i}p_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)}\right)\left(\sum_{j}\sqrt{p(j|j)}\right)\right).

(5.73)

We should address what happens to
p(i|i)=\min\{1,\frac{q_{i}}{p_{i}}\}
when
p_{i}=0
. Because
p_{i}=0
,
p(i|i)
does not affect the outcome of applying the map to the relevant state. As the value of
p(i|i)
does not matter, we take the choice
p(i|i)=1
.

With this we can calculate the limit. Let
\rho,\sigma
be commuting states in a finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{S}
of dimension
n
, such that the joint support of
\rho
,
\sigma
is
\mathcal{H}_{S}
. For a dimension
d\geq n
we have a finite dimensional Hilbert space and a natural embedding that allows us to consider
\rho
and
\sigma
in this space. We can take a basis in which
\rho
and
\sigma
are diagonal and compute the cost.
\sum_{i}p_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)}
is fixed regardless of dimension.
\sum_{j}\sqrt{p(j|j)}
has a fixed part, the sum of
p(j|j)
in the support of
\rho
, and
d-n
times
p(j|j)=1
for the part not in the support, which add up to
d-n
. We call these fixed parts
N
and
M
, respectively, and calculate the cost:

\begin{split}\mathcal{K}_{d}(\rho,\sigma)&=\frac{1}{d}\left(d-N\left(M+d-n\right)\right)=1-\frac{NM}{d}-N+\frac{Nn}{d}\\
&\xrightarrow[d\to\infty]{}1-N=1-\sum_{i}p_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)}.\end{split}

(5.74)

We can further develop this expression to write it as a function of
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
, the diagonal elements of
\rho
and
\sigma
, only:

\begin{split}\mathcal{K}_{\infty}(\rho,\sigma)&=1-\sum_{i}p_{i}\sqrt{p(i|i)}=\sum_{i}p_{i}(1-\sqrt{p(i|i)})=\sum_{i}\sqrt{p_{i}}(\sqrt{p_{i}}-\sqrt{p(i|i)p_{i}})\\
&=\sum_{q_{i}<p_{i}}\sqrt{p_{i}}(\sqrt{p_{i}}-\sqrt{q_{i}}),\end{split}

(5.75)

where the last equality comes from the definition of the optimal
p(i|i)=\min\{1,\frac{q_{i}}{p_{i}}\}
in
Eq.

5.68
.

Using
Theorem

5.7
the general case is immediate:

Theorem 5.13
.

Let
\rho,\sigma
be states in a finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{S}
of dimension
n
, such that the joint support of
\rho
,
\sigma
is
\mathcal{H}_{S}
. Let
d\geq n
and
\mathcal{H}_{d}=\mathcal{H}_{S}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\bot}

be a finite dimensional Hilbert space of dimension
d
. In
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, consider the cost matrix

\tilde{K}_{d}=\openone_{d}-\frac{1}{d}\mathcal{S}_{d}
. We denote the cost associated to the embedded

\rho,\,\sigma
in a larger Hilbert space with cost matrix
\tilde{K}_{d}
as
\mathcal{K}_{d}(\rho,\sigma)
. Then,

\mathcal{K}_{\infty}(\rho,\sigma)=\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathcal{K}_{d}(\rho,\sigma)=1-\max_{\left\{E_{k}\right\}}\sqrt{\sum_{k}\absolutevalue{\Tr\cwich{\rho\Kr_k}}^{2}}=1-\max_{\mathcal{E}:\,\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\sigma}F(\rho,\mathcal{E}),

(5.76)

where the maximisation is over all admissible Kraus representations

\{E_{k}\}
of channels
\mathcal{E}
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{S}
, and where

F(\rho,\mathcal{E}):=\sqrt{\sum_{k}\absolutevalue{\Tr(\rho E_k)}^{2}}

denotes the entanglement fidelity (also called channel fidelity)
[
45
,
55
]
of the state
\rho
under the channel
\mathcal{E}
.

The entanglement fidelity measures how well the channel
\mathcal{E}
preserves not only the state
\rho
, but also its entanglement with a reference system.

Before we give the proof, note that we now have two optimisation problems in the support Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{S}
, the original problem, with cost function equivalent to

\sum_{k}\Tr\left[E_{k}\rho\right]\Tr\left[E_{k}^{*}\right]

(5.77)

and the limit problem, with cost function

\sqrt{\sum_{k}\absolutevalue{\Tr\cwich{\rho\Kr_k}}^{2}}.

(5.78)

Both are optimised over the same set of quantum channel. As the costs are non-equivalent, these two problems are different they in general should have different optimal costs and channels. That said, numerical evidence suggests that these channels are equal or at least very close.

Proof.

Using
Eq.

5.40
in
Theorem

5.7
we can immediately obtain the result:

\begin{split}\mathcal{K}_{\infty}(\rho,\sigma)&=\lim_{d\to\infty}1-\frac{1}{d}\max_{\{E_{k}\}}\left(\mathfrak{Re}\left(\sum_{k}\Tr\left[E_{k}\rho\right]\Tr\left[E_{k}^{*}\right]\right)+(d-n)\sqrt{\sum_{k}\absolutevalue{\Tr\cwich{\rho\Kr_k}}^{2}}\right)\\
&=1-\max_{\{E_{k}\}}\sqrt{\sum_{k}\absolutevalue{\Tr\cwich{\rho\Kr_k}}^{2}},\end{split}

(5.79)

as claimed.
∎

As the set of Kraus operators that optimises
Eq.

5.76
is
a priori
different to the optimal set of Kraus operators on any finite dimension, it is unclear if
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}(\rho,\sigma)
is efficiently computable. In the following Remark we see that we can efficiently compute
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}(\rho,\sigma)
, as well as the optimal channel, through an SDP on the joint support of
\rho,\sigma
.

Remark 5.14
.

The cost in the limit,
\mathcal{K}_{\infty}(\rho,\sigma)
, can be efficiently calculated from the SDP

\begin{split}\max_{J}&\quad\quad\;\Tr\left[\rho\mathcal{S}\rho J\right]\\
\text{s.t.}&\quad\>\left\{\begin{aligned} &\Tr_{A}\left[\rho J\right]=\sigma\\
&\Tr_{B}J=\openone\\
&J^{T_{A}}\geq 0\end{aligned}\right.,\end{split}

(5.80)

where

Asymmetry and discontinuity of the cost function

We will consider a similar setting as we had, with the symmetric cost matrix
\tilde{K}_{0}=\openone-\frac{1}{d}\mathcal{S}

and
\sigma=(1-p)\rho+p\;\openone/d

\mathcal{K}(\rho,\sigma)-\mathcal{K}(\sigma,\rho)

The symmetry gap also shows a discontinuity when

\mathcal{Q}(\sigma,\rho)=\{\sigma\otimes\rho\}
. This is not true anymore for
\sigma=\rho
, since the states over time associated to all the unitary channels that send
\rho
to itself (including the identity channel) are now feasible. This discontinuity in the feasible set causes a discontinuity in
\mathcal{K}(\sigma,\rho)
, which translates to the symmetry gap. We can see that analytically with an example.

Let
\rho=(1-\varepsilon)\outerproduct{0}{0}+\varepsilon\openone/d
and
\sigma=\outerproduct{+}{+}
. If
\varepsilon>0
, there is a single admissible state over time:
Q=\rho\otimes\sigma
. The associated cost is

\begin{split}\mathcal{K}(\rho(\varepsilon),\sigma)&=1-\frac{1}{d}\Tr\left[\mathcal{S}(\rho\otimes\sigma)\right]=1-\Tr\left[\rho\sigma\right]\\
&=1-\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{1}{2}(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon\frac{1}{d}\right)\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{}1-\frac{1}{2d}.\end{split}

(5.85)

If we consider
\varepsilon=0
,
\rho
and
\sigma
are pure and we can use
Proposition

\mathcal{K}(\rho(0),\sigma)=\frac{1}{d}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(d+2\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)=1-\frac{d+2-2\sqrt{2}}{2d}.

(5.86)

As

\mathcal{K}(\rho(0),\sigma)<\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\mathcal{K}(\rho(\varepsilon),\sigma).

6
Conclusions and open problems

We have introduced a formulation of optimal transport cost for quantum states as an
application of the formalism of states over time (
stotes
), in an attempt to base
it on a notion of cost bilinear in the initial quantum state (mass distribution)
and quantum channel (transport plan).
This formalism was introduced to expand on our current understanding of spatial
quantum correlations, expressed in joint density matrices, to incorporate temporal
correlations induced by a given time evolution. In it, stotes are Jordan products
of density matrices with Jamiołkowski matrices of quantum channels.
This has allowed us to define a formalism of optimal transport with a straightforward
physical interpretation for couplings, albeit outside the realm of density matrices. Despite the clear interpretation of the couplings, and the mathematical properties observed by the stote and state-channel cost function
\kappa
, a physical meaning of the optimal cost remains a crucial open question in this setting.

After introducing the necessary notions, we set out to explore the new definition
of cost, in particular in view of the possibility of obtaining interesting
metrics on the set of quantum states.
The biggest open problem we, and in fact the stote formalism as a whole, face is
that there is currently no concise characterisation of the convex hull of the set of
stotes, nor of the convex cone generated by it, nor the dual cone. The latter
cone encodes all information required in the selection of a suitable cost operator:
it should be in the dual cone of stotes, and the same dual cone plays an important
role in deciding the triangle inequality of a given cost. Our original motivation
was to be able to design cost matrices that can be interpreted in physical terms,
such as showing energy differences for a given Hamiltonian. Currently this is work
in progress.
The stote cone itself enters in each attempt of calculating
the optimal cost for a given cost operator. However, at least fixing cost operator
as well as initial and final density matrix, this optimisation is an SDP.

As a case study and because of its distinguished symmetry, we have investigated in detail
the unitary invariant cost, which is analogous to the trivial metric in the classical
case. We have calculated this cost for commuting states and pure states. These examples
have allowed us to observe some properties and facts regarding our formalism.

A surprising fact can be observed from the second item in
Example

5.5
. One of
our main motivations for this formalism was the linearity of the state over time with
respect to both the initial state and the channel. Other approaches to quantum transport

[
14
,
18
]
led to the observation that to save the triangle inequality,
a square root of the cost had to be taken. Likewise, from the example one notices that
our cost behaves like the square of a distance, indicating that the square root would
be necessary here, too, to preserve the triangle inequality.
Nothing similar has been observed in the classical case, where the roots only appear
when taking powers of distances as cost functions (as seen in the Wasserstein distances
[
32
,
52
]
). This contrast motivates us to conjecture that the
appearance of a square root to preserve the triangle inequality could be a quantum
feature of optimal transport costs.

Other features of our optimal quantum transport cost are that even when the cost
operator is exchange symmetric (as the unitary invariant is), the resulting
optimal cost is not necessarily symmetric, adding to doubts that this approach
can yield meaningful metrics on states. On top of that, the examples of asymmetry
exhibit even instances of discontinuity of the optimal cost in the two states.

The second item of
Example

5.5
allows for another observation.
In every dimension, we considered the initial/final states
\rho=\outerproduct{0}{0}
,

\sigma=(\alpha\ket{0}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\ket{1})(\alpha\bra{0}+\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\bra{1})
.
The optimal channels turn out to be the conjugation by unitaries having
a block structure: a direct sum of a
2\times 2
-unitary and an identity of
rank
d-2
.
Only the first summand is relevant to joint support of
\rho
and
\sigma
, but the cost
is a function of the dimension nonetheless, as seen in
Eq.

5.24
. The calculation of
the optimal unitary also shows that the cost for a given channel is sensitive to the behaviour
of the channel outside the space spanned by the relevant states.
In fact, this is a general feature for arbitrary states.
This contrasts classical optimal transport, where the behaviour of the channel on regions
where the input probability is zero has no effect on the cost. This feature is reminiscent
of the Aharonov-Bohm effect
[
1
]
, a purely quantum effect where the magnetic field
far away from a charged particle can affect interference fringes of its wave function.
Motivated by this, in in
Section

5.2
we considered the limit of larger
and larger ambient Hilbert spaces, for a given pair of states. This leads to a certain
renormalisation of the cost (always in the unitary invariant case), in particular
in the limit we obtain a formula for the optimal transport cost that manifestly
“feels” only the supports of the two states. It remains for future investigation
to determine whether this leads to well-behaved metrics with interesting properties.
