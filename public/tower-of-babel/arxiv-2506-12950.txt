On Hierarchies of Fairness Notions in Cake Cutting: From Proportionality to Super Envy-Freeness
Arnav Mehra, Alexandros Psomas

Abstract
We consider the classic cake-cutting problem of producing fair allocations for n n agents, in the Robertson–Webb query model. In this model, it is known that: (i) proportional allocations can be computed using O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log n) queries, and this is optimal for deterministic protocols; (ii) envy-free allocations (a subset of proportional allocations) can be computed using O ⁡ ( n n n n n n ) O\left(n^{n^{n^{n^{n^{n}}}}}\right) queries, and the best known lower bound is Ω ⁡ ( n 2 ) \Omega(n^{2}) ; (iii) perfect allocations (a subset of envy-free allocations) cannot be computed using a bounded (in n n ) number of queries. In this work, we introduce two hierarchies of new fairness notions: Complement Harmonically Bounded (CHB) and Complement Linearly Bounded (CLB). An allocation is CHB- k k , if for any subset of agents S S of size at most k k , and every agent i ∈ S i\in S , the value of i i for the union of all pieces allocated to agents outside of S S is at most n − | S | n − | S | + 1 \frac{n-|S|}{n-|S|+1} ; for CLB- k k allocations, the upper bound becomes n − | S | n \frac{n-|S|}{n} . Intuitively, these notions of fairness ask that, for every agent i i , the collective value that a group of agents has (from the perspective of agent i i ) is limited. CHB- k k and CLB- k k coincide with proportionality for k = 1 k=1 . For all k ≤ n k\leq n , CHB- k k allocations are a superset of envy-free allocations (i.e., easier to find). On the other hand, for k ∈ [ 2 , ⌈ n / 2 ⌉ − 1 ] k\in[2,\lceil n/2\rceil-1] , CLB- k k allocations are incomparable to envy-free allocations. For k ≥ ⌈ n / 2 ⌉ k\geq\lceil n/2\rceil , CLB- k k allocations are a subset of envy-free allocations (i.e., harder to find), while CLB- n n coincides with super envy-freeness: the value of each agent for her piece is at least 1 / n 1/n , and her value for the piece allocated to any other agent is at most 1 / n 1/n . We prove that CHB- n n allocations can be computed using O ⁡ ( n 4 ) O(n^{4}) queries in the Robertson–Webb model. On the flip side, finding CHB- 2 2 (and therefore all CHB- k k for k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 ) allocations requires Ω ⁡ ( n 2 ) \Omega(n^{2}) queries, while CLB- 2 2 (and therefore all CLB- k k for k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 ) allocations cannot be computed using a bounded (in n n ) number of queries. Our results show that envy-free allocations occupy a curious middle ground, between a computationally impossible notion of fairness, CLB- ⌈ n / 2 ⌉ \lceil n/2\rceil , and a computationally “easy” notion, CHB- n n .

Introduction

We consider the classic problem of cake cutting, proposed by Hugo Steinhaus
[
28

A major focus has been the complexity of computing fair allocations. The complexity of discrete cake-cutting protocols is measured by the number of queries they require in the query model suggested by Robertson and Webb

Another major notion of fairness is

1.1
Our contributions

We introduce two new hierarchies of fairness: Complement Harmonically Bounded (
CHB
) and Complement Linearly Bounded (
CLB
). An allocation
A
satisfies
\textsc{CHB}\text{-}k
if, for every subset of agents
S
such that
|S|\leq k
, and for every agent
i\in S

i
for the union of pieces allocated to agents not in
S
is at most
\frac{n-|S|}{n-|S|+1}
, i.e.,
\sum_{j\notin S}V_{i}(A_{j})\leq\frac{n-|S|}{n-|S|+1}
. For
\textsc{CLB}\text{-}k
, the upper bound is
\frac{n-|S|}{n}
instead. The existence of
\textsc{CHB}\text{-}k
allocations is implied by the existence of envy-free allocations. Consider an envy-free allocation
A
; we have that, for all agents
i,j\in N
,
V_{i}(A_{j})\leq V_{i}(A_{i})
. Adding up for all
j\notin S
we have
\sum_{j\notin S}V_{i}(A_{j})\leq(n-|S|)V_{i}(A_{i})\leq(n-|S|)(1-\sum_{j\notin S}V_{i}(A_{j}))
; re-arranging gives the desired inequality. The existence of
\textsc{CLB}\text{-}k
allocations is similarly implied by the existence of perfect allocations.

Our new fairness notions are interesting precisely because they occupy a natural but largely unexplored middle ground between proportionality and envy-freeness.
\textsc{CHB}\text{-}k
allocations limit how much value groups of agents collectively derive from other agents’ shares; thus, they may be particularly suited for contexts where collective perceptions of fairness are important.
\textsc{CLB}\text{-}k
allocations impose stronger conditions, which makes them appealing when perfection is desired (where the computational cost is prohibitive/unbounded). By providing a spectrum of fairness guarantees, these notions offer a more nuanced toolkit, enabling practitioners to better navigate the trade-off between computational complexity and fairness requirements.

However, intuitively, such a step forces us to find an allocation of the residue that is weighted proportional, where agents have different weights (or, equivalently, find an allocation such that agents’ utilities meet specific utility lower bounds). Unfortunately, this leads us to a dead-end: there is no bounded protocol for finding a proportional division with unequal shares
[
9

\epsilon

Preliminaries

We have an infinitely divisible resource, the “cake,” denoted by the interval
[0,1]
. A piece of cake refers to a finite set of disjoint intervals of
[0,1]
.
Our goal is to allocate the cake among a set
N
of
n
agents. An allocation
A=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
of the cake to the agents consists of
n
pieces, where
A_{i}
is the piece allocated to agent
i
, such that
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
for all pairs of agents
i,j
. An allocation
A
is
complete
if
\cup_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}=[0,1]
. For an allocation
A
and a subset of agents
S\subseteq N
, we will use notation
A_{S}=\cup_{i\in S}A_{i}

to denote the union of all pieces allocated to the agents in
S
. We also use notation
\bar{S}=N\setminus S
for the complement of a set
S
.

Every agent
i\in N
has a valuation function
V_{i}
that assigns a non-negative value to any subinterval of
[0,1]
. It is convenient to think of these values as being induced by a density function
v_{i}
. That is, for an interval
I=[a,b]
,
V_{i}(I)=\int_{x=a}^{b}v_{i}(x)dx
. Valuations are (i) normalized:
V_{i}([0,1])=\int_{x=0}^{1}v_{i}(x)dx=1
, (ii) additive: for a set of disjoint intervals
I_{1},\dots,I_{m}
,
V_{i}(\cup_{j=1}^{m}I_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}V_{i}(I_{j})
, (iii) non-atomic:
\forall x,y\in[0,1],\lambda\in[0,1],\exists z\in[x,y]
where
V_{i}([x,z])=\lambda\cdot V_{i}([x,y])
.

2.1
Robertson-Webb Model

We study the complexity of cake-cutting algorithms in the model suggested by Robertson and Webb
[
24
]
and later formalized (and named) by Woeginger and Sgall
[
31
]
. This model allows for two types of queries:

1.

\textsc{Eval}_{i}(I)
: Given an agent
i
and an interval
I\subseteq[0,1]
, this query returns
V_{i}(I)
.

2.

\textsc{Cut}_{i}(x,v)
: Given an agent
i
, a point
x\in[0,1]
, and a value
v\in[0,1]
, this query returns the smallest point
x^{\prime}\in[x,1]
such that
V_{i}([x,x^{\prime}])=v
.

To the best of our knowledge, every discrete cake-cutting algorithm can be (and has been) analyzed in this model. As a simple example, the cut-and-choose algorithm can be implemented by two queries as follows. First, a
\textsc{Cut}_{1}(0,1/2)
will return the point
y
such that
V_{1}([0,y])=V_{1}([y,1])=1/2
. The algorithm can determine whether
[0,y]
or
[y,1]
should be allocated to agent
1
, by making a
\textsc{Eval}_{2}([0,y])
and checking whether the value returned is at most
1/2
.

2.2
Fairness notions

Our goal is to produce allocations that are fair.
An allocation
A
is
proportional
if
V_{i}(A_{i})\geq 1/n
for all
i\in N
. An allocation
A
is
envy-free
if for all
i,j\in N
,
V_{i}(A_{i})\geq V_{i}(A_{j})
. An allocation
A
is
\epsilon
-perfect
if for all
i,j\in N
,
1/n+\epsilon\geq V_{i}(A_{j})\geq 1/n-\epsilon
; an allocation
A
is
perfect
if it is
0
-perfect.
Finally, an allocation,
A
, is
super envy-free
if every agent values her own piece at least
1/n
, and values any other agent’s piece at most
1/n
, i.e.,
V_{i}(A_{i})\geq\frac{1}{n}\geq V_{i}(A_{j})
, for all
i,j\in N
.

We write
Prop
,
EF
,
Super-EF
, and
Perf
for the set of all complete proportional, envy-free, super envy-free, and perfect allocations, respectively. For complete allocation, every perfect allocation is super envy-free, every super envy-free allocation is envy-free, and every envy-free allocation is proportional, and there exist perfect allocations that are not super envy-free, super envy-free allocations that are not envy-free, as well as envy-free allocations that are not proportional; that is,
\textsc{Prop}\supsetneq\textsc{EF}\supsetneq\textsc{Super-EF}\supsetneq\textsc{Perf}
.

In this paper, we define the following new notions of fairness.

Definition 1

(Complement Harmonically Bounded-
k
(
\textsc{CHB}\text{-}k
))
.

Let
k
be an integer, such that
n\geq k\geq 1
. An allocation
A
is Complement Harmonically Bounded-
k
(
\textsc{CHB}\text{-}k
) if, for every non-empty subset of agents
S\subseteq N
such that
|S|\leq k
, and every
i\in S
,
V_{i}(A_{\bar{S}})=\sum_{j\notin S}V_{i}(A_{j})\leq\frac{n-|S|}{n-|S|+1}
.

It is easy to see that the definition of Complement Harmonically Bounded-
1
coincides with the definition of proportionality. Slightly overloading notation, we write
\textsc{CHB}\text{-}k
for the set of all complete Complement Harmonically Bounded-
k
allocations.

Definition 2

(Complement Linearly Bounded-
k
(
\textsc{CLB}\text{-}k
))
.

Let
k
be an integer, such that
n\geq k\geq 1
. An allocation
A
is Complement Linearly Bounded-
k
(
\textsc{CLB}\text{-}k
) if, for every non-empty subset of agents
S\subseteq N
such that
|S|\leq k
, and every
i\in S
,
V_{i}(A_{\bar{S}})=\sum_{j\notin S}V_{i}(A_{j})\leq\frac{n-|S|}{n}
.

Slightly overloading notation, again, we write
\textsc{CLB}\text{-}k
for the set of all complete Complement Linearly Bounded-
k
allocations.
Notice that every Complement Linearly Bounded-
k
allocation is Complement Harmonically Bounded-
k
, since
\frac{n-|S|}{n}\leq\frac{n-|S|}{n-|S|+1}
for all
|S|\geq 1
.
