Inter‐Set Blood‐Flow Restriction Increases Peripheral Physiological Stress While Preserving Repeated‐Sprint Mechanical Output in Trained Athletes
Authors: Tianhao Zheng, Leyi Jiang, Yan chen, Dapeng Bao, Olivier Girard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTInter‐set blood‐flow restriction (BFR) may increase the physiological stimulus of repeated‐sprint exercise without compromising mechanical output, but evidence in trained athletes is limited. In a randomized crossover design, 26 national‐level male badminton players completed three 5 × 5‐s cycling sprints (25‐s intra‐set rest, 3.5‐min inter‐set recovery). During the first two recoveries, cuffs were inflated to 130 mmHg (BFR) or 20 mmHg (sham‐BFR). Outcomes included power output, vastus lateralis muscle oxygenation (SmO2), heart rate, exercise‐related sensations, and vertical jump. Mean and peak power declined across sets with no condition or interaction effects. Sprint decrement scores showed no differences. Muscle oxygenation kinetics revealed time and condition × time effects: recovery SmO2 was lower with BFR during the first two inter‐set recoveries (Δ = 16.04 and 15.50 pp; p ≤ 0.05), whereas this difference reversed during the non‐occluded third recovery (Δ = −6.00; p = 0.010). Early SmO2 resaturation slopes (0–30 s) were slower with BFR in recoveries 1–2 (Δ = +0.250 and + 0.209 pp/s; p < 0.001 and < 0.05), but not recovery 3. Heart rate and breathing difficulty increased across sets, without condition effects. Response showed condition × set interactions, with higher leg discomfort (Set 2) and perceived exertion (Sets 2–3) under BFR. After exercise, countermovement height decreased in BFR (p < 0.001), not in sham‐BFR (p = 0.093). Inter‐set BFR increased peripheral physiological and perceptual stress and altered muscle oxygenation recovery without compromising repeated‐sprint mechanical performance or cardiovascular responses. This strategy may provide a practical method to enhance training stress in trained athletes, although greater post‐exercise reductions in jump performance suggest potential neuromuscular fatigue.

Introduction

Repeated‐sprint exercise (RSE) involves multiple short (≤ 10 s) all out efforts interspersed with brief recoveries (≤ 60 s) (Girard et al. 2011). RSE targets key high‐intensity intermittent characteristics of competition and effectively improves physical attributes such as peak sprint speed, repeated‐sprint ability, vertical jump performance, and intermittent running capacity (J. Taylor et al. 2015; Thurlow et al. 2024). Owing to its specificity and high physiological demands, RSE is commonly prescribed for well‐trained athletes (Bishop et al. 2011; J. M. Taylor, Macpherson, McLaren, et al. 2016).

Completion of RSE protocols imposes considerable physiological strain and leads to significant fatigue development across sprint repetitions (Girard et al. 2011). Mean heart rate and oxygen uptake can reach ∼90% of maximum, blood lactate concentrations may exceed 10 mmol·L−1, and perceived exertion is typically rated as “very hard,” with ∼5% reductions in vertical jump height from pre‐to post‐exercise (Thurlow et al. 2023). Although RSE prescription can be manipulated through variables such as sprint duration, number of repetitions, set structure, recovery duration, exercise mode, and work‐to‐rest ratio, increasing the physiological stimulus without reducing sprint quality remains challenging (Thurlow et al. 2024). Attempts to increase total volume by adding sprints often compromise maximal efforts across repetitions, reducing RSE training effectiveness (Girard et al. 2011). Thus, the key challenge is to enhance the training stimulus (i.e., physiological responses) while maintaining training quality (i.e., mechanical output) required for adaptations.

Localized hypoxic interventions, such as blood‐flow restriction (BFR), provide a promising strategy (McKee, Girard, Peiffer, Dempsey, et al. 2024). By inflating pneumatic cuffs to restrict arterial inflow and venous return (Mckee et al. 2023), BFR induces localized muscle hypoxia without corresponding increases in external workload, thereby augmenting metabolic stress at a given mechanical output (Mitchell et al. 2019). Specifically, BFR disrupts phosphocreatine resynthesis, increases reliance on anaerobic glycolysis, and promotes metabolite accumulation (e.g., H+ and Pi), collectively stimulating improvements in muscle buffering capacity (McKee et al. 2024).

Applying BFR during exercise can impair performance, particularly by reducing mechanical output during high‐intensity efforts (McKee, Girard, Peiffer, Dempsey, et al. 2024; Willis et al. 2018). To mitigate this, researchers have proposed applying BFR exclusively during inter‐set recovery (Papoti et al. 2023). Studies adding BFR during inter‐sprint (C. W. Taylor et al. 2016) or during inter‐set recovery (Ienaga et al. 2022) reported larger muscle deoxygenation levels without compromising subsequent all out sprint performance. However, these studies primarily recruited recreationally active individuals. By contrast, high‐level athletes exhibit greater neural drive and superior intermuscular coordination, enabling faster achievement and maintenance of higher absolute outputs and, under intense RSE tasks, deeper peripheral deoxygenation (Paquette et al. 2020). It is therefore necessary to examine the effectiveness of inter‐set BFR during RSE in highly‐trained athletes. Because BFR is applied only during recovery, we will use phase‐specific near infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) acquisition and reporting, analyzing sprint, recovery‐with‐inflation, and recovery‐with‐deflation epochs independently. This approach minimizes the loss of phase‐specific detail that can occur with session‐level averaging and enhances mechanistic resolution (Ienaga et al. 2022; McKee, Girard, Peiffer, Dempsey, et al. 2024).

Accordingly, this study aimed to investigate the effects of applying BFR during inter‐set recovery in RSE on mechanical output, muscle oxygenation trends, psycho‐physiological responses, and vertical jump ability in high‐level athletes. We hypothesized that, although BFR might cause a slight performance decline, it would significantly increase recovery‐phase muscle deoxygenation (i.e., indicating increased metabolic stress), thereby enhancing the training stimulus without markedly compromising mechanical output.

Methods

Participants

Twenty‐six adult male badminton players volunteered (age 21.1 ± 3.6 years, height 177.5 ± 6.2 cm, body mass 69.4 ± 5.4 kg, badminton experience 6.4 ± 2.1 y). All were students at Beijing Sport University and classified as national first‐level athletes, corresponding to Tier 3 (McKay et al. 2022). Exclusion criteria included musculoskeletal injuries in previous 3 months and hematological or cardiovascular contraindications to BFR(Patterson et al. 2019). An a priori G*Power analysis (v3.1) indicated that a sample of 26 participants would provide 80% power (α = 0.05) to detect an effect size of 0.4 (Cohen's dz) in mean power during RSE (Beard et al. 2019). The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Approval No.2025082H) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013). All participants provided written informed consent.

Study Design

The study employed a randomized crossover design comprising one familiarization visit and two experimental trials (conditions: BFR vs. sham‐BFR), separated by 4 ± 1 day and conducted at the same time of day (± 2 h). Participants refrained from strenuous exercise, caffeine, and alcohol for 24 h before each trial.

Familiarization

Participants completed the RSE protocol with inter‐set BFR. After a 5‐min warm‐up on a Wattbike Pro at 1.0 W·kg−1 (air = 4, magnet = 1), two countermovement jumps (CMJ) were performed, followed by two sets of repeated sprints (5 × 5‐s maximal sprints per set) with 25 s of passive recovery. Sprints started from a seated, stationary position with the trunk at ∼45°. During the 3.5‐min (210 s) inter‐set recovery, participants dismounted and lay supine. Cuffs (10 cm) were inflated to target pressure within ∼15 s (t = 0–15 s), maintained for 2 min at either 20 or 130 mmHg (t = 15–135 s), and then deflated (t = 135 s). This was followed by a 60‐s recovery without occlusion (t = 135–195 s), after which ∼15 s were used for remounting and set‐up for the next sprint set (t = 195–210 s). After completing the second sprint set, participants rested passively for 1 min before performing two post‐exercise CMJs.

Experimental Trials

Procedures mirrored the familiarization session, including the same warm‐up, gearing settings, sprint posture, and CMJ testing protocol. The RSE protocol consisted of three sets of 5 × 5‐s maximal sprints, with 25‐s passive recovery between sprints and 3.5‐min rest intervals between sets. This design was intended to replicate the intermittent high‐intensity demands of badminton, where rallies lasts ∼7 s on average (Phomsoupha and Laffaye 2015). The 25‐s recovery interval followed previous closed‐loop cycling RSE studies using BFR, which typically employed work‐to‐rest ratios of 1:4 to 1:6 (Ienaga et al. 2022; McKee, Girard, Peiffer, Dempsey, et al. 2024). Before the RSE protocol, bilateral pneumatic lower‐limb cuffs (Theratools, 109 × 10 cm) were positioned around the proximal thighs and remained in place throughout the protocol. During the first two inter‐set recoveries, participants dismounted and lay supine while the cuffs were inflated to 130 mmHg within 15 s using a manual pump. An absolute cuff pressure of 130 mmHg was selected to maintain procedural consistency across trials, and this pressure is consistent with the range commonly applied in previous intermittent BFR protocols (Papoti et al. 2023). However, this pressure was not individualized to each participant's arterial occlusion pressure (AOP) and therefore did not standardize the relative degree of occlusion across participants. The pressure was maintained for 2 min and then rapidly released. Participants remained supine for 1 min with the cuffs fully deflated. During all sprint sets, the cuffs remained on the thighs but were fully deflated, and no cuff pressure was applied during sprinting. Participants remounted the ergometer within the final ∼15 s, and each set began exactly 3.5 min after the previous one ended. The detailed protocol is illustrated in Figure 1. In the sham‐BFR condition, procedures were identical except the cuff pressure was set to 20 mmHg, providing no meaningful restriction.

Protocol overview.

Repeated Sprint Exercise Protocol

All sprints were performed seated to ensure consistency among participants without cycling specialization (Reiser et al. 2002), with strong verbal encouragement to elicit maximal effort. Peak and mean power outputs were displayed in real time on the Wattbike Pro monitor and manually recorded after each sprint. The sprint decrement score (S dec) was calculated as: Sdec(%)=1−S‾×5Sbest×5×100 where S‾ is the mean power output of all sprints within a set and S best is the highest mean power output of a single sprint (Girard et al. 2011). Mean peak and average power values across repetitions were calculated for each set for subsequent analyses.

Repeated sprint exercise (RSE) consisted of 3 sets of 5 × 5‐s cycling sprints (25‐s recovery) and 3.5‐min inter‐set recovery. Thigh cuffs were positioned on the proximal thighs throughout the protocol and inflated only during the first two inter‐set recoveries (130 vs. 20 mmHg) for 120 s. Deflated indicates the 60‐s period during which the cuffs were fully deflated and no cuff pressure was applied. Continuous heart rate and muscle oxygenation (SmO2) were monitored. ERS indicates exercise‐related sensations, including overall rating of perceived exertion (RPE), lower‐limb discomfort, and breathlessness; these ratings were recorded after each sprint set and at the end of the subsequent recovery. Countermovement jumps were performed before and 1 min after completion of the RSE protocol.

Near‐Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS)

Muscle oxygenation (SmO2) of the left vastus lateralis was monitored continuously using a portable NIRS device (MOXY Monitor, Fortiori Design LLC, USA). The device uses four emission wavelengths (680, 720, 760, 800 nm) and source‐detector separations of 12.5 and 25 mm (McManus et al. 2018), with a sampling frequency of 0.5 Hz (i.e., raw data unsmoothed). Sensor placement followed established procedures: two‐thirds of the distance from the anterior superior iliac spine to the superior patella border (Feldmann et al. 2019). Before placement, two skinfold thickness measurements were obtained at the measurement site with a caliper (Baseline Economy Skinfold Caliper; CV = 3.7%) to confirm values were below the manufacturer's threshold. Hair was shaved and skin cleaned with alcohol to reduce optical interference. The sensor was affixed to the distal vastus lateralis with the detector facing upward, secured using a black elastic bandage to minimize movement and block ambient light. A transparent film was placed between the probe and skin for waterproofing. All data were collected under identical experimental conditions, and inter‐session placement was kept consistent using identical skin markings and strapping.

To avoid data‐driven analyses, we pre‐specified SmO2 summary variables and grouped them into four contrast families. To avoid confusion with individual sprint numbers, set labels are written in full: Family A: Set 1–Set 3 RecMean (recovery‐period mean, confirmatory primary endpoint); Family B: Set 1–Set 2 RecMax (recovery‐period maximum, secondary/confirmatory), because only the first two inter‐set recoveries included the standardized cuff‐inflation and deflation procedure; Family C: Set 1–Set 3 ExMean (sprint‐phase mean, exploratory); and Family D: Set 1–Set 3 ExMin (sprint‐phase minimum, exploratory). The contrast direction was defined as Δ = sham‐BFR − BFR (in percentage points), where positive values indicate higher SmO2 in sham‐BFR. In addition, following prior work (Eserhaut et al. 2024), we calculated the SmO2 resaturation rate during the first 0–30 s of each recovery period, estimated as the linear upslope of SmO2 with ordinary least squares (SmO2_resat; percentage points/s). These metrics (i.e., labeled Recovery 1–Recovery 3 in figures) were analyzed separately from Families A–D and the gatekeeping procedure, with multiplicity controlled with Holm's step‐down correction across the three tests (α = 0.05). Because Recovery 3 occurred after the final sprint set without cuff inflation, it was interpreted as reflecting residual effects of the preceding inter‐set BFR exposure rather than a direct cuff‐inflation comparison.

Physiological and Perceptual Responses

Heart rate (HR) was continuously monitored with a chest strap (Polar H10, Polar Electro Oy, Finland). Electrodes were moistened and positioned below the pectorals with sternal crossing. Data were exported, resampled at 1 Hz, and segmented by event markers into sprint and inter‐set recovery phases. For each phase, the expected number of samples was determined from the phase duration after resampling. Available HR data were defined as non‐missing samples successfully recorded by the chest‐strap monitor. Phases were retained when ≥ 80% of the expected 1‐Hz samples were available. No missing segments were manually imputed.

At the end of each sprint set and at the end of the subsequent inter‐set recovery period, participants rated overall perceived exertion (RPE), lower‐limb discomfort, and breathlessness using the modified Borg CR10 scale (0 = “nothing at all” to 10 = “maximal”) (Foster et al. 2001; Laurent et al. 2011). Recovery ratings reflected residual exercise‐related sensations at the end of the preceding recovery period rather than baseline ratings before the next sprint set. Standardized instructions clarified constructs (overall RPE: whole‐body effort; lower‐limb discomfort: muscular discomfort in the legs; breathlessness: dyspnea). Ratings were anchored to personal memory references to ensure consistency (Malleron et al. 2023; Peñailillo et al. 2018).

Vertical Jump Ability

To assess neuromuscular fatigue induced by the sprint protocol, two countermovement jumps (CMJs) were performed before the RSE protocol and again 1 min after completion of the protocol at the same location (Benítez‐Flores et al. 2023). Participants performed each CMJ on a portable Kistler force platform sampling at 1000 Hz, executed a rapid countermovement to a self‐selected depth, and were instructed to jump as high as possible. Arm swing was not allowed, and participants kept their hands on their hips throughout each jump. Vertical ground‐reaction force data were low‐pass filtered (fourth‐order, zero‐phase, 20 Hz). Jump height was computed from force using double integration after subtracting body weight, with take‐off and landing identified by standard thresholds. The mean height of the two jumps was analyzed. Data processing was performed in MATLAB (MathWorks).

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were conducted in MATLAB R2023b (MathWorks, USA). Repeated measures were modeled with linear mixed‐effects models (LME) with Subject as a random intercept. Random slopes for Set/Recovery were considered but excluded due to convergence and design constraints. Fixed effects varied by outcome: (i) power, HR, and sprint‐phase RPE: Condition (BFR, sham‐BFR), Set (1–3), and Condition × Set interaction; (ii) 12 pre‐specified SmO2 nodes: Condition, Time (12 levels), and Condition × Time interaction; and (iii) 0–30 s SmO2 resaturation slope: Condition, Recovery (Recovery 1–Recovery 3), and Condition × Recovery interaction. Model terms were evaluated with marginal F‐tests using residual degrees of freedom and a two‐sided α = 0.05.

Multiplicity was controlled using a pre‐registered gatekeeping procedure across SmO2 families (A→B→C→D), applying Holm's step‐down correction within each family. Exploratory analyses (15 sprint‐by‐sprint power tests; Recovery 1–Recovery 3 resaturation‐slope contrasts) also used Holm correction within their respective sets.

Effect sizes are reported as partial η 2 for omnibus F‐tests and Hedges' g for pairwise contrasts. Hedges' g values are reported as absolute magnitudes, with the direction of the effect described in the text, tables, or figure captions. Signed mean differences (Δ) were retained to indicate the direction of between‐condition differences. NIRS Δ values are expressed in percentage points, and resaturation slopes in points per second (pp/s). Unless otherwise specified, data are presented as mean ± SD. Figures 2 and 3 are presented as mean ± SE to illustrate uncertainty around the plotted condition means; descriptive variability is reported as SD in tables unless otherwise specified (Figure 4).

Results

Mechanical Output

Both mean and peak power showed a significant main effect of Set (mean power: F(2,150) = 23.65, p = 1.19 × 10−9, ηp 2 = 0.240; peak power: F(2,150) = 17.13, p = 1.99 × 10−7, ηp 2 = 0.186), indicating a monotonic decline across sets (Set 1 > Set 2 > Set 3). No significant main effects of Condition or Condition × Set interaction were observed. Holm‐adjusted pairwise comparisons revealed that, for peak power within the BFR condition, the Set 1‐2 difference was not significant (p(Holm) = 0.077), while all other within‐condition contrasts were significant (p(Holm) ≤ 0.001). Exploratory sprint‐by‐sprint analyses (15 Holm‐adjusted comparisons) showed no significant differences (Figure 2). The smallest adjusted p‐value occurred at sprint 15 (p(Holm) = 0.425), with sprints 8 and 10 yielding 0.490 and 0.589, respectively. Effect sizes at these sprints were moderate (g = 0.60/0.57/0.63), all favoring sham‐BFR. The S dec showed no significant effect of Condition (F(1,150) = 0.229, p = 0.633), Set (F(2,150) = 0.079, p = 0.924), or their interaction (F(2,150) = 0.723, p = 0.487) (Table 1).

SmO2 Outcomes

The LME showed significant effects of Time (F(11,600) = 234.09, p < 0.001, ηp 2 = 0.811) and a Condition × Time interaction (F(11,600) = 7.61, p < 0.001, ηp 2 = 0.123), but no main effect of Condition (F(1,600) = 0.05, p = 0.829). Applying the pre‐specified gatekeeping procedure (Holm correction within families), only Family A (recovery‐period means) showed significant between‐condition differences. Using Δ (sham‐BFR—BFR, in percentage points), results were: Set 1 RecMean Δ = 16.04 (95% CI 11.48–20.59; p(Holm) = 3.6 × 10−11; g = 1.66), Set 2 RecMean Δ = 15.50 (10.95–20.06; p (Holm) = 1.07 × 10−10; g = 1.33), and Set 3 RecMean Δ = −6.00 (−10.56 to −1.45; p (Holm) = 0.0099; g = 0.49, favoring BFR). These findings indicate higher SmO2 in sham‐BFR during Sets 1 and 2, but a reversal in Set 3 where BFR exceeded sham‐BFR. Family B (recovery maxima) showed no significant differences (both p (Holm) = 0.690), halting the gatekeeping process. Families C and D (exercise means and minima) were exploratory and non‐significant after Holm correction (all p (Holm) ≥ 0.255) (Figure 3A).

Early Resaturation Slope (0–30 s)

The LME showed significant main effects of Condition (F(1,150) = 15.43, p = 0.00013, ηp 2 = 0.093), Recovery (F(2,150) = 37.39, p < 0.001, ηp 2 = 0.333), and their interaction (F(2,150) = 17.92, p < 0.001, ηp 2 = 0.193). Between‐condition comparisons (Holm across Recovery 1–Recovery 3; Δ in pp/s) showed significantly faster early SmO2 resaturation in sham‐BFR during Recovery 1 and Recovery 2, but not Recovery 3: Recovery 1 Δ = +0.250 (95% CI 0.124–0.375; p (Holm) < 0.001; g = 1.29), Recovery 2 Δ = +0.209 (0.084–0.335; p (Holm) < 0.05; g = 0.87), and Recovery 3 Δ = −0.111 (−0.237 to 0.014; p (Holm) = 0.081; g = 0.41, numerically favoring BFR). Within‐condition tests (Holm‐adjusted) showed BFR slopes increased from Recovery 1 and Recovery 2 to Recovery 3 (both p (Holm) ≤ 1.0 × 10−11), whereas sham‐BFR slopes remained stable (all p (Holm) ≥ 0.603). Thus, sham‐BFR exhibited faster early resaturation during Recovery 1 and Recovery 2, with between‐condition differences disappearing by Recovery 3 (Figure 3B).

Recovery‐Phase Physiological and Perceptual Responses

The LME showed a main effect of Recovery for HR and difficulty breathing, both increased from R1 to R3. No main effect of Condition or Condition × Recovery interaction was noted (both p > 0.07). Between‐condition effects were small‐to‐moderate (HR: g = +0.50; difficulty breathing: g = +0.53). Limb discomfort exhibited a significant Condition × Recovery interaction (F(2,150) = 9.11, p = 1.85 × 10−4), with ratings increasing across recovery periods. Holm‐adjusted simple effects showed higher values at R2 (5.92 ± 0.89 vs. 4.08 ± 0.93; p (Holm) = 3.78 × 10−9; g = +1.99). No between‐condition differences were found at R1 (p (Holm) = 0.053; g = +0.61) or R3 (p (Holm) = 0.315; g = +0.28). Overall RPE also increased across recovery periods (main effect of Recovery (F(2,150) = 27.13, p < 0.001), with no significant Condition × Recovery interaction (p = 0.173). Pairwise comparisons (Holm‐adjusted) indicated higher readings at R2 (p = 0.0011; g = 0.92) and R3 (p = 0.0236; g = 0.63), but not at R1 (p = 0.278; g = 0.30) (Table 2).

Vertical Jump Ability

The LME showed a main effect of Time but no main effect of Condition (p = 0.648) or interaction (p = 0.486). A secondary change‐score analysis (Δ = Post − Pre) showed a greater decline with BFR (ΔBFR = −0.04 ± 0.04 m) than sham‐BFR (Δsham = −0.01 ± 0.04 m), with a between‐condition difference of −0.03 m (95% CI −0.05 to −0.01), t(50) = −2.45, p = 0.018, g = 0.67, indicating a greater decline with BFR. Within conditions, CMJ height decreased from 0.45 to 0.41 m in BFR (p < 0.001), but not in sham‐BFR (0.44–0.43 m; p = 0.093) (Figure 4).

Discussion

This study recruited high‐level athletes to evaluate the effects of applying BFR during inter‐set recovery in RSE on mechanical output, muscle oxygenation, physiological/perceptual responses, and vertical jump ability. The main findings were: (1) Mean and peak power declined across sets, with no main effect of condition or condition × set interaction; (2) BFR elicited greater muscle deoxygenation during sprints and slower early reoxygenation during recovery (reflected by lower mean SmO2 and flatter 0–30 s resaturation slope), but recovery‐end SmO2 did not differ between conditions, and differences diminished or reversed by set 3; (3) leg discomfort and overall RPE were higher with BFR in Sets 2 and 3, whereas heart rate and difficulty breathing increased similarly across sets in both conditions; (4) post‐session CMJ decline was greater under BFR. Taken together, inter‐set BFR during RSE did not compromise mechanical output but accentuated muscle deoxygenation, perceived exertion, and post‐session reductions in vertical jump ability. Applying external limb compression only during inter‐set recovery appears to increase local oxygenation‐related and perceptual responses without negatively affecting repeated‐sprint mechanical output.

Performance Outcomes

No significant differences were observed between BFR and sham‐BFR for overall mean or peak power output. Exploratory sprint‐by‐sprint contrasts based on the LMM (Holm‐adjusted) suggested medium‐sized power reductions toward the end of post‐occlusion sets (e.g., sprints 8, 10, and 15), but these trends did not remain significant after multiplicity correction and should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, Sdec did not differ between conditions, indicating that inter‐set occlusion did not impair within‐set fatigue resistance. However, because these sprint‐by‐sprint differences were not statistically significant after multiplicity correction, they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of impaired local reoxygenation. Previous research has shown that BFR during recovery lowers tissue saturation index, thereby promoting metabolite (i.e., lactate, H+) accumulation and constraining PCr resynthesis (Ienaga et al. 2022). Overall, our finding of no additional power decrement aligns with research where BFR was applied only during recovery (Ienaga et al. 2022). Notably, our cohort comprised high‐level athletes and used shorter recovery intervals (3.5 vs. 5 min in the literature) (Ienaga et al. 2022). These conditions likely result in higher absolute power outputs and motor unit recruitment at the same relative intensity (Škarabot et al. 2024), raising metabolic and O2 delivery demands and reliance on O2‐dependent PCr resynthesis. Under such constraints, incomplete early recovery of muscle oxygenation may have contributed to subtle end‐of‐set performance trends, although these sprint‐by‐sprint differences were not statistically significant after Holm correction. Studies applying BFR during sprint efforts have reported power reductions (McKee, Girard, Peiffer, Dempsey, et al. 2024); however, the present findings should be interpreted specifically within an inter‐set recovery BFR protocol. In this context, inter‐set recovery BFR did not deteriorate repeated‐sprint mechanical output relative to sham‐BFR while increasing local oxygenation‐related and perceptual responses.

Muscle Oxygenation

During the early phase of recovery, BFR imposed a larger deoxygenation load. Hence, recovery‐period mean SmO2 was lower than sham‐BFR in Sets 1 and 2, and the 0–30 s resaturation slope (R1, R2) was attenuated. This pattern may explain why RecMean, but not RecMin or RecMax, differed between conditions: RecMean integrates the entire recovery period and therefore captures the sustained reduction in muscle oxygenation during cuff inflation, whereas RecMin likely reflects the immediate post‐sprint nadir and RecMax reflects late‐recovery reoxygenation after cuff deflation, when between‐condition values had partly converged. From a training perspective, this sustained reduction in recovery‐period SmO2 may provide a useful local hypoxic stimulus during routine badminton repeated‐sprint training, potentially increasing peripheral physiological stress without requiring additional sprint volume or mechanical load. However, end‐recovery SmO2 did not differ between conditions, which may help explain the preserved mean and peak power outputs throughout the session. In Set 3 (no occlusion), differences in recovery‐period mean SmO2 diminished or even reversed (BFR ≳ sham‐BFR), and the slope difference disappeared, suggesting a “rebound” effect consistent with reactive hyperemia or ischemic‐preconditioning (Incognito et al. 2016; Perlet et al. 2024). These observations suggest that the first two occluded recoveries may acutely alter O2 delivery/utilization kinetics in subsequent sets. Importantly, despite slower reoxygenation under BFR, end‐recovery SmO2 did not differ significantly, indicating that muscle oxygenation alone is unlikely to fully account for the exploratory medium‐sized power decrements observed at the end of some sets (Holm‐adjusted, not significant). This points to additional mechanisms (i.e., not measured here), including neuromuscular factors, that may influence performance under BFR beyond local O2 supply (Girard et al. 2011).

Perceptual and Cardiovascular Responses

Under the current protocol, BFR primarily exaggerated peripheral and perceptual load without markedly affecting systemic cardiovascular load. The heightened perceptual responses align with the classic view that metabolite accumulation augments discomfort and effort via group III/IV afferents (Amann et al. 2015). Additionally, cuff‐induced blood‐flow restriction and mechanical pressure may further elevate limb discomfort (Scott et al. 2015). In contrast, heart rate increased across sets but did not differ between conditions. Although partial occlusion can increase HR through compensatory responses to reduced venous return and reduced cardiac preload (Hollander et al. 2010), this response may have been attenuated in the present protocol because BFR was applied only during brief inter‐set recovery periods, the cuffs were fully deflated during sprinting, and the occlusive stimulus was localized to the thighs. Moreover, HR during repeated‐sprint exercise is strongly influenced by central command, baroreflex, and metaboreflex responses to the sprint work itself (Amann 2012; Williamson et al. 2006), which may have outweighed any additional HR effect of the short recovery‐only BFR exposure.

Leg Power

The primary analysis showed a main effect of time, with no main effect of condition and no condition × time interaction. Within conditions, CMJ height significantly decreased in BFR (0.45–0.41 m; p < 0.001), but not in sham‐BFR (0.44–0.43 m; p = 0.093). This aligns with prior RSE literature, indicating that completion of a RSE protocol acutely induces neuromuscular fatigue in lower extremities (Morcillo et al. 2015). Nonetheless, a secondary change‐score analysis revealed a larger CMJ decline with BFR, suggesting that inter‐set BFR may exacerbate acute leg power alterations. Mechanistically, our NIRS data suggests that reduced reoxygenation and greater deoxygenation early in recovery under BFR may elevate metabolite accumulation and enhance group III/IV muscle afferent feedback (Allen et al. 2008; Amann et al. 2015), contributing to greater peripheral fatigue and impaired explosive performance. Future studies should use larger samples and include follow‐up assessments over 0–48 h to capture delayed effects and better inform training periodization.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. First, we did not measure blood lactate or other blood markers, so conclusions about the “metabolite accumulation–reflex–RPE/HR” pathway remain indirect. Second, participants were high‐level badminton athletes whose match play involves frequent changes of direction and stretch‐shortening actions, which may not reflect the muscle recruitment demands of a 5‐s cycle sprint; thus, generalization to other populations and sports should be cautious. Third, we used a fixed cuff pressure (130 mmHg) rather than individualizing pressure relative to each participant's arterial occlusion pressure, which does not account for inter‐individual differences in limb size, cuff fit, or vascular responsiveness.

Practical Applications

Under the current protocol and sample, applying BFR during inter‐set recovery did not reduce repeated‐sprint mechanical output but increased local hypoxia and exercise‐related sensations. In routine badminton training, inter‐set BFR may provide a practical way to add a local hypoxic stimulus during repeated‐sprint work when the goal is to increase peripheral physiological stress while maintaining sprint output. However, because BFR was associated with a greater post‐exercise CMJ decline, coaches should consider the potential trade‐off between increasing peripheral/perceptual load and preserving subsequent explosive performance. These strategies require validation in larger cohorts and across different sports, and should be tailored to individual responses.

Conclusion

In high‐level athletes producing high power outputs, inter‐set BFR during repeated‐sprint exercise increased local oxygenation‐related responses—characterized by lower recovery‐period SmO2 and slower early reoxygenation—while preserving repeated‐sprint mechanical output across sets. It also increased perceptual load (greater leg discomfort and session exertion), whereas heart‐rate responses rose similarly between conditions. Neuromuscular function integrity declined post‐session in both conditions, with the secondary change‐score analysis suggesting a slightly greater decrement with BFR. Future work should explore manipulating the work‐to‐rest ratio to increase peripheral physiological stress without impairing repeated‐sprint mechanical output or subsequent explosive performance, thereby enhancing adaptation while maintaining training quality.
