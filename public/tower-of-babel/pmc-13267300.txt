Effect of an oral care bundle on salivary pH, dry mouth and ventilator-associated pneumonia in intubated patients: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Gül Güneş Aktan, İsmet Eşer, Mehmet Uyar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMore serious and costly problems can be prevented with systematic and holistic oral care, which seems to be less important among nursing practices.ObjectivesTo determine the effectiveness of oral care bundle on oral health, saliva volume, saliva pH and VAP in intubated patients.MethodsThis randomized controlled trial data was collected between January and December 2021 in the Anesthesiology and Reanimation ICUs at a university hospital. Patients were randomly assigned to intervention and control groups. While routine oral care was applied to the control group (abeslang wrapped in gauze), the intervention group was given care with the oral care bundle created by the researchers (brushing teeth, applying ice water, moisturizing). The primary outcome was the oral health score (BOE), and the primary endpoint was defined as the BOE score at Day 5. Secondary outcomes included saliva volume, salivary pH, ventilator-associated pneumonia. Repeated measures were analyzed using two-way repeated measures ANOVA for normally distributed variables and the Brunner–Langer model for non-normally distributed data.ResultsA total of 35 patients were analyzed (intervention: n = 17; control: n = 18). A significant group × time interaction was observed for the BOE score (p < 0.001), indicating an improvement in oral health over time in the intervention group compared with the control group. Similarly, a significant group × time interaction was found for saliva volume (p < 0.001). For salivary pH, a statistically significant interaction was also observed (p < 0.001); however, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the subjective nature of the measurement method. Although the incidence of VAP and bacterial growth tended to be lower in the intervention group, the differences were not statistically significant between groups.ConclusionsThe intervention improved oral health outcomes in intubated ICU patients, particularly in terms of BOE score and saliva volume. However, findings related to salivary pH and VAP should be interpreted with caution due to methodological limitations and limited statistical power. Further studies with larger samples and more objective measurement methods are needed.Trial registrationThe study has been retrospectively registered on clinicaltrials.gov (Clinical Trials ID: NCT06258603/05.02.2024).

Introduction

The needs of intensive care unit (ICU) patients who cannot perform self-care activities independently are met by nurses [1]. Nurses face a wide range of priorities in care practices [2, 3]. The preservation of oral mucosal integrity is crucial because of its close relationship with systemic health, disease risk, self-image, comfort and nutrition. This problem becomes even more important when patients require endotracheal tube (ET) or mechanical ventilation (MV) support [4]. The loss of fibronectin, which has a protective role in the oral mucosa, occurs because of factors such as patches used for ET detection, high-intensity oxygen therapy, stress, and decreased salivation [1, 5]. Additionally, endotracheal tube placement provides a direct pathway for bacteria to enter the body, reduces the cough reflex, and decreases salivary flow, thereby inhibiting the mechanical removal of plaque by saliva [6].

Saliva content and flow rate are very important for maintaining oral and dental health [7]. Insufficient saliva volume and low salivary pH accelerate bacterial proliferation in the oral cavity and cause halitosis. Bacteria adhere to inflamed or bleeding gums, causing life-threatening sepsis. Patients breathing with MV should receive regular oral care to prevent bacterial infections [4, 8].

In the literature, oral care practices performed in accordance with protocols minimize the risk of developing complications, and bundle care practices used in different care areas increase the consistency of care [9, 10]. Considering the factors that negatively affect oral health, studies are continuing to determine the most effective regimen for ICU patients [11–14]. According to the literature, oral care of intubated patients is performed by the traditional method, which uses a single care method such as tooth brushing, cleaning with foam cloths, or wiping with gauze using various oral care solutions [11, 15–17]. However, effective oral care involves a comprehensive set of interventions that include hydration of the oral mucosa, moisturizing of the lips, and tooth brushing that mechanically removes plaque [18]. As mentioned, providing comprehensive care requires the creation of care bundle. A care bundle consists of the combined application of several parameters, each of which, when implemented individually, positively impacts the patient’s recovery and outcomes, while when implemented together, they achieve a better outcome than when implemented individually [10]. Although care bundle are often used in the literature to prevent VAP [19–21], in recent years they have begun to be used for the purposes of dry mouth and thirsty [9, 22], improving salivary pH (Jang & Shin, 2016), reducing microorganisms in the oral cavity [23] and maintaining oral health [4].

In this study, researchers developed an oral care bundle for patients. The study’s uniqueness stems from the diversity of the oral care bundle content and the measurement methods used to assess outcomes.

Study aim

The aim of this study was to determine the effectiveness of an oral care bundle on oral health, salivary pH, salivary volume, and VAP development in intubated patients.

Methods

Study design

This study used an experimental design as a randomized controlled trial with an intervention and a control group to evaluate the effectiveness of a combined oral care bundle. The study followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guidelines and flowchart (Fig. 1). The study has been retrospectively registered on clinicaltrials.gov (Clinical Trials ID: NCT06258603/05.02.2024).

Setting and subjects

This study was conducted with intubated patients followed in the Anesthesia and Reanimation ICU of a university hospital between January - December 2021 in TÜRKİYE. The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (a) aged between 18 and 70 years, (b) had received MV support and (c) were intubated orally and in the first 24 h of intubation. The exclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (a) were diagnosed with pneumonia or Sjögren’s syndrome, (b) had received radiotherapy and/or chemotherapy, (c) did not have teeth, (e) had head and neck trauma, (f) had platelet counts less than 50 thousand, and (g) had a semi-Fowler position that was not allowed.

Sampling

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.2 based on the primary outcome variable, namely the BOE score. The primary endpoint of the study was defined as the BOE score at Day 5. Based on previous studies conducted in intubated patients [4, 24], the mean BOE score was assumed to be 13 ± 3 in the intervention group and 15 ± 3.5 in the control group, representing a clinically meaningful difference. Under these assumptions, with a two-sided α level of 0.05 and a statistical power of 80%, the required sample size was calculated as 43 participants per group. Considering a potential attrition rate of approximately 20% in this critically ill population, the sample size was increased accordingly, and 50 patients were randomized to each group at baseline to ensure adequate statistical power despite possible losses during follow-up. Due to clinical and practical constraints, including loss to follow-up and the challenges associated with the COVID-19 pandemic (e.g., reduced patient availability, restricted clinical access, and increased workload in intensive care settings), the study was completed with 35 patients (17 in the intervention group and 18 in the control group). Based on the observed Day 5 BOE scores (12.64 ± 2.08 in the intervention group and 15.00 ± 2.05 in the control group), the standardized effect size was large (Cohen’s d = 1.14), and the achieved statistical power with the final sample was approximately 90% at a two-sided α level of 0.05.

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was performed using R version 3.1.3. Patients were stratified into two age groups (19–44 years and 45–70 years), and block randomization was applied within each stratum to ensure balanced group allocation. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent statistician who was not involved in patient recruitment, intervention, or outcome assessment. Allocation concealment was ensured using sealed, opaque envelopes. The randomization list was delivered to an ICU nurse, and group assignment was revealed only immediately before the intervention. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding of nurses and the researcher responsible for administering the intervention was not possible. Outcome assessment procedures were as follows:

Because the same researcher was involved in both intervention delivery and outcome assessment, outcome assessors were not blinded to group allocation. Therefore, the study should be considered as having non-blinded outcome assessment. To minimize potential bias, all data were coded prior to analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted by an independent statistician who was blinded to group allocation. Group codes were disclosed only after completion of the statistical analysis.

Data collection tools and methods

The case report form

The form includes nine data items concerning demographic and clinical descriptive characteristics (age, gender, ET size, Gloskow Coma Scale (GCS) score, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score, co-morbidity ect.) of the patients.

Bedside Oral Exam (BOE) tool

This tool was developed by Prendergast et al. by revising the Oral Assessment Guide. There are eight subheadings in the tool: swallowing, lips, tongue, saliva, mucous membrane, gums, teeth or dentures, and odor [5]. The validity and reliability study of the Turkish version of the tool was conducted by Aktan et al. (2025), who confirmed that the instrument is a valid and reliable tool for use in intensive care settings (Fig. 2). The BOE provides objective assessment with visual, numerical and verbal evaluation criteria that can be used in the oral health assessment of intubated patients. The total score ranges from eight points (excellent oral health) to 24 points (impaired oral health) [25].

Patient follow-up form

This form was created by the researchers. The form includes data such as the BOE score, saliva volume, pH and VAP diagnosis.

Outcome

The primary outcome was the BOE score, with the primary endpoint defined as the BOE score at Day 5. Secondary outcomes were saliva volume, salivary pH, and VAP.

Interventions

To determine the functionality of the prepared forms and the oral care bundle, a pilot study was conducted with five patients from each group. Patients who participated in the pilot study were not included in the sample. The same researcher performed the interventions common to both groups. Descriptive data were collected on the first day that participants were included in the study. Oral health was assessed using the BOE tool and a light source before the first oral care every morning for five consecutive days. Saliva volume and pH were measured before the first oral care on Day 1, 3 and 5. Both groups received oral care four times a day.

Saliva volume was measured using the ERC Schirmer Tear Test Strip® (ERC Health Products Inc., Ankara, TÜRKİYE). The strip was held with forceps at the tip and placed under the tongue; after three minutes, the wetted length was read in millimeters. Saliva pH was measured using the Merck® Universal pH 0–14 indicator (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, GERMANY). The indicator strip was placed on the tongue and held for one minute, after which the resulting color change was compared with the reference color scale on the bundle. The color similarity was determined by consensus between the patient’s primary nurse and the researcher during the measurement. However, as this method is based on visual interpretation, no formal intra- or inter-rater reliability analysis was conducted, and the measurement may be subject to observer-related variability.

VAP diagnosis was monitored daily. VAP was diagnosed by a physician according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria for hospital-acquired infections.

Intervention group- combination oral care bundle

The bundle developed by the researchers included combined care such as brushing teeth, applying ice water, wiping with a foam swab, and moisturizing the inside of the mouth and lips. Tooth brushing, considered the gold standard in oral care, effectively cleans teeth and prevents plaque formation [2, 26], so it was designated as the primary care practice in this bundle. The care bundle, designed to prevent VAP, emphasizes oral care and recommends chlorhexidine gluconate as a solution [10]. Chlorhexidine gluconate solution was chosen for the oral care bundle developed based on this evidence. Despite the widespread use of chlorhexidine, it is known to cause mucosal dryness, and dry mouth disrupts the integrity of the oral mucosa [23]. Furthermore, because salivation is essential for maintaining oral health, moisturizing gel [22, 27] and ice water application [28] were added to the oral care bundle. The oral care of the intervention group was performed with the oral care bundle.

General procedure

Oral care was provided four times a day. Care 1 (09:00) and Care 3 (17:00), which included attempts to brush the teeth, wipe the oral cavity with ice water, and moisturize the oral cavity and lips, were carried out by the researcher. Care 2 (13:00) and Care 4 (03:00), which included wiping the oral cavity with Kloreben® solution and moisturize the oral cavity, were performed by the primary nurse. The primary nurse received practical training on oral care. Prior to each oral care, nurses adhered to standard hygiene protocols. The procedure was explained to the patient, who was positioned appropriately. ET cuff pressure was checked, and oral as well as intratracheal secretions were aspirated.

Brushing teeth

This procedure was performed in Care 1 and 3. To ensure patient safety and prevent dislodgement of the ET during the care, the patient’s primary nurse assisted the researcher. The ET tube ligament was gently loosened with the assistance of the primary nurse. If the patient refused to open their mouth or clenched their teeth, an oral airway was inserted. The oral cavity was conceptually divided into four quadrants: right upper/lower jaw and left upper/lower jaw. Brushing was performed from the most posterior tooth toward the midline, including the gingiva and all tooth surfaces. The right and left buccal mucosa, palate, and sublingual surfaces were brushed sequentially using a soft toothbrush [29, 30]. Brushing teeth were performed with a pediatric toothbrush, Oral-B® and a commercial solution containing 0.12% chlorhexidine gluconate and 0.15% benzydamine hydrochloride, Kloreben®. The toothbrush was dipped in solution, with excess solution drained prior to brushing. Tooth surfaces were cleaned using short horizontal (back-and-forth) and circular motions, applying gentle pressure with approximately five brush strokes per surface for two minutes [30, 31]. Residues removed from the teeth during brushing and accumulated liquids in the oral cavity were cleaned using a foam swab. Then ET is fixed on the other side of the mouth.

Cleaning the mouth with foam swab

This procedure was performed by the primary nurse in Care 2 and 4. A foam swab was dipped in the Kloreben® solution, and the excess solution was drained. The inside of the mouth was cleaned with the foam swab in the order specified for tooth brushing.

Ice water application

This procedure was performed in Care 1 and 3. Ice cubes used for oral care were prepared in disposable ice bags with drinking water at a pH of 7.5. The temperature of the prepared ice water was checked with a thermometer. Foam swabs were dipped in 7–15 °C ice water, and the excess solution was drained. The inside of the mouth was wiped in the same order as for tooth brushing. The ice water application was performed one hour after tooth brushing.

Moisturizing the mouth and lips.

Four times a day after each oral care moisturizing gel Konix® was applied to the mouth and lips with a foam swab.

Control group- Gauze-wrapped tongue depressor

Measurements and evaluations were performed by the same researcher. Oral care for the control group was performed four times a day by the primary nurses, using a tongue depressor wrapped in gauze soaked in Kloreben® solution. To minimize differences in practice among the nurses, the researcher provided oral care training to the nurses.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed with Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL) 22.0 software program. The homogeneity of descriptive characteristics was tested via the Pearson chi-square test and the Fisher chi-square test. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean (standard deviation) for normally distributed variables and median (minimum–maximum) for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were expressed as number (n) and percentage (%). Baseline characteristics between the intervention and control groups were assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), as recommended for randomized studies. For categorical variables with more than two categories, SMD was calculated based on pooled proportions, while for binary variables, SMD was calculated separately for each category. The effects of the intervention over time were analyzed using two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables (BOE score and saliva volume), including group (intervention vs. control), time, and group × time interaction effects. When significant effects were detected, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction. For salivary pH, which did not meet normality assumptions, a nonparametric approach was applied using the Brunner–Langer model (LD-F1 design), and Wald-type statistics were reported.

Results

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of participants. A total of 35 patients were included in the analysis, with 17 patients in the intervention group and 18 in the control group. The mean age of the patients was similar between groups (intervention: 49.11 ± 16.27; control: 46.66 ± 16.04; SMD = 0.152). Likewise, baseline scores were comparable, including the GCS (intervention: 8.63 ± 2.61; control: 7.38 ± 2.56; SMD = 0.484) and APACHE II score (intervention: 17.76 ± 8.63; control: 22.22 ± 5.59; SMD = 0.613). The distribution of sex was relatively balanced between groups (female: 41.2% vs. 22.2%; SMD = 0.410). For endotracheal tube (SMD = 0.778) size and identified in the reason for ICU admission (SMD = 1.310) a noticeable imbalance was observed between groups. Regarding comorbidities, heart disease (SMD = 0.408) and cancer (SMD = 0.330) demonstrated relatively higher imbalance compared to other conditions. Similarly, for medication use, several variables showed moderate to large imbalances, particularly diuretics (SMD = 0.514), antiarrhythmic drugs (SMD = 0.470), beta blockers (SMD = 0.470), anesthetics (SMD = 0.884), and inhalers (SMD = 0.686) (Table 1).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the effects of an oral care bundle containing combined oral care protocols that we have not previously encountered in the literature on oral health, saliva volume, pH and VAP. Our findings show that the application of an oral care bundle has a significant positive effect on oral health, saliva volume, and pH. However, some of the measurement parameters, particularly salivary pH, were based on visual assessment and may therefore include a degree of subjectivity. In addition, BOE scoring was not fully blinded, and certain evaluations were performed by the same researcher, which may have introduced observer-related variability.

General oral health

Nurses should diagnose patients’ oral health using appropriate assessment tools and plan care that includes combinations of care tailored to each patient’s needs. Nurses face difficulties in the oral care practices of intubated patients, such as intraoral visibility, patient cooperation, and the placement of care equipment into the mouth, and these difficulties may cause patients to receive incomplete and inadequate care [32]. The findings showed that oral health was moderately dysfunctional according to the BOE score at the beginning of the study in both groups. In the present study, the BOE score, which reflects oral health status, showed a significant improvement over time in the intervention group compared with the control group. This finding suggests that the oral care bundle may contribute to maintaining and improving oral health in intubated patients, who are at high risk of oral health deterioration during ICU stay. However, BOE assessment was not fully blinded, and the same researcher was involved in both intervention delivery and outcome assessment. This may have introduced observer-related bias.

The findings revealed the improvement effect of the oral care bundle on oral health. Similar to these findings, in a six-day follow-up study comparing the combined oral care protocol (toothbrush, nonfoaming toothpaste, chlorhexidine, and mouth moisturizer) with routine care (chlorhexidine-soaked gauze) in intubated patients, the oral health of patients receiving combined care improved over time, and the difference between groups was significant [33]. Although oral health deterioration was detected over time in the control group, no statistically significant difference was found in the subscales of the BOE instrument (swallowing, lips, tongue, saliva, mucous membrane, gums, teeth or dentures, and odor) between Day 1 and Day 5.

Saliva volume

Equipment such as ET, orogastric catheters, and airways can cause xerostomia by exposing the mouth to constant air and disrupting salivary flow [4]. Within 48 h after extubation, most patients had a 43% decrease in unstimulated salivary flow, thick saliva, and dry mouth [34]. In salivary secretion studies, saliva secretion < 25 mm/3 min was considered hyposalivation [35–37]. A study comparing stimulated salivary flow in preoperative patients and intubated ICU patients found that inadequate salivary flow in intubated patients caused severe xerostomia (Dennesen et al., 2003). In the present study, according to the initial measurement, hyposalivation was detected in both groups. As time progressed, saliva volume showed a significant improvement over time in the intervention group and decrease in the control group. This finding suggests that the oral care bundle, particularly the use of iced water and moisturizing applications, may support salivary secretion and have a healing effect on dry mouth in intubated patients. Adequate saliva plays a key role in preserving oral mucosal integrity and reducing the risk of oral complications. Therefore, the observed increase in saliva volume may be considered a clinically relevant outcome in this patient population. There are studies in the literature that support the study findings. In the study of Vonstein et al. (2019), ice water and a lip moisturizer were regularly applied to ICU patients eight times a day, and a decrease in the severity of dry mouth and thirst was detected in the intervention group compared with the control group [38]. The application of a thirst bundle containing sterile cold water spray and lip moisturizer reduces the thirst and severity of dry mouth in ICU patients [27]. Jang et al. (2016) reported that the severity of dry mouth was lower in patients who received a combined oral care bundle [4].

Saliva pH

ET causes flora changes, mucosal dryness and inflammation. This situation causes dental plaque and organisms to increase rapidly, saliva production decreases, and the pH decreases, creating a suitable environment for microorganisms to multiply [39]. The mean pH of unstimulated saliva is approximately 6.8 [40]. In the present study, according to the initial measurement, the salivary pH in both groups was lower than the value expected in healthy individuals. Salivary pH showed a significant group × time interaction, indicating a relative improvement in the intervention group over time and decreased in the control group. This finding suggests that the oral care bundle may contribute to maintaining oral pH balance in intubated patients. However, salivary pH was assessed using a colorimetric method based on visual interpretation, and the final value was determined by consensus between the primary nurse and the researcher. In addition, outcome assessment was not fully blinded. These factors may have introduced measurement bias and limited the precision of the pH assessment. Similarly, in one study, after combined care, the saliva pH of the intervention group increased, and that of the control group decreased. Additionally, while there was no difference in pH values before the intervention, the difference between the groups was found to be significant after the intervention [4]. In the study by Moon et al. (2015), when the oral mucosa was wiped with cold water or cold saline, the saliva pH increased in both groups [41]. In parallel with these studies, wiping the oral mucosa with ice water may have contributed to the increase in saliva pH.

Brushing teeth

According to the American Dental Association’s recommendation of brushing teeth twice a day to maintain oral health, removing plaque biofilms, which are a potential source of infection in critically ill patients, is an important practice [42]. In a study comparing the care protocol (brushing teeth, wiping the teeth, tongue and palate with antiseptic solution, moisturizing the lips, and suctioning the mouth and pharynx) with routine care, the development of VAP differed significantly in patients who applied the care protocol [43]. In studies where tooth brushing was applied (chlorhexidine gluconate was used), the difference between the groups was significant, and tooth brushing significantly reduced VAP, and the results are different from those of this study [15, 44, 45].

In the study conducted by Lorente et al. (2012), the control group received oral care by impregnating chlorhexidine gauze and injecting it into the oral cavity, while the intervention group manually brushed their teeth with a chlorhexidine-impregnated brush, and no difference was found between the groups [46]. In a meta-analysis evaluating the effect of toothbrushing on VAP, Alhazzani et al. (2013) reported that four studies demonstrated lower VAP rates in the intervention groups, although the difference reached statistical significance in only one study [47]. Similarly, a systematic review by Hua et al. (2016) found no significant impact of toothbrushing on VAP incidence [48], while another review by Roberts and Moule (2011) reported inconsistent findings but concluded that toothbrushing was beneficial in maintaining oral health [49]. The study results are consistent with this study findings. Furthermore, a separate study in the literature examined bacteria grown on toothbrushes used by intubated patients; 35 brushes (97%) were found to have bacterial contamination, 27 brushes grew at least two bacteria, and 13 contained antimicrobial-resistant bacteria. The most common bacteria were Klebsiella pneumoniae (21%) and Acinetobacter Baumannii (18%) [50].

VAP and lung secretions

MV is associated with increased clinical risks and healthcare costs, beginning with deterioration in oral health and extending to complications such as VAP [4]. Impairment of oral health, alterations in salivary flow, and changes in oral microbial composition may facilitate the colonization of pathogenic microorganisms and increase the risk of lower respiratory tract infections [51]. In the present study, Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli were the most frequently identified microorganisms in lung secretions in both groups. No statistically significant difference was found between the groups in terms of VAP incidence. The lower rate of VAP in the intervention group (11.8%) compared with the control group (33.3%). Although the incidence of VAP appeared to be lower in the intervention group, the study was not adequately powered to detect statistically significant differences for this outcome. Previous studies have shown that oral health deteriorates during intubation and partially recovers after extubation, while colonization with gram-negative bacteria and yeast increases during the intubation period [52]. Similarly, Prendergast et al. (2009) reported an increased incidence of VAP during MV, with pathogens such as Enterobacter, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Staphylococcus aureus being commonly identified ([52]. In addition, studies have demonstrated a strong relationship between oral and respiratory pathogens, showing that similar microorganisms may be present in both oral biofilms and tracheal aspirates, particularly in patients who develop VAP [53]. Dry mouth may disrupt salivary pH balance and compromise the protective functions of saliva, thereby increasing susceptibility to pathogenic colonization [54]. In the present study, more pronounced hyposalivation and lower salivary pH were observed in the control group. Previous evidence suggests that reduced salivary flow and lower pH facilitate the adhesion and colonization of pathogens such as Klebsiella pneumoniae on oral epithelial surfaces [55]. Furthermore, interventions aimed at maintaining oral moisture have been shown to reduce microbial contamination; for example, the use of moisturizing agents has been associated with lower cuff contamination rates in ventilated patients [56].

Strengths and limitations

Since the application was performed with intubated patients, saliva pH and measurements were difficult, especially in trauma patients. Chlorhexidine gluconate solution was routinely used in the oral care of intubated patients in the clinic where the study was conducted, and the solution was not changed. To standardize the routine oral care to be applied to the study control group, the study was conducted only in an ICU, and the study was completed with a limited number of patients who met the criteria. In addition, since the study was conducted during the COVID-19 outbreak, the clinics of infected patients were separated, so the number of patients reached decreased. Another limitation is that a limited literature can be found on combined oral care. Access to more advanced aspirator toothbrushes instead of the pediatric toothbrush used in the study was not available. One important limitation of this study is that salivary pH was assessed using a colorimetric method based on visual interpretation without formal evaluation of intra- or inter-rater reliability. This may have introduced subjective bias and limited the precision of the pH measurements. Finally, lack of blinding may have introduced measurement bias.

Conclusion

As a result, studies have shown that the oral health, saliva volume and salivary pH of intubated patients are impaired. Furthermore, these findings suggest that combining oral care with different tools and methods may be more effective in terms of oral health, saliva volume, and pH compared to oral care using a single method.

Implications and recommendations for practice

Nurses should systematically diagnose oral health with a valid oral health assessment scale and apply individualized oral care that includes different care combinations according to the patient’s needs. Measurement tools that can objectively measure variables such as saliva volume, pH and dental plaque can be included in care. Patients’ behaviors, such as covering the mouth, coughing, biting care materials, and convulsions, were observed more frequently. To avoid endangering patient safety, the use of different alternative materials instead of the foam swabs used in the wiping process is recommended.
