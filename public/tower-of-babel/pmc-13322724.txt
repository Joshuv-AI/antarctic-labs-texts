A prospective study of anterior versus posterior approach for traumatic thoracolumbar fractures: A systemic review
Authors: Deepak Ranade, Apurva Satish Lachake, Ramis Aziz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:The objective of this study was to compare the results of reconstruction of the spine with anterior corpectomy and fusion versus pedicle screw fixation after the laminectomy technique in the treatment of thoracolumbar burst fractures (TLBFs).Summary:TLBFs account for the most common spinal trauma. There exists no single consensus on the best approach to surgical fixation of thoracolumbar fractures. However, its appropriate management remains a point of bias. Traditionally, it has been considered that the anterior approach has more morbidity in terms of more blood loss and longer operation time and complications. However, from the standpoint of the neurological and functional outcome, there exists no statistical difference between these two approaches. Yet, explaining from a biomechanical perspective, there exist some studies which provide the anterior approach with few, if, not many, advantages over the posterior approach. This has led to a renewed interest in anterior approaches to thoracolumbar traumatic burst fractures. We explore this finding with our case series on thoracolumbar fracture treatment with the anterior approach in terms of intra-op events: duration and blood loss, postoperative outcome – neurological improvement status, and long-term follow-up and functional outcome.Materials and Methods:A total of 32 patients (from year January 2015 to December 2017) with TLBFs were divided into two groups randomly. Group A was treated with an anterior approach and Group B was treated with a posterior approach with pedicle screw fixation after laminectomy and decompression. Patients with a minimum follow-up period of 24 months were prospectively evaluated. We reviewed their neurological and clinical outcomes in their follow-up period. Then, we performed a literature review via PubMed for publications based on the comparison between anterior and posterior approaches and we included 29 articles based on our criteria. We focused on three main determinants-acute perioperative events (which include blood loss, and long duration of surgery,) neurological deterioration of function, and lastly, functional outcome—Frankel score for neurological improvement and any presence of adverse construct defects in hardware.Results:All patients in this study who underwent anterior corpectomy and fusion achieved solid fusion, with significant neurological improvement. Results showed significant differences between the American Spinal Injury Association motor score, and the Frankel scale (P < 0.05) between the two Groups A and B. At the final follow-up, both groups showed improvement in their neurological function (Group A > Group B). In Group B, the operative time was shorter (P < 0.05), and intraoperative blood loss (P < 0.05) was less.

INTRODUCTION

Approximately 90% of spine fractures occur in the thoracolumbar junction between T11 and L2. This is called the transitional zone from the upper rigid kyphotic thoracic spine, while the lordotic lumbar spine is more mobile.[12] Burst fractures, particularly, comprise approximately 50% of all thoracolumbar trauma.[3] The etiology is typically related to powerful axial compression forces, such as falls from heights (34%–54%), motor vehicle accidents (51%–65%), or high-intensity sports (9%). While many of these can be treated conservatively, the surgery is indicated in cases of neurologic deficit or when there is potential or observed instability.

Historically, burst fractures have been classified as involving the anterior and middle spinal columns, as explained by the Denis three-column spinal model.[1] The creation of thoracolumbar injury classification and severity score has provided a valuable tool for help in surgical decision-making. However, even after an overwhelming amount of descriptive and clinical studies addressing traumatic thoracolumbar burst fractures (TLBFs), debate remains on the appropriate surgical approach. Conventionally, an anterior approach was preferred for decompression and stabilization, but with improved stability through modern pedicle screw constructs, all posterior approaches have gained acceptance as an adequate alternative.

More recently, despite the controversies in clinical decision-making, it was believed that anterior decompression and reconstruction supplemented with instrumentation was superior to posterior pedicle screw instrumentation for highly unstable burst fracture.[45678910] The development of posterior pedicle screw segmental instrumentation could allow more rigid fixation.[611121314151617] However, it is generally believed that canal decompression is limited and often incomplete by the posterior approach for the treatment of TLBF associated with neurological deficit.

In this review, we will present our data for anterior approach/anterior reconstruction for the treatment of TLBFs concerning biomechanical considerations, canal decompression, and neurologic function, as well as perioperative complications and patient outcomes. This will be followed by a review of the literature.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

In this prospective study, 32 patients were included, who had a single-level TLBF. These patients were divided into two groups comprising 16 patients in each group: Group A was treated through the anterior approach and Group B was treated through a posterior approach. The patients with a single-level Denis type A, type B, or type C burst fracture of the thoracolumbar spine (T12-L2) associated with neurological deficit, aged from 14 to 70 years, with confirmed spinal cord compression on imaging studies with a duration from injury to surgery was not more than a week duration were included in the study. The patients with Denis type D or E burst fractures, pathologic or osteoporotic fractures, major fractures of other sites (other than T12-L2), and a history of spine surgery were the exclusion criteria. Written informed consent was taken from all patients included in the study.

Surgical technique

In Group A, patients underwent a right-side anterolateral retroperitoneal approach with single-level corpectomy and decompression, followed by bony fusion and reconstruction of the spine. A titanium mesh cage filled with an autologous bone graft which was derived from the resected vertebral body (corpectomy) was used for the reconstruction of the spine. Each patient was evaluated immediately and 3, 6, and 12 months after surgery, and annually after that. Restrictions of activity were done for at least 2 weeks after surgery; vigorous activities, heavy work, and sports were restricted for 6 months.

In Group B, patients were operated in a prone position using a standard posterior midline approach. Pedicle screws were placed from 2 vertebral bodies above the fractured site to 2 vertebral bodies below it. Rods were placed on either side temporarily to fix the fractured vertebral body. Laminectomy was performed in the vertebrae which were fixed using a pedicle screw and rod, to achieve decompression of the cord. The temporary fixation was loosened and refixed after an appropriate pressure.

Clinical outcome

Frankel scale, American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) motor score, operative time, intraoperative blood loss were used to evaluate the patient.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as means ± standard deviation. Statistical evaluation, including separate unpaired t-test, Pearson w2 test, and contingency table w2 test, was conducted for comparison between Groups A and B, and the Mann–Whitney test was used as a nonparametric test to compare the distributions in the two treatment groups. The level of significance was set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

From January 2015 to December 2017, a total of 32 patients who had TLBFs (single level) associated with neurological deficits were included in the prospective study. Both groups were comparable (P > 0.05) concerning the age, sex, duration from injury to surgery, time of follow-up visit, the levels of fracture, and severity of fractures [Table 1]. In both treatment groups, patients were prospectively followed up for minimum of 24 months (range, 24–72 months; average, 46.2 ± 14.5 months in Group A vs. 43.5 ± 13.8 months in Group B, P > 0.05). DISCUSSION

The bias of which operative approach should be used for the surgical treatment of thoracolumbar burst fracture associated with neurological deficit remains controversial. Subtotal corpectomy, decompression followed by placement of a structural bone graft, and anterior instrumentation [Figures 1-5] have allowed direct reconstruction of the weight-bearing column and fusion of the column.[910181920212223242526272829] These authors showed that neurological deficit was always caused by impact and compression to the ventral surface of the spinal cord in most patients with thoracolumbar fractures, and the anterior approach could provide optimal direct exposure for visualization of the ventral aspect of the dura mater during surgical decompression. Therefore, these authors suggested that such a procedure would offer patients with neurological deficits a better chance at improvement than others. But this is surgically more challenging. Although laminectomy and posterior stabilization (posterior instrumentation) may provide spine stabilization sufficient decompression and neurological recovery in thoracolumbar burst fractures is better with an anterior approach to obtain satisfactory spinal canal decompression. This study has shown that the Frankel Scale and ASIA motor score on admission and at the final follow-up Group A showed better neurological improvement than Group B. Therefore, we were sure to think that the decompression by anterior approach was an as complete and better treatment option for decompression, fixation, and reconstruction of the spine.

According to Denis, the middle column of the spine is the key determinant of spinal stability and its involvement is a sufficient criterion for instability. This concept was supported by the findings of biomechanical studies.[30] Titanium mesh cages filled with autologous bone grafts have been used to reconstruct the defects created by corpectomy done to decompress the cord in thoracolumbar burst fractures.[31323334] This allows the correction of kyphosis and restoration of height.

Therefore, to reinstate from a biomechanical perspective, an anterior approach is preferable in patients with high load-sharing scores yet, in whom we wish to preserve levels and there is a less kyphotic deformity. Therefore, taking biomechanical consideration and alignment perspective, short-segment posterior instrumentation may be inadequate alone.

It is well known that the anterior approach can provide excellent exposure for the reconstruction of the spine with titanium mesh cages filled with autologous bone graft for fracture with loss of support of the anterior and middle columns. Furthermore, posterior muscle structures, which are important for the dynamic stabilization of the spine, are spared in the anterior approach. Laminectomy may destabilize the spine with the progression of the deformity without bony fusion, though posterior pedicle screw segmental instrumentation could allow rigid fixation. The anterior approach with a screw-rod on the vertebral body provides good decompression and solid fusion.[10] Some authors[213536] recommended the combined anterior and posterior approach for the management of burst fractures with significant posterior column injury, but in this approach, the operative time is longer and the surgical trauma is higher.

McLain et al. reviewed 52 cases with thoracolumbar injuries, 19 of which underwent short-segment posterior fixation and on follow-up had a high rate of construct failure, most often characterized by screw bending.[37] Similar studies by Carl et al. found that screws were bent or broken.[9] Thus, several studies have demonstrated high failure rates when the posterior fixation is not augmented with an anterior reconstruction.

Sagittal alignment does appear to be improved with anterior column reconstruction. Been et al. performed a retrospective review of 46 patients who sustained thoracolumbar burst fractures and were treated with either anterior-posterior stabilization or posterior-only distraction and stabilization. Loss of reduction >5° and instrumentation failure were significantly higher in all posterior groups.

Sasso et al. compared patients undergoing anterior-only reconstruction and short-segment posterior instrumentation. While there were no hardware failures in either group, the posterior group had an average loss of 8.1° of sagittal plane correction compared to only 1.8° in the anterior group.[9] Thus, inferring from the above data, anterior reconstruction appears superior to short-segment posterior instrumentation.

Neurological and functional outcome

For patients with a burst fracture presenting with a neurologic deficit, the goals of surgery include decompression of the neural elements as well as stabilization. Proponents of anterior decompression argue that the anterior approach is ideal for visualization and decompression, as the majority of compression is secondary to the retropulsion vertebral body, which is easily accessible through this approach. Furthermore, it is well known that the anterior approach can provide excellent exposure for the reconstruction of the spine with titanium mesh cages filled with autologous bone graft for fracture with loss of support of the anterior and middle columns. The anterior approach has better decompression without enormous manipulation of neural structures. In other words, adequate decompression can be easily achieved via direct access as compared to the posterior approach which requires retraction of nerve roots and dural sac to access the posterior wall. The disc structures adjacent to the fractured vertebral bodies are often torn and interspersed. The anterior approach enables the surgeon to remove the destroyed disc fragments under direct vision completely and achieve stabilization of the anterior spine with the implantation of bone grafts.

CONCLUSION

The Frankel scale and the ASIA motor score on follow-up were improved better in a patient with an anterior approach than in posterior pedicle screw segmental instrumentation. This study has provided evidence for the efficacy of reconstruction of the spine with anterior corpectomy and fusion over posterior pedicle screw fixation after laminectomy (segmental instrumentation) for surgical treatment of thoracolumbar burst fractures.

Given our case series, we want to conclude that it is quite evident that the anterior approach is better than the posterior approach for the treatment of thoracolumbar burst fractures both from a biomechanical perspective and from the neurological and functional outcome.
