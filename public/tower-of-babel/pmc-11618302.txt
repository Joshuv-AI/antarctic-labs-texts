Biogenic synthesis of copper oxide nanoparticles using Eucalyptus globulus Leaf Extract and its impact on germination and Phytochemical composition of Lactuca sativa
Authors: Sadaf Anwaar, Faiza Altaf, Tauseef Anwar, Huma Qureshi, Ejaz Hussain Siddiqi, Walid Soufan, Wajid Zaman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGreen nanomaterials are increasingly used to improve plant growth and phytochemical traits. This study employed Eucalyptus globulus leaf extract, a medicinal plant, as a bio-reductant and capping agent to synthesize copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs), which were applied as seed primers for Lactuca sativa (lettuce), an annual species prized for its short germination time and rich bioactive compounds. Characterization of CuO-NPs using FTIR, XRD, SEM, and EDX confirmed their purity, crystalline structure, and an average particle size of 74.66 nm. The CuO-NPs were applied at concentrations of 0.01 mg/ml, 0.02 mg/ml, 0.03 mg/ml, and 0.04 mg/ml. At the highest concentration (0.04 mg/ml), significant reductions in physical growth parameters were observed, with plant length, height, and width measuring 7.85 cm, 5.50 cm, and 3.48 cm, respectively, compared to 13.70 cm, 11.52 cm, and 11.18 cm in control plants. Phytochemical analysis identified tannins, alkaloids, phytosterols, saponins, flavonoids, and glycosides in all methanolic extracts, while carotenoids were absent at higher concentrations (0.03 mg/ml and 0.04 mg/ml) due to phytotoxicity. FTIR analysis revealed a prominent peak at 858 cm⁻¹ at 0.01 mg/ml, indicating the presence of antioxidant-rich aromatic phenyl compounds. In conclusion, the study demonstrates that CuO-NPs synthesized using Eucalyptus globulus extract enhance phytochemical constituents at optimal concentrations but inhibit growth and reduce key phytochemicals at higher doses. Future research should optimize nanoparticle concentrations to minimize phytotoxicity while maximizing beneficial effects on plant growth and bioactive compounds.Keywords: Green synthesis, Eco-beneficial, Sustainable, Environmentally friendly, Plant-mediated synthesisSubject terms: Plant sciences, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Lactuca sativa (lettuce), belonging to the family Asteraceae, is an annual herb characterized by a thin taproot and an erect, aerial stem. The plant typically has a short stem bearing broad leaves, with leaf shapes varying from elliptic to triangular or orbicular, and can grow to a height of 10–100 cm. It was first cultivated by the ancient Egyptians, later spreading to Greece and Rome before becoming established in Europe1,2. L. sativa, a cool-weather crop, thrives in spring and fall, with an optimal germination temperature of 16–18 °C (60–65 °F). According to FAOSTAT data from 2010, lettuce production in Pakistan reached 135 tons, with a yield of 0.13 tons per hectare3,4. Globally, China leads in lettuce production, followed by the U.S. (22%) and Western Europe (13%). L. sativais rich in bioactive compounds such as folate, beta-carotene, lutein, phenolics, and vitamins, which contribute to its therapeutic properties, including antidiabetic, cholesterol-lowering, antioxidant, anticancer, antiviral, and anti-inflammatory effects, as demonstrated in both in vivo and in vitro studies5. It was chosen as the test plant in this study due to its fast growth, non-heading type, loose leaves, and thick stem, making it the most frequently cultivated variety in Pakistan and India.

Over the past decade, nanotechnology has advanced significantly and emerged as a highly dynamic field of research. Nanoparticles, with dimensions ranging from 1 to 100 nm, exhibit unique and measurable physicochemical properties6. The green synthesis of nanoparticles using medicinal plants has gained popularity due to the plants’ rich content of reducing and stabilizing biochemical molecules7. In this study, Eucalyptus globulus leaf extract serves as a stabilizer in the green synthesis of copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs). E. globulus, known for its anti-diabetic, anthelminthic, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and antioxidant properties, is rich in phenols and eucalyptus oil, as well as tannins, saponins, glycosides, reducing sugars, terpenoids, and flavonoids, which play key roles in the bio-reduction of metal ions8–10.

Bio-based methods for nanoparticle synthesis are well-characterized, highly stable, and considered safer than traditional chemical methods11,12. In nanobiotechnology, nano-sensors and nanomaterials are increasingly utilized for the controlled release of pesticides, fertilizers, and nutrients, enhancing food quality and safety13. Historically, nanoparticle production relied on physical and chemical methods, which were often toxic, hazardous, and costly. Recent advancements in plant nanotechnology have introduced green synthesis methods, offering a novel and eco-friendly approach14,15. This green synthesis involves three fundamental components: the solvent medium, reducing agents, and stabilizing agents. Plant extracts, with their photochemical constituents, function as both reducing and stabilizing agents in this process16. Consequently, green nanotechnology has paved the way for the development of nanomaterials with high efficacy and potential17. However, it is important to note that at high concentrations, metal nanoparticles can cause DNA damage, disrupt cell division, and inhibit gene expression regulatory mechanisms18.

Nanoparticles, when used as nano-fertilizers or in nano-emulsions, have been reported to enhance the availability of micronutrients to plants, particularly during the seedling stage. This improvement in nutrient availability promotes plant growth and reduces reliance on chemically toxic and environmentally harmful fertilizers. Research indicates that copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs) can boost growth and increase dry biomass across various plant species, even at low concentrations. Consequently, this study hypothesizes that treating Lactuca sativaseedlings with CuO-NPs will enhance nutrient bioavailability to the roots and other plant parts, thereby improving seed vigor and germination potential19,20.

Seed priming with nanoparticles has shown promising results in various crops; for example, Zea mays treated with ZnO-NPs, Solanum lycopersicum with AgNPs, and Gossypiumwith CeO2-NPs exhibited improved growth performance, including enhanced germination rates and photosynthetic attributes21. Copper, an essential metal element for plant growth, plays a critical role in redox reactions, acts as a cofactor in superoxide dismutase and phenol oxidases, and is involved in electron transport within photosynthetic and respiratory chains22. Plant-mediated synthesis of nanoparticles, including CuO-NPs, is increasingly favored for its stability, specificity, and eco-friendly characteristics23. The growing interest in the biosynthesis of CuO-NPs is driven by their environmental sustainability, economic viability, and procedural simplicity24.

This study focuses on synthesizing copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs) through a green synthesis approach and characterizing them using a range of analytical techniques. The objectives include evaluating the effectiveness of CuO-NPs on Lactuca sativa by priming seeds with various concentrations and growing them under controlled greenhouse conditions. The study aims to record physical growth parameters and perform phytochemical analyses on both treated and control plant extracts. Additionally, it seeks to investigate the impact of CuO-NPs on the occurrence, displacement, and rearrangement of functional groups in plant extracts using Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. UV-Vis spectroscopy will also be employed to analyze the methanolic extracts from both treated and control groups.

Materials and methods

Green synthesis of copper oxide nanoparticle

The plant material of Eucalyptus globuluswas collected from the Islamabad area (N 33° 39’ 41.13”, E 73° 1’ 32.7684”) and thoroughly washed with tap water. The leaves were then dried in the shade and ground into a fine powder25. The drying process ensured that the plant material remained uncontaminated, as the leaves were kept in non-humid conditions, preventing fungal or bacterial growth. For the preparation of the plant extract, 40 grams of the powdered plant material were accurately weighed using an electronic balance. This powder was then mixed with 400 ml of distilled water in an 800 ml flask. The mixture was subjected to uniform shaking and homogenization in a shaking incubator set at 25°C and 250 rpm26. After extraction, the mixture was filtered. The sequential procedure is shown in Fig. 1.

Sequential steps in green synthesis of copper oxide nanoparticles.

For the reaction mixture, 400 ml of the prepared plant extract was combined with 7.265 g of copper acetate salt solution. This mixture was gradually added to a hot plate set at 50 °C and 1500 rpm and maintained at this temperature and agitation for 3 h. The amount of copper acetate salt was calculated using the formula provided below.

The molecular weight of salt/100 × (volume of plant extract).

A distinct color change in the reaction mixture on the hot plate initially detected the formation of copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs). Following this observation, the mixture was subjected to centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 15 min27. After centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully drained from each falcon tube. The remaining pellet was washed with distilled water two to three times to ensure thorough cleaning28. The cleaned pellets were resuspended in distilled water and spread across petri dishes. These petri dishes were then placed in a drying oven overnight to allow the pellets to dry completely. After approximately 24 h, the petri dishes were removed from the oven, and the dried pellets were scraped into a China dish29. The fine powder of nanoparticles collected in the China dish was then annealed in a blast furnace30. The annealed CuO-NPs were analyzed using a series of radiation tests, including UV-Vis spectroscopy, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDX), to confirm that the synthesized product was indeed copper oxide nanoparticles.

Characterization of copper oxide nanoparticles

The bio-synthesized copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs) were characterized using various analytical techniques. UV-Vis spectroscopy was employed to analyze the nanoparticles across a wavelength range of 190–800 nm, utilizing a Beckman DU-50 spectrometer with a resolution of 1 nm. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted with a Perkin Elmer Spectrum Analyzer, providing data from 4000 to 400 cm − 1 with an average of 47 scans26. The crystalline structure and phase types of the CuO-NPs were examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD). The diffraction patterns were recorded with a Panalytical X’Pert Diffractometer, utilizing Cu Kα1 radiation (λCu = 1.54060 Å). The XRD analysis was performed in PSD mode with a scanning voltage of 45 kV. The scan type was continuous, with a step size of 0.0130° and a scan step time of 2.2950 s31,32. Additionally, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) was used to confirm the shape and elemental composition of the CuO-NPs.

Treatment of L. sativa seeds with copper oxide nanoparticles

L. sativa seeds were obtained from the National Agricultural Research Centre (NARC) in Islamabad, with freshness ensured. Initial attempts using higher concentrations of copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs)—0.1 mg, 0.2 mg, 0.3 mg, and 0.4 mg—resulted in no germination due to adverse effects, prompting a reduction in nanoparticle concentrations. For the revised experiment, CuO-NPs were precisely weighed at 0.01 mg, 0.02 mg, 0.03 mg, and 0.04 mg and dissolved in distilled water to prepare suspensions with concentrations of 0.01%, 0.02%, 0.03%, and 0.04% v/v. These suspensions were ultrasonicated for 30 min to ensure uniform dispersion and deagglomeration. Each of the four petri dishes was filled with one of the ultrasonicated suspensions, containing fifteen seeds of L. sativa. The petri dishes were stirred and left to stand for 15 min to allow complete absorption of the nanoparticles by the seeds. After treatment, the primed seeds were carefully removed with forceps, dried on filter paper, and then sown in prepared pots under greenhouse conditions.

Greenhouse experiments

The pots were prepared with fertile soil, ensuring clumps were broken up and a small amount of grey sand was added to prevent further clump formation. The soil mix consisted of one-third cow dung, with the remaining two-thirds comprising regular soil and a bit of sand. The cow dung used was moist, partially decomposed, and purely organic, rich in beneficial plant bacteria and essential nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. Fifteen pots were selected for sowing, with five seeds planted in each pot. The pots were organized into five groups, each containing triplicates. The seeds, sourced from NARC and of Pakistani origin, were sown on January 10, 2023, under controlled greenhouse conditions. The greenhouse-maintained temperatures of 18–19 °C during the day and 10–11 °C at night, with partial shade and humidity levels between 40% and 60%.

The triplicate groups were labeled as follows: 0.01 mg/ml, 0.02 mg/ml, 0.03 mg/ml, 0.04 mg/ml, and a control group with untreated seeds. The seeds were monitored and watered every 3–4 days. Germination occurred within one week. After 35 days of growth, the Lactuca sativa plants were transplanted into new pots. Compost tea, made from cow dung and dried leaves mixed in tap water, was applied twice over two months to enhance soil fertility (Fig. 2a-d).

Growth of lettuce plants under greenhouse conditions at (a) 21 days, (b) 28 days, (c) 35 days, and 56 days.

After two months of growth, a comparative study was conducted on the physical parameters of L. sativa. Progress was systematically recorded in tabular form. Lettuce’s rapid sprouting time makes it ideal for greenhouse cultivation, and mature plants were quantitatively assessed over this period. Weekly photographs documented the growth stages, beginning with seed germination observed after seven days. The images indicated a reduction in growth with increasing concentrations of copper oxide nanoparticles, while lower concentrations showed progressive growth compared to the control group. Growth measurements were tracked over eight successive weeks.

Phytochemical analysis

Extract Preparation

After the growth period in the greenhouse, leaves from both the treated plant groups and the control group were harvested, washed, and ground using a mortar and pestle in the laboratory to prepare extracts for phytochemical analysis. The total mass of the yield from all groups was measured using an electronic balance. Five extracts were prepared with methanol and placed in a shaking incubator overnight. Following this, the extracts were filtered through muslin cloth, making them ready for phytochemical analysis. Methanol was chosen for extraction due to its odorless nature, lower cost compared to ethanol, lower boiling point, and effectiveness in extracting phytochemical compounds from plant materials (Table 1).

Characterization of copper oxide nanoparticles

The reaction mixture’s dark green color signaled successful nanoparticle synthesis. After annealing, the final product was a fine black powder, confirming the presence of CuO nanoparticles, with a total weight of approximately 2 g. This product was stored in an Eppendorf tube. To identify and confirm the biomolecules responsible for capping and reducing the nanoparticles, the FTIR spectrum was analyzed and interpreted, as shown in Fig. 4.

FTIR spectrum of CuO-NPs.

The FTIR spectrum reveals strong peaks at 3366 cm⁻¹ and 1086 cm⁻¹, corresponding to O-H and C-O stretching vibrations, respectively. A peak at 1638 cm⁻¹ indicates C = C stretching. Most notably, the peaks at 465 cm⁻¹ and 790 cm⁻¹ are attributed to metal oxide vibrations, specifically Cu-O vibrations, which confirm the presence of copper oxide nanoparticles26.

XRD analysis was performed to determine the phase and purity of the biosynthesized CuO-NPs36. Discussion

The green synthesis of nanoparticles is recognized for its simplicity, environmental friendliness, and cost-effectiveness. In this study, we successfully synthesized copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs) using a green method, resulting in a distinctive black quantum dot formation. This process utilized the biomolecules present in Eucalyptus globulus leaf extract as capping and reducing agents. The formation of CuO-NPs was confirmed by the noticeable color change in the reaction mixture, transitioning from brown to dark green under continuous stirring. The use of E. globulusleaf extract in nanoparticle synthesis is well-documented, including its application in the production of various metal and metal oxide nanoparticles such as lead oxide, iron oxide, lanthanum oxide, titanium dioxide, zinc oxide, copper oxide, magnesium oxide, nickel oxide, gold, and silver41–43. This plant extract is not only non-toxic but also readily available, making it an ideal candidate for sustainable nanomaterial synthesis38.

The FTIR analysis revealed peaks at 465 cm⁻¹ and 790 cm⁻¹, which correspond to Cu-O vibrations, confirming the presence of copper oxide in the nanoparticles. The UV-visible absorption spectrum of the CuO-NPs showed a distinct peak at 252 nm, indicative of their optical properties. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis provided insight into the crystalline structure of the synthesized CuO-NPs. The XRD spectrum displayed characteristic peaks at (110), (002), (111), (− 202), (020), (202), (− 113), (113), (311), and (400), which align with reported diffraction planes35. Among these, the peaks at (002) and (111) were the most prominent. Using the Debye–Scherrer equation, the particle size (D) calculated from the (002) peak was found to be 74.66 nm. The XRD data indicated high crystallinity, as evidenced by sharp and narrow peaks for CuO-NPs with low glucose content, while broader peaks suggested smaller crystallite sizes11. This analysis confirms the crystallization of the bio-organic phase on the surface of CuO-NPs. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) provided a detailed view of the nanoparticle structure, revealing hexagonal crystalline shapes with uniform size distribution. Additionally, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) accurately determined the elemental composition of the nanoparticles, with clear absorption peaks for copper (Cu) and oxygen (O).

Plants play a critical role in the environment and are constantly exposed to various abiotic and biotic stresses throughout their life cycles. These stresses can either enhance or hinder plant growth. In this study, the impact of different concentrations of copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs) on the physical parameters of lettuce plants was assessed. The parameters evaluated included leaf length, width, height, and the number and abundance of leaves per plant.

As shown in Fig. 8(a), the average leaf lengths for the control group and CuO-NP treated groups (0.01 mg/ml, 0.02 mg/ml, 0.03 mg/ml, and 0.04 mg/ml) were 13.70 cm, 13.10 cm, 11.52 cm, 11.03 cm, and 11.18 cm, respectively. For plant height, the average values were 15.20 cm for the control group and 13.67 cm, 12.52 cm, 11.87 cm, and 11.32 cm for the treated groups, as depicted in Fig. 8(b). The average leaf widths were 7.85 cm for the control group and 5.50 cm, 4.78 cm, 4.12 cm, and 3.48 cm for the CuO-NP concentrations, as shown in Fig. 8(c). Variance values for leaf length, height, and width among the control and treated groups were calculated to be 22.239, 30.46, and 15.894, respectively.

The highest growth in length, width, and height was observed at a CuO-NP concentration of 0.01 mg/ml, while the lowest growth was recorded at 0.04 mg/ml. Notably, the control group demonstrated the greatest growth, surpassing even the 0.01 mg/ml concentration group. The final yields for the control and CuO-NP treated groups were 50 g, 40 g, 40 g, 30 g, and 20 g, respectively. The number and abundance of leaves were consistent across all groups. These findings are consistent with research conducted by Jeevanandam et al. in 201844.

In hydroponic conditions, Oryza sativa(rice) exhibited phytotoxicity in response to copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs). The study revealed that CuO-NPs at a concentration of 62.5 mg/L caused significant toxicity, resulting in shorter roots and shoots compared to the control group. Furthermore, the weight of rice leaves treated with CuO-NPs at concentrations of 62.5 mg/L, 125 mg/L, and 250 mg/L was reduced to 38.4%, 62.7%, and 72.8% of the control weight, respectively. These results indicate that the phytotoxicity observed may be due to oxidative damage, disruption in carotenoid synthesis, or a reduction in chlorophyll content. The research suggested that multiple factors could contribute to the observed phytotoxicity, including the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), the release of metal ions from the nanoparticles, and potential DNA damage45.

Other studies have also documented the phytotoxicity of metallic nanoparticles, specifically copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs), on L. sativa. These studies reported that concentrations exceeding 40 mg/mL of CuO-NPs inhibited seed germination and root growth. At high concentrations, the likelihood of nanoparticle agglomeration increases due to reduced surface area, which may inhibit their permeation into plant tissues. Such agglomeration can exacerbate oxidative stress in plants, leading to the production of radical species. Oxidative stress can severely damage cell membranes, potentially resulting in cell death45,46. Additionally, research into the toxic effects of nanoparticles on lettuce has shown that elevated nanoparticle concentrations lead to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in roots. This oxidative stress is associated with an increase in malondialdehyde content compared to cytokinin levels, which contributes to membrane damage. In summary, root toxicity is likely due to a combination of nanoparticle uptake, ion release, and ROS generation, all of which adversely affect root cell metabolism46–55.

Phytochemical screening of freshly prepared leaf extracts from both treated and control lettuce plants was conducted using simple chemical tests to detect the presence of various active phytochemicals, including phytosterols, alkaloids, flavonoids, glycosides, carotenoids, saponins, and tannins. These phytoconstituents can act as effective reducing and stabilizing agents in biochemical processes, making phytochemical analysis essential for understanding the chemical composition of plant extracts. The analysis revealed positive results for phytosterols, alkaloids, flavonoids, glycosides, saponins, and tannins in the leaf extracts of all plant groups, including both treated and control plants, highlighting their significant attributes56–61. However, carotenoids were absent in the 0.03 mg/mL and 0.04 mg/mL treated plant extracts, likely due to phytotoxicity induced by CuO-NPs treatment49.

To identify various chemical bonds and functional groups in the methanolic plant extracts of treated versus control groups, FTIR spectroscopy was employed. The FTIR spectra (Fig. 9(a-e)) for the control group displayed peaks at 1048 cm–1, 1490 cm–1, 1685 cm–1, 2346 cm–1, 2979 cm–1, and 3537 cm–1, corresponding to CO-O-CO in anhydrides, C = C in the fingerprint region, C = O in conjugated aldehydes, O = C = O in carbon dioxide, C-H in alkanes, and O-H in alcohols, respectively. In the treated groups, shifts in functional group peaks were observed. For the 0.01 mg/mL group, peaks at 858 cm–1, 1048 cm–1, 1694 cm–1, 2340 cm–1, 2979 cm–1, and 3613 cm–1 indicated the presence of C-H, CO-O-CO, C = O, O = C = O, C-H, and O-H groups, respectively. In the 0.02 mg/mL group, peaks at 1054 cm–1, 1504 cm–1, 1687 cm–1, 2352 cm–1, 2992 cm–1, and 3587 cm–1 identified functional groups C-O, N-O, C = O, O = C = O, N-H, and O-H. The 0.03 mg/mL group showed peaks at 1060 cm–1, 1516 cm–1, 1700 cm–1, 2352 cm–1, 2986 cm–1, and 3568 cm–1, corresponding to C-O, N-O, C = O, O = C = O, N-H, and O-H groups, respectively. In the 0.04 mg/mL group, peaks at 1060 cm–1, 1668 cm–1, 2359 cm–1, and 3562 cm–1 were associated with C-O, C = O, O = C = O, and O-H functional groups.

Notably, the 0.01 mg/mL group exhibited a distinctive FTIR peak at 858 cm–1, indicative of aromatic phenyl compounds, which often contain hydroxyl groups and act as antioxidants. These compounds are commonly found in plants as hydroxybenzoic acids and hydroxycinnamic acids, contributing to their antioxidant properties.

To assess the concentration of the sample and the light absorbance tendencies of various chemical substances, the plant extracts from different groups (control, 0.01 mg/mL, 0.02 mg/mL, 0.03 mg/mL, and 0.04 mg/mL) were analyzed using UV-Vis spectroscopy. The spectra revealed peak absorbance values at 230 nm for the control group, 228 nm for the 0.01 mg/mL and 0.02 mg/mL groups, 220 nm for the 0.03 mg/mL group, and 227 nm for the 0.04 mg/mL group. The percentage of absorbance was found to be similar across all plant extracts and groups at these different wavelengths62–66.

Conclusion

Biogenic synthesis has proven to be a viable, biocompatible, and cost-effective method for producing fine nano-sized metallic oxide nanostructures. These nanomaterials typically exhibit dimensions on the nanoscale, less than 100 nm. In the present study, the application of biogenic copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs) in pot experiments with Lactuca sativa revealed distinctive FTIR peaks at 858 cm⁻¹ in the 0.01 mg/ml CuO-NPs treated plant extracts. At elevated concentrations, CuO-NPs adversely impacted physical growth parameters and final yield, likely due to oxidative damage. The data indicates that the effects of nanoparticles are highly concentration-dependent, with higher levels posing potential risks to sensitive plants. Further research is needed to explore the impacts of low-concentration CuO-NPs on other edible plants and to understand their bioaccumulation mechanisms.
