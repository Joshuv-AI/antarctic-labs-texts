Poster 257. Interscalene Brachial Plexus Block with and without Liposomal Bupivacaine for Postoperative Pain Control in Total Shoulder Arthroplasty
Authors: Heinz R. Hoenecke, Imran Gruhonjic, Jordan K. Penn, Matthew G. Hanley, Keith A. Alfieri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Interscalene brachial plexus nerve blocks are commonly used to reduce postoperative pain in patients undergoing shoulder surgery. Although effective, these blocks typically last 24 hours or less. Liposomal bupivacaine (LB) acts as a longer lasting amide anesthetic and may improve postoperative analgesia, however there is limited data regarding its use for total shoulder arthroplasty. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of interscalene blocks with liposomal bupivacaine and interscalene blocks using bupivacaine alone for postoperative pain analgesia in patients undergoing total shoulder arthroplasty. We hypothesized that patients receiving interscalene blocks with liposomal bupivacaine would experience improved postoperative pain control and reduced opioid use relative to those receiving bupivacaine alone.Methods:A total of 126 patients were randomized into two groups: LB and bupivacaine group (n=63), and control group (n=63). Of the patients randomized, 113 received the allocated nerve block (LB = 56; control = 57), and 13 were removed before receiving the nerve block (LB = 7; control = 6). The anesthesiologist was the only providers unblinded to the patient's group assignment. The surgeon, post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) and hospital floor nurses were blinded to the patient's assignment throughout the study period. The LB group received a solution consisting of 10 mL of bupivacaine [0.25%] combined with 10 mL of liposomal bupivacaine [133 mg]. The control group received 20 mL of bupivacaine [0.25%] alone. The interscalene brachial plexus block was performed under ultrasound guidance. Outcome measures included the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score, Brief Pain Inventory short form (BPI-sf), and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain. Patients recorded VAS pain twice a day, in the morning and evening, and pain medication consumption for one week after surgery. Opioid consumption was converted to oral morphine milligram equivalents (MME).Results:Thirteen patients did not complete the primary outcome and were not included in the analysis (LB = 5; control = 8). A total of 100 patients completed the study and were included in the final analysis (LB = 51, control = 49). Overall, the cohort consisted of 62 (62%) male and 38 (38%) female patients, with a mean age of 70.9 ± 7.6 years. Baseline characteristics were comparable between groups, with no statistically significant differences. Concomitant procedures at the time of TSA were performed in 8 (15.7%) of LB patients and 5 (10.2%) of control patients (p=0.415). TSA approach significantly differed between groups with 63 anatomic (LB = 37; control = 26) and 37 reverse (LB = 14; control = 23) (p=0.044). Postoperative narcotic consumption in hospital differed between groups with 38 (77.6%) control patients not taking any narcotics after surgery as compared to 28 (54.9%) LB patients (p=0.017). MME consumption between groups did not significantly differ at any postoperative time point (Figure 1). At one-week, there was no difference between groups for mean BPI-sf pain severity (2.8 ± 2.0 for LB patients and 2.9 ± 2.0 for control patients; p=0.833) nor BPI-sf pain interference scores (2.1 ± 2.7 for LB patients and 2.3 ± 3.3 for control patients; p=0.643). Preoperative and postoperative ASES scores did not differ significantly between groups. Although not significant, liposomal bupivacaine patients had lower mean ASES pain scores (43.0 ± 1.2) than control patients (46.1 ± 0.8) at 4-weeks postoperatively (p=0.053).Conclusions:Patients undergoing total shoulder arthroplasty had comparable early postoperative pain reduction when receiving an interscalene brachial plexus nerve block using liposomal bupivacaine versus standard bupivacaine alone.
