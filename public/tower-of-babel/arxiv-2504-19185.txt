Quantum computation with the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis instead of wavefunction preparation
Thomas E. Baker

Abstract
The eigenstate thermalization hypothesis connects a time-average of expectation values for non-integrable systems to the normalized trace of an operator. It is shown here that the hypothesis can be a suitable way to take an expectation value over an equal superposition of eigenstates on the quantum computer. The complexity of taking the expectation value relies on an efficient time-evolution implementation with complexity O ⁡ ( T ) O(T) where T T is the cost of a single time-step going as O ⁡ ( log 2 ​ N ) O(\log_{2}N) and sampling at each time step. The resulting algorithm is demonstrated for the expectation value of the inverse of an operator and then generalized to the gradient of a logarithm-determinant. The method scales as O ⁡ ( τ ​ T ​ M / ε 3 ) O(\tau TM/\varepsilon^{3}) with a factor M M relating to the number of digits of precision, ε \varepsilon , and τ \tau the averaging time. This is independent of the condition number of the input operator and does not require a quantum memory. There is a potential for a many-body localized state–which would increase the thermalization time–but many circuits of practical interest are uniform enough to avoid this, and it is also expected that the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis holds beyond the currently available system sizes that are studied. The boundary where this algorithm would be considered a classical versus quantum algorithm is also considered.

Eigenstate thermalization hypothesis.–

We can identify that the time-evolution operator of an arbitrary input
A
on a random vector is

e^{-iAt}|r\rangle=\sum_{p=1}^{N}c_{p}e^{-iE_{p}t}|p\rangle=|\Psi(t)\rangle

(2)

for some eigenbasis
\{|p\rangle\}
. When we go to take an expectation value of the time evolved vector,

\langle\Psi(t)|A|\Psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{p=1}^{N}a_{pp}|c_{p}|^{2}

(3)

with
A=\sum_{pq}a_{pq}|p\rangle\langle q|
, then the starting-state dependence seems to always be retained due to the unitarity of quantum physics. This is the case as it is applies to integrable systems.

The time-evolution for non-integrable operators is not as straightforward. Imagining that the expectation value of any operator
A
is no longer integrable, then we have

\overline{\langle\Psi(t)|A|\Psi(t)\rangle}\overset{!}{=}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\langle p|A|p\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\mathrm{Tr}(A)

(4)

from ETH
Deutsch 1991
;
Srednicki 1994
;
D’Alessio et al. 2016
;
Deutsch 2018
where an over-bar represents a time-average. The idea is that the eigenstates are allowed to transition between each other and thermalize or erase the initial state dependence in the time-average. ETH is more frequently discussed in the context of rectifying the unitarity of quantum mechanics with the ability for classical systems to exhibit chaos, but there is an opportunity to use ETH to replace wavefunction preparation.

Quantum circuits are inherently designed to solve problems that are not integrable because of our desire to solve problems beyond what is possible mathematically or efficiently classically. The time required to average can vary depending on the system’s complexity in the decomposition of the time-evolution operator. For systems that display many-body localization (MBL)
Nandkishore and Huse 2015
;
Sierant et al. 2025
, the time can be very long to thermalize. There is some indication that this time is not necessarily infinite for MBL
Sels and Polkovnikov 2021
, but for the purposes here the resolution of whether MBL is a phase or merely has a long equilibration time is likely not a hurdle for envisioned application.

The time to thermalize does not necessarily go up with system size. The fluctuations can decrease and the time-average can be very short, even in larger systems. ETH has been primarily tested on small systems and the proposal here is envisioned for large-scale quantum computation. If the ETH does not hold for this algorithm, which is a possibility due to a lack of a firm proof of ETH
Rigol et al. 2008
, it would open up many questions about the thermalization of operators that has been verified in many previous articles in the literature.

Vector form.–

An algorithm is now developed to find the expectation value of the inverse of an operator. What is needed is a random starting wavefunction
|r\rangle

2

The generation of the term

\sqrt{\Upsilon}|\Psi(t)\rangle|E_{p}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{E_{p}}}|\Psi(t)\rangle|E_{p}\rangle

(11)

A swap test with the input vector

will generate the overlap squared between the two vectors,

Operator form.–

The previous vector-form of the operator assumes that we have the state
\Phi

\langle B\rangle=\sum_{p}\langle p|\Delta|p\rangle/E_{p}

The generalization of the vector formulation is to then take the time-averaged expectation value of the form

\displaystyle\langle B\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\langle\Psi(t)|\langle 0|^{\otimes m}(\mathrm{QPE}^{\dagger})(\Delta\otimes\Upsilon)(\mathrm{QPE})|\Psi(t)\rangle|0\rangle^{\otimes m}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\langle\Psi(t)|\langle E_{p}|^{\otimes m}(\Delta\otimes\Upsilon)|\Psi(t)\rangle|E_{p}\rangle^{\otimes m}}

(12)

where
\Upsilon
produces a weight of
1/E_{p}
instead of the square root as before. This is a time-average of the expectation value of the operator
(\Delta\otimes\Upsilon)
over
\Psi(t)
with a QPE used to determine the eigenvalues of the superposition.

Gradient of the logarithm-determinant.–

It was pointed out in Ref.
Baker and Greasley 2025
that the gradient of the logarithm-determinant is a ubiquitous quantity in theoretical physics and computer science and can be efficiently evaluated on the quantum computer. The basic quantity of the form
Baker and Greasley 2025

\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\ln\mathrm{det}A=\sum_{p=1}^{N}\frac{\delta E_{p}(\alpha)}{E_{p}}

(15)

which is only reliant on the eigenvalue
E_{p}
and its gradient
\delta E_{p}(\alpha)
with respect to
\alpha
(signifying a certain derivative with respect to elements of
A
). This quantity appears in many places including quantum field theories, statistical physics, and many more

Adapting the quantum gradient of the logarithm-determinant (QGLD) to the given framework allows us to propose a more easily implementable operator than the extension of the oracle from the quantum gradient algorithm (QGA)

that can be difficult to implement. Notably, in that formulation a controllable parameter
L
should be as low as possible, making only the perturbing operator
\Delta
the only operator that controls the appearance of gradients. To understand

\displaystyle\partial_{\alpha}E_{p}=\langle p|\partial_{\alpha}A|p\rangle

which follows from
\langle p|p\rangle=1

\partial_{\alpha}(\langle p|p\rangle)=0

\partial_{\alpha}(\langle p|p\rangle)=\left(\partial_{\alpha}\langle p|\right)|p\rangle+\langle p|\left(\partial_{\alpha}|p\rangle\right)=0

\frac{\partial A}{\partial\alpha}=\underset{h\rightarrow 0}{\lim}\frac{(A+h\Delta)-A}{h}=\Delta

\Delta

was that the quantum algorithm can sum all gradients simultaneously,

\langle p|\Delta|p\rangle\overset{!}{=}\sum_{ij\in\Delta}\delta E_{p}(\alpha)

(18)

where the sum over

and

\Delta
are non-zero

This leaves only one sum over all eigenvectors in

\Sigma

The generalization of the QGLD shows that the framework here applies in a far broader sense than just for the inverse of a given matrix. For example, the operator
\Delta

Other algorithms.–

There is no reason that ETH-
\Sigma
cannot be useful for other algorithms. One would merely need to supply an auxiliary qubit register that contained the appropriate weights for each state. The goal here was to compute the expectation value of the inverse, which was shown to only require the eigenvalues. If the auxiliary qubits could be used to provide another value, then the same procedure would work with a modified
\Upsilon
.

Because ETH-
\Sigma
computes the entire Hilbert-space worth of eigenstates, this can be viewed as being analogous to a full configuration interaction (FCI) for quantum chemistry applications
Helgaker et al. 2014
. Conservation of particle number will need to be explored in this method. ETH is normally discussed without regard to quantum number symmetries, but there is the opportunity to conserve these numbers during thermalization.

Large-scale applications.–

Large-scale applications may suffer from two issues. It is not clear what the thermalization time is for an arbitrary quantum circuit. However, the suggested use cases for both previous algorithms and the QGLD
Baker and Greasley 2025
will be available to this sampling technique, including the computation of exact quantities in density functional theory
Baker and Greasley 2025
;
Baker and Poulin 2020
. Many quantum circuits of interest do not appear to possess the level of disorder required for MBL.

Where there can be a quantum advantage.–

This section will discuss under what conditions the ETH-
\Sigma
would revert to a classical algorithm. A framework will be suggested in order to exactly discuss this intricate topic.

We can differentiate the types of speedups.

Definition 1

(Quantum utility)
.

A quantum algorithm that scales better than classical algorithms as a polynomial factor.

A definition for advantage would involve poly-logarithmic complexity of the quantum case.

Definition 2

(Quantum advantage)
.

A quantum algorithm that scales better than classical algorithms by an exponential factor.

The quantum advantage of the ETH-
\Sigma
is that the QPE applies onto each eigenstate
p
in the superposition and generates a prefactor
1/E_{p}
. This operator need only be applied once onto the entire superposition and the application to each state in the superposition is therefore guaranteed. The natural question is then, when would this constitute a quantum advantage and when would this procedure revert to a classical algorithm as has been noted for previous algorithms of this type
Aaronson 2015
?

The framework that would allow us to discuss the potential advantages of the quantum algorithm over the classical algorithm should properly begin with the character of the random initial vector as given in Eq. (
1
) and take note of two possible types of classical algorithms.

Definition 3

(Classical algorithm I)
.

A classical algorithm costs at least
N
operations on a vector of size
N
.

The classical algorithm can be formalized as applying an operator or set of operators onto each of the states
|p\rangle
. There are
N
of them, the total operations to capture all of the contributions from each state in the superposition.

Definition 4

(Classical algorithm II)
.

A classical algorithm costs
\log_{2}N
operations when the operator is applied onto a mean-field vector and for each operator the deviations from the mean-field are small.

If we consider the application of
n
operations onto a single wavefunction,
\Xi
, then we have

\langle\Xi|\prod_{j=1}^{n}\hat{C}_{j}|\Xi\rangle

(19)

and if the mean-field wavefunction
\Xi
is a good approximation to its spectral decomposition
|\Xi\rangle=\sum_{p}c_{p}|p\rangle
for all operators
\hat{C}_{j}
, then we would have the condition that

\langle\Xi|\prod_{j=1}^{n}\hat{C}_{j}|\Xi\rangle\overset{?}{\approx}\sum_{p,q=1}^{n}\langle q|c_{q}^{*}c_{p}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\hat{C}_{j}|p\rangle

(20)

where it is assumed that the sum on the right is over
n
instead of
N
. If the sum would be over
N
instead, the number of operations would be exponentially sized and the case would be like the first type of classical algorithm.

What has been described in this second case is one of low condition number (
i.e.
replace all factors of
1/E_{p}
with an equal number or nearly equal here), or the case where a single operator applied on all of the eigenstates can be done in one (or a few) operations. If the wavefunction
\Xi
acts as a mean-field and the possible deviations from this mean-field are small (
i.e.
, the terms in the double sum over
p
and
q
are less than the full Hilbert space or ideally where
\Xi
captures all eigenstates uniformly). In this case, the classical mean-field vector well approximates the problem to the degree that if only
\log_{2}N
operators are applied, then the quantum algorithm can reduce to the classical algorithm.

The core distinction between classical and quantum algorithms–as proposed in this framework–can be distilled into the following proposed statement.

Proposition 1

(Classical vs. quantum algorithm)
.

An algorithm is quantum when its scaling is poly-logarithmic and all equivalent classical algorithms cost more (potentially up to an exponential amount more) to achieve the same task.

There is reason to conjecture that ETH-
\Sigma
is a quantum algorithm because it can handle arbitrary condition number and requires only one operator (a QPE) to generate all possible terms in the summation. It effectively performs a sum over all states
N
in a single time-average. While the case of low- and high-condition number may be classical algorithms in all cases
Aaronson 2015
, the case where a Hilbert-space sized number of states may fall outside of these limits. The application of this algorithm therefore should focus on situations that fall outside of both classical cases used here, where an exponential number of operations would be required by the classical algorithm to compute all
N
deviations from a mean field.

The explicit construction of the ETH-
\Sigma
algorithm shows that there is no hidden oracle because of known efficient time-evolution techniques
Low et al. 2023
, only an problem-dependent question about the thermalization time. Since many operators of interest do not possess the required characteristics of MBL, a non-exponential thermalization times can be posited for applications of interest. This makes a quantum advantage possible.

The capability of the QPE to produce non-uniform wavefunctions means that any previous condition number limitation
Aaronson 2015
has been lifted in this method. Previous quantum algorithms with fine-tuned superpositions would be much harder to create and add factors to the complexity that may destroy the quantum advantage, notably for wavefunction preparation
Tang 2021
. Thus, ETH-
\Sigma
offers a possible route to compute problems for arbitrary condition number, potentially beyond classical algorithms, having eliminated wavefunction preparation.

Summary.–

The algorithm presented here uses eigenstate thermalization hypothesis to sample an equal superposition of eigenstates as a time average. This is instead of a direct wavefunction preparation or use of a quantum memory. This was shown to give the expectation value of the inverse of matrices with arbitrary condition number. The method was then extended to compute the gradient of the logarithm-determinant. The complexity of the algorithm is poly-logarithmic in terms of the size of the input operator. Quantum hardware must improve the connectivity on real chips in order to fully run the quantum phase estimation that was necessary here.

If the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis is found not to hold for large-scale systems with this method, then that would open up a several important research questions related to the thermalization of quantum systems.

One natural question that one might wonder is how the QPE should be treated in the context of ETH here. Eq. (
12
), and the example surrounding it, thermalize the operator
\Delta
, but when there is the presence of the eigenvalues
E_{p}
, we have that those variables do not evolve in time. The question may come up as to how ETH remains the correct model in this case. What is shown is that the
\Delta
operator is weighted by a function of the eigenvalues and then time-evolved. This effectively recasts the problem as a new operator with different diagonal elements, but one that is still time-evolved under ETH.

Proposition 2

(Re-weighting of
\Delta
by QPE)
.

The role of the quantum phase estimation is to re-weight the input
\Delta
operator to include
E_{p}
on each entry.

The justification for this proposition can be derived from one of the forms that is used to justify ETH. Consider the time evolution of the operator
A
such that ETH is true and

A_{pq}=\overline{A}\delta_{pq}+\ldots

(21)

where other terms can be neglected here. From this form, it is clear that the diagonal elements are the remaining terms that are kept in a time-evolution.

A more general statement may be to take the general form
\Delta=\Delta_{pq}|p\rangle\langle q|
and
A=A_{pp}|p\rangle\langle p|
and define

\tilde{\Delta}=(\mathrm{QPE}^{\dagger})(\Delta\otimes\Upsilon)(\mathrm{QPE})=\begin{cases}\Delta_{pp}f(E_{p}),&p=q\\
\Delta_{pq},&p\neq q\end{cases}

(22)

which effectively re-weights the terms of
\Delta
along the diagonal (in the eigenvalue basis) as having an extra factor of a function of the eigenvalues,
f(E_{p})
. The new operator,
\tilde{\Delta}
, then has ETH applied to it as normal.

Another way to see this is that the starting vector
|r\rangle
in principle projects onto each eigenstate, albeit with potentially a small amplitude. The QPE would then commute with the time evolution and produce the same factor in
\Delta
, implying that the constancy of
E_{p}
is not affected by time-evolution. This implies that ETH is still the correct picture even with the re-weighting by a function of the eigenvalues regardless of the form of
\Delta
.
