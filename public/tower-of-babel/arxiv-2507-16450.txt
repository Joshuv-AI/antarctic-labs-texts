RIS-aided Latent Space Alignment for Semantic Channel Equalization
Tomás Hüttebräucker, Mario Edoardo Pandolfo, Simone Fiorellino, Emilio Calvanese Strinati, Paolo Di Lorenzo

Abstract
Semantic communication systems introduce a new paradigm in wireless communications, focusing on transmitting the intended meaning rather than ensuring strict bit-level accuracy. These systems often rely on DNN to learn and encode meaning directly from data, enabling more efficient communication. However, in multi-user settings where interacting agents are trained independently—without shared context or joint optimization—divergent latent representations across AI-native devices can lead to semantic mismatches , impeding mutual understanding even in the absence of traditional transmission errors. In this work, we address semantic mismatch in MIMO ( MIMO ) channels by proposing a joint physical and semantic channel equalization framework that leverages the presence of RIS ( RIS ). The semantic equalization is implemented as a sequence of transformations: (i) a pre-equalization stage at the transmitter; (ii) propagation through the RIS-aided channel; and (iii) a post-equalization stage at the receiver. We formulate the problem as a constrained MMSE ( MMSE ) optimization and propose two solutions: (i) a linear semantic equalization chain, and (ii) a non-linear DNN -based semantic equalizer. Both methods are designed to operate under semantic compression in the latent space and adhere to transmit power constraints. Through extensive evaluations, we show that the proposed joint equalization strategies consistently outperform conventional, disjoint approaches to physical and semantic channel equalization across a broad range of scenarios and wireless channel conditions.



I
Introduction

For the last seven decades, communication systems have been designed with the main objective of reliably transmitting symbols through noisy communication channels, typically disregarding the interpretation and impact of these symbols upon reception.
Following this principle, communication networks have achieved significant advancements in bit transmission rate and reliability, fundamental metrics for data-centric applications such as video and audio streaming, where communication itself is the primary objective. However, many emerging
6G
(
6G
) applications, including
IoT
(
IoT
), smart cities, and autonomous vehicles, do not treat communication as an end but rather as a means to enable interaction and coordination among intelligent agents
[
1
]
. This paradigm shift in the utilization of communication systems calls for a corresponding transformation in their design, moving the focus from optimizing accurate data transmission to maximizing the relevance and utility of the transmitted information for users’ decision-making processes. In this context,
SemComs
(
SemComs
) have emerged as a promising new paradigm for the design of communication networks
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
. Unlike conventional communication systems, which aim for perfect data recovery, the
SemComs
paradigm focuses on preserving the underlying meaning of the transmitted data, relevant to a given task at the receiver. By discarding irrelevant information,
SemComs
can potentially reduce communication overhead while maintaining, or even enhancing, downstream task performance.

First introduced by Weaver in 1949
[
5
]
and formally addressed by Carnap and Bar-Hillel in 1952
[
6
]
, the concept of
SemComs
traces back to the early development of digital communications. Despite its long-standing origins,
SemComs
attracted limited attention from the scientific community in subsequent decades and was largely regarded as a theoretical or philosophical construct. In recent years, however, the widespread deployment of interconnected devices—threatening to overwhelm the capacity of communication networks—has renewed interest in the development of a theory of
SemComs

[
7
,
8
]
, albeit with limited success. Practical realizations of
SemComs
systems have also remained limited, primarily due to the inherent difficulties in formally defining, extracting, and interpreting semantic content from real-world data. Recent advancements in
AI
(
AI
), particularly in
DL
(
DL
), have addressed many of these challenges by enabling the development of
SemComs
systems capable of learning a
semantic protocol
—a structured method for extracting, transmitting, and reconstructing task-relevant meaning from data. This is typically achieved by leveraging
DNN
to model semantic encoding and decoding functions, with
ML
(
ML
) algorithms guiding their training for specific tasks. For instance, in semantic source coding
DNN
are used to compress data based on an imperfect but semantically equivalent reconstruction. This approach has been shown to outperform traditional source coding methods both in compression and performance on the objective task
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. However, semantic source coding does not account for the characteristics of the communication channel, which are fundamental to achieving efficient and reliable transmission. To address this limitation,
JSCC
(
JSCC
) has emerged as an alternative that simultaneously considers both task performance and channel conditions. When implemented using
DNN
, this approach is known as
DJSCC
(
DJSCC
) and has been widely explored in the
SemComs
literature. First introduced in
[
12
]
,
DJSCC
integrates source coding, channel coding, and modulation into a single end-to-end trainable
DNN
-based model optimized for either reconstruction quality or task performance. This approach has demonstrated advantages over conventional communication systems across various applications, including image
[
12
]
, text
[
13
,
14
]
, and audio
[
15
]
transmission, as well as task-oriented scenarios such as image classification
[
16
,
17
]
, token communication
[
18
]
, and sequential decision-making in
MDP

[
19
]
. Furthermore, its flexibility has facilitated its application to complex communication settings, including
MIMO

[
20
]
,
OFDM
(
OFDM
)
[
21
]
, and feedback
[
22
]
channels.

While
DNN
-based
SemComs
systems offer notable advantages over traditional communication networks, they also introduce several challenges. Among these, one of the most significant is
semantic noise
, which refers to all distortions that lead to misinterpretation of meaning, independently of the syntactic (physical) channel noise
[
7
,
23
]
. Semantic noise can arise from various sources, each with distinct characteristics and corresponding mitigation strategies. One such source is the inherent limitation of
DL
models, which may produce inaccurate outputs due to restricted model capacity, limited training data, or poor generalization to unseen inputs. Closely related is adversarial noise
[
24
]
, which consists of small, semantically imperceptible perturbations deliberately designed to mislead the model—often leading to substantial performance degradation. While these sources of semantic noise have been studied and continue to receive attention in both the
SemComs
and
ML
literature, only recently a new source of semantic noise,
semantic mismatch
, has been identified
[
25
]
. This type of noise occurs when the transmitter and receiver interpret meaning differently—often due to discrepancies in their methods of extracting and interpreting semantic information. In
DNN
-based
SemComs
, such mismatch typically stems from inconsistencies in the learned latent representations between the transmitter and receiver, which typically occurs when their modules are not jointly trained. Semantic mismatch is often overlooked since most works assume that a joint training process across both ends of the communication link is possible when designing a
SemComs
system. This assumption, however, poses a major limitation for real-world, multi-user deployments of
SemComs
, where agents may use different neural architectures, datasets, or task objectives. In reality, such discrepancies are the norm—especially in multi-vendor environments where stakeholders are either unwilling or unable to share models, training data, or other proprietary resources. In these settings, joint end-to-end training or exchanging deep neural network models is often impractical due to privacy concerns and intellectual property restrictions. As a result, establishing a robust semantic alignment mechanism is not just beneficial—it is essential for ensuring consistency and interoperability across heterogeneous, real-world
SemComs
systems.

A straightforward solution to semantic mismatch is joint fine-tuning, where all interacting agents briefly re-train their models to align communication protocols
[
26
]
. However, this approach presents two major drawbacks. First, the energy and communication costs associated with re-training multiple
DNN
models can outweigh the practical benefits of
SemComs
. Second, agents may still be unable or unwilling to modify their models due to compatibility issues, operational constraints, or the aforementioned privacy concerns—making collaborative fine-tuning infeasible in many real-world scenarios. As an alternative to this, the concept of semantic channel equalization has been recently proposed
[
25
]
. Inspired by traditional channel equalization techniques, this approach introduces the
semantic channel equalizer
—a module, potentially distributed between the transmitter and receiver—that transforms semantic symbols to ensure consistent interpretation.

Although still a relatively new concept, semantic channel equalization has already gained significant attention, with several studies exploring its design and practical implementation. In
[
25
]
, the authors propose a semantic channel equalization module based on the concept of semantic space partition. Briefly, a partition of the semantic space (i.e., the space where semantic symbols are represented) is a grouping of its elements into non-empty and non-overlapping subsets, called atoms. Each atom in the partition is associated with a meaning or concept of interest. Based on the semantic space partition, they leverage a set of linear transformations tailored to align corresponding atoms transmitter and receiver. This approach has been extended to Markov Decision Process (MDP) scenarios
[
27
]
, where self-supervised learning of the semantic partitioning has also been explored
[
28
]
. Alternatively, the concept of
RR
(
RR
)
[
29
]
has been utilized for semantic alignment assuming errorless communication. This approach establishes a common semantic space by encoding information relative to a shared subset of data, known as anchors. Notably, this shared space remains largely consistent across independently trained models and tasks. In
[
30
]
, they leverage
RR
to unify the semantic representation spaces of multiple encoders. Using a set of trained relative decoders, they perform dynamic optimization of the encoding model and network resources for efficient task completion. In
[
31
]
, they propose a
RR
-based semantic channel equalization that requires no relative decoder and works with a small number of data samples. However, all the aforementioned works in semantic channel equalization either rely on a digital communications scheme or parallel analog
SISO
(
SISO
) channels for transmission, effectively separating semantic channel equalization from physical channel equalization.

Contributions:
In this work, we present a novel methodological equalization framework for
SemComs
, integrating both physical and semantic channel equalization in a
MIMO
communication setting. Additionally, we investigate the potential of
RIS
for semantic channel equalization. While recent studies have explored
RIS
in the context of
SemComs

[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
, its specific application to semantic channel equalization remains unexplored. Building on our preliminary conference work
[
37
]
, and extending it to incorporate the presence of RIS in the semantic communication scenario, our equalization approach is structured into three stages: a pre-equalizer at the transmitter, a post-equalizer at the receiver, and a
RIS
-based equalizer at the wireless the channel. We formulate the equalization problem as a constrained
MMSE
problem, where constraints are imposed on the
RIS
parameters and transmitted power. Moreover, by varying the communication budget, our formulation incorporates also a semantic compression of the latent space. We propose both linear and
DNN
-based solutions. In the linear approach, the problem is decomposed into three subproblems, one for each set of variables, and solved through alternating optimization. The neural approach, on the other hand, is addressed using standard stochastic gradient descent algorithms. Moreover, we investigate the impact of various
RIS
sizes, focusing on the trade-off between performance, compression, and complexity. Finally, we explore the potential of
RIS
for adaptability to channel variations in
SemComs
, where only the
RIS
values are updated to enable successful semantic communications under channel variations.

In summary, the main contributions of our work are:

1.

We introduce a novel Multi-User
SemComs
system model including semantic mismatch,
MIMO
channel and
RIS
. Following this model, we propose a holistic approach to perform joint compression as well as physical and semantic channel equalization. We pose the equalization problem as an MMSE optimization problem for which we obtain a Linear and a
DNN
-based solution.

2.

We explore the potential of
RIS
as a channel equalizer in the proposed scenario. Showing not only its capability to equalize channel changes but also to reduce semantic mismatch by introducing extra computation and optimization parameters.

3.

We compare our solutions extensively against baselines, showing their superior performance and exploring the tradeoff between compression,
SNR
(
SNR
), and performance.

II
System Model

We consider the semantic communication framework depicted in
fig.

1
, where a transmitter and a receiver communicate by leveraging
DNN
-based semantic encoding and decoding modules. In the single-user case, the transmitter and the receiver can jointly learn the encoding
{{E}_{\theta}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{\theta}}}
and decoding
{{D}_{\theta}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{\theta}}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}_{\theta}
functions to enable effective communication. Here,
{\theta}
denotes the shared model architecture and training process for both
{{E}_{\theta}}
and
{{D}_{\theta}}
. The set
{\mathcal{X}}
represents the input data space,
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\theta}
is the (agent-specific) action space, and
{N_{\theta}}
specifies the dimensionality of the semantic (latent) space to which semantic symbols are mapped.
Then, given an observation
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
, the transmitter extracts a semantic feature vector,
{{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}}={{E}_{\theta}}\left({\mathbf{x}}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{\theta}}}
, which is transmitted to the receiver. Upon reception, the receiver utilizes
{{D}_{\theta}}
to infer an action,
{\mathbf{y}}_{\theta}
, from the set of possible actions
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\theta}
, based on the task at hand.

In multi-user semantic communications, the transmitter and receiver may employ independently learned communication strategies. Specifically, while the transmitter encodes the data
{\mathbf{x}}
using the aforementioned
{{E}_{\theta}}
, the receiver might employ a different decoder
{{D}_{\gamma}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{\gamma}}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}_{\gamma}
(trained in conjunction with a corresponding encoder
{{E}_{\gamma}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{{N_{\gamma}}}
) for interpretation. This introduces a critical challenge: due to the independent training, naive transmission becomes prone to semantic interpretation errors resulting from a representation mismatch. That is, for the same data
{\mathbf{x}}
, the transmitted
{{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}}
signal may not align with the adequate symbol for the receiver, represented by
{{\mathbf{s}}_{\gamma}}={{E}_{\gamma}}\left({\mathbf{x}}\right)
. This mismatch can arise from many sources, such as differences in the training data distribution or task goals (
{\mathcal{Y}}_{\gamma}\neq{\mathcal{Y}}_{\theta}
) and mismatches in the architectures (
{N_{\gamma}}\neq{N_{\theta}}
). Even when the training data, the optimization objective, and the architecture are fixed for both training procedures, the inherent stochasticity of
ML
algorithms will likely cause a discrepancy in the learned protocols
[
29
]
.

In this work, we focus on
equalizing
the
semantic channel
, the underlying cause of semantic mismatch, jointly with the physical channel (hereafter referred to simply as the channel). For this, we consider a multi-user semantic communication scenario with a physical
MIMO
channel with
N_{t}
transmit and
N_{r}
receive antennas, and a parallel diagonal
RIS
channel with
N_{\mathbf{\phi}}
elements. The first step in designing the equalizer is to adapt the source semantic symbols into a complex representation. Assuming that
{N_{\theta}}
is even (otherwise,
{{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}}
can be zero-padded), we define
{\mathbf{z}}_{\theta}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\frac{{N_{\theta}}}{2}}}
as a complex vector whose real and imaginary parts are formed by splitting
{{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}}
into two equal halves. We assume that, for each
{\mathbf{z}}_{\theta}
, the transmitter can use the channel
K
times leveraging a pre-equalizer
{\mathbf{f}}_{\eta}:{\mathbb{C}}^{{\frac{{N_{\theta}}}{2}}}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{KN_{t}}
that maps the semantic representation
{\mathbf{z}}_{\theta}
into
KN_{t}
complex channel symbols. We model the channel as comprising a direct line-of-sight component, denoted by
\mathbf{H}_{d}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N_{r}\times N_{t}}
, and a reflective component induced by the
RIS
, given by
\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{\phi}}=\mathbf{H}_{2}{\text{diag}\left({\mathbf{\phi}}\right)}\mathbf{H}_{1}
, where
{\text{diag}\left({\mathbf{\phi}}\right)}
is a diagonal matrix representing the RIS reflection coefficients. Here,
{\mathbf{\phi}}=\{{\mathbf{\phi}}_{1},{\mathbf{\phi}}_{2},\ldots,{\mathbf{\phi}}_{N_{\mathbf{\phi}}}\}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N_{\mathbf{\phi}}}
is the array of phase shifts introduced by the
RIS
,
\mathbf{H}_{1}
denotes the channel from the transmitter to the
RIS
and
\mathbf{H}_{2}
the channel from the

to the receiver. The

enables successful communication in cases where the main channel
\mathbf{H}_{d}
is attenuated. At the receiver side, the post-equalizer
{\mathbf{g}}_{\nu}:{\mathbb{C}}^{KN_{r}}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{{\frac{{N_{\gamma}}}{2}}}

{\mathbf{z}}_{\theta}

\zeta=\frac{KN_{t}}{\frac{{N_{\theta}}}{2}}.

Furthermore, since labels are not strictly necessary, the complexity of data collection is significantly reduced. The semantic pilots

III
Linear Semantic Channel Equalization

We first propose to address the channel-aware semantic alignment using a linear approach. We introduce the linear pre-equalizer
{\mathbf{F}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{KN_{t}\times{\frac{{N_{\theta}}}{2}}}
and the linear post-equalizer
{\mathbf{G}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\frac{{N_{\gamma}}}{2}}\times KN_{r}}
to be jointly optimized with the RIS parameters
{\mathbf{\phi}}
. Defining the data matrices
{\mathbf{Z}}_{\theta}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\frac{{N_{\theta}}}{2}}\times N}
and
{\mathbf{Z}}_{\gamma}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\frac{{N_{\gamma}}}{2}}\times N}
, where the
i

{\mathbf{Z}}_{\theta}
and
{\mathbf{Z}}_{\gamma}
are the semantic pilots
{\mathbf{z}}_{\theta}^{(i)}
and
{\mathbf{z}}_{\gamma}^{(i)}
respectively, problem (

\displaystyle\min_{{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{G}},{\mathbf{\phi}}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\left\|{\mathbf{Z}}_{\gamma}-{\mathbf{G}}\mathbf{H}_{e}^{{\mathbf{\phi}}}{\mathbf{F}}{\mathbf{Z}}_{\theta}\right\|^{2}_{F}+\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathbf{G}}^{H}{\mathbf{G}}\Sigma_{\mathbf{w}}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathbf{F}}^{H}{\mathbf{F}}\Sigma_{{\mathbf{z}}_{\theta}}\right)\leq P

&\qquad\text{s.t.}\quad\left|{\mathbf{\phi}}_{i}\right|=1\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,\ldots,N_{\mathbf{\phi}}\}\end{aligned}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{S}}^{\left(k+1\right)}={\mathbf{S}}^{\left(k\right)}+{\mathbf{F}}^{\left(k+1\right)}-{\mathbf{R}}^{\left(k+1\right)}

(14)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{e}^{{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\left(k+1\right)}}={\mathbf{I}}_{K}\otimes\left(\mathbf{H}_{d}+\mathbf{H}_{2}\text{diag}\left({\mathbf{\phi}}^{\left(k+1\right)}\right)\mathbf{H}_{1}\right)

(15)

where
k
denotes the iteration step,
\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{P}}(\cdot)
denotes the orthogonal projection onto set
\mathcal{P}
, and
\rho>0
is the regularization coefficient for the augmented Lagrangian
[
39
]
. Each of the optimization steps in
eq.

10
–
eq.

13
involves solving a smaller subproblem, whose solutions are detailed in the sequel.

III-A

{\mathbf{G}}
step

The step for
{\mathbf{G}}
in
eq.

10
can be solved in a closed form by setting the (conjugate) gradient
\nabla_{{\mathbf{G}}^{H}}
of the objective to zero, thus yielding the following expression:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{G}}^{\left(k+1\right)}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{Z}}_{\gamma}\tilde{{\mathbf{Z}}}^{H}\left(\tilde{{\mathbf{Z}}}\tilde{{\mathbf{Z}}}^{H}+N\Sigma_{\mathbf{w}}\right)^{-1},

(16)

where
\tilde{{\mathbf{Z}}}=\mathbf{H}_{e}^{{\mathbf{\phi}}^{(k)}}{\mathbf{F}}^{\left(k\right)}{\mathbf{Z}}_{\theta}
is the signal received by the decoder in the absence of channel noise.

III-B

{\mathbf{F}}
step

Similarly, setting the conjugate gradient
\nabla_{{\mathbf{F}}^{H}}
of
eq.

11
to zero yields the expression of the optimal
{\mathbf{F}}

\mathbf{A}{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)}+{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)}\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{C}

(17)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\left({\mathbf{G}}^{(k+1)}\mathbf{H}_{e}^{{\mathbf{\phi}}^{(k)}}\right)^{H}\left({\mathbf{G}}^{(k+1)}\mathbf{H}_{e}^{{\mathbf{\phi}}^{(k)}}\right)

(18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}=N\rho\left({\mathbf{Z}}_{\theta}{\mathbf{Z}}_{\theta}^{H}\right)^{-1}

(19)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=N\rho\left({\mathbf{R}}^{(k)}-{\mathbf{S}}^{(k)}\right)+\left({\mathbf{G}}^{(k+1)}\mathbf{H}_{e}^{{\mathbf{\phi}}}\right)^{H}{\mathbf{Z}}_{\gamma}{\mathbf{Z}}_{\theta}^{H}.

(20)

Equation
eq.

17
is the well-known Sylvester Equation which can be efficiently solved for
{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)}
using the Bartels-Stewart algorithm
[
40
]
.

III-C

{\mathbf{R}}
step

The
{\mathbf{R}}
step in (
12
) requires a projection onto the set
\mathcal{P}
of matrices with the required power constraint.
Letting
\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}={\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)}+{\mathbf{S}}^{(k)}
, we have:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{R}}^{(k+1)}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Proj}_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}\right)

(21)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\mathbf{R}}\quad\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}-{\mathbf{R}}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}}^{H}\right)<P.

Problem
eq.

21
is a convex problem and can thus be solved optimally by imposing the
KKT
(
KKT
) conditions:

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\mathbf{R}}^{H}}\left(\left\|\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}-{\mathbf{R}}\right\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}}^{H}\right)-P\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0}

(22)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}}^{H}\right)-P

\displaystyle\leq 0

(23)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle\geq 0

(24)

\displaystyle\lambda\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}}^{H}\right)-P\right)

\displaystyle=0.

(25)

From
eq.

22
we obtain

{\mathbf{R}}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}.

(26)

Knowing that
\operatorname{Tr}\left({\mathbf{R}}{\mathbf{R}}^{H}\right)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left(1+\lambda\right)^{2}}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}^{H}}\right)
, the optimal
\lambda
is given by

\lambda^{*}=\begin{cases}0,\qquad&\text{if }\operatorname{Tr}\left(\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}^{H}\right)\leq P\\
\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}\left(\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}\tilde{{\mathbf{R}}}^{H}}{P}\right)}-1&\text{else.}\end{cases}

(27)

Thus, the final update for
{\mathbf{R}}
reads as:

{\mathbf{R}}^{(k+1)}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda^{*}}\left({\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)}+{\mathbf{S}}^{(k)}\right).

(28)

III-D

{\mathbf{\phi}}
step

Updating the
RIS
parameters involves solving the constrained optimization problem in
eq.

13
, which can be equivalently reformulated as the following constrained
MSE
(
MSE
) minimization problem:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\phi}}^{(k+1)}=\argmin_{\mathbf{\phi}}

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{v}}-\tilde{{\mathbf{D}}}\left(\mathbf{1}_{K}\otimes{\mathbf{\phi}}\right)\right\|^{2}_{F}

(29)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\left|{\mathbf{\phi}}_{i}\right|=1\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,\ldots,N_{\mathbf{\phi}}\},

where
\mathbf{1}_{K}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{K}
denotes the
K
-dimensional vector of all ones, and the vector
{\mathbf{v}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\frac{{N_{\gamma}}}{2}}N}
and the matrix
\tilde{{\mathbf{D}}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\frac{{N_{\gamma}}}{2}}N\times KN{\mathbf{\phi}}}
are defined as:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}}=\text{vec}\left({\mathbf{Z}}_{\gamma}-{\mathbf{G}}^{(k+1)}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{K}\otimes\mathbf{H}_{d}\right){\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)}{\mathbf{Z}}_{\theta}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle\text{col}_{j}\left(\tilde{{\mathbf{D}}}\right)=\text{vec}\Big(\text{col}_{j}\left(\mathbf{P}\right)\text{row}_{j}\left(\mathbf{Q}\right)\Big),

(31)

with
\mathbf{P}={\mathbf{G}}^{(k+1)}\left({\mathbf{I}}_{K}\otimes\mathbf{H}_{2}\right)
and
\mathbf{Q}=\left({\mathbf{I}}_{K}\otimes\mathbf{H}_{1}\right){\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)}{\mathbf{Z}}_{\theta}
. The operations
\text{col}_{j}
and
\text{row}_{j}
denote the selection of the
j
-th column and row, respectively. This way,
\text{col}_{j}\left(\mathbf{P}\right)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\frac{{N_{\gamma}}}{2}}\times 1}
,
\text{row}_{j}\left(\mathbf{Q}\right)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{1\times N}
and the product
\text{col}_{j}\left(\mathbf{P}\right)\text{row}_{j}\left(\mathbf{Q}\right)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\frac{{N_{\gamma}}}{2}}\times N}
. Finally,
\text{vec}\left(\cdot\right)
denotes the vectorization operation which flattens a matrix into a vector by stacking its columns. Problem
eq.

29
can also be reformulated to avoid the Kronecker product, introducing the matrix
{\mathbf{D}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{{\frac{{N_{\gamma}}}{2}}N\times N_{\mathbf{\phi}}}
, whose elements are defined as:

\left[{\mathbf{D}}\right]_{i,j}=\sum_{l=0}^{K-1}\left[\tilde{{\mathbf{D}}}\right]_{i,j+N_{\mathbf{\phi}}l},

(32)

where
[\cdot]_{i,j}
denotes the element in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column of a matrix. Each column of
{\mathbf{D}}
is the sum of the columns of
\tilde{{\mathbf{D}}}
which correspond to the same
{\mathbf{\phi}}
element. Thus, problem
eq.

29
can be equivalently recast as:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\phi}}^{(k+1)}=\argmin_{\mathbf{\phi}}

\displaystyle\quad\left\|{\mathbf{v}}-{\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{\phi}}\right\|^{2}_{F}

(33a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad\left|{\mathbf{\phi}}_{i}\right|=1\quad\forall i\in\{1,2,\ldots,N_{\mathbf{\phi}}\}.

(33b)

Unfortunately, problem
eq.

33
is still non-convex due to the constraint
eq.

33b
, which makes the optimal solution difficult to obtain. Moreover, even if
{\mathbf{\phi}}^{(k+1)}
were optimal for
eq.

33
, it would not necessarily be optimal for the overall problem defined in
eq.

7
, since the variables
{\mathbf{G}}
and
{\mathbf{F}}
are updated in subsequent steps. Therefore, we adopt an approximate solution using the iterative
PGD
(
PGD
) method. Rather than fully solving the subproblem at each iteration, we perform only one
PGD
step to allow incremental adaptation of
{\mathbf{\phi}}
. This approach is justified by the alternating nature of the main algorithm, where the subproblem for
{\mathbf{\phi}}
is redefined each iteration following updates to
{\mathbf{G}}^{(k+1)}
and
{\mathbf{F}}^{(k+1)}
. Our goal at each step is not to find a locally optimal
{\mathbf{\phi}}
, but to improve upon its previous value, ensuring steady progress with lower computational cost. Finally, defining the component-wise projection function

\Pi({\mathbf{\phi}}_{i})=\frac{{\mathbf{\phi}}_{i}}{\left|{\mathbf{\phi}}_{i}\right|}

(34)

and the vector projection function

\mathbf{\Pi}({\mathbf{\phi}})=\left\{\Pi({\mathbf{\phi}}_{1}),\Pi({\mathbf{\phi}}_{2}),\ldots,\Pi({\mathbf{\phi}}_{N_{\mathbf{\phi}}})\right\},

(35)

the
{\mathbf{\phi}}
step reads as

{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\left(k+1\right)}=\mathbf{\Pi}\left({\mathbf{\phi}}^{\left(k\right)}-\alpha{\mathbf{D}}^{H}\left({\mathbf{D}}{\mathbf{\phi}}^{\left(k\right)}-{\mathbf{v}}\right)\right),

(36)

where
{\mathbf{D}}
and
{\mathbf{v}}
are defined in
eq.

32
and
eq.

30
respectively.

IV
Neural Semantic Channel Equalization

While the proposed linear solution benefits from low complexity, it has a limited capability to equalize non-linear mismatches between the source and target languages. Therefore, we propose to perform equalization based on
DNN
. Specifically, we let
{\mathbf{f}}_{\eta}
and
{\mathbf{g}}_{\nu}
be Complex-
DNN
for which the optimization in
eq.

4
is done over the network weights
\eta
and
\nu
respectively. Leveraging the dataset
\mathbf{X}
defined in
eq.

5
an the corresponding semantic pilots
\left({\mathbf{z}}_{\theta}^{(i)},{\mathbf{z}}_{\gamma}^{(i)}\right)
the

\mathcal{L}\left(\eta,\nu,{\mathbf{\phi}}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\left\|{\mathbf{z}}_{\gamma}^{(i)}-{\mathbf{g}}_{\nu}\left(\mathbf{H}_{e}^{{\mathbf{\phi}}}{\mathbf{f}}_{\eta}\left({\mathbf{z}}_{\theta}^{(i)}\right)+{\mathbf{w}}^{(i)}\right)\right\|^{2}\right].

(37)

where,
{\mathbf{w}}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{CN}\left(0,\Sigma_{\mathbf{w}}\right)
. Following traditional
ML
approaches, the models are iteratively optimized using stochastic gradient-based methods where each
{\mathbf{w}}^{(i)}
is re-sampled in every iteration. The transmit power
eq.

4b
and passive
RIS

eq.

4c

{\mathbf{f}}_{\eta}
is normalized and the effective channel is defined as

\mathbf{H}_{e}^{{\mathbf{\phi}}}={\mathbf{I}}_{K}\otimes\left(\mathbf{H}_{d}+\mathbf{H}_{2}\text{diag}\left({\mathbf{\Pi}\left({\mathbf{\phi}}\right)}\right)\mathbf{H}_{1}\right),

(38)

where
\mathbf{\Pi}
is the projection defined in
eq.

35
. These operations are captured by the gradients
\nabla_{\eta}\mathcal{L}
and
\nabla_{\mathbf{\phi}}\mathcal{L}
and therefore, the optimization by stochastic gradient-based
DL
algorithms is constraint-aware.

To improve the efficiency of the neural equalizer, we induce sparsity in the model’s parameters
\nu
and
\eta
through hard thresholding. Specifically, after each training iteration, we apply a thresholding operator that sets to zero all weights whose magnitude is below a fixed value. This process explicitly enforces sparsity by removing low-magnitude parameters that are assumed to contribute minimally to the learned transformation. Given a sparsity factor
\beta
and learning rate
\tau

\beta\tau

These results highlight that although

V-A
2
Effect of the compression factor
\zeta

Varying the compression factor
\zeta=\frac{2KN_{t}}{N_{\theta}}
with respect to
K
has two main effects on the proposed methods. First, a higher compression factor implies a larger number of parameters to optimize. For linear functions
{\mathbf{F}}
and
{\mathbf{G}}
, the number of parameters is
N_{\theta}KN_{t}
and
KN_{r}N_{\gamma}
, respectively. In the case of the neural equalizer, the parameter count for
{\mathbf{f}}_{\eta}
is
N_{\gamma}(N_{\theta}+1)+N_{\gamma}(KN_{t}+1)
, while for
{\mathbf{g}}_{\nu}
it is
N_{\gamma}(N_{\gamma}+1)+KN_{r}(N_{\gamma}+1)
. As such, increasing
\zeta
enhances the expressive capacity of the models, thereby improving their ability to align the communication protocols. Secondly,
\zeta
defines a bottleneck in the information flow between the encoder and decoder. Due to power constraints and channel noise, the number of transmitted symbols per image imposes a fundamental ceiling on the achievable information transfer. Therefore, the compression factor inherently limits the capacity, regardless of model complexity. As expected, as we can see from
fig.

2
, increasing
\zeta
(which corresponds to higher model complexity and more transmitted symbols) leads to improved performance in the proposed methods.

RIS

The
RIS
size also affects the performance of the methods in multiple ways. Similarly to the semantic compression factor, the
RIS
size defines a bottleneck in the communication. As the direct channel
\mathbf{H}_{d}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N_{r}\times N_{t}}
is blocked, the information travels through the
RIS
, effectively projected by
\mathbf{H}_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{N_{\mathbf{\phi}}\times N_{t}}
in a complex vector of
KN_{\mathbf{\phi}}
dimensions. On the other hand, the

size will affect the overall norm of the effective channel
\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\mathbf{\phi}}={\mathbf{I}}_{K}\otimes\left(\mathbf{H}_{d}+\mathbf{H}_{2}\text{diag}\left({\mathbf{\phi}}\right)\mathbf{H}_{1}\right)
. While each element in
{\mathbf{\phi}}
is fixed to an absolute value of 1, including more elements enables the transmission of a more powerful signal, which makes it more robust to noise. This is, indeed, the main idea behind the implementation of
RIS
. Finally, even if less relevant compared to the pre and post equalizer, the
RIS
also introduces more optimization parameters which can yield a performance gain given by increased complexity. From both
fig.

2(a)
and
fig.

2(b)
we can see that performance increases with the
RIS
size, related to the aforementioned factors. As shown in
fig.

2(b)
, for both the Linear and Neural equalization methods, increasing the compression factor
\zeta
beyond a certain threshold no longer yields performance improvements when the
RIS
size is limited to
2\%
or
6\%
. This plateau highlights the bottleneck imposed by the
RIS
, which restricts the system’s ability to leverage additional complexity. In contrast, this effect is not observed at higher
RIS
sizes, where performance continues to improve with increasing
\zeta
, suggesting that the limiting factor shifts from the
RIS
to model complexity. Similarly, no such bottleneck is observed in
fig.

2(a)
, where the
RIS
size scales proportionally with
K
, and thus increases with
\zeta
, as reflected in the figure.

While the Neural equalizer consistently outperforms the Linear one across all experiments, it incurs higher computational complexity. To quantify the trade-off between complexity and performance, we evaluate the number of

Additionally, the Neural equalizer is less sensitive to the size of the

Since the equalization process must adapt to dynamic changes in both the channel and the users, the design complexity of the equalizer is critically important. As demonstrated in previous results, the Linear equalizer performs best with smaller dataset sizes, which directly impacts training complexity and may make it more suitable for certain scenarios. We leave a thorough investigation of the complexity of the training process both for a Linear and a Neural (dense and sparse) equalizer for future work.

\mathbf{H}_{e}^{\mathbf{\phi}}=\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_{d}^{\prime}+\mathbf{H}_{\mathbf{\phi}}
) and we optimize the

and

or any type of adaptation, and the

-based equalizer, respectively. We set
\alpha_{d}=10
,
\alpha_{d}^{\prime}=10^{6}
,
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=10

, leveraging the same dataset previously used to train the Linear and Neural equalizers. We perform only 10

As expected, with no adaptation, the performance of the system completely degrades, with accuracy falling to 10%, i.e., random decision. On the other hand, the
RIS
-based adaptation is able to compensate for the modification of the channel change. As the number of
RIS
elements

increases, the adaptation is better, leading to reduced

and increased Accuracy. In

For the Linear Equalizer, when the

channel, the performance of the adapted system surpasses that of the pre-channel change. This can be explained by the fact that each
RIS

{\mathbf{F}}
and
{\mathbf{G}}

For the Neural equalizer, this phenomenon is not observed as performance is limited by the pre-channel change scenario. This can be explained by the fact that a Neural Equalizer is more complex and therefore benefits less from the inclusion of

increases, the

RIS
can not only work as a channel equalizer but also enhance the performance of a semantic equalizer.

VI
Conclusions

In this work, we proposed a novel methodological framework for semantic equalization that jointly performs semantic alignment, data compression, and channel equalization, distributed across the transmitter, receiver, and channel via a
RIS
module. We formulated the equalization task as a constrained
MMSE
optimization problem, where the goal is to jointly determine the transmitter-side (pre), receiver-side (post) equalizers, and the
RIS
phase configuration to minimize the semantic distortion between the transmitted and recovered symbols. Adopting a data-driven methodology, we leveraged a dataset of semantic pilots and developed two strategies to solve the problem. The first is a linear approach, where both pre and post equalizers are linear models, and the optimization is solved using an
ADMM
algorithm. The second solution is based on
DNN
, trained via conventional gradient-based
ML
techniques. Experimental results demonstrate that both proposed methods significantly outperform disjoint semantic and physical channel equalization schemes under various compression levels,
RIS
sizes,
SNR
conditions, and training dataset sizes. We performed a complexity assessment of the proposed methods and showed that a Neural Equalizer trained with sparsity constraints requires a similar number of
FLOP
per sample to achieve comparable performance as the Linear model, while the Linear model might benefit from lower training complexity due to its effectiveness on smaller datasets. Furthermore, we investigated the adaptability of the
RIS
in response to channel variations. Our results reveal that, beyond its classical role in channel adaptation, the
RIS
can enhance semantic alignment by embedding computational capabilities within the channel, thus contributing directly to the end-to-end semantic communication process. While the chosen optimization metric in this work is the
MSE
between the transmitter and receiver latent spaces, our results indicate that it may be suboptimal due to its relatively weak correlation with task-specific performance, such as classification accuracy. This observation, also reported in
[
31
]
, highlights the need for performance-oriented alignment metrics that better reflect end-task objectives. Inspired by the results obtained with the proposed baselines, we argue that a weighted version of the
MSE
—where errors in larger components are prioritized over smaller ones—could serve as a promising alternative to the standard
MSE
. This approach has the advantage of being easily integrated into our algorithm without requiring any additional information about the receiver. In contrast, task-specific metrics like classification cross-entropy depend on detailed knowledge of the receiver’s output space, which may not always be available or practical to obtain.

Our proposed framework lays a solid foundation for design, deploying and operating practical Multi-User
SemComs
in real-world scenarios by addressing the semantic misalignment problem while incorporating the physical characteristics of the communication channel. Although this work constitutes a significant first step, several research directions remain open to fully realize the potential of
SemComs
. First, in this study, we employed generic
ML
models that are communication-agnostic. A natural next step is to integrate our semantic equalization framework with dedicated, communication-aware
DJSCC
models, which are optimized for transmission tasks. While our proposed methods can work without any further adaptation in this case, the communication aware nature of
DJSCC
protocols could be leveraged to reduce the complexity of the proposed solutions. Such integration is essential to evaluate the practicality and performance of the system in real-world deployments.
Finally, a deeper investigation into the role of
RIS
as an equalization mechanism, particularly in comparison to traditional equalization strategies is needed. Understanding the full potential of programmable smart wireless environments in semantic alignment and joint communication-computation tasks may unlock new capabilities for next-generation wireless systems
[
45
]
.
