1.
Introduction

This paper concerns new characterizations of the
BMO
-Sobolev spaces
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
for
0<\alpha<2
in terms of geometric square functions which
measure multiscale approximation by constants for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and by linear functions for
\alpha\in[1,2)
. The spaces
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
were
introduced by Strichartz in
[
Str
]
and
[
Str2
]
. They can be thought of as the image of
BMO
under the
\alpha
th
Riesz potential, although
to be rigorous one needs to define them as an appropriate class of distributions, which in turn coincide with functions. These spaces occur naturally
in the theory of commutators of fractional singular integrals (see for example
[
CDH
]
,
[
CDZ
]
,
[
CHO
]
,
[
CTWX
]
,
[
HLY
]
, and
[
M
]
) and the theory of parabolic geometric measure theory (more on this below.) The new characterization of these spaces
established in this paper provide a connection between these spaces and the theory of higher-order rectifiability.

Before stating the main Theorem of this paper, we provide a history of previous results which motivate our present inquiry. We recall the notion of a “
\beta
-number,” which measures how well a measure
is approximated by a plane at a given point and scale. Given a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an integer
k\in{1,...,d-1}
, we can define
the
k
-dimensional
\beta
-numbers as

(1.1)

\displaystyle\beta_{2,k}(X,r)=\inf_{\text{$P$ a $k$-plane}}\left(\fint_{B_{r}(X)}\left(\frac{\operatorname{dist}(Y,P)}{r}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

“
\beta
-numbers” like the one above have a rich history which is too long to discuss in full, but we will
provide some relevant context.
Such quantities were first introduced by Peter Jones in
[
J
]
, where the author defined an
L^{\infty}
version of the quantity above.
In
[
DS1
]
, David and Semmes show
that, in the presence of appropriate scale-invariant regularity of the surface measure, Carleson measure estimates involving the

\beta
-numbers as defined in (
1.1
) are equivalent to a number of quantitative conditions, including boundedness of singular integral convolution operators
and the existence of a “corona decomposition,” a multiscale decomposition of the dyadic grid of the set into trees which are
well approximated by Lipschitz graphs. The aforementioned Carleson measure condition is

(1.2)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(X)\cap\Sigma}\beta_{2,k}(Y,r)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mu(Y)\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\leq CR^{k},\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\text{for all $X\in\Sigma$, $R\in(0,\diam\Sigma)$}.

We also define, for
k\in\{1,...,d-1\}
the
upper
k
-density
and
lower
k
-density

\mu

\theta^{k,*}(\mu)(X)=\limsup_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\mu(B_{r}(X))}{(2r)^{k}},

\theta^{k}_{*}(\mu)(X)=\liminf_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\mu(B_{r}(X))}{(2r)^{k}}.

For
k\in\{1,...,d-1\}
, a say that a measure
\mu
is
k
-rectifiable if there are Lipschitz functions
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\mu\bigg(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\bigcup_{i}f_{i}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\bigg)=0.

The definition of
C^{1,\alpha}
rectifiability is nearly identical, the only difference being that the Lipschitz functions
f_{i}
are replaced by
C^{1,\alpha}
functions.

]

2.
Notation, Definitions

We will consider functions having domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, endowed with the standard metric. Given
r>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we let

B_{r}(x)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x-y|<r\}.

When denoting function spaces, we will omit any mention of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For example, we will use the notation
L^{2}
,
BMO
,
H^{1}
to denote the spaces

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
BMO(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(here
H^{1}
is the Hardy space.)

The letters
C
and
c
to denote harmless constants
which depend only on the structural parameters of a given hypothesis. We will use the notation
a\lesssim b
to mean
a\leq Cb
,
a\gtrsim b

to mean
a\geq Cb
, and
a\approx b
to mean that both
a\lesssim b
and
a\gtrsim b
hold. Given a constant
D>0
, we will
often we will adopt the notation
a\lesssim_{D}b
,
a\leq CDb
,
for some constant
C
which is independent of
D
.
a\approx_{D}b
and
a\gtrsim_{D}b
are defined analogously.

Of particular interest to us is the space
BMO
.

Definition 2.1
.

The space
BMO
is the set of all equivalences classes of functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
endowed with the equivalence
relation which says that
f\sim g
if
f-g
is a constant and such that

\|f\|_{*}:=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},r>0}\fint_{B_{r}(x)}|f(y)-\fint_{B_{r}(x)}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z|\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty.

We note that the quantity above is unchanged if
one adds a constant to
f
. We will often use the
notation

\langle f\rangle_{B_{r}(x)}=\fint_{B_{r}(z)}f(z)\,\mathrm{d}z.

We want to define the space
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
. First, we need to define the
\alpha
th
fractional integral of a Schwartz function. We
adopt the notation
\mathcal{S}
for the Schwartz space on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
and
0<\alpha<d
we define

I_{\alpha}(\varphi)(x)=c_{d,\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\varphi(y)}{|x-y|^{d-\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y,

where
c_{n,\alpha}=2^{-\alpha}\pi^{-d/2}\Gamma((d-\alpha)/2)\Gamma(\alpha/2)^{-1}
. Alternatively, for any
\alpha>0
, we can define
I_{\alpha}(\varphi)
on the
Fourier transform side as

I_{\alpha}(\varphi)(x)=\big((2\pi|\xi|)^{-\alpha}\hat{\varphi}(\xi)\check{\big)}(x),

When
0<\alpha<d
, these definitions coincide.

Definition 2.3
.

Given
\alpha>0
, we define the
\alpha
th
fractional derivative of a function
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
via the multiplier
(2\pi)^{\alpha}|\xi|^{\alpha}
, i.e.

(D_{\alpha}\varphi)(x):=(2\pi)^{\alpha}(|\xi|^{\alpha}\hat{\varphi}(\xi)\check{)}(x).

Observe that for
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
and any
\alpha>0
,
I_{\alpha}D_{\alpha}\varphi=D_{\alpha}I_{\alpha}\varphi=\varphi
.

We let
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
denote the space of tempered distributions. Given an integer
l\geq 0
let
P_{l}
denote the polynomials of degree at most
l
, and
define
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}/P_{l}
to be the set of “tempered distributions modulo polynomials of degree
l
.” By this, we mean the
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}

endowed with the equivalence relation that says
f,g\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
are equivalent if
f-g\in P_{l}
.

We now define the functions which have dual space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}/P_{l}
.

\mathcal{S}_{l}
is the subset
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
such that, for every multi-index
\gamma
with
|\gamma|\leq l
, one has

\int x^{\gamma}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=0.

We endow
\mathcal{S}_{l}
with the topology inherited from
\mathcal{S}
.

We state the following Proposition, which is proved in the appendix of this paper.

Proposition 2.4
.

The dual space of
\mathcal{S}_{l}
can be identified with
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}/P_{l}
.

We are now ready to define the space
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
.

Definition 2.5
.

Given
\alpha>0
,
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
is the subset of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}/P_{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}
consisting of distributions
I_{\alpha}(b)
,
b\in BMO

defined via the following action on
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}

I_{\alpha}(b)(\varphi):=\int b(x)I_{\alpha}(\varphi)(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Remark 2.6
.

In the appendix we prove that the integral in the definition of
I_{\alpha}(b)
above is well-defined.

As mentioned above, the spaces
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
coincide with functions modulo polynomials (constants in the case that
0<\alpha<1

and linear functions when
1\leq\alpha<2
.) We will often abuse language and simply refer to “functions in
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
.” The following Proposition is proved in the appendix.

Proposition 2.7
.

For
0<\alpha<1
, members of
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
coincide with functions modulo constants. In other words, given a tempered distribution modulo constants
f\in I_{\alpha}(BMO)
, we can identify

with a function modulo
constants. If
1\leq\alpha<2
, members of
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
coincide with functions modulo linear polynomials.

Given
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
will adopt the following
notation for the
C^{\alpha}
norm of

,

0<\alpha<1

[f]_{\alpha}=\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}}.

We will need the following Proposition about the continuity properties
of
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
for
0<\alpha<2
, which is proved in the paper of Strichartz
[

]
.

Proposition 2.8
.

For
0<\alpha<1
, (equivalence classes of) functions in
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
are Hölder continuous with exponent
\alpha
. For
1<\alpha<2
, (equivalence classes of)
functions in
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
are differentiable with

-Hölder continuous gradients. If
\alpha=1
, (equivalence classes of) functions in

are differentiable almost everywhere with gradients in

for

, and

for

.

3.
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
Implies Square Function Bounds

In this section we show that functions in
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
necessarily satisfy the square function bounds (
1.13
) and (
1.14
) for
0<\alpha<1
and
1\leq\alpha<2
,
respectively. We begin by showing that localizations of
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
functions satisfy appropriate bounds which comport with the scale of the localizations. As we shall see,
the case when
\alpha=1
is fundamentally different. We should note that Propositions

3.1
and
3.5
are somewhat
similar to a Lemma proved in the appendix of
[
H2
]
, although our case is somewhat more difficult because we need to analyze the singular integral
which makes an appearance in the final term of
our estimate in the case that
1\leq\alpha<2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
f\in C^{\infty}
,
1<\alpha<2
. Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be smooth, equal to
1
on
B_{2}(0)
, equal to
0

outside of
B_{4}(0)
, and such that
0\leq\phi\leq 1
everywhere. Given
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
R>0
, define

\displaystyle g(x):=\phi\left(\frac{x-z}{R}\right)\big(f(x)-f(z)-\nabla f(z)\cdot(x-z)\big).

Then we have the following bound

(3.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big||\xi|^{\alpha}\hat{g}(\xi)\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\leq CR^{d}.

Where
C=C(d,\alpha)\|D_{\alpha}f\|_{*}^{2}
.

Proof.

Recall that, for
\alpha\in(1,2)
,
[\nabla f]_{\alpha-1}\lesssim\|D_{\alpha}\|_{*}
. Over the course of this proof, we will bound terms on the order of
[\nabla f]_{\alpha-1}^{2}R^{d}
, which is dominated by

\|D_{\alpha}f\|_{*}^{2}R^{d}
.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\hat{g}(\xi)|\xi|^{\alpha}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\approx\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(p.v.\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{g(x)-g(y)}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

The display above uses standard facts about integral representation of the fractional Laplacian for
\alpha
in the range
0<\alpha<2
.
We bound expression on the right hand side above up to
a harmless constant as follows

|\hat{\psi}(r)||e^{-2\pi iy\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}}-2\pi iy\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}-1|\lesssim|y|^{2}\leq r^{2}.

So, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{B_{r}(0)}|e^{-2\pi iy\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}}-2\pi iy\cdot\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\hat{\psi}(r)-\hat{\psi}(r)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\frac{\,\mathrm{d}r}{r^{d+1+2\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}r^{d+4}r^{-d-1-2\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}r+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}r}{r^{1+2\alpha}}<\infty.

So, we have proved the case where
1\leq\alpha<2
as well.
∎

Before continuing, we need to collect a few facts about the fractional Laplacian.

Proposition 3.12
.

Suppose
f\in I_{\alpha}(BMO)
,
0<\alpha<2
. We have, for all
R>0
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the estimate

(3.13)

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\overline{\nu}_{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}^{f}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\leq CR^{d},

where the constant
C
is comparable to
\|D_{\alpha}f\|_{*}^{2}
.

Proof.

We split the proof to cover three cases:
1<\alpha<2
,
\alpha=1
, and
0<\alpha<1
.
The case when
\alpha=1
differs substantially from the other two cases. The case when
0<\alpha<1
is only slightly different from the case
when
1<\alpha<2
.

We now prove the case when
\alpha=1
. Fix
f\in I_{1}(BMO)
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
R>0
. We localize
f
to
B_{R}(z)
as follows

g(x):=\phi\bigg(\frac{x-z}{R}\bigg)\big(f(x)-f(z)+\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{R})(z)\cdot(x-z)\big).

where
\phi
is the same as in the statement of Proposition
3.1
.

Notice that
\nu_{1}^{g}(x,r)=\nu_{1}^{f}(x,r)
for
x\in B_{R}(z)
and
0<r\leq R
. So, we necessarily have

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\overline{\nu}_{1}^{f}(x,r)\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}=\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\overline{\nu}_{1}^{g}(x,r)\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}.

We apply Proposition
3.9
to say

(3.14)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\overline{\nu}_{1}^{g}(x,r)\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\hat{g}(\xi)|\xi|\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\approx\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla g(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\int|I(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int|II(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Where
I(x)
and
II(x)
are obtained by estimating
|\nabla g|
as follows

|\nabla g(x)|\\
\leq\left|R^{-1}(\nabla\phi)\left(\frac{x-z}{R}\right)\big(f(x)-f(z)-\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{R})(z)\cdot(x-z)\big)\right|+\left|\varphi\left(\frac{x-z}{R}\right)\big(\nabla f(x)-\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{R})(z)\big)\right|\\
=:I(x)+II(x).

We estimate
\int|I(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x
first

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|I(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq R^{-2}\int_{|x-z|\lesssim R}|f(x)-f(z)-\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{R})(z)\cdot(x-z)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=R^{-2}\int_{|x-z|\lesssim R}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla f(tx-(1-t)z)\cdot(x-z)\,\mathrm{d}t-\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{R})(z)\cdot(x-z)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\int_{|x-z|\lesssim R}\left|\nabla f(tx+(1-t)z)-\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{R})(z)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}t^{-d}\int_{|x-z|\lesssim tR}\left|\nabla f(x)-\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{tR})(z)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}R^{d}\left|\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{R})(z)-\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{tR})(z)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=T_{1}+T_{2}.

We will show that both terms on the last line of the display above are bounded on the order of
R^{d}\|\nabla f\|_{BMO}^{2}
. Term
T_{1}
can
be seen to bounded on the order of
R^{d}\|\nabla f\|_{BMO}^{2}
by noting that
\|\varphi(x)-1_{B_{1}(0)}\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim 1
, which, together
with standard John-Nirenberg estimates and
H^{1}
-
BMO
duality, yield the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}t^{-d}\int_{|x-z|\lesssim tR}\left|\nabla f(x)-\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{tR})(z)\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}t^{-d}\int_{|x-z|\lesssim tR}|\nabla f(x)-\fint_{|y-z|\leq tR}\nabla f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+R^{d}|\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{tR})(z)-\nabla(f\ast(tR)^{-d}1_{B_{tR}(0)})(z)|^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle R^{d}\|\nabla f\|_{*}^{2}.

For term
T_{2}
we do the following calculation

\left|\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{R})(z)-\nabla(f\ast\varphi_{tR})(z)\right|\\
\left|\int(\nabla f)(z-y)\big(\varphi_{R}(y)-\varphi_{tR}(y)\big)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|=\left|\int(\nabla f)(z-(Rt)y)\big(t^{d}\varphi(ty)-\varphi(y)\big)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\\
\leq\|\nabla f\|_{*}\|t^{d}\varphi(t\cdot)-\varphi(\cdot)\|_{H^{1}}\lesssim\|\nabla f\|_{*}.

It remains to estimate
\int|II(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x
. This uses the same techniques for the first term, but is even easier. We omit the details, and conclude the proof
in the case when
\alpha=1
.

Now we consider the cases when
\alpha\neq 1
. We will use Propositions
3.1
and
3.5
along with an approximation argument.

Fix
f\in I_{\alpha}(BMO)
,
1<\alpha<2
,
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
R>0
. We localize
f
to
B_{R}(z)
as follows

g(x):=\phi\bigg(\frac{x-z}{R}\bigg)\big(f(x)-f(z)-\nabla f(z)\cdot(x-z)\big).

where
\phi
is a smooth, positive function with
\phi\equiv 1
on
\overline{B_{2}(0)}
,
\phi=0
outside of
B_{4}(0)
, and
0\leq\phi\leq 1
everywhere.

Notice that
\nu_{1}^{g}(x,r)=\nu_{1}^{f}(x,r)
for
x\in B
and
0<r\leq R
. So, we necessarily have

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\overline{\nu}_{1}^{f}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}=\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\overline{\nu}_{1}^{g}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}.

If we can show that the right hand side is bounded on the order of
R^{d}
, we will have proved the Proposition
in the case
1<\alpha<2
. Let
\{\psi_{t}\}_{t>0}
be an approximation of the identity and set

\displaystyle f^{t}(x)

\displaystyle=(f\ast\psi_{t})(x),

\displaystyle g^{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\psi\left(\frac{x-z}{R}\right)\big(f^{t}(x)-f^{t}(z)-\nabla f^{t}(z)\cdot(x-z)\big).

Notice that for
t>0
,
f^{t}\in C^{\infty}
, and hence we can apply Lemma
3.1
to conclude

(3.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big||\xi|^{\alpha}\hat{g^{t}}(\xi)\big|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\lesssim\|D_{\alpha}f^{t}\|_{*}^{2}R^{d}.

On the other hand, using arguments which are now familiar to the reader, we have

(3.16)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\overline{\nu}^{f^{t}}_{1}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\hat{g}^{t}(\xi)|\xi|^{\alpha}\big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

One can easily check that
\|D_{\alpha}f^{t}\|_{*}\leq C\|D_{\alpha}f\|_{*}
uniformly in
t
. Using
the Hölder bound on
\nabla f
we derive the bound

(3.17)

\displaystyle|\overline{\nu}_{1}^{f^{t}-f}(x,r)^{2}|\lesssim\fint_{B_{r}(x)}\left(\frac{|(f^{t}(y)-f(y))-(f^{t}(x)-f(x))-\nabla(f^{t}(x)-f(x))\cdot(y-x)|}{r}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\lesssim t^{2(\alpha-1)}.

Hence,

\int_{\sqrt{t}}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\overline{\nu}_{1}^{f}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\lesssim\int_{\sqrt{t}}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\overline{\nu}_{1}^{f^{t}}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}+R^{d}t^{\alpha-1}\\
\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(\widehat{g^{t}}(\xi)|\xi|^{\alpha}\big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi+R^{d}t^{\alpha-1}\lesssim\|D_{\alpha}f\|_{*}^{2}R^{d}+R^{d}t^{\alpha-1}.

Taking

to
0
yields the Proposition in the case that
1<\alpha<2
. As usual, the case
0<\alpha<1
is almost exactly the same, but easier. In this case we set

\displaystyle f^{t}(x)

\displaystyle=(f\ast\psi_{t})(x),

\displaystyle g^{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\psi\left(\frac{x-z}{R}\right)\big(f_{t}(x)-f_{t}(z)\big).

The only other difference between this case and the case when

is that we now have the bound

|\overline{\nu}_{1}^{f^{t}-f}(x,r)^{2}|=\fint_{B_{r}(x)}\left(\frac{|(f^{t}(y)-f(y))-(f^{t}(x)-f(x))|}{r}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\lesssim t^{2\alpha}.

But one can then check

So, the same argument as the one given above works in this case as well.
∎

We now note that, in the case

implies the tempered growth condition
(
1.15
) for
\epsilon>\alpha
. This is proved in
[
Str
]
, see Theorem

4.1

.

4.
From Square Function Bounds to
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
.

In this section we prove that the square function bounds (
1.13
) and (
1.14
) put a function in
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
,
0<\alpha<1
and
1\leq\alpha<2
, respectively (note: for the latter we also need
to assume (
1.15
) for
\epsilon>\alpha
.) To do this we will verify a criterion of Strichartz. The following Theorem is proved
in
[
Str
]
.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
Str
]
)
.

For
0<\alpha<1
, a function
f
is in
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
if and only if

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{Q}\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\int_{Q}\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=B<\infty.

where the supremum above is taken over cubes (note: we can take it over balls as well, changing
B
only by a multiplicative constant depending on dimension.)
For
0<\alpha<2
,
f\in I_{\alpha}(BMO)
if and only if
f
satisfies the following “tempered growth estimate” for all
\epsilon>\alpha

(4.3)

\displaystyle\int\frac{|f(x)|}{1+|x|^{d+\epsilon}}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty,

and also the estimate

(4.4)

\displaystyle\sup_{Q}\left(\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\int_{Q}\frac{|2f(x)-f(x+y)-f(x-y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{d+2\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=B<\infty.

In both cases, the constant
B
is comparable to
\|D_{\alpha}f\|_{*}
.

Remark 4.5
.

We note that in
[
Str
]
the author refers
to
4.3
as the “tempered growth condition”, but does not specify that it needs to hold for
\epsilon>\alpha
. However, if one carefully reads the proof provided in that paper, they will see that
\epsilon>\alpha

is the correct range for the statement.

We need to replace the optimal polynomials of the
\nu
coefficients with “approximate Taylor polynomials.” The content of the next proposition
shows that one can replace the infimum in the definition of the
\nu
coefficients with an approximate Taylor polynomial and still retain the square function estimate.
When
0<\alpha<1
, we define

(4.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}_{0}^{f}(x,r)=\left(\fint_{\frac{r}{2}\leq|y|\leq r}\left(\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)|}{r}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

and, when
1\leq\alpha<2
, we define

(4.7)

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}_{1}^{f}(x,r)=\left(\fint_{\frac{r}{2}\leq|y|\leq r}\left(\frac{|f(x+y)-f(x)-(\phi_{r}\ast\nabla f)(x)\cdot y|}{r}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}y\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
\phi_{r}
is some approximate identity.

Lemma 4.8
.

For
0<\alpha<2
and
f
satisfying

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\nu_{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}^{f}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\leq C_{1}R^{d}

for all
R>0
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have the estimate

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\tilde{\nu}_{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}^{f}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\lesssim C_{1}R^{d}.

Proof.

We now prove the Lemma is the case that
0<\alpha<1
. Given
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r>0
, let

c_{(x,r)}
be the constant minimizing
\nu^{f}_{0}(x,r)
. For
f
as in the statement of the Lemma,
R>0
, and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\tilde{\nu}_{0}^{f}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\\
\lesssim\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\fint_{\frac{r}{2}\leq|y|\leq r}\left(\frac{|f(x)-c_{(x,r)}|}{r}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}yr^{1-2\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r\\
+\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\fint_{\frac{r}{2}\leq|y|\leq r}\left(\frac{|f(x+y)-c_{(x,r)}|}{r}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}yr^{1-2\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r\\
=I+II.

Term
II
is trivially bounded up to a harmless constant
by

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\nu_{0}^{f}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\leq C_{1}R^{d}.

For term
I
, we change variables in
y
so the range of integration in the inner integral is over
r/2\leq|y-x|\leq r
. Then, we notice that
if
|z-x|\leq R
and
|x-y|<r
, then
|z-y|\leq 2R
. So, we can use Fubini to bound the integral from above by

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{2R}(z)}\fint_{\frac{r}{2}\leq|x-y|\leq r}\left(\frac{|f(x)-c_{(x,r)}|}{r}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}xr^{1-2\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}r.

This integral is clearly bounded on the order of
C_{1}R^{d}
.

Now we prove the case when
1\leq\alpha<2
. We now let
l_{(x,r)}
denote the affine function
minimizing
\nu_{1}^{f}(x,r)
. We note that for any affine function
L(x)
one has
L(x+y)=L(x)+y\cdot\big(\phi_{r}\ast\nabla L(x)\big)
. We apply this fact to
l_{(x,r)}
to obtain
the estimate

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\tilde{\nu}_{1}^{f}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\lesssim\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\fint_{r/2\leq|y|\leq r}\left(\frac{|f(x+y)-l_{(x,r)}(x+y)|}{r}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}yr^{1-2\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r\\
+\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\fint_{r/2\leq|y|\leq r}\left(\frac{|f(x)-l_{(x,r)}(x)|}{r}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}yr^{1-2\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r\\
+\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\fint_{r/2\leq|y|\leq r}\left(\frac{|y\cdot(\phi_{r}\ast\nabla(f-l_{(x,r)}))(x)|}{r}\right)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}yr^{1-2\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r\\
=I+II+III.

We need to show that
I
,
II
, and
III
are all bounded on the order of
R^{d}
. By the definition of
l
, it is clear that

I\lesssim\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B^{\prime}_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\nu_{1}^{f}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\lesssim C_{1}R^{d}.

Term
II
is estimated in the exact same way that term
I
in the case
0<\alpha<1
was estimated, which will show that it is bounded on the order of

C_{1}R^{d}
. For term
III
we move the gradient in the convolution
off
f-l_{(x,r)}
and onto
\phi_{r}
. This bounds the inner integrand in term
III
by

\left(\frac{y\cdot\frac{1}{r}((\nabla\phi_{r})\ast(f-l_{(x,r)})(x)}{r}\right)^{2}\lesssim\left(\fint_{B_{r}(x)}\frac{|f(\zeta)-l_{(x,r)}(\zeta)|}{r}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\right)^{2}\leq\nu_{1}^{f}(x,r)^{2}.

Where the last inequality above is an application of Hölder’s inequality. This clearly allows us to bound term
III
on the order of
C_{1}R^{d}
.
∎

Proposition 4.9
.

Suppose
0<\alpha<2
, and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B^{\prime}_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\nu^{f}_{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\leq C_{1}R^{d}.

for all
R>0
and all

. Additionally,
in the case that

, assume

satisfies

1.15

. Then

, and
\|D_{\alpha}f\|_{*}^{2}\lesssim C_{1}

Proof.

Fix
R>0
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. By Lemma

, our hypothesis implies

\int_{0}^{R}\int_{B_{R}(z)}\left(\frac{\tilde{\nu}_{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}^{f}(x,r)}{r^{\alpha-1}}\right)^{2}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}r}{r}\lesssim C_{1}R^{d}

[

]
,
stated in Theorem

The same Theorem also says that, for

,

and

Proof of Proposition
2.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{S}_{l})^{*}

\mathcal{S}_{l}

be the restriction of

\mathcal{S}_{l}

is such that

, i.e.
\langle u,\varphi\rangle=0
for all

\langle\hat{u},\check{\varphi}\rangle=0
for all
\check{\varphi}\in\mathcal{S}
supported away from the origin, and so
u
must be a polynomial.
Suppose, by way of contradiction, that the degree of
u
is
k
and
k>l
. Suppose one term of
u
of degree
k
is

ax_{i_{1}}^{n_{1}}...x_{i_{j}}^{n_{j}}

a\neq 0
, and

\theta:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be smooth, equal to
1

B_{1}(0)
, and supported in
B_{2}(0)
. Set

\phi(x):=\theta(x)\left(ax_{i_{1}}^{n_{1}}...x_{i_{j}}^{n_{j}}\right).

We note that
\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
. This is true because any derivative of order up to
l
of
\hat{\phi}
must evaluate to
0
at

. We also have

\left(\partial_{i_{1}}^{n_{1}}...\partial_{i_{j}}^{n_{j}}\right)\phi(x)\bigg|_{x=0}\neq 0.

Moreover, if
cx_{i^{\prime}_{1}}^{n_{1}^{\prime}}...x_{i^{\prime}_{j^{\prime}}}^{n_{j}^{\prime}}
is any term other term of degree
\leq k
(a term of any polynomial, not just of
u
), then

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{i^{\prime}_{1}}^{n_{1}^{\prime}}...\partial_{i_{j^{\prime}}}^{n_{j}^{\prime}}\right)x_{i_{1}}^{n_{1}}...x_{i_{j}}^{n_{j}}\bigg|_{x=0}=0.

So, we have
\langle\check{u},\hat{\phi}\rangle\neq 0
and
\phi\in\mathcal{S}_{l}
, a contradiction. So, the degree of
u
must be less than or equal
to
l
. So,
\ker S\subseteq P_{l}
. The other inclusion is obvious from the definitions, hence
\ker S=P_{l}
.

To finish the proof, it is enough so show that the range of
S
is
(\mathcal{S}_{l})^{*}
. Given
u\in(\mathcal{S}_{l})^{*}
,
u
is a linear functional
defined on
\mathcal{S}_{l}
, which is a subspace of
\mathcal{S}
. So, there is a finite sum of
Schwartz seminorms
Q
such that, for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{l}

|\langle u,\varphi\rangle|\leq CQ(\varphi).

An application of the Hahn-Banach theorem tells us that
u
has an extension to all of
\mathcal{S}
, and hence that
S
is a surjective mapping.
∎

We need the following Proposition to show
that the distributions
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
are well
defined.

Proposition 5.1
.

Given
b\in BMO
, the integral

\int b(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

is well defined and absolutely convergent for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
. Moreover,
b
can be identified with a distribution in

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}/P_{0}=(\mathcal{S}_{0})^{*}
. In other words, there is a finite sum of Schwartz seminorms
Q_{b}
such that for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}

|\langle b,\varphi\rangle|:=\left|\int b(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq CQ_{b}(\varphi).

Proof.

The fact that the integrals in question are well defined is clear because our test functions have mean value zero. Additionally, for
b\in BMO
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
,

\int|b(x)\varphi(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x\lesssim\int\frac{|b(x)|}{1+|x|^{d+1}}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty,

because
BMO
functions satisfy
1.15

for every
\epsilon>0
. So the integral in
question is absolutely convergent.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
be a function with mean value zero. We use the fact that

\int\frac{|b(x)-\langle b\rangle_{B_{1}(0)}|}{(1+|x|)^{n+1}}\leq c\|b\|_{*}.

For a proof of this fact, see
[
FS
]
. Because
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
,
|\varphi(x)|\leq c(\varphi)(1+|x|)^{-n-1}
,
where
c(\varphi)
is comparable to a finite sum of Schwartz seminorms of
\varphi
(and these seminorms are the same as
\varphi
varies.)
So, we have the estimate

\left|\int b(x)\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|=\left|\int(b(x)-\langle b\rangle_{B_{1}(0)})\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq c(\varphi)\|b\|_{*}.

By our note above about the nature of
c(\varphi)
, the Proposition follows.
∎

We recall Definition
2.5
.

Definition 2.5

Given
\alpha>0
,
I_{\alpha}(BMO)
is the subset of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}/P_{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}
consisting of distributions
I_{\alpha}(b)
,
b\in BMO

defined via the following action on
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}

I_{\alpha}(b)(\varphi):=\int b(x)I_{\alpha}(\varphi)(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Let’s discuss why these distributions are well defined. The issues at hand are absolute convergence of the integral and that we need to have
I_{\alpha}(\varphi)\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
when

\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}
. For the latter to be true, it is enough to verify that for
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{\lfloor\alpha\rfloor}

|\xi|^{-\alpha}\hat{\varphi}(\xi)\in\mathcal{S}
and

\lim_{\xi\rightarrow 0}|\xi|^{-\alpha}\hat{\varphi}(\xi)=0.

We leave verification of these facts to the reader.
Once we know that
I_{\alpha}(\varphi)\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
, the Proposition above
tells us that the integral is well defined and absolutely convergent.

Now we want to verify that
I_{\alpha}(b)
can be identified with a function when
b\in BMO
.

Proof of Proposition
2.7
.

We first note that if
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
and
\alpha>0

I_{\alpha}(\varphi)(x)=\int\varphi(y)\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|x|^{n-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y.

So,

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(b)(\varphi)=\int b(x)\int\varphi(y)\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|x|^{n-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x.

It would be nice if we could use Fubini to switch the order of integration, but it isn’t clear that the integral is absolutely convergent. We replace
b
with

\displaystyle b_{k}(x)=\begin{cases}k&b(x)\geq k\\
b(x)&-k\leq b(x)\leq k\\
-k&b(x)\leq-k\end{cases}

For the estimates we are about to perform we need that

\||\cdot-y|^{\alpha-d}-|\cdot|^{\alpha-d}\|_{H^{1}}=c|y|^{\alpha}\approx\||\cdot-y|^{\alpha-d}-|\cdot|^{\alpha-d}\|_{L^{1}}
. This is
shown in the proof of Theorem 3.4 in
[
Str
]
. We have

\displaystyle\int\kern-3.39996pt\int\left|b_{k}(x)\varphi(y)\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|x|^{d-\alpha}}\right)\right|\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\leq k\int|y|^{\alpha}|\varphi(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y<\infty.

So, we can apply Fubini to conclude that

\int b_{k}(x)\int\varphi(y)\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|x|^{d-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=\int\kern-3.39996pt\int b_{k}(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|x|^{d-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

So, we have

\left|\int b(x)\int\varphi(y)\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|x|^{d-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x-\int\kern-3.39996pt\int b(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|x|^{d-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\\
\leq\left|\int(b(x)-b_{k}(x))\int\varphi(y)\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|x|^{d-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x\right|+\left|\int\kern-3.39996pt\int(b(x)-b_{k}(x))\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|x|^{d-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right|\\
\leq\|b-b_{k}\|_{*}\|I_{\alpha}(\varphi)\|_{H^{1}}+\|b-b_{k}\|_{*}\int|y|^{\alpha}|\varphi(y)|\,\mathrm{d}y.

So, taking
k
to infinity yields

\int b(x)\int\varphi(y)\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|x|^{d-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=\int\int b(x)\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|x|^{d-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\varphi(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

Hence, we can associate the distribution
I_{\alpha}(b)
with the function

I_{\alpha}(b)(x):=\int b(y)\left(\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d-\alpha}}-\frac{1}{|y|^{d-\alpha}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}y.

This proves the case when
0<\alpha<1
. Now
we turn our attention to the case when

1\leq\alpha<2
.

Suppose that
f\in I_{\alpha}(BMO)
for some
\alpha\in[1,2)
. Let
b\in BMO(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be such that

f=I_{\alpha}(b)
. The partial derivatives of
f
can be defined distributionally as

\displaystyle\partial_{i}f(\varphi)=-f(\partial_{i}\varphi),\,\,\,\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}.

Notice that when
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
,
\partial_{i}\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, so this definition makes sense. Let

b\in BMO
be the function such that

f(\varphi)=\langle b,I_{\alpha}(\varphi)\rangle.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{i}
denote the
i
th
Riesz transform. Notice that

I_{1}(\partial_{i}\varphi)=\mathcal{R}_{i}(\varphi)
for any Schwartz function
\varphi
, and some fixed constant
c
which does not depend on
i
.
Let
H_{0}^{1}
denote the subspace of
H^{1}
which consists of finite sums of
H^{1}
atoms. We can define the Riesz transform of
b
through an action
on
H_{0}^{1}
functions

\mathcal{R}_{i}b(\varphi):=\langle b,-\mathcal{R}_{i}\varphi\rangle,\,\,\,\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}.

Notice that the mapping sending
\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{1}
to
\langle b,-\mathcal{R}_{i}\varphi\rangle
is a bounded linear functional on
H_{0}^{1}
, and
hence extends continuously to a bounded linear functional on all of
H^{1}
because
H_{0}^{1}
is dense in
H^{1}
. Moreover, because
BMO

is the dual space of
H^{1}
, there exists some
\tilde{b}_{i}\in BMO
such that

\mathcal{R}_{i}b(\varphi)=\langle\tilde{b}_{i},\varphi\rangle

for all
\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}
. So, we can identify
\mathcal{R}_{i}b
with
\tilde{b}_{i}
. Notice that
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is a subset of
H^{1}
for every

i\geq 0
, and hence we can say that, as a member of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}/P_{0}
, the distribution
\mathcal{R}_{i}b
coincides with
\tilde{b}_{i}
.

For each
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}
, we have

\partial_{i}f(\varphi)=-\langle\tilde{b}_{i},I_{\alpha-1}(\varphi)\rangle=-\langle I_{\alpha-1}(\tilde{b}_{i}),\varphi\rangle.

From the results above, we know that
I_{\alpha-1}(\tilde{b}_{i})
makes sense as a function. Hence,
\partial_{i}f
coincides with the function

I_{\alpha-1}(-\tilde{b}_{i})\in I_{\alpha-1}(BMO)
. We let
\nabla f=(\partial_{1}f,...,\partial_{d}f)
.

Consider the function

\tilde{f}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}(\nabla f)(tx)\cdot x\,\mathrm{d}t.

We are going to show that the distribution
f
coincides with
\tilde{f}
. We have, for
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
,

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{f},\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{1}I_{\alpha-1}(-\tilde{b}_{i})(tx)x_{i}\,\mathrm{d}t\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I_{\alpha-1}(-\tilde{b}_{i})(tx)x_{i}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}I_{\alpha-1}(-\tilde{b}_{i})(x)\frac{x_{i}}{t}\varphi\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)t^{-d}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

We justify the interchanging the variables of integration by noting that the integral above converges absolutely, which
can be seen after noting that
|I_{\alpha-1}(-\tilde{b}_{i})(tx)-I_{\alpha-1}(-\tilde{b}_{i})(0)|\lesssim t^{\alpha-1}|x|^{\alpha-1}

and
|x|^{\alpha-1}x_{i}\varphi(x)
,
x_{i}\varphi(x)
are both rapidly decaying. We now take the Fourier transform in
x
to obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\langle\widehat{I_{\alpha}(b)}(\xi),\xi\cdot(\nabla\check{\varphi})(t\xi)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\langle\widehat{I_{\alpha}(b)}(\xi),\int_{0}^{1}\xi\cdot(\nabla\check{\varphi})(t\xi)\,\mathrm{d}t\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle I_{\alpha}(b),\varphi\rangle.

Hence,
\tilde{f}
coincides with
I_{\alpha}(b)
. In the display above, one can justify moving the integration in
t
inside the brackets
using the fact that the Riemann sums of the integral converge to
\hat{\varphi}
in the topology of Schwartz functions and the continuity of
I_{\alpha}(b)

as a functional.
∎
