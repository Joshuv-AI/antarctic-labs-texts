Psychometric evaluation of the Persian version of the return-to-sport scale after shoulder instability in Iranian athletes
Authors: Aynollah Naderi, Hossein ChoromZadeh, Heydar Abdollahi, Hans Degens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPsychological readiness is critical for successful return to sport (RTS) after shoulder instability, yet no validated Persian-language tool exists for this purpose. This study aimed to translate, adapt, and evaluate the psychometric properties of the Persian Shoulder Instability–Return to Sport Index (SI-RSI-Pr) in Iranian athletes.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 93 athletes (mean age = 27.8 ± 5.0 years) with unilateral shoulder instability. The SI-RSI-Pr was translated using forward–backward procedures. Structural validity was assessed via exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Construct validity was evaluated through correlations with the Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index (WOSI), Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK-17), and Walch-Duplay Score (WDS). Known-groups validity compared athletes who returned to sport versus those who did not. Reliability was tested via internal consistency (Cronbach’s α) and test–retest reliability (ICC) over a 7–10-day interval.ResultsEFA confirmed a three-factor structure (Confidence in RTS, Fear and Anxiety about RTS, Psychological Burden of RTS), explaining 78% of variance with strong factor loadings (0.744–0.955) and good model fit (RMSEA = 0.070, TLI = 0.966). The SI-RSI-Pr showed high internal consistency (α = 0.89–0.94) and test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.90–0.95). Construct validity was supported by correlations with WDS (r = 0.74), TSK-17 (r = -0.58), and WOSI (r = -0.57). Known-groups validity was confirmed, distinguishing returners from non-returners (AUC = 0.765, cut-off = 61.67, specificity = 92.86%). The scale exhibited strong feasibility and minimal floor/ceiling effects.ConclusionsThe SI-RSI-Pr, rigorously adapted for Persian-speaking athletes, is a reliable and valid tool for assessing psychological readiness to RTS after shoulder instability, supporting return-to-sport decisions in Persian-speaking athletes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01394-x.

Introduction

Refers to the increased tendency of the glenohumeral joint to undergo subluxation or complete dislocation, placing it at heightened risk for such events [1]. It typically presents through recurrent episodes of partial or full dislocation, often accompanied by pain and functional impairment [1]. This condition is particularly common among athletes, with an estimated incidence of 0.12 injuries per 1,000 training exposures [1]. Surgical interventions, such as the Latarjet procedure and arthroscopic Bankart repair (ABR), are commonly used and have demonstrated effectiveness in restoring mechanical stability to the shoulder [2]. However, despite these surgical corrections, the recurrence rate of shoulder instability remains relatively high, ranging from 9% to 37% [3, 4], Furthermore, many athletes demonstrate considerable variability in their ability to return to their pre-injury level of sport participation, reflecting the complex, multifactorial nature of the recovery process [3].As athletes progress through physical rehabilitation, determining their ‘readiness to return to sport’ (RTS) is a critical consideration—not only for athletes themselves but also for stakeholders such as coaches, medical professionals, teammates, and families [5, 6]. The RTS decision carries far-reaching implications for performance, safety, and potential medicolegal consequences. Traditionally, such decisions have relied on physical assessments and functional tests [5, 6]. However, growing evidence supports the need of a more comprehensive, biopsychosocial framework that considers psychological, contextual, and social factors alongside physical recovery [7, 8]. Among these domains, psychological readiness has emerged as a pivotal component in determining RTS outcomes, where fear of reinjury, confidence, emotional readiness, and perception of risk significantly influence whether an athlete successfully returns to sport—and remains injury-free [6].

In response to this new paradigm, several tools have been developed to assess psychological readiness following injuries [9–11]. One such tool is the Shoulder Instability Return to Sport Index (SI-RSI), a patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) specifically designed to assess psychological readiness following shoulder instability [12–14]. The SI-RSI is conceptually derived from the well-established Anterior Cruciate Ligament Return to Sport after Injury (ACL-RSI) scale [15], originally developed in 2008 with 12 items to assess psychological readiness following ACL reconstruction [16, 17]. The ACL-RSI has been widely validated, shortened to a 6-item version [18], and translated into numerous languages, establishing its role as a key predictive tool for RTS after knee injuries [17].

Similarly, the SI-RSI was introduced in a 12-item version [16] and later shortened to a more concise 5-item version [13], aiming to provide a standardized and efficient assessment of psychological readiness in athletes with shoulder instability. The SI-RSI evaluates four domains: (i) confidence in performance, (ii) fear of reinjury and risk appraisal, (iii) emotional and mental state, and (iv) perceptions of surgical/rehabilitative progress [15]. Notably, studies have demonstrated that lower SI-RSI scores are associated with lower rates of RTS, underscoring its predictive validity [19].

Although the long form of the SI-RSI has been validated in several languages—including English [15], French [14], Italian [12], and Turkish [20] — the short version has only been psychometrically assessed in English [13]. Currently, no validated Persian-language tool exists to evaluate psychological readiness to RTS following shoulder instability. This represents a critical gap in clinical practice for Persian-speaking populations.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to translate the long form of the SI-RSI into Persian and to evaluate its psychometric properties—including validity, reliability, and floor and ceiling effects—in Iranian athletes with a history of shoulder instability. This study hypothesized that the Persian version of the long-form SI-RSI questionnaire would demonstrate strong psychometric properties in Iranian athletes with a history of shoulder instability. Specifically, it was expected to show excellent face and content validity based on expert evaluations, as well as good construct validity through significant correlations with related psychological and functional measures. The questionnaire was also anticipated to exhibit high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha ≥ 0.80) and test–retest reliability (ICC ≥ 0.80). Furthermore, it was hypothesized that the Persian SI-RSI would be responsive to clinical changes over time and demonstrate minimal floor and ceiling effects, with fewer than 15% of participants scoring the lowest or highest possible scores.

Materials and methodology

Study design

This cross-sectional observational study was designed and reported in accordance with established methodological guidelines: the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist for cross-sectional studies [21], the CHERRIES (Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys) for online data collection [22], and, most importantly, the COSMIN (Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments) guideline for studies on the measurement properties of PROMs [23]. The COSMIN guideline informed the design, analysis, and interpretation of all psychometric evaluations, including assessments of reliability, validity, and interpretability.

Participants

Ninety-three athletes (27.8 ± 4.9 years) from different sports with unilateral (anterior, posterior, or multidirectional) shoulder instability were included. The clinical diagnosis of shoulder instability was made by history (pain, feeling of instability, or recurrent dislocations) and physical examination, and confirmed with radiographic and MRI studies by an experienced shoulder surgeon.

Participants were required to meet the following inclusion criteria: be aged between 18 and 45 years to ensure a homogeneous sample of adult athletes and to minimize potential confounding effects related to developmental or age-associated psychological differences; have a clinical diagnosis of unilateral traumatic shoulder instability; be at least six months post-surgical intervention; and have no cognitive impairments that would affect comprehension or self-reporting. All participants were required to have engaged in regular weekly sports activities prior to injury and to either plan to RTS or have already resumed sporting activities. Additionally, they needed to be fluent in Persian to accurately understand and complete the study questionnaires, and willing to provide detailed information about their injury and recovery process. Exclusion criteria included bilateral shoulder instability, inherent multidirectional instability, coexisting conditions such as rotator cuff tears or bone fractures, and the inability to complete the forms.

Participants were enrolled progressively between March 1, 2025, and May 17, 2025, through in-person visits and online advertising at sports clubs and physiotherapy clinics in Shahrood, Ahvaz, and Tehran. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sport Sciences Research institute of Shahrood University of Technology (approval code: IR.SHAHROODUT.REC.1404.007) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki [24]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study.

Following recruitment, all participants attended an in-person evaluation session at designated clinics in the selected cities. During this session, they completed a demographic and clinical questionnaire collecting information including age, sex, type and level of sport participation, time since the last shoulder injury or surgery, and classification of shoulder instability according to the FEDS system (Forward/Posterior Instability, Epileptic Shoulder, Dislocation-prone Shoulder, and Self-induced/Subluxation).

All participants also completed the Persian version of the SI-RSI-Pr questionnaire, along with other validated PROMs, including the Western Ontario Shoulder Instability Index (WOSI), the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK), and the Work and Disability Scale (WDS). To assess test–retest reliability, the SI-RSI-Pr was administered a second time, 8.30 ± 1.05 days later, via a secure online platform. All participants followed the same sequence of administration—first in person, then online—to ensure consistency and reduce potential order effects. No therapeutic intervention or physical treatment was provided during this interval to ensure that the participants’ clinical condition remained unchanged.

Phase 1: Cross-cultural translation of SI-RSI questionnaire

The SI-RSI questionnaire was translated following the recommendations outlined in the Guidelines for the Process of Cross-Cultural Adaptation of Self-Report Measures [25] and the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) guidelines [26]. This sample size meets the minimum recommendation of 10–12 participants for pretesting in cross-cultural adaptation studies [25]. The adaptation process included the following steps: two independent forward translations were performed by native Persian speakers—a physiotherapist and a sports scientist—both fluent in English. A third bilingual translator synthesized these versions through consensus discussion facilitated by the project leader. The synthesized Persian version was then back-translated into English by two native English speakers who were blinded to the study’s purpose, and the results were merged into a single version. A multidisciplinary expert committee reviewed all translations to resolve discrepancies and ensure conceptual equivalence. The pre-final version was pilot-tested on 12 eligible participants (6 men, 6 women; mean age: 21.5 ± 1.6 years), with feedback collected via semi-structured interviews. Participants reported high clarity and relevance, and minor revisions were made accordingly. Finally, an expert committee approved the final SI-RSI-Pr for use in the psychometric evaluation phase of the study.

Phase 2: evaluation of the measurement properties of SI-RSI-Pr

Validity

To assess the structural validity of the SI-RSI-Pr, an Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed using a Promax rotation, as the factors are expected to be correlated, which is suitable for psychological constructs. Prior to analysis, the suitability of the data was evaluated using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Factors were extracted using principal axis factoring, followed by Promax rotation to account for potential correlations among factors. Factor retention was guided by eigenvalues greater than 1, visual inspection of the scree plot, and the conceptual interpretability of item loadings (Fig. 1). Construct validity was evaluated through examining correlations between SI-RSI-Pr scores and scores from established reference instruments: the WOSI [27], the TSK-17 [28], and the WDS [29] in accordance with the COSMIN guidelines [30]. Our a priori hypotheses for construct validity were based on theoretical expectations regarding the psychological and functional dimensions of RTS. We hypothesized strong correlations (|r| ≥ 0.50) between the SI-RSI-Pr and established PROMs assessing closely related constructs: A strong positive correlation with the Walch-Duplay Score (WDS), reflecting its focus on functional recovery and return to activity. strong negative correlations with the TSK-17 (kinesiophobia), WOSI Total (shoulder instability impact), WOSI-Sport (sports-specific limitations), and WOSI-Emotions (psychological distress), as higher levels of fear, disability, and emotional concerns are expected to be associated with lower readiness to RTS. We also hypothesized moderate correlations (|r| ≥ 0.30) with the WOSI-Symptoms and WOSI-Lifestyle subscales, as these domains relate more to physical symptoms and general quality of life, which may indirectly influence confidence and fear, but are less directly tied to the core psychological construct of return-to-sport readiness. These hypotheses were tested using Spearman correlation coefficients. Construct validity was rated as good, fair, or poor based on the proportion of confirmed hypotheses (≥ 75%, 50–74%, < 50%, respectively), in accordance with COSMIN guidelines [31].

Known-groups construct validity was evaluated by testing a priori hypotheses regarding expected differences in SI-RSI-Pr scores between clinically meaningful subgroups. Based on theoretical expectations, it was hypothesized that no significant difference would be observed between individuals who underwent surgical intervention and those managed conservatively, as both groups were at least six months post-treatment and in a comparable phase of recovery. Similarly, no significant difference was expected between participants with dislocation-induced versus subluxation-induced shoulder instability, given that both conditions result in functional limitations and psychological barriers to returning to sport. In contrast, a significant difference was anticipated between athletes who had returned to sport and those who had not, with the former expected to demonstrate higher SI-RSI-Pr scores, reflecting greater psychological readiness and confidence.

Reliability

Test-retest reliability evaluates whether the same results are obtained when a test is repeated in subjects whose health status has not changed [30]. To assess test-retest reliability, the SI-RSI-Pr questionnaire was administered twice with an 8.30 ± 1.05 days interval, and the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC, 2-way mixed-effects, absolute agreement) was used to measure reliability. Additionally, the standard error of measurement (SEM) was calculated as an index of measurement precision, and the minimal detectable change (MDC) was determined to identify the smallest amount of change that exceeds measurement error. Internal consistency

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha (α), with values between 0.70 and 0.95 considered acceptable per COSMIN guidelines, indicating adequate inter-item correlation without significant risk of redundancy among the items [30, 32]. A sensitivity analysis was also performed by recalculating alpha after removing each item to identify any that might compromise scale coherence, guiding potential refinements.

Feasibility and interpretability

Feasibility was evaluated by was assessed by examining the rate of missing responses, which reflects participant burden and ease of completion. A low percentage of missing data indicates good feasibility and acceptability of the SI-RSI-Pr in the target population. Interpretability was evaluated through floor and ceiling effects defined as the percentage of participants scoring the minimum (0) or maximum (10) on each item. A floor or ceiling effect exceeding 15% was considered indicative of limited scale range or potential issues with content validity, as it may suggest that the scale fails to capture variability at the extremes of the construct (e.g., very low or very high readiness to RTS) [33].

Self-report questionaries

Shoulder return to sport after injury scale (SI-RSI scale)

The SI-RSI scale is based on the ACL-RSI scale. The original version of the SI-RSI is a 12-item scale, and its short form contains 5 items. It assesses psychological readiness to RTS after shoulder instability by evaluating their emotions, confidence in performance, and perceived risk. Athletes rate each item on a 10-point Likert scale, where 0 means “not at all” and 10 means “very much.” To calculate the total score, the scores of all 12 items are summed. Then, the total score is converted into a percentage of the maximum possible score (which is 120) by dividing the sum by 120 and multiplying by 100. Higher scores indicate a more positive psychological response [27].

Western ontario shoulder instability index (WOSI)

The WOSI is a 21-item PROM designed to assess shoulder instability symptoms and function over the preceding week. It includes four domains: physical symptoms (10 items), sports/recreation/work function (4 items), lifestyle function (4 items), and emotions (3 items). Each item is scored on a Likert scale ranging from 0 to 100. The total score ranges from 0 to 2100, with higher scores indicating worse shoulder function [27, 34].

Tampa scale for kinesiophobia (TSK)

The TSK-17 is a self-report questionnaire designed to measure fear of movement or re-injury due to pain. The TSK typically consists of 17 items each rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). The total score ranges from 17 to 68, with higher scores indicating greater fear of movement or re-injury [28].

Walch-duplay score (WDS)

The WDS is a clinician-reported outcome measure widely used to assess shoulder function and stability in patients with recurrent shoulder instability. It evaluates four key domains: pain (0–30 points), stability (0–25 points), mobility (0–20 points), and return to activity (0–25 points), with a total score ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate better shoulder function, categorized as excellent (91–100), good (76–90), fair (51–75), or poor (≤ 50) [29].

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 29.0; IBM). Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, summarized the demographic characteristics and questionnaire results of the participants. Reliability was assessed through internal consistency and test-retest reliability. Internal consistency, measured by Cronbach’s alpha (α), was considered acceptable at 0.70, good at 0.80, and excellent at 0.90, with an assumed α of 0.80 based on previous studies [30, 32]. Relative reliability (test-retest reliability) was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), categorized as poor (< 0.50), moderate (0.51–0.75), good (0.76–0.90), or excellent (> 0.90) [35]. Construct validity was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rₛ) by examining the relationships between SI-RSI-Pr scores and the scores of reference scales, including the WDS, TSK-17, WOSI, and its subscales. Correlation strengths were classified as weak (rS ≤ 0.35), moderate (0.36 ≤ rS < 0.67), and strong (0.68 ≤ rS < 1.0) [36]. Known-groups validity was assessed by comparing the group of participants who had returned to play with the group who had not, using the Mann-Whitney test. Floor and ceiling effects were considered present if 15% of participants reported the minimum or maximum possible score. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Translation of the SI-RSI scale

The translation process of the SI-RSI Scale proceeded smoothly without encountering any linguistic challenges. Preliminary testing confirmed that patients had no difficulty comprehending the items, eliminating the need for any modifications to the text. All participants reported a clear understanding of the questions, ensuring the scale’s effectiveness in the target population.

Participants

In this study, out of 97 initially enrolled participants, 4 were excluded due to incomplete data, leaving 93 for analysis. The participants had a mean age of 27.8 years (SD = 5.0), with 58 men (62.4%). Internal consistency

The SI-RSI-Pr and its subscales demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.89 for the Total SI-RSI-Pr Score, 0.94 for ConfRS, 0.92 for FearRS, and 0.93 for BurdenRS, all aligning with COSMIN’s recommended range (α ≥ 0.70). these findings confirm that the SI-RSI-Pr and its subscales form a reliable and well-structured instrument for assessing psychological readiness to return to sport (Table 5).

Feasibility and interpretability

The SI-RSI-Pr demonstrated excellent feasibility and good interpretability. The rate of missing responses was only 4.1%, indicating high completeness, minimal participant burden, and strong acceptability of the questionnaire in the target population. Regarding interpretability, floor and ceiling effects were evaluated as the percentage of participants scoring the minimum (0) or maximum (10) on each item. The mean floor effect across all items was 9.86% (range: 5.1–14.2%), and the mean ceiling effect was 6.8% (range: 3.0–13.6%). Notably, no individual item exceeded the 15% threshold for either floor or ceiling effects. These findings suggest that the SI-RSI-Pr has an adequate response range, effectively captures variability in psychological readiness to return to sport, and is unlikely to suffer from significant content validity issues due to item extremity bias.

Discussion

The process of translating the SI-RSI into Persian followed standard cross-cultural adaptation procedures, ensuring that the Persian version maintained conceptual and semantic equivalence with the original scale. The translation process was executed in multiple phases, including forward and backward translations, expert reviews, and pilot testing. This rigorous methodology allowed for the development of a culturally appropriate tool that could be easily understood by the target population. The results of the pilot test confirmed that participants had no difficulty comprehending the items, which indicates that the SI-RSI-Pr is suitable for use with Iranian athletes recovering from shoulder instability.

Translation of the SI-RSI scale

The lack of significant linguistic issues during the translation process also supported the psychometric properties of the scale. These findings are consistent with other cultural adaptations of the SI-RSI scale, such as the Italian and Turkish versions, which similarly reported no major translation difficulties [15, 20]. Thus, the SI-RSI-Pr has been successfully adapted for the Persian-speaking population without compromising the accuracy and reliability of the original instrument.

Validity

EFA of the SI-RSI-Pr revealed a robust three-factor structure explaining 78% of the total variance, comprising ConfRS, FearRS, and BurdenRS, with strong factor loadings (0.744–0.955) and moderate inter-factor correlations (0.28–0.47). This multidimensional solution diverges from the unidimensional model reported in the Turkish validation (73.8% variance, KMO = 0.91) [20], but resonates more closely with the Italian version, where unidimensionality was questioned due to high RMSEA values (0.148 initially, 0.117 after adjustments) and a weak loading for Item 2 (λ = 0.251) [12]. These cross-cultural discrepancies underscore differences in athletes’ perceptions of psychological readiness, with the Iranian sample capturing distinct yet interconnected domains of confidence, fear, and emotional strain. Although some prior validations of the SI-RSI have utilized a total score, our findings suggest a multidimensional structure, which implies that summing all items may obscure distinct psychological dimensions. Following COSMIN recommendations, we advocate for the use of subscale scores (ConfRS, FearRS, BurdenRS) in both clinical practice and research to preserve construct validity. Nevertheless, further confirmatory factor analysis in larger samples is recommended to refine and validate the factorial model.

Our findings provide strong evidence for the construct validity of the SI-RSI-Pr as a psychological assessment tool in shoulder instability rehabilitation. As anticipated, the SI-RSI-Pr showed a strong positive correlation with the WDS (r = 0.74) and negative correlations with both the TSK-17 (r = −0.58) and the WOSI (r = −0.57). These associations indicate that athletes reporting greater psychological readiness to return to sport also demonstrate better shoulder function and reduced fear of re-injury, consistent with prior studies [5, 16]. Moreover, correlations between the SI-RSI-Pr subscales and the WOSI— ConfRS (r = 0.66) and FearRS (r = −0.39)—underscore the multidimensional nature of psychological readiness. Together, these results highlight the central role of fear of re-injury and anxiety in return-to-sport decisions, confirming both the scale’s construct validity and its clinical relevance in capturing key psychological aspects of rehabilitation [15].

The known-groups validity of the Persian version of the SI-RSI-Pr was confirmed by significant differences in scores between athletes who had RTS and those who had not. Returners scored notably higher on all subscales, with particularly pronounced differences in ConfRS (75.0 vs. 53.8) and BurdenRS (60.0 vs. 23.3), highlighting the scale’s ability to differentiate based on actual return status. These results align with findings from previous validations of the SI-RSI in English, Turkish, and French, reinforcing its cross-cultural consistency in capturing psychological readiness [14, 15, 20]. Notably, the absence of significant differences between surgically and conservatively treated athletes suggests that the mode of treatment does not substantially affect psychological readiness, indicating that both groups face similar psychosocial challenges during recovery, regardless of medical intervention.

ROC analysis further supported the clinical utility of the SI-RSI-Pr, demonstrating good overall discriminatory power (AUC = 0.765(. A total score above 61.67 provided high specificity (92.86%) with moderate sensitivity (54.05%), making it effective in identifying athletes at risk of not returning to sport. Among subscales, ConfRS showed the highest sensitivity (81.08%), ideal for detecting returners, while BurdenRS offered high specificity (82.14%), useful for flagging non-returners. Compared to the French version [14]—which emphasizes specificity—the Persian adaptation achieves a more balanced sensitivity-specificity profile, potentially due to cultural nuances, a homogeneous sample, and distinct athlete-clinician dynamics. The slightly higher cut-off score may reflect a more cautious approach to RTS decisions in the Iranian context. Overall, the SI-RSI-Pr is a reliable, culturally adapted tool that should be integrated into comprehensive, multidimensional return-to-sport evaluations.

Reliability

The present study demonstrated that the SI-RSI-Pr exhibits excellent test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.95), low measurement error, and appropriate MDC values, indicating its sensitivity in detecting clinically meaningful changes. Specifically, the MDC for the total score was calculated as 13.86, suggesting that changes exceeding this threshold likely reflect true improvements or declines in psychological readiness rather than measurement error. These findings are consistent with other validated language versions of the SI-RSI, including the original (ICC = 0.93), French (ICC = 0.97), Italian (ICC = 0.926), and Turkish (ICC = 0.95) [12, 14, 15, 20], reinforcing the scale’s cross-cultural validity and generalizability. This reliability and responsiveness make the SI-RSI-Pr a valuable tool for monitoring athlete progress throughout rehabilitation and supporting informed return-to-sport decisions. In the context of evidence-based practice, having a reliable and valid instrument like the SI-RSI-Pr is essential for providing accurate and meaningful assessments of psychological readiness across diverse athletic populations.

Internal consistency

The Persian version of the SI-RSI-Pr demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.89 for the total scale and ranging from 0.92 to 0.94 across its subscales (ConfRS, FearRS, and BurdenRS), all well above the acceptable threshold of α ≥ 0.70 recommended by COSMIN [23]. These results indicate strong homogeneity among items and confirm that the SI-RSI-Pr reliably measures a coherent construct of psychological readiness in Persian-speaking athletes. The high internal consistency aligns closely with findings from other validated language versions—such as the original (α = 0.96) [15], Turkish (α = 0.92) [20], Italian (α = 0.935) [12], and French (α = 0.83) [14]—supporting the cross-cultural robustness of the scale. Notably, previous studies have highlighted subtle cultural or linguistic influences on item performance, such as the Italian version where Item 2 showed low item-total correlation [12], suggesting that careful psychometric evaluation remains essential during translation and adaptation. In this study, no such issues were observed, indicating that the SI-RSI-Pr is both linguistically appropriate and psychometrically sound for use in Iranian athletic populations.

Feasibility and interpretability

The SI-RSI-Pr demonstrated strong feasibility, with a low item non-response rate (4.1%) and acceptable floor and ceiling effects (< 15%), confirming its ease of use and interpretability. This indicates the scale’s good usability, acceptability, and sensitivity across different stages of recovery. These results align closely with previous validations of the SI-RSI in other languages: the original English version reported no missing data or floor/ceiling effects [15], while both the English [16] and Turkish [20] versions showed similarly high feasibility. The French version observed low floor (4.5%) and moderate ceiling effects (10.2%), both within recommended ranges [14]. The Italian version demonstrated near-perfect feasibility, with minimal participants at score extremes [12]. Although the SI-RSI-Pr’s floor and ceiling effects were slightly higher than the Italian version’s, they remained within acceptable limits, affirming its practical utility. Collectively, these findings reinforce the SI-RSI-Pr as a feasible and user-friendly tool, consistent with the strong feasibility profile seen across the SI-RSI family globally.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the convenience sampling and mixed recruitment strategy may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. Moreover, the predominance of male and contact-sport participants could limit applicability to female athletes and those in non-contact disciplines. Future research should aim to validate the Persian SI-RSI in more diverse cohorts, including female and non-contact sport populations, to ensure broader generalizability. Third, the cross-sectional design precluded the assessment of changes in psychological readiness over time or its predictive value for actual return-to-sport outcomes. We did not include a pain intensity measure (e.g., visual analog scale) to examine its relationship with psychological readiness; future studies should integrate pain assessment to explore this interplay. Additionally, the cohort’s heterogeneity in treatment types and recovery timelines reduced the ability to stratify findings by recovery stage. Given the cross-sectional design, we could not assess responsiveness to clinical change. Therefore, longitudinal studies are warranted to examine the sensitivity of the Persian SI-RSI to changes over rehabilitation, as well as to determine key interpretability metrics such as the minimal detectable change (MDC) and minimal clinically important difference (MCID). The study’s findings are limited to physically active adults aged 18–45 and may not generalize to adolescents, older adults, or recreational athletes. Since psychological and physiological responses to sports injury can vary across age groups, future research should assess the Persian SI-RSI’s validity and reliability in a broader age range to ensure its wider applicability. Although EFA was used to assess the factor structure in the Persian context, future research should apply confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to validate the established one-factor model and evaluate its unidimensionality for clinical use.

Practical implications

The findings indicate that the Persian version of the SI-RSI (SI-RSI-Pr) is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing psychological readiness to return to sport among Iranian athletes following injury. Its three-dimensional structure— ConfRS, FearRS, and BurdenRS —captures central psychological barriers to return, particularly fear of reinjury. The instrument’s brevity, ease of administration, and minimal rate of missing responses support its applicability in both clinical and research contexts. Routine use of the SI-RSI-Pr may enable practitioners to systematically monitor psychological readiness alongside physical recovery and to identify athletes who may benefit from targeted psychological interventions.

Conclusion

This study successfully translated and validated the SI-RSI-Pr. The scale’s three-factor structure— ConfRS, FearRS, and BurdenRS —was confirmed, exhibiting robust psychometric properties, including excellent validity, reliability, internal consistency, and feasibility. The SI-RSI-Pr is a valuable clinical tool for assessing the psychological readiness of Persian-speaking athletes returning to sport after shoulder instability, supporting more comprehensive return-to-sport decision-making. We recommend that future users administer and interpret the SI-RSI-Pr at the subscale level to accurately reflect the multidimensional nature of psychological readiness in athletes recovering from shoulder instability. Future research should focus on establishing responsiveness, developing refined short-form versions, and exploring predictive validity through longitudinal designs.
