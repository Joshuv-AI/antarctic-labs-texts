Air pollution exposure and the burden of pediatric upper respiratory tract infections in emergency departments: a retrospective time-series study
Authors: Ewelina Wawryk-Gawda, Nadia Miga-Orczykowska, Joanna Matusiak, Dominika Mańdziuk, Kamila Giżewska, Violetta Opoka-Winiarska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUpper respiratory tract infections (URTI) are a major public health problem and a leading cause of pediatric Emergency Department (ED) visits. Approximately 81% of emergency outpatient visits in children are due to URTI, and respiratory diseases account for 12% of all deaths worldwide. Air pollution is considered an important factor contributing to the increased incidence of respiratory infections. The aim of this study was to examine the association between ambient air pollutant concentrations and the number of pediatric ED admissions for URTI. We conducted a retrospective analysis including 2,572 children admitted to the ED between 2015 and 2020 with a diagnosis of URTI. Daily concentrations of particulate matter (PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀), carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen oxides (NOx and NO2), ozone (O3), and sulfur dioxide (SO2) were obtained from a certified environmental monitoring station. Negative binomial regression models were used to estimate associations, adjusting for season, temperature, humidity, and influenza activity. The highest number of URTI-related visits was observed in winter 2019, coinciding with the peak levels of air pollutants. In univariate analyses, higher concentrations of PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, CO, and SO2 were significantly associated with increased URTI visits. After multivariable adjustment, only carbon monoxide (IRR = 1.72, p = 0.05) and sulfur dioxide remained independently associated with higher URTI incidence, while the associations for particulate matter and nitrogen dioxide were attenuated. Ozone showed a weak inverse association, likely reflecting seasonal confounding.Conclusion: Our findings indicate that air pollution is strongly associated with the frequency of pediatric URTI-related ED visits. While seasonality plays an important role, CO and SO2 appear to be key independent factors driving this association. These findings suggest that exposure to ambient CO and SO₂ may increase the risk of pediatric upper respiratory infections, highlighting the importance of air quality in the prevention of respiratory morbidity in children. Introduction

Upper respiratory tract infections (URTI) are among the most common reasons for Emergency Department (ED) visits in children and have become a significant public health concern. These infections, predominantly of viral origin, are influenced by multiple factors, including age, staying in large groups, anatomical and immunological predisposition, and exposure to both household and outdoor air pollution [1, 2].

Air pollutants comprise a complex mixture of chemical and biological substances released into the atmosphere, forming dynamic blends of gases and particulate matter. Their sources and composition vary considerably across regions and over time [2–6].

Exposure to particulate matter with diameters of 2.5 µm and 10 µm (PM₂.₅, PM₁₀), carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen oxides (NOₓ and NO₂), ozone (O₃), and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) is known to pose significant health risks [6]. The World Health Organization’s Global Air Quality Guidelines provide reference thresholds and recommended limits for these substances [7–9].

Children are particularly vulnerable to the effects of air pollution. They spend more time outdoors engaged in physical activities, which increases their exposure to higher doses of pollutants. Due to their shorter stature, children inhale air closer to the ground, where concentrations of many toxicants are greater. Physiological characteristics such as higher rates of mouth breathing, higher minute ventilation relative to body size, and less effective filtration in the nasal passages further facilitate pollutant penetration into the respiratory tract. In addition, children’s smaller airways, underdeveloped mucociliary clearance, and immature immune and detoxification systems amplify the potential for harm and may interfere with normal respiratory development [1, 5, 10, 11].

Although the relationship between air pollution and respiratory infections has been extensively studied in some regions, data specific to European pediatric populations remain limited [12–15]. While parents and healthcare professionals often focus on medications, vaccinations, nutrition, and supplementation to prevent infections, less attention is given to environmental exposures. In some households, children continue to be exposed to passive smoking, further compounding respiratory risks [14, 16].

The present study aimed to investigate the association between ambient air pollutant levels and the number of pediatric ED admissions due to upper respiratory tract infections. We hypothesized that higher concentrations of PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, CO, NOx, NO2, O3, and SO2 would be significantly associated with an increased frequency of ED visits for URTI, after adjusting for seasonal variation.

Methods

This retrospective observational study focused on key ambient air pollutants, including particulate matter (PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀), carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen oxides (NOx and NO2), ozone (O3), and sulphur dioxide (SO2). Data on pediatric Emergency Department (ED) admissions were extracted from the electronic medical records of the University Children’s Hospital in Lublin. Records included the date of admission, diagnosis codes according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), and basic demographic information. The dataset comprised 2,572 patients aged 0–18 years who were admitted to the ED between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2020 with a diagnosis of the common cold (J00) or acute upper respiratory tract infection of multiple and unspecified sites (J06). Cases classified as J06 also included subcategories J06.0 (acute laryngopharyngitis), J06.8 (other acute upper respiratory infections of multiple sites), and J06.9 (acute upper respiratory infection, unspecified). To ensure diagnostic accuracy and minimize potential underestimation of URTI cases, each record identified by ICD-10 codes J00 or J06 was subsequently reviewed by the investigator. The clinical notes from the visit were examined to verify that the reported symptoms and findings were consistent with an acute upper respiratory tract infection as defined by the assigned ICD-10 code. We excluded data of patients with only chronic upper respiratory conditions (chronic tonsillar and adenoid hypertrophy, allergic rhinitis) without acute infection; visits with missing or incomplete diagnostic information.

Daily concentrations of the selected air pollutants (24-h averages) were obtained from a certified ambient air monitoring station operated by the Chief Inspectorate for Environmental Protection, located on Obywatelska Street in Lublin (station code: PL507A). Although data were collected from a single monitoring site, this location was considered representative of urban background exposure for the city’s population. Lublin is the eighth largest city in Poland, with approximately 330,400 inhabitants. The region is characterized by a temperate climate, with average annual temperatures ranging between + 7.0 °C and + 8.0 °C. The warmest months are July and August (mean + 19 °C), while the coldest are January and February (mean − 5.0 °C). The province combines agricultural and industrial activities, with the food industry and vehicle manufacturing among the main sectors of the local economy.

Epidemiological data on influenza cases and suspected cases were obtained from weekly bulletins published by the National Institute of Public Health – National Institute of Hygiene (NIZP–PZH) in Poland. The data covered the period from 2015 to 2020 and included: weekly number of influenza patients, mean daily incidence rate per 100,000 inhabitants in two age group breakdowns, with focus on children aged 0–4 years and 5–14 years. Only data for the Lublin Voivodeship were included in the analysis to match the location of meteorological data and hospital catchment area.

Meteorological data (temperature and humidity) were sourced from the Institute of Meteorology and Water Management (IMGW-PIB). Daily measurements for the Lublin-Radawiec synoptic station (station ID: 495) were obtained for the same six-year period (2015–2020). The data included: daily average temperature (°C) and daily average relative humidity (%).

Data completeness was high across the study period. Pediatric URTI visit counts were complete for all 2,193 days and epidemiological data on influenza cases for 288 weeks. Among air pollutant variables, the proportion of missing data ranged from 0.18% (NO₂ and NOₓ) to 1.96% (PM₂.₅), while meteorological variables had < 0.1% missingness. After excluding days with any missing exposure or covariate data, the final analytic sample for multivariable models comprised 2,113 days (96.3%).

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, we used Stata BE 18 software (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA). Non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis tests was used analyzed differences in seasonal pollutant concentrations. To investigate the relationship between daily ambient air pollutant concentrations and the frequency of pediatric Emergency Department (ED) visits for upper respiratory tract infections (URTI), we used negative binomial regression (NBR) models. This approach was chosen to appropriately account for the count nature of the outcome variable (number of daily URTI visits) and the presence of overdispersion observed in preliminary Poisson models. We first conducted univariate NBR models to examine the independent associations of individual pollutants (PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, CO, NO₂, O₃, SO₂) with URTI incidence. Subsequently, multivariable NBR models were constructed, adjusting for the following potential confounders: seasonality (categorical variable: spring, summer, autumn, winter), daily mean temperature (°C), daily mean relative humidity (%), influenza activity, measured as age-specific weekly incidence rates of influenza-like illness (ILI) among children aged 0–4 and 5–14 years in the Lublin Voivodeship. To address multicollinearity, Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) were calculated for all predictor variables. High correlation was identified between PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀, as well as between NO₂ and NOₓ. As a result, these collinear variables were not included in the same model. Effect estimates from the NBR models are presented as regression coefficients (β) and incidence rate ratios (IRRs), along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-values. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Model diagnostics (e.g., log-likelihood, likelihood ratio tests, pseudo R2) are reported to assess model fit. We used a stratified visual analysis by dividing pollutant concentrations into tertiles to assess URTI visit distributions under low, medium, and high exposure conditions. For each of the six pollutants, PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), ozone (O₃), and sulfur dioxide (SO₂), daily values were divided into three equal-sized groups (tertiles) based on their distribution across the entire study period (2015–2020). We then constructed boxplots showing the distribution of daily URTI-related Emergency Department (ED) visits across these tertile groups. This approach allowed for a comparative visualization of the variability and central tendency of case numbers under different levels of pollutant exposure. Days with missing pollutant or URTI data were excluded from the respective analysis. The plots were generated using the seaborn.catplot function in Python (version 3.12.6), with boxplot parameters displaying median, interquartile ranges (IQR), and potential outliers.

To assess the robustness of the findings, several sensitivity analyses were performed. First, we re-estimated the main negative binomial models after excluding influenza-peak weeks, defined as calendar weeks in which the weekly influenza/ILI incidence among children aged 0–15 years exceeded the 90th percentile during the study period. Second, we repeated the analyses after excluding the COVID-19 pandemic period (from March 1, 2020) to minimize potential distortions in healthcare-seeking behavior. In addition, age-stratified analyses were conducted to examine potential effect modification by age. For this purpose, daily Emergency Department (ED) visits were aggregated into three age categories: 0–4 years, 5–9 years, and 10–18 years. Separate negative binomial regression models were then fitted for each age group using the same covariate structure as in the primary models (meteorological variables, day of the week, month, and year). The results are reported as incidence rate ratios (IRR) per interquartile range (IQR) increase in pollutant concentration with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI), (Table 4S and 5S in Supplementary material).

Institutional approval for the use of anonymized patient data was obtained from the hospital director. According to national regulations, separate bioethics committee approval was not required for this retrospective analysis of routinely collected administrative data (decision of Bioethics Committee at the Medical University of Lublin No BKB/10/01/2025).

Associations between pollutants and URTI incidence

Across the study period (2015–2020), the mean concentrations of particulate matter exceeded World Health Organization (WHO) recommended limits, with average PM₂.₅ of 23.3 µg/m3 and PM₁₀ of 30.4 µg/m3 (Supplementary Table 1S). Seasonal variation was observed, with higher concentrations of PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, CO, and SO₂ during winter months, while ozone (O₃) levels peaked in summer. The magnitude of pollution varied between years, with peak winter PM₂.₅ levels reaching 40 µg/m3 in 2017, compared to 26.6 µg/m3 in 2020 (Fig. 3.).

Spearman rank correlations confirmed weak positive associations for CO (r = 0.13) and PM₂.₅ (r = 0.10) with URTI visits, whereas O₃ showed a weak inverse correlation (r = –0.12).

Negative binomial regression models estimated for each pollutant individually revealed statistically significant associations between daily concentrations of PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, CO, O₃, and SO₂ and the number of pediatric URTI visits (Supplementary Table 2S). In a multivariable model adjusting for meteorological conditions and seasonality, only PM₂.₅ and CO remained significant predictors of URTI incidence (Supplementary Table 3S).

Full regression coefficients, 95% confidence intervals, p-values, and IRRs for all models are reported in the main Results section and Supplementary Tables 2S and 3S.

To investigate potential multicollinearity among environmental variables, Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) were calculated for all included air pollutants and meteorological covariates. High collinearity was found particularly between PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀, as well as between NO₂ and NOₓ, with VIF values exceeding commonly accepted thresholds (VIF > 10). To further assess shared variance, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted. The first two components explained approximately 70% of the total variance, confirming a substantial common structure among the pollutants. Based on these findings, we implemented a modelling strategy using single-pollutant models and avoided the simultaneous inclusion of highly collinear variables, such as PM₂.₅ with PM₁₀, and NO₂ with NOₓ, to enhance model stability and interpretability.

Multivariable negative binomial models were recalculated with a revised strategy to avoid collinearity by not including highly correlated pollutants (PM₂.₅ with PM₁₀; NO₂ with NOₓ) simultaneously. These analyses confirmed that CO and PM₂.₅ remained statistically significant predictors of URTI-related ED visits, while NO₂, NOₓ, and O₃ showed no consistent association. SO₂ reached statistical significance only in models excluding NO₂, suggesting that collinearity may have masked its effect in previous models. PM₁₀ also demonstrated a significant negative association with ED visits when PM₂.₅ was excluded. These findings reinforce the robustness of the associations while highlighting the importance of careful model specification in the presence of correlated environmental variables.

Monthly averaged data demonstrated clear seasonal patterns for both pollutant concentrations and pediatric URTI visits (Fig. 3). Peaks in PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, CO, and SO₂ levels coincided with winter months, during which ED visits for URTI were most frequent. In contrast, ozone (O₃) concentrations were higher during summer months, when respiratory infections were less prevalent. Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) displayed more variable patterns, without a consistent monotonic association with URTI visits. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis confirmed statistically significant positive associations between daily concentrations of PM₂.₅ (ρ = 0.10, p < 0.001), CO (ρ = 0.13, p < 0.001), SO₂ (ρ = 0.11, p < 0.001), and PM₁₀ (ρ = 0.08, p = 0.002) and the number of pediatric ED visits for URTI. O₃ showed a weak inverse correlation (ρ = − 0.12, p < 0.001), while NO₂ did not reach statistical significance (ρ = 0.04, p = 0.072).

Discussion

This study demonstrated a significant association between exposure to ambient air pollutants and pediatric Emergency Department (ED) visits for upper respiratory tract infections (URTI). In single-pollutant negative binomial models, we observed positive correlations between daily concentrations of PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, CO, SO₂, and URTI incidence. O₃ displayed an inverse association, while NO₂ showed no consistent pattern. In multivariable models adjusting for meteorological variables and seasonal trends, carbon monoxide (CO) and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) emerged as independent and statistically significant predictors of pediatric URTI visits. Notably, while PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ were associated with URTI visits in univariable models, these associations became attenuated or reversed after adjusting for temperature and seasonal confounders, suggesting that their apparent effects may be partially mediated by meteorological conditions or co-pollutants.

Our findings are partly consistent with previous studies conducted worldwide by different research groups operating in regions with varying levels of air pollution. Kanellopoulos et al. reported that PM₂.₅ levels > 25 µg/m3 in Volos, Greece, were associated with increased URTI-related ED visits in children, particularly during winter and spring [17]. While we observed a similar seasonal trend (higher pollution and URTI incidence in winter), we did not find a persistent association between PM₂.₅ and URTI in multivariable models.

In contrast to our findings, the ESCAPE project [18] and Rodríguez-Fernández et al. [11] reported significant associations between NO₂ and PM₁₀ and respiratory morbidity in children across Europe. The case–control study from Antwerp [19] observed strong associations between 31-day exposure to PM₁₀ and NO₂ and hospitalizations for bronchiolitis in infants under two years of age. This discrepancy may reflect differences in outcome definition (hospitalization vs. ED visits) and study period. Our findings are consistent with the meta-analysis by Ziou et al. [12], which confirmed that particulate matter exposure increases URTI risk in children but also highlighted considerable heterogeneity in effect sizes. As emphasized by Li et al. [6], PM₂.₅ composition rather than total mass may better explain respiratory health effects [20]. The absence of an independent association between PM₂.₅ and URTI in our study supports this view and underscores the need for more detailed pollutant characterization in European pediatric populations.

The findings of several Chinese studies are partially consistent with ours. These studies reported positive associations between PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, NO₂, SO₂, and pediatric URTI visits, while we confirmed the role of SO₂ and CO, but not PM or NO₂, after multivariable adjustment. The stronger associations with particulate matter in Chinese studies may reflect higher baseline pollution levels, differences in pollution source profiles, or urban density [5, 21–24]. Furthermore, seasonal patterns observed in China, stronger associations in winter, mirror our findings [25, 26]. The stronger winter signal likely results from increased heating, higher combustion emissions, and greater atmospheric inversion events, all contributing to pollutant accumulation. In India, numerous studies showed marked increases in pediatric respiratory morbidity during biomass burning and smog episodes, often with extremely elevated PM₂.₅ concentrations [27, 27]. Our findings are more moderate, likely due to lower pollution levels and differences in fuel types (e.g., coal vs. biomass), reinforcing the notion that pollutant composition and source type shape observed health effects [28–30]. The inverse association observed between ozone (O₃) and URTI incidence in unadjusted models was eliminated after controlling for meteorological and seasonal variables, consistent with previous reports attributing this pattern to the confounding effect of seasonality [4, 24]. Our study fills an important gap in European pediatric epidemiology by providing region-specific evidence on the respiratory effects of air pollution at relatively low exposure levels.

The observed association between carbon monoxide (CO) and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and increased pediatric URTI incidence is biologically plausible and supported by existing mechanistic studies. Both pollutants are recognized markers of incomplete combustion, typically arising from sources such as residential heating, vehicular emissions, and industrial activity [15, 31, 32]. Experimental data suggest that these substances can disrupt the integrity of the respiratory epithelial barrier, facilitating the translocation of pathogens from the airway lumen into deeper tissue compartments [2, 16, 33]. Inhalation of CO and SO₂ has also been shown to impair host immune defenses by reducing the phagocytic capacity of macrophages and altering the production of key cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) [3, 34]. Furthermore, these pollutants promote oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the airway epithelium, which compromise mucociliary clearance and increase susceptibility to both viral and bacterial respiratory infections [16, 35]. Although particulate matter (PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀) is similarly implicated in airway inflammation and immune disruption, the associations observed in our study were not robust in adjusted models. This may reflect mediation by meteorological variables or confounding from co-occurring combustion-related gases such as CO and SO₂. Our findings therefore support prior conclusions that the total mass concentration of particulate matter may be an insufficient proxy for biologically relevant exposure [6].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Exposure was estimated using data from a single ambient air monitoring station in Lublin. Although located near the pediatric emergency department, this does not capture intra-urban variation and may result in non-differential exposure misclassification, potentially underestimating true associations. Moreover, mean daily pollutant levels were used as proxies for individual exposure, without accounting for personal activity patterns or indoor environments. Diagnostic data were derived from ICD-10 codes J00–J06 recorded in an emergency setting, encompassing a broad range of URTIs. While this reflects real-world clinical practice, the heterogeneity of conditions may attenuate specific pollutant-disease associations. Additionally, the study was conducted in one hospital, which may limit generalizability to other regions. Finally, missing data were minimal and randomly distributed across time, suggesting non-differential bias. Although unlikely to distort associations, this remains a limitation of the exposure assessment.

Conclusion

Our findings confirm that exposure to certain ambient air pollutants, most notably carbon monoxide (CO) and sulfur dioxide (SO₂), is independently associated with increased pediatric emergency visits for upper respiratory tract infections (URTI). This relationship remained significant after adjusting for seasonality, weather, and influenza activity. Although PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ showed weak or inconsistent associations in multivariable models, CO and SO₂ emerged as the most robust predictors. Ozone (O₃) was inversely correlated with URTI incidence, likely reflecting seasonal patterns, while nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) showed no significant effect. Our findings align with European and global literature, supporting calls for stronger air quality interventions to protect children’s respiratory health. It is also important to raise awareness among parents about the harmful effects of pollutants on their children.

These results underscore the health impact of air pollution on children and support public health measures aimed at reducing exposure. Raising awareness among caregivers and clinicians about environmental triggers of respiratory illness remains essential.
