Comparison of quantitative light-induced fluorescence and near-infrared intraoral scanner imaging for proximal caries across predefined ICCMS radiographic thresholds: a diagnostic accuracy study
Authors: Ahmad Bittar, Elif Alkan, Özlem Kanar, Bora Korkut, Dilek Tağtekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProximal caries detection remains challenging because early lesions are often inaccessible to visual inspection and bitewing radiography exposes patients to ionizing radiation. Quantitative light-induced fluorescence (QLF) and near-infrared imaging integrated into intraoral scanners are proposed as non-ionizing adjuncts. This study compared the diagnostic performance of a handheld QLF device (QrayPen C, Aiobio) and an intraoral scanner with near-infrared imaging (NIRI) (iTero Element 5D, Align Technology) for proximal caries across clinically relevant ICCMS radiographic thresholds.MethodsIn this single-center comparative diagnostic accuracy study, 66 adults contributed 297 posterior proximal sites. Each site was assessed using QrayPen (QS score, 0–3) and the iTero Element 5D near-infrared imaging system (NIRI score, 0–3). Bitewing radiographs were scored according to the International Caries Classification and Management System (ICCMS) and served as the reference standard. Diagnostic accuracy was evaluated at three pre-specified thresholds: any caries (R0 vs. R1–R6), dentin involvement (R0–R2 vs. R3–R6), and deeper dentin involvement (R0–R3 vs. R4–R6). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and predictive values were calculated. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsFor any caries, AUCs were similar for QS and NIRI (0.729 vs. 0.727; p = 0.902). At the pre-specified cut-off (score ≥ 1), QS showed higher specificity than NIRI (88.1% vs. 78.2%), while sensitivities were comparable (54.7% vs. 61.1%). For dentin involvement (cut-off ≥ 2), AUC was 0.909 for QS and 0.896 for NIRI (p = 0.605); both methods showed sensitivity of 81.8%, with specificity of 95.5% for QS and 93.9% for NIRI. For radiographically deeper dentin involvement (cut-off = 3), NIRI demonstrated a slightly higher AUC than QS (0.978 vs. 0.961; p = 0.036); sensitivity/specificity were 72.2%/96.4% for QS and 83.3%/97.1% for NIRI. QS and NIRI scores were strongly correlated (Spearman ρ = 0.735; p < 0.001) with substantial ordinal agreement (linear-weighted κ = 0.707).ConclusionsBoth QS (QrayPen C) and NIRI (iTero Element 5D) demonstrated moderate accuracy for identifying any radiographic proximal caries and higher accuracy for radiographically deeper lesions. NIRI showed a small but statistically significant advantage for the radiographically deeper dentin involvement threshold. These non-ionizing methods may be useful adjuncts for proximal caries assessment, but do not eliminate the need for bitewing radiography when radiographic staging information is clinically required. Bitewing radiography under-detects enamel lesions, and site-level analyses did not model within-participant clustering; therefore, estimates should be interpreted as exploratory.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08559-1.

Background

Dental caries is a biofilm-mediated, diet-modulated disease driven by ecological shifts in the oral microbiome, and its early identification is central to contemporary preventive and minimally invasive care [1]. Clinical management has progressively shifted from purely operative strategies toward risk-based, lesion-oriented decision making, where detecting non-cavitated lesions and monitoring progression are as important as identifying cavitation [2–4].

Visual inspection remains the most common clinical approach, yet proximal surfaces are intrinsically difficult to assess because of limited access and a high likelihood that early lesions are hidden from direct view. Evidence syntheses show that visual methods have variable accuracy and tend to miss early lesions, especially interproximal ones [5]. Bitewing radiography therefore remains a key adjunct, but radiographs have imperfect sensitivity for enamel lesions and require exposure to ionizing radiation [6, 7].

To enable standardized detection, staging, and management decisions, structured scoring systems such as the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) and the International Caries Classification and Management System (ICCMS) have been developed and widely adopted in research and clinical education [8–10]. In vivo, ICCMS-based bitewing staging provides an operational reference for radiographic lesion extent that can be linked to management thresholds, although it still represents an imperfect reference standard for very early demineralization [9, 10].

Optical methods have been proposed to complement visual and radiographic assessment. Quantitative light-induced fluorescence (QLF) estimates mineral loss by measuring fluorescence changes in dental hard tissues and has been investigated for caries detection and monitoring [11, 12]. A Cochrane review concluded that fluorescence devices can provide clinically useful information, but accuracy varies by device, surface, and lesion stage and should be interpreted as adjunctive rather than definitive [13]. Recent studies evaluating contemporary QLF systems report moderate-to-high diagnostic performance under controlled acquisition conditions [14, 15].

Near-infrared imaging is another non-ionizing approach that exploits optical differences between sound and demineralized tissues. Near-infrared transillumination has demonstrated promising performance for proximal caries detection in vivo [16–18], and a systematic review and meta-analysis reported that near-infrared light transillumination can be comparable to bitewing radiography for interproximal caries detection in permanent teeth [17]. More recently, intraoral scanners integrating near-infrared imaging have been validated clinically for interproximal caries detection [19].

Despite growing interest in digital, non-ionizing caries detection, direct head-to-head comparisons between handheld QLF devices and near-infrared imaging integrated into intraoral scanners remain limited, particularly when performance is assessed across clinically meaningful ICCMS radiographic thresholds. Therefore, we compared QrayPen C QLF scoring (QS) and iTero Element 5D near-infrared imaging (NIRI) for proximal caries detection using ICCMS bitewing radiographic categories as the reference, and evaluated diagnostic performance for any caries, dentin involvement, and radiographically deeper dentin involvement thresholds.

Study hypotheses: We tested the null hypothesis that QS (QrayPen C) and NIRI (iTero Element 5D) have equal diagnostic discrimination (AUC) for each predefined ICCMS radiographic threshold (any caries, dentin involvement, and radiographically deeper dentin involvement). We additionally hypothesized that QS and NIRI ordinal scores would show a positive monotonic association and substantial ordinal agreement.

Methods

Study design and reporting

This was a single-center, cross-sectional, comparative diagnostic accuracy study with a paired design, because the same proximal site was assessed by both index tests. The manuscript was prepared in accordance with the STARD 2015 reporting guideline [20].

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at the Department of Restorative Dentistry, Faculty of Dentistry, Marmara University (Istanbul, Turkey). Adult patients attending the clinic were consecutively screened (Figs. 1 and 2).

Participants were recruited between 4 July 2025 and 9 January 2026.

Participants were eligible if they were 18–65 years old, classified as ASA I–II, had completed periodontal treatment with good oral hygiene, and had at least one posterior tooth with suspected proximal caries on bitewing radiography scored between ICCMS R1 and R5. Exclusion criteria were pregnancy, periapical pathology on radiographs, active orthodontic appliances with metal components, enamel hypoplasia, and severe tooth wear or parafunctional habits likely to interfere with optical assessment.

One quadrant per participant was assessed. The quadrant containing the first eligible radiographic proximal lesion identified during screening was selected; all eligible posterior proximal sites within that quadrant were included. The unit of analysis was the proximal site (surface).

All index tests (QS and NIRI) and bitewing radiographs were obtained during the same visit for included sites, with no intervening treatment.

Image quality was assessed immediately during acquisition. If an image was judged poor quality or non-interpretable, acquisition was repeated whenever possible during the same appointment. When re-acquisition required a recall visit and the participant did not return, the affected site(s) were excluded from analysis.

Index tests

Before QrayPen C and iTero Element 5D NIRI imaging, plaque and debris were removed by toothbrushing and dental floss, and the surfaces were dried with compressed air. QrayPen C images were acquired in a darkened room to minimize ambient-light interference. The imaging axis was kept as perpendicular as possible to the occlusal plane, with cheek/lip/tongue retraction and use of a mouth mirror when required. For NIRI acquisition, surfaces were kept clean and dry and the manufacturer’s scanning illumination was used; image quality was considered potentially affected by moisture, plaque, access, and angulation. Images judged poor quality or non-interpretable were repeated when possible; otherwise the site was excluded.

Index test 1: near-infrared imaging

Proximal sites were scanned using an iTero Element 5D intraoral scanner (Align Technology, San Jose, CA, USA) with the near-infrared imaging (NIRI) feature enabled. NIRI is a near-infrared spectroscopy–based modality (~ 850 nm) in which the relative translucency of dental tissues is displayed as grayscale brightness: sound enamel appears darker, whereas dentin and carious changes scatter/reflect light and appear brighter/opaque [19, 21]. NIRI images were reviewed in the iTero software interface; de-identified scans were archived within the iTero system (myitero.com) for subsequent blinded scoring. Two calibrated examiners independently assigned an ordinal NIRI score (0–3) for each proximal site using the NIRI images. Scores were prespecified to reflect increasing apparent lesion extent on NIRI images (surrogate categories) as follows: 0, sound (no bright region); 1, enamel changes; 2, lesion appearance reaching the dentino-enamel junction (DEJ) region; and 3, dentin-level category (surrogate) (Fig. 3). Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Index test 2: Quantitative light-induced fluorescence

After plaque removal (toothbrushing and dental floss) and air-drying, QLF images were acquired using QrayPen C (AIOBIO, Seoul, Republic of Korea; software version 1.45) in a darkened room. Images were taken with the optical axis as perpendicular as possible to the occlusal plane using a mouth mirror when required. For each proximal site, a QS score (0–3; higher scores indicating greater lesion severity) was independently assigned by two calibrated examiners based on the captured QrayPen images, according to the QS scoring criteria (Fig. 4). Disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Reference standard

Standardized bitewing radiographs were obtained at the Marmara University Faculty of Dentistry Restorative Dentistry Clinic using an intraoral X‑ray unit (Dürr Dental, Germany) and size 2 films (Dürr Dental, Germany) at 70 kV and 8 mA with an exposure time of 0.12 s, using standardized holders. Films were processed using an automatic X‑ray film developer (Dürr Dental, Germany) and the radiographs were inspected using an X‑ray viewer (Dürr Dental, Germany). Radiographs were scored according to the ICCMS/ICDAS radiographic scoring system (0, RA1–RA3, RB4, RC5–RC6), which served as the reference standard for radiographic lesion extent classification [9, 10, 22]. For consistency within this manuscript, these categories are denoted as R0–R6 (R0 = 0; R1 = RA1; R2 = RA2; R3 = RA3; R4 = RB4; R5 = RC5; R6 = RC6). For analysis, ICCMS scores were collapsed into four categories: sound (R0), enamel radiolucency (R1–R2; RA1–RA2), outer-third dentin radiolucency (R3; RA3), and radiographically deeper dentin involvement (R4–R6; RB4–RC6).

Blinding and reading protocol

Radiographs and index tests were scored by different examiners who were mutually blinded to each other’s results. Because eligibility screening required the presence of at least one radiographically suspicious proximal surface, complete masking of radiographic information at the screening stage was not feasible. However, QrayPen C and iTero Element 5D NIRI images were coded and scored by examiners blinded to the ICCMS bitewing scores used for analysis. Bitewing radiographs were scored independently by two examiners blinded to index test results; disagreements were resolved by consensus and a third examiner arbitrated unresolved discrepancies.

Before formal scoring, the examiners underwent calibration and training using representative example images (atlas) and a pilot set of proximal sites not included in the final analysis to standardize scoring criteria for ICCMS, QS, and NIRI.

Inter- and intra-examiner agreement for ICCMS bitewing scoring, QS scoring, and NIRI scoring was assessed using weighted Cohen’s kappa calculated on the initial independent readings (before consensus). For intra-examiner repeatability, a random sample of 150 proximal sites (approximately half of the dataset) was rescored after a 4-week interval by the same examiner(s) who performed the initial scoring, and intra-examiner kappa was calculated between the two scoring sessions. Consensus scores were used for the primary diagnostic accuracy analyses.

Outcomes and predefined radiographic thresholds

The primary endpoint was the diagnostic accuracy of QS and NIRI scores against ICCMS bitewing radiographic categories for detecting radiographic proximal caries. Three clinically meaningful binary thresholds were predefined using ICCMS radiographic categories as the reference: (1) any caries (R0 vs. R1–R6), (2) dentin involvement (R0–R2 vs. R3–R6), and (3) radiographically deeper dentin involvement (R0–R3 vs. R4–R6). Index-test positivity cut-offs (≥ 1, ≥ 2, and = 3) were prespecified based on the ordinal score definitions; no data-driven threshold optimization was performed.

Statistical analysis

Diagnostic performance of QS and NIRI scores for each ICCMS threshold was quantified using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals estimated using the DeLong method [23]. Correlated AUCs (paired ROC curves) were compared using the DeLong test [23]. For the prespecified cut-offs (score ≥ 1 for any caries; score ≥ 2 for dentin involvement; score = 3 for radiographically deeper dentin involvement), sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy were calculated with 95% confidence intervals. Paired comparisons of sensitivity and specificity between index tests were performed using the McNemar test (exact) [24]. Association between ordinal scores (QS and NIRI) and ICCMS categories was evaluated using Spearman rank correlation, and agreement between QS and NIRI was assessed using Cohen’s kappa with linear and quadratic weights [25]. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. No imputation was performed; analyses were conducted on complete cases. ROC curves were plotted for visual comparison of QS and NIRI performance at each prespecified ICCMS threshold.

Primary analyses were conducted at the site (proximal surface) level. Because multiple proximal sites could originate from the same participant, precision estimates may be optimistic if within-participant correlation is present; this should be considered when interpreting confidence intervals and p values. Future studies should incorporate hierarchical or cluster-robust models when participant identifiers allow.

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A post hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) to determine the statistical power of the study [26]. Since the primary outcome measure was the area under the ROC curve (AUC), the analysis was based on testing the null hypothesis that AUC = 0.5.

Assuming a large effect size (Cohen’s d ≈ 1.5–2.0), derived from the observed difference between the AUC values (0.73–0.97) and the null value of 0.5, with an alpha level of 0.05 and a total sample size of approximately 297 surfaces, the calculated statistical power exceeded 0.99.

These findings indicate that the study had sufficient power to detect statistically significant differences in diagnostic performance.

Inter-examiner agreement was high for ICCMS bitewing scoring (weighted κ = 0.91), QrayPen QS scoring (weighted κ = 0.92), and iTero Element 5D NIRI scoring on the 0–3 scale (weighted κ = 0.88). Intra-examiner agreement was assessed on a randomly selected sample of 150 proximal sites rescored after 4 weeks and was high for ICCMS staging (weighted κ = 0.93), QrayPen QS scoring (weighted κ = 0.94), and NIRI scoring (weighted κ = 0.95).

Eighty-two participants were assessed for eligibility and 66 were enrolled; 16 were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The mean age was 28.1 years (range 18–42), and 37 (56%) participants were female; all participants met the systemic health (ASA I–II) and good oral hygiene eligibility criteria (Table 1). A total of 310 proximal sites were initially captured. Of these, 21 sites had poor-quality or non-interpretable images and were recalled for re-acquisition; 8 sites were successfully re-acquired and 13 were excluded due to non-return, leaving 297 proximal sites for analysis. According to ICCMS bitewing scoring, 202/297 (68.0%) sites were sound (R0), 62/297 (20.9%) were enamel caries (R1–R2), 15/297 (5.1%) showed outer dentin involvement (R3), and 18/297 (6.1%) showed radiographically deeper dentin involvement (R4–R6) (Table 2).

Discussion

In this paired diagnostic accuracy study of 297 posterior proximal sites staged by ICCMS bitewing radiography, both QS and NIRI scores demonstrated moderate discrimination for detecting any radiographic caries and higher discrimination for thresholds indicating dentin involvement. While overall discrimination was similar for the “any caries” and “dentin involvement” thresholds, NIRI showed a small but statistically significant AUC advantage for the radiographically deeper dentin involvement threshold. Because ICCMS bitewing categories represent radiographic lesion extent (radiolucency) rather than histologic penetration, terms such as “radiographically deeper” are used here as surrogates rather than true histologic penetration. Accordingly, we failed to reject the null hypothesis of equal AUCs for the “any caries” and “dentin involvement” thresholds, but rejected it for the radiographically deeper dentin involvement threshold (DeLong p = 0.036).

The modest AUCs for detecting any radiographic caries (≈ 0.73 for both methods) are consistent with the general challenge of identifying early proximal enamel lesions without cavitation. Visual inspection and radiographic assessment both show stage-dependent performance and tend to miss early lesions [5–7]. Because bitewing radiography is less sensitive for very early demineralization, using ICCMS bitewing scores as the reference standard may also misclassify some early lesions, which can attenuate apparent accuracy of optical methods when “any caries” is defined as R1–R6 [6, 7, 9, 10].

For QLF-based assessment, our findings are aligned with the broader evidence that fluorescence methods can support caries detection but show variability by setting, surface, and lesion severity. A Cochrane review concluded that fluorescence devices may be useful adjuncts, although diagnostic performance is heterogeneous and should not be interpreted in isolation [13]. Recent evaluations of QLF systems report moderate-to-high performance under controlled conditions [14, 15]. In our data, QS favored specificity over sensitivity for the “any caries” threshold, which is consistent with an approach that prioritizes fewer false positives when optical changes are subtle and confounded by plaque remnants, stains, dehydration variability, or surface irregularities [13–15].

Near-infrared imaging has a growing evidence base for proximal caries assessment. Clinical studies and a meta-analysis indicate that near-infrared light transillumination can be comparable to bitewing radiography for detecting interproximal lesions in permanent dentition [16–18]. Importantly, intraoral scanners with near-infrared imaging features have been validated in vivo [19]. In a multicenter prospective study conducted in private practices, NIRI acquired with an iTero Element 5D was compared with bitewing radiography and showed higher sensitivity for early enamel lesions and comparable performance for lesions reaching the DEJ [27]. In our study, NIRI had slightly lower specificity than QS for the “any caries” threshold, suggesting a higher false-positive tendency at the lowest cut-off. This could reflect the high optical sensitivity of near-infrared modalities to changes at the enamel surface or DEJ, which may not always correspond to radiographically visible changes, and may also be influenced by image acquisition conditions or interpretation thresholds [16–19, 27].

The radiographically deeper dentin involvement threshold (R4–R6; RB4–RC6), which corresponds to radiolucency reaching the middle third of dentin or deeper, is clinically relevant because such lesions are more often associated with cavitation and heavily infected dentin than enamel-limited changes [3, 4, 9, 10, 22]. For this threshold, NIRI exhibited a small AUC advantage over QS. One plausible explanation is that near-infrared signal contrast increases with lesion severity and extent, while QLF-derived ordinal scoring may saturate for more advanced lesions depending on the field of view and lesion geometry. However, the number of radiographically deeper lesions was limited (n = 18), so estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Agreement and correlation analyses indicated that QS and NIRI scores captured broadly similar ordinal information (Spearman ρ = 0.735; linear-weighted κ = 0.707). This supports the concept that both devices can provide convergent non-ionizing information on proximal lesion severity, although they are based on different optical principles (fluorescence loss/red fluorescence versus near-infrared reflectance).

This study has limitations. First, bitewing radiography is an imperfect reference standard particularly for enamel lesions, many of which may not be radiographically visible [22] and histologic validation is not feasible in vivo. Second, ICCMS categories were collapsed and NIRI scoring was performed on a simplified 0–3 ordinal scale to facilitate direct comparison with QS, which reduces granularity and may influence estimates. Third, the iTero Element 5D user manual notes that NIRI cannot detect progression of caries beyond the dentino-enamel junction (DEJ) [21]; therefore, analyses involving dentin-category scores should be interpreted as associations with radiographic staging rather than validated determination of tissue penetration. In addition, some high NIRI score sites that underwent operative intervention during routine care were observed to have dentin involvement on cavity opening; however, operative validation was not part of the study protocol, was not recorded systematically, and is subject to selection bias, so it cannot be used as an independent reference standard for histologic penetration. Fourth, the study was performed at a single center and did not evaluate generalizability across operators, devices, or clinical workflows. Fifth, three diagnostic thresholds were analyzed without adjustment for multiple comparisons; therefore, p values especially for the radiographically deeper dentin involvement AUC difference should be interpreted cautiously. Sixth, no anterior teeth were included; therefore, this study cannot evaluate diagnostic-performance differences between anterior and posterior proximal surfaces. The findings should be interpreted as applying to posterior proximal sites only. This focus was clinically intentional because posterior proximal surfaces are less accessible to direct visual inspection and are more likely to require adjunctive diagnostic methods than anterior proximal surfaces. Finally, the site-level analysis can be affected by within-participant clustering; we did not model this explicitly in the primary analyses; future work could incorporate hierarchical modeling with participant-level random effects.

Clinically, these findings suggest that both QLF (QrayPen C) and near-infrared intraoral scanner imaging (iTero Element 5D NIRI) can serve as adjuncts for proximal caries assessment, particularly when the aim is monitoring lesion status over time or reducing reliance on radiography in low-risk situations. However, when management decisions require radiographic staging information especially for dentin involvement bitewing radiography remains important.

Conclusions

In a paired diagnostic accuracy comparison using ICCMS bitewing scoring as reference, QS and NIRI imaging showed similar, moderate performance for detecting any radiographic proximal caries and higher performance for thresholds indicating dentin involvement. NIRI demonstrated a small AUC advantage for the radiographically deeper dentin involvement threshold. These non-ionizing tools may complement, but not replace, bitewing radiography when radiographic staging information is needed for clinical decision making. The prespecified ordinal cut-offs should not be treated as clinically validated decision thresholds; external validation in independent cohorts is needed.
