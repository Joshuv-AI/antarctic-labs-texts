Ideal Surgical Incision Lines Minimizing Tension: A Proposal Based on Observations of Hypertrophic Scars and Keloids
Authors: Chenyu Huang, Whitney L. Quong, Yukie Kamii, Rei Ogawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Hypertrophic scars and keloids can develop from surgical incisions. These pathologic scars grow progressively due to chronic dermal inflammation, which is exacerbated by skin tension. Postsurgical wound tension can be mitigated by various surgical tactics. One underrecognized aspect is surgical incision orientation. Here, we discuss the principles underlying existing surgical incision line systems, including Langer and Pinkus lines, and propose a new system based on our observations of pathological scar growth.Methods:We review the biological mechanisms that drive pathological scar growth, describe existing surgical incision line systems and their limitations, and propose our “ideal surgical incision line” system.Results:Keloids grow in the direction of predominant skin-stretching tension. The main surgical incision line systems are those of Borges (relaxed skin tension lines caused by anatomical features), Kraissl (dynamic lines driven by muscle contraction), and Lemperle (striae distensae). Borges lines only consist of static lines; Kraissl lines are generally not accurate for dynamic wrinkles caused by multidirectional or multilayered muscle contraction; and not every patient bears striae distensae. Our experience with pathologic scars led us to develop an “ideal surgical incision” system for the face and entire body that incorporates static wrinkles, dynamic wrinkles caused by simple and multidirectional muscle contraction, and classical folds/creases/grooves. We also propose that when ideal lines cannot be used for practical reasons, Z-incisions and Z-plasties can mitigate skin tension.Conclusions:Our ideal surgical incision line system may help guide surgeons in their choice of surgical incision lines, thus reducing the risk of pathological scarring.

Takeaways

Question: There are numerous historical guidelines for surgical incision lines. What are the advantages and disadvantages of each?

Findings: It is known that pathological scars, such as keloids and hypertrophic scars, tend to spread and enlarge in response to mechanical tensions. In other words, by observing the direction in which keloids and hypertrophic scars form, one can identify ideal incision lines that help prevent pathological scarring.

Meaning: By following the incision lines we propose, it is possible to minimize conspicuous scarring.

INTRODUCTION

Hypertrophic scars (HSs) and keloids are pathological dermal lesions that grow continuously in the vertical direction (HSs) or both vertical and horizontal directions (keloids). They can significantly reduce patient quality of life due to their symptoms (itch, pain, tightness), unappealing appearance, ability to impair joint function, refractoriness to nonsurgical therapies, and ready recurrence after surgical monotherapy. Surgery is a significant cause of these pathological scars.

HS/keloid growth is driven by aberrant wound healing that prolongs the initial inflammatory phase well beyond epithelialization. This chronic dermal inflammation generates and maintains hyperreactive local (myo)fibroblasts, which unendingly secrete extracellular matrix.1–4 Chronic inflammation is caused by multiple factors that likely interact to dictate the severity of scar growth, including genetics,5 systemic factors (eg, hormones),6 and local factors.1,7,8 An important local factor is skin tension on the dermis. This article focuses on this factor. We will discuss the evidence linking pathological scarring to skin tension further on.

Unlike the other risk factors for pathological scarring, skin tension can potentially be mitigated. In particular, there are multiple perioperative tactics that can reduce skin tension on postsurgical wounds. In this article, we will focus on an early surgical step, namely, the orientation of the initial incision. The importance of this step for preventing pathological scarring has received limited attention to date. Thus, we will herein review the known surgical incision line systems and describe their underlying principles and limitations. We will then present our new “ideal surgical incision line” system for the face and whole body, which we developed based on our decades-long clinical experience with pathological scars. We will also describe the approach to take when it is not possible to use an ideal surgical incision line.

SKIN TENSION DRIVES HYPERTROPHIC SCAR AND KELOID GROWTH

HSs and keloids can be seen as different forms of the same fibroproliferative skin disorder9: although HSs largely lack the genetic component of keloids, they nonetheless share their pathological features, namely, chronic dermal inflammation, abnormal fibroblast accumulation, and excessive extracellular matrix deposition.1–4 Skin tension, which is a mechanical force, is a major risk factor for these scars. This is best evidenced by our analysis of the anatomical distribution of 1500 keloids in 483 Japanese patients that were not caused by deliberate wounding (eg, earlobe piercing, cesarean section).10 This indicated that keloids had a strong predilection for regions with high tension and cyclical skin stretching, namely, the anterior chest (48.9%), scapula (26.9%), and lower jaw/neck (12.1%). Keloids were also completely absent on the nonstretching skin of the scalp and anterior lower leg.10 Moreover, keloids adopt site-specific shapes that reflect the predominant directions of skin stretching: examples are the crab’s claw/butterfly on the anterior chest and the scapular dumbbell. In addition, disrupting or altering local tension changes scar morphology.11 Finally, using tension-reducing tactics during surgery together with inflammation-suppressing postoperative radiotherapy has reduced the keloid recurrence rates in our institution to less than 10% (versus 50%–80% previously).12

Thus, skin tension on a surgical wound is intimately linked to the progression of pathological scarring. A crucial mechanism likely involves the deposition and subsequent accumulation of stiff collagen during wound healing, which behaves like a macroscopic rope. Because this structure cannot dissipate tension, it impairs collagen matrix–mediated intercellular mechanocommunication. The transmitted tension activates mechanosignaling pathways in resident cells, generating a vicious cycle in which these hyperactive cells secrete increasing amounts of inflammatory mediators and stiff collagen. Consequently, mechanosensitivity and mechanosignaling through the collagen network are progressively diminished.13

Tension-reducing perioperative tactics include modifying the incision design; using Z-plasties to disrupt skin tension14; using flaps to release tension and/or cover wounds that cannot be closed primarily without tension15; suturing such that tension is placed on the subcutaneous tissue/fascia and not the dermis; and wound fixation with tape/gel sheets. We propose here that another important tension-reducing tactic is to purposefully orient the surgical incisions along low-tension skin lines.

EXISTING SURGICAL INCISION LINE SYSTEMS

Some low-tension skin lines are readily detected, including skin folds, creases, and wrinkles, such as the nasolabial fold, the tear trough, joint creases, palmar creases, crow’s feet wrinkles, and glabellar grooves. These folds/creases are accompanied by noticeable skin laxity. In some cases, they are created by muscle movement (eg, facial expressions), and age can deepen many of them into permanent wrinkles. Thus, the readily evident folds can be divided into those that are continuously present (static lines) and those that appear with muscle movement (dynamic lines). These lines are ideal for incisions because they are clearly free from tension. However, there are many body sites that involve a larger surface area that is not interrupted by specific anatomical structures: examples are the back and the legs. To help surgeons plan their incisions in these body sites, several surgical incision line systems have been proposed during the past 1.5 centuries (Table 1). Here, we will discuss the underlying principles, intercorrelations, and limitations of 3 of these systems, as well as 2 historical systems that are sometimes mentioned in textbooks/the literature.

Historical Attempts to Describe Natural Tensions Through Skin Lines

Relaxed Skin Tension Lines

The surgeon Albert Borges16 proposed relaxed skin tension lines (RSTLs) (also called Borges lines) in 1962. These lines are predominantly determined by protrusions/hollows caused by underlying tissues (eg, bony structures), although they can be temporarily changed by muscle contraction. They are generally not visible at rest but can be seen by pinching the skin at specific angles: this relaxes/reduces the tension on the skin and reveals skin furrows that bear reduced tension (Fig. 1). RSTLs thus reflect the hidden but constant tension on the skin, including during sleep. However, in regions such as the upper limb, which undergoes complex movements (eg, pronation, supination, flexion, and extension), RSTLs can be difficult to identify and interpret correctly, leading to inappropriate incision lines. Indeed, the upper-limb RSTLs shown in Figure 1 do not reflect actual skin laxity.

RSTLs: RSTLs (Borges lines) are skin furrows that appear when the skin is relaxed by pinching the skin at specific angles. However, it can be difficult to reproducibly identify these lines.

Kraissl lines. Kraissl lines are dynamic wrinkles that lie perpendicular to muscular contraction. They can be observed by actively contracting the muscles or gently compressing the relaxed skin. However, they are likely less accurate in areas where the underlying muscular action is mediated by multidirectional or multilayered muscles.

Kraissl Lines

In 1951, the surgeon Cornelius Kraissl17 traced the facial wrinkles of an older patient on a photograph and realized that these dynamic wrinkles ran perpendicular to the muscular contraction in the face. Thus, he suggested that muscular contraction determines the direction of dynamic wrinkles, developed his surgical incision lines system (Fig. 2), and proposed that incisions should run perpendicular to muscle action. Kraissl lines can be revealed by actively contracting the muscles or gently compressing the relaxed skin.17 Because facial wrinkles are produced by relatively simple muscle contraction, Kraissl lines are particularly useful for facial incisions. However, they may be less suitable for incisions elsewhere due to the presence of muscles with multidirectional and multilayer fibers and individual variations in muscle contour/distribution. Moreover, wrinkles are relatively ill-defined in young people.18,19 Notably, Kraissl lines differ from RSTLs in the nose, upper crow’s feet, and chin.18

Lemperle Striae Distensae

Striae distensae (stretch marks) are often observed in physiological and pathological conditions such as growth spurts and Cushing syndrome, respectively. Striae distensae lines can be identified in nearly all body regions except the scalp and feet.20,21 Notably, they often orient themselves parallel or slightly oblique to the direction of muscular contraction, namely, perpendicular to the direction of the musculoskeletal-defined tension lines (Fig. 3). They correlate poorly with RSTLs overall, but given their relationship with muscle activity, they are consistent with Kraissl lines in the shoulder, chest, lower abdomen, and extremities. However, they differ from Kraissl lines in the upper, mid, and lower back and buttocks. Lemperle et al21 recently suggested that because striae distensae contain lax skin due to collagen stretching, they could help determine surgical incision lines. However, striae distensae can only be used in choosing surgical incision lines when they are present, which is not the case for many patients.

Lemperle system (striae distensae). Lemperle suggested that striae distensae (stretch marks) can serve as surgical incision lines because they contain lax, stretched skin. These lines can be observed in nearly all body regions and are oriented parallel or slightly oblique to the direction of muscular contraction. However, not all patients have striae distensae.

Langer Lines

The oldest incision line system was produced by the anatomist Karl Langer in 1861.22 He discovered Langer lines (also called skin cleavage lines or skin tension lines) when he punched round holes in the skin of cadavers and noted that the holes elongated eventually into oval shapes. It was believed that this reflected the predominant direction of skin tension. The Langer line schematic was promoted 30 years later by the surgeon Emil Theodor Kocher as a guide to optimal surgeon incisions (Fig. 4).23 Some Langer lines do not correspond to RSTLs: for example, the Langer lines on the scalp and forehead run perpendicular to the RSTLs.18 Moreover, some wrinkles such as crow’s feet cross Langer lines at right angles. There are also significant discrepancies between Langer lines and Kraissl lines on the sternum, lower abdomen, and anterior neck. Similarly, although they correlate well with striae distensae in the lower back and anterior thigh, there are discrepancies in the lower abdomen, upper back, buttocks, and posterior thigh. It is thought that Langer lines, in fact, reflect artifactual combined effects of collagen fiber microanatomy in the reticular dermis and muscle stiffening in rigor mortis. Thus, despite their frequent mention in surgical textbooks, Langer lines are not suitable guides for surgical incisions.

Langer lines. Langer lines (also called skin cleavage lines or skin tension lines) refer to the direction of elongation of round holes punched in the skin of cadavers. They are likely artifacts of skin microanatomy and muscles in rigor mortis rather than being bona fide skin tension lines. Thus, Langer lines cannot serve as practical surgical guides.

Pinkus Main-folding Lines

In 1927, the dermatologist Felix Pinkus24 was perhaps the first to question Langer lines as a useful guide for incisions. He instead proposed his main-folding lines, which align with natural skin creases/folds that are formed by body movements (Fig. 5).24 However, the schematic produced by Pinkus involved multiple intersecting lines20 and was confusing for surgeons. Consequently, there was little uptake of these lines by the surgical community until Lemperle23 revived them recently.

Pinkus lines. Pinkus lines (main-folding lines) are based on the major body folds seen when the body moves. However, the lines involve multiple intersecting paths that can be confusing for surgeons. This system was largely ignored until recent works by Lemperle.

THE NEW IDEAL SURGICAL INCISION LINE SYSTEM

Our analysis of the extant incision line systems shows that these systems are based on different principles, can conflict with each other, and are associated with practical disadvantages. There is thus a need for a similarly comprehensive but clearer system that can guide surgical incision decision-making. Here, we propose our whole-body and facial “ideal surgical incision line” system (Fig. 6). This system is based on our extensive practical experience with pathological scar behavior and our understanding of its intimate relationship with skin tension. Our system is suitable for elective surgical incisions or wherever the skin incision can be controlled. We will describe its underlying principles and show how it can be used as needed, including with surgical sites that are not covered here. Our purpose is not to cover all complex and dynamic clinical situations with a single fixed solution, but instead to present an approach to optimizing incisions for commonly encountered surgical cases. We have applied the principles of this system in clinical practice at Nippon Medical School since 2008 and have reported in a system evaluation study that it reduced the recurrence rate of keloids treated with the same surgical/postoperative radiotherapy protocol from 12.3% (2003–2007, 317 lesions) to 11.0% (2008–2012, 327 lesions). Further refinements to the radiotherapy protocol have subsequently reduced recurrence rates to less than 10%.

This system includes both static and dynamic wrinkles/creases, including wrinkles/creases caused by multilayered and multidirectional muscle fibers. It can be used for elective surgical planning or whenever the skin incision can be controlled.

Principles Underlying the Ideal Surgical Incision Line System

RSTLs and Kraissl lines largely reflect skin tension imposed by underlying anatomical structures and muscle activity, respectively. This means the directions of stress imposed by these tension-inducing elements may not align perfectly and could even be perpendicular to each other: in these cases, the direction of the predominant tension line would be determined by the relative strengths of the contributing elements. We therefore propose that when defining the ideal surgical incision line for a specific anatomical site, it is necessary to consider the dynamic relationship between these tension-inducing elements. We thus considered every part of the body and face in this light. This, together with our observations of pathological scar behavior, led us to define the ideal surgical incision lines shown in red in our schematics (Fig. 6). On examining these lines, we found they could be classified into 4 types based on the relationship between underlying anatomical structures and muscular contraction.

Type I: Static Visible Lines (Joint Areas)

These are creases that occur in joint areas with a marked range of motion. They are clearly visible to the naked eye and lie 90 degrees (ie, perpendicular) to the direction of muscle contraction or tendon extension. Typical examples are the perpetual creases at the inner elbow and finger joints. It is ideal to make incisions along (ie, parallel to) these creases. The usefulness of these creases is highlighted by the predilection of the joints to develop hypertrophic scar contractions, particularly after burns but also following surgery. This reflects the powerful longitudinal stretching tension on the skin caused by joint movement.

Type II: Dynamic Lines (Areas Where Wrinkles Become Visible on Muscular Contraction)

These are wrinkles that arise when superficial muscles beneath the skin contract. These wrinkles lie 90 degrees (ie, perpendicular) to the direction of muscle contraction and only become visible to the naked eye after contraction (unless aging deepens the wrinkles). Typical examples are the horizontal forehead wrinkles caused by frontalis muscle contraction and the diagonal wrinkles on the forearm caused by wrist flexion. The anterior chest wrinkles that are due to the pectoral muscle, which can be seen when the shoulders are drawn forward and together, are another example. It is ideal to make incisions along (ie, parallel to) these wrinkles. For instance, the ideal incision line on the anterior chest is 90 degrees to the direction in which the pectoral muscle pulls. Thus, the ideal incision line on the chest midline should be vertical, and the line should become diagonally oriented as it approaches the axilla. The importance of these lines is demonstrated by the horizontally spreading butterfly keloids that can arise from acne lesions on the anterior chest (Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E531). This reflects the strong, repetitive lateral tension on the chest skin caused by arm movements.

Type III: Semidynamic Lines (Areas With Well-defined 3-dimensional Contours or Intrinsic Grooves/Folds)

If there are nearby and/or surrounding structures that create folds/grooves or prominences, the ideal incision line should follow the contours created by these structures. For example, incisions should lie parallel to the mandibular margin/contour or run along the deep grooves formed by the eyelid, inframammary fold, and nasolabial fold. The importance of these lines is demonstrated by the rarity of pathological scarring after such incisions.

Type IV: No Visible Lines (Areas With Minimal Muscular Contraction and Fixed Skin)

The type-IV areas include regions such as the crown of the head or the anterior tibia, which bear skin that is relatively fixed. These areas almost never develop keloids, even in patients who are highly susceptible to these scars. Because incisions in these areas can theoretically be made in any direction, other considerations will dictate incision orientation. For example, it is preferable to make horizontal incisions on the crown of the head so that they follow the wrinkles/lines on the adjacent forehead or occipital regions. Another type-IV area is the earlobe. Although the earlobe is prone to keloids, this reflects repeated injuries such as attachment and detachment from ear piercings that promote chronic inflammation rather than skin tension.25 Thus, theoretically, incisions on the earlobe can also take any direction. Practically, however, a diagonal excision is preferred because it preserves the maximal radial length of the earlobe.

Cases Where Ideal Incision Lines Cannot Be Used

There are situations where it is not possible to use an ideal incision line directly. For example, in knee arthroplasty, the ideal incision line is horizontal. However, because this yields a small surgical field of view, a vertical incision must be chosen (Fig. 7A, B), which can lead to pathological scars, especially in Asian/African patients.26 In such cases, the risk of pathological scarring can be mitigated by judicious use of plastic surgery techniques. In particular, it is important to avoid creating a long, straight scar. This can be achieved by using techniques that break up the scar and disrupt tension. These techniques include Z-incision (making a zigzag incision and suturing as is), and Z-plasty (where flaps created by the zigzag incision are interposed). The zigzag angles are usually between 30 and 60 degrees, but 90-degree angles can also be used. For example, when a vertical incision must be made in the knee, the risk of pathological scarring can be reduced by using a 60- or 90-degree zigzag incision (Fig. 7C, D). The power of this approach is exemplified by the scar revision case shown in Figure 8. Here, a keloid had developed along the long axis of the upper arm due to childhood Bacillus Calmette-Guérin vaccination-induced inflammation, which was sustained by limb growth and shoulder movement. The keloid had to be excised along the same axis, even though this axis corresponded to the predominant direction of skin stretching. To prevent keloid regrowth, Z-plasties were used to close the wound, which healed without recurrence. The key here is to orient the central leg of each “Z” along the low-tension line in the area. Figure 9 demonstrates this approach for the face and whole body. This zigzag approach not only disrupts the tension on to the wound, but also helps prevent wound dehiscence and scar widening, making the scar less obvious. Thus, orienting Z-incisions and Z-plasties so that their central leg runs along ideal incision lines can reduce the risk of pathological scarring when direct use of these lines is not feasible (Fig. 8E). Given the good outcomes of Z-incisions/plasties, we recommend them for nonideal incisions in general, particularly in patients with pathological scar risk factors (eg, keloid history).

CONCLUSIONS

Pathological scars are extreme examples of the potentially pernicious effect of local mechanical tension on wound healing. Lessons drawn from them can inform plastic surgery in general. Here, we show that surgical tactics to mitigate tension on the wound/scar should include the purposeful placement of incisions along lines of low tension. Our observations of pathological scar growth patterns and the surgical incision directions associated with pathological scarring/recurrence suggest that current surgical incision line systems may not be adequate guides sometimes. To address this, we propose an “ideal surgical incision” system of low-tension incision lines for the face and body. Unlike other incision line systems, our proposed system (1) accounts for both major anatomical contours/landmarks and the impact of contraction by simple and multidirectional/multilayered muscles, thus incorporating both dynamic and static low-tension lines; (2) takes into account the natural skin characteristics of the living human body; (3) does not rely on striae distensae, which may be absent; (4) can be used in young patients without wrinkles; and (5) covers all body regions. We also show that combining our line system with Z-incisions and Z-plasties can help reduce the risk of pathological scarring in cases where ideal surgical incision lines cannot be used directly.
