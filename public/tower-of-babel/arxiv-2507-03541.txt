Foundation versus Domain-specific Models: Performance Comparison, Fusion, and Explainability in Face Recognition
Redwan Sony, Parisa Farmanifard, Arun Ross, Anil K. Jain

Abstract
In this paper, we address the following question: How do generic foundation models (e.g., CLIP, BLIP, LLaVa, DINO) compare against a domain-specific face recognition model (viz., AdaFace or ArcFace) on the face recognition task? Through a series of experiments involving several foundation models and benchmark datasets, we are able to report the following findings: (a) In all datasets considered, domain-specific models outperformed zero-shot foundation models. (b) The performance of zero-shot generic foundation models improves on over-segmented face images than tightly cropped faces thereby suggesting the importance of contextual clues. For example, at a False Match Rate (FMR) of 0.01%, the True Match Rate (TMR) of OpenCLIP improved from 64.97% to 81.73% on the LFW dataset as the face crop increased from 112 × \times 112 to 250 × \times 250 while the TMR of domain-specific AdaFace dropped from 99.09% to 77.31%. (c) A simple score-level fusion of a foundation model with a domain-specific FR model improved the accuracy at low FMRs. For example, the TMR of AdaFace when fused with BLIP improved from 72.64% to 83.31% at an FMR of 0.0001% on the IJB-B dataset and from 73.17% to 85.81% on the IJB-C dataset. (d) Foundation models, such as ChatGPT, can be used to impart explainability to the FR pipeline (e.g., “Despite minor lighting and head tilt differences, the two left-profile images show high consistency in forehead slope, nose shape, chin contour…”). In some instances, foundation models are even able to resolve low-confidence decisions made by AdaFace (e.g., “Although AdaFace assigns a low similarity score of 0.21, both images exhibit high visual similarity…and the pair is likely of the same person”), thereby reiterating the importance of combining domain-specific FR models with generic foundation models in a judicious manner.

Introduction

Automated Face Recognition (FR) is an important biometric technology that uses face images or videos to recognize an individual
[
19
]
.
Face recognition systems typically operate in the verification or identification mode. In verification (1:1 matching), the goal is to determine whether two face images are of the same individual. In identification (1:N matching), the input face image (probe) is compared against a database of enrolled face images (gallery) in order to determine the best match.

State-of-the-art (SoTA) face recognition models have been observed to achieve high accuracy on challenging benchmark datasets

In this paper, we raise the following questions.

1.

On the task of face recognition - where domain-specific models have been effective so far - what are the benefits of using generic foundation models?

2.

Can foundation models be used alongside domain-specific models to further improve recognition accuracy?

3.

Can foundation models be used to impart explainability to the face recognition task?

In order to investigate these questions, we conduct an extensive study involving 35 foundation models (corresponding to 14 different families) and 5 domain-specific FR models (corresponding to 2 different matchers) on 5 benchmark datasets.

Our contributions and findings are as follows:

1.

We show that domain-specific models are vastly superior over zero-shot foundation models on the task of face recognition.

2.

The accuracy of a foundation model improves when the face images are
loosely
cropped from the background. This suggests the importance of contextual information in the case of foundation models.

3.

Fusing a foundation model with a domain-specific model at the score level improves the recognition accuracy.

4.

Conversational foundation models can be used to impart explainability to face recognition by providing a rationale for a match or a non-match.

5.

In some instances, conversational foundation models can be used to resolve low-confidence decisions, where a domain-specific model outputs a match score that is very close to the decision boundary, i.e., threshold.

In summary, this work demonstrates the benefit of using foundation models in conjunction with domain-specific models to advance face recognition.

4.2
Fusion of FR Models with FM Models

As previously observed in Section
2
, domain-specific models require tightly cropped facial images, while foundation models benefit from the inclusion of contextual information in loosely cropped face images. On challenging benchmarks such as IJB-B, IJB-C, and IJB-S where facial regions are tightly localized, even SoTA FR models exhibit performance limitations. To integrate local facial details from domain-specific models with contextual cues from foundation models, we perform score-level fusion. For example, when we combine the best-performing foundation model (BLIP2-O-6.7B) with AdaFace, the TMR improves from 72.64% to 83.31% on IJB-B and from 73.17% to 85.81% on IJB-C at an FMR of
0.0001\%
. As can be seen in Table
5
, these gains are more pronounced at lower FMRs. For the IJB-S Still-Still benchmark, the fusion yields only modest overall gains. The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves in Figure
4
highlight notable improvements at lower FMR thresholds, where combining foundation models with AdaFace enhances TMR. Figure
3
illustrates cases from the IJB-B dataset where the AdaFace model fails, but fusion with the foundation model (BLIP) model results in the correct decision. This suggests that foundation models are extracting complementary cues that improve recognition in challenging cases.

[

significantly affected both performance and explanation quality. When the prompt explicitly mentioned “AdaFace,” ChatGPT often anchored its explanation around AdaFace’s known criteria—justifying decisions using concepts such as embedding similarity and “face-print” geometry. To control for this, we repeated the analysis with Prompt 2, replacing “AdaFace” with a generic phrase like
a face recognition algorithm/metric
. This neutral phrasing led to slight improvements in explanation accuracy, though the model still referenced typical decision factors used by SoTA FR systems.

The most significant improvement came with Prompt 3, as follows, where all references to models and scores were removed:
“Please examine these two face images. Are they of the same person or not? Explain your reasoning”
. Using this fully neutral prompt, ChatGPT correctly classified all 200 image pairs across the four ground-truth categories (TM, TNM, FM, FNM), as shown in Table
6
. Notably, the explanations remained accurate even when AdaFace failed—particularly in False Match and False Non-Match cases. For example, although AdaFace misclassified some pairs due to background clutter, occlusion, or poor detection crops, ChatGPT still provided correct visual interpretations based on observable identity cues although it appears gender cues were not used. It pointed out when faces were cropped differently or when one image contained a different person entirely due to failed detection. Even in low-confidence AdaFace matches (e.g., scores around 0.21–0.24), ChatGPT accurately highlighted subtle geometric alignments in facial structure and explained why these were (or were not) sufficient for a genuine match.

These results underscore that foundation models like ChatGPT can generate accurate, detailed, and interpretable reasoning grounded in facial features—independent of any embedding space or classifier score. Even when AdaFace failed (e.g., in False Match/False Non-Match pairs), ChatGPT’s explanations aligned well with human facial analysis, reinforcing its value for post-hoc explainability in biometric systems.

Summary

In this work, we compared the performance of zero-shot foundation models with domain-specific face recognition models. Based on a series of experiments, we draw the following conclusions:

1.

Domain-specific models are vastly superior that zero-shot foundation models on all the datasets considered in this work.

2.

The performance of foundation models was observed to improve when the face region was over-segmented, i.e., loosely cropped, thereby suggesting the importance of broader contextual clues in the case of foundation models. Domain-specific models, on the other hand, were observed to degrade in accuracy when face images were over-segmented.

3.

The score-level fusion of domain-specific models with foundation models resulted in an improvement in recognition accuracy thereby suggesting that complementary information was being encoded by these models.

4.

Foundation models could provide explainability via textual reasoning when comparing two face images and, in some instances, resolve ambiguous decisions made by the domain-specific model.
