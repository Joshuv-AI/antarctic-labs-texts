Partial Identification of Causal Effects for Endogenous Continuous Treatments
Abhinandan Dalal, Eric J. Tchetgen Tchetgen

Abstract
No unmeasured confounding is a common assumption when reasoning about counterfactual outcomes, but such an assumption may not be plausible in observational studies. Sensitivity analysis is often employed to assess the robustness of causal conclusions to unmeasured confounding, but existing methods are predominantly designed for binary treatments. In this paper, we provide natural extensions of two extensively used sensitivity frameworks – the Rosenbaum and Marginal sensitivity models – to the setting of continuous exposures. Our generalization replaces scalar sensitivity parameters with sensitivity functions that vary with exposure level, enabling richer modeling and sharper identification bounds. We develop a unified pseudo-outcome regression formulation for bounding the counterfactual dose–response curve under both models, and propose corresponding nonparametric estimators which have second order bias. These estimators accommodate modern machine learning methods for obtaining nuisance parameter estimators, which are shown to achieve L 2 L^{2} -consistency, minimax rates of convergence under suitable conditions. Our resulting estimators of bounds for the counterfactual dose-response curve are shown to be consistent and asymptotic normal allowing for a user-specified bound on the degree of uncontrolled exposure endogeneity. We also offer a geometric interpretation that relates the Rosenbaum and Marginal sensitivity model and guides their practical usage in global versus targeted sensitivity analysis. The methods are validated through simulations and a real-data application on the effect of second-hand smoke exposure on blood lead levels in children.



1
Introduction

Causal inference in observational studies commonly proceeds under the assumption of no unmeasured confounding (NUC), also known as no endogeneity: treatment assignment is effectively randomized within strata defined by a sufficiently rich set of observed covariates. Although this assumption enables identification of causal parameters and effective inference through propensity score matching
Rosenbaum 2010
, inverse-probability weighting
Horvitz and Thompson 1952
and doubly robust and double-machine learning estimators
Robins et al. 1994
;
Van Der Laan and Rubin 2006
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
— it is fundamentally untestable from the observed data without an alternative assumption and may be violated in practice.

Lack of sufficient control for confounding can lead to different conclusions from observational and experimental studies, and hence can threaten the validity of conclusions obtained from the former. For example, observational studies had raised concerns about the possible harm associated with the use of calcium channel blockers like nifedipine (
Psaty et al. 1995
;
Pahor et al. 2000
) with respect to myocardial infarction (commonly known as heart attack), only to be settled definitively about a decade later via randomized controlled trials that nifedipine was safe (
Psaty and Furberg 2004
). In-depth analysis revealed that calcium channel blockers were used to treat hypertension, which in itself was a risk factor associated with heart attacks (see
Rutter 2007
for more details).

To assess the robustness of causal conclusions about violations of NUC, researchers increasingly rely on sensitivity analysis to quantify the impact of
potential
unmeasured confounding. In response to concerns about unmeasured confounding dating back to
Fisher 1958
about the impact of smoking on lung cancer,
Cornfield et al. 1959
conducted the first known formal sensitivity analysis to establish that an unmeasured confounder would need to be nine times more prevalent among smokers than non-smokers to explain the observed association, a magnitude deemed implausible. Since then, there have been immense interest in developing methods for sensitivity analysis, the works of
Robins 1999
;
Rosenbaum 2010
;
VanderWeele and Arah 2011
;
Imbens and Rubin 2015
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2021
;
Kallus et al. 2019
;
Zhao et al. 2019
;
Bekerman et al. 2024
;
Nabi et al. 2024
are some notable works in this regard.

However, most existing works have primarily focused on binary exposures. Continuous exposures provide unique challenges to causal inference problems, since (i) the probability of observing any
\{T=t\}
is zero, and (ii) there would be infinitely many potential outcome means
\{\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
to identify. Existing literature on causal inference with continuous exposure have been considered in
Lu et al. 2001
;
Fogarty et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023
etc. for matching methods, and
Kennedy et al. 2017
;
Colangelo and and 2025
;
Schindl et al. 2024
etc. for double robust estimation, but these works have not included sensitivity analysis to NUC. CATE estimation with continuous covariates also deploys similar techniques as continuous exposures, as illustrated in
Kennedy 2023
and
Semenova and Chernozhukov 2021
.

Sensitivity analysis with continuous exposures in matched studies has recently been considered in
Zhang et al. 2024
and
Frazier et al. 2024
, and interestingly, the first work establishes hardness results for sensitivity analysis in matched studies with continuous exposure and outcome. A more general class of
f
-sensitivity models have also been considered in
Jin et al. 2022
and
Frauen et al. 2024
, where they bound a more general convex-link
f
of the odd’s ratio, instead of the identity link, to accommodate the unbounded support of unmeasured confounders.
Bonvini et al. 2022
also studied sensitivity analysis in marginal structural models with continuous exposures, introducing approaches based on propensity scores (see also
Jesson et al. 2022
;
Marmarelis et al. 2023
), outcome regression, and subset confounding that differ from standard formulations; notably, they bound density ratios in the former model rather than odds ratios (see Section
6
and Appendix
C
for further details), and leverage low-dimensional structure to achieve
\sqrt{n}
-rate inference. In this work, we deviate from their strategy and instead consider double-robust partial identification of the dose-response curve, and we directly estimate corresponding bounds under less restrictive non-parametric regression conditions.

Although the need for sensitivity analysis is widely accepted, there remains a broad range of sensitivity models to choose from. We focus on continuous exposure extensions of two well-established canonical binary exposure models: Rosenbaum’s model (
Rosenbaum 2002
), and the marginal model of Tan (
Tan 2006
). Both models, originally devised for binary exposures, bound the odds ratio of receiving exposure for different levels of an unmeasured confounder
U
. Moreover, both models include the NUC data-distribution within permissible distributions for any non-trivial value of their sensitivity parameter. We extend these models to the continuous exposure case, and derive novel identification bounds for the dose-response curve under both models. Our work builds upon the existing extensions to multi-level exposures of
Rosenbaum 1989
, however, we develop our framework for partial inference to respect key inequality constraints anchoring our sensitivity analyses. It may be worthwhile to mention that both aforementioned models belong to a class of
L^{\infty}
models, which bounds the maximum deviation of the data-distribution from NUC. Recent works have also explored
L^{2}
class of models, which arguably being less interpretable than
L^{\infty}
models (
Zhang and Zhao 2024
;
Huang and Pimentel 2025
), produce narrower bounds for the potential outcome mean, and could hence be appealing to researchers. Nevertheless, in this work, we focus on the specified
L^{\infty}
models in the case for continuous exposures and defer the development of analogous bounds under
L^{2}
or more general models to future work.

1.1
Our Contributions

We delineate our contributions in this paper as the following:

(i)

We introduce the notion of a
sensitivity function
for continuous exposures, allowing bounds to vary with exposure levels. This generalizes traditional scalar sensitivity parameters and accommodates rich parametric and semi-parametric models, enabling more flexible, exposure-specific bounds on the dose-response curve. Moreover, replacing a uniform sensitivity scalar with an exposure-specific function yields narrower partial identification bands for the dose–response curve.

(ii)

We characterize a class of admissible sensitivity functions for Rosenbaum and Marginal sensitivity models, respectively. All functions in this class satisfy naturally occurring model restrictions but otherwise vary freely. Crucially, as we show for any such function, there exists a full-data distribution law compatible with the bound.

(iii)

We derive bounds on the potential outcome mean under each model, using a new characterization of a bound for the conditional average counterfactual regression with continuous treatment, a result which may be of independent interest. Specifically, building upon the work of
Yadlowsky et al. 2022
, we map exposure density ratio constraints to likelihood ratio bounds for potential outcomes, and devise a common framework that can derive bounds under both the Rosenbaum and Marginal models. The key functional under the Rosenbaum model is shown to be related to a relevant expectile (
Newey and Powell 1987
;
Waltrup et al. 2015
), and under the marginal model to a corresponding quantile and the conditional value-at-risk function (CVar) (
Rockafellar et al. 2000
;
Rockafellar et al. 2006
; see Section
3
for further details).

(iv)

Next, we derive doubly robust estimators using pseudo-outcomes and semi-parametric techniques from
Kennedy et al. 2017
and
Yang et al. 2023
. While the bounds themselves are not pathwise-differentiable under continuous exposures, we derive relevant influence functions for certain marginalized versions of the bounds, which define pseudo-outcomes used to estimate the targeted bounds nonparametrically via flexible machine learning techniques, with certain small-bias guarantees we characterize.

(v)

We establish
L^{2}
-consistency and asymptotic normality for the bound estimators using non-parametric ordinary least-squares over a dense basis system. The pseudo-outcome construction leads to second order bias terms, enabling minimax-optimal convergence even with slower rates of nuisance estimation. We also establish consistency for a variance estimator, enabling the construction of valid pointwise confidence interval for the dose-response curve.

(vi)

Next, we discuss the differences between the Rosenbaum and Marginal models in extensive detail. We illustrate a geometric intuition to both models by identifying a set, such that the Rosenbaum model bounds the diameter of that set, while the Marginal model bounds a relevant radius. This also allows us to extend familiar relationships between both models derived in
Zhao et al. 2019
under binary exposures to their continuous counterparts.

(vii)

Finally, we show that both the Rosenbaum and Marginal models with any sensitivity function from the function class in (ii) are compatible with the observed data distribution, but only the Rosenbaum model goes a step further by being compatible with arbitrary specifications of the marginal distribution of the unmeasured confounder. This distinction motivates a discussion of
global
versus
targeted
sensitivity analysis, and the contexts in which each model is more appropriate.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
we introduce the sensitivity analysis setup, and formally introduce the notion of sensitivity functions and the Rosenbaum and Marginal sensitivity models. Section
3
produces bounds on the dose-response curve using a common regression formulation. Section
4
derives the double robust mapping using semi-parametric techniques, while Section
5
establishes the
L^{2}
-consistency and asymptotic normality in the second-step of counterfactual regression. In Section
6
, we dive into a comparative discussion between the two models and identify contexts we believe are appropriate for each. Finally in Section
7
, we evaluate the performance of our methods through simulations, and apply our method to measuring the effect of second hand lead exposure on blood lead levels in children.

We assume access to
n
independent and identically distributed samples of
(W_{1},\cdots,W_{n})
, where
W=(X,T,Y)
, with
X\in\mathcal{X}
denoting a rich set of measured confounders,
T\in\mathcal{T}
is a continuous exposure (or treatment, used interchangeably), and
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
is the outcome of interest. We adopt the potential outcome framework to delineate our causal quantities, denoting
Y(t)
as the potential outcome that would have been observed had
T
been set to
t
(
Neyman 1923
;
Rubin 1974
)
Throughout this paper, we are interested in the counterfactual quantity
\{\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
, referred to as
the average dose-response curve
.

Suppose
T|X
admits a density with respect to some base measure
\lambda_{b}
. We make two standard causal assumptions of consistency and positivity.

Assumption 1
.

(i) Consistency:
Y(T)=Y
.
(ii) Positivity:
f_{T|X}(t|x)\geq p_{\text{min}}>0\ \ \forall x\in\mathcal{X}
.

The consistency assumption bridges the observed outcomes
W
to the counterfactual quantity of interest
Y(\cdot)
, and implicitly assumes away complex situations like interference and spillover effects across units, and multiple versions of the same treatment.
While the no-unmeasured confounding (NUC) assumption, alluded to in Section
1
and formalized as
\{Y(t)\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\perp\!\!\!\perp T|X
, in conjunction with Assumption
1
allows us to identify the potential outcome mean, NUC is generally not plausible in observational studies. Instead, one may imagine an unmeasured confounder
U\in\mathcal{U}
such that, one may generalize the confounding condition, as in Assumption
2
.

Assumption 2

(Latent Ignorability)
.

\{Y(t)\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\perp\!\!\!\perp T|X,U.

This is a generalization of the standard no-unmeasured confounding assumption (also known as treatment exchangeability or ignorability in the causal inference literature), whereby, while conditioning on
X
alone does not ensure exchangeability, further conditioning on
U
does. Assumption
2
is quite mild, since
U
is not measured; however, the counterfactual dose response curve is no longer identifiable under the condition, without a different condition.

To address unmeasured confounding by
U
, one strategy is to impose additional structure on their distribution, possibly involving auxiliary variables have been measured, such as instrumental variables or proxies, which may, under stringent conditions, lead to point identification of the counterfactual dose response curve (
Heckman and Urzua 2010
;
Newey and Powell 2003
;
Miao et al. 2018
).
In contrast, we adopt a different approach: we aim to partially identify the potential outcome mean under interpretable assumptions about the relationship between the unmeasured confounders and the exposure. A common strategy is to specify a sensitivity model, following the conceptual framework introduced by Rosenbaum (
Rosenbaum 2002
). In this work, we consider a generalized version of such a model that accommodates continuous exposures.

Assumption 3

(Rosenbaum sensitivity model)
.

For a specified
\Gamma:\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{T}\to[1,\infty)
satisfying
\Gamma_{t^{\prime},t}=\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}
and
\Gamma_{t,t}=1
, the law of
(T,X,U)
satisfies

\dfrac{1}{\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}}\leq\dfrac{f_{T|U,X}(t|u,X)/f_{T|U,X}(t|\tilde{u},X)}{f_{T|U,X}(t^{\prime}|u,X)/f_{T|U,X}(t^{\prime}|\tilde{u},X)}\leq\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}\text{ a.s. }X,\ \ \ \forall t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T};u,\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{U}.

(1)

Assumption
3
bounds the ratio of conditional densities of
T
given
X
and two distinct values of
U
by a prespecified function of
t
.
Note that taking
\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}=1
is equivalent to no unmeasured confounding, since the conditional density of
T|U=u,X
would no longer depend on
u
. In case of binary
T\in\{0,1\}
, replacing the conditional density of
T
by its natural conditional probability analogue yields the classical Rosenbaum sensitivity model on the odds ratio scale, with
\Gamma_{1,0}=\Gamma\geq 1
. In fact, the model imposed by Assumption
3
can be considered a generalization of the sensitivity model with many-ordered treatments introduced in
Rosenbaum 1989
. It is worth emphasizing that, although we follow the binary-treatment literature in omitting
X
from the sensitivity function
\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}
, our theory and methods extend directly to sensitivity functions that also depend on
X
, without requiring any additional technical assumptions.

Another relatively more recent sensitivity model is the Marginal sensitivity model of
Tan 2006
, also see
Zhao et al. 2019
;
Dorn and Guo 2023
;
Dorn et al. 2024
, introduced in the context of binary exposures. A natural generalization for continuous exposures entails:

Assumption 4

(Marginal sensitivity model)
.

For a specified
\Lambda:\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{T}\to[1,\infty)
symmetric in its arguments, with
\Lambda_{t,t}=1\ \forall t\in\mathcal{T}
, the law of
(T,X,U)
satisfies

\dfrac{1}{\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}}\leq\dfrac{f_{T|U,X}(t|u,X)/f_{T|X}(t|X)}{f_{T|U,X}(t^{\prime}|u,X)/f_{T|X}(t^{\prime}|X)}\leq\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}\ \forall t,t^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T};u\in\mathcal{U}.

(2)

Similar to Assumption
3
, the Marginal Sensitivity Model in Assumption
4
also bounds the ratio of density ratios, but while the Rosenbaum model compares the density ratios between different strata of the unmeasured confounder
U
, the Marginal model on the other hands compares the full-data conditional density ratio to its marginal observed data counterpart. We provide a detailed discussion of the merits and possible use-cases of each framework in Section
6
.

It is important to note that, unlike usual sensitivity models, both Assumption
3
and Assumption
4
are not parametrized by a scalar sensitivity parameter, but rather a general
sensitivity function
. Replacing a scalar sensitivity parameter with a sensitivity function as ours allows several standard parametric and semi-parametric models to be considered – for instance, if
U
has a compact support and
(A,Y)|X,U
has a multivariate normal distribution, then no scalar parameter exists that satisfies the bounds of either Equation (
1
) or (
2
). One could in principle potentially produce scalar bounds by truncating the exposure level, but this approach may not be appropriate for unbounded exposure, and even if appropriate, the approach could yield unnecessarily wide confidence intervals, by replacing a naturally varying sensitivity function with a uniform worst-case bound. However to make our models useful, we need to obtain a rich class of sensitivity functions well-suited to our goal. In Proposition
1
we achieve this goal, and we describe a rich class of admissible sensitivity functions which are provably compatible with Assumptions
3
and
4
, from which an analyst can draw good candidates for conducting a given sensitivity analysis.

Proposition 1
.

Consider the set of functions

\mathcal{F}=\left\{\left.\max\left\{\dfrac{\Upsilon_{t}}{\Upsilon_{t^{\prime}}},\dfrac{\Upsilon_{t^{\prime}}}{\Upsilon_{t}}\right\}\right|\Upsilon:\mathcal{T}\mapsto(0,\infty)\text{ is an arbitrary measurable function.}\right\}

(i)

For any function
\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists a law
(T,U,X)
that satisfies the Rosenbaum model in Assumption
3
with parameter
\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}
.

(ii)

For any function
\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists a law
(T,U,X)
that satisfies the Marginal model in Assumption
4
with parameter
\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}
.

1

3
Partial identification of potential outcome mean

Although Assumption
3
effectively restricts the degree of unmeasured confounding bias, it does not in of itself provide point-identification of the potential outcome mean. The key identification challenge stems from the fact that:

\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]=\mathbb{E}_{X}\left[\int\mathbb{E}[Y(t)|T=t^{\prime},X]\,dF_{T^{\prime}|X}(t^{\prime}|X)\right],

but since
Y(t)
is not observed unless
t^{\prime}=t
, the above expression cannot be identified under Assumptions
1
and
2
.

Yadlowsky et al. 2022
and
Dorn and Guo 2023
considered the Rosenbaum model and the Marginal model respectively for binary treatments, and derived partial identification strategies for the potential outcome mean. In this section, we extend their results to the case of continuous exposures. To simplify the exposition, our theoretical developments primarily focus on identifying lower bounds for
\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]
under the Rosenbaum and Marginal models, respectively; corresponding upper bounds can be be obtained by analogy, upon redefining
Z(t):=-Y(t)
and obtaining the negative value of the infimum of
\mathbb{E}[Z(t)]
over all counterfactual distributions under the respective sensitivity models.

Let
\theta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t):=\inf\{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[Y(t)|T=t^{\prime},X=x]:Q\in\mathcal{R}_{x}\}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{x}
is the set of all laws of
(\{Y(t)\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}},T,U)
conditional on
X=x
satisfying Assumptions
1
,
2
and the Rosenbaum model
3
. Theorem
1
provides a formal characterization of
\theta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)
in terms of the observed data on which our empirical approach shall be based.

Theorem 1

(Partial identification under Rosenbaum Sensitivity Model)
.

Define
\psi_{\theta}^{t,t^{\prime}}(y):=(y-\theta)_{+}-\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}(y-\theta)_{-}
. If
|\theta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)|<\infty
, then
\theta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)
satisfies

\theta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)=\sup\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{E}[\psi^{t,t^{\prime}}_{\mu}(Y(t))|T=t,X]\geq 0\}.

Moreover, if
Y|T=t,X
has a positive density a.s. on the convex hull of its support, then
\theta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)
is the unique solution to

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\psi^{t,t^{\prime}}_{\theta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)}(Y(t))\,\right|\,T=t,X\right]=0.

Furthermore,
\theta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)
is also the unique (up to measure-zero sets) minimizer of the optimization

\arg\min_{g}\mathbb{E}[(Y-g(X,t))^{2}_{+}+\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}(Y-g(X,t)))^{2}_{-}|T=t,X],

(3)

provided
\mathbb{E}[(Y-\theta_{t^{\prime}}(X,t))^{2}_{+}+\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}(Y-\theta_{t^{\prime}}(X,t))^{2}_{-}|T=t,X]<\infty.

Remark 1
.

For any
t^{\prime}
fixed,
\theta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)
solves the following marginal minimization problem as well:

\min_{g}\mathbb{E}[(Y-g(X,T))^{2}_{+}+\Gamma_{T,t^{\prime}}(Y-g(X,T)))^{2}_{-}].

This is a simple consequence of the interchange of integral and infimum for normal integrands (
Rockafellar and Wets 2009
, Theorem 14.60). Hence one may consider the marginal optimization problem, simpler than its conditional counterpart in Theorem
1
, when estimating
\hat{\theta}_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)
empirically, for continuous exposure
T
.

Theorem
1
produces partial identification of
\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{R}_{x}}\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]
by marginalizing over
\theta_{T}(X,t)
, which is identified by the conditional expectile of the distribution
Y|T=t,X
as a function of
\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}
. Interestingly, expectiles have recently emerged in the literature as an alternative to quantiles (
Philipps 2022
), and the result in Theorem
1
, analogous to the case of binary treatments in
Yadlowsky et al. 2022
, underscores the importance of expectiles as a critical causal quantity under sensitivity models. The theorem, proved in Appendix
E.1
, is the consequence of a key restriction on the likelihood ratio of
L_{t,t^{\prime}}(y,x)=\frac{d\mathbb{P}(Y(t)|T=t^{\prime},X)}{d\mathbb{P}(Y(t)|T=t,X)}
, given by
L_{t,t^{\prime}}(y,x)\leq\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}L_{t,t^{\prime}}(\tilde{y},x)
for all
y,\tilde{y}
, which is imposed by the Rosenbaum Model (
1
).

Analogously, the Marginal model also imposes a restriction on the likelihood ratio, given by
L_{t,t^{\prime}}(y,x)\in[\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}^{-1},\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}]
. Let us denote
\zeta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t):=\inf\{\mathbb{E}_{Q}[Y(t)|T=t^{\prime},X=x]:Q\in\mathcal{M}_{x}\}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{x}
is the set of all full data laws conditional on
X=x
that satisfy Assumptions
1
,
2
and the Marginal model
4
. Then the aforesaid restriction imposed by the marginal model provides an identification of
\zeta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)
, as illustrate in Theorem
2
and proved in Appendix
E.2
.

Theorem 2

(Partial Identification under Marginal Sensitivity Model)
.

Define
\rho^{t,t^{\prime}}_{\mu}(y):=\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}^{-1}(y-\mu)_{+}-\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}(y-\mu)_{-}.
If
|\zeta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)|<\infty
, then
\zeta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)
is of the form

\zeta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)=q_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)+\mathbb{E}[\rho_{q_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)}^{t,t^{\prime}}(Y)|T=t,X=x],

(4)

where
q_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)=Q_{Y|X=x,T=t}((\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}+1)^{-1})
, ie, the
(\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}+1)^{-1}
-th quantile of
Y|X=x,T=t
.

Remark 2
.

If
Y|T=t,X=x
is continuous, a simple calculation reveals that

\zeta_{t^{\prime}}(x,t)=\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}\mathbb{E}[Y|T=t,X=x]-(\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}-1)\mathbb{E}[Y|Y>q_{t^{\prime}}(x,t),T=t,X=x].

(5)

Thus if the above condition holds, Equation (
5
) could be used to estimate
\zeta
as a linear combination of the conditional mean, and the truncated tail mean function
\mathbb{E}[Y|Y>q_{t^{\prime}}(X,T),T,X]
function, sometimes referred to as the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVar) function (
Rockafellar et al. 2000
). It is interesting to note that Theorem
2
recovers the adversarial regression formulation of
Dorn et al. 2024
in the case of continuous exposure, despite our very distinct proof strategies. Note that both Theorem
1
and
2
use outcome regression formulations to identify the estimand of interest, which is a convenient strategy for continuous exposures. An adversarial propensity score construction strategy, like that in
Dorn and Guo 2023
, is difficult to apply in the continuous exposure setting, due to the appearance of Dirac-delta at
t
terms when attempting to partially identify
\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]
. Some recent work has considered kernel based localization to circumvent this issue (eg:
Colangelo and and 2025
) in the context of NUC; but we defer exploring such an approach in our setting to future work. Although we emphasize that while the identification strategies in Theorems
1
and
2
bypass the need to incorporate propensity scores (ie, conditional density in this case), they still play a key-role in efficiently estimating the estimands defining our bounds, as highlighted in the following sections.

4
Debiased Mapping through pseudo-outcome formulation

Let
r(t)=\mathbb{E}[\theta_{T}(X,t)]
and
m(t)=\mathbb{E}[\zeta_{T}(X,t)]
be the lower bounds on
\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]
under the Rosenbaum and Marginal models respectively. If
r(t)
and
m(t)
were parametrized by finite-dimensional structures, standard tools from semi-parametric efficiency theory (

;
Tsiatis 2006
;
Hines et al. 2022

is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and no
\sqrt{n}
-consistent estimators can exist (
Bickel et al. 1993
;
Díaz and van der Laan 2013
).

To address this challenge, one can adopt a pseudo-outcome approach analogous to
Kennedy et al. 2017
;
Yang et al. 2023
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2024
. We define
\psi_{R}=\mathbb{E}[r(T)]
and
\psi_{M}=\mathbb{E}[m(T)]
, where the outer expectations are taken with respect to the marginal distribution
F_{T}
of the observed exposure. Unlike
r(t)
and
m(t)
, the population level functionals
\psi_{R}
and
\psi_{M}
are now both pathwise-differentiable under standard regularity conditions and therefore admit influence functions under an appropriately defined semiparametric model, which can in turn be used to define so-called pseudo-outcomes as corresponding un-centered influence functions. The key insight of pseudo-outcomes is that under conditions of Theorem 2 of
Yang et al. 2023
, the influence function-based estimators of
\psi_{R}
and
\psi_{M}
inherit a conditional second-order bias property even for their conditional counterparts, such as
r(t)
and
m(t)
, thereby providing improved estimators for such nonregular, i.e. non
\sqrt{n}
-estimable, functionals.

5
Estimation and Inference

In this section, we elaborate on the non-parametric OLS regression of Step 2 in Section
4
, and provide theoretical guarantees for the convergence of
\hat{r}(t)
and
\hat{m}(t)
to their respective population analogues.

Let
\lambda
denote the Lebesgue measure, and let
\Psi=\{\phi_{1}(\cdot)\equiv 1,\phi_{2}(\cdot),\phi_{3}(\cdot),\cdots\}
be a sequence of functions such that linear combination of these functions are dense in
L_{2}(\lambda)
. Examples of such sequences include polynomial series, Fourier series, regression splines (
Huang 2003
), local polynomial partition series (
Cattaneo and Farrell 2013
), wavelet series, etc. The idea is to use these basis functions to approximate any
L^{2}
function from the sample at hand. For any
J\geq 1
, let
\bar{\phi}_{J}=(\phi_{1},\cdots,\phi_{J})
be a collection of the first
J
basis functions. Also, for any
f\in L^{2}(\lambda)
, define
E_{J}^{\Psi}(f):=\inf_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{J}}\|f-b^{T}\bar{\phi}_{J}\|_{L^{2}(\lambda)}
as the error of approximating
f
by the first
J
functions of
\Psi
. The choice of a dense basis ensures that
\mathbb{E}_{J}^{\Psi}(f)\to 0
as
J\to\infty
for any
f\in L^{2}(\lambda)
.

Let
Q=\mathbb{E}[\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)^{T}]
, and let
\hat{Q}_{|I_{1}|}=\frac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})^{T}
be its empirical counterpart in
I_{1}
. The non-parametric OLS estimator for
a(t)
,
a\in\{r,m\}
is given by

\hat{a}_{J}(t):=\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)^{T}\hat{\beta}_{a},

where

\hat{\beta}_{a}:=\arg\min_{b\in\mathbb{R}^{J}}\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}(\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i})-b^{T}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i}))^{2}=\hat{Q}_{|I_{1}|}\cdot\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i}),\ a\in\{r,m\}.

To consider the theoretical properties of this estimator, let us define
\xi_{J}:=\sup_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)\|
, which plays a key role in the choice of
J
. When
\mathcal{T}
is compact, for polynomial bases, we have
\xi_{J}\lesssim J
, whereas for bases like the Fourier series, splines, wavelets, local polynomial partitions etc.,
\xi_{J}\lesssim\sqrt{J}
(
Belloni et al. 2015
). We establish our results under the following technical conditions.

Assumption 5
.

(i)

\exists 0<l_{\min}\leq l_{\max}<\infty
such that the eigenvalues of
Q

l_{\max}

(ii)

(iii)

\xi_{J}^{2}\log J/|I_{1}|\to 0

(iv)

on

(v)

Y|T=t,X=x
admits a bounded density with repect to the Lebesgue measure.

Assumption
5
(i) ensures that the components of
\bar{\phi}_{J}
are not too co-linear, while maintaining that any linear combination of
\bar{\phi}_{J}
maintains a bounded variance. Such a stability condition can be simply ensured if the components of
\Psi
are orthonormal on
(\mathcal{T},\lambda)
, and

is bounded above and away from zero (
Belloni et al. 2015
). (ii) is a mild regularity condition required to quantify the
L^{2}
-error of estimation. (iii) quantifies the rate of growth of
J
in comparison to the sample size. Condition (i) and (iii) allows
\hat{Q}_{|I_{1}|}
to concentrate around

, and hence obtain necessary rates of consistency. We note that the requirement that
\xi_{J}^{2}\log J/|I_{1}|
could be relaxed to a lesser degree using potentially different learners than the OLS, for instance the Forster-Warmuth learner considered in
Yang et al. 2023
. Here, we primarily focus on the OLS regression due to its familiarity and well-established theoretical properties. Condition (iv) allows us to express the
L^{2}
rate of convergences in terms of the approximation bias
E_{J}^{\Psi}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

Condition (v) guarantees sufficient smoothness of the mapping
\hat{\theta}_{t^{\prime}}(X,T)\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\psi^{T,t^{\prime}}_{\theta_{t^{\prime}}(X,T)}(Y)|X,T]
or that of
q_{t^{\prime}}(X,T)\mapsto\mathbb{E}[\rho^{T,t^{\prime}}_{q_{t^{\prime}}(X,T)}(Y)|X,T]
. On closer inspection, the proof of forthcoming Theorems
5
and
6
show that Condition

(v) could be relaxed: if

and

, alongwith their estimates, have ranges

and
\mathcal{A}_{m}
respectively, then one could instead have
{\text{ess}\sup}_{X}\sup_{y\in\mathcal{A}_{a}(X)}f_{Y|T,X}(y|t,x)<\infty,
which is satisfied when

is binary and

, as the estimates

and
\hat{q}
will eventually be inside

and

would have no mass on any Borel set not containing 0 or 1.

7.2
Application: Effect of second hand smoke exposure on blood lead levels in children

In this subsection we apply our proposed methods to estimate the effect of second-hand smoke exposure on blood-lead levels in children. While the link between active smoking and elevated blood lead levels is well-established, we focus here on the impact of passive exposure—a critical public health question given the heightened vulnerability of children’s developing nervous systems. Elevated blood lead in children has been linked to reduced intelligence, stunted growth, and anaemia (
Council et al. 1993

Mannino et al. 2003
found an association between second-hand smoke exposure and higher blood lead levels using data from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). However, the observational nature of the data raises potential concerns about unmeasured confounding.
Zhang and Small 2020
addressed this by conducting a sensitivity analysis, finding strong evidence for a causal effect even after accounting for moderate unmeasured confounding. Their analysis used cotinine— a metabolite of nicotine— as a biomarker, dichotomizing its levels to define exposure.

We extend this line of work using more recent NHANES data from 2003–2004 and 2011–2016. Following
Mannino et al. 2003
, we restrict to children aged 4-16, and having cotinine levels at most 15.0 ng/mL to exclude active smokers, resulting in 6616 children to be included in our dataset. Measured confounders include poverty-income ratio, age, sex and number of rooms at home. Our method estimates the effect of relative changes in cotinine on blood lead levels. Plausible unmeasured confounders in our analysis includes school type (more active smokers or older lead-based paint in public school buildings
of Pediatrics Committee on Environmental Health
et al. 2005
), or source of drinking water (
Gibson et al. 2020
).

For lower cotinine levels, the NUC curve is tightly enveloped by the upper and lower bounds from both the Marginal and Rosenbaum sensitivity models, suggesting robustness to even strong unmeasured confounding. At higher cotinine levels, the bounds begin to widen, possibly due to increased heterogeneity in blood lead levels or smaller sample sizes (reflected in wider standard error estimates) in that exposure range.

8
Conclusion

This work extends the scope of sensitivity analysis in causal inference by introducing generalizations of the Rosenbaum and Marginal sensitivity models to the setting of continuous exposures. Our framework replaces classical scalar sensitivity parameters with sensitivity functions that vary with the exposure level, enabling richer modeling of exposure-confounder relationships and sharper identification bounds for the dose–response curve. In addition, we offer a flexible collection of sensitivity functions—each compatible with the observed data—allowing researchers to tailor their choice based on the contextual demands of the application.

We obtained novel partial identification results for the dose-response curve
\{\mathbb{E}[Y(t)]\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}}
. Our results indicate that both the sensitivity bounds depend on tail behavior of the outcome distribution– expectiles for the Rosenbaum model and quantiles for the Marginal models. We also establish influence function-based estimators for the sensitivity bounds using a debiased pseudo-outcome formulation, and the resulting estimators are shown to be consistent, achieve the minimax rate, and asymptotically normal under weaker second-order convergence rates of the nuisance functions.

A common theme we observe across both sensitivity models is that the resulting bounds depend on the tail behavior of the conditional outcome distribution—through expectiles, tail CDFs, quantiles, or CVaR. This matches our intuition — unmeasured confounding can cause the likelihoods of
Y(t)|T,X
and
Y|T,X
to differ markedly, with the worst-case departure quantified by the sensitivity model. The corresponding worst-case likelihood ratio therefore relates to the behavior of the outcome at the extremes, making the functionals tail-dependent.
This highlights a key challenge in estimating sensitivity bounds — as the gap between the bounding sensitivity functions widens, the estimand increasingly depends on tail properties of the outcome distribution, making nonparametric estimation more difficult and data-intensive.

We also provide a novel geometric connection between the Rosenbaum and Marginal models, relating to the diameter and radius of a set of density ratios. Moreover, we explore the practical implications of using one model over the other — the Rosenbaum model is more suited to global sensitivity statements, while the Marginal model provides a targeted approach in settings where one has auxillary information or substantive domain knowledge about the nature of the unmeasured confounder.

Overall, we hope that this work contributes toward a more flexible and interpretable toolkit for sensitivity analysis in the continuous treatment setting, and provides a foundation for future methodological and applied developments in this domain.

Appendix C
Bonvini-Kennedy-Ventura-Wasserman (BKVW) model on Bounding Density Ratios

Some recent works in the continuous exposure literature have considered bounding the density ratio directly, rather than the ratio of density ratios. One such model was introduced in
Bonvini et al. 2022
and
Jesson et al. 2022
and is given by the following:

BKVW model:
The law of
(T,X,U)
satisfies

\dfrac{1}{\Delta}\leq\dfrac{f_{T|U,X}(t|u,X)}{f_{T|X}(t|X)}\leq\Delta\ \ \forall t\in\mathcal{T},u\in\mathcal{U}.

(8)

Although adopting a relatively simple form, the BKVW model contains subtle interpretational challenges that need to be addressed. First, note that the sensitivity bound
\Delta
in Equation (
8
) is assumed to be uniform over all
t\in\mathcal{T}
. This uniformity can be difficult to satisfy, unless the exposure space
\mathcal{T}
is compact. While compactness is often a benign assumption in non-parametric inference, its explicit imposition in a sensitivity model complicates interpretation: the value of
\Delta
then becomes dependent on how the exposure space is truncated, which can obscure its meaning.

A potential solution one might consider is to allow the sensitivity bound to depend on
t
, ie,

\dfrac{1}{\Delta_{t}}\leq\dfrac{f_{T|U,X}(t|u,X)}{f_{T|X}(t|X)}\leq\Delta_{t}\ \forall t\in\mathcal{T},u\in\mathcal{U},\ \ \text{a.s. }X,

a deeper issue remains: the interpretation of the sensitivity parameter becomes entangled with the observed distribution of the exposure
T
. To illustrate this, consider the case of no observed covariates, and a joint distribution of

f_{T,U}(t,u)=f(t,u)g(u)f_{T}(t)/\delta_{T}(t),

where
\delta_{T}(t)
is chosen such that
f_{T,U}
is compatible with the observed distribution
f_{T}
of
T
.
Under such a model, we obtain,

\dfrac{f_{T|U}(t|u)}{f_{T}(t)}=\dfrac{f(t,u)/\delta_{T}(t)}{\mathbb{E}[f(T,u)/\delta_{T}(T)]}

Assuming
U
is compactly supported — a standard assumption in sensitivity analyses — the right hand side is uniformly bounded in
u
. However, this implies that the sensitivity function in the BKVW model depends not only on the strength of confounding through
f(t,u)
, but also on the observed distribution of the exposure
T
via the denominator
\mathbb{E}[f(T,u)/\delta_{T}(t)]
, where
T\sim f_{T}(\cdot)
. In particular, the interpretation of the sensitivity parameter is no longer solely about the unmeasured confounding structure—it also hinges on the behavior of the marginal distribution of
T
. This dual dependence complicates the interpretability of the BKVW model in practice.

To see this in action, consider a treatment
T^{*}
, and
T
to be a truncation of
T^{*}
to the region
[-B,B]
. Such truncations are often common to remove outliers, or as technical non-parametric regression assumptions, as in Assumption
5
, and are generally considered benign. However, the interpretation of the BKVW model becomes tricky under such truncations. To consider a concrete example, suppose
T^{*}|U=u
has an exponential distribution with rate
\gamma U
, and
U\sim[0,1]
. Let
T
be the truncation of
T^{*}
to
[0,B]
(ie, we discard any

\dfrac{f_{T|U}(t|u)}{f_{T}(t)}=\left(\dfrac{1-\frac{1-e^{-\gamma B}}{\gamma B}}{1-e^{-\gamma uB}}\right)\left(\dfrac{\gamma^{2}t^{2}ue^{-\gamma ut}}{1-(\gamma t+1)e^{-\gamma t}}\right).

Analogous expressions appear if

truncated at
\pm B

\dfrac{f_{T|U}(t|u)}{f_{T}(t)}=\dfrac{\mathbb{E}[\Phi(B-\gamma U)-\Phi(-B-\gamma U)]}{\Phi(B-\gamma u)-\Phi(-B-\gamma u)}\cdot\dfrac{\gamma\phi(t-\gamma u)}{\Phi(t)-\Phi(t-\gamma)}.

The first terms here depend non-monotonically on

Proof of Theorem

As in Appendix

denote the space of all non-negative real valued measurable functions

, which is sufficient to consider based on Lemma
2

Hence we need to obtain

\mathcal{M}_{t,t^{\prime}}
is convex, non-empty (since it contains the constant 1 function), and has a non-empty intersection with the feasibility restriction
\mathbb{E}[L_{t,t^{\prime}}(Y(t))|T=t]=1
again via the constant 1 function, we apply strong duality to obtain:

\zeta_{t,t^{\prime}}(y)=\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\inf_{L\in\mathcal{M}_{t,t^{\prime}}}\{\mathbb{E}[(Y-\mu)L(Y)|T=t]+\mu\}.

\mu\in\mathbb{R}

and
\Lambda_{t,t^{\prime}}^{-1}

, since any other function

\displaystyle=\int(\hat{f}(t^{\prime}|x)-f(t^{\prime}|x))^{4}\dfrac{f_{T}(t^{\prime})}{f_{T|X}(t^{\prime}|x)}dF_{T|X}(t^{\prime})dF_{X}(x)

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{1-p_{\text{min}}}{p_{\text{min}}}\int(\hat{f}(t^{\prime}|x)-f(t^{\prime}|x))^{4}dF_{T|X}(t^{\prime})dF_{X}(x)\lesssim\|\hat{f}(T|X)-f(T|X)\|^{4}_{4|\hat{h}}

Collecting the respective components, and noting that
\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}d_{K}^{2}}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}|d_{K}|
, the proof is complete.
\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem

: Pointwise Asymptotic Normality

Recall that,

\hat{a}_{J}(t)=\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)^{T}\hat{Q}_{|I_{1}|}^{-1}\cdot\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i}),

\hat{Q}_{|I_{1}|}=\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})^{T}
, and
a\in\{r,m\}
. Consider the estimator which one could have obtained if the pseudo-outcome
Y_{a}(w)
were known —

a_{J}(t):=\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)^{T}\hat{Q}_{|I_{1}|}^{-1}\cdot\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})Y_{a}(W_{i}).

Then, one can write

\sqrt{|I_{1}|}(\hat{a}_{J}(t)-a(t))=\sqrt{|I_{1}|}(\hat{a}_{J}(t)-a_{J}(t))+\sqrt{|I_{1}|}(a_{J}(t)-a(t)).

(36)

Now,

\displaystyle\hat{a}_{J}(t)-a_{J}(t)

\displaystyle=\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)^{T}\hat{Q}^{-1}_{|I_{1}|}\cdot\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})(\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i})-Y_{a}(W_{i}))

\displaystyle=\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)^{T}\hat{Q}^{-1}_{|I_{1}|}\cdot\left(\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})(\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i})-Y_{a}(W_{i}))-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}(\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W)))\right)

(
EP
)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)^{T}\hat{Q}^{-1}_{|I_{1}|}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}(\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W))).

(
Bias
)

We shall control the empirical process term (
EP
) and the bias term (
Bias
) separately. This will allow us to demonstrate that the asymptotic normality is driven by the first term in Equation (
36
), henceforth referred to as the driving term.

Step 1: Empirical Process Term

To control the empirical process term, note that

\displaystyle\left|\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)^{T}\hat{Q}_{|I_{1}|}^{-1}\cdot\left(\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})(\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i})-Y_{a}(W_{i}))-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}(\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W)))\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)\|\left\|\hat{Q}_{|I_{1}|}^{-1}\cdot\left(\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})(\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i})-Y_{a}(W_{i}))-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}(\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W)))\right)\right\|.

(37)

Now, since
\xi_{J}^{2}\log J/n\to 0
by Assumption
5
(iii), one can apply matrix Law of Large numbers (Lemma 6.2 of
Belloni et al. 2015
) to obtain that
\|\hat{Q}-Q\|\overset{p}{\to}0
. Since all eigen values of
Q=\mathbb{E}[\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)^{T}]
are bounded away from 0, by following the argument of the proof of Theorem 4.1 of
Belloni et al. 2015
, one can obtain from Equation (
37
) that

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{Q}_{|I_{1}|}^{-1}\cdot\left(\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})(\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i})-Y_{a}(W_{i}))-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}(\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W)))\right)\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{P}\left\|\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})(\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i})-Y_{a}(W_{i}))-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}(\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W)))\right\|.

(38)

Since the right hand side of Equation (
38
), conditional on
\hat{h}
, is a mean of iid terms, we can thus apply Lemma 2 of the supplementary materials of
Kennedy et al. 2020
to obtain

\left\|\hat{Q}_{|I_{1}|}^{-1}\cdot\left(\dfrac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in I_{1}}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T_{i})(\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i})-Y_{a}(W_{i}))-\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}(\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W)))\right)\right\|\lesssim_{P}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{|I_{1}|}}\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}-Y_{a})\|_{2|\hat{h}}.

Now,
\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}-Y_{a})\|_{2|\hat{h}}\leq\sup_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)\|\|\hat{Y}_{a}-Y_{a}\|_{2|\hat{h}}\leq\xi_{J}\|\hat{Y}_{a}-Y_{a}\|_{2|\hat{h}}
. Moreover,
\|\bar{\phi}(t)\|\leq\lambda_{\max}(\Omega)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|s(t)\|
. Thus,
we obtain that the empirical process term,

\ref{ep}=O_{p}\left(\dfrac{\|s(t)\|\xi_{J}}{\sqrt{|I_{1}|}}\|\hat{Y}_{a}-Y_{a}\|_{2|\hat{h}}\right).

(39)

Since
\|\hat{h}-h\|_{2|\hat{h}}=o_{p}(1/\xi_{J})
, a quick calculation for
a\in\{r,m\}
reveals
\xi_{J}\|\hat{Y}_{a}-Y_{a}\|_{2|\hat{h}}\lesssim_{P}\xi_{J}\|\hat{h}-h\|_{2|\hat{h}}=o_{p}(1)
, and thus from Equation (
39
),
\ref{ep}=o_{p}(\|s(t)\|/\sqrt{|I_{1}|})
.

Step 2: Bias Term

To control the bias term (
Bias
), we note that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}[\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W))]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}[\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\mathbb{E}_{T}[\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W)]]=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}[\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(H_{h}^{a}(T)-a(T)))].

Also, using the same matrix LLN argument as while controlling the empirical process term, we can bound the bias term by the following.

Bias

\displaystyle\leq\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)\|\|\hat{Q}^{-1}_{|I_{1}|}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}(\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W)))\|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{P}\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)\|\|\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}(\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W))\|\leq\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)\|\|\mathbb{E}_{\hat{h}}[\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)(H_{h}^{a}(T)-a(T))]\|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{P}\|s(t)\|\|\phi_{J}(T)\|_{2}\|H_{h}(T)-m(T)\|_{2|\hat{h}}

(40)

Now,

\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)^{T}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)]=\text{tr}(Q)\leq Jl_{\max}\implies\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\|_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{J}.

(41)

Following steps analogous to Step 2 of Appendix
G.3
, and using Equation (
41
) we thus obtain
\ref{bp}=o_{p}(\|s(t)\|/\sqrt{|I_{1}|})
.

Step 3: Driving term

For
\sqrt{|I|_{1}}(a_{J}(t)-a(t))
we can apply the asymptotic normality result by
Belloni et al. 2015
,

\dfrac{\sqrt{|I_{1}|}}{\|s(t)\|}(m_{J}(t)-m(t))\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

(42)

if the following conditions hold:

(i)

(Invertibility and finiteness condition) The eigen-values of
Q=\mathbb{E}[\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)]^{T}
are bounded above and away from 0.

(ii)

(Lindeberg condition) With
\varepsilon_{a}=Y_{a}(W)-a(T)
is such that

\sup_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{a}^{2}\mathbbm{1}(|\varepsilon_{a}|>M)|T=t]\to 0\text{ as }M\to\infty.

(iii)

(Non-degeneracy condition)
\exists C>0
such that
\inf_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{a}^{2}|T=t]\geq C.

(iv)

(Basis condition)
\sqrt{\dfrac{\xi_{J}^{2}\log J}{n}}(1+\sqrt{J}l_{J})\to 0.

(v)

(Under-smoothing) Let
e(t)=a(t)-\bar{\phi}_{J}(t)^{T}\beta^{a}_{J}
. Then,
\sqrt{n}e(t)=o(\|s(t)\|).

(i) holds by Assumption
5
(i).

To show (ii), note that

\mathbb{E}[|\varepsilon_{a}|^{2+\delta}|T=t]\geq\mathbb{E}[|\varepsilon_{a}|^{2+\delta}\mathbbm{1}(|\varepsilon_{a}|>M)|T=t]\geq M^{\delta}\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{a}^{2}\mathbbm{1}(|\varepsilon_{a}|>M)|T=t],

and hence for (ii) to hold it is enough that
\sup_{t}\|\varepsilon_{a}\|_{2+\delta|t}
is finite. Now, by Minkowski’s inequality, we obtain,

\|\varepsilon_{a}\|_{2+\delta|T=t}=\|Y_{a}(W)-a(T)\|_{2+\delta|T=t}\leq\|Y_{a}(W)\|_{2+\delta|T=t}+a(t).

Now, if
a=r
,

\displaystyle\|Y_{r}(W)\|_{2+\delta|T=t}\leq\|\theta_{T}(X,t)\|_{2+\delta}+\left\|\dfrac{f_{T}(t)}{f_{T|X}(t|X)}\int\dfrac{\psi^{t,t^{\prime}}_{\theta_{t^{\prime}}(X,t)}(Y)}{\nu_{t^{\prime}}(X,t)}f_{T|X}(t^{\prime}|X)dt^{\prime}\right\|_{2+\delta|T=t}

Next note that
f_{T|X}
and hence
f_{T}
are bounded above and below uniformly in
\mathcal{T}
, and
\nu_{t^{\prime}}(X,t)\geq 1
. Moreover,
\psi^{t,t^{\prime}}_{\theta}(Y)\leq\Gamma_{t,t^{\prime}}(y-\theta)
, we thus have, the second term to be

\lesssim_{P}\|Y-\theta_{t^{\prime}}(X,t)\|_{2+\delta|T=t}\leq\|Y\|_{2+\delta|T=t}+\|\theta_{t^{\prime}}(X,t)\|_{2+\delta|T=t}.

(ii) thus follows by taking supremum over
t
, and considering Assumption
6
(ii). One can show
\sup_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\|Y_{m}(W)\|_{2+\delta|T=t}
analogously under Assumption
6
(ii).

To show (iii), first let’s consider when
a=r
. Then,

Y_{r}(W)-r(T)=\dfrac{1}{|I_{2}|}\sum_{j\in I_{2}}(\theta_{T_{j}}(X_{j},T)-\mathbb{E}_{T}[\theta_{T_{j}}(X_{j},T)])+\mathbb{E}_{T}[\theta_{T_{j}}(X_{j},T)]+\dfrac{f(T)}{f(T|X)}\int\dfrac{\psi^{T,t^{\prime}}_{\theta_{t^{\prime}}(X,T)}(Y)}{\nu_{t^{\prime}}(X,T)}f(t^{\prime}|X)dt^{\prime}.

\text{Var}[Y_{r}(W)-r(T)|T=t]\geq\text{Var}(\theta_{T}(X,t))>0

(iv) holds by Assumptions
5

6

Thus, Equation (

Proof of Theorem
7
:

Let
\hat{Z}=\hat{\varepsilon}_{a}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)
, and
Z=\varepsilon_{a}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)
, where
\varepsilon_{a}=Y_{a}(W)-a(T)
, and
\hat{\varepsilon}_{a}=\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-\hat{a}_{J}(T)
. Furthermore, define
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{a}=Y_{a}(W)-a_{J}(T)
, and
\tilde{Z}=\tilde{\varepsilon}_{a}\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)
.

We shall use the notation
\mathbb{E}_{n}
to denote the empirical average, as
\mathbb{E}_{n}Z=\frac{1}{|I_{1}|}\sum_{i\in|I_{1}|}Z_{i}
, and
\mathbb{E}Z=\mathbb{E}[Z|\hat{h}]
. Then, we have,

\mathbb{E}_{n}\hat{Z}\hat{Z}^{T}-\mathbb{E}ZZ^{T}=\mathbb{E}_{n}(\hat{Z}\hat{Z}^{T}-\tilde{Z}\tilde{Z}^{T})+\mathbb{E}_{n}\tilde{Z}\tilde{Z}^{T}-\mathbb{E}ZZ^{T}.

Now, by
Belloni et al. 2015
Theorem 4.6, we already have

\|\mathbb{E}_{n}\tilde{Z}\tilde{Z}^{T}-\mathbb{E}ZZ^{T}\|\lesssim_{P}(|I_{1}|^{1/(2+\delta)}+l_{J})\sqrt{\dfrac{\xi_{J}^{2}\log\xi_{J}}{|I_{1}|}}.

Thus, we need to control the term
\mathbb{E}_{n}(\hat{Z}\hat{Z}^{T}-\tilde{Z}\tilde{Z}^{T}).
Note that,

(\hat{Z}\hat{Z}^{T}-\tilde{Z}\tilde{Z}^{T})=\phi_{J}(T)\phi_{J}(T)^{T}[(\hat{Y}_{a}-\hat{a}_{J}(T))^{2}-(Y_{a}-a_{J}(T))^{2}].

Also,

\displaystyle(\hat{Y}_{a}-\hat{a}_{J}(T))^{2}-(Y_{a}-a_{J}(T))^{2}

\displaystyle=[(\hat{Y}_{a}-\hat{a}_{J}(T))-(Y_{a}-a_{J}(T))]^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+2(Y_{a}-a_{J}(T))[(\hat{Y}_{a}-\hat{a}_{J}(T))-(Y_{a}-a_{J}(T))]

\displaystyle\leq 2\left[(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W))^{2}+|(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W))(Y_{a}(W)-a_{J}(T))|\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\left.(\hat{a}_{J}(T)-a_{J}(T))^{2}+|(Y_{a}(W)-a_{J}(T))(\hat{a}_{J}(T)-a_{J}(T))|\right].

Thus,

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{E}_{n}(\hat{Z}\hat{Z}^{T}-\tilde{Z}\tilde{Z}^{T})\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{n}\|\hat{Z}\hat{Z}^{T}-\tilde{Z}\tilde{Z}^{T}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{n}\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)^{T}\|(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W))^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+2\mathbb{E}_{n}\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)^{T}\||(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W))(Y_{a}(W)-a_{J}(T))|

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+2\mathbb{E}_{n}\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)^{T}\|(\hat{a}_{J}(T)-a_{J}(T))^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt+2\mathbb{E}_{n}\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)^{T}\||(Y_{a}(W)-a_{J}(T))(\hat{a}_{J}(T)-a_{J}(T))|.

The third and fourth terms are
\lesssim_{P}(|I_{1}|^{1/(2+\delta)}+l_{J})\sqrt{\dfrac{\xi_{J}^{2}\log J}{|I_{1}|}}
, as established in the proof of Theorem 4.6 in
Belloni et al. 2015
. The first term can be controlled by

\displaystyle E_{n}\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)^{T}\|(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W))^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i\in I_{1}}(\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i})-Y_{a}(W_{i}))^{2}\mathbb{E}_{n}\|\phi_{J}\phi_{J}(T)^{T}\|

\displaystyle\lesssim_{P}\|\hat{h}-h\|^{2}_{\infty|\hat{h}}O_{p}(1)\lesssim_{P}\|\hat{h}-h\|^{2}_{\infty|\hat{h}}.

For the second term,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)^{T}\||(\hat{Y}_{a}(W)-Y_{a}(W))(Y_{a}(W)-a_{J}(T))|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i\in I_{1}}|(\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i})-Y_{a}(W_{i}))(Y_{a}(W_{i})-a_{J}(T_{i}))|\mathbb{E}_{n}\|\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)\bar{\phi}_{J}(T)^{T}\|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i\in I_{1}}|\hat{Y}_{a}(W_{i})-Y_{a}(W_{i})|\max_{j\in I_{1}}|Y_{a}(W_{i})-a_{J}(T_{i})|O_{p}(1)\lesssim_{P}\max_{i\in|I_{1}|}|\hat{h}(W_{i})-h(W_{i})|(|I_{1}|^{\frac{1}{2+\delta}}+l_{J})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{h}(|I_{1}|^{\frac{1}{2+\delta}}+l_{J}).

Combining all the terms, we obtain

\|\mathbb{E}_{n}\hat{Z}\hat{Z}^{T}-\mathbb{E}ZZ^{T}\|\lesssim_{P}(|I_{1}|^{\frac{1}{2+\delta}}+l_{J})\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{\xi_{J}^{2}\log J}{|I_{1}|}}+\lambda_{h}\right)+\|\hat{h}-h\|^{2}_{\infty|\hat{h}}.

The proof is thus complete by noting that
\|\hat{Q}-Q\|\lesssim_{P}\sqrt{\dfrac{\xi_{J}^{2}\log\xi_{J}}{|I_{1}|}}
, and the definitions of
\Omega
and
\hat{\Omega}
.
\blacksquare
