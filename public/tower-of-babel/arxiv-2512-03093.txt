New Identity for Cayley’s First Hyperdeterminant with Applications to Symmetric Tensors and Entanglement
Isaac Dobes

Abstract
In this article, a new formula for computing Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant in terms of the Levi-Civita symbol is given. It is then shown that this formula can be used to compute the hyperdeterminant of symmetric hypermatrices in polynomial time with respect to their order (assuming fixed side length). Applications to the quantum entanglement of bosons are then discussed. Additionally, in order to obtain the fast calculation of the hyperdeterminant on symmetric hypermatrices, hypermatrix generalizations of elimination and duplication matrices are defined, and explicit formulas for them are derived in the appendix of this article.

Introduction

Originally introduced in 1844 by Sir Arthur Cayley
[
6
]
, Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant (aka the combinatorial hyperdeterminant) was nearly forgotten for the next 150 years. Indeed, only in the last 30 years has there been significant research interest into the applications of Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant, and in this time numerous applications to both mathematics and physics have been uncovered! For example, in 1997 P. Zappa expressed Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant in terms of the difference in even and odd Latin squares of order
N

[
25
]
, in 2003 J. G. Luque and J. Y. Thibon relate Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant of Hankel hypermatrices to Selberg Integrals
[
16
]
, in 2008 S. Matsumoto expresses a particular type of Jack function in terms of Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant
[
19
]
, in 2021 P. Lammers utilizes Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant to characterize a generalized Kasteleyn theory
[
14
]
, also in 2021 A. Amanov and D. Yeliussizov used Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant to establish lower bounds of certain tensor ranks
[
1
]
, and in 2024/2025 I. Dobes and N. Jing prove that Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant generalizes the concurrence, an important entanglement measure in quantum information
[
9
,
10
]
. Therefore, while one may initially consider Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant to be a naive generalization of the usual determinant, it is in fact an important mathematical object with a wide variety of applications.

In this paper, we derive a new formula for computing Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant in terms of the Levi-Civita symbol. In particular, we show that if
\mathcal{A}
is an arbitrary cubical hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
, then Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant of
\mathcal{A}
is equal to the multilinear matrix product

\frac{1}{d!}\bigotimes\limits_{k=1}^{N}\epsilon*(\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A}),...,\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})),

where
\epsilon
denotes the
d
-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol, ”
\bigotimes
” denotes the tensor Kronecker product, and
\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})
denotes the
(\mathbf{n},1)
-matricization (i.e. a hypermatrix vectorization) of
\mathcal{A}
. The major benefit to this new identity for computing Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant applies to symmetric hypermatrices.

In general, the run time cost for computing Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant of an arbitrary cubical hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
is exponential in both
N
and
d

[
13
,
2
]
. However, after generalizing the notions of half-vectorization and duplication matrices to symmetric hypermatrices of order
N
with side length
d
, it is shown in this article that the run time cost for computing Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant on symmetric hypermatrices is in fact polynomial in the order
N
(assuming
d
fixed). We then discuss how this result can be used to efficiently calculate the
2n
-way entanglement of
2n
-qudit bosons (which are quantum states whose corresponding hypermatrix representation is symmetric). Lastly in the appendix, we derive an explicit formula for the generalized duplication matrix, which the fast calculation of the hyperdeterminant on symmetric hypermatrices depends on.

\mathcal{A}*(X^{(1)}Y^{(1)},...,X^{(N)}Y^{(N)})=[\mathcal{A}*(X^{(1)},...,X^{(N)})]*(Y^{(1)},...,Y^{(N)}).

3
Vectorization of Hypermatrices

3.1
Canonical Vectorization of a Hypermatrix

Recall that for an arbitrary
m\times n
matrix

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}a_{11}&a_{12}&...&a_{n1}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}&...&a_{n2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{m1}&a_{n2}&...&a_{mn}\end{array}\right],

the vectorization of
A
is given by

\mathrm{vec}(A)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a_{11}\\
a_{21}\\
\vdots\\
a_{m1}\\
a_{12}\\
a_{22}\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
a_{mn}\end{array}\right].

Note in particular that the entries of
A
in
\mathrm{vec}(A)
are ordered in the reflected lexicographic order. Moreover, note that we may express
A
as the sum

A=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{m,n}a_{ij}e_{i}\circ e_{j}

where ”
\circ
” denotes the outer product,
e_{i}\in F^{m}
, and
e_{j}\in F^{n}
. It can be checked by direct computation that
e_{i}\otimes e_{j}
gives the
\big(j+n(i-1)\big)^{th}
-element in the ordered basis of
F^{mn}
, hence

\mathrm{vec}(A)=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{m,n}a_{ij}e_{j}\otimes e_{i}=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{m,n}(A^{t})_{ij}e_{i}\otimes e_{j}.

Now, let
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{N}j_{1}...j_{M}}]\in F^{n_{1}\times...\times n_{N}\times m_{1}\times...\times m_{M}}
,
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},...,i_{N})
,
\mathbf{j}=(j_{1},...,j_{M})
,
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},...,n_{N})
,
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},...,m_{M})
,
|n|=n_{1}\cdot...\cdot n_{N}
, and
|m|=m_{1}\cdot...\cdot m_{M}
. Recall that the
(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m})
-
matricization
of
\mathcal{A}

[
7
]
is the
|n|\times|m|
matrix
M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{m}}
such that

\left(M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{n}\times\mathbf{m}}\right)_{\psi(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{n}),\psi(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{m})}=\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{i}\mathbf{j}}=a_{i_{1}...i_{N}j_{1}...j_{M}},

with

\psi(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{n})=i_{1}+n_{1}(i_{2}-1)+...+n_{1}\cdot...\cdot n_{N-1}(i_{N}-1)

and similarly

\psi(\mathbf{j},\mathbf{m})=j_{1}+m_{1}(j_{2}-1)+...+m_{1}\cdot...\cdot m_{M-1}(j_{M}-1).

Therefore in particular, if
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}]\in F^{n_{1}\times...\times n_{N}}
, the
(\mathbf{n},1)
-matricization of
\mathcal{A}
turns
\mathcal{A}
into the vector

M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{n}\times 1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a_{11...1}\\
a_{21...1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{n_{1}1...1}\\
a_{12...1}\\
a_{22...1}\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
a_{n_{1}n_{2}...n_{N}}\end{array}\right]=:\mathrm{hvec}(A)

As with the usual vectorization operator on hypermatrices, this gives us a vector consisting of the entries of
\mathcal{A}
ordered in the reflected lexicographic order, thus yielding a natural generalization of the usual vectorization operator on matrices applied to hypermatrices. For this reason, we denote this particular vectorization process for the hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}
as
\mathrm{hvec}(A)
, with the ”h” part being short for ”hypermatrix.” Furthermore, we note the following observation:

Fact 1
.

Let
e_{i_{1}}\in F^{n_{1}}
,
e_{i_{2}}\in F^{n_{2}}
,…, and
e_{i_{N}}\in F^{n_{N}}
. Then
e_{i_{1}}\otimes e_{i_{2}}\otimes...\otimes e_{i_{N}}
is the

\big(i_{N}+n_{N}(i_{N-1}-1)+...+n_{N}n_{N-1}...n_{2}(i_{1}-1)\big)^{th}

element in the standard ordered basis of
F^{|n|}
. Consequently,

e_{i_{N}}\otimes...\otimes e_{i_{2}}\otimes e_{i_{1}}=e_{\mathbf{\psi(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{n})}}\in F^{|n|}.

In summary, we have the following definition

Definition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}]\in F^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times...\times n_{N}}
,
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},...,i_{N})
,
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},...,n_{N})
, and
|n|=n_{1}\cdot...\cdot n_{N}
. The
canonical hypermatrix vectorization
of
\mathcal{A}
is the

\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A}):=M_{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbf{n}\times 1}=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{i}\in F^{n_{1}\times...\times n_{N}}}\mathcal{A}_{\mathbf{i}}e_{\psi(\mathbf{i},\mathbf{n})}=\sum\limits_{i_{1},...,i_{N}=1}^{n_{1},...,n_{N}}a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}e_{i_{N}}\otimes...\otimes e_{i_{i}}.

Equivalently,

\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})=\sum\limits_{i_{1},...,i_{N}=1}^{n_{N},...,n_{1}}(\mathcal{A}^{\pi_{T}})_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}e_{i_{1}}\otimes...\otimes e_{i_{N}},

where
\pi_{T}
is the permutation given by the following product of transpositions:

(1N)(2(N-1))...\left(\left\lfloor\frac{N}{2}\right\rfloor\left\lceil\frac{N+1}{2}\right\rceil\right).

For convenience, we call
\pi_{T}
the
generalized transpose
of

.

The latter equation will be useful in the next section. Now, note that the run time cost of preparing
\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})
given

is
\mathcal{O}\big((n_{1}...n_{N})\big)
since each of its

3.2
Generalizing the Half Vectorization Operator to Symmetric Hypermatrices

For an arbitrary symmetric matrix
A=[a_{ij}]_{d\times d}\in F^{d\times d}
, we have that
a_{ij}=a_{ji}
for each
1\leq i,j\leq d
, and so listing only the entries in the set
\{a_{ij}:d\geq i\geq j\geq 1\}
yields the same amount information as
\mathrm{vec}(A)
. Thus, we have the notion of ”half-vectorization” for symmetric matrices, where only the entries in the aforementioned set are listed; i.e., recall the half-vectorization of
A
is defined to be

\mathrm{vech}(A):=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a_{11}\\
a_{21}\\
\vdots\\
a_{d1}\\
a_{22}\\
\vdots\\
a_{2d}\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
a_{d-1,d-1}\\
a_{d-1,d}\\
a_{dd}\end{array}\right].

Note that
\mathrm{vech}(A)\in F^{\frac{d(d+1)}{2}}
, or equivalently stated:
\mathrm{vech}(A)\in F^{\binom{d+1}{2}}
.

We can extend the notion of a half-vectorization to symmetric hypermatrices. Recall that a cubical hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}]\in F^{d\times...\times d}
of order
N
with side length
d
is a
symmetric
if

a_{i_{\sigma(1)}...i_{\sigma(N)}}=a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}\qquad\forall\sigma\in S_{N}.

For example, if
\mathcal{A}
is a symmetric cubical matrix of order
3
with side length
3
then it is of the following form:

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|ccc|ccc}a_{111}&a_{211}&a_{311}&a_{211}&a_{221}&a_{321}&a_{311}&a_{321}&a_{331}\\
a_{211}&a_{221}&a_{321}&a_{221}&a_{222}&a_{322}&a_{321}&a_{322}&a_{332}\\
a_{311}&a_{321}&a_{331}&a_{321}&a_{322}&a_{332}&a_{311}&a_{332}&a_{333}\\
\end{array}\right].

Therefore, a natural way to generalize the half-vectorization of
\mathcal{A}
would be to define it as the following vector

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a_{111}\\
a_{211}\\
a_{311}\\
a_{221}\\
a_{321}\\
a_{331}\\
a_{222}\\
a_{322}\\
a_{332}\\
a_{333}\end{array}\right],

which we’ll call the
1/3^{rd}
-hypermatrix vectorization of
\mathcal{A}
and denote it as
\mathrm{hvec}_{1/3}(\mathcal{A})
.

We may generalize this further to symmetric hypermatrices of arbitrary order. In particular, since

a_{i_{\sigma(1)}...i_{\sigma(N)}}=a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}

for every
\sigma\in S_{N}
, the order of the subscripts in each entry
\mathcal{A}
does not matter, only value that each subscript takes. We may therefore define the equivalence relation:
a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}\sim a_{j_{1}...j_{N}}
if and only if there exists
\sigma\in S_{N}
such that

j_{k}=i_{\sigma(k)}

for each
1\leq k\leq N
. Each equivalence class may be represented by the entry
a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}
with
i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq...\geq i_{N}
. Then each of these equivalence class representatives represents a (potentially) unique element of
\mathcal{A}
, and so therefore a natural way to generalize the half-vectorization to symmetric cubical hypermatrices would be to form a column vector consisting of these equivalence class representatives ordered in the reflected lexicographic way from least to greatest. In summary,

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}]\in F^{d\times...\times d}
be a symmetric hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
. Then the
1/N^{th}
-hypermatrix vectorization
of
\mathcal{A}
, denoted as
\mathrm{hvec}_{1/N}(\mathcal{A})
, is defined to be the vector consisting of the equivalence class representatives mentioned above, ordered in the reflected lexicographic way from least to greatest. In other words,

\mathrm{hvec}_{1/N}(\mathcal{A}):=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a_{111...11}\\
a_{211...11}\\
\vdots\\
a_{d11...11}\\
a_{221...11}\\
\vdots\\
a_{d21...11}\\
a_{331...11}\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
a_{dd1...11}\\
a_{dd2...11}\\
\vdots\\
a_{ddd...11}\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
\vdots\\
a_{ddd...d-1,d-1}\\
a_{ddd...d-1,d}\\
a_{ddd...dd}\end{array}\right].

Fact 2
.

The length of
\mathrm{hvec}_{1/N}(\mathcal{A})
is
\binom{d+N-1}{N}
; that is,
\mathrm{hvec}_{1/N}(\mathcal{A})\in F^{\binom{d+N-1}{N}}
.

This fact follows from the fact that each symmetric cubical hypermatrix represents a unique symmetric tensor; alternatively, one may directly prove this fact using the ”stars and bars” counting method.

4
Main Result

4.1
Cayley’s First Hyperdeterminant in Terms of the Levi-Civita Symbol

Recall that for a cubical hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}]\in F^{d\times...\times d}
of order
N
,
Cayley’s First hyperdeterminant
(aka the combinatorial hyperdeterminant) of is defined as

\frac{1}{d!}\sum\limits_{\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{N}\in S_{d}}\left(\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\right)\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}a_{\sigma_{1}(i)...\sigma_{N}(i)}=:\mathrm{hdet}(A).

Note in particular that for odd order hypermatrices, the combinatorial hyperdeterminant is identically zero
0
, but for even order hypermatrices the hyperdeterminant is equal to the function

\sum\limits_{\sigma_{2},...,\sigma_{N}\in S_{d}}\left(\prod\limits_{k=2}^{N}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\right)\prod\limits_{i=1}^{d}a_{i\sigma_{2}(i)...\sigma_{N}(i)}

which in general is non-trivial
[
15
]
.

Recall that the
d
-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol
\epsilon=[\epsilon_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{d}}]\in F^{d\times...\times d}
, an important tensor in geometry and physics
[
3
]
, is the order
d
cubical hypermatrix with side length
d
given by

\epsilon_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{d}}=\begin{cases}\begin{cases}1&\text{if }(i_{1}i_{2}...i_{d})\text{ is an even permutation of }(12...d)\\
-1&\text{if }(i_{1}i_{2}...i_{d})\text{ is an odd permutation of }(12...d)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\end{cases}

That is, if

\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&2&...&d\\
i_{1}&i_{2}&...&i_{d}\end{array}\right)

is a permutation, then the entry
\epsilon_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{d}}
is the corresponding sign of the permutation; otherwise,
\epsilon_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{d}}
is
0
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}]\in F^{d\times...\times d}
be a cubical hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
. Then

\mathrm{hdet}(\mathcal{A})=\frac{1}{d!}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{k}^{(1)},...,i_{k}^{(d)}=1\\
k\in[N]\end{subarray}}^{d}\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N}\epsilon_{i_{k}^{(1)},...,i_{k}^{(d)}}\prod\limits_{l=1}^{d}a_{i_{1}^{(l)},...,i_{N}^{(l)}}

(1)

Proof.

The product
\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N}\epsilon_{i_{1}^{(k)},...,i_{d}^{(k)}}
is nonzero only if for each
k\in[N]
,
(i_{1}^{(k)},...,i_{d}^{(k)})
is a permutation of
S_{d}
. Therefore, assuming
(i_{1}^{(k)},...,i_{d}^{(k)})
is in fact a permutation of
S_{d}
, and denoting said permutation as
\sigma_{k}
, it then follows that the sum in line (
1
) can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{d!}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{k}\in S_{d}\\
k\in[N]\end{subarray}}\left(\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N}\epsilon_{\sigma_{k}(1)...\sigma_{k}(d)}\right)\prod\limits_{l=1}^{d}a_{\sigma_{1}(l)...\sigma_{N}(l)}.

By definition
\epsilon_{\sigma_{k}(1)...\sigma_{k}(d)}=\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{k})
, hence the sum in line (
1
) is in fact equal to

\frac{1}{d!}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{k}\in S_{d}\\
k\in[N]\end{subarray}}\left(\prod\limits_{k=1}^{N}\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{k})\right)\prod\limits_{l=1}^{d}a_{\sigma_{1}(l)...\sigma_{N}(l)}

which is precisely the formula for
\mathrm{hdet}(\mathcal{A})
.
∎

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}]\in F^{d\times...\times d}
be a cubical hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
. Then

\mathrm{hdet}(\mathcal{A})=\frac{1}{d!}\bigotimes\limits_{k=1}^{N}\epsilon*\big(\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A}),...,\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})\big),

where
\epsilon
denotes the
d
-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol,
\bigotimes
denotes the tensor Kronecker product, and the tuple

\big(\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A}),...,\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})\big)
has length
d
.

Proof.

Observe

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d!}\bigotimes\limits_{k=1}^{N}\epsilon*\big(\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A}),....,\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})\big)

5.3
Application to Bosonic Quantum Systems

One of the most elusive physical properties of quantum states and simultaneously the most powerful resource in quantum computation is quantum entanglement. Entangled quantum states exists in a tensor product of Hilbert spaces, and one of the problems considered in quantum physics/information is the quantification of a quantum system’s entanglement for a given state. In general, this is a complicated endeavor because quantum states in a tensor product of 3 or more Hilbert spaces can be entangled in different, physically inequivalent ways
[
11
]
. Nonetheless, it is widely agreed throughout the literature (a few authoritative sources affirming this include
[
5
,
22
,
23
,
12
,
4
]
) that any proposed entanglement measure
E
should at a bare minimum satisfy the following criteria:

1.

E
vanishes on separable states,

2.

E
is invariant under local unitary transformations, and

3.

E
is non-increasing on average under local operations and classical communications (LOCC).

The three properties listed above are sometimes referred to as the entanglement axioms.

Qubits are the simplest type of quantum states and the fundamental building blocks of quantum computers. Qudits are a higher-dimensional analogue of qubits. More precisely, for any positive integers
n,d\geq 2
, an
n
-qudit is a quantum state
\ket{\psi}\in(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes n}
which can be written as a sum

\ket{\psi}=\sum\limits_{i_{1},...,i_{n}=0}^{d-1}\psi_{i_{1}...i_{n}}\ket{i_1...i_n}

such that
\sum\limits_{i_{1},...,i_{n}=0}^{d-1}|\psi_{i_{1}...i_{n}}|^{2}=1
. When
d=2
we call the state an
n
-qubit. Note in particular that
\ket{i_1...i_n}
denotes the Kronecker product
\ket{i_1}\otimes...\otimes\ket{i_n}
. Replacing the Kronecker product
\otimes
with the outer product
\circ
yields a cubical hypermatrix of order
n
with side length
d
:

\sum\limits_{i_{1},...,i_{n}=0}^{d-1}\psi_{i_{1}...i_{n}}\ket{i_1}\circ...\circ\ket{i_n}=:\widehat{\psi},

which has Frobenius norm
1
. In particular, there is a bijection between the space of
n
-qudits and the space of cubical hypermatrices of order
n
with side length
d
and Frobenius norm
1
given by the map

\ket{\psi}\mapsto\widehat{\psi}.

The concurrence is a popular entanglement measure on
2n
-qubits that measures the
2n
-way entanglement involving all qubits
[
8
,
24
]
. In
[
9
]
, Dobes & Jing prove that the concurrence
C
satisfies the equation

C(\ket{\psi})=2|\mathrm{hdet}(\widehat{\psi})|,

and in
[
10
]
they show that
\mathrm{hdet}
more generally on
2n
-qudits
\mathrm{hdet}
satisfies the entanglement axioms. Thus, Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant may be considered a physically meaningful generalization of the concurrence.

A special class of
n
-qudits of particular interest to physicists are bosons
[
18
]
. Explicitly, a boson is an
n
-qudit
\ket{\psi}
such that

\ket{\psi}=P_{\pi}\cdot\ket{\psi}\qquad\forall\pi\in S_{n}

(7)

where

P_{\pi}\cdot\ket{\psi}:=\sum\limits_{i_{1},...,i_{n}=0}^{d-1}\psi_{i_{\pi^{-1}(1)},...,i_{\pi^{-1}(n)}}\ket{i_{\pi^{-1}(1)}...i_{\pi^{-1}(n)}}.

It follows from the bijection
\ket{\psi}\mapsto\widehat{\psi}
that equation (
7
) is true if and only if

\widehat{\psi}=\widehat{\psi}^{\pi}

for any
\pi\in S_{n}
; that is,
\ket{\psi}
is a boson if and only if its corresponding hypermatrix is symmetric. Therefore, to calculate the
2n
-way entanglement of a
2n
-qudit boson reduces to calculating the hyperdeterminant on its corresponding hypermatrix. Algorithm 1 therefore provides an efficient way for one to calculate the
2n
-way entanglement of
2n
-qudit bosons.

6
Conclusion/Future Work

In summary, in this article we have derived a new formula for computing Cayley’s first hyperdeterminant
\mathrm{hdet}
in terms of a multilinear matrix product involving the Levi-Civita symbol and the canonical vectorization operator. For symmetric hypermatrices of order
N
with side length
d
, we may consider their
1/N
-hypermatrix vectorization
\mathrm{hvec}_{1/N}
, which contains the same amount of information as their vectorization but requires only
\binom{d+N-1}{N}
rather than
d^{N}
. This therefore substantially reduces the time complexity for computing
\mathrm{hdet}
in terms of
N
assuming
d
fixed. An important application for this is the efficient calculation of the
2n
-way entanglement for entangled even order bosons.

One potential direction for future research building on this work could be applying the formula for
\mathrm{hdet}
given in Theorem 1 to non-cubical hypermatrices. In particular, for any arbitrary hypermatrix
\mathcal{A}\in F^{d_{1}\times...\times d_{N}}
, one may consider

\frac{1}{d_{1}\cdot...\cdot d_{N}}\bigotimes\limits_{k=1}^{N}\epsilon_{d_{k}}*(\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A}),...,\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})),

where
\epsilon_{d_{k}}
denotes the
d_{k}
-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol. Does this generalization of the hyperdeterminant have any broader physical or mathematical significance? Furthermore, for subgroups
G\leq S_{N}
, one may consider
G
-symmetric
hypermatrices which satisfy the property

a_{i_{\sigma(1)}...i_{\sigma(N)}}=a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}\qquad\forall\sigma\in G

and define
G
-analogues of
\mathrm{hvec}_{1/N}
and
D_{d}^{(N)}
for
G
-symmetric hypermatrices. Generalizations of the latter structure are bound to have rich algebraic properties and may potentially have applications to classes of entangled quantum states that are only partially symmetric.

in the list above. Note that among such tuples,

(j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{N})>(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N})\iff\exists k\in[N]:\begin{cases}\begin{cases}j_{k}>i_{k};&k=N\\
j_{k}>i_{k}\text{ and }j_{k+1}=i_{k+1},...,j_{N}=i_{N};&k<N\end{cases}.\end{cases}

Therefore,
(j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{N})>(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N})
implies that the first
k-1
entries in
(j_{1},...,j_{N})
can take any value so long as
d\geq j_{1}\geq j_{2}\geq...\geq j_{k}\geq i_{k}+1
, in which case there
\binom{(d+k-(i_{k}+1)}{k}
such tuples. Since
k
may be any integer from
1
to
N
, it follows that there exactly
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\binom{d+k-(i_{k}+1)}{k}
many tuples that follows
(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{k})
when ordered in the reflected lexicographic order, from least to greatest. Thus, when listing all such tuples above in the lexicographic order from least to greatest, the placement of tuple
(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N})
is given by

\binom{d+N-1}{N}-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\binom{d+k-(i_{k}+1)}{k}.

In summary,

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{d}^{(N)}:=\{(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N}):d\geq i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1\}
. Then the function

\displaystyle P:\mathcal{S}_{d}^{(N)}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\left[\binom{d+N-1}{N}\right]

\displaystyle\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N})

\displaystyle\mapsto\binom{d+N-1}{N}-\sum\limits_{k=1}^{N}\binom{d+k-(i_{k}+1)}{k}

gives the placement of
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N})
among all other tuples in
\mathcal{S}_{d}^{(N)}
when listed in reflected lexicographic order from least to greatest.

We may use this result to define the following unit vectors which will be used in the definition of generalized elimination and duplication matrices.

Definition 7
.

For each tuple
(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N})\in\mathcal{S}_{d}^{(N)}
, let
u_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}\in F^{\binom{d+N-1}{N}}
denote the unit vector with a
1
in its
P(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N})
-coordinate, and
0
’s in all other coordinates. That is,

u_{i_{1}...i_{N}}=e_{P(\mathbf{i})}\in F^{\binom{d+N-1}{N}}.

Note that when
N=2
,
u_{i_{1}i_{2}}
reduces to the unit vector with a
1
in its
\left(i_{1}+d(i_{2}-1)-\frac{1}{2}i_{2}(i_{2}-1)\right)
-coordinate, and a
0
in all other coordinates (i.e. it is the same vector
u_{ij}
used in the original definition of duplication matrices, setting
i_{1}=i
and
i_{2}=j
). Note also that with this notation, if
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}]\in F^{d\times...\times d}
is any symmetric hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
, then

\mathrm{hvec}_{1/N}(\mathcal{A})=\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}u_{i_{1}...i_{N}}.

(8)

Definition 8
.

Let
E_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}\in F^{d\times...\times d}
denote the cubical hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
, and with a
1
in its
(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N})
-entry and
0
’s in all other entries. Equivalently,

E_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}=e_{i_{1}}\circ e_{i_{2}}\circ...\circ e_{i_{N}}

where
\circ
denotes the outer product and
e_{i_{k}}\in F^{d}
for each
k
.

Note that

\mathrm{hvec}(E_{i_{1}...i_{N}})=e_{i_{N}}\otimes...\otimes e_{i_{1}}=e_{\psi(\mathbf{i})}\in F^{d^{N}}

(9)

where

\psi(\mathbf{i})=i_{1}+d(i_{2}-1)+...+d^{N-1}(i_{N}-1),

i.e.
\psi(\mathbf{i}):=\psi\big((i_{1},...,i_{N}),(d,...,d)\big)
from the definition of matricization. With this notation we may now generalize the notion of elimination matrices first proposed in
[
17
]
(and closely related to duplication matrices) to apply to symmetric cubical hypermatrices and their
1/N^{th}
-hypermatrix vectorization.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a symmetric cubical hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
. Then the
\binom{d+N-1}{N}\times d^{N}
matrix

L_{d}^{(N)}:=\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}u_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}\mathrm{hvec}(E_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}})^{T}

satisfies

L_{d}^{(N)}\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{hvec}_{1/N}(\mathcal{A}).

For this reason, we refer to
L_{d}^{(N)}
as the
generalized elimination matrix
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}]\in F^{d\times...\times d}
be any symmetric hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
. Then observe that

\displaystyle L_{d}^{(N)}\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}u_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}\mathrm{hvec}(E_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}})^{T}\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}u_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}\left(\sum\limits_{j_{1},...,j_{N}=1}^{d}\overbrace{(E_{i_{1}...i_{N}})_{j_{1}...j_{N}}}^{\delta_{i_{1}j_{1}}...\delta_{i_{N}j_{N}}}e_{j_{N}}\otimes...\otimes e_{j_{1}}\right)^{T}\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A}),\quad\text{by definition}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}u_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}(e_{i_{N}}^{T}\otimes...\otimes e_{i_{1}}^{T})\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}u_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}\sum\limits_{j_{1},...,j_{N}=1}^{d}a_{j_{1}...j_{N}}(\underbrace{e_{i_{N}}^{T}e_{j_{N}}}_{\delta_{i_{N}j_{N}}})\otimes...\otimes(\underbrace{e_{i_{1}}^{T}e_{j_{1}}}_{\delta_{i_{1}j_{1}}})\text{, by linearity of Kronecker products}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}u_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{hvec}_{1/N}(\mathcal{A}),\quad\text{by equation }\eqref{1/N-hypermatrix vectorization in terms of u}.

∎

Definition 9
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}
denote the cubical hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
given by

\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{N}/\mathrm{Stab}(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N})}E_{i_{\sigma(1)}i_{\sigma(2)}...i_{\sigma(N)}}.

That is,
\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}
consists of the sum of all hypermatrices
E_{j_{1}j_{2}...j_{N}}
such that
(j_{1},j_{2},...,j_{N})
is a distinct permutation of
(i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{N})
.

For example when
N
=3 and
d=3
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{111}

\displaystyle=E_{111}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{211}

\displaystyle=E_{211}+E_{112}+E_{121}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{311}

\displaystyle=E_{311}+E_{113}+E_{131}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{221}

\displaystyle=E_{221}+E_{212}+E_{122}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{321}

\displaystyle=E_{321}+E_{213}+E_{132}+E_{231}+E_{123}+E_{312}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{331}

\displaystyle=E_{331}+E_{313}+E_{133}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{222}

\displaystyle=E_{222}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{322}

\displaystyle=E_{322}+E_{223}+E_{232}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{332}

\displaystyle=E_{332}+E_{323}+E_{233}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{333}

\displaystyle=E_{333}.

Note in particular that

\sum\limits_{3\geq i\geq j\geq k\geq 1}\mathcal{T}_{ijk}=\sum\limits_{p,q,r=1}^{3}E_{pqr}.

Indeed, it is not difficult to see that in general we have the following fact:

Fact 3
.

\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}...i_{N}}=\sum\limits_{j_{1},...,j_{N}=1}^{d}E_{j_{1}...j_{N}}.

Hence, by linearity of
\mathrm{hvec}
it follows that

\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}...i_{N}})=\sum\limits_{j_{1},...,j_{N}=1}^{d}\mathrm{hvec}(E_{j_{1}...j_{N}})

or equivalently by equation (
9
)

\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}\left(\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{N}/\mathrm{Stab}(i_{1},...,i_{N})}e_{\psi(\mathbf{i}_{\sigma})}\right)=\sum\limits_{(j_{1},...,j_{N})\in F^{d\times...\times d}}^{d}e_{\psi(\mathbf{j})}

where
\mathbf{i}_{\sigma}:=i_{\sigma(1)},...,i_{\sigma(N)}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be any symmetric hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
. The
d^{N}\times\binom{d+N-1}{N}
matrix

D_{d}^{(N)}:=\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq i_{2}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}})u_{i_{1}i_{2}...i_{N}}^{T}

satisfies

D_{d}^{(N)}\mathrm{hvec}_{1/N}(\mathcal{A})=\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A}).

For this reason we refer to
D_{d}^{(N)}
as the
generalized duplication matrix
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}]\in F^{d\times...\times d}
be any symmetric hypermatrix of order
N
with side length
d
. First, note that

\displaystyle D_{d}^{(N)}\mathrm{hvec}_{1/N}(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\geq i_{1}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1\\
d\geq j_{1}\geq...\geq j_{N}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}...i_{N}})\underbrace{u_{i_{1}...i_{N}}^{T}u_{j_{1}...j_{N};d}}_{\delta_{i_{1}j_{1}}...\delta_{i_{N}j_{N}}}a_{j_{1}...j_{N}},\quad\text{by equation}\eqref{1/N-hypermatrix vectorization in terms of u}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{T}_{i_{1}...i_{N}})a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}\left(\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{N}/\mathrm{Stab}(i_{1},...,i_{N})}\mathrm{hvec}(E_{i_{\sigma(1)}...i_{\sigma(N)}})\right)a_{i_{1}...i_{N}},\quad\text{by definition}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}a_{i_{1}...a_{N}}\left(\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{N}/\mathrm{Stab}(i_{1},...,i_{N})}e_{\psi(\mathbf{i}_{\sigma})}\right),\quad\text{by equation}\eqref{Hypermatrix vectorization of E}.

The vector
e_{\psi(\mathbf{i}_{\sigma})}\in F^{d^{N}}
. Moreover, it follows from Fact 3 that the sums:

\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{N}/\mathrm{Stab}(i_{1},...,i_{N})}e_{\psi(\mathbf{i}_{\sigma})}

for all tuples
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},...,i_{N})
such that
d\geq i_{1}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1
, iterate through all tuples
(j_{1},...,j_{N})\in\overbrace{[d]\times...\times[d]}^{N\text{ times}}
exactly once. Therefore, the sum

\sum\limits_{d\geq i_{1}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1}a_{i_{1}...a_{N}}\left(\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{N}/\mathrm{Stab}(i_{1},...,i_{N})}e_{\psi(\mathbf{i}_{\sigma})}\right)

yields the
d^{N}
-dimensional vector with
a_{i_{1}...i_{N}}
in its
\psi(\mathbf{j})
-entry for all tuples
(j_{1},...,j_{N})
which are permutations of
(i_{1},...,i_{N})
, for each combination of subscripts
d\geq i_{1}\geq...\geq i_{N}\geq 1
. Since
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric, this is precisely
\mathrm{hvec}(\mathcal{A})
, and the result is thus proven.
∎
