An Intraductal Papillary Mucinous Neoplasm–Like Lesion Manifesting as a Duodenal Polyp: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Suna Yu, Min Joo Yoon, Eui Sun Jeong, Euno Choi, Hye-Kyung Jung, Seong-Eun Kim, Seryun Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs) typically originate within the pancreatic ductal system. However, the occurrence of an IPMN–like mucinous neoplasm outside the pancreas, presenting as an isolated duodenal polyp, is extremely rare. An asymptomatic 64-year-old woman underwent routine screening endoscopy, which revealed a 25-mm Yamada type IV pedunculated polypoid lesion in the second portion of the duodenum. En bloc endoscopic mucosal resection was performed without procedural complications, although histological evaluation revealed focal involvement of the deep resection margin. Histopathological examination demonstrated a mucinous neoplasm confined within the duodenal submucosa with papillary architecture and low-grade dysplasia, which was designated as a duodenal IPMN–like lesion. Post-procedural CT showed no concurrent pancreatic involvement, and endoscopy at 6 months demonstrated a stable post-resection scar without local recurrence. Extended follow-up with dedicated pancreatic imaging is ongoing. This case report describes a particularly uncommon duodenal IPMN–like lesion presenting as a polyp. This case highlights the importance of careful histological evaluation and the inclusion of pancreaticobiliary-type neoplasms in the differential diagnosis of atypical periampullary lesions, along with long-term surveillance.Keywords: Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm, Duodenal neoplasms, Intestinal polyps, Endoscopic mucosal resection

INTRODUCTION

Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMNs) are typically characterized as mucin-producing epithelial tumors arising from main or branch pancreatic ducts [1]. While they are a well-recognized entity within the pancreas, occurrences outside of the pancreas-such as those arising from heterotopic pancreatic tissue or aberrant ducts in the gastrointestinal tract-are exceptionally rare [2]. In the duodenum, incidentally found polypoid lesions are most commonly benign adenomas, Brunner’s gland hyperplasia, or subepithelial tumors (SETs) like neuroendocrine tumors [3]. The distinction between these common mucosal or submucosal lesions and pancreaticobiliary-derived mucinous neoplasms is a challenging task for clinicians, especially when endoscopic findings resemble simple pedunculated polyps without specific mucosal changes [4].

We present a rare case of an IPMN–like mucinous neoplasm located entirely within the duodenal submucosa and manifesting as a polypoid lesion, hereafter designated a duodenal IPMN–like lesion for the reasons detailed in the Discussion. This lesion was discovered incidentally during a routine health screening and managed successfully with endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR).

CASE REPORT

A 64-year-old female patient with a medical history including chronic hepatitis B, hyperlipidemia, and pulmonary tuberculosis was referred for a routine health screening. The patient exhibited no symptoms, and denied experiencing abdominal pain, weight loss, or jaundice. The patient’s physical examination did not reveal any significant findings, and all laboratory tests, including a complete blood count and liver function tests, were within normal limits. Tumor marker levels were found to be unremarkable, with a carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level of 2.6 ng/mL and a carbohydrate antigen (CA) 19-9 level of 12.1 U/mL.

A preliminary esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) revealed a duodenal polyp, and a subsequent follow-up EGD revealed a 25-mm Yamada type IV (pedunculated) polypoid lesion in the second portion of the duodenum, located proximally to the ampulla of Vater (Fig. 1). The lesion displayed a smooth surface, devoid of any ulceration or friability. The EMR procedure was successfully executed, resulting in en bloc resection without immediate or delayed complications, including hemorrhage or perforation.

The resected specimen consisted of a formalin-fixed mucosectomy fragment measuring 2.2×1.0×0.8 cm. Histopathological examination revealed a cystic lesion measuring 1.7×1.0×0.4 cm, confined within the duodenal submucosa. The lateral resection margin was free of neoplasm, whereas focal involvement of the deep (vertical) resection margin was identified at a single focus; precise measurement of the extent of this involvement was not feasible owing to cautery artifact at the resection base, which is inherent to EMR specimens. The cyst was lined by tall columnar, mucin-producing epithelium arranged in a papillary architecture supported by delicate fibrovascular cores, consistent with a duodenal IPMN–like lesion with low-grade dysplasia (Fig. 2). Dysplasia was graded according to the current World Health Organization (WHO) classification of tumors of the digestive system: the epithelial nuclei were basally located and uniform, with preserved polarity, minimal pseudostratification, and no significant nuclear enlargement, hyperchromasia, or loss of nuclear orientation; mitotic figures were sparse; and there was no cribriform or complex architecture, no budding, and no necrosis. There was no evidence of an underlying adenomatous component or of high-grade cytologic atypia. The subepithelial stroma consisted of scant fibrous tissue, with no dense spindle-celled ovarian- type stroma at any point beneath the epithelium. Immunohistochemical staining for mucin core proteins was performed on the resected specimen. The neoplastic epithelium showed diffuse cytoplasmic positivity for MUC5AC and was negative for MUC1 and MUC6, with only focal positivity for MUC2 and CDX2 that was not accompanied by any corresponding villous architecture or pseudostratified, elongated nuclei indicative of intestinal-type morphology (Fig. 3). Adjacent non-neoplastic Brunner’s glands and the overlying duodenal mucosa served as internal positive controls for MUC6 and for MUC2/CDX2, respectively. This profile excluded a pancreaticobiliary phenotype and did not meet the established criteria for either the gastric or the intestinal epithelial subtype of pancreatic IPMN [5,6]. Accordingly, the lesion was reported without assignment to a specific epithelial subtype. Post-procedural contrast-enhanced abdominal CT revealed no residual tumor at the resection site, no dilatation of the main pancreatic duct, and no evidence of synchronous pancreatic IPMN. The patient’s clinical course remained stable following the procedure, and a follow-up EGD performed six months later revealed only a post-resection scar at the previous EMR site, with no evidence of local recurrence or other abnormalities.

DISCUSSION

IPMNs are mucin-producing epithelial tumors that usually arise from the main or branch pancreatic ducts. Their occurrence outside the pancreas, particularly as a polypoid submucosal lesion in the duodenum, is exceptionally rare. Two mechanisms have been proposed. The first is neoplastic change within an ectopic or aberrant duct, such as an extension of the accessory pancreatic duct into the duodenal wall. The second is origin from heterotopic pancreatic tissue in gastrointestinal submucosa [7,8]. Only 23 cases arising from gastrointestinal heterotopic pancreas have been reported to date [2].

By strict definition, an IPMN arises from the pancreatic ductal system. Our lesion was confined to the duodenal submucosa, and no communication with the main or accessory pancreatic duct was demonstrable. No pancreatic parenchyma or residual native duct was found in any section. This differs from the cases cited above, in which heterotopic pancreatic tissue was histologically evident adjacent to the neoplasm [2,7,8]. An origin from heterotopic pancreas or an aberrant ductal remnant therefore remains a hypothesis in our case, and the unqualified term “IPMN” would overstate what our material can show.

One finding nonetheless argues against a simple primary duodenal mucinous neoplasm. The neoplastic epithelium showed diffuse MUC5AC expression with negative MUC1 and MUC6, and only focal MUC2 and CDX2 positivity. This pattern does not match normal duodenal epithelium, conventional intestinal-type duodenal adenoma, or Brunner’s gland-derived lesions, although MUC5AC may also be expressed in gastric foveolar metaplasia. The mucin phenotype is therefore distinct from that of native duodenal epithelium and is consistent with, though not proof of, a pancreaticobiliary lineage.

We considered each of the alternative designations proposed for this lesion. “Duodenal mucinous cystic neoplasm” was not adopted, because mucinous cystic neoplasm is defined by subepithelial ovarian-type stroma, which was entirely absent here. Designations specifying gastric-type epithelium were also not adopted, because our mucin panel did not support assignment to any single epithelial subtype. Designations using the word “cyst,” such as “isolated duodenal mucinous cyst with IPMN–like features,” were inadequate, because “cyst” implies a non-neoplastic retention process, whereas our specimen showed a true papillary proliferation of dysplastic epithelium. We therefore designate this lesion a duodenal IPMN–like lesion. This term keeps the identity of the entity and its presumed pancreaticobiliary lineage visible, while acknowledging that the intraductal origin required by the strict definition could not be shown. Throughout this report, “IPMN” without qualification refers only to bona fide pancreatic IPMNs.

Most duodenal polyps seen at endoscopy are adenomas, Brunner’s gland hyperplasia, or inflammatory polyps, and the absence of specific mucosal surface changes makes them difficult to distinguish clinically from a pancreaticobiliary-type mucinous neoplasm [3]. Once histology shows a submucosal mucinous cyst, a wider differential applies. A duodenal duplication cyst, including the variant with mucinous metaplasia, requires a well-formed muscularis propria in its wall and lacks cytologic atypia, and a simple retention cyst is lined by flat, bland epithelium. Neither applied here. A Brunner’s gland cyst is surrounded by lobules of Brunner’s glands, which were absent, and both this lesion and pyloric gland adenoma, the main gastric-phenotype neoplasm of the duodenum, express MUC6 [9] which our lesion did not. Mucinous cystic neoplasm was excluded by the absence of ovarian-type stroma. A primary duodenal mucinous neoplasm arising from the duodenal epithelium itself requires separate consideration, since the absence of ovarian-type stroma excludes only mucinous cystic neoplasm and not this broader category. The submucosal location and the mucin profile favor a pancreaticobiliary over a duodenal epithelial phenotype, but this alternative cannot be excluded with certainty without demonstrable pancreatic tissue.

Two features separated our lesion from non-specific or reactive mucinous change. The epithelium formed true papillae supported by delicate fibrovascular cores, rather than the pseudopapillary infoldings seen when a simple cyst is cut tangentially. It also showed low-grade dysplasia, rather than the bland lining of a retention cyst or a focus of foveolar metaplasia. Neither feature is pathognomonic on its own, which is why histology combined with mucin immunohistochemistry, rather than any single finding, is needed to reach this diagnosis.

Pancreatic IPMNs with low-grade dysplasia follow an indolent course after complete resection, and similar behavior may be expected here by analogy, although published data on IPMN–like lesions at this site are too limited for firm prognostic inference. When a lesion is confined to the submucosa and does not communicate with the main pancreatic duct, en bloc endoscopic resection is a safe and minimally invasive option. In our case, however, the lesion was approached as a simple pedunculated polyp, and the focal deep margin involvement shows that EMR may not achieve complete vertical clearance when a submucosal component is unrecognized. Where preprocedural imaging raises the possibility of a subepithelial or cystic lesion, endoscopic submucosal dissection or full-thickness resection merits consideration. Because of the deep margin involved, closer long-term endoscopic and radiologic follow- up is warranted.

Several limitations should be noted. Neither magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) nor endoscopic ultrasonography was performed before the procedure. The lesion appeared as an unremarkable pedunculated polyp, the CT showed normal pancreatic parenchyma, and CEA and CA 19-9 were normal, so the probability of a synchronous pancreatic lesion was judged low, and screening MRCP was deferred, a decision also influenced by resource considerations in our setting. Given the resolution limits of CT, a subtle communication with the accessory pancreatic duct or small synchronous branch-duct IPMNs could not be excluded, and the absence of synchronous pancreatic disease cannot be regarded as established. In retrospect, pre-procedural endoscopic ultrasonography would probably have identified the submucosal and cystic nature of the lesion and might have altered the resection strategy. The extent of the material examined is a further limitation. The specimen was submitted in its entirety, and no pancreatic parenchyma was found, but the focally involved deep margin indicates that the base of the lesion lay outside the plane of resection. Heterotopic pancreatic tissue typically occupies the deep submucosa or muscularis propria, so any such component beneath this lesion would not have been available for examination. The absence of pancreatic parenchyma therefore neither excludes nor establishes a heterotopic origin. Finally, six months of follow-up is too short for a definitive prognostic statement. In line with the Kyoto guidelines [10], MRCP is scheduled at 12 months after resection with annual imaging thereafter, and endoscopy will be repeated at 12 months and annually thereafter.

In summary, this case describes an exceptionally rare duodenal IPMN–like lesion arising within the duodenal submucosa and presenting endoscopically as a benign-appearing polyp. Because visual distinction from common adenomas or SETs is limited, diagnosis depends on complete endoscopic resection and careful histopathological evaluation. Pancreaticobiliary mucinous neoplasms should be considered in the differential diagnosis of atypical periampullary lesions, together with complete removal and appropriate long-term surveillance.
