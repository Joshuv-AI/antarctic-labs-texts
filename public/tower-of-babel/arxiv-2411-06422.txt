Low bit-flip rate probabilistic error cancellation
Mathys Rennela, Harold Ollivier

Abstract
Noise remains one of the most significant challenges in the development of reliable and scalable quantum processors. While quantum error correction and mitigation techniques offer potential solutions, they are often limited by the substantial overhead required. To address this, tailored approaches that exploit specific hardware characteristics have emerged. In quantum computing architectures utilizing cat-qubits, the inherent exponential suppression of bit-flip errors can significantly reduce the qubit count needed for effective error correction. In this work, we explore how the unique noise bias of cat-qubits can be harnessed to enhance error mitigation efficiency. Specifically, we demonstrate that the sampling cost associated with probabilistic error cancellation (PEC) methods can be exponentially reduced with the depth of the circuit when gates act on cat-qubits and preserve the noise bias. Similar results also hold for Clifford circuits and Pauli channels. Our error mitigation scheme is benchmarked across various quantum machine learning circuits, showcasing its practical advantages for near-term applications on cat-qubit architectures.



1
Introduction

Context.

Quantum error correction techniques aim to undo computational errors that unavoidably arise during the execution of quantum circuits. However, the physical qubit count overhead they impose make them impractical for smaller machines. In contrast, quantum error mitigation techniques focus on improving the accuracy of expectation values obtained by sampling the output from quantum circuits, thereby shifting the cost of handling errors from a space overhead to a time overhead. Although the scaling of the overhead for error mitigation is not favorable for larger circuits, it is currently the best approach to address noise for small quantum devices and unlocking their computational power for near term applications.

One such technique is Probabilistic Error Cancellation (PEC), which approximates the expectation value of an observable measured on a quantum state prepared by a noiseless circuit as the average value of measurement results taken over a quasi-probabilistic distribution of noisy quantum circuits (see
[
32
]
for the original techniques and, e.g.
[
10
,
30
,
31
,
12
]
for improvements and guarantees). By replacing the execution of a noiseless circuit with that of noisy but easily implementable ones, PEC yields an unbiased estimator for the expectation value of observables on the noiseless circuits.

Thanks to its appealing feature of being hardware and algorithm agnostic, PEC is easy to use and has been successfully applied to various use cases, including superconducting quantum architectures with crosstalk
[
34
]
, mitigation of Markovian noise
[
11
]
, and learning the noise associated with a given system
[
29
]
.

Yet, this also means that PEC remains unaware of the error propagation structure within the quantum circuits of interest, potentially leading to suboptimal performance. This raises an important question, posed in
[
6
, Open Question 3]
: can PEC be further optimized by tailoring it to a specific hardware or algorithm? Among current hardware proposal, cat-qubits—superconducting qubits with highly biased noise, considered as promising candidates for building fault-tolerant architectures
[
16
]
—are of great interest. Can their unique properties be leveraged to reduce the overhead of error mitigation? More broadly, do cat-qubits offer advantages outside the fault-tolerant regime and into the NISQ regime, where error mitigation is crucial for unlocking the potential of near-term quantum devices?

Overview of results.

In this paper, we provide a positive answer to the previous open questions. We do so by introducing a variant of Probabilistic Error Cancellation (PEC) that outperforms the standard approach in terms of sampling cost overhead for achieving a given target variance for the expectation value of an observable measured at the output of the noiseless circuit.

Indeed, the overhead of PEC is due to the necessity to sample noisy circuits to construct an approximation of an ideal noiseless circuit given as input. More precisely, when the ideal circuit is provided as a sequence of layered gates, PEC constructs a collection of noisy circuits from each layer by independently adding extra gates that mitigate the effect of the noise on average. This results in a quantum sampling cost that increases exponentially with the number of layers.

To address this limitation, we propose a modified error mitigation procedure, which we refer to as
Block-PEC
. By grouping the added gates together and commuting them through the various noisy layers of the circuit, we can combine them into a single additional layer, effectively performing PEC through block sampling. This allows us to reduce the base and exponent of the exponential quantum sampling cost, trading part of the quantum sampling cost for a classical pre-processing step that calculates quasi-probabilities. As a result, Block-PEC achieves a quantum sampling cost exponential in the qubit count of the circuit, rather than exponential in both the qubit count
and
the circuit depth. We complement our theoretical analysis by giving performance guarantees under various biased noise models as well as numerical evalutation of the gain for several concrete circuit classes.

By considering circuits that are bias-preserving on a given subspace of the full Hilbert space, we can further relax the requirements of Block-PEC. This allows us to demonstrate that Block-PEC can be effectively applied to circuits with biased noise. Our numerical simulations using small-size circuits show that Block-PEC provides a clear improvement for parameterized quantum circuits formed by matchgates, particularly Reconfigurable Beam Splitter (RBS) gates, which are widely used in quantum machine learning algorithms for NISQ computers
[
25
,
17
,
15
,
8
,
7
]
.

Related work.

PEC is a well established error mitigation technique available in various open source packages (see e.g.
[
21
]
). From
[
30
]
, we know that standard PEC is optimal (in terms of sampling cost) when applied on one
n
-qubit layer subjected to dephasing noise. Moreover, it has been established that using a continuous set of ideal gates, containing all
Z
-axis rotations for arbitrary angles, would not help lower the cost of PEC, as explained in
[
30
, Sec. 5]
.

Previous works have nonetheless provided improvements to PEC by optimizing the sampling cost taking into account the gates within the past light-cone of a given observable, as only those contribute to the errors affecting the measured expectation value
[
33
]
.

Outline.

After an overview of the mathematical formalism of probabilistic error cancellation (Section
2
), we introduce Block-PEC, and we outline the conditions under which it can be implemented (Section
3
). We then provide performance guarantees for Block-PEC (Section
4
), followed by a numerical analysis of the implementation of Block-PEC on concrete applications (Section
5
). We conclude in Section
6
, focusing on the prospects of this approach, and placing it in the context of hardware-specific error mitigation techniques.

Preliminaries

2.1
Quantum channels

Quantum channels are completely positive trace preserving operators acting on density matrices. They will be written using sans-serif fonts such as
\mathsf{C}
. A unitary channel associated to a unitary operator
U
is defined as
\mathsf{U}(\cdot)=U\cdot U^{\dagger}
. By convenience, we will often refer to
\mathsf{U}
as a unitary operator.

While noise channels can in principle correspond to any CPTP maps, we restrict our study to convex combinations of unitary channels
\mathsf{N}=\sum_{\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha(\mathsf{V})\mathsf{V}
with
\alpha(\mathsf{V})\geq 0
,
\sum_{\mathsf{V}}\alpha(\mathsf{V})=1
, and where
\mathbb{V}
is a set of unitary channels (e.g. Pauli channels where the unitary belongs to the Pauli group).

Definition 2.1

(Noisy unitary channel)
.

The noisy version of a unitary channel
\mathsf{U}
under the action of the noise channel
\mathsf{N}
is written

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}=\mathsf{N}\circ\mathsf{U}=\sum_{\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha(\mathsf{V})\mathsf{V}\circ\mathsf{U}.

(1)

Throughout the rest of the manuscript “tilded” channels refer to their noisy version.

A layered circuit is a circuit which can be defined as a succession of unitaries
U=U_{d}\ldots U_{1}
so that
d
is the depth of the circuit, where
U_{l}
is the unitary corresponding to the
l
-th layer of the circuit. With our notation,
\mathsf{U}_{l}
is the channel for the
l
-th layer, so that the full circuit is a unitary channel
\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{U}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\mathsf{U}_{1}
. The noisy variant of the quantum channel
\mathsf{U}
is given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}=\sum_{\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{V}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{V}_{d}}\alpha(\mathsf{V})\mathsf{V}_{d}\circ\mathsf{U}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\mathsf{V}_{1}\circ\mathsf{U}_{1},

(2)

where
\alpha(\mathsf{V})=\prod_{l=1}^{d}\alpha_{l}(\mathsf{V}_{l})
for
\mathsf{V}=(\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{d})
, and each
\mathbb{V}_{l}
defined as the generating set of unitaries associated to the noise channel
\mathsf{N}_{l}=\sum_{\mathsf{V}_{l}\in\mathbb{V}_{l}}\alpha_{l}(\mathsf{V}_{l})\mathsf{V}_{l}
.

2.2
Cat-qubits and dephasing noise

Cat-qubits are superconducting qubits which can be physically implemented in such a way that bit flip errors are exponentially suppressed, at the cost of a linear increase of the phase flip error rate (see
[
16
]
for a detailed presentation of cat-qubits). In other words, cat-qubits are associated with a biased noise model, heavily biased towards phase flip errors.

There is experimental evidence that phase flips errors are drastically rarer than bit flip errors (
10^{8}
times rarer in the experiments in
[
5
]
, for example). In the present work, we choose to ignore bit flips altogether (we further discuss this choice in Appendix
B
). To make our results more concrete, we make use of two simplified dephasing noise models.

The first one inserts correlated phase flips immediately after each gate of the circuit. More precisely, we chose to consider that after a gate acting on the set of
n
qubits
A
, the following channel acts upon these qubits:

\displaystyle\mathsf{C}^{[A]}_{p}=(1-p)\mathsf{I}^{\otimes n}+\sum_{I^{\otimes n}\neq B\subseteq A}\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}pZ_{[B]},

(3)

where

p
is the
1
-qubit phase flip error probability, and
Z_{[B]}
is the Pauli unitary operator defined for any set
B\subseteq A
of qubits by

\displaystyle Z_{[B]}=\left(\bigotimes_{i\in B}Z_{i}\right)\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{i\in A\setminus B}I_{i}\right),

(4)

so that
Z_{\emptyset}=I^{\otimes n}
. Such correlations have been observed in cat-qubit architectures for 1 and 2-qubit gates (see e.g.
[
16
]
).

The second model is a more drastic approximation but yields simpler analytic calculations. It consists of applying single qubit dephasing channels on all the qubits a gate is acting upon. In effect, for a gate acting on the set of qubits
A
, the channel applied after the gate is

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{p}^{[A]}=\sum_{B\subseteq A:|B|=m}(1-p)^{n-m}p^{m}\mathsf{Z}_{[B]}.

(5)

In practice, this latter model neglects correlations between the errors within the set of qubits affected by the gate. In Appendix
E
, we justify that Block-PEC retains its performance when error correlations are taken into account.

2.3
Overview of Probabilistic Error Cancellation

To compensate the effect of noise, error mitigation techniques such as PEC intend to apply the inverse of the noise channel to the affected quantum systems. While it is not a physically realizable operation as soon as the noise channel is not unitary, it is nonetheless well defined mathematically.

Indeed (see
[
32
,
10
]
and also Appendix
A
), for large classes of noise models, one can rewrite the channel
\mathsf{U}
corresponding to a noiseless
d
-layered circuit as a quasi-probabilistic distribution of noisy circuits
[
10
,
32
]
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}=\sum_{\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha(\mathsf{V})\mathsf{N}_{d}\circ\mathsf{V}_{d}\circ\mathsf{U}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\mathsf{N}_{1}\circ\mathsf{V}_{1}\circ\mathsf{U}_{1},

(6)

where
\alpha(\mathsf{V})=\alpha_{1}(\mathsf{V}_{1})\cdots\alpha_{d}(\mathsf{V}_{d})
for
\mathsf{V}=(\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{d})\in\mathbb{V}
where the
\alpha_{l}(\mathsf{V}_{l})
’s are
given by the resolution of the system of equations defined for each layer by

\displaystyle\mathsf{I}^{\otimes n_{l}}=\sum_{\mathsf{V}_{l}\in\mathbb{V}_{l}}\alpha_{l}(\mathsf{V}_{l})\mathsf{N}_{l}\circ\mathsf{V}_{l},

(7)

where
\sum_{\mathsf{V}_{l}}\alpha_{l}(\mathsf{V}_{l})=1
, and
n_{l}
is the number of qubits that the
l
-th layer
\mathsf{U}_{l}
acts non-trivially on.

Under the assumption that the noisy unitary channels
\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{l}}=\mathsf{N}_{l}\circ\mathsf{V}_{l}\circ\mathsf{U}_{l}
correspond to physically implementable gates,
PEC determines the expectation value of the noiseless circuit
\mathsf{U}
by randomly sampling a noisy gate
\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{l}}
for each layer, and executing the circuit corresponding to
\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{d}}\cdots\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{1}}
.

The noiseless expectation value
\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}=\Tr[O\mathsf{U}(\rho_{0})]
for some observable
O
(without loss of generality,
\|O\|_{\infty}=1
), and given the initial state
\rho_{0}=\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}
is such that

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}=\sum_{\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{d}\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha_{\mathsf{V}_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{\mathsf{V}_{d}}\cdots\langle O\rangle_{\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{d}}.

(8)

where

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle_{\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{d}}=\Tr[O\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{d}}\cdots\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{1}}(\rho_{0})].

(9)

Hoeffding’s inequality states that
the number of samples
S
required to attain precision
\delta
with PEC, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
, is
S\geq\frac{\gamma_{\text{total}}^{2}}{2\delta^{2}}\ln(\frac{2}{\varepsilon})
, where
\gamma_{\text{total}}=\gamma_{d}\cdots\gamma_{1}
.
In this context, the sampling cost
\gamma_{\text{total}}
entirely determines the performance of PEC as an error mitigation technique.

In a conventional use of PEC, the sampling cost
\gamma_{\text{total}}
is in
O(\gamma^{nd})
, where
n
is the qubit count,
d
is the circuit depth, and
\gamma\simeq 1+kp+O(p^{2})
for some constant
k
determined by the noise model (for dephasing noise,
k=2
), with
1
-qubit error rate
p
. This makes PEC a reasonable mitigation strategy in the presence of weak gate noise
[
30
]
.

2.4
Inverting Pauli noise

As outlined in the previous section, the key requirement for PEC is the ability to write perfect gates as a quasi-probabilistic combination of noisy ones. Whenever the noise channel applied after each ideal gates is a convex combination of Pauli channels, this can be performed rather straightforwardly by writing the inverse as a Taylor series.

Additionally, and this will be crucial for Block-PEC, whenever the above convex combination can be restricted to subgroup of the Pauli group, so does the quasi-probabilistic distribution of the inverse.

Theorem 2.2

(Adapted from
[
12
]
)
.

The inverse of a Pauli-noise channel
\mathsf{N}=(1-p)\mathsf{I}+p\mathsf{A}
, where
\mathsf{A}
is a convex combination over Pauli channels, is a quasi-probabilistic distribution of Pauli channels.

Furthermore, if the non trivial Pauli-channels in
\mathsf{A}
correspond to Pauli operators that form a group, so do the non trivial terms in the quasi-probabilistic distribution of
\mathsf{N}^{-1}
.

\mathsf{N}^{-1}
can be approximated by a channel of the form
(1+p)\mathsf{I}-p\mathsf{A}
.

Proof.

Consider a noise channel
\mathsf{N}=(1-p)\mathsf{I}+p\mathsf{A}=(1-p)\mathsf{I}+\sum_{\mathsf{Q}\in\mathbb{Q}}p_{\mathsf{Q}}\mathsf{Q}
, where each
\mathsf{Q}
is a Pauli channel associated to a probability
p_{\mathsf{Q}}
, so that
\sum_{\mathsf{Q}}p_{\mathsf{Q}}=1
, and such that
\mathbb{Q}
is a subgroup of the Pauli group.

Then,

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left((1-p)\mathsf{I}+p\mathsf{A}\right)^{-1}

(10)

\displaystyle=(1-p)\left(\mathsf{I}+\sum_{i>0}\left(-\frac{p}{1-p}\mathsf{A}\right)^{i}\right)

(11)

\displaystyle\approx(1+p)\mathsf{I}-p\mathsf{A}.

(12)

The first and second claim of the theorem follow from the group structure of
\mathbb{Q}
which guarantee that each term in the expansion of
\mathsf{A}^{i}=\left(\sum_{\mathsf{Q}\in\mathbb{Q}}p_{\mathsf{Q}}\mathsf{Q}\right)^{i}
is in
\mathbb{Q}
.

The third claim (Eq.
12
) is obtained by truncating the Taylor series to order 1.
∎

Note that this inversion assumes that the noise channel
\mathsf{N}
is close to the identity. In accordance with this assumption, the gates
\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{l}}
that will be used to invert
\mathsf{N}
will introduce a noise that will be of similar magnitude. This means, following Eq,
12
, that this effect will be of order 2 and can thus be neglected in our computation.

3
Block Probabilistic Error Cancellation

The standard PEC procedure outlined in Section
2.3
is oblivious to the structure of the implementable gate set and noise model save for the actual quasi-probabilistic decomposition of each noisy gate. In this section, we go beyond this generic approach by using a property of the implementable gate set that we call
Pauli-
Z
compatibility
to modify the Monte Carlo sampling performed in the PEC procedure.

This yields a lower sampling cost — i.e. the number of quantum circuits to run — provided some classical pre-computations can be performed efficiently. We call this improved method
block probabilistic error cancellation
(Block-PEC), as it rearranges the sampling of noisy gates on a per-block basis rather than layer by layer.

After defining Pauli-
Z
compatibility, we detail how to implement Block-PEC, how to efficiently (classically) compute the coefficients of its quasi-probabilistic distribution, and extend the technique to consider generic gate sets which contain gates which are not Pauli-
Z
compatible.

3.1
Pauli-
Z
compatibility

In order to capture a useful commutation property between implementable gate sets and noise, we define the following property.

Definition 3.1

(Pauli-
Z
compatibility)
.

Let
\mathbb{N}
,
\mathbb{U}
and
\mathbb{V}
be three sets of unitary operators acting on at most
n
qubits, where,

(P1)

\mathbb{V}
is a group, and a subset of
\mathbb{U}
,

(P2)

\mathbb{V}
is stable by conjugation with elements of
\mathbb{U}
, i.e.

\forall\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{V},\forall\mathsf{U}\in\mathbb{U},\exists\mathsf{V}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{V},s.t.\mathsf{U}\circ\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}^{\prime}\circ\mathsf{U};

(13)

(P3)

\mathbb{V}
commutes with all
\mathbb{N}
, i.e.

\forall\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{V},\forall\mathsf{N}\in\mathbb{N},\mathsf{N}\circ\mathsf{V}=\mathsf{V}\circ\mathsf{N}.

(14)

In such case,
(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V})
is said to be
\mathbb{N}
-
compatible
. When
\mathbb{N}
is the set of convex combinations of unitary channels made of
Pauli-
Z
strings
— i.e.
n
-fold tensor product of single qubit
I
and
Z
— we say that
(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V})
is
Pauli-
Z
compatible
.

For convenience, we will say that a gate is Pauli-
Z
compatible if is in a set
\mathbb{U}
such that
(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V})
is Pauli-
Z
compatible. A circuit is Pauli-
Z
compatible if it is generated from a Pauli-
Z
compatible gate set.

In this definition,
\mathbb{U}
above represents perfect implementable gates for a given architecture. (P1) states that it is possible to single-out a subgroup
\mathbb{V}
of
\mathbb{U}
that will be used to apply additional controls after each intended gate. (P2) states that given a sequence of implementable gates, one can apply the additional controls described above at the very end of the circuit.

To make this concrete imagine a 2-gate circuit
\mathsf{U}_{2}\circ\mathsf{U}_{1}
with
\mathsf{U}_{1},\mathsf{U}_{2}\in\mathbb{U}
, and that one wants to implement instead
\mathsf{V}_{2}\circ\mathsf{U}_{2}\circ\mathsf{V}_{1}\circ\mathsf{U}_{1}
, with
\mathsf{V}_{1},\mathsf{V}_{2}\in\mathbb{V}
. Then, using (P2), there exists
\mathsf{V}^{\prime}_{1}
such that
\mathsf{V}_{2}\circ\mathsf{U}_{2}\circ\mathsf{V}_{1}\circ\mathsf{U}_{1}=\mathsf{V}_{2}\circ\mathsf{V}^{\prime}_{1}\circ\mathsf{U}_{2}\circ\mathsf{U}_{1}
. Using (P1),
\mathsf{V}_{2}\circ\mathsf{V}^{\prime}_{1}
can be replaced by
\mathsf{V}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{V}
. Finally (P3) guarantees that, in spite of noise operators chosen in
\mathbb{N}
, the previous pulling of additional controls at the end of the circuit can be performed without modification. For this, let
\mathsf{N}_{1}
and
\mathsf{N}_{2}
in
\mathbb{N}
be the noise channels applied after
\mathsf{U}_{1}
and
\mathsf{U}_{2}
respectively, then (P3) gives
\mathsf{V}_{2}\circ\mathsf{N}_{2}\circ\mathsf{U}_{2}\circ\mathsf{V}_{1}\circ\mathsf{N}_{1}\circ\mathsf{U}_{1}=\mathsf{V}^{\prime}\circ\mathsf{N}_{2}\circ\mathsf{U}_{2}\circ\mathsf{N}_{1}\circ\mathsf{U}_{1}
, with the same
\mathsf{V}^{\prime}
as in the noiseless case.

With cat-qubit architectures in mind, we can already develop an example of Pauli-
Z
compatibility. For instance, take
\mathbb{U}=\mathbb{V}\cup\{\mathsf{Z}(\theta),\mathsf{ZZ}(\theta),\mathsf{CNOT}\}
, where
Z(\theta)=\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta}{2}Z\right)
,
ZZ(\theta)=\exp\left(-i\frac{\theta}{2}Z\otimes Z\right)
, and
\mathbb{V}
are all Pauli-
Z
strings. Then, a straightforward calculation leads to establishing the Pauli-
Z
compatibility of
(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V})
.

Moreover, the noise model associated to
\mathbb{N}
is dephasing, and therefore, the physical intuition behind Pauli-
Z
compatibility is that any circuit made of gates in
\mathbb{U}
can be sampled from as a block, by performing all the additional controls of the error cancellation procedure at the end of the circuit, rather than after each gate.

Note that if one instead restricts
\mathbb{U}
to a subset of the Clifford group such that it contains the Pauli group, then for
\mathbb{V}
the Pauli group and
\mathbb{N}
the set of Pauli channels, properties P1 to P3 above are satisfied so that it can be said that
(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V})
is
Pauli-compatible
.

3.2
Block probabilistic error cancellation

Consider
(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V})
and
\mathbb{N}
satisfying P1 to P3 above, and a layered circuit
\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{U}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\mathsf{U}_{1}
with layers
\mathsf{U}_{l}\in\mathbb{U}
, each associated to their own noise channel
\mathsf{N}_{l}
, forming a non-uniform noise model. Each layer
\mathsf{U}_{l}
can be decomposed into a quasi-probabilistic distribution of layers obtained by applying a perfect additional control:

\mathsf{U}_{l}=\sum_{\mathsf{V}_{l}\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{l}}(\mathsf{V}_{l})\mathsf{V}_{l}\circ\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}_{l},

(15)

where
\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{l}}(\mathsf{V}_{l})
are real numbers that depend on the target transformation
\mathsf{U}_{l}
and the effective noise
\mathsf{N}_{l}
at layer
\mathsf{U}_{l}
.

We can now commute all the additional controls
\mathsf{V}_{l}
to the end of the circuit. This results into a quasi-probabilistic distribution corresponding to
\mathsf{U}
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{U}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\mathsf{U}_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathsf{V}_{1},\cdots\mathsf{V}_{d}\in\mathbb{V}}\Bigr[\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{d}}(\mathsf{V}_{d})\cdots\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{1}}(\mathsf{V}_{1})\times

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\left.\left(\mathsf{V}^{\prime}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\mathsf{V}^{\prime}_{1}\right)\circ\left(\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}_{1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathsf{V}\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathsf{V}^{\prime})\mathsf{V}^{\prime}\circ\widetilde{\mathsf{U}},

(16)

where the last equality uses the group structure of
\mathbb{V}
, so that
\mathsf{V}^{\prime}
is associated to a depth-1 circuit formed by the product
\prod_{l=1}^{d}\mathsf{V}^{\prime}_{l}
for
\mathsf{V}^{\prime}_{l}
defined as the result of commuting
\mathsf{V}_{l}
through the noise channels
\mathsf{N}_{k}
for
k\geq l
and the gates
\mathsf{U}_{k}
for
k>l
, and where

\displaystyle\alpha_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathsf{V}^{\prime})=\sum_{\mathsf{V}_{1},\cdots\mathsf{V}_{d}\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{d}}(\mathsf{V}_{d})\cdots\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{1}}(\mathsf{V}_{1}),

(17)

for
\mathsf{V}_{1},\cdots\mathsf{V}_{d}
such that
\mathsf{V}^{\prime}_{d}\cdots\mathsf{V}^{\prime}_{1}=\mathsf{V}^{\prime}
.

Now, we can apply a similar decomposition for
\mathsf{V}^{\prime}=\sum_{\mathsf{W}}\beta_{\mathsf{V}^{\prime}}(\mathsf{W})\widetilde{\mathsf{W}}
so that when replaced in Eq.
16

\mathsf{U}
, using only noisy implementable gates:

\mathsf{U}=\sum_{\mathsf{W}\in\mathbb{V}}\delta_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathsf{W})\widetilde{\mathsf{W}}\circ\widetilde{\mathsf{U}},

(18)

with
\delta_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathsf{W})=\sum_{\mathsf{V}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathsf{V}^{\prime})\beta_{\mathsf{V}^{\prime}}(\mathsf{W})
.

The interest is that instead of requiring a sample for each layer of the circuit, only a single layer needs to be sampled as long as the new coefficients
\delta_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathsf{W})
can be efficiently computed classically as to ensure that sampling from this distribution can be done efficiently.

\mathsf{V}_{i}
as well as
\mathsf{V}
can be applied without introducing additional noise. The standard probabilistic error cancellation gives:

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}=\sum_{\mathsf{V}_{1},\cdots,\mathsf{V}_{d}\in\mathbb{V}}\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{1}}(\mathsf{V}_{1})\cdots\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{d}}(\mathsf{V}_{d})\mathsf{V}_{d}\circ\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\mathsf{V}_{1}\circ\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}_{1},

(19)

which in turn requires a sampling overhead that scales as the square of

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{std}}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots\mathsf{V}_{d}\in\mathbb{V}}|\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{1}}(\mathsf{V}_{1})\cdots\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{d}}(\mathsf{V}_{d})|,

(20)

see
[
32
]
.

For our per-block method this is instead equal to

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{blk}}\coloneqq\sum_{\mathsf{V}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{V}}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{d}\in\mathbb{V}\\
\mathsf{V}^{\prime}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\mathsf{V}^{\prime}_{1}=\mathsf{V}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{1}}(\mathsf{V}_{1})\cdots\alpha_{\mathsf{U}_{d}}(\mathsf{V}_{d})\right|.

(21)

The inequality

follows from the triangle inequality.

Equations

that this is the case for broad classes of circuits and realistic noise models. Section

explore the gain on end-to-end applications, some of which can run entirely on bias-preserving gates, adding to the interest of Block-PEC for cat-qubits.

We will show in Section

3.3
Computing Block-PEC coefficients

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{V}_{d}\in\mathbb{V}\\

3.4
Hybrid Block-PEC for circuits with generic gates

We have previously established that
(\mathbb{U},\mathbb{V})
is Pauli-
Z

\mathbb{V}
is the set of all Pauli-
Z

Nonetheless, our per-block technique can be applied to Pauli-
Z

The advantage given by the Block-PEC technique remains, as the lower sampling cost of each sub-circuit directly reduces the sampling cost of the full circuit.

In this context, it would be beneficial to implement circuit rewriting rules which optimize quantum circuit layouts for our error mitigation schemes for example, by optimizing the positions of Hadamard gates within the circuit or to move Hadamard gates along the wires of the circuit. We leave this for future research.

\mathcal{P}\ket{0}^{\otimes n}=\sum_{x}\sqrt{p_{x}}\ket{x}
and a payoff unitary
\mathcal{R}\ket{x}\ket{0}=\ket{x}\left(\sqrt{f(x)}\ket{1}+\sqrt{1-f(x)}\ket{0}\right)
.

The unitary
\mathcal{R}
implements a piece-wise linear function

as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}:\ket{j}\ket{0}\mapsto\ket{j}\left(\cos(f_j)\ket{0}+\sin(f_j)\ket{1}\right),

(40)

where

The term

is implemented by the

Y
-rotation of angle
\theta_{j}=2^{j}f_{1}
on the ancilla qubit, at the exception

which is implemented by a
Y
-rotation on the ancilla qubit.

\displaystyle Y(\theta)=SHZ(\theta)HS^{\dagger}

(41)

with the phase gate implemented as

Z

Y

Z

\displaystyle CY(\theta)=Y\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)_{k}CNOT_{j,k}Y\left(-\frac{\theta}{2}\right)_{k}CNOT_{j,k}.

(42)

We refer the interested reader to
[
28
]
for a detailed explanation of the definition of the angles
\theta_{j}
in order to compute the expectation value of an option with payoff
f
.

The unitary
\mathcal{R}
is implemented by the circuit

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=CY(\theta_{n})_{n-1,n}\cdots CY(\theta_{1})_{0,n}Y(\theta_{0})_{n}.

(43)

Each implementation of

U_{(a)}

6
Conclusion

We have introduced Block-PEC, a variation of the probabilistic error cancellation for error mitigation that takes into account the specifics of the noise model and the propagation of errors to lower the overhead of using noisy quantum computers in lieu of perfect ones. The model that we consider corresponds to biased noise qubits as implemented by superconducting cat-qubit architectures.

Our technique provides a provable advantage for single layer circuits and extends to an exponential advantage when several bias-preserving gates follow one another. It constructs an aggregate noise model that can be inverted all at once instead of a per gate basis. This results in a lower sampling cost for achieving a given precision at the cost of classical pre-computation.

We performed numerical simulations for assessing typical performance on several classes of circuits. In accordance with our theoretical results, Block-PEC is most efficient for deeper circuits, as the cost of the classical pre-computation increases exponentially with the width of the circuit but only linearly with its depth. For example, for 9-qubit circuits with
RBS
gates in three repetitions of a
SWAP
network, the gain is already about 4 even at
p=0.001
, and for pyramids of
RBS
gates it is expected to reach 12 for 30 qubits under the same noise strength. Using these simulation results, we argued that for applications such as option pricing or orthogonal neural networks, Block-PEC leads to a reduction of the sampling variance.

We have shown that this advantage carries over whenever sub-circuits made out of several bias-preserving gates can be identified within the full computation. This is possible by performing a hybrid simulation of the full circuit. The bias-preserving sub-circuits are sampled according to the aggregate noise model defined by Block-PEC while the other gates are simply sampled according to the regular PEC technique. Then, one run of the hybrid simulation corresponds to independently sampling each of the sub-circuits and non bias-preserving gates. Similar results could be obtained for Clifford circuits under Pauli noise.

Moreover, applying our per-block reasoning to Noise-Extended PEC (NEPEC)
[
22
]
, which generalizes both PEC and Zero-Noise Extrapolation (ZNE), means that mitigating errors by block can be an efficient strategy in the context of ZNE.

Finally, this work also exemplifies that cat-qubits architectures can be advantageous to use in the NISQ-regime thanks to their noise model that can be more efficiently handled by error mitigation techniques. As such this raises two related questions: what other error mitigation techniques can benefit from biased noise qubits and, more generally, are there architectures for which error mitigation techniques can be tailored and made particularly efficient for end-to-end applications?

Appendix A
A primer in probabilistic error cancellation

Quantum error mitigation techniques aim to minimize the estimation bias when sampling from noisy circuits. The Probabilistic Error Cancellation (PEC
[
32
]
) approach performs a Monte Carlo simulation of the inverse of the noise model, in order to remove this estimation bias: from a generating set of noisy (implementable) gates, the inverted noise model is approximated as a linear combination of noisy gates. Therefore, bounds on the sampling cost of PEC are tied to the quality of available physical implementations.

The Monte Carlo sampling is applied for a given circuit
\mathsf{U}=\mathsf{U}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\mathsf{U}_{1}
formed by
d
gates where each ideal gate
\mathsf{U}_{l}
is decomposed as the linear combination of implementable noisy gates

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}_{l}=\gamma_{l}\sum_{1\leq j\leq J}p_{j,l}s_{j,l}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j,l}},

(44)

and under the assumption that
p_{j}=|\alpha_{j,l}|/\gamma_{l}
,
\gamma_{l}=\sum_{j}|\alpha_{j,l}|
and
s_{j,l}\in\{-1,1\}
are efficiently computable.

This stochastic simulation operates as follows:

1.

Sample a noisy gate
\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j,l}}
for each ideal gate
\mathsf{U}_{l}
with probability
p_{j,l}
;

2.

Execute the circuit corresponding to the channels
\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j,d}}\cdots\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j,1}}
, and measure the observable
O
, obtaining outcome
o
;

3.

Multiply the outcome
o
by
\gamma_{\text{total}}=\prod_{l}\gamma_{l}
and
\text{sgn}_{\text{total}}=\prod_{j}s_{j}
;

4.

Repeat those three steps
S
times and compute the average
\mu
of the product
o
.

In this stochastic simulation, an unbiased estimator of
\mathsf{U}_{l}
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{U}}_{l}=\gamma_{l}s_{j_{l}}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j_{l}}},

(45)

with variance
O(\gamma_{l}^{2})
, so that an unbiased estimator of
\mathsf{U}
is given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{U}}_{d}\cdots\widehat{\mathsf{U}}_{1}=\gamma_{\text{total}}\text{sgn}_{\text{total}}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j_{d}}}\ldots\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j_{1}}},

(46)

with variance
O(\gamma_{\text{total}}^{2})
.

In other words, the stochastic simulation aims to approximate the expectation value
\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}=\Tr[O\mathsf{U}(\rho_{0})]
for some observable
O
, given the initial state
\rho_{0}=\outerproduct{0}{0}^{\otimes n}
. From the quasi-probabilistic distribution defined in Equation
44
, the expectation value of
\mathsf{U}
can be calculated from the expectation value

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}}=\Tr[O\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j_{d}}}\ldots\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j_{1}}}(\rho_{0})]

(47)

of the noisy gates
\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j_{d}}},\ldots,\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j_{1}}}
, that is

\displaystyle\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}=\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}}\alpha_{j_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{j_{d}}\langle O\rangle_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}}.

(48)

It is the sampling cost
\gamma_{\text{total}}
which determines the efficiency of PEC as a quantum error mitigation approach.

Hoeffding’s inequality states that the probability that the estimate
\mu
differs from the true mean value by more than
\delta>0
is at most
2\exp\left(-\frac{2S\delta^{2}}{\gamma_{\text{total}}^{2}}\right)
,
so that the sample average deviates from the true mean value with variance
\sigma^{2}\in O\left(\frac{\gamma_{\text{total}}^{2}}{S\delta^{2}}\right)
. Thus the number of samples
S
required to attain precision
\delta>0
, with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
, is

\displaystyle S\geq\frac{\gamma_{\text{total}}^{2}}{2\delta^{2}}\ln(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}).

(49)

A.1
Solving the noise inversion equation

Consider the noisy layered circuit

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}=\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}_{d}\circ\cdots\circ\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}_{1}

(50)

defined by a succession of implementable noisy gates, which each satisfy the equality
\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}_{l}=\mathsf{N}_{l}\circ\mathsf{U}_{l}
,
where
\mathsf{N}_{l}
is the noise map associated to the gate
\mathsf{U}_{l}
.

Now, assuming that
\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}_{l}
and
\mathsf{U}_{l}
are known, one can express the inverse
\mathsf{N}^{-1}_{l}=\mathsf{U}_{l}\circ\widetilde{\mathsf{U}}_{l}^{-1}
of the noise map,
as a linear combination of unitary channels and preparation channels

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}_{l}^{-1}=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j,l}\mathsf{V}_{j,l},

(51)

so that each unitary
\mathsf{U}_{l}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}_{l}=\mathsf{N}_{l}\circ\mathsf{N}^{-1}_{l}\circ\mathsf{U}_{l}=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j,l}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}}_{j,l},

(52)

We define
\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j,l}}=\mathsf{N}_{l}\circ\mathsf{V}_{j,l}\circ\mathsf{U}_{l}
to be the noisy gate associated to each unitary/preparation channel
\mathsf{V}_{j,l}
.

The inverse
\mathsf{N}_{l}^{-1}
of the noise channel can easily be obtained by observing that determining coefficients
\alpha_{j,l}
such that Equation
51
holds means solving the equation

\displaystyle\mathsf{I}^{\otimes n_{l}}=\mathsf{N}_{l}\circ\sum_{j}\alpha_{j,l}\mathsf{V}_{j,l},

(53)

where
n_{l}
is the number of qubits on which the operator
\mathsf{U}_{l}
acts non-trivially.

A.2
Parameters influencing the sampling cost

Minimizing the number of samples needed means solving the following Linear Program (LP) for each
\mathcal{U}_{l}
(which acts non-trivially on
n_{l}
qubits):

\displaystyle\text{minimize }\sum_{j=1}^{J}|\alpha_{j}|

\displaystyle\text{subject to }\mathsf{U}_{l}=\sum_{1\leq j\leq J}\alpha_{j}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j,l}},

(54)

where all the noisy implementable unitary operations
\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j,l}}
are in the support of
\mathsf{U}_{l}
(as a linear combination), assuming that
\mathsf{U}_{l}
has a non-trivial support and on few qubits.
We take the solution (if feasible) of this LP to be
\gamma_{n_{l}}
, so that
p_{j}=\frac{|\alpha_{j}|}{\gamma_{n_{l}}}
and
s_{j}=\text{sgn}(\alpha_{j})
.

We do so for all
\mathsf{U}_{l}
, so that
\mathsf{U}
is obtained as a product of the solutions of the LPs:

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}=\gamma_{\text{total}}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}}\alpha_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}}\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j_{d},d}}\cdots\widetilde{\mathsf{B}_{j_{1},1}},

(55)

with
\gamma_{\text{total}}=\gamma_{n_{d}}\cdots\gamma_{n_{1}}
and
\alpha_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}}=\alpha_{j_{d}}\cdots\alpha_{j_{1}}
,
where
\alpha_{j_{l}}=p_{j_{l}}s_{j_{l}}\gamma_{n_{l}}

for each
j_{l}
,
so that
p_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}}=p_{j_{d}}\cdots p_{j_{1}}
and
s_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}}=s_{j_{d}}\cdots s_{j_{1}}
.

Appendix B
Dephasing noise channels

In the present work, dephasing noise channels are linear combinations of Pauli-
Z
channels. A dephasing noise channel over a set of qubits
A
can be mathematically represented as linear combination

\sum_{B\subseteq A}\text{prob}(B)\mathsf{Z}_{[B]}
of Pauli quantum channels
\mathsf{Z}_{[B]}
(where prob is a probability distribution over the partition of
A
), associated to the Pauli unitary
Z_{[B]}
defined for
B\subseteq A
by

\displaystyle Z_{[B]}=\left(\bigotimes_{i\in B}Z_{i}\right)\otimes\left(\bigotimes_{i\in A\setminus B}I_{i}\right),

(56)

so that
Z_{\emptyset}=I^{\otimes n}
, and
m=|A|
is the number of qubits on which
Z_{A}
acts non-trivially.

B.1
Inverting uncorrelated dephasing noise

Typically (see e.g.
[
31
]
), uncorrelated dephasing noise channels are defined over a set of qubits
A
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{p}^{[A]}

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{j\in A}\left((1-p)\mathsf{I}_{j}+p\mathsf{Z}_{j}\right)

(57)

Now, observe that
\mathsf{F}_{p}^{[A]}
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{p}^{[A]}=\sum_{B\subseteq A:|B|=m}(1-p)^{n-m}p^{m}\mathsf{Z}_{[B]},

(58)

The inverted noise channel
\left(\mathsf{F}_{p}^{[\{j\}]}\right)^{-1}
on one qubit is given by

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{F}_{p}^{[\{j\}]}\right)^{-1}=\gamma_{1}\left((1-p)\mathsf{I}_{j}-p\mathsf{Z}_{j}\right)

(59)

for
\gamma_{k}=\frac{1}{(1-2p)^{k}}
.

Then, for an arbitrary qubit set
A
of size
n
, we define the inverted noise channel
\left(\mathsf{F}_{p}^{[A]}\right)^{-1}
by

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{F}_{p}^{[A]}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}\left(\bigotimes_{j\in A}\left((1-p)\mathsf{I}_{j}-p\mathsf{Z}_{j}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}\sum_{B\subseteq A:|B|=m}(1-p)^{n-m}(-p)^{m}\mathsf{Z}_{[B]}.

(60)

One can verify that

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{total}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}\sum_{m=0}^{n}{p^{m}(1-p)^{n-m}}{n\choose m}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}1=\gamma_{1}^{n}.

(61)

B.2
Approximating impure dephasing noise

Let us justify our use of a pure dephasing noise model, instead of a model which allows for some depolarizing noise. For simplification we omit in this section the indices of the qubits that noise channels are defined on.

Consider the local depolarizing noise model given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}^{(1)}_{p}

\displaystyle=(1-p)\mathsf{I}+\frac{p}{3}(\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}+\mathsf{Z}),\text{ and }\mathsf{D}^{(n)}_{p}=\left(\mathsf{D}_{p}^{(1)}\right)^{\otimes n}.

(62)

One can define an impure local dephasing noise model, which allows for some depolarizing noise, as the noise channels given for any
q\geq 0
by

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}^{(1)}_{p,q}

\displaystyle=(1-p)\mathsf{I}+p\left(\frac{q}{q+1}\mathsf{Z}+\frac{1}{3}\frac{1}{q+1}(\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}+\mathsf{Z})\right),

(63)

\displaystyle\mathsf{L}_{p,q}^{(n)}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathsf{L}_{p,q}^{(1)}\right)^{\otimes n},

(64)

so that
\mathsf{L}_{p,q}^{(n)}
is the local depolarizing noise for
q=0
and the local dephasing noise when
q
tends to infinity.

The distance between the quantum channels
\mathsf{F}^{(1)}_{p}
and
\mathsf{L}^{(1)}_{p,q}
is defined as the diamond norm of their difference, which is such that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathsf{F}^{(1)}_{p}-\mathsf{L}^{(1)}_{p,q}\right\|_{\diamond}=\frac{2p}{3(q+1)}\left\|\mathsf{Z}-\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y})\right\|_{\diamond}.

(65)

Therefore, the bias incurred by the impure dephasing noise model, quantified as the distance between the pure and impure dephasing noise channels, is proportional to
\frac{2p}{3(q+1)}
.

Observing that

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{F}^{(1)}_{p}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\left(\left(1-p\right)\mathsf{I}-p\mathsf{Z}\right),

(66)

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{L}^{(1)}_{p,q}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\left((1-p)\mathsf{I}-p\frac{3q+1}{3(q+1)}\mathsf{Z}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-p\frac{1}{3(q+1)}\left(\mathsf{X}+\mathsf{Y}\right)\right),

(67)

with
\gamma_{1}=\frac{1}{1-2p}
,
it follows that the distance
\left\|\left(\mathsf{F}^{(1)}_{p}\right)^{-1}-\left(\mathsf{L}^{(1)}_{p,q}\right)^{-1}\right\|_{\diamond}
between the pure and impure dephasing inverted noise channels is proportional to
\gamma_{1}\frac{2p}{3(q+1)}
.

For cat-qubit architectures, the effective bit flip rate is proportional to
e^{-c\overline{n}}
where
c
is a constant and
\overline{n}
is the mean number of photons (the “cat size”), which comes at the cost of a higher phase flip rate, with a rate increase proportional to
\overline{n}

[
16
]
.

For a realistic description of the noise model of cat-qubit architectures, one could take the noise channel
\mathsf{L}^{(1)}_{p,q}
with
q=\frac{e^{c\overline{n}}}{\overline{n}}-1
, so that
\frac{1}{q+1}=\overline{n}e^{-c\overline{n}}
. However, for
c=2
,
q
is of the order of
10^{3}
and
10^{7}
for
\overline{n}
equal to
5
and
10
respectively (which are both reasonable values for
\overline{n}
under current experiments
[
5
,
16
,
26
]
). Therefore, in the context of PEC-like error mitigation protocols, pure dephasing noise channels constitute a good approximation of the noise associated to bias-preserving circuits implemented on cat-qubits.

Appendix C
Bias-preserving gates

Informally, bias-preserving gates are quantum gates which have the property that they do not turn phase-flip errors into bit-flip errors. In other words, phase-flip errors are mapped to phase-flip errors, preserving phase-flip biases. We show that bias-preserving gates have matrix representations which can be described as generalized permutation matrices.

Definition C.1
.

A
n
-qubit gate is bias-preserving if its unitary matrix representation
G
is such that for every
n
-by-
n
unitary diagonal matrix
D
, there is a
n
-by-
n
unitary diagonal matrix
D^{\prime}
such that
GDG^{\dagger}=D^{\prime}
, i.e.
GD=D^{\prime}G
.

In other words, a
n
-qubit gate is bias-preserving if its matrix representation is in the normalizing group
N(\mathcal{D}_{n})
of the group
\mathcal{D}_{n}
of
n
-by-
n
unitary diagonal matrices. To characterize
N(\mathcal{D}_{n})
, we introduce the notion of generalized permutation matrix.

Definition C.2
.

A generalized permutation matrix is a matrix such that each column/row has exactly one non-zero entry.

Generalized permutation matrices are known to be the normalizer of diagonal matrices, that is, they form the largest subgroup of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
such that diagonal matrices are normal (i.e. for every
g\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, if
d
is a
n
-by-
n
diagonal matrix then so does
gdg^{\dagger}
). Considering unitary matrices within this context leads to the following proposition.

Proposition C.3
.

A gate is bias-preserving if its matrix representation is in
N(\mathcal{D}_{n})=\mathcal{P}_{n}\mathcal{D}_{n}
.

Proof.

Elements of the group
N(\mathcal{D}_{n})
are unitary generalized permutation matrices. Observe that for every generalized permutation
n
-by-
n
matrix
M
, there is a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
and a complex vector
m=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle M_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}m_{j}&\mbox{if }i=\sigma(j)\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Now, consider two generalized permutation matrices
M
and
M^{\prime}
, respectively characterized by the pairs
(\sigma,m)
and
(\sigma^{\prime},m^{\prime})
. Then the multiplication of
M
by
M^{\prime}
is given componentwise by

\displaystyle(MM^{\prime})_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}m_{\sigma^{\prime}(j)}m^{\prime}_{j}&\mbox{if }i=\sigma\sigma^{\prime}(j)\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Writing
M_{\sigma,m}
for the generalized permutation matrix characterized by the permutation
\sigma
and the complex vector
m
, we observe that

\displaystyle M_{\sigma,m}=M_{\sigma,(1,\ldots,1)}M_{\text{id},m}

(72)

where
M_{\sigma,(1,\ldots,1)}
is a permutation and
M_{\text{id},m}
is a diagonal matrix.
In other words, every generalized permutation matrix is the product of a permutation and a diagonal matrix. This statement is in particular true for unitary generalized permutation matrices, for which the characteristic vector
m
is such that
m_{j}\neq 0
and
m_{j}\overline{m_{j}}=1
(since the eigenvalues of any unitary matrix have modulus 1).
∎

To put it simply, bias-preserving gates are normalizers of diagonal matrices in the group of unitary matrices. This normalizer is fully characterized by products of permutation matrices and diagonal unitaries.

Appendix D
Partially bias-preserving gates

This section recalls the definition of partially bias-preserving gates, introduces
RBS
gates, before giving a detailed proof that
RBS
gates are partially bias-preserving.

Definition D.1
.

Consider
\mathcal{S}
to be a subspace of the Hilbert space of
n
quantum states.
A gate is
\mathcal{S}
-bias-preserving if its unitary matrix representation
G
is:

1.

stable under
\mathcal{S}
, i.e.
\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{S}\implies G\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{S}
;

2.

such that for every
n
-by-
n
unitary diagonal matrix
D
, there is a
n
-by-
n
unitary diagonal matrix
D^{\prime}
such that, for every state
\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{S}
,
GD\ket{\phi}=D^{\prime}G\ket{\phi}
.

A natural strategy for error mitigation when sampling from such gates is to exclude measurement outcomes which do not belong to the set
\mathcal{S}
(i.e. measurement outcomes which are not of the form
\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\ket{\phi_j}
for
\ket{\phi_j}\in\mathcal{S}
), and then apply the same error mitigation strategies that we develop for bias-preserving gates.

D.1
Reconfigurable Beam Splitter (RBS) gates

The
RBS
(Reconfigurable Beam Splitter) gate is a gate which performs a rotation in the Hilbert space spanned by the unit vector
\ket{01}
and
\ket{10}
, so that

\displaystyle RBS(\theta)\ket{01}

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta)\ket{01}-\sin(\theta)\ket{10}

(73)

\displaystyle RBS(\theta)\ket{10}

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta)\ket{10}+\sin(\theta)\ket{01}

(74)

while acting as the identity on
\ket{00}
and
\ket{11}
.

This
RBS
gate is particularly useful when its input subspace is restricted to states of Hamming distance 1, as the
RBS
gate preserves this property. The gate iRBS shares the same properties, since the gate iRBS is such that

\displaystyle iRBS(\theta)\ket{01}

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta)\ket{01}-i\sin(\theta)\ket{10},

(75)

\displaystyle iRBS(\theta)\ket{10}

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta)\ket{10}-i\sin(\theta)\ket{01}.

(76)

while acting as the identity on
\ket{00}
and
\ket{11}
.

Using combinations of
RBS
gates with appropriately defined angles, one can load bit strings into an unary encoded quantum state. Unary data loaders are quantum circuits used to construct states of the form

\displaystyle\ket{x}=\frac{1}{\|x\|}\sum_{1\leq j\leq d}x_{j}\ket{e_j}

(77)

for a given
d
-dimensional vector
\vec{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
, where
\ket{e_j}
is the unary representation of the number
j
, i.e.
e_{j}=0^{j-1}10^{d-j}
. They are examples of amplitude encoding routines, as they encode classical data in the amplitudes of a quantum state.

The state in Equation
77
can for example be constructed as follows. Start with the all-
0
-state
\ket{0\ldots 0}
and flip its first qubit. Then, define a set of angles for
RBS
gates so that when those gates are applied to a qubit
q_{n}
and its neighbor
q_{n+1}
successively, one obtains the target state. Writing
\theta_{0}=\arccos(x_1),\theta_{1}=\arccos(x_2 / \sin(\theta_0)),\theta_{2}=\arccos(x_3 / (\sin(\theta_0)\sin(\theta_1))),\ldots
,
and applying
RBS(\theta_{n})
to the pair of qubits
(q_{n},q_{n+1})
, one can construct the target state in Equation
77
. This is the diagonal unary data loader, as represented by the circuit in Figure
12
for an
8

This approach to the encoding of vectors can be extended to matrices. A

\displaystyle\ket{A}=\frac{1}{\|A\|}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{i,j}\ket{e_j}\ket{e_i}.

(78)

The use of

RBS gates can also be used to implement the inner product of two vectors
x

D.2
RBS gates are partially bias-preserving

While
RBS
gates have interesting properties with respect to the unary encoding, they are not bias-preserving in the strict sense of the term. Fortunately,
RBS
gates are partially bias-preserving.

Theorem D.2
.

RBS gates
RBS(\theta)
are
\mathcal{S}_{1}
-bias-preserving gates, where
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is the subspace spanned by all
2
-qubit quantum states of Hamming weight
1
(that is, unary quantum states spanned by
\ket{01}
and
\ket{10}
).

Proof.

First, observe that any state in
\mathcal{S}_{1}
is a linear combination of unary quantum states and that

\displaystyle RBS\left(\alpha\ket{01}+\beta\ket{10}\right)=\alpha^{\prime}\ket{01}+\beta^{\prime}\ket{10},

(79)

for complex numbers
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta,\beta^{\prime}
satisfying:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\alpha^{\prime}&=\alpha\cos(\theta)+\beta\sin(\theta)\\
\beta^{\prime}&=\beta\cos(\theta)-\alpha\sin(\theta).\end{array}\right.

Consider an arbitrary state
\ket{\phi}=\alpha\ket{01}+\beta\ket{10}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
. Then
RBS(\theta)\ket{\phi}
. Moreover, for every state
\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
, it holds that

\displaystyle Z_{1}Z_{2}\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=-\ket{\phi},

(80)

\displaystyle Z_{1}\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=-Z_{2}\ket{\phi}.

(81)

From Equation
73
and
74
, the action of the
RBS
gate on the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{1}
can be described as

\displaystyle RBS(\theta)\ket{\phi}=\cos(\theta)\ket{\phi}+\sin(\theta)Z_{1}X_{1}X_{2}\ket{\phi},

(82)

and therefore

\displaystyle RBS(\theta)Z_{1}\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=\cos(\theta)Z_{1}\ket{\phi}+\sin(\theta)X_{1}X_{2}\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=Z_{1}\left(\cos(\theta)\ket{\phi}+\sin(\theta)Z_{1}X_{1}X_{2}\ket{\phi}\right)

\displaystyle=Z_{1}RBS(\theta)\ket{\phi}.

(83)

Now, consider an arbitrary diagonal unitary
D
defined by

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=Z_{1}(\mu_{1})Z_{2}(\mu_{2})ZZ_{1,2}(\mu_{1,2})

(84)

where

\displaystyle Z_{j}(\mu_{j})

\displaystyle=\cos(\frac{\mu_j}{2})\ket{\phi}-i\sin(\frac{\mu_j}{2})Z_{j}\ket{\phi},

(85)

\displaystyle ZZ_{1,2}(\mu_{j})

\displaystyle=\cos(\frac{\mu_{1,2}}{2})\ket{\phi}-i\sin(\frac{\mu_{1,2}}{2})Z_{1}Z_{2}\ket{\phi}.

(86)

Observing that

\displaystyle RBS(\theta)ZZ_{1,2}(\mu)\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=ZZ_{1,2}(\mu)RBS(\theta)\ket{\phi},

(87)

\displaystyle\text{ and }RBS(\theta)Z_{j}(\mu)\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=Z_{j}(\mu)RBS(\theta)\ket{\phi},

(88)

it follows that
RBS(\theta)D\ket{\phi}=D\cdot RBS(\theta)\ket{\phi}
, and therefore the
RBS
gate is
\mathcal{S}_{1}
-bias-preserving.
∎

This property holds not only for
(i)RBS(\theta)
, but for other parity-preserving gates such as
\text{A}(\theta,\phi)
(see e.g.
[
4
,
14
,
13
]
),
\text{Givens}(\theta)
(see e.g.
[
2
,
20
]
),
\text{XY}(\theta)
(see e.g.
[
1
]
).

In this section, we calculate the Block-PEC sampling cost of the circuits

We write
\alpha_{0}=\frac{1-p}{1-2p}

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}_{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}\mathsf{I}_{2}\circ\mathsf{U}_{2}+\alpha_{1}\mathsf{Z}_{2}\circ\mathsf{U}_{2}

(89)

\displaystyle\mathsf{U}_{1,2}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{0}^{2}\mathsf{I}_{1}\mathsf{I}_{2}\circ\mathsf{U}_{1,2}

The per-block approach to PEC still yields an improvement in terms of sampling cost when dealing with correlated dephasing noise. Indeed, consider the correlated

\displaystyle\left(\mathsf{C}^{[A]}_{p}\right)^{-1}=\gamma_{1}\left((1-p)\mathsf{I}^{\otimes n}-\sum_{I^{\otimes n}\neq B\subseteq A}\frac{1}{2^{n}-1}pZ_{[B]}\right),

with
\gamma_{1}=\frac{1}{1-2p}

Writing
\alpha_{0}=\gamma_{1}(1-p)

\gamma_{\text{blk}}(U_{(b)})=\beta_{0}-3\beta_{1}

\displaystyle\gamma_{\text{blk}}(U_{(b)})=\frac{3-4p^{2}}{3(1-2p)}<\frac{1}{(1-2p)^{2}}=\gamma_{1}^{2}=\gamma_{\text{std}}(U_{(b)})

for correlated dephasing noise.
