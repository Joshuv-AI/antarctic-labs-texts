An integrated molecular classification system identifies distinct prognostic clusters of non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer
Authors: Kezhi Liu, Zhenhua Chen, Xiaohan Jin, Hui Liang, Haihua Liu, Jinwei Chen, Chengpeng Gui, Yuhang Chen, Ziran Dai, Hao Zhou, Zheyu Ai, Zhu Wang, Qiong Deng, Jieyan Wang, Meiyu Jin, Junhang Luo, Wei Chen, Zihao Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecular characterization of non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) provides critical insights into the heterogeneity of clinical outcomes and treatment responses. The integration of existing transcriptomic subtyping systems to reconcile inconsistencies would facilitate clinical translation. The Markov cluster algorithm was utilized to construct a network integrating five published bladder cancer classification systems based on transcriptional profiles, aiming to establish an integrated molecular classification framework and identify different prognostics clusters. Four integrated molecular clusters (IMC1-4) with distinct transcriptomic, genomic, proteomic and clinical characteristics were identified. IMC2 exhibited significant enrichment of FGFR3 mutations, whereas IMC4 showed predominant TP53 alterations. IMC1 and IMC3 demonstrated improved PFS following Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) instillation in patients recommended for BCG treatment per the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines. Using transcriptomic profiles, a single-sample classifier was developed to categorize tumors into integrated molecular clusters. This study provides an integrated classification system to understand the heterogeneity of NMIBC and establish a preclinical framework for clinical management, therapeutic targets, and monitoring of clinical trials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10142-026-01884-2.

Introduction

Bladder cancer ranks as the tenth most common cancer worldwide, accounting for 3% of all cancer diagnoses, with an annual global incidence of 573,278 new cases(Sung et al. 2021). Compared with other regions globally, Western Europe in 2021 demonstrated the highest burden of bladder cancer, as reflected by its incidence (1.22 × 10⁵), prevalence (7.09 × 10⁵), and mortality (0.48 × 10⁵)(Zi et al. 2024). Based on the tumor’s relationship with the muscular layer, bladder cancer is classified into muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) and non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC). Up to 20% of NMIBC patients (with variation across stages and grades) eventually progress to MIBC(Babjuk et al. 2017). Although NMIBC generally exhibits a better prognosis than MIBC, with a 5-year survival rate of 60%(Kamoun et al. 2020), the cumulative medical costs associated with NMIBC are higher. This disparity arises from the long-term, procedure-intensive surveillance and therapeutic interventions required to prevent recurrence and progression(Svatek et al. 2014). As defined by the EAU using the WHO 2004/2016 or WHO 1973 grading classification systems(Gontero et al. 2024), NMIBC is categorized into low-, intermediate-, high-, and very high-risk groups. High- or very high-risk patients are considered candidates for BCG instillation as the first-line treatment. However, patients receiving intravesical BCG immunotherapy exhibit heterogeneous treatment responses(Jong et al. 2023). Intravesical BCG instillation is also associated with local and systemic toxicity, with an incidence rate of 73%(Sylvester et al. 2003). Moreover, 30% of patients discontinue or delay treatment due to adverse effects(Sylvester et al. 2003). Thus, it is crucial to predict individual therapeutic responses accurately. Additionally, alternative therapies are urgently required to address global BCG supply shortages(Mostafid et al. 2015).

Molecular classification systems based on transcriptomic profiling represent a promising approach to NMIBC management by enabling individualized prognostic predictions and therapeutic strategy recommendations. Numerous expert teams have developed molecular subtyping systems applicable to NMIBC(Jong et al. 2023, Sjödahl et al. 2012, Blaveri et al. 2005, Tan et al. 2019, Dyrskjøt et al. 2003, Lindgren et al. 2010, Lindskrog et al. 2021, Marzouka et al. 2018, Mo et al. 2018, Robertson et al. 2020), and identified distinct subgroups with varying clinical prognoses. These systems provide enhanced comprehension of NMIBC heterogeneity at the molecular level, facilitating the development of personalized treatment strategies(Jong et al. 2023, Lindskrog et al. 2021).

However, these subtyping systems originated from independent datasets processed through different analytical pipelines. They encompass varying numbers of subtypes and are designed to address distinct primary objectives (Table S1). An integrated classification system based on these subtyping systems could integrate their diverse focuses and reveal underlying homogeneity, which could help the translation to the clinical practice. Moreover, molecular classification holds particular clinical relevance for predicting BCG treatment outcomes. The Erasmus subtyping system specifically predicts BCG response in high-risk patients, stratifying them into three subtypes and demonstrating that BRS3-subtype patients exhibit the poorest PFS following BCG instillation(Jong et al. 2023). However, whether BCG treatment improves or fails to alter PFS in a specific group of patients, remains unclear.

In this study, we constructed an integrated classification system integrating five published subtyping systems, elucidating transcriptomic, genomic, and proteomic heterogeneity associated with distinct clinical outcomes in NMIBC. Our analysis revealed that approximately 31% of the patients recommended for BCG therapy per EAU guidelines showed no significant improvement in PFS post-treatment. These findings provide critical insights for refining clinical management strategies and identifying potential therapeutic targets in NMIBC.

We have followed the TITAN Guidelines 2025 to ensure transparency in the reporting of AI use in our study (Transparency 2025).

Materials and methods

Overall design

The work has been reported in line with the REMARK criteria(McShane et al. 2005). The overall research pipeline is illustrated in Fig. S1. We collected 13 datasets (1602 samples in total) and five subtyping systems (24 subtypes in total) to construct the integrated molecular classification system. Details of subtyping systems and the datasets are given in Table S1, Table S2 and Supplementary Material 1.

Transcriptional data preparation

For datasets obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), probe-to-gene mapping was performed using the platform-specific annotation files (e.g., GPL soft files). For the EGAS00001004693(Jong et al. 2023) and EGAS00001006879(Kim et al. 2010), pre-mapped expression matrices were downloaded directly from the original publications. For genes represented by multiple probes, the average expression value was calculated. In the rare event that a probe mapped to multiple genes, only the first gene symbol was retained. To enable cross-platform integration, all gene symbols were harmonized to GRCh38.

Network structuring and integrated clusters determination

The 13 NMIBC transcriptomic datasets, including 1602 samples, were classified into 24 specific subtypes by the five classifiers of the subtyping systems, respectively. Subtyping proportions in different datasets are shown in Figure S2. Classification results from five published subtyping systems were merged for subsequent analysis. The workflow comprised two principal steps: network structuring and integrated clusters determination, with full methodological details provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Network structuring. Classification results from multiple systems were integrated into a logical matrix (subtypes × samples). A Cohen’s Kappa similarity matrix D was computed, where D(x,y) represents agreement between subtypes x and y. A weighted network was constructed with nodes as subtypes and edges weighted by Kappa.Integrated clusters determination. The Markov clustering algorithm (MCL) (Dongen 2008) was applied for clustering analysis. The inflation parameter (I) was tested (range: 1.4–15, step: 0.2). For each I, cluster stability was assessed via 1,000 iterations of 80% subsampling. A clustering matrix was generated from co-assignment frequencies across iterations. Subtype stability scores were computed as the frequency of within-cluster associations consistent with the full-sample MCL network.

The inflation factor (I = 3.4) corresponding to the MWSW 0.91 and K = 4 was selected as the final clustering (Figure S3). The rationale underlying this selection is detailed in Supplementary Material 1 and shown in Figures S3 and S4.

Construction of the single-sample classifier

Using the hypergeometric test, 822 core samples representative of specific IMCs were identified (details in Supplementary Material 1). Based on two datasets, UROMOL 2020 (n = 251) and GSE163209 (n = 121), candidate genes were selected using differential expression analysis. The final classifier, which uses 1430 genes and the Pearson nearest-centroid algorithm trained on the UROMOL 2020 core samples, achieved an optimal classification accuracy of 0.97 (Figure S5). Pearson correlation is invariant to linear transformations (scaling and shifting) of expression values. Since all datasets we analyzed have been log‑transformed (either as log2(FPKM + 1) for RNA‑seq or log2‑transformed expression intensities for microarrays), the relative expression patterns across genes are largely preserved across platforms and normalization methods. Thus, further standardization was neither necessary nor performed. All samples were categorized into an IMC. Distribution of the IMCs across the data sets and in the input subtyping systems was shown as Figure S6, with detailed information provided in Table S4. Additionally, we performed MCL clustering analysis in the absence of each dataset individually and found that the clustering results remained stable. This stability illustrates the internal homogeneity between subtypes across different subtyping systems, as shown in Figure S5D. The single-sample classifier is publicly available as an R package at https://github.com/redmele/NMIBCClassifier.

Analysis of the transcriptomic features of IMCs

The transcriptomic features of the IMCs were characterized using multiple analytical approaches. For gene set enrichment, immune infiltration, and immune balance analyses, we first performed one‑sided Mann‑Whitney U tests within each dataset to compare a given IMC against all other samples. The resulting p values were then aggregated across datasets using Stouffer’s method to obtain a combined p value. For epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and regulon activity analyses, data from individual datasets were pooled prior to analysis, and the two-sided Fisher exact test was used to calculate p values. Details of this analysis are provided in Supplementary Material 1. The corresponding gene lists and p values are presented in Table S3 and Table S5, respectively.

Survival analysis and multivariable Cox regression

We merged 6 datasets (UROMOL 2020, de Jong cohort A, de Jong cohort B, GSE154261_73, GSE163209, and GSE128959) with either available PFS data or RFS data (n = 1110). Specifically, 605 patients were treated with BCG instillation. Progression is defined as progression to muscle-invasive or metastatic bladder cancer. In the absence of detailed risk factor information, patients with T1HG, T1G3, or CIS were classified as those recommended for BCG treatment (high-risk and very high-risk groups), while others were not, as described by EAU guidelines(Gontero et al. 2024). A multivariable time‑dependent Cox model stratified by clinical stage was constructed. Details of clinical data are provided in Table S7.

Statistics

For categorical variables, Fisher’s exact test was performed. For continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test was performed. For genetic variables, Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p values were calculated for genetic variables and the testing family consisted of all genetic variables tested within each independent analysis, as these may represent potential drug targets or diagnostic biomarkers. Otherwise, unadjusted p values were reported.

All statistical and bioinformatics analyses were performed with R (v 4.4.1).

Results

The integrated molecular classification system identifies four IMCs

We collected 13 bladder cancer data sets (n = 1601 patients) as described in Table S2, along with five published molecular subtyping systems (24 subtypes in total) for NMIBC (Erasmus(Jong et al. 2023), UROMOL21(Lindskrog et al. 2021), Chicago(Robertson et al. 2020)), as well as both NMIBC and MIBC (Baylor(Mo et al. 2018) and Lund(Marzouka et al. 2018)). Using the published classifiers, samples from each dataset were individually assigned to specific subtypes. Subsequently, the subtyping results were aggregated into a single matrix, and an integrated subtyping system for NMIBC was developed using the Markov cluster algorithm (MCL). The overall design is illustrated in Figure S1.

We identified four integrated molecular clusters: IMC1, IMC2, IMC3, and IMC4, comprising eight, seven, five, and four published subtypes, respectively (Fig. 1A). IMC2 constituted the largest cluster (39.3%), whereas IMC3 exhibited a comparable proportion (39.1%). IMC4 represented the smallest cluster (8.1%). A differential distribution of published subtypes across IMCs was observed (Fig. 1B and C). The rationale for selecting IMCs results is detailed in the “Methods”.

Transcriptomic features of the IMCs

We analyzed the transcriptomic data and performed gene set variation analysis (GSEA) using hallmarks from MSigDB. The results demonstrated a high level of immune infiltration in IMC1 with overexpression of the hallmarks of interferon γ response, inflammatory response, complement, IL6-JAK-STAT3, and IL2-STAT5 signaling. Additionally, IMC1 exhibits a tendency toward epithelial-mesenchymal transition and/or stronger fibroblast infiltration, consistent with Fig. 2B characterized by downregulated differentiation markers (e.g., uroplakins) and upregulated cancer stem cell markers. To validate these findings, additional EMT analysis was conducted, as presented in Figure S7B. These results indicate that the entire IMC1 population exhibits a mesenchymal tendency. However, the remaining three IMCs exhibited relatively weak immune activity. IMC3 and IMC4 were enriched in the hallmarks associated with proliferation processes (Fig. 2A). Both have higher expression of E2F targets and G2M checkpoints. Specifically, upregulated MYC targets were observed in IMC3 while the mitotic spindle is more active in IMC4. Moreover, IMC4 demonstrates DNA damage with high DNA repair activity, accompanied by a low activity of the TP53 pathway. IMC2 demonstrates a behavior opposite to that of IMC3 and IMC4 in terms of proliferation and DNA repair, while sharing a common feature of low immune infiltration.

Next, clinically relevant signatures of NMIBC were carefully selected and compared across IMCs (Fig. 2B). The fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (FGFR3) co-expressed genes are strongly activated in IMC2, suggesting that IMC2 may respond to the FGFR3-targeted therapy, warranting further investigation. IMC4 and IMC3 are featured by downregulated early cell cycle and hypoxia, upregulated late cell cycle, and DNA replication, indicating improved outcomes with intravesical chemotherapy or radiotherapy (Gabrielson et al. 2023, Horsman and Overgaard 2016, Pawlik and Keyomarsi 2004). For IMC1, overexpression of immune checkpoint genes (PD-1 and PD-L1) and a highly immune-infiltrated microenvironment were found (Fig. 2C and D and S7C-S7F), underscoring its potential as a promising target for immune checkpoint inhibition (ICI) therapy. However, previous studies have indicated that activated fibroblast TGF-β attenuates tumor response to PD-L1 blockade(Mariathasan et al. 2018). To address this, we constructed a linear model to predict the balance between the CD8 effector signature score and the pan-fibroblast TGF-beta response signature (F-TBRS) as well as EMT-Stroma based on the calculated residuals(Rose et al. 2021) (Figure S8A). Notably, higher F-TBRS and EMT-Stroma residuals, which are linked to poor ICI responses(Rose et al. 2021), were prominent in IMC1 (Figure S8B). Thus, the potential benefit of combining anti-TGF-β with ICI warrants further investigation(Mariathasan et al. 2018, Gulley et al. 2022). Analysis of the progression and CIS scores among the three clusters revealed that IMC2 had the lowest score (Figure S7G), suggesting a favorable prognosis for IMC2.

To elucidate transcriptomic differences among IMCs, we analyzed networks of genes associated with NMIBC prognosis(Olislagers et al. 2025), bladder cancer-related transcription factors(Lindskrog et al. 2021, Robertson et al. 2017), and chromatin remodeling regulators(Lindskrog et al. 2021). Distinct regulon activity patterns were observed across IMCs (Figs. 2E, S7H and S7I). The overall information on the prognostic biomarkers is summarized by Olislagers et al.(Olislagers et al. 2025) FGFR3, whose upregulation is relevant to a favorable progression-free survival (PFS), is dominant in IMC2, indicating a better prognosis. Other biomarkers associated with progression are abundant in IMC1, IMC3, and IMC4 to varying extents. For example, E2F1, CCNE1, FOXM1, CENPF, DHCR24, and COL1A1 are highly expressed in IMC4. Their overexpression correlated with elevated hazard ratios (HR) for progression(Olislagers et al. 2025). The analysis of the 20 bladder-cancer-related transcription factors revealed a stronger regulon activity of STAT3, RARB, PGR, HIF1A, GATA6, FGFR1, and ESR1 in IMC1, whereas IMC2 is associated with high TP63, RXRA, RARG, PPARG, and AR activity. Similar regulon activity is observed in the IMC3 and IMC4, although, higher ESR1 and AR activity is observed in IMC4.

The profiles of the chromatin remodeling regulons revealed distinct epigenetic states across IMCs, which may lead to different biological behaviors (Figure S7H). For example, high EZH2 regulon activity was observed in IMC4, which is found to be overexpressed in many cancer types and may be associated with poorer clinical outcomes(Duan et al. 2020). High CREB1 levels may enhance cell proliferative and invasive capacities in IMC3(Guo et al. 2018). High FLI1 regulon activity in IMC1 is associated with epithelial cell migration tendency and weakened cell junction organization(Guneri-Sozeri et al. 2023). DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) is inactive in IMC2, suggesting a downregulated DNA methylation state.

Taken together, we validated the molecular difference among the four IMCs and drew a transcriptional landscape of the IMCs which indicates IMC2 is associated with better prognosis, and others have higher HR of progression.

The genomic alteration features of the IMCs

Our primary analysis utilized a dataset from Prip et al.(Prip et al. 2025), with three additional datasets employed for validation. Each sample was classified as IMC based on gene expression data. We selected frequently mutated genes in bladder cancer, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. And top 20 significantly altered gene mutations are shown in Figure S9A. IMC2 is enriched in FGFR3 mutation (76.4%, p < 0.001) aligning with the results from the other two datasets (Figure S9B). And IMC2 shows a higher proportion of KDM6A (50.5%, p < 0.001) and STAG2 (32.5%, p < 0.001) alterations. IMC3 and IMC4 demonstrate enrichment of ARID1A mutation, accounting for 41.2% and 36.4% respectively. Additionally, IMC4 features TP53 mutation (95.5%, p < 0.001) and RB1 (50%, p < 0.001, Fig. 3A). A larger proportion of TP53 mutation is also observed in the other three datasets (Figure S9C). Next, we identified several critical genes involved in the TP53 pathway (TP53, ATM, RB1, MDM2, E2F3, and ATR) across all four datasets to evaluate alterations in this pathway. Alterations were defined as any genetic changes in these genes, including mutations or copy number variations. As shown in Fig. 3B, IMC4 showed significantly higher frequencies of TP53 pathway alterations compared to IMC2 and IMC3. Considering that TP53 plays an important role in DNA repair, we further investigated the mutation load. The results reveal that IMC4 exhibits a significantly higher TMB compared to the other IMCs (Fig. 3C).

According to the two different genomic classifications described by Prip et al. (iCluster)(Prip et al. 2025) and Lindskrog et al. (Genomic class)(Lindskrog et al. 2021) respectively, all IMC4 and a large proportion of IMC3 patients were classified as iClus4 or GC3 (Figures S9D and S9E), subtypes associated with maximal genomic instability and the worst PFS.

To evaluate the intra-tumor heterogeneity, weighted in silico pathology (WISP 2025) was applied (WISP 2025), which first filtered pure samples and then calculated the centroids of the pure samples based on bulk transcriptomic profiling. Consequently, each sample was assigned a weight across the three weight clusters as described in “Methods” and the associated p values are presented in Table S6. All clusters were found to exhibit heterogeneity, with IMC1 displaying the lowest degree of heterogeneity while IMC2 is the greatest (Figure S10A). Transcriptomic and genomic analyses of weight clusters revealed conserved biological features (Figures S10B-D): weight cluster 3 and 4 demonstrated potential sensitivity to conventional cytotoxic therapies, with weight cluster 4 showing TP53 and RB1 mutations; weight cluster 1 displayed immune-infiltrated characteristics marked by elevated PD-1, PD-L1, and TGF-β expression; weight cluster 2 was defined by FGFR3, STAG2, and KDM6A mutations. These results indicate the robustness of IMC-defining features.

Taken together, IMC4 is characterized by TP53 pathway alteration with the heaviest mutational load, and IMC2 features a high FGFR3 mutation rate.

The proteomic features of the IMCs

We employed a dataset from Groeneveld et al.(Groeneveld et al. 2024), which integrates transcriptomic and proteomic data (Figure S11) to analyze and validate proteomic features. The unsupervised proteomic groups (uPGs) A, B, and C demonstrate overlap with the IMC3 and IMC4, whereas IMC2 is associated with the group E (Figure S11A). Furthermore, FGFR3 genomic alterations are predominantly observed in IMC2, aligning with previous findings. To further elucidate the proteomic landscape of the IMCs, we selected the 25 most variable proteins across the uPGs as previously described(Groeneveld et al. 2024) and quantified their abundance patterns across IMCs, thus revealing the heterogeneity. As shown in prior studies, elevated STAG2 protein expression correlates with increased HR for disease progression(Taber et al. 2021, Lelo et al. 2018). Notably, IMC3 exhibited elevated STAG2 protein levels, which correlated with reduced PFS (Figures S11B and S11C).

The association of the integrated molecular clusters with clinical features, survival outcomes, and intravesical BCG instillation response

We observed distinct distributions of clinical variables across IMCs (Figs. 4A and S12A). Regardless of clinical stage, WHO 1973, or WHO 2004 grading, IMC4 exhibits a higher proportion of high-grade cases (p < 0.001). In contrast, IMC2 shows weaker progression potential, holding a majority population of low-grade (p < 0.001). IMC1 and IMC3 demonstrated intermediate characteristics across all three parameters.

Survival analysis reveals a strong association between IMCs and PFS but showed no significant differences in recurrence-free survival (RFS) (Fig. 4B). IMC3, IMC4, and IMC1 exhibit poorer PFS, whereas IMC2 demonstrates more favorable outcomes. Given that secondary MIBC progressing from post-treatment NMIBC often shows worse outcomes than primary MIBC (Pietzak et al. 2019, Grossmann et al. 2022), PFS was designated as the primary endpoint. To assess the independent prognostic value of the IMC subtypes, we performed multivariable Cox regression analysis adjusted for WHO grade 2004, clinical stage, and BCG treatment. Because the proportional hazards assumption was violated for clinical stage (p = 0.009) and BCG treatment (p = 0.039, Figure S12B), we used a stratified Cox model with stage as the stratification factor and included a time‑dependent coefficient for BCG treatment. In this model, all IMC subtypes were compared against IMC2 as the reference. IMC1 (HR = 2.18, 95% CI 1.22–3.88, p = 0.008), IMC3 (HR = 2.88, 95% CI 1.70–4.88, p < 0.001), and IMC4 (HR = 2.51, 95% CI 1.33–4.76, p = 0.005) were each independently associated with a significantly increased risk of progression (Figure S12B).

We further examined the ability of IMCs to stratify PFS across clinical stages (T1 vs. Ta). As shown in Fig. 4C, IMC3 shows the poorest PFS while IMC2 was associated with improved outcomes, indicating prognostic value beyond clinical staging. Application of the integrated molecular classification to MIBC samples (TCGA-BLCA) revealed the subtype shift: reduced IMC2 prevalence, with increases in IMC3 and IMC4 proportions (Figure S12C). This indicates that while developed using NMIBC data, this classification system captures critical gene signatures associated with clinical outcomes and tumor biology.

Furthermore, we conducted survival analyses to show whether different responses to BCG treatment were shown among IMCs. BCG-stratified survival analysis revealed improved PFS only in IMC3, with no significant changes in other subtypes (Figure S12D). Additionally, we conducted subgroup survival analysis based on guideline groups (Fig. 4D and E). Without the information on risk factors, patients with T1HG, T1G3, or CIS were classified as the high-risk and very high-risk group(Gontero et al. 2024), for whom BCG treatment is the first-line therapy, while all other patients were classified as the low-risk and intermediate-risk group. In the low-risk/intermediate-risk group, all IMCs show no significant PFS improvement after PFS. In the high-risk/very high-risk group, IMC1 and IMC3 are associated with improved PFS after BCG instillation (p = 0.011 and < 0.001 respectively). This finding could spare IMC2/IMC4 patients (31% of the high-risk/very high-risk group) unnecessary BCG overtreatment while optimizing therapy for the potential responsive subtypes.

To facilitate clinical translation, we developed a progression risk nomogram integrating IMC and clinical stage (Figure S13A). The IMC-based model demonstrated superior predictive performance to clinical staging alone (improved AUC), with further AUC improvement when combining both variables for predicting 5- or 7-year progression (Figure S13B).

Our integrated molecular classification system enables PFS stratification independent of the clinical stage. IMC2 was associated with optimal outcomes. IMC1 and IMC3 in the high-risk/very high-risk group may derive clinical benefit from BCG instillation, suggesting therapeutic utility for specific molecular subtypes.

Discussion

In this study, four IMCs were identified, each characterized by distinct transcriptomic, genomic, and clinical features, thereby addressing the intertumoral heterogeneity of NMIBC, as summarized in Fig. 5. To facilitate clinical application, a single-sample classifier was developed using a Pearson nearest centroid algorithm implemented in R, leveraging transcriptomic data. In consideration of intratumoral heterogeneity, the classifier not only assigns samples to specific clusters but also provides Pearson correlation coefficients for each cluster. Clinically, the classifier annotates a tumor sample’s transcriptome with an IMC label and would be used in clinical practice. Our results revealed distinct degrees of intertumoral heterogeneity among IMCs and further studies are required to further elucidate their prognostic and clinical implications.

Given the heterogeneous therapeutic responses to BCG in non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer, accurately forecasting treatment effectiveness remains paramount. Due to the absence of BCG-treated data, the previous transcriptome-based models (Baylor(Mo et al. 2018), Lund(Marzouka et al. 2018)) do not specifically address the prediction of BCG response. The Baylor is designed to stratify and predict the overall survival of bladder cancer, including NMIBC and MIBC. The Lund uses IHC data in addition to defining more detailed subtypes of NMIBC and MIBC. The UROMOL21(Lindskrog et al. 2021) identifies four NMIBC subtypes with varying PFS and RFS. Although it focused on the BCG response of class 2a and class 2b, no statistically significant differences were observed. The Chicago(Robertson et al. 2020) focuses on the RFS of patients at the T1 stage following BCG treatment, yet the original article reported no significant difference. Recently, de Jong et al.(Jong et al. 2023) (the Erasmus) leveraged a large BCG-treated cohort of HR-NMIBC, categorizing samples into three subtypes, among which BRS3 has the worst PFS after BCG treatment. However, the absence of untreated control data limits its ability to identify patients who may benefit from BCG instillation. Here, we sought to refine risk stratification system. Our results indicate that, in high-risk/very high-risk group, IMC1 and IMC3 derive significant benefit from BCG treatment, whereas IMC2 and IMC4 exhibit limited response, suggesting that approximately 31% of patients could potentially be spared overtreatment. This approach may facilitate better identification of candidates who are most likely to benefit from BCG instillation. Prospective studies and clinical trials are warranted for further validation.

The integrated molecular classification system provides a valuable framework for guiding treatment strategies. As noted earlier, IMC3 and IMC1 in the high-risk/very high-risk group were associated with improved PFS after BCG treatment. IMC3 and IMC4 are characterized by active proliferation, DNA replication, and late cell cycle, which indicates the potential responsiveness of chemotherapy. IMC1 exhibited immune suppression, marked by high PD-1, PD-L1, and pan-fibroblast TGF-beta expression. Elevated TGF-beta activity is likely to impede T-cell infiltration into tumors(Mariathasan et al. 2018). Preclinical studies support the use of M7824, a drug that simultaneously blocks the PD-L1 pathway and inhibits TGF-beta signaling, as a promising therapeutic candidate for urothelial cancer(Grenga et al. 2018), with ongoing clinical trials(Niglio et al. 2021). A combination of ICI and anti-TGF beta may improve the prognosis of IMC1 patients. IMC2, which exhibits the most favorable PFS, may primarily require surveillance alone. FGFR3 mutations, prevalent in ~ 60% of NMIBC cases, are known to play a critical role in tumorigenesis(Noeraparast et al. 2024). High FGFR3 mutation and expression indicate that FGFR3 inhibitors could be effective for IMC2. Oral Erdafitinib (FGFR3 inhibitor) has been shown to prolong PFS versus intravesical chemotherapy in patients with FGFR3/2 alterations following recurrence after BCG treatment; however, it is associated with a non-negligible incidence of systemic side effects(Catto et al. 2024). TAR-210, a novel drug delivery system designed to release Erdafitinib in the bladder locally and minimize systematic toxicities(Vilaseca et al. 2023), represents a promising alternative.

This study has several limitations, including the retrospective nature of clinical data collection. Also, external cohorts would be needed for further validation. The integrated classification system was constructed based on five transcriptomic classification schemes, which may not fully capture NMIBC complexity. Multi-omics subtypes are required to comprehensively characterize intertumoral heterogeneity in bladder cancer. Also, bulk-RNA expression lacks sufficient insights into intratumoral heterogeneity; single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics should be prioritized in future studies. The single-sample classifier was constructed based on 1,430 genes to ensure classification accuracy. We acknowledge that this gene set may entail substantial sequencing costs in clinical settings. Future efforts should focus on dimensionality reduction to identify a more parsimonious gene signature that retains high classification performance, thereby facilitating clinical applicability and translational feasibility. Prospective studies and randomized clinical trials are necessary before clinical adoption, and a simplified gene panel should be validated on an independent multi‑center cohort.

We developed an integrated molecular classification system for NMIBC by integrating five transcriptomic profiling-based molecular subtyping frameworks. This approach identified four IMCs exhibiting distinct transcriptomic, genomic, and clinical characteristics. Notably, we identified IMC1 and IMC3 associated with improved PFS following BCG instillation in the high-risk/very high-risk group. The proposed integrated molecular classification system establishes a preclinical framework for optimizing intravesical BCG treatment regimens and informs therapeutic strategy development. Our findings underscore the value of harmonizing molecular classification systems to refine NMIBC management and prognostic stratification, paving the way for personalized clinical management.
