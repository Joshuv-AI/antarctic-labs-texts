On the Limit of the Tridiagonal Model for β \beta -Dyson Brownian Motion
Alan Edelman, Sungwoo Jeong, Ron Nissim

Abstract
. In previous work, a description of the result of applying the Householder tridiagonalization algorithm to a G β \beta E random matrix is provided by Edelman and Dumitriu. The resulting tridiagonal ensemble makes sense for all β > 0 \beta>0 , and has spectrum given by the β \beta -ensemble for all β > 0 \beta>0 . Moreover, the tridiagonal model has useful stochastic operator limits which was introduced and analyzed in subsequent studies. In this work, we analogously study the result of applying the Householder tridiagonalization algorithm to a G β \beta E process which has eigenvalues governed by β \beta -Dyson Brownian motion. We propose an explicit limit of the upper left k × k k\times k minor of the n × n n\times n tridiagonal process as n → ∞ n\to\infty and k k remains fixed. We prove the result for β = 1 \beta=1 , and also provide numerical evidence for β = 1 , 2 , 4 \beta=1,2,4 . This result provides a speculation for the form of a dynamical β \beta -stochastic Airy operator with smallest k k eigenvalues evolving according to the n → ∞ n\to\infty limit of the largest, centered and re-scaled, k k eigenvalues of β \beta -Dyson Brownian motion. Unfortunately, additional numerical simulations and a perturbative calculation which we provide do not substantiate this speculation.



1.
Introduction

Dyson Brownian motion (DBM) introduced by Freeman Dyson in 1962
[
14
]
is a widely studied stochastic model for a system of particles that possess pairwise repulsive behavior. For any
\beta>0
,
\beta
-Dyson Brownian motion (
\beta
-DBM) is the dynamics of a system
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
undergoing the following system of stochastic differential equations

(1.1)

d\lambda_{i}=\left(\sum_{1\leq j\neq i\leq n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{2}\right)dt+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\beta}}dB_{j}.

As long as
\lambda_{1}(0),\dots,\lambda_{n}(0)
, are distinct, it is known that (
1.1
) has a unique strong solution and
\lambda_{1}(t),\dots,\lambda_{n}(t)
are a.s. distinct for all
t>0
as long as
\beta\geq 1
. Moreover
\beta
-DBM has a unique invariant distribution given by the
\beta
-ensemble (where
\mathcal{Z}
is for normalization)

(1.2)

\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\prod_{i<j}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}|^{\beta}\prod_{i=1}^{n}e^{-\lambda_{i}^{2}/4}dx_{i}.

In the random matrix context, for
\beta=1,2,4
,
\beta
-DBM describes the evolution of the eigenvalues of matrices evolving according to the G
\beta
E process, which is the following matrix valued Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process

(1.3)

dM=-Mdt+\sqrt{2}dB_{G\beta E},

that leaves the G
\beta
E distribution invariant
[
4
,
18
]
. And as a consequence G
\beta
E matrices (alternatively known as
\beta
-Hermite ensembles) have eigenvalue distribution given by (
1.2
).

For
\beta=1,2,4
the G
\beta
E matrices coincide with the highly studied Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE), Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE), and Gaussian Symplectic Ensemble (GSE) respectively. These three G
\beta
Es are described by the canonical random symmetric, Hermitian, and self-dual matrix models, respectively, endowed with Gaussian entries. For general
\beta>0
, there is no immediate generalization.

However, by applying the Householder tridiagonalization used in numerical linear algebra
[
5
,
37
]
on G
\beta
E matrices, one obtains the following symmetric tridiagonal model
[
10
]
:

(1.4)

\begin{pmatrix}N(0,2)&\chi_{\beta(n-1)}&&&&&\\
\chi_{\beta(n-1)}&N(0,2)&\chi_{\beta(n-2)}&&&&\\
&\chi_{\beta(n-2)}&\ddots&\ddots&&&\\
&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
&&&\ddots&N(0,2)&\chi_{2\beta}&\\
&&&&\chi_{2\beta}&N(0,2)&\chi_{\beta}\\
&&&&&\chi_{\beta}&N(0,2)\end{pmatrix}.

It was verified
[
10
]
that for all
\beta>0
one has (
1.2
) as the eigenvalue distribution for the tridiagonal matrix ensemble (
1.4
). In addition to providing a matrix model for the the
\beta
ensemble for all
\beta

In this work, we consider applying the Householder tridiagonalization procedure to the
n\times n
G
\beta
E process, for each

Although at a fixed time these processes are described simply as in (

), as processes they appear to be considerably more complicated. For instance, our numerical results in Section

indicate that the diagonal entries are not Gaussian processes despite their Gaussianity at a fixed time. Nevertheless, as the dimension of the matrices

, a fixed

(1.5)

\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}\mathrm{OU}(1)&\mathrm{ROU}_{\beta(n-1)}(1)&\\
\mathrm{ROU}_{\beta(n-1)}(1)&\sqrt{2}\mathrm{OU}(3)&\hskip-56.9055pt\ddots\\
&\ddots&\hskip-85.35826pt\ddots&\hskip-199.16928pt\ddots\\
&\hskip 42.67912pt\ddots&\hskip-42.67912pt\ddots\\
&&&\mathrm{ROU}_{\beta(n-k+1)}(k-1)\\
&&\hskip-28.45274pt\mathrm{ROU}_{\beta(n-k+1)}(k-1)&\sqrt{2}\mathrm{OU}(2k-1)\end{pmatrix}.

(
\mathrm{OU}(m)
denotes a stationary Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with parameters
\theta=m,\sigma=\sqrt{2m}
, and
\mathrm{ROU}_{d}(m)
is a radial Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with dimension parameter
d

\mathrm{ROU}_{d}(m)

Our main result, Theorem

, states that for
\beta=1
, the tridiagonalization of the
n\times n
G
\beta
E process, denoting diagonal entries by

and off-diagonal entries by

, as

\displaystyle a_{j}(t)

\displaystyle\to\sqrt{2}\mathrm{OU}(2j-1),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta n}}(b_{j}(t)^{2}-\beta n)

\displaystyle\to\sqrt{2}\mathrm{OU}(2j),

for entries in the
k\times k
upper-left corner submatrix process, i.e.,

. Moreover all of these limiting Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes are independent of each other. Although we only prove the statement for
\beta=1

\beta=2,4

This result suggests a simple tridiagonal model which has the same largest few eigenvalues as
\beta
-DBM in the

k=n
) and this tridiagonal model should converge to a time evolving stochastic Airy operator defined in Section

5
we further discuss conjectures and further open problems related to tridiagonal processes and their eigenvalues.

In addition to proving the main theorem, we perform numerical experiments to support our result. Since we study large matrix processes our simulations are implemented on parallel machines to increase efficiency. See Section
4
for details.

2.1.
GOE, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck Process, and Dyson Brownian Motion

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
m^{(n)}(t)
denote an
n\times n
GOE Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. That is,
\{(m^{(n)}(0))_{i,j}\}_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}
are all independent,
(m^{(n)}(0))_{i,j}=(m^{(n)}(0))_{j,i}
for all
i,j\in[n]
, and

(2.1)

\begin{cases}(m^{(n)}(0))_{i,j}\sim N(0,1)\hskip 8.53581pt\text{if }i\neq j\\
(m^{(n)}(0))_{i,j}\sim N(0,\sqrt{2})\hskip 8.53581pt\text{if }i=j\end{cases}

and
m^{(n)}(t)
evolves according to

(2.2)

\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{m^{(n)}}(t)=-m^{(n)}(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}+\sqrt{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{B^{(n)}_{\mathrm{GOE}}}(t)

where
B^{(n)}_{\mathrm{GOE}}(t):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(B^{(n)}(t)+B^{(n)}(t)^{T})
and
B^{(n)}(t)
is an
n\times n
matrix with independent standard Brownian Motion entries.
It is well known that this SDE is globally well-posed and the unique solution
m^{(n)}(t)
is a stationary matrix valued Gaussian process with covariances given by

(2.3)

\mathbb{E}[(m^{(n)}(t))_{i_{1},j_{1}}(m^{(n)}(t))_{i_{2},j_{2}}]=\delta_{\{i_{1},j_{1}\},\{i_{2},j_{2}\}}(1+\delta_{i_{1},j_{1}})e^{-|t-s|}.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
we take a (continuous) copy of
m^{(n)}(t)
which will all be processes living in a single probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.

Let us also define for more general choice of parameters, the stationary Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.

Definition 2.1
.

The normalized stationary scalar Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process
\mathrm{OU}(c)
, is the unique solution to

(2.4)

\begin{split}&dx(t)=-mx(t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}+\sqrt{2m}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{W(t)}\\
&x(0)\sim N(0,1)\end{split}

for a standard one dimensional Brownian motion
W(t)
. Or equivalently,
\mathrm{OU}(m)
is a stationary Gaussian process for all time with covariances given by
\mathbb{E}[x(t)x(s)]=e^{-m|t-s|}
. Furthermore if
m=(m_{1},...,m_{n})
denote by
\mathbf{OU}_{n}(m)
the vector valued Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process that has independent
\mathrm{OU}(m_{i})
entries. If
c
is scalar, the notation
\mathbf{OU}_{n}(m)
will be short for
\mathbf{OU}_{n}((m,...,m))
.

Lastly we define the stationary radial Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process.

Definition 2.2
.

The normalized stationary radial Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process
\mathrm{ROU}_{d}(m)
, is the unique solution to

(2.5)

\begin{split}&dR(t)=\bigg(\frac{m(d-1)}{R(t)}-mR(t)\bigg)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{t}+m\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{W(t)}\\
&R(0)\sim\chi_{d}\end{split}

for a standard one dimensional Brownian motion
W(t)
.

Remark 2.3
.

In the case that
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathrm{ROU}_{d}(m)=||\mathbf{OU}_{d}(m)||
in distribution.

2.2.
Householder Matrices and Tridiagonalization

Definition 2.4

(Householder Reflector)
.

For a given unit vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, an
n\times n
matrix
H=I-2vv^{T}
is called a Householder reflector.

Remark 2.5
.

The matrix
H
geometrically represents a flip about the plane orthogonal to
v
. For any given vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, letting
v=\frac{x-\|x\|e_{1}}{\|x-\|x\|e_{1}\|}
we obtain a Householder reflector
H
by Definition
2.4
. Then we have

Hx=\begin{pmatrix}\|x\|\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover any Householder reflector
H
is both symmetric and orthogonal.

Next we recall the well-known Householder matrix based tridiagonalization procedure which starts with a symmetric matrix
A
and outputs a symmetric tridiagonal matrix
T
which is similar to
A
.

Algorithm 2.6

(Householder Tridiagonalization)
.

(1)

Given a symmetric
n\times n
matrix
A
, set
A=A_{0}
and
k=0

(2)

Write the
(n-k)\times(n-k)
matrix
A_{k}
in the block form

(2.6)

A_{k}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&x_{k}^{T}\\
\hline\cr x_{k}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\tilde{A}_{k}\end{pmatrix}

where
a_{k}
is a
1\times 1
,
x_{k}
is
(n-k-1)\times 1
and
\tilde{A}_{k}
is
(n-k-1)\times(n-k-1)

(3)

Take
v_{k}:=\frac{x_{k}-\|x_{k}\|e_{k+2}}{\|x_{k}-\|x_{k}\|e_{k+2}\|}
, where
x_{k}
is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by taking the first
k+1
entries to be
0
, and define the Householder matrix
H_{k}=I_{n}-2v_{k}v_{k}^{T}
. Note that
H_{k}
can be written in the following block form,

(2.7)

H_{k}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&0\\
\hline\cr 0&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\tilde{H}_{k}\end{pmatrix},

where
\tilde{H}_{k}
is an
(n-k-1)\times(n-k-1)

(4)

Preform the similarity transformation

(2.8)

\begin{split}H_{k}^{T}A_{k}H_{k}&=\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\|x_{k}\|&0\\
\hline\cr\begin{matrix}\|x_{k}\|\\
0\end{matrix}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&\tilde{H}_{k}^{T}\tilde{A}_{k}\tilde{H}_{k}\end{pmatrix}\\
&=:\begin{pmatrix}a_{k}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&b_{k}&0\\
\hline\cr\begin{matrix}b_{k}\\
0\end{matrix}&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&A_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}\end{split}

(5)

If:
k<n-2

and go back to Step 2.

Else: Return a tridiagonal matrix

(2.9)

T:=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
b_{1}&a_{2}&b_{2}&&\vdots\\
0&b_{2}&a_{3}&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&b_{n-1}\\

Lemma 2.9
.

For any
T,\epsilon>0
, as long as
n>k+3
,

(2.11)

\mathbb{P}\left(x_{k}\in C^{1/2-\epsilon}([0,T]),\vartheta_{k}(t)-\|\vartheta_{k}(t)\|e_{k+2}\neq 0\text{ for all }t\in[0,T]\right)=1.

Proof.

The proof is by induction. For the
k=0
case
x_{0}(t)
is a vector-valued Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, so
x_{0}\in C^{1/2-\epsilon}([0,T])
. Additionally,
\|x_{0}(t)-\|x_{0}(t)\|e_{2}\|\geq\|(x_{0}^{(2)}(t),...,x_{0}^{(n+1)}(t))\|
which is the norm of the vector with all but the first component. But since
n>3
, by classical facts about Brownian motion (e.g.,
[
19
]
), this norm is a.s. non-zero for all
t\in[0,T]
.

For the inductive step,
M(t)\in C^{1/2-\epsilon}([0,T])
, and since there was a.s. no division by zero in the algebraic operations producing
x_{k}(t)
, we clearly have
x_{k}(t)\in C^{1/2-\epsilon}([0,T])
. Now it suffices to prove that
\tilde{x}_{k}(t)=(\tilde{x}_{k}^{(1)}(t),...,\tilde{x}_{k}^{(n-k)}(t)):=(x_{k}^{(k+2)}(t),...,x_{k}^{(n+1)}(t))\neq 0
for all
t\in[0,T]
almost surely. To see this, divide the interval
[0,T]
into
N+1
equally spaced points
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with spacing
|t_{i+1}-t_{i}|=\delta=T/N
. Then using the fact that
\left\{\tilde{x}_{k}^{(j)}(t_{1})\right\}_{j=1}^{n-k}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussians (i.e. Remark
2.8
), after fixing any constant
C>0
,

(2.12)

\begin{split}~&\mathbb{P}(\tilde{x}_{k}(t)=0\text{ for some }t\in[0,T])\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\|\tilde{x}_{k}(t)\|_{C^{1/2-\epsilon}}>C\text{ or }\|\tilde{x}_{k}(t_{i})\|<C\delta^{1/2-\epsilon}\text{ for some }i\right)\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}(\|\tilde{x}_{k}\|_{C^{1/2-\epsilon}}>C)+N\mathbb{P}(\|\tilde{x}_{k}(t_{1})\|<C\delta^{1/2-\epsilon})\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}(\|\tilde{x}_{k}\|_{C^{1/2-\epsilon}}>C)+N\mathbb{P}(|\tilde{x}_{k}^{(j)}(t_{1})|<C\delta^{1/2-\epsilon}\text{ for }j=1,\dots,n-k)\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}(\|\tilde{x}_{k}(t)\|_{C^{1/2-\epsilon}}>C)+N(C\delta^{1/2-\epsilon})^{n-k-1}.\end{split}

Taking
N\to\infty
(
\delta=T/N\to 0
), and then
C\to\infty
, as long as
(1/2-\epsilon)(n-k-1)>1
, we have
\mathbb{P}(\tilde{x}_{k}(t)=0\text{ for some }t\in[0,T])=0
. Since

, as long as
\epsilon<1/6
, this argument holds.

2.3.
Chebyshev Polynomials

In this section we collect a few facts and identities about the Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind, which can be found in
[
1
]
, that will be used in the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

The Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind
U_{k}(x)
are orthogonal on
L^{2}([-1,1],\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}dx)
. They have the three term recurrence,
U_{0}(1)=1
,

U_{k+1}(x)=2xU_{k}(x)-U_{k-1}(x).

Let us also define
P_{k}(x)=U_{k}(x/2)
which are monic, orthonormal on
L^{2}([-2,2],\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{1-\frac{x^{2}}{4}}dx)
, and satisfy the recurrence,
P_{0}(1)=1
,

(2.13)

P_{k+1}(x)=xP_{k}(x)-P_{k-1}(x).

From the recursion its easy to prove the following formula by induction

(2.14)

P_{k}(x)=\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{[k/2]}(-1)^{k^{\prime}}\binom{k-k^{\prime}}{k^{\prime}}x^{k-2k^{\prime}}.

Also,
P_{k}(x)
satisfies the following product-sum identity,

P_{j}(x)P_{k}(x)=\sum_{\ell\leq[(j+k)/2]}P_{j+k-\ell}(x).

As a direct corollary,
P_{k}(x)^{2}-P_{k-1}(x)^{2}=P_{2k}(x)

(P_{k}(x)-P_{k-2}(x))P_{k-1}(x)=P_{2k-1}(x)

.

The set of non-crossing pair partitions

for

For each

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[(M_{i})_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}(M_{j})_{\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime}}\right]=\left(\delta_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2}),(\ell_{1}^{\prime},\ell_{2}^{\prime})}+\delta_{(\ell_{1},\ell_{2}),(\ell_{2}^{\prime},\ell_{1}^{\prime})}\right)\varphi_{i,j}.\end{split}

2.5.
Concentration for Empirical Distribution Moments

Here we recall the following central limit theorem and deduce an easy corollary.

Theorem 2.16
.

Let
M(t)
be the stationary GOE processes. Then for
k
fixed times
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
and exponents
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}
the vector

\left(n^{-j_{1}/2}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{1})^{j_{1}})-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{1})^{j_{1}})]\right),\dots,n^{-j_{2}/2}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{k})^{j_{k}})-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{k})^{j_{k}})]\right)\right),

converges both weakly and in the sense of moments to a Gaussian vector, as
n\to\infty
. Moreover, as a process

\left(n^{-j_{1}/2}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(M(t)^{j_{1}})-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t)^{j_{1}})]\right),\dots,n^{-j_{k}/2}\left(\mathrm{Tr}(M(t)^{j_{k}})-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t)^{j_{k}})]\right)\right),

converges weakly in
C[0,\infty)
to a Gaussian process.

For a proof see for instance
[
4
, Theorem 4.3.20]
.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
M(t)
be the stationary GOE processes. Then for
k
fixed times
t_{1},\dots,t_{k}
and exponents
j_{1},\dots,j_{k}
we have,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{1})^{j_{1}})\dots\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{k})^{j_{k}})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{1})^{j_{1}})\right]\dots\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{k})^{j_{k}})\right]+O(n^{(j_{1}+\dots+j_{k})/2+k-1}).

Proof.

We can write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{1})^{j_{1}})\dots\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{k})^{j_{k}})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{1})^{j_{1}})]+(\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{1})^{j_{1}})-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{1})^{j_{1}})])\right)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\dots\left.\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{k})^{j_{k}})]+(\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{k})^{j_{k}})-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{k})^{j_{k}})])\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\subset[k]}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{i\in K}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{i})^{j_{i}})]\prod_{i^{\prime}\notin K}(\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{i^{\prime}})^{j_{i^{\prime}}})-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{i^{\prime}})^{j_{i^{\prime}}})])\bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\subset[k]}n^{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[k]\backslash K}j_{i^{\prime}}/2}\prod_{i\in K}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{i})^{j_{i}})]\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{i^{\prime}\notin K}n^{-j_{i^{\prime}}/2}(\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{i^{\prime}})^{j_{i^{\prime}}})-\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{i^{\prime}})^{j_{i^{\prime}}})])\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{1})^{j_{1}})]...\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{k})^{j_{k}})]+O(n^{(j_{1}+...+j_{k})/2+k-1}).

In the last line we used Theorem
2.16
with the fact that
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(M(t_{i})^{j_{i}})]=O(n^{j_{i}/2+1})
.
∎

3.
Main Result

3.1.
Statement of Main Result

We begin by stating the main result of this work.

Theorem 3.1

(Convergence of Tridiagonal Entries)
.

Fix a
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\{a_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n},\{b_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n-1}
be the tridiagonal entry (processes) as in (
2.9
), from the
n\times n
tridiagonalized GOE process. Define

E_{n}(t):=\left(a_{1}(t),\dots,a_{k}(t),\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(b_{1}(t)^{2}-n),\dots,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(b_{k}(t)^{2}-n)\right).

Then,
E_{n}(t)
converges weakly in
C[0,\infty)
to a vector process
E(t)=(A_{1}(t),\dots,A_{k}(t),B_{1}(t),\dots,B_{k}(t))
as
n\to\infty
, where

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}E(t)\sim\mathbf{OU}_{2k}(1,3,\dots,2k-1,2,4,\dots,2k).

More explicitly, the components
A_{j}(t)\in C[0,\infty)
and
B_{j}(t)\in C[0,\infty)
are jointly-independent centered Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes with covariances given as
\mathrm{Cov}(A_{j}(t),A_{j}(s))=2e^{-(2j-1)|t-s|}
and
\mathrm{Cov}(B_{j}(t),B_{j}(s))=2e^{-2j|t-s|}
, for
j=1,\dots,k
.

3.2.
Outline for proof of Theorem
3.1

The proof of Theorem
3.1
can be divided into two main steps, Theorems
3.2
and
3.4
. The proofs of these steps can be found in Sections
6
and
7
respectively. The following theorem is the first step, with detailed proof given in Section
6
. We approximate
E_{n}(t)
by a random vector using approximated tridiagonal entries in Definition
2.20
whose moments are easier to compute.

Theorem 3.2
.

For some constants
C_{k,T}
and
n_{0}(k,T)
, we almost surely have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|a_{j}(t)-\tilde{a}_{j}(t)|\leq C(k,T)\frac{\log(n)^{j}}{\sqrt{n}},

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\left|b_{j}(t)^{2}-\tilde{b}_{j}(t)^{2}\right|\leq C(k,T)\frac{\log(n)^{2j}}{\sqrt{n}},

for all
n\geq n_{0}(k,T)
and
j\leq k
. In particular, letting

\tilde{E}_{n}(t):=\left(\tilde{a}_{1}(t),\dots,\tilde{a}_{k}(t),\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\tilde{b}_{1}(t)^{2}-n),\dots,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\tilde{b}_{k}(t)^{2}-n)\right),

we almost surely have

(3.1)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|E_{n}(t)-\tilde{E}_{n}(t)\|=0.

Remark 3.3
.

These
\tilde{a}_{j}
and
\tilde{b}_{j}
can be expressed in terms of the Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind. This is not so surprising as
n\to\infty
the tridiagonal model for G
\beta
E has spectral measure given by the semicircle law, which has Chebyshev polynomials of the second kind as its associated orthogonal polynomials, and the entries of infinite Jacobi matrices can be expressed in terms of the orthogonal polynomials with respect to their spectral measure (for further details see
[
8
]
).

The key observation for proving Theorem
3.2
, is that the algorithm for obtaining the vectors
\{\vartheta_{j}(t)\}_{j=0}^{k}
from the Householder tridiagonalization procedure, is approximately the result of applying the Gram-Schmidt process to the set of vectors
\left\{(\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})^{j}\theta(t)\right\}_{j=0}^{k}
. However, due to the large dimension
n
, concentration inequalities tells us that the inner products concentrate around their expectation and thus for a fixed
t\in[0,T]
,

\begin{split}\left((\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})^{j_{1}}\theta(t)\right)^{T}\left((\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})^{j_{2}}\theta(t)\right)&\approx\mathbb{E}\left[\left((\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})^{j_{1}}\theta(t)\right)^{T}\left((\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})^{j_{2}}\theta(t)\right)\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left((\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})^{j_{1}}(\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})^{j_{2}}\right)\right]\\
&\approx\int_{-2}^{2}x^{j_{1}}\cdot x^{j_{2}}\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^{2}}dx,\end{split}

where the last step follows by the semicircle law. As a result, we naturally have the approximation
\vartheta_{j}(t)\approx\theta_{j}(t)=P_{j}(\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})\theta(t)
where
\{P_{j}(x)\}
are the orthogonal polynomials with respect to the semicircle measure
\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^{2}}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|\leq 2}
.

With slightly more careful approach we obtain
\vartheta_{k}(t)\approx\frac{\theta_{k}(t)}{\|\theta_{k-1}(t)\|}
. Thus after plugging into the expressions for
a_{j}(t)
and
b_{j}(t)
, after some calculation, we obtain

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}a_{j+1}(t)\approx(\theta_{j}(t)-\theta_{j-2}(t))^{T}\theta_{j}(t),

and

\frac{1}{n}(b_{j+1}(t)^{2}-n)\approx\frac{\|\theta_{j}(t)\|^{2}}{\|\theta_{j-1}(t)\|^{2}}-1.

Finally the exact expressions for
\tilde{a}_{j}(t)
and
\tilde{b}_{j}(t)
come from applying an algebraic identity for Chebyshev polynomials to the above expressions. The precise concentration and chaining bounds, which are needed to make all the approximations uniform, are deferred to the appendix.

The second step, carried out in detail in Section
7
, is to prove the weak convergence for the approximate entries of our tridiagonal matrix.

Theorem 3.4

(Weak Convergence of Approximate Tridiagonal Entries)
.

Fix a
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
T>0
and define a vector of approximated tridiagonal entry processes,

\tilde{E}_{n}(t):=\left(\tilde{a}_{1}(t),\dots,\tilde{a}_{k}(t),\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\tilde{b}_{1}(t)^{2}-n),\dots,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(\tilde{b}_{k}(t)^{2}-n)\right).

Then,
\tilde{E}_{n}(t)
converges weakly in
C[0,T]
to a vector process
E(t)=(A_{1}(t),\dots,A_{k}(t),B_{1}(t),\dots,B_{k}(t))
, distributed as
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}E(t)\sim\mathbf{OU}_{2k}(1,3,\dots,2k-1,2,4,\dots,2k)
. More explicitly, the components
A_{j}(t)\in C[0,T]
and
B_{j}(t)\in C[0,T]
are jointly-independent centered Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes with covariances
\mathrm{Cov}(A_{j}(t),A_{j}(s))=2e^{-(2j-1)|t-s|}
and
\mathrm{Cov}(B_{j}(t),B_{j}(s))=2e^{-2j|t-s|}
, for
j=1,\dots,k
.

The proof of Theorem
3.4
can be broken down into the convergence of finite dimensional marginals, and showing tightness. Tightness will follow from quantitative continuity estimates which are proven in the same section of the appendix as the chaining argument for the first step above.

The convergence of finite dimensional distributions will follow from the convergence of moments. A key quantity in the calculation will be
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(P_{j_{1}}(\tilde{M}(t_{1})/\sqrt{n}))P_{j_{2}}(\tilde{M}(t_{2})/\sqrt{n}))]
. From the semicircle law we know the result is
\approx\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}}
when
t_{1}=t_{2}
. A combinatorial argument involving non-crossing partitions and an explicit formula for
P_{j}(x)
, allows us to compute the value for any
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. We will prove that

(3.2)

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left(P_{j_{1}}(\tilde{M}(t_{1})/\sqrt{n}))P_{j_{2}}(\tilde{M}(t_{2})/\sqrt{n})\right)\right]\approx\delta_{j_{1},j_{2}}e^{-j_{1}|t_{1}-t_{2}|}.

After this preliminary computation, Wick’s formula will express joint moments of
\tilde{E}_{n}(t)
as a sum indexed by pairings
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\{1,2\}\times J)
for some index set
J
. Another combinatorial argument allows us to convert this sum into another one indexed by permutations in
\mathcal{S}(J)
. At this point the leading order terms are extracted through combining (
3.2
) together with Lemma
2.17
.

Combining our two steps, we can prove the main theorem as follows:

Proof of Theorem
3.1
given Theorems
3.2
and
3.4
.

For any
f\in C_{b}(C[0,T])
,

(3.3)

\mathbb{E}[f(E_{n})]=\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{E}_{n})]+\mathbb{E}[f(E_{n})-f(\tilde{E}_{n})]

but
\mathbb{E}[f(\tilde{E}_{n})]\to\mathbb{E}[f(E)]
by Theorem
3.2
, and
\mathbb{E}[f(E_{n})-f(\tilde{E}_{n})]\to 0
by the bounded convergence theorem, since
f
is bounded and
f(E_{n})-f(\tilde{E}_{n})\to 0
a.s. by continuity of
f
and the fact that
\|E_{n}-\tilde{E}_{n}\|_{C[0,T]}\to 0
by Theorem
3.4
. Thus,
E_{n}\to E
weakly in
C[0,T]
.

Next we can equip
C[0,\infty)
with the metric
d(f,g):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\max_{t\in[0,k]}(1\wedge|f(t)-g(t)|)
. Moreover take a sequence of functions
\eta_{m}(t)\in C[0,\infty)
with
\eta_{m}\equiv 1
on
[0,m]
,
\eta\equiv 0
outside of
[0,2m]
, and
\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}=1
. Fixing any
f\in C_{b}(C[0,\infty))
, for all
\nu(t)\in C[0,2m]
, we can define
f_{m}(\nu):=f(\eta_{m}\nu)
. We clearly have
f_{m}\in C_{b}(C[0,2m])
. From the triangle inequality we now have,

\begin{split}&\limsup_{n\to\infty}|f(E_{n})-f(E)|\\
&\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}|f_{m}(E_{n})-f_{m}(E)|+\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|f\|d(\eta_{m}E_{n},E_{n})\\
&\leq\|f\|2^{1-m}.\end{split}

Since this is true for all
m
, we have
f(E_{n})\to f(E)
.
∎

5.
Past Work, Future Directions, and Open Problems

5.1.
Past Work

First, we briefly summarize two related previous works
[
3
,
23
]
on matrix models for
\beta
-DBM. In particular
[
23
]
provides tridiagonal models for
\beta
-DBM in a different direction. To explain their results we first recall that in
[
10
]
, the authors establish a bijection between symmetric tridiagonal matrices and their eigenvalues together with the spectral weights (first entry of each eigenvector). Thus, to construct a symmetric tridiagonal model for
\beta
-DBM, we have the freedom of choosing the evolution of the spectral weights. In
[
23
]
, the authors classify the tridiagonal models for
\beta
-DBM through an SDE involving the spectral weights, for a large class of spectral weights. Moreover, in the frozen (constant) spectral weight case, they provide asymptotics for finite upper left blocks as
n\to\infty
through a limiting SDE they satisfy. Our result concerns a different situation since the spectral weights for the GOE ensemble are a.s. not constant. Moreover our approach also largely differs in proof techniques. In another paper
[
28
]
, the author proposes a dynamical tridiagonal
\beta
-Hermite ensemble, however, this model does not exactly have eigenvalues evolving according to
\beta
-DBM, and when contrasted with our main result, does not have a form which results from applying the Householder tridiagonalization procedure to the G
\beta
E process.

Lastly in a series of recent works
[
22
,
13
,
24
]
, the authors found explicit formulas for the moments and Laplace transform of the
\beta
-Airy line ensemble by finding formulas for the G
\beta
E corners process and taking appropriate scaling limits. The proofs of these impressive results do not use the tridiagonalization of the G
\beta
E process studied in this paper.

5.2.
Future Directions and Open Problems

Our proof of Theorem
3.1
should generalize in a straightforward manner to the
\beta=2,4
cases. The precise statement for this generalization is the following.

Problem 1:
Suppose
M^{(n)}(t)
is a sequence of stationary
n\times n
matrix processes evolving according to the G
\beta
E process for
\beta\in\{2,4\}
. After applying the Householder tridiagonalization to obtain diagonal entries
a_{1}^{(n)}(t),a_{2}^{(n)}(t),\dots,a_{n}^{(n)}(t)
and off-diagonal entries
b_{1}^{(n)}(t),b_{2}^{(n)}(t),\dots,b_{n}^{(n)}(t)
, prove that for any fixed
k
,

\begin{split}~&\left(a_{1}(t),\dots,a_{k}(t),\frac{1}{\beta\sqrt{n}}(b_{1}(t)^{2}-\beta n),\dots,\frac{1}{\beta\sqrt{n}}(b_{k}(t)^{2}-\beta n)\right)\\
&\hskip 142.26378pt\to\left(A_{1}(t),\dots,A_{k}(t),B_{1}(t),\dots,B_{k}(t)\right),\end{split}

as
n\to\infty
, with the entries of the limiting vector being jointly-independent as in the
\beta=1
case.

A less straightforward direction is to try to extend our results to the
\beta
-Laguerre and
\beta
-Jacobi ensembles. Here we briefly pose a problem for the L
\beta
E process.

Problem 2:
Take
\beta\in\{1,2,4\}
. Suppose
M^{(n)}(t)
is a sequence of
n\times n
stationary matrix process evolving according to
\beta
-Laguerre diffusion. Can we extract a limit of the
k\times k
upper left corner of
M^{(n)}(t)
for
k
fixed and
n\to\infty
?

The next problem is to prove that the pattern extends to entries further down the tridiagonal. One way to formulate this problem would be the following.

Problem 3:

For what threshold
k(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, can we prove that for any
k_{1}(n),\dots,k_{j}(n)\to\infty
and
k_{1}(n),\dots,k_{j}(n)\leq k(n)

\begin{split}~&\left(a_{k_{1}(n)}^{(n)}(t/k_{1}(n)),\dots,a_{k_{j}(n)}^{(n)}(t/k_{j}(n)),\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta(n-k_{1}(n))}}\left(b_{k_{1}(n)}^{(n)}(t/k_{1}(n))^{2}-\beta(n-k_{1}(n))\right),\right.\\
&\hskip 142.26378pt\dots,\left.\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta(n-k_{1}(n))}}\left(b_{k_{j}(n)}^{(n)}(t/k_{j}(n))^{2}-\beta(n-k_{j}(n))\right)\right)\\
&\hskip 227.62204pt\to(A_{1}(t),\dots,A_{j}(t),B_{1}(t),\dots,B_{j}(t))\end{split}

weakly in
C[0,\infty)
, where the components
A_{i}(t)\in C[0,\infty)
and
B_{i}(t)\in C[0,\infty)
are independent centered Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with covariances given as
\mathrm{Cov}(A_{i}(t),A_{i}(s))=2e^{-2|t-s|}
and
\mathrm{Cov}(B_{j}(t),B_{j}(s))=2e^{-2|t-s|}
for

.

The proofs in this paper should be fairly easily modified if one takes

for some
\alpha
for instance, however we would like to be able to take

Finally we state the problem which initially motivated this paper.

If

If one assumes that the pattern of entries in Theorem

Definition 5.1
.

We define Ornstein–Uhlenbeck space-time white noise as a one parameter family of random continuous functions of time into space distributions
\xi_{a}:[0,\infty)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}((0,\infty))
, such that for any test functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,\infty))
,
(\langle\xi_{a}(t_{1}),f_{1}\rangle,\dots,\langle\xi_{a}(t_{k}),f_{k}\rangle)
is a centered Gaussian process, and

(5.1)

\mathbb{E}[\langle\xi_{a}(t),f_{i}\rangle\langle\xi_{a}(s),f_{j}\rangle]=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-a|t-s|x}f_{i}(x)f_{j}(x)dx.

Remark 5.2
.

One can verify that
\xi_{a}\in C([0,\infty):C^{-1/2-\epsilon}(0,\infty))
for any
\epsilon>0

Definition 5.3

We define a time–dependent
\beta

(5.2)

\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(t):=-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+x+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\beta}}\xi_{1}(t).

More precisely, for

, define
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(t)f
as the distribution such that for any

\langle\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(t)f,g\rangle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}(f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(x)+xf(x)g(x))dx+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\beta}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)g(x)\xi_{1}(t)[dx],

with
\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)g(x)\xi_{1}(t)[dx]=\langle\xi_{1}(t),f(x)g(x)\rangle
.

The following two lemmas will be used to define the eigenvalues of

Lemma 5.4
.

For any

(5.4)

\langle f,\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(t)g\rangle,

is well defined as
\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle f_{n},\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(t)g\rangle

Lemma 5.5
.

For any fixed
t

(5.5)

\lambda_{1}(t):=\inf_{f\in H_{0}^{1,1},||f||_{L^{2}}=1}\langle f,\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(t)g\rangle,

the smallest eigenvalue of
\mathcal{H}_{\beta}(t)

The smallest
k

Remark 5.6

(

In this remark we discuss a perturbative calculation showing that the operator of definition

should not be the conjectured limit of Problem 4
[

A first order perturbation of the operator of Definition

about the deterministic Airy operator,
-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+x
suggests the expansion,

\lambda_{i}(t)=-a_{i}+\frac{2}{\sqrt{\beta}}\langle\xi_{1}(t),\psi_{i}^{2}\rangle+\dots,

where
\psi_{i}(x):=\frac{\mathrm{Ai}(x+a_{i})}{\mathrm{Ai^{\prime}}(a_{i})}
is the
i
th eigenfunction of the Airy operator (Recall
\mathrm{Ai}(x)
is the Airy function and
a_{i}
is the
i
th zero of the Airy function). So plugging in this perturbation appropriate for large

(5.6)

\mathrm{Cov}(\lambda_{i}(t_{1}),\lambda_{j}(t_{2}))\approx\frac{4}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{i}(x)^{2}\psi_{j}(x)^{2}e^{-x|t_{1}-t_{2}|}dx.

On the other hand, there already exist rigorously derived expressions for the first order in
\beta^{-1/2}
perturbation of the two time covariance between eigenvalues in the Airy
\beta
–line ensemble (i.e.
[
20
, Theorem 1.2]
and
[

(5.7)

\mathrm{Cov}(\lambda_{i}(t_{1}),\lambda_{j}(t_{2}))=\frac{2}{\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-x|t_{1}-t_{2}|}}{x}\psi_{i}(x)\psi_{j}(x)dx+o(\beta^{-1}).

We now give an argument why (
5.6

) do not agree in general. Take

,
t_{1}=0
, and

. Observe that
\psi_{1}(0)=\frac{\mathrm{Ai}(a_{1})}{\mathrm{Ai}^{\prime}(a_{1})}=0
, and
\psi_{1}^{\prime}(0)=\frac{\mathrm{Ai}^{\prime}(a_{1})}{\mathrm{Ai}^{\prime}(a_{1})}=1

\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{1}(x)^{4}e^{-tx}dx\sim\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{4}e^{-tx}dx\sim\frac{4!}{t^{5}},

Proof of Theorem
3.2

The main ingredient for proving Theorem
3.2
is the following proposition, which is proved at the end of this section.

Proposition 6.1
.

For any fixed
k
, there exist constants
C(T,k),C^{\prime}(T,k)
such that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|a_{j}^{(n)}(t)-\tilde{a}_{j}^{(n)}(t)|>C(T,k)\frac{\log(n)^{j}}{\sqrt{n}}\text{ for some }j\leq k\bigg)<C^{\prime}(T,k)n^{-2},

and

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}|b_{j}^{(n)}(t)^{2}-\tilde{b}_{j}^{(n)}(t)^{2}|>C(T,k)\frac{\log(n)^{2j}}{\sqrt{n}}\text{ for some }j\leq k\bigg)<C^{\prime}(T,k)n^{-2}.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
, given Proposition
6.1
.

We have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|a_{j}^{(n)}(t)-\tilde{a}_{j}^{(n)}(t)|>C(T,k)\frac{\log(n)^{j}}{\sqrt{n}}\text{ for some }j\leq k\bigg)\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}C^{\prime}(T,k)n^{-2}<\infty.

Thus, by the Borel-Cantelli lemma we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|a_{j}^{(n)}(t)-\tilde{a}_{j}^{(n)}(t)|>C(T,k)\frac{\log(n)^{j}}{\sqrt{n}}\text{ for some }j\leq k,\text{ for infinitely many }n\bigg)=0,

and in particular almost surely there exists an
n_{0}(T,k)
such that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|a_{j}^{(n)}(t)-\tilde{a}_{j}^{(n)}(t)|\leq C(T,k)\frac{\log(n)^{j}}{\sqrt{n}}
for all
n\geq n_{0}(T,k)
. An identical argument proves there exists an
n_{0}^{\prime}(T,k)
such that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|b_{j}^{(n)}(t)^{2}-\tilde{b}_{j}^{(n)}(t)^{2}|\leq C(T,k)\frac{\log(n)^{2j}}{\sqrt{n}}
for all
n\geq n_{0}(T,k)
. Redefining
n_{0}(T,k)
as the maximum of the two completes the proof.
∎

, with number of terms and coefficients only depending on
k
. This completes the proof of (
6.6
). The proof of the inductive step will involve repeated applications of (
6.6
) in tandem with the induction hypothesis and Lemma
6.3
.

For the inductive step, we seek to prove (
6.5
) in the
k+1
case. First, from the construction of the Householder matrix,
Q_{k}(t)e_{k+2}=\frac{\vartheta_{k}(t)}{||\vartheta_{k}(t)||}
. Once again recalling that
|\vartheta_{j}(t)^{T}\vartheta_{j^{\prime}}(t)-\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}|\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}
and
|\vartheta_{j}(t)^{T}e_{j^{\prime}}|\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}
for
j,j^{\prime}\leq k
, we have

\begin{split}~&Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k}(t)e_{k+2}\\
&=\bigg(I+\sum_{i<k,j\leq k}(c_{i,j}(t)\vartheta_{i}(t)e_{j}^{T}+d_{i,j}(t)e_{j}\vartheta_{i}(t)^{T})+\sum_{j,j^{\prime}<k}f_{j,j^{\prime}}(t)\vartheta_{j}(t)\vartheta_{j^{\prime}}(t)^{T}\bigg)\frac{\vartheta_{k}(t)}{||\vartheta_{k}(t)||}\\
&=\frac{\vartheta_{k}(t)}{||\vartheta_{k}(t)||}+\varepsilon_{k}(t),\end{split}

where
||\varepsilon_{k}(t)||\leq C_{k}\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}
, and
\varepsilon_{k}(t)\in\mathrm{Span}\{\vartheta_{j}(t)\}_{j\leq k-1}+\mathrm{Span}\{e_{j}(t)\}_{2\leq j\leq k+1}\subset\Theta_{k-1}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k}(t)
. Applying the induction hypothesis to the last equation display, we can write

Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k}(t)e_{k+2}=\left(c_{k}(t)+\mu_{k}(t)\right)\frac{\theta_{k}(t)}{||\vartheta_{k}(t)||}+\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}(t),

where
||\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}(t)||\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)^{k}}{\sqrt{n}}
, and
\varepsilon_{k}^{\prime}(t)\in\Theta_{k-1}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k}(t)
.

Using the three term recurrence for the Chebyshev polynomials (
2.13
) and the norm bound of Lemma
6.3
, we have,

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\tilde{M}(t)Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k}(t)e_{k+2}\\
&=\frac{c_{k}(t)+\mu_{k}(t)}{||\vartheta_{k}(t)||}(\theta_{k+1}(t)+\theta_{k-1}(t))+\varepsilon_{k+1}(t)\\
&=\frac{c_{k}(t)+\mu_{k}(t)}{||\vartheta_{k}(t)||}(\theta_{k+1}(t)+\vartheta_{k-1}(t))+\varepsilon_{k+1}^{\prime}(t),\end{split}

where
||\varepsilon_{k+1}(t)||,||\varepsilon_{k+1}^{\prime}(t)||\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)^{k+1}}{\sqrt{n}}
, and
\varepsilon_{k+1}(t),\varepsilon_{k+1}^{\prime}(t)\in\Theta_{k}(t)+\tilde{M}(t)\mathcal{E}_{k}(t)\subseteq\Theta_{k}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(t)
. Then once again using the induction hypothesis, Lemma
6.3
, and equation (
6.6
), we get

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}Q_{k-2}(t)\cdots Q_{0}(t)\tilde{M}(t)Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k}(t)e_{k+2}\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt=\frac{c_{k}(t)+\mu_{k}(t)}{||\vartheta_{k}(t)||}(\theta_{k+1}(t)+\vartheta_{k-1}(t))+\varepsilon_{k+1}^{\prime\prime}(t),\end{split}

where
||\varepsilon_{k+1}^{\prime\prime}(t)||\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)^{k+1}}{\sqrt{n}}
, and
\varepsilon_{k+1}^{\prime\prime}(t)\in\Theta_{k}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k}(t)+\tilde{M}(t)\mathcal{E}_{k}(t)\subseteq\Theta_{k}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(t)
. Recall that
Q_{k-1}(t)\vartheta_{k-1}(t)=||\vartheta_{k-1}(t)||e_{k+1}
, so applying
Q_{k-1}(t)
on the left we have

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}Q_{k-1}(t)\cdots Q_{0}(t)\tilde{M}(t)Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k}(t)e_{k+2}\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt=\frac{c_{k}(t)+\mu_{k}(t)}{||\vartheta_{k}(t)||}(\theta_{k+1}(t)+||\vartheta_{k-1}(t)||e_{k+1})+\varepsilon_{k+1}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t),\end{split}

where
||\varepsilon_{k+1}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)||\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)^{k+1}}{\sqrt{n}}
, and
\varepsilon_{k+1}^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\in\Theta_{k}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(t)
. Finally, applying
Q_{k}(t)
on the left we obtain

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}Q_{k}(t)Q_{k-1}(t)\cdots Q_{0}(t)\tilde{M}(t)Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k}(t)e_{k+2}\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt=\frac{c_{k}(t)+\mu_{k}(t)}{||\vartheta_{k}(t)||}(\theta_{k+1}(t)+||\vartheta_{k-1}(t)||e_{k+1})+\varepsilon_{k+1}^{(4)}(t),\end{split}

with
||\varepsilon_{k+1}^{(4)}(t)||\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}
, and
\varepsilon_{k+1}^{(4)}(t)\in\Theta_{k}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(t)
.

For the next step we estimate the error from
M(t)\approx\tilde{M}(t)
. Using (
6.6
) and the fact that the first
k
entries of
\vartheta_{k}(t)
are zero by definition, we first see that

(6.7)

Q_{k}(t)Q_{k-1}(t)\cdots Q_{0}(t)e_{1}=e_{1}.

So plugging in the definition
\tilde{M}(t)=(I-e_{1}e_{1}^{T})M(t)(I-e_{1}e_{1}^{T})
and applying (
6.7
),

\begin{split}~&\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}Q_{k}(t)Q_{k-1}(t)\cdots Q_{0}(t)(M(t)-\tilde{M}(t))Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k}(t)\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}Q_{k}(t)\cdots Q_{0}(t)\left(e_{1}e_{1}^{T}M(t)+M(t)e_{1}e_{1}^{T}-e_{1}e_{1}^{T}M(t)e_{1}e_{1}^{T}\right)\\
&Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k-1}(t)\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(e_{1}e_{1}^{T}M(t)Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k-1}(t)\\
&+(e_{1}e_{1}^{T}M(t)Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k-1}(t))^{T}+e_{1}e_{1}^{T}M(t)e_{1}e_{1}^{T})\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(e_{1}e_{1}^{T}M(t)+M(t)e_{1}e_{1}^{T}+e_{1}e_{1}^{T}M(t)e_{1}e_{1}^{T})+E_{k}(t)\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(M(t)-\tilde{M}(t))+E_{k}(t),\end{split}

where
\|E_{k}(t)\|\leq C_{k}\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}
, and
\mathrm{Range}(E_{k}(t))\subset\Theta_{k-1}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(t)
. These properties of the error term in the above equation display,
E_{k}(t)
, follow from equation (
6.6
) and Lemma
6.3
once again. In particular, we have

\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}Q_{k}(t)\cdots Q_{0}(t)(M(t)-\tilde{M}(t))Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k}(t)e_{k+2}\|\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}},

with
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}Q_{k}(t)\cdots Q_{0}(t)(M(t)-\tilde{M}(t))Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-1}(t)Q_{k}(t)e_{k+2}\in\Theta_{k}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k+1}(t)
.

Combining everything so far, we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}M_{k+1}(t)e_{k+2}=\frac{c_{k}(t)+\mu_{k}(t)}{||\vartheta_{k}(t)||}\theta_{k+1}(t)+\varepsilon_{k+1}^{(5)}(t),\end{split}

with
\|\varepsilon_{k+1}^{(5)}(t)\|\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)^{k+1}}{\sqrt{n}}
, and
\varepsilon_{k}^{(5)}(t)\in\Theta_{k}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k}(t)+\tilde{M}(t)\mathcal{E}_{k}(t)
. However, by the induction hypothesis we have

(6.8)

\begin{split}||\vartheta_{k}(t)||^{2}&=(c_{k}(t)+\mu_{k}(t))^{2}||\theta_{k}(t)||^{2}+2(c_{k}(t)+\mu_{k}(t))\epsilon_{k}(t)^{T}\theta_{k}(t)+||\epsilon_{k}(t)||^{2}\\
&=c_{k}(t)^{2}||\theta_{k}(t)||^{2}+O(\log(n)^{2k}/n),\end{split}

and thus similarly
\frac{1}{\|\vartheta_{k}(t)\|}=\frac{1}{c_{k}(t)\|\theta_{k}(t)\|}+O(\log(n)^{2k}/n)
. Finally we deduce that

\begin{split}\vartheta_{k+1}(t)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}M_{k+1}(t)e_{k+2}-\|\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\|e_{k+1}-\frac{a_{k+1}(t)}{\sqrt{n}}\\
&=\bigg(\frac{1}{\|\theta_{k}(t)\|}+O(\log(n)^{2k}/n)\bigg)(\theta_{k+1}(t)+\|\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\|e_{k+1}+\epsilon_{k+1}^{\prime}(t))\\
&\hskip 199.16928pt-\|\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\|e_{k+1}-\frac{a_{k+1}(t)}{\sqrt{n}}\\
&=c_{k+1}(t)\theta_{k+1}(t)+\mu_{k+1}(t)\theta_{k+1}(t)+\epsilon_{k+1}(t).\end{split}

With
c_{k+1}(t),\mu_{k+1}(t),\epsilon_{k+1}(t)
all satisfying the desired properties. Here we used the fact that
|a_{k+1}(t)|\leq C_{k,T}
which is easy to prove by the induction hypothesis and Lemma
6.3
. This completes the proof.
∎

We are now almost in the position to analyze the tridiagonal entries

and

Corollary 6.5
.

There is a
C=C(T,k)>0
such that on a set

with
\mathbb{P}(A_{k})>1-c(T,k)n^{-2}
the following holds.

(1)

For any deterministic unit vector

In particular, the inequality also holds for

(2)

For

(6.10)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\vartheta_{j}(t)^{T}\vartheta_{\ell}(t)-\delta_{j,\ell}|\leq\frac{C\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}.

(3)

For
j,\ell=0,1,\dots,k
, we have

(6.11)

\begin{split}~&\vartheta_{j}(t)=(c_{j}(t)+\mu_{j}(t))\theta_{j}(t)+\\
&\bigg(\vartheta_{j}(t)^{T}\frac{\theta_{j-1}(t)}{\|\theta_{j-1}(t)\|^{2}}-c_{j}(t)\theta_{j}(t)^{T}\theta_{j-1}(t)+\tilde{\mu}_{j}(t)\bigg)\theta_{j-1}(t)+\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}(t)\end{split}

with

,
\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}(t)\in\Theta_{j-2}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{j}(t)
,
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}||\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}(t)||\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)^{k}}{\sqrt{n}}
, and
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}(|\mu_{j}(t)|+|\tilde{\mu}_{j}(t)|)\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)^{2(k-1)}}{n}
.

Proof.

The first two statements above are immediate corollaries of (
6.5
) and Lemma
6.3
. For the third statement, by (

), we must be able to express

(6.12)

\vartheta_{j}(t)=(c_{j}(t)+\mu_{j}(t))\theta_{j}(t)+\tilde{c}_{j}(t)\theta_{j-1}(t)+\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}(t),

with some coefficient
\tilde{c}_{j}(t)
, and some
\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}(t)\in\Theta_{j-2}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{j}(t)
, with

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}||\tilde{\epsilon}_{j}(t)||\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)^{k}}{\sqrt{n}}
where we have implicitly used the approximate orthogonality between

and

from Lemma

. To solve for

\vartheta_{j}(t)^{T}\theta_{j-1}(t)=(c_{j}(t)+\mu_{j}(t))\theta_{j}(t)^{T}\theta_{j-1}(t)+\tilde{c}_{j}(t)\|\theta_{j-1}(t)\|^{2}+\nu_{j}(t),

. Rearranging the equation above yields the desired result.
∎

On the event

defined in Lemma

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|a_{k+1}(t)-\sqrt{n}(\theta_{k}(t)-\theta_{k-2}(t))^{T}\theta_{k-1}(t)|\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)^{k}}{\sqrt{n}},

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|b_{k+1}(t)^{2}-\frac{\|\theta_{k}(t)\|^{2}}{\|\theta_{k-1}(t)\|^{2}}\right|\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)^{2(k-1)}}{n}.

Proof.

First, the proof of (

6.5

To prove (

Q_{k-1}(t)

(6.16)

\begin{split}Q_{k-2}(t)\vartheta_{k-1}(t)&=\vartheta_{k-1}(t)-\frac{2(\vartheta_{k-2}(t)-\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)\|e_{k})^{T}\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)-\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)\|e_{k}\|^{2}}(\vartheta_{k-2}(t)-e_{k})\\

where using the fact that the first

are

\begin{split}\delta(t)&=\frac{2(\vartheta_{k-2}(t)-\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)\|e_{k})^{T}\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)-\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)\|e_{k}\|^{2}}e_{k}-\frac{2e_{k}^{T}\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)-\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)\|e_{k}\|^{2}}\vartheta_{k-2}(t)\\

and
\|\delta(t)\|\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)^{k-1}}{\sqrt{n}}
by Corollary
6.5
. Combining this computation (
6.16
) with the expansion of the product of the Householder matrices (
6.6
), starting from the definition of

, we obtain,

\begin{split}a_{k+1}(t)&=e_{k+1}^{T}M_{k}(t)e_{k+1}\\

&=\frac{\vartheta_{k-1}(t)^{T}}{\|\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\|}Q_{k-2}(t)\cdots Q_{0}(t)M(t)Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-2}(t)\frac{\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\|}\\
&=\bigg(\vartheta_{k-1}(t)-\frac{2\vartheta_{k-2}(t)^{T}\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)-\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)\|e_{k}\|^{2}}\vartheta_{k-2}(t)+\delta(t)\bigg)^{T}Q_{k-3}(t)\cdots Q_{0}(t)\\
&M(t)Q_{0}(t)\cdots Q_{k-3}(t)\bigg(\vartheta_{k-1}(t)-\frac{2\vartheta_{k-2}(t)^{T}\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)-\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)\|e_{k}\|^{2}}\vartheta_{k-2}(t)+\delta(t)\bigg)\\
&=\bigg(\frac{\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\|}-\frac{2\vartheta_{k-2}(t)^{T}\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\|\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)-e_{k}\|^{2}}\vartheta_{k-2}(t)+\delta^{\prime}(t)\bigg)^{T}\\
&M(t)\bigg(\frac{\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\|}-\frac{2\vartheta_{k-2}(t)^{T}\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\||\vartheta_{k-2}(t)-e_{k}\|^{2}}\vartheta_{k-2}(t)+\delta^{\prime}(t)\bigg),\end{split}

where
\delta^{\prime}(t)\in\Theta_{k-3}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k-2}(t)
, with
\|\delta^{\prime}(t)\|\leq C_{k,T}\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}}
. Next, by Corollary
6.5
and in particular (
6.11
), we have,

\begin{split}&\frac{\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\|}-\frac{2\vartheta_{k-2}(t)^{T}\vartheta_{k-1}(t)}{\|\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\|\cdot\|\vartheta_{k-2}(t)-e_{k}\|^{2}}\vartheta_{k-2}(t)+\delta^{\prime}(t)\\
&=(\theta_{k-1}(t)+(\vartheta_{k-1}(t)^{T}\theta_{k-2}(t)-\theta_{k-1}(t)^{T}\theta_{k-2}(t))\theta_{k-2}(t)-\theta_{k-2}(t)^{T}\vartheta_{k-1}(t)\theta_{k-2}(t)+\delta^{\prime\prime}(t)+\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\\
&=\theta_{k-1}(t)-(\theta_{k-1}(t)^{T}\theta_{k-2}(t))\theta_{k-2}(t)+\delta^{\prime\prime}(t)+\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(t),\end{split}

with
\delta^{\prime\prime}(t)\in\mathrm{Span}\{\theta_{k-1}(t),\theta_{k-2}(t)\}
,
\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)\in\Theta_{k-3}(t)+\mathcal{E}_{k-1}(t)
, along with the bounds

, and
||\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(t)||\leq C_{k}\frac{\log(n)^{k-1}}{\sqrt{n}}

\begin{split}a_{k+1}(t)&=(\theta_{k-1}(t)-\theta_{k-2}(t)^{T}\theta_{k-1}(t)\theta_{k-2}(t)+\delta^{\prime\prime}(t)+\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(t))^{T}\\

&=(\theta_{k-1}(t)-\theta_{k-2}(t)^{T}\theta_{k-1}(t)\theta_{k-2}(t)+\delta^{\prime\prime}(t)+\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}(t))^{T}\\

,

. Namely, on

,

(6.17)

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|a_{k+1}(t)-\sqrt{n}\theta(t)^{T}P_{2k-1}(\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})\theta(t)|\leq C_{k}\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}},\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(b_{k+1}(t)^{2}-n)-\sqrt{n}\theta(t)^{T}P_{2k}(\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})\theta(t)|\leq C_{k}\frac{\log(n)}{\sqrt{n}},\end{split}

. These are equivalent to Proposition

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

Recall the Chebyshev polynomial identity from Section
2.3
,

P_{j}(x)P_{k}(x)=\sum_{\ell\leq[(j+k)/2]}P_{j+k-\ell}(x).

As a direct corollary,
P_{k}(x)^{2}-P_{k-1}(x)^{2}=P_{2k}(x)
, and
(P_{k}(x)-P_{k-2}(x))P_{k-1}(x)=P_{2k-1}(x)
, and therefore,

\begin{split}\frac{||\theta_{k}(t)||^{2}}{||\theta_{k-1}(t)||^{2}}-1&=\frac{1}{||\theta_{k-1}(t)||^{2}}(||\theta_{k}(t)||^{2}-||\theta_{k-1}(t)||^{2})\\

&=(1+O(\log(n)/\sqrt{n}))\theta(t)^{T}P_{2k}(\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})\theta(t),\end{split}

and similarly

\begin{split}(\theta_{k}(t)-\theta_{k-2}(t))^{T}\theta_{k-1}(t)&=\theta(t)^{T}(P_{k}(\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})-P_{k-2}(\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n}))P_{k-1}(\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})\theta(t)\\
&=\theta(t)^{T}P_{2k-1}(\tilde{M}(t)/\sqrt{n})\theta(t).\end{split}

The proof now follows from (
6.14
) and (
6.15
).
∎

Recall in Section

P_{k}(x)
to be the orthonormal polynomials with respect to the semicircular measure
\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathbbm{1}_{|x|\leq 2}\sqrt{4-x^{2}}dx

as

Lemma 7.1
.

We have

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(P_{j}(\tilde{M}(t_{1})/\sqrt{n})P_{k}(\tilde{M}(t_{2})/\sqrt{n}))]=\delta_{j,k}e^{-k|t_{1}-t_{2}|}+O(1/n).

In what follows we will use the definitions and theorem from Section
2.4
. To begin with, let
j+k=2m
and
\tilde{M}(t)
be defined as in Section
2.6
. We can compute directly from Theorem

that,

\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Tr}((\tilde{M}(t_{1})/\sqrt{n})^{j}(\tilde{M}(t_{2})/\sqrt{n})^{k})\right]\\
&=\sum_{\pi\in NC[2m]}\prod_{(i_{1},i_{2})\in\pi}(\delta_{\{i_{1},i_{2}\leq j\}\cup\{i_{1},i_{2}>j\}}+\delta_{\{i_{1}\leq j<i_{2}\}\cup\{i_{2}\leq j<i_{1}\}}e^{-|t_{1}-t_{2}|})+O(1/n)\\
&=\sum_{\ell=0}^{j\wedge k}C_{n,j,\ell}e^{-\ell|t_{1}-t_{2}|}+O(1/n),\end{split}

where
C_{m,j,\ell}
is the number of
\pi\in NC_{2}[2m]
with
\pi
having exactly
\ell
pairs with one element from
\{1,\dots,j\}
and one from
\{j+1,\dots,2m\}
. For convenience we will also define

(7.1)

\kappa^{j,k}_{\pi}:=\prod_{(i_{1},i_{2})\in\pi}(\delta_{\{i_{1},i_{2}\leq j\}\cup\{i_{1},i_{2}>j\}}+\delta_{\{i_{1}\leq j<i_{2}\}\cup\{i_{2}\leq j<i_{1}\}}e^{-|t_{1}-t_{2}|}),

for any

. Now we are ready to prove Lemma

.

Proof.

(Proof of Lemma
7.1
)
Since the
P_{j}
are monic polynomials, it suffices to prove that for
j\leq k
,

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}((\tilde{M}(t_{1})/\sqrt{n})^{j}P_{k}(\tilde{M}(t_{2})/\sqrt{n}))]=\delta_{j,k}e^{-k|t_{1}-t_{2}|}+O(1/n),

and for

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}(P_{j}(\tilde{M}(t_{1})/\sqrt{n})(\tilde{M}(t_{2})/\sqrt{n})^{k})]=\delta_{j,k}e^{-k|t_{1}-t_{2}|}+O(1/n).

Divide
V=\{1,\dots,j+k\}
into
V_{1}=\{1,\dots,j\}
and
V_{2}=\{j+1,\dots,j+k\}
. There is exactly one non-crossing pair partition which pairs every element of

to an element in
V_{2}
if
j=k
, and
0
such partitions otherwise. Also observe for any
\pi\in NC_{2}[V]
, if two numbers in
V_{2}
get paired together, there must be at least one adjacent pair of numbers in
V_{2}
paired. Thus we see that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{n}&\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Tr}((\tilde{M}(t_{1})/\sqrt{n})^{j}P_{k}(\tilde{M}(t_{2})/\sqrt{n}))]\\

2.14

described by the second sum. The second to last equality follows from the inclusion–exclusion principle, and finally the last equality follows from the earlier observation that the last sum only contains one partition if

(5)

Set

&=\mathbb{E}\left[|\tilde{G}(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))-G(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))|\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{|\tilde{G}(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))-G(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))|\neq 0}\right]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}\left[|\tilde{G}(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))-G(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\\
&\hskip 85.35826pt\times\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|\tilde{G}(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))-G(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))|\neq 0\bigg)^{1/2}\\
&\leq Cn^{c(d)}e^{-c(T,k)\frac{n}{\log(n)}}\\
&\leq Ce^{-c(T,k,d)\frac{n}{\log(n)}},\end{split}

for sufficiently large
n
. This ends the proof.
∎

Lemma
6.2
now follows by combining (
A.3
), (
A.8
), and (
A.9
) through the triangle inequality. Lastly we record a quantitative continuity estimates which is used to prove tightness that is already introduced in Proposition
7.8
.

Lemma A.5
.

Fixing a
\delta>0
, for
n
large enough we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t,s\in[0,T]}\bigg|\frac{G(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))-G(M(s)/\sqrt{n},\theta(s))}{|t-s|^{1/2-\delta}}\bigg|>u\bigg)\leq Ce^{-c\frac{u^{2}}{\log(n)}}+Ce^{-c\frac{n}{\log(n)}},

where
C,c
may depend on
T
and
\delta
.

Proof.

From Lemma
A.8
, we have
G(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))=\tilde{G}(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))
on all of
[0,T]
. Thus it suffices to prove

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t,s\in[0,T]}\bigg|\frac{\tilde{G}(M(t)/\sqrt{n},\theta(t))-\tilde{G}(M(s)/\sqrt{n},\theta(s))}{|t-s|^{1/2-\delta}}\bigg|>u\bigg)\leq Ce^{-cu^{2}}.

Towards this goal, we use a classic result in the theory of Brownian motion
[
19
]
, that

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t,s\in[0,T]}\frac{||\theta(t)-\theta(s)||}{|t-s|^{1/2-\delta}}>u\bigg)<Ce^{-cnu^{2}}.

Next recall we previously obtained the estimate

\mathbb{P}(||M(t)-M(s)||_{op}>\sqrt{(2+\epsilon)|t-s|n}+u)<Ce^{-cu^{2}},

and thus

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\frac{|M(t)-M(s)|}{|t-s|^{1/2-\delta}}>\sqrt{(2+\epsilon)|t-s|^{\delta}n}+u|t-s|^{\delta-1/2}\bigg)<Ce^{-cu^{2}}.

So on an interval
I
of size
\leq n^{-1/\delta}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\frac{|M(t)-M(s)|}{|t-s|^{1/2-\delta}}>u\bigg)<Ce^{-cu^{2}}.

Taking a union of
n^{1/\delta}
such intervals and recalling once again properties for the maximum of sub-Gaussian random variables, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t,s\in I}\frac{||M(t)-M(s)||_{op}}{\sqrt{n}|t-s|^{1/2-\delta}}>u\bigg)<Ce^{-cn\frac{u^{2}}{\log(n)}}.

The Lemma now follows from Lipschitz continuity of
\tilde{G}
.
∎

We finally prove Proposition
7.8
.

Proof of Proposition
7.8
.

By Lemma
A.5
with
G(A,x)=x^{T}P_{2j-1}(A)x
, and
G(A,x)=x^{T}P_{2j}(A)x
for
j\leq k-1
, for any
\epsilon,\delta>0
there is ball
B\in C^{1/2-\delta}
such that
\mathbb{P}(E_{n}\in B)>1-\epsilon
for each
n
, and recalling the compact embeddings of Holder spaces,
\overline{B}
embeds compactly in
C[0,T]
, thus
\{\tilde{E}_{n}\}
is tight in
C[0,T]
.
∎
