Flexible Catechol-Conjugated Chitosan/Gelatin Bilayer Patches for Topical Hemostasis in Laparoscopic Surgery
Authors: Se-ah Kim, Hyun Ho Shin, Jeong Yun Lee, Seong Uk Jang, Keumyeon Kim, Seongyeon Jo, Youngjoon Kim, Sang-woo Lee, Ji Hyun Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, mussel-inspired catechol-conjugated chitosan (CHI–C)-based topical hemostats have been developed to arrest intraoperative bleeding in patients with coagulopathy through interactions between CHI–C and blood proteins. Pseudo-active blood coagulation, defined as material-assisted clot formation through CHI–C–blood protein complexation without exogenous coagulation proteins, is achieved using CHI–C/gelatin (CHI–C/Geln) bilayer structures. However, these bilayer hemostats still have limitations in laparoscopic surgical settings owing to their stiffness, which can impair trocar deliverability. In this study, we optimized CHI–C/Geln bilayer patches for laparoscopic surgical application by controlling patch thickness through compression. We prepared three types of CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm to compare their tissue adhesiveness, mechanical properties, trocar deliverability, and hemostatic capability. The CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1 and 3 mm could pass through 10–12 mm trocars without fragmentation during rolling or repeated folding. In addition, the 3-mm-thick CHI–C/Geln patches exhibited effective tissue adhesiveness, bursting pressure, and hemostatic ability. Thus, these flexible adhesive patches could serve as topical hemostats that function effectively in laparoscopic surgery.

Introduction

Hemorrhage management during surgical procedures is highly significant for the morbidity and mortality of patients, particularly during laparoscopic surgery. , Surgical smoke, thermal injury, and tissue charring caused by the use of electrosurgical and ultrasonic devices in laparoscopic surgery can compromise the surgery by promoting hemorrhage. − In this surgical setting, hemostasis is commonly achieved using sutures, clips, electrosurgical and ultrasonic devices, and topical hemostats, such as powders, solutions, hydrogels, and patch/sheet-type materials. , These are widely used to accelerate rapid blood coagulation, resulting in successful hemostasis with restored visualization. A significant issue in the development of topical hemostats for laparoscopic surgery is their delivery through the trocar. These hemostats can withstand bending and compressive loads, conform to the target surface without fragmentation, and avoid adhesion to instruments. , Thus, the usability of a topical hemostat should be assessed not only by its hemostatic performance but also by key metrics, including trocar deliverability, ease of deployment, and non-adhesion to surgical instruments in laparoscopic settings.

Topical hemostatic agents exhibit distinct pharmacologic and physical profiles according to their mechanisms of action and formulations, and clinical selection is tailored to the bleeding pattern and anatomic context. For instance, active-component formulations (e.g., thrombin and fibrin) accelerate hemostasis by directly engaging the coagulation cascade or by forming a fibrin gel, thereby shortening the time to hemostasis while suppressing oozing hemorrhage. In addition, sprayable powders, injectable solutions, and hydrogels rapidly induce micro-clot formation and establish a thin sealing layer for irregular resection beds or deep defects. , In particular, patch-type hemostats (i.e., oxidized cellulose, chitosan, and gelatin and collagen patches) promote platelet adhesion and aggregation at the tissue surface by forming physical barriers that are effective in the initial control of diffuse oozing hemorrhage frequently encountered on broad dissection planes. − However, trocar deliverability (rolling or folding without fragmentation), non-adhesiveness to surgical instruments (minimal residue), curvature compliance (maintenance of interfacial contact), and wet adhesion of patch-type topical hemostats are considerable in laparoscopic surgical procedures.

Chitosan-based hemostatic agents have been extensively developed to arrest bleeding. As previously reported, chitosan promotes the adsorption and aggregation of platelets, erythrocytes, and plasma proteins at the tissue interface, resulting in effective initial control of oozing hemorrhage commonly encountered on broad dissection planes. − However, chitosan alone exhibits limited solubility and persistence of adhesion in wet or bloody fields. , To address these limitations, catechol-conjugated chitosan (CHI–C) has been developed to increase both solubility and adhesiveness. − CHI–C-based topical hemostatic agents rapidly form physical blood-protein barriers with plasma proteins, providing rapid hemostasis with effective surface sealing. In addition, double-layered hemostats consisting of CHI–C and gelatin (CHI–C/Geln) show effective hemostatic ability by synergistic effects of chitosan’s intrinsic hemostatic properties, the strong interactions between catechol groups and blood/tissue components, and the mechanical stability provided by the Geln patch. The thin CHI–C layer of CHI–C/Geln hemostats promotes rapid coagulation with adhesion, and the Geln layer acts as a blood-resistant barrier that suppresses upward blood penetration and reduces adhesion to surgical instruments and gauze under wet conditions, consistent with the relatively high water contact angle previously reported for the Geln side. However, the thick Geln layers of CHI–C/Geln hemostats are brittle during delivery through the trocars (Figure a). Thus, additional reinforcement of the flexibility and curvature compliance of CHI–C/Geln hemostats is required in laparoscopic settings.

Schematic illustration of the compression-assisted reinforcement process for trocar deliverability. (a) Fracture of the brittle hemostatic patch during trocar passage. (b) Compression-assisted formation of flexible patches. (c) Flexible hemostatic patch without fragmentation during trocar passage.

In this study, we developed relatively flexible CHI–C/Geln hemostats by simply compressing patches without loss of hemostatic ability for laparoscopic delivery (Figure b). When the CHI–C/Geln patches without thickness control were applied in laparoscopic surgical environments, the patches were structurally damaged during trocar insertion. To overcome the brittleness of the patches, the compressed CHI–C/Geln patches were designed to improve flexibility and trocar deliverability. This flexible CHI–C/Geln hemostat preserves the CHI–C hemostatic principle based on the complexation between CHI–C and plasma proteins to form physical barriers with interfacial adhesion and sealing. The pressed flexible CHI–C/Geln patches showed favorable trocar deliverability (Figure c), with sustained adhesiveness of the CHI–C layers and reduced adhesion of the Geln layers to surgical instruments. In addition, the flexible CHI–C/Geln patches passed through 10–12 mm trocars without fragmentation during rolling or repeated folding. Thus, our study suggests that flexible CHI–C/Geln patches can be used as topical hemostats in laparoscopic settings.

Results & Discussion

Preparation and Characterization of Flexible CHI–C/Geln Patches

To prepare flexible CHI–C/Geln patches with different thicknesses (1, 3, and 5 mm), CHI–C was synthesized using carbodiimide chemistry (Figure a). To confirm catechol conjugation to the chitosan backbones, CHI–C was characterized by 1H NMR and UV–Vis spectroscopy. As shown in Figure b, peaks between 6.5 and 6.8 ppm in the 1H NMR spectrum of CHI–C were observed for catechol protons. As previously reported, the catechol group exhibits characteristic aromatic proton signals at 6.7–7.0 ppm in the NMR spectrum. , In addition, the absorbance at 280 nm wavelength in the UV–Vis spectrum of CHI–C was observed (Figure c). Catechol showed a characteristic absorption peak at 280 nm in the UV–Vis spectra, whereas chitosan alone displayed absorption primarily in the 200–210 nm range, as reported previously. The degree of catechol substitution on the chitosan backbone was determined to be 11.97% using UV–Vis spectroscopy.

Synthesis of CHI–C and preparation of CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm. (a) Synthesis and chemical structure of CHI–C. (b,c) Characterization of CHI–C using (b) 1H NMR and (c) UV–Vis spectra. (d) Preparation of CHI–C/Geln patches with a controlled thickness.

The CHI–C/Geln patches were prepared by casting the blue-dyed catechol-conjugated chitosan solution into a mold (Figure d, first panel), placing a preformed Geln patch on top (Figure d, second panel), and subsequent lyophilization (Figure d, third panel). The initial thickness of the CHI–C/Geln bilayer patch was 5 mm. To obtain different thicknesses, the 5-mm patches were further compressed to a predetermined thickness using a Thomson press (Figure d, fourth panel). CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm were prepared (Figure d, fifth panel). The morphology of CHI–C/Geln patches with different thicknesses was analyzed using SEM. As shown in Figure a, the side, bottom, and top views showed the distinct formation of a relatively dense CHI–C layer and a porous Geln layer with an integrated bilayer interface. Notably, the 1- and 3-mm groups exhibited a more densely compressed internal pore structure than the 5-mm group. Apparent porosity was further quantified from surface SEM images of the CHI-C and Geln layers (Figure b). The apparent porosities of CHI–C layer were 6.8 ± 0.6%, 19.3 ± 6.6%, and 21.8 ± 4.0% and those of Geln layer were 21.4 ± 2.1%, 19.3 ± 7.2%, and 28.0 ± 2.8% for the CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm, respectively. For the CHI–C surface, the 1-mm group showed lower apparent porosity than the 3- and 5-mm groups, whereas the Geln surface showed no significant difference among the thickness groups. Additional cross-sectional apparent porosity analysis from side-view SEM images is shown in Figure S1. Furthermore, layer thickness analysis confirmed the controlled bilayer structure of the CHI–C/Geln patches (Figure c). The thicknesses of CHI–C layers were 0.3 ± 0.0 mm, 0.9 ± 0.1 mm, and 1.4 ± 0.1 mm and those of Geln layers were 1.1 ± 0.2 mm, 2.6 ± 0.4 mm, and 3.5 ± 0.2 mm for the CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm, respectively. Compression-based thickness control reduced both layers, but the reduction was not equal between the CHI–C and Geln layers. These results indicate that mechanical compression modulated the layer dimensions of the CHI–C/Geln patches while maintaining the porous and integrated bilayer architecture.

Rheological analysis was performed to monitor the differences in the viscoelastic properties of the CHI–C/Geln patches with varying thicknesses. CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm were preconditioned by immersion in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C for 24 h prior to measurement. To minimize compression-induced measurement artifacts, the testing gap was adjusted based on the hydrated thickness of each sample. In addition, amplitude sweep measurements confirmed the linear viscoelastic region (LVR). CHI–C alone showed low elastic (G′) and viscous modulus (G″) values in the frequency ranges of 0.1 to 10 Hz (Figure a). In contrast, Geln showed increased G′ values at 1 Hz (G′ = 431.70 Pa), indicating that the Geln patches could be freestanding in aqueous solution while maintaining their integrity (Figure b). Furthermore, the thickness-controlled CHI–C/Geln bilayer patches (1, 3, and 5 mm) exhibited enhanced viscoelastic properties compared with CHI–C alone (Figure c–e). The average G′ values of the CHI–C/Geln bilayer patches at 1 Hz were 1050.0 ± 47.1, 461.8 ± 28.8, and 611.5 ± 76.6 Pa for the 1-, 3-, and 5-mm patches, respectively. The corresponding G″ values were 60.0 ± 0.9, 25.8 ± 4.7, and 33.9 ± 4.0 Pa, respectively (Figure f). In addition, rheological studies were further employed to evaluate the interaction between CHI–C and blood components. CHI–C (2 wt %) was mixed with defibrinated sheep blood (1:1, v/v). The CHI–C/blood mixture exhibited higher G′ values than the chitosan/blood mixture (Figure g). The average G′ values of chitosan, chitosan/blood, CHI–C, and CHI–C/blood were 0.2 ± 0.03 Pa, 1.1 ± 0.6 Pa, 9.6 ± 1.6 Pa, and 1460.0 ± 8.9 Pa, respectively (Figure h). Notably, CHI–C interacted with blood components, forming a mechanically reinforced blood-containing elastic matrix.

To further monitor the differences in the characteristics of CHI–C/Geln patches with different thicknesses, their mass swelling ratios, volume expansion, dimensional change, and in vitro stability were evaluated. As shown in Figure a, the equilibrium swelling ratios of the thickness-controlled CHI–C/Geln patches were 2532.6 ± 187.4%, 2220.8 ± 143.9%, and 2133.2 ± 218.4% for thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm, respectively, which were similar to those of the Geln patch alone (2611.2 ± 95.6%). A slight decrease in the swelling ratios was observed as the patch thickness increased; however, these changes were not significant. Volumetric changes were then evaluated to examine the structural behavior of the CHI–C/Geln bilayer patches upon hydration (Figure b). The Geln patch exhibited a pronounced volume decrease of −49.1 ± 6.4% after hydration. The volumetric changes of CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm were 77.4 ± 24.1%, 24.7 ± 13.6%, and −22.5 ± 11.0%, respectively. The increased volume changes of the CHI–C/Geln patches with decreasing thickness were due to patch compression, subsequent fluid uptake, and volumetric recovery. In fact, hydration-induced volume changes in patch-type biomaterials can affect bleeding-site coverage, local tissue compression, and surgical handling in confined spaces, as previously reported. , Thus, optimization of the thickness of the CHI–C/Geln patches is required to sustain hemostatic functions with increased flexibility. To further identify the dimensional basis of the hydration-induced volumetric changes, changes in length, width, and thickness were evaluated using 10 mm × 10 mm patches (Figure c). The length and width of Geln and CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm showed no significant differences among the groups. However, the 1-mm CHI–C/Geln patches showed a greater thickness change (66.7 ± 28.9%) after hydration that was far higher than those of 3-mm (22.2 ± 19.2%) and 5-mm (−18.9 ± 1.9%) CHI–C/Geln patches. Considering the laparoscopic application and surgical settings, CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 3 and 5 mm might be appropriate for use as hemostats due to the balanced hydration response with minimal lateral expansion and moderate thickness recovery. The in vitro stability of CHI–C/Geln was evaluated by measuring the remaining weight as a function of time (Figure d). The Geln patches dissociated relatively rapidly and were almost completely dissociated after 14 days. In contrast, the CHI–C hydrogel maintained its highest remaining mass for 14 days. The CHI–C/Geln bilayer patches showed enhanced durability, retaining 87.5 ± 4.3% for 1 mm, 87.2 ± 3.5% for 3 mm, and 86.1 ± 2.2% for 5 mm of their initial mass at day 7. By day 14, the bilayer patches preserved substantial mass fractions of 46.8 ± 4.8% (1 mm), 43.4 ± 0.9% (3 mm), and 36.3 ± 6.8% (5 mm). Compared to the Geln patch, these results indicate a markedly delayed degradation behavior, suggesting that the CHI–C layer within the bilayer structure modulates water penetration and reinforces the cohesion of the overall matrix.

Flexibility of CHI–C/Geln Patches

For qualitative assessment, the rollability of CHI–C/Geln patches was examined by manual rolling and unfolding (Figure S2a). The CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1 and 3 mm recovered their original shapes without damage, whereas the CHI–C/Geln patches (5 mm in thickness) showed structural fracture after rolling/unfolding. To evaluate trocar deliverability, CHI–C/Geln patches with different thicknesses were rolled and delivered through trocars with inner diameters of 5, 10, and 12 mm (Figure a). As a result, none of the patches were successfully delivered through the trocar with an inner diameter of 5 mm. In contrast, all patches passed through the trocars with inner diameters of 10 and 12 mm and could be redeployed and reoriented after delivery (Figure b). Structural damage after trocar passage was further evaluated (Figure c). After successful delivery, the 1- and 3-mm CHI–C/Geln patches showed no visible cracks, whereas the 5-mm CHI–C/Geln patches exhibited cracks in the Geln layer (Figure S2b). These results indicate that the 1- and 3-mm patches had better structural stability during trocar delivery.

To evaluate folding-induced microstructural damage, the folded regions of the patches were examined using SEM after a single 180° folding/unfolding deformation (Figure d). The CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1 and 3 mm maintained their structural integrity without observable cracks or tearing. In contrast, the 5-mm group exhibited severe fracturing, with the internal Geln structure being exposed through the ruptured CHI–C layer. To quantify crack formation after folding/unfolding CHI–C/Geln patches, crack width and crack area fraction were monitored (Figure e,f). No visible crack was observed in the 1- and 3-mm groups, whereas the 5-mm group showed a crack width of 0.63 ± 0.22 mm (Figure e). The crack area fraction of the 5 mm group was 17.5% in a representative SEM image, further confirming extensive fracture formation after folding/unfolding deformation (Figure f). As previously reported, the reduced bending resistance is associated, in part, with decreased patch thickness. , Although lyophilized porous patches are typically brittle and mechanical compression can induce local deformation of the porous framework, − thickness reduction and mechanical compaction occurred simultaneously in the present fabrication process. However, the contributions of both factors (i.e., thickness changes and mechanical compression) to flexibility could not be separated. In this context, the uncompressed CHI–C/Geln patches with a thickness of 5 mm were more susceptible to structural damage during rolling and folding. However, CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1 and 3 mm maintained their structural integrity, supporting their improved handling stability for minimally invasive surgery.

Subsequently, the flexibility was quantitatively assessed by measuring the displacement at failure using a syringe pump-driven bending setup (Figure a). The 1- and 3-mm CHI–C/Geln patches showed no observable fracture or cracking, even at a maximum displacement of 40 mm (Figure b). Uncompressed CHI–C/Geln patches with a thickness of 5 mm underwent complete structural failure at a displacement of 19.9 ± 1.0 mm. To further characterize the bending behavior of the thickness-controlled CHI–C/Geln patches, three-point bending tests were performed using a tensile/compression tester equipped with a custom-made bending jig (Figure c). The force–displacement (F–D) curves showed thickness-dependent bending resistance, with the 1- and 3-mm CHI–C/Geln patches requiring lower force than the 5-mm CHI–C/Geln patch during deformation (Figure d). This thickness-dependent increase in bending resistance reflects the greater flexural stiffness associated with increased thickness. In addition, the minimum bending radius experiments demonstrated that the 1- and 3-mm CHI–C/Geln patches could withstand bending to a radius of 1 mm without visible structural failure, whereas the 5-mm patch required a larger bending radius of 2 mm to avoid fracture (Figure e). SEM analysis after the test confirmed crack formation only in the CHI–C/Geln patches with a thickness of 5 mm (Figure S3a). Folding endurance examinations further confirmed the enhanced durability of the compressed patches under repeated folding/unfolding deformation (Figure f). The 1- and 3-mm CHI–C/Geln patches maintained their structural integrity up to 30 folding cycles, whereas the 5-mm patch exhibited structural failure after one folding cycle. SEM images after folding endurance examinations similarly showed cracks only in the patches with a thickness of 5 mm (Figure S3b). Collectively, these results indicate compression-based thickness control reduced the bending resistance and improved the overall mechanical flexibility of the CHI–C/Geln patches. − , Thus, the improved deformation tolerance of the 1- and 3-mm CHI–C/Geln patches can support their suitability for minimally invasive delivery applications.

Adhesive Properties and Bursting Pressures

To assess the tissue adhesive properties, thickness-controlled CHI–C/Geln patches were tested using a UTM (Figure a). All patches were fabricated to a size of 1 cm × 1 cm, and CHI–C was used as the control. The adhesive strengths of the thickness-controlled CHI–C/Geln patches were measured using fresh porcine liver tissue (Figure b). The CHI–C group exhibited the highest adhesive strength, with a value of 106.0 ± 10.2 kPa. Among the thickness-controlled CHI–C/Geln patch groups, the adhesive strength increased with patch thickness, yielding 38.4 ± 9.0 kPa, 56.9 ± 9.1 kPa, and 77.0 ± 7.6 kPa for the 1-, 3-, and 5-mm patches, respectively. These results indicate that the CHI–C/Geln patches retained tissue adhesive properties with a thickness-dependent increase among the bilayer patch groups. In addition, the tissue adhesive properties of thickness-controlled CHI–C/Geln patches with blood-exuding fresh porcine liver tissue were further monitored. As shown in Figure c, the CHI–C group showed an adhesive strength of 110.3 ± 19.1 kPa. For the thickness-controlled CHI–C/Geln patch groups, the adhesive strength increased with thickness, yielding 45.5 ± 2.1 kPa, 57.7 ± 8.0 kPa, and 69.9 ± 12.2 kPa for 1-, 3-, and 5-mm CHI–C/Geln patches, respectively. Although these values were lower than those in the CHI–C group, this result was attributed to the double-layer structure of the patch, in which a non-adhesive Geln layer was integrated to prevent surgical instrument adhesion. Notably, a gradual increase in adhesive strength was observed as the patch thickness increased, suggesting that a greater thickness may enhance the structural integrity and mechanical engagement with the tissue surface. ,

To evaluate the non-adhesive property of the instrument-facing Geln layer, stainless-steel adhesion was measured using UTM under wet conditions. CHI–C/Geln patches and stainless-steel plates were prepared with the same contact area of 1 cm × 1 cm. After wetting the patch surface with PBS, either the CHI–C or Geln surface was brought into contact with the stainless-steel plate for 5 s, and the detachment stress was measured. As shown in Figure d, the CHI–C surface showed detachment stresses of 2.3 ± 0.5 kPa, 1.8 ± 0.5 kPa, and 1.8 ± 0.4 kPa for the 1-, 3-, and 5-mm patches, respectively. In contrast, the Geln surface exhibited markedly lower detachment stresses of 0.7 ± 0.1 kPa, 0.6 ± 0.1 kPa, and 0.8 ± 0.1 kPa, respectively. These results indicate that the Geln layer effectively reduced adhesion to stainless steel under wet conditions, supporting its role as an instrument-facing non-adhesive barrier while preserving the adhesive CHI–C layer for tissue attachment.

To evaluate the physical sealing performance of the thickness-controlled CHI–C/Geln patches, the bursting pressure was measured using a porcine intestinal tissue model with an 8 mm defect (Figure a). Untreated defects (holes), CHI–C patches, Geln patches, and intact intestinal tissues (normal) were used as control groups. The untreated hole group was unable to withstand any internal pressure and exhibited immediate air leakage, whereas normal tissue showed a bursting pressure of 205.0 ± 6.9 mmHg, reflecting the intrinsic mechanical strength of the native tissue (Figure b). The CHI–C patches demonstrated tissue adhesion but lacked sufficient mechanical support, resulting in a low bursting pressure of 53.8 ± 1.9 mmHg. Similarly, the Geln patch failed to form an effective seal owing to the absence of adhesive interactions, yielding a bursting pressure of 30.0 ± 1.2 mmHg. In contrast, as shown in Figure b, the CHI–C/Geln bilayer patches exhibited superior sealing performance across all thickness groups, outperforming the control groups (CHI–C patches and Geln patches). Specifically, the 1-, 3-, and 5-mm patches achieved bursting pressures of 63.3 ± 4.3 mmHg, 69.8 ± 3.1 mmHg, and 84.3 ± 4.7 mmHg, respectively, demonstrating their ability to reliably prevent leakage under conditions relevant to clinical settings. Notably, the bursting pressure increased progressively with patch thickness, indicating that a greater patch thickness provided a thicker physical barrier capable of resisting internal air pressure and delivering enhanced mechanical support against external forces. CHI–C/Geln patches (3 mm in thickness) showed a bursting pressure within the range of previously reported values for the representative commercial fibrin sealant patch TachoSil (approximately 62–69 mmHg).

To further evaluate sealing performance under blood-contaminated conditions, defibrinated sheep blood was applied to the tissue-contacting surface of each patch immediately before attachment to the intestinal defect (Figure c). The bursting pressures of Geln, CHI–C, and CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm were 31.3 ± 1.1 mmHg, 54.5 ± 4.5 mmHg, 60.8 ± 0.8 mmHg, 67.5 ± 11.7 mmHg, and 79.0 ± 5.6 mmHg, respectively (Figure d). Although blood contamination slightly reduced the overall bursting pressure, all CHI–C/Geln bilayer patches showed higher sealing performance than the Geln control.

In Vivo Biocompatibility of CHI–C/Geln Patches

The short-term in vivo biocompatibility of the CHI–C/Geln patch was evaluated using the 3-mm-thick formulation. During the 7 day observation period, the normalized body weights of animals in the CHI–C/Geln group remained stable and showed no significant difference from those of the CTRL group (Figure a). The absence of implantation-associated body-weight loss suggests that the CHI–C/Geln patch did not induce appreciable acute systemic distress. Histological evaluation was subsequently performed 1 week after implantation. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections of the peripheral site of implantation showed preservation of the overall epidermal, dermal, and subcutaneous tissue architecture in the CHI–C/Geln group (Figure b). Compared with the CTRL group, no significant inflammatory cell accumulation, tissue necrosis, or severe structural disruption was observed (Figure b). Furthermore, histological examination of the major organs, including the heart, lung, liver, spleen, and kidney, revealed tissue morphologies comparable to those of the CTRL group, without notable treatment-related pathological abnormalities (Figure c). Collectively, the stable body-weight profiles and the absence of marked local or systemic histopathological changes support the favorable short-term in vivo biocompatibility of the 3-mm CHI–C/Geln patch. Nevertheless, longer implantation periods, together with quantitative analyses would be required to further establish its long-term safety and degradation-associated tissue response.

Hemostatic Ability

To confirm the effects of the CHI–C/Geln patch thickness, the hemostatic performance was assessed using a rat liver incision hemorrhage model. We previously reported that both CHI–C and CHI–C/Geln patches rapidly arrested bleeding23 in this animal model. Hemostasis was monitored in the animals within 2 min. As shown in Figure a and Table S1, CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 3 and 5 mm achieved 100% hemostasis in all rats within 2 min (Figure b). However, CHI–C/Geln patches with a thickness of 1 mm failed to achieve hemostasis in two rats. In addition, none of the rats in the gauze-treated or Geln patch-treated groups achieved hemostasis within 2 min. Blood loss was measured to further compare the hemostatic performance of each group (Figure c). The average blood losses were 1093.1 ± 767.8 mg, 344.8 ± 112.6 mg, 238.5 ± 285.9 mg, 100.4 ± 31.5 mg, and 102.1 ± 32.7 mg for the gauze, Geln, 1-, 3-, and 5-mm CHI–C/Geln patch groups, respectively. The gauze group showed the highest blood loss, whereas the CHI–C/Geln patch groups showed lower mean blood loss than the Geln group. Our results suggest that CHI–C/Geln patches with a thickness of 3 mm are useful in laparoscopic surgery owing to their enhanced flexibility, tissue adhesiveness, bursting pressures, and hemostatic ability. Our study has some limitations related to animal models. Further assessment should be conducted in medium- and large-sized animal models that more closely simulate laparoscopic surgery.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed and evaluated thickness-controlled CHI–C/Geln bilayer patches designed to satisfy both trocar deliverability and hemostatic performance requirements in a laparoscopic surgical setting. Our results demonstrate that patch thickness is a critical factor influencing physical flexibility and surgical maneuverability. The 1- and 3-mm-thick patches maintained their structural integrity without fragmentation or cracking during the repeated rolling and folding processes required for passage through the 10–12 mm trocars, whereas the uncompressed 5-mm patches passed through the trocars but exhibited cracking in the Geln layer. Although the overall adhesive strength was lower than that of the CHI–C group owing to the integration of a non-adhesive Geln layer, this design was intended to prevent adhesion to surgical instruments. Notably, a gradual increase in adhesive strength (from 38.4 ± 9.0 to 77.0 ± 7.6 kPa) and bursting pressure (from 63.3 ± 4.3 to 84.3 ± 4.7 mmHg) was observed as the patch thickness increased from 1 to 5 mm. This trend was attributed to enhanced structural stability and mechanical engagement with the tissue surface provided by the greater patch thickness. The 3- and 5-mm patches achieved 100% hemostasis within 2 min in a rat liver incision model. In contrast, the 1-mm CHI–C/Geln patches showed inconsistent performance, failing to arrest bleeding in some subjects owing to their insufficient thickness. In conclusion, the 3-mm-thick CHI–C/Geln patch represents the optimal formulation, providing an ideal balance of superior flexibility for trocar-based delivery, reliable tissue adhesiveness, and robust hemostatic capability. These findings suggest that thickness-optimized CHI–C/Geln patches can overcome the mechanical limitations of conventional patch-type hemostats and serve as promising topical hemostatic agents in minimally invasive surgery.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Medical-grade chitosan (MW = 100 kDa, 70% deacetylated) was purchased from Heppe Medical Chitosan GmbH (Germany). Hydrocaffeic acid (HCA, 3,4-dihydroxy hydrocinnamic acid) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Blue dye (FD&C Blue 1) was purchased from DyStar (Singapore), and 1-ethyl-3-(3-(dimethylamino)­propyl)­carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) was purchased from TCI-SU (Tokyo, Japan). All other chemicals were of analytical grade.

Synthesis of Catechol-Conjugated Chitosan (CHI–C)

CHI–C was synthesized via carbodiimide chemistry, as previously reported. Briefly, chitosan (1 g) was dissolved in distilled and deionized water (DDW, 100 mL) with 1 N HCl (1 mL), and the pH was adjusted to 5.0 using 1 N NaOH. HCA (1.13 g) and EDC (0.96 g) were dissolved in 10 mL DDW and ethanol (1:1, v/v). The prepared HCA and EDC solutions were slowly added to the chitosan solution and reacted for 12 h while maintaining a pH of 5.0–5.5. After the reaction, the mixture was purified by dialysis against 10 mM NaCl solutions adjusted to pH 5.0 with HCl for 2 days, followed by dialysis against DDW for 4 h. The final product was then lyophilized. Catechol conjugation of CHI–C was confirmed using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) and ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy. The 1H NMR spectrum of CHI–C in D2O was obtained using 1H NMR spectroscopy (Bruker Avance III, 500 MHz; Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA). The UV–Vis spectra of CHI–C were obtained using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2450, Z-202201147260; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) at the Wonkwang University Core Facility (Biomedical Materials Analysis & Imaging) supported by the National Research Facilities and Equipment Center (NFEC). To calculate the degree of catechol substitution, the absorbance of CHI–C at 280 nm was compared to a standard curve of HCA concentrations (0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, and 0.5 mg/mL).

Preparations of CHI–C/Gelatin (CHI–C/Geln) Bilayer Patches with Different Thicknesses

To prepare the CHI–C/Geln patches, CHI–C (37 mg) was dissolved in DDW (3.5 mL). The solution was dyed blue to allow for visual distinction from the Geln layer. The prepared CHI–C solution was cast into a mold and covered with a Geln patch (5 mm in thickness). The layers were then lyophilized to obtain CHI–C/Geln bilayer patches. The final patches were prepared with a size of 50 mm × 50 mm. The patches were compressed to thicknesses of 1 and 3 mm using a Thomson press (custom-built, Hyosung Machinery Co., Ltd., Republic of Korea) to obtain CHI–C/Geln patches of varying thicknesses (Figure S4). The 5-mm CHI–C/Geln patch was used without additional compression. The prepared CHI–C/Geln patches were compressed by adjusting the lower platen height and the compressive force. The lower platen height refers to the upward displacement of the lower platen from its initial position (Figure S4). To obtain a 1-mm CHI–C/Geln patch, the lower platen height was set to 26.9 mm, and a force of 170 N was applied for 1 s. For a 3-mm patch, the lower platen height was set to 26.6 mm with the same force (170 N, 1 s). The load was released immediately after 1 s, and the compression pressure was approximately 68 kPa based on the 50 mm × 50 mm patch area. After preparation, the actual dry thickness of each patch was measured at the center (n = 3). The measured dry thicknesses were 1.4 ± 0.2, 3.5 ± 0.5, and 4.9 ± 0.1 mm for the 1-, 3-, and 5-mm CHI–C/Geln patches, respectively. After hydration in PBS for 24 h, the corresponding hydrated thicknesses were 3.0 ± 0.0, 3.8 ± 0.3, and 4.0 ± 0.0 mm, respectively. These thickness values were measured from the prepared 50 mm × 50 mm patches.

Morphological Analysis

Morphological analysis of the CHI–C/Geln patches was performed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM; Hitachi S-4800, Z-202201147255; Hitachi, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) at the Wonkwang University Core Facility, supported by NFEC. To observe the layered structure and morphology, surface (bottom and top) and cross-sectional SEM images were obtained for CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm. In addition, to evaluate folding-induced microstructural damage, CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm were subjected to a single 180° folding/unfolding deformation. The folded regions were then observed by SEM to examine the surface and cross-sectional morphologies. SEM observation was also performed after the minimum bending radius and folding endurance tests to examine crack formation and surface damage. All specimens were sputter-coated with platinum prior to obtaining SEM images. SEM images were analyzed using ImageJ software (Version 1.54 g, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to quantify apparent porosity, layer thickness, and crack-related parameters after folding/unfolding. Apparent porosity was quantified from three non-overlapping regions of interest (ROIs) selected from SEM images of the CHI–C and Geln layer surfaces. After image thresholding, apparent porosity was calculated as the percentage of pore area relative to the total analyzed area, and the mean value from three ROIs was used for each image. Cross-sectional apparent porosity was additionally quantified from side-view SEM images using the same thresholding procedure. For layer thickness analysis, CHI–C layer thickness was measured from cross-sectional SEM images as the distance from the outer CHI–C surface to the CHI–C/Geln interface. The total thickness of each bilayer patch specimen was macroscopically measured, and Geln layer thickness was estimated by subtracting the CHI–C layer thickness from the total patch thickness. Measurements were performed at multiple positions from independent specimens. Crack formation after folding/unfolding was quantified from SEM images. Crack width was measured at 15 positions along the visible crack, and crack area fraction was calculated as the percentage of crack area relative to the total analyzed area in a representative SEM image of the CHI–C layer surface. Samples without observable cracks in SEM images were recorded as no visible crack.

Rheological Analysis

The rheological properties of the CHI–C/Geln patches were analyzed using a rotational rheometer (Kinexus Lab+; Netzsch, Selb, Germany). Briefly, CHI–C/Geln patches were prepared at thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm and trimmed to a diameter of 20 mm to obtain uniform specimens compatible with a 20 mm parallel plate geometry. Before measurement, CHI–C/Geln patches with diameters of 20 mm (1, 3, and 5 mm in thickness) were immersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4, 5 mL) solution at 37 °C for 24 h. After hydration, excess surface moisture was gently removed using filter paper, and the hydrated thickness of each specimen was measured. For the Geln and CHI–C/Geln patch samples, the testing gap was set to 95% of the hydrated thickness, corresponding to 5% pre-compression, to minimize measurement errors caused by excessive compression. Before frequency sweep measurements, amplitude sweep tests were performed at 1 Hz to determine the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) of each sample. Based on the amplitude sweep results, frequency sweep measurements of all CHI–C, Geln, and CHI–C/Geln patch groups were performed at 0.1% strain within the LVR. The normal force was monitored during the measurements, and the recorded values remained close to 0 N for all sample groups. The elastic (G′) and viscous (G″) moduli were then determined by oscillatory frequency sweep measurements (25 °C, 0.1–10 Hz). To evaluate the effect of blood on the elastic properties of the CHI–C layer, chemically unmodified chitosan and CHI–C were prepared at an initial polymer concentration of 2 wt % in aqueous solution (pH 2) and DDW, respectively. Defibrinated sheep blood was mixed with each polymer solution at a volume ratio of 1:1 (v/v), resulting in a final polymer concentration of 1 wt % for all groups. Oscillatory frequency sweep measurements were then performed under the same experimental conditions described above. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Study on Swelling Behavior and Dimensional Stability

The swelling ratio of the CHI–C/Geln patches (10 mg) was measured in pH 7.4 PBS solutions. CHI–C/Geln patches (1, 3, and 5 mm in thickness) were immersed in PBS (2 mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After swelling, the samples were gently blotted with filter paper for 5 s to remove excess surface water. The CHI–C and Geln patches were used as controls. Values are reported as means of three replicates (n = 3). The swelling ratio was calculated as follows Swellingratio(%)=Wwet−WdryWdry×100(%) where W wet is the swollen mass and W dry is the dried mass.

The volumetric expansion of the CHI–C/Geln patches was evaluated by measuring changes in the geometric volume. Briefly, the CHI–C/Geln patches (1, 3, and 5 mm in thickness) were trimmed into 10 mm × 10 mm square specimens, and the dry length (L d), dry width (W d), and dry thickness (T d) were measured. Each specimen was immersed in PBS (pH 7.4, 2 mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow for complete hydration. After incubation, excess surface moisture was gently removed using filter paper for 5 s, and the swollen dimensions, length (L w), width (W w), and thickness (T w) were measured. The Geln patches served as controls. The volume (V) in each state was calculated as V = L × W × T. Values are reported as means of three replicates (n = 3). The volume change was calculated using the following equation Volumechange(%)=Vwet−VdryVdry×100(%) where V dry and V wet represent the volumes in the dry and swollen states, respectively.

The dimensional changes in the CHI–C/Geln patches after hydration were evaluated to assess their hydration-induced shape stability. Briefly, CHI–C/Geln patches (1, 3, and 5 mm in thickness) were trimmed into 10 mm × 10 mm square specimens, and their dry length (L d), width (W d), and thickness (T d) were measured. Each specimen was immersed in PBS (pH 7.4, 2 mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to allow complete hydration. After incubation, excess surface moisture was gently removed using filter paper for 5 s, and the swollen length (L w), width (W w), and thickness (T w) were measured. The length, width, and thickness of each specimen were measured at multiple positions, and the mean values were used for analysis. The Geln patches served as controls. Values are reported as means of three replicates (n = 3). Dimensional change was calculated as the percentage change in each dimension after hydration relative to the corresponding dry dimension. Length change, width change, and thickness change were calculated using the following equations Lengthchange(%)=Lwet−LdryLdry×100(%) Widthchange(%)=Wwet−WdryWdry×100(%) Thicknesschange(%)=Twet−TdryTdry×100(%) where L dry, W dry, and T dry represent the dry length, width, and thickness, respectively, and L wet, W wet, and T wet represent the swollen length, width, and thickness, respectively.

In Vitro Stability Measurements

The in vitro stability of the CHI–C/Geln patches was evaluated by measuring the remaining weight as a function of time. The CHI–C/Geln patches (1, 3, and 5 mm in thickness) were immersed in PBS (pH 7.4, 2 mL). At predetermined intervals (1, 3, 5, 7, and 14 days), samples were collected, and excess surface water was removed by gentle blotting with filter paper for 5 s. The specimens were then rinsed by dipping them in distilled water at 37 °C for 5 s, blotted again as described above, and lyophilized. The CHI–C and Geln patches were used as controls. Values are reported as means of three replicates (n = 3). The remaining weight was calculated using the following equation Remainingweight(%)=WtWini×100(%) where W t represents the dry mass at time t and W ini represents the initial dry mass.

Flexibility Tests

The flexibility and trocar deliverability of the CHI–C/Geln patches were evaluated qualitatively and quantitatively. Briefly, the patches were manually rolled into cylindrical shapes for qualitative assessment of rollability. After maintaining the rolled state for 10 s, the patches were unfolded for visual inspection of the structural damage (i.e., cracks, fragmentation, or delamination of the bilayer interface). To further evaluate laparoscopic applicability, trocar deliverability was assessed using commercially available laparoscopic trocars with inner diameters of 5, 10, and 12 mm. CHI–C/Geln patches (50 mm × 50 mm) with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm were manually rolled into cylindrical shapes and gently pushed through each trocar using tweezers. After passage, the patches were redeployed on PBS-wetted filter paper in a Petri dish and oriented with the CHI–C surface facing downward. Successful delivery was defined as complete passage of the patch through the trocar while maintaining its overall structural integrity. The patches were subsequently inspected for visible crack formation, and the delivery success rate and crack incidence were recorded. In addition, CHI–C/Geln patches were subjected to a single 180° folding/unfolding deformation, and the folded regions were observed by SEM to evaluate post-folding microstructural damage.

Subsequently, the flexibility was further monitored by measuring the bending displacement at failure using a syringe infusion pump (Pump 11 Elite; Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA, USA) for quantitative analysis. The instrument was set to infuse-only mode; a syringe with an inner diameter of 1 mm was advanced at approximately 2.65 mm/s, and the displacement at failure (mm) was recorded. CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm were prepared as 50 mm × 50 mm specimens for the analysis. To evaluate the mechanical bending behavior of CHI–C/Geln patches with different thicknesses, three-point bending tests were performed. The tests were conducted using a tensile/compression tester (MCT-2150W; A&D Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 50 N load cell. A custom-made 3D-printed three-point bending jig, consisting of two lower supports and a central loading point, was fabricated and mounted on the tensile/compression tester. CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm were prepared as rectangular specimens (50 mm × 15 mm) and placed on the 3D-printed jig with a support span of 30 mm. The specimens were center-loaded at 20 mm/min up to a displacement of 10 mm, and force–displacement curves were plotted after initial contact point correction.

To determine the minimum bending radius that the CHI–C/Geln patches could withstand without structural failure, minimum bending radius tests were performed using cylindrical mandrels with different diameters. Mandrels with diameters of 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 mm were used, corresponding to bending radii of 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 mm, respectively. CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm were prepared as rectangular specimens (20 mm × 10 mm). Each specimen was wrapped around the mandrel for 10 s, removed, and unfolded for visual inspection of structural failure. The minimum bending radius was defined as the smallest bending radius that the patch could withstand without visible structural failure.

In addition, folding endurance tests were performed to evaluate the structural stability of the CHI–C/Geln patches under repeated folding and unfolding deformation. CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm were prepared as rectangular specimens (20 mm × 10 mm). Each specimen was completely folded at the same position, maintained for 5 s, and then unfolded. This process was repeated for 1, 5, 10, 20, and 30 cycles. After each cycle, the patches were visually inspected for structural failure, including visible cracks, fragmentation, or CHI–C/Geln bilayer interface delamination. Folding endurance was defined as the cycle number at which visible structural failure was first observed. Minor surface wrinkling without visible cracking was not considered structural failure.

Mechanical flexibility measurements were performed in triplicate, and values are reported as mean ± SD.

Adhesive Properties

The tissue adhesion of the CHI–C/Geln patches was evaluated by a tensile adhesion test using a universal testing machine (UTM; SFM-100kN; United Calibration, India) to measure the tensile strength. Briefly, PET films were cut to 1 cm × 1 cm, and fresh porcine liver tissue was cut to 1 cm × 1 cm. The tissue pieces were attached to PET films using a commercial cyanoacrylate adhesive. To evaluate tissue adhesive strength, fresh porcine liver tissue was immersed in DDW to remove residual blood, and excess surface moisture was gently removed before use. CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm (1 cm × 1 cm) were placed between the two tissue pieces. After patch placement, the tissue–patch–tissue construct was gently pressed for 10 s to ensure interfacial contact. For comparison, CHI–C was used as a control. The tensile strength was measured as the force recorded when the UTM crosshead was pulled at a loading rate of 1 mm/min. All measurements were performed in triplicate. For blood-contaminated tissue adhesion analysis, the same tensile adhesion procedure was performed using blood-exuding fresh porcine liver tissue. Only excess surface blood was gently removed without drying the tissue surface. CHI–C/Geln patches were applied for 10 s before testing, and CHI–C was used as controls.

To quantitatively evaluate the adhesive properties of the CHI–C and Geln surfaces with stainless-steel surgical instruments, tensile testing was performed using the same UTM used for the tissue adhesion test, equipped with a custom-made 3D-printed jig. CHI–C/Geln patches (1 cm × 1 cm) with thicknesses of 1, 3, and 5 mm were fixed onto the lower jig using double-sided adhesive tape, while a stainless-steel plate (1 cm × 1 cm) was mounted onto the upper jig. Prior to testing, 15 μL of PBS was applied to the patch surface to simulate a wet surgical environment. The stainless-steel plate was brought into contact with either the CHI–C or Geln surface and maintained for 5 s before measuring the maximum detachment force at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Bursting Pressure Measurements

The bursting pressures of the CHI–C/Geln patches were evaluated using a pressure chamber and fresh porcine intestinal tissue. Briefly, fresh porcine intestinal tissue was cut into 20 cm × 20 cm pieces, and a central hole (8 mm) was created using an 8 mm biopsy punch. The tissue was mounted on a pressure chamber, and 20 mm × 20 mm CHI–C/Geln patches (1, 3, and 5 mm in thickness) were attached to cover the hole in the porcine intestine. After allowing 1 min for the adhesion to stabilize, air was injected into the chamber while the pressure was monitored. Bursting pressure was defined as the pressure at which a sharp drop occurred due to air leakage outside the tissue. The CHI–C and Geln patches as well as untreated (hole) and normal tissues were used as controls. To evaluate bursting pressure under blood-contaminated conditions, 30 μL of defibrinated sheep blood was evenly distributed over the adhesive surface of Geln, CHI–C, and CHI–C/Geln patches (1, 3, and 5 mm in thickness) immediately before application to the porcine intestinal tissue. The patches were maintained for 1 min to allow adhesion stabilization before pressure testing. Bursting pressure was then measured using the same pressure chamber and testing procedure described above. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Animal Experiments

Animal experiments were conducted at the Wonkwang University Animal Research Laboratory and Seoul National University School of Dentistry Animal Research Laboratory. These experiments were approved by the Animal Review Committee of Wonkwang University (WKU24-79) and Seoul National University (SNU-260608-1). All procedures were performed in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Twenty-five normal male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (DooYeol Biotech, Seoul, Korea), aged 9 weeks, with body weights ranging from 203 to 234 g, were used for the hemostasis experiments. A total of 10 eight-week-old male ICR mice (DooYeol Biotech, Seoul, Korea) with body weights ranging from 25 to 30 g were used for in vivo biocompatibility tests. All animals were individually housed in cages within a temperature-controlled room set at 23 °C. They were provided with a standard laboratory diet and access to tap water and maintained on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle.

The animal models for hemostasis experiments were used based on a previously described method. Twenty-five rats were randomly assigned to five groups of five rats each: (1) control (gauze applied), (2) Geln (gelatin patch applied), (3–5) CHI–C/Geln patches with thicknesses of (3) 1 mm, (4) 3 mm, and (5) 5 mm. The rats were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation, and the fur on the dorsal skin was removed using an electric clipper. After the abdominal incision, the liver was carefully washed with sterile saline to remove foreign matter. Liver hemorrhage was induced by creating a 1 cm-long incision using a scalpel. Hemostasis time was measured to compare the hemostatic abilities of CHI–C/Geln patches with different thicknesses. Blood loss was also measured during the hemostasis experiment by weighing the blood absorbed by pre-weighed gauze, and blood loss was calculated as the weight difference before and after blood absorption. Hemostasis achieved within 2 min was classified as a success, whereas failure to achieve hemostasis within 2 min was classified as a failure. The percentage of animals achieving hemostasis within 2 min was calculated as (number of successes/total number of animals) × 100 (%).

For in vivo biocompatibility tests, eight-week-old male ICR mice (DooYeol Biotech, Seoul, Korea) were used. The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the dorsal hair was removed. A 2 cm skin incision was made using surgical scissors, and a hemostatic material (6 mm in diameter) was implanted into the subcutaneous tissue. The incision was closed with 6–0 black silk sutures, and the wound was disinfected with povidone-iodine. Body weight was monitored daily for 7 days. At the end of the study, the mice were euthanized, and the skin at the implantation site, as well as the heart, lungs, liver, spleen, and kidneys, were harvested for histological analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons, in GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For datasets involving two independent variables, ordinary two-way ANOVA followed by Sidak’s multiple comparisons test was performed using the same software. Hemostasis achievement was analyzed using pairwise Fisher’s exact tests. Blood loss was analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05.
