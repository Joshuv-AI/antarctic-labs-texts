Ethnic foods of Northeast India: insight into the light of food safety
Authors: Madhuchhanda Das, Anup Kumar Ojha, Pallab Sarmah, Dimpu Gogoi, Karma G Dolma, Tapan Majumdar, Suranjana Chaliha Hazarika, Dilem Modi, Goutam Chowdhury, Chaitali Konwar, Reema Nath, Samaresh Das, Thandavarayan Ramamurthy
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional and fermented foods are widely consumed by the ethnic population of Northeast India. These foods are not only very nutritious, easily available, and reasonably priced, but also boost immunity and protect from various seasonal infections and have been reported through several investigations. However, pathogens transmitted by these foods have never been reported. The process of preparation and preservation of fermented traditional foods involves various steps that can prompt contamination risks. Using the Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR)-FoodNet surveillance data, we report the profile of enteric bacteria and mycotoxin-producing fungal pathogens identified in traditional and fermented food items and their importance in public health.MethodsFood samples were collected from rural areas of Assam, Arunachal Pradesh, Tripura, and Sikkim. Bacterial and fungal pathogens were tested as per the standard operating procedure at respective ICMR-FoodNet laboratories and MycoNet laboratory at Assam. Samples were collected under six broad categories: milk products, edible insects, non-vegetarian, legumes/cereals, veggies, and alcoholic beverages. Data compilation was done centrally through a digital data portal. Quality assurance was done by the ICMR-National Institute for Research in Bacterial Infections (NIRBI), Kolkata.ResultA total of 1227 samples were collected, among which 78 samples (6.8%) were positive for enteric pathogens, with Bacillus cereus being the most prevalent one (n = 59). Furthermore, 71 fungal isolates were detected from different food categories. The most common mycotoxin-producing fungal genera include Aspergillus [A. fumigatus (n = 7)], Rhizopus [R. arrhizus (n = 13)], and Penicillium (n = 2).ConclusionTraditional foods have immense social, and cultural value and popularity among locals. However, processing, fermentation, and preservation of these foods without any preservatives and refrigerators, increases the possibility of contamination and growth by pathogenic organisms. It is crucial to understand the contamination risk involved during preparation so that the safety of these traditional foods can be ensured and local delicacies can be relished without any fear of foodborne infections and outbreaks, particularly when consumed by masses during festivals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20672-w.Keywords: Ethnic foods, Enteric pathogens, Mycotoxin, Foodborne infections, Food safety

Introduction

The Northeast region of India is very unique, considering its geographical location and bio diversity, rich natural resources, multi-cultural tribal and non-tribal communities, and their traditional food habits and practices. Most of these Indian states have their boundaries with five other countries (Bangladesh, Bhutan, China, Myanmar, and Nepal). Hence, culture of people and their food habits are influenced by their neighboring countries [1–3]. Many ethnic communities still use their traditional knowledge from their ancestors to prepare fermented and processed food from natural ingredients or consume seasonal invertebrates. Fermented foods are the typical characteristic of Northeastern states. Soybeans, bamboo shoots, local vegetables, meat, fish, and alcoholic beverages are fermented traditionally to prepare more palatable and tangy food at home. Rice is fermented to prepare beer which is very popular among tribal population. Silkworm, snails and edible insects are also a local delicacy, which not only provides protein and minerals but also provides food source for local tribes [4–8]. Some insects are also used as a traditional oral medicine to cure gastritis, fever, cough, asthma, arthritis, rheumatism, and diabetes [9, 10]. The cost of traditional foods is low and some of it may contain natural preservatives that can be active for a few days to months. Some of such perishable foods are perineal, and some are prepared during festivals/seasons. Most of the fermented foods and alcoholic beverages is made at the household level. Generally, these foods are not cooked well, consumed fresh right after the preparation, and also stored for a long time without refrigeration. Most of these foods could be contaminated during preparation due to the lack of hygiene practices and food safety awareness among the communities. Consumption of uncleaned insects, like scarab beetle may cause cardiac malfunction, kidney diseases, and gut inflammation as they produce different allergens like phytate, oxalate, saponins, and alkaloids [10]. These foods have not been investigated from a food safety point of view. Although a plethora of nonpathogenic microorganisms are reported from these traditional fermented food items, the pathogenicity of these microbes in the human gut has not been studied well [11]. These unapproved insect-based foods are not commercially available and are prepared at the household level. They are sold in local shops, food stalls, and small markets, which are out of reach to food safety officials.

Most of these foods contain a lot of unknown/unreported microbes, and have the potential to cause foodborne diseases and outbreaks [12, 13]. Hence, it is important to establish the safety of traditional foods, which are the local delicacies, used frequently and thus have constant demand in different Northeast Indian regions. In this work, we are reporting surveillance data of foodborne bacterial and mycotoxin-producing fungal pathogens in different edible investigated ethnic and fermented food items of Northeast India, which have the potential to cause foodborne outbreaks and several direct impacts on public health. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report on foodborne bacterial and fungal pathogens in traditional foods of Northeast India.

Methods

The ICMR Task Force project has been conducting a foodborne pathogen surveillance study since 2020 in Northeast India (Fig. 1). The present study was conducted from October 2023 to March 2024 with state-specific food items from different categories including alcoholic beverages, edible insects/small animals, non-veg, soybean/legumes/cereals, vegetables, and milk products (Table 1). Each food items were tested for enteric pathogens (Table 2) and mycotoxin-producing fungi. These specific food items were collected from Assam (Dibrugarh and Guwahati), Tripura, Arunachal Pradesh, and Sikkim (Fig. 1). Data was collected in a centralized digital data platform and maintained by the Centre for Development of Advanced Computing (C-DAC), Kolkata, the data management partner of this study. Data validation was done by site Project Investigators (PIs) and external quality assurance was made by the ICMR- National Institute for Research in Bacterial Infections (NIRBI) Kolkata, and Internal Quality assurance was done by ICMR-Regional Medical Research Centre (RMRC), Dibrugarh, site coordinator of the project.

Sample collection

Initially, a field survey was conducted to prepare a list of locally available traditional foods. Based on one month of field survey data, a final list of common and state-specific traditional food items was finalized (Table 1; Fig. 2), which was approved by the Technical Review Committee (TRC). Samples were collected from tribal household and local vendors. In the field, each investigating team was assisted by an epidemiologist, a social worker, and a local guide. Verbal consent was taken from the Gram Pradhan (village head), before interacting with villagers/local communities. The entire process of food preparation was observed by the investigating team. Careful inspection of food hygiene was also observed and documented using a standardized Case Report Form (CRF) (Fig S1). The samples collected in the study were divided into six broad categories namely- alcoholic beverages, edible insects/ small animals, non-veg, soybean/legumes/cereals, vegetables, and milk products. Each of these categories was further subcategorized into different groups.

The alcoholic beverages were mainly based on rice and millet. A locally made wine called ‘Guras ko rakshi’ is prepared from the Rhododendron plant was also collected. Edible insects comprised of silkworm, cricket, locust, snail, grasshopper, and stink bug (Tari) were collected. Samples from the non-veg categories were fish and meat-based. The meat-based items were the meat from beef, pork, hen, chicken, and squirrels. Samples collected from cereals/seed-based food include soybean, rice, and millet. A variety of fermented vegetables or herbs-based foods such as bamboo shoots, local vegetables (like banana flowers, chilies, nettle leaves, betel nut, cane culms, red cherry, mustered leaves/shoots, etc.), cotton flower, wood potato stem, elephant foot yam, and mushroom were collected from vegetable samples. Milk-based fermented products like cheese and buttermilk were included in the study (Table 1). Images of some of the collected samples are shown in Fig. 2. A range of ethnic food samples were tested to identify fungal pathogens, including Xajor pitha, Til pitha, Tel pitha, Mua dry fish, Sela dry fish, Dorikona dry fish, Shingara dry fish, Goroi dry fish, Puthi dry fish, Panitenga, Xukotimas, Pitha guri, Rice wine, Bamboo shoot, Khar, Kesa pitha, Bet gaj, and Sunga mas (Table 1).

Sample transport & processing

Collected samples were, transported to the laboratories, and processed as per the Standard Operating procedure (SOP) [14]. All the centers used the listed standard strains in this SOP as reference bacteria. Briefly, the samples were homogenized and incubated in enrichment broth before putting it into the specific agar medium for isolation of the bacterial pathogens i.e. DEC, Salmonella, Shigella-MAC agar, SMAC agar; Staphylococcus-Baired Parker agar; Salmonella and Shigella spp -XLD agar, HE agar; Vibrio- TCBS agar; Listeria monocytogenes-PALCAM agar; B cereus-MYP. Food samples were tested for enteric bacteria in ICMR-FoodNet centers at ICMR-RMRC, Dibrugarh, Gauhati Medical College and Hospital (GMCH), Guwahati, Agartala Medical College, Tripura, Sikkim Manipal Institute of Medical Sciences, Sikkim, Bakin Pertin General Hospital (BPGH), Pasighat, Arunachal Pradesh. For the identification, biochemical and molecular (PCR) approaches were used to confirm the enteric pathogenic species using the SOP made for this study [14] (Table S1 & Fig S2). In addition, food samples were also tested for the presence of mycotoxin-producing fungi, at Assam Medical College, which is the advanced mycology diagnostic and research center (AMDRC) of ICMR (MycoNet center). The fungal isolates were detected using both conventional (Microscopic culture identification) and molecular approaches (MALDI-TOF/PCR) [15–19]. Isolated bacterial and fungal isolates were tested for their antimicrobial resistance (AMR) following the standard guidelines [20, 21]. For the determination AMR, disc diffusion method was used to test bacterial isolates whereas, for fungal isolates, micro broth dilution method was employed for the evaluation of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC). The antimicrobials tested both for bacterial and fungal isolates are given in Table S2.

Data management and analysis

A secured web platform (https://www.icmrfoodnet.in/) was designed to collate data centrally from all the centers. A digital Case Report Form (eCRF) was designed with a drop-down menu to minimize data entry errors. For confirmation, a double data entry method was followed. Data validation was done by the principal investigators.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Institute Ethics Committee of the participating Institutes as well as the ICMR-Central Ethics Committee on Human Research (ICMR-CECHR) (Reference Number: CECHR 003/2023).

Result

We have collected a total of 1227 ethnic food items (Tables 1 and 3) from all four Northeast states. Among them, 78 (6.8%) were positive for different enteric pathogens. The pathogens from enteric bacteria were mainly- Bacillus cereus (n = 59), Enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC) (n = 1), Staphylococcus aureus (n = 6), Listeria monocytogenes (n = 9), Salmonella enterica (n = 1), Yersinia enterocolitica (n = 1), and Vibrio cholera (n = 1). The positivity rate (%) for enteric pathogens from ethnic food samples is given in Table 3. A maximum positivity rate (4.8%) was recorded for B. cereus. This suggests that, out of all the collected samples, B. cereus was the most common enteric pathogen.

The efficacy of several antimicrobials against the isolated enteric pathogens was assessed by antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST). The AST’s results are displayed in Table S3. Nearly 70% of tested isolates of B. cereus were resistant to ampicillin and ceftazidime, while > 90% of the isolates were sensitive to clindamycin, erythromycin, gentamicin, rifampicin, and vancomycin. S. aureus, showed a high resistance (100%) to ceftriaxone, imipenem, and nalidixic acid. Salmonella was resistant (100%) to azithromycin, cefoxitin, ceftazidime, nalidixic acid, and tetracycline. Listeria monocytogenes was resistant (100%) to nalidixic acid and rifampicin. Yersinia showed high resistance (100%) to ampicillin, nalidixic acid, and tetracycline.

Discussion

Most of the published literature revealed the awareness of preparing traditional foods and their health benefit as it comprises beneficial bacteria like Lactobacillus. A review of the literature published by Chandra and Tiwari (2022), reported the presence of non-pathogenic microorganisms like Lactic Acid Bacteria (LAB), Candida sp., Bacillus sp., and Saccharomyces cerevisiae in fermented northeast food products [11]. As these foods are not commercially available in the market, they are prepared at the household level and sometimes sold in the local markets in limited quantities. Hence, the collection and testing of these food items are challenging. Contamination of traditional foods by pathogens is not uncommon [22–24]. Therefore, consumption of these contaminated foods/drinks may be harmful to health, particularly for a vulnerable rural population.

As population of the Northeastern region is of different tribal communities and their common food practice involves the fermentation of locally available vegetables, fish, and other items which are abundantly available in nature (the size of the population consuming these foods are given in Table S5). Consumption of edible insects and other small animals are also being used as traditional remedy to cure gastritis, fever, cough, asthma, arthritis, rheumatism, and diabetes [9, 10]. Most of these traditional food items are reported to have antioxidants, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antimicrobial, and antibacterial inhibitors of the pancreatic lipase enzyme, insulin regulators, and glycemic inhibitors [8, 25]. In addition, these low-cost foods are very nutritious, can be fermented at home using traditional methods without any preservatives and can be stored without refrigeration [2, 3, 26–28]. However, as these foods contain diverse microbial communities, including probiotics as well as potentially the pathogens that may cause infections. From the food safety point of view, it is important to investigate the safety of these foods. Also, it is important to determine the contaminating source comprising raw foods and other components, processing methods, etc. It has been recognized that scarab insect consumption can cause gut inflammation, neurological disorders, cardiac malfunctions, kidney stones, and allergies [10].

The results of our investigation provide a crucial insight into the safety aspects of traditional foods in Northeast India. The identification of pathogens in a variety of food items highlights the importance of the constant surveillance and comprehensive food safety protocols to safeguard public health. The alcoholic drinks were also contaminated, which were thought to go through a fermentation process that generally inhibits bacterial growth. In addition, concerns about food handling procedures, storage conditions, and hygiene requirements in processing facilities are highlighted due to the presence of enteric bacteria, mostly in fish and meat products. A potential risk to consumer health from these products could be due to cross-contamination during processing or insufficient cooking/drying temperatures.

Identification of enteric bacteria in soybean products broadens the scope of the analysis. Soybean is an important component in many traditional meals around the world and it is frequently used for its nutritional values and adaptability in ethnic cuisines. Pathogens found in soy-based foods emphasize the significance of source control and the application of good sterilization techniques in processing and farming environments [29–31].

The most common ingredient in different ethnic cuisines is vegetables, especially bamboo shoots. The presence of enteric bacteria in these items highlights the possibility of contamination during production, harvesting, and handling after harvest.

Identification of enteric pathogens like B. cereus, S. aureus, and Vibrio spp. indicates the risk of outbreaks associated with consumption of traditional food preparations. B. cereus is a foodborne pathogen causing serious illness due to the production of emetic and diarrheal enterotoxin in the intestinal tract of the host. The findings in this study are consistent with the previous reports from the Northeast region [22, 32–34]. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing indicated the presence of susceptible pathogens to most of the drugs. These results indicate that illnesses caused by these pathogens can still be treated with efficient antimicrobials. However, it is important to note that antimicrobials such as ampicillin and ceftazidime for B. cereus and ceftriaxone, imipenem, and nalidixic acid for S. aureus might not be effective for the infections caused by the respective pathogens. To prevent AMR, it is crucial to use some of these antimicrobials judiciously [35] Since the number of other pathogens are less in number, it is difficult to ascertain their AMR trends.

Mycotoxin in foods is due to the presence/growth of different species of toxin-producing fungi. Mycotoxins in humans induce chronic but acute infections [36]. Direct consumption of toxigenic fungi contaminated foods can affect humans and animals. In 2004, 125 people died and nearly 200 others required medical treatment after eating maize contaminated with aflatoxin in Kenya [37]. Aflatoxin, produced by the molds, Aspergillus flavus and A. parasiticus, and A. nomius. Aflatoxins are mutagenic, teratogenic, carcinogenic, and immunosuppressive agents in animals and possibly in humans [38–40].

Patulin toxin is produced by Penicillium, Aspergillus, and Byssochlamys growing on fruits including apples, pears, grapes and other fruits. Ochratoxin A (OTA) toxin is produced by Penicillium, and Aspergillus ochraceus. OTA is associated with hepatic and nephrotoxin that has been linked to tumors in the human urinary tract. Citrinin produced by Penicillium and Aspergillus and is also a potent nephrotoxin. Ergot produced by Claviceps affecting the central nervous system [41, 42]. Fusarium toxins are produced by over 50 species of Fusarium. Rhizopus microsporus can produce two types of mycotoxins; rhizoxins and rhizonins. In this study, we have identified mycotoxigenic Aspergillus, and Rhizopus in different food items and they have the potential to exert health hazards. Saccharomyces cerevisiae was also identified in various food products, which is known for its anti-mycotoxin additives (AMA) to neutralize mycotoxin and have beneficial effects [43].

Numerous fungal species linked to ethnic food samples were found in the current investigation, which raises questions about possible mycotoxin contamination. Notably, A. niger (n = 2) and A. flavus (n = 1) are identified in ethnic food samples. These species are known to produce aflatoxins, a type of highly carcinogenic toxin [12, 44]. Rhizopus microsporus (n = 3) can produce two types of mycotoxins; rhizoxins and rhizonins has been reported from fermented foods earlier [45]. These results indicate the possibility of mycotoxin contamination in ethnic foods. The wide variety of fungus species detected in this study highlights the need for appropriate handling and storage procedures to prevent the growth of toxigenic fungi in these food products.

For sanitary standards in India, the Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) has developed extensive microbiological criteria that apply to a variety of food items. These standards are intended to guarantee food safety and quality by establishing thresholds for certain pathogens and contaminants. FSSAI sets maximum permissible limits for the foodborne pathogens in certain food products (https://gfrs.fssai.gov.in/2023/files/docs/1-Microbiological%20criteria%20for%20foodstuffs-2005-2073-08-03-2020.pdf). These guidelines and standards are followed strictly for the foods produced commercially. However, for ethnic foods, no such standards are reported except that the norms and standards have been traditionally developed by the people over the time and applied to the ethnic food in India. Natural methods such as exposure to the sun which removes the moisture thereby reducing water activity can prevent the food from spoilage. The water activity of the food can also be lowered by using salt, treating food with honey [46].

Conclusion

Food safety is a neglected public health issue but is one of the important components of a disease-free world. Although it is difficult to ensure contamination-free foods in the complex food chain, systematic surveillance of food and waterborne pathogens and their route of transmission can help in generating and implementing food safety policies and reduce the transmission of infection to humans. Recent initiatives of Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) 2030 and Global 20 (G20) ensures global food safety and protection human health. In a country like India where many communities have unique food practices, and prepare traditional foods during different festivals around the year and believe that these foods give protection from various diseases. Through this surveillance study, we identified the presence of bacterial and fungal pathogens in the traditional food items in North-East India that impose a health risk to consumers and highlight the need for constant surveillance and implementation of food safety protocols in the region. In this regard, we have already taken the initiative to create a network with State Health Authorities (STA), and Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme (IDSP) in the region and established a chain of regional centers to conduct surveillance, testing, outbreak investigation, and awareness programmes in this area to ensure public health safety. The most common pathogens found in the study are B. cereus, Listeria monocytogenes, S. aureus, Aspergillus, and Rhizopus arrhizus, which might contribute to sporadic/outbreak infection in this region and need to be monitored constantly. As communities are unequivocally attached to traditional foods, any public health intervention must be approached with caution considering the cultural sensitivity, and deeply held beliefs. Adequate sanitary practices can be made such as using cleaned raw material, proper cooking, avoid longer storage without preservatives, proper storage and processing of the food to avoid contamination. The risk can be reduced by creating awareness about food safety in the community. Knowledge and health guidelines based on scientific evidence would be the game changers for the country.
