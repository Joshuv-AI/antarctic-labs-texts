Diagnostic yield and histopathological assessment of endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial mediastinal cryobiopsy: a prospective comparison of two tunnelling approaches

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) has revolutionised the diagnostic approach to mediastinal and hilar lymph node pathologies, particularly in lung cancer staging and the investigation of thoracic malignancies [1–3]. Its minimally invasive nature, real-time imaging and high safety profile have established EBUS-TBNA as the gold standard for obtaining cytological samples [1, 3]. However, despite its success, EBUS-TBNA has notable limitations, particularly in diagnosing lymphoproliferative disorders, benign mediastinal diseases and rare tumours, where histological assessment and tissue architecture are critical [4, 5].

Recent advancements have introduced EBUS-guided transbronchial mediastinal cryobiopsy (EBUS-TMC) as an adjunctive or alternative diagnostic tool to overcome the limitations of EBUS-TBNA [6, 7]. EBUS-TMC utilises cryoprobes to retrieve larger, intact tissue samples with minimal crush artefacts, enabling detailed histopathological and molecular analyses [2, 8]. Studies have demonstrated that EBUS-TMC offers a superior diagnostic yield, particularly in conditions requiring extensive tissue characterisation, such as lymphomas, granulomatous disorders and advanced stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [4, 6]. Recent findings have highlighted the potential of trans-oesophageal cryobiopsy guided by EBUS as an adjunct to conventional TBNA, emphasising its ability to acquire larger tissue samples while maintaining cellular integrity [9]. Despite its promise, EBUS-TMC remains underutilised and the optimal procedural approach is still under debate. Factors such as needle size, freezing time and the number of cryopasses are critical determinants of diagnostic yield and safety [8, 10, 11]. Furthermore, while both 22-G and 19-G needles are employed for tunnelling access to the lesion, their comparative efficacy and feasibility for EBUS-TMC remain poorly defined [8].

This study aims to address these knowledge gaps by comparing the diagnostic yield, histopathological assessment, feasibility and safety of two distinct EBUS-TMC approaches: one utilising a 22-G needle and the other a 19-G needle for tunnelling access. Through a prospective cohort design, we evaluated the diagnostic yield, feasibility, procedure duration, sample size, complications and overall diagnostic performance. The findings contribute to optimising procedural strategies for EBUS-TMC, advancing its clinical integration as a robust diagnostic tool for mediastinal diseases.

Material and methods

Study design and patients

This is a single-centre prospective study conducted on patients undergoing a diagnostic bronchoscopy for mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathies with EBUS-TBNA and EBUS-TMC in the same procedure between August 2024 and June 2025 at the Department of Pneumology and Critical Care Medicine, University Clinic in Giessen (Germany). Patients were undergoing diagnostic EBUS-TBNA followed by cryobiopsy, by using two different approaches for tunnelling to the target lesion: a 22-G TBNA needle (SonoTip TopGain, three-point crown-cut tip; Medi-Globe, Achenmuehle, Germany) and a 19-G needle (ViziShot 2 FLEX; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), as presented in figure 1a. Bleeding was assessed and classified as: mild <10 mL, moderate 10–40 mL or severe >40 mL [1]. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Justus Liebig University, Giessen (AZ 96/24). All patients included in this study provided written informed consent prior to bronchoscopy.

EBUS-TBNA and EBUS-TMC procedure

All procedures were performed by an experienced pulmonologist under moderate sedation (midazolam and propofol) using a flexible EBUS bronchoscope (190 series; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) through a 7.5-mm uncuffed tracheal tube (BronchoFlex, Rüsch, Germany). Vital parameters were continuously monitored. After identification of mediastinal lesions via EBUS, their location, size and vascularisation were documented. Three TBNA samples were obtained using either a 22-G or 19-G needle and fixed in formalin for cytological analysis (supplementary figure S1a–e and figure 2c–g). The tunnelling of the lymph node or lesion was performed as described previously [1, 12]. A 1.1-mm cryoprobe (Erbecryo 20402-401; Erbe, Tübingen, Germany) (supplementary figure S1c–e, figure 2c–g and supplementary video S1) was inserted through the working channel and the position confirmed under EBUS guidance. The probe was cooled for 3–5 s with liquid carbon dioxide; the bronchoscope and probe were retracted together with the attached frozen samples to preserve tissue integrity. Specimens were measured and histologically assessed. Lymphatic tissue without diagnostic changes or absent tissue was deemed non-diagnostic; granuloma or anthracosis indicated benign disease. Diagnostic yield was calculated per lymph node, based on the same target sampled by both TBNA and cryobiopsy. All patients underwent post-procedural chest radiography and 2-week monitoring for delayed complications. Further details are provided in the supplementary material.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean and standard error of the mean, with group comparisons illustrated using bar graphs and error bars. Scatter plots display individual data points per group. For comparisons between two groups, t-tests were applied: paired t-tests when data were obtained from the same lymph node within the same patient and unpaired t-tests when comparing independent groups. The statistical test applied, along with the exact p-values, are indicated in the relevant figure legends and table footnotes. All analyses were conducted using Prism version 9.4.0 (GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Patient characteristics

Between July 2024 and June 2025, a total of 90 patients underwent EBUS-TBNA followed by EBUS-TMC using two different tunnelling approaches: 22-G and 19-G TBNA needles. Each group comprised 45 patients. The mean age of the cohort was 65.07±11.64 years for the 22-G group and 67.04±9.31 years for the 19-G group, with 37.7% (n=17) female/62.3% (n=28) male participants for the 22-G group and 33.3% (n=15) female/66.7% (n=30) male participants for the 19-G group. 64.5% (n=29) of the patients from the 22-G group and 77.8% (n=35) of the patients from the 19-G group were either current or former smokers (supplementary figure S2a–e). The mean short-axis diameter of target lymph nodes or masses, as determined by chest computed tomography (CT) or positron emission tomography-CT, was 34±1.4 mm for the 22-G group and 35±1.08 mm for the 19-G group. Tissue acquisition using the cryoprobe was unsuccessful in two patients (one per group); a second target station had to be selected due to inadequate initial access. Two further cases initially assigned to the 19-G approach had to be converted to the 22-G needle: one involved a lesion at the position of lymph node station 11 in the left upper lobe and the other involved a right lower lobe lesion adjacent to the lower lobe segment bronchus at lymph node station 12, where tunnelling was not feasible with the 19-G needle. In total, five patients with distal lesions in lymph node station 12 underwent procedures using the 22-G needle, taking advantage of its greater flexibility. A flowchart of the study design and patient allocation is shown in figure 1b, and patient demographics along with targeted lymph node stations are summarised in table 1. The distribution of patient demographics, including age, body mass index and smoking status, as well as group-wise comparisons of lesion size, sample size and operation time, are presented in figure 3 and supplementary figure S2a–e.

Diagnostic yield

The overall diagnostic yield of EBUS-TMC was high in both tunnelling approaches, with 91.1% for the 22-G needle and 93.3% for the 19-G needle (table 2 and figure 3d–e). When comparing EBUS-TBNA and EBUS-TMC within each group, diagnostic yield increased from 68.8% to 91.1% in the 22-G group (p=0.0005) and from 77.7% to 93.3% in the 19-G group (p=0.006), respectively, highlighting a consistent advantage of cryobiopsy over TBNA regardless of the needle used for tunnelling (table 2 and figure 3d–e). Among the patients, 25 were diagnosed with lung cancer from the 22-G group, 36 were diagnosed with lung cancer from the 19-G group and others were diagnosed with other diseases (e.g. lymphoma, metastatic disease and benign disease (sarcoidosis, schwannoma, anthracosilicosis, hyperplastic or reactive lymphadenopathy)) (table 2). In two cases, in distant lesions in lymph node station 12 in the right lower lobe and lymph node 11 in the left upper lobe, the needle had to be switched from 19-G to 22-G with a better flexibility in distal lesions due to technical or anatomical limitations. In four patients the location site in the 19-G EBUS-TMC approach was re-tunnelled for penetrating the lesion versus two patients in the 22-G EBUS-TMC group. A second lymph node was selected in two patients in both groups because of calcification and fibrotic alterations.

EBUS-TBNA demonstrated a lower diagnostic yield compared to EBUS-TMC across both approaches in cases of benign disease and rare tumours, including lymphomas. For benign diseases, the diagnostic yield was 75% with EBUS-TBNA versus 91.6% with EBUS-TMC in the 22-G group and 60% versus 80% in the 19-G group (p=0.16 and p=0.37, respectively). In patients with suspected or confirmed lung cancer (n=25 in the 22-G group and n=36 in the 19-G group), EBUS-TBNA provided adequate tissue for immunohistochemistry in 20 (74%) and 31 (81.5%) cases, respectively. In contrast, cryobiopsy enabled significant successful histological and molecular assessment in 25 (92.5%) cases with the 22-G approach and in 36 (95%) cases with the 19-G approach (both p=0.02) (table 2 and figure 3f–j). Thus, cryobiopsy significantly improved tissue quality and suitability for histological assessment in both cohorts. Despite the high diagnostic performance, in four cases (8.9%) using the 22-G approach and in three cases (6.7%) using the 19-G approach, neither EBUS-TBNA nor EBUS-TMC yielded a definitive diagnosis. Of these, from the 22-G group, three patients were diagnosed with transbronchial biopsy and one had to have a second biopsy, whereas in the 19-G group, two patients had necrosis and underwent a second biopsy, and one patient had normal tissue and diagnosis was confirmed by transbronchial biopsy. A comparative summary of diagnostic performance across both approaches is presented in table 2 and figure 3d–j.

Correlation of EBUS-TMC specimen size with histological assessment and diagnostic yield

Specimens were considered adequate and representative material by pathologists in both approaches. The mean diameter in the long axis from cryobiopsy was 3.5±0.9 mm and 3.6±1.1 mm for EBUS-TMC with the 22-G and 19-G needle tunnelling approaches, respectively (p=0.76). No significant differences in sample size were observed among the different cryobiopsy approaches. An illustrative sample size is shown in supplementary figure S1e and figure 2f, figure 3b and table 3. Repeated EBUS-TMC procedures (two or more passes) were associated with larger specimen sizes. However, specimens >4 mm in diameter did not correlate with improved histological quality or diagnostic yield (figure 2 and table 2).

Histopathological assessment of EBUS-TBNA and EBUS-TMC specimens

Figure 4 presents representative cytological and histological findings from lymph node and mass samples obtained using EBUS-TBNA and EBUS-TMC with both the 22-G and 19-G needles. Across various pathological entities, including sarcoidosis, schwannoma, sarcomatoid carcinoma, adenocarcinoma and neuroendocrine carcinoma, EBUS-TMC specimens consistently demonstrated superior tissue architecture, higher cellularity and better preservation of histological features compared to cytoblocks obtained with EBUS-TBNA. In sarcoidosis (figure 4a and b), EBUS-TBNA cytology showed only isolated epithelioid cells, whereas cryobiopsy provided well-formed granulomas essential for diagnosis. In the second case, in schwannoma (figure 4c and d), cytology revealed spindle cells, but cryobiopsy showed chronic inflammation, so failed to capture the tumour, highlighting sampling variability. For malignant cases, including lung sarcomatoid carcinoma, adenocarcinoma and neuroendocrine carcinoma (figure 4e–p), cryobiopsy consistently enabled more robust histological subtyping and immunohistochemical evaluation, with intact tissue fragments and reduced crush artefacts compared to the often fragmented TBNA specimens. Moreover, cryobiopsy samples in lung cancer cases were uniformly adequate for molecular profiling. Overall, these findings demonstrate that EBUS-TMC enhances diagnostic accuracy and tissue quality, complementing EBUS-TBNA particularly in complex or diagnostically challenging mediastinal lesions.

Procedure time

The overall procedure time for EBUS-TBNA and EBUS-TMC was 25.09±3.9 min for the 22-G approach and 26.67±5.3 min for the 19-G approach. The procedure time for EBUS-TMC with the 19-G approach was slightly longer, but not significantly different compared to EBUS-TBNA with the 22-G approach (p=0.11) (table 3 and figure 3c).

Safety profile and adverse events

There were no major complications or adverse events observed in this study. Mild bleeding was observed in five patients and moderate bleeding in one patient in the 19-G group versus six patients with mild bleeding and two patients with moderate bleeding in the 22-G group (p=0.75). In addition, no increased adverse events were observed from repeated tunelling or when a number of cryobiopsies were taken from the same lymph node. Adverse events are listed in table 3.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study confirms the high diagnostic yield, feasibility, better histological assessment and safety of EBUS-TMC using two distinct tunnelling approaches, involving either a 22-G or 19-G TBNA needle.

Our diagnostic yield for EBUS-TMC (91.1% for 22-G and 93.3% for 19-G) aligns closely with previous reports from both randomised and real-world studies, which also demonstrated superior yield of cryobiopsy compared to TBNA, particularly in conditions where evaluation of tissue architecture is essential for diagnosis (table 2) [2, 7, 13]. In line with these results, we observed that cryobiopsy significantly enhanced diagnostic performance in benign diseases (91.6% in the 22-G group and 80% in the 19-G group), outperforming EBUS-TBNA (75% and 60%, respectively) (table 2 and figure 3). Notably, in the diagnosis of lymphoproliferative disorders within the 22-G group, two cases were successfully diagnosed only through EBUS-TMC, following previous non-diagnostic EBUS-TBNA procedures. These differences are clinically meaningful given the increasing importance of histological confirmation in managing sarcoidosis, granulomatous disorders and lymphoproliferative diseases.

Multiple studies have emphasised that cryobiopsy provides larger, more architecturally intact specimens compared to forceps or TBNA [4, 6]. Our data support this, demonstrating cryobiopsy sample sizes of 3.57±0.9 mm and 3.64±1.1 mm for EBUS-TMC in the 22-G and 19-G groups, respectively, which were consistently sufficient for complete immunohistochemical and molecular workups, especially in patients with lung cancer, where EBUS-TBNA alone failed in some cases. Notably, in one case of schwannoma, the diagnosis was established solely by EBUS-TBNA cytology, as the cryobiopsy sample failed to capture the tumour, instead retrieving inflammatory tissue. This highlights that while cryobiopsy yields larger specimens, needle biopsy, especially with multiple insertions, may access deeper or broader areas of heterogeneous lesions. In such cases, EBUS-TBNA and EBUS-TMC complement each other in capturing the diagnosis in complex, large lesions.

In cases of lung cancer, our findings indicate that EBUS-TMC yielded superior histological specimens suitable for immunohistochemistry and molecular testing in more cases than TBNA. These findings are consistent with recent studies, emphasising the value of cryobiopsy in ensuring adequate tissue quantity and quality for next-generation sequencing and biomarker analyses [4, 11]. Notably, our study also supports recent meta-analytic evidence which highlighted the significantly improved yield of EBUS-TMC compared to TBNA (OR 2.63 (95% CI 1.86–3.72)), especially in lymphoma and benign pathology [14].

Importantly, both tunnelling strategies were safe and well tolerated, with no major complications observed. Interestingly, our findings also highlight that despite the similar overall yield between 19-G and 22-G guided cryobiopsy, the 22-G approach appeared more adaptable for distal or anatomically challenging lesions, due to better flexibility and needle size. This observation aligns with the technical difficulty reported with 19-G TBNA needles in the literature, such as in the GUESS trial, which noted increased difficulty during puncture with the 19-G needle, albeit with no improvement in diagnostic sensitivity compared to the 22-G needle [15]. Minor bleeding occurred in a limited number of patients with no significant difference between the two groups. Furthermore, the robust safety profile we observed, with no significant complications such as pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum or major bleeding, is consistent with data from multiple studies [2, 10, 16]. This is also consistent with the safety profiles reported by other studies, which found that EBUS-TMC (including via the trans-oesophageal route) was associated with low complication rates and high technical success, supporting its integration into routine practice [9, 10, 17].

Regarding procedural feasibility, the 22-G approach did not offer significantly shorter procedure time but did show better manoeuvrability in distal lesions, whereas the 19-G approach was more suited for central or bulky mediastinal lesions. This reaffirms that EBUS-TMC is a feasible addition to diagnostic bronchoscopy workflows without significant extension of procedure time or resource demands, findings corroborated by different studies which described manageable procedure durations and comparable safety profiles [2, 14, 18, 19]. These findings also reflect practical experience and observations from other teams, who highlighted procedural adaptability depending on lesion location, needle type and freezing parameters [8]. Although we observed no significant difference in specimen size between approaches, repeated cryopasses were associated with larger biopsies. Interestingly, specimen size above a certain threshold (>4 mm) did not translate into higher diagnostic yield, suggesting that optimised sampling strategies rather than maximal tissue acquisition may offer the best balance between yield and safety [8].

In the context of sarcoidosis, Crombag et al. [20] showed that EBUS and trans-oesophageal bronchoscopic ultrasonography are complementary but often fall short of providing sufficient histology in granulomatous diseases, reinforcing the need for improved tissue acquisition tools such as cryoprobes. Furthermore, in recent studies, intranodal forceps biopsy was evaluated as an adjunct to EBUS-TBNA, highlighting the growing interest in techniques that retrieve core tissue, a concept directly aligned with our cryobiopsy approach [21]. Finally, it was reported that even in early-stage NSCLC with centrally located tumours, EBUS-TBNA may miss small-volume N2 metastases [22]. These studies collectively underscore a shared recognition that enhanced tissue quality, particularly via EBUS-TMC, is key for accurate diagnosis, staging and molecular testing.

The 2003 World Association for Bronchology and Interventional Pulmonology Expert Panel consensus statement on EBUS quality indicators highlighted diagnostic performance and molecular adequacy as key benchmarks, recommending >90% sensitivity for lung cancer and <10% inadequate samples for molecular testing [23]. In line with recent studies, our findings further support the limitations of EBUS-TBNA alone in certain clinical scenarios. For instance, Vial et al. [24] demonstrated that EBUS-TBNA detected only 40% of N2 disease in early-stage NSCLC patients, underscoring a critical need for improved diagnostic strategies in positron emission tomography-negative mediastinum. Similarly, other groups emphasised the cytological limitations of TBNA, particularly when architectural preservation is required for histopathological diagnosis [25, 26]. Our study addresses this gap by demonstrating that EBUS-TMC offers superior tissue quality and diagnostic yield, especially in challenging cases such as benign conditions and lymphoproliferative disorders. Moreover, a recent study found no clear diagnostic advantage of 19-G over 22-G TBNA needles, which aligns with our observation that needle selection should be guided by lesion accessibility and procedural flexibility rather than gauge size alone [27]. To determine statistical significance between needle types and disease-specific diagnostic yield, larger patient cohorts are needed. Additionally, future studies should include detailed cytological criteria to assess both the quality and quantity of the obtained samples.

Our study demonstrates that EBUS-TMC, using both the 22-G and 19-G approaches, consistently provided superior diagnostic yield and histopathological quality compared to EBUS-TBNA alone. The complementary use of both EBUS-TBNA and EBUS-TMC enhances the likelihood of obtaining diagnostic material, particularly in complex or large lesions with mixed tissue composition, such as in the case of schwannoma. Furthermore, tunnelling with 22-G or 19-G TBNA needles may serve as a practical alternative to the use of high-frequency needle-knifes, which have been associated with increased technical demands, but with no potentially higher risk of complications [2, 10, 28]. The needle-based tunnelling approach demonstrated consistent procedural safety and accessibility for cryoprobe insertion in our cohort.

Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the follow-up duration for non-diagnostic cases was limited; although re-biopsy confirmed the diagnosis with another approach, longer-term surveillance would provide stronger confirmation of diagnostic reliability. Second, while systematic reviews report >90% sensitivity for NSCLC, our study included a substantial proportion of benign, rare or lymphoproliferative disorders where TBNA typically underperforms. A further limitation is that cryobiopsy was performed using TBNA-based tunnelling also in patients without peripheral lesions; moreover, most patients in our cohort had peripheral lesions, which reduced the perceived redundancy but may still have introduced additional procedural steps and costs. Third, while we applied a standardised freezing time of 3–5 s, future investigations may optimise cryoprobe activation parameters and assess the role of needle selection in varied anatomical contexts, including trans-oesophageal approaches, as recently explored in emerging studies [9, 17, 29].

In conclusion, our findings support the adoption of EBUS-TMC as a complementary tool to EBUS-TBNA in the evaluation of mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathies. Both the 22-G and 19-G approaches are safe and effective, with the 22-G needle offering more flexibility for complex anatomies and distal lesions, and the 19-G needle potentially for central lesions. EBUS-TMC provides superior tissue sampling, particularly in diseases where histological and molecular data are essential. Its integration into standard diagnostic algorithms where high tissue quality is essential may reduce the need for surgical biopsies, improving diagnostic yield and patient outcomes.
