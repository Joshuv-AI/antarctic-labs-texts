The Effects of Different Coronal Plane Angulations during Foraminoplasty on Lumbar Stability: A Combined Clinical and Biomechanical Study
Authors: Zhihua Wu, Jiahui He, Huantong Cheng, Ziyang Liang, De Liang, Xiaobing Jiang, Jianchao Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the clinical effects and biomechanical properties of various coronal plane angulations during foraminoplasty in transforaminal endoscopic lumbar discectomy.MethodsFrom December 2018 to December 2020, 72 patients who underwent transforaminal endoscopic lumbar discectomy were enrolled retrospectively. The patients were divided into 3 groups according to the different coronal plane angulations during foraminoplasty: group A, 0° < coronal plane angulation ≤ 10°; group B, 10°< coronal plane angulation ≤ 30°; and group C, 30° < coronal plane angulation ≤ 50°. The 3 groups were compared in terms of general data, clinical outcomes, and imaging parameters. In addition, a standard L4-5 3-dimensional finite element model was established and validated. A trephine with an 8.5-mm diameter was designed to simulate foraminoplasty at angles α1 = 10°, α2 = 30°, and α3 = 50° with the coronal plane of the normal model. Three foraminoplasty models were constructed. The differences in range of motion and L4–5 intradiscal pressure were compared among the models.ResultsThere were 13 cases in group A, 43 cases in group B, and 16 cases in group C. Sex, age, surgical segment, length of hospital stay, duration of operation, intraoperative bleeding, time of fluoroscopy, and preoperative, postoperative, and 1-year follow-up clinical outcome scores and imaging parameters did not differ significantly between the 3 groups (p > 0.05). However, as the coronal plane angulation increased, postoperative foraminal height tended to decrease, as did the proportion of lumbar instability. There was no significant difference in foraminal height and width at 1 year postoperatively compared to preoperative values in group A (p > 0.05), but there was in groups B and C (p < 0.05). All groups showed a statistically significant decrease in intervertebral space height when compared to preoperative values (p < 0.05). The finite element analysis results were consistent with the clinical results. When compared to the normal model, the range of motion and intradiscal pressure of foraminoplasty models were significantly increased with extension, left bending, and left rotation, and the increasing trend was α1 < α2 < α3.ConclusionsWhen performing foraminoplasty, reducing coronal plane angulation and depressing the trephine to a near horizontal position could be considered an operative technique that protects lumbar stability.Keywords: Intervertebral disc displacement, Discectomy, Foraminoplasty, Biomechanics, Spinal stability

Transforaminal endoscopic lumbar discectomy (TELD) has been widely used for the treatment of lumbar disc herniation (LDH). It has similar efficacy to open surgery and microendoscopic lumbar discectomy and has advantages such as a small incision, less bleeding, rapid postoperative recovery, and early bedside discharge.1,2) However, due to the anatomical characteristics of the intervertebral foramen, when the working tube is inserted into the foramen, it may compress the exit nerve root, resulting in exit nerve injury.3) The narrow intervertebral foraminal space can lead to a lack of smooth access of the working tube to the spinal canal. Then, the restricted operating space can make the procedure more difficult or incomplete in terms of decompression, making it difficult and inefficient. Therefore, to provide adequate operating space, intraoperative foraminoplasty is frequently performed by removing a portion of the facet joint.4)

Previous research has shown that the lumbar facet joint plays a crucial role in maintaining lumbar stability, and when the facet joint’s integrity is disrupted, the lumbar stability is also altered.5) Clinically, we discovered that some patients still have residual back pain after TELD, and some studies have reported similar issues.6,7) As one of the important weight-bearing structures of the lumbar, partial resection of the lumbar facet joint may affect the lumbar stability, resulting in back pain.8) Since the extent of synovectomy cannot be precisely controlled during TELD, there is a need to find a highly operable and clinically practical foraminoplasty method to reduce the risk of secondary lumbar instability in patients after surgery.

One of the main differences between the various foraminoplasty approaches reported in the previous literature and in clinical practice is the angle between the trephine and the coronal plane during foraminoplasty.9) The coronal plane angulation during foraminoplasty is defined as the angle between the foraminoplasty trajectory and the computed tomography (CT) coronal plane. Clinically, the significance of the coronal plane angulation during foraminoplasty lies in its representation of the degree of trephine flattening relative to the coronal plane when the patient is positioned prone during surgery. Although coronal plane angulation during foraminoplasty refers to the coronal plane, we measured it on CT axial images, as this provides a more consistent and accurate view for evaluating the foraminoplasty trajectory. We hypothesized that larger angles may lead to an increased risk of lumbar instability, whereas smaller angles may better preserve lumbar stability. As a result, we propose to optimize the foraminoplasty protocol by comparing different coronal plane angulations during foraminoplasty for TELD using clinical study and finite element analysis.

METHODS

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (No. JY[2021]268). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The requirement of written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

Clinical Study

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria are (1) patients had single-segment LDH, with clinical symptoms and signs consistent with imaging; (2) systemic conservative treatment for more than 3 months was ineffective, and TELD was performed; (3) medical records and imaging data were complete, and patients cooperated with follow-up and review; and (4) there was no previous history of lumbar surgery. Exclusion criteria are (1) degenerative scoliosis requiring surgical treatment; (2) other spinal diseases such as ankylosing spondylitis, spinal tumors, spinal tuberculosis, and so on; and (3) lumbar surgery performed again during follow-up.

General information

From December 2018 to December 2020, 72 patients who underwent TELD for LDH and met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were included retrospectively. The coronal plane angulation was defined as the angle between the superior articular process on the foraminoplasty side and the horizontal line of the vertebral posterior edge on the post-operative CT cross-section. The patients were divided into 3 groups based on the coronal plane angulation. group A, 0° < coronal plane angulation ≤ 10°; group B, 10° < coronal plane angulation ≤ 30°; group C, 30° < coronal plane angulation ≤ 50°. All patients were operated on by the same group of senior surgeons (XJ). The comparison of the general information of the 3 groups is shown in Table 1, and there were no statistically significant differences in sex, age, and surgical segment (p > 0.05).

Surgical approach

The patient was positioned prone on the operating table. The entrance was about 12–14 cm away from the midline. The needle reached the medial and ventral surfaces of the superior articular process, according to fluoroscopy. Then a guidewire was used to replace the needle. A serial dilator was adopted and twisted to enlarge the subcutaneous tract. A protective tube was inserted into the intervertebral foramen. The end of the protective tube was fixed ventrally to the superior articular process, and the angle of the trephine was adjusted using the ventral side of the superior articular process as a fulcrum. The trephine was pressed downward to make it nearly parallel to the horizontal plane to obtain a smaller coronal plane angulation; the angle of the trephine was adjusted to make it closer to the midline to obtain a larger coronal plane angulation. The trephine (Spinendos) was introduced through the tube. After that, the trephine was utilized to perform foraminoplasty An endoscope (Elliquence) was attached. Then, the nerve root was revealed, and herniated nucleus pulposus was excised endoscopically. The endoscope was removed and the procedure was completed.

Evaluation indicators

Patients were assessed for intraoperative bleeding, duration of operation, length of hospital stay, time of intraoperative fluoroscopy, Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) scores, and Oswestry disability index (ODI) preoperatively, on postoperative day 2, and 1 year postoperatively. Preoperative and 1-year postoperative imaging indices included foraminal height, foraminal width, intervertebral space height, vertebral slip distance, and sagittal angulation at the surgical segment; postoperative coronal plane angulation, foraminal height, foraminal width, and intervertebral space height of the surgical segment. As shown in Fig. 1, the coronal plane angulation was measured on the postoperative CT cross-section, and the rest of the indexes were measured in the digital radiography. The intervertebral space height is the average height between the upper and lower endplates of the anterior and posterior edges of the vertebral body on the lateral radiographs. The sagittal angulation is the angle measured on functional radiographs between the lower endplate of the upper vertebral body and the upper endplate of the lower vertebral body, and it was recorded as the absolute value of the difference between hyperextension and hyperflexion. On functional radiographs, the vertebral slip distance is the distance the upper vertebral body slips relative to the lower vertebral body and it was recorded as the absolute value of the difference between hyperextension and hyperflexion. Ross and Moore10) concluded that lumbar instability could be defined as vertebral slip distance > 3 mm, L5-S1 sagittal angulation ≥ 15°, or sagittal angulation greater than L5 ≥ 10°. Furthermore, the articular surface damage was assessed using a postoperative CT sagittal plane of the foraminal level and a cross-section on the foraminoplasty side.

Statistical Analysis

The IBM SPSS version 25.0 software (IBM Corp.) was used for statistical analysis. The quantitative data are expressed as means ± standard deviations (x̅ ± s), and the qualitative data as the number of cases. analysis of variance was used to compare quantitative data between groups, and the chi-square test was used to compare qualitative data. The paired t-test was used to compare measurement data within groups. A statistically significant difference was defined as p < 0.05.

Biomechanical Study

Establishment of a 3-dimensional element model of the normal lumbar

A 24-year-old healthy male volunteer was selected, and his lumbar spine was scanned using 64-row CT to obtain a DICOM file with a layer thickness of 0.625 mm and a matrix of 512 × 512, which was then imported into Mimics 16.0 software (Materialise) to produce a 3-dimensional (3D) finite element model containing the cortical bone, cancellous bone, and posterior column structure of the L4-5 vertebral body. The model was processed smoothly in Geomagic Studio 2013 (Geomagic) and the remaining lumbar structures were reconstructed in SolidWorks 2017 (SolidWorks). The posterior column structure is difficult to accurately segment into cortical and cancellous bone, defining it as a solid structure as a whole. Endplates, intervertebral discs, articular cartilage, and spinal ligaments were all reconstructed. The spinal ligaments include the anterior longitudinal ligament, posterior longitudinal ligament, ligamentum flavum, joint capsule ligament, intertransverse process ligament, interspinous process ligament, and supraspinous process ligament. Finally, material assignment, meshing, contact definition, and constraint definition were performed in Abaqus 2016 (Dassault System). All structures were made of isotropic elastic materials, with cancellous bone, cortical bone, posterior column structure, intervertebral disc, and articular cartilage defined as solid sections, and ligaments defined as trusses, without compression. Each structure in the model had a reference to relevant literature assigned to it (Table 2).11,12) Mesh division was performed using different element types for specific structures. C3D8R cells were used for the fiber ring matrix, C3D10 cells were applied to the remaining components of the model, and T3D2 cells were assigned to the ligaments and fiber ring fibers. The mesh size for the C3D10 cells was set to 2 mm, for the ligaments to 1 mm, and for the fiber ring matrix to 40 mm. Contact interactions between surfaces were defined as surface-to-surface contacts. Coupling constraints were established between the ligaments and the corresponding surfaces. The constraint definition specified that the interaction between the facet joint and the articular cartilage was modeled as a frictional contact, with a coefficient of friction set at 0.2.

Establishment of different foraminoplasty models

To build the trephine model in SolidWorks 2017, an 8.5-mm diameter cylinder was used instead. The upper edge of the trephine was placed at the tip of the superior articular process of the L5 vertebral with a sagittal tilt of 25° to the cephalad side, according to the clinical surgical strategy. The angle between the trephine and the lumbar coronal plane was set as α. In the assembly, the trephine was combined with the normal L4-5 model at angles of α1 = 10°, α2 = 30°, and α3 = 50°, respectively. Three foraminoplasty models were established by simulating foraminoplasty to remove part of the left superior articular process of L5, and the models obtained were defined as α1, α2, and α3 (Fig. 2).

Three different foraminoplasty models.

Boundary constraints and loads

All nodes on the L5 vertebra’s lower surface were constrained to move in any direction. A reference point R1 was set at the center of the upper surface of the L4 vertebra, and a radial coupling restraint type was used to distribute the load uniformly. A concentrated force load of 150 N with a vertical downward direction and a bending moment load of 10 N·m were created and applied to R1. The direction of the bending moment was adjusted to simulate 6 states of human motion: flexion, extension, left and right bending, and left and right rotation. The range of motion (ROM) and the intradiscal pressure (IDP) of the different foraminoplasty models were recorded and compared to the normal model under the 6 working conditions.

DISCUSSION

In recent decades, TELD has been widely used in clinical settings due to its numerous advantages. This study demonstrated that TELD provides substantial relief from symptoms of LDH, as evidenced by significant improvements in JOA, ODI and VAS scores for back and leg pain. It confirmed that TELD is effective and significantly improves the quality of life. In TELD, adequate decompression and exploration are essential to ensure the success of the procedure, and having sufficient space to maneuver can help the operator complete the procedure successfully. As the articular eminence is the primary barrier to endoscopic spinal canal access during TELD, partial resection of the superior articular process for foraminoplasty is almost always required.16) Blind or excessive resection of the articular facet joint may result in loss of lumbar stability.

Clinical studies found a general decrease in foraminal height, foraminal width, and intervertebral space height in patients at the 1-year follow-up when compared to the preoperative status. Kuo et al.17) concluded that when disc degeneration or surgery is performed and the intervertebral height decreases, pressure within the disc increases, potentially increasing the risk of disc injury in adjacent segments. The results of the 1-year follow-up in this study showed a trend of decreasing intervertebral foraminal height as the coronal plane angulation of foraminoplasty increased. The reduced foraminal height may disrupt the original equilibrium of the articular facet joint, which is capable of resisting compression, axial rotation, and shear loads from adjacent vertebrae.18) When the facet joint degenerates or is damaged, the lumbar stability suffers as a result of biomechanical changes. Furthermore, increased lumbar stress can hasten the degeneration of the intervertebral disc, compromising spine stability.19) In patients with coronal plane angulations less than 10°, we found no significant decrease in foraminal height and width at the 1-year follow-up compared to preoperative values. In contrast, patients with a coronal plane angulation greater than 10° experienced a significant decrease, implying that foraminoplasty with a coronal plane angulation less than 10° has the potential to reduce the effect on lumbar stability.

In this study, we simulated different coronal plane angulations for foraminoplasty on the validated L4-5 3D finite element model and analyzed the changes in biomechanical properties of the lumbar after foraminoplasty with different coronal plane angulations. Compared with the normal model, the ROM and IDP increased in extension, affected-side bending, and affected-side rotation for each foraminoplasty model under the same loading conditions, which were consistent with the findings of Okawa et al.20) The ROM of the model also increased significantly in extension and affected-side bending after the graded removal of the articular eminence in the study by Zeng et al.21) Previous research examined ROM to assess changes in lumbar stability. Furthermore, TELD still requires consideration of disc forces, and IDP is one of the most important parameters for determining internal disc forces. According to studies, increasing the annulus fibrosus stress with axial rotation after increasing the IDP could result in annulus rupture and nucleus pulposus re-projection, causing LDH recurrence.22) Moreover, IDP plays an important role in maintaining disc height, and an increase in IDP may result in a decrease in disc height and subsequently increase facet joint pressure, causing spinal instability.23,24) The comparison of the 3 foraminoplasty models in the results of this study revealed that the increasing trend of both ROM and IDP was α1 < α2 < α3. It could also be seen in Fig. 2 that α3 removed more synovial structures than α1, which might damage some joint capsule ligaments and lead to the destruction of lumbar stress structures.25) Ruetten et al.26) also concluded that a smaller horizontal angle reduces damage to the ventral structures of the articular process and allows access to the space outside the dural sac for decompression surgery. Li et al.27) also depressed the trephine close to the horizontal position to preserve more articular structures when using TELD for lateral recess stenosis. As a result, depressing the trephine close to the horizontal position during foraminoplasty to reduce the coronal plane angulation to 10° can be considered a technique that preserves lumbar stability.

Lumbar instability causes stress redistribution between facet joints and vertebrae, increasing facet joint momobility and stress, thus accelerating facet joint degeneration. Degeneration, trauma, and inflammation can all cause damage to the articular cartilage of the facet joint, resulting in the release of inflammatory mediators that stimulate nerves within the joint capsule, accelerating facet joint degeneration and possibly causing facet joint-derived back pain.28,29) Fukui D et al.30) used histological analysis and a 3D CT scan to discover lumbar instability and degeneration in rats after 7 weeks of articular process removal. In this study, the percentage of lumbar instability decreased in patients with coronal plane angulations ≤ 10° at 1 year postoperatively compared to the preoperative value, but increased in patients with coronal plane angulations > 10°. This suggests that a larger coronal plane angulation may increase the risk of lumbar instability. Although there was no statistically significant difference among the 3 groups, this may be related to factors such as the choice of follow-up time points and sample size. Further verification with multi-time-point follow-ups and large-sample clinical trials is required in the future. A change in the angle of removal of the articular process causes a change in the mechanical structure, which increases the likelihood of post-operative instability. As a result, decreasing the coronal plane angulation of foraminoplasty may reduce the effect on the mechanical structure while maintaining efficacy. To minimize the impact of foraminoplasty on lumbar stability, the surgeon should carefully read the imaging findings and measure the size of the intervertebral foramen to determine the direction and angle of puncture. In the case of surgical safety and convenient operation, the skin entry point should be as far away as possible. The trephine should be depressed to make it as horizontal as possible when performing foraminoplasty.

Although the above findings may have clinical implications, they are not without limitations. First, the role of muscle tissue was not taken into account in the finite element model, and the material properties of the spine were defined as homogeneous, linear, and isotropic for simplicity. Second, all of the cases in this study came from a single center, which has the disadvantage of having a relatively small number of cases. Therefore, this study utilized a combination of clinical outcomes and biomechanical analysis, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the effects of different coronal plane angulations on lumbar stability. Furthermore, the follow-up period of 1 year provides substantial insights into the long-term effects of TELD and foraminoplasty on lumbar stability. Third, because surgeons in our study group rarely perform coronal plane angulations greater than 50° during foraminoplasty for fear of causing patient injury, only finite element simulations can be used to conclude that greater than 50° is indeed more damaging. Finally, due to the retrospective nature of this study and given that it focused on imaging changes in lumbar stability, data on the precise location of the skin entry point and patients’ body mass index were lacking. Including body mass index would have provided a more comprehensive analysis. As a result, future multicenter cohort studies with prospective, large samples and longer follow-up as well as large-scale biomechanical experiments are still needed to confirm our findings.

In conclusion, TELD for LDH is effective, and intraoperative foraminoplasty can affect the lumbar biomechanical properties. Clinically, an appropriate surgical plan should be developed based on the anatomical characteristics of the patient. On the premise of being suitable for the patient, foraminoplasty should be performed by depressing the trephine as much as possible so that it tends to be as horizontal as possible and reducing the angle of the foraminoplasty, thus potentially reducing the impact on lumbar stability.
