MicroRNAs from Yishen Tongluo formula can repair sperm DNA damage caused by benzo(a)pyrene
Authors: Chenming Zhang, Ruimin Ma, Wenbang Liu, Sicheng Ma, Zulong Wang, Zixue Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextPlant microRNAs (miRNAs) present in Yishen Tongluo formula (YSTL, a traditional Chinese herbal medicine formula) are considered as potential therapeutic drugs for reducing the sperm DNA fragmentation index (DFI).ObjectiveTo study the effectiveness of plant miRNAs in YSTL for repairing mouse sperm DNA damage caused by benzo(a)pyrene (BaP).Methods and materialsTwenty-four male SPF ICR (CD1) mice were divided into control, BaP and YSTL groups. A BaP-induced (100 mg/kg) sperm DNA damage model was established in the BaP and YSTL groups, and the mice in the YSTL group were treated with YSTL (23.78 g/kg) for 8 weeks. Sperm DFI was determined via a sperm chromatin structure assay (SCSA). MicroRNAs in the testes of the mice were analysed via RNA-seq, and the top four plant miRNAs were screened, identified and overexpressed in GC cells. The effects of plant miRNAs on the viability and DNA integrity of GC cells exposed to benzo(a)pyrene diol epoxide (BPDE) (1 μM) were tested using CCK8 and comet assays.ResultsCompared with that of the BaP group, the DFI of the YSTL group decreased (9.57% vs. 18.54%, F = 18.645, p = 0.0236). miR166-y, miR894-x, miR822-x and miR396-x were screened. The CCK8 and comet assays revealed that the DFI of the mimic group was significantly lower than that of the BPDE (IC50 = 1.006 μM) group, with the most significant difference in the miR396-x group.Discussion and ConclusionsPlant miRNAs such as miR396-x can penetrate the blood–testis barrier through the digestive system to repair sperm DNA.

Introduction

miRNAs, which are short (19–24 nucleotides) noncoding RNAs, act as posttranscriptional gene expression regulators in eukaryotes (Weber et al. 2010) and can remain stable in the blood circulation and other bodily fluids (Miyamoto et al. 2015). Many studies have shown that miRNAs in plants can enter the blood through the digestive tract of animals and exist in a stable state (Sala-Cirtog et al. 2015; Wu et al. 2023). Many Chinese medications are derived from plants. Even after being cooked at high temperatures, the herbal miRNAs remain detectable in the decoction (Wang et al. 2019b), and can also remain stable in human blood (Huang et al. 2019).

The Yishen Tongluo formula, also known as YSTL, consists of Salvia miltiorrhiza Bunge (Lamiaceae) (origin: Henan, batch number 200901), Cuscuta australis R.Br. (Convolvulaceae) (origin: Neimenggu, batch number 2009302), Epimedium brevicornu Maxim (Berberidaceae) (origin: Gansu, batch number 200901), Astragalus membranaceus Bunge (Leguminosae) (­origin: Gansu, batch number 2010173), Rehmannia glutinosa Libosch (Scrophulariaceae) (origin: Henan, batch number 201002), Cyathula officinalis K.C.Kuan (Amaranthaceae) (origin: Sichuan, batch number 2011211), and Whitmania pigra Whitman (Haemopidae) (origin: Jiangsu, batch number 200601). This combination has proven effective in treating oligoasthenospermia in clinical settings, as demonstrated by studies conducted by Chen et al. (2020), Chen et al. (2021) and Zhang et al. (2020). Furthermore, our prior research revealed that YSTL also decreased the DNA fragmentation index (DFI) (Chen et al. 2020) and inhibited DNA methylation in sperm (Sun et al. 2019). To date, thousands of potential miRNA candidates have been identified for use in more than 50 medicinal plants, with over 30 miRNAs expected to control the synthesis of bioactive compounds (Li et al. 2021).

Considering the effective treatment of sperm DNA damage by YSTL decoction, we theorized that plant miRNAs from YSTL may be absorbed by the human body via the digestive system to provide therapeutic benefits. By replicating a mouse model of sperm DNA damage caused by benzo(a)pyrene (BaP) and administering YSTL, it was demonstrated that YSTL has the ability to restore sperm DNA in this particular model. The possible exogenous miRNAs were screened via RNA-seq by comparing the levels of miRNAs in plants between different groups. Then, the selected plant miRNA sequences were used to generate miRNA overexpressing spermatocyte (a GC cell line) cell lines. The discovery that these cell lines could withstand DNA damage from benzo(a)pyrene diol epoxide (BPDE), an oxide of BaP in vivo, was unexpected.

Materials and methods

Animals and cells

Twenty-four male SPF ICR (CD1) mice weighing 30–40 g were acquired from Henan Skbex Biotechnology Co., Ltd., in Anyang, China (license number SCXK (YU) 2020–0005). The mice were individually weighed, assigned numbers, and then randomly grouped on the basis of a table of random numbers generated by SPSS software (IBM, SPSS v. 29.0). Approval for both of the in vivo and in vitro experiments was granted by the Ethics Committee of Henan Province Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. PZ-HNSZYY-2020-019). The animal experimental protocols were performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The mice were observed daily for the following indicators of distress: altered weight, hair loss, aberrant eye opening, decreased physical activity, and abnormal posture to reduce animal suffering and identify humane endpoints. Table S1 displays the standards for identifying humanitarian endpoints (Lee et al. 2019). Throughout the study, the animals were kept in an individually ventilated cage (IVC) system located in the central laboratory at the Henan Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine (HPHTCM, License No. SYXK (Henan) 2011–0002). The IVC system maintained a temperature range of 21–25 °C, humidity between 38–60%, and ventilation rate of 22 times per h. The light/dark cycle alternated every 12 h, with one week of adaptation allowed before the formal experiment. Standard animal feed and purified water were given to the animals.

The cell line GC-2spd (ts) (mouse spermatocytes) was purchased from BeNa Culture Collection (#307026, Xinyang, Henan, China).

Materials

BaP was purchased from Beijing Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd., China (cat. no. C11287800) and was an analytical grade chemical with 96% purity. BPDE (with a purity of 90%, cat.no.: B287550) was purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals (Toronto, Ontario, Canada). YSTL consists of seven herbal medicines and was obtained from the pharmacy located at HPHTCM. Three hundred grams of Salvia miltiorrhiza (roots), 200 g of Cuscuta australis (seeds), 200 g of Epimedium brevicornu (leaves), 200 g of Astragalus membranaceus (roots), 100 g of Rehmannia glutinosa (roots), 100 g of Cyathula officinalis (roots) and 60 g of Whitmania pigra (whole) were decocted twice at 100 °C for 2 h each time after being combined at a 1:8 weight ratio with distilled water. The decocting solution mentioned above was then further condensed into 386.6 mL of a concentrated mixture equivalent to 3 g of crude herbal medicine per mL. YSTL was stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C for future use. The names and doses of each Chinese herbal ingredient are displayed in Table 1. Every herb name has been verified in the International Plant Names Index (IPNI), the W3Tropicos web database and the Chinese Pharmacopeia, 2020.

Examination of YSTL via ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography–Q-extractive mass spectrometry (UHPLC–QE–MS)

One hundred milligrams of YSTL solution (33 μL) were added to 500 μL of 50% methanol, which included an internal standard at 10 μg/mL. The sample was ultrasonically oscillated for 1 h at −40 °C, 1 h at 4 °C, and 15 min at 12000 rpm following 30 sec of eddy current. To conduct the LC–MS/MS investigations, the supernatant was aspirated and transferred into a new 2-mL tube.

The LC–MS/MS assessment utilized a UPLC BEH C18 (1.7 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) column (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) and an Agilent ULTRA high-performance liquid chromatography 1290 UPLC platform (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) under the following conditions: a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min, an injection volume of 5 μL, and a mobile phase consisting of a 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution (A) and a 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile solution (B). The gradient elution program used was as follows: 0–3.5 min, 95–85% A; 3.5–6 min, 85–70% A; 6–6.5 min, 70–70% A; 6.5–12 min, 70–30% A; 12–12.5 min, 30–30% A; 12.5–18 min, 30–0% A; 18–25 min, 0–0% A; 25–26 min, 0–95% A; 26–30 min, 95–95% A.

Xcalibur software (v. 2.0.7, Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and a Q Exactive Focus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) were used for the acquisition of MS and MS/MS data in IDA collection mode. The quality range for each collection cycle was set at 100–1500, with the first three bits of each cycle being selectively screened to generate the corresponding MS/MS data. The spraying voltage was maintained at 4.0 kV (positive) or −3.6 kV (negative), the collision energy in NCE mode was set at 15/30/45, the sheath gas flow was 45 Arb, the capillary temperature was 400 °C, the auxiliary gas flow was 15 Arb, and the MS resolution was 70,000.

Animal experiments

Following the adjustment phase, the mice were randomly assigned to three groups: the control, BaP, and YSTL groups, each consisting of eight mice. The control group was intragastrically injected with 0.1 mL/10 g normal saline, and the other two groups of mice were intragastrically injected in the morning with 100 mg/kg BaP according to methods described in the literature (Jeng et al. 2015) to establish a DNA damage model of sperm. YSTL was diluted with distilled water to 2.378 g/mL. The YSTL group was administered 100 mg/kg BaP in the morning, and YSTL decoction was given at 0.1 mL/10 g body mass in the afternoon. The YSTL decoction dose was 23.78 g/kg, matching the daily oral dose for the adult body surface area according to Nair and Jacob (2016). All of the groups were gavaged daily for 8 w. The experiment started in September 2020 and ended in June 2021. The in vivo experiments were conducted by Dr. Chenming Zhang from September 1, 2020 to June 1, 2021. This experiment was preceded by a pre-experiment according to the reference (Jeng et al. 2015), and all the animals were successfully modelled in the formal experiment.

Following the final treatment, surgery was conducted to excise the testicles and epididymis. Following the intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital for anaesthesia (effective: 80 mg/kg; maintenance: 17 mg/kg), the mice were weighed and their testicles and epididymides were extracted. Following the removal of the epididymis, the blood was rinsed with sterile saline solution, and the tail of the epididymis was cut under a microscope. After being exposed to a shock for 1–3 min in 1 mL of normal saline, the sperm cells were completely released, and the liquid became extremely cloudy. The sperm were stored at −80 °C until the DFI was calculated. The testicles were frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80 °C until RNA extraction. At the end of the animal experiments, the cervical spines of all the mice were subsequently dislocated to ensure euthanasia. All the procedures were performed by Dr. Chenming Zhang. The dead animal carcasses were packaged, labelled, and temporarily stored in the laboratory animal centre and sent to the pathological waste bin for unified disposal.

Sperm chromatin structure assay (SCSA)

To date, the SCSA, a flow cytometry test that gauges the sensitivity of sperm DNA to acid-induced DNA degradation in situ, has been the most reliable clinical threshold test for identifying sperm DNA integrity (Bungum et al. 2011). SCSA was performed in the HPHTCM Reproductive Laboratory according to the instructions provided by Evenson (2016). The thawed sperm sample was quickly warmed, transferred to 800 μL of chilled TNE solution (10 ×, abs9267, Absin, Shanghai, China), and filtered through 100-μm nylon mesh (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The sperm concentration in the filtered sperm suspension was decreased to 2–3 × 106/mL with identical buffer. Two hundred microliters of acid detergent solution was added to a FACS tube (Corning, Wycombe, UK), followed by the addition of 100 μL of sperm suspension. After 30 s, the sperm cells were treated with a 600 μL solution of acridine orange (AO) stain. Flow cytometry (BD AccuriTM C5) was utilized to evaluate and isolate 5,000 sperm from each sample (488 nm laser channel). We used Accuri™ C6 plus software (BD) to calculate the ratio of red single-stranded to green double-stranded fluorescence (% DFI). The compensation settings were as follows: the flow rate of sperm needed to be controlled at 100–300 sperm per sec. If the speed was too fast, i.e., greater than 300/s, the sample was rediluted.

RNA extraction

Following TRIzol extraction, RNA molecules between 18 and 30 nucleotides in length were isolated via polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). The basic rationale of miRNA range selection (18–30 nt) involves several key steps. Initially, miRNAs are transcribed within the nucleus by RNA polymerase II (polII), yielding a primary transcript known as a pri-miRNA. This pri-miRNA possesses a cap structure (7MGpppG) and a polyadenylated tail (AAAAA). Subsequently, the nuclease Drosha and its cofactor Pasha process the pri-miRNA into a 70-nucleotide precursor called pre-miRNA. The pre-miRNA is transported to the cytoplasm by RNA-GTP and exportin 5. In the cytoplasm, the enzyme Dicer further cleaves the pre-miRNA, yielding mature miRNAs approximately 18–30 nucleotides in length (Hu et al. 2009).

PAGE procedures and conditions: An agarose gel was prepared with 1× TAE electrophoresis buffer covering the surface. On a clean bench, 4 μl of total RNA sample was pipetted onto a sealing film. Then, 5 μl of 1× TAE electrophoresis buffer and 1 μl of 10 × loading buffer were mixed with the sample and it was loaded into a well of the gel. The power supply was 100 V for RNA electrophoresis from the negative to the positive electrode. After approximately 30 min, the gel was stained with EB solution for 5 min and briefly rinsed with water before the results were observed via a UV transilluminator.

Next, 3′ adapters were introduced, leading to the enrichment of RNAs ranging from 36 to 44 nucleotides in length. Then, 5′ adapters were connected to the RNAs. After reverse transcription of the ligation products, PCR amplification was performed to enrich the 140–160 bp PCR products for the creation of a cDNA library. The library was then sequenced using an Illumina HiSeqTM 2500 at Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. in Guangzhou, China.

Plant miRNA identification

Currently, comprehensive miRNA information is lacking for many species in the miRBase database, while only a small portion of certain species’ miRNAs are included. To fill this gap, researchers have used a comparative approach, leveraging conserved miRNA forms across species. This method supplements existing data even for species with limited representation in miRBase. The identification process involves removing tag sequences associated with known miRNAs and comparing the remaining miRNA tags with sequences from other species in miRBase using Bowtie software (parameters: Comparison genome: -v 0 –best – strata-a; Comparison mirbase: −2 –best – strata-a –norc). Filtering criteria are applied on the basis of the precursor sequence comparison, retaining tags that align with the position of the miRNA within a 2 bp extension. We then compared exogenous miRNA content differences between specific groups, such as the YSTL and BaP groups. This approach streamlines the identification and analysis of miRNAs across species, enhancing our understanding of gene regulatory mechanisms.

GC cell culture

The culture medium used for the cells was high-glucose DMEM (PM150210, Procell, China) supplemented with 10% FBS (164210, Procell, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PB180120, Procell, China). An ultraclean workbench was prepared by sterilization beforehand. Then, the culture medium was emptied from the container onto a highly sanitary surface, the container was rinsed with PBS twice, 1 mL of pancreatic enzyme (T1300, Solarbio, China) was added, and the digestion was observed through a microscope. Two milliliters of the above medium was added to stop the digestion process. The cells were subsequently transferred to a 15-millilitre centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1000 rotations per min for 5 min. The liquid above the sediment was removed, mixed with 1 mL of the above medium, and counted. The cells were transferred into new culture bottles at 2 × 105 pieces/mL, 4 mL of media was added to each bottle, and the culture was continued in an incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Cell transfection

Experimental grouping

The experiments involved the following groups: control, model, BPDE (1 μM), Overexpressed no-load (NC) + BPDE (1 μM), miR166-y mimics + BPDE (1 μM), miR396-x mimics + BPDE (1 μM), miR822-x mimics + BPDE (1 μM), and miR894-x mimics + BPDE (1 μM).

Experimental procedure

Around 18 to 24 h before transfection, the cells were placed in 96-well plates with 8 × 103 cells per well, with three wells per group being used as replicates. The transduction efficiency of the lentivirus was approximately 70%.

For transfection reagent preparation, 5 μL of Opti-MEM + 0.4 μL of P3000 + 1 μL of miRNA was mixed and incubated for 5 min, whereas for Liquid B, 5 μL of Opti-MEM + 0.3 μL of Lip3000 was mixed and incubated for 5 min. The liquids were combined and incubated for 15 min. Meanwhile, the medium in the wells was replaced with 88.3 μL of fresh complete medium. After 15 min, the transfection mixture was added to the wells, gently mixed, and incubated in a cell culture incubator. The complete medium was replaced 6 h later. After 24 h of transfection, 100 μL of BPDE (1 μM) was added to Groups 2 through 7. After BPDE treatment, cell viability was evaluated with the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay at the 24-h mark.

CCK8 detection

The liquid was removed from the 96-well plate without touching the bottom of the well with a pipette tip. Then, 100 μL of D-PBS was added to each well, the liquid was gently removed, and the process was repeated. Next, 110 μL of CCK8 mixture (comprising 100 μL of complete medium and 10 μL of CCK8 solution) was added to each well. The plate was placed in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and incubated for 1 to 4 h. The optical density (OD) was determined with an ELISA plate reader at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Comet assay

For sample preparation, 1 × lysis buffer, alkaline solution, and electrophoresis solution were added, and the samples were cooled to 4 °C. The agarose was liquefied in a water bath at 90–95 °C for 20 min and then cooled at 37 °C for 20 min. The comet slide was placed in a 37 °C environment, 100 μL of agarose was added per well, and then the slide was cooled horizontally at 4 °C for 15 min. The cell samples were collected and centrifuged, the supernatant was removed, and the samples were rinsed with precooled PBS before being suspended in PBS. The cells were mixed with agarose (1:10 ratio), the mixture was transferred onto a comet slide, and the mixture was cooled at 4 °C for 15 min. Then, the mixture was lysed with precooled 1 × lysis buffer for 30–60 min at 4 °C, the solution was replaced with alkaline solution, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min at 4 °C.

For TBE, the alkaline solution was removed, precooled TBE solution was added, and the mixture was soaked for 5 min; this process was repeated twice. The comet slide was placed in a horizontal bath and covered with precooled TBE solution. The voltage was applied at 1 V/cm for 10–15 min (e.g., 35 V for electrodes 35 cm apart) and adjusted as needed. The comet slide was transferred to precooled deionized water (approximately 25 mL/slide) and soaked for 2 min; this process was repeated three times. The water was removed, precooled 70% ethanol (approximately 25 mL/slide) was added, and the mixture was soaked for 5 min and air-dried horizontally. After drying, 50 μL of 1 × PI dye was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at room temperature for 10 min in the dark. After washing once with PBS, the comet slide was observed with a fluorescence microscope, and images were captured.

Statistical analysis

The statistical software SPSS (IBM, SPSS v. 29.0) was used for the analyses. The means and standard deviations are used to describe all of the data. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Dunnett’s post hoc multiple comparison test were used to compare the groups. p < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Animal numbers were determined by power analysis using G*power software (G*power software 3.1.9.2 Germany). The effect size was calculated to be 0.7 based on our pre-experiments. Power analysis for experiments dictated at least n = 8 animals per group to achieve 80% power for α = 0.05.

Using log dosage vs. response analysis, the IC50 of BPDE was determined. Logistic regression was used to fit the data, and the IC50 value was determined.

The four plant miRNAs did not restore the viability of GC cells exposed to BPDE

We examined the response of cell viability to logarithmic doses of 0, 0.1, 1, 5, and 10 μM BPDE at 24 and 48 h to calculate the IC50 (Figure 2(B)). The IC50 values of BPDE were 1.006 μM (24 h) and 0.345 μM (48 h). As a result, 1 μM of BPDE was used in the subsequent experiments.

The results of the CCK-8 assay revealed that cell viability in the BPDE group (35.1%, 95% CI: 32.232, 38.008) was significantly lower than that in the control group (99.9%, 95% CI: 95.553, 104.441; p = 0.000, F = 429.737). There were no significant differences between the NC group, miR166-y mimics group, miR894-x mimics group, miR822-x mimics group and miR396-x mimics group and the BPDE group. The details are shown in Figure 2(C).

The four plant miRNAs could repair the DNA of GC cells damaged by BPDE

On the basis of the results of the CCK-8 assay, we then performed a comet assay to analyze DNA damage in each group (Figure 3(A)). The headDNA%, tailDNA% and olive tail moments (OTM) were used to evaluate the degree of DNA damage (Figure 3(B)). Compared with those in the control group, the headDNA% in the BPDE group was lower, while the tailDNA% and abundance were greater, suggesting that BPDE is capable of inducing DNA damage in GC cells. Compared with those of the BPDE group, the tailDNA% and the OTM of the four mimic groups were significantly lower, suggesting that the four plant miRNAs repaired GC cell DNA. Among them, the differences in headDNA% and OTM in the miR396-x mimic group were more obvious than those in the other mimic groups, suggesting that miR396 may be the most effective plant miRNA for sperm DNA repair and is worthy of further study.

Discussion

Since testicles and sperm are extremely sensitive to temperature, even at 37 °C, we are particularly strict about temperature and humidity control during experiments. In addition, when storing sperm suspensions or cell samples, we transfer the samples to −80 °C as quickly as possible. Freezing does not increase the sperm DFI (Liu et al. 2022). Moreover, the preservation of mRNA is also recommended at −80 °C, which can stably preserve mRNA for at least three years (Wong et al. 2004).

This research confirmed the ability of YSTL to repair sperm DNA by utilizing a mouse model of DNA damage caused by BaP. Exogenous miRNAs were identified through RNA-seq and then introduced into GC cells, resulting in resistance to DNA damage induced by BPDE, a metabolite of BaP.

In discussing the repair effects of YSTL, we must address its components and possible molecular mechanisms. Previous studies have confirmed that the ingredients contained in YSTL, such as Salvia miltiorrhiza, have antioxidant (Ji et al. 2022) and anti-inflammatory (Jiang et al. 2019) properties, which may help reduce the oxidative stress and inflammatory response caused by BaP, thereby protecting the integrity of sperm DNA.

miRNAs were previously thought to be unstable, but new evidence shows that miRNAs are extremely stable in plasma and cell culture media and can be transferred horizontally between different species (Turchinovich et al. 2011). In the process of preparing traditional Chinese medicine decoctions, the ingredients are often subjected to boiling for 1–2 h or more. In this process, many miRNAs are destroyed. Nonetheless, a significant number of miRNAs that are relatively stable will persist and travel through the bloodstream via the gastrointestinal system to distribute throughout the entire body (Zhang et al. 2012; Zhou et al. 2015). Although the dose may not be high at one time, Chinese medicine practitioners generally instruct patients to take the decoction for several days so that bioaccumulation can be achieved in the body.

miR396 is a miRNA present in many plants, including Arabidopsis, rice, Salvia miltiorrhiza Bunge (Lamiaceae), and ginseng (Jung et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2019a, 2019b; Liu et al. 2023). In a model of Arabidopsis DNA damage induced by ultraviolet B (UV-B) radiation, miR396 may modulate the DNA damage response by regulating the levels of SOG1 and ATR transcripts (Gomez et al. 2019). Numerous miR396 families have been identified in rice, influencing the expression of CMKLR1, which is linked to heart disease, and targeting SCAMP5 and SYVN1, which are associated with autism and colon cancer, respectively (Rakhmetullina et al. 2020).

miR166 and miR894 were also found in our study. Other studies have reported similar results. After 6 h of drought stress, the expression levels of seven plant miRNAs, including miR166 and miR894, in Salvia miltiorrhiza significantly increased (Shu-Fang et al. 2013). miR166 and its possible mechanism of action have also been reported in other plants (Wang et al. 2019a). miRNAs extracted from purified Moringa oleifera Lam (Moringaceae) seeds are classified into 18 well-known families, with miR166 being identified as a potential regulator of BCL2, IL2RA, TNF, and VAV1, genes associated with the cell cycle, apoptosis, immune response, and mechanism of HIV pathogenesis, respectively (Minutolo et al. 2020).

In summary, many studies have shown that TCMs and their extracts, including plant miRNAs, have good clinical efficacy (Lundstrom 2016). TCM miRNAs are also recommended for potential clinical use in treating diseases related to sperm DNA damage. This could include applications such as assisted fertility treatments and the prevention of genetic diseases. Furthermore, further research is needed to determine whether Chinese medicinal miRNAs can effectively prevent genetic diseases. Chinese medicinal miRNAs could have a significant impact on the development and progression of genetic disorders by fixing DNA damage and preventing genetic mutations in offspring.

Conclusions

Plant miRNAs such as miR396 in YSTL can repair sperm DNA damage. They can withstand high-temperature decoction, enter the blood through the digestive tract, and cross the blood–testis barrier to play a role. These findings elucidated the therapeutic mechanisms of Chinese herbal medicine miRNAs and underscore their potential applications in reproductive medicine. Further research is warranted to explore the clinical translation of these findings for the management of diseases associated with sperm DNA damage.
