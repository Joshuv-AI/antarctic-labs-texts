Properties of plactic monoid centralizers
Bruce E. Sagan, Chenchen Zhao

Abstract
Let u u be a word over the positive integers ℙ {\mathbb{P}} . Motivated by a question involving crystal graphs, Sagan and Wilson initiated the study of the centralizer of u u in the plactic monoid which is the set C ⁡ ( u ) = { w ∣ u ​ w is Knuth equivalent to w ​ u } . C(u)=\{w\mid\text{$uw$ is Knuth equivalent to $wu$}\}. In particular, they conjectured the following stability phenomenon: for any u u there is a positive integer K K depending only on u u such that C ⁡ ( u k ) = C ⁡ ( u K ) C(u^{k})=C(u^{K}) for k ≥ K k\geq K . We prove that this property holds for various u u including words consisting of only ones and twos, as well as permutations. Sagan and Wilson also considered c n , m ​ ( u ) c_{n,m}(u) which is the number of w ∈ C ⁡ ( u ) w\in C(u) of length n n and maximum at most m m . They showed that c n , m ​ ( 1 ) c_{n,m}(1) is a polynomial in m m of degree n − 1 n-1 and conjectured properties of the coefficients when it is expanded in a binomial coefficient basis. We prove some of these conjectures, for example, that the coefficients are always nonnegative integers.

Motivated by a question in representation theory, Sagan and Wilson

Our first results have to do with a stability phenomenon. Call

(

)

In the next section, we show that if

Sagan and Wilson also considered the cardinalities

Various conjectures are scattered throughout.

Lemma 2.7

If

\#R_{2}(wu)=\#R_{2}(w)+\#R_{2}(u)+\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(w)).

To compute

, we can take any word Knuth equivalent to

. So we can assume that

u^{\prime}=(21)^{\#R_{2}(u)}\text{ and }u^{\prime\prime}=1^{c_{1}(u)}2^{c_{2}(u)}.

By construction,

into

to the second row. Thus, we have
R_{i}(wu^{\prime})=i^{\#R_{2}(u)}\ R_{i}(w)

. Next, we insert

. During this insertion, every 1 from

Hence, the total number of 2’s in

\#R_{2}(wu)=\#R_{2}(w)+\#R_{2}(u)+\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(w)).

as desired.

We will now give characterizations of words in the centralizer of a

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
u\in[2]^{n}
with
1\leq m_{1}(u)<m_{2}(u)
. Then
w\in C(u)
if and only if
P(w)
satisfy following two conditions.

(a)

c_{1}(w)=c_{1}(u)\leq c_{2}(w)
or
c_{1}(w)=c_{2}(w)<c_{1}(u)
.

(b)

\max R_{i}(w)\leq 2
for
i\leq 2
.

Proof.

We first show that (a) and (b) imply
w\in C(u).
Let
\overline{w}
be the restriction of
w
to its 1s and 2s. Since (b) holds, we can apply Lemma
2.3
to obtain
R_{i}(wu)=R_{i}(\overline{w}u)
and
R_{i}(uw)=R_{i}(u\overline{w})
for
i\leq 2
and
R_{i}(wu)=R_{i}(uw)
for
i\geq 3.
Hence, it suffices to verify that
R_{i}(\overline{w}u)=R_{i}(u\overline{w})
for
i\leq 2
. But since
\overline{w}
and
u
only consist of 1s and 2s this is equivalent to proving
P(\overline{w}u)=P(u\overline{w}).

Applying Lemma
2.7
we see that the total number of 2’s in
R_{2}(\overline{w}u)
is

\#R_{2}(\overline{w}u)=\#R_{2}(\overline{w})+\#R_{2}(u)+\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(\overline{w})).

(1)

Interchanging the roles of of
\overline{w}
and
u
in the same lemma gives

\#R_{2}(u\overline{w})=\#R_{2}(u)+\#R_{2}(\overline{w})+\min(c_{1}(\overline{w}),c_{2}(u)).

(2)

Since
c_{i}(\overline{w})=c_{i}(w)
for
i\leq 2
and the given condition
m_{1}(u)<m_{2}(u)
implies
c_{1}(u)<c_{2}(u)
, we now compare the two minima using the restrictions in part (a) of the theorem. If
c_{1}(u)=c_{1}(w)\leq c_{2}(w)
, then

\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(\overline{w}))=c_{1}(u)=c_{1}(w)=\min(c_{1}(\overline{w}),c_{2}(u)).

If
c_{1}(u)>c_{1}(w)=c_{2}(w)
, then

\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(\overline{w}))=c_{2}(w)=c_{1}(w)=\min(c_{1}(\overline{w}),c_{2}(u)).

Thus,
\#R_{2}(\overline{w}u)=\#R_{2}(u\overline{w})
. Since
\overline{w}u
and
u\overline{w}
contain the same number of 1s and 2s and no other elements, their
P
-tableaux are uniquely determined by the length of the second row. It follows that
P(\overline{w}u)=P(u\overline{w})
so that, by the previous discussion,

For the converse, suppose that
w\in C(u)
, so that
P(wu)=P(uw)
. Since
u
contains both one and two, condition (b) holds by Lemma
2.6
. Applying Lemma
2.7
again to both

, the equality

yields

\#R_{2}(w)+\#R_{2}(u)+\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(w))=\#R_{2}(u)+\#R_{2}(w)+\min(c_{1}(w),c_{2}(u)),

which simplifies to

\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(w))=\min(c_{1}(w),c_{2}(u)).

(3)

Since
m_{2}(u)>m_{1}(u)
, we have

. In particular,

c_{2}(u)>\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(w))=\min(c_{1}(w),c_{2}(u)).

Thus, both minima must equal

, which implies that either
c_{1}(w)=c_{1}(u)\leq c_{2}(w)
or
c_{1}(w)=c_{2}(w)\leq c_{1}(u).

∎

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
u\in[2]^{n}
with
m_{1}(u)=m_{2}(u)\geq 1
. Then
w\in C(u)
if and only if

(a)

\min(c_{1}(w),c_{2}(w))\geq c_{1}(u)

(b)

\max R_{i}(w)\leq 2

Proof.

The proof follows the same structure as the demonstration of the previous theorem. We first show that (a) and (b) imply

. As before, let
\overline{w}
be the restriction of
w
to its ones and twos so that it suffices to show
P(\overline{w}u)=P(u\overline{w})
.

We compare
\#R_{2}(\overline{w}u)
and

using equations (

) and (

c_{i}(\overline{w})=c_{i}(w)
for

and the given condition

implies
c_{1}(u)=c_{2}(u)
. Now compare the two minima using the restrictions in part (a) of the theorem. If

\min\{c_{1}(w),c_{2}(w)\}\geq c_{1}(u)
, then
\min\{c_{1}(\overline{w}),c_{2}(\overline{w})\}\geq c_{1}(u)

\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(\overline{w}))=c_{1}(u)=\min(c_{1}(\overline{w}),c_{1}(u))=\min(c_{1}(\overline{w}),c_{2}(u)).

If

, then

\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(\overline{w}))=c_{2}(w)=c_{1}(w)=\min(c_{1}(\overline{w}),c_{2}(u)).

So, in either case,
\#R_{2}(\overline{w}u)=\#R_{2}(u\overline{w})
.
It follows from the same reasoning as in the proof of the previous theorem that

For the converse, suppose that

, so that
P(wu)=P(uw)
.
As in the previous proof, condition (b) holds and we can assume equation (

Since

, we have

.
So (

\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(w))=\min(c_{1}(u),c_{1}(w)).

There are two cases depending on whether

or

. In the first case, both minima are equal to

, which implies

, we must have

∎

The proof again follows the same structure as the demonstration of the previous theorem. We first verify the reverse implication. Let

\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(\overline{w}))=\min(c_{1}(\overline{w}),c_{2}(u))

We have

On the other hand, the second condition implies

. Thus

\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(\overline{w}))=c_{2}(\overline{w})=c_{1}(\overline{w})=\min(c_{1}(\overline{w}),c_{2}(u)).

So in either case we have equal minima.

For the converse, suppose that

, so that

. Then condition (b) holds by Lemma

. And, as before, equation (

) holds.
Since

, we have

, and hence

c_{1}(u)>\min(c_{1}(w),c_{2}(u))=\min(c_{1}(u),c_{2}(w)).

Both minima must therefore equal

, which implies that either
c_{2}(w)=c_{2}(u)\leq c_{1}(w)
or
c_{1}(w)=c_{2}(w)<c_{2}(u)
.
∎

Using these characterizations of words

over

First we need two final lemmas comparing the number of singleton columns in

Lemma 2.11

Let

.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on

, where the case

is obvious. Assume

for some

and consider

Applying Lemma

, inserting

into

gives

\displaystyle\#R_{2}(u^{k+1})

\displaystyle=\#R_{2}(u^{k})+\#R_{2}(u)+\min(c_{1}(u^{k}),c_{2}(u)).

Using the inductive hypothesis we obtain
c_{1}(u^{k})=c_{1}(u)
. And the assumption
m_{1}(u)\leq m_{2}(u)
implies
c_{1}(u)\leq c_{2}(u)
. Thus

For any word

has two rows, and

Since

\displaystyle c_{1}(u^{k+1})

\displaystyle=(k+1)m_{1}(u)-\bigl(\#R_{2}(u^{k})+\#R_{2}(u)+c_{1}(u)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(km_{1}(u)-\#R_{2}(u^{k})\bigr)+\bigl(m_{1}(u)-\#R_{2}(u)-c_{1}(u)\bigr)

\displaystyle=c_{1}(u^{k}),

where the last equality follows from the identities

and
c_{1}(u)=m_{1}(u)-\#R_{2}(u)

c_{1}(u^{k+1})=c_{1}(u^{k})=c_{1}(u)
which completes the induction.
∎

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
u\in[2]^{n}
with
1\leq m_{2}(u)\leq m_{1}(u)
. For all

The argument follows the same inductive pattern as in Proposition

Since

for binary

c_{2}(u^{k+1})=c_{2}(u^{k})=c_{2}(u)

∎

We now use our characterizations to prove strong stability.

If

Proof.

If

The other possibility is that

) gives

c_{n,1}(1)=a_{1}
. There is a unique
w\in C(1)
of length
n
and maximum
1
, namely
w=1^{n}
. This confirms the value of
a_{1}
.

(c) By
[
SW25
, Corollary 6.3]
, we have

c_{n,2}(1)=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

On the other hand, using (
16
) as well as parts (a) and (b) gives

c_{n,2}(1)=\binom{2}{1}+a_{2}\binom{2}{2}=2+a_{2}.

Equating the two expressions for
a_{2}
finishes the proof.

(d) By Proposition
4.1
we have that the coefficient of
m^{n-1}
in
c_{n,m}(1)
is
1/(n-1)!
. But in (
16
), the only term contributing to this power of

is

a_{n-1}\binom{m}{n-1}=\frac{a_{n-1}}{(n-1)!}m(m-1)\cdots(m-n+2).

Thus
a_{n-1}=1
.

(e) By
[
SW25
, equation (2)]
we have

c_{m,n}(1)=\sum_{\lambda\vdash n}f^{\lambda}g_{m}^{\lambda}

(17)

where
f^{\lambda}
is the number of standard Young tableaux of shape
\lambda
and
g_{m}^{\lambda}
is a polynomial in
m
which we will describe below. Note that
f^{\lambda}
is a positive integer for all
\lambda
. So we will consider the binomial expansion of
g_{m}^{\lambda}
.

If
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{l})
then let
\lambda^{\prime\prime}=(\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{l})
and suppose
\lambda^{\prime\prime}\vdash n^{\prime\prime}
. Then
g_{m}^{\lambda}
is the coefficient of
x^{m-2}
in the power series expansion of

\frac{\sum_{\pi}x^{\des\pi}}{(1-x)^{n^{\prime\prime}+1}}=\sum_{\pi}x^{\des\pi}\sum_{m\geq 0}\binom{m+n^{\prime\prime}}{n^{\prime\prime}}x^{m}

(18)

where
\pi
runs over the linear extensions of the poset
{\mathfrak{P}}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
associated with
\lambda^{\prime\prime}
, and
\des\pi
is the number of descents of
\pi
.
Thus the coefficient of
x^{m-2}
in the term of this expansion corresponding to
\pi
is

\binom{m+n^{\prime\prime}-\des\pi-2}{n^{\prime\prime}}.

(19)

We now break the proof into two cases depending on whether
\lambda^{\prime\prime}
is a column or not.

First consider the contribution of some
\lambda
where
\lambda^{\prime\prime}
is not a column, that is,
\lambda_{2}\geq 2
. It follows that
{\mathfrak{P}}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
has (at least) two integers
k,k^{\prime}
such that
k<k^{\prime}
in
{\mathfrak{P}}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
and
k<k^{\prime}
as integers. So, the descending permutation
\pi=n^{\prime\prime}\ldots 21
is not a linear extension of
{\mathfrak{P}}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
and any
\pi
in (
18
) satisfies
\des\pi\leq n^{\prime\prime}-2
. Letting
p:=n^{\prime\prime}-\des\pi-2
we see that
0\leq p\leq n^{\prime\prime}-2
. We can now write, using Vandermonde’s convolution,

\binom{m+p}{n^{\prime\prime}}=\sum_{i=2}^{n^{\prime\prime}}\binom{p}{n^{\prime\prime}-i}\binom{m}{i}.

Since the coefficients
\binom{p}{n^{\prime\prime}-2}
are nonnegative integers for
i\in[n-1]
, we are done with this case.

Now consider what happens when
\lambda^{\prime\prime}=(1^{n^{\prime\prime}})
for some
0\leq n^{\prime\prime}\leq n-1
. Then the poset
{\mathfrak{P}}_{\lambda^{\prime\prime}}
is the chain
n^{\prime\prime}<n^{\prime\prime}-1<\ldots<1
. So, the only possible linear extension is
\pi=n^{\prime\prime}\ldots 21
with
\des\pi=n^{\prime\prime}-1
. Using (
19
) we see that the contribution in (
18
) to
g_{m}^{\lambda}
for this
\lambda^{\prime\prime}
is

\binom{m-1}{n^{\prime\prime}}
. And in this case
\lambda=(n-n^{\prime\prime},1^{n^{\prime\prime}})
so that, by the hook formula,
f^{\lambda}=\binom{n-1}{n^{\prime\prime}}
. Thus, by applying the binomial recursion twice, the total contribution to (
17
) of these
\lambda

\sum_{n^{\prime\prime}=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{n^{\prime\prime}}\binom{m-1}{n^{\prime\prime}}=\sum_{n^{\prime\prime}=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-2}{n^{\prime\prime}-1}\binom{m}{n^{\prime\prime}}.

The coefficients
\binom{n-2}{n^{\prime\prime}-1}
are positive integers for
n^{\prime\prime}\in[n-1]
. So we can conclude that the
a_{i}
are in
{\mathbb{P}}
, as desired.
∎

To better understand the numbers
c_{n,m}(u)
, we refine them according to the number of distinct letters used in a word. For
m,n\geq 1
, let

\mathcal{C}_{n,m,k}(u):=\{w\in C(u)\mid|w|=n,\ \max w\leq m,\text{ and there are $k$ distinct elements in $w$}\}

and write

c_{n,m,k}(u)=|\mathcal{C}_{n,m,k}(u)|.

Clearly,

c_{n,m}(u)=\sum_{k=1}^{\min(m,n)}c_{n,m,k}(u).

(20)

Let
w
be a word whose set of distinct elements is
S=\{s_{1}<s_{1}<\dots<s_{k}\}.

The
standardization
of
w
is the word
\std(w)

obtained by replacing each element
a_{j}
with its rank
j
in the set
S
. Thus the standardization is
k
-packed as defined in the previous section.
Let

\mathcal{B}_{n,k}:=\{w\in C(1)\mid|w|=n\text{ and }w\text{ is $k$-packed}\}

and

b_{n,k}=|\mathcal{B}_{n,k}|.

Theorem 4.3
.

Fix
n\geq 1
. For all
m,k\geq 1
we have

c_{n,m,k}(1)=b_{n,k}\binom{m-1}{k-1}.

Proof.

Let
m,k\geq 1
be given. For a word
w\in\mathcal{C}_{n,m,k}(1)\subseteq C(1)
, we will show that
v=\std(w)\in{\cal B}_{n,k}(1)
.
Proposition
2.5
(b) shows that the first row of
P(w)
consists entirely of 1’s, so in particular,
1\in w.
Hence
w
contains a set
S\subseteq\{2,\dots,m\}
of
k-1
additional distinct elements.
Because of the presence of the
1
’s, the alphabet of
v
is exactly
[k]
. Since standardization is order-preserving, it also preserves the length of a longest weakly increasing subsequence.
Moreover, the presence of 1 in
w
forces standardization to fix 1, so
v
and
w
have the same length of the longest weakly increasing subsequence ending at 1. By condition (c) of Proposition
2.5
,
v\in C(1).

Therefore
v\in\mathcal{B}_{n,k}(1)
.

Conversely, given an
(k-1)
-element subset
S\subseteq\{2,\dots,m\}
and a word
v\in\mathcal{B}_{n,k}
, the inverse relabeling replaces
2,\dots,k
by the elements of
S
in increasing order, producing a unique word
w
of length
n
with
k
distinct letters from
[m].
Since the condition of Proposition
2.5
(c) is preserved under such relabelings, we have
w\in\mathcal{C}_{n,m,k}(1)
.

Thus, the words counted by
c_{n,m,k}(1)
are in bijection with pairs
(S,v)
where
S
is an
(k-1)
-element subset of
\{2,\dots,m\}
and
v\in\mathcal{B}_{n,k}
. Since there are
b_{n,k}
choices for
v
, it follows that
c_{n,m,k}(1)=b_{n,k}\binom{m-1}{k-1}.
∎

Combining the previous theorem with (
20
), we immediately get the following result.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
n
be fixed and suppose
m\geq n
. Then

c_{n,m}(1)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{n,k}\binom{m-1}{k-1}.

(21)

We now investigate the properties of the coefficients in this expansion. Because we have a combinatorial interpretation for the
b_{n,k}
, our proofs are simpler than for the coefficients
a_{k}
in Theorem
4.2
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Suppose
n
is fixed and
m\geq n
, write

c_{n,m}(1)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}b_{k}\binom{m-1}{k-1}

(22)

where
b_{k}=b_{n,k}
. We have the following.

(a)

b_{k}\in{\mathbb{P}}
for all
k\in[n]
.

(b)

b_{1}=1
.

(c)

b_{2}=\displaystyle\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}-1

(d)

b_{n}=1
.

Proof.

(a) Let
k\in[n]
. Consider the word
w=k(k-1)\dots 21^{n-k+1}.
It is easy to see that
\text{lwi}(w)=\text{lwi}(w,1)
. By Proposition
2.5
(c), this implies that
w\in C(1).
Since
w
has length
n
and alphabet
[k]
, we have
w\in\mathcal{B}_{n,k}
. Hence,
b_{k}=b_{n,k}\in\mathbb{P}.

(b) There is a unique
1
-packed word of length
n
, namely
w=1^{n}
. Thus,
b_{1}=b_{n,1}=1.

(c) Notice that by definition,
c_{n,2}(1)=b_{n,1}+b_{n,2}
. By
[
SW25
, Corollary 6.3]
, we have

c_{n,2}(1)=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

Using part (b) gives

b_{2}=b_{n,2}=\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}-1.

(d) There is a unique
n
-packed word of length
n
, namely
w=n(n-1)\dots 1
. Thus,
b_{n}=b_{n,n}=1.
∎
