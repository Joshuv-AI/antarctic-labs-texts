Ambient particulate matter exposure and acute type A aortic dissection: A retrospective time-stratified case-crossover study in northern Italy
Authors: Alberto Campailla, Alan Gallingani, Giulia Pampuri, Alessia Kurti, Daniela Aschieri, Giampaolo Niccoli, Francesco Nicolini, Davide Carino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsAmbient particulate matter triggers acute cardiovascular events, but its relationship with acute type A aortic dissection is poorly characterised in Europe. We examined whether ambient PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations were associated with Stanford type A acute aortic dissection (AAD) in northern Italy.MethodsWe performed a retrospective, single-centre, time-stratified case-crossover study of 40 patients with confirmed Stanford type A AAD (2018-2022). Case days were compared with referent days on the same weekday within the same month and year. Daily mean PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations from the fixed Piacenza-Parco Montecucco urban-background monitoring station in Piacenza were evaluated for the same calendar day and a 30-day window including the index day. Separate conditional logistic regression models were adjusted for ambient temperature.ResultsA doubling of same-day PM2.5 was associated with increased odds of a case day (adjusted OR 4.78, 95% CI 1.61-14.29; nominal p = 0.005). Associations in the same direction were observed for 30-day PM2.5 and for PM10 in both windows. Estimates were imprecise and correlated and should be considered exploratory.ConclusionIn this selected cohort, higher same-day and 30-day particulate concentrations were associated with Stanford type A AAD case days. Larger multicentre studies using individual-level and strictly pre-event exposure estimates are required before clinical or public-health inferences can be drawn.

Lay Summary

Air pollution from tiny airborne particles (particulate matter) is a well-known trigger of heart attacks and cardiac arrest, but its possible link to acute aortic dissection—a life-threatening tear in the body's main artery—has been little studied in Europe.

In this small study of 40 patients with Stanford type A dissection, case days were associated with higher fine-particle (PM2.5) concentrations. Because the number of patients was small and the exposure windows could include measurements after symptom onset, these results are uncertain and cannot establish that air pollution causes dissection.

Rather than supporting any immediate change in clinical practice or air-quality policy, the study raises a hypothesis that deserves to be tested in larger, multi-centre investigations. If confirmed, such work could help clarify whether the aorta, like the coronary arteries, is vulnerable to short-term increases in air pollution.

Introduction

Ambient air pollution is a major modifiable cardiovascular exposure worldwide. State-of-the-art reviews and joint statements from the World Heart Federation, American College of Cardiology, American Heart Association, and European Society of Cardiology identify polluted air as an important determinant of cardiovascular risk [[1], [2], [3]]. Particulate matter, particularly the fine (PM2.5) and inhalable (PM10) fractions, is the most consistently implicated component; short-term increases have been linked to acute coronary syndromes, ischaemic stroke, ventricular arrhythmias, and out-of-hospital cardiac arrest [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]].

Acute aortic dissection (AAD) is a catastrophic cardiovascular emergency with high early mortality. The established predisposing factors—arterial hypertension, heritable aortopathies, bicuspid aortic valve, and degenerative medial disease—explain the susceptibility of the aortic wall, but they do not account for the precise timing of the event [9,10]. Transient perturbations in wall stress, endothelial integrity, or systemic inflammatory burden could plausibly lower the threshold for intimal tear in an already vulnerable aortic segment, and it is in this context that acute environmental triggers become relevant.

Several biological pathways provide a credible bridge between particulate inhalation and acute aortic injury. Inhaled particulate matter promotes systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, autonomic imbalance, and acute elevations in blood pressure [1,7,11]. Experimental work in murine models has shown that PM2.5 exposure can aggravate thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection through smooth-muscle-cell apoptosis and activation of the MAPK pathway [12]. A small but growing body of observational evidence—predominantly from East Asia— has reported associations between particulate exposure and AAD hospitalisation, suggesting that the aorta may belong to the same cardiovascular vulnerability landscape already described for coronary and arrhythmic events [[13], [14], [15], [16]].

European data on this relationship remain sparse. We therefore conducted a retrospective time-stratified case-crossover study to examine whether ambient PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations were associated with the occurrence of Stanford type A AAD in a single-centre cohort from northern Italy. Given the rarity of the event and the constraints of a single-centre series, the study was conceived as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating investigation rather than a confirmatory one.

Methods

Study design and reporting framework

We used a time-stratified case-crossover design, a method well established for the study of transient environmental exposures and acute health events [17,18]. In this design each patient serves as his or her own control, so that all time-invariant individual characteristics - age, sex, genotype, baseline aortic anatomy, chronic comorbidity, and habitual behaviour - are matched by design and cannot confound the exposure-outcome association. Reporting was guided by the STROBE statement for observational studies [19].

Study population

Patients were eligible if they underwent operative repair for confirmed Stanford type A AAD at the Cardiac Surgery Unit of the Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria di Parma between 1 January 2018 and 31 December 2022. Fifty-two surgically treated patients were assessed. Twelve patients were excluded because their residential address was more than 10 km from Piacenza-Parco Montecucco, leaving 40 patients in the analytic cohort. The diagnosis was confirmed by cross-sectional imaging in every case. Selection of control periods and time stratification

For each case, we applied a bidirectional, time-stratified scheme to select referent days. The stratum was defined by calendar year, calendar month, and day of the week: referent days were all other days within the same month and year that shared the same day of the week as the case day. This approach yields three or four referent days per case and controls by design for season, long-term temporal trends, and day-of-week patterns. The case day was the calendar date of diagnosis; exact event time was not used in the exposure assignment. The matched non-case days within the stratum served as referent periods.

Exposure assessment

Validated daily mean PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations were assigned from the fixed Piacenza-Parco Montecucco monitoring station, located in Piacenza and operated by ARPAE Emilia-Romagna. ARPAE classifies Piacenza-Parco Montecucco as a regional urban-background station in the Pianura Ovest zone, equipped to monitor both PM10 and PM2.5. The same station was used for every participant; neither nearest-station assignment nor averaging across stations was performed. Residence-to-station distance was used only to apply the 10-km eligibility threshold and was not entered as a continuous exposure variable. We did not reconstruct daily means from hourly records or impose additional author-defined completeness criteria; official agency-validated daily mean values were used as supplied.

Two exposure windows were evaluated. The same-day window was defined as the midnight-to-midnight daily mean concentration for the calendar day assigned to the event and, for referent periods, for the corresponding matched days. Depending on event time, this metric could include concentrations measured after dissection onset. The 30-day window was defined as the mean concentration over the index day and the preceding 29 calendar days (Fig. 1). It was therefore not a strictly pre-onset metric, although any post-onset contribution was diluted across 30 daily values. Statistical analysis

Associations between particulate matter and Stanford type A AAD were estimated by conditional logistic regression, with risk sets defined by each case and its matched referent days. PM2.5 was modelled on a log2 scale, so that the OR corresponds to the change in odds per doubling of concentration; PM10 was modelled per 10 μg/m3 increment. For each pollutant and window, we fitted a crude model and a model adjusted for ambient temperature, entered as a continuous covariate (same-day temperature for the same-day window and 30-day mean temperature for the 30-day window); seasonality and secular trend were controlled by the time-stratified matching. ORs are reported with 95% confidence intervals. All p values are two-sided and nominal, and no adjustment for the four pollutant-window comparisons was applied. A nominal p < 0.05 threshold was used descriptively. Given the exploratory design, results were interpreted with emphasis on direction and precision rather than as confirmatory significance tests. No distributed-lag or multi-pollutant modelling was attempted. Analyses were performed using Stata software, Version 15 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Because pollutant fractions and moving-average windows are correlated, agreement across the four estimates was not treated as independent replication.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Comitato Etico AVEN (Area Vasta Emilia Nord), Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria di Parma, in June 2024 (protocol no. 148/INT/2024). The requirement for individual written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because of the retrospective, registry-based design and the use of anonymised clinical and environmental data. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Study cohort

Of 52 patients who underwent operative repair for confirmed Stanford type A AAD during the 2018-2022 study period, 12 were excluded because their residential address was more than 10 km from Piacenza-Parco Montecucco. The analytic cohort therefore comprised 40 patients, all of whom met the residential eligibility criterion. The baseline characteristics of the cohort are presented in Table 1. The patients had a mean age of 64.8 ± 12.3 years, were predominantly male (70.0%), and carried a high burden of arterial hypertension (77.5%); all dissections were Stanford type A, and in-hospital mortality was 12.5%. Heritable aortopathy was uncommon (Marfan syndrome in one patient), and the burden of metabolic and renal comorbidity was modest (diabetes mellitus in 15% and chronic kidney disease in 10%). Two patients had undergone previous cardiac surgery (one coronary artery bypass grafting and one valve replacement), and none had a history of previous aortic dissection or aortic surgery. Because each patient acted as his or her own control, these time-invariant characteristics were matched within the case-crossover strata and could not bias the exposure-outcome estimates.

Association between particulate matter and stanford type A AAD

Higher ambient particulate matter concentrations were associated with case days for Stanford type A AAD. Among the four exploratory pollutant-window associations, the largest adjusted point estimate was observed for same-day PM2.5: after adjustment for temperature, a doubling of PM2.5 was associated with a nearly fivefold increase in the odds of a case day (OR 4.78, 95% CI 1.61-14.29; nominal p = 0.005) (Fig. 2, Table 2). Associations in the same direction were observed for the 30-day PM2.5 window (adjusted OR 3.86, 95% CI 1.09-13.67; nominal p = 0.037) and for PM10 per 10 μg/m3 at both the same-day (adjusted OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.002-1.148; nominal p = 0.046) and 30-day (adjusted OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.01-1.20; nominal p = 0.028) windows. Discussion

Principal findings

In this single-centre retrospective case-crossover study of Stanford type A AAD, higher ambient PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations were associated with case days, with estimates in the same direction across the same-day and 30-day windows. A doubling of same-day PM2.5 corresponded to an approximately fivefold increase in the adjusted odds of a case day. This estimate is imprecise and derived from only 40 events. Agreement across correlated pollutant fractions and overlapping exposure windows provides descriptive coherence but is not independent replication; the numerical magnitude of each estimate should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Interpretation of the effect magnitude

The point estimate is considerably larger than the per-unit risk increments typically reported for acute coronary events or cardiac arrest, where ORs per 10 μg/m3 generally fall between 1.01 and 1.12 [5,6]. Several factors caution against over-interpreting this difference. First, the exposure metric is not comparable: a doubling of concentration is a much larger contrast than a fixed 10 μg/m3 increment, and in a region where particulate levels vary widely a doubling can represent a substantial absolute change. Second, the small sample inevitably inflates and destabilises the estimate, a phenomenon well recognised when rare outcomes are studied in single centres. Third, it is biologically conceivable, though unproven, that a structurally vulnerable aortic wall responds to particulate-induced haemodynamic and inflammatory stress with a steeper risk gradient than the coronary circulation. We regard the first two explanations as the most likely and present the third only as a hypothesis.

Comparison with prior literature

Time-series analyses spanning hundreds of cities have documented dose–response relationships between particulate exposure and cardiovascular mortality [20], while case-crossover and time-series studies have linked particulate matter to myocardial infarction [4], stroke [8], and out-of-hospital cardiac arrest [5,6]. Working in the same highly polluted area of northern Italy, Moderato and colleagues reported independent associations between particulate matter and cardiac arrest that persisted at short- and medium-term windows [5]. Evidence specific to aortic disease has been accumulating largely in East Asian populations: Chen et al. described a time-series association between fine particulate matter and AAD in Shanghai [13], Xie et al. reported meteorological and air-quality correlates of type A and type B dissections [14], Zhang et al. identified short-term pollution effects on AAD in a cold-climate setting [15], and Lin et al. linked pollutant exposure to mortality after dissection [16]. Our data extend this signal, with all the caveats of sample size, to a European context. Geographical replication is important because pollutant composition, climate, baseline particulate levels, and population structure differ appreciably between East Asian and European settings.

Biological plausibility

Several mechanisms could connect particulate exposure to failure of the aortic wall (Fig. 3). Inhaled fine particles provoke systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial activation [1,7]; in a susceptible aorta these processes may enhance matrix metalloproteinase activity, degrade extracellular-matrix integrity, and promote smooth-muscle-cell apoptosis. Particulate exposure also elicits acute rises in blood pressure and shifts in autonomic balance [11,21], thereby increasing wall shear stress on an already weakened media. Ming and colleagues provided direct experimental support, showing that PM2.5 aggravated thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection through MAPK-mediated apoptosis in a murine model [12]. In patients with coronary disease, short-term particulate exposure has also been associated with plaque vulnerability and instability on intracoronary imaging [22], and particulate matter is consistently placed among the leading environmental determinants of acute cardiovascular events [23]. These external environmental influences act on a host whose internal milieu—blood pressure control, metabolic state, and lifestyle—also shapes vascular vulnerability; the complementary contribution of modifiable external and internal environmental factors to cardiovascular health has been articulated, for example, in the E(e)SEEDi lifestyle framework [24]. The 2018-2022 study period and the COVID-19 pandemic

Because the study spanned the years 2018 to 2022, part of the observation period coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic. During the early pandemic in New York City, the volume of surgically treated type A aortic dissection fell markedly, raising concern that emergency presentation and access to care had been disrupted [25]. Individual SARS-CoV-2 infection status and pandemic-related healthcare-seeking behaviour were not available in our dataset and could not be modelled. The time-stratified design mitigates broad temporal influences by matching case and control days within the same narrow calendar window, but it cannot eliminate all confounding associated with abrupt changes in infection prevalence, mobility, healthcare utilisation, and ambient pollution during the pandemic period. We therefore regard this interval as an additional source of uncertainty that reinforces the exploratory nature of our findings.

Clinical and preventive implications

We deliberately frame the clinical implications in cautious terms. These data are insufficient to justify changes to air-quality standards, targeted pollution warnings for patients with aortic disease, or incorporation of environmental exposure into aortic-risk surveillance. The study adds a small European dataset to the hypothesis that the aorta, like the coronary and cerebral circulations, may be sensitive to short-term variation in ambient particulate matter. The Po Valley is a relevant setting in which to explore this question because substantial particulate pollution has been documented in northern Italy [5]. Confirmation in adequately powered studies would be required before periods of poor air quality could be considered clinically meaningful risk windows for patients with known aortic pathology.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations, most of which follow from its design and setting and should temper interpretation. The foremost is the sample of only 40 events, which limits statistical power, produces wide confidence intervals, and may yield unstable or exaggerated estimates. The single-centre design further restricts generalisability, as does the selected cohort, which consisted exclusively of surgically treated Stanford type A dissections. Of 52 operated patients assessed during the study period, 12 were excluded because their residential address was more than 10 km from Piacenza-Parco Montecucco. Patients who died before referral, were managed non-operatively, had type B dissection, or otherwise fell outside the operative source cohort are not represented. This selection may limit external validity and could introduce selection bias; the findings should not be extrapolated to all acute aortic dissections.

A central methodological challenge in any case-crossover study of a rare event is the identification of appropriate control periods. We addressed this by using a time-stratified, bidirectional scheme that matches each case day to control days of the same weekday within the same month and year, thereby controlling by design for season, day-of-week effects, and long-term trend, and by allowing each patient to serve as his or her own control, which removes confounding by all time-invariant individual characteristics. This design cannot, however, account for short-term time-varying confounders—such as transient physical exertion, emotional stress, acute infection, or abrupt blood-pressure surges—that may coincide with both high-pollution days and the event itself; residual confounding of this kind remains possible.

Exposure misclassification is a further important limitation. Pollutant concentrations were derived from one fixed ambient monitoring station rather than personal monitoring and therefore do not capture time spent indoors, occupational exposure, residential mobility, or local traffic-related gradients. The same-day exposure was a midnight-to-midnight daily mean and may include concentrations measured after symptom onset, particularly for events occurring early in the day; it therefore cannot be interpreted as a strictly antecedent exposure. The 30-day mean also included the index day and could contain a smaller post-onset contribution. Moreover, the 30-day moving averages for case and control days within the same month overlap extensively, reducing independent within-stratum exposure contrast and potentially increasing estimate instability. The single-station assignment assumes that Piacenza-Parco Montecucco adequately represents all eligible residences within the 10-km eligibility radius. Non-differential exposure error often attenuates associations, but the direction and magnitude of bias cannot be assumed here because temporal alignment and selection may also be imperfect. We restricted the analysis to PM2.5 and PM10 and did not include gaseous co-pollutants or multi-pollutant models, so the independent contribution of particulate matter cannot be separated from correlated pollutants. Temperature was modelled linearly using the same aggregation window as the particulate exposure; residual confounding by nonlinear or lagged meteorological effects cannot be excluded. Four correlated pollutant-window associations were examined without multiplicity adjustment; the p values are nominal, and the small sample increases the risk of false-positive and sparse-data findings. Robustness to strictly pre-event windows, influential strata, and alternative exposure scaling remains to be established. Finally, the retrospective observational design does not support causal inference, and the overlap with the COVID-19 pandemic adds the uncertainties discussed above.

Future directions

Replication in larger, geographically diverse, multicentre cohorts is the clear priority and the principal way to resolve the imprecision of the present estimates. Future studies should link environmental data to detailed clinical registries that include aortic imaging, dissection classification, biomarker profiles, comorbidity, and outcomes, and should incorporate gaseous co-pollutants within multi-pollutant models and, where feasible, personal or model-based individual exposure assessment. Pre-specified analysis of multiple lag structures would help characterise the temporal relationship between exposure and event. Only through such work can the hypothesis raised here be properly tested.

Conclusions

In this small single-centre retrospective case-crossover study of Stanford type A AAD, higher same-day and 30-day ambient PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations were associated with case days. A doubling of same-day PM2.5 corresponded to an approximately fivefold increase in the adjusted odds of a case day, but this estimate was imprecise and derived from only 40 events. Interpretation is limited by potential temporal and spatial exposure misclassification, unmeasured short-term confounders, cohort selection, correlated analyses, and the circumstances of the 2018-2022 period. The findings remain hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger multicentre studies using strictly pre-event exposure windows before ambient particulate matter can be considered in aortic-risk assessment.
