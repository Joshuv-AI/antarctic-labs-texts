Determining the Environmental and Ecological Factors Associated With Poultry Farm Spillover of Highly Pathogenic Avian Influenza (H5N1) in British Columbia, Canada
Authors: Riley Oremush, Pascale Aubry, E. Jane Parmley, Zvonimir Poljak, Amy Greer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionHighly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) H5N1, clade 2.3.4.4b is a global threat causing widespread outbreaks on poultry farms and high mortality among wild bird populations in Canada. Wild aquatic birds are generally recognised as key reservoir hosts for avian influenza viruses (AIV's). Furthermore, environmental weather variables have been hypothesised to play a critical role in the spread and persistence of HPAI in the external environment. During the 2022 outbreak of HPAI (H5N1) in Canada, many positive farm cases were identified via epidemiological investigations as independent introductions, likely a result of virus spillover from the external environment into the poultry farm. We investigated the association between recently occurring meteorological and wild bird occurrence factors and the likelihood of poultry farms in British Columbia testing positive for HPAI (H5N1) due to independent viral introductions.MethodsUsing a retrospective case‐crossover analysis, this study produced a multivariable conditional logistic regression model examining weekly‐averaged meteorological factors (n = 49) and a series of univariable models to examine wild bird occurrence across case and control periods for independent introductions of HPAI onto poultry farms in British Columbia (n = 48).ResultsDuring case periods, we found significant associations between the following variables: our multivariable analysis found elevated levels of precipitation and decreasing maximum relative humidity increased odds of independent introduction events. Additionally, the increasing occurrence of dabbling duck species groups, snow geese ( Anser caerulescens ) and short‐billed gulls (Larus brachyrhynchus) during case periods was associated with increased odds of independent introduction events.ConclusionsThis research identifies key weather variables and increased occurrence of specific waterbird species groups in the weeks directly prior to case occurrence as predictive factors for the occurrence of independent introductions of HPAI (H5N1) onto British Columbia poultry farms. These findings help us better understand the weather and wild bird factors likely associated with the risk of spillover of HPAI to poultry farms in this region.

Introduction

The rapid emergence and spread of highly pathogenic avian influenza HPAI (H5N1) clade 2.3.4.4b has reshaped the landscape of infectious disease threats, posing significant risks to wildlife, agriculture and public health across the globe. In Canada, the first detection of HPAI (H5N1), clade 2.3.4.4b was identified on a poultry farm in Newfoundland and Labrador in December 2021 (Caliendo et al. 2022). In the following year (2022), 235 poultry premises with HPAI (H5N1) detections, across nine provinces were depopulated (Canadian Food Inspection Agency 2023a). The ongoing impact of the HPAI H5N1 outbreak in Canada, has been further characterised by observations of high mortality among wild bird populations as well as both terrestrial and marine mammal species (Avery‐Gomm et al. 2024; Gamarra‐Toledo et al. 2023; Renaud et al. 2023). Wild birds, particularly aquatic species, are generally recognised as primary reservoir hosts for avian influenza viruses (AIV), including HPAI (H5N1) and play a critical role in pathogen transmission (Environment and Climate Change Canada 2015). HPAI is a significant concern for Canada because of the major agricultural & economic consequences (Pasick et al. 2009).

Among wild birds, HPAI (H5N1) is primarily transmitted via faecal‐oral pathways and environmental contamination—largely from water sources (US National Science Foundation 2023). These transmission pathways may lead to viral contamination of the external environment around poultry farms.

Environmental factors such as lower temperature and ultraviolet radiation have been shown to increase AIV persistence under laboratory conditions, particularly in aquatic environments (Davidson et al. 2010; Spickler 2016; Stallknecht and Brown 2009). In addition, research on previous AIV outbreaks has indicated the potential for environmental factors including presence of standing water, relative humidity and wind speed to influence virus persistence and transmissibility (An et al. 2021; Domanska‐Blicharz et al. 2010; European Food Safety Authority et al. 2024; Jonges et al. 2015; Nagy et al. 2025). Combined, this suggests that wild birds shed virus into the environment and under certain environmental conditions, the virus persists in the environment, creating opportunities for the virus to be introduced from the environment into poultry farms.

In accordance with the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH), the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) classifies ‘poultry farms’ to include both commercial and non‐commercial operations, and is defined by the rearing of birds (chicken, turkey, duck, geese) for either the production of animal products or for breeding (Canadian Food Inspection Agency 2023b; World Organisation for Animal Health 2023). In contrast, ‘non‐poultry farms’ keep birds for use within the same household, or for reasons other than the production of animal products (Canadian Food Inspection Agency 2023b; World Organisation for Animal Health 2023).

Cases of HPAI (H5N1) on poultry farms are characterised by the presumed origin of the virus introduction, as identified during the outbreak investigation. Upon the initial suspicion of an outbreak of HPAI on a poultry farm, regional government health units (local district CFIA officials) enforce a quarantine of the flock until verification of a strain of concern is identified (Canadian Food Inspection Agency 2014; Ontario Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Agribusiness 2022). Positive farms are broadly labelled as either ‘independent introductions’, where HPAI is believed to have been introduced through indirect exposure to a contaminated environment or wild birds; or as ‘epidemiologically‐linked cases’, where the introduction of HPAI is most likely due to transmission from a separately infected farm case (Canadian Food Inspection Agency 2014, 2022). Over the course of the 2022 H5N1 outbreak in Canada, a large proportion of HPAI detections on poultry farms were identified as independent introduction events, the majority of which were in the province of British Columbia (Canadian Food Inspection Agency 2022; Youk et al. 2023). While independent introductions are often assumed to originate from the external environment via wild birds (Youk et al. 2023), the environmental and ecological factors shaping these transmission events remain poorly understood. Understanding the mechanisms influencing the occurrence of independent introduction events is important for mitigating future cases. This research uses a case‐crossover study design to identify possible associations between ecological and environmental factors and the likelihood of poultry farms testing positive for HPAI (H5N1) due to independent introductions.

Methods

Case‐Crossover Analysis

A retrospective case‐crossover design was used to explore the associations between recent meteorological factors and wild bird occurrence and identified independent introductions of HPAI (H5N1) on poultry farms in British Columbia, Canada during 2022. Case‐crossover analyses examine the influence of transient exposures on acute outcomes, in addition to studying changes in exposure levels over time (Mittleman et al. 1995; Mittleman and Mostofsky 2014). This self‐matched study design compares exposures during a time period correlating with case occurrence, known as a case period, to the exposures during a time period outside of case occurrence, known as a control period.

To assess the association of meteorological variables and wild bird species occurrence with independent introduction events, we used a 2‐week exposure window for both case‐ and control‐periods. This exposure window follows a 2‐week lag from on‐farm HPAI detection, defined as the date of first clinical signs, and was chosen to account for the time it takes for environmental contamination of H5N1 and the incubation period of HPAI (H5N1) at the flock level (BC Centre for Disease Control 2024; Hobbelen et al. 2020; World Organisation for Animal Health 2023) (Figure 1). Given that biosecurity efforts following confirmation of an HPAI (H5N1)‐positive farm case resulted in the immediate depopulation of birds on the poultry farm, only time periods prior to the case period were considered for the selection of the control periods. Case‐ and control‐periods were matched for each farm, with a 2‐week lag employed to ensure separation between periods (Figure 1).

Visualisation of retrospective case‐crossover design, illustrating comparison of the case‐period (yellow) with the control period (blue) to assess differences in exposure related to independent introduction events, from the initial detection of HPAI, defined as the date of first clinical signs on poultry farms.

Case‐Data

National data about HPAI (H5N1) outbreaks on Canadian poultry farms in 2022 were obtained via a data‐sharing agreement with the Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA). The presumed origin of infection of farm cases as independent introductions or epidemiologically linked was identified by the outbreak investigation of the farm and further confirmed via genetic analysis. It was determined that 110 independent introduction events had been identified among the 235 total commercial and non‐commercial poultry farm cases of HPAI (H5N1) across Canada. The majority (45%) of Canadian independent introduction events were found to have occurred in the province of British Columbia (Figure 2). This study investigated independent introduction events reported from British Columbia, Canada as it had the largest proportion of these cases within a defined area.

Flowchart describing the breakdown of highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) (H5N1) cases between 25 March 2022 and 31 December 2022. † Two farm cases were excluded from analysis due to a lack of weather stations within a 65‐km radius, or an insufficient amount of spatially distinct recordings of wild birds within a 25‐km radius of the farm.

Meteorological Data

Historical meteorological data were compiled from Environment and Climate‐Change Canada weather stations from across the province of British Columbia (Environment and Climate Change Canada 2025). Relevant weather stations (n = 7, Table 1) were selected based on proximity to farm cases (within 65 km) and availability of daily data recordings for temperature, precipitation, wind speed and relative humidity. The 65 km radius was selected to maximise the use of available weather data, while also remaining within the 100 km threshold for representative meteorological information for biological activity in British Columbia (Lawson and Armitage 2008). For continuous weather variables, daily data values were aggregated as weekly rolling averages. Weekly mean temperature (°C), weekly maximum humidity (%) and weekly maximum wind gust speed (km/h) were incorporated as continuous data due to their direct and ongoing impact on how long viruses remain viable and their degree of potential transmission in the environment. Precipitation data were categorised to assess rainfall events capable of producing standing water, a presumed risk factor for attracting wild birds and facilitating viral contamination of the local environment. Weekly total precipitation was divided into three categories: no precipitation weeks (0 mm); low precipitation weeks (> 0 mm to < 10 mm); and elevated precipitation weeks (≥ 10 mm), representing total weekly rainfall levels likely to generate standing water. The threshold of 10 mm was selected based on the presumed capacity to generate standing water, enabling viral contamination of the local environment by congregating wild birds (Burt and Weerasinghe 2014; McErlich et al. 2023).

Wild Bird Data

Wild bird presence data were compiled from the Cornell eBird database (Sullivan et al. 2009; Strimas‐Mackey et al. 2025). eBird is a citizen‐science collaborative enterprise to document bird distributions, abundance, habitat use and trends through checklist data collected within a simple, scientific framework (Sullivan et al. 2009). Selection of wild bird species was based on previous research identifying species with increased susceptibility and prevalence to avian influenza viruses (AIV's) in the Fraser Valley region of British Columbia and the northwestern United States (Blagodatski et al. 2021; Brown et al. 2006; Burns et al. 2012; McDuie et al. 2022; Samuel et al. 2015). To account for potential observational biases, certain bird species frequently misidentified were aggregated into broader multi‐species groups (hereafter referred to as bird species groups) for analysis (Table 2) (Canadian Wildlife Service 2015).

The data extracted from eBird included complete, non‐duplicate checklists reporting observations of specific bird species, serving as an approximation for bird species occurrence (Deomurari et al. 2023). The use of eBird data creates potential spatial, temporal and reporting biases (Zhang 2020). Complete checklists were exclusively used to correct for potential reporting bias inherent in citizen science data and to standardise observation efforts. Furthermore, potential spatial and temporal bias due to uneven reporting efforts between regions or over time are inherently corrected for due to the self‐matched nature of the case‐crossover study design. Bird species occurrence was defined as the number of locations where a given bird species group was observed per week, within a 25 km radius of each H5N1‐positive farm. The 25 km radius was selected to account for the local density and movement of birds in the weeks surrounding a spillover event to a farm and provides a representative spatial scale for capturing the local avian‐agricultural interface (Masto et al. 2022; McDuie et al. 2019). Previous studies have independently used citizen science recorded observations to approximate species occurrence successfully, with eBird data specifically demonstrating sufficient temporal granularity for weekly assessments (Coxen et al. 2017; Deomurari et al. 2023; Fink et al. 2020; Snäll et al. 2011). This methodology was chosen over the use of predicted or relative abundance estimates derived from historical trends, as it provides an accurate proxy for the spatial distribution and environmental pressure of potential reservoir species at specific time‐points (Coxen et al. 2017; Snäll et al. 2011). To further reduce potential risk of reporting bias, avoid zero‐inflation and filter out low‐surveillance regions, bird species groups within the 25 km radius of each farm with fewer than 30 spatially distinct geographic locations were excluded from the analysis (Deomurari et al. 2023).

Statistical Analyses

To analyse the associations between H5N1 independent introduction events, univariable conditional logistic regression models were produced for various environmental and wild bird occurrence variables across case and control periods (Therneau 2024). All statistical analyses were conducted using R and R‐Studio (R Core Team 2025; Posit team 2025). Weather variables which indicated a significant association univariably were then incorporated into a multivariable case‐crossover model. The correlation between independent variables was assessed using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. If the correlation between variables exceeded |0.75|, the more epidemiologically plausible variable was retained for subsequent multivariable modelling. Collinearity between covariates was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). If the VIF value for two variables was greater than 4, the more epidemiologically plausible variable was retained in the model. Linearity was assessed between each continuous independent variable and the log odds of the outcome using locally weighted linear regression (LOWESS) and by assessing the statistical significance of adding a quadratic term. Interaction terms between variables retained in the multivariable model were also assessed.

Multivariable modelling incorporating multiple wild bird variables was not performed due to the inherent biological co‐occurrence and high spatial temporal collinearity among migratory waterfowl species during the study period. The species occurrence between these waterfowl species groups acts as highly correlated proxies for the same theoretical environmental pressure. Furthermore, the limited number of case data in tandem with highly correlated wild bird species occurrence would increase the risk of overfitting. A significance level of 5% (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant and used for all univariable and multivariable modelling.

Results

Descriptive Results

Of the 50 independent introductions of HPAI (H5N1) on poultry farms in the province of British Columbia, Canada, beginning with the first case on 7 April and continuing through 28 December 2022, the final sample size varied by analysis based on data availability. For the weather analyses (n = 49), one farm case was excluded because there was no weather station within a 65 km radius of the case. For the wild bird analyses (n = 48), two farm cases were excluded due to fewer than 30 recorded wild‐bird observations within the 25‐km vicinity of the farm. The majority of H5N1 cases identified in British Columbia during this time period were located within or in close proximity to the Fraser‐Valley region (Figure 3), with the province having largely been affected during the second epidemic wave in Canada (September 2022–December 2022). Some degree of clustering appears to be present, with most cases in BC detected during the late fall—early winter of 2022, while fewer cases were reported during the summer months of June to August.

In the univariable conditional logistic regression analyses, mean temperature, maximum relative humidity, maximum wind gusting speed and high levels of precipitation (> 10 mm) during case‐period weeks were significantly associated with independent introductions of H5N1 on poultry farms. Detailed coefficient estimates and odds ratios for all univariable models are provided in the Supporting Information (Appendix S1).

To assess the independent effects of these environmental drivers, significant variables were incorporated into a multivariable case‐crossover model (Figure 4). To ensure the full sample size, maximum wind gust speed was excluded from the multivariable analysis due to a lack of available data across all study locations (weather stations for 10 farm cases did not record wind speed data during the relevant study weeks). In the final multivariable model, precipitation and maximum relative humidity remained strong independent predictors of H5N1 introduction (Table 4). Compared to weeks with light precipitation, poultry farms with case periods experiencing elevated precipitation (> 10 mm) were significantly associated with increased odds of an independent introduction event (OR, 7.14, 95% CI, 1.46–34.9). Maximum relative humidity maintained a strongly significant inverse relationship, with a 1% increase in maximum humidity during case periods reducing the odds of an introduction event by 24% (OR, 0.76, 95% CI, 0.64–0.90). While in the univariable analysis, mean temperature demonstrated a significant protective effect, this association was no longer statistically significant when accounting for variance explained by precipitation and humidity.

Wild Bird Occurrence Results

Descriptive statistics for bird species occurrence by species groups are presented in Table 5. Bird species group occurrence was analysed using a series of univariable case‐crossover analyses. This research identified significant associations between higher occurrence of particular waterbird species groups within a 25 km radius of farms and HPAI (H5N1) cases resulting from independent introductions (Table 6). It was found that case periods with increasing occurrence of all duck species groups examined (mallard, northern pintail and teal), swans (trumpeter & mute swans), snow geese and short‐billed gulls were also associated with increased odds of independent introductions of H5N1 on poultry farms (Figure 5). Particularly strong associations were found among case periods with increased occurrence of snow geese (OR, 1.78; 95% CI, 1.46–2.18) and teal (OR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.43–2.10) in case periods compared to control periods. No significant association was identified with the occurrence of Canada geese (OR, 1.052; 95% CI, 0.998–1.110).

Discussion

Role of Environmental Factors in HPAI Independent Introduction Events

British Columbia poultry farms were more likely to test positive for H5N1 during periods with elevated precipitation levels and lower maximum relative humidity, relative to matched control periods, based on multivariable analyses. While lower mean temperature and increasing wind gusting speeds demonstrated significant associations when assessed univariably.

Acute weather events as well as changes in transient environmental factors may influence disease transmission opportunities by altering pathogen viability and persistence in the environment. Surface water has been found to enable AIV spread and can act as a potential medium for amplification of the pathogen among aquatic birds (Ahrens et al. 2022). In a 2021 study from Australia, high rainfall events were similarly associated with increased risk of avian influenza outbreaks on poultry farms (Ferenczi et al. 2021). Our study determined that independent introduction events of H5N1 were over seven times more likely to occur following weeks with elevated rainfall (over 10 mm), compared to weeks with low precipitation (between 0 and 10 mm). This is thought to be due to sufficient rainfall to produce standing water, attracting waterfowl presence and amplifying AIV transmissibility in close proximity to farms. Due to the potential intervening relationship of waterfowl presence between precipitation and other meteoroligcal variables with H5N1 independent introduction events, wild bird species occurrence was assessed separately.

In addition to precipitation, this research highlighted the significant role of humidity in influencing H5N1 introduction events. Laboratory studies indicate that reduced humidity may increase viral transmission of AIV (Božič and Kanduč 2021; Lowen et al. 2007; Wood et al. 2010), while epidemiological findings from outbreaks of HPAI in Asia have shown variability in the influence of humidity (An et al. 2021; Fang et al. 2005). Our study contributes to this body of evidence, revealing that even when controlling for precipitation and temperature, case‐periods with higher maximum relative humidity resulted in a significant reduction in the odds of an independent introduction event occurring.

While historical literature has found that lower temperatures prolong the survival of AIV pathogens across both aqueous and dry external environments (Domanska‐Blicharz et al. 2010; Kurmi et al. 2013; Martin et al. 2018), our multivariable analysis suggests temperature may be confounded by additional weather processes. In our univariable analyses, increasing mean temperatures appeared protective; however, this association was weakened and lost statistical significance upon inclusion into the multivariable model. This research indicates that the risk previously attributed to specific temperature drops in British Columbia is more accurately explained by elevated precipitation and relative humidity (Wang and Sun 2022).

Finally, the role of wind‐mediated spread of avian influenza has been theorised as highly plausible, but results from field studies have been mixed. Field reports from previous avian influenza outbreaks on a broad range of farm sizes have identified the potential for airborne spread of contaminated dust or particulates at relatively low virus levels and only over short distances (Jonges et al. 2015; Torremorell et al. 2016). Other studies have reported the potential for wind‐mediated spread of AIV between poultry farms, estimating wind may account for 18% to 24% of short‐distance transmission (Ypma et al. 2013). However, the authors acknowledged that potential unmeasured mechanisms may have contributed to these transmission patterns. In our univariable analyses, we found that increasing maximum gusting speeds during case‐period weeks significantly increased the odds of H5N1 independent introduction events on poultry farms. However, due to the lack of complete wind speed data across local weather stations, this variable was excluded from the multivariable model to preserve the study's sample size. While wind gusts remain a factor of interest, it is unclear whether the same proposed mechanisms generating wind‐mediated between‐farm spread of AIV's, such as airborne viral particulates, are involved in the observed independent introduction events in our study. This is particularly relevant considering the differences in animal density and viral shedding between wild bird populations and poultry farms.

Role of Wild Bird Occurrence in HPAI Dynamics

Environmental contamination with H5N1 via wild bird reservoir species appears to be crucial in predicting independent introduction events on poultry farms. Previous research exploring the influence of different bird species and their influence on HPAI transmission has indicated the role of different waterbird species as potential reservoirs for HPAIV's (Giacinti et al. 2024; Pohlmann et al. 2022; Webster et al. 1992). Species‐specific differences in the association between independent introduction events and local bird species group occurrence were identified between the various waterbird species groups examined in this study. Specifically, the occurrence of teals and snow geese showed the strongest associations with the occurrence of independent introductions of H5N1 onto poultry farms. Recent research on species‐specific prevalence of H5N1 during the 2022 HPAI outbreak in Canada has suggested that dabbling duck species, including teals, mallards and pintails may have a greater role in AIV transmission and maintenance compared to other waterbird species (Giacinti et al. 2024). Similar research has also been observed with dabbling ducks (including mallards, teals and pintails) carrying some of the highest prevalence estimates of LPAIV's worldwide and high diversity of AIV subtypes (Brown et al. 2006; Giacinti et al. 2024; Olsen et al. 2006). In addition to dabbling duck species, previous research has indicated that snow geese have high seroprevalence for AIV's (Samuel et al. 2015). These findings support our study's results, demonstrating significant increases in bird species groups with known prevalence of HPAIVs during case periods. While common waterfowl species, including Canada geese and mallards, are considered major reservoir hosts for HPAI, their near constant presence in many regions may contribute to a consistent transmission pressure. In addition to their widespread presence, individual species‐specific viral disease dynamics may help explain why there is no association and a weaker association between increased Canada goose and mallard occurrence and independent introduction events, respectively. In contrast, episodic spikes in the abundance of less commonly observed bird species groups may serve as stronger indicators of viral presence in the external environment, as their periodic abundance could signal acute periods of heightened risk.

Limitations

While this retrospective case‐crossover analysis provides valuable insight into the role of environmental factors and wild bird occurrence in independent introductions of H5N1 onto poultry farms, there are several limitations to acknowledge. First, the use of univariable regression models limits the ability to account for potential correlations and interactions between covariates. In addition, there exists potential inaccuracy in spatial and temporal resolution which may have resulted in some non‐differential misclassification of the weather variables. This is to be particularly emphasised for wind speed data, as wind is highly variable over longer geographic distances (Cradden et al. 2014). The use of citizen science databases like eBird provides inherent biases, including likely regional differences in reporting rates of bird species occurrence based on the activity of eBird users, in addition to potential misidentification of similar species (Robinson et al. 2022). Despite attempts to rectify potential biases by using complete checklists and aggregating commonly misidentified bird species, these inherent limitations are a trade‐off for the broad spatial and temporal coverage offered by citizen science data. A limitation of citizen‐science data is the inability to distinguish true biological absence from surveillance underreporting. Enforcing a 30‐location minimum threshold corrected for this bias but inherently excluded areas with true low species occurrence, though this conservative approach ultimately resulted in the exclusion of only two farm cases. Finally, the findings presented in this research are specific to the 2022 HPAI outbreak in British Columbia, and their generalisability to other regions and time periods may be limited.

Conclusion

Highly pathogenic avian influenza (H5N1) represents an economically devastating disease which is also a global public health threat due to prolonged ongoing outbreaks across North America, endemicity of the virus in wild birds, and the increasing potential for zoonotic spillover (United States Department of Agriculture 2024). A large portion of poultry farm outbreaks in North America have been identified as independent introductions, spillover events originating from the external environment and/or indirect contact with wild birds. Furthermore, given the persistent circulation of HPAI viruses in both wild and domestic hosts, in addition to zoonotic spillover of HPAI to a teenager in British Columbia, the risk for human spillover is ever present (Jassem et al. 2025; O'Keeffe and Goulding 2024). This research demonstrates the interplay between environmental and ecological factors associated with H5N1 transmission dynamics at the wildlife, environment, poultry interface. By examining the association between key weather variables and particular wild bird species groups with independent introduction events of H5N1 on poultry farms in British Columbia, Canada, this study presents a window of time associated with likely spillover of HPAI to poultry farms. This research identifies key weather variables of interest including elevated precipitation levels, higher wind‐gust speeds, lower temperature and maximum relative humidity, and increased species occurrence of dabbling ducks, including teals, mallards and pintails, and snow geese in potentially predicting independent introduction events of HPAI. Future studies should investigate the specific mechanisms by which these weather conditions and the presence of waterbird species contribute to increased HPAI spillover risk. These conditions may collectively contribute to enhanced environmental persistence of the virus, increased spread and elevated contact rates between wild birds and poultry operations.
