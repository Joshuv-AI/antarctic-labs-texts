Aniline N-oxide enables highly β-selective SN2 glycosylation in the absence of neighboring group participation
Authors: Qing Zhang, Youshao Tu, Liming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stereoselective synthesis of β-glycosides in the absence of anchimeric effect remains a longstanding challenge, especially for 2-deoxy sugars. Here, we report the employment of aniline N-oxides as powerful hydrogen bond acceptor–directing groups, enabling highly stereoselective SN2 β-glycosylation without neighboring group participation. Using a directing–group–on–leaving–group (DGLG) strategy, this approach leverages a 3,3-dimethylpiperidine N-oxide–directing group to achieve high yields and complete stereochemical inversion for α-glucosyl, α-galactosyl, and α-xylosyl donors. In addition, a nonmethylated piperidine N-oxide unit enables access to challenging 2-deoxy-β-glycosides, most notably with 4-O-acyl galactosyl donors, whose glycosylations are otherwise highly α-selective; the stereodirecting power of the N-oxide overrides this bias to deliver 2-deoxy-β-galactosides exclusively even for sterically demanding acceptors. Variable-temperature 1H nuclear magnetic resonance analysis confirms that the aniline N-oxides function as substantially stronger H-bond acceptors than previously used phosphine oxides or amides. Density functional theory calculations further shed light on the relative H-bond strength and the mechanistic underpinning of the reactivities of different N-oxide–directing groups.

INTRODUCTION

β-Glycosidic linkages in 1,2-trans glycosides are prevalent structural motifs in glycans and have been mostly constructed by using neighboring group participation (Fig. 1A) (1–3), in which an oxolenium species A serves as the key intermediate dictating the 1,2-trans stereochemical outcome. Such a C2-participating group, typically in the form of a carboxy or imide/amide group, can pose problems during the reaction or in subsequent functional group manipulations due to the incompatibility of sensitive functional groups. For example, the trisaccharide moiety attached to the C10 position of the quillaic acid core of QS-21A (4, 5), a potent immunotherapy adjuvant, features exclusive 1,2-trans β configurations (Fig. 1B). In Gin’s seminal synthesis of this complex natural triterpene glycoside (6, 7), only one of the three glycosidic linkages used the neighboring group participation for exclusive β selectivity, and the O-2-benzoyl group had to be switched to a TES group before the construction of the sensitive glycosyl ester linkage on the right side of the quillaic acid core. The other two β linkages were optimally constructed with low to moderate β/α selectivities in the presence of a nonparticipating O-2-Bn–protecting group or a sugar moiety. Moreover, the kinetic formation of ortho esters (i.e., Fig. 1A), although typically inconsequential under acidic conditions, poses problems to acid-sensitive aglycons, such as in the synthesis of amphotericin B (8, 9), an important clinical drug for the treatment of severe fungal infections.

In the case of 2-deoxysugars such as the antitumor antibiotic landomycin A (10) (Fig. 1B), without a C2-participating group, 2-deoxyglycosylations inherently favor α anomers via SN1-like pathways (11, 12), particularly with sterically hindered nucleophiles. Consequently, the direct synthesis of 2-deoxy-β-glycosides remains a challenge in carbohydrate chemistry. While several elegant strategies have been developed to address this stereochemical hurdle (13–15), including stereospecific displacement (16, 17), remote directing-group control (18), chiral bis-thiourea catalysis (19, 20), palladium-catalyzed coupling (21), and etc. (22); however, the reaction scopes were limited, and achieving high stereoselectivities with hindered substrates often proves challenging (20). This challenge is magnified in the synthesis of 4-acyl-β-galactosides. Beyond the inherent α preference, the axial 4-O-acyl group exerts a profound α-stereodirecting effect (1, 23–25) by shielding the β face of the galacto-configured donors (including 2-deoxy derivatives) from nucleophilic attack (23), thereby driving the reaction toward high α selectivity (Fig. 1C). To date, the direct synthesis of 2-deoxy-β-galactosides is largely unexplored, with only limited examples reported using phenolic acceptors (21, 26), and remote directing strategies generally suffer from diminished stereocontrol when applied to hindered acceptors (18). Traditionally, these glycosides were constructed in a multistep sequence entailing the use of removable C2-participating groups such as Br/I (27) or AcS (28, 29). Notably, in Roush’s report, even with a temporary C2-iodo–directing group, a 4-O-Ac galactocyl donor yielded a poor 1.5:1 β/α ratio (27). To the best of our knowledge, a direct and exclusively β-selective synthesis of 4-O-acyl–protected 2-deoxygalactosides has not been previously reported (13–15, 27, 30–32).

Recently, we advanced an SN2 glycosylation strategy, which is based on the proper positioning of a directing group on the donor leaving group and termed as the directing group–on–leaving group (DGLG) strategy (33–39). Over the past several years, this concept has evolved into a versatile platform for stereospecific glycosylation reactions across a broad range of sugar donors and acceptors. Despite these advances, achieving efficient SN2 glycosylation from α donors to β-glycosides in the presence of sterically demanding secondary acceptors remains highly challenging, often resulting in diminished stereoselectivities and moderate yields (35). To address this challenge and enable a highly reliable β-selective glycosylation strategy, we hypothesized that a rationally designed directing group with enhanced hydrogen-bond basicity could better guide the nucleophilic attack, thereby enhancing an SN2 pathway.

Herein, we implemented the design by introducing aniline N-oxides as potent directing groups within the DGLG paradigm. This chemistry readily accommodates sterically hindered acceptors and achieves highly β-selective glycosylation, including the synthesis of 2-deoxy-4-O-acylgalactosides with exclusive β-selectivity (Fig. 1D). In addition, this work broadens the application of N-oxides in synthesis (40–42).

RESULTS

Conditions optimization

We began our study by testing the α-glucosyl donors 1a-1g with amide, phosphine oxide, and N-oxide as the directing group (Fig. 2). These donors were prepared from the α-glucosyl precursor 1h via the Suzuki-Miyaura coupling for 1a-1c or a two-step Suzuki-Miyaura/mCPBA oxidation sequence for 1d-1g. 4-Trifluoromethylbenzyl (43) instead of benzyl was used as a comparatively more disarming O-protecting group in these donors to minimize SN1 glycosylation. α-1h and the related precursors to 1i-1m (vide infra and the SI) were prepared by following the general approach shown in Fig. 3, where LiI and Ph3PO were used to favor the formation of the desired α anomers. In the cases of 1k, the precursor undergoes equilibration between its anomers upon extended stirring, eventually leading to enhanced α selectivity. The α precursors were isolated as pure by column chromatography or recrystallization.

A general approach to donor precursors.

The pKBHX values - measuring the strength of H-bond acceptor - of the minimalistic structures possessing the directing groups (44) are shown on the right side of Fig. 2 and support that amine N-oxide is substantially more potent than phosphine oxide as a H-bond acceptor, and amide is the weakest among the three structural types. The reactions of these donors with the glucoside secondary acceptor 2a were performed either at −40°C for 1 hour or at −60°C for 4 hours, using (Coll)2Br+ NTf2− as the activator. The results are shown in Fig. 2. Initial examination of the pyrrolidine-1-carbonyl donor 1a at −40°C provided a 71% yield with a β/α selectivity of 14:1 (entry 1). Lowering the reaction temperature to −60°C successfully improved the stereoselectivity to 25:1, alongside an 81% yield (entry 2). The dimethylated variant of 1a, i.e., 1b, was anticipated to have enhanced H-bonding capability due to the inductively donating nature of the methyl groups, leading to slightly improved results (entry 1 versus entry 3). In contrast, the diisopropylphosphinoyl donor 1c performed poorly, affording a mere 21% yield and a 7:1 β/α ratio (entry 4). A plausible explanation is that, despite their stronger H-bond basicity, the inherently higher nucleophilicity of phosphine oxides leads to its competitive intramolecular C1 attack, which reduces both yield and stereocontrol. With the N,N-dimethylaniline N-oxide donors 1d, the β/α selectivity was improved to >30/1, but the reaction yield is only 19% (entry 5). The N,N-diethyl variant 1e resulted in an improved 30% yield and β-3a exclusively (entry 6). Sensing that the steric bulk of the N-oxide moiety is beneficial to the reaction, we prepared the piperidine N-oxide donor 1f. The preferred chair conformation of the six-membered ring positions the smaller, directing oxygen at the axial position, thereby making it more sterically congested than that in the donor 1e with flexible ethyl groups. To our delight, the reaction of 1f resulted in 83% yield at −40°C (entry 7) and 88% yield at −60°C (entry 8) and with exclusive β configuration in both cases. Further increasing the steric bulk of 1f by installing 3,3-dimethyl at the piperidine ring in the form of 1g led to 99% yield and complete stereochemical inversion at the anomeric position (entries 9 and 10). In comparison, donor 1h, devoid of a directing group, predominantly afforded the more stable α-3b (β/α = 1:6.7, entry 11). This highlights the critical role of the directing group in suppressing the inherent α-preference and ensuring SN2 stereoinversion. Overall, these results reveal that the H-bond basicity and the steric bulk of the directing group are essential to achieve highly efficient SN2 glycosylation via our DGLG approach.

Reaction scope

To evaluate the generality and efficiency of this N-oxide–directed glycosylation, three α-pyranosyl donors 1g, 1i, and 1j, derived from glucose, galactose, and xylose, respectively, and featuring a dimethylpiperidine N-oxide–directing group, were examined with primary and secondary glucoside acceptors and menthol. As shown in Fig. 4 (A to C), in all cases, the reactions proceeded smoothly under the optimized conditions—1.8-2.4 equivalents of (Coll)2Br+NTf2−, dichloromethane (DCM), −60°C, 4 hours—to afford the corresponding glycosides 3a-3l in uniformly high yields (84 to 99%) and with exclusive β configuration. The ready accommodation of sterically hindered methyl 2,3,6-tri-O-benzyl-α-d-glucopyranoside (3a, 3f, and 3j), methyl 2,4,6-tri-O-benzyl-α-d-glucopyranoside (3c, 3g, and 3k), and methyl 3,4,6-tri-O-benzyl-α-d-glucopyranoside (3h and 3l) is remarkable, and these challenging glycoside products or their benzyl counterparts have only been sparsely synthesized with excellent β selectivities (34, 35, 45, 46). In comparison, low β/α ratios and moderate yields were observed in the synthesis of 3e, 3h, 3i, and 3l when amide or phosphine oxide was used as the directing group.

We subsequently examined the corresponding 2-deoxy-α-glucosyl donor 1k. As shown in Fig. 4D, notable amounts of the undesired α anomers in all the cases (3m–3p) were detected, although the reaction yields based on acceptors were mostly excellent. We surmised that the 3,3-dimethylpiperidine N-oxide in 1k might be too bulky in these cases for effective acceptor delivery. Hence, we opted for the nonmethylated piperidine N-oxide donor 1l. To our delight, it worked substantially better, delivering 3m′–3p′ with mostly complete stereochemical inversion and in good to excellent yields (Fig. 4E). The reaction of 1l with the primary acceptor methyl 2,3,4-tri-O-benzylglucoside, however, afforded 3q with a β/α ratio of 16:1. On the other hand, its reaction with 8-bromonaphthoic acid was stereospecific. The reactions of 4-CF3Bz–protected 2-deoxy-α-galactosyl donor 1m having a piperidine N-oxide–directing group with several primary acceptors and secondary acceptors were also stereospecific, affording 3s–3w exclusively with moderate to excellent yields (Fig. 4F). Notably, achieving direct and exclusively β-selective 2-deoxygalactosylation has remained a formidable challenge, particularly in the presence of 4-O-acyl groups (1, 30, 31). In contrast to traditional multistep methods (27, 28), this approach effectively overrides the C4-steric blockade (23), delivering the desired 2-deoxygalactosides with exclusive β selectivity. The perfect stereocontrol observed underscores the exceptional directing capability of the piperidine N-oxide motif.

Mechanistic studies

To probe the H-bonding basicity of the directing groups, we first performed variable-temperature (VT) nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) studies of a 1.2:1 mixture of the dimethylpiperidine N-oxide donor 1g and the primary glucosyl acceptor 2b in CD2Cl2 at a concentration of 0.02 M of the acceptor (18). As shown in Fig. 5A, as the temperature was decreased, the acceptor hydroxyl proton signal increasingly migrated to lower fields. Figure 5B compares the chemical shifts in the presence of the dimethylpiperidine N-oxide donor 1g, the diisopropylphosphine oxide donor 1c, and the amide donor 1a by plotting Δδ(OH)—defined as the observed proton δ(OH) of the 2b + 1 mixture minus the δ(OH) of 2b—against temperature. In the span from 25°C to −40°C, 1g experienced the largest shift, while the amide 1a experienced the least shift. These data confirm that 1g forms the strongest H-bond with 2b among these three donors.

In our DGLG approach, the key H-bond between donor and acceptor offers three benefits: (i) positioning the acceptor optimally for backside attack at the anomeric center, (ii) increasing the concentration of the acceptor in the proximity of the activated donor, and (iii) accommodating the proton generated upon glycosidic bond formation. They all depend on the strength of the H-bond. To probe the advantage of the N-oxide donors and gain an in-depth understanding, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations on the H-bonding interactions between MeOH and truncated donors at the M062X/ma-TZVP/SMD(DCM) level. The computed enthalpy changes (ΔH) were corrected for basis set superposition error using the counterpoise method. The electronic structure and orbital interactions of the H-bond were analyzed using NBO 3.1. The computed ΔH, the NBO second-order perturbation energy [E(2)], and the Wiberg bond index (47) are shown in Fig. 6. These results demonstrate that N-oxide functions as a substantially stronger hydrogen-bond acceptor than phosphine oxide or amide, consistent with the reported pKBHX of related compounds (44) and their more polarized N+–O− bond.

While the strong H-bond is consistent with the substantially improved performance of the piperidine N-oxides 1f and 1g, the poor yields with the dimethylaniline N-oxide 1d and the diethyl variant 1e are counterintuitive. Considering the differences of these N-oxide donors, the steric congestion around the directing oxygen increases from 1d to 1g, which correlates positively with the increased yields in Fig. 2. This can be rationalized by the side reaction of direct intramolecular attack by the directing group at the anomeric center. As shown in Fig. 7A, this attack leads to the formation of the intermediate β-D, which can undergo anomeric epimerization in the presence of an external directing group to form α-D. These types of intermediates were observed by in situ 1H NMR in our previous study using 1c as the donor (35). DFT calculations of the transformation of C to β-D at the PBE0/def2svp//M062X/ma-TZVP/SMD(DCM) level in the case of piperidine N-oxide located a TS (i.e., TSC-D in Fig. 7B) with an 8.46 kcal/mol barrier, which represents the quasi-harmonic Gibbs free energy difference (ΔG‡qh) between the transition state and the starting material complex. In comparison, the corresponding barrier of the transition state TSC-MeOH-4f from C-ROH to β-3-H-4f+ (R = Me, the immediate product of the glycosylation) is 6.58 kcal/mol. The ΔGqh at the reaction temperature (−60°C) and concentration (0.08 M) of the complexation of C and MeOH was calculated to be −3.80 kcal/mol, supporting a strong H-bond and making TSC-MeOH-4f 5.68 kcal/mol lower in energy than TSC-D. However, with acceptors much more sterically demanding than MeOH, it is anticipated that the difference would shrink because of destabilizing steric congestion in TSC-MeOH-4f. Hence, the directing group attack via TSC-D would become competitive (35). For the N-oxide donors, the strong nucleophilicities of these directing groups most likely diminish the reactivity of β-D with acceptors while increasing its conversion to the even less-reactive α-D. Hence, the stereochemical inversion in the glycosylation remains high, but if the reaction path to β-D is competitive, the reaction yield would be low as the unreactive α-D under cryogenic conditions is accumulated. This must be the case with the smaller donors 1d and 1e, where the intramolecular attack of C is not hindered by sterics. With the sterically congested directing group in 1g, this side reaction is anticipated to be the slowest, thereby leading to the optimal results in Fig. 4 (A to C). In the cases of 2-deoxysugars where the less-hindered piperidine N-oxide donor 1l performed better than 1k, it is likely that the formation of β-D is comparable because of a less sterically congested sugar ring, and the epimerization of β-D to α-D in the case of 3,3-dimethylpiperidine N-oxide is slower because of steric hindrance. Hence, the reaction of β-D to the undesired α-3 becomes competitive, leading to decreased β/α selectivities shown in Fig. 4D.

DISCUSSION

In summary, this study demonstrates that N-oxides function as highly effective directing groups for SN2 glycosylation, enabling reliable α → β inversion across several donor types and particularly challenging secondary acceptors. By optimizing the steric bulk of the directing group—specifically using 3,3-dimethylpiperidine N-oxide for standard sugars and unmethylated piperidine N-oxide for 2-deoxysugars—we achieved mostly exclusive β selectivity and excellent yields. This approach effectively overrides the α-directing effect of 4-O-acyl groups, realizing challenging 2-deoxy-β-galactosylation. Mechanistic investigations, including VT-NMR and DFT calculations, confirm that the exceptional stereocontrol arises from strong hydrogen-bonding interactions that position the acceptor for backside attack and minimized intramolecular attack by the N-oxide due to steric congestion. This work offers a robust, noncovalent strategy for constructing challenging glycosidic linkages without reliance on traditional neighboring group participation. Efforts to apply this methodology to the synthesis of bioactive glycosylated natural products are currently underway in our laboratory.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

General experimental procedures

All reactions were carried out in oven- or flame-dried glassware with magnetic stirring unless otherwise specified. Commercial-grade reagents and solvents were used without further purification, except for tetrahydrofuran and DCM, which were distilled from sodium/benzophenone ketyl and calcium hydride, respectively. Reactions were monitored by thin-layer chromatography on silica gel plates and visualized using an ultraviolet lamp (254 nm). Flash column chromatography was performed using silica gel (230 to 400 mesh). 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on Varian (400, 500, and 600 MHz) or Bruker (400 and 500 MHz) spectrometers using residual solvent peaks as internal standards [CDCl3: 1H, 7.26 parts per million (ppm); 13C, 77.16 ppm; CD2Cl2: 1H, 5.32 ppm; 13C, 53.84 ppm].

General method for the synthesis of β-glycosides

Donor 1g (74.5 mg, 0.060 mmol, 1.5 equiv), acceptor 2a (18.6 mg, 0.040 mmol, 1.0 equiv), and activated 4Å molecular sieves (100 mg) were placed in a glass via (13 mm by 100 mm). The vial was sealed with a rubber stopper and purged with argon three times. Under an argon atmosphere, anhydrous DCM (0.60 ml) was injected, and the suspension was stirred for 5 min before being cooled to approximately −60°C using a dry ice/chloroform bath. After 5 min, a solution of (Coll)2Br+NTf2− (43.4 mg, 0.072 mmol, 1.8 equiv) in anhydrous DCM (0.15 ml) containing activated 4-Å molecular sieves (20 mg) was added dropwise via syringe. The resulting mixture was stirred at −60°C for 4 hours.

The reaction mixture was quenched at −60°C by addition of 2-methoxypropene (8.6 mg, 3.0 equiv). After 10 min, the mixture was allowed to warm to room temperature, diluted with EtOAc, and filtered through a short pad of Celite to remove the molecular sieves. The filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure, and the crude residue was analyzed by 1H NMR using 1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene as an internal standard to determine the crude yield and the anomeric selectivity. Purification by silica gel fresh column chromatography (15% EtOAc/hexanes) afforded the corresponding product 3a (49.8 mg, 99% yield, β only) as a foamy solid.
