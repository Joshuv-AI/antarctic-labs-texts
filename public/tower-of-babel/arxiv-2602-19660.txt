The Welfare Gap of Strategic Storage: Universal Bounds and Price Non-Linearity
Zhile Jiang, Xinhao Nie, Stratis Skoulakis

Abstract
This paper studies the efficiency of battery storage operations in electricity markets by comparing the social welfare gain achieved by a central planner to that of a decentralized profit-maximizing operator. The problem is formulated in a generalized continuous-time stochastic setting, where the battery follows an adaptive, non-anticipating policy subject to periodicity and other constraints. We quantify the efficiency loss by bounding the ratio of the optimal welfare gain to the gain under profit maximization. First, for linear price functions, we prove that this ratio is tightly bounded by 4 / 3 4/3 . We show that this bound is a structural invariant: it is robust to arbitrary stochastic demand processes and accommodates general convex operational constraints. Second, we demonstrate that the efficiency loss can be unbounded for general convex price functions, implying that convexity alone is insufficient to guarantee market efficiency. Finally, to bridge these regimes, we analyze monomial price functions, where the degree controls the curvature. For specific discrete demand scenarios, we demonstrate that the ratio is bounded by 2 2 , independent of the degree.



1
Introduction

The stability and predictability of the electricity market are not merely operational conveniences, they are fundamental pillars for modern societal function and long-term economic development
[
10
]
. Beyond the direct technical requirement of balancing physical power flows, market stability translates directly into reliable price signals. These signals act as the primary drivers for industrial investment and provide the general economic certainty necessary for sustainable growth.

However, this established stability currently faces unprecedented pressure from two converging forces that are fundamentally reshaping grid dynamics. On the supply side, the global need for a green energy transition has necessitated a rapid and massive integration of renewable sources. The inherent intermittency of wind and solar generation introduces systemic volatility, complicating the delicate balance of grid operations and making supply curves increasingly unpredictable
[
20
,
31
,
35
]
. Simultaneously, on the demand side, the digital economy creates new strains. Emerging sectors, particularly data centers powering large language models, impose exceptionally high, continuous processing loads. These facilities demand near-perfect reliability while introducing massive, often fluctuating power requirements that challenge traditional dispatch models and exacerbate price volatility
[
13
]
.

In response to these dual pressures, Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) have become a critical technological intervention. Unlike traditional generation, BESS possesses the special capability of temporal arbitrage, i.e., absorbing excess energy during periods of surplus and injecting it back into the grid during peak demand
[
18
,
38
]
. This flexibility is essential for maintaining equilibrium in a renewable-heavy grid. Yet, a fundamental tension exists: while BESS operators are typically profit-maximizing entities focused on exploiting price differentials, the grid operator’s goal is to produce the required energy demand at the smallest possible cost. Although extensive literature explores the operational optimization of individual BESS units (e.g., maximizing revenue through sophisticated, algorithmic charging schedules
[
21
,
32
,
34
,
36
,
38
]
), the impact of these strategic, self-interested agents on overall market efficiency remains underexplored.

Existing studies that investigate the inefficiencies arising from strategic storage, such as
[
4
,
9
,
27
,
37
]
, provide valuable insights but often suffer from significant methodological limitations. These models tend to be heavily context-dependent, relying on specific optimization frameworks or detailed empirical simulations tied to specific regional market rules and physical transmission constraints. While such a level of detail is useful for local case studies, it fails to yield analytically tractable conclusions that hold across different market designs. Consequently, the findings are difficult to generalize to future grid architectures or diverse regulatory environments. Policy and mechanism design require more than simulation-based evidence; they require robust theoretical guarantees that hold regardless of specific parameter settings. The absence of a universal, analytically solid framework for quantifying the efficiency loss caused by this objective misalignment represents a critical gap in the current literature.

A pivotal step toward addressing this limitation was taken by Anunrojwong et al.
[
4
]
, who introduced a new direction based on game theory and mechanism design. This work demonstrated that the strategic behavior of batteries and the resulting market distortions could be strictly characterized in closed form. This shift in perspective established that theoretical models could yield structural insights that purely numerical simulations cannot.

In this paper, we advance this analytical frontier by establishing a rigorous theoretical foundation for analyzing the inefficiencies inherent in markets populated by strategic BESS. We propose a highly general framework that intentionally abstracts away from the details of specific market rules and spatial transmission constraints. This abstraction allows us to focus mainly on the implications of temporal coupling, specifically, the interaction between stochastic demand and inter-temporal storage constraints. Under the assumption of linear price functions, we derive a universal upper bound on efficiency loss, providing a guarantee that remains robust regardless of demand patterns. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the assumption of linearity acts as a critical boundary for stability. We identify cases where profit-maximizing strategies under general convex pricing fail to reduce social costs completely. Finally, we examine the intermediate regime of monomial price functions. For this non-linear analysis, we focus on canonical instances with deterministic piecewise-constant demand. This allows us to derive strict bounds and demonstrate how the mathematical properties of non-linearity directly correlate with the difficulty of maintaining market efficiency.

1.1
Our Contribution

We advance the theoretical understanding of electricity market efficiency through the following contributions:

A Universal Guarantee for Linear Markets

We develop a theoretical framework designed to go beyond the limitations of specific market simulations. By abstracting away spatial network constraints, we are able to fully capture the implications of the temporal dimension, modeling the market with arbitrary stochastic demand distributions and a broad class of convex structural constraints. Within this general setting, we provide a complete characterization of market performance for linear price functions, establishing a tight Price of Anarchy (PoA) bound, i.e., the ratio between the cost improvement achieved by a centrally
controlled versus profit-maximizing battery, of
4/3
(Theorem
1
). This result resolves an open question posed in
[
4
]
, which derived this bound only for a restricted case with piecewise constant demand and conjectured its validity for more general settings. A key aspect of this contribution is the development of a simplified, high-level proof. This proof strategy reveals that the
4/3
efficiency loss is a structural invariant, i.e., it is intrinsic to the linear objective misalignment and remains totally unaffected by the external environment. Importantly, this means the bound holds independently of operational structure; neither the stochastic nature of demand nor the presence of strictly binding physical constraints worsens the inefficiency beyond this constant factor. This insight offers a robust certification of market performance that specific simulations cannot provide.

The Boundary of Stability and the Risks of Convexity.
To determine the precise limits of market stability, we investigate the transition from linear to general convex price functions. We prove that linearity is not just a convenient modeling assumption but a necessary condition for bounded inefficiency in the general case. We demonstrate that for broader classes of convex price functions, the Price of Anarchy can become unbounded, even in simple settings with deterministic demand and no additional constraints (Theorem
2
). And our construction can also be generalized to convex polynomial price functions (Corollary
8
). This finding serves as a theoretical warning to policymakers: it suggests that without specific regulatory interventions or structural properties, the interaction between strategic storage and high price sensitivity can theoretically lead to arbitrarily high social costs. This justifies the necessity of our granular analysis of specific function classes and highlights the potential risks of relying on purely profit-driven storage in markets with steep supply curves.

Characterizing the Impact of Non-linearity.
We bridge the gap between the well-behaved linear regime and the unbounded convex regime by analyzing monomial price functions in the simplest environment where the demand and battery operation are modeled as deterministic piecewise constant functions. (This model is similar to that in
[
4
]
.) This analysis highlights the intrinsic mathematical difficulty introduced by non-linearity. We show that as the pricing function shifts from linear to polynomial, the efficiency analysis becomes deeply reliant on the properties of the underlying polynomials. By deriving upper and lower bounds for monomials where the degree directly controls the price curvature (Theorem
3
and
5
) and providing a tight characterization for the quadratic case (Theorem
4
), we show a direct correspondence between the degree of the monomial and the potential efficiency loss. This result effectively maps the “price of non-linearity”, showing how the mathematical degree of price sensitivity dictates the fundamental limits of what strategic behavior can achieve in terms of social welfare.

Model and Notations

Stochastic Demand.
We model the electricity market as a continuous system over a normalized time interval
T=[0,1]
. Let the inelastic demand
D=\{D(t)\}_{t\in[0,1]}
be a stochastic process defined on a probability space
(\Xi,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. Here,
\Xi
represents the sample space of all possible demand trajectories,
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
represents the filtration or information flow available over time, and
\mathbb{P}
denotes the probability measure. To facilitate the analysis without loss of generality, we normalize the system scale such that the demand is bounded and falls within the unit interval
[0,1]
almost surely.

While we strive for model parsimony, the intrinsic uncertainty and complex temporal correlations in power demand necessitate this generalized stochastic formulation. This framework is essential to rigorously capture the continuity of load profiles, while remaining sufficiently general to encompass deterministic scenarios as specific realizations.
To make this concrete, we provide Example
1
showing a possible stochastic process controlling the demand curve and Fig
1
illustrating the behavior of this process over a single cycle.

Example 1
.

Consider the demand
D
defined by the following stochastic process:

\displaystyle D(t)=m(t)+\sigma(t)\cdot Z(t),

where the deterministic component
m(t)=0.7+0.2\sin(2\pi t)
characterizes the baseline diurnal trend of the load. To capture the temporal correlation inherent in the power system, arising from physical inertia and gradual load changes, the noise term
Z(t)
is modeled as a non-Markovian process derived from a Gaussian Process with a squared-exponential kernel. This specification ensures that the demand trajectories exhibit realistic smoothness (differentiability) rather than erratic, discontinuous jumps. The term
\sigma(t)
represents time-varying volatility, strictly constrained to ensure the realized demand remains within physical bounds.

:

. The gray dashed line depicts the deterministic mean trend. The solid blue curves show two realizations of the stochastic process, while the gray shaded region defines the fluctuation envelope.

Battery Operation.
The operation of a battery is defined by its charging/discharging rate schedule
B=\{B(t)\}_{t\in[0,1]}
. Crucially,
B
is a
non-anticipating
(adaptive) decision policy. This imposes a strict causality constraint: at any specific time
t
, the decision
B(t)
is determined solely based on the realization of the demand history
D_{[0,t]}
(i.e., the information contained in
\mathcal{F}_{t}
) and the known distributional properties of the future, without access to future realizations.Formally, we express this dependency using sample path notation. Let
\xi\in\Xi
denote a specific realization of the world, i.e., a single sample path of the demand trajectory. The value of demand at time
t
in this scenario is denoted by
D(t,\xi)
. Consequently, the battery decision is not a fixed function of time, but a state-contingent functional
B(t,\xi)
. The non-anticipating requirement implies that for any two scenarios that share the same history, the battery action must be identical:

\displaystyle B(t,\xi)=B(t,\xi^{\prime}),\quad\forall\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi\text{ such that }D(s,\xi)=D(s,\xi^{\prime})\text{ for almost all }s\in[0,t].

(1)

Note that the set of policies satisfying this condition forms a convex set. For the remainder of the paper, we suppress the explicit dependence on
\xi
for notational brevity, unless necessary for clarity.

A positive value
B(t)>0
denotes discharging (injecting power), while
B(t)<0
denotes charging (withdrawing power). We assume, without loss of generality, that the battery capacity is scaled relative to demand such that the following physical constraints hold almost surely for any realization:

\displaystyle\Pr_{\xi\sim\Xi}\left[0\leq D(t,\xi)-B(t,\xi)\leq 1,\forall t\in[0,1]\right]=1.

(2)

This guarantees that the net demand, defined as
N(t)=D(t)-B(t)
, satisfies
0\leq N(t)\leq 1
.
Another fundamental operational constraint is the conservation of energy, specifically that the battery’s state-of-charge must restore to its initial level by the end of the cycle:

\displaystyle\Pr_{\xi\sim\Xi}\left[\int_{0}^{1}B(t,\xi)\mathrm{d}t=0\right]=1.

(3)

This periodicity constraint ensures the market operates in a steady state, preventing the battery from being a net generator or consumer over the cycle. We denote the set of all policies
B
satisfying Constraints (
1
), (
2
), and (
3
) as
\mathcal{B}
.

The Simplest Environment.

Among all possible instances, the simplest ones are with deterministic, 2-piece-wise constant demands. These instances characterize a discrete scenario, similar to the model in the work by
[
4
]
. In this case, we further assume that the decisions are also discrete and without any structural constraints introduced below and define the following family of instances. This simplified model serves as the basis for our analysis of non-linear price functions.

Definition 1
.

Define the family of instances
\mathcal{I}_{\text{step}}
as the set of all market configurations
I
satisfying the following properties:

-

The deterministic demand
D
is a 2-piece-wise constant function, (in other words, step function), i.e.,

\displaystyle D(t)=\begin{cases}D_{1},&t\in[0,t_{1}]\\
D_{2},&t\in(t_{1},1]\end{cases}.

-

The battery operation policy
B
is also a 2-piece-wise constant function following the demand, i.e.,

\displaystyle B(t)=\begin{cases}B_{1},&t\in[0,t_{1}]\\
B_{2},&t\in(t_{1},1]\end{cases}.

Structural Constraints.
Beyond the fundamental limitations, the operation may be subject to additional technological limitations, denoted by the feasible set
\Omega
.
We require
\mathcal{B}\cap\Omega
to be a convex set containing the zero function (implying that the ”do-nothing” policy is always admissible). Consistent with the stochastic setting, any constraint in
\Omega
is assumed to hold almost surely. We provide some possible constraints as examples:

•

The power constraint: The power (charging/discharge rate) is bounded by
\gamma
, i.e.,

\displaystyle|B(t)|\leq\gamma\quad\forall t\in[0,1].

•

The energy capacity constraint: The total energy stored or released between any two time points cannot exceed the physical capacity
c>0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}B(t)dt\right|\leq c\quad\forall t_{1},t_{2}\in[0,1].

Notice that the above inequalities are equivalent to the existence of an initial charge that ensures the state-of-charge of the battery is between
[0,c]
.

•

The ramp rate constraint: The rate of change of the battery power is limited by
\delta
, i.e.,

\displaystyle|B^{\prime}(t)|\leq\delta\quad\forall t\in[0,1].

These constraints are convex and representative of real-world storage constraints discussed in the literature. And there might be more other constraints.

Energy Pricing and Social Cost.
The price of electricity at time
t
is determined by an increasing price function
P:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, mapping the net demand
N(t)
to a market-clearing price. The social generation cost is
G(N)=\int_{0}^{N}P(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
. The price formulation assumes a Pay-as-Clear (or Uniform Pricing) mechanism which is widely used in practice. Under this mechanism, all dispatched generators are compensated at the market-clearing price established by the marginal unit. In a theoretically idealized market populated by a continuum of infinitesimal generators, strategic behavior is eliminated, and the unique equilibrium involves all generators bidding their true marginal costs. Consequently, the price function
P(N)
rigorously represents the system’s marginal cost curve at net demand level
N
, so the social generation can be represented as the integral over marginal cost.

Let
I=(D,P,\Omega)
denote a specific market instance. Given the stochastic nature of
D
, we focus on the expected performance. We define the
expected improvement on social cost
achieved by a battery policy
B
as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{WEL}_{I}(B)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}G(D(t))-G(D(t)-B(t))\mathrm{d}t\right].

Efficiency loss.
We compare the outcomes of two distinct operational paradigms.

Centralized Battery

A system operator controls the battery to maximize the expected improvement in social cost:

\displaystyle\max_{B\in\mathcal{B}\cap\Omega}\mathrm{WEL}_{I}(B).

(4)

Decentralized Battery

A profit-maximizing entity operates the battery to maximize its expected arbitrage revenue:

\displaystyle\max_{B\in\mathcal{B}\cap\Omega}\mathrm{REV}_{I}(B)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}B(t)\cdot P(D(t)-B(t))\mathrm{d}t\right].

(5)

Remark 1
.

In the special case where the demand
D
is deterministic, the expectation operator
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
can be removed. The adaptive nature of
B
simplifies to a standard trajectory optimization problem where the entire demand curve
D_{[0,1]}
is known a priori. In this setting, the problem reduces to the classical deterministic formulation where constraints and objectives are evaluated on a single realized path.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{\text{CB}}(I)
and
\mathcal{B}_{\text{DCB}}(I)
be the set of the optimal solutions to Programming (
4
) and (
5
) for instance
I
, respectively. We define the Price of Anarchy (
PoA
), i.e., the ratio of cost improvement achieved by two kinds of battery, as:

\displaystyle PoA(I)\overset{\text{def}}{=}\begin{cases}\max\frac{\mathrm{WEL}_{I}(B_{\text{CB}})}{\mathrm{WEL}_{I}(B_{\text{DCB}})},&\text{if }\min\mathrm{WEL}_{I}(B_{\text{DCB}})>0\\
1,&\text{if }\min\mathrm{WEL}_{I}(B_{\text{DCB}})=0\text{ and }\max\mathrm{WEL}_{I}(B_{\text{CB}})=0\\
\infty,&\text{if }\min\mathrm{WEL}_{I}(B_{\text{DCB}})\leq 0\end{cases}.

The definition looks complicated for mathematical rigor. Recall that the zero function is always feasible; we know the optimal improvement on social cost is non-negative. The core part of the Price of Anarchy is the first case in the definition where both batteries improve the social cost. We take the maximum since there might be multiple optimal solutions to Programming (
5
) and we consider the one contribute least to improving social cost. In the second case, where even the centralized battery cannot improve the social cost, meaning the battery is not necessary, we define the Price of Anarchy as
1
. The last case covers the situation where the decentralized battery might harm the social cost.

For a class of instances
\mathcal{I}
, the Price of Anarchy is defined as
PoA(\mathcal{I})\overset{\text{def}}{=}\sup_{I\in\mathcal{I}}PoA(I)
. And we omit the dependence of
I
or
\mathcal{I}
unless necessary for clarity in the rest of the paper.

2
and Fig.
2
to clarify the concepts.

. The left panel compares the original demand with the net demand adjusted by Centralized (CB) and Decentralized (DCB) batteries. Example 2
.

Consider a deterministic demand
D(t)=1+\sin(2\pi t)
and a linear price function
P(z)=z/2
giving a social generation cost function
G(z)=z^{2}
. Assuming there is no structure constraints, the centralized battery can minimize the social generation cost by setting
B_{CB}(t)=\sin(2\pi t)
, which improves the social generation cost by

\displaystyle\mathrm{WEL}(B_{CB})=\int_{0}^{1}(1+\sin(2\pi t))^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{1}1^{2}\mathrm{d}t=1/2.

And the decentralized battery will set
B_{CB}(t)=\sin(2\pi t)/2
to maximize the revenue. The improvement of social cost is

\displaystyle\mathrm{WEL}(B_{DCB})=\int_{0}^{1}(1+\sin(2\pi t))^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{1}\left(1+\frac{\sin(2\pi t)}{2}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}t=3/8.

In this case,
PoA=\mathrm{WEL}(B_{CB})/\mathrm{WEL}(B_{DCB})=4/3
.

3
Overview of Results

Our analysis begins with the class of linear price functions, a standard assumption in the literature. For this broad class of instances, we establish a universal upper bound on the Price of Anarchy. Remarkably, this performance guarantee is robust. It holds for any demand distribution
D
(encompassing both deterministic and complex stochastic processes) and any time-invariant convex constraint set
\Omega
containing the zero function. Furthermore, the worst-case efficiency loss occurs when the additional operational constraints are non-binding (i.e.,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\Omega
), leaving only the fundamental constraints
\mathcal{B}
active.

Theorem 1
.

Define the family of instances
\mathcal{I}_{\text{lin}}
as the set of all market configurations
I=(D,P,\Omega)
such that the price function
P(z)=a\cdot z+b
where
a>0,b\geq 0
. We have
PoA(\mathcal{I}_{\text{lin}})=4/3
.

Theorem
1
substantially generalizes the specific findings in
[
4
]
. While prior work relied on fixed demand shapes and unconstrained operations, our result confirms that the
4/3
bound is a structural invariant of linear markets, unaffected by the probability space of the demand or the geometry of the feasible set. Our proof strategy adopts a high-level perspective that decouples the efficiency loss from the instance specifics. It reveals that the Price of Anarchy stems solely from the fundamental misalignment between the social cost objective and the revenue-maximizing objective inherent to linear pricing. Consequently, the stochasticity of demand and the presence of complex structural constraints do not exacerbate the efficiency loss beyond this constant factor.

Given the stability of linear markets, a critical theoretical question arises: Does this robustness extend to general convex price functions? We prove that the assumption of price functions is not merely a simplifying heuristic but a requisite boundary condition for bounded inefficiency. By restricting our attention to the simplest possible environment where there is just a deterministic, two-piecewise constant demand representing a basic peak/off-peak cycle (i.e., instances in
\mathcal{I}_{\text{step}}
), we demonstrate that convexity alone can lead to catastrophic efficiency losses. Theorem
2
serves as an impossibility result, showing that without specific restrictions on the price function, the Price of Anarchy is unbounded in the simplest environment.

Theorem 2
.

There exists at least one instance
I\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{step}}
,
with a convex price function such that
PoA(I)
is unbounded.

To bridge the gap between the well-behaved linear regime and the unbounded general convex regime, we investigate the structure of non-linearity through monomial price functions. The analysis here becomes significantly more intricate: the curvature of the price function introduces higher-order polynomial terms into the optimization objectives, making exact PoA analysis challenging even in deterministic, discrete scenarios. In this setting, we seek to quantify how the degree of the monomial influences the efficiency loss. We first demonstrate that for any monomial degree, the efficiency loss remains bounded by a constant factor of
2
.

While the upper bound of
2
holds generally, stricter bounds can be derived by fixing the polynomial degree. We specifically analyze the case
d=2
, deriving a tight upper bound that improves upon the general result. Furthermore, the worst-case construction identified in Theorem
4
provides a template for constructing lower-bound examples for monomials of arbitrary degree
d
, as detailed in Theorem
5
.

Theorem 3
.

Define the family of instances
\mathcal{I}_{\text{mono, step}}\subset\mathcal{I}_{\text{step}}
where all instance has a monomial price function
P(z)=\alpha z^{d}
where
\alpha>0
and without any extra structural constraints.
We have
PoA(\mathcal{I}_{\text{mono, step}})\leq 2
.

By restricting the monomial’s degree to
2
, we can have a better upper bound. And the worst case in Theorem
4
can be generalized as lower-bound examples for monomials of degree
d
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{2\text{, step}}
be the subset of
\mathcal{I}_{\text{mono,step}}
where the price function is monomial of degree
2
, we have
PoA(\mathcal{I}_{2\text{, step}})=27/19
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{d\text{, step}}
be the subset of
\mathcal{I}_{\text{mono,step}}
where the price function is monomial of degree
d
, we have

PoA(\mathcal{I}_{d\text{, step}})\geq 1/(1-(\frac{d}{d+1})^{d+1})
. When the degree
d
approaches to infinity,
PoA
is lower-bounded by
e/(e-1)
.

Observing the tightness in the linear case (
d=1
,
PoA=4/3
) and the quadratic case (
d=2
,
PoA=27/19
), and supported by extensive numerical verification, we conjecture that the lower bound expression derived in Theorem
5
represents the tight Price of Anarchy for any positive integer degree
d
. This suggests a direct, monotonic relationship between the algebraic degree of the price function and market inefficiency.

4
Linear Price Functions

This section is the formal proof of Theorem
1
. We first define some notation to improve the readability.

Definition 2
.

For any stochastic process
X
and
Y
on probability space
(\Xi,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, we define

\displaystyle\langle X,Y\rangle\overset{\text{def}}{=}\int_{\xi\in\Xi}\left[\int_{0}^{1}X(t,\xi)\cdot Y(t,\xi)\mathrm{d}t\right]\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}X(t)\cdot Y(t)\mathrm{d}t\right].

And we denote
\|X\|^{2}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\langle X,X\rangle
.

The operation
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is a generalized inner product since taking expectation and integration are both linear operation.

Proof of Theorem
1
.
We consider an instance
I=(D,P,\Omega)\in\mathcal{I}_{lin}
with
P(x)=a\cdot x+b
where
a>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{WEL}(B)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}a\cdot(D(t))^{2}+b\cdot(D(t))-(\frac{1}{2}a\cdot(D(t)-B(t))^{2}+b\cdot(D(t)-B(t)))\mathrm{d}t\right]

Lemma 6

(Optimality Conditions)

Let

Proof of Theorem

Now, consider a deterministic demand

is pretty small. By definition, we have the following generation cost function

It is straightforward to verify that, within the interval

Under the definition of

, Programming (

\max_{x\in[0,1]}\frac{1}{2}x(P(1-x)-P(x))

\displaystyle x(P(1-x)-P(x))

\displaystyle=x(\frac{-x+\delta}{\delta^{2}}+\frac{1}{\delta}-2)=x(-\frac{x}{\delta^{2}}+\frac{2}{\delta}-2)

In Theorem
3
, restricting convex functions to monomials yields a constant upper bound. One might naturally attempt to relax this restriction to seek a similar bound. In the example presented in this section, however, we provide a counterexample demonstrating that this is not possible for general convex functions. We further strengthen this counterexample to show that even if we relax the restriction to general convex polynomials, a constant upper bound on the PoA remains unattainable. The motivation for this construction stems from Bernstein polynomials in approximation theory
[
7
]
. Their favorable properties for approximating convex functions, i.e., uniform convergence and approaching the limit from above monotonically
[

]
, serve as the foundation for bounding the PoA.

Discussion and Future Research Directions

In this work, we have established a rigorous theoretical foundation for quantifying the efficiency loss of battery storage in stochastic electricity markets. Our analysis identifies a clear boundary regarding the boundedness of inefficiency: while linear price functions offer a robust performance guarantee with a tight Price of Anarchy of
4/3
, we demonstrate that convexity alone is insufficient to prevent unbounded welfare losses. This contrast suggests that the mathematical structure of the price function is the main determinant of market efficiency.

Our findings open several directions for future research. While the linear regime proves robust to stochasticity and convex operational constraints, generalizing this framework to the interaction of multiple competitive storage systems remains a significant challenge. Future work must also address the details of physical transmission, particularly transmission losses. Unlike the convex constraints modeled here, transmission losses introduce non-convexities and dependencies on grid topology. A promising approach to these multi-agent, network-constrained problems lies in the perspective of congestion games. However, a fundamental distinction must be maintained: unlike standard congestion games, where agents typically optimize a localized component of a global potential function, the inefficiency in this setting is driven by a clear objective misalignment, i.e., the inherent conflict between the operator’s profit maximization and the central planner’s social welfare minimization.

Furthermore, our impossibility result regarding general convex functions (and even general convex polynomial functions) serves as a theoretical warning for market design. The potential for unbounded efficiency loss suggests that in markets characterized by steep marginal cost curves, relying solely on price signals to coordinate storage may be risky. Specific regulatory interventions or price caps may be required to protect welfare. This operational need is becoming increasingly urgent; as emerging high-load sectors, such as AI data centers, introduce massive and fluctuating demands, the likelihood of the grid operating in these extreme, high-curvature regions of the cost curve rises significantly.

From a theoretical standpoint, the ”price of non-linearity” presents significant open challenges. While we linked efficiency loss to monomial degrees, establishing tight bounds for arbitrary degree
d
remains open. A promising generalization involves extending bounds from pure monomials to polynomials with positive coefficients. Although arbitrary polynomials may yield unbounded inefficiency, analyzing positive combinations could explain how algebraic structure affects market performance. Additionally, future work should improve model realism. Unlike our robust linear results, our non-linear bounds currently assume simplified discrete demand. Bridging this gap by analyzing convex pricing under generalized continuous-time stochastic settings would complete the characterization of market efficiency, providing guarantees that account for both price curvature and demand uncertainty.
