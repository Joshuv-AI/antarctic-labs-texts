Indigenous knowledge of traditional medicinal plants in Gondar City Administration, Amhara National Regional State, northwest Ethiopia
Authors: Tiruye Ayenew, Yirgalem Melkamu, Getinet Masresha, Daniel Tadesse, Worku Misganaw, Birtukan Asrie, Abiyu Enyew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEthiopian people possess deep knowledge of how to use plant resources and are dependent on plant values mainly for traditional medicine. However, most ethnobotanical studies are restricted to rural areas, leaving urban centers poorly documented, which implies the need for further study. Thus, this study was conducted in Gondar City Administration, aimed at investigating medicinal plants to fill the traditional knowledge documentation gap.MethodsThe study was conducted from February 2024 to January 2025 in 12 kebeles selected purposively based on vegetation cover, availability of knowledgeable practitioners and representation of both urban and rural settings. Data were collected using interviews, focus group discussions, guided field walks, and market surveys with 120 randomly selected general informants and 60 purposively selected key informants. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze the basic ethnobotanical data. An independent sample t-test and two-way ANOVA were used to analyze socio-demographic effects of informants on their indigenous knowledge. Different ethnobotanical ranking and clustering methods, Rahman’s similarity index (RSI) and Jaccard’s coefficient of similarity were also used.ResultsA total of 109 medicinal plants distributed across 95 genera and 54 families were recorded to treat 76 ailment types. Asteraceae was the foremost family with 9 (8.26%) species. Shrub was the dominant habit (39.45%) and leaves were the most valuable plant parts used for 33.80% of remedy preparations. Remedies were prepared mainly from fresh forms (76.39%) by crushing (20.37%) and administered through the dermal route (41.20%). Significant knowledge variation on medicinal plants was observed between key and general informants (P = 0.000), rural and urban kebeles (P = 0.001), and between age groups (P = 0.013). Informant type (general vs. key informant) and age had a highly significant interaction effect on the medicinal plant knowledge (P = 0.000). About 14.68% of all recorded species were reported to treat hepatitis. From those, Clutia lanceolata was the most preferred. The highest informant consensus factor value (98%) was associated with respiratory conditions. The RSI ranged from 0.5 to 13.79%, and the JSI ranged from 3.5 to 36%. After a systematic search was performed across various reputable databases (Scopus, PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Google Scholar), unique ethnobotanical information on the therapeutic roles of 12 medicinal plant species that have not been reported previously was documented.ConclusionThis finding indicates that the rich diversity of medicinal plants in Gondar City, along with unique ethnomedicinal findings, is an indicator of alternative use of traditional medicine by urban inhabitants for their healthcare system. However, urban ethnobotany is a distinct field in which is expected to evolve knowledge systems influenced by migration. So, these knowledge systems could experience an accelerated loss due to urbanization-related factors unless prior documentation is made.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-00965-z.Keywords: Ethnobotany, Indigenous knowledge, Traditional medicine, Unique findings

Introduction

Traditional medicine is the culmination of all information and methods passed down through generations for the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of health problems [1]. This is more common in many developing countries, including Ethiopia, where a large proportion of the population relies on traditional medicinal plants to meet healthcare needs [2]. Earlier studies suggest that up to 80% of the Ethiopian population depends on traditional medicine with plant-based remedies as dominant components of traditional healthcare practices [3]. However, such figures may vary across regions, between urban dwellers and rural dwellers, through time and should be interpreted with caution.

The diverse climatic conditions of Ethiopia, resulting from extraordinary topographic variability, have favored the evolution and diversity of diseases and their causative agents [4, 5]. In parallel, Ethiopia harbors many plant species [6] and almost all Ethiopian flora are used medicinally [5]. The widespread use of traditional medicinal plants in both urban and rural populations of the country could be attributed to their cultural acceptability, efficacy, physical accessibility, and economic affordability compared to modern medicine [5]. Ethnobotanical studies have also indicated that the local people in Ethiopia are endowed with deep knowledge of how to use plant resources in their healthcare system [4, 6, 7].

In contrast, the diversity of medicinal plants with associated indigenous knowledge is in danger of being lost as habits and value systems change with the environment [8]. Along with this, Ethiopia’s indigenous knowledge system is under-documented compared to the country’s multiethnic, cultural, and floral diversity [4, 9]. Although many ethnobotanical studies have been conducted in the country [10–12], they have predominantly focused on rural communities, often overlooking urban contexts or failing to provide comparative analyses between rural and urban populations. However, Ladio and Acosta [13] reported the widespread use of traditional medicinal plants in urban centers across the globe, and Ethiopia is no exception. This highlights the need for further systematic investigation of urban ethnobotanical practices and their interactions with rural knowledge systems in the country.

Gondar City and its rural surroundings are parts of the country where traditional knowledge and practices are not fully addressed, even though the area harbors many indigenous plant species [14]. In parallel, the majority of the people in the area are partially or fully dependent on traditional plant medicine due to their former habit, the inaccessibility or absence of some modern drugs for certain ailment types, such as cancer, and the skyrocketing prices of modern drugs. In addition, immigrants from different rural parts of the country come to the town with their indigenous traditional knowledge. However, plant species and the associated knowledge in the area are being lost rapidly due to different anthropogenic factors mainly urbanization and increased need of firewood and charcoal [14]. Therefore, it is high time to document and preserve indigenous ethnomedicinal knowledge and practices before the total loss. Therefore, the objectives of this work were: (1) to identify and document traditional medicinal plants and the associated indigenous knowledge; (2) to analyze the socio-demographic effects of informants on indigenous medicinal plants knowledge; and (3) to identify marketable medicinal plants.

Methods

Study area description

The study was conducted in Gondar Administrative City. It is located in the Amhara Regional State, northwestern parts of Ethiopia, situated between 12° 29′ 0″ N–12° 40′ 0″ N latitudes and 37° 20′ 30″ E–37° 31′ 30″ E longitudes (Fig. 1). It is surrounded by Lay-Armacheho District in the north, Dembya District in the west, and Gondar Zuriya District in the south and southeast, with a total area of approximately 292.80 km2 [15]. It is a city administration comprising six sub-cities with 25 urban and 11 rural kebeles (the smallest administrative unit). Of which, the study purposively included 12 kebeles which were selected using a purposive sampling approach based on vegetation cover, representation of both urban and rural settings and availability of knowledgeable traditional healers.

Map of the study area

It is situated in an elevation range of 1833–2773 m above sea level and a mean of 2200 m with undulating mountainous topography. According to 15 years of meteorological data (from 2010 to 2024) obtained from the Bahir Dar Station (the nearest station with full data), the mean annual rainfall and temperature within the town were 1232 mm and 21.2 °C, respectively (Fig. 2). There are four main rivers and one dam (Angereb Dam) which contribute to ecological variability of the area along with its undulating topography. Based on the Koppen and Bornstein [16] climate classification system, the climate of Gondar is categorized as a subtropical highland climate or monsoon-influenced temperate oceanic climate. This moderate climatic condition with higher ecological variability makes it suitable for vegetation diversity and attractive for living.

Walter–Lieth standard climadiagram of the study area showing monthly averages of temperature and precipitation over 15 years (2010–2024)

The natural vegetation of Gondar City Administration is predominantly composed of dry evergreen afromontane forests and grassland complexes. However, in recent periods, most natural vegetation has been restricted to sacred areas as patch forests, mainly as Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahido Church Forests [14]. These forest patches can serve as important reservoirs for biodiversity and sources of traditional medicinal plants. Other areas do not have mature natural vegetation due to different anthropogenic activities, including firewood collection, urbanization, and agricultural expansion. As a result, vegetation is available in scrub lands, farmland margins, and some inaccessible areas. There are also many plantations which are mainly dominated by exotic Eucalyptus camaldulensis [14]. These plantations play a role in meeting local fuelwood and construction demand. However, Eucalyptus camaldulensis has a great negative influence on the availability and distribution of indigenous plant species.

Study site and informant sampling techniques

A reconnaissance survey of the study area was conducted in January 2024 to collect general information about the number and distribution of both rural and urban kebeles, the occurrence of knowledgeable practitioners, the availability of former residents, and vegetation diversity, as described by Martin [17]. This gave a general impression to identify the study sites. Based on the information obtained during the survey, 12 sample kebeles (six rural and six urban) incorporating one-third of the entire kebeles were selected purposively based on the availability of knowledgeable practitioners, kebele type (either urban or rural), and vegetation diversity in each kebele (Fig. 1). Data saturation was used to determine the number of informants because the number of households is unknown. Accordingly, a total of 180 informants aged 20–87 were selected from previously sampled kebeles by giving each kebele an equal chance (Table 1). Of these, 120 were randomly selected as general informants, and the remaining 60 were selected purposefully as key informants. All key informants were selected based on their recognition either by the community or the government, as well as their lifelong healing experience following the methods used by former authors [1, 17]. Prior to the beginning of the interview, all participants were well-versed about the aim of the study. Data collection was conducted in accordance with the International Society of Ethnobiology (ISE) Code of Ethics 2006 (http://ethnobiology.net/code-of-ethics/).

Data collection methods

Ethnobotanical data of medicinal plants were collected from February 2024 to January 2025 using individual interviews, guided field walks, focus group discussions, and market surveys. Individual interviews were conducted with all informants through independent face-to-face contact to ensure their freedom to deal with confidential information. To confirm the accuracy and consistency of data, informants were contacted twice in a three-month interval, and similar responses were considered as valid data. The interviews were held in Amharic (the language of the inhabitants) based on a checklist of semi-structured questions prepared beforehand. The back-translation process was performed to know the correct Amharic names of some plant species and ailment types having local dialects or nuances. Apart from the informants’ demographic data, the interviews were highly valuable for collecting the main ethnobotanical data, including valuable medicinal plants, sources of medicinal plants, ailment types to be treated, plant parts used, conditions of plant parts used, preparation modes of remedies, and routes of utilization in the area, as described by previous authors [1, 17, 18]. This method was also the best for collecting secret and sensitive information about traditional medicinal plants, which is in line with the report of Martin [17].

Focus group discussion with all 60 key informants at a time was difficult to manage. Accordingly, a total of 11 discussions each having five to seven pre-assigned key informant members from different study sites, age groups, and sex were performed following the previous author [17]. The groups were encouraged to discuss freely, politely, and openly with each other and were reassured that their knowledge and practice of traditional medicine would not be compromised, following the former author [17]. The aim of the discussions was to verify and enrich the qualitative information obtained during the in-person interviews. In addition, discussions were used to rank the therapeutic and other uses of medicinal plant species. Thus, this method was used to solicit participants’ attitudes and perceptions, knowledge and experiences, and practices shared in the course of interaction with different people. All the necessary information used to address the objectives of the study was recorded and noted in a relatively short time.

Guided field walks with selected informants were also conducted for the practical demonstration of medicinal plants in the field. During the field walks, a number of field observations were performed, and ethnobotanical data, including the availability of the plants in the area, habit, and habitat of the plant, were collected. In addition, the mode of collection of medicinal plants and their distribution were assessed in this study. Along with these, each specimen was collected, numbered and pressed and dried. Identification was made using Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea with the help of experts at the University of Gondar and was confirmed using Plants of the World Online (https://powo.science.kew.org/). Finally, voucher specimens were deposited in the Herbarium of the University of Gondar. Market surveys were also conducted in four open-air local markets of the town (Arada, Bilko, Shewa Dabo, and Azezo) to determine the presence of marketable medicinal plants, as described by Martin [17]. In addition, the survey was conducted on the street sides. During the survey, individual interviews with both permanent and mobile vendors were conducted. Data on medicinal plants sold in local markets, measuring materials, their cost, source, and market chain were collected through interviews and then recorded. Unique ethnomedicinal findings were also identified in this study after a systematic search was performed across various reputable databases (Scopus, PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and Google Scholar).

Ethnobotanical data analysis

The collected data were organized using a Microsoft Office Excel Spreadsheet 2007. Descriptive statistics, such as percentage, sum, frequency, and average, were used to analyze basic ethnobotanical data, including plant habits, parts used, remedy preparation methods, and administration routes, as previously recommended [1, 17]. An independent sample t-test was used to analyze knowledge variation between younger and older age groups, general and key informants, urban and rural study sites, and sex groups, using SPSS software version 26.0. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was also applied to show the relationship between two or three independent variables (study site, informant’s age, sex, and informant type) and the traditional medicinal plant knowledge of the inhabitants.

Data were also analyzed using recommended clustering and ranking methods, including preference ranking, informant consensus factor (ICF), and fidelity level, to test consistency, agreement, and priority [1, 17, 18]. Preference ranking was performed on eight medicinal plants against hepatitis (an ailment claimed to be treated by many plant species) using nine purposively selected key informants based on their traditional medicinal knowledge depth related to hepatitis. The ranking criterion was the efficacy of the plant against hepatitis. The values given for the ranking exercise ranged from eight (for the most efficacious plant species) to one (for the least efficacious plant species). Finally, the values given for each plant species by each informant were summed, and the total scores were used to rank the species according to their therapeutic value. This ranking was used for the wise identification of the most useful plant species against hepatitis.

The level of information homogeneity provided by different informants for the traditional medicinal value of plants was analyzed using ICF, following Cotton [1]. To perform this, all recorded ailments were grouped into 11 ailment categories in accordance with World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision (ICD-11) [19]. The number of plant species reported to treat each ailment category was then counted. Likewise, the number of reports of species from all informants for the treatment of each ailment category was counted and summed. Finally, ICF analysis was performed by using the formula; ICF=Nur-Nt/Nur-1, where Nur = number of use reports for a particular ailment category and Nt = number of species used for that ailment category.

Fidelity level analysis was performed on the ten most frequently reported ailment types to check the faithfulness of medicinal plants for a particular ailment type, following Alexiades [18]. To perform this, the number of informants who claimed to use plant species to treat a particular disease and the number of informants who used the plant to treat any given disease type were counted separately. Then, the analysis was conducted following [20] using a formula; FL(%)=Np∗100/N, where Np = number of informants who cited a use of plant species to treat particular ailment and N is the number of informants mentioned the plant to treat any given ailment.

Cultural similarities in traditional knowledge between communities in different areas of the country were evaluated using Rahman’s similarity index (RSI) based on common plant species found in those areas and their uses [21]. It was calculated using the formula: RSI=d/(a+b+c-d), where “a” is the number of unique species in area A; “b” is the number of unique species in area B; “c” is the number of common species in both areas (area A and area B) and “d” is the number of common species used for similar ailments in both areas. Finally, the similarity percentage was calculated by multiplying the RSI by 100%. Accordingly, the current study findings were compared with 20 similar studies conducted in different areas of the country with nearly similar vegetation ecology.

Jaccard’s similarity coefficient (JSI) was applied to compute traditional medicinal plant species composition similarity between the current study area and 20 preceding studies in other areas of the country. It was computed following [22] using the formula; JSI=c/(a+b+c), where a stand for the number of plants in area “a”, b represents the number of plants in area “b” and c represents the number of plants common to both area a and area b.

Results

Socio-demographic effect of informants on indigenous medicinal plants knowledge

This study analyzed the impact of informants’ socio-demographic profiles on their indigenous medicinal plant knowledge. Accordingly, a highly significant knowledge variation (P < 0.05) between general and key informants, as well as between rural and urban kebeles, was observed (Table 2). Key informants had higher traditional knowledge than general informants, and rural kebeles had higher indigenous medicinal knowledge than urban kebeles. Significant variation was also observed between age groups, with elder informants aged between 46–87 years having higher indigenous knowledge than younger informants aged between 20–45 years (P = 0.013). In contrast, a significant knowledge variation between the sexes was not recorded (P > 0.05) (Table 2). Informant type (general vs. key informant) and age had a highly significant interaction effect on the medicinal plant knowledge (P = 0.000). While other independent variables analyzed had no any interaction effect on the local people’s medicinal plants knowledge (Table 3).

Taxonomic diversity and habitats of traditional medicinal plants

A total of 109 medicinal plants distributed across 95 genera and 54 families were recorded, along with their local and botanical names (Additional file 1). Asteraceae was a dominant family represented by 9(8.26%) species followed by Poaceae and Solanaceae with 7(6.42%) and 6(5.5%) species, respectively. However, the other 33 families were represented by a single species (0.92%) each (Fig. 3). Shrubs were the dominant plant habit, represented by 43(39.45%) of the recorded medicinal plants, followed by herbs with 40(36.7%) species. In contrast, climbers were the least represented plant habit, with only 11(10.1%) medicinal plant species (Fig. 4).

Habits of medicinal plants with their percentage

Wild natural habitats, such as forests, riverines, grazing areas, and roadsides, were the primary sources of the documented medicinal plants (54.1%). Homesteads harbored 31.2% of the medicinal plants, whereas 14.7% were found in both homestead and wild areas (Additional file 1).

Number and type of ailments and the associated medicinal plants

Of the total identified medicinal plants, 81 (74.31%) species from 72 genera and 40 families were used to treat 56 human ailments in the study area. Nineteen (17.43%) species belonging to 19 genera and 18 families were used for the treatment of six human and livestock ailments, and nine (8.26%) species belonging to eight genera and seven families were used for the treatment of 14 livestock ailments only, with a total of 76 ailments (Table 4).

Plant parts used and conditions of remedy preparation

Leaves were the most commonly used plant parts (33.80%), followed by roots (18.52%). Conversely, the fewest remedies were prepared from root bark, shoot meristem sap, stem sap and the combination of fruits and leaves, each accounting for a single preparation (Table 6).

Medicinal plant parts for remedy preparation and their percentage

The different plant parts applicable for remedies were prepared in fresh, dried, or both forms. Fresh forms were the most widely used, accounting for 76.39%, whereas fresh/dried forms were the least (4.63%) applicable conditions for remedy preparation (Fig. 5).

Modes of remedies preparation and routes of administration

Crushing was the most common remedy preparation method, accounting for 20.37%, followed by boiling, squeezing, and unprocessed form (direct use) at 12.96%, 12.04%, and 11.11%, respectively. Other preparation methods accounted for 0.47–8.33% each (Table 7). Most of the remedies (80.56%) were prepared from a single plant species, and the rest (19.44%) were prepared from combinations of two or more than two medicinal plant species (Additional file 1). Twenty two (11.40%) remedies required additional ingredients, including butter (5.18%), milk (2.59%), salt (1.55%), sugar (1.04%), butter/honey (0.52%), and milk/tea (0.52%).

Fidelity value of medicinal plants for ten most frequently reported ailments

The ICF for the 11 ailment categories revealed that the highest percentage (98%) was linked to problems associated with respiratory conditions. This was followed by two ailment categories (skin and dermatological conditions and spiritual conditions), each with a 97% ICF value. A local market survey identified only six marketable plant species with medicinal value in open markets (Table 13, Fig. 7). Unique ethnobotanical results

The new traditional medicinal roles of 12 plant species that have not been previously reported in Ethiopia are recorded in this study. Of these, 11 were also new recordings to the world for their specific ailment types. Although the species are reported as new for specific ailment types, they have other medicinal values reported in similar studies. To cite them, Convolvulus arvensis against snake and scorpion venoms, Acanthus sennii to impede postpartum hemorrhage, Arisaema schimperianum to treat rheumatism, Brucea antidysenterica and Melia azadirachta against spider infection, Eleusine coracana against hemorrhoids, Myrica salicifolia to prevent rabies, Stephania abyssinica to avoid itch/pruritus, and Stereospermum kunthianum to give relief from fire burns are the first recordings throughout the world. Likewise, Clematis simensis for hemorrhoids, Prunus persica to treat hepatitis, and Ricinus communis to prevent snake bites are the first records in Ethiopia, although related records are available in other nations outside of Ethiopia.

Discussion

Socio-demographic effects of informants on indigenous medicinal plants knowledge

The significant difference in indigenous knowledge observed between key and general informants in the current study is in line with previous reports [37, 42, 43]. This might be due to the lifelong and regular contact of key informants with medicinal plants compared to general informants. Likewise, a high and significant use of medicinal plants by rural communities compared to urban communities was observed. Modernization and inflation in the use of modern medication might have made urban communities less dependent on traditional remedies. The limited accessibility of medicinal plants in urban areas may also make urban communities less dependent on traditional medications. Elderly individuals aged 46–87 years had significantly more knowledge than youths aged 20–45 which might be linked to the long-lasting and positive attitude of the elderly toward medicinal plants. Conversely, rapid degradation of medicinal plants, exposure of youngsters to modern education, and decreased interest in learning and practicing traditional medication systems could minimize the indigenous knowledge of younger informants. This is in accordance with a previous report [4].

A significant difference in medicinal knowledge of plants was not observed between sex groups, which is consistent with other findings in Ethiopia [37, 44] and elsewhere [45, 46]. However, the mean was slightly higher for females, contrary to the reports of the above-mentioned authors. The frequent contact of females with their children might have made them knowledgeable about remedies, since kids are mostly exposed to different ailments due to their lower immunity level and are more prone to infection.

Diversity and habitats of traditional medicinal plants

The number of medicinal plants recorded in the study area indicates the depth of indigenous knowledge of local people regarding traditional medicine. It also underscores the presence of considerable diversity in medicinal plant species in the town and its rural surroundings. The number of medicinal plant species was comparable to that reported in other towns in Ethiopia [23, 47]. This confirmed that urban areas harbor a substantial number of medicinal plants and that ethnobotanical knowledge is not limited to rural communities but is also found in urban inhabitants. In contrast, a lower number of medicinal plants have been reported in other towns in the country [24–26]. Similarly, fewer medicinal plants have been reported in the border rural districts of Gondar City [27, 28]. These might be related to the immigration of individuals to the town along with their indigenous knowledge, which could be further practiced in their new urban destination.

The results showed Asteraceae as the leading plant family in species richness, followed by Poaceae. The dominance of Asteraceae is also consistent with many studies elsewhere in Ethiopia [23, 29–32, 47–49]. This might be associated with the presence of several taxa within the Ethiopian flora [33, 50, 51] and its worldwide cosmopolitan nature [52]. This might also be linked to the occurrence of rich secondary metabolites, such as steroids, terpenes, and alkaloids, in this family that are used as anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antipyretic, and antioxidant agents, as reported by Sílvia et al. [53]. The succeeding dominance of Poaceae might be associated with the grassy nature of most species within it, which can regenerate easily following different degradations. Most plants of the Poaceae family can survive environmental disturbances by maintaining genetic diversity owing to the dormancy of their seeds in the soil [54] and their competitive nature [55].

The recorded results confirmed that shrubs were the leading plant habit, while climbers were the least. The overriding of shrubs was also in agreement with other reports [26, 34]. Likewise, the least distribution of climbers was also agreed with other results elsewhere in the country [9, 23, 30, 56]. The absence of mature and protected natural forests and the removal of trees for different livelihood values could make shrubs advantageous for their dominance due to the absence of competition from trees for resources such as sunlight. In contrast, the absence of natural forests might be the cause of the least diversity of climbers, which need trees for their climbing habit.

Although homesteads harbor considerable numbers of medicinal plants, wild habitats were the richest sources of medicinal plants in the study area. This finding is consistent with those of other studies [32, 35, 48, 57]. As Wondimu et al. [24] reported, wild biodiversity is rich in medicinal plants throughout the country. This indicates a further need to grow medicinal plants in homesteads to save time and energy in searching for plants in the degrading wild environments.

Number and type of ailments and associated medicinal plants

This study revealed that the residents of Gondar City and its vicinity use the natural vegetation of their surroundings to treat different human and livestock ailments. However, in comparison, they were more knowledgeable about and paid more attention to medicinal plants for human ailments than for livestock ailments. This could be related to the higher human population density, which is prone to a higher probability of infectious disease distribution than the livestock population. This result is consistent with previous findings [56, 58, 59]. Although modern medication is accessible and different health institutions are available, they practice traditional medicine for their healthcare system. This might be associated with their previous use of plant remedies, the skyrocketing cost of modern medications, the unavailability of medications for some ailments, and traditional beliefs. For instance, hepatitis and the evil eye were claimed to be treated by many medicinal plants. As data obtained during the interview, the informants believed that using modern medications to treat hepatitis was lethal. For this reason, they preferred and advised the use of traditional medications to treat the ailment. As a result, they preferred to use traditional plant remedies to get relief within short time periods. Likewise, the evil eye is a spiritual disease, and local people do not consider it to be treated by modern medication. Local healers diagnose the type of human ailments by asking patients about the symptoms, touching the affected body part, and visually inspecting visible symptoms, such as eye color, urine color, and lip dryness.

Plant parts used and conditions of remedy preparation

The communities in the study area used different plant parts to prepare remedies. However, leaves were the most commonly used medicinal plant parts, followed by roots. This is in line with previous reports from Ethiopia [26, 36, 49, 56, 59]. Based on informants’ emic perspectives, leaves are more efficacious than other plant parts as a gift from GOD. However, this might be related to the presence of many types of bioactive secondary metabolites with antibiotic effects compared to other plant parts, as reported previously [37, 60]. Ease of collection and preparation might also contribute to the preference for leaves. Healers might prefer to use leaves to conserve plants, as harvesting leaves does not have detrimental effects on the survival of medicinal plants.

Most of the remedies were prepared from fresh plant parts, which is consistent with previous reports [9, 34, 61]. Healers were believed to use fresh forms so as to enhance the efficacy of the remedies, which is in line with the preceding report [36]. This could be associated with the higher levels of active ingredients in fresh forms that could be lost on drying owing to their volatile nature, as described by Otsyina et al. [62]. Ba et al. [63] also reported that active components of fresh plant parts, such as flavonoids, alkaloids and essential oils, give them medicinal properties.

Modes of remedies preparation and routes of administration

Practitioners used different methods of remedy preparation for the treatment of different ailments, which is in line with previous reports [26, 49, 59]. This might be related to the type of plant part used, the condition of the plant part used, the type of ailment to be treated, and/or their traditional beliefs. Crushing was the paramount remedy preparation method, followed by squeezing, which agrees with other reports in Ethiopia [38, 39, 64]. Crushing and squeezing might facilitate the release of bioactive chemicals from the plant with less damage than other methods, such as exposure to heat. This could provide greater relief to patients. In addition, remedy preparation through crushing mainly using fixed stones and squeezing via hand are easy tasks that could be performed everywhere without using any other material. So, any ailment which could happen suddenly and need immediate treatment could be treated on site by preparing remedies via crushing or squeezing.

The results showed that most remedies (90.16%) were prepared from a single plant, which is in line with the findings of [65]. However, [66] reported that polyherbal treatments, which involve a mixture of medicinal plants, are more effective than single-plant treatments. Additional ingredients, such as butter, milk, salt, sugar, and honey, were used to prepare some remedies. These ingredients might be used to improve flavor, minimize bitterness and sour taste, and minimize side effects such as vomiting and diarrhea, as reported previously [33, 40, 49].

Practitioners preferred the dermal route, followed by oral and nasal routes, to administer the prepared remedies. These might be linked with their straightforward, efficient, and effective nature in enhancing healing as reported before [49]. This might also be coupled with the availability of many dermatological ailments and other external injuries, such as wounds, which require external treatment. However, other reports from Ethiopia showed a reciprocal value for the oral and dermal routes [26, 32, 56, 67].

Ethnobotanical scoring and clustering

The high preference for Clutia lanceolata, followed by Justicia schimperiana and Calpurnia aurea, to treat hepatitis might be related to the presence of secondary metabolites with antiviral effects in these plant species. This highlights the need for further phytochemical and pharmacological investigations of these species. Previous ethnobotanical studies have also mentioned these plants for their traditional medicinal roles. Clutia lanceolata has been reported to treat hepatitis [68, 69], hemorrhoids, malaria, giardiasis [29], and diarrhea [29, 40]. Justicia schimperiana was also reported for the treatment of hepatitis [40] and rabies [26, 40]. Likewise, Calpurnia aurea has been reported to be effective against gonorrhea [31], diarrhea [36], and rabies [68, 70].

Fidelity level analysis of medicinal plants for the ten most frequently reported ailments showed that approximately 13 species had 100% fidelity to their respective ailment types. This reflects the faithfulness of each species in treating a specific type of ailment. In contrast, species with low fidelity values, such as Cynoglossum coeruleum, indicated their lower faithfulness to a single ailment type and their ability to treat a number of ailments.

The ICF analysis results showed high values ranging from 80 to 98% for all 12 ailment categories. This suggests a remarkable consensus among the informants regarding the therapeutic uses and efficacy of specific medicinal plant species. As Baydoun et al. [71] reported, if information is exchanged between informants or there is a well-defined selection criterion in the community, ICF approaches one (100%). Therefore, this result indicates the presence of higher information exchange between the communities in Gondar City and its vicinities regarding medicinal plants. Comparatively, the highest ICF value (98%) was for respiratory conditions, including common cold, coughing, and tonsillitis, and the lowest ICF (80%) was associated with organ diseases. The highest ICF value for respiratory conditions indicated that ailments grouped in this category are common in the town. This might be related to the presence of bad smells due to poor sanitation of toilets and poor disposal habits of household and industrial effluents.

Rahman’s similarity index

The RSI showed an important distribution pattern of indigenous knowledge across different cultural groups. The RSI percentage value ranged from 0.5 to 13.79%, which is comparable with the RSI reports of some previous studies [72, 73]. The highest similarity in indigenous knowledge with communities of the Gondar City Administration was observed in communities around Simien Mountain National Park [37], closely followed by communities living around Tara Gedam [40], Sedie Muja District [31], and Lay-Armachiho District [28]. All the above-mentioned study areas are somewhat closer to each other than the other areas analyzed. In contrast, they had lower RSI (0.5%) with communities living in Ongota/Birale District [30] and then Minjar-Shenkora District [38] which are found far from the current study area. This suggests the effect of geographical and cultural proximity on the distribution of indigenous knowledge between communities.

Jaccard’s similarity index

Comparative analysis between the current study area and previous studies showed a wide range of indigenous knowledge distributions across the country. The highest similarity was observed in communities living in the vicinities of Tara Gedam [40], followed by those in the Sedie Muja District [31] and Simien Park vicinities [37]. However, the similarity index is lower than another report [73]. This might be attributed to the cultural diversity and widespread use of medicinal plants among communities throughout the country, as stated before [24].

Marketability of traditional medicinal plants

The number of plants sold entirely for medicinal purposes in open local markets was low. Although there are many marketable medicinal plants in the area, they are mainly sold for their non-medicinal uses. This finding is consistent with previous reports in Ethiopia [38, 48]. In most cases, the communities collect these plants themselves from the natural habitat or prefer to go directly to local healers for treatment instead of buying from local markets. This is consistent with former reports [4, 41] and is an indicator of the poor habit of using medicinal plants as an alternative income source, unlike in other countries such as India and China.

The number of medicinal plants sold in open local markets (six species) was equal to the number sold in the Habru District of Ethiopia [36]. However, it was lower than the number sold in the Dessie Zuria District [23] and higher than the number sold in some other areas [40, 57, 65]. Of the six marketable plants specifically used for their medicinal role, three, Artemisia afra, Echinops kebericho, and Lepidium sativum, were also reported as marketable in Southern Ethiopia [74]. Echinops kebericho has been reported as the rarest species that is not easily available. This might be related to the overharvesting of its roots, which is a declining factor for the diversity of medicinal plants, as reported by Chen et al. [75]. In fact, if roots and whole plant parts are harvested for remedy preparation, the plant’s existence in the future is questionable.

Unique ethnobotanical results

The unique ethnobotanical findings of the current study along with previous studies [37, 49, 72, 73] are good indicators of the need for further ethnobotanical investigations. The therapeutic role of Convolvulus arvensis against snake and scorpion venoms is the first scientific report across the globe, even though its antioxidant, antibacterial, and antifungal properties have been analyzed previously [76]. The watery juice of the root detoxifies the venom when it is swallowed after chewing the root. The use of Acanthus sennii to impede postpartum hemorrhage without any side effects, merely by tying a piece of root externally on the neck or arm, is also a new record. However, this species was previously documented for the treatment of rabies [31], evil eye, rheumatism [40], malaria, tapeworm, and diabetes [32]. The Arisaema schimperianum fresh fruit was also recorded for the first time worldwide to treat rheumatism even though it was reported for the treatment of ailments like wound and skin conditions [77]. The therapeutic roles of Brucea antidysenterica and Melia azadirachta against spider infections are also new findings worldwide. The medicinal roles of Brucea antidysenterica against toothache, and leprosy [25], warts [28]; and Melia azadirachta against poultry disease, dandruff, common cold, malaria, and hypertension [78] have been reported previously.

This study also incorporates the first report of Eleusine coracana for the treatment of hemorrhoids, despite the fact that over 50 species have been reported to do so in Ethiopia [79]. However, [37] reported Eleusine coracana to cure dysentery. Likewise, administering powdered and diluted stem bark of Myrica salicifolia to dogs for rabies prevention is a unique finding of this study. However, other therapeutic roles like common cold [40, 73] and bleeding prevention [40] were recorded in Ethiopia. It has also been reported for the treatment of HIV and AIDS-related opportunistic diseases in Tanzania [80]. Crushing a fresh leaf of Stephania abyssinica on a fixed stone and creaming it over the skin by mixing it with butter to treat itching is another new finding. It has been previously reported for the treatment of pneumonia [36], eye disease, and amoeba [39] and diarrhea [73]. Ricinus communis is also used to prevent snake bites by tying a piece of root around the neck or arm without any side effects. This is the first worldwide report, although the oral intake of its seeds and roots has been reported to cure scorpion bites in Pakistan [81]

Study limitations

Although the study afforded central insights into the ethnobotanical use of medicinal plants, it has several limitations. Numbers of informants were determined by data saturation due to an unknown and continually increasing number of households and individuals, which may bias representativeness. Side effects of remedies related to dosage were not assessed and phytochemical analyses were not performed. Likewise, floristic diversity, abundance, and current status of medicinal plants were not addressed. In addition, assessment of the factors affecting medicinal plants’ diversity and the related traditional conservation methods was outside the study’s scope.

Conclusions

Gondar City Administration is endowed with a variety of plant species that are reported by local communities to have medicinal uses. This medicinal plant diversity along with the indigenous knowledge is a good display for further ethnobotanical studies in other urban centers of the country where such studies remain limited. Even though the study population had easy access to modern medications, they were still dependent on traditional medicinal plants as an alternative health care system which may reflect cultural preferences and perceived benefits. However, ongoing urbanization could present challenges to the accessibility of these plant resources. Therefore, continuous efforts to conserve medicinal plants both in their wild natural habitats and in homesteads may facilitate their availability for future use.
