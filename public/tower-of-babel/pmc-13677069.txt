UcTCRp: a TCRβ-based framework for identifying and profiling MAIT- and iNKT-associated repertoire signals
Authors: Linlin Chen, Yueyi Li, Shiwen Shan, Kaixuan Wang, Chao Feng, Yunsheng Dou, Qihang Xu, Linru Cai, Hao Wang, Haiyang Wang, Xiaochen Bo, Jian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells and invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells are conventionally identified using invariant or semi-invariant TCRα chains, antigen-loaded tetramers, or transcriptomic phenotypes. These requirements limit their detection in public and clinical immune-repertoire datasets that contain only TCRβ sequences. Here we present UcTCRp, a TCRβ-only framework for detecting and profiling relative MAIT- and iNKT-associated signals in bulk immune repertoires. We first identified model-informative TCRβ features, including biased V-gene usage and reproducible CDR3β amino acid composition, in collected human and mouse MAIT and iNKT TCRβ sequences. UcTCRp subsequently integrates these features using species-specific transformer-based representation pretrained separately on large human and mouse TCRβ repertoires, followed by independent supervised modeling with curated MAIT, iNKT, and conventional T cell references for each species. The resulting species-specific models generalized robustly across independent human and mouse cohorts. In paired scRNA-seq/scTCR-seq datasets, UcTCRp recovered transcriptome-defined MAIT and iNKT cells and identified additional MAIT-like candidates supported by receptor evidence but missed by expression-only annotation. Bulk calibration against paired single-cell references and synthetic spike-in experiments established operating characteristics for repertoire-level abundance estimation. These results establish unpaired TCRβ repertoires as an actionable substrate for detecting relative unconventional T cell-associated immune signals, enabling archived repertoire resources to be repurposed for systems-level studies of tissue immunity, disease, and therapeutic response.Keywords: unconventional T cells, T cell receptor (TCR), mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells, invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells, deep learning, computational immunology

Introduction

The human T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire exhibits extraordinary diversity, underpinning adaptive immunity across infections, tumors, autoimmunity, and immune-mediated tissue pathology [1]. Advances in immune repertoire sequencing have generated vast datasets that capture TCR diversity at unprecedented scale [2]. Bulk TCRβ sequencing, in particular, enables systematic characterization of clonal architecture, antigen-driven expansion, and repertoire diversity across large clinical and population cohorts [3]. However, most bulk repertoire datasets lack paired TCRα chains, transcriptomes, and phenotypic measurements. This limitation restricts the ability to assign observed clonotypes to defined T cell populations, especially rare or specialized lineages whose identities are typically established using information not directly encoded in bulk TCRβ data.

Among the cell populations most affected by this interpretability gap are unconventional αβ T cells, including mucosal-associated invariant T (MAIT) cells and invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells [4]. MAIT cells recognize MR1-presented microbial riboflavin-derived metabolites [5], whereas iNKT cells recognize CD1d-presented lipid antigens [6]. Although iNKT cells are typically rare in human peripheral blood and MAIT cell frequencies vary substantially across blood, age, disease state, and tissue context, both lineages are enriched or functionally specialized at barrier and non-lymphoid sites and can mount rapid effector responses [4]. Through cytokine production, cytotoxicity, and tissue-localized immune regulation, MAIT and iNKT cells contribute to antimicrobial defense [7, 8], tissue surveillance [9, 10], inflammation [11–13], tumor immunity [14–16], metabolic regulation [17, 18], and immune homeostasis [19]. Thus, the ability to recover MAIT- and iNKT-associated signals from existing TCRβ datasets would substantially expand opportunities to study unconventional T cell immunity across infection, aging, cancer, inflammatory disease, and tissue-specific immune perturbations. Yet current experimental approaches have not scaled to the breadth of available TCRβ sequencing resources.

Existing MAIT and iNKT annotation strategies rely on experimental or molecular information that is typically absent from bulk TCRβ datasets [20–22]. Antigen-loaded MR1 and CD1d tetramers provide direct cellular detection in targeted experiments [20, 21], whereas flow cytometric marker combinations define phenotypic subsets in fresh or well-preserved samples [23]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) coupled with paired V(D)J profiling further links receptor identity with transcriptional state [24], and canonical TCRα-chain features, such as TRAV1–2–TRAJ33/20/12 for human MAIT cells and TRAV10–TRAJ18 for human iNKT cells [4], are widely used as high-confidence molecular identifiers. Yet none of these measurements is available in most archived bulk TCRβ datasets, limiting their use for retrospective or population-scale studies of unconventional T cell immunity. Recent computational work has suggested that MAIT-associated receptor features can be learned from TCR sequence data, supporting the feasibility of TCR-based inference for unconventional T cell annotation [25]. Moreover, experimental evidence that the MAIT TCRβ chain contributes to ligand- and infection-dependent discrimination provides a biological basis for extracting lineage-relevant information from β-chain sequences [26]. It remains unclear; however, whether TCRβ-encoded lineage constraints extend beyond MAIT cells, generalize across species and can be calibrated into repertoire-level state estimates.

To address this gap, we present UcTCRp, a framework for inferring MAIT- and iNKT-associated immune states from TCRβ sequencing alone. UcTCRp captures conserved sequence features within unconventional T cell repertoires, integrates V gene context and CDR3β composition, and aggregates single-clonotype predictions into repertoire-level estimates of MAIT- and iNKT-associated signals. We benchmarked UcTCRp across curated reference repertoires, independent cohorts, paired single-cell TCR and transcriptomic data, spike-in mixtures, and sequencing-depth perturbations. By enabling inference of relative MAIT- and iNKT-associated signals from bulk and archival TCRβ datasets, UcTCRp provides a scalable and generalizable strategy to link TCR sequence architecture with unconventional T cell immunity, disease states, and potential therapeutic response.

Materials and methods

TCRβ sequence collection and annotation

TCRβ sequences representing MAIT cells, iNKT cells, and conventional αβ T cells were compiled from multiple sources, including the UcTCRdb [27] (a manually curated database of unconventional T cells) and primary dataset retrieved from published bulk TCR sequencing study [28] with CD8+, CD4+, and Treg subsets. For inclusion, datasets were required to contain annotated CDR3β amino acid sequences with matched V and J gene assignments. The final dataset encompassed 22 386 unconventional TCRβ sequences from human samples and 4096 from murine sources. Subsets included 20 117 MAIT and 2269 iNKT TCRβs from human repertoires, and 1786 MAIT and 2310 iNKT TCRβs from murine repertoires. For conventional αβ T cells (CD4+, CD8+, and Treg), each subset comprised more than 1 million TCRβ sequences, serving as the negative class during model training and evaluation.

Before model training and evaluation, all TCRβ sequences compiled from the public datasets underwent rigorous quality control and deduplication. Only productive, in-frame CDR3β sequences were retained. Sequences were required to conform to IMGT-defined structural boundaries: beginning with a cysteine (C) and terminating in a phenylalanine (F), with lengths constrained to 8–26 amino acids. Sequences containing stop codons, nonstandard amino acid characters, or unresolved annotations were excluded. Duplicate entries were removed based on the combination of CDR3β amino acid sequence, V gene, and J gene identifiers. Given the heterogeneity in V and J gene annotations across datasets (e.g. differences between “TRBV” and “TCRBV” nomenclature, and inconsistent allele-level resolution), a unified gene-mapping dictionary was constructed based on the IMGT and Emerson et al. [29] reference tables. In cases where multiple alleles were ambiguously assigned or reported as merged variants (e.g. “TRBV06-02/06-03”), a standardized composite label (“TCRBV06-02/03”) was assigned. V and J gene annotations not found in the curated list or corresponding to pseudogenes were excluded to ensure consistency in downstream embedding and classification tasks. For datasets lacking allele resolution or reporting ambiguous V gene calls (e.g. “TCRBV06-02, TCRBV06-03”), consensus-based collapsing rules were applied, and a majority-vote identifier was retained.

Transformer-based pretraining of TCR embeddings (TCR-V-BERT)

To extract generalizable representations of TCRβ sequences and capture their intrinsic sequence grammar, we developed a TCR-specific transformer model (TCR-V-BERT) based on the Bidirectional Encoder Representation from Transformers (BERT) architecture. This model was pretrained on a large-scale corpus of unlabeled TCRβ sequences using a masked language modeling (MLM) objective, enabling downstream transfer to classification tasks involving unconventional T cell identification.

(1) Pretraining dataset and preprocessing

(1) Pretraining dataset and preprocessing

A total of 153 170 151 human TCRβ sequences annotated with V gene information were obtained from a publicly available cytomegalovirus-associated repertoire study [29]. To ensure high-quality input, sequences containing illegal characters, stop codons, or invalid amino acid residues were removed. CDR3β sequences were required to be in-frame, productive, and within the length range of 8–26 amino acids. Duplicated entries were collapsed. Given the class imbalance across different TRBV gene families, we implemented a stratified downsampling strategy to ensure adequate representation of all V gene classes. Sequences associated with low-frequency V genes were fully retained, whereas those from high-frequency V genes were randomly subsampled, yielding a final balanced corpus of ~1 million sequences for model pretraining. This dataset was split into training and validation sets at a 1000:1 ratio.

(2) Model architecture

(2) Model architecture

The TCR-V-BERT model adopts the standard BERT [30] encoder structure. Inputs to the model consisted of tokenized CDR3β amino acid sequences and V gene identifiers. Each sequence was augmented with special tokens ([CLS]) and padded to a uniform length. Input representations were constructed from four types of embeddings:

Token embeddings: 20 canonical amino acids (plus special tokens) mapped to 768-dimensional vectors.

V gene embeddings: Unique V gene identifiers encoded and projected to 768 dimensions.

These embeddings were summed and passed through a 12-layer transformer encoder, each layer comprising multi-head self-attention and feed-forward submodules with layer normalization.

Model training was implemented in PyTorch v1.12.1 with the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 3 × 10−5, betas = 0.9 and 0.999), batch size = 64, and GELU activation. Training was run for 50 epochs on a Supermicro 7049GP-TRT server equipped with NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080Ti GPUs under Ubuntu 18.04. (4) Mouse-specific model extension

(4) Mouse-specific model extension

To support downstream analyses in murine TCR repertoires, we constructed an additional TCR-V-BERT model pretrained on mouse TCRβ sequences following the same preprocessing, downsampling, and architectural pipeline. This parallel model enabled unbiased embedding and classification of both human and mouse TCRs across all experiments.

Supervised classification of unconventional TCRs (UcTCRp)

To enable automated identification of MAIT and iNKT TCRβ sequences from bulk repertoires, we fine-tuned the pretrained TCR-V-BERT embeddings using a supervised multi-class classifier. The task was formulated as a three-class classification problem: MAIT, iNKT, and conventional TCRβs.

(1) Classifier architecture

(1) Classifier architecture

The classification module (UcTCRp) was implemented as a three-layer feed-forward neural network. Input embeddings were computed as the mean of hidden states from the eighth transformer layer of the 12-layer TCR-V-BERT model, a strategy empirically shown to improve generalization in biological sequence modeling tasks [31]. The network consisted of two fully connected layers (from 768 to 256 and from 256 to 64 dimensions), each followed by batch normalization and a ReLU activation. Dropout was applied after each hidden layer (rates = 0.5 and 0.3, respectively), and a final linear layer mapped the 64-dimensional representation to three output logits. Class probabilities were obtained by applying a softmax function to these logits. The model was trained using a weighted cross-entropy loss to address class imbalance, with class weights set inversely proportional to the observed class frequencies. Because human iNKT cells predominantly utilize TRBV25–1, we introduced an additional training label for conventional TRBV25–1+ TCRβ sequences to prevent the model from overfitting to this feature; at inference, predictions for this auxiliary class were merged with those for the conventional T-cell class.

(2) Training and evaluation dataset construction

(2) Training and evaluation dataset construction

Training and validation sets were assembled from curated high-confidence human and murine TCRβ sequences with known subset identities (see above). To mitigate biases due to unequal class representation and TRBV usage imbalance, we adopted a dual-sampling strategy for generating negative (conventional) TCRs:

V gene-matched negatives (×5): For each unconventional TCR, five conventional sequences were sampled that shared the same TRBV gene.

V gene-matched negatives (×5): For each unconventional TCR, five conventional sequences were sampled that shared the same TRBV gene.

Unmatched negatives (×5): Five additional conventional sequences were randomly sampled from unrelated donors to capture global repertoire diversity.

Unmatched negatives (×5): Five additional conventional sequences were randomly sampled from unrelated donors to capture global repertoire diversity.

All sequences were required to be productive, in-frame, and contain valid TRBV, TRBJ, and CDR3β annotations. To minimize potential false-negative contamination of the conventional T-cell class, conventional sequences with similar V-gene usage and CDR3β sequences (Hamming distance <3) to any unconventional TCRβ sequence were excluded. The final training dataset included 243 438 human and 42 823 mouse sequences. A held-out test set comprising 100 sequences per class was reserved for final performance evaluation. The remaining data were randomly split into training (90%) and validation (10%) sets, with the validation set used for early stopping and model selection. Detailed dataset descriptions are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

(3) Model training and performance evaluation

(3) Model training and performance evaluation

Fine-tuning was performed using the Adam optimizer with L2 regularization (weight decay = 2 × 10−6, learning rate = 8 × 10−5, batch size = 256) for 16 epochs. Early stopping was applied based on validation loss. Classification performance was evaluated using AUROC, precision, recall, and F1 score, computed separately for each class.

(4) External test

(4) External test

To evaluate the generalizability of our model, we tested its performance on three external datasets encompassing different platforms, cell types, and species:

External Test Set 1: scTCR-seq of human MAIT and non-MAIT memory T cells (n = 36 333), with subset identity determined by antigen stimulation and tetramer enrichment [32].

External Test Set 2 and 3: scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq of human PBMCs with annotated MAIT (n = 17 340) and iNKT (n = 324) cells, defined based on gene expression-derived cluster identities [33].

External Test Set 4: TCRβ repertoires from flow-sorted MAIT T cells isolated from mouse thymus (n = 845), identified via MR1 tetramer staining following antigen stimulation [34].

The model retained strong performance on all cohorts supporting its robustness across platforms, donors, and species. Detailed cohort descriptions are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

(5) Single-cell paired-chain validation

(5) Single-cell paired-chain validation

A public paired single-cell dataset containing scRNA-seq and scTCR-seq profiles from 1.1 million T cells was used for paired-chain validation [35]. Of these, 483 838 cells had paired transcriptome and TCR information. The source study annotated 35 T cell populations, including MAIT cells (n = 48 158) and CD8.c16.MAIT.SLC4A10 cells (n = 2990); iNKT cells were not annotated separately. UcTCRp MAIT and iNKT scores were computed for 431 515 cells with usable TCRβ sequences. Canonical MAIT α-chain-positive cells were defined as those expressing TRAV1-2 paired with TRAJ33, TRAJ20, or TRAJ12. Three MAIT annotation layers were compared: source transcriptome-based annotation, canonical α-chain annotation and UcTCRp β-chain annotation (MAIT score ≥ 0.9). For cells not annotated as MAIT in the source dataset, discordant annotation groups were defined as β-chain-only, α + β-supported and α-chain-only. These groups were compared across source cell-type labels and for expression of MAIT-associated genes (ZBTB16, KLRB1, and SLC4A10). Cells with iNKT-associated UcTCRp scores were additionally assessed for canonical iNKT α-chain usage, defined as TRAV10 paired with TRAJ18.

Ablation analysis of UcTCRp components

To quantify the contributions of V-gene context, CDR3β sequence information, pretrained representations, and native V-gene-CDR3β pairing, we constructed a series of baseline and pretrained ablation models using the same three-class classification framework as UcTCRp.

(1) Linear baseline models

(1) Linear baseline models

V genes were represented using species-specific one-hot vocabularies, resulting in 49-dimensional vectors for human TCRβ sequences and 35-dimensional vectors for mouse TCRβ sequences. CDR3β sequences were padded to a fixed length of 26 amino acids and encoded using the five-dimensional Atchley physicochemical factors, yielding a 130-dimensional flattened representation. The V-gene-only model mapped the corresponding 49- or 35-dimensional one-hot vector directly to the three output classes using a single linear layer. The CDR3β-only model used the 130-dimensional Atchley representation as input to the same linear classifier. For the combined V-gene + CDR3β model, the V-gene and CDR3β representations were concatenated, resulting in 179-dimensional human and 165-dimensional mouse input vectors, followed by a single linear classification layer.

(2) Pretrained single-feature ablation models

(2) Pretrained single-feature ablation models

For the pretrained V-gene-only model, the original V-gene input was retained, whereas the CDR3β sequence was replaced by a uniform sequence composed of the padding character “$,” thereby removing sequence-specific CDR3β information while preserving V-gene context. For the pretrained CDR3β-only model, the original CDR3β sequence was retained while the V-gene input was fixed across all samples. TRBV5-7 was used for human datasets and TRBV8 for mouse datasets, as these genes were not preferentially enriched in any of the TCR classes analyzed here. This design minimized class-associated information contributed by V-gene usage while preserving CDR3β-dependent pretrained representations.

(3) Pretrained shuffled ablation models

(3) Pretrained shuffled ablation models

To evaluate whether native V-gene-CDR3β correspondence contributed to prediction, we generated pretrained shuffled V-gene + CDR3β models. Within each class label, V-gene identities were randomly permuted among CDR3β sequences, disrupting native V-gene-CDR3β pairing while preserving overall V-gene usage distributions and class composition. CDR3β sequences and class labels were unchanged during this procedure. The resulting shuffled V-gene-CDR3β pairs were processed using the same pretrained encoding and classification pipeline as UcTCRp.

(4) Model evaluation

(4) Model evaluation

Early stopping was applied based on validation loss to prevent overfitting. Model performance was primarily evaluated using AUROC. Lineage-specific AUROC values were calculated for MAIT-versus-conventional and iNKT-versus-conventional TCRβ classification in the held-out test sets. The same evaluation procedure was applied to all baseline, ablation, and full-model configurations.

Threshold calibration and repertoire-level abundance estimation

UcTCRp generates a probability distribution over MAIT, iNKT, and conventional T-cell classes for each TCRβ sequence. For repertoire-level analysis, MAIT- and iNKT-associated fractions were calculated as the proportions of TCRβ sequences with class-specific prediction scores exceeding predefined thresholds.

(1) Threshold calibration and external test

(1) Threshold calibration and external test

Threshold calibration was performed using External Test Set 2, comprising 166 healthy donors with paired TCRβ sequences and transcriptome-defined MAIT and iNKT abundances [33]. Candidate score cutoffs from 0.5 to 0.9 were evaluated by comparing UcTCRp-derived repertoire fractions with transcriptome-defined abundances. Thresholds retaining at least 90% of the maximum Spearman correlation observed across candidate cutoffs were first identified, and the threshold with the lowest mean absolute error (MAE) among these candidates was selected.

For MAIT, calibration was performed using all 166 donors. Because transcriptome-defined iNKT abundance was zero in a large proportion of donors, iNKT calibration was restricted to the 74 donors with detectable iNKT abundance to prevent zero-reference samples from disproportionately influencing the error criterion. This procedure yielded score cutoffs of 0.9 for MAIT and 0.7 for iNKT. The selected thresholds were fixed before evaluation in the independent Wang cohort and were not further optimized.

Association between UcTCRp-derived and transcriptome-defined abundances was assessed using Spearman correlation. Numerical agreement was evaluated using calibration slope and intercept, MAE, root mean squared error (RMSE), and mean signed bias.

(2) Simulation of mixed repertoires

(2) Simulation of mixed repertoires

To assess repertoire-level estimation under controlled abundance conditions, synthetic repertoires were generated by mixing MAIT or iNKT TCRβ sequences with conventional CD4+ or CD8+ TCRβ sequences. Each simulated repertoire contained 1000 TCRβ sequences, with unconventional TCRβ sequences introduced at 50 predefined proportions ranging from 0% to 10%. Conventional sequences were randomly sampled to maintain the total repertoire size at 1000 sequences. UcTCRp-derived repertoire fractions were calculated using the fixed thresholds determined during calibration (0.9 for MAIT and 0.7 for iNKT). Predicted fractions were compared with the known spike-in proportions using Spearman correlation, calibration slope and intercept, MAE, and RMSE to evaluate both relative abundance tracking and numerical agreement.

(3) Downsampling robustness assessment

(3) Downsampling robustness assessment

To assess the effect of sequencing depth on abundance estimation, we performed downsampling analysis using TCRβ repertoires from the independent Wang et al. cohort [36], with stochastic subsampling conducted at increasing depths of 100, 500, 1000, 1500, 2000, 2500, 3000, and 3500 unique TCRβ sequences. Each depth condition was repeated with 10 replicates. Predicted frequencies stabilized when sample sizes exceeded 1000 unique TCRβs, with minimal variance across replicates and no bias in under- or overestimation. Below this threshold, predictions became noisier and less reliable. Accordingly, samples with at least 1000 unique TCRβ sequences were retained for downstream analyses based on this empirical stability criterion.

(4) Abundance metric definitions

(4) Abundance metric definitions

Repertoire-level MAIT- and iNKT-associated abundances were calculated on a cell-weighted basis using single-cell TCR datasets. Specifically, abundance was defined as the fraction of TCRβ-bearing cells whose class-specific UcTCRp scores exceeded the corresponding threshold. Cells sharing the same TCRβ sequence were counted independently according to their observed cellular abundance, such that clonally expanded TCRs contributed proportionally to the number of cells carrying them. Fixed score thresholds of 0.9 for MAIT and 0.7 for iNKT were used throughout downstream analyses.

For bulk TCRβ repertoires, MAIT- and iNKT-associated signals were calculated using the clone frequencies reported by the TCR-seq processing pipeline. Specifically, the signal was defined as the sum of the normalized frequencies of clonotypes whose class-specific UcTCRp scores met the corresponding threshold. Thus, clonotypes were weighted by their observed clone frequencies rather than treated equally. Because clone frequencies in bulk TCR-seq data may reflect read or template representation and do not necessarily correspond directly to cellular frequencies, the resulting metric was interpreted as a relative MAIT- or iNKT-associated repertoire signal rather than a validated estimate of cellular abundance.

Results

Conserved TCRβ features provide a basis for MAIT and iNKT inference

We first asked whether MAIT and iNKT TCRβ repertoires contain reproducible sequence information that could support TCRβ-only inference. To address this, we compiled a comprehensive dataset from the publicly accessible manually maintained database, UcTCRdb [27]. The dataset encompassed 20 117 MAIT TCRβs and 2269 iNKT TCRβs from humans, as well as 1786 MAIT TCRβs and 2310 iNKT TCRβs from mice. Given the pivotal role of V and J genes in shaping the TCR, we first examined the gene usage patterns of these unconventional TCRβs compared to conventional CD4+ and CD8+ TCRβs. Despite the widely reported differences between conventional CD4+ and CD8+ T cells [37, 38], we found that TRBV20-1, TRBV6-1,2,3,4, TRBV4-2,3, and TRBV15 were preferentially utilized in human MAIT cells, and TRBV25-1 was significantly enriched in human iNKT cells compared with both CD4+ and CD8+ TCRβs (Fig. 1A). In mice, Trbv13-2,3, Trbv19, and Trbv4 were dominant in MAIT cells, while Trbv13-1,2 and Trbv29 were enriched in iNKT cells (Fig. 1B). Across both human and mouse MAIT and iNKT cells, J gene usage was relatively diverse despite some subset-specific preferences (e.g. TRBJ2-1,3 in human MAIT/iNKT and Trbj2-3 in mouse MAIT cells), contrasting with the strong restriction and enrichment characteristic of V gene usage (Fig. 1A and B). While these patterns are consistent with previous reports [39, 40] and reinforce the notion that MAIT and iNKT cells relay on highly restricted V segments linked to their evolutionarily conserved antigen specificity, our analysis of D gene reveals an additional layer of bias. Specifically, in the D gene annotation subsets of MAIT and iNKT TCRβ, we observed a statistically significant enrichment of the TRBD2 gene fragment compared to conventional T cells (Fig. 1C), while the Trbd1 gene was significantly elevated in iNKT cells in the mouse dataset (Fig. 1D). In addition to differences in gene usage, the unconventional TCRβs show a subtle but significant increase in CDR3 length compared to conventional T cells in both species (Fig. 1E). Nevertheless, amino-acid conservation analysis shows that, despite biased gene usage and longer CDR3s, the CDR3 regions of unconventional TCRβ sequences remain highly diverse, as illustrated by the sequence-logo plots (Fig. 1F and G).

To further dissect the compositional constraints underlying unconventional TCRβ sequences, we quantified NMI across all residue pairs in CDR3β loops. In both human and murine samples, we observed elevated NMI at the N- and C-terminal regions of the CDR3β loop of fixed length (14 amino acids) (Fig. 1F and G), as well as across other lengths (12, 13, 15 amino acids, Supplementary Fig. 1), consistent with these positions being derived from germline-encoded V and J gene segments. In contrast, the central residues (positions 5–8 of length 14), which are shaped predominantly by random nucleotide additions during thymic recombination, exhibited markedly lower inter-residue dependence (Fig. 1F and G). This pattern highlights somatic diversification of the unconventional CDR3β loops.

Given this contrast and in light of the conserved ligand recognition modes of unconventional αβ T cells [41–43], we next asked whether the compositionally diverse CDR3β cores might nonetheless encode subset-specific features relevant to antigen engagement. We then compared amino acid usage between the central positions of unconventional and conventional CDR3βs. Strikingly, the biochemical composition of the unconventional CDR3β core was highly conserved between human and mouse. In both species, MAIT cells exhibited strong enrichment of glycine (G) together with negatively charged amino acids, specifically aspartic acid (D) and glutamic acid (E), whereas iNKT cells were consistently enriched for these negatively charged residues (D and E) (Fig. 1H and I). Although minor interspecies differences were also observed, for example, human MAIT and iNKT cells were enriched in serine (S), whereas their murine counterparts preferentially used threonine (T) (Fig. 1H and I), this cross-species consistency suggests that these biochemical features in the CDR3β core may be functionally relevant for antigen recognition in each unconventional T cell subset. Motif analysis further revealed that such amino acid usage biases are broadly distributed across the central region of the CDR3β (Fig. 1J and K), suggesting that although CDR3β regions are inherently diverse, unconventional T cells retain conserved amino acid preferences that likely underlie their capacity to engage invariant microbial or self-derived ligands.

To further substantiate these findings, we conducted additional analysis by subdividing conventional T cells into three distinct subtypes: CD4+ conventional T cells (Tconv), CD4+ regulatory T cells (Tregs), and CD8+ T cells. The results were mostly consistent with the initial observations (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, to address potential confounding effects, we refined our analysis to exclusively focus on TCRβs that shared the same V gene usage. Encouragingly, the identified amino acid usage patterns mostly persisted in both human (Supplementary Fig. 3) and mice (Supplementary Fig. 4), suggesting that CDR3β composition is shaped by selective pressures beyond germline-encoded V gene constraints. These collective findings underscore the consistency and prevalence of specific amino acid preferences or structural features within the intermediate sequences of unconventional T cells, emphasizing the potential functional significance of these patterns in the recognition of conserved antigens.

UcTCRp integrates V-gene context and CDR3β features to classify unconventional TCRβs

Having established that MAIT and iNKT TCRβ repertoires contain conserved sequence constraints, we next developed UcTCRp, a TCRβ-only model designed to learn these constraints and classify unconventional TCRβ sequences. Considering the relatively limited fraction and diversity of unconventional TCRs (~1%–10% of the total T cell pool), we recognized the necessity of fortifying the model with robust stability and generalization capabilities. In recent years, the feature extraction capability of large-scale language models and their ability to capture underlying grammatical rules and hidden sequence patterns in a self-supervised manner based on unlabeled datasets have been demonstrated and successfully applied to protein sequence analysis [31, 44]. Therefore, we construct a TCRβ multi-classification model by incorporating advanced pretraining technique based on TCR language model (Fig. 2A).

UcTCRp architecture and sequence-level classification performance. (A) Overview of the UcTCRp workflow. Input TCRβ sequences are encoded using V-gene context and CDR3β sequence, passed through a pretrained transformer representation, scored as MAIT-, iNKT-, or conventional-associated states. (B, C) Precision-recall curves and normalized confusion matrices illustrating model performance on human (B) and mouse (C) held-out test sets.

Specifically, to achieve a robust representation of TCRβs, we meticulously trained a BERT [30] model on a dataset comprising one million TCRβ sequences at first step. This model, denoted as TCR-V-BERT, comprises 4 embedding layers and 12 transformer encoder modules, incorporating both V gene and CDR3 amino acid sequences as input. The J gene was excluded due to its limited variability and redundant information relative to the CDR3β region, which already captures its contribution to sequence composition [45, 46]. To capture the underlying syntax and semantics of TCRβ sequences, we employed a MLM pretraining strategy, which enables the model to learn generalizable sequence representations and global dependencies across V and CDR3 regions. The resulting embeddings provide informative features that can be transferred to downstream tasks, such as predicting antigen specificity or classifying T cell subsets. For our classification objective, we extracted sequence embeddings from the pretrained model and passed them through three fully connected layers trained with a weighted cross-entropy loss to address class imbalance in multi-class prediction.

During model training, we accounted for the known enrichment of specific V genes in unconventional TCRβs by implementing a sampling strategy to ensure balanced representation. For each unconventional TCRβ, 5-fold conventional TCRβs were randomly selected to match V gene usage and 5-fold were selected without V gene matching from publicly available repertoires (see Methods). This strategy simultaneously controlled for V gene biases and preserved realistic background variability, thereby mitigating the risk of the model learning V gene driven shortcuts. The resulting dataset, comprising both conventional and unconventional sequences, was randomly partitioned into training and validation sets at a 9:1 ratio. A held-out test set was constructed by sampling 100 sequences from each of the four defined subsets. Human and mouse UcTCRp models were trained and evaluated independently using species-specific TCRβ repertoires and annotations, with no cross-species data mixing during model development. Full details are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

As shown in Fig. 2B, the human model demonstrated high classification performance across all unconventional T cell subsets, with area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) values of 0.87 for MAIT cells and 0.94 for iNKT cells. Most misclassifications occur between MAIT cells and conventional T cells rather than among unconventional subsets. This may reflect the presence of unannotated or latent MAIT TCRβs within the conventionally labeled negative samples, particularly given the relatively high prevalence of MAIT cells in peripheral blood and potential overlaps in V gene usage. The mouse model displayed slightly reduced performance relative to the human model, though it consistently achieved AUPRC values close to 0.8 across subsets (Fig. 2C). Misclassification between murine iNKT and MAIT cells was more frequent, likely due to their shared V gene usage and convergent CDR3 features, as observed in Fig. 1B, I, and K.

Species-specific UcTCRp models generalizes across independent cohorts

To further assess the robustness and generalizability of UcTCRp, we evaluated the model on three external cohorts that were not used during model development. These datasets included human MAIT TCRβ sequences from an independent study (n = 36 333) [32], human MAIT (n = 17 340) and iNKT (n = 324) TCRβ sequences from a second cohort [33], and mouse MAIT TCRβ sequences (n = 845) [34] (Supplementary Table 3). UcTCRp retained strong performance across all external test datasets. The model achieved AUROC values of 0.90 and 0.87 in human MAIT datasets, 0.92 for human iNKT dataset and 0.85 for mouse MAIT dataset (Fig. 3A). Classification performance was also stable across additional metrics, with F1 scores of 0.84, 0.81, 0.86, and 0.76, respectively (Fig. 3A). Thus, the pretrained model discriminated unconventional TCRβ sequences across external human cohorts and retained predictive performance in mouse MAIT repertoires without retraining. To further examine whether performance depended on sequences closely resembling those in the training data, we repeated the evaluation after excluding sequences sharing the same V gene and having a CDR3β Hamming distance of <3 from any training sequence. After filtering, AUROC was maintained in the human iNKT dataset and showed only slight decreases across the three MAIT datasets (Supplementary Fig. 5). Although more than half of the sequences were removed from the original evaluation sets, UcTCRp retained substantial discriminatory performance across all four filtered datasets, supporting its ability to generalize to TCRβ sequences less similar to those encountered during training.

Prediction score distributions confirmed these results. In both human MAIT datasets, MAIT TCRβ sequences showed a clear shift toward higher predicted MAIT scores relative to non-MAIT memory or conventional T cells (Fig. 3B and C). Human iNKT sequences were similarly enriched at high predicted iNKT scores compared with negative controls (Fig. 3D). Mouse MAIT sequences also showed higher predicted MAIT scores than non-MAIT conventional T cells, although separation was more moderate than in the human datasets (Fig. 3E).

We next stratified prediction scores by V gene usage to determine whether UcTCRp predictions were driven primarily by known V gene enrichment or whether the model could still distinguish unconventional from conventional TCRβ sequences within the same V-gene background. In both human MAIT datasets, MAIT TCRβ sequences remained distinguishable from negative sequences across multiple V gene strata (Fig. 3F and G). In the human iNKT dataset, high scores were concentrated in TRBV25-1, whereas sequences using other V genes generally received low scores. Importantly, within the TRBV25-1 background, iNKT TCRβ sequences remained clearly separated from conventional sequences (Fig. 3H). Mouse MAIT sequences also showed V gene-stratified separation despite greater variability across strata (Fig. 3I). Together, these results indicate that although V gene context contributes to UcTCRp predictions, the model does not rely on V gene usage alone and retains discriminative capacity within matched V gene backgrounds.

We next evaluated whether pretrained TCR representations provided additional information beyond manually encoded sequence features. Pretrained CDR3β-only models consistently improved over their non-pretrained counterparts, suggesting that self-supervised TCR representations capture higher-order sequence features beyond simple sequence composition. In contrast, pretrained V-gene-only models showed limited improvement over baseline V-gene models, indicating that the predictive advantage of UcTCRp does not arise simply from enhanced recognition of V-gene biases.

To assess the contribution of native V-gene-CDR3β relationships, we disrupted V-gene and CDR3β pairing within each class and trained pretrained shuffled V-gene+CDR3β models. Although shuffled models retained substantial predictive capacity, they consistently underperformed the full UcTCRp model, particularly in external cohorts. For MAIT, AUROC values increased from 0.86, 0.84, and 0.81 in shuffled models to 0.90, 0.87, and 0.85 with UcTCRp across the three external test cohorts. These results indicate that the native relationship between V-gene context and CDR3β sequence features provides additional discriminatory information beyond their independent contributions.

Overall, UcTCRp achieved the strongest or jointly strongest performance across evaluated datasets (Table 1). Together with V-gene-controlled analyses, these results demonstrate that UcTCRp is not solely dependent on lineage-associated V-gene usage but instead integrates complementary information from germline context, CDR3β sequence patterns, pretrained TCR representations, and their native sequence relationships.

Single cell validation supports β-chain-based unconventional T cell annotation

Having established generalization across independent datasets and species, we next evaluated UcTCRp using orthogonal single-cell evidence. We applied the model to a large public scRNA-seq dataset [35] coupled with paired V(D)J profiling to compare β-chain-based predictions with transcriptomic annotations and canonical α-chain-based MAIT definitions. The dataset contained 1.1 million T cells, including 483 838 cells with paired transcriptome and TCR information across 35 annotated cell types (Fig. 4A). The original study annotated two MAIT-related groups: MAIT cells (n = 48 158; 9.95%) and CD8.c16.MAIT.SLC4A10 cells (n = 2990; 0.62%). iNKT cells were not annotated as a separate population in the original dataset. UcTCRp scores were computed for 431,515 cells with suitable TCRβ information. We then compared three MAIT annotation layers: original transcriptomic annotation, canonical α-chain annotation based on TRAV1-2 paired with TRAJ33, TRAJ20, or TRAJ12, and β-chain annotation based on UcTCRp scores.

The three annotation strategies showed substantial but incomplete overlap (Fig. 4B). In total, 43 862 cells were jointly supported by transcriptomic, α-chain and β-chain annotations, corresponding to 75.7% of the combined MAIT-like set. UcTCRp and α-chain annotation also identified additional cells not labeled as MAIT in the original transcriptomic annotation. To characterize these discordant cells, we defined three groups: β-chain-only annotation (Group 1; n = 2619), α + β chain annotation outside the original MAIT label (Group 2; n = 2097), and α-chain-only annotation outside the original MAIT label (Group 3; n = 3378) (Fig. 4B). These cells were distributed across multiple transcriptomic cell types and were relatively enriched among ITGAE+ tissue-resident memory-like cells (Fig. 4C), suggesting that gene-expression-based annotation alone may miss MAIT-like cells in certain phenotypic states.

Marker expression supported this interpretation. Original transcriptomic MAIT cells showed the highest expression of MAIT-associated markers including ZBTB16, KLRB1, and SLC4A10. Among cells not originally labeled as MAIT, the α + β-supported group showed the strongest marker expression, whereas α-only and β-only groups also showed significantly higher MAIT marker expression than conventional T cells (Fig. 4D). These results indicate that TCR-based annotations capture biologically relevant MAIT-like cells that may be under called by transcriptomic clustering alone.

We further examined discordance between α- and β-chain evidence. Cells annotated by β-chain score alone (Group 1) tended to pair with non-classical MAIT-like α chains rather than the canonical TRAV1-2–TRAJ33/20/12 combination (Fig. 4E), suggesting that UcTCRp may identify MAIT-associated TCRβ signatures in cells lacking canonical MAIT α-chain usage. To further evaluate these β-chain-defined predictions, we applied tcrpheno [47], which integrates paired α/β TCR information to derive a TCRinnate score reflecting an innate-like, PLZF-high T-cell phenotype. Group 1 (β-chain-only annotation) showed significantly higher TCRinnate scores than conventional T cells (Mann–Whitney U test, P < .001; Fig. 4F), providing independent paired-chain support for their innate-like characteristics. Conversely, α-chain-only cells (Group 3) that did not pass the UcTCRp threshold nevertheless had significantly higher β-chain prediction scores than conventional T cells (Fig. 4G), indicating that α- and β-chain MAIT features are partially coupled even when one chain falls below the annotation threshold. Moreover, UcTCRp detected a small group of transcriptome-labeled MAIT cells with iNKT-associated β-chain scores. Most of these cells carried TRAV10–TRAJ18 α chains, providing independent receptor-level support for this assignment. Although canonical TRAV10–TRAJ18/TRBV25-1 cells were rare in this dataset (n = 97), this concordance suggests that UcTCRp can recover rare iNKT-associated signals not resolved by the original transcriptomic annotation.

Aggregated UcTCRp scores capture repertoire-level unconventional T-cell signals

Having established the sequence-level classification performance of UcTCRp, we next investigated whether aggregated prediction scores could capture variation in MAIT- and iNKT-associated signals at the repertoire level. Score thresholds were determined using human PBMC scRNA-seq coupled with paired V(D)J profiling dataset (External Test Set 2) according to a prespecified criterion designed to balance preservation of inter-donor variation with numerical error. Specifically, candidate thresholds were first required to retain at least 90% of the maximum Spearman correlation observed in the calibration dataset, after which the threshold with the lowest MAE was selected.

For MAIT, threshold calibration was performed across all 166 donors in this dataset. Rank association increased progressively with more stringent thresholds and reached a maximum at a cutoff of 0.9 (Spearman ρ = 0.755). Because only the 0.9 cutoff retained at least 90% of the maximum correlation, it was selected for subsequent analysis. For iNKT, transcriptome-defined abundance was zero in 92 of 166 donors; therefore, threshold calibration was restricted to the 74 donors with detectable iNKT abundance to prevent the optimization criterion from being dominated by zero-reference samples. The maximum rank association was observed at a cutoff of 0.6 (ρ = 0.489). Both 0.6 and 0.7 retained at least 90% of this maximum correlation, whereas the 0.7 cutoff yielded a lower MAE (0.00238 versus 0.00386). Accordingly, cutoffs of 0.9 and 0.7 were selected for MAIT and iNKT, respectively, and fixed before independent evaluation (Fig. 5A and B).

The prespecified thresholds were subsequently evaluated without further optimization in the independent cohort [36] comprising 56 human PBMC donors. UcTCRp-derived MAIT estimates showed a strong positive association with transcriptome-defined MAIT abundance (Fig. 5C; Spearman ρ = 0.818, P = 1.37 × 10−14). Numerical agreement in absolute scale was nevertheless incomplete, with an MAE of 1.22 percentage points and a mean bias of −0.92 percentage points, indicating a tendency to underestimate MAIT abundance. Thus, UcTCRp robustly captured inter-individual variation in MAIT-associated repertoire signals, although its output should not be interpreted as a directly calibrated measure of absolute MAIT cell frequency.

For iNKT, application of the prespecified 0.7 cutoff across the complete independent cohort yielded a moderate positive association with transcriptome-defined abundance (Fig. 5D; Spearman ρ = 0.432, P = 1.12 × 10−3). Because iNKT cells were undetectable by transcriptome-based annotation in a substantial fraction of samples and the abundance range was highly compressed, we further performed a sensitivity analysis focused on samples with detectable reference iNKT abundance, 33 samples remained for this analysis. UcTCRp-derived and transcriptome-defined iNKT abundances remained positively associated (Spearman ρ = 0.403, P = .020), indicating that the observed repertoire-level relationship was not solely attributable to zero-reference samples. Nevertheless, the weaker association relative to MAIT highlights the greater difficulty of quantitatively resolving the low-abundance iNKT population from TCRβ repertoires alone.

Together, these analyses demonstrate that aggregated UcTCRp scores robustly capture relative MAIT-associated repertoire variation and provide moderate tracking of iNKT-associated variation across independent samples. Numerical agreement with transcriptome-defined cell frequencies remains imperfect, particularly for iNKT, supporting interpretation of UcTCRp-derived repertoire estimates primarily as relative lineage-associated receptor signals rather than fully calibrated measurements of absolute unconventional T-cell abundance.

Controlled spike-in and sequencing-depth analyses of repertoire-level estimates

To further evaluate repertoire-level behavior under controlled conditions, we generated simulated bulk repertoires by spiking MAIT or iNKT TCRβ sequences into unseen conventional CD4+ or CD8+ TCRβ backgrounds at defined proportions. The score cutoffs selected in External Test Set 2 (0.9 for MAIT and 0.7 for iNKT) were fixed throughout these simulations. UcTCRp-derived repertoire fractions increased consistently with the true spike-in proportions across both CD4+ and CD8+ backgrounds (Fig. 6A and B). In addition to Spearman correlation, numerical agreement was assessed using linear calibration slope and intercept, MAE, and root mean squared error (RMSE). For MAIT, the predicted repertoire fractions showed strong association with the imposed spike-in proportions (Spearman ρ = 0.937–0.958), with calibration slopes of 0.39 and 0.41 and MAEs of 24.61% and 24.69% in the CD4+ and CD8+ backgrounds, respectively (Fig. 6A and B). Similar analyses for iNKT showed Spearman correlations of 0.975–0.968, calibration slopes of 0.50 and 0.53, and MAEs of 22.37% and 17.73% (Fig. 6C and D). These results indicate that aggregated UcTCRp scores track controlled changes in unconventional TCRβ representation, while deviations from the identity relationship quantify the remaining differences in absolute scale.

We next assessed the sensitivity of repertoire-level estimates to sequencing depth using TCRβ repertoires from 56 healthy donors in the independent Wang et al. cohort [36], which was not involved in any stage of UcTCRp model development or parameter selection. For each donor, repertoires were randomly subsampled to depths ranging from 100 to 3500 unique TCRβ sequences, with 10 replicate subsamples generated at each depth. Predictions were obtained using the same fixed score cutoffs. Variability in UcTCRp-derived estimates increased markedly at low repertoire depths and progressively decreased as the number of sampled TCRβ sequences increased (Fig. 6E and F). Estimates became comparatively stable above approximately 1000 unique TCRβ sequences in these simulations, indicating that repertoire depth is an important determinant of the precision of sample-level estimates.

Together, these analyses show that aggregated UcTCRp scores respond consistently to controlled changes in unconventional TCRβ abundance and become increasingly stable with greater repertoire depth. The combination of correlation, calibration, and error analyses further distinguishes relative abundance tracking from exact numerical recovery, supporting the use of UcTCRp-derived repertoire estimates primarily for comparative analyses rather than as fully calibrated absolute cell frequencies.

Discussion

T cell immunity has traditionally been interpreted through the recognition of peptide antigens presented by MHC class I or class II molecules. This paradigm, however, does not fully capture unconventional, non-MHC-restricted T cells. MAIT and iNKT cells recognize conserved microbial or self-derived antigens presented by MR1 and CD1d, respectively, and contribute to tissue surveillance, antimicrobial defense and immune regulation. Yet these lineages remain difficult to study at scale because their conventional identification relies on paired TCRα chains, antigen-loaded tetramers or cellular phenotypes, whereas most public and clinical immune-repertoire datasets contain only unpaired TCRβ sequences. Here we show that UcTCRp can extract learnable and calibratable relative MAIT- and iNKT-associated signals from such incomplete repertoire data, enabling single-cell annotation support and bulk repertoire-level inference without redefining these lineages by β chain alone.

A key implication of this work is that MAIT- and iNKT-associated β chains are not merely passive partners of invariant or semi-invariant α chains. Across human and mouse repertoires, these lineages showed recurrent TRBV and TRBJ biases, CDR3β-associated sequence features and conserved amino acid enrichment within the junctional core. These patterns were maintained across species and phenotypic contexts, suggesting that unconventional TCRβ repertoires are shaped by conserved selective pressures rather than by dataset-specific sampling effects. The persistence of predictive signal after V-matched negative sampling and V-stratified evaluation further indicates that these features are not reducible to germline-segment enrichment alone. Instead, MAIT and iNKT TCRβ repertoires appear to encode distributed lineage-associated constraints that can be learned from large-scale sequence data and calibrated for repertoire-level analysis.

The enrichment of negatively charged residues within the CDR3β core provides a plausible link between sequence-level constraints and antigen-recognition biology. Aspartic acid (D) and glutamic acid (E) residues may shape the electrostatic environment of the TCR-ligand interface or stabilize conformations compatible with MR1- or CD1d-restricted recognition. Structural studies of MAIT TCR-MR1-antigen complexes indicate that CDR3β can contribute to the TCR-MR1-antigen interface and modulate complex stability [41, 42], although direct antigen contacts by CDR3β are not consistently observed. Rather than acting as a deterministic contact motif, CDR3β may support recognition through conformational adaptability and induced-fit-like rearrangements, as illustrated by the conformational shift of E99β in the MR1-Ac-6-FP complex [42]. Similar principles have been described for iNKT TCR recognition of CD1d-lipid complexes [39], where β-chain features can influence docking geometry and ligand discrimination. Thus, the conserved charge distributions identified here may reflect structural constraints that tune receptor engagement rather than simple sequence motifs. Future structure-aware models could test this hypothesis more directly.

These observations motivated the development of UcTCRp as a framework for extracting unconventional T cell-associated information from TCRβ-only data. Prior computational work has suggested that MAIT-associated receptor features can be learned from TCR sequence data [25]. UcTCRp extends this concept by modeling multiple unconventional T cell lineages, incorporating human and mouse repertoires, and translating sequence-level predictions into repertoire-level estimates. Its design combines V-gene context, CDR3β sequence information and self-supervised transformer pretraining, allowing the model to learn distributed receptor features rather than rely on single-gene enrichment or simple motif rules. This architecture is aligned with the structure of most public and clinical repertoire resources, which often contain only TCRβ sequences.

The paired single-cell validation places this β-chain signal in the context of established annotation modalities. UcTCRp recovered transcriptome-defined MAIT cells and identified additional candidate MAIT-like cells supported by αβ receptor evidence but not captured by expression-only annotation. This is consistent with the expectation that transcriptomic clustering, canonical α-chain usage and β-chain inference capture overlapping but non-identical aspects of MAIT identity [32, 48]. Transcriptional profiles vary with activation state, tissue localization, and stimulation context, whereas canonical α-chain rules are highly specific for classical MAIT cells but may miss non-classical MR1-reactive or MAIT-associated receptor configurations [49, 50]. In this setting, UcTCRp provides an orthogonal receptor-based annotation layer for paired single-cell datasets and a scalable inference layer for bulk datasets in which α-chain and transcriptomic information are absent.

At the repertoire level, aggregated UcTCRp scores enable relative MAIT- and iNKT-associated signals to be approximated from bulk TCRβ data, representing one of the method’s most practical applications. In large archived cohorts, the relevant question is often not whether an individual clonotype can be definitively assigned to a lineage, but whether a sample carries increased or decreased unconventional T cell-associated receptor signals. By calibrating sequence-level predictions against paired single-cell references and controlled spike-in mixtures, UcTCRp converts clonotype-level predictions into sample-level repertoire summaries (Figs 5 and 6). Although UcTCRp-derived frequencies did not fully align with transcriptome-defined frequencies in absolute scale, the latter should not be considered a gold-standard measure of MAIT or iNKT abundance, as transcriptome-based annotations can be influenced by cell state, tissue context, and marker-selection criteria [22, 32, 51]. In addition, absolute UcTCRp estimates depend on the score threshold used for sequence classification and therefore reflect a sensitivity–precision trade-off. Accordingly, UcTCRp-derived frequencies should be interpreted primarily as relative measures of MAIT- or iNKT-associated repertoire signals rather than as directly calibrated estimates of absolute cell abundance. Future datasets incorporating orthogonal lineage identification, such as MR1- or CD1d-tetramer-based measurements coupled with paired TCR sequencing, will provide a more rigorous basis for threshold optimization and absolute-frequency calibration.

Nonetheless, several limitations remain. The biological interpretation of MAIT- and iNKT-associated CDR3β features remains partly inferential because TCR recognition is shaped by paired αβ receptor geometry, antigen-presenting molecule, ligand context, and cellular state. Although UcTCRp detects lineage-associated receptor signals from TCRβ sequences, it does not establish antigen specificity, activation state, or functional subset identity for individual clonotypes. Further validation through structural modeling, ligand-binding assays and antigen-loaded tetramer experiments will therefore be needed to determine whether recurrent β-chain features contribute directly to antigen recognition or instead reflect lineage-associated receptor constraints. Explicit modeling of antigen context, including microbial metabolite, lipid antigen, and presenting molecule information, may further distinguish lineage-associated signals from ligand-specific recognition features. Finally, integrating orthogonal modalities such as transcriptomics, surface proteomics, and antigen-reactivity measurements could enable more comprehensive models of unconventional T cell identity, state, and function. Extending this framework to additional semi-invariant unconventional populations, including other MR1-reactive, CD1-restricted, and HLA-E-restricted T cells [4], will help determine whether partial receptor sequences can more broadly support inference of non-peptide and non-classical antigen recognition.

Conclusion

In summary, UcTCRp demonstrates that unpaired TCRβ repertoires encode structured, lineage-associated information about MAIT and iNKT immune states. By learning these constraints, validating them against single-cell evidence, and observing related repertoire features through parallel species-specific analyses in human and mouse datasets, the framework links receptor sequence architecture to unconventional T cell biology at population scale. More broadly, this work supports a general principle for immune-repertoire analysis: incomplete receptor measurements can contain biologically organized cell-state information that, when appropriately modeled and calibrated, can transform archived clonotype catalogues into quantitative maps of immune-state variation.
