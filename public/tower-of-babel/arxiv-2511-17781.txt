SAFE-SMART: Safety Analysis and Formal Evaluation using STL Metrics for Autonomous RoboTs
Kristy Sanano*, Jianyu An*, Dinesh Manocha, Huan Xu

Abstract
We present a novel, regulator-driven approach for post hoc safety evaluation of learning-based, black-box autonomous mobile robots, ensuring ongoing compliance with evolving, human-defined safety rules. In our iterative workflow, human safety requirements are translated by regulators into Signal Temporal Logic (STL) specifications. Rollout traces from the black-box model are externally verified for compliance, yielding quantitative safety metrics, Total Robustness Value (TRV) and Largest Robustness Value (LRV), which measure average and worst-case specification adherence. These metrics inform targeted retraining and iterative improvement by model designers. We apply our method across two different applications: a virtual driving scenario and an autonomous mobile robot navigating a complex environment, and observe statistically significant improvements across both scenarios. In the virtual driving scenario, we see a 177% increase in traces adhering to the simulation speed limit, a 1138% increase in traces minimizing off-road driving, and a 16% increase in traces successfully reaching the goal within the time limit. In the autonomous navigation scenario, there is a 300% increase in traces avoiding sharp turns, a 200% increase in traces reaching the goal within the time limit, and a 49% increase in traces minimizing time spent near obstacles. Finally, we validate our approach on a TurtleBot3 robot in the real world, and demonstrate improved obstacle navigation with safety buffers.

II
Background and Related Work

Current evaluation of presently deployed, learning-based black-box autonomous vehicles often relies on surrogate strategies that approximate or over-simplify real safety issues
[
17
,
18
]
. These include approaches that pre-define requirements through hand-crafted finite state machines
[
19
]
, run-time assurance (RTA) mechanisms that supervise the behavior with fallback safe actions
[
20
]
, and scenario-based testing where black-box controllers are subjected to parameterized environments to observe performance
[
21
]
. Other approaches quantify risk statistically by estimating failure probabilities through repeated rollouts in simulation
[
22
]
. While valuable for early assessments, these approaches lack formal guarantees and provide limited guidance on improving system safety or reliability of learning-based black-boxes.

Formal methods
(FM) provide mathematically rigorous techniques for the specification, development, and verification of cyber-physical systems
[
23
]
. These methods enable precise and unambiguous descriptions of system behavior, allowing designers to verify correctness and safety properties in the model prior to real-world deployment.

Recent FM research has extended those benefits to learning-based systems, where common techniques for establishing correct behavior include reachability analysis, model checking, and static property verification
[
24
,
25
]
. However, these FM techniques typically assume full access to a discrete, tractable model and struggle with the scale and complexity of learning-based controllers. Discrete abstractions, required for tractable analysis, lose meaningful expressiveness in continuous, highly-dimensional spaces, and are especially inadequate for capturing rich temporal sequence requirements of autonomous robots
[
26
,
27
]
. These FM techniques also fail to generate constructive feedback for retraining models or improving learned safety behaviors.

Attempting to address the temporal domain, recent FM techniques for verifying learning-based policies employ satisfiability solvers that encode property constraints as solvable logic formulas
[
28
]
, and optimization and search methods that find violating counterexamples
[
29
,
30
]
. Despite their effectiveness, these approaches face scalability challenges when applied to large neural networks, which are common in learning-based robotic systems.

Another subclass of FM techniques to verify control policies - classical and learning-based alike - is certificate-based verification methods, which have demonstrated reasonable scalability success for small- or medium-sized networks. These methods provide mathematical guarantees by constructing functions (ex: Lyapunov functions, barrier functions, or contraction metrics) that satisfy inequality bounds, ensuring system stability within certain regions in the state space
[
31
]
. Nevertheless, even certificate methods cannot scale to large networks and have poor robustness to unknown real-world uncertainties. Most importantly, across these approaches, the ability to naturally express temporal properties of trajectories - such as sequencing, persistence, or avoidance requirements over time - remains limited.

A particularly expressive formalism technique for specifying temporal behaviors of continuous-time, real-valued signals is Signal Temporal Logic (STL)
[
32
]
. STL defines system specifications as combinations of atomic predicates (e.g., inequalities on signal values), Boolean operators, and bounded temporal operators such as globally (
G
), eventually (
F
), and until (
U
). STL specifications are human-readable yet mathematically precise, and can be evaluated over system traces for compliance or violation. A key advantage of STL is its quantitative semantics, which assigns a robustness score indicating the degree to which a system trace satisfies or violates a specification
[
33
]
. A comprehensive discussion of STL can be found in
[
16
]
.

The fundamental gap is twofold: (1) existing FM techniques were devised for classical control systems where the model is fully known and tractable, not for black-box controllers with opaque internal logic; and (2) those FM techniques break down in the face of complex, continuous, and high-dimensional control tasks, where discretization destroys model expressiveness and leads to intractable state space explosion.

To the best of our knowledge, few prior works have attempted to bring STL-based verification into learning-enabled autonomous robot policies, particularly those realized as black-box neural networks. STL’s blend of human interpretability and formal rigor makes it a promising tool for specifying and quantifying temporal safety requirements for modern learning-based, black-box autonomous robots.

III
Approach

SAFE-SMART uses an iterative approach for evaluating the performance of a learning-based, black-box autonomous mobile robot against dynamic, human-defined safety rules. As shown in Figure

Designers
have access to the learning-based, black-box model and seek to ensure it satisfies the safety requirements defined by the regulators.
Regulators
, as members of the Test & Evaluation (T&E) community, oversee the verification process ensuring autonomous mobile robots comply with required safety and performance standards. These specifications may encompass safety, social compliance, mission objectives, or legal regulations, and are ultimately defined by domain-experts
[
34
,
35
]
. Our formulation assumes that the designers possess a pre-trained learning-based autonomous robot model ready for evaluation, while regulators have a clear understanding of the safety rules the robot must follow.

, we evaluate the compliance of learning-based controllers over rollout traces, and extract the robustness values for each rule. We use these robustness metrics to quantify the degree to which the system satisfies or violates the safety specification. The resulting information, indicating how well the system adheres to each requirement, is then communicated back to the model designers to guide further development and improvement of the autonomous robot system.

In our application of SAFE-SMART to autonomous mobile robot scenarios, the designer trained a reinforcement learning (RL) agent based on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a popular on-policy algorithm known for balancing training stability and sample efficiency
[
37
]
. PPO has been applied across domains ranging from robotic manipulation
[
38
]
and multi-agent AI games
[

]
to training language models
[
40

. For our scenarios, using existing baseline implementations of PPO
[

[

were sufficient. SAFE-SMART is agnostic to the specific black-box learning algorithm, and we therefore omit PPO implementation details for brevity. In practice, a wide range of learning-based methods could be treated as black-boxes, with the best choice determined by the complexity of the problem and its corresponding safety requirements.

The regulators subsequently collect a history of the model’s performance (Figure
3
). In our experiments, we collect 100 traces in simulation, a computationally feasible number for our verification software, where each rollout trace consists of the robot’s state trajectory over an entire episode. In practice, such trace data will often be gathered through simulation, but it may also be collected with real-world experiments if a controlled environment is available for testing. These traces are then evaluated for safety compliance.

Human-defined safety rules are initially articulated in natural language and subsequently translated into Signal Temporal Logic (STL) formulas. In our approach, this process is done manually, though recent work has shown progress in using large language models to convert natural language rules into formal temporal logic s
[
43
,
44
]
. We provide examples of such translations in Sections
IV-A
,
V-A
. Readers are referred to
[

]
for a comprehensive discussion of Signal Temporal Logic (STL) formulas.

Next, the collection of traces are verified against STL specifications using TeLEx
[
36
]
, a verification software. TeLEx identifies and reports instances where the specifications are violated, producing compliance metrics such as Boolean violation indicators and robustness scores, which intuitively quantifies the degree of satisfiability
[
33
]
. Robustness
\rho(\varphi,x)
for STL formula
\varphi
across the trace
x
reflects the degree of specification satisfaction, illustrated in Equation
1
:

\text{robustness}=\begin{cases}>0&\text{satisfies the specification robustly},\\
=0&\text{exactly satisfies the specification},\\
<0&\text{violates the specification}.\end{cases}

(1)

We aggregate robustness values using two metrics derived from
[
45
]
, the Total Robustness Voting (TRV) and Largest Robustness Voting (LRV). Here,
\Phi
denotes the set of all robustness values computed across all 100 rollout traces,

TRV=\sum_{\rho\in\Phi}{\rho(\varphi,x)},

(2)

LRV=\min_{\rho\in\Phi}\rho(\varphi,x).

(3)

The TRV metric reflects the overall aggregated safety margin across all rollout traces, providing a holistic measure of how well the model adhered to the safety specifications on average. A higher TRV indicates that most scenarios maintain a comfortable safety margin, suggesting overall system reliability under typical or normal operating conditions. In contrast, the LRV metric identifies the single most critical instance of rule violation among the rollout traces. It highlights the worst-case safety breach and indicates the greatest vulnerability of the system under specific conditions.

The compliance results inform model designers by guiding retraining or fine-tuning of the black-box model. Based on this feedback, the designer may opt to switch to a new model, or refine the existing one after updating the reward structure (as demonstrated in Sections
IV
and
V
). The updated model generates new rollout traces that are again evaluated by the regulator, suggesting an iterative cycle that continues until a desired compliance threshold is met. Specifications can be adjusted if desired, reflecting changing expectations or evolving requirements. The approach is depicted in Figure
3
.

To demonstrate the workflow’s generalizability and effectiveness, we apply SAFE-SMART to two distinct case studies related to autonomous systems: (1) a virtual racing game providing a controlled environment for safety analysis (Section
IV

V
). In each case study, a
Pre-Analysis model
was evaluated using SAFE-SMART. Based on the compliance metrics generated in the verification analysis, the reward structure was updated. The updated
Post-Analysis model
were again evaluated by SAFE-SMART to showcase better compliance to safety metrics using our approach.

The Virtual Driving Scenario uses
Mario Kart (SNES)

[

These features create a diverse set of unpredictable scenarios that challenge an autonomous agent’s ability to make rapid decisions and comply with human-defined specifications. Though a stylized simulation engine, Mario Kart provides a controlled and reproducible platform for evaluating autonomous decision-making under safety specifications.

The agent, using a stable-baseline implementation of PPO
[

VI
Conclusions, Limitations, and Future Work

SAFE-SMART presents a scalable, human-in-the-loop verification framework that bridges the gap between formal methods and practical evaluation of learning-based, black-box autonomous mobile robots. By incorporating domain experts as humans-in-the-verification-loop, oversight can be achieved without requiring expertise in formal methods. Expressing safety requirements in human-readable STL specifications provides a common language for domain experts and regulators. Further, SAFE-SMART operates on rollout traces, which bypasses scalability issues associated with directly verifying larger neural networks. Scalability is thus primarily determined by the length of rollout traces and the complexity of STL specifications.

A limitation of our approach is the sequential evaluative cycle between Designers and Regulators. Each cycle requires considerable time and computational resources to run rollout traces through the STL verification software, and to fine-tune the black-box models. Furthermore, the number of iterations needed before the black-box model reaches an acceptable state for safety analysis is difficult to predict.

Since SAFE-SMART uses the rollout traces for evaluation, the quality and coverage of the traces directly affect the quality of the evaluation. SAFE-SMART may not perform well with traces that have readings with large uncertainties, or with traces that do not adequately capture the underlying distribution of the environment. SAFE-SMART may not be well suited for evaluating safety specifications that rarely or never occur in rollout traces. Future work on ensuring complete coverage of rollout traces in representative environments may mitigate this issue. Additionally, future directions may consider methods to handle conflicting specifications by prioritizing or weighting different safety rules, enabling a more nuanced tradeoff resolution during analysis.
