Correction of iodine intake estimates from salt in Faroese dietary scenarios: implications for iodine fortification strategies
Authors: Herborg Líggjasardóttir Johannesen, Stig Andersen, Pál Weihe, Maria Skaalum Petersen, Marin Strøm, Claus Bogh Juhl, Anna Sofía Veyhe
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Extending our paper, The iodine content of Faroese food items to support iodine nutrition in the North Atlantic [1], we have identified an error in the handling of iodine in salt used in the dietary intake calculations presented in Table 1, and our adjustments may be potentially important for planning and monitoring of iodine fortification programmes. The issues relate to the reporting of the unit of iodine concentration in salt, the use of mean rather than median concentration of iodine in salt and the dual counting of iodine in salt in processed foods as well as in table salt. The present letter adds details on the methods, clarifies the modelling assumptions and adjusts Table 1 accordingly. While the initial conclusion remains unchanged, the lessons from these adjustments may be important for iodine fortification programmes in populations in the North Atlantic and globally.

Firstly, during the calculation of the iodine intake scenarios, the salt iodine concentration was expressed in µg/kg, resulting in a substantial underestimation of iodine intake from table salt. Secondly, the original report [1] listed the iodine concentration in salt with a mean of 24 µg/g and a range of 9–72 µg/g. The distribution was skewed to the right with a median of 11 µg/g. The present Table 2 shows details on the different salts included for blinded analysis, results of the blinded measurements performed twice and the iodine content of all samples. Thirdly, the iodine contribution from salt was reported based on an assumed table salt intake of 8 g/day. The estimated daily salt intake used in the dietary scenarios was too high as salt is already analytically represented in several food products included in the dietary scenarios, such as bread, butter and processed foods, as also illustrated by the iodine contents of these food items (Table 1). Retaining 8 g/day as additional salt would therefore result in double-counting of salt already present in analysed foods.

To address these issues, iodine intake scenarios have been recalculated (a) using the unit mg/kg, (b) using the median (11 mg/kg) rather than the mean (24 mg/kg) iodine concentration of table salt samples analysed (Table 2), and (c) using the revised estimate of table salt of 2 g/day, representing salt added during home cooking or at the table [2,3]. Under the latter assumption, table salt contributes approximately 22 µg iodine/day. The four dietary scenarios have been recalculated using the original food quantities and beverage intakes, with correction of the salt iodine concentration units and revision of estimated salt intake, and Table 1 adjusted accordingly.

The revised estimates illustrate the importance of including all salts in an iodine fortification programme, as adding iodine to table salt only contributes a mere 25% of the total daily intake from supplemented iodine. Hence, fortifying only table salt is likely to undermine iodine supplementation programmes.

In conclusion, the revision of iodine scenarios shows that iodine intake from table salt was underestimated and iodine intake from all salt was overestimated due to double-counting of salt already present in the analysed food items. As the median urinary iodine concentration in Faroese adults was around 100 µg/L [4–6], the interpretation of scenarios aligns with the ongoing dietary shift among populations in the North Atlantic and may support dietary recommendations. Finally and importantly, including only table salt in an iodine supplementation programme may cause a marked deficit in raising the iodine intake in a population, and the findings support the inclusion of all salts in iodine fortification programmes.
