FedERL: Federated Efficient and Robust Learning for Common Corruptions
Omar Bekdache, Naresh Shanbhag

Abstract
Federated learning (FL) accelerates the deployment of deep learning models on edge devices while preserving data privacy. However, FL systems face challenges due to client-side constraints on computational resources, and from a lack of robustness to common corruptions such as noise, blur, and weather effects. Existing robust training methods are computationally expensive and unsuitable for resource-constrained clients. We propose FedERL, federated efficient and robust learning, as the first work to explicitly address corruption robustness under time and energy constraints on the client side. At its core, FedERL employs a novel data-agnostic robust training (DART) method on the server to enhance robustness without access to the training data. In doing so, FedERL ensures zero robustness overhead for clients. Extensive experiments demonstrate FedERL’s ability to handle common corruptions at a fraction of the time and energy cost of traditional robust training methods. In scenarios with limited time and energy budgets, FedERL surpasses the performance of traditional robust training, establishing it as a practical and scalable solution for real-world FL applications.

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a popular machine learning paradigm that enables training on data available on edge devices in a privacy-preserving manner

)
and out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs

Background and Related Works

2.1
Federated Learning

In the original FL algorithm FedAvg
(
McMahan et al. 2017
)
, clients independently train local models on their private data and transmit model parameters to the server. The server aggregates these parameters and returns the updated global model back to the clients for further local training. More recent works study the impact of data-heterogeneity where client datasets follow different distributions, and propose various methods to mitigate the resulting performance drop
(
Karimireddy et al. 2020
;
Kim, Kim, and Han 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
. Energy efficiency has also emerged as a significant area of interest in FL research. For instance,
(
Khowaja et al. 2021
)
and
(
Kim and Wu 2021
)
explore energy-efficient solutions, while
(
Yang et al. 2020
)
presents an algorithm designed to minimize energy consumption in FL systems under latency constraints. However, none of these works address the problem of training robust models.
Our work is the first to train common corruption robust FL models under time and energy constraints
.

2.2
Robust Training for Common Corruptions

The most common approaches for training models robust to common corruptions augment the original training dataset by combining randomized image transformations and incorporate tailored loss functions
(
Hendrycks et al. 2019
;
Modas et al. 2022
)
. For instance,
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
utilizes an image-to-image network with randomly distorted parameters to generate augmented samples for training. However, these methods incur significant overhead (Fig.
1
) and require the original dataset, imposing undue burden on the clients in FL.
In contrast, our work (DART) employs a publicly available unlabeled dataset as a proxy in the server thereby reducing the computational burden on the clients while preserving data privacy
.

2.3
Common Corruptions Robust FL

FL under common corruptions has been investigated by
(
Li et al. 2022
;
Stripelis, Abram, and Ambite 2022
)
to address the problem of malicious clients injecting corrupted data during training to degrade model performance.
In contrast, we solve a different problem: improving robustness by generalizing to corruptions unseen at training time
. The method in
(
Fang, Ye, and Yang 2023
)
(AugHFL) ignores client-side computational resource constraints to improve model robustness by having clients implement AugMix
(
Hendrycks et al. 2019
)
.
In contrast, FedERL transfers robust training entirely to the server thereby minimizing the computational burden on the clients
.

Notations and Preliminaries

Setup:

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[C]
be a
C

f(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{C}
is used to denote the soft output of classifier
f
with parameters
\mathbf{w}
and input
\mathbf{x}
. The FL setup consists of
K
clients and one central server. Each client
c_{k}\in\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{K}\}
owns a private labeled dataset
D_{k}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{k},\mathbf{y}_{i}^{k})\}_{i=1}^{N_{k}}
consisting of
N_{k}
samples with
D_{k}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{in}}
. The server is equipped with a public unlabeled dataset
D_{0}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{0}\}_{i=1}^{N_{0}}
comprising
N_{0}
samples, which is out-of-distribution, i.e.,
D_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{out}}
.

Training:

Each client
c_{k}
trains a classifier
f(\mathbf{w}_{k})
. While the structure of the classifier is the same across all clients, the parameters
\mathbf{w}_{k}
vary as each client
c_{k}
trains locally on its respective dataset
D_{k}
. These parameters are periodically synchronized by the server during the global update phase, resulting in a global model
f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
. Synchronization occurs a total of
T_{\text{g}}
times, once every
T_{\text{l}}
epochs of local training.

Testing:

At test-time, client
c_{k}
encounters images that are ”clean” (
\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{in}}
) and ”corrupt” (
\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{corr}}
). We define the classification accuracy as
\mathcal{A}=\Pr(\hat{\mathbf{y}}=\mathbf{y})
, where
\hat{\mathbf{y}}=f(\mathbf{w}_{0},\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}})
. For samples
(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{in}}
, the accuracy is referred to as clean accuracy
\mathcal{A}_{\text{cln}}
, while for samples
(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{corr}}
, the accuracy is termed robust accuracy
\mathcal{A}_{\text{rob}}
.
The sample
(\mathbf{x}_{\text{corr}},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{corr}}
is generated from
(\mathbf{x}_{\text{in}},\mathbf{y})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{in}}
using the transformation
\mathbf{x}_{\text{corr}}=\kappa(\mathbf{x}_{\text{in}},s)
, where
\kappa
is a corruption filter and
s
is the severity level.

4
Proposed Method

In Section
4.1
, we introduce FedERL, our client-side resource efficient and robust FL algorithm. FedERL employs a novel data-agnostic robustness training (DART) method, described in
4.2
.

4.1
FedERL

In FedERL, clients focus solely on improving the predictive performance of their local models using their private data,
D_{k}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{in}}
, while the server is responsible for model aggregation and robust training.

Client-Side Training:

Each client
c_{k}
initializes its local model
f(\mathbf{w}_{k})
using the global model,
f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
, provided by the server. It then performs
T_{\text{l}}
epochs of clean training on its private dataset
D_{k}
by minimizing the cross-entropy loss. The updated model
f(\mathbf{w}_{k})
is then sent to the server. This process repeats in each communication round with the updated global model. Thus, unlike existing robust FL methods,
FedERL imposes no additional computation on clients
.

Server-Side Aggregation and Robustification:

After receiving the local models from clients, the server aggregates them using the standard FedAvg algorithm
(
McMahan et al. 2017
)
to obtain aggregated server model
f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
:

f(\mathbf{w}_{0})=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(\mathbf{w}_{i})

(1)

In standard FL, the aggregated model
f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
is immediately returned to clients for the next round of local training. In FedERL, after every
T_{\text{rob}}
global rounds, the server robust trains the aggregated model
f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
via the DART method (Section
4.2
), producing a robustness enhanced model
f(\mathbf{w}_{\text{rob}})
that is sent to clients. This is achieved using a public unlabeled dataset
D_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{out}}
, which is independent of the client’s data thereby ensuring privacy.

4.2
Data-Agnostic Robustness Training (DART)

Figure 3:
Diagram summarizing the data-agnostic robustness training (DART) method. Initially, the pre-trained model
f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
achieves high clean accuracy (
\mathcal{A}_{\text{cln}}
) but poor robustness (
\mathcal{A}_{\text{rob}}
). DART initializes both the teacher and student model parameters to
\mathbf{w}_{0}
and then minimizes
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DART}}
by updating the student model. After applying DART, the resulting model
f(\mathbf{w}_{\text{rob}})
achieves improved robustness while preserving clean accuracy of the original model
f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
.

To enable FedERL, we develop DART to preserve the privacy guarantee of FL by avoiding access to client data for robust training. Current approaches described in Section
2.2
, require the possession of data from the target learned data distribution
\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{in}}
, making them unsuitable for server-side deployment. In contrast, DART operates on a separate unlabeled dataset
D_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{out}}
, and transforms the aggregated model
f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
into a robust model
f(\mathbf{w}_{\text{rob}})
, while maintaining clean accuracy
\mathcal{A}_{\text{cln}}
and improving robust accuracy
\mathcal{A}_{\text{rob}}
. Even though DART operates on the server dataset
D_{0}\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{out}}
, all performance evaluations, in terms of clean accuracy (
\mathcal{A}_{\text{cln}}
) and robust accuracy (
\mathcal{A}_{\text{rob}}
), are conducted on samples drawn from
\mathcal{D}_{\text{in}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{corr}}
, respectively. While DART is designed to enhance robustness in FL, its applicability extends to any scenario where access to the original training data is restricted. Such situations may arise due to privacy constraints, prohibitive transmission costs of large datasets, or intellectual property restrictions.

DART Loss Function:

The DART loss is defined as a weighted sum of two components,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{d}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\text{c}}
, each representing a learned objective:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DART}}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{d}}+\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\text{c}}

(2)

where
\alpha
is a hyperparameter controlling the ratio of both losses. When performing DART, the following optimization formulation is used to update the server model:

f(\mathbf{w}_{\text{rob}})=\argmin_{f(\mathbf{w})}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DART}}(f(\mathbf{w}))

(3)

The consistency loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{c}}
promotes output similarity for semantically identical images under different perturbations. The distillation loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{d}}
ensures that the robust model
f(\mathbf{w}_{\text{rob}})
retains the clean accuracy
\mathcal{A}_{\text{cln}}
of the aggregated model
f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
. Together, these losses guide the model to better predict corrupted inputs
\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{corr}}
while preserving performance on clean data
\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{in}}
, resulting in an accurate and robust model.

For DART training and validation, the public dataset
D_{0}
is partitioned into disjoint subsets:
D_{\text{tr}}
for training and
D_{\text{val}}
for validation. We apply early stopping based on the validation loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{DART}}
computed on
D_{\text{val}}
. Next, we detail each loss component computed using the proxy dataset
D_{0}
.

Enhancing Consistency:

From each training input
\mathbf{x}_{\text{tr}}\in D_{0}
, two augmented samples,
\mathbf{x}_{\text{tr1}}
and
\mathbf{x}_{\text{tr2}}
, are generated using the AugMix
(
Hendrycks et al. 2019
)
data augmentation scheme, described in the appendix. These three images are passed through the model
f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
to produce the predictions
\mathbf{p}_{\text{tr}}
,
\mathbf{p}_{\text{tr1}}
, and
\mathbf{p}_{\text{tr2}}
. Using these predictions, we compute the consistency loss as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{c}}=\operatorname{JS}\left(\mathbf{p}_{\text{tr}},\mathbf{p}_{\text{tr1}},\mathbf{p}_{\text{tr2}}\right)

(4)

where
\operatorname{JS}
denotes Jensen-Shannon divergence, a symmetric and smooth variant of the Kullback–Leibler (
\operatorname{KL}
) divergence. Minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{c}}
by updating the model’s parameters steers the model towards similar predictions for clean and augmented versions of the same input, aligning the model’s predictions for images representing the same information. However, optimizing solely for
\mathcal{L}_{\text{c}}
degrades both
\mathcal{A}_{\text{cln}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\text{rob}}
, as it does not preserve the model’s ability to make accurate predictions.

Maintaining Accuracy:

To address this issue, DART incorporates a second loss,
\mathcal{L}_{\text{d}}
, to retain the model’s predictive knowledge. We adopt a teacher-student framework, where the teacher model is fixed as the aggregated pre-trained model,
f_{\text{t}}(\mathbf{w}_{t})=f(\mathbf{w}_{0})
, and the student model,
f_{\text{s}}(\mathbf{w}_{s})
, is updated from
\mathbf{w}_{0}
to
\mathbf{w}_{\text{rob}}
. The consistency loss
\mathcal{L}_{\text{c}}
is evaluated using the student model. The input
\mathbf{x}_{\text{tr}}
is passed through both teacher and student models to generate predictions,
\mathbf{p}_{\text{t}}
and
\mathbf{p}_{\text{s}}
. The distillation loss is then computed as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{d}}=\operatorname{KL}\left(\mathbf{p}_{\text{t}}\parallel\mathbf{p}_{\text{s}}\right)

(5)

Minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{d}}
encourages the student model to closely match the predictions of the fixed pre-trained teacher model. This ensures that the student preserves the aggregated model’s clean accuracy. The overall DART procedure is illustrated in Fig.

6
Conclusion

We introduced FedERL, the first federated efficient and robust learning system. It enhances model robustness while preserving data privacy and maintaining client-side resource efficiency. Extensive experiments demonstrate that FedERL enhances robustness with minimal drop in clean accuracy and performs well under limited time and energy budgets. This contrasts with conventional robust FL methods, which, under stringent resource constraints, exhibit both clean and robust accuracies that are lower than those achieved by standard non-robust training. Key to FedERL’s success is a data-agnostic robustness training method, DART, which improves the robustness of any pre-trained non-robust model without requiring access to the original dataset. Under time and energy constraints, FedERL offers a practical and scalable solution for real-world FL applications.

Appendix A
Data Augmentation

The DART method adopts the standard AugMix
(
Hendrycks et al. 2019
)
augmentation strategy, which stochastically mixes diverse transformation operations such as posterize, equalize, rotate, translate, and shear. This procedure introduces multiple sources of randomness, including the selection of operations, their severity levels, the depth of transformation chains, and mixing weights.

First, three operations,
\text{op}_{1},\text{op}_{2},\text{and }\text{op}_{3}
, are sampled from the set of available operations,
\mathcal{O}
. The sampled operations are sequentially composed to generate transformation chains of increasing depth:

\text{op}_{12}=\text{op}_{2}\circ\text{op}_{1}\text{, and }\text{op}_{123}=\text{op}_{3}\circ\text{op}_{2}\circ\text{op}_{1}

(6)

Then one chain is sampled uniformly at random from
\{\text{op}_{1},\text{op}_{12},\text{op}_{123}\}
. This process is repeated
S
times, where
S
is a hyperparameter denoting mixture width, resulting in
S
operation chains
\{\text{chain}_{i}\}^{S}_{i=1}
.
S
is set to 3 by default
(
Hendrycks et al. 2019
)
.

The augmented sample
\mathbf{x}_{\text{aug}}
is generated from a sample
\mathbf{x}
using:

\mathbf{x}_{\text{aug}}=\eta\mathbf{x}+(1-\eta)\sum_{i=1}^{S}m_{i}\times\text{chain}_{i}(\mathbf{x})

(7)

where
(m_{1}\dots m_{i})
and
\eta
are random weights sampled from
\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha\dots\alpha)
and from
\text{Beta}(\alpha,\alpha)
distributions, respectively. As can be seen, the augmentation procedure mixes the output of the operation chains. It also includes direct contribution from the original image
\mathbf{x}
to prevent the loss of sematic information.

This diverse augmentation process is designed to improve the robustness of machine learning models by exposing them to a wide range of data during training.
