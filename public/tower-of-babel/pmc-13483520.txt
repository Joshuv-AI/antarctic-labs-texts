Impact of residual cholesterol and inflammation risk on non-culprit plaque progression in patients with acute coronary syndrome: a serial optical coherence tomography study
Authors: Dirui Zhang, Luping He, Boling Yi, Yishuo Xu, Chen Zhao, Ming Zeng, Yuhan Qin, Ziqian Weng, Ning Wang, Xue Feng, Lulu Li, Yini Wang, Jingbo Hou, Gary S Mintz, Sining Hu, Haibo Jia, Bo Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) patients face recurrent cardiovascular events due to non-culprit plaque progression despite optimal secondary prevention, but the differential impacts of residual cholesterol risk (RCR, on-treatment LDL-C ≥ 1.8mmol/L) and residual inflammation risk (RIR, on-treatment hs-CRP ≥2 mg/L) on plaque microstructure dynamics remain undefined. This study aimed to clarify how residual cholesterol and inflammatory risk factors differentially modulate the progression of non-culprit plaques in patients with ACS using serial optical coherence tomography (OCT).MethodsA retrospective cohort of 243 ACS patients underwent baseline and 1-year (9–15 months) follow-up OCT. Patients were stratified into four groups (RCR, RIR, combined residual cholesterol-inflammation risk [RCIR], no residual risk). OCT assessed plaque morphology (e.g., thin-cap fibroatheroma [TCFA]) and quantitative parameters (lumen area, fibrous cap thickness [FCT], lipid arc). Multivariate logistic regression identified factors associated with plaque progression (defined as a decrease in minimal lumen area > 0.5 mm² from baseline to follow-up) and TCFA persistent.ResultsOf 583 evaluable non-culprit lesions, RIR was independently associated with patient-level plaque progression (odds ratio [OR]: 2.915, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.005–9.212, P = 0.046) and was linked to significant reductions in reference, mean, and minimal lumen area (all P < 0.05) vs. the no-risk group. RCR was associated with attenuated lipid plaque regression compared with the no residual risk group, as evidenced by smaller min-FCT increases (20.0 [-13.3, 73.3] µm vs. 50.0 [18.3, 86.7] µm, P = 0.014) and attenuated mean lipid arc reductions (-8.8 ± 27.1° vs. -17.7 ± 23.6°, P = 0.027). RCR (OR: 4.462, 95%CI: 1.723–12.521, P = 0.003) and RCIR (OR: 4.892, 95%CI: 1.623–15.467, P = 0.005) were independently associated with follow-up TCFA persistent.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated distinct associations of RIR and RCR with non-culprit plaque progression: RIR was linked to luminal narrowing, while RCR attenuated lipid plaque stabilization. These findings provide imaging-based insights into plaque dynamics and may inform future risk stratification and residual risk management strategies in ACS patients.Graphical abstract This study evaluated how residual cholesterol (RCR) and inflammation risk (RIR) affect non-culprit plaque progression in 243 acute coronary syndrome (ACS) patients (583 non-culprit plaques) via 1-year serial optical coherence tomography (OCT) follow-up. Patients were stratified into four groups: RIR (21 patients, LDL-C<1.8 mmol/L and hs-CRP≥2 mg/L), RCR (75 patients, LDL-C≥1.8 mmol/L and hs-CRP<2 mg/L), dual residual risk (RCIR, 45 patients), and no residual risk (102 patients). Introduction

Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) remains a leading cause of global cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, posing a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide [1]. Despite remarkable advances in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and evidence-based secondary prevention strategies—including intensive lipid-lowering therapy and dual antiplatelet treatment—ACS patients still experience major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), highlighting the persistence of significant residual risk [2]. Mounting evidence indicates that the progression of non-culprit plaque—atherosclerotic lesions unrelated to the index ischemic event—represents a key driver of these recurrent events [3, 4]. The pathophysiological basis of atherosclerosis progression involves complex interactions between metabolic dysregulation and chronic inflammation. However, a substantial proportion of patients still have either residual cholesterol risk (RCR, defined as on-treatment LDL-C ≥ 1.8mmol/L) or residual inflammation risk (RIR, defined as on-treatment hs-CRP≥2 mg/L) [5, 6].

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is an intravascular imaging modality that enables accurate in vivo assessment of the characteristics of atherosclerotic plaques. Specifically, OCT can detect thin-cap fibroatheromas (TCFA), which are generally recognized as the main morphology defining high-risk plaques [7–9]. Additionally, it can also perform longitudinal tracking of the dynamic changes in lesions, thereby providing unique insights into the plaque progression or regression mechanisms of lesions [10, 11].

However, most existing data are cross-sectional, lacking longitudinal evidence on how the “lipid-inflammation” axis modulates non-culprit plaque microstructural changes over time [5, 6, 12]. Addressing this gap is essential for refining risk stratification and developing targeted interventions for ACS patients with residual risk.

Against this backdrop, the present study aimed to investigate the combined effect of residual cholesterol and inflammation risk on non-culprit plaque progression in ACS patients using serial OCT imaging.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study retrospectively recruited ACS patients who underwent both baseline and 1-year (9–15 months) follow-up OCT examinations at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Harbin, China) between September 2013 and June 2021. The main inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) primary diagnosis of ACS, including ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-ST-segment elevation acute coronary syndromes (NSTE-ACS) [2]; and (2) presence of de novo non-culprit lesions. Exclusion criteria included: (1) suboptimal OCT image quality (e.g., significant artifact or poor blood clearance) or massive thrombus obscuring plaque assessment; (2) insufficient pullback length (< 20 mm) or inability to achieve reliable image coregistration between baseline and follow-up examinations; (3) absence of analyzable non-culprit plaques in the major epicardial coronary arteries; (4) absence of hs-CRP or LDL-C results.

Patients were categorized into four groups according to the levels of LDL-C and hs-CRP measured during follow-up treatment [6, 13]:Residual cholesterol risk (RCR): LDL-C≥1.8mmol/L and hs-CRP <2mg/L;Residual inflammatory risk (RIR): LDL-C<1.8 mmol/L and hs-CRP≥2mg/L;Residual cholesterol and residual inflammation risk (RCIR): LDL-C≥1.8mmol/L and hs-CRP≥2mg/L;No residual risk (None): LDL-C<1.8mmol/L and hs-CRP<2mg/L.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (YJSKY2024-333).

Clinical and laboratory data collection

Baseline and 1-year follow-up clinical data were retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Collected information included demographic characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors, medical history, laboratory test results, and details of medication use (including the type and dosage) during follow-up. Fasting blood samples were collected during the index hospitalization and at follow-up. All laboratory parameters were measured using standard hospital assays. Detailed definitions have been provided in the Supplemental Material.

OCT image acquisition and analysis

OCT imaging was performed using a commercially available C7/ILUMIEN system (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, California). All OCT images were submitted to the Intravascular Imaging and Physiology Core Lab of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. Analyses were performed offline using a dedicated review workstation (Abbott Vascular) for OCT analyses by two independent investigators blinded to clinical and follow-up data. Any discordance was resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. The intra- and inter-observer agreements for identifying TCFA and microstructures were substantial, as described in our previously published paper [14].

The culprit lesion was identified by integrating electrocardiographic, coronary angiographic, and OCT findings. In patients with multiple stenoses, the lesion with the most severe stenosis or with evidence of acute thrombus on OCT was considered to be the culprit plaque. After successful intervention of the culprit lesion (including stent and 5-mm proximal and distal edges), the management of non-culprit lesions was at the discretion of the interventional cardiologist, and lesions scheduled for staged revascularization were excluded.

Non-culprit plaques were identified by OCT as untreated coronary segments with luminal narrowing and loss of normal vascular wall architecture (qualitative assessment of loss of normal vascular wall architecture with inward remodeling of the lumen contour on OCT) [7]. Plaques were considered separate if there was a 5 mm relatively disease-free segment between them. Baseline and follow-up OCT images were matched using multiple anatomical landmarks, including side branches, calcification deposits, coronary ostia, and stent edges (for lesions in treated vessels). Only lesions with successful coregistration were included for longitudinal analysis.

According to their composition, non-culprit plaques are classified into lipid-rich plaque (LRP), fibrous plaques, and calcified plaques. Quantitative and qualitative analyses of plaques were conducted in accordance with previously established criteria and consensus guidelines, and the detailed procedures have been described in the Supplemental Material [15].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was luminal narrowing, defined as a decrease in OCT-measured MLA > 0.5 mm² from baseline to one-year follow-up. This threshold was chosen based on previous studies of intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) and angiography that defined progression as exceeding twice the standard deviation of measurement error, thus accounting for variability introduced by repeated imaging and measurements [10, 16]. With regard to optical coherence tomography (OCT) specifically, previous studies have reported that the intra-observer measurement error of minimal lumen area (MLA) is 0.04 ± 0.19 mm², while the limits of agreement for MLA between different pullback procedures range from − 0.58 mm² to 0.48 mm² [17, 18]. The > 0.5 mm² threshold aligns with these data.

Patient-level progression was defined as the presence of at least one qualifying plaque exhibiting plaque progression (ΔMLA > 0.5 mm²). For lipid-rich plaques, changes in plaque composition (lipid arc, length) and min-FCT were analyzed longitudinally.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous data were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and non-normally distributed variables as median (interquartile range [IQR]). For patient-level data, Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, and the Pearson chi-squared test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. For lesion-level data, generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable correlation structure were used to account for the potential clustering effect of multiple non-culprit plaques within the same patient.

Multivariable logistic regression models were employed to identify factors associated with plaque progression and the persistent of TCFA at follow-up, with adjusting for potential clinical and imaging confounders. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. Interactions between key variables (residual risk, sex, and diabetes) were tested. Intra- and inter-observer agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

This longitudinal cohort study initially included 365 ACS patients who presented between September 2013 and June 2021. All participants underwent serial OCT imaging at baseline and during follow-up (9–15 months) to assess changes in coronary luminal morphology (MLA) in non-culprit lesions. Following exclusion criteria (detailed in Fig. 1), 122 patients were excluded. The final analytical cohort comprised 243 patients with 583 evaluable non-culprit lesions. Using protocol-defined biomarker thresholds during follow-up, patients were stratified into four groups based on residual cholesterol and inflammatory risk. The distribution of patients and lesions across these phenotypic categories has been presented in Fig. 1.

Phenotype-specific biomarker patterns were clinically significant. RIR patients showed markedly elevated baseline inflammatory burden evidenced by a median hs-CRP of 8.38 mg/L [IQR 3.66, 12.08] vs. 4.28 mg/L [1.40, 11.52] in None (P = 0.036), despite comparable lipid parameters. RCR and RCIR groups exhibited pronounced dyslipidemia with significantly higher LDL-C (3.29 ± 1.07 and 3.32 ± 1.08mmol/L vs. 2.61 ± 0.78mmol/L in None; overall P < 0.001) and ApoB levels (1.11 ± 0.29 g/L and 1.11 ± 0.33 g/L vs. 0.91 ± 0.22 g/L; P < 0.001). Notably, culprit plaque localization and morphological characteristics assessed by OCT were comparable across all groups (all P > 0.05). Pharmacotherapy analysis demonstrated near-universal implementation of secondary prevention.

Baseline OCT characteristics of non-culprit plaques

Baseline OCT characterization of the non-culprit plaques stratified by residual risk phenotypes has been systematically detailed in Table 2. Overall, 242 plaques (41.5%) were located within the culprit vessel, with plaque distribution patterns demonstrating phenotypic homogeneity in the epicardial coronary arteries. Coronary artery distribution showed comparable prevalence among groups (P = 0.397). Plaque morphological classifications revealed consistent distributions of different subtypes without significant phenotypic differences (P = 0.698). Comprehensive quantitative analysis further confirmed phenotypic similarity at baseline.

Discussion

This study enrolled 243 ACS patients and used baseline and 1‑year follow‑up OCT to evaluate how residual inflammatory and cholesterol risks affect non‑culprit plaque progression. The principal findings are as follows: (1) Residual inflammatory and cholesterol risks remained common in ACS patients despite 1‑year statin and standard secondary prevention. (2) Residual inflammatory risk was associated with luminal narrowing: Residual inflammatory risk was independently associated with luminal narrowing (MLA reduction > 0.5 mm²) at the patient level. (3) Residual cholesterol risk attenuated lipid plaque regression (evidenced by reduced min-FCT increase and lipid core arc reduction) and was independently associated with TCFA persistent in non-culprit plaques during follow-up.

Clinical significance of residual risk persistence

The findings of this study confirm that despite the widespread application of contemporary guideline-directed therapy based on statins, a substantial proportion of ACS patients still exhibit persistent residual risks. Specifically, 58.0% of the 243 enrolled patients had either RCR, RIR, or both (RCIR), with only 42.0% achieving the dual target of LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L and hs-CRP < 2 mg/L. This is consistent with the results of landmark trials such as PROVE-IT and IMPROVE-IT, where the proportion of patients achieving dual residual risk-free targets was 44% and 39%, respectively [19, 20]. However, this proportion is notably higher than the 14% reported in the U.S. VIRGO registry, which specifically focuses on young patients with myocardial infarction [21]. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors: first, the patients enrolled in this study were mainly recruited from the EROSION study series and other prospective cohort studies [22, 23]. All these studies adopted the standardized follow-up protocol developed in the present research, which ensured more consistent medication adherence among patients and standardized medical guidance; second, the inclusion of patients from a single center with uniform clinical practice standards, reducing variability in treatment delivery; and third, differences in patient demographics (e.g., age, comorbidity profiles) between the cohorts.

The high prevalence of residual risk underscores the limitations of current single-target therapeutic strategies. For example, in lipid management, current single-target approaches primarily focus on LDL-C reduction, yet even with the addition of novel agents such as PCSK9 inhibitors or ezetimibe to statin therapy, a subset of patients remains unable to reach optimal LDL-C levels. More importantly, in patients who have achieved the target LDL-C level, the driving factors of recurrent atherosclerotic cardiovascular events are speculated to be more closely associated with innate and adaptive immune responses [12, 24]. These patients are defined as having “residual inflammatory risk”, which is reflected by elevated hs-CRP levels. Notably, such patients may not obtain incremental benefits from further lipid-lowering therapy; instead, anti-inflammatory therapy represents a promising strategy a novel strategy for reducing residual risk in this patient population [25]. This suggests that residual risk is a multifactorial construct, and these findings provide mechanistic insights into the roles of lipid and inflammatory pathways in plaque stability.

Differential impacts of RIR and RCR on plaque progression

A key novel insight from this study is the distinct roles of RIR and RCR in modulating non-culprit plaque luminal changes and lipid core stability. At the patient level, RIR was independently associated with significant luminal narrowing. Patients with RIR exhibited significant reductions in RLA, mean LA, and MLA at 1-year follow-up compared to the “No residual risk” group—changes that were not observed in patients with RCR alone. This aligns with recent pathophysiological understanding: inflammation drives atherosclerotic progression primarily through promoting vascular wall remodeling, including medial hypertrophy, endothelial dysfunction, and the accumulation of inflammatory cells (e.g., macrophages) in the vessel wall [26, 27]. These processes lead to a reduction in luminal area over time, even in the absence of significant lipid core expansion—a critical distinction, as luminal narrowing may occur due to vascular remodeling independent of plaque volume increase. Previous studies have confirmed that the high expression of hs-CRP is closely correlated with the high incidence of adverse cardiovascular clinical events and the phenotypic characteristics of vulnerable plaques (e.g., TCFA and intraplaque macrophage infiltration) [28, 29]. However, relevant existing studies have not effectively eliminated the confounding interference of blood lipid metabolism indicators. In the present study, an independent stratified analysis of inflammatory factor effects was performed, which further clarified and illustrated the independent mechanism and regulatory value of inflammatory responses in the progressive destabilization of coronary artery plaques.

Notably, the RCIR group did not show greater luminal narrowing than the RIR group, which may be attributed to several factors. First, statins (used in all groups) have pleiotropic anti-inflammatory effects [30], which may mitigate the pro-atherosclerotic effect of hs-CRP in patients with RCR. Second, the small sample size of the RCIR group (n = 45) may limit statistical power to detect an additive effect. Third, potential counterbalancing biological mechanisms (e.g., lipid-lowering reducing plaque volume while inflammation promotes remodeling) could offset each other, leading to no significant incremental luminal narrowing.

In contrast, RCR exerted its effects primarily on plaque composition and stability rather than luminal stenosis. Patients with RCR (or RCIR) showed attenuated regression of lipid-rich plaques. Furthermore, multivariate analysis identified RCR and RCIR as independent predictors of TCFA persistent at follow-up. TCFA are well-recognized high-risk atherosclerotic plaques, characterized by a thin fibrous cap (≤ 65 μm) and a large lipid core. They are strongly associated with future plaque rupture and subsequent acute coronary events [31]. Lipid-lowering therapy directly targets the two critical structural features that render TCFAs prone to rupture: it reduces the volume of the underlying lipid core and may stabilize or thicken the thin fibrous cap—a key hallmark of TCFA vulnerability.

Implications for therapeutic strategy

The differential associations of RIR and RCR with plaque dynamics provide mechanistic insights for future research. For patients with RIR, targeted anti-inflammatory pathways may be explored as potential intervention targets. Low-dose colchicine is a promising option in this context: recent trials such as COLCOT and LoDoCo2 have demonstrated that colchicine reduces the risk of MACE in patients with ACS or chronic coronary disease by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation, a key driver of vascular inflammation [32, 33]. Notably, colchicine exhibits cost-effectiveness and favorable tolerability, rendering it a feasible adjunct to standard therapy. Furthermore, the 2023 Guidelines of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) explicitly state that the cardiovascular event-reducing benefit of colchicine has been verified as consistent, irrespective of whether patients have a history of prior ACS or the timing of such an event [2]. Other anti-inflammatory agents also hold potential for RIR management. For instance, canakinumab—a therapeutic monoclonal antibody targeting interleukin-1β (IL-1β)—was confirmed in the Canakinumab Anti-inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study (CANTOS) to reduce the risk of cardiovascular diseases independently of lipid levels [34]. However, despite the successful inhibition of IL-1β activity by canakinumab, patients still exhibit residual inflammatory risk associated with interleukin-18 and interleukin-6 [35]. These findings support the further development of atherosclerotic thrombosis therapies, including those targeting IL-18 or IL-6 signaling pathways. Such therapies may provide additional treatment options for patients’ intolerant to colchicine.

For patients with RCR, lipid-lowering pathways may be relevant for plaque stability. Potential therapeutic strategies include escalating the statin dose (if well tolerated), adding ezetimibe (a cholesterol absorption inhibitor), or initiating PCSK9 inhibitors (either monoclonal antibodies or small interfering RNAs). Notably, 2023 ESC Guidelines for the Management of ACS explicitly recommend PCSK9 inhibitors for very high-risk ACS patients (e.g., those with recurrent cardiovascular events, comorbid diabetes mellitus, or multivessel coronary artery disease) who fail to achieve target LDL-C levels despite optimal statin and ezetimibe therapy [2]. Additionally, emerging therapeutic agents targeting lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] or angiopoietin-like protein 3 (ANGPTL3) have shown potential to address residual lipid-related risk in patients with refractory hypercholesterolemia. However, the efficacy of these agents in promoting atherosclerotic plaque stabilization remains to be further verified through serial imaging-based clinical studies.

For patients with RCIR (both RIR and RCR), a dual-target strategy combining intensified lipid-lowering and anti-inflammatory therapy may be a direction for future research. Future large-scale studies are needed to evaluate whether dual therapy provides incremental benefit in this high-risk subgroup.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this study was conducted over an extended period (2013–2021), during which treatment guidelines evolved. The lack of contemporary adjunctive therapies (e.g., PCSK9 inhibitors, colchicine) in this cohort may affect the prevalence and impact of residual risks as defined here, and limits generalizability to current practice. Second, the observational design cannot exclude residual confounding by unmeasured or imprecisely measured factors, such as dietary habits, physical activity, and medication adherence. Third, 1-year serial OCT captures only short-term plaque dynamics, leaving longer-term morphological changes uncharacterized. Fourth, the detection results of biomarkers at a single time point may fail to reflect the temporal fluctuations of residual dyslipidemia and inflammatory risks during the follow-up period. These phenotypes may reflect not only underlying biology but also the intensity of and adherence to medical therapy during the follow-up interval, limiting causal inference. Fifth, OCT analysis was restricted to select non-culprit lesions, excluding the entire coronary tree and potentially causing selection bias. Finally, the lack of clinical outcome data precludes establishing a direct link between the observed OCT changes and hard cardiovascular events. Larger prospective studies with objective adherence monitoring and long-term clinical follow-up are warranted.

Conclusions

In ACS patients receiving contemporary guideline-directed therapy, serial OCT imaging revealed differential correlations between biomarker-defined residual risk phenotypes (residual cholesterol risk and residual inflammatory risk) and short-term morphological changes in non-culprit plaques. These mechanistic findings provide imaging-based insights into plaque dynamics and may guide future investigations into residual risk management.
