Potential return of spontaneous circulation in Japanese out-of-hospital cardiac arrest patients with pseudo–pulseless electrical activity: a post hoc analysis of a prospective multicentric study

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAs of yet, there are few studies of Asian patients with pseudo-pulseless electrical activity (PEA). Japanese patients with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) tend to be elderly, experience cardiac arrest while at home, and present with severe illness. The present study evaluated the prognostic implications of pseudo-PEA in a Japanese cohort with a high risk of OHCA.MethodsThe present study is a post hoc analysis of SOS-KANTO 2017, a prospective, multicentric study which included OHCA patients with PEA on hospital arrival who underwent echocardiographic assessment during resuscitation. The patients were categorized into a pseudo-PEA and a true PEA group. The primary outcome was the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). The secondary outcomes included survival to hospital admission, 30-day survival, and a favorable neurological status at discharge. Logistic regression was performed with cardiac activity as the primary predictor after adjusting for covariates.ResultsOf 894 eligible patients, 345 had pseudo-PEA, and 549 had true PEA. In total, 49.3% achieved ROSC. The pseudo-PEA group had a significantly higher rate of ROSC (87.2% vs. 25.5%; p < 0.01). On multivariable logistic regression, the pseudo-PEA group was independently associated with a high ROSC rate (odds ratio [OR]: 22.1; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 14.2–34.3), survival to hospital admission (OR: 17.8; 95% CI: 11.2–28.2), 30-day survival (OR: 51.9; 95% CI: 7.33–367), and favorable neurological outcome at discharge (OR: 14.3; 95% CI: 1.62–125).ConclusionPseudo-PEA may be associated with a higher rate of ROSC and favorable, short and middle-term outcomes in Japanese patients with OHCA. Early echocardiographic assessment of cardiac activity may help prognostication and guide resuscitation even in high-risk populations.

Introduction

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is a life-threating medical emergency having a global annual incidence of approximately 96 per 100,000 population.1 Despite progress in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and post-resuscitation care, the survival rate remains low; globally, only 10–11% of patients survive 30 days or achieve hospital discharge with a favorable neurological outcome (Cerebral Performance Categories 1–2).2

In recent years, point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has emerged as an invaluable, diagnostic tool in emergency medicine enabling rapid, bedside acquisition and interpretation of imaging findings. Its role has also expanded to the management of OHCA, where promptly identifying reversible causes can be crucial to guiding resuscitation and improving patients’ outcomes.3, 4, 5, 6 In particular, POCUS can help distinguish pseudo-pulseless electrical activity (PEA) from true PEA. The former involves cardiac wall motion on echocardiography despite the absence of a palpable pulse in a patient with PEA and was operationally defined in this study as a condition manifesting cardiac wall motion on echocardiography during resuscitation in the absence of a palpable pulse. True PEA was defined as the absence of cardiac wall motion in the same clinical context. Ultrasound may also detect faint, residual, myocardial contractions indicating that the heart is not completely inert and that the patient may have a good chance of achieving return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) if given appropriate resuscitation. True PEA is associated with a very low likelihood of ROSC and thus obviates the need for aggressive intervention.7, 8

Previous studies of pseudo-PEA in non-Asian patients suffered from the lack of an adequate sample population.7, 9, 10, 11, 12 Furthermore, the epidemiological profile of OHCA in Japan differs substantially from that in Western countries: (1) more than 80% of OHCA patients in Japan are aged 65 years or older13; (2) the majority experience cardiac arrest at home14; and (3) Japanese emergency medical services often transport severely ill patients who may not be ideal candidates for resuscitation or transport elsewhere.15 These demographic and clinical factors contribute to forming a patient cohort with greater, overall illness severity. Given the limited evidence on pseudo-PEA in Asians and the unique characteristics of OHCA in Japan, the present study aimed to evaluate the prognostic implications of pseudo-PEA in this distinct, high-risk population.

Methods

Study design and setting

The present study is a post hoc secondary analysis of data derived from the Survey of Survivors after Cardiac Arrest in the Kanto Area (SOS-KANTO) 2017 Study Group, which was conducted by the Kanto division of the Japanese Association of Acute Care Medicine (JAAM-KANTO) between September 2019 and March 2021. The SOS-KANTO 2017 study was a prospective, multicentric, observational study of patients with OHCA living in the Kanto region of Japan. Established in 2002, the SOS-KANTO study group has conducted a series of prospective, observational studies with preregistered hypotheses in order to clarify various, clinical issues related to OHCA. The data provided by these studies have advanced our understanding of OHCA, improved patient outcomes, and informed clinical practices and policies, particularly those pertaining to out-of-hospital cardiac arrest management.16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review board of all 42 participating institutions (approval number: 2-117). The requirement for informed consent was waived because the data were anonymized.

The post hoc analysis addressing the association between cardiac activity on echocardiography during cardiopulmonary resuscitation and clinical outcomes was conceived after completion of the original SOS-KANTO 2017 data collection and was not prespecified in the original study protocol. No additional echocardiographic examinations were performed for the purpose of this post hoc analysis. The study relied on prospectively collected data from the original SOS-KANTO 2017 database.

Study participants

This study included patients with OHCA who presented to the emergency department (ED) with ongoing CPR, had PEA as the initial documented rhythm, and underwent cardiac echocardiography during resuscitation. Cardiac arrest was defined as the absence of mechanical cardiac activity as confirmed by the lack of clinical signs of circulation. None of the patients had achieved ROSC prior to the echocardiographic assessment.

Data collection and definitions

Out-of-hospital data were prospectively collected by emergency medical services (EMS) personnel using the Utstein reporting template. The following, prehospital variables were recorded: age, sex, and out-of-hospital information, including location at the time of cardiac arrest, witnessed status, presence of bystander(s), and the initial cardiac rhythm on EMS arrival. In-hospital data were collected at the participating institutions using the SOS-KANTO 2017 registry case report form, a template comprising predefined, structured items designed for the collection of clinical data unavailable in the prehospital, Utstein-based dataset, including patient demographics, clinical findings on hospital arrival, results of laboratory tests, resuscitation-related interventions, and echocardiographic findings during resuscitation.22 Echocardiography was performed after hospital arrival and during CPR by emergency physicians as part of their clinical practice. No standardized imaging protocol was implemented across the institutions, and only the presence or absence of cardiac wall motion was documented.

In the SOS-KANTO 2017 case report form, echocardiographic findings were categorized as: (1) not performed; (2) cardiac wall motion present; (3) no cardiac wall motion detected; and (4) unknown or other. Based on these predefined categories, patients were divided into a (I) pseudo-PEA and (II) true PEA group. The former was defined as a condition involving cardiac wall motion on echocardiography during resuscitation in the absence of a palpable pulse, and true PEA was defined as a condition without cardiac wall motion in the same clinical context. Patients without echocardiographic assessment or with missing data or other classifications were excluded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was ROSC in the ED. The secondary outcomes included survival to hospital admission, survival at 30 days, and a favorable neurological status at hospital discharge, defined as a Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) score of 1 or 2.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as the mean and the standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were expressed as a number and a percentage. Baseline differences were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test or Student’s t-test.

To evaluate the association between pseudo-PEA and the clinical outcomes, multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to obtain odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Cardiac activity on echocardiography was included as the primary explanatory variable. Based on previous studies and clinical relevance, the models were adjusted a priori for age (>65 years), sex, witnessed arrest, bystander CPR, initial shockable rhythm, adrenaline administration, transient ROSC during transport, transport time (<30 min), initial pH (<7.0), lactate level (>9 mmol/L), gasping respiration at hospital arrival, and presence of a pupillary light reflex at hospital arrival.23, 24, 25, 26, 27 These variables were included in the primary models as potential confounders to estimate the independent association between pseudo-PEA and clinical outcomes in the entire cohort. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted by sex, age (≥65 years or <65 years), cause of cardiac arrest (cardiac or non-cardiac), and transport time (≤30 min or >30 min). These subgroup analyses were performed separately as exploratory assessments of potential effect modification. Within each subgroup, the adjusted odds ratio for ROSC was estimated for pseudo-PEA using true PEA as the reference category.

Missing data were handled using multiple imputation with 20 imputations to reduce potential bias associated with complete case analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 for Windows (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study, which investigated the association between pseudo-PEA and the prognosis of Japanese patients with OHCA, found that pseudo-PEA was strongly associated with a higher rate of ROSC, survival to hospital admission, and 30-day survival as well as with a favorable neurological outcome at discharge. These associations remained significant on multivariable analysis and were consistent across the subgroups, indicating that pseudo-PEA is an independent predictor of both short and middle-term outcomes.

Almost all previous studies of the association between PEA and cardiac activity were conducted in European patients and had a relatively small sample pool (n = 34–225). Thus, Asian patients are under-represented.7, 9, 10, 11, 12 Our study identified 894 patients who had received echocardiography and enrolled the largest pseudo-PEA cohort to date. The results of this study corroborated previous findings, underscoring the fact that pseudo-PEA is associated with better outcomes than true PEA.9, 28, 29, 30 Furthermore, our study included a high-risk, Japanese population characterized by advanced age and severe illness. Notably, compared with a no-ROSC group described in a previous study,31 our cohort had lower pH, higher pCO2, lower HCO3–, and higher lactate, indicating that these patients had a more severe illness at the time of resuscitation. Furthermore, a previous, Japanese, monocentric study reported lactate ≥8.9 mmol/L and pH ≤ 7.05 as cutoff values associated with a poor prognosis.23 However, the mean values in our cohort were even worse, thus supporting the extrapolability of our findings to other, aging populations and comparable emergency systems.

The association between pseudo-PEA and a favorable prognosis may be explained by residual cardiac activity, which allows more effective perfusion during resuscitation and may result in ROSC. Identifying patients with pseudo-PEA using POCUS in the ED may have clinical benefits for aggressive resuscitation. Incorporating bedside echocardiography into standard OHCA management may improve prognostication and guide the allocation of resources, particularly in populations with severe illness. However, previous reports suggest that echocardiography performed during CPR may prolong interruptions in chest compressions and potentially worsen outcomes.32, 33, 34 In line with these concerns, the 2025 AHA and ERC resuscitation guidelines emphasize that intra-arrest ultrasound should only be performed by trained operators without causing additional pauses in chest compressions and caution against using cardiac motion on POCUS as the sole criterion for terminating resuscitation. These guideline statements highlight both the potential value and the limitations of POCUS in the real-time assessment of cardiac arrest physiology.35, 36

The present study classified patients solely according to the presence or absence of cardiac wall motion on echocardiography, which may include a heterogeneous group of physiological states. Specifically, the pseudo-PEA may encompass not only patients with effective residual cardiac output but also those with very low-flow ROSC accompanied by severe myocardial stunning, in whom cardiac motion is present but forward flow is insufficient to produce a palpable pulse. The SOS-KANTO registry did not capture additional, hemodynamic measurements, such as invasive arterial pressure, end-tidal CO2, and confirmation of aortic valve opening. Thus, misclassification may have occurred, and caution should be exercised when interpreting the prognostic implications of pseudo-PEA.

On multivariable analysis, the proportion of ROSC cases and secondary outcomes was significantly higher in the pseudo-PEA group than in the true PEA group. The large sample size enabled a reliable estimate of the primary outcome whereas the small number of events relevant to the secondary outcomes widened the confidence intervals. Importantly, the subgroup analyses based on sex, age, cause of cardiac arrest, and transportation time demonstrated a similarly consistent association, further reinforcing the prognostic value of cardiac activity. These findings suggest that identifying cardiac activity during resuscitation may help clinicians recognize potentially reversible states earlier although whether maintaining pseudo-PEA status or preventing deterioration directly improves outcomes requires further investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, it was observational, and despite multivariable adjustments, residual confounding was unable to be excluded. Second, only patients who had undergone echocardiographic assessment were included, thereby introducing the risk of selection bias. In fact, patients with echocardiography had better outcomes, suggesting that the study cohort may not have been fully representative of the general PEA population at hospital arrival. Third, the study enrolled a regional, Japanese cohort; hence, the findings may not be generalizable to other countries or healthcare systems. Fourth, the underlying causes of cardiac arrest were classified only as cardiogenic or non-cardiogenic; no details were available. Fifth, detailed procedural information regarding echocardiography, such as the exact timing and number of examinations during resuscitation, duration of chest compression interruptions during image acquisition, and the level of training or experience of the operators, was unavailable in the registry and could not be evaluated. Finally, echocardiography was performed without a standard protocol common to all the participating institutions. Accordingly, variation in the timing and performance of echocardiographic assessment, as well as operator dependency, may have introduced a misclassification bias into the distinction between pseudo-PEA and true PEA. More detailed physiological distinctions, such as whether myocardial activity generated effective forward flow or reflected very low-flow ROSC with severe myocardial stunning, could not be assessed. In addition, several estimates had a wide confidence interval, indicating statistical imprecision and the need for cautious interpretation, particularly for the secondary analyses. Future studies using standardized imaging protocols and additional hemodynamic measurements are needed to better characterize PEA subtypes and their prognostic significance.

Conclusions

In Japanese patients with OHCA, pseudo-PEA was more strongly associated than true PEA with an increased ROSC rate and improved short and middle-term outcomes. Distinguishing pseudo-PEA from true PEA during CPR may be beneficial to patient outcomes, and POCUS during CPR may be integrated into emergency care protocols.
