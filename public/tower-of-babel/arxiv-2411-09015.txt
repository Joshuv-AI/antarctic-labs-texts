Labelled Graphs as Morita equivalence invariants for a class of inverse semigroups Thanks: The authors were supported by an NSF grant (DMS-2149921).
Zachary Duah, Stian Du Preez, David Milan, Shreyas Ramamurthy, Lucas Vega

Abstract
. We investigate the use of labelled graphs as a Morita equivalence invariant for inverse semigroups. We construct a labelled graph from a combinatorial inverse semigroup S S with 0 0 admitting a special set of idempotent 𝒟 \mathcal{D} -class representatives and show that S S is Morita equivalent to a labelled graph inverse semigroup. For the inverse hull S S of a Markov shift, we show that the labelled graph determines the Morita equivalence class of S S among all other inverse hulls of Markov shifts.

1.
Introduction

In his influential work on the tight groupoid of an inverse semigroup, Exel
[
5
]
summarized the construction of
C^{*}
-algebras from combinatorial objects with the following diagram:

\framebox{combinatorial object}\to\framebox{inverse semigroup}\to\framebox{groupoid}\to\framebox{$C^{*}$-algebra}

The standard example of such a construction starts with a directed graph
\Gamma
and concludes with the graph
C^{*}
-algebra of
\Gamma
.

In this paper, we are interested in reversing the first arrow. We want to recover combinatorial data from an inverse semigroup that is sufficient to characterize the inverse semigroup, at least up to Morita equivalence. This has potential applications in
C^{*}
-algebra theory, since for example one could develop transformations similar to the graph moves in Sørenson
[
9
]
on a broader collection of combinatorial objects as a path towards “geometric” classification results for large classes of inverse semigroup
C^{*}
-algebras.

Inspired by the characterization of graph inverse semigroups in
[
7
]
, it was shown in
[
8
]
that from an inverse semigroup
S
one can construct a directed graph whose vertices are the nonzero
\mathcal{D}
-classes of
S
and whose edges are defined using the natural partial order on the idempotents. This directed graph is a Morita equivalence invariant for inverse semigroups. It is a complete invariant for combinatorial inverse semigroups with
0
satisfying two additional properties:

(1)

for
e,f,g\in E(S)
with
e,f\leq g
and
ef\neq 0
,
e
and
f
are comparable.

(2)

for
0\neq e\leq f
, there are finitely many idempotents between
e
and
f
.

Moreover, the inverse semigroups that satisfy the above properties are precisely the inverse semigroups that are Morita equivalent to graph inverse semigroups.

If
S
does not satisfy property (1), we say that
S

contains a diamond
. If
S
contains a diamond, then the directed graph associated with
S
will be insufficient to characterize Morita equivalence—we need a more detailed invariant than a directed graph. We replace (1) with the property that there is a partial order
\preceq
on the
\mathcal{D}
-classes of
S
making
S/\mathcal{D}
into a meet semilattice and satisfying a number of other properties. We can then pass to a Morita equivalent inverse semigroup
S^{\preceq}
that admits a
coherent
set
\mathcal{C}
of idempotent
\mathcal{D}
-class representatives (see Definition
5.1
). Given a coherent set
\mathcal{C}
, we construct a labelled graph and consider its inverse semigroup which is essentially the same as the one defined and studied by Boava, de Castro, and de L. Mortari in
[
4
]
. We show in Theorem
5.17
that any combinatorial inverse semigroup with
0
having a coherent set
\mathcal{C}
and finite intervals is Morita equivalent to the inverse semigroup of the labelled graph. This generalizes results of
[
8
]
to many inverse semigroups containing diamonds. We use this result to prove in Theorem
7.9
that the labelled directed graph associated to the inverse semigroup of a Markov shift is a complete invariant among inverse hulls of Markov shifts. Consequently, to determine Morita equivalence of two inverse hulls of Markov shifts, one only needs to examine a finite portion of each semilattice.

2.
Morita Equivalence

In this section we give a number of definitions and useful results pertaining to Morita equivalence of inverse semigroups. For a more thorough treatment of the subject, see the thesis of Afara
[
1
]
,
[
11
]
, or
[
6
]
. Among the many equivalent definitions of Morita equivalence, the one given in terms of category equivalence is usually the most convenient. The
idempotent splitting
of an inverse semigroup
S
is a category
C(S)
with objects
E(S)
and morphisms
\{(e,s,f):e,f\in E(S),s\in eSf\}
. Composition is given by

(e,s,f)(f,t,g)=(e,st,g).

Two objects (idempotents)
e,f
are isomorphic in
C(S)
if and only if they are
\mathcal{D}
-related in
S
. We write
[s]
for the
\mathcal{D}
-class of
s
in
S
. Given inverse semigroups
S
and
T
,
S
is
Morita equivalent
to
T
if and only if the categories
C(S)
and
C(T)
are equivalent. Let
T
be an inverse subsemigroup of an inverse semigroup
S
. Then
S
is an
enlargement
of
T
if
STS=S
and
TST=T
. We will make use of the fact that if
S
is an enlargement of
T
, then
S
is Morita equivalent to
T
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
S
and
T
are combinatorial inverse semigroups and
\mathcal{F}:C(S)\to C(T)
is an equivalence functor. Then

(1)

the map
\sigma([e])=[\mathcal{F}(e)]
defines a bijection
\sigma:S/\mathcal{D}\to T/\mathcal{D}
, and

(2)

for
e\in E(S)
and
f\in E(T)
with
\mathcal{F}(e)\mathcal{D}f
, there exists an isomorphism
\tau_{e,f}:eSe\to fTf
such that for all
g\leq e
, we have
\sigma([g])=[\tau_{e,f}(g)]
.

Proof.

For
e\in E(S)
, let
\sigma([e])=[\mathcal{F}(e)]
. Since two objects in the idempotent splitting are isomorphic if and only if they are
\mathcal{D}
-related, it is well known that
\sigma
is a bijection between
S/\mathcal{D}
and
T/\mathcal{D}
.

Fix
e\in E(S)
and
f\in E(T)
with
f\mathcal{D}\mathcal{F}(e)
. Let
\tau_{e,f,1}:eSe\to\mathcal{F}(e)T\mathcal{F}(e)
be the isomorphism defined by the equivalence functor, that is
\tau_{e,f,1}(s)=t
where
\mathcal{F}(e,s,e)=(\mathcal{F}(e),t,\mathcal{F}(e))
. Choose
x_{e,f}\in T
such that
x_{e,f}^{*}x_{e,f}=\mathcal{F}(e)
and
x_{e,f}x_{e,f}^{*}=f
. Define
\tau_{e,f,2}:\mathcal{F}(e)T\mathcal{F}(e)\to fTf
by
\tau_{e,f,2}(t)=x_{e,f}tx_{e,f}^{*}
. This is known to be an isomorphism that preserves
\mathcal{D}
-classes. Then
\tau_{e,f}=\tau_{e,f,2}\circ\tau_{e,f,1}
is an isomorphism from
eSe
to
fTf
. Finally, let
g\leq e
. Choose
y
such that
\mathcal{F}(e,g,g)=(\mathcal{F}(e),y,\mathcal{F}(g))
. Then
yy^{*}=\tau_{e,f,1}(g)
and
y^{*}y=\mathcal{F}(g)
. Since
\tau_{e,f,2}
preserves
\mathcal{D}
-classes

\sigma([g])=[\mathcal{F}(g)]=[\tau_{e,f,1}(g)]=[(\tau_{e,f,2}\circ\tau_{e,f,1})(g)]=[\tau_{e,f}(g)].

∎

A number of results in this paper assume we have chosen a
complete set
\mathcal{C}
of idempotent representatives
of the
\mathcal{D}
-classes of an inverse semigroup
S
. By this we mean that
\mathcal{C}
is a set of idempotents such that each
\mathcal{D}
-class of
S
has a unique representative in
\mathcal{C}
. We now give some useful facts related to this situation.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose
S
is an inverse semigroup and that
\mathcal{C}\subseteq E(S)
contains a representative of each
\mathcal{D}
-class of
S
. Then
S
is Morita equivalent to the inverse semigroup
\mathcal{C}S\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Let
T=\mathcal{C}S\mathcal{C}
. We will prove that
S
is an enlargement of
T
. One can quickly show that
T
is an inverse subsemigroup of
S
for which
T=TST
. We prove that
S=STS
. First, we clearly have
STS\subseteq S
. Let
s\in S
. Then there exists
x\in S
such that
s^{*}s=x^{*}x
and
xx^{*}\in\mathcal{C}
. Then
s=sx^{*}x=sx^{*}(xx^{*})x\in STS
. So
S=STS
.
∎

Afara and Lawson
[
2
]
showed how to construct all inverse semigroups Morita equivalent to a given inverse semigroup
S
using McAlister functions. A map
p:I\times I\to S
is called a
McAlister function
if it satisfies the following properties:

(MF1):

p_{i,i}
is an idempotent for all
i\in I
.

(MF2):

p_{i,i}p_{i,j}p_{j,j}=p_{i,j}
.

(MF3):

p_{i,j}=p_{j,i}^{-1}
.

(MF4):

p_{i,j}p_{j,k}\leq p_{i,k}
.

(MF5):

For each
e\in E(S)
there exists
i\in I
such that
e\leq p_{i,i}
.

From a McAlister function one constructs the inverse semigroup
IM(S,I,p)
consisting of equivalence classes
[i,s,j]
under a relation
\gamma
defined on triples
(i,s,j)
where
ss^{*}\leq p_{i,i}
and
s^{*}s\leq p_{j,j}
. The equivalence relation
\gamma
is defined by
(i,s,j)\gamma(k,t,l)
if and only if
s=p_{i,k}tp_{l,j}
and
t=p_{k,i}sp_{j,l}
. Multiplication in
IM(S,I,p)
is given by
[i,s,j][k,t,l]=[i,sp_{j,k}t,l]
. Then
IM(S,I,p)
is Morita equivalent to
S
. Moreover, if
T
is any inverse semigroup that is Morita equivalent to
S
, then
T
is isomorphic to
IM(S,I,p)
for some set
I
and McAlister function
p
.

Suppose now that
S
is an inverse semigroup with
0
and let

\mathcal{C}=\{e_{v}:v\in S/\mathcal{D}\}

be a complete set of idempotent representatives of the
\mathcal{D}
-classes of
S
. We assume that
\mathcal{C}
is
dominating
in the following sense: for every idempotent
e\in E(S)
, there exists
v\in S/\mathcal{D}
such that
e\leq e_{v}
. We also assume that there is a partial order
\preceq
on
S/\mathcal{D}
making it into a meet semilattice and satisfying the following for all
u,v,w\in S/\mathcal{D}
:

(1)

if
u\wedge v\neq 0
, then
e_{u}e_{v}=e_{u\wedge v}
, and

(2)

if
u,v\preceq w
, then
e_{u}e_{v}=e_{u\wedge v}
.

Let
I=S/\mathcal{D}
and define
p:I\times I\to S
by
p_{u,v}=e_{u\wedge v}
. Then
p
satisfies (MF1)-(MF5). To see (MF4), fix
u,v,w\in I
and suppose that
0\neq p_{u,v}p_{v,w}
. Since
u\wedge v,v\wedge w\preceq v
we have

0\neq p_{u,v}p_{v,w}=e_{u\wedge v}e_{v\wedge w}=e_{u\wedge v\wedge w}.

In particular,
0\neq u\wedge v\wedge w\preceq u\wedge w
. Thus

p_{u,v}p_{v,w}=e_{u}e_{v}e_{w}\leq e_{u}e_{w}=p_{u,w}.

Let
S^{\preceq}=IM(S,I,p)
. From the work of Afara and Lawson, we have that
S
is Morita equivalent to
S^{\preceq}
.

We now describe the idempotents and the partial order on
S^{\preceq}
. One can quickly check that
(u,s,v)\gamma(w,t,z)
if and only if

(1)

s=t
,
u\wedge w\neq 0
, and
v\wedge z\neq 0
, or

(2)

s=t=0
.

So
S^{\preceq}
has a zero with
[u,s,v]=0
if and only if
s=0
. We also have

\displaystyle E(S^{\preceq})

\displaystyle=\{[u,ss^{*},u]:u\in S/\mathcal{D},ss^{*}\leq p_{u,u}\}

\displaystyle=\{[u,e,u]:u\in S/\mathcal{D},e\in e_{u}^{\downarrow}\}.

We leave the proof of the following proposition to the reader.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
S^{\preceq}=IM(S,I,p)
as described above and suppose that
[a,f,a]
and
[b,g,b]
are idempotents in
S^{\preceq}
. Then
[a,f,a]\mathcal{D}[b,g,b]
if and only if
f\mathcal{D}g
.

Finally, we describe the natural partial order.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
S^{\preceq}=IM(S,I,p)
as described above and suppose
[u,s,v]\neq 0
. Then
[u,s,v]\leq[w,t,z]
in
S^{\preceq}
if and only if
s\leq t,u\wedge w\neq 0,
and
v\wedge z\neq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose
[u,s,v]\leq[w,t,z]
. Then

[u,s,v]=[w,t,z][v,s^{*},u][u,s,v]=[w,tp_{z,v}s^{*}e_{u}s,v].

So
s=tp_{z,v}s^{*}e_{u}s
and
u\wedge w\neq 0
. Also, since
[u,s,v]\neq 0
we have
z\wedge v\neq 0
and

s=tp_{z,v}s^{*}e_{u}s=te_{z}e_{v}s^{*}e_{u}s=ts^{*}s.

The converse is similar.

∎

3.
Labelled Graph Inverse Semigroups

In this section we consider inverse semigroups associated with labelled graphs. The semigroups we consider are essentially the same as the ones defined in Boava, de Castro, and de L. Mortari
[
4
]
. One difference is that we use a different convention for the composition of paths. More significantly, we remove the requirement that a certain collection of vertices is closed under unions. This does not change the definition of the product in the inverse semigroup associated with a labelled graph space.

Definition 3.1
.

Given a set
\mathcal{A}
, called an
alphabet
, containing
letters
, a
labelled graph

(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
, over
\mathcal{A}
consists of a directed graph
\mathcal{E}=(\mathcal{E}^{0},\mathcal{E}^{1},r,s)
and a map
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{E}^{1}\to A
, called a
labelling map
.

Let
\mathcal{A}^{*}
be the set of all finite words over
\mathcal{A}
including the empty word,
\omega
, and extend the map
\mathcal{L}
to a map
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{E}^{n}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}
defined by
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\mathcal{L}(\lambda_{1})\ldots\mathcal{L}(\lambda_{n})
for
\lambda=\lambda_{1}\ldots\lambda_{n}\in\mathcal{E}^{1}
.

The elements of
\mathcal{L}^{n}:=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{E}^{n})
are called
labelled paths of length
n
. If
\alpha
is a labelled path, a path on the graph
\lambda
s.t.
\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\alpha
is called a
representative
of
\alpha
, and conversely
\alpha
is the
label
of
\lambda
. Since any two representatives of
\alpha
have the same length, one can define the
length
of
\alpha
, denoted by
|\alpha|
, as the length of any one of its representatives. We also consider
\omega
as a labelled path, with
|\omega|=0
. The set
\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}=\cup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{L}^{n}
is the set of all labelled paths of positive finite length. We also define
\mathcal{L}^{*}=\{\omega\}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
.

Given two labelled paths
\alpha,\beta
, we say that
\beta

is a sublabel of

\alpha
if
\alpha=\beta\alpha^{\prime}
for some labelled path
\alpha^{\prime}
. We say that
\alpha
and
\beta
are
comparable
if
\alpha
is a sublabel of
\beta
or
\beta
is a sublabel of
\alpha
.

For
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{*}
and
A\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{E}^{0})
, the
relative source of
\alpha
with respect to
A
, denoted by
s(A,\alpha)
, is the set

s(A,\alpha)=\{s(\lambda):\lambda\in\mathcal{E}^{*},\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\alpha,r(\lambda)\in A\}

if
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
and
s(A,\alpha)=A
if
\alpha=\omega
. The
source
of
\alpha
, denoted by
s(\alpha)
, is the set

s(\alpha)=s(\mathcal{E}^{0},\alpha)

For
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
we also define the
range
of
\alpha
as the set

r(\alpha)=\{r(\alpha)\in\mathcal{E}^{0}:\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\alpha\}

Observe that, in particular,
s(\omega)=\mathcal{E}^{0}
, and if
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
then
s(\alpha)=\{s(\lambda)\in\mathcal{E}^{0}:\mathcal{L}(\lambda)=\alpha\}
. The definitions above allow us to extend the
s,r
maps to
s,r:\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{E}^{0})
. Also, if
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L}^{*}
are such that
\beta\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{*}
then
s(s(A,\beta),\alpha)=s(A,\beta\alpha)
. Finally, for
A,B\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{E}^{0})
and

, it holds that

.

Let
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
be a labelled graph and
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{E}^{0})
. We say that
\mathcal{B}
is
closed under relative sources
if
s(A,\alpha)\in\mathcal{B}
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
and all
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{*}
. If additionally
\mathcal{B}
is closed under finite intersections and contains all

for
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
, we say that
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B})
is a
labelled graph space
.

Remark 3.2
.

This is where our definition differs from the one in
[
4
]
. We do not assume that
\mathcal{B}

4.
Strongly Right Resolving Labelled Graphs

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
be a labelled graph. We say
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
is
strongly right resolving
if for all
e,f\in\mathcal{E}^{1}
,
\mathcal{L}(e)=\mathcal{L}(f)
implies
r(e)=r(f)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{E}=(\mathcal{E}^{0},\mathcal{E}^{1},r,s)
is a directed graph,
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{E}^{1}\to A
is a labelling map, and
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
is strongly right resolving. Further suppose that
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{E}^{0})
contains the empty set, is closed under finite intersections, and contain all
s(\alpha)
for
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
, then
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B})
is a weakly right resolving labelled graph space.

Proof.

First, we show that
\mathcal{B}
is closed under relative sources. Let
A\in\mathcal{B},\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{*}
. If
\alpha=\omega
, then
s(A,\omega)=A\in\mathcal{B}
, so assume otherwise. Then since
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
is strongly right resolving, the range of all representatives of
\alpha
must be the same, let
a\in\mathcal{E}^{0}
be this range. If
a\notin A
, then clearly
s(A,\alpha)=\emptyset
. If
a\in A
, then
s(A,\alpha)=s(\alpha)\in A
, since the ranges of all representatives of
\alpha
are in
A
. Thus
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B})
is a labelled graph space.

Next we show that
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B})
is weakly right resolving. Let
A,B\in\mathcal{B},\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{*}
. If
\alpha=\omega
, then clearly
s(A\cap B,\omega)=A\cap B=s(A,\omega)\cap s(B,\omega)
, so suppose otherwise. Again, let
a\in\mathcal{E}^{0}
be the range of all the representatives of
\alpha
. If
a\in A\cap B
, then
s(A\cap B,\alpha)=s(\alpha)=s(\alpha)\cap s(\alpha)=s(A,\alpha)\cap s(B,\alpha)
and we are done. Suppose
a\notin A\cap B
. Without loss of generality, suppose
a\notin A

s(A\cap B,\alpha)=\emptyset=\emptyset\cap s(B,\alpha)=s(A,\alpha)\cap s(B,\alpha)

∎

If
S
is the labelled graph inverse semigroup of the labelled graph space
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B})
where
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})

is a singleton for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
. We will use

to refer to the element and not the set containing one element moving forward.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B})
be a labelled graph space where
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
is strongly right resolving. For

and

\emptyset&\text{if }r(a)\notin A.\end{cases}

Proof.

Suppose

, then

s(A,a)=\{s(e):e\in\mathcal{E}^{*},\mathcal{L}(e)=a,r(e)\in A\}

Since
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
is strongly right resolving,
r(e)=r(a)\in A
and

s(A,a)=\{s(e):e\in\mathcal{E}^{*},\mathcal{L}(e)=a\}=s(a).

On the other hand, if
r(a)\notin A
, then no representative of
a
has a range in

, so

.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B})
be a labelled graph space where
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
is strongly right resolving. If

for

.

Since
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
, there exists a representative
\lambda\in\mathcal{E}^{*}
, so

. Let

be arbitrary, then

\displaystyle s(\alpha)

\displaystyle=s(a_{1}\ldots a_{n})

\displaystyle=s(s(a_{1}\ldots a_{i-1}),a_{i},\ldots a_{n})

\displaystyle=s(s(s(a_{1}\ldots a_{i-1}),a_{i}),a_{i+1}\ldots a_{n})

\displaystyle=s(s(s(s(a_{1}\ldots a_{i-1}),a_{i}),a_{i+1}),a_{i+2}\ldots a_{n})

Thus, if
r(a_{i+1})\notin s(a_{i})
, then

∎

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
S
be a labelled graph inverse semigroup coming from the labelled graph space

is strongly right resolving. For

and moreover, if

This follows as a corollary from the previous two propositions.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let

5.
The Labelled Graph of an Inverse Semigroup

In this section we construct a labelled graph from an inverse semigroup satisfying some key properties. Ultimately we seek to generalize the construction in
[
8
]
of a directed graph from a combinatorial inverse semigroup
S
with
0
.

For idempotents
e,f
we write
e\ll f
to denote that
e
is
immediately below

f
. That is,
e<f
and there does not exist
g
such that
e<g<f
. We say that an inverse semigroup
has finite intervals
if for idempotents
e,f
with
0\neq e\leq f
there exists idempotents
h_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq n
such that:

0\neq e=h_{1}\ll h_{2}\ll\dots\ll h_{n}=f

We will use a set of idempotent
\mathcal{D}
-class representatives to construct our labelled graph. Recall that we denote the
\mathcal{D}
-class of
s
by
[s]
.

Definition 5.1
.

A complete set
\mathcal{C}=\{e_{v}:v\in S/\mathcal{D}\}
of idempotent representatives of the
\mathcal{D}
-classes of
S
is a
coherent set for
S
if

(1)

e_{u}e_{v}\in\mathcal{C}
for all
u,v\in\mathcal{C}
,

(2)

0\neq e_{u}\leq f\leq e_{v}
implies
f\in\mathcal{C}
for all idempotents
f
, and

(3)

if
f,g\ll e_{v}
with
f
and
g
incomparable, then
fg\in\mathcal{C}
.

Note that we will frequently make use of the observation that
f\in\mathcal{C}
if and only if
f=e_{[f]}
.

Standing Assumptions
.

Throughout this section we assume that
S
is a combinatorial inverse semigroup with
0
having finite intervals and admitting a coherent set
\mathcal{C}
of idempotent representatives of the
\mathcal{D}
-classes of
S
.

We will prove that any inverse semigroup
S
with the above properties is Morita equivalent to a labelled graph inverse semigroup. Later, this result is used to show that the labelled graph associated to the inverse hull of a Markov shift is a complete Morita equivalence invariant among all inverse hulls of Markov shifts.

Remark 5.2
.

Given a coherent set
\mathcal{C}=\{e_{v}:v\in S/\mathcal{D}\}
for
S
, there is a partial order
\preceq
on
S/\mathcal{D}
defined by
u\preceq v
if and only if
e_{u}\leq e_{v}
. By Definition
5.1
(1),
(S/\mathcal{D},\preceq)
is a meet semilattice. We write
u\wedge v
for the meet of
u,v\in S/\mathcal{D}
.

Define the labelled graph
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
associated with
\mathcal{C}
as follows. First, the vertex set is

\mathcal{E}^{0}:=S/\mathcal{D}-\{0\},

and for each
v\in\mathcal{E}^{0}
, we define
B_{v}:=\{w\in\mathcal{E}^{0}:w\preceq v\}
. Let

\mathcal{B}:=\{B_{v}:v\in\mathcal{E}^{0}\}\cup\{\emptyset\}.

Fix a vertex
v\in\mathcal{E}^{0}
and a nonzero idempotent
f
with
f\ll e_{v}
and
f\neq e_{[f]}
. Define an edge
x_{v,f,u}
for all
u\in B_{[f]}
with

r(x_{v,f,u})=v\text{ and }s(x_{v,f,u})=u.

Also assign the label
\mathcal{L}(x_{v,f,u})=(v,f)
. We now have a directed graph
\mathcal{E}=(\mathcal{E}^{0},\mathcal{E}^{1},r,s)
and a labelling
\mathcal{L}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

The triple
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B})
is a labelled graph space. Moreover the labelled graph
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
is strongly right reductive.

Proof.

We first show that
\mathcal{B}
is closed under finite intersections. For
u,v\in\mathcal{E}^{0}
, if
B_{u}\cap B_{v}=\emptyset
, then we are done since
\emptyset\in\mathcal{B}
. Otherwise,
e_{u}e_{v}\neq 0
(so
u\wedge v\in\mathcal{E}^{0}
) and we claim that
B_{u}\cap B_{v}=B_{u\wedge v}
. Let
w\in B_{u}\cap B_{v}
. Then
0\neq e_{w}\leq e_{u},e_{v}
. Thus
e_{w}\leq e_{u}e_{v}
and
w\preceq u\wedge v
. So
w\in B_{u\wedge v}
. Conversely, if
w\in B_{u\wedge v}
then
w\preceq u
and
w\preceq v
. So
w\in B_{u}\cap B_{v}
. It follows by induction that
\mathcal{B}
is closed under finite intersections.

Next we show that
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
is strongly right reductive. Suppose that
x_{v_{1},f,u_{1}}
and
x_{v_{2},f,u_{2}}
are edges with the same label. Then
(v_{1},f)=(v_{2},f)
. In particular,
r(x_{v_{1},f,u_{1}})=v_{1}=v_{2}=r(x_{v_{2},f,u_{2}})
.

Finally, we show that
\mathcal{B}
is closed under relative sources. By Proposition
4.5
, it suffices to prove
s(a)\in\mathcal{B}
for each
a=(v,f)
in
\mathcal{L}^{1}
. If
s(a)=\emptyset
, we are done. Otherwise, notice that
u\in s(a)
if and only if there exists an edge
(v,f,u)
where
u\in B_{[f]}
. Thus
s(a)=B_{[f]}\in\mathcal{B}
.

∎

5.1.
S
contains the labelled graph inverse semigroup

Let
S_{\mathcal{C}}
be the labelled graph inverse semigroup of
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{B})
. We now show that
S_{\mathcal{C}}
embeds in
S
.

For each
(v,f)\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
, we know that
f\not=e_{[f]}
. Let
s_{v,f}\in S
be the unique element such that
s_{v,f}^{*}s_{v,f}=e_{[f]}
and
s_{v,f}s_{v,f}^{*}=f
. For
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
with
\alpha=x_{1}\ldots x_{n}
where
x_{i}=(v_{i},f_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, define

s_{\alpha}:=s_{x_{1}}\ldots s_{x_{n}}\text{ and }e_{\alpha}:=s_{\alpha}s_{\alpha}^{*}.

Also, for
B_{v}\in\mathcal{B}
, let
e_{B_{v}}=e_{v}
. In this way, we have defined
e_{s(\alpha)}
for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
.

Proposition 5.4
.

For each
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
, we have
s_{\alpha}^{*}s_{\alpha}=e_{s(\alpha)}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=x_{1}\ldots x_{n}
be a label where
x_{i}=(v_{i},f_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Since
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
is strongly right reductive,
v_{i+1}=r(x_{i+1})\in s(x_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
. Thus,
v_{i+1}\in B_{[f_{i}]}
, and in particular
v_{i+1}\preceq[f_{i}]
. Thus
e_{v_{i+1}}\leq e_{[f_{i}]}=s_{x_{i}}^{*}s_{x_{i}}
. Recall also that
s_{x_{i}}s_{x_{i}}^{*}=f_{i}\leq e_{v_{i}}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Thus

\displaystyle s_{\alpha}^{*}s_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=s^{*}_{x_{n}}\ldots s_{x_{3}}^{*}s_{x_{2}}^{*}(s_{x_{1}}^{*}s_{x_{1}})(s_{x_{2}})s_{x_{3}}\ldots s_{x_{n}}

\displaystyle=s^{*}_{x_{n}}\ldots s_{x_{3}}^{*}s_{x_{2}}^{*}(e_{[f_{1}]}e_{v_{2}})s_{x_{2}}s_{x_{3}}\ldots s_{x_{n}}

\displaystyle=s^{*}_{x_{n}}\ldots s_{x_{3}}^{*}s_{x_{2}}^{*}(e_{v_{2}}s_{x_{2}})s_{x_{3}}\ldots s_{x_{n}}

\displaystyle=s^{*}_{x_{n}}\ldots s_{x_{3}}^{*}(s_{x_{2}}^{*}s_{x_{2}})(s_{x_{3}})\ldots s_{x_{n}}

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle=s^{*}_{x_{n}}s_{x_{n}}=e_{[f_{n}]}

Since
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{L})
is strongly right reductive,
s(\alpha)=s(x_{n})
. Notice that the representatives of
x_{n}
are exactly the set of
x_{v_{n},f_{n},u}
where
u\in B_{[f_{n}]}
, and that the source of each such representative is
u
. Thus
s(x_{n})=B_{[f_{n}]}
. So

s_{\alpha}^{*}s_{\alpha}=e_{[f_{n}]}=e_{s(x_{n})}=e_{s(\alpha)}

as desired.
∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Given the labelled graph space
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{L})
defined above, we have the following properties:

(1)

for
x,y\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
,
s_{x}^{*}s_{y}\not=0
if and only if
x=y
,

(2)

for
x,y\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
,
e_{x}=e_{y}
if and only if
x=y
, and

(3)

for
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
,

e_{s(\alpha)}s_{\beta}=\begin{cases}s_{\beta}&\text{ if $r(\beta)\in s(\alpha)$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

For (1), let
x=(v,f),y=(u,g)
. If
x=y
, then
s_{x}^{*}s_{x}=e_{[f]}\neq 0
by definition. Conversely, suppose that
s_{x}^{*}s_{y}\not=0
. Then
(s_{x}^{*}s_{x}s_{x}^{*})(s_{y}s_{y}^{*}s_{y})\not=0
, so
(s_{x}s_{x}^{*})(s_{y}s_{y}^{*})\not=0
. Thus

0\not=(s_{x}s_{x}^{*})(s_{y}s_{y}^{*})=fg\leq e_{v}e_{u}.

It follows that
e_{u\wedge v}=e_{u}e_{v}\neq 0
. We claim that
u=v=u\wedge v
. For contradiction, suppose that
e_{u\wedge v}<e_{v}
. Since
S
has finite intervals, there exists
h
such that
0\neq e_{u\wedge v}\leq h\ll e_{v}
. Then
h\in C
and hence
h\neq f
. Since
h\ll e_{v}
and
f\ll e_{v}
we know that
h
and
f
are incomparable. Thus
hf=0
. We have
0\neq fg\leq e_{u\wedge v}f=e_{u\wedge v}hf=0
, a contradiction. Thus
v=u\wedge v
and similarly
u=u\wedge v
.

We have
f\ll e_{u}
and
g\ll e_{u}
. Since
fg\neq 0
and
fg\not\in\mathcal{C}
, we conclude that
f
and
g
are comparable. Say without loss of generality that
f\leq g\leq e_{u}
. Then since
f\ll e_{u}
it follows that
f=g
or
g=e_{u}
. Since
g\not\in\mathcal{C}
, we must have that
f=g
. Thus
x=(v,f)=(u,g)=y
.

For (2), suppose that
e_{x}=e_{y}
, where

. Then
s_{x}s_{x}^{*}s_{y}s_{y}^{*}=e_{x}e_{y}=e_{x}\neq 0
. So
s_{x}^{*}s_{y}\neq 0
and hence

. The converse is clear.

To prove (3), we first show for
B_{u}\in\mathcal{E}^{0}
and

e_{B_{u}}s_{x}=\begin{cases}s_{x}&\text{if }r(x)\in B_{u}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Suppose

, i.e.,
v\preceq u
. Thus
f\ll e_{v}\leq e_{u}
, so

e_{B_{u}}s_{x}=e_{u}(s_{x}s_{x}^{*})s_{x}=e_{u}fs_{x}=fs_{x}=s_{x}s_{x}^{*}s_{x}=s_{x}.

Conversely, suppose
v=r(x)\notin B_{u}
. Then
e_{v}\not\leq e_{u}
. Suppose for the sake of obtaining a contradiction that
fe_{u}=fe_{v}e_{u}
is nonzero. Since
e_{v}\not\leq e_{u}
, it must be that
e_{u}e_{v}<e_{v}
. We consider three cases, each resulting in a contradiction. If

and
e_{u}e_{v}
are incomparable, then by Definition

. Since

by Definition

(2). This is a contradiction. Similarly, if

then

The general case follows from the fact that if
s(\alpha)=B_{u}
for some

, and

where

and

\displaystyle e_{s(\alpha)}s_{\beta}

\displaystyle=e_{B_{u}}s_{x_{1}}\ldots s_{x_{n}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}s_{x_{1}}\ldots s_{x_{n}}&\text{if }r(x_{1})\in B_{u}\\
0s_{x_{2}}\ldots s_{x_{n}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}s_{\beta}&\text{if }r(\beta)\in s(\alpha)\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

∎

Corollary 5.6
.

For any word
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
, we have
s_{\alpha}\not=0
and
e_{\alpha}\not=0
.

Proof.

Clearly
s_{x}\not=0
for all

\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}x
where

and
\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}^{*}

, then
s_{\alpha}=s_{x}\not=0

Also, if

∎

Proposition 5.7

For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}

∎

Proposition 5.8

For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}

If
\alpha=\beta
, then

\beta

\alpha

\beta

for letters
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}

e_{\alpha}=e_{\beta}

s_{\alpha}s_{\alpha}^{*}=s_{\beta}s_{\beta}^{*}

\displaystyle s_{\alpha}^{*}s_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}^{*}(s_{\beta}s_{\beta}^{*})s_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(s_{\alpha}^{*}s_{\beta})(s_{\alpha}^{*}s_{\beta})^{*}

\displaystyle=s_{x_{1}}\ldots s_{x_{n}}s_{x_{n}}^{*}\ldots s_{x_{1}}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq s_{x_{1}}s_{x_{1}}^{*}.

Let

\beta

.
∎

We now define a map
\sigma:S_{\mathcal{C}}\to S
by

\sigma(\alpha,B_{v},\beta)=\begin{cases}s_{\alpha}e_{v}s_{\beta}^{*}&\text{if }\alpha,\beta\not=\omega\\
s_{\alpha}e_{v}&\text{if }\alpha\not=\omega=\beta\\
e_{v}s_{\beta}^{*}&\text{if }\beta\not=\omega=\alpha\\
e_{v}&\text{if }\alpha,\beta=\omega\end{cases}

. We will show that

Proposition 5.9
.

For
\alpha\in\mathcal{L}^{\geq 1}
, we have that
e_{\alpha}\not\in\mathcal{C}
and
e_{\alpha}<e_{v}
for some

.

Proof.

Let
\alpha=x_{1}\ldots x_{n}
, and let

where

. Then

\displaystyle e_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}s_{\alpha}^{*}

\displaystyle=s_{x_{1}}\ldots s_{x_{n}}s_{x_{n}}^{*}\ldots s_{x_{1}}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq s_{x_{1}}s_{x_{1}}^{*}=f.

If
e_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{C}
, then

since

, by Proposition

(2). By contradiction, so
e_{\alpha}\not\in\mathcal{C}

∎

Proposition 5.10
.

For
t\in S_{\mathcal{C}}
,
\sigma(t)=0
if and only if
t=0
.

Proof.

If
t=0
, then
\sigma(t)=0
by defintion. Conversely, suppose

with

. We also supppose
\alpha,\beta\neq\omega
, as the cases where one or both are empty labels are similar. There exists

and
e_{v}\leq e_{u}

\displaystyle\sigma(t)\sigma(t)^{*}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}e_{v}s_{\beta}^{*}s_{\beta}e_{v}s_{\alpha}^{*}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}e_{v}e_{s(\beta)}e_{v}s_{\alpha}^{*}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}e_{v}e_{u}s_{\alpha}^{*}

But then

∎

Proposition 5.11
.

The map
\sigma:S_{\mathcal{C}}\to S
is injective.

Proof.

Since
S_{\mathcal{C}}
is combinatorial, it suffices to prove that
\sigma
is injective on idempotents. We may assume both idempotents are nonzero by Proposition
5.10
. Let
(\alpha,B_{v},\alpha)
and
(\gamma,B_{u},\gamma)
be nonzero idempotents in
S_{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\sigma(\alpha,B_{v},\alpha)=\sigma(\gamma,B_{u},\gamma)
. There are four cases to consider. First suppose
\alpha=\gamma=\omega
. Then
e_{v}=\sigma((\omega,B_{v},\omega))=\sigma((\omega,B_{u},\omega))=e_{u}.
So

and
(\omega,B_{v},\omega)=(\omega,B_{u},\omega)
.

Next suppose that
\alpha=\omega\neq\gamma
. Then

\displaystyle e_{u}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\omega,B_{u},\omega)

\displaystyle=\sigma(\gamma,B_{v},\gamma)

\displaystyle=s_{\gamma}e_{v}s_{\gamma}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq s_{\gamma}s_{\gamma}^{*}=e_{\gamma}.

We know by Proposition
5.9
that
e_{\gamma}<e_{w}
for some vertex

and

. However, by Definition

implies that

, a contradiction. The case where
\alpha\neq\omega=\gamma
is similar. Finally, suppose that
\alpha,\gamma\neq\omega
. We have

s_{\alpha}e_{v}s_{\alpha}^{*}=s_{\gamma}e_{u}s_{\gamma}^{*}.

Since

and

, we know that
e_{v}\leq e_{s(\alpha)}=s_{\alpha}^{*}s_{\alpha}
and
e_{u}\leq e_{s(\gamma)}=s_{\gamma}^{*}s_{\gamma}
. Notice that
s_{\alpha}e_{v}s_{\alpha}^{*}s_{\gamma}e_{u}s_{\gamma}^{*}=s_{\alpha}e_{v}s_{\alpha}^{*}\neq 0

s_{\alpha}^{*}s_{\gamma}\neq 0

\alpha

\gamma

\gamma

∎

Proposition 5.12
.

The map
\sigma:S_{\mathcal{C}}\to S
is a homomorphism.

Proof.

Let
t_{1}=(\alpha,B_{v},\beta),t_{2}=(\gamma,B_{u},\delta)\in S_{\mathcal{C}}
. We will examine the cases where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\not=\omega
. The other cases follow similarly. First suppose that
\beta=\gamma
. Then

\displaystyle\sigma(t_{1})\sigma(t_{2})

\displaystyle=\sigma(\alpha,B_{v},\beta)\sigma(\gamma,B_{u},\delta)

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}e_{v}s_{\beta}^{*}(s_{\gamma})e_{u}s_{\delta}^{*}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}(e_{v}e_{u})s_{\delta}^{*}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}e_{v\wedge u}s_{\delta}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\alpha,B_{u\wedge v},\delta)

\displaystyle=\sigma(\alpha,B_{v}\cap B_{u},\delta)

\displaystyle=\sigma((\alpha,B_{v},\beta)(\gamma,B_{u},\delta))=\sigma(t_{1}t_{2}).

Next suppose that
\gamma=\beta\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}\not=\omega,
and
r(\gamma^{\prime})\in B_{v}
. Let
s(\gamma)=B_{w}
where

.

\displaystyle\sigma(t_{1})\sigma(t_{2})

\displaystyle=\sigma(\alpha,B_{v},\beta)\sigma(\gamma,B_{u},\delta)

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}e_{v}s_{\beta}^{*}(s_{\gamma})e_{u}s_{\delta}^{*}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}(e_{v}s_{\gamma^{\prime}})e_{u}s_{\delta}^{*}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha}(s_{\gamma^{\prime}})e_{u}s_{\delta}^{*}

\displaystyle=(s_{\alpha}s_{\gamma^{\prime}})e_{s(\gamma^{\prime})}e_{u}s_{\delta}^{*}

\displaystyle=s_{\alpha\gamma^{\prime}}(e_{w}e_{u})s_{\delta}^{*}

\displaystyle=\sigma(\alpha\gamma^{\prime},B_{w\wedge u},\delta)

\displaystyle=\sigma(\alpha\gamma^{\prime},B_{w}\cap B_{u},\delta)

\displaystyle=\sigma(\alpha\gamma^{\prime},s(\gamma^{\prime})\cap B_{u},\delta)

\displaystyle=\sigma((\alpha,B_{v},\beta)(\gamma,B_{u},\delta))=\sigma(t_{1}t_{2}).

The next case,
\beta=\gamma\beta^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}\not=\omega,
and

, is similar to the previous case. Finally, suppose that
\beta
and

\sigma(t_{1}t_{2})=\sigma((\alpha,B_{v},\beta)(\gamma,B_{u},\delta))=\sigma(0)=0.

∎

5.2.
S
is Morita equivalent to
S_{\mathcal{C}}

We continue with our standing assumptions that
S
is a combinatorial inverse semigroup with
0
having finite intervals and admitting a coherent set
\mathcal{C}
of idempotent representatives of the
\mathcal{D}
-classes of
S
.

Our next goal is to show that
S
is Morita equivalent to the labelled graph inverse semigroup
S_{\mathcal{C}}
. We accomplish this by showing that

is an enlargement of
\sigma(S_{\mathcal{C}})
. Recall that a coherent set
\mathcal{C}=\{e_{u}:u\in S/\mathcal{D}\}
on
S
induces a partial order
\preceq
on

if and only if

is a meet semilattice with

Throughout we let
\sigma:S_{\mathcal{C}}\to S
be the embedding of the inverse semigroup of the labelled graph space

into

\sigma(S_{\mathcal{C}})=\mathcal{C}S\mathcal{C}.

Let
f\in E(\mathcal{C}S\mathcal{C})

f\mathcal{D}e_{[f]}
, there exists an
s_{f}\in S
s.t.
s_{f}^{*}s_{f}=f
and
s_{f}s_{f}^{*}=e_{[f]}
. We can also define an isomorphism
\tau_{f}:fSf\to e_{[f]}Se_{[f]}
by
\tau_{f}(x)=s_{f}xs_{f}^{*}

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
g\leq f\leq e
for
g,f,e\in E(\mathcal{C}S\mathcal{C})
. If
\tau_{e}(f)=e_{[f]}
, then
\tau_{e}(g)=\tau_{f}(g)
.

Proof.

Let
t=s_{e}s_{f}^{*}s_{f}=s_{e}f
. Then
t^{*}t=fs_{e}^{*}s_{e}f=fef=f=s_{f}^{*}s_{f}
and
tt^{*}=s_{e}ffs_{e}^{*}=s_{e}fs_{e}^{*}=\tau_{e}(f)=e_{[f]}=s_{f}s_{f}^{*}
. Since
S
is combinatorial,
s_{f}=t=s_{e}s_{f}^{*}s_{f}
. Thus
s_{f}\leq s_{e}
. It follows that

\tau_{e}(g)=s_{e}gs_{e}^{*}=s_{e}(s_{f}^{*}s_{f})g(s_{f}^{*}s_{f})s_{e}^{*}=s_{f}gs_{f}^{*}=\tau_{f}(g).

∎

Proposition 5.14
.

For every idempotent
e\in E(\mathcal{C}S\mathcal{C})
, there exists
u\in S/\mathcal{D}
and
f_{1},g_{1},\ldots,f_{n},g_{n}\in E(\mathcal{C}S\mathcal{C})
such that

e=f_{1}\leq g_{1}\ll f_{2}\leq g_{2}\ll\cdots\ll f_{n}\leq g_{n}=e_{u},

where
\tau_{g_{i}}(f_{i})=e_{[f_{i}]}
for
1\leq i\leq n
and
\tau_{f_{i+1}}(g_{i})\not=e_{[g_{i}]}
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
.

Proof.

The proposition holds trivially in the case that
e=0
, since
e=0\leq 0=e_{[0]}
and
\tau_{0}(0)=0
. Let
0\neq e\in E(\mathcal{C}S\mathcal{C})
. Then
e=e_{u_{1}}se_{u_{2}}
where
u_{1},u_{2}\in S/\mathcal{D}
and
s\in S
. Notice
0\neq e\leq e_{u}
where
u=u_{1}\wedge u_{2}
. Since
S
admits finite intervals, there exists idempotents
h_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq m
such that

0\neq e=h_{1}\ll h_{2}\ll\dots\ll h_{m}=e_{u}.

We proceed by induction on
m
. For
m=1
we have
e=e_{u}
. In this case, let
f_{1}=g_{1}=e=e_{u}
and notice that
\tau_{g_{1}}(f_{1})=\tau_{e}(e)=e=e_{u}=e_{[f_{1}]}
. Next suppose that claim holds for any chain of idempotents of length
k
and that

. By the inductive hypothesis applied to the chain
h_{2}\ll\dots\ll h_{m}=e_{u}
, there exists
f_{i},g_{i}
for
2\leq i\leq n
such that

h_{2}=f_{2}\leq g_{2}\ll f_{3}\leq g_{3}\ll\dots\ll f_{n}\leq g_{n}=e_{u},

where
\tau_{g_{i}}(f_{i})=e_{[f_{i}]}
for
2\leq i\leq n
and
\tau_{f_{i+1}}(g_{i})\not=e_{[g_{i}]}
for
2\leq i\leq n-1
. We consider two cases. First suppose that
\tau_{h_{2}}(e)\neq e_{[e]}
. In this case we let
f_{1}=g_{1}=e
. Then
e=f_{1}\leq g_{1}\ll f_{2}\leq g_{2}\ll\cdots\ll f_{n}\leq g_{n}=e_{u}
. Notice
\tau_{g_{1}}(f_{1})=e_{[e]}=e_{[f_{1}]}
,
\tau_{f_{2}}(g_{1})=\tau_{h_{2}}(e)\neq e_{[e]}=e_{[g_{1}]}
, and the remaining conditions follow from the chain obtained by the inductive hypothesis.

Next, suppose that
\tau_{h_{2}}(e)=e_{[e]}
. In this case, define
f^{\prime}_{1}=e
,
g^{\prime}_{1}=g_{2}
,
f^{\prime}_{i}=f_{i+1}
and
g^{\prime}_{i}=g_{i+1}
for

. We have

e=f^{\prime}_{1}\leq g^{\prime}_{1}\ll f^{\prime}_{2}\leq g^{\prime}_{2}\ll\dots\ll f^{\prime}_{n-1}\leq g^{\prime}_{n-1}=e_{u}.

Notice that
e=f^{\prime}_{1}\leq h_{2}\leq g_{2}=g^{\prime}_{1}
. Since

\tau_{g^{\prime}_{1}}(f^{\prime}_{1})=\tau_{g_{2}}(e)=\tau_{h_{2}}(e)=e_{[e]}
by Lemma
5.13
. Also,
\tau_{f^{\prime}_{2}}(g^{\prime}_{1})=\tau_{f_{3}}(g_{2})\neq e_{[g_{2}]}=e_{[g^{\prime}_{1}]}

∎

Proposition 5.15
.

For all

Proof.

Let

e_{[e]}\not=0

, we know there exists

,

\tau_{f_{i+1}}(g_{i})\not=e_{[g_{i}]}

Thus

is a path of length

. Let

for

. First suppose that

. Since

6.
Inverse Hulls, Cores, and Labelled Graphs

We start this section with a brief review of the inverse semigroup associated to a Markov shift by Starling
[
10
]
. We will rely on a number of structural properties proved in
[
3
]
. Those properties are summarized below. We define a partial order on the
\mathcal{D}
-classes of such semigroups using the idea of a core set of idempotents. Given a finite alphabet
A
and a matrix
T=\{T_{a,b}\}_{a,b\in A}
with
T_{a,b}\in\{0,1\}
for each
a,b\in A
, such that each row of
T
has a nonzero entry, we refer to
T
as a
Markov transition matrix
. Associated to
T
is a semigroup
L_{T}\cup\{0\}
, where
L_{T}
is the set of allowable words defined by
T
and multiplication is given by

x*y=\begin{cases}xy&\text{if $xy\in L_{T}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

For each
a\in A
there is a partial bijection

\theta_{a}:\{w\in L_{T}:aw\in L_{T}\}\to\{v\in L_{T}:v=aw\text{ for $w\in L_{T}$}\}

defined by
\theta_{a}(w)=aw
. The
inverse hull of
T
is the inverse semigroup
H(T)
generated by the partial bijections
\{\theta_{a}:a\in A\}\cup\{0\}
. The following is proved in
[
3
]
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
T
be a Markov transition matrix over a finite alphabet
A
. Then
H(T)
is a combinatorial inverse semigroup with
0
. Moreover, the set of idempotents
\mathcal{O}=\{\theta_{a}\theta_{a}^{-1}:a\in A\}
satisfies the following properties:

(O1)

the elements of
\mathcal{O}
are mutually orthogonal,

(O2)

every idempotent in
H(T)
is comparable to some element of
\mathcal{O}
,

(O3)

both
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}\cup\{0\}
and
(\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O})\cup\{0\}
are closed under multiplication,

(O4)

elements of
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
are uniquely determined by the set of idempotents in
\mathcal{O}
that they lie above in the natural partial order, and

(O5)

for each
e\in\mathcal{O}
, the
\mathcal{D}
-class of
e
contains at most one element of
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
.

We also have that

(\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O})=\{\theta_{a_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{a_{1}}\cdots\theta_{a_{n}}^{-1}\theta_{a_{n}}:a_{i}\in A\text{ for $1\leq i\leq n$}\}.

Every element in
H(X)
can be written in the form

\theta_{s}\theta_{x_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{x_{1}}\cdots\theta_{x_{n}}^{-1}\theta_{x_{n}}\theta_{w}^{-1},

for some
n\geq 1
,
x_{i}\in A
, and
s,w\in L_{T}^{1}
. Such an element is idempotent if
s=w
.

Proposition 6.2
.

For every nonzero
\mathcal{D}
-class of
H(T)
,
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
contains exactly one element in that class.

Proof.

What need to show that if

e=\theta_{x_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{x_{1}}\cdots\theta_{x_{n}}^{-1}\theta_{x_{n}}\;\mathcal{D}\;\theta_{y_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{y_{1}}\cdots\theta_{y_{m}}^{-1}\theta_{y_{m}}=f

then
e=f
. Choose
s
such that
s^{*}s=e
and
ss^{*}=f
and write

s=\theta_{v}\theta_{z_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{z_{1}}\cdots\theta_{z_{k}}^{-1}\theta_{z_{k}}\theta_{w}^{-1}.

Then

\theta_{x_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{x_{1}}\cdots\theta_{x_{n}}^{-1}\theta_{x_{n}}=\theta_{w}\theta_{z_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{z_{1}}\cdots\theta_{z_{k}}^{-1}\theta_{z_{k}}\theta_{w}^{-1},

\theta_{y_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{y_{1}}\cdots\theta_{y_{m}}^{-1}\theta_{y_{m}}=\theta_{v}\theta_{z_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{z_{1}}\cdots\theta_{z_{k}}^{-1}\theta_{z_{k}}\theta_{v}^{-1}.

So we see that
v=w=1
. And thus

\theta_{x_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{x_{1}}\cdots\theta_{x_{n}}^{-1}\theta_{x_{n}}=\theta_{z_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{z_{1}}\cdots\theta_{z_{k}}^{-1}\theta_{z_{k}}=\theta_{y_{1}}^{-1}\theta_{y_{1}}\cdots\theta_{y_{m}}^{-1}\theta_{y_{m}},

as desired.
∎

We want to define a partial order on the
\mathcal{D}
-classes of the inverse hull of a Markov shift that will later be used to define a labelled graph. We need only look at the set of idempotents in
(\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O})\cup\{0\}
to determine this relation as this set already contains a representative of each nonzero
\mathcal{D}
-class. The partial order we define will be induced by some of the relations in the natural partial order on
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
. The essential idea is to include those relations that appear between idempotents in certain diamonds, while excluding the others. We formalize this idea using the notion of a core set of idempotents.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup with
0
that has finite intervals. For
e\in E(S)
we define
\mathcal{C}_{e}
to be the collection of subsets
C\subseteq e^{\downarrow}
satisfying:

(1)

e\in C
.

(2)

If
f,g\in C
with
fg\not=0
, then
fg\in C
.

(3)

For each
h_{1},h_{2}\in C
with
f\ll h_{1}
and
g\ll h_{2}
, if
f
and
g
are incomparable and
fg\not=0
, then
f,g\in C
.

(4)

For
e_{1},e_{2}
in
C
, if
e_{1}<g<e_{2}
, then
g\in C
.

Then we call
C_{e}=\bigcap_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{e}}C
, the
core
of
e
.

One can quickly verify that
C_{e}\in\mathcal{C}_{e}
. We include here a useful fact for later that can be proved by a quick induction argument using property (3).

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
S
be an inverse semigroup with
0
having finite intervals. Suppose that
e,f,g
are nonzero idempotents with
f\ll e
and
g<e
such that
f
and
g
are incomparable and
fg\neq 0
. Then
f,g\in C_{e}
.

Definition 6.5
.

For
a,b\in S/\mathcal{D}
, we write
a\preceq^{\prime}b
if there exists
e\in E(S)
and
f,g\in C_{e}
where
[f]=a
and
[g]=b
and
f\leq g
. It is reflexive since if
a=[e]
, then
e\in C_{e}
and hence
a\preceq^{\prime}a
. Let
\preceq
be the transitive closure of
\preceq^{\prime}
together with the additional relations that
0\preceq a
for every
a\in S/\mathcal{D}
.

We will prove that
\preceq
is antisymmetric when
S
is the inverse hull of a Markov shift. First we show that this relation is preserved under Morita equivalence. Recall that if
S
and
T
are Morita equivalent then there is an equivalence functor
\mathcal{F}:C(S)\to C(T)
. Moreover, the map
\sigma([e])=[\mathcal{F}(e)]
is a well-defined bijection from the
\mathcal{D}
-classes of
S
to the
\mathcal{D}
-classes of
T
(see Lemma
2.1
).

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
S
and
T
be inverse semigroups with
0
. If
S
is Morita equivalent to
T
via an equivalence functor
\mathcal{F}:C(S)\to C(T)
, then
a\preceq_{S}b
if and only if
\sigma(a)\preceq_{T}\sigma(b)
where
\sigma([e])=[\mathcal{F}(e)]
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
a\preceq^{\prime}_{S}b
if and only if
\sigma(a)\preceq^{\prime}_{T}\sigma(b)
. Suppose
a,b\in S/\mathcal{D}
with
a\preceq_{S}^{\prime}b
. Let
e,f,g\in E(S)
where
f,g\in C_{e},[f]=a
, and
[g]=b
and
f\leq g
. Choose any
e^{\prime}\in E(T)
such that
\sigma([e])=[e^{\prime}]
. Let
\tau:eSe\to e^{\prime}Te^{\prime}
be the isomorphism defined in Lemma
2.1
. Then
\tau(f),\tau(g)\in C_{e^{\prime}}
with
\tau(f)\leq\tau(g)
. By part (2) of the lemma we have

\sigma(a)=[\mathcal{F}(f)]=[\tau(f)]\preceq^{\prime}_{T}[\tau(g)]=[\mathcal{F}(g)]=\sigma(b).

The converse is similar.
∎

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
S
be the inverse hull of a Markov shift. For
a,b\in S/\mathcal{D}
, if
0\neq a\preceq b
then there exists
f,g\in\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
with
[f]=a,[g]=b
and
f\leq g
.

Proof.

For any
e\in\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
, one can show that
(\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O})\cap e^{\downarrow}
satisfies the four properties in Definition
6.3
. Thus
C_{e}\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
. Suppose
0\neq a\preceq^{\prime}b
. Since every
\mathcal{D}
-class of
S
has a unique representative in
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
, we may choose
e,f,g\in\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
such that
f,g\in C_{e}
, with
[f]=a,[g]=b,
and
f\leq g
. Now suppose
0\neq a\preceq b
. Then
a=a_{0}\preceq^{\prime}a_{1}\preceq^{\prime}a_{2}\preceq^{\prime}\dots\preceq^{\prime}a_{n}=b
for some
\mathcal{D}
-classes
a_{0},a_{1},\dots a_{n}
. For each
1\leq k\leq n
we may choose idempotents
f_{k},g_{k}
in
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
such that
[f_{k}]=a_{k-1},[g_{k}]=a_{k}
and
f_{k}\leq g_{k}
. Since each nonzero
\mathcal{D}
-class has a unique representative in
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
, we conclude that
g_{k}=f_{k+1}
for
1\leq k\leq n-1
. Thus
[f_{1}]=a,[g_{n}]=b
and
f_{1}\leq g_{n}
.

∎

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
S
be the inverse hull of a Markov shift. Then
\preceq
is a partial order making
(S/\mathcal{D},\preceq)
into a meet semilattice.

Proof.

We know that
\preceq
is reflexive and transitive. We first show that
\preceq
is antisymmetric. Suppose
a\preceq b
and
b\preceq a
. If either
a
or
b
is
0
then
a=b=0
since the only relation of the form
c\preceq 0
is when
c=0
. We may then assume
a,b\neq 0
. By Lemma
6.7
there exists
f_{1},f_{2},g_{1},g_{2}
in
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
such that
[f_{1}]=a,[g_{1}]=b,[f_{2}]=b,[g_{2}]=a
where
f_{1}\leq g_{1}
and
f_{2}\leq g_{2}
. Since
\mathcal{D}
-classes in
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
have unique representatives, we have
f_{1}\leq g_{1}=f_{2}\leq g_{2}=f_{1}
. Thus
f_{1}=f_{2}
and
a=b
.

Next we show that
(S/\mathcal{D},\preceq)
has meets. Suppose that
a,b\in S/\mathcal{D}
. If there is no nonzero
\mathcal{D}
-class
c
such that
c\preceq a,b
, then
a\wedge b=0
. Suppose there exists a nonzero
\mathcal{D}
-class
c
such that
c\preceq a,b
. Then there are idempotents
e_{a},e_{b},
and
e_{c}
in
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
such that
e_{c}\leq e_{a},e_{b}
. We wish to show that
a\wedge b=[e_{a}e_{b}]
. We first prove that
[e_{a}e_{b}]\preceq a
. If
e_{c}=e_{a}e_{b}
we are done, so suppose that
e_{c}<e_{a}e_{b}
. Find
g_{k}
in
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
such that
e_{c}=g_{n}\ll\cdots\ll g_{1}\ll g_{0}=e_{a}
where
[g_{k+1}]\preceq^{\prime}[g_{k}]
for
1\leq k\leq n-1
. Choose the largest
k
so that
e_{a}e_{b}\leq g_{k}
. Notice
k<n
and that
e_{a}e_{b}
and
g_{k+1}
are incomparable. It follows from Lemma
6.4
that
e_{a}e_{b}\in C_{g_{k}}
. So

[e_{a}e_{b}]\preceq^{\prime}[g_{k}]\preceq[e_{a}]=a.

A similar proof shows that
[e_{a}e_{b}]\preceq b
. We now show that
c\preceq[e_{a}e_{b}]
. Let
h_{k}=g_{k}e_{b}
for
0\leq k\leq n
. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
c\not\preceq[h_{k}]
for some
k
and choose the largest such
k
. Note
k<n
. Notice that
h_{k}g_{k+1}=h_{k+1}\neq 0
. Also, since
c\preceq[g_{k}]
, we must have
h_{k}<g_{k}
. Now
g_{k+1}
and
h_{k}
are incomparable. Indeed,
g_{k+1}\leq h_{k}
implies that
g_{k+1}\leq h_{k}\leq g_{k}
. But then
h_{k}=g_{k}
or
h_{k}=g_{k+1}
as
g_{k+1}\ll g_{k}
. This is impossible since
c\preceq[g_{j}]
for each
j
. Alternatively,
h_{k}\leq g_{k+1}
implies that
h_{k}=h_{k}g_{k+1}=h_{k+1}
contradicting the maximality of
k
. Since
g_{k+1}
and
h_{k}
are incomparable it follows that
g_{k+1},h_{k}\in C_{g_{k}}
. But then
h_{k+1}=h_{k}g_{k+1}\in C_{g_{k}}
. Thus
c\preceq[h_{k+1}]\preceq^{\prime}[h_{k}]
, a contradiction. Thus
c\preceq[h_{0}]=[e_{a}e_{b}]
.

Thus we have shown that
(S/\mathcal{D},\preceq)
is a meet semilattice where
a\wedge b=0
if there is no nonzero
c
such that
c\preceq a,b
and otherwise
a\wedge b=[e_{a}e_{b}]
where
e_{a},e_{b}
are
\mathcal{D}
-class representatives of
a
and
b
respectively chosen from
\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
.
∎

7.
Morita Equivalence of Markov Shifts

By the last two propositions, we know that if
S
is the inverse hull of a Markov shift and we choose the set of
\mathcal{D}
-class representatives of
S
to be
\{e_{a}:a\in S/\mathcal{D}\}=(\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O})\cup\{0\}
, then the induced partial order
\preceq
satisfies the conditions given in section 2. Thus we have an inverse semigroup
S^{\preceq}
that is Morita equivalent to
S
. We will now show that
S^{\preceq}
is Morita equivalent to a labelled graph inverse semigroup.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
S
be the inverse hull of a Markov shift and

\{e_{a}:a\in S/\mathcal{D}\}=(\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O})\cup\{0\}

be a set of idempotent
\mathcal{D}
-class representatives for
S
. We have the following

(1)

if
a,b\in S/\mathcal{D}
with
a\wedge b\neq 0
, then
e_{a}e_{b}=e_{a\wedge b}
, and

(2)

if
a,b,c\in S/\mathcal{D}
with
a,b\preceq c
, then
e_{a}e_{b}=e_{a\wedge b}
.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in S/\mathcal{D}
with
a\wedge b\neq 0
. Then
a\wedge b=[e_{a}e_{b}]
. Therefore we have that
e_{a\wedge b}=e_{[e_{a}e_{b}]}=e_{a}e_{b}
.

Next suppose
a,b,c\in S/\mathcal{D}
with
a,b\preceq c
. If
e_{a}e_{b}=0
then
a\wedge b=0
and we are done. Otherwise we can find
g_{i}
such that
e_{a}=g_{n}\ll\cdots\ll g_{1}\ll g_{0}=e_{c}
and
[g_{i+1}]\preceq^{\prime}[g_{i}]
for
0\leq i\leq n-1
. Also there exists
f_{j}
such that
e_{b}=f_{m}\ll\cdots\ll f_{1}\ll f_{0}=e_{c}
and
[f_{j+1}]\preceq^{\prime}[f_{j}]
for
0\leq i\leq m-1
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
f_{j}
and
g_{i}
are incomparable for
i,j>0
(we could replace
c
with
c^{\prime}=[f_{i}g_{j}]\preceq c
where
i
and
j
are appropriately chosen if necessary). Notice then that for all
i,j
nonzero we have
g_{i}
and
f_{j}
incomparable with nonzero product. Using Lemma
6.4
we have that
e_{a},e_{b}\in C_{e_{c}}
. Thus
e_{a}e_{b}\in C_{e_{c}}
and
0\neq[e_{a}e_{b}]\preceq a,b
. Thus

e_{a\wedge b}=e_{[e_{a}e_{b}]}=e_{a}e_{b}.

∎

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
S
be the inverse hull of a Markov shift and let
S^{\preceq}
be as defined above. Then

\mathcal{C}=\{[a,e_{a},a]:e_{a}\in\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}\}\cup\{0\}
is a coherent set for
S^{\preceq}
. Moreover,
(\mathcal{C},\leq)
is order isomorphic to
(S/\mathcal{D},\preceq)
.

Proof.

We first show that the map
\sigma:S/\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}
where
\sigma(a)=[a,e_{a},a]
is an order isomorphism. Since
[a,e_{a},a]=[b,e_{b},b]
if and only if
e_{a}=e_{b}
it follows that
\sigma
is a bijection. Next, suppose that
a\preceq b
with
a\neq 0
. By Lemma
6.7
, we have
e_{a}\leq e_{b}
. Since
e_{a}\leq e_{b}
and
a\wedge b=a\neq 0
, it follows by Proposition
2.4
that
[a,e_{a},a]\leq[b,e_{b},b]
. Conversely, suppose that
0\neq[a,e_{a},a]\leq[b,e_{b},b]
. Then
e_{a}\leq e_{b}
and
a\wedge b\neq 0
. By Proposition
7.1
(1),
e_{a\wedge b}=e_{a}e_{b}=e_{a}
. Thus
a\preceq b
.

Next we prove that
\mathcal{C}
satisfies the three properties given in Definition
5.1
. Let
[a,e_{a},a],[b,e_{b},b]
. Since
0\in\mathcal{C}
, we may assume that

[a,e_{a},a][b,e_{b},b]=[a,e_{a}e_{a\wedge b}e_{b},b]\neq 0.

Thus
a\wedge b\neq 0
and

[a,e_{a}e_{a\wedge b}e_{b},b]=[a,e_{a\wedge b},b]=[a\wedge b,e_{a\wedge b},a\wedge b]

where the last equality follows from Proposition
2.4
. Thus
\mathcal{C}
is closed under multiplication.

Next suppose that
0\neq[a,e_{a},a]\leq f\leq[b,e_{b},b]
, for some idempotent
f\in S^{\preceq}
. Then
f=[c,e,c]
for some idempotent
e\leq e_{c}
. Moreover,
0\neq e_{a}\leq e\leq e_{b}
. It follows that
e\in\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
. Write
e=e_{d}
. We have
0\neq e_{a}\leq e_{d}\leq e_{b}
with
a\wedge b\neq 0
. Then
e_{a\wedge b}=e_{a}e_{b}=e_{a}
, so
a\preceq b
. It follows by an argument similar to the proof of Proposition
7.1
(2), that
d\preceq b
. Moreover, we have that
[c,e,c]=[b\wedge c,e,b\wedge c]
since
b\wedge c\neq 0
. Also
a\wedge b\wedge c=a\wedge c\neq 0
. So, replacing
c
with
b\wedge c
if necessary, we may assume that
c\preceq b
.

Since
c,d\preceq b
, we have
e_{c\wedge d}=e_{c}e_{d}=e_{d}
. So
d\preceq c
. It follows that
f=[c,e_{d},c]=[d,e_{d},d]
. Thus
f\in\mathcal{C}
.

Finally, suppose that
[a,f,a],[b,g,b]\ll[c,e_{c},c]
with
[a,f,a]
and
[b,g,b]
incomparable. We show that

[a,fe_{a\wedge b}g,b]\in\mathcal{C}.

If
[a,fe_{a\wedge b}g,b]=0
then we are done, so we may assume that
fg\neq 0
and
a\wedge b\neq 0
. Then
[a,f,a]\neq 0
and hence
a\wedge c\neq 0
. We have
[a,fe_{a\wedge b}g,b]=[a,fg,b]
. By replacing
a
with
a\wedge c
if necessary, we may assume that
a\preceq c
. Similarly, we may assume that
b\preceq c
.

We have
fg\neq 0
with
f,g\ll e_{c}
and
a\wedge b\neq 0
. If
f=g
then
[a,f,a]=[b,g,b]
, which contradicts the assumption that the two elements are incomparable. Thus
f,g
are incomparable (since each is immediately below
e_{c}
). So
fg,e_{a},e_{b}\in C_{e_{c}}\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
. So
[fg]\preceq a\wedge b
and we have

[a,f,a][b,f,b]=[a,fe_{a\wedge b}g,b]=[a,fg,b]=[[fg],fg,[fg]]\in\mathcal{C}.

∎

At this point, by Theorem
5.17
, we have shown that the inverse hull
S
of a Markov shift is Morita equivalent to a labelled graph inverse semigroup. Moreover, as we will soon see, one can construct the labelled graph associated with
S
using the following “combinatorial” data inside
S
: the set of cores
C_{e}
over all
e\in\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}
together with the idempotents immediately below each core. First we extend the coherent set
\mathcal{C}
to a larger set
CD(S)
of idempotents in
S^{\preceq}
that we view as the combinatorial data associated with
S
.

Definition 7.3
.

Let
S
be the inverse hull of a Markov shift and let
S^{\preceq}
be defined as above. Define

\mathcal{C}^{\ll}=\{[b,f,b]:0\neq f\ll e_{b},\text{and either $f\neq e_{[f]}$ or $[f]\not\preceq b$}\}.

We refer to the set
CD(S)=\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{C}^{\ll}
as the
combinatorial data
of
S
.

The sets
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\ll}
are disjoint. To see this, suppose
0\neq[a,e_{a},a]=[b,f,b]
where
f\leq e_{b}
. Then
a\wedge b\neq 0
and
f=e_{a}
. So
f=e_{[f]}
. Moreover, since
[f]\wedge b\neq 0
we have
e_{[f]\wedge b}=e_{[f]}e_{b}=e_{[f]}
. Thus
[f]\preceq b
. It follows that
[b,f,b]=[[f],e_{[f]},[f]]\in\mathcal{C}
. Recall that a nonzero idempotent of an inverse semigroup is said to be
primitive
if it is minimal in the set of nonzero idempotents.

Proposition 7.4
.

The set
\text{CD}(S)
is a subsemigroup of
S^{\preceq}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{C}^{\ll}
is the set of primitive idempotents of
\text{CD}(S)
.

Proof.

We know
\mathcal{C}
is closed under multiplication by Proposition
7.2
. Let
[a,f,a]\in\mathcal{C}^{\ll}
and
[b,e_{b},b]\in\mathcal{C}
. We will show that
[a,f,a][b,e_{b},b]\neq 0
if and only if
a\preceq b
.
First suppose that
[a,f,a][b,e_{b},b]\neq 0
. Then
e_{b}f\neq 0
and
a\wedge b\neq 0
. Thus
e_{a\wedge b}=e_{a}e_{b}
and
[b,e_{b},b][a,f,a]=[a\wedge b,e_{b}f,a\wedge b]
. There are two cases to consider. First suppose that
f\neq e_{[f]}
. In that case
e_{b}f\in\mathcal{O}
and, since products of distinct elements of
\mathcal{O}
are zero, we must have
f\leq e_{b}
. Hence
f\leq e_{a}e_{b}
. But
f\ll e_{a}
, so either
f=e_{a}e_{b}
or
e_{a}=e_{a}e_{b}
. The first case is impossible since
f\in\mathcal{O}
. In the second case,
e_{a}\leq e_{b}
. But
a\wedge b\neq 0
then implies that
a\preceq b
.

Next, suppose
f=e_{[f]}
and
[f]\not\preceq a
. We claim that
f\leq e_{a}e_{b}
. If
f=e_{[f]}
and
e_{a}e_{b}
are incomparable, then since
e_{[f]}e_{a}e_{b}=fe_{b}\neq 0
it follows that
e_{[f]},e_{a}e_{b}\in C_{e_{a}}
. Thus
[f]\preceq a
, a contradiction. But if
e_{a}e_{b}\leq e_{[f]}
, then

[a\wedge b,e_{a}e_{b},a\wedge b]\leq[a,f,a]\leq[a,e_{a},a]

implies
[a,f,a]\in\mathcal{C},
another contradiction. We conclude that
f\leq e_{a}e_{b}
. Since
f\ll e_{a}
we have
f=e_{a}e_{b}
or
e_{a}\leq e_{b}
. We have already seen that
f=e_{a}e_{b}
implies
[a,f,a]\in\mathcal{C}
. So we conclude that
e_{a}\leq e_{b}
, and hence
a\preceq b
. Conversely, if
a\preceq b
then
f\leq e_{a}\leq e_{b}
and

[a,f,a][b,e_{b},b]=[a,fe_{a}e_{b},b]=[a,f,a].

So we have shown that
[a,f,a][b,e_{b},b]=[a,f,a]
, when the product is nonzero.

Next suppose that
[a,f,a],[b,g,b]\in C^{\ll}
. We claim that

[a,f,a][b,g,b]=\begin{cases}[a,f,a]&\text{if $a=b$ and $f=g$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Of the four cases to consider, we prove the claim when
f=e_{[f]}
,
g=e_{[g]}
,
[f]\not\preceq a
, and
[g]\not\preceq b
. The other cases involve similar arguments. Suppose that
[a,f,a][b,g,b]\neq 0
. Then
a\wedge b\neq 0
and
fg\neq 0
. We consider the pair of idempotents
f
and
e_{a}e_{b}
. Suppose that they are incomparable. Since
fe_{a}e_{b}\neq 0
, we have
f,e_{a}e_{b}\in C_{e_{a}}
. Thus
[f]\preceq a
, a contradiction. So
f
and
e_{a}e_{b}
are comparable. But if
e_{a}e_{b}\leq f\leq e_{a}
we have
[f]\preceq a
. So
f\leq e_{a}e_{b}
. Since
f\ll e_{a}
, we know
f=e_{a}e_{b}
or
e_{a}e_{b}=e_{a}
. The first case is impossible since it implies that
[f]=a\wedge b\preceq a
, so
e_{a}e_{b}=e_{a}
and hence
a\wedge b=a
. That is,
a\preceq b
. A similar argument considering the pair
g
and
e_{a}e_{b}
shows that
b\preceq a
. So
a=b
. Since
f,g\ll e_{a}
with nonzero product we quickly see that
f=g
.

The proof that
C^{\ll}
is the set of primitive idempotents in
CD(S)
now follows quickly from the rules for multiplication that we have proved.

∎

Proposition 7.8
.

Suppose that
S
is the inverse hull of a Markov shift. Then the labelled graph associated with
S
is isomorphic to the labelled graph associated with the coherent set
\mathcal{C}
in
S^{\preceq}
.

Proof.

Consider the map
\sigma:S/\mathcal{D}\to S^{\preceq}/\mathcal{D}
defined by
\sigma([f])=[f^{\prime}]
, where
f^{\prime}=[[f],f,[f]]
. By Proposition
2.3
,
\sigma
is a well-defined bijection and the restriction to nonzero
\mathcal{D}
-classes defines a bijection
\sigma:\mathcal{E}^{0}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}^{0}_{2}
. Let
x_{a,f,b}
be an edge in the labelled graph associated with
S
. Then
f\ll e_{a}
and
b\preceq[f]
. Also, either
f\neq e_{[f]}
or
[f]\not\preceq a
. Let
\overline{f}=[a,f,a]
. We claim that
x_{\sigma(a),\overline{f},\sigma(b)}
is an edge in the labelled graph associated with
S^{\preceq}
. First note that
0\neq[a,f,a]\ll[a,e_{a},a]
. Also
e_{b}\leq e_{[f]}
by Lemma
6.7
, so

[b,e_{b},b]=[[f],e_{b},[f]]\leq[[f],e_{[f]},[f]].

Thus,
\sigma(b)\in B_{[\overline{f}]}
. Finally, suppose for the sake of contradiction that
\overline{f}=e_{[\overline{f}]}
. Then
[a,f,a]=[[f],e_{f},[f]]
. So
f=e_{[f]}
and
a\wedge[f]\neq 0
. Then
e_{[f]}=e_{[f]}e_{a}=e_{[f]\wedge a}
. Thus
[f]\preceq a
. So we have that
f=e_{[f]}
and
[f]\preceq a
, a contradiction. This shows that
\overline{f}\neq e_{[\overline{f}]}
, and we conclude
x_{\sigma(a),\overline{f},\sigma(b)}
is an edge. It is easily verified that
\pi_{1}:\mathcal{E}^{1}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}^{1}_{2}
defined by

\pi_{1}(x_{a,f,b})=x_{\sigma(a),\overline{f},\sigma(b)}

is a bijection.
By the same reasoning, the map
\pi_{2}
sending
(a,f)\mapsto(\sigma(a),\overline{f})
defines a bijection on labels. One can quickly check that the maps satisfy
s(\pi_{1}(e))=\sigma(s(e))
,
r(\pi_{1}(e))=\sigma(r(e))
, and
\mathcal{L}(\pi_{1}(e))=\pi_{2}(\mathcal{L}(e))
.

Finally we prove the main theorem of the paper which shows that the labelled graph of the inverse hull of a Markov shift is a complete Morita equivalence invariant among inverse hulls of Markov shifts.

Theorem 7.9
.

Suppose that
S
and
T
are the inverse hulls of Markov shifts. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

S
is Morita equivalent to
T
.

(2)

There exists a
\mathcal{D}
-class preserving isomorphism

\pi:CD(S^{\preceq})\to CD(T^{\preceq}).

(3)

The labelled graphs associated with
S
and
T
are isomorphic with the map on vertices being an order isomorphism.

Proof.

Throughout the proof assume that we have chosen sets

\{e_{a}:a\in S/\mathcal{D}\}=(\mathcal{O}_{S}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}_{S})\cup\{0\}\text{ and }\{f_{v}:v\in T/\mathcal{D}\}=(\mathcal{O}_{T}^{\uparrow}-\mathcal{O}_{T})\cup\{0\}

of
\mathcal{D}
-class representatives of
S
and
T
respectively.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2): First, suppose that
S
is Morita equivalent to
T
via an equivalence functor
\mathcal{F}:C(S)\to C(T)
. We will prove that there is a
\mathcal{D}
-class preserving isomorphism
\pi:CD(S^{\preceq})\to CD(T^{\preceq})
. By Proposition
2.1
(1), there exists a bijection
\sigma:S/\mathcal{D}\to T/\mathcal{D}
, such that
\sigma([e])=[\mathcal{F}(e)]
for all idempotents
e\in S
. We define
\pi
on
\mathcal{C}_{S}
by
\pi([a,e_{a},a])=[\sigma(a),f_{\sigma(a)},\sigma(a)]
. Next, for a given nonzero
a\in S/\mathcal{D}
, let
e_{a}^{\ll}=\{f:0\neq f\ll e_{a}\}
. Then
e_{a}^{\ll}
is the disjoint union of the sets
e_{a,b}^{\ll}=\{f\in e_{a}^{\ll}:[f]=b\}
over all nonzero
b\in S/\mathcal{D}
. By Proposition
2.1
(2), there is a
\mathcal{D}
-preserving isomorphism
\tau_{a}:e_{a}Se_{a}\to f_{\sigma(a)}Tf_{\sigma(a)}
.

For nonzero
b\in S/\mathcal{D}
with
b\not\preceq a
, the restriction of
\tau_{a}
to
e_{a,b}^{\ll}
gives a bijection
\pi_{a,b}:e_{a,b}^{\ll}\to f_{\sigma(a),\sigma(b)}^{\ll}
. Next suppose
b\preceq a
. We claim that
e_{b}\ll e_{a}
if and only if
f_{\sigma(b)}\ll f_{\sigma(a)}
. Suppose
e_{b}\ll e_{a}
. Then
e_{b}\in C_{e_{a}}
and there exists
f\in\tau_{a}(C_{e_{a}})=C_{f_{\sigma(a)}}
with
f\ll f_{\sigma(a)}
and
f\mathcal{D}f_{\sigma(b)}
. As noted in the proof of Lemma
6.7
,
C_{f_{\sigma(a)}}\subseteq\mathcal{O}^{\uparrow}_{T}-\mathcal{O}_{T}
. Thus
f=f_{\sigma(b)}
. The converse is similar. It follows that we can find a bijection
\pi_{a,b}:e_{a,b}^{\ll}-\{e_{b}\}\to f_{\sigma(a),\sigma(b)}^{\ll}-\{f_{\sigma(b)}\}
, possibly different than the restriction of
\tau_{a}
. For
[a,f,a]\in\mathcal{C}^{\ll}_{S^{\preceq}}
we define
\pi([a,f,a])=[\sigma(a),\pi_{a,[f]}(f),\sigma(a)]
. One can quickly verify that
\pi
is well-defined. (Note the significant detail in the case that
[f]\preceq a
that the range of
\pi_{a,[f]}
does not include
f_{\sigma{([f])}}
.)

By construction,
\pi
is a
\mathcal{D}
-class preserving bijection. We show that
\pi
is an order isomorphism (and hence a semilattice isomorphism as well). Let
E,F\in CD(S^{\preceq})
with
E\leq F
. We have already considered the case that
E,F\in\mathcal{C}_{S^{\preceq}}
. We may assume
0\neq E\in\mathcal{C}^{\ll}_{S^{\preceq}}
. If
F\in\mathcal{C}^{\ll}_{S^{\preceq}}
, then by Proposition
7.4
,
F=E
and
\pi(E)=\pi(F)
. Suppose
F\in\mathcal{C}_{S^{\preceq}}
and write
E=[a,f,a],F=[b,e_{b},b]
. From the proof of Proposition
7.4
we know that
a\preceq b
. Thus
f\ll e_{a}\leq e_{b}
. We have that
\pi_{a,[f]}(f)\leq f_{\sigma(a)}
. Moreover
a\preceq b
implies
f_{\sigma(a)}\leq f_{\sigma(b)}
. Thus
\pi_{a,[f]}(f)\leq f_{\sigma(a)}\leq f_{\sigma(b)}
and hence
\pi(F)\leq\pi(E)
. By a similar argument, if
\pi(E)\leq\pi(F)
then
E\leq F
. Thus
\pi
is a
\mathcal{D}
-class preserving isomorphism.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3): Next suppose that
\pi:CD(S^{\preceq})\to CD(T^{\preceq})
is a
\mathcal{D}
-class preserving isomorphism and let
(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1})
and
(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{L}_{2})
be the labelled graphs with label sets
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
associated with
S
and
T
respectively. Define a map
\sigma_{0}:\mathcal{E}^{0}_{1}\to\mathcal{E}^{0}_{2}
by
\sigma(a)=u
if and only if
\pi([a,e_{a},a])=[u,e_{u},u]
. Note that
[v,e_{v},v]=[u,e_{u},u]
if and only if
u=v
, so
\sigma_{0}
is a well-defined bijection. We also have
a\preceq b
if and only if
\sigma_{0}(a)\preceq\sigma_{0}(b)
, so that
\sigma_{0}
is an order isomorphism.

Next, let
[a,f,a]\in\mathcal{C}_{S}^{\ll}
and write
\pi([a,f,a])=[u,g,u]
, where
[u,g,u]\ll[u,f_{u},u]
. We aim to show that
u=\sigma_{0}(a)
. We have that
[u,g,u]\ll[u,f_{u},u]
and
[u,g,u]\ll\pi([a,e_{a},a])=[\sigma_{0}(a),f_{\sigma_{0}(a)},\sigma_{0}(a)]
. Thus
u\wedge\sigma_{0}(a)\neq 0,g\ll f_{u},
and
g\ll f_{\sigma_{0}(a)}
. If
f_{u}
and
f_{\sigma_{0}(a)}
are incomparable, then it follows that
g=e_{[g]}
with
[g]\preceq u
, a contradiction. Then since
g\ll f_{u},
and
g\ll f_{\sigma_{0}(a)}
we must have
f_{u}=f_{\sigma_{0}(a)}
and
u=\sigma_{0}(a)
.

Suppose now that
x_{a,f,b}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1}
. We define a map
\sigma_{1}:\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1}\to\mathcal{E}_{2}^{1}
by
\sigma_{1}(x_{a,f,b})=x_{\sigma_{0}(a),g,\sigma_{0}(b)}
where
\pi([a,f,a])=[\sigma_{0}(a),g,\sigma_{0}(a)]
. We verify that
\sigma_{1}(x_{a,f,b})
is indeed an edge in
\mathcal{E}_{2}
. Then it can be quickly shown that
\sigma_{1}
is a well-defined bijection. Since
x_{a,f,b}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1}
, we have
f\ll e_{a}
,
b\preceq[f]
, and either
f\neq e_{[f]}
or
[f]\not\preceq a
. Now
g\ll f_{\sigma_{0}(a)}
with either
g\neq f_{[g]}
or
[g]\not\preceq\sigma_{0}(a)
. Also,

\displaystyle[\sigma_{0}(b),e_{\sigma_{0}(b)},\sigma_{0}(b)]

\displaystyle=\pi([b,e_{b},b])

\displaystyle\leq\pi([[f],e_{[f]},[f]])

\displaystyle=[\sigma_{0}([f]),e_{\sigma_{0}([f])},\sigma_{0}([f])]

implies that
\sigma_{0}(b)\leq\sigma_{0}([f])
. Thus
\sigma_{1}:\mathcal{E}_{1}^{1}\to\mathcal{E}_{2}^{1}
is a bijection. Similarly,
(a,f)\mapsto(\sigma_{0}(a),g)
defines a bijection
\sigma_{2}
from
\mathcal{A}_{1}
to
\mathcal{A}_{2}
. For each edge
e
we have
s(\sigma_{1}(e))=\sigma_{0}(s(e))
,
r(\sigma_{1}(e))=\sigma_{0}(r(e))
, and
\mathcal{L}(\sigma_{1}(e))=\sigma_{2}(\mathcal{L}(e))
. Thus
(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1})
is isomorphic to
(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{L}_{2})
.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1): Finally, let
(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1})
and
(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{L}_{2})
be the labelled graphs associated with
S
and
T
respectively and suppose that
\pi=(\pi_{0},\pi_{1},\pi_{2})
is an isomorphism from
(\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{1})
to
(\mathcal{E}_{2},\mathcal{L}_{2})
and
\pi_{0}
is an order isomorphism. By Proposition
7.8
, the labelled graphs associated with the coherent sets
\mathcal{C}_{S}
and
\mathcal{C}_{T}
are isomorphic via a map
\pi^{\prime}=(\pi^{\prime}_{0},\pi^{\prime}_{1},\pi^{\prime}_{2})
where
\pi^{\prime}_{0}([a,e_{a},a])=[\pi_{0}(a),e_{\pi_{0}(a)},\pi_{0}(a)]
is an order isomorphism. It follows that the labelled graph spaces associated with
\mathcal{C}_{S}
and
\mathcal{C}_{T}
are isomorphic and hence the corresponding labelled graph inverse semigroups are isomorphic. Thus
S^{\preceq}
is Morita equivalent to

and hence

is Morita equivalent to

.

∎
