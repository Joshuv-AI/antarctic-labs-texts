The effect of intravenous anesthesia on postoperative cognitive function in patients undergoing painless gastroscopy: a meta-analysis
Authors: Zhen Jia, Yubai Leng, Yetie Fan, Wanwan Ji, Bin Zhou, Zhiming Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWith the development of comfortable medical care, the application of intravenous anesthesia in painless gastroscopy is becoming increasingly widespread. However, anesthetic drugs may have adverse effects on postoperative cognitive function, and the risk of postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) is particularly worthy of attention in the elderly population. Our aim is to evaluate the effect of intravenous anesthesia on postoperative cognitive function (POCF) in patients undergoing painless gastroscopy, with a focus on identifying potential risks of POCD and guiding clinical anesthesia practices.MethodsAnalyzing randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in English that assessed the impact of intravenous anesthesia on POCF in patients over 60 years old undergoing elective gastrointestinal endoscopy. The literature search spanned databases including PubMed、Embase、Web of Science、Scopus、Cochrane, and Clinical Key. Study inclusion and exclusion criteria were rigorously defined, and the Cochrane bias risk assessment tool was utilized to evaluate study quality. Meta-analyses were performed using RevMan 5.3, with heterogeneity assessed via I-square statistics.ResultsFrom a total of 432 articles identified, 7 studies involving 219 patients met the inclusion criteria. The meta-analysis revealed no significant difference in the incidence rates of POCD on Day 1 and Day 3 postoperatively between patients undergoing intravenous versus inhalation anesthesia. However, on Day 7, the incidence of POCD was significantly lower in the intravenous anesthesia group, with a combined Odds Ratio (OR) of 0.96 (95% Confidence Interval (CI): 0.73–1.26, I^2 = 34%, n = 7, P < 0.00001). Furthermore, plasma levels of S-100β protein, a marker for neural injury, were significantly lower in the intravenous anesthesia group, with a Mean Difference (MD) of 0.34 (95% CI: 0.23–0.48, I^2 = 0%, n = 5, P < 0.00001).ConclusionIntravenous anesthesia for painless gastroscopy appears to be associated with a lower incidence of POCD on Day 7 postoperatively, suggesting a potentially reduced risk of early postoperative cognitive decline compared to inhalation anesthesia. The findings indicate that intravenous anesthesia may be preferable in minimizing the risk of POCD, particularly in older adults undergoing painless gastroscopy. However, further high-quality, large-scale RCTs are warranted to validate these results and explore the effects across different patient demographics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04403-y.Keywords: Postoperative cognitive dysfunction, Intravenous anesthesia, Painless gastroscopy, Meta-Analysis, Postoperative cognitive function

Background

Gastroscopy is a critical diagnostic tool for evaluating the upper gastrointestinal tract, often requiring sedation or anesthesia to ensure patient comfort and procedure efficacy [1]. With the advancements in medical practices, painless gastroscopy under intravenous anesthesia has become increasingly prevalent, providing a more tolerable experience for patients [2]. However, the implications of anesthesia, particularly on postoperative cognitive function (POCF), have garnered considerable attention, given the potential risks associated with cognitive impairment after surgical procedures. This concern is particularly acute in the elderly and those with pre-existing cognitive deficits, where the effects of anesthesia could exacerbate postoperative cognitive decline [3].

Postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) represents a common complication affecting a subset of patients following anesthesia and surgery, characterized by impairments in memory, concentration, and overall cognitive capacity [4]. While the exact mechanisms remain unclear, proposed theories suggest that factors such as systemic inflammation, neuroinflammation, and direct neurotoxicity may contribute to its development. The variability in anesthetic agents and techniques, combined with patient-specific variables, complicates the understanding of POCD’s pathophysiology and risk factors [5]– [6]. Meta-analysis serves as a powerful tool to synthesize research findings, offering insights into patterns and effects that individual studies might not detect [7]– [8]. By aggregating data from various studies examining the impact of intravenous anesthesia on POCF in patients undergoing painless gastroscopy, a meta-analysis can provide a comprehensive overview of the evidence, elucidate potential risk factors, and guide clinical practices to mitigate adverse cognitive outcomes [9].

This study aims to conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis of existing literature to assess the effect of intravenous anesthesia on postoperative cognitive function in patients undergoing painless gastroscopy. By evaluating the extent and nature of cognitive changes post-procedure, this research seeks to contribute to a deeper understanding of POCD, enhance patient care, and inform anesthesia practices in gastroscopy and potentially broader surgical contexts.

Research methodology

Literature search strategy

The search period for this study covers the period from its establishment to December 2024. The specific search strategy is provided in the supplementary material. This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) (https://www.equator-network.org/) guidelines and has been registered in INPLASY (INPLASY202590084, on inplasy.com).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Study Type: Clinical research published domestically and internationally, limited to English-language publications; Design: Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs); Participants: Patients aged over 60 years, scheduled for elective gastrointestinal endoscopy (e.g., gastroscopy and gynecological surgeries); ASA Classification: Grades I-III; Other Criteria: Surgeries expected to last more than 2 h, patients capable of reading without severe hearing and visual impairments; Data Requirements: Studies must include the purpose of the study, detailed statistical methods, and results that can provide weighted mean differences with 95% CI or data convertible to OR values with 95% CI, along with the years of study conduct and publication; Comparison Groups: Control group undergoing intravenous anesthesia, with experimental groups receiving inhalational or non-intravenous forms of anesthesia.

Exclusion criteria

Language: Non-Chinese or English publications; Health Conditions: Severe cardiovascular, respiratory, liver, kidney, or central nervous system diseases, life expectancy less than 3 months; Duplication: Repetitive reports; Cognitive Impairment: Mini-Mental State Examination scores below 23; Current Medication: Use of sedatives, antidepressants, or corticosteroids; Incomplete Data: Missing crucial information, such as group case numbers; Publication Type: Calls for papers, conference announcements, and reviews.

Outcome measures

Monitoring heart rate, non-invasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry, ECG, respiratory rate, and BIS during anesthesia; recording gastroscopy operation time, awakening time (from the end of the examination to the moment the patient can accurately respond with their name and age), dosage of intravenous anesthetics like propofol, and discharge time (from awakening to meeting discharge criteria based on “Expert Consensus on Sedation/Anesthesia for Digestive Endoscopy in China”). Mental activity tests are conducted 30 min before painless gastroscopy and upon meeting discharge criteria. If mental activity test results do not revert to preoperative levels upon meeting discharge criteria, a third test is conducted after resting for 30 min. Mental activity tests include: (1) Digit Cancellation Test, (2) Digit Symbol Test, and (3) Pegboard Test.

Literature quality assessment

Two researchers independently evaluate the quality of the literature and discuss their findings. In cases of disagreement, a third researcher participates in the discussion to reach a decision. This study employs the Cochrane bias risk assessment tool for RCTs, which includes seven items rated as “low risk,” “high risk,” or “unclear” of bias. A grade is assigned based on the likelihood of various biases occurring: “A” for minimum possibility, “B” for moderate possibility, and “C” for a high possibility of bias.

Statistical methods

Meta-analyses are performed using the analysis module in RevMan 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark), with Relative Risks (RRs) analyzed within a 95% confidence interval. Heterogeneity among the included studies is assessed using I-square statistics and the heterogeneity Chi-square test before combining study results. Values of I2 > 50% or P < 0.10 indicate significant heterogeneity among studies. The overall RR or SMD scores are calculated using a random-effects model in the presence of heterogeneity; otherwise, a fixed-effects model is used.

Results

Literature search outcomes

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, Cochrane, and Clinical Key databases, targeting titles, abstracts, and keywords, yielding a total of 432 articles. Utilizing EndNote X9, duplicate publications were identified and removed, leaving 54 articles, of which 32 clinical trials were retained. Further screening of the remaining studies’ abstracts and titles identified 26 studies relevant to intravenous anesthesia, inhalation anesthesia, or patient-related outcomes. A full-text review of these studies ultimately selected 7 for inclusion. Bias risk assessment and study quality

We utilized RevMan 5.3 to summarize the bias of the included studies, as illustrated in Figs. 2 and 3. The risks were categorized as high, low, or unclear. Among the 7 studies [10–16] assessed for bias risk, one was identified as a retrospective study with a high overall risk of bias. Eight studies mentioned the methods used for random sequence generation, and seven described how allocation concealment was implemented. Six studies did not blind participants and researchers, and four studies did not blind outcome assessment.

Meta-analysis summary

The incidence of postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) on Day 1, with a sample size of 98, reported the occurrence rates of POCD. In three studies, the incidence rates of POCD in the intravenous anesthesia group on Day 1 were 2, 3, and 5, respectively. The combined Odds Ratio (OR) was 0.96 (95% Confidence Interval (CI): 0.38–2.40), with an I^2 of 30%, as shown in Fig. 4. Using a fixed-effect model, the result was not statistically significant (P = 0.93).

Incidence of POCD at 1days postoperative comparison between intravenous anesthesia and inhalation anesthesia

The incidence of POCD on Day 3 was reported in studies with a sample size of 168. In five studies, the combined OR was 0.02 (95% CI: −0.07 to 0.11), with an I^2 of 0%, as shown in Fig. 5, using a fixed-effect model. The result was not statistically significant (P = 0.60).

Incidence of POCD at 3 days postoperative comparison between intravenous anesthesia and inhalation anesthesia

The incidence of POCD on Day 7, with a sample size of 176, was reported. In five studies, the incidence rates of POCD in the intravenous anesthesia group on Day 7 were 21, 8, 12, 12, and 11, respectively. The combined OR was 1.87 (95% CI: 1.48–2.36), with an I^2 of 32%, as shown in Fig. 6. Using a fixed-effect model, the result showed a significant difference (P < 0.00001).

Incidence of POCD at 7 days postoperative comparison between intravenous anesthesia and inhalation anesthesia

For plasma S-100 β protein levels, in six studies, the plasma levels of S-100 β protein (ng/mL) in the intravenous anesthesia group were 1.57 ± 0.45, 0.98 ± 0.56, 0.87 ± 0.32, and 1.34 ± 0.34, 1.65 ± 0.43, and 1.32 ± 0.54. The combined Mean Difference (MD) was 0.34 (95% CI: 0.23–0.46), favoring the intravenous anesthesia group, with an I^2 of 0%, n = 5, as shown in Fig. 7. Using a fixed-effect model, the result was statistically significant (P < 0.00001).

Plasma S-100 β protein levels (ng/mL) postoperative comparison between intravenous anesthesia and inhalation anesthesia

The incidence of delayed neurocognitive recovery 5–7 days post-surgery was reported in three studies, with the intravenous anesthesia group showing incidence rates of 6, 7, and 4, while the inhalation anesthesia group had 7, 8, and 2. The combined OR was 1.06 (95% CI: 0.45–2.49), n = 3, as shown in Fig. 8. Using a fixed-effect model, the result was not statistically significant (P = 0.90).

Discussion

In this study, we primarily focused on the impact of intravenous anesthesia on postoperative cognitive function (POCF) during painless gastroscopy procedures [17]. With advancements in medical practices leading to the widespread adoption of painless gastroscopy, intravenous anesthesia has become essential for ensuring patient comfort and procedural efficacy [18]. However, the effects of anesthesia, especially concerning POCF, have raised widespread concern due to the occurrence of postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) in some patients undergoing anesthesia and surgery [19]. This condition affects memory, attention, and overall cognitive capabilities. Through a systematic review and meta-analysis, we analyzed seven studies encompassing 219 patients undergoing painless gastroscopy. The findings indicate that there was no significant difference in the incidence rates of POCD in the early postoperative period (Day 1 and Day 3) between patients undergoing intravenous and inhalation anesthesia. However, on Day 7 postoperatively, the incidence of POCD was significantly lower in the intravenous anesthesia group, suggesting a potentially lower risk of early postoperative cognitive decline. Additionally, the lower levels of plasma S-100β protein in the intravenous anesthesia group may imply a reduced risk of neural injury, as S-100β protein is a biomarker for neurodamage.

Different anesthetic drugs may have different effects on the occurrence of POCD, with a study of 96 elderly patients undergoing general anesthesia for craniotomy showing that patients who received sufentanil anesthesia on the first day after surgery had a lower incidence of POCD than those who received fentanyl anesthesia. However, given that POCD may occur in the later stages after surgery, longer-term longitudinal tracking of these anesthetics differences is needed. Contradictory results have also appeared in different studies. For example, in a study of 296 elderly patients undergoing hip replacement surgery, patients who received propofol supplementation during lumbar plex us and T12 paravertebral nerve blockade had a higher incidence of POCD on the third and seventh days after surgery compared to those who received remifentanil [20]. Therefore, it is reasonable that findings in our manuscript indicated difference postoperative 7 days.

The relationship between the incidence of POCD and the mode of anesthesia remains a critical focus in anesthesiology research. Our findings on Day 7 postoperatively, which suggest a potential mitigating effect of intravenous anesthesia on early postoperative cognitive decline, are particularly noteworthy. The lower plasma levels of S-100β protein further support this observation, indicating a possibly reduced risk of neuro damage.

POCD occurs in approximately 10% to 54% of patients in the weeks following surgery. Volatile anesthetics are considered potential pathogenic factors for POCD, possibly by altering the phosphorylation of microtubule and tau proteins. Although existing meta-analyses do not provide evidence that a specific volatile an esthetic increases the risk of POCD, animal studies have shown that exposure to a single dose of is of lurane can cause neuronal apoptosis in the hippocampus of aged animals. Additionally, in transgenic and wild-type mice, both sevoflurane and is of lurane have been shown to increase accumulation of amyloid-β1–42 (Aβ1–42) and cell apoptosis. Other animal models have demonstrated that sevoflurane is associated with accumulation of phosphorylated tau protein in the hippocampus. Different anesthetics may have different effects on the occurrence of POCD, as a study of 96 elderly patients who underwent craniotomy under general anesthesia showed that the incidence of POCD was lower in patients who received sufentanil anesthesia on the first post operative day than in those who received fentanyl anesthesia. However, given that POCD may occur in later stages after surgery, longer-term longitudinal tracking of these anesthetics’ differences is necessary. Contradictory results have also been reported in different studies. For example, in a study of 296 elderly patients undergoing hip replacement surgery, patients who received propofol supplementation during lumbar plexus and T12 paravertebral block had a higher incidence of POCD on the third and seventh post operative days than those who received remifentanil.

In terms of improving cognitive impairment, intravenous anesthesia is associated with an increase in MM SE and Mo CA scores, as well as a decrease in serum S100β and NSE release. Intravenous anesthesia can also reverse systemic stress and inflammatory responses caused by procedures such as tracheal intubation, as evidenced by lowered levels of cortisol and IL-6. An esthesia and surgery can induce central nervous system inflammation and neuronal apoptosis, promoting the elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, and the reduction of regulatory cytokine IL-10, playing a crucial role in the occurrence of POCD. S100 protein, as an acid calcium-binding protein, is a damage-associated molecular pattern molecule inducing subsequent inflammatory processes and can serve as a biomarker for pro-inflammatory and acute brain injury. NSE, acting as a cytoplasmic enzyme, is a biomarker for neuronal damage when its serum concentration increases. The increased release of S100β, NSE, and IL-6 may worsen the severity of POCD [21]. Intravenous anesthesia can reduce neurotrauma by decreasing the release of inflammatory factors. Therefore, the lower incidence of POCD on the seventh postoperative day in the intravenous anesthesia group in this study is understandable [22–24].

Despite the valuable insights provided, this study is not without limitations. Firstly, the limited number of included studies may affect the generalizability of the results. Secondly, the majority of the study samples originate from specific regions, potentially limiting the global applicability of the findings [25]– [26]. Lastly, differences in the assessment methods for POCD across studies could impact the comparability of the results [27]– [28].

Overall, our findings lend support to the beneficial impact of intravenous anesthesia on POCF in the context of painless gastroscopy, particularly in terms of reducing the risk of early postoperative POCD and lowering the risk of neural injury [29]. However, given the study’s limitations, further high-quality, large-scale randomized controlled trials are warranted to validate these findings and explore the effects of intravenous anesthesia on POCF across different patient demographics, especially among the elderly and those with pre-existing cognitive impairments [30]– [31]. Understanding the specific impacts of different anesthetics and administration methods on the risk of POCD will provide essential guidance for clinical anesthesia practice. Future research should focus on the long-term effects of various drugs used in intravenous anesthesia on cognitive function and explore potential strategies to reduce the incidence rate of POCD, such as optimizing the selection and administration of anesthetic drugs [32]– [33]. Additionally, considering the potential impact of POCD on patients’ quality of life and their families, future studies should also assess the effects of different anesthesia methods on patients’ postoperative quality of life [34].

Conclusion

In conclusion, while this study presents positive evidence regarding the use of intravenous anesthesia to reduce the risk of postoperative cognitive dysfunction in painless gastroscopy, further research is needed to explore and optimize anesthesia management strategies to ensure the cognitive health and overall well-being of patients postoperatively. Through in-depth investigation and comprehensive analysis, we can better understand the relationship between intravenous anesthesia and POCD, providing evidence-based guidance for clinical practice to enhance the quality of postoperative recovery for patients.
