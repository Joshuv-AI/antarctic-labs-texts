Standardization of surgical technique of the fundoring method for laparoscopic gastric bypass
Authors: Oral Ospanov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOne of the successful strategies for avoiding reflux esophagitis and other issues following gastric bypass is the FundoRing method of gastric bypass with fundoplication employing the excluded (remnant) portion of the stomach. This study aims to describe the standardization of the different specific steps of surgical techniques of FundoRing for performing one anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB) and describes the two-year outcomes of the standardized procedure compared to non-standardized surgery. This work describes the update of the details of standardization of the surgical technique of the FundoRing method for laparoscopic gastric bypass based on previously non-standardized experiments. The study design was a clinical, comparative, two-year follow-up study involving two groups using a one-anastomotic gastric bypass (OAGB). FundoRingOAGB was performed in the first (standardization) group, and in the second group (a non-standardized group), the NissenOAGB was used. The specific steps of the FundoRing surgical procedure are: mandatory division of the gastrosplenic ligament for the creation of a circular fundoplication 5 cm in length, and supplemented by a partial fundoplication of another 2 cm. The total size of the fundoplication wrap is 7 cm. A suture of fundoplication wrap is created with autologous tissues at 3 and 6 o’clock on the “dial” of the gastric pouch between the left (anterior) and right (posterior) parts of the wrap, thus performing double calibration, and the FundoRing is ready. At 24 months post-treatment, BMI changes were as follows: from 40.7 ± 5.9 (31–53) kg/m2 to 24.3 ± 2.8 (19–29) kg/m2 in “after standardisation” (first) group, versus 40.9 ± 6.3 (33–53) kg/m2 to 28.1 ± 3.9 (24–34) kg/m2 in “before standardisation” (second) group. Over 2 years, no cases of serious complications were observed in the FundoRingOAGB group (standardization group) compared to the NissenOAGB (non-standardization group), which had 11 (22%) complications (p = 001). The surgical technique of FundoRing has specific, distinctive steps. It involves a wider mobilization of the fundus and the need to perform double calibration of the wrap when creating a circular or partial fundoplication, oriented toward the stapler suture of the gastric pouch to create a living ring (band) from autologous tissue. Introduction

One-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB) has entered everyday bariatric practice1,2 and is recognized by IFSO3.

The innovative contribution to the development of OAGB has also been published4,5. Authors proposed various modifications for stapleless and stapler use in OAGB6,7.

Yet, criticism of OAGB due to the possibility of postoperative bile reflux esophagitis remains8. Some prominent surgeons believe that the clinical significance of bile reflux is exaggerated9.

However, when persistent marginal ulcers and bile reflux occur, most surgeons perform a revision conversion of OAGB to the Roux-en-Y method (RYGB), with a conversion rate of 4.1%10–12. RYGB does not rule out the possibility of acid reflux esophagitis after the surgical procedure13. Interestingly, a significant proportion of patients with obesity and GERD continue to suffer from reflux after RYGB, and even some obese individuals without GERD develop de novo GERD after RYGB14.

The FundoRing method for OAGB is an invention that has been studied for several years and is increasingly being used in some countries15. This approach has resulted in better results than NissenOAGB16,17. However, a detailed update describing the standard of surgical technique for the FundoRing method and results compared with the Nissen (nonstandard) method is presented for the first time. We consider our FundoRing method to be a modification of the Nissen procedure, but unfortunately, despite individual publications, the Nissen method is not standardized for gastric bypass and not described in the necessary detail for precise reproducibility by different surgeons, and, consequently, obtaining the same surgical results.

This study aims to describe the different specific steps of surgical techniques for the standard FundoRing method for one anastomosis gastric bypass (FundoRingOAGB) and describes the two-year outcomes of the standardized procedure compared to the non-standardized (NissenOAGB method).

Materials and methods

This study updates a paper about our randomized experiment that was published in the journal Obesity Surgery18. The “FundoRing trial” study protocol was previously published19. The scientific significance of standardization includes technical codification based on the experience of previously conducted clinical trials. The adoption of uniform standards for FundoRing was based on previously obtained and published weight loss data, a reduction in the incidence of complications, including a decreased likelihood of postoperative reflux esophagitis, and a reduction in the incidence of marginal ulcers16,18,19.

The multimedia article also described the FundoRing surgical technique and sizes20. However, it has not been standardized. Therefore, we will provide details of the main stages of the developed procedure.

The study design was a clinical, prospective, comparative, interventional parallel assignment, two-year follow-up study involving two parallel groups using a one-anastomotic gastric bypass (OAGB). FundoRingOAGB was performed in the first (standardization) group (n = 50) and in the second group (a non-standardized group) (n = 50), the NissenOAGB was used.

Patient positioning and trocar placement

The procedure is conducted on the operating table with the patient in a supine position and with the inclination of the table at 45°. Safety straps are employed to secure the legs and arms. The surgeon and nurse stand on the left of the patient, whereas the assistant, who handles the camera, stands on the patient’s right (Fig. 1a).

(A) Patient and surgeon position. (B) Trocar placement ©Oral Ospanov, 2025.

The carbon dioxide (CO2) is insufflated through a Veress needle into the abdominal cavity with a pressure of 12–14 mm Hg. Subsequently, the trocars are inserted at the designated point (Fig. 1b). The following steps are performed for port placement: the first trocar (T1, 11 mm) for the laparoscope is placed at the midline (ML) down 15 cm down from the xiphoidal process. The second trocar (T2, 11 mm) is placed at the left anterior axillary line (AAL), 4 cm from the costal margin. The third trocar (T3, 13 mm) is placed midway (MW T1-T2) between the first and second trocars. The fourth trocar (T4, 13 mm) is positioned at the right midclavicular line (MCL), 4 cm from the costal margin. The fifth trocar (T5, 6 mm) is temporarily used under the xiphoidal process (XP) only to create holes for the introduction of the Nathanson retractor (Cook Medical) for liver retraction at this point.

The difference steps of undoring after gastric pouch creation

After standardizing the creation of a gastric pouch with a length of 10 cm for OAGB or RYGB and using a 32 Fr. bougie, performing a gastric bypass according to the FundoRing method has significant differences.

Dividing the gastrosplenic ligament with the short gastric vessels

The fundus of the stomach’s excluded portion must be mobilized, and the gastrosplenic ligament must meet with the short gastric vessels. Dissection is performed along the lower edge of the fundus of the stomach without contact with the spleen. For this, we use the LigaSure device (Fig. 2A). In cases of severe obesity, when it is difficult to identify the location of the gastrosplenic ligament and spleen visually, we recommend starting mobilization from the short gastric vessels distally at the border of the fundus and body of the stomach.

(A) Division of the gastrosplenic ligament. (B) The division of the hepatogastric ligament of the lesser omentum. ©Oral Ospanov, 2025.

The gastric fundus was mobilized with a length of at least 8 cm. If the gastric wall’s serosa layer is inadvertently damaged, the defect must be sutured using absorbable suture material.

Dividing the hepatogastric ligament of the lesser omentum

The next step is dividing the hepatogastric ligament of the lesser omentum (Fig. 2B). This part of the surgery is necessary as a preparatory stage for creating the retroesophageal window and subsequent fundoplication. Begin by crossing the hepatogastric ligament at the pars flaccida in the avascular zone and proceed to the pars condensa. However, a complete intersection of the pars condensa is not required. As a rule, it is enough to cross half of it. If there is an additional hepatic vessel in the hepatogastric ligament, it must be crossed, leaving two clips on each side along the edges of the crossed vessel.

Creating the retrogastroesophageal window (canal)

After laparoscopic examination, if no hiatal hernia of the diaphragm was found, cruroraphy was not performed; if there was a hiatal hernia, then cruroraphy should be performed. Next, the space between the right crus of the diaphragm and the esophagus and stomach was divided to create a channel (Fig. 3A). The creation of the retrogastroesophageal window begins with the opening of the peritoneum sheet on the visible vertical line on the border of the right diaphragmatic crus and fatty tissue on the stomach side. After pushing away the fatty tissue containing the vascular and nerve structures, laparoscopic graspers were used to widen the channel that had been formed. On the left side, one branch of the laparoscopic grasper slides along the surface of the right diaphragmatic crus, whereas on the right side, the opposite branch of the grasper pushes aside the fatty tissue next to the vessels of the stomach. It is important to note that the point of creation of the retrogastroesophageal canal should be below the esophageal hiatus and created in the lower part of the visible zone on the surface of the right diaphragmatic crus. Creating a window near the esophagus and posterior vagus and near the hepatoduodenal ligament and ramus of the artery gastric sinistra is forbidden. After establishing a narrow retroesophageal window using LigaSure, the peritoneum was additionally dissected along the vertical line at the edge of the right diaphragmatic crus. The created retrogastroesophageal window must be at least 4 cm wide.

(C) Mobilized fundus is captured and passed behind the intra-abdominal segment of the esophagus from left to right. From the side of the left diaphragmatic crus, the peritoneal layer was dissected between the spread branches of the grasper (Fig. 3B).

Movement of fundus through retrogastroesophageal window around the esophagus

For the creation wrap, we captured this elongated fundus and passed it behind the esophagus’s intra-abdominal segment from left to right (Fig. 3C). To do this, we inserted a soft laparoscopic clamp into the retroesophageal window; on the opposite side, we grasped the upper edge of the stapler line on the fundus between the branches. The fundus was moved through the window, and in the case of a narrowly created window, the edges were additionally dissected toward the right diaphragmatic crus with the intersection of its perimysium layer. The length of the displaced gastric fundus flap to the right of the esophagus should be at least 8 cm (Fig. 3D).

Creation of fundoplication wrap by “FundoRing”

Start of creation of circular fundoplication with the first calibration of wrap without fixation of wrap.

The remaining portion of the fundus on the left side of the esophagus was sutured in place following the earlier procedures that involved the fundus’ passage through the esophagogastric canal. Stitching began in the upper (proximal) part on the opposite side of the stapler line of the excluded part of the stomach (Fig. 4A).

(A) Stitching began in the upper (proximal) part of the excluded part of the stomach. (B) The posterior and anterior parts of the mobilized fundus of the excluded part of the stomach were sutured over the surface of the gastric pouch. (C) The lower edge of the wrap was fixed to the upper edge of the stapler line of the gastric pouch. (D) A circular fundoplication of 5 cm in length was created. ©Oral Ospanov, 2025.

The posterior and anterior parts of the mobilized fundus of the excluded part of the stomach were sutured over the surface of the gastric pouch (Fig. 4B). The esophagus and stomach were not sutured into the wrap sutures. The stapler line of the fundus was oriented inside the wrap.

Position and fixation of circular fundoplication

We calibrated the wrap for fundoplication in all cases using a 32 Fr bougie.

The anterior and posterior parts of the mobilized fundus are joined using nonabsorbable suture (Ethibond 2/0) stitches at 2–3 o’clock (the 12 o’clock dial point corresponds to the medial line of the anterior surface of the esophagus, and the 6 o’clock side of the spine). Two-thirds of the circular wrap should be on the esophagus, and one-third of the wrap should be at the upper part of the gastric pouch. Circular wrap length should be 5 cm.

In contrast to the Nissen technique, the wrap’s front and back edges are not fixed to the esophageal wall. The created wrap is fixed after freely creating a 3 cm long fundoplication wrap, which is shifted upward onto the esophagus, while the lower edge of the wrap is fixed to the upper edge of the stapler line of the gastric pouch (Fig. 4C). Then, the circular fundoplication is supplemented by another 2 cm, capturing the gastric pouch’s stapler line. Thus, a circular fundoplication of 5 cm in length is created (Fig. 4D).

Partial fundoplication

The next step after the circular fundoplication was the creation of a partial wrap. The nonabsorbable suture (Ethibond 2/0) of the anterior part of the fundus is continued downward along the line of staples of the gastric pouch (Fig. 5A). To reinforce the antireflux mechanism, stop wrapper slippage, and improve the stapler suture’s dependability, a partial wrap is required. Thus, the circular fundoplication of 5 cm is supplemented by a partial fundoplication of another 2 cm. The size of the wrap is 7 cm (Fig. 5B).

(D) The fundoplication ring (“FundoRing”) is created from autologous tissues at 5–6 o’clock of the “clock face.” ©Oral Ospanov, 2025.

Second calibration of wrap with creation of “FundoRing”

Another significant difference between the FundoRing and the Nissen methods is the implementation of a second calibration of the wrap. We performed stitching using a nonabsorbable suture (Ethibond 2/0) at the edges between the left (anterior) and right (posterior) parts of the wrap at 6 o’clock on the conventional dial (Fig. 5C). The calibration involved creating a narrowing effect for the living ring by applying traction to the previously tied thread with a force of 5 H (Fig. 5D). This narrowed the wrap around the back of the stomach pouch with each suture stitch. The stitches for calibration in the distal direction continued as the stitches for the peritonization suture line on the remnant stomach. To prevent bleeding and a leak of the gastric pouch, the stapler line should be covered using continuous suture (Ethibond 2/0). At the same time, the fundoplication ring (“FundoRing”) is created from autologous tissues at 5–6 o’clock on the “clock face.” After double calibration, the creation of the “FundoRing” is completed.

All other stages of the procedure completion, including the creation of a gastroenteroanastomosis, are performed in a standard manner for OAGB or RYGB. The main differences between the various methods are shown schematically in Fig. 6.

Differences in the surgical technique of fundoplication (FundoRing vs. Nissen) and in gastric pouch size (10 cm in the first group and 18 cm in the second group) resulted in significant differences in clinical outcomes.

The average operative time of the procedures was longer for the first group: 84 ± 9.8 min versus 67.4 ± 3.7 min. in the second group (p = 0.04). This was due to the fact that FundoRingOAGB involved more steps, such as mobilization, creating a longer gastric fundus with transection of the gastrosplenic ligament, and performing a double calibration of the fundoplication wrap.

There was no difference in the mean length of hospital stay: 1.3 ± 0.85 days vs. 1.7 ± 0.35 days in the first and second groups, respectively (p = 0.9). At 24 months post-treatment, BMI changes were as follows: from 40.7 ± 5.9 (31–53) kg/m2 to 24.3 ± 2.8 (19–29) kg/m2 in “after standardisation” (first) group, versus 40.9 ± 6.3 (33–53) kg/m2 to 28.1 ± 3.9 (24–34) kg/m2 in “before standardisation” (second) group. After 2 years of follow-up up changes of BMI were 16.4 ± 0.8 vs. 12.8 ± 1.1 kg/m2 in the first and second group, respectively (p = 0.03). There were no fatal outcomes in either group. The number of complications (Table 2) was statistically higher in the group where there was no standardisation: 11 (22%) vs. 0 in the standardization group (p = 0.001, X2 = 12.36).

Compared to the Nissen method used in the second group, we observed no cases of acid and bile reflux in the first group, which used the FundoRing method. This is because the dual-calibration fundoplication with a long wrap has a better antireflux effect.

One case of bile reflux esophagitis in the second group required conversion of NissenOAGB to RYGB.

Another noteworthy result is the absence of marginal ulcers in the standardized FundoRing (first) group, which can be attributed to the short gastric pouch (10 cm) compared to the long gastric pouch (18 cm) in the Nissen (second) group. However, the observed difference is statistically insignificant and requires further study using a larger sample size.

Discussion

The prevalence of obesity is growing at a rate that threatens other national health systems22. The metabolic syndrome in obesity has an impact on reduced life expectancy23.

Currently, the third most common primary bariatric surgical technique performed globally is the one-anastomosis gastric bypass24. Severe bile reflux is a serious long-term complication after OAGB, with 4.1% of patients needing conversion to RYGB10. Unfortunately, standard RYGB is not an ideal procedure for treating and preventing new reflux esophagitis. The lengthening of the gastric pouch to prevent reflux esophagitis leads to an increased incidence of marginal ulcers25. We saw this trend in our own observations, when in the second group with a gastric pouch length of 18 cm, there were 3 cases of marginal ulcers, and in the first group with a gastric pouch length of 10 cm, there were no cases of marginal ulcers. Both the anatomical and physiological changes after OAGB or RYGB are responsible for de novo GERD. Among the proposed mechanisms, lower esophageal sphincter (LES) hypotension after bariatric surgery26. Thus, our study’s antireflux outcomes were enhanced by the LES mechanism following FundoRing.

Due to the above-mentioned disadvantages of OAGB, we developed a new variant called “FundoRing”27,28. After gaining convincing positive experience in performing FundoRingOAGB procedures, we were able to standardize this method, which we described in this article.

We have standardized the creation of a gastric pouch of FundoRing with a length of no more than 10 cm and a capacity (volume) of 20 ml. This is a small-volume gastric pouch size29, comparable to the size of a classically (routinely) Roux-en-Y gastric bypass when the pouch capacity is 20 to 60 ml30–32. With our new standard, the capacity of the gastric pouch is less (20 ml) than that of a routine one-anastomosis gastric bypass (>50 ml). According to publications by other authors, the length of its gastric pouch of standard OAGB ranges from 15 to 18 cm, and its volume ranges from 50 to 150 ml33,34.

Thus, the main difference of our surgical method from previously accepted sizes is the small width (no more than 2.5 cm) and the short gastric pouch, where, instead of 15 cm (12–18), no more than 10 cm of length is used. Accordingly, instead of the pouch volume of 50–75 ml, we use a volume of 20 ml35. Our choice of gastric pouch size is in line with the recommendations of other authors with extensive experience, who recommend a gastric pouch length of approximately 10 cm36.

The classification of combinations of gastric bypass and fundoplication (CGB&F) described in the publications27 can be divided into three main types:

Type 1 CGB&F is a primary one-stage simultaneous gastric bypass and fundoplication. We first performed a combined one anastomosis gastric bypass with a modified Nissen fundoplication (“FundoRing”) using the excluded portion of the stomach as a primary operation in 2017 on a patient with GERD but without a hiatal hernia27,28. Soprani et al.37 reported the results of a retrospective study of 22 patients, one group of whom was treated with a combination of OAGB and Nissen fundoplication. Of the 22 patients, 17 (77.3%) underwent primary surgery, and 5 (22.7%) underwent Nissen surgery as a revision surgery after bariatric interventions.

Type 2 CGB&F is a two-stage sequential gastric bypass after a previous fundoplication with: (2a) complete removal of the fundoplication wrap; (2b) partial removal of the fundoplication wrap; and (2c) no removal of the wrap.

Type 2a is a gastric bypass in patients with a previous fundoplication with complete removal of the fundoplication wrap. This combination is rarely used. Due to the technical complexity and duration of the above-mentioned procedures, as well as the high rate of stapler failure in the angle of His (up to 28.6%)38, such procedures should be performed with extreme caution.

Type 2b is gastric bypass surgery performed in patients with previous fundoplication with partial removal (disconnection) of the fundoplication wrap39. The main difference in this type is that the posterior portion of the fundoplication wrap is left in place without further dissection or manipulation. The anterior portion is stapled and remains attached to the excluded (retained) stomach.

Type 2c is gastric bypass surgery performed after previous antireflux surgery without removal of the previous fundoplication40. Leaving the antireflux valve reduces intraoperative technical difficulties and postoperative complications. Unfortunately, this type is associated with less subsequent weight loss.

Type 3 CGB&F is a two-stage sequential procedure with fundoplication following a previous bariatric surgery.

Type 3 CGB&F represents fundoplication as a repeat procedure following bariatric surgery41.

Limitation

The limitations of this article include the use of only one surgeon’s experience and the lack of a multicenter approach to the clinical trial. Another limitation of this study is the sample size. A significantly larger sample size in the two comparison groups is required to provide a more robust evidence base for the conclusions reached.

Conclusion

The surgical technique of FundoRing has specific, distinctive steps. It involves a wider mobilization of the fundus and the need to perform double calibration of the wrap when creating a circular or partial fundoplication, oriented toward the stapler suture of the gastric pouch to create a living ring (band) from autologous tissue.

The standardization of the surgical technique of the FundoRing method for laparoscopic gastric bypass improves weight loss outcomes and complication prevention.
