MiR-125b-5p alleviates pulmonary fibrosis by inhibiting TGFβ1-mediated epithelial-mesenchymal transition via targeting BAK1
Authors: Shuang Zhou, Wenzhao Cheng, Yifei Liu, Hongzhi Gao, Liying Yu, Yiming Zeng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the role and potential mechanisms of microRNA-125b-5p (miR-125b-5p) in pulmonary fibrosis (PF). PF is a typical outcome of many chronic lung diseases, with poor prognosis and the lack of appropriate medical treatment because PF’s molecular mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, using in vitro and in vivo analyses, we find that miR-125b-5p is likely a potent regulator of lung fibrosis. The findings reveal that, on the one hand, miR-125b-5p not only specifically decreases in the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of lung epithelial cells, but also shows a downregulation trend in the lung tissues of mice with PF. On the other hand, overexpression of miR-125b-5p on the cellular and animal levels downregulates EMT and fibrotic phenotypes, respectively. To clarify the molecular mechanism of the “therapeutic” effect of miR-125b-5p, we use the target prediction tool combined with a dual luciferase assay and complete a rescue experiment by constructing the overexpression vector of the target gene Bcl-2 homologous antagonist/ killer (BAK1), thus confirming that miR-125b-5p can effectively inhibit EMT and fibrosis process by targeting BAK1 gene. MiR-125b-5p inhibits the EMT in lung epithelial cells by negatively regulating BAK1, while overexpression of miR-125b-5p can alleviate lung fibrosis. The findings suggest that MiR-125b-5p/BAK1 can serve as a potential treatment target for PF.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12931-024-03011-w.Keywords: MiR-125b-5p, BAK1, Epithelial‑mesenchymal transition, Pulmonary fibrosis, TGF-β1

Introduction

Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a common chronic lung disease characterized by inflammation and damage to lung tissue, as well as formation of scar tissue (fibrosis), which results in impaired lung function and ultimately makes patients feel short of breath and experience difficulty in breathing [1]. Among numerous different types of PF, the most common is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), which seriously affects patients’ quality of life [2]. Despite extensive previous research on possible PF treatment, to date, no complete cure pathway for PF is known. Because of the limited understanding of pathogenesis of this disease, PF symptoms are typically alleviated and the disease’s progression is curbed via limited medications, supportive therapies, and lung transplants [3, 4].

Extensive previous research indicates that an important biological process in PF is EMT [5, 6]. Inducing a loss of typical epithelial properties in alveolar epithelial cells, EMT causes these cells to acquire mesenchymal cell traits and change into fibroblast-like cells [6]. As a result, epithelial cells become more invasive and prone to migration and proliferation, which contributes to fibrosis onset and progression [6, 7]. In addition, the cellular transformation induced by EMT may prompt the cells to release more extracellular matrix (ECM) components, which ultimately leads to the thickening of the alveolar wall that directly exacerbates the degree of lung fibrosis [5, 8]. In recent years, research on EMT has become a hotspot in PF studies, many of which indicated that a targeted inhibition of EMT may be an effective means of treating PF [9, 10].

A crucial role in regulating EMT is played by TGF-β; therefore, blocking the TGF-β signaling pathway is a highly effective method to prevent EMT. Currently, a variety of drugs targeting the TGF-β signaling pathway, including antibodies and small molecule inhibitors, are being tested in clinical trials [4, 11]. Yet, other investigators suggested that, in view of the complexity of the pathway and its enormous impact on cells, the direct targeting of the TGF-β signaling pathway to inhibit fibrosis is risky [12]. Instead, as argued by [13], effective alternative therapeutic targets should be sought through a deeper understanding of the TGF-β-regulated fibrosis pathway.

Furthermore, it is currently known that the pro-fibrotic effects of TGF-β are positively and negatively regulated by interactions with other signaling pathways, as well as by non-coding RNAs [14]. In recent years, particular research attention was focused on the role of microRNA (miRNA) in the occurrence and development of PF [15, 16]. MiRNAs, a class of short non-coding RNA molecules that play an important role in the regulation of gene expression [17, 18], are closely related to the mechanism of PF.

The specific ways in which miRNAs are related to PF are as follows. First, miRNAs can directly regulate expression of fibrosis-related genes and thus affect the PF process. For instance, targeted regulation of TGFBR3 by miR-23b-3p and miR-27b-3p can achieve regulation of atrial fibroblasts [19, 20]. Second, miRNAs can regulate the fibrosis process by controlling proliferation, apoptosis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transformation of key cells. For example, miR-29c [21] and miR-184 [22] were previously reported to block PF by regulating renewal and apoptosis of epithelial cells. In several previous studies related to skin scars, some miRNAs (miR-519d and miR-9-5p) were found to regulate fibroblast proliferation [23, 24]. In liver fibrosis, extant research mainly focused on the activation and proliferation regulation of miRNAs on hepatic stellate cells [25–27]. In addition, recent evidence suggests that tandem long non-coding RNAs usually interact with related signaling pathways through regulating miRNAs, thereby indirectly affecting the fibrosis process [28]. Yet another important way in which miRNAs regulate the fibrosis process is through control of inflammation and immune regulation [29, 30]. Finally, miRNAs were also reported to regulate fibrosis by participating in synthesis and degradation of ECM, which is closely related to activation of fibroblasts and differentiation of myofibroblasts [28, 31–33]. Interestingly, by delivering miR-142-3p to lung fibroblasts and alveolar epithelial cells, exosomes generated from macrophages were found to slow advancement of lung fibrosis [34].

Among the miRNAs associated with PF and cancer pathogenesis, miR-125b is consensually considered to be the “star” miRNA [35]. MiR-125b is an essential component of pro-fibrotic signaling in the heart [36]. In addition, activation of RhoA signaling by miR-125b was found to promote hepatic stellate cell activation and liver fibrosis [37]. However, the role of miR-125b in the PF process remains uncertain.

In this study, miR-125b-5p was identified as having low expression in fibrotic mouse lungs through miRNA sequencing combined with RT-qPCR. To elucidate the role of miR-125b-5p in lung fibrosis, we observed the impact of miR-125b-5p on fibrosis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) processes using a mouse lung fibrosis model and a lung/bronchial epithelial cell line. Subsequently, we conducted cellular experiments to explore the regulatory mechanism of miR-125b-5p. The results allowed us to determine that miR-125b-5p inhibits the EMT process in lung epithelial cells by targeting BAK1, thereby inhibiting activation of lung myofibroblasts, which, in turn, contributes to attenuating lung fibrosis.

Materials and methods

Animal modeling and sample collection

C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks, 18∼20 g, male) were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) and randomly divided into four groups (15 for control, 11 for BLM, 11 for Agomir-NC, and 11 for Agomir). Animals in the BLM group were intratracheally administered a 1 mg/kg dose of bleomycin (BLM) (MedChemExpress LLC, China) dissolved in 50 µL sterile saline. The control group received 50 µL sterile saline intratracheally in the same manner. Animals in the Agomir group received a tail vein injection of Agomir-125b-5p or Agomir-NC (Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd) at a dose of 4 mg/kg dissolved in 100 µL sterile saline on day 7 of BLM treatment. The mice were euthanized on day 14 after BLM administration, after blood oxygen monitoring and pulmonary function testing [38]. Lung tissues were collected for fixation, embedding, and sectioning. Subsequently, H&E, Masson staining, and immunohistochemical assays were performed. Fresh lung tissues were also required for protein extraction and Western blot (WB) assays. All animal procedures were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (2019 − 103).

Lung histology, trichromatic staining, immunohistochemistry (IHC) and tyramine signal amplification (TSA)

Mouse lung tissue was soaked in a 10% neutral buffered formalin solution overnight, dehydrated according to standard procedures, degreased in xylene embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5 μm- thick slices. The slices were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The fractional area of pathological alterations in the lung parenchyma was calculated using the Image J software (v1.8.0). To observe the degree of lung fibrosis, the slices were stained using the Masson Trichrome Staining Kit (Cat # 77148, Guangzhou Wexis Biotech Ltd.), and the volume fraction (%) of collagen fibers was analyzed using Image J. Fibrosis markers (FN1 and α-SMA) and BAK1 (12105 S, CST) expression were evaluated using IHC [39]. After dewaxing, the slices were incubated in a 0.01 mol/L citric acid buffer, pH 6.0, in a microwave oven for 15 min for antigen repair. The slices were then cooled and incubated in 3% H2O2 for 10 min to inactivate endogenous peroxidase, then blocked with a non-immune goat serum at room temperature for 10 min. Next, the primary antibody was added overnight at 4 ℃. Thereafter, on adding the MaxVisionTM (KIT-5005 MaxVisionTM HRP-Polymer anti-rabbit IHC Kit) reagent, the tissue slices were incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Finally, the freshly prepared DAB color developing solution was added. After 5 min, the stained cells were mounted and viewed with a light microscope.

For TSA experiments on paraffin sections of mouse lungs, the kit (G1236, Servicebio® TSAPLus, Wuhan servicebio Co., Ltd, China) was used, and before incubation of the antibodies, the tissue slices were autoclaved with sodium citrate for 3 min, endogenous peroxidase was eliminated with 3% H2O2, and blocked with goat serum for half an hour. The staining schemes were iF488-Tyramide-SFTPC (AB3786, Millipore), iF555-Tyramide-BAK1 (12105 S, CST) and iF647-Tyramide-CC10 (ab40873, Abcam). It is worth noting that a sealing step with goat serum is required after each stripping of antibodies before subsequent antibody incubation can take place.

MicroRNA sequencing

Fresh mouse lung tissue was cut into 5 mm small tissue blocks and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. The total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018, USA). The microRNA sequencing was conducted by Haplox (Jiang Xi, China). The concentration, quality, and integrity of the total RNA were measured using a Qubit RNA HS Assay Kit in the Qubit ® 3.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyser (Agilent Technologies, USA). Next, for each qualified sample, a 3 µg RNA was used to establish the small RNA library. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, NEBNext Multiplex Small RNA Library Prep Set for Illumina (NEB, USA) and index codes were assigned to attribute sequences to each sample, and clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using TruSeq PE ClusterKit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina, USA). After cluster generation, the prepared library was sequenced on an Illumina platform. Raw sequencing data were deposited on the NCBI SRA database (accession number PRJNA1165890).

Cell culture and cell transfection

The alveolar epithelial type II cell line A549 was obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (serial number SCSP-503, Shanghai, China). Beas-2B, the human bronchial epithelial cell line, was purchased from IMMOCELL (Xiamen, Fujian, China). Human embryonic kidney cell line 293T was purchased from the Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology. All cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS. The cells were maintained in a 37 ℃, 5% CO2 incubator in a medium of DMEM or 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS (A5669701, Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin G, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Solarbio, P1400). In the cell experiments section, three biological replicates were utilized for the experiments, and for each cell plating, six technical replicates were included to ensure data reliability.

MiRNA mimic and inhibitor and plasmid transfection

The miR-125b-5p mimic and inhibitor were purchased from GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. constructed BAK1 overexpression plasmids by synthesizing and cloning the sequences into the pcDNA3.1(+) vector. Transfection of A549 and Beas-2B cells was performed under optimal growth conditions at 50–70% confluence using Lipofectamine® 3000 (Cat # L3000015; Invitrogen Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). A 150 pM transfection of miR-125b-5p mimics and inhibitors was performed at 37 ℃ for 48 h. The cells were then harvested for subsequent experimentation. The BAK1 3’ UTR fragment containing the miR-125b-5p binding site was inserted into the pmirGLO vector (Promega Corporation) and named pmirGLO-BAK1-WT. Meanwhile, the mutation vector was constructed and named pmirGLO-BAK1-MUT. The BAK1 3’ UTR WT and MUT sequences are listed in Table 1.

MiRNA target prediction

To accurately identify the core target of microRNA has-miR-125b-5p, we used the following five databases: ENCORI, miRDB, RNAInter, TargetScan, and miRWalk. The Encyclopedia of RNA Interactomes (ENCORI, formerly known as starBase, https://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/) is a public platform for RNA-RNA and RNA-protein interactions identified from CLIP-seq and other high-throughput sequencing data [40]. The miRNA regulators were selected using seven target-predicting programs (PITA, RNA22, miRmap, DIANA-microT, miRanda, PicTar, and TargetScan) of the miRNA-Target “miRNA-mRNA” database in ENCORI. MiRDB (http://www.mirdb.org/) uses a bioinformatics tool to predict miRNA-target interactions, including 3.5 million predicted targets regulated by 7000 miRNAs [41]. RNAInter v4.0 (http://www.rnainter.org/), a complete resource of RNA interactome data from the literature and other databases, contains over 47 million RNA-related interactions [42]. For further analyses, we used the mRNA–miRNA interactions targeted by > 4 programs, score > 90, and score > 0.5 in ENCORI, miRDB, and RNAInter, respectively. MiRNA–mRNA interactions were additionally retrieved from TargetScan 8.0 (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/) [43]. MiRWalk (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/) holds data predicted by a machine learning algorithm, including experimentally validated miRNA-target interactions [44]. MiRNAs interacting with mRNAs commonly determined by > 5 databases (ENCORI, miRDB, RNAInter, TargetScan, miRWalk) were considered as mRNA regulators. Venogram was used to visualize the targets predicted by different databases.

Luciferase reporter assays

The cells were seeded into 96-well plates (2 × 104 cells/well). Co-transfection of reporter plasmids pmirGLO-BAK1-WT and pmirGLO-BAK1-MUT (200 ng each) with miR-125b-5p mimic or mimic-NC was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 at 37 ℃ for 48 h according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Firefly luciferase activity was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega, Beijing, China) and normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from mouse tissues and cell lines using the TRIzol® reagent (Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). 500ng of total RNA were reversely transcribed with specific primers using the TAKARA TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (Code No. RR420A) for miRNA and mRNA. The AceQ® qPCR SYBR® Green Master Mix kit (Code No. Q131, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) was used for cDNA amplification that was conducted as follows: 94 ℃ for 5 min, then 35 cycles of 94 ℃ for 1 min, 60 ℃ for 30 s, and 72 ℃ for 1 min, followed by a final cycle of 72 ℃ for 10 min. The qRT-PCR was performed and analyzed in an ABI QuantStudio5 Q5 (Applied Biosystems, USA). The relative expression of miR-125b-5p was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCT method. U6 was selected as an internal control for miRNA. GAPDH or β-actin was used as an internal control for mRNA. The sequences of the miRNA mimics (Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd.) are listed in Table 1. The type of negative control used was non‑targeting. The specific primers (Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd.) are listed in Table 2.

Differentially expressed gene analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the IPF (n = 30) and health control (n = 49) groups from RNA-seq dataset GSE124685 were determined using the limma software (v3.5.1) [45]. The genes expressed in less than 75% of samples were filtered. DEGs were defined as genes with p-value < 0.05 and |Fold Change| > 2. Expression of the target DEG was visualized using the ggboxplot function in the ggpubr R package.

Western blot (WB)

A solution of RIPA: PMSF (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was used to extract total protein. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). After separating on 11% SDS-PAGE, the proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane. Next, the membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 2 h, which was followed by incubation with primary antibodies (Vimentin, 1:1000; FN1, 1:100; E-cadherin, 1:1000; N-cadherin, 1:1000; α-SMA,1:20000; 1:1000; BAK1, 1:1000; β-actin, 1:1000) at 4 ℃ overnight. The used antibodies are listed in the Supplementary Table S1. Next, the primary antibodies were washed with the TBST buffer, and the membranes were incubated with the secondary antibodies for 1 h. The chemiluminescent signal analysis was performed using an imaging system (Invitrogen™, iBright™ CL1500 Imaging System) and quantified by the Image J software (v1.8.0).

Measurement of blood oxygen, pulmonary inspiratory resistance, and vital capacity

The mice were deeply anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (40 mg/mL) and tested for respiratory health using the Small Animal Vital Signs Monitor, MouseOX® Small Animal Vital Signs Monitor (STRR, USA). Then, the animals were tracheostomized and placed into the body cavity of the system. The trachea was connected to the system tubing and ventilated for pulmonary function testing. Respiratory function indices, including relative pulmonary inspiratory resistance (RI) and pulmonary capacity (FVC), were measured using the Buxco Pulmonary function test system (DSI Buxco, PFT Controller, St. Paul, USA) [38]. The mice that died from respiratory failure or before the end of the experiment were not analyzed.

Analysis of BAK1 expression in different cell types from single-cell transcriptomic data of IPF clinical samples

The scRNA-seq data were downloaded from the NCBI GEO database under accession numbers GSE1437061 (IPF: HC = 7:5) and GSE1329152 (IPF: HC = 3:3), and included 43,691 cells from 10 IPF patient samples and 8 healthy controls. Figure S1 shows the range of number of genes detected in each sample, the depth of sequencing, and the percentage of mitochondrial, ribosomal, and erythroid content. We excluded cells with less than 500 expressed genes and more than 40,000 UMI. In addition, we excluded cells with more than 20% mitochondrial content, > 50% ribosomal content, and > 2% erythrocyte content. After these rigorous quality control screens to remove low-quality cells, we obtained 40,153 cells for downstream analysis. Based on the highly consistent results between patients and the poor results of using the Harmony algorithm to remove heterogeneity between individual patient IPF cells, we chose the batch effect correction algorithm.

The genes were normalized by the functions Normalize Data and Scale Data. Variable genes were then selected and subjected to principal component analysis using the FindVariableFeatures function. The most appropriate number of PCs (PCs = 30) was determined using Dim Plot and Elbow Plot (Figure S2). The first 30 principal components and Resolution 1.0 were used with the Find Clusters function to generate cell clusters. Clusters were manually annotated by publicly known biomarkers for each cell type (Figure S3). On this basis, we demonstrated the expression values of BAK1 gene in different cell populations in the IPF and HC groups.

Statistical analyses

The data were expressed as means ± SEM and analyzed using the GraphPad Prism software (v8.0). One-way ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons. Student’s t-test was used only for two-group comparisons. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

MiR-125b-5p is downregulated in mice with PF and TGFβ1-induced lung epithelial cells

Using unbiased miRNA array methods, we found that miR-125b-5p expression was significantly reduced in lungs of BLM-induced PF mice (Fig. 1I) (the sequencing raw data have been uploaded to NCBI, the Accession number is PRJNA1165890). To explore the potential role of miR-125b-5p in the fibrosis process, we constructed a mouse model of PF by an endotracheal injection of BLM (Fig. 1A). The results of H&E staining showed that pathological damage deposition of lung tissue in the BLM group was more obvious than that in the control group (Fig. 1B). To further understand the substance of the lung lesions, we performed a WB analysis on the lung tissues of the two groups of mice, and the results revealed that the expressions of fibrosis markers FN1, Collagen I, Vimentin, and N-cad were increased in the lung tissues of the BLM group, while the expression of epithelial markers E-cad was decreased (Fig. 1C, upper). Meanwhile, the expression of TGF-β signaling pathway-related proteins TGF-β1, p-Smad2, and p-Smad3 was upregulated (Fig. 1C, lower). On this basis, we detected miR-125b-5p in the lungs of the control group and fibrotic mice using qRT-PCR and found that miR-125b-5p was decreased in the fibrotic mice (Fig. 1H). Conversely, we also conducted corresponding experiments on the cellular level, using TGF-β1 to induce the EMT process in A549 and Beas-2B cells, which resulted in changes in cell morphology (Fig. 1D). To further confirm the successful induction of the EMT phenotype, we conducted WB experiments to assess the expression levels of EMT proteins following the addition of TGF-β1. The results for A549 cells are shown in Fig. 1F, while the results for Beas-2B cells are presented in Fig. 1G. Then, using qRT-PCR to detect the expression of miR-125b-5p, we observed different degrees of downregulation of miR-125b-5p at the three-time points of 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h in the two EMT cell models (Fig. 1E).

Up-regulation of MiR-125b-5p inhibits TGFβ1-induced EMT process in lung epithelial cell lines

Considering that epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) plays a crucial role in fibrosis progression, we investigated the impact of miR-125b-5p on the TGF-β1-induced EMT process to gain further insights into its influence on PF development. To this end, we transfected miR-125b-5p mimics or inhibitors into A549 and Beas-2B cells pre-treated with TGF-β1. On determining transfection efficiency, we observed that inhibitors and mimics substantially decreased (Fig. 2B and F) and increased miR-125b-5p (Fig. 2A and E), respectively. Next, we evaluated the alterations in EMT markers following the overexpression of miR-125b-5p, and the results revealed a decrease in the expression of EMT markers in A549 and Beas-2B cells (Fig. 2C and G). The effect of downregulation of miR-125b-5p on EMT was less pronounced than the one observed in the mimic group (Fig. 2D and H).

MiR-125b-Agomir mitigates BLM-induced PF in mice

Furthermore, we established a mouse model of miR-125b-5p over-expression in lung tissues (Fig. 3A). On day 7, upon the endotracheal infusion of BLM (the BLM group), the mice with fibrosis were given an appropriate amount of miR-125b-Agomir intravenously (the Agomir group). The degree of PF of control, BLM, Agomir-NC, and Agomir groups were evaluated on day 14, including the detection of pulmonary function, blood oxygen, histopathology, and fibrosis-related markers. The results showed that, as compared to the BLM group, the Agomir group exhibited reversed changes in body weight, blood oxygen, respiratory rate, RI, and FVC (Fig. 3F and G). We also observed alleviated pathological damage and collagen deposition of lung tissue in the Agomir group (Fig. 3B). These findings suggest that miR-125b-Agomir can reduce the degree of BLM-induced PF. IHC assays demonstrated that the protein expression of FN-1 and α-SMA were markedly up-regulated in the BLM group as compared to the control group, while the opposite pattern was observed in the Agomir group (Fig. 3C and D). Furthermore, the results of WB analysis revealed a decrease in the expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and an increase in the fibrosis markers FN1 and Vimentin in the BLM group as compared to the control group. Conversely, the Agomir group exhibited the opposite pattern of expression (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these results suggest that overexpression of miR-125b-5p mitigated BLM-induced PF in vivo.

BAK1 is a downstream target of miR-125b-5p

To understand the role of miR-125b-5p in the TGFβ1-induced EMT process, we predicted the target of miR-125b-5p through five bioinformatics databases (ENCORI, miRDB, RNAInter, TargetScan, miRWalk). BAK1 was identified as the downstream target of miR-125b-5p by intersection (Fig. 4A). Meanwhile, using WB experiments, we determined whether the expression of BAK1 decreased or increased when miR-125b-5p was overexpressed (Fig. 4B) or inhibited (Fig. 4C) during EMT in A549 and Beas-2B. In short, the changing trend of BAK1 and miR-125b-5p was always the opposite (Fig. 4B and C). In addition, in the IHC experiments of BAK1 in murine lungs, the expression of BAK1 protein both in the BLM and Agomir-NC group was significantly higher than the one in the control group (Fig. 4F). Similar results were observed in the corresponding WB experiments on murine lung tissues (Fig. 4G). However, the BAK1 protein level in the Agomir group was significantly lower than that in the BLM and Agomir-NC groups. In the subsequent analysis of the human RNA sequencing dataset (GSE124685), we found that BAK1 expression significantly increased in IPF patients (Fig. 4H and I).

BAK1 is a verified target of MiR-125b-5p

To confirm whether miR-125b-5p directly targets BAK1, we cloned the BAK1 WT 3’-UTR into the pmirGLO Luciferase reporter vector and mutated the putative miR-125b-5p binding site in the BAK1 3’-UTR (Fig. 4D). Subsequently, co-transfecting WT or MUT plasmids with miR-125b-5p mimics into 293T cells revealed that BAK1 WT 3’-UTR transfection led to a significantly repressed luciferase activity in the mimic group compared to the mimic-NC group. By contrast, we observed no difference in luciferase activity between the mimic group and the mimic-NC group after BAK1 MUT 3’-UTR transfection (Fig. 4E).

MiR-125b-5p alleviates EMT via BAK1, and overexpression of BAK1 reverses the effects of miR-125b-5p on TGF-β1-stimulated EMT

To determine whether the regulation of the EMT process by miR-125b-5p depended on BAK1, we constructed the BAK1 overexpression plasmid pcDNA3.1(+)-HA-BAK1 and transfected it with miR-125b-5p mimic into A549 cells stimulated by TGF-β1. The expression of BAK1 dramatically increased on the mRNA and protein levels after the transfection of A549 cells with the miR-125b-5p mimic and pcDNA3.1(+)-HA-BAK1 as compared to the cells transfected with miR-125b-5p mimic and pcDNA3.1(+) vector (Fig. 5A and B). Moreover, the upregulation of BAK1 reversed the inhibitory effect of miR-125b-5p mimic on the TGF-β1-stimulated EMT (Fig. 5A and B).

The TSA results revealed that BAK1 was mainly expressed in the trachea, as suggested by a strong co-expression relationship between BAK1 and CC10 (a biomarker of trachea). Discussion

Due to its high prevalence and lack of effective medication, PF disease remains a major public health concern [46]. Most previous studies demonstrated that EMT is a crucial link in the development of fibrosis, and TGF-β is an effective inducer of EMT [47]. While uncontrolled inflammatory response is an essential factor in inducing fibrosis, inflammatory factors and microenvironment can further activate macrophages and epithelial cells to cause the EMT process, resulting in activation and proliferation of myofibroblasts, extracellular matrix deposition, and PF [48, 49]. The formation of fibroblast foci dominated by myofibroblast (MF) is an important pathological feature of IPF [50]. The activation of myofibroblasts during the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway is a crucial factor in the development of fibrosis [51].

MiR-125b is a microRNA closely related to the regulatory mechanisms of various diseases [52]. However, while substantial evidence is available showing that MiR-125b plays an important regulatory role in tumors [53], liver fibrosis [54], and myocardial fibrosis [55], its regulatory role in PF remains unclear. MiR-125b is tissue-specific, and its specific function depends on upstream regulatory factors and downstream targets [56]. When practiced with cell proliferation and apoptosis signaling pathways, MiR-125b will affect cell proliferation or apoptosis and influence cell differentiation related to histiocytic differentiation [57].

In this study, we first compared miRNA differences between mice with PF and controls on the animal level, from which we determined that miR-125b-5p was explicitly reduced in fibrotic lung; these results were confirmed by the qRT-PCR assay. We also determined that miR-125b-5p specifically decreased EMT levels in both fibrotic lung and epithelial cells. This finding implies that miR-125b-5p may be a protective factor in PF.

Next, to determine the regulatory role of miR-125b-5p on PF, we overexpressed miR-125b-5p on the cellular EMT level and in an animal model. The significantly reduced degree of EMT on the cell level after adding mimic/ Agomir, as well as the decreased degree of fibrosis and better indicators of mouse lung function on the animal level, further demonstrated that miR-125b is indeed a protective factor and may serve as a potential therapeutic target for PF.

To further clarify how specifically miR-125b-5p regulates EMT, we conducted bioinformatic analyses using five miRNA databases to screen the target gene BAK1. The results of the dual luciferase reporting assay confirmed that miR-125b-5p can directly target BAK1. Moreover, we concurrently overexpressed miR-125b-5p and BAK1 on the cellular EMT level, and the “therapeutic” effect of miR-125b-5p was partially offset.

This study is the first to identify—on several levels, from cell and animal to molecular, combined with miRNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis—that miR-125b-5p alleviates the EMT process and PF by targeting the downstream BAK1 gene, thus providing a new target for PF therapy.

However, a limitation of our study is that we did not try to inhibit the expression of BAK1 concurrently to the overexpression of miR-125b-5p, which was also because the BAK1 gene was already decreased when miR-125b-5p was overexpressed; further downregulation of BAK1 may have initiated the negative feedback mechanism of cells. Furthermore, the BAK1 gene is a pro-apoptotic factor that was identified to play a vital role in the apoptosis of human cancer cells or mitochondrial cells after various stimuli. The increase of miR-125b-5p can inhibit the expression of pro-apoptotic factor BAK1 in lung epithelial cells, which also means cell proliferation will be relatively enhanced. The specific relationship between this disguised pro-proliferation effect and the inhibition of the EMT process warrants further research. Finally, the accuracy of our results would have been enhanced if patient specimens had been validated. Regrettably, the current scarcity of patient specimens prevented us from proceeding with this aspect of the project at this time.

Conclusions

In summary, we found that miR-125b-5p is down-regulated in BLM-induced fibrotic lung tissue in mice and TGF-β1-stimulated lung epithelial cells (A549 and Beas-2B). MiR-125b-5p targets the BAK1 gene, and overexpression of miR-125b-5p promotes the inhibition of BAK1, thereby inhibiting EMT process and fibrosis. Taken together, these results suggest that the miR-125b-5p/BAK1 axis is a promising new therapeutic target for PF.
