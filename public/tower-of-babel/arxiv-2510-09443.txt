The Impact of Sanctions on decentralised Privacy Tools: A Case Study of Tornado Cash
Raffaele Cristodaro, Benjamin Kraner, Claudio J. Tessone

Abstract
This paper investigates the impact of sanctions on Tornado Cash, a smart contract protocol designed to enhance transaction privacy. Following the U.S. Department of the Treasury’s sanctions against Tornado Cash in August 2022, platform activity declined sharply. We document a significant and sustained reduction in transaction volume, user diversity, and overall protocol utilization after the sanctions were imposed. Our analysis draws on transaction data from three major blockchains: Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain, and Polygon. We further examine developments following the partial lifting and eventual removal of sanctions by the U.S. Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) in March 2025. Although activity partially recovered, the rebound remained limited. The Tornado Cash case illustrates how regulatory interventions can affect decentralized protocols, while also highlighting the challenges of fully enforcing such measures in decentralized environments.



I
Introduction

Blockchain technology has transformed the way value is exchanged, enabling decentralised, transparent, and trust-minimized financial systems. At the same time, this transparency poses challenges for privacy. In response, a number of privacy-enhancing protocols have emerged, with Tornado Cash standing out as one of the most widely used. Tornado Cash is a non-custodial mixer that allows users to obscure the origin of funds by pooling assets of equal denominations and facilitating withdrawals to new addresses.

While Tornado Cash was designed to provide stronger transactional privacy, it also attracted scrutiny from regulators. The U.S. Department of the Treasury, through its Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC), oversees the implementation of economic and trade sanctions to advance national security and foreign policy objectives. Traditionally, these measures have been directed at specific countries, organizations, and individuals, entities that operate within frameworks where legal and enforcement mechanisms can be readily applied. However, the rise of digital currencies introduces new complexities. Unlike conventional financial systems that rely on identifiable intermediaries, cryptocurrencies operate on decentralised infrastructures designed to resist censorship and external control. This structure limits the government’s ability to regulate or disrupt illicit financial flows through conventional legal pathways.

The intersection of digital assets with cybercrime has elevated them as a priority in sanction enforcement. A pivotal moment came on the 8
th
of August, 2022, when OFAC sanctioned Tornado Cash, a decentralised privacy protocol, under Executive Order 13694, which targets entities involved in harmful cyber-enabled activities. By placing Tornado Cash on the Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons (SDN) list, the Treasury alleged the platform had facilitated the laundering of over $7 billion in virtual assets since its launch in 2019. This action marked a significant extension of sanctions policy into the realm of autonomous software systems and set a precedent for regulating decentralised finance tools
[
1
]
.

This paper investigates the impact of these sanctions on Tornado Cash activity across three major blockchains: Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain, and Polygon. Using on-chain data, we examine changes in user participation, transaction volume, and protocol usage before and after the sanctions were introduced. We further analyse how the ecosystem responded following the eventual removal of sanctions by OFAC in March 2025.

Our findings show a sharp and sustained decline in activity immediately following the initial sanctions, suggesting that even decentralised protocols are not immune to regulatory pressure. However, the partial recovery observed after the sanctions were lifted reveals a more nuanced picture, pointing to both the influence and limitations of enforcement actions in decentralised environments.

This study contributes to ongoing discussions about the role of regulation in Web3 ecosystems. The case of Tornado Cash has become a focal point for policymakers and researchers alike, including institutions such as the Federal Reserve and the Swiss National Bank, who have examined the implications of regulatory interventions in decentralised systems
[
2
]
.

II
Background and Related Work

II-A

How Tornado Cash Works

Tornado Cash is a decentralised privacy protocol that operates through self-executing smart contracts on public blockchains. Designed to obscure transaction trails, it enables users to detach the publicly visible link between deposit and withdrawal addresses by employing cryptographic techniques to mix assets.

The protocol works by allowing users to deposit fixed quantities of cryptocurrencies, such as native tokens (e.g., ETH, MATIC, BNB) or popular ERC-20 tokens like USDC, into shared contract-based pools. Before initiating a deposit, users generate a random secret value off-chain and compute a corresponding cryptographic commitment using a Pedersen hash
[
3
,
4
]
. This commitment is included in the deposit transaction and recorded on-chain as a leaf in a Merkle tree that tracks all past deposits. Each commitment is unique, ensuring that no two deposits can be confused or linked by design. The underlying secret remains private and is never exposed on-chain.

Because no relayer is used, the user pays only the gas fee and receives the entire withdrawal amount.

When users want to redeem their funds, they generate a zero-knowledge proof, specifically a zk-SNARK, that proves they know a secret tied to a previously made deposit without revealing which one it was. This proof is verified by the smart contract, and if valid, the contract authorizes a transfer to a new address. This effectively masks the path between the original deposit and the eventual withdrawal.

To further enhance privacy, users can delegate the actual submission of the withdrawal transaction to an external actor known as a relayer. The user privately shares the zero-knowledge proof and transaction parameters off-chain with the relayer, who then broadcasts the withdrawal on-chain, pays the gas fees, and receives a small fee as compensation. Since the relayer only sees the proof and not the original deposit, they cannot link the sender and recipient addresses. Moreover, using a relayer prevents on-chain analysis of gas payment patterns that could otherwise reveal connections between transactions.

The relayer model helps eliminate the final traceable link between the deposit origin and the withdrawal recipient. However, relying on relayers introduces potential risks: users must ensure the relayer is trustworthy to avoid issues like censorship or fund misdirection.

A crucial design choice of the protocol is its use of fixed denominations. This constraint ensures that all deposits and withdrawals are indistinguishable based on value alone, which is vital for maintaining a sufficiently large anonymity set.

Despite the strong cryptographic foundation, the effectiveness of Tornado Cash in providing anonymity depends significantly on how users interact with the protocol. Poor operational practices, such as immediate withdrawals, reusing addresses, or interacting with known wallets, can undermine privacy protections and introduce patterns susceptible to analysis.

In this study, we examine Tornado Cash activity across Ethereum, Polygon, and BNB Smart Chain. Although these networks differ in terms of fees, transaction speeds, and infrastructure, the core privacy mechanisms of the protocol remain consistent across all three.

th

This dataset enables a longitudinal view of activity surrounding Tornado Cash, capturing both direct interactions with the protocol and broader financial behaviour by associated participants. V
Analysis

To measure the effect of sanctions on Tornado Cash, we analyse four complementary indicators across Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain, and Polygon from January 2020 to June 2025: (i) weekly deposits and withdrawals for every pool denomination, (ii) chain-level transaction volumes, (iii) smart contract balances and net capital flows, and (iv) first-time user addresses. Examining these metrics before, during, and after the August 2022 OFAC designation and the March 2025 delisting reveals how sanctions altered activity levels, liquidity, and participant diversity, and how those changes varied by chain and pool size.

The multi-chain view reveals both immediate and enduring effects:

Ethereum

As the original (and largest) deployment, Ethereum shows the earliest and most pronounced contraction. All four pools drop sharply, with the 100 ETH pool almost vanishing. Smaller pools erode more gradually, implying that lower-stakes users were either less responsive to enforcement risk or less concerned about reduced privacy.

BNB Smart Chain

BSC appears briefly resilient, sustaining activity in the 0.1, 1, and 10 BNB Smart Chain pools for several weeks. A clear down-trend nevertheless sets in, led by the high-denomination pool. Even without extensive US-based infrastructure, reputational risk alone curtails usage, and volumes remain depressed throughout 2023–2025.

Polygon

Already the smallest network, Polygon sees activity in the 10’000 and 100’000 MATIC pools vanish almost immediately, while the 100 / 1’000 pools persist at low but non-zero levels. The sharp exit from large pools underscores how a shrinking anonymity set deters further participation.

Across all chains, the partial removal of addresses from the SDN list on 21
st
March 2025 triggers no observable resurgence. By then, more than two-and-a-half years of reduced turnover and liquidity have entrenched user disengagement. The evidence shows that decentralised applications can suffer lasting functional impairment through non-technical means: usage plummets post-sanctions, high-value pools empty first, and activity does not rebound even when formal pressure eases. Tornado Cash thus illustrates how legal and reputational shocks can override the nominal censorship resistance afforded by immutable smart contracts.

Before the sanctions, Ethereum averaged more than 3’800 deposits and 2’200 withdrawals per week; BNB Smart Chain often exceeded 4’000 weekly transactions; Polygon peaked at roughly 500. Within a month of the designation, volumes on every chain contracted by more than 90 percent. The decline persists throughout 2023–2025, with weekly counts stabilising in the low hundreds on Ethereum and BNB Smart Chain and in double digits on Polygon.

The synchronised collapse across heterogeneous networks shows that legal and reputational signals alone can disable a decentralised privacy tool, regardless of its immutable code or hosting infrastructure. Once liquidity and user confidence dissipate, neither the partial removal of sanctions nor the continued technical availability of the contracts proves sufficient to restore activity.

Ethereum pools, which previously attracted weekly net inflows above 45’000 ETH and held more than 200’000 ETH in aggregate, switch to persistent outflows and lose over two-thirds of their locked value within 18 months. BNB Smart Chain follows the same pattern, flipping from inflows exceeding 56’000 BNB to sustained weekly outflows of up to 20’000 BNB; total balances more than halve by early 2024. The synchronised reversal across chains reflects a classic “run” dynamic. Once users perceived legal and reputational risk, they withdrew capital irrespective of the contracts’ continued technical availability. The resulting loss of liquidity further reduced anonymity, reinforcing exit incentives. That balances keep falling after the March 2025 delisting shows that confidence, once lost, is difficult to restore; legal removal alone does not replenish the liquidity on which privacy guarantees depend.

Before sanctions, Ethereum regularly recorded over 600 new depositors and 900 new withdrawers per week; BNB Smart Chain reached 860 and 1’500 respectively; Polygon peaked at roughly 120 and 260. Within four weeks of the designation, counts on all networks had fallen by more than 90 per cent. The decline is steeper for new withdrawers, suggesting that even existing users hesitated to complete exits once legal risk became salient. The partial lifting of sanctions in March 2025 leaves onboarding flat: weekly totals remain in the low tens on Polygon and the low hundreds on Ethereum and BSC.

The sustained suppression of new entrants confirms that reputational and legal signals, rather than technical barriers, dictate user growth in privacy protocols. Without a steady inflow of fresh addresses, the anonymity set shrinks, further eroding the protocol’s perceived utility and reinforcing exit incentives. Tornado Cash thus illustrates how enforcement actions can throttle decentralised systems not only by driving current users away but by cutting off the lifeblood of new participation.

V-E

Cross-Plot Synthesis and Implications

Viewed as a whole, the four figures trace the same story from multiple angles. Transaction counts, aggregate volumes, liquidity balances, and new-user flows all pivot in the week of 8
th
August 2022 and never return to their pre-sanction trajectories, regardless of chain or pool size. The alignment across metrics shows that sanctions affect every stage of the usage cycle: entry, ongoing activity, and exit; and that delisting in March 2025 does not reverse the damage. In short, Tornado Cash remains technically online yet economically fragile: once confidence and liquidity drain away, the shrinking anonymity set deters fresh participation and locks in decline. The case therefore illustrates how legal and reputational pressure can impose de-facto deplatforming on decentralised privacy infrastructure, even without touching the underlying code.

VI
Discussion

The empirical results show
what
happened to Tornado Cash after the 2022 OFAC designation; the purpose of this section is to consider
why
it happened, what it implies for the design of privacy protocols, and how it should inform both regulatory strategy and future research. We focus on themes that the preceding analysis did not address in depth.

The sanctions produced an immediate behavioural shock even though the smart contracts remained callable. Three extra-technical channels appear decisive:

•

Interface gatekeeping: Major wallets and dApp browsers disabled the Tornado Cash front-end or displayed warning banners, raising the user’s perceived compliance cost without touching on-chain code.

•

Infrastructure self-censorship: RPC providers, bridge relays, and some block builders refused to relay Tornado Cash calls, increasing failed-transaction risk and latency.

•

Social reputation: Influential builders and auditors publicly distanced themselves, reframing Tornado Cash use as legally suspect and socially disapproved. The sharp onboarding collapse suggests that this reputational narrative deterred newcomers more effectively than any technical blocklist.

Privacy mixers differ from conventional DeFi pools in that utility rises, rather than falls, with crowd size; a shrinking anonymity set directly degrades protection. Once large holders withdrew, the marginal privacy benefit for everyone else fell, prompting further exits.
Designers of future mixers must therefore treat capital stickiness, not merely contract immutability, as a first-order resilience condition.

BSC and Polygon experienced nearly the same percentage decline as Ethereum despite weaker ties to U.S. infrastructure. Two spill-over paths seem plausible: users often bridge funds through Ethereum‐centric routes before mixing, and major wallets reuse shared compliance modules across all EVM chains. Sanctions aimed at a single jurisdiction thus propagate horizontally through shared tooling, confirming that
jurisdictional ‘arbitrage‘
is harder in practice than in theory.

Our results cast doubt on the long-run viability of large, shared-liquidity mixers. Alternatives include:

•

Peer-to-peer or “pay-to-join” batching: (e.g.
join-splits
), which removes the honeypot of locked capital but at the cost of higher coordination overhead.

•

Stateless relays: where users generate zero-knowledge proofs client-side and pay intermediaries only for forwarding, limiting surface area for liquidity freezes.

•

Layer-2 privacy rollups: that compress many transfers into a single L1 proof, reducing visibility to the base layer while avoiding static pool balances.

Each design shifts the attack surface yet creates new trade-offs, such as throughput, user experience, and auditability, that deserve systematic comparison.

From a regulator’s perspective, Tornado Cash demonstrates that signalling alone can suppress illicit finance routes without direct protocol disruption. Yet the same signal strands legitimate funds, fragments user privacy, and incentivises migration to less transparent venues. A more graduated approach, such as blacklisting specific addresses, providing claw-back channels, or offering whitelisting under attestation, could target illicit flows while preserving lawful privacy.

Future mixers could integrate compliance-sensitive screening mechanisms to mitigate collapse risks while retaining privacy guarantees. For example, protocols might interoperate with blockchain analytics providers (e.g., Chainalysis, Merkle Science, TRM Labs) to restrict usage by addresses flagged for sanctions exposure, while still permitting legitimate flows. Recent research
[
2
]
suggests that regulators are increasingly attentive to such hybrid approaches, where analytics and governance layers complement zero-knowledge proofs. Embedding selective access controls could reduce the likelihood of wholesale exclusion while maintaining resilience against reputational and infrastructure shocks.

This aligns with prior work demonstrating the effectiveness of blockchain analytics in uncovering illicit flows
[
12
]
and the limits of existing privacy technologies when subjected to empirical scrutiny
[
19
]
.

Two caveats temper our conclusions. First, our dataset cannot observe off-chain swaps or private cross-chain bridges that users may have adopted post-sanction; the true displacement of mixing activity remains unknown. Second, to better understand the impact, it would help to have a similar mixing service that wasn’t affected by sanctions for comparison. Future work should combine address-graph heuristics, bridge telemetry, and qualitative interviews to map migration paths and to model the elasticity of privacy demand under varying compliance regimes.

VII
Conclusion

This research examined the effect of the 8
th
of August 2022 OFAC designation, and its partial restoration on 21
st
of March 2025, on Tornado Cash activity across Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain, and Polygon. Weekly transactional data, balances, net flows lags, and new-user addresses show a consistent narrative. A rapid decline in activity came within weeks of the sanction (with higher-value pools being drained first), onboarding new participants abruptly ceased midway through 2022, and net outflows became persistent negative outflows. Critically, no activity metrics bounced back into positive territory following the 2025 delisting, indicating the impact of reputational and legal deterrents were sticky in nature.

Our findings show that immutability does not equate to usability. Coordinated interface bans, infrastructure self-censorship, and social stigma coalesced to render the contracts technically accessible but economically inactive. Activity within BSC and Polygon, weaker direct exposure to US jurisdiction, but strong reputational contagion and shared tooling indicate that compliance pressure can migrate across ecosystems due to shared tools, practices and protocols.

For policy makers, the case shows that signalling alone can inhibit participation, but at the expense of entrenching legitimate deposits and displacing activity to less transparent venues. A safe harbour regulatory framework which differentiates illicit flows from lawful privacy use could reduce such collateral damage.

Consideration for future work involves monitoring liquidity movement to peer-to-peer or zero-knowledge options, and uncover spill-overs into adjacent DeFi protocols. Qualitative work with developers, relayers, and users would illustrate how decentralised communities interpret and respond to regulatory signals.

Taken in total Tornado Cash demonstrates that while decentralised code may find resilience, user trust is not. Legal and reputational pressures can precipitate capital flight, investor onboarding freezes, and ultimately shrink the anonymity set, barriers as unavoidable as any technical exploit. That said, finding the appropriate compromise between privacy, durability and compliance is likely to be a significant hurdle for the next wave of blockchain applications.
