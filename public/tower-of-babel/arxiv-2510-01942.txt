Bilinear and Fractional Leibniz Rules Beyond Euclidean Spaces: Weighted Besov and Triebel–Lizorkin Estimates
The Anh Bui

Abstract
. We establish fractional Leibniz rules in weighted settings for nonnegative self-adjoint operators on spaces of homogeneous type. Using a unified method that avoids Fourier transforms, we prove bilinear estimates for spectral multipliers on weighted Hardy, Besov, and Triebel–Lizorkin spaces. Our approach is flexible and applies beyond the Euclidean setting—covering, for instance, nilpotent Lie groups, Grushin operators, and Hermite expansions—thus extending classical Kato–Ponce inequalities. The framework also yields new weighted bilinear estimates including fractional Leibniz rules for Hermite, Laguerre, and Bessel operators, with applications to scattering formulas and related PDE models.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
State of the art

The fractional Leibniz rules, also known as Kato–Ponce inequalities, describe how fractional differentiation interacts with products of functions. More precisely, for
f,g\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
one has inequalities of the form

\|D^{s}(fg)\|_{L^{p}}\lesssim\|D^{s}f\|_{L^{p_{1}}}\|g\|_{L^{p_{2}}}+\|f\|_{L^{p_{1}}}\|D^{s}g\|_{L^{p_{2}}},

and analogues with the Bessel potential operator
J^{s}
. Here
D^{s}f(\xi)=|\xi|^{s}\widehat{f}(\xi)
and
J^{s}f(\xi)=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{s/2}\widehat{f}(\xi)
, with restrictions on the exponents depending on
s
, the dimension, and the integrability indices. Such inequalities play a central role in the analysis of nonlinear PDEs, including the Euler and Navier–Stokes equations
[
38
]
, dispersive models such as the Korteweg–de Vries equation
[
14
,
39
]
, and the smoothing effects of Schrödinger semigroups
[
31
]
. More recent advances address endpoint cases and nonclassical ranges of exponents; see for example
[
30
,
41
,
11
]
.

Beyond simple products, one can replace
fg
by bilinear pseudodifferential operators

T_{\sigma}(f,g)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}e^{2\pi ix\cdot(\xi+\eta)}\,\sigma(x,\xi,\eta)\,\widehat{f}(\xi)\,\widehat{g}(\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta,

with symbol
\sigma
. Such operators include bilinear multipliers, and their mapping properties have been investigated in a wide variety of function spaces. Examples include results for homogeneous Hörmander classes via molecular decompositions
[
12
]
, estimates in Lebesgue and mixed-norm spaces under minimal smoothness conditions
[
34
]
, and boundedness results in Besov, Sobolev, Triebel–Lizorkin, and related scales
[
43
,
10
]
.

Classical approaches to the fractional Leibniz rule rely heavily on the Fourier transform, restricting their applicability to Euclidean frameworks. However, many modern problems involve non-Euclidean such as sub-Laplacians on Lie groups or Schrödinger operators with potentials, where methods based on the Fourier transform are no longer effective. Extending Leibniz rules beyond the Euclidean setting has therefore become a central and challenging theme in harmonic analysis and PDEs. For instance, fractional Leibniz rules have been investigated on Lie groups in the scale of
L^{p}
and Sobolev spaces
[
18
,
3
]
. Estimates in Besov and Triebel–Lizorkin spaces for nilpotent Lie groups and the Grushin operator setting were obtained in
[
13
]
, while fractional Leibniz rules on stratified Lie groups were studied in
[
24
]
. In
[
42
]
, the fractional rules associated with Hermite operators were considered in both
L^{\infty}
and Besov/Triebel–Lizorkin spaces. On spaces of homogeneous type, fractional Leibniz rules in the Hardy scale have been derived for restricted ranges of fractional orders
[
40
]
. Nevertheless, existing results often impose limitations on the smoothness parameter
s
and the integrability and summability parameters
p
and
q
in the Besov and Triebel-Lizorkin spaces.

In this work, we establish a unified framework for fractional Leibniz rules on spaces of homogeneous type, associated with nonnegative self-adjoint operators, in the general scale of new weighted Besov and Triebel-Lizorkin spaces. Our results apply to not only obtain new results, but also significantly improve existing results in settings including the Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the sub-Laplacian on Lie groups, Schrödinger operators on stratified groups, the Grushin operators on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and the Hermite operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This yields a robust and flexible method for analyzing bilinear forms in settings where the classical Fourier analysis is unavailable.

The novelty of our approach lies in completely bypassing Fourier methods, instead combining bilinear spectral multiplier theory with heat semigroup techniques. To our knowledge, this constitutes the first comprehensive and unified treatment of fractional Leibniz rules in non-Euclidean framework.

1.2.
Main results

Let
X
be a space of homogeneous type with a distance
d
and a nonnegative Borel measure
\mu
satisfying the doubling property

(1)

V(x,2r)\leq C\,V(x,r),\qquad x\in X,\ r>0,

where
V(x,r)=\mu(B(x,r))
and
B(x,r)=\{y\in X:d(x,y)<r\}
.
This implies the following estimates

(2)

V(x,\lambda r)\leq c_{\star}\,\lambda^{n}V(x,r),\qquad\lambda\geq 1,

and

(3)

V(x,r)\leq C\Big(1+\frac{d(x,y)}{r}\Big)^{n}V(y,r),\qquad x,y\in X,\ r>0.

We consider a nonnegative self-adjoint operator
L
on
L^{2}(X)
generating a semigroup
\{e^{-tL}\}_{t>0}
with kernel
p_{t}(x,y)
, subject to the following conditions:

(A1)

There exist constants
C,c>0
such that

|p_{t}(x,y)|\leq\frac{C}{V(x,\sqrt{t})}\exp\Big(-\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{ct}\Big),\qquad x,y\in X,\ t>0.

(A2)

There exists
\delta_{0}\in(0,1]
such that

|p_{t}(x,y)-p_{t}(\overline{x},y)|\leq\Big(\frac{d(x,\overline{x})}{\sqrt{t}}\Big)^{\delta_{0}}\frac{C}{V(x,\sqrt{t})}\exp\Big(-\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{ct}\Big)

for all
t>0
and
x,\overline{x},y\in X
with
d(x,\overline{x})<\sqrt{t}
.

Denote by
E_{L}(\lambda)
the spectral (projection-valued) resolution of the nonnegative self-adjoint operator
L
. By the spectral theorem, for any bounded Borel function
F:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{C}
the operator

F(L)=\int_{0}^{\infty}F(\lambda)\,dE_{L}(\lambda)

is bounded on
L^{2}(X)
. In what follows, for a smooth function
F
we denote by
F(L)(x,y)
the integral kernel of
F(L)
(see Proposition
2.7
for the existence of this kernel). We make the following assumption.

(A3)

For all even functions
\varphi,\psi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
, all integers
k\geq 0
, and all
N>0
, there exists a constant
C_{N,k,\varphi,\psi}>0
such that, for every
t>0
and every
x,y,z\in X
,

L^{k}_{x}\big[\varphi(t\sqrt{L})(x,y)\,\psi(t\sqrt{L})(x,z)\big]\leq C_{N,k,\varphi,\psi}\;t^{2k}\,D_{t,N}(x,y)\,D_{t,N}(x,z),

where
L^{k}_{x}
denotes the operator
L^{k}
acting on the
x
-variable,
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
is the Schwartz space on
\mathbb{R}
, and
D_{t,N}(\cdot,\cdot)
is defined in (
23
) below.

The assumptions (A1) and (A2) are known as the Gaussian upper bound and Hölder continuity. The condition (A3) may appear to be technical; however, in many situations it is simply a consequence of the Gaussian upper bounds for higher-order derivatives of the heat kernel (see Section 5 for more details). The condition (A3) is essential to obtain the full range for the smoothness index
s
in the fractional Leibniz rules. See Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
below. Without (A3), we only expect a limited range for

. See Theorem
1.3
below.
In this paper, we do not assume that the operator
L
satisfies all conditions (A1)–(A3). Throughout, we always assume (A1) and (A2), and explicitly indicate whenever (A3) is required.

Assumptions (A1) and (A2) allow us to define bilinear form in this general setting.
Let
L_{1}=L\otimes 1
and
L_{2}=1\otimes L
be the tensor product operators acting on
L^{2}(X\times X)
with product measure
\mu\times\mu
. By the spectral theorem, there exists a unique spectral decomposition
E
such that for all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

E(A)\text{ is a projection on }L^{2}(X\times X),\quad E(A_{1}\times A_{2})=E_{L_{1}}(A_{1})\otimes E_{L_{2}}(A_{2}).

Hence for any function
F:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
one can define the operator by the formula

(4)

F(L_{1},L_{2})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\,dE(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})

with domain

\mathcal{D}=\{g\in L^{2}(X\times X):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|F(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})|^{2}\,d\langle E(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})g,g\rangle<\infty\}.

A straightforward variation of classical spectral theory arguments shows that for any bounded
Borel function
F:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
the operator
F(L_{1},L_{2})
is continuous on

L^{2}(X_{1}\times X_{2})
and its norm is bounded by
\|F\|_{\infty}
. See for example
[
51
]
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfy

(5)

\big|\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}\partial_{\eta}^{\beta}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(\xi,\eta)\big|\leq C_{\alpha,\beta}\,(|\xi|+|\eta|)^{\gamma-(|\alpha|+|\beta|)},

for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}
and some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
We associate to
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}
the bilinear form

(6)

B_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}},L}(f,g)(x)=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(L_{1},L_{2})(f\otimes g)(x,x),

for

and

, where

denotes the class of test functions associated with

. When

is the Laplacian on

, the class

is identical to the subspace of functions in

2.3
.
In general, since the diagonal

has zero measure in
(X\times X,\mu\times\mu)

6

, identity (
6

6.1
for a detailed proof.

Finally, for
w\in A_{\infty}
(see Subsection 2.1 for the definition of Muckenhoupt weights), set

\tau_{w}=\inf\{\tau\in(1,\infty):w\in A_{\tau}\}.

Given

\tau_{p,q}(w):=n\Big(\frac{1}{\min(p/\tau_{w},q,1)}-1\Big),\qquad\tau_{p}(w):=n\Big(\frac{1}{\min(p/\tau_{w},1)}-1\Big).

We denote by

, and

the weighted Besov, Triebel–Lizorkin, and Hardy spaces associated with

.

Let
L

Theorem

extend the fractional Leibniz rules to a broad non-Euclidean framework through the bilinear multiplier estimates on weighted Besov, and Triebel–Lizorkin spaces associated with operators. They not only recover the classical Euclidean case but also significantly generalize previous results obtained in more restrictive settings such as nilpotent Lie groups or Hermite operators. Importantly, the bilinear multiplier formulation goes beyond the pointwise product, encompassing a large class of bilinear pseudodifferential operators. This unified framework thus provides new tools for operator-adapted harmonic analysis and for nonlinear PDEs on spaces of homogeneous type, where bilinear estimates are crucial. Finally, it emphasizes that our method is flexible and can be applied to obtain fractional Leibniz-type rules in a variety of function spaces, including Triebel–Lizorkin and Besov spaces based on Lorentz and Morrey spaces, as well as variable-exponent Lesbesgue spaces.

Proof strategy.

Since our framework extends beyond the Euclidean setting and lacks the Fourier transform, the standard techniques of
[
43
]
are inapplicable. Even the definition of the bilinear form (
6
) requires careful examinations. To address this, we employ the space of test functions introduced in
[
46
]
(see also
[
5
]
) and define bilinear forms via multivariate spectral multipliers, combining this with singular integral theory and the recent development of function spaces beyond the classical Calderón–Zygmund setting
[
21
,
5
,
35
,
37
,
56
]
.

In comparison, the approach of
[
40
]
to fractional Leibniz rules on spaces of homogeneous type assumes, besides (A1)–(A2), additional constraints such as the Markov property
e^{-tL}1=1
, reverse-doubling, and non-collapsing conditions on

. Crucially, their methods do not extend to Besov and Triebel–Lizorkin scales. By contrast, our approach is both flexible and robust, yielding a unified framework for bilinear estimates and fractional Leibniz rules beyond the Euclidean settings.

1.3.
Applications to scattering properties in PDEs

We now discuss an application to scattering properties of solutions to certain systems of partial differential equations. We consider the system

(13)

\begin{cases}u_{t}=vw,\\
v_{t}+a(L)v=0,\\
w_{t}+b(L)w=0,\\
u(0,x)=0,\quad v(0,x)=f(x),\quad w(0,x)=g(x),\end{cases}

where
a(L)
and
b(L)
are general operators associated with a positive self-adjoint operator
L
on
L^{2}(X)
. The operators
a(L)
and
b(L)
cover a wide range of phenomena, from classical and fractional diffusion to more abstract evolutions on manifolds or Lie groups, capturing both local and nonlocal effects. Note that the particular case
L=-\Delta
the Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
was investigated in
[
43
]
.

To make the problem tractable while retaining essential features, we focus on the symmetric case

(14)

a(L)=b(L)=L^{\gamma},\quad\gamma>0,

reducing (
13
) to

(15)

\begin{cases}u_{t}=vw,\\
v_{t}+L^{\gamma}v=0,\\
w_{t}+L^{\gamma}w=0,\\
u(0,x)=0,\quad v(0,x)=f(x),\quad w(0,x)=g(x).\end{cases}

In this setting, the solutions to the linear equations can be explicitly written as

v(t)=e^{-tL^{\gamma}}f,\qquad w(t)=e^{-tL^{\gamma}}g,

so that

u(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\big(e^{-sL^{\gamma}}f\big)(x)\,\big(e^{-sL^{\gamma}}g\big)(x)\,ds.

Ignoring convergence issues, assume that
\lim_{t\to\infty}u(t,x)
exists and denote

u_{\infty}(x)=\lim_{t\to\infty}u(t,x)
. Then we may write

\displaystyle u_{\infty}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(e^{-sL^{\gamma}}f\big)(x)\,\big(e^{-sL^{\gamma}}g\big)(x)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s\big(L^{\gamma}\otimes I+I\otimes L^{\gamma}\big)}\big[f\otimes g\big](x,x)\,ds

\displaystyle=\big(L^{\gamma}\otimes I+I\otimes L^{\gamma}\big)^{-1}\big[f\otimes g\big](x,x)=B_{\mathbf{m},L}\big[f\otimes g\big](x,x),

where

\mathbf{m}(\xi,\eta)=\frac{1}{\xi^{\gamma}+\eta^{\gamma}}.

It is easy to check that the symbol
\mathbf{m}
above satisfies (
5
) with
\gamma
replaced by
-\gamma
. As a consequence of Theorem
1.1
, we obtain scattering estimates for
u_{\infty}
in the scale of weighted Besov and Triebel–Lizorkin spaces associated with
L
.

2.1.
Muckenhoupt weights and Fefferman-Stein inequality

To simplify notation, we will often use
B
for
B(x_{B},r_{B})
.
Given
\lambda>0
, we denote by
\lambda B
the
\lambda
-dilated ball, i.e. the ball centered at
x_{B}
with radius
r_{\lambda B}=\lambda r_{B}
.

A weight
w
is a nonnegative, measurable, and locally integrable function on
X
.
For any measurable set
E\subset X
, we define

w(E):=\int_{E}w(x)d\mu(x),\qquad V(E)=\mu(E).

We also use

\fint_{E}h(x)d\mu(x)=\frac{1}{V(E)}\int_{E}h(x)d\mu(x).

For
1\leq p\leq\infty
, let
p^{\prime}
denote the conjugate exponent of
p
, i.e.
1/p+1/p^{\prime}=1
.

We say that a weight
w\in A_{p}
,
1<p<\infty
, if

(16)

[w]_{A_{p}}:=\sup_{B:{\rm balls}}\Big(\fint_{B}w(x)d\mu(x)\Big)^{1/p}\Big(\fint_{B}w(x)^{-1/(p-1)}d\mu(x)\Big)^{(p-1)/p}<\infty.

For
p=1
, we say that
w\in A_{1}
if there exists
C>0
such that for every ball
B\subset X
,

\fint_{B}w(y)d\mu(y)\leq Cw(x)\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in B.

We set
A_{\infty}=\cup_{p\geq 1}A_{p}
.

For
w\in A_{\infty}
and
0<p<\infty
, the weighted Lebesgue space
L^{p}(w)
is defined by

\Big\{f:\int_{X}|f(x)|^{p}w(x)d\mu(x)<\infty\Big\}

with the norm

\|f\|_{L^{p}(w)}=\Big(\int_{X}|f(x)|^{p}w(x)d\mu(x)\Big)^{1/p}.

For
w\in A_{\infty}
we define

\tau_{w}=\inf\{q:w\in A_{q}\}.

Let
w\in A_{\infty}
and
0<r<\infty
. The weighted Hardy–Littlewood maximal function
\mathcal{M}_{r,w}
is defined by

\mathcal{M}_{r,w}f(x)=\sup_{x\in B}\Big(\frac{1}{w(B)}\int_{B}|f(y)|^{r}w(y)d\mu(y)\Big)^{1/r},

where the supremum is taken over all balls
B
containing
x
.
We drop the subscripts
r
or
w
when
r=1
or
w\equiv 1
.

It is well known that if
w\in A_{\infty}
and
0<r<\infty
, then

(17)

\|\mathcal{M}_{r,w}f\|_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}(w)}

for all
p>r
.

Moreover, for
0<r<\infty
and
p>r
,

(18)

\|\mathcal{M}_{r}f\|_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{p}(w)}

holds for all
w\in A_{p/r}
. In particular,

(19)

\|\mathcal{M}f\|_{L^{p}(w)}\leq c_{n,p}^{\star}[w]_{A_{p}}^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\|f\|_{L^{p}(w)},\quad w\in A_{p},\ 1<p<\infty,

where
c_{n,p}^{\star}
depends only on
n
and
p
.

We recall the Fefferman-Stein vector-valued maximal inequality and a variant from
[
29
]
. For
0<p<\infty
,
0<q\leq\infty
,
0<r<\min\{p,q\}
and
w\in A_{p/r}
, one has for any sequence of measurable functions
\{f_{\nu}\}
,

(20)

\Big\|\Big(\sum_{\nu}|\mathcal{M}_{r}f_{\nu}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q}\Big\|_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim\Big\|\Big(\sum_{\nu}|f_{\nu}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q}\Big\|_{L^{p}(w)}.

Finally, combining Young’s inequality with (
20
) yields: if
\{a_{\nu}\}\in\ell^{q}\cap\ell^{1}
, then

(21)

\Big\|\sum_{j}\Big(\sum_{\nu}|a_{j-\nu}\mathcal{M}_{r}f_{\nu}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q}\Big\|_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim\Big\|\Big(\sum_{\nu}|f_{\nu}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q}\Big\|_{L^{p}(w)}.

2.3.
Distributions and Peetre maximal functions

Fix a reference point
x_{0}\in X
. The class of test functions
\mathcal{S}
associated with
L
is defined as the set of all functions
\phi\in\cap_{m\geq 1}D(L^{m})
such that

(33)

\mathcal{P}_{m,\ell}(\phi)=\sup_{x\in X}(1+d(x,x_{0}))^{m}|L^{\ell}\phi(x)|<\infty,\ \ \forall m>0,\ell\in\mathbb{N}.

It was proved in
[
46
]
that
\mathcal{S}
is a complete locally convex space with topology generated by the family of seminorms
\{\mathcal{P}_{m,\ell}:\,m>0,\ell\in\mathbb{N}\}
. It was proved in
[
46
]
that when
L=-\Delta
the Laplacian on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the class
\mathcal{S}
coincide with the Schwartz class
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. As usual, we define the space of distributions
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
as the set of all continuous linear functionals on
\mathcal{S}
, with the inner product defined by

\langle f,\phi\rangle=f(\overline{\phi}),

for all
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
and
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
.

The space
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
can be used to define the inhomogeneous Besov and Triebel–Lizorkin spaces. However, to study their homogeneous counterparts, some modifications are required.

Following
[
28
]
, we define the space
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
as the set of all functions
\phi\in\mathcal{S}
such that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
g_{k}\in\mathcal{S}
with
\phi=L^{k}g_{k}
. Note that such
g_{k}
, if it exists, is unique (see
[
28
]
).

The topology of
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
is generated by the family of seminorms

\mathcal{P}^{*}_{m,\ell,k}(\phi)=\mathcal{P}_{m,\ell}(g_{k}),\ \forall m>0;\ell,k\in\mathbb{N},

where
\phi=L^{k}g_{k}
.

When
L=-\Delta
, it can be verified that the class
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
is identical to the subspace of functions in
\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that have vanishing moments of all orders. We then denote by
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{\prime}
the space of all continuous linear functionals on
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
.

For
\lambda>0,j\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbb{R})
, the Peetre-type maximal function is defined, for
f\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
, by

(34)

\varphi_{j,\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})f(x)=\sup_{y\in X}\frac{|\varphi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f(y)|}{(1+2^{j}d(x,y))^{\lambda}}\,,\quad x\in X,

where
\varphi_{j}(\lambda)=\varphi(2^{-j}\lambda)
.

Clearly,

\varphi_{j,\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})f(x)\geq|\varphi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f(x)|,\ \ \ x\in X.

Similarly, for
s,\lambda>0
we set

(35)

\varphi_{\lambda}^{*}(s\sqrt{L})f(x)=\sup_{y\in X}\frac{|\varphi(s\sqrt{L})f(y)|}{(1+d(x,y)/s)^{\lambda}},\ \ \ f\in\mathcal{S}_{\infty}.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathscr{S}([0,\infty))
supported in
[0,4]
. For
0<p<\infty
,

and
\lambda>\max\{n\tau_{w}/p,n/\tau_{w}\}
we have

\sup_{s>0}\varphi^{*}_{\lambda}(s\sqrt{L})f\lesssim\|\varphi\|_{W^{\ell}_{\infty}}\sup_{s>0}(e^{-sL})^{*}_{\lambda}f,

\Big\|\sup_{s>0}(e^{-sL})^{*}_{\lambda}f\Big\|_{L^{p}(w)}\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{p}_{L}(w)},

where
\varphi^{*}_{\lambda}(s\sqrt{L})f
and

are defined as in (

\ell=\lfloor 2n+\lambda\rfloor+2
and
\|\varphi\|_{W^{\ell}_{\infty}}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leq\ell}\|\partial^{\alpha}f\|_{L^{\infty}}

Proof.

Set

C_{\varphi,\ell}=\|\varphi\|_{\infty}+\|\varphi^{(\ell)}\|_{\infty},\quad\ell=\lfloor 2n+\lambda\rfloor+2.

Fix

. By integration by parts,

\int_{s}^{\infty}(t^{2}u)^{m}e^{-t^{2}u}\,\frac{dt}{t}=P(us),

where

Hence,

(38)

I=P(s^{2}L)e^{-s^{2}L}+\int_{0}^{s}(t^{2}L)^{m}e^{-tL}\,\frac{dt}{t}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\varphi(s\sqrt{L})f

\displaystyle=P(s^{2}L)e^{-s^{2}L}\varphi(s\sqrt{L})f+\int_{0}^{s}(t^{2}L)^{m}e^{-t^{2}L}\varphi(s\sqrt{L})f\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle=P(s^{2}L)e^{-s^{2}L}\varphi(s\sqrt{L})f+\int_{0}^{s}\Big(\frac{t}{s}\Big)^{2m}e^{-t^{2}L}(s\sqrt{L})^{2m}\varphi(s\sqrt{L})f\,\frac{dt}{t}.

By Theorem
2.7
and Lemma

,

\displaystyle\frac{|\varphi(s\sqrt{L})f(y)|}{(1+d(x,y)/s)^{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{\varphi,\ell}\Big(1+\frac{d(x,y)}{s}\Big)^{-\lambda}\int_{X}D_{s,\lambda+n+1}(y,z)|e^{-s^{2}L}f(z)|\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle+C_{\varphi,\ell}\Big(1+\frac{d(x,y)}{s}\Big)^{-\lambda}\int_{0}^{s^{2}}\Big(\frac{t}{s}\Big)^{2m}\int_{X}D_{s,\lambda+n+1}(y,z)|e^{-t^{2}L}f(z)|\,d\mu(z)\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{\varphi,\ell}\sup_{s>0}\sup_{z}\frac{|e^{-s^{2}L}f(z)|}{(1+d(x,z)/s)^{\lambda}}\int_{X}D_{s,n+1}(y,z)|e^{-s^{2}L}f(z)|\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle+C_{\varphi,\ell}\int_{0}^{s^{2}}\Big(\frac{t}{s}\Big)^{2m-\lambda}\int_{X}D_{s,n+1}(y,z)\Big(1+\frac{d(z,x)}{t}\Big)^{-\lambda}|e^{-t^{2}L}f(z)|\,d\mu(z)\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{\varphi,\ell}\sup_{s>0}\sup_{z}\frac{|e^{-s^{2}L}f(z)|}{(1+d(x,z)/s)^{\lambda}}.

This implies

\sup_{s>0}\varphi^{*}_{\lambda}(s\sqrt{L})f\lesssim C_{\varphi,\ell}\sup_{s>0}(e^{-s^{2}L})^{*}_{\lambda}f,

which completes the first part.

For the second part, choose

with
\lambda\theta>n
and

. By (

Indeed, from (

e^{-s^{2}L}f=P(s^{2}L)e^{-s^{2}L}e^{-s^{2}L}f+\int_{0}^{s}\Big(\frac{t}{s}\Big)^{2m}e^{-t^{2}L}(s^{2}L)^{m}e^{-s^{2}L}f\,\frac{dt}{t}.

2.7

\displaystyle\frac{|e^{-s^{2}L}f(y)|}{(1+d(x,y)/s)^{\lambda}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(1+\frac{d(x,y)}{s}\Big)^{-\lambda}\int_{X}D_{s,\lambda}(y,z)|e^{-s^{2}L}f(z)|\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle+\Big(1+\frac{d(x,y)}{s}\Big)^{-\lambda}\int_{0}^{s}\Big(\frac{t}{s}\Big)^{2m}\int_{X}D_{s,\lambda}(y,z)|e^{-t^{2}L}f(z)|\,d\mu(z)\,\frac{dt}{t}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{X}\frac{1}{V(z,s)}\Big(1+\frac{d(x,z)}{s}\Big)^{-\lambda}|e^{-s^{2}L}f(z)|\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{s^{2}}\Big(\frac{t}{s}\Big)^{2m-\lambda}\int_{X}\frac{1}{V(z,t)}\Big(1+\frac{d(x,z)}{t}\Big)^{-\lambda}|e^{-t^{2}L}f(z)|\,d\mu(z)\,\frac{dt}{t}.

It follows that

\displaystyle(e^{-s^{2}L})^{*}_{\lambda}f(x)

\displaystyle\lesssim\big[(e^{-s^{2}L})^{*}_{\lambda}f(x)\big]^{1-\theta}\int_{X}\frac{1}{V(z,s)}\Big(1+\frac{d(x,z)}{s}\Big)^{-\lambda\theta}|e^{-s^{2}L}f(z)|^{\theta}\,d\mu(z)

Let
\psi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})

It is easy to see that
\phi\in\mathscr{S}([0,\infty))
and
\operatorname{supp}\phi\subset[0,2]
; moreover, for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}

\phi_{k}(\xi):=\phi(2^{-k}\xi)=\sum_{j\leq k}\psi_{j}(\xi).

We write

(40)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(\xi,\eta)\psi_{k}(\xi)\psi_{j}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}\atop j\leq k}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(\xi,\eta)\psi_{k}(\xi)\psi_{j}(\eta)+\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}\atop k\leq j-1}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(\xi,\eta)\psi_{k}(\xi)\psi_{j}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(\xi,\eta)\psi_{k}(\xi)\phi_{k}(\eta)+\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(\xi,\eta)\phi_{j-1}(\xi)\psi_{j}(\eta).

Consequently,

B_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}},L}(f,g)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}B_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{1,k},L}(f,g)+\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}B_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{2,j},L}(f,g),

\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{1,k}(\xi,\eta)=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(\xi,\eta)\psi_{k}(\xi)\phi_{k}(\eta)
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{2,j}(\xi,\eta)=\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(\xi,\eta)\phi_{j-1}(\xi)\psi_{j}(\eta)
.

Since these two terms above are similar, we need only to estimate the first term

\displaystyle\sum_{k}B_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{1,k},L}(f,g).

Note that

(41)

\operatorname{supp}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{1,k}\subset\{(\xi,\eta):2^{k-2}\leq|\xi|\leq 2^{k+1},\;0\leq|\eta|\leq 2^{k+1}\}.

Following
[
16

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{1,k}(\xi,\eta)

Let
\Psi,\Phi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\operatorname{supp}\Psi(\xi)\subset[1/3,3],\qquad\Psi(\xi)=1\quad\text{if }|\xi|\in[1/2,2]

Applying Lemma

\displaystyle B_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{1,k},L}(f,g)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{(n_{1},n_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}c_{k}(n_{1},n_{2})\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\sum_{R\in\mathcal{D}_{k}}\omega_{Q}\Psi_{k,n_{1}}(\sqrt{L})f(x_{Q})\widetilde{\Gamma}_{k}(x,x_{Q})\omega_{R}\Phi_{k,n_{2}}(\sqrt{L})g(x_{R})\widetilde{\Gamma}_{k}(x,x_{R}).

(44)

\displaystyle(\sqrt{L})L^{s/2}\big[B_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}_{1,k},L}(f,g)\big](x)

5.
Applications

In this section, we discuss some applications of our main results, focusing on Theorem
1.1
and Corollary
1.2
, since several examples of operators covered by Theorem
1.3
were given in the introduction. The list below is not exhaustive but illustrates the flexibility and effectiveness of our method.

5.1.
Fractional Leibniz Rule on nilpotent Lie groups

We start by recalling some standard definitions and facts concerning nilpotent Lie groups (see
[
27
,
44
,
54
]
).
Throughout, let
G
denote a connected, simply connected nilpotent Lie group, and let
\mu
be its bi-invariant Haar measure.

Consider a finite family of left-invariant vector fields

\mathbf{X}=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell}\}

on
G
that satisfies Hörmander’s condition, meaning that
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell}\}
together with their commutators up to order
k_{0}
generate the tangent space at every point of
G
.
The differential operator associated with this system,

(52)

L:=-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}X_{i}^{2},

is referred to as the
sub-Laplacian
.

The system
\mathbf{X}
naturally defines a control metric
\rho
on
G
, which is left-invariant and induces the topology of
G
.
For
x\in G
, we set
|x|:=\rho(x,e)
, where
e
denotes the identity.
By left-invariance,

\rho(x,y)=|y^{-1}x|=|x^{-1}y|,\qquad x,y\in G.

For
r>0
, let
B(x,r)
denote the open
\rho
-ball of radius
r
centered at
x
, and write
V(x,r):=\mu(B(x,r))
.
There exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that for all
x\in G
,

(53)

C_{1}^{-1}r^{d}\leq V(x,r)\leq C_{1}r^{d},\qquad r\in(0,1],

and

(54)

C_{1}^{-1}r^{D}\leq V(x,r)\leq C_{1}r^{D},\qquad r\in[1,\infty),

where the constants
d>0
and
D>0
are called the
local dimension
and the
dimension at infinity
of
G
, respectively.
Since
G
is simply connected, one always has
0<d\leq D
(see
[
54
, Remark IV.5.9]
).

In particular, the measure
\mu
obeys the doubling property (
2
) with
n=D
, so that
(G,\rho,\mu)
is a homogeneous type space in the sense of Coifman–Weiss.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, define the set of multi-indices

\mathcal{I}(\ell,k):=\{1,\ldots,\ell\}^{k}=\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}):1\leq i_{j}\leq\ell\ \text{for all }j\}.

By convention, let
\mathcal{I}(\ell,0):=\{(0)\}
, and set

\mathcal{I}(\ell):=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\mathcal{I}(\ell,k).

Because
\mathbf{X}
satisfies Hörmander’s condition, the collection
\{X^{I}:I\in\mathcal{I}(\ell)\}
spans the algebra of left-invariant differential operators on
G
.
For a multi-index
I=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in\mathcal{I}(\ell,k)
, define

X^{I}:=\begin{cases}X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{k}},&k\geq 1,\\
\mathrm{Id},&k=0,\end{cases}

and write
|I|:=k
for its length.

Proposition 5.1
.

(
[
54
, Theorem IV.4.2]
)

Let
h_{t}(x,y)
be the heat kernel of the sub-Laplacian
L
. Then, for every
I\in\mathcal{I}(\ell)
,

|X^{I}_{x}h_{t}(x,y)|\leq C\frac{1}{t^{|I|/2}V(x,\sqrt{t})}\exp\left(-\frac{|y^{-1}x|}{ct}\right)

for all
x,y\in G
and
t>0
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
L
be a sub-Laplacian on a stratified Lie group
G
, and let
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}
satisfy (
5
) for some
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
0<q\leq\infty
, and let

satisfy (
7
), and

If

,

Proof.

Obviously, by Proposition

For (A3), note that by Proposition

Fractional Leibniz rules on stratified Lie groups:
We now consider an important subclass of nilpotent Lie groups, which is stratified Lie groups. A connected and simply connected Lie group is said to be
stratified
if its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
admits a stratification, i.e., a direct sum decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{\kappa}

such that

(3.22)

[V_{1},V_{i-1}]=V_{i}\quad\text{for }2\leq i\leq\kappa,\qquad[V_{1},V_{\kappa}]=\{0\}.

Obviously, such a Lie group is nilpotent of step
\kappa
. Let
m=\dim(V_{1})
, and let
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}
be an arbitrary basis of
V_{1}
. Identifying
\mathfrak{g}
with the Lie algebra of all left-invariant vector fields on
G
, it follows from (
3.22
) that the system
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\}
satisfies the Hörmander condition. Consider the sub-Laplacian operator

L:=-\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2},

which we still denote by
L
without risk of confusion.

A key feature of these spaces is that the weighted Besov and Triebel-Lizorkin spaces are independent of the choices of the sub-Laplacians. More precisely, we have the following result.

Proposition 5.4

(
[
36
]
)
.

Let
G
be a stratified Lie group, and let
L
and
\widetilde{L}
be two arbitrary sub-Laplacians on
G
. Then:

(i)

For
p,q\in(0,\infty]
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and
w\in A_{\infty}
,

\dot{B}^{s,L}_{p,q}(w)=\dot{B}^{s,\widetilde{L}}_{p,q}(w)

with equivalent quasi-norms.

(ii)

For
p\in(0,\infty),q\in(0,\infty]
,
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and
w\in A_{\infty}
,

\dot{F}^{s,L}_{p,q}(w)=\dot{F}^{s,\widetilde{L}}_{p,q}(w)

with equivalent quasi-norms.

In fact,
[
36
, Theorem 7]
proved the coincidence of the unweighted spaces; however, the same argument also works for the weighted case. Hence, from the above result, we can omit the symbol
L
in the notation of the Besov and Tribel-Lizorkin spaces associated to
L
to briefly write
\dot{B}^{s}_{p,q}(w)
and
\dot{F}^{s}_{p,q}(w)
. It is important to notice that in the particular case

,
\dot{F}^{0}_{p,2}(w)
becomes the Hardy spaces
H^{p}
introduced in
[
26
]
. Hence, from Theorem
5.8
and Corollary
5.9
we deduce the following results.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let

be a sub-Laplacian on the stratified Lie group

, and let
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}

5.2.
Fractional Leibniz rule associated with Grushin operators

For
x=(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the Grushin operator

(70)

L_{G}=-\Delta_{x^{\prime}}-|x^{\prime}|^{2}\partial_{x^{\prime\prime}}^{2},\qquad\Delta_{x^{\prime}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{x^{\prime}_{j}}^{2}.

The operator
L_{G}
is hypoelliptic and is homogeneous of degree
2
with respect to the anisotropic dilations

\delta_{r}(x)=(rx^{\prime},r^{2}x^{\prime\prime}),\qquad r>0,

in the sense that

L_{G}(f\circ\delta_{r})=r^{2}(L_{G}f)\circ\delta_{r}.

Set

(71)

X_{j}=\partial_{x^{\prime}_{j}},\quad X_{n+j}=x^{\prime}_{j}\partial_{x^{\prime\prime}},\qquad j=1,\dots,n,

and let
X=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{2n}\}
. Then

L_{G}=-\sum_{j=1}^{2n}X_{j}^{2},

and a direct computation gives the nontrivial commutator

[X_{j},X_{n+j}]=\partial_{x^{\prime\prime}},\qquad j=1,\dots,n,

so the Hörmander condition is satisfied.

The operator
L_{G}
is symmetric and nonnegative on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
; in fact it is essentially self-adjoint. We equip
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the control (Carnot–Carathéodory) distance
d
associated to the vector fields
X
(see, e.g.,
[
48
]
). This distance is homogeneous with respect to
\delta_{r}

d(\delta_{r}x,\delta_{r}y)=r\,d(x,y),

and satisfies the two-sided comparability

d(x,y)\simeq|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|+\min\!\Big\{\frac{|x^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime\prime}|}{|x^{\prime}|+|y^{\prime}|},\;|x^{\prime\prime}-y^{\prime\prime}|^{1/2}\Big\}.

If
|\cdot|
denotes Lebesgue measure and
B(x,r)
the ball for
d
, then

|B(x,r)|\simeq r^{n+1}\,(r+|x^{\prime}|),

and consequently for

\Big(\frac{R}{r}\Big)^{n+1}\lesssim\frac{|B(x,R)|}{|B(x,r)|}\lesssim\Big(\frac{R}{r}\Big)^{n+2}.

Thus
(\mathbb{R}^{n+1},d,|\cdot|)
is doubling and satisfies the geometric conditions used below.

For multi-index notation, let for
h\in\mathbb{N}

\mathcal{I}^{h}=\{I=(i_{1},\dots,i_{h}):i_{j}\in\{1,\dots,2n\}\}.

For

5.3.
Fractional Leibniz Rules associated with Hermite operators

For each nonnegative integer
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the Hermite polynomial of order
k
is defined by

H_{k}(t)=(-1)^{k}e^{t^{2}}\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}\big(e^{-t^{2}}\big).

In the one-dimensional case, the Hermite function of degree
k
is given by

h_{k}(t)=\big(2^{k}k!\sqrt{\pi}\big)^{-1/2}H_{k}(t)\,e^{-t^{2}/2},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}.

For higher dimensions, if
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{n}
is a multi-index, the associated Hermite function is constructed as

h_{\xi}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}h_{\xi_{j}}(x_{j}),\qquad x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

These functions serve as eigenfunctions of the Hermite operator
\mathcal{L}
, satisfying

\mathcal{L}(h_{\xi})=(2|\xi|+n)h_{\xi},

and they constitute a complete orthonormal system in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see, for example,
[
53
]
).

For each
k\geq 0
, denote

W_{k}:=\mathrm{span}\{h_{\xi}:|\xi|=k\},\qquad V_{N}:=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{N}W_{k}.

The orthogonal projection of a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
onto
W_{k}
is given by

\mathbb{P}_{k}f=\sum_{|\xi|=k}\langle f,h_{\xi}\rangle\,h_{\xi},

with the corresponding kernel representation

\mathbb{P}_{k}(x,y)=\sum_{|\xi|=k}h_{\xi}(x)h_{\xi}(y).

Given a ‘symbol’
\textbf{m}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{C}

the
bilinear Hermite pseudo-multiplier

T_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}
is defined through the formula

(76)

T_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}},\mathcal{L}}(f,g)(x)=\sum_{j,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(x,\lambda_{j},\lambda_{k})\,\mathbb{P}_{j}f(x)\,\mathbb{P}_{k}g(x),

where, for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
,
\lambda_{\ell}=2\ell+n
are the eigenvalues of

\mathcal{L}
and
\mathbb{P}_{\ell}
are orthogonal projectors onto spaces spanned by Hermite functions.

In this section, we assume that there exists
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}
and
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,

(77)

\big|\partial_{x}^{\sigma}\partial_{\xi}^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}_{\eta}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}(x,\xi,\eta)\big|\lesssim_{\kappa,\alpha,\beta}\big(\xi+\eta\big)^{\gamma-(\alpha+\beta)+|\sigma|},\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

The following result address the boundedness of the bilinear Hermite pseudo-multiplier.

(1-2\varepsilon)\|\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})f\|_{2}^{2}\leq\langle V_{j}f,f\rangle\leq\|\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})f\|_{2}^{2}.

Consequently,

\langle R_{j}f,f\rangle\leq 2\varepsilon\|\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})f\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\varepsilon\|f\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus
\|R_{j}\|_{2\to 2}\leq 2\varepsilon
.

Define

T_{j}:=({\rm Id}-R_{j})^{-1}={\rm Id}+\sum_{k\geq 1}R_{j}^{k}=:{\rm Id}+S_{j}.

Since
\Gamma_{j}^{2}(\sqrt{L})\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f=\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f
, we obtain

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f

\displaystyle=T_{j}\circ\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f-T_{j}\circ R_{j}\circ\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f

\displaystyle=T_{j}\circ\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f-T_{j}\circ\Gamma_{j}^{2}(\sqrt{L})\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f+T_{j}\circ V_{j}\circ\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f

\displaystyle=T_{j}\circ V_{j}\circ\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle V_{j}\circ\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\omega_{Q}\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x,x_{Q})\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f(x_{Q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\omega_{Q}\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x,x_{Q})\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f(x_{Q}),

where we used
\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f=\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f
.

Therefore,

\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\omega_{Q}\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f(x_{Q})\,T_{j}\big[\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,x_{Q})\big](x).

Set

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(x,y):=T_{j}\big[\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,y)\big](x).

Then

\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f(x)=\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\omega_{Q}\psi_{j}(\sqrt{L})f(x_{Q})\,\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(x,x_{Q}),

which proves (iii).

We next prove that
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}
satisfies (i)-(ii). Firstly, from Lemma
2.10
and
2.11
, it follows that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(96)

|R_{j}(x,y)|\lesssim 2c_{\sharp}c_{\diamond}E_{2^{-j},\kappa_{0}}(x,y),\qquad x,y\in X,

(97)

|L^{k}R_{j}(x,y)|\lesssim 2^{2jk}E_{2^{-j},\kappa_{0}}(x,y),\qquad x,y\in X,

and

(98)

|R_{j}(x,y)-R_{j}(\overline{x},y)|\lesssim(2^{j}d(x,\overline{x}))^{\delta_{0}}E_{2^{-j},\kappa_{0}}(x,y),

for all
x,\overline{x},y\in X
with
d(x,\overline{x})<2^{-j}
.

From (
96
) and Lemma
2.11
, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and for all
x,y\in X
,

|R_{j}^{k}(x,y)|\leq c^{k-1}_{\sharp}(2c_{\sharp}c_{\diamond})^{k}E_{2^{-j},\kappa_{0}}(x,y).

On the other hand, for
k\geq 2

\displaystyle|R_{j}^{k}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq\|R_{j}(x,\cdot)\|_{2}\|R_{j}\|_{2\to 2}^{k-2}\|R_{j}(\cdot,y)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{(2c_{\sharp}c_{\diamond})^{2}\varepsilon^{k-2}}{\sqrt{V(x,2^{-j})V(y,2^{-j})}},

where the constant
C
only depends on
n
and
\kappa_{0}
.

Interpolating these two inequalities,

(99)

|R_{j}^{k}(x,y)|\lesssim(2c^{2}_{\sharp}c_{\diamond}\varepsilon)^{(k-2)/2}E_{2^{-j},\kappa_{0}}(x,y)=2^{-(k-2)/2}E_{2^{-j},\kappa_{0}}(x,y),

since
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{2c_{\sharp}}
.

We now prove the estimate (i). Indeed, by Lemma
2.10
,

|\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x,y)|\lesssim c_{\diamond}E_{2^{-j},\kappa_{0}}(x,y).

Moreover, from
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(x,y):=T_{j}\big[\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,y)\big](x)
and
T_{j}={\rm Id}+S_{j}
, we have

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(x,y)=\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x,y)+\int_{X}S_{j}(x,z)\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(z,y)\,d\mu(z).

Hence, from this and Lemma
2.11
, it suffices to show that

|S_{j}(x,y)|\lesssim E_{2^{-j},\kappa_{0}/2}(x,y).

To do this, using (
99
),

\displaystyle|S_{j}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}|R_{j}^{k}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\lesssim E_{2^{-j},\kappa_{0}/2}(x,y)+\sum_{k\geq 2}2^{-(k-2)/2}E_{2^{-j},\kappa_{0}/2}(x,y)

\displaystyle\lesssim E_{2^{-j},\kappa_{0}/2}(x,y),

as desired, and therefore (i) holds for
m=0
.

In addition, note that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x,y)+\int_{X}S_{j}(x,z)\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(z,y)\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x,y)+\int_{X}R_{j}(x,z)\,\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(z,y)\,d\mu(z),

where we used
S_{j}=\sum_{k\geq 1}R_{j}^{k}=R_{j}\sum_{k\geq 0}R_{j}^{k}=R_{j}T_{j}
.

This, together with (i) for
m=0
, (
97
), (
98
), and Lemmas
2.10
and
2.11
, proves (i) and (ii).

It remains to prove (iii). To do this, we compute

\displaystyle L^{m}\big[\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(\cdot,y)\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(\cdot,w)\big](x)

\displaystyle=L^{m}\big[\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,y)\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,w)\big](x)

\displaystyle+\int_{X}L^{m}\big[\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,y)R_{j}(\cdot,z)\big](x)\,\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(z,w)\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle+\int_{X}L^{m}\big[\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,w)R_{j}(\cdot,z)\big](x)\,\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(z,y)\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle+\int_{X}\int_{X}L^{m}\big[R_{j}(\cdot,z_{1})R_{j}(\cdot,z_{2})\big](x)\,\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(z_{1},y)\widetilde{\Gamma}_{j}(z_{2},w)\,d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})

\displaystyle=:E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3}+E_{4}.

By condition (A3),

E_{1}\lesssim 2^{2jm}D_{2^{-j},N}(x,y)D_{2^{-j},N}(x,w).

The estimates of
E_{2},E_{3}
are similar and simpler, so we only detail
E_{4}
. Recall that

R_{j}(x,y)=\Gamma^{2}_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x,y)-\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\omega_{Q}\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x,x_{Q})\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x_{Q},y),

which implies that

\displaystyle L^{m}

\displaystyle\big[R_{j}(\cdot,y)R_{j}(\cdot,z)\big](x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{m}\big[\Gamma^{2}_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,y)\Gamma^{2}_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,z)\big](x)

\displaystyle-\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\omega_{Q}L^{m}\big[\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,x_{Q})\Gamma^{2}_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,z)\big](x)\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x_{Q},y)

\displaystyle-\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\omega_{Q}L^{m}\big[\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,x_{Q})\Gamma^{2}_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,y)\big](x)\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x_{Q},z)

\displaystyle+\sum_{Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\omega_{Q}\omega_{Q^{\prime}}L^{m}\big[\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,x_{Q})\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(\cdot,x_{Q^{\prime}})\big](x)\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x_{Q},y)\Gamma_{j}(\sqrt{L})(x_{Q^{\prime}},z)

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}+F_{4}.

We now estimate
F_{4}
. For
M=N+2n
, by (A3) and Theorem
2.7
,

\displaystyle F_{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{2mj}\sum_{Q,Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\omega_{Q}\omega_{Q^{\prime}}D_{2^{-j},M}(x,x_{Q})D_{2^{-j},M}(x,x_{Q^{\prime}})D_{2^{-j},M}(x_{Q},y)D_{2^{-j},M}(x_{Q^{\prime}},z)

\displaystyle\simeq 2^{2mj}\Big[\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\omega_{Q}D_{2^{-j},\sigma}(x,x_{Q})D_{2^{-j},M}(x_{Q},y)\Big]\Big[\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\omega_{Q^{\prime}}D_{2^{-j},M}(x,x_{Q^{\prime}})D_{2^{-j},M}(x_{Q^{\prime}},z)\Big]

\displaystyle\simeq 2^{2mj}\Big[\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\int_{Q}D_{2^{-j},\sigma}(x,w)D_{2^{-j},M}(w,y)d\mu(w)\Big]\Big[\sum_{Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{j}}\int_{Q^{\prime}}D_{2^{-j},M}(x,w)D_{2^{-j},M}(w,z)d\mu(w)\Big]

\displaystyle\simeq 2^{2mj}\Big[\int_{X}D_{2^{-j},M}(x,w)D_{2^{-j},M}(w,y)d\mu(w)\Big]\Big[\int_{X}D_{2^{-j},M}(x,w)D_{2^{-j},M}(w,z)d\mu(w)\Big]

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{2mj}D_{2^{-j},M}(x,y)D_{2^{-j},M}(x,z),

where in the last inequality we used Lemma
2.5
.

Similarly,

F_{1}+F_{2}+F_{3}\lesssim 2^{2mj}D_{2^{-j},N+n}(x,y)D_{2^{-j},M}(x,z).

Therefore,

L^{m}\big[R_{j}(\cdot,y)R_{j}(\cdot,z)\big](x)\lesssim 2^{2mj}D_{2^{-j},N+n}(x,y)D_{2^{-j},M}(x,z).

This, together with Lemma
2.5
, implies that

\displaystyle E_{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{2mj}\int_{X}\int_{X}D_{2^{-j},N}(x,z_{1})D_{2^{-j},N}(x,z_{2})D_{2^{-j},N}(z_{1},y)D_{2^{-j},N}(z_{2},w)d\mu(z_{1})d\mu(z_{2})

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{2mj}D_{2^{-j},N}(x,y)D_{2^{-j},N}(x,w).

This completes the proof of (iii).
∎

We now give the proof of the inequality (
50
). In fact, the inequality (
50
) is a direct consequence of the following proposition.

Proposition 6.7
.

Let
L
satisfy the condition (A1) and let
w\in A_{\infty}
,
r\in(0,1]
,
k,n_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\lambda>0
. Then we have

\mathcal{M}_{r,w}\Big(\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g\Big)(x)\simeq\mathcal{M}_{r,w}\Big(\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g\Big)(y)

for
x,y\in Q\in\mathcal{D}_{k}
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the claim for
r=1
and
w\equiv 1
, since the case
r\in(0,1)
and
w\in A_{\infty}
follows by the same argument with minor modifications. By definition,

\mathcal{M}\big(\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g\big)(x)=\sup_{B\ni x}\fint_{B}\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g(z)\,d\mu(z).

Split the supremum according to the radius of the ball:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\big(\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g\big)(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\ni x\\
r_{B}\geq 4\ell(Q)\end{subarray}}\fint_{B}\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g(z)\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\ni x\\
r_{B}<4\ell(Q)\end{subarray}}\fint_{B}\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g(z)\,d\mu(z).

If
B\ni x
and
r_{B}\geq 4\ell(Q)
, then for every
y\in Q
we have
y\in B
. Hence for any fixed
y\in Q
,

\fint_{B}\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g(z)\,d\mu(z)\leq\mathcal{M}\big(\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g\big)(y),

and therefore

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\ni x\\
r_{B}\geq 4\ell(Q)\end{subarray}}\fint_{B}\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g(z)\,d\mu(z)\leq\mathcal{M}\big(\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g\big)(y).

If
B\ni x
and
r_{B}<4\ell(Q)
, then for any
z\in B
and any
y\in Q
(with
Q
containing
x
) we have

\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g(z)\lesssim\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g(y).

Therefore

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\ni x\\
r_{B}<4\ell(Q)\end{subarray}}\fint_{B}\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g(z)\,d\mu(z)

\displaystyle\lesssim\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g(y)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{M}\big(\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g\big)(y).

Combining the two cases yields

\mathcal{M}\big(\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g\big)(x)\lesssim\mathcal{M}\big(\Phi_{k,n_{2},\lambda}^{*}(\sqrt{L})g\big)(y),

for any fixed
y\in Q
, as required. This completes the proof.

∎
