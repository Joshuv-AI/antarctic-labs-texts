A novel parameter for risk stratification in patients with takotsubo syndrome
Authors: Gassan Moady, Amir Khoury, Lihi Levi-Gofman, Alon L. Rougin, Shaul Atar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsTakotsubo syndrome (TTS) is an acute reversible cardiomyopathy characterized by transient reduction in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) with typical echocardiographic appearance. The syndrome is often preceded by emotional or physical trigger and predominantly affects postmenopausal women. Several biomarkers and scores have been suggested for risk stratification in TTS. In this retrospective study, we aimed to evaluate the association between fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR) and outcomes in patients with TTS.MethodsWe assessed the association of FAR with LVEF, in-hospital complications, and readmissions in patients with TTS.ResultsA total of 116 patients (94% female, mean age 70 years) were included in the final analysis. The mean LVEF was 45% and the mean FAR was 129. In-hospital complications occurred in 34 patients (29%) while 26 patients (22%) were readmitted. A higher FAR was associated with a lower LVEF [Odds ratio (OR) 1.48, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.25–1.74, p < 0.001] and in-hospital complications (OR 1.18, 95% CI 1.02–1.37, p < 0.025), including heart failure (HF) signs and arrhythmia. Patients with higher FAR values also had a higher rate of readmissions. A FAR cutoff value of 136 may help identify patients at increased risk of complications.ConclusionIn patients with TTS, higher FAR values were associated with lower LVEF and increased rates of in-hospital complications and readmissions for HF. FAR may represent a promising biomarker for risk stratification, however, its prognostic value requires validation in larger, external prospective cohorts.

Introduction

Takotsubo syndrome (TTS), also known as apical ballooning syndrome or stress- induced cardiomyopathy, is characterized by an acute transient reduction in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) that typically occurs in older women following mental or physical stressors [1], [2], [3]. Its clinical presentation typically mimics acute myocardial infarction (AMI), with chest pain, electrocardiographic (ECG) changes, elevated cardiac biomarkers, and characterized wall-motion abnormalities on echocardiography [4]. Chest pain is the most common presenting symptom, followed by dyspnea and syncope. Less common symptoms include thromboembolic events such as cerebrovascular accidents as well as malignant arrhythmia and sudden cardiac death [1], [5]. Electrocardiographic (ECG) changes include ST-segment elevation in the precordial leads, typically without reciprocal ST depression and without the development of Q waves, deep T wave inversion, and QT-segment prolongation [6], [7], [8], [9]. Echocardiography plays a central role in diagnosis and risk stratification in TTS and helps to differentiate between the different types of TTS [10]. The most common echocardiographic pattern is apical akinesia with basal hypercontractility, commonly referred to as apical ballooning or typical TTS, which accounts for approximately 80% of cases [10], [11]. Less common variants include the reverse type, characterized by basal akinesis with apical hypercontractility, as well as midventricular and focal TTS [10], [11], [12], [13]. Recovery of cardiac function is typically rapid and complete, although the time course varies. Many patients demonstrate substantial improvement within 48–72 h, while complete normalization of LVEF generally occurs within 7–14 days in uncomplicated cases and may take up to 4–8 weeks in others [14], [15], [16]. The MAYO criteria, traditionally used for the diagnosis of TTS, include transient cardiac dysfunction, the absence of coronary plaque rupture, new ECG abnormality or modest biomarker elevation, and the exclusion of pheochromocytoma and myocarditis [4]. The more recent InterTAK diagnostic criteria emphasize that TTS can coexist with coronary artery disease (CAD), that an emotional or physical trigger may be present or absent, that cardiac function is expected, that ECG changes may include QT prolongation, and that right ventricular involvement may occur [2]. Although TTS is considered reversible, severe cases complicated by malignant arrhythmia, acute heart failure (HF), and cardiogenic shock may occur [1], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]. Several biomarkers have been investigated for the diagnosis and risk stratification of TTS, including N-terminal-prohormone B type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), troponin, the BNP-to-troponin ratio, and C-reactive protein (CRP) [19], [20], [21], [22]. The fibrinogen-to-albumin ratio (FAR) is a relatively novel, readily available biomarker that has been evaluated for risk stratification in various cardiovascular and inflammatory diseases [23], [24]. During systemic inflammation, fibrinogen levels generally increase whereas albumin acts as a “negative” acute phase reactant, with low levels potentially reflecting a more pronounced inflammatory response [25]. Higher FAR values have been associated with adverse outcomes in ischemic heart disease (IHD), HF, and myocarditis [26], [27], [28]. In the present study, we aimed to evaluate the association between FAR levels and outcomes in patients with TTS.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study included patients hospitalized with TTS between 2018 and 2024. Three patients were excluded because of missing laboratory or echocardiographic data. TTS was diagnosed according to contemporary diagnostic criteria based on the clinical presentation, electrocardiographic abnormalities, cardiac biomarker levels, echocardiographic findings, and coronary angiography to exclude obstructive CAD as the primary cause of the presentation. Nevertheless, the coexistence of CAD was not considered an exclusion criterion, in accordance with the InterTAK diagnostic criteria. Left ventriculography was also performed during coronary angiography to demonstrate the characteristic apical ballooning pattern. All included patients had the typical form of TTS, characterized by apical akinesia and basal hypercontractility. A detailed medical history was obtained to identify potential emotional or physical triggers preceding symptom onset. Patients were admitted to the cardiology department and underwent close clinical monitoring, serial ECG, echocardiography, routine laboratory testing, and coronary angiography. Patients with signs or symptoms of HF were treated, as clinically indicated, with diuretics, beta-blockers, and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors. Atrial fibrillation was managed with rhythm control therapy and anticoagulation when appropriate. Sustained ventricular tachycardia was treated according to the patient's hemodynamic status, including observation, pharmacological cardioversion, or electrical cardioversion. In patients with cardiogenic shock related to left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction, inotropic therapy was discontinued, and intravenous beta-blockers and fluid administration were considered, as clinically appropriate. The study was approved by the local institutional ethics committee. Approval number: NHR-0018-26.

ECG and laboratory findings

A 12‑lead ECG was obtained and analyzed upon admission. ECG abnormalities considered suggestive of TTS included ST-segment elevation in the precordial leads, new deep T-wave inversion, and ST-segment depression. QT-interval prolongation was also documented. Laboratory parameters included complete blood count, renal function tests, troponin, fibrinogen, and albumin levels. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin I (hs-cTnI) was measured using the ARCHITECT assay (Abbott Laboratories). Values above 20 ng/L in men and 30 ng/L in women were considered elevated. Fibrinogen levels were expressed in mg/dL, with an upper reference limit of 430 mg/dL, and albumin levels were expressed in g/dL, with a reference range of 3.5–5.4 g/dL. The FAR was calculated using the following formula: FAR = fibrinogen (mg/dL) / albumin (g/dL). All laboratory measurements used in the analysis were obtained within the first two days of admission.

Echocardiographic data

Echocardiography was performed using a Philips Epiq-7 machine with EPIQ X8-2t transducer (Phillips, Andover, MA, USA). LVEF was calculated by Simpson's biplane method in the apical four-chamber and two-chamber views using the following formula: LVEF = (end-diastolic volume- end-systolic volume)/ end-diastolic volume X100. Typical TTS was defined by echocardiography showing apical hypokinesia/akinesia with basal hypercontractility. In patients with severe cardiac dysfunction or cardiogenic shock, follow-up echocardiography was performed before hospital discharge. After discharge, follow-up echocardiographic data were available for 89 patients (77%). All echocardiographic examinations were performed and interpreted by experienced cardiologists.

Outcomes

Study outcomes included complications occurring during the index hospitalization, in-hospital mortality, and readmission within one year. In-hospital complications included HF, arrhythmias, LVOT obstruction, and thromboembolic events. HF was defined by clinical or radiographic evidence of volume overload, including pulmonary congestion or pulmonary edema. LVOT obstruction was documented by echocardiography, with or without associated mitral regurgitation. Arrhythmias included both supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias. In-hospital mortality and readmissions (for HF symptoms or chest pain) within one year of discharge were also recorded.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations (SDs) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), as appropriate. The association between FAR and LVEF was assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient. Logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the association between FAR and in-hospital complications, with multivariable models used to adjust for relevant covariates. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to assess the ability of FAR to discriminate between patients with and without in-hospital complications and between patients with preserved and reduced LVEF (cutoff of 50%). The optimal FAR cutoff value for predicting complications was determined from the ROC curve. Because of the limited number of readmission events, Cox proportional hazards regression produced unstable estimates. Therefore, incidence-rate ratios were calculated as an approximate measure of the relative event rate. Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test were used to compare one-year readmission-free survival between patients with high and low FAR values. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 116 patients were included in the final analysis; the mean age was 70 years, and 94% were female. Neurological and psychiatric disorders were present in 14% and 12% of patients, respectively. An emotional or physical trigger preceding symptom onset was identified in 75% of patients. All patients were admitted to either the cardiology ward or the cardiac intensive care unit, according to their hemodynamic status. Chest pain was the most common presenting symptom (63%), followed by dyspnea and syncope. Discussion

In the present study, we evaluated the association between FAR and clinical outcomes in patients with TTS. Higher FAR values were independently associated with reduced LVEF and with higher rates of in-hospital complications and readmissions. FAR is an inflammation-related biomarker that can be readily calculated from routinely available blood tests and, when clinically indicated, measured serially. Previous studies have shown that elevated FAR values are associated with adverse outcomes in IHD, including both acute and chronic presentations, as well as in myocarditis and HF [26], [27], [28]. During sepsis, elevated fibrinogen levels primarily reflect increased synthesis, whereas hypoalbuminemia appears to result mainly from enhanced vascular permeability and circulatory clearance rather than reduced hepatic production [29]. Elevated levels of interleukin-6, CRP, and other inflammatory biomarkers during the acute phase of TTS, together with the increased occurrence of TTS in patients with sepsis, further support a potential role of systemic inflammation in the pathophysiology of the syndrome [22], [30], [31]. Growing evidence suggests that residual inflammation at discharge may influence long-term outcomes, including persistent left ventricular dysfunction and increased cardiovascular mortality [32], [33], [34]. In one study, hypoalbuminemia was present in 32% of patients at admission and was independently associated with higher in-hospital mortality [35]. By contrast, the role of fibrinogen appears more complex, as its levels may vary throughout the disease course. Fibrinogen typically rises during the acute phase and subsequently normalizes or declines [36]. In TTS, fibrinogen may contribute to cytokine release in parallel with the intensity of the stress response and the severity of the disease. Our findings provide further insight into the potential role of FAR as a marker of disease severity, as higher FAR values were associated with reduced LVEF. This observation is consistent with previous studies demonstrating a pronounced inflammatory response during the acute phase of TTS [33]. Although fibrinogen has a half-life of approximately 3–5 days in the blood, fluctuations in the acute phase of inflammation is common [37]. We also found that high FAR levels were associated with increased rate of complications. In a recent report, complications and mortality still substantial (about 6.5%) in TTS, with no improvement over years, with main complications included cardiogenic shock, acute HF, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and cardiac arrest [38]. Although the association between inflammatory biomarkers and adverse outcomes in TTS is well established, findings regarding markers of myocardial injury, such as troponin, have been less consistent. TTS is generally characterized by transient myocardial dysfunction rather than extensive myocardial necrosis. Consequently, troponin elevation is typically modest and disproportionate to the severity of ventricular dysfunction, whereas natriuretic peptide levels are often markedly elevated [39], [40], [41]. Patients with high FAR values (with cutoff value of 136) were at increased risk for readmissions for HF. However, it should be noted that the thresold found in our study needs further external validation since it is based on a relatively small and single-center cohort. Readmissions after TTS are generally within weeks from discharge since cardiac function recovers in few days-weeks following the acute episode. In a large retrospective study, approximately 12% of patients with TTS were readmitted within 30 days, with nearly two-thirds of these readmissions occurring during the first two weeks. HF was the most common cause of readmission [42]. Beta-blocker therapy has been associated with lower mortality; however, there is no clear evidence that it reduces TTS recurrence or accelerates recovery of cardiac function [43]. Because no single variable reliably identifies patients at increased risk of mortality or readmission, risk stratification should integrate both clinical and laboratory parameters. Among the clinical factors, reduced LVEF, male sex, and selected comorbidities appear to be among the strongest predictors of adverse outcomes [44]. Biomarkers and imaging modalities also play important roles in the diagnosis and prognostic assessment of TTS. Natriuretic peptides are well-established markers of disease severity, whereas the prognostic value of troponin is less clearly defined. A marked and rapidly rising troponin concentration strongly favors AMI, while a more modest elevation is more characteristic of TTS [45]. Although markedly elevated troponin levels may be associated with adverse outcomes, such elevations generally reduce the likelihood of TTS and should raise suspicion for AMI. Persistent inflammation, reflected by elevated CRP levels at discharge, has also been proposed as a prognostic marker [46]. Other emerging biomarkers include copeptin, fetuin-A, soluble ST2, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [47], [48], [49], [50], [51], [52]. These observations highlight the potential importance of inflammatory biomarkers, rather than markers of myocardial injury such as troponin, in the risk stratification of patients with TTS. Commonly used risk scores include the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score, originally developed for patients with atrial fibrillation, as well as the GEIST and InterTAK scores [53], [54], [55]. These scores incorporate clinical comorbidities, the severity of left ventricular dysfunction, and, in the case of the InterTAK score, NT-proBNP levels. More recently, the addition of CRP to the InterTAK score has been proposed to improve its ability to predict one-year mortality [46]. Our findings suggest that FAR, a simple and readily available laboratory parameter, may be useful for risk stratification during the acute phase of TTS. Consistent with previous studies demonstrating the prognostic importance of residual inflammation at discharge, FAR may also help identify patients at increased risk of subsequent readmission. Patients with elevated FAR levels at discharge may benefit from closer clinical follow-up; however, this hypothesis requires confirmation in larger prospective cohorts. At present, evidence supporting long-term benefit from beta-blockers or RAAS inhibitors in TTS remains limited. Therefore, improving outcomes may depend primarily on the early identification of high-risk patients and the provision of structured follow-up. Future studies should also evaluate whether incorporating FAR, together with NT-proBNP, into the InterTAK prognostic score improves its predictive performance.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and retrospective design limit the strength and generalizability of the conclusions. Because the pathophysiology of TTS is complex, residual confounding may remain despite adjustment for relevant clinical variables. Second, NT-proBNP was not included in the analysis because of substantial missing data. As NT-proBNP is an established biomarker for risk stratification in TTS, future studies should compare its prognostic performance with that of FAR and assess whether combining both biomarkers improves existing clinical risk scores. Such an approach may provide complementary information regarding systemic inflammation, reflected by FAR, and myocardial wall stress, reflected by NT-proBNP. Third, most patients in our cohort were hemodynamically stable, and the incidence of major complications, including cardiogenic shock, was relatively low. Therefore, our findings may not be generalizable to patients presenting with hemodynamic instability or severe TTS. The relatively favorable clinical course may partly reflect early recognition, prompt diagnostic evaluation, and timely management. Nevertheless, unstable patients should be managed according to their hemodynamic status, degree of ventricular dysfunction, and the presence of LVOT obstruction, irrespective of biomarker levels. Fourth, serial FAR measurements were not available, although changes in FAR over time may provide further information regarding disease progression and recovery. Fifth, we did not perform subgroup analysis by trigger type (emotional vs. physical) due to the relatively low rate of complications which may further decrease the statistical power. The prognosis and the course of the disease may vary according to the trigger type, and we recommend that further studies should also address this issue. Finally, the study included only patients with the typical apical form of TTS; therefore, the findings may not be applicable to less common morphological variants.

Conclusions

In patients with TTS, higher FAR values are associated with lower LVEF and increased rate of in-hospital complications and readmissions for HF. FAR may be promising candidate biomarker that requires external prospective validation.
