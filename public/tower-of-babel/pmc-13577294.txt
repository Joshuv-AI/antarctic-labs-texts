Patterns of antibiotic use among adult patients across seven hospitals in Malawi: opportunities and challenges for antimicrobial stewardship programmes
Authors: Adriano F Lubanga, Akim N Bwanali, Watipaso Kamanga, Sibongile Kondowe, Ellen Nzima, Annie Mwale, Enerico Chrisp, David Nyirenda, Steward Mudenda, Joseph Chizimu, Samuel Mpinganjira, Cynthia Chitule, Thomas Nyirenda, Collins Mitambo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the top threats to global health, with a disproportionately higher burden in low-and middle-income countries. It is largely driven by excessive and improper use of antibiotics. Optimising antibiotic use has been shown to curb the propagation of AMR. However, this requires data on pre-existing levels and trends of antibiotic use in the healthcare setting that would inform the initiation of appropriate antimicrobial stewardship programmes. Therefore, we carried out this point prevalence survey (PPS) to determine the current patterns of antibiotic use among adult patients across seven facilities in Malawi.We conducted a point prevalence survey using Global Point Prevalence survey forms across three secondary level and four tertiary level hospitals in Malawi between 30 September and 11 October 2024. The survey included all patients who were admitted before 8.00am on the days of data collection, and data were collected on the same day. Adult patient-level antibiotic use data were extracted from medical records using a modified Global point prevalence survey form. Data collected were entered into an Excel sheet and analysed using IBM SPSS version 26 using simple descriptive statistics.ResultsThe survey included a total of 2050 patients across all Hospitals. Overall, antibiotic use was 41.0%, with the highest usage recorded in the Intensive care unit at 62.5%. Ceftriaxone was the most used antibiotic. The highest usage proportion was registered at Mangochi District Hospital (61.5%). The most common indication for antibiotic use was central nervous system infections. Most (74.2%) of the prescriptions were not accompanied by infection biomarkers. However, there was a good self-reported adherence to treatment guidelines.ConclusionThe PPS shows rising antibiotic use across AWaRe classes, with more empiric treatments, highlighting the urgent need for antimicrobial stewardship to prevent misuse, resistance, and reduced effectiveness.KEYWORDS: Antibiotics, point prevalence, antimicrobial resistance, antimicrobial stewardship, Malawi

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a rapidly escalating public health concern that threatens effective prevention and control of infectious diseases (Dadgostar, 2019; Watkins & Bonomo, 2016). In 2019, drug-resistant infections were estimated to contribute to a devastating 4.95 million deaths, and 1.27 million deaths were attributed to AMR globally (Focus Lubanga et al., 2024). The highest burden of AMR impact is felt in southern Africa, registering the highest mortality rate (23.5 deaths per 100,000 population) attributable to AMR compared to other regions (Murray et al., 2022). The high AMR burden in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs) is attributed to overuse and misuse of antibiotics, poor sanitation, low vaccination rates, and poor infection prevention and control practices in LMICs (Iskandar et al., 2020; Moyo et al., 2023).

The primary driver of AMR is the misuse of antibiotics in humans, animals, and plants because of uncontrolled access, inappropriate and excessive prescribing by healthcare practitioners, and the lack or non-utilisation of treatment guidelines in case management (Engler et al., 2021; Kariuki et al., 2022). The Mapping Antimicrobial Resistance and Antimicrobial Use Partnership (MAAP) Country Reports in 2017 indicated 14 countries in SSA having high antimicrobial consumption rates and a high drug resistance index, particularly for antibiotics in the Access and Watch categories of the WHO antibiotic classification (Mapping Antimicrobial Resistance and Antimicrobial Use Partnership (MAAP) Country Reports – Africa CDC, n.d.). Recent point prevalence surveys (PPSs) conducted across sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) consistently demonstrate excessive antibiotic use among hospitalised adult patients, with prevalence frequently exceeding 50% (Boltena et al., 2024; Siachalinga et al., 2023). Excessive use of antibiotics in SSA is driven by multiple factors, including lack of appropriate diagnostic services, which accelerates empiric antibiotic prescribing, lack of antimicrobial prescribing guidelines, and lack of dedicated antimicrobial stewardship programmes (ASPs) to enforce adherence to appropriate prescription practices (Cox et al., 2017; Godman et al., 2021).

Recent PPS evidence further shows disproportionate use of Watch-group antibiotics under the WHO AWaRe classification, with limited Access-group utilisation and negligible Reserve-group stewardship oversight, reflecting weak antimicrobial governance structures in many SSA hospitals (Moja et al., 2024; Mutatina et al., 2025; WHO, 2023). Studies from Zambia and Ethiopia report Watch-group antibiotics accounting for over 60% of prescriptions, raising concerns about accelerated resistance selection in resource-limited settings where diagnostic capacity is constrained (Cox et al., 2017; Fadare et al., 2019; Godman et al., 2021).

To combat the growing AMR burden, the May 2015 World Health Assembly adopted the Global Action Plan and advised member states to develop and adopt corresponding National Action Plans (NAPs) that should form frameworks for guiding and coordinating locally and contextually appropriate interventions against AMR (Kariuki et al., 2022). A key component of NAPs among hospitals is undertaking PPS studies to establish ongoing antibiotic utilisation patterns to guide the initiation of targeted quality improvement and ASPs (Fuller et al., 2022; Godman et al., 2022).

PPSs provide real-time actionable data to inform prescribing practices, thereby guiding ASPs to combat the AMR burden through evidence-based multicomponent strategies, including AMR surveillance, education, and guidance to encourage rational antimicrobial utilisation (Dyar et al., 2017; Singh et al., 2019). Demonstrating the crucial role of PPS, several studies have reported low knowledge levels on optimal antibiotic use to be a key challenge in implementation of ASPs (Fabre et al., 2023; Harun et al., 2024; Wesangula et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2022).

Malawi, a low-income country in SSA, mirrors the aforementioned trends in AMR burden and antibiotic utilisation. In Malawi in 2021, there were an estimated 3080 deaths attributable to AMR and 12,900 deaths associated with AMR (Lester et al., 2022; Musicha et al., 2017). Despite developing its first NAP on AMR (2017–2022), recent facility-level data on antibiotic utilisation remain limited, while implementation of the second NAP (2023–2030) has been delayed (WHO, 2022).

In the context of Malawi’s high burden of AMR, limited recent data on antibiotic utilisation, and the delayed implementation of the second NAP on AMR, up-to-date PPS data are essential to inform the design, prioritisation, and monitoring of interventions under the new NAP (2023–2030).

Materials and methods

Study design, period, and setting

A Point Prevalent Survey (PPS) was conducted across seven facilities in Malawi, consisting of 4 tertiary hospitals and 3 secondary-level facilities between 30th September and 11th October 2024 using the Global Point Prevalent Survey forms (GPPS) 2023. The facilities were chosen purposively based on region and level of the hospital and included all the central hospitals in all the regions. Figure 1 represents a geographical map of the surveyed areas:

Geographic location of the surveyed areas.

All the facilities were purposively selected based on existing antimicrobial stewardship committees and availability of functioning laboratory systems with good diagnostic capabilities. All the facilities offer both inpatient and outpatient services, including general medical, maternity, surgical, obstetrics and gynecology, dental, and many others.

The study population comprised adult inpatients admitted to the participating health facilities at the time of the point prevalence survey. Inclusion criteria were: (i) patients aged 16 years and above, (ii) admitted to medical, surgical, obstetrics and gynecology, or other adult inpatient wards, and (iii) present in the ward before 08:00 h on the day of the survey as per the GPPS methodology recommendation. Exclusion criteria included: (i) pediatric patients aged <16 years, (ii) outpatients and day-case patients, (iii) patients admitted to pediatric wards, (iv) patients discharged, transferred, or deceased before data collection, and (v) patients admitted after the survey had commenced. Only patients with complete clinical and prescription records at the time of data collection were included in the analysis. Additionally, all patients that got antivirals and antifungal medications were excluded from the analysis, as the survey targeted antibiotics and no other antimicrobial medicines.

Ethical consideration

The point prevalence survey was part of the ongoing efforts by the Public Health Institute of Malawi through the National Antimicrobial Resistance Coordinating Centre in order to improve ASPs in the country. Permission to conduct the survey was obtained from the National Health Sciences Research Ethics Committee (NHSRC) and from the respective ethics committees in the surveyed facilities. This baseline was part of the bigger study which aimed at rationalising antibiotic use for the treatment of blood stream and urinary tract infections (Protocol # 23/11/4048). The survey involved retrospective collection of information from medical records and notes and did not involve interventions or interactions with patients.

Sample size estimation

As per the Global Point Prevalence Survey (GPPS) methods, all patients that met the inclusion criteria were included in the study. These were patients admitted to the hospital on the days of the survey.

Data collection

Data were collected between 30th September and 11th October 2024 using the GPPS forms. The information collected included the hospital, (ii) ward, (iii) patient, (iv) indication, and (v) antibiotic use.

We also categorised the prescribed antibiotics by AWaRe classification (Antibiotic usage was classified into Access, Watch, and Reserve categories based on the World Health Organization (WHO) AWaRe Classification of antibiotics, as outlined in the WHO Model List of Essential Medicines and the AWaRe framework. Results

In this survey, a total of 2050 patients were included, with 413 being from Zomba Central Hospital, 551 from Kamuzu Central Hospital, 311 from Mzuzu Central Hospital, 457 from Queen Elizabeth Central Hospital, 33 from Malamulo, 155 from Mzimba District Hospital, and 130 from Mangochi District Hospital. Overall, the proportion that were on antibiotics across all facilities was 41.0%. The highest prescribing proportion of antibiotics was noted from Mangochi District Hospital (61.5%), seconded by Malamulo Hospital (48.5%), with the least being Mzimba District Hospital (29.0%) (Table 1).

Discussion

This was the first PPS involving multiple facilities conducted in Malawi. The study reveals a 41.0%-point prevalence of antibiotic usage across all seven hospitals, most of which is empiric. There is increasing usage of the Watch category of antibiotics in the AWaRe classification, predominantly ceftriaxone.

The 41% is slightly high as compared to compared to the standard reference value (< 30%) of globally introduced WHO prescribing indicators (Desalegn, 2013). However, compared to other countries in southern Africa, the antibiotic consumption rates in our study fall on the lower side. Antibiotic usage in most countries is mostly above 50% and as high as 80% in some facilities in Nigeria (Anand Paramadhas et al., 2019; Boltena et al., 2024; Siachalinga et al., 2023). This could be attributed to a massive scale-up of AMR efforts in Malawi since the year 2015, which has included AMR surveillance and establishment of well-coordinated ASPs primarily in tertiary facilities that promote responsible use of antibiotics (Kamalo et al., 2023). However, rational antibiotic use goes beyond modest antibiotic usage, as it also encompasses appropriate dosing and duration, as well as access to a wide range of efficacious antibiotics for multiple therapeutic options. As this PPS shows high rates of antibiotic use, especially in secondary health facilities, it is imperative to comprehensively extend stewardship principles to them. Furthermore, building on the current antibiotic usage entails further research to understand the drivers of high consumption in some facilities. As it was not a focus of this study, it is crucial to establish prescribing practices of healthcare practitioners and accordingly align them with antimicrobial stewardship.

The study reports increased use of antibiotics from the Watch category of the WHO AWaRe classification. This finding derails the WHO national-level target of at least 70% of antibiotic consumption being in the Access category that was meant to be achieved in 2023 as a way of optimising antibiotic use (Klein et al., 2021). However, the finding is in contrast to the 2017 MAAP findings, which reported that the average national consumption of antibiotics in Malawi was dominated by Access antibiotics, accounting for 92.5% of the total consumption, while Watch and Reserve antibiotics were used minimally at 7.5% and 0.0%, respectively (MAAP Annual Report, 2017). The difference could be explained by the fact that this survey focused on in-patients whilst the MAAP report entails both in-patients and outpatients. Further in support of that, a more recent study detailing antibiotic dispensing practices across community pharmacies and drugstores found that antibiotics from the Access category were the most dispensed antibiotics (Lubanga, et al., 2024). Furthermore, it could be due to changes in the supply chain, as antibiotic procurement is also determined by resistance patterns observed in routine hospital AMR surveillance. Therefore, it is imperative to enhance monitoring of antibiotic use patterns across hospitals, community pharmacies, and communities to establish current consumption rates and any shifts in utilisation based on AWaRe classification.

The survey reveals that ceftriaxone was the most prescribed antibiotic, which is in the Watch category. Since its introduction into the Malawian National formulary in 2005, ceftriaxone, a third-generation cephalosporin, is a commonly used antibiotic for in-patients and sometimes out-patients, and is often prescribed empirically without the backing of microbiological tests (Mithi et al., 2024; Musicha et al., 2017). The high utilisation of ceftriaxone observed in this survey is likely partly explained by its favourable pharmacokinetic profile, including good penetration across the blood–brain barrier, making it a recommended first-line agent for the empirical management of CNS infections, which were the most commonly documented indication for antibiotic prescribing in our study (Prášil et al., 2010). The high use of ceftriaxone corroborates reports from other studies done in Africa. Consequently, there have been rapid increases in the incidence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Enterobacteriaceae (ESBL-E) in Malawi, and these are untreatable due to scarcity of locally available treatment alternatives (Lester et al., 2022; Musicha et al., 2017). A similar resistance pattern linked to cephalosporin overuse has also been observed across SSA, including the neighbouring Zambia (Kasanga et al., 2024; Onduru et al., 2021). Resistance to cephalosporins poses a big challenge in the treatment of infectious diseases, as they are often used as first-line and last-line antibiotics in many secondary health facilities in Malawi, and the resulting treatment failure increases mortality rates in hospitalised patients (Lester et al., 2022; Mithi et al., 2024). Carbapenem antibiotics have been the treatment option for ESBL-E, usually in tertiary-level facilities (Lewis et al., 2020). Of concern, carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae have been detected in Malawi, which could be attributed to the increasing use of carbapenems in tertiary hospitals, after these were introduced to the Malawian essential medicine list in 2015 (Kamalo et al., 2023; Lewis et al., 2020). This is a classic example of the well-documented impact of antibiotic overuse on AMR (Kariuki et al., 2022). Given the role that the reduction in antibiotic use is predicted to play in reducing AMR, it is crucial to fortify strategies to regulate antibiotic use in hospital settings. It is of utmost importance to strengthen the supply chain system to ensure availability of a wide range of antibiotics to allow prescribers to prescribe what is appropriate and not simply what is available at the health facility. Additionally, the introduction of sustained AMS programmes leads to a reduction in the usage of third-generation cephalosporins in adult inpatients (Lester et al., 2020).

Additionally, there was high self-reported adherence to national treatment guidelines among prescribers. This is an improvement from guideline adherence observed in a PPS done at a secondary health facility in the country in 2022 (Mithi et al., 2024). The improvement could be attributed to ongoing AMR awareness that has been scaled up over the past couple of years (WHO, 2022). However, the finding in our study could be biased as it was self-reported by healthcare workers in the involved facilities. Elsewhere across SSA, there have been concerns with adherence to recommended antibiotic treatments and doses for infectious diseases in Tanzania and Kenya (Okoth et al., 2018; Wiedenmayer et al., 2021). Reported factors influencing adherence rates include their routine availability, ease of access and referencing, the extent of consensus on their content, extent of training in their use, monitoring of subsequent prescribing against agreed suggestions, and whether regularly updated (Campbell et al., 2021). However, a study at QECH in 2021 in Malawi showed a commendable 84% adherence to antibiotic prescription guidelines (Sibande et al., 2022). This lays a good foundation that increasing awareness of AMR and availability of guidelines among healthcare workers in Malawi could prove instrumental in sustaining good prescription practices. Antibiotic guidelines are a key component of antimicrobial stewardship, and adherence to antibiotic prescribing regulations proves to improve patient outcomes.

The most common indicator for antibiotic prescription was CNS infection, followed by pneumonia. Most facilities (especially secondary level health facilities) lack the capacity to investigate for CNS infections in Malawi due to a lack of diagnostic microbiological and radiological services. As a result, most treatments are empiric, with no accompanying biomarkers as reflected in our findings. Empiric treatments are quite common in LMICs due to the high burden of infectious diseases coupled with a lack of microbiological diagnostic services (Kariuki et al., 2022; Nkengasong & Tessema, 2020). However, studies have shown that only around 60% of empirically started antibiotics are considered appropriate (Okoth et al., 2018). Empiric treatments, though, sometimes standardised as syndromic management in SSA, provide a relatively easy outlet for antibiotic prescription, thereby increasing antibiotic consumption (Godman et al., 2022). It has also been noted that despite the capacity of laboratories to run microbiology tests, some healthcare providers still opt for empiric treatment (Iregbu et al., 2019; Skodvin et al., 2019). This shows underutilisation of microbiology tests to guide prescriptions and necessitates the development of local antibiograms. Therefore, it is imperative to enhance the effective use of available laboratory services and foster efforts to improve the availability of laboratory tests to improve prescriptions and patient outcomes.

Limitations of the study

Despite our study generating some key insights into the patterns of antibiotic use across multiple hospitals in Malawi, the findings need to be interpreted with caution. The study targeted individuals on antibiotics during the day of the survey, and was done during the summer period. The findings may vary due to seasonality effects; for instance, in the rainy season there is an increase in the number of febrile cases, which may lead to higher prescription of antibiotics as compared to what is presented in our study. Our study population also did not include pediatric which are among the major consumers of antibiotics, and that would have an impact on the overall percentage of antibiotics used. The numerator included those on antibiotics without excluding those who had a real reason to be on antibiotics, necessitating further studies to complement such gaps.

In addition, as this study was designed as a descriptive point prevalence survey, inferential analyses exploring factors associated with antibiotic prescribing and AWaRe antibiotic utilisation were not undertaken. Future analytical studies using longitudinal patient-level data should investigate demographic, clinical and facility-level predictors of antibiotic use and stewardship outcomes.

Conclusion

Despite registering a good antibiotic use rate, the PPS shows an increasing consumption of AWaRe antibiotics in hospital settings, increased antibiotic use in some facilities, as well as increased empiric treatments. This threatens to derail efforts to curb the increasing burden of AMR in Malawi. Therefore, this calls for enforcing antimicrobial stewardship campaigns across health facilities to promote rational prescribing practices. Further evidence is also required to determine prescribing practices in healthcare settings and associated drivers, as well as comprehensively assess the role of primary and secondary facilities, as well as the community, in propagating and addressing AMR.

Recommendations

Optimisation of antibiotic use is a core strategic element of the response to AMR, addressing misuse and overuse as primary drivers of AMR. Efforts to improve prescribing practices in health facilities should therefore be accompanied by deliberate measures to improve antibiotic use in the community to limit consumption of antibiotics in humans, animals and the environment. This could include education campaigns at the community level on appropriate antibiotic use and fostering rational antibiotic dispensing practices in private pharmacies and drugstores. In addition, Hospitals need to adhere to WHO recommendations for antibiotic usage rates as per the WHO AWaRe classification.

Additionally, there is a need to enhance AMR surveillance at the primary and secondary levels. This goes together with strengthening research capacity in these facilities and the informal sector to detect any changes in resistance patterns and potential driving factors in order to curb the rising threats with urgency.

Finally, optimising antimicrobial use and addressing the AMR burden in Malawi requires a multi-sectoral, systematic and holistic approach to ensure that policies, regulations and guidelines are not only in place, but also enforced to guide all efforts against AMR. Therefore, it is essential to promote the ‘One Health' approach to address AMR comprehensively across the environment, animal and human health sectors.
