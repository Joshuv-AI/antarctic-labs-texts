Facilitators and barriers affecting healthy lifestyle behaviors among suburban Javanese women living in Indonesia: a qualitative study
Authors: Yogi Fitriadi, Iztihadun Nisa, Regita Rahma Maharatri, Bernadette Josephine Istiti Kandarina, Emy Huriyati, Adi Heru Sutomo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA healthy lifestyle is essential for preventing and managing noncommunicable diseases (NCDs). The Javanese are the largest ethnic group in Indonesia and are highly at risk of contracting NCDs. Women have a greater risk of NCDs than men. Efforts to prevent and control NCDs are significant for adult women. Therefore, the perceptions of adult Javanese women should be explored to design health promotion programs aimed at engendering healthy lifestyles and preventing also controlling NCDs, specifically among suburban Javanese women.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted on 20 participants using the cognitive, opportunity, motivation–behavior (COM–B) theoretical framework to identify facilitators and barriers to a healthy lifestyle. This included 12 in-depth interviews and one focus-group discussion (FGD) with eight participants comprising community members, health cadres, health workers, and community leaders. This study aimed to identify factors that support and inhibit healthy lifestyles among adult Javanese women in suburban areas.ResultsWithin the COM–B framework, women’s capabilities to adopt a healthy lifestyle were limited by physical constraints and psychological barriers, including boredom and medication-related misconceptions. Their opportunities were shaped by supportive elements such as digital exercise resources and screenings for NCD risk factors during social activities. Still, they were hindered by domestic workload, low community participation, and cultural norms such as “ewuh pakewuh.” Women’s motivations, influenced by perceived health benefits but weakened by fear of screenings and reliance on herbal remedies, finally shaped their engagement in healthy behaviors.ConclusionsThe study highlights both supporting and inhibiting factors that impact healthy lifestyles among adult Javanese women living in suburban areas. Strengthening community engagement and addressing cultural and motivational barriers are essential to fostering sustained adoption of a healthy lifestyle among Javanese adult women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26494-2.

Background

The global prevalence of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) continues to increase each year. NCDs significantly affect both men and women. Most NCD-related deaths occur in developing countries [15]. NCDs become the most significant burden to women’s lives. They contribute to 73% of deaths among women globally. Most deaths from NCDs are due to cardiovascular diseases, cancer, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of death from NCDs in women worldwide [18]. This situation is similar to that in Indonesia, where cardiovascular diseases are also the leading cause of death from NCDs [5]. The incidence of NCDs, specifically those associated with cardiovascular diseases, can be prevented by adopting a healthy lifestyle. Standard healthy lifestyle practices include a nutritious diet, regular exercise, reducing alcohol and tobacco consumption, screening for NCD risk factors, and adherence to medication regimens for patients with NCD [15].

The prevalence of NCDs, such as hypertension, in Indonesia is higher among adult women (35.4%) than men (31.0%) [30]. Increased risk factors for NCDs related to healthy living also vary among different ethnic groups, and the social, cultural, and behavioral influences of specific ethnic groups can affect their health behaviors, potentially serving as either protective factors or factors contributing to the increased prevalence of NCDs [32]. Cultural norms within society, such as Javanese cultural values, play an important role in shaping healthy lifestyle behaviors among Javanese individuals. Javanese cultural practices, such as deference or reluctance to challenge older individuals, may shape behavioral patterns in social interactions within the community [24].

The Javanese are the largest ethnic group in Indonesia. Most of them are Muslim, with some still adhering to Javanese culture and beliefs [41]. Therefore, barriers and facilitators of healthy lifestyle behaviors should be addressed to prevent NCDs among Javanese adult women, as this population has the most significant impact on NCD prevalence among adults in Indonesia, by evaluating behavioral aspects at both the individual and social relationship levels. These insights may serve as essential considerations for healthcare providers and policymakers in designing NCD prevention programs through healthy lifestyle promotion that align with the sociocultural context for adult women of Javanese ethnicity, the largest ethnic group in Indonesia, in the coming years.

Suburban populations encounter a broad range of economic, social, educational, and health-related challenges across their life course. The repercussions of these challenges extend beyond this demographic group and can influence the broader community [22]. Moreover, the structural characteristics of suburban environments commonly hinder the adoption of healthy behaviors, thereby increasing sedentary lifestyles, heightening dependence on automobiles, and reducing social interaction. Consequently, individuals residing in suburban areas are more susceptible to NCDs, such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and stroke [28].

Recent studies in Indonesia have investigated the facilitators and barriers to adopting healthy lifestyle behaviors, such as unhealthy dietary patterns driven by limited knowledge of how to prepare nutritious meals; social factors associated with shared eating practices involving unhealthy foods; and insufficient self-efficacy to engage in physical activity [4, 33]. Previous studies have not addressed the supporting and inhibiting factors associated with healthy living among the Javanese, especially women living in suburban areas [13]. Previous studies employing socioecological and socio-cognitive theoretical frameworks have not thoroughly examined the contextual domains of behavior arising from internal (physical and psychological) and external (social) factors that are directly relevant to the development of health promotion interventions aimed at enhancing healthy lifestyle behaviors. Therefore, this study explores the facilitators and barriers to adopting healthy lifestyle behaviors among suburban Javanese women using the cognitive, opportunity, motivation–behavior (COM–B) model.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

Qualitative approaches were used to explore experiences with facilitators and barriers to healthy lifestyle behaviors among Javanese adult women living in a suburban area of Yogyakarta Province, Indonesia. Our study was conducted in the Cupwatu I suburban area located in the Kalasan district, which has the highest prevalence of NCDs, such as hypertension and obesity, in Sleman Regency, Yogyakarta Province, Indonesia [16]. The Kalasan district is located in Sleman Regency, Yogyakarta Province, a lowland area, with most of its population in the productive age group (40–44 years) and having a senior high school level of education. Most residents in this area are Javanese and Muslim, employed at private sector or factory workers, or work as housewives and laborers in the surrounding area [37].

Study Participants

Our inclusion criteria for study participants were Javanese women aged 18–65 years, with or without a history of NCDs, residing in Cupuwatu I, Kalasan district, for at least the past year. The Javanese are one of the largest ethnic groups in Indonesia. Among Indonesia’s various ethnic groups, the Javanese comprise the most significant proportion, accounting for 40% of the population. Javanese communities are primarily concentrated in Central Java, East Java, and the Special Region of Yogyakarta, with sizable populations also residing in Jakarta, Banten, Lampung, and several regions of Sumatra [3]. This ethnic group is characterized by distinct cultural, linguistic, and culinary traditions, as well as a communication style marked by gentle, refined expression. Javanese culture is widely recognized for its deep philosophical foundations, specifically the pursuit of wisdom, which emphasizes understanding one’s origins and life purpose, and these values are reflected throughout the community’s life-cycle ritual [21].

The study participants were recruited using purposive sampling techniques based on inclusion criteria. Those with diverse demographic characteristics, such as income (lower/higher minimum regional wage), occupation (informal, formal worker, housewife), and education (junior, senior high school, diploma, and university), were also included to maximize variability. Participants with NCDs and complications such as stroke or heart disease, pregnant women, and those with disabilities were excluded from the study. Furthermore, local health workers and community health workers, also known as cadres/health cadres, in Indonesia, were interviewed about existing health promotion programs related to healthy living in the community [35]. A health cadre in Indonesia is a community member who volunteers and is chosen by their community to support primary healthcare activities. They act as intermediaries between formal health services (such as Puskesmas/Public Health Center) and the community by promoting health, conducting basic monitoring or screening, and mobilizing local participation in health programs.

Recruitment and Data Collection

The researchers obtained permission from community leaders and coordinated with health cadres during the recruitment process. The researchers held meetings with health cadres and explained the planned activities to the community, both during sessions and at a community social event. Health cadres helped researchers continue socializing with potential participants, based on inclusion and exclusion criteria, and provided the contact information of those who wanted to participate in this study. The researchers then contacted the potential participant. Health cadres assisted in identifying participants based on information obtained during meetings with the research team, particularly individuals residing in Cupuwatu I who demonstrated either active engagement in healthy lifestyle behaviors (such as regular physical activity) or a sedentary lifestyle. Recruitment through health cadres aimed at enhancing participants’ trust and engagement in research activities. Before data collection, the researchers explained the purpose and objectives to the participants, and participants signed informed consent forms.

The qualitative data collection was conducted from May 2024 to July 2024. Our interview and FGD guidelines were developed by all the researchers through discussion and pilot-tested with three participants before being used for data collection. A pilot study was conducted to evaluate the usability of the in-depth interview (IDI) and FGD guides for data collection. Several additional questions were identified from the pilot study, particularly regarding the inclusion of items on the role of social and community activities as facilitators or inhibitors of healthy lifestyle behavior. IDIs were conducted with 12 participants and one FGD with eight participants, for a total of 20 participants in the qualitative data collection. IDIs were conducted to explore participants’ experiences with healthy behaviors. Subsequently, FGD were conducted in a group setting to determine whether differences occurred between perspectives shared individually and those expressed in a group. Data were collected at the participant’s home via face-to-face or telephone call. The first author (YF) facilitated the FGD, and the second author (IN) took field notes. Both authors (YF and IN) also conducted IDIs. Both authors have experience completing training programs and conducting qualitative studies. Neither the interviewer nor the author was related to the participants. All IDIs and the FGD were conducted in Indonesian, with some use of Javanese. Data collection started with IDIs, followed by FGD. The FGD was conducted with the standard number of participants (6–10) per FGD guidelines. Each IDI lasted 40–60 min, and the FGD lasted 70 min. Data collection was stopped once saturation was reached. Data saturation was reached at 10 IDIs and 1 FGD; however, the researchers conducted two additional IDIs to confirm that saturation had indeed been achieved. Data saturation was reached when no new codes or subthemes emerged during data collection.

Data Analysis

A deductive thematic analysis was conducted based on qualitative methods previously employed [8]. The COM–B model served as a guide in designing the IDI and FGD questions. Themes were first mapped to the COM–B theoretical framework, and a detailed analysis of the barriers and facilitators affecting healthy behavior practices among Javanese women was then conducted. The data analysis process includes transcription and verification, familiarization, coding, subtheme generation, and mapping predefined COM–B components [27].

Data collections were recorded using an audio recorder and then transcribed. The professional transcription service www.transkrip.id assisted the transcription process. YF, IN, and RRM reviewed the transcription result. After completing the first draft of our data transcription, we return the results to the participants and ask for their feedback. The first author (YF) and second author (IN) each conducted iterative analyses of the transcripts throughout all stages of the data analysis process. The two authors jointly discussed the coding and subtheme results to reach consensus, and any discrepancies were resolved through AHS consultation. The code was developed deductively, based on a transcript with thematic analysis guided by Braun and Clarke [9, 10]. Data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel [11]. Microsoft Excel was used because it is widely accessible, cost-efficient, and capable of managing large datasets, making it appropriate for organizing, coding, subtheming, and identifying themes in qualitative data, particularly when the analysis follows a deductive approach with predefined themes.

Qualitative data were analyzed in Bahasa Indonesia and Javanese to preserve the subtle linguistic nuances present in participants’ accounts. The first author (YF) translated the key quotations into English, and these translations were subsequently reviewed for accuracy by the second author (IN) during manuscript preparation [42]. The final translated quotations were subsequently reviewed by the IK, EH, and AHS, who are familiar with the sociocultural context of Javanese communities. The manuscript was prepared following the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research guidelines [23]. The Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Public Health, and Nursing at Universitas Gadjah Mada approved the study protocol, with approval number KE/FK/0338/EC/2024.

Barriers to and Facilitators of Healthy Behavior Practices among Javanese Women

Category 1: Cognitive–Physical Capability

Overall, participants described various barriers and facilitators influencing their ability to engage in healthy behaviors. A central challenge is women’s physical limitations, which limit their participation in certain physical activities.

"If it’s physical activity, the most I do is walking in the morning. From the mosque, I walk around once, then I return home to continue with other activities. But I can’t run; I’ve had multiple surgeries before." (P04)

"If it’s physical activity, the most I do is walking in the morning. From the mosque, I walk around once, then I return home to continue with other activities. But I can’t run; I’ve had multiple surgeries before." (P04)

In addition to physical constraints, women reported time as a recurrent barrier, because domestic responsibilities limit opportunities for exercise.

"…mothers in the morning have many activities … so there’s no time (for exercise)." (P01)

"…mothers in the morning have many activities … so there’s no time (for exercise)." (P01)

Despite these challenges, participants also identified facilitators that enhance physical activity engagement. The increasing availability of online exercise content, primarily through YouTube, enables women to practice physical activity at home or at work.

"Exercise doesn’t have to be in person; you can do it from home via YouTube". (P09)

"Exercise doesn’t have to be in person; you can do it from home via YouTube". (P09)

"We also learn exercises from YouTube." (P01)

"We also learn exercises from YouTube." (P01)

Alongside digital resources, existing community structures, such as Family Empowerment and Welfare (PKK) gatherings, offer opportunities to detect NCD risk factors early. Cadres who measure blood pressure (BP) and collect anthropometric data help women become aware of their health status.

"Some cadres measure blood pressure and body weight during PKK gatherings." (P01)

"Some cadres measure blood pressure and body weight during PKK gatherings." (P01)

Category 2: Cognitive–Psychological Capability

Building on the capability-related factors, many participants demonstrated sound knowledge of healthy lifestyle practices, particularly in dietary choices and medication adherence.

"I eat rice in small portions. I eat more vegetables and fruits. We follow a healthy lifestyle, including eating regularly, eating fruits, eating vegetables, and exercising regularly."(P04)

"I eat rice in small portions. I eat more vegetables and fruits. We follow a healthy lifestyle, including eating regularly, eating fruits, eating vegetables, and exercising regularly."(P04)

However, psychological barriers, such as boredom and lack of motivation, hinder consistent practice of healthy behaviors.

"Sometimes when I’m lazy, there are no vegetables, so I make chicken stew with eggs." (P09)

"Sometimes when I’m lazy, there are no vegetables, so I make chicken stew with eggs." (P09)

Misconceptions also influence behavior, particularly the belief that long-term antihypertensive medication may damage the kidneys.

"Does taking high blood pressure medication continuously affect the kidneys?" (P15)

"Does taking high blood pressure medication continuously affect the kidneys?" (P15)

Although health cadres provide screening information, their limited skills in individualized counseling reduce the effectiveness of feedback for NCD (e.g., hypertension and obesity) prevention and control.

Category 3: Physical Opportunity

Beyond individual capabilities, environmental and structural conditions also shape women’s health practices. Supportive features include access to public exercise facilities, regular blood pressure and anthropometric screenings during PKK activities, and monthly community-led morning walks.

"Every first week…there is a healthy walk." (P01)

"Every first week…there is a healthy walk." (P01)

Nevertheless, insufficient time due to household responsibilities continuously restricts participation in these healthy lifestyle programs and affects adult women’s motivation to exercise.

"Sometimes it’s laziness, tiredness, or other activities, because I also cook. Sometimes I stock up; it seems like there’s no time, so I don’t exercise." (P09)

"Sometimes it’s laziness, tiredness, or other activities, because I also cook. Sometimes I stock up; it seems like there’s no time, so I don’t exercise." (P09)

Category 4: Social Opportunity

Social opportunities further facilitate or hinder the adoption of healthy behaviors. Digital communication platforms, such as WhatsApp, enable cadres and health workers to disseminate health-related information.

"It only shares information through the WhatsApp group… So, both the cadres and health workers use the WhatsApp group to share such information." (P02)

"It only shares information through the WhatsApp group… So, both the cadres and health workers use the WhatsApp group to share such information." (P02)

Family member support, especially husbands, also plays a crucial role, both emotionally and practically, in doing exercise.

"…my husband made something for lifting weights, like dumbbells…" (P01)

"…my husband made something for lifting weights, like dumbbells…" (P01)

However, low community participation in group exercise activities discourages attendance and reduces social motivation.

"not everyone, only a few people, sometimes 10 people, sometimes eight people…not many people join." (P02)

"not everyone, only a few people, sometimes 10 people, sometimes eight people…not many people join." (P02)

Additionally, some participants’ own cultural perceptions serve as barriers to practicing healthy lifestyle behaviors. A sense of “ewuh pakewuh” (awkwardness) was observed when women visit their neighbors but are reluctant to refuse the food provided by the host, even if it is objectively unhealthy.

"I broke my diet when visiting my in-laws… I was hesitant." (P02)

"I broke my diet when visiting my in-laws… I was hesitant." (P02)

Moreover, cultural influences surrounding activities such as the consumption of sweet beverages and foods that have become habitual among the community, particularly in Central Java and Yogyakarta, help provide sweet snacks and beverages at community social events.

"People from Java, we can’t drink (sweet) tea in the morning." (P07)

"People from Java, we can’t drink (sweet) tea in the morning." (P07)

Furthermore, perceptions that being overweight indicates good health discourage weight-control efforts.

"My weight, if it’s like this, I think it’s just right. If it’s less than this (weight), my cheeks look like this (pointing to her cheeks)." (P08)

"My weight, if it’s like this, I think it’s just right. If it’s less than this (weight), my cheeks look like this (pointing to her cheeks)." (P08)

While cadres indeed provide screening information, their guidance often lacks detail, partly due to insufficient training and limited collaboration with health center staff.

"There’s no training, maybe there hasn’t been (health information provided)." (P07)

"There’s no training, maybe there hasn’t been (health information provided)." (P07)

Several barriers include the lack of coordination between cadres and health center staff, insufficient support and training from health centers for cadres, erratic implementation of activities, and frequently incomplete explanations provided by cadres.

"It wasn’t like training. Just regular Q&A. Like ordinary socialization. For direct collaboration with residents. I don’t think it ever happened." (P05)

"It wasn’t like training. Just regular Q&A. Like ordinary socialization. For direct collaboration with residents. I don’t think it ever happened." (P05)

Category 5: Reflective Motivation

Participants also described reflective motivations that support lifestyle changes. Community leaders and cadres serve as role models, sharing personal experiences regarding healthy living. Women also perceive tangible benefits, such as feeling “lighter” or “fresher,” which further reinforces their motivation.

"I feel lighter, more refreshed, even though I’m still a bit chubby." (P07)

"I feel lighter, more refreshed, even though I’m still a bit chubby." (P07)

In addition, some women are motivated to adopt a healthy lifestyle because they want to live long enough to care for their children into adulthood.

"We also want to accompany our children throughout their lives. So, we must start now. What’s it called, managing lifestyle, managing diet, so the hope is to live a long life." (P02)

"We also want to accompany our children throughout their lives. So, we must start now. What’s it called, managing lifestyle, managing diet, so the hope is to live a long life." (P02)

Conversely, reliance on traditional herbal remedies as alternatives to antihypertensive medications results in hesitation toward formal treatment.

"There’s sereh, turmeric, galangal, ginger, and bay leaves… Well, if it is bay leaves… Then, it was soaked in water. Drink it about 15 min later with honey or palm sugar. It can reduce fatigue. It also helps with high blood pressure a bit." (P15).

"There’s sereh, turmeric, galangal, ginger, and bay leaves… Well, if it is bay leaves… Then, it was soaked in water. Drink it about 15 min later with honey or palm sugar. It can reduce fatigue. It also helps with high blood pressure a bit." (P15).

Fear of discovering high blood pressure readings also discourages some women from participating in screening.

"My blood pressure became 180… I couldn’t sleep… due to worry and stress." (P04)

"My blood pressure became 180… I couldn’t sleep… due to worry and stress." (P04)

Category 6: Automatic Motivation

Finally, fear of severe NCD complications automatically motivates many women to engage in preventive behaviors, including exercise and medication use.

"I’m also afraid when someone dies from diabetes, and their blood sugar goes up to 900 and remains undetected, so if a stroke occurs, we’re scared…we must be disciplined ourselves, motivate ourselves to be healthy…" (P01)

"I’m also afraid when someone dies from diabetes, and their blood sugar goes up to 900 and remains undetected, so if a stroke occurs, we’re scared…we must be disciplined ourselves, motivate ourselves to be healthy…" (P01)

Body discomfort associated with excess weight also prompts spontaneous engagement in physical activity.

"If I don’t move, it feels stiff, uncomfortable, and tiring even though I’m not doing anything. So eventually, I exercise to sweat." (P08)

"If I don’t move, it feels stiff, uncomfortable, and tiring even though I’m not doing anything. So eventually, I exercise to sweat." (P08)

Nevertheless, some women believe that the long-term use of antihypertensive medication to manage NCDs (hypertension) could result in kidney damage, which causes anxiety regarding the continuous use of antihypertensive drugs.

"I am afraid that if I take medication for my blood pressure every day, my kidneys will be damaged." (P15)

"I am afraid that if I take medication for my blood pressure every day, my kidneys will be damaged." (P15)

Discussion

This study demonstrates that healthy lifestyle behaviors among adult women are characterized by a dynamic interaction of capability, opportunity, and motivation, as conceptualized by the COM–B framework. Rather than functioning as isolated determinants, these components collectively shape Javanese adult women’s healthy lifestyle behaviors, which are influenced by sociocultural norms, structural conditions, and personal beliefs [2]. These interdependencies should be understood to design interventions that truly shift behavior, especially within culturally embedded communities, such as Javanese adult women.

The findings reveal that women generally have adequate knowledge about NCD (e.g., hypertension and obesity) prevention with healthy lifestyle behavior; however, this psychological–cognitive capability rarely transforms into sustained behavioral action. Misconceptions, such as beliefs that antihypertensive medication damages the kidneys or that being overweight signifies good health, undermine informed decision-making. These misconceptions indicate limitations in psychological capability, not merely informational gaps.

Physical capability interacts with psychological–cognitive capability. Pain, discomfort, or mobility limitations decrease women’s ability to exercise even when they are aware of its benefits. This aligns with recent studies reporting that digital and tailored physical activity interventions improve capability only when they simultaneously address pain, fatigue, and self-efficacy [1, 6]. Smartphone-based exercise videos observed in this study enhance capability by offering guided, time-flexible routines that support both physical and psychological capability domains. However, their impact is limited if embedded cultural beliefs are not addressed.

Opportunity emerges as a significant limiting factor, comprising both physical (environmental resources) and social (interpersonal influences) opportunities. Limited availability of health cadres, inconsistent screening services, and weak coordination with primary health centers result in fragmented access to preventive care. This restricts opportunities for regular monitoring, counseling, and reinforcement, thereby creating a gap between intention and action. Some obstacles encountered during community NCD risk factor screening for prevention efforts include inadequate record-keeping practices and a lack of uniform guidelines, resulting in less thorough NCD risk factor assessments [39]. Similar structural constraints have been emphasized globally, where the lack of community health workforce capacity reduces the reach of NCD prevention initiatives [26].

Social opportunity is equally influential. Javanese cultural norms, particularly “ewuh pakewuh,” create intense social pressure to accept foods served during gatherings, often sweet and high in calories. These norms contradict women’s intentions to limit sugar intake, illustrating how opportunity can override capability [34]. Conversely, family support serves as a powerful facilitator. Spousal and child encouragement reinforces lifestyle change efforts [12]. This duality influence is consistent with previous studies on COM–B, which recognize social norms as both barriers and enablers of behavior depending on context [19]. A recent behavioral nutrition study also confirms that digital health tools are practical when opportunities are enhanced by supportive social environments [7]. Where community support is weak, as in the absence of structured NCD community posts, behavioral change becomes more difficult, regardless of knowledge or motivation [40].

Drawing on Triandis’ Theory of Subjective Culture, collectivism theory, Hofstede’s power distance, and social norms theory, "ewuh pakewuh" among Javanese women reflects a culturally rooted normative belief that prioritizes social harmony, hierarchical respect, and collective identity over individual health intentions. These cultural dynamics shape health behaviors, particularly food acceptance and physical activity, by making unhealthy descriptive norms (e.g., routine consumption of high-calorie foods) and injunctive norms (e.g., accepting food to show respect) socially obligatory, especially in settings with strong collectivism and high-power distance. Evidence suggests that reducing “ewuh pakewuh” requires aligning healthy behaviors with culturally valued roles and expectations, such as framing lifestyle changes as fulfilling familial and community duties, leveraging peer-based and leader-endorsed health groups, and using dynamic descriptive and injunctive-norm feedback to signal growing community approval. When health messages are endorsed by legitimate authorities, embedded in communal activities, and delivered through egalitarian, non-threatening peer networks, they reduce social risk, preserve harmony, and render healthy practices socially legitimate and sustainable [17, 20, 25, 31, 43].

Motivation, both reflective and automatic, is considered the pivotal force that determines whether capability and opportunity are meaningfully translated into action. Positive physical sensations after exercising (feeling fitter, lighter) enhance reflective motivation, consistent with a study reporting that experiential benefits are powerful behavioral reinforcers [38]. Automatic motivation is shaped by cultural values, such as the desire to be a role model for children, a strong internalized driver rooted in Javanese familial values [36]. However, motivation can also be undermined by fears, particularly fear of medication side effects or appearing impolite in social eating contexts. These emotional reactions indicate how motivation is deeply intertwined with psychological capability and social opportunity [29].

The findings extend the COM–B framework by demonstrating that capability, opportunity, and motivation shape behaviors, the components deeply conditioned by culturally embedded norms, particularly “ewuh pakewuh,” hierarchical deference, and collective expectations, which are not fully captured in the original model. Although COM–B accounts for social opportunity, our results reveal that cultural obligations (e.g., accepting food to maintain harmony, avoiding appearing disrespectful to elders, or prioritizing domestic roles over exercise) can override individual capability and motivation even with adequate knowledge and resources, indicating that cultural norms function as supra-structural influences that shape each COM–B component simultaneously. This challenges the assumption that enhancing capability or physical opportunity alone will change behaviors, as women with sufficient knowledge, access to exercise content, or supportive environments still struggle due to culturally bound face-saving norms and fears of social judgment. Moreover, reflective and automatic motivation were intertwined with culturally informed beliefs, such as fear of discovering high blood pressure, use of herbal alternatives, perceptions of ideal body size, and misconceptions about antihypertensive medication, underscoring the need to integrate cultural cognition and normative pressures into behavioral-change designs. These findings align with recent arguments that COM–B should be expanded to incorporate cultural determinants and social hierarchy to explain health behaviors in collectivist societies better [14, 25].

Triangulation was conducted using the method and investigator triangulation. Thematic analysis was performed to systematically compare data from IDIs and FGD. Two researchers independently coded the data and subsequently discussed coding discrepancies to reach agreement. Triangulation results demonstrated strong convergence across methods for the theme of physical capability (e.g., the lack of time as a barrier for physical activity), confirming that participants consistently experienced these issues. FGD data also complemented interview findings by providing additional contextual explanations that participants felt “ewuh pakewuh” when negotiating health-related decisions within their households. Overall, triangulation strengthened the findings by showing that multiple data sources and analysts supported the key interpretations.

This study offers several novel contributions to the literature on suburban Javanese women’s health behavior and NCD prevention in Indonesia. First, it is one of the few in-depth qualitative examinations that use the complete COM–B framework to explore how cognitive, psychological, social, cultural, and structural factors simultaneously shape healthy behavior practices among Javanese women, a population often underrepresented in behavioral health research. Second, the study identifies unique cultural mechanisms, particularly the influence of “ewuh pakewuh,” a Javanese norm related to politeness, social harmony, and reluctance to refuse others, as a barrier to dietary adherence, an aspect rarely documented in previous NCD behavioral studies. Third, this study identifies a new pattern of digital adaptation, in which women increasingly use YouTube and social media as alternative modes of physical activity in response to time constraints and limited community exercise opportunities. Fourth, the study highlights previously underreported system-level gaps, including limited counseling competencies among cadres, inconsistent coordination between cadres and public health centers, and incomplete feedback loops during community NCD screening. Finally, the findings reveal emotion-driven behavioral mechanisms, such as fear of high blood pressure readings, anxiety about long-term antihypertensive medication, and automatic motivation triggered by discomfort from being overweight, which have not been comprehensively described in this cultural context. Together, these insights generate a richer, culturally grounded understanding of Javanese women’s healthy lifestyle behaviors and provide novel pathways for designing more contextually responsive interventions.

Implications for Policy Programs

Based on the integrated COM–B interpretation, effective interventions must be multilevel and culturally grounded. It is essential to establish capability by correcting misconceptions about a true healthy lifestyle and NCD caused by lifestyle, using culturally adapted education. We can also use digital tools (videos, apps) that build practical skills and accommodate physical limitations for Javanese adult women who cannot go anywhere to do exercise/physical activity because of their condition.

Strengthening community health cadres’ systems through training, monitoring, supervision, and better coordination between public health center workers is very important to enhance social opportunity, by increasing health cadres’ knowledge and skills so they can help health workers provide education on a healthy lifestyle to Javanese adult women in the community; creating supportive social norms by involving families, community leaders, and local organizations to develop a culturally sensitive health promotion program that suits the regional context; and establishing structured community posts on NCD prevention to increase information dissemination and screen for NCDs (hypertension and obesity) to raise community awareness as part of the public health center and Indonesian National Health Department program (free health check).

Narrative-based counseling for health workers that resonates with cultural values (e.g., being role models for children) is essential to strengthen motivation. Also, health workers can emphasize immediate benefits (feeling fit, reduced symptoms) to reinforce experiences. Health workers and the community can also develop a health promotion intervention model that provides continuous feedback loops to sustain community engagement in healthy lifestyle practices.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

The strength of this study lies in its exploration of Javanese women’s perceptions of healthy lifestyles. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to analyze these perceptions using the COM–B behavioral theory framework. To support the study’s credibility, multiple data collection methods were used, including IDIs and FGD. Data collection involved community groups, health cadres, and community leaders, with consideration of sociodemographic characteristics to ensure data variation and research credibility. Validation was achieved by reconfirming the information participants provided during data collection.

Some study limitations include the limited number of participants in the data collection, which limits the generalizability of the results to a broader population, as this study covers only the suburban areas of Yogyakarta. Another limitation is that participants were from the same community and that FGD was performed in group settings; thus, responses may have been influenced by social desirability bias. Additionally, although quotations were translated and cross-checked, translation or interpretation bias may slightly affect the nuances of participants’ expressions. As most participants had middle-to-higher–level educational backgrounds, this may limit the generalizability of these findings to individuals with lower educational attainment (junior high school or below). Despite these limitations, the findings offer essential culturally informed insights to guide intervention and policy development for NCD prevention among Javanese women.

Conclusion

This study highlights the complex interplay of cognitive, psychological, social, cultural, and structural factors that shape healthy behavior practices among Javanese women. Time constraints due to domestic responsibilities, cultural norms such as “ewuh pakewuh,” misconceptions about antihypertensive medications, and inconsistent cadre support remain key barriers. Conversely, digital access to exercise content, existing community structures, and strong family support promote healthier practices. Women’s aspirations to maintain their families’ well-being further reinforce their motivation for change.

Priority directions moving forward include incorporating cultural constructs into behavioral-change strategies, strengthening cadre training and health system coordination, designing gender-responsive digital and home-based interventions that accommodate women’s time burdens, and developing family-inclusive community programs. Policy efforts should reinforce culturally competent NCD prevention within village-level health promotion systems. Future research should evaluate culturally grounded behavioral models and scalable community interventions tailored to women’s lived realities.
