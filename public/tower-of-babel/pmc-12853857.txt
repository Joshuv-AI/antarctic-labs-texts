Prevalence of smoking, occupational stress level, and associated factors among nursing faculty members in Palestine: a multicenter nationwide survey
Authors: Ashraf Jehad Abuejheisheh, Rabia H Haddad, Fatma Kamal Ali, Ibrahim Ghannam, Farid Ghrayed, Rania Abu Seir, Adham Basalat, Ayat Joulani, Ahmad Aladdin, Basil Husary, Mohamed Amera, Mohamed Atef, Qusay Nasser, Sabreen Matani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmoking is a significant global health issue, causing millions of deaths annually and contributing to severe illnesses like lung cancer, coronary heart disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). In addition, Tobacco use continues to pose a significant global health issue, especially alarming when it is widespread among healthcare professionals who are expected to act as role models.AimThis study examined the prevalence and associated factors of smoking and occupational stress levels among nursing faculty members in Palestinian universities and colleges.Design and methodsA quantitative, cross-sectional design was used. The data were collected from 164 participants from 17 educational institutions through a self-administered questionnaire that included smoking practices and the occupational stress domain from the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scales (DASS-21).ResultsFindings indicated a smoking prevalence of 37.2%, with cigarettes being the most commonly used tobacco product. Key factors influencing smoking behavior included peer influence, stress relief, and cultural norms. Gender and marital status were also significant, with females and single participants demonstrating higher smoking rates. In terms of occupational stress level among participants, nearly half (47%) experienced moderate to extremely severe levels of stress. The results showed a moderate negative correlation between occupational stress and age (r = -0.275, p < 0.001). Also, smoking status was significantly associated with occupational stress levels (p-value = 0.027), with current smokers reporting higher occupational stress levels than non-smokers and former smokers.ConclusionThis study offers critical insights into the high prevalence of smoking among Palestinian nursing faculty, occupational stress, socio-cultural norms, and limited smoking cessation resources that contribute to high smoking prevalence among nursing faculty. Targeted interventions, including culturally tailored smoking cessation programs and institutional tobacco-free policies, are recommended to promote healthier behaviors. These findings are crucial for shaping public health strategies and for improving educators’ role modeling of health behaviors in the healthcare sector.Keywords: Smoking, Occupational stress level, Palestine, Nursing faculty members, Cultural norms, Tobacco use

Introduction

Worldwide, tobacco use is a major contributor to morbidity and mortality, posing a persistent public health risk. Smoking is responsible for approximately 6 million deaths annually, underscoring its impact on global health [1]. Smoking is associated with numerous health symptoms and illnesses, including chronic cough, shortness of breath, and cardiovascular complications, which can progress to severe conditions like lung cancer, coronary heart disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [2, 3].

Healthcare professionals, including nursing faculty, are particularly vulnerable to the adverse health effects of smoking due to high-stress work environments. Their role as health advocates and educators makes the smoking rates among them especially concerning, as their smoking habits not only impact their health but can also compromise their ability to promote healthy behaviors effectively [4]. Nursing faculty members who smoke may experience chronic respiratory and cardiovascular symptoms, which can impair their job performance and may reinforce smoking behaviors as a coping mechanism for occupational stress [5]. For Palestinian nursing faculty, this issue is further compounded by socio-political and economic challenges and limited access to smoking cessation programs, highlighting the need for targeted interventions [6]. Despite the critical role that nursing faculty members play in fostering healthy lifestyles, the smoking prevalence among nursing faculty members remains a source of concern. Research suggests that healthcare professionals, especially educators, are more likely to smoke due to factors such as occupational stress, workplace culture, and sociodemographic features [3, 7].

Studies show that 34.5% of Palestinian healthcare workers smoke, with a higher prevalence observed among men and those with a family history of smoking [8]. This elevated rate suggests that occupational stress and other related factors significantly influence smoking behaviors among healthcare professionals in Palestine [9]. For instance, occupational stress, driven by long hours, heavy workloads, and high levels of responsibility, is a well-documented contributor to smoking among healthcare professionals [7]. For nursing faculty, smoking often serves as a coping strategy to mitigate the physical and mental toll of work-related stress. This occupational stress-induced smoking not only poses a personal health risk but may also compromise the credibility of educators in their roles as health promoters.

Moreover, the smoking rates among healthcare workers, especially in Palestine, may be influenced by various social, cultural, and occupational factors. Economic pressures, social norms, and cultural attitudes can increase the difficulty of quitting, even for those in health professions [8, 10]. The demanding nature of healthcare work, with its teaching, patient care, and administrative responsibilities in a unique socio-economic environment in Palestine, can heighten occupational stress levels and contribute to smoking rates among faculty members [7]. However, there is a knowledge gap in Palestine on how smoking behaviors affect health promotion programs and nursing faculty’s role-modeling duties due to a lack of studies on the prevalence and contributing causes of smoking among this demographic [8]. To aid efforts to develop healthier learning environments in Palestinian healthcare institutions, the prevalence and contributing factors of smoking among this population must be researched [2, 10].

Addressing smoking among Palestinian nursing faculty is critical for promoting health both within the healthcare system and in educational settings, thus eliminating smoking, improving healthcare education, and promoting public health. Research highlights that healthcare professionals who smoke may struggle to effectively encourage patients and students to quit, thus compromising their role in health promotion [2]. Targeted, locally tailored interventions are necessary to reduce smoking rates among this demographic, supporting nursing faculty in adopting healthier lifestyles and fostering a health-conscious culture that benefits both educators and students.

Finally, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to examine baseline data on smoking behaviors among Palestinian nursing faculty members and link variables of smoking, occupational stress level, and associated factors. The findings will be crucial in formulating public health policies, highlighting the importance of customized support networks and cessation programs for medical workers. Understanding nursing faculty’s smoking habits will also help establish health-conscious learning settings and promote institutional reforms that enhance nursing faculty members’ role as role models for healthy living.

The conceptual framework guided this study was based on the Transactional model of occupational stress and coping [11]. Smoking among nursing faculty members is an initial behavioral coping response; they adopt to reduce occupational stress. Based on this model, the nursing faculty members work under multi-tasks and workload pressure may lead to adopting smoking behaviors as an occupational stress-reliever strategy [12].

The purpose of this study was to identify the smoking prevalence and risk factors among Palestine’s nursing faculty, with a focus on analyzing the main behavioral, occupational, and sociodemographic traits that influence smoking in this group. The specific aims were:

Methods

Design

This study employed a cross-sectional methodology to examine the prevalence of smoking and other characteristics (e.g., sociodemographic and factors related to smoking such as illness, number of cigarettes per day, and type of smoking…etc) among Palestine’s nursing faculty members. A cross-sectional survey collects data from participants at a single point, allowing for a basic study of the population’s prevalence and relationships across variables [13]. Data were collected between December 1, 2024, to January 31, 2025.

Setting

Data collected from nursing faculty members of 17 health educational institutions in the West Bank, including: Al-Quds University, An-Najah National University, Birzeit University, Palestine Polytechnic University, Hebron University, Bethlehem University, Arab American University (AAUP), Al-Istiqlal University, Modern University College, Makassed Islamic Charitable Society Hospital School of Nursing, Ibn Sina College of Health Sciences, Al Andaleeb College, Al Rawda College, The Women’s Community College in Ramallah - Al-Tira, Hebron Nursing College, Al-Rayyhan Campus at Arab American University (AAUP).

Population

The target population of this study was 175 nursing faculty members in West Bank universities and colleges. The significance of nursing faculty members as health advocates and instructors to aspiring nurses is the reason behind the selection of this demographic. Each full-time and part-time faculty member employed in these regions at the time of data collection was included in the projected number of eligible participants.

Sample and sampling

The sample size for this study was 164 nursing faculty members, based on the number of nursing faculty members at each university and college. A convenience sample technique was used. With a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error, this method allowed for a balanced representation among institutions.

Data collection procedure

After obtaining all ethical approvals from all universities and colleges. The data collection process was carried out in a structured manner to ensure a smooth, ethical, and reliable gathering of information. Below are the detailed steps followed during the collection of data:

Distribution method

To suit participants’ interests and ensure convenience, the questionnaire was provided exclusively electronically. The electronic mode was used to allow participants to complete the questionnaire at their own pace and from any location. The online version of the questionnaire was sent via a secure platform, such as Google Forms, to ensure that replies were anonymous and entered directly into a digital database. The use of electronic distribution also helps to ensure secrecy and minimize logistical constraints, like as travel or scheduling issues for participants, as we are still in danger due to the war that started after October 7, 2023, here in the West Bank.

Informed consent and confidentiality

Each participant received a comprehensive permission document alongside the questionnaire. This document clearly outlined the study’s purpose, procedures, and the voluntary nature of participation. It emphasized that participants had the right to withdraw at any time without any consequences. Informed consent was granted from each participant, as at the beginning of the questionnaire, there was a clear statement that informed the participants they were agreeing to participate in this study if they filled out the questionnaire. To ensure ethical standards, the document also detailed the steps taken to maintain confidentiality. All personal data and responses were kept secure, with no identifying information linked to the collected data. Electronic submissions were encrypted to prevent unauthorized access.

Questionnaire completion and guidance

Upon receiving the questionnaire, participants were instructed on how to fill it out. If any participants had questions or needed clarification, assistance was provided, either in person or via email, depending on the distribution method. For electronic responses, an email address was provided for technical support and additional guidance. This ensured that all participants, regardless of their familiarity with the technology, felt comfortable completing the questionnaire accurately.

Follow-up and reminders

To improve response rates and ensure a representative sample, follow-up reminders were sent out after one week of the initial questionnaire distribution. The reminders were sent via email for those who completed the electronic version, while phone calls or text messages were used for in-person participants who provided their contact details. The reminder emphasized the importance of their participation and reassured them that their responses would remain confidential.

Data handling and storage

Once collected, the data were entered into a secure database for analysis. The electronic responses, the platform used for survey distribution, automatically compiled the responses, and the data was stored in an encrypted, password-protected system to prevent unauthorized access.

Instruments

We used three main sections of instruments. The first two were to examine the prevalence of smoking and associated factors among nursing faculty members in Palestine. These two sections were developed by the authors based on the literature, and the questions were presented to a panel of two experts with PhD in Nursing and Public Health for content and face validity. The third instrument was the occupational stress level based on the universal Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scales (DASS-21). We used the stress subscale only from the DASS-21.

In a study conducted at Arsi University, the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales (DASS-21) were used to evaluate mental health symptoms among medical students, demonstrating strong internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha values above 0.7 for each subscale. The construct validity was supported by the correlation of the DASS-21 with substance use behaviors, such as smoking, and by the association with levels of depression, anxiety, and stress among students. Ethical considerations

Before beginning the study, we acquired ethical approval from the Research Ethics Sub-Committee at Al-Quds University (AQU) under the ID number (RESC/2025-3). The current study was also ethically approved by Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) at all of the partnering universities. Participants were informed about the study’s details and requested to provide informed consent, and they provided consent to participate in this study. Participation was fully voluntary, and replies were kept strictly confidential. Identifiable information was removed, and data were anonymized to safeguard participants’ identities during analysis.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software version 27.0 IBM, applying descriptive statistics including frequencies and percentages. In addition, inferential statistics include Cronbach’s alpha, Pearson’s correlation coefficient, chi-square, independent sample t-test, and one-way ANOVA.

Differences in smoking status with demographic variables

Differences in smoking behaviors were observed across demographic variables. A chi-square test revealed that females were significantly more likely to smoke than males (p < 0.001). The chi-square test also demonstrated a significant association between marital status and smoking behavior, with single individuals more likely to smoke compared to their married counterparts (df = 2, p < 0.001).

In contrast, no significant associations were found between smoking behaviors and other factors, such as educational level (p = 0.529), year of experience (p = 0.572), or workplace tobacco culture (p = 0.178). Similarly, there was no significant relationship between occupational stress levels and health-related symptoms or conditions, including asthma, diabetes, or hypertension (p = 0.289).

For age, a one-way ANOVA was used to assess differences between groups. The results showed a statistically significant difference in occupational stress levels (p-value = 0.014), η² = 0.06 indicating that age plays a role in occupational stress, with younger participants (20–30 years) reporting higher occupational stress levels compared to those aged 51 and above, who experienced the lowest occupational stress levels.

Marital status also showed a significant difference in occupational stress levels (p-value = 0.005), with divorced participants reporting much higher occupational stress levels than both single and married participants.

Regarding educational level, the one-way ANOVA indicated no significant difference in occupational stress levels between participants with different academic backgrounds (p-value = 0.370), suggesting that education does not affect occupational stress levels significantly. Years of experience, the results from the one-way ANOVA (p-value = 0.122) also showed no significant association, indicating that work experience does not influence occupational stress levels in this study.

In terms of employee status, there was no significant difference in occupational stress levels based on whether participants were full-time, part-time, or clinical instructors, as indicated by the p-value of 0.372. Income level did not significantly affect occupational stress levels, with a p-value of 0.153, as per the results from the one-way ANOVA. Finally, smoking status was significantly associated with occupational stress levels (p-value = 0.027), with current smokers reporting higher occupational stress levels than non-smokers and former smokers.

A bivariate Pearson correlation was conducted to examine the relationship between age and occupational stress Level. The results showed a moderate negative correlation (r = −0.275, p < 0.001).

Overall, the findings highlight that age, marital status, and smoking status are significant factors influencing occupational stress levels, while sex, educational level, years of experience, employee status, and income level do not appear to have a significant impact.

Discussion

This study explored the prevalence of smoking and associated factors among nursing faculty members in Palestinian universities and colleges. The results revealed that 37.2% of participants are current smokers, with cigarettes being the most commonly used tobacco product (24.6%). Smoking was influenced by factors such as peer pressure, stress relief, and familial or cultural norms. Occupational stress and limited cessation resources were also significant contributors. These findings align with global and regional trends, underscoring the complexity of smoking behaviors among healthcare professionals.

Prevalence and behaviors

The prevalence of smoking among nursing faculty members in this study (37.2%) is considerably higher than the global average for healthcare professionals, as observed in studies like Shahbazi et al. (2014), which reported a 9% prevalence among healthcare workers in Texas [7]. This disparity might be attributable to the socio-political and economic pressures in Palestine, as noted by Abu-Rmeileh et al. (2022), who highlighted the role of occupational stress and cultural factors in shaping smoking behaviors among Palestinian healthcare professionals [15]. Similarly, Zinonos et al. (2016) reported a 28.2% smoking prevalence among healthcare providers in Cyprus, emphasizing gender, marital status, and occupational stress as significant factors [2].

In contrast, a study by Allen et al. (2016) in the UK found a lower smoking prevalence (12.3%) among healthcare professionals, but it noted that socio-economic factors, such as lower income and education, were more likely to increase smoking rates [16]. In Palestine, the economic constraints may influence people to smoke more, as shown in the results of this study, where nursing faculty members have a relatively high prevalence rate. This study highlighted the impact of workplace health programs and anti-smoking initiatives, which were found to be more prevalent in the UK, a factor that could explain the lower smoking rate compared to Palestine.

Additionally, a study by Nilan et al. (2019) in Australia found a smoking prevalence of 21.5% among healthcare workers [17]. This study also pointed out the influence of workplace culture, where smoking was more common among staff in departments with higher levels of burnout and job dissatisfaction. These findings are consistent with the stress-related factors identified in this study, where occupational stress and job dissatisfaction were key contributors to smoking behaviors.

Influencing factors

The results of this study were consistent with studies by Taheri et al. (2015) [18] and Khubaib et al. (2016) [6]. Peer influence and occupational stress were found to be primary motivators for smoking. However, this study uniquely highlights the gendered societal norms in Palestine, where smoking is more socially accepted among men but stigmatized for women. This aligns with findings from Al-Kaabba et al. (2011) in Saudi Arabia, where cultural and familial influences heavily impacted smoking behaviors [10]. The link between occupational stress and smoking, observed in this study, echoes findings from Laroussy et al., who noted occupational stress as a common factor among healthcare students transitioning into smoking [19].

In addition, a study by Perdikaris et al. (2010) observed that social support was a mitigating factor in smoking behaviors among healthcare workers, with those receiving more positive reinforcement from colleagues and family members being less likely to smoke. This contrasts with the peer pressure effects found in this study but provides an interesting perspective on how different types of social influence may play contrasting roles in shaping smoking behaviors [9].

Furthermore, a study by Alkhatatbeh et al. in Jordan found that cultural and familial pressures were significant drivers of smoking among healthcare workers [20]. In addition, another study in Jordan using A Grounded Theory Study indicated that constant comparative analysis revealed that the progression to heavy smoking is a psychosocial process shaped by four key themes: the acceptance of smoking as a normal behavior, navigating uncertainty, dealing with conflicts in the workplace, and confronting occupational stress. The study specifically highlighted that, in Arab cultures, smoking is often used as a socializing tool, especially among men [21]. This finding further supports the notion that smoking is deeply ingrained in the cultural context in Palestine, where social norms influence smoking behaviors.

Occupational stress and coping mechanisms

The DASS-21 assessment revealed that nearly half of the participants experienced moderate to severe occupational stress levels, correlating with higher smoking rates. This aligns with findings by Babapour et al. (2022), which demonstrated that occupational stress significantly impacts smoking behaviors among nurses [22]. Younger faculty members (20–30 years) reported higher occupational stress levels, corroborating Martínez et al., who emphasized early career stress as a critical period for smoking initiation [23].

Additionally, a study by Perdikaris et al. (2010) found a strong correlation between occupational stress and smoking among healthcare workers, particularly those in high-pressure specialties such as emergency medicine [9]. The study noted that coping mechanisms like smoking were often used to deal with occupational stress, similar to the findings in this study. However, unlike the Palestinian context, the Indian study also highlighted the role of organizational support, such as counseling services and occupational stress management workshops, in reducing smoking rates. Moreover, a study conducted among 302 healthcare professionals in Pakistan revealed a strong association between higher smoking rates and perceived level of occupational stress [24]. The results of this study and the previous studies ensure the association between occupational stress and smoking status. This emphasizes integrating strategies to reduce the occupational stress level, which may diminish or cease smoking among healthcare professionals, including nursing faculty members. For instance, physical exercises have been proven to decrease occupational stress levels and prevalence of smoking among healthcare professionals [24].

Another study by Lee et al. (2021) in South Korea reported that occupational stress management programs significantly reduced smoking rates among nurses [25]. This study pointed to the effectiveness of workplace interventions, such as mindfulness training and peer support networks, in mitigating occupational stress and reducing smoking behavior. The findings in this study support the idea that workplace interventions can play a crucial role in addressing smoking behaviors, which is an area that could be explored further in Palestine.

Conclusion

This study provides critical insights into the high prevalence of smoking among Palestinian nursing faculty, driven by occupational stress, cultural norms, and workplace challenges. Addressing these factors through tailored interventions and policy reforms can not only reduce smoking rates but also enhance the role of nursing faculty as health role models. Future efforts should prioritize culturally sensitive, gender-specific approaches to ensure sustainable health promotion in academic and healthcare settings.
