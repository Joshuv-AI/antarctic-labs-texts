TCR-JANUS engager proteins enable bispecific targeting to overcome tumor heterogeneity in TCR-T cell therapy
Authors: Chenxi Cheng, Ting Liang, Pengju Yao, Wenling Wang, Huxuan Chen, Xiaowen Li, Ying Tian, Jie Wang, Ni Yan, Danli Xie, Fuming Qiu, Shuguang Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
T cell receptor (TCR)–based immunotherapy is limited by tumor antigen heterogeneity, which frequently leads to relapse. We developed a bispecific TCR-JANUS engager that functions synergistically with TCR engineered T cells (TCR-T). In contrast to conventional T cell engagers targeting CD3, TCR-JANUS engages both the variable region of a transgenic TCR (TRBV) and a tumor surface antigen. Using a model system targeting a humanized KRAS-G12V–specific TCR and trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (Trop2), we show that TCR-JANUS effectively redirects TCR-T cells to lyse Trop2-expressing tumor cells while preserving intrinsic specificity toward the cognate pHLA target, thereby enabling simultaneous dual-antigen recognition. In heterogeneous tumor models, the combination of TCR-JANUS with TCR-T cells, an integrated system termed T Cell Dual Arsenal Recon, potently suppressed tumor growth by clearing antigenically divergent populations. Moreover, TCR-JANUS maintained enhanced T cell functionality with reduced exhaustion compared to anti-CD3–based engagers upon chronic stimulation. Together, this approach offers a targeted and durable strategy to overcome antigenic heterogeneity, expanding the clinical prospects of TCR-T cell therapy for solid tumors.

INTRODUCTION

T cell receptor (TCR)–targeting immunotherapy has demonstrated promising clinical potential for solid tumors since the approval of KIMMTRAK (targeting gp100) for uveal melanoma and Afami-cel (targeting MAGE-A4) for synovial sarcoma. Nevertheless, TCR-engineered T cell (TCR-T cell) therapy faces persistent barriers to achieving durable complete remissions, including tumor heterogeneity, limited intratumoral infiltration, and immunosuppressive microenvironments (1). Among these, tumor antigen heterogeneity represents a primary driver of adaptive resistance and relapse following TCR-T cell therapy (2).

Tumor antigens comprise both intracellular and extracellular targets. Conventional antibody-based cellular therapies [e.g., chimeric antigenic receptor T cells (CAR-T cells)] are restricted to surface antigens, whereas TCR-T cells specifically recognize both extracellular and intracellular antigens presented by major histocompatibility complex [MHC; human lymphocyte antigen (HLA) in humans] molecules, enabling broader antigen targeting (1, 3). Shared neoantigens from oncogenic driver mutations, such as recurrent KRAS mutations in non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), colorectal cancer, and pancreatic cancer, represent prime targets for TCR-T cell therapy. We and others have developed TCR-T cells specific for KRAS-G12 mutant neoantigens, demonstrating promising therapeutic efficacy (NCT05933668) (4, 5). Beyond KRAS mutation, surface antigens such as trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (Trop2) and folate receptor α (FRα) are highly expressed in multiple tumors, with multiple antibody-based therapeutics currently under clinical investigation (6, 7). Notably, adoptively transferred TCR-T cells persist long-term in vivo, sustaining antitumor immunity (8). Thus, leveraging this persistence to simultaneously target intracellular neoantigens (via TCR) and extracellular antigens (via engager molecules) presents a strategic approach to overcoming tumor heterogeneity.

Strategies targeting dual antigens to overcome tumor heterogeneity in immunotherapy have been reported, primarily using dual-targeting CAR-T cells or T cell engagers (TCEs). CAR-T cells engineered to cotarget two tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) with optimized costimulatory domains demonstrated improved antitumor activity and reduced tumor escape mediated by heterogeneity (9, 10). Notably, engineering T cells to coexpress a CAR and a transgenic TCR—combining both CAR and TCR technologies—was shown to enhance and prolong antitumor activity compared to mixed CAR-T/TCR-T cell populations (11). However, most prevalent tumor surface antigens such as Trop2, DLL-3, FRα, and claudin 18.2 belong to TAAs, which are also expressed at low levels in normal human tissues. This inherent property inevitably leads to severe on-target off-tumor toxicities when adopting persistently functional CAR-T cell therapy (12). In contrast, protein-based TCEs emerge as a safer and more advantageous therapeutic modality. Because of their rapid in vivo clearance rate and flexible, precise dosage regulation, TCEs effectively mitigate off-tumor adverse effects, rendering them a more clinically viable strategy for targeted intervention against these widely expressed TAAs (13, 14).

The selection of targeting domains in TCR/CD3 complex is crucial for the design of TCR-T cell engager proteins, which could specifically recruit and activate TCR-T cells to target an additional tumor antigen for heterogeneous tumors (12). The TCR/CD3 complex of αβ T cell consists of the TCRαβ and the CD3 complex, which is composed of γ, δ, ε, and ζ subunits (15). The tumor-targeting TCEs were usually designed to target the CD3ε subunit to engage any type of T cells with tumor cells and subsequently enable the formation of immunological synapses to stimulate T cells for tumor cell cytotoxicity (13). However, conventional TCEs designed to target CD3ε subunit do not discriminate subpopulations of T cells, and the resultant systemic inflammatory toxicity remains a major disadvantage for engager protein design (16). The TCR variable domain specifically marks a small population of T cells, and targeting the variable domain of TCR would substantially improve the targeting specificity to the adoptively transferred TCR-T cells, although small populations (<5%) of nonspecific T cell subpopulation would also be targeted. We therefore propose “TCR engager” for these bispecific engager proteins composing of an anti-TCR β chain variable-domain single-chain variable fragment (scFv) of specific monoclonal antibody (specific to TCR-T cells) and an antitumor surface antigen scFv. The TCR engagers would redirect persisting TCR-T cells to engage with tumor surface antigen while preserving their intrinsic specificity for pHLA antigens. However, whether the TCR engager proteins would induce T cell activation against heterogenous tumor cells and retain intrinsic pMHC-mediated T cell responses remains unknown.

In this study, we engineered a bispecific TCR engager protein targeting TRBV6 of a humanized KRAS-G12V–specific TCR and the tumor surface antigen Trop2. This molecule mediates dual recognition: It engages surface-expressed Trop2 while enabling indirect targeting of intracellular KRAS-G12V via TCR engagement. We designated this type of engager TCR-JANUS, reflecting its dual antigen-binding capability. TCR-JANUS induces potent activation of TCR-T cells upon binding surface antigen, such as Trop2, on tumor cells. When combined with TCR-T cell therapy, TCR-JANUS acts synergistically to overcome tumor heterogeneity, forming an integrated T-DART (T Cell Dual Arsenal Recon) system. Crucially, unlike conventional anti-CD3–based engagers, TCR-JANUS sustains more durable TCR-T cell activity with reduced exhaustion. This combination strategy—TCR-JANUS with TCR-T cells—holds substantial promise to effectively mitigate tumor heterogeneity and broaden the clinical utility of TCR-T cell therapy.

RESULTS

Anti-Trop2 TCR-JANUS engager protein mediates antitumor immunity in combination with TCR-T cells

The anti-Trop2 TCR-JANUS engager protein, termed α-Trop2-V6 to denote its specificity, was designed by fusing an anti-Trop2 scFv to an anti-TRBV6 scFv. This engager protein specifically recruits TCR-T cells expressing the TRBV6 β chain variable domain (via the anti-TRBV6 scFv) to mediate cytotoxicity against Trop2-positive target cells (via the anti-Trop2 scFv) (fig. S1A). Furthermore, α-Trop2-V6 preserves the pHLA engagement capacity of the coengaged TCR. Therefore, when combined with h1-2C TCR-T cells, which express a humanized KRAS-G12V–specific h1-2C TCR using the TRBV6-4 subtype, α-Trop2-V6 enables dual targeting of both Trop2-positive tumor cells and KRAS-G12V neoantigen–positive tumors (Fig. 1A) (4).

The α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins were expressed with human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293F cells and purified by affinity chromatography and gel filtration chromatography (fig. S1B). The conformational stability of the α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins was evaluated using nano differential scanning fluorimetry (NanoDSF) through thermal denaturation (fig. S1C). The analysis demonstrated a single unfolding peak at a Tm of 63.1°C, suggesting the high thermal stability of the engager proteins. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) binding analyses revealed that α-Trop2-V6 binds to the h1-2C TCR with a KD of 318 nM, while exhibiting a substantial higher affinity (KD = 4.14 nM) for Trop2 (Fig. 1B). This affinity difference is expected to promote preferential accumulation of α-Trop2-V6 within the tumor microenvironment. Cell-based binding assays confirmed that α-Trop2-V6 binds substantially to tumor cells or HEK-293T cells overexpressing either h1-2C TCR or Trop2 (Fig. 1, C and D, and fig. S1, D to F). To further determine whether α-Trop2-V6 engagers could mediate adhesion between T cells and target cells, we performed cell-based avidity assays using z-Movi technology (14). In this setup, DLD1 target cells were precoated on microfluidic chips (Fig. 1E). The chips were then incubated with both T cells and α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins. An acoustic force ranging from 0 to 1000 pN was gradually applied to the system, and T cell detachment from target cells was recorded as the force increased. The results demonstrated that T cells remained bound to the target cells in the presence of the α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins were substantially higher than control proteins (Fig. 1F). The T cell activation potential of α-Trop2-V6 was assessed by coculturing h1-2C TCR-T cells with various target cells in its presence (fig. S1, E to G). h1-2C TCR-T cells efficiently recognized and killed HLA-A*11 and KRAS-G12V–positive tumor cells (SW620-A11 and CFPAC1-A11) and induced substantial interferon-γ (IFN-γ) production (fig. S2, A and B). IFN-γ enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISPOT) and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assays demonstrated substantial induction of IFN-γ–secreting T cells and elevated IFN-γ production against DLD1 tumor cells (endogenously Trop2-positive) upon stimulation with α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins (fig. S2, C and D). Notably, the responses against SW620-A11 cells (endogenously KRAS-G12V–positive) were unaffected by α-Trop2-V6, confirming the preserved specific reactivity of the h1-2C TCR-T cells against the KRAS-G12V neoantigen. Intracellular staining for IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α) revealed specific T cell activation by α-Trop2-V6 against Trop2-positive DLD1 cells in both CD4+ and CD8+ h1-2C TCR-T cell subsets (Fig. 1, G and H, and fig. S2, E and F). In contrast, only CD8+ TCR-T cells responded to KRAS-G12V–positive SW620-A11 target cells. Dose-response analyses showed that IFN-γ production by h1-2C TCR-T cells against DLD1 cells increased in a dose-dependent manner with α-Trop2-V6 concentration (Fig. 1I). Conversely, responses against SW620-A11 cells showed no substantial difference across α-Trop2-V6 concentrations (Fig. 1I). Luciferase-based cytotoxicity assays demonstrated substantial killing of Trop2-positive DLD1 target cells mediated by α-Trop2-V6 (Fig. 1J). The h1-2C TCR-T cells retained their intrinsic cytotoxicity against KRAS-G12V–positive SW620-A11 target cells in the presence of the engager proteins. Collectively, these results indicate that the α-Trop2-V6 engager protein effectively mediates specific immune responses against Trop2-positive tumor cells while preserving the inherent responsiveness of h1-2C TCR-T cells against KRAS-G12V neoantigen–positive tumor cells.

The in vivo tumor suppression efficacy of α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins was evaluated using nonobese diabetic (NOD)–Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/NjuCrl (NCG) mice subcutaneously engrafted with Trop2-positive DLD1 tumor cells stably expressing luciferase. On day 4 postengraftment, h1-2C TCR-T cells (2 × 107) were intravenously infused into tumor-bearing NCG mice. Six intravenous doses of α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins were administered, and tumor burden was subsequently monitored (Fig. 2A). Mock T cells (lacking h1-2C TCR transduction) served as a negative control to exclude background anti-TRBV6 reactivity from endogenous TCRs. α-Trop2-V6 engager protein treatment induced substantial tumor suppression (Fig. 2B). Both luciferase intensity and tumor volumes in the α-Trop2-V6 engager–treated group receiving TCR-T cells were significantly reduced compared to control groups (Fig. 2, C to F). At the experimental end point, tumors are dissected from the tumor-bearing mice, followed by photographic documentation and weighing of the tumors. The results demonstrated that the tumor burden in α-Trop2-V6 engager–treated group receiving TCR-T cells was substantially inhibited in comparison with control groups (Fig. 2G and fig. S3, A and B). Although there are small TRBV6-positive population in mock T cells, no substantial antitumor reactivity was observed (Fig. 2G). This also indicates the necessity to combine with TRBV6 TCR-T cells for α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins to induce antitumor efficacy. These results demonstrate that α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins effectively suppress tumor growth when combined with TCR-T cells.

Anti-Trop2 TCR-JANUS proteins induce sustained antitumor efficacy

While substantial tumor suppression by TCR-JANUS likely requires multiple administrations, prolonged exposure to TCE proteins can induce T cell exhaustion and diminish functional potency, as observed with anti-CD3/anti-CD19 bispecific antibodies (17). Therefore, we compared the functional profiles of TCR-T cells exposed to α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins versus Trop2-specific anti-CD3 (α-Trop2-CD3) engager proteins (Fig. 3A). The α-Trop2-CD3 engager proteins demonstrated substantial binding to CD3 and Trop2 proteins (fig. S4, A to C).

Following 4 days of coculture with three doses of α-Trop2-V6 engager protein, TCR-T cells exhibited enhanced proliferation compared to those cultured with α-Trop2-CD3 engagers (Fig. 3B). In addition, α-Trop2-CD3 engagers reduced CD3 expression on T cells (Fig. 3C) and diminished expression of the transduced TCR, as detected by pHLA tetramer staining (Fig. 3, D and E). Analysis of T cell differentiation subtypes revealed that α-Trop2-V6 engager protein increased the population of central memory T cells (TCM; CD45RO+/CD62L+) relative to α-Trop2-CD3 engagers (Fig. 3, F and G). Central memory subsets in CAR-T cell therapy are multipotent, capable of generating diverse effector and memory populations, and confer long-term persistence and immunologic memory upon adoptive transfer (18, 19). Thus, the enrichment of TCM subsets suggests the potential for prolonged efficacy and potent antitumor immunity within the context of immune reconstitution in TCR-JANUS-coupled T-DART therapy.

To assess the functional impact of prolonged engager exposure on T cell cytotoxicity, we further evaluated T cell potency of the TCR-T cells. The results showed that TCR-T cells cocultured with α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins preserved cytotoxicity against Trop2-positive DLD1 target cells comparable to controls, whereas cytotoxicity was substantially reduced with TCR-T cells exposed to α-Trop2-CD3 engagers (Fig. 3H). Similarly, cytotoxicity against SW620-A11 cells was significantly diminished after coculture with anti-Trop2-CD3 engager protein (Fig. 3I). The production of IFN-γ of the h1-2C TCR-T cells upon coculturing with Trop2-positive DLD1 cells or KRAS-G12V–mutated SW620-A11 target cells was also substantially reduced after coculturing with repeated addition of α-Trop2-CD3 engager proteins compared with α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins (fig. S4, D to F). To further investigate the exhaustion phenotype of TCR-T cells, we analyzed PD-1 and LAG-3 expression by flow cytometry after repeated coculture with DLD1 tumor cells in the presence of α-Trop2-V6 or α-Trop2-CD3 engagers (Fig. 3J). The results revealed that α-Trop2-V6 induced significantly lower PD-1 and/or LAG-3 expression in either CD4+ or CD8+ T cells than α-Trop2-CD3, which was consistent with the sustained cytotoxic activity observed for α-Trop2-V6 (Fig. 3K and fig. S4, G to I). These findings indicate that sustained exposure to α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins preserves the functional potency of TCR-T cells, preserving both their intrinsic reactivity against pHLA antigens and engager-mediated reactivity against surface antigens.

To further compare the functional profiles of the α-Trop2-V6 and α-Trop2-CD3 engagers, we evaluated their activity in h1-2C TCR-T cells and an additional 1G4 TCR-T cell (TRBV6-5 subtype), which recognizes the NY-ESO-1157–166 peptide presented by HLA-A*02:01. The binding affinity of α-Trop2-V6 for the 1G4 TCR (KD = 1.49 nM) was substantially higher than its affinity for the h1-2C TCR yet lower than the affinity of the α-Trop2-CD3 engager for CD3 (KD = 46.6 nM) (figs. S4C and S5A). Both engagers induced comparable cytotoxicity against DLD1 target cells in both TCR-T cell models. However, α-Trop2-V6 triggered significantly lower cytokine production than α-Trop2-CD3, suggesting a potentially reduced risk of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) (fig. S5, B to G). In the DLD1 tumor xenograft model, α-Trop2-V6 achieved tumor suppression efficacy comparable to that of α-Trop2-CD3 yet induced markedly lower serum cytokine levels (fig. S5, H to N). Collectively, these data demonstrate that α-Trop2-V6 elicits robust antitumor activity equivalent to α-Trop2-CD3, while inducing modest cytokine release. This favorable profile likely arises from its selective recruitment of specific TCR-T cells, rather than the nonspecific pan–T cell activation triggered by CD3-targeted engagers.

Synergistic activity of α-Trop2-V6 engager and TCR-T cells elicits potent antitumor responses against heterogeneous tumors

Tumor heterogeneity is characterized by both the expression of distinct antigens across different tumor cells and the variation in antigen expression levels within tumors. To evaluate the T cell reactivity of TCR-JANUS engager proteins against tumors with differing antigen densities, we first assessed their activity on HT29 tumor cells sorted by Trop2 expression level (Fig. 4A and fig. S6A). Specifically, we prepared clones exhibiting low, medium, and high Trop2 expression (HT29-Trop2Low, HT29-Trop2Medium, and HT29-Trop2High) to test the responsiveness of α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins to these heterogeneous target populations (Fig. 4B). The results demonstrated that α-Trop2-V6 engager protein–mediated cytokine production was substantially higher against HT29-Trop2High target cells compared to HT29-Trop2Medium and HT29-Trop2Low cells (Fig. 4C). Cytotoxicity analyses revealed substantial killing of HT29-Trop2Low target cells, although the efficiency was markedly lower than that observed against HT29-Trop2Medium and HT29-Trop2High cells (Fig. 4D). Flow cytometry–based cytotoxicity analyses also revealed substantial depletion of HT29 target cells with h1-2C TCR-T cells in combination with α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins (fig. S6, B and C). These findings indicate that α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins can mediate cytotoxicity against tumors with low antigen burden, with cytotoxicity efficiency increasing alongside elevated antigen expression levels.

We further investigated α-Trop2-V6 engager protein activity against heterogeneous tumors expressing distinct antigens. In this assay, SW620-A11 cells (expressing KRAS-G12V neoantigen) and DLD1 cells (expressing Trop2) were mixed and cocultured with h1-2C TCR-T cells in the presence of α-Trop2-V6 engager or control proteins (Fig. 4, E and F). Flow cytometry analysis of remaining target cells showed that SW620-A11 cells were eliminated upon coculturing with h1-2C TCR-T cells alone, whereas both SW620-A11 and DLD1 cells were depleted in the presence of α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins. This result demonstrates that combining α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins with h1-2C TCR-T cells enables cytotoxicity against heterogeneous tumors expressing different antigens. Furthermore, we tested α-Trop2-V6 engager protein–coupled T-DART therapy against varied mixtures of SW620-A11, DLD1, and HT29-Trop2Low target cells (Fig. 4, G and H). Administration of α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins significantly increased IFN-γ levels and cytotoxicity efficacy against SW620-A11 cells mixed with either DLD1 or HT29-Trop2Low cells. Notably, elevated IFN-γ production and cytotoxicity were also observed with the T-DART therapy against the triple mixture of SW620-A11, DLD1, and HT29-Trop2Low cells. Collectively, these results indicate that α-Trop2-V6 engager protein–coupled T-DART can effectively mediate clearance of heterogeneous tumors.

To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins against heterogeneous tumors in vivo, we further tested these proteins in NCG mice bearing subcutaneous SW620-A11/DLD1 heterogeneous tumor xenografts. Administration of h1-2C TCR-T cells alone resulted in significant tumor suppression in mice engrafted with homogeneous SW620 tumors, even in the absence of α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins (Fig. 5, A and B). Both tumor volume and weight were significantly reduced in the h1-2C TCR-T cell treatment group compared with the mock T cell control group (Fig. 5, C and D, and fig. S7, A to E). In contrast, in the SW620-A11/DLD1 heterogeneous tumor model, h1-2C TCR-T cells alone failed to inhibit tumor growth (Fig. 5, E and F). However, when coadministered with α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins, substantial tumor inhibition was observed (Fig. 5, F to J). Moreover, tumor weights in the α-Trop2-V6 treatment group were significantly lower than those in the α-Trop2-NC control group (Fig. 5, K and L). No substantial body weight loss was observed in mice (fig. S7F). These results demonstrate that α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins can synergize with h1-2C TCR-T cells to elicit potent antitumor responses against heterogeneous tumors.

The functional potency of TCR-JANUS engager proteins against tumors with heterogeneous antigen expression was further evaluated using FRα-positive models. FRα, overexpressed in ovarian cancer, triple-negative breast cancer, NSCLC, and other malignancies, is a validated therapeutic target. While FRα-targeting antibody-drug conjugates are clinically approved for ovarian cancer, TCEs and CAR-T cells against FRα also demonstrate substantial antitumor efficacy (20, 21). We designed an anti-FRα TCR-JANUS engager (termed α-FRα-V6) by fusing an anti-FRα scFv to an anti-TRBV6 scFv (Fig. 6A). The immune responsiveness and cytotoxicity of α-FRα-V6–mediated TCR-T cell reactivity were further evaluated against three FRα-positive tumor cells, i.e., A375, HeLa, and SKOV3 (fig. S8, A to C). The results demonstrate that the α-FRα-V6 could induce substantial IFN-γ production against A375, HeLa, or SKOV3 target cells in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6C). Cytotoxicity analyses reveal that the α-FRα-V6 engager proteins mediated substantial cytotoxicity of the TCR-T cells against A375, HeLa, or SKOV3 target cells and induced substantial IFN-γ production (Fig. 6, D and E). To evaluate efficacy in heterogeneous tumors, we mixed FRα-positive cells (A375, HeLa, or SKOV3) with KRAS-G12V–positive SW620-A11 cells. IFN-γ–ELISA and luciferase cytotoxicity assays showed that the α-FRα-V6 engager proteins induced substantially increased cytokine production and cytotoxicity against mixed heterogenous tumor cells compared with TCR-T cells cocultured with control proteins (Fig. 6, F and G). Flow cytometry–based cytotoxicity analyses confirmed that depletion of both FRα-positive tumor (A375, HeLa, or SKOV3) and SW620-A11 tumor populations can only be achieved in the presence of α-FRα-V6 engager proteins (Fig. 6H). These results indicate that the α-FRα-V6 engager proteins effectively overcome tumor heterogeneity by directing potent cytotoxicity against tumors with differential antigen expression.

To validate the applicability of TCR-JANUS engager proteins in combination with other TRBV6 TCRs, we further evaluated the antitumor reactivity of the α-Trop2-V6 engager protein together with the 1G4 TCR (Fig. 6I). Functional assessments were performed using three NY-ESO-1–expressing tumor cell lines (A375, HCC-827, and MCF-7), among which HCC-827 and MCF-7 were also Trop2 positive (fig. S8D). Binding analyses revealed that the α-Trop2-V6 engager proteins exhibited specific binding to 1G4 TCR-T cells (fig. S5, A to C). We found that 1G4 TCR-T cells mediated substantial IFN-γ production and cytotoxicity against all three NY-ESO-1–positive target cells (Fig. 6J and fig. S8E). Notably, substantial cytotoxicity against HCC-827 and MCF-7 cells was observed at a high effector-to-target (E:T) ratio of 4:1, whereas cytotoxic activity was abolished for HCC-827 and only minimally detectable for MCF-7 at an E:T ratio of 1:1 (fig. S8, F to I). Notably, both IFN-γ production and cytotoxicity of 1G4 TCR-T cells were significantly enhanced in the presence of the α-Trop2-V6 engager protein, even at a substantially lower E:T ratio of 1:4 (Fig. 6, K to M). In contrast, neither cytokine production nor cytotoxicity was augmented in Trop2-negative A375 cells. These results demonstrate that TCR-JANUS engager proteins can potently enhance cytokine release and cytotoxic activity against target cells that coexpress heterogeneous intracellular and extracellular tumor antigens.

DISCUSSION

TCR-T cell therapy represents a promising strategy for targeting intracellular tumor antigens, particularly neoantigens that are attractive immunotherapy targets. However, maximizing the therapeutic efficacy of infused TCR-T cells remains challenging. To address tumor heterogeneity faced in TCR-T cell therapy, we designed TCR-JANUS engager proteins. By targeting the TRBV of the specific TCRs, these bispecific molecules mediate recruitment of specific TCR-T cells to target additional tumor antigens to tackling tumor heterogeneity. Specifically, the anti-Trop2 TCR-JANUS engagers induced h1-2C TCR-T cell responsiveness against Trop2-expressing tumors while preserving their intrinsic reactivity against cognate KRAS-G12V pHLA antigens. In both in vitro and in vivo heterogeneous tumor models, TCR-JANUS proteins substantially enhanced cytotoxicity against tumors coexpressing the KRAS-G12V neoantigen and Trop2. Notably, unlike anti-CD3–based TCEs that cause T cell exhaustion and reduced cytotoxicity upon repeated exposure, TCR-JANUS engagers mediated sustained antitumor efficacy.

Tumor heterogeneity, antigen loss, and impaired antigen processing or presentation represent major obstacles to the efficacy of targeted immunotherapies such as CAR-T cells, TCR-T cells, and TCEs (22, 23). Under the selective pressure imposed by these therapies, tumor cells lacking the target antigen may proliferate and lead to relapse. For instance, clinical studies of NY-ESO-1–specific TCR-T cells in myeloma revealed sustained on-target activity accompanied by a decline in NY-ESO-1 expression postinfusion; however, a subset of patients experienced relapse with antigen-negative disease (24). Frankiw et al. (25) reported a clinical case receiving autologous NY-ESO-1–specific TCR-T cell therapy that tumor NY-ESO-1 expression was completely lost after initial tumor regression but developed disease progression (25). These observations underscore the need for targeting multiple antigens as promising strategies to overcome tumor heterogeneity. For example, cotargeting of ADGRE2 and CLEC12A with a dual CAR approach has shown superior antitumor efficacy compared to ADGRE2-directed CAR-T cells alone and mitigated antigen escape in acute myeloid leukemia (26). Similarly, CD3-based TCEs targeting both BCMA and CD38 exhibited enhanced cytotoxic activity against tumor cells with heterogeneous expression of these antigens (27). Furthermore, engineering T cells with dual targeting capability via TCR/CAR constructs can suppress tumors with varying antigen expression, although TCR-mediated recognition may influence CAR activity depending on TCR-antigen engagement (28). Collectively, these findings support the notion that multiantigen targeting can more effectively control heterogeneous tumors.

Given the prolonged persistence of TCR-T cells in vivo and the precise redirection capability of TCR-JANUS engager proteins toward tumor surface TAAs, we propose that the T-DART platform represents a promising therapeutic strategy to address tumor heterogeneity. The results from this study demonstrate that anti-Trop2 TCR-JANUS engagers effectively recruit KRAS-G12V–neoantigen–specific h1-2C TCR-T cells and elicit robust antigen-specific antitumor activity, while preserving the intrinsic reactivity of the TCR. Although the TCR-JANUS engager only moderately enhanced the cell avidity between TCR-T cells and tumor cells, its robust induction of cytokine production and cytotoxicity demonstrates that the engager protein confers high functional sensitivity with a low signaling threshold for efficient T cell activation. Instead of relying solely on high binding avidity, TCR-JANUS engagers may facilitate the spatial assembly and sustained signaling of TCR complexes. This localized signal amplification enables small changes in intrinsic binding strength to be translated into potent functional responses. In the SW620-A11/DLD1 heterogeneous model, administration of anti-Trop2 TCR-JANUS engager proteins, in combination with h1-2C TCR-T cells, led to significant tumor suppression. Furthermore, TCR-JANUS engagers targeting FRα were also shown to induce substantial tumor inhibition in various heterogeneous tumor models when used together with KRAS-G12V–neoantigen–specific h1-2C TCR-T cells. Thus, TCR-JANUS engagers can act as an “aide-de-camp” for TCR-T cells, enabling simultaneous targeting of additional surface TAAs and enhancing therapeutic efficacy against heterogeneous tumors.

Conventional TCEs primarily target the CD3ε subunit (which forms CD3γε/CD3δε heterodimers surface-exposed on the TCR complex), enabling T cell recruitment via dual targeting of tumor antigens and CD3. However, this approach often causes nonspecific T cell activation, leading to systemic inflammation and CRS in clinical settings. While engineering strategies such as low-affinity anti-CD3 domains can mitigate systemic toxicity by reducing splenic accumulation, our TRBV-targeting TCR-JANUS engagers offer an alternative strategy with three key advantages. First is specific T cell recruitment. TCR-JANUS selectively engages exogenously infused TCR-T cells. Although endogenous T cells expressing TRBV6 exist (typically <5% of total T cells), they do not induce systemic toxicity comparable to anti-CD3 TCEs. Second is sustained T cell reactivity. Unlike anti-CD3 TCEs that substantially impair T cell function during long-term coculture, TCR-JANUS preserves TCR-T cell reactivity under chronic exposure. The exhaustion phenotypes of TCR-T cells were substantially lower for TCR-JANUS engager proteins than anti-CD3 TCEs. However, the NCG immunodeficient mouse model has inherent limitations for evaluating tumor-infiltrating T cell exhaustion. Therefore, the in vivo exhaustion phenotypes of TCR-T cells, especially for T cells infiltrated in tumor microenvironments, and subsequent antitumor efficiency compared with anti-CD3 engagers still need further investigations. Third is enhanced tumor infiltration. The natural low affinity of TCR-pHLA interactions (1 to 100 μM) contrasts with the high affinity of TCR-JANUS for tumor surface target and TCR. This affinity differential promotes tumor accumulation of TCR-JANUS engager proteins and facilitates enhanced TCR-T cell infiltration into the tumor microenvironment. In addition, the responsiveness of TCR-JANUS engager proteins would boost the TCR-T cells and result in expanded TCR-T cell population to further function against their intrinsic targets. Besides, the expression of tumor antigens may change during TCR-T cell therapy, and adaptively selecting targeted TCR-JANUS engagers based on real-time dynamic changes in tumor antigen expression is also crucial for effectively addressing tumor heterogeneity.

In summary, we designed a bispecific TCR-JANUS engager protein that simultaneously targets the TRBV domain of a specific TCR and a TAA on the tumor surface. When combined with TCR-T cell therapy, this molecule can specifically recruit TCR-T cells to engage additional surface antigens while preserving their intrinsic pHLA-targeting capability, thereby overcoming tumor heterogeneity. This integrated approach, termed T-DART (TCR-JANUS engager protein–coupled TCR-T cell therapy), represents a promising strategy to effectively mitigate tumor heterogeneity and expand the clinical applicability of TCR-T cell–based treatments.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Culture of cell lines

The cell lines used in this study included 293T, human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell line SW620 [American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), CCL-227], DLD1 (ATCC, CCL-221), HT29 (ATCC, HTB-38), human ovarian cancer cell line SKOV3 (ATCC, HTB-77), human malignant melanoma cell line A375 (ATCC, CRL-1619), human cervical adenocarcinoma cell line HeLa (ATCC, CCL-2), and human non–small cell lung carcinoma cell line HCC-827 (ATCC, CRL-2868). All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere.

For bioluminescence-based cytotoxicity assays, SW620, DLD1, HT29, SKOV3, A375, and Hela cells were transduced with genes encoding a green fluorescent protein (GFP)/Firefly luciferase fusion protein (GFP/Luc), while SW620 cells were transduced with HLA-A*11:01/Firefly luciferase (A11/Luc). All cell lines were obtained from the Cell Resource Center, Peking Union Medical College. Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from healthy donors was reviewed and approved by the Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences of Research Ethics Committee with assigned number authority APIMCAS2022075.

Purification of protein

α-Trop2-V6, α-Trop2-NC, α-Trop2-CD3, α-FRα-V6, α-FRα-NC, h1-2C TCR, 1G4 TCR and Trop2 proteins were expressed using the 293F cell expression system. Plasmids (pCAGGS-α-Trop2-V6, pCAGGS-α-Trop2-NC, pCAGGS-α-Trop2-CD3, pCAGGS-h1-2C-β, pCAGGS-h1-2C-α, and pCAGGS-Trop2) were transfected using PEI (Yesen, 40816ES01) transfection reagent. SMM 293-TII expression medium (Sino Biological) was supplemented with 35 ml of SMS 293-SUPI expression medium supplement (Sino Biological) per liter at 24 and 72 hours posttransfection. The culture supernatant was collected on day 6. Proteins were purified using a His-tag affinity column (GE Healthcare), followed by size exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare). Purity was confirmed by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. All proteins were stored at −80°C until use.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

IFN-γ was detected using a commercial ELISA kit (BD Biosciences, 555142). IFN-γ capture antibodies were coated onto high-binding ELISA plates in coating buffer (Solarbio, C1055) and incubated overnight at 4°C. Plates were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (Solarbio, C1055) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.05% Tween 20 (PBST) at room temperature for 1 hour. Cell culture supernatants were added and incubated for 2 hours at room temperature. After three washes with PBST, plates were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime, A0201) and detection antibody diluted 1:250 in blocking buffer at room temperature for 1 hour. HRP activity was detected at 450 nm using a trimethylboron single-component substrate solution (Beyotime, P0209) and stopped with 2 M hydrochloric acid (HCl). Absorbance was measured using an ELISA plate reader (EL-808, Biotek).

IFN-γ–specific ELISPOT assay

ELISPOT assays were performed to evaluate IFN-γ–specific cytokine secretion by T cells. Briefly, 96-well ELISPOT plates was coated overnight at 4°C with capture antibody specific for IFN-γ (BD Biosciences,551873). Plates were then blocked with RPMI 1640 containing 10% FBS at room temperature for 2 hours.

T cells (1 × 104 per well) were added to the wells in triplicate and stimulated with target cells or appropriate controls. After incubation for 18 hours at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator, cells were removed by washing with PBST. Plates were then incubated with biotinylated detection antibody at 1:200 for 2 hours at room temperature, followed by incubation with streptavidin–HRP (BioLegend, 405210) for 1 hour. Cytokine spots were developed using 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole substrate solution and allowed to dry completely. Spots were counted using an automated ELISPOT reader (CTL, model ImmunoSpot S6 Ultimate), and results were expressed as spot-forming units per 104 cells.

SPR measurements

SPR measurements were performed using a Biacore T200 system (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) with a CM5 sensor chip. h1-2C TCR and Trop2 proteins were immobilized on the chip surface via standard amine coupling chemistry according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Analyte samples at various concentrations were injected over the chip surface in running buffer (PBS with 0.005% Tween-20). Binding data were analyzed using Biacore Insight Evaluation Software (Cytiva).

Nano differential scanning fluorimetry

α-Trop2-NC, α-Trop2-V6, and α-Trop2-CD3 proteins at a concentration of 1 mg/ml were subjected to a thermal gradient ranging from 25° to 95°C on the Prometheus NT.48 NanoDSF instrument (NanoTemper Technologies). The data were analyzed using Nano Analyze software, which was paired with the instrument. Fitting results were exported and plotted using Origin Prism10 software.

Cell binding avidity assay

Cell-cell binding avidity was assessed on a z-Movi Cell Avidity Analyzer (LUMICKS). DLD1 cells were adhered to concanavalin A poly-l-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich, Solarbio Life Sciences, 11029-07-0)–coated microfluidic chips for 4 hours at 37°C. h1-2C TCR-T cells were labeled with CellTrace Far Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The chip with target cells was mounted on the z-Movi stage. Labeled T cells were mixed with α-Trop2-NC or α-Trop2-V6 proteins (10 μg/ml) and injected into the chip after a 10-min preincubation. An acoustic force was ramped from 0 to 1000 pN to progressively detach bound cells; detachment events were recorded and analyzed with Ocean (LUMICKS) software.

Lentivirus preparation

The h1-2C TCR and 1G4 TCR genes were synthesized by GenScript and subcloned into the lentiviral vector pCMV-SFFV-MCS. The pCDH-CAG-A11-luci and pCDH-CAG-luci-GFP vectors used in this study were stored in our laboratory. Lentivirus production was performed by transfecting 293T cells with the lentiviral vectors and packaging plasmids (pLP1, pLP2, and vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein) using PEI (MKbio, catalog no. MX2202-1G). Six hours posttransfection, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 2% FBS, penicillin (100 IU/ml), streptomycin (100 μg/ml), and 0.12% sodium butyrate (Sigma-Aldrich, catalog no. B5887-250MG). Lentiviral supernatants were collected 48 hours posttransfection to obtain high-titer lentiviruses.

Generation of TCR-T cells

PBMCs were isolated from healthy donors using Ficoll-Paque density gradient centrifugation (Tianjin Haoyang) and sorted with a pan–T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-091-156). T cells were activated using CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-111-160) and cultured in UniEx T cell serum-free medium (ExCell) supplemented with 2.5% Helios UltraGRO-Advanced (AventaCell), human interleukin-2 (IL-2; 300 IU/ml; Novoprotein), human IL-7 (10 ng/ml; Novoprotein), streptomycin (100 μg/ml) and penicillin (100 IU/ml; Invitrogen). T cells were expanded and maintained at a density of 106 cells/ml. After 36 hours, T cells were washed and transduced with lentiviral supernatant.

Flow cytometry

Surface expression of Trop2 and FRα on tumor cells was assessed by flow cytometry using phycoerythrin (PE)–conjugated anti-human Trop2 (Abclonal, A26262) and PE-conjugated anti-human FRα (BioLegend, 908304) antibodies, respectively. TCR expression was detected using KRAS-G12V/HLA-A*11:01 tetramer and NY-ESO-1/HLA-A*02:01 tetramer. TCR-T cells were stained with Fixable Viability Stain 780 (BD Biosciences, 565388), PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-human CD3 (BioLegend,300430), APC anti-human CD4 (BioLegend, 317416), fluorescein isothiocyanate anti-human CD8a (Abclonal, A27984), APC anti-human CD45RO (BioLegend, 304210), PE anti-human CD62L (BioLegend, 384816), PE anti-human IFN-γ (BioLegend, 506507), and PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-human TNF-α (BioLegend, 502926).

For surface marker detection, T cells were collected, washed with PBS, and 1 × 106 cells were resuspended in 100 μl of PBS containing 0.1 μl of Fixable Viability Stain 780 (BioLegend) for 15 min at 4°C. Cells were then incubated with specific surface antibodies for 20 min at 4°C. For intracellular cytokine staining, T cells were first stained for surface markers and then fixed, permeabilized, and stained with PE anti-human IFN-γ and PerCP-Cy5.5 anti-human TNF-α using the BD Fix/Perm buffer (BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Data were acquired on a BD FACSAria III flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, version X).

Cytotoxicity assay

TCR-T cell cytotoxicity was assessed using a bioluminescence-based assay. Tumor cells expressing luciferase (SW620-A11, SW620, A375, DLD1, HCC-827, HT29, and SKOV3) were seeded in triplicate at 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates. Cells were cocultured with nontransduced T cells (Mock-T cells), h1-2C TCR-T cells, or 1G4 TCR-T cells at E:T ratios of 8:1, 4:1, 2:1, and 1:1. To evaluate the effect of combining TCR-T cells with α-Trop2-V6 antibody on antitumor activity, cells were cocultured at E:T ratios of 1:1 and 1:2 in medium containing α-Trop2-V6 antibody (0.55 μg/ml).

After 24 hours, the supernatant was removed and the collected cells were treated with 50 μl of 1× lysis buffer (TransGen Biotech, FR101-01-V2) for 10 min at room temperature. Then, 10 μl of lysate supernatant were mixed with 50 μl of Luciferase reagent in a Luma Plate-96 white plate (Greiner, 655074). Luminescence signals were measured using a plate reader (BioTek, EL-808). Specific lysis (%) = [(Maximum Luciferase Activity − Experimental Luciferase Activity)/Maximum Luciferase Activity] × 100.

In vivo antitumor activity in a xenograft tumor model

All animal experiments were approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of the Institute of Microbiology, Chinese Academy of Science and conducted in accordance with the policies and certification of the International Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (SQ-SQIMCAS206075). Animals were maintained under specific pathogen–free conditions at the animal facilities of the Institute of Zoology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Six- to 8-week-old male NOD-Prkdcscid IL-2rgnull (NPG) mice were obtained from GemPharmatech.

Six- to 8-week-old male NCG mice were used for all xenograft experiments. All procedures were performed in accordance with Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines. For the SW620-A11 tumor model, 1 × 106 SW620-A11 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of 7-week-old mice. Four days postinoculation, tumor size was assessed via bioluminescence imaging, and mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 4). Tumor-bearing mice were then intravenously infused via the tail vein with 2 × 107 Mock-T cells or h1-2C TCR-T cells to evaluate TCR-T cell efficacy.

To assess the combination therapy of TCR-T cells with α-Trop2-V6 antibody, 5 × 105 DLD1-Luci cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of 7-week-old mice. Mixed tumor models were generated by subcutaneously injecting tumor cells (5 × 105 DLD1-Luci cells and 1 × 106 SW620-A11 cells) into the right flank of 7-week-old mice. Four days later, mice were intravenously infused with 2 × 107 Mock-T cells or h1-2C TCR-T cells. α-Trop2-V6 or α-Trop2-CD3 engager proteins were administered intravenously at 100 mg/kg on days 0 to 5, with α-Trop2-NC as the negative control. Recombinant IL-2 (2 × 105 U per mouse) was injected subcutaneously three times during the treatment period.

Tumor progression was monitored weekly using the IVIS Spectrum Imaging platform. Tumor growth was measured with calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: ½ × length × width2. Experimental end points were reached at the earliest stage when significant differences were observed among treatment groups or when mice reached maximum tumor burden, defined as tumor volume > 4000 mm3, tumor weight > 10% of body weight, or presence of ulceration, infection, or necrosis.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as means ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Differences between two groups were evaluated using the unpaired Student’s t test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance is indicated by asterisks as follows: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.
