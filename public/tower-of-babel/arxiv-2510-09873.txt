Abstract
. We study perfect state transfer and multiple state transfer in oriented normal Cayley graphs. We construct examples in a variety of groups, ranging from abelian to nonsolvable, and establish some general restrictions and nonexistence results.



1.
Introduction

An oriented graph is a directed graph with no loops or multiple edges. Given a directed graph
X
, we denote by
\mathrm{V}(X)
the vertex set, and by
\mathrm{A}(X)\subset\mathrm{V}(X)\times\mathrm{V}(X)
the set of arcs. The adjacency matrix of an oriented graph
X
is the matrix
A_{X}
whose rows and columns are indexed by
\mathrm{V}(X)
(in some order) satisfying

A_{a,b}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $(a,b)\in\mathrm{A}(X)$,}\\
-1&\text{if $(b,a)\in\mathrm{A}(X)$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Zimborás et al.
[
17
]
and Chavas et al.
[
3
, Theorem 2.1]
have shown that the Hermitian matrix

iA_{X}
can be realized as the restriction to the
1
-excitation subspace of a suitable Hamiltonian
for a system of
n
qubits. The time evolution of some initial state in this subspace is what
is meant by a quantum walk on an oriented graph
X
, and is found by solving the
Schrödinger equation. Thus, a quantum walk on an oriented graph is determined by an initial state and the time-dependent transition matrices,
U_{X}(t)=e^{-it(iA_{X})}=e^{tA_{X}}
.
This extension of quantum walks on undirected graphs to oriented graphs was first defined in
[
2
]
. Of central importance to the theory is the phenomenon of
perfect state transfer (PST)
. Given a vertex
b\in V(X)
and a matrix
B
indexed by
V(X)
in its rows and columns, we shall denote by
Bb
the column of
B
corresponding to
b
, which is equivalent to identifying
b\in V(X)
with the corresponding canonical vector of
b
in
\mathbb{C}^{V(X)}
. We say that perfect state transfer occurs between a vertex
a\in\mathrm{V}(X)
to a vertex
b\in\mathrm{V}(X)
at a time
\tau
if
U_{X}(\tau)a=\lambda b
, for some complex number
\lambda
of norm
1
.
If
a=b
, then we say
a
is
periodic
. As
U_{X}(\tau)
is a real matrix, we must have
\lambda=\pm 1
. The quantity
\lambda
is known as the phase. (We drop the subscripts in
A_{X}
and
U_{X}(t)
if the graph is clear from the context.)

For
a\in V(X)
, let

\mathcal{S}_{a}=\left\{b\in V(X)\ |\ U(\tau)a=\lambda b,\text{for some $\tau>0$ and $|\lambda|=1$}\right\}.

Suppose PST occurs from
a
to
b\ (\neq a)
in
X
.
It follows from Theorem 8.1 and Lemma 8.3 of
[
7
]
that
a
is periodic and
a\in\mathcal{S}_{a}
. Let
b
and
c
be distinct vertices in
\mathcal{S}_{a}
. For
u\in\{a,b,c\}
, let
\tau_{u}>0
denote the minimum time where
U(\tau_{u})a=\lambda_{u}u
, for some phase
\lambda_{u}
.
Note that
\tau_{b},\tau_{c}\leq\tau_{a}
.
If
\tau_{b}<\tau_{c}
then PST occurs from
b
to
c
at time
(\tau_{c}-\tau_{b})
,
and PST occurs from
c
to
b
at time
(\tau_{a}-\tau_{c}+\tau_{b})
.
Hence PST occurs between every pair of distinct vertices in
\mathcal{S}_{a}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{b}=\mathcal{S}_{a}
for
b\in\mathcal{S}_{a}
.
When
|\mathcal{S}_{a}|\geq 3
,
X
is said to have
multiple state transfer
(MST) on
\mathcal{S}_{a}
. This phenomenon cannot be observed in undirected graphs as PST in such graphs is monogamous (c.f
[
14
]
). However,
[
2
,
9
,
1
,
10
]
provide examples of multiple state transfer in oriented graphs.

The purpose of the present paper is to study state transfer in oriented normal Cayley graphs. The spectral
theory of such a graph is closely related to
the character theory of the underlying group,
which provides powerful machinery for our investigation.
We have two main goals. The first is to construct examples of perfect and multiple state transfer for as wide a variety of groups as possible, ranging from abelian to nonsolvable groups. The second is to find theoretical restrictions on the existence of state transfer on these graphs.

The paper is organized as follows.
We start in §
2
with some background on oriented graphs in Bose-Mesner algebras, of which
normal oriented Cayley graphs are a particular case.
A key result is that, for any vertex
a
involved in PST,
\mathcal{S}_{a}
is permuted cyclically by a permutation matrix in the Bose-Mesner algebra. Moreover,
we show that
|\mathcal{S}_{a}|
must lie in the set
\{2,3,4,6\}
.

Next, in §
3
we develop the theory for oriented normal Cayley graphs. The cyclic permutation of
\mathcal{S}_{a}
of §
2
takes the form of a
central element of order
|\mathcal{S}_{a}|
. We obtain a characterization of PST in terms of the characters of the group and its values on the connection set and this central element. This characterization is used later for the construction of examples of PST/MST, but also provides an easily-checked criterion for nonexistence of PST in any oriented normal Cayley graph on a given group based on the field of character values.

In §
4
we show that for solvable groups the case
|\mathcal{S}_{a}|=6
can be ruled out.
The proof is an application of the nonexistence criterion, together with the theory of
p
-special characters of solvable groups from
[
5
]
.
The general question of existence of an oriented graph
with MST on a set of size 6 remains open.
Our results show that, in order for such a graph to be an oriented normal Cayley graph, the underlying group must be nonsolvable.

The remaining sections, which describe examples of PST in various classes of groups, may be read independently of each other.

In §
5
, we consider PST in oriented Cayley graphs on
{\mathbb{Z}_{3}}^{n}
and
{\mathbb{Z}_{4}}^{n}
, constructing examples of PST and
ruling out MST on sets of size 4.

Examples of MST on sets of size 3 in oriented normal Cayley graphs on nonabelian groups are constructed on
extraspecial
3
-groups in §
6

and some nonsolvable examples are described in §
7
.

Examples of MST on sets of size 4 in oriented normal Cayley graphs on nonabelian groups are constructed on
the modular maximal cyclic
2
-groups in §
8
.

In the final section §
9
we show that if an oriented normal Cayley graph on a group
G
admits PST, then there is an associated graph
admitting PST on the wreath product
G\wr S_{n}
,
which provides a general method to construct nonabelian examples, including examples of MST on sets of size 4
on nonsolvable groups.

2.
Oriented graphs in Bose-Mesner algebras

An
association scheme
is a set
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{0},\ldots,A_{d}\}
of
v\times v

01
-matrices satisfying

i.

A_{0}=I
,

ii.

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{d}A_{j}=J
, the matrix of all ones,

iii.

A_{j}^{T}\in\mathcal{A}
, for
j=0,\ldots,d
,

iv.

A_{i}A_{j}=A_{j}A_{i}\in\mathrm{span}\ \mathcal{A}
, for
i,j=0,\ldots,d
.

We call
\mathrm{span}\ \mathcal{A}
the
Bose-Mesner algebra of
\mathcal{A}
, which is a commutative algebra closed under matrix multiplication, the Schur product and matrix transposition. In particular, there exist integers
k_{0},\ldots,k_{d}
such that

A_{j}J=JA_{j}=k_{j}J
for
j=0,\ldots,d
. Further, if
j\neq 0

then
A_{j}
has zero diagonal entries.

For
a\in V(X)
, recall that

\mathcal{S}_{a}=\left\{b\in V(X)\ |\ U(\tau)a=\lambda b,\text{for some $\tau>0$ and $|\lambda|=1$}\right\}.

Define the relation
\equiv
on
V(X)
such that for
a,b\in V(X)
,

\displaystyle a\equiv b\mbox{ if and only if PST occurs at a certain time $\tau>0$ from $a$ = $b$}.

Clearly,
\equiv
is an equivalence relation on
V(X)
, and the equivalence class containing
a
is the set
\mathcal{S}_{a}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X
be an oriented graph whose adjacency matrix
A
belongs to the Bose-Mesner algebra of
\mathcal{A}
.
If PST occurs from
a
to
b
where
a\neq b
at time
\tau
, then
U(\tau)\in\mathcal{A}
is the permutation
matrix of a fixed-point-free automorphism of
X
.

Proof.

First observe that
U(t)\in\mathrm{span}\ \mathcal{A}
, for any time
t
. If PST occurs from
a
to
b
at time
\tau
then the
(b,a)
-entry is the only non-zero entry in column
a
of
U(\tau)
.
We see the
U(\tau)=\lambda A_{j}
for some
A_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
with
k_{j}=1
. Since
(A_{j})_{a,a}=0
,
j\neq 0
and
A_{j}
is a fixed-point-free permutation.

Now
A
is a sum of matrices of the form
A_{i}-A_{i}^{T}
. Since
A_{i}J=A_{i}^{T}J
, the vector of all ones
{\bf 1}
is an eigenvector of
A
with eigenvalue
0
.
Whence

\lambda{\bf 1}=\lambda A_{j}{\bf 1}=U(\tau){\bf 1}=e^{0\tau}{\bf 1},

and
\lambda=1
.
Since
A_{j}
commutes with
A
,
A_{j}
is the permutation matrix of an automorphism of
X
.
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X
be an oriented graph with adjacency matrix
A
belongs to the Bose-Mesner algebra of some association scheme
\mathcal{A}
.
Let
\tau>0
be the smallest time at which PST occurs from
a
to another vertex in
X
.
Then, the following statements hold.

(a)

U(\tau)
is an automorphism of
X
that acts cyclically on
\mathcal{S}_{a}
.

(b)

The matrix
U(\tau)
has order
|\mathcal{S}_{a}|
.

(c)

For any two vertices
a,b\in V(X)
such that
a\not\equiv b
, we have
|\mathcal{S}_{a}|=|\mathcal{S}_{b}|

Proof.

(a) Let
\mathcal{S}_{a}=\{u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\}
where
u_{0}=a
.
For
j=1,\ldots,m
, let
\tau_{j}
be the minimum PST time from
a
to
u_{j}
, and let
\tau_{0}=0
. Without loss of generality, we assume

\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\cdots<\tau_{m}.

Note that
\tau_{m}
is less than the minimum period of
a
.

For
j=1,\ldots,m
, PST from
u_{j-1}
to
u_{j}
occurs at time
(\tau_{j}-\tau_{j-1})
, hence

U\left(\tau_{j}-\tau_{j-1}\right)=\lambda_{j}P_{j},

for some fixed-point-free permutation matrix
P_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
satisfying

P_{j}u_{j-1}=u_{j}
and phase
\lambda_{j}
.

Suppose there exists
k<m
such that
\tau_{1}=\tau_{j}-\tau_{j-1}
,
for
j=1,\ldots,k
, and
\tau_{1}\neq\tau_{k+1}-\tau_{k}
.
Then
P_{1}=\cdots=P_{k}
and
P_{k+1}
are distinct permutation matrices in
\mathcal{A}
and
P_{1}u_{j}\neq P_{k+1}u_{j}
, for
j=0,\ldots,m
.

If
\tau_{k+1}-\tau_{k}<\tau_{1}
then

U(\tau_{k+1}-\tau_{k})a=\lambda_{k+1}P_{k+1}a.

So PST occurs from
a
to
P_{k+1}a
at time strictly less than
\tau_{1}
, which contradicts our choice of
u_{1}
.

If
\tau_{k+1}-\tau_{k}>\tau_{1}
then

U(\tau_{1}+\tau_{k})a=U(\tau_{1})U(\tau_{k})a=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{k}P_{1}u_{k}

and PST occurs from
a
to
P_{1}u_{k}\neq P_{k+1}u_{k}(=u_{k+1})
at time
(\tau_{1}+\tau_{k})
.
Since
\tau_{1}+\tau_{k}<\tau_{k+1}
is less than the minimum period of
a
,
P_{1}u_{k}\neq a
.
Further

P_{1}u_{k}\not\in\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}\}

because
P_{1}
is a permutation matrix satisfying
P_{1}u_{j-1}=u_{j}
,
for
j=0,\ldots,k
. The occurrence of PST from
a

to
P_{1}u_{k}\not\in\{u_{0},\ldots,u_{k+1}\}
contradicts our choice of
u_{k+1}
.

We conclude that
P_{1}=\cdots=P_{m}
, and when restricted to
\mathcal{S}_{a}
, this permutation is
the cycle
\left(u_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{m}\right)
.

(b) Let
P=U(\tau)
. We have seen previously that
P\in\mathcal{A}
is a permutation matrix. Let
k
be the multiplicative order of
P
. When restricted onto
\mathcal{S}_{a}
, we know from (a) that the permutation corresponding to
P
is an
|\mathcal{S}_{a}|
-cycle. Hence,
P^{|\mathcal{S}_{a}|}
has diagonal entries equal to
1
. Since
P\in\mathcal{A}
, we must have that
P^{|\mathcal{S}_{a}|}=I
. In other words, the multiplicative order of
P
is equal to
|\mathcal{S}_{a}|
, for
a\in V(X)
. (c) follows immediately from the latter.
∎

The first part of the following result is a special case of Theorem 6.4.3 in
[
15
]
.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
X
be an oriented graph with adjacency matrix
A
belongs to the Bose-Mesner algebra of some association scheme
\mathcal{A}
.
If PST occurs from
a
to another vertex in
X
at time
\tau
then
|\mathcal{S}_{a}|\in\{2,3,4,6\}
.
Moreover,
\tau\in\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\mathbb{Q}
if
|\mathcal{S}_{a}|\in\{3,6\}
, and
\tau\in\pi\mathbb{Q}
if
|\mathcal{S}_{a}|=4
.

Proof.

Let
A=\sum_{r}\theta_{r}E_{r}
be the spectral decomposition of
A
.
Since
A\in\mathrm{span}\ \mathcal{A}
, the eigenvalue support of
a
,
\Phi_{a}
, contains all eigenvalues of
A
.
By Theorem 6.1 of
[
9
]
, there exists a square-free positive integer
\Delta
such that
\Phi_{a}\subset{\mathbb{Z}}(i\sqrt{\Delta})
.

From Theorem
2.2
,
U(\tau)=P
is a permutation matrix of order
m=|\mathcal{S}_{a}|
. For
r=0,\ldots,d
,
PE_{r}=\omega_{r}E_{r}
for some
m
-th root of unity
\omega_{r}
. Since
P
is a permutation matrix, it follows from
[
15
, Lemma 2.10.3]
that
\omega_{r}\in\mathbb{Q}(i\sqrt{\Delta})
. Hence
m\in\{2,3,4,6\}
.

Suppose
m\in\{3,6\}
. There exists
r
such that
\omega_{r}
is a primitive
m
-th root of unity, so
\Delta=3
.
It follows from
PE_{r}=\omega_{r}E_{r}
and Theorem 2.10.3 of
[
15
]
that
\sqrt{3}i\in\mathbb{Q}(\theta_{r})
. Hence
\theta_{r}\in\sqrt{3}i\mathbb{Z}
, and
\tau\in\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\mathbb{Q}

by Corollary 6.1.3 of
[
15
]
.

Similarly, when
m=4
, there exists
r
such that
PE_{r}=iE_{r}
which implies
i\in\mathbb{Q}(\theta_{r})
.
Thus
\theta_{r}\in i\mathbb{Z}
and
\tau\in\pi\mathbb{Q}
.

∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
X
be an oriented graph whose adjacency matrix
A
belongs to the Bose-Mesner algebra of some association scheme
\mathcal{A}
.
If PST occurs in
X
at time
\tau
then there exist
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{s}
such that
V(X)
is the disjoint union of
\mathcal{S}_{a_{1}},\cdots,\mathcal{S}_{a_{s}}
, and
|\mathcal{S}_{a_{j}}|=|V(X)|/s
, for
j=1,\ldots,s
.

Proof.

Let
m
be the order of the permutation matrix
P=U(\tau)
as in the proof of Theorem
2.2
. Since
P\in\mathcal{A}
, up to re-ordering of vertices,
P
is a block diagonal matrix in which every block is a
m\times m
permutation matrix of order
m
. Let
a_{j}
be a vertex in the
j
-th block and the result follows.
∎

3.
Oriented normal Cayley graphs

The Cayley digraph
\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
on a group
G
with a connection set
C
is the graph with
G
as its vertex set and
\{(g,\ cg)\ :\ c\in C\}
as the set of its arcs. We note that
\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
is oriented if and only if
C\cap C^{-1}=\emptyset
.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{C}_{d}
be the conjugacy classes of
G
with

\mathcal{C}_{0}=\{e\}
. For
j=0,\ldots,d
, let
A_{j}
be the adjacency matrix of the Cayley graph
\mathrm{Cay}(G,\mathcal{C}_{j})
. Then
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{0},A_{1},\ldots,A_{d}\}
is an association scheme, called the
conjugacy class scheme of
G
.
A Cayley graph
\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
is a
normal
Cayley graph if
its connection set
C
is a union of conjugacy classes of
G
.
That is, its adjacency matrix belongs to
\mathrm{span}\ \mathcal{A}
.

Suppose PST occurs between two vertices in an oriented normal Cayley graph
\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
.
By vertex transitivity, we may assume without loss of generality that PST occurs from the identity
e
of
G
.
Let
\tau>0
be the minimum time at which PST occurs from
e
to some vertex
z
.
By Lemma
2.1
,
U(\tau)
is the adjacency matrix of
\mathrm{Cay}(G,\{z^{-1}\})
.
Since
U(\tau)\in\mathcal{A}
,
\{z^{-1}\}
is a conjugacy class of
G
which implies
z
is a central element of
G
. It follows from Theorem
2.2
and Corollary
2.3
that
\mathcal{S}_{e}=\langle z\rangle
and
z
has order
2
,
3
,
4
or
6
.

Theorem 3.1
.

If
\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
is an oriented normal Cayley graph admitting PST, then
\mathcal{S}_{e}
is a cyclic subgroup of
Z(G)
that has order
2
,
3
,
4
, or
6
.

Given
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, let
E_{\chi}
denote the
G\times G
matrix with
E_{\chi}(g,h)=\dfrac{\chi(hg^{-1})\chi(e)}{|G|}
. The matrix
E_{\chi}
is an idempotent projector onto the
\chi
-isotypic subspace of the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[G]
.
Given a subset
S\subset G
, by
A_{S}
, denote the
G\times G
matrix with
A_{S}(g,h)=\delta_{S}(hg^{-1})
, where
\delta_{S}
denoting the indicator function of
S
in
G
. In the case
S
is a conjugacy class,
A_{S}
satisfies the following decomposition as a linear combination of orthogonal idempotents.

(1)

A_{S}=\sum\limits_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\dfrac{\chi(S)}{\chi(e)}E_{\chi}.

Let
X:=\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
be an oriented normal Cayley graph, that is,
C
is a union of conjugacy classes and
C\cap C^{-1}=\emptyset
. We observe that
A:=A_{C}-A_{C^{-1}}
is the adjacency matrix of
X
. Using (
1
), we observe that the spectrum of
A
is

\{\theta_{\chi}\ :\ \chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)\}
, with
\theta_{\chi}:=\dfrac{\chi(C)-\overline{\chi(C)}}{\chi(e)}
for all
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
. We also have

(2)

A=\sum\limits_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\theta_{\chi}E_{\chi}.

Recall that
U(t)=e^{tA}
and thus, we have

(3)

U(t)=\sum\limits_{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}e^{t\theta_{\chi}}E_{\chi}.

Lemma 3.2
.

In an oriented normal Cayley graph,
PST occurs from
a
to
b
at time
\tau
if and only if PST occurs from
e
to
ba^{-1}
.
Further,
ba^{-1}
is a central element of
G
.

Proof.

Let
P
be the permutation matrix of the automorphism of
X
that maps
g
to
ga^{-1}
.
Then
PU(\tau)=U(\tau)P
, and
U(\tau)a=b
if and only if
U(\tau)e=ba^{-1}
.
By Theorem
2.2
,
ba^{-1}\in\mathcal{S}_{e}\subset Z(G)
.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

PST occurs from
e
to
z
at time
\tau
in an oriented normal Cayley graph
\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
if and only if the following hold.

(1)

z\in Z(G)
.

(2)

For
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
,

\dfrac{\chi(z)}{\chi(e)}=\exp\left({\tau\ \dfrac{\chi(C)-\overline{\chi(C)}}{\chi(e)}}\right).

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2
, if PST occurs from
e
to
z
then
z\in Z(G)
.
From (
3
),
U(\tau)e=z
if and only if

e^{\tau\theta_{\chi}}E_{\chi}e=E_{\chi}z,\quad\text{for $\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)$}

which is equivalent to

(4)

e^{\tau\theta_{\chi}}\chi(g^{-1})=\chi(zg^{-1}),\quad\text{for $\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)$ and $g\in G$.}

Since
z\in Z(G)
,
z
acts on every irreducible module as a scalar matrix
and for
g\in G

\dfrac{\chi(zg^{-1})}{\chi(g^{-1})}=\dfrac{\chi(z)}{\chi(e)}.

Condition (
2
) follows immediately from (
4
).
∎

From the above, it follows that if PST occurs at a time
\tau
from
e
to
z
, then PST occurs at the time
n\tau
from
e
to
z^{n}
. This helps us conclude the following result.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
X:=\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
be a normal oriented Cayley graph, and
z
be a central element satisfying Condition (
2
) in Theorem
3.3
. Then
X
has multiple state transfer on the subgroup
\left\langle z\right\rangle
.

We end this section with a non-existence result. Before stating it, we recall some standard notation. Given a character
\chi
, let
\mathbb{Q}(\chi)
denote its minimal splitting field over
\mathbb{Q}
and let
\mathrm{ker}(\chi)
denote its kernel.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
z\in Z(G)
be such that there exists a subset
Y\subset\mathrm{Irr}(G)
satisfying:
(i)
z\notin\mathrm{ker}(\chi)
, for all
\chi\in Y
; and (ii)
\bigcap\limits_{\chi\in Y}\mathbb{Q}(\chi)=\mathbb{Q}
. Then no normal Cayley graph on
G
admits multiple state transfer on
\left\langle z\right\rangle
.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.4
, it suffices to show that there is no normal Cayley graph on
G
admits PST from
e
to
z
. We assume the contrary, that is, there is a normal Cayley graph
\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
that has PST from
e
to
z
, at a time
\tau
. Using Condition (
2
) of Theorem
3.3
, it follows that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\chi(z)}{\chi(e)}

\displaystyle=e^{\tau\theta_{\chi}},

for all
\chi\in Y

8.
Multistate transfer in modular maximal-cyclic group.

Given a positive integer
n
, the modular maximal-cyclic group of order
2^{n}
, denoted by
M_{2}(n)
, is a group defined by the following presentation:

M_{2}(n)=\left\langle x,\sigma\ \big\lvert\ x^{2^{n-1}}=\sigma^{2}=e,\ \sigma x\sigma=x^{2^{n-2}+1}\right\rangle.

It is well known and elementary to observe that
\langle x\rangle\trianglelefteq M_{2}(n)
,
M_{2}(n)=\langle x\rangle\rtimes\langle\sigma\rangle
,
Z(M_{2}(n))=\langle x^{2}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n-2}}
, and
[M_{2}(n),\ M_{2}(n)]=\langle x^{2^{n-2}}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. In this section, we construct an oriented normal Cayley graph on
M_{2}(n)
, which admits MST on a set of
4
vertices.

We now describe the conjugacy classes of
M_{2}(n)
.
These are well known and can be derived easily. As mentioned above, even powers of
x
are central. Given any pair of non-commuting elements
h,k
, we have
hkh^{-1}k^{-1}\in[M_{2}(n),\ M_{2}(n)]\setminus\{e\}=\{x^{2^{n-2}}\}
, so
hkh^{-1}=x^{2^{n-2}}k
. Thus, the conjugacy class of any non-central element
h\in M_{2}(n)
is the two-element set
\{h,x^{2^{n-2}}h\}
.

We now describe the characters of
M_{2}(n)
. The linear characters are exactly the same as those of its abelianization,
M_{2}(n)/\langle x^{2^{n-2}}\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n-2}}
. We need the following result about non-linear irreducible characters. Given
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(\langle x\rangle)
, we define
\psi^{\sigma}\in\mathrm{Irr}(M_{2}(n))
to be the character satisfying
\psi^{\sigma}(y)=\psi(\sigma y\sigma)=\psi(y^{2^{n-2}+1})
, for all
y\in\langle x\rangle
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Given a non-linear character
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(M_{2}(n))
, then (i)
\chi(g)=0
, for all

; and (ii)
\chi(x^{2^{n-3}})=\pm 2i
.

Proof.

By Clifford theory (
[
12
, Corollary 6.19]
, since
[M_{2}(n):\langle x\rangle]=2
, given any non-linear irreducible character
\chi
, its restriction
\chi|_{\langle x\rangle}
is a sum of distinct conjugates of a linear character of
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(\langle x\rangle)
. The set of conjugates of
\psi
is
\{\psi,\ \psi^{\sigma}\}
and thus
\chi|_{\langle x\rangle}=\psi+\psi^{\sigma}
. Since
\psi\neq\psi^{\sigma}
, we must have
o(\psi)=2^{n-1}
. Thus,
\psi(x)
must be a primitive
2^{n-1}
th root of unity. Therefore, (ii) follows from the fact that
\chi|_{\langle x\rangle}=\psi+\psi^{\sigma}
.

Given a non-central element
g
, let

be such that
hgh^{-1}g^{-1}\neq e
. Since
\{e,\ x^{2^{n-2}}\}
is the commutator, it follows that

, and thus

. Therefore,

\chi(g)=\chi(hgh^{-1})=\chi(x^{2^{n-2}}g)=\dfrac{\chi(x^{2^{n-2}})}{\chi(e)}\chi(g),

with the last equality following from the fact that

is a central element. As

is an element of order

, and thus
\chi(x^{2^{n-2}})=-2

(\pi/4)\theta_{\chi}
must be an even multiple of
\pi
. Therefore (
8
) is satisfied for all linear characters of order
4
.

Case 3:

We now consider the case
o(\chi)=2
. In this case, must have
\chi(x)=\pm 1
and
\chi(\sigma)=\pm 1
. Therefore,
\chi(g)\in\mathbb{Q}
, and thus,
\theta_{\chi}=0
. Since
\chi(x)=\pm 1
, we have
\chi(x^{2^{n-3}})=1
, and thus (
8
) holds for linear characters of order
2
as well.

We now move on to non-linear irreducible characters. Using Lemma
8.1
, it follows that for a non-linear irreducible
\chi
, we have
\chi(x^{2^{n-3}})/\chi(1)=\pm i
and thus
\theta_{\chi}=2\chi(x^{2^{n-3}})/\chi(1)=\pm 2i
, with the same sign. It follows that (
8
) holds for all non-linear irreducible characters as well.
∎

Remark
.

For
M_{2}(2)
it is an easy exercise to check that for
C=\{x^{2},x\sigma,x^{5}\sigma\}
we have MST on the central subgroup
\langle x^{2}\rangle

of order
4
.

9.
Wreath products

Let
X:=\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
be an oriented normal Cayley graph that admits PST from
e
to
z
, at a time
\tau
. In this section, we construct an oriented normal Cayley graph on
G\wr S_{n}
, which admits PST.

We first describe the group
G\wr S_{n}
. By
G^{n}
, we denote the
n
-fold direct product of
G
. We use boldfaced lower case letters to represent elements of
G^{n}
; and given
\mathbf{x}\in G^{n}
and
i\in G
, we define
x_{i}\in G
to be such that
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. The symmetric group
S_{n}
acts on the group
G^{n}
by permuting the indices of its elements:
\pi\cdot x=\pi\cdot(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=(x_{\pi^{-1}(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi^{-1}(n)})
, for all
\pi\in S_{n}
and
\mathbf{x}\in S_{n}
. The
wreath product

G\wr S_{n}
is the semidirect product
G^{n}\rtimes S_{n}
defined by the above action. We express elements of
G\wr S_{n}
as tuples of the form
(\mathbf{x};\ \pi)
, where
\mathbf{x}\in G^{n}
and
\pi\in S_{n}
.

We first describe the conjugacy classes and all the (complex) irreducible characters of
G\wr S_{n}:=G^{n}\rtimes S_{n}
. The description of these are well known and can be found in many standard texts on character theory of symmetric groups. For instance, the reader may refer to
[
13
, Chapter 4]
and
[
16
, Appendix B of Chapter 1]
.

Consider
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in G^{n}
and an
r
-cycle
\kappa\in S_{n}
. Let
a
be the smallest positive integer such that
\kappa=(a,\kappa(a)\ldots\kappa^{r}(a))
. We define the product (as elements of
G
)

(10)

\mathbf{y}_{\kappa}:=y_{a}y_{\kappa^{-1}(a)}\cdots y_{\kappa^{-i}(a)}\cdots y_{\kappa^{-r+1}(a)}.

This is known as the
cycle product
(cf.
[
13
, 4.2.1]
) associated with
\kappa
with respect to
\mathbf{y}
. As an example, given
\mathbf{g}=(a,b,c)\in G^{3}
,
\sigma=(1,2,3)
, and
\beta=(2,3)
, we have
\mathbf{g}_{\sigma}=acb
,
\mathbf{g}_{\sigma^{-1}}=abc
, and
\mathbf{g}_{\beta}=bc
. It is an easy exercise to observe that
(\mathbf{x},\alpha)\in G\wr S_{n}
is conjugate to
(\mathbf{y},\ \kappa)
if and only if (a)
\alpha
is also an
r
-cycle and (b)
\mathbf{x}_{\alpha}
is conjugate to
\mathbf{y}_{\kappa}
in
G
. More generally, given
g=(\mathbf{x};\pi)\in G\wr S_{m}
, with
\pi=\prod\pi_{i}
as a decomposition of
\pi
into disjoint cycles, and a conjugacy class
\mathfrak{K}
of
G
, define
m_{g,r}(\mathfrak{K})
to be the number of
r
-cycles
\kappa
in
\pi
such that
\mathbf{x}_{\kappa}\in\mathfrak{K}
. Let
\mathrm{ty}_{g}(\mathfrak{K})
denote the partition
\left\langle 1^{m_{g,1}(\mathfrak{K})},\ldots,r^{m_{g,r}(\mathfrak{K})},\ldots\right\rangle
. We have now defined a partition-valued function
\mathrm{ty}_{g}
on the set
\mathrm{Cl}(G)
of conjugacy classes of
G
, with
\sum\limits_{\mathfrak{K}\in\mathrm{Cl}(G)}|\mathrm{ty}_{g}(\mathfrak{K})|=n
. It is an easy exercise to show that
g,h\in G\wr S_{n}
are conjugate if and only if
\mathrm{ty}_{g}=\mathrm{ty}_{h}
. Conversely, given a partition-valued function
P
on
\mathrm{Cl}(G)
with
\sum|P(\mathfrak{K})|=n
, there is some
g\in G\wr S_{n}
such that
P=\mathrm{ty}_{g}
.

To describe the irreducible characters of
G\wr S_{n}
we must consider certain irreducible characters of
G\wr S_{m}
, for
m\leq n
.
Consider
\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
and let
V
be the
G
-module affording
\phi
as its character. Then
\bigotimes^{m}V
is a
G^{m}
-module affording
\otimes^{m}\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{m})
as its character. We can view
\bigotimes^{m}V
as a
G\wr S_{m}
-module via the action defined by

((x_{1},\ \ldots x_{m});\ \pi)\cdot\bigotimes\limits_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}=\bigotimes\limits_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}\cdot v_{\pi^{-1}(i)},

for all
((x_{1},\ \ldots x_{m});\ \pi)\in G\wr S_{m}
and
\bigotimes\limits_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\in\bigotimes^{m}V
. Let
\widetilde{\otimes^{m}\phi}
denote the character of
G\wr S_{m}
, afforded by
\bigotimes^{m}V
. It is well known that
\widetilde{\otimes^{m}\phi}\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\wr S_{m})
, for all
\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
. We also have
\widetilde{\otimes^{m}\phi}|_{G^{n}}=\otimes^{m}\phi
. As
G^{m}\lhd G\wr S_{m}
, any irreducible character of
S_{m}
can be identified as an element of
\mathrm{Irr}(G\wr S_{m})
. It is well known that the irreducible characters of
S_{m}
are in one-one correspondence with integer partitions of
m
. Given
\lambda\vdash m
, we denote
\rho_{\lambda}
to be the irreducible character associated with
\lambda
.
Given
\lambda\vdash n
and
\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, the character

(11)

\widetilde{\phi^{m}_{\lambda}}:=\widetilde{\otimes^{m}\phi}\otimes\rho_{\lambda},

is an irreducible character of
G\wr S_{m}
. Every character in
\mathrm{Irr}(G\wr S_{n})
can be expressed in terms of elements of
\{\widetilde{\phi^{m}_{\lambda}}\ :\ m\leq n\ \&\ \lambda\vdash m\}
.

Given an
\mathrm{Irr}(G)
-valued function
f
on
[n]
, define
\chi_{f}:=\bigotimes\limits_{i=1}^{n}f(i)
. It is well known that given
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{n})
, there is an
\mathrm{Irr}(G)
-valued function
f
on
[n]
such that
\chi=\chi_{f}
. Irreducible characters of
G\wr S_{n}
can be characterized using the well-known method of “little groups” due to Wigner and Mackey. Given
g\in G\wr S_{n}
and
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G^{n})
,
\chi^{g}
is the character satisfying
\chi^{g}(\mathbf{x})=\chi(g^{-1}\mathbf{x}g)
, for all
\mathbf{x}\in G^{n}
. By a
conjugate
of
\chi
, we mean a character of the form
\chi^{g}
, for some
g\in G\wr S_{n}
. The
inertia subgroup
of
\chi
is the group
\{g\in G\wr S_{n}\ :\ \chi^{g}=\chi\}

The set
\{\chi_{f\circ\pi}\ :\ \pi\in S_{n}\}
is the set of conjugates of
\chi_{f}
. Given
A\subseteq[n]
, let
S_{A}\leq S_{n}
denote the pointwise stabilizer of
[n]\setminus A
. Given a disjoint partition
\mathcal{B}
of
[n]
, let
S_{\mathcal{B}}\leq S_{n}
be the subgroup of permutations preserving
\mathcal{B}
. A
Young subgroup
is a subgroup of the form
S_{\mathcal{B}}
, for some partition
\mathcal{B}
of
[n]
.
Let
\mathcal{B}_{f}
be the partition
\{f^{-1}(\phi)\ :\ \phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)\}
. Then the inertia subgroup of
\chi_{f}
in
G\wr S_{n}
, is precisely the group
G\wr S_{\mathcal{B}_{f}}
. Consider a partition-valued function
\Lambda
on
\mathrm{Irr}(G)
such that
\Lambda(\phi)
is an integer partition of
|f^{-1}(\phi)|
, for all
\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
. Then
\widetilde{\phi^{|f^{-1}(\phi)|}_{\Lambda(\phi)}}\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\wr S_{f^{-1}(\phi)})
(cf. (
11
)), and thus

\bigotimes_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\left(\widetilde{\phi^{|f^{-1}(\phi)|}_{\Lambda(\phi)}}\right)\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\wr S_{\mathcal{B}_{f}})

is an irreducible character of the inertia group of
\chi_{f}
, whose restriction to
G^{n}
has
\chi_{f}
as an irreducible summand.

Now, by Clifford theory, given
f:[n]\to\mathrm{Irr}(G)
and given a partition-valued function
\Lambda
on
\mathrm{Irr}(G)
with
|\Lambda(\phi)|=|f^{-1}(\phi)|
, for all
\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, we have

(12)

\psi_{f,\Lambda}:=\mathrm{Ind}_{G\wr S_{\mathcal{B}_{f}}}^{G\wr S_{n}}\left[\bigotimes_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\left(\widetilde{\phi^{|f^{-1}(\phi)|}_{\Lambda(\phi)}}\right)\right]\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\wr S_{n}).

Observing that we have as many distinct
\psi_{f,\Lambda}
s as the number of conjugacy classes in
G\wr S_{n}
, we note that every irreducible character of
G\wr S_{n}
is of the form
\psi_{f,\Lambda}
. Before moving on, we note that given
\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
and
\lambda\vdash n
, we have
\widetilde{\phi^{n}_{\lambda}}=\psi_{f_{\phi},\Lambda_{\phi,\lambda}}
, where
f_{\phi}:[n]\to\mathrm{Irr}(G)
is such that
f_{\phi}(i)=\phi
, for all
i\in[n]
, and
\Lambda_{\phi,\lambda}
is the partition-valued function on
\mathrm{Irr}(G)
which takes
\phi
to
\lambda
and every other irreducible character to the zero partition.

Taking Theorem
3.3
into consideration, it helps to compute quantities of the form
\dfrac{\psi(g)-\overline{\psi(g)}}{\psi(1)}
, for
g\in G
and
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
. The first such computations we need is the following result.

Lemma 9.1
.

Consider
f:[n]\to\mathrm{Irr}(G)
and a partition-valued function
\Lambda
on
\mathrm{Irr}(G)
satisfying
|\Lambda(\phi)|=|f^{-1}(\phi)|
, for all
\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
. Then, we have

\dfrac{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(((x,e,\ldots,e);(1)))-\overline{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(((x,e,\ldots,e);(1)))}}{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)}=\sum\limits_{\mu\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\dfrac{|f^{-1}(\mu)|(\mu(x)-\overline{\mu(x)})}{n\mu(1)}.

Proof.

We recall that the set
\{\chi_{f\circ\pi}\ :\ \pi\in S_{n}\}
is the set of conjugates of
\chi_{f}
.
Using Clifford theory(
[
12
, Theorems 6.2 and 6.11]
), we have

(13)

\mathrm{Res}^{G\wr S_{n}}_{G^{n}}\psi_{f,\Lambda}=\prod\limits_{\phi\in f\left([n]\right)}\rho_{f,\phi}(1)\times\left[\sum\limits_{h\in\{f\circ\pi\ :\ \pi\in S_{n}\}}\chi_{h}\right].

By a conjugate of
f
, we mean an element of the set
\{f\circ\pi\ :\ \pi\in S_{n}\}
. Given
i\in[n]
and
\mu\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, let
\|f\|_{\mu}
denote the number of conjugates
h
of
f
, with
h(1)=\mu
. Elementary counting arguments yield that

\|f\|_{\mu}=\dfrac{(n-1)!}{\left(|f^{-1}(\mu)|-1\right)!\prod\limits_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)\setminus\{\mu\}}|f^{-1}(\phi)|!}

Using (
13
), we have

\displaystyle\psi_{f,\Lambda}(((x,e,\ldots,e);(1)))

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\rho_{\Lambda(\phi)}(1)\times\left[\sum\limits_{\mu\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\|f\|_{\mu}\mu(x)\prod\limits_{\nu\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)\setminus\{\mu\}}\nu(1).\right]

The result follows by noting that

\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)=\left(\prod\limits_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\rho_{\phi,f}(1)\phi(1)\right)\times\|f\|_{\mu}\times n\times|f^{-1}(\mu)|^{-1}.

∎

We also make use of the following formula.

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
f:[n]\to\mathrm{Irr}(G)
be a function, let
\Lambda
a partition-valued function on
\mathrm{Irr}(G)
satisfying
|\Lambda(\phi)|=|f^{-1}(\phi)|
, for all
\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, and let
\pi\in S_{n}
be an
n
-cycle.
Then the following statements are true for all
\mathbf{x}\in G^{n}
.

(1)

If
|f([n])|>1
, then

\dfrac{\psi_{f,\Lambda}((\mathbf{x};\pi)-\overline{\psi_{f,\Lambda}((\mathbf{x};\pi)}}{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)}=0;

and

(2)

if
f([n])=\{\phi\}
, then

|G|^{n-1}\times\dfrac{\psi_{f,\Lambda}((\mathbf{x};\pi)-\overline{\psi_{f,\Lambda}((\mathbf{x};\pi)}}{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)}=k_{f,\Lambda}|Z(G)|^{(n-1)}\dfrac{\phi\left(\mathbf{x}_{\pi}\right)-\overline{\phi\left(\mathbf{x}_{\pi}\right)}}{\phi(1)\times(n-1)!},

for some
k_{f,\Lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

We recall from the definition (
12
) that
\psi_{f,\Lambda}
is a character induced from the subgroup
G\wr S_{\mathcal{B}_{f}}
, where
S_{\mathcal{B}_{f}}
is the subgroup of
S_{n}
preserving the partition
\mathcal{B}_{f}=\{f^{-1}(\mu)\ :\mu\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)\}
of
[n]
. We note that
S_{\mathcal{B}_{f}}
contains an
n
-cycle if and only if
|f([n])|=1
. Therefore, in case
|f([n])|>1
, no element of
G\wr S_{n}
is conjugate to
(\mathbf{x};\pi)
. Now, the Frobenius formula for induced characters implies part (1).

We now assume that
f([n])=\{\phi\}
. In this case, we have
S_{\mathcal{B}_{f}}=S_{n}
;
\lambda:=\Lambda(\phi)\vdash n
and
\Lambda(\mu)=[0]
, for all
\mu\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)\setminus\{\phi\}
; and
\psi_{f,\Lambda}=\widetilde{\phi^{n}_{\lambda}}
. Using the well-known formula (see
[
13
, Lemma 4.3.9]
or
[
16
,
(8.2)
, Appendix B]
) for values of
\widetilde{\phi^{n}_{\lambda}}
, we have

\displaystyle|G|^{n-1}\times\dfrac{\psi_{f,\Lambda}((\mathbf{x};\pi)-\overline{\psi_{f,\Lambda}((\mathbf{x};\pi)}}{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)}

\displaystyle=|G|^{n-1}\times\dfrac{\rho_{\lambda}(\pi)\phi(\mathbf{x}_{\pi})-\overline{\rho_{\lambda}(\pi)\phi(\mathbf{x}_{\pi})}}{\phi^{n}(1)\rho_{\lambda}(1)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\rho_{\lambda}(\pi)}{\rho_{\lambda}(1)}\times\dfrac{|G|^{n-1}}{\phi^{n}(1)}\dfrac{\phi(\mathbf{x}_{\pi})-\overline{\phi(\mathbf{x}_{\pi})}}{\phi(1)}.

It is well known that
\frac{|G|}{\phi(1)}\equiv 0\pmod{|Z(G)|}
. (see
[
12
, 3.12]
). There are exactly
(n-1)!
number of
n
-cycles in
S_{n}
. Using a well-known result
[
12
, Theorem 3.7]
on integrality of character values and the fact that irreducible characters of
S_{n}
take on integer values, it follows that
\dfrac{\rho_{\lambda}(\pi)\times(n-1)!}{\rho_{\lambda}(1)}
is an integer. Thus, the quantity
k_{f,\Lambda}=\dfrac{|G|^{n-1}}{\phi^{n-1}(1)|Z(G)|^{n-1}}\times\dfrac{\rho_{\lambda}(\pi)\times(n-1)!}{{\rho_{\lambda}(1)}}
. This concludes the proof of part (2).
∎

Theorem 9.3
.

Let
n\geq 2
be a natural number and let
X:=\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
be an oriented normal Cayley graph that admits PST from
e
to
z\in Z(G)
, at a time
\tau
.
Let

(14a)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{i\in[n]}\left\{((x_{1},\ldots x_{n});(1))\ :\ x_{i}\in C\ \&\ x_{j}=e,\ \forall j\neq i\right\}

(14b)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\mathbf{x};\pi)\ :\ \text{$\pi$ is an $n$-cycle}\ \&\ \mathbf{x}_{\pi}\in C\right\}.

Then
X\wr n:=\mathrm{Cay}(G\wr S_{n},\ \tilde{C}_{1}\cup\tilde{C}_{2})
admits PST at time
\tau
from
(e,e,\ldots,e)
to
(z,z,\ldots,z)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.3
, it suffices to show that

\dfrac{\overline{\psi(z,\ldots,z)}}{\psi(1)}=\exp\left(\tau\theta_{\psi}\right),

for all
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\wr S_{n})
. Here,

(15)

\theta_{\psi}=\dfrac{\psi(\tilde{C}_{1})+\psi(\tilde{C}_{2})-\overline{\psi(\tilde{C}_{1})-\psi(\tilde{C}_{2})}}{\psi(1)}.

Consider some
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\wr S_{n})
.
Following the description of
\mathrm{Irr}(G\wr S_{n})
given at the start of this section, given
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\wr S_{n})
, there exists
f:[n]\to\mathrm{Irr}{G}
and a partition-valued function
\Lambda
on
\mathrm{Irr}(G)
with
|\Lambda(\phi)|=|f^{-1}(\phi)|
(for all
\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
) such that
\psi=\psi_{f,\Lambda}
(as defined in (
12
)). We first compute
\theta_{\psi_{f,\Lambda}}
.

Case 1:

Assume that
|f([n])|>1
. Using Lemma
9.2
, it follows that

\theta_{\psi_{f,\Lambda}}=\dfrac{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(\tilde{C}_{1}))-\overline{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(\tilde{C}_{1})}}{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)}.

We note that every element of
\tilde{C}_{1}
is conjugate to an element of the form
((x,e,\ldots,e);(1))
for some
x\in C
, and that the number of
S_{n}
-conjugates in
\tilde{C}_{1}
of the element
((x,e,\ldots,e);(1))
(with
x\in C
) is
n
. It now follows from Lemma
9.1
that

\displaystyle\dfrac{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(\tilde{C}_{1})}{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)}=n\sum\limits_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\dfrac{|f^{-1}(\phi)|\phi(C)}{n\phi(1)},

and therefore, if
\theta_{\phi}
is the eigenvalue associated with
\phi
, of
\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
, we have

(16)

\theta_{\psi_{f,\Lambda}}=\sum\limits_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}|f^{-1}(\phi)|\theta_{\phi},\ \text{provided $|f([n]|>1$.}

Case 2:

Now, we assume that
f([n])=\{\phi\}
. Set
\lambda:=\Lambda(\phi)
. Fix an
n
-cycle,
\sigma\in S_{n}
. We note that every element in
\tilde{C}_{2}
is conjugate to an element of the form
(\mathbf{x};\sigma)
with
\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}\in C
. We count that for each
c\in C
, that
X_{c}:=\{(\mathbf{x};\sigma)\ :\mathbf{x}_{\sigma}=c\}
is a set of size
|G|^{n-1}
. Since every element of
X_{c}
lies in the same conjugacy class, using Lemma
9.2
, we have

(17)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{g\in X_{c}}\dfrac{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(g)-\overline{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(g)}}{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)}

\displaystyle=k_{f,\lambda}|Z(G)|^{n-1}\dfrac{\phi(c)-\overline{\phi(c)}}{\phi(1)\times(n-1)!},

for some
k_{f,\Lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We recall that there are exactly
(n-1)!
number of
n
-cycles in
S_{n}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(\tilde{C}_{2})-\overline{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(\tilde{C}_{2})}}{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)}

\displaystyle=(n-1)!\sum\limits_{c\in C}\sum\limits_{g\in X_{c}}\dfrac{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(g)-\overline{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(g)}}{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{c\in C}k_{f,\Lambda}|Z(G)|^{n-1}\dfrac{\phi(c)-\overline{\phi(c)}}{\phi(1)}\ \text{(using \eqref{eq:formulac2})}

\displaystyle=k_{f,\Lambda}|Z(G)|^{n-1}\theta_{\phi}.

Arguing as in Case 1, using Lemma
9.1
, we have

\dfrac{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(\tilde{C}_{1})-\overline{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(\tilde{C}_{1})}}{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)}=n\theta_{\phi},

and therefore, we have

(18)

\theta_{\psi_{f,\Lambda}}=n\theta_{\phi}+k_{f,\Lambda}|Z(G)|^{n-1}\theta_{\phi},\ \text{, for some $k_{f,\Lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}$, provided $f([n])=\{\phi\}$.}

Using, (
15
), (
16
) and (
18
), we have

(19)

\displaystyle\exp\left(\tau\theta_{\psi_{f,\Lambda}}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\prod\limits_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\left[\exp\left(\tau\theta_{\phi}\right)\right]^{|f^{-1}(\phi)|}&\text{if $|f([n])|>1$,}\\
\exp{(\tau\theta_{\phi})}^{n}\times\exp(\tau\theta_{\phi})^{k_{\psi}|Z(G)|^{n-1}}&\text{if $f([n])=\{\phi\}$.}\end{cases}

Since
\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
admits PST from
e
to
z
at time
\tau
, by Theorem
3.3
, we have

\exp\left(\tau\theta_{\phi}\right)=\frac{\overline{\phi(z)}}{\phi(1)},

for all
\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
. Applying the above in (
19
), yields

\displaystyle\exp\left(\tau\theta_{\psi_{f,\Lambda}}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\prod\limits_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\left(\frac{\overline{\phi(z)}}{\phi(1)}\right)^{|f^{-1}(\phi)|}&\text{if $|f([n])|>1$,}\\
\left(\dfrac{\overline{\phi(z)}}{\phi(1)}\right)^{n}\times\left(\dfrac{\overline{\phi(z)}}{\phi(1)}\right)^{k_{f,\Lambda}|Z(G)|^{n-1}}&\text{if $f([n])=\{\phi\}$.}\end{cases}

Since
z\in Z(G)
and since
\dfrac{\overline{\phi(z)}}{\phi(1)}
is a
|z|
-th root of unity, it follows that
\dfrac{\overline{\phi(z)}}{\phi(1)}^{|Z(G)|}=1
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\exp\left(\tau\theta_{\psi_{f,\Lambda}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod\limits_{\phi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)}\left(\dfrac{\overline{\phi(z)}}{\phi(1)}\right)^{|f^{-1}(\phi)|}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\overline{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(z,\ldots,z)}}{\psi_{f,\Lambda}(1)}.

The result now follows from Theorem
3.3
.
∎

Remark
.

Recall that in §
7
, we constructed connected oriented normal Cayley graphs on nonsolvable groups, which admit MST on sets of size
3
. It is natural to ask if there are “nonsolvable” examples in the case of MST on sets of size
4
. Theorem
9.3
helps us construct infinitely many such examples. Let
G
,
\mathcal{C}
,
\tilde{C}_{1}
, and
\tilde{C}_{2}
be as in Theorem
9.3
. We recall the well-known fact that
\mathrm{Cay}(G,\mathcal{C})
has as many (strongly) connected components as the index
[G:\left\langle C\right\rangle]
, with each component being isomorphic to
\mathrm{Cay}(\left\langle C\right\rangle,\ C)
. Assume that
C
generates
G
. We note that
\tilde{C}_{1}
generates
G^{n}
. We recall the fact that
n
-cycles in
S_{n}
generate
S_{n}
when
n
is even and
A_{n}
when
n
is odd. Therefore, the subgroup
\left\langle\tilde{C}_{1}\cup\tilde{C_{2}}\right\rangle
is
G\wr S_{n}
when
n
is even and
G\wr A_{n}
, when
n
is odd.
As
A_{n}
is a nonabelian simple group when
n\geq 5
, both
G\wr S_{n}
and
G\wr A_{n}
are nonsolvable for
n\geq 5
. Theorem
9.3
allows us to construct examples of MST of size 4 in connected oriented normal Cayley graphs of nonsolvable groups. For example, if
X
is one of the examples in
[
8
, 10.2]
which exhibits MST of size 4, then
X\wr 6
, is a connected oriented normal Cayley graph on a group with
S_{6}
as one of its composition factors. Theorem
9.3
asserts that
X\wr 6
admits MST on a set of size
4
.

We end this section by observing that a version of Theorem
9.3
for unoriented normal Cayley graphs holds
mutatis mutandis
. We state the same, without proof.

Theorem 9.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
be a natural number and let
X:=\mathrm{Cay}(G,C)
be a normal Cayley graph that admits PST from
e
to
z\in Z(G)
, at a time
\tau
.
Let

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigcup\limits_{i\in[n]}\left\{((x_{1},\ldots x_{n});(1))\ :\ x_{i}\in C\ \&\ x_{j}=e,\ \forall j\neq i\right\}

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{(\mathbf{x};\pi)\ :\ \text{$\pi$ is an $n$-cycle}\ \&\ \mathbf{x}_{\pi}\in C\right\}.

Then
X\wr n:=\mathrm{Cay}(G\wr S_{n},\ \tilde{C}_{1}\cup\tilde{C}_{2})
admits PST at time
\tau
from
(e,e,\ldots,e)
to
(z,z,\ldots,z)
.
