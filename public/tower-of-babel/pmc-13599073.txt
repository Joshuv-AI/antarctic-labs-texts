Daily temporal associations among AI replacement anxiety, state anxiety, and academic burnout indicators in medicine and nursing students: a 10-day diary study
Authors: Haoqian Li, Zheng Zhang, Bo Yang, Chiahsuan Liu, Jian Sun, Jiansong Zhou, Xia Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly entering medicine and nursing education, raising concerns about how healthcare students respond to AI-related occupational uncertainty. AI replacement anxiety (ARA) may become more or less salient across academic days, but little is known about its short-term within-person temporal associations with state anxiety and day-level academic burnout indicators.MethodsThis 10-day weekday diary study included 751 medicine and nursing students who provided 4,625 observed diary records after quality screening. Daily surveys assessed ARA and three academic burnout constructs using single day-adapted proxy indicators, together with a two-item state-anxiety measure. Two-level Bayesian dynamic structural equation modeling tested six primary dimension-specific models in the ARA-to-state-anxiety and state-anxiety-to-ARA directions. Supplementary joint multivariate models, a stricter 695-participant sensitivity sample, and a targeted calendar-spacing sensitivity analysis examined unique effects, posterior contrasts, and robustness.ResultsIn the ARA-to-state-anxiety direction, higher daily ARA was associated with higher subsequent cynicism, and higher cynicism was associated with later state anxiety. This M2 component pattern remained supported when the weekend interval was represented using calendar-day spacing. In the reverse direction, higher state anxiety was consistently associated with a higher subsequent score on the academic-inefficacy proxy indicator, but the subsequent association of this indicator with ARA was less robust across model specifications and was attenuated in the weekend-spacing sensitivity analysis. The model-based indirect effects remained uncertain, with 95% credible intervals including zero. Joint models indicated that the clearest cross-dimension differences were concentrated mainly in first-stage associations rather than complete unique downstream indirect processes.ConclusionsThe findings support dimension-specific temporal associations among ARA, state anxiety, and academic burnout-related daily indicators, highlighting cynicism and academic-inefficacy-related daily appraisals as particularly relevant components of the temporal links between ARA and state anxiety among medicine and nursing students. These findings provide a more differentiated basis for future research on targeted support and intervention in health-professions education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05275-7.Keywords: AI replacement anxiety, State anxiety, Academic burnout, Daily diary, Dynamic structural equation modeling, Medicine and nursing students

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly transforming higher education and the future of professional work. In university settings, AI is increasingly discussed as a source of support, efficiency, and innovation in teaching and learning [1, 2]. At the same time, generative AI has raised concerns about how students’ learning, assessment, and future professional preparation may change [1]. These concerns may be especially salient in medicine and nursing education. Students in these fields are preparing for occupations traditionally regarded as highly interpersonal and difficult to automate, yet these occupations are now increasingly shaped by AI-assisted diagnosis, intelligent care systems, algorithmic decision support, and automated documentation [3–4]. Recent empirical evidence indicates that AI-related concern is already relevant to future healthcare professionals: medicine students have reported AI-related anxiety and altered specialty preferences when they anticipate displacement or reduced employment opportunities, and nursing students’ anxiety about AI has been linked with attitudes, self-efficacy, and readiness to use AI-based healthcare technologies [5–7]. Under such conditions, concerns about technological replacement are no longer abstract. Rather, they may become psychologically meaningful in students’ everyday academic and career preparation. Building on this context, the present study focuses on AI replacement anxiety (ARA), that is, anxiety-related concern that one’s future role, competence, or work value may be threatened by smart technologies, AI, robotics, or algorithms [8].

A growing body of research suggests that AI-related anxiety is not a unitary phenomenon. Instead, it encompasses concerns about replacement, loss of control, uncertainty, and adaptation to rapid technological change [9–10]. Recent evidence further indicates that AI-related information environments may intensify such concerns through mechanisms such as information exposure, overload, and literacy gaps [11]. However, most existing studies remain cross-sectional and static. They are informative in showing that AI-related concerns are associated with psychological strain, but they are less informative about how these experiences unfold over time in daily life. This limitation is important because the psychological impact of ARA is unlikely to be purely trait-like. For medicine and nursing students, AI-related replacement concerns may become more or less salient across days as students encounter AI-related coursework, use generative-AI tools, hear teachers or peers discuss automated clinical work, read news about changing healthcare roles, or receive academic or clinical feedback that makes competence and future work value especially salient. On such days, students may not only think more about replacement risk, but also experience more immediate worry about whether their current learning, competence, and future professional value are sufficient. Conversely, students’ daily emotional state may also shape how threatening AI feels: when state anxiety is elevated, students may appraise academic demands and future career uncertainty more negatively, making replacement-related concerns more salient than they would be on less anxious days. Studying ARA at the daily level therefore addresses not only whether short-term elevations in AI-related threat are followed by changes in academic functioning and state anxiety, but also whether students’ daily anxiety and academic self-evaluations may feed back into later replacement concern. This distinction is practically meaningful because educational responses to AI-related concerns may need to address proximal academic experiences, competence appraisals, and emotional states, not only stable attitudes toward technology.

One useful framework for understanding these bidirectional processes is Conservation of Resources (COR) theory. COR theory proposes that stress occurs when individuals experience actual resource loss, perceive threats of resource loss, or invest resources without obtaining sufficient return [12]. Later developments of COR theory further emphasize that resource loss plays a primary role in the stress process and may accumulate in loss spirals, making individuals increasingly vulnerable over time [13]. From this perspective, ARA can be understood as a form of resource threat. For students preparing for future careers, AI may be perceived as threatening valued resources such as professional relevance, competence, control, and future occupational security. Such threat perceptions may elicit anxiety directly, but they may also unfold through resource-depleting academic processes. At the same time, COR theory also allows the reverse dynamic: when students experience heightened state anxiety, they may perceive their academic and professional resources as more fragile, and this perceived vulnerability may make AI-related replacement threats more personally relevant. This interpretation is also consistent with adjacent work on technostress, which shows that technology-related demands, uncertainty, and insecurity can generate strain and undermine functioning [14]. Together, COR theory and technostress research suggest that AI-related replacement concerns and state anxiety may reinforce one another through daily academic resource processes, rather than operating only as static individual differences.

A particularly plausible academic process in this context is academic burnout. Academic burnout is multidimensional, and student-focused burnout measures distinguish exhaustion from forms of psychological distancing or disengagement [15, 16]. For the purposes of the present diary study, we focused on three related but non-interchangeable burnout-related experiences: exhaustion, cynicism, and academic inefficacy. Exhaustion captures depletion of emotional and physical energy, cynicism reflects psychological distancing and reduced enthusiasm toward study, and academic inefficacy reflects diminished confidence in one’s learning-related competence. From a COR perspective, these experiences may signal different ways in which academic resources are threatened, depleted, or appraised as insufficient, and may therefore occupy different positions in the temporal association between ARA and state anxiety. Examining these experiences separately is therefore important for clarifying the specific academic processes through which AI-related replacement concerns and state anxiety may become linked, a question that would be obscured if academic burnout were considered only as an undifferentiated global construct.

This dimensional perspective is especially relevant for medicine and nursing students, who are already at elevated risk for academic burnout. Prior research has shown that nursing students frequently experience burnout in the context of heavy workload, emotional burden, professional uncertainty, and insufficient support [17–21]. More recent work has also highlighted the complexity of academic burnout in nursing-related training, showing that students’ experiences often involve not only tiredness, but also disengagement, frustration, and doubts about academic meaning and competence [20–21]. In the context of AI-related uncertainty, these dimensions may be temporally associated with ARA and state anxiety in different ways. In the ARA-to-anxiety direction, perceiving AI as a threat to future professional value may be followed by greater cynicism, reflected in reduced enthusiasm toward academic investment, which may in turn be associated with later state anxiety. In the reverse direction, heightened daily anxiety may be followed by a more negative appraisal of learning-related effectiveness or engagement, which could coincide with later replacement concern. These sequences are theoretically plausible but were treated as candidate temporal component associations rather than established mechanisms. COR theory also supports a competing possibility: sustained threat monitoring and coping with AI-related uncertainty may deplete energetic resources and increase exhaustion. Thus, we expected the burnout dimensions to show non-uniform temporal associations across directions, while treating relative differences among exhaustion, cynicism, and academic inefficacy as theoretically motivated and exploratory rather than as a fixed hierarchy.

A second limitation of the existing literature is methodological. Most studies on AI-related anxiety, burnout, and student well-being rely on cross-sectional or conventional longitudinal designs, which are poorly suited to detecting short-term within-person processes. Yet the associations of interest here may be daily, context-sensitive, and temporally ordered. Daily diary designs are therefore particularly valuable because they distinguish stable between-person differences from within-person day-to-day fluctuations [22]. In turn, dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM) provides a suitable analytic framework for estimating lagged component associations, autoregressive carryover, and between-person heterogeneity within the same multilevel model [23]. For the present research question, this approach makes it possible to examine both whether day-specific elevations in ARA are followed by changes in academic burnout indicators and state anxiety on subsequent scheduled days and whether day-specific elevations in state anxiety are followed by changes in burnout indicators that are associated with later ARA.

Accordingly, the present study used a 10-day weekday diary design and two-level DSEM to examine direction-specific daily temporal associations among ARA, academic burnout indicators, and state anxiety among medicine and nursing students. Rather than treating burnout as a single global construct, we examined exhaustion, cynicism, and an academic-inefficacy-related indicator separately. This design allowed us to address two central questions: (a) whether day-specific elevations in ARA were followed by changes in each burnout indicator and then by later state anxiety; and (b) whether day-specific elevations in state anxiety were followed by changes in each burnout indicator and then by later ARA. The second question was included to examine whether students’ daily emotional distress and burnout-related academic appraisals were also temporally associated with subsequent AI-related replacement concern. The six separate dimension-specific models constituted the primary analyses of these candidate component associations, whereas supplementary joint models tested whether apparent dimension-specific patterns remained after overlap among the burnout indicators was considered.

Method

Participants and procedure

This study used an intensive longitudinal daily diary design to capture short-term within-person fluctuations in psychological experiences. Data were collected between May and June 2025 across 10 scheduled weekdays over two consecutive workweeks (Monday through Friday); no surveys were administered on weekends. On each study day, participants received a reminder through their class WeChat group between 7:00 PM and 9:00 PM. The survey remained available until midnight and was then closed, so entries could not be completed retrospectively the following day. A separate survey link was used for each study day. The six focal diary items and their response formats remained identical across the 10 assessments; each survey also included two instructed-response attention checks. The online platform restricted each device to one submission per day. The exported dataset retained the scheduled study day but not the exact clock time of submission; therefore, variation in the precise evening completion time could not be examined.

Participants were recruited from undergraduate medicine and nursing programs at a university in northern China. Recruitment was facilitated by class advisors, who distributed the study invitation through official WeChat class groups. Participation was voluntary, and no monetary or material incentives were provided. To link repeated diary reports while minimizing identifying information, participants entered the last four digits of their mobile phone number; these digits were used only to match reports across days and were replaced by an anonymous study ID in the analytic dataset. Although recruitment included both medicine and nursing programs, individual-level disciplinary affiliation was not retained in the analytic dataset.

A total of 1,099 students submitted at least one diary survey. We first removed diary entries completed in one minute or less. We then counted each participant’s remaining submissions and retained participants with at least three submissions at this stage. This participant-level criterion excluded 348 students and yielded 751 participants with 4,950 diary records. No participant was excluded on the basis of age. Each daily survey contained two instructed-response attention checks placed at separate positions. Each attention check asked participants to select a specified response option rather than provide a substantive self-report response. Failure of either attention check resulted in deletion of that day’s entire diary record, rather than exclusion of the participant. This day-level screening removed 325 records, leaving 4,625 observed diary records for the primary analyses. Because the at-least-three-day criterion was applied before attention-check exclusions, 56 retained participants subsequently had fewer than three observed records (31 had one record and 25 had two records). To evaluate robustness to a stricter inclusion threshold, a sensitivity sample re-applied the at-least-three-valid-record criterion after all day-level quality-control exclusions, yielding 695 participants and 4,544 observed records.

All diary items were mandatory, so submitted records contained no item-level missing values on the focal variables. Missingness occurred at the day level because a scheduled diary was not submitted or because a submitted record was removed during quality screening. The primary sample therefore contained 4,625 observed records across 7,510 planned participant-days, leaving 2,885 unavailable participant-day observations (38.4%). Reasons for noncompletion were not assessed at each missing occasion, so the possibility that daily nonresponse was related to participants’ psychological states could not be evaluated directly.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board. Before the first survey, all students read an electronic informed-consent form and could proceed only after indicating agreement. The form described the study purpose, confidentiality procedures, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw without penalty. The approved protocol permitted enrolled university students aged 17 years to provide electronic self-consent without separate guardian consent.

Measures

All six focal diary items were preceded by a common instruction asking participants to report their actual experiences during the current day, and each item itself contained an explicit current-day reference. The item content and response format remained unchanged across the 10 assessments. To minimize repeated-assessment burden, ARA and each academic burnout dimension were represented by one day-adapted proxy indicator, whereas state anxiety was represented by two items. Throughout this article, the term “proxy indicator” is used deliberately to distinguish these brief observed items from comprehensive multi-item measures of the corresponding constructs. A proxy indicator was intended to index a narrow, theoretically relevant aspect of a broader construct at the day level rather than to reproduce a validated state scale or a complete subscale score. This terminology reflects both the practical need to minimize repeated-assessment burden and the measurement limitations inherent in single-item assessment, including restricted construct breadth and the inability to estimate conventional internal consistency. Brief indicators can be useful in intensive longitudinal research when repeated administration of full scales would substantially increase burden; however, their adequacy depends on the construct and item [22, 24]. Accordingly, psychometric evidence for the parent instruments is reported below as background information but was not assumed to establish the reliability or validity of the present day-adapted proxy indicators. Exact English equivalents of the administered Chinese items, response anchors, and scoring procedures are also provided in Supplementary Note 1.

Daily AI replacement anxiety (ARA)

ARA was assessed with one day-adapted proxy indicator based on the replacement-concern content of the Smart Technology, Artificial Intelligence, Robotics, and Algorithms (STARA) awareness scale [8]. The original four-item STARA awareness measure loaded on one factor that explained 71.9% of the variance, with factor loadings ranging from 0.700 to 0.922 and internal consistency of α = 0.85 [8]. The item administered in the present diary was: “I worry that what I learned today and what I did today may be replaced by AI in the future.” Responses ranged from 1 (not at all applicable) to 5 (completely applicable), with higher scores indicating greater ARA on that day. The item was selected because it directly represented replacement concern and could be answered with reference to the current day.

Daily state anxiety

State anxiety was assessed with two current-day mood items-“I felt anxious today” and “I felt worried today”-that were conceptually aligned with the tension-anxiety domain of the Profile of Mood States (POMS) and had been used in prior daily-diary research [25–26]. The parent POMS Short Form has shown internal consistency comparable to the original POMS and correlations exceeding 0.95 between corresponding short- and full-form subscale scores [27]. Participants rated the intensity of each feeling from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very strongly). The two items were summed, yielding scores from 2 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater state anxiety that day.

Daily academic burnout

Daily academic burnout was represented by three day-adapted proxy indicators, with each indicator informed by an established measure selected for its conceptual correspondence with the targeted burnout-related experience. The exhaustion indicator was adapted from the exhaustion dimension of the student version of the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory, which distinguishes exhaustion from disengagement and has been validated for assessing academic burnout in student samples [15]. The cynicism indicator was adapted from the mental-distance dimension of the Burnout Assessment Tool for Students, which captures reluctance, psychological distancing, and cynicism toward study [16]. The academic-inefficacy indicator was informed by the Competence domain of the Academic Self-Efficacy Scale for Filipino Junior High School Students, a multidimensional measure of academic self-efficacy with evidence of reliability and convergent and concurrent validity [28]. Because only one content-relevant item from each parent measure was adapted for repeated day-level assessment, these indicators were not administered, scored, or interpreted as complete subscales of their respective parent measures.

Specifically, exhaustion was indexed by “When I got up this morning, I felt tired at the prospect of facing today’s studies.” This wording was adapted to the current-day academic context from an Oldenburg Burnout Inventory–Student Version exhaustion item describing feeling tired before arriving at class or beginning to study [15]. Cynicism was indexed by “Today, my enthusiasm for studying decreased.” This indicator was adapted to the current-day context from the Burnout Assessment Tool for Students mental-distance item referring to difficulty finding enthusiasm for one’s studies [16]. The academic-inefficacy proxy indicator was “Today, I did not learn anything interesting in my studies.” This indicator was informed by Item 19 of the Competence domain of the Academic Self-Efficacy Scale for Filipino Junior High School Students, which assesses confidence in mastering the concepts and topics taught in class [28]. For the diary, the general positive competence appraisal was reformulated as a negative current-day appraisal of the learning experience. Participants received the item directly in this negative form, and no post hoc numerical reverse scoring was applied. Its content is narrower than the broader construct of academic inefficacy and may also reflect a negative learning appraisal, reduced interest, boredom, or disengagement. All three burnout-related indicators were rated from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating more of the targeted burnout-related experience on that day. The indicators were analyzed separately and were not combined into a total burnout score.

Data analytic strategy

Data preprocessing was conducted in R 4.4.2, and the multilevel time-series analyses were performed in Mplus 8.3. Two-level dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM) was used to separate stable between-person differences from within-person deviations and to estimate lagged temporal associations, autoregressive carryover, and between-person heterogeneity in the within-person paths [23–29]. Scheduled assessments were coded DAY = 1–10. The primary sample contained 4,625 observed records across 7,510 planned participant-days. With participants specified as clusters and TINTERVAL = DAY(1), an unobserved scheduled weekday between two retained reports remained an all-missing occasion rather than causing the reports on either side to be treated as adjacent. This procedure created 889 all-missing scheduled occasions between participants’ first and last retained assessments, yielding 5,514 analysis-grid occasions. Scheduled days before a participant’s first retained report or after their last retained report were not represented as internal all-missing occasions in the DSEM grid, and no response values were imputed. Accordingly, lag 1 denotes the next scheduled weekday assessment and lag 2 denotes two scheduled assessment intervals. The fixed Day 5-to-Day 6 transition crossed the weekend and therefore covered a longer calendar interval than the other lag-1 transitions; the primary coefficients should consequently be understood as next-scheduled-assessment associations rather than uniformly one-calendar-day effects.

The six separate DSEM models constituted the primary dimension-specific analyses (the framework is shown in Fig. 1). In Models 1–3, ARA at time t-2 predicted one academic burnout proxy indicator at time t-1, which in turn predicted state anxiety at time t; Models 1, 2, and 3 used exhaustion, cynicism, and the academic-inefficacy proxy indicator, respectively. Models 4–6 tested the reverse ordering, with state anxiety at time t-2 predicting the corresponding burnout indicator at time t-1 and ARA at time t. Because each burnout indicator was entered in a separate model, these analyses estimate marginal dimension-specific temporal component associations. They were not used to infer that a coefficient in one separate model was statistically larger than a coefficient in another.

At the within-person level, each separate model included the first-stage path from the predictor to the burnout indicator (AP), the second-stage path from the burnout indicator to the outcome (BP), the lag-2 direct path from predictor to outcome (CP), and lag-1 autoregressive paths for all focal variables. At each scheduled occasion, the within-person innovation residuals of ARA, state anxiety, and the relevant burnout indicator were allowed to covary pairwise. These terms modeled same-occasion innovation residual associations; they did not remove unmeasured time-varying common causes operating across multiple days. In Models 2–6, AP, BP, and CP were specified as random slopes. At the between-person level, age and gender predicted AP, BP, CP, the person-specific mean of ARA, and the person-specific mean of the relevant burnout indicator; the state-anxiety mean was not uniformly regressed on age and gender in the separate-model syntax. The AP-BP random-slope covariance was estimated. Thus, the model-based average indirect effect was computed as E(AP x BP) = E(AP)E(BP) + Cov(AP, BP), rather than as the simple product of the two mean slopes. These indirect effects were reported on the unstandardized model scale, whereas the core path coefficients were reported as within-level standardized estimates averaged over clusters. In repaired M1, BP was fixed, age and gender predicted AP, CP, and the indirect effect was E(AP) x BP.

Bayesian estimation was used with the Mplus default prior specification. The original Models 1–6 requested 10,000 iterations with two chains and thinning set to 1. Convergence was evaluated from normal model termination and the TECH8 potential scale reduction (PSR) history, using PSR < 1.10 as the prespecified criterion. The original fully random M1 did not converge: its final PSR was 2.388, and the unstable parameter was the BP random-slope variance. A targeted repair therefore fixed BP while retaining AP and CP as random slopes; no priors or other substantive paths were changed. The repaired model used four chains and requested up to 20,000 iterations, terminated normally after the automatic convergence criterion was reached, and had a final PSR of 1.080. Final PSRs for M2-M6 were 1.053, 1.078, 1.060, 1.065, and 1.072, respectively. The joint models also met the prespecified criterion (J1 = 1.031; J2 = 1.040), as did the strict-sample sensitivity models (M2 = 1.065; M6 = 1.082). Satisfactory model-level convergence was not taken to imply precise estimation of each participant’s person-specific dynamic slopes.

To permit formal cross-dimension comparisons, two supplementary joint multivariate DSEM models were also estimated. J1 simultaneously included exhaustion, cynicism, and the academic-inefficacy proxy indicator in the ARA -> burnout indicators -> state anxiety direction; J2 included all three indicators in the reverse direction. These models estimated the unique AP and BP paths for each indicator after accounting for overlap among the three burnout indicators, together with pairwise posterior contrasts among corresponding AP paths, BP paths, and model-based indirect effects. Age and gender were grand-mean centered in the joint models so that random-slope means and their contrasts were evaluated at the sample-average covariate profile; the within-person model structure was otherwise unchanged. Both joint models used four chains and 5,000 fixed Bayesian iterations. The separate and joint models therefore address complementary estimands: marginal dimension-specific component associations versus unique paths and formal dimension contrasts. Because the joint models more directly evaluate dimension-specific uniqueness, their findings were used to define the evidential boundary of the separate-model patterns.

Bayesian DSEM used all available observed records under an ignorable-missingness assumption conditional on the modeled variables and scheduled time structure; this assumption cannot be verified directly from the observed data. As a stricter inclusion-threshold sensitivity analysis, Models 2 and 6—the two separate models underlying the principal sequential component patterns—were re-estimated with their original specifications in the 695 participants who retained at least three valid observations after all day-level quality-control exclusions. This sample contained 4,544 observed records and 837 all-missing scheduled occasions, yielding a 5,381-occasion analysis grid. The strict-sample analysis tests sensitivity to retaining participants with very few final valid records; it does not test whether individual missing diary occasions were related to participants’ psychological states. Because the analyses were Bayesian and exploratory, no frequentist family-wise alpha correction was applied. We instead used 95% credible intervals, conducted direct posterior contrasts in the joint models, and avoided treating a difference in statistical significance across separate models as evidence of a statistically significant coefficient difference.

To quantify the sequential information available for the lagged analyses, we additionally audited observed response patterns in the final data. The 751-participant primary sample contained 3,345 observed lag-1 consecutive pairs, 2,799 lag-2 endpoint pairs, and 2,479 complete three-occasion sequences with observed data at t − 2, t − 1, and t; 687, 624, and 560 participants, respectively, contributed at least one such pattern. Participants with a single final valid record could not directly contribute an observed lagged transition, and participants with two records could contribute a lag-1 or lag-2 pair only when the records occurred at the corresponding scheduled spacing; neither group could contribute a complete three-occasion sequence. In the 695-participant strict sample, 3,337 lag-1 pairs, 2,795 lag-2 endpoint pairs, and the same 2,479 complete three-occasion sequences remained. These counts are descriptive indices of observed sequential availability rather than counts of records exclusively used by the hierarchical likelihood.

We also conducted a targeted calendar-spacing sensitivity analysis for M2 and M6 to address the longer Day 5-to-Day 6 interval. In this analysis, the first workweek retained calendar positions 1–5 and the second workweek was coded as positions 8–12, so that Saturday and Sunday were represented as unobserved calendar occasions. TINTERVAL remained one calendar-day unit, and the Friday-to-Monday transition was therefore no longer treated as an ordinary lag-1 transition. M2 and M6 were selected because they were the two separate models underlying the principal two-component patterns in the primary analyses.

Results

Descriptive statistics and multilevel structure

Following the screening procedures described above, the primary analytic sample comprised 751 participants aged 17 to 27 years (M = 19.90, SD = 1.51), of whom 220 (29.3%) were male and 531 (70.7%) were female. These participants contributed 4,625 observed diary records after all day-level quality-control exclusions (M = 6.16 records per participant, range = 1–10), corresponding to 61.6% of the 7,510 planned participant-days and leaving 38.4% unavailable. TINTERVAL retained 889 all-missing scheduled occasions between participants’ first and last retained assessments, so the DSEM output reported a 5,514-occasion analysis grid. The observation-pattern audit identified 3,345 observed lag-1 pairs, 2,799 lag-2 endpoint pairs, and 2,479 complete three-occasion sequences in the primary sample. The strict 695-participant sample retained 3,337 lag-1 pairs, 2,795 lag-2 endpoint pairs, and all 2,479 complete three-occasion sequences.

Table 1 summarizes descriptive statistics, ICCs, and within-/between-person correlations. ICCs ranged from 0.580 to 0.706, including 0.706 for ARA (29.4% within-person variance). Within-person correlations were positive but small to modest (r = .077–0.252), whereas between-person correlations were stronger, particularly for cynicism and the academic-inefficacy proxy indicator (r = .845). The ICCs indicate that the focal variables contained substantial between-person clustering while also retaining meaningful within-person day-to-day variability, underscoring the importance of separating the two levels in the multilevel analyses.

Within-person DSEM results

Core within-person DSEM estimates from the six separate models are presented in Table 2; Fig. 2. In repaired M1, none of the exhaustion component coefficients was supported (AP = − 0.015, 95% CrI [-0.065, 0.039]; BP = 0.010, 95% CrI [-0.033, 0.048]; CP = 0.003, 95% CrI [-0.063, 0.058]). In the ARA-to-state-anxiety direction, M2 was the only separate model in which both component associations were supported: higher ARA was associated with higher cynicism at the next scheduled assessment (AP = 0.068, 95% CrI [0.016, 0.119]), and higher cynicism was associated with higher state anxiety at the following scheduled assessment (BP = 0.067, 95% CrI [0.019, 0.112]); CP was not supported (CP = − 0.011, 95% CrI [-0.068, 0.046]). In M3, the ARA-to-academic-inefficacy-proxy association was not supported, whereas a higher score on the academic-inefficacy proxy indicator was associated with later state anxiety (BP = 0.126, 95% CrI [0.074, 0.180]).

Joint and sensitivity analyses were used to evaluate the evidential boundaries of the separate-model findings. In J1, ARA uniquely predicted cynicism, and the ARA-to-cynicism path exceeded the ARA-to-exhaustion path; no unique positive downstream indirect pathway was credible. In J2, state anxiety uniquely predicted cynicism and the academic-inefficacy proxy indicator, and the anxiety-to-academic-inefficacy-proxy path exceeded the corresponding exhaustion and cynicism paths; no unique BP path or positive indirect effect completed a downstream mediation process. In the stricter 695-participant sample, M2 replicated both component associations, whereas M6 showed the same directional pattern but the academic-inefficacy-proxy-to-ARA interval was at the rounding boundary (Supplementary Tables S4-S6). Thus, the joint and strict-sample results already indicated greater uncertainty around the second-stage reverse-direction association.

The targeted weekend-spacing sensitivity analysis provided a further robustness check (Supplementary Table S9). In M2, both component associations remained supported after calendar-day spacing was used: ARA was associated with subsequent cynicism (AP = 0.075, 95% CrI [0.029, 0.129]) and cynicism was associated with subsequent state anxiety (BP = 0.056, 95% CrI [0.007, 0.111]); CP remained unsupported (CP = − 0.035, 95% CrI [-0.107, 0.052]), and the final PSR was 1.092. In M6, the first-stage association from state anxiety to the academic-inefficacy proxy indicator remained supported (AP = 0.086, 95% CrI [0.044, 0.126]), whereas the subsequent association from the proxy indicator to ARA was attenuated and its credible interval included zero (BP = 0.036, 95% CrI [-0.012, 0.083]); CP was unsupported (CP = 0.007, 95% CrI [-0.046, 0.068]), and the final PSR was 1.012. The M2 component pattern was therefore comparatively robust to the alternative time metric, whereas the M6 second-stage association was less robust across specifications.

Discussion

This 10-day daily diary study examined population-average within-person temporal associations among day-to-day elevations in AI replacement anxiety (ARA), state anxiety, and academic burnout-related proxy indicators among medicine and nursing students. Three features of the results were most informative. First, in the ARA-to-state-anxiety direction, higher ARA was followed by higher cynicism and higher cynicism was followed by higher state anxiety; both component associations were retained in the weekend-spacing sensitivity analysis. Second, in the reverse direction, higher state anxiety was consistently followed by a higher score on the academic-inefficacy proxy indicator, whereas evidence that this indicator was subsequently associated with ARA was less robust: the second-stage coefficient was supported in the primary separate model, was at the rounding boundary in the strict sample, was not unique in J2, and included zero in the weekend-spacing sensitivity analysis. Third, none of the primary model-based indirect-effect credible intervals excluded zero, and the joint models indicated that the clearest cross-dimension differences were concentrated mainly in first-stage reactivity rather than complete unique downstream indirect processes. The findings are therefore interpreted as temporally ordered component associations with differing robustness across specifications, not as confirmed mediation or causal pathways.

Before interpreting these associations substantively, the term “proxy indicator” warrants emphasis. We use this term because the single day-adapted ARA and burnout items were intended to capture narrow day-level manifestations of broader constructs, not to function as comprehensive measures of those constructs. Accordingly, the temporal coefficients concern the specific daily content represented by each administered item and should not be generalized automatically to an error-free latent construct or complete subscale. This distinction is particularly important for the academic-inefficacy proxy indicator, whose content may also reflect a negative learning appraisal, reduced interest, boredom, or disengagement.

The distinction between component associations and mediation is important for interpreting the present findings. Intensive longitudinal mediation models require attention not only to temporally ordered component paths but also to the model-based indirect effect and its uncertainty [29]. The six separate models addressed the marginal question of whether each burnout proxy indicator, considered one at a time, showed the expected temporal ordering. The joint models addressed the stronger question of whether a given indicator retained a unique role after overlap with the other burnout indicators was considered, and were therefore given greater interpretive weight when evaluating dimension-specificity. In J1, cynicism showed distinctive first-stage reactivity to ARA; in J2, the academic-inefficacy proxy indicator showed distinctive first-stage reactivity to state anxiety. However, no burnout indicator uniquely completed a credible positive downstream indirect process. Together with the weekend-spacing results, the most defensible interpretation is therefore that the study identified dimension-specific temporal component associations, not fully independent or confirmed mediating pathways.

The ARA-to-cynicism-to-state-anxiety component pattern was the comparatively most robust two-stage pattern, but it should still be interpreted as a theoretically informed temporal association rather than a directly measured mechanism. ARA concerns whether one’s learning, effort, and future professional value may be displaced by AI [8–10]. In health-professions education, such concerns may be especially salient because students are preparing for roles in which competence, responsibility, human judgment, and patient care are central to professional identity while AI-enabled systems are increasingly entering education and practice [3–7]. When ARA is elevated on a given day, reduced enthusiasm or psychological distancing from study may become more salient on a subsequent day, consistent with the content represented by the cynicism proxy indicator. Greater cynicism may then be followed by higher state anxiety. This interpretation is consistent with COR theory [12–13], but the present study did not directly measure resource loss, professional identity threat, or specific AI-related daily encounters; these mechanisms should therefore be tested directly in future research.

The reverse-direction results require greater qualification. The most consistent finding was that higher state anxiety was followed by a higher score on the academic-inefficacy proxy indicator. Given the actual item content, this result is better understood as a more negative appraisal of the subsequent day’s learning experience rather than direct evidence that anxiety reduced academic competence. Reporting that one had not learned anything interesting could reflect reduced interest, boredom, disengagement, perceived lack of effectiveness, or a combination of these experiences. The subsequent association of this proxy indicator with later ARA was less robust across specifications: it was supported in the primary M6 model, approached the boundary in the strict sample, was not a unique BP association in J2, and was attenuated to include zero in the weekend-spacing sensitivity analysis. We therefore do not infer a stable competence-mediated reverse pathway. A narrower possibility is that days of elevated anxiety are followed by more negative learning appraisals, which may sometimes coincide with heightened subsequent replacement concern; this possibility requires replication with multi-item daily measures that can distinguish competence, interest, boredom, and disengagement.

The weaker role of exhaustion should also be interpreted cautiously. COR theory clearly permits an exhaustion pathway: sustained monitoring of AI-related uncertainty and coping with perceived replacement threat could consume energetic resources [12–13]. Exhaustion is also a central dimension across student burnout measures [15, 16] and has been widely documented among nursing and healthcare students [17–21]. The present results therefore should not be taken to mean that exhaustion is irrelevant to AI-related stress or student well-being. Rather, exhaustion did not show the same short-term component-path pattern in the present daily models. One possibility is that exhaustion is more cumulative and less tightly linked to same-week fluctuations in AI-related threat than meaning- and competence-oriented burnout dimensions. Another possibility is methodological: the ARA-to-exhaustion-to-anxiety model required a targeted repair after the BP random-slope variance failed to converge, and exhaustion was represented by a single morning-oriented item. These features limit strong conclusions about the exhaustion pathway and support caution against treating burnout dimensions as interchangeable.

The study contributes to the emerging literature on AI-related anxiety by locating ARA within students’ daily academic experiences. Prior work has shown that AI anxiety, readiness, and perceived career implications are relevant to medicine and nursing students [5–7], and broader AI-anxiety research has largely relied on cross-sectional or between-person designs [9–11]. The present within-person findings suggest that AI-related replacement concern is intertwined with daily fluctuations in academic meaning, learning appraisal, and anxiety. This perspective is useful because students may not experience ARA only as a stable attitude toward technology. Instead, ARA may become more or less salient on days when AI-related information, academic demands, or competence feedback make future professional uncertainty personally relevant.

The educational implications should be framed cautiously because the standardized within-person associations were modest, the measures were brief proxy indicators, and the design does not establish intervention effects. At the level supported by the data, the findings suggest that responses to AI-related concern in health-professions education may benefit from considering professional meaning, competence support, and human-AI collaboration alongside technical AI literacy [3–4]. However, the present study cannot determine whether medicine and nursing students differ in these temporal associations because individual disciplinary affiliation was not retained. Possible discipline-specific emphases, such as AI-assisted diagnosis and clinical reasoning in medical education or patient-centered care, communication, documentation, and human-AI collaboration in nursing education [3–7], should therefore be treated as hypotheses for future comparative research.

Limitations and future work

Several limitations should be noted. First, the daily measures were intentionally brief. ARA and the three academic burnout dimensions were each represented by a single day-adapted proxy indicator, and state anxiety was represented by two items. As defined in the Measures section, the proxy terminology is intended to signal that each single item captures a narrow day-level manifestation rather than a comprehensive measure of the broader construct. Although brief measures can reduce participant burden in intensive longitudinal designs [22], and carefully selected single items can show useful concurrent or predictive validity in ecological momentary assessment [24], parent-scale psychometric evidence cannot be assumed to transfer directly to the present day-level adaptations [8, 15–16, 27–28]. Single-item indicators provide restricted construct breadth, do not permit conventional internal-consistency or factor-analytic evaluation, and in observed-variable DSEM do not allow measurement error to be separated from substantive within-person fluctuation. The academic-inefficacy proxy indicator is particularly narrow and may reflect lack of interest, boredom, disengagement, or a negative learning appraisal as well as perceived effectiveness. The ARA ICC similarly demonstrates within-person variation in the observed item but should not be treated as definitive evidence of state-level construct validity. Future research should use validated multi-item day-level measures when feasible and assess the daily contexts in which ARA becomes salient.

Second, the amount and pattern of missing diary data limit inference. Of 7,510 planned participant-days, 2,885 (38.4%) were unavailable after nonsubmission and quality-control exclusions. Although Bayesian DSEM used the available records under an ignorable-missingness assumption conditional on the modeled information, that assumption cannot be verified from these data. Missingness may have been informative: students experiencing greater negative affect, anxiety, poor sleep, fatigue, academic overload, examination stress, burnout-related experiences, physical discomfort, or stronger AI-related concern may have been less likely to complete a subsequent diary. If so, the observed records could overrepresent relatively lower-distress days, potentially affecting estimated mean levels as well as autoregressive and cross-variable lagged associations. The 695-participant sensitivity analysis addresses the influence of participants with very few final valid records but does not test this day-level missingness mechanism.

Third, the observational design and lagged ordering do not establish a direction-specific causal process. Unmeasured time-varying common causes may influence ARA, state anxiety, and the burnout proxy indicators simultaneously or with different temporal delays. Examples include examinations, academic workload, performance feedback, general negative affect, sleep disruption, fatigue, physical condition, other daily stressors, and salient AI-related encounters. A night of poor sleep, for example, could be reflected immediately in the morning-oriented exhaustion indicator, contribute to anxiety later that day, and influence subsequent disengagement or negative learning appraisal on a different timescale. The same-occasion innovation residual covariances modeled residual association within an occasion after the specified lagged structure, but they do not eliminate common causes whose effects persist or unfold across multiple days. Apparent temporal ordering could therefore partly reflect differently timed responses to a shared unmeasured condition.

Fourth, the diary sampled scheduled weekdays across two workweeks, so the Day 5-to-Day 6 interval crossed a weekend and was longer than the other adjacent weekday intervals. The primary scheduled-day coding treated this as one scheduled-assessment lag. The calendar-spacing sensitivity analysis showed that the M2 component pattern was retained, whereas the M6 second-stage association was attenuated and its credible interval included zero. The latter association should therefore be regarded as sensitive to the treatment of the weekend interval. The primary lagged coefficients are best interpreted as next-scheduled-assessment associations rather than uniformly one-calendar-day effects, and the models did not estimate separate weekday-specific or weekend-recovery processes.

Fifth, individual time series were short and uneven. Participants were scheduled for 10 assessments and contributed an average of 6.16 final valid records. Although the full sample provided 3,345 observed lag-1 pairs and 2,479 complete three-occasion sequences, only 560 participants contributed at least one complete t − 2, t − 1, t sequence. The large number of participants supports estimation of population-average within-person associations in a hierarchical model, but it does not fully compensate for limited information when estimating each participant’s serial dynamics, random-slope variances and covariances, or model-based indirect effects. Numerical convergence should therefore not be equated with high precision of individual-specific dynamic parameters.

Sixth, the sample was drawn from medicine and nursing students in one educational context. This focus was theoretically appropriate because AI is increasingly relevant to healthcare education and professional formation [3–7], but it also limits generalizability. Individual-level disciplinary affiliation was not retained in the analytic dataset, so medicine-versus-nursing subgroup analyses could not be conducted and no discipline-specific difference or recommendation can be inferred from the present analyses. The sample was also predominantly female, and gender composition may partly overlap with unmeasured disciplinary composition. Future studies should recruit larger and more balanced samples and explicitly retain discipline, training stage, clinical exposure, and AI-use information so that possible professional-context differences can be tested directly.

Finally, the study involved multiple exploratory component associations and modest within-person effects. We addressed this issue by reporting credible intervals, giving the joint models greater interpretive prominence for formal dimension comparisons, and conducting both a strict-sample and a targeted weekend-spacing sensitivity analysis. Even so, isolated credible intervals should be interpreted cautiously until replicated. The original fully random exhaustion model also did not converge stably because of the BP random-slope variance; the targeted repaired model fixed this parameter while retaining random AP and CP slopes. This model-specific simplification, combined with the single-item exhaustion proxy indicator and the limited individual time-series length, constrains strong conclusions about exhaustion-related dynamics.

Future work should examine whether these population-average daily associations differ across disciplines, training stages, institutional contexts, and levels of AI exposure. It would also be valuable to test whether similar patterns emerge outside healthcare education, where AI-related replacement concerns may be tied to different professional meanings. More comprehensive daily measurement of professional identity, AI competence beliefs, academic meaning, boredom, engagement, perceived effectiveness, and perceived AI threat would clarify which experiences are represented by the present proxy indicators and why ARA becomes salient on some days. Experimental, intervention, or longer and denser intensive-longitudinal designs could further evaluate causal processes and determine whether AI literacy, competence support, and professional identity development reduce maladaptive AI-related concerns while preserving adaptive engagement with emerging technologies.

Conclusions

The present study identifies dimension-specific, population-average within-person temporal associations among ARA, state anxiety, and academic burnout-related proxy indicators rather than established burnout mediation pathways. The ARA-to-cynicism-to-later-state-anxiety component pattern was comparatively robust across the primary, strict-sample, and weekend-spacing specifications. In the reverse direction, state anxiety was consistently associated with a higher subsequent academic-inefficacy proxy score, but evidence that this narrow negative-learning-appraisal indicator was then associated with later ARA was less robust across model specifications. None of the primary model-based indirect effects established mediation. These findings therefore highlight how AI-related replacement concern, daily learning-related experiences, and emotional distress may become temporally intertwined in health-professions education while also defining important measurement and design boundaries for future research.
