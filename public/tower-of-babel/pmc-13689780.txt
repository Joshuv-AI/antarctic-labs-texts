Dyspnea as an Anginal Equivalent
Authors: Isaac Gorgy, Soham Rege, Anilkumar Mehra, Brandon M. Wiley, Emily K. Zern
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDynamic ischemic mitral regurgitation (IMR), provoked by ischemia or exercise, may escape detection at rest and is associated with adverse outcomes.Case SummaryA 65-year-old man with ischemic cardiomyopathy (left ventricular ejection fraction, 20%) and recurrent left circumflex in-stent restenosis refractory to multiple interventions presented with progressive dyspnea and chest pain. Resting filling pressures were mildly abnormal, though symptoms remained out of proportion to findings. During balloon angioplasty of the left circumflex, transient ischemia provoked large V waves on the pulmonary catheter wedge pressure tracing; simultaneous echocardiography confirmed severe MR that resolved on deflation of the intracoronary balloon. Stress echocardiography corroborated exercise-induced severe MR. Despite revascularization and medical therapy optimization, symptoms persisted, and mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) was performed with successful resolution of symptoms.DiscussionDynamic IMR may be underdiagnosed when diagnostic studies are performed at rest. This case demonstrates concordant invasive and noninvasive provocative strategies for diagnosis, with M-TEER for refractory symptoms.Take-Home MessagesDynamic IMR requires a high index of suspicion and provocative multimodality testing. M-TEER may benefit patients with severe IMR refractory to revascularization and optimized medical therapy.

History of Presentation

A 65-year-old man with ischemic cardiomyopathy (left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF], 20%) and multivessel coronary artery disease (CAD) presented with progressive exertional dyspnea and recurrent chest pain. His coronary history was remarkable for a chronic total occlusion of the right coronary artery and multiple drug-eluting stents in the left anterior descending and left circumflex (LCx) arteries. Over the preceding 5 years, his course was marked by recurrent LCx in-stent restenosis (ISR), requiring multiple prior percutaneous coronary interventions, including stent implantation and serial balloon angioplasties, each providing only transient symptomatic relief before recurrence of angina and dyspnea. The patient had previously declined coronary artery bypass grafting. Despite maximally tolerated guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), including 4-pillar heart failure therapy, his functional capacity had progressively declined to NYHA functional class III.

At the index evaluation, exertional dyspnea occurred with minimal effort and resolved with rest. He also endorsed intermittent exertional chest pressure. He denied palpitations, lightheadedness, or syncope. Cardiovascular examination revealed normal S1 and S2 without murmurs, rubs, or gallops. Jugular venous pressure was not elevated. There was no peripheral edema. Lungs were clear bilaterally.

Past Medical History

He has a history of chronic sinusitis, sleep apnea, prior tobacco use, and treated Helicobacter pylori–related gastritis.

Differential Diagnosis

The pattern of recurrent exertional chest pain and dyspnea, with transient relief after each LCx revascularization, was highly suggestive of recurrent ischemia, with dyspnea potentially representing an anginal equivalent. Decompensated heart failure and/or low cardiac output syndrome were considered but deemed unlikely, given euvolemia on examination and point-of-care ultrasound with adequate markers of peripheral perfusion. Dynamic valvular heart disease was also entertained; however, serial resting echocardiograms had consistently demonstrated only mild functional mitral regurgitation (MR), and no new murmur was appreciated on examination. Because chest pain dominated the presentation, initial evaluation emphasized recurrent ischemia rather than provocative valvular assessment.

Investigations

Resting echocardiography demonstrated stable, severe LV systolic dysfunction (LVEF 20%) with global hypokinesis, midinferolateral akinesis, and mild-to-moderate functional MR. The electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with an old anterior infarct pattern, nonspecific St-T wave changes, and a QRS duration of 115 milliseconds.

Laboratory studies showed a hemoglobin level of 15.0 g/dL, a serum creatinine level of 1.29 mg/dL, and an N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide level of 1,049 pg/mL; cardiac troponin was not obtained.

Given progressive symptoms and high suspicion for recurrent ischemia, right heart catheterization and coronary angiography were performed, with a pulmonary artery (PA) catheter maintained in situ for continuous hemodynamic monitoring during coronary angiography.

Resting hemodynamics were as follows: right atrial pressure, 4 mm Hg; right ventricular pressure, 35/6 mm Hg; PA pressure, 35/17 mm Hg (mean, 23 mm Hg); and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), 15 mm Hg with V waves to 30 mm Hg (Figure 1A). Cardiac index was 2.3 L/min/m2 by thermodilution and 2.5 L/min/m2 by the Fick method. Coronary angiography revealed severe recurrent LCx ISR (Video 1, Figure 2), and balloon angioplasty was pursued. During LCx balloon inflation, a striking change in hemodynamics was observed: PA pressure surged to 75/30 mm Hg with giant V waves to 75 mm Hg on the PCWP tracing, accompanied by systemic hypotension (aortic pressure falling from 144/52 to 112/47 mm Hg) (Figure 1B). The patient simultaneously developed acute severe dyspnea and chest pressure, and the surface electrocardiogram showed new dynamic ST-T wave changes consistent with acute ischemia. An intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) was emergently placed, after which PA pressures improved to 46/14 mm Hg, with V waves decreasing to 40 mm Hg (Figure 1C). Management

Balloon angioplasty of the LCx ISR was completed using 2.5-mm and 3.0-mm noncompliant and cutting balloons (Figure 5). The patient was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit with IABP support for hemodynamic stabilization and afterload reduction.Figure 5Postintervention Angiographic OutcomeFinal angiography after balloon angioplasty of left circumflex in-stent restenosis with 2.5-mm and 3.0-mm noncompliant and cutting balloons with good results.

A multidisciplinary Heart Team meeting recommended further GDMT optimization and outpatient provocative assessment to further characterize the dynamic MR before considering mitral valve intervention.

Outpatient stress echocardiography, performed 3 months later on optimized GDMT and after LCx revascularization, confirmed exercise-induced progression of MR from mild-to-moderate at rest (E-wave, 0.5 m/s) to severe (E-wave, 1.1 m/s), achieved at a low level of exercise (4.7 metabolic equivalents of task, heart rate 53-74 beats/min with stress) (Figure 6). The study was terminated early due to dyspnea. Given concordant invasive and noninvasive evidence of severe dynamic IMR persisting despite revascularization and optimized GDMT, the Heart Team recommended mitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER). The patient underwent successful M-TEER with deployment of a MitraClip XTW (Abbott) to the A2-P2 position and a MitraClip NT placed medially to the XTW. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was discharged on the first postoperative day on optimized GDMT. At 2-month follow-up, he reported marked functional improvement and was able to ambulate multiple blocks without dyspnea.

Echocardiography at 14 months post-M-TEER confirmed durable reduction to mild residual MR with a mean transmitral gradient of 3 mm Hg at a heart rate of 60 beats/min in sinus rhythm. He required repeat percutaneous coronary intervention for recurrent ISR of an obtuse marginal branch of the LCx 14 months after M-TEER, treated with a drug-coated balloon with a satisfactory angiographic result. At that reintervention, repeat right heart catheterization showed normalized resting hemodynamics (right atrial pressure mean, 3 mm Hg; PA pressure, 25/17 mm Hg [mean, 20 mm Hg]; PCWP mean, 12 mm Hg with V waves to 14 mm Hg), compared with the index resting wedge of 15 mm Hg and provoked V waves to 76 mm Hg; MR was not reprovoked post-M-TEER. With only mild residual regurgitation on the concurrent echocardiogram, these data support durable repair despite recurrent ischemia.

Discussion

Ischemic MR (IMR) is an often-underdiagnosed entity that represents a complex interplay between CAD, left ventricular remodeling, and valvular/annular dysfunction. It is strongly associated with adverse outcomes, including heart failure hospitalizations and all-cause mortality, independent of ejection fraction.1,2 Severity of IMR has been shown to predict adverse outcomes, with even moderate IMR (effective regurgitant orifice area ≥20 mm2) associated with a 20% higher 5-year mortality than patients without IMR.3 Critically, IMR severity can fluctuate with loading conditions and ischemic burden, and dynamic IMR exacerbated by ischemia or exercise may be substantially underdiagnosed when evaluations are performed exclusively at rest.3

This case is instructive for several reasons.

First, it demonstrates the value of a multimodality diagnostic approach to unmasking dynamic IMR. During cardiac catheterization, transient LCx occlusion provoked severe MR with dramatic hemodynamic compromise, confirmed in real time by simultaneous echocardiography and corroborated by point-of-care lung ultrasound showing dynamic B-lines. Importantly, resting hemodynamics, notable for mild pulmonary hypertension and mildly elevated PCWP, did not reflect the severity of valvular dysfunction or symptomatology. The pathophysiology was further validated noninvasively by stress echocardiography, which demonstrated exercise-induced severe MR at a low workload. The concordance across invasive hemodynamic monitoring, catheterization laboratory echocardiography, and outpatient stress echocardiography provides robust diagnostic certainty and highlights complementary roles for each modality. The reproducibility of decompensation with each inflation, the concordant ischemic St-T wave changes and regional wall-motion abnormality, and the corroborating exercise findings argue against well-developed collateral circulation and confirm hemodynamically significant ischemia during the provoked regurgitation.

Mechanistically, this case combines a chronically remodeled, globally dysfunctional ventricle (LVEF 20%) that provided a permissive, tethered substrate with an acute, territorial ischemic trigger: LCx ischemia enlarged the inferolateral wall-motion abnormality—which subtends the posteromedial papillary muscle—displacing the papillary muscle, augmenting leaflet tethering, and reducing the transmitral closing force. Kaul et al4 showed experimentally that IMR relates to left ventricular dysfunction and mitral geometric distortion rather than to isolated papillary muscle dysfunction, while subsequent 3-dimensional echocardiographic work linked segmental dysfunction and localized remodeling to papillary muscle displacement and leaflet tethering.5 Both mechanisms were operative here, producing dynamic severe regurgitation that resolved with reperfusion.

The dynamic, fully reversible regurgitation is reminiscent of “eclipsed” MR, a paroxysmal form of severe functional regurgitation classically described in patients with preserved ventricular function and a nonischemic substrate, producing transient pulmonary edema that resolves spontaneously.6 This entity and its consequences have previously been described, including cases managed with TEER.7,8 Our case differs in its demonstrably ischemic trigger and ischemic cardiomyopathy substrate, yet shares the central lesson that a normal resting echocardiogram does not exclude intermittently severe MR and that provocative imaging is required for diagnosis.

Second, this case illustrates exertional dyspnea as an anginal equivalent in a patient whose symptoms had been attributed to recurrent coronary ischemia alone. The absence of a resting murmur and the presence of only mild-to-moderate MR on serial resting echocardiograms initially obscured the valvular contribution to symptoms. It was only through provocative testing, initially incidental during catheterization and subsequently deliberate with stress echocardiography, that the true mechanism was revealed. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for dynamic IMR in patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy and exertional symptoms out of proportion to resting findings. The diagnostic sequence is instructive: at the index clinic visit, chest pain predominated over dyspnea, directing evaluation toward ischemia and coronary angiography; thus, cardiac catheterization unmasked the dynamic MR before it was confirmed by exercise echocardiography. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology/European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery valvular heart disease guidelines endorse exercise echocardiography to identify the cause of dyspnea and to unmask dynamic MR when symptoms exceed resting findings;9 in retrospect, this patient's symptom-finding discordance satisfied that indication earlier in his course, and the prior interventions—performed without simultaneous hemodynamic and echocardiographic monitoring—had not captured the valvular contribution to the patient's symptomatology.

Third, when severe IMR persisted despite revascularization and GDMT optimization, M-TEER provided meaningful and durable symptomatic benefit. These findings align with the COAPT (Cardiovascular Outcomes Assessment of the MitraClip Percutaneous Therapy for Heart Failure Patients with Functional Mitral Regurgitation) trial, which demonstrated that M-TEER reduced heart failure hospitalizations and mortality in patients with heart failure and disproportionate secondary MR on maximally tolerated GDMT.10 Although MITRA-FR (Percutaneous Repair with the MitraClip Device for Severe Functional/Secondary Mitral Regurgitation) reported neutral outcomes, differences in patient selection, particularly the severity of MR relative to LV dilation, may account for the discordant results.11 More recent data from the RESHAPE-HF2 (Randomized Investigation of the MitraClip Device in Heart Failure: Second Trial in Patients With Clinically Significant Functional Mitral Regurgitation) trial further support M-TEER in selected patients with secondary MR.12

Limitations include the absence of a standardized provocative research protocol and relatively short follow-up beyond 14 months. In addition, the degree to which hemodynamic responses during balloon occlusion precisely mirror spontaneous ischemia during daily activity is uncertain, though the concordant stress echocardiographic findings provide external validation. Quantitative assessment of LVEF and myocardial strain during balloon occlusion was not performed; characterization of the ischemic wall-motion change is therefore qualitative.

Conclusions

Dynamic IMR is a diagnostically challenging entity that may be missed on resting evaluation. This case demonstrates that concordant provocative assessment—combining invasive hemodynamic monitoring with simultaneous echocardiography during induced ischemia and outpatient stress echocardiography—can unmask severe MR and clarify the mechanism of unexplained exertional symptoms in patients with CAD. When severe IMR persists despite revascularization and optimized GDMT, M-TEER may offer a viable therapeutic option in appropriately selected patients.
