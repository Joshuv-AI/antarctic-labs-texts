A comparative analysis of bone mineral density in cerebral palsy and spinal muscular atrophy
Authors: Brianna G Bowden, Brett Klamer, Robin Alexander, Mariah Eisner, Garey Noritz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with cerebral palsy (CP) and spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) with immobility are at risk for low bone mineral density (BMD) and fragility fractures. This study investigates whether BMD differs between these two groups given their distinct pathological mechanisms.MethodsData were collected from non-ambulatory patients diagnosed with CP (GMFCS IV-V) and SMA (type I–II), aged five years and older, who were seen at a metabolic bone clinic between January 2012 and July 2023. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) assessed BMD at the lumbar spine (LS) and lateral distal femur (LDF), region 3 (R3). Z-scores were calculated and compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank sum test.ResultsThis study included 274 children with CP and 16 with SMA. Unadjusted LS Z-scores were low and comparable in both groups (CP: −2.3, SMA: −2.6; p = 0.7). After adjusting for height, LS Z-scores were in the normal range (CP: −0.9, SMA: −1.6; p = 0.3). However, the SMA group had significantly lower BMD in R3 of the LDF compared to the CP group (CP: −2.9, SMA: −3.9; p = 0.047).ConclusionBoth groups had normal height adjusted LS BMD and low R3 LDF BMD, with the SMA group significantly lower. We theorize that it may be due to the differences in muscle tonicity on the bone. We recommend including the distal femur bone density in routine bone health assessments and that BMD should be adjusted for height when possible.Keywords: Cerebral palsy, Spinal muscular atrophy, Bone mineral density, Dual-energy X-ray, Absorptiometry

Introduction

Children with limited mobility are at risk for low bone mineral density and fragility fractures (Kannikeswaran et al., 2022). The mechanical force exerted on bones by exercise and weight bearing activities stimulates osteoblasts to increase bone formation, making them stronger (Chang et al., 2022; Hind and Burrows, 2007). Immobility decreases osteoblast activity, reducing bone formation, while osteoclasts continue resorption, leading to decreased bone mass. Additionally, when muscles are stronger, the force they put on bones is stronger. When muscles atrophy due to limited movement, bones weaken (Bettis et al., 2018).

Cerebral Palsy (CP) is the most common motor disability of childhood (McGuire et al., 2019). It is a static disorder due to disturbances in the brain during fetal or infant development (Rosenbaum et al., 2007). The prevalence is 3.2 per 1000 children (McGuire et al., 2019). There is a wide range of functional abilities, which is classified using the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS). At Level I, patients can perform normal gross motor skills with few limitations in speed, balance, and coordination, while those at Level V require a wheeled mobility device in all settings and have limited head and trunk postural control. Patients who are classified at GMFCS level IV and V are considered non-ambulatory (Palisano et al., 1997).

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is an inherited autosomal recessive disease characterized by weakness and paralysis in voluntary proximal muscles due to degeneration of spinal alpha motor neurons (Mercuri et al., 2022). The prevalence is 1–2 per 10,000 persons (Verhaart et al., 2017). The most common and severe type of SMA is type I, affecting 50 % of those diagnosed. These patients have an onset prior to 6 months of age with severe hypotonia and flaccid paralysis, typically affecting head control. They cannot sit independently, and without intervention, usually die before two years of age. SMA type II has an onset between 6 and 18 months of age and accounts for about 20 % of cases. These patients sit independently but are unable to ambulate. SMA types III and IV are less severe, with a later onset, and these patients can walk independently. Patients with SMA types I and II are considered non-ambulatory (Mercuri et al., 2022).

Fractures are common in both motor conditions. The incidence of fracture in non-ambulatory patients with CP is reported to be 6–9 % per year (Stevenson et al., 2006; Linton et al., 2022). At least 38 % of patients with SMA have had a fragility fracture (Wasserman et al., 2017).

In addition to their non-ambulatory status, children with CP and SMA often have additional risk factors for low bone density, such as dysphagia, which can lead to malnutrition and abnormal mineral intake and metabolism (Trivić and Hojsak, 2019; McGrattan et al., 2021). A large proportion of patients with CP require anticonvulsant medications (Henderson et al., 2002) many of which have been linked with decreases in bone mass (Lee et al., 2010).

Dual Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA) scans are the most common method for estimating bone density in children. This is a noninvasive imaging modality that imparts a very low radiation exposure, approximately 5–8 uSV, similar to the natural background radiation over 24 h (Guss et al., 2021). Children who are non-ambulatory commonly undergo DXA scan as a screening test for low bone mass, or in response to a fragility fracture (Houlihan and Stevenson, 2009).

The goal of the current study is to compare DXA results in non-ambulatory children with CP to non-ambulatory children with SMA. Clinical observations from the authors suggest that children with SMA may experience more severe reductions in bone mineral density (BMD) compared to those with CP. We sought to determine whether this difference truly exists. If it does, we may need to investigate whether the distinct pathological mechanisms of these disorders influence function in ways that could inform treatment considerations.

Methods

A retrospective chart review was conducted to compare bone health parameters between non-ambulatory patients with CP to non-ambulatory patients with SMA. The Electronic Health Record (Epic, Verona WI) was used to gather data in all patients with CP and SMA, aged 5 years and older, who had undergone DXA scanning at a large quaternary children's hospital in the Midwest between January 1, 2012, and July 1, 2023. Demographic and clinical variables collected include age, gender, type of medical insurance, diagnosis, and ambulatory status. It should be noted that the data on patients with SMA was collected prior to the advent of disease specific therapies (Day et al., 2022), so all patients with Type I and II SMA were non-ambulatory. Bone mineral density (BMD) measurements by DXA, history of fractures, and bisphosphonate treatment were collected. Patients were excluded if they were under five years of age, ambulatory, or had invalid BMD measurements that were unreadable because of contractures or positioning issues in the opinion of the primary investigator.

Dual Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA; Hologic Delphi, Waltham, MA) was used to measure BMD at the lumbar spine (L1-L4) and region 3 of the lateral distal femur (Weber et al., 2019).

The lateral distal femur (Fig. 1) is used as an alternative site of BMD measurement in children with disabilities as images of this site are more easily obtained in children with scoliosis or contractures, and there is rarely surgical hardware in the distal femur to interfere with the measurement. The regions of interest (ROIs) are determined mathematically using the width of the diaphysis and growth plate. Region 1 is the anterior distal metaphysis, mostly trabecular bone; region 2 is the diaphysis, a mixture of trabecular and cortical bone; region 3 is the diaphysis, mostly cortical bone. We focused on region 3's cortical bone in this study. Because the ROIs are constructed for each scan using the size of the bone, height adjustment is not necessary to compare bigger or smaller bones.

Children with CP and SMA commonly have scoliosis and hip dislocation, so many have had surgery with metallic implants, such as spinal rods, screws, and plates. These interfere with BMD measurement and falsely elevate the BMD. Proper assessment of the BMD by DXA is also dependent on having the bones properly positioned in the scanner. Abnormally shaped bones, or contractures around joints, may make it impossible or uncomfortable to place the patient in the standard positions. The measurement is compromised when the patient is not in the same position as those children in the normative database. The BMD may thus be falsely elevated or diminished for technical reasons.

Z-scores, which compare a patient's BMD to that of age- and sex-matched healthy peers, were calculated using the manufacturer's normative database. For patients under 18 years of age, the most recent raw areal BMD, Z-score, and height-adjusted Z-score were collected. The height-adjusted Z-score compares the patient's BMD to healthy children of the same height, rather than age, providing an alternative normalization method.

Descriptive statistics are summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR) or frequency and percent. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare BMD values between the CP and SMA groups. Because the CP and SMA groups differed in age distribution, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using optimal pair matching (2:1 [CP: SMA] ratio) to reduce imbalance on age. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was then re-applied to the matched sample. Statistical analysis was performed using R version 4.5.1 (Team RC, 2025) with add-on package MatchIt (version 4.7.2) (Ho et al., 2011). The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board.

Results

A total of 398 patients were evaluated, of whom 290 met our inclusion criteria and were included in the analysis (CP: n = 274; SMA: n = 16). The median age in the CP group was 14 (Stevenson et al., 2006; Weber et al., 2019) years, and the median age in the SMA group was 9 (Mercuri et al., 2022; Henderson et al., 2002) years.

Some patients had DXA scans at both the lumbar spine and distal femur, while others had scans at only one site due to metallic implants, contractures, or software limitations. The median lumbar spine Z-scores were low and comparable in both CP and SMA groups (−2.3 vs. −2.6, respectively; p = 0.7). However, the median height-adjusted lumbar spine Z-scores for both groups were in the normal range, with no significant difference between groups (−0.9 vs. −1.6; p = 0.3). Discussion

Multiple studies have reported low BMD in non-ambulatory children (Kannikeswaran et al., 2022) and children with CP (Henderson et al., 2002) and SMA (Wasserman et al., 2017), but to our knowledge, this is the first study to compare BMD between these two groups. We found that non-ambulatory children with SMA and CP had normal height adjusted BMD Z-scores in the lumbar spine. Both the SMA and CP groups had low BMD Z-scores at region 3 of the distal femur, with the SMA group significantly lower. We theorize that this difference may be due to the differential effects of high or low muscle tone on the bone.

Previous studies indicate that voluntary mechanical loading forces play a key role in bone strength, helping to reduce the risk of fragility fractures. According to the “Mechanostat theory,” mechanical load influences bone mass and remodels bone architecture to keep the force of strain on the bone at equilibrium (Frost, 2003). In the case of SMA patients who have severe hypotonia, the reduced muscular force on the bones may explain the lower BMD than the patients with CP. In contrast, hypertonia, commonly seen in CP patients, may lead to higher levels of muscular resistance, resulting in better bone preservation.

Other genetic disorders associated with hypotonia, such as Down Syndrome (DS) and Prader-Willi Syndrome (PWS), have also been shown to have lower BMD than their peers (Baptista et al., 2005; Stagi et al., 2015). Patients with these disorders are typically ambulatory but have reduced physical activity compared to typical peers (Shields et al., 2018; Castner et al., 2014). While reduced physical activity levels are known to contribute to decreased BMD, it is suggested that genetic factors may have a greater influence (Baptista et al., 2005; da Silva et al., 2010). When comparing males with intellectual disabilities with and without DS, both groups exhibited decreased muscle strength; however, only the group with DS had low BMD compared to the control group (da Silva et al., 2010). Similarly, when subjects with PWS were compared to individuals with obesity (matched for BMI and body fat percentage by DXA), those with PWS had significantly lower BMD. The authors theorized that the reduced BMD seen in PWS may be related to the lack of bone mineral deposits during growth and decreased sex or hormone production in addition to hypotonia (Butler et al., 2001).

Muscle spasticity has been shown to be a protective factor for BMD of the femur in individuals with spinal cord injuries (SCI). One study found greater muscle mass in individuals with high spasticity compared to those with low or absent spasticity. They concluded that both spasticity and muscle mass significantly preserved the total and trabecular BMD in the distal epiphysis of the femur, with these factors having a stronger influence than systemic or lifestyle factors (Eser et al., 2005).

Long bone fractures are known to be prevalent in non-ambulatory patients despite relatively normal lumbar bone density measurements. Clinically, it can be more practical to measure the BMD in the LDF than the LS due to metallic implants or contractures that affect the accuracy of measurements (Wasserman et al., 2017). Studies have shown that patients with low BMD show a greater difference between the LS and LDF measurements compared to patients with close to normal BMD, with the LDF typically being lower (Henderson et al., 2002; Henderson, 1997). This may lead to inaccurate clinical impressions when assessing bone health if only the LS is utilized.

Additionally, individuals that are non-ambulatory and use wheelchairs may maintain or experience an increase in BMD at the LS. A study of women with complete spinal cord injuries (SCI) found that their lumbar BMD was either comparable to or higher than that of the control group. This increase or maintenance in BMD was not observed in the hip or knee; they experienced a decrease in BMD similar to other non-weight bearing individuals (Garland et al., 2001). The increase in LS BMD, but not the lower extremities, may be related to weight bearing in the spine from sitting, which might protect against bone loss in the lumbar spine. This may partially explain why the BMD in our patients had relatively normal lumbar BMD.

This single-center retrospective study may be limited due to local differences in the target population, study sample, and analytic sample. Many patients had missing information for variables of interest. The number of SMA patients was small in comparison to CP and power to detect significant differences between these two groups was limited. Further studies could expand on this with a prospective study using a larger sample size drawn from multiple institutions, with more complete data around fracture rates and additional risk factors for low BMD.

In summary, we found that non-ambulatory children with CP and SMA had low BMD when measured in the distal femur, and it was significantly lower for the children with SMA. This correlates with the high fracture rate in both groups. We recommend that distal femur bone density should be part of the routine assessment of bone health in all non-ambulatory patients and remind clinicians that BMD should be adjusted for height when able.
