DEALing with Image Reconstruction: Deep Attentive Least Squares
Mehrsa Pourya †, Erich Kobler ‡, Michael Unser †, Sebastian Neumayer ⋆

Abstract
State-of-the-art image reconstruction often relies on complex, highly parameterized deep architectures. We propose an alternative: a data-driven reconstruction method inspired by the classic Tikhonov regularization. Our approach iteratively refines intermediate reconstructions by solving a sequence of quadratic problems. These updates have two key components: (i) learned filters to extract salient image features, and (ii) an attention mechanism that locally adjusts the penalty of filter responses. Our method achieves performance on par with leading plug-and-play and learned regularizer approaches while offering interpretability, robustness, and convergent behavior. In effect, we bridge traditional regularization and deep learning with a principled reconstruction approach.



1. Introduction

Image reconstruction plays a fundamental role in computational imaging and computer vision
[
30
,
50
]
.
The task is to recover an unknown image of interest
{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from noisy measurements
{\bf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
Their relation is often modeled as
{\bf{y}}={\bf{H}}{\bf{x}}
, where the forward operator
{\bf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
encodes the acquisition process.
If
{\bf{H}}
is ill-conditioned, one resorts to regularized reconstruction

\hat{{\bf{x}}}\in\argmin_{{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\rVert{\bf{H}}{\bf{x}}-{\bf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\mathcal{R}({\bf{x}}).

(1)

The data-fidelity
\left\rVert{\bf{H}}{\bf{x}}-{\bf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
controls the consistency of the reconstruction with the measurements.
The regularizer
\mathcal{R}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
encodes prior information about the solution and is also intended to make the problem well-posed.
Both terms are balanced by the hyperparameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
Throughout this paper,
{\bf{x}}
is the vectorized version of a (color or grayscale) image with shape
N_{\mathrm{in}}\times H\times W
.

From classic signal processing to the advent of deep learning, a significant body of research focuses on the design of the regularizer
\mathcal{R}
.
In the context of data-driven methods, two primary approaches emerged: (i) the explicit modeling of
\mathcal{R}
, and (ii) the modeling of operators associated with
\mathcal{R}
, such as its proximal operator, which is required in Plug-and-Play (PnP) reconstruction algorithms
[
46
]
.
Following the explicit approach, the starting point for this work is the
fields-of-experts model
[
40
]
, which reads

\mathcal{R}_{{\bf{m}}}\colon{\bf{x}}\mapsto\sum_{c=1}^{N_{\mathrm{C}}}\bigl\langle{\bf{m}}_{c},\psi_{c}({\bf{W}}_{c}{\bf{x}})\bigr\rangle.

(2)

Here, each
{\bf{W}}_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{HW\times d}
convolves
{\bf{x}}
with a filter template
w_{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathrm{in}}\times k_{s}\times k_{s}}
.
Then, the nonnegative potentials
\psi_{c}\in\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R})
are applied entry-wise to the
{\bf{W}}_{c}{\bf{x}}
.
Finally, the weights
{\bf{m}}_{c}\in[\epsilon_{M},1]^{HW}
with
\epsilon_{M}>0
determine the (spatially varying) contribution of
\psi({\bf{W}}_{c}{\bf{x}})
to the regularizer.
In principle, every component of (
2
) can be learned.
Most implementations so far have used constant weights
{\bf{m}}_{c}={\bf{1}}
in (
2
).
Recent works differ in the parameterization of
\psi_{c}
and
{\bf{W}}_{c}
for the learning process
[
6
,
12
,
49
]
or incorporate non-linear feature transforms
[
27
,
20
]
.
While learning
{\bf{W}}_{c}
and
\psi_{c}
is extensively studied, little research is performed regarding the local weights
{\bf{m}}_{c}
.

Employing spatially varying
{\bf{m}}_{c}
in (
2
) is referred to as anisotropic regularization.
For instance,
{\bf{m}}_{c}={\bf{M}}({\bf{y}})
can be derived from the data
{\bf{y}}
using heuristics
[
5
,
13
]
or a neural network
[
21
,
23
]
.
When
{\bf{M}}
extracts features from an estimated reconstruction, as proposed by
Neumayer and Altekrüger [31]
, it is natural to consider refining
{\bf{m}}_{c}
iteratively.
Specifically, the reconstruction from (
1
) can be fed back into
{\bf{M}}
to obtain an
improved

{\bf{m}}_{c}
for (
2
).
This leads to the attentive reconstruction process

{\bf{x}}_{k+1}\in\argmin_{{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\rVert{\bf{H}}{\bf{x}}-{\bf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\mathcal{R}_{{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}}_{k})}({\bf{x}}),

(3)

studied by
Pourya et al. [35]
for
\psi_{c}=|\cdot|
.
We note that their scheme is computationally demanding since each update involves a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-type problem.

Contribution

The updates (
3
) are the starting point of our investigation.
Our contributions are as follows.

•

We simplify the updates (
3
) by choosing
\psi_{c}(\cdot)=(\cdot)^{2}
for the
\mathcal{R}_{{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}}_{k})}
from (
2
).
Then, each update amounts to solving a linear equation, which we handle efficiently through the conjugate gradient method.

•

We learn the components of
\mathcal{R}_{{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}}_{k})}({\bf{x}})
, namely the filters
\{{\bf{W}}_{c}\}_{c=1}^{N_{c}}
and the attention mechanism
{\bf{M}}
, based on a denoising task in such a way that the iterations (
3
) converge.
Then, any fixed point of (
3
) is a consistent reconstruction.
Given a linear inverse problem with forward
{\bf{H}}
, we then apply our learned model with only two remaining hyperparameters: (i) the noise level and (ii) the regularization strength
\lambda
.

•

On the theoretical side, we establish the uniqueness of each update in (
3
), the existence of a fixed point, a condition for the convergence of (
3
), and a stability result for the resulting reconstruction operator.

•

In our experimental evaluation, we achieve results on par with state-of-the-art approaches for various inverse problems.

•

We underline the interpretability, convergence, and robustness of our method. We provide illustrative examples to visualize the learned attention mechanism.

2. Related Literature

Learned Regularization

Classical regularizers for (
1
) leverage sparsity in various domains, such as image gradients
[
41
]
or wavelets
[
29
]
.
The parametric model (
2
) introduced by
Roth and Black [40]
has since spurred extensive research.
Key areas of investigation include learning paradigms
[
6
,
10
]
, parametrization strategies
[
49
]
, and intrinsic properties like convexity
[
11
,
12
]
.
More complex architectures build upon strategies such as autoencoders
[
27
]
, algorithm unrolling
[
20
]
, adversarial training
[
28
,
37
]
, and energy modeling
[
48
]
.

In parallel, implicit regularization methods were developed.
Here, of-the-shelf denoisers are incorporated into iterative reconstruction algorithms by, for instance, replacing proximal operators
[
46
,
54
]
or drawing inspiration from adaptive Laplacians
[
39
]
.
If the denoiser is non-expansive or homogeneous, which is hard to ensure for learned ones
[
38
,
14
]
, this leads to a convergent scheme.
Recently, weaker conditions have been proposed by
Pesquet et al. [34]
,
Hurault et al. [17]
.

Spatial Adaptivity

An overview of spatially adaptive regularizers with the form (
2
) is given in
Pragliola et al. [36]
.
The authors
Hintermüller et al. [15]
,
Van Chung et al. [45]
,
Kofler et al. [21]
focus on the total variation regularizer
[
41
]
as a special case, using either heuristics or deep learning to compute the weights
{\bf{m}}_{c}
.
More general instances are considered by
Lefkimmiatis and Koshelev [23]
,
Neumayer et al. [32]
,
Neumayer and Altekrüger [31]
.
The first work deploys non-smooth potentials
\psi_{c}
and majorization minimization to solve the nonsmooth problem (
1
).
Similar to (
3
), this leads to a series of quadratic problems.
The latter works deploy differentiable
\psi_{c}
and solve (
1
) with accelerated gradient descent.
All approaches have in common that they update the weights
{\bf{m}}_{c}
only once.
In particular, they do not refine
{\bf{m}}_{c}
and the reconstruction iteratively as in (
3
).

Iterative Refinement

Lenzen et al. [24]
,
Lenzen and Berger [25]
propose to iteratively refine the weights
{\bf{m}}_{c}
for total variation.
They update the
{\bf{m}}_{c}
using some heuristic.
For the more general model (
2
) with
\psi_{c}=|\cdot|
, a refinement based on neural networks was considered by
Pourya et al. [35]
.
Their
{\bf{M}}
has a simple architecture comparable to ours.
Outside of this setting, iterative refinement can be found, for example, in
Saharia et al. [42]
for superresolution and in
Darestani et al. [8]
for MRI.

Nonlocal Laplacians

Quadratic potentials
\psi_{c}
lead to an optimality condition for (
3
) induced by a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix.
A different approach to getting such updates is (iterative) filtering with carefully designed graph Laplacians
[
33
]
.
Recently, this idea was incorporated into deep architectures for image denoising
[
51
,
44
]
and scene flow
[
43
]
.

3. Methodology

We specify the regularizer (
2
) using quadratic potentials as

\mathcal{R}_{{\bf{m}}}({\bf{x}})=\sum_{c=1}^{N_{c}}\bigl\langle{\bf{m}}_{c}^{2},({\bf{W}}_{c}{\bf{x}})^{2}\bigr\rangle=\left\rVert{\bf{M}}{\bf{W}}{\bf{x}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

(4)

with the shorthands
{\bf{W}}=[{\bf{W}}_{1}^{\top}\ \ldots\ {\bf{W}}_{N_{c}}^{\top}]^{\top}
and
{\bf{M}}=\mathrm{Diag}(\mathrm{Diag}({\bf{m}}_{1}),\ \ldots,\ \mathrm{Diag}({\bf{m}}_{N_{c}}))
, where
\mathrm{Diag}
returns a diagonal matrix whose entries are the input vector.
This leads to the quadratic reconstruction problem

\hat{{\bf{x}}}\in\argmin_{{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\rVert{\bf{H}}{\bf{x}}-{\bf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\rVert{\bf{M}}{\bf{W}}{\bf{x}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(5)

Now, the question arises about the proper choice of
{\bf{M}}
and
{\bf{W}}
.
Regarding a filter-based interpretation, the
{\bf{W}}
should be independent of
{\boldsymbol{y}}
.

{\bf{M}}
modulates the response
{\bf{W}}{\bf{x}}
at each location.
Ideally, this modulation should depend on the structure of the (unknown)
\hat{{\bf{x}}}
.
With abuse of notation, we introduce
{\bf{M}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[\epsilon_{M},1]^{N_{c}HW}
, which leads to our attentive refinement scheme

\displaystyle{\bf{x}}_{k+1}

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{T}({\bf{x}}_{k},{\bf{y}})\quad\text{with}\quad{\bf{x}}_{0}={\bf{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}({\bf{z}},{\bf{y}})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\rVert{\bf{H}}{\bf{x}}-{\bf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lambda\left\rVert{\bf{M}}({\bf{z}}){\bf{W}}{\bf{x}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

(7)

The process (
6
) can be interpreted as an infinite-depth neural network.
Hence, we name it deep attentive least squares (DEAL) for image reconstruction.
If
{\bf{x}}_{k}\to\hat{{\bf{x}}}
, we get
\hat{{\bf{x}}}\in\mathcal{T}(\hat{{\bf{x}}},{\bf{y}})
, namely that
\hat{{\bf{x}}}
is a fixed-point of the operator (
7
).
We restrict
k<K_{\mathrm{out}}
and terminate the iterations (
6
) when
\left\rVert{\bf{x}}_{k+1}-{\bf{x}}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}/\left\rVert{\bf{x}}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{out}}
with
\epsilon_{\mathrm{out}}>0
.

3.1. Architecture

Next, we specify how the DEAL iterates (
6
) can be cast as a deep neural network structure.
Figure
1
visualizes the interplay of its essential building blocks – reconstruction and mask generation – which exchange information repeatedly.

3.1.1
Reconstruction Block

At the heart of DEAL, the reconstruction block solves the spatially-adapted optimization problem (
7
) for given attentive weights
{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}}_{k})
(see Section
3.1.2
).
The optimality condition for problem (
7
) is given by the linear system

{\bf{A}}_{k}{\bf{x}}_{k+1}={\bf{b}}

(8)

with
{\bf{A}}_{k}={\bf{H}}^{\top}{\bf{H}}+\lambda{\bf{W}}^{\top}{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}}_{k})^{2}{\bf{W}}
and
{\bf{b}}={\bf{H}}^{\top}{\bf{y}}
.

Figure 1:
DEAL generates a sequence of reconstructions
{\bf{x}}_{k}
via (
6
) from the inputs
{\bf{y}}
and
{\bf{H}}
, initalization
{\bf{x}}_{0}={\bf{0}}
, and hyper-parameters
\sigma
and
\lambda
.
When the stop condition is met, it returns
\hat{{\bf{x}}}
.

The multi-convolution block
{\bf{W}}
, see Section
3.1.3
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
are learnable.
To avoid scaling ambiguities, we impose
\left\rVert{\bf{W}}\right\rVert_{2}=1
by spectral normalization.
The data
{\bf{y}}
and the forward
{\bf{H}}
are problem-specific inputs that are not learnable.
We solve (
8
) by the conjugate-gradient (CG) algorithm with
{\bf{x}}_{k}
as the initial guess.
We use a batched CG with at most
K_{\mathrm{in}}
steps, where each sample terminates individually if its residue satisfies
\left\rVert{\bf{A}}_{k}-{\bf{b}}{\bf{x}}_{k+1}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{in}}
for
\epsilon_{\mathrm{in}}>0
.

3.1.2
Mask Generation Block

To estimate
{\bf{M}}
for (
5
) from local image structures, we use the following shallow CNN with learnable nonlinearities

{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}})=(\boldsymbol{\phi}^{\sigma}\circ{\bf{W}}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{2}\circ\varphi_{2}\circ{\bf{W}}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{1}\circ\varphi_{1}\circ{\bf{W}}_{\mathrm{mask}})({\bf{x}}),

(9)

see also Figure
2
.
This choice is inspired by anisotropic diffusion
[
47
,
4
]
, where
{\bf{M}}
is typically computed from the gradients of a smoothed image using pixel-wise non-linearities.

The first multi-conv layer
{\bf{W}}_{\mathrm{mask}}
(see Section
3.1.3
) in (
9
) extracts
N_{c}
spatial features using the same architecture as the
{\bf{W}}
from the reconstruction block.
The two subsequent convolution layers
{\bf{W}}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{1}
and
{\bf{W}}_{\mathrm{mix}}^{2}
mix the
N_{c}
feature channels using kernels of size
3\times 3
and no bias.
The layers are connected via learnable point-wise non-linearities
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
, for which we follow
Bohra et al. [3]
.
Specifically, each
\varphi_{i}
is parametrized as a linear spline with
N_{n}
equally distributed knots on
[0,r]
.
On
(r,\infty)
the splines are linearly extended and we enforce symmetry by setting
\varphi(x)=\varphi(-x)
if
x<0
.

Architecture of the mask generation block.

In addition, we constrain them to be increasing for
x>0
.
Removing both constraints has not led to significant performance improvements.

To guarantee the numerical stability of (
8
), the outputs of
{\bf{M}}
must remain in
(\epsilon_{M},1]
.
Hence, we process each channel
c\in\{1\ldots N_{c}\}
individually by

\phi^{\sigma}_{c}(x)=\max\left(\min\left(\varphi_{3}(\alpha_{c}(\sigma){\bf{x}}),1\right),\epsilon_{M}\right),

(10)

where
\varphi_{3}
is a symmetric linear spline as before.
In contrast to the former splines,
\varphi_{3}
must be non-increasing on
[0,\infty)
.
The underlying rationale is that the
{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}})
should be close to
1
for small filter responses (constant image regions) and close to
0
for strong filter responses (salient edges).
Following
Goujon et al. [12]
, we enable multi-level noise training using the positive scalings

\alpha_{c}(\sigma)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{s_{c}(\sigma)}}{\sigma+10^{-5}}

(11)

with learnable linear splines
\{s_{c}\}_{c=1}^{N_{C}}
.
By design of
{\bf{M}}
, the first reconstruction block consists of a non-varying problem.

3.1.3
Multi-Conv Block

The Multi-Conv block advocated by
Goujon et al. [11]
consists of multiple convolution layers with no non-linearities in between.
It enables the efficient construction of large receptive fields.
We use three convolution layers, all with kernels of size
(9\times 9)
.
Thus, the effective field of view for this block is
(25\times 25)
.
For inputs with
N_{\mathrm{in}}
channels, the number of output channels of the convolution layers are
4N_{\mathrm{in}}
,
8N_{\mathrm{in}}
, and
N_{C}
, respectively.
We set the group size and stride to one, and do not use a bias.
In all our experiments, we use
N_{C}=128
.
This block appears in two places: once in
{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}})
as
{\bf{W}}_{\mathrm{Mask}}
and second in the reconstruction block as
{\bf{W}}
, i.e., the convolutions in (
4
).

3.2. Training

We learn the parameters
\theta
of DEAL for image denoising with additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) of varying standard variation
\sigma_{n}\in[0,50]
.
The associated denoiser
D_{\theta(\sigma_{n})}^{K_{\mathrm{out}}}({\bf{y}})
takes the measurements
{\bf{y}}
and
\sigma_{n}
as input, and returns the solution of (
6
) with at most
K_{\mathrm{out}}
iterations.
Choosing denoising as the training task has two reasons: (i) our learned model should also work for other inverse problems (often called universality), as demonstrated by
Hurault et al. [17]
,
Goujon et al. [12]
; (ii) it simplifies the computations for the reconstruction block as
{\bf{H}}={\bf{I}}
. We provide details on initializations and hyperparameters with a short ablation study in
A
.

Dataset and Loss

For the training dataset
\mathsf{D}=\{{\bf{x}}^{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, we use the images proposed in
Zhang et al. [54]
.
The images
{\bf{x}}
are corrupted by AWGN as
{\bf{y}}={\bf{x}}+\sigma_{n}{\bf{n}}
and fed into DEAL, leading to a sequence of denoised images
\{{\bf{x}}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K_{\mathrm{out}}}=D_{\theta(\sigma_{n})}^{K_{\mathrm{out}}}({\bf{y}})
.
To estimate the parameters
\theta
from the training data, we use the loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\mathbb{E}_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\bf{x}}\sim\mathsf{D}\\
\sigma_{n}\sim\mathcal{U}([0,50])\\
{\bf{n}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf{0}},{\bf{I}})\end{subarray}}\Big[\left\rVert{\bf{x}}_{K_{\mathrm{out}}}-{\bf{x}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{N_{c}}\left\rVert{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}}_{K_{\mathrm{out}}})-{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}}_{K_{\mathrm{out}}-1})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\Big]+\gamma\mathrm{TV}^{2}(\theta)\Bigg\}

with
\gamma=10^{-4}
.
This loss consists of three parts: (i) a squared error enforcing that the output matches the clean image; (ii) a squared penalty on the weight changes for the last two updates of (
6
); and (iii) an accumulated second-order total variation regularization of all learnable splines.
This last penalizes changes in their slopes (kinks) and thereby promotes simpler splines
[
3
,
9
]
.
The second term in (
12
) vanishes if the generated weights
{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}}_{k})
converge.
To promote convergence of (
6
) to a fixed point, we sample
K_{\mathrm{out}}
uniformly from
[15,60]

[
1
]
.

We minimize the loss (
12
) using Adam
[
18
]
.
At each step of the optimizer, we sample
16
patches of size
(128\times 128)
randomly from
\mathsf{D}
.
We have two training phases; first, we train the gray and color model for
70\,000
and
40\,000
steps respectively, using an initial learning rate
5\text{\times}{10}^{-4}
that is reduced to
4\text{\times}{10}^{-4}
by a cosine annealing scheduler.
Then, we continue training the gray and color model for
10\,000
and
5000
steps, respectively, with an initial learning rate
2\text{\times}{10}^{-4}
that is reduced to
1\text{\times}{10}^{-7}
by annealing.
We set
\epsilon_{\mathrm{out}}=\epsilon_{\mathrm{in}}=$1\text{\times}{10}^{-4}$
and the maximal of CG steps to
K_{\mathrm{in}}=50
.
To select the best-performing model, we evaluate its performance every
1000
training step and keep the checkpoint with the best validation performance.
We use the set3 and set12 datasets to validate the color and grayscale models.

Gradient Tracking

We train DEAL through the deep equilibrium framework
[
2
]
in the Jacobian free mode.
Specifically, we perform at most
K_{\mathrm{out}}-1
iterations without gradient tracking.
Then, after convergence, we perform one additional update (
6
) with gradient tracking.
For this, it is crucial to have an efficient backward path for the reconstruction block, namely for
\partial_{\theta}{\bf{x}}_{k+1}
as defined in (
6
).
Since backpropagating through the CG algorithm is prohibitively memory extensive, we need another solution.

From (
8
), we have that

{\bf{x}}_{k+1}={\bf{y}}-\lambda{\bf{L}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf{L}}_{k}{\bf{x}}_{k+1},

(13)

where
{\bf{L}}_{k}={\bf{M}}({\bf{x}}_{k}){\bf{W}}
.
Using the product rule, we get

\partial_{\theta}{\bf{x}}_{k+1}=-\lambda{\bf{L}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf{L}}_{k}{\bf{\partial}}_{\theta}{\bf{x}}_{k+1}-\lambda\partial_{\theta}({\bf{L}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf{L}}_{k}){\bf{x}}_{k+1}.

(14)

It follows

{\bf{A}}_{k}\partial_{\theta}{\bf{x}}_{k+1}={\bf{d}}_{k+1},

(15)

where the matrix
{\bf{A}}_{k}
is the one from (
8
), and only the right-hand-side changes to
{\bf{d}}_{k+1}=-\lambda\partial_{\theta}({\bf{L}}_{k}^{\top}{\bf{L}}_{k}){\bf{x}}_{k+1}
.
We use auto-differentiation to obtain the gradient estimate
{\bf{d}}_{k+1}
. We then find
\partial_{\theta}{\bf{x}}_{k+1}
by solving (
15
) with CG.

4. Theoretical Results

All the proofs are provided in Appendix
B
.
Proposition
4.1
guarantees the uniqueness of the updates (
6
).

Proposition 4.1
.

If
\ker({\bf{H}})\cap\ker({\bf{M}}({\bf{x}}_{k}){\bf{W}})=\{{\bf{0}}\}
, then
{\bf{A}}_{k}
is positive definite and (
8
) has a unique solution.
Moreover, if
{\bf{M}}^{2}({\bf{x}}_{k})\succeq\epsilon_{M}\mathrm{Id}
, then
{\bf{A}}_{k}\succeq{\bf{H}}^{\top}{\bf{H}}+\epsilon_{M}{\bf{W}}^{\top}{\bf{W}}
and uniqueness holds if
\ker({\bf{H}})\cap\ker({\bf{W}})=\{{\bf{0}}\}
.

The next results involve an estimate of the smallest eigenvalue of
{\bf{A}}_{k}\colon

\lambda_{\epsilon}=\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}({\bf{H}}^{\top}{\bf{H}}+\epsilon_{M}{\bf{W}}^{\top}{\bf{W}})
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If
\ker({\bf{H}})\cap\ker({\bf{W}})=\{{\bf{0}}\}
and
{\bf{M}}({\bf{x}})^{2}\succeq\epsilon_{M}\mathrm{Id}
, then
\mathcal{T}({\bf{x}},\cdot)\colon\mathbb{R}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
\|{\bf{H}}\|_{2}/\lambda_{\epsilon}
.

Next, we establish the existence of fixed points for
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,{\bf{y}})
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume
\ker({\bf{H}})\cap\ker({\bf{W}})=\{{\bf{0}}\}
and
{\bf{M}}^{2}({\bf{x}})\succeq\epsilon_{M}\mathrm{Id}
.
Then,
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,{\bf{y}})\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\ B_{r}({\bf{0}})
maps into a ball around
{\boldsymbol{0}}
with radius
r=\left\rVert{\bf{H}}{\bf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}/\lambda_{\epsilon}
.
If
{\bf{M}}^{2}\colon B_{r}({\bf{0}})\to[\epsilon,1]^{N_{\mathrm{out}}HW}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L
, then
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,{\bf{y}})
admits a fixed point and

\|\mathcal{T}({\bf{x}}_{1},{\bf{y}})-\mathcal{T}({\bf{x}}_{2},{\bf{y}})\|_{2}\leq\frac{L\left\rVert{\bf{H}}{\bf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\lambda^{2}_{\epsilon}}\|{\bf{x}}_{2}-{\bf{x}}_{1}\|_{2}.

(16)

The Lipschitz estimate (
16
) is very conservative and
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,{\bf{y}})
appears to often be even a local contraction.
If
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,{\bf{y}})
is contractive for every
{\bf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we get the following result.

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume
\ker({\bf{H}})\cap\ker({\bf{W}})=\{{\bf{0}}\}
and
{\bf{M}}^{2}({\bf{x}})\succeq\epsilon_{M}\mathrm{Id}
.
If
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,{\bf{y}})\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is contractive, i.e., if
\|\mathcal{T}({\bf{x}}_{1},{\bf{y}})-\mathcal{T}({\bf{x}}_{2},{\bf{y}})\|_{2}\leq q\|{\bf{x}}_{1}-{\bf{x}}_{2}\|_{2}
with
q<1
, then the iterations (
6
) converge to a unique fixed point
\hat{{\bf{x}}}
and

\|{\bf{x}}_{k}-\hat{{\bf{x}}}\|_{2}\leq q^{k-1}\|{\bf{x}}_{1}-{\bf{x}}_{0}\|_{2}.

(17)

In particular, we have exponential convergence of (
6
).
Moreover, if
\hat{{\bf{x}}}=\mathcal{T}(\hat{{\bf{x}}},{\bf{y}}_{1})
and
\hat{{\bf{z}}}=\mathcal{T}(\hat{{\bf{z}}},{\bf{y}}_{2})
, then it holds

\|\hat{{\bf{x}}}-\hat{{\bf{z}}}\|\leq\frac{1}{1-q}\frac{\|{\bf{H}}\|_{2}}{\lambda_{\epsilon}}\|{\bf{y}}_{1}-{\bf{y}}_{2}\|_{2}.

(18)

7. Conclusion

We have presented deep attentive least squares (DEAL) for image reconstruction.
DEAL builds upon classic signal processing ideas, which we blended with recent advances in deep learning, particularly, infinite-depth networks.
It consists of two parts: (i) an iterative refinement of intermediate reconstructions based on a least-square-type problem; and (ii) a recurrent attention mechanism that adapts the problem spatially.
We achieved competitive performance on different tasks while being able to provide interpretability, universality, and theoretical guarantees.

So far, we only trained DEAL on the denoising task.
If sufficient data is available, it appears possible to fine tune all components of DEAL to further improve its performance.
Moreover, DEAL is designed for the
\ell_{2}
data fidelity, and devising extensions for other data-fidelity terms is an interesting direction of future work.

B.1. Proof of Proposition
4.1

Proof.

Assume that there exists
{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{{\bf{0}}\}\in\ker({\bf{A}}_{k})
, namely with
{\bf{x}}^{\top}{\bf{A}}_{k}{\bf{x}}={\bf{0}}
.
By definition of
{\bf{A}}_{k}
, this implies
{\bf{x}}^{\top}{\bf{H}}^{\top}{\bf{H}}{\bf{x}}={\bf{0}}
and
{\bf{x}}^{\top}{\bf{W}}^{\top}{\bf{M}}^{2}({\bf{x}}_{k}){\bf{W}}{\bf{x}}={\bf{0}}
.
Hence, we get
{\bf{x}}\in\ker({\bf{H}})
and
{\bf{x}}\in\ker({\bf{M}}({\bf{x}}_{k}){\bf{W}})
, which is a contradiction.
For
{\bf{M}}^{2}({\bf{x}}_{k})\succeq\epsilon_{M}\mathrm{Id}
, we estimate
{\bf{A}}_{k}\succeq{\bf{H}}^{\top}{\bf{H}}+\epsilon_{M}{\bf{W}}^{\top}{\bf{W}}
and the uniqueness as in the first part.
∎

B.2. Proof of Lemma
4.2

Proof.

Let
\hat{{\bf{x}}}=\mathcal{T}({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}}_{1})
and
\hat{{\bf{z}}}=\mathcal{T}({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}}_{2})
, namely
{\bf{A}}_{{\bf{x}}}\hat{{\bf{x}}}={\bf{H}}^{\top}{\bf{y}}_{1}
and
{\bf{A}}_{{\bf{x}}}\hat{{\bf{z}}}={\bf{H}}^{\top}{\bf{y}}_{2}
.
This implies that
{\bf{A}}_{{\bf{x}}}(\hat{{\bf{x}}}-\hat{{\bf{z}}})={\bf{H}}^{\top}({\bf{y}}_{1}-{\bf{y}}_{2})
and we estimate

\|\hat{{\bf{x}}}-\hat{{\bf{z}}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|{\bf{H}}\|_{2}}{\lambda_{\epsilon}}\|{\bf{y}}_{1}-{\bf{y}}_{2}\|_{2}.\qed

(19)

B.3. Proof of Theorem
4.3

Proof.

First, we investigate the range of
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,{\bf{y}})
.
By definition of
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,{\bf{y}})
, it holds for any
{\bf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that

\|\mathcal{T}({\bf{x}},{\bf{y}})\|_{2}=\|{\bf{A}}_{k}^{-1}{\bf{H}}^{\top}{\bf{y}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\left\rVert{\bf{H}}{\bf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\lambda_{\epsilon}}.

(20)

For the second part, we want to apply Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem.
To this end, we must prove that
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,{\bf{y}})
is continuous.
Let
{\bf{x}}_{1},{\bf{x}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{1}=\mathcal{T}({\bf{x}}_{1},{\bf{y}})
and
\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{2}=\mathcal{T}({\bf{x}}_{2},{\bf{y}})
.
Then, it holds

\displaystyle{\bf{A}}_{1}\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{1}-{\bf{A}}_{2}\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{2}

\displaystyle={\bf{0}}

\displaystyle{\bf{A}}_{1}\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{1}-{\bf{A}}_{1}\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{2}

\displaystyle={\bf{A}}_{2}\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{2}-{\bf{A}}_{1}\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{2}

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{1}-\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{2}

\displaystyle={\bf{A}}_{1}^{-1}({\bf{A}}_{2}-{\bf{A}}_{1})\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{2}.

(21)

Incorporating (
20
) and the normalization
\|{\bf{W}}\|_{2}=1
, we further infer

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{1}-\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{2}\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\|{\bf{A}}_{1}^{-1}\|_{2}\|{\bf{W}}\|_{2}^{2}\|{\bf{M}}^{2}({\bf{x}}_{2})-{\bf{M}}^{2}({\bf{x}}_{1})\|_{2}\|\hat{{\bf{x}}}_{2}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{L\left\rVert{\bf{H}}{\bf{y}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\lambda^{2}_{\epsilon}}\|{\bf{x}}_{2}-{\bf{x}}_{1}\|_{2}.

(22)

Hence,
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,{\bf{y}})
is Lipschitz continuous and a fixed-point exists.
∎

B.4. Proof of Theorem
4.4

Proof.

Due to the Banach fixed point theorem, the exponential convergence rate (
17
) holds.
To estimate the difference of
\hat{{\bf{x}}}=\mathcal{T}(\hat{{\bf{x}}},{\bf{y}}_{1})
and
\hat{{\bf{z}}}=\mathcal{T}(\hat{{\bf{z}}},{\bf{y}}_{2})
, we use the contractivity of
\mathcal{T}(\cdot,{\bf{y}}_{1})
and Lemma
4.2
to get

\displaystyle\|\hat{{\bf{x}}}-\hat{{\bf{y}}}\|_{2}=\|\mathcal{T}(\hat{{\bf{x}}},{\bf{y}}_{1})-\mathcal{T}(\hat{{\bf{z}}},{\bf{y}}_{2})\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{T}(\hat{{\bf{x}}},{\bf{y}}_{1})-\mathcal{T}(\hat{{\bf{z}}},{\bf{y}}_{1})\|_{2}+\|\mathcal{T}(\hat{{\bf{z}}},{\bf{y}}_{1})-\mathcal{T}(\hat{{\bf{z}}},{\bf{y}}_{2})\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle q\|\hat{{\bf{x}}}-\hat{{\bf{z}}}\|_{2}+\frac{\|{\bf{H}}\|_{2}}{\lambda_{\epsilon}}\|{\bf{y}}_{1}-{\bf{y}}_{2}\|_{2}.

(23)

From this, we readily infer (
18
), namely

\|\hat{{\bf{x}}}-\hat{{\bf{y}}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\|{\bf{H}}\|_{2}}{(1-q)\lambda_{\epsilon}}\|{\bf{y}}_{1}-{\bf{y}}_{2}\|_{2}.\qed

(24)

Appendix D
Visualization of Model Components

Now, we inspect the components of our learned grayscale model. In Figure
13
, we depict the impulse response of
{\bf{W}}
, i.e., the block’s equivalent convolution kernels (a.k.a filters).
The convolutions of
{\bf{W}}_{\mathrm{mask}}
have similar structure as we see in Figure
14
.
In addition, we depict the learned splines in Figure
15
.
For
\phi_{c}^{\sigma}
, we visualize three different noise levels
\sigma\in\{5,15,25\}
and channels
c\in\{44,93,99\}
. The three visualized channels correspond to vertical edge filters of various scales, see Figure
13
.
They resemble threshold functions that set high responses to zero. This results in less regularization in the regions that have high responses to the filters that are often activated by the image structures. This is a desirable behavior as the structure of the image should not contribute to the regularization cost. Additionally, the widths of the last spline
\phi_{c}^{\sigma}
are increasing for all channels with respect to the noise level
\sigma
. Thus, more regularization is performed for higher noise levels. Moreover, we show the channel-wise average of the masks for the noisy and the denoised
castle
image in Figure
16
.
The masks remove the undesirable contribution of the image to the filter responses
{\bf{W}}{\bf{x}}
of the regularizer.
This results in lower penalization of the edges and yields sharper solutions.
