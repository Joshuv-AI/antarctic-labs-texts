Innovative essential oil formulations for in vitro inhibition of Biofilm-Forming Extended-Spectrum β-Lactamase–Producing Escherichia coli isolated from canine infectious diarrhea
Authors: Orathai Thongjuy, Sookruetai Boonmasawai, Sivapong Sungpradit, Phingphol Charoonrut, Kunaporn Homyog, Pathomporn Sresuwadjarey, Tipayawadee Jooypan, Pongsakorn Photcharatinnakorn, Supawan Nutakom, Arpron Leesombun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe emergence of bacteria producing extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs) has presented a substantial challenge to veterinary and human medicine. ESBL-producing Escherichia coli exhibits biofilm-forming capabilities that enhance its persistence in domestic animals, contribute to chronic infectious diarrhea, and increase the risk of treatment failure. This study investigated ESBL-producing E. coli in dogs and evaluated the antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities of NHK-EO, an innovative essential oil formulation derived from Coleus amboinicus (N), Ocimum basilicum (H), and Ocimum tenuiflorum (K).MethodsThe chemical composition of NHK-EO was analyzed using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Fifty-six rectal swab samples were randomly collected from dogs aged 3 months to 10 years, including 30 clinically healthy and 26 diarrheic animals of both sexes. Bacterial isolation and identification were conducted using conventional biochemical tests and the VITEK automated system. ESBL production was confirmed through genotypic detection of blaCTX−M using polymerase chain reaction, and antimicrobial susceptibility was evaluated to determine multidrug resistance profiles. Twenty isolates (10 from healthy and 10 from diarrheic dogs) were selected for further analyses. The antimicrobial activity of NHK-EO was assessed using the broth microdilution method, and biofilm formation and inhibition were evaluated using crystal violet staining. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation from three independent experiments.ResultsGC–MS analysis of NHK-EO revealed three major constituents: carvacrol (16.37%), eugenol (15.04%), and linalool (13.97%). Among these, four blaCTX−M–positive E. coli isolates (three from healthy dogs and one from a diarrheic dog) exhibited strong biofilm-forming ability. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of NHK-EO against the isolates was 1024–2048 µg/mL. The MIC of NHK-EO against the four blaCTX−M–positive E. coli isolates was 1024 µg/mL. NHK-EO at 1/4× MIC (256 µg/mL) exerted significant inhibitory effects on both prebiofilm and postbiofilm formation, as confirmed using scanning electron microscopy. Treated samples exhibited notably reduced biofilm mass and decreased bacterial density compared with untreated controls.ConclusionsNHK-EO possesses significant antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities against E. coli, including ESBL-producing strains, indicating its potential as an alternative therapeutic agent to mitigate antimicrobial resistance. Background

Escherichia coli, a Gram-negative bacterium, is a natural component of the gut flora with crucial importance in human and veterinary medicine. Dogs and cats, as companion animals, can serve as reservoirs for pathogenic E. coli strains, thereby posing a potential zoonotic risk to humans, including their owners (Tudu et al. 2022). The emergence of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing bacteria has posed a major global health threat in veterinary and human medicine (Peirano and Pitout 2019). ESBL-producing E. coli strains, particularly those harboring blaCTX−M, blaTEM, or blaSHV, are the most prevalent worldwide and have been widely disseminated across clinical settings, livestock, wildlife, and the environment (Zeynudin et al. 2018; Castanheira et al. 2021; Giedraitiene et al. 2022). These bacteria are associated with treatment failure because of their ability to hydrolyze several β-lactam antibiotics, broad-spectrum oxyiminocephalosporins, including third- and fourth-generation cephalosporins, and monobactams, such as aztreonam. However, carbapenems are still used to treat infections caused by these bacteria (Husna et al. 2023).

Bacterial biofilms enhance bacterial survival by protecting against mechanical, chemical, and biological threats. In addition, biofilms facilitate the horizontal transfer of antimicrobial resistance genes among bacteria. E. coli can form biofilms, and E. coli harboring blaCTX−M–type genes exhibit an increased capacity for biofilm formation (Surgers et al. 2019; Laconi et al. 2023).

Essential oils (EOs) and their components possess antibacterial and antibiofilm-producing abilities. EOs are rich in volatile monoterpenes such as thymol and carvacrol, which typically exhibit antibacterial activity, and EOs can be used individually and in combination with ampicillin to target ESBL-producing E. coli. EOs more effectively inhibit the growth of ESBL-producing E. coli than β-lactams, which have become obsolete for these types of strains (Bastida-Ramírez et al. 2024). EOs derived from Coleus amboinicus, Ocimum basilicum, and Ocimum tenuiflorum have been extensively studied for their antibacterial and antibiofilm activities. C. amboinicus extracts and EOs have demonstrated activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, including Staphylococcus aureus and Salmonella Typhimurium, with reported minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) ranging from 250 to 1024 µg/mL (Vasconcelos et al. 2017; Leesombun et al. 2023). To enhance antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities, O. basilicum EO was encapsulated into chitosan nanoparticles; the resulting product showed efficacy against S. aureus and E. coli, primarily through disruption of the bacterial cell membrane (Cai et al. 2022). Tulsi (O. tenuiflorum) EO at concentrations of 2.25% and 4.5% inhibited the growth of S. aureus (including methicillin-resistant S. aureus) and E. coli; however, it exhibited limited activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa (Yamani et al. 2016). EOs from C. amboinicus, O. basilicum, and O. tenuiflorum represent promising treatment modalities for bacterial infections. Synergistic effects may be achieved by optimizing the proportions of the major bioactive components in each EO when used in combination (Bassolé and Juliani 2012). Therefore, the development of innovative EO-based formulations derived from these plants is of particular interest for investigating their antibacterial and antibiofilm properties.

This study investigated the antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities of NHK-EO, which includes EOs from C. amboinicus L. (N), O. basilicum L. (H), and O. tenuiflorum L. (K), against ESBL-producing E. coli isolates from the rectal swab samples of both healthy dogs and dogs with bacterial diarrhea, specifically focusing on the detection of blaCTX−M. The findings of this study will facilitate the discovery of new alternative treatments against drug-resistant bacterial infections in pets.

Material and methods

EO extraction and preparation

EOs from C. amboinicus L., O. basilicum L., and O. tenuiflorum L. were obtained from Nonthaburi Province, Central Thailand. Botanical identification and authentication of the specimens were conducted, and voucher specimens were deposited at the Sireeruckhachati Nature Learning Park, Faculty of Pharmacy, Mahidol University (C. amboinicus: PBM No. 005507−8; O. basilicum: PBM No. 005872−3; O. tenuiflorum: PBM No. 005879−80).

EOs were extracted via steam distillation as previously described (Leesombun et al. 2023). The obtained EOs were stored in a light-protected cabinet at 4 °C until use. Specific proportions of EOs derived from C. amboinicus, O. basilicum, and O. tenuiflorum were combined to prepare NHK-EO. A stock solution was prepared at a concentration of 40 mg/mL prior to use, and all experiments were performed using the same batch of NHK-EO.

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS)

The chemical composition of NHK-EO was analyzed using GC–MS (Model 7890 A-MS5975C, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The system was equipped with a DB-5HT capillary column (30 m × 250 μm × 0.1 μm, Agilent Technologies). The sample was prepared in methanol and injected in split mode with a split ratio of 1:10. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1 mL/min. The injection port temperature was maintained at 250 °C. The column temperature program was set as follows: an initial temperature of 40 °C was maintained for 5 min, followed by an increment of 10 °C/min until reaching 250 °C, which was maintained for an additional 5 min (Leesombun et al. 2023).

MS was conducted under the following conditions: ion source temperature of 250 °C, ionization energy of 70 eV, and mass scan range of 30–550 m/z. The detected compounds were identified by comparing their mass spectra with entries in the Wiley 10th edition (W10N14.L) and NIST 2014 spectral libraries, comparing their retention times with authentic standards, including eugenol, carvacrol, linalool, methyl chavicol, β-caryophyllene, geraniol, and thymol (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA).

Sample collection

This study was conducted in strict accordance with animal care recommendations. The protocol was approved by the Faculty of Veterinary Science-Animal Care and Use Committee (MUVS-2021-11−46) and Faculty of Veterinary Science-Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC/MUVS-B-019–2566).

Samples were collected from dogs treated at Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital, Prasu Arthorn Animal Hospital (Nakhon Pathom, Thailand). Rectal swabs were collected using Amies Agar Gel Transport Swabs (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). In total, 56 rectal swabs were randomly collected from both male and female dogs aged 3 months to 10 years, including 30 clinically healthy individuals and 26 with diarrhea. All dogs had a history of at least one antimicrobial treatment in the preceding year. In the diarrheic group, bacterial diarrhea was diagnosed through clinical evaluation (history and physical examination), exclusion of parasitic and viral causes (via fecal smear/flotation and rapid antigen testing), and confirmation using bacterial culture. To prevent repeated or clustered sampling, only one specimen was collected per dog, and cohoused animals were excluded. A CONSORT flow diagram is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Bacterial culture and identification

Each sample was streaked onto MacConkey agar (Clinical Diagnostics Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) and incubated at 35 °C ± 2 °C overnight. Presumptive identification of all isolates was achieved using Gram staining and a panel of biochemical tests, including indole, methyl red, Voges–Proskauer, citrate, triple sugar iron, and oxidase (Odonkor and Ampofo 2013). Identification was subsequently confirmed with the VITEK MS V3.1 system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). Furthermore, all isolates were subjected to molecular analysis using specific primers targeting E. coli and blaCTX−M (Saladin et al. 2002; Woodford et al. 2006; Yadav et al. 2009; Magray et al. 2011).

E. coli DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from E. coli isolates using a modified boiling method (Bai et al. 2010). In brief, a single colony was picked and resuspended in 100 µL of RNase-free water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The mixture was then incubated at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min to pellet the cell debris. The supernatant containing the extracted DNA was transferred to a new tube. The DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a Nanodrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) by calculating the 260/280 and 260/230 nm ratios. The DNA was subsequently stored at − 20 °C until further analysis.

Amplification and detection of the β-lactamase gene

DNA extracted from boiled bacterial colonies was used as a template to amplify a 543-bp fragment of blaCTX−M via conventional PCR (Saladin et al. 2002). PCR was performed using a standard PCR master mix containing TopTaq master mix (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), dNTPs, and buffer, along with specific primers targeting blaCTX−M. An ESBL-producing E. coli strain harboring blaCTX−M group 1 and nuclease-free water served as the positive and negative controls, respectively. To verify DNA quality, the 16 S rRNA was amplified using universal 16 S rRNA primers (Yadav et al. 2009; Magray et al. 2011). Samples positive for blaCTX−M were further analyzed using multiplex PCR to determine the specific gene groups. Amplification was performed using a T100™ thermal cycler (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Supplementary Table 2 presents the thermocycling conditions. The PCR products were separated on a 2% agarose gel stained with GelRed™ (Biotium, Fremont, CA, USA) and visualized using a c150 UV transilluminator (Azure Biosystems, Dublin, CA, USA).

DNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

PCR products from blaCTX−M–positive E. coli isolates were purified and sent to Macrogen Laboratory (Seoul, South Korea) for sequencing. DNA sequencing was performed using fluorescent dye–terminator technology on an ABI 3730xl sequencer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The resulting sequences were compared with the GenBank database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST; http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), and sequence alignments were conducted with Clustal W (Larkin et al. 2007).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing for all 56 E. coli isolates was conducted using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA, Clinical Diagnostics Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) following Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (CLSI 2023). E. coli isolates were prepared by suspending colonies in 0.85% sodium chloride (NaCl) to an optical density (OD) equivalent to the 0.5 McFarland standard turbidity (approximately 1 × 108 colony-forming units [CFU]/mL) using a densitometer (DEN-1B, Biosan, Gibthai Co., Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand).

The bacterial suspension was streaked onto MHA using sterile cotton swabs, followed by incubation with selected antimicrobial disks from various antimicrobial classes, including penicillins (ampicillin, 10 µg); β-lactamase inhibitors (amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, 30 µg; piperacillin-tazobactam, 110 µg); cephalosporins including second-generation (cefoxitin, 30 µg), third-generation (cefotaxime, 30 µg; ceftriaxone, 30 µg; ceftazidime, 30 µg; cefpodoxime, 10 µg), and fourth-generation drugs (cefepime, 30 µg); fluoroquinolones (enrofloxacin, 5 µg; ciprofloxacin, 5 µg; norfloxacin, 10 µg); aminoglycosides (amikacin, 30 µg; gentamicin, 10 µg); carbapenems (imipenem, 10 µg; meropenem, 10 µg); sulfonamides/dihydrofolate reductase inhibitors (sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim, 25 µg); and tetracyclines (doxycycline, 30 µg) (Clinical Diagnostics Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand).

Then, agar plates with antimicrobial disks were incubated at 35 °C ± 2 °C for 16–20 h. The inhibition zone diameter was recorded (mm). Isolates were categorized as susceptible, intermediate, or resistant based on antimicrobial susceptibility according to the breakpoint criteria recommended by the CLSI (CLSI 2023), with E. coli ATCC 25922 used as the quality control strain.

Determination of MIC and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

In total, 20 isolates (10 from healthy dogs and 10 from diarrheic dogs) were selected for these assays. Within each group, all ESBL-producing isolates were included by default, and the remaining isolates were randomly selected from the non-ESBL pool. NHK-EO was prepared as a stock solution (40 mg/mL) with 1% Tween-80 and then serially diluted 2-fold in Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB, Clinical Diagnostics Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) to obtain final concentrations of 64–4096 µg/mL. Subsequently, 100 µL of each concentration was added to the wells of a 96-well plate. The highest concentration of Tween-80 (0.1%) used for solubilization was included as the control. E. coli inoculum was prepared by suspending a single colony in 0.85% NaCl and adjusting the turbidity to match a 0.5 McFarland standard. The bacterial suspension was then diluted in MHB to achieve a final concentration of approximately 5 × 105 CFU/mL. One hundred microliters of the bacterial suspension was added to each well of a 96-well plate containing various concentrations of NHK-EO. The final volume per well was 200 µL. The treated plates were incubated at 35 °C ± 2 °C for 24 h. E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as the quality control strain, and ciprofloxacin served as the positive control. The treatments were performed in triplicate wells and repeated for all experiments.

After the incubation period, 30 µL of resazurin (0.03%) was added to each well, followed by further incubation for 2–4 h. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of NHK-EO that did not change the color of resazurin (remained blue). The MBC, defined as the lowest concentration of the treatment at which no bacterial colonies were present (Larsuprom et al. 2019), was determined by taking 10 µL from each well that did not change the color of resazurin, streaking it onto a nutrient agar plate (Clinical Diagnostics Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand), and incubating the plate at 35 °C ± 2 °C for 24 h.

Biofilm classification and antibiofilm-producing activity

A single colony from each of the 20 selected E. coli isolates was suspended in 0.85% NaCl to achieve an OD equivalent to that of the 0.5 McFarland standard. The suspension was subsequently inoculated into brain heart infusion (BHI) broth (Clinical Diagnostics Ltd., Bangkok, Thailand) and adjusted to a bacterial concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. One hundred microliters of each bacterial suspension was transferred into the individual wells of a 96-well microtiter plate and incubated at 35 °C ± 2 °C for 24 h, followed by three gentle washes with 200 µL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove nonadherent cells. The biofilms were fixed by adding 200 µL of 95% methanol for 15 min, after which the methanol was discarded. The biofilms were stained with 150 µL of 0.3% crystal violet for 15 min at room temperature. Excess stain was removed by washing the wells three times with PBS, and the plates were allowed to air-dry at room temperature.

To quantify biofilm formation, the crystal violet–stained biofilm was solubilized by adding 150 µL of 33% (v/v) glacial acetic acid to each well. The resulting solution was transferred to a new 96-well plate, and OD was measured at 590 nm using a micro-ELISA plate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). The strains were categorized into four groups based on OD relative to the mean OD of the negative control (ODc) as follows: nonadherent (OD ≤ ODc), weak biofilm producers (ODc < OD ≤ 2 × ODc), moderate biofilm producers (2 × ODc < OD ≤ 4 × ODc), and strong biofilm producers (OD > 4 × ODc) (Ramírez Castillo et al. 2023).

The antibiofilm activity of NHK-EO was evaluated in two stages: (1) inhibition of prebiofilm formation and (2) disruption of established biofilms, following the method of Leesombun et al. (2023). Twenty isolates were tested. For the inhibition assay, bacterial suspensions were inoculated into wells containing NHK-EO at concentrations of 1/4×, 1/2×, 1×, and 2× MIC. Wells containing medium and NHK-EO without bacteria served as blanks. Plates were incubated at 35 °C ± 2 °C for 24 h. For the disruption assay, biofilms were allowed to form for 24 h under the same conditions, after which NHK-EO was added at the same concentrations and incubated for another 24 h. Biofilm biomass was then quantified using crystal violet staining. All experiments were performed in triplicate (technical replicates) and independently repeated three times (biological replicates).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

A suspension of E. coli at a concentration of 5 × 105 CFU/mL in BHI broth was prepared and cultured in a 12-well plate containing sterile round coverslips (18 mm diameter; NUNC™, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each well was supplemented with NHK-EO at concentrations equivalent to 1/8×, 1/4×, and 1/2× MIC. The plates were incubated at 35 °C ± 2 °C for 24 h. The supernatant was carefully removed after incubation, and each well was washed three times with PBS to eliminate nonadherent cells.

The coverslips were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 60 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the attached cells and biofilms were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100%), with each step lasting 10 min at room temperature. The coverslips were then subjected to critical point drying and coated with gold using a sputter coater to prepare them for SEM (JSM-6610LV scanning electron microscope, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) (Shi et al. 2016).

Statistical analysis

The percentage of biofilm inhibition is presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between treated and untreated controls for each isolate were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a 95% confidence interval. When the assumptions for parametric testing were not met, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was employed as a nonparametric alternative. The relationship between health status and biofilm categories was analyzed using the chi-square test of independence. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 21.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Screening of ESBL-producing E. coli and blaCTX−M

In total, 78 Gram-negative bacterial isolates were obtained from the 56 rectal swab samples. Among these, 56 isolates (71.79%) were identified as E. coli. Of these 56 E. coli isolates, 4 (7.14%) tested positive for blaCTX−M (4/56 blaCTX−M–positive isolates) (Supplementary Fig. 3). The PCR results revealed that three isolates (75%), including two from healthy dogs (H5 and H11) and one from a dog with bacterial diarrhea (D13), belonged to blaCTX−M−9, whereas one isolate (25%) from a healthy dog (H16) belonged to blaCTX−M−1 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

BLAST analysis revealed that isolates H5, H11, and D13 (blaCTX−M−9) shared 100% sequence similarity with E. coli isolate 14 blaCTX−M−98 (accession number KP317201). Meanwhile, isolate H16 (blaCTX−M−1) exhibited 99% similarity to E. coli strain Ajums AN3 blaCTX−M (accession number OQ320799). Notably, only 73% similarity was observed between the sequences from groups 1 and 9 (Supplementary Fig. 5). The following accession numbers were assigned: D13 (PV809613), H5 (PV809614), H11 (PV809615), and H16 (PV809616).

Antimicrobial activity of NHK-EO

The MICs of NHK-EO ranged 1024–2048 µg/mL across isolates from both healthy dogs and dogs with bacterial diarrhea, indicating a consistent inhibitory effect across different clinical conditions.

The MIC at which ≥ 50% of the isolates were inhibited (MIC50) was 1024 µg/mL for both groups. However, MIC90 was 1024 µg/mL for isolates from healthy dogs versus 2048 µg/mL for isolates from dogs with bacterial diarrhea. The four blaCTX−M–carrying E. coli isolates had an MIC of 1024 µg/mL and MBC of 2048 µg/mL (Table 2). The MBC/MIC ratio of NHK-EO indicated a bactericidal effect against all isolates.

Antibiofilm activity of NHK-EO

NHK-EO exhibited significant inhibitory effects against both prebiofilm and postbiofilm formation in E. coli isolates. Among the isolates obtained from healthy dogs and those with bacterial diarrhea, NHK-EO at concentrations of 1/4× MIC (256 µg/mL) or higher effectively inhibited prebiofilm formation by more than 40% (Fig. 3a, b). Notably, prebiofilm formation was inhibited by more than 80% in isolates H5, H11, H16, and D13, which harbored blaCTX−M, following treatment with NHK-EO at 1/4× MIC (Fig. 3c).

In the postbiofilm inhibition assay, NHK-EO at 1/4× MIC (256 µg/mL) inhibited biofilm formation by E. coli isolates obtained from healthy dogs by more than 50% (Fig. 4a, b). In isolates obtained from dogs with bacterial diarrhea, NHK-EO at the same concentration inhibited biofilm formation by more than 75%. In particular, biofilm formation was inhibited by more than 50% among isolates H5, H11, H16, and D13 (Fig. 4c). SEM

The ability of NHK-EO to inhibit biofilm formation by E. coli was further corroborated through SEM (Figs. 5 and 6). After 24 h of incubation, E. coli ATCC 25922 formed well-defined biofilms when cultured in medium alone. These biofilms comprised planktonic cells entrapped within an extracellular matrix (Figs. 5a and 6a and e). E. coli cells displayed appendages that facilitated attachment to other cells, contributing to the formation of a robust biofilm. A reduction in biofilm production was observed in E. coli treated with NHK-EO at 1/8×, 1/4×, and 1/2× MIC, along with a decrease in bacterial density (Fig. 6b–d and f–h). Notably, following treatment at 1/2× MIC (Fig. 6d, h), the characteristic appendages of E. coli cells were no longer detectable, and morphological alterations were observed in comparison to the findings in the untreated control group.

Discussion

The increasing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance, particularly among ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, represents a significant global health challenge. Healthy dogs can act as asymptomatic carriers of ESBL-producing E. coli, which has also been detected in livestock and wildlife populations globally (Benavides et al. 2021). ESBL-producing E. coli can cause infections in multiple systems, including the urinary (Facchin et al. 2025) and gastrointestinal tracts, leading to bacterial diarrhea (Tudu et al. 2022). Both healthy dogs and those with bacterial diarrhea have been identified as potential reservoirs for these resistant strains (Formenti et al. 2021), and this was considered a critical issue within the One Health framework (Sartori et al. 2023). A study conducted in Thailand revealed the presence of blaCTX−M in dogs (21.69%), cats (71.97%), veterinarians (27.78%), and pet owners (21.43%). In addition, blaTEM and blaSHV have been reported in these populations (Buranasinsup et al. 2023). The risk factors associated with the emergence of these strains include hospitalization, prior antimicrobial use, exposure to veterinary clinics, and international travel (Karkaba et al. 2019; Facchin et al. 2025). The prevalence of blaCTX−M was higher among ESBL-producing E. coli than among other isolates (Formenti et al. 2021).

In this study, we identified blaCTX−M–carrying E. coli belonging to different groups, including blaCTX−M−1 and blaCTX−M−9, in 10.00% of healthy dogs and 3.85% of dogs with bacterial diarrhea, emphasizing the role of dogs as significant carriers of these resistant pathogens. All dogs had a history of antimicrobial exposure within the past year, which may have contributed to the observed prevalence of ESBL-producing E. coli, particularly among healthy carriers. These findings highlight the potential role of dogs as important reservoirs for resistant pathogens. This observation is consistent with that of a previous study conducted in Thailand in 2018, which reported a prevalence of ESBL-producing E. coli in 25.84% of fecal samples collected from healthy dogs (Thepmanee et al. 2018), further emphasizing the interconnected transmission of antimicrobial resistance genes among humans, animals, and the environment.

Beining et al. found that ESBL-producing E. coli isolates exhibited complete resistance to several antibiotics, including ampicillin, cefazolin, cephalexin, and clindamycin (Werhahn Beining et al. 2023). Similarly, blaCTX−M–carrying E. coli isolates obtained in this study exhibited 100% resistance to ampicillin, cefotaxime, ceftriaxone, cefpodoxime, and gentamicin while remaining 98.21% susceptible to imipenem. Carbapenems, such as imipenem and meropenem, are considered the last treatment options for ESBL-producing E. coli. Piperacillin/tazobactam is also used in clinical settings because tazobactam effectively inhibits the hydrolytic activity of most ESBL enzymes. However, the efficacy of this regimen against inhibitor-resistant ESBL variants might be limited. Notably, piperacillin is a good substrate for both ESBLs and narrow-spectrum β-lactamases (Horiyama et al. 2015). Among the isolates from healthy dogs, one strain (H5) exhibited intermediate susceptibility to imipenem and resistance to both meropenem and piperacillin/tazobactam. Given this resistance profile, screening for carbapenemase genes should be considered. Carbapenem resistance can develop through the acquisition of carbapenemase genes or through mechanisms such as reduced outer membrane permeability and/or increased efflux activity, which lower intracellular carbapenem concentrations (Logan et al. 2017). A previous study on ESBL-producing Enterobacterales reported that the development of carbapenem resistance typically requires both the presence of an ESBL and a reduction in outer membrane porin function (Tängdén et al. 2013). The present findings highlight the urgent need for vigilant monitoring and implementation of effective management strategies to address the emergence of carbapenem-resistant E. coli. Furthermore, broader genotypic investigations of blaCTX−M–harboring E. coli in larger canine populations are crucial for improving treatment precision.

Bacterial biofilm formation enhances antimicrobial resistance, posing a significant challenge in infection control. E. coli isolates obtained from street food exhibited a biofilm production rate of 46.34% (19/41 isolates), with most biofilm-producing strains (17/19) demonstrating multidrug resistance. Furthermore, E. coli isolates carrying blaCTX−M showed a significantly higher prevalence of multidrug resistance (7/9 isolates) than those lacking this gene. These findings suggest potential correlations between biofilm formation and multidrug resistance, blaCTX−M presence, and antimicrobial resistance (Adhikari et al. 2023). In this study, ESBL-producing E. coli isolates carrying blaCTX−M were strong biofilm producers. However, the correlation between ESBL production and biofilm formation varied across studies. For example, Giedraitiene et al. and Ballén et al. reported no significant association between the presence of ESBL genes and biofilm formation (Ballén et al. 2022; Giedraitiene et al. 2022). However, Shrestha et al. identified a weak positive correlation (r = 0.157) between biofilm formation and ESBL production in uropathogenic E. coli (Shrestha et al., 2019). Although the present study reported strong biofilm-forming ability among blaCTX−M–carrying ESBL-producing E. coli, further investigations involving a larger number of isolates are necessary to more accurately assess the correlation between ESBL production and biofilm formation.

Ongoing research on EOs has emphasized their significant potential as antibacterial and antibiofilm agents. EOs exert effects against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, with Gram-positive bacteria generally exhibiting greater susceptibility than Gram-negative bacteria (Chouhan et al. 2017). A possible mechanism of the antibacterial activity of EOs is cell membrane destruction, resulting in the leakage of intracellular materials (Nagoor Meeran et al. 2017; Iseppi et al. 2020). Owing to their lipophilic properties, EOs and their constituents, such as carvacrol, can diffuse through the polysaccharide matrix of biofilms, leading to their destabilization. The antibiofilm activity of EOs is also attributed to the ability of certain phytochemicals to inhibit quorum sensing, a mechanism that regulates bacterial virulence and biofilm formation (Gómez-Sequeda et al. 2020). These mechanisms may also contribute to the antibacterial and antibiofilm effects exerted by NHK-EO.

Given the recent development of drug resistance in blaCTX−M–carrying E. coli, a specific EO mixture was developed to address this critical problem. The EO of C. amboinicus comprises three primary compounds: carvacrol (38.26%), γ-terpinene (12.29%), and p-cymene (8.62%) (Leesombun et al. 2023). Similarly, O. basilicum EO contains linalool (41.2%) and estragole (30.1%) among its 37 identified components (Mohamed Abdoul-Latif et al. 2022). Another report indicated that the major constituents of O. basilicum EO are methyl eugenol (39.3%) and methyl chavicol (38.3%), which collectively account for 98.6% of the total oil (Joshi 2014). The EO of O. tenuiflorum contains caryophyllene oxide (23.8%), β-caryophyllene (18.2%), germacrene D (14.6%), α-copaene (14.4%), and eugenol (10.0%) as its predominant components (Kunihiro et al. 2022).

Our study on the innovative EO blend demonstrated enhanced antibacterial activity against drug-resistant bacteria. The newly developed formulation, NHK-EO, contained high levels of carvacrol, eugenol, and linalool and exhibited strong antibacterial and antibiofilm effects. The combination of phenolic compounds and monoterpenes produced synergistic activity against several microorganisms. In particular, synergistic interactions between phenolics such as thymol and carvacrol as well as their combination with eugenol were observed against E. coli strains. However, a previous study reported additive rather than synergistic effects for these combinations (Bassolé and Juliani 2012). Therefore, the enhanced activity of NHK-EO may result from synergistic interactions among the active constituents of its component oils.

This study demonstrated that NHK-EO possessed antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities against E. coli, including ESBL-producing strains. Notably, only four blaCTX−M–carrying E. coli isolates were tested in the NHK-EO assays. Although these findings provide meaningful insights, further studies involving larger collections of ESBL-producing isolates are needed to validate and expand upon these results. SEM of the reference strain E. coli ATCC 25922 supported the antibiofilm effect, revealing a marked reduction in bacterial density and biofilm structure along with clear morphological alterations compared with the untreated control. Future studies incorporating SEM analysis of ESBL-positive isolates are necessary to confirm whether similar biofilm disruptions occur across different resistance profiles.

Despite these promising results, the observed MIC values (1024–2048 µg/mL) were relatively high compared with concentrations typically achievable in systemic therapy. This suggests that systemic administration may be impractical, whereas topical applications such as for skin, wound, or oral infections could be more feasible. A key limitation of this study is the absence of cytotoxicity and ecotoxicity assessments as well as the lack of in vivo experiments, leaving the safety profile of NHK-EO undetermined. Moreover, further studies are needed to evaluate potential synergistic effects, optimize formulation strategies and delivery systems, and establish pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic feasibility before considering clinical use.

Conclusions

NHK-EO demonstrated in vitro antibacterial and antibiofilm activities against E. coli, including blaCTX−M–harboring strains. However, given the relatively high MIC values and the absence of toxicity and in vivo efficacy data, additional studies on formulation development, synergy testing, and safety evaluation are warranted before considering clinical applications.
