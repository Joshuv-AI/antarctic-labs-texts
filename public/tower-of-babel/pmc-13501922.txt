Real-world use of novel β-lactams in immunologically vulnerable patients: insights from an Italian retrospective cohort
Authors: Matilde Bognetti, Federico Fama, Claudia Conflitti, Fabio Brivio, Marco Piscaglia, Monica Schiavini, Maria Vittoria Cossu, Stefania Vimercati, Elisabetta Rossin, Teresa De Falco, Cristina Pagani, Sara Giordana Rimoldi, Andrea Gori, Marta Colaneri, Emanuele Palomba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesMultidrug-resistant gram-negative bacteria pose a significant global health threat, particularly among vulnerable populations, such as the elderly and immunocompromised patients. Novel β-lactam (NBL) antibiotics offer promising treatment options, but real-world evidence on their efficacy in these populations remains limited. To describe the use of NBLs in clinical practice, focusing on immunologically vulnerable patients, including those with immunosuppression due to underlying disease or immunosuppressive therapy, as well as elderly individuals. Clinical characteristics, outcomes and microbiological data were descriptively analysed.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study was conducted at two university hospitals in Milan, Italy, from 2019 to 2024. Adult patients receiving NBLs (cefiderocol, ceftazidime/avibactam, ceftolozane/tazobactam, imipenem/relebactam or meropenem/vaborbactam) were included. Immunologically vulnerable patients were identified by at least one of the following conditions: active oncological disease, solid organ transplantation, autoimmune or rheumatological disorder requiring immunosuppressive therapy, HIV infection or age >85 years. A descriptive analysis of clinical and microbiological data was performed, and the clinical course was described using clinical and biochemical parameters.ResultsOverall, 233 patients were included, 80 (34.3%) of whom were immunologically vulnerable; 308 NBL prescriptions were recorded. Ceftazidime/avibactam was the most prescribed agent (61.4%). Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (50.0%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (41.4%) were predominant pathogens. Despite greater baseline clinical severity among non-vulnerable patients, all-cause mortality and clinical improvement at T1 were similar between the two groups.ConclusionsIn a real-world Italian cohort spanning 5 years, NBLs were widely used in immunologically vulnerable patients, a population often underrepresented in pivotal antibiotic trials, with K. pneumoniae carbapenemase–producing K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa as predominant pathogens. Unadjusted comparisons showed similar clinical outcomes despite greater baseline severity among non-vulnerable patients, a hypothesis-generating finding warranting confirmation in adjusted, prospective studies.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance, driven by multidrug-resistant gram-negative bacteria (MDR-GNB), is a pressing global health crisis. In 2019, antimicrobial resistance was linked to approximately 4.95 million deaths worldwide, with 1.27 million directly attributable to multidrug-resistant bacteria.1 The World Health Organization’s 2024 Bacterial Priority Pathogens List identifies carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB), carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) and third-generation cephalosporin-resistant Enterobacterales as critical priorities, with carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa classified as high priority.2 In Italy, MDR-GNB, including extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacterales, CRE, carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa and CRAB, are endemic, complicating treatment strategies.3–5

Against this backdrop, high-risk populations represent a key concern in the epidemiology of MDR-GNB infections, with immunocompromised patients particularly affected owing to impaired immune responses, frequent healthcare exposure and a high comorbidity burden. Immune impairment spans a spectrum of disease- and treatment-related factors, including oncological conditions, immunosuppressive therapy and transplantation, alongside age-related immunosenescence and frailty-related factors, such as sarcopenia, malnutrition, cognitive impairment and polypharmacy, all of which increase susceptibility to MDR-GNB colonization and infection.6–8 This burden is well documented in European cohorts of haematologic patients and is further confirmed by Italian data on MDR-GNB bloodstream infections9 and P. aeruginosa infections in immunocompromised populations.10 In response to the growing threat of resistance and limited treatment options, novel β-lactam (NBL) antibiotics (including cefiderocol and β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, such as ceftazidime/avibactam, ceftolozane/tazobactam, imipenem/cilastatin/relebactam and meropenem/vaborbactam) have been introduced as crucial additions to the therapeutic armamentarium against MDR-GNB infections, targeting resistant pathogens through mechanisms, such as siderophore-mediated uptake and enhanced β-lactamase inhibition.

Elderly and severely immunocompromised patients, among those most affected by MDR-GNB infections, are frequently excluded from or underrepresented in the pivotal trials underpinning regulatory approval of novel antibiotics, including the NBLs themselves.11

Nonetheless, several recent multicentre observational studies have evaluated the real-world use of NBLs in these groups, including multinational data for ceftolozane/tazobactam and Italian cohorts investigating cefiderocol and meropenem/vaborbactam.12–14

However, few studies have specifically focused on immunologically vulnerable patients. This is likely due to several contributing factors, including the marked heterogeneity of conditions associated with immunocompromise and the lack of a universally accepted classification framework. However, this population is expected to grow steadily as a result of the expanding use of novel immunomodulatory therapies across different medical specialties, together with increasing life expectancy among medically fragile populations, highlighting the need for dedicated research in this field. In this context, the present study sought to describe the real-world use of NBLs in immunologically vulnerable patients, adopting a pragmatic, composite definition to reflect this heterogeneous population, through a descriptive, hypothesis-generating approach.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective observational study was conducted at two university hospitals in Milan, Italy (Luigi Sacco Hospital and Fatebenefratelli Hospital) between January 2019 and December 2024, including adults (aged ≥18 years) with complete clinical records who received at least one dose of a NBL antibiotic (cefiderocol, ceftazidime/avibactam, ceftolozane/tazobactam, imipenem/cilastatin/relebactam or meropenem/vaborbactam) for suspected or confirmed MDR-GNB infections. The study was approved by the Comitato Etico Territoriale Lombardia 1 (22/01/2025, protocol number CET 29-2025). Informed consent was managed in accordance with Article 110 of the Italian Data Protection Code (Legislative Decree 196/2003, as amended), following a favourable opinion from the reviewing Ethics Committee. Data were collected from paper drug request forms completed by infectious disease specialists and archived by hospital pharmacies, identified by medical record number and cross-referenced with digital medical records for accuracy. Collected variables included demographics, comorbidities (e.g. hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, oncohaematological malignancies and other state of immunosuppression), infection details (site, microbiological isolates and resistance patterns) and antibiotic therapy (NBL prescribed and prior antibiotics, if available).

Clinical and laboratory parameters, including body temperature, oxygen therapy, presence of shock and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, were assessed at baseline (T0) and 72 h post-treatment initiation (T1).

Patients were followed for the whole hospital stay until discharge, transfer to another hospital or death. Data were entered into a REDCap database for standardized collection.15

Definitions

Immunologically vulnerable patients were defined as those with at least one of the following conditions: (i) solid or haematological malignancies under active treatment or with evidence of active disease within the previous 12 months; (ii) history of solid organ transplantation; (iii) HIV infection; (iv) age >85 years16 and (v) autoimmune or rheumatological disorder requiring immunosuppressive therapy. The definition of immunosuppressive therapy is provided in Supplementary Materials S0 (available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).17–21

This composite definition was applied for pragmatic clinical purposes, reflecting patients commonly recognized in routine infectious diseases practice as being at increased infectious risk, rather than a standardized or previously validated immunological classification. The individual criteria were not weighted or combined into a severity score and were treated as equally qualifying for inclusion in the vulnerable group.

Microorganisms were defined as carbapenem resistant when resistant to meropenem or imipenem according to the EUCAST breakpoints.22 For P. aeruginosa, difficult-to-treat resistance (DTR) was defined as non-susceptibility to all of the following: piperacillin/tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefepime, aztreonam, meropenem, imipenem/cilastatin, ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin.23

A history of MDR-GNB infection, antibiotic therapy or exposure to NBLs within the 90 days preceding the index episode was recorded.

Fever was defined as having a body temperature >37.8°C; fever resolution was defined as having a body temperature <37.2°C at T1. Biochemical response was assessed at T0 and T1 (±24 h) via the CRP-ratio trend, with CRP resolution defined as a CRP-ratio at T1 of <0.4.24

Improvement in respiratory status was defined as transitioning from higher to lower levels of respiratory support (in order: mechanical ventilation, high-flow nasal cannula or non-invasive ventilation, Venturi mask, nasal cannula and no oxygen therapy), including the achievement of ambient air breathing.

Shock was defined as hypotension requiring vasopressors with evidence of organ dysfunction and persistent lactate elevation despite adequate fluid resuscitation.25

An infection was defined as microbiologically confirmed when physician diagnosis was supported by a positive culture from a clinically relevant specimen.

Microbiological methods

Microbiological data were retrospectively collected from laboratory reports available in the hospital electronic medical records.

Pathogen identification and susceptibility testing

Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were performed by the respective clinical microbiology laboratories, according to routine diagnostic procedures. Bacterial strains were identified by Mass spectrometry Maldi-toff (bioMérieux, France); automated systems (e.g. VITEK® 2, bioMérieux) were used for susceptibility testing; and the results were interpreted following current EUCAST guidelines at the time of the analysis.22

Resistance mechanism identification

Resistance mechanisms were determined using genotypic molecular assays (Cepheid Gene Xpert, USA), as deemed necessary by the treating clinicians.

Screening protocols and timing for MDR-GNB colonization

Screening for MDR-GNB colonization followed local infection control policies. In general, rectal swabs were collected at hospital admission and, when indicated, periodically during hospitalization (e.g. weekly or at the onset of infection). The presence or absence of colonization was recorded based on the available laboratory reports.

Objectives

The study aimed to provide a descriptive analysis of the real-world use of NBLs, reflecting routine clinical practice, with a particular focus on immunologically vulnerable patients. Specifically, the study characterized the demographic, clinical and microbiological features of these patients. Clinical course was assessed, including length of hospital stay (LOS), time to fever resolution, oxygen therapy de-escalation, shock resolution, CRP trends and in-hospital overall and 30 days mortality. A secondary analysis was conducted on patients with microbiologically confirmed infection, focusing on available isolates to further explore microbiological features.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as medians with IQRs and categorical variables as percentages. Differences between immunologically vulnerable and non-vulnerable patients, and across study years, were assessed as unadjusted descriptive comparisons using χ2 or Fisher's exact tests for proportions, the Mann–Whitney test for continuous variables and the Kaplan–Meier estimator with the log-rank test for survival. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1) and validated by an independent statistician.

Overall, 34.3% of patients (80/233) were immunologically vulnerable, primarily due to active solid tumours (47.5%, 38/80), followed by age >85 years (22.5%, 18/80), haematological malignancies (20%, 16/80), HIV infection (12.5%, 10/80), autoimmune diseases (10%, 8/80) and solid organ transplant (0.7%, 1/80); 13 out of 80 were classified based on the basis of age alone.

NBL prescriptions

Of the 308 prescriptions, 104 (33.8%) were administered to immunologically vulnerable patients and 204 (66.2%) to non-vulnerable patients (Table 2). Ceftazidime/avibactam was the most prescribed NBL (61.4%, 189/308), followed by cefiderocol (14.9%, 46/308), ceftolozane/tazobactam (14.6%, 45/308), meropenem/vaborbactam (8.4%, 26/308) and imipenem/relebactam (0.6%, 2/308). ICU prescriptions were significantly more frequent among non-immunologically vulnerable patients (32.8% versus 17.3%, P = 0.0060), while medical ward prescriptions were similarly distributed (64.4% versus 58.3%); surgical ward (13.5% versus 6.4%) and emergency department (4.8% versus 1.5%) prescriptions were more frequent among vulnerable patients (Table 2).

Prior antibiotic use within 90 days was reported in 86.4% of cases (266 prescriptions), with no significant differences between groups; about one-third of all NBL prescriptions (33.1%) were administered to patients already receiving a carbapenem.

NBL use increased progressively over the study period, from 16 prescriptions in 2019 to 62 in 2024 (peak 2023, n = 83), with ceftazidime/avibactam accounting for the largest proportion throughout. Cefiderocol use increased from 2021 onwards, while ceftolozane/tazobactam and meropenem/vaborbactam showed variable trends; imipenem/relebactam was introduced only in 2024. Patterns were similar when stratified by fragility status, although formal trend analyses were limited by small subgroup sizes (Figure S1).

Infection types and microbiology

Overall, positive cultures were obtained in 232 of 308 prescriptions (75.3%), with comparable proportions of microbiological confirmation between immunologically vulnerable and non-vulnerable patients (78.9% versus 73.7%). Figure 2 and Table S1 shows the distribution of infection types by patient group. Pneumonia was more frequent among non-vulnerable patients (30.9% versus 18.3%, P = 0.0256), whereas abdominal infections were more common among immunologically vulnerable patients (12.5% versus 2.0%, P = 0.002); no other infection sites differed significantly between the two groups. Klebsiella pneumoniae was the most common pathogen (50.0%, 116/232), followed by P. aeruginosa (41.4%, 96/232) and A. baumannii (5.2%, 12/232); other pathogens (Citrobacter spp., Enterobacter spp., Escherichia coli, Klebsiella oxytoca, Klebsiella aerogenes, Proteus mirabilis and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia) were less frequent. Carbapenem resistance was observed in 85.0% of isolates (87.7% in immunologically vulnerable patients, 83.7% in non-vulnerable patients, P = 0.7146). For K. pneumoniae, resistance mechanisms were characterized in 108 of 116 isolates (93.1%): K. pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC) was predominant (56.5%, 61/108), followed by New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM, 13%, 14/108) and KPC + NDM co-production (25.9%, 28/108). Other mechanisms, including Verona integron-encoded metallo-β-lactamase (VIM) and oxacillinase-48 (OXA-48), were rare. Patterns were similar between groups, except for KPC + NDM co-production, which was more frequent in non-vulnerable patients (9.5% versus 36.4%, P = 0.0040; Table 2, Figure S3).

Rectal colonization was documented in 60.7% of prescriptions (187/308), most frequently with KPC-producing bacteria (58.8%, 110/308) and vancomycin-resistant enterococci (23%, 43/308), followed by DTR-P. aeruginosa (9.6%, 18/308), ESBL–producing Enterobacterales (7.5%, 14/308) and CRAB (1.6%, 3/308). Colonization by metallo-β-lactamase producers was more frequent in non-vulnerable patients (18.6% versus 5.2%, P = 0.0284; Table 2).

Susceptibility profiles to NBLs were analysed by patient group. For K. pneumoniae (Figure S2a), a higher proportion of isolates exceeded the EUCAST MIC breakpoint among vulnerable patients for ceftazidime/avibactam, with a similar trend for meropenem/vaborbactam; though not statistically significant. For P. aeruginosa (Figure S2b), MIC distribution for ceftazidime/avibactam was similarly shifted higher in vulnerable patients, although without statistical significance, while ceftolozane/tazobactam showed significantly more isolates above breakpoint among vulnerable patients (P = 0.0356), indicating a more favourable susceptibility profile in non-vulnerable patients. No cefiderocol-resistant isolates were identified in either species, though susceptibility data were unavailable for a substantial proportion; two A. baumannii–resistant isolates occurred, both in non-vulnerable patients, though small numbers preclude meaningful comparison.

Clinical course and outcomes

As the following comparisons were not adjusted for baseline differences in clinical severity, they are reported descriptively rather than as evidence of equivalent outcomes between groups.

At baseline (T0), fever and elevated CRP levels were similar between groups, but clinical severity differed significantly: non-vulnerable patients had higher rates of endotracheal intubation (ETI; 29.4% versus 13.0%) and shock (24.2% versus 11.0%), while vulnerable patients more often required no oxygen support (59.0% versus 36.9%). Clinical improvement metrics at T1 was similar except for fever resolution, more frequent in non-vulnerable patients (35.6% versus 20.0%). Non-vulnerable patients also had a higher rate of ICU admission after NBL prescription (50.5% versus 27.9%, P = 0.0002; Table 3).

In-hospital all-cause mortality was 25.0% in immunologically vulnerable patients (26/104) and 23% in non-vulnerable patients (47/204, P = 0.8095); median LOS was significantly shorter in vulnerable patients (33 days, IQR 20.8–61.21 versus 48.0 days, IQR 24.8–97.0, P = 0.0014). Kaplan–Meier survival curves were largely overlapping (Figure S4), with no significant difference by log-rank test (P = 0.7300); median survival was approximately 140 days in both groups. The analysis restricted to 30-day mortality showed a similar pattern (Figure S5).

A secondary analysis restricted to microbiologically confirmed infections (232/308, 75.0%, Table S2) showed similar baseline fever and CRP levels between groups. Vulnerable patients required less oxygen support (no oxygen need: 65.4% versus 33.80%, P = 0.0003), while invasive oxygen therapy was more frequent among non-vulnerable patients (ETI: 30.3% versus 14.1%); shock occurred in 14.1% versus 24.3% (P = 0.1058).

Clinical improvement at T1 was similar between groups; the fever resolution difference seen in the full cohort was not replicated in this subgroup. In-hospital mortality was 26.8% in vulnerable patients (22/82) and 21.3% in others (26/150, P = 0.4328); median LOS was shorter in vulnerable patients [35.0 days (IQR 21–61) versus 46 days (IQR 22–96), P = 0.0385].

Kaplan–Meier analysis showed median survival of 125 days in the vulnerable group, while in the non-vulnerable group the survival curve did not fall below 0.5, indicating median survival above 125 days (Figure S6).

Discussion

In this retrospective observational study conducted at two Italian university hospitals, we described the real-world use of NBL antibiotics over a 5-year period, with a particular focus on immunologically vulnerable patients, who accounted for around one-third of the cohort. Our findings provide a descriptive overview of prescribing patterns, microbiological characteristics and clinical course in this population, reflecting routine practice in a country endemic for MDR-GNB. Pneumonia was more frequent among non-vulnerable patients, whereas immunologically vulnerable patients exhibited a more heterogeneous distribution of infection sites, including a higher proportion of abdominal infections; no other infection sites differed significantly, underscoring the complexity of infection patterns in oncological and immunocompromised populations.26

From a microbiological perspective, K. pneumoniae (50.0%) and P. aeruginosa (41.4%) were predominant, with carbapenem resistance in 85% of isolates. The high prevalence of KPC-producing K. pneumoniae (56.5%) and KPC + NDM co-production (25.9%) reflect Italy's MDR-GNB epidemiology, where KPC-producing strains remain dominant despite a gradual shift towards metallo-β-lactamase–producing organisms27,28; this complexity underscores the need for careful clinical reasoning when selecting NBL therapy.

NBL prescriptions rose progressively over the study period, with ceftazidime/avibactam remaining the most frequently prescribed agent, consistent with its role as first-line choice. The gradual increase in meropenem/vaborbactam and imipenem/relebactam use between 2021 and 2024 reflects evolving resistance among Italian KPC-producing Enterobacterales, including increasing ceftazidime/avibactam resistance29; interpretation should also account for drug availability, as imipenem/relebactam was introduced in 2020, meropenem/vaborbactam and cefiderocol in 2021 and ceftolozane/tazobactam was temporarily unavailable due to a product recall.30 This evolving landscape makes NBL selection increasingly dependent on detailed local epidemiology and rapid phenotypic characterization to guide timely, optimal management.

The co-production of KPC and metallo-β-lactamases in Enterobacterales adds an additional layer of complexity: cefiderocol's limited activity against NDM-producing strains makes combination regimens, such as ceftazidime/avibactam plus aztreonam or aztreonam/avibactam, the more reliable choice, whereas VIM production (in Enterobacterales, A. baumannii or P. aeruginosa) still supports preferential cefiderocol use.31 Despite sharing similar bacterial targets, these agents’ clinical positioning differs, making early identification of resistance mechanisms and local epidemiological awareness essential to guide therapy and antibiotic stewardship.

Immunologically vulnerable patients presented at baseline with lower clinical severity than non-vulnerable patients, including lower rates of shock, ETI and ICU admission, whereas in-hospital mortality and clinical improvement at T1 were observed at similar rates between the two groups.

As these comparisons were not adjusted for the observed differences in baseline severity, they should be interpreted as descriptive observations rather than evidence that immunological vulnerability does not influence outcome; residual confounding by indication and unmeasured severity cannot be excluded.

This pattern was consistent when the analysis was restricted to microbiologically confirmed infections treated with NBLs as targeted therapy, further underscoring the importance of a comprehensive diagnostic work-up at the time of infection suspicion.

Taken together, these observations raise the hypothesis that timely access to targeted NBL therapy may attenuate expected outcome differences related to host immunological vulnerability—a hypothesis that our descriptive, unadjusted design cannot confirm, but which could be formally tested in future prospective studies with adjustment for baseline severity.

These findings should also be interpreted in the context of the limitations of pivotal trials for novel antibiotics. A recent review of Phase 2/3 trials supporting regulatory approval of WHO Reserve-category antibiotics (including the NBLs analysed in this work) found that the patient populations most affected by MDR-GNB infections, including elderly and severely immunocompromised patients, were largely excluded or underrepresented, and that only a minority of trials required a documented carbapenem-resistant infection as an inclusion criterion.11 In our own real-world cohort, however, such patients were far from marginal: over one-third (34.3%) of all patients receiving NBL therapy met at least one criterion of immunological vulnerability, underscoring how substantial this population is in routine clinical practice despite its limited representation in registrative trials. Real-world observational data, such as those presented here, therefore remain an important source of evidence on the use of these agents in the populations most likely to need them in routine clinical practice.

This study must be considered in light of its limitations. Its retrospective design introduces risks of selection bias and confounding; data came from only two centres in the same Italian region, without a specialized transplant ward, limiting generalizability. Immunologically vulnerable patients were identified pragmatically, combining conditions with substantially different degrees and mechanisms of immune impairment; this non-standardized definition may limit comparability with other studies, although it reflects patients generally considered vulnerable in routine practice. Similarly, as NBLs are active only against gram-negative organisms, data collection did not systematically capture co-existing gram-positive or fungal infections.

The absence of multivariable or propensity-score adjustment reflects this descriptive design and the small size of some subgroups (e.g. one patient with solid organ transplantation); larger prospective studies are needed to formally assess the independent association between immunological status and outcome. Analyses also included empiric prescriptions, consistent with real-world evidence supporting early NBL use in immunocompromised hosts; pathogen identification in 75% of episodes, with a high prevalence of carbapenem-resistant strains; and the subanalysis restricted to confirmed infections help strengthen the findings.32

Despite these limitations, the study has notable strengths. Systematic pharmacy-based data collection and a standardized REDCap database ensured precise tracking and reliability. Conducted in a real-world, MDR-GNB–endemic setting, the study spans a broad range of NBLs and prescribing patterns across diverse clinical settings (medical wards, ICUs, surgical units and emergency departments) over 5 years, capturing both empiric and targeted therapy and thus reflecting routine clinical decision-making. It also provides a practical overview of local pathogen epidemiology in Italian hospitals, including microbiological characterization and antibiogram data.

In conclusion, this descriptive study offers real-world insights into the use of NBLs in a country endemic for MDR-GNB, characterizing prescribing patterns, local resistance epidemiology and clinical course in immunologically vulnerable and non-vulnerable patients. Observed outcomes were similar between groups despite differing baseline severity, a hypothesis-generating finding warranting confirmation in adjusted, prospective studies. Rapid diagnostics and individualized therapy remain critical for optimal MDR-GNB management, and further research is needed to refine treatment strategies in these underrepresented populations.
