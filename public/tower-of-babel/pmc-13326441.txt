A multi-modal parcellation of human globus pallidus and its implications for symptom prediction and stratification in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Tianyi Yan, Kexin Wang, Ziteng Han, Yunxiao Ma, Xiu Wang, Kai Zhang, Guoyuan Yang, Tiantian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe globus pallidus (GP) is a critical basal ganglia nucleus for motor and cognitive control. However, traditional single-modality parcellations provide an incomplete understanding of its internal organization, thereby increasing the risk of imprecision and side effects during therapeutic targeting of its subregions.MethodsIn this study, we developed a multi-modal approach that combines structural and functional connectivity, termed hybrid pallidum parcellation (HPP), to parcellate the GP into anterior (aGP), middle (mGP), and posterior (pGP) subregions. This approach was rigorously evaluated for reproducibility and homogeneity in healthy individuals and behavioral prediction accuracy in healthy individuals and patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD). Connectivity and behavioral prediction analyses were further used to explore the structural and functional differences in the GP subregions.ResultsCompared with unimodal (structural or functional connectivity-based) and two public parcellations, HPP demonstrated superior reproducibility and homogeneity. In addition, the HPP-based subregions revealed a posterior-to-anterior structural and functional connectivity gradient in GP, reflecting a transition from primary to higher-order networks. Specifically, the pGP dominated in sensorimotor function prediction and the aGP in emotional processing, while the mGP exhibited a more flexible pattern in cognition and higher-order motor control. Furthermore, the HPP parcellation demonstrated generalizability and superiority in better predicting behavioral measures than other parcellations in both healthy individuals and patients with PD.ConclusionsOverall, HPP provides new insights into the GP’s hierarchical functional organization and its subregional roles in PD motor and non-motor symptoms, offering potential to improve patient stratification for PD and related disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04919-4.

Background

The globus pallidus (GP), a core structure within the basal ganglia, plays an important role in motor control [1]. It has long been recognized as a key node in motor circuitry, and its dysfunction is intrinsically linked to the pathophysiology of several movement disorders [2], including Parkinson’s disease (PD) [3]. While the subthalamic nucleus (STN) is often considered the primary target for deep brain stimulation (DBS) in PD, the GP is a crucial alternative [4, 5] with comparable motor benefits, and is particularly favored in cases with severe dyskinesia [6, 7]. Recent studies have revealed its critical involvement in non-motor domains, including emotion processing [8] and the promotion of reward consumption [9]. This functional heterogeneity in non-motor functions presents a fundamental challenge to developing more precise therapeutic strategies, as interventions on motor functions may affect unintended functional circuits. A detailed parcellation atlas of the GP, which delineates these distinct internal regions, is therefore of paramount importance for gaining a deeper understanding of its complex organization and for guiding more effective therapeutic strategies for PD and related diseases.

Traditional approaches to segmenting the GP have primarily relied on anatomical imaging, using techniques like manual labeling and the contrast provided by T1-weighted images to distinguish the nucleus from surrounding white matter [10, 11]. These approaches have divided the GP into its external (GPe) and internal (GPi) subregions, highlighting their distinct yet interconnected roles [1]. In detail, the GPe serves as a major hub for inhibitory projections within the basal ganglia circuitry, regulating motor output and refining motor commands [12]. Similarly, the GPi is viewed as the principal output nucleus, directly modulating thalamocortical activity and motor execution [13]. Nonetheless, this traditional anatomical model is challenged by evidence that both the GPe and GPi are critical not only in motor control but also in a range of non-motor processes, such as emotional regulation and decision-making [14, 15]. Furthermore, recent reviews suggest that these motor and non-motor functions may be organized along a functional gradient that follows an anteroposterior axis [16, 17], rather than adhering to the classical GPe-GPi anatomical boundary. This mismatch between traditional segmentation and non-motor functional distribution presents significant clinical challenges. For example, therapeutic interventions targeting motor pathways [18] may inadvertently modulate adjacent networks involved in non-motor processes, which can result in unintended cognitive or emotional side effects [19, 20]. Therefore, developing a fine-grained, functionally-defined GP parcellation that transcends traditional anatomical frameworks to separate overlapping motor and non-motor functions is essential. This, in turn, could enhance the precision and efficacy of therapeutic interventions.

To achieve more functionally relevant delineations of brain regions, researchers have increasingly adopted connectome-based parcellation, driven by a growing understanding of connectivity properties [21]. Specifically, to refine the parcellation of the GP, functional connectivity gradients derived from resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI) have been utilized to reveal organizational patterns that extend beyond its anatomical boundaries [22]. By providing detailed depictions of functional subdivisions and connectivity patterns, these parcellations serve as critical resources for refining our understanding of brain function and optimizing the selection of therapeutic targets, such as those for DBS [23, 24]. Complementing this functional perspective, structural connectivity derived from diffusion MRI-based tractography has also been extensively employed. Notably, recent studies dealing with high-resolution data have successfully subdivided both the GPi and GPe into distinct zones based on their cortical connectivity profiles. These studies demonstrated that connectivity within the GP is not uniform but follows a topographic organization along the anterior-posterior axis, corresponding to limbic, associative, and sensorimotor loops [25, 26]. These findings provide critical neuroimaging evidence supporting a longitudinal functional gradient within the GP, challenging the strict segregation of the traditional anatomical model.

Despite these significant advances, current unimodal parcellation approaches face specific challenges, particularly when applied to population-level analyses. First, the dynamic nature of resting-state signals introduces temporal variability, such as fluctuations in functional connectivity over time, which complicates the reproducibility and consistency of fMRI-based parcellation results across studies and conditions [27, 28]. Similarly, structural parcellation approaches are constrained by the inherent limitations of tractography algorithms, such as the potential for false-positive connections arising from image artifacts [29–31]. Besides, these structural methods predominantly rely on “winner-takes-all” algorithms that assign GP voxels to predefined cortical atlases [32]. This dependency makes the results sensitive to the choice of the cortical template and may overlook the intrinsic functional coherence within the GP itself. Furthermore, the small volume of the human GP imposes constraints on the granularity of stable parcellations. Even with high-resolution data (e.g., 7T), the GP contains a limited number of voxels (approximately < 600 voxels per hemisphere). Consequently, attempts to simultaneously subdivide the GP along both the medial–lateral (GPi/GPe) and anterior–posterior axes at the individual level—as seen in previous high-field studies—may result in over-fragmented regions that lack statistical stability and reproducibility across large cohorts [22, 33]. Considering the limitations of single modality parcellation using functional or structural connectivity, it is necessary to integrate structural and functional connectivity through multi-modal approaches, enhance the reproducibility of parcellations, and achieve a more comprehensive understanding of GP organization based on its connectivity patterns.

This study aims to refine and delineate the subregions of the GP via a multi-modal approach and to explore the distinct motor and non-motor functions of these subregions. By integrating structural and functional connectivity, we constructed a robust parcellation of the GP and compared it with unimodal approaches and two publicly available parcellations with respect to reproducibility and homogeneity. We subsequently examined the structural and functional connectivity of each subregion with the cortex to identify and characterize differences in their cortical connectivity patterns. Finally, the associations between GP subregions and behavioral measures were investigated in both healthy individuals and patients with PD, highlighting their roles in effectively dissecting the hierarchical heterogeneity of information processing. This study aims to enhance our understanding of the GP and improve therapeutic targeting and personalized treatment in PD and related movement disorders.

Methods

Data and preprocessing

HCP dataset

The Human Connectome Project (HCP) S1200 release data of 170 participants (age range, 22–36; 68 males) were used in this study [34]. The participants were scanned in a 7T scanner for rsfMRI data and a 3T scanner for diffusion MRI data. Fifty-eight behavioral measures were used to explore the brain–behavior relationships [35]. The participants were randomly divided into Dataset 1 (n = 80) for parcellation and Dataset 2 (n = 90) to validate the between-datasets reproducibility. Notably, the HCP 7T protocols demonstrate that this high spatial resolution yields increased functional contrast-to-noise ratios with significantly less partial volume effects compared to standard 3T protocols, thereby preserving spatial features in susceptibility-prone subcortical regions [36, 37]. Furthermore, 3T fMRI and 3T diffusion MRI data from the HCP’s 100 unrelated subjects were also included. After removing 8 participants who overlapped with the 170 participants mentioned above, this dataset comprised the remaining 92 individuals.

7T rsfMRI was performed using a gradient-echo echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence (repetition time [TR] = 1000 ms, echo time [TE] = 22.2 ms, flip angle [FA] = 45°, field of view [FOV] = 208 × 208 mm², and voxel dimension = 1.6 mm isotropic), including four runs of approximately 16 min each. In addition to standard surface-based fMRI preprocessing with HCP pipelines [38], which explicitly incorporate TOPUP-based susceptibility distortion correction using spin-echo field maps and FMRIB’s ICA-based X-noiseifier (ICA-FIX) denoising to handle geometric distortions and non-neuronal artifacts, the fMRI data were further processed with 4-mm full width at half maximum (FWHM) smoothing to improve the subcortical signal-to-noise ratio [22]. For each run, the first 15 frames were removed to avoid magnetic saturation effects, and bandpass filtering (0.01 ~ 0.08 Hz) was performed to increase neural signal sensitivity and reduce noise [39].

3T rsfMRI was performed using a multiband gradient-echo echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence (TR = 720 ms, TE = 33.1 ms, FA = 52°, FOV = 208 × 180 mm², and voxel dimension = 2.0 mm isotropic), including four runs. The fMRI data were preprocessed following a similar procedure as described above, with the only modification being the use of a 6-mm FWHM smoothing kernel.

Diffusion MRI was performed using a spin‒echo EPI sequence (TR = 5520 ms, TE = 89.5 ms, FA = 78°, FOV = 210 × 180 mm², and voxel dimension = 1.25 mm isotropic), including two runs with a reversed-phase encoding direction. Each gradient table consisted of approximately 90 diffusion-weighted directions and 6 interspersed b = 0 acquisitions per run. The diffusion weights consisted of 3 shells of b values of 1000, 2000, and 3000 s/mm2. In addition to standard diffusion preprocessing with HCP pipelines, we performed linear and nonlinear co-registration of diffusion MR images to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) structural template via T1-weighted images. First, skull-stripped T1-weighted images were linearly co-registered to the participant’s b0 images using FSL’s FLIRT algorithm. Then, linear and nonlinear transformations between the T1 image and the MNI template were derived. Based on these transformations, forward and inverse transformations between diffusion images and MNI space were generated [40].

PD dataset

This dataset included 49 patients with moderate PD (age: 61.8 ± 10.0 years, 23 males) who were recruited and provided informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional ethical guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Beijing, China. All participants were diagnosed with PD according to the Movement Disorder Society Clinical Diagnostic Criteria [41] and were in Hoehn–Yahr (H&Y) stage 3.0–3.5 and the medication-off state. Exclusion criteria included significant brain atrophy, dementia, and dyskinesia to avoid confounding effects on fMRI data [42]. Motor functions were evaluated by the third part of the UPDRS-III, emotional disorders were assessed by the Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD) and the Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), and cognitive status was assessed by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE).

The rsfMRI data from PD patients were acquired in the medication-OFF state using a 3T Prisma scanner (Siemens Medical System, South Iselin, NJ) at Beijing Tiantan Hospital. An EPI sequence was used with the following parameters: TR = 750 ms, TE = 30 ms, FA = 54°, FOV = 192 × 192 mm², matrix = 74 × 74. Forty-eight axial slices were acquired at 3.0 mm thickness with no gap, using a multiband acceleration factor of 4, resulting in a voxel resolution of 2.6 × 2.6 × 3.0 mm³. The total scan time was 5 min, yielding 400 volumes. Preprocessing of the rsfMRI data was performed using the Data Processing & Analysis for Brain Imaging (DPABI) toolbox (http://www.rfmri.org/dpabi). Specifically, the preprocessing steps included discarding the first 10 volumes, slice-timing correction, realignment to the first volume with a head motion threshold of ≤ 3.0 mm or ≤ 3.0°, removal of nuisance covariates (including head motion, cerebrospinal fluid, and white matter signals), bandpass filtering (0.01–0.1 Hz), and normalization to the standard MNI template with resampling to a resolution of 3 × 3 × 3 mm3.

Connectivity-based hybrid pallidum parcellation (HPP) of the GP

The globus pallidus (GP) was delineated in each individual’s T1-weighted images using FreeSurfer 5.1 for the automated segmentation of subcortical gray matter structures [34, 38]. Specifically, the set of voxels corresponding to the GP in each participant was mapped to a standard set of subcortical parcels based on the atlas containing probabilistic information on the location of structures [43]. This process ensured consistent spatial alignment of the GP between participants and the standard MNI space, thereby enabling reliable cross-participant comparison.

For diffusion images, we applied probabilistic tractography for each individual by sampling 5000 streamline fibers for each voxel in the seed region (GP) [44] to estimate the whole-brain connectivity profile. The metric for this connectivity profile was the number of streamlines connecting a given seed voxel to every other gray matter voxel in the brain, which was downsampled to 5 mm isotropic resolution [45]. On the basis of the connectivity profile, we calculated the correlation matrix between each pair of voxels in the seed region (GP) as the similarity matrix of diffusion images [45]. For the rsfMRI images, we calculated a connectional fingerprint for each voxel in the GP based on its relationship with all cortical vertices as well as subcortical and cerebellar gray-matter voxels (including accumbens, amygdala, brainstem, caudate, cerebellum, diencephalon, hippocampus, pallidum, putamen, and thalamus). First, for each individual, the fMRI signals from all cortical vertices and subcortical voxels were arranged in a time-by-voxels matrix. To reduce the high dimensionality of this gray matter signal for computational tractability [46], the Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to this matrix, retaining the top T-1 principal components, where T represents all points in time for each person. Next, the connectional fingerprint for each GP voxel was defined as its pattern of connectivity with these principal components. This was calculated as Fisher’s z-transformed Pearson correlation between the GP voxel’s time series and each of the T-1 component time series. Finally, the similarity between the connectional fingerprints of any two voxels within the GP was quantified using the η2 coefficient [22]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, to create a robust, group-level multi-modal representation for parcellation, we performed a two-step process using the data from the HCP dataset. For each participant, the structural and functional connectivity similarity matrices were concatenated to form multi-modal connectivity similarity matrices at the individual level, which were then averaged to form the similarity matrix at the group level. The city-block distance was chosen to measure the distance matrix of the multi-modal connectivity similarity [39].

The shared nearest neighbor-based density peak clustering (SNN-based DPC) algorithm was used to obtain both unimodal and multi-modal parcellations while effectively avoiding issues such as round parcels, spatially disconnected regions, and unstable one-step allocation [47]. Compared with the traditional DPC algorithm, SNN-DPC uses SNN similarity measures to define local density and interpoint distances. In addition, a two-step allocation strategy was employed: “inevitable subordinate” points were assigned on the basis of strong shared neighbor relationships, and “possible subordinate” points were allocated iteratively by evaluating neighborhood affiliations. The cluster centers were determined using a combination of the decision graph, silhouette coefficient, sum of squared errors (SSE), and split-half reproducibility. The decision graph was used to visualize each voxel’s local density (ρ) and its distance to the nearest higher-density point (δ), with points exhibiting high ρ and δ identified as cluster centers [48]. The silhouette coefficient and SSE were used to help determine and validate the number of cluster centers [49]. Additionally, to verify the stability of the parcellation, we performed a split-half reproducibility analysis by calculating the Dice coefficient between the parcellations independently derived from Dataset 1 and Dataset 2.

Quantitative evaluation of HPP and comparison with other parcellations

HPP was compared with unimodal parcellations—structural connectivity-based (SC-based) and functional connectivity-based (FC-based)—as well as with two publicly available parcellations, including the GP parcellation from the Melbourne subcortical parcellation [22] and the HCPex parcellation [50]. The Melbourne parcellation used functional connectivity gradients to obtain functional parcellations, whereas the HCPex parcellation used high-resolution T1- and T2-weighted structural images to perform anatomical segmentation of the subcortical regions. Besides, we further evaluated the robustness of our framework through ablation and sensitivity analyses. Specifically, we compared HPP with the single-modality and late fusion strategy (consensus clustering) and assessed the stability of the parcellation results across different numbers of clusters.

Given the absence of a definitive ground truth, the quality of the parcellations was assessed using two key criteria: reproducibility and homogeneity. Reproducibility was evaluated both within and between datasets. Within-dataset reproducibility was quantified using the Dice coefficient between parcellations from two independent datasets (Dataset 1 and Dataset 2), while between-dataset reproducibility (3T vs. 7T comparison) was assessed using normalized mutual information (NMI). Parcel homogeneity was quantified as the consistency of whole-brain connectivity profiles among the voxels within each parcel, following established methods [22, 51]. For each voxel within a given parcel, we first computed its structural and functional connectivity profile based on Schaefer400 parcellation. These profiles were concatenated to create a single feature vector for each voxel. For a given parcel, these voxel-wise feature vectors were compiled into a matrix, and homogeneity was defined as the variance explained by the first principal component (PC1). A higher variance explained by PC1 indicates greater similarity in connectivity profiles across voxels, and thus higher parcel homogeneity. Reproducibility and homogeneity were compared between HPP and the other parcellations.

Beyond these group-level comparisons, we further assessed the spatial stability of HPP at the individual level. To quantify inter-subject variability, we implemented a machine-learning-based personalized parcellation pipeline. Briefly, an “uncertainty zone” was defined around each group-consensus boundary by morphological dilation, and a Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier was trained to assign each voxel within this zone to its corresponding subregion based on its connectivity fingerprint with the rest of the brain [22]. Two methodological choices are worth highlighting. First, to avoid any form of circularity or data leakage, the group-averaged parcellation and the SVM classifier were generated and trained exclusively on Dataset 1, and the trained model was subsequently applied to the Dataset 2 to derive personalized parcellations. Second, unlike previous approaches that rely solely on functional connectivity, our SVM feature space integrated both functional and structural connectivity similarities between voxels, providing a more comprehensive anatomical–functional fingerprint for boundary delineation. Individual-level stability was then assessed by computing, for each subject and subregion, the Dice coefficient and the centroid displacement (along the anterior–posterior, left–right, and inferior–superior axes) between the personalized parcellation and the group-averaged parcellation.

Mapping structural connectivity patterns between the GP and cortex

Probabilistic fiber tracking was used to estimate the connectivity pattern of the GP subregions by sampling 5000 streamline fibers per voxel (starting from the seed voxels in the GP subregions) for each participant [52]. Fiber pathways were constrained to ipsilateral connectivity [53] using an exclusion mask at the midline to eliminate interhemispheric pathways. Two steps were implemented to minimize false positives in fiber tracking. First, raw tracts were thresholded at a connectivity probability of P ≥ 2.0, representing ≥ 0.04% of the 5000 samples generated per seed voxel [52]. Second, a group-representative connectome was derived by selecting the top 30% of edges with the lowest coefficients of variation across participants [54, 55]. For each subregion, binarized fiber tracts were averaged to create a probability fiber map, which was thresholded at 50% to derive the group-level whole-brain connectivity pattern [40]. The connectivity probabilities between the GP subregions and target regions of interest (ROIs) were normalized by adjusting for the seed and target sizes. Individual streamline counts were divided by the total number of samples (5000 × seed mask size), scaled by target size, and further rescaled to the average target size to account for size variability in both seeds and targets [56, 57]. To better delineate these target relationships, we normalized the connection strengths of all the subregions with one target summing to 1.

To quantify the associations between GP subregional connectivity patterns and hierarchical cortical organization, we calculated Pearson correlations between structural connectivity strength and S-A axis ranks. The S-A axis ranks were related to the cortical hierarchy, which was derived from ten different types of cortical maps [58]. The S-A axis ranks based on Schaefer400 parcellations were publicly available from https://github.com/PennLINC/S-A_ArchetypalAxis. To account for distance-dependent spatial autocorrelation, which can inflate the significance of correlations between cortical feature maps, we performed 1,000 spin test permutations [59] to generate a null distribution for each cortical feature. This null distribution was generated by randomly rotating the connectivity maps across the cortical surface, effectively preserving spatial contiguity while controlling for spatial autocorrelation. Permutation P values were computed by determining the proportion of null correlations that exceeded (for positive correlations) or fell below (for negative correlations) the observed correlation, divided by the total number of permutations. Spin tests were conducted using the neuromaps toolbox, which is accessible at https://github.com/netneurolab/neuromaps.

Given that the mGP occupies an intermediate position between the aGP and pGP in terms of structural hierarchy, we quantified its structural connectivity and hierarchical similarity with the aGP and pGP. The structural similarity was calculated as the cosine similarity of the structural connectivity strength between each GP subregion and the cortex in each participant [60, 61]. To assess hierarchical similarity, we applied Fisher’s z-transformation to the correlation coefficients between S-A axis ranks and structural connectivity strength for each participant. The absolute difference between Fisher-transformed correlations was calculated and subtracted from 1, yielding the final similarity measure.

Mapping functional connectivity patterns between the GP and cortex

The functional connectivity strength was determined by calculating the Pearson correlation between the extracted time series of the GP subregions and the time series for each cortical surface vertex and converting the correlation to z values using Fisher’s z transformation. To obtain the functional connectivity patterns of the three subregions, we performed a one-sample t test across all participants to generate vertex-wise statistical t-maps. Group-level connectivity statistics were corrected for multiple comparisons using FDR correction, with a statistical significance threshold of q < 0.01. To calculate network connectivity patterns, we thresholded the connectivity strength between subregions and cortical vertices at the top 10% to retain the most functionally relevant connections while minimizing noise from weaker signals [62]. The resulting connections were binarized to simplify network structure and increase comparability across participants by reducing variability in absolute connectivity strength [63]. The connectivity pattern of each GP subregion was computed by averaging the binarized connections across functional connectivity. As with structural connectivity, the total strength of functional connectivity across the three subregions was normalized to 1. The correlation between functional connectivity strength and cortical hierarchy rank was subsequently calculated to evaluate the hierarchical similarity.

To investigate the role of GP subregions in intercortical connectivity, we calculated the triple-wise correlation (twCC) between each GP subregion and all pairs of cortical regions using rsfMRI data [64]. The twCC is a linear index that quantifies the synchronization among three blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal time series—in this study, the time series were from two cortical regions and one GP subregion. The computation involves first constructing the covariance matrix for the triplet, followed by extracting the eigenvalues using principal component analysis. Finally, the twCC is calculated as the ratio of the difference between the two largest eigenvalues to the sum of all three eigenvalues. twCC values range from 0 to 1, with higher values reflecting stronger shared synchronization among the signals. In this study, the twCC quantified the statistical associations among two cortical regions and each GP subregion, forming a three-dimensional cortical–GP–cortical correlation matrix (400 × 6 × 400) defined by the Schaefer400 parcellation. This metric captures the interaction patterns defining the edges in a multigraph framework.

Given the intermediate anatomical position of the mGP and the connectivity gradient in the GP from lower-order to higher-order networks, we quantified the functional connectivity and twCC similarity between the mGP and both the aGP and pGP to assess the functional similarity of the subregions and explore the potential transitional role of the mGP within the pallidal circuitry. The similarity between twCCs was measured as the cosine similarity of the twCC profiles between the GP subregions and all the cortical region pairs in each participant.

Correlations between GP-cortical connectivity and behavior in healthy individuals

Kernel ridge regression (KRR) [35] was used to predict 57 behavioral measures, assessing the performance of the different parcellations (HPP, Melbourne, HCPex, and single-modality parcellations) and comparing the differences in the role of different GP subregion connectivity profiles in behavior prediction. The Penn Emotion Recognition Test measure for correct happy identifications (ER40HAP) was excluded from analysis due to insufficient variability across participants. We calculated functional and structural connectivity between GP subregions and the cortical areas of the Schaefer400 parcellation for participants selected from the HCP with available behavioral measures. To incorporate multi-modal brain features, kernel similarity matrices were constructed for both functional and structural connectivity, enabling the integration of multi-modal information into multi-KRR [35, 65]. All participants were grouped into 10 groups. A nested leave-three-out cross-validation was performed for each behavioral measure, with 120 replications to ensure robust validation. Behavioral measures were preprocessed by regressing out head motion (mean framewise displacement), age, and sex before cross-validation. Prediction performance was evaluated by computing the correlation between predicted and actual measures [65].

To complement the functional definitions of the GP subregions, a term-based meta-analysis using NeuroSynth was performed to generate probabilistic mappings between cognitive function and brain regions [66]. NeuroSynth is a platform for large-scale automated meta-analysis using the MKDA chi-square method for fMRI data (https://www.neurosynth.org). We excluded topics that were not behaviorally relevant, such as ‘cerebellar_cerebellum_basal’ and ’cortex_anterior_cingulate’, from the v7-topics-50 set. The reverse probability [67] was used to interpret the probability of the topic given the activation of the ROI defined by HPP.

To further refine the functional characterization of the GP subregions, we examined their structural and functional connectivity profiles with specific large-scale cortical networks [68, 69]. We selected three categories of reference networks: (1) the inter-effector network, which is implicated in integrative motor control; (2) effector-specific regions, representing the somatotopic areas for the hand, face, and foot; and (3) the action-mode network (AMN), which supports higher-order motor control functions, such as processing of instructional cues and action planning. For each GP subregion (aGP, mGP, and pGP), we calculated the average structural connectivity strength and functional connectivity strength with these networks.

Correlations between GP-cortical connectivity and behavior in PD patients

As in healthy individuals, we used KRR to predict behavioral measures in PD. This analysis served two purposes: to assess prediction differences across parcellations, and to compare the contributions of different functional domains within GP subregions. Behavioral measures were preprocessed by regressing out head motion (mean framewise displacement), age, and sex before cross-validation. Furthermore, considering the potential influence of disease progression, we additionally included disease duration (calculated as age minus age of onset) as a nuisance covariate to regress out its potential effects, supplementing the standard control for head motion, age, and sex.

We examined the differences in functional connectivity between GP subregions and cortical areas across various symptoms to investigate the roles of the GP subregions in PD and identify biomarkers associated with motor and nonmotor symptoms. Six behavioral measures were analyzed and categorized into three domains: emotional measures (HAMA and HAMD), cognitive measures (MMSE and MoCA), and motor measures (UPDRS-III on and UPDRS-III off). These measures were selected to comprehensively assess diverse aspects of behavioral functioning in the participant cohort. The HAMA and HAMD are widely used to evaluate anxiety and depression symptoms, the MMSE and MoCA are standard tools for assessing cognitive function, and the UPDRS-III on and off scores help evaluate the effectiveness of treatment and the progression of motor symptoms in PD patients. To minimize the confounding effects of clinical heterogeneity, PD patients were classified into three subgroups according to symptom severity: (1) mild and severe motor symptoms (mild: UPDRS-III on score < 33; severe: UPDRS-III on score ≥ 33) [70], (2) mild and severe emotional symptoms (severe: HAMA score ≥ 15 and HAMD score ≥ 17) [71, 72], and (3) intact and impaired cognitive status (impaired: MMSE score ≤ 26 and MoCA score ≤ 24) [73, 74]. For each subgroup comparison, we calculated the differences in functional connectivity between GP subregions and cortical subregions and quantified the number of significant connections differentiating each pair of subgroups, enabling the construction of connectivity-based biomarkers specific to emotional, motor, and cognitive impairments in PD patients.

Results

Hybrid Pallidum Parcellation (HPP) outperforms other unimodal and public parcellations

The shared nearest neighbor-based density peak clustering (SNN-based DPC) segmented the GP into three subregions using structural and functional connectivity data from Dataset 1 (Fig. 1). The optimal number of subregions was determined for both hemispheres based on the convergence of intrinsic topological features and quantitative validation metrics. Specifically, the decision graph consistently revealed three distinct density peaks, which was strictly corroborated by statistical measures—including the maximum silhouette coefficients, a clear ‘elbow’ in SSE at cluster number K = 3, and superior stability (high Dice coefficient) in the split-half reproducibility analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Additionally, the parcellation remained robust (Dice > 0.85) across a wide range of the nearest neighbor parameter k in the SNNDPC algorithm k = 20 ~ 50, validating the reliability of our selected parameter k = 30 (Additional file 1: Fig. S2).

HPP was compared with unimodal parcellations (SC-based and FC-based) and two public parcellations (Melbourne and HCPex), and all parcellations were visualized in a 3D model (Fig. 2A). Specifically, a within-dataset analysis across Dataset 1 and Dataset 2 revealed that HPP achieved a higher Dice coefficient (0.95) than both SC- and FC-based parcellations did, indicating enhanced reproducibility (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, a between-dataset reproducibility analysis was conducted to evaluate the generalizability of our parcellation. The group-level parcellation derived from the 3T data demonstrated significant correspondence with the original 7T-based parcellation, yielding a normalized mutual information (NMI) score of 0.73 and Dice coefficient of 0.75, a value significantly greater than chance (p < 0.01) (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). To evaluate external validity, we applied parcellations derived from Dataset 1 to independent data in Dataset 2, assessing homogeneity via structural and functional connectivity. As shown in Fig. 2C, the homogeneity of HPP (47.55% ± 3.79%) was significantly greater than that of SC-based parcellation (46.94% ± 3.90%, q = 0.0071) and FC-based parcellation (43.62% ± 4.15%, q < 0.001). HPP was subsequently compared with two publicly available parcellations, including the GP parcellations from the Melbourne and the HCPex parcellations (Fig. 2A). As shown in Fig. 2C, the homogeneity of HPP (47.55% ± 3.79%) was significantly greater than that of Melbourne (41.85% ± 4.73%, q < 0.001) and HCPex (38.03% ± 5.34%, q < 0.001). To further verify that this superior performance was robust to parameter variations and methodological choices, we conducted additional sensitivity and ablation analyses. These evaluations demonstrated that HPP consistently maintained higher parcellation homogeneity compared to the public atlases across a wide range of cluster numbers (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Moreover, HPP achieved significantly higher homogeneity than single-modality parcellations, confirming the critical benefit of multi-modal integration. Notably, HPP exhibited comparable homogeneity to the late fusion strategy (p = 0.03, uncorrected), suggesting that while integrating multi-modal information significantly enhances internal coherence, the specific stage of fusion (early vs. late) yields similar results for this metric (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). Finally, to assess the robustness of the parcellation at the individual level, we generated personalized parcellations in Dataset 2 using an SVM classifier trained on Dataset 1, and quantified their spatial correspondence with the group-averaged parcellation. All six subregions showed high spatial overlap across subjects (mean Dice > 0.87). Centroid displacement analyses along the anterior–posterior, left–right, and inferior–superior axes further revealed that systematic shifts were largely confined to the left aGP. However, the magnitude of these displacements remained within a sub-millimeter range, indicating no deviations of practical significance. Together, these results confirm that the aGP, mGP, and pGP are spatially stable across individuals (Additional file 1: Fig. S5).

Structural connectivity patterns of the human GP exhibit hierarchical organization

A primary goal of this section was to investigate the structural and functional patterns of the human GP based on the HPP. Connectivity profiles for each GP subregion were identified through diffusion probabilistic tractography on the HCP participants from Dataset 1 and Dataset 2. The structural connectivity patterns for each subregion in both hemispheres are shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S6. For each subregion, binarized fiber tracts were averaged to create a probability fiber map, which was thresholded at 50% to derive the group-level whole-brain connectivity pattern. Based on the structural connectivity patterns observed, the aGP appears to be predominantly associated with regions implicated in higher-order cognitive functions, such as the dorsolateral superior frontal gyrus, medial superior frontal gyrus, precuneus, supplementary motor area, and middle frontal gyrus. The mGP exhibits connections with areas involved in both cognitive and motor functions, including the dorsolateral superior frontal gyrus, precentral gyrus, middle frontal gyrus, medial superior frontal gyrus, precuneus, and supplementary motor area. Moreover, the pGP shows connectivity with regions that are critical for sensory processing and motor control, such as the postcentral gyrus, precentral gyrus, precuneus, superior parietal cortex, inferior temporal gyrus, and dorsolateral superior frontal gyrus. These distinct connectivity profiles suggest that each GP subregion may play a unique role in integrating cognitive, motor, and sensory information within the brain’s functional networks.

To highlight the difference between the GP subregions in connection mode, structural connectivity fingerprints for the three GP subregions were generated by mapping the connectivity profiles to the 7-network solution from Yeo et al. [75]. As shown in Fig. 3A, in both hemispheres, the aGP displayed greater connectivity with the LIM, whereas the mGP showed stronger connectivity with the frontoparietal network (FPN) and ventral attention network (VAN). The pGP demonstrated more prominent connections with the visual network (VIS), somatomotor network (SMN), and dorsal attention network (DAN). These differences were statistically significant after FDR correction with q < 0.05.

Next, we aimed to determine whether the spatial distribution of structural connectivity between these three GP subregions and the cortex aligns with the S–A axis. The S–A axis is a fundamental gradient of cortical organization, ranging from primary sensorimotor areas (lowest ranks) to multi-modal association cortices (middle ranks) and transmodal regions involved in high-order cognition (highest ranks) [76]. This axis reflects systematic variations in structural, molecular, and functional properties across the cortex and aligns with anatomical, functional, and evolutionary hierarchies [58]. To examine the different roles of GP subregions in cortical functional hierarchy organization, correlations between the average structural connectivity of GP subregions across participants and the sensorimotor-association (S-A) axis ranks were calculated. Significant spatial differences were found between the anterior, middle, and posterior regions of the GP and the cortical hierarchy (Fig. 3B). Specifically, the aGP exhibited a weak significant positive correlation with the S-A axis ranks (r = 0.27, Pspin = 0.07), whereas the pGP exhibited a significant negative correlation with the S-A axis ranks (r = -0.41, Pspin = 0.01). In contrast, the mGP was not significantly correlated with the S-A axis ranks (r = 0.14, Pspin = 0.56). Individual-level correlations of GP structural connectivity with S-A axis ranks were calculated, as shown in Fig. 3C. To facilitate the comparison of these correlations across different subregions, we applied Fisher’s z-transformation to convert the correlation coefficients into a normally distributed metric, known as the Fisher transformed correlation coefficient. The statistical analysis revealed that the correlation coefficients for aGP and mGP exhibited a significant positive association with the S-A axis ranks (q < 0.0001). Conversely, the pGP demonstrated a significant negative correlation with the S-A axis ranks (q < 0.0001). These findings indicated that the structural connectivity of the aGP with the cortex aligns with the S-A axis, predominantly engaging heteromodal and paralimbic transmodal association cortices, which correspond to high S-A ranks [77]. In contrast, the pGP is more involved with primary and unimodal sensorimotor cortices, which are reflective of low rank regions, whereas the mGP displays an intermediate connectivity profile. The above results further demonstrated a tendency in the structural connectivity pattern of the GP, showing a progression from the primary functional network to the higher-order functional network as it extends from the posterior to the anterior.

To further elucidate the positioning of the transitional mGP within the GP subregions under structural connectivity patterns, we investigated its similarity relative to the aGP and pGP. The cosine similarity of structural connectivity and the similarity of the correlations between structural connectivity and S-A axis ranks indicated that the mGP’s structural connectivity pattern was more similar to that of the aGP than to that of the pGP (q < 0.001).

Functional connectivity patterns of the human GP exhibit hierarchical organization

To investigate the functional connectivity patterns of different GP subregions, t values for each connectivity were calculated via one-sample t tests, which were corrected for multiple comparisons via FDR correction, with a statistical significance threshold of q < 0.01. As shown in the diagonal of Additional file 1: Fig. S7, GP subregions exhibit significant functional connectivity with a broad range of cortical areas. Paired t tests were used to reveal significant differences in functional connectivity between each pair of GP subregions in the upper and lower triangles in Additional file 1: Fig. S7. Notably, the mGP displayed the most extensive connectivity pattern among the three subregions, suggesting its potential role as a central hub within the GP. Furthermore, compared with the pGP, the aGP demonstrated stronger functional connectivity, primarily with the cingulate gyrus and frontal lobe, whereas the pGP exhibited significantly stronger connectivity than the aGP in the precentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus, and lateral occipital cortex. We then thresholded the functional connectivity matrix at the top 10% to binarize the connectivity strength. Connectivity patterns with Yeo’s seven intrinsic networks [75] revealed distinct profiles: the aGP displayed greater connectivity with the LIM, FPN, and DMN; the mGP with the VAN and DAN; and the pGP with the VIS and SMN (Fig. 4A). These differences were statistically significant after FDR correction with q < 0.05.

The average correlations between GP subregions with cortical area (Schaefer400) connectivity and S-A axis ranks across participants (Fig. 4B) showed a strong negative correlation for the pGP (r = -0.47, Pspin < 0.01) but no significant correlation for the aGP (r = 0.09, Pspin = 0.51) and no significant correlation for the mGP (r = -0.24, Pspin = 0.26). Additionally, individual-level Fisher-transformed correlation coefficients between GP functional connectivity and S-A axis ranks indicated that aGP was significantly positively correlated with hierarchical ranks (q = 0.01), suggesting a potential role in higher-order cognitive processes associated with higher S-A axis ranks. In contrast, the mGP and pGP correlations were significantly negative (q < 0.001; Fig. 4D), which may imply their involvement in more primary sensory and motor functions linked to lower S-A axis ranks.

The triple-wise correlation coefficient (twCC) between each GP subregion and every pair of cortical regions was calculated to examine the role of the GP in inter-cortical connectivity. As shown in Fig. 4C, the aGP is more prominently involved in mediating inter-network connectivity, particularly among networks such as the DMN and LIM. In contrast, the mGP appears to play a larger role in linking the VAN and DAN, whereas the pGP mainly contributes to connectivity within the VIS and SMN. The results for the right hemisphere were similar to those for the left hemisphere, as shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S8. Consistent with structural connectivity findings, GP functional connectivity follows a posterior-to-anterior gradient, reflecting a transition from primary to higher-order networks.

The cosine similarity of functional connectivity and twCC between GP subregions and cortical regions (Fig. 4E) indicated that the functional connectivity pattern of the mGP was more similar to that of the pGP than to that of the aGP (two-sided two-sample t test, P < 0.001). As a transition region in the GP subregions, the mGP highlights inconsistencies in structure and function.

Behavioral associations with GP in healthy individuals

Multi-kernel regression (multi-KRR) was utilized to predict each behavioral phenotype via both structural and functional connectivity for Dataset 1 and Dataset 2 on the basis of HPP, Melbourne, and HCPex parcellations. We found that the connectivity-based behavioral phenotype predictions based on HPP outperformed those based on the Melbourne and HCPex parcellations across most behavioral types (Additional file 1: Fig. S9A) and overall behaviors (Fig. 5A). These results demonstrated that our HPP was more suitable for the parcellation of the GP in healthy individuals with superior predictive power for behavior.

To further explore the specific behavioral roles of different GP subregions in healthy participants, we categorized the behavioral phenotypes into five distinct domains: Alertness, Emotion, Personality, Cognition, and Sensorimotor. We separately analyzed the difference in the prediction accuracy of the connectivity profiles in different GP subregions for different behavioral types (Fig. 5B). The connectivity profile of the aGP showed the highest prediction accuracy for behaviors related to alertness, emotion, and personality. In comparison, the mGP exhibited superior predictive performance, particularly for personality-related behaviors. However, pGP exhibited higher prediction accuracy for cognition and sensorimotor behaviors.

To further investigate functional distinctions among GP subregions, a meta-analysis was conducted via NeuroSynth, a platform for large-scale automated meta-analysis of fMRI data. As illustrated in Fig. 5C, we calculated the top associated terms for each subregion based on the activation, with the font size in the word clouds representing the reverse probability. The terms with significant associations (q < 0.05) are highlighted. The results indicated that activated aGP was more closely associated with reward feedback and pain sensory behaviors, whereas mGP and pGP were related to lower-level motor behaviors, such as hand movements.

To disentangle the specific motor roles of mGP and pGP—both of which exhibited broad motor-related associations in the meta-analysis—we further quantified their structural and functional connectivity with components of the Somato-Cognitive Action Network (SCAN), including inter-effector regions, effector-specific networks, and the action-mode network (AMN) (Fig. 5D). The analysis revealed a distinct dissociation in connectivity profiles. The pGP demonstrated the strongest structural and functional connectivity with the inter-effector regions and effector-specific networks among all subregions. In contrast, the mGP exhibited preferential structural and functional connectivity with the AMN. These results indicate that although both mGP and pGP are functionally related to the motor system, they engage with distinct processing levels: pGP is preferentially linked to the effector level, whereas mGP is associated with the action mode level.

Behavioral associations with GP in PD patients

To explore the utility of HPP in PD patients and investigate the behavioral associations of HPP subregions, kernel ridge regression (KRR) was repeated in PD patients using connectivity derived from HPP, Melbourne, and HCPex parcellations. The structural and functional connectivity-based behavioral phenotype predictions obtained from HPP outperformed those obtained from the Melbourne and HCPex parcellations across the UPDRS-III score in the med-off and med-on states and the HAMA (Additional file 1: Fig. S9B) and overall behaviors (Fig. 6A, q < 0.01). To further validate the contribution of our multi-modal fusion strategy to this predictive superiority, we compared HPP against single-modality and late fusion approaches. This ablation analysis revealed that HPP yielded significantly higher predictive accuracy for PD clinical scores compared to both single-modality (SC/FC-based) and late fusion strategy, confirming the distinct advantage of feature-level multi-modal integration in capturing clinically relevant patterns (Additional file 1: Fig. S10B, S11D).

To explore the impact of different subregions of the GP in PD patients, all PD patients were classified into subgroups across three separate dimensions: motor impairment (mild vs. severe), emotional impairment (mild vs. severe), and cognitive status (intact vs. impaired). Connectivity differences between GP subregions and cortical regions were analyzed across these dimensions based on HPP and Schaefer400 parcellations (Fig. 6B). Among PD patients with emotional impairment, the connections between the aGP and cortical regions accounted for the largest proportion of all GP-cortical connectivity differences. In individuals with cognitive impairment, connections between both aGP and mGP constituted a greater proportion. Notably, pGP-cortical connections accounted for the majority of significant associations with motor impairment. This pattern indicates that the functional connectivity within the GP subregions differs across the dimensions of PD impairment, highlighting the complex role of GP subregions in disease manifestation.

To validate the functional heterogeneity among GP subregions in PD patients across different types of impairments, we utilized the KRR method to predict various clinical indices by leveraging the functional connectivity between GP subregions and cortical areas. As illustrated in Fig. 6C, the connectivity profile of the aGP demonstrated higher prediction accuracy for emotion-related indices such as the HAMA. Moreover, the mGP showed greater accuracy for cognition-related assessments such as the MMSE. In contrast, the pGP exhibited superior predictive accuracy for motor evaluations, including the UPDRS-III scores, in both the med-off and med-on states. Importantly, these subregion-specific predictive patterns remained highly consistent when validated using an alternative cortical parcellation [44], confirming the robustness of our results independent of the chosen cortical atlas (Additional file 1: Fig. S12). Collectively, these findings underscore the distinct functional contributions of GP subregions to the clinical manifestations of PD and highlight the potential of HPP parcellation in advancing PD subtyping and guiding personalized therapeutic interventions.

Discussion

This study presents a novel multi-modal parcellation of the GP, termed the hybrid pallidum parcellation (HPP), dividing it into three subregions based on structural and functional connectivity and exploring the structural and functional patterns of each subregion separately. A posterior-to-anterior functional gradient was identified, with the mGP showing structural similarity to the aGP but functional similarity to the pGP. The associations between GP subregions and behavioral functions were investigated in both healthy individuals and PD patients, revealing associations between the pGP and sensorimotor functions and between the aGP and emotional processing, while the mGP exhibited a more flexible functional profile in cognition and higher-order motor control. Overall, this study provides a comprehensive characterization of the GP subregions that is crucial for understanding their role in motor and non-motor brain functions.

Using multi-modal connectivity, the current study divides the GP along the longitudinal axis into anterior (aGP), middle (mGP), and posterior (pGP) subregions. This approach diverges from the traditional parcellation along the medial-lateral axis, which is based on structural MRI data and is insufficient to capture functional refinement. By combining both structural and functional information, our parcellation may capture multi-modal differences across GP subregions. Moreover, the biological plausibility of our three-subregion parcellation is strongly supported by evolutionary neuroanatomy and functional circuit evidence. Specifically, histological and tract-tracing studies in non-human primates have consistently demonstrated that both GPi and GPe are organized into segregated territories distributed along a longitudinal axis, which is strictly associated with the limbic, associative, and sensorimotor functional loops [16, 78]. More specifically, the anteroventral GP is connected to the limbic cortex in cortico-basal ganglia circuits [79]. This organization mirrors the well-established topography of the striatum [80], suggesting that functional segregation is inherited downstream rather than reorganized. Consistent with the posterior-to-anterior structural and functional connectivity gradient revealed in our HPP-based subregions—reflecting a transition from primary motor to higher-order cognitive processing—previous human tractography studies [25, 26] have also identified a similar longitudinal connectivity gradient within the GP. In these studies, the core functional components correspond to limbic, associative, and sensorimotor circuits arranged along the longitudinal axis. Therefore, our identified subregions likely represent these parallel processing channels, providing a robust biological basis for the longitudinal axis division in this study.

On the other hand, our HPP method integrates structural and functional connectivity, ensuring a comprehensive representation of both spontaneous brain functional activity and white matter connections [28, 81]. This method is based on the data-driven SNN-DPC approach, which does not rely on brain priors, providing new insights into GP parcellation. In addition, our study compares the parcellation derived from the multi-modal approach with those based on unimodal methods and existing parcellations, demonstrating the superior performance of HPP in capturing complex connectivity patterns. The integration of structural and functional data allows HPP to outperform unimodal methods in terms of reproducibility and homogeneity. It is worth noting that the HPP parcellation spatially resembles the SC-based parcellation (Dice coefficient = 0.83) more closely than the FC-based parcellation (Dice coefficient = 0.72). Furthermore, analysis of the intrinsic feature space revealed that the HPP distance matrix was highly correlated with the SC-based matrix (r = 0.99) compared to the FC-based matrix (r = 0.88), reflecting the dominant influence of structural stability on the clustering geometry. However, this similarity does not imply functional or clinical equivalence. Our detailed analyses revealed that multi-modal fusion significantly alters the hierarchical organization of subregions along the sensorimotor-association axis compared to SC alone. Crucially, this distinction confers a specific advantage in pathological contexts: HPP demonstrated significantly superior predictive performance for clinical scores in PD compared to single-modality atlases (Additional file 1: Fig. S11D). This suggests that by integrating functional insights with structural constraints, HPP captures complementary biological information essential for characterizing disease-specific alterations that structural data alone may miss. In addition, HPP achieved significantly greater homogeneity and behavioral prediction accuracy in healthy individuals and PD patients compared with two publicly established parcellations, such as the Melbourne subcortical parcellation along the longitudinal axis, which is based on functional gradients [22] and the HCPex parcellation along the medial‒lateral axis, which is based on structural MRI data [10, 50]. These findings underscore the utility of multi-modal approaches in capturing the complex connectivity patterns inherent to subcortical structures such as the GP. Beyond these group-level advantages, HPP also demonstrated strong stability at the individual level, with personalized parcellations derived from an independent dataset showing high spatial overlap with the group-averaged parcellation and only sub-millimeter centroid displacements. This individual-level robustness suggests that HPP can serve as a reliable parcellation for subject-specific analyses, an essential property for translating parcellation-based findings into precision-medicine applications.

The primary objective of this study was to systematically assess whether the functional organization of GP subregions with the cortex follows a hierarchical pattern. Our findings demonstrate a posterior-to-anterior gradient within the GP, transitioning from primary motor functions to higher-order cognitive and emotional functions. These findings can be supported by previous GP studies. In detail, the aGP’s structural connectivity to regions such as the dorsolateral superior frontal gyrus, medial superior frontal gyrus, and middle frontal gyrus links it to higher-order cognitive functions in decision-making, executive control, and working memory [82, 83], which is consistent with its involvement in the limbic circuit of the basal ganglia [84]. The mGP structural connections with both motor-related areas (e.g., precentral gyrus) and cognitive regions (e.g., medial superior frontal gyrus, precuneus) reflect its integrative role [85]. Stronger functional cortical connectivity supports the mGP as a transitional subregion, bridging the cognitive and motor domains. The pGP’s prominent structural connections to sensorimotor areas (postcentral gyrus, precentral gyrus) underline its role in motor control and sensorimotor integration [86]. Additional links to parietal and temporal regions suggest broader involvement in visual-motor integration and spatial coordination. In addition, by investigating structural connectivity fingerprints and functional connectivity fingerprints, we find that this functional axis, progressing from primary sensorimotor processing in the posterior GP to higher-order cognitive functions in the anterior GP, suggests that the internal structural and functional organization of the GP corresponds to the well-established hierarchical structure of the cerebral cortex. To quantitatively test this correspondence, we correlated the two connectivity patterns of GP subregions with the sensorimotor-association (S-A) axis, a principal gradient of cortical organization. Our analysis confirmed this gradient: the aGP’s connectivity pattern exhibited a positive correlation with the S-A axis rank, indicating greater connectivity strength with higher-order association cortices. Conversely, the pGP’s pattern showed a significant negative correlation, reflecting its stronger structural and functional connectivity with primary sensorimotor regions. This quantitative result demonstrates that the GP’s internal organization corresponds to the fundamental hierarchy of the cerebral cortex.

In this study, the GP subregions exhibited a clear gradient not only in their direct structural and functional connectivity but also in their capacity to mediate communication between cortical regions. To further explore their influence on the cortex, we investigated their capacity to mediate inter-cortical communication. This mediating role was quantified using the twCC, a metric that specifically measures the synchronized influence of a third region (here, a GP subregion) on the communication between any two cortical areas. The twCC analysis revealed that the aGP integrates widespread higher-order networks, whereas the pGP focuses on integrating connections within primary sensorimotor networks. This functional specialization suggests that the aGP is involved in integrating sensory-motor functions with advanced cognitive processes, whereas the pGP is involved in integrating visual and motor functions associated with primary sensorimotor networks. This supports the hypothesis that the GP acts as a mediator between brain networks [26]. Aligning with previous findings on large-scale brain organization [87], this evidence confirms the GP’s dual role in mediating not only cortico-subcortical pathways but also information transfer between cortical networks, highlighting the contribution of its subregions to the brain’s hierarchical information processing [88].

By comparing the structural and functional connectivity profiles of the mGP with those of the aGP and pGP, this study reveals a significant structural-functional decoupling in the GP. Structurally, the mGP resembles the aGP, with connections to high-order cognitive and limbic networks (e.g., medial superior frontal gyrus) that suggest a role in bridging executive and emotional processes. Functionally, however, its connectivity aligns with the pGP, indicating involvement in sensorimotor integration. This structural-functional divergence is rooted in the mGP’s multifaceted role, which is consistent with previous research identifying the mGP as a critical relay node within the associative circuit of the basal ganglia [26, 89]. This circuit plays an essential role in motor function [90] as well as emotion-specific and other higher cognitive functions [91]. This phenomenon reveals its flexibility in cognitive and motor tasks, further emphasizing the integration and overlap of functions as well as the separation of structure and function in GP subregions.

As a transitional hub in the GP, the mGP receives cognitive information from the aGP and coordinates motor information with the pGP, functioning like the brain’s association cortex to integrate specialized networks for complex tasks [92]. In the basal ganglia, the functional and structural overlap between the GP and striatum underlies complex circuits in neurodegenerative movement disorders [93], and partially overlapping striatal subregions may work synergistically to mediate complex behaviors [80]. Various frontal inputs from the striatum are combined and integrated to greatly affect the GP [94]. Therefore, we propose that mGP plays a similar role in overlapping and integrating regions within the basal ganglia. This functional integration is also supported by the fact that the mGP has the strongest functional connectivity with whole-brain regions. In addition, the mGP’s structural and functional role fluctuates between higher-order cognitive and lower-order motor tasks. This fluctuation is also attributed to the different timescales of connectivity: stable, long-term structural connections provide a foundation, while functional connectivity allows for flexible, task-dependent reconfiguration [95, 96]. This allows the mGP to act as a dynamic hub, selectively integrating information from diverse cortical inputs. The structural and functional ambiguity of the mGP arises from an interplay between two properties: its functional overlap with adjacent regions, which drives integration, and its dynamic functional flexibility, which allows its network allegiance to shift according to task demands [97, 98]. This capacity to balance dynamic and integrated network states is essential for normal function and for compensatory mechanisms in diseases [99], like PD, suggesting new therapeutic avenues.

Our analysis revealed distinct behavioral correlates for each GP subregion: the pGP was predominantly associated with primary sensorimotor execution, the mGP with cognitive control and higher-order motor control, such as action planning, and the aGP with higher-order emotional behaviors, highlighting a clear pattern of functional specialization in healthy individuals and PD patients. In healthy individuals, the aGP was linked to higher-order behaviors like reward processing and emotional regulation, supported by its connectivity to the LIM and DMN networks, aligning with prior studies [86, 100, 101]. Conversely, the pGP, with its strong connectivity to the SMN and VIS, was predominantly associated with cognition and sensorimotor behaviors, which is consistent with its role in motor execution and sensory integration [102, 103]. Here, the incorporation of the Somato-Cognitive Action Network (SCAN) [68] and action-mode network (AMN) [69] framework provides a critical perspective for refining this functional characterization, particularly for the mGP. While our meta-analysis indicated broad motor associations for both pGP and mGP, our specific connectivity analysis with SCAN networks revealed a hierarchical dissociation: the pGP connects preferentially to inter-effector and effector-specific regions (supporting direct execution), whereas the mGP exhibits the strongest connectivity with the Action Mode Network (AMN). This implies that the mGP is involved in the higher-order motor control—such as action planning, task initiation, and goal maintenance—rather than simple execution [69]. In healthy individuals, the mGP’s predictive performance for personality and cognitive traits was not the highest. This may be partially attributed to this structural and functional ambiguity and the dual role in the AMN, which undermines the predictive specificity of the joint structure-function model used in mGP. Nonetheless, this work highlights its contributory role within the cortico-subcortical networks underpinning these behaviors.

In this study, PD patients in a critical transitional phase [104, 105] (Hoehn and Yahr stages 3–3.5) were stratified based on motor [70], emotional [71, 72], and cognitive [73, 74] symptom severity to minimize confounds. Patient stratification revealed that during this transitional period, GP subregions act as potential biomarkers for specific symptoms: the aGP for emotional disturbances, the mGP for cognitive impairments, and the pGP for motor deficits. These findings were confirmed using six behavioral scales (covering motor, emotional, and cognitive functions), which reaffirmed the distinct roles of each GP subregion—a pattern of functional specialization similar to that found in healthy participants. Our findings offer significant potential for future translational applications, particularly in two key areas. First, the distinct connectivity profiles of the HPP-derived subregions could serve as robust biomarkers, enabling more precise patient stratification and objective monitoring of disease progression or treatment response [106]. Second, HPP offers a promising avenue for optimizing therapeutic interventions like DBS. For example, DBS may help mitigate risks such as exacerbating cognitive decline in patients with severe motor symptoms undergoing DBS by targeting the motor-related posterior GP [20]; this underscores the value of individualized treatment strategies, where HPP can guide the personalization of interventions, balancing the management of motor dysfunctions with the preservation of cognitive and emotional functions and ultimately advancing precision medicine in PD care.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged to provide context for the interpretation of the findings. First, given that the GP is a small and intricate structure, the spatial resolution of conventional MRI may be insufficient to fully capture its fine-grained anatomical and functional topography [11]. Similarly, fMRI’s low temporal resolution limits capturing rapid neural dynamics [107]. Integrating electroencephalography (EEG) [108] and magnetoencephalography (MEG) with fMRI improves understanding of the GP’s dynamic functions [109] but poses technical challenges due to modality differences and signal alignment across timescales [110]. Second, the mGP exhibits a degree of ambiguity in both the structural and functional domains, highlighting the potential for incorporating advanced analytic frameworks, such as deep learning, to better differentiate the contributions of the structural and functional dynamics of the mGP [29, 111]. Besides, while this study emphasizes the potential of HPP in guiding interventions such as DBS, the findings are purely correlational. Future research should incorporate longitudinal data and intervention trials to establish causal links across GP subregions, their connectivity patterns, and clinical outcomes in PD patients. A key limitation is our modest PD sample size, which impacts the statistical power of our brain-behavior analyses and the generalizability of our results to the wider, clinically diverse patient population. Consequently, our findings should be interpreted as preliminary and require validation in larger, multi-site cohorts to confirm their robustness and clinical relevance [112]. Finally, while we controlled for medication status by scanning all PD patients in a standardized ‘medication-off’ state, we acknowledge that our findings could still be influenced by clinical heterogeneity, such as variations in disease duration and symptom severity. Future studies with larger samples are necessary to systematically disentangle these potential confounds and refine the clinical applicability of our results. Addressing these limitations will enhance the clinical applicability of HPP and strengthen its role in personalized medicine.

Conclusions

This study established a multi-modal parcellation of the GP into anterior, middle, and posterior subregions, revealing a posterior-to-anterior gradient from motor to cognitive-affective functions. Beyond the classical motor-centric view, we identified specialized roles of the aGP in emotion, mGP in cognitive integration and higher-order motor control, and pGP in motor control. Applying this stratification to Parkinson’s disease cohorts, we further demonstrated its capacity to disentangle symptom heterogeneity, linking each subregion to specific behavioral and clinical profiles. These findings refine the understanding of basal ganglia organization and provide a framework for more precise, personalized interventions in neurodegenerative diseases.
