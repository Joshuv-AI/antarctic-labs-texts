Comparative evaluation of long-pulsed Nd: YAG and pulsed dye lasers in the treatment of venous lake lesions: a prospective clinical study
Authors: Amirhossein Behrouzeh, Nazila Heidari, Amirhossein Heidari, Maryam Daneshpazhooh, Maryam Nasimi, Hamidreza Mahmoudi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Venous lakes (VL) are benign vascular malformations found mainly on the lips and face. Various treatment modalities have been proposed, with laser therapy emerging as an effective and minimally invasive option. This study aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of a long-pulsed neodymium-doped yttrium aluminium garnet (Nd: YAG) laser and a pulsed dye laser (PDL) in the treatment of VLs. A total of 23 patients with VL lesions were treated using pulsed dye laser (PDL; n = 8) or Nd: YAG laser (n = 15), with sessions at 6-week intervals. Data on patient demographics, lesion characteristics, treatment parameters, and outcomes were collected. Two experienced dermatologists evaluated treatment effectiveness by comparing pre- and post-treatment images, focusing on reductions in lesion size and improvements in color. The mean age of the participants was 48.8 ± 12.3 years, and the mean number of treatment sessions was 3.17 ± 1.02. Overall, lesion size decreased significantly from 1.14 ± 0.71 to 0.53 ± 0.72 cm (P < 0.001), corresponding to a mean relative reduction of 58.3%, while mean color improvement was 65.2%. A ≥ 75% reduction in lesion size was achieved in 39.1% of patients and was more frequent with Nd: YAG than with PDL (53.3% vs. 12.5% ). A ≥ 75% improvement in lesion color occurred significantly more often with Nd: YAG (60.0% vs. 0%, P = 0.007). Complete clearance was observed in 17.4% of patients, all of whom were treated with Nd: YAG. Mean absolute lesion size reduction was greater with Nd: YAG than with PDL (0.68 ± 0.34 vs. 0.40 ± 0.30 cm), although the difference was not statistically significant, whereas mean color improvement was significantly greater with Nd: YAG (0.75 ± 0.27 vs. 0.46 ± 0.19; P = 0.007). Treatment was generally well tolerated, with one case of post-treatment scarring. Nd: YAG was associated with greater improvement than PDL, particularly in lesion color, although larger randomized studies are needed to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Venous lakes (VL) represent frequent benign vascular lesions that arise from dilatation of superficial venules within the upper dermis. They typically appear as soft, dome-shaped macules or papules that are mildly elevated and display a dark blue to violaceous coloration [1]. These lesions are most frequently observed in middle-aged and elderly individuals, typically in sun-exposed areas, with the lips being the most common site of occurrence [2]. Their diameter usually ranges from 2 to 10 mm, and once developed, they tend to persist throughout life [3]. Based on histopathological examinations, VLs are lined by a flat layer of endothelial cells and enclosed by a thick fibrous wall. These lesions are generally asymptomatic except in cases of trauma-induced bleeding, and they are most often treated for cosmetic reasons. Nevertheless, due to their cosmetic implications, VLs may negatively affect patients’ quality of life by causing psychological distress. Moreover, their dark nodular appearance may mimic melanoma, raising concern for malignancy among patients [4].

Various therapeutic modalities have been proposed for VLs, including cryosurgery with liquid nitrogen, laser photocoagulation, surgical excision, laser vaporization, infrared coagulation, and sclerosing agents [5]. Conventional methods such as surgery may be associated with complications and discomfort. For instance, management of oral or labial lesions with surgical excision or sclerosing agents may pose cosmetic challenges, especially for large (> 10 mm) or hemorrhagic lesions. Moreover, surgical intervention is frequently associated with pain, higher costs, and prolonged recovery [6]. Under such circumstances, light- and laser-based modalities represent safe and effective alternatives, with several studies confirming their efficacy, especially long-pulsed neodymium-doped yttrium aluminium garnet (Nd: YAG) lasers (1064 nm) [7].

Several vascular lasers have been employed in the management of VLs, including argon, diode, CO₂, Nd: YAG, and pulsed dye lasers (PDLs) [8]. Among these, Nd: YAG has demonstrated superior outcomes compared with other systems. Long-wavelength lasers, such as Nd: YAG, are essential for effectively clearing deeper, thicker, nodular lesions [6, 7]. Although argon and diode lasers produce more intense, longer-lasting pulses, they dissipate heat less effectively, increasing the risk of collateral tissue injury [9]. Moreover, Nd: YAG treatment has generally been reported to be better tolerated than PDL [10]. PDLs, typically operating at wavelengths of 585–595 nm, are particularly suitable for superficial, hemoglobin-rich vascular lesions and have demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety across a range of vascular and erythematous conditions [11]. Notably, in combined wavelength therapy, the sequential application of PDL (595 nm) and Nd: YAG (1064 nm) reduces the exposure time required for effective treatment, enhances dermal penetration, and lowers the risk of post-treatment purpura [12]. Multiple studies have consistently highlighted additional advantages of Nd: YAG lasers, including their noninvasive nature, rapid procedure time, high efficacy often achieved in a single session, minimal need for retreatment, and low risk of scarring [13–15]. Overall, lasers with deeper dermal penetration, such as Nd: YAG and diode systems, have demonstrated superior efficacy compared with other modalities in treating VLs [16]. The aim of our study was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of Nd: YAG and PDL in reducing or clearing VL lesions.

Methods

Study design

In a prospective observational study, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of Nd: YAG and PDL lasers for the treatment of VLs in subjects aged 18 or older with a history of VLs referred to the dermatology clinic at Razi Hospital, Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran.

Eligibility criteria and case selection

The study enrolled patients who presented with lip-localized VL lesions to the Dermatology Clinic of Razi Hospital, Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran, between January 2022 and March 2023. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) a diagnosis of VL confirmed by the attending dermatologist at presentation; and (3) provision of written informed consent. Furthermore, exclusion criteria were applied based on the following conditions: (1) age < 18 years (2), refusal to provide informed consent (3), incomplete clinical or follow-up data, and (4) treatment with surgical modalities. Written informed consent was obtained from all eligible patients following a thorough explanation of the study procedures.

Study procedure and treatment intervals

Treatment was administered using two laser platforms; 15 patients were treated with a long-pulsed Nd: YAG laser: SP Dynamis system (Fotona d.o.o., Ljubljana, Slovenia) equipped with the R33-T handpiece (MatrixView™, variable spot size: 2–10 mm, wavelength: 1064 nm) using a 7 mm spot size, 70–90 J/cm² fluence, and 25 ms pulse duration. The remaining eight patients were treated with a PDL: Vbeam® system (Candela Medical, Wayland, MA, USA; wavelength: 595 nm) equipped with an integrated Dynamic Cooling Device™ (DCD™), using a 7 mm spot size, a 10–12 J/cm² fluence, and a 1.5 ms pulse duration. Epidermal cooling was provided during treatment using the device’s integrated DCD. Allocation to the treatment groups was non-randomized and determined by the treating physician’s clinical judgment. No predefined allocation algorithm was applied, resulting in unequal group sizes, with 15 patients receiving Nd laser treatment and eight receiving PDL.

Data collection and outcome assessment

Clinical and demographic variables, including age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), lesion duration, number of treatment sessions, laser wavelength, lesion size, and pulse duration, were extracted from patients’ medical records. Treatment outcomes were assessed by comparing standardized photographs obtained before and after intervention. Standardized photographs of the lesions were taken at baseline, immediately post-procedure, and at follow-up visits. Two independent dermatologists with relevant clinical experience evaluated the clinical images and assessed outcomes based on 1) reduction in lesion size and 2) changes in lesion color. Treatment response was categorized as < 75% or ≥ 75% improvement, with complete clearance defined as full resolution of both lesion size and color. All collected data were recorded in a structured Excel database and prepared for subsequent statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Pre- and post-treatment continuous outcomes were compared using the paired t-test when normally distributed. Between-group continuous variables were compared using the independent-samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the efficacy of two vascular lasers, Nd: YAG and PDL, in treating VL lesions, based on clinical assessment of pre- and post-treatment photographs. Our findings demonstrated that all patients, regardless of laser type, experienced some improvement in lesion size and color. However, a higher proportion of patients treated with Nd: YAG achieved marked responses: 53.3% showed a ≥ 75% reduction in lesion size, and 60.0% showed a ≥ 75% improvement in lesion color. By contrast, only 12.5% of patients in the PDL group achieved a ≥ 75% reduction in lesion size, and none demonstrated a ≥ 75% improvement in color. Although the difference in lesion size reduction between groups did not reach statistical significance, the difference in color improvement was statistically significant in favor of Nd: YAG. These results suggest that both laser modalities are effective in managing VL lesions, yet Nd: YAG may provide superior cosmetic outcomes, particularly with respect to color resolution.

In our study, although the proportion of female participants was considerably higher than that of males (83%), no significant differences in laser treatment efficacy were observed between sexes with respect to lesion size reduction or color improvement. Treatment responses varied among both men and women, and neither group demonstrated a clear advantage in therapeutic outcomes. The predominance of female participants may reflect greater concern regarding cosmetic issues [17], particularly when VL lesions occur in visible areas such as the face or lips. Women are generally more likely to pursue cosmetic treatments and medical interventions for benign yet aesthetically concerning lesions [18], a pattern that may be influenced by socio-cultural expectations or individual perceptions of appearance [19]. This gender-related difference in treatment-seeking behavior, particularly in dermatology and cosmetic contexts, is consistent with findings from previous studies [20]. However, analysis of the association between patient age and treatment efficacy revealed no statistically significant relationship with either lesion size reduction or color improvement. Although patients with ≥ 75% improvement were numerically older than those with smaller improvements, these differences were not statistically remarkable and should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size. Therefore, our findings do not support age as a determinant of treatment response in this cohort.

In addition, our findings revealed notable differences between the two vascular lasers under investigation, PDL and Nd: YAG, in the treatment of VL lesions. Although both modalities reduced lesion size and improved color, Nd: YAG was associated with significantly greater color improvement and a numerically greater reduction in lesion size. Among patients treated with Nd: YAG, 53.3% achieved a ≥ 75% reduction in lesion size, while 60.0% achieved a ≥ 75% improvement in lesion color; in the PDL group, only one patient reached this threshold for size reduction, and none demonstrated comparable improvement in color. The significant difference in color improvement (P = 0.007) supports a potential advantage of Nd: YAG for color resolution in this cohort. This difference may partly reflect its longer wavelength and greater tissue penetration (1064 nm) compared with PDL (595 nm), allowing Nd: YAG to more effectively target deeper vascular structures (16, 21). Nevertheless, because treatment allocation was nonrandomized and the two groups differed in laser parameters, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of the superiority of Nd: YAG over PDL.

The difference in response between the two lasers may partly reflect their distinct optical and thermal properties. At 595 nm, PDL is strongly absorbed by oxyhemoglobin and is generally most effective in small, superficial vessels [21]. Its penetration, however, may be insufficient for thicker venous structures. The 1064-nm wavelength of Nd: YAG is absorbed less strongly by hemoglobin but penetrates deeper into the dermis, allowing thermal injury to occur within larger-caliber vessels [7, 16]. This feature is particularly relevant to VLs, which are composed of dilated venules and may extend beyond the depth effectively reached by PDL. Bekhor et al. [8] reported that this similarly attributed the high clearance rate achieved with long-pulsed Nd to the combination of deeper penetration and longer pulse widths.

Pulse duration may also have contributed to the difference observed in our study. The Nd: YAG treatments were delivered with a pulse duration of 25 ms, whereas the PDL treatments used a pulse duration of 1.5 ms. Because larger vessels have longer thermal relaxation times [22], a longer pulse can maintain heating across the vessel wall and promote coagulation without relying on rapid, highly localized energy deposition [16, 23]. Previous reports of Nd: YAG treatment for VLs have likewise used millisecond pulse durations and achieved substantial clearance [8, 24]. These properties provide a plausible explanation for the greater color improvement observed with Nd: YAG in our cohort, although the present study was not designed to isolate the effects of wavelength, fluence, and pulse duration. The non-randomized allocation and small, unequal treatment groups also preclude a definitive mechanistic or comparative conclusion.

Furthermore, our results are generally consistent with previous reports describing favorable outcomes with Nd: YAG for VL lesions [16]. For instance, a 2006 study demonstrated that long-pulsed Nd: YAG laser treatment for VL lesions of the lips and cheeks was highly effective, achieving complete clearance in 94% of patients after a single session, with no significant adverse effects [8]. Additionally, another study reported two cases of labial VL that showed over 90% response after a single Nd: YAG laser treatment session [24]. These results align with our observations, highlighting the strong efficacy of Nd: YAG in reducing lesion size and improving discoloration. Furthermore, John et al. [4] employed an Nd: YAG laser with a 20 ms pulse duration to treat 31 patients with VL lesions of the lips and perioral area; 87% achieved complete healing without recurrence. Conversely, a 2007 study reported that treatment with 595-nm PDL achieved satisfactory improvement in only 3 of 8 patients [25], which may be attributed to insufficient thermal energy for complete vascular closure. This finding is consistent with the lower response observed with PDL in our cohort. Taken together, our findings add to previous evidence suggesting a potential advantage of Nd: YAG for VL treatment, particularly with respect to color improvement.

Study limitations

The current study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which limits the generalizability of the findings and the power of subgroup analyses across laser modalities. Second, treatment allocation was non-randomized and based on the treating physician’s clinical judgment, without a predefined allocation algorithm. The resulting unequal group sizes and potential differences in baseline or unmeasured clinical characteristics may have introduced selection bias and residual confounding. Third, the follow-up period was limited, preventing evaluation of long-term recurrence rates and durability of treatment outcomes. Fourth, standardized outcome assessment relied primarily on photographic comparison, which, while practical, may be subject to inter-observer variability despite dual dermatologic review. Additionally, variations in treatment parameters, such as fluence and number of sessions, may have influenced results and limited direct comparability between patients. Future studies with larger, randomized cohorts, longer follow-up, and standardized treatment protocols are warranted to confirm these findings and refine optimal therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that vascular laser therapy is an effective and safe approach for treating VL lesions. Both PDL and Nd: YAG systems provided measurable improvements in lesion size and color, with Nd: YAG associated with significantly greater color improvement and numerically greater reductions in lesion size and in complete clearance. No significant associations were identified between treatment response and the evaluated patient or lesion characteristics. Overall, these findings suggest a potential advantage of Nd: YAG over PDL, particularly for color improvement; however, larger randomized studies with standardized treatment protocols and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm the comparative efficacy and safety of these modalities.
