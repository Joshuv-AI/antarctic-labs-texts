RESPONSE : Benchmarking the Ability of Language Models to Undertake Commonsense Reasoning in Crisis Situation
Aïssatou Diallo, Antonis Bikakis, Luke Dickens, Anthony Hunter, Rob Miller

Abstract
An interesting class of commonsense reasoning problems arises when people are faced with natural disasters. To investigate this topic, we present RESPONSE , a human-curated dataset containing 1789 annotated instances featuring 6037 sets of questions designed to assess LLMs’ commonsense reasoning in disaster situations across different time frames. The dataset includes problem descriptions, missing resources, time-sensitive solutions, and their justifications, with a subset validated by environmental engineers. Through both automatic metrics and human evaluation, we compare LLM-generated recommendations against human responses. Our findings show that even state-of-the-art models like GPT-4 achieve only 37% human-evaluated correctness for immediate response actions, highlighting significant room for improvement in LLMs’ ability for commonsense reasoning in crises.



1
Introduction

As LLMs are being developed to handle different kinds of reasoning, there are questions about their ability in diverse commonsense reasoning situations. An interesting class of commonsense reasoning scenarios arises when people are faced with natural disasters. For this, it would be desirable for LLMs to be able to identify problems and solutions that are based on clear steps of commonsense reasoning, starting from the particular circumstances of the disaster scenario.

While it may seem concerning to rely on LLMs for disaster management advice, the reality is that people are already turning to these systems for guidance in various situations. At the same time, LLMs are becoming indispensable tools, serving in several roles such as dialogue agents, data analysts, and decision support. One critical area where LLMs could make a significant impact is in disaster analysis and response, particularly during preparation and recovery phases when immediate danger isn’t present. To exploit this potential effectively, we must evaluate their alignment with human commonsense in real-world disaster scenarios. If aligned, LLMs could provide valuable assistance to both individuals and agencies - offering support when traditional aid is limited, and helping agencies with scenario planning to develop more effective response strategies.

The range of emergencies is vast, from localized flooding to widespread wildfires. Their consequences, both immediate and long-term, can be devastating. To ensure LLMs are truly helpful, we must test them against a variety of scenarios, incorporating factors like time constraints, resource shortages, and specific geographic contexts. This will help us determine if LLMs can reason beyond regurgitating generic best practices.
More specifically, a proper evaluation should consider all aspects that make a remedial solution to an incident functional. When prompted with a incident-related question with a specific time setting, is the response
sufficient and appropriate
for the situation? Is the recommended course of action
useful
? Does the response contain all the information needed to act? And finally,
would a human follow this solution?
These are the questions explored in this work.

We propose a comprehensive dataset specifically designed to test the quality of LLM responses in disaster situations. This allows us to evaluate how LLMs interpret information, plan courses of action, and ultimately, provide good quality advice and recommendations during critical incidents.

Building on existing work on incident and natural disaster detection
Weber et al. (2020)
;
Weber et al. (2022)
, we have compiled a natural language dataset covering a variety of natural disasters, contextualized in different locations and time frames.
Specifically, the dataset consists of natural language questions, each addressing a specific incident-related problem that humans might face. We collected human annotations identifying the main problem and the missing resource as short sentences. Additionally, the annotators were asked to provide time-dependent solutions to the identified problem and explanations for their reasoning. Then, we automatically generated the questions using the identified problem and missing resource. These are contextualized in a specific temporal time frame and location. Furthermore, each set of questions is associated with the solutions and explanations collected. An example of a question from
RESPONSE
is:
"In the event of an ice storm leaving vehicles stranded in a
snowfield
, what is the
first step
to take to assist those trapped?"
, and the associated gold answer is
"plow snow enough to get a pathway for cars"
. The underlined text represents the location and time frame, respectively.

We made the deliberate choice to collect annotations from general annotators to test how LLMs align with human commonsense in disaster management
. To validate the quality of these annotations, we consulted environmental engineers who evaluated a sample of the collected responses. This dataset is not intended to: (i)
Replace human expertise
: the dataset aims to assess LLM performance in comparison to human judgments, while acknowledging the critical role of human experts in real-world disaster situations. (ii)
Provide definitive solutions
: while the dataset includes human-annotated solutions that have been partially validated by domain experts, these are not necessarily the only possible responses. The goal is to evaluate how well LLMs can align with human reasoning when providing relevant advice.

Next, we used the resulting dataset as a benchmark to evaluate thecommonsense recommendations of two of the best performing proprietary LLMs. We evaluated the responses using common evaluation metrics as well as human evaluation. Our findings show that, (1) LLMs generate solutions and explanations for incident related questions that are judged better than humans only in 37% of cases for the immediate time frame, and this value increases to 58% as the time horizon increases; (2) none of the commonly used evaluation metrics can properly discriminate the solutions.

Contributions
In summary, our contributions are: (1) we created
RESPONSE
, a question-answer (QA) dataset focusing on commonsense reasoning in disaster management, with annotations validated by two environmental engineers to ensure response quality. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first dataset of natural disaster management requiring commonsense reasoning to answer incident related questions with short natural language explanations. (2) We perform experiments on state-of-the-art LLMs to benchmark the commonsense reasoning of LLMs in disaster situations. (3) We analyze the alignment between LLM-generated disaster management strategies and those proposed by humans.

LLMs and Human Behaviour

There is a growing literature investigating LLMs acting as simulators of human agents
Argyle et al. (2023)
;
Horton (2023)
;
Park et al. (2023)
. These models encode a wide range of human behavior from their training data
Bommasani et al. (2021)

Horton (2023)
, simulated political surveys
Sorensen et al. (2022)
, and generated synthetic data
Hämäläinen et al. (2023)
using this approach. LLMs have also been used to create interactive human behavior for users to engage with, such as in interactive fiction
Freiknecht and Effelsberg (2020)
and text adventure games
Callison-Burch et al. (2022)
. Our study aims to supplement this growing literature by assessing how LLMs simulate and understand human behaviour in contexts that require situational awareness, disaster management knowledge and commonsense to react to incidents.

LLMs for Disaster Management

ChatGPT’s capabilities in natural disaster management were explored in
Xue et al. (2023)
. The study builds upon the potential of LLMs to streamline disaster response across pre-disaster, during-disaster, and post-disaster stages. Their work highlights the limitations of current methods in real-time data handling and achieving expert-level accuracy in complex scenarios. Similarly, this work
Goecks and Waytowich (2023)
propose the DisasterResponseGPT framework, leveraging LLMs for rapid action plan generation. However, both approaches face limitations inherent in general-purpose LLMs like ChatGPT. First, the lack of specialized training or domain-specific knowledge might lead to doubt in ChatGPT’s ability to provide sufficiently accurate human commonsense answers concerning complex disaster prevention and mitigation strategies. Secondly, the challenge of explaining and verifying generated responses creates difficulties for users in assessing their reliability and trustworthiness. Our dataset specifically addresses these two key shortcomings, in order to fully assess the true potential of LLMs in disaster management with respect to commonsense reasoning.

3
The RESPONSE Dataset

The objective of this study is to present a high-quality and challenging dataset of QA sets with the corresponding explanations. We developed an extensive data collection pipeline using the crowdsourcing platform CloudConnect Research.

Motivation

Our dataset focuses on evaluating the ability of LLMs to answer questions about disaster using commonsense, rather than relying on general expert-defined rules. This approach is motivated by the real-world challenges faced by decision-makers in disasters, where they often need to make informed judgments based on available information and general understanding. By focusing on missing resources, we isolate a critical factor that can significantly impact decision-making. Temporal horizons are included to simulate the dynamic nature of disaster response, where different actions may be appropriate at different stages.

Explanations are required to assess LLMs’ understanding of the problem, their ability to identify missing resources, and their justification for proposed solutions.
While expert annotations could provide valuable insights, our reliance on human raters aligns with the real-world scenario where decision-makers often rely on their own judgment and general knowledge for decision-making. Moreover, it allows us to investigate the role of commonsense.

Closer to a probe than a benchmark

While the potential for incorrect or misleading advice from LLMs is a serious concern, our dataset serves as an analytical tool to understand model behavior in disaster response scenarios. Rather than serving as a benchmark for optimization, our dataset helps researchers identify where models succeed or fail in using commonsense when providing practical disaster management advice, particularly in understanding temporal dependencies and resource constraints.

Task Formulation

The dataset need to provide insights into the strengths and weaknesses of LLMs in navigating these complex situations, allowing us to measure their suitability for supporting human decision-making in incident and disaster response.
Like the example in
Figure

1
,
RESPONSE
consists of N sets each containing a problem
p_{i}
, a missing resource
m_{i}
, a question
q^{t}_{i}
and the corresponding
a^{t}_{i}
.
t
represents the time frame for a specific question ["immediate", "after a week", "after a month"]. Previous works investigated reasoning through multiple choice
Lu et al. (2022)
, retrieval
Asai et al. (2019)
, and partial generation
Dalvi et al. (2021)
, however their task formulation has often the drawback of not revealing the reasoning process. For this reason, we opt for a generative task. The QA format evaluates the ability of LLMs to understand problems, identify missing resources, propose solutions, and justify their reasoning.

Preprocessing

We began with sampling 10000 data instances from Incidents 1M
Weber et al. (2022)
which ensures that the initial set of images are related to incidents and disasters. Incidents 1M is a large-scale dataset comprising over one million labeled images, designed for the task recognizing and categorizing incidents within visual scenes. It contains 43 incidents (e.g. landslide, flooding, earthquake, etc) and 49 location categories (e.g. building outdoor, highway, mountain). From this set of incidents, we exclude all the traffic related ones as well as the incidents were human survival is unlikely such as nuclear explosion, or again incidents that the common person is unlikely to observe such as "volcanic eruption". Then, we downloaded the images corresponding to this subset. Due to the inherent noise in the images from such a large dataset, we manually select the most expressive images.

Data Collection

We recruited college-educated annotators from English-speaking regions through CloudConnect Research, requiring basic familiarity with disaster scenarios. Using a structured interface, annotators viewed incident images and identified: (1) the main problem someone might face, (2) key missing resources, and (3) time-sensitive solutions with explanations for three horizons (immediate, week, month). Solutions were limited to 15 words. For each scenario, we collected annotations from two different annotators and had environmental engineers validate a sample for quality assurance. The interface facilitated structured responses by providing separate fields for each component, as shown in
Figure

2
.

Natural Language Question Generation

After manually verifying the quality of the annotations, we prompted GPT-4 to create natural questions for the collected samples in a 1-shot manner. We provided a handcrafted example of the input data and output questions. Specifically, we first prompted the LLM to provide all the possible combinations of place and incident labels. Then, we asked GPT-4 to create one question for each tuple
(incident, place)
, from the original Incidents 1M dataset classes. We specified in the prompt, that the questions should mention the main problem, place and incident label and be related to the missing resource. Solutions and explanations were not included in the prompt. Additionally, each tuple
(incident, place)
should correspond to three natural questions, one for each time frame.

4.2
Automatic Evaluation Metrics

The still developing area of text generation has measures and proxies for correctness of text sequences based on similarity. For this reason, we used automatic evaluation metrics
1
1

1

https://huggingface.co/evaluate-metric
that measure surface similarity of the predictions to the references.
BLEU

Papineni et al. (2002)
(Papineni et al., 2002) is one of the most known evaluation metrics for text generation. It computes the geometric average of the precision over output text’s n-grams. We use SacreBLEU
Post (2018)
that produces comparable and reproducible BLEU scores.

BertScore

Zhang et al. (2019)
uses BERT
Devlin et al. (2019)
embeddings and matches words in the output and reference sentences by cosine similarity. It then computes precision, recall, and F1 measures based on the resulting matching. We use
microsoft/deberta-xlarge-mnli
as the model for the score and report the F1.

BLEURT

Sellam et al. (2020)
is a learned evaluation metric based on BERT. It is first pre-trained on synthetic examples and then fine-tuned on human judgments for the task of machine translation.

4.3
LLM-based Evaluation

We employed GPT-4o in a structured evaluation framework using a carefully designed template with specific criteria and scoring guidelines to ensure consistent and interpretable evaluations across three practical dimensions: Resource Management, Temporal-Location Feasibility, and Individual Impact. Each dimension is scored on a 1-5 scale with justification, assessing aspects like resource utilization, contextual appropriateness, and solution safety that traditional metrics cannot capture. This structured analysis bridges the gap between surface-level metrics and human evaluation by focusing on operational feasibility. We found a strong correlation (r = 0.82) between the
human evaluation (setting
win, tie
and
lose
to (1, 0.5, -1) respectively) and overall judgment. The prompt is available in the appendix.

4.4
Human Evaluation

Following
Goyal et al. (2022)
’s finding that automatic metrics may not reliably evaluate LLM outputs, we conducted human evaluation using 50 evaluators recruited through CloudConnect Research. Evaluators were college-educated residents of English-speaking countries with basic familiarity with disaster scenarios. They assessed 100 randomly sampled question-response pairs for each time frame, comparing LLM outputs against
RESPONSE
references using a Win/Tie/Lose framework.
Evaluators rated responses on three criteria:
(1)
Sufficiency
: Whether the answer-explanation pair provides complete, situation-appropriate information
(2)
Usefulness
: How actionable and valuable the information is for implementation
(3)
Correctness
: Whether the evaluator would follow the given advice
This evaluation framework helps assess both the quality and practicality of LLM-generated responses in disaster scenarios.

4.5
Human Performance

For the sake of completeness, we sampled 10 questions for each time frame among the ones evaluated by humans and submit those questions to a pool of 30 human annotators excluding the ones who participated to the data collection and the human evaluation efforts. We collected their answers and evaluated them using the automated evaluation metrics described earlier to confirm the initial findings.

5
Results

In this section, we present the numerical results of the experiments detailed earlier and discuss our findings. All the human-evaluated pairs of answer and explanation are outputs from GPT-4.

5.1
Automatic Evaluation

The automatic evaluation results in
Table

2
prove inconclusive for three main reasons. First, most metrics show limited sensitivity, with BLEU scores (range [0.0, 100.0]) clustering between 0.42-0.68 and similarly compressed ranges for BLEURT. Second, BertScore values are consistently high across all experiments, contradicting human evaluation results and suggesting it may overemphasize surface-level similarities. Third, the metrics show inconsistent rankings - for instance, in the immediate timeframe, BLEU favors Claude-3 while BertScore favors GPT-4. These inconsistencies suggest that current automatic metrics may be insufficient for evaluating LLM performance on this task, highlighting the need for more sophisticated evaluation approaches.

5.2
LLM-based Evaluation

The structured evaluation reveals that human responses consistently outperform LLM solutions across all assessment dimensions, achieving an overall score of 0.81. Among the LLMs, Claude (0.75) performs better than GPT-4 (0.68), though both fall short of human performance. Notably, all sources score highest on Resource Management (Human: 0.83, Claude: 0.77, GPT-4: 0.71), suggesting that identifying and addressing missing resources is handled more effectively than other aspects. The lower scores in Individual Impact (Human: 0.78, Claude: 0.72, GPT-4: 0.64) indicate that providing safe, actionable guidance remains a challenge, particularly for LLMs. These findings align with our human evaluation results, where LLMs showed better performance in longer time horizons but struggled with immediate response scenarios. We evaluated whether LLM responses provide necessary information for taking appropriate action in given scenarios. Following the protocol in
Section

However, LLM performance improved for longer timeframes, with evaluators preferring generated responses in 56% of week-horizon and 62% of month-horizon cases. This suggests LLMs are more reliable for longer-term planning than immediate response guidance.

Are the answers of LLMs useful and informative?

This question measures which pair best provides a clear answer with sufficient details to guide someone towards a successful and commonsensical remedial action. We follow the same setting of the previous paragraph. The results are presented in columns 4 to 6 of

This indicate that the model exhibits a stronger ability to create informative pairs for longer timeframes (weeks and months) compared to immediate ones.

Would you follow this recommendation?

This question is used as a proxy for correctness as it aims to measure how well the generated answers and explanations aligns with human commonsense in incident and disaster situations. This final question follows the same trend of the previous paragraphs. Analyzing
Table

This suggests a need for improvement in remedial recommendation in a context of urgency. The ’Win’ column shows an increase in preference for the model’s pairs (58% and 57.9% respectively). In summary, the model appears to improve at generating solutions that better match human preferences when asked for remedial actions for incidents spanning longer time horizons.

5.5
Human performance

To establish a human performance baseline, we collected responses from a new set of annotators for a sample of questions and evaluated them using both automatic metrics and human judgment. The results reveal several important insights:
(i)
Metric Limitations
: When evaluating human-generated responses against
RESPONSE
gold answers using automatic metrics (BLEU, BLEURT, and BertScore), we observed scores within similar ranges as LLM-generated responses. This suggests these metrics may not effectively capture the qualitative differences that matter in disaster response recommendations.
(ii)
Human Agreement
: When we presented both human-generated responses and
RESPONSE
gold answers to evaluators, approximately 70% indicated no clear preference between the two. This high rate of "ties" suggests that multiple valid approaches often exist for handling disaster situations, and validates the quality of our dataset’s gold answers.
(iii)
Evaluation Consistency
: The structured quality analysis scores (shown in
Table

3
) demonstrate a clearer distinction between human and LLM performance than traditional metrics, particularly in Resource Management and Individual Impact dimensions. This suggests our structured analysis framework may better capture meaningful differences in response quality.
These findings highlight the importance of developing more nuanced evaluation methods for disaster response recommendations, where success often depends on practical feasibility rather than textual similarity to a single "correct" answer.

6
Conclusion

We release
RESPONSE
, a question answering dataset for evaluating LLM’s reasoning ability in disaster management scenarios. The dataset includes problem identification and time-sensitive solutions, reflecting real-world disaster response complexities. Testing state-of-the-art LLMs on RESPONSE reveals that even GPT-4 achieves only 37% human-level accuracy for immediate remedial actions. Our results show common evaluation metrics are unsuitable for this task, lacking correlation with human evaluation and disagreeing with each other. This highlights both the need for improved LLM commonsense reasoning in critical decision-making scenarios and the development of better automatic evaluation measures for such tasks.

Limitations

Despite the advantages of
RESPONSE
, we would like to acknowledge certain limitations. Firstly, the dataset inherit inaccuracies of the original Incident1M. Despite efforts to manually select the most expressive images, the labels associated to the images are not always made evident by the image itself. This is mostly reflected in the automatic context generation and in the contextualization of the questions. Second, comparing LLM recommendations with human responses is complex due to the variability in human decision-making. Human responses can vary widely, influenced by personal experiences, expertise, and intuitive thinking, which may not always be consistent or optimal. Hence, aligning machine recommendations with such a varied baseline can be challenging. Third, the subjectivity of the annotations can be a limitation in evaluating LMs as generating a ’correct’ answer may depend significantly on individual or cultural perspectives which may not be adequately represented. Additionally, the diversity in types of disasters, cultural contexts, and unforeseen variables present significant challenges that might not be fully addressed.

Ethical Statement

For data collection, we relied on annotators located in English speaking regions (Australia, Canada, New Zealand, the United Kingdom, and the United States). The average hourly pay is 15.00 US dollars, which exceeds the income requirements proposed in the human subjects research protocols. We do not collect personal information about the workers. The interfaces are displayed in the appendix. The proposed dataset does not contain inappropriate suggestions that could potentially exacerbate emergency situations or lead to harm. Due to the experimental nature, limited production applicability, and relatively small dataset scale, we believe the potential for misuse or harm is negligible.
