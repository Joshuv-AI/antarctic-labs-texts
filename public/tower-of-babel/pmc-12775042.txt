Prevalence and influencing factors of dementia in the elderly iranian population: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Esmat Karimian, Afrooz Saffari Fard, Mehri Golparvar, Hossein Khodaei, Masoumeh Salehi, Gholamreza Hajati, Fatemeh Khamseh, Farshad Sharifi, Yousef Veisani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe global rise in dementia prevalence, a major neurocognitive disorder, poses a significant challenge to aging populations. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of dementia and its associated risk factors among community-dwelling elderly in selected provinces of Iran.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2022 to March 2024, enrolling 58,228 individuals aged 60–75 years from two provinces (Qazvin and Yazd) selected via multi-stage, stratified random sampling. A multi-stage diagnostic protocol was employed, using validated Persian versions of the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), Abbreviated Mental Test Score (AMTS), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Functional Assessment Staging (FAST), and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) with standardized cut-offs. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with dementia.ResultsAfter exclusions, the final analytical sample was 56,456 participants. The overall prevalence of dementia was 10.1%. Prevalence was significantly higher in women (13.2%) than in men (7.0%). In the adjusted model, female gender (aOR: 2.04, 95% CI: 1.92–2.15), older age (70–79 years vs. 60–64 years, aOR: 1.70, 95% CI: 1.59–1.82), widowhood (aOR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.05–2.16), and lower educational attainment (illiterate vs. academic, aOR: 6.55, 95% CI: 5.43–7.91) were significantly associated with higher odds of dementia.ConclusionsThis large-scale study reveals a substantial burden of dementia in the studied Iranian provinces. The identified risk factors, including female sex, advanced age, widowhood, and low education, highlight vulnerable subgroups and underscore the urgent need for targeted public health strategies focused on early detection, risk reduction, and support systems for the elderly in Iran.Keywords: Dementia, Epidemiology, Aging, Risk Factors, Iran, Cognitive Reserve

Introduction

The World Health Organization classifies individuals over 65 as elderly. Globally, the annual growth rate of the elderly population over 65 years is 2.5%. In Iran, this growth rate decreased from 3.4% in 1996 to 2.5% in 2006, and it is projected to exceed 2.8%, with the elderly population in Iran expected to represent 26% of the total population by 2050 [1]. As the elderly population increases alongside the prevalence of dementia in older age, early and rapid diagnosis and treatment of dementia can help slow its progression and enhance the quality of life for these individuals [2]. Dementia is a significant global health issue that poses serious challenges to healthcare systems. The World Health Organization defines dementia as a syndrome characterized by a gradual decline in cognitive function beyond normal aging [3, 4]. Alzheimer’s disease is the most prevalent type of dementia. The incidence of dementia rises with age, peaking in individuals over 80 years old. In 2020, around 50 million people worldwide were living with dementia, a figure projected to rise to 152 million by 2050 [5]. Prevalence rates for dementia are higher in high-income countries compared to low- and middle-income countries, attributed to factors like population aging, lifestyle choices, and healthcare access [6]. Risk factors associated with the development of dementia include age, genetics, lifestyle habits, and specific medical conditions such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease [7].

Recent research has explored dementia rates among older adults in Iran. A national survey revealed an age-standardized dementia prevalence of 8.1% in individuals aged 60 and above, with women experiencing higher rates (9.6%) compared to men (6.5%) [8]. However, a systematic review and meta-analysis reported a lower overall crude prevalence of 14% [9]. Hospital-based studies showed varying prevalence rates, with one study reporting that 36.6% of elderly inpatients had some form of cognitive impairment[10]. In contrast, another multicenter study found that 30.7% of older patients were diagnosed with major neurocognitive disorders [11]. Dementia-related factors included age, gender, and number of children [12, 13]. Nevertheless, the limited studies on dementia prevalence in Iran have shown varying results, with estimates between 6.5% and 36.6%. 4, 5 These discrepancies highlight the need for further research to obtain a more precise estimate. Moreover, dementia prevalence is believed to double every five years,6 underscoring the importance of an updated report.

Studies on dementia prevalence are crucial for building evidence-based knowledge about its epidemiology and aiding in healthcare planning and resource allocation. Given the growing elderly population and the economic and social burdens posed by dementia, there is an increasing need for effective prevention and management strategies. This study aims to estimate the prevalence rate and identify factors influencing it among individuals aged 60–75 in Iran.

Material and methods

Study type and population

This cross-sectional study focuses on the elderly Iranian population, aged 60–75. We obtained the demographic information of older people from the national census data from October 25 to November 25, 2016. We conducted a multi-stage and stratified population selection. The strata used to access the sample under study included geographical region, province, county, age, and gender distribution. Two out of Iran’s five major geographical areas were randomly selected to capture diverse national demographic and socio-economic profiles. From these two regions, one province was randomly chosen from each, resulting in the selection of Qazvin (representing the north) and Yazd (representing the south/center).The population aged 60 to 75 years in Qazvin province was 82,722, and in Yazd province, it was 70,459. The process of selecting participants in the study was based on the proportion of the elderly population in the counties of the two provinces. The study included all eligible elderly individuals aged 60–75 years, as registered in the general census. Those in nursing homes were excluded, and participants with incurable diseases were removed. In total, 88 screening centers were responsible for screening the population, each consisting of a physician, a psychologist (interviewer), and other staff. The Iranian Alzheimer’s Association ultimately approved the study’s execution protocol.

Procedures and diagnostic criteria

Face-to-face interviews were conducted with all eligible participants by trained screening experts. A comprehensive training program was implemented, consisting of 16 h of theoretical and practical instruction for interviewers on administering the study instruments and using the data collection software. Physicians received 4 h of specialized training. Refresher courses were held two months later to ensure protocol adherence. To guarantee data quality, the inter-rater reliability among interviewers was required to exceed 0.90, and random phone calls were made to 5% of participants for quality control. All data were stored on a secure central server.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was calculated using a formula for estimating a population proportion. With an assumed dementia prevalence of 9% based on previous national studies, a design effect of 2.0 to account for the multi-stage cluster sampling, a margin of error of 5%, and a confidence level of 95%, a minimum sample of 60,000 individuals (30,000 per province) was targeted to ensure precise estimates.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic characteristics of the study population. Prevalence rates for dementia are presented as percentages with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To identify factors independently associated with dementia, we constructed a multivariable binary logistic regression model. The model included the following covariates simultaneously: age group (60–64 as reference), gender (male as reference), educational level (academic as reference), and marital status (single as reference). The results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% CIs. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata software, version 17, and a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

This study was conducted from October 5, 2022, to March 25, 2024. Initially, 58,228 older persons were enrolled. After the exclusion of 1,772 participants due to incomplete information (n = 1,135, 64%), invalid questionnaires (n = 512, 29%), or data errors (n = 122, 7%), the final analytical sample comprised 56,456 individuals. Among these, 29,484 (52.2%) were male and 28,744 (47.8%) were female.

The largest age group was 60–64 years, comprising 29,388 individuals (52.1%). Most participants had an elementary-level education (35.1%) and were married (82.8%). Notable gender disparities were observed in marital status and education. Widowhood was more common among women (23.3%) than men (2.5%). Furthermore, women were twice as likely as men to have no formal education (52.6% vs. 24.3%), while men were more likely to have at least a high-school education (Table 1).

A total of 5,856 elderly individuals were diagnosed with dementia, yielding a prevalence rate of 10.1%. The study comprised 2,052 men with a prevalence of 7.0% and 3,804 women with a prevalence of 13.2%. The prevalence of dementia increased with age in both genders. Among those aged 70–75, men had a prevalence of 9.8% and women had a prevalence of 17.6%. Analysis by marital status showed that men who had remarried had a prevalence of 9.0%, while women who had lost their spouses had the highest prevalence among all groups at 16.5%. The highest prevalence of dementia across both sexes was observed in illiterate individuals, with a rate of 11.0% in men and 17.9% in women (Table 2).

Increasing Dementia Prevalence with Advancing Age

The prevalence of dementia exhibited a clear upward trend with advancing age in both genders, as shown in Fig. 2. Among men, the prevalence rate increased from 0.058 in the youngest age group to 0.098 in the oldest. A more pronounced increase was observed among women, with prevalence rising from 0.111 to 0.176 across the same age groups. Furthermore, the absolute number of dementia cases was consistently higher in women for every age category (Fig. 2).

The association between marital status and dementia was independent of other factors. The death of a widowed increased the odds of dementia compared to single individuals (aOR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.05, 2.16; p = 0.026). Furthermore, a strong inverse relationship was found with educational level. When compared to individuals with academic degrees, those with no formal education had over six times the odds of dementia (aOR: 6.55, 95% CI: 5.43, 7.91; p < 0.001), while those with only an elementary education had 3.54 times the odds (aOR: 3.54, 95% CI: 2.93, 4.28; p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Discussion

This large, population-based study in two Iranian provinces provides a dementia prevalence estimate of 10.1% among adults aged 60–75. The primary value of our findings lies not in claiming national representativeness, but in the methodological rigor of a large, multi-stage sampled cohort with a standardized diagnostic protocol. When placed in the context of the existing literature, our estimate necessitates a critical examination of the methodological and demographic factors driving disparities in reported prevalence rates.

Our prevalence of 10.1% is higher than the 8% reported in a recent 2024 meta-analysis [9] and the 7.9% from a 2012 study. These discrepancies are not contradictory but rather informative. The earlier study by Sharifi et al. [19] relied on a much smaller sample [1, 350 participants], which increases the margin of error and reduces the precision of the estimate. Our larger sample (n = 56,456) provides a more stable and reliable point estimate for the populations studied.

More importantly, the difference from the meta-analysis [9] likely stems from key methodological choices. Our study utilized the MoCA, which is recognized for its high sensitivity in detecting mild cognitive impairment and early dementia, whereas many older studies included in such meta-analyses may have relied on less sensitive instruments like the MMSE. This methodological difference alone could account for the higher case identification in our cohort [9, 19]. Furthermore, our study was conducted in two specific provinces. It is plausible that the unique genetic, environmental, or socio-economic profiles of these regions contribute to a different underlying risk profile compared to the national average generated by pooling diverse studies.

The robust associations we observed with sex and education warrant a deeper exploration of potential mechanisms. The finding that women had nearly twice the odds of dementia, even after multivariable adjustment, aligns with global literature but is often poorly explained. Beyond the frequently cited biological hypothesis of post-menopausal estrogen decline [20, 21], we must consider socio-structural factors. In the context of the studied provinces, older women historically had significantly fewer educational and occupational opportunities. This lifetime of lower cognitive stimulation may contribute to a reduced cognitive reserve, making them more vulnerable to expressing neuropathological changes clinically. Thus, the gender disparity we observe is likely not a matter of biology alone but a complex interplay of biology and lifelong social experience.

Similarly, the powerful, graded inverse relationship between educational attainment and dementia risk strongly supports the cognitive reserve hypothesis. Education is thought to enhance the brain’s neural networks and promote more efficient cognitive processing. This built-in resilience allows the brain to better compensate for age- or disease-related pathology, thereby delaying the clinical onset of dementia symptoms. In our study, the dramatically elevated odds in illiterate individuals (aOR: 6.55) underscore education not merely as a variable, but potentially as a foundational social determinant of cognitive aging. This suggests that public health initiatives aimed at increasing literacy and educational access could have profound long-term impacts on dementia incidence.

The association between widowhood and increased dementia risk highlights the role of psychosocial factors. The loss of a spouse often leads to profound loneliness, a known risk factor for cognitive decline. This can be compounded by a reduction in shared cognitive activities, social engagement, and emotional support, all of which are vital for maintaining cognitive health in later life [22].

Limitations

The interpretation of our findings must be considered in the context of several important limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes the establishment of causal relationships between the identified risk factors and dementia. Secondly, while the random selection of two provinces enhances internal validity, the findings cannot be extrapolated to the entire Iranian nation and are most representative of its northern and central regions, limiting geographical generalizability. Thirdly, although we employed a standardized multi-stage protocol with validated cut-offs, our diagnostic approach remains a screening and assessment algorithm; a definitive clinical diagnosis typically requires more extensive investigation, including neuroimaging and longitudinal follow-up, which were not feasible in this large-scale study. Finally, cultural stigma surrounding cognitive decline and variable health-seeking behaviors could have led to under-reporting or misrepresentation of symptoms, potentially influencing the accuracy of prevalence estimates.

Implications and Future Directions

Despite these limitations, our findings have clear implications. For public health policy, they argue for integrating cognitive screening into primary care for the elderly, with a special focus on high-risk groups: women, the less educated, and the widowed. For research, they highlight the necessity of future longitudinal studies in Iran to establish causality and the critical need for truly national, harmonized surveillance to map the country’s diverse dementia landscape.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a robust estimate of dementia prevalence in two major Iranian provinces, revealing a significant burden strongly linked to female sex, lower education, and widowhood. The differences from previous estimates [8, 9] underscore the impact of methodology and geography. Addressing the growing challenge of dementia in Iran will require targeted public health strategies that address these key sociodemographic risk factors.
