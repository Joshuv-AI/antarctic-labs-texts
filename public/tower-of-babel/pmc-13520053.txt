DATS: A Depth-Aware Transformer Hybrid Framework for Geological Parameter Prediction
Authors: Hengyang Lv, Jianhong Guo, Qing Zhao, Zhansong Zhang, Xin Nie, Chong Zhang, Jian Song, Wenjun Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate prediction of reservoir permeability constitutes a central task in hydrocarbon field development, but it remains challenging because core measurements are sparse and heterogeneous reservoirs commonly exhibit nonunique logging responses. Conventional machine learning methods typically treat each core measurement as an independent sample for end-to-end permeability prediction, thereby overlooking the stratigraphic context embedded in depth-ordered core sequences. In this study, we propose a depth-aware Transformer-based hybrid modeling framework, termed DATS, for geological parameter prediction from sparsely sampled core data. DATS constructs sequential samples using sliding windows of core measurements and incorporates depth information and stratigraphic priors through three key components: a continuous Fourier-based depth encoding with learnable frequency parameters, an attention mechanism incorporating depth–distance bias penalties, and a two-stage training strategy that bridges continuous well-logging data and discrete core measurements. Applied as a feature extractor to carbonate reservoirs in the study area, DATS was evaluated by using 2856 core samples. Through comparison among three feature combination strategies and 14 downstream models, the best-performing model, DATS-XGBoost_Combined, achieved an R 2 of 0.9404. Ablation experiments indicate that each constituent module of DATS contributes positively to the overall model performance. SHAP values and attention matrix analyses further suggest that the performance improvement is associated with the ability of DATS to capture depth-dependent stratigraphic patterns consistent with petrophysical principles. These results suggest that DATS can serve as a supplementary tool for permeability prediction in sparsely cored reservoirs and assist parameter evaluation in uncored or undersampled wells.

Introduction

Permeability, as a fundamental parameter characterizing the fluid flow capacity of reservoirs, plays a pivotal role in production forecasting and in guiding hydrocarbon field development. , However, its precise determination in practice is constrained by a critical trade-off: core analysis experiments provide the most accurate measurements of reservoir permeability, yet their application is limited by the high cost of coring, rendering full-well coverage impractical; in contrast, geophysical well logging, as a principal technique for subsurface reservoir characterization, offers clear advantages in depth continuity and spatial coverage relative to core experiments. Consequently, the establishment of reliable and high-precision mapping models between well-logging data and permeability is essential for improving the accuracy of permeability evaluation in unconventional reservoirs. −

The advent of machine learning has provided new avenues for addressing this challenge. − In recent years, models such as random forests and gradient boosting decision trees have been widely adopted in well-log interpretation, − consistently achieving predictive performance superior to that of traditional empirical approaches. Nevertheless, these methods generally treat each core measurement and its corresponding logging data as independent instances, thereby overlooking the depth-wise sequential context inherent in both logging curves and core samplesspecifically, the stratigraphic dependencies arising from depositional and diagenetic processes.

With the continued advancement of artificial intelligence, increasing attention has been directed toward the vertical sequential dependencies inherent in well-logging curves, prompting the adoption of deep learning approaches capable of modeling such sequence relationships. For instance, recurrent neural networks (RNNs), long short-term memory networks (LSTMs), and gated recurrent units (GRUs) have been employed to capture temporal patterns along depth sequences, while convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been utilized to extract localized features from continuous well intervals. − To further enhance model sensitivity to critical stratigraphic intervals and improve interpretability, recent studies have also incorporated attention mechanisms into these architectures, enabling dynamic weighting of logging information across different depths. ,

Since its introduction, the Transformer architecture has demonstrated remarkable context-aware modeling capabilities across domains such as natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision, and has more recently been progressively adopted in geoscientific applications. , Building upon this foundation, a growing body of research has explored the potential of Transformer-based models in well-log data analysis. For instance, Sun et al. incorporated a Transformer as the core weak learner within an AdaBoost ensemble framework for lithology identification, achieving accuracies of 95.2% and 95.5% on two logging data sets; Yin et al. combined Transformers with deep reinforcement learning (DRL), employing the former to capture complex dependencies in logging sequences and the latter to dynamically select high-quality data, thereby attaining over 95% accuracy in reservoir fluid identification in the Tarim Oilfield; Sun et al. further enhanced porosity prediction by integrating dynamic convolutional kernels into the Transformer architecture, achieving an R 2 of 93.99% on the test set; Xie et al. leveraged the multihead self-attention mechanism of Transformers in conjunction with the long-sequence modeling capacity of LSTM to achieve high-accuracy lithology classification; Ta et al. adapted the attention mechanism to propose TabNet, a model tailored for tabular data, and applied it to facies classification; and Liu et al. employed a Transformer-based architecture for reconstructing missing well-log curves, developing a Missing Well Log Transformer (MWLT) that significantly outperformed both the traditional Gardner equation and data-driven approaches in the Songliao Basin. Collectively, these studies underscore the distinctive advantages of Transformers in well-log evaluation; however, most existing research has focused on lithology classification, fluid identification, or log reconstruction using continuous full-well data, whereas applications to core-derived reservoir parameter prediction remain relatively limited. This limitation is closely related to the sparse and discontinuous nature of the core measurements. Unlike continuous logging curves, core-derived permeability data are available only at discrete depth points, making it difficult for conventional Transformer models to construct complete depth sequences and learn stable vertical dependencies. In addition, most existing Transformer-based approaches treat depth mainly as a generic sequence index and insufficiently incorporate stratigraphic boundaries or formation-level geological priors. As a result, they may overlook discontinuous core sampling patterns and weaken the geological consistency across stratigraphic units.

To address these challenges, we propose a depth-aware Transformer-based hybrid modeling framework (DATS) tailored for core-derived reservoir parameter prediction. Built upon an encoder-only Transformer backbone, DATS incorporates depth positional encoding, a depth–distance-biased attention mechanism, multisource information fusion guided by stratigraphic priors, and a two-stage training strategy. These components are designed to improve the use of sparse core samples, preserve depth neighborhood relationships, and introduce formation-level geological constraints into sequence representation learning. The embedding features extracted by DATS are subsequently fed into downstream ensemble learning models, enabling the prediction framework to integrate the sequence modeling capacity of deep learning with the robust generalization of ensemble methods. Using 2856 core permeability samples from an oilfield in Iraq as the primary data set, the optimal model, DATS-XGBoost_Combined, was identified among 42 candidate configurations and achieved an R2 of 0.9404, RMSE of 0.2965, and MAE of 0.2174 during model selection. Compared with end-to-end models based solely on raw logging curves, this approach yields a substantial improvement in predictive accuracy, while ablation studies further support the contributions of each constituent module.

Methodology

DATS (Depth-Aware Transformer) is a feature-processing framework specifically designed for prediction tasks in which core experimental data serve as the target variable. It addresses key limitations of conventional modeling approaches, which often fail to effectively exploit depth-wise contextual information, geological prior knowledge, and local stratigraphic continuity. Built upon an encoder-only Transformer architecture (Figure a), DATS differs fundamentally from generative Transformer models typified by GPT. Representative encoder-only Transformersincluding BERT, Vision Transformer (ViT), and TabTransformerare primarily employed for representation learning, encoding, and feature extraction, rather than for sequence generation.

Notably, DATS employs a rigorous two-stage training strategy, which serves as a critical mechanism for ensuring that its depth awareness is grounded in a coherent understanding of continuous stratigraphic structures.

Construction of Depth-Sequential Samples

In contrast to conventional modeling approaches that treat each depth point as an independent sample, DATS constructs model inputs as depth sequences using a sliding-window strategy. Training samples are first grouped according to well identifiers and stratigraphic units, within which local depth intervals are extracted using a fixed window length, typically defined as 2n + 1, with a stride of 1. This design enables DATS to systematically traverse each target point along with its n preceding an n succeeding neighboring samples, thereby equipping the model with the capacity to characterize the geological properties of the central position by leveraging local contextual information.

However, because the training targets are derived from core experimentscharacterized by relatively large and irregular sampling intervalsdirect application of the aforementioned sliding-window strategy can easily lead to the erroneous concatenation of distant stratigraphic intervals into a single sequence, thereby causing the attention mechanism within the DATS encoder to be influenced by long-range, geologically unrelated samples. To ensure that input sequences more faithfully reflect true vertical stratigraphic structures and to prevent large depth gaps from distorting the model’s understanding of contextual relationships, DATS introduces a maximum depth-interval constraint during the sliding-window construction process: when the depth difference between two adjacent samples exceeds a predefined threshold, they are automatically treated as discontinuous. All samples within each group are accordingly partitioned into multiple continuous subsequences at these discontinuities, and sliding windows are constructed exclusively within each continuous segment. For cases in which insufficient neighboring samples exist at the boundaries of a segment, a boundary replication strategy is adopted to complete the window, thereby preserving window integrity while ensuring that all samples participate in modeling, without introducing artificial or spurious values.

During Stage A self-supervised training, the input samples consist of continuous well-logging curves which, after data preprocessing and cleaning, exhibit neither missing values nor discontinuities; consequently, the maximum depth-interval constraint is not required, as illustrated in Figure a. This constraint is primarily applied during Stage B supervised fine-tuning, where the training samples are derived from core experimental data; in this case, the sliding-window construction follows the scheme illustrated in Figure b.

Joint Encoding of Multisource Information

At the input representation stage, DATS adopts the concept of token representation from natural language processing, mapping the multidimensional well-logging feature vector at each core-sample depth position into a unified latent space as input tokens. , In this context, a well-logging window sequence of length T is denoted as 1 X=[x1,x2,x3,...,xT]∈RT×F where F denotes the dimensionality of the well-logging features. For the tth depth position in the sequence, the corresponding raw logging feature vector xt is projected into a token representation via a linear transformation, expressed as follows: 2 ht(f)=Wfxt+bf where W f denotes the feature projection matrix and b f represents the corresponding bias term. Through this transformation, the raw well-logging features are converted into a sequence of feature tokens suitable for processing by the Transformer encoder. This design enables the model to learn, within a unified embedding space, both the combinatorial relationships among different logging features and the dependency structures across neighboring depth intervals.

In contrast to the original Transformer, which relies primarily on generic sinusoidal positional encoding to capture sequence order, depth sequences derived from well-logging and core experiments are inherently discontinuous, rendering such positional encodings insufficient for representing the true depth-dependent characteristics of core samples. To address this limitation, the present study explicitly incorporates stratigraphic information together with absolute and relative depth information during token construction, thereby achieving a joint encoding of multisource information. Specifically, stratigraphic unit indices are mapped via an embedding layer into stratigraphic prior vectors, which are then replicated along the sequence dimension and added to each token representation, enhancing the model’s ability to distinguish formation boundaries and stratigraphic context. Furthermore, given that depth information conveys not only sequential order but also explicit spatial meaning in reservoir parameter prediction, DATS performs continuous encoding of both absolute and relative depth, projecting them into the same embedding space as the tokens. Accordingly, the overall input representation can be formulated as 3 Z(0)=XWf+Ehor+Eabs+Erel where XW f denotes the tokenized representation of well-logging features, E hor represents the stratigraphic embedding, and E abs and E rel correspond to the encoded representations of absolute and relative depth, respectively.

In terms of depth encoding, unlike NLP tasks where inputs consist of discrete tokens, the inputs in this study are quasi-continuous physical variables. Accordingly, DATS employs a learnable continuous Fourier encoding scheme to map depth variables across multiple frequencies. This approach enables the direct transformation of scalar depth values into high-dimensional, smooth, and learnable feature representations. Specifically, absolute depth encoding primarily captures global-scale depth information, whereas relative depth encoding emphasizes local-scale variations; their integration enhances the model’s capacity to represent depth-dependent patterns across multiple scales. For an arbitrary depth scalar d, the depth encoding can be expressed as 4 φ(d)=[sin(dβ0),cos(dβ0),...,sin(dβk−1),cos(dβk−1)] where d denotes the sample depth, β k represents the kth frequency base, and k is the number of frequency bases; each frequency base generates a two-dimensional component of the form [sin­(d/β k ),cos­(d/β k )], thereby constituting the Fourier-based depth positional encoding.

The resulting depth encodings are subsequently projected into the embedding space via a linear transformation to obtain the corresponding depth representations. As illustrated in Figure , the continuous Fourier depth encoding exhibits distinct frequency-dependent patterns across different depth ranges: the absolute depth encoding shown in Figure a displays smooth, long-wavelength variations over the full well depth, with even high-frequency components capable of representing complete cycles at large scales; in contrast, the relative depth encoding in Figure b adopts smaller frequency bases, producing denser periodic variations within local windows, thereby effectively capturing fine-grained positional relationships among neighboring depth points. Compared with the standard sinusoidal positional encoding of Transformers shown in Figure c, which relies on discrete token indices (with the vertical axis representing sequence positions), the Fourier-based depth encoding directly takes physical depth values as input (with the vertical axis representing depth), enabling a more faithful characterization of periodicity and relative variation across multiple scales. This design allows the model to preserve awareness of global depth structure while learning local stratigraphic relationships and is therefore better suited to the irregular and discontinuous nature of depth sequences in core-derived data than conventional deep learning approaches.

Depth–Distance-Biased Attention Mechanism

ALiBi (Attention with Linear Biases) positional encoding, as one of the most computationally efficient positional encoding strategies, introduces a linear penalty term proportional to the relative distance between tokens into the attention scores, thereby biasing the attention distribution toward neighboring tokens while progressively attenuating attention to more distant ones. Inspired by the ALiBi paradigm, DATS extends this concept to stratigraphic depth sequences by incorporating an attention mechanism based on the relative depth differences. Specifically, a depth–distance bias term is constructed by using a quadratic penalty formulation, which imposes increasingly stronger penalties on distant samples. This results in a depth-aware attention mechanism tailored for sequential depth modeling, enabling DATS to preserve global dependency modeling while maintaining a heightened sensitivity to vertically adjacent geological samples. The quadratic penalty further encourages the model to allocate greater attention weights to more proximal stratigraphic intervals.

Specifically, after the input sequence is augmented with stratigraphic, absolute depth, and relative depth encodings, it undergoes layer normalization and dropout operations consistent with the standard Transformer architecture and is subsequently fed into a customized encoder composed of multiple Transformer blocks. This encoder is specifically tailored to accommodate the discrete nature of stratigraphic parameter targets, with each layer incorporating a multihead self-attention module constrained by relative depth, together with a feed-forward network.

Two-Stage Training Strategy

Given the limited availability of core-derived labels and the sparsity of supervision in reservoir parameter prediction tasks, directly performing end-to-end supervised training on core data would result in both the depth–distance-biased attention mechanism and depth positional encoding in DATS being learned solely from the discrete depth distribution of core samples. As core measurements are typically characterized by sampling intervals much larger than those of well-logging data, their depth distribution is inherently sparse and discontinuous. Consequently, depth-aware attention patterns learned directly from such data would be confined to the relationships among discrete core points, with a limited capacity to generalize to the continuous depth sequences of well-logging data. Therefore, the two-stage training strategy is not merely intended to enhance model performance under limited data conditions but rather constitutes a fundamental design principle for enabling DATS to effectively learn and generalize depth-sequence representations.

To this end, the proposed model adopts a two-stage training strategy, whereby self-supervised pretraining on well-logging curves is followed by supervised fine-tuning on core data, effectively decoupling sequence representation learning from target parameter regression. , This design enhances the capability of DATS to learn from the continuous stratigraphic depth sequences.

The model first undergoes Stage A self-supervised learning: building upon the incorporation of stratigraphic priors, absolute depth, and relative depth information, it is pretrained on large volumes of unlabeled well-logging data, essentially following a reconstruction-based self-supervised representation learning paradigm. The primary objective of this stage is to enable the depth–distance-biased attention mechanism and depth positional encoding to establish a coherent understanding of vertical stratigraphic structures under densely and continuously sampled depth distributions. In other words, the model first learns to interpret the continuous subsurface space represented by well-logging curves, capturing prior knowledge such as lithological variations, interlog correlations, and depth-wise proximity relationships, thereby providing an accurate depth-referenced framework for subsequent depth-sequence modeling of discrete core samples.

Subsequently, Stage B-supervised fine-tuning is conducted using labeled core data. During this phase, only a limited subset of modules directly related to the prediction task is unfrozen, including the final normalization layer, the prediction hidden layer, and the output layer. These components are fine-tuned with a relatively small learning rate with the objective of enabling the model to adapt its high-level representations to the specific regression task while preserving the generalizable features learned during pretraining.

From the perspective of the loss function, the overall optimization objective of the two-stage training framework can be expressed as 8 L=λrecLrec+λyLy where Lrec and Ly denote the reconstruction loss in Stage A and the target parameter prediction loss in Stage B, respectively; and λ rec and λ y represent the corresponding weighting coefficients for the two terms.

Application Example

Study Area and Data Set

The study area is located in an oilfield in southeastern Iraq, where the principal hydrocarbon-producing intervals occur within the Oligocene–Miocene Asmari Formation beneath an evaporite sequence. As illustrated in Figure a, the region is characterized by a typical mixed carbonate–clastic platform depositional system. In the northeastern part of the study area, a restricted to semirestricted carbonate platform environment prevails, with relatively gentle topography that is conducive to the accumulation of terrigenous clastic sediments. During phases of restricted deposition, extensive evaporative exposure, together with internal environments such as tidal flats and lagoons, resulted in the vertical coexistence of dolomite and limestone. During certain periods, enhanced transport dynamics of terrigenous clastics or transient humid conditions associated with subtropical monsoonal influences led to the development of subaqueous distributary channels (Figure a), facilitating the inward transport of clastic materials onto the platform and forming interbedded or intercalated sandstones and carbonates. In the early Neogene, a further regional fall in sea level, coupled with increasingly arid climatic conditions, resulted in basin-wide carbonate deposition. ,,

Evaluation Metrics

To rigorously evaluate the predictive performance of the DATS model for reservoir parameters derived from well-logging data, three key metrics are employed: mean absolute error (MAE), root-mean-square error (RMSE), and the coefficient of determination (R 2). , Both MAE and RMSE quantify the discrepancy between predicted and observed values, with smaller values indicating lower prediction error, whereas R 2 reflects the model’s explanatory power with respect to the variance of the target variable, with higher values indicating better goodness of fit. Data Preprocessing

During the self-supervised learning (SSL) stage, to ensure a streamlined, efficient, and scalable experimental workflow, no elaborate feature selection or additional curve-smoothing procedures for noise reduction were performed. Instead, all available variables were incorporated into the DATS model, enabling it to learn hierarchical representations directly from the well-logging data. This design choice reduces reliance on manual preprocessing, mitigates the risk of information loss associated with excessive data treatment, and preserves high-frequency variations that may contain critical geological information, thereby enhancing the model’s generalization capability and adaptability.

All variables incorporated into the model include caliper (CAL), gamma-ray (GR), neutron porosity (NPHI), bulk density (RHOB), compressional slowness (DT), and deep resistivity (RD). The overall preprocessing workflow consists of the following steps: (1) stratigraphic identifiers are numerically encoded so that they can be used as conditional inputs to the model while preserving their depth-index information; (2) invalid values at the top and bottom of logging curves are manually removed, and for short missing segments (fewer than 20 measurements), forward and backward filling is performed within groups defined by well identifier and stratigraphic unit, thereby avoiding information leakage across different wells or stratigraphic intervals (no missing segments are present in the study area); (3) input features, absolute depth, relative depth, and target values are normalized using the RobustScaler method; and (4) sequence samples are constructed using a depth-based sliding-window approach, whereby data from each well and each stratigraphic unit are traversed point by point along the depth axis with a fixed window, and each window includes five neighboring points before and after the central measurement.

The same preprocessing procedure was applied during Stage B-supervised fine-tuning. Notably, in step (4), the depth continuity constraint described in Section is introduced to prevent attention dispersion in sliding-window sequences caused by excessively large depth intervals.

Experimental Design

In this study, DATS is not designed to generate predictions directly as an end-to-end model; rather, it serves as an upstream feature extractor that produces high-level semantic embeddings through multilayer attention mechanisms and nonlinear transformations. These embeddings encode information not directly available from the original well-logging features, including cross-feature interactions, depth-neighborhood integration, and stratigraphic conditioning. They are then supplied to downstream conventional machine learning models, thereby enabling algorithms that are structurally simple, training-stable, and readily interpretable to indirectly acquire the sequence-modeling capacity of deep learning. In this way, a robust framework can be established that remains capable of delivering reliable predictive performance even in scenarios with limited training data, such as logging–core prediction tasks.

The central objective of DATS is to enable accurate prediction of reservoir parameters in uncored wells. In the present study, core permeability prediction is taken as an example, and the test set is defined using a Leave-One-Well-Out (LOWO) strategy: one well is selected as an independent test well, while the remaining 2551 core permeability measurements from the other 23 cored wells are used for training. This strategy provides a realistic evaluation of the model’s cross-well generalization capability under industrial application conditions. Within the training set, hyperparameter optimization is likewise conducted using a group-wise validation strategy, in which the well whose sample size is closest to the median is designated as the validation well, and the remaining wells are used for training. This design prevents adjacent depth points from the same well from appearing simultaneously in both the training and validation subsets, thereby avoiding overestimation of validation performance caused by depth-sequence correlations and associated data leakage. The hyperparameters of DATS are determined through a combination of literature review and grid-search experiments, with the final configuration summarized in Table . Absolute depth encoding and relative depth encoding are each projected into a 64-dimensional space before being combined; the frequency parameters for absolute depth encoding are initialized to correspond to stratigraphic cyclicities on scales of 50–1000 m, whereas those for relative depth encoding are initialized to cover approximately 0.5–5 complete cycles within the interval from 0 to 1, with both sets of parameters subsequently optimized adaptively through gradient descent. With respect to the loss functions, the reconstruction head employs normalized mean squared error (Normalized MSE) to avoid gradient explosion arising from differences in the physical scales of the logging curves, whereas the prediction head adopts the Huber loss (δ = 1.0), which combines the advantages of MSE and MAE and exhibits strong robustness to anomalous core samples or abnormal experimental measurements.

In the downstream feature selection scheme, the optimal subset size of embedding dimensions (emb Top-k) is further determined by searching over candidate values using leave-one-well-out cross-validation (LOWOCV) within the training set, thereby reducing selection bias and enhancing the robustness of downstream conventional machine learning models. Each downstream model is trained and evaluated under three feature input schemes: (i) Raw Only, where only the original well-logging curves are used as input features; (ii) Embedding Only, where only the multidimensional embedding features extracted by DATS are used, with the optimal subset selected via LOWOCV; and (iii) Combined, where the original well-logging features are concatenated with the embedding features, and the most relevant emb Top-k dimensions are likewise selected via LOWOCV to comprehensively assess the complementarity between the two feature types.

It can be observed that mean and attention pooling strategies are not well suited for reservoir parameter prediction tasks based on core samples. Mean pooling is more appropriate for tasks in which features are uniformly distributed and insensitive to positional information, whereas attention pooling is better suited for selectively emphasizing specific positions within a sequence. In contrast, center pooling, which is adopted in DATS, aligns more naturally with the model design: the sliding-window construction of depth-sequential samples is centered on the target position while simultaneously incorporating contextual contributions from neighboring points. From both a modeling perspective and in terms of geological priors, center pooling is therefore more appropriate for DATS. Although RSA pooling is, in principle, applicable to this type of task, the benefits are limited in this framework; since DATS already integrates depth-sequence context and geological prior information, the additional complexity introduced by RSA may yield a negligible improvement or even lead to a slight degradation in predictive performance.

Discussion

Interpretability Analysis of DATS

The preceding sections have demonstrated the strong potential of the DATS framework for reservoir permeability prediction; however, deep-learning methods are often criticized for the opacity of their internal decision-making processes, making it difficult to determine whether the trained model is consistent with fundamental geophysical principles. To address this issue, an interpretability analysis was further conducted on the optimal downstream model, DATS-XGBoost_Combined, using the SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) method.

The SHAP framework originates from the concept of Shapley values in cooperative game theory and quantifies the contribution of each feature by evaluating all possible feature combinations. In deep learning prediction tasks, it provides a theoretically grounded framework for feature attribution. , As shown in Figure a, the SHAP summary beeswarm plot of the top 15 most important features reveals that embedding dimensions dominate the predictive contributions, with the mean absolute SHAP value of emb_7 exceeding those of all original well-logging features. This result suggests that the depth-sequential contextual information extracted by DATS possesses an independent predictive value and captures complementary information beyond the single-point raw logging features. In terms of color distribution, embedding dimensions such as emb_7, emb_27, and emb_47 exhibit pronounced monotonic trends. Taking emb_7 as an example, high feature values correspond to positive SHAP contributions, whereas low feature values correspond to negative contributions, indicating that these embedding dimensions are unlikely to represent random noise from the logging curves but instead reflect stratigraphic depth-sequence patterns that are consistent with geological interpretation.

SHAP-based interpretability analysis of the DATS-XGBoost_Combined model: (a) SHAP beeswarm plot of the top 15 most important features, (b) SHAP dependence plot for DT, and , (c) SHAP dependence plot for NPHI.

Among the raw logging features, DT and NPHI rank as the two most influential parameters, both of which are highly sensitive indicators in reservoir porosity evaluation. According to the Kozeny–Carman relationship, porosity constitutes a prerequisite for reservoir fluid flow pathways, and permeability generally exhibits a positive correlation with porosity, such that larger porosity tends to correspond to higher permeability. These features therefore also play an important role in permeability prediction. This further supports the complementary effect of integrating DATS embedding features with the original well-logging curves. By contrast, the SHAP contributions of GR and CAL are weaker and more dispersed, indicating their relatively low correlation with permeability, consistent with the observations shown in Figure .

As shown in the DT dependence plot in Figure b, DT exhibits a clear physical threshold. When DT is lower than approximately 70 μs/ft, the corresponding SHAP contributions are concentrated in the negative region, and the model tends to predict relatively low permeability; however, no obvious linear positive correlation is observed, indicating that when DT is small, permeability does not continue to decrease with further reductions in DT. In contrast, when DT exceeds approximately 70 μs/ft, the corresponding SHAP contributions are concentrated in the positive region and display a strong linear positive correlation, indicating that permeability tends to increase with increasing DT. This nonlinear pattern is consistent with the petrophysical behavior of carbonate reservoirs in the study area.

As shown in the NPHI dependence plot in Figure c, the corresponding SHAP values generally exhibit a weak positive linear trend: higher NPHI values tend to contribute positively to permeability prediction, whereas lower NPHI values tend to contribute negatively, in agreement with the geological understanding of the reservoir. However, because this trend is not sufficiently strong to determine permeability independently, this result suggests that the DATS framework captures differences in the significance of identical measurements under different stratigraphic contexts through sequential modeling. It also indicates that the joint interpretation of embedding features and encoded depth-sequence information can improve the reliability of the permeability prediction.

SHAP analysis provides a static, posthoc explanation of model predictions. To further elucidate the dynamic decision-making process of DATS along the sequence dimension, the multihead attention matrices within the Transformer encoder are visualized, enabling direct inspection of its sequence-awareness mechanism. , As shown in Figure , the model predictions for a test well are presented, where the logging curves from left to right correspond to CAL, GR, RHOB, NPHI, DT, RD, and PERM. On the basis of the core measurements, the predicted permeability exhibits high accuracy. The colored backgrounds from top to bottom represent four stratigraphic units (A2, A3, B1, and B2). For each central point, four attention heads (H0, H1, H2, and H3) produce corresponding attention weight matrices; considering two sliding windows associated with the PERM prediction curve, a total of eight attention matrices are obtained. In these matrices, rows and columns correspond to query and key positions, respectively, representing depth points that initiate queries and provide contextual information. In essence, within the self-attention mechanism, both the query and key originate from the same sequence window of core samples, and each matrix element reflects the degree to which a query depth references a key depth during prediction.

Model prediction performance and attention matrix visualization.

At the 2930–2935 m interval, the sliding window spans the boundary between the A2 and A3 units, indicating two distinct depositional regimes. All four attention heads exhibit pronounced blue bands in the lower-left and upper-right regions of the matrices, suggesting that samples near the stratigraphic boundary struggle to establish stable dependencies. Samples above the boundary primarily attend to A2, while those below attend to A3, resulting in dispersed attention and reduced focus, which in turn contributes to larger prediction errors at the central core point within this window. In contrast, within the 2970–2975 m interval, where lithological variations are evident and permeability reaches values on the order of thousands of millidarcies, the predicted and observed values show strong agreement. The corresponding attention matrices display a clear diagonal pattern, indicating that DATS assigns stronger attention to both shallower and deeper neighboring samples within the window. Notably, high attention weights are observed in the upper-left region of H0 and the lower-right region of H1, suggesting that these two heads preferentially focus on shallower and deeper features, respectively.

Overall, the multihead attention patterns suggest that DATS can adaptively respond to stratigraphic structures, while also revealing limitations in attention allocation near stratigraphic boundaries, where predictive accuracy is correspondingly reduced.

Cross-Regional Application of DATS

To further evaluate the applicability of DATS across different geological settings, a cross-regional case study was conducted by using data from an oilfield in the Santos Basin along the Atlantic margin. As a typical passive margin basin, this region has undergone multiple phases of subsidence and uplift throughout its geological history, resulting in a complex and heterogeneous sedimentary sequence. The Cretaceous strata, which constitute the principal hydrocarbon-bearing intervals, are predominantly composed of limestone, shale, and sandstone. ,

A total of 1071 core experimental samples from this region were used to validate the DATS framework. Among the evaluated downstream models, ExtraTrees_Combined achieved the best performance (R 2 = 0.8371, RMSE = 0.6418, MAE = 0.4725). However, the overall predictive performance is lower than that observed in the original study area. This decrease can be attributed not only to the limited number of local core samples, but also to the stronger geological heterogeneity of the Santos Basin reservoirs, which weakens the stability of the logging–permeability relationship.

Furthermore, a Leave-One-Well-Out evaluation was conducted, in which the core samples from a single well were used as the test set in each iteration. The corresponding results are illustrated in Figure a–c, which present the prediction performance for three representative wells.

Petrographic data collected from the Santos Basin indicate that the studied interval contains diverse lithofacies, including rudstone, floatstone, mudstone, grainstone, stromatolite, dolomite, spherulitite, and silexite, reflecting strong lithofacies heterogeneity. More importantly, the thin-section examples in Figure show that pronounced permeability variation can occur over an extremely short depth interval. Although the two samples are separated by only approximately 0.3 m and exhibit comparable porosity, their permeability differs markedly. This abrupt change is mainly attributed to differences in pore structure and pore connectivity, including variations in interparticle, intraparticle, moldic, intercrystalline, and fracture-related pores, as well as diagenetic modification caused by local dissolution and cementation. − These observations indicate that permeability in the Santos Basin is controlled not only by porosity but also by pore throat connectivity and small-scale pore structure heterogeneity. Therefore, even when the DATS framework is directly trained and evaluated using Santos Basin data, such strong small-scale vertical heterogeneity may weaken the geological similarity among neighboring depth samples. This reduces the effectiveness of the depth–distance-biased attention mechanism in extracting stable local sequence relationships, thereby contributing to the lower prediction accuracy than that obtained in the original study area.

Conclusion

This study proposes a depth-aware Transformer-based hybrid modeling framework, DATS, specifically designed for prediction tasks in which the training data are derived from core experiments. By coupling the DATS encoder with downstream machine learning models, the framework enables effective extraction and utilization of depth-sequential information from well-logging data at core sampling points.
