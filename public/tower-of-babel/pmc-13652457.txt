Isolated supernumerary nipple along embryonic milk line in an adult male patient successfully treated by surgical excision: a case report

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Supernumerary nipples are the most common congenital anomaly of the mammary ridge, resulting from incomplete regression of the embryonic milk line. Introduction

Supernumerary nipple, or polythelia, is the most common congenital anomaly of the mammary ridge, resulting from incomplete regression of the embryonic milk line during fetal development. The reported prevalence ranges from 0.2% to 5.6% in the general population, with males affected slightly more frequently than females[1,2]. Most lesions occur along the thoracic portion of the milk line and are classified using the Kajava system[3]. The most frequently encountered presentation is isolated nipple formation without associated glandular tissue, designated as Kajava Type VI[4]. The majority of affected individuals remain asymptomatic throughout life and are managed conservatively. Surgical excision is generally reserved for cosmetic concerns, psychological distress, symptomatic lesions, or diagnostic uncertainty. Although generally benign, accessory mammary tissue has a rare but documented potential for malignant transformation, underscoring the importance of examining all excised specimens[5].

While a supernumerary nipple is a well-recognized and frequently reported congenital anomaly, most existing case reports emphasize its epidemiological, anatomical, or oncological features, with limited attention to the psychosocial dimension of the condition. The present case is distinctive in explicitly characterizing the psychosocial burden experienced by the patient and demonstrating its resolution following surgical excision, thereby addressing a psychosocial aspect of the condition that remains underrepresented in the literature. This case report has been prepared in line with the SCARE 2025 checklist and TITAN reporting standards[6,7].

Case presentation

A 19-year-old Nepali male presented to our tertiary care center with concerns regarding an additional nipple on the right side of his chest. The lesion had been present since early childhood and had remained stable in size and appearance. He denied pain, tenderness, discharge, bleeding, ulceration, swelling, cyclical changes during puberty, or preceding trauma. As he grew older, particularly during adolescence, he became increasingly self-conscious about its cosmetic appearance, prompting medical consultation. The patient had no significant past medical or surgical history and was not taking any regular medications. There was no history of congenital anomalies, developmental abnormalities, urinary symptoms, or systemic illness. Family history was negative for supernumerary nipples, breast disorders, congenital malformations, or hereditary syndromes. His birth and developmental history were unremarkable. He had no known drug or food allergies. He was a non-smoker, did not consume alcohol, and denied any history of recreational drug use.

General examination revealed a healthy young man with normal vital signs. Local examination demonstrated a solitary accessory nipple–areolar complex approximately 5 cm inferior and slightly medial to the right nipple–areolar complex, along the embryonic milk line. The lesion measured approximately 5 × 5 mm and appeared as a small, pigmented, well-circumscribed papule with a rudimentary areolar component (Fig. 1). It was non-tender and freely mobile, with no associated breast tissue, palpable mass, induration, ulceration, or inflammation. Examination of the contralateral chest, axillae, and other systems was unremarkable. Based on its characteristic morphology and location, the lesion was clinically diagnosed as a supernumerary nipple. Focused ultrasonography demonstrated the absence of underlying glandular tissue, cystic lesions, or soft-tissue masses. The lesion was classified as Kajava Type VI polythelia. As no features suggested any associated congenital anomalies, further systemic investigations were not performed. Routine preoperative laboratory investigations were within normal limits.

Following a discussion of management options, the patient elected to undergo surgical excision for cosmetic reasons. Under intravenous anesthesia and with aseptic precautions, a simple elliptical excision was performed with preservation of surrounding healthy skin. The wound was closed primarily using layered sutures to optimize cosmesis (Fig. 2). Histopathological examination demonstrated epidermal and dermal structures consistent with a supernumerary nipple, without glandular tissue, dysplasia, or malignancy, confirming Kajava type VI polythelia (Fig. 3). The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was discharged on the same day, and follow-up showed satisfactory wound healing with minimal scarring and no infection, wound dehiscence, or recurrence. He reported high satisfaction with the cosmetic outcome and improved self-confidence following surgery.

Discussion

In this case report, we have discussed a surgically managed isolated supernumerary nipple in a young adult male, highlighting how a seemingly minor congenital anomaly can exert a disproportionate psychosocial burden. Although supernumerary nipples are generally regarded as benign developmental remnants, their visibility during adolescence and early adulthood may adversely affect body image, self-esteem, and quality of life. Our case demonstrates that careful clinical assessment, appropriate imaging, and definitive surgical excision can provide both diagnostic certainty and meaningful psychological benefit.

Supernumerary nipple, also known as polythelia, is a minor congenital malformation characterized by the presence of an additional nipple with or without associated breast tissue. Although generally considered a benign and often incidental finding, its clinical significance, from a surgical perspective, lies in the cosmetic, psychological, symptomatic, and occasionally oncological implications that may necessitate operative intervention[8,9]. The majority of affected individuals remain asymptomatic throughout life, resulting in substantial underreporting and delayed presentation to surgical services.

Most lesions occur along the thoracic portion of the embryonic milk line (extending from the axilla to the groin), although ectopic locations outside the milk line have also been reported[3]. The morphological variants of supernumerary breast tissue are commonly classified using the Kajava classification (Table 2), which categorizes the anomaly into eight types based on the presence of nipple, areola, glandular tissue, or hair[10]. Among these, isolated nipple formation (Type VI polythelia) is the most frequently observed presentation[4]. The decision to pursue operative management is individualized and guided by the nature and severity of the presenting concern. The primary indications for surgical excision reported in the literature include cosmetic concerns, psychological distress, local irritation from clothing, cyclic pain, enlargement during puberty or pregnancy, suspicion of malignancy, and diagnostic uncertainty[11]. Cosmetic dissatisfaction is consistently reported as the most frequent indication for surgery, particularly among adolescents and young adults. In our case, surgical excision was undertaken because of cosmetic dissatisfaction, which is consistent with the most common operative indications reported in previously published cases.

The choice of surgical technique is principally determined by the lesion’s size, depth, and histological composition. For isolated polythelia without underlying glandular tissue, simple elliptical excision with layered primary closure is regarded as the standard approach and is associated with excellent cosmetic and functional results. When accessory glandular tissue is present (polymasty), a more extensive resection may be required to achieve complete excision and prevent postoperative contour deformity[1,11]. In recent years, minimally invasive approaches combining liposuction and limited skin excision have been reported for larger lesions, particularly in cosmetically sensitive regions[12,13]. Similarly, surgical outcomes reported in the literature are generally excellent. Most patients experience complete symptom resolution, high satisfaction rates, and minimal postoperative complications[14].

An important surgical consideration is the possibility of pathological changes within accessory mammary tissue. Ectopic breast cancers account for less than 1% of all breast malignancies, and only a minority arise from supernumerary nipple tissue[5,15]. Cases of invasive ductal carcinoma, ductal carcinoma in situ, and fibroadenoma arising within supernumerary breast tissue have been reported in the literature[5,16]. This potential for malignant transformation constitutes a rationale for surgical excision and routine histopathological examination of all excised specimens, particularly in cases involving enlarging or clinically atypical lesions, or those with demonstrable glandular tissue on imaging or palpation[5,16]. The surgical outcomes reported across the published literature are consistently favorable[11,17,18].

Congenital cosmetic anomalies can meaningfully affect body image, self-esteem, and psychosocial well-being, particularly during adolescence and young adulthood when appearance-related self-consciousness is heightened. This is supported by evidence from other congenital deformities, where surgical correction has produced measurable improvements in disease-specific quality of life, self-esteem, and perceived body image. Similarly, studies of congenital chest wall deformities have found that dissatisfaction with appearance can substantially reduce quality of life, underscoring the need to factor psychosocial impact into surgical decision-making[19,20]. Although formal quality-of-life assessment was not performed in our patient, his reported improvement in confidence and satisfaction following excision mirrors these findings, suggesting that removal of a visible supernumerary nipple–areolar complex can offer psychosocial benefits even without physical symptoms. This supports psychosocial distress as a legitimate, stand-alone indication for surgery. Future studies using validated body-image and quality-of-life tools would help confirm this benefit more rigorously.

Strength and limitation

This case report is strengthened by thorough, explicit documentation of the patient’s psychosocial burden and its resolution following surgery, an aspect that remains largely underrepresented in the existing literature on supernumerary nipples, which has traditionally focused on epidemiological and histopathological features rather than patient-reported psychological and quality-of-life outcomes. However, as a single case, the findings cannot be generalized, and the reported psychosocial benefit reflects subjective patient experience rather than a validated outcome measure, since no formal body-image or quality-of-life assessment tool was used. Follow-up was also relatively short, limiting conclusions about the durability of the psychological benefit and the long-term risk of recurrence. Therefore, larger studies with standardized assessment tools and longer follow-up are necessary to validate these observations.

Conclusion

Supernumerary nipple is a common but frequently underreported congenital anomaly that rarely necessitates surgical intervention. When operative management is indicated, most commonly for cosmetic or psychological reasons, simple elliptical excision is safe, technically straightforward, and associated with excellent outcomes. Histopathological examination of all excised specimens remains mandatory given the rare but documented potential for malignant transformation within accessory mammary tissue.
