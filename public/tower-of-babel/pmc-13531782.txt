Background

Aortic dissection is one of the most life-threatening conditions in cardiac surgery, with an extremely high mortality rate reaching 1–2% during the first 24 h [1]. Malperfusion syndromes significantly increase mortality in this patient cohort [2], particularly coronary malperfusion [3]. Involvement of the left coronary artery in aortic dissection with associated malperfusion is exceedingly rare; according to some authors, it accounts for only 7–9% of all cases of coronary ostial dissection [3]. Management of this condition requires rapid diagnosis and prompt decision-making. The literature describes cases treated using endovascular approaches, such as stenting of the left main coronary artery [4, 5], as well as surgical coronary artery bypass grafting [6]. This case report describes the successful surgical treatment of a patient with acute Stanford type A aortic dissection, Neri type B dissection of the left coronary artery ostium, and acute coronary malperfusion.

Case presentation

Patient K., 58-year-old male, with a history of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and a 43-year smoking history, but no evidence of hypertension, was admitted to the clinic on evening 26/12/2023 at 6:07 PM with a diagnosis of Stanford type A (De Bakey II) acute aortic dissection and core complaint of severe chest pain. At the time of admission, the patient’s medication regimen included metoprolol 25 mg three times daily (TID), rosuvastatin (Rosvator) 20 mg in the evening, telmisartan (Micardis) 20 mg in the morning, omeprazole 40 mg at midday, and spironolactone 25 mg in the morning. Upon admission, the patient’s condition was stable without any sings of myocardial ischemia. Ultrasound examination showed type A aortic dissection with severe aortic valve insufficiency (vena contracta 0,6 cm), end-diastolic volume (EDV) = 156 ml, ejection fraction (EF) = 52%, aortic annular diameter = 27 mm, aortic root diameter = 48 mm, ascending aorta diameter = 54 mm, with no evidence of hemopericardium or malperfusion. There was no evidence of concomitant cardiac pathologies.

In the next few hours, during the preparation for the emergency operation, the patient`s condition progressively deteriorated, severe chest pain appeared and intensified; ECG at 7:21 PM showed ST-segment elevation in leads V3-V6, and troponin I levels raised to 6.74 ng/ml (normal range up to 0.16 ng/ml) and peak level of CK-MB 243 ng/mL (Fig. 1).

EF decreased to 35% involving the anteroapical and interventricular septal wall segments. Urgent coronary angiography (CAG) at 8:12 PM, revealed the extension of the dissection into the left coronary artery (LCA) ostium (Fig. 2).

After CAG the patient was admited to the operating room for surgical correction

A median sternotomy was performed at 9:46 PM. Intraoperatively, a Stanford type A (DeBakey II) aortic dissection was confirmed. The entry tear was located 1 cm above the sinotubular junction (STJ), with the dissection extending proximally toward the LCA and distally to the level 1 cm proximal to the brachiocephalic trunk. Arterial cannulation was established via the left femoral artery and for venous return the two-stage cannula was placed into the right atrium. After aortic crossclamp and incision the LCA ostial dissection Neri type B was revealed. There was no evidence of false lumen thrombosis. Cardioplegic solution (Custodiol) was administered retrogradely via the coronary sinus and partially anterogradely, selectively into the right coronary artery and the LCA after the ostial repair. The LCA ostium and false lumen was carefully examined and layer approximation feasibility was checked which showed high opportunity to repair of the ostium. A meticulous repair was performed using 6 − 0 Prolene sutures to secure the intima and media in an inside-out fashion, reinforced with Teflon pledgets placed 1 cm from the coronary ostium, followed by antegrade cardioplegia delivery and flow assessment. For the brain protection during the revision of the arch, retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP) was selected, taking into account the dynamic changes in symptoms and the suspected extension of the dissection. A decision was made to perform supracoronary ascending aortic replacement after a precise revision of the arch. The distal anastomosis was performed under aortic cross-clamping in Ishimaru Zone 0 (proximmal to the innominate artery) using an Intergard 28 mm prosthesis under mild hypothermia (rectal temperature of 28 °C). Intraoperative inspection revealed no structural pathology of the valve leaflets; the mechanism of aortic insufficiency was solely attributable to commissural detachment, prompting the decision to perform aortic valve resuspension (using pledged mattress sutures placed in an inside-out fasion) without aortic root replacement. The cardiopulmonary bypass (CBP) time was 120 min, the aortic cross-clamping time was 89 min, and the RCP time was 46 s during revision of the aortic arch. Postoperatively, the patient required a dobutamine infusion at 2,4 µg/kg/min, which was gradually reduced to 0,3 µg/kg/min over 3 days.

In the postoperative period on 05/01/2024, a control coronary angiography was performed, confirming successful repair with no compression of LCA or RCA ostia (Fig. 3).

Postoperatively, the patient did not report any complaints of chest pain, and the postoperative ECG showed no signs of myocardial ischemia, (Fig. 3) ejection fraction (EF) raised to = 45% and no evidence of aortic regurgitation. The patient was discharged on the 13th day in satisfactory condition.

On the 8-mounth check-up, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) was performed. The CT scan showed favourable outcome with no signs of ostial compressions and no PANE (proximal anastomotic new entry) or DANE (distal anastomotic new entry) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Acute aortic dissection is a life-threatening condition that may be complicated by malperfusion syndrome. Organ-specific malperfusion caused by compression or thrombosis of major arterial branches occurs in 8–14% of cases, whereas coronary malperfusion is among the complications associated with the highest mortality, reaching up to 26% when present [7]. According to some authors, mortality in patients with myocardial infarction caused by extension of the dissection into the coronary ostia may be as high as 69% [8].

The first classification of coronary ostial involvement was proposed by Neri et al. [9], who described type A lesions as ostial dissection, type B as dissection with extension into a false lumen of the coronary artery, and type C as complete avulsion of the coronary ostium. Tong et al. later introduced an additional category, termed COIT, defined as an intimal tear of the coronary ostium without complete avulsion [3].

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is the gold standard for the diagnosis of acute aortic dissection. When combined with electrocardiographic gating, CT also allows assessment of the coronary ostia and may raise suspicion of their involvement. In relatively rare cases, coronary angiography is performed in patients with acute aortic dissection to enable precise evaluation of the coronary circulation.

Clinical manifestations of angina pectoris and ST-segment elevation on electrocardiography are often the first signs of coronary malperfusion [10–13]. This presentation represents one of the factors contributing to misdiagnosis of acute aortic dissection in up to 80% of cases [14]. Nevertheless, cases of patients with chronic aortic dissection and occlusion of the coronary ostium have been reported in the literature [15].

Surgical management of coronary ostial lesions largely depends on the extent and type of involvement. Kawahito [16] recommended coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) for type B and type C lesions, whereas Tong et al. advocated ostial reconstruction with preservation of the native orifice using a pericardial patch or autologous vein graft [3]. There is general agreement regarding the indication for CABG in type C coronary ostial dissection. However, approaches to the management of type B and COIT lesions remain controversial. In such cases, the decision is often guided by the adequacy of ostial perfusion, with CABG recommended in the presence of impaired flow [17].

In addition, some authors have reported the use of coronary stenting as an initial strategy to achieve myocardial reperfusion, followed by definitive surgical repair [5, 18].

In the present case, the patient exhibited a type B coronary ostial lesion according to the Neri classification, with up to 90% luminal occlusion of the left coronary artery. The involvement of the LCA may be partially attributed to its close proximity to the primary entry tear. Clinical signs of myocardial ischaemia were present, including elevated cardiac enzyme levels and characteristic changes on electrocardiography and transthoracic echocardiography. Ostial reconstruction was successfully performed, largely owing to rapid and accurate diagnosis. Although mortality associated with left coronary ostial involvement is higher, timely diagnosis and prompt surgical intervention remain the key determinants of outcome.

Conclusions

Performing suture repair for LCA ostial dissection Type B according to Neri, with accompanying coronary malperfusion, may be a feasible option for correcting this pathology in selected cases. Timely diagnosis of myocardial ischemia and prompt surgical correction of aortic dissection can lead to satisfactory postoperative outcomes in these high-risk patients.
