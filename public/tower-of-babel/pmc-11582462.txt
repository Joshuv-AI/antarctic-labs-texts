Does the timing of blood pressure medication really matter?
Authors: Yuan-Yuan Kang, Ji-Guang Wang
Published: 2024

The results of two recent studies on the timing of blood pressure medication, namely, the BedMed and BedMed-Frail trials, were presented at the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Congress 2024. These two studies consistently showed that bedtime dosing, compared with morning dosing, of antihypertensive drugs did not reduce the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events. The presenter, Dr Garrison Scott, emphasized that according to the results of these two trials, patients should be advised that they take their regular antihypertensive medications at a convenient time that minimises any undesirable effects.

The BedMed study was a prospective randomised, open-label, blinded end-point trial in five Canadian provinces from March 2017 to Dec 2023, which included from primary care 3357 patients who were treated with at least one blood pressure lowering medication and free from glaucoma [1]. Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to take all of their usual antihypertensive medications either in the morning or evening. Primary outcome was a composite of all-cause death or hospitalisations for myocardial infarction/acute-coronary syndrome, stroke or congestive heart failure. Results showed that median age at study entry was 67.0 years (Interquartile range 60–73) and 56 % were female. After a median of 4.6 years follow-up, the primary endpoint events occurred in 163 (9.7 %) participants assigned to bedtime treatment (2.3 events per 100 patient-years) and 173 (10.3 %) assigned to morning treatment (2.44 events per 100 patient-years; adjusted hazard ratio 0.96 [95 % CI 0.77–1.19]; p = 0.70). No safety concerns were identified.

The design of BedMed-Frail trial was similar to that of the BedMed trial [2]. But the participants were from the continuing care residents recruited from 17 residential care homes in Alberta, Canada from May 2020 to Feb 2024. In the BedMed-Frail trial, the 776 participants had a mean age of 88 years and included 72 % women. After a median of 1.1 years follow-up, the primary endpoint events occurred in 160 (40.6 %) participants assigned to bedtime treatment (29.4 events per 100 patient-years) and 160 (41.9 %) assigned to morning treatment (31.5 events per 100 patient-years; adjusted hazard ratio 0.88 [95 % CI 0.71–1.11]; p = 0.28). There was no difference in any other than first occurrence of all cause unplanned hospitalization/emergency department visits.

In addition, a meta-analysis of trials that compared timing of antihypertensive medication and included individual-patient data of the BedMed and BedMed-Frail trials was also presented at the ESC 2024 and showed that there was no difference between morning and evening dosing of antihypertensive drugs with regard to safety and efficacy.

While the results of these studies are quite consistent, whether time-specific, often masked, hypertension, such as morning and nocturnal hypertension, would require timed dosing remains under investigation. With the increasing use of out-of-office blood pressure measurement, both ambulatory and home blood pressure monitoring, these time-specific forms of hypertension would be increasingly diagnosed. In fact, these special forms of hypertension were associated with increased risks of cardiovascular events, and might require timed dosing of antihypertensive drugs [3,4]. Future studies should address the issue of the timing of antihypertensive medication in these patients with either morning or nocturnal hypertension.
