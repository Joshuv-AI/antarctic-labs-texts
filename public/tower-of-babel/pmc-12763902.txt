Cost-effectiveness analysis of gene-expression-based prognostic tests for patients with early-stage breast cancer in South Korea
Authors: Daewon Kang, Jaeok Lim, Lahn Kim, Sang-Eun Choi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) is a common treatment modality for patients with breast cancer; however, not all patients benefit from this therapy. To identify these patients, gene expression profiling tests, such as Oncotype DX (ODX), have been developed and are widely used. However, performance differences based on ethnicity have been reported for ODX. Therefore, it is necessary to compare the performance and cost-effectiveness of these tests against those developed for Asian patients, such as the GenesWell Breast Cancer Test (BCT). This study evaluated the cost-effectiveness of using ODX, BCT, and no testing to determine the need for ACT in patients with breast cancer by comparing each option.MethodsWe used decision tree and Markov models over a ten-year horizon to forecast ODX and BCT cost-effectiveness. The results are presented as the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio from the perspective of the payer of Korean National Health Insurance. Uncertainty was assessed using scenario, one-way, and probabilistic sensitivity analyses.ResultsCompared to the no-test alternative, BCT gained 0.0977 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) with savings of US$1,091, while the ODX gained 0.0906 QALYs with savings of US$544, both demonstrating dominance over the no-test. When comparing BCT to ODX, the outcomes were similar, with the BCT strategy achieving a cost savings of US$547 and an additional gain of 0.0071 QALYs.ConclusionThe comparison of gene profiling tests, ODX and BCT, with the no-testing strategy demonstrated comparable levels of QALY improvement and cost savings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-025-00679-9.Keywords: Gene expression profile test, Adjuvant chemotherapy, Cost-effectiveness analysis, Early-stage breast cancer, Reimbursement, Decision making, South Korea

Background

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in women, with 2.3 million new cases reported in 2020, and its burden is continuously increasing [1]. The economic burden of breast cancer has increased over the last two decades. Specifically, the total cost of medical coverage for breast cancer rose from US$542 million to US$ 736 million between 2016 and 2018, equating to a compound annual growth rate of 16.5% [2].

The most common subtype of breast cancer in South Korea is hormone receptor-positive (HR+), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-negative (HER2-) breast cancer [3]. According to data from the Korean Breast Cancer Society registry, 63.1% of patients diagnosed with breast cancer in 2019 were diagnosed with the HR+/HER2- subtype [4], for which surgery, endocrine therapy, and chemotherapy are the most common treatments. Notably, the choice of treatment significantly affects patients’ quality of life and economic burden. In particular, chemotherapy may either contribute to saving costs and improving the quality of life or increase one’s burden [5], depending on the regimen, timing, and patient’s condition [6, 7]. Moreover, many studies suggest that adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) may not be necessary, depending on the stage and type of cancer. Therefore, decision-making based on clinical evidence has been emphasized [8].

Since the 2000s, diagnostic devices have been increasingly used to determine the need for ACT in patients with breast cancer to reduce the patient burden and minimize unnecessary therapy [9–11]. Such evaluations are based on gene expression profile tests, with Oncotype DX (ODX) and MammaPrint as representative options [12]. Thus, the United States and several European Union countries have incorporated gene expression profile tests into their practice guidelines, making it a standard step for supporting ACT decision making [13, 14]. In the 2010s, ODX was reimbursed in the US, UK, Switzerland, Ireland, Greece, France, Germany, and Spain. As of September 2023, ODX is reimbursed in Japan to enhance patient accessibility [15–17].

Gene expression profile tests offer financial and clinical benefits to patients. They help to reduce unnecessary aggressive treatments, such as ACT, for low-risk cancer patients, thereby minimizing potential toxicity and side effects. However, concerns have been raised regarding the applicability of existing test results and evidence to the Asian genome [18, 19]. To address these concerns, the GenesWell Breast Cancer Test (BCT) was developed as an Asian genome-focused diagnostic tool. This test aids in decision making regarding ACT for patients with early stage HR+/HER2-and pN0–N1 (0 or 1 node involvement) breast cancer. The Ministry of Food and Safety (MFDS) granted approval for the BCT in 2016.

While ODX categorizes patients into high-, intermediate-, and low-risk groups based on 21 gene expressions and varies its scoring criteria depending on menopausal status, BCT classifies patients solely into high- and low-risk groups. Furthermore, BCT evaluates gene expression along with clinicopathological factors such as tumor size, lymph node status, and histologic grade [20].

The Korean Breast Cancer Society’s guidelines for breast cancer treatment [21] strongly advocate for using gene expression profile tests to guide decisions about the necessity of ACT. Among these tests, ODX is the most frequently employed in major hospitals throughout Korea. Despite this, the MFDS has not granted approval for ODX, and the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) in Korea currently does not offer coverage for either ODX or BCT. A cost-effectiveness analysis of ODX and BCT will provide important insights into whether expanding accessibility to more individuals is desirable.

To this end, we conducted a cost-effectiveness analysis comparing the three most common approaches for early-stage HR+/HER2- breast cancer treatment decision-making in South Korea: BCT, ODX, and no testing (hereinafter the “no-test alternative”). Our study assessed the economic and health outcomes associated with each approach, focusing on their impact on the use of ACT, quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), and overall healthcare costs. By analyzing the cost-effectiveness of BCT and ODX in comparison to no-test alternatives, we sought to generate evidence to support their adoption in clinical practice and their potential inclusion in the national health insurance system.

Methods

Overview of the analysis

To assess the impact of treatment decisions based on BCT, ODX, and no-test alternatives, we used data from Kwon’s study comparing BCT and ODX results in Korean breast cancer patients [22]. This study evaluated the risk classification of ODX and BCT for patients who utilized ODX in their treatment decision-making process. We calculated the transition probability of distant recurrence (DR) for both high- and low-risk groups using BCT, drawing from this dataset. The no-test alternative was included because the NHIS does not cover any of the tests.

We created an integrated model combining a decision tree with a Markov model to simulate a cohort of 1,000 breast cancer patients, and implemented it using Microsoft Excel ®. The decision tree illustrated the proportion of patients receiving ACT based on the treatment decisions guided by each test and the absence of testing, whereas the Markov model simulated the progression to DR and death over a 10-year period following surgery.

The results of the analysis are presented as the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), which was calculated based on the incremental cost per QALY. All QALYs and costs were discounted at a rate of 4.5% per year [23]. The analysis was conducted from the perspective of the Korean NHIS. To enhance the robustness of the results, we performed scenario analysis using different sources for the clinical outcomes of BCT. Uncertainty was analyzed for each transition probability, utility, and cost using one-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses. We defined willingness to pay (WTP) as Korea’s per-capita GDP of US$33,000 and judged interventions with a lower ICER as cost-effective. Model structure

Figure 1 illustrates the decision tree employed to determine ACT treatment strategies, based explicitly on the assumption that each patient undergoes one of the testing options—ODX, BCT, or no test. The model assumes that decisions regarding the administration of ACT are made on the basis of the results from the respective test or the absence thereof. According to the latest National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines [14], the ODX test advises against ACT for postmenopausal and premenopausal patients with lymph node involvement with a recurrence score (RS) of 25 or below, whereas it recommends ACT for those with an RS of 26 or above. For premenopausal patients with no lymph node involvement, an RS of 15 or lower would classify them as low-risk, and ACT would not be performed, whereas an RS of 26 or higher would classify them as high-risk. For patients with an RS of 16–25, the administration of ACT was determined at the discretion of the treating clinician. Figure 1 depicts this group as an intermediate group within the ODX alternative.

In the BCT alternative, patients with a score < 4 were classified as low-risk and assumed not to receive ACT. In contrast, those with a BCT score of 4 or higher were classified as high-risk and were assumed to receive ACT. Figure 1 presents the decision tree for treatment alternatives:

The Markov model consists of three health states: DR-free (DRF), DR, and death. A simulation was run for a total duration of 10 years, with each cycle representing one year. A 10-year time horizon was selected for the analysis, as the primary objective of the BCT is to assess the risk of distant recurrence over a 10-year period. The DRF state is defined as the absence of DR and death, and transitions to either DR or death can occur. The DR state represents the occurrence of secondary cancer outside the breast and can transition to death. It was assumed that the transitions from DR to DRF did not occur. Comparison of gene profiling test results

An analysis was conducted using the data of 759 patients from Kwon’s study [22] for whom follow-up data were available. The original data from Kwon’s study were collected from samples obtained between 2010 and 2017 at five tertiary education hospitals in South Korea (Samsung Medical Center, Asan Medical Center, Korea University Guro Hospital, Gangnam Severance Hospital in Seoul, and the National Cancer Institute in Gyeonggido). For the 759 patients, the ODX results were used to guide treatment strategies, and these patients were followed up for an average of 7 years. Subsequently, the results for the same sample were obtained using BCT. Patient data included age, tumor size, lymph node status, BCT score, RS, and ACT use.

When the patients were classified using the BCT, 239 (31.5%) were categorized as high-risk and 520 (68.5%) as low-risk. In contrast, when the ODX test was used, 114 (15.0%) patients were categorized as high risk, 238 (31.4%) patients were categorized as intermediate risk, and 407 (53.6%) as low-risk. Among them, 70 patients (9.2%) were classified as high-risk by both tests, whereas 316 (41.6%) were classified as low-risk by both tests, with a concordance rate of 50.8%. Patient categorization results were incorporated into the BCT and ODX alternatives within the model. Table 2 presents patient characteristics.

Clinical input and transition probabilities

In the no-test alternative, the transition probability was derived from Kim [24]. Kim’s study assessed treatment outcomes in Korean breast cancer patients by molecular subtype and presented the results via Kaplan-Meier survival curves. We digitized these curves to extract the 10-year distant recurrence-free survival rates for HER2-/HR + patients, which were then used to calculate transition probabilities. The study cohort included both patients who received ACT and those who did not. For the no-test alternative, uniform transition probability from DRF to DR was applied. The probability of receiving ACT in the no-test scenario was sourced from Lee [25].

The base case transition probability from DRF to DR within each risk group for the ODX and BCT was calculated using patient information directly from Kwon’s study [22]. When assigning patients to each branch within the ODX alternative, factors such as RS score, patient age, and node status were considered, as recommended by the NCCN guidelines and actual ACT usage. To estimate transition probabilities, the low-risk group analysis included 592 low-risk patients who did not receive ACT, excluding 53 low-risk patients who underwent ACT since treatment decisions are assumed to be based on test results. Similarly, calculations for the high-risk group included 96 high-risk patients who received ACT, excluding 18 high-risk patients who did not receive ACT. The model incorporated the proportion of patients in the intermediate group who underwent ACT compared to those who did not. To estimate transition probabilities for the groups defined by BCT, we utilized survival data from two cohorts: 73 high-risk patients who received ACT and 444 low-risk patients who did not undergo ACT. We excluded 166 high-risk patients who did not undergo ACT and 76 low-risk patients who received ACT (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Mortality rates for the DRF and DR states were determined using data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program (SEER) of the National Cancer Institute [26]. These data are from women diagnosed with HR+/HER2- breast cancer, specifically focusing on Asians and Pacific Islanders, while excluding Hispanics. The one-year transition probabilities for DR and death were calculated from each data source. Following this, we determined the weighted probabilities of mortality transitions using data from the SEER. The weighting factor applied was the proportion of patients who were under 50 years old.

All transition probabilities were derived by converting survival rates in each state to one-year transition probabilities, using a standard formula that assumes a constant hazard rate over time (Table 3).

Model validation

Model validation was performed by comparing the modeled distant recurrence-free survival (DRFS) curve with the observed DRFS derived from the cohort utilized in Kwon’s study. Agreement between predicted and observed survival was assessed both visually, via graphical overlay, and quantitatively, through calculation of the root mean squared error (RMSE) [27, 28]. DRFS data from all 759 patients in the reference cohort were incorporated into the validation procedure.

Costs and utility input

The analysis considered only direct medical costs, including those for ACT, surgery, follow-up care, treatment for DR, and diagnostic tests. All medical costs, excluding non-reimbursed diagnostic test costs, were extracted by analyzing the public dataset of breast cancer stages provided by the Korea Clinical Data Utilization Network for Research Excellence (K-CURE) [29]. It is a cohort data of 16,870 breast cancer patients treated in Korea from 2012 to 2022, including claims data and clinical information, such as genotype, lymph node status, and cancer stage. The average annual treatment costs for patients with HR+/HER2- breast cancer who did not have DR, excluding adjuvant chemotherapy, were calculated up to the 10th year after breast cancer diagnosis. The average annual treatment cost for DR was calculated up to the 10th year after the first occurrence of DR. ACT costs were calculated by summing medical claim fees for chemotherapy drugs commonly used in breast cancer ACT—including doxorubicin, epirubicin, cyclophosphamide, 5FU, paclitaxel, carboplatin, docetaxel, vinorelbine, capecitabine, and gemcitabine—administered within 180 days after surgery. The cost calculation is based on 2019 costs because it is the most recent year that new breast cancer diagnosis occurred within the data, and all costs were adjusted to 2024 using the healthcare prices index. The 2024 average exchange rate was applied to convert KRW to US dollars (1 USD = 1,366.63 KRW).

Patients receiving ACT incur the associated costs only once (in the first year), since most ACT for breast cancer is performed within 6 months. Similarly, the BCT and ODX costs, which include the cost of diagnostic kits, sample transportation, and result analysis, were applied once (in the first year).

The utility values for patients were sourced from Korean studies to accurately represent the Korean healthcare system and societal health preferences [23, 30]. The utility for DFR and the disutility associated with chemotherapy were obtained from Kim [31] while the utility for DR was taken from a report by the Health Insurance Review & Assessment Service (HIRA) [32]. The utility decrement related to ACT was derived by comparing the utility values of patients not receiving chemotherapy to those receiving chemotherapy at the time of the utility survey. This decrement is applied once for individuals who underwent ACT, as the discomfort associated with ACT persists throughout the treatment, which lasts less than a year.

Sensitivity analysis

An analysis under a different scenario was performed because Kwon’s study [22] only reflects the clinical outcomes of ODX use. Given the lack of follow-up information for patients who used BCT to guide ACT usage in this situation, we implemented a relative risk reduction (RRR) approach to baseline risk [10, 33, 34].The baseline risk of DR for the high- and low-risk groups categorized by BCT was derived from Fujiki et al. [35], who conducted a survival analysis of Korean and Japanese patients with early stage HER2-/HR + breast cancer who had not received ACT. The Korean data from Fujiki’s study were selected as the baseline risk because of their close clinical similarity to Kwon’s study [22]. The effectiveness of ACT in reducing the risk of DR in high-risk pN0 patients was derived from Kwon et al. ‘s study [36]. For patients with pN1 high-risk cancer, the risk reduction rate of ACT was calculated using data from the Early Breast Cancer Trialists’ Collaborative Group (EBCTCG) report on general early–stage breast cancer patients [37], as no previous studies have examined the benefits of chemotherapy for patients with pN1 status across BCT risk groups. The transition probability for the high-risk group was determined by calculating the weighted average of the probabilities for patients with and without lymph node invasion. The other variables remained constant in this scenario. The clinical characteristics of the data sources used in this study are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

One-way and probabilistic sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the impact of uncertainty. One-way sensitivity analyses were conducted to compare ODX with no test and BCT with no test. Transition probabilities, treatment costs, the share of patients receiving ACT in each strategy, and the discount rate were varied to 80–120% of base-case values. Utilities for DF and DR, the disutility of ACT, and the cost of ACT were varied across their 95% confidence intervals.

Subsequently, in the probabilistic sensitivity analysis, 1,000 simulations were performed, where variations were assumed in all transition probabilities, costs, and utility values. The results are presented as scatterplots depicting the incremental cost and incremental QALYs for the use of BCT and ODX compared to the no-test alternative. We used Microsoft Excel ® to conduct sensitivity analysis and R studio to create figures).

Base case analysis

In a 10-year simulation of a 1,000-patient cohort, the BCT and ODX tests led to reductions of 111 and 230 adjuvant chemotherapy treatments, respectively, compared to the no-test option, while also preventing 22 and 19 DR and 26 and 24 deaths, respectively.

According to the base case analysis, the costs incurred over the 10-year period per patient were US$ 41,268.68 for the no-test alternative, US$ 40,177.44 for BCT, and US$ 40,724.73 for ODX. The QALYs for the no test, BCT, and ODX groups were 8.0046, 8.1023, and 8.0952, respectively.

When comparing BCT with the no-test alternative, the incremental cost was US$ -1,091.24, with an incremental QALY of 0.0977 per patient, resulting in an ICER of US$ -11,167.05 per QALY. Thus, BCT has been shown to be the dominant strategy. Similarly, when comparing ODX with the no-test alternative, the incremental cost was US$-543.95, with an incremental QALY of 0.0906, resulting in an ICER of US$ -6,003.88. Hence, ODX also appeared to be a dominant strategy over the no-test alternative. When comparing BCT with ODX, BCT saved US$ 547.29 and gained an additional 0.0071 QALYs. Discussion

Results of analysis

In this study’s base case analysis, which was conducted over a 10-year period using the Markov model, the BCT and ODX were found to be dominant compared to the no-test alternative. When comparing BCT with ODX, the former provided a marginally higher QALY at a lower cost. However, PSA revealed substantially overlapping outcomes between the two approaches.

The comparison between the ODX and no-test alternatives can be evaluated by considering previous research findings. For example, Holt [38] determined the ICER in patients with an average age of 60.55 years to be US$ 9,971. In the same study, sensitivity analysis indicated that ICER increased rapidly with age. In the present study, the mean age of the patients was relatively low at 48 years, which likely contributed to the lower ICER compared with other studies. This suggests that the data used in this study are well suited for analyses within the Korean population, as Asian patients with breast cancer tend to be diagnosed at a younger age [4].

Additionally, this study’s consideration of side effects distinguishes it from that of other studies. Unlike several previous ODX cost-effectiveness studies, this study did not include acute myeloid leukemia (AML) as a health state in the Markov model. This decision was based on reports of low probability of AML occurrence due to ACT in South Korea. Berdunov calculated the AML incidence rate to be 0.62%, while Curtit calculated the 6-month AML incidence rate to be 0.28%, and these rates were applied in both studies’ ODX cost-effectiveness models [39, 40]. Meanwhile, a Korean study calculated 10-year AML incidence rates of 0.221% for those using anthracycline and 0.122% for non-anthracycline users, which were significantly lower than those reported in other studies [41]. Therefore, the utility decrement due to AML side effects was assumed to be included in the overall utility decrement due to the ACT.

One potential factor contributing to the lower QALYs observed for ODX is racial differences. Most participants in leading ODX clinical trials, such as RxPONDER [42] and TAILORx [43], which are often used in cost-effectiveness studies, are Caucasian. However, studies have demonstrated that the effectiveness of ODX can differ across races [34, 44–48]. The benefits of ACT, as validated by BCT especially for Asian patients, support its potential use in clinical practice in South Korea [48].

According to this study’s one-way sensitivity analysis that compared the tests with the no-test alternative, the number of patients receiving ACT in the no-test scenario significantly influenced the ICER. This finding highlights the importance of the tests’ role in avoiding unnecessary ACT, which can be achieved when the diagnostic tool has a high specificity. The specificity was found to be 85.64% and 69.08% for ODX and BCT, respectively, indicating an advantage of ODX. However, BCT resulted in higher QALY gains than ODX, even though it was associated with greater disutility due to increased ACT utilization. This finding is attributed to the higher sensitivity of BCT in identifying high-risk individuals who would benefit from ACT than that of ODX (50.0% vs. 32.14%). Supporting this, Ahn and colleagues analyzed data from Kwon’s study and reported that within the subgroup of patients classified as low risk by ODX who did not receive ACT, patient stratification by BCT showed statistically significant differences in both recurrence-free survival and distant recurrence-free survival, corroborating our findings [49]. Moreover, the lower cost of BCT diagnostics compared with ODX appears to be attributable to the lower cost of the BCT.

Data and the model

Kwon, Lee, Fujiki’s studies [22, 25, 35] and the SEER database were chosen as the primary data sources for this study because they have focused on Asian populations. It is noteworthy that the Kwon’s study, used as the source of clinical outcomes for the ODX, shows a lower risk of DR compared to other studies that include only pN0 patients, such as those by Sparano [41]. This underscores the importance of using data sources with comparable patient characteristics when assessing the clinical outcomes. The SEER database allowed this study to perform a detailed extraction of age, race, and other relevant characteristics for patients with HR+/HER2 − subtype, facilitating a robust analysis of mortality rates.

The costs input into the model were derived through an analysis of claims data provided by the K-CURE. A notable strength of this approach is that it calculates the actual medical expenses incurred by breast cancer patients using representative data. The observed expenditure pattern, which is high during the first year and subsequently decreases to approximately 20–30% of the initial year’s expenses from the second year, is consistent with the findings of a previous study on the financial burden of cancer conducted in South Korea [50]. Furthermore, the costs associated with cancer treatment and ACT in the first year showed an increasing trend (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). Considering the continuous upward trend in treatment and ACT costs, the use of gene profiling technology is anticipated to result in more significant cost-saving benefits in the future.

In addition to the exclusion of AML from the Markov model, the omission of locoregional recurrence as a health state warrants further discussion. In breast cancer, locoregional recurrence can occur prior to DR. Various studies that have conducted economic evaluations of breast cancer therapies or screening have reflected this by including four health states in their Markov models [51, 52]. However, in this study and other economic evaluations of prognostic diagnostic devices, Markov models often progressed directly to DR and death without including locoregional recurrence [40, 53, 54]. This approach is employed because prognostic diagnostic tests mainly aim to predict distant recurrence, which has a far greater influence on clinical outcomes and healthcare costs than locoregional recurrence. Including locoregional recurrence as a health state could result in biased outcomes because of insufficient robust data that links locoregional recurrence with test results, and it may also make the model’s interpretation more complex [12, 20].

For the scenario analysis, we obtained RRR estimates from supplementary studies. The RRR applied to the BCT high-risk pN1 group, based on the EBCTCG report, inevitably included patients who would have been categorized as low risk under BCT criteria. This overlap may lead to an underestimation of ACT’s effect in the pN1 group, making the scenario analysis less favorable for BCT.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, since prospectively validated outcome data for the BCT had not yet been reported, we relied on Kwon’s study, which did not incorporate the clinical impact of the BCT. Using data from Kwon’s study may introduce selection bias, as the same cohort could comprise different subsets of patients who received or did not receive ACT in the absence of ODX, potentially resulting in divergent clinical outcomes. To mitigate selection bias, we conducted a scenario analysis in which RRR was applied to baseline risk, derived by using BCT to classify risk groups among patients whose ACT decisions had been made without gene profiling tests at the time of treatment.

Although the results of the scenario and sensitivity analyses aligned with those of the base-case analysis, careful interpretation and generalization are warranted given the limitations of the data. In particular, while it is reasonable that gene-profiling tests could reduce ACT use, lower costs, and yield greater QALYs than no testing, the QALY gains and costs for BCT and ODX were very similar, underscoring the need for confirmation using real-world data.

Second, this study did not address additional values of prognostics. Prognostic devices that predict the risk of DR can significantly enhance patients’ confidence in their treatment strategies and alleviate anxiety. Several studies have demonstrated that prognostic devices can alleviate anxiety [55–58]. These considerations may offer an advantage over the no-test alternatives. Since the anxiety-reducing effects can be assumed to be comparable between ODX and BCT, this study not separately accounting for these effects need not be viewed as a major limitation.

Third, this study did not consider the time required to obtain test results. The timing of ACT has long been controversial. Some studies have reported that performing ACT within 4–6 weeks after surgery is critical for improving overall survival (OS) [6, 7], whereas others have suggested that ACT performed up to 12 weeks after surgery does not significantly affect OS [59, 60]. Furthermore, Yu [61] reported that ACT performed within 8 weeks after surgery did not significantly affect the OS or disease-free survival of patients with HR + and HER2- breast cancer. According to gene profiling tests such as ODX and BCT, approximately 70–85% of these patients do not benefit from ACT, which may explain why the timing of ACT does not significantly affect outcomes in this group. Therefore, future research should investigate whether delaying ACT impacts patients predicted to benefit from the treatment based on gene profiling tests.

If the timing of ACT proves to be crucial for the high-risk HER2-/HR2 + group, promptly receiving the test results would be essential. For the ODX test, the usual turnaround time is 2–3 weeks, as the sample must be sent to the United States for analysis. Moreover, if the sample is unsuitable and requires retesting, the process can be extended to 4–6 weeks post-surgery. In contrast, the BCT results were obtained more rapidly because the analysis was conducted locally in South Korea.

Although the importance of reducing patient anxiety and determining the optimal timing for initiating ACT in high-risk patients was not considered, both ODX and BCT emerged as dominant alternatives compared with no testing.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the economic and health outcomes of patients for employing two genomic tests, BCT and ODX, and compared them with the no-test alternative for managing patients with breast cancer over a 10-year period. The base case analysis revealed that the BCT and ODX save costs and provide better health outcomes in terms of QALY than the no-test alternative. Furthermore, although the probabilistic sensitivity analysis indicates some uncertainty, comparative evidence shows that BCT and ODX yield similar QALY improvements and comparable cost savings. To overcome the limitations of this study, a long-term prospective cohort study utilizing BCT is necessary to provide real-world evidence. Moreover, exploring how the timing of ACT after surgery influences disease-free survival and OS in patients identified as likely to benefit from ACT through gene profiling tests is imperative. Such research could offer critical insights for the optimization of treatment strategies to enhance patient outcomes while providing foundational data to be used for making health insurance coverage decisions in Korea.
