Optimal information deletion and Bayes’ theorem
Hans Montcho, Håvard Rue

Abstract
In this same journal, Arnold Zellner published a seminal paper on Bayes’ theorem as an optimal information processing rule. This result led to the variational formulation of Bayes’ theorem, which is the central idea in generalized variational inference. Almost 40 years later, we revisit these ideas, but from the perspective of information deletion. We investigate rules which update a posterior distribution into an antedata distribution when a portion of data is removed. In such context, a rule which does not destroy or create information is called the optimal information deletion rule and we prove that it coincides with the traditional use of Bayes’ theorem.



Keywords:
Information theory, Bayesian unlearning.

1
Introduction

In
1988
, Arnold Zellner published a paper entitled
Optimal information processing and Bayes’ theorem
. In that seminal paper
(
Zellner 1988
)
, he proved from first principles that Bayes’ theorem, more than the simple application of conditional probability rules, is the optimal way to update a prior information into a posterior information in light of new data. This result led to the variational formulation of Bayes’ theorem, which has recently seen a resurgence and is the central idea in generalized variational inference
(
Bissiri et al. 2016
,
Knoblauch et al. 2022
)
.

Almost 40 years later, we revisit these ideas, but from the perspective of data removal. Although the original motivation for this work is cross validation in statistics or data deletion in computer science, a general framework of information deletion is used to investigate a rule which updates a posterior distribution into an
antedata distribution
when a subset of data is removed. In such context, a rule which does not destroy or create information is called the
optimal information deletion rule
and coincides with the traditional use of Bayes’ theorem. In this work, we prove this new result with the same variational calculus’ formalism used by Zellner.

The structure of this work is fully inspired by Zellner’s paper; and any similarity is not just a coincidence, but intentionally by design. Therefore, we organize it as follows: in section
2
, we briefly review the ideas in
Zellner 1988
, then in section
3
we define the data removal concept, formalize it as an information deletion problem, and derive the functional to minimize in order to conserve information. In section
4
, we obtain the minimum of the previous functional, and in section
5
, we prove that this minimum coincides with the solution obtained via Bayes’ theorem. Finally, in section
6
we discuss some implications of these results.

2
Optimal information processing rule

Zellner started by hypothesizing a parametric statistical model
\pi(y|\theta)
indexed by
\theta\in\Theta
, for given data
y
, he then assumed a distribution
\pi(\theta|I)
for
\theta
based on prior information
I
, and together the prior and likelihood represent the input information that needs to be processed. The outputs are given by what he called the
postdata
distribution
q(\theta|y)
, and the probability distribution for the data
\displaystyle\pi(y|I)=\int\pi(y|\theta)\pi(\theta|I)d\theta
. An information processing rule (IPR) is a procedure that transforms input into output information, and the optimal IPR is one that does not destroy or add some nonexistent information. We depict the IPR in figure

An important remark is the difference in terminology, as Zellner did not name
q(\theta|y)
, the posterior distribution. In fact, one of the most important results from the paper is that the most efficient IPR, i.e. the best
q^{*}(\theta|y)
is exactly the posterior distribution obtained via Bayes’ theorem
q^{*}(\theta|y)=\pi(\theta|y)=\pi(\theta|I)\pi(y|\theta)\bigg/\int_{\Theta}\pi(\theta|I)\pi(y|\theta)
, making Bayes’ theorem
100\%
IPR efficient.

For this purpose, he used the Shannon’s entropy to measure the information content, then defined the information loss as
\Delta[q(\theta|y)]=(\text{Output Information}-\text{Input Information})
in Equation (
1
), and finally derived the optimal IPR by minimizing
\Delta[q(\theta|y)]
over the space of all possible distributions
q(\theta|y)

\displaystyle\Delta[q(\theta|y)]

\displaystyle=

Output Information - Input Information

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|y)}\bigg[\log q(\theta|y)+\log\pi(y|I)\bigg]

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|y)}\bigg[\log\pi(\theta|I)+\log\pi(y|\theta)\bigg]

\displaystyle\Delta[q(\theta|y)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|y)}\bigg[\log\frac{q(\theta|y)}{\pi(\theta|I)}\frac{\pi(y|I)}{\pi(y|\theta)}\bigg].

(1)

Using a variational calculus formalism, Zellner proved that the optimal IPR in Equation (
2
) coincides exactly with Bayes’ theorem.

\displaystyle q^{*}(\theta|y)=\pi(\theta|I)\pi(y|\theta)\bigg/\int_{\Theta}\pi(\theta|I)\pi(y|\theta)d\theta.

(2)

An interesting implication of this result comes from the interpretation of Bayes’ theorem as the solution of an infinite dimensional optimization problem, therefore approximate solutions could be obtained by restricting the optimization space. More specifically, optimizing over a smaller space
\mathcal{Q}
gives an approximate
\tilde{q}(\theta|y)
in Equation (
2
) and represents a formal justification for variational inference
(
Blei et al. 2017
)
as an approximate information processing rule.

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(\theta|y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{q\;\in\;\mathcal{Q}}{\text{argmin}}\;\Delta[q(\theta|y)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{q\;\in\;\mathcal{Q}}{\text{argmin}}\;\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigg[\log\frac{q(\theta|y)}{\pi(\theta|I)}\frac{\pi(y|I)}{\pi(y|\theta)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{q\;\in\;\mathcal{Q}}{\text{argmin}}\;-\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log(y|\theta)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigg[\log\frac{q(\theta|y)}{\pi(\theta|y)}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}_{q}[\pi(y|I)]

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(\theta|y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{q\;\in\;\mathcal{Q}}{\text{argmin}}\;-\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log(y|\theta)\right]+\text{KL}\left\{q|\pi(\theta|I)\right\}+C.

(3)

In the rest of this work we answer the backward question: what is the optimal information processing rule if one seeks to remove some information from the posterior distribution given the complete data ? we call such a rule
the optimal information deletion rule

3
Information concept and optimal information deletion rule

We motivate the need for data removal with two problems, namely, cross validation and data deletion. In cross validation
(
Vehtari & Ojanen 2012
)
, a subset of the data is used to fit a statistical model, while the left out subset is used to assess the quality of the fitted model using a scoring rule, and the procedure is repeated over a designed partition of the complete data. To avoid multiple model refits, we usually resort to approximate cross validation and aim for an approximate leave-data-out posterior distribution. In comparison, in data deletion
(
Bourtoule et al. 2021
)
, the goal is to delete user information, not only from databases, but also from a statistical model already fitted and deployed, without refitting it from scratch. Both problems share a commonality, the need to update a posterior distribution given the complete data after removing some information, i.e. some data. The strategy to obtain the optimal information deletion rule (IDR) is similar to the IPR in
Zellner 1988
and requires the definition of input and output information. Then, the optimal IDR is defined as the one that minimizes some information loss.

The inputs to our IDR are the outputs from the IPR in section
2
, that is, the posterior distribution given the complete data
\pi(\theta|y)
and the distribution for the data
\pi(y|I)
given prior information
I
. We divide the data into two parts
y=(y_{g},y_{-g})
, where
y_{g}
represents the subset whose information content needs to be removed from
\pi(\theta|y)
, and we denote its likelihood contribution by
\pi(y_{g}|\theta)
, which is also an input. Using
\pi(y_{g}|\theta)
as the likelihood contribution for
y_{g}
implicitly assumes conditional independence, but this is purely for readability and is not required at all, as we explain in Appendix
7.4
. Regarding the outputs, after removing the information content of
y_{g}
, we are left with the distribution for the rest of the data
\displaystyle\pi(y_{-g}|I)=\int_{\Theta}\pi(y_{-g}|\theta)\pi(\theta|I)d\theta
given prior information
I
, and the
antedata
distribution
q(\theta|y_{-g})
. We summarize the components of the information deletion process in figure

.

4
Derivation of the optimal information deletion rule

The proof follows the exact steps as in
Zellner 1988
by defining a class of neighboring functions that we replace in the functional in Equation (
5
). Then, we define the Lagrangian that we minimize with the constraint that the optimum must be a proper density.

More specifically, let
\tilde{q}(\theta|y_{-g})=q(\theta|y_{-g})+\epsilon\eta(\theta)
be a class of neighboring functions, and we require the same conditions for
\epsilon
and
\eta(\theta)
, i.e.
\epsilon
is small,
\eta(\theta)
is a continuous function that decays to zero at the boundary of the integration domain and
\displaystyle\int_{\theta}\eta(\theta)^{2}d\theta<\infty
. We state the result on the optimal information deletion rule in
1
and give its proof in Appendix
7.1
and
7.2
.

Theorem 1
(Optimal information deletion rule)

Under the previous assumptions, the minimum of the functional
\Delta[q(\theta|y_{-g})]
is given by:

\displaystyle q^{*}(\theta|y_{-g})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi(\theta|y)}{\pi(y_{g}|\theta)}\bigg/\int_{\Theta}\frac{\pi(\theta|y)}{\pi(y_{g}|\theta)}d\theta.

(6)

We stress that within the Bayesian unlearning framework,
q^{*}(\theta|y_{-g})

is not an approximation, but rather the optimal information deletion rule
.

5
Equivalence between the optimal Information deletion rule and Bayes’ theorem

The second aspect of this work is to prove that the minimum
q^{*}(\theta|y_{-g})
is equivalent to the leave data
y_{g}
out posterior distribution
\pi(\theta|y_{-g})
from Bayes’ theorem.

First, we note that
\pi(\theta|y_{-g})
can play two roles, either be the posterior distribution obtained by combining the prior distribution
\pi(\theta|I)
with the likelihood
\pi(y_{-g}|\theta)
, or be the prior distribution to which the likelihood
\pi(y_{g}|\theta)
is added to obtain the posterior distribution
\pi(\theta|y)
. This is further illustrated in figure
3
.

\pi(\theta)
\pi(\theta|y_{-g})
\pi(\theta|y_{-g},y_{g})
+\pi(y_{-g}|\theta)
+\pi(y_{g}|\theta)

Figure 3
:
The figure illustrates the role of
\pi(\theta|y_{-g})
as prior or posterior distribution.

Therefore, to show the equivalence between
q^{*}(\theta|y_{-g})
and
\pi(\theta|y_{-g})
, we need to choose one of the dashed routes in figure
3
and prove that
q^{*}(\theta|y_{-g})
and
\pi(\theta|y_{-g})
are exactly the same. The most natural option in our context is to choose the case where
\pi(\theta|y_{-g})
is the prior distribution for
\pi(\theta|y)
. We state the equivalence in theorem
2
and prove it in Appendix
7.3
.

Theorem 2
(Equivalence of the IDR and Bayes’ theorem)

The leave data-out posterior distribution
\pi(\theta|y_{-g})
obtained from Bayes’ theorem and the optimal information deletion rule are equivalent:

\displaystyle\pi(\theta|y_{-g})=q^{*}(\theta|y_{-g})=\frac{\pi(\theta|y)}{\pi(y_{g}|\theta)}\bigg/\int_{\Theta}\frac{\pi(\theta|y)}{\pi(y_{g}|\theta)}d\theta.

(7)

The first important implication of the previous equivalence result is the duality between learning and unlearning, meaning that Bayes’ theorem, not only, is an optimal information processing rule while adding information, but it also remains optimal while removing information; or paraphrasing Lavoisier “in Bayesian learning or unlearning, information is not created, information is not lost, information is just transformed”.

The second important implication of this result is a practical strategy to obtain different approximations
\tilde{q}(\theta|y_{-g})
to the leave-data-out posterior

\pi(\theta|y_{-g})
, by using the variational formulation that led to
q^{*}(\theta|y_{-g})
. More specifically, instead of optimization over the space of all distributions, we can minimize the functional
\Delta[q(\theta|y_{-g})]
over a restricted family
\mathcal{Q}
as in Equation (
5
). This strategy was also coined as variational Bayesian unlearning
(
Nguyen et al. 2020
)
or posterior correction
(
Khan 2025
)
, and our approach gives a formal justification to these variational inference like perspective.

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(\theta|y_{-g})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{q\;\in\;\mathcal{Q}}{\text{argmin}}\;\Delta[q(\theta|y_{-g})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{q\;\in\;\mathcal{Q}}{\text{argmin}}\;\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|y_{-g})}\bigg[\log\frac{q(\theta|y_{-g})}{\pi(\theta|y)}\frac{\pi(y_{-g}|I)\pi(y_{g}|\theta)}{\pi(y|I)}\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{q\;\in\;\mathcal{Q}}{\text{argmin}}\;\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log(y_{g}|\theta)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|y_{-g})}\bigg[\log\frac{q(\theta|y_{-g})}{\pi(\theta|y)}\bigg]+\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|y_{-g})}\bigg[\frac{\pi(y_{-g}|I)}{\pi(y|I)}\bigg]

\displaystyle\tilde{q}(\theta|y_{-g})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underset{q\;\in\;\mathcal{Q}}{\text{argmin}}\;\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log(y_{g}|\theta)\right]+\text{KL}\left\{q|\pi(\theta|y)\right\}+C.

(8)

Equation (
5
) provides a clear interpretation of the information deletion procedure. The minimization of the first term is equivalent to forgetting the information content of
\pi(y_{g}|\theta)
, while the second term acts as a regularization, preventing
\tilde{q}(\theta|y_{-g})
from departing too much from the posterior distribution given the complete data
\pi(\theta|y)
. Additionally, the regularization term illustrates the importance of an accurate posterior distribution, that is, improved accuracy in
\pi(\theta|y)
should translate into better approximations
\tilde{q}(\theta|y_{-g})
.

6
Discussion

In this work, we have revisited the result of
Zellner 1988
from an information deletion perspective, and we showed that Bayes’ theorem is both an optimal information processing rule for learning and unlearning, in other words, for adding and removing some information to the prior and from the posterior, respectively. To state the results more clearly, optimizing the information loss over the space of feasible distributions does not lead to some approximation or ad-hoc trick, but rather the optimal processing rule. A practical consequence of our result is the variational formulation of data removal, which allows better approximations to any leave-data-out posterior distribution.

Some extensions are worth exploring: first, as in
Zellner 1988
, we have used Shannon’s entropy to quantify information, thus it would be interesting to investigate if our result holds under different information measures. Second, and still related to the first point, in the generalized variational inference literature, the likelihood and KL divergence in the variational formulation of Bayes’ theorem can be replaced by a general loss function and divergence, respectively. Therefore, what are the consequences of such choices with respect to information deletion or unlearning ? We conjecture from Equation (
5
) that the same result holds and one might only need to replace the log likelihood and the KL divergence by the equivalent loss or divergence.

7.4
On the conditional independence assumption

Throughout this work, we have denoted the contribution of the data
y_{g}
as
\pi(y_{g}|\theta)
, which implicitly assumes that
\pi(y|\theta)=\pi(y_{-g}|\theta)\pi(y_{g}|\theta)
. Indeed, this
assumption is not needed

\pi(y_{g}|\theta)
by
\pi(y_{g}|\theta,y_{-g})
and following the exact same steps as before. As a result,

\Delta[q(\theta|y_{-g})]=\mathbb{E}_{q(\theta|y_{-g})}\bigg[\log\frac{q(\theta|y_{-g})}{\pi(\theta|y)}\frac{\pi(y_{-g}|I)\pi(y_{g}|\theta,y_{-g})}{\pi(y|I)}\bigg]
