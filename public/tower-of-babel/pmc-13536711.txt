Comprehensive characterization of MET exon 14 skipping mutations in non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Jie Lin, Guojie Xia, Yijuan Wu, Lingfeng Chen, Yaru Zhang, Ya Ma, Jiani C Yin, Zhenyu Zhang, Xiaojie Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMET exon 14 skipping mutation (METΔex14) is a key driver event in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and can emerge as an acquired drug resistance mechanism to MET, EGFR or ALK inhibitors. The clinical and genomic features of METΔex14 in NSCLC require further characterization.MethodsOur study included a total of 585 patients with METΔex14 + NSCLC, comprising 556 baseline samples, 53 samples from patients exhibiting resistance to MET inhibitors, and 16 samples from patients resistant to EGFR/ALK inhibitors. Genomic data from targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) of tissue and/or plasma samples using GeneseeqPrime™ (a 425 pan-cancer gene panel) were analyzed.ResultsOverall, METΔex14 exhibited a prevalence of 1.02% (n = 585) in the screened NSCLC population, with a higher incidence in patients with a sarcomatoid histology. METΔex14 was predominantly detected at the splice donor site, though the non-coding region adjacent to the splice acceptor site contributed considerably to the complexity of METΔex14. Common concurrent alterations identified at baseline included those in TP53 (40.8%), CDK4 (16%) and EGFR (12.4%). Concurrent MET amplification and cell cycle pathway mutations were both associated with worse outcomes in patients treated with crizotinib, with significant co-occurrences observed also among these concurrent genomic variations. In addition, increased chromosomal instability and intra-tumoral heterogeneity correlated with a poorer response to crizotinib. Mechanisms of acquired resistance to MET inhibitors were primarily attributed to on-target MET D1228X/Y1230X mutations or off-target alterations within genes in the RTK/RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways. Intriguingly, our exploratory analysis also identified the FGFR3::TACC3 fusion as a potential resistance mechanism to savolitinib. Moreover, METΔex14 was identified in 16 patients following progression on EGFR and ALK inhibitors, highlighting the need for developing tailored therapeutic strategies to overcome resistance.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive characterization of METΔex14 in NSCLC, revealing its dual role as a primary driver of oncogenesis and a potential resistance mechanism to EGFR/ALK inhibitors. The identification of concurrent genetic alterations and potential resistance mechanisms enhances our molecular understanding of treatment responses. Background

The mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition (MET) gene encodes the hepatocyte growth factor receptor (MET), a receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) pivotal in activating downstream signaling pathways involved in key biological processes, including cell proliferation. Dysregulated MET activity is a recognized oncogenic driver in various cancers, especially non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1–4]. One of the primary genomic alterations leading to MET activation is the MET exon 14 skipping mutation (METΔex14). MET exon 14 encodes the protein’s juxtamembrane domain, crucial for the binding of the E3 ubiquitin ligase Cbl, which facilitates protein degradation and thus negative regulation of MET activity [5, 6]. In METΔex14, various mutations around the splice acceptor (SA) or splice donor (SD) sites of MET exon 14 can disrupt RNA splicing, causing the whole exon 14 to be lost in the mRNA transcript and consequently the MET protein, impairing its degradation [7–9].

The prevalence of METΔex14 in NSCLC has been reported at 1–4% [1–4]. Targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) for MET can be divided into two types, depending on whether they inhibit the active (type I) or inactive (type II) state of MET. Type I MET-TKIs are further classified into two groups. Type Ia MET-TKIs, mainly represented by crizotinib, rely on interaction with the G1163 solvent front residue, whereas type Ib TKIs, such as capmatinib, tepotinib, and savolitinib, do not [10, 11]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines® recommends crizotinib for METΔex14 + patients, while capmatinib and tepotinib have recently gained approval by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for showing improved response rates [1, 12, 13]. Savolitinib has been granted approval in China demonstrating robust anti-tumor efficacy [4]. Nevertheless, treatment outcomes vary among patients receiving the same TKIs, underscoring the need to thoroughly explore potential predictive markers and resistance mechanisms to such therapies.

Previous genomic studies have characterized the METΔex14 mutational landscape, including the distribution of various METΔex14 subtypes and the baseline concurrent alterations such as TP53 mutation and MET amplification [7–9, 14–21]. However, few studies have comprehensively assessed the impact of these genomic features on MET-TKI efficacy or prognosis. In this study, we present findings from an extensive cohort of METΔex14 + NSCLC patients, aiming to further elucidate their mutational landscape and potential association with survival outcomes. We sought to identify primary or secondary resistance mechanisms to crizotinib or savolitinib, and examine the significance of acquired METΔex14 that confer resistance to EGFR- or ALK-TKIs. We hope that our results will enhance understanding of METΔex14 + NSCLC and contribute to improved treatment strategies.

Methods

Patients

We examined the clinico-genomic database of 57,521 patients diagnosed with NSCLC recruited from multiple centers, whose tissue or liquid biopsy samples underwent comprehensive genomic profiling using a targeted panel of 425 cancer-related genes (GeneseeqPrime™) at Geneseeq Technology Inc. (Nanjing, China) between July 2016 and May 2022, to identify samples harboring METΔex14-related variants. Relevant clinical information for patients with METΔex14 were also extracted from the database, including sex, age, disease stage, histology, and available treatment outcomes. Disease progression was assessed by physicians in accordance with the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours (RECIST) guideline (version 1.1). For patients with sufficient clinical follow-up data, progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the beginning of TKI treatment to the date of progressive disease. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Nanjing Geneseeq Medical Laboratory (NSJB-MEC-2023–01), and written informed consent was obtained from all the patients before sample collection.

DNA sequencing

DNA sequencing was carried out in a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified and College of American Pathologists (CAP)-accredited laboratory (Geneseeq, Nanjing, China). Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh or formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue samples with the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen), respectively, following manufacturer’s instructions. Circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was extracted from plasma samples using the QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen). Genomic DNA from white blood cells were extracted with the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen) and used as a control for germline variations.

Sequencing libraries were prepared by the KAPA Hyper DNA Library Prep Kit (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). The libraries were then enriched for 425 solid tumor-related genes, using a probe-based hybridization capture panel designed by Geneseeq Technology Inc., as previously described [22]. After each step, DNA concentration was quantified by a Qubit FluoroMETer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq4000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). A minimum tumor purity of 20% was required for tissue samples. The assay yielded a mean effective sequencing depth of 1,663 × for tissue samples and 5,706 × for liquid biopsy samples, with mean on-target rates exceeding 79%.

Sequencing data analysis

Generated FASTQ files underwent quality control using Trimmomatic [23] and low-quality reads were removed. The remaining reads were mapped to the human reference genome (hg19) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner [24]. PCR duplicates were then removed with Picard (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard), and local realignments around indels were performed with the Genome Analysis Toolkit [25] to ensure base quality. VarScan2 [26] was used to identify single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels, with a minimum variant allele frequency threshold of 0.01 for tissue samples and 0.001 for plasma samples. Variants were further filtered with the following parameters: (i) minimum read depth = 20; (ii) minimum base quality = 15; (iii) minimum variant supporting reads = 3 for plasma and 5 for tissue; (iv) variant supporting reads mapped to both strands; (v) strand bias no greater than 10%; (vi) if present in > 1% population frequency in the 1000 g or ExAC database and vii) through an internally collected list of recurrent sequencing errors using a normal pool of 100 samples. To further ensure high specificity, a multi-layered filtering strategy was applied. For liquid biopsy samples, unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were utilized to suppress PCR and sequencing errors. Site-specific sequencing noise and background artifacts were filtered using an internal normal pool of healthy samples. Additionally, to exclude biological false positives arising from clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP), a machine-learning model incorporating variant allele fractions, population frequency, and cfDNA fragment features was applied [27, 28].

Copy-number variants (CNV) and fusion variants were detected using CNVkit [29] and DELLY [30], respectively. Depth ratios of above 2.0 (tissue) or 1.6 (cfDNA) and below 0.6 were considered as CNV gain and CNV loss, respectively. A CNV ratio > 4.0 or < 0.6 was defined as “High” and the ratio in between 0.6 and 4.0 as “Low”, adapted from criteria for gene hyperamplification [31].

Chromosomal instability (CIS) was measured as the proportion of the genome with abnormal copy numbers (segment-level copy number ≤ 1 or ≥ 3), as defined by Xu et al. [32]. Intra-tumoral heterogeneity (ITH) was assessed by dividing the minimal variant allele frequency (VAF) by the maximal VAF in each sample. Optimal cut-off values for both CIS and ITH were determined in a data-driven manner using the maximally selected rank statistics method, implemented via the surv_cutpoint function in the R package “survminer”. Based on this analysis, a cut-off of 0.1 was identified for both metrics and used to distinguish between high and low groups. Tumor mutation burden (TMB) was evaluated as the number of base substitutions and indels in the coding region of the targeted genes, which included synonymous variations to reduce sampling noise and excluded driver mutations that were over-represented during panel sequencing [22]. Consistent with previous methodologies [32], tertile cut-points were used to categorize TMB levels, distinguishing between TMB-High (top tertile) and TMB-Low (bottom tertile) groups.

Variants of METΔex14 were manually filtered. Specifically, MET alterations located outside of intron 13 and exon 14 were excluded from the analysis. Variants were considered as METΔex14 if they involved the 3’ splice acceptor (SA) site (c. 2885–2889), 5’ splice donor (SD) site (c.3027–3031), the adjacent poly-pyrimidine tract (c.2064–2879), or the branch site (c.2859). Missense mutations within exon 14 that were known to be pathogenic, such as p.D1002X, p.Y1003X, p.R1004X [5, 6], were also included in the analysis. To confirm the accuracy of NGS-detected METΔex14 events, orthogonal validation was performed using droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) on 29 representative samples (9 positives, 20 negatives). The assay demonstrated 100% overall percent agreement (OPA) with ddPCR, maintaining concordance across a VAF range of 0.37% to 51.3%.

Statistical analysis

The R software (version 3.5.3) was used for statistical analysis and data visualization. Mutational profiles were illustrated using the “RColorBrewer”, “stringr”, and “ComplexHeatmap” packages in R. Genomic variants were clustered and pathway enrichment were performed using the gene lists concluded by Sanchez-Vega et al. (2018). Interactions among the detected genomic variants and their respective pathways were analyzed using the “RColorBrewer” package. For survival analysis, Kaplan–Meier curves were compared using the log-rank test and univariate Cox proportional hazards models, with the “survival” and “survminer” packages. To control the false discovery rate (FDR) in the analysis of multiple genomic biomarkers, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Pairwise comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using the Wilcoxon rank sum test, while the Kruskal–Wallis’s test was used for comparisons involving multiple groups, unless otherwise specified. For categorical variables, Fisher’s exact test was applied. Differences were considered statistically significant if the two-tailed P value was less than 0.05.

Results

Overview of the study cohort

We identified a total of 585 cases with METΔex14 mutations, reflecting a prevalence of 1.02% among the NSCLC samples examined (Figure S1). The median age of the METΔex14 cohort was 69 years (range: 33–92), with females comprising 47.7% (n = 279) of the population. The majority of patients were diagnosed at stage IV (58.0%). Adenocarcinoma was the predominant histological type among METΔex14 + NSCLC patients, accounting for 87.3% of cases (Table 1). Notably, METΔex14 was most prevalent in patients with sarcomatoid (9.7%, 19/196) and adenosquamous (4.0%, 18/449) NSCLC (Table S1).

Across the study cohort, 266 unique METΔex14 mutations were detected. The majority of the diversity (63.9%, 170/266) arose from mutations around the SA site (Fig. 1A). Unique base substitutions in the non-coding regions adjacent to the SA site, such as the branch site or the poly-pyrimidine tract, accounted for 40.2% of the variations, followed by indels (21.4%) and base substitutions (2.3%) directly at the SA site. Mutations at the SD site exhibited lower complexity (30.1%, Fig. 1A), yet were present in 61.5% (360/585) of the patients (Fig. 1B). Among these, 290 patients (49.6%) had base substitutions and 70 (12.0%) had indels at the SD site (Fig. 1B). Whole exon 14 deletions were detected in eight cases (1.4%), while point mutations, such as D1002/Y1003/R1004, were found in 14 cases (2.4%). Specifically, there were six cases of Y1003X, five of R1004X, two of D1002X, and one case with both Y1003 and R1004 deletions. Baseline genomic landscape of METΔex14 NSCLC

Among the 585 METΔex14 + patients, 556 had treatment-naïve baseline samples (Figure S1). The majority of these baseline samples were FFPE tissue (n = 489, 87.9%), followed by pleural effusion (n = 29, 5.2%) and plasma (n = 38, 6.8%) (Figure S1). Comparison of genomic profiles between tissue and liquid biopsy samples revealed a generally consistent mutational landscape (Figure S5). Mutational profiling of these samples revealed that TP53 mutations were the most prevalent concurrent variation, detected in 40.8% (227/556) of the patients. Alterations in CDK4, CDKN2A and MYC were also found to co-occur with METΔex14 mutations in 16%, 7.6% and 7.6% of patients, respectively (Fig. 2A). Concomitant driver mutations with known oncogenic significance were identified in 42 cases (7.6%), including EGFR driver mutations in exons 19–21 (5.6%), KRAS G12C (0.9%), ERBB2 activating mutations (0.9%), RET (0.4%) and ALK (0.2%) rearrangement, and BRAF V600E (0.2%). MET amplification was also detected in 44 (7.9%) METΔex14 + patients.

Further analysis revealed notable interactions among genomic variants. For instance, TP53 mutation tended to co-occur with RB1 deletion but were mutually exclusive with CDK4 amplification (Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.01; Figure S2A). On the other hand, CDK6 and MYC amplification frequently co-existed with MET amplification (P < 0.01).

Notably, an enrichment of concurrent CDK4 amplification was observed in patients with adenosquamous NSCLC, as compared to those with adenocarcinoma or squamous histology (P < 0.01, Figure S2B). Although patients with concurrent MET amplification did not show significant associations with clinical features (Table S3), these patients exhibited a trend toward higher tumor mutational burden (TMB, P = 0.09; Figure S2C) and chromosomal instability scores (CIS, P < 0.0001; Figure S2D) and carried more genomic variants involved in pathways that mediate cell cycle progression, affecting genes including TP53, CDKN2A/B, CDK6 and MYC (P < 0.05; Fig. 2B).

Patients with concomitant driver mutations displayed comparable clinical features to those without (Table S4). However, we observed that patients with base substitution at the SA site of MET (P = 0.07) or with D1002/Y1003/R1004 point mutations (P = 0.08) exhibited a trend toward carrying a concomitant driver mutation (Fig. 2C and Table S4).

Mechanisms of primary resistance to MET-TKIs

Our cohort included a total of 53 patients with sufficient clinical follow-up data who were treated with MET-TKIs, specifically crizotinib and savolitinib, either as first-line or subsequent therapies. Of the 42 patients who received crizotinib as their first-line therapy, the median PFS was 10.1 months (Figure S3A). In comparison, 11 patients were treated with savolitinib. Of these, eight were treatment-naïve, and three had progressed on prior therapies. The median PFS (mPFS) for the savolitinib group (n = 11) and for those treated with first-line savolitinib (n = 8) was both 10.2 months (Figure S3B), while the single third-line patient achieved a PFS of 7.8 months. Given the small sample size, these efficacy data for savolitinib should be interpreted as exploratory.

We first examined NGS data from pre-treatment samples of 33 crizotinib users and 9 savolitinib users to identify potential biomarkers that may affect treatment outcomes (Fig. 3A). METΔex14 mutations were categorized primarily into two groups: those occurring at the SA site and those at the SD site. A crizotinib user with whole exon 14 deletion was not included in the survival comparison, and had progressed with a PFS of 17.0 months. PFS was comparable between these METΔex14 subtypes for both MET-TKIs (crizotinib users: mPFS for SD vs SA: 8.5 vs 13.4 months, HR [95% CI]: 1.51 [0.73, 3.09], P = 0.26; savolitinib users: mPFS for SD vs SA: 12.0 vs 8.1 months, HR [95% CI]: 0.36 [0.06, 2.22], P = 0.25) (Fig. 3B-C). To assess potential confounding factors, we performed univariate analysis on clinical characteristics. None of the clinical variables (age, sex, histology, sample type) showed a significant association with PFS (Table S5). Given the limited sample size and lack of significant clinical covariates, we focused on genomic biomarkers and applied FDR adjustment to ensure statistical rigor. For patients treated with crizotinib, a shorter PFS was associated with the presence of concurrent mutations, including amplifications of MET (mPFS 5.2 vs 10.1 months, HR [95% CI]: 6.43 [1.63, 25.41], P = 0.002, adjusted P = 0.009), MYC (mPFS 4.0 vs 12.2 months, HR [95% CI]: 4.88 [1.78, 13.41], P < 0.001, adjusted P = 0.004) and RECQL4 (mPFS 6.2 vs 10.1 months, HR [95% CI]: 3.48 [1.10, 11.02], P = 0.03, adjusted P = 0.072) (Figure S4A-C and Table S5). In addition, patients with mutations in cell cycle pathway genes also displayed a trend towards worse PFS outcomes following MET-TKI treatment, though this did not reach statistical significance (mPFS 6.1 vs 10.1 months, HR [95% CI]: 2.44 [0.90, 6.61], P = 0.07, adjusted P = 0.121) (Figure S4D and Table S5). The negative associations of genetic amplifications with survival outcome might be attributed to chromosomal instability. Thus, it is not surprising that we also observed a worse PFS outcome in patients with high chromosomal instability, either as higher CIS (mPFS 7.1 vs 17.0 months, HR [95% CI]: 2.28 [1.04–5.04], P = 0.04, adjusted P = 0.086) (Fig. 3D and Table S5) or CNV (mPFS 6.1 vs 12.7 months, HR [95% CI]: 4.22 [1.57, 11.32], P < 0.01) (Figure S4E). Moreover, higher ITH showed a trend toward shorter PFS (mPFS 9.6 vs 13.2 months, HR [95% CI]: 2.11 [0.96–4.66], P = 0.06, adjusted P = 0.118) (Fig. 3E and Table S5), whereas TMB levels appeared to have no prognostic significance (mPFS for TMB-low vs TMB-high: 10.1 vs 10.7 months, HR [95% CI]: 1.16 [0.46, 2.88], P = 0.46) (Figure S4F).

Secondary resistance mechanisms to crizotinib or savolitinib

To identify mechanisms of acquired resistance to MET TKIs, we compared genomic alterations in patients at baseline and following progression on crizotinib or savolitinib (Figure S1). Given the clinical challenges of re-biopsy, the sample distribution shifted towards liquid biopsies in the post-treatment setting. For crizotinib-treated patients with paired samples (n = 32), baseline profiling was primarily tissue-based (24 tissue, six pleural effusion, two plasma samples), whereas post-progression analysis included 12 tissue, four pleural effusion, and 16 plasma samples. Similarly, the savolitinib paired cohort (n = 8) included seven tissues and one pleural effusion sample at baseline, compared to two tissue, two pleural effusion, and four plasma samples at progression. The predominant resistance mechanism identified for both TKIs included the emergence of on-target MET alterations, such as p.Y1230X or p.D1228X, which emerged in 17.9% (5/28) of the patients after first-line crizotinib treatment and 20% (1/5) after first-line savolitinib treatment (Fig. 4). In two patients carrying p.D1228X mutations, we also detected the emergence of MET amplification. Acquisition of off-target alterations in genes involved in bypass or downstream pathways, such as EGFR amplification, BRAF K601E, KRAS G12V, and PIK3CA E542K, occurred in 17.9% (5/28) of patients resistant to crizotinib, but was not observed in those receiving savolitinib as first-line therapy. Specifically, a total of 71.4% (20/28) of crizotinib users developed gain-of-function alterations affecting the RTK/RAS/MAPK and PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways. Among second-line savolitinib-treated patients, a putative resistance mechanism involving the FGFR3::TACC3 fusion was identified in one case (PFS = 12.2 months). This fusion was undetectable in the baseline tissue (1,378 ×), and detected in the post-progression plasma at a VAF of 0.23% (mean depth: 6,912 ×). EGFR amplification was found to emerge in another patient (PFS = 6.1 months). Notably, a patient progressing on savolitinib as a third-line treatment developed the p.Y1230S resistance (PFS = 7.8 months). Moreover, 10.7% (3/28) of the crizotinib users and 37.5% (3/8) of the savolitinib users exhibited loss of their original METΔex14 mutations following disease progression.

MET activation as a resistance mechanism to EGFR/ALK-TKIs

Intriguingly, our study identified 16 patients who exhibited MET alterations following disease progression on EGFR or ALK-TKIs (Fig. 5). Review of prior treatment history revealed that patients had progressed on various specific agents, including gefitinib (n = 5), icotinib (n = 6), erlotinib (n = 2), afatinib (n = 2), and alectinib (n = 1). Detailed clinical and genomic characteristics of these 16 patients are summarized in Table S6. In the single case with paired samples, the patient presented with EGFR L858R, EGFR amplification and a RET fusion at baseline. Following treatment with icotinib, this patient developed both METΔex14 (c.3028G > T) and MET amplification, alongside additional EGFR on-target mutations, including T790M and C797S. For the remaining 15 patients, only post-progression samples were available, revealing the presence of METΔex14. For instance, one patient had progressed on first-line alectinib after achieving a PFS of 12 months. Post-progression sequencing revealed the presence of METΔex14 (c. 2888-12_2888-9dup) with a VAF of 42.2%. In addition, the patient exhibited what appeared to be an on-target ALK G1202R mutation (VAF = 0.2%) after progression, along with the EML4::ALK driver mutation (VAF = 2.8%).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically characterized the mutational landscape associated with METΔex14 in a large cohort of 585 patients diagnosed with NSCLC. Our findings highlight co-occurrence of MET amplification and aberrations in the cell cycle pathway, both of which were associated with suboptimal outcomes for crizotinib. We also identified high chromosomal instability and intra-tumoral heterogeneity as potential negative prognosis markers. In addition, this study provided preliminary insights into the largely underexplored resistance mechanisms arising from savolitinib, revealing both pre-existing and acquired mutations related to resistance to crizotinib and savolitinib. Furthermore, we discussed the implications of MET∆ex14 alterations as potential contributors to resistance against other TKIs.

The prevalence of METΔex14 in the study cohort was 1.02% (585/57,521), which aligns closely with prior studies reporting prevalence rates of 0.9% (12/1,296) and 1.1% (125/11,306) for the Chinese population [16, 20]. Our findings provide additional evidence that METΔex14 mutations are rarer in Chinese NSCLC patients compared to Caucasians, where reported prevalence rates range from 2 to 3% [7, 9, 19, 33]. In addition, we observed higher rates of METΔex14 in sarcomatoid and adenosquamous carcinomas, which was consistent with previous reports [4, 7, 9, 33]. These MET alterations activate c-Met/HGF signaling, facilitating epithelial-mesenchymal transition, which could partly explain the increased prevalence of METΔex14 in these cancer types characterized by prominent mesenchymal features [34].

Regarding METΔex14 subtypes, we found that mutations at the SD site, particularly base substitutions, occurred more frequently compared to other subtypes. On the other hand, mutations around the SA site, especially those affecting the intronic regions downstream of SA, contributed to the overall diversity and complexity of the variant landscape. The pattern was also concordant with previous data [7, 9, 20]. The presence of non-coding domains next to the SA site, such as the branch site and the poly-pyrimidine tract domains, likely enhances the potential for splice-related abnormalities and exon 14 skipping [35, 36]. D1002, Y1003, and R1004 constitute the MET protein site crucial for Cbl binding. Our data corroborate previous reports regarding the prevalence and functional significance of mutations at the D1002, Y1003, and R1004 loci, which are essential for Cbl binding and subsequent regulation of MET activity [5–9, 20]. Our study also reports eight cases of whole exon 14 deletions at the genomic level, a finding that has been rarely documented [9].

Co-occurring mutations in key cell cycle regulatory genes, such as TP53, CDK4/6, MYC and CDKN2A, were frequently identified in our study cohort. The high prevalence of TP53 mutations has been widely reported and is often associated with a poor prognosis [37, 38]. CDK4 amplification is also a well-recognized concurrent variation for METΔex14 + NSCLC patients [7]. The high prevalences of concurrent TP53 and CDK4 mutations from our cohort were comparable to previous findings [7–9, 15, 20]; additionally, we report the mutual exclusivity between these two alterations, a pattern not addressed previously. Concurrent MET amplification has been reported with similar frequencies and associated with poorer crizotinib prognosis for METΔex14 + NSCLC patients [7, 18, 20]. We also extended our understanding of the impact of MET amplification on treatment outcomes, revealing a strong correlation between higher levels of overall CNV or CIS and diminished PFS following crizotinib treatment. The only savolitinib-user harboring concurrent MET amplification also had cell cycle aberrations and a PFS shorter than the median, although this case may not be representative and more data on savolitinib is required.

In general, previously reported mPFS ranges for crizotinib (mPFS 7.3–12.4 months) [1, 2] and savolitinib (mPFS 6.8–10.7 months) [3, 4] were consistent with our real world findings, with an mPFS of 10.1 months for crizotinib and 10.2 months for savolitinib. Cross study differences may relate to variations in treatment line distribution. Minor discrepancies in survival outcomes may be attributed to differences in treatment lines and the high burden of concurrent alterations (e.g., TP53, MET amplification) in our real-world cohort compared to selected clinical trial populations. Although selective MET inhibitors are now preferred, our study cohort (2016–2022) provides a robust reference for crizotinib, which remains listed as a first-line therapeutic option in the NCCN Guidelines [39]. Understanding crizotinib resistance remains clinically relevant, particularly in global settings with limited access to newer agents. Interestingly, we found that varying METΔex14 subtypes did not result in differential PFS for crizotinib or savolitinib, concordant with previous results [19, 20]. However, patients with base substitutions at the MET SA site or at D1002/Y1003/R1004 were more likely to carry concomitant driver mutations at baseline, especially compared to those with mutations in the non-coding region adjacent to SA. Driver mutations were considered mutually exclusive with METΔex14 [18], but the frequency of co-existing driver mutations in our study was relatively high (7.6%). Concurrent driver mutations have been recognised as negative predictive factors in NSCLC [40], but our understanding in their therapy-induced clonal evolution is still limited [41], and this signifies the importance of dynamic molecular monitoring during disease management. Although ITH and TMB both measure tumor heterogeneity, TMB does not reflect the clonal distribution of such heterogeneity, and their results may be independent of each other [42]. Our study suggested a potential differential utility of ITH and TMB to predict prognosis, suggesting the significance of clonal interactions in METΔex14 NSCLC, although further validation is needed.

The recurrent on-target resistance mechanisms identified in our study, namely the D1228X and Y1230X mutations, affect the MET activation loop and are known to interfere with drug binding [43]. They have been frequently reported in both in vitro experiments [11] and clinical cases [21, 43–47], which develop in response to type I MET inhibitors such as crizotinib and savolitinib. For patients who develop these resistance mutations, they may respond to type II MET inhibitors. However, overcoming the D1228X alteration may be more challenging than targeting the Y1230X mutations, warranting further investigation [11, 45, 48]. While we also noted MET amplification as a secondary resistance mechanism [44], it demonstrated weaker potential to confer resistance independently compared to point mutations. Specifically, in our study, MET amplification developed concurrently with the D1228X mutation in both cases. In addition, other previously reported on-target MET alterations, such as G1163X and L1195X [11, 21, 44], were not detected in our patients.

In addition to on-target resistance mutations, activation of bypass or downstream pathways, such as EGFR amplification and KRAS G12X, was another common mechanism of acquired crizotinib resistance in our study, with frequencies that are consistent with previous reports [21, 44]. Notably, off-target resistance mechanisms to savolitinib monotherapy have been inadequately studied, as most available data have been focused on combination therapies involving savolitinib [49, 50]. We identified an FGFR3::TACC3 fusion that emerged as a potential resistance mutation after progression on savolitinib, as the original METΔex14 was lost and this fusion was the only remaining oncogenic mutation [51, 52]. Previous studies have established FGFR3::TACC3 as an oncogenic driver with constitutive kinase activity. In glioblastoma [53], it is a well-recognized therapeutic target. In NSCLC [51, 54], it has been characterized as a primary driver and as an acquired resistance mechanism to EGFR-TKIs [52]. This collective evidence supports its potential role as a bypass resistance mechanism to savolitinib in our patient. Notably, this fusion was undetectable in the paired baseline tissue sample despite relatively high-depth sequencing (median effective depth: 1,378x). Consequently, its detection at progression likely reflects the clonal expansion of a pre-existing ultra-low frequency subclone or a de novo event driven by therapeutic pressure. However, in the absence of functional validation and given the single-case nature of this observation, this finding remains hypothesis-generating.

Moreover, our findings suggest that METΔex14 may also be associated with resistance to other TKIs, such as EGFR and ALK inhibitors. While the lack of paired baseline profiling for the majority of these patients limits our ability to distinguish between de novo acquisition and the clonal selection of pre-existing low-frequency subclones, the enrichment of METΔex14 at the time of progression reflects strong therapeutic selective pressure. Notably, the definitive emergence of METΔex14 in the single patient with paired samples supports its clinical relevance as a potential resistance mechanism. Nevertheless, the limited availability of comprehensive longitudinal data in this retrospective cohort prevented a granular temporal analysis of resistance emergence relative to specific treatment durations.

Upregulated MET activity is a well-recognized resistance mechanism to EGFR-TKI [55] or ALK-TKI [56, 57] through bypass activation, and respective combination therapies have already been tested in clinical trials [55, 57, 58]. However, although METΔex14 has been recognized to confer resistance to other RTK inhibitors [56, 59, 60], the specific implications for clinical trial design and patient management remain underexplored [55, 58]. Our study proposes that the presence of MET∆ex14 should be considered as an independent inclusion criterion in clinical trials evaluating MET-targeted therapies, given its potential role in resistance mechanisms post-TKI treatment.

The study has several limitations. Firstly, only DNA sequencing results were available for the studied population. Transcriptome sequencing or immunohistochemical analysis were not conducted to validate if detected METΔex14 led to high MET expression levels as expected, and there may be discordance in results at different levels of detection [7, 14, 16, 61]. Secondly, our cohort included a mixture of tissue and liquid biopsy (plasma and pleural effusion) samples. While this allowed for a larger real-world cohort and reflected clinical practice where re-biopsy is difficult, the varying sensitivity between tissue and plasma sequencing could potentially introduce bias in detecting low-frequency resistance mutations.

In addition, the availability of paired NGS samples for drug resistance analysis was limited, and the interpretation resistance mechanisms based solely on post-progression samples carries inherent bias. Specifically, within the EGFR/ALK-TKI cohort, the absence of baseline profiling precludes a definitive distinction between de novo acquisition and clonal selection of pre-existing subclones, while the lack of comprehensive longitudinal clinical data limits our ability to perform detailed temporal analyses. Furthermore, the identification of rare resistance alterations, such as the FGFR3::TACC3 fusion and MET point mutations, relies on observational genomic data without experimental functional validation (e.g., orthogonal RNA sequencing). As such, alternative explanations including tumor heterogeneity cannot be fully excluded, and these findings regarding novel resistance mechanisms should be considered hypothesis-generating. Larger studies employing functional characterizations or clinical studies are necessary to validate the potential resistance mechanisms proposed and improve our understanding of therapy-induced NSCLC clonal evolution. Finally, the retrospective nature of our study results in limited clinical information. Data on smoking status, prior therapies, and metastatic burden were not consistently available, preventing adjustment for these potential confounders. Additionally, due to the challenges of long-term follow-up in a real-world setting, patients without confirmed progression dates were excluded from the survival analysis. This approach, while ensuring the accuracy of progression events for biomarker correlation, likely represents a conservative estimation of PFS compared to standard administrative censoring used in prospective clinical trials. The retrospective design also constrained the sample size for specific analyses, particularly the savolitinib subgroup (n = 11). Consequently, findings regarding savolitinib resistance and other subgroup analyses should be considered exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts. Future systematic investigations of METΔex14 + NSCLC are anticipated to validate and expand upon our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of the mutational landscape associated with METΔex14 in NSCLC, revealing the prevalence of concurrent genomic alterations such as TP53, CDK4, and MET amplification that may significantly influence treatment outcomes with MET-targeted therapies. The identification of primary and secondary resistance mechanisms to crizotinib and savolitinib highlights the need for dynamic molecular monitoring and individualized therapeutic approaches. Our findings also suggest that METΔex14 status may confer resistance to other targeted therapies, underscoring its potential relevance in clinical trial design and future treatment strategies for NSCLC. It is hoped that these insights will enhance our understanding of this genomic variation and potentially contribute to improved outcomes for patients with METΔex14 + NSCLC.
