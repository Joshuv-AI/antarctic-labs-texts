Brokerage in the Black Box: Swing States, Strategic Ambiguity, and the Global Politics of AI Governance
Ha-Chi Tran

Abstract
The U.S.–China rivalry has placed frontier dual-use technologies, particularly Artificial Intelligence (AI), at the center of global power dynamics, as techno-nationalism, supply chain securitization, and competing standards deepen bifurcation within a weaponized interdependence that blurs civilian–military boundaries. Existing research, yet, mostly emphasizes superpower strategies and often overlooks the role of middle powers as autonomous actors shaping the techno-order. This study examines Technological Swing States (TSS) - middle powers with both technological capacity and strategic flexibility - and their ability to navigate the frontier technologies’ uncertainty and opacity to mediate great-power techno-competition regionally and globally. It reconceptualizes AI opacity not as a technical deficit, but as a structural feature and strategic resource, stemming from algorithmic complexity, political incentives that prioritize performance over explainability, and the limits of post-hoc interpretability. This structural opacity shifts authority from technical demands for explainability to institutional mechanisms - such as certification, auditing, and disclosure - converting technical constraints into strategic political opportunities. Drawing on case studies of South Korea, Singapore, and India, the paper theorizes how TSS exploit the interplay between opacity and institutional transparency through three strategies: (i) delay and hedging, (ii) selective alignment, and (iii) normative intermediation. These practices enable TSS to preserve strategic flexibility, build trust among diverse stakeholders, and broker convergence across competing governance regimes, thereby influencing institutional design, interstate bargaining, and policy outcomes in global AI governance.

Within this bifurcated landscape, states confront three strategic options: (i) alignment with the U.S.-led alliance system, (ii) integration into the Chinese-led bloc, or (iii) pursuit of non-alignment. Unlike the relatively insulated geopolitical context of the original Cold War, contemporary states are deeply interdependent and embedded within dense global networks

Within this context, this paper investigates the concept of technological swing states as influential intermediaries within the current techno-order. Drawing inspiration from U.S. domestic swing states, where electoral outcomes are unpredictable and decisive, these countries leverage their geopolitical positions to chart independent technology-related strategies. By controlling key nodes in global technology supply chains, possessing the capacity to develop core technologies, and maintaining advanced infrastructure, they can decisively influence the international balance of power. They are relatively stable actors with independent technology agendas, capable of translating these agendas into tangible outcomes. These states demonstrate strategic flexibility, dynamism, and autonomy, regardless of their alignment with any superpower blocs, either Washington or Beijing.

This paper investigates how technological swing states operate within the opacity and uncertainty inherent in emerging technologies, particularly AI, to sustain and, in some cases, expand their influence at regional and global levels. Rather than advancing a comprehensive network theory or presuming fully articulated strategic intent, the article develops a comparative case-based analysis that semi-formalizes recurring patterns through which case-studied states engage with technological opacity and institutional transparency under conditions of structural constraint.

First, it explains why the opacity, or “black-box” character, of contemporary AI systems is not a transient technical limitation but a durable structural condition. This persistence stems from two interrelated factors: (1) the intrinsic complexity of advanced machine-learning architectures and the limits of post-hoc interpretability techniques, and (2) political and commercial incentives that prioritize performance, scalability, and proprietary advantage over explainability, rendering full model-level transparency largely unattainable. Consequently, AI governance has increasingly shifted away from demands for complete technical disclosure toward institutional and procedural forms of transparency, including certification, auditing, and compliance regimes, which have emerged as critical sites of authority.

Second, the article examines how swing states engage with this configuration of technological opacity and institutional transparency in ways that generate governance influence, without assuming that structural position automatically produces leverage or that observed outcomes necessarily reflect deliberate strategic choice. The analysis proceeds through a logic of fit rather than a fully specified causal model, tracing how practices such as calibrated delay and hedging, selective alignment, and normative intermediation align with particular structural positions within global technological and institutional networks. Through comparative case studies of South Korea, Singapore, and India, the article illustrates how opacity might function as a conditional asset within AI governance, offering analytically grounded but deliberately bounded insights into the evolving geopolitics of technological authority and agency in international relations.

Opacity by Design: Complexity, Political-economic Incentives, and Limits of Explainable AI

2.1
Algorithmic Complexity and the Limits of Explainable AI

The opacity of contemporary machine-learning systems, particularly large-scale deep learning architectures, should be understood as a structural and enduring property rather than a transient technical shortcoming. As models scale in parameters, training data, and optimization complexity, the mapping between inputs and outputs becomes increasingly non-linear, distributed, and irreducible to simple causal narratives, producing the so-called “black box” models
[
68
,
13
,
33
]
. State-of-the-art language and multimodal models now operate with hundreds of billions or even trillions of parameters, trained on vast, heterogeneous datasets that cannot be exhaustively curated or audited
[
8
]
. Optimization across high-dimensional spaces with dense cross-layer interactions generates emerging behaviors or capacities not explicitly encoded or anticipated
[
85
,
89
]
. Even complete access to model weights or architecture might still not allow reconstruction of the causal pathways behind specific outputs. Opacity, therefore, is intrinsic to the mathematical and representational complexity of these systems, not merely the product of secrecy or inadequate documentation.

This structural opacity is further compounded by the probabilistic nature of modern machine learning. Unlike rule-based systems with deterministic mappings, contemporary models approximate probability distributions and generate outputs by sampling. Predictions are therefore non-reproducible, and explanations cannot reliably identify causal mechanisms. Outputs reflect dispersed statistical correlations rather than singular causes, creating a persistent mismatch between human expectations of coherent causal reasoning and the statistical logic of model inference
[
11
]
, a translation gap between machine representations and human conceptual schemas. Empirical studies illustrate this divergence: in computer vision, humans classify animals using semantic features such as eyes or fur, whereas neural networks often rely on imperceptible pixel patterns or latent frequency cues. For instance, models have classified huskies as wolves when the image background contained snow, demonstrating reliance on spurious correlations rather than salient semantic features
[
72
]
. Even when influential features are identified, they rarely yield cognitively meaningful explanations, as these signals do not map onto human categories of perception or reasoning.

These characteristics contribute directly to what is widely described as the reproducibility crisis. Failures of replication may arise from methodological shortcomings or from naïve applications of AI systems, including issues such as data leakage
[
12
]
. They may also result from the intrinsic sensitivity of machine learning models to opaque and often uncontrollable variables, such as stochastic initialization, hyperparameter selection, and preprocessing pipelines
[
39
]
. While open access to datasets and source code can partially mitigate these challenges, it is insufficient to fully resolve them. Meaningful replication typically requires carefully controlled and fully reproducible computational environments, yet researchers rarely have the time to evaluate algorithms across all relevant conditions, nor the space within publications to document every hyperparameter configuration explored, along with institutional pressures to publish rapidly
[
39
]
. In the absence of stable causal reproducibility, even system developers are unable to reliably predict or reproduce model outputs for purposes of verification. Reproducibility failures, therefore, should not be understood as merely temporary technical limitations but rather as a constitutive source of uncertainty in large-scale machine learning systems. This uncertainty links structural opacity to enduring epistemic asymmetries and, ultimately, to the uneven distribution of power within global AI ecosystems.

Within this context, Explainable AI (XAI) was initially heralded as a remedy, promising transparency in increasingly complex systems
[
65
,
74
,
75
]
. Yet, these most contemporary techniques for rendering opaque AI systems interpretable are post hoc
[
74
]
. That is to say, they generate explanations after the fact rather than reconstructing a model’s internal reasoning. This produces a paradox. XAI outputs frequently function less as disclosures of epistemic processes than as plausible rationalizations, narratives calibrated to human expectations of causality and justification.

Current XAI techniques cluster around two broad strategies. The first, model simplification, approximates complex architectures with interpretable surrogates such as linear regressions, decision trees, or locally faithful proxies
[
88
,
65
]
. While cognitively tractable, these proxies inevitably sacrifice fidelity by stripping away higher-order interactions and latent features that drive actual model behavior, leading to a "ersatz understanding" of black-box models
[
65
]
. In other words, such approximations thus yield ex post narrative explanations that legitimate outputs without revealing their epistemic foundations.

The second approach, feature attribution or feature importance, assigns marginal contributions to inputs using methods such as SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) or LIME (Linear Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations)
[
88
,
79
,
72

. Although mathematically rigorous, these techniques highlight correlations rather than causal mechanisms and rarely address the “why” questions central to legal, ethical, and policy reasoning. A credit-scoring model reporting that “Feature X contributed 0.16 to the risk score” offers little normative justification. Additionally, empirical studies demonstrate that different XAI methods can yield contradictory assessments of feature importance for the same outputs of an opaque AI model
[
45
]
. Given that the internal functioning of the models themselves remains only partially understood, it is difficult to ascertain whether XAI explanations faithfully reflect the true decision processes of the underlying model or merely produce plausible-sounding post hoc rationalizations.

This highlights a structural tension that explanations optimized for interpretability often sacrifice fidelity, while faithful accounts remain cognitively inaccessible. The stakes are governance-relevant. Post hoc explanations deployed for compliance are legally fragile and ethically precarious, leaving systems vulnerable to manipulation, bias concealment, and adversarial exploitation. High-profile cases, such as the COMPAS risk-assessment tool in U.S. criminal justice and proprietary credit-scoring platforms, illustrate how interpretability features are often mobilized less for accountability than for rationalization, legitimating opaque systems under the guise of transparency and entrenching systemic inequalities
[

]

2.2
Political-Economic Logic of Opacity: Performance Over Transparency

The persistence of AI opacity cannot be fully explained by technical complexity alone. Political-economic dynamics play an equally critical role. Rather than being a neutral byproduct of engineering constraints, opacity reflects structural incentives that prioritize speed, scalability, and competitive advantage over interpretability. In the global AI economy, where first-mover advantages and rapid iteration are treated as strategic imperatives, transparency is increasingly framed not as a public good but as a frictional obstacle. This dynamic is further amplified by the securitization of AI, which positions it as both a critical infrastructure and a locus of geopolitical rivalry. Under these conditions, opacity ceases to indicate a governance deficit. Instead, it becomes an institutionalized condition, deliberately cultivated through state policies, corporate practices, and techno-industrial competition.

A central tension concerns whether interpretability and performance can be jointly optimized. While contested, a prevailing view holds that interpretability often trades off against efficiency and accuracy, a tradeoff embedded in the economic imperatives driving AI development
[
31
,
14
]
. Venture-capital-driven commercialization privileges rapid scaling and market dominance, reinforcing incentives to favor capability over interpretability. Advocates of inherently interpretable models, or while-box AI, argue that, in high-stakes domains such as healthcare or criminal justice, sacrificing transparency for marginal performance gains is normatively indefensible
[
74
]
. Empirical evidence, however, suggests economic and competitive pressures routinely outweigh normative concerns. In autonomous driving, for instance, Tesla relies on opaque end-to-end neural architectures rather than modular or rule-based alternatives
[
84
]
, despite recurring safety controversies
[
82
,
81
]
. Its Full Self-Driving system, trained on massive datasets via deep learning, exemplifies a structural reality: interpretability does not drive market adoption, whereas speed, functionality, and scalability remain existential imperatives.

Geopolitical competition reinforces these dynamics. AI is increasingly treated as dual-use infrastructure, and disclosure of architectures, training data, or parameter weights is framed as a security vulnerability. In the U.S., the 2023 Executive Order 14110 mandates federal agencies to assess and report risks associated with frontier AI models, exemplifying oversight without full public transparency. Recommendations from the National Telecommunications and Information Administration further restrict public access to certain model weights
“if the U.S. government assesses that the risks of their wide availability sufficiently outweigh the benefits”

[

]

Even within the European Union, a jurisdiction traditionally associated with strong norms of transparency and accountability, opacity persists in domains where industrial competitiveness and strategic positioning are at stake. During the drafting of the AI Act, early proposals included stringent interpretability and explainability requirements for high-risk AI systems, reflecting normative commitments to public oversight and safety
[

. However, these provisions risk becoming largely symbolic, particularly in light of the specific language embedded in Article 78 of the EU AI Act, which permits disclosure only when deemed “strictly necessary,”

[

[

,

, particularly relative to faster-moving U.S. and Chinese firms. A 2022 joint survey conducted by Applied AI found that half of AI startups believed the EU AI Act would slow down AI innovation within the EU, while 16% reported considering halting AI development or relocating operations outside the region
[
7

. In other words, even in a jurisdiction with strong formal commitments to transparency, regulatory discourses have struggled to navigate the tension between normative aspirations on transparency and market or geopolitical imperatives in favor of opacity.

Corporate narratives increasingly align with state logics by reframing opacity in generative AI not as a constraint, but as a necessary safeguard. This discursive strategy legitimizes the enclosure of foundational technologies under the dual guise of public safety and responsible innovation. In practice, however, opacity operates as a competitive asset. Open-source large language models consistently lag 5–12 months behind proprietary systems in benchmark performance

The underlying rationale extends beyond commercial competitiveness; it also addresses potential misuse of open-source models, such as fine-tuning for malicious purposes, where access to model weights could enable adversarial actors to circumvent system safeguards. This approach reflects a broader consensus among major AI firms that releasing the source code of one model may expose vulnerabilities in others
[

2.3
From Model Transparency and Explainability to Institutional and Procedural Oversight

When technical, economic, and geopolitical factors transform opacity from a contingent design feature into a legitimized norm, it becomes an asset to be managed rather than a deficit to be corrected. Regulatory strategies premised on universal explainability face systemic resistance, as speed and capability remain existential imperatives. Opacity should therefore be understood as an institutionalized feature of AI ecosystems, deeply embedded in capitalist and geopolitical logics. Governance challenges lie not in eliminating opacity, but in designing oversight mechanisms capable of functioning under conditions where opacity is both structurally incentivized and politically valorized.

In this context, institutional and procedural transparency becomes crucial. While model-level transparency is often technically unattainable, legitimacy can be secured through formal arrangements that document, scrutinize, and make AI-related decisions contestable. This includes recording design choices, clarifying responsibilities, and establishing mechanisms for oversight, appeal, and redress. When technical opacity limits interpretability, accountability derives less from understanding internal model logics and more from the integrity, auditability, and procedural rigor of governance frameworks.

Analogies from pharmaceutical regulation illustrate this principle. Drugs such as modafinil and aspirin have been widely deployed despite incomplete mechanistic understanding and uncertain causal pathways
[
95
]
. Regulators did not demand full causal clarity; instead, they constructed layered governance regimes — including clinical trials, phased approvals, disclosure obligations, adverse-event monitoring, and compensation frameworks — that rendered decision-making transparent and contestable despite epistemic gaps. Importantly, these procedural mechanisms require continuous modification and adaptation to respond to evolving technologies, just as phased approvals can be adjusted to account for new information or changes in drug behavior. Legitimacy in these contexts depended not on causal comprehension but on the institutional capacity to manage uncertainty responsibly. Applied to AI, this logic treats opaque models like complex scientific phenomena, whose use and application are trusted not through full understanding of their internal operations, but through rigorous research processes and methodological safeguards. Procedural mechanisms act as compensatory structures, allowing institutions to manage and govern these complex systems effectively. In this way, institutional and procedural transparency generates trust in AI just as it does in scientific models or pharmaceuticals, grounded in empirical adequacy, predictive reliability, explanatory power, and theoretical coherence.

This shift toward institutional and procedural transparency is evident across global AI governance frameworks, reflecting adaptive strategies designed to manage technical opacity. In the U.S., Executive Order 14110 operationalizes accountability through standardized testing, model evaluation, provenance tracking, and inter-agency reporting, rather than disclosure of model weights or training data
[
29
]
. Complementary practices such as red-teaming, incident reporting, and model labeling illustrate a broader move toward process-based governance, enabling oversight despite persistent epistemic uncertainty. In China, accountability is enforced under the control of the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) through centralized monitoring, algorithm filing, and state-auditable security assessments aligned with national priorities
[
80
]
. The European Union’s AI Act codifies transparency and human oversight through risk-based documentation, third-party audits, and impact assessments
[
62
]
. International organizations, including the OECD and UNESCO, provide normative guidance for trustworthy, rights-respecting AI, emphasizing procedural accountability over technical interpretability
[
66
]
. Across these diverse contexts, adaptive governance mechanisms, such as regulatory sandboxes, phased approvals, and experimental oversight, emerge as practical tools to navigate the uncertainties inherent in opaque AI systems. These approaches demonstrate that transparency increasingly functions as a property of institutional design rather than as a reflection of model-level explainability.

Yet, these same institutional structures across global juridictions that operationalize transparency simultaneously embed and reproduce forms of opacity. Institutional design determines who is required to disclose what, to whom, under which evidentiary standards, and within which liability frameworks. In doing so, governance mechanisms shape the distribution of transparency, risk, accountability, and legitimacy: they define whether oversight privileges the state, market actors, or the public; whether harms are socialized or privatized; and whether legitimacy derives primarily from procedural rigor, normative commitments, or a combination of both. In this sense, transparency is not absolute but strategically conditioned by the architecture of governance itself.

In this context, technical opacity generates additional layers of opacity that shape state behavior. As stated, the first is the information opacity, which persists not simply because frontier models are difficult to interpret, but because their operations remain only partially knowable even to developers. This limits the capacity of states or firms to make credible disclosures: actors cannot be transparent about capabilities, vulnerabilities, or risks they themselves cannot reliably observe or forecast. Information asymmetries are further amplified by commercial secrecy, computing and data concentration, and security-driven restrictions. Firms with privileged access to proprietary datasets and large-scale infrastructure possess epistemic advantages they are incentivized to protect, while governments limit disclosure to manage national security, export controls, and supply-chain vulnerability. In this environment, incomplete information is not merely a strategic choice; it is structurally produced by the technology and the political economy surrounding it.

Second, normative opacity emerges because deep epistemic uncertainty inhibits the articulation of stable evaluative criteria for what counts as safe, fair, or trustworthy AI. When system behavior is probabilistic, path-dependent, and influenced by latent variables that defy ex ante specification, states lack a stable reference frame from which to derive standards. As a result, norm formation becomes contingent on political philosophy, regulatory capacity, and risk tolerance. This indeterminacy is reflected in diverging regulatory templates: ex ante vs. ex post oversight, risk-based vs. capability-based regimes, and centralized vs. decentralized governance models. Each reflects a distinct conjecture about how AI risk materializes and which institutional arrangements can contain it. The absence of global baselines allows jurisdictions to intentionally delay, dilute, or strategically blur their normative positions while awaiting greater technological maturity or the emergence of more widely accepted international standards.

Third, informational and normative uncertainties create the structural conditions for strategic opacity. States face deep ambiguity about which technological ecosystems will prevail, which regulatory paradigms will solidify into global norms, and which geopolitical alignments will secure long-term access to compute, talent, supply chains, and markets. In an environment defined by export controls, supply-chain securitization, techno-industrial alliances, and dual-use risk, premature commitments risk imposing high, and sometimes irreversible, costs, including technological lock-in, regulatory incompatibility, and political misalignment with future dominant coalitions. Under such indeterminacy, even determining what constitutes a rational course of action becomes difficult. States may therefore deliberately refrain from explicit commitments, cultivating ambiguity to preserve strategic flexibility, maintain access to multiple ecosystems, and avoid costly entanglements. This reticence creates an international environment in which actors remain uncertain not only about their own future trajectories, but also about the intentions, constraints, and likely alignments of rivals and partners. Strategic opacity thus emerges not merely as a state strategy but as a systemic feature of a geopolitical environment where technological and regulatory baselines remain fundamentally unsettled.

Opacity as Resource: Brokerage Power in A Fragmented AI Governance

3.1
Network Position, Centrality, and the Rise of Technological Swing States

If the contemporary international system is conceptualized as a hyper-connected network anchored by two highly central yet rivalrous nodes, the U.S. and China, then states situated between them occupy intermediary positions defined by dense but asymmetrical ties to both powers. Unlike earlier eras of bipolarity, in which states could shelter within relatively discrete blocs, today’s international environment constitutes a single giant component, stitched together by global supply chains, transnational data infrastructures, multilateral institutions, and overlapping diplomatic commitments
[
59
,
92
]
. This network is characterized by high connectivity, short path lengths, and multi-scalar interdependencies: even adversarial powers remain directly linked through trade cooperation, standards bodies, financial architectures, and shared vulnerabilities in technological supply chains
[
60
]
. Within such a system, influence derives less from hierarchical dominance than from structural position, especially from how actors are embedded within flows of information, resources, authority, and technological governance.

As the preceding section has shown, the structural opacity of AI does not merely undermine mode-level explainability but reshapes the architecture of global governance itself. The shift from epistemic transparency to institutional and procedural transparency is inseparable from the rise of informational, normative, and strategic opacity, each reinforcing the others. However, this uncertain and partially opaque technological and geopolitical environment is not merely a constraint. For technologically capable yet geopolitically vulnerable jurisdictions, these layered forms of opacity create new levers of influence, enabling strategic maneuvering, standard-setting participation, and selective alignment across competing ecosystems. It is within this context that the concept of technological swing states becomes analytically salient.

Originally developed in the context of U.S. domestic politics, the notion of swing states refers to actors whose positions are decisive precisely because they are not durably aligned with any single pole of power
[
44
]
. At the international level, the concept has been extended to describe states that engage with major powers without exclusive allegiance, thereby preserving strategic autonomy in foreign policy
[
76
,
32
]
. This paper adapts the concept to the U.S.–China technological rivalry by introducing technological swing states. Unlike great powers, which can unilaterally shape global technological agendas, these states occupy an intermediate position: they possess substantial technical and political capacity but lack the coercive authority to impose rules system-wide. Their influence instead derives from flexibility, brokerage, and the ability to operate across opaque and contested governance environments.

Technological swing states are defined by a constellation of interrelated attributes that together shape their strategic potential, though not all need be present simultaneously. These include robust domestic technological capacity—such as advanced research infrastructure, innovation ecosystems, and highly skilled human capital—as well as geostrategic positioning within critical technology supply chains, where they may function as production hubs, component providers, or gatekeepers of essential resources. They may also exercise control over strategic infrastructures, including satellites, submarine cables, or data centers, and exert influence within governance forums by shaping regulatory debates, technical standards, and multilateral negotiations. The specific configuration of these attributes determines the scope and form of their leverage. Positioned within this intermediate space, technological swing states wield substantial yet non-coercive power, enabling them to pursue national technological agendas while maintaining autonomy from great-power pressures.
While this paper does not primarily apply mathematical network analysis and relies on descriptive analysis and case studies, insights from network theory provide a useful conceptual lens by offering a language for conceptualizing the strategic positionality of technological swing states by analytically distinguishing different types of nodes based on their centrality within a network’s topology. Centrality measures, degree, betweenness, and eigenvector centrality
[
54
]
, capture different structural properties of nodes and, by extension, different mechanisms through which influence, constraint, and leverage may arise. Yet, these measures are not interchangeable: each encodes a specific aspect of network structure, ranging from local connectivity and path dependence to global embeddedness among influential actors

While network theory offers a wide range of centrality measures
[
54
]
, this paper focuses on degree, betweenness, and eigenvector centrality because these analytically distinct and widely validated metrics map most directly onto the three case studies. Other measures (e.g., closeness) capture vital but largely out-of-scope dimensions. In a densely interconnected international system where virtually all states maintain direct or indirect ties through overlapping networks, variation in distance-based reach provides limited additional leverage for explaining differences in strategic positioning.

Degree centrality is a purely local measure that captures the number of direct connections a node maintains
[
54
,
37
]
. High-degree nodes, thus, might derive influence from dense patterns of regional and sectoral integration rather than from proximity to the network’s global core. In technological networks, such extensive local connectivity renders these actors indispensable coordinators of infrastructures, supply-chain interdependencies, and production networks, even when they do not lie directly along the primary axis of U.S.–China rivalry. Their strategic value, therefore, lies less in contestation than in stabilization: they enhance systemic resilience, facilitate interoperability across technological components, and anchor regional ecosystems of innovation and manufacturing.

Eigenvector centrality assigns greater weight to connections with greater nodes, thereby capturing a recursive form of influence rooted in global network embeddedness rather than sheer connectivity
[
54
,
37
]
. States exhibiting high-eigenvector profiles are positioned near the network’s dominant actors (U.S. and China), where interactions among the most influential players concentrate most densely. Applied to the technological domain, such nodes tend to occupy the most contestational pathways linking the U.S. and China. Their centrality derives not from the volume of ties but from their proximity to actors that anchor rival technological ecosystems. This positional embeddedness exposes them to heightened geopolitical pressure and reputational scrutiny, which in turn incentivizes strategies oriented toward preserving optionality, maintaining modular commitments, and delaying irreversible alignment decisions. These structural incentives shape the strategic choice sets available to actors without mechanically determining their behavior.

Betweenness centrality measures the extent to which a node lies on the shortest paths connecting other nodes, thereby capturing its potential role as an intermediary or broker within the network to maintain connectedness
[
54
,
37
]
. High-betweenness nodes occupy positions that bridge otherwise weakly connected governance, regulatory, and institutional clusters. Where such brokerage is constituted through participation in high-prestige standards-setting bodies, regulatory forums, and epistemic communities, these actors can exert disproportionate influence over the diffusion of norms, the shaping of technical standards, and the mediation of governance across fragmented technological domains. Crucially, this form of influence does not depend on material dominance; it emerges instead from the capacity to translate, align, and arbitrate across competing institutional and normative regimes.

Importantly, centrality characteristics are not mutually exclusive: a node may simultaneously exhibit high degree, betweenness, and eigenvector centrality. The analysis, thus, does not impose rigid typologies but instead identifies dominant structural profiles that illuminate distinct mechanisms of strategic response among technological swing states. To this end, the following section examines South Korea, Singapore, and India, three technologically capable yet geopolitically vulnerable states pursuing distinct strategies amid intensifying U.S.–China competition. Each case exemplifies a different structural logic conceptualized in network theory. South Korea displays a high-eigenvector profile: deep simultaneous embeddedness with both rival powers, exposure to bilateral pressures along contestational pathways in semiconductors and AI, and criticality where U.S.-China competition is most intense. Singapore exhibits high-degree characteristics: influence stemming from extensive regional connectivity within Southeast Asia, where it functions as an infrastructural and coordination hub. India reflects betweenness-based leverage: its brokerage spans multiple global governance arenas, Global South leadership, Quad membership, ASEAN partnerships, G20 presidency, positioning it to connect institutional and regulatory clusters. These distinct profiles illuminate how different forms of embeddedness enable different mechanisms of strategic agency.

3.2
Delay and Hedging: The Case of South Korea

States that prefer to exploit delay, ambiguity, and hedging typically exhibit strategic profiles characterized by positioning along critical pathways through which economic interdependence, technological diffusion, and geopolitical rivalry circulate. Rather than functioning as alternative hubs, such states mediate great-power competition from positions embedded near, rather than distant from, the network core of two dominant states. Where these contestational pathways link intermediary states directly to both the U.S. and China, their strategic positioning corresponds to eigenvector-dominant profiles: influence derived from proximity to the most powerful actors in the global technology network rather than from spanning disconnected peripheries.

This structural position generates both leverage and constraint. Embedded within these transmission lines, such states cannot fully align with one power without incurring substantial economic, technological, and security costs, nor can they disengage without forfeiting influence. Their agency derives from mutual dependencies with the great powers, yet these same dependencies expose them to coercive pressures. Actors in these intermediary positions face limited autonomy: alignment choices are path-dependent, potentially triggering irreversible shifts with far-reaching consequences. Real Options Theory

Accordingly, states in these intermediary positions rationally prioritize the preservation of option value. They cultivate multiple pathways of engagement and postpone irrevocable commitments as a form of strategic insurance against technological discontinuities, shifts in the distribution of power, and the unpredictable evolution of great-power rivalry. Leveraging strategic ambiguity, in this context, is not a sign of indecision but an optimization strategy: delaying firm alignment while sending ambiguous strategic signals, these states maximize expected payoffs under uncertainty and retain the flexibility to recalibrate their strategic orientation as circumstances change. South Korea’s strategic posture in the China-U.S. technology rivalry offers a paradigmatic illustration of the delay-and-hedging mechanism characteristic of technological swing states. Situated at a structurally pivotal brokerage position in the global semiconductor and AI networks, Seoul leverages strategic opacity to transform uncertainty in frontier-technology governance into bargaining power. As geopolitical competition intensifies, the structural value of such intermediary nodes increases: the potential costs arising from their misalignment rise for both great powers, thereby altering the payoff landscape in ways that endow Seoul with enhanced yet pressured strategic leverage. Washington and Beijing must each internalize the risks of prematurely alienating a technologically indispensable actor, particularly one whose delayed commitments directly shape the competitive balance in AI hardware, computing capacity, and next-generation telecom standards.

In network-theoretic terms, South Korea’s structural profile aligns with high-eigenvector characteristics: embeddedness near both U.S. and Chinese cores, vulnerability to bilateral pressures, and criticality in domains where great-power competition is most intense.

3.3
Selective Alignment: The Case of Singapore

Selective alignment as a strategic mechanism operates through three mutually reinforcing components: active multi-homing, task-specific partnerships, and modular network positioning. Active multi-homing allows states to engage with multiple partners across economic, technological, and security domains, reducing dependence on any single power and vulnerability to coercion. Task-specific partnerships enable pragmatic, issue-focused cooperation, such as AI governance, digital trade, or cybersecurity, without implying broader ideological alignment, allowing states to leverage their comparative advantages. Modular network positioning amplifies strategic agency by situating a state as a connector within regional and global governance networks, facilitating coordination among fragmented actors, translating regional concerns into global norm-setting, and diffusing international standards back into the region.

Singapore, situated at the crossroads of Southeast Asia, exemplifies the application of selective alignment. Unlike South Korea, whose delay-and-hedging strategy reflects structural exposure to dual dependencies on the U.S. and China, Singapore pursues a proactive, engagement-oriented approach. This strategy allows the city-state to cooperate with competing powers while preserving strategic autonomy and shaping regional and global governance architectures without entering irreversible alignment pathways. It involves the calibrated adoption, adaptation, and harmonization of external standards in ways that both ensure compatibility across multiple governance blocs and enable Singapore to actively shape the trajectory of emerging norms within and outside its borders. In the context of AI, the structural opacity of advanced systems, where technical details remain inherently inaccessible, combined with the global turn toward institutional transparency, amplifies Singapore’s influence: credibility in institutional frameworks becomes a primary source of trust and leverage in international governance.

A critical factor enabling Singapore’s flexibility is the nature of its technological power. South Korea’s strengths lie in hardware-based industries, which are capital-intensive, deeply embedded in global supply chains, and path-dependent, creating high switching costs and exposing Korea to great-power pressure. In contrast, Singapore’s technological capabilities, including digital infrastructure, data governance, cybersecurity expertise, and AI standards, are modular, software-driven, and interoperable, providing greater reversibility in partnerships. Because Singapore’s tech power is derived from infrastructural centrality and governance credibility rather than fixed manufacturing dependencies, the state faces lower sovereignty costs and is less susceptible to strategic entrapment. This configuration expands Singapore’s strategic margin of maneuver, allowing it to adjust partnerships without undermining core capabilities.

Singapore’s structural power is grounded in sophisticated physical and institutional infrastructure. Its leading submarine cable network anchors Singapore as a central node in global data flows, reinforced by a dense ecosystem of hyperscale data centers operated by AWS, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure

Singapore’s selective alignment is reinforced by its dual positioning as an influential ASEAN node and a credible interlocutor in global digital governance. Through active multi-homing, the city-state engages with multiple global powers across economic, technological, and security domains. Its task-specific partnerships in digital governance and AI regulation allow Singapore to collaborate selectively with the U.S., China, and the EU without broader ideological commitments. Meanwhile, its technological assets embed Singapore deeply within global digital flows, generating material interdependencies that regional regulators, multinational firms, and neighboring states cannot bypass. As partners cannot directly verify proprietary algorithms or training data, they must rely on institutional mechanisms such as certification, auditing, and compliance, rendering Singapore’s procedural architectures a scarce and valuable source of normative authority.

Institutional transparency operationalizes selective alignment by providing auditable mechanisms without necessitating intrusive disclosure of proprietary details. Through certification schemes, independent audits, and structured risk-management protocols, Singapore signals reliability across diverse governance regimes. This approach allows it to bridge multiple standards ecosystems simultaneously: OECD ethical guidelines, ISO technical standards, and Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN)-oriented data-protection rules are integrated into a coherent domestic framework, ensuring compatibility with U.S., EU, and regional regulatory regimes. By positioning institutional transparency as a functional substitute for unattainable model-level disclosure, Singapore effectively transforms opacity into a bargaining resource, making its frameworks indispensable to actors seeking credible oversight in a fragmented regulatory environment.

The proactive character of selective alignment distinguishes Singapore’s approach from defensive hedging. Rather than deferring commitments, Singapore aligns selectively and incrementally with multiple blocs to shape outcomes in its favor. The strategic efficacy of selective alignment manifests on two primary fronts. First, it consolidates strategic autonomy by preventing overdependence on any single power bloc while ensuring interoperability across multiple regimes. Second, it enhances Singapore’s regional influence by positioning it as a mediator of standards and practices within ASEAN and between Asia and the transatlantic governance space. By serving as a platform for interoperability and a trusted source of institutional transparency, Singapore increases its bargaining power and ensures that its small size does not translate into marginalization. Embedding accountability and trust into institutional procedures, the city-state transforms uncertainty into a durable source of authority and influence. Unlike passive delay strategies, selective alignment leverages opacity to engage, negotiate, and shape outcomes, positioning Singapore as both a regional governance hub and a global norm entrepreneur. This case illustrates that even small but technologically capable states can reconfigure asymmetries in the global AI order into sustained sources of agency and influence.

3.4
Normative Intermediation: The Case of India

India’s approach to AI governance exemplifies the strategic logic of normative intermediation, which operates not through unilateral rule-export or tight policy convergence but through translation, discourse-shaping, and multilateral brokerage. Whereas Singapore’s selective alignment derives influence from interoperable rule design, verifiable institutional transparency, and modular regulatory adoption, India’s normative intermediation works at a different level of the governance game. It leverages India’s embeddedness within diverse diplomatic and epistemic networks to redefine the focal points, interpretive frames, and payoff structures that govern the behavior of states and firms in a technologically uncertain environment. If Singapore reduces information opacity through auditability and procedural clarity, India addresses a deeper form: normative opacity, the ambiguity surrounding what the rules ought to be, which principles should matter, and how competing governance models should be reconciled. This distinction is fundamental. Selective alignment stabilizes expectations around existing standards; normative intermediation creates, translates, and reweights standards themselves.

This divergence of strategies corresponds to differences in structural profiles that network theory helps illuminate. Singapore’s authority stems from infrastructural centrality, as a logistics, data-hosting, and regulatory hub embedded in dense regional supply chains, allowing it to export trusted procedures as compatibility layers across jurisdictions. India, by contrast, displays multi-network embeddedness: simultaneous participation in governance communities. India is simultaneously a major node in Global South diplomatic circuits, an increasingly indispensable partner in U.S.-led technology coalitions such as the Quad and iCET, a participant in ASEAN-centred digital forums, and a leader in broad multilateral platforms like the G20. Its influence does not arise from occupying a chokepoint in a single network but from bridging heterogeneous governance communities whose normative expectations diverge sharply. This bridging capacity explains why India, unlike Singapore, can engage in normative entrepreneurship, using its large epistemic communities—AI talent, global diaspora, digital-infrastructure expertise—to translate and mediate between alternative governance paradigms.

Indeed, normative intermediation corresponds not to Singapore’s signaling-based creation of interoperability equilibria, but to a mechanism of bargaining mediation and preference transformation in multilateral settings. India alters the strategic landscape by shifting how actors define the problem itself. Central to this mechanism is India’s sophisticated use of normative opacity. In environments where large AI models, algorithmic risks, and cross-border data flows remain technically and conceptually opaque, states often lack stable expectations about long-term consequences. India exploits this uncertainty by proposing focal normative frames—conceptual templates that reduce interpretive ambiguity and reorient the discourse. By promoting frames such as “AI for All” or “AI for Development,” India essentially reweights the payoff matrix for other actors. When a normative frame becomes focal, powerful states must incorporate it into negotiations to avoid appearing obstructive or indifferent to developmental equity. In other words, India transforms ambiguity into leverage: by defining what counts as an acceptable objective in global governance, it influences what counts as a feasible agreement.

The 2023 G20 New Delhi Summit represents a clear example of this dynamic

After New Delhi, major actors began referencing development-sensitive approaches as part of the global AI conversation, even when negotiating issues such as cross-border data governance or AI risk evaluation. This discursive shift is best understood as the creation of a new focal point, one that powerful states had to internalize to maintain legitimacy in multilateral arenas. India’s forthcoming 2026 Global AI Summit is poised to extend this normative momentum, reinforcing the country’s role as a central broker in the evolving governance network.

India’s brokerage is reinforced by its domestic governance architecture, which provides procedural authority without requiring full algorithmic transparency. Through the National AI Strategy, sector-specific ethical guidelines, and hybrid public–private audit mechanisms, India offers credible frameworks for risk assessment, certification, and compliance translation

Crucially, they do not seek to eliminate model opacity; instead, they make India’s interpretation of that opacity indispensable to cross-border collaboration. As network theory would predict, India increases its centrality not by controlling information but by controlling the interface through which information and norms are interpreted.

India’s role in the Quad’s AI Working Group demonstrates this interpretive leverage. While cooperating with the U.S., Japan, and Australia to articulate principles for trustworthy AI

Similarly, through engagement with ASEAN digital initiatives, India has advanced the idea of ethical interoperability—a concept blending elements of GDPR-style protections, OECD principles, and local innovation needs. These efforts reduce normative friction and lower transaction costs, enabling convergence without requiring strict alignment.

India’s technological trajectory further reinforces its capacity for normative intermediation. Although it is not yet a superpower in advanced hardware or foundation model development, India is a global leader in software services, AI engineering talent, and digital public infrastructure. These capabilities generate epistemic legitimacy, the authority to interpret and mediate technical issues in ways credible to multiple audiences. Additionally, India’s strategic shift toward closer cooperation with the U.S.—manifest in initiatives such as iCET, semiconductor partnerships through the CHIPS Act, and the INDUS-X defense innovation ecosystem—strengthens its position in U.S.-aligned tech networks. Yet India simultaneously remains the pre-eminent political voice in the Global South, ensuring that its normative proposals have a broad and receptive audience. This dual embeddedness allows India to align with Western technology governance without forfeiting its autonomy or its role as a representative of developing states.

Compared with Singapore, the contrast is now clear. Selective alignment is a technically adept, operationally precise strategy centred on harmonization and accountability. It derives influence from the credibility of domestic institutions and the ability to provide stable procedural templates. But it remains structurally bounded: it scales outward from Southeast Asia toward global influence only when adoption evidence accumulates. Normative intermediation, by contrast, is a political-discursive strategy. It aims not to harmonize rules but to redefine what the rules are about, building coalitions across ideational and geopolitical divides. Its authority comes not from infrastructural centrality but from the ability to reshape conceptual frameworks, reduce normative opacity, and propose focal points that multilateral negotiations must acknowledge, which require a higher betweenness for connecting several blocs. The strategic payoff is significant. Through normative intermediation, India converts systemic fragmentation—divergent regulatory models, opaque technologies, heterogeneous preferences—into a source of power. By shaping interpretive frames and mediating between governance paradigms, it exerts influence disproportionate to its material capabilities and positions itself as a central node in the emerging architecture of global AI governance.

Conclusion and Limitation

This paper examines how technological swing states exercise strategic agency within an increasingly fragmented and polycentric global AI governance landscape. These states convert structural opacity into strategic leverage and use institutional transparency as a functional substitute for otherwise unattainable technical disclosure, transforming uncertainty into procedural and positional power. South Korea employs calibrated delay and policy hedging to maintain flexibility, Singapore pursues selective alignment to build institutional credibility and attract transnational cooperation, and India engages in normative intermediation to broker standards across divergent governance regimes. Together, these patterns illustrate how swing states can institutionalize influence through intermediary governance, redistributing technological authority away from traditional centers of bilateral dominance toward a more networked and diffused mode of rule-making. Positioned at the nexus of global supply chains, infrastructural networks, and multilateral forums, these states emerge not as passive rule-takers but as active rule-brokers, shaping the management of opacity, the operationalization of transparency, and the legitimacy of global AI governance.

Importantly, this analysis does not assume that such outcomes necessarily result from fully articulated, ex ante strategic designs or coherent self-perceptions of agency. Rather, it identifies mechanisms through which influence is exercised regardless of intentionality, as states operate within, and adapt to, the constraints and opportunities generated by their structural positions. Regardless of how policymakers themselves interpret their actions, these states exert influence through the interplay of technological opacity and institutional transparency, whether via strategic ambiguity, selective alignment, or normative intermediation. Accordingly, the argument does not rest on the assumption that network centrality automatically translates into leverage or deliberate strategic choice. Instead, the paper primarily operates through a logic of fit between structural position, institutional practice, and observable governance effects, rather than advancing a fully specified causal theory of strategic behavior.

Nevertheless, this study is limited in both scope and generalizability. Its analytical focus on the three illustrative cases provides comparative insight but remains necessarily selective, offering an initial exploration rather than an exhaustive account of each state’s strategic environment. Moreover, by centering on sovereign states as the primary units of analysis, the paper underexamines the role of supranational or interstate actors that often operate as cohesive regulatory blocs, most notably the European Union, whose internal coordination and collective regulatory capacity may condition or constrain the room for maneuver available to individual swing states. This state-centric focus also excludes other technologically emergent actors such as Brazil, Indonesia, or the United Arab Emirates, whose trajectories may diverge significantly due to distinct regional institutional frameworks, political economies, or technological endowments. In addition, the analysis gives limited attention to nonstate actors, including multinational corporations, transnational standard-setting bodies, and civil society coalitions, which increasingly shape both the operational and normative boundaries of global AI governance.

Future research should therefore broaden both the empirical and theoretical reach of this inquiry. Empirically, extending the analysis to additional cases and regional contexts would allow for deeper process-tracing of how intermediary positions are formed, sustained, or eroded over time, and how different configurations of market structure, regulatory capacity, and societal demand condition the governance roles of swing states. Theoretically, this study should be understood as a contribution to case-based analysis and semi-formalization, rather than as the articulation of a fully developed network theory. The central concepts employed, such as centrality, opacity, and intermediation, are intended as analytical lenses for comparison and dialogue with existing theories, rather than as a foundational framework in their own right. Further work is therefore required to refine these concepts into testable propositions, specify causal mechanisms more explicitly, and integrate insights from transnational governance, political economy of technology, and science and technology studies. Longitudinal analyses, in particular, could trace how shifting multipolar alignments, evolving AI alliances, and changing accountability pressures reshape the capacity of swing states to sustain influence amid accelerating technological and geopolitical change.
