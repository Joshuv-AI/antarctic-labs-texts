Between Markov and restriction: Two more monads on categories for relations
Cipriano Junior Cioffo, Fabio Gadducci, Davide Trotta

Abstract
The study of categories abstracting the structural properties of relations has been extensively developed over the years, resulting in a rich and diverse body of work. A previous paper offered a survey providing a modern and comprehensive presentation of these “categories for relations” as instances of gs-monoidal categories, showing how they arise as Kleisli categories of suitable symmetric monoidal monads. The end result was a taxonomy that organised numerous related concepts in the literature, including in particular Markov and restriction categories. This paper further enriches the taxonomy: it proposes two categories that are once more instances of gs-monoidal categories, yet more abstract than Markov and restriction categories. They are characterised by an axiomatic notion of mass and domain of an arrow, the latter one of the key ingredient of restriction categories, which generalises the domain of partial functions. The paper then introduces mass and domain preserving monads, proving that the associated Kleisli categories in fact preserve the corresponding equations and that these monads arise naturally for the categories of semiring-weighted relations.



Keywords:
String diagrams, categories for relations, gs-monoidal categories, restriction categories, Markov categories, semiring-weighted monads.

Introduction

In recent years, the study of categories abstracting the properties of relations has been extensively developed both in mathematics and computer science, making it difficult
to identify a single basic notion that captures all the relevant aspects of these categories. However, a rich part of the literature is built on the notion of symmetric
monoidal category, which represents the basic data of a category abstracting the set-theoretic product of relations, that is a leading example of monoidal product that
is not cartesian.

Taking this fact as starting point, in

The taxonomy included some characterisation results for families of monads, for example illustrating that the Kleisli category of an affine monad on
a Markov category is also Markov, or that the same property holds for relevant monads on restriction categories, generalising the well-known fact that the Kleisli category
of a commutative monad on a cartesian category is symmetric monoidal. Given the relevance of Kleisli categories for the theory of computation, alongside
the seminal work by Moggi

This paper further extends the survey
[

Its starting point lies in the serendipitous discovery that the Kleisli category of an affine monad on a restriction category satisfies one the key axiom of
restriction categories, which is called the domain equation in the literature, and which so far had never been studied in isolation.
We thus identified two more categories laying between of gs-monoidal, Markov and restriction categories,
sharing the same structural arrows, hence suitable as models for categories of relations.
More precisely, we introduce what we call
mass

More precisely, in a gs-monoidal category we have the right structure to define the

Domain categories are those gs-monoidal categories where the domain equation holds for every arrow.

We thus end up with the taxonomy below, which now includes mass, domain and weakly Markov categories, further proving that Markov categories
are precisely those that are both mass and weakly Markov categories. Furthermore, we consider mass and domain preserving monads, showing
under which conditions the associated Kleisli categories lift the structure of the base categories.

5
Looking at Kleisli categories

It is now a well-known result that a symmetric monoidal monad
T
on a Markov category
\mathcal{C}
is affine if and only if the Kleisli category
\mathcal{C}_{T}
is again a Markov category,
and the same occurs for relevant monads with respect to restrictions categories.

Now, let us say that a symmetric monoidal monad is domain/unital domain/mass preserving if the underlying functor is so.
We can generalise the result above for restriction (hence also for cartesian) categories.

We first provide a simple, yet lengthy, technical lemma. The proof is in Appendix
B

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a restriction category and

a symmetric monoidal monad on it. For every arrow

of

\operatorname{dom}(f);^{\sharp}f=\nabla_{X}^{\sharp};^{\sharp}(f\otimes^{\sharp}f);^{\sharp}(\operatorname{id}\otimes^{\sharp}!^{\sharp}_{Y});^{\sharp}(\rho^{-1})^{\sharp}=f;\nabla_{TY};(T(\operatorname{id}_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}));c_{Y,I};T(\rho^{-1})

\operatorname{dom}(f);^{\sharp}\operatorname{mass}(f)=\nabla_{X}^{\sharp};^{\sharp}(f\otimes^{\sharp}f);^{\sharp}(!^{\sharp}_{Y}\otimes^{\sharp}!^{\sharp}_{Y});^{\sharp}(\rho^{-1})^{\sharp}=f;\nabla_{TY};(T(!_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}));c_{I,I};T(\rho^{-1})

We now move to the key result concerning domain presrvation.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a restriction category and
T
a symmetric monoidal monad on
\mathcal{C}
. Then
T
is domain preserving if and only if
\mathcal{C}_{T}
is a domain category.

Proof.

Let us consider an arrow

in
\mathcal{C}_{T}
.
By Lemma
5.1
, we have that

\operatorname{dom}(f);^{\sharp}f=\nabla_{X}^{\sharp};^{\sharp}(f\otimes^{\sharp}f);^{\sharp}(\operatorname{id}\otimes^{\sharp}!^{\sharp}_{Y});^{\sharp}(\rho^{-1})^{\sharp}=f;\nabla_{TY};(T(\operatorname{id}_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}));c_{Y,I};T(\rho^{-1}).

Now if
T
is domain preserving then

\nabla_{TY};(T(\operatorname{id}_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}));c_{Y,I}=\nabla_{TY};c_{Y,Y};(T(\operatorname{id}_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}))=T(\rho)

Hence, we have that

\operatorname{dom}(f);^{\sharp}f=f

Viceversa, if
\mathcal{C}_{T}
is a domain category, i.e.
\operatorname{dom}(f);^{\sharp}f=f
for every arrow
f
, in particular, we will have that the equation holds for

of
\mathcal{C}_{T}

\operatorname{dom}(\operatorname{id}_{FY});^{\sharp}\operatorname{id}_{FY}=\nabla_{TY};(T(\operatorname{id}_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}));c_{Y,I};T(\rho^{-1})=\operatorname{id}_{FY}

and hence that

\nabla_{TY};(T(\operatorname{id}_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}));c_{Y,I}=\nabla_{TY};c_{Y,Y};(T(\operatorname{id}_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}))=T(\rho).

Then, we can conclude that the monad is domain preserving.
∎

The result above implies as a corollary the starting point of our investigation, as recalled in the introduction.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a restriction category and
T
an affine monad. Then
\mathcal{C}_{T}
is a domain category.

Now we move to the key result concerning mass preservation.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a restriction category and
T

\mathcal{C}

Proof.

By Lemma

\operatorname{dom}(f);^{\sharp}f;^{\sharp}!_{Y}^{\sharp}=f;^{\sharp}!_{Y}^{\sharp}

6
A case study: weighted relations

In
[
5
, Section 2.3.2]
we already tackled the issue of characterising instances of the monad semiring
such that the associated Keisli categories are either Markov of restriction categories.
We recall here those results, further showing how the newly introduced monads can be characterised
in terms of the properties of the underlying semiring.

6.1
Some established facts

Consider a semiring
(M,\oplus,\odot,0,1)
. It is easy to check that
there exists a functor
\mathcal{M}:\mathbf{Set}\to\mathbf{Set}
that sends every set
X
to

\mathcal{M}(X)=\left\{h:X\to M\ |\ h\ \text{has finite support}\right\}

where finite support means that
h(x)\neq 0
for a finite number of elements
x\in X
, and every function
f:X\to Y
to the function
\tilde{f}:\mathcal{M}(X)\to\mathcal{M}(Y)
which sends every
M
-valued function
h:X\to M
with finite support
to

\tilde{f}(h)(y)=\underset{x\in f^{-1}(y)}{\bigoplus}h(x)

Explicitly, the image of the composition
g\circ f
of
f:X\to Y
and
g:Y\to Z
is

\widetilde{g\circ f}(h)(z)=\tilde{g}(\underset{x\in f^{-1}(y)}{\bigoplus}h(x))=\underset{y\in g^{-1}(z)}{\bigoplus}(\underset{x\in f^{-1}(y)}{\bigoplus}h(x))

Recall that
(\mathbf{Set},\times,\{\bullet\})
is cartesian monoidal with respect to the direct product.
The above functor is lax symmetric monoidal with respect to that monoidal structure, with the obvious
coherence arrows

\psi_{X,Y}:\mathcal{M}(X)\times\mathcal{M}(Y)\to\mathcal{M}(X\times Y)\qquad\psi_{0}:\{\bullet\}\to\mathcal{M}(\{\bullet\})

given by
\psi_{X,Y}(h,k)(x,y)=h(x)\odot k(y)
and
\psi_{0}(\bullet)(\bullet)=1
.

Finally, note that there are two additional endofunctors
\mathcal{M}_{r}
and
\mathcal{M}_{a}
on
\mathbf{Set}

that are both lax symmetric monoidal with respect to the same monoidal structure
(\mathbf{Set},\times,\{\bullet\})
. These are defined as follows

\mathcal{M}_{r}(X)=\left\{h:X\to M\ |\ h\ \text{has support at most one and is idempotent}\right\}

\mathcal{M}_{a}(X)=\left\{h:X\to M\ |\ h\ \text{has finite support and is normalised}\right\}

where idempotent means that
\forall x\in X.\,h(x)=h(x)\odot h(x)
and normalised that
\bigoplus_{x\in X}h(x)=1
.
What is noteworthy is that

\mathcal{M}_{r}
is relevant and
\mathcal{M}_{a}
is affine,
as it is easily checked out noting that
\widetilde{!_{X}}(h)(\bullet)=\bigoplus_{x\in X}h(x)
and

\widetilde{\nabla_{X}}(h)(\langle x,y\rangle)=h(x)
if
x=y
, and
0
otherwise.

Remark 6.1
.

The setting allows to recover various relational structures in the literature.
If e.g.
M
is the Boolean semiring
\{0,1\}
, then
\mathcal{M}
is the lax symmetric monoidal
functor
\mathcal{P}
associating to
X
its finite subsets, and it is neither relevant nor affine.
The relevant functor
\mathcal{P}_{r}
is restricted to subsets of at most one element,
while the affine functor
\mathcal{P}_{a}
is restricted to subsets with at least one element.
If
M
is the semiring of positive real numbers
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, then
\mathcal{M}
is the monad of
unnormalised probability distributions, the affine functor
\mathcal{M}_{a}

is the monad of normalised probability distributions, while the relevant functor

\mathcal{M}_{r}
associates to
X
the functions with codomain
\{0,1\}
, behaving
essentially as
\mathcal{P}_{r}
.

6.2
Characterisations for domain and mass preservation

Consider two more endofunctors
\mathcal{M}_{m}
and
\mathcal{M}_{d}
on
\mathbf{Set}
,
all lax symmetric monoidal with respect to
(\mathbf{Set},\times,\{\bullet\})

and defined as follows

\mathcal{M}_{m}(X)=\left\{h:X\to M\ |\ h\ \text{has finite support and is image-idempotent}\right\}

\mathcal{M}_{d}(X)=\left\{h:X\to M\ |\ h\ \text{has finite support and is absorptive}\right\}

where image-idempotent means that

\bigoplus_{x\in X}h(x)=\bigoplus_{x\in X}h(x)\odot\bigoplus_{x\in X}h(x)

and absorptive means that
\forall x\in X.\,h(x)=h(x)\odot\bigoplus_{x\in X}h(x)
.
Note also that for the singleton
\{\bullet\}
we have

\mathcal{M}_{u}(\{\bullet\})=\mathcal{M}_{d}(\{\bullet\})
.

Clearly, we have that
\mathcal{M}_{r}
is a sub-monad of
\mathcal{M}_{m}
,
meaning that
\mathcal{M}_{r}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{m}(X)

and the coherence arrows are defined in terms of the same formulas.
Analogously,

\mathcal{M}_{a}
is a sub-monad of
\mathcal{M}_{d}
,
which in turn is a sub-monad of
\mathcal{M}_{m}
.
Note however that, differently from
\mathcal{M}_{r}
and
\mathcal{M}_{a}
, the definitions above do not impose restrictions either on the
cardinality of the support (except finiteness) or on the value of
\bigoplus_{x\in X}h(x)
.
In fact,
\mathcal{M}_{m}
and
\mathcal{M}_{d}
are symmetric monoidal monads,
yet they are neither affine nor relevant.

Let us now look at the various arrows involved in the definitions of mass and domain preservation.
By definition,

\widetilde{\rho^{-1}_{X}}(h)(x)=h(x,\bullet)
,
hence the canonical semigroup arrow for
\mathcal{M}(X)
is given by

\Delta_{\mathcal{M}(X)}=\widetilde{\rho^{-1}_{X}}\circ\widetilde{\operatorname{id}_{X}\otimes!_{X}}\circ\psi_{X,X}

for

\Delta_{\mathcal{M}(X)}(h,k)(x)=h(x)\odot\bigoplus_{x\in X}k(x)
.
Thus we now have that

\Delta_{\mathcal{M}(X)}\circ\nabla_{\mathcal{M}(X)}(h)(x)=h(x)\odot\bigoplus_{x\in X}h(x)

which means that
\mathcal{M}_{d}
is a domain preserving monad. Should
X=\{\bullet\}
, we would have

\Delta_{\mathcal{M}(\{\bullet\})}\circ\nabla_{\mathcal{M}(\{\bullet\})}(h)(\bullet)=h(\bullet)\odot h(\bullet).

So,
\mathcal{M}_{m}
is unital domain preserving. Hence, since
\mathbf{Set}
is a cartesian monoidal category,
it is also mass preserving.

Remark 6.2
.

Let us now look at which properties of the semiring
(M,\oplus,\odot,0,1)
guarantee the conditions of idempotency and/or absorptiveness for all functions

h:X\to M
, so that the corresponding functors above coincides with
\mathcal{M}
.
Such characterisation is not possible for
\mathcal{M}_{r}
and
\mathcal{M}_{a}
, given the conditions
they must satisfy on cardinality and values.

Now, as for
\mathcal{M}_{m}
, it means to require M to be idempotent, i.e. such that
a\odot a=a
for all
a\in M
.
As for
\mathcal{M}_{d}
,
h
being absorptive
can be stated from
a\odot(a\oplus b)=a
for all
a,b\in M
, which directly implies that
\odot
is idempotent and that

1\oplus b=b
for all
b\in M
, the latter property called in fact absorptiveness in semiring literature
[
17
]
. Finally, note that since
\odot

distributes over
\oplus
in

M
, it also holds that
\oplus
is idempotent and that
a\oplus(a\odot b)=a
, which means that
M
is
a distributive lattice.

As a consequence, the two monads coincide if
M
is a distributive lattice.

Example 6.3
.

Consider now the Boolean semiring from Remark
6.1
.
It is a distributive lattice, so that even if the lax symmetric monoidal functor
\mathcal{P}

associating to
X
its finite subsets is neither relevant nor affine, it is domain preserving.
Thus, also the associated Kleisli category of relations
is domain preserving.

Moreover, if
M
is either the semiring
([0,1],\text{max},\text{min},0,1)
or the semiring
([0,1],\text{max},\cdot,0,1)

one obtains Golubtsov’s categories of
fuzzy information transformer
, see
[
18
]
.
The former is a distributive lattice, hence the monad
\mathcal{M}
is domain preserving.

6.3
About weakly affine functors

The pattern developed in the previous sections can be instantiated to obtain a large class
of weakly affine monads, extending the key example in
[
13
, Example 3.7]
.

\mathcal{M}_{i}(X)=\left\{h:X\to M\ |\ h\ \text{has finite support at least one and is invertible}\right\}

where invertible means that
\forall x\in X.\,(h(x)=0)\vee(\exists m\in M.h(x)\odot m=1)
:
such
m
is clearly unique and we denote it by
\widehat{h(x)}
.
Also,

is lax symmetric monoidal with respect to

is not isomorphic to

Now, for the arrow
\iota_{\mathcal{M}_{i}(I)}(I):\mathcal{M}_{i}(I)\to\mathcal{M}_{i}(I)

\Delta_{\mathcal{M}(I)}\circ(\operatorname{id}_{\mathcal{M}_{i}(I)}\otimes\iota_{\mathcal{M}_{i}(I)})\circ\nabla_{\mathcal{M}(I)}(h)(\{\bullet\})=h(\{\bullet\})\odot\iota_{\mathcal{M}_{i}(I)}(h)(\{\bullet\})

\mathrel{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{!}}_{\mathcal{M}(I)}\circ!_{\mathcal{M}(I)}(h)(\{\bullet\})=1

which is ensured by defining

.

Remark 6.4
.

The condition on being invertible can be dropped if
M
is actually a semifield, i.e. if each element except

has a multiplicative inverse.
In that case,
\mathcal{M}_{i}(X)
includes all the functions of finite support with domain

, except for the empty function.
This is e. g. the case for the semiring of positive real numbers
\mathbb{R}^{+}
in Remark

monad of unnormalised, non empty probability distributions,

Also, note that if

is a semifield, then
\mathcal{M}_{a}
is a submonad of
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.

7
Conclusions and further works

The present work builds on the results presented in
[
5
]
,
where it was proposed a taxonomy for some variants of symmetric monoidal categories,
such as Markov and restriction categories, which in recent years had been introduced
with computational and graphical aims.
The survey was further complemented by a series of results concerning the
structures of Kleisli categories, putting some order also on those
variants of (enriched) symmetric monoidal monads proposed in the literature.

This paper strives for further extending the taxonomy, including the already
known weakly Markov categories and the new domain and
mass preserving categories, and establishing a connection with the corresponding
monads and their Kleisli categories.

Domain and mass preserving categories are instances of gs-monoidal categories, hence part of the taxonomy,
yet they are intermediate between Markov and restriction categories, making more precise
the underlying algebraic structure in terms of special and Hopf monoid objects.
Indeed, an outcome of such description is the characterisation of Markov categories via weakly
Markov and unital domain preserving categories.

Our work thus partly fulfils one of the promises in
[
5
]
: to further
investigate “alternative notions of Markov categories
[
10
,
13
]

and affine monads
[
19
,
13
]
, aimed
at distilling a categorical presentation of probability theory, see e.g.
[
20
]
.”
Besides the investigation into partial Markov categories
[
10
,
25
]
,
future threads of research include the combinatorial presentation of
free gs-monoidal categories in terms of classes of graphs, as shown for string
diagrams and hyper-graphs in e.g.
[
3
]
. Another line of work concerns the extension
of the taxonomy towards traced gs-monoidal categories
[
21
,
9
]
. And finally,
the exploration of completeness theorems for functorial semantics, see e.g.
[
14
]
.

Appendix B
Proof of Lemma
5.1

Let
f:X\to Y
be an arrow in
\mathcal{C}_{T}
, namely an arrow
f:X\to T(Y)
in
\mathcal{C}
. Let us consider the composition

\nabla_{X}^{\sharp};^{\sharp}(f\otimes^{\sharp}f);^{\sharp}(\operatorname{id}\otimes^{\sharp}!^{\sharp}_{Y})

in
\mathcal{C}_{T}
that is equal to

\nabla_{X};\eta_{X\times X};T(f\times f);T(c_{Y,Y});\mu_{Y\times Y};T(\eta_{Y}\times(!_{Y};\eta_{I}));T(c_{Y,I});\mu_{Y\times I}

By naturality of
\eta
, we have that this composition is equal to

\nabla_{X};(f\times f)\eta_{TY\times TY};T(c_{Y,Y})\mu_{Y\times Y};T(\eta_{Y}\times(!_{Y};\eta_{I}));T(c_{Y,I});\mu_{Y\times I}

and this is equal to

\nabla_{X};(f\times f);c_{Y,Y};\eta_{T(Y\times Y)};\mu_{Y\times Y};T(\eta_{Y}\times(!_{Y};\eta_{I}));T(c_{Y,I});\mu_{Y\times I}

that is

\nabla_{X};(f\times f);c_{Y,Y};T(\eta_{Y}\times(!_{Y};\eta_{I}));T(c_{Y,I});\mu_{Y\times I}

This is equal to

\nabla_{X};(f\times f);c_{Y,Y};T(\operatorname{id}_{Y}\times!_{Y});T((\eta_{Y}\times\eta_{I});c_{Y,I});\mu_{Y\times I}

Now, since
(\eta_{Y}\times\eta_{I});c_{Y,I}=\eta_{Y\times I}
(because the monad is symmetric monoidal), this is equal to

\nabla_{X};(f\times f);c_{Y,Y};T(\operatorname{id}_{Y}\times!_{Y})

but now
c_{Y,Y};T(\operatorname{id}_{Y}\times!_{Y})=(T(\operatorname{id}_{T})\times T(!_{Y}));c_{Y,I}
, hence

\nabla_{X};(f\times f);(T(\operatorname{id}_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}));c_{Y,I}

that is

f;\nabla_{TY};(T(\operatorname{id}_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}));c_{Y,I}.

Hence, we have proved that

\nabla_{X}^{\sharp};^{\sharp}(f\otimes^{\sharp}f);^{\sharp}(\operatorname{id}\otimes^{\sharp}!^{\sharp}_{Y})=f;\nabla_{TY};(T(\operatorname{id}_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}));c_{Y,I}

and hence, since
(\rho^{-1}_{Y})^{\sharp}=\rho^{-1}_{Y};\eta_{Y}
, we can conclude that

\operatorname{dom}(f);^{\sharp}f=\nabla_{X}^{\sharp};^{\sharp}(f\otimes^{\sharp}f);^{\sharp}(\operatorname{id}\otimes^{\sharp}!^{\sharp}_{Y});^{\sharp}(\rho^{-1})^{\sharp}=f;\nabla_{TY};(T(\operatorname{id}_{Y})\times T(!_{Y}));c_{Y,I};T(\rho^{-1}).

For the second statement, we can use a similar argument, but now we consider the composition
\nabla_{X}^{\sharp};^{\sharp}(f\otimes^{\sharp}f);^{\sharp}(!^{\sharp}_{Y}\otimes^{\sharp}!^{\sharp}_{Y});^{\sharp}(\rho^{-1})^{\sharp}

in
\mathcal{C}_{T}
that is equal to

\nabla_{X};\eta_{X\times X};T(f\times f);T(c_{Y,Y});\mu_{Y\times Y};T((!_{Y};\eta_{Y})\times(!_{Y};\eta_{I}));T(c_{Y,I});\mu_{I\times I}

By naturality of
\eta
, the above is equal to

\nabla_{X};(f\times f)\eta_{TY\times TY};T(c_{Y,Y})\mu_{Y\times Y};T((!_{Y};\eta_{Y})\times(!_{Y};\eta_{I}));T(c_{Y,I});\mu_{I\times I}

and this is equal to

\nabla_{X};(f\times f);c_{Y,Y};\eta_{T(Y\times Y)};\mu_{Y\times Y};T((!_{Y};\eta_{Y})\times(!_{Y};\eta_{I}));T(c_{Y,I});\mu_{I\times I}

that is

\nabla_{X};(f\times f);c_{Y,Y};T(!_{Y}\times!_{Y});T(\eta_{I}\times\eta_{I});T(c_{I,I});\mu_{I\times I}

Now, since
(\eta_{I}\times\eta_{I});c_{I,I}=\eta_{I\times I}
(because the monad is symmetric monoidal), this is equal to

\nabla_{X};(f\times f);c_{Y,Y};T(!_{Y}\times!_{Y})

which is equal to

\nabla_{X};(f\times f);T(!_{Y})\times T(!_{Y});c_{I,I}

the statement now follows by the hypothesis that
\mathcal{C}
is a restriction category.
