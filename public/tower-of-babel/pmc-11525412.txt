Sheeppox virus genome sequences from the European outbreaks in Spain, Bulgaria, and Greece in 2022–2024
Authors: Floris C Breman, Andy Haegeman, Wannes Philips, Nina Krešić, Stefan Hoffman, Sigrid C J De Keersmaecker, Nancy H C Roosens, Montserrat Agüero, Ruben Villalba, Aleksandra Miteva, Emiliya Ivanova, Konstantia E Tasioudi, Serafeim C Chaintoutis, Aikaterini Kirtzalidou, Nick De Regge
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn 2022-2024, three outbreaks of sheeppox (SPP) were reported in the European Union. These occurred in Spain, Bulgaria, and Greece and had serious economic consequences due to animal losses and trade restrictions. Five sheeppox virus (SPPV) whole-genome sequences (WGSs) were determined from samples collected during these outbreaks and analyzed in the context of all other published WGSs. Sheeppox virus strains can be divided in two, or possibly three, main groups. The isolates from the recent outbreaks belong to clade A2, which includes strains historically circulating in the Middle East and Northern Africa. Sequence divergence was low among the isolates that caused the recent European outbreaks. These results highlight the need for more regular and dense surveillance in under-sampled areas and the use of WGS to increase the chance of pinpointing the origin of an introduction, identifying potential introduction routes, and providing insights into SPPV evolution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00705-024-06165-6.

Introduction

The taxonomy of the genus Capripoxvirus (family Poxviridae, subfamily Chordopoxvirinae) is well established, and three species are currently recognized by the ICTV: Capripoxvirus goatpox (goatpox virus; GTPV), Capripoxvirus lumpyskinpox (lumpy skin disease virus; LSDV), and Capripox sheeppox (sheeppox virus; SPPV), whose members are relatively easy to distinguish based on genomic data [1–4]. The genome of capripoxviruses is composed of a single linear dsDNA molecule with a length of ~151 kb. Little is known about the functions of the proteins encoded in the SPPV genome, and the gene annotation is based on what is known from LSDV, which in turn is based on what has been studied using variola virus [5], which belongs to a different genus of the family Poxviridae (Orthopoxvirus).

Sheeppox has been declared a notifiable disease by the World Organization for Animal Health (WOAH) because of its infectiousness and impact on animal health. The disease leads to significant economic losses due to reduced productivity, animal losses, and trade restrictions. Both morbidity and mortality rates for SPPV in sheep (Ovis aries) vary greatly and depend on a number of factors such as breed, age, health condition, and ecological factors [6], among others. However, mortality ranges from ~1% to almost 100% in naïve populations or in young animals [7, 8]. Furthermore, European sheep breeds are more susceptible than African and Asian breeds [9]. SPPV typically spreads via direct animal contact [9], but it can also be transmitted via indirect contact with fomites [7]. Transmission by vectors has also been described [7], but this appears to be of less importance in SPPV epidemiology [10–13].

Sheeppox virus is thought to have been around for more than 2000 years and has historically been reported in Asia and Europe [14, 15]. In contrast, reports of sheeppox in Africa are more recent, with the first being in the 1970s, although it could go back further, to the 1920s [16]. Whether this lack of information is due to a more recent history of the disease in Africa or a lack of scientific reporting is not known. In the 20th century, SPPV seems to have disappeared from Europe [9] due to eradication efforts, although no records or scientific literature could be found for countries declaring SPPV eradicated. Currently, sheeppox is endemic in Asia, Africa north of the equator, the Middle East, and Turkey [13]. Some of these regions border the European Union (EU), and this poses a permanent threat for introduction of the virus into the EU, as evidenced by the occasional outbreaks in previous decades in Greece and Bulgaria. [11, 13, 17, 18].

For the first time since the eradication of the disease in 1968, Spain reported a sheeppox outbreak in the province of Granada on September 19, 2022. In total, Spain reported 30 outbreaks across two regions (Andalusia and Castilla-La Mancha), and the last confirmed case in Spain occurred on May 17, 2023. The control measures in Spain were consequently lifted on August 1, 2023. In contrast to the general assumption that European sheep breeds develop clear clinical signs upon SPPV infection, very low mortality and morbidity were observed during the outbreak in Spain. However, this was probably mainly due to vigorous monitoring, which allowed early detection of newly infected herds and stringent application of control measures [19]. In the same year, on September 16, 2023, Bulgaria reported an outbreak in the border region with Turkey (Burghas). This outbreak remained limited to one farm. Approximately one month later, on October 25, 2023, Greece reported an outbreak on the Island of Lesvos, followed by subsequent outbreaks on the Greek mainland in the regional unit of Fthiotida. The latest reported positive herd in Greece dates from March 7, 2024, and control measures were still in place at the time of writing this manuscript. In this study, we determined the complete genome sequences of SPPV present in five samples originating from the three recent sheeppox outbreaks in Europe, which occurred in Spain, Bulgaria, and Greece. These sequences are compared to other available sequences, and phylogenetic analysis was performed to determine their relationships to known strains of the virus and to obtain insights into their possible origin.

Materials and methods

Skin samples and DNA extraction

Several skin samples from clinically diseased sheep were received from Spain, Bulgaria, and Greece. Details on the time of sampling and the sample origin are provided in Table 1. After an overnight digestion step, DNA was extracted from the samples using a Macherey-Nagel tissue DNA extraction kit. The presence of capripox-specific viral DNA in all received skin samples was confirmed using a previously described real-time panCapx PCR assay [20], which targets the D5r region of the viral genome, as well as the GADPH housekeeping gene of the host as an internal control. For each outbreak, the DNA extract from the sample with the highest virus/host DNA ratio (i.e., the most viral DNA relative to the host DNA) was used for Illumina sequencing. Details about these selected samples can be found in Table 1.

Whole-genome sequencing

Whole-genome sequencing was performed directly on the DNA extract using an Illumina MiSeq run for the sample from 2022 (GenBank accession no. PP843160) and an iSeq run for the samples from 2023 (GenBank accession nos. PP886239, PP886238, PP886236, and PP886237). Library preparation for MiSeq sequencing was done using a Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina) in combination with a Nextera XT Index Kit v2 (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The library was subsequently sequenced using a MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 600c (Illumina) for paired-end reads of 250 bp, with the aim of maximal coverage (one sample in the run, 83000x theoretical coverage if only viral material is present). Library preparation for iSeq sequencing was done using a TruSeq Nano DNA sequencing kit (Illumina) in combination with an IDT for Illumina TruSeq DNA UD Indexes Kit v2 (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Paired-end reads of 150 bp length with an estimated insert size of 150 bp were generated using an iSeq Sequencer (iSeq 100 i1 Reagent v2 (300-cycle) chemistry), with the aim of 5000x coverage. The aimed coverages are theoretical values based on the output of the run (12,500 Mb for a MiSeq run and 1400 Mb for an iSeq run), the number of samples in the run (1 for the MiSeq run, 2 per run for the iSeq runs, with the sequencing effort equally divided over both samples), and the genome size of the virus (+/- 150 kb). The viral read coverage of the SPPV genomes is expected to be lower when there is more of the host material present in a sample. The resulting data were de-multiplexed, and adapter sequences and Illumina indices were removed before continuing with downstream analysis.

Genome mapping

The resulting data were mapped against the designated reference sequence (GenBank accession no. NC_004002). The isolation history of the reference strain was as follows: The virus was isolated from an outbreak of sheeppox in Turkey in the late 1970s, underwent six passages in primary lamb testis cells and one passage in sheep choroid plexus cells and was subsequently re-isolated in 2000 from lung lesions in an experimentally infected sheep. The sequence was determined using the Sanger method [5]. Assembly and mapping of genome sequences were performed using the Sciensano Galaxy server for genomics analysis. For read mapping, BWA Mapper was employed. Because the viral read yield was very low (see Results), no quality filtering on the reads was applied. All reads from the raw data were included in the assembly. A first-draft consensus sequence was generated using Samtools. Read depth and coverage were also evaluated using Samtools [21]. The total assembly was evaluated using the program ‘Tablet’ [22], and a majority rule was applied when assessing variable indel positions. A threshold of 10% read variation per base position was used to call ambiguous, non-indel sites (i.e., if >10% of the mapped reads show a consistent variant, the base was called as being ambiguous). The consensus sequences were assessed manually and curated to verify polymorphisms and indel sites. The resulting sequences have been submitted to the GenBank database under the accession numbers published in this paper.

Confirmation of a variable region by PCR

One region displayed unstable/unreliable read mapping and consensus reconstruction. We therefore decided to use PCR to verify the sequence at this location. This region is located in the intergenic spacer between the SPPV129 and SPPV130 exons. The PCR primers, composition of the reaction mixture, and cycling conditions, as well as electrophoresis results, can be found in Supplementary materials 1.

Sequence comparisons and phylogenetic analysis

Twenty WGSs from field and vaccine samples [5, 23–26] were downloaded from the GenBank database in March 2024. These sequences were selected based on their completeness (at least 140 kb and <1% N’s). Five WGSs were from direct submissions and could not be associated with a published paper (for a detailed list, see Supplementary materials 2). These sequences were analyzed together with the five sequences generated in this study. The resulting dataset included 25 sequences, which were aligned using the MAFFT online server with default settings [27], and the resulting alignment was refined manually. Refinement, visualization, and comparison of the sequences were done in MEGA v11.0.13 [28], which was also used for calculation of p-distances. Phylogenetic analysis based on WGS was done using IQ-tree [29], using automated model detection, allowing for free rate heterogeneity. To assess node support, an ultrafast bootstrap (BS) [30] analysis with 10,000 replicates was performed as well as a Shimodaira–Hasegawa (SH)-like approximate likelihood ratio test (SH-aLRT) [31]. Finally, a Bayesian approximation test was also performed to assess node support. Reference sequences for goatpox virus and lumpy skin disease virus (GenBank accession no. NC_004003 and NC_003027.1, respectively) were used as outgroups. Nodes were considered supported if the BS and SH-aLRT evaluation methods displayed values >75 and the posterior probability values exceeded 0.95. A node was considered weakly supported if one of these values was below the established threshold, but the other two analysis methods still supported it. Because vaccine-derived sequences constitute >25% (7/25) of the available whole-genome SPPV sequences, they may influence the structure of the phylogenetic tree. To evaluate a possible effect of the vaccine-derived sequences, we removed them from the WGS-based sequence alignment and reconstructed the phylogeny again using the same approach as described above.

Results

Detection of SPPV by real-time PCR

Ct values for SPPV in the skin samples varied between 13.65 and 17.17, and the virus/host Ct value ratios ranged between 0.70 and 0.77. Details for each sample are listed in Table 1.

Whole-genome sequencing and mapping results

Each sample yielded enough reads to cover the entire reference genome sequence. For the Spanish sample from 2022, the total number of reads was ~53.4 × 106. Of these, ~2.7 × 106 reads mapped to the SPPV reference genome. This means that ~5.1% of the reads obtained from the Illumina sequencing libraries could be associated with and mapped to the SPPV reference genome. The average genome coverage was 4097 reads (+/- stdev 3746), with a maximum of 231,551 reads and a minimum coverage of 266 reads.

The Spanish sample from 2023 yielded ~3.2 × 106 read pairs. Of these, ~21 × 103 reads mapped to the SPPV reference sequence. This means that ~0.7% of the reads could be associated with, and mapped to, the SPPV reference genome. The average genome coverage was 22 reads (+/- stdev 5), and the maximum and minimum coverage was 48 and two reads, respectively. The Bulgarian sample yielded a total of ~4.38 × 106 reads. Of these, ~73 × 103 reads mapped to the SPPV reference sequence. This means that ~1.7% of the reads could be associated with and mapped to the SPPV genome. The average genome coverage was 73 reads (+/- stdev 11), and the maximum and minimum coverage was 115 and 18 reads, respectively.

The Greek samples yielded ~4.2 × 106 reads (Fthiotida sample) and 4.0 × 106 reads (Lesvos sample). Of these, ~1 ×105 and 0.8 × 105 reads, respectively, mapped to the SPPV reference. This means that ~2.4% (Fthiotida) and 2.0% (Lesvos) of the reads could be associated with, and mapped to, the SPPV reference genome sequence. The average genome coverage was 101 reads (+/- stdev 17) for Fthiotida and 80 (+/- stdev 12) reads for Lesvos. The maximum was 174 reads for Fthiotida and 140 reads for Lesvos, and the minimum coverage was 41 reads for Fthiotida and 16 reads for Lesvos. The results are summarized in Table 2.

Sequence differences and phylogenetic placement

There were very few differences among the five newly determined sequences, with a total of only 29 variable positions. The p-distances for these sequences are listed in Table 3. The largest distance was between the samples from Spain and the samples from Greece, at 0.018%. Two indel differences were found between the Spanish samples. At positions 130,494 and 146,191 (numbering based on our alignment of the five new genome sequences), there were poly-A regions in which the 2022 sequences were A(16) and A(12) and the 2023 sequences were A(17) and A(13). The two Greek sequences were completely identical to each other and differed at seven positions from the Bulgarian strain. The length difference between the Greek and Bulgarian isolates was caused by four small indels: one insertion in the Bulgarian strain isolate to the Greek isolates and three single-nucleotide insertions in the Greek isolates relative to the Bulgarian isolates. The phylogeny obtained for all available WGSs of SPPV supports two main clades, labeled A1 and A2, and one unsupported cluster, labeled A3, which roughly correspond to three geographic regions (see Fig. 2). Clade A1 includes strains and derived vaccines from Asia, including Russia, but excluding the Middle East. Clade A2 includes strains from the Middle East, Europe (including the five new ones), and Northern Africa. Cluster A3 includes the single field isolate from sub-Saharan Africa (Nigeria) and three vaccine strains that were used in Saudi Arabia in response to an outbreak [23]. However, there was no statistical support for this node with any of the methods that were used (Fig. 2). This means that these three vaccines might have been based on an unrelated and unknown strain for which no WGS is available.

Discussion

Poxviruses are DNA viruses that evolve slowly when compared to RNA viruses [31]. Capripoxviruses have mutation rates that are similar to those of other poxviruses and are therefore also slowly evolving viruses [32]. Therefore, sequencing of the entire genome increases the chance of observing variations between isolates when sampled over short intervals.

We found the vaccine-related sequences to be placed in correspondence with what is reported in the literature with regard to their origin, except for the three in cluster A3 (Fig. 1). There was no statistical support for the placement of these vaccine strains with the single Nigerian field isolate, and they might have been developed using a strain for which no WGS is available. The vaccines appear to have been developed ‘ad-hoc’ from the strains circulating in a given location at the point in time when the need arose [5, 23]. In our analysis, the vaccine-derived sequences did not disrupt the overall clade structure (Fig. 1).

Interestingly, given its sequence divergence and separate placement in our phylogeny, the single field sample from sub-Saharan Africa forms a separate cluster that might represent an ancient introduction into Africa (see below) and might have evolved separately from the strains in Asia and Europe. The lack of a more-dense sampling of African isolates, however, precludes any final conclusions. Overall, the general lack of WGSs of SPPV strains makes us reluctant to assign more-nuanced clades to our phylogeny as was done recently for LSDV [33], as more strains may be circulating in undersampled areas. However, we think this phylogeny will provide a good starting point for further study and discussion of the evolution and distribution of SPPV, as was recently done for LSDV [33, 34]. We recommend that each outbreak of SPPV infection be accompanied by a WGS, which would aid in understanding the dispersal and evolution of SPPV and would allow for a more precise and nuanced phylogeny.

Between September 2022 and December 2023, three seemingly independent SPPV outbreaks occurred in Europe, namely in Spain, Bulgaria, and Greece. We successfully reconstructed five complete SPPV genome sequences associated with these outbreaks. All five of these viruses were placed in clade A2, indicating a relationship between the strains causing the recent European outbreaks to SPPV strains circulating in Northern Africa, Turkey, and the Middle East. A more precise determination of their origin cannot be made at this moment because of the lack of available WGSs. Having a better overview of the SPPV strains circulating globally would allow us to pinpoint the origin of introduction during an outbreak and would be helpful for identifying potential introduction routes. Nevertheless, the slowly evolving nature of capripoxvirus genomes would probably not allow for very precise tracing, as dispersal rates may outpace the rate of nucleotide substitutions.

An analysis of the variable region between SPPV129 and SPPV130 demonstrated that all five sequences were similar to those of the wild-type reference isolates. We attribute the variation in this region to difficulties with read mapping.

Very little is known about SPPV evolution in general, but it is hypothesized that SPPV originated in Asia. Research has indicated that sheep evolved in Eurasia and that the ancestors of modern domesticated sheep also originated in Asia [35–37]. The evidence for a historical presence of SPPV in Asia [15] makes this hypothesis likely and is consistent with our phylogeny, which does not indicate other more-likely scenarios. Adding more sequences from undersampled areas as well as historical sequences could greatly increase our knowledge about the evolution of the virus [9] and may cause the current hypothesis to be altered.

Sheeppox is a neglected disease, and the lack of knowledge highlighted above is in stark contrast to the impact of the disease and the efforts that must be undertaken to get the disease under control once an outbreak occurs. Sheeppox has a disproportionate impact on people who depend on sheep for their livelihood [7, 31, 38, 39]. Eradication measures typically involve stamping out, animal movement restrictions, and sometimes vaccination. In addition, surveillance and monitoring, biosecurity measures, animal tracing and identification, education and public awareness, and coordination and communication are key control measures that can be taken against the disease. Only live attenuated vaccines are available for sheeppox and goatpox, and these are not licensed within the EU. It can be hard to get vaccines to rural regions, and vaccination efforts may be hindered by distrust from livestock holders, especially since the occurrence of an LSDV vaccine spillover event [40]. Transport limitations and the establishment of exclusion zones may be hard to enforce, especially when governments are weak or the political situation is unstable. Finally, stamping out, while very effective and compulsory in the EU, is likely to encounter resistance as well, especially when no compensation is available. If stamping out is not compulsory or even possible in other parts of the world, the disease is likely to resurface again. The contribution of sheep to the overall economy of most countries is small and highly localized, and hard economic considerations currently impede the containment or eradication of the disease [9]. Therefore, we would encourage more-detailed studies of SPPV (and GTPV), not only regarding their evolution but also how specific changes affect the virulence and transmissibility of the virus, so that more-targeted control measures may be implemented and the impact of an outbreak might be reduced or at least more predictable.
