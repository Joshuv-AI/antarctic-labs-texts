Abstract
. In this paper, we employ probabilistic techniques to derive sharp, explicit two-sided estimates for the heat kernel of the nonlocal kinetic operator Δ v α / 2 + v ⋅ ∇ x , α ∈ ( 0 , 2 ) , ( x , v ) ∈ ℝ d × ℝ d , \Delta^{\alpha/2}_{v}+v\cdot\nabla_{x},\quad\alpha\in(0,2),\ (x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}, where Δ v α / 2 \Delta^{\alpha/2}_{v} represents the fractional Laplacian acting on the velocity variable v v . Additionally, we establish logarithmic gradient estimates with respect to both the spatial variable x x and the velocity variable v v . In fact, the estimates are developed for more general non-symmetric stable-like operators, demonstrating explicit dependence on the lower and upper bounds of the kernel functions. These results, in particular, provide a solution to a fundamental problem in the study of nonlocal kinetic operators.



1.
Introduction

Heat kernel estimates are a crucial tool in the study of partial differential equations (PDEs), particularly in the analysis of diffusion processes and heat flow. They provide detailed information about how solutions to diffusion-type equations, such as the heat equation, evolve over time. More generally, heat kernel estimates describe how heat (or probability, in the case of diffusion processes) spreads through space, with applications ranging from geometry to probability theory and mathematical physics (see
[
12
]
). The classical Laplacian heat equation is given by:

\partial_{t}u=\Delta u,

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian operator. The fundamental solution to this equation, starting from a point source (Dirac delta function), is the
Gaussian heat kernel
:

p_{t}(x,y)=(4\pi t)^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{4t}\right),

where
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are spatial points, and
t>0
is the time variable.

Beyond the Laplacian heat equation, a second-order differential operator of divergence form typically takes the form:

{\mathscr{L}}^{\rm div}_{\rm loc}f={\rm div}(a\cdot\nabla f)+{\rm div}(bf),

and the general non-divergence second-order differential operator takes the form:

{\mathscr{L}}^{\rm ndiv}_{\rm loc}f=\text{tr}(a\cdot\nabla^{2}f)+b\cdot\nabla f,

where
a(x)
is a symmetric diffusion matrix and
b(x)
represents the drift term. These operators often describe diffusion processes with drift, such as stochastic processes like Brownian motion with drift. The heat kernel provides the probability distribution of a particle at time
t
, starting from point
x
and moving to point
y
.
The heat kernel estimates for second-order operators are usually given in the Gaussian form of upper and lower bounds for
p_{t}(x,y)
, depending on the distance between
x
and
y
, time
t
, and the regularities of its coefficients. For the divergence form operator
{\mathscr{L}}^{\rm div}_{\rm loc}
, under bounded measurable and uniformly elliptic assumptions, a celebrated result due to Aronson
[
2
]
provides sharp two-sided Gaussian-type estimates:

\displaystyle C_{0}t^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\lambda_{0}\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{t}\right)\leqslant p_{t}(x,y)\leqslant C_{1}t^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\lambda_{1}\frac{|x-y|^{2}}{t}\right),

(1.1)

where the constants
\lambda_{0},\lambda_{1}>0
and
C_{0},C_{1}>0
only depend on the bounds and the dimension.
It was shown in
[
26
]
that the above estimates are equivalent to the parabolic Harnack inequality. For the operator
{\mathscr{L}}^{\rm ndiv}_{\rm loc}
of non-divergence form, when
a
is bounded, uniformly elliptic, and Hölder continuous, and
b
is bounded and measurable, the two-sided estimates (
1.1
) were established in
[
27
]
. These two-sided estimates play a crucial role in many analytical problems, and heat kernel estimates remain a central topic in modern mathematical physics. More profound results on heat kernel estimates on manifolds can be found in the monographs
[
25
,
29
]
.

Although the heat kernel estimates for local operators
{\mathscr{L}}^{\rm div}_{\rm loc}
and
{\mathscr{L}}^{\rm ndiv}_{\rm loc}
are extensively studied in the literature, nonlocal operators have also garnered great interest due to their mathematical challenges and practical applications. In recent years, significant attention has been paid to the study of heat kernel estimates for nonlocal operators, such as the fractional Laplacian
\Delta^{\alpha/2}
, where
\alpha\in(0,2)
, defined via Fourier’s transform:

\widehat{(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}f}(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}\hat{f}(\xi).

The heat equation corresponding to the fractional Laplacian is:

\partial_{t}u={-(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}u=:\Delta^{\alpha/2}u.}

This operator describes processes with jumps or long-range interactions, typical in Lévy flights and anomalous diffusion (cf.
[
14
]
). The heat kernel for the fractional Laplacian does not have a simple closed-form expression like the Gaussian kernel, except in special cases (e.g.,
\alpha=1
). However, sharp two-sided estimates are established by
Pólya
[
48
]
for
d=1
and Blumenthal and Getoor
[
7
]
for
d\geqslant 1
:
for
\alpha\in(0,2)
, the heat kernel
p_{t}(x,y)
of
\Delta^{\alpha/2}
satisfies:

p_{t}(x,y)\asymp t\left(t^{1/\alpha}+|x-y|\right)^{-d-\alpha},

(1.2)

where
\asymp
means that the two sides are comparable up to a constant depending on the dimension
d
and
\alpha
. These estimates capture the spread of heat for nonlocal operators, highlighting the long-range effects of the fractional Laplacian.

Over the last two decades, the study of nonlocal operators has made significant progress, including fine potential theoretical properties and the development of De Giorgi-Nash-Moser-Aronson-type theory for symmetric and nonsymmetric nonlocal operators; see, e.g.,
[
44
,
40
,
6
,
17
,
9
,
18
,
15
,
10
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
37
,
5
,
8
]
and the references therein. In particular, Chen and Kumagai
[
17
]
showed that, for every
\alpha\in(0,2)
and for any symmetric measurable function
\kappa(x,y)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\kappa_{0}\leqslant\kappa(x,y)\leqslant\kappa_{1}
, the symmetric nonlocal operator

{\mathscr{L}}^{\rm sym}_{\rm nloc}f(x)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|y-x|>\epsilon\}}(f(y)-f(x))\frac{\kappa(x,y)}{|x-y|^{d+\alpha}}\,{\rm d}y,

(1.3)

defined in the weak sense, admits a jointly Hölder continuous heat kernel
p_{t}(x,y)
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which satisfies (
1.2
) for all
t>0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where the implicit constant depends only on
d,\alpha,\kappa_{0}
, and
\kappa_{1}
. The two-sided estimate of heat kernel and Harnack inequalities for symmetric nonlocal operators on metric measure space can be found in
[
16
]
and references therein.
Meanwhile, consider the following nonsymmetric nonlocal operators:

{\mathscr{L}}^{\rm nsym}_{\rm nloc}f(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(f(x+y)-f(x)-{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{|y|\leqslant 1}y\cdot\nabla f(x){{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\alpha\in[1,2)})\frac{\kappa(x,y)}{|y|^{d+\alpha}}\,{\rm d}y,

where
\kappa_{0}\leqslant\kappa(x,y)\leqslant\kappa_{1}
is a bi-measurable function and
\kappa(x,-y)=\kappa(x,y)
.
When
\kappa
is Hölder/Dini continuous in
x
uniformly in
y
, sharp two-sided estimates of heat kernel were obtained in
[
20
,
21
,
43
,
46
,
23
]
, see also the references therein.
We emphasize that the constants appearing in all the above results depend on
\kappa_{0}
and
\kappa_{1}
implicitly, rather than explicitly.

The local and nonlocal operators discussed above are non-degenerate in the sense that diffusion occurs uniformly in all directions. However, in many applications, particularly in kinetic theory, one encounters degenerate operators where diffusion is not isotropic. These so-called
kinetic operators
often arise in the study of Boltzmann-type equations and kinetic models, such as the Boltzmann equation and the Fokker–Planck equations with nonlocal interactions, which describe the statistical behavior of a gas (see
[
11
,
51
]
). A typical kinetic operator takes the form:

{\mathscr{L}}_{\rm kin}f(x,v)=v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(x,v)+\mathscr{C}f(x,\cdot)(v),

where
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
represents the velocity variable,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the spatial variable, and
\mathscr{C}
is an integral-differential operator, such as a collision operator. The solution to a kinetic equation describes the evolution of a probability distribution in phase space
(x,v)
over time (see
[
11
]
). It models dynamics that involve both random jumps in velocity (captured by
\mathscr{C}
) and transport in space (captured by the drift term
v\cdot\nabla_{x}
).
A toy model of kinetic operators is of the form (see
[
1
]
):

\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{v}+v\cdot\nabla_{x},\quad\alpha\in(0,2].

The coupling between space and velocity introduces significant challenges in the analysis of heat kernel estimates for kinetic operators. When
\alpha=2
, in terms of the Brownian diffusion equation, Kolmogorov
[
39
]
first wrote down the fundamental solution of
\Delta_{v}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}
as

p_{t}(x_{0},v_{0},x,v)=\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t^{2}}\right)^{d}\exp\left\{-\frac{|x-x_{0}-tv_{0}|^{2}}{2t}+\frac{3(x-x_{0}-tv_{0})(v-v_{0})}{2t^{2}}-\frac{3|v-v_{0}|^{2}}{2t^{3}}\right\}.

From this expression, it is easy to derive the following two-sided estimate:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t^{2}}\right)^{d}\exp\left\{-\frac{4+\sqrt{13}}{4}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}-tv_{0}|^{2}}{t}+\frac{|v-v_{0}|^{2}}{t^{3}}\right)\right\}\leqslant p_{t}(x_{0},v_{0},x,v)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi t^{2}}\right)^{d}\exp\left\{-\frac{4-\sqrt{13}}{4}\left(\frac{|x-x_{0}-tv_{0}|^{2}}{t}+\frac{|v-v_{0}|^{2}}{t^{3}}\right)\right\}.

Recent research has made progress in deriving sharp two-sided heat kernel estimates for more general local kinetic operators. In
[
24
]
, Delarue and Menozzi studied Gaussian-type two-sided estimates for general second-order hypoelliptic operators. More recently, the authors in
[
13
]
and
[
49
]
investigated heat kernel estimates for the density of kinetic SDEs with rough coefficients using probabilistic techniques. These estimates are crucial for understanding the behavior of solutions to kinetic equations and for obtaining precise gradient bounds in both space and velocity.
It is worth noting that kinetic SDEs, also known as Langevin diffusion processes, have been extensively studied in terms of their long-time behavior and convergence to a stationary measure (see
[
47
,
33
,
52
]
).

As previously discussed, in the context of heat kernel estimates for kinetic operators, one often encounters
non-local
operators, particularly those with
\alpha\in(0,2)
, acting on the velocity variable. This non-locality introduces additional complexity compared to the local case. On one hand,
L^{p}
-maximal regularity and Schauder estimates for non-local kinetic operators have been studied in
[
22
,
34
]
and
[
31
,
35
]
, respectively. On the other hand, it is well known through Fourier analysis that the heat kernel of a non-local kinetic operator can be expressed as

p_{t}(x,v):=p_{t}(0,0,x,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(x\cdot\xi+v\cdot\eta)}\mathrm{e}^{-\int_{0}^{1}|s\xi+\eta|^{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}s}\,{\rm d}\xi{\rm d}\eta.

Although the above expression appears simple, since the seminal work of Pólya
[
48
]
and
Blumenthal and Getoor
[
7
]
, obtaining two-sided asymptotic estimates for
p_{t}(x,v)
as
x,v\to\infty
has remained an open problem for a considerable time.

The purpose of this paper is to provide a conclusive solution to this question by establishing sharp, explicit, two-sided asymptotic estimates for
p_{t}(x,v)
as
(x,v)\to\infty
. It is anticipated that these two-sided estimates will prove valuable in the future analysis of the behavior of solutions to the Boltzmann equation (cf.
[
36
]
).

1.1.
Main result

In this section, we present our main result. Before doing so, we first introduce the kinetic operators under consideration and outline the primary assumptions. Specifically, we consider general stable-like non-local operators, which are commonly used to study kinetic operators with variable coefficients. Let
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(0,\infty)
be a measurable function satisfying

\displaystyle 0<\kappa_{0}\leqslant\kappa(x)\leqslant\kappa_{1},\quad\int_{\{|\omega|=r\}}\omega\kappa(\omega){\rm d}\omega=0,\quad\forall r>0.

(1.4)

For simplicity of notation, we define

\nu({\rm d}x):=\frac{\kappa(x)}{|x|^{d+\alpha}}{\rm d}x,

which is called the Lévy measure. The second condition in (
1.4
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\int_{R_{0}\leqslant|x|\leqslant R_{1}}x\,\nu({\rm d}x)=0,\quad 0<R_{0}\leqslant R_{1}<\infty.

(1.5)

Let
L_{t}=L^{\nu}_{t}
be a
d
-dimensional Lévy process with characteristic exponent

\displaystyle\psi(\xi)=\psi_{\nu}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\left(1-{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi\cdot x}+\mathrm{i}\xi\cdot x{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\{|x|\leqslant 1\}}\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}x).

(1.6)

By the Lévy-Khintchine formula (see
[
50
]
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi\cdot L_{t}}\right)={\rm e}^{-t\psi(\xi)},\quad t\geqslant 0,\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The condition (
1.5
) ensures that
L_{t}
has zero mean; otherwise,
L_{t}
would include a linear drift term. Furthermore, (
1.5
) implies the following scaling property: for
\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle(L^{\nu}_{\lambda t})_{t\geqslant 0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\lambda^{1/\alpha}(L^{\nu_{\lambda}}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0},\quad\nu_{\lambda}({\rm d}x):=\frac{\kappa(\lambda^{1/\alpha}x)}{|x|^{d+\alpha}}{\rm d}x.

(1.7)

Indeed, by a change of variables, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi\cdot L^{\nu}_{\lambda t}}\right)={\rm e}^{-\lambda t\psi_{\nu}(\xi)}={\rm e}^{-t\psi_{\nu_{\lambda}}(\lambda^{1/\alpha}\xi)}=\mathbb{E}\left({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\xi\cdot\lambda^{1/\alpha}L^{\nu_{\lambda}}_{t}}\right).

For a given
z_{0}=(x_{0},v_{0})
, consider the Markov process
Z_{t}(z_{0})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
starting from
z_{0}
,

Z_{t}(z_{0}):=\left(x_{0}+tv_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}L_{s}\mathrm{d}s,v_{0}+L_{t}\right)=:(x_{0}+tv_{0}+X_{t},v_{0}+V_{t}).

By Itô’s formula, we have (see
[
22
]
)

\partial_{t}\mathbb{E}f(Z_{t}(z_{0}))=\mathbb{E}\mathscr{K}_{\nu}f(Z_{t}(z_{0})),

where
\mathscr{K}_{\nu}
is the non-local kinetic operator defined by

\mathscr{K}_{\nu}f(x,v)=\mathscr{L}^{\nu}f(x,\cdot)(v)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(x,v),

and
\mathscr{L}^{\nu}
is the infinitesimal generator of
L
, given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}^{\nu}f(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(f(v+w)-f(v)-{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\{|w|\leqslant 1\}}w\cdot\nabla f(v)\right)\nu({\rm d}w).

As shown in
[
22
, Lemma 2.5]
, under condition (
1.4
) (although
[
22
]
considers the symmetric case, the result can be extended to the general case (
1.4
), see Lemma
2.2
below), for any
t>0
, the process
Z_{t}(z_{0})
admits a smooth density
p_{t}(z_{0},z)
, meaning that for any bounded measurable
f:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}f(z)p_{t}(z_{0},z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\mathbb{E}f(Z_{t}(z_{0})).

(1.8)

In particular, the density
p_{t}(z_{0},\cdot)
of
Z_{t}(z_{0})
is the heat kernel of the kinetic operator
\mathscr{K}_{\nu}
, i.e.,

{\partial}_{t}p_{t}(z_{0},z)=\mathscr{K}_{\nu}^{*}p_{t}(z_{0},\cdot)(z).

Moreover, by (
1.8
), we easily see that

\displaystyle p_{t}(z_{0},z)=p_{t}(0,z-\theta_{t}z_{0}),\quad\theta_{t}z:=(x+tv,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

(1.9)

Hence, to obtain two-sided estimates of
p_{t}(z_{0},z)
, it suffices to consider
p_{t}(0,z):=p_{t}^{\nu}(z)
, which is the density of

\displaystyle Z^{\nu}_{t}:=Z_{t}(0)=\left(\int_{0}^{t}L_{s}\mathrm{d}s,L_{t}\right)=(X_{t},V_{t})=:Z_{t}.

(1.10)

1.2.
Related work

In recent years, the study of nonlocal kinetic operators, particularly regarding Harnack inequalities and heat kernel estimates, has garnered significant attentions. In
[
32
]
, Hao, Röckner, and Zhang investigated the well-posedness of nonlinear and nonlocal kinetic Fokker-Planck equations with measure-valued initial values. The singular interaction kernels they considered include the Newtonian potential, Coulomb potential, and even the Riesz potential.
In
[
3
,
4
]
, Auscher, Imbert, and Niebel developed a comprehensive theory for weak solutions to nonlocal kinetic equations with rough coefficients. Specifically, they established results on the regularity, existence, and uniqueness of weak solutions. While, Kassmann and Weidner
[
38
]
constructed a counterexample demonstrating the failure of the Harnack inequality for the kinetic operator
v\cdot\nabla_{x}+\Delta^{\alpha/2}
, highlighting a key difference compared to local kinetic operators (see
[
28
]
).
On the other hand, for a class of nonlocal hypoelliptic equations in divergence form, Loher
[
45
]
proved a strong Harnack inequality and derived rough bounds for the fundamental solution.

After this paper was posted on arXiv, Grube informed us of his preprint
[
30
]
. Using the Fourier transform, he established the following two-sided estimate for the heat kernel of
v\cdot\nabla_{x}+\Delta^{\alpha/2}
when
d=1
(see
[
30
, Theorem 1.1]
):

\displaystyle p_{1}(x,v)\asymp\frac{1}{(1+|x|+|v|)^{2+\alpha}}\frac{1}{(1+(|2x-v|-|v|)_{+})^{\alpha}},

(1.18)

where
a_{+}:=a\vee 0
for
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
When
\alpha=1
, Grube also derived an explicit formula for
p_{1}(x,v)
(see
[
30
, Theorem 5.1]
).

To make a comparison, for
z=(x,v)
, let
\bar{x}:=\frac{x}{|x|}
,
\bar{v}:=\frac{v}{|v|}
, and
\omega_{z}:=\langle\bar{x},\bar{v}\rangle
. Noting that

|x-v|^{2}=|x-\langle x,\bar{v}\rangle\bar{v}|^{2}+|\langle x,\bar{v}\rangle\bar{v}-v|^{2}=2|x|^{2}(1-\omega_{z}^{2})+||x|\omega_{z}-|v||^{2},

where we adopt the convention
\bar{0}:=0
, it follows that

1+|x|\sqrt{1-\omega_{z}^{2}}+\left(\big|2|x|\omega_{z}-|v|\big|-|v|\right)_{+}\asymp\begin{cases}1+|x|,&\omega_{z}\leqslant 0,\\
1+|x|\sqrt{1-\omega_{z}^{2}},&0<\omega_{z}\leqslant\frac{|v|}{|x|},\\
1+|x-v|,&\frac{|v|}{|x|}<\omega_{z}\leqslant 1.\end{cases}

Thus, by (
1.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\beta}(z)\asymp\frac{1}{(1+|z|)^{1+\beta}}\left(1+|x|\sqrt{1-\omega_{z}^{2}}+\left(\big|2|x|\omega_{z}-|v|\big|-|v|\right)_{+}\right)^{1-\beta}.

(1.19)

Compared to (
1.18
), there is an additional term
|x|\sqrt{1-\omega_{z}^{2}}
, which vanishes for
x,v\neq 0
when
d=1
.
In particular, when
d=1
, our two-sided estimate
\mathcal{N}_{1+\alpha}(z)
coincides with (
1.18
).

1.3.
Strategy of proof

Motivated by
[
53
]
and following
[
21
]
, we apply Lévy-Itô’s decomposition to decompose
Z_{t}
into a small jump part and a large jump part:

Z_{t}=Z_{t}^{(0)}+Z_{t}^{(1)},

where

\displaystyle Z_{t}^{(i)}:=(X_{t}^{(i)},V_{t}^{(i)})=\left(\int_{0}^{t}L_{s}^{(i)}\,\mathrm{d}s,L_{t}^{(i)}\right),\quad i=0,1,

and
L_{t}^{(i)},i=0,1
are two independent Lévy processes with characteristic exponent
\psi_{\nu^{(i)}}
,
i=0,1
(see (
1.6
)), and

\nu^{(0)}(\mathrm{d}x):={{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\{|x|\leqslant 1\}}\frac{\kappa(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}{|x|^{d+\alpha}},\quad\nu^{(1)}(\mathrm{d}x):={{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\{|x|>1\}}\frac{\kappa(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}{|x|^{d+\alpha}}.

Under assumption (
1.4
), the small jump part
Z_{t}^{(0)}
has a smooth, strictly positive density
q_{t}(z)
that decays at any polynomial rate, which allows us to handle it more easily using Fourier transform techniques (see Lemmas
2.2
and
2.6
). In particular, since
Z_{t}^{(0)}
and
Z_{t}^{(1)}
are independent, one has

\displaystyle p^{\nu}_{t}(z)=\mathbb{E}\left(q_{t}(z-Z_{t}^{(1)})\right).

(1.20)

Thus, the large jump part
Z_{t}^{(1)}
governs the decay rate in the two-sided estimate. We must point out that although
L_{t}^{(1)}
is a compound Poisson process (see
[
50
]
), finding a suitable explicit function
\mathcal{E}:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to(0,\infty)
to obtain a sharp two-sided estimate of the form

\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}^{(1)}-z|\leqslant 1)\asymp\mathcal{E}(t,z),

remains a challenge due to the degenerate nature of the process (see Lemma
2.3
below).

In the next two sections, we provide complete proofs of Theorem
1.1
and Lemma
1.5
. Throughout this paper, by
A\asymp B
,
A\lesssim B
, and
A\gtrsim B
, we mean that for some unimportant constant
C\geqslant 1
that only depends on
d,\alpha
,

C^{-1}B\leqslant A\leqslant CB,\quad A\leqslant CB,\quad\text{and}\quad A\geqslant C^{-1}B.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We first recall a well-known result. For reader’s convenience, we provide detailed proofs.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
L_{t}
be a Lévy process with characteristic exponent
\psi
and
Z_{t}
be as in (
1.10
). Then for any
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
t\geqslant 0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(\xi,\eta)\cdot Z_{t}}\right)=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{t}\psi\left(s\xi+\eta\right)\mathrm{d}s\right\}.

(2.1)

Proof.

Given
t>0
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k=1,\cdots,n
,
define
\Delta_{k}:=L_{kt/n}-L_{(k-1)t/n}
and

{Z}_{t}^{(n)}:=\left(\frac{t}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}L_{kt/n},L_{t}\right)=\left(\frac{t}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Delta_{j},\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Delta_{k}\right).

Then for each
t>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(\xi,\eta)\cdot{Z}_{t}^{(n)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{t}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\xi\cdot\Delta_{j}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\eta\cdot\Delta_{k}\right)\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\exp\left\{\mathrm{i}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{(n-k+1)t\xi}{n}+\eta\right)\cdot\Delta_{k}\right)\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\frac{t}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\psi\left(\frac{(n-k+1)t\xi}{n}+\eta\right)\right\},

where in the last equality we used the fact that
\{\Delta_{k},1\leqslant k\leqslant n\}
is independent and the definition (
1.6
) of
\psi
. Since
L_{t}
is right-continuous with left hand limit almost surely, we see that the path of
L
is almost surely Riemann integrable. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(\xi,\eta)\cdot Z_{t}}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(\xi,\eta)\cdot{Z}_{t}^{(n)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\exp\left\{-\frac{t}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\psi\left(\frac{(n-k+1)t\xi}{n}+\eta\right)\right\}=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{t}\psi\left((t-s)\xi+\eta\right)\mathrm{d}s\right\},

which implies (
2.1
).
∎

For
\lambda,t>0
, we have the following scaling property:

\displaystyle p^{\lambda\nu}_{t}(x,v)=\lambda^{d}p^{\nu}_{\lambda t}(\lambda x,v)=\lambda^{d}(\lambda t)^{-\frac{2d}{\alpha}-d}p^{\nu_{\lambda t}}_{1}(\mathbb{T}^{\alpha}_{\lambda t}(\lambda x,v)),

(2.2)

where
\mathbb{T}^{\alpha}_{t}
is defined by (
1.11
) and

\nu_{\lambda t}({\rm d}x)=\frac{\kappa((\lambda t)^{1/\alpha}x)}{|x|^{d+\alpha}}{\rm d}x.

Indeed, by Fourier’s transform and the change of variable, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(\xi,\eta)\cdot z}p^{\lambda\nu}_{t}(z)\,{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\lambda\int_{0}^{t}\psi\left(s\xi+\eta\right)\,\mathrm{d}s\right\}=\exp\left\{-\int_{0}^{\lambda t}\psi\left(s\xi/\lambda+\eta\right)\,\mathrm{d}s\right\}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(\xi/\lambda,\eta)\cdot z}p^{\nu}_{\lambda t}(z)\,{\rm d}z=\lambda^{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}(\xi\cdot x+\eta\cdot v)}p^{\nu}_{\lambda t}(\lambda x,v)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}v,

which gives the first equality. The second equality follows from (
1.7
) (see
[
22
]
).

Therefore, by choosing
\lambda=\kappa_{0}
, we shall assume

\displaystyle 1\leqslant\kappa(x)\leqslant\kappa_{1},\ \ \int_{\{|\omega|=r\}}\omega\kappa(\omega)\,{\rm d}\omega=0,\ \ \forall r>0.

(2.3)

Under (
2.3
), to prove Theorem
1.1
, it suffices to consider
p^{\nu_{t}}_{1}
. By (
1.20
), we will separately estimate
q_{1}
and
Z_{1}^{(1)}
.
We begin by demonstrating the following regularity estimates, which state that the small jump part possesses a smooth density that decays at any polynomial rate.

Lemma 2.2
.

Under (
2.3
), for any
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\beta\geqslant 0
, there exists a constant
C=C(j,\beta,d,\alpha)>0
such that for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,

|\nabla^{j}q_{1}(z)|\lesssim_{C}\kappa^{\beta}_{1}(1+|z|)^{-\beta}.

Proof.

Let
\psi_{\nu^{(0)}}
be the characteristic exponent of
L^{(0)}_{t}
defined as in (
1.6
).
Then, it is easily seen that under (
2.3
),

\displaystyle\mbox{Re}(\psi_{\nu^{(0)}}(\xi))\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\left(1-\cos(\xi\cdot x)\right)\frac{{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\{|x|\leqslant 1\}}\,{\rm d}x}{|x|^{d+\alpha}}.

(2.4)

For simplicity of notation, we write

w=(\xi,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d},\quad\phi(w):=\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{\nu^{(0)}}(s\xi+\eta)\,{\rm d}s.

By (
2.4
) and
[
22
, (2.37) and (2.38)]
, for any
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there exist constants
C,c>0
only depending on

such that for all
w\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,

\displaystyle\mbox{Re}(\phi(w))\geqslant c\left(|w|^{2}\land|w|^{\alpha}\right),\quad|\nabla^{j}\phi(w)|\lesssim_{C}\kappa_{1}\left(|w|^{j}+1\right).

(2.5)

By Fourier’s inverse transform and Lemma
2.1
, we have

q_{1}(z)=(2\pi)^{-2d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}w\cdot z}{\rm e}^{-\int_{0}^{1}\psi_{\nu^{(0)}}(s\xi+\eta)\,{\rm d}s}\,{\rm d}w=(2\pi)^{-2d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}w\cdot z}{\rm e}^{-\phi(w)}\,{\rm d}w.

Therefore, for any
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{2d})
,
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{2d})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and

j_{1},\dots,j_{2d}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{2d}j_{i}=j
, we have

(1+|z|^{2})^{m}\nabla^{j_{1}}_{z_{1}}\cdots\nabla^{j_{2d}}_{z_{2d}}q_{1}(z)=(2\pi)^{-2d}{\rm i}^{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}w\cdot z}\prod_{i=1}^{2d}w_{i}^{j_{i}}\times\left((1-\Delta_{w})^{m}{\rm e}^{-\phi(w)}\right)\,{\rm d}w,

where
\Delta_{w}
stands for the Laplacian and
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
By (
2.5
),
|{\rm e}^{-\phi(w)}|={\rm e}^{-\mbox{Re}(\phi(w))}
, and the elementary chain rule, we get for some
C=C(m,j,d,\alpha)>0
,

(1+|z|^{2})^{m}\left|\nabla^{j_{1}}_{z_{1}}\cdots\nabla^{j_{2d}}_{z_{2d}}q_{1}(z)\right|\leqslant(2\pi)^{-2d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|w|^{j}\left|(1-\Delta_{w})^{m}{\rm e}^{-\phi(w)}\right|\,{\rm d}w\leqslant C\kappa_{1}^{2m}.

By interpolation, we obtain the desired estimate.
∎

Next, we focus on establishing the crucial two-sided sharp estimate for the large jump entering any cube that is far from the original point.
For

(2.6)

Lemma 2.3
.

Under (
2.3
), there exist two constants

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{1}^{(1)}\in Q_{1}(z)\right)\lesssim_{C_{1}}\frac{\kappa_{1}^{3+2d+2\alpha}}{(|z|+1)^{d+\alpha}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\rm d}s}{(|\Gamma_{s}z|+1)^{d+\alpha}},\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d},

where
\Gamma_{s}z:=x-sv
for

, and for
c_{0}:=\int_{|y|\geqslant 1}\frac{{\rm d}y}{|y|^{d+\alpha}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}^{(1)}\in Q_{1}(z))\gtrsim_{C_{0}}\frac{{\rm e}^{-c_{0}\kappa_{1}}}{(|z|+1)^{d+\alpha}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(|\Gamma_{s}z|+1\right)^{-d-\alpha}\,{\rm d}s,\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

Proof.

In the following proof, all constants depend only on
d
and
\alpha
. We fix a point
z=(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and divide the proof into three steps.

(Step 1).
In this step we present a series representation for
\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}^{(1)}\in Q_{1}(z))
.
Let
\{\tau_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
\{\xi_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be two independent families of i.i.d. random variables in
[0,\infty)
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with exponential distributions of parameter
\lambda:=\nu^{(1)}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mu:=\frac{\nu^{(1)}}{\lambda}

S_{0}^{\tau}=S_{0}^{\xi}=0
and define

S_{n}^{\tau}:=\tau_{1}+\cdots+\tau_{n},\quad S_{n}^{\xi}:=\xi_{1}+\cdots+\xi_{n},\quad n\geqslant 1.

Now, define

N_{t}:=\max\{n:S_{n}^{\tau}\leqslant t\},\quad H_{t}:=S_{N_{t}}^{\xi},\quad t>0.

Then,
H_{t}
is a compound Poisson process with Lévy measure
\nu^{(1)}
, which implies that

(H_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}(L_{t}^{(1)})_{t\geqslant 0}.

Noticing that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
s>0
,

\{N_{s}=k\}=\{S_{k}^{\tau}\leqslant s<S_{k+1}^{\tau}\},

we have

\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}S_{k}^{\xi}\int_{0}^{1}{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\{N_{s}=k\}}\,\mathrm{d}s=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}S_{k}^{\xi}\left(\tau_{k+1}{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\{S_{k+1}^{\tau}\leqslant 1\}}+(1-S_{k}^{\tau}){{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\{S_{k}^{\tau}\leqslant 1<S_{k+1}^{\tau}\}}\right).

Hence, for fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,
recalling the definition of
B_{1}
in (
2.6
), we have the following series expression for the probability of
Z^{(1)}_{1}
lying in
Q_{1}(z)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}^{(1)}\in Q_{1}(z))=\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{0}^{1}H_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\in x+B_{1},H_{1}\in v+B_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}S_{k}^{\xi}\tau_{k+1}+S_{n}^{\xi}(1-S_{n}^{\tau})\in x+B_{1},S_{n}^{\xi}\in v+B_{1},S_{n}^{\tau}\leqslant 1<S_{n+1}^{\tau}\right)=:\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(z).

Below, for the simplicity of notation, we write for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\boldsymbol{s}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}),\ \boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}),\ \Lambda_{n}:=\{\boldsymbol{s}\in[0,1]^{n}:s_{1}+\cdots+s_{n}\leqslant 1\},

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}:=\mathrm{d}s_{1}\cdots\mathrm{d}s_{n},\ \ \boldsymbol{\mu}({\rm d}\boldsymbol{y}):=\mu({\rm d}y_{1})\cdots\mu({\rm d}y_{n})\in{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{R}^{nd}).

(2.7)

Since
\tau_{k}\sim\lambda{\rm e}^{-\lambda s}{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{s\geqslant 0}
and

are independent,
by elementary calculus, we have

\displaystyle I_{n}(z)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n+1}\int_{[0,\infty)^{n+1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{nd}}{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}s_{k+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}(1-s_{1}-\cdots-s_{n})\in x+B_{1}\right\}}{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}\in v+B_{1}\right\}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\left\{s_{1}+\cdots+s_{n}\leqslant 1<s_{1}+\cdots+s_{n+1}\right\}}{\rm e}^{-\lambda(s_{1}+\cdots+s_{n+1})}\mu(\mathrm{d}y_{1})\cdots\mu(\mathrm{d}y_{n}){\rm d}s_{1}\cdots{\rm d}s_{n}{\rm d}s_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n}{\rm e}^{-\lambda}\int_{\Lambda_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{nd}}{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}s_{k+1}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}(1-s_{1}-\cdots-s_{n})\in x+B_{1}\right\}}{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}\in v+B_{1}\right\}}\boldsymbol{\mu}({\rm d}\boldsymbol{y}){\rm d}\boldsymbol{s}

Next, by the change of variable

(1-s_{1},1-s_{1}-s_{2},\cdots,1-s_{1}-\cdots-s_{n})\to(s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{n}),

I_{n}(z)=\lambda^{n}{\rm e}^{-\lambda}\int_{0\leqslant s_{n}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant s_{1}\leqslant 1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{nd}}{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}s_{j}\in x+B_{1}\right\}}{{\mathbbm{1}}}_{\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}\in v+B_{1}\right\}}\boldsymbol{\mu}({\rm d}\boldsymbol{y}){\rm d}\boldsymbol{s}.

(2.9)

(Step 2).

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}^{(1)}\in Q_{1}(z))=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}(z)\lesssim\frac{\kappa_{1}^{3+2d+2\alpha}}{(|z|+1)^{d+\alpha}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{(|\Gamma_{s}z|+1)^{d+\alpha}},

(

by the definition (

(

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{a}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|L_{s}^{(0)}|\mathrm{d}s\right)\leqslant\mathbb{E}\varphi_{a}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|L_{s}^{(0)}|\mathrm{d}s\right)\leqslant\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{a}(|L_{s}^{(0)}|)\mathrm{d}s<\infty.

(2.33)

Therefore, by
\varphi_{a}(|z|)\leqslant(\varphi_{a}(2|x|)+\varphi_{a}(2|v|))/2
, we have for any
a\in(0,1/2)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{a}\left(|Z_{t}^{(0)}|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{a}\left(2\int_{0}^{t}|L_{s}^{(0)}|\mathrm{d}s\right)+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{a}(2|L_{t}^{(0)}|)

\displaystyle\leqslant C_{a}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{2a}\left(\int_{0}^{t}|L_{s}^{(0)}|\mathrm{d}s\right)+C_{a}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{2a}(|L_{t}^{(0)}|)<\infty,

(2.34)

where in the last inequality we used inequality
\varphi_{a}(2r)\leqslant C_{a}\varphi_{2a}(r)
.
Noticing that

\displaystyle Z_{1}^{(0)}=\left(\int_{0}^{1/2}(L_{s+1/2}^{(0)}-L_{1/2}^{(0)})\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{1/2}L_{s}^{(0)}\mathrm{d}s+\frac{1}{2}L_{1/2}^{(0)},L_{1}^{(0)}-L_{1/2}^{(0)}+L_{1/2}^{(0)}\right),

by the independence of the increments, we have

\displaystyle q_{1}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}q_{1/2}(z^{\prime})q_{1/2}(z-\tilde{z}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}=:I_{1}+I_{2},

where
\tilde{z}^{\prime}:=(x^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}v^{\prime},v^{\prime})
and

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle:=\int_{|z-\tilde{z}^{\prime}|\leqslant|z|/2}q_{1/2}(z^{\prime})q_{1/2}(z-\tilde{z}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z^{\prime},

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle:=\int_{|z-\tilde{z}^{\prime}|>|z|/2}q_{1/2}(z^{\prime})q_{1/2}(z-\tilde{z}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}.

For
I_{1}
, since
|z-\tilde{z}^{\prime}|\leqslant|z|/2
implies that

\tfrac{1}{2}|z|\leqslant|\tilde{z}^{\prime}|=\sqrt{\left|x^{\prime}+\tfrac{1}{2}v^{\prime}\right|^{2}+|v^{\prime}|^{2}}\leqslant|z^{\prime}|+\tfrac{1}{2}|v^{\prime}|\leqslant\tfrac{3}{2}|z^{\prime}|\Rightarrow|z^{\prime}|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{3}|z|.

Therefore, it holds that

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|q_{1/2}\|_{\infty}\int_{|z^{\prime}|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{3}|z|}q_{1/2}(z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}=\|q_{1/2}\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{1/2}^{(0)}|\geqslant\frac{1}{3}|z|\right).

(2.35)

For
I_{2}
, using the change of variable, we have

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{|z^{\prime}|>|z|/2}q_{1/2}\left(x-x^{\prime}-\frac{v-v^{\prime}}{2},v-v^{\prime}\right)q_{1/2}(z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}\leqslant\|q_{1/2}\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{1/2}^{(0)}|\geqslant\frac{1}{2}|z|\right).

(2.36)

Combining (
2.35
), (
2.36
) and the Markov inequality, fixing any
a\in(0,1/2)
, we conclude that

\displaystyle q_{1}(z)\leqslant 2\|q_{1/2}\|_{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|Z_{1/2}^{(0)}|\geqslant\frac{1}{3}|z|\right)\leqslant\frac{2\|q_{1/2}\|_{\infty}}{\varphi_{a}(|z|/3)}\mathbb{E}\varphi_{a}\left(|Z_{1/2}^{(0)}|\right).

(2.37)

Combining (
2.34
) and (
2.37
), we get the upper bound.
∎

Now we show the following quantitative lower bound estimate for the general small jump part.

Lemma 2.6
.

Under (

), there is a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(d,\alpha)>0
such that for

(2.38)

Proof.

By the Lévy-Itô decomposition, assume that

, where
L_{t}^{(0,0)}
and
L_{t}^{(0,1)}
are two independent Lévy processes with characteristic exponents

and

and

Define

Let
q_{1}^{(0)}
be the density of
Z_{1}^{(0,0)}
. Since
Z^{(0)}_{1}=Z_{1}^{(0,0)}+Z_{1}^{(0,1)}
, by independence, we have

\displaystyle q_{1}(z)=\mathbb{E}\left(q_{1}^{(0)}(z-Z_{1}^{(0,1)})\right).

(2.39)

Let

be the Poisson random measure associated with
L_{t}^{(0,1)}
. By the Lévy-Itô decomposition and (
1.10
), we can write

where

is the compensated Poisson random measure. By Itô’s isometry, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\left|L_{t}^{(0,1)}\right|^{2}\right)=t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}|x|^{2}\nu_{0,1}(\mathrm{d}x\leqslant t\kappa_{1}\int_{\{0<|x|\leqslant 1\}}\frac{|x|^{2}}{|x|^{d+\alpha}}\,\mathrm{d}x=:C_{2}t\kappa_{1}.

Thus, for any
R>0

|y-x|^{2}\leqslant 2|y|^{2}+2|x|^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\int_{0}^{1}L_{s}^{(0,1)}\,\mathrm{d}s-x\right|>R\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2}{R^{2}}\left(|x|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\int_{0}^{1}L_{s}^{(0,1)}\,\mathrm{d}s\right|^{2}\right)\right)\leqslant\frac{2}{R^{2}}\left(|x|^{2}+C_{2}\kappa_{1}\right),

(2.40)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|L_{1}^{(0,1)}-v\right|>R\right)\leqslant\frac{2}{R^{2}}\left(|v|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left(\left|L_{1}^{(0,1)}\right|^{2}\right)\right)\leqslant\frac{2}{R^{2}}\left(|v|^{2}+C_{2}\kappa_{1}\right).

(2.41)

Now, for given
z=(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and
R>0
, combining (
2.26
), (
2.39
), (
2.40
) and (
2.41
), we get

\displaystyle q_{1}(z)

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf_{w\in Q_{R}(0)}q_{1}^{(0)}(w)\,\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}^{(0,1)}\in Q_{R}(z))\geqslant C_{0}{\rm e}^{-\delta_{0}(R+2)\log(R+2)}\mathbb{P}(Z_{1}^{(0,1)}\in Q_{R}(z))

\displaystyle\geqslant C_{0}{\rm e}^{-\delta_{0}(R+2)\log(R+2)}\left(1-\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\int_{0}^{1}L_{s}^{(0,1)}\,\mathrm{d}s-x\right|>R\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\left|L_{1}^{(0,1)}-v\right|>R\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant C_{0}{\rm e}^{-\delta_{0}(R+2)\log(R+2)}\left(1-\frac{2}{R^{2}}(|z|^{2}+2C_{2}\kappa_{1})\right).

In particular, for
z\in Q_{1}(0)
, by taking

, we obtain

\displaystyle\inf_{z\in Q_{1}(0)}q_{1}(z)\geqslant C_{0}{\rm e}^{-\delta_{0}(R+2)\log(R+2)}\inf_{z\in Q_{1}(0)}\left(1-\frac{2}{R^{2}}(|z|^{2}+2C_{2}\kappa_{1})\right)\geqslant\frac{C_{0}}{2}{\rm e}^{-\delta_{0}(R+2)\log(R+2)},

which in turn implies (
2.38

as

∎

The right-hand side of the inequality in Lemma

involves an integral, which can be challenging to work with directly. To address this, we introduce a comparable quantity that is crucial for establishing the upper bound estimate for the heat kernel. This new quantity is more explicit and easier to handle, facilitating our analysis.

Lemma 2.7
.

For
\beta>1
, there is a constant

such that
for all

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\rm d}s}{(|\Gamma_{s}z|+1)^{\beta}}\asymp_{C}\frac{\left(\inf_{s\in[0,1]}|\Gamma_{s}z|+1\right)^{1-\beta}}{|z|+1}=:\frac{{\mathcal{M}}_{\beta}(z)}{|z|+1}.

Proof.

In the following proof, all the constants only depend on
d,\beta
. We fix
z=(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
When
|z|<3
, since
|\Gamma_{s}z|\leqslant 2|z|
for

, it holds that

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\rm d}s}{(|\Gamma_{s}z|+1)^{\beta}}\asymp 1\asymp\frac{{\mathcal{M}}_{\beta}(z)}{|z|+1}.

Now in the following proof, we assume that
|z|\geqslant 3
.
If

, then

which implies that for any

In this case, we see that when

Below we assume that

\displaystyle|z|\geqslant|v|\geqslant\tfrac{1}{3}|z|\geqslant 1.

(2.43)

Let

Then we have the following estimate:

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{s}z|+1\asymp\sqrt{|\Gamma_{s}z|^{2}+1}=\sqrt{|y|^{2}+|s-s_{0}|^{2}|v|^{2}+1}\asymp|y|+|s-s_{0}||v|+1.

(2.44)

We consider three cases:

(
Case 1:
s_{0}<0
.
) In this case, by (

\displaystyle\inf_{s\in[0,1]}|\Gamma_{s}z|+1\asymp|y|+\inf_{s\in[0,1]}|s-s_{0}||v|+1=|y|+|s_{0}||v|+1.

(2.45)

Combining (
2.44
) and the change of variable, it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\rm d}s}{(|\Gamma_{s}z|+1)^{\beta}}\asymp\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\rm d}s}{(|y|+(s-s_{0})|v|+1)^{\beta}}=\frac{1}{|v|}\int_{|s_{0}||v|+|y|}^{(1+|s_{0}|)|v|+|y|}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{(s+1)^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{(\beta-1)|v|}\left(\frac{1}{(|s_{0}||v|+|y|+1)^{\beta-1}}-\frac{1}{((1+|s_{0}|)|v|+|y|+1)^{\beta-1}}\right).

(2.46)

Using (
2.43
), we see that

|z|=\sqrt{|v|^{2}+|x|^{2}}=\sqrt{|y|^{2}+|s_{0}|^{2}|v|^{2}+|v|^{2}}\geqslant\tfrac{|s_{0}||v|+|y|+1}{3},

and

\displaystyle(1+|s_{0}|)|v|+|y|+1\geqslant|s_{0}||v|+|y|+1+\tfrac{|z|}{3}\geqslant\tfrac{10}{9}(|s_{0}||v|+|y|+1).

(2.47)

Combining (
2.46
) and (
2.47
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\rm d}s}{(|\Gamma_{s}z|+1)^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{1}{|z|+1}\left(\frac{1}{(|s_{0}||v|+|y|+1)^{\beta-1}}-\frac{1}{((1+|s_{0}|)|v|+|y|+1)^{\beta-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{1}{|z|+1}\frac{1}{(|s_{0}||v|+|y|+1)^{\beta-1}},

which together with (
2.45
) yields (
2.42
).

(
Case 2:
s_{0}>1
.
) In this case, by (
2.44
) we have

\displaystyle\inf_{s\in[0,1]}|\Gamma_{s}z|+1\asymp|y|+\inf_{s\in[0,1]}|s-s_{0}||v|+1=|y|+(s_{0}-1)|v|+1.

(2.48)

Similar to (
2.46
), it follows from (
2.44
) that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\rm d}s}{(|\Gamma_{s}z|+1)^{\beta}}\asymp\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\rm d}s}{(|y|+(s_{0}-s)|v|+1)^{\beta}}=\frac{1}{|v|}\int_{(s_{0}-1)|v|+|y|}^{s_{0}|v|+|y|}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{(s+1)^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{(\beta-1)|v|}\left(\frac{1}{((s_{0}-1)|v|+|y|+1)^{\beta-1}}-\frac{1}{(s_{0}|v|+|y|+1)^{\beta-1}}\right).

Using (
2.43

|z|=\sqrt{|v|^{2}+|x|^{2}}=\sqrt{|y|^{2}+|s_{0}|^{2}|v|^{2}+|v|^{2}}\geqslant\tfrac{(s_{0}-1)|v|+|y|+1}{2},

and

(

s_{0}\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\rm d}s}{(|\Gamma_{s}z|+1)^{\beta}}\asymp\int_{0}^{1}\frac{{\rm d}s}{(|y|+|s-s_{0}||v|+1)^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{|v|}\int_{|y|}^{s_{0}|v|+|y|}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{(s+1)^{\beta}}+\frac{1}{|v|}\int_{|y|}^{(1-s_{0})|v|+|y|}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{(s+1)^{\beta}}

(2.49)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\frac{2}{|v|}\int_{|y|}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{(s+1)^{\beta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{AA3}}}{{\lesssim}}\frac{1}{|z|+1}\frac{1}{(|y|+1)^{\beta-1}}=\frac{{\mathcal{M}}_{\beta}(z)}{1+|z|}.

For the lower bound, using the fact that
\max\{s_{0},1-s_{0}\}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}

by (

Recalling that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{(|\Gamma_{s}z|+1)^{\beta}}\gtrsim\frac{1}{|z|+1}\frac{1}{(|y|+1)^{\beta-1}}\left(1-\left(\tfrac{6}{7}\right)^{\beta-1}\right).

(2.50)

Combining (
2.49
) and (

2.42
).

), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\kappa_{1}^{2+3d+3\alpha}{\mathcal{M}}_{d+\alpha}(z)}{(|z|+1)^{d+\alpha+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sup_{w\in Q_{1}(z_{i})}\sqrt{\left|\nabla^{j}_{w}q_{1}(w)\right|}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{C_{2}\kappa_{1}^{3+4d+3\alpha}{\mathcal{M}}_{d+\alpha}(z)}{(|z|+1)^{d+\alpha+1}}=C_{2}\kappa_{1}^{4+4d+3\alpha}\mathcal{N}_{d+\alpha}(z).

Thus, we have proven (
2.52
), and hence

\left|\nabla^{j}_{z}p_{1}(z)\right|\lesssim{\kappa_{1}^{3+4d+3\alpha}}\mathcal{N}_{d+\alpha}(z).

For general
t>0
and
\kappa_{0}>0
, the result follows by (
2.2
). The proof is now complete by Lemma
2.7
.
∎

3.
Proof of Lemma
1.5

Proof.

For
v\neq 0
, let
Q_{v}
be the rotation matrix such that for
\bar{v}=v/|v|
, we have

Q_{v}^{T}\bar{v}=(1,0,\cdots,0)=:{\rm e}_{1}.

Since
Q_{v}
is an orthogonal matrix, i.e.,
Q_{v}Q_{v}^{T}=\mathbb{I}
, for any
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\langle Q_{v}x,\bar{v}\rangle=\langle x,Q_{v}^{T}\bar{v}\rangle=x_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad|Q_{v}x-v|=|x-Q_{v}^{T}v|=|x-|v|{\rm e}_{1}|.

Thus, for each fixed
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
and
\beta>0
, by definition we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\beta}(Q_{v}x,v)=\frac{1}{(1+|z|)^{1+\beta}}\begin{cases}(1+|x|)^{1-\beta},&x_{1}<0,\\
(1+\sqrt{|x|^{2}-x_{1}^{2}})^{1-\beta},&0\leqslant x_{1}\leqslant|v|,\\
(1+|x-|v|{\rm e}_{1}|)^{1-\beta},&x_{1}>|v|.\end{cases}

(3.1)

Below, we frequently use the following simple fact: for
a,b\geqslant 0
,

a\vee b\leqslant a+b\leqslant 2(a\vee b).

Since
\det(Q_{v})=1
and
|Q_{v}x|=|x|
, by a change of variables, we can write

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{q}\mathcal{N}_{\beta}(x,v)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|Q_{v}x|^{q}\mathcal{N}_{\beta}(Q_{v}x,v)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{x_{1}<0}\frac{|x|^{q}}{(1+|z|)^{1+\beta}}(1+|x|)^{1-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{x_{1}\in[0,|v|]}\frac{|x|^{q}}{(1+|z|)^{1+\beta}}(1+\sqrt{|x|^{2}-x_{1}^{2}})^{1-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{x_{1}>|v|}\frac{|x|^{q}}{(1+|z|)^{1+\beta}}(1+|x-|v|{\rm e}_{1}|)^{1-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=:H_{\beta}(v)+K_{\beta}(v)+L_{\beta}(v).

(3.2)

For
H_{\beta}(v)
, using symmetry and polar coordinates, we have

\displaystyle H_{\beta}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|x|^{q}}{(1+|z|)^{1+\beta}}(1+|x|)^{1-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}x\asymp\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r^{q}}{(1+|v|+r)^{1+\beta}}(1+r)^{1-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r^{q}(1\vee r)^{1-\beta}}{((1+|v|)\vee r)^{1+\beta}}\,\mathrm{d}r\asymp\int_{0}^{1+|v|}\frac{r^{q}(1\vee r)^{1-\beta}}{(1+|v|)^{1+\beta}}\,\mathrm{d}r+\int_{1+|v|}^{\infty}r^{q-2\beta}\,\mathrm{d}r.

Since
q<2\beta-1
, we obtain

\displaystyle H_{\beta}(v)\lesssim(1+|v|)^{q-2\beta+1}.

(3.3)

For
K_{\beta}(v)
, by a change of polar coordinates, we have

\displaystyle K_{\beta}(v)

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r^{d-2}}{(1+r)^{\beta-1}}{\rm d}r\int_{0}^{|v|}\frac{(r+x_{1})^{q}}{(1+|v|+r+x_{1})^{1+\beta}}{\rm d}x_{1}

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r^{d-2}}{(1+r)^{\beta-1}(1+|v|+r)^{1+\beta}}{\rm d}r\int_{0}^{|v|}(r+x_{1})^{q}{\rm d}x_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{r^{d-2}|v|}{(1+r)^{\beta-1}(1+|v|+r)^{1+\beta}}\int^{1}_{0}(r+s|v|)^{q}{\rm d}s{\rm d}r

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{|v|}{(1+|v|)^{1+\beta}}\int_{0}^{1+|v|}\frac{r^{d-2}}{(1+r)^{\beta-1}}\int^{1}_{0}(r+s|v|)^{q}{\rm d}s{\rm d}r

\displaystyle\quad+|v|\int_{1+|v|}^{\infty}r^{-2-2\beta+d}\int^{1}_{0}(r+s|v|)^{q}{\rm d}s{\rm d}r=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

For
I_{1}
, for

r<1+|v|
and
|v|\geqslant 1
,
since
\int^{1}_{0}(r+s|v|)^{q}{\rm d}s\asymp(1+|v|)^{q},

we have

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{|v|(1+|v|)^{q}}{(1+|v|)^{1+\beta}}\int_{0}^{1+|v|}\frac{r^{d-2}}{(1+r)^{\beta-1}}{\rm d}r

\displaystyle\asymp(1+|v|)^{q-\beta}\left[\int_{0}^{1}r^{d-2}{\rm d}r+\int^{|v|+1}_{1}r^{d-1-\beta}{\rm d}r\right]\asymp(1+|v|)^{q-\beta}.

For
I_{2}
, by
q<2\beta-d+1
, we have for
|v|\geqslant 1
,

I_{2}\asymp|v|\int_{1+|v|}^{\infty}r^{-2-2\beta+d+q}{\rm d}r\asymp(1+|v|)^{q+d-2\beta}.

Hence,

\displaystyle K_{\beta}(v)\asymp(1+|v|)^{q-\beta}.

(3.4)

Finally, we consider the term
L_{\beta}(v)
. By changing to polar coordinates, we have

\displaystyle L_{\beta}(v)

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{|v|}^{\infty}\frac{(r+x_{1})^{q}r^{d-2}}{(1+|v|+r+x_{1})^{1+\beta}(1+r+x_{1}-|v|)^{\beta-1}}\,\mathrm{d}x_{1}\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-2}\,\mathrm{d}r\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(r+|v|+s)^{q}}{(1+2|v|+r+s)^{1+\beta}(1+r+s)^{\beta-1}}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-2}\,\mathrm{d}r\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{((r+|v|)\vee s)^{q}}{((1+2|v|+r)\vee s)^{1+\beta}((1+r)\vee s)^{\beta-1}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

(3.5)

If
q=2\beta-d
, then

\displaystyle L_{\beta}(v)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int_{1+|v|}^{\infty}r^{d-2}\,\mathrm{d}r\int_{r}^{\infty}\frac{((r+|v|)\vee s)^{q}}{((1+2|v|+r)\vee s)^{1+\beta}((1+r)\vee s)^{\beta-1}}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{1+|v|}^{\infty}r^{d-2}\,\mathrm{d}r\int_{r}^{\infty}s^{-d}\,\mathrm{d}s\stackrel{{\scriptstyle s=ru}}{{=}}\int_{1+|v|}^{\infty}r^{-1}\,\mathrm{d}r\int_{1}^{\infty}u^{-d}\,\mathrm{d}u=\infty.

This implies (
1.16
).

Next, we assume
q<2\beta-d
. For simplicity, we define:

a:=1+r,\quad b:=|v|+r,\quad c:=1+2|v|+r,

and

\mathcal{R}(r):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(b\vee s)^{q}}{(c\vee s)^{1+\beta}(a\vee s)^{\beta-1}}\,\mathrm{d}s.

We can then split
\mathcal{R}(r)
into parts:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(r)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{a}+\int_{a}^{b}+\int_{b}^{c}+\int_{c}^{\infty}\right)\frac{(b\vee s)^{q}}{(c\vee s)^{1+\beta}(a\vee s)^{\beta-1}}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=b^{q}c^{-1-\beta}a^{2-\beta}+b^{q}c^{-1-\beta}\int_{a}^{b}s^{1-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+c^{-1-\beta}\int_{b}^{c}s^{q+1-\beta}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{c}^{\infty}s^{q-2\beta}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim b^{q}c^{-1-\beta}a^{2-\beta}+b^{q+2-\beta}c^{-1-\beta}+c^{q+1-2\beta}.

Since
q<2\beta-d\Rightarrow q+1-2\beta<0
, for
|v|\geqslant 1
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(r)

\displaystyle\lesssim(|v|+r)^{q}(1+2|v|+r)^{-1-\beta}\left[(1+r)^{2-\beta}+(|v|+r)^{2-\beta}\right]+(1+2|v|+r)^{q+1-2\beta}

\displaystyle\lesssim(|v|+r)^{q-1-\beta}(1+r)^{2-\beta}+(|v|+r)^{q+1-2\beta}.

Thus, for
|v|\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle L_{\beta}(v)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{d-2}\left[(|v|\vee r)^{q-1-\beta}(1\vee r)^{2-\beta}+(|v|\vee r)^{q+1-2\beta}\right]\,\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\lesssim|v|^{q-1-\beta}\left[1+\int_{1}^{|v|}r^{d-2}\left(r^{2-\beta}+|v|^{2-\beta}\right)\,\mathrm{d}r\right]+\int_{|v|}^{\infty}r^{d+q-1-2\beta}\,\mathrm{d}r\lesssim|v|^{q-1-\beta}.

(3.6)

Combining (
3.2
), (
3.3
), (
3.4
), and (
3.6
), we obtain (
1.17
).
∎
