Association of B cells and the risk of Esophageal cancer: a bidirectional two-sample mendelian randomization study
Authors: Jinzhou Guo, Gao Si, Xuejie Song, Fuchun Si
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesCurrently, research on the role of B cells in esophageal cancer (EC) is limited, and existing studies on their impact are controversial. Therefore, this study was conducted to elucidate the complex causal relationship between B cells and EC, expand the understanding of esophageal cancer immunology.MethodsBidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) was performed to assess the causal relationships between 190 B cell phenotypes and EC. To complement the MR analysis, Bayesian Weighted Mendelian Randomization (BWMR) was employed, and sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the robustness of the findings. Positive results were further validated in independent cohorts of esophageal cancer studies. In addition, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were utilized for validation, incorporating B cell-related gene expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis to support the MR findings.ResultsIn the primary analysis, significant causal relationships were observed between 5 B cell types and the risk of EC; the onset of EC was causally linked to 3 B cell phenotypes. Validation in other cohorts revealed that 4 outcomes aligned with the primary analysis, included were CD19 on IgD + CD38-, CD20 on IgD- CD27-, CD20 on IgD- CD38br, and CD38 on PB/PC. Further validation using RNA-seq data showed that CD38 mRNA was significantly overexpressed in EC tissues, whereas CD19 and MS4A1 mRNA levels did not differ significantly between tumor and normal tissues. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that CD19, MS4A1, and CD38 are involved in multiple tumor-related immune pathways, suggesting their pivotal role in regulating the tumor immune microenvironment.ConclusionsOur study suggests a potential connection between B cell phenotypes and EC through bidirectional two-sample MR combined with BWMR analysis, providing a preliminary basis for future research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-024-13166-w.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) is a common malignancy of the digestive system, ranking eighth in incidence and sixth in mortality worldwide among all cancers [1]. EC consists of two primary histological subtypes: esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), which is more prevalent in East Asia and other regions, and esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC), which is increasingly common in Western populations [2]. Each subtype presents distinct challenges regarding risk factors, pathogenesis, and treatment. ESCC is closely linked to smoking, alcohol use, and dietary habits, while EAC is associated with gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and Barrett’s esophagus [3, 4]. Despite these differences in etiology, immunological mechanisms are key to the progression and response to treatment in both subtypes [5]. Surgery remains the primary treatment for esophageal cancer. However, the early diagnosis rate of this disease is low, with many patients already presenting with metastasis upon detection, rendering treatment more challenging [6]. Despite some advancements in drug therapies, the overall 5-year survival rate for esophageal cancer is less than 30%, dropping to a mere 5% for advanced cases [7]. Immunotherapy, which harnesses the patient’s own immune system to combat cancer cells [8–10], has demonstrated notable success in various cancer types [11]. In recent years, immunotherapy has made significant strides in the field of cancer treatment, offering new hope for esophageal cancer patients. However, due to the presence of primary or acquired resistance, only a minority of patients benefit from immunotherapy, the overall response rate (ORR) of immunotherapy in patients with EC is lower than 30% [12]. therefore, the identification of biomarkers capable of predicting and assessing immunotherapy responses is key to enhancing treatment outcomes for esophageal cancer patients.

B cells constitute a crucial subset of lymphocytes within the adaptive immune system, participating in humoral immune responses. Nonetheless, immunooncology research primarily focuses on T cells, particularly tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes (TIL-T) [13], often overlooking the role of B cells in cancer immunotherapy. Furthermore, the functional implications of B cells in human cancer have been contentious, with conflicting conclusions drawn from various studies [14]. B cells have been suggested to possess both antitumor [15–17] and protumo [18, 19] functions in tumor immunity and immunotherapy [20, 21].These discrepant findings may be attributed to the significant heterogeneity in surface immunophenotypes and functions among B cell populations [22]. Presently, there is limited research on the role of B cells in esophageal cancer, and existing studies on their impact remain controversial [23].Thus, a comprehensive investigation into the intricate relationship between B cells and esophageal cancer holds significant implications for advancing understanding of the disease and the development of immunotherapeutic strategies. It is noteworthy that due to the complex interplay between the immune system and tumors, research findings are susceptible to interference from reverse causation, underscoring the critical importance of selecting appropriate research methodologies.

Mendelian Randomization (MR) is a statistical analysis method that adheres to the principles of Mendelian randomization and the law of independent assortment, utilizing single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer potential causal relationships between exposure risk factors and outcomes [24, 25]. MR employs genetic variants as instrumental variables, which are fixed at conception, to perform causal inferences regarding the effects of modifiable risk factors. This approach can effectively mitigate certain types of confounding [26], thereby offering plausible causal associations. Additionally, we employed Bayesian Weighted Mendelian Randomization (BWMR) for causal inference [27]. This method explicitly accounts for the uncertainty associated with weak effects arising from polygenicity and addresses violations of the IV assumption by employing Bayesian-weighted outlier detection. Consequently, this study employs a bidirectional two-sample MR approach, leveraging large-scale GWAS databases to investigate the causal interplay between B cell phenotypes and EC. This research provides a novel direction for the treatment and management of EC.

Materials and methods

Study design

The present study employed a bidirectional two-sample MR approach to investigate the causal relationships between 190 B cell traits and EC. To ensure the robustness of causal inference, instrumental variables (IVs) were required to adhere to three fundamental assumptions: (1) Each IV must establish direct connections with the exposure factors; (2) Each IV should not be correlated with potential confounding variables lying between the exposure and the outcome; and (3) Each IV should not influence the outcome through a pathway unrelated to the exposure [25]. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE-MR guidelines for Mendelian Randomization studies. All MR analyses were conducted using publicly available summary statistics, thereby obviating the necessity for additional ethical approval or informed consent. Instrumental variable selection

Additionally, to ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted a power analysis using the mRnd tool (http://glimmer.rstudio.com/kn3in/mRnd/) [35], selecting a threshold of ≥ 80% power, which is widely accepted in genetic epidemiology studies. This ensured our study had sufficient power to detect true causal effects between B cell phenotypes and esophageal cancer.

MR analysis and BWMR analysis

Utilizing the R software package “Two Sample MR”, we conducted a bidirectional MR analysis to investigate the causal interrelationship between 190 B cell phenotypes and esophageal cancer. The primary analytical method utilized was inverse variance weighting (IVW) [36]. Additionally, we employed four other methods: simple mode [37], weighted median [38], weighted mode, and MR-Egger [39], to assess the reliability and consistency of the results. The bidirectional MR analysis serves as mutual validation, acting as reverse MR validations, thereby further bolstering the reliability and practical significance of this study. BWMR analysis complements two-sample MR by addressing polygenic structure and pleiotropy, factors often ignored in MR analysis [40]. Given that 190 B cell phenotypes were analyzed in the present study, a False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons, with an FDR-corrected p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Positive results from MR analysis were validated in other cohorts using methods using IVW. The series of analyses undertaken has contributed to the enhancement of result stability and reliability, thereby bolstering causal inference within epidemiological research.

Sensitivity analysis

The positive findings from the MR analysis underwent thorough validation through heterogeneity testing, horizontal pleiotropy testing, and leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, with the goal of bolstering confidence in the results. Heterogeneity among individual estimates of genetic variation was assessed using the Cochran Q test, revealing no significant heterogeneity when P > 0.05 [41]. The MR-Egger intercept, along with the Mendelian Randomization Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) method, was utilized to evaluate the impact of horizontal pleiotropy. Given the inclusion of the intercept term in regression analysis by the MR-Egger method, a horizontal multiple validity test was employed to compare it with the IVW method, enabling the assessment of horizontal pleiotropy among instrumental variables (IVs). If P > 0.05, signifying the absence of horizontal pleiotropy, the IVW method is employed for the MR analysis outcome [42]. MR-PRESSO, equipped to test for pleiotropy or outliers, served as an indicator for assessing and rectifying the level of heterogeneity [43]. The MR-PRESSO analysis was conducted using the R package “MRPRESSO.” Furthermore, sensitivity analysis was carried out using a “leave-one-out” approach to gauge the influence of individual IVs on the overall MR analysis results, offering robust evidence for the stability of the findings.

Validation of MR analysis results based on RNA-seq data

B cell-related gene expression analysis

The RNA sequencing data of esophageal cancer, including 13 normal tissue samples and 183 tumor tissue samples, were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://www.cancer.gov). All data were raw count data and were summarized and processed using Perl (version v5.30.0). The expression differences of B cell-related genes between normal and tumor tissues were analyzed and visualized using the R software (version 4.2.1) and the “ggplot2”, “stats”, and “limma” packages. First, the gene expression data were log2-transformed to reduce the impact of skewness. Then, Wilcoxon rank-sum test or two-sample t-test was used to compare the gene expression differences between normal and tumor tissues. A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was set.

Functional enrichment analysis

The tumor tissue samples were divided into high-expression and low-expression groups based on the median expression level of B cell-related genes. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the high-expression and low-expression groups were identified using the “limma” package in R software, with the selection criteria set as log2|fold change| ≥ 1 and False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05. After the DEGs were identified, heatmaps and volcano plots were generated using the “pheatmap” and “ggplot2” packages in R software. Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were further performed on the identified DEGs. GO analysis was used to explore the potential roles of the DEGs in biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions, while KEGG analysis identified the signaling pathways involved. All analyses were performed using the “clusterProfiler” (version 4.10.1) R package, with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Exploration of the causal effect of B cell phenotypes on EC

The tests revealed no significant heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy among the IVs (P > 0.05). Thus, the outcomes of the IVW analysis were employed as the results of the MR analysis, with a fixed-effects model utilized to present the final findings. The results of MR analysis combined with BWMR analysis showed significantly causal relationships between five kinds of B cell traits and EC. Specifically including: (1) CD25 on IgD + CD38dim (IVW OR = 1.117, 95% CI = 1.008 ~ 1.237, P = 0.035); (2) CD20 on IgD- CD38br (IVW OR = 0.898, 95% CI = 0.811 ~ 0.994, P = 0.038); (3) CD38 on PB/PC (IVW OR = 1.220, 95% CI = 1.003 ~ 1.483, P = 0.047); (4) CD19 on IgD + CD38- (IVW OR = 0.896, 95% CI = 0.829 ~ 0.968, P = 0.006); (5) CD20 on IgD- CD27- (IVW OR = 0.742, 95% CI = 0.609 ~ 0.904, P = 0.003)(Fig. 2). Scatter plots were utilized to illustrate the causal effect estimates (Fig. 3). Additionally, the robustness of the MR analysis results was assessed by leave-one analysis (Fig. 4) and funnel plot (Fig. 5).

B cell-related gene expression analysis

Based on the MR analysis, we selected three key B cell-related genes: CD19, MS4A1 (CD20), and CD38 for further investigation.The gene expression differences of these three CD molecules were analyzed between normal esophageal tissues and esophageal cancer tissues using RNA sequencing data from the TCGA database. The results indicated that the expression levels of CD19 and MS4A1 mRNA were not significantly different between cancerous and normal tissues (Fig. 11A-D). In contrast, both unpaired and paired sample analyses demonstrated a significant overexpression of CD38 mRNA in esophageal cancer tissues compared to normal tissues (P < 0.05, Fig. 11E, F).

Discussion

This study used GWAS genetic variation data to explore the causal relationships between 190 B cell phenotypes and esophageal cancer (EC). To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the potential causal relationships between multiple B cell phenotypes and EC using bidirectional two-sample MR combined with BWMR. After applying strict inclusion criteria and conducting sensitivity analyses, we identified four B cell phenotypes that show a significant causal relationship with EC risk. These phenotypes include CD19 on IgD + CD38-, CD20 on IgD- CD27-, CD20 on IgD- CD38br, and CD38 on PB/PC. Further gene expression analysis revealed that CD38 mRNA expression was significantly increased in tumor tissues, while CD19 and MS4A1 mRNA levels showed no significant changes. This finding is consistent with the MR results. However, it is important to note that although CD19 and MS4A1 mRNA were not significantly expressed in adjacent non-cancerous tissues, their functional regulation may still be crucial.

While validating findings across different ethnic groups enhances generalizability, the results in European and East Asian cohorts, though directionally consistent, were not statistically significant. This could be due to smaller sample sizes, genetic differences, environmental factors, or population stratification bias. The distinct distribution of esophageal cancer subtypes may also contribute to the inconsistent results, as squamous cell carcinoma is more common in Asia, while adenocarcinoma predominates in Europe. These subtype differences could influence the role of B cell phenotypes in esophageal cancer. In addition, although the initial analysis showed a significant causal relationship between EC onset and three B cell traits, the inconsistent results in other cohorts may also be due to these factors. The immune system’s response to EC may also vary after onset, which could be another important factor. Further studies with larger, more diverse datasets and better control of confounding factors are needed to explore these interactions.

The first trait is CD19 on IgD + CD38-. In our study, CD19 on IgD + CD38- was found to be negatively correlated with the risk of EC. The human CD19 antigen is a 95 kDa transmembrane glycoprotein belonging to the immunoglobulin (Ig) superfamily [44]. CD19 plays a key role in modulating both B cell receptor (BCR)-dependent and -independent signal transduction, crucial for establishing the intrinsic signaling threshold of B cells [45]. It plays a role in antigen-independent development as well as immunoglobulin-induced B cell activation. Therefore, CD19 is essential for eliciting optimal immune responses in the body. In our enrichment analysis, CD19 was shown to be involved in key immune pathways, including B cell receptor signaling and peptidase inhibitor activity, highlighting its role in immune regulation. Additionally, its enrichment in fat digestion and absorption suggests a link between immune function and metabolic regulation. These findings support CD19’s role in both immune responses and metabolic processes, aligning with its negative correlation with EC risk observed in our study. In a clinical retrospective study, it was demonstrated that esophageal cancer patients with a peripheral blood lymphocyte CD19 lymphocyte percentage exceeding 10% exhibited significantly better survival outcomes compared to patient groups with CD19 lymphocyte percentages below 5% and between 5–10% [46]. Furthermore, based on its surface molecule expression profile, CD19 on IgD + CD38- is categorized as naïve B cells [47]. naïve B cells constitute a critically important subset of immune cells, forming the foundation of immune defense. In a study on prostate cancer (PCa), it was found that PCa patients exhibit a significant reduction in the peripheral blood CD19 B-cell compartment compared to healthy donors, with a predominance of naïve B cells [48]. While the role of naïve B cells in peripheral blood in esophageal cancer has not been reported to date, our findings suggest the presence of protective factors against esophageal cancer risk within the naïve B cells phenotype. This discovery offers a novel perspective for understanding the pathogenesis of esophageal cancer.

The second and third involve CD20 on IgD- CD27-, and CD20 on IgD- CD38br. CD20 belongs to the membrane-spanning 4-domains subfamily A (MS4A) protein family, characterized by a molecular weight of 33–37 kDa and is non-glycosylated. Research has demonstrated that CD20 is considered a crucial surface marker for maintaining the resting state of B cells [49]. Hence, it can be inferred that these two B-cell phenotypes are indicative of resting B cells. CD20 + B cells have been shown to potentially augment Interferon gamma (IFN-γ) production, thereby bolstering anti-tumor immunity [50]. Conversely, CD20- B cells have been implicated in fostering tumor progression through the secretion of immunosuppressive molecules like Interleukin-10 (IL-10) and Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β). Previous research has demonstrated that a heightened presence of CD20 + Tumor-Infiltrating Lymphocytes (TILs) correlates with a more favorable prognosis in squamous cell carcinoma [51]. Our research findings indicate a negative correlation between CD20 on IgD- CD27- and CD20 on IgD- CD38br with the risk of EC, consistent with previous studies. Specifically, higher expression of CD20 on B cells is associated with reduced risk of developing EC. Although MS4A1 (CD20) mRNA expression showed no significant difference between adjacent non-cancerous tissues and tumor tissues, its functional regulation may still be critical in the immune system. Our enrichment analysis revealed that CD20 is significantly involved in several immune-related and metabolic pathways, such as C-C chemokine receptor activity and chemokine binding, suggesting its role in immune cell recruitment and activation. Additionally, CD20’s enrichment in protein digestion and absorption and retinol metabolism pathways indicates its potential influence on immune function through metabolic regulation. These findings support CD20’s key role in reducing the risk of esophageal cancer and further suggest that it may function through both metabolic and immune pathways in the tumor immune microenvironment. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that CD20 on IgD- CD27- represents a double-negative B cell phenotype. Based on the expression of IgD and CD27, B cells can be classified into four categories [52], among which double-negative B cells are a rare B cell subset, comprising approximately 5% of all peripheral B cells in healthy individuals [53]. In the field of oncology, IgD − CD27 − B cells have been detected in two types of cancer: non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [54] and nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) [55]. Unfortunately, double-negative B cells are enriched in the tumor microenvironment (TME) of both NSCLC and NPC. Compared to normal lung tissue, the proportion of DN B cells in tumor samples is significantly increased. Therefore, despite the negative correlation observed between CD20 on IgD- CD27- phenotype and esophageal cancer in our results, further investigation into its role in esophageal cancer is warranted.

The final parameter is CD38 on PB/PC. Upon encountering antigens, B cells undergo activation and differentiate into plasmablasts (PB), and plasmacytes (PC). Under physiological conditions, the ratio of PB/PC to lymphocytes maintains a relatively elevated level in the peripheral blood [56]. In our study, we found that CD38 on PB/PC is positively correlated with the risk of EC, which may be associated with the expression of CD38. CD38 may be associated with tumor progression and immune evasion in solid tumors. Our study found that CD38 mRNA expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues, and enrichment analysis revealed that CD38 is involved in several tumor-related immune regulatory pathways, including the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, calcium signaling pathway, and antigen processing and presentation. These pathways play important roles in the tumor microenvironment, particularly by regulating immune cell activation and antigen presentation. CD38 may contribute to tumor progression by promoting immune evasion and inhibiting effective anti-tumor immune responses. Additionally, GO analysis indicated that CD38 has unique functions, such as MHC protein binding and peptide antigen binding, further supporting its key role in tumor immune evasion. Previous studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between CD38 expression levels and increased adenosine production, a key immunosuppressive factor in the tumor microenvironment [57]. In certain solid tumors, high expression of CD38 can lead to abundant adenosine production in the tumor microenvironment. Adenosine recruits immunosuppressive cells such as Treg cells, MDSCs, and CAFs, thereby suppressing the activity of the immune system [58]. Data from melanoma, glioblastoma, EC, cervical cancer, and lung cancer suggest that CD38 exhibits immunosuppressive functions [59]. In these cases, CD38 appears to act as a factor promoting tumor progression. It is noteworthy that in our study, we observed a negative correlation between the expression of CD20 on IgD- CD38br phenotype of resting B cells and the risk of EC. Previous research has suggested that the re-expression of CD20 can restore the resting phenotype of B cells [49], indicating that our findings may not be contradictory. This underscores the potential benefits of restoring the resting state of B cells. However, these results also indicate the potential risks associated with solely targeting specific surface markers on B cells or pursuing immunotherapy targeting a single marker. A more comprehensive understanding of the immune system is necessary to effectively mitigate errors and adverse reactions.

Our study has inherent limitations. Firstly, although we attempted to minimize the influence of confounding factors as much as possible, we could not entirely eliminate the potential impact of other confounding variables on the results, particularly those related to genetic and environmental factors. Secondly, although validating findings across different ethnic groups enhances the generalizability and robustness of our conclusions, it also introduces the potential for population stratification bias, which could affect the accuracy of our results. Thirdly, the lack of individual-level data prevented us from performing stratified analyses based on specific characteristics, such as age, gender, and disease subtype. This limitation may have resulted in less precise conclusions, especially given the distinct pathogenesis, pathological characteristics, and population distribution of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma. Furthermore, the limited availability of GWAS data specific to these subtypes restricts our ability to perform further in-depth analyses on the subtypes of esophageal cancer. Lastly, although the validation in both the European and East Asian cohorts showed consistent directional trends, the results did not reach statistical significance. This limits the generalizability of our findings and reduces the ability to confidently confirm the associations across different populations. This highlights the need for further investigation to confirm these findings in more diverse populations and different histological subtypes of EC. Additionally, the flexible nature of the outcome assessment criteria in this study may have increased the probability of false-positive results.

Conclusions

In summary, our study identified a potential connection between B cell phenotypes and EC using bidirectional two-sample MR combined with BWMR analysis. While our approach minimized the influence of reverse causality and some confounding factors, limitations such as population stratification and sample size in validation cohorts indicate that further research is required to confirm these findings. Nonetheless, these results offer a novel perspective for exploring the biological mechanisms underlying EC and provide valuable insights for future research in tumor immunology, which may contribute to the development of early intervention and treatment strategies.
