Network pharmacology and experimental validation reveal the anti-inflammatory mechanisms of andrographolide in the treatment of periodontitis
Authors: Xia Chen, Yu Luo, Xiaoyan Zou, Hui Ji, Mingji Yang, Fang Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontitis is a prevalent chronic inflammatory disease characterized by progressive destruction of periodontal tissues, driven by an exaggerated host immune response. Andrographolide, a bioactive compound extracted from Andrographis paniculata, has demonstrated significant anti-inflammatory potential in various disease models, yet its precise mechanisms of action in periodontitis remain unclear.MethodsAn integrated network pharmacology approach was employed to identify the intersecting targets of andrographolide and periodontitis, followed by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction and topological analysis were conducted to identify hub genes, which were further evaluated through molecular docking. Finally, in vitro experiments using LPS-stimulated macrophages were performed to validate the anti-inflammatory effects of andrographolide via ELISA, RT-qPCR, and Western blotting.ResultsA total of 97 common targets were identified between andrographolide and periodontitis. Enrichment analyses revealed significant involvement in inflammatory and immune-related pathways, including the IL-17, TNF, Toll-like receptor, and NF-κB signaling pathways. Four hub genes (TP53, STAT3, NFKB1, and JUN) were identified as central nodes in the PPI network, and molecular docking confirmed favorable binding affinities with andrographolide. In vitro validation demonstrated that andrographolide significantly reduced LPS-induced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-17, TNF-α, and MCP1) at both the transcriptional and protein levels in a dose-dependent manner.ConclusionThis study elucidates the multi-target anti-inflammatory mechanisms of andrographolide in the context of periodontitis and highlights its potential as a promising therapeutic agent for modulating periodontal inflammation.Keywords: Andrographolide, Periodontitis, Network pharmacology, Inflammation, Macrophages, Cytokines, Molecular docking, Experimental validation

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic, multifactorial inflammatory disease initiated by microbial dysbiosis and sustained by an aberrant host immune response, ultimately resulting in the destruction of periodontal connective tissue and alveolar bone [1–4]. It represents a leading cause of tooth loss in adults and is closely linked to systemic diseases such as diabetes mellitus, atherosclerosis, and adverse pregnancy outcomes [5–9]. While the initial trigger of periodontitis lies in the accumulation of subgingival biofilms, the progression and severity of the disease are largely driven by the host-mediated inflammatory response, including excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [10–12]. Conventional treatment strategies primarily rely on mechanical debridement and adjunctive antibiotics; however, these approaches are often insufficient to fully resolve inflammation or prevent recurrence, and long-term antibiotic use raises concerns about resistance and microbiome disruption [13–15]. Consequently, increasing attention has been directed toward the development of novel host-modulatory therapies aimed at controlling the inflammatory component of periodontitis.

Natural compounds derived from medicinal plants have gained significant interest due to their pharmacological potential and relatively low toxicity profiles [16–18]. Among these, andrographolide, a diterpenoid lactone extracted from Andrographis paniculata, has been widely reported to exert potent anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and antioxidant effects [19–21]. Mechanistic studies have shown that andrographolide can inhibit the activation of key inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB, JAK/STAT, and MAPK, suppress macrophage polarization, and reduce the production of pro-inflammatory mediators including TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-17 [22–26]. While these properties suggest a strong therapeutic potential in inflammatory disorders, the molecular targets and pathways through which andrographolide may modulate host responses in the context of periodontitis remain to be systematically elucidated.

With the advancement of systems pharmacology and bioinformatics, network pharmacology has emerged as a powerful approach to uncover the multi-target mechanisms of natural compounds and their interactions within disease-related biological networks [27–29]. Coupling this strategy with molecular docking and experimental validation enables a comprehensive exploration of candidate drug actions from computational prediction to experimental evidence.

In this study, we adopted an integrated strategy combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vitro validation to explore the potential therapeutic mechanisms of andrographolide in the treatment of periodontitis. First, the intersection between andrographolide-associated targets and periodontitis-related genes was identified and subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analysis to investigate their biological functions and signaling involvement. A PPI network was constructed to identify hub genes using topological algorithms, followed by molecular docking to evaluate the binding affinity between andrographolide and these core targets. Finally, in vitro experiments using LPS-stimulated macrophages were conducted to assess the effects of andrographolide on cytokine production at both the mRNA and protein levels.

This study provides novel insights into the multi-target mechanisms of andrographolide against periodontitis, highlights its potential regulatory roles in host inflammatory responses, and lays a theoretical foundation for future therapeutic applications of natural compounds in periodontal disease.

Methods

Collection of andrographolide- and periodontitis-related targets

The chemical structure and corresponding SMILES format of andrographolide were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Potential targets of andrographolide were predicted using multiple public platforms, including SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), PharmMapper (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/), and SEA (http://sea.bkslab.org/), with species restricted to “Homo sapiens.” Redundant targets were removed, and gene symbols were normalized using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/).Periodontitis-related genes were obtained from the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), DisGeNET (https://www.disgenet.org/), and OMIM (https://www.omim.org/) databases by searching the keyword “periodontitis.” The intersecting genes between andrographolide targets and periodontitis-associated genes were identified using the VennDiagram package in R software (version 4.2.2).To reduce false-positive and low-relevance entries, only genes with a GeneCards relevance score above the median value were retained. For DisGeNET, genes with a gene–disease association score greater than 0.1 were included. Genes retrieved from OMIM were retained when direct disease relevance to periodontitis was indicated. After merging the results from the three databases, duplicate entries were removed and gene symbols were standardized using the UniProt database.

Disease enrichment and network construction

To explore the pathological relevance of the intersecting targets, disease enrichment analysis was performed using the DisGeNET database, and the top enriched terms were visualized. A compound–target–disease network was constructed using Cytoscape (version 3.9.1) to illustrate the interaction among andrographolide, core targets, and periodontitis.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were carried out using the clusterProfiler package in R. GO terms were classified into three categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). KEGG pathways with P < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched and visualized using the ggplot2 and enrichplot packages. A pathway–gene interaction network was built to highlight the key inflammatory cascades, and representative targets were mapped onto the IL-17 signaling pathway using the KEGG Mapper tool.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis and hub gene identification

To reveal the interaction patterns among the intersecting genes, a PPI network was constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) with a minimum required interaction score > 0.4. The network was visualized and analyzed in Cytoscape. Hub genes were identified using the CytoHubba plugin based on five topological algorithms: Degree, MCC, MNC, EPC, and Closeness. The top 10 genes from each method were overlapped using Venn analysis to determine the core targets.

Molecular docking analysis

The 3D structure of andrographolide was obtained from PubChem and prepared using AutoDock Tools. The crystal structures of TP53, STAT3, NFKB1, and JUN were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank, with PDB IDs of 3DCY, 6NJS, 8TQD, and 1JNM, respectively. Before docking, water molecules and original ligands were removed from the protein structures, and polar hydrogen atoms were added. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina. Binding poses were visualized using PyMOL, and hydrogen bond interactions were analyzed to evaluate binding stability.

Cell culture and treatment

RAW264.7 murine macrophages were purchased from Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. Cells were pre-treated with andrographolide (10, 20, or 30 µM) for 2 h, followed by stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 µg/mL) for 24 h to induce an inflammatory response.

Cell viability assay

The cytotoxicity of andrographolide was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. RAW264.7 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated with various concentrations of andrographolide (1–40 µM) for 24 h. CCK-8 reagent (10 µL) was added, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm after 2 h using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage of the control.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

After treatment, culture supernatants were collected to quantify the levels of IL-6, IL-17, TNF-α, and MCP1 using commercial ELISA kits (from Elabscience, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard curves generated in parallel.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (TAKARA). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix on a kit template. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with β-actin as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against IL-6 (Proteintech, 21865-1-AP, Rabbit pAb, 1:1000), IL-17 (Proteintech, 26163-1-AP, Rabbit pAb, 1:1000), TNF-α (Proteintech, 17590-1-AP, Rabbit pAb, 1:1000), and β-actin (Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig, Mouse mAb clone 2D4H5, 1:5000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG(H + L) secondary antibody (Proteintech, SA00001-2, 1:5000) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG(H + L) secondary antibody (Proteintech, SA00002-2, 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to statistical testing, data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare differences among multiple groups, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of common targets between andrographolide and periodontitis

To investigate the potential therapeutic targets of andrographolide in the treatment of periodontitis, we first identified the intersection between the predicted targets of andrographolide and the known disease-related targets of periodontitis. As shown in the Venn diagram (Fig. 1B), a total of 97 common targets were identified, among 148 andrographolide-related targets and 3,582 periodontitis-related genes.To explore the biological relevance of these intersecting targets, we performed a disease enrichment analysis using the DisGeNET database. The top enriched disease terms included various malignant tumors such as breast carcinoma, colon cancer, and ovarian cancer, as well as chronic inflammatory diseases like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and obstructive lung disease (Fig. 1A). These results suggest that the common targets are involved not only in tumor progression but also in inflammation-related pathological processes, which are highly relevant to the etiology of periodontitis.Furthermore, we constructed a compound–target–disease network to visualize the interaction among andrographolide, the 97 common targets, and periodontitis (Fig. 1C). In this network, andrographolide was connected to multiple key inflammatory and immune-related genes, such as TNF, IL6, MMP9, CXCL8, PTGS2, and NFKB1, indicating its potential to modulate host immune responses and inflammatory cascades associated with periodontitis.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of common targets

To further elucidate the biological functions and signaling pathways involved in the therapeutic effects of andrographolide on periodontitis, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed based on the 97 intersecting targets.As shown in Fig. 2A, the GO enrichment analysis covered three categories: BP, CC, and MF. In the BP category, the common targets were significantly enriched in immune and inflammatory responses, such as response to lipopolysaccharide, response to molecule of bacterial origin, cellular response to oxidative stress, and regulation of apoptotic signaling pathways. In the CC category, most targets were localized to membrane raft, plasma membrane raft, and RNA polymerase II transcription regulator complex. Regarding MF terms, enriched items included cytokine receptor binding, DNA-binding transcription factor activity, and ubiquitin-like protein ligase binding. These results suggest that andrographolide may exert therapeutic effects through modulating immune response, inflammation, and transcriptional regulation.KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified multiple significantly enriched pathways (P < 0.05), among which the IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, and NF-κB signaling pathway were particularly prominent (Fig. 2B).Notably, the IL-17 signaling pathway, which has been extensively implicated in periodontal inflammation and bone destruction, was highlighted as a core pathway (red box). The pathway-target network (Fig. 2C) further illustrated that multiple hub genes including MMP9, IL6, NFKB1, CXCL8, and MAPK1 were shared across multiple inflammation-related signaling pathways, underscoring their pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of periodontitis.To visually illustrate the potential molecular mechanism, we mapped the common targets onto the KEGG IL-17 signaling pathway (Fig. 2D). Several key targets such as IL6, CXCL8,MMP9 were highlighted, suggesting that andrographolide may alleviate periodontal inflammation through suppressing IL-17–mediated downstream cascades, including pro-inflammatory cytokine production, chemokine signaling, and matrix degradation.

Identification of hub genes via PPI network analysis

To identify the key targets involved in the therapeutic effects of andrographolide against periodontitis, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network based on the 97 intersecting genes using the STRING database and visualized it in Cytoscape (Fig. 3A). The network consisted of 97 nodes and 1,365 edges, indicating a highly connected interactome.To further screen for potential hub genes, we applied five topological analysis algorithms in the CytoHubba plugin, namely MCC (Maximal Clique Centrality), MNC (Maximum Neighborhood Component), Degree, EPC (Edge Percolated Component), and Closeness. The top 10 genes ranked by each algorithm were visualized respectively (Fig. 3C–F). Among them, TP53, STAT3, NFKB1, and JUN were consistently ranked in the top across multiple algorithms.A Venn diagram was constructed to determine the overlapping hub genes identified by all five algorithms (Fig. 3G). Four genes—TP53, STAT3, NFKB1, and JUN—were found to be the shared core genes, suggesting their central role in the PPI network and potential as key molecular targets mediating the anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects of andrographolide in periodontitis.These hub genes are well-known regulators of immune and inflammatory pathways. For instance, TP53 is involved in inflammation-induced apoptosis, STAT3 regulates cytokine signaling and macrophage activation, NFKB1 is a master regulator of NF-κB–mediated transcription, and JUN contributes to the AP-1–dependent expression of pro-inflammatory mediators. Together, they may constitute a core regulatory module through which andrographolide modulates periodontal inflammation.

Molecular docking analysis of andrographolide with core targets

To further validate the binding affinity between andrographolide and the identified core targets (TP53, STAT3, NFKB1, and JUN), molecular docking analysis was conducted using AutoDock software. The three-dimensional interaction models were visualized using PyMOL (Fig. 4), and the molecular docking results were shown in Table 1. As shown in Fig. 4A, andrographolide binds to TP53 (PDB ID: 3DCY) through two key hydrogen bonds, forming interactions with ARG-10 (2.2 Å) and PRO-333 (2.0 Å), suggesting a stable binding conformation within the TP53 active pocket. For STAT3 (PDB ID: 6NJS), andrographolide was embedded within the SH2 domain of the protein (Fig. 4B), indicating potential interference with STAT3 dimerization and transcriptional activity, though detailed binding residues were not shown in this image.In the case of NFKB1 (PDB ID: 8TQD), andrographolide formed hydrogen bonds with SER-112 (3.0 Å) and ARG-270 (2.1 Å) (Fig. 4C), which are located near the DNA-binding interface, potentially blocking its transcriptional activation.As for JUN (PDB ID: 1JNM), the compound was docked into the basic leucine zipper (bZIP) domain, the DNA-interacting region (Fig. 4D), suggesting that andrographolide may disrupt AP-1 transcriptional function through competitive binding.These results indicate that andrographolide has favorable binding potential with all four hub proteins. The presence of multiple hydrogen bonds and docking at functional domains suggest that andrographolide may exert its regulatory effects through direct interaction with key transcription factors involved in inflammation and immune responses.

In vitro validation of andrographolide’s anti-inflammatory effects

To investigate the anti-inflammatory potential of andrographolide, an in vitro inflammation model was established using lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 µg/mL) to stimulate cytokine production in cultured cells. The chemical structure of andrographolide is shown in Fig. 5A. A CCK-8 assay was first performed to evaluate cell viability following treatment with increasing concentrations of andrographolide (1–40 µM). As shown in Fig. 5B, concentrations up to 20 µM showed no significant cytotoxicity, while 30 µM caused a slight decrease (**P < 0.01), and 40 µM resulted in a marked reduction in viability (***P < 0.001). Based on these results, concentrations of 10–30 µM were selected for subsequent analyses.To assess the anti-inflammatory effects, ELISA was used to quantify the levels of IL-6, IL-17, TNF-α, and MCP1 in the culture supernatants. As shown in Figs. 5C–F, LPS stimulation markedly increased the secretion of all four cytokines compared to the unstimulated control (***P < 0.001), confirming successful induction of an inflammatory response. Treatment with andrographolide significantly inhibited cytokine production in a dose-dependent manner. IL-6 and MCP1 secretion were both sharply reduced at all tested concentrations (###P < 0.001), while IL-17 and TNF-α levels also decreased significantly with increasing andrographolide doses (#P < 0.05 to ###P < 0.001). These results demonstrate that andrographolide effectively suppresses LPS-induced pro-inflammatory cytokine release in vitro, supporting its potential therapeutic role in modulating inflammation.

Validation of inflammatory gene expression at the mRNA and protein levels

To further confirm the anti-inflammatory effects of andrographolide at the transcriptional and protein levels, we examined the expression of IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α in LPS-stimulated cells using RT-qPCR and Western blotting.As shown in Figs. 6A–C, LPS treatment significantly upregulated the mRNA levels of all three cytokines compared with the unstimulated control (***P < 0.001). Andrographolide treatment effectively downregulated the expression of IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α in a dose-dependent manner. The most pronounced inhibitory effect was observed at concentrations of 20–30 µM, with statistical significance ranging from *P < 0.05 to ***P < 0.001 across all targets. Consistently, Western blot analysis revealed that LPS markedly increased the protein levels of IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α, while andrographolide treatment resulted in a clear reduction in their expression (Fig. 6D). The inhibitory effect became more evident with increasing concentrations of andrographolide, confirming its suppressive action on pro-inflammatory signaling at both the transcriptional and translational levels.These findings, together with the ELISA results, reinforce the conclusion that andrographolide exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects by downregulating key cytokines implicated in LPS-induced inflammatory responses.

Discussion

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by persistent immune activation and tissue destruction in response to bacterial infection [4, 30–32]. In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to the therapeutic potential of natural compounds with anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties in periodontal therapy [18, 33]. In this study, we systematically explored the potential mechanisms by which andrographolide, a diterpenoid lactone isolated from Andrographis paniculata, may exert protective effects against periodontitis by integrating network pharmacology, molecular docking, and experimental validation.

By intersecting the predicted targets of andrographolide with disease-related genes from the DisGeNET database, we identified 97 common targets potentially involved in the treatment of periodontitis. These targets were enriched in inflammation- and immunity-related pathways, including the IL-17, TNF, Toll-like receptor, and NF-κB signaling pathways, which are known to play pivotal roles in periodontal inflammation and tissue destruction. In particular, the IL-17 pathway emerged as a core regulatory axis, consistent with previous findings that IL-17 contributes to neutrophil recruitment, osteoclast activation, and matrix degradation in periodontal lesions.Compared with NF-κB and Toll-like receptor (TLR) pathways, which primarily serve as upstream mediators of pathogen recognition and general inflammatory cytokine transcription, the IL-17 pathway plays a more specialized role in sustaining chronic inflammation and mediating tissue-destructive responses. While TLRs initiate innate immune responses by detecting bacterial components such as LPS, and NF-κB broadly controls transcription of inflammatory genes, IL-17 signaling bridges innate and adaptive immunity by promoting recruitment of neutrophils and synergizing with TNF-α and IL-6 to amplify inflammation. This highlights the importance of IL-17 as a potential intervention target in chronic periodontal conditions.

PPI network analysis further highlighted four hub genes—TP53, STAT3, NFKB1, and JUN—that may act as central regulators of the therapeutic action of andrographolide. These genes are closely linked to transcriptional control of inflammatory mediators. For example, STAT3 and NFKB1 are critical transcription factors downstream of multiple inflammatory signaling cascades, while JUN participates in AP-1–mediated cytokine transcription. Among the hub genes identified, TP53 plays a complex role beyond tumor suppression, acting as a key regulator in immune responses and inflammation. In macrophages, p53 has been shown to modulate the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α, and to control apoptotic pathways that influence immune cell survival and resolution of inflammation. In the context of periodontitis, increased p53 expression has been observed in inflamed gingival tissues, suggesting its involvement in immune activation and tissue destruction. Our molecular docking results indicate that andrographolide may stably bind to TP53, potentially affecting its transcriptional activity and downstream inflammatory cascades. This interaction could contribute to the observed reduction of inflammatory cytokines, further supporting the therapeutic potential of andrographolide in periodontal inflammation.Docking simulations suggested that andrographolide may interact with the functional domains of these proteins, indicating potential binding affinity. However, these results do not by themselves demonstrate direct functional inhibition.Among the top-ranked targets identified in the PPI network, additional genes such as RELA, MAPK1, AKT1, and IL6 also exhibited high degree centrality and potential relevance to inflammatory signaling. However, TP53, STAT3, NFKB1, and JUN were prioritized for molecular docking due to their well-established regulatory roles in transcriptional activation of cytokines and prior reports linking them to macrophage-driven immune responses in periodontitis. These four genes also appeared at the intersection of multiple key pathways, suggesting their centrality in the andrographolide-target interaction network.

Our experimental validation supported these predictions. In vitro assays demonstrated that andrographolide significantly reduced the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-17, TNF-α, and MCP1 in LPS-stimulated cells without inducing overt cytotoxicity at concentrations ≤ 30 µM. Moreover, qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed the downregulation of these cytokines at both the mRNA and protein levels. These findings indicate that andrographolide may suppress LPS-induced inflammatory responses through inhibition of key signaling pathways and transcription factors, consistent with its reported effects in other inflammation-related disease models.Importantly, our molecular docking simulations revealed that andrographolide could form stable interactions with key transcription factors STAT3 and NFKB1. This is functionally relevant, as both STAT3 and NF-κB are master regulators of inflammatory cytokine production. STAT3 promotes the transcription of IL-6 and MCP1, while NF-κB controls the expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-17. Therefore, Potential modulation of these transcription factors by andrographolide may be associated with the observed reduction in cytokine expression. These findings provide a mechanistic link between the in silico binding profiles and the in vitro anti-inflammatory effects observed in our study.

It is worth noting that while our study provides a comprehensive overview of andrographolide’s anti-inflammatory potential in periodontitis, there are several limitations. First, the in vitro inflammation model employed LPS as a pro-inflammatory stimulus, which mimics bacterial challenge but may not fully recapitulate the complex microbial-host interactions in periodontal tissues. Second, the current analysis is limited to predicted molecular targets and selected cytokines. Further studies employing transcriptomic or proteomic approaches could provide a more global view of the regulatory network. In vivo validation using experimental periodontitis models is needed to confirm the protective effects and bioavailability of andrographolide in the periodontal microenvironment.Another limitation concerns the molecular docking analysis. Although docking provides useful structural predictions of potential ligand–target interactions, the present study did not include redocking validation, benchmarking against known ligands, or an independent assessment of docking accuracy. Therefore, the docking results should be interpreted as preliminary computational evidence suggesting possible binding interactions rather than definitive proof of direct functional inhibition. Further studies using biochemical binding assays, reporter assays, phosphorylation analysis, or target-specific perturbation experiments are required to validate whether andrographolide directly modulates the activity of these predicted targets.Additionally, it should be noted that our in vitro model, based on LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages, reflects a simplified inflammatory setting and does not fully represent the complex multi-cellular environment of the periodontium. The interactions among epithelial cells, fibroblasts, osteoclasts, and the subgingival microbiota are not captured in this model.Another methodological consideration is that the computationally predicted targets were mainly derived from human databases, whereas RAW264.7 murine macrophages were used for in vitro validation. RAW264.7 cells are widely used as a macrophage inflammatory model; however, species differences may limit the direct extrapolation of these findings to human periodontitis. Therefore, future validation using human macrophages or primary periodontal tissue-derived cells would further strengthen the translational relevance of this study.Therefore, future studies utilizing co-culture systems or in vivo periodontitis models will be essential to validate the broader immunomodulatory effects of andrographolide.Finally, considering its phytochemical nature, andrographolide has been reported to exhibit poor oral bioavailability due to low aqueous solubility and rapid metabolism. These pharmacokinetic limitations may hinder its therapeutic concentrations in periodontal tissues, highlighting the need for optimized delivery systems in future translational studies.

In conclusion, our integrative analysis suggests that andrographolide may exert anti-inflammatory effects relevant to periodontitis by modulating multiple inflammation-associated pathways and hub regulators, including TP53, STAT3, NFKB1, and JUN. However, the predicted ligand–target interactions and pathway associations require further experimental validation to establish direct causal mechanisms.These findings provide mechanistic insights into its therapeutic potential and support further exploration of andrographolide as a promising natural agent for adjunctive periodontal therapy.
