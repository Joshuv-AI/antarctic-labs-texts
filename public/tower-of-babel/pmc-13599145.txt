The immediate and follow-up effects of EMDR therapy for adults with post-traumatic stress disorder: a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Han Yang, Zhiheng Xiong, Xiangxiang Chen, Yijing Wo, Hao Tang, Mengxuan Qiao, Yu Chen, Zhilin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR) therapy is recommended as an effective psychotherapy for adults with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Prior meta-analyses lacked follow-up data or data on comorbid symptoms, and exhibited high heterogeneity. The aims of this meta-analysis were to investigate the immediate and follow-up effects of EMDR on adult patients with PTSD, especially those with comorbid anxiety and depression.MethodsSixty-five studies (including 3342 participants) were analyzed to assess intervention effects and moderating factors such as trauma type, control group type, geography, early intervention, and intervention duration.ResultsThis study found that EMDR approach has immediate and follow-up effects on PTSD, with sustained effects at follow-up. It also has an immediate effect on anxiety and depression. Some factors like control group type, trauma type, moderated the immediate effects on PTSD, while for depression, factors like early intervention and number of sessions had moderating effects.ConclusionEMDR is an effective intervention for adults with PTSD, with sustained effects and additional benefits for anxiety and depression. Early, brief EMDR shows superior efficacy.Clinical trial numberTrial registration PROSPERO registration number: CRD420251012356.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05349-w.Keywords: EMDR, Adults, Post-traumatic stress disorder, Meta-analysis, Trauma

Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a common and debilitating mental health condition that arises after exposure to extremely threatening or horrific events, such as military combat, sexual assault, natural disasters, or serious accidents [1–3] The disorder is characterized by intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance of trauma-related stimuli, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and marked hyper-arousal [1]. The lifetime prevalence of PTSD in the general population is estimated to range from 3.9% to 10%, with substantial variation across regions and sociodemographic groups [2, 4, 5]. PTSD frequently co-occurs with other psychiatric disorders, most notably major depressive disorder (MDD) and anxiety disorders [6, 7]. Approximately 50–60% of individuals with PTSD meet criteria for MDD during their lifetime [8, 9], and 30–50% present with a comorbid anxiety disorder [10, 11]. This high level of comorbidity leads to greater symptom severity, poorer treatment response, higher chronicity, and worse functional outcomes compared to PTSD alone [10]. Consequently, understanding how psychological treatments affect both core PTSD symptoms and comorbid affective symptoms is a key clinical priority.

Eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) is one of the most widely recognized evidence-based psychotherapies for PTSD. Developed by Shapiro, EMDR is grounded in the adaptive information processing (AIP) model, which posits that traumatic memories are maladaptively stored due to insufficient processing [12, 13]. Under normal conditions, the brain naturally processes disturbing experiences and integrates them into adaptive memory networks; however, trauma can overwhelm this system, leaving memories “frozen” in their original, distressing form [13]. The AIP model suggests that these unprocessed memories give rise to PTSD symptoms and that reactivating the memory while engaging the brain’s information-processing system can restore adaptive resolution [12, 14]. Through a standardized eight-phase protocol that includes bilateral stimulation (e.g., horizontal eye movements, auditory tones, or tactile tapping), EMDR facilitates the reprocessing of traumatic memories, allowing them to be integrated into more adaptive semantic networks [15–17]. In contrast to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), EMDR does not require detailed verbal descriptions of the traumatic event, prolonged exposure, or between-session homework, rendering it suitable for patients with low treatment tolerance or difficulty engaging in exposure-based protocols [3].

EMDR is recommended as a first-line treatment for adult PTSD by multiple authoritative guidelines, including those from the World Health Organization [18] and the American Psychiatric Association (APA) [19, 20]. The 2018 International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies (ISTSS) guidelines [21, 22], the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline [23, 24], and the Australian clinical practice guidelines [25] all endorse EMDR as a standard early intervention for adults with clinically significant PTSD symptoms. This consistent recommendation across diverse guidelines is supported by a robust and growing body of evidence from randomized controlled trials demonstrating EMDR’s efficacy in reducing symptom severity, achieving remission, and sustaining treatment gains. Zhao et al. conducted a randomized controlled trial to demonstrate that EMDR treatment was more effectively reduce the traumatic, psychotic and emotional symptoms than wait-list condition [26]. A Bayesian network meta-analysis by Hoppen et al. [27], which included 157 RCTs, found that all trauma-focused psychological interventions, including EMDR, trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (TF-CBT), present-centered therapy, and non-trauma-focused interventions, were more effective than control conditions at post-treatment [27]. Although TF-CBT demonstrated marginally higher short-term, mid-term, and long-term efficacy than non-trauma-focused interventions, EMDR and TF-CBT did not differ significantly in their efficacy or acceptability [3]. Simpson et al. reviewed that EMDR was statistically superior to waitlist or usual care but again not significantly different from TF-CBT, and concluded that beneficial long-term effects extending beyond one-month post-treatment are limited to trauma-focused interventions, including EMDR and TF-CBT [28]. Chen et al. systematically reviewed the large effect sizes for EMDR relative to wait-list, and later meta-analyses have generally supported these findings, although the magnitude of the effect varies across studies [29, 30]. Moreover, EMDR has been shown to be effective in diverse populations, including refugees, survivors of childhood abuse, and military veterans [31–33].

However, there are still unanswered questions in the field of EMDR therapy intervention in adult PTSD, such as: (1) the durability of EMDR effects beyond the immediate post-treatment period has received less systematic attention. The available follow-up evidence is often limited by small sample sizes, variable follow-up durations, and high heterogeneity. Whether the initial gains of EMDR are fully maintained, partially decay, or even increase over time remains uncertain, and this has direct implications for clinical decision-making and guideline development; (2) although PTSD is highly comorbid with depression and anxiety, the immediate and long-term effects of EMDR on these affective symptoms have not been comprehensively quantified in an updated large-scale meta-analysis that also accounts for study quality and risk of bias. Some earlier reviews reported inconsistent effects of EMDR on comorbid depression [9, 11]; (3) and critically, it remains unclear which clinical and methodological characteristics moderate the effects of EMDR on PTSD, especially comorbid with depression and anxiety. Potential moderators such as control group type (e.g., wait-list, TAU, CBT, active treatment), trauma type (e.g., accidental disaster, sexual assault, war-related), geographical region, early versus delayed intervention (within three months of trauma vs. later), number of sessions, session duration, and reporter type (self-report vs. clinician-rated) have been examined inconsistently across studies. Few meta-analyses have examined these moderators simultaneously within a single large dataset and stratified by outcome domain (PTSD, anxiety, depression). Such information is essential for tailoring EMDR to specific patient subgroups, optimizing treatment protocols, and designing future trials.

To address these gaps, we conducted an updated systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs evaluating EMDR for adult PTSD. The primary aims were: (a) to estimate the immediate and follow-up effects of EMDR on PTSD symptoms, using the largest and most current set of eligible RCTs; (b) to quantify the immediate and follow-up effects of EMDR on comorbid anxiety and depressive symptoms; (c) to systematically test a broad range of moderators of EMDR effects on PTSD, anxiety and depression; and (d) to perform comparison-stratified analyses (e.g., EMDR vs. CBT, EMDR vs. waitlist/TAU) within early versus delayed intervention designs. By providing robust pooled estimates, assessing heterogeneity and publication bias, and exploring clinically meaningful moderators, this study aims to inform clinical practice, guideline development, and future research on EMDR for adults with PTSD, particularly those with comorbid depression or anxiety symptoms.

Methods

Article search and screening

The protocol of this meta-analysis was registered with the International Prospective Register for Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (No. CRD420251012356, available at http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO). Our study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines. The following databases were utilized for a systematic search: Pubmed, Springer, EBSCO, Scopus, Sage, Web of Science, Taylor & Francis, Cochrane Library, Wiley Online Library, Elsevier ScienceDirect. All selected articles were published between January 1991 and March 2026.

There were no limits to individual differences in the severity of the traumatic symptoms and the type of traumatic event. We utilized the MeSH terms to search the articles across all databases and set a filter for RCTs only. The MeSH items included: EMDR, eye movement desensitization reprocessing, adults, PTSD, post-traumatic stress disorder, RCT, randomized controlled trial.

Eligibility criteria

We outlined five eligibility criteria for this meta-analysis: (1) all studies were randomized controlled trial design, only published in English and Chinese; (2) all populations were adults (≥ 18 years old); (3) a primary diagnosis was PTSD, according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) (up through ICD-10), or the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) (up through DSM-5); (4) EMDR therapy was conducted either in individual or group format (eight-phase manualized therapy or less standard therapy); (5) other therapies including therapy as usual (TAU), waiting list-control, any other psychological intervention were included for comparison.

In addition, relevant articles in the Journal of Trauma and Stress, PILOTS, and the International Society for Traumatic Stress Studies (ISTSS) treatment guidelines were manually searched [22]; grey literature on post-traumatic stress disorder-related websites such as the World Health Organization was searched by consulting abstracts of relevant conferences such as the European and International Association for the Study of Traumatic Stress; reference lists of included studies were double-checked to identify other studies that met the inclusion criteria.

Data extraction

Titles and abstracts from the included studies were independently screened by two researchers, and full texts of eligible studies were retrieved. Studies in which EMDR combined with medication were excluded, as well as studies that lacked reliable data for calculating the effect size.

The information extracted from the trials included participant characteristics (post-treatment assessment finishers), diagnosis (criteria to diagnose and clinical features), intervention (number of session, duration of session, frequency of treatment, total time of therapy), and outcomes (improvement of depressive or anxiety symptoms). Any disagreements were resolved by discussion with a third researcher.

Risk of bias evaluation

All selected articles underwent a risk of bias (RoB2) assessment using the Cochrane Collaboration Tool for Assessing Risk of Bias in Randomized Trials. Two independent researchers assessed the RoB separately. There were seven domains with potential RoBs as follows: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, other sources of bias.

Heterogeneity test

In this study, Q test and I² test were administered to measure whether there was heterogeneity between studies. Theoretically, a fixed-effect model should be applied for homogeneous studies, and a random-effects model should be applied for the pooling of results between studies where there is heterogeneity. However, in fact, there were different degrees of heterogeneity among different studies, and through a review of the literature, it was found that the effect of EMDR therapy in the intervention of PTSD may be related to the clinical severity of the symptoms, the type of trauma, the duration of the session, and the number of sessions; therefore, a random-effects model was selected for our meta-analysis.

Outcome measures

The outcome data in our meta-analysis included: (1) symptoms of PTSD, measured by the Impact of Event Scale (IES), Clinician Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS), the PTSD Checklist (PCL), Posttraumatic Stress Scale (PSS), the Short PTSD Rating Interview (SPRINT), Subjective Units of Distress scale (SUD), the Mississippi PTSD scale (MISS), the Structured Interview for PTSD (SI-PTSD), the Impact of Events scale (IOE), the Harvard Trauma Questionnaire (HTQ), Davidson PTSD Symptom Scale (DAV), Post-traumatic Stress Diagnostic Scale (PDS), and the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV Axis I Disorders (SCID-I); (2) symptoms of anxiety, measured by State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-A for anxiety, HADS-D for depression), the Validity of Cognitions scale (VoC), State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STATE), Hopkins Symptom Checklist (HSCL-25), Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), and Generalized Anxiety Disorder Assessment (GAD-7); (3) symptoms of depression, measured by the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD), Beck Depression Inventory (BECK), the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS), and Depression Anxiety Stress scale (DASS).

Result

Description of studies

As the PRISMA flow chart shown in Fig. 1, a total of 1844 studies were retrieved through the systematic search and the number was reduced to 891 after checking for duplications. 676 studies were excluded during the titles and abstracts phase. The number of remaining studies was 215. After carefully reading the full texts, 146 articles were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria which included 77 non-randomized controlled studies, 38 studies with subjects younger than 18 years old, and 31 studies without a formal diagnosis of PTSD. After data collection, 4 studies with incomplete data were excluded because there was no response after emailing the authors. Finally, 65 RCTs met the inclusion criteria and had complete data.

PRISMA flow chart of systematic review process

Study characteristics

All descriptive data in Supplementary 1 were collected from the articles in our review sample. According to the basic information of the RCTs included in the analysis and the results of previous studies, factors such as the type of control group, type of trauma, regional type, early intervention, number of treatment sessions, and duration of a session were selected as the moderating variables into the analysis. In the control group, CBT therapy included imaginary exposure and prolonged exposure. AT therapy included supportive counseling, free mood techniques, stabilization, medication, etc. Accidental disasters in the types of traumas included instantaneous trauma such as traffic accidents, earthquakes, explosions, etc. Mixed trauma refers to the different types of traumas.

Risk of bias of included studies

Risk of bias across the 65 included studies was predominantly judged as either some concerns or high according to the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. Overall, only 2 studies were rated as low risk of bias [31, 34], whereas 34 were judged to raise some concerns and 29 were judged to be at high risk of bias (see Fig. 2). At the domain level, the most frequent concerns arose in bias due to deviations from intended interventions and bias due to missing outcome data. Specifically, for deviations from intended interventions, 35 studies were judged as having some concerns and 11 as high risk, while for missing outcome data 18 studies were judged as high risk. Bias in the randomization process was also common, with 24 studies rated as having some concerns and 4 as high risk, suggesting that random sequence generation and/or allocation concealment were often insufficiently reported. In contrast, bias in selection of the reported result was the least problematic domain, with 53 studies judged as low risk and only 2 as high risk. Overall, these findings indicate that confidence in the pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, as the evidence base is largely informed by studies at non-low risk of bias.

Heterogeneity across studies

The immediate and follow-up effect of EMDR intervention for the total symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder were separately tested for heterogeneity. A total of 56 studies reported immediate effects presented 85.76% (by I² value) of the heterogeneity by Q test (Q = 303.13, p < 0.001, I²=85.76%), indicating there were other moderators that influenced the traumatic psychotherapy. We referred to the calculation rules by Simpson et al. (2025) that calculated an SMD, and specifically states that CI can be converted to SD and that a correlation of 0.5 can be used when only pre/post data are available [28]. Any study that still lacks enough information after those steps will be excluded automatically and listed by the code. For the follow-up effect, 32 studies were included. Heterogeneity was also statistically significant, Q(31) = 235.26, p < 0.001, with I²=89.72%, again indicating substantial between-study heterogeneity.

Publication bias

We conducted the funnel plots, Egger’s regression test, fail-safe N, and Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill procedure.

For the immediate effect size, visual inspection of the funnel plot suggested some asymmetry, with several studies distributed toward the right side of the funnel. Egger’s regression test was significant (z = 3.96, p < 0.001), indicating possible funnel plot asymmetry and suggesting potential small-study effects or publication bias. However, the fail-safe N was 687, indicating that a large number of unpublished null studies would be required to reduce the overall result to non-significance. In addition, the trim-and-fill analysis estimated no missing studies on the left side of the funnel, and the pooled effect size remained unchanged after adjustment. Therefore, these findings suggest that although some asymmetry was present in the immediate-effect analysis, the overall result appeared relatively robust (see Fig. 3a).

a The immediate effect size of EMDR therapy for PTSD. b The follow-up effect size of EMDR therapy for PTSD

For the follow-up effect size, the funnel plot appeared relatively more balanced, although some asymmetry was still visible. Egger’s regression test was marginally significant (z = 1.97, p = 0.049), indicating possible funnel plot asymmetry. The fail-safe N was 92, suggesting a lower degree of robustness than in the immediate analysis, but still indicating that additional null studies would be needed to overturn the significant pooled result. The trim-and-fill analysis again estimated no missing studies on the left side of the funnel, and the pooled follow-up effect remained unchanged after adjustment (see Fig. 3b). Overall, the follow-up analysis showed limited evidence of publication bias, although the findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Efficacy analysis

The immediate and follow-up effects of EMDR on PTSD symptoms were examined using random-effects meta-analysis. The pooled effect size was statistically significant (g = 0.96, 95%CI[0.71,1.21], p < 0.001), indicating that EMDR significantly reduced PTSD symptoms relative to control conditions at post-treatment from baseline to post intervention (see Fig. 4a). The heterogeneity test was also significant, suggesting substantial between-study variability.

a The immediate effectiveness of EMDR in the treatment of PTSD symptoms. b The follow-up effectiveness of EMDR in the treatment of PTSD symptoms

The pooled effect size remained statistically significant (g = 0.80, 95%CI[0.41,1.19], p < 0.001), indicating that the beneficial effect of EMDR on PTSD symptoms was maintained over time (see Fig. 4b). Heterogeneity again reached a high level. Although the follow-up effect was somewhat smaller than the immediate effect, both forest plots showed pooled estimates in favor of EMDR. Overall, these findings support the conclusion that EMDR has both significant immediate and sustained therapeutic effects on PTSD symptoms in adults, while also indicating considerable heterogeneity across studies.

Moderator analysis of immediate effect of EMDR in PTSD symptoms

Based on the moderate degree of heterogeneity in the immediate effect of EMDR treatment, we selected the regulating variables such as control group type, trauma type, location, early versus delayed intervention, number of sessions, reporter type, and session length. into moderator analysis to explore whether significant differences of the effect size existed. Significant omnibus subgroup differences were observed for control group type (QB = 32.69, df = 3, p < 0.001), trauma type (QB = 16.30, df = 6, p = 0.012), location (QB = 30.90, df = 6, p < 0.001), early versus delayed intervention (QB = 15.26, df = 1, p < 0.001), number of sessions (QB = 18.41, df = 3, p < 0.001), and reporter type (QB = 4.04, df = 1, p = 0.045). By contrast, session length was not a significant moderator (QB = 0.81, df = 2, p = 0.667) (see Supplementary 2).

For control group type, EMDR showed significant immediate effects across all comparison conditions, although the magnitude of effect varied substantially. The pooled effect was largest (see Supplementary 2) when EMDR was compared with wait-list (g = 1.59, 95% CI[1.16,2.03], k = 23) and treatment as usual (g = 1.27, 95% CI [0.60,1.93], k = 6), and smaller when compared with CBT (g = 0.42, 95% CI [0.18,0.66], k = 26) or active treatment (g = 0.34, 95% CI[0.04,0.64], k = 12). Significant differences were also found for trauma type, where were observed for accidental disaster (g = 1.06, 95%CI[0.53,1.59], k = 16), bereavement (g = 0.80, 95%CI [0.13,1.48], k = 2), disease-related trauma (g = 1.23, 95%CI [0.60,1.86], k = 7), mixed trauma (g = 0.44, 95%CI [0.22,0.67], k = 22), occupational trauma (g = 2.13, 95%CI[0.72,3.53], k = 5), and war-related trauma (g = 0.80, 95%CI [0.30,1.31], k = 11). The sexual assault did not reach statistical significance (g = 0.84, 95%CI [-0.19,1.87], k = 4).

The immediate effect of EMDR also differed significantly by locations. Significant pooled effects were found in East Asia (g = 1.01, 95% CI[0.46,1.55], k = 3), West Asia (g = 1.35, 95%CI [0.79,1.91], k = 8), Europe (g = 0.77, 95% CI 0.47 to 1.06, k = 26), North America (g = 0.53, 95%CI[ 0.16,0.90], k = 11), and South America (g = 2.35, 95% CI[1.36,3.35], k = 7). In contrast, there were not statistically significant in Oceania (g = 0.30, 95% CI [-0.12, 0.71], k = 8) or in mixed-location samples (g = 0.15, 95% CI[-0.73 to 1.04, k = 4).

A significant moderating effect was also found for timing of intervention. Studies categorized as early intervention showed a larger pooled effect (g = 1.92, 95%CI[1.10,2.74], k = 11) than those categorized as delayed intervention (g = 0.66, 95%CI[0.45,0.88], k = 36). Similarly, number of sessions significantly moderated treatment effects. The largest pooled effect was observed in studies delivering 1–3 sessions (g = 1.66, 95%CI[1.09,2.23], k = 17). Significant effects were also found for 4–6 sessions (g = 0.81, 95%CI[0.44,1.19], k = 17) and 10–12 sessions (g = 0.68, 95%CI[0.30,1.05], k = 18), whereas the 7–9 session subgroup was not significant (g = 0.16, 95%CI[-0.15,0.46], k = 10). Finally, reporter type significantly moderated the immediate treatment effect. Studies using self-report measures yielded a larger pooled effect (g = 1.04, 95%CI[0.74,1.34], k = 47) than studies using clinician-report measures (g = 0.52, 95% CI[0.28,0.76], k = 20) (see Supplementary 2).

Immediate and follow-up effect of EMDR in PTSD with anxiety and depression symptoms

EMDR demonstrated significant immediate benefits for both depressive and anxiety symptoms in adults with PTSD. This analytic framework is consistent with the comparative change-score approach described by Simpson et al., in which symptom change from baseline is compared between EMDR and the control condition [28].

For anxiety, the immediate pooled effect was statistically significant and favored EMDR (k = 27, g = 0.66, 95%CI [0.45, 0.87], p<0.001), with moderate to substantial heterogeneity (Q = 63.63, p<0.001; I² =60.70%). The follow-up pooled effect also remained significant (k = 6, g = 0.91, 95% CI [0.32,1.50], p=0.002), but heterogeneity was high (Q = 18.71, p=0.002; I²=77.94%). Thus, EMDR appeared to confer both immediate and follow-up benefits for anxiety symptoms in adults with PTSD. However, the smaller number of follow-up comparisons and the high between-study heterogeneity suggest that the long-term anxiety findings should be interpreted more cautiously .

A similar pattern was observed for depression, the immediate pooled effect was statistically significant and favored EMDR over the control conditions (k = 38, g = 0.54, 95%CI[0.35,0.72], p<0.001). Heterogeneity was moderate to substantial (Q = 98.79, p<0.001; I² = 63.36%), indicating that the magnitude of the immediate antidepressant effect varied across studies. The follow-up analysis also showed a significant pooled effect favoring EMDR (k = 11, g = 0.58, 95%CI [0.28, 0.88], p < 0.001), with moderate heterogeneity (Q = 21.59, p=0.017; I² =52.06%). These findings suggest that EMDR was associated with both immediate and sustained improvement in depressive symptoms among adults with PTSD, although the follow-up evidence base was substantially smaller than the immediate evidence base.

Moderator analysis of immediate effect of EMDR in PTSD accompanied with anxiety and depression symptoms

For anxiety symptoms, none of the tested moderators reached statistical significance. Although control-group type showed the closest approach to significance, this effect did not meet the conventional threshold (QM = 7.25, p=0.064). Trauma type, location, early versus delayed intervention, number of sessions, session duration, and reporter type were likewise non-significant (see Supplementary 3).

The pattern was somewhat different for depressive symptoms. Among the examined moderators, only control-group type significantly moderated the immediate effect of EMDR (QMp = 0.0017), whereas other moderators were all non-significant. Subgroup analyses showed that the largest immediate effects were observed when EMDR was compared with wait-list (g = 0.94, 95%CI [0.66,1.23]) and treatment as usual (g = 0.70, 95% CI [0.18,1.22]) (see Supplementary 4). By contrast, the effect was smaller when EMDR was compared with CBT (g = 0.30, 95%CI [-0.01,0.60]) and smallest against active treatment controls (g = 0.16, 95%CI [-0.08,0.39]).

Comparison-specific analysis of EMDR effects on anxiety and depression symptoms

Since control-group type emerged as the only significant moderator of immediate depressive outcomes in the preceding analysis, and comparator effects may differ further according to treatment timing, comparison-specific analyses were subsequently stratified by both comparator family and timing family. In delayed-treatment studies comparing EMDR with CBT, the pooled immediate effect for anxiety significantly favored EMDR (k = 7, g = 0.62, 95% CI[0.32,0.92]), whereas the corresponding immediate effect for depression was not significant (k = 9, g = 0.31, 95% CI[-0.06,0.69]). Follow-up evidence for CBT comparisons was limited to a single study for each outcome and was therefore summarized narratively only. In delayed-treatment studies comparing EMDR with waitlist or treatment as usual, significant immediate benefits were observed for both anxiety (k = 5, g = 1.11, 95% CI[0.54,1.69]) and depression (k = 7, g = 0.96, 95% CI[0.55,1.38]). For delayed-treatment depression at follow-up, the pooled effect remained positive in direction but did not reach conventional statistical significance (k = 3, g = 0.80, 95%CI [-0.04,1.63]), while delayed-treatment anxiety follow-up evidence was again too sparse for meta-analysis. In early treatment or prevention studies, all eligible wait-list or treatment-as-usual comparisons favored EMDR. Formal pooling was possible only for depression, for which significant effects were found both immediately (k = 5, g = 0.88, 95%CI [0.38,1.38]) and at follow-up (k = 3, g = 0.46, 95% CI[0.08,0.84]). For anxiety in early treatment or prevention, only two studies were available at each assessment point, so these findings were retained as narrative evidence rather than pooled estimates. Overall, these comparison-specific analyses indicate that the relative advantage of EMDR was most evident against wait-list or treatment as usual, whereas comparisons with CBT suggested a more differentiated pattern, with significant superiority for anxiety but not for depression.

Discussion

Based on 65 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), this meta-analysis is one of the largest quantitative syntheses of EMDR for adult PTSD to date. EMDR produced significant and durable improvements in PTSD symptoms at both post-treatment and follow-up. It also significantly reduced comorbid anxiety and depressive symptoms over the short and long term. Together, these findings confirm that EMDR is an effective and durable intervention for both core and affective symptoms in adults with PTSD.

Our analyses further revealed substantial between-study heterogeneity and identified key factors shaping treatment outcomes. The effects of EMDR on depression and anxiety were robust, with variability largely explained by control group type. A novel finding was that in delayed intervention settings, EMDR showed a greater advantage over CBT for anxiety, but not for depression, which may support personalized treatment matching. Session duration did not significantly influence efficacy, whereas the number of sessions was a meaningful predictor of treatment response.

Clinical interpretation of moderator effects for PTSD

Control group type was the strongest moderator. EMDR showed the largest effects against passive control conditions (e.g., wait-list, TAU) and significantly smaller effects when compared with active treatments such as CBT. This pattern is well documented in psychotherapy meta-analyses, where active comparators consistently reduce effect sizes relative to passive controls [35–37].

Trauma type significantly moderated outcomes, with larger treatment benefits observed for occupational trauma, disease-related trauma, and accidental disaster. Evidence from recent meta-analyses suggests that EMDR is particularly effective for specific trauma types, including natural and man-made disasters [38–40]. The pooled effect for sexual assault was not statistically significant, but this should not be interpreted as evidence that EMDR is ineffective for this population. Few studies were available and they were under-powered. Sexual assault often involves betrayal, shame, and complex emotional responses that may require longer or more intensive treatment. This finding highlights the need for high-quality RCTs focused on sexual assault survivors, ideally with distinction between simple and complex PTSD [41].

Geographical location also significantly moderated effects. The largest benefits were observed in South America, West Asia, and East Asia; moderate effects in Europe and North America; and non-significant effects in Oceania and samples from mixed locations. A recent meta-analysis examining EMDR outcomes across diverse populations found that effect sizes varied significantly by geographic region, with larger effects reported in non-Western settings [42]. The non-significant findings in Oceania may reflect more frequent use of active comparators, higher chronicity in veteran samples, and a stronger historical emphasis on CBT in clinical guidelines, which could affect EMDR treatment fidelity. Studies in South America and West Asia often included refugee or post-conflict samples with greater baseline severity, providing greater scope for measurable improvement [42]. Cultural adaptation of EMDR protocols can further enhance real-world effectiveness [43].

Early intervention yielded substantially larger benefits than delayed intervention. Roberts et al. systematically reviewed early psychological interventions and found clinically important evidence for EMDR in reducing traumatic symptoms when initiated within three months of trauma exposure [44]. These results support the role of EMDR in the secondary prevention of chronic PTSD, possibly by targeting a critical window of neuroplasticity in which traumatic memories undergo ongoing consolidation [40]. Stratified analyses by comparator type confirmed that early intervention effects remained robust even when restricted to comparisons with waitlist or TAU controls, indicating that the advantage is not merely an artifact of comparator selection.

Number of sessions significantly moderated immediate PTSD outcomes, with the strongest effects seen in studies delivering 1–3 sessions. Carletto et al. reviewed eleven studies to find a significant positive association between the number of EMDR sessions and treatment effect size, confirming session number as a meaningful predictor of treatment response [45]. Prior studies were typically early interventions, in which symptoms may not yet have been fully consolidated and passive controls were common. Brief protocols are highly effective for early, single-event trauma, whereas chronic, complex PTSD requires longer protocols [14]. Session dosage should be individually tailored based on trauma complexity, comorbidity, and clinical response [46].

Reporter type also moderated outcomes: self-report measures yielded larger apparent effect sizes than clinician-rated tools. Evidence from NICE clinical guidelines indicates that effect sizes for self-report PTSD symptoms are consistently larger than those for clinician-rated symptoms when comparing active treatments to waitlist or usual care [47]. These differences may reflect greater sensitivity of self-report scales to subjective distress, imperfect blinding in many trials, and potential expectancy effects when clinicians also serve as therapists [48]. Future trials should use independent, blinded outcome assessment and report both self-report and clinician-rated outcomes.

Specific effects of EMDR on anxiety and depression symptoms

For anxiety symptoms, none of the tested moderators reached statistical significance, although control group type approached significance. This consistent treatment response indicates that the anxiolytic effects of EMDR are relatively robust across diverse clinical populations and methodological study designs, likely stemming from the unique mechanism of bilateral stimulation in directly modulating emotional arousal and regulating stress-related neural activity [31, 49, 50]. Clinically, patients with prominent anxiety symptoms can achieve meaningful symptom relief from EMDR intervention, regardless of trauma subtype, when treated with an adequate and standard session dosage. This high stability of anxiolytic effects aligns with recent meta-analytic evidence documenting homogeneous, large effect sizes of EMDR for anxiety reduction across trauma-exposed and substance-using populations, with minimal methodological heterogeneity [51]. Such consistent efficacy further validates EMDR’s core strength in targeting hyperaroused anxiety symptoms across varied clinical contexts [52].

In contrast, for depressive symptom outcomes, only control group type emerged as a significant moderator of EMDR treatment efficacy. Short-term therapeutic gains were largest when EMDR was compared with wait-list or TAU controls, attenuated when contrasted with cognitive behavioral therapy, and reduced further against other active psychological interventions. This gradient pattern of treatment benefit confirms that EMDR’s apparent antidepressant superiority is most pronounced against minimal or non-specific control conditions, with effect sizes progressively diminishing as the comparator becomes more therapeutically active. This finding aligns with recent meta-regression evidence showing that control condition type is a primary source of between-study heterogeneity in EMDR’s depression outcomes, whereas symptom severity and treatment dosage exert limited moderating effects [45]. Consequently, the apparent antidepressant advantage of EMDR is most pronounced against minimal or non-specific comparators and attenuates substantially when the control condition involves an active psychological intervention—a pattern that explains why early independent trials reached inconsistent conclusions [42, 45].

In delayed-treatment head-to-head comparisons, EMDR was superior to CBT for anxiety but not for depression. This novel dissociation provides an empirical basis for personalized treatment selection: EMDR may be preferentially indicated for patients with prominent comorbid anxiety, whereas shared decision-making is more appropriate for those whose primary presentation is dominated by depression [30].

Limitations and future

Most included studies were not at low risk of bias per the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. Common issues included deviations from intended intervention protocols and missing outcome data–challenges that are difficult to avoid in psychotherapy trials. We found some evidence of publication bias for the immediate therapeutic effects of EMDR; however, sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the overall result. Findings for follow-up outcomes should be treated as more tentative.

Several other limitations include: high unexplained heterogeneity; limited and variable follow-up data for anxiety and depression; restriction to English- and Chinese-language publications; exclusion of some studies due to missing data; small subgroups in certain stratified analyses; and lack of cost-effectiveness data.

In addition, our work departed from the PROSPERO protocol (CRD420251012356) in two respects. First, we did not perform the planned GRADE assessment, because high heterogeneity would have produced an aggregate rating that is misleadingly low. Secondly, the moderator analyses were not pre-specified but were conducted post hoc, as the initial protocol did not include a detailed plan for quantitative synthesis. Although exploratory, these analyses helped clarify important sources of the observed heterogeneity.

Future research should prioritize large, low-bias multicenter RCTs with long-term follow-up and active comparators; systematic separate reporting of anxiety and depression outcomes; preregistered moderator analyses focused on trauma type and intervention timing; examination of mediating processes including therapeutic alliance and cognitive change; head-to-head comparisons of brief and extended protocols; assessment of functional outcomes and quality of life; and implementation research to scale up EMDR in low-resource and post-conflict settings.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis confirms that EMDR is an effective and durable treatment for adult PTSD, with substantial immediate and sustained benefits for comorbid anxiety and depression. Treatment efficacy varies by comparator type, trauma type, timing of intervention, number of sessions, and outcome reporter type. Early intervention and brief protocols can produce substantial symptom reductions following single-event trauma. However, the evidence base is limited by a high risk of bias, substantial unexplained heterogeneity, and potential publication bias. Future high-quality, large-scale RCTs with rigorous methodology and long-term follow-up are urgently needed to increase confidence and support precise, personalized clinical recommendations.
