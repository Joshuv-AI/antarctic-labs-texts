Self-concordant Schrödinger operators: spectral gaps and optimization without condition numbers
Sander Gribling, Simon Apers, Harold Nieuwboer, Michael Walter

Abstract
Spectral gaps play a fundamental role in many areas of mathematics, computer science, and physics. In quantum mechanics, the spectral gap of Schrödinger operators has a long history of study due to its physical relevance, while in quantum computing spectral gaps are an important proxy for efficiency, such as in the quantum adiabatic algorithm. Motivated by convex optimization, we study Schrödinger operators associated with self-concordant barriers over convex domains and prove non-asymptotic lower bounds on the spectral gap for this class of operators. Significantly, we find that the spectral gap does not display any condition-number dependence when the usual Laplacian is replaced by the Laplace–Beltrami operator, which uses second-order information of the barrier and hence can take the curvature of the barrier into account. As an algorithmic application, we construct a novel quantum interior point method that applies to arbitrary self-concordant barriers and shows no condition-number dependence. To achieve this we combine techniques from semiclassical analysis, convex optimization, and quantum annealing.

Introduction

The Schrödinger operator

\mathcal{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+f,

where
\Delta=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{j}^{2}
is the Laplacian and
f
a potential function, is a central object in quantum mechanics.
It describes the energies and eigenstates of a quantum system via the time-dependent Schrödinger equation,
\mathcal{H}{|{\psi}\rangle}=E{|{\psi}\rangle}
, as well as the system’s dynamics via the time-independent Schrödinger equation,
i\partial_{t}{|{\psi}\rangle}=\mathcal{H}{|{\psi}\rangle}
.
Among these energies and eigenstates, the most studied are the
ground state
and its energy, as well as the
spectral gap
, which is the energy gap between the ground state and the first excited state.
Spectral gaps have long been studied for their relevance in physics and mathematics (cf.
[
Wit82
,
Sim83
,
Sim84
]
), and this led to a plethora of results on the asymptotics of the spectral gap in varying scenarios
[
CFKS87
]
.
One noteworthy line of results is based on
semiclassical analysis

[
Zwo12
]
, where the family
-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\gamma^{2}f
for
\gamma\to\infty
is considered.
Particularly relevant to this work is the insight that, for a convex potential
f
, the spectral gap is asymptotically governed by a local quadratic approximation of
f

[
Sim83
]
.

More recently, the rise of quantum computing and quantum algorithms has shown that spectral gaps are also an important proxy for efficient algorithms, as is the case for example in the quantum adiabatic algorithm
[
FGGS00
]
.
This strongly suggests to try and exploit the aforementioned line of work on spectral gaps in the design and analysis of quantum algorithms.
Unfortunately, the traditional asymptotic analysis is
not sufficiently quantitative for this purpose, as it typically omits key dependencies on input parameters such as dimensions and condition numbers.
Indeed, many results based on semiclassical analysis seem to not have been strengthened enough to become useful for quantum algorithms.
(A notable exception are results on the “fundamental gap”
[
AC11
]
, where the dependence is explicit.

)
What is required for algorithmics is a
non-asymptotic
semiclassical analysis, which establishes a gap for an explicit choice of
\gamma>0
and with an explicit quantitative dependence on all relevant input parameters.

In this work, we demonstrate how the theory of convex optimization can give such a strengthening and, in turn, yield new quantum algorithms for convex optimization.
More specifically, we combine the theory of
self-concordance
from interior point methods
[
NN94
,
Ren01
]
with that of semiclassical analysis to prove non-asymptotic bounds on spectral gaps of Schrödinger operators with convex potentials that are self-concordant.
To begin with, we use a non-asymptotic, semiclassical analysis of the Schrödinger operator to show that its spectral gap does indeed scale with input parameters such as the dimension and the condition number of the quadratic approximation.
To remedy this, and this is our main contribution, we show that the condition number dependency can be circumvented by replacing the Laplace operator
\Delta
with a
Laplace–Beltrami operator

L
that takes into account second-order information such as the local curvature of the potential.
Such an analysis requires bounds on the stability of the local curvature, and this is where we make critical use of self-concordance.

While these structural results are of independent interest, we go on to demonstrate their algorithmic use.
We develop a novel
quantum interior point method
(IPM) for convex optimization that combines the Schrödinger operator with a quantum annealing approach.
We prove efficiency and correctness of the quantum IPM using our non-asymptotic semiclassical analysis.
As a key distinction from other works, we develop the IPM directly at the level of the continuous Schrödinger operator, by introducing, among others, a “continuous-variable” quantum algorithm for quantum annealing that involves coupling to an external quantum harmonic oscillator. This approach can be seen as a quantum variant of classical Markov chain algorithms based on continuous Langevin dynamics and Hamiltonian dynamics (on manifolds)
[

,
GC11
]
; it also shares similarities with recent works on continuous-variable quantum algorithms
[

,
BCCRK24
]
.

1.1
Technical overview: Spectral gap bounds

We consider a convex function
f\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
on a convex subset
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of Euclidean space.
We associate to it a first family of Schrödinger operators

\mathcal{H}^{E}\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\gamma^{2}f

(1.1)

for semiclassical parameter
\gamma>0
,
\Delta
the Laplace operator, and superscript
E
referring to the Euclidean setting.
We impose vanishing (Dirichlet) boundary conditions.
To provide some intuition, the energy of a state
\psi
with respect to the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{E}
can be written as
\frac{1}{2}\int\|\nabla\psi(x)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}x+\gamma^{2}\int f(x)\psi(x)^{2}\mathrm{d}x
. The ground state thus balances localization around the minimizer of
f
with the size of its gradients (the larger
\gamma
, the more the localization).
The Laplacian depends on, and the localization in the above formula is quantified using the Euclidean geometry of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
However, this geometry need not be well-adapted to the shape of the potential, leading to, as we will see, the occurrence of condition numbers.

For this reason we introduce a second family of Schrödinger operators.
Here, we consider
M
as a Riemannian manifold, with metric
g
defined by the Hessian of the convex potential
f
, and define

\mathcal{H}^{R}\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}L+\gamma^{2}f,

(1.2)

where
L
is now the Laplace–Beltrami operator on
M
, which takes into account the local geometry, and the superscript
R
refers to the Riemannian setting.
In this case, the local geometry of
M
reflects the curvature in the potential (e.g., the local volume element at
x
is scaled by
\sqrt{\det D^{2}f_{x}}
) – as does the Laplace–Beltrami operator
L
, which is a second-order differential operator (like the Laplacian) but with coefficients that likewise depend on the metric.
While seemingly more abstract, this construction is natural: it is “basis-independent” (that is, independent of the Euclidean structure) and using second-order information of
f
has proved highly effective in a variety of settings including optimization (e.g. Newton’s method) and sampling (e.g. Riemannian Langevin diffusion), as we discuss in
Section

1.4
.

Under mild conditions, both of the operators
\mathcal{H}^{E}
and
\mathcal{H}^{R}
have a discrete spectrum (eigenvalues) and the ground state is unique.
Our goal then is to give a non-asymptotic lower bound on the spectral gap.
For this we rely on ideas from semiclassical analysis, which allows us to argue that for
\gamma
sufficiently large, the low-energy properties of both operators are well approximated by their “harmonic approximation”.
In the Euclidean setting, for
\mathcal{H}^{E}
, this is a quantum harmonic oscillator with potential
\gamma^{2}\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax
, where
A
is the Hessian of
f
at its minimizer.
2
2

2

W.l.o.g., we can assume that the minimizer is at the origin and that
f(0)=0
.

Consequently, its spectral gap scales linearly in
\gamma
, with coefficient
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A})
, the square root of the smallest eigenvalue of
A
.
Critically, in the Riemannian setting, as a consequence of the aforementioned basis-independence of
\mathcal{H}^{R}
, the harmonic approximation of
\mathcal{H}^{R}
is the
standard
quantum harmonic oscillator with potential
\frac{1}{2}x^{T}x
. This operator has a constant spectral gap, which partly explains the condition-number independence of our spectral gap bound. The other part of the explanation is more subtle, and for this we require that
L
is defined based on the metric associated to the Hessian of
f
.
In the following, we give a more detailed overview, focusing first on the parts that are common to both the Euclidean and Riemannian settings, and then on the differences.

IMS-localization.

In both settings, our goal is to compare a Schrödinger operator
\mathcal{H}
to a quantum harmonic oscillator
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
To do so, we will use a technique known as
IMS-localization
, as in
[
Sim83
]
.
This technique is based on a smooth partition of unity given by nonnegative functions
J,\bar{J}
that satisfy
J^{2}+\bar{J}^{2}=1
. The IMS-localization formula then implies the identity

\mathcal{H}=J\mathcal{H}_{0}J+J(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{0})J+\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}-\|\nabla J\|^{2}-\|\nabla\bar{J}\|^{2}.

(1.3)

For both
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{E}
and
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{R}
, the term
J(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{0})J
involves the multiplication operator
\gamma^{2}J(f-q)J
, where
q
is the second-order Taylor expansion of
f
around its minimizer. To ensure localization, we choose
J
such that
J(x)=1
for
x
within distance
r:=\gamma^{-2/5}
of the minimizer, and
J(x)=0
for
x
distance at least
2r
. In both cases, distance will be measured in the
local norm
associated to the Hessian of
f
at the minimizer. To bound the multiplication operator
\gamma^{2}J(f-q)J
, we thus need to bound the third derivatives of
f
for all
x
that lie within distance
r
of the minimizer, in the norm specified by the second derivative of
f
. This is a first reason to use
self-concordant
functions.

Self-concordance.

As mentioned, this is a key concept in modern convex optimization
[
NN94
,
Ren01
]
and we comment more on its relevance for optimization below.
A (thrice-differentiable) convex function
f\colon D\to\mathbb{R}
is
self-concordant

[
NN94
, Section 2.5]
if for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\lvert D^{3}f(x)[u,u,u]\rvert\leq 2\,D^{2}f(x)[u,u]^{3/2}.

This property for example ensures that
f-q
can be bounded by an absolute constant
C
times
r^{3}
on the support of
J
, and thus
\gamma^{2}|J(f-q)J|\leq C\gamma^{4/5}
. Another consequence of self-concordance is that the Hessian of
f
changes slowly, locally. We will make extensive use of this in the Riemannian setting. We now discuss our results in the two settings separately.

Euclidean setting.

Here we largely make more precise and quantitative the celebrated asymptotic analysis of Simon
[
Sim83
]
, see also e.g. the textbooks
[
CFKS87
,
HS12
]
.
At its core is the fact that increasing
\gamma
localizes the low energy states of
\mathcal{H}^{E}
around the minimizer of
f
, which corresponds to the region where
\mathcal{H}^{E}
is well approximated by the harmonic approximation
\mathcal{H}^{E}_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}x^{T}Ax
. Here the local norm of
x
will be
\sqrt{x^{T}Ax}
.
An upper bound on
\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}^{E})
then follows by considering the ansatz ground state
J{|{\psi_{0}}\rangle}
, with
{|{\psi_{0}}\rangle}
the ground state of
\mathcal{H}^{E}_{0}
.
For the lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{E})
, starting from the IMS-localization formula (
1.3
), we roughly show that

\mathcal{H}^{E}\succeq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{E}_{0})+F+O(\|A\|\gamma^{4/5}),

with
F
a rank-1 operator.
By the variational or Rayleigh–Ritz principle, this implies that
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{E})\geq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{E}_{0})+O(\|A\|\gamma^{4/5})
.
The error term
O(\|A\|\gamma^{4/5})
is less than
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{E}_{0})-\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}^{E}_{0})=\gamma\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A})
when
\gamma\in\Omega((\|A\|/\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))^{5})
, showing the appearance of a condition number.
After also taking into account dependencies on the dimension
n
, we arrive at the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Spectral gap of
\mathcal{H}^{E}
, informal)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{E}
and
\mathcal{H}^{E}_{0}
be as above.
If
\gamma\gg(n\|A\|/\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))^{5}
then

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{E})-\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}^{E})\geq\gamma\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A})/2.

We point out that our result illustrates the dependence on the conditioning of the potential, but it is independent of the geometry of the domain. The “fundamental gap” result of
[
AC11
]
that we mentioned earlier is complementary: for any convex potential
V:M\to\mathbb{R}
, it establishes a spectral gap for the operator
-\Delta+V
that depends only on the diameter of
M
(but not on
V
).

Riemannian setting.

Turning to the Riemannian setting, we pursue a similar strategy.
The key challenge is that now

\mathcal{H}^{R}-\mathcal{H}^{R}_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}(L-\Delta)+\gamma^{2}(f-q),

and so we have to additionally bound the approximation of the Laplace–Beltrami operator
L
by the Euclidean Laplacian
\Delta
.
To overcome this challenge, we can use the basis-independence of
\mathcal{H}^{R}
: this allows us to choose our coordinates in such a way that the Hessian of
f
at the minimizer is equal to the identity, i.e., we can compare
\mathcal{H}^{R}
with a standard quantum harmonic oscillator.
Since
L
is based on the metric induced by the Hessian of
f
, and
\Delta
essentially corresponds to a “flat metric”, we get that
JLJ
approximates
J\Delta J
provided that the Hessian is sufficiently stable close to the minimizer of
f
.
This is the second reason that self-concordance is natural within such a semiclassical analysis.
Using the stability of Hessians provided by self-concordance, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2

(Spectral gap of
\mathcal{H}^{R}
, informal)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{R}
and
\mathcal{H}^{R}_{0}
be as above.
If the potential
f
is a self-concordant function and
\gamma\gg(n\log(n))^{5}
then

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}^{R})-\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}^{R})\geq\gamma/2.

Here the
n
-dependence is due to the ground state of the harmonic approximation: it corresponds to an
n
-dimensional Gaussian measure
\mathcal{N}(0,(2\gamma I)^{-1})
and thus it concentrates on a ball of radius roughly
\sqrt{n/\gamma}
. To ensure that this region coincides with the support of
J
, whose radius is
\gamma^{-2/5}
, we require
\sqrt{n/\gamma}\leq\gamma^{-2/5}
and thus roughly
\gamma\geq n^{5}
.

1.2
Technical overview: Quantum interior point method

We now describe how our bounds on spectral gaps can be utilized to develop a quantum interior point method for convex optimization with non-asymptotic performance guarantees.

Classical path-following.

Self-concordance was originally introduced in the context of
interior point methods
(IPMs)
[
NN94
]
.
To solve a constrained convex optimization problem
\mathrm{min}_{x\in D}c^{T}x
, for
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a convex domain
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, IPMs introduce a barrier function
f
such that
f(x)\to\infty
as
x\to\partial D
, and then solve the unconstrained problem

\mathrm{min}_{x}\;\eta c^{T}x+f(x)

for
\eta>0
.
Now if
f
is self-concordant function with “barrier parameter”
\vartheta
, then the central path
\{x_{\eta}=\mathrm{argmin}_{x}\left(\eta c^{T}x+f(x)\right)\}_{\eta\geq 0}
approaches a minimizer at a known rate:
c^{T}x_{\eta}\leq\mathrm{min}_{x\in\overline{D}}c^{T}x+\vartheta/\eta
.
Moreover, self-concordance also gives a bound on the stability of the central path: namely,
\eta\leq\eta^{\prime}\leq(1+{\delta}/{\sqrt{\vartheta}})\eta
implies that
\|x_{\eta}-x_{\eta^{\prime}}\|_{x_{\eta}}\leq O(\delta)
, where
\|y\|_{x}=\sqrt{D^{2}f_{x}[y,y]}
.
Such a bound is critical for classical path-following methods that use Newton steps to trace out the central path and find an approximate minimizer.

Quantum path-following.

In almost direct analogy, we can describe a quantum path-following method by considering a sequence of Hamiltonians

\mathcal{H}^{R}(\eta)=-L+\gamma^{2}(\eta c^{T}x+f)

with corresponding ground states
{|{\psi_{\eta}}\rangle}
.
From our semiclassical analysis we then learned two things.
First, for a large
\gamma\in\widetilde{\Omega}(n^{5})
, the spectral gap of
\mathcal{H}^{R}(\eta)
is proportional to
\gamma
. Importantly,
\gamma
is
independent of
\eta
.
Second, for such a choice of
\gamma
, each ground state
{|{\psi_{\eta}}\rangle}
is close to a Gaussian wave function concentrated around
x_{\eta}
.
Combining this with the stability of the central path
\{x_{\eta}\}
allows us to prove that
\braket{\psi_{\eta}|\psi_{\eta^{\prime}}}\geq 1/2
for
\eta^{\prime}=(1+O(1/\sqrt{\gamma\vartheta}))\eta
.
This implies that there exists a sequence
1=\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{T}=\vartheta/\varepsilon
with
T\in O(\sqrt{\gamma\vartheta}\log(\vartheta/\varepsilon))
such that the “quantum central path”
\{{|{\psi}\rangle}_{\eta}\}
is similarly stable: it satisfies
\braket{\psi_{\eta_{\ell}}|\psi_{\eta_{\ell+1}}}\geq 1/2
for all
\ell
.

Combining these two observations allows us to use a technique called
quantum annealing
to algorithmically follow this “quantum central path” from the initial state
{|{\psi_{\eta_{0}}}\rangle}
(which we assume can be prepared efficiently
3

3

At least knowing
x_{\eta_{0}}
is a typical assumption in IPMs and relatively mild: any point in the interior of
D
lies on
a
central path (for a suitably chosen objective), and one can follow this path backwards to the point where all central paths meet (corresponding to

). See for example

, Sec. 2.4.2]
.
) to the final state
{|{\psi_{\eta_{T}}}\rangle}
(which is localized around the minimizer of our optimization problem).
Following e.g. the approach by Wocjan and Abeyesinghe
[

]
, we can obtain an
\varepsilon
-approximation of
{|{\psi_{\eta_{T}}}\rangle}
by making a total of
O(T\log(T/\varepsilon))
calls to the
\pi/3
-rotations

R_{\ell}=I+(e^{i\pi/3}-I){|{\psi_{\eta_{\ell}}}\rangle}\!{\langle{\psi_{\eta_{\ell}}}|}

and their inverse, for varying
\ell
.

Unbounded Hamiltonian ground state projector.

Having reduced our path-following method to rotating around the ground state of a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{R}(\eta)
, we are faced with a new challenge.
In finite dimensions, it is known that given black-box access to the (controlled) time-evolution operator
e^{iHt}
one can use quantum phase estimation to implement a projection on (and thus rotation around) its ground state.
The operator
\mathcal{H}^{R}(\eta)
, however, is infinite-dimensional and even
unbounded
, and this leads to unwanted aliasing effects when trying to use quantum phase estimation.
We instead propose a different approach, which is arguably more native to the type of continuous-variable operator that we are considering.
We show that by controlling the evolution
e^{it\mathcal{H}^{R}(\eta)}
on an extra continuous-variable register, initialized as a Gaussian state, we can effectively implement the
imaginary-time evolution operator

e^{-\tau(\mathcal{H}^{R}(\eta))^{2}}
. If we assume the ground state energy is
0
, then for sufficiently large
\tau
the operator
e^{-\tau(\mathcal{H}^{R}(\eta))^{2}}
approximates a projector on the ground state of
\mathcal{H}^{R}(\eta)
.
At the core of the idea is the Hubbard-Stratonovich transform, which for
x\in\mathbb{R}
states that

e^{-x^{2}/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-z^{2}/2}e^{-ixz}\,\mathrm{d}z,

and thus shows how a (continuous) linear combination of Hamiltonian evolutions corresponds to an imaginary-time evolution.
While similar ideas appeared in previous works
[
CS17
,
ACNR22
]
, these were restricted to finite-dimensional operators.

We emphasize that our contribution here is mostly conceptual and not (yet) competitive with the current state of the art: we use a different computational model, and require assumptions that need justification (e.g. how does one estimate the ground state energy of
\mathcal{H}^{R}(\eta)
).
However, we view our algorithm as a first concrete step towards a new type of quantum algorithm for optimization that holds the potential to improve over the state of the art (e.g. by avoiding condition-number dependencies of prior work, see
Section

1.4
). At the same time, our work raises several new questions, which we discuss below.

1.3
Open questions

Our work leaves open many directions for future work, here we list a few.

Spectral gap bounds for Schrödinger operators.

We establish our spectral gap bounds via semiclassical arguments. More specifically, we compare our Schrödinger operators to quantum harmonic oscillators. As discussed in
Section

1.1
, the concentration of the ground state of the harmonic oscillator forces us to choose a semiclassical parameter that scales polynomially with the dimension (and other factors in the Euclidean setting). A first natural question is whether one can improve, or even remove, this dependence by comparing to a Schrödinger operator whose ground state exhibits stronger concentration (i.e., whose potential grows faster than quadratically).

Using a semiclassical analysis allows us to use
local
properties of our Schrödinger operator. What about using more
global
properties? In the related setting of Markov diffusion processes (see
Section

1.4
), more global properties such as Poincaré- or (log)-Sobolev-inequalities have been successfully used to quantify the rate of convergence towards the stationary distribution. A striking result here is that Newton-Langevin diffusion (
NLD
) converges at a rate
e^{-t}
for any strictly log-concave target distribution, due to the Brascamp-Lieb inequality.

In the Riemannian setting, we use the Hessian of the potential
f\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
to define a metric on
M
. This allows us to view
M
as a Riemannian manifold and we study the operator
-L+f
where
L
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator. In particular, the potential and the metric are directly related to each other. Can these be decoupled to some extent? Such results are known in the Riemannian Langevin diffusion literature. One concrete example is the mirror-Langevin diffusion process
[
AC21
]
, where the self-concordant barrier
\phi
and the convex potential
V
need only be related to each other via
\alpha
-relative strong convexity and
\beta
-relative smoothness which requires
\alpha\nabla^{2}\phi(x)\preceq\nabla^{2}V(x)\preceq\beta\nabla^{2}\phi(x)
for all
x\in M
. Even for time-discretized dynamics, this led to bounds that only depend on
\alpha,\beta
, and the dimension.

Algorithms (for optimization) based on unbounded operators.

Turning our attention to algorithms, we proposed a quantum path-following method (an interior point method) for convex optimization based on the technique of quantum annealing. Our contribution here is conceptual and not (yet) competitive with the state of the art. We design an algorithm that performs controlled-Hamiltonian simulation with the continuous-variable Schrödinger operator
\mathcal{H}
directly. Using a continuous-variable control register initialized in a Gaussian state, we can implement for example a projector on the ground state of
\mathcal{H}
. We did so under assumptions, see
Section

5
, one of which is the ability to estimate the ground state energy of
\mathcal{H}
(which essentially amounts to estimating the minimum value of
f+\eta c^{T}x
). We leave removing such assumptions for future work.

Another natural question concerns the computational model: our approach fits naturally with the continuous-variable operator that we consider, but it does not fit naturally in the usual qubit-based model of computation. A better understanding of the complexity of continuous-variable algorithms is needed. For background information, see
[
GKP01
,
LB99
,
BSBN02
,
FOP05
,
WPGP+12
]
; for recent progress, see e.g.
[
CJMM25
]
. In a similar spirit, we obtained improved spectral gap bounds by going from the Euclidean setting to the Riemannian, but what is the complexity of simulating the Riemannian Schrödinger operator? Can one effectively incorporate the geometry of the space into a Hamiltonian simulation algorithm?

In a complementary direction, our path-following method is based on quantum annealing. In the finite-dimensional setting, however, an alternative would be to use adiabatic quantum computation
[
FGGS00
]
. Here, a spectral gap estimate also plays a central role. However, there is typically also a dependence on the operator norm of the Hamiltonian (and its derivatives), which prohibits their direct application in the unbounded setting. Recent work has made progress towards an adiabatic theorem that is applicable to unbounded operators, though it requires introducing an “energy cutoff”
[
ML23
]
.
As a clear direction for future work, we leave open the question of developing a suitable (non-asymptotic) adiabatic theorem applicable to our setting.

1.4
Related work

Here we give an overview of related work, emphasizing connections between conditioning and second-order information.

Quantum algorithms for convex optimization.

We focus here on methods for constrained convex optimization, as this is the setting relevant to our work.
For a more complete treatment of quantum algorithms for optimization, we refer the reader to the recent survey
[
AAA+24
]
.

The earliest proposals were quantum algorithms for semidefinite programming (SDP). Two classes of quantum SDP solvers emerged, each seeking to reduce the per-iteration complexity of a classical framework by incorporating quantum subroutines. The works in
[
vAGGdW20
,
vAG19a
,
BS17
,
BKL+19
]
accelerated the multiplicative weights method
[
AK16
]
using the observation that the candidate solutions generated at each iterate are
Gibbs states
, which can be efficiently prepared on quantum computers. The early proposals of
quantum interior point methods
(QIPMs)
[
KP20
,
ANTZ23
]
sought to leverage the fact that one can perform certain linear algebraic operations (such as solve linear systems) on quantum states and unitaries faster than can be carried out for classical vectors and matrices
[
GSLW19
,
CGJ19
]
within the IPM framework. The quantum multiplicative weights and QIPM frameworks for SDP were specialized to linear programming in
[
vAG19b
,
BGJ+23
,
GJLW23
]
and
[
MFT24
,
MFWT25
]

A second line of research that is most relevant to our work are protocols that approach convex optimization by simulating the time dynamics defined by a Schrödinger operator. While quantum algorithms based on dynamical simulation have been studied since the early days of the field
[

]
, they traditionally focused on discrete optimization problems. This changed with the work of Leng et al.
[

]

]

Convergence to the global optimum (in the convex setting) can be established using a Lyapunov argument, and a refined analysis is provided in
[
CHW+25
]
. A number of works have extended the ideas underlying QHD to develop new simulation-based quantum algorithms. One can obtain a quantum analogue of stochastic gradient descent upon considering dynamics of a system coupled with an infinite heat bath
[
CLW+25
]
. Augustino et al.
[

]
generalized these techniques to the constrained setting by deriving a Schrödinger operator that captures the time dynamics of the central path in linear programming.

We remark that the results in
[

a quantum simulation algorithm for simulating quantum dynamics in real-space from

an adiabatic theorem for unbounded Hamiltonians, to prove convergence. Regarding

, the algorithm in
[

]
seems to not properly control all forms of error that can impact the quality of the solution and the resources required to carry out the simulation, see
[

for a detailed discussion of these issues. As for

One approach is to use a form of discretization, after which one can apply a finite-dimensional adiabatic theorem. Rigorously analyzing the error incurred by discretization, however, remains a challenging open problem. Using a grid to discretize space, for instance, inevitably leads to dependencies on the size of the domain, as well as conditioning parameters of e.g. the ground state (the size of the first derivatives determines the spacing needed in the grid). An alternative would be to use an adiabatic theorem that directly applies to the infinite-dimensional setting. Such results are however not readily available in the literature, see our discussion in

Gradient flow and Newton flow.

Differential equations naturally arise if one takes the continuous-time limit of iterative methods such as gradient descent or Newton’s method (alternatively, one may also discretize a differential equation’s evolution to obtain an iterative method). We refer the interested reader to a recent survey

One of the earliest examples is the

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x_{t}=-\nabla f(x_{t})

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}f(x_{t})=-\|\nabla f(x_{t})\|^{2}.

If we assume some additional structure on

\alpha(f(x)-f(x^{*}))\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2},\qquad\forall x,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x_{t}=-(\nabla^{2}f(x_{t}))^{-1}\nabla f(x_{t}).

Exponential convergence rates hold under a Newton-like analogue of the PL inequality:

Markov chains.

Here we consider a canonical sampling problem, which can be thought of as the sampling analogue of convex optimization; in the next paragraph we discuss its connection to a particular class of Schrödinger operators. Given a convex potential
V:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

Witten Laplacian.

We mention here a particularly well-studied Schrödinger operator: the Witten Laplacian, used by by Witten in his proof of the Morse inequalities
[
Wit82
]
. For ease of notation, we restrict here to the Euclidean setting, but we note that the objects below have analogues in the Riemannian setting (see e.g.
[
CZS22
,
Hsu02
,
GC11
,
BGL14
,
GV22
,
AC21
]
). Given a potential function
V

it takes the form

-\Delta+\|\nabla V\|^{2}-\Delta V.

(1.6)

The Witten Laplacian is strongly connected to the Langevin diffusion process discussed above.
In
Eq.

LD
we describe the Langevin diffusion process via a stochastic differential equation. Equivalently, one can study the Markov semigroup associated to
Eq.

LD
, we refer to
[
BGL14
,
Che25
]
for more information. The generator of the Markov semigroup associated to (

2V
, is (cf.
[
Che25
, Example 1.2.4]
)

\mathcal{L}=\Delta-2\nabla V\cdot\nabla.

This generator is related to the Witten Laplacian via the relation

-\Delta+\|\nabla V\|^{2}-\Delta V=-e^{-V}\circ\mathcal{L}\circ e^{V},

where we view
e^{V}
as a multiplication operator and
\circ
denotes composition.
In particular, this relates the spectral gap of the Witten Laplacian to that of
\mathcal{L}
and it shows that the ground state of the Witten Laplacian is proportional to
e^{-V}
, the pointwise square-root of the stationary distribution of the Langevin diffusion process.
This is a first crucial difference between the Schrödinger operators we study in our work and the Witten Laplacian: in our setting the ground state is not known. The second crucial difference is that for the Witten Laplacian one can leverage the connection to Langevin diffusion to analyze the spectral gap, whereas no such connection is known for
-\Delta+f
. The connection between Langevin diffusion and the Witten Laplacian has recently been used to derive improved quantum algorithms for sampling
[
LDCL25
]
.

\Dom(\mathcal{H})
:

\Dom(\mathcal{H})=\{u\in W_{0}^{1,2}(M):(-\Delta+\gamma^{2}f)u\in L^{2}(M)\}.

For the purpose of determining the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}
, after translation, we may assume
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the minimizer of
f
and
f(0)=0
.
Let
\nabla^{2}f(0)=A
and note that
A
is positive definite (cf.
Definition

2.1
).
Write

q(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax.

We use the harmonic approximation

\mathcal{H}_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\gamma^{2}q,

whose spectrum is well known. In particular, its spectrum is (purely) discrete and the smallest two points are
\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})=\frac{\gamma}{2}\Tr[\sqrt{A}]
and
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})=\frac{\gamma}{2}(\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+2\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(A)})
. Throughout, we let
{|{\psi_{0}}\rangle}
(or
\psi_{0}
) denote the eigenstate of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
corresponding to
\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})
.

The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(Spectral gap of
-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\gamma^{2}f
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\gamma^{2}f
for a self-concordant function
f
.
Then there exists a universal constant
c>0
such that if
\gamma
satisfies

\gamma\geq c\,\mathrm{max}\{(n\sqrt{1+\|A\|})^{5}\log^{5}(n(1+\|A\|)),(\|A\|/\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))^{5}\},

then we have

\gap(\mathcal{H})\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}).

Notably, the semiclassical parameter
\gamma
that is needed to establish a quantitative spectral gap depends polynomially on the conditioning of
A
, and thus
\gamma
cannot in general be treated as a constant.

3.1
Proof strategy

We now explain the proof strategy.
We will analyze
\mathcal{H}
by “localizing” it on the region where
q
is a good approximation of
f
. Specifically, we will use the so-called IMS-localization formula, named after Ismagilov, Morgan and Simon, and popularized by I.M. Sigal. We refer to, e.g.,
[
CFKS87
, Sec. 3]
for the version below.
In
Section

4
we will state (and prove) a version on Riemannian manifolds.

Lemma 3.2

(IMS localization formula)
.

Let
J,\bar{J}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be such that
J^{2}+\bar{J}^{2}=I
.
Then

\mathcal{H}=J\mathcal{H}J+\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}-\|\nabla J\|^{2}-\|\nabla\bar{J}\|^{2}.

Using the IMS localization formula we can write

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=J\mathcal{H}_{0}J+J(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{0})J+\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}-\|\nabla J\|^{2}-\|\nabla\bar{J}\|^{2}.

(3.1)

Our general proof strategy is now simple: we construct a function
J
for which we have good control over the terms on the right hand side of
Eq.

3.1
. In particular, in the next section we will construct a function
J
that has the following properties:

•

inner region:
\|J(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{0})J\|\leq O(\gamma^{4/5})
, see
Corollary

3.4
.

•

boundary:
\|\nabla J\|^{2},\|\nabla\bar{J}\|^{2}\leq O(\|A\|\gamma^{4/5})
, see
Lemma

3.5
.

•

outer region:
\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}\succeq\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{6/5}(\bar{J})^{2}
for
\gamma=\Omega(1)
, see
Lemma

3.6
.

•

ground state:
\|\bar{J}\psi_{0}\|^{2}\leq\exp(-ctn)
for some universal constant
c>0
if
\gamma\geq\big(tn\sqrt{\|A\|}\big)^{5}
, see
Lemma

3.9
.

The first two items show that the
J(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{0})J
,
\lVert\nabla J\rVert^{2}
,
\lVert\nabla\bar{J}\rVert^{2}
terms in
Eq.

3.1
are of order
\gamma^{4/5}
.
By contrast, the spectral gap of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
scales like
\gamma
, hence we expect these terms to be irrelevant.
The other terms must be dealt with separately for the lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H})
and the upper bound on
\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H})
, which we do in
Sections

3.3
and
3.4
, respectively.

3.2
Construction of the localization

We will construct a localization function
J
in the following way.
Given
\gamma>0
, let
j,\bar{j}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be as in
Lemma

2.4
. That is,
j^{2}+\bar{j}^{2}=1
and
j(t)=1
for
\lvert t\rvert\leq\gamma^{-2/5}
,
j(t)=0
for
\lvert t\rvert\geq 2\gamma^{-2/5}
, and such that
\lvert j^{\prime}(t)\rvert,\lvert\bar{j}^{\prime}(t)\rvert\leq 5\gamma^{2/5}
.
We then consider
J\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
defined by

J(x)=j(\sqrt{x^{T}Ax})=j(\lVert x\rVert_{A}).

(3.2)

To show that
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is a good approximation of
\mathcal{H}
when we restrict to the support of
J
, we observe that
J(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{0})J
is the multiplication operator

\gamma^{2}J(f-q)J
.
The pointwise estimate from the corollary below is thus a bound on its operator norm
\lVert\gamma^{2}J(f-q)J\rVert
. The difference between
f
and
q
is precisely the difference between
f
and its second-order Taylor expansion around the minimizer. This difference can be expressed in terms of the third-derivative of
f
. Due to the localization, we need to bound this third-derivative for all points that are close to the minimizer with respect to the norm induced by the second-derivative. Self-concordance allows one to get a clean bound.

Lemma 3.3

(Third-order bound
[
Ren01
, Theorem 2.2.2]
)
.

Let
f
be self-concordant,
x\in M
, and let
q_{x}
be the quadratic Taylor approximation of
f
at
x
. Then, for
y
such that
\|y-x\|_{x}<1
, we have

\lvert f(y)-q_{x}(y)\rvert\leq\frac{\lVert y-x\rVert_{x}^{3}}{3(1-\lVert y-x\rVert_{x})}

Corollary 3.4

(Inner region)
.

Let
f
be self-concordant and assume
0<2\gamma^{-2/5}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Then we have, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\gamma^{2}\lvert J(x)(f(x)-q(x))J(x)\rvert\leq\frac{8}{3}\gamma^{4/5}.

Proof.

For
y\in\supp J
, we have
\lVert y-x\rVert_{x}\leq 2\gamma^{-2/5}\leq\frac{1}{2}
, and so
\lvert f(y)-q_{x}(y)\rvert\leq 8\gamma^{-6/5}/3
.
∎

Lemma 3.5

(Boundary)
.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\lVert\nabla J(x)\rVert^{2},\lVert\nabla\bar{J}(x)\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{\lVert A\rVert}{4}\cdot C\gamma^{4/5},

where
C>0
is a universal constant.

Proof.

We give the proof for
J
; the proof for
\bar{J}
is analogous, as we assume the same estimates on the derivatives of
\bar{j}
as we do for
j
.
We have
\nabla J(x)=Dj(\lVert x\rVert_{A})\cdot Ax/(2\sqrt{x^{T}Ax})
, and so

\lVert\nabla J(x)\rVert^{2}=\lvert Dj({\lVert x\rVert_{A}})\rvert^{2}\frac{\lVert Ax\rVert^{2}}{4\lVert x\rVert_{A}^{2}}=\lvert Dj({\lVert x\rVert_{A}})\rvert^{2}\frac{x^{T}A^{1/2}AA^{1/2}x}{4x^{T}Ax}\leq\frac{\lVert A\rVert}{4}\cdot O(\gamma^{4/5}).

The last inequality follows from the upper bound on the sup-norm of
Dj
from
Lemma

2.4
.
∎

Lemma 3.6

(Outer region)
.

For a self-concordant
f
with
f(0)=0
as minimum,
\mathcal{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\gamma^{2}f
,
J
and
\bar{J}
as above, and
\gamma\geq\Omega(1)
, we have

\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}\succeq\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{6/5}(\bar{J})^{2}.

Proof.

We first note that
-\Delta\succeq 0
and therefore
\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}\succeq\gamma^{2}f(\bar{J})^{2}
. It thus suffices to give a lower bound on
f
on the support of
\bar{J}
. The support of
\bar{J}
is contained in the set
\{x:\|x\|_{A}\geq\gamma^{-2/5}\}
where
A=\nabla^{2}f(0)
.
Consider the function
\rho(r)=-r-\ln(1-r)
.
Then
\rho(r)=\frac{1}{2}r^{2}+O(r^{3})
for small
r
, and
\rho(r)\geq\frac{1}{4}r^{2}
when
\lvert r\rvert\leq 1
.
When
1>\lVert x\rVert_{A}
, self-concordance of
f
yields

f(x)\geq f(0)+\rho(-\lVert x\rVert_{A})\geq f(0)+\frac{1}{4}\lVert x\rVert_{A}^{2},

see e.g.
[
Nes18
, Theorem 5.1.8]
.
Due to convexity of
f
, this implies
f(x)-f(0)\geq\frac{1}{4}
whenever
\|x\|_{A}\geq 1
as well.
In particular this shows
\gamma^{2}f(x)\geq\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{6/5}
whenever
\lVert x\rVert_{A}\geq\gamma^{-2/5}
.
∎

3.3
Lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\gamma^{2}f)

We now use the properties of the localization function to prove a lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H})
.
Namely,
Corollaries

3.4
,
3.5
and
3.6
imply that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=J\mathcal{H}_{0}J

\displaystyle+J(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{0})J

\displaystyle+\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}

\displaystyle-\lVert\nabla J\rVert^{2}-\|\nabla\bar{J}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\succeq J\mathcal{H}_{0}J

\displaystyle-\frac{8}{3}\gamma^{4/5}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{6/5}(\bar{J})^{2}

\displaystyle-O(\|A\|\gamma^{4/5}).

To lower bound the first term, we can use the inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}

\displaystyle\succeq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})I-(\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})-\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})){|{\psi_{0}}\rangle}\!{\langle{\psi_{0}}|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{2}\left((\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+2\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))I-2\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}){|{\psi_{0}}\rangle}\!{\langle{\psi_{0}}|}\right).

This gives us

\mathcal{H}\succeq\frac{\gamma}{2}(\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+2\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))J^{2}-\gamma\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A})J{|{\psi_{0}}\rangle}\!{\langle{\psi_{0}}|}J+\gamma^{6/5}(\bar{J})^{2}-O((\|A\|+1)\gamma^{4/5}).

Whenever

\gamma\geq\left[\frac{1}{2}(\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+2\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))\right]^{5},

(3.3)

we can further combine the terms containing
J^{2}
and
\bar{J}^{2}
,
using
J^{2}+\bar{J}^{2}=1
, to obtain

\mathcal{H}\succeq\frac{\gamma}{2}(\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+2\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))I-\gamma\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A})J{|{\psi_{0}}\rangle}\!{\langle{\psi_{0}}|}J-O((\|A\|+1)\gamma^{4/5}).

(3.4)

Using that
J{|{\psi_{0}}\rangle}\!{\langle{\psi_{0}}|}J\preceq I
, this implies that

\mathcal{H}\succeq\frac{\gamma}{2}\Tr[\sqrt{A}]-O((\|A\|+1)\gamma^{4/5}).

and so

\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H})\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\Tr[\sqrt{A}]-O((\|A\|+1)\gamma^{4/5}).

(3.5)

Towards the upper bound, we notice that (
3.4
) is a lower bound of the form

\mathcal{H}\succeq\frac{\gamma}{2}(\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+2\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))I+F-O((\|A\|+1)\gamma^{4/5}),

(3.6)

where
F
has rank 1.
This proves that
\mathcal{H}
can only have a single eigenvalue below
\frac{\gamma}{2}(\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+2\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))-O((\|A\|+1)\gamma^{4/5})
. In other words, it gives a lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H})
:

Theorem 3.7
.

For a self-concordant function
f
and
\gamma\geq\left[\frac{1}{2}(\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+2\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))\right]^{5}

we have

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H})\geq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})-O(\|A\|\gamma^{4/5})=\frac{\gamma}{2}(\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+2\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))-O(\|A\|\gamma^{4/5}).

3.4
Upper bound on
\lambda_{0}(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\gamma^{2}f)

To prove an upper bound on
\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H})
, it suffices to construct a test function
\psi
which has small energy. In particular, we want it to satisfy the following inequality:

\frac{\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle}\leq\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})+O(\|A\|\gamma^{4/5})=\frac{\gamma}{2}\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+O(\|A\|\gamma^{4/5}).

To do so, we will use
\psi=J\psi_{0}
where
\psi_{0}
is the (known) ground state of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
For convenience, we recall that
\mathcal{H}_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}x^{T}Ax
corresponds to a quantum harmonic oscillator with ground state

\psi_{0}(x)=C_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma}{2}x^{T}\sqrt{A}x\right),

where
C_{0}
is the normalization constant for which
\langle\psi_{0},\psi_{0}\rangle=1
.

Concretely, we establish the following theorem.

Theorem 3.8
.

For a self-concordant function
f
and
\gamma\geq\Omega((n\sqrt{\|A\|})^{5}\cdot\polylog(n,\|A\|))
, we have

\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H})\leq\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})+O(\|A\|\gamma^{4/5})=\frac{\gamma}{2}\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+O(\|A\|\gamma^{4/5}).

Together with
Theorem

3.7
and
Eq.

3.5
, this will imply
Theorem

3.1
.
Moreover, if additionally
\gamma\in\Omega((\|A\|/\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))^{5})
then

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H})-\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H})\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}).

We start by proving a lemma on ground state concentration.

Lemma 3.9

(Ground state of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
: concentration)
.

There exists some universal constant
c_{0}>0
such that for
t\geq 1
and for
\gamma\geq\big(tn\sqrt{\|A\|}\big)^{5}
, we have
\langle J\psi_{0},J\psi_{0}\rangle\geq 1-2\exp(-c_{0}tn)
.

Proof.

Using the identity
J^{2}+\bar{J}^{2}=1
, we have

\langle J\psi_{0},J\psi_{0}\rangle=\langle\psi_{0},\psi_{0}\rangle-\langle\bar{J}\psi_{0},\bar{J}\psi_{0}\rangle

and so we can equivalently prove an upper bound on
\langle\bar{J}\psi_{0},\bar{J}\psi_{0}\rangle
.
We will use that

\langle\bar{J}\psi_{0},\bar{J}\psi_{0}\rangle\leq\psi_{0}^{2}(\mathrm{sup}\bar{J}),

so that it suffices to bound the probability of
\mathrm{sup}\bar{J}
under the measure
\psi_{0}^{2}
.
Since the measure
\psi_{0}^{2}
corresponds to the high-dimensional Gaussian measure
\mathcal{N}(0,(2\gamma\sqrt{A})^{-1})
, we can use standard concentration results.
E.g., from the Hanson-Wright inequality (
[
Ver18
, Thm. 6.2.1]
) we can derive that if
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)
then there exists a universal constant
c_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\|x\|_{\Sigma^{-1}}^{2}\geq(t+1)n\right)\leq 2\exp(-c_{0}tn),\quad\forall t\geq 1.

(3.7)

Recalling that on the support of
\bar{J}
we have
\|x\|_{A}\geq\gamma^{-2/5}
, we get that

\psi_{0}^{2}(\mathrm{sup}\bar{J})\leq\mathbb{P}(\|x\|_{A}\geq\gamma^{-2/5})

for
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(2\gamma\sqrt{A})^{-1})
.
We can bound this by noting that

\|x\|_{2\gamma\sqrt{A}}^{2}=2\gamma x^{T}\sqrt{A}x\geq 2\gamma x^{T}Ax/\sqrt{\|A\|}=2\gamma\|x\|_{A}^{2}/\sqrt{\|A\|},

and so

\mathbb{P}(\|x\|_{A}\geq\gamma^{-2/5})\leq\mathbb{P}(\|x\|^{2}_{2\gamma\sqrt{A}}\geq 2\gamma^{1/5}/\sqrt{\|A\|})

For
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(2\gamma\sqrt{A})^{-1})
and
2\gamma^{1/5}/\sqrt{\|A\|}\geq(t+1)n
, and using (
3.7
), this is bounded by
2\exp(-c_{0}tn)
.
∎

We now state two technical lemmas that will allow us to estimate the ground energy of
\mathcal{H}
in
Lemma

3.12
.

Lemma 3.10
.

There exist some universal constants
c_{0},c_{1}>0
such that for
t\geq 1
and for
\gamma\geq\big(tn\sqrt{\|A\|}\big)^{5}
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla\psi_{0}(x)\|^{2}\bar{J}(x)^{2}dx=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(x^{T}Ax)\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\bar{J}(x)^{2}dx\leq c_{1}\gamma\sqrt{\|A\|}n\exp(-c_{0}tn).

Proof.

We first note that
\|\nabla\psi_{0}(x)\|^{2}=\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}x^{T}Ax\cdot\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\leq\frac{\gamma^{2}}{4}\|A\|^{1/2}x^{T}\sqrt{A}x\cdot\psi_{0}(x)^{2}
. Then, using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x^{T}\sqrt{A}x\cdot\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\bar{J}(x)^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(x^{T}\sqrt{A}x)^{2}\cdot\psi_{0}(x)^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bar{J}(x)^{4}\psi_{0}(x)^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}.

We bound the two factors separately. First, substituting
z=(2\gamma\sqrt{A})^{1/2}x
, we can write the first factor as
\mathbb{E}[(2\gamma)^{4}(z^{T}z)^{2}]
where
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. Using
\mathbb{E}[z_{i}^{2}]=1
and
\mathbb{E}[z_{i}^{4}]=3
, this gives

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(x^{T}\sqrt{A}x)^{2}\cdot\psi_{0}(x)^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\|x\|_{\sqrt{A}}^{4}]^{1/2}=(2\gamma)^{-1}\mathbb{E}[\|z\|^{4}]^{1/2}\leq(2\gamma)^{-1}\sqrt{n^{2}+2n}.

For the remaining factor we use that
\bar{J}(x)^{2}\leq 1
to obtain

\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bar{J}(x)^{4}\psi_{0}(x)^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}\leq\sqrt{\langle\bar{J}\psi_{0},\bar{J}\psi_{0}\rangle}\leq\sqrt{2}\exp(-c_{0}tn/2),

where the last inequality uses
Lemma

3.9
.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

There exists some universal constant
c_{0}>0
such that for
t\geq 1
and for
\gamma\geq\big(tn\sqrt{\|A\|}\big)^{5}
, we have

\langle J^{2}\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}_{0}J^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle=\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})+O((\gamma^{4/5}\|A\|+\gamma\sqrt{\|A\|}n)\exp(-c_{0}tn)).

In particular, we have

\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},-\Delta\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle=O((\gamma^{4/5}\|A\|+\gamma\|A\|^{1/2}n)\exp(-c_{0}tn)).

Proof.

Using the identity
1=J^{2}+\bar{J}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle J^{2}\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}_{0}J^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(1-\bar{J}^{2})\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}_{0}(1-\bar{J}^{2})\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}_{0}\psi_{0}\rangle-2\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}_{0}\psi_{0}\rangle+\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}_{0}\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})-2\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},\psi_{0}\rangle+\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}_{0}\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle.

We now note that
\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},\psi_{0}\rangle\leq 2\exp(-c_{0}tn)
by
Lemma

3.9
. It thus remains to analyze
\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}_{0}\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle
. For this we recall that
\mathcal{H}_{0}=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\frac{1}{2}\gamma^{2}x^{T}Ax
and hence

\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}_{0}\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle=\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},-\frac{1}{2}\Delta\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle+\gamma^{2}\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},(\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax)\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle.

We now compute the two terms separately. First, we have

\gamma^{2}\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},(\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax)\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle\leq\gamma^{2}\langle\bar{J}\psi_{0},(\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax)\bar{J}\psi_{0}\rangle\leq 2c_{1}\gamma\sqrt{\|A\|}n\exp(-c_{0}tn),

where the last inequality is due to
Lemma

3.10
. For the remaining term, we recall that for
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
, we have

\langle\phi,-\Delta\phi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla\phi(x)\|^{2}dx.

(3.8)

Using this we can rewrite

\displaystyle\langle\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0},-\Delta\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla(\bar{J}^{2}\psi_{0})(x)\|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|2\bar{J}(x)\psi_{0}(x)\nabla\bar{J}(x)+\bar{J}^{2}\nabla\psi_{0}(x)\|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}2\left(\|2\bar{J}(x)\psi_{0}(x)\nabla\bar{J}(x)\|^{2}+\|\bar{J}(x)^{2}\nabla\psi_{0}(x)\|^{2}\right)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}8\|\nabla\bar{J}(x)\|^{2}\bar{J}(x)^{2}\psi_{0}(x)^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla\psi_{0}(x)\|^{2}\bar{J}(x)^{2}dx

Using
Lemmas

3.5
and
3.9
we can upper bound the first term by
O(\gamma^{4/5}\|A\|\exp(-c_{0}tn))
.
Lemma

3.10
upper bounds the second term by
O(\gamma\sqrt{\|A\|}n\exp(-c_{0}tn))
.
∎

We can now show the main claim of this section, proving that
J\psi_{0}/\|J\psi_{0}\|
has low energy with respect to
\mathcal{H}
.

Lemma 3.12

(Ground energy comparison)
.

Let
\psi_{0}
be the ground state of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
J
as above.
There exist universal constants
c,C>0
such that if
\gamma\geq c(n\sqrt{1+\|A\|})^{5}\log^{5}(n(1+\|A\|))
, then

\frac{\langle J\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}J\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle J\psi_{0},J\psi_{0}\rangle}\leq\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})+C(\|A\|+1)\gamma^{4/5}.

Proof.

For a fixed
t\geq 1
(which we will specify later), assume that
\gamma\geq(tn\sqrt{\|A\|})^{5}
.
Then by
Lemma

3.9
we have
\langle J\psi_{0},J\psi_{0}\rangle\geq 1-2\exp(-c_{0}tn)
. We thus have
\frac{\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle}\leq(1+4\exp(-c_{0}tn))\langle J\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}J\psi_{0}\rangle
(assuming
2\exp(-c_{0}tn)\leq 1/2
).
We now use IMS-localization (in particular,
Eq.

3.1
) again to obtain

\displaystyle\langle J\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}J\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle J\psi_{0},\left(J(\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{0})J+J\mathcal{H}_{0}J+\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}-\|\nabla J\|^{2}-\|\nabla\bar{J}\|^{2}\right)J\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=c_{0}\gamma^{4/5}+\langle J\psi_{0},J\mathcal{H}_{0}JJ\psi_{0}\rangle+\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle-c_{1}\|A\|\gamma^{4/5}.

where
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
are the constants from
Corollary

3.4
and
Lemma

3.5
respectively. It remains to analyze
\langle J\psi_{0},J\mathcal{H}_{0}JJ\psi_{0}\rangle
and
\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle
.
By
Lemma

3.11
we have
\langle J\psi_{0},J\mathcal{H}_{0}JJ\psi_{0}\rangle\leq\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})+O((\gamma^{4/5}\|A\|+\gamma\sqrt{\|A\|}n)\exp(-c_{0}tn))
.
We finally upper bound the remaining term
\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle
. Using the definition of
\mathcal{H}
, we have

\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle=\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta)\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle+\gamma^{2}\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}f\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle.

For the first term we use
Eq.

3.8
and the identity

\nabla(\bar{J}(x)J(x)\psi_{0}(x))=J(x)\psi_{0}(x)\nabla\bar{J}(x)+\bar{J}(x)\psi_{0}(x)\nabla J(x)+J(x)\bar{J}(x)\nabla\psi_{0}(x)

to obtain

\displaystyle\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}(-\Delta)\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\nabla(\bar{J}(x)J(x)\psi_{0}(x))\|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq 3\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}J(x)^{2}\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\|\nabla\bar{J}(x)\|^{2}+\bar{J}(x)^{2}\psi_{0}(x)^{2}\|\nabla J(x)\|^{2}+J(x)^{2}\bar{J}(x)^{2}\|\nabla\psi_{0}(x)\|^{2}dx.

By
Lemma

3.5
the first two terms are
O(\gamma^{4/5}\|A\|)
, while the last term is
O(\gamma\sqrt{\|A\|}n\exp(-c_{0}tn))
by
Lemma

3.10
.
This shows that

\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}(-\frac{1}{2}\Delta)\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle=O\left(\gamma^{4/5}\|A\|+\gamma\sqrt{\|A\|}n\exp(-c_{0}tn)\right).

It remains to upper bound
\gamma^{2}\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}f\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle

Corollary

3.4
shows

\gamma^{2}\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}f\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle\leq\gamma^{2}\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}(\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax)\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle+O(\gamma^{4/5}).

We next upper bound
\langle J\psi_{0},\bar{J}(\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax)\bar{J}J\psi_{0}\rangle
by
O(\gamma\sqrt{\|A\|}n\exp(-c_{0}tn))
using
Lemma

3.10
.

Combining these estimates yields the final bound

\frac{\langle J\psi_{0},\mathcal{H}J\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle J\psi_{0},J\psi_{0}\rangle}=O\left(\gamma^{4/5}\|A\|+\gamma^{2}\sqrt{\|A\|}n\exp(-c_{0}tn)\right).

For
\gamma\geq e
we can set

for a large enough constant

, and obtain the claimed bound.
A sufficient bound on
\gamma
is then

\gamma\geq\left(Cn\sqrt{1+\|A\|}\log(\gamma(1+\|A\|)n)\right)^{5}.

Applying
Lemma

2.5

x=\gamma(1+\|A\|)n

for some universal constant

∎

As a corollary, we can prove a convenient bound on the overlap between the ground state of our Schrödinger operator and that of its harmonic approximation.
This will be useful for our later quantum path-following algorithm.

Corollary 3.13

(Ground state overlap)

Let
\psi

\psi_{0}
denote the ground states of

and
\mathcal{H}_{0}

For any

\gamma\in\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\mathrm{max}\{\log(1/\delta)n(1+\|A\|)^{5/2},(1+\|A\|)^{5}/(\delta\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\sqrt{A}))^{5}\}\right)

\lvert\braket{\psi,\psi_{0}}\rvert\geq 1-\delta.

Proof.

By
Lemma

3.9
we know that
\lvert\braket{\psi,\psi_{0}}\rvert\geq\lvert\braket{\psi,J\psi_{0}}\rvert-\delta/2
if
\gamma\in\Omega((\log(1/\delta)tn\sqrt{\lVert A\rVert})^{5})
for some
t\geq c\log(\gamma\|A\|n)
for a large enough universal constant

.
8
8

8

Note that
\braket{\psi_{0},J\psi_{0}}\geq\braket{J\psi_{0},J\psi_{0}}
, and so
\lvert\braket{\psi,\psi_{0}-J\psi_{0}}\rvert\leq\lVert\psi_{0}-J\psi_{0}\rVert\lVert\psi\rVert=\sqrt{1+\lVert J\psi_{0}\rVert^{2}-2\braket{\psi_{0},J\psi_{0}}}\leq\sqrt{1-\lVert J\psi_{0}\rVert^{2}}

2.5

Semiclassical analysis: Riemannian setting

Let

E=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

\mathcal{H}=-\frac{1}{2}L+\gamma^{2}f,

L

Our goal is to prove that this operator has a

\gamma\geq Cn^{c}
for some universal constants
C,c>0

More precisely, we will show the following result:

Theorem 4.1

(Spectral gap of
-\frac{1}{2}L+\gamma^{2}f
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=-\frac{1}{2}L+\gamma^{2}f
for a self-concordant function

.
Then there exists a universal constant

such that if

\gamma\geq c(n\log(n))^{5}

\gap(\mathcal{H})\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}.

Unlike in the Euclidean case (

3

Along the way, we also construct a state

\mathcal{H}
.

This section is organized as follows.
We first introduce the necessary notation and definitions in
Section

4.1
.
We review basic properties of the Laplace–Beltrami and Schrödinger operators in
Section

4.2
.
In

4.3

In

4.4

\mathcal{H}
.
We establish a suitable local approximation of
\mathcal{H}
by a Laplace operator in

4.5
.
We then lower bound
\lambda_{1}(H)
in

4.6

in

4.7
.
In

.

4.1
Riemannian geometry

Because
M
is an open subset of the Euclidean space
E=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it naturally has the structure of an
n
-dimensional oriented manifold.
The tangent space at any point
x\in M
can be identified as
T_{x}M=E
.
We briefly recall basic notions of differential and Riemannian geometry, and refer the reader to
[
Lee12
,
Lee18
]
for more information.

Metric induced by a convex function.

Given a convex function
f\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
with positive definite Hessian
H(x)
(with respect to the coordinates of
E=\mathbb{R}^{n}
), we can use the local inner product (
2.1
) to define a Riemannian metric on
M
:

\displaystyle g_{x}(u,v)=\braket{u,v}_{x}=\braket{u,H(x)v}_{E}=D^{2}f_{x}[u,v]\quad\text{for}\penalty\ u,v\in T_{x}M=E,

If
f
is a self-concordant function and
M
is relatively compact in
E
, then the resulting manifold is complete
[
NT02
, Thm. 2.1]
.
9
9

9

The result is stated for self-concordant barriers, but actually only depends on “strongly non-degenerate” self-concordance as in
Definition

2.1
.

Tangent and cotangent vectors.

The metric
g
allows us to identify tangent vector and cotangent vectors.
Any tangent vector
u\in T_{x}M
determines a cotangent vector
\ell=g_{x}(u,\cdot)\in T_{x}^{*}M
, and vice versa.
In this way, the metric
g
induces a dual inner product on the cotangent spaces, which we will denote by
g_{x}^{*}\colon T_{x}^{*}M\times T_{x}^{*}M\to\mathbb{R}
.
It is defined such that if
\ell=g_{x}(u,\cdot)
and
\ell^{\prime}=g_{x}(v,\cdot)
, then
g_{x}^{*}(\ell,\ell^{\prime})=g_{x}(u,v)
.
There is also a variational characterization: for
\ell\in T^{*}_{x}M
, we have

g_{x}^{*}(\ell,\ell)=\mathrm{sup}_{0\neq u\in T_{x}M}\frac{\lvert\ell(u)\rvert^{2}}{g_{x}(u,u)}.

(4.1)

This implies that the dualization is order-reversing in the following sense:
if one has two inner products
g
and
\tilde{g}
on
T_{x}M
(or any other vector space) such that
g\preceq\tilde{g}
, i.e.,
g(u,u)\leq\tilde{g}(u,u)
for all
u\in T_{x}M
, then the dual inner products satisfy
g^{*}\succeq{\tilde{g}}^{*}
, that is, for every
\ell\in T_{x}^{*}M

g^{*}(\ell,\ell)=\mathrm{sup}_{0\neq u\in T_{x}M}\frac{\lvert\ell(u)\rvert^{2}}{g(u,u)}\geq\mathrm{sup}_{0\neq u\in T_{x}M}\frac{\lvert\ell(u)\rvert^{2}}{\tilde{g}(u,u)}=\tilde{g}^{*}(\ell,\ell).

(4.2)

Both
g_{x}
and
g_{x}^{*}
induce norms on
T_{x}M
and
T_{x}^{*}M
, respectively; we shall denote both by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{R}
.

We will write
d\psi
for the
differential
of a function
\psi\in C^{\infty}(M)
.
It is formally a smooth map
M\to T^{*}M
such that
x\mapsto d\psi_{x}\in T_{x}^{*}M
.
In our setting
M\subseteq E
, and the differential can be concretely given by
d\psi_{x}(u)=D\psi_{x}[u]=\partial_{t=0}\psi(x+tu)
; for the general definition see
[
Lee12
, Ch. 3]
.
The object
d\psi
is an example of a covector field.
We denote its norm by

\lVert d\psi\rVert_{R}\colon M\to\mathbb{R},\quad\lVert d\psi\rVert_{R}(x)\coloneqq\sqrt{g_{x}^{*}(d\psi_{x},d\psi_{x})}=\mathrm{sup}_{0\neq u\in T_{x}M}\frac{\lvert d\psi_{x}(u)\rvert}{\sqrt{g_{x}(u,u)}}.

(4.3)

the smooth function that assigns to any
x\in M
the norm of the differential of
\psi
at this point.

The
gradient

\grad^{g}\psi
is the vector field defined by
g_{x}(\grad_{x}^{g},\cdot)=d\psi_{x}
.
Unlike the differential, the gradient depends on the choice of the Riemannian metric.

Integration.

Our choice of Riemannian metric also induces a measure on the manifold
M
, which we denote by
d\mathrm{vol}_{g}
.
If one chooses linear coordinates
x
with respect to a basis of
E
and considers the metric
g_{x}
as a positive definite matrix, the measure
d\mathrm{vol}_{g}
has density
\sqrt{\det g}
with respect to the corresponding Lebesgue measure
dx
.
10
10

10

It is a standard fact that
d\mathrm{vol}_{g}
does not depend on the local choice of coordinates (up to a sign, which is fixed by the orientation), see
[
Lee18
, Prop. 2.41]
.

We denote by
L^{2}(M;\mathrm{vol}_{g})
the Hilbert space of square-integrable real-valued functions with respect to the measure
\mathrm{vol}_{g}
, and denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{R}
the corresponding
L^{2}
-inner product.
Explicitly, we have

\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{R}=\int_{M}\phi(x)\psi(x)\,d\mathrm{vol}_{g}(x)=\int_{M}\phi(x)\psi(x)\sqrt{\det g}\,dx.

We will also use the
L^{2}
-inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{E,z}
of functions induced by the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{z}
on
E
for some fixed
z\in M
.
If we choose linear coordinates
\xi
with respect to an orthonormal basis for the latter, this is given simply by

\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{E,z}=\int_{M}\phi(\xi)\psi(\xi)\,d\xi,

where
d\xi
denotes the standard Lebesgue measure.

4.2
Laplace–Beltrami and Schrödinger operators

Next, we define the Laplace–Beltrami operator, which is a natural generalization of the Laplace operator to the manifold setting, and we recall some fundamental facts about the corresponding Schrödinger operators.

4.2.1
The Laplace–Beltrami operator

There are various ways to understand the Laplace–Beltrami operator, which we (for now) define as a map

L\colon C_{c}^{\infty}(M)\to C_{c}^{\infty}(M).

First off, it is the unique operator with the property that for any two functions
\phi,\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
we have the following integration by parts type formula:

\langle\phi,L\psi\rangle_{R}=\int_{M}\phi(L\psi)\,d\mathrm{vol}_{g}=-\int_{M}g_{x}^{*}(d\phi_{x},d\psi_{x})\,d\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\int_{M}(L\phi)\psi\,d\mathrm{vol}_{g}.

Note that
d\phi,d\psi
are
covector
fields here, so we use the inner product
g_{x}^{*}
on
T_{x}^{*}M
as opposed to
T_{x}M
.

The Laplace–Beltrami operator can also succinctly be defined by the formula
L\psi=\Tr[\nabla(\grad\psi)]
, see e.g.
[
Lee18
, Exercise 5.14]
.
Here,
\grad^{g}\psi
is the gradient vector field (as defined in
Section

4.1
) and
\nabla
is the so-called “Levi-Civita connection” associated with
g

Thus, the object
\nabla(\grad^{g}\psi)
is an “operator field”, i.e. for every
x
it gives a linear map
T_{x}M\to T_{x}M
.
11
11

11

In fact it yields the Riemannian definition of the Hessian, not to be confused with the Euclidean Hessian
H(x)
defined earlier.

Therefore we can take its trace.

In smooth local coordinates, if one expresses the metric
g
as a positive-definite matrix valued function, it is given by

L\psi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(g^{ij}\sqrt{\det g}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{j}}\right).

Here
g^{ij}\coloneqq(g^{-1})_{ij}
is the
(i,j)
-th entry of the inverse matrix
g^{-1}
.
If we use the standard coordinates of
E=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
g
is the Euclidean Hessian
H(x)
and
g^{ij}
are the entries of its inverse.
In particular:

1.

If
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
g_{x}(u,v)=u^{T}v
, then the Laplace–Beltrami operator equals the usual Laplacian.

2.

If
M=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
g_{x}(u,v)=u^{T}Hv
where
H
is a fixed positive definite matrix, then

L\psi=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}H^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x_{j}}=\Delta(\psi\circ H^{-1/2}).

This is because
H^{ij}
and
\sqrt{\det g}
do not depend on
x
.
After a change to orthonormal coordinates for the inner product (e.g.,
\xi=H^{-1/2}x
), one recovers the usual Laplacian.
Note that this change of coordinates is implemented by a
unitary

L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};\sqrt{\det(H)}\,dx)\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n};d\xi)
.

4.2.2
Schrödinger operators

We recall some basic facts about Schrödinger operators

\mathcal{H}=-\frac{1}{2}L+V

in the setting of complete Riemannian manifolds. Throughout, we will consider the setting where
V\in C^{\infty}(M)
is a smooth function on
M
whose sublevel sets are compact (i.e.,
V\in C^{\infty}(M)
and for every
C\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\{x\in M:V(x)\leq C\}
is compact).
This encompasses the setting where the potential is self-concordant: the sublevel sets of self-concordant functions are compact due to
Lemma

2.2
, and the associated manifold is complete due to
[
NT02
, Thm. 2.1]
.

Domain.

We initially consider
\mathcal{H}
as an operator from
C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
to
C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
. It is known that this is an essentially self-adjoint operator, meaning it has a unique self-adjoint extension. This follows for example from
[
Ole94
, Thm. 1]
, where we use that, since
V\in C^{\infty}(M)
has compact sub-level sets, the potential is bounded from below by a constant function and belongs in particular to
L_{\mathrm{loc}}^{\infty}(M)
. We refer the interested reader to
[
BMS02
]
for a discussion of essential self-adjointness of Schrödinger operators under different assumptions. We will from now on consider the unique self-adjoint extension of this operator, the Friedrichs extension, as an operator in
L^{2}(M;\mathrm{vol}_{g})
, here we follow e.g.
[
KS99
]
. We will denote the operator domain of
\mathcal{H}
by
\Dom(\mathcal{H})
:

\Dom(\mathcal{H})=\{u\in H_{1}:\mathcal{H}u\in L^{2}(M)\},

where
H_{1}
is the completion of
C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
with respect to the norm
\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\langle u,(V-V_{\mathrm{min}}+1)u\rangle_{R}+\langle\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle_{R}
, where the potential is shifted by
V_{\mathrm{min}}=\mathrm{min}_{x\in M}V(x)
plus one to ensure
\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}
is a norm.

Spectrum.

The completeness of
M
and the assumption of compactness of the sublevel sets of
V
implies that the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}
is (purely) discrete and bounded from below.
In other words, the spectrum consists of a sequence of eigenvalues

\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H})<\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H})<\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{H})<\ldots,

such that
\lambda_{k}(\mathcal{H})\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
,
and each eigenvalue has finite multiplicity.
This is well known (cf.
[
Ura93
,
Bra25
]
), but the proof for our setting of non-compact manifolds is not easily found in the literature, so we provide it in
Definition

A.1
.

Regularity.

We observe that
\mathcal{H}
is an elliptic operator.
This holds because
V
is a smooth function and the Laplace–Beltrami operator is a second-order elliptic operator.
To see the latter, note that its principal symbol is
P_{2}(x,\xi)=\sum_{i,j}g^{ij}(x)\xi_{i}\xi_{j}
, and hence
P_{2}(x,\xi)\neq 0
for any
x\in M
and
\xi\neq 0
. From elliptic regularity, it follows that eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{H}
belong to
C^{\infty}(M)
.
12
12

12

To see this, for an eigenfunction
\phi\in\Dom(\mathcal{H})
of
\mathcal{H}
corresponding to eigenvalue
\lambda
, one can for instance apply
[
Hö03
, Cor. 8.3.2]
to
P=\mathcal{H}-\lambda I
and
u=\phi
. The corollary shows that the wavefrontset of
\phi
is empty, which implies that there exists a smooth representation of
\phi
.

4.3
IMS localization on manifolds

We briefly recall IMS localization on manifolds, see for example
[
Sim83
]
and
[
Shu96
]
for the setting of differential operators on vector bundles.
First we need the following lemma.

Proposition 4.2

(cf.
[
Shu96
, Lem. 3.1]
,
[
CFKS87
, Eq. (11.37)]
)
.

Let
h\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
and
L
the Laplace–Beltrami operator.
Then

[h,[h,L]]=-2\lVert dh\rVert_{R}^{2},

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{R}
is defined in
Eq.

4.3
, and the functions
h
and
\lVert dh\rVert_{R}^{2}
are interpreted as multiplication operators on
L^{2}(M;\mathrm{vol}_{g})
.

We provide a proof in
Appendix

B
that only relies on the formal properties of
L
.
As in
Section

3
, the above proposition can be used to derive a localization formula.

Lemma 4.3

(IMS localization formula)
.

Let
J,\bar{J}\in C^{\infty}(M)
be such that
J^{2}+\bar{J}^{2}=I
.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=J\mathcal{H}J+\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}-\lVert dJ\rVert_{R}^{2}-\lVert d\bar{J}\rVert_{R}^{2},

where
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{R}
is defined in
Eq.

4.3
.

Proof.

By
Proposition

4.2
we have that

J^{2}\mathcal{H}+\mathcal{H}J^{2}-2J\mathcal{H}J=[J,[J,\mathcal{H}]]=-2\lVert dJ\rVert_{R}^{2}.

We obtain a second equation by replacing
J
by
\bar{J}
in this equation.
Summing the resulting equations (and using that
J^{2}+\bar{J}^{2}=I
) proves the lemma.
∎

4.4
Proof strategy

Here we give an outline of the proof strategy, deferring formal statements to the later sections.
For the given self-concordant function
f
and parameter
\gamma>0
, our goal is to study the Schrödinger operator defined as in
Section

4.2.2
,

\mathcal{H}=-\frac{1}{2}L+\gamma^{2}f.

The idea is (again) to relate
\mathcal{H}
to another operator,
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, which is defined as follows.
Let
z
be the unique minimizer of
f
.
We assume that
f(z)=0
; if not, we may replace
f
by
f-f(z)
without changing the spectral gap.
Consider the vector space
E
but equipped with the local inner product

\langle u,v\rangle_{z}=D^{2}f_{z}[u,v].

We denote the associated Laplace operator by
\Delta^{z}
(defined as in Example
2
in
Section

4.2.1
), and also define the function

r_{z}(x)=\lVert x-z\rVert_{z}

(4.4)

The operator
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is then given by the Euclidean Schrödinger operator

\mathcal{H}_{0}\coloneqq-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{z}+\gamma^{2}q,\qquad\text{where }q(x)=\frac{1}{2}r_{z}(x)^{2}.

which is an essentially self-adjoint unbounded operator on
L^{2}(E)
with the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{E,z}
defined earlier.
That is, if one chooses affine coordinates
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
with respect to a
\braket{\cdot,\cdot}_{z}
-orthonormal basis of
E
such that
z
has coordinate
\xi=0
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\xi_{i}^{2}}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\xi^{T}\xi

is a standard harmonic oscillator.
To show that
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is a good approximation of
\mathcal{H}
, we will show that, after suitable localization near
z
,

-\Delta^{z}
is a good approximation of
-L
and
f
is well approximated by
q
; this will also imply that their ground states have high overlap.

The main difference with the previous section is that now
\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{0}
captures not just an approximation in potential, but also an approximation in the differential operator.
To see this, we write

\mathcal{H}-\mathcal{H}_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(-L+\Delta^{z})+\gamma^{2}(f-q).

(4.5)

Given the IMS-localization formula from
Lemma

4.3
, we thus need bounds on
J(-L+\Delta^{z})J
and
\gamma^{2}J(f-q)J
for suitable cutoff functions
J
.
Informally, the cutoff function allows us to establish such bounds
locally
, i.e., we only need to consider the operators applied to functions whose support is contained in a small region around the minimizer of
f
.
The main difference with the Euclidean setting is that we now have to control the operator
J(-L+\Delta^{z})J
.
To this end, we take as the cutoff function

J(x)\coloneqq j(r_{z}(x))=j\left(\lVert x-z\rVert_{z}\right),

(4.6)

where
j\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is the univariate function constructed in
Lemma

2.4
.
Then the support of
J
is contained in a Dikin ellipsoid of small radius
2\gamma^{-2/5}
.
In particular, having
2\gamma^{-2/5}<1
suffices to ensure that the support of
J
is contained in
M
.
Then we can use properties of self-concordant functions to compare
L
and
\Delta^{z}
, see
Theorem

4.8
. The multiplication operator
\gamma^{2}J(f-q)J
can be dealt with in a similar fashion as in the previous section, using
Corollary

3.4
.

To argue about the ground state energy and the energy of the first excited state, we will use the Rayleigh–Ritz quotient:

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{H},\psi)\coloneqq\frac{\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}\psi\rangle_{R}}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}}.

Crucially, we know the spectrum of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
because it is a standard quantum harmonic oscillator.
Using IMS-localization we can bound the difference between
\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}\psi\rangle_{R}
and
\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}_{0}\psi\rangle_{E,z}
, see
Theorem

4.9
for a formal statement.
In particular, we arrive at an estimate of the following form: for all
\psi\in\Dom(\mathcal{H})
, we have

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{H},\psi)\geq(1-\poly(n,\gamma^{-1}))\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})-\gap(\mathcal{H}_{0})\mathcal{R}(F,\psi)\right)-o(\gamma),

(4.7)

where
F
is a rank-
1
operator. This gives the desired lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H})
.
To upper bound
\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H})
, we use
\psi=J\psi_{0}
, where
\psi_{0}
is the ground state of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, as a test function.
We show that

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{H},J\psi_{0})\leq\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})+o(\gamma).

For
\gamma\in\Omega((n\log n)^{5})
, combining these estimates gives a lower bound on the spectral gap of
\mathcal{H}
, see
Theorem

4.1
:

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H})-\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H})\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}.

4.5
Locally approximating
\mathcal{H}
by
\mathcal{H}_{0}

Here we show that, locally,
\mathcal{H}
is well-approximated by
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
The main result of this section is
Theorem

4.9
.
We first prove some bounds on the cut-off function
J
from
Eq.

4.6
.
Let
\bar{J}
be such that
J^{2}+\bar{J}^{2}=1
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
J,\bar{J}\in C^{\infty}(M)
as above.
If
2\gamma^{-2/5}\leq 1-1/\sqrt{2}
, then we have, for all
x\in M
,

\lVert dJ\rVert_{R}^{2}(x)\leq C\gamma^{4/5}\quad\text{and}\quad\lVert d\bar{J}\rVert_{R}^{2}(x)\leq C\gamma^{4/5}

for a universal constant
C>0
.

Proof.

Recall from
Eq.

4.3
that
\lVert d\psi\rVert_{R}(x)=\mathrm{sup}_{0\neq u\in T_{x}M}{\lvert d\psi_{x}[u]\rvert}/{\sqrt{g_{x}(u,u)}}
for any
\psi\in C^{\infty}(M)
.
By the chain rule, we have
dJ_{x}(u)=j^{\prime}(r_{z}(x))\,d(r_{z})_{x}(u)
and hence

\displaystyle\lVert dJ\rVert_{R}^{2}(x)=\lvert j^{\prime}(r_{z}(x))\rvert^{2}\cdot\lVert d(r_{z})\rVert_{R}^{2}(x).

Since
j^{\prime}(t)\neq 0
only when
\lvert t\rvert\leq 2\gamma^{-2/5}
, we may restrict to
x\in M
such that
r_{z}(x)\leq 2\gamma^{-2/5}
, hence in particular
1-\lVert x-z\rVert_{z}\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
by the assumption of the lemma.

Using that
r_{z}(x)=\sqrt{\langle x-z,x-z\rangle_{z}}
, we get
d(r_{z})_{x}[u]=\langle x-z,u\rangle_{z}/r_{z}(x)
.
Let
\ell_{x}\colon T_{x}M\to\mathbb{R}
be given by
u\mapsto\langle x-z,u\rangle_{z}
.
Then,
\lVert d(r_{z})\rVert_{R}=\lVert\ell_{x}\rVert_{R}/r_{z}(x)
, where

\displaystyle\lVert\ell_{x}\rVert_{R}^{2}=\mathrm{sup}_{0\neq u\in E}\frac{\lvert\ell_{x}(u)\rvert^{2}}{g_{x}(u,u)}\leq 2\,\mathrm{sup}_{0\neq u\in E}\frac{\lvert\ell_{x}(u)\rvert^{2}}{\langle u,u\rangle_{z}}=2\,\mathrm{sup}_{0\neq u\in E}\frac{\lvert\langle x-z,u\rangle_{z}\rvert^{2}}{\langle u,u\rangle_{z}}=2\,\langle x-z,x-z\rangle_{z}=2r_{z}(x)^{2};

for the inequality we used the self-concordance of
f
(
Eq.

2.2
) and that
1-\lVert x-z\rVert_{z}\geq 1/\sqrt{2}
.
It follows that
\lVert d(r_{z})\rVert_{R}^{2}\leq 2
, and hence

\lVert dJ\rVert_{R}^{2}(x)\leq 2\,\lvert j^{\prime}(r_{z}(x))\rvert^{2}\leq 50\,\gamma^{4/5}
,
because
\lvert j^{\prime}(t)\rvert\leq 5\gamma^{2/5}
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
The same argument applies to
\bar{J}
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

For any self-concordant
f
with
f(z)=0
as minimum,
\mathcal{H}=-L+\gamma^{2}f
,
J
and
\bar{J}
as above, and
\gamma\geq\Omega(1)
, we have

\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}\succeq\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{6/5}{\bar{J}}^{2}.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to the one of
Lemma

3.6
, using instead that
-L\succeq 0
and that the support of
\bar{J}
is contained in the set
\{x:r_{z}(x)\geq\gamma^{-2/5}\}
.
∎

We now restrict to compactly supported functions
\psi
whose support lies within a small Dikin ellipsoid around
z
and show that: (1) we can bound the difference between
\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}
and
\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{E,z}
(
Theorem

4.6
) , and (2) we can bound the difference between
\langle\psi,L\psi\rangle_{R}
and
\langle\psi,\Delta^{z}\psi\rangle_{E,z}
(
Theorem

4.8
).

Theorem 4.6

(Approximating inner products on Dikin ellipsoids)
.

Let
r\in(0,1)
and let
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
be a compactly supported function such that
r_{z}(x)\leq r
for
x\in\supp\psi
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\left((1-r)^{n}-1\right)\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{E,z}\leq\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}-\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{E,z}\leq\left((1-r)^{-n}-1\right)\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{E,z}.

Proof.

Recall that if one chooses linear coordinates
x
with respect to a basis of
E
and considers the metric
g_{x}
as a positive-definite matrix with respect to this basis, then
d\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\sqrt{\det g_{x}}\,dx
, with
dx
the Lebesgue measure in these coordinates.
Since self-concordance is invariant under linear transformations, when
r_{z}(x)=\lVert x-z\rVert_{z}\leq r
, we have

(1-r)^{2}g_{z}\preceq g_{x}\preceq(1-r)^{-2}g_{z}
.
If we work with an orthonormal basis with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{z}
, then
g_{z}=I
.
Then,
\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{E,z}=\int_{M}\lvert\psi(x)\rvert^{2}dx
and

\displaystyle\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}=\int_{M}\lvert\psi\rvert^{2}\,d\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\int_{M}\lvert\psi(x)\rvert^{2}\sqrt{\det g_{x}}\,dx

is between
(1-r)^{n}\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{E,z}
and
(1-r)^{-n}\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{E,z}
because
\psi
is supported only on
x
such that
r_{z}(x)\leq r
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

We remark that in the above theorem we obtain a
(1-r)^{n}
-dependence by using that the operator norm of
g_{z}^{-1/2}g_{x}g_{z}^{-1/2}-I
is upper bounded by roughly
r
. Certain self-concordant barriers satisfy a stronger notion of self-concordance, called
strong self-concordance
, which allows one to bound the Frobenius norm by
r
, resulting in an improved
(1-r)^{\sqrt{n}}
-dependence. This notion was introduced in
[
LLV20
]
in a sampling context; it is known to hold for instance for the standard logarithmic barrier for linear programming as well as the entropic barrier (for general convex sets).

Theorem 4.8

(Approximating
L
by
\Delta^{z}
, on Dikin ellipsoids)
.

Let
r\in(0,1)
and let
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
be a compactly supported function such that
r_{z}(x)\leq r
for
x\in\supp\psi
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\left((1-r)^{n+2}-1\right)\langle\psi,-\Delta^{z}\psi\rangle_{E,z}\leq\langle\psi,-L\psi\rangle_{R}-\langle\psi,-\Delta^{z}\psi\rangle_{E,z}\leq\left((1-r)^{-(n+2)}-1\right)\langle\psi,-\Delta^{z}\psi\rangle_{E,z}

Proof.

As in the preceding proof we work in linear coordinates
x
determined by an orthonormal basis for
E
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{z}
, identify
g_{x}
for
x\in M
with a positive definite matrix, and use that
d\mathrm{vol}_{g}=\sqrt{\det g_{x}}\,dx
.
Then, identifying
T^{*}_{x}M=E
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi,-L\psi\rangle_{R}=\int_{M}g_{x}^{*}(d\psi_{x},d\psi_{x})\sqrt{\det g_{x}}\,dx\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\psi,-\Delta^{z}\psi\rangle_{E,z}=\int_{M}g_{z}^{*}(d\psi_{x},d\psi_{x})\,dx.

(4.8)

Thus it suffices to estimate the bilinear form

\displaystyle g_{x}^{*}\sqrt{\det g_{x}}-g_{z}^{*}

As before, the self-concordance of
f
yields the estimate

(1-r)^{2}g_{z}\preceq g_{x}\preceq(1-r)^{-2}g_{z}

when
r_{z}(x)\leq r
.
Then the same relation holds for
g_{z}^{*}
and
g_{x}^{*}
, see
Eq.

4.2
.
Moreover,
(1-r)^{n}\leq\sqrt{\det g_{x}}\leq(1-r)^{-n}
, using that
g_{z}=I
in the chosen coordinates.
Combing the two estimates, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left((1-r)^{n+2}-1\right)g_{z}^{*}\preceq g_{x}^{*}\sqrt{\det g_{x}}-g_{z}^{*}\preceq\left((1-r)^{-(n+2)}-1\right)g_{z}^{*},

which in view of
Eq.

4.8
implies the statement of theorem.
∎

We finally show that, locally,
\mathcal{H}
is well-approximated by
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.

Theorem 4.9

(Approximating
\mathcal{H}
by
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, on Dikin ellipsoids)
.

Let
r\leq 2\gamma^{-2/5}
and let
\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
be a compactly supported function such that
r_{z}(x)\leq r
for all
x\in\supp\psi
.
Then we can bound

\displaystyle\left((1-r)^{n+2}-1\right)\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}_{0}\psi\rangle_{E,z}-\frac{8}{3}\gamma^{4/5}\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}

\displaystyle\leq\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}\psi\rangle_{R}-\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}_{0}\psi\rangle_{E,z}

\displaystyle\leq\left((1-r)^{-(n+2)}-1\right)\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}_{0}\psi\rangle_{E,z}+\frac{8}{3}\gamma^{4/5}\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}.

Proof.

We have

\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}\psi\rangle_{R}-\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}_{0}\psi\rangle_{E,z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\langle\psi,-L\psi\rangle_{R}-\langle\psi,-\Delta^{z}\psi\rangle_{E,z}\right)+\gamma^{2}\left(\langle\psi,f\psi\rangle_{R}-\langle\psi,q\psi\rangle_{E,z}\right).

We bound the two terms separately. For the first term we use
Theorem

4.8
to obtain the estimates

\left((1-r)^{n+2}-1\right)\langle\psi,-\Delta^{z}\psi\rangle_{E,z}\leq\langle\psi,-L\psi\rangle_{R}-\langle\psi,-\Delta^{z}\psi\rangle_{E,z}\leq\left((1-r)^{-(n+2)}-1\right)\langle\psi,-\Delta^{z}\psi\rangle_{E,z}.

(4.9)

For the second term we further decompose

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}\left(\langle\psi,f\psi\rangle_{R}-\langle\psi,q\psi\rangle_{E,z}\right)

\displaystyle=\langle\psi,\gamma^{2}(f-q)\psi\rangle_{R}+\left(\langle\psi,\gamma^{2}q\psi\rangle_{R}-\langle\psi,\gamma^{2}q\psi\rangle_{E,z}\right).

Corollary

3.4
then shows that

\displaystyle\lvert\langle\psi,\gamma^{2}(f-q)\psi\rangle_{R}\rvert\leq\frac{8}{3}\gamma^{4/5}\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}.

(4.10)

To conclude, observe that we can write the positive definite quadratic function
q(x)=\frac{1}{2}\langle x-z,x-z\rangle_{z}
as a sum of squares,
q=\sum_{j}k_{j}^{2}
.
Applying
Theorem

4.6
to
\gamma k_{j}\psi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
and summing the resulting estimates gives

\left((1-r)^{n}-1\right)\langle\psi,\gamma^{2}q\psi\rangle_{E,z}\leq\langle\psi,\gamma^{2}q\psi\rangle_{R}-\langle\psi,\gamma^{2}q\psi\rangle_{E,z}\leq\left((1-r)^{-n}-1\right)\langle\psi,\gamma^{2}q\psi\rangle_{E,z}.

(4.11)

Now the theorem follows by combining the estimates in
Eqs.

4.9
,
4.11
and
4.10
.
∎

4.6
Proof of the lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(-\frac{1}{2}L+\gamma^{2}f)

Here we prove a more explicit version of
Eq.

4.7
, which we restate here for convenience:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{H},\psi)\coloneqq\frac{\langle\psi,\mathcal{H}\psi\rangle_{R}}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}}\geq(1-\poly(n,\gamma^{-1}))\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})-\gap(\mathcal{H}_{0})\mathcal{R}(F,\psi)\right)-o(\gamma),

where
F
is a rank-
1
operator.

Theorem 4.10
.

There exist universal constants
C,c>0
such that the following holds.
Let
\mathcal{H}=-\frac{1}{2}L+\gamma^{2}f
and let
J
as in
Eq.

4.6
.
Let
\psi_{0}
be the ground state of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
(it is unique up to a scalar), and let
F=\frac{\psi_{0}\langle\psi_{0},\cdot\rangle_{E,z}}{\langle\psi_{0},\psi_{0}\rangle_{E,z}}
.
If
\gamma\geq cn^{5}
, then, for all
\psi\in C^{\infty}(M)\cap\Dom(\mathcal{H})
, we have

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{H},\psi)\geq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})(1-2\gamma^{-2/5})^{2n+2}-\gap(\mathcal{H}_{0})(1-2\gamma^{-2/5})^{2}\mathcal{R}(JFJ,\psi)-C\gamma^{4/5}.

In particular, it holds that

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H})\geq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})(1-2\gamma^{-2/5})^{2n+2}-C\gamma^{4/5}.

Proof.

The IMS localization formula from
Lemma

4.3
states that

\mathcal{H}=J\mathcal{H}J+\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}-\lVert dJ\rVert_{R}^{2}-\lVert d\bar{J}\rVert_{R}^{2}.

Lemma

4.4
establishes a pointwise upper bound on the right-hand side functions:
there exists a universal constant
\tilde{C}>0
such that
\lVert dJ\rVert_{R}^{2}(x),\lVert d\bar{J}\rVert_{R}^{2}(x)\leq\tilde{C}\gamma^{4/5}
for all
x\in M
.
We thus have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{H},\psi)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\langle\psi,J\mathcal{H}J\psi\rangle_{R}+\langle\psi,\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}\psi\rangle_{R}}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}}-2\tilde{C}\gamma^{4/5}.

(4.12)

By
Lemma

4.5
we have
\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}\succeq\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{6/5}\bar{J}^{2}
and thus

\displaystyle\langle\psi,\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}\psi\rangle_{R}\geq\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{6/5}\langle\psi,\bar{J}^{2}\psi\rangle_{R}.

(4.13)

We now lower bound
\langle\psi,J\mathcal{H}J\psi\rangle_{R}
. Let
r=2\gamma^{-2/5}
.
Then
Theorem

4.9
(applied to
J\psi
) implies that

\displaystyle\langle\psi,J\mathcal{H}J\psi\rangle_{R}

\displaystyle\geq(1-r)^{n+2}\langle\psi,J\mathcal{H}_{0}J\psi\rangle_{E,z}-\frac{8}{3}\gamma^{4/5}\langle\psi,J^{2}\psi\rangle_{R}

\displaystyle\geq(1-r)^{n+2}\langle\psi,J\mathcal{H}_{0}J\psi\rangle_{E,z}-\frac{8}{3}\gamma^{4/5},

(4.14)

where we also used that
J^{2}\leq 1
and hence
\langle\psi,J^{2}\psi\rangle_{R}\leq\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}
.
Writing
\gap(\mathcal{H}_{0})=(\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})-\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0}))
, and using that
\psi_{0}
is the unique ground state of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, hence
\mathcal{H}_{0}\succeq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})I-\gap(\mathcal{H}_{0})F
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi,J\mathcal{H}_{0}J\psi\rangle_{E,z}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})\langle\psi,J^{2}\psi\rangle_{E,z}-\gap(\mathcal{H}_{0})\langle\psi,JFJ\psi\rangle_{E,z}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})(1-r)^{n}\langle\psi,J^{2}\psi\rangle_{R}-\frac{\gap(\mathcal{H}_{0})}{(1-r)^{n}}\langle\psi,JFJ\psi\rangle_{R},

(4.15)

where the second inequality uses
Theorem

4.6
(applied to
J\psi
and
FJ\psi
, respectively).
Combining the estimates from
Eqs.

4.12
,
4.13
,
4.14
and
4.15
gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{H},\psi)\geq\frac{\langle\psi,J\mathcal{H}J\psi\rangle_{R}+\langle\psi,\bar{J}\mathcal{H}\bar{J}\psi\rangle_{R}}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}}-2\tilde{C}\gamma^{4/5}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(1-r)^{n+2}\left(\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})(1-r)^{n}\langle\psi,J^{2}\psi\rangle_{R}-\frac{\gap(\mathcal{H}_{0})}{(1-r)^{n}}\langle\psi,JFJ\psi\rangle_{R}\right)+\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{6/5}\langle\psi,\bar{J}^{2}\psi\rangle_{R}}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}}-\left(2\tilde{C}+\frac{8}{3}\right)\gamma^{4/5}.

By our choice
r=2\gamma^{-2/5}
, there exists a universal constant
c>0
such that when
\gamma\geq cn^{5}
, we have

\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{6/5}\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}(n+2)\geq\frac{\gamma}{2}(n+2)(1-2\gamma^{-2/5})^{2n+2}=\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})(1-r)^{2n+2}.

This allows us to combine the terms
\langle\psi,J^{2}\psi\rangle_{R}
and
\langle\psi,\bar{J}^{2}\psi\rangle_{R}
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{H},\psi)\geq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H}_{0})(1-2\gamma^{-2/5})^{2n+2}-\gap(\mathcal{H}_{0})(1-2\gamma^{-2/5})^{2}\frac{\langle\psi,JFJ\psi\rangle_{R}}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{R}}-\left(2\tilde{C}+\frac{8}{3}\right)\gamma^{4/5}.

This proves the lower bound on the Rayleigh–Ritz quotient for
\psi\in C^{\infty}(M)\cap\Dom(\mathcal{H})
. Since the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{H}
are smooth and the perturbation
JFJ
has rank
1
, the lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H})
follows.
∎

4.7
Proof of the upper bound on
\lambda_{0}(-\frac{1}{2}L+\gamma^{2}f)

Here we show that a localization of the ground state of the harmonic approximation
\mathcal{H}_{0}
has energy with respect to
\mathcal{H}
near
\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})
for sufficiently large
\gamma
, giving us a good upper bound on the ground state energy of
\mathcal{H}
.
Because this energy is significantly below
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{H})
, this also implies that the ground state of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
has significant overlap with the ground state of
\mathcal{H}
, see
Corollary

4.12
below.

Theorem 4.11
.

There exist universal constants
c,C>0
such that the following holds.
Let
\psi_{0}
be the ground state of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
.
If
\gamma\geq c(n\log(n))^{5}
, then

\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H})\leq\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{H},J\psi_{0})\leq\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}_{0})(1-2\gamma^{-2/5})^{-2n-2}+C(1-2\gamma^{-2/5})^{-2n-2}\gamma^{4/5}.

Proof.

The first inequality holds by the variational principle, so we only have to prove the upper bound on the Rayleigh–Ritz quotient.
To this end, let
\phi=J\psi_{0}
and note that
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(M)
is a compactly supported function such that
r_{z}(x)\leq r

5
A quantum path-following method

We now show how our non-asymptotic semiclassical analysis can be used in an optimization context. Specifically, our goal is to solve the convex optimization problem
\mathrm{min}_{x\in M}c^{T}x
.
We let
f
be a self-concordant barrier for
M
, and
\{x_{\eta}\}
the central path associated to the barrier
f
and objective
c
(see
Eq.

2.4
for details).

Our quantum path-following algorithm builds on two sequences of Hamiltonians, parametrized by
\gamma>0
and
\eta\geq 0
, defined as

\mathcal{H}^{E}(\eta)\coloneqq-\Delta+\gamma^{2}(\eta c^{T}x+f),

and

\mathcal{H}^{R}(\eta)\coloneqq-L+\gamma^{2}(\eta c^{T}x+f),

with superscripts
E
and
R
referring to the Euclidean and Riemannian manifold setting (following
Sections

3
and
4
); for statements that apply to both, we leave out the superscript.
From our semiclassical analysis, we know that the ground states
{|{\psi_{\eta}}\rangle}
of
\mathcal{H}(\eta)
have large overlap with Gaussians centered at
x_{\eta}
, and we make this more precise in
Section

5.2
.
The idea then is to use a quantum annealing algorithm to trace out a “quantum central path”
\{{|{\psi_{\eta_{0}}}\rangle},{|{\psi_{\eta_{1}}}\rangle},\dots,{|{\psi_{\eta_{T}}}\rangle}\}
, with
\eta_{T}=\vartheta/\varepsilon
so that
{|{\psi_{\eta_{T}}}\rangle}
concentrates at the minimizer of our optimization problem (see
Eq.

2.4
for details).
Our algorithm builds on the following assumptions:

1.

Initial state:
We can prepare a state
{|{\widetilde{\psi}_{\eta_{0}}}\rangle}
that has constant overlap with
{|{\psi_{\eta_{0}}}\rangle}
.

2.

Ground state energy estimates:

For any
\eta
, we have a
\delta
-additive approximation of the ground state energy
\lambda_{0}(\mathcal{H}(\eta))
, with
\delta\in\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\Delta(\mathcal{H}(\eta))/T)
for
\Delta(\mathcal{H}(\eta))
the spectral gap of
\mathcal{H}(\eta)
and
\varepsilon>0
the goal precision.

3.

(Controlled) Hamiltonian simulation:
We need to implement Hamiltonian simulation
e^{i\mathcal{H}(\eta)}
controlled on a continuous-variable register that contains a standard Gaussian state: for any
{|{\phi}\rangle}\in\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{H}(\eta))
and
{|{\psi_{g}}\rangle}=(2\pi)^{-1/4}\int e^{-z^{2}/4}{|{z}\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}z
, this corresponds to the operation

U{|{\phi}\rangle}{|{\psi_{g}}\rangle}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/4}}\int e^{-z^{2}/4}e^{i\mathcal{H}(\eta)z}{|{\phi}\rangle}{|{z}\rangle}\,\mathrm{d}z.

Given that the Gaussian concentrates around the origin, we assume that this is charged at the same cost as that of implementing
e^{i\mathcal{H}(\eta)}
.

Under these assumptions, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

Under assumptions 1.-3., we describe a quantum path-following method that returns with constant probability a state
\varepsilon
-close to
{|{\psi_{\eta}}\rangle}
, at the cost of simulating
\mathcal{H}(\eta)
for total time
T^{E}
and
T^{R}
in the Euclidean and Riemannian settings respectively, where

T^{E}=\poly(n,\vartheta,h^{-}(\varepsilon),h^{+}(\varepsilon),\log(1/\varepsilon)),

where
h^{-}(\varepsilon)=\mathrm{max}_{\eta_{0}\leq\eta\leq\vartheta/\varepsilon}\|g_{x_{\eta}}^{-1/2}\|
and
h^{+}(\varepsilon)=\mathrm{max}_{\eta_{0}\leq\eta\leq\vartheta/\varepsilon}\|g_{x_{\eta}}^{1/2}\|
, and

T^{R}=O\big((n\log(n))^{5/2}\sqrt{\vartheta}\log(\vartheta/\varepsilon)\big).

In particular,
T^{R}
does
not
depend on any condition numbers.

Let us briefly reflect on the assumptions.
The
ground state energy estimates
are used for doing ground-state projection, and such estimates are also required in the usual approach of doing quantum phase estimation on bounded Hamiltonians (see e.g.
[
GTC19
]
).
Moreover, our semiclassical analysis tells us that the ground state energy of
\mathcal{H}(\eta)
is well-approximated by that of its harmonic approximation. The ground state energy of the harmonic approximation is in turn well known:
\frac{\gamma}{2}\Tr[\sqrt{A}]+\gamma^{2}\mathrm{min}_{x}f(x)
. In the Euclidean setting,
A
is the Hessian of
f
at the minimizer. In the Riemannian setting,
A=I
. Estimating the ground state energy can thus be done by estimating the minimum of
f
, and in the Euclidean setting also the Hessian at the minimizer.
Regarding the
Hamiltonian simulation
, our complexity measure is the time for which we need to do controlled-Hamiltonian simulation with respect to
\mathcal{H}
.
In light of the harmonic approximation
\mathcal{H}_{0,\gamma}=-\Delta+\gamma^{2}\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax
, a reasonable assumption would be that the cost of simulating
\mathcal{H}
for one unit of time scales with
\gamma
. Indeed, applying a substitution
x\leftarrow\gamma x
shows that
\mathcal{H}_{0,\gamma}
is equivalent to
\gamma\mathcal{H}_{0,1}
.

5.1
Quantum annealing algorithm

A natural approach to carry out our algorithm would be to use the quantum adiabatic theorem.
It roughly states that if we start in the ground state of
\mathcal{H}(\eta_{0})
and we evolve with
\mathcal{H}(\eta)
for
\eta
slowly going from
\eta_{0}
to
\eta_{T}
, then the system should remain in the instantaneous ground state and hence end up in the target ground state of
\mathcal{H}(\eta)
.
Unfortunately we did not manage to find an appropriate, non-asymptotic version of the adiabatic theorem that applies to our setting of unbounded Hamiltonians (see also the open questions in
Section

1.3
).

To get around this, we use a

(but continous-space!) quantum annealing approach as described by Wocjan and Abeyesinghe
[
WA08
]
, and based on Grover’s fixed-point

-algorithm.
For a sequence of states
\{{|{\psi(\ell)}\rangle}\}
, the algorithm is essentially a sequence of
\pi/3
-rotations

around each of these states, defined by

R(\ell)=I+(\omega-1){|{\psi(\ell)}\rangle}\!{\langle{\psi(\ell)}|},

where
\omega=e^{i\pi/3}
.

Corollary 5.2

(
[
WA08
, Corollary 1]
)
.

Let
{|{\psi(0)}\rangle},\ldots,{|{\psi(T)}\rangle}
be a sequence of states with

. Then, given the state
{|{\psi(0)}\rangle}
, we can prepare a state
{|{\phi}\rangle}
such that

\|{|{\phi}\rangle}-{|{\psi(T)}\rangle}\|\leq\varepsilon

using the unitaries

, at most
O(T\log(T/\varepsilon)/w_{*}^{2})
many times.
13
13

13

One might be able to improve the

dependency to

using ”optimal fixed-point search”

, but in our application we will have

.

Our goal is thus twofold:

1.

Construct a sequence
\eta_{0},\ldots,\eta_{T}=\vartheta/\varepsilon
such that the sequence of Hamiltonians and corresponding ground states

around
{|{\psi_{\eta_{\ell}}}\rangle}
for each
\ell

In

In

5.2
Quantum central path

Here we define a sequence

5.2.1

In

The latter satisfy

let

We can now invoke known estimates on the distance between Gaussian distributions.
The following estimate follows from an explicit expression for the KL-divergence between two normal distributions and Pinsker’s inequality.

Here we describe how to implement a rotation around the ground state

This leads to unwanted aliasing effects, and we therefore cannot follow the same approach as for bounded operators.

Spectra of Schrödinger operators

Definition A.1

(Spectrum)
.

Let
T
be a closed operator on a Hilbert space
H
. Then a complex number
\lambda
belongs to the resolvent set
\rho(T)
of
T
if the operator
T-\lambda I
has a bounded, everywhere on
H
defined, inverse
\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}(T)=(T-\lambda I)^{-1}
. The set
\sigma(T)=\mathbb{C}\setminus\rho(T)
is called the
spectrum
of
T
.

Let
L
be the Laplace–Beltrami operator on a complete Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
, as in
Section

4.2.1
. Let
V\in C^{\infty}(M)
be a potential with
V(x)\to\infty
as
x\to\infty
. Here we give a (short) proof that the spectrum of the Schrödinger operator
\mathcal{H}=-L+V
is discrete, for this we follow the proof of
[
Ura93
]
. For comparison, on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
a similar statement can be found, e.g., in
[
Sch12
, Prop. 12.7]
.

Theorem A.2

(cf.
[
Ura93
, Thm. 1.8]
)
.

Let
L
be the Laplace–Beltrami operator on a complete Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
and let
V\in C^{\infty}(M)
with compact sublevel sets.
Then the Schrödinger operator
\mathcal{H}=-L+V
has a spectrum consisting only of eigenvalues with finite multiplicities.

Proof.

Let
V_{\mathrm{min}}\coloneqq\mathrm{min}_{x\in M}V(x)
, then
-(|V_{\mathrm{min}}|+1)
belongs to the resolvent of
\mathcal{H}
, that is, the operator
\mathcal{H}+|V_{\mathrm{min}}|+1:\Dom(\mathcal{H})\to L^{2}(M)
has a bounded inverse
R=(\mathcal{H}+|V_{\mathrm{min}}|+1)^{-1}
.
The spectrum of
\mathcal{H}
consists only of eigenvalues with finite multiplicities if and only if
R
is a compact operator (cf.
[
Sch12
, Prop. 2.11]
). We thus show that
R
is a compact operator. To that end, let
S
be a bounded subset of
L^{2}(M)
. Let
C>0
be such that
\|f\|_{L^{2}(M)}\leq C
for all
f\in S
. Let
\{f_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence in
S
and define the sequence
\{u_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
via
u_{m}=Rf_{m}
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We show that
\{u_{m}\}
has a convergent subsequence in
L^{2}(M)
.

First note that
u_{m}
is a bounded sequence in
H^{1}(M)
. Indeed, writing
u=u_{m}
, we have

\displaystyle\|u\|_{H^{1}(M)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|du\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}+\|u\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\langle u,Lu\rangle+\|u\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\langle u,(-L+V+\lvert V_{\mathrm{min}}\rvert)u\rangle+\|u\|_{L^{2}(M)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle u,(\mathcal{H}+|V_{\mathrm{min}}|+1)u\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|_{L^{2}(M)}\|(\mathcal{H}+|V_{\mathrm{min}}|+1)u\|_{L^{2}(M)}

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|_{H^{1}(M)}C,

where in the last line we use that
(\mathcal{H}+|V_{\mathrm{min}}|+1)u=f_{m}\in S
.

Now fix a reference point
x_{0}\in M
and let
\{r_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a sequence with the property that if

, then
V(x)\geq k
(here we use that the sublevel sets of
V
are compact). For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
B_{k}=\{x\in M:d(x,x_{0})\leq r_{k}\}
. We may assume without loss of generality that the
B_{k}
are smooth manifolds with boundary; otherwise, we can replace
r_{k}
by a slightly larger

, and avoid critical values of

by appealing to Sard’s theorem
[
Lee12

, which is enough to guarantee smoothness of the sublevel set
[
Lee12
, Prop. 5.47]
.
Let
B_{k}^{\circ}=\{x\in M:d(x,x_{0})<r_{k}\}=B_{k}\setminus\partial B_{k}
.
Then the sequence
\{u_{m}\}
is bounded in

for any

. Then, the Rellich–Kondrachov theorem (

) shows the existence of a subsequence
\{u_{1,m}\}
of
\{u_{m}\}
that converges in
L^{2}(B_{1}^{\circ})
, and a subsequence
\{u_{2,m}\}
of
\{u_{1,m}\}
that converges in

, and so on. We let

for

and we show that

. Fix

and let

be such that

. For any

\displaystyle\int_{M\setminus B_{N}^{\circ}}|u_{m}|^{2}d\mathrm{vol}_{g}

\displaystyle=\int_{M\setminus B_{N}^{\circ}}(V+|V_{\mathrm{min}}|)^{-1}\left\lvert\sqrt{V+|V_{\mathrm{min}}|}\,u_{m}\right\rvert^{2}d\mathrm{vol}_{g}

\displaystyle\leq(N+|V_{\mathrm{min}}|)^{-1}\int_{M\setminus B_{N}^{\circ}}\left\lvert\sqrt{V+|V_{\mathrm{min}}|}\,u_{m}\right\rvert^{2}d\mathrm{vol}_{g}
