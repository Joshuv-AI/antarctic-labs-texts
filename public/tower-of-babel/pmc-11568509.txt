Exploring Death Cafés as a Novel Intervention for Burnout Reduction among Intensive Care Unit Clinicians
Authors: Regis Goulart Rosa
Published: 2024

Burnout, a pervasive issue in modern critical care, is characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment (1). Among intensive care unit (ICU) clinicians, the prevalence of high-level burnout ranges from 33% to 50% in physicians and from 35% to 55% in nurses (2). The impact of burnout among ICU providers is far-reaching, affecting the mental health of professionals, compromising the quality of patient care, jeopardizing patient safety, and increasing healthcare costs, particularly due to high turnover rates among ICU staff (3–5). As we continue to search for effective interventions to mitigate burnout, innovative approaches that address the multifaceted nature of this syndrome are essential. One such approach is the death café, an informal gathering where people discuss death and dying in a relaxed setting to break the taboo around these topics, which are frequent in the ICU context. The rationale for using death cafés to reduce burnout in ICU clinicians is that such discussions can help them process their experiences, reduce existential anxiety, and build a supportive community. This reflective practice can enhance emotional resilience and well-being, potentially mitigating the high levels of stress and burnout common in the ICU environment.

In this issue of the Journal, Bateman and colleagues (pp. 1572–1582) present a multicenter randomized controlled trial conducted from July 2020 to December 2022 with clinicians (physicians, nurses, pharmacists, therapists) from 10 ICUs in Louisiana to assess whether participation in regular death cafés can reduce burnout (6). Participants were randomized to attend four psychotherapist-facilitated virtual death cafés or be part of a control group receiving standard burnout management interventions. The primary outcome was the reduction in burnout levels, measured by the Maslach Burnout Inventory–Human Services Survey at 6 months. Secondary outcomes included measures of anxiety, depression, and qualitative data on stressors, coping, and death café experiences.

The findings of this trial are compelling yet complex. Among the 251 participants (mean age, 31.0 ± 6.8 years; 63% female; 72% White; 37% nurses, 27% residents, 25% interns, 11% other) who completed the 6-month follow-up, there were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups for the primary outcome of burnout (18% vs. 25%; unadjusted odds ratio, 0.64; 95% confidence interval, 0.26–1.57; P = 0.33). Similarly, there were no significant differences in anxiety or depression levels. However, an intriguing finding was the significant improvement in the Maslach Burnout Inventory–Human Services emotional exhaustion subscale for the intervention arm compared with the control at 6 months, though these results were not consistent in longitudinal analysis. Among the 61 participants who attended death cafés and rated their experience, 56 (92%) reported agreeing or strongly agreeing with the statement that death cafés were a valuable experience.

The study faced several limitations, notably an inability to achieve target enrollment and a high attrition rate (46%). These factors may have influenced the results because it is possible that participants with more severe symptoms might have been lost to follow-up. Additionally, the high number of follow-up losses limited the ability to detect significant differences between the groups, contributing to a potential type II error. The transition from in-person to virtual death cafés due to the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic might have diluted the effect of the intervention as well. Virtual settings, although necessary during the pandemic, may not provide the same level of engagement and community building as in-person meetings, potentially impacting attendance and participation rates as a result of phenomena such as virtual meeting fatigue. Given these limitations, the research question regarding the effectiveness of death cafés in reducing burnout among ICU clinicians remains unanswered.

The potential of death cafés as a burnout intervention is undeniable. This approach highlights the importance of addressing the existential dimensions of burnout, which are often overlooked in traditional interventions. By creating a safe space for discussing death, death cafés may offer a unique pathway to emotional healing and resilience. Given the complexity of the intervention, future trials might benefit from using complex intervention frameworks to ensure an appropriate implementation process (7, 8). Within this context, the use of strategies aimed to increase adherence and retention, as well as process evaluation (“what actually happened”), including quantitative (e.g., data measuring successful component delivery) and qualitative methods, might be useful to advance our understanding of the potential of death cafés for reducing burnout among ICU clinicians.

In conclusion, the randomized clinical trial of Bateman and colleagues on death cafés for burnout reduction provides valuable insights into the potential of this unconventional intervention, and further research building on these findings is eagerly anticipated. Although the study did not find significant differences in the occurrence of burnout, it may have been underpowered to detect a clinically important difference. The observed improvement in emotional exhaustion suggests that there may be benefits worthy of further exploration. As we continue to seek effective strategies to combat burnout, integrating death cafés into broader wellness programs in research contexts could represent a significant step forward.
