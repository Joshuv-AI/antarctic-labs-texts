Preoperative and intraoperative risk factors for impaired pulmonary oxygenation after cardiopulmonary bypass: a single-center retrospective analysis
Authors: Tatsuya Horibe, Yohei Uchino, Kenyu Hashimoto, Jun Takaki, Kosuke Nakata, Takafumi Hirota, Hideaki Hidaka, Takashi Yoshinaga, Toshihiro Fukui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative pulmonary dysfunction is a frequent complication after cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Identifying preoperative and intraoperative risk factors for impaired oxygenation is essential for perioperative optimization.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 202 patients who underwent open-heart surgery with CPB at Kumamoto University Hospital between June 2022 and October 2024. Patients were categorized into two groups on the basis of the partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/ Fraction of Inspired Oxygen (FiO₂) ratio (P/F ratio) from arterial blood gas analysis under FiO₂ 1.0 at intensive care unit (ICU) admission: impaired oxygenation (P/F < 300) and normal oxygenation (P/F ≥ 300). Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify risk factors for impaired pulmonary oxygenation.ResultsOf the 202 patients, 53 (26%) developed impaired oxygenation. Multivariate analysis revealed high preoperative body mass index (BMI), a history of hypertension, and an elevated preoperative respiratory index (RI) as independent predictors. The model showed good discrimination (Area Under the Curve (AUC) = 0.746, 95% CI: 0.669–0.823) and calibration.ConclusionsPreoperative RI, BMI, and hypertension are significant predictors of impaired oxygenation after CPB. RI, a readily accessible metric derived from arterial blood gas analysis, may be integrated into routine preoperative assessments to identify high-risk patients and guide tailored lung-protective strategies.Trial registrationThe study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kumamoto University Hospital (Approval No. 3040). The approval date is August 30, 2024.

Background

Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) remains a cornerstone of modern cardiac surgery, enabling complex intracardiac repairs while maintaining systemic perfusion. However, CPB introduces a nonphysiologic environment that predisposes patients to systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), ischemia‒reperfusion injury, and postoperative pulmonary dysfunction [1–3]. These effects are particularly concerning given that over 2 million patients undergo cardiac surgery annually worldwide [4].

Pulmonary complications post-CPB often present as impaired gas exchange, reduced pulmonary compliance, and alveolar collapse. Such dysfunction not only delays extubation and prolongs the intensive care unit (ICU) stay but also has been associated with increased morbidity and healthcare costs [5].

Previous research has suggested that factors such as advanced age, obesity, preexisting pulmonary disease, duration of CPB, and transfusion volume may influence pulmonary outcomes [1, 6]. Several studies have explored risk factors associated with postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs), and the focus has often been on endpoints such as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), pneumonia, or ventilator dependence rather than quantifiable oxygenation indices. A standardized predictive model specific to CPB-induced gas exchange impairment has not been established.

With respect to preoperative pulmonary function assessment, several risk prediction scores for postoperative respiratory complications have been proposed, primarily for patient populations undergoing noncardiac surgery [7, 8]. Currently, comprehensive risk assessments for postoperative lung injury specific to open-heart surgery are lacking. And spirometry is considered high-risk and is generally not performed on patients before open-heart surgery, including those with aortic aneurysms, severe valvular disease, or ischemic heart disease, at our institution.

This study aims to address this gap by evaluating preoperative and intraoperative predictors of impaired oxygenation—defined by a partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2)/ fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) ratio (P/F ratio) < 300 at ICU admission—in patients undergoing cardiac surgery with CPB. Particular attention is given to the respiratory index (RI), a physiologically meaningful parameter calculated from arterial blood gases, to determine its prognostic value.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study included 361 adult patients who underwent open-heart surgery under CPB at Kumamoto University Hospital between June 2022 and October 2024. Of these, 202 patients met the inclusion criteria and were selected in the analysis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: cases of aortic surgery requiring intraoperative hypothermia (body temperature below 28 °C) and circulatory arrest (123 cases), cases of minimally invasive cardiac surgery with separate lung ventilation (15 cases), cases of ventricular septal perforation and cases requiring annular repair such as mitral annular calcification requiring intentional deep sedation and intubation for 3–5 days after surgery to prevent left ventricular rupture after surgery (12 cases), cases with a P/F ratio of less than 200 before surgery that resulted in moderate or severe oxygenation impairment and were considered to have abnormal measurement values ​​at the time of blood gas sampling (5 cases) [9], and cases with missing data (4 cases).

Definition of impaired oxygenation

Impaired pulmonary oxygenation was defined via the Berlin criteria for ARDS as a P/F ratio < 300 on arterial blood gas analysis at ICU admission under FiO₂ 1.0 [9–11]. Patients were categorized into two groups: the impaired oxygenation group (n = 53) and the normal oxygenation group (n = 149).

Data collection and statistical analysis

Clinical data, including patient demographics, comorbidities, preoperative blood gas data, intraoperative variables, and postoperative outcomes, were extracted from the electronic medical records. Approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Kumamoto University Hospital.

Respiratory parameters were calculated as follows:

P/F ratio = PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio.

Alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient (AaDO₂) = (713 × FiO₂) - (carbon dioxide (PaCO2)/0.8) - PaO₂.

Respiratory Index (RI) = AaDO₂/PaO₂ [12].

Continuous variables were compared via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and categorical variables were compared via Fisher’s exact test. Variables with a p value ≤ 0.1, as determined by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Fisher’s exact test, were included in the multivariable analysis. Multicollinearity was assessed via Pearson’s correlation coefficient; one of any two highly correlated variables (|r| ≥ 0.7) was retained.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed using the selected candidate risk factors. The final model was constructed through backward stepwise elimination. To evaluate the predictive performance of the final multivariable logistic regression model, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was constructed using the predicted probabilities derived from the model. The optimal cutoff point for the ROC curve was determined using Youden’s Index. Model calibration was then assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with a p-value > 0.05 indicating good fit. All the statistical analyses were performed via SPSS 29 J for Mac (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). All p values were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Anesthesia and surgical technique

Standard anesthesia for cardiac surgery includes induction with midazolam (30–100 mcg/kg), fentanyl (2–7 mcg/kg), and rocuronium (0.6–0.9 mg/kg). A continuous venous infusion of propofol (3 mg/kg/h) and rocuronium bolus (10–20 mg at 0.5–1 h) was used to maintain anesthesia during surgery. Fentanyl (30 µg/kg) was added before sternotomy. Our standard protocol for CPB was as follows: (1) perfusion index of 2.6 L/min/m2 and mean blood pressure of 40–80 mmHg; (2) rectal temperature of 34.0 °C; (3) PaO2 of 200–400 mmHg, PaCO2 of 35–45 mmHg, and pH of 7.35–7.45, base excess of -8–0; (4) Na of 135–145 mEq/L, K of 3.0–5.0 mEq/L, Ca ≧ 1.0 mEq/L; (5) Hb of 8.0–10.0 g/dL, Hct of 25–30%; and (6) SaO2 of 100%, SvO2 ≧ 70%. All surgeries were performed via median sternotomy. At our institution, ascending aortic cannulation is the preferred method. In cases of significant ascending aortic calcification or re-do sternotomy, alternative cannulation sites such as the subclavian or femoral artery are used. Cardioplegia was initially achieved in all patients via antegrade and retrograde blood cardioplegia. Cardioplegia was administered antegrade or retrograde at intervals of 25–30 min. Warm blood cardioplegia was induced immediately before the aorta was unclamped.

Ventilator setting, blood gas analyses, and extubation criteria

Mechanical ventilation was initiated immediately after anesthesia induction. The volume-control mode was used with the following settings: tidal volume, 7–10 mL/kg; respiration rate, 8–14/min; and FiO2, 0.4–0.8. During cardiopulmonary bypass, the ventilator was stopped, and only 1 L/min of oxygen circulated. After weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass, the ventilator was restarted with the same settings as before, and the patient was managed. Blood gas samples were routinely taken immediately after anesthesia induction, when the activated clotting time (ACT) was measured, when the patient was weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass. and when the patient entered the ICU, blood gases were collected 5–10 min after admission to the ICU, and mechanical ventilation with FiO2 1.0 was initiated. Therefore, the blood gas samples at these three points were used for the analysis. After admission to the ICU, the patient was managed via volume-controlled ventilation (VCV), with an FiO2 of 1.0, a positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5–10, and a volume control (VC) of 10–20. Our hospital’s ICU was semiclosed, and the settings were changed appropriately on the basis of blood gas data at the time of admission. Patients were extubated when they met the following criteria: (1) were awake and able to respond to commands with no increased intracranial pressure; (2) had stable hemodynamics and a low risk of myocardial ischemia; (3) had a sufficient cough reflex and minimal airway secretions; and (4) had spontaneous breathing trials under pressure support ≤ 5 cm H2O and an end expiratory pressure ≤ 5 cm H2O.

Results

Among the 202 patients included, 53 (26.2%) developed impaired oxygenation postoperatively (P/F ratio < 300), whereas the remaining 149 (73.8%) maintained adequate oxygenation at ICU admission (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of the study cohort

Patients in the impaired oxygenation group had a significantly greater body mass index (BMI), more frequent history of hypertension, and elevated preoperative RI values (Table 1). Other preoperative variables, such as age, sex, and history of smoking, did not differ significantly between the groups. The impaired oxygenation group included a greater proportion of patients who required alternative cannulation strategies (e.g., femoral or axillary artery) and cannulation of the right atrium; had a longer operative time; increased intraoperative red blood cell and platelet transfusion volumes; increased blood loss; and increased intraoperative fluid volume (Table 2). Patients with impaired oxygenation required significantly longer mechanical ventilation. However, there was no statistically significant difference in overall in-hospital mortality or the incidence of postoperative pneumonia between the groups (Table 3).

Discussion

Pulmonary dysfunction remains one of the most frequent and clinically relevant complications of cardiac surgery via CPB. It manifests as atelectasis, reduced lung volume, ventilation‒perfusion (V/Q) mismatch, intrapulmonary shunting, and, in severe cases, refractory hypoxemia or ARDS. These disturbances are attributable to multiple pathophysiologic insults, including SIRS, ischemia‒reperfusion injury, cessation of pulmonary blood flow during CPB, and disruption of normal pulmonary mechanics due to general anesthesia [1–3]. Pulmonary dysfunction have been strongly linked to increased length of ICU stay, delayed extubation, and elevated perioperative mortality [5]. A study comparing postoperative lung damage in on-pump coronary artery bypass grafting and off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting reported that this cohort demonstrated superior gas exchange and earlier extubation despite increased intraoperative fluid administration in the off-pump group [12]. Additional studies have also indicated a higher pulmonary shunt rate with on-pump coronary artery bypass grafting [13, 14]. These findings suggest a plausible correlation between CPB and impaired lung function.

In this context, the identification of preoperative risk factors for impaired postoperative oxygenation is of paramount importance for tailoring perioperative care. In our study, multivariate analysis revealed that a high preoperative RI, increased BMI, and history of hypertension were independent predictors of postoperative oxygenation impairment. These findings are noteworthy because they rely on simple, reproducible, and clinically accessible parameters before the onset of surgery.

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines a BMI of 25 or more as overweight, and 30 or more as obesity. Obesity is a well-established risk factor for pulmonary dysfunction across surgical disciplines, including cardiothoracic surgery [15, 16]. The mechanisms are multifactorial: elevated BMI is associated with decreased lung compliance, diminished functional residual capacity, reduced expiratory reserve volume, and increased airway resistance. These mechanical changes predispose obese patients to perioperative atelectasis, which is exacerbated during CPB by temporary cessation of mechanical ventilation to ensure visibility in the surgical field. Obesity is characterized by a chronic low-grade systemic inflammatory state that can amplify CPB-induced cytokine storms and worsen pulmonary endothelial dysfunction [17–19]. Our findings further validated the deleterious effects of elevated BMI on postoperative pulmonary function and emphasized the importance of preoperative optimization, including nutritional and respiratory therapy, in obese patients.

WHO standards generally define high blood pressure as a systolic blood pressure of 140mmHg or higher, or a diastolic blood pressure of 90mmHg or higher. Hypertension, although not traditionally considered a primary risk factor for pulmonary dysfunction, emerged as an independent contributor to impaired oxygenation in our analysis. Chronic hypertension leads to increased systemic vascular resistance and arterial stiffness, which may compromise pulmonary microcirculatory perfusion during CPB [20, 21]. Moreover, patients with hypertension frequently exhibit left ventricular diastolic dysfunction and elevated left atrial pressure, which can lead to subclinical pulmonary venous congestion and reduced alveolar‒capillary gas exchange efficiency [22, 23]. This effect may be further magnified in the perioperative setting by fluid shift, hemodynamic instability, and systemic inflammation.

The most novel and clinically significant observation of our study was the predictive power of the preoperative RI, calculated as AaDO₂/PaO₂ under FiO₂ = 1.0. The RI reflects both the alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient and the efficiency of oxygen uptake. As such, it incorporates the effects of V/Q mismatch, intrapulmonary shunt, and diffusion impairment, which are the critical pathophysiological components of postoperative hypoxemia. Unlike spirometric evaluations, which require patient cooperation and may not be feasible in urgent cases or high-risk populations, the RI can be rapidly obtained from arterial blood gas analysis and offers a real-time snapshot of pulmonary gas exchange efficiency. Because the RI value varies greatly depending on FiO2, quantitative evaluation is impossible unless FiO2 is set to a constant value; however, in this study, the RI was set at FiO2 1.0. During the perioperative period, FiO2 can be easily set via a ventilator, and an FiO2 of 1.0 is often used, especially during anesthesia induction, making the clinical application of this method easy. Research has seldom explored the RI as a preoperative predictive tool, with most studies focusing on postoperative metrics such as the P/F ratio or the occurrence of pneumonia or ARDS [10].

Although we observed a statistically significant prolongation of extubation time in patients with impaired oxygenation, other clinical outcomes, such as ICU stay or the incidence of major complications, were not significantly different. This may be explained by the relatively low severity of oxygenation impairment in our cohort and the generally optimized surgical environment at our institution. Additionally, known predictors, such as prior pneumonia, transfusion volume, and operative duration, did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to sample size limitations [1, 5]. Nevertheless, the extended duration of mechanical ventilation represents an important burden on both patients and healthcare resources.

Given the identification of the RI, BMI, and hypertension status as modifiable or readily assessable risk factors, our findings have practical implications for the implementation of individualized perioperative lung protection strategies. Recent evidence supports several interventions that may improve oxygenation and reduce pulmonary dysfunction, especially in high-risk patients [24–26]. Lung-protective ventilation strategies employing low tidal volumes (6–8 mL/kg of predicted body weight) and minimum PEEP of 5 cmH₂O during surgery and in the ICU have been shown to mitigate ventilator-induced lung injury [24, 25]. It has been reported that the continuation of mechanical ventilation during cardiopulmonary bypass may contribute to improved postoperative oxygenation [26]. Following cardiopulmonary bypass, carefully applied recruitment maneuvers can reopen atelectatic lung regions while minimizing the risk of barotrauma [25]. In addition, goal-directed fluid management is essential, especially in patients with obesity or hypertension, given their predisposition to pulmonary congestion [27].

By stratifying patients on the basis of the preoperative RI and comorbid conditions, clinicians may be able to implement these strategies aggressively and selectively, potentially reducing the incidence and severity of pulmonary complications.

This study also provides the groundwork for several important areas of future investigation. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate the RI as a routine preoperative risk assessment tool and to determine its utility across various cardiac procedures. Integrating RIs into existing perioperative risk models, such as the EuroSCORE II, may increase their predictive accuracy for pulmonary outcomes. Furthermore, interventional trials that apply targeted lung-protective measures to patients with high preoperative RIs could establish causality and clarify the optimal management protocols. Additional research combining the RI with imaging (e.g., lung ultrasound or computed tomography) or biomarkers (e.g., surfactant proteins and inflammatory cytokines) may allow more precise phenotyping of at-risk patients. Finally, emerging technologies, such as machine learning-based prediction tools, may further refine individualized perioperative strategies in the era of precision medicine.

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted as a single-center retrospective analysis, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Although institutional standards for intraoperative anesthesia management were followed, variations in individual anesthesiologists’ practices could have influenced intraoperative parameters and postoperative outcomes. Future multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate these results and minimize center- or practitioner-related variability.

Based on the findings of both previous studies and the current research, several factors are anticipated to act as potential confounders. For instance, intraoperative blood transfusion and fluid overload have been reported to contribute to postoperative oxygenation impairment and prolonged extubation time through mechanisms such as transfusion-related acute lung injury or pulmonary congestion [28, 29]. Although our multivariate analysis did not reveal a significant difference, both factors showed a higher tendency in the group with oxygenation impairment. Pulmonary hypertension is physiologically a strong candidate for a preoperative risk factor for oxygenation impairment, as it is associated with hypoxemia via ventilation/perfusion mismatch, reduced diffusion capacity, and low cardiac output [30]. While no significant difference was found in our study, the prevalence was higher in the group that experienced oxygenation impairment. Interestingly, the group of patients diagnosed with dyslipidemia by an internist and taking HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors like statins showed a significant difference in our analysis. While no previous reports directly link dyslipidemia to oxygenation impairment, it may contribute to impaired oxygen supply, primarily through enhanced systemic inflammation [31, 32]. Alternatively, although the correlation between dyslipidemia and BMI was not high in our study, we hypothesize that dyslipidemia could lead to obesity and higher BMI, consequently causing oxygenation impairment. Finally, no prior reports have established a difference in postoperative oxygenation impairment based on the cannulation site. The primary difference between ascending aortic and femoral arterial cannulation is antegrade versus retrograde perfusion. Femoral arterial cannulation, in particular, may lead to insufficient oxygen supply to the upper body, potentially causing pulmonary ischemia-reperfusion injury. However, no significant difference was found in our study’s multivariate analysis. While all of the aforementioned factors could act as confounders, the limited sample size of our study prevented sufficient verification, which we acknowledge as a significant limitation.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified a high preoperative RI, elevated BMI, and history of hypertension as independent predictors of impaired oxygenation following open-heart surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass. The RI represents a readily obtainable, noninvasive, and physiologically meaningful index that may offer a valuable addition to preoperative pulmonary risk assessment, especially in patients for whom traditional spirometry is not feasible.

Our model demonstrated moderate discrimination ability and may be useful as a clinical decision support tool. Preoperative identification of at-risk patients can enable targeted implementation of lung-protective strategies, fluid optimization, and ultimately improving perioperative outcomes and resource utilization.

Prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate the utility of the RI and its incorporation into comprehensive perioperative risk models for cardiac surgery patients.
