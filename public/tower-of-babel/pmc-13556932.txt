Near-peer education by medical students and its association with HPV vaccination knowledge and attitudes among Japanese adolescents: An exploratory intervention study
Authors: Kanon Nakajima, Asami Yagi, Kokoro Taira, Runa Otsubo, Riki Wakugawa, Hidesane Arai, Kae Yahagi, Yohei Fujino, Takagui (Kawakami) Hanaka, Tatsuki Ishimoto, Natsume Okamoto, Erika Yukimitsu, Rintaro Kiyota, Yutaka Ueda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination is a key preventive measure against cervical cancer. In Japan, proactive recommendations for HPV vaccination were suspended from 2013 to 2022, resulting in persistently low coverage. We explored the post-intervention knowledge, attitudes, and short -term self-reported HPV vaccination behavior following a near-peer educational intervention by medical students. Between February 2022 and March 2024, medical students conducted 1-hour sessions covering HPV transmission, cervical cancer prevention, and vaccine efficacy. The sessions incorporated quizzes, discussions, and workshops in which high school students created role-play scenarios or social media messages to promote HPV vaccination. A follow-up survey was administered 1–2 weeks after the sessions to assess vaccination uptake, knowledge, and self-reported explanatory ability. Of 864 junior and senior high school students who participated in the sessions, 332 were included in the followed-up analysis. Of these, 248 were unvaccinated before the session: 134 females and 114 males. Within two weeks, 10 female (7.5%) and one male (0.9%) reported receiving HPV vaccination. Students with positive vaccination intentions demonstrated higher comprehension scores than those without such intentions. Voluntary participants had higher knowledge scores than whole-class attendees. Subjective and objective comprehension scores were positively correlated, although there were some discrepancies. Vaccinated respondents were more likely than unvaccinated respondents to correctly identify HPV-preventable cancers. Near-peer outreach sessions delivered by medical students may represent a useful approach to enhancing adolescents’ understanding of HPV vaccination. Leveraging the near-peer position of medical students may be a promising approach to address vaccine hesitancy in Japan.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV)-related cancers, particularly cervical cancer, represent a global public health concern.1,2 HPV is primarily transmitted through sexual contact and infects both women and men.3,4 Among more than 200 identified HPV types, approximately 15 are classified as high-risk types that cause cancer, including HPV types 16 and 18, which are most strongly associated with cervical cancer.5 Cervical cancer carries the highest disease burden among HPV-related cancers,6 while in men, oropharyngeal cancer represents a major burden. HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer is reportedly increasing in incidence, and it has become the most common HPV-related malignancy.7 Among HPV-related cancers, cervical cancer is unique in that there are well-established prevention strategies, including primary prevention through HPV vaccination and secondary prevention through cervical cancer screening.8 In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer as a Public Health Problem.9

In Japan, HPV vaccination was introduced nationwide in 2010 and incorporated into the national routine immunization program for girls aged 12–16 y in April 2013, in response to the rising incidence of cervical cancer and low screening coverage.10–12 However, soon after its implementation, media reports of various adverse events led to the suspension of its proactive recommendation for routine HPV vaccination in June 2013, although HPV vaccination remained part of the national routine immunization program.13 Subsequent evaluations by international regulatory agencies and systematic reviews found no evidence of a causal association between HPV vaccination and postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome or complex regional pain syndrome, and no overall increase in serious adverse events, supporting the safety profile of HPV vaccines.14–17 Despite these reassuring safety assessments, the proactive recommendation remained suspended in Japan, and public concern persisted. The prolonged suspension of the proactive recommendation and extensive media coverage may have contributed to the decline in HPV vaccination coverage in Japan to below 1%, among the lowest levels worldwide.18,19 This raised concerns about a potential rise in future incidence and mortality.20

In 2021, a group of motivated Japanese medical students established ‘Vcan’, a student-led initiative aimed at promoting HPV vaccination.21 By leveraging their unique position as intermediaries between healthcare professionals and the public, the medical students involved in Vcan seek to provide accurate and accessible information through outreach activities in schools and local communities and they conduct surveys related to the HPV vaccine.22 Previous HPV education interventions in Japan have included programs targeting health science teachers and community-based interventions in middle schools.23,24 As near-peer educators, medical students may combine health knowledge with greater age and social proximity to adolescents than senior healthcare professionals, which may facilitate communication and questions about sensitive health topics.25 Outreach classes as part of the Vcan initiative, delivered by medical students using physician-supervised educational materials and targeting junior and senior high school students, represent a novel approach within Japan.

This study aimed to explore students’ post-intervention knowledge, attitudes, and short-term self-reported HPV vaccination behavior following a near-pear educational intervention. The findings may inform future controlled studies evaluating whether peer-proximal education can be a viable strategy for reducing vaccine hesitancy among adolescents in Japan.

Methods

Study design, setting and participant

We conducted a single-group, posttest-only exploratory study between February 2022 and March 2024 across 14 junior (ages 12–15) and senior high schools (ages 15–18) in Japan (Figure 1). These comprised three junior high schools and one high school in Yamagata Prefecture, one junior high school and one high school in Tokyo, one high school in Gifu Prefecture, two high schools in Shiga Prefecture, one junior high school in Kyoto Prefecture, one junior high school and two high schools in Tottori Prefecture, and one junior high school in Hiroshima Prefecture. Study design.

Eligible participants were students enrolled in any grade of the participating junior or senior high schools (approximately 12–18 y old) who attended participation in the outreach session. Both female and male students were eligible, and no additional exclusion criteria were applied to participation in the outreach session. Eight of the 14 schools implemented the program for entire classes, while six schools conducted the program for students who voluntarily chose to participate.

In Japan, routine HPV vaccination is currently recommended for girls from the sixth grade of elementary school through the first year of senior high school (approximately 11–16 y old). Furthermore, a catch-up vaccination program was implemented between April 2022 and March 2025 for those who had missed their opportunity for vaccination. This study also therefore included second- and third-year senior high school students (16–18 y old) as eligible participants. Although the national routine HPV vaccination program targets only girls, male students were included because HPV-related health issues are also relevant to males. In addition, Nanyō City in Yamagata Prefecture, where the local government provides financial support for HPV vaccination among boys.

The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Graduate School of Medicine, Osaka University (Approval No. 14361) on October 8, 2021. The principal investigator was affiliated with Osaka University at the time the study was reviewed and approved; the current affiliation differs due to a subsequent institutional transfer. Participation in the questionnaire survey was voluntary. For participants younger than 16 y, written informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian before participation, and completion of the anonymous questionnaire by the student was regarded as assent to participate. Participants aged 16 y or older provided electronic informed consent by indicating their agreement before beginning the questionnaire. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Intervention

This study implemented a two-component educational intervention targeting junior high and senior high school students. The content and implementation plan of this study were discussed with the representative teachers of each participating school, and their final approval was obtained prior to implementation. Of the typical 60-minute session, the first 30 minutes consisted of a lecture delivered by one to three healthcare students using slide presentations, covering HPV transmission, its relationship to cervical cancer, and the efficacy of the HPV vaccine. The slide presentation was reviewed and validated by a board-certified gynecologic specialist. Rather than relying solely on didactic instruction, the sessions incorporated interactive elements such as quizzes and frequent opportunities for questions to enhance participant engagement. The remaining 30 minutes were allocated to a workshop. In some schools, only 45 minutes were available, so a truncated version comprising a 25-minute lecture and a 20-minute workshop was conducted. In the workshop component, students worked in groups of four to six to engage in activities such as role-playing how to communicate HPV vaccination to their parents and creating mock social media posts aimed at sharing accurate information with peers. Each group exchanged ideas with adjacent teams, fostering active engagement and reinforcing their understanding of the material.

The second part of the intervention was conducted one to two weeks after the classroom session. Participants completed a follow-up six-item questionnaire and a four-item test to assess their comprehension of HPV vaccination that required approximately 5 to 10 minutes to complete. These were administered via Google Forms and distributed to each class by homeroom teachers, along with a written request for participation. Responses were collected within seven days after the second part of the session. In four of the 14 participating schools, paper-based questionnaires were used instead; the researchers manually transcribed the students’ responses into the dataset. The questionnaire items were identical across both formats.

Data collection

The questionnaire and knowledge test were developed specifically for this exploratory study and were not previously validated psychometric instruments. The items were designed to assess distinct aspects of HPV vaccination, including vaccination history and intention, attitudes, self-reported explanatory ability, and knowledge related to the educational content.

The post-intervention survey questionnaire and knowledge test were distributed to 864 students who participated in the single outreach session. The questionnaire items included school grade, sex, school name, HPV vaccination history including intention, and two attitudinal questions. HPV vaccination history, including vaccination intention, was assessed using a single question that simultaneously captured participants’ vaccination history and post-intervention vaccination intention (Supplementary Table 1). The two attitudinal items were used to assess the perceived impact of the lecture and students’ subjective level of understanding: Did the Vcan lecture influence your decision to receive (or not receive) the HPV vaccine?’ Response options: ‘’Had an impact/‘Had no impact/‘Other. Part of the investigation was conducted in Nanyō City (three junior high schools, one senior high school). We were able to receive data from Nanyō City on the monthly number of first-dose recipients for routine vaccinations in financial years (Fys) 2022 and 2023, as well as the monthly number of first-immunization recipients for catch up vaccinations in FY2022 and FY2023.

After excluding ten responses with insufficient questionnaire data for analysis, 332 individuals were included in the analysis. Some respondents had missing responses for individual questionnaire items. These items were treated as missing values, and analyses were based on available responses.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for the demographic variables respondents answered on the follow-up questionnaire. The outcomes of interest included the perceived influence of the outreach classes on HPV vaccination decision-making, HPV vaccination intention, understanding of HPV vaccines, and vaccination behavior.

A single questionnaire item captured both participants’ vaccination history and their post-intervention vaccination intention. Each response option was classified according to vaccination status (vaccinated vs. unvaccinated) and vaccination intention (positive vs. not positive toward vaccination), as shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Students’ comprehension scores were calculated from the four knowledge questions. Scores of 100%, 75%, 50%, 25%, and 0% corresponded to four, three, two, one, and no correct answers, respectively. Missing responses to the knowledge questions were treated as incorrect answers. For other questionnaire items, missing responses were treated as missing values, and analyses were based on available responses.

Monthly HPV vaccination uptake was also examined using anonymized aggregate data provided by Nanyō City. We compared the number of first-dose routine vaccinations and catch-up vaccinations in fiscal years 2022 and 2023 before and after the January lecture.

Fisher’s exact test was used to assess differences between two groups. Chi-square tests were used to evaluate differences among three or more groups. For categorical variables showing statistically significant differences (p < .05), standardized residual analysis was performed to identify cells contributing to the overall significance. Cells with standardized residuals exceeding ±1.96 were considered significant. The significance level was set at p = .05.

Comprehension scores were compared according to participants’ responses to whether the lecture influenced their vaccination intention. The percentage of students who achieved 100% comprehension was significantly higher among those who were influenced by the class than among those who were not. When stratified by type of class participation, in sessions attended by self-selected volunteers, scores were: 0% in three students (3.3%), 25% in 19 students (20.7%), 50% in 34 students (37.0%), 75% in 30 students (32.6%), and 100% in six students (6.5%). In sessions delivered to entire classes (or year groups), scores were: 0% in 27 students (11.3%), 25% in 61 students (25.4%), 50% in 96 students (40.0%), 75% in 48 students (20.0%), and 100% in eight students (3.3%).

Comprehension scores were also examined across levels of self-reported ability to explain the reasons for receiving or not receiving the HPV vaccine to family or friends, allowing comparison between subjective understanding and objective comprehension. Students who answered that they could explain confidently to the question ‘Can you explain your reasons for receiving or not receiving the HPV vaccine to your family or friends? showed a significantly higher percentage reaching 100% understanding. Meanwhile, students who answered ‘cannot explain at all’ showed a significantly higher percentage with 0% understanding. No association was observed between class comprehension and whether students received the HPV vaccine within 1 to 2 weeks after the lecture.

Discussion

This study primarily explored the students’ post-intervention knowledge, attitudes, and short-term self-reported vaccination behavior following a near-peer HPV vaccine outreach class. Using a post-intervention questionnaire, we assessed students’ knowledge and attitudes toward HPV vaccination. A small proportion of students (4.4%, 11/248) reported receiving the HPV vaccine after attending the outreach class. Although causality cannot be inferred due to the absence of a control group and the brief follow-up period, the observed vaccination behavior cannot be attributed to the outreach session.

Regarding sex differences in vaccination behavior, uptake among male students was notably lower than among female students. This likely reflects the limited availability of subsidized or free HPV vaccination for boys during the study period (February 2022 to March 2024), when HPV vaccination for boys was not included in the national routine immunization program and financial support was available only through some local municipalities.26 Reduced financial accessibility may have contributed to the lower vaccination rates among male adolescents.27 These findings highlight the importance of expanding financial support to promote HPV vaccination among male students.

A variety of educational approaches have been examined in previous studies, including sessions delivered to health science teachers,28 community-based programs involving students and parents,29 and interventions implemented within established school-based vaccination systems.30 Other research has explored the use of printed materials or social media as channels for disseminating HPV-related information.31 These findings collectively suggest that multiple modalities can support HPV vaccine education among adolescents.

In our study, the students that expressed a positive intention to be vaccinated demonstrated higher comprehension scores. This association is consistent with previous studies reporting that HPV-related knowledge is associated with vaccination acceptance or intention among adolescents.32 However, reverse causality is also plausible: students who were already positively inclined toward vaccination may have been more attentive during the session, resulting in higher scores. Similarly, students who voluntarily attended the outreach class had higher comprehension scores than those who participated as part of an entire class or year. This finding suggests that preexisting interest or motivation may influence engagement with near-peer health education and should be considered when interpreting educational outcomes.

Subjective comprehension, which is the self-reported ability to explain HPV vaccination to others, showed a general positive correlation with objective comprehension as measured by quiz scores. Nevertheless, several students who reported high confidence achieved scores of only 25% or 0%, indicating a notable discrepancy between perceived and actual knowledge. In our sample, this discrepancy indicates that self-reported understanding did not always correspond with objective knowledge scores.

Among unvaccinated respondents (n = 248), 51.6% answered incorrectly regarding the cancers preventable by the HPV vaccine, compared with 34.5% of vaccinated respondents (n = 84), a statistically significant difference (Tables 4 and 5). Even after attending the session, unvaccinated respondents demonstrated lower levels of understanding, which suggests that limited knowledge may be associated with lower likelihood of vaccination. This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting associations between HPV-related knowledge and vaccination acceptance or intention among adolescents.32 Moreover, among respondents without positive vaccination intention (n = 202), 31.2% selected ‘cervical cancer only,’ compared with 45.4% in the positive intention group (n = 130). Insufficient understanding of the vaccine’s broader preventive benefits may therefore be linked to lower vaccination intention and warrant further investigation.

In Nanyō City, the outreach tour for junior and senior high schools may have been a contributing factor in encouraging vaccination. However, the observed short-term increase in vaccination uptake occurred alongside several other public health initiatives, such as remarks by the mayor promoting HPV vaccination during coming-of-age and high school entrance ceremonies, personalized notices offering subsidized vaccination to male residents, and renewed recommendations for unvaccinated second-year junior high school students. These simultaneous efforts make it difficult to attribute changes in vaccination uptake solely to the outreach sessions.

A key strength of this study is that the outreach sessions were delivered by medical students. With formal training in virology and public health, medical students generally demonstrate higher levels of knowledge about cervical cancer and HPV vaccination than their non-medical peers, and their vaccination coverage tends to be higher than that of the general young adult population.30,31 Previous school-based peer education interventions have reported benefits particularly in health-related knowledge, although evidence regarding longer-term behavioral effects is less consistent.33 Medical student-led sexual health education in secondary schools has also been associated with improved health-related knowledge among adolescents.34 As they are well positioned between expert physicians and the public, medical students may serve as useful intermediaries, communicating accurate, evidence-based information in a manner that is accessible to adolescents.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-group, posttest-only study without baseline measurements or a comparison group. Therefore, the observed knowledge, attitudes, comprehension, and self-reported vaccination behavior cannot be attributed to the outreach session, and changes resulting from the intervention could not be assessed. Second, the interval between the outreach session and the follow-up survey was short (only one or two weeks) due to logistical constraints. Third, despite the number of students who completed the survey, a high proportion of invalid responses was observed, raising the possibility of misclassification and perhaps reducing the robustness of the results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, outreach sessions delivered by medical students may have potential value as a near-peer approach to HPV vaccine education among adolescents. By combining peer relatability with accurate medical knowledge, medical students are well positioned to help address vaccine hesitancy among adolescents. Further controlled research incorporating baseline assessments, comparison groups, and longer follow-up periods is needed to clarify the extent to which such near-peer educational programs can influence vaccination behavior.
