Fertility-Sparing Management in Ovarian Cancer: A Systematic Review
Authors: Iqbal Anugrah Fitriyanto, Siti Salima, Wiza Iswanti, Jihan Nur Azizah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFertility-sparing management (FSM) has emerged as an important consideration in young women with ovarian cancer, particularly as survival outcomes improve and preservation of reproductive potential becomes a key quality-of-life priority. However, the oncologic safety and reproductive efficacy of FSM remain incompletely defined. We conducted a systematic review in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines (PROSPERO ID: CRD420261368106) to evaluate oncologic and reproductive outcomes following FSM in ovarian tumors. PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, and Cochrane CENTRAL were searched for studies published between January 1995 and September 2025. Eligible studies included women undergoing FSM with reported oncologic and/or reproductive outcomes. Of 464 records identified, 56 studies involving approximately 7000 participants across multiple countries met the inclusion criteria. Included populations encompassed borderline ovarian tumors (BOT), epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), malignant ovarian germ cell tumors (MOGCT), and sex cord-stromal tumors (SCST), predominantly at early-stage disease. Recurrence rates ranged from 5% to 76% in BOT, 5% to 20% in early-stage EOC, and were generally below 10% in MOGCT and SCST. Five-year disease-free survival ranged from 80% to 95% in BOT and early-stage EOC and exceeded 85–95% in MOGCT and SCST, while overall survival exceeded 90% in BOT and 85–95% in early-stage EOC. Pregnancy rates ranged from 25% to 88%, with live birth rates between 20% and 80%. Most pregnancies occurred spontaneously, although assisted reproductive technologies improved reproductive success without increasing recurrence risk. Fertility-sparing management appears to be oncologically safe in carefully selected patients while preserving meaningful reproductive potential. Despite heterogeneity across studies, future research should focus on prospective designs with standardized outcome reporting and longer follow-up to optimize patient selection and management strategies.

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is the eighth most common malignancy in women and remains the leading cause of gynecologic cancer-related mortality worldwide.1 Prognosis largely depends on the stage at diagnosis, with early detection associated with significantly improved survival outcomes.2 Approximately 12–15% of ovarian cancer cases occur in women younger than 40 years, many of whom have not yet completed childbearing.3 A SEER registry–based study reported that 5-year survival was approximately 89% for FIGO Stage I, 71% for Stage II, but dropped substantially to 41% and 20% for Stage III and IV, respectively, underscoring the dramatic decline in prognosis with advancing stage.4 This epidemiological trend highlights the need to consider fertility preservation as an integral component of treatment planning for young patients.

Conventional management of ovarian cancer has traditionally involved radical surgery, including bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and hysterectomy, which effectively eliminates reproductive potential.5 However, with improvements in surgical techniques and adjuvant therapies, survival in early-stage ovarian cancer has markedly improved.6,7 Consequently, quality-of-life concerns, particularly regarding fertility preservation, have gained importance in oncologic care.8 Fertility-sparing management (FSM), encompassing conservative surgery and adjunctive oncofertility strategies, has emerged as a promising approach for selected patients with borderline ovarian tumors (BOT), early-stage epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC), and non-epithelial malignancies.9,10

Several retrospective studies, multicenter trials, and meta-analyses suggest that FSM can provide oncologic outcomes comparable to radical surgery in appropriately selected early-stage disease while allowing subsequent pregnancies.11–13 Advances in assisted reproductive technologies (ART), including in vitro fertilization (IVF), oocyte or embryo cryopreservation, ovarian tissue banking, and the use of gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists during chemotherapy, have further expanded reproductive options for survivors.14–16 Nonetheless, important concerns persist regarding recurrence risks in high-grade histologies, incomplete staging procedures, and the long-term gonadotoxic effects of chemotherapy.17,18 Although fertility-sparing management is widely accepted for carefully selected patients with FIGO stage IA grade 1–2 epithelial ovarian cancer, its role in patients with stage IC or grade 3 disease remains controversial because of concerns regarding recurrence risk and long-term oncologic safety. In addition, the oncologic implications of incomplete surgical staging continue to be debated.2,19 Heterogeneity of reported outcomes and lack of consensus across guidelines further complicate patient selection and clinical decision-making.20

Given these uncertainties, a comprehensive synthesis of available evidence is warranted to clarify the oncologic safety and reproductive efficacy of FSM in ovarian cancer. Therefore, this systematic review aims to synthesize current evidence regarding the oncologic safety and reproductive efficacy of FSM by evaluating oncologic and reproductive outcomes, identifying prognostic factors influencing these outcomes, and highlighting current evidence gaps to inform clinical practice and future research in oncofertility.

Materials and Methods

Protocol and Registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.21 No institutional review board approval was required, as only previously published, de-identified data were analyzed. All authors contributed to the development of the search strategy and the definition of inclusion and exclusion criteria. The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration ID: CRD420261368106).

Research Question

The primary research question was “What are the oncologic and reproductive outcomes of FSM in women with ovarian cancer?”. Secondary research questions included “Which prognostic factors (stage, histology, grade, completeness of staging, adjuvant therapy) influence outcomes following FSM?”, “What is the role of ART in improving reproductive success after FSM?”. Literature Search

A comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Scopus, and Cochrane CENTRAL databases from January 1995 to September 2025. Boolean operators were applied using the following search string: (“ovarian neoplasms” OR “ovarian cancer” OR “epithelial ovarian carcinoma” OR “borderline ovarian tumor” OR “germ cell tumor”) AND (“fertility-sparing surgery” OR “fertility preservation” OR “conservative surgery” OR “fertility-sparing management”) AND (“oncologic outcomes” OR “survival” OR “recurrence” OR “reproductive outcomes” OR “pregnancy” OR “live birth”). Reference lists of relevant articles and reviews were hand-searched to identify additional eligible studies.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

All studies retrieved from the search were independently screened by two reviewers. Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were assessed against predefined inclusion criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers using a standardized form. Extracted data included study characteristics (author, year, country, design, sample size), patient characteristics (age, histology, stage, grade), details of fertility-sparing management and completeness of staging, use of adjuvant therapy, oncologic outcomes (recurrence, disease-free survival, overall survival), reproductive outcomes (pregnancy, live birth, ART use), and duration of follow-up. Risk of Bias and Quality Assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using validated tools according to study design. The risk of bias in non-randomized studies was evaluated using the ROBINS-I tool.23 Cohort studies were appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), assessing selection, comparability, and outcome domains, and visualized using the NOS-TLPlot tool.24 Randomized controlled trials were assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool.25 The results are presented in Figures 3–5. Due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the included studies, a formal meta-analysis was not performed, and the findings were synthesized qualitatively. Results

Study Selection

A total of 464 records were identified through database searches. After removing 37 duplicates, 427 records were screened by title and abstract, of which 229 were excluded due to irrelevance. A total of 198 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 142 studies were excluded for the following reasons: case reports or small case series (n = 26), review articles and meta-analyses (n = 22), non-relevant population (n = 27), non-fertility-sparing management interventions (n = 22), non-English full-text articles (n = 8), and insufficient data for analysis (n = 6). Finally, 56 studies were included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis.

Study Characteristics

A total of 56 studies involving approximately 7,000 patients were included, with sample sizes ranging from 13 to 660 participants. The majority of studies were retrospective cohort designs, with a smaller proportion of prospective and multicenter cohorts. Study populations consisted of women of reproductive age with ovarian tumors undergoing fertility-sparing surgery, including BOT, EOC, MOGCT, and SCST. Most patients presented with early-stage disease, although several studies included advanced-stage cohorts. Fertility-sparing approaches varied across studies and included cystectomy, unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, bilateral cystectomy, and combinations with surgical staging procedures. Follow-up duration ranged widely from approximately 26 months to over 15 years. Reproductive Outcomes

Reproductive outcomes following FSS were consistently favorable across studies, with pregnancy rates ranging from approximately 25% to over 80% among women attempting conception.26–30,32–36,41,43,44,56,59–61,63,67,74–76,78,82 High pregnancy rates were reported in several cohorts, particularly when analysis was restricted to patients actively attempting pregnancy, where success rates frequently exceeded 70–80%.27,29,57 Long-term data demonstrated cumulative pregnancy rates approaching 85% in selected populations.50 Live birth rates were similarly high, generally exceeding 40–80% of pregnancies, with some studies reporting near-universal term delivery among those who conceived.33,35,45,50,58,68,76,77,79 Obstetric outcomes were favorable, with the majority resulting in term deliveries and low rates of obstetric complications.35,42,69

Most pregnancies occurred spontaneously, indicating preserved ovarian function after FSS. The use of assisted reproductive technologies (ART), including IVF and ovulation induction, was reported in a minority of patients, generally ranging from 5% to 30% depending on the cohort.34,39,41 Importantly, ART use was not associated with increased recurrence risk or worse oncologic outcomes.32 Additionally, pregnancy after FSS did not negatively impact survival and may even be associated with reduced recurrence risk in some studies.64

Several factors were identified as influencing reproductive success. Younger age, particularly ≤35 years, was consistently associated with higher pregnancy and live birth rates, whereas increasing age significantly reduced fertility potential35,38,59 The number of surgical interventions also played a critical role, with repeated surgeries negatively impacting fertility outcomes.50 Additionally, underlying gynecological conditions, such as endometrial pathology and endometriosis, were associated with poorer reproductive outcomes despite ovarian preservation.51

Surgical technique also influenced fertility outcomes. While more conservative procedures such as cystectomy preserved more ovarian tissue and were associated with higher pregnancy rates, they carried a higher recurrence risk, highlighting a trade-off between oncologic safety and fertility preservation.69,82 Overall, FSS enables meaningful preservation of reproductive potential, particularly in early-stage disease, with a high likelihood of spontaneous conception and favorable obstetric outcomes.

Oncologic Outcomes

Across the included studies, recurrence rates following FSS showed wide variability, ranging from approximately 3–10% in early-stage disease to over 70% in selected high-risk or advanced-stage cohorts.30,36,38,45 Comparative studies consistently demonstrated higher recurrence rates in patients undergoing FSS compared with radical surgery, particularly in advanced-stage BOT and bilateral disease.27,44,66 However, most recurrences were borderline, non-invasive, and predominantly confined to the remaining ovary, allowing for effective salvage surgery and repeat fertility-preserving procedures.35,41,49

In malignant ovarian germ cell tumors, oncologic outcomes were particularly favorable, with 5-year OS exceeding 90–95% across studies.57,65,81 Recurrence rates were generally low (approximately 5–15%) and, when present, were often highly responsive to chemotherapy or salvage surgery.55,65 Similarly, in early-stage epithelial ovarian cancer, studies consistently reported excellent survival outcomes, with no significant survival disadvantage associated with FSS compared with radical surgery.43,45,63

Key prognostic factors associated with recurrence and worse DFS included advanced FIGO stage, invasive peritoneal implants, micropapillary patterns, tumor grade, bilateral ovarian involvement, elevated CA-125 levels, and incomplete cytoreduction.33,34,43,44,57 Histology-specific differences were also observed, with mucinous and clear-cell subtypes demonstrating distinct recurrence patterns and prognosis.69,70 Importantly, surgical approach influenced recurrence risk, with cystectomy-based procedures consistently associated with higher recurrence compared to unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy.60,66,69

Summary of Findings

Overall, FSM in young women with early-stage ovarian cancer demonstrated oncologic safety comparable to radical surgery in low-risk disease, while preserving meaningful chances of pregnancy and live birth. Recurrence risks remain higher in high-grade tumors and incomplete staging cases, underscoring the importance of strict patient selection. Integration of ART and fertility preservation techniques further enhances reproductive outcomes, providing a balanced approach between survival and quality of life.

Discussion

This systematic review demonstrates that FSM is oncologically safe in carefully selected women with early-stage ovarian cancer, particularly those with Stage IA–IC, Grade 1–2 epithelial ovarian EOC, BOT, and germ cell tumors. Across the included studies, overall survival (OS) consistently remained high, frequently exceeding 90–95%, with no significant differences between FSM and radical surgery in most comparative analyses.27,45,63,77 These findings are consistent with previous reviews and meta-analyses, which report comparable survival outcomes when comprehensive staging protocols are followed.2,5–7 In these cohorts, five-year overall survival frequently exceeded 90%, and recurrences, when present, were often salvageable.1,8–10,82 These data support the notion that, in appropriately selected cases, FSM does not compromise oncologic safety.

From a reproductive perspective, our synthesis confirms that FSM enables meaningful reproductive potential. Across included studies, pregnancy rates varied widely, ranging from approximately 20% to over 80% among women attempting conception, with live birth rates commonly exceeding 60% of achieved pregnancies.29,42,50,54 These outcomes highlight the importance of fertility preservation strategies as integral to survivorship care. The growing role of assisted reproductive technologies (ART), including in vitro fertilization (IVF), oocyte and embryo cryopreservation, and ovarian tissue banking, has further expanded opportunities for conception among survivors.14–16,83 Importantly, the use of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists during chemotherapy may mitigate gonadotoxic effects and preserve ovarian reserve.18

Despite these encouraging outcomes, several controversies remain. FSM is associated with increased recurrence risk in patients with high-grade histologies, advanced-stage disease, or when surgical staging is incomplete.17,33,44,84 This emphasizes the need for careful patient selection and adherence to staging protocols. Moreover, surgical technique influences outcomes, with cystectomy-based approaches consistently associated with higher recurrence rates compared to unilateral salpingo-oophorectomy.60,66,69 Among the major multicenter collaborative studies, the GINECO–TMRG OPTIBOT study demonstrated favorable oncologic and reproductive outcomes following fertility-sparing surgery in patients with FIGO stage I borderline ovarian tumors. In contrast, the MITO14 study evaluated a higher-risk population with FIGO stage II–III serous borderline ovarian tumors and extra-ovarian invasive implants, reporting higher recurrence rates despite acceptable long-term survival outcomes.36,41

Clinically, FSM should be considered within a multidisciplinary framework involving gynecologic oncologists, reproductive endocrinologists, and fertility counselors. Early referral to fertility specialists and integration of oncofertility services are crucial to optimize reproductive outcomes. Shared decision-making is essential to balance oncologic safety with reproductive goals, particularly given the psychosocial impact of infertility in young women with cancer.

Future research should focus on prospective, multicenter studies with standardized reporting of both oncologic and reproductive endpoints. The development of risk-stratification models integrating molecular markers, tumor biology, and fertility parameters may improve patient selection. Long-term follow-up is also needed to evaluate the impact of ART and fertility preservation interventions on survival and recurrence.

Strengths and Limitations

This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of current evidence on fertility-sparing management (FSM) in ovarian tumors, encompassing a large number of studies with diverse histologic subtypes and long-term follow-up. The inclusion of both oncologic and reproductive outcomes offers a clinically relevant perspective for oncofertility decision-making.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Most included studies were retrospective and heterogeneous in design, patient selection, surgical approach, completeness of surgical staging, outcome reporting, and follow-up duration, which may introduce bias. Differences in the definitions of recurrence further limited direct comparison across studies and precluded formal quantitative meta-analysis. In addition, important confounding factors, including disease stage, treatment selection, ethnicity, family history, and genetic predisposition, were not consistently reported or controlled across the included studies. These factors may have influenced the reported oncologic and reproductive outcomes and should be considered when interpreting and generalizing the findings.

Conclusion

This systematic review confirms that FSM is a safe and effective strategy for carefully selected young women with early-stage ovarian cancer. Oncologic outcomes, particularly overall and disease-free survival, are comparable to radical surgery in low-risk patients, while meaningful reproductive potential can be preserved. Pregnancy and live birth rates following FSM are encouraging, and the integration of ART further enhances reproductive success. Based on the available evidence, FSM should be considered a standard treatment option for carefully selected patients with borderline ovarian tumors, malignant ovarian germ cell tumors, and FIGO stage IA grade 1–2 epithelial ovarian cancer, whereas caution is warranted in patients with high-grade histology or more advanced-stage epithelial ovarian cancer.
