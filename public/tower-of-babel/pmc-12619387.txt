Factors predicting holistic nursing competence in a sample of nurses caring for older adults
Authors: Yasin Jafari Rahim Abad, Kiyana Saadati, Fatemeh Ghaffari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe significant growth of the older adult population globally, including in Iran, has further underscored the need for comprehensive, effective, and competency-based nursing care. Holistic Nursing Competence (HNC) is a multidimensional concept. In the care of older adults, it is influenced by ethical, attitudinal, individual, and occupational factors.ObjectiveThe objective of this study was to identify the predictors of HNC among nurses providing care to older adults in Iran.MethodsThis was a descriptive study with a correlational design, conducted from 20 July 2024 to 15 January 2025 in the west of Mazandaran Province, Iran. A total of 784 clinical nurses were enrolled via convenience sampling. Data were collected using the Holistic Nursing Competence Scale (HNCS), the Ethical Attitude Scale in Nursing Care (EASNC), the Ethical Care Competence Scale (ECCS), an ageism questionnaire, and a demographic and occupational information questionnaire. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and multiple linear regression with SPSS software version 26.ResultsThe mean HNC score (96.96 ± 30.28) was in the upper range of the moderate category, indicating a moderately high level. The results of multiple regression analysis (stepwise method) indicated that ethical care competence (β = 0.62, large effect, p < 0.001), ethical attitude for nursing care (β = 0.11, medium effect, p < 0.001), history of in-service training in holistic nursing empowerment (β = 0.07, small effect, p = 0.002), and ageist attitudes (β = 0.07, small effect, p = 0.014) were significant predictors of HNC. Among these, ethical care competence was identified as the strongest predictor of HNC in the care of older adults. Furthermore, correlation analyses revealed that contextual variables such as age, work experience, job satisfaction, employment type, and place of work were also significantly correlated with HNC, underscoring the importance of considering individual, professional, and organizational dimensions in promoting holistic care.ConclusionsThe findings of this study indicate that HNC in the care of older adults is influenced by ethical competencies, attitudes towards aging, professional training, and work-related environmental factors. Therefore, developing structured in-service training programs, promoting ethical conduct in nursing, and reducing ageist attitudes are considered effective strategies for enhancing HNC. The results of this research can inform policies in nursing education and human resource development.

Background

The older adult population is markedly increasing worldwide, with projections indicating a rise from 962 million in 2017 to over 1.2 billion by 2050 [1].In Iran, this group is also expected to constitute a significant portion of the population [2], increasing the demand for high-quality nursing care [3].

Older adults are the largest consumers of health services due to chronic conditions and require comprehensive care [4]. Geriatric nursing aims to address their physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs [5, 6], as part of a holistic and person-centered approach [7]. This approach not only enhances quality of care and patient satisfaction [8], but also contributes to reduced hospital costs and improved clinical outcomes [9, 10].

Nurses, as the frontline healthcare providers [11], face challenges such as staff shortages and increased workload that hinder the delivery of comprehensive care [12]. Although advances in health technology can potentially support care provision [13], the effectiveness of such interventions depends on nurses’ holistic competence to integrate these tools meaningfully into care practices [14]. Holistic Nursing Competence (HNC) is defined as the ability to address the multidimensional needs of patients and is linked to improved care quality, nurse satisfaction, and reduced turnover [13, 15, 16]. According to Takase & Teraoka, it includes professional development, collaboration, communication, and care management abilities [17]. Yet many nurses lack this competence due to limited experience or inadequate training, often reducing care to physical needs while overlooking psychological, social, and spiritual aspects [18–20]. In-service training based on evidence and needs assessments can play a key role in strengthening HNC by improving nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and skills [21]. However, barriers such as time constraints, motivation, resource limitations, and organizational challenges impede this process [22–24]. Individual factors such as age and work experience also influence competence levels [25–27], but there is growing evidence that ethical constructs and attitudinal dimensions specifically, Ethical Attitude in Nursing Care (EANC), Ethical Caring Competence (ECC), and ageist attitudes are also important predictors of HNC [28–30].

EANC promotes patient dignity and improves interpersonal relationships in care [28], while ECC enables nurses to navigate ethical dilemmas effectively [31].]. Supportive ethical environments have been shown to enhance ECC and care outcomes [30]. In contrast, ageist attitudes rooted in negative stereotypes lead to reduced motivation for providing comprehensive care to older adults, limiting care to physical aspects and resulting in dissatisfaction and lower care quality [32–35]. In Iran, the complexity of clinical environments and advancements in medical technologies have increased expectations of nurses, compounding workload and raising risks of burnout especially during crises [36–38]. These pressures threaten the acquisition of core competencies such as HNC, particularly in caring for older adults [17, 19]. Although several studies have examined clinical competence in Iran [39–42], there is a lack of focused research on HNC and its predictors, especially in geriatric settings.

From a theoretical standpoint, holistic nursing is underpinned by multiple frameworks that emphasize integrated, multidimensional care. Watson’s Theory of Human Caring [43], Rogers’ science of unitary human beings [44, 45], Newman’s theory of expanding consciousness [46], and Parse’s Human Becoming Theory [47] collectively assert that patients must be approached as whole beings whose physical, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions are interdependent. These theories form the conceptual foundation for HNC, which requires nurses to develop the ability to perceive and respond to patients’ complex human experiences. Additionally, Benner’s Novice to Expert Model [29]. Illustrates how clinical expertise including ethical and relational competence evolves through experiential learning, forming a pathway for acquiring HNC.

EANC and ECC also derive from established theoretical models. EANC represents the internalization of ethical principles that promote human dignity in care, influencing the nurse’s ability to provide holistic services. ECC, as described in Fry and Johnstone’s model [48], encompasses ethical awareness, judgment, and behavior skills essential for navigating the complex ethical landscapes inherent in holistic care. Furthermore, ageism theory explains how negative stereotypes about aging can hinder comprehensive care delivery, especially when psychosocial needs of older adults are over looked due to implicit bias [49].

In this study, the selection of ECC, EANC, and ageist attitudes as predictors of HNC was grounded in an integrated theoretical framework. Drawing from Benner’s Novice to Expert Model, Watson’s Theory of Human Caring, and Ageism Theory, we conceptualized HNC as a multidimensional construct encompassing technical proficiency, ethical reflection, and attitudinal readiness. These three predictors represent core domains skills, values, and biases that interactively shape the nurse’s ability to provide holistic care, particularly to older adults. Their integration enables a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying competent and compassionate care delivery.

These theoretical underpinnings guided the conceptualization of this study, which aims to investigate the predictors of HNC in the care of older adults, particularly focusing on ethical and attitudinal variables often neglected in previous research. Given the growing aging population and the importance of holistic care, identifying the predictors of HNC is essential for developing targeted training and policy interventions. Therefore, this study was designed to explore the key factors influencing HNC in the care of older adults in Iran. This research seeks to bridge the gap by providing scientific evidence for future competency-based improvements in nursing practice.

Methods

Design

This was a descriptive study with a correlational design, conducted from 20 July 2024 to 15 January 2025 in the west of Mazandaran Province, Iran. The study population comprised all nurses working in hospitals affiliated with Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences.

Samples

Sampling was conducted using a convenience sampling method. Although convenience sampling may limit the generalizability of the findings, it was chosen due to practical constraints in accessing a random sample across multiple hospitals.

To minimize sampling bias, efforts were made to recruit nurses from diverse clinical wards and hospitals in different regions, increasing the heterogeneity and representativeness of the sample. Inclusion criteria were: having at least one year of experience providing healthcare services to older adult patients and at least one year of employment as a clinical nurse. The exclusion criterion was the incomplete completion of research instruments. The minimum sample size obtained from this calculation was then increased by approximately 10% to account for potential nonresponse or incomplete instruments, resulting in a final target of 784 participants. A total of 7 hospitals affiliated with Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences and located in the western region of Mazandaran province were included in the study. The number of nurses recruited from each hospital was proportionate to the total number of eligible nurses working in that hospital, with recruitment numbers approximately as follows: ~25 participants in small hospitals (~ 50 eligible nurses), 60–70 in medium hospitals (~ 120 eligible nurses), and 170–180 in large hospitals (~ 300 eligible nurses). This proportional allocation ensured representation reflective of hospital size.

Formal permissions were obtained in advance from hospital administrations and ward supervisors. The research team visited all participating hospitals in person during all work shifts (morning, evening, night) to capture diverse ward contexts and ensure access to all eligible nurses according to the inclusion criteria. At each site, the researcher coordinated with head nurses, introduced the study objectives and procedures both verbally and via a written information sheet, and invited nurses to participate voluntarily. Confidentiality and anonymity were assured, and written informed consent was obtained before instruments completion. Participants were offered flexibility to complete the instruments at convenient times, including outside working hours, and were allowed to do so in multiple sessions if needed. Given that the study involved multiple and relatively lengthy instruments, several measures were implemented to minimize respondent burden and maintain data quality, such as advance notification of the estimated completion time, providing a quiet and interruption-free environment whenever possible, and allowing rest breaks. To minimize bias arising from researcher contact with participants during face-to-face interactions, all instructions were standardized, and the researcher refrained from offering any interpretative assistance while participants completed the instruments.

Following this, a total of 800 instruments were distributed, of which 784 were fully completed and included in the final analysis, yielding a response rate of approximately 98%. Minimal item-level missing data (< 2% of all responses) were addressed using pairwise deletion during statistical analysis. Examination of missing-data patterns revealed no systematic trends, thereby supporting the assumption that data were missing completely at random (MCAR), a prerequisite for the appropriate application of this method. Pairwise deletion was chosen to maximize the use of available data and to avoid unnecessary loss of statistical power, in line with methodological recommendations for low-level MCAR missingness [50]. Given the low level of missingness, this approach was deemed suitable; however, as noted in the limitations, the exclusion of incomplete forms may still pose a potential risk of bias. During the eight-month data-collection period, completed instruments were periodically reviewed for consistency and completeness. To minimize bias related to researcher-participant contact during face-to-face administration, standardized instructions were provided, and the researcher refrained from offering any interpretative assistance while participants completed the instruments. Participation was entirely voluntary, and instruments were typically completed after work shifts or during break times and returned directly to the researcher.

Measurement

All instruments, except the Ageism Questionnaire and the Demographic and Occupational Information Questionnaire, were used in Persian versions that had not been previously published. These Persian versions were translated and culturally adapted for the first time in the present study by the research team, following the six‑stage cross‑cultural adaptation process described by Beaton et al. [51]. This process included forward translation, backward translation, review by an expert committee, pre‑testing with the target population, and evaluation of both qualitative and quantitative face and content validity. The psychometric properties of the final Persian versions were examined and confirmed before initiating the main study. Internal consistency reliabilities (Cronbach’s α) were assessed, and stability was established via a two‑week test–retest procedure with a sample of 30 nurses from teaching hospitals affiliated with Mazandaran University of Medical Sciences. The following instruments were used for data collection:

Demographic and Occupational Information Questionnaire: This instrument included variables such as age, gender, work experience, work shift, job satisfaction, income adequacy, financial status, educational level, history of in-service training in holistic nursing empowerment, level of professional autonomy in clinical decision-making, ward of employment, marital status, and employment status.Holistic Nursing Competence Scale (HNCS): This instrument was developed by Takase et al. [17]. It consists of 36 items and has two parts. 1- A subscale named “General aptitude” which assesses items related to general behaviors of nurses. Scoring for this part is on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = never to 7 = always. 2- Subscales including “Staff education and management”, “Ethically-oriented practice”, “Nursing care in a team” and “Professional development”. Scoring for these subscales is on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = cannot do at all to 7 = can do excellently. The final score of the scale is obtained by summing the scores of all subscales. A higher score indicates higher competence among nurses. The reliability of this instrument (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.967) was reported by Takase et al. [17]. The reliability of this scale in the present study was 0.975. The HNCS has a theoretical range of 36–193 and, as stated by its original developers, no predetermined cut-off point or categorical interpretation. The reliability of this scale in the present study was 0.957.Ethical Attitude Scale For Nursing Care (EASNC): This instrument was developed by Özçiftçi [53] . It contains 34 items and originally has one factor. Scoring is on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 5 = strongly agree to 1 = strongly disagree. The score range is from 34 to 170. A higher score indicates a more positive ethical attitude in nursing care. The overall reliability of this instrument (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.96) has been reported [53]. In the present study, after examining the psychometric properties of this instrument, the results of an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) led to the identification of four factors: “Responsive Human-Centered Ethics”, “Humanistic Care-Oriented Ethics”, “Principled Ethical Responsibility” and “Empowering Professional Ethics”. Statistical analyses

To examine the relationships of Ethical Caring Competence (ECC), ageism, and Ethical Attitude for Nursing Care (EANC) with nurses’ Holistic Nursing Competence (HNC) in caring for older adult patients, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was applied. Assumptions of normality for correlation analyses were evaluated using skewness and kurtosis indices. Additionally, associations between demographic variables and HNC were analyzed using parametric tests, including the independent t-test, one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), and Pearson’s correlation. Predictive variables were identified through stepwise multiple linear regression. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26. Prior to regression modelling, the fundamental assumptions of multiple regression were examined. First, univariate normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis values; according to Kline’s (2023) criteria, all variables met the acceptable range (skewness < 3, kurtosis < 10), indicating no deviation from normality [55]. Second, error independence was verified by the Durbin–Watson statistic (2.25), which fell within the acceptable range of 1.5–2.5. Third, multicollinearity was evaluated using tolerance coefficients and Variance Inflation Factors (VIF). Tolerance values ranged from 0.42 to 0.93 and VIF values from 1.08 to 2.35, well within conservative limits (Tolerance >0.10, VIF < 5; maximum acceptable VIF = 10), indicating the absence of problematic collinearity [56]. Finally, inspection of the residuals scatterplot confirmed homoscedasticity. Given that all assumptions were satisfied, the application of stepwise multiple regression was considered appropriate.

Table 2 presents the descriptive statistics of the study’s key variables. The total score of HNC among participants was 96.96 ± 30.28. The Holistic Nursing Competence Scale (HNCS) has a theoretical range of 36–193 and no predetermined cut-off point. In this study, the mean score was slightly above the scale’s midpoint (114.5) and located in the upper half of the possible range, which consistent with the classification approach in previous studies using HNCS was interpreted as representing a moderately high level of competence. Among HNC subscales, “Staff Education and Management” scored the highest (27.90 ± 9.76), indicating that participants were relatively more confident in educational and managerial roles. Conversely, “Professional Development” received the lowest mean score (10.88 ± 4.30), suggesting potential gaps in continuous learning or career advancement engagement among nurses. Regarding ECC, the total score was 46.48 ± 11.78, reflecting a moderate to high ethical competency level. Notably, nurses scored highest on “Acting while thinking about how to provide better care” (18.54 ± 4.96), while scoring lowest on “Acting to learn what better care is” (6.46 ± 2.01), potentially indicating a greater focus on real-time ethical action than reflective learning. For EASNC, the total score was 60.55 ± 15.04, representing generally positive ethical attitudes. “Empowering Professional Ethics” emerged as the most endorsed dimension (23.05 ± 6.28), while “Principled Ethical Responsibility” had the lowest mean (10.32 ± 3.22), which may reflect challenges in ethical consistency or accountability under clinical pressures. Discussion

This study aimed to identify predictors of Holistic Nursing Competence (HNC) in the care of older adult patients. The results revealed that participating nurses demonstrated a moderately high level of holistic competence, which may reflect systemic investments in competency-based education and collaborative nursing models in Iran [17, 26, 57]. This relatively strong baseline enables a more focused evaluation of specific areas of strength and weakness.

Among the six HNC dimensions, “Staff Education and Management” received the highest score, reflecting robust capabilities in leadership, supervision, and conflict resolution—skills aligned with the administrative responsibilities nurses often assume in Iran’s hierarchical healthcare system. This pattern corresponds with findings from other structured systems where nurses’ competence tends to be more pronounced in team coordination than in individualized development [13, 16, 22, 58]. Conversely, the lowest score appeared in “Professional Development”, suggesting institutional limitations in fostering continuous learning, research engagement, and academic progression. This finding aligns with regional trends identified in studies such as Girma et al. [59], which employed similar regression methodologies and highlighted how lower job satisfaction and aging contribute to reduced professional growth. These results underscore the importance of integrating ongoing training opportunities and professional support mechanisms into nursing workforce strategies to ensure sustained development of HNC.

The present study identified ECC, EANC, and ageist attitudes as key predictors of HNC among nurses caring for older adults. While ECC and EANC were positively associated with HNC, ageism also showed a paradoxical positive correlation an unexpected yet illuminating finding.

ECC scores revealed moderate levels overall, with nurses performing best in the dimension of “Acting while thinking about how to provide better care”, but weakest in “Acting to learn what better care is.” This suggests that while reflective thinking is present, it lacks integration with structured learning—echoing the findings of Katayama et al. [52] and Mohajer et al. [60], who stressed the importance of experiential and guided ethical training [52, 60]. These findings suggest that while Iranian nurses may engage in intuitive reflection, they lack structured pathways to translate reflection into updated evidence-based care, likely due to insufficient clinical training opportunities or mentorship. This gap underscores the need to move beyond abstract ethical awareness toward experiential, reflective, and evidence-integrated ethical training [57, 61].

The findings also reveal a relatively high mean score for EANC, especially in “Empowering Professional Ethics”, suggesting that Iranian nurses conceptualize ethics as a proactive, professional asset. However, the lowest score in “Principled Ethical Responsibility” may reflect systemic barriers such as role ambiguity or hierarchical pressures that hinder ethical action despite positive beliefs [28, 48, 62]. Similar discrepancies have been observed internationally, particularly in settings with rigid institutional hierarchies where ethical autonomy is constrained [61, 63].

In the present study, the relationships between ECC, EANC, and ageist attitudes with HNC were examined. The results revealed that all three variables were significantly and positively associated with HNC. Among these, the strongest correlation was observed between ECC and HNC, highlighting the pivotal role of ethical knowledge, moral reasoning, and value-based action in delivering comprehensive nursing care. This result aligns with previous findings by Katayama et al. [52] and Mohajer et al. [60], which underscore that ethical decision-making and reflective thinking are foundational to multidimensional, person-centered care [52, 60]. In essence, without ethical competence, holistic care becomes fragmented and mechanistic [57]. Furthermore, the relatively strong positive correlation between EANC and HNC emphasizes that nurses’ internalized ethical beliefs, particularly regarding dignity, empowerment, and humanistic values, facilitate more effective spiritual, psychosocial, and physical care integration [28, 61].

One of the most conceptually significant and paradoxical findings of this study was the moderate yet positive correlation between ageism and holistic nursing competence (HNC). At a theoretical level, ageist attitudes are typically assumed to undermine holistic care, particularly in geriatric nursing, where empathy, person-centeredness, and intergenerational sensitivity are essential. Therefore, this unexpected association challenges prevailing assumptions in the literature and calls for a nuanced interpretation that considers the multi-layered nature of nursing competence in practice. A key explanation may lie in the differentiation between technical competence and attitudinal readiness. It is plausible that nurses particularly those trained within systems emphasizing task completion, standardization, and efficiency can meet clinical expectations and perform effectively on measurable HNC indicators while remaining emotionally or ethically disengaged from the deeper relational dimensions of care. In such cases, competence may reflect role adherence rather than value-driven engagement, a form of “performative professionalism” where procedural excellence coexists with implicit bias. Additionally, social desirability bias must be considered, especially in self-report measures related to competence and attitudes. In healthcare environments where ethical commitment and holistic care are idealized, nurses may consciously or unconsciously tailor their responses to align with normative expectations, thus inflating perceived competence even in the presence of underlying ageist beliefs. This discrepancy between belief and behavior reflects what Fry and Johnstone [48] described as ethical dissonance, wherein positive ethical ideals do not consistently translate into practice under real-world pressures [48].The institutional culture also plays a pivotal role. In high-demand or high-acuity clinical settings, efficiency, documentation accuracy, and risk avoidance may be rewarded more than relational depth or humanistic care. Such environments may foster a culture in which ageism remains unchallenged, especially if older patients are perceived as time-intensive, emotionally demanding, or less clinically “urgent.” This context further explains how systemic reinforcement of procedural competence may overshadow attitudinal shortcomings, allowing ageist biases to persist beneath a veneer of professional adequacy. These insights resonate with prior research. Allen [34] emphasized the implicit and often invisible nature of ageism among healthcare providers, which may not surface through overt behaviors but can still erode the ethos of compassionate care [34]. Similarly, North and Fiske [64] identified ageism as a culturally ingrained but cognitively modifiable bias, responsive to structured interventions such as intergenerational contact, simulation training, and reflective dialogue [64]. Ünver et al. [28] and other scholars have also highlighted the attenuation of ageist tendencies through targeted education and exposure, particularly when embedded in emotionally resonant learning experiences [28]. This finding should not be viewed as a contradiction in measurement, but rather as evidence of a conceptual misalignment between technical competence and ethical orientation. It highlights the need to redefine nursing competence as a balance of skill, empathy, and moral integrity. To address this gap, nursing education must integrate age sensitivity and ethical reflection through methods such as narrative-based learning, scenario-based ethics, and intergenerational empathy training. In parallel, routine assessments of attitudes, alongside clinical evaluations, can provide a fuller picture of readiness for elder care. Ultimately, confronting implicit ageism is not only an ethical necessity but a strategic pathway to enhancing geriatric care quality by ensuring that holistic competence is both practiced and genuinely internalized.

In addition to attitudinal variables, several demographic and occupational factors were significantly associated with HNC. Male and single nurses demonstrated higher HNC scores, possibly due to greater self-efficacy and fewer domestic constraints findings consistent with Girma et al. [59] and Flinkman et al. [26, 59].Job satisfaction was also a strong predictor, with more satisfied nurses exhibiting better holistic performance, aligning with evidence from Turkey and other contexts [33, 65, 66]. From a training perspective, in-service education on holistic empowerment significantly improved HNC scores, reinforcing the role of structured professional development [57, 60]. Nurses under service commitment contracts outperformed peers, potentially due to higher engagement in training or probationary motivation [65]. Interestingly, age and work experience showed a negative correlation with HNC, suggesting potential professional stagnation in the absence of ongoing education an issue similarly noted in Finnish research [67]. These findings collectively underscore the importance of targeted support for both early-career and experienced nurses to sustain holistic competence.

To determine the predictors of HNC in geriatric care, stepwise multiple regression analysis was used. This analysis revealed four significant predictors ECC, EANC, participation in holistic training, and ageist attitudes that together explained 56% of the variance in HNC. Among these, ECC was the strongest predictor, with a standardized beta coefficient of 0.62. This finding emphasizes that nurses’ ability to apply ethical reasoning, act with empathy, uphold fairness, and respect human dignity is central to effective holistic care. In other words, without a solid ethical foundation, holistic care risks devolving into fragmented, technical task performance. This aligns with Maluwa et al. [68], who demonstrated that ethical competence encompasses not only knowledge but also value-driven behaviors and situational judgment, which must be deeply integrated into clinical routines [68]. Similarly, Mohamed & Modanloo [69] emphasized that ethical competence in gerontological care requires sensitivity to cultural, generational, and psychosocial nuances, and advocated for scenario-based ethical simulations and experiential learning as effective strategies to elevate ECC and consequently HNC [69].

EANC also emerged as a significant predictor with a beta coefficient of 0.11, indicating that nurses’ internal value frameworks, especially in regard to humanistic, justice-oriented, and dignity-focused care, are essential for the development of holistic nursing skills. Taşkıran & Turk [16] further validated this, showing that negative ethical attitudes significantly suppressed HNC, particularly in intensive care contexts [16].

A history of in-service holistic care training significantly predicted HNC, underscoring the importance of structured, ongoing education a finding also supported by Girma et al. [59], who noted its impact on teamwork and psychosocial care competencies [59]. A particularly paradoxical yet significant predictor was ageist attitudes (β = 0.07). Although ageism is generally associated with diminished care quality, its positive association with HNC here may reflect a disconnect between technical performance and attitudinal orientation. Nurses may perform well procedurally while maintaining implicit biases, driven by institutional norms or social desirability in self-reporting. A low standardized beta coefficient (e.g., β = 0.07) can still achieve statistical significance when the sample size is sufficiently large and variability is adequately controlled. This reflects a consistent, albeit small, effect, and highlights the distinction between statistical significance and practical effect size. Prior research supports this complexity. Jeyasingam et al. [70] linked ageism to burnout and mechanical care [70]. Ha & Kim [71] found it reduces willingness to engage in elder care [71] and Mohamed & Modanloo [69] emphasized that poor elder-centric cultural competence undermines holistic practice [69]. These findings suggest that addressing ageism is not only ethically essential but also strategically critical for enhancing HNC. Integrating gerontology education, cultural sensitivity, and intergenerational learning into training frameworks may help align clinical competence with truly person-centered care.

Strengths and limitations

The present study has several important strengths that distinguish it from similar research. First, the large sample size (784 participants) and the inclusion of nurses from diverse hospital departments increase the external validity of the findings and allow for advanced statistical analyses. Such a sample size, especially in correlational and regression studies, plays a decisive role in increasing statistical power and the precision of estimates. Second is the comprehensive design of the study’s conceptual and analytical model, which simultaneously examined a set of diverse variables related to holistic nursing competence. These variables included ethical caring competency, ethical attitude, ageist views, job satisfaction, work shift, service department, employment type, history of in-service training, level of autonomy in clinical decision-making, and variables such as age, gender, marital status, work experience, financial status, and income adequacy. The simultaneous use of this wide range of variables has led to a more multilayered and precise analysis of the factors affecting holistic competence. Third, this study used validated psychometric tools with confirmed validity and reliability, all of which had a Cronbach’s alpha above 0.85. Fourth, the study’s statistical analysis was performed using stepwise multiple regression, and demographic and professional confounding variables were included in the model as controls. This action resulted in the extraction of the real and net effects of the key research variables on HNC. Among these, ethical caring competency, ethical attitude, history of in-service training, and ageist views were identified as the strongest predictors of HNC. Fifth, the study’s focus on the care of older adults as a national and global need aligns it with Iran’s demographic trends and the strategic priorities of its health system. Furthermore, introducing a variable like ageist views into the analytical model is a significant innovation that has been less addressed in the domestic research literature. Sixth, the present study meticulously examined the impact of employment type, service department, and nurses’ work shifts, demonstrating that these managerial factors can also influence holistic nursing competence. These results provide important evidence for organizational reforms and human resource planning. A unique contribution of this study lies in its integration of ageist attitudes as a predictive variable for holistic nursing competence a novel angle in the current nursing literature. While prior research has focused on technical skills or general ethical behavior, few have empirically tested the influence of discriminatory attitudes on holistic care performance, especially in geriatric contexts.

This insight opens new pathways for policy-level interventions targeting attitudinal development in nursing education and workforce management. Moreover, the study offers actionable recommendations for healthcare administrators and policymakers such as integrating anti-ageism training modules into continuing education, prioritizing ethical empowerment workshops, and aligning shift schedules with nurse well-being to enhance holistic competence in practice.

Despite its strengths, this study was also accompanied by limitations. Among these limitations is the study’s cross-sectional design. This approach does not allow for the inference of causal relationships. For the analysis of causal links, it is suggested that future studies use longitudinal and interventional methods. Additionally, the use of a convenience sampling method may limit the generalizability of the results to other regions and medical centers in the country. The self-report instruments used for data collection may be subject to social desirability bias. In future studies, the use of direct observation methods and clinical performance evaluation is recommended to increase the external validity of the findings. Another limitation involves the potential for social desirability bias in self-reported responses, especially on ethically sensitive topics such as ageism and holistic competence. Although anonymity was preserved, participants may have answered in ways that reflect socially expected behavior rather than their actual attitudes or practices. The exclusion of incomplete forms, although affecting less than 2% of the total sample, may still introduce a potential risk of bias and should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. Additionally, the length and comprehensiveness of the study instruments may have affected concentration and response accuracy for some participants, potentially contributing to minor measurement error or increased item-level missingness.

Moreover, the cultural and organizational context of Iran including norms in nurse-patient interaction, professional hierarchies, and training protocols may influence nurses’ ethical attitudes and holistic competence. As such, the findings may not be directly generalizable to countries with different healthcare systems or cultural values. Additionally, since participants were recruited from a limited number of hospitals, potential clustering effects may have influenced the independence of responses, which is a limitation not accounted for in the current regression model.

Implications

The findings of this study show that HNC is influenced by a set of individual, professional, and attitudinal variables that can form the basis for policy-making, education, and human resource management in the field of nursing. Identifying variables such as ECC, EANC, a history of in-service training in holistic nursing empowerment, and the level of ageist attitudes as strong predictors of HNC indicates that educational and managerial interventions should be focused on these areas.

From an educational perspective, it is suggested that the design and implementation of training courses based on nursing ethics, holistic care, and gerontology be considered at both university and intra-organizational levels. Training based on a reflective approach, experiential learning, and clinical decision-making skills can sustainably strengthen the ethical and holistic competencies of nurses. Furthermore, the findings confirm that nurses who participated in in-service training courses on holistic empowerment had higher HNC; therefore, the development and expansion of these courses should be systematically institutionalized within the health system. From a managerial perspective, hospital managers can provide the necessary foundation for increasing the quality of holistic care by creating participatory work environments, offering professional support, and reducing psychological and physical pressures. Additionally, establishing regular structures for the continuous assessment of nurses’ competence and designing data-driven professional development programs based on job needs and analysis are recommended.

From a policy-making perspective, the findings of this study can serve as a basis for developing national standards for holistic nursing competence. In particular, policies that focus on promoting ethical practice in clinical settings, reducing discriminatory views towards older adults, and expanding intergenerational training can play a fundamental role in elevating the quality of nursing care at the national level. Given that nurses with a positive attitude towards older adults demonstrated higher HNC, it is essential to revise university educational content and design psychosocial interventions to reduce ageism.

Conclusions

The present study showed that HNC in Iranian nurses is influenced by a combination of ethical, attitudinal, educational, and professional factors. The findings indicated that ECC, EANC, a history of in-service training in holistic nursing empowerment, and a positive attitude towards older adults are the strongest predictors of HNC. Furthermore, variables such as job satisfaction, type of employment, work shift, age, work experience, and service department were also significantly associated with the level of nurses’ holistic competence. These findings emphasize the importance of promoting professional ethics, strengthening structured in-service training, and correcting negative attitudes towards older adults to enhance the quality of nursing care. On the other hand, the role of organizational variables like job satisfaction and opportunities for independent decision-making highlights the necessity of creating supportive and motivational work environments.
