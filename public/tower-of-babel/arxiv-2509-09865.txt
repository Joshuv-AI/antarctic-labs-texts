Linear fractional relative risk aversion
Kristian Behrens, Yasusada Murata

Abstract
We characterize the family of utility functions satisfying linear fractional relative risk aversion (LFRRA) in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric functions. We apply this family, which nests various utility functions used in different strands of literature, to monopolistic competition and obtain a closed-form solution for the profit-maximizing price by generalizing the Lambert W W function. We let firm-level data decide whether the RRA in each sector or in the aggregate economy is increasing, decreasing, or constant, which in turn determines whether markups are decreasing, increasing, or constant with respect to marginal costs. Keywords: linear fractional relative risk aversion; Gauss hypergeometric functions; monopolistic competition; generalization of the Lambert W W function; markups. JEL Classification: D43; D21; D22; D11.



Introduction

The relative risk aversion (RRA) of a utility function
u
of a quantity
q
, defined as
-\frac{qu^{\prime\prime}(q)}{u^{\prime}(q)}
, dates back to Arrow (1963, 1971) and Pratt (1964). While it originates from the economics of uncertainty, it appears repeatedly in different contexts such as the utility maximization problem of intertemporally optimizing consumers and the profit maximization problem of monopolistically competitive firms.
1
1

1

See, e.g., Blanchard and Fisher (1989) for the former and
Krugman (1979) for the latter.

Therefore, the RRA has been widely used in various fields including economic growth, international trade, urban economics, as well as the economics of uncertainty.

In these fields of economics, different parametric specifications of
u
have been used for deriving qualitative and quantitative results. Examples include the utility functions with hyperbolic relative risk aversion (HARA)---which nests both constant relative risk aversion (CRRA) and constant absolute risk aversion (CARA) as special cases---and the constant revenue elasticity of marginal revenue (CREMR) utility function, which also nests CRRA but does not belong to HARA.
2
2

2

See, e.g., Merton (1971) for HARA and Mrázová et al. (2021) for CREMR.

We propose a parametric family of utility functions that not only unifies these existing specifications into a single framework but also allows for new ones. To this end, we start with
linear fractional relative risk aversion
(LFRRA), defined as
-\frac{qu^{\prime\prime}(q)}{u^{\prime}(q)}=\frac{\alpha q+\beta}{\alpha\sigma q+1}
, where
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\sigma
are parameters. We then show that this second-order differential equation can be rewritten as a hypergeometric differential equation,
z(1-z)v^{\prime\prime}(z)+(\beta-\frac{z}{\sigma})v^{\prime}(z)=0
, using the change of variables
z=-\alpha\sigma q
with

u(q)=v(z)
. Solving this differential equation allows us to characterize the family of utility functions satisfying LFRRA in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric functions.

We apply
the LFRRA family of utility functions thus obtained to a model of monopolistic competition
with heterogeneous firms,
where the profit-maximizing price
p
, given
marginal cost
m
, is defined as the solution to

\frac{p-m}{p}=-\frac{qu^{\prime\prime}(q)}{u^{\prime}(q)}
.
We derive the closed form solution for
p=\frac{m}{\mu}
,
where

\mu
is the inverse markup function
parametrized by

\{\alpha,\beta,\sigma\}
. It turns out that
\mu
generalizes the Lambert
W
function (Lambert, 1758; Euler, 1783; Corless et al., 1996), which has been used for
the inverse markup for the CARA utility function in
Behrens et al. (2014, 2017, 2020).
Our generalized Lambert
W
function reduces to the original when
\alpha>0
and
\beta=\sigma=0
.

The LFRRA family
also reduces to
the CRRA or
the constant elasticity of substitution (CES)
utility function in Melitz (2003)
when
\alpha=0
or
\beta=\frac{1}{\sigma}
, and constitutes a parametric version of the relative love for variety (RLV) in Zhelobodko et al. (2012)
in that the sign of the derivative of the RRA (or RLV) is
expressed by the sign of
\alpha(1-\beta\sigma)
. To illustrate our approach, we
further present a parsimonious way to estimate the key parameters
\{\alpha,\beta,\sigma\}
using the
product-level markup and quantity data for a sample of firms from eleven sectors in
De Loecker et al. (2016).
Our estimates
tell us which sectors display increasing, decreasing, or constant RRA, which in turn determines whether markups are decreasing, increasing, or constant with respect to marginal costs, respectively.

We believe that our framework is useful for the further development of qualitative and quantitative analyses for at least three reasons.

First, existing parametric approaches to the RRA typically rely on
\alpha=0
or
\beta=\frac{1}{\sigma}
(CRRA)
or consider either of two special cases, namely
\beta=0
(HARA) or
\beta=1
(CREMR), or a subcase of the former, such as the CARA (
\sigma=0
), quadratic (
\sigma=-1
), or log (
\sigma=1
) case.
3
3

3

One can also verify that the HARA and CREMR cases
nest the CRRA case
by letting
\alpha\to\infty
.

We show that the LFRRA family encompasses not only all of these specifications but also
new ones, while remaining
amenable to theoretical analysis by virtue of the generalized Lambert
W
function. Thus, our approach may serve to enrich the qualitative analysis in economic growth, international trade, urban economics or the economics of uncertainty, where the RRA has played a central role.

Second, in recent quantitative trade and urban models, researchers often impose the above-mentioned parameter restrictions.
4
4

4

See, e.g., Redding and Rossi-Hansberg (2017) for the CRRA or CES; Behrens et al. (2014, 2017, 2020) for the CARA; Simonovska (2015) for the (translated) log; Mraźová and Neary (2017) and Arkolakis et al. (2019) for the Pollak (1971) family; and Mraźová et al. (2021) for the CREMR. As shown below, the demand function derived from the Pollak family used in Mraźová and Neary (2017) and Arkolakis et al. (2019) is isomorphic to that from the HARA.

However, we do not know a priori whether a dataset satisfies these restrictions.
Our LFRRA family, which encompasses all these specifications,
is useful for addressing this question since it allows us to let the data decide the right specification by estimating these parameters. Our estimates of
\beta
are significantly different from
0
and
1
in
ten out of eleven sectors and in the aggregate economy. The numbers of sectors that display increasing, decreasing, or constant RRA are five, four, and two, respectively, thus showing that having a flexible model accommodating these different cases is useful. We also find that the estimates for all sectors pooled are consistent with increasing RRA.

Last,
we show that our application to monopolistic competition based on directly additive preferences can be extended to the case of implicitly additive preferences. The LFRRA family of utility functions allows us to treat these two types of preferences in a unified manner and to explore various parametric specifications that are not covered by Zhelobodko et al. (2012).
Furthermore, by replacing the Lambert
W
function in Behrens et al. (2014, 2017, 2020) with our generalized version, one can incorporate the LFRRA family of utility functions
into general equilibrium models à la Dixit and Stiglitz (1977) and Melitz (2003). This will
make the quantitative analysis of
monopolistic competition models more flexible
in various fields of economics.

concludes.

Linear fractional relative risk aversion and the utility function

In this section, we derive the family of utility functions that have linear fractional relative risk aversion (LFRRA), which is formally defined as follows.

Definition 1
(Linear fractional relative risk aversion (LFRRA))

Let

-\frac{qu^{\prime\prime}(q)}{u^{\prime}(q)}=\frac{\alpha q+\beta}{\alpha\sigma q+1},

(1)

\alpha\in(-\infty,\infty)

\sigma\in(-\infty,\infty)

As shown below, this family encompasses the existing parametric approaches to the RRA, including
the HARA (
\beta=0

\beta=1

\sigma=0

\sigma=-1
), and log (
\sigma=1
) subcases.
Observe that the CRRA can be obtained from (
1

\alpha=0

or
\beta=\frac{1}{\sigma}
.

5

The case with

Monopolistic competition

There is a mass
L

Let

There is a continuum of firms producing differentiated varieties

\omega

Lambert (1758) analyzed the equation

Z

(Z/x)^{\xi}\tau(Z)-(Z/x)^{\check{\xi}}=(\xi-\check{\xi})x^{-\check{\xi}}\psi(Z)Z^{\xi+\check{\xi}}
, which implies
(Z/x)^{\xi-\check{\xi}}\tau(Z)-1=(\xi-\check{\xi})\psi(Z)Z^{\xi}

,
\check{\xi}=1+\sigma

\psi(Z)=1/Z-1

Fitting the monopolistic competition model to data

We fit our monopolistic competition model to the data from De Loecker et al. (2016), who provide quantities and estimated markups at the product level for a sample of firms from eleven Indian manufacturing sectors from 1989 to 2001. Their markup data are suitable for our purpose as they are obtained without imposing any restrictions on
consumer demand and thus on preferences.
To stay close to our theoretical setting, we focus on single-product firms only. Hence, a variety
\omega
in our model corresponds to the single product of a firm in the dataset. Dropping all observations where markups are either missing or less than one—and trimming the top and bottom three percent of the sectoral markup and quantity distributions (as in De Loecker et al., 2016)—yields 9,232 firm-year observations.

We quantify the model using the LFRRA specification (
1
) and
the first-order condition for profit maximization (
21
), which implies
1-\mu(\omega)=\frac{\alpha q(\omega)+\beta}{\alpha\sigma q(\omega)+1}
.
Using observed firm-level markups
\frac{1}{\mu(\omega)}
and quantities
q(\omega)
, we estimate the values of
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\sigma
by minimizing the following residual sum of squares (RSS):

\min_{\alpha,\beta,\sigma}\,{\rm RSS}=\sum_{\omega=1}^{\Omega}\left[1-\mu(\omega)-\frac{\alpha q(\omega)+\beta}{\alpha\sigma q(\omega)+1}\right]^{2}

(38)

subject to the theoretical constraints
\alpha q(\omega)+\beta>0
,

\alpha\sigma q(\omega)+1>0
, and
0<\frac{\alpha q(\omega)+\beta}{\alpha\sigma q(\omega)+1}\leq 1
(see
Appendix A
for more details).
We solve the minimization problem (
38
) at the sectoral and aggregate levels
to obtain the fitted values
\{\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\beta},\widehat{\sigma}\}
that are consistent with our LFRRA preferences.
We also verify that
the
second-order conditions (
24
) evaluated at
\{\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\beta},\widehat{\sigma}\}
are satisfied
for all quantities
q(\omega)\geq 0
.

\{\alpha,\beta,\sigma\}
by bootstrapping as follows.
We first randomly draw, with replacement,
a sample of markup and quantity observations from the data such that the generated and observed sample sizes are identical. We then compute
\{\widetilde{\alpha},\widetilde{\beta},\widetilde{\sigma}\}
by solving the minimization problem (
38
) using the
procedure explained in
Appendix A

We repeat this process 200 times at the sectoral and aggregate levels to obtain the distributions of parameters
\{\widetilde{\alpha}_{b},\widetilde{\beta}_{b},\widetilde{\sigma}_{b}\}_{b=1}^{200}

Implicitly additive preferences

Consider the implicitly additively separable utility function
U
, defined as
\int_{\Omega}\Upsilon\bigl(\frac{q(\omega)}{U}\bigr){\rm d}\omega=1
, where
\Omega
denotes the set of varieties,
q(\omega)\geq 0
is consumption of variety
\omega
, and
\Upsilon
is an increasing and concave sub-function (Kimball, 1995; Mrázová et al., 2021). Minimizing expenditure
\int_{\Omega}p(\omega)q(\omega){\rm d}\omega
subject to the utility constraint
\int_{\Omega}\Upsilon\bigl(\frac{q(\omega)}{U}\bigr){\rm d}\omega=1
gives the first-order condition
p(\omega)=\frac{\nu^{\rm imp}}{U}\Upsilon^{\prime}\bigl(\frac{q(\omega)}{U}\bigr)
, where
\nu^{\rm imp}>0
is the Lagrange multiplier.

Using this condition, the profit maximization problem in the monopolistic competition model in Section
3
can be replaced with

\max_{q}\bigl[\frac{\nu^{\rm imp}}{U}\Upsilon^{\prime}\bigl(\frac{q}{U}\bigr)-m\bigr]LU\frac{q}{U}-f
.
Let
z=\frac{q}{U}
denote scaled quantities. We can then rewrite the problem as

\max_{z}\bigl[\frac{\nu^{\rm imp}}{U}\Upsilon^{\prime}(z)-m\bigr]LUz-f
.
Since there is a continuum of firms, no firm can affect
\nu^{\rm imp}
and
U
. Thus, the first-order condition for profit maximization is given by
\frac{\nu^{\rm imp}}{U}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(z)LUz+\bigl[\frac{\nu^{\rm imp}}{U}\Upsilon^{\prime}(z)-m\bigr]LU=0
. The profit maximizing price
p(m)
hence satisfies:

\frac{p(m)-m}{p(m)}=1-\mu(m)=-\frac{z(m)\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(z(m))}{\Upsilon^{\prime}(z(m))},

(39)

where
\mu(m)
denotes the inverse of the markup. The second-order condition (SOC) for profit maximization is given by

\displaystyle\textstyle\frac{\nu^{\rm imp}}{U}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)LUz+\frac{\nu^{\rm imp}}{U}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(z)LU+\frac{\nu^{\rm imp}}{U}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(z)LU

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu^{\rm imp}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)Lz+2\nu^{\rm imp}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(z)L

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle-L\nu^{\rm imp}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(z)\left[-\frac{z\Upsilon^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(z)}-2\right]<0.

Since
-L\nu^{\rm imp}\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(z)>0
, the SOC reduces to
-\frac{z\Upsilon^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(z)}<2
.

As in Section
2
, we assume that
-\frac{z(m)\Upsilon^{\prime\prime}(z(m))}{\Upsilon^{\prime}(z(m))}
is of the linear fractional form,
\frac{\alpha z(m)+\beta}{\alpha\sigma z(m)+1}
, so that (
39
) becomes

1-\mu(m)=\frac{\alpha z(m)+\beta}{\alpha\sigma z(m)+1}.

(40)

We can fit (
40
) to data in the same way as in Section
4
by noting that
1-\mu(m)=\frac{\alpha z(m)+\beta}{\alpha\sigma z(m)+1}=\frac{\alpha\frac{q(m)}{U}+\beta}{\alpha\sigma\frac{q(m)}{U}+1}=\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}q(m)+\beta}{\widetilde{\alpha}\sigma q(m)+1}
, where
\widetilde{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha}{U}
,
\beta
, and
\sigma
are the parameters to be estimated.

Conclusion

In this paper we first characterize the LFRRA family of utility functions in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric functions. This family, which is parametrized by
\{\alpha,\beta,\sigma\}
,
nests the CES, CARA, quadratic, log, HARA, CREMR, incomplete gamma, incomplete beta, and exponential integral utility functions as special cases. We then embed this family into a monopolistic competition model and derive the price equilibrium. We show that the inverse markup function is expressed as the generalized Lambert
W
function,
{\cal W}(x,\alpha,\beta,\sigma)
, which reduces to the original Lambert
W
function when
\beta
and
\sigma
are set to zero. We finally take the relationship between the inverse markup and the LFRRA to firm-level data and estimate
\{\widehat{\alpha},\widehat{\beta},\widehat{\sigma}\}
. The estimates are used to test whether each sector displays IRRA, DRRA, or CRRA, which in turn determines whether markups are decreasing, increasing, or constant in marginal costs.
Our results show that all cases are present in the data, thus highlighting the importance of
taking a flexible parametric approach.

We believe that the LFRRA will be useful to fill the gap between the empirical literature on markups, which shows that markups are heterogeneous across firms in each sector, and quantitative trade and urban models, where markups are typically assumed to be constant. Extending the LFRRA family to such general equilibrium analysis will require to replace the inverse markup function or the Lambert
W
function in Behrens et al. (2014, 2017, 2020) with our generalized Lambert
W
function.
Observe that the benefit of using the LFRRA is not limited to the price equilibrium in trade and urban models. It would also spur the development of qualitative and quantitative analysis in various other contexts such as economic growth and the economics of uncertainty, where the RRA matters.

Appendix Appendix A
Details on the empirical implementation

Numerical procedure.

We solve the minimization problem

\min_{\{\alpha,\beta,\sigma\}}\ \text{RSS}=\sum_{\omega=1}^{\Omega}\left[1-\mu(\omega)-\frac{\alpha q(\omega)+\beta}{\alpha\sigma q(\omega)+1}\right]^{2}

(A.1)

subject to the theoretical constraints
\alpha q(\omega)+\beta>0
,
\alpha\sigma q(\omega)+1>0
, and
0<\frac{\alpha q(\omega)+\beta}{\alpha\sigma q(\omega)+1}\leq 1
. As shown by (
A.1
), conditional on observed markups
\mu(\omega)
and quantities
q(\omega)
, only the product
\alpha q(\omega)
matters for the LFRRA. Without loss of generality, we therefore scale quantities by the mean quantity
\overline{q}
in the sample and use

q(\omega)/\overline{q}
as our measure of quantities. Doing so
improves the numerical computations since quantities are extremely skewed.
In what follows we refrain from interpreting
the magnitude
of
\alpha
as it depends on the scaling.

Following Arkolakis et al. (2019), we split the estimation procedure into an inner loop and an outer loop. In the inner loop, we fix the value of
\alpha
and minimize the RSS in
(
A.1
)
with respect to
\beta
and
\sigma
, which yields

\widehat{\beta}(\alpha)
and

\widehat{\sigma}(\alpha)
.
In the outer loop, we do a grid search for the value
\widehat{\alpha}
that minimizes the RSS in (
A.1
) conditional on
\widehat{\beta}(\alpha)
and
\widehat{\sigma}(\alpha)
.

To implement the procedure, we have written a recursive Mathematica solver. At iteration
t
, the solver starts with a given domain
[\underline{\alpha}_{t},\overline{\alpha}_{t}]
and solves the system for
i=1,2,\ldots,100
equally-spaced values
\alpha_{t}^{i}
, in that domain.
Let
\widehat{\alpha}_{t}
denote the value of
\alpha_{t}^{i}
such that
\{\widehat{\alpha}_{t},\widehat{\beta}(\widehat{\alpha}_{t}),\widehat{\sigma}(\widehat{\alpha}_{t})\}
minimizes the RSS on the domain
[\underline{\alpha}_{t},\overline{\alpha}_{t}]
.
If

\widehat{\alpha}_{t}

is interior to the domain
[\underline{\alpha}_{t},\overline{\alpha}_{t}]
, say at
\alpha_{t}^{i}
, we bracket that domain by letting
\underline{\alpha}_{t+1}=\alpha^{i-1}_{t}
and
\overline{\alpha}_{t+1}=\alpha^{i+1}_{t}
and solve the system again for
100
points over the new domain
[\underline{\alpha}_{t+1},\overline{\alpha}_{t+1}]
. If the solution is not interior, i.e., the minimizer hits the upper bound
\overline{\alpha}_{t}
or the lower bound
\underline{\alpha}_{t}
of the domain, we shift and expand the domain in that direction. For example, if we hit the upper bound at iteration
t
, we let
\underline{\alpha}_{t+1}=\overline{\alpha}_{t}
and
\overline{\alpha}_{t+1}=\xi\times\overline{\alpha}_{t}
, with
\xi>1
and solve the system again for 100 equally-spaced points over the new domain. We run the procedure until we converge to a solution, where convergence at iteration
t
requires that
|\widehat{\alpha}_{t}-\widehat{\alpha}_{t-1}|<10^{-12}
.

Since our grid search (like any grid search) may pick up local minima, we implement our procedure in both ascending and descending
orders.
In the ascending version, we start with a domain of
\alpha
between
\underline{\alpha}_{0}=-\frac{1}{q_{\rm max}}
(where
q_{\rm max}
is the largest quantity we observe in the data) and
\overline{\alpha}_{0}=450
and expand the search domain if required (the
choice of

\underline{\alpha}_{0}

comes from
\alpha q+\beta>0
for all
q
and
\beta\leq 1
).
In the descending version, we start with a domain of
\alpha
between
\underline{\alpha}_{0}=-\frac{1}{q_{\rm max}}
and

\overline{\alpha}_{0}=1.5\times 10^{8}
and
reduce the search domain if required. If both procedures
yield different values, we pick the solution that gives the smaller RSS.
