Rainbow subgraphs of star-coloured graphs
Allan Lo, Klas Markström, Dhruv Mubayi, Katherine Staden, Maya Stein, Lea Weber

Abstract
An edge-colouring of a graph G G can fail to be rainbow for two reasons: either it contains a monochromatic cherry (a pair of incident edges), or a monochromatic matching of size two. A colouring is a proper colouring if it forbids the first structure, and a star-colouring if it forbids the second structure. In this paper, we study rainbow subgraphs in star-coloured graphs and determine the maximum number of colours in a star-colouring of a large complete graph which does not contain a rainbow copy of a given graph H H . This problem is a special case of one studied by Axenovich and Iverson on generalised Ramsey numbers and we extend their results in this case.



1
Introduction

Given a graph
G
, let
v(G)=|V(G)|
and
e(G)=|E(G)|
. An
edge-colouring
of a graph
G
is a function which maps its edge set
E(G)
to a set of colours. We say that an edge-coloured graph
H
is
rainbow
if each edge receives a different colour.
An edge-colouring of a graph
G
can fail to be rainbow for two reasons: either it contains a monochromatic
cherry

K_{1,2}
or it contains a monochromatic matching of size 2. Edge-colourings without the first structure are called
proper
. In edge-colourings which avoid monochromatic matchings, every colour class is either a star or a triangle. We will call such colourings without triangles
star-colourings
.
Before discussing these two types of colourings, we take a step back to consider the more general question of which subgraphs appear in
any
edge-coloured complete graph.
This is answered by the famous
canonical Ramsey theorem
of Erdős and Rado
[
14
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(The canonical Ramsey theorem
[
14
]
)
.

For any positive integer
k
, any sufficiently large edge-coloured complete graph contains a clique
K_{k}
which either

•

is monochromatic,

•

is rainbow,

•

has a
lexical colouring
; that is, its vertex set can be ordered as
v_{1}<\cdots<v_{k}
such that for
i<j
and
i^{\prime}<j^{\prime}
, the colour of
v_{i}v_{j}
is the same as the colour of
v_{i^{\prime}}v_{j^{\prime}}
if and only if
i=i^{\prime}
.

Rainbow subgraphs of properly edge-coloured graphs have been extensively studied.
Since proper colourings avoid both
monochromatic and
lexically coloured cliques
K_{k}
(unless
k\leq 2
),
Theorem
1.1
implies that given any graph
H
, every sufficiently large properly coloured complete graph contains a rainbow copy of
H
.
Less is known about graphs
H
whose size is comparable with that of the host graph.
Since a proper colouring of
K_{n}
can have as few as
n-1
colours, we can only hope to find rainbow copies of
H
with
e(H)\leq n-1
.
Regarding long paths, Maamoun and Meyniel
[
26
]
found an example of a proper colouring of
K_{n}
without a rainbow Hamilton path for
n\geq 4
a power of
2
. In 1989, Andersen
[
3
]
conjectured that one can however guarantee a rainbow path of length
n-2
.
Even finding a rainbow path of length
n-o(n)
was open for a long time, and was eventually achieved by Alon, Pokrovskiy and Sudakov in 2017
[
2
]
.
It has been shown that one can guarantee almost spanning cycles
[
2
]
, trees
[
28
]
, and bounded degree graphs
[
11
]
. See the survey
[
31
]
of Pokrovskiy for further results.

Now, we turn to edge-colourings of
K_{n}
that are not necessarily proper. If they use sufficiently many colours, one can still guarantee a rainbow copy of a given graph
H
. The
anti-Ramsey number

{\mathrm{ar}}(n,H)
of
H
, introduced by Erdős, Simonovits and Sós in 1975
[
12
]
, is defined as

{\mathrm{ar}}(n,H):=\max\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\text{there exists an }s\text{-coloured }K_{n}\text{ without a rainbow copy of }H\}.

Define the
extremal number

{\rm ex}(n,\mathcal{H})
of a family
\mathcal{H}
of graphs as

{\rm ex}(n,\mathcal{H}):=\max\{e:\text{there exists an $n$-vertex graph with }e\text{ edges and no copy of any }H\in\mathcal{H}\}

and let
{\rm ex}(n,H):={\rm ex}(n,\{H\})
.
It has been shown that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{ar}}(n,C_{k})=\left(\frac{k-2}{2}+\frac{1}{k-1}\right)n+O(1),\quad{\mathrm{ar}}(n,K_{k})={\rm ex}(n,K_{k-1})+1,

(1.1)

where
C_{k}
is the cycle of length
k
, and further,

\displaystyle{\rm ex}(n,\mathcal{F}^{-}_{H})+1\leq{\mathrm{ar}}(n,H)\leq{\rm ex}(n,H)

(1.2)

for any graph
H
, where
\mathcal{F}^{-}_{H}:=\{H\setminus e:e\in E(H)\}
.
The first result is due to Montellano-Ballesteros and Neumann-Lara
[
27
]
, proving a conjecture from
[
12
]
,
and the other results are due to
[
12
]
.
Anti-Ramsey numbers have been studied for many more
H
, see the dynamic survey
[
16
]
for a more comprehensive overview.

In this paper, we initiate the study of rainbow subgraphs of star-coloured graphs.
In particular we are interested in
determining the maximum number of colours in a star-colouring of the complete graph which avoids a rainbow copy of a given graph
H
.
We say that a colouring is an
s
-star-colouring
if it is a star-colouring with
s
colours.
Given a graph
H
and an integer
n
, we define the
star-anti-Ramsey number

{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)
of
H
as

{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H):=\max\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\text{there exists an }s\text{-star-coloured }K_{n}\text{ without a rainbow copy of }H\}.

Clearly, we have

{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)\leq{\mathrm{ar}}(n,H).

(1.3)

If
n<v(H)
, then
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)={\mathrm{ar}}(n,H)=\binom{n}{2}
, so we only consider the case
n\geq v(H)
.
Note that the third type of colouring in Theorem
1.1
is a star-colouring, so this theorem does not imply bounds on
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)
for general
H
.

1.1
Background

We are not aware of
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)
being studied directly before.
Our starting point is a closely-related result of Axenovich and Iverson
[
4
]
which demonstrates a connection between
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)
and the so-called vertex arboricity of
H
.
Before stating it, we recall the famous theorem of Erdős and Stone
[
13
]
relating the extremal number
{\rm ex}(n,H)
and the chromatic number
\chi(H)
. (Part (ii) is a weak consequence of another famous theorem of Kővári, Sós and Turán
[
24
]
which we state later in Theorem
2.1
.)
The
chromatic number

\chi(H)
of
H
is the minimum number of parts in a vertex partition of
H
such that
H
induces an edgeless graph in each part.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
13
,
24
]
)
.

Let
H
be a graph and let
n
be sufficiently large.

(i)

If
\chi(H)\geq 3
, then

{\rm ex}(n,H)=(1+o(1))\left(1-\frac{1}{\chi(H)-1}\right)\binom{n}{2}.

(ii)

If
\chi(H)\leq 2
, then there exists
c=c(H)>0
such that
{\rm ex}(n,H)\leq n^{2-1/c}
.

A huge literature has developed studying
{\rm ex}(n,H)
for bipartite
H
(that is, with chromatic number at most
2
), and more precise bounds than what is stated above are known, as well as even more precise results for specific
H
of interest. We will require some of these results later.

Let
{\mathrm{va}}(H)
denote the
vertex arboricity
of
H
, which is the minimum number of parts in a vertex partition of
H
such that
H
induces a forest in each part.
Axenovich and Iverson
[
4
]
proved that, in a broad sense,
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)
and
{\mathrm{va}}(H)
are related to each other in the same way as
{\rm ex}(n,H)
and
\chi(H)
.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
H
be a graph and let
n
be sufficiently large. Then the following hold.

(i)

If
{\mathrm{va}}(H)\geq 3
, then

{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)=(1+o(1))\left(1-\frac{1}{{\mathrm{va}}(H)-1}\right)\binom{n}{2}.

(ii)

If
{\mathrm{va}}(H)\leq 2
, then there exists
c=c(H)>0
such that
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)\leq n^{2-1/c}
.

We explain how this result follows from
[
4
]
.
Let
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{H}
be families of graphs and let
n
be a positive integer. Define the
generalised Ramsey number

R_{\mathcal{F}}(n,\mathcal{H})

(sometimes called the
mixed Ramsey number
) to be the maximum number of colours in an edge-colouring of
K_{n}
without a monochromatic copy of any
F\in\mathcal{F}
or a rainbow copy of any
H\in\mathcal{H}
.
Thus

{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)=R_{\{M_{2},K_{3}\}}(n,\{H\})

where
M_{2}
is the matching of size two.
Axenovich and Iverson
[
4
]
showed that, whenever
F
is not a star, the conclusion of Theorem
1.3
holds with
R_{\{F\}}(n,\{H\})
replacing
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n;H)
. We have
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)\leq R_{\{M_{2}\}}(n,\{H\})
, while there is a lower bound construction for
R_{\{M_{2}\}}(n,\{H\})
which is a star-colouring; thus we obtain Theorem
1.3
.

In conclusion, we know the correct order of magnitude of
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)
when
{\mathrm{va}}(H)\geq 3
, while very little is known when
{\mathrm{va}}(H)\leq 2
.
The purpose of this paper is to examine the regime
{\mathrm{va}}(H)\leq 2
.

Before proceeding further, it is helpful to make two simple observations (see Lemma
3.1
for their short proofs).
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
H
be a graph.

•

Every star-colouring of
K_{n}
has at least
n-1
colours.

•

For sufficiently large
n
,
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)
exists if and only if
{\mathrm{va}}(H)\geq 2
.

Thus, we need only consider those
H
with
{\mathrm{va}}(H)=2
(and such
H
satisfy
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)\geq n-1
).

For more related work, see
[
5
,
10
,
19
,
22
,
25
]
.

1.2
Results

Now we state our main results, starting with (mainly sharp) results for specific
H
fulfilling
{\mathrm{va}}(H)=2
.
First, note that clearly,
{\mathrm{va}}(C_{k})=2
for any integer
k\geq 3
.
Our first main result is for cycles of all lengths. Write
\overrightarrow{C_{k}}
for the oriented graph with vertex set
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}
and arc set
\{\overrightarrow{v_{i}v_{i+1}}:i=1,\ldots,k\}
, where subscripts are taken modulo
k
.
Recall that a tournament is an orientation of a complete graph.

Theorem 1.4
.

For all integers
n\geq k\geq 3
we have
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,C_{k})=n+{k-2\choose 2}-1
.

Moreover, every rainbow
C_{k}
-free star-colouring of
K_{n}
with
n+\binom{k-2}{2}-1=n-k+1+{k-1\choose 2}
colours has the following structure. Let
A,B
be disjoint vertex sets of sizes
n-k+1,k-1
respectively and let
\overrightarrow{T}
be an oriented graph obtained from a
\overrightarrow{C_{k}}
-free tournament on
A
and adding all possible directed edges from
A
to
B
.
For each vertex
x\in A
and
y\in N_{\overrightarrow{T}}^{+}(x)
, colour
\overrightarrow{xy}
with
x
. Give each pair in
B
a new distinct colour.

In particular, Theorem
1.4
covers the case
C_{3}=K_{3}
, giving
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,K_{3})=n-1
. Since for
K_{k}
with
k\geq 5
we have that
{\mathrm{va}}(K_{k})\geq 3
(and thus Theorem
1.3
applies),
the only clique for which the approximate value of
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,K_{k})
does not follow from existing results is
K_{4}
. We give a sharp result for this case.

Theorem 1.5
.

For all
n\geq 4
,
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,K_{4})=2n-3
.

This theorem (together with (
1.1
)) shows that
{\mathrm{ar}}(n,H)
and
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)
can be very far apart.
There are many extremal colourings for
K_{4}
, and we are unable to characterise them, but describe some such colourings in Section
3.6
. This perhaps explains why the proof of Theorem
1.5
is by far the most involved of the paper.

Given an integer
k\geq 3
, denote by
K_{k}^{-}
the graph obtained from
K_{k}
by removing one edge.
We prove sharp bounds for
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,K_{4}^{-})
and
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,K_{5}^{-})
. Note that both
K_{4}^{-}
and
K_{5}^{-}
have vertex arboricity
2
.

Theorem 1.6
.

For all
n\geq 2
,
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,K_{4}^{-})=\lfloor 3(n-1)/2\rfloor
.

Moreover, every rainbow
K_{4}^{-}
-free star-colouring of
K_{n}
with
\lfloor 3(n-1)/2\rfloor
colours has the following structure.
There is an ordering

of its vertices such that we colour

with colour

,
and
then we give a new, distinct colour to each component in the
following graph

:

•

if

is odd, then
E(J)=\{v_{1}v_{2},v_{3}v_{4},\ldots,v_{2m-1}v_{2m}\}
;

•

if
n=2m

E(J)=\{v_{1}v_{2},\ldots,v_{2a-3}v_{2a-2}\}\cup E(S)\cup\{v_{2a+2}v_{2a+3},\ldots,v_{2m-2}v_{2m-1}\},

where

is any graph on vertex set
\{v_{2a-1},v_{2a},v_{2a+1}\}
with one or two edges and

.

Note that in Theorem
1.6
, if
n
is even and

is a path of length

, then we can assume that its middle point is

.

Extremal star-colourings for

. On the left,

; on the right,

Theorem 1.7
.

As
n\rightarrow\infty
,

(1+o(1))\cdot\left(\tfrac{n}{2}\right)^{3/2}\leq{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,K_{5}^{-})\leq(1+o(1))16\cdot\left(\tfrac{n}{2}\right)^{3/2}.

Our final main result is a vast generalisation of Theorem
1.7
, where we obtain the order of magnitude of
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,G)
for a variety of graphs

.
The
join

G_{1}+G_{2}
of two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is obtained by taking vertex-disjoint copies of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
and adding
all edges between
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.
The join

of
two nontrivial forests
is a natural example of a graph with
{\mathrm{va}}(H)=2
.
Note that
K_{5}^{-}
is the join of the (unique) trees on two and three vertices respectively, and therefore Theorem

is a special case of a result on

for a tree

(see Lemma

).
We obtain lower and upper bounds for the star-anti-Ramsey number of the join of two trees each on at least three vertices.
Write

for the graph obtained from

by removing one edge.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
T_{1}
and

be trees on

.
Then there is

such that

{\rm ex}(n,K_{s,t}^{-})/2\leq{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,T_{1}+T_{2})\leq cn^{2-1/s}.

The order of magnitude of
{\rm ex}(n,K_{s,t}^{-})
is known to be

when

is much larger than

.
The lower bounds for both Theorems

and

are obtained by taking a lexically coloured complete graph and adding a sparse rainbow graph (as in Theorem

1.2.1
An open problem

A major open problem in extremal graph theory is to determine for which
r\in[1,2]
there is a graph
H
such that
{\rm ex}(n,H)=\Theta(n^{r})
. We say that such an
r
is
extremal realisable
.

Bukh and Conlon
[
7
]
, solving a weaker but also longstanding problem, showed that, if we replace
H
by a family
\mathcal{H}
of graphs, then every rational
r\in[1,2]
is extremal realisable.
Since then, many
r
have been shown to be extremal realisable (by a single graph
H
), including

1+p/q
for positive integers
p,q
with
q>p^{2}

[
21
]
, and
2-a/b
for
b\geq\max\{a,(a-1)^{2}\}

[
9
]
.

We can ask analogous questions in the context of star-colourings. We say that
r
is
star realisable
if there is a graph
H
with
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)=\Theta(n^{r})
.

Problem 1.9
.

Which
r\in[1,2]
are star realisable?

Theorem
1.8
combined with existing results about the extremal numbers of very unbalanced complete bipartite graphs yields a countable set of star realisable densities.

Corollary 1.10
.

For every integer
s\geq 1
, we have that
2-1/s
is star realisable.

We state several more open problems in Section
8
.

We will use capital letters, e.g.
G
to denote a graph, script letters, e.g.
\mathscr{G}
to denote a star-coloured graph, and arrows over capital letters, e.g.
\overrightarrow{G}
to denote a digraph. Often,
\overrightarrow{G}
will denote an orientation of a graph
G
.

Given a graph
G
, we write
v(G)
and
e(G)
for its number of vertices and edges respectively.
Given a subset
A\subseteq V(G)
and a subset
B\subseteq E(G)
, we write
G-A
for the graph obtained by removing all vertices in
A
and all edges incident to any vertex of
A
,
and
G{\setminus}B
for the graph with vertex set

and edge set

.
If
A=\{v\}
we write
G-v:=G-\{v\}
and if

we write
G{\setminus}e:=G{\setminus}\{e\}
.
We write

.
We make analogous definitions for star-coloured graphs and digraphs.

Given a star-coloured graph
\mathscr{G}
, we write
C(\mathscr{G})
for the set of colours appearing in the colouring. Given an edge

in
\mathscr{G}
, we write
c(xy)
for the colour of

.
We simply write

to mean the set of all edges of some colour inside

.
We say that a vertex

of a star of colour

For each vertex

An

2
Preliminaries

2.1
The Zarankiewicz problem

Given integers
s\leq t\leq m,n
, let
z(m,n;s,t)
be the maximum number of edges in a bipartite graph with parts
A,B
of sizes
m,n
respectively such that there are no
S\subseteq A
and
T\subseteq B
with
|S|=s
and
|T|=t
such that
G[S,T]
induces a copy of
K_{s,t}
.
Determining
z(m,n;s,t)
is known as the
Zarankiewicz problem
.
We have

2\cdot{\rm ex}(n,K_{s,t})\leq z(n,n;s,t)\leq 4\cdot{\rm ex}(n,K_{s,t}).

(2.1)

Indeed, for the first inequality, given any graph, we can obtain a bipartite graph by taking two copies of its vertex set and adding the pairs of edges
xy
whenever
x,y
are in different parts; this preserves
K_{s,t}
-freeness.
For the second, given a
K_{s,t}
-free bipartite graph
G
with parts
A,B
each of size
n
, let
a\in A
and
b\in B
be such that
ab\notin E(G)
. If
n
is even, take an equipartition
A=A_{1}\cup A_{2}
and similarly for
B
. If
n
is odd, take an equipartition
A{\setminus}\{a\}=A_{1}^{\prime}\cup A_{2}^{\prime}
and let
A_{i}:=A_{i}^{\prime}\cup\{a\}
and similarly define
B_{i}^{\prime},B_{i}
using
b
for
i=1,2
. Then
G=G[A_{1}^{\prime},B_{1}]\cup G[A_{2},B_{1}^{\prime}]\cup G[A_{1},B_{2}^{\prime}]\cup G[A_{2}^{\prime},B_{2}]
since
ab\notin E(G)
, and each of these four graphs has exactly
n
vertices.

We need the following results on the Zarankiewicz problem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
2\leq s\leq t
be integers.

(i)

(Kővári–Sós–Turán,
[
24
]
)
There is a constant
c=c(s,t)
such that for all
n
, we have
z(n,n;s,t)<cn^{2-1/s}
.

(ii)

(Kollár–Rónyai–Szabó,
[
23
]
)
Suppose
t>(s-1)!
. Then there is a constant
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(s,t)
such that for all
n
, we have
z(n,n;s,t)>c^{\prime}n^{2-1/s}
.

(iii)

(Füredi,
[
18
]
)

{\rm ex}(n,K_{2,t+1})=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{t}n^{3/2}+O(n^{4/3})
for all
t\geq 1
.

2.2
Probabilistic tools

The following version of dependent random choice is an easy adaptation of Lemma 2.1 and Theorem 3.1 in
[
15
]
.

Lemma 2.2

(Dependent random choice,
[
15
]
)
.

Let
n,s,b
be positive integers where
n
is sufficiently large.
Let
c:=2\max\{a^{1/s},3b/s\}

and let
\overrightarrow{G}
be an
n
-vertex digraph with
e(\overrightarrow{G})\geq cn^{2-1/s}
. Then there is a subset
A
of
V(\overrightarrow{G})
with
|A|=a
such that all
s
-subsets of
A
have at least
b
common outneighbours.

Proof.

Pick
s
vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{s}
(with repetition) at random and let
A^{\prime}
be the common inneighbourhood of all of them.
Then by linearity of expectation and convexity,

\mathbb{E}(|A^{\prime}|)=\sum_{v\in V(\overrightarrow{G})}\left(\frac{d^{+}(v)}{n}\right)^{s}\geq n\left(\frac{cn^{1-1/s}}{n}\right)^{s}=c^{s}.

The expected number of

and have common outneighbourhood of size less than

is at most

{n\choose s}\left(\frac{b}{n}\right)^{s}<\left(\frac{3n}{s}\right)^{s}\left(\frac{b}{n}\right)^{s}=\left(\frac{3b}{s}\right)^{s}.

By linearity of expectation, there exists a choice of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{s}
such that the size of this difference is at least

c^{s}-(3b/s)^{s}\geq a
. Remove one vertex from each
s

that has fewer than

satisfies the properties of the lemma and has size at least

∎

3.3
The lexical colouring

We first observe some simple facts about star-colourings (recall that (i) was stated towards the end of Section

Lemma 3.1

Let

Lemma 3.2

The lexical colouring of

(i)

3.4
A related Ramsey parameter

It is helpful to introduce a further extremal parameter. Let

{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(H):=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\text{ every star-colouring of }K_{n}\text{ contains a rainbow }H\}.

We can use the canonical Ramsey theorem, Theorem

, to determine when

and

Lemma 3.5
.

Let

be a graph. The following are equivalent:

(1)

(2)

is acyclic,

(3)

Proof.

Suppose

exists and is equal to

. Then clearly

does not exist for

. Thus (1) implies (3).
Moreover, (3) implies (2) by Lemma

(ii).
Finally, (2) implies (1) since by Theorem

, any sufficiently large star-coloured

contains a rainbow

or a lexically coloured

(i), each of these contains a rainbow

Thus
{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(H)

∎

Later we will require the following bounds on

for a tree

the path with

Lemma 3.6
.

(i)

For all integers

(ii)

If

Proof.

For (i), consider any star-colouring of a complete graph and take a tournament which is an induced orientation. This tournament has a directed Hamilton path by Rédei’s theorem
[

The lower bound in (ii) is clear since

The upper bound follows from a result of Jamison, Jiang and Ling

who showed that, given any trees

3.5
Inductive lower bounds

The next simple ‘linear separation lemma’ will be useful.
We first extend the definition of star-anti-Ramsey numbers to families of graphs.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a family of graphs, and define

\displaystyle{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,\mathcal{H}):=\max\{s\in\mathbb{N}:\

\displaystyle\text{there exists an }s\text{-star-coloured }K_{n}\text{ without a rainbow copy of }H

\displaystyle\text{for any }H\in\mathcal{H}\}.

Given a graph

, let
\mathcal{J}^{-}_{H}:=\{H-v:v\in V(H)\}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

For all graphs
H
and integers

, we have

{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)\geq n-1+{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n-1,\mathcal{J}^{-}_{H}).

Proof.

Given a star-colouring of

which does not contain any rainbow

we can add a new vertex
w
to form a
K_{n}
. Colouring each edge from
w
with a new colour adds

new colours without creating a rainbow

, since the restriction to
K_{n}-w
would have to contain a rainbow

.
∎

This lemma may be compared to the first inequality in (
1.2
).
It is useful in the following form.

Corollary 3.8
.

For all graphs
H
and integers
n\geq 2
, if
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n-1,\mathcal{J}^{-}_{H})\geq c(n-1)+a
, then
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)\geq(c+1)n+(a-1-c)
.
∎

Another lower bound is the following.

Lemma 3.9
.

For all graphs
H
and integers
n
where
n\geq v(H)
, we have

{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)\geq\binom{v(H)-1}{2}+(\delta(H)-1)(n-v(H)+1).

Proof.

Start with a rainbow
K_{v(H)-1}
. At each stage, take an arbitrary partition of the vertex set into
\delta(H)-1
parts

, add a new vertex

and add
\delta(H)-1
colours which each have centre

. By induction, this colouring contains no rainbow copy of

.
∎

3.6

Our proof of Theorem

Corollary 3.10

We give two explicit families of colourings with

Lemma 4.1
.

For all

, if a star-colouring of

has a rainbow

, then it has a rainbow

Proof.

Assume there is a rainbow

be the star-coloured subgraph induced by its vertices. Orient the edges of

cyclically and orient each of the stars with an edge in

consistenly with this edge, either all edges towards the centre or all away from it, while orienting each of the remaining stars of
\mathscr{G}
either away from its centre or towards it. The resulting orientation of

is strongly connected.

So by Moon’s result, the directed graph contains directed cycles of all lengths from 3 to

\mathscr{G}
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

be an integer, let

Proof ideas

By Corollary

(or Lemma

(iv)), it suffices to show that

First we discuss the ideas of the proof.
We proceed by induction on

.
Consider a star-colouring
\mathscr{G}
of

without a rainbow

and with

For ease of notation, we will write

It is not difficult to show that the statement is true if

Let

Now suppose that (

7
Joins of graphs

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.7
and
1.8
, as well as deriving Corollary
1.10
.

Recall that the
join

G_{1}+G_{2}
of two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is obtained by taking vertex-disjoint copies of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
and adding the edge
x_{1}x_{2}
for every
x_{i}\in V(G_{i})
,
i\in[2]
.
The
internal
edges of
G_{1}+G_{2}
are the ones induced by
G_{1}
or by
G_{2}
.

In this section, we will obtain bounds for
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,T_{1}+T_{2})
for trees
T_{1},T_{2}
.
The upper bounds are in terms of the Zarankiewicz number introduced in Section
2.1
and the parameter
{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(T)
introduced in Section
3.4
.
Known results on Zarankiewicz numbers (Section
2.1
) then imply that for trees
T_{1},T_{2}
where
s:=v(T_{1})
and
T_{2}
is significantly larger than
T_{1}
, these bounds match and we have
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,T_{1}+T_{2})=\Theta(n^{1-1/s})
.

For the upper bounds, we obtain an uncoloured oriented graph by orienting each star from its centre (choosing a centre arbitrarily for single-edge stars) and keeping one arc per star.
If there are enough colours, this graph is sufficiently dense to find a set of vertices such that any small subset has many common outneighbours. We will use dependent random choice for this to prove Theorem
1.8
, while for Theorem
1.7
we find a complete bipartite oriented graph.
There is a rainbow copy
\mathscr{T}_{1}
of the smaller tree in this subset, which may reduce the number of common outneighbours due to colours used by this copy.
The larger tree will be embedded in the common outneighbourhood of the vertices of
\mathscr{T}_{1}
, and none of the colours appearing in this set have been used before by construction.
Recall that
{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(T)
was defined in Section
3.4
.

x_{1},x_{2}
contains the required copy of
K_{2}+T
.
∎

Note that
K_{5}^{-}\cong K_{2}+P_{2}
.
We can use Lemma
7.1
to prove rather tight bounds for
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(K_{5}^{-},n)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

For the lower bound,
we have
{\mathrm{ea}}(K_{5}^{-})\geq 3
by (
3.8
) (in fact there is equality).
By Lemma
3.12
(ii) and (
3.7
), we have
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,K_{5}^{-})\geq{\rm ex}(n,\mathcal{C}_{\leq 5})=(1+o(1))\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)^{3/2}
.

For the upper bound, we have
{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(P_{2})=3
by Lemma
3.6
(i) and
{\rm ex}(m,K_{2,4})=m^{3/2}+O(m^{4/3})
by Theorem
2.1
(iii).
By (
2.1
), we have

z((1+o(1))\tfrac{n}{2},(1+o(1))\tfrac{n}{2};2,4)\leq 4\cdot{\rm ex}((1+o(1))\tfrac{n}{2},K_{2,4})\leq 4(1+o(1))\cdot\left(\tfrac{n}{2}\right)^{3/2}.

The result now follows from Lemma
7.1
.
∎

Next, we prove Theorem
1.8
on the star-anti-Ramsey number of the join
T_{1}+T_{2}
of two trees each on at least three vertices.
The proof of Lemma
7.1
generalises from
K_{2}
to an arbitrary tree
S
to give an upper bound of the order
n^{1-1/{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(S)}
, which is only of the correct order when
{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(S)=v(S)
. Thus we need a slightly different argument in general.

The lower bound comes from a modified lexical colouring. If there is a rainbow copy
\mathscr{H}
of
T_{1}+T_{2}
, then the non-modified part can only contain a forest subgraph, so most of the graph must lie in the modified part.
The key lemma is the following which we will use to quantify this ‘most’.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
T_{1},T_{2}
be trees on
s,t
vertices respectively where
s,t\geq 3
.
Let
F
be a subgraph of
T_{1}+T_{2}
which is a forest. Then
(T_{1}+T_{2}){\setminus}F

either contains an odd cycle or contains
K^{-}_{s,t}
as a subgraph.

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

For the upper bound, let

t^{\prime}:=\max\{{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(T_{1})+1,{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(T_{2})+s-1\}\quad\text{and}\quad c:=\max\{{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(T_{1})^{1/s},3t^{\prime}/s\}.

Suppose we have a star-colouring of
K_{n}
with
cn^{2-1/s}
colours.
Orient each star from its centre (where we choose an arbitrary centre for single-edge stars) and keep one arc of each colour, chosen arbitrarily.
Apply Lemma
2.2
with
{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(T_{1}),t^{\prime}
playing the roles of
a,b
to find a set
A
of size
{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(T_{1})
such that every
s
-subset of
A
has at least
t^{\prime}\geq{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(T_{2})+s-1
common outneighbours.
By the definition of
{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(T_{1})
, there is a copy
T^{\prime}_{1}
of
T_{1}
in
A
, with vertex set
X
, so
|X|=s
.
Let
Y
be the common outneighbourhood of
X
, so
X\cap Y=\emptyset
and
|Y|\geq{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(T_{2})+s-1
. Note that
\mathscr{G}[X,Y]
is rainbow. Delete any
y\in Y
such that there is
xx^{\prime}\in E(T^{\prime}_{1})
with the same colour as
\overrightarrow{xy}
or
\overrightarrow{x^{\prime}y}
.
We delete at most one such
y
for each edge
xx^{\prime}
, so delete at most
e(T^{\prime}_{1})=s-1
vertices
y
in total.
Thus at least
{\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(T_{2})
vertices remain in
Y
.
None of the edges between them use any colours we have already used.
Thus we can find a rainbow
T_{2}
among them which completes the rainbow
T_{1}+T_{2}
.

We obtain the lower bound using Lemma
7.2
.
Let
\mathcal{J}
be the family consisting of
K^{-}_{s,t}
and all odd cycles.
Let
H:=T_{1}+T_{2}
and let
F
be a forest.
Lemma
7.2
implies that
H{\setminus}F

contains some
J\in\mathcal{J}
.
Lemma
3.12
(i) then implies that
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)\geq{\rm ex}(n,\mathcal{J})
.
Since every graph has a bipartite subgraph containing at least half of its edges, we have
{\rm ex}(n,\mathcal{J})\geq{\rm ex}(n,K^{-}_{s,t})/2
, completing the proof.
∎

It remains to prove Lemma
7.2
.

Proof of Lemma
7.2
.

Colour the edges of
F
blue and the edges of
T:=(T_{1}+T_{2})\setminus F
red. For contradiction, assume there is no red odd cycle, no red
K_{s,t}^{-}
and no blue cycle.
We say that
T_{i}
is
mixed
if it contains both red and blue edges. We claim that for
i=1,2
the following holds:

If
T_{i}
is mixed then
T_{3-i}
is not.

(7.1)

Indeed, assume
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are both mixed. Then there are vertices
x_{i},y_{i},z_{i}\in V(T_{i})
such that
x_{i}y_{i}
is blue and
y_{i}z_{i}
is red. As there are no blue cycles, we know that one of
x_{1}y_{2}
,
x_{2}y_{1}
is red, say
x_{1}y_{2}
. As there are no red triangles,
x_{1}z_{2}
is blue, and thus
y_{1}z_{2}
is red, and hence
y_{1}y_{2},z_{1}z_{2}
are blue. As
x_{1}y_{1}y_{2}x_{2}
and
y_{1}y_{2}x_{2}
do not form blue cycles, we see that
x_{1}x_{2},y_{1}x_{2}
are red. Now
x_{1}y_{2}z_{2}y_{1}x_{2}
is a red odd cycle, a contradiction. This proves (
7.1
).
Next, we show that

If
T_{i}
is blue, then
T_{3-i}
is blue.

(7.2)

For this, assume
T_{3-i}
contains a red edge
xy
. Observe that each of
x,y
sends at most one blue edge to
V(T_{i})
, as there are no blue cycles. Since
|V(T_{i})|\geq 3
, it follows that there is a vertex
z\in V(T_{i})
such that
xz,yz
are red. So there is a red triangle
xyz
, a contradiction. This proves (
7.2
).

Observe that if
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are both blue, then at most one edge between
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
is blue, and thus there is a red
K_{s,t}^{-}
, which we assumed was not the case. So by (
7.2
),
T_{i}
is not entirely blue for both
i=1,2
. Hence by (
7.1
), there is an
i\in\{1,2\}
such that
T_{i}
is red. Say
i=1
, i.e. we have

T_{1}\text{ is red}.

(7.3)

Root
T_{1}
at any vertex and let
L_{o}
and
L_{e}
be the union of its odd and even levels respectively (where the root belongs to the even levels). Assume
|L_{e}|\geq|L_{o}|
, the other case in analogous. Then
|L_{e}|\geq 2
and
|L_{o}|\geq 1
.
Let
S_{1}\subseteq V(T_{2})
be the set of all those vertices
x
such that
xy
is blue for all
y\in L_{o}
, and set
S_{2}:=V(T_{2})\setminus S_{1}
. Since there are no red odd cycles, all edges from
S_{2}
to
L_{e}
are blue.
As there are no blue cycles and
|L_{e}|\geq 2
, this means that
|S_{2}|\leq 1
and therefore
|S_{1}|\geq 2
. So again using the fact that there are no blue cycles, we see that
|L_{o}|=1
, and there are no blue edges inside of
S_{1}
.
We have shown that

\displaystyle\text{every edge between $x_{o}$ and $S_{1}$ is blue};

(7.4)

if
S_{2}=\{y\}
, then every edge from
y
to
V(T_{1}){\setminus}\{x_{o}\}=L_{e}

is blue and
yx_{o}
is red.

(7.5)

Suppose
S_{1}
contains a red edge
xz
.
Then one of its endpoints, say
x
, sends only blue edges to
V(T_{1})
, because otherwise
x
and
z
together with a suitable path in
T_{1}
span an odd red cycle, which is not possible.
But then
S_{2}=\emptyset
as otherwise its single vertex lies in a blue
4
-cycle with
x
. Thus the blue graph
F
is a spanning double-star (one star has centre
x_{o}
and leaves
V(T_{2})=S_{1}
, and one star has centre
x
and leaves
V(T_{1}){\setminus}\{x_{o}\}=L_{e}
.
Hence there is a red
K_{s,t}^{-}
, a contradiction.

So we can assume there is no red edge inside
S_{1}
and thus

T_{2}
is a star centred at the only vertex
y
of
S_{2}
.
By (
7.4
) and (
7.5
), there is a blue spanning subgraph consisting of two stars between the parts, and hence there is a red
K_{s,t}^{-}
, a contradiction.
∎

Finally, we can derive Corollary
1.10
.

Proof of Corollary
1.10
.

We know that
1
is star realisable (e.g. by
K_{3}
),
and
3/2
is star realisable by
K_{5}^{-}
(Theorem
1.7
)
so we need to show that
2-1/s
is star realisable for all
s\geq 3
.
Take integers
3\leq s\leq t
where
t>(s-1)!+1
. Then
{\rm ex}(n,K_{s,t}^{-})\geq{\rm ex}(n,K_{s,t-1})\geq c^{\prime}n^{2-1/s}
by Theorem
2.1
(ii) where
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(s,t)
.
Let
T_{1},T_{2}
be trees with
s,t
vertices respectively.
Theorem
1.8
implies that
c^{\prime}n^{2-1/s}\leq{\rm ex}(n,K_{s,t}^{-})\leq{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,T_{1}+T_{2})\leq cn^{2-1/s}
for some constant
c=c(s,t)
.
Thus
2-1/s
is star realisable, as required.
∎

8
Concluding remarks and open problems

8.1
Specific graphs

We recall that Theorem
1.3
of Axenovich and Iverson determines
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)
asymptotically when
{\mathrm{va}}(H)\geq 3
.
We recall that
{\mathrm{va}}(K_{k})\geq 3
when
k\geq 5
, and that
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,K_{3})
follows from work of Erdős, Simonovits and Sós
[
12
]
, while we determined the exact value of
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,K_{4})
(see Theorem
1.5
).
Lemma
3.11
gives a lower bound for cliques on at least
5
vertices.

Problem 8.1
.

What is the exact value of
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,K_{k})
for
k\geq 5
?

Another natural graph to consider is the
3
-dimensional cube
Q_{3}
, which has
{\mathrm{va}}(Q_{3})=2
.
Lemma
3.9
implies that
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,Q_{3})\geq\binom{7}{3}+2(n-7)=2n+21
.
In particular, is it true that
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,Q_{3})
is linear in
n
?

8.2
Linear star-anti-Ramsey number

More generally, one can ask the following.

Problem 8.2
.

For which graphs
H
do we have
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)=\Theta(n)
?

Given an integer
k\geq 3
and a tree
T
, there are constants
c_{k},c_{T}>0
such that for all sufficiently large
n
we have
{\rm ex}(n,\mathcal{C}_{\leq 2k})\geq n^{1+c_{k}/k}
, and
{\rm ex}(n,T)<c_{T}n
.
Thus we can characterise those
H
with linear extremal number:
{\rm ex}(n,H)=\Theta(n)
if and only if
H
is a forest with at least two edges.

Schiermeyer and Soták
[
34
]
proved the corresponding characterisation for the anti-Ramsey problem: if a graph
H
contains at least two cycles, then
{\mathrm{ar}}(n,H)
is superlinear, while if
H
is unicyclic then
{\mathrm{ar}}(n,H)=\Theta(n)
.

The corresponding problem for star-colouring seems harder.
Recall from Theorem
1.3
and Lemma
3.1
that to solve the problem, we need to determine which
H
with
{\mathrm{va}}(H)=2
satisfy
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)=O(n)
.
Every graph
H
with linear star-anti-Ramsey number has edge arboricity at most two:

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
H
be a graph with
{\mathrm{ea}}(H)\geq 3
. Then
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)=\Omega(n^{1+1/(v(H)-1)})
.

Proof.

This follows from Lemma
3.12
(ii), (
3.6
), and the fact that a graph
H
has girth at most
v(H)
.
∎

Since we need only consider
H
with
{\mathrm{va}}(H)=2
, this leaves open the question of determining the magnitude of
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)
when
{\mathrm{ea}}(H)=2
.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
H
be a graph with an edge partition into a star and a forest
F
. Then

n-1\leq{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)\leq({\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(F)-1)n.

Proof.

Since
2={\mathrm{ea}}(H)\geq{\mathrm{va}}(H)
, Lemma
3.1
(ii) implies the lower bound.
Let
a:={\mathrm{n}{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}}(F)
.
Suppose there is a vertex which is the centre of
\geq a
stars and take one arbitrary leaf of each. The graph spanned by these vertices uses disjoint colours from the stars and contains a rainbow copy of
F
, giving the required rainbow copy of
H
.
Thus we may assume that every vertex is the centre of at most
a-1
stars, so
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)\leq(a-1)n
.
∎

Problem 8.5
.

Let
H
be a graph with
{\mathrm{ea}}(H)=2
. Is
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,H)=\Theta(n)
?

Here it would be interesting to better understand the case of 4-regular graphs. Recall that by the tree-packing theorem of Nash-Williams
[
30
]
and Tutte
[
36
]
, a well-connected, in the sense defined in the two papers,
4-regular graph
H
contains two edge-disjoint spanning trees. Hence the graph has
{\mathrm{ea}}(H)=3
, with a partition into two spanning trees and a forest on two edges. So by deleting two edges from
H
we can get a graph
H^{\prime}
with
{\mathrm{ea}}(H^{\prime})=2
. Note that the number of deleted edges does not depend on the size of
H
. In particular it would be interesting to determine
{\mathrm{ar}^{\star}}(n,Q_{4})
.
