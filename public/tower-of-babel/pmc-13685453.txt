Effects of starch structure on the absorption of encapsulated amino acids in laying hens fed low-protein diets
Authors: Zhengkai Yue, Minghui Wang, Min Liu, Xiaojuan Wang, Jingpeng Zhao, Hongchao Jiao, Hai Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLow-protein (LP) diets supplemented with encapsulated crystalline amino acids (CAAs) have been proven to improve amino acid absorption kinetics and post-absorptive amino acid balance. This study investigated the effects of dietary starch structure, expressed as the amylose-to-amylopectin ratio (AAR), on encapsulated amino acid (EAA) utilization and laying performance in hens fed LP diets. A total of 672 healthy 34-wk-old Jingfen laying hens (1534.7 ± 5.4 g) were randomly assigned to seven treatments with eight replicates of 12 hens each for 10 weeks. The dietary treatments included a control (16% crude protein [CP]) diet, a LP diet (13% CP) supplemented with CAAs, the LP diet supplemented with EAAs, and the LP diet supplemented with EAAs containing four AARs (0.128, 0.228, 0.428, and 0.528, respectively). The results showed that, compared with the control, both CAA and EAA diets maintained comparable laying performance (P > 0.05) but reduced body weight gain (P = 0.041). Increasing dietary AAR linearly increased laying rate and egg mass (P < 0.05) and reduced feed conversion ratio (P = 0.005). Compared with CAA, EAA supplementation improved eggshell breaking strength (P = 0.023) and reduced plasma uric acid concentration (P = 0.004), which further declined linearly with increasing AAR (P = 0.018). The AAR0.428 diet significantly increased plasma Lys, Met, and Thr concentrations compared with the control (P < 0.05). Moreover, compared with the control, ileal apparent digestibility of Lys and Thr was higher in the CAA group (P < 0.05), whereas EAA supplementation alone had no significant effect (P > 0.05); however, most amino acid digestibility increased linearly with increasing AAR (P < 0.05) except Cys, Tyr, Phe, His, and Pro. The mRNA expressions of CAT1 and EAAT3 in the jejunum and PepT1 and y+LAT1 in the ileum were elevated (P < 0.05) by AAR0.428 and AAR0.528 treatments compared with the other two AAR groups. In conclusion, LP diets (13% CP) supplemented with CAAs alone could not fully achieve comparable production performance to that of the hens fed a normal-protein diet (16% CP). However, increasing dietary amylose proportion in LP diets enhanced laying performance, egg mass, and body weight gain, suggesting that synchronized glucose and amino acid release improves the efficiency of LP diets. These findings provide a theoretical basis for the precision formulation of LP diets in laying hens.Keywords: Low-protein diet, Amylose-to-amylopectin ratio, Encapsulated amino acid, Laying hens

Introduction

With the availability of industrially synthesized amino acids, the application of low-protein (LP) diet in the poultry industry has become increasingly feasible. Supplementation of LP diets with crystalline amino acids (CAAs) reduces dietary crude protein (CP) levels and nitrogen excretion, thereby mitigating environmental pollution (Wang et al., 2009). However, CAAs differ significantly from protein-bound amino acids in their digestion and absorption kinetics. These differences can result in asynchronous amino acid absorption, leading to imbalances in postabsorptive amino acid availability and consequently impairing metabolic homeostasis and growth performance (Sawadogo et al., 1997; Yen et al., 2004; Wu, 2009; Prandini et al., 2013). In the LP diets, CAAs are rapidly absorbed and catabolized in the proximal small intestine, with a substantial proportion diverted toward energy-yielding oxidation rather than protein synthesis (Zhou et al., 2022). Furthermore, the rapid appearance of free amino acids in circulation increases nitrogen losses in feces, elevates ammonia emissions, compromises animal welfare, and ultimately depresses productive performance (Powers and Angel, 2008; Li et al., 2018). To overcome these limitations, encapsulated CAAs have been developed to delay amino acid release along the gastrointestinal tract, thereby better synchronizing the availability of free amino acids with protein-bound amino acids and glucose. A previous study demonstrated that encapsulated CAAs can resist gastric acid conditions and prolong the release of amino acids in the intestine (Prandini et al., 2013). Compared with crystalline Lys-HCl and protein-bound lysine, microencapsulated Lys reduced dietary protein requirements and synthetic amino acid supplementation in growing-finishing pigs while decreasing fecal nitrogen excretion and maintaining growth performance and carcass quality (Prandini et al., 2013). Similarly, supplementation with encapsulated Lys and DL-Met allowed approximately a 20% reduction in amino acid supplementation without compromising broiler growth performance (Sun et al., 2020). The slow and sustained release of amino acids in the intestinal tract helps maintain stable plasma amino acid concentrations, thereby suppressing protein degradation as well as improving the overall protein retention (He and Giuseppin, 2014).

In LP poultry diets, corn typically constitutes 60% to 70% of the formulation (Iio et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023), making corn starch the primary dietary energy source. Although corn starch is almost completely digested (>95%) by the terminal ileum (Hamaker et al., 2019; Córdova-Noboa et al., 2020), differences in starch structure can substantially influence nutrient metabolism and nitrogen utilization efficiency in livestock and poultry (Zhou et al., 2022). Starch digestion kinetics are closely linked to its molecular structure, particularly the relative proportions of amylose and amylopectin (Svihus, 2014). Amylopectin, characterized by a highly branched, larger enzyme contact surface, is digested more rapidly than amylose, which exhibits a more compact and enzyme-resistant structure (Li et al., 2024). Modulating the dietary amylose-to-amylopectin ratio (AAR) has been shown to reduce amino acid oxidation in the intestinal epithelium and decrease endogenous nitrogen losses, thereby enhancing plasma amino acid availability for whole-body protein synthesis (Ravindran, 2021; Gao et al., 2023). Efficient protein utilization requires synchronization between energy and amino acid availability (Geiger, 1950). Deviations from optimal glucose release rates, whether excessive or insufficient, can impair growth performance and nutrient utilization, as demonstrated in piglets fed LP diets (Zhou et al., 2022). Consistently, supplementation with slow digestible starch has been reported to improve nitrogen utilization and productive performance in both broilers and laying hens (Herwig et al., 2019a, Herwig et al., 2019b; Zhou et al., 2023). These findings suggest that manipulating dietary starch structure may provide a strategy to better coordinate glucose release with amino acid availability, thereby enhancing the utilization efficiency of encapsulated amino acids (EAAs) in LP diets. Therefore, in the present study, it was hypothesized that the absorption and utilization of EAAs in LP diets could be optimized by modifying dietary starch composition.

In the study, the effects of starch AAR and EAA supplementation on laying performance, starch and amino acid digestibility, and metabolic responses in laying hens were evaluated. Specifically, laying performance, plasma biochemical indices, starch digestibility, and nitrogen deposition efficiency were assessed. The findings from the present study highlight the regulatory role of dietary starch structure and amino acid supplementation form in nitrogen utilization, providing a theoretical basis for precision formulation of LP diets in poultry production.

Materials and methods

Animal ethical statement

All animal experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong Agricultural University (Approval No. SDAUA-2024 to 225, approved on Aug. 10, 2024) and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines for animal welfare.

Encapsulated amino acids and starch sources

L-Lys·HCl and L-Thr were obtained from Fufeng Group Ltd., Qingdao, Shandong, China. DL-Met was supplied by Evonik Methionine SEA Pte Ltd. (Singapore) and L-Arg was purchased from CJ Biotechnology Pte Ltd. (Singapore). Encapsulated amino acids were manufactured by Shandong Hemeihua Group Co., Ltd. (Jinan, Shandong, China) using palm stearate and monoglycerides as coating materials. The encapsulated products contained 50% active amino acids, including L-Lys·HCl, Met, Thr, and Arg. High-amylose starch was purchased from Beijing Quanyinxiangyu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), containing 76.79% amylose and 23.21% amylopectin. High-amylopectin starch was obtained from Shanghai Quanwang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), containing 2.75% amylose and 97.25% amylopectin.

Animal management and experimental design

A total of 672 Jingfen No. 6 laying hens aged 34 weeks, with similar body weight (1534.7 ± 5.4 g) and laying rate (90.7 ± 0.6%), were used in this study. The hens were randomly allocated to seven dietary treatments: a corn-soybean meal-based control diet (16% CP; control), a corn-soybean meal-based LP diet (13% CP, AAR0.328) supplemented with CAA or EAA, or the LP diet (13% CP) with different AARs (0.128, 0.228, 0.428, and 0.528, respectively) through the incorporation of high-amylose and high-amylopectin starches.

Crystalline Lys, Met, Thr, and Arg were added to the CAA diet to match the amino acid profile of the control diet, whereas their encapsulated forms were used in the EAA and AAR diets. Each treatment consisted of 8 replicates with 12 hens per replicate. The hens were housed in cages (57 cm × 45 cm × 45 cm), and each replicate had three cages. Each cage had four hens and was equipped with a feed trough (11.25 cm per hen) and a nipple drinker. Ingredient composition and nutrient levels of all experimental diets are presented in Table 1. Dry matter (method 930.15; AOAC 2006) and CP (method 984.13; AOAC 2006) contents were analyzed using standard methods. Diet formulation was based on the China Feed Database (2020), and values for calcium, amino acid digestibility, and available phosphorus were calculated according to China National Standard (2020; GB/T 39235–2020).

Sample collection

Throughout the 10-week experimental period, egg number and egg weight were recorded daily, and feed intake was measured weekly. Initial and final body weights were recorded, and the feed conversion ratio (FCR) was calculated as grams of feed consumed per gram of egg mass produced.

At the end of the experiment, one hen with body weight close to the replicate mean was randomly selected from each replicate. Blood samples were collected from the wing vein under fed conditions, centrifuged at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and plasma was stored at −20 °C for subsequent analyses. Thereafter, the hens were euthanized by exsanguination after cervical dislocation. The intestinal mucosa samples from the mid-duodenum, mid-jejunum, and mid-ileum were collected, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for disaccharidase activity assays. In addition, samples of the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum and muscle tissues (Pectoralis major [PM] and Biceps femoris [BF]) were also collected, snap-frozen, and stored at −80 °C for further analyses.

Nutrient digestibility

One week before the end of the experiment, one hen per replicate was transferred to an individual cage for digestibility measurements. Diets contained 0.5% titanium dioxide (TiO2) as an inert digestibility marker. Following a 3-d adaptation phase, excreta were continuously collected for 3 d and homogenized with 10% H2SO4 (10 mL per 100 g feces) for nitrogen conservation. The excreta samples were stored at −20 °C for subsequent analysis. Thereafter, the distal ileum (approximately 15 cm proximal to the ileocecal junction, excluding the terminal 2 cm) was excised, and the digesta were gently flushed with 0.9% NaCl solution. The ileal digesta were lyophilized (Alpha 1–4 LSCb, Martin Christ Gefriertrocknungsanlagen GmbH, Osterode am Harz, Lower Saxony, Germany) and stored at −20 °C for subsequent analyses.

Amino acids in ileal contents

Freeze-dried ileal digesta samples were ground to a fine powder. Approximately 0.1 g (accurate to 0.0001 g) of each sample was transferred to a hydrolysis bottle for amino acid preparation.

The samples underwent acid hydrolysis with 8 mL of 6 mol/L HCl under sealed vortexing. After flash-freezing (−20 °C, 10 min) and nitrogen purging (2 min), sealed hydrolysis proceeded at 110 ± 10 °C for 22 h. Hydrolysates were transferred to 50-mL volumetric flasks, diluted with ddH2O, and 100-μL aliquots aspirated into 1.5-mL tubes. Following N2-evaporator drying and reconstitution in 1 mL of 0.02 mol/L HCl, solutions were membrane-filtered (0.22 μm) for L-8900 amino acid analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) analysis.

Starch fractions in ileal digesta

Total starch, amylose, and amylopectin contents in ileal digesta were analyzed using a commercial assay kit (BC6105, Beijing Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions (Lu et al., 2025). The amylose was selectively precipitated, while amylopectin and total starch were enzymatically hydrolyzed to glucose. The resulting glucose was quantified spectrophotometrically at 510 nm following oxidation reactions catalyzed by glucose oxidase and peroxidase.

Crude protein in excreta, muscle, and eggs

The excreta samples were dried at 65 °C for 72 h to determine dry matter content and ground through a 1 mm sieve. The dry matter (method 930.15; AOAC, 2006) and CP (method 984.13; AOAC, 2006) were analyzed using standard methods. Muscle and egg samples were freeze-dried for 72 h to constant weight and analyzed for CP content using AOAC (2006) (method 984.13).

Disaccharidase activities

Sucrase, maltase, and lactase activities were analyzed in the intestinal mucosa samples. Tissue specimens (approximately 0.3 g) were homogenized in physiological saline at a 1:9 (g/mL) ratio. Following centrifugation (2500 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), the supernatants were collected for enzymatic assay and cryopreserved at −20 °C. The enzyme activities were analyzed colorimetrically using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Then the protein concentrations were analyzed using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (CoWin Bioscience, Inc., Taizhou, Jiangsu, China).

Egg quality

During the final week of the experiment, all eggs collected over the last three consecutive days were used for egg quality evaluation. Using a Robotmation multi-functional system (EMT-5200, Robotmation Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), egg weight, albumen height, and Haugh units were recorded. Eggshell thickness and strengths were quantified with specialized testers (ETG-1061 and EFG-0503; Robotmation Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), while the egg shape index was measured using a vernier caliper. For each replicate, egg quality parameters were averaged and used for statistical analysis.

Plasma metabolite and free amino acids

Plasma glucose, uric acid, and urea concentrations were analysized using a L-7020 analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Plasma free amino acid profiles were analyzed by ion-exchange chromatography using a L-8900 analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). For analysis, samples underwent 4 °C thawing followed by aliquoting 800 μL into 1.5-mL centrifuge tubes. Then, the plasma samples were thawed at 4 °C, deproteinized with salicylic acid, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 30 min, filtered through 0.22-μm membranes, and analyzed according to standard procedures.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from duodenal, jejunal, and ileal samples using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen Corporation, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using an Eppendorf BioPhotometer (Eppendorf SE, Hamburg, Germany). The quality was verified based on A260/A280 ratios. The complementary DNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (CoWin Bioscience, Inc., Taizhou, Jiangsu, China). Then, the RT-qPCR was performed using an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Foster City, CA, USA). The relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the reference gene. The primer sequences are provided in Table 2. The gene symbols are defined in Table S1.

Results

Laying performance

Compared with the control group, both CAA and EAA treatments significantly reduced body weight gain (P = 0.041) but had no significant effects (P > 0.05) on laying rate, egg mass, or FCR (Table 3). Hence, receiving AAR treatments exhibited laying comparable to that of the control group with respect to body weight gain, feed intake, laying rate, egg mass, and FCR (P > 0.05). However, relative to the CAA group, hens fed the AAR0.428 and AAR0.528 diets showed significantly higher body weight gain, laying rate, and egg mass (P < 0.05). In addition, body weight gain in the AAR0.228 group was also higher than that in the CAA group (P = 0.041).

Plasma metabolites and free amino acids

Plasma glucose and urea concentrations were not significantly affected by dietary treatments (P > 0.05), except that hen fed the AAR0.428 diet showed lower plasma glucose levels compared with those fed the AAR0.128 and AAR0.228 diets (P = 0.043; Table 5). Plasma triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TCHO) concentrations were higher in the CAA, AAR0.228, and AAR0.528 groups than in the AAR0.428 group (P < 0.05). Plasma uric acid (UA) concentrations were significantly lower in the AAR0.228 and AAR0.528 groups compared with CAA and AAR0.128 groups (P = 0.001). In addition, EAA supplementation resulted in lower UA concentrations compared with the CAA group (P = 0.004).

Discussion

Dietary starch structure has been widely recognized as a key determinant of nutrient utilization efficiency in monogastric animals (Martens, 2019; Van Erp, 2019). Previous studies reported that broilers fed moderate-to high-amylose diets exhibited reduced growth performance and inferior feed conversion efficiency, likely due to insufficient glucose availability in the proximal intestine (Gutierrez del Alamo et al., 2009). Acute exposure to high-amylose diets may induce intestinal glucose deficiency, thereby impairing nutrient absorption and utilization (Gutierrez del Alamo et al., 2009). However, inclusion of cassava starch with a relatively high AAR (0.46) in LP diets modulated postprandial glucose and hormone responses in growing pigs, resulting in improved feed intake, weight gain, and post-absorptive nitrogen utilization efficiency (Zhou et al., 2022). Similarly, supplementation of LP diets with microencapsulated amino acids (MAAs) has been shown to reduce dietary amino acid requirements by up to 20% without compromising broiler performance (Sun et al., 2020). The present study evaluated the effects of starch structure on carbohydrate and amino acid metabolism in laying hens fed LP diets supplemented with EAAs. Although laying performance in the CAA- and EAA-supplemented groups did not differ statistically from the control group, both treatments resulted in reduced body weight gain, indicating that amino acid supplementation alone could not fully offset the unfavorable effects induced by dietary protein reduction. The laying performance and body weight in the AAR0.428 and AAR0.528 groups were comparable to those of the control, suggesting that hens fed LP diets supplemented with EAAs may require a higher amylose proportion or lower amylopectin proportion to achieve optimal performance. The interaction between starch digestion rate and intestinal amino acid oxidation is critical for mucosal energy metabolism (Luo et al., 2024). Slowly digestible starch, such as that provided by an intermediate AAR (0.428), likely ensures adequate glucose supply in the mid-to-distal small intestine, thereby reducing the oxidation of intestinal mucosal amino acids for energy (Weurding et al., 2003; Yin et al., 2019) and supporting laying performance and body weight gain. However, this interpretation should be made cautiously, as higher soybean oil inclusion was required to maintain consistent dietary metabolizable energy levels across AAR treatments, and the potential confounding effect of increased dietary oil cannot be completely excluded.

The bioavailability of both glucose and amino acids in the intestine is closely linked to mucosal metabolic processing (Macelline et al., 2022). Differences in the hydrolysis rates of amylose and amylopectin directly affect glucose release dynamics along the gastrointestinal tract. Due to its resistance to enzymatic digestion, amylose produces a more sustained glucose release compared with amylopectin (Jiang et al., 2015). In the present study, reduced expression of SGLT1 and GLUT2 in the jejunum of hens fed the AAR0.528 diet indicated delayed glucose liberation and diminished glucose absorption in the proximal small intestine, consistent with previous findings (Kellett and Helliwell, 2000). However, higher amylose diets increased sucrase activity, tended to increase maltase activity, and upregulated SGLT1 and GLUT2 expression in the ileum, suggesting that amylose supports sustained glucose supply and enhances glucose uptake in the distal small intestine.

Efficient protein synthesis requires synchronized availability of energy and amino acids (Geiger, 1950). While starch hydrolysis and glucose absorption mainly occur in the duodenum and proximal jejunum, protein digestion and amino acid absorption extend further into the jejunum and ileum (Low, 1979). In LP diets, supplementation with CAAs may result in premature amino acid release and absorption, thereby disrupting the synchronization between glucose and amino acid availability and reducing nitrogen retention (Wu et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2022). Plasma uric acid (UA), the primary nitrogenous waste product in poultry, is widely used as an indicator of amino acid utilization efficiency (Whang et al., 2003; Namroud et al., 2008; Donsbough et al., 2010). The observed inverse relationship between plasma UA concentration and AAR suggests that an intermediate amylose proportion (AAR = 0.428) optimizes amino acid utilization and nitrogen retention. This interpretation is further supported by the increased the ATTD of CP and elevated protein content in the PM.

Postprandial plasma amino acid profiles reflect dietary amino acid composition, and are shaped by digestion kinetics, absorption efficiency, and visceral first-pass metabolism (Gloaguen et al., 2012). In the present study, although no significant differences in plasma Lys, Met, Thr, and Arg concentrations were observed between the CAA and EAA groups, the altered levels of Ser, Glu, Val, Ile, and Leu in EAA-fed hens indicate modified amino acid absorption and metabolism. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that encapsulated lysine and DL-Met influence the metabolism of other amino acids (Sun et al., 2020). Compared with protein-bound amino acids, CAAs are absorbed more rapidly (Yen et al., 2004; Morales et al., 2019), whereas encapsulation delays amino acid release, facilitating synchronization with protein-derived amino acids (Piva et al., 2007). The higher ileal apparent digestibility of amino acids in the CAA group compared with the control and EAA groups further demonstrates that amino acid form markedly influences digestion and absorption dynamics.

The plasma amino acids concentrations did not differ substantially between the EAA (AAR = 0.328) and AAR0.428 groups, suggesting that starch structure had limited effects on systemic amino acid profiles. However, this should be interpreted cautiously, as plasma amino acids were analyzed only in the AAR0.428 group among the AAR treatments. The linear increase in amino acid digestibility with increasing AAR indicates that higher amylose content enhances amino acid absorption in LP diets supplemented with EAAs.

Under conditions of high glucose availability, competitive inhibition at sodium-dependent cotransporters may impair amino acid absorption, leading to increased intestinal amino acid loss (Bröer, 2022). This competition predominantly occurs in the proximal small intestine, where amino acid concentrations are highest (Yin et al., 2019). In this study, similar expression levels of SGLT1 and GLUT2 among the control, CAA, and EAA groups suggest that amino acid form did not affect glucose uptake. However, higher expression of SGLT1 and GLUT2 in the duodenum and lower expression in the ileum of the AAR0.128 group compared with AAR0.428 indicates a shift in glucose absorption toward the distal intestine as AAR increased, consistent with reduced total starch digestibility at higher AAR levels. Alterations in amino acid transporter expression further support the role of starch structure in modulating amino acid utilization.

The downregulated jejunal expression of b0,+AT in the CAA group and rBAT in both CAA and EAA groups contrasts with previous findings (Sun et al., 2020) and may indicate localized impairment of bo,+-type amino acid transport in the jejunum (Wang et al., 2009). Within the AAR treatments, increased jejunal expression of CAT1 and EAAT3, elevated ileal expression of Pept1 and y + LAT1, and reduced rBAT expression in the AAR0.428 and AAR0.528 groups indicate enhanced amino acid transport capacity associated with higher amylose content. These changes correspond with the observed increases in ileal amino acid digestibility. With elevated AAR. However, as the CAA group exhibited the highest overall amino acid digestibility, further investigation is required to identify starch structures that optimally match different amino acid forms.

Rapid glucose supply has been shown to impair nitrogen retention (Sun et al., 2022). In this study, higher AAR diets (AAR0.428 and AAR0.528) increased the ATTD of CP compared with AAR0.128. This improvement may be partly attributed to prolonged digestion and fermentation of amylose in the cecum, providing sustained energy for microbial protein synthesis (Regmi et al., 2011; Obianwuna et al., 2022).

However, insufficient distal intestinal energy supply in high-amylopectin diets may limit nitrogen retention. In this study, the increased protein content in PM and BF muscles of EAA-hens compared with the control and CAA groups further indicates that EAA supplementation favors skeletal muscle protein deposition. Moreover, the linear or quadratic responses of muscle protein content to increasing AAR suggest that synchronizing amino acid and glucose release through the combined use of EAAs and higher amylose proportions can mitigate the negative effects of LP diets. Further studies are needed to clarify the temporal and spatial interactions between protein and starch digestion in the intestine.

Previous studies have shown that amylose can slow gastrointestinal transit, prolong calcium solubilization and absorption, and improve eggshell characteristics (Herwig et al., 2019a, Herwig et al., 2019b). However, in the present study, dietary amylose levels did not significantly affect egg quality parameters. Importantly, eggshell quality was improved in the EAA group compared with the CAA group, suggesting that EAAs may positively influence eggshell formation.

Conclusion

Taken together, the results obtained in the present study showed that LP diets (13% CP) supplemented with CAAs alone could not fully achieve production performance comparable to that of laying hens fed a normal-protein diet (16% CP). However, supplementation with EAAs enhanced protein deposition in the PM and BF muscles. Increasing dietary amylose content in LP diets linearly improved laying rate, egg mass, and body weight gain, indicating that starch structure significantly influences amino acid absorption and metabolism. These results suggest that synchronizing the release of amino acids and glucose enhances the utilization efficiency of a LP diet. Hence, this study provides theoretical support for the precision formulation of LP diets in laying hens. Further research is needed to elucidate the spatial and temporal interactions between dietary protein and starch digestion in the intestine and their implications for protein utilization.
