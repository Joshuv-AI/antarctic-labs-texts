Synthesis, characterization and application of new nanodemulsifier in oil industries
Authors: Naeem A. Basheer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The energy production industry is confronted with a multitude of obstacles, particularly within the field of oil production. One such challenge pertains to the formation and stability of oil emulsions. The water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion is stabilized by native crude oil emulsifiers, which migrate and concentrate at the water/oil interface. The water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion is stabilized by native crude oil emulsifiers, which migrate and concentrate at the water/oil interface and form a barrier preventing droplet coalescence. Asphaltenes, resins, waxes, and fine solid particles are natural emulsifiers that stabilize emulsion. Therefore, it is crucial to address the challenges inherent to oil production by employing efficacious techniques to treat emulsions at the earliest stages of oil extraction. This approach can effectively minimize supplementary production costs associated with the transportation and storage of crude oil. The chemical method of demulsification represents the most prevalent approach to emulsion resolution in oil fields. The unique properties of nanoparticles come from their size, surface area, and many other unique physicochemical properties. A large number of nanoparticles have been investigated in demulsification applications for enhanced oil recovery, either alone or in combination with surfactants. This work presents a discussion of the preparation of a new nanocomposite through the interaction of ZrO₂.B₂O₃ nanoparticles with surfactant cationic cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) and evaluates its efficacy as a demulsifier for the separation of water-in-oil emulsions. The structure and morphology of the prepared ZrO₂.B₂O₃-[CTAB] were characterized by XRD, EDS, FESEM, TEM and FT-IR analytical methods. In addition, the effectiveness of the prepared nanocomposite as a demulsifier was tested using the bottle test method. In order to comprehend the mechanism of action of the prepared nanocomposite, the interfacial tension of the crude oil emulsion was measured in the presence and absence of a nanodemulsifier using the Nouy ring method. Additionally, the critical micelle concentration was calculated using the molar conductivity method to determine the minimum concentration required to initiate the aggregation of nanodemulsifier particles. In addition to the semi-experimental determination of the compatibility of the nano-demulsifier for application in the demulsification process, the equilibrium values of the hydrophilic-lipophilic balance were calculated and found to be equal to 9.8, which is deemed suitable for the destabilization of water-in-oil emulsions. Practical measurements demonstrated a gradual decrease in the IFT of the oil/water mixture from 24.22 to 7.56 mN/m in the presence of ZrO₂.B₂O₃-[CTAB]. Moreover, the experimental findings demonstrated that the nanocomposite exhibited optimal demulsification efficiency within a 90-min time frame, 60 °C, and at a concentration of 80 ppm was achieved at 72.4%. The study also investigated the effect of some factors on the demulsification efficiency and identified temperature, settling time, and dosage as influential factors in the process of demulsifying W/O emulsion. A comparative analysis was conducted between the effectiveness of the prepared nanocomposite and the commercial demulsifier (DRCI1286) utilized in Halfaya oil fields.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31152-5.

Introduction

Energy sources are classified into three principal categories: fossil, renewable, and fissile (nuclear). Fossil fuels were formed during the early geological periods. Petroleum and natural gas are the most well-known fossil fuel sources1,2. Shale oil is a complex mixture of various materials consisting of gaseous, liquid, and solid hydrocarbons. The composition of the hydrocarbon mixture is contingent upon the environmental provenance. Consequently, there are instances in which two adjacent wells yield entirely distinct crude oils3,4. The aforementioned components may be deposited during the course of oil production operations at various stages, including drilling, transportation, and surface facilities. The deposition of these materials causes production problems5–7.

The process of oil extraction from wells is ordinarily concurrent with the dissemination of the aqueous phase within the oil phase. This dynamic interplay gives rise to the formation of diverse emulsions. The formation of an emulsion occurs when two or more non-mixable liquids combine to produce a continuous and dispersed phase. In most cases, the phase containing the smaller amount is designated as the dispersed phase, while the phase containing the larger amount is referred to as the continuous phase. However, in instances where the amounts in these phases are comparable, a more complex dynamic may emerge. Alternative parameters can be used to classify continuous and dispersed phases8. Beyond strong mixing, natural emulsifying agents can adsorb at the interface of oil and water, forming a thin layer over dissolved drops and impeding coalescence. This tension between phases is called interfacial tension (IFT). An elevated IFT results in a significantly more stable emulsion9,10. Following this, emulsions are categorized as O/W, W/O, or W/O/W and O/W/O. Oil-in-water emulsions are formed when oil drops are in the internal phase of a water-based mixture, while water drops are in the external phase. This is known as a reversal emulsion. In contrast, W/O emulsions were formed when water drops were in the continuous phase of an oil-based mixture11. The emulsifying properties of emulsions come from solid particles, crude oils, and surface-active composites11,12.

The water-in-oil emulsion is stable, mesostable, entrained water, and unstable. The classification of these emulsions is predicated on their stability. Factors influencing this classification include asphaltenes, resin, the density and viscosity of the initial oil, elasticity, and rheological properties. The appearance of the emulsions has also been analyzed using over 400 oil samples and petroleum products, and it is concluded that only stable and mesostable water-in-oil emulsions are classified as emulsions13,14. These categories are distinguished by time of separation under laboratory conditions, stability, and appearance14. Stable water-in-oil emulsions are composed of an aqueous phase, an oil phase, and an emulsifying agent.The emulsifying agent that exists at the oil interphase is commonly known as particles or surfactants.This agent facilitates the formation of a stabilized water-in-oil emulsion15.

The emulsions cause operational and economic issues. Crude oil must be treated and water separated7,16,17. Moreover, emulsions often form during extraction. Specific chemical compounds in the oil mixture cause emulsion formation and stabilization.Further, more water and less heat make W/O emulsions more viscous18,19. The formation of emulsions is significantly influenced by the presence of surfactants, ionic components, the pH of the water, and other factors. Indeed, in instances where emulsions are designed to remain stable for long periods of time, tight control of these parameters is necessary to prevent emulsion destabilization mechanisms such as flocculation, creaming, ostwald ripening, and coalescence20. In addition, oil emulsions form during crude oil production , which includes drilling, transportation, and petroleum treatment. Understanding mechanisms and properties of emulsions is essential for controlling and improving production21–23.

The challenges of emulsification attracted much attention, especially in the petroleum industry.Current research focuses on demulsification methods.Demulsification is a process that results in the separation of the emulsion into two distinct phases. There are three demulsification methods: chemical, physical, and biological. These methodologies are effective because they reduce the emulsion’s instability until the two phases separate24. Chemical demulsifiers are the most cost-effective and prevalent of the available methods4. Many patents and articles examined the feasibility of various chemical substances for the destabilization of emulsions in the petroleum industry. The development of effective demulsifier chemicals for the separation of W/O emulsions depends on specific procedures, including adding a sufficient amount of a properly selected chemical to the emulsion and ensuring it’s thoroughly incorporated through mixing25. In the same context, deemulsification processes are recognized as being somewhat complex. This complexity is primarily attributable to the multifaceted nature of demulsification knowledge, encompassing the disciplines of colloid chemistry, surfactant science, and interfacial phenomena24. Surfactants play a pivotal role in the treatment of oil emulsions, typically by means of interfacial tension reduction. The interfacial tension can be defined as the attractive force between the molecules existing at the interface of two fluids26. In water-oil-surfactant systems, the presence of surfactant molecules leads to a replacement of some oil and water molecules at the original oil-water interface through surfactant adsorption. The surfactant’s hydrophobic and hydrophilic components interact with oil and water, reducing interfacial tension27.

Surfactants were used to separate crude oil emulsions because of their capacity to disperse in both the water and oil phases. Beyond their ability to adsorb to surfaces, these compounds modify the characteristics of the interfacial film, altering its properties and facilitating emulsion separation28. Surfactants are surface-active agents that reduce interfacial tension in solutions29. These compounds are usually organic, with a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail.The charge on the hydrophilic head distinguishes four types of surfactants. These classifications include non-ionic, anionic, cationic, and zwitterionic30. Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) is an example of a cationic surfactant. Its efficacy in enhancing oil recovery has been demonstrated in numerous studies. The mechanisms responsible for this improvement of oil recovery include the ability of CTAB to reduce the oil–water interfacial tension31. Cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) is a cationic surfactant that is suitable for a wide range of applications, including scattering, self-assembly, and pore-making32. On the other hand, a substantial body of research has indicated that surfactant-modified nanoparticles have the capacity to demulsify crude oil emulsions33.

Nanoscience has caused a shift in modern science. The design of nanomaterials is important in several applications34–37. The petroleum and natural gas industry can use nanotechnology to address oilfield development difficulties. Research has documented the transition from theory to practice in the field of oil improvement through nanotechnology38–40.

Nanoparticles (NPs) are particles with a diameter smaller than 100 nm41. These particles possess unique properties, including a large surface area and the capacity for surface modification. Their performance is contingent on factors such as composition, synthesis method, dimensions, and surface structures.NPs are effective at demulsification because they converge towards asphaltenes and are positioned at the oil–water interface. Another approach is to attach NPs permanently to a supporting matrix, resulting in nanocomposites. Another approach involves the permanent attachment of functional NPs to a supporting matrix, resulting in nanocomposites. In comparison with NPs, nanocomposites exhibit higher mechanical stability, processability, and some advantages caused by NP-matrix interactions. In addition, the NPs were demonstrated to accelerate the demulsification process by adsorbing at the interfacial surfaces of emulsion droplets42,43. The interfacial activity exhibited by NPs is a hallmark of their functionality, which is typically introduced through reactions that introduce specific functional groups44. A plethora of studies have demonstrated the efficacy of a wide variety of NPs in enhanced oil recovery. These NPs include magnesium oxide, aluminum oxide, zinc oxide, zirconium oxide, tin oxide, iron oxide, nickel oxide, hydrophobic silicon oxide, and silicon oxide treated with silane45.

Zirconium(IV) oxide (ZrO₂) NPs have high hardness, high melting point, chemical stability, density, wear resistance, relative corrosion resistance, insulation, and mechanical strength. This oxide’s microstructure is highly uniform46,47. Zirconia crystal phases are divided into three categories: monoclinic, tetragonal, and cubic. Studies show zirconia’s toughness increases after transforming from tetragonal to monoclinic43,48. Furthermore, the interfacial tensions of different systems containing both surfactants and ZrO₂ NPs were studied.Anionic, cationic (CTAB), and nonionic surfactants effectively decrease the interfacial tension of emulsions. However, negatively charged ZrO₂ NPs significantly alter the surface activity of the selected surfactants. ZrO₂ NPs enhance surfactant activity and reduce interfacial tension.Consequently, nanocomposite offers potential applications in demulsification processes49,50. Similarly, oxide (B₂O₃) was shown to form a network structure in materials, a property that has attracted the attention of researchers. This interest is due to its distinctive characteristics, including a low melting point, high chemical durability, transparency for a broad range of wavelengths, and good thermal stability.A notable phenomenon is the transformation of triangle (BO3) units into tetrahedral (BO4) units.Moreover, the combination of B2O3 with ZrO2 NPs has been demonstrated to enhance mechanical strength, thermal stability, chemical, and electrical resistivity51. The motivation for this study was to synthesize and analyze a novel nanocomposite that incorporates a cationic surfactant (CTAB) and NPs of ZrO₂.B₂O₃ and is tested as a water-in-oil demulsifier. Furthermore, to the best of my knowledge, there is a paucity of research that has addressed the use of hybrid nanooxides with cationic surfactants. Consequently, this study was conducted to address this gap.

Synthesis and application of surfactant anchored onto nanoparticles in demulsification processes (water-in-oil emulsions) have been investigated by many researchers. Feng, Xiao-Jun, et al.52 synthesized polydopamine coating on nanoparticles surfaces (Fe₃O₄@PDA) and anchored it with three commercial polyether demulsifiers (AE1910, SP169, and AR321) to test its effectiveness in dewatering water-in-oil (W/O) emulsions. The three kinds of nanocomposites exhibited high demulsification efficiency.The findings indicated the presence of Fe₃O₄@PDA@AE1910 resulted in a low interfacial tension and rapid adsorptive behavior at the oil–water interface. In the study by Chen, Yuning, et al.53 the efficacy of nanocomposites in demulsification was examined, with a particular focus on the rapid removal of water-in-oil emulsions.The ferroferric oxide (Fe₃O₄) particles were coated with amorphous silicon dioxide (SiO₂) and subsequently modified with a commercial dodecyltrimethoxysilane solution (KH-1231). This method has been shown to accelerate the demulsification process, with the efficiency and rate of this process contingent upon the amount of demulsifier utilized, attaining a plateau upon the attainment of a critical level. The addition of further demulsifier had a negligible effect on the process. Huang, Zhiming, et al. Huang, Zhiming, et al.46 published a study on novel environmentally friendly and interfacially active carbon nanotubes/SiO₂ nanomaterials (CNTs/SiO₂) for emulsified crude oil separation prepared via grafting nano-SiO₂ on the surface of oxidized carbon nanotubes (Ox-CNTs). The study concluded that a new type of environmentally friendly and interfacially active CNT/SiO₂ composite nanomaterial that exhibits good interfacial activity and high demulsification efficiency has been synthesized using commercially available raw materials. It was determined that the concentration and sedimentation time of these nanomaterials can improve demulsification. The demulsification efficiency was 87.40%.Hadi, Haider Ahmed, et al.54 investigated the utilization of three kinds of demulsifiers, namely polyethylene glycol (PEG), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and cetyltrimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB), with aluminum oxide (Al₂O₃) NPs. The maximum efficiency improved within the range of 88–90% of water separation from oil was obtained with the use of a blend of surfactants with Al₂O₃ at optimum values of parameters. Javadian, Soheila, et al.55 published a study on the demulsification of water in an oil emulsion by surface-modified SiO₂ nanoparticles, in which SiO₂ NPs were used for the dehydration of crude oil. To enhance the NPs’ hydrophobicity and hydrophilicity, they were modified with oleic acid (OA) and sodium dodecyl benzene sulfonate (SDBS), respectively. A GO-SiO₂ nanocomposite was synthesized using graphene oxide (GO) to create a superhydrophilic demulsifier. The bottle test results showed the GO-SiO₂ nanocomposite performed best, with the oil being dehydrated within 90 min.

The present study’s innovation lies in the synthesis of the nanocomposite ZrO₂.B₂O₃-[CTAB], which has not been previously prepared. The method for preparing modified NPs is both effective and economical. Furthermore, the crude oil samples from the Halfaya field were employed directly, without the preparation of W/O emulsions. The objective of conducting tests related to separation efficiency was to evaluate the prepared nanocomposite as a demulsifier, utilizing state-of-the-art spectroscopic and analytical techniques for characterization. This encompasses a comparative analysis of the results obtained with a commercial demulsifier, as well as an examination of the impact of certain parameters on separation efficiency.

Materials and methods

All chemical substances originating from Merck/ Germany were used without additional purification in this study. Ethyl alcohol (CH3CH2OH, 99%, CAS Number: 64-17-5), cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) (C19H42BrN, 98%, CAS Number: 64-19-7), zirconium acetate (Zr(CH3COO)4, 99%, CAS Number: 7585-20-8), acetic acid (CH3COOH, 99%, CAS Number: 64-19-7), urea (NH2CONH2, 99%, CASNumber: 57-13-6), and boric acid (H3BO3, 98.5%, CAS Number: 10043-35-3).

Preparation of nanoparticles ZrO2.B2O3

The modified approach was employed for the hybrid oxide manufacture of ZrO₂·B₂O₃ NPs. The aqueous solution consists of zirconium acetate (0.05 M) in 100 mL of deionized water (DI), with the incorporation of urea (1 M) under magnetic stirring. The acidity of the solution should be adjusted to a pH value of 4.0 by the addition of glacial acetic acid. Subsequently, the combination was subjected to hydrothermal treatment for a period of 12 h at 80 °C.Then, the white precipitate Zr(OH)₄ was washed with DI and dehydrated at 60 °C. After that, the zirconium hydroxide precipitate was subjected to calcination for a duration of one hour at 250 °C in air to produce nanocrystals of zirconium oxide (ZrO₂). Subsequently, the ZrO₂ prepared in the preceding step and H₃BO₃ were combined in a mass ratio of 1:3 in DI and stirred for a period of 3–6 h. The precipitate was washed and dried at 50 °C for 15 h, yielding ZrO₂. B2O356.

Preparation nanocomposite ZrO2.B2O3- [CTAB]

ZrO₂.B₂O₃-[CTAB] nanocomposite was prepared by mixing cetyltrimethyl bromide (CTAB) with ZrO₂.B₂O₃ in a mass ratio of 1:1 in 100 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, a 25% ammonium solution was added until the precipitate was formed, and the mixture was treated with ultrasonication for 30 min. Then, the mixture was subjected to continuous stirring for a duration of 2 h.The precipitate was then filtered, thoroughly washed with deionized water, and dried under vacuum57 , as illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig.1Steps of preparation of the nanocomposite ZrO2.B2O3- [CTAB].

Diagnostic techniques

The present investigation elucidates the distinctive attributes of NPs through the utilization of diverse analytical and optical methodologies. The prepared ZrO2.B2O3 NPs were examined using XRD , providing valuable insight about the crystal structure of solids, the size of crystalline grains, and particle size. An examination was conducted on NPs and nanocomposites using the XRD (PW1730, Philips, Netherlands) within a specified 2θ range of 10 to 79.9, employing Cu Kα radiation. To confirm the existence of elements in prepared materials, an energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) (INCAPentaFET- × 3, Oxford Instruments ,UK) scan was also done. Moreover, the morphology of the prepared nanocomposites was verified by the TEM (JEM-2100,JEOL,USA). Also, by examining FE-SEM (MIRA3,TESCAN,Czech), its structure, size, and regularity were realized. In addition, FT-IR (IRTracer-100 ,Shimadzu,Japan) is used to determine the atomic vibrations and their absorption within the infrared spectrum region of the prepared compounds.

Bottle tests

The bottle test was employed for the purpose of evaluating the efficacy of the demulsifier in the demulsification process under a variety of conditions.This technique has been demonstrated to be especially beneficial in the context of oil industry applications involving the use of demulsifiers for enhancing reliability. Firstly, the ZrO₂·B₂O₃-[CTAB] was combined with an organic solvent (toluene 2 mL) to create a demulsifier. Then, the required concentrations (50, 60, 70, 80, 90, and 100 ppm) of the nanodemulsifier solution were prepared and added to 10 mL of crude oil in the bottle test. Thereafter, the samples were placed in a water bath, and the readings were recorded at designated intervals and temperatures (30 °C and 60 °C). These variables were selected based on their potential impact on the observed outcomes. Moreover, the separation efficiency of the nanocomposite prepared experimentally was compared with that of the standard demulsifier (DRCI1286), which is employed for the treatment of petroleum emulsions in the Halfaya oil fields in Iraq, from which the study samples were collected.

Interfacial tension measurement (IFT)

The Du Nouy ring method is one of the methods for measuring surface tension in liquids. This apparatus consists of a precision-machined platinum/iridium ring that is lowered into the liquid, placed in a glass container, and gradually withdrawn. The weight of the ring increases due to the added weight of the liquid adhering to it, and the maximum vertical force increase is a measure of the surface tension20. The water/oil interfacial tension (IFT) was measured in the presence and absence of the nanodemulsifier ZrO₂·B₂O₃-[CTAB] by means of the ring tensiometer (KRUSS K12, Germany) method.The interfacial activity of the nanodemulsifier was evaluated by comparing the IFT of water separated after demulsification with oil and the IFT of distilled water/crude oil. All experiments were conducted at a temperature of 25.0 ± 0.1 °C, and a series of measurements were performed on the nanodemulsifier ZrO₂.B₂O₃-[CTAB] at 10 different concentrations.

The term “micelle” refers to an aggregate form of surfactant molecules dispersed in a liquid colloid. The critical micelle concentration (CMC) is the concentration at which micelle formation begins. At the CMC, many physicochemical properties of the solution (e.g., thermal and electrical conductivities, viscosity, and surface tension) vary abruptly66. In general, the selection of surfactants for the preparation of O/W or W/O emulsions is frequently made on an empirical basis. One such semi-empirical scale for selecting surfactants is the Hydrophilic-Lipophilic Balance (HLB) number, which was developed by William Griffin. This scale is predicated on the relative percentage of hydrophilic to lipophilic (hydrophobic) groups in the surfactants. In the context of a W/O emulsion, the hydrophilic groups are localized within the water droplet, while the lipophilic groups are situated within the hydrocarbon phase. The HLB number of the system is a critical factor in determining its behavior70. The emulsifiers that will stabilize O/W emulsions will most likely demulsify W/O emulsions of similar physicochemical parameters, and vice versa. For W/O emulsions that are stabilized by low HLB surfactants, the design and formulation of their demulsifiers must target high HLB surfactants71. Recent research has focused on the investigation of ionic and nonionic demulsifiers. The findings of these studies have indicated that the presence of increased hydrophilic components within demulsifiers can result in enhanced separation of water from oil. Effect of some factors on the separation efficiency of ZrO₂.B₂O₃- [CTAB]

Concentration

It has been demonstrated in numerous studies that the presence of high concentrations of emulsifiers necessitates the employment of correspondingly high concentrations of demulsifiers. Consequently, the quantity of demulsifier required may be insufficient, while an excess may enhance the stability of the emulsion. However, the HLB of the substances and the molecular weight of the demulsifier determine the optimal dose for disrupting the emulsion. In this context, the combination of alkali and asphaltenes in a W/O emulsion requires a reduced concentration of a hydrophilic demulsifier.Moreover, the dimensions of the separation vessel also influence the optimal dosage of emulsion separation. To illustrate, increasing the layer height in a coalescer separator increases the efficiency of oil emulsion disruption. In this study, a range of concentrations of the ZrO₂.B₂O₃-[CTAB] demulsifier were tested on the crude oil emulsion. It was noted from the experimental results that the separation efficiency of the prepared demulsifier demonstrates a proportional change with increasing. Furthermore, the optimal separation value is determined by the maximum effectiveness of the dosage at a specific concentration, even if the concentration and separation time are lower.

Temperature

Temperature has a significant impact on the physical attributes of a given system. To illustrate, the temperature and other thermodynamic parameters exert a marked effect on the solubility of the emulsifying agent and the properties of the water–oil interface. Moreover, temperature has a significant effect on viscosity. Additionally, elevated temperatures can accelerate the destabilization of emulsions, thereby increasing the frequency of impacts between water droplets74. Consequently, bottle tests were conducted at 30 °C and 60 °C in this study to determine the most effective concentration of the nanodemulsifier and separation efficiency. Figures 8 and 9 depict the results of demulsification at two distinct temperatures, 30°C and 60°C, respectively. It was observed that the volume of water isolated from the oil emulsion samples increased in the presence of the ZrO2.B2O3-[CTAB] with rising temperature. The effect of temperature on emulsion destabilization is a crucial factor. The separation efficiency effects of the ZrO2.B2O3-[CTAB] can be attributed to a number of factors, including: As the temperature increased, the discrepancies between the viscosity and density of the W/O phase began to diverge. Furthermore, droplet collisions and reduced oil viscosity weaken stabilizing chemicals at the droplet level, increasing the likelihood of settling.

Time

In order to facilitate the study of emulsion flow in oil facilities, it is essential to utilize emulsions that demonstrate minimal alteration in their intrinsic properties, such as viscosity and stability, over time. The bottle test was employed to assess the efficacy of ZrO2.B2O3-[CTAB] in the separation of emulsions and to ascertain the impact of the time factor on this efficacy.The quantity of resolved water is an optimal indicator of emulsion stability, as the coalescence of the droplet phase represents the limiting step in the destabilization process. The findings pertaining to the efficacy of the prepared demulsifier indicate that as the separation time increases, the quantity of separated water also rises, leading to demulsifications. This phenomenon can be attributed to the diffusion of the demulsifier molecules and the disruption of the film that surrounds the droplets, which allows for the separation of the two phases.

Suggested demulsification mechanism

In recent times, a considerable body of research has been undertaken to examine the adsorption behavior of nanocomposites as surfactants in emulsion media. However, the mixing of the nanoparticles and the surfactant remains unaltered by their presence on the surface, which consequently results in the aggregation of the particles. The incorporation of nanoparticles into the prepared demulsifier enhances the efficiency of the separation process and modifies the internal surface tension of the emulsion. According to the experimental findings on interfacial tension (IFT), the observed reduction in IFT signifies the disruption of the interfacial film of the emulsion by the nano-demulsifier. This phenomenon consequently enables the separation of the two phases. Surfactant cationic cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), which has one head and a linear tail (Fig. 8S2). The organic compound CTAB with a carbon chain facilitates the migration of the nanoparticle to the water/oil interface at a faster rate, thereby demonstrating that the nanocomposite markedly reduces the interfacial tension between oil and water. The incorporation of a minimal quantity of ZrO2.B2O3-CTAB has been demonstrated to elicit a decline in the interfacial tension of the emulsion. The dispersion of ZrO2.B2O3 is stabilized through the adsorption of the surfactant (CTAB) to the particles, forming monolayers. The quantity of nanocomposite adsorbed is observed to increase with an increase in the concentration of surfactant, until the critical micelle concentration (CMC) is reached. On the one hand, adsorption of CTAB on the surface of the NPs enhances the hydrophilicity of NPs. Furthermore, the combination of ZrO2.B2O3 and cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide has been demonstrated to exhibit the most effective performance in the demulsification of W/O. This is due to the presence of multiple hydrophilic groups provided by inorganic nanoparticles bearing negative charges, including oxygen, borates, zirconium oxide, and the head group in the compound, which exhibits the capacity to form bonds with water molecules.

Conclusions

In this study, the novel nanocomposite ZrO2.B2O3-[CTAB] was prepared and characterized employing a range of analytical techniques. Furthermore, the efficacy of the nanocomposite was evaluated as a demulsifier for water/oil emulsions. The results confirmed that the separation efficiency improved in the presence of nanoparticles, which further increased the absorption of the prepared nano-demulsifier. Additionally, the results of the conductivity measurements, which were employed to ascertain the critical micelle concentration (CMC), indicated a positive correlation between the efficacy of the nanodemulsifier and a decline in these values. Moreover, the hydrophilic-lipophilic balance (HLB) was calculated to determine the theoretical preference for the utilization of the demulsifier with the appropriate type of emulsifier. The findings of this study have confirmed that the nanocomposite ZrO2.B2O3-[CTAB] has the ability to destabilize a W/O emulsion to separate the two phases. Nevertheless, further investigation is necessary to ascertain the influence of additional variables on the efficiency of the separation process. This research investigates the impact of a number of factors on the rate of destabilization of the emulsion. The factors that were found to influence the efficiency of separation in this study were concentration, temperature, and time. These were determined by calculating the ratio of the volume of separated water in the sample to the total volume of water. It is important to note that an increase in concentration or separation time does not necessarily result in enhanced effectiveness in breaking water-in-oil emulsions. At a certain point, the reduction in interphase surface tension and interphase film thickness reaches its maximum potential. This is corroborated by the experimental results obtained. The IFT of the oil/water mixture gradually decreased from 24.22 to 7.56 mN/m with increasing concentrations of ZrO2.B2O3- [CTAB] in this study. The nanodemulsifier has been demonstrated to soften the oil–water interface, which is stabilized by the surface-active components present in the crude oil. This effect leads to accelerated coalescence of water droplets, resulting in a more rapid separation of oil and water. The study’s findings are significant for the oil industry, showing the potential of ZrO₂.B₂O₃-[CTAB] to enhance demulsification performance while reducing environmental impact. Future research should explore the influence of different ZrO₂.B₂O₃-[CTAB] properties as well as the effects of varying temperature conditions and industrial-scale application.
