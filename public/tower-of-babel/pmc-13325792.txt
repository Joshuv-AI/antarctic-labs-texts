The impact of structured training on the management of agitated pediatric patients in the emergency department: changes in knowledge and self-efficacy perceptions of physicians
Authors: Tugce Nalbant, Yüksel Bıcılıoglu, Gamze Gokalp, Gonca Özyurt, Yeşim Ece Ünsal, Gizem Buket Yayla Coşgun, Esin Ergonul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren and adolescents presenting with acute behavioral agitation are increasingly admitted to pediatric emergency departments. Agitation management requires a structured and multidisciplinary approach that integrates effective communication strategies with pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. However, evidence-based guidance in this field remains limited, and healthcare professionals’ knowledge and training levels are heterogeneous.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the impact of a structured training program on pediatric and child psychiatry residents’ knowledge and perceived self-efficacy in the management of agitation.MethodsThis quasi-experimental educational study was conducted in a tertiary pediatric emergency department between January and August 2025. A total of 39 physicians, including pediatric residents, pediatric emergency medicine fellows, and child psychiatry residents, participated in a training program developed using the ADDIE instructional design model. The program combined theoretical instruction with case-based workshops focusing on verbal de-escalation, pharmacological management, and ethical application of physical restraint. Knowledge and self-efficacy were assessed before and after the intervention using structured questionnaires.ResultsThirty-nine physicians completed the study. Post-test knowledge scores were significantly higher than pre-test scores (Z = − 3.56; p < 0.001; r = 0.56), and score improvement was observed in 64.1% of participants. No significant association was found between professional experience and pre-test or post-test scores; however, a significant negative correlation was identified between professional experience and score difference (post-test – pre-test) (r = − 0.480; p = 0.002). This finding indicates that participants with lower baseline knowledge levels achieved greater gains from the training.ConclusionsStructured, case-based, and multidisciplinary training significantly enhances physicians’ knowledge and self-efficacy in managing agitated pediatric patients in emergency settings. These findings demonstrate that systematically designed, institution-specific training programs can support physician competency in pediatric emergency departments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-06972-7.Keywords: Pediatric agitation, Multidisciplinary training, Pediatric emergency department, Behavioral crisis, Physician education, Self-efficacy

Introduction

Agitation is a condition characterized by heightened excitability, disruption of normal functions, and excessive verbal and motor behaviors [1]. In pediatric emergency departments, agitation can pose a serious safety risk for both patients and healthcare professionals. Therefore, the management of agitated patients should not only involve prompt and effective interventions but also be guided by a humane approach that considers the patient’s age, developmental level, and psychosocial status. Emergency care teams must ensure adequate preparation for such situations. Management should be individualized, multidisciplinary, and based on collaboration [2]. The cooperation of pediatrics, pediatric emergency medicine, and child psychiatry disciplines plays a critical role in diagnosis and treatment planning [3].

In the literature, the management of agitated pediatric patients is recommended to involve structured algorithms, effective communication techniques, and the combined use of pharmacological and non-pharmacological strategies [4, 5]. However, randomized controlled trials or guideline recommendations based on expert consensus regarding the methods, medications, and their efficacy and safety in this field remain limited. Current evidence suggests first identifying the underlying causes of agitation, then implementing non-pharmacological stress-reduction strategies, and only selecting medication appropriate for the patient’s specific condition when necessary. Physical restraint should be considered as a last resort and avoided whenever possible. Nevertheless, due to the unique conditions of pediatric emergency departments, pharmacological treatment and physical restraint may be initiated earlier, particularly in units lacking child psychiatry support or adequately trained personnel. It has also been reported that healthcare professionals’ knowledge and attitudes on this issue are heterogeneous and that they often lack sufficient educational background [2, 6].

In this context, targeted training programs for healthcare professionals in pediatric emergency departments have been shown to improve safety and enhance the quality of clinical practice in the management of agitated patients [6]. This study aimed to assess the impact of a structured training program for pediatric and child psychiatry residents on their knowledge and perceived self-efficacy in managing agitation.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This study was designed as quasi-experimental, intervention-based educational research conducted in the Pediatric Emergency Department of Izmir City Hospital. The aim of the study was to improve physicians’ knowledge and perceived self-efficacy in the management of agitated pediatric patients.

The study was approved by the İzmir Katip Çelebi University Ethics Committee (Approval No: 0239, dated 15.05.2025). All procedures were executed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki and national research ethics standards.

The training program was developed over a three-month preparation period (January–March 2025), incorporating the analysis, design, and development phases. Implementation was completed in April 2025, and the evaluation process was conducted between May and August 2025.

The training program was structured based on the ADDIE instructional design model (Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, and Evaluation) [7].

The instructional design and implementation of the program were led by a faculty member from the Department of Medical Education who holds a master’s degree in medical education and has prior experience applying the ADDIE model in curriculum development and structured training programs.

Analysis phase

Through needs assessment discussions conducted with pediatric emergency and child psychiatry residents, knowledge and practice gaps were identified in the following areas:

* Assessment of agitation severity.

* Differential diagnosis.

* Verbal de-escalation techniques.

* Pharmacological treatment options.

* Safe physical restraint practices.

In addition, current literature and international guidelines on pediatric agitation management were reviewed during this phase.

Design phase

Based on the identified needs, learning objectives were defined, and a structured training program was designed to strengthen clinical decision-making and perceived self-efficacy. A draft institutional agitation management program tailored to the departmental workflow was developed during this phase.

Development phase

A multidisciplinary academic team prepared:

* Educational slide materials.

* Case-based discussion scenarios.

* An institution-specific agitation management algorithm.

All case examples were fully de-identified. The draft program was piloted with a small group of physicians, and minor revisions were made based on feedback to improve clarity and flow.

Implementation phase

The program was delivered in April 2025 in a face-to-face workshop format.

Structure and content of the training program

The training program consisted of five sessions, each lasting approximately 20 min, with five-minute coffee breaks between sessions, and was completed in a single day.

The sessions covered the following topics:

Definition and clinical characteristics of agitation, severity grading, and differential diagnosis.

Verbal de-escalation strategies.

Verbal de-escalation strategies.

Principles of pharmacological management.

Indications, monitoring, and ethical considerations of physical restraint.

Presentation of the institutional agitation management algorithm.

Presentation of the institutional agitation management algorithm.

Sessions included interactive question-and-answer components and brief case-based discussions. The workshop component involved applied discussions based on clinical scenarios involving moderately and severely agitated patients. This approach aimed to enhance participants’ perceived self-efficacy in clinical decision-making.

Evaluation phase

Knowledge level and perceived self-efficacy were assessed using a structured questionnaire developed by the researchers (The questionnaire is available in Appendix 1).

The instrument included:

* Multiple-choice knowledge questions (twelve questions).

* Self-efficacy and confidence statements rated on a 5-point Likert scale (four questions).

Assessments were conducted at two time points: pre-training (T1) and post-training (T2). The content remained identical in both administrations; however, the order of questions and response options was modified.

Participants and recruitment

Physicians involved in the management of agitated pediatric patients were invited to participate through a closed, institution-based professional WhatsApp communication group that included only physicians working in the Pediatric Emergency Department.

The purpose of the study and the voluntary nature of participation were announced through this group. Physicians who expressed interest were provided with an informed consent form.

A separate WhatsApp group was created for those who agreed to participate. At the beginning of the face-to-face training session, a secure Google Forms link containing the pre-test questionnaire was shared with this group. The post-test link was distributed through the same group immediately following completion of the training.

A total of 43 physicians participated in the training; four participants who did not complete the training or the post-test were excluded. Consequently, 39 physicians who completed the training in full were included in the study, representing 47.1% of physicians managing agitated pediatric patients in the Pediatric Emergency Department.

Participants were categorized according to their field of practice:

* Group 1: Pediatric Emergency Medicine fellows.

* Group 2: Child Psychiatry residents.

* Group 3: Pediatrics residents.

All responses were collected anonymously via Google Forms. No identifying personal data were collected. Data were stored on a password-protected institutional computer accessible only to the researchers.

All cases used during the training were fully de-identified. As no identifiable patient information was used, individual patient or parental consent was not required.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27. Descriptive statistics were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. The distribution of variables was examined with the Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests. The assumption of normality was not satisfied, so nonparametric tests were applied. Because participants’ confidence levels were assessed pre- and post-training using a 5-point Likert scale in a paired data structure, differences between the two time points were analyzed with the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test. To estimate the magnitude of change, effect sizes (r) were calculated for each variable, defined as the test statistic Z divided by the square root of the sample size (r = Z / √N).

Differences in pre- and post-test scores among specialties (pediatric emergency medicine fellows, child psychiatry and pediatric residents) were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Bonferroni post-hoc analysis was performed to determine specific group differences following significant results in the Kruskal-Wallis test. Associations between years of service, age, and knowledge scores were assessed using Spearman’s correlation analysis. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all analyses.

Differences between pre-test and post-test scores

The difference between participants’ pre-test and post-test scores was evaluated using the non-parametric Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test. The analysis revealed that post-test scores were significantly higher compared to pre-test scores (Z = − 3.56; p < 0.001). This finding indicates that the training provided a statistically significant improvement in participants’ knowledge levels.

An analysis of post-test scores showed that 64.1% (n = 25) of participants improved their scores, while 23.1% (n = 9) remained unchanged, and 12.8% (n = 5) showed a decrease. The effect size calculated from the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was r = 0.56, which corresponds to a large effect according to Cohen’s criteria.

Relationships between age, length of professional experience, and test scores

According to the results of the Spearman correlation analysis, no significant relationship was found between professional experience and pre-test, post-test scores, or score differences (p > 0.05). A significant negative correlation was identified between participants’ pre-test scores and score differences (r = − 0.480, p = 0.002). This finding suggests that participants with lower baseline knowledge levels benefited more from the training.

When the confounding effect of age was controlled for, no significant relationship was found between length of professional experience and pre-test, post-test, or score difference values (p > 0.05). These results indicate that benefiting from the training was independent of length of experience.

Comparison of pre-test, post-test, and score changes by professional group

The Kruskal–Wallis test showed a significant difference in pre-test scores among Pediatric Emergency Medicine fellows, Child Psychiatry residents, and Pediatrics residents (KW H = 8.57; p = 0.014). According to the Bonferroni post-hoc analysis, the Child Psychiatry group scored higher than the other groups (p = 0.001). In addition, no significant difference was found between the groups in terms of score change (KW H = 0.176; p = 0.916). These results suggest that although baseline knowledge levels differed by group, the training produced a similar level of improvement across groups. Furthermore, a similar comparison conducted for post-test scores revealed no significant difference between the groups (KW H = 5.43; p = 0.066).

Self-efficacy outcomes

Participants’ self-efficacy in managing agitation was assessed using the Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test to compare pre- and post-training scores. A significant improvement was observed in “confidence in managing agitated patients in the emergency department” following the training (Z = − 3.346; p < 0.001). Significant gains were also noted in the following domains:

· Effective use of verbal de-escalation techniques (Z = − 2.422; p = 0.015).

· Knowledge of pharmacological treatment selection (Z = − 3.214; p = 0.001).

· Rapid and accurate decision-making in the use of physical restraint (Z = − 3.013; p = 0.003).

Overall, these results indicate that the training program enhanced participants’ self-efficacy in the management of agitated patients (Table 3).

Self-efficacy differences by groups

In the pre-training assessments, the child psychiatry residents group had the highest mean rank values in many domains. In particular:

“How confident do I feel in managing agitated patients in the emergency department?” (KW H = 10.421; p = 0.005).

“I have sufficient knowledge to determine the appropriate pharmacological treatment for an agitated patient.” (KW H = 10.539; p = 0.005).

“I believe I can effectively apply verbal de-escalation techniques.” (KW H = 11.013; p = 0.004).

“I believe I can effectively apply physical restraint techniques.” (KW H = 6.987; p = 0.030).

In these items, the child psychiatry group demonstrated higher self-efficacy perceptions compared to other groups. This suggests that, prior to the training, this group felt more competent in managing agitated patients.

In the post-training assessments, the pattern changed, and Pediatric Emergency Medicine fellows achieved the highest mean rank values. In particular, significant increases were observed in the following items:

“How confident do I feel in managing agitated patients in the emergency department?” (KW H = 15.595; p < 0.001).

“I believe I can effectively apply verbal de-escalation techniques.” (KW H = 11.720; p = 0.003).

“I have sufficient knowledge to determine the appropriate pharmacological treatment for an agitated patient.” (KW H = 14.656; p < 0.001).

“I believe I can effectively apply physical restraint de-escalation techniques.” (KW H = 11.911; p = 0.003).

Relationships between age, length of professional experience, self-efficacy

A significant, strong, and positive relationship was found between participants’ age and length of professional experience (r = 0.802, p < 0.001). Before the training, no statistically significant relationship was identified between age or length of service and self-efficacy perceptions regarding overall agitation management, knowledge of pharmacological treatment, and decision-making about physical restraint (p > 0.05).

A partial correlation analysis was conducted to control for the confounding effect of age. When age was controlled, no statistically significant relationship were found between length of professional experience and any pre-training self-efficacy domain (p > 0.05).

In post-training assessments, length of professional experience were found to be very strongly and significantly associated with confidence in pharmacological management (r = 0.911, p < 0.001), decision-making regarding physical restraint (r = 0.903, p < 0.001), and verbal de-escalation (r = 0.899, p < 0.001).

A partial correlation analysis was again conducted to control for the confounding effect of age. When age was controlled, significant positive correlations were found between length of professional experience and confidence in “physical restraint decision-making” (partial r = 0.666), “pharmacological management knowledge” (partial r = 0.655), “verbal de-escalation skills” (partial r = 0.572), and “general agitation management” (partial r = 0.538).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that a locally developed, half-day structured training workshop can significantly improve both knowledge and self-efficacy among junior medical staff involved in the management of agitated pediatric patients. Importantly, these improvements were observed regardless of prior clinical experience or years of practice, highlighting the broad applicability of the intervention. The greatest gains were observed among pediatric emergency medicine trainees, suggesting that clinicians with more frequent exposure to agitation cases may particularly benefit from targeted educational programs. These findings underscore the value of brief, institution-specific training models in addressing critical knowledge and confidence gaps in pediatric emergency settings. Furthermore, this model may represent a scalable and reproducible approach for institutions lacking structured agitation management training.

The management of agitated pediatric patients requires a collaborative, multidisciplinary approach. However, the literature shows that both adult and pediatric emergency teams have received limited training in recognizing and managing this patient group, not only in our country but also globally [8–14].

In a study involving centers managing agitated pediatric patients, it was reported that only 9.6% had a standard agitation scale, 12.9% had a written management guide, and 24.2% provided mandatory training on this topic. Among the most requested resources were guidelines for medication use (82.5%) and sample care algorithms (57.1%) [15]. The National Academy of Medicine of the United States defines high-quality care as safe, effective, patient-centered, timely, efficient, and equitable. To achieve these standards in the management of agitated pediatric patients, it is recommended to adopt a multidisciplinary team approach and develop institutional protocols and algorithms [3, 12, 13, 16]. As part of our training program, the “Agitated Pediatric Patient Management Algorithm,” developed in collaboration with the child psychiatry department, has been shared with participants and has begun to be implemented in our institution. It is expected that this algorithm will contribute to structure-process-outcome-based quality improvement initiatives as recommended in the literature [17, 18]. The statistically significant increase in the final test scores of the participants and the large effect size (r = 0.56) suggest that the structured training program is effective in increasing their knowledge levels. It is planned to repeat this training at certain intervals and to evaluate the outcomes of the algorithm’s implementation on the quality of patient care.

In our study, the lack of a significant relationship between professional experience and knowledge scores or score changes, and the fact that participants with lower initial knowledge levels achieved greater gains, indicate that the training program had a more pronounced effect on participants with knowledge gaps, independent of experience duration. Despite differences in initial knowledge levels, the similarity in score changes and final test results across the groups suggests that the training provided comparable gains regardless of expertise. These results are consistent with the international literature, which reports that education-based interventions are effective in the management of agitated pediatric patients [6, 12, 13].

The study results indicate that the training program also increased the participants’ self-efficacy in managing agitated patients. Pre-training assessments showed that Child Psychiatry residents reported significantly higher self-efficacy across many areas than other groups; however, the greatest post-training gains were observed among Pediatric Emergency Medicine residents. This situation is consistent with the findings of the APPLIED study. In the aforementioned study, clinicians who more frequently treat agitated patients benefited more from structured educational interventions [6, 19].

The inability to communicate effectively with agitated pediatric patients is one of the most important factors affecting the management of these patients [20]. In the training content we conducted, the use of verbal sedation methods was explained via patient examples, and significant increases in participants’ self-efficacy perceptions were observed in this regard. The significant improvement in verbal de-escalation skills directly addresses one of the fundamental deficiencies highlighted in Hoffmann et al.‘s qualitative study, which is the lack of effective communication [21]. It is noteworthy that participants’ perceptions of self-efficacy increased significantly, even for high-risk practices such as pharmacological interventions and physical restraints. This finding indicates that the training program not only increases participants’ confidence in low-risk applications but also in the most critical and sensitive interventions.

The findings indicate that, prior to training, age and professional experience did not play a decisive role in self-efficacy perception; after training, however, professional experience became strongly associated with self-efficacy perception, particularly in the areas of pharmacological management, physical restraint decision-making, and verbal de-escalation.

The literature frequently considers age and professional experience as interchangeable or overlapping variables. However, the advanced statistical analyses conducted in this study demonstrated that length of service remained an independent factor influencing self-confidence, even after controlling for age. This finding suggests that, in dynamic environments such as emergency departments, specific clinical skills—such as the management of agitated patients—may be shaped more directly by case exposure and time spent in clinical practice (seniority) rather than by general life experience. Furthermore, the results indicate that training may integrate more effectively with the existing clinical experience of more senior participants, with experience level functioning as an independent factor associated with greater post-training increases in confidence.

Significant knowledge gaps persist in the management of agitation in pediatric emergency departments, as many current practices are adapted from adult guidelines. Effective management requires developing and evaluating system-based approaches that incorporate multidisciplinary team training, trauma-informed care, individualized management plans, standardized algorithms, and structured feedback mechanisms [22].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The predominance of pediatric residents among participants limits the generalizability of the findings to the broader pediatric emergency healthcare professional population. Furthermore, reliance on data from a single center constrains the external validity of the results. Finally, the absence of long-term follow-up to assess the sustainability of post-training gains represents an additional limitation.

In addition, the self-efficacy and knowledge tests used in this study do not directly reflect actual changes in clinical behavior or their impact on patient outcomes, which constitutes a methodological limitation. Therefore, future studies with larger samples and conducted across multiple centers should include long-term follow-up.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that structured, case-based, multidisciplinary training in the management of agitated pediatric patients led to significant improvements in knowledge and perceived self-efficacy. Such gains have the potential to enhance the quality of patient care, safety, and multidisciplinary teamwork in clinical practice.

Future research should expand training programs to include nurses and other healthcare professionals and evaluate their long-term effects. Moreover, the regular repetition of such programs, supported by institutional standard algorithms, may help achieve national and international benchmarks for quality and equity in the management of agitated patients in pediatric emergency care.
