Most Upper Extremity Injuries in Elite Summer League Baseball Players Occur From Overuse, Affect the Shoulder, and Result in Missed Playing Time
Authors: David F Painter, Matthew Quinn, Ryan Snow, Edward J Testa, Rory A Byrne, Michele Marie Pavlu, Brett D Owens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo characterize the epidemiology of upper extremity injuries among elite college baseball players in 1 league over 5 seasons.MethodsDeidentified arm injury reports were recorded from the 2018, 2019, 2021, 2022, and 2023 Cape Cod Baseball League (CCBL) seasons. Included injuries were those that happened to CCBL players during the above 5 seasons, impacted the upper extremity, occurred during official CCBL practice or gameplay settings, and were recorded by League athletic trainers. Injuries were recorded by team athletic trainers according to a standardized reporting guide and then reviewed and compiled by the league's athletic training coordinator. Data were analyzed with descriptive and inferential statistics, including chi‐square tests of independence and linear regression.ResultsA total of 162 upper extremity injuries were reported across 5 summer seasons for a mean of 32.4 ± 4.2 injuries per season. The average upper extremity injury rate across all 5 seasons was 2.22 injuries per 1000 athletic exposures. There was no significant injury trend from 2018 to 2023 (R2 = .434, P = .227). Almost 40% (61/154) of injuries were reinjuries, and overuse (120/158, 75.9%) was more common than trauma (38/158, 24.1%) regarding mechanism of injury. Muscles/tendons (e.g., strain) (97/161, 60.2%) were the most injured structure, and the shoulder (92/162, 56.8%) was the most frequently injured anatomic region. More than half (89/158, 56.3%) of injuries occurred in pitchers. Nearly all (146, 90.1%) injuries occurred during games instead of during practice (injury rate ratio = 9.1 [5.5, 15.3]). Significantly more injuries were time‐loss injuries (105/162, 64.8%) than non–time‐loss (P < .001).ConclusionsAmong upper extremity injuries recorded across 5 seasons in the CCBL, injuries were predominantly due to overuse, most often involved shoulder anatomy, and frequently resulted in missed playing time. More than half of the injuries occurred in pitchers, and reinjuries were common.Level of EvidenceLevel IV, epidemiological case series.

Upper extremity injuries are a substantial concern for baseball players. This is especially true among collegiate players with aspirations of playing professionally. Alarmingly, these injuries continue to occur at high rates among college athletes. 1 The injury rate among amateur players has consistently increased over the past few decades. According to the American Sports Medicine Institute, 10% of ulnar collateral ligament surgical procedures in 1995 were performed on youth and high school pitchers, a percentage that increased to more than 40% by 2020. These types of upper extremity injuries carry both short‐ and long‐term morbidity for players due to prolonged recovery periods and altered career trajectories. 2 , 3 Prior research has shown that overuse‐related arm injuries are common among collegiate baseball players. 4 In this population, noncontact and overuse injuries are the 2 primary mechanisms of injury. For both professional and collegiate players, such injuries can lead to considerable time away from competition and variable quality of recovery. 5 , 6

For collegiate baseball players, summer leagues are highly competitive and coveted for their ability to expose players to future playing opportunities. Scouts from Major League Baseball (MLB) and college programs regularly attend games in evaluative roles. Further, these summer leagues provide an opportunity for further skill development, offering a competitive advantage to participating athletes. However, summer leagues add to the demanding workload that players encounter annually throughout the preseason and regular season. In addition to the National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) regular season, which can include more than 55 games for Division I players, the Cape Cod Baseball League (CCBL) involves a 40‐game regular season in addition to playoff games. This schedule, along with regular practice throughout the summer, adds to the physical burden of a demanding schedule and is a theoretical injury risk to players.

The CCBL is generally considered the most prestigious summer league in the United States. As of October 2019, 1400 CCBL alumni had subsequently participated in at least 1 MLB game. 7 , 8 Roughly 1 in every 7 players in the MLB participated at some point in the CCBL. 9 Because of this, the CCBL represents an optimal population for studying the impact of overuse injuries on amateur players with professional aspirations.

Previous research has focused on risk factors for arm injury among amateur baseball players at the youth, high school, college, and professional levels. Studies in baseball athletes of all levels published over the past decade have suggested risk factors for upper extremity injury, including overuse/greater pitch count workload, 10 , 11 fatigue, 12 and single‐sport specialization. 13 Of note, none of these reports studied the incidence, severity, and impact of arm injuries in a collegiate summer league such as the CCBL. A prior study focused on hamstring injuries among CCBL players and showed the value and viability of this population for researching sports injuries and their impacts on amateur athletes. 14 One additional study used the Kerlan‐Jobe Orthopaedic Clinic score to highlight that players with prior arm injury and treatment frequently carry that burden into the summer season. 15 In a study by Boltz et al., the overall injury baseball injury rates across the 2014 to 2019 academic years was 3.16 per 1000 athletic events, with a significantly higher injury rate in the preseason than in the regular season or postseason. 1

The purpose of this study was to characterize the epidemiology of upper extremity injuries among elite college baseball players in 1 league over 5 seasons. We hypothesized that summer league injuries in the CCBL would occur primarily in pitchers due to an overuse mechanism.

METHODS

This study was conducted under approval by the Brown University Institutional Review Board Individual team athletic trainers initially completed electronic throwing arm injury reports from the 2018, 2019, 2021, 2022, and 2023 seasons (5 total). The 2020 CCBL season was canceled due to the COVID‐19 pandemic. The inclusion criteria were incident upper extremity injuries in CCBL players between the 2018 and 2023 seasons, specifically occurring during official CCBL practice or gameplay settings, and recorded by League athletic trainers. The exclusion criteria were any injuries unrelated to official baseball activities (with “official baseball activities” defined as occurring during sanctioned League practice or game settings) or impacting nonupper extremity structures. Arm injury reports were completed and submitted electronically by athletic trainers within 24 h of an injury occurring during a practice or game. For in‐season throwing arm injury reports, athletic trainers were instructed by the CCBL Athletic Training Coordinator (M.M.P.) to report details according to the recommendations outlined by the Fédération Internationale de Football Association Medical Assessment and Research Centre's Injury Consensus Group. 16 When completing injury reports, team trainers were instructed to categorize injury types by International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) codes. The Athletic Training Coordinator was able to access all submitted injury reports. After the season, the training coordinator compiled all the reports into deidentified versions that were then electronically transferred to and statistically analyzed by the remaining authors. Of note, flexor‐pronator strains/tendinopathy (ICD‐10‐CM S56.21) were classified as either elbow or forearm injuries, depending on whether the athlete's pain was most localized to the common flexor‐pronator tendon origination at the elbow or more distally along the muscle‐tendon unit affecting the flexor‐pronator mass in the forearm.

Although each of the 10 CCBL teams typically rosters up to 60 to 65 players throughout the season, many players are temporary substitutes and only participate in a few games before being released. At any given time, each team carries an active roster of 30 players.

An athlete exposure (AE) is defined as 1 player participating in 1 game or practice. Each CCBL team plays approximately 40 regular season games and holds 7 formal practices, the latter typically occurring during the first week of the season. As a result, the annual total team exposures are 1410 (47 sessions × 30 players), and the total League exposure count is 14,100 (1410 × 10 teams). In calculating AEs, it is important to note that we aimed to estimate the team‐ and League‐based AEs, not the total exposures at the level of the individual player. For that reason, roster adjustments and consideration of individual player variability are not needed in the total League AE exposure calculation because the exposure count is based on who actively participates each day.

The injury rate ratio (IRR) compares the injury rate in one group (e.g., players with game‐acquired injuries) with that in another group (e.g., players with practice‐acquired injuries). It is calculated using the following formula: IRR = InjuryRate1/InjuryRate2, where InjuryRate1 = games and InjuryRate2 = practices. IRR by year was reported with a 95% confidence interval (CI). CIs were considered statistically significant when they did not contain the value representing the null hypothesis (i.e., significant CIs were those for which the 95% range did not cross the value 0).

Time‐loss (TL) injuries were defined as those that resulted in at least 1 day of practice or game competition missed or those that precipitated release from the team within 5 days of the injury. Injuries with zero competition days missed and no release within 5 days were categorized as non‐TL (NTL). To determine whether injuries were a reinjury or a first‐time injury, athletic trainers were instructed to ask the player at the time of treatment whether they had ever experienced the same injury before. Reinjuries were classified as such and documented according to player response.

Graphs were made using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA). Data were analyzed with descriptive and inferential statistics, including chi‐square tests of independence and linear regression. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and evaluated for significance using a P value of <.05.

RESULTS

A total of 162 upper extremity injuries were reported across 5 summer seasons for a mean of 32.4 ± 4.2 injuries per season (Table 1). Most injuries (93/154, 60.4%) had not been experienced before, whereas 61 (39.6%) were reinjuries. More than half (97/161, 60.2%) of all reported injuries affected muscles/tendons (e.g., strain). The next highest proportion of injuries (42/161, 26.1%) involved joints/ligaments (e.g., sprain). Peripheral nerve injuries (e.g., ulnar nerve pathology) accounted for 8/161 injuries (5.0%), and fractures and contusions each accounted for 4.3% (7/161) of injuries (Figure 1). The shoulder was the most injured anatomic region, accounting for more than half (92, 56.8%) of injuries. The elbow (41, 25.3%) was the next most injured region of the upper extremity, followed by the forearm, which accounted for 16 (9.9%) injuries. The remaining 13 (8.0%) injuries were to “all other” upper extremity regions, including the wrist and hand.

Of the 158 injuries for which player position was specified, more than half occurred in pitchers (89, 56.3%); infielders suffered 37 injuries (23.4%), outfielders experienced 19 injuries (12.0%), and catchers experienced 13 (8.2%) injuries.

There was no significant trend over time among raw injury counts from 2018 to 2023 by linear regression (R2 = .434, P = .227). The average upper extremity injury rate across the 5 seasons was 2.22 injuries per 1000 athletic exposures (AEs). By year, the injury count per 1000 AEs was 2.09 (2018), 1.83 (2019), 1.99 (2021), 2.48 (2022), and 2.77 (2023). There was a trend of consistently increasing injury rates across the 2021, 2022, and 2023 seasons following the COVID‐19 hiatus in 2020, although this trend did not reach significance (R2 = .979, P = .094) (Figure 2).

Injuries by year. Upper extremity injuries per 1000 athletic exposures (AEs) by CCBL season. (CCBL, Cape Cod Baseball League.)

Nearly all (146, 90.1%) injuries occurred during games versus just 16 (9.9%) that occurred in the practice setting. The IRR for games versus practices across 5 seasons was 9.1 [5.5, 15.3]. There were significantly more injuries during games than practices within all 5 years. Note that the IRR in 2021 was undefined because zero injuries occurred during practice that season.

Significantly more injuries (105/162, 64.8%) were TL than NTL (P < .001). However, within specific positions, only pitchers experienced significantly more TL injuries (60/89, 67.4%) than NTL injuries (P = .001); the proportion of TL injuries was 63.2% (n = 12) in outfielders (P = .251), 62.2% (n = 23) in infielders (P = .139), and 61.5% (n = 8) in catchers (P = .405). In 53.3% (56/105) of TL injuries, the athlete did not return to play on the Cape due to being immediately released by their team. Among the remaining 49 (46.7%) TL injuries, the mean time between injury and return to CCBL play was 5.45 ± 4.87 days. Overall, there was a nonsignificant decrease in the proportion of TL injuries per year across all positions from 2018 to 2023 (P = .124, R2 = .599).

Overuse (120, 75.9%) was the primary mechanism of injury, whereas the remaining 38 (24.1%) of the specified 158 injuries were traumatic in nature, representing a significant difference (P < .001). Pitchers were significantly more likely to suffer overuse (n = 74, 83.1%) than traumatic (n = 15, 16.9%) injuries (P < .001). Similarly, infielders were significantly more likely to experience overuse (n = 25, 71.4%) than traumatic (n = 10, 28.6%) injuries (P = .011). However, outfielders were no more likely to experience overuse (n = 9, 47.4%) than traumatic (n = 10, 52.6%) injuries (P = .819), and the difference between overuse (n = 7, 63.6%) and traumatic (n = 4, 36.4%) injuries was also not significant in catchers (P = .366) (Table 2).

A total of 12 tears to upper extremity structures were documented over the 5 seasons: 9 labrum tears, 2 medial ulnar collateral ligament tears, and 1 biceps tendon tear. Tears occurred in 5 pitchers‐only (41.7%), 1 first baseman (8.3%), 1 catcher (8.3%), and 5 players with multiple positions (41.7%). The multiple‐position players included infield (1), shortstop/centerfield (1), catcher/outfield (1), RHP/outfield (1), and first base/third base (1). Seven of the 12 (58.3%) tears were overuse injuries, and 9/12 (75.0%) were reinjuries, ranging in recency from 48 days to 3 years prior. One pitcher in particular had a history of labrum tear with cortisol injections during the preceding college baseball season and was actively seeking physical therapy for shoulder rehabilitation during the CCBL season.

DISCUSSION

The most important findings of this study were that more than 50% of all injuries occurred in pitchers and that muscular and tendinous injuries of the shoulder were the most frequent injuries. Seventy‐five percent of injuries were due to overuse, and there was a 40% rate of recurrent injury. Nearly all injuries occurred during games, and most injuries resulted in loss of playing time. These results are important for understanding the offseason injury experiences of a large summer baseball league.

The upper extremity injury rate across 5 CCBL seasons was 2.22 injuries per 1000 AEs or 22.2 injuries/10,000 AEs. Cross et al. utilized the NCAA Injury Surveillance Program, which uses similar injury reporting protocols as this study, to report a shoulder injury rate of 4.02/10,000 AEs and an elbow injury rate of 2.44/10,000 AEs from 2004 to 2014. 17 Their data included injuries from approximately February to June (i.e., the NCAA regular season, not inclusive of the preseason or summer season). Although our overall upper extremity injury rate is higher than both isolated shoulder and elbow injuries reported by Cross, our rate is more similar to that reported by Boltz et al. in a study that also used the NCAA Injury Surveillance Program to provide a national estimate of NCAA baseball injury rates across the 2014 to 2019 academic years. The “academic year” in Boltz's study included preseason, regular season, and some or all postseason. They reported an overall injury rate of 3.16 per 1000 AEs, with a significantly higher injury rate in the preseason than in the regular season or postseason. 1 The upper extremity–only injury rate reported by the current study is generally comparable to the rate of all baseball injuries as reported by Boltz. Boltz's study emphasizes the significantly higher rate of injuries in preseason baseball, whereas our study underscores the continued incidence of injuries during the competitive summer postseason. Taken together, it is clear that collegiate baseball players, often with year‐round training schedules, are at comparable risk for injury during all settings of formal gameplay. With substantial research devoted to data‐based throwing programs to mitigate overuse injuries in both youth and higher‐level baseball players, each venue or opportunity for baseball must be weighed carefully against the aggregate injury risk. 18 , 19

Prior research in the CCBL population showed that players carry the burden of previous arm injuries into the summer league season, which significantly lowers self‐reported Kerlan‐Jobe Orthopaedic Clinic scores. 15 Given a nearly year‐round throwing program for many players, featuring high‐workload preseason training and a lengthy regular season that immediately transitions into summer ball, these athletes are at risk for chronic injuries and reinjuries due to overuse. 20 A recent study in 12 collegiate baseball players used the Athletic Shoulder upper‐body isometric test to reveal a significant 9% reduction in throwing arm strength from before to after participation in a summer baseball league, showing the considerable impact of the summer season on arm health. 21 The results of the current study show that more than one‐third of incident summer upper extremity injuries are reinjuries, illustrating the prominence of persistent injuries and underscoring a risk for recurrent injuries during the summer season. For this reason, particular attention with symptomatic monitoring should be paid to athletes entering the summer season with a history of recent injury.

The results of the current study show that the shoulder was the most injured anatomic region, accounting for more than half of injuries, whereas the elbow was the next most frequently injured region of the upper extremity. These findings are concordant with a prior study examining the prevalence of all injuries in Division I collegiate baseball players from a single conference, in which Ishikawa et al. reported shoulder injuries (specifically affecting the rotator cuff tendon) followed by arm/elbow injuries (specifically affecting the ulnar collateral ligament) to be the 2 most common injury types. 22 The shoulder has also been shown to be the upper extremity region most prone to injury in MLB players. 23 Although accounting for less than 10% of injuries in our dataset, forearm injuries are also important to address because forearm flexor injuries have been associated with later injury requiring ulnar collateral ligament reconstruction in throwing athletes. 24 Moreover, more than half (56.3%) of injuries occurred in pitchers, which is in accordance with Ishikawa et al., who also reported that pitchers suffered the most baseball‐related injuries. 22 This phenomenon also scales to professional levels, with 1 study showing that MLB pitchers sustain the greatest total proportion of in‐game defensive injuries across all body regions. 25

There have been conflicting reports on injury rates during game versus practice settings in the high‐level baseball epidemiologic literature. Dick et al. reported that the rate of injury in NCAA baseball from 1988 to 2004 was 3 times higher during games than practices (5.78 vs 1.85 injuries per 1000 AE), with a rate ratio of 3.1. 26 Similarly, Cross et al. showed IRRs of 1.81 and 2.19 for the shoulder and elbow, respectively, when comparing injuries sustained by collegiate baseball players during organized competition versus training. 17 This agrees with the overall direction of the findings of the CCBL, as injuries occurred significantly more often in games than in practices, with an IRR of 9.1. There was, however, noticeably more exposure to potential injury for CCBL players in games compared with practices. Prior literature has intuitively theorized that this discrepancy is secondary to heightened intensity during competition, prompting players to not only play harder but also take more risks or play through preexisting pain. It follows that this trend is also seen in the CCBL, where stakes are high and competition for professional play is pressing.

Significantly more injuries resulted in TL than NTL in the current study, with pitchers more likely to experience TL injuries and no difference found within infielders, outfielders, and catchers. These findings agree with the broader literature in that injuries in similar populations commonly result in TL from competition, particularly in pitchers. 1 , 4 , 22 , 27 For example, a study of collegiate baseball players found that approximately 60% of injuries resulted in TL of at least 7 days, with pitchers being the most commonly injured position. 4 Similarly, the study by Ishikawa et al. found that pitchers were the most frequently injured group and that nearly 70% of injuries resulted in TL. 22 Taken together, these findings underscore the substantial burden that upper extremity injuries impose on player availability, especially among pitchers who have amassed high throwing volumes between regular season and summer competition. In light of this, it is imperative that both players and medical staff prioritize targeted injury prevention strategies for high‐risk groups during the summer competition period.

In the current cohort, the primary mechanism of injury was overuse, accounting for 75% of the injuries. When stratified by position, both pitchers and infielders were significantly more likely to suffer overuse than traumatic injuries, whereas outfielders showed no difference in the mechanism of injury. The predominance of overuse injuries in the current study contrasts with previous studies, which have shown that overuse injuries make up approximately 20% of injuries in collegiate baseball players. 1 , 4 , 22 This discrepancy may be attributable to the unique context of the CCBL, where athletes continue to compete at an elite level shortly after completion of a full NCAA season, often with minimal recovery time. It is well documented that increased cumulative throwing volume, higher throwing velocity, inadequate rest, and year‐round participation are significant risk factors for overuse injuries in baseball players, particularly in pitchers. 3 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 As such, the stark increase in the proportion of overuse injuries in the current cohort likely reflects the additive stress of extended competitive play without sufficient time for musculoskeletal recovery and once again underscores the critical nature of player‐specific workload management and comprehensive, consistent preventive arm care for all athletes. In an effort to reduce the incidence of upper extremity injuries, players, coaches, and trainers should consider a dedicated rest and recovery period at some point during the postseason. This study could be important in identifying which injuries and subpopulations to target for preventive injury measures before and during the summer league season. Research such as this may also interest MLB, which consistently draws from the CCBL talent pool and currently employs hundreds of CCBL alumni. 7

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. Reporting bias exists because it is possible that some injuries were not reported by athletes or recognized by trainers. Additionally, differences in diagnostic tendencies between trainers could result in injury reports of varying diagnostic accuracy or consistency. All efforts were made to maximize the standardization of reporting, although we recognize that minor injuries not requiring loss of playing time may have been under‐reported. Another limitation is that the classification of injuries as “reinjuries” relied on the subjective reports of players, as attained by athletic trainers at the time of treatment, rather than a more objective review of medical records. Finally, no follow‐up of the ultimate impact of the injuries is available in the current dataset.

CONCLUSIONS

Among upper extremity injuries recorded across 5 seasons in the CCBL, injuries were predominantly due to overuse, most often involved shoulder anatomy, and frequently resulted in missed playing time. More than half of the injuries occurred in pitchers, and reinjuries were common.
