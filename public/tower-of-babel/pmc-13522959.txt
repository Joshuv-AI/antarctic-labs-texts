A Clinically Aligned 18-Item Instrument for Measuring Inflammatory Breast Cancer Knowledge
Authors: Anh N. Tran, Alexandra Bennion, Kalyani Sonawane, Samantha M. Thomas, Alisa N. Blumenthaler, Katherine Reddy, Beau Blass, Jennifer K. Plichta, Gayathri R. Devi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This survey study evaluates preliminary, clinically informed exploratory indices to evaluate inflammatory breast cancer knowledge, including symptom and risk-factor recognition.

Introduction

Inflammatory breast cancer (IBC) is a rare but aggressive subtype of breast cancer characterized by the rapid onset of erythema, edema, and skin changes, often without a palpable mass. Although IBC accounts for only 1% to 5% of breast cancer, it contributes disproportionately to mortality, with delayed diagnosis partly driven by limited recognition of its atypical presentation.1,2 Public health messaging has historically emphasized lump detection, potentially contributing to gaps in awareness of hallmark IBC symptoms.6,4 To examine clinically meaningful recognition of IBC, we developed preliminary, clinically informed exploratory indices to evaluate IBC knowledge, including symptom and risk factor recognition. We hypothesized that general awareness alone would not reflect meaningful IBC knowledge, and that assessing recognition of specific symptoms would identify clinically relevant knowledge gaps.

Methods

The IBC knowledge instrument was developed through cognitive interviews with 10 patients previously diagnosed with IBC, followed by creation and refinement of survey items assessing general awareness, symptom recognition, risk factor recognition, breast health behaviors, and sociodemographic characteristics. Patient narratives informed item wording and structure, while established IBC diagnostic criteria and consensus symptom descriptions supported clinical relevance.3

A cross-sectional online public survey (eAppendix in Supplement 1) of US adults (aged ≥18 years) was conducted from January 2022 to December 2025 using purposive sampling to assess IBC knowledge. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize sociodemographics, general awareness of IBC, symptom recognition, and IBC knowledge scores. Knowledge scores were modeled as continuous outcomes using negative binomial regression to estimate rate ratios (RRs) and 95% CIs by sociodemographics and breast health variables. Complete-case analyses were used for variables included in each model. Statistical significance was tested at a 2-sided P < .05, with interpretation limited by the exploratory design. Analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 11.0.0 (GraphPad).

Composite Awareness Score (CAS) and Weighted Composite Awareness Score (WCAS) were evaluated as preliminary, clinically informed exploratory indices rather than validated instruments. The final scoring set included 18 items: 8 symptom items and 10 risk-factor items. CAS was calculated as the sum of correct responses (range, 0-18). WCAS applied expert-derived weights emphasizing diagnostically relevant IBC symptoms (range, 0-31), with higher scores indicating greater IBC knowledge. A detailed description of the procedures is available in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

The Duke institutional review board approved this study. Participants provided informed consent electronically before beginning the survey. This report follows the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) reporting guidelines for survey studies. Race and ethnicity were self-reported and assessed as social variables to explore potential disparities in IBC knowledge across participant groups.

Results

Among 177 respondents, 139 (78.5%) were women; 122 (71.3%) were aged 18 to 39 years; 18 (10.2%) were American Indian or Alaskan Native, 37 (20.9%) were Black, and 95 (53.7%) were White; 147 (83.1%) lived in urban areas; and 138 (78.0%) had previously heard of IBC (Table 1). Despite relatively high overall awareness of IBC, recognition of its hallmark clinical features was limited. The most frequently missed signs included skin thickening (89 respondents [50.3%]), nipple inversion (87 [49.2%]), peau d’orange (76 [42.9%]), changes in breast size or shape (73 [41.2%]), and breast redness (64 [36.2%]). In contrast, 111 respondents (62.7%) identified a breast lump, a finding that is common in breast cancer overall but is less characteristic of IBC.

In unadjusted comparisons, older adults, men, rural residents, individuals with lower income or education, and those from racial and ethnic minority groups demonstrated low knowledge, with mean CAS and WCAS scores less than 10 and 20, respectively, despite high general IBC awareness (Table 1). In exploratory multivariable models, being White, having a prior breast cancer diagnosis, and mammography history were statistically significantly associated with higher CAS and WCAS scores (Table 2).

Discussion

This survey study evaluated 2 preliminary, clinically informed exploratory indices of IBC knowledge and recognition among surveyed US adults. Findings suggest that prior awareness of IBC does not necessarily translate into recognition of hallmark symptoms that may prompt timely evaluation. While most respondents recognized a lump as a clinical symptom, several hallmark IBC signs, including skin thickening, nipple inversion, peau d’orange, and breast size or shape change were frequently missed. These findings underscore the importance of public education that shifts awareness beyond breast lump detection to include the rapid onset of unilateral breast changes characteristic of IBC.

The low level of IBC knowledge observed in the current study is consistent with prior studies demonstrating that even health care professionals remain largely unaware of the signs and symptoms of IBC, underscoring the contribution of inadequate symptom recognition to diagnostic delays.4,5,6 The risk factor domain included broader breast cancer risk factors for educational and exploratory purposes and should not be interpreted as an IBC-specific risk prediction model. Additional limitations include purposive sampling, reliance on self-reported measures, and limited generalizability due to the sample size. Accordingly, the CAS and WCAS should not be interpreted as validated instruments. Although both indices were developed using a theory-driven, expert-derived approach, further validation in larger, more representative populations is warranted to evaluate reliability, factor structure, item discrimination, test-retest performance, and empirical weighting. In conclusion, general awareness of IBC does not reflect clinically meaningful IBC symptom recognition, underscoring the value of CAS and WCAS as preliminary indices as a first step toward systematically identifying recognition deficits.
