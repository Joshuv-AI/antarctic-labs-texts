Nodular Cutaneous Amyloidosis on the Nose Confirmed Using Electron Microscopy: A Case Report
Authors: Go Woon Choi, Dong Hyun Kim
Published: 2023

Dear Editor:

Nodular cutaneous amyloidosis (NCA) is characterized by the deposition of light chain-type amyloid fibril protein in the dermis1. On electron microscopy, amyloids show the characteristic structure of filaments. We report a case of NCA on the nose that was confirmed using electron microscopy.

A 60-year-old man presented with a 6-year history of a firm, erythematous nodule on the nose measuring 20×20 mm (Fig. 1A). He had no history of local trauma or underlying diseases. Histopathological examination revealed homogeneous eosinophilic deposit material throughout the dermis (Fig. 1B). The deposits stained positive with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) and Congo red (Fig. 2A), but there was no birefringence under polarized light with Congo red staining. Electron microscopy revealed 7 to 10 nm, straight, non-branching filaments within the dermis, which is consistent with amyloid formation (Fig. 2B). There was no clinical or laboratory evidence of systemic amyloidosis in the patient. He was diagnosed with NCA and treated using a carbon dioxide laser based on the findings (Fig. 1A).

We received the patient’s consent form about publishing all photographic materials. (B) Skin biopsy showing deposits of homogeneous, pale, eosinophilic material in the entire dermis (H&E, ×100).

(A) Immunohistochemical staining (left: PAS positive ×100, right: Congo red positive ×100; but no birefringence under polarized light). (B) Electron microscopy showing 7~10 nm, straight, non-branching filaments within the deposits (×80,000).

Primary localized cutaneous amyloidosis (PLCA) refers to extracellular amyloid deposits in the skin without systemic involvement2. PLCA has three distinct subtypes: macular, lichen, and nodular2. NCA, a rare variant of PLCA, manifests clinically as one or more ovoid, waxy, yellow-brown nodules or plaques located on the face, trunk, extremities, or genitalia. Histological findings show homogeneous deposits of pale eosinophilic material in the entire dermis and occasionally the subcutaneous fat or blood vessel walls, with a marked infiltration of plasma cells3. In some cases, these findings may not be sufficient to differentiate NCA from colloid milium. Colloid milium, a cutaneous colloid deposition, can also appear as yellow-brown nodules and show eosinophilic homogeneous masses in the dermis3. Application of apple-green birefringence under polarized light with Congo red staining is considered to be the chief method to confirm amyloid deposits4. However, because of the limited presence of amyloids, <8-µmthick tissues, early onset of diseases, or technical variability can produce a false negative1,4. These factors can lead to an incorrect diagnosis of amyloid presence. In these cases, electron microscopy is the gold standard for distinguishing NCA from colloid milium. On one hand, amyloid is composed of long, non-branching straight filaments with a diameter of 6.0 to 10.0 nm that are randomly arranged without bundle formation. A colloid, on the other hand, is composed of granulofibrillar amorphous material with short, fine, branching wavy filaments with a diameter of 1.5 to 2.0 nm3. Although various treatment options for NCA were reported including laser, surgical excision, triamcinolone acetate injection, and cryotherapy, there is no gold standard treatment and local recurrence is common2,5. Patients with NCA may need workup including routine blood tests, electrocardiogram and serum/urine protein electrophoresis to assess the progression to systemic amyloidosis3,5.

In conclusion, our case demonstrates the importance of electron microscopy as a tool for confirming amyloids. Although it is not always easy to use electron microscopy in clinical practice, clinicians should consider using it to confirm NCA diagnosis, especially in case of negative Congo red staining results.
