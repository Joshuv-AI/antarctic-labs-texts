Groups with a finite Busemann boundary are virtually cyclic Thanks: Research supported by the Israel Science Foundation, grant no. 954/21.
Corentin Bodart, Liran Ron-George, Ariel Yadin

Abstract
. This note is a continuation of the study of the relationship between the geometry of Cayley graphs and the size of its metric-functional boundary. We show that if there exists a Cayley graph with finitely many Busemann points then the underlying group is virtually cyclic. Together with previous works, this completes the full characterization of groups with finite metric-functional boundaries. The main new notion introduced is that of annihilators .



1.
Introduction

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. Fix a basepoint
x_{0}\in X
and consider the so-called

Busemann functions

b_{x}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
given by
b_{x}(y)=d(x,y)-d(x,x_{0})
.
If we identify
X
with the set
\{b_{x}\ :\ x\in X\}
, we can compactify
X
by taking
the closure of
\{b_{x}\ :\ x\in X\}
under the topology of pointwise convergence.
This results in a compact space denoted
\overline{(X,d)}
.
The “new” part of this space,
\partial(X,d):=\overline{(X,d)}\setminus\{b_{x}\ :\ x\in X\}

is called the
metric-functional boundary
, and its elements are called
metric-functionals
.
(If we were to use the topology of uniform convergence on bounded sets instead, this construction would result
in something known as the
horofunction boundary

[
5
]
.
Throughout this note we only consider countable discrete spaces
X
, so there is no distinction between
horofunctions and metric-functionals.)

It has been proposed by A. Karlsson to use metric-functionals to study general metric spaces,
in analogy to the way that linear-functionals are used to study vector spaces. This has been quite fruitful,
see
e.g.,
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
and references therein.

Our focus is for the case where
X=G
is a finitely generated group. In this case, the metrics of special interest are
left-invariant
metrics
d
,
i.e.,
metrics satisfying
d(zx,zy)=d(x,y)
for all
x,y,z\in G
.
The canonical basepoint to choose for a group
G
is, of course, the identity element
x_{0}=1
.

A group
G
acts on
\{f\colon G\to\mathbb{R}\ |\ f(1)=0\}
by
x.f(y)=f(x^{-1}y)-f(x^{-1})
.
For a left-invariant metric
d
on a group
G
one observes that
x.b_{y}=b_{xy}
,
which implies that
\partial(G,d)
is an invariant compact set.
This metric-functional boundary of a group has proved quite useful in studying the geometry of a group,
especially in the context of
Cayley graphs
, see
e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
6
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
among many other works.

Thinking of metric-functionals as analogs of linear-functionals, a natural idea is to consider
annihilators
(defined below).
As an application we completely characterize the case of a finite metric-functional boundary,
showing that this property does not depend on the specific choice of Cayley graph, and is equivalent
to having a finite-index cyclic subgroup.

1.1.
Notation

All groups considered are assumed to be countable, unless explicitly stated otherwise.
The identity element in a group
G
is denoted by
1=1_{G}
.

A metric
d
on a group
G
is called
left-invariant
if
d(zx,zy)=d(x,y)
for all
x,y,z\in G
. For such a metric we write
|x|=|x|_{d}:=d(x,1)
. We will also assume that our metrics are
proper
, i.e., the ball
\{y\in G:d(x,y)\leq r\}
is compact for all
x\in G
and
r\geq 0
.

Given a finitely generated group
G
, and a finite, symmetric generating set
S
,
define the
Cayley graph

\Gamma(G,S)
as the graph whose vertex set is
G

and edge set is
\{\{x,xs\}\ :\ x\in G\ ,\ s\in S\}
.
The graph metric on
\Gamma(G,S)
is denoted by
d_{S}
.
It is easily seen to be left-invariant. We write
|x|_{S}=|x|_{d_{S}}
.

A metric
d
is called a
Cayley metric
if
d=d_{S}
for some finite, symmetric generating set
S
. Groups equipped with their Cayley metrics are (coarsely) geodesic spaces.

It can be shown that if
\gamma=(\gamma_{n})_{n}
is an infinite geodesic in a Cayley graph
\Gamma(G,S)
(see Section
2
below for precise definitions),
then the limit
\gamma_{\infty}:=\lim_{n}b_{\gamma_{n}}\in\overline{(G,d_{S})}
exists and is
an element of the metric-functional boundary
\partial(G,d_{S})
(see
e.g.,
[
12
, Lemma 3.2]
for a proof).
Such a metric-functional, arising as the limit along a geodesic, is called a
Busemann point
.
The set of all Busemann points in
\partial(G,d_{S})
is denoted by
\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
.

For a group property
\mathcal{P}
, we say that a group
G
is
virtually
-
\mathcal{P}

if there is a finite index subgroup
[G:H]<\infty
such that
H
is
\mathcal{P}
.
For example,
G
is virtually-
\mathbb{Z}
if there exists a finite index subgroup
[G:H]<\infty
such that

H\cong\mathbb{Z}
(where
\mathbb{Z}
denotes the additive group of integers).

1.2.
Main results

Our main result is the following.

Theorem
\@upn
1.1

Let
G
be a finitely generated, infinite group.
The following are equivalent.

•

For any Cayley metric
d_{S}
on
G
, the metric-functional boundary
\partial(G,d_{S})
is finite.

•

There exists a Cayley metric
d_{S}
on
G
such that the set of Busemann points
\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
is finite.

•

G
is virtually-
\mathbb{Z}
.

The fact that all Cayley graphs of virtually-
\mathbb{Z}
groups have only finitely many Busemann points was shown in
[
14
]
. An alternative short proof was provided by Sam Shepperd, see
[
12
]
.
The other direction,
i.e.,
the implication that if every Cayley graph has a finite metric-functional boundary
then the group is virtually-
\mathbb{Z}
, was shown in
[
12
]
. This note complements these previous works
proving that if there exists one Cayley graph with a finite set of Busemann points, then the group must be virtually-
\mathbb{Z}

(and thus all Cayley graphs have a finite metric-functional boundary).

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
, we must first
analyze the virtually-Abelian case.

Theorem
\@upn
1.2

Let
d_{S}
be a Cayley metric on a finitely generated, infinite, virtually Abelian group
G
.
Then,
\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
is finite if and only if
G
is virtually-
\mathbb{Z}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is in Section
4
.

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
we require the notion of
annihilators
,
which will be introduced in the next section.
The main result is Lemma
1.5
, see below.
The proofs of Theorem
1.1
and of Lemma
1.5
are in Section
5
.

1.3.
Annihilators

Fix some left-invariant metric
d
on a finitely generated group
G
.

In studying the structure of the set of Busemann points, a natural subgroup
arises, as we now explain.

A group
G
acts naturally on
\overline{(G,d)}
by
x.h(y)=h(x^{-1}y)-h(x^{-1})
.

A subset
L\subseteq\overline{(G,d)}
is called
invariant
if

x.L=\{x.h\ :\ h\in L\}=L
for all
x\in G
.

Note that
x.b_{y}=b_{xy}
, so
\{b_{x}:x\in G\}
and its complement
\partial(G,d)
are invariant. Also, if
d_{S}
is a Cayley metric, then for any infinite geodesic
\gamma=(\gamma_{n})_{n}

we have that
(x\gamma_{n})_{n}
is also a geodesic, so
\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
is an invariant set.

Given a subset
L\subseteq\partial(G,d)
we define the
annihilator
of
L
to be

N_{L}:=\bigl\{x\in G\ :\ \forall\ h\in L\ ,\ h(x)=0\bigr\}.

We write
N=N_{\partial(G,d)}
, the annihilator of the whole metric-functional boundary.
Note that the smaller
L
is, the larger
N_{L}
becomes.

Proposition
\@upn
1.3

If
L\subseteq\partial(G,d)
is invariant, then
N_{L}
is a subgroup.

Proof.

This is immediate from
h(x^{-1}y)=x.h(y)-h(x)
and
h(1)=0
.

\sqcap
\sqcup

We prove that the annihilators for two most prominent invariant subsets
L
coincide.

Theorem
\@upn
1.4

Let
d_{S}
be a Cayley metric on a group
G
.

Then,
N_{\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})}=N
.

The proof of Theorem
1.4
is at the end of Section
2
.

The annihilator provides a key step in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, stated in the following lemma and proved in Section
5
.

Lemma
\@upn
1.5

Let
d_{S}
be a Cayley metric on a group
G
.

If the set of Busemann points
\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
is finite then
N
is finite.

Finally, we have the following observation which could be of independent interest.

Proposition
\@upn
1.6

Let
d
be a proper left-invariant metric on
G
taking integer values. Then
N
is a locally finite group;
i.e.,
any finitely generated subgroup of
N
is finite.

The proof of Proposition
1.6
is in Section
3
.

Given a group
H
we write
L(H)\in\{1,2,\ldots,\infty\}
to be the supremum over the sizes of a locally finite subgroup of
H
.

Lemma
\@upn
1.7

Let
d
be a proper, left-invariant, integer valued metric on a group
G
.
Let
N=N_{\partial(G,d)}
be the annihilator subgroup.
Then,

|N|\leq\inf_{H\leq G}[G:H]\cdot L(H).

Proof.

Let
H\leq G
be some subgroup. Notice that
[N:H\cap N]\leq[G:H]
, since whenever
(g_{i})_{i\in I}\in N
are in distinct cosets of
N\cap H
, then they are in distinct cosets of
H
.
Now,
H\cap N
is a locally finite subgroup of
H
, implying that

|N|\leq[G:H]\cdot|H\cap N|\leq[G:H]\cdot L(H).

\sqcap
\sqcup

Corollary
\@upn
1.8

If
H\leq G
is a subgroup such that
H\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
then for any proper, left-invariant integer valued metric
the annihilator satisfies
|N|\leq[G:H]
.

Proof.

If
H\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, then
H
is torsion free, and specifically
L(H)=1
.

\sqcap
\sqcup

Of course, this raises the question whether
N
itself can be infinite, or if it must be finite (equivalently, finitely generated). We give an example at the end of Section
3
. However, the following question remains open:

Question
\@upn
1.9

Does there exists a Cayley metric
d_{S}
for which
N=N_{\partial(G,d_{S})}
is infinite?

We conclude this section with two examples of Cayley metrics for which
N\neq\{1\}
.

Example
\@upn
1.10

Consider
G=\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, generated by
S=\{(\pm 1,0),(\pm 1,\pm 1)\}
. We have

d_{S}\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr)=\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|

as soon as
\left|x-x^{\prime}\right|\geq n-1
. It follows that
d_{S}\bigl((0,y),g\bigr)=d_{S}\bigl(1,g\bigr)
for all but finitely many
g\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, hence
N=\{(0,y):y\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\}
by Lemma
3.1
.

Figure 1.
The Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, with
N
in red.

Example
\@upn
1.11

Consider a group
G
, a finite symmetric generating set
S_{1}
and a finite subgroup
F
. We take
S=FS_{1}F
and prove that
N=N_{\partial(G,d_{S})}\geq F
.

For all
f\in F
and
y\in G-F
, we have
d_{S}(f,y)=d_{S}(1,y)
. Indeed, if
y=s_{1}s_{2}\ldots s_{\ell}
with
s_{i}\in S
, then
f^{-1}y=(f^{-1}s_{1})s_{2}\ldots s_{\ell}
and
f^{-1}s_{1}\in S
, proving that

d_{S}\bigl(f,y\bigr)=\left|f^{-1}y\right|_{S}\leq\left|y\right|_{S}=d_{S}\bigl(1,y\bigr).

The reverse inequality follows from the same argument applied to
f^{\prime}=f^{-1}\in F
and
y^{\prime}=f^{-1}y\in G-F
. We conclude that
f\in N
using Lemma
3.1
.

2.
Geodesics

Let
d_{S}
be a Cayley metric on a finitely generated group
G
.

A
finite geodesic
from
x
to
y
is a sequence
x=\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{\ell}=y

such that for all
0\leq i,j\leq\ell
we have
d_{S}(\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j})=\left|i-j\right|
.
An
infinite geodesic
is a sequence
(\gamma_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
such that
for all
i,j\geq 0
we have
d_{S}(\gamma_{i},\gamma_{j})=\left|i-j\right|
.
The
length
of a geodesic
\gamma
is
|\gamma|=\ell
if
\gamma=(\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{\ell})
,
and
|\gamma|=\infty
if
\gamma
is an infinite geodesic.

Recall that
\gamma_{\infty}:=\lim_{n}b_{\gamma_{n}}\in\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
.

For a metric space
(X,d)
and
x,y\in X
define the
segment

between
x,y
to be

[x,y]:=\bigl\{z\in X\ :\ d(x,y)=d(x,z)+d(z,y)\bigr\}.

Note that if
(\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{\ell})
is a geodesic from
x
to
y

then
\{\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{\ell}\}\subseteq[x,y]
.

The following is a simple but important observation.

Lemma
\@upn
2.1

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.
For any
y\in X
and any
z\in[x_{0},y]
we have that
b_{z}\geq b_{y}
.

Proof.

Compute:

\displaystyle b_{z}(x)

\displaystyle=d(z,x)-d(z,x_{0})=d(z,x)-d(y,x_{0})+d(z,y)\geq d(y,x)-d(y,x_{0})=b_{y}(x).\;\hbox{\hbox to0.0pt{$\sqcap$\hss}$\sqcup$}

Lemma
\@upn
2.2

Let
\gamma^{(n)}=(1=\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{\ell_{n}})

be a sequence of geodesics starting at
1
and of growing length
\ell_{n}\to\infty
.

Then, there exists a subsequence
(n_{k})_{k}
such that
(\gamma^{(n_{k})})_{k}

converge to an infinite geodesic
\gamma
starting at
\gamma_{0}=1
, in the following sense:
For all
\ell
, there is some
k_{0}(\ell)
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}(\ell)
we have

(\gamma_{0},\ldots,\gamma_{\ell})=(\gamma^{(n_{k})}_{0},\ldots,\gamma^{(n_{k})}_{\ell})
.

Proof.

This is just the compactness of the space
\{0,1\}^{G}

with respect to pointwise convergence.

Let
F_{n}=\{\gamma^{(n)}_{0},\ldots,\gamma^{(n)}_{\ell_{n}}\}
, and
f_{n}=\mathbf{1}_{F_{n}}
.
Compactness of
\{0,1\}^{G}
provides a subsequence
(f_{n_{k}})_{k}
converging pointwise to

f_{n_{k}}\to f\in\{0,1\}^{G}
.
For any
x\in f^{-1}(1)
there is some
k_{0}(x)
such that
for all
k\geq k_{0}(x)
we have
f_{n_{k}}(x)=1
.
Since
(\gamma^{(n)}_{0},\ldots,\gamma^{(n)}_{\ell_{n}})

is a geodesic, it must be that
\gamma^{(n_{k})}_{|x|}=x
for all
k\geq k_{0}(x)
.
Setting
\gamma_{|x|}=x
for all
x\in f^{-1}(1)
provides us with the required infinite geodesic.

\sqcap
\sqcup

As a consequence we have:

Lemma
\@upn
2.3

Let
d_{S}
be a Cayley metric on a group
G
. For any
h\in\partial(G,d_{S})
, there exists a Busemann point
\gamma_{\infty}\in\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
such that
\gamma_{\infty}(x)\geq h(x)
for all
x\in G
.

Proof.

Choose a sequence
(y_{n})_{n}
such that
b_{y_{n}}\to h
in
\partial(G,d_{s})
. In particular, we have
\ell_{n}:=|y_{n}|\to\infty
. Let
\gamma^{(n)}
be a geodesic from
1
to
y_{n}
. Passing to a subsequence, we may suppose that
(\gamma^{(n)})
converges to an infinite geodesic
\gamma
in the sense of Lemma
2.2
.

For each
x\in G
, there exists
r
such that
b_{\gamma_{r}}(x)=\gamma_{\infty}(x)
.
Moreover, for all
n
large enough, we have
\gamma_{r}=\gamma_{r}^{(n)}\in[1,y_{n}]
. By Lemma
2.1
, we have

\gamma_{\infty}(x)=b_{\gamma_{r}}(x)\geq b_{y_{n}}(x)\to h(x)

hence
\gamma_{\infty}(x)\geq h(x)
.

\sqcap
\sqcup

We now prove Theorem
1.4
, stating that
N_{\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})}=N
for Cayley metrics
d_{S}
on
G
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let
M=N_{\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})}
.
It suffices to prove that
M\subseteq N
, since the other direction always holds.

Let
x\not\in N
. Since
M
is a subgroup, it suffices to prove that either
x\not\in M
or
x^{-1}\not\in M
.

Since
x\notin N
, there exists
h\in\partial(G,d_{S})
such that
h(x)\neq 0
. Let us first suppose that
h(x)>0
. By Lemma
2.3
, there is a Busemann point
\gamma_{\infty}\in\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
such that
\gamma_{\infty}(x)\geq h(x)>0
, implying that
x\not\in M
. If instead
h(x)<0
, then

x^{-1}.h(x^{-1})=-h(x)>0

and the same argument implies that
x^{-1}\notin M
.

\sqcup

3.
The annihilator is locally finite

In this section, we prove Proposition
1.6
, stating that the annihilator
N
is a locally finite group. We start with a lemma characterising when an element
x\in G
belongs to
N
.

Lemma
\@upn
3.1

Let
d
be a proper integer-valued metric on
G
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

x\in N
.

(2)

d(x,y)=d(1,y)
for all but finitely many
y\in G
.

(3)

There exists
r(x)\geq 0
such that
d(x,y)=d(1,y)
for all
\left|y\right|\geq r(x)
.

Proof.

We prove
\neg(1)\Leftrightarrow\neg(2)
and
(2)\Leftrightarrow(3)
.

Let
x\notin N
. There exists
h\in\partial(G,d)
such that
h(x)\neq 0
, and
y_{n}\in G
such that
b_{y_{n}}\to h
. We have
d(x,y_{n})-d(x,1)=b_{y_{n}}(x)=h(x)\neq 0
for all
n\geq n_{0}
, and
\{y_{n}:n\geq n_{0}\}
must be infinite (otherwise
h=b_{y}
for some
y\in G
, see
[
12
, Proposition 2.2]
).

Suppose that we can find a sequence
(y_{n})_{n}
with
|y_{n}|\to\infty
and
b_{y_{n}}(x)\neq 0
for all
n
. Since
d
is integer-valued, we have
\left|b_{y_{n}}(x)\right|\geq 1
. We can extract a subsequence
(y_{n_{k}})_{k}
such that
b_{y_{n_{k}}}\to h\in\partial(G,d)
. We obtain that
\left|h(x)\right|\geq 1
, proving that
x\notin N
.

Finally
(2)\Leftrightarrow(3)
follows from
(G,d)
being discrete and proper.

\sqcap
\sqcup

Proof of Proposition
1.6
.

We consider a finite subset
U\subseteq N
such that
U=U^{-1}
, and prove that
H:=\langle U\rangle
is finite. Let
r=\max\{r(u)\ :\ u\in U\}
.

Consider the set
A=\{y\in H\ :\ \left|y\right|>r\}
.
Assume for a contradiction that
A\neq\emptyset
.
Then, we can choose
y\in A
so that
|y|_{U}
is minimal.
That is,
|z|_{U}\geq|y|_{U}
for all
z\in A
.

Now, we can choose
z\in H
and
u\in U
such that
|y|_{U}=|z|_{U}+1
and
y=uz
.
Because
|y|>r\geq r(u)
, we have that

|z|=d(u,y)=d(1,y)=|y|
, so
z\in A
, a contradiction!

We conclude that
H\subseteq\{x\in G\ :\ |x|\leq r\}
, which is a finite set.

\sqcap
\sqcup

Remark
\@upn
3.2

The assumption that
d
takes values in a uniformly discrete set cannot be dropped. For instance, for
G=H_{3}(\mathbb{Z})
and
d
any homogeneous metric on
H_{3}(\mathbb{R})
, we have
N_{\partial(G,d)}=[G,G]\simeq\mathbb{Z}
by
[
4
, Proposition 2.4]
.

Finally, we give an example of integer-valued metric for which
N
is infinite.

Example
\@upn
3.3

We fix
G
a finitely generated group and
H
an infinite locally finite subgroup. We can exhaust
H
as an increasing union of finite subgroups
F_{n}\nearrow H
. For instance, we can take
G=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\wr\mathbb{Z}
,
H=\bigoplus_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and
F_{n}=\bigoplus_{\left|m\right|\leq n}\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. We define increasingly large finite subsets that will serve as balls for the metric
\left|\,\cdot\,\right|
:

•

B_{0}=\{1_{G}\}
,

•

B_{1}\ni 1_{G}
is a finite symmetric generating set for
G
, and

•

B_{n}=F_{n}\Bigl(\bigcup_{k=1}^{n-1}B_{k}B_{n-k}\Bigl)F_{n}
for all
n\geq 2
.

Finally, we define
\left|g\right|=n
for all
g\in B_{n}\setminus B_{n-1}
. Fix
f\in H
, say
f\in F_{n}
. Then, for all
g\in G
such that
\left|g\right|\geq n
, we have
f\in F_{n}\subseteq F_{\left|g\right|}
and
g\in B_{\left|g\right|}
hence

f^{-1}g\in F_{\left|g\right|}B_{\left|g\right|}=B_{\left|g\right|},

i.e.,
\left|f^{-1}g\right|\leq\left|g\right|
. The same argument gives
\left|g\right|\leq\left|f^{-1}g\right|
as soon as
\left|f^{-1}g\right|\geq n
. Therefore, if
\left|g\right|\geq n
and
\left|f^{-1}g\right|\geq n
(which is true for all but finitely many elements
g\in G
), then
d(g,f)=d(g,1)
. It follows that
f\in N
, hence
N\geq H
is infinite.

\bigtriangleup\bigtriangledown\bigtriangleup

4.
Virtually abelian groups

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
.
Since Cayley graphs of virtually-
\mathbb{Z}
groups always have finite metric-functional boundaries (see
[
12
]
), it suffices to prove the following.

Theorem
\@upn
4.1

Lemma
\@upn
4.2

Let
G
be a group and assume that
H
is a finite index subgroup
[G:H]<\infty

such that
H\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Assume that
f\colon H\to\mathbb{R}
is a
1
-Lipschitz homomorphism (so that
f(x)\leq|x|_{S}
for every
x\in H
)
with the following properties:
There exists
x\in H
and a positive integer
p
such that
f(x^{p})=|x^{p}|_{S}
.
Also, for any
y\in H
we have that if
f(y)=|y|_{S}
then
y\in\langle x\rangle
.

Then the following hold:

•

For every
y\in H
, the sequence
(b_{yx^{pn}})_{n}
converges to a Busemann point.

•

Two sequences
(b_{yx^{pn}})_{n}
and
(b_{zx^{pn}})_{n}
converge to the same Busemann point only if
y^{-1}z\in\langle x\rangle
.

In particular, if
d\geq 2
then
|\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})|=\infty
.

Proof.

Write
w=x^{p}
.
By Lemma 3.4 in
[
12
]
, the assumption that
f(w)=|w|_{S}
implies that there exists a geodesic
\gamma
in
\Gamma(G,S)
such that
\gamma_{n|w|_{S}}=w^{n}
for every
n
, as required. So for every
y\in H
,
b_{yw^{n}}=y.b_{w^{n}}
converges to
y.\gamma_{\infty}\in\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
.

Assume that
b_{yw^{n}}
and
b_{zw^{n}}
converge to the same Busemann point, and let
\gamma
be as above. Proposition 2.1 in
[
15
]
tells us that since the two geodesics
(yw^{n})_{n}
and
(zw^{n})_{n}
converge to the
same limit, there exists a third geodesic that intersects each of them infinitely many times.
Specifically, there exist
n,m,k
such that
n\geq m
and

d_{S}(yw^{m},zw^{k})+d_{S}(zw^{k},yw^{n})=d_{S}(yw^{m},yw^{n})=d_{S}(w^{m},w^{n}).

Also, since
f
is a
1
-Lipschtiz homomorphism,

f(w^{-m}y^{-1}zw^{k})\leq d_{S}(yw^{m},zw^{k})\and f(w^{-k}z^{-1}yw^{n})\leq d_{S}(zw^{k},yw^{n})

so that

d_{S}(yw^{m},zw^{k})+d_{S}(zw^{k},yw^{n})\geq f(w^{-m}y^{-1}zw^{k})+f(w^{-k}z^{-1}yw^{n})=f(w^{n-m}).

Since
f(w^{n-m})=(n-m)|w|_{S}=d_{S}(w^{m},w^{n})
we get equality throughout:

f(w^{-m}y^{-1}zw^{k})=|w^{-m}y^{-1}zw^{k}|_{S}\and f(w^{-k}z^{-1}yw^{n})=|w^{-k}z^{-1}yw^{n}|_{S}.

By our assumptions on the function
f
,
this implies that

w^{-m}y^{-1}zw^{k}\in\langle x\rangle
and thus
y^{-1}z\in\langle x\rangle
, as required.

Finally, since
\langle x\rangle
is cyclic and
H\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, if
d\geq 2
we have
[H:\langle x\rangle]=\infty
and so the set
\{y.\gamma_{\infty}\ |\ y\in H\}\subset\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
is infinite.

Proof of Theorem

By the Lemma

-Lipschitz homomorphism

and some
x\in H

and positive integer

such that
f(x^{p})=|x^{p}|_{S}

Without loss of generality, we can assume
H
is a normal subgroup of
G
.
Let
\xi\colon H\to\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be an isomorphism. Since
H
is normal, for every
g\in G
the map

is an automorphism of

. In fact, this defines a representation

Consider the Cayley graph

\phi_{e}(p)\leq 1
for all

\phi_{e}(p)=1

Define:

is abelian (so

only depends on

and the coset of

in

\pi_{g}
for all

Define

is

and every

\pi_{g}(e)
is also an extreme point of

.

If

by definition.

Also, if

.

Step I.

\frac{\xi(x)}{|x|_{S}}\in P

Assume that

y\in H

\frac{\xi(y)}{|y|_{S}}\in P
.
Let

|x|_{S}=n
.
Since

(s_{1},s_{2},...,s_{n})
labels a cycle in

.
If it is a simple cycle,
\frac{\xi(x)}{|x|_{S}}\in P

If not, as before, there exist

(s_{i+1},\ldots,s_{j})
labels a simple cycle.
Denote

and

, so that
x=gyg^{-1}z
and

.
Since

and

.

Note that

By the induction hypothesis we get that both

Since

Now, let

and reversing the roles of

It remains to show that there exists

such that
f(x^{p})=|x^{p}|_{S}

By possibly passing to

Lemma
\@upn
4.3

Let
d_{S}
be a Cayley metric on a group
G
such that
|\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})|<\infty
.
Let
K=\{x\in G\ :\ \forall\ h\in\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})\ ,\ x.h=h\}
be the kernel of the action of
G

on
\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
.

Then, for any
g,h\in\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
there exists
q\in\{\pm 1\}
such that

g\big|_{K}\equiv q\cdot h\big|_{K}
.

As a consequence,
|g(x)-q\cdot h(x)|\leq 2[G:K]
for all
x\in G
.

Proof.

When
|\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})|<\infty
we have that
[G:K]<\infty
.
Let
g,h\in\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
and define
\varphi\colon K\to\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by
\varphi(x)=(g(x),h(x))
. It is simple to verify that both
g
and
h
are unbounded
[
12
, Proposition 2.2]
, hence
\varphi
is a non-trivial homomorphism.

Since
[G:K]<\infty
, we know that
K
is finitely generated. Let
V=\mathsf{span}\ \varphi(K)
be the vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
spanned by the vectors
\{\varphi(k):k\in K\}
.
We claim that
\dim V=1
.

To prove this claim, assume for a contradiction that
\dim V\geq 2
. Since
V\leq\mathbb{Q}^{2}
it must be that
V=\mathbb{Q}^{2}
. This implies that
\varphi(K)

is a finitely generated torsion free Abelian group
of rank greater than
1
.
So
K
has a quotient isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Note that
[K,K]
is normal in
G
since
K
is normal in
G
and
[K,K]
is characteristic in
K
. It follows that

G/[K,K]\geq_{\mathrm{f.i.}}K/[K,K]\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times T

with
d\geq 2
and
T
finite, that is,
G
has a quotient which is virtually-
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for
d\geq 2
. Combining Theorem
4.1
and Corollary 1.11 in
[
2
]
, we get that
|\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})|=\infty
, a contradiction!
Since
\varphi
is a non-trivial homomorphism, we conclude that
\dim V=1
.

Therefore, there exists
0\neq q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
g\big|_{K}\equiv q\cdot h\big|_{K}
.

Let
x\in G
. Choose
r\in G
with
|r|_{S}\leq[G:K]
such that
x\in Kr
.
So
y:=xr^{-1}\in K
, and
we have that
g(x)=g(yr)=g(y)+g(r)
and
h(x)=h(yr)=h(y)+h(r)
. Hence,

\displaystyle|g(x)-qh(x)|

\displaystyle=\left|g(yr)-g(y)+qh(y)-qh(yr)\right|

\displaystyle\leq|g(yr)-g(y)|+|q|\cdot|h(y)-h(yr)|

\displaystyle=\left|g(r)\right|+|q|\cdot\left|h(r)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(1+|q|)\cdot|r|_{S}

\displaystyle\leq(1+|q|)\cdot[G:K],

and this bound holds uniformly over
x\in G
.

We are left with showing that
q\in\{\pm 1\}
.

Let
\gamma
and
\eta
be geodesics such that
g=\gamma_{\infty}
and
h=\eta_{\infty}
. For every
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\Big|d_{S}(\gamma_{m},\eta_{n})-|\gamma_{m}|_{S}\Big|\leq|\eta_{n}|_{S}=n

For fixed
n
, there exists
C=C(n)
such that
|C|\leq(1+|q|)\cdot[G:K]
and
g(\eta_{n})=qh(\eta_{n})+C=-qn+C
.
Also, since
b_{\gamma_{m}}\to g
, there exists
m_{0}
such that for all
m\geq m_{0}
,

n\geq\Big|d_{S}(\gamma_{m},\eta_{n})-|\gamma_{m}|_{S}\Big|=|g(\eta_{n})|=|-qn+C|.

We conclude that
|q-\frac{C(n)}{n}|\leq 1
for all
n
, where
|C(n)|\leq(1+|q|)\cdot[G:K]
.
Taking
n\to\infty
we find that
|q|\leq 1
.

We now repeat the argument with the roles of
g,h
interchanged, and
\frac{1}{q}
instead of
q
.
(Indeed,
h\big|_{K}\equiv q^{-1}\cdot g\big|_{K}
and therefore
|h(x)-q^{-1}g(x)|\leq(1+\tfrac{1}{|q|})\cdot[G:K]

for all
x\in G
.) This yields
\frac{1}{|q|}\leq 1
and finally
|q|=1
, completing the proof.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

As mentioned the fact that for any virtually-
\mathbb{Z}
group all Cayley graphs have only finitely many Busemann points was
shown in
[
14
]
.
So we only need to show that if for some Cayley metric
|\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})|<\infty
, then
G
is virtually-
\mathbb{Z}
.

So assume that
G
is a finitely generated infinite group with a Cayley metric
d_{S}
such that
the set of Busemann points is finite
|\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})|<\infty
.
By Lemma
1.5
, the annihilator
N=N_{\partial(G,d_{S})}=N_{\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})}
is a finite subgroup of
G
.

Write
\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\}
and define

\varphi\colon G\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
by
\varphi(x)=(h_{1}(x),\ldots,h_{n}(x))
.
Let
K=\{x\in G\ :\ \forall\ h\in\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})\ ,\ x.h=h\}
be the kernel of the action of
G
on
the Busemann points
\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
.
It is easy to verify that
\varphi\big|_{K}
is a homomorphism with kernel
\ker\varphi\big|_{K}=N\cap K
.
Since
\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})
is finite we know that
[G:K]<\infty
,
and specifically,
K
is an infinite group.

We conclude that
K/(K\cap N)
is isomorphic to an infinite subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and specifically

K/(K\cap N)
is an infinite finitely generated Abelian group.
It is well known that the fact that
K\cap N
is finite then implies that
K
is virtually Abelian.

(Indeed, since
K/(K\cap N)
is Abelian, we have that
[K,K]\lhd K\cap N
is a finite subgroup.
Therefore, for any
x\in K
the set
\{y^{-1}xy\ :\ y\in K\}
is finite (because
y^{-1}xy=[y,x^{-1}]x\in[K,K]x
).
This implies that
C(x):=\{y\in K\ :\ [x,y]=1\}
is of finite index in
K
.
Since
K
is finitely generated, taking generators
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
for
K
we have
that the center of
K
satisfies
Z(K)=\cap_{j=1}^{n}C(x_{j})
, which is finite index in
K

as a finite intersection of finite index subgroups.)

Since
[G:K]<\infty
and
[K:Z(K)]<\infty
we have that
Z(K)
is an Abelian group of finite index in
G
.
Using Theorem
1.2
with the virtually Abelian group
G
, we have that
|\partial_{b}(G,d_{S})|<\infty

implies that
G
is virtually
\mathbb{Z}
.
