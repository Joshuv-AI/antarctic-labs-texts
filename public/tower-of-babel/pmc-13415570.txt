Deep-penetrating geochemical techniques for concealed deposits covered by semi-humid grassland: Zhaishang gold deposit, Western Qinling Orogen, China
Authors: Yuying Huyan, Ke Yang, Yiwei Song, Zhenyu Ma, Wang Zhang, Zhanbin Wang, Yongbao Gao, Liyong Wei, Baoyi Hui, DongXiang Jiang, Wei Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Western Qinling Orogen (WQO) is a major gold region in China, with gold reserves exceeding 1, 200 tons. Its complex topography, climate, and semi-humid grassland cover provide an ideal setting for evaluating deep-penetrating geochemical (DPG) methods. In this study, three DPG methods—the Fine-Grained Soil Prospecting Method (FGSPM), leaching of mobile forms of metals in overburden (MOMEO), and soil gas geochemical survey (H2S, SO2, and CO2)—were employed in the southern Zhaishang concealed gold deposit, which is covered by semi-humid grassland and various overburden materials. Results indicate that no single method reliably identifies concealed orebodies or alteration zones, yet their combination improves detection, especially for deep and thin ores. Gas geochemistry exhibits higher sensitivity. Differences in response between FGSPM and MOMEO likely stem from variations in sampling and chemical analysis. For FGSPM, fine-grained soil fractions (< 75 μm and 75–125 μm) exhibit similar capacities for adsorbing mobile Au, predominantly occurs in water-soluble and clay-adsorbed forms, with strong spatial heterogeneity. H2S and SO2 serve as effective gas indicators in semi-humid regions, though uncertainties affected by various factors (e.g., biological activity) require further verification. These findings offer a valuable case study for DPG applications and enhance the understanding of geochemical exploration in covered terrains.

Introduction

The exploration of concealed mineral deposits has become an increasingly important global objective. Geophysical and geochemical techniques have significantly contributed to the search for hidden orebodies in covered areas1–5, leading to the discovery of massive world-class ore deposits such as the Olympic Dam Cu-U-REE deposit and the New Missouri Pb-Zn deposit over the past years. As a key geochemical exploration approach, deep‑penetrating geochemistry (DPG) techniques have been widely applied in diverse covered terrains, including aeolian, alluvial, colluvial, grassland, and glacial environments, as well as in various deposit types such as orogenic gold, sandstone-type uranium, and Mississippi Valley‑Type (MVT) Pb-Zn deposits2–16. To date, numerous DPG methods have been successfully employed in detecting concealed deposits with promising results, such as geogas6,7, electrogeochemistry8,9, MOMEO (leaching of mobile forms of metals in overburden)10–12, the fine-grained soil prospecting method (FGSPM)1,13–15, biochemical prospecting (using plants)16,17, and soil gas geochemical surveys (SGS)18–21. Nevertheless, geochemical signals from deep deposits are often attenuated or obscured by thick overburdens, posing challenges for reliable detection. For example, geochemical anomalies can be difficult to accurately delineate and are often subject to ambiguous interpretation. Consequently, no major breakthroughs have been achieved in deep mineral exploration, especially for the semi-humid, grass-covered regions—a significant cover type (30%–40% of the Earth’s surface) representing one of the most widespread natural landscapes22. Such grassland areas are particularly common in the Western Qinling Orogen (WQO), a region known for its abundant gold resources in China23,24.

The FGSPM (a physical separation technique) and MOMEO (a chemical extraction method) primarily target fine-grained soil fractions. Both methods focus on isolating fine particles to concentrate mobile metal elements, thereby enhancing geochemical anomalies associated with buried orebodies14,20,21,25–28. The recommended particle size for analysis is typically below 120 mesh (< 125 μm) or 200 mesh (< 75 μm), depending on the landscape and grain‑size experiments15,25,27. However, the widespread application of SGS has been constrained by variable landscape effects and inconsistent gas-processing equipment, which reduce accuracy in mineral exploration. Recently, a new portable multi-component gas analyzer (PMGA) has been developed, offering significantly improved detection limits, high sensitivity, and cost-effectiveness20. This instrument enables on-site, real-time analysis of H2S, SO2, and CO2, and has proven effective in exploring concealed ore deposits in semi-arid regions18,21,29,30, even contributing to the discovery of a new Ag-Pb-Zn deposit20.

Previous studies on concealed deposits have predominantly focused on applying single DPG techniques. Additionally, SGS employing sulfur-bearing gases has proven effective in arid and semi-arid regions29,31–35, however, its applicability in humid environments remains limited and poorly understood21. Therefore, to evaluate the effectiveness of DPG methods in detecting buried mineral deposits and to explore the potential of sulfur‑bearing gases as geochemical indicators in humid regions, a comparative experimental study was conducted in the southern Zhaishang gold deposit, a semi‑humid grassland‑covered terrain. This sediment‑hosted deposit, with reserves of 115 tons, is located in the northern metallogenic belt of the WQO36, which lies mainly within Gansu Province, China, and exhibits substantial gold potential (exceeding 1,200 tons in more than 100 gold deposits37, such as Zhaishang36, Zaozigou38, Suolong39, Xiahe-Hezuo30 and Huyagou deposits40. As surface and shallow-buried deposits diminish, concealed mineralization has become a key exploration target in the WQO. In this study, results from three DPG methods—FGSPM, MOMEO, and SGS using PMGA (H2S, SO2, and CO2)—were compared for the first time in the southern Zhaishang gold deposit. A detailed evaluation of this integrated geochemical survey was presented. The aim of this research was to assess the potential of the hidden gold deposit in the WQO using sustainable and green techniques. More importantly, it may provide valuable insights for the geochemical exploration of concealed gold deposits in semi‑humid grassland‑covered regions.

Geography and geology

The Zhaishang Au deposit is located in the eastern of WQO (Fig. 1c), which is a major portion of the Central Orogenic Belt in China. The nearby subtectonic units are Qilian Orogen, North Qinling Block (NCB), North Qinling Terrane (NQT), South Qinling Terrane (SQT), Songpan-Gartze Basin and Sorth Qinling Block (NCB). The prospect area is situated around 20 km north of Min County, characterized by dirverse climatic zones from semi-humid to alpine humid, along with varied topography including gullies, ravines, terraces, river valleys, and mountains. The mean annual temperature ranges from 4.9 to 6.1 °C, with monthly extremes varying from − 6.9 °C in January to 16.1 °C in July. Annual precipitation averages between 590 and 650 mm, with about 70% occurring from June to August. Elevations in the area range from 2600 to 3300 m, and the terrain is predominantly rugged and mountainous. With the advances in exploration, the southern segment of the Zhaishang deposit, bounded by the F5 fault (Fig. 1a), has emerged as the primary exploration target, particularly in the deep subsurface, based on drilling, trenching, and geochemical anomalies. Most of the southern mining area is primarily covered by semi-humid grassland (Fig. 2a and b), followed by farmland and trees. The overburden is multiple, including residual slope deposits, Quaternary alluvium (e.g., aeolian loess), organically rich soil (Fig. 2e), and Neogene strata (e.g., red mud, sand, and conglomerate). These cover layers have a thickness varying from 0.2 to 10 m.

The exposed strata in the southern ore belt primarily consist of Devonian carbonaceous sandstone, slate, sandy slate, limestone, and bioclastic limestone (Figs. 1b and 2b–d). The distribution of four main gold orebodies is largely controlled by ESE-trending faults (Fig. 1b). These orebodies are generally subparallel, with spacing ranging from 200 to 300 m. They dip to the south at shallow levels and to the north at greater depths, with dip angles ranging from 30° to 88° (Fig. 2d). The gold deposits reach depths of up to 200 m. Associated secondary faults, folds (Fig. 2c and d), fractures (Fig. 3b), joints, and mineralized alteration zones are widely developed in these main gold orebodies/alteration zones. In addition, gold (Au) occurs primarily in pyrite (Fig. 3a and d), with minor native gold also observed (Fig. 3c). Polymetallic sulfides such as chalcopyrite, tetrahedrite, and sphalerite were commonly found in the Zhaishang deposit (Fig. 3e and f).

Methods

Two standard profiles (prospecting line 143 and Line 175) in the southern ore belt were selected to conduct the comparative DPG experiment. The PMGA18,20, is used to perform the SGS. Field procedures involved drilling holes 0.5–1 m deep into the surface cover layers with a spiral drill, followed by insertion of the probe to extract soil gases. Gas concentrations were typically displayed on the device within about 30 s. Given the instability of CH4, measurements focused on H2S, SO2, and CO2, with detection ranges of 0.001–5 ppm, 0.001–10 ppm, and 1–100,000 ppm, respectively. Test points were spaced at 30 m intervals (e.g., the distance from point 1 to point 2), based on geological features and orebody distribution. Before the survey, the consistency and reproducibility of the PMGA were verified according to the method by Cheng20.

The FGSPM and MOMEO are both based on chemical analysis of soil samples. Soil samples were collected at a spacing of 60 m, consistent with the SGS. These soil materials are composed of clastic minerals and clay minerals. The sampled materials consist of clastic minerals and clay minerals. The coarse‑grained soil fraction contains abundant quartz, feldspar, and carbonate minerals (e.g., dolomite and calcite), whereas the fine-grained soil is rich in clay minerals, iron-manganese (Fe-Mn) oxides, and organic matter6,15,28. Sampling was conducted in areas free from open mining and human pollution, with each sample consisting of 3–5 subsamples within a 5 m radius of the sampling site. The sampling and analytical procedures differ between FGSPM and MOMEO. For FGSPM, soils were collected at a depth of 0.2–1 m, targeting the bottom of the B horizon (subsoil) or the top of C horizon (parent rock)15,25,27 using a shovel. Samples were air‑dried and sieved into two particle-size fractions: 125–75 μm and < 75 μm. At least 150 g of each fraction was weighed for laboratory analysis.

Inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‑MS), Atomic emission spectroscopy (AES), and hydride generation atomic fluorescence spectrometry (HG‑AFS) were used for chemical elements analysis, with the specific detection limits listed in Table 1. Standardized quality control was applied, including: (1) duplicate samples (3% of the total); (2) blanks equivalent to 10% of laboratory replicate samples; and (3) certified standard reference materials (SRMs). The reported rate for analytical results was 100%. The qualification rate for standard reference materials (GBW07401–GBW07408, GBW07423–GBW07426) and blank samples was 100%, and that of duplicate samples was 97.9%. For the MOMEO10, during fieldwork, finer‑grained soil samples were collected from low‑lying areas and gully bottoms, particularly where white saline‑alkali soil was present. Sampling was performed at a depth of 0–5 cm, with each sample weighing approximately 500 g. After collection, samples were air‑dried, sieved to < 75 μm, and a final weighed portion > 150 g was prepared for laboratory analysis. Samples were then treated with a gold‑specific extractant (main composition: ammonium citrate‑thiourea‑sodium thiosulfate) to extract mobile Au, which was subsequently measured by ICP‑MS. The reporting rate for Au was 95.3%, and the qualification rate for the standard solution was 100%. Results

The fine-grained soil prospecting method (FGSPM)

Table 2 summarizes the minimum (Min), maximum (Max), arithmetic mean (AV), median, enrichment factor (EF), and other relevant statistics. Along line S143, the Au concentration in the 125–75 μm soil fraction ranges from 0.73 to 5.36 ppb, with a mean of 2.25 ppb and a median of 1.60 ppb. The < 75 μm fraction exhibits higher concentrations, averaging 2.50 ppb (median: 1.79 ppb; range: 0.78–6.97 ppb). In the line S175, the 125–75 μm fraction shows a mean Au concentration of 3.48 ppb and a median of 2.09 ppb (range: 0.78–13.37 ppb), while the < 75 μm fraction also displays elevated values, with a mean of 3.53 ppb and a median of 2.13 ppb (range: 0.78–12.74 ppb). All average Au concentrations in the analyzed grain-size fractions are significantly higher than the continental crustal average for mainland China (1.40 ppb)41. Statistical results for other elements (Ag, Sn, As, Sb, Bi, Hg, Cu, Pb) are also provided in Table 2. Notably, the fine-grained soil fraction (< 75 μm) demonstrates markedly higher concentrations of elements such as Au and Ag compared to the coarser fraction (125–75 μm). The degree of dispersion among elements, as illustrated in Fig. 4, varies substantially; for instance, Ag and Hg show the lowest concentrations, whereas Mn presents the highest.

The MOMEO

The concentrations of mobile (or active) Au obtained via the MOMEO are substantially lower than those derived from FGSPM. For line S143, mobile Au concentrations range from 0.18 to 4.50 ppb, with an average of 0.94 ppb and a median of 0.81 ppb. In line S175, concentrations vary between 0.14 and 18.14 ppb, averaging 0.69 ppb with a median of 0.45 ppb (Table 3; Fig. 5). Additionally, mobile Au in the soil sample is primarily distributed among four forms: water-soluble state (Water), clay minerals adsorption state (Clay), organic binding state (OM), and iron‑manganese oxide (Fe + Mn). In line S143, water-soluble Au exhibits the highest average concentration (0.61 ppb), followed by Clay (0.17 ppb), OM (0.14 ppb), and Fe + Mn (0.08 ppb). In line S175, water-soluble Au shows the highest mean value (0.29 ppb), while Clay and OM are comparable at 0.26 ppb and 0.25 ppb, respectively. The Fe + Mn fraction remains the lowest, with an average concentration of 0.09 ppb (Table 4). Figure 6 illustrates the distribution of mobile Au at each test point along lines S143 and S175. The relative proportions of the four forms of mobile Au vary significantly among test points. For example, along line S143, water-soluble Au (Water, Fig. 6a) dominated at points 3–5, whereas at points 1 and 6, mobile Au was predominantly associated with clay minerals. Similarly, from north to south in the line S175 (points 1–10), the proportions of Clay and OM increase, while the Water decreased. That indicates the distribution of mobile Au is strongly affected by the mineral composition and moisture content of surface soils. In other words, surface heterogeneity possibly results in mobile Au anomalies.

Soil gas geochemical survey (SGS)

Determining the threshold value is essential for distinguishing “anomalies” from “background” and for detecting signals of buried mineral deposits. Various methods have been used to calculate thresholds, including the 85% cumulative frequency42, mean ± k standard deviations (where k ranges from 1 to 3), deep learning43, fractal models44, and geostatistics45. Among these, the geometric mean (GM) has proven to be a more reliable threshold for soil gases (SO2, H2S, and CO2) compared to the arithmetic mean20,21. When no signal is detected by the instrument because the tested concentration falls below the detection limit, the detection limit value itself is used for the records. According to Table 5, the concentrations of H2S and SO2 are generally much lower (typically < 1 ppm) than those of CO2. For line S143, the GM values are 0.010 ppm for H2S (0.001–0.056 ppm), 0.154 ppm for SO2 (0.020–0.406 ppm), and 1326 ppm for CO2 (70–15.900 ppm). In line S175, H2S concentrations range from 0.001 to 0.045 ppm (GM = 0.007 ppm), which are lower than the SO2 concentrations (range: 0.046–0.364 ppm, GM = 0.120 ppm). The GM of CO2 for this line is 1010 ppm (100–4500 ppm).

Discussion

Applicability comparison

Both lines S143 and S175 are situated in the semi-humid grassland covered areas with limited ore deposits. Line S143 is characterized by three main alteration zones, confirmed by drilling, trenching, and field observation on sporadically exposed alteration features. The southeast part of line S143 has a thickness of 1.5 m Au bodies with 2.8 g/t. In contrast, only four thinner alteration zones without economic Au mineralization (grade < 1 g/t) were present along line S175, as inferred from field observations and one drill hole. Thus, line S175 can be regarded as a “background” geological profile relative to line S143. Along line S143, the FGSPM, MOMEO, and SGS all showed responses that correlate spatially with the buried ore bodies and alteration zones. Notably, at test points 3, 5, 8, and 9, high Au values (peaks) were observed in FGSPM, MOMEO, and the gas indices of CO2, H2S, and SO2, which spatially indicate the locations of the buried zones. In particular, point 9, representing a deep alteration zone, correlated well with peaks in the MOMEO data and the gas indices of SO2 and H2S. These positive indicators are highlighted with pink rectangles in Fig. 7. Compared to FGSPM and MOMEO based on chemical analysis, H2S and SO2 displayed greater sensitivity and synchronicity, showing more pronounced anomaly peaks. For example, FGSPM and MOMEO showed no peaks (point 2, Fig. 7d and e), whereas H2S and SO2 demonstrated significant gas anomalies at the same point (Fig. 7b and c). In addition, CO2 concentrations exhibited a relatively flat distribution from point 1 to point 15. However, a distinct peak in CO2 can be observed at points 4–5, which correlates well with the deep-seated No. 20 ore body (Fig. 7a). A similar pattern was observed along line S175, where there were thinner alteration zones and no economic ore bodies.

Along line S175, at least two integrated methods show spatially consistent responses to alteration zones. Gas anomalies (CO2, H2S, and SO2, at point 5; Fig. 8a–c) and MOMEO results (point 3; Fig. 8d) clearly identify the deep and thin No. 8 alteration zone. Additionally, the FGSPM (point 6, Fig. 8e) and the gas anomalies (point 10 and point 11, Fig. 8e) correlate well with the No. 10 and No. 11 alteration zones, respectively. However, distinct differences in response were observed among the three techniques. For example, gas indices respond only to the No. 09 zone, showing a significant increase in CO2 and a slight peak of SO2 at point 8 (Fig. 8a and b). At point 4, the FGSPM (Fig. 8e) exhibits a weak peak indicative of the deep No. 09 alteration zone. Interestingly, several “false responses” (highlighted in yellow) were observed. These unrelated signals may be attributed to human interference during gas surveys, sample contamination, or near-surface metal migration. Such locations were classified as potential target areas requiring further verification. For example, MOMEO (point 7, Fig. 8e) and H2S/SO2 anomalies (point 13, Fig. 8b and c) exhibited peaks along line S143, yet drilling confirmed the absence of mineralization in these areas.

Therefore, DPGs alone may not be sufficient to accurately identify hidden deposits. However, integrating the three DPG methods can significantly enhance the effectiveness of detecting alteration zones, even when they are thin, deep, and covered by thick overburden. For FGSPM, suitable sampling grain sizes in the southern belt of Zhaishang are < 75 μm and 125-75 μm, with only minor differences observed between the two fractions. Nevertheless, the optimal sampling grain size should be determined through pre-experimental trials in other covered terrains, considering overburden characteristics and cost. Moreover, the differing spatial responses observed between the high Au values by FGSPM and MOMEO may result from variations in sampling and chemical analysis. Despite inherent gas variability, the gas indices H2S, SO2, and CO2 remain applicable in semi‑humid grassland environments, offering a rapid and cost-effective approach. However, further evidence is needed to distinguish gas anomalies on mineralization from those influenced by non‑ore factors such as near‑surface processes.

Mechanism analysis

The key to DPG methods for identifying buried ore bodies or alteration zones lies in their ability to capture geochemical signals that have been obscured by overburden materials. Endogenic and exogenic geological processes, including tectonics, weathering, and climates, determine the various cover terrains. Moreover, the thickness and structure of cover layers, as well as biological activity and vegetation, can significantly impact surface geochemical anomalies, leading to variability in DPG performance26–29. Nevertheless, the underlying mechanism that generates these anomalies is broadly consistent and reflects geochemical migration processes5.

The MOMEO and FGSPM

In the southern Zhaishang region, concealed Au deposits are overlain by semi-humid grassland cover. The MOMEO and FGSPM techniques share a common multi-agent migration mechanism from the ore body to the surface via geogas5,46. However, MOMEO emphasizes chemical separation, whereas FGSPM emphasizes physical separation10,27. Compared to previous migration mechanisms identified in shallow cover layers19,47–50, geogas serves as the primary medium and driving force that enables long-distance (thick cover) migration and carries active elements (e.g., ions, complexes, nano-micron particles) to the Earth’s surface23,51,52. Faults, microfractures, pores, and joints act as pathways for this migration1,5,6. As mobile Au approaches the shallow surface, other driving forces, including capillary rise, plant root absorption, and evaporation, become significant in facilitating its further migration.

When active Au elements reach the surface, particularly in alluvial soils with developed grass and trees, they tend to be absorbed by these cover layers in various forms (e.g., ions and nanoparticles in the form of/alloys/compounds colloids)1,5,26. Previous studies1,15,27,28,53 have shown that fine‑grained soil samples, which generally contain less quartz, are dominated by clay minerals, organic matter, and iron‑manganese (Fe‑Mn) oxides. These components have high specific surface areas and charged surfaces, giving them a strong capacity to adsorb mobile Au. Moreover, the presence of clay minerals enhances soil conductivity, which further promotes the adhesion of mobile Au nanoparticles. Thus, the physical separation of fine-grained fractions (typically < 125 μm or 120 mesh) can amplify geochemical signals from ore bodies. The key to FGSPM lies in rigorous physical sieving to obtain soil from the B + C horizons weathered from the parent bedrock. Secondary fine-grained soils (e.g., aeolian dust and tailing contamination) should be eliminated due to their false geochemical anomalies unrelated to ore bodies. In this study, for example, deeper soil samples (0.2–1 m depth) were collected before sieving to minimize external contamination. Only through this process can the resulting geochemical anomalies from buried Au deposits be considered reliable. This technique is relatively stable and less affected by climatic variations (e.g., rainfall and temperature).

Compared to the FGSPM, the MOMEO primarily relies on selective chemical extraction applied to finer soil samples (< 75 μm), which directly captures mobile Au elements (regardless of their specific occurrence) adsorbed onto soil particles, rather than all forms of Au (e.g., Au hosted in sulfidic and silicate minerals). This explains why Au concentrations obtained by MOMEO are much lower than those from FGSPM. A crucial step in MOMEO involves collecting surface soil from low-lying depressions, especially in areas with developed surface salinization. Such soils are more likely to be enriched in “mobile Au,” making them an effective medium for detecting weak geochemical signals from deep ore bodies. MOMEO can indirectly reflect the four main mobile forms of Au at the surface through its selective extraction steps for active Au10. The higher extractable Au values for the Water‑, Clay‑, and OM‑fractions in Table 4; Fig. 5 indicate that the dominant mobile Au occurrences in the study area are likely nano/micron Au particles (alloys/compounds)1 adsorbed onto soil and ionic Au dissolved in water. In other words, in semi-humid regions (using southern Zhaishang as an example), groundwater and water-filled channels play important roles in transporting active Au to the surface, and surface moisture can affect Au occurrence and distribution.

In addition, Fig. 6 showed pronounced spatial heterogeneity in the distribution of mobile Au, as mobile Au was variably distributed among different phases at each sampling point, which was likely attributable to the variation of the humid climate and well‑developed vegetation. Such spatial variability also explains why FGSPM and MOMEO did not consistently correlate well with concealed ores or alteration zones at certain locations due to the complex surface processes and geochemical ambiguity. Identifying cover terrains is important for the application of DPGs. Not only the mineral composition of overburden materials, but also environmental factors such as rainfall, temperature, and vegetation play crucial roles, particularly in semi-humid grassland regions. Secondly, appropriate sampling strategies and highly sensitive chemical analysis can improve the accuracy of FGSPM and MOMEO.

The soil gas of H2S, SO2 and CO2

Compared to chemical analyses of mobile metal elements extracted from soil samples, the precise and stable collection of surface gases related to ore deposits is critical for SGS. Gases such as H2S, SO2, and CO2 exhibit strong potential as indicators due to their close association with Au and other sulfide deposits18,20,29,54. H2S and SO2 are the most common gas anomalies observed in many mining areas18,19,55. Their formation is primarily attributed to oxidation and hydrolysis processes in sulfide-rich ore bodies and alteration zones21,56, with additional contributions from bacterial sulfate reduction57 and amino acid-sulfur decomposition58. CO2 may originate from four potential sources: gas-liquid inclusions in ore deposits59, chemical reactions between carbonate minerals (e.g., ores, rocks, or soils) and acids36,54, the decomposition of organic matter60, and deep degassing processes61,62. Driven by gas diffusion, substantial gases migrate upward from the subsurface to the surface in the form of geogas bubbles6,7. In loose overburden, gases migrate directly upward through pores, leading to gas anomalies that appear directly above buried ore bodies. In contrast, within low‑porosity, tight strata, gases tend to migrate preferentially along fractures or faults. The distribution of faults can cause gas anomaly peaks to occur ahead of or behind the location of deep‑seated mineralization21. In other words, the morphology of gas anomalies can indirectly reflect fault development29. For example, the narrower gas anomaly peaks at point 3 on line S143 (Fig. 7) may indicate steeper fault dip angles, whereas the broader anomalies at points 13–15 on line S175 (Fig. 8) suggest gentler dip angles, in addition to being influenced by the size of the ore bodies and alteration zones. Deep gases such as CO2 and CH4 can also facilitate gas migration. Moreover, geogas bubbles can carry active Au elements adsorbed on their surfaces to near-surface soils, thereby generating the geochemical anomalies detected by MOMEO and FGSPM. When these gases reach the overlying cover layers, some H2S and other reduced sulfur gases are oxidized to SO2 within abundant O2, resulting in the co-occurrence of H2S and SO2 anomalies20, which are finally captured by gas detection instruments such as the PMGA.

Several factors can influence gas concentrations and anomalies, particularly for the low-content H2S and SO2 in the study area. High temperatures and well‑developed vegetation increase the possibility of ore-related gases in soils. Additionally, soils with low porosity, high permeability, and high clay-mineral content are favourable for preserving H2S21. In semi-humid regions, elevated moisture and root growth can also accelerate weathering depths, thereby promoting the release of sulfur gases from concealed gold-related sulfide deposits. Conversely, high water solubility, chemical instability, biological activity (e.g., animal excreta), and mining activities can introduce uncertainty into H2S, SO2, and CO2 signals, leading to “false anomalies” that may obscure the actual location of concealed mineralization. This complicates the use of such gases as indicators of buried deposits. A clear example is point 13 on line S143 (Fig. 7b and c), where distinct gas peaks exist despite the absence of corresponding ore bodies or alteration zones in the southern Zhaishang gold deposit. Overall, these gas indices can still effectively indicate buried alteration zones and deep‑seated signals in semi‑humid grassland regions, and they demonstrate greater sensitivity than FGSPM and MOMEO, which exhibit no response in the same sampling points (Figs. 7 and 8). Conducting SGS under warm, high‑temperature conditions is recommended to achieve higher concentrations of target gases in soil.

Conclusion

Three DPG techniques—FGSPM, MOMEO, and SGS (H2S, SO2, CO2) were compared in the southern Zhaishang Au deposit covered by semi‑humid grassland. While each method alone cannot precisely locate concealed Au ore bodies and alteration zones, their combined application appears effective even for deeply buried, thinly distributed mineralization. SGS exhibits greater sensitivity than FGSPM and MOMEO.Both FGSPM and MOMEO are based on the migration of mobile metal elements from depth to surface. This case provides a reliable case for using sulfur‑bearing gases as geochemical targets in warm and humid environments.
