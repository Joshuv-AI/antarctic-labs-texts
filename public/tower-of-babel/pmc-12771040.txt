Hesperidin and Its Polylactic-co-glycolic Acid-Nanoformulation Mitigate Rhabdomyolysis-Induced Acute Kidney Injury via Attenuation of Oxidative Stress and Inflammation in BALB/c Mice
Authors: Kevin Joseph Jerome Borges, Bushra Wasim, Abdul Hameed, Talat Yasmeen, Muhammad Raza Shah, Tooba Jabri, Komal Rao, Jabbar Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background & Aim: Preservation of renal morphology and function is essential in rhabdomyolysis (RM)-induced acute kidney injury (RIAKI) for which there is no established treatment. Hesperidin (HSP), a natural bioflavonoid with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory potential, seems promising for RIAKI prevention. Here, we evaluated the nephroprotective potential of HSP and compared it with its polylactic-co-glycolic acid (HSP-PLGA) nanoformulation in RM. Methodology: HSP-PLGA was developed by nanoprecipitation and validated by zeta potential, scanning electron microscopy, and Fourier-transform infrared analysis. BALB-c mice were pretreated with intraperitoneal HSP and HSP-PLGA. RM was induced using intramuscular glycerol. Serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase (CK), creatinine, and urea were estimated. H&E, PAS, and immunohistochemistry for hemoxygenase-1, tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α), and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) were done. Tissue levels of lipid peroxidation (LP), superoxide dismutase (SOD), reduced glutathione (GSH), and nitric oxide (NO) were determined. ELISA was used to estimate interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), and interferon-γ levels. Results: A significant increase in serum CK, LDH, urea, and creatinine validated the RIAKI model. Treatment with HSP and HSP-PLGA significantly reduced serum urea and creatinine levels. Intervention also enhanced antioxidant defense by lowering LP and NO, and increasing SOD and GSH levels. HSP and HSP-PLGA reduced inflammation by suppressing TNF-α, iNOS, IL-6, and interferon-γ, and increasing IL-10. Histologically, this translated into reduced hyaline casts and tubular necrosis. These promising findings were validated by increased expression of the protective enzyme hemeoxygenase-1, thus highlighting the compound’s potential in mitigating RIAKI. Moreover, nanoeffect calculation on different parameters exhibited more pronounced effects of HSP-PLGA over HSP. Conclusion: HSP decreases oxidative stress and inflammation, thus preserving renal morphology and function. HSP-PLGA enhances the nephroprotective effects of HSP.

Introduction

Rhabdomyolysis (RM) is a potentially life-threatening condition in which sarcoplasmic contents, including myoglobin, creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and ions such as potassium and phosphate, are released into the circulation, causing multiorgan damage, particularly in the kidney tubules. Approximately 85% of patients with crush injuries develop clinical or subclinical RM. It is most prevalent in road traffic accidents and natural disasters, especially earthquakes. In animal models for research, however, glycerol is the most commonly used substance for induction of RM.

The most common complication of RM is acute kidney injury (AKI), which on its own can be fatal. Over 50% of cases of RM result in AKI, hence being one of its leading causes. The etiology of RM can be diverse, with road traffic accidents, alcoholism, and drugs being involved in more than 80% of cases.

The most critical content released from a damaged myocyte is myoglobin, the prime target of which is the kidney tubules. Damaged myocytes also tend to hold water within themselves, which creates a systemic environment similar to hypovolemia, thus exacerbating tubular damage. Myoglobin, on entering the renal tubular cells, induces oxidative stress with resultant injury to the membrane-bound organelle. In response, renal tubules secrete inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukins (ILs), which attract neutrophils, natural killer cells, and mononuclear cells. These cells further release myeloperoxidase, which directly damages the cell membrane, and also interferon gamma (IFN-γ), which causes further release of TNF-α, thus initiating a vicious cycle. Moreover, excess myoglobin in the lumen of the renal tubules leads to the formation of hyaline casts. , Recently, nitric oxide­(NO)-induced damage has also been implicated to be active in tubular injury caused by RM.

Histologically, RM primarily affects the proximal convoluted tubule (PCT), typically characterized by loss of the proximal tubule brush border, detachment of the tubular basement membrane, dilation of the tubules, and cast formation. Although AKI is a reversible condition, it has the potential to quickly turn into acute tubular necrosis (ATN). Thus, most therapies are directed toward preventing AKI once the risk develops.

Recently, natural compounds have gained significant attention for their protection against RM-induced AKI (RIAKI). Compounds such as vanillin, various polyphenols, flavonoids, and terpenoids have been extensively studied, but none have been established as a first-line therapy. − Among these, hesperidin (HSP) (3′,5,7-trihydroxy-4’-mehoxyflavonone-7-rutinoside), a flavanone glycoside with a rich traditional history and broad pharmacological activity, seems safe and promising in RIAKI. HSP is found mainly in the white part of citrus peels and peppermint leaves. It has been shown to have protective activity against oxidative stress in different organs. , Numerous studies have reported its beneficial effects in cardiovascular and liver disease, as well as cancer. −

Despite its expected potential in managing RIAKI, safety, and natural compatibility, one challenge is increasing the bioavailability of HSP at its target site, i.e., the renal tubules. Enhancing this aspect would be an effective strategy to further improve the potential, safety, and efficacy of HSP as an anti-AKI drug lead.

Nanotechnology plays a remarkable role in gaining surplus characteristic features to existing drug molecules, including improved solubility of poor water-soluble drugs, desired pharmacokinetics, targeted release, and increased bioavailability of the drug for an extended period of time. The advent of nanomedicine has introduced us to a wide range of hybrids, including nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems. Nanoparticles range from 1 to 100 nm in size in at least one dimension. , These systems enable therapeutic agents to accumulate specifically at diseased sites without interfering with other organ systems, thus considerably reducing the dose as well as untoward effects. − A commonly used nephrospecific nanovehicle is polylactic-co-glycolic acid (PLGA). It localizes 7 times more in the renal tubular cells than any other organ, thus making it ideal to deliver kidney-specific drugs. Experiments have shown that drugs used to treat AKI when delivered through the PLGA drug-delivery, have increased affinity for renal tubular cells.

HSP conjugated to PLGA (HSP-PLGA) has been extensively studied in recent times. Research has demonstrated its potential in modulating pathways of apoptosis in cancer models such as Ehrlich Ascites Carcinoma and breast cancer, as well as in colorectal carcinoma cell lines. In these, HSP-PLGA enhanced the compounds' solubility as well as bioavailability, improving the desired outcomes of therapy. − However, the potential of HSP-PLGA has not been evaluated in RIAKI, a health concern that still lacks an established intervention.

Hence, we aim to evaluate the effects of HSP on RIAKI. Furthermore, we synthesize an HSP-loaded PLGA nanoformulation and use it as a payload HSP-nanocarrier to evaluate whether it further augments its promising anti-AKI potential.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis of HSP-Loaded and Blank Nanoparticles

To prepare HSP-loaded-PLGA-nanoparticles (HSP-PLGA), a nanoprecipitation approach was utilized. For this, a HSP solution (10 mg/mL) and PLGA (100 mg/mL) were prepared in DMSO and sonicated until a clear solution was obtained. Subsequently, the HSP and PLGA solutions were mixed and stirred to make a homogeneous oil phase. The oil phase was added dropwise to 5 mL of double-distilled water containing 1% Tween 80 (0.1 mL) as a stabilizer while being magnetically stirred at 1400 rpm for 30 min. This resulted in the self-assembly of HSP-PLGA. After that, the nanoparticles were purified by centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 10 min to remove any unbound compounds and DMSO in the solution. HSP unloaded counterpart (PLGA) was also synthesized by utilizing the above-mentioned methodology. For this, PLGA (100 mg/mL) solution in DMSO was added dropwise to the continuously stirred double-distilled water with 1% Tween 80 (0.1 mL) at 1400 rpm for 30 min. The obtained nanoself-assembly was purified by centrifuging at 2000 rpm for 10 min to remove DMSO. (HPLC grade solvents were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA. PLGA, Tween 80, and HSP ≥97% purity were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Germany.)

Characterization of HSP-Loaded and Blank Nanoparticles

HSP Loading/Entrapment Efficiency

To determine HSP encapsulation (HSP-PLGA), a spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, UV-240, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) was used. Nanoparticles were dissolved in a known volume of acetone, and their concentration was examined by using spectrophotometry at 283 nm. The following equation was used to calculate the HSP encapsulation efficiency: EncapsulationEfficiency(%)=Wi−WfWi×100 Where W i represents the initial amount of drug loaded, whereas W f is the amount of drug in the supernatant.

Size, PDI, and Zeta Potential Analysis

The average hydrodynamic diameter, Zeta potential, and polydispersity index of PLGA and HSP-PLGA were analyzed using a Zetasizer (Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments; Malvern, United Kingdom). The nano suspensions of (0.05 mg/mL) were prepared in DI water and were introduced into a transparent cuvette, and then the cuvette was placed in a spectrometer. The medium viscosity, pressure, and refractive index were set at 1.0, 80.4, and 1.33, respectively. For Zeta potential measurements, a dip cell was used. The autocorrelation function from DLS measurements was analyzed using the CONTIN algorithm (as implemented in Zetasizer software) to determine the hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index. Each experiment was repeated three times, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Scanning Electron Microscopy Analysis

The morphological investigation of HSP-PLGA and unloaded PLGA was performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Apreo 2C LoVac Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The mounted samples on aluminum stubs were coated with gold and palladium by using a sputter coater (SC7620 Quorum Technologies).

Fourier-Transform Infrared Analysis

FTIR measurements of all samples, including HSP, PLGA, and HSP-PLGA, were performed by using a Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrophotometer (Thermo Nicolet, Madison, WI). The samples were analyzed in the range of 400–4000 cm–1 using the attenuated total reflectance (ATR) technique, in which the solid samples were placed directly onto the ATR crystal without further preparation.

Animals and Treatment Regime

Animal Research: Reporting of in vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines 2.0. were strictly followed, and ethical committee approval for the care and use of laboratory animals was obtained from the university’s Animal Ethics Committee (Protocol Number: 2022–08/KJ/FHS). The mice were bred and maintained at the animal house of Ziauddin University, Karachi. Conventional cages were used to house the animals, and free access to water and rodent chow was given. Room temperature was maintained at 22–23 °C with a 12-h light-dark cycle.

Experimental Grouping

6-week-old male BALB-c mice were divided into four groups of 6 animals each using the Resource Equation Method of sample size calculation. The power of the study was evaluated using the error degree of freedom method (error DF) and was calculated to be 20 (range = 10–20). This value is considered to be the “sweet spot” allowing sufficient balance between statistical power and utilization of resource as identified by the Resource Equation Method.

All animals were acclimatized for 24 h. The control group (CN) contained healthy nondiseased animals, which were given 0.5 mL of normal saline intraperitoneal (IP) for 5 days; no glycerol was given to this group. The RM group had animals in whom 0.5 mL normal saline IP was given for 5 days, followed by intramuscular (IM) 50% glycerol 10 mL/kg on the fifth day. The HSP treatment group (HR) was pretreated with HSP 200 mg/kg IP for 5 days, followed by IM 50% glycerol 10 mL/kg on the fifth day. , The HSP-nanoformulation (HSP-PLGA treatment group (HPR) was pretreated with HSP-PLGA at 20 mg/kg IP, i.e., 10 times lower dose than the pure compound, for 5 days, followed by IM 50% glycerol 10 mL/kg on the fifth day. All animals were water-deprived on the fourth day, 24 h before the glycerol injection. After giving I/M glycerol, they were once again given free access to water. On day 6, i.e., 24 h after glycerol injection, the animals were sacrificed humanely under general anesthesia using Inj: Pentothal Sodium at a dose of 60 mg/kg IP.

Blood Collection

After the animals were dissected, blood was drawn from the heart and collected in a gel tube, without any additives. The serum was separated via centrifugation at 3000G for 15 min. Serum creatinine (Cr), urea, CK, and LDH levels were measured via spectrophotometric analysis following the kit manufacturer’s protocol (BioResearch – Urea - ref: CS017 2100; LDH - ref: CZ008 0225) (Cr, HUMAN-ref:10051) (CK – ELMIC -ref: CKNA-0026).

Kidney Histopathological Examination

Kidney tissues were harvested and dissected into halves along their long axis. Half of each kidney was stored at −80 °C in 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes. The other halves were fixed in 10% formaldehyde solution overnight, dehydrated in increasing concentrations of alcohol, washed with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. These were then cut into 4 μm-thick sections and taken on glass slides for histomorphological and immunohistochemical examination. After drying, kidney sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stain and periodic acid Schiff’s (PAS) stain. The stained slides were visualized using the BestScope (BS-2038B) microscope. Images were captured with TCapture software using a BestScope microscope camera (BUC2B-320C). To quantify the damage to tubules, the tubular injury score was calculated. Tubules with gross dilation, necrosis, cast formation, detachment of the basement membrane, and loss of the brush border were considered damaged. The percentage of damaged tubules in a field of 400× was calculated using the software QuPath version 0.5.1. (H&E – Carl-Roth; Periodic Acid – BDH-Proabo; Schiff Reagent – Merck)

Kidney Immunohistochemical Examination

Four μm-thick kidney sections were obtained on positively charged glass slides. After deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval followed by Roti block was done. Hydrogen peroxide was used to block endogenous peroxidase activity. The tissues were then treated with rabbit-antimouse polyclonal hemoxygenase-1 (HO-1), TNF-α, and inducible nitric-oxide synthase (iNOS) (NYSE: TMO CAT# PA5-27338, PA1-40281, PA1-036) primary antibodies for 1 h in an incubator at 40 °C. After this, tissues were treated with donkey-antirabbit secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature, followed by DAB chromogen staining (Dako EnVision Dual Link System-HRP (DAB+) Code K4045). The tissues were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated with graded alcohol, cleared with xylene, and mounted with DPX. The immunoreactive score was calculated to quantify the expression difference of each antibody between the groups. This scoring is based on the product of the proportion of cells stained and the intensity of the staining. Proportion of cells stained are categorized as follows: No staining = 0; <10% staining = 1; 11–50% staining = 2; 51–80% staining = 3; >80% staining = 4. The intensity is then categorized from 0 to 3, depending on no color, mild, moderate, or strong staining, respectively. The evaluator calculating this score was blinded to the treatment groups.

Assays for Lipid Peroxide and Glutathione in Tissue Lysates

Lysates produced via the aforementioned protocol were used to evaluate levels of lipid peroxidation (LP), reduced glutathione (GSH), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and NO levels.

LP Levels

LP was estimated via the thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) assay, which calculates the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA). This assay gives us a direct calculation of the total oxidative stress. 50 μL of tissue homogenate was mixed with 50 μL of 8.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate in a test tube. 375 μL of 20% acetic acid was added, followed by 375 μL of 8% thiobarbituric acid (Macklin – CAS:504-17-6). The mixture was heated at 95 °C for 60 min. After this, 1250 μL of butanol: pyridine (15:1) (Samchun CAS No: 71-36-3; Daejung CAS No: 110-86-1) mixture was added to each tube. The tubes were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. 200 μL of the upper organic layer was taken in a 96-well plate, and optical density was noted using a spectrophotometer at 532 nm against blanks. Results were calculated in comparison with the total protein in lysates. (Pierce BCA Kit CAT# 23227).

Reduced GSH Levels

GSH levels were estimated using Ellman’s Reagent (ER) method. The standard curve was obtained by using known concentrations of GSH. ER was prepared by dissolving 5,5′-dithiobis 2-nitrobenzoic acid (Macklin-CAS:69-78-3) in 0.3 M phosphate buffer saline (PBS) 1% sodium citrate, yielding a 0.1 mM solution. 50 μL of previously prepared tissue lysate was taken in each test tube, and 450 μL of ER was added. The mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature for 15 min. Absorbance values against appropriate blanks were recorded at 412 nm. Levels of GSH were obtained against the standard curve.

SOD Levels

SOD was estimated using the nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) method. Superoxide anion radicals were generated in a flat-bottom 96-well plate by adding 150 μL of Tris–HCl buffer (16 mM, pH 8.0), containing 25 μL of NBT (CAT# 00225) (0.3 mM), 25 μL of NADH (0.936 mM) solution (CAS No: 53-84-9), and 50 μL extract. The reaction was initiated by adding 25 μL phenazine methosulfate (CAS No: 299-11-6) solution (0.12 mM) to the mixture, incubating at 25 °C for 5 min, and then the absorbance was measured at 560 nm against a blank sample.

NO Levels

NO activity was measured by using the Griess Reagent (GR) method. Sodium nitroprusside (Sigma-Aldrich Lot # BCCK0585) was dissolved in PBS to make a 10 mM solution. 200 μL of this solution was mixed with 50 μL of the sample. 50 μL of PBS pH 7.4 was added to the mixture. This mixture was incubated at 25 °C for 150 min.

GR was prepared just before use by mixing 1.0 mL of sulfanilic acid reagent (Sigma-Aldrich Lot # BCCK8777) (0.33% in 20% glacial acetic acid (BDH AnalaR Lot # B261 651423) and letting it react at room temperature for 5 min. Then 1.0 mL of naphthyl ethylenediamine dichloride (0.1% w/v) (Sigma-Aldrich CAS No: 1465-25-4) was added to the mixture to complete the reagent.

After 150 min of incubation, 50 μL of the incubated solution was withdrawn and mixed with 50 μL of the freshly prepared GR in a flat-bottom 96-well plate. This was incubated at room temperature for 30 min.

Absorbance was measured at 546 nm using a spectrophotometer. NO levels were measured against a standard curve using known concentrations of sodium nitrite.

ELISA to Detect IL-6, IL-10, and IFN-γ in Tissue Lysates

Kidney tissues that were stored at −80 °C were thawed and washed with cold PBS. 300 mg of total kidney tissue was taken and lysed with 1 mL of Tris-HCl lysis buffer. It was then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was aspirated into fresh tubes. These were used in ELISA kits to detect the expression of IL-6, IL-10, and IFN-γ following the kit manufacturers’ protocols (Cloud-Clone: IL-6 – SEA079Mu; IL-10 – SEA056Mu; IFNγ – SEA049Mu).

Calculation of Nanoeffect

The impact of the nanoformulation (nanoeffect) for each variable was calculated by the formula {(value of RM – value of HPR)/dose of nanoformulation}/{(value of RM – value of HR)/dose of pure compound}. ,

Statistical Analysis

Data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism v8.0.1 and MS Excel 2019. All quantitative variables are represented by the mean and standard deviation. Shapiro-Wilk was used to test the normality of the data. Data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc analysis for intergroup comparison, in which the disease group was compared with the control for model validation, whereas the treatment groups were compared with the disease group to evaluate response to treatment. In all analyses, a p-value of less than 0.01 was considered significant.

Results

Synthesis of HSP-Loaded and Blank Nanoparticles

We attempted to employ HSP-PLGA nanoparticles using a nanoprecipitation method, which involves dispersing PLGA in an organic solvent and combining it with an aqueous phase. This approach involved the single-step entrapment of the therapeutic agent within the polymer carrier. The choice of the liquid phase containing surfactants significantly influences the properties of the resulting nanoparticles. Organic solvents dissolve both hydrophobic and hydrophilic compounds, forming a homogeneous phase. Surfactants in the aqueous phase help lower surface tension and inhibit nanoparticle aggregation or coalescence. ,

Characterization of HSP-Loaded and Blank Nanoparticles

Size, PDI, and Zeta Potential Analysis

One important parameter for assessing the efficacy of a drug delivery mechanism is vesicle size because it affects the therapy as well as safety and efficacy. Furthermore, nanoparticles with a diameter of less than 200 nm have the longest blood circulation time because of their size and biocompatibility, which prevents the monoclonal phagocytic system cells from identifying them. The hydrodynamic size of the nanoparticles was investigated through DLS. The synthesized nanoparticles, PLGA and HSP-PLGA, showed average sizes of 144.3 and 120.9 nm with PDI values of 0.25 and 0.32, respectively (Table and Figure ). The mean diameter size of HSP-loaded nanoparticles was slightly less than that of unloaded polymeric nanoparticles, indicating the successful entrapment of HSP into the polymeric core. However, it is noteworthy that the sizes of the nanoparticles are uniform throughout the nanoscale range, which may be effectively used to achieve deep tissue penetration into the site of action. The particle surface charge, also known as the zeta potential, is a crucial indicator for the stability of nanocarrier systems. A comparatively high surface charge could act as a repellent between the particles, enhancing the system’s stability. The zeta potential values for PLGA nanoparticles and HSP-PLGA were found to be −20.5 ± 0.33 and −12.1 ± 0.25 mV, respectively, given in Table and Figure . Based on the findings of the zetasizer study, the negative surface charges for nanoparticles indicate better in vivo performance and higher stability.

SEM Analysis

The SEM technique was utilized to examine the surface morphologies of PLGA and HSP-PLGA. The ability of PLGA to self-assemble in nanostructures with spherical morphologies was confirmed by SEM investigation. As seen in Figure , the PLGA nanoparticles and HSP-PLGA both exhibited spherical surface morphologies with smooth surfaces. The SEM image revealed a cross-linked dense particle, suggesting that the polymer was sufficiently packed to function in the formulation as a controlled release agent.

SEM images of PLGA (A) and HSP-PLGA­(B).

The particle sizes observed by SEM were slightly smaller than those measured by DLS, which reflects the different measurement principles; DLS provides the hydrodynamic diameter of particles in suspension, including the solvent shell and any swelling, while SEM shows the dry-state particle size. For soft materials, SEM imaging at high acceleration voltage (e.g., 15 kV) can potentially induce slight deformation. In our study, this voltage was chosen to enhance image contrast and resolution after gold coating, which minimized charging effects. Any minor size changes or shrinkage caused by coating or high voltage were mitigated by optimizing imaging parameters and reducing the beam exposure time. The resulting morphologies showed no evidence of deformation or collapse and were consistent with the expected sample structures.

FTIR Analysis

The FT-IR results are displayed as transmittance spectra, showing the characteristic absorption bands of HSP, revealing the characteristic peaks for the stretching modes of the O–H and C–H bonds at 3417.2 and 2919.6 cm–1, respectively. The peak for the stretching mode of the CO bond is located at 1643.9 cm–1. The peak for the stretching mode of aromatic CC bond is located at 1515.7 cm–1. , The stretching vibration of the atoms is responsible for the large peak at 3502 cm–1 in the PLGA FTIR spectra. The distinctive band observed at 2871.9 cm–1 is responsible for the stretching vibrations of aliphatic C–H. In PLGA, the peak for the stretching mode of the carbonyl group is located at 1756.3 cm–1. The peak for the stretching mode of the C–O group is visible at 1087.6 cm–1. The in-plane and out-of-plane bending modes seen in the PLGA are responsible for the later bands in the FTIR spectrum.

The HSP-PLGA shows the peak for the stretching mode at 3487.3 cm–1, which corresponds to the O–H stretching vibration of the hydroxyl group. The peak for the stretching mode of alkane C–H was located at 2876.9 cm–1. The peak for the stretching mode of carbonyl CO appears at 1750 cm–1, which indicates intermolecular interaction between HSP and PLGA matrix, and a stretch for C–O was observed at 1168.1 cm–1, while the peak for the stretching mode of aromatic CC was identified at 1515.2–1385.6 cm–1. The spectra of HSP and HSP-PLGA share several characteristic peaks, but clear differences are observed in the carbonyl region. This could be the result of chemical interaction between HSP and PLGA matrix (Figure ).

Total Drug Content

10 mg portion of HSP-PLGA was dissolved in 100 mL of buffered PBS pH 7.4 and further diluted to 10 μg/mL. The final drug concentration was determined by using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-240 UV–vis spectrophotometer, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) with a quartz cuvette having a 1 cm path at 284 nm. Concentration of the drug in the nanoformulation was calculated to be 90 μg/mg.

Nanoeffect of HSP-Loaded PLGA vs HSP on Different Parameters

HSP Does Not Affect Serum LDH levels

Serum LDH levels were also significantly increased in the model group (RM = 319.5 ± 31.62 U/L vs CN = 169.9 ± 5.33 U/L; p < 0.001). Again, HSP exerts no statistically significant effects on the levels of LDH (HR = 309.5 ± 11.74 U/L vs RM = 319.5 ± 31.62 U/L; p = 0.8). Similarly, there were no significant effects of HSP-PLGA on LDH levels either (HPR = 305.7 ± 12.82 U/L vs RM = 319.5 ± 31.62 U/L; p = 0.64) (Figure B; Table ).

Prevention of an Increase in Serum Cr Levels

We observed that IM glycerol significantly increased the levels of serum Cr (RM = 1.19 ± 0.11 mg/dL vs CN = 0.47 ± 0.33 mg/dL; p < 0.001). Treatment with HSP significantly improved these levels (HR = 0.55 ± 0.24 mg/dL vs RM = 1.19 ± 0.11 mg/dL; p < 0.001). Noteworthy here is that HSP-PLGA, which was given at 10 times lower dose than HSP, significantly reduced the levels of Cr in RM (0.51 ± 0.25 mg/dL vs RM = 1.19 ± 0.11 mg/dL; p < 0.001). On calculating the nanoeffect, we determined that HSP-PLGA is 10.6 times more potent than HSP in preventing serum Cr levels from rising in RM (Figure C; Table ).

Prevention of an Increase in Serum Urea Levels

The effect of HSP and HSP-PLGA on the serum urea was evaluated. A substantial increase in the serum urea was observed in RM (RM = 107.5 ± 33.51 mg/dL vs CN = 38.47 ± 10.74 mg/dL; p < 0.001). However, HSP was observed to cause a substantial reduction in serum urea compared to the RM group (HR = 41.47 ± 9.89 mg/dL vs RM = 107.5 ± 33.51 mg/dL; p < 0.001). This reduction in serum urea was slightly enhanced by HSP-PLGA, which was given at 1/10th the dose of the pure compound (HPR = 38.82 ± 13.02 mg/dL vs RM = 107.5 ± 33.51 mg/dL; p < 0.001). The calculated nanoeffect thus reveals that the nanoformulation is 10.4 times more effective in reducing serum urea than the pure compound (Figure D; Table )

Preservation of Renal Tubular Architecture

Reduction of Tubular Necrosis, Cast Formation, and Infiltration

H&E-stained sections of the kidney reveal considerable tubular necrosis and cast formation in the RM group, along with neutrophilic infiltration. These findings have decreased to a minimum in the HR group, with mostly normal tubular architecture. Discussion

RM is a leading cause of AKI, the mainstay treatment strategy of which, once the risk develops, is prevention. Flavonoids like quercetin, naringin, fisetin, luteolin, kaempferol, and baicalein, as well as polyphenols like curcumin, have been tested on AKI models, but due to their high therapeutic dose, poor absorption, effects on nontarget organs, and improper bioavailability at the target site, none of these have been able to clinically establish themselves in RIAKI. −

To address this issue in the modern era of research, nanoformulation and nanodrug delivery systems open a promising arena. These include the folic acid-based hierarchical targeting nanodrug (HAND), polyphenol nanoparticles, and also PLGA conjugated to curcumin and quercetin. Although these have shown initial potential in ameliorating oxidative stress and inflammation in AKI models, they have yet to establish themselves as a standard treatment. Moreover, their nanoeffect superiority against their pure forms as well as over each other has not been determined and warrants vigorous comparative testing. −

The biodegradable and biocompatible polymer PLGA has been significant in the development of drug-delivery systems. PLGA is a versatile material used for regulated and sustained drug release because of its special properties. The FDA has approved a PLGA-based drug-delivery system for a wide range of therapeutic uses.

Physical properties like particle size, surface modification, as well as degradation products, raise concerns about the toxicity of PLGAespecially with repeated use, as these can affect in vivo interactions with biological systems. , Moreover, sustained release of compounds from these particles can affect immune-modulating patterns. However, PLGA nanoparticles have undergone extensive in vitro and in vivo assessments, demonstrating generally low cytotoxicity. Also, the products of degradation of these particles, namely lactic acid and glycolic acid, are readily metabolized in the body, thus aiding in their biocompatibility. There are increasingly promising results of its safety derived from recent research in specific conditions like cancer, autoimmune diseases, and vaccine formation. PLGA is thus placed in the GRAS (generally recognized as safe) category of use by the FDA. ,

Inspired by the concept, we evaluated the nephroprotective effects of the bioflavonoid HSP and the enhanced efficacy of its PLGA-conjugated nanoformulation (HSP-PLGA) in RIAKI, a model mimicking the human pathogenesis of AKI.

In our study, we successfully incorporated HSP into the PLGA-based drug delivery system to increase its delivery to the target, expecting it to efficiently and safely mitigate RIAKI. We successfully developed the RM model as demonstrated by increased levels of CK and LDH, the key enzymes released by damaged muscles. , The glycerol-induced RM model was used as it has proven to be the most efficient and reliable method of inducing skeletal myocyte damage. HSP exerts no pronounced effects on muscle protection, as indicated by no substantial variation in the levels of CK and LDH in the glycerol-induced RM model.

The elevated levels of urea and Cr are key indicators of renal dysfunction. Urea is produced in the liver as a neutralized end product of ammonia coming from amino acid metabolism. It is primarily cleared by the kidney tubules via a corticomedullary concentration gradient maintained by the tubular epithelial cells. Cr, produced by the breakdown of meat in the diet, is exclusively secreted by the kidneys and is thus used to estimate glomerular filtration rate. − Both of these indicators of renal function are considerably decreased by HSP, suggesting its promising nephroprotective potential. As indicated by the nanoeffect calculation, the HSP-PLGA nano drug-delivery system’s enhanced effect on lowering urea and Cr suggests the success of HSP delivery and increased efficacy to the target.

To further validate this evidence of improved renal function, histological evaluation through H&E and PAS staining was performed to see the direct effect of HSP on the renal architecture. Once in the serum, myoglobin readily crosses the filtration barrier and is quickly reabsorbed through the PCTs via receptor-mediated endocytosis. When the concentration of myoglobin is higher than the threshold of reabsorption, it begins to accumulate in the renal tubules. Being acidic, it rapidly decreases the filtrate pH. Moreover, intercalated cells in the distal convoluted tubules and collecting tubules maintain the pH of the filtrate by balancing the secretion of hydrogen and bicarbonate ions in response to the alterations in pH. This balancing is achieved via ATPase-dependent channels on the luminal and basolateral surfaces of these cells, which, in RM, become ATP-deficient. Diminished functioning of these channels results in a rapid drop in pH of the filtrate. As a consequence, myoglobin precipitates with Tamm-Horsfall proteins secreted by cells of the loop of Henle tubules, forming casts that obstruct the parts of the nephron lying distally. Proximal to the obstruction, increased filtrate pressure causes mechanical damage to the tubular epithelium. ,

The earliest morphological change in RAIKI is the loss of the tubular epithelial brush border. As damage worsens, the tubules begin to dilate and lose their attachment to the basement membrane. Cast formation in the tubular lumen is also seen. Finally, as the tubular cells begin to die, coagulative necrosis sets in. In our study, we report preservation of renal tubular architecture in the disease groups treated with HSP and HSP-PLGA, which directly contributes to the improvement of renal function. The improvement in the HSP-PLGA-treated group is of prime importance, as it is conducive to enhanced HSP delivery to the target, thus enabling it to perform its function more efficiently.

Our study also demonstrates the role of HSP and its PLGA-nanoformulation in triggering a key protective enzyme, HO-1, that enhances breakdown of myoglobin products in tubular cells. It thus indirectly increases its uptake, decreasing its concentration in the lumen, eventually limiting cast formation. Oxidative stress, initiated by tubular cell membrane damage in RM, causes dissociation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) from its inhibitor Kelch-like ECH-associated protein (Keap-1). Activated Nrf2 translocates into the nucleus and binds to the antioxidant response element (ARE) on the promoter region of the HO-1 gene with resultant increased expression. − The improvement of this signalosome again makes HSP a promising drug candidate to tackle the issue.

Another important signalosome that needs to be addressed in RM is the triggering of Fenton-reaction-mediated oxidative stress. In the renal tubular cell cytoplasm, lysosomal enzymes break down myoglobin into a globin chain and ferrihemate. HO-1 breaks down ferrihemate into ferrous ion, carbon monoxide, and biliverdin. Ferrous ions then enter the Fenton reaction, resulting in a substrate-based supply of hydroxyl ions, thus initiating a vicious cycle. Cell death induced by this mechanism is commonly termed ferroptosis. Moreover, free ferrous ions in the cytoplasm activate nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB). NF-κB is an important link between oxidative stress and inflammation (discussed later). In vitro and in vivo studies on other tissues have shown potent antioxidant properties of HSP via reduction in levels of hydroxyl ions in other cells and tissues.

LP, which represents oxidative stress, is increased in RM. Moreover, it has an inverse relation to the levels of SOD and reduced GSH, which are potent antioxidants.

LP begins with damage to the cell membrane and the release of free electrophiles. In a controlled environment, these bind to proteins, forming protein-adducts that eventually activate antioxidant responses via activation of Nrf2. Conversely, if these free electrophiles exceed the antioxidant response, they can further damage the cell and membrane-bound organelles, thus creating a vicious cycle eventually, leading to ferroptosis. Our results demonstrate that HSP has the potential to decrease LP levels in RM-affected renal tissues. Calculations also suggest that HSP-loaded-PLGA has improved the efficacy of HSP in lowering the LP.

SOD, the “mother of all antioxidants”, catalyzes the conversion of superoxide molecules to hydroxyl ions, which are then neutralized to molecular oxygen and water by GSH and catalase (CAT) enzymes. SOD and GSH are considered markers for the evaluation of antioxidant potential in the drug discovery process. As antioxidants, their levels in RM are decreased. In a similar manner to HO-1 expression, antioxidant response is generated through activation of the Nrf2/ARE pathway, which results in increased expression of antioxidant enzymes like SOD, glutathione s-transferase (GST), and catalase. GST is a key enzyme responsible for the activity of GSH. , In our study, we found HSP to be a potential drug candidate that, alone and yet more potently in its PLGA nanoform, counteracts ROS by increasing the levels of SOD and GSH. Collectively, these effects of HSP, when considered together, allow us to confidently suggest that it exerts its nephroprotective function by reducing oxidative stress in the kidneys.

A very important pathway involved in RIAKI that is often overlooked is nitrosative stress. NO is produced via activation of the inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) gene by NF-κB as well as interferon regulatory factor-1 (IRF-1). , Moreover, the availability of ferrous ions, which are an integral part of the structure of iNOS, further enhances its production. iNOS catalyzes the conversion of l-arginine to citrulline, producing NO as a byproduct. NO reacts with the superoxide ion to form peroxynitrite ions, which exert the same effect on the cell membrane as LP. Our findings suggest that HSP has inhibitory effects on both iNOS and NO production, thus limiting nitrosative stress.

The cumulative effect of intracellular oxidative stress, nitrosative stress, and increased filtrate pressure in RM causes injury to the tubular epithelial cells. Due to these effects, NF-κB is produced, which functions as an important cross-talk bridge between oxidative stress and inflammation. NF-κB enters the nucleus and binds to the promoter regions of TNF-α and IL-6, thus activating transcription. TNF-α also stabilizes the mRNA of IL-6 via activation of the MAPK pathway. Similarly, IL-6 also activates the STAT3 pathway, increasing TNF-α production. Thus, these two synergistically increase each other’s concentration in the cytosol. , Moreover, NF-κB also binds to the promoter region of the IL-10 gene, but in this case, it inhibits its production. Thus, the combined effect is activation of pro-inflammatory and inhibition of anti-inflammatory cytokines.

TNF-α and IL-6 are both potent cytokines that recruit inflammatory cells such as neutrophils, natural killer (NK) cells, and helper T-cells (Th1). Neutrophils mediate myeloperoxidase-mediated injury to the tubular cell membrane, further exacerbating the production of LP. , NK cells, however, secrete another potent cytokine, IFN-γ, which enters the tubular cell via receptor-mediated uptake, causing downstream production of IRF-1. IRF-1 enters the nucleus and attaches itself to the same promoter region of the TNF-α gene, thus enhancing its production. This creates another positive feedback loop, leading to the uncontrolled release of pro-inflammatory cytokines that enhance the recruitment of infiltrates, exacerbating tubular cell damage. , Interestingly, our study exhibits that HSP exerts a promising protective effect on this signalosome, as well as protecting renal tissues from inflammatory damage. This effect of HSP is attributed to its inhibitory effect on both TNF-α and IL-6. This anti-inflammatory potential of HSP, along with its antioxidant effect, makes it a promising drug candidate against RIAKI.

Along with its potential to reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines, HSP remarkably improved the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 to almost normal levels, hence reducing the RM-induced inflammation. IL-10 is known to exert its anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting the production of TNF-α and IL-6, as well as by modulating the activation of macrophages and other immune cells. , We thus implicate that HSP and HSP-PLGA reduce inflammation via limiting alterations in the IL-6/IL-10 and TNF-α/IL-10 axes in RIAKI.

Studies have shown that HSP-PLGA can be taken up by immune system cells like macrophages, ultimately contributing to alterations in their cellular responses, activation states, and cytokine secretions. This cross-interaction may lead to a reduction in pro-inflammatory mediators and enhanced production of anti-inflammatory cytokines, as observed in our study as well. Moreover, the controlled release of HSP within these cells may further enhance these effects, eventually reducing inflammation, as well as oxidative stress. This immune mediation effect, alongside the direct effects of HSP released in renal tubular cells, may result in synergistically enhanced nephroprotection. −

Finally, we report greater potency of HSP-PLGA as compared to HSP in reducing pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidative markers, as well as in increasing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant markers. PLGA nanoparticles, due to characteristics like size-dependent distribution, surface characteristics like zeta potential, and controlled release, have the ability to potentiate the pharmacological effects of HSP in the renal tubules. Pathophysiological changes in the injured tubular cells, such as enhanced permeability, also facilitate the accumulation. Moreover, the biodegradability of PLGA also accounts for the controlled release of HSP in renal tissues, enhancing local therapeutic effects. The interplay between physical characteristics of PLGA along with altered biology of the injured tissue eventually affects the distribution of HSP-PLGA, allowing it to express preeminence over pure form HSP in mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation in RIAKI.

Conclusion

HSP in its pure state, as well as PLGA-nanoform, attenuates oxidative stress by lowering LP and increasing the levels of SOD and GSH. It also reduces nitrosative stress by decreasing iNOS and NO production. Moreover, it reduces inflammation by lowering the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IFN-γ, as well as by increasing the levels of IL-10. HSP preserves renal tubular morphology by reducing hyaline casts by increasing the expression of HO-1 in renal tubular cells. These factors collectively result in the preservation of renal tubular cell architecture, ultimately preserving renal function. The nanoeffect potential of HSP-PLGA in performing these nephroprotective effects is far more eminent than that of its pure form.

Limitations and Future Recommendations

This study is limited to the extensive outcomes of the acute model of RM-induced kidney injury. Further studies are warranted to study aspects of chronicity, including fibrosis and long-term recovery outcomes. Moreover, though our study calculated the nanoeffect to compare the nanoformulation with pure form HSP, pharmacokinetic data like differences in distribution, metabolism, excretion, and bioavailability would further validate these findings.
